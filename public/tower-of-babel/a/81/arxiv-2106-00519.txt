On (local) analysis of multifunctions via subspaces contained in graphs of generalized derivatives
Helmut Gfrerer, Jiří V. Outrata

Abstract




Abstract.
The paper deals with a comprehensive theory of mappings, whose local behavior can be described by means of linear subspaces, contained in the graphs of two (primal and dual) generalized derivatives. This class of mappings includes
the graphically Lipschitzian mappings and thus a number of multifunctions, frequently arising in optimization and
equilibrium problems. The developed theory makes use of new generalized derivatives, provides us with some calculus
rules and reveals a number of interesting connections. In particular, it enables us to construct a modification of the semismooth* Newton method with improved convergence properties and to derive a generalization of Clarke’s Inverse Function Theorem to multifunctions together with new efficient characterizations of strong metric (sub)regularity and tilt stability.

Key words.
generalized derivatives, second-order theory, strong metric (sub)regularity, semismoothness
∗
.

AMS Subject classification.
65K10, 65K15, 90C33.

1
Introduction

When implementing the semismooth
∗
Newton method
[
14
]
for solving an inclusion of the form

0\in F(x)

with some set-valued mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we observed that it is advantageous to work with linear subspaces
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
having dimension
n
and contained in the graph of the limiting coderivative, i.e.,

L\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)

at points
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

However, this paper goes far beyond the analysis of the above issue and presents a comprehensive study of a class of mappings, whose local behavior can be described by appropriately constructed linear subspaces. This class turns out to be rather broad and the developed theory helps us both to suggest an efficient modification of the semismooth
∗
Newton method as well as to derive a number of new results concerning strong metric subregularity, strong metric regularity and tilt stability. More precisely, for the mentioned mapping
F
, primal and dual generalized derivatives are introduced, whose elements are subspaces of dimension
n
. In order to define these derivatives, we consider points in the graph of the mapping where the tangent cone amounts to a subspace and then perform an outer limiting operation in a certain compact metric space.

Our construction is motivated by the definition of the B-subdifferential (Bouligand-subdifferential) for single-valued mappings, whose elements are given as limit of Jacobians at points where the mapping is Fréchet differentiable. Note that the tangent cone to the graph of a map is a subspace whenever the mapping is differentiable at the point under consideration. Instead of computing limits of matrices, we consider the limit of subspaces given by the graph of the linear mappings induced by the matrices. When the mapping is Lipschitzian, then we obtain a one-to-one correspondence between the new primal generalized derivative and the B-subdifferential. However, for non-Lipschitzian single-valued mappings there will be a difference because we are considering limits of subspaces in a compact metric space whereas the underlying matrices can be unbounded.

There are some relations between our generalized derivatives and existing ones. The dual derivative consists of subspaces contained in the limiting coderivative and the elements of the primal derivative are subspaces contained in the so-called outer limiting graphical derivative. To the best of our knowledge, the latter has not yet been considered in the literature and is contained in the so-called strict graphical derivative.

Our theory is not applicable to arbitrary mappings. However, as already mentioned, the class of mappings which are suited for our approach, is rather broad and important for applications. In particular, every mapping which is graphically Lipschitzian, i.e., its graph coincides under some change of coordinates with the graph of a locally Lipschitzian mapping, belongs to this class. Graphically Lipschitzian mappings have been already considered by Rockafellar
[
33
]
. E.g., locally maximally hypomonotone mappings like the subdifferential mapping of prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions possess this property
[
27
]
. Thus, our approach is particularly suitable for second-order theory and we will establish a strong relationship with the so-called quadratic bundle introduced in the recent paper
[
35
]
.
Note that in
[
33
]
also a limit of tangent spaces has been considered. However, in
[
33
]
an inner limit with respect to the usual set-convergence has been used yielding a different sort of results.

Within the framework of the new theory one can introduce a new regularity notion leading to an adaptation of the semismooth
∗
Newton method. This notion is weaker than metric regularity and enables us to streamline the algorithm and to relax the assumptions, ensuring its locally superlinear convergence. Under the respective regularity condition it is also possible to show that a semismooth
∗
mapping is strongly metrically subregular, not only at the reference point itself but also on a neighborhood of it. It seems that this somewhat extended property of strong metric subregularity
around
the reference point has not been considered yet. As a byproduct, we present a characterization of this property by means of the outer limiting graphical derivative.

Finally we turn our attention to the property of strong metric regularity. Since strongly regular mappings are graphically Lipschitzian by the definition, the preceding theory enables us to reveal some interesting new connections. In particular, one obtains a generalization of Clarke’s Inverse Function Theorem to set-valued mappings and, when applied to specific problem classes, these results lead to new characterizations of strong metric regularity for locally maximally monotone operators and to a new characterization of tilt-stability. Compared with existing characterizations, the new ones have the advantage, that not the whole strict graphical derivative or limiting coderivative must be checked (as, e.g., in
[
20
,
8
,
28
]
) but only a condition on the subspaces contained in its graph. In this way the arsenal of available criteria for strong metric regularity (cf.
[
31
,
9
,
20
,
15
,
8
,
10
]
) and tilt stability (cf.
[
28
,
11
,
24
]
) is enriched.

The plan of the paper is as follows. After the Preliminaries, devoted to relevant notions from variational analysis, in Section 3 we introduce and analyze the crucial class of SCD (subspace containing derivative) mappings. In their analysis we make use of the mentioned generalized derivatives, for which some basic calculus rules are developed and exact formulas in case of graphically Lipschitzian mappings are provided. In Section 4 we introduce the notion of SCD-regularity, which plays a central role in the subsequent sections. Section 5 deals with the adaptation of the semismooth
∗
Newton method to SCD mappings. The property of strong metric subregularity around a point is characterized in Section 6. Finally, in Section 7 we present a generalization of Clarke’s Inverse Function Theorem and new characterizations of strong metric regularity and tilt stability for various classes of SCD mappings.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the whole paper, we will frequently use the following basic notions of modern
variational analysis. All the sets under consideration are supposed to be
locally closed
around the points in question without further mentioning.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a set in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
and let
\bar{x}\in A
. Then

(i)

The tangent (contingent, Bouligand) cone to
A
at
\bar{x}
is given by

T_{A}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{A-\bar{x}}{t}

and the paratingent cone to
A
at
\bar{x}
is given by

T^{P}_{A}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{A}\bar{x}\atop t\downarrow 0}}}\frac{A-x}{t}

(ii)

The set

\widehat{N}_{A}(\bar{x}):=(T_{A}(\bar{x}))^{\circ}

is the regular (Fréchet) normal cone to
A
at
\bar{x}
, and

N_{A}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}}}\widehat{N}_{A}(x)

is the limiting (Mordukhovich) normal cone to
A
at
\bar{x}
. Given a direction
d\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
,

N_{A}(\bar{x};d):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\downarrow 0}}{{d^{\prime}\rightarrow d}}}\widehat{N}_{A}(\bar{x}+td^{\prime})

is the directional limiting normal cone to
A
at
\bar{x}
in direction
d
.

In this definition ”
\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}
” stands for the Painlevé-Kuratowski
outer (upper) set limit
, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
.
If
A
is convex, then
\widehat{N}_{A}(\bar{x})=N_{A}(\bar{x})
amounts to the classical normal cone in
the sense of convex analysis and we will write
N_{A}(\bar{x})
. By the definition, the limiting normal
cone coincides with the directional limiting normal cone in direction
0
, i.e.,

N_{A}(\bar{x})=N_{A}(\bar{x};0)
, and
N_{A}(\bar{x};d)=\emptyset
whenever
d\not\in T_{A}(\bar{x})
.

The above listed cones enable us to describe the local behavior of set-valued maps via various
generalized derivatives. All the set-valued mappings under consideration are supposed to have
locally closed graph
around the points in question.

Definition 2.2
.

Consider a multifunction
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

(i)

The multifunction
DF(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
\mathrm{gph}\,DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is called the graphical derivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(ii)

The multifunction
D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=T^{P}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is called the strict (paratingent) derivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(iii)

The multifunction
\widehat{D}^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{D}^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\mid\;(x^{*},-y^{*})\in\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}

is called the regular (Fréchet) coderivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(iv)

The multifunction
D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\mid\;(x^{*},-y^{*})\in N_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}

is called the limiting (Mordukhovich) coderivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(v)

Given a pair of directions
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
multifunction

D^{\ast}F((\bar{x},\bar{y});(u,v)):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\ast}F((\bar{x},\bar{y});(u,v))=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\mid\;(x^{*},-y^{*})\in N_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}((\bar{x},\bar{y});(u,v))\}

is called the directional limiting coderivative of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
in direction
(u,v)
.

The directional limiting
normal cone and coderivative were introduced by the first author in
[
12
]
and various properties
of these objects can be found also in
[
13
]
and in the references therein. Note that
D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=D^{\ast}F((\bar{x},\bar{y});(0,0))
and that
\mathrm{dom}\,D^{\ast}F((\bar{x},\bar{y});(u,v))=\emptyset
whenever
v\not\in DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)
.

Note that by
[
34
, Proposition 6.6]
and the definition of the limiting coderivative we have

\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y).

(1)

If
F
is single-valued, we can omit the second argument and write
DF(x)
,
\widehat{D}^{*}F(x),\ldots
instead of
DF(x,F(x))
,
\widehat{D}^{*}F(x,F(x)),\ldots
. However, be aware that when considering limiting objects at

where
F
is not continuous, it is not enough to consider only sequences
x_{k}\to x
but we must work with sequences
(x_{k},F(x_{k}))\to(x,F(x))
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping. The B-subdifferential of
F
at
x\in U
is defined as

\overline{\nabla}F(x):=\{A\mid\;\exists x_{k}\to x:\mbox{$F$ is Fr\'{e}chet differentiable at $x_{k}$ and }A=\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla F(x_{k})\}

(2)

Recall that the Clarke Generalized Jacobian is given by
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)
, i.e., the convex hull of the B-subdifferential.

There exists the following relation between the B-subdifferential and the coderivative of
F
, which states that every element from the B-subdifferential defines a certain subspace contained in the graph of the coderivative.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping. Let
F
be continuous at
x\in U
and let
A\in\overline{\nabla}F(x)
. Then

(y^{*},A^{T}y^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x)\ \forall y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Proof.

Consider
A\in\overline{\nabla}F(x)
together with some sequence
x_{k}\to x
such that
\nabla F(x_{k})\to A
as
k\to\infty
. By
[
34
, Example 9.25(b)]
we have
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},F(x_{k}))=\{(u,\nabla F(x_{k})u)\mid\;u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
and therefore
(y^{*},\nabla F(x_{k})^{T}y^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{D}^{*}F(x_{k})
,
\forall y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. By passing to the limit, the assertion follows from the definition of the limiting coderivative.
∎

If the mapping
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is Lipschitz continuous, by Rademacher’s Theorem
F
is differentiable almost everywhere in
U
and
\|\nabla F(x)\|
is bounded there by the Lipschitz constant of
F
. Thus
\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})\not=\emptyset
for Lipschitz continuous mappings
F
.

Let
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be an extended-real-valued function with the domain and the epigraph

\mathrm{dom}\,q:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\;q(x)<\infty\},\quad\mathrm{epi}\,q:=\{(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid\;\alpha\geq q(x)\}.

The
(limiting/Mordukhovich) subdifferential
of
q
at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
is defined geometrically by

\partial q(\bar{x}):=\{x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\;(x^{*},-1)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}\,q}(\bar{x},q(\bar{x}))\}.

This subdifferential is a general extension of the classical gradient for smooth functions and of the classical
subdifferential of convex ones.

If
q(\bar{x})
is finite, define the parametric family of second-order difference quotients for
q
at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\Delta_{t}^{2}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})(w):=\frac{q(\bar{x}+tw)-q(\bar{x})-t\langle{\bar{x}^{\ast}},w\rangle}{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\quad\mbox{ with $w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $t>0$.}

The
second-order subderivative
of
q
at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
is given by

{\rm d^{2}}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})(w)=\liminf_{{\textstyle{t\downarrow 0\atop w^{\prime}\to w}}}\Delta_{t}^{2}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})(w^{\prime}).

q
is called
twice epi-differentiable
at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
, if the functions
\Delta_{t}^{2}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
epi-converge to
{\rm d^{2}}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
as
t\downarrow 0
.

Let us now recall the following regularity notions.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

1.

F
is said to be metrically subregular at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if there exists
\kappa\geq 0
along with some neighborhood
X
of
\bar{x}
such that

\ddist(x,F^{-1}(\bar{y}))\leq\kappa\ddist(\bar{y},F(x))\ \forall x\in X.

(3)

The infimum over all
\kappa\geq 0
such that (
3
) holds for some neighborhood
X
is denoted by
{\rm subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

2.

F
is said to be strongly metrically subregular at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if it is metrically subregular at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and there exists a neighborhood
X^{\prime}
of
\bar{x}
such that
F^{-1}(\bar{y})\cap X^{\prime}=\{\bar{x}\}
.

3.

F
is said to be metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if there is
\kappa\geq 0
together with neighborhoods
X
of
\bar{x}
and
Y
of
\bar{y}
such that

\ddist(x,F^{-1}(y))\leq\kappa\ddist(y,F(x))\ \forall(x,y)\in X\times Y.

(4)

The infimum over all
\kappa\geq 0
such that (
4
) holds for some neighborhoods
X,Y
is denoted by
{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

4.

F
is said to be strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if it is metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
F^{-1}
has a single-valued localization around
(\bar{y},\bar{y})
, i.e., there are open neighborhoods
Y^{\prime}
of
\bar{y}
,
X^{\prime}
of
\bar{x}
and a mapping
h:Y^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

such that
\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime})=\{(h(y),y)\mid\;y\in Y^{\prime}\}
.

[

is equivalent with the property of

Further,

. In this paper we will frequently use the following characterization of strong metric regularity.

( cf.

.

The properties of (strong) metric regularity and strong metric subregularity are stable under Lipschitzian and calm perturbations, respectively, cf.
[

Definition 2.8
.

We say that the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}

(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F

In this case we will also speak about (strong) metric subregularity on a neighborhood.

Proposition 2.10

In what follows we denote by
{\cal Z}_{n}
the metric space of all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
equipped with the metric

d_{\cal Z}(L_{1},L_{2}):=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|

where
P_{i}
is the symmetric
2n\times 2n
matrix representing the orthogonal projection on
L_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

Sometimes we will also work with bases for the subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Let
{\cal M}_{n}
denote the collection of all
2n\times n
matrices with full rank
n
and for
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
we define

{\cal M}(L):=\{Z\in{\cal M}_{n}\mid\;{\rm rge\;}Z=L\},

i.e., the columns of
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
are a basis for
L
. Further we denote by
{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L)
the set of all matrices
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
with
Z^{T}Z=I
, i.e., the columns of
Z
are an orthogonal basis for
L
. Recall that, given any matrix
\bar{Z}\in{\cal M}(L)
( or
\bar{Z}\in{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L)
), there holds

{\cal M}(L)=\{\bar{Z}B\mid\;B\mbox{ nonsingular $n\times n$ matrix}\}\quad({\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L)=\{\bar{Z}B\mid\;B\mbox{ orthogonal $n\times n$ matrix}\}).

Further recall that the
2n\times 2n
matrix
P
, representing the orthogonal projection on some
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
, admits the representations

P=Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}Z^{T},\ Z\in{\cal M}(L)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P=ZZ^{T},\ Z\in{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L).

(14)

Lemma 3.1
.

(i)

Let
Z_{k}\in{\cal M}_{n}
be a sequence converging to some
Z\in{\cal M}_{n}
. Then
{\rm rge\;}Z_{k}
converges in
{\cal Z}_{n}
to
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}
.

(ii)

Let
L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
be a sequence converging to
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Then there is a sequence
Z_{k}\in{\cal M}(L_{k})
converging to some
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
.

(iii)

Let
A_{k}
be a sequence of nonsingular
2n\times 2n
matrices converging to a nonsingular matrix
A
and let
L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
be a sequence converging to
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(A_{k}L_{k},AL)=0
.

(iv)

The metric space
{\cal Z}_{n}
is compact.

(v)

Let
L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
be a sequence and let
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Then
L_{k}
converges to
L
in
{\cal Z}_{n}
, i.e.,
\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L_{k},L)=0
, if and only if
\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}=L
in the sense of Painlevé-Kuratowski convergence.

Proof.

The first statement follows immediately from
Z(Z^{T}Z)^{-1}Z^{T}=\lim_{k\to\infty}Z_{k}(Z_{k}^{T}Z_{k})^{-1}Z_{k}^{T}
together with (
14
). In order to prove (ii), choose
Z_{k}\in{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L_{k})
and
Z\in{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L)
. Then
ZZ^{T}=\lim_{k\to\infty}Z_{k}Z_{k}^{T}
due to
L_{k}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal Z}_{n}}L
and consequently
Z=ZZ^{T}Z=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{Z}_{k}
with
\tilde{Z}_{k}:=Z_{k}(Z_{k}^{T}Z)
. Hence, for sufficiently large
k
we have
\tilde{Z}_{k}\in{\cal M}_{n}
and
\tilde{Z}_{k}\in{\cal M}(L_{k})
follows. This proves (ii) and (iii) follows from (ii) and (i).
In order to prove the compactness of
{\cal Z}_{n}
, consider a sequence
L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
together with basis matrices
Z_{k}\in{\cal M}^{\rm orth}(L_{k})
. By possibly passing to a subsequence we may assume that
Z_{k}
converges to some
Z
. Since
Z^{T}Z=\lim_{k\to\infty}Z_{k}^{T}Z_{k}=I
, we conclude
Z\in{\cal M}_{n}
and
{\rm rge\;}Z=\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
by (i). Hence the metric space
{\cal Z}_{n}
is (sequentially) compact. Finally, by
[
34
, Example 5.35]
there holds
L_{k}\to L
in the sense of Painlevé-Kuratowski convergence if and only if the projections
P_{L_{k}}
on
L_{k}
converge graphically to the projection
P_{L}
on
L
. Since the projections on subspaces in
{\cal Z}_{n}
are linear mappings with norm equal to
1
, graphical convergence of
P_{L_{k}}
to
P_{L}
is equivalent to uniform convergence
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|P_{L_{k}}-P_{L}\|=0
by
[
34
, Theorems 5.43, 5.44]
.
∎

We treat every element of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as a column vector. In order to keep our notation simple we write
(u,v)
instead of
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
v\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
when this does not lead to confusion. In order to refer to the components of the vector
z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
v\end{array}\right)
we set
\pi_{1}(z):=u,\ \pi_{2}(z):=v
.

Let
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
and consider
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
, which can be written in the form
Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A\\
B\end{array}\right)
. But we will rather write it as
Z=(A,B)
; thus
{\rm rge\;}(A,B):=\{(Au,Bu)\mid\;u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\doteq\Big\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}Au\\
Bu\end{array}\right)\mid\;u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\Big\}=L
. Similarly as before, we will also use
\pi_{1}(Z):=A
,
\pi_{2}(Z):=B
for referring to the two
n\times n
parts of
Z
.

Further, for every
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
we define

\displaystyle L^{*}

\displaystyle:=\{(-v^{*},u^{*})\mid\;(u^{*},v^{*})\in L^{\perp}\},

(15)

where
L^{\perp}
denotes as usual the orthogonal complement of
L
. Note that

(L^{*})^{\perp}=\{(v,u)\mid\;\langle v,-v^{*}\rangle+\langle u,u^{*}\rangle=0\ \forall(u^{*},v^{*})\in L^{\perp}\}=\{(v,u)\mid\;(u,-v)\in(L^{\perp})^{\perp}\}

and therefore

\displaystyle(L^{*})^{*}=\{(u,v)\mid\;(v,-u)\in(L^{*})^{\perp}\}=\{(u,v)\mid\;(-u,-v)\in L\}=L.

We denote by
S_{n}
the
2n\times 2n
orthogonal matrix

S_{n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{matrix}\right),

so that
L^{*}=S_{n}L^{\perp}
. If
P
represents the orthogonal projection on
L
then

is the orthogonal projection on
L^{\perp}
and
S_{n}(I-P)S_{n}^{T}
is the orthogonal projection on
L^{*}
. Given two subspaces

with orthogonal projections

, we obtain

d_{\cal Z}(L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*})=\|S_{n}(I-P_{1})S_{n}^{T}-S_{n}(I-P_{2})S_{n}^{T}\|=\|S_{n}(I-P_{1}-(I-P_{2}))S_{n}^{T}\|=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|=d_{\cal Z}(L_{1},L_{2}).

Thus the mapping

defines an isometry on
{\cal Z}_{n}
and a sequence
(L_{k})
converges in
{\cal Z}_{n}
to some

if and only if the sequence
(L_{k}^{*})
converges to

.

Consider the following relation between the graphical derivative and differentiability in case of single-valued mappings.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
open and a point
x\in U
. Then one has:

(i)

If
f
is Fréchet differentiable at
x
, then
DF(x)
is a single-valued linear mapping,
DF(x)(u)=\nabla f(x)u
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and consequently
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(x,f(x))={\rm rge\;}(I,\nabla f(x))\in{\cal Z}_{n}
.

(ii)

Conversely, if
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(x,f(x))\in{\cal Z}_{n}
and
f
is calm at
x
, i.e., there is some
\kappa\geq 0
such that the estimate
\|f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\|\leq\kappa\|x^{\prime}-x\|
holds for all
x^{\prime}
sufficiently close to
x
, then
f
is Fréchet differentiable at
x
.

Proof.

The statement (i) follows immediately from
[
34
, Exercise 9.25]
. In order to show (ii), we first prove that there is an
n\times n
matrix
A
such that

T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(x,f(x))={\rm rge\;}(I,A)
. Considering any
Z\in{\cal M}(T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(x,f(x)))
, we will show that
B:=\pi_{1}(Z)
is nonsingular. Assuming on the contrary that
B
is singular, there is some
p\not=0
with
Bp=0
. Then
v:=\pi_{2}(Z)p\not=0
because otherwise
Zp=0
which is not possible. Hence
(0,v)=Zp\in T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}
and there exists sequences
t_{k}\downarrow 0
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(u,v)
such that
f(x)+t_{k}v_{k}=f(x+t_{k}u_{k})

\forall k
implying
t_{k}\|v_{k}\|=\|f(x+t_{k}u_{k})-f(x)\|\leq\kappa t_{k}\|u_{k}\|
and
\|v\|=\lim_{k\to\infty}\|u_{k}\|=0
, a contradiction. Hence
B
is nonsingular and we obtain
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(x,f(x))={\rm rge\;}(B,\pi_{2}(Z))={\rm rge\;}(I,\pi_{2}(Z)B^{-1})
proving our claim with
A=\pi_{2}(Z)B^{-1}
. Hence
Df(x)u=Au
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the assertion follows once more from
[
34
, Exercise 9.25]
.
∎

Note that, when
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is Fréchet differentiable at

, then we even have
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(u,f(u))={\rm Lim}_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1}(\mathrm{gph}\,f-(u,f(u)))
.

We now introduce new generalized derivatives for set-valued mappings. We confine ourselves to the particular case
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and, as we will see in the sequel, this restriction still permits a considerable number of applications.

Definition 3.3
.

Consider a mapping
F:R^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.

We say that
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)=\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y)\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Further we denote by
{\cal O}_{F}
the set of all points where
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
.

2.

Associate with
F
the four mappings
\widehat{\cal S}F
,
\widehat{\cal S}^{*}F
,
{\cal S}F
,
{\cal S}^{*}F
, all of which map
\mathrm{gph}\,F\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{n}
and are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal S}F(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\{\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y)\}&\mbox{if $(x,y)\in{\cal O}_{F}$,}\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\{\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y)^{*}\}&\mbox{if $(x,y)\in{\cal O}_{F}$,}\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle{\cal S}F(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(u,v)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(x,y)}\widehat{\cal S}F(u,v)

\displaystyle=\{L\in{\cal Z}_{n}\mid\;\exists(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal O}_{F}}}(x,y):\ \lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L,\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k}))=0\},

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(u,v)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(x,y)}\widehat{\cal S}^{*}F(u,v)

\displaystyle=\{L\in{\cal Z}_{n}\mid\;\exists(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal O}_{F}}}(x,y):\ \lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L,\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})^{*})=0\}.

3.

(a)

We say that
F
has the SCD (subspace containing derivative) property at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\not=\emptyset
.

(b)

We say that
F
has the SCD property around
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if there is a neighborhood
W
of
(x,y)
such that
F
has the SCD property at every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
.

(c)

Finally, we call
F
an SCD mapping if

F
has the SCD property at every point of its graph.

Apart from the collections
{\cal S}F(x,y)
and
{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
of subspaces we will sometimes use the unions

\bigcup{\cal S}F(x,y):=\bigcup_{L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)}L,\quad\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y):=\bigcup_{L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)}L.

(16)

Remark 3.4
.

By definition of the regular coderivative there holds

\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{D}^{*}F(x,y)=\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y)^{*},\ (x,y)\in{\cal O}_{F}.

Remark 3.5
.

Since
L\mapsto L^{*}
is an isometry on
{\cal Z}_{n}
and
(L^{*})^{*}=L
, we have

{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)=\{L^{*}\mid\;L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)\},\quad{\cal S}F(x,y)=\{L^{*}\mid\;L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\}.

Hence,
F
has the SCD property at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if and only if
{\cal S}F(x,y)\not=\emptyset
.

Since we consider convergence in the compact metric space
{\cal Z}_{n}
, we obtain readily the following result.

Lemma 3.6
.

A mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}

Lemma 3.13

Proof.

We prove only the first equation. The inclusion
{\cal S}F(x,y)\subseteq\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(u,v)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(x,y)}{\cal S}F(u,v)=:S
follows easily from the definition of
{\cal S}F(x,y)
together with
\widehat{\cal S}F(u,v)\subseteq{\cal S}F(u,v)
,
(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. In order to show the reverse inclusion, consider
L\in S
together with sequences
(u_{k},v_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(x,y)
and
L_{k}\in{\cal S}F(u_{k},v_{k})
with
L_{k}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal Z}_{n}}}L
. By definition, for every
k
we can find
(u_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})\in{\cal O}_{F}
and
L_{k}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\cal S}F(u_{k},v_{k}))
such that
\|(u_{k},v_{k})-(u_{k}^{\prime},v_{k}^{\prime})\|\leq\frac{1}{k}
and

. Thus
(u_{k}^{\prime},v_{k}^{\prime})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal O}_{F}}}(x,y)
and
L_{k}^{\prime}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal Z}_{n}}}L

.
∎

We now provide some calculus rules.

Proposition 3.14
.

Given a mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, consider the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathrm{gph}\,F=\{(x,y)\mid\;\Phi(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,G\}.

Then for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
such that
\Phi
is continuously differentiable in some neighborhood of
(x,y)
and
\nabla\Phi(x,y)
is nonsingular, there holds

\displaystyle{\cal S}F(x,y)=\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))(:=\{\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}L\mid\;L\in{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))\}),

(20)

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)=S_{n}\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{T}S_{n}^{T}{\cal S}^{*}G(\Phi(x,y)).

(21)

Proof.

By the classical Inverse Function Theorem, there is some open neighborhood
W
of
(x,y)
such that
\Phi
is a one-to-one mapping from
W
to the open neighborhood
\tilde{W}:=\Phi(W)
of
\Phi(x,y)
and
\nabla\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is nonsingular for every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in W
.
By
[
34
, Exercise 6.7]
we have
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\nabla\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{-1}T_{\mathrm{gph}\,G}(\Phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))
for all
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
and it follows that
{\cal O}_{G}\cap\tilde{W}=\Phi({\cal O}_{F}\cap W)
. Consider
L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)
together with sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}(x,y)
and
L_{k}\in\widehat{\cal S}F(x_{k},y_{k})
converging to
L
. Then for all
k
sufficiently large we have
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,G}(\Phi(x_{k},y_{k}))=\nabla\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},y_{k})=\nabla\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})L_{k}\in\widehat{\cal S}G(\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})
showing
\nabla\Phi(x,y)L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})L_{k}\in{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))
by Lemma
3.1
(iii). This proves that
{\cal S}F(x,y)\subseteq\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))
.

To show the reverse inclusion, consider
L\in{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))
together with sequences
z_{k}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{G}\cap\tilde{W}}\Phi(x,y)
and
L_{k}\in\widehat{\cal S}G(z_{k})
with
L_{k}\to L
.
It follows that the sequence
(x_{k},y_{k}):=\Phi^{-1}(z_{k})\cap W
converges to
(x,y)
and
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},y_{k})=\nabla\Phi(x_{k},y_{k})^{-1}L_{k}\in\widehat{\cal S}F(x_{k},y_{k})
implying
\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)
by Lemma
3.2
(iii). Hence
\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}{\cal S}G(\Phi(x,y))\subseteq{\cal S}F(x,y)
and equation (
20
) follows. To prove the equation (
21
), just use Remark
3.5
together with the fact that for any

we have
(\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}L)^{\perp}=\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{T}L^{\perp}
implying

(\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{-1}L)^{*}=S_{n}\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{T}L^{\perp}=S_{n}\nabla\Phi(x,y)^{T}S_{n}^{T}L^{*}.

∎

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}

\nabla h(x)&I\end{matrix}\right)L\mid\;L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)\Big\}

(22)

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{*}(F+h)(x,y+h(x))=\Big\{\left(\begin{matrix}I&0\\
\nabla h(x)^{T}&I\end{matrix}\right)L\mid\;L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\Big\}

(23)

Proof.

We have

3.14

∎

Remark 3.18
.

Note that for a graphically Lipschitizian mapping

Corollary 3.19
.

Let

.

\displaystyle{\cal S}F(x,y)

\displaystyle=\{{\rm rge\;}(B,I-\nabla h(x)B)\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}(F+h)^{-1}(y+h(x))\}

(25a)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}0&I\\
I&-\nabla h(\bar{x})\end{matrix}\right){\cal S}(F+h)^{-1}(\bar{y}+h(\bar{x}),\bar{x}),

\displaystyle=\{{\rm rge\;}(B^{T},I-\nabla h(x)^{T}B^{T})\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}(F+h)^{-1}(y+h(x))\}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}0&I\\

of

Some examples of graphically Lipschitzian mappings
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
n
were already given in
[
33
,
27
]
. Next, we extend these examples and give an explicit description of the subspaces contained in
{\cal S}F(x,y)
and
{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
, respectively.
Recall that a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be monotone if

\langle y_{1}-y_{2},x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for all $(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F,\ i=1,2$.}

It is maximally monotone if, in addition, there holds
\mathrm{gph}\,F=\mathrm{gph}\,T
for every monotone mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{gph}\,F\subset\mathrm{gph}\,T
.
Next we define several types of local monotonicity.

Definition 3.20
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. We say the following:

1.

F
is locally monotone at
(x,y)
if there is an open neighborhood
X\times Y
of
(x,y)
such that

\langle y_{1}-y_{2},x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for all $(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X\times Y),\ i=1,2$}.

(26)

It is locally maximally monotone if, in addition, there holds
\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X\times Y)=\mathrm{gph}\,T\cap(X\times Y)
for every monotone mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X\times Y)\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,T
.

2.

F
is locally (maximally) hypomonotone at
(x,y)
if
\gamma I+F
is locally (maximally) monotone at
(x,\gamma x+y)
for some
\gamma\geq 0
.

Related with maximally monotone operators are the so-called firmly nonexpansive mappings.

Definition 3.21
.

1.

A mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called firmly nonexpansive if
\langle f(u_{1})-f(u_{2}),u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle\geq\|f(u_{1})-f(u_{2})\|^{2}
,
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

An
n\times n
matrix
B
is called firmly nonexpansive, if the linear mapping
u\to Bu
is firmly nonexpansive, i.e.,
\langle Bv,v\rangle\geq\|Bv\|^{2}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Note that an
n\times n
matrix
B
is firmly nonexpansive if and only if
\|2B-I\|\leq 1
, see, e.g.,
[
3
, Fact 1.1]
. Further, a firmly nonexpansive matrix
B
is positive semidefinite and satisfies
\|B\|\leq 1
and, when
B
is symmetric, these conditions are also sufficient for
B
being firmly nonexpansive.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Then
F
is graphically Lipschtzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with transformation mapping
\Phi(x,y)=(x+y,x)
and consequently
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Further, for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and for every subspace
L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)
there is a firmly nonexpansive
n\times n
matrix
B
such that
L={\rm rge\;}(B,I-B)
and
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(B^{T},I-B^{T})
.

Proof.

Let
F_{\rm loc}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given by
\mathrm{gph}\,F_{\rm loc}=\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X\times Y)
, where
(X\times Y)
is as in Definition
3.20
. Then
F_{\rm loc}
is monotone, has a maximally monotone extension
\tilde{F}
, cf.
[
34
, Proposition 12.6]
and
\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap(X\times Y)=\mathrm{gph}\,\tilde{F}\cap(X\times Y)
. By
[
2
, Corollary 23.8]
,
{\rm rge\;}(I+\tilde{F})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the resolvent
f:=(I+\tilde{F})^{-1}
is a single-valued, firmly nonexpansive mapping on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
f(\bar{x}+\bar{y})=\bar{x}
, we can find an open neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}+\bar{y}
such that
f(u)\in X
and
u-f(u)\in Y
for all
u\in U
. It follows that
(I+F)^{-1}(u)=(I+\tilde{F})^{-1}(u)
and therefore
I+F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{x}+\bar{y})
. Thus
F
is graphically Lipschitzian at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by Corollary
3.19
. Now consider a subspace
L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)
, where
(x,y)
is close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. By (
25a
) there is a matrix
B\in\overline{\nabla}f(x+y)
such that
L={\rm rge\;}(B,I-B)
. Since
f
is firmly nonexpansive, for every
(u^{\prime},f(u^{\prime}))\in{\cal O}_{f}
we have
\langle\nabla f(u^{\prime})v,v\rangle\geq\|\nabla f(u^{\prime})v\|^{2}
and
\|Bv\|^{2}\leq\langle Bv,v\rangle
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
follows. This completes the proof.
∎

If
F
is only locally maximally hypomonotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, it follows that
(1+\gamma)I+F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},(1+\gamma)\bar{x}+\bar{y})

.

.

Let

Corollary 3.24
.

A mapping

which is locally maximally hypomonotone on a dense subset of its graph is an SCD mapping.

We now consider the subdifferential mapping
\partial q
of some lsc function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 3.25
.

1.

A function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
if
q
is locally lsc around
\bar{x}
and there
exist
\epsilon>0
and
\rho\geq 0
such that for all
x^{\prime},x\in{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})
with
|q(x)-q(\bar{x})|\leq\epsilon

q(x^{\prime})\geq q(x)+\langle x^{*},x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}\quad\mbox{whenever}\quad x^{*}\in\partial q(x)\cap{\cal B}_{\epsilon}({\bar{x}^{\ast}}).

When this holds for all
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
,
q
is said to be prox-regular at
\bar{x}
.

2.

A function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
if for any sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q}}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}q(x_{k})=q(\bar{x})
. When this holds for all
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
,
q
is said to be subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
.

Proposition 3.26
.

Suppose that
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{x}^{*}\in\partial q(\bar{x})

\partial q
is locally maximally hypomonotone at
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})

\lambda>0

\partial q

(\bar{x},\bar{y})

\partial q

(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})

(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q

(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})

{\cal S}^{*}\partial q(x,x^{*})={\cal S}\partial q(x,x^{*})

Let
\tilde{q}(x):=q(x)-\langle{\bar{x}^{\ast}},x\rangle

\partial\tilde{q}(\cdot)=\partial q(\cdot)-{\bar{x}^{\ast}}

\tilde{q}
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
0

\tilde{q}(x^{\prime})\geq\tilde{q}(\bar{x})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|x^{\prime}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\ \forall x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})

for some
\epsilon>0
and some
\rho\geq 0

\hat{q}:=\tilde{q}+\delta_{{\cal B}_{\epsilon}}
fulfills the baseline assumption of
[
27

[
27

the subdifferential mapping
\partial\hat{q}
is locally maximally hypomonotone around
(\bar{x},0)
and, by the proof of
[
27

\lambda\in(0,\frac{1}{\rho})

\partial\hat{q}
is graphically Lipschitzian with transformation mapping

[
27

(I+\lambda\partial\hat{q})^{-1}
is locally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{x})
and there holds

\nabla e_{\lambda}\hat{q}(u)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(I-(I+\lambda\partial\hat{q})^{-1}\big)(u)

(27)

for all

sufficiently close to
\bar{x}

e_{\lambda}\hat{q}(y):=\inf_{x}\{\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|x-y\|^{2}+\hat{q}(x)\}

\hat{q}

(x,\hat{x}^{*})
close to
(\bar{x},0)

\nabla\Phi(x,\hat{x}^{*})=\left(\begin{matrix}I&\lambda I\\
I&0\end{matrix}\right),\ \nabla\Phi(x,\hat{x}^{*})^{-1}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&I\\
\frac{1}{\lambda}I&-\frac{1}{\lambda}I\end{matrix}\right).

Since
(I+\lambda\partial\hat{q})^{-1}
is locally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{x})

is positive semidefinite. Further, by (

\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B)\in\overline{\nabla}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\hat{q})(x+\lambda\hat{x}^{*})

[

,
\overline{\nabla}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\hat{q})(x+\lambda\hat{x}^{*})
consists of symmetric matrices and consequently
B
is symmetric. Since

, we obtain

and

follows. Now, by taking into account that

and
\partial\hat{q}

{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
, it follows that all the shown properties do not hold only for
\partial\hat{q}
but also for
\partial q
.
∎

Corollary 3.27
.

For every lsc function

for

on a dense subset of

holds true with

.

Example 3.29
.

Let

for

. Thus

. Given

, for every sufficiently small neighborhood

,

{\cal S}N_{C}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})={\cal S}^{*}N_{C}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})=\{({\cal F}-{\cal F})\times({\cal F}-{\cal F})^{\perp}\mid\;\mbox{${\cal F}$ is face of ${\cal K}_{C}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})$}\}.

Of course, for every face

of

Let us compare the representation (

) with the limiting coderivative

. It was shown in

, Proof of Theorem 2]
that

has a simpler structure than the limiting coderivative

whenever the critical cone

Given a function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\cal S}^{*}\partial q(x,x^{*})
amounts to a generalized derivative of the subgradient mapping and constitutes therefore some generalized second-order derivative of
q
. In the framework of a study of sufficient conditions for local optimality, Rockafellar
[
35
]
has introduced another type of generalized second-order derivative as an epigraphical limit of certain second-order subderivatives.

Definition 3.30

(
[
35
]
)
.

1.

A function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called a generalized quadratic form, if
\phi(0)=0
and the subgradient mapping
\partial\phi
is generalized linear, i.e.,
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

A function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called generalized twice differentiable at
x
for a subgradient
x^{*}\in\partial q(x)
, if it is twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
with the second-order subderivative
{\rm d}^{2}q(x,x^{*})
being a generalized quadratic form.

3.

Given a function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
and a pair
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q
, the quadratic bundle of
q
at
x
for
x^{*}
is defined by

{\rm quad\,}q(x,x^{*}):=\left[\ \begin{minipage}[284.52756pt]the collection of generalized quadratic forms $\phi$ for which $\exists(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\to(x,x^{*})$ with $q$
generalized twice differentiable at $x_{k}$ for $x_{k}^{*}$ and such that the generalized quadratic forms $\phi_{k}={\rm d}^{2}q(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})$ converge epigraphically to $\phi$.
\end{minipage}\right.

We establish now a strong relationship between
{\cal S}\partial q
and
{\rm quad\,}q
for prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions
q
. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.31
.

Let
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
x
for
x^{*}\in\partial q(x)
. Then
(x,x^{*})\in{\cal O}_{\partial q}
if and only if
q
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
, and in this case one has

\widehat{\cal S}\partial q(x,x^{*})=\{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}{\rm d^{2}}q(x,x^{*})\big)\}.

(29)

Proof.

By
[
34
, Theorem 13.40]
, the subgradient mapping
\partial q
is proto-differentiable at
(x,x^{*})
if and only if
q
is twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
, and then

D(\partial q)(x,x^{*})=\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}{\rm d^{2}}q(x,x^{*})\big).

By Proposition
3.26
,
\partial q
is graphically Lipschitzian of order
n
at
(x,x^{*})
. Hence, if
(x,x^{*})\in{\cal O}_{\partial q}
then
\partial q
is proto-differentiable at
(x,x^{*})
by Remark
3.18
and consequently
q
is twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
and
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}{\rm d^{2}}q(x,x^{*})\big)=\mathrm{gph}\,D(\partial q)(x,x^{*})=T_{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q}(x,x^{*})
is a subspace. This verifies that
q
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
. Conversely, if
q
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
x^{*}
, then
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q}(x,x^{*})=\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}{\rm d^{2}}q(x,x^{*})\big)
is a subspace. The dimension of this subspace must be
n
because
\partial q
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
, and
(x,x^{*})\in{\cal O}_{\partial q}
follows.
∎

We will also make use of the following variant of Attouch’s theorem, see, e.g.,
[
34
, Theorem 12.35]
, which states the connection between graphical convergence of
subdifferential mappings and epi-convergence of the functions themselves, when the functions are convex.

Lemma 3.32
.

Let
\phi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be proper lsc functions and assume that there is some
\rho\geq 0
such that the functions
\hat{\phi}_{k}:=\phi_{k}+\rho\|\cdot\|^{2}
are convex. Then the following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

The functions
\phi_{k}
epi-converge to
\phi
.

(ii)

The mappings
\partial\phi_{k}
converge graphically to
\partial\phi
,
\hat{\phi}:=\phi+\rho\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex and there is some sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
converging to some
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi
such that
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi_{k}

\forall k
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi_{k}(x_{k})=\phi(\bar{x})
.

Proof.

Observe that assertion (i) is equivalent to epi-convergence of
\hat{\phi}_{k}\to\hat{\phi}
by
[
34
, Exercise 7.8]
, and in this case the epigraphical limit
\hat{\phi}
is convex, cf.
[
34
, Theorem 7.17]
. Similarly,
\partial\phi_{k}
converges graphically to
\partial\phi
if and only if
\partial\hat{\phi}_{k}
converges graphically to
\hat{\phi}
. Indeed, since
\partial\hat{\phi}_{k}(x)=\partial\phi_{k}(x)+2\rho x
, we easily obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{gph}\,\hat{\phi}_{k}=\{(x,x^{*}+2\rho x)\mid\;(x,x^{*})\in\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{gph}\,\phi_{k}\},

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{gph}\,\hat{\phi}_{k}=\{(x,x^{*}+2\rho x)\mid\;(x,x^{*})\in\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm inf}}_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{gph}\,\phi_{k}\}

and the claim follows. Finally we have
\phi(\bar{x})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi_{k}(x_{k})
for some sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\to(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi
with
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi_{k}
, if and only if
\hat{\phi}(\bar{x})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\hat{\phi}_{k}(x_{k})
and
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\hat{\phi}_{k}\ni(x_{k},x_{k}^{*}+2\rho x_{k})\to(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}}+2\rho\bar{x})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\hat{\phi}
. Now the equivalence between (i) and (ii) follows from Attouch’s theorem
[
34
, Theorem 12.35]
applied to the convex functions
\hat{\phi}_{k}
and
\hat{\phi}
.
∎

Proposition 3.33
.

Suppose that
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{x}^{*}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
. Further assume that for all
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
the function
q
is subdifferentially continuous at
x
for
x^{*}
. Then

{\cal S}^{*}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})={\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})=\{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)\mid\;\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\}.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon
and
\rho\geq 0
be as in Definition
3.25
. Then we can find an open neighborhood
W
of
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
such that
W\subset{\cal B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\bar{x})\times{\cal B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}({\bar{x}^{\ast}})
and for all
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q\cap W
the function
q
is subdifferentially continuous at
x
for
x^{*}
and
|q(x)-q(\bar{x})|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.
Consider
(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q\cap W
. Then for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\hat{x})
with
|q(x)-q(\hat{x})|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
and all
x^{*}\in\partial q(x)\cap{\cal B}_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(\hat{x}^{*})
we have
x,x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})
,
|q(x)-q(\bar{x})|\leq\epsilon
and
x^{*}\in\partial q(x)\cap{\cal B}_{\epsilon}({\bar{x}^{\ast}})
implying

q(x^{\prime})\geq q(x)+\langle x^{*},x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}
. Thus
q
is prox-regular at
\hat{x}
for
\hat{x}^{*}
and therefore graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{*})
. Further, by
[
34
, Proposition 13.49]
and its proof, we may conclude that
{\rm d^{2}}q(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{*})+\rho\|\cdot\|^{2}
is a lsc convex function.

Now consider
\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
together with some sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\to(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
such that
q
is generalized twice differentiable at
x_{k}
for
x_{k}^{*}
and the generalized quadratic forms
\phi_{k}:={\rm d}^{2}q(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
converge epigraphically to
\phi
. We may assume that
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q\cap W

\forall k
and thus
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\in{\cal O}_{\partial q}\cap W
by Lemma
3.31
. By Lemma
3.32
,
\partial\phi_{k}
converges graphically to
\partial\phi
, i.e.,
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi_{k}\to\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\phi
in the sense of Painlevé-Kuratowski convergence. Now it follows from Lemma
3.1
(v) and (
29
) that
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)\in{\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
.

Conversely, consider a subspace
L\in{\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
together with sequences
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\in{\cal O}_{\partial q}\cap W
and
L_{k}\in\widehat{\cal S}\partial q(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L_{k},L)=0
. According to Proposition
3.26
we can find symmetric positive semidefinte matrices
B_{k}
,
B
such that
L_{k}={\rm rge\;}(B_{k},\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B_{k}))
,
L={\rm rge\;}(B,\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B))
with
\lambda=1/(\rho+1)
. Now let
{\cal U}:={\rm rge\;}B
and set
Q:=\frac{1}{\lambda}(B^{\dagger}-BB^{\dagger})
, where
B^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse of
B
. Since
B
is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so is
B^{\dagger}
as well. Further,
BB^{\dagger}B=B
and
BB^{\dagger}=B^{\dagger}B
is the orthogonal projection onto
{\rm rge\;}B
, so that
I-B^{\dagger}B
is the orthogonal projection onto
{\cal U}^{\perp}=\ker B
. Now consider the generalized quadratic form
\phi(x):=\langle Qx,x\rangle+\delta_{\cal U}(x)
. Since
Q
is symmetric, we obtain

\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)(x)=\begin{cases}Qx+{\cal U}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{\lambda}(B^{\dagger}-BB^{\dagger})x+{\cal U}^{\perp}&\mbox{if $x\in{\cal U}$,}\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\big(Bp,\frac{1}{\lambda}(B^{\dagger}-BB^{\dagger})Bp+(I-B^{\dagger}B)v\big)\mid\;p,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\Big(B\big(B^{\dagger}Bp+(I-B^{\dagger}B)v\big),\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B)\big(B^{\dagger}Bp+(I-B^{\dagger}B)v\big)\Big)\mid\;p,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\Big\}

\displaystyle={\rm rge\;}(B,\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B))=L.

The matrix
Q+\frac{1}{\lambda}I=\frac{1}{\lambda}B^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-BB^{\dagger})
is positive semidefinite as the sum of two positive semidefinite matrices and therefore the function
\hat{\phi}:=\phi+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\cdot\|^{2}=\phi+(\rho+1)\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex. For each
k
, the function
\phi_{k}:={\rm d}^{2}q(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
fulfills
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}q)=L_{k}
by Lemma
3.31
and
\hat{\phi}_{k}:=\phi_{k}+(\rho+1)\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex. Since
(0,0)\in L_{k}=\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi_{k})
, we have
0\in\partial\phi_{k}(0)
. Further,
\phi_{k}(0)=\phi(0)=0
and
0\in\partial\phi(0)
. Since convergence of
L_{k}
to
L
implies that
\partial\phi_{k}
converges graphically to
\partial\phi
, it follows from Lemma
3.32
that
\phi_{k}
converges epigraphically to
\phi
and we conclude
\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
. Thus
L\in\{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)\mid\;\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\}
verifying
{\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})=\{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)\mid\;\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\}
. By Proposition
3.26
we have
{\cal S}^{*}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})={\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.34
.

For every lsc proper convex function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
and for every pair
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q
we have

{\cal S}^{*}\partial q(x,x^{*})={\cal S}\partial q(x,x^{*})=\{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial(\frac{1}{2}\phi)\mid\;\phi\in{\rm quad\,}q(x,x^{*})\}.

4
SCD regularity

In this section we present the definition and basic properties of a certain property called SCD regularity, which has various applications as we will demonstrate in the subsequent sections.

Definition 4.1
.

1.

We denote by
{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
the collection of all subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
such that

(y^{*},0)\in L\ \Rightarrow\ y^{*}=0.

(30)

2.

A mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called SCD regular around
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
F
has the SCD property around
(x,y)
and

(y^{*},0)\in\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\Rightarrow\ y^{*}=0,

(31)

i.e.,
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
for all
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
. Further, we will denote by

{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y):=\sup\{\|y^{*}\|\mid\;(y^{*},x^{*})\in\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y),\|x^{*}\|\leq 1\}

the modulus of SCD regularity of
F
around
(x,y)
.

In the following proposition we state some basic properties of subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given a
2n\times n
matrix
Z
, there holds
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
if and only if the
n\times n
matrix
\pi_{2}(Z)
is nonsingular. Thus,
for every
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
there is a unique
n\times n
matrix
C_{L}
such that
L={\rm rge\;}(C_{L},I)
. Further,
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(C_{L}^{T},I)\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
,

\langle x^{*},C_{L}^{T}v\rangle=\langle y^{*},v\rangle\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L\ \forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(32)

and

\|y^{*}\|\leq\|C_{L}\|\|x^{*}\|\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L.

(33)

Proof.

Clearly, if
\pi_{2}(Z)
is nonsingular then
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Further, given
(y^{*},0)\in{\rm rge\;}Z
, there is some
p
with
y^{*}=\pi_{1}(Z)p
,
0=\pi_{2}(Z)p
implying
p=y^{*}=0
and therefore
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Conversely, consider
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
and
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
. Because
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
, the matrix
Z
has full column rank
n
and therefore there cannot exist
p\not=0
with
Zp=0
. Thus, if
A:=\pi_{2}(Z)
were singular, there is some
0\not=p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
Ap=0
and
Bp\not=0
with
B:=\pi_{1}(Z)
, implying
(Bp,0)\in L
and
Bp\not=0
which is not possible because of
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. This proves that
A
is nonsingular and
L={\rm rge\;}ZA^{-1}={\rm rge\;}(C_{L},I)
with
C_{L}=BA^{-1}
follows.
Clearly,
C_{L}
is uniquely given by
L
and does not depend on the particular choice of
A
and
B
.
From
L={\rm rge\;}(C_{L},I)
we deduce
L^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}(I,-C_{L}^{T})
and
L^{*}=S_{n}L^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}(C_{L}^{T},I)
. Further, for every
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
(p,-C_{L}^{T}p)\in L^{\perp}
, implying

\langle p,y^{*}\rangle-\langle C_{L}^{T}p,x^{*}\rangle=\langle p,y^{*}-C_{L}x^{*}\rangle=0\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L

and (
32
) follows. Finally, for every
(y^{*},x^{*})\in L
there is some
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
y^{*}=C_{L}p
,
x^{*}=p
implying (
33
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that for every
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
there holds
C_{L}=\pi_{1}(Z)\pi_{2}(Z)^{-1}
for every
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
.

In case of SCD regularity we obtain from Proposition
4.2
that

\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)=\{(C_{L}p,p)\mid\;L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y),p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

and consequently

\displaystyle{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|C_{L}p\|\mid\;L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y),p\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\|p\|\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|C_{L}\|\mid\;L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\}.

(34)

Remark 4.4
.

In case of a single-valued, locally Lipschitzian mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by virtue of Lemma
3.11
, SCD regularity of
F
around
\bar{x}
means that all matrices belonging to the B-subdifferential are nonsingular. This is exactly the so-called BD-regularity property from
[
29
]
.

By isometry of the mapping
L\mapsto L^{*}
and Proposition
4.2
we obtain readily the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

The mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if and only if

(u,0)\in\bigcup{\cal S}F(x,y)\ \Rightarrow\ u=0.

(35)

Further,

{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)=\sup\{\|u\|\mid\;(u,v)\in\bigcup{\cal S}F(x,y),\ \|v\|\leq 1\}=\sup\{\|C_{L}\|\mid\;L\in{\cal S}F(x,y)\}.

Note that SCD regularity is weaker than the metric regularity of
F
around
(x,y)
. Indeed,
condition (
6
) for metric regularity of
F
near
(x,y)
can be equivalently written as

(y^{*},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)\ \Rightarrow\ y^{*}=0

and
\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
is contained in
\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
by Lemma
3.7
.
The next example shows that SCD regularity is even strictly weaker than metric regularity, see also Example
6.5
below.

Example 4.6
.

Consider the SCD mapping

F(x):=-x+N_{R_{-}}(x)=\partial q(x)\ \mbox{ with }q(x)=-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\delta_{R_{-}}(x)

at
(0,0)
. Then

D^{*}F(0,0)(y^{*})=-y^{*}+\begin{cases}\{0\}&\mbox{if $y^{*}<0$}\\
\mathbb{R}&\mbox{if $y^{*}=0$}\\
\mathbb{R}_{+}&\mbox{if $y^{*}>0$}\end{cases}

and therefore the only subspaces contained in
\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(0,0)
are
\{(y^{*},-y^{*})\mid\;y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}
. Hence
F
is SCD regular at
(0,0)
, but
F
is not metrically regular near
(0,0)
because of
0\in D^{*}F(0,0)(1)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be SCD regular around
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)<\infty
.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary that there are sequences
L_{k}\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
and
(y_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*})\in L_{k}
such that
\|y_{k}^{*}\|\geq k
and
\|x_{k}^{*}\|\leq 1
. By possibly passing to some subsequence we can assume that
y_{k}^{*}/\|y_{k}^{*}\|
converges to some
y^{*}
with
\|y^{*}\|=1
and
L_{k}
converges in the compact metric space
{\cal Z}_{n}
to some
L
. Then
(y^{*},0)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(y_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*})/\|y_{k}^{*}\|\in L
and
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
by Lemma
3.13
contradicting the assumption of SCD regularity.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then
F
is SCD regular around every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

\limsup_{(x,y)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)\leq{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

Proof.

By contraposition. If any of the assertions does not hold, we can find some
\kappa>{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,F}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
L_{k}\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x_{k},y_{k})
and
(y_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*})\in L_{k}
with
\|y_{k}^{*}\|\geq\kappa
and
\|x_{k}^{*}\|\leq 1
. By possibly passing to some subsequence we can assume that
(y_{k}^{*},x_{k}^{*})/\|y_{k}^{*}\|
converges to some
(y^{*},x^{*})
and
L_{k}
converges to some
L
. Then
\|y^{*}\|=1
,
\|x^{*}\|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}
,
(y^{*},x^{*})\in L
and
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by Lemma
3.13
. Since
L
is a subspace, we also have
(\kappa y^{*},\kappa x^{*})\in L\subseteq\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
implying together with
\|\kappa x^{*}\|\leq 1
the contradiction
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\geq\|\kappa y^{*}\|=\kappa
.
∎

5
On semismooth* Newton methods for SCD mappings

Consider the inclusion

0\in F(x),

(36)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume that
\bar{x}
is a reference solution of (
36
).
The idea behind the semismooth
∗
Newton method
[
14
]
for solving (
36
) is as follows. If
F
is semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)
and we are given some point
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
close to
(\bar{x},0)
, then for every
(y^{*},x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
there holds

\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle=\langle y^{*},y-0\rangle+o(\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},0)\|\|(x^{*},y^{*})\|)

by the definition of the semismoothness* property. We choose now
n
pairs
(y_{i}^{*},x_{i}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, compute a solution
\Delta x
of the system

\langle x_{i}^{*},\Delta x\rangle=-\langle y_{i}^{*},y\rangle,\ i=1,\ldots,n

(37)

and expect that
\|(x+\Delta x)-\bar{x}\|=o(\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},0)\|
. When dealing with SCD mappings
F
we can simplify this procedure by choosing the pairs
(y_{i}^{*},x_{i}^{*})
as a basis of some subspace
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
, which allows us to weaken the notion of semismoothness* along the lines of Proposition
2.11
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\|(x^{*},y^{*})\|

(38)

holds for all
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and all
(y^{*},x^{*})\in\bigcup{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
.

We say that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD semismooth
∗
around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if there is some neighborhood
W
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
.

The chosen subspace
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)
should have the property that the resulting system (
37
) has a unique solution. Taking the pairs
(y_{i}^{*},x_{i}^{*})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, as columns of a
2n\times n
matrix
Z
, this yields the requirement that
\pi_{2}(Z)
is nonsingular, which in turn is equivalent to
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
by Proposition
4.2
.
Comparing (
32
) with (
37
), we see that
\Delta x=-C_{L}^{T}y
is a solution to (
37
) in this case.

We are now in the position to describe the iteration step of the SCD variant of the semismooth
∗
Newton method introduced in
[
14
]
. Assume we are given some iterate
x^{(k)}
. Since we cannot expect in general that
F(x^{(k)})\not=\emptyset
or that
0
is close to

F(x^{(k)})
, even if
x^{(k)}
is close to a solution
\bar{x}
, we first perform some preparatory step which
yields
(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
as an approximate projection of
(x^{(k)},0)
onto
\mathrm{gph}\,F
. We require that

\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

(39)

for some constant
\eta>0
. E.g., if

\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(x^{(k)},0)\|\leq\beta\ddist((x^{(k)},0),\mathrm{gph}\,F)

holds with some
\beta\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(x^{(k)},0)\|+\|(x^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta\ddist((x^{(k)},0),\mathrm{gph}\,F)+\|(x^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq(\beta+1)\|(x^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|

and (
39
) holds with
\eta=\beta+1
. Further we require that
{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}\not=\emptyset
and compute the new iterate as
x^{(k+1)}=\hat{x}^{(k)}-C_{L}^{T}\hat{y}^{(k)}
for some
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. In fact, in a numerical implementation we will not calculate the matrix
C_{L}
, but two
n\times n
matrices
A,B
such that
L={\rm rge\;}(B^{T},A^{T})
, compute
\Delta x^{(k)}
as a solution of the system
A\Delta x=-B\hat{y}^{(k)}
and set
x^{(k+1)}=\hat{x}^{(k)}+\Delta x^{(k)}
.

This leads to the following conceptual algorithm.

Algorithm 1

(SCD semismooth
∗
Newton-type method for inclusions)
.

1. Choose a starting point
x^{(0)}
, set the iteration counter
k:=0
.

2. If
0\in F(x^{(k)})
, stop the algorithm.

3.

Approximation step:
Compute

(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F

satisfying (
39
) such that
{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}\not=\emptyset
.

4.

Newton step:
Select
n\times n
matrices
A^{(k)},B^{(k)}
with
L^{(k)}:={\rm rge\;}\big({B^{(k)}}^{T},{A^{(k)}}^{T})\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
, calculate the Newton direction
\Delta x^{(k)}
as a solution of the linear system
A^{(k)}\Delta x=-B^{(k)}\hat{y}^{(k)}
and obtain the new iterate via
x^{(k+1)}=\hat{x}^{(k)}+\Delta x^{(k)}.

5. Set
k:=k+1
and go to 2.

We have
\Delta x^{(k)}=-C_{L^{(k)}}^{T}\hat{y}^{(k)}
and therefore
(\Delta x^{(k)},-\hat{y}^{(k)})\in-{L^{(k)}}^{*}={L^{(k)}}^{*}\in{\cal S}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})
by Proposition
4.2
. Thus, alternatively we can perform the Newton step also in the following way:

4.

Newton step:
Select
n\times n
matrices
A^{(k)},B^{(k)}
with
{\rm rge\;}\big({B^{(k)}},{A^{(k)}})\in{\cal S}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
, compute a solution
p
of the linear system
{A^{(k)}}p=-\hat{y}^{(k)}
and compute the new iterate
x^{(k+1)}=\hat{x}^{(k)}+\Delta x^{(k)}
with Newton direction
\Delta x^{(k)}=B^{(k)}p
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that
-\hat{y}^{(k)}\in D^{\sharp}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})(\Delta x^{(k)})
but we do not necessarily have
-\hat{y}^{(k)}\in DF(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})(\Delta x^{(k)})
as it is the case in Newton methods based on the graphical derivative, cf.
[
7
,
17
,
25
]
.

Which possibility for calculating the Newton direction is actually chosen, depends on the availability of the respective derivative. Let us analyze the two alternatives for the special case when
F
is single-valued and continuously differentiable at
x^{(k)}
. In Algorithm
1
, the matrices
A^{(k)},B^{(k)}
with
{\rm rge\;}\big({B^{(k)}}^{T},{A^{(k)}}^{T})\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})
fulfill
{A^{(k)}}^{T}=\nabla F(\hat{x}^{(k)})^{T}{B^{(k)}}^{T}
and thus the Newton direction is computed by solving the linear system
\big(B^{(k)}\nabla F(\hat{x}^{(k)})\big)\Delta x=-B^{(k)}\hat{y}^{(k)}
. On the other hand, given
A^{(k)},B^{(k)}
with
{\rm rge\;}\big({B^{(k)}},{A^{(k)}})\in{\cal S}F(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})
, we have
A^{(k)}=\nabla F(\hat{x}^{(k)})B^{(k)}
and in this case the Newton direction is computed via
\Delta x^{(k)}=B^{(k)}p=-B^{(k)}\big(\nabla F(\hat{x}^{(k)})B^{(k)}\big)^{-1}\hat{y}^{(k)}
. The structure of the second approach resembles the adjoint system method known from PDE-constrained optimization and optimal control.

We now consider convergence of Algorithm
1
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that the inequality

\|x-C_{L}^{T}(y-\bar{y})-\bar{x}\|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{n(1+\|C_{L}\|^{2})}\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|

(40)

holds for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and every
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
.

Proof.

Pick
\epsilon>0
and choose
\delta>0
such that (
38
) holds. Now consider any

(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and any
L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. By Proposition
4.2
we have
L={\rm rge\;}(C_{L},I)
and therefore
(C_{L}e_{i},e_{i})\in L
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th unit vector. From (
38
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\langle e_{i},\bar{x}-x\rangle-\langle C_{L}e_{i},\bar{y}-y\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle e_{i},x-C_{L}^{T}(y-\bar{y})-\bar{x}\rangle|\leq\epsilon\|(e_{i},C_{L}e_{i})\|\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\sqrt{1+\|C_{L}\|^{2}}\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|

and

\|x-C_{L}^{T}(y-\bar{y})-\bar{x}\|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{n(1+\|C_{L}\|^{2})}\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|

follows.
∎

Given
\eta,\kappa>0
, we now define for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the set

{\cal G}^{\eta,\kappa}_{F,\bar{x}}(x):=\{(\hat{x},\hat{y},L)\mid\;(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F,\ \|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x-\bar{x}\|,L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x},\hat{y})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg},\|C_{L}\|\leq\kappa\}.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and assume that there are
\eta,\kappa>0
such
that for every
x\not\in F^{-1}(0)
sufficiently close to
\bar{x}
we have

{\cal G}_{F,\bar{x}}^{L,\kappa}(x)\not=\emptyset
. Then there exists some
\delta>0
such that for every
starting point
x^{(0)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})
Algorithm
1
either stops after
finitely many iterations at a solution or produces a sequence
x^{(k)}
which converges
superlinearly to
\bar{x}
, provided we choose in every iteration
(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)},L^{(k)})\in{\cal G}_{F,\bar{x}}^{\eta,\kappa}(x^{(k)})
.

Proof.

Using Proposition
5.3
with
\bar{y}=0
, we can find some
\bar{\delta}>0
such that (
40
)
holds with
\epsilon=\frac{1}{2\eta\sqrt{n(1+\kappa^{2})}}
for all
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\bar{\delta}}(\bar{x},0)
and all

L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x,y)\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Set
\delta:=\bar{\delta}/\eta
and consider an iterate

x^{(k)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})\not\in F^{-1}(0)
. Then

\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|\leq\bar{\delta}

and consequently

\|x^{(k+1)}-\bar{x}\|\leq\frac{1}{2\eta\sqrt{n(1+\kappa^{2})}}\sqrt{n(1+\kappa^{2})}\|(\hat{x}^{(k)},\hat{y}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

by Proposition
5.3
.
It follows that for every starting point
x^{(0)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})
Algorithm
1

either stops after finitely many iterations with a solution or produces a sequence
x^{(k)}

converging to
\bar{x}
. The superlinear convergence of the sequence
x^{(k)}
is now an easy
consequence of Proposition
5.3
.
∎

So far Algorithm
1
is only a straightforward adaption of the semismooth
∗
Newton method from
[
14
]
to SCD mappings. However, in
[
14
]
the semismooth
∗
Newton method was only guaranteed to converge under the assumption of strong metric regularity, whereas we will now prove that for its SCD variant a less restrictive condition is sufficient.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be SCD regular around
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then for every
\eta>0
and every
\kappa>{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},0)
there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}

such that for every
x\in U
the set
{\cal G}^{\eta,\kappa}_{F,\bar{x}}(x)
is nonempty and amounts to

{\cal G}^{\eta,\kappa}_{F,\bar{x}}(x)=\big\{(\hat{x},\hat{y},L)\mid\;(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F,\ \|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x-\bar{x}\|,L\in{\cal S}^{*}F(\hat{x},\hat{y})\big\}.

(41)

Proof.

By Proposition
4.8
we can find some positive radius
\rho
such that
F
is SCD regular around
(x,y)
with modulus
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)\leq\kappa
for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\rho}(\bar{x},0)
. By taking
U:={\cal B}_{\rho/\eta}(\bar{x})
, for every
x\in U
and every
(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
with
\|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x-\bar{x}\|
we have
(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in{\cal B}_{\rho}(\bar{x},0)
. By (

\|C_{L}\|\leq\kappa
whenever

∎

Since in (

) the right hand side does not depend on
\kappa
, we obtain the following corollary of Theorem

.

Corollary 5.6
.

Assume that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and SCD regular around
(\bar{x},0)
. Then for every
\eta>0
there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that
for every starting point

is well-defined and either stops after finitely many iterations at a solution of (

) or produces a sequence

.

Remark 5.7
.

Note that Corollary

guarantees not only locally superlinear convergence, but also that the method is locally well-defined, which is an advantage in comparison with the Josephy-Newton method from
[

.
In Theorem

, the mapping

is strongly metrically subregular at

, in such a case the convergence of the Josephy-Newton method is also locally superlinear, provided the method is well-defined. This, however, need not be the case as illustrated in

, where the assumptions of Corollary

6
Strong metric subregularity on a neighborhood

We first present a characterization of strong metric subregularity on a neighborhood, cf. Definition
2.8
, by means of the outer limiting graphical derivative defined in Definition
3.8
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then
F
is strongly metrically subregular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if the condition

0\in D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)\ \Rightarrow u=0

(42)

holds and in this case one has

{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\sup\{\|u\|\mid\;(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\ \|v\|\leq 1\}.

(43)

Proof.

We prove the ”if”-part by contraposition. Assume that (
42
) holds but
F
is not strongly metrically subregular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Then we can find a sequence
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that either
F
is not strongly metrically subregular at
(x_{k},y_{k})
for infinitely many
k
or
\limsup_{k\to\infty}{\rm subreg\,}F(x_{k},y_{k})=\infty
. After possibly passing to some subsequence and taking into account Theorem
2.7
, in both cases there is a sequence
\kappa_{k}\to\infty
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})
with
\|v_{k}\|\leq 1
such that
\|u_{k}\|>\kappa_{k}\|v_{k}\|
. Defining
(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k}):=(u_{k},v_{k})/\|u_{k}\|\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})
, we have
\|\tilde{v}_{k}\|\leq 1/\kappa_{k}
implying
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{v}_{k}=0
. By possibly passing to a subsequence once more,
\tilde{u}_{k}
converges to some
u
with
\|u\|=1
and from the definition of
D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
we obtain
(u,0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
contradicting (
42
). This proves the ”if”-part.

In order to show the ”only if”-part assume that (
42
) does not hold, so that there is some
u\not=0
with
(u,0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. By definition of
D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there are sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(u,0)
with
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})
. If
F
is not strongly metrically subregular at
(x_{k},y_{k})
for infinitely many
k
, then it is not strongly metrically subregular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by definition. On the other hand, if
F
is strongly metrically subregular at
(x_{k},y_{k})
then
v_{k}\not=0
and
{\rm subreg\,}F(x_{k},y_{k})\geq\|u_{k}\|/\|v_{k}\|
, which follows from Theorem
2.7
. Hence,
\limsup_{k\to\infty}{\rm subreg\,}F(x_{k},y_{k})=\infty
and
F
is again not strongly metrically subregular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. This proves the ”only if”-part. There remains to show (
43
). By definition we have

{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\limsup_{(x,y)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}\sup\{\|u\|\mid\;(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x,y),\|v\|\leq 1\}

and therefore there are sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})
with
\|v_{k}\|\leq 1
and
\|u_{k}\|\to{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})<\infty
. By possibly passing to a subsequence,
(u_{k},v_{k})
converges to some
(u,v)
with
\|v\|\leq 1
. By definition of
D^{\sharp}F
we have
(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\|u\|\leq\xi:=\sup\{\|u\|\mid\;\exists v:\ (u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\ \|v\|\leq 1\}
follows. Next consider a sequence
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with
\|v_{k}\|\leq 1
and
\|u_{k}\|\to\xi
. Then for every
k
there are
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and
(u_{k}^{\prime},v_{k}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gph}\,DF(x_{k},y_{k})
such that
\|(x_{k},y_{k})-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\leq\frac{1}{k}
and
(\|u_{k}^{\prime},v_{k}^{\prime})-(u_{k},v_{k})\|\leq\frac{1}{k}
and

{\rm subreg\,}F(x_{k},y_{k})\geq\xi_{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\|u_{k}^{\prime}\|}{\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|}&\mbox{if $\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|>1$}\\
\|u_{k}^{\prime}\|&\mbox{if $\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|\leq 1$}\end{cases}

follows. In case when
\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|>1
we have

|\frac{\|u_{k}^{\prime}\|}{\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|}-\|u_{k}^{\prime}\||=\frac{\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|-1}{\|v_{k}^{\prime}\|}\|u_{k}^{\prime}\|\leq\frac{1}{k+1}\|u_{k}^{\prime}\|

and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\xi_{k}=\xi
follows. Hence
{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\xi_{k}=\xi
and relation (
43
) is established.
∎

From Lemma
3.10
together with Lemma
4.5
, we may conclude that strong metric subregularity around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
implies SCD regularity around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})

and that
{\rm l\mbox{-}subreg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\geq{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Next we show that, conversely,
SCD regularity in conjunction with SCD semismoothness* provides a sufficient condition for strong metric subregularity.

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then for every
\kappa>{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there is a neighborhood
W
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that
F
is strongly metrically subregular with modulus
{\rm subreg\,}F(x,y)<\kappa
at every point
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
where
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
.

Proof.

Fixing
\kappa>\tilde{\kappa}>{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, by Proposition
4.8
there is an open neighborhood
W
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
the mapping
F
is SCD regular around
(x,y)
with modulus
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)\leq\tilde{\kappa}
. Consider
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
where
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
. Assume now that
F
is not metrically subregular at
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
or that
{\rm subreg\,}F(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})>\tilde{\kappa}
. Then there is some
\kappa^{\prime}>\tilde{\kappa}
and a sequence
x_{k}
converging to
\tilde{x}
such that
\ddist(x_{k},F^{-1}(\tilde{y}))>\kappa^{\prime}\ddist(\tilde{y},F(x_{k}))

\forall k
. Consider
y_{k}\in F(x_{k})
with
\ddist(\tilde{y},F(x_{k}))=\|y_{k}-\tilde{y}\|
. Then
y_{k}
converges to
\tilde{y}
and for all
k
sufficiently large we have
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
. Pick
L_{k}\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x_{k},y_{k})
. Using Proposition
5.3
and (
34
) we have that
\|C_{L_{k}}\|\leq\tilde{\kappa}
and

\|x_{k}-C_{L_{k}}^{T}(y_{k}-\tilde{y})-\tilde{x}\|\leq\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{n(1+\|C_{L_{k}}\|^{2})}\|(x_{k}-\tilde{x},y_{k}-\tilde{y})\|\leq\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{n(1+\tilde{\kappa}^{2})}(\|x_{k}-\tilde{x}\|+\|y_{k}-\tilde{y}\|)

implying

(1-\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-\tilde{x}\|\leq\|C_{L_{k}}(y_{k}-\tilde{y})\|+\alpha_{k}\|y_{k}-\tilde{y}\|\leq(\tilde{\kappa}+\alpha_{k})\|y_{k}-\tilde{y}\|,

where
\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{n(1+\tilde{\kappa}^{2})}
. Since
\alpha_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, we have
(\tilde{\kappa}+\alpha_{k})/(1-\alpha_{k})<\kappa^{\prime}
for all
k
sufficiently large and therefore
\|x_{k}-\tilde{x}\|<\kappa^{\prime}\|y_{k}-\tilde{y}\|
in contrary to our assumption. This shows that
F
is metrically subregular at
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
and
{\rm subreg\,}F(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\leq\tilde{\kappa}<\kappa
. Further
\tilde{x}
must be an isolated point in
F^{-1}(\tilde{y})
. Assume on the contrary that there is a sequence
x_{k}\in F^{-1}(\tilde{y})
converging to
\tilde{x}
. Taking
L_{k}\in{\cal S}^{*}F(x_{k},\tilde{y})
and applying Proposition
5.3
with
\epsilon=1/(2\sqrt{n(1+\tilde{\kappa}^{2})}
, we obtain for all
k
sufficiently large

\|x_{k}-\tilde{x}\|=\|x_{k}-C_{L_{k}}^{T}(\tilde{y}-\tilde{y})-\tilde{x}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|(x_{k}-\tilde{x},\tilde{y}-\tilde{y})\|=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-\tilde{x}\|,

a contradiction. This shows that
F
is even strongly metrically subregular at
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
.
∎

7
On strong metric regularity

Our results on strong metric regularity pertain again mappings
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and are partly expressed in terms of certain bases for the subspaces
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

Given a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
with transformation mapping
\Phi
according to Definition
3.16
, we denote by

\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
the collection of all
2n\times n
matrices
Z
such that
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
\pi_{1}(\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})Z)=I
.

Note that by Proposition
3.17
for every
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there exists a unique
Z\in\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\cap{\cal M}(L)
. Since for every Lipschitzian mapping
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, the B-subdifferential
\overline{\nabla}f(u)
is compact for every
u\in U
, we conclude from (
24
) that
\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is compact as well.

The next statement provides us with an upper approximation of the graphs of the strict derivative and the limiting coderivative, respectively.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be graphically Lipschitzian at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
with transformation mapping
\Phi
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subseteq\bigcup_{Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})}{\rm rge\;}Z,

(45)

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subseteq\bigcup_{Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})}({\rm rge\;}Z)^{*}

(46)

Proof.

According to Definition
3.16
consider the open neighborhoods
W
of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
U
of
\bar{w}
and the Lipschtitzian mapping
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\Phi(\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W)=\mathrm{gph}\,f
, where
\bar{w}=\pi_{1}(\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y}))
. Consider
(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
together with sequences
(x^{1}_{k},y^{1}_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
(x^{2}_{k},y^{2}_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
t_{k}\downarrow 0
such that
(u,v)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(x^{2}_{k}-x^{1}_{k},y^{2}_{k}-y^{1}_{k})/t_{k}
. For each
k
let
w^{i}_{k}
,
i=1,2
be given by
w^{i}_{k}=\pi_{1}(\Phi(x^{i}_{k},y^{i}_{k}))
.
Then

\displaystyle(w^{2}_{k}-w^{1}_{k},f(w^{2}_{k})-f(w^{1}_{k}))

\displaystyle=\Phi(x^{2}_{k},y^{2}_{k})-\Phi(x^{1}_{k},y^{1}_{k})=\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})(x^{2}_{k}-x^{1}_{k},y^{2}_{k}-y^{1}_{k})+o(\|(x^{2}_{k}-x^{1}_{k},y^{2}_{k}-y^{1}_{k})\|)

\displaystyle=\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})(x^{2}_{k}-x^{1}_{k},y^{2}_{k}-y^{1}_{k})+o(t_{k})

implying that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{(w^{2}_{k}-w^{1}_{k},f(w^{2}_{k})-f(w^{1}_{k}))}{t_{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\frac{(x^{2}_{k}-x^{1}_{k},y^{2}_{k}-y^{1}_{k})}{t_{k}}=\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}f(\bar{w}).

Hence
\pi_{2}(\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v))\in D_{*}f(\bar{w})(\pi_{1}(\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v)))
and by
[
34
, Theorem 9.62]
there is some
B\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{w})
satisfying
\pi_{2}(\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v))=B\pi_{1}(\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v))
which is the same as
\nabla\Phi(\bar{x})(u,v)\in{\rm rge\;}(I,B)
.
B
can be expressed as a convex combination
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}B_{i}
with
B_{i}\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{w})
,
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=1
and therefore

(u,v)\in\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}{\rm rge\;}(I,B)=\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}{\rm rge\;}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
B_{i}\end{array}\right)\Big]={\rm rge\;}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
B_{i}\end{array}\right)\Big].

Denoting
Z_{i}:=\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
B_{i}\end{array}\right)
we have
{\rm rge\;}Z_{i}\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by (
24
) and
\pi_{1}(\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})Z_{i})=I
yielding

. Thus
(u,v)\in{\rm rge\;}Z
with

verifying (
45
).

Now consider
(y^{*},x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
which is the same as

S_{n}^{T}(y^{*},x^{*})\in N_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}N_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(\bar{w},f(\bar{w}),

where the second equality follows from
[
34
, Exercise 6.7]
. Hence
z^{*}:=S_{n}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-T}S_{n}^{T}(y^{*},x^{*})\in S_{n}N_{\mathrm{gph}\,f}(\bar{w},f(\bar{w})=\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}f(\bar{w})
implying
\pi_{2}(z^{*})\in D^{*}f(\bar{w})(\pi_{1}(z^{*}))
. By
[
34
, Theorem 9.62]
there is some
B\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{w})
such that
\pi_{2}(z^{*})=B^{T}\pi_{1}(z^{*})
which is the same as
z^{*}\in{\rm rge\;}(I,B^{T})
and

(y^{*},x^{*})\in{\rm rge\;}\Big[S_{n}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}S_{n}^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
B^{T}\end{array}\right)\Big]

follows. Taking into account that

{\rm rge\;}\Big[S_{n}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}S_{n}^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
B^{T}\end{array}\right)\Big]^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}\Big[S_{n}\nabla\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{-1}S_{n}^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B\\

As we have shown above, the latter subspace equals to
{\rm rge\;}Z
with

and

∎

On the basis of Proposition
7.1
we can now establish the following characterization of strong metric regularity.

Theorem 7.2
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

(i)

If
F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
then it is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with transformation mapping
\Phi(x,y)=(y,x)
and one has that
\{{\rm rge\;}Z\mid\;Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}\subseteq{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Further,
F
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})={\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(ii)

Conversely, if
F
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with some transformation mapping
\Phi
such that
\{{\rm rge\;}Z\mid\;Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}\subseteq{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
then
F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

Proof.

If
F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, then by Theorem
2.6
it is clearly graphically Lipschitzian with the given transformation mapping
\Phi
and from Proposition
3.17
we obtain

{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{{\rm rge\;}(B,I)\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})\}
where
f
denotes the Lipschitz continuous localization of
F^{-1}
around
(\bar{y},\bar{x})
. Thus

\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\Big\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B\\
I\end{array}\right)\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})\Big\}

and consequently every matrix
Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is of the form
Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B\\
I\end{array}\right)
with
B\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})
. From this we can easily deduce that
L:={\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
and
B=C_{L}
showing that
F
is SCD regular. In order to verify the formula for the modulus of strong metric regularity we use (
9
). Let
\epsilon>0
and consider
(u,v)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with
\|v\|\leq 1
and
\|u\|\geq{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})-\epsilon
. By Proposition
7.1
there is some
Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
(u,v)=Zw
. Thus there is
\bar{B}\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})
such that
u=\bar{B}w
and
v=w
yielding

\displaystyle{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})-\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|\leq\|\bar{B}\|\|v\|\leq\|\bar{B}\|\leq\sup\{\|B\|\mid\;B\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\|B\|\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{y})\}=\sup\{\|C_{L}\|\mid\;L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}={\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})

by Lemma
4.5
.
Since
\epsilon>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, there holds
{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\leq{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. On the other hand, we have
\bigcup{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
implying
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\leq{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by Lemma
4.5
and (
9
). This proves (i).

The statement (ii) follows from Theorem
2.7
together with Proposition
7.1
. If
0\in\mathrm{gph}\,D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)
then there is some
Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that
(u,0)\in{\rm rge\;}Z
and
u=0
follows from
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Similarly, if
0\in D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(y^{*})
then there is some
Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
such that
(y^{*},0)\in({\rm rge\;}Z)^{*}
. Since
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
, we have
({\rm rge\;}Z)^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
by Proposition
4.2
and
y^{*}=0
follows. Hence, both (
8
) and (
6
) are fulfilled and strong metric regularity of
F
has been established.
∎

Consider the special case of a single-valued Lipschitzian mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\Phi(x,y)=(x,y)
. Then
\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(x,F(x))=\{(I,B)\mid\;B\in\overline{\nabla}F(x)\}
by Lemma
3.11
and therefore

{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(x,F(x))=\{(I,B)\mid\;B\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)\}.

Thus the requirement in Theorem
7.2
(ii) that
\{{\rm rge\;}Z\mid\;Z\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}\subseteq{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
is equivalent to the condition that every matrix
B
belonging to Clarke’s generalized Jacobian
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)
is nonsingular. Therefore we may consider Theorem
7.2
(ii) as a generalization of Clarke’s Inverse Function Theorem, see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 7.1.1]
, to set-valued mappings.

Note that
{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})={\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
whenever
F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. This fact will not be repeated in the following results, where we present sufficient conditions for strong metric regularity.

The sufficient condition for strong metric regularity in Theorem
7.2
(ii) depends on the particular basis representation
\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
of
{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. The next results are stated in terms of the elements
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
which do not depend on a basis.

Corollary 7.3
.

Given
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, assume that
{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{\bar{L}\}
is a singleton. Then
F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if
F
is graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
\bar{L}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
.

When
{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a singleton and
F
is not graphically Lipschitzian at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
then
F
cannot be strongly metrically regular by Theorem
7.2
(i). However, if
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at (around)
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, then it is at least strongly metrically subregular at (around)
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Consider the following example.

Example 7.4
.

Let
q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
q(x)=\frac{2}{3}{\rm sign\,}(x)|x|^{\frac{3}{2}}
. Then
\partial q(x)=|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}
is not graphically Lipschitzian of dimension
1
at
(0,0)

but it is an SCD mapping and SCD semismooth
∗
. Further,
{\cal S}\partial q(0,0)=\{\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\}
is a singleton and clearly
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Thus we deduce from Corollary
6.4
that
\partial q
is strongly metrically subregular around
(0,0)
. However,
\partial q
is not strongly metrically regular around
(0,0)
because
\partial q^{-1}(y)=\emptyset
for every
y<0
.

We will now present a basis-independent characterization of strong metric regularity for locally maximally hypomonotone mappings.

Theorem 7.5
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is locally maximally hypomonotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

F
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and for every
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
the matrix
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite.

(ii)

F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

\liminf_{(x^{1},y^{1}),(x^{2},y^{2})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}\frac{\langle x^{1}-x^{2},y^{1}-y^{2}\rangle}{\|x^{1}-x^{2}\|\|y^{1}-y^{2}\|}\geq 0

(47)

with the convention
0/0:=0
.

Proof.

We first prove (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). By Corollary
3.23
there is some
\lambda\geq 1
such that
F
is graphically Lipschitzian at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with transformation mapping
\Phi(x,y)=(\lambda x+y,x)
and for every
Z\in\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there is a firmly nonexpansive
n\times n
matrix
B
, such that
Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B\\
I-\lambda B\end{array}\right)
. Since
F
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
\pi_{2}(Z)=I-\lambda B
is nonsingular and
C_{{\rm rge\;}Z}=B(I-\lambda B)^{-1}
. Consider
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set
v:=(I-\lambda B)u
. By the posed assumption,
B(I-\lambda B)^{-1}
is positive semidefinite and we obtain

0\leq\lambda\langle v,B(I-\lambda B)^{-1}v\rangle=\lambda\langle v,Bu\rangle=\lambda\langle(I-\lambda B)u,Bu\rangle

implying
\langle u,\lambda Bu\rangle\geq\|\lambda Bu\|^{2}
. Thus
\lambda B
is firmly nonexpansive and, consequently,
\|2\lambda B-I\|\leq 1
. Since
I-\lambda B
is nonsingular, we deduce from
[
3
, Theorem 3.3]
that
\|\lambda B\|<1
. Now consider
\bar{Z}\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. It follows that
\bar{Z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{B}\\
I-\lambda\bar{B}\end{array}\right)
, where
\bar{B}
is some convex combination of matrices
B_{i}
with
\|\lambda B_{i}\|<1
and
\|2\lambda B_{i}-I\|\leq 1
. It follows that
\|\lambda\bar{B}\|<1
and
\|2\lambda\bar{B}-I\|\leq 1
. Thus
\pi_{2}(\bar{Z})=I-\lambda\bar{B}
is nonsingular and from Proposition
4.2
we may deduce that
{\rm rge\;}\bar{Z}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
. Now strong regularity of
F
follows from Theorem
7.2
(ii). Next we prove (
47
) by contraposition. Assume on the contrary that there are sequences
(x^{i}_{k},y^{i}_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
,
i=1,2
, and some
\eta>0
such that
\langle x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k},y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2}\rangle<-\eta\|x_{k}^{1}-x_{k}^{2}\|\|y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2}\|
for all
k
. By local hypomonotonicity,

\langle x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k},y^{1}_{k}-y^{2}_{k}\rangle\geq-(\lambda-1)\|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k}\|^{2}
and therefore
\lambda>1
and
\|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k}\|\geq\frac{\eta}{\lambda-1}\|y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2}\|
. On the other hand, by strong metric regularity, choosing
c>{\rm reg\,}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, we have
\|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k}\|\leq c\|y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2}\|
for all
k
sufficiently large. Let
t_{k}:=\|y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2}\|+\|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k}\|
. By possibly passing to a subsequence,
(x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{k},y_{k}^{1}-y_{k}^{2})/t_{k}
converges to some
(u,v)\in D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with
\|u\|+\|v\|=1
,
\frac{\eta}{\lambda-1}\|v\|\leq\|u\|\leq c\|v\|
and
\langle u,v\rangle\leq-\eta\|u\|\|v\|
. We deduce that both
u
and
v
are nonzero and thus
\langle u,v\rangle<0
. By Proposition
7.1
there is some
\bar{Z}\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and some
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
(u,v)=\bar{Z}p
. As shown above,
\bar{Z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{B}\\
I-\lambda\bar{B}\end{array}\right)
for some
n\times n
matrix
\bar{B}
with
\|2\lambda\bar{B}-I\|\leq 1
, i.e.,
\lambda\bar{B}
is firmly nonexpansive. It follows that

0\leq\langle p,\lambda\bar{B}p\rangle-\|\lambda\bar{B}p\|^{2}=\lambda\langle\bar{B}p,p-\lambda\bar{B}p\rangle=\lambda\langle u,v\rangle,

contradicting
\langle u,v\rangle<0
. Hence the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is verified.

To show the reverse implication note that strong metric regularity implies SCD regularity by Theorem
7.2
. We prove that
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite for every
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by contradiction. Assume that there exists some
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\langle C_{L}p,p\rangle<0
. Since
(C_{L}p,p)\in L\subseteq D^{\sharp}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\subseteq D_{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, there are sequences
t_{k}\downarrow 0
,
(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(C_{L}p,p)
with
(x_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime}):=(x_{k},y_{k})+t_{k}(u_{k},v_{k})
. It follows that
(x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k})/\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|=u_{k}/\|u_{k}\|\to C_{L}p
,
(y_{k}^{\prime}-y_{k})/\|y_{k}^{\prime}-y_{k}\|=v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|\to p/\|p\|
and therefore

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\langle x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k},y_{k}^{\prime}-y_{k}\rangle}{\|x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k}\|\|y_{k}^{\prime}-y_{k}\|}=\frac{\langle C_{L}p,p\rangle}{\|C_{L}p\|\|p\|}<0

(48)

contradicting (
47
).
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be locally monotone around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

F
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(ii)

F
is metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

(iii)

F
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

In this case, the matrices
C_{L}
,
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, are positive semidefinite.

Proof.

The equivalence between (i) and (ii) follows from the definitions of (strong) metric regularity and
[
10
, Theorem 3G.5]
. In view of Theorem
7.5
, in order to verify (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii), we only have to show that
F
is locally maximally monotone. By taking into account that
\mathrm{gph}\,F^{-1}=\{(y,x)\mid\;(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\}
, it follows readily from the definition that
F
is locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if and only if
F^{-1}
is locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{y},\bar{x})
.
F^{-1}
has a Lipschitz continuous monotone localization and is therefore locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{y},\bar{x})
by
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
. This proves (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii). We now claim that (iii) implies that
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite for every
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Assuming that
C_{L}
is not positive semidefinite for some
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
7.5
can be used to obtain (
48
) contradicting the local monotonicity of
F
. Hence our claim holds true and the implication (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) follows from Theorem
7.5
.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

Theorem
7.5
improves the sufficient conditions for strong metric regularity obtained by Nghia et al
[
26
]
. E.g., in
[
26
, Corollary 3.11]
it is shown that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if

(a)

F
is locally hypomonotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

(b)

D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is positive definite in the sense that

\langle u^{*},v^{*}\rangle>0
holds for all
u^{*}\in D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v^{*})
,
v^{*}\not=0
.

We now show that these assumptions imply the assumptions of Theorem
7.5
(i).
Indeed, by the positive definiteness of the coderivative
D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
together with the Mordukhovich criterion (
6
) we may deduce that
F
is metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and therefore SCD regular as well. Further, for every
L\in{\cal S}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
we have
{\rm rge\;}(C_{L}^{T},I)\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by Proposition
4.2
and Remark
3.5
implying
\langle C_{L}^{T}p,p\rangle>0
for all
p
with
C_{L}^{T}p\not=0
by assumption (b). Hence,
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite. By assumption (a) there is some
\gamma\geq 0
such that
\gamma I+F
is locally monotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and, since
D^{*}(\gamma I+F)(\bar{x},\gamma\bar{x}+\bar{y})=\gamma I+D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is positive definite, we conclude from the Mordukhovich criterion that
\gamma I+F
is metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\gamma\bar{x}+\bar{y})
. Thus, by Corollary
7.6
, the mapping
\gamma I+F
is locally maximally monotone at
(\bar{x},\gamma\bar{x}+\bar{y})
and
F
is locally maximally hypomonotone at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by the definition.
Hence, we have shown that the assumptions of Theorem
7.5
(i) are weaker than those of
[
26
, Corollary 3.11]
. When we now consider, e.g., the mapping
F(x_{1},x_{2})=(-x_{2},x_{1})
and an arbitrary reference point
(\bar{x},F(\bar{x}))
, we observe that the positive definiteness assumption (b) is not fulfilled. Nevertheless, Theorem
7.5
works well and so it in fact improves the mentioned statement in
[
26
]
.

Next we turn our attention to the characterization of tilt-stable minimizers by SCD regularity of the subdifferential.

Definition 7.8

(
tilt-stable minimizers).

Let
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
. Then:

(i)

\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable local minimizer of
q
if there is a number
\gamma>0
such that the mapping

M_{\gamma}(x^{\ast}):=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\big\{q(x)-\langle x^{\ast},x\rangle\mid\;x\in{\cal B}_{\gamma}(\bar{x})\big\},\quad x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(49)

is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous in some neighborhood of
\bar{x}^{*}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
M_{\gamma}(0)=\{\bar{x}\}
.

(ii)

The exact bound of tilt stability of
q
at
\bar{x}
is defined by

\displaystyle{\rm tilt\;}(q,\bar{x}):=\limsup_{{\textstyle{v^{*},w^{*}\to 0\atop v^{*}\not=w^{*}}}}\frac{\|M_{\gamma}(v^{*})-M_{\gamma}(w^{*})\|}{\|v^{*}-w^{*}\|}.

(50)

The theory developed in Section
5
enables us to provide a new characterization of tilt-stable local minimizers.

Theorem 7.9
.

For a function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
having
0\in\partial q(\bar{x})
and such that
q
is both prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}=0
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable local minimizer of
q
.

(ii)

\partial q
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},0)
and
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite for every
L\in{\cal S}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
.

Further, if
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
q
then
{\rm tilt\;}(q,\bar{x})={\rm scd\,reg\;}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
.

Proof.

If
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer, we may conclude from
[
28
, Theorem 1.3]
that the mapping
M_{\gamma}
is a single-valued Lipschitzian localization of
\partial q^{-1}
around
(0,\bar{x})
so that
\partial q
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},0)
and
{\rm tilt\;}(q,\bar{x})={\rm reg\,}\partial q(\bar{x},0)={\rm scd\,reg\;}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
.
By
[
28
, Theorem 1.3]
, statement (i) is equivalent to the condition

(iii)

The coderivative
D^{*}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
is positive definite in the sense that

\langle v^{*},u^{*}\rangle>0\quad\mbox{whenever}\quad u^{*}\in D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v^{*}),\ v^{*}\not=0.

(51)

So it suffices to prove the equivalence (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii).

Proof that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
. By Proposition
3.26
, there is some
\lambda>0
such that
\partial q
is graphically Lipschitzian with transformation mapping
\Phi(x,x^{*})=(x+\lambda x^{*},x)
. Further, any
Z\in\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
satisfies
{\rm rge\;}Z=({\rm rge\;}Z)^{*}
and is of the form
Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}B\\
\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B)\end{array}\right)
, where
B
is some symmetric positive semidefinite
n\times n

Z\in\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
and set
B:=\pi_{1}(Z)
. Since
\partial q
is SCD regular around
(\bar{x},0)
, the matrix
\pi_{2}(Z)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B)
is nonsingular by Proposition
4.2
and
C_{{\rm rge\;}Z}=\lambda B(I-B)^{-1}
. If the eigenvalues of
B
are denoted by
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
, the eigenvalues of
C_{L}
are
\lambda\mu_{i}/(1-\mu_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and, together with
\mu_{i}\geq 0
, we conclude that
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite if and only if
\max\mu_{i}=\|B\|<1
. Since
\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}\partial q(\bar{x};0)
is compact, it follows that
\eta:=\max\{\|\pi_{1}(Z)\|\mid\;Z\in\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}\partial q(\bar{x};0)\}<1
. Now consider
(v^{*},u^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. By Proposition
7.1
there is some
\bar{Z}\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}{}^{\Phi}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
such that
(v^{*},u^{*})\in({\rm rge\;}\bar{Z})^{*}
. The matrix
\bar{B}:=\pi_{1}(\bar{Z})
is a convex combination of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
B_{i}
satisfying
\|B_{i}\|\leq\eta
. Thus
\bar{B}
is symmetric positive semidefinite and
\|\bar{B}\|\leq\eta<1
. By taking into account
\pi_{2}(\bar{Z})=\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-\bar{B})
,
({\rm rge\;}\bar{Z})^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}(\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-\bar{B},-\bar{B})
and
({\rm rge\;}\bar{Z})^{*}={\rm rge\;}\bar{Z}
follows. Thus we may find some
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
(v^{*},u^{*})=(\bar{B}p,\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-\bar{B})p
to obtain

\langle v^{*},u^{*}\rangle=\frac{1}{\lambda}p^{T}\bar{B}(I-\bar{B})p.

The matrix
\bar{B}(I-\bar{B})
is symmetric and has eigenvalues
\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i})
, where
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
\bar{B}
. Since

\bar{B}(I-\bar{B})
is positive semidefinite implying
\langle v^{*},u^{*}\rangle\geq 0

is a linear combination of eigenvectors associated with the zero eigenvalues of
\bar{B}
, i.e.,
\bar{B}p=v^{*}=0
and (
51
) follows .

Proof that (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
.

\partial q

(\bar{x},0)
. Further, for every

we have
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(C_{L}^{T},I)\subseteq\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}\partial q(\bar{x},0)
by Proposition

and Lemma

and therefore
(C_{L}^{T}p,p)\in\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}\partial q(\bar{x},0)

\forall p
. From (

) we deduce
\langle C_{L}^{T}p,p\rangle>0
for all
p
with
C_{L}^{T}p\not=0
and the positive semidefiniteness of
C_{L}^{T}
and

8
Conclusion

Subspaces contained in the graph of the limiting coderivative may definitely serve as a basis for
construction of suitable local approximations in the broad class of SCD multifunctions. It came to us,
however, as a surprise that these subspaces and their counterparts in case of the limiting outer graphical
derivative contain a lot of information about stability behavior of the considered mappings.
The developed theory makes use of notions, mimicking the generalized derivatives and coderivatives
in the ”standard” generalized differential calculus. However, their structure is, in most cases, somewhat simpler when
compared with the standard notions and so the derived new characterization of strong metric
(sub)regularity and tilt stability are typically easier to work with.
Finally, let us point out that the property of strong metric subregularity around the reference point,
characterized via the subspaces contained in the limiting outer graphical derivative, seems to be a weak
stability property ensuring the locally superlinear convergence of the semismooth* Newton
method.
