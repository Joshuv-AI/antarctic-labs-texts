1.
Introduction

In statistical physics, point processes play a central role as a mathematical model for gasses of interacting particles: the gas is modeled as a random point configuration in a ground space
\mathcal{X}
, where every point in a configuration represents the location of a particle.
A corner stone of this approach for modelling gasses are Gibbs point processes, which historically arise from describing the distribution of particles as the solution to a variational principle that aims at minimizing the mean free excess energy of the system (see for example
[
7
, Section 1.3]
).
While these ideas date back all the way to Gibbs
[
15
]
(and even further to the work of Boltzmann and Maxwell), the theory of Gibbs point processes was substantially developed further throughout the 60s and 70s by the work of Dobrushin
[
9
]
, Lanford and Ruelle
[
26
]
, Preston
[
32
]
, Georgii
[
14
]
and many others, and it remains an active area of research till the present days
[
19
,
28
,
16
,
29
,
8
,
21
,
2
]
(see also
[
35
]
for a classical and
[
7
,
20
]
for more modern introductions to the topic).

Given a finite-volume region
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{X}
of the ground space
\mathcal{X}
(for simplicity, think
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
), a Gibbs point process on
\Lambda
is described by a probability density with respect to a Poisson point process on
\Lambda
of intensity 1.
This probability density is specified by an activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and an energy function
H
, which assigns a value from
(-\infty,\infty]
to finite point sets
\eta\subset\mathcal{X}
.
Given
\lambda
and
H
, every finite point set
\eta\subset\Lambda
has a density proportional to
\lambda^{\left\lvert\eta\right\rvert}\textup{e}^{-H(\eta)}
.

Opposed to this finite-volume setting, statistical physics is usually interested in large particle systems.
Such systems are more accurately modeled by considering point processes on regions of infinite volume, while the configuration space is extended to all locally finite point sets (i.e., we only require finitely many points in every bounded subregion).
In this case, the definition via probability densities often fails as the density cannot be normalized properly and configurations typically have infinite energy.
Instead, given an activity
\lambda
and energy function
H
, an infinite-volume Gibbs point process is defined by the
Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle formalism

[
9
,
26
]
(DLR), which imposes a set of consistency requirements: any finite-volume projection of an infinite-volume Gibbs point process should be consistent with its finite-volume definition discussed above (see
Definition

2
).
Due to the non-constructive nature of this approach, two main questions arise.
Given an activity
\lambda
and an energy function
H
:

•

Existence:
is there at least one point process on
\mathcal{X}
that satisfies the DLR formalism?

•

Uniqueness:
is there at most one point process on
\mathcal{X}
that satisfies the DLR formalism?
1
1

1

Note that usually in mathematics, uniqueness means that there is exactly one object that satisfies the requirements. However, for Gibbs point processes, this task is usually split into proving that there is a most one and at least one Gibbs measure. Hence it will be more convenient to refer to these two problems as existence and uniqueness respectively.

While the existence of an infinite-volume Gibbs measures can be proven under fairly general assumptions, which are usually stated in terms of the energy function
H
alone, proving uniqueness is notoriously more difficult
[
7
,
20
,
8
]
.
In particular, conditions for uniqueness often involve that the activity
\lambda
is sufficiently small, depending on various properties of the energy function (see for example
[
17
,
29
,
28
]
).

On top of being a genuinely intriguing mathematical question, uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs measures in the sense of DLR gained a lot of attention in mathematical physics as a probabilistic notion of
absence of phase transitions

[
20
,
29
,
28
,
19
,
2
]
.
As such, it poses an alternative to (much older) analytic notions, such as analyticity of the infinite-volume pressure (see
[

for a detailed introduction), which are usually proven by studying the convergence of series expansions
[
30
,
33
,
23
]
or the contraction integral identities
[

,
16
]
.
While in many cases such analytic proof techniques can be modified to yield uniqueness in the DLR sense (see for example
[
21
]
and
[
29
]
) and although both notions of absence of phase transitions often hold in similar activity regimes, to the best of our knowledge no general rigorous connection has been proved.
We defer a more detailed discussion to
Section

6
and proceed to sketching our main result.

1.1.
Main result and proof technique

In this paper, we focus on giving an activity regime for uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs point process for a class of energy functions that result from pair interactions,
that is, the case that there is some pair potential
\phi:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to(-\infty,\infty]
such that
H(\eta)=\sum_{x,y\in\binom{\eta}{2}}\phi(x,y)
, where we sum over unordered pairs of distinct points in
\eta
.
Moreover, we assume the following:

•

Weak temperedness:
We call
\phi
weakly tempered if

\hat{C}_{\phi}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}1-\textup{e}^{-\left\lvert\phi(x,y)\right\rvert}\mathrm{d}y<\infty,

and we refer to
\hat{C}_{\phi}
as the weak temperedness constant. This condition should be thought of as assuming that the total influence of every point on the rest of the space is bounded.
2
2

2

While this definition has appeared before in the literature (e.g.,
[
33
]
), we are not aware of it having a name. We call it weak temperedness due to its resemblance to the temperedness condition
C_{\phi}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\lvert 1-\textup{e}^{-\phi(x,y)}\rvert\mathrm{d}y<\infty
and the fact that
\hat{C}_{\phi}\leq C_{\phi}
.

•

Local stability:
We call
\phi
locally stable with constant
L\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
if for every finite
\eta\subset\mathcal{X}
with
H(\eta)<\infty
and every
x\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that

H(\eta\cup x)-H(\eta)\geq-L.

This condition should be thought of as assuming that for any realizable configuration of points
\eta
, adding a point
x
decreases the energy of the configuration by at most
L
.

•

Bounded range:
We say that the has bounded range if there exists some
R\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\phi(x,y)=0
for every pair of points
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
of distance at least
R
.

Our main result is the following uniqueness statement for Gibbs point processes in Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem

(Main)
.

For every bounded-range pair potential
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is locally stable with constant
L
and weakly tempered with constant
\hat{C}_{\phi}
, and every activity

\lambda<(\textup{e}^{L}\hat{C}_{\phi})^{-1}
,
there exists a unique Gibbs point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the sense of the Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle formalism.

The formal version of the above theorem is given in
Theorem

32
and
Corollary

33
.
We defer a detailed comparison between our activity bound and the existing literature to
Section

6
, where we compare it to various results that prove absence of a phase transition (in the sense of DLR or analytically) in a similar setting.
However, we remark that, for bounded-range locally stable potentials, our bound provides an improvement by a factor of
\textup{e}C_{\phi}/\hat{C}_{\phi}\geq\textup{e}
over the classical Penrose–Ruelle bound
[
34
,
30
]
, which implies uniqueness via contraction of the Kirkwood–Salsburg equations, and which constituted the best known bound in this setting for more than 50 years.
We proceed with discussing how we obtain our result.

In the setting of our main theorem, existence of the infinite-volume Gibbs point process follows from known results (see
Theorem

3
), and our main contribution is to prove that the infinite-volume Gibbs measure is unique.
Our argument is purely probabilistic and inspired by the work of Dyer, Sinclair, Vigoda and Weitz
[
10
]
for discrete hard-core gasses on a lattice.
The main idea is to construct a Markov process (in our case a spatial birth-death process as introduced by Preston
[
31
]
) for every finite-volume region
\Lambda
such that the process is reversible (hence stationary) with respect to the finite-volume Gibbs measure on
\Lambda
.
We then establish two properties for this family of Markov processes.

Firstly, we prove a
rapid mixing
property, meaning that these processes converge rapidly to their stationary distribution in total variation distance.
We show this rapid mixing result for all activities
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{L}\hat{C}_{\phi})^{-1}
by arguing that the local stability and weak temperedness assumptions imply contraction of a suitably constructed coupling.
This step is fairly robust with respect to the underlying space (it holds on arbitrary complete separable metric spaces) and does not rely on the bounded range of the potential.

Secondly, we prove
slow disagreement percolation
, which means that, for each of these Markov processes, local disagreements between initial configurations spread slowly.
For this step, we use the local stability along with the bounded range assumption and the fact that Euclidean space “grows slowly”.

Combining both properties above shows that, under the conditions of the main theorem, finite-volume Gibbs point processes satisfy a
spatial mixing
property: different parts of a random point configuration become asymptotically independent as the distance increases.
We use this observation to show that any pair of point processes satisfying the DLR formalism must have identical finite-volume projections.
By a standard result
[
6
, Corollary 9.1.IX]
, every point process is uniquely characterized by its finite-volume projections, and hence there is at most one infinite-volume Gibbs measure.

The proof technique we sketched above was previously applied in the continuous setting by Helmuth, Perkins and Petti
[
17
]
, who used a discrete-time Markov chain to study the special case of uniqueness of the infinite-volume hard-sphere model.
Our work may be seen as a natural extension to more general potentials.
Moreover, spatial birth-death processes have been used before to study different aspects of Gibbs point processes
[
1
,
36
,
25
,
24
,
12
]
, sometimes under different name such as
continuum Glauber dynamics
.
In particular the generator and its spectral gap have been studied in both the finite-volume
[
1
]
and infinite-volume setting
[
25
,
24
]
, and the results have been used to prove absence of a phase transition for various classes of models
[
24
]
.
However none of these results imply or are implied by ours.
Moreover, as our result is based on a simple coupling argument, most of the theory on spatial birth-death processes that we require holds in the setting of jump processes on general state spaces as presented in the classical texts of Feller
[
11
]
and Blumenthal and Getoor
[
3
]
.
This allows us to keep the presentation of our result and our proofs surprisingly elementary.

1.2.
Outline of the paper

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
In
Section

2
we formally introduce finite- and infinite-volume Gibbs point processes along with several useful properties.
In particular, we present a sufficient condition for uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs measures, which essentially constitutes a spatial mixing property.
As we aim to keep most of our discussions as general as possible, we introduce point processes in the setting of complete separable metric spaces using the notion of random counting measures.
However, switching between this perspective and the intuition of random point sets is straightforward.
In
Section

3
we give a short introduction to jump processes, which contain the spatial birth-death processes we aim to use.
We further prove various lemmas that will allow to bound the speed of convergence to the stationary distribution and to obtain tail bounds on certain hitting times, the latter of which will be useful for the disagreement percolation.
In
Section

4
we then construct the family of spatial birth-death processes that we study and prove the desired rapid mixing and slow disagreement percolation.
Finally, in
Section

5
, we prove our main uniqueness result that we sketched above.
We end our paper by discussing our result and compare our bound with the existing literature in
Section

6
.

Spaces and basic notation

We denote by

We equip

Further, we write

for the set of finite counting measures on

For

We denote by

2.2.
Integration and factorial measures

In the above setting, it is well known that every
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
can be written as a sum of Dirac measures
\eta=\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{x_{i}}
, where
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is a sequence of points in
\mathcal{X}
, and the index set is either
I=\{1,\dots,\eta(\mathcal{X})\}
if
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
or
I=\mathbb{N}
if
\eta\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
(see
[
6
, Proposition 9.1.III]
).
As a consequence, we have for every
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
and every measurable
f:\mathcal{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
that
\int f\mathrm{d}\eta=\sum_{i\in I}f(x_{i})
.
This identity extends to measurable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
provided that the negative or positive part of the integral is finite, which particularly holds if
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
.

Further, given a locally-finite counting measure
\eta=\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{N}
and a positive integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\eta^{(k)}
for the
k^{\text{th}}
factorial measure of
\eta
, which is a counting measure on the product space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})^{\otimes k}
given by
\eta^{(k)}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in I}^{\neq}\delta_{(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}})}
, where the sum is taken over
k
tuples of distinct indices from
I
.
Analogously to before, we have
\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}f\mathrm{d}\eta^{(k)}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in I}^{\neq}f(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}})
for every measurable
f:\mathcal{X}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
such that the negative or positive part of the integral is finite.

2.3.
Support set and operations on counting measures

An alternative way of writing a locally finite counting measure
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
on a complete separable metric space involves the notion of the
support set

S_{\eta}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid\eta(x)>0\},

where we write
\eta(x)
for
\eta(\{x\})
.
It can be shown that
S_{\eta}
is at most countable, and, for every
x\in S_{\eta}
, the fact that
\eta
is locally finite implies
\eta(x)<\infty
(see again
[
6
, Proposition 9.1.III]
).
We can now write
\eta
as

\eta=\sum_{x\in S_{\eta}}\eta(x)\cdot\delta_{x}.

Using this, we can define the following operations for pairs of locally finite counting measures
\eta,\xi\in\mathcal{N}
:

\displaystyle\eta\cap\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S_{\eta}\cap S_{\xi}}\min(\eta(x),\xi(x))\cdot\delta_{x}

\displaystyle\eta\cup\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S_{\eta}\cup S_{\xi}}\max(\eta(x),\xi(x))\cdot\delta_{x}

\displaystyle\eta\setminus\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S_{\eta}}\max(0,\eta(x)-\xi(x))\cdot\delta_{x}

\displaystyle\eta\oplus\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\eta\setminus\xi)+(\xi\setminus\eta).

Thinking of locally finite counting measures as point (multi-)sets, these operation correspond directly to intersection, union, set difference and symmetric difference.
It is easily checked that
\eta\cup\xi=(\eta\oplus\xi)+(\eta\cap\xi)
for all
\eta,\xi\in\mathcal{N}
, which will turn out to be a particularly useful identity.

2.4.
Point processes

We call a probability measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
a
point process
on
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}
if
P
is only supported on
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
(i.e.,
P(\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda})=0
).
Note that every point process
P
on
\Lambda
can be uniquely identified with a probability measure on
(\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda},\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda})
.
Moreover, for
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}
and a point process
P
on
\mathcal{X}
, we write
P[\Lambda]
for the pushforward of
P
under
\eta\mapsto\eta_{\Lambda}
, viewed as a map from
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
to itself.
That is, for all
A\in\mathcal{R}
we have

P[\Lambda](A)=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\Lambda}\in A\}}P(\mathrm{d}\eta).

Note that
P[\Lambda]
is a point process on
\Lambda
.
We call
P[\Lambda]
the projection of
P
to
\Lambda
.

In fact, point processes are fully characterized by their projections to finite-volume regions.
This is implied by the following even stronger fact, which states that two point processes are equal if they induce the same joint distributions of point counts on families of bounded sets.
To this end, recall that we defined a measurable map
N_{\Lambda}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\},\eta\mapsto\eta(\Lambda)
for each
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 1

(
[
6
, Corollary 9.1.IX]
)
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
.
Suppose for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{\ell}\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
it holds that

P(N_{\Lambda_{1}}=m_{1},\dots,N_{\Lambda_{\ell}}=m_{\ell})=Q(N_{\Lambda_{1}}=m_{1},\dots,N_{\Lambda_{\ell}}=m_{\ell}).

Then
P=Q
.

2.5.
Finite-volume Gibbs point processes

Let
\phi:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be symmetric and measurable.
We call
\phi
a
(stable) pair potential
if there is a constant
S\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all

\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
it holds that

H(\eta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\phi(x,y)\eta^{(2)}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq-S\cdot\eta(\mathcal{X}).

We call every such
S
a
stability constant
for
\phi
, and we call
H:\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
the
energy function
induced by
\phi
.
Note that such an energy function satisfies the following important properties:

(1)

It holds that
H(\mathbf{0})=0
(
non-degenerate
).

(2)

Suppose
\eta,\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
are such that
\xi\leq\eta
and
H(\xi)=\infty
; then
H(\eta)=\infty
(
hereditary
).

Given a pair potential
\phi
and a bounded set
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}
, we define a function
H_{\Lambda}:\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}\times\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
as

H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)\coloneqq H(\eta)+\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus\Lambda}\phi(x,y)\xi(\mathrm{d}y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x),

where we set
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
whenever the latter integral fails to converge absolutely.

Next, we introduce the grand-canonical partition function, which serves as the normalizing constant of the Gibbs point process.
For
\Lambda
as above, we define the
grand-canonical partition function
on
\Lambda
with boundary condition
\xi\in\mathcal{N}
and activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)\coloneqq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\int_{\Lambda^{n}}\exp\left(-H_{\Lambda}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}\Big|\xi\Big)\right)\nu^{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}).

Obviously, it holds that
\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)\geq 1
.
If further
\xi
is such that
\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)<\infty
, we define the
finite-volume Gibbs point process
(or finite-volume Gibbs measure) on
\Lambda
with boundary condition
\xi
at activity
\lambda
as a probability distribution
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
with

\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(A)\coloneqq\frac{\textup{e}^{\nu(\Lambda)}}{\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)}\int_{A}\lambda^{\eta(\Lambda)}\textup{e}^{-H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)}Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

for
A\in\mathcal{R}
, where
Q_{\Lambda}
is the Poisson point process with intensity measure
\nu(\cdot\cap\Lambda)
.
Note that
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
is a point process on
\Lambda
since
Q_{\Lambda}
is only supported on
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
.

We proceed by introducing some additional terminology related to pair potentials.
As sketched in the introduction, we say a pair potential
\phi
is
weakly tempered
3
3

3

C_{\phi}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|1-\textup{e}^{-\phi(x,y)}|\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
. We remark that the term ’weak’ does not refer to weak temperedness being a weaker condition (as
\hat{C}_{\phi}<\infty
if and only if

), but to the fact that
\hat{C}_{\phi}\leq C_{\phi}
, where the inequality is strict if
\phi
is tempered and as a non-trivial negative part.
if

\hat{C}_{\phi}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}1-\textup{e}^{-\left\lvert\phi(x,y)\right\rvert}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty,

and we call

the

of

.
Moreover, we say that
\phi
has bounded range

if for all

with

it holds that
\phi(x,y)=0
.
Finally, we call
\phi

locally stable
with constant

if, for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
with

2.6.
Infinite-volume Gibbs point process

Given an activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a pair potential
\phi
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
the set of infinite-volume Gibbs point processes that are compatible with
\lambda
and
\phi
, which we characterize via the Dobrushin-Lanford-Ruelle formalism.

Definition 2

(DLR formalism)
.

Given an activity
\lambda
and potential
\phi
, we say a point process
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
satisfies the DLR formalism if for all
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
the following holds:

(1)

for
\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}=\{\xi\in\mathcal{N}\mid\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)<\infty\}
we have
\mu(\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda})=1
, and

(2)

for all measurable
f:\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we have

\int_{\mathcal{N}}f(\eta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\eta)=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}f(\eta+\xi_{\mathcal{X}\setminus\Lambda})\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi).

In this case, we call
\mu
an
infinite-volume Gibbs point process
(or infinite-volume Gibbs measure) compatible with
\lambda
and
\phi
.
Further, we write
\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)
for the set of such infinite-volume Gibbs point processes.

As discussed earlier, we will mostly focus on proving conditions for which
\left\lvert\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)\right\rvert\leq 1
(i.e., uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs point processes).
However, at this point, we would like to point out that, in the setting studies in this paper, it also holds that
\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)\neq\emptyset
(i.e., there also exists an infinite-volume Gibbs point process).
This is formally given by the following standard result.

Theorem 3

(Existence,
[
20
, Theorem 5.6]
)
.

Suppose
\phi
is locally stable and has bounded range, then for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
it holds that
\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)\neq\emptyset
.

We remark that this is not the most general setting in which existence of infinite-volume Gibbs measures can be proven, but it suffices for our purposes.
The interested reader should refer to
[
7
, Section 2]
and
[
20
, Section 5]
for a better overview.

2.7.
Useful properties.

We proceed by proving a list of properties of Gibbs point processes that will be useful throughout the rest of the paper.
We start by constructing the space of
feasible counting measures

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{H}\coloneqq\{\eta\in\mathcal{N}\mid\text{ for all $\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}$ with $\xi\leq\eta$ it holds that $H(\xi)<\infty$}\}.

Feasible counting measures will play an important role in this work as boundary conditions.
In particular, in light of the DLR formalism in
Definition

2
, the following lemma allows us to restrict to boundary conditions from
\mathcal{N}_{H}
when characterizing infinite-volume Gibbs measures.

Lemma 4
.

For every pair potential
\phi
it holds that
\mathcal{N}_{H}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Moreover, for every activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and all
\mu\in\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)
it holds that
\mu(\mathcal{N}_{H})=1
.

Proof.

Fix some
x\in\mathcal{X}
and, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Lambda_{n}=\{y\in\mathcal{X}\mid d(x,y)<n\}
.
If
\eta\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{H}
then, by definition, there is some
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
with
\xi\leq\eta
and
H(\xi)=\infty
.
By the fact that
H
is hereditary and
\xi
is finite, we can find some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
H(\eta_{\Lambda_{n}})=\infty
.
Moreover, if such an
n\in\mathbb{N}
exists, then obviously
\eta\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{H}
.
By setting
A_{n}=\{\eta\in\mathcal{N}\mid H(\eta_{\Lambda_{n}})=\infty\}
and noting that
A_{n}\in\mathcal{R}
, we have
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{H}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Next, suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)
.
By the union bound, it suffices to show that
\mu(A_{n})=0
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Observe that for all
\xi\in\mathcal{N}
and all
\eta\in A_{n}
it holds that
H_{\Lambda_{n}}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
.
Therefore,
\mu_{\Lambda_{n}\mid\xi}(A_{n})=0
for all
\xi
such that
\mu_{\Lambda_{n}\mid\xi}
is well-defined.
But then, by the DLR formalism in
Definition

2
, it follows that
\mu(A_{n})=0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

As we will see shortly, configurations from
\mathcal{N}_{H}
are feasible in the sense that, for every
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
, the finite-volume Gibbs measure
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
is always well defined (i.e., the partition function converges).
Before we prove this, it will be convenient to introduce the following notion of
influence
of a configuration
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
on a single point
x\in\mathcal{X}
by setting

W(x,\eta)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x,y)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)

whenever the integral converges absolutely and
W(x,\eta)\coloneqq\infty
otherwise.
This definition of influence is particularly useful due to the following decompositions of
H_{\Lambda}
.

Lemma 5
.

For all
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
,
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{N}
it holds that

(2)

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\eta-\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)+\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})\eta(\mathrm{d}x).

Moreover, for all
x\in\Lambda
it holds that

(3)

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(\eta+\delta_{x}\mid\xi)=H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)+W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}}).

Proof.

To prove (
2
), we start by observing that
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
.
First, note that

H(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\eta-\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x),

which can be seen by writing both sides as finite sum of terms in
(\infty,\infty]
.
Moreover, note that
\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(x,y)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
if and only if
\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(x,y)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
for every
x\in S_{\eta}
.
If this is the case, then

\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(x,y)\xi(\mathrm{d}y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})\eta(\mathrm{d}x).

Otherwise, we have
\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)=\infty
and
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
by definition, proving identity (
2
).

For (
3
), we proceed similarly.
By rewriting
H(\eta+\delta_{x})
and
W(x,\eta)
as a sum of finitely many terms in
(\infty,\infty]
, observing that

H(\eta+\delta_{x})=H(\eta)+W(x,\eta).

Further, if
\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(y,z)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}z)(\eta+\delta_{x})(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
then

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(y,z)\xi(\mathrm{d}z)(\eta+\delta_{x})(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(y,z)\xi(\mathrm{d}z)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(x,z)\xi(\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(y,z)\xi(\mathrm{d}z)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)+W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})

with absolutely converging integrals.
Hence, in this case we get

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(\eta+\delta_{x}\mid\xi)

\displaystyle=H(\eta)+W(x,\eta)+\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(y,z)\xi(\mathrm{d}z)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)+W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})

\displaystyle=H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)+W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}}).

Otherwise, if
\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(y,z)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}z)(\eta+\delta_{x})(\mathrm{d}y)=\infty
then, by definition,
H_{\Lambda}(\eta+\delta_{x}\mid\xi)=\infty
.
Moreover, it must hold that
\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(y,z)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}z)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\infty
, which implies
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)
, or
\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(x,z)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}z)=\infty
, which implies
W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})=\infty
.
In both cases, identity (
3
) holds.
∎

We use (
2
) in conjunction with the following lower bound on
W(x,\eta)
to upper-bound the partition function under feasible boundary conditions.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
\phi
is a locally stable pair potential with constant
L
.
For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
it holds that
W(x,\eta)\geq-L
.

Proof.

First, we consider the case that
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{H}\cap\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
and observe that, by local stability,

W(x,\eta)=H(\eta+\delta_{x})-H(\eta)\geq-L.

Next, suppose that
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{H}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
, and let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}},y\mapsto\max(0,-\phi(x,y))
.
Note that to prove our claim, it suffices to show that
\int f\mathrm{d}\eta\leq L
.
Let
\eta=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{x_{i}}
for some sequence
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define
\eta_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
and note that
\eta_{n}\to\eta
setwise monotonically.
Therefore, we obtain

\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)\eta_{n}(\mathrm{d}y).

Next, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
S_{n}
be the set of indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\phi(x,x_{i})\leq 0
and let
\eta_{S_{n}}=\sum_{i\in S_{n}}\delta_{x_{i}}
.
Observe that
\eta_{S_{n}}\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
with
\eta_{s_{n}}\leq\eta
and, since
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
,
H(\eta_{S_{n}})<\infty
.
Consequently, by local stability, we have

\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)\eta_{n}(\mathrm{d}y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})=-\sum_{i\in S_{n}}\phi(x,x_{i})=-(H(\eta_{S_{n}}+\delta_{x})-H(\eta_{S_{n}}))\leq L.

Taking the limit
n\to\infty
concludes the proof.
∎

We proceed to show that
\mathcal{N}_{H}
provides boundary conditions for which finite-volume Gibbs measures are well-defined.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose
\phi
is locally stable with constant
L
.
For all
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
it holds that
\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)<\infty
.
In particular,
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
is well-defined.

Proof.

The main idea is to show that, for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
, it holds that
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)\geq-CL\eta(\Lambda)
for some absolute constant
C
.
If
H(\eta)=\infty
then
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
.
Otherwise, we use identity (
2
) and
Lemma

6
to obtain

H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\eta-\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)+\int_{\Lambda}W(x,\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\geq-\frac{3}{2}L\eta(\Lambda).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)\leq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{n}\textup{e}^{\frac{3}{2}Ln}\nu(\Lambda)^{n}}{n!},

which proves the claim.
∎

Knowing that
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
is well-defined for every
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
, (
3
) can be used to derive the following identity, which was independently proven by Georgii
[
14
]
and Nguyen and Zessin
[
38
]
, and which is hence known as GNZ equation.
As some of our definitions differ slightly from those of the original papers, we briefly reprove it.

Lemma 8

(Finite-volume GNZ equation
[
14
,
38
]
)
.

Suppose
\phi
is locally stable with constant
L
, and let
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
.
For all non-negative measurable
F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{N}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
it holds that

\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\int_{\Lambda}F(x,\eta)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\lambda\textup{e}^{-W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})}F(x,\eta+\delta_{x})\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

Proof.

Denoting by
Q_{\Lambda}
a Poisson point process with intensity measure
\nu(\cdot\cap\Lambda)
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\int_{\Lambda}F(x,\eta)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\textup{e}^{\nu(\Lambda)}}{\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\int_{\Lambda}F(x,\eta)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\lambda^{\eta(\Lambda)}\textup{e}^{-H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)}Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\int_{\Lambda}G(x,\eta)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

for
G(x,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{\textup{e}^{\nu(\Lambda)}}{\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)}F(x,\eta)\lambda^{\eta(\Lambda)}\textup{e}^{-H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)}
.
Applying the Mecke equation (see
[
27
, Proposition 4.1]
) and the decomposition in (
3
) then yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\int_{\Lambda}G(x,\eta)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}G(x,\eta+\delta_{x})Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\frac{\textup{e}^{\nu(\Lambda)}}{\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\lambda)}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\lambda\textup{e}^{-W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})}F(x,\eta+\delta_{x})\lambda^{\eta(\Lambda)}\textup{e}^{-H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)}Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}}\lambda\textup{e}^{-W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})}F(x,\eta+\delta_{x})\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\nu(\mathrm{d}x),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we introduce a restriction of
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}
to configurations that are consistent with a given boundary condition
\xi\in\mathcal{N}
by

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}\coloneqq\{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}\mid\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}}\in\mathcal{N}_{H}\}.

Using similar arguments as for
\mathcal{N}_{H}
, it can be proven that
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}\in\mathcal{R}
.
We denote the trace of
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
in
\mathcal{R}
by
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
.
Rather than studying the finite-volume Gibbs point process
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
on
(\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda},\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda})
, we will restrict it to
(\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi},\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda\mid\xi})
, which is justified for every
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
due to the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose
\phi
is locally stable with constant
L
, and let
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
. For every
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
it holds that
\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi})=1
.

Proof.

We show that
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
.
If
H(\eta)=\infty
this obviously holds.
Hence, assume
H(\eta)<\infty
.
By definition of
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}
and the fact that
H
is hereditary, we can find some
\zeta\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{f}}
,
\zeta\leq\xi_{\Lambda^{c}}
such that
H(\eta+\zeta)=\infty
.
Since
\xi\in\mathcal{N}_{H}
, it further holds that
H(\zeta)<\infty
.
Next, note that

H(\eta+\zeta)=H(\eta)+H(\zeta)+\int_{\Lambda}\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\phi(x,y)\zeta(\mathrm{d}y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x).

Hence, there must be some
x\in S_{\eta},y\in S_{\zeta}
such that
\phi(x,y)=\infty
.
But then, since
\zeta\leq\xi_{\Lambda^{c}}
, we know that
\int_{\Lambda^{c}}\left\lvert\phi(x,y)\right\rvert\xi(\mathrm{d}y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)=\infty
and hence
H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)=\infty
.
Thus, we have

\mu_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}^{c})=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda\mid\xi}}\lambda^{\eta(\Lambda)}\textup{e}^{-H_{\Lambda}(\eta\mid\xi)}Q_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}\eta)=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we introduce the main lemma that we use to show uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs point processes.
It is a simple criterion for equivalence of infinite-volume Gibbs measures that is derived from the DLR formalism.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{G}(\lambda,\phi)
for an activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and potential
\phi
.
Suppose there is a non-decreasing sequence
(\Lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
, such that

(1)

for every
\Delta\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
it holds that
\Delta\subseteq\Lambda_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and

(2)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\esssup_{\xi_{1}\sim\mu_{1},\xi_{2}\sim\mu_{2}}\left\lvert\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{1}}[\Lambda_{k}]-\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{2}}[\Lambda_{k}]\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}=0,

where
\left\lvert\cdot-\cdot\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}
denotes the total variation distance, and the essential supremum is taken over pairs
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
and with respect to the product measure
\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2}
.

Then
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1
, it suffices to show that
\mu_{1}(A)=\mu_{2}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{R}
of the form

A=\{N_{\Delta_{1}}=m_{1},\dots,N_{\Delta_{\ell}}=m_{\ell}\}

for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{\ell}\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{b}}
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
By assumption, we can find some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Delta_{j}\subseteq\Lambda_{k}
for all
1\leq j\leq\ell
.
This implies
\eta(\Delta_{j})=\eta_{\Lambda_{k}}(\Delta_{j})
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
and all
1\leq j\leq\ell
.
In particular, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda_{k+n}}
and all
\xi\in\mathcal{N}
, we have that
\eta+\xi_{\mathcal{X}\setminus\Lambda_{k+n}}\in A
if and only if
\eta_{\Lambda_{k}}\in A
.
By the DLR formalism, we obtain for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(A)=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\int_{\Lambda_{k+n}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\Lambda_{k}}\in A\}}\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi}[\Lambda_{k}](A)\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}\xi).

Applying the same argument to
\mu_{2}
yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mu_{1}(A)-\mu_{2}(A)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}}\int_{\mathcal{N}}\left\lvert\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{1}}[\Lambda_{k}](A)-\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{2}}[\Lambda_{k}](A)\right\rvert\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}\xi_{1})\mu_{2}(\mathrm{d}\xi_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}}\int_{\mathcal{N}}\left\lvert\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{1}}[\Lambda_{k}]-\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{2}}[\Lambda_{k}]\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}\xi_{1})\mu_{2}(\mathrm{d}\xi_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\esssup_{\xi_{1}\sim\mu_{1},\xi_{2}\sim\mu_{2}}\left\lvert\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{1}}[\Lambda_{k}]-\mu_{\Lambda_{k+n}\mid\xi_{2}}[\Lambda_{k}]\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}.

Since this holds for all
n
, taking the limit
n\to\infty
shows
\mu_{1}(A)=\mu_{2}(A)
, concluding the proof.
∎

3.
Jump processes

3.1.
Terminology and notation

We recall basic terminology related to kernel and transition groups.
Given a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
, a
kernel
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
is a map
K:E\times\mathcal{E}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
such that:

(1)

For all
x\in E
,
K(x,\cdot)
is a measure on
(E,\mathcal{E})
.

(2)

For all
A\in\mathcal{E}
,
K(\cdot,A)
is measurable.

Further we say a kernel
K
is

•

finite
if
K(x,\cdot)
is a finite measure for all
x\in E
,

•

sub-stochastic
if
K(x,E)\leq 1
for all
x\in E
, and

•

stochastic
if
K(x,E)=1
for all
x\in E
.

In particular, if
K
is stochastic then
K(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure for all
x\in E
.

Occasionally, it will be useful to associate a kernel with an operator on the space of non-negative measurable functions
f:E\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.
More precisely, we define the function
Kf:E\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
by setting

(Kf)(x)\coloneqq\int_{E}f(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)

for
x\in E
.
Note that
Kf
is again measurable (this can be seen by taking monotone limits of simple functions).

Based on these definitions, a
sub-Markov transition family
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
is a collection
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
such that

(1)

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
P_{t}
is a sub-stochastic kernel.

(2)

For all
x\in E
and
A\in\mathcal{E}
we have
P_{0}(x,A)=\delta_{x}(A)
.

(3)

(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
satisfies the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation: for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and all
x\in E
,
A\in\mathcal{E}
it holds that

P_{t+s}(x,A)=\int_{E}P_{s}(y,A)P_{t}(x,\mathrm{d}y).

When talking about a sub-Markov transition family, we will usually drop the index set and simply write
(P_{t})
instead of
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.
Further, we say that
(P_{t})
is a
Markov transition family
if
P_{t}
is stochastic for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

3.2.
Jump kernel and associated transition families

We proceed by introducing the basic terminology related to Markov jump processes with values in a general measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
We will always assume that
\mathcal{E}
contains all singletons (i.e.,
\{x\}\in\mathcal{E}
for all
x\in E
).

Throughout the paper, we mostly characterize Markov jump processes by their
jump kernel
.
A function
K:E\times\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a jump kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
if:

(1)

K
is a finite kernel.

(2)

For all
x\in E
we have
K(x,\{x\})=0
.

Given a jump kernel
K
, we call
\kappa:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},x\mapsto K(x,E)
the
associated rate function
.
Further, note that
\Pi:E\times\mathcal{E}\to[0,1]
with

(5)

\displaystyle\Pi(x,A)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\kappa(x)}\cdot K(x,A)&\text{ if $\kappa(x)>0$}\\
\delta_{x}(A)&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

defines a Markov kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
, which we call the
associated transition kernel
.

Given a jump kernel
K
with jump rate function
\kappa
, we recursively define a sub-Markov transition family
(P^{(n)}_{t})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as follows.
For every
x\in E
,
A\in\mathcal{E}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we set

(6)

\displaystyle P_{t}^{(0)}(x,A)=\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}\cdot\delta_{x}(A).

Further, for
n\geq 1
, we set

(7)

\displaystyle P_{t}^{(n)}(x,A)=\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}\cdot\delta_{x}(A)+\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)s}\int_{E}P_{t-s}^{(n-1)}(y,A)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}s.

The following properties can easily be checked via induction over
n
(see for example
[
11
]
):

(1)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
(P_{t}^{(n)})
is a well-defined sub-Markov transition family, and for every
A\in\mathcal{E}
the map
(t,x)\mapsto P_{t}^{(n)}(x,A)
is measurable.

(2)

For every
x\in E
,
A\in\mathcal{E}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, it holds that
P_{t}^{(n)}(x,A)
is non-decreasing in
n
.

It immediately follows by monotone convergence that the limit
P_{t}(x,A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{t}^{(n)}(x,A)
exists and defines a sub-Markov transition family
(P_{t})
.
We call
(P_{t})
the
sub-Markov transition family associated to

K
.
Note that
(P_{t})
is in fact Markov whenever
P_{t}(x,E)=1
for all
x\in E
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
In that case, we say that the jump kernel
K
is
non-explosive
and simply call
(P_{t})
the
transition family associated to

K
.

To check if a given jump kernel is non-explosive, we are going to use the following simple criterion.

Lemma 11

(
[
5
, Theorem
16
]
)
.

Let
K
be a jump kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
and let
\kappa
denote its rate function.
Suppose there exists a sequence
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
E_{n}\in\mathcal{E}
and a non-negative measurable function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that the following holds:

(1)

E_{n}
converges to
E
monotonically.

(2)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\sup_{x\in E_{n}}\kappa(x)<\infty
.

(3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{x\in E\setminus E_{n}}f(x)=\infty
.

(4)

There exists some
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\in E
we have

(Kf)(x)\leq(c+\kappa(x))\cdot f(x)

Then
K
is non-explosive.

3.3.
Stationary distribution and reversibility

Given a Markov transition family
(P_{t})
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
, we call a probability distribution
\pi
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
a
stationary distribution
(or
invariant probability measure
) for
(P_{t})
if for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A\in\mathcal{E}
it holds that

\int_{E}P_{t}(x,A)\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=\pi(A).

A sufficient criterion for
\pi
to be a stationary distribution is if for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
it holds that

\int_{A}P_{t}(x,B)\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{B}P_{t}(x,A)\pi(\mathrm{d}x),

in which case we say that
(P_{t})
is
reversible
with respect to
\pi
.
Note that the definition of reversibility extends to sub-Markov transition families.
However, it only implies that
\pi
is an invariant measure if
(P_{t})
is stochastic (hence Markov).

If
(P_{t})
is generated by a jump kernel
K
, reversible measures can be identified using
K
.
This is given by the following lemma, which we believe was first proven by Chen
[
4
]
.

Lemma 12

(
[
37
, Proposition 2]
)
.

Let
K
be a jump kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
with associated (sub-)Markov transition family
(P_{t})
.
If a probability measure
\pi
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
satisfies

\int_{A}K(x,B)\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{B}K(x,A)\pi(\mathrm{d}x)

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
then
(P_{t})
is reversible with respect to
\pi
.
Further, if
K
is non-explosive then
\pi
is invariant under
(P_{t})
.

3.4.
Coupling and convergence

Given probability measures
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
on
(E_{1},\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
(E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{2})
, a coupling of
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
is a probability measure
\pi
on the product space
(E_{1}\times E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2})
with
\pi(A_{1}\times E_{2})=\pi_{1}(A_{1})
for all
A_{1}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\pi(E_{1}\times A_{2})=\pi_{2}(A_{2})
for all
A_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
A useful aspect of couplings is that they provide a way of bounding the total variation distance between probability distributions.

Lemma 13

(
[
18
, Theorem 2.4]
)
.

Let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
be probability measures on
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
Suppose
(E,\mathcal{E})
is such that
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}
contains the diagonal set
D\coloneqq\{(x,x)\mid x\in E\}
then, for every coupling
\pi
of
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, it holds that

\left\lvert\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\leq 2\cdot\pi(D^{c}),

where
\left\lvert\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\coloneqq\sup_{A\in\mathcal{E}}\left\lvert\pi_{1}(A)-\pi_{2}(A)\right\rvert
denotes the total variation distance between
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
.

The notion of a coupling extends directly to Markov transition families as follows.

Definition 14
.

Suppose
(P_{t})
,
(P^{\prime}_{t})
are Markov transition families on
(E_{1},\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
(E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
We call a Markov transition family
(Q_{t})
on
(E_{1}\times E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2})
a coupling of
(P_{t})
,
(P^{\prime}_{t})
if for all
x\in E_{1},y\in E_{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
it holds that
Q_{t}((x,y),\cdot)
is a coupling of
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
P^{\prime}_{t}(y,\cdot)
.

We wish to construct couplings between (jump) Markov transition families based on their jump kernels.
Following the ideas in Chen
[
5
]
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 15
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be jump kernels on
(E_{1},\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
(E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
We call a jump kernel
K
on
(E_{1}\times E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2})
a coupling of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
if for all non-negative bounded measurable functions
f_{1}:E_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},f_{2}:E_{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and all
x_{1}\in E,x_{2}\in E_{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{E_{1}\times E_{2}}(f_{1}(y_{1})-f_{1}(x_{1}))K((x_{1},x_{2}),\mathrm{d}y_{1}\times\mathrm{d}y_{2})=(K_{1}f_{1})(x_{1})-\kappa_{1}(x_{1})\cdot f_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle\int_{E_{1}\times E_{2}}(f_{2}(y_{2})-f_{2}(x_{2}))K((x_{1},x_{2}),\mathrm{d}y_{1}\times\mathrm{d}y_{2})=(K_{2}f_{2})(x_{2})-\kappa_{2}(x_{2})\cdot f_{2}(x_{2}).

The following result relates the two notions of a coupling that are given in
Definitions

14
and
15
.

Lemma 16

(
[
5
, Theorems
13
and
25
]
)
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be non-explosive jump kernels on
(E_{1},\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
(E_{2},\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
If
K
is a coupling of
K_{1},K_{2}
in the sense of
Definition

15
then
K
is non-explosive, and the Markov transition family associated to
K
is a coupling of the Markov transition families associated to
K_{1},K_{2}
in the sense of
Definition

14
.

A central part of this paper will be to bound the total variation distance of certain jump Markov transition families from their stationary distribution (provided it exists) after a sufficiently large time span
t
.
The following technical lemma will be useful for that.

Lemma 17
.

Let
K
be a jump kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
with jump rate function
\kappa
and (sub-)Markov transition family
(P_{t})
.
Suppose
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a non-negative measurable function such that there exists some
\delta>0
such that for all
x\in E
it holds that

(Kf)(x)\leq(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot f(x).

Then, for all
x\in E
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we have

(P_{t}f)(x)\leq\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(x).

Proof.

We show by induction that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
it holds that

(P_{t}^{(n)}f)(x)\leq\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(x),

where
(P^{(n)}_{t})
is as defined in (
6
) and (
7
).
The desired result then follows from the fact that, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
x\in E
,
P_{t}^{(n)}(x,\cdot)\to P_{t}(x,\cdot)
setwise and monotonically as
n\to\infty
.

For the base case
n=0
, we have by (
6
) that

(P_{t}^{(0)}f)(x)=\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}f(x).

Hence, we need to show
\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}f(x)\leq\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(x)
for all
x\in E
.
Since this holds trivially if
f(x)=0
, we focus on
x\in E
such that
f(x)>0
.
Because
f
is non-negative, our assumptions imply
0\leq(Kf)(x)\leq(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot f(x)
.
Thus, we have
\delta\leq\kappa(x)
for all
x\in E
with
f(x)>0
and the desired inequality follows.

For the induction step, combining (
7
) and the induction hypothesis yields

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{(n)}f)(x)

\displaystyle=\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}\cdot f(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)s}\int_{E}(P_{t-s}^{(n-1)}f)(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}\cdot f(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)s}\int_{E}\textup{e}^{-\delta\cdot(t-s)}f(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}\cdot f(x)+\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot s}(Kf)(x)\mathrm{d}s.

Using
(Kf)(x)\leq(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot f(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{(n)}f)(x)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}+\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot s}(\kappa(x)-\delta)\mathrm{d}s\right)\cdot f(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)t}+\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\left(1-\textup{e}^{-(\kappa(x)-\delta)\cdot t}\right)\right)\cdot f(x)

\displaystyle=\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(x),

which concludes the induction step and proves the claim.
∎

We can now combine
Lemma

17
with a suitable coupling of a non-explosive jump kernel to derive information about the speed of convergence of the associated Markov transition family to its stationary distribution.

Lemma 18
.

Let
K
be a non-explosive jump kernel on
(E,\mathcal{E})
and assume the associated Markov transition group
(P_{t})
has a stationary distribution
\pi
.
Suppose there is a coupling
\tilde{K}
of
K
with itself (in the sense of
Definition

15
) and a non-negative measurable function
f:E\times E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that the following hold:

(1)

There exists some
c>0
such that
f(x,y)\geq c
if and only if
x\neq y
.

(2)

There exists some
\delta>0
such that for all
x,y\in E

(\tilde{K}f)(x,y)\leq(\tilde{\kappa}(x,y)-\delta)\cdot f(x,y),

where
\tilde{\kappa}
is the rate function of
\tilde{K}
.

Then, for all
x\in E
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, it holds that

\left\lvert P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\leq\frac{1}{c}\cdot\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{E}f(x,y)\pi(\mathrm{d}y).

Proof.

By
Lemma

16
we know that
\tilde{K}
is non-explosive and that the associated Markov transition family
(Q_{t})
is a coupling of
(P_{t})
with itself in the sense of
Definition

14
.
Further, by (
2
) and
Lemma

17
, we have for all
x,y\in E
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that
(Q_{t}f)(x,y)\leq\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(x,y)
.

Next, for
x\in E
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A\in\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}
define

Q_{x,t}(A)\coloneqq\int_{E\times E}Q_{t}(z,A)(\delta_{x}\otimes\pi)(\mathrm{d}z),

where
\delta_{x}\otimes\pi
is the product probability measure of
\delta_{x}
and
\pi
.
Note that for all
x
and
t
it holds that
Q_{x,t}
is a probability measure on
(E\times E,\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E})
.
Using Fubini’s theorem and the fact that
(Q_{t})
is a coupling of
(P_{t})
with itself, we obtain for all
A\in\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle Q_{x,t}(A\times E)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\int_{E}Q_{t}((z_{1},z_{2}),A\times E)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}z_{1})\pi(\mathrm{d}z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\int_{E}P_{t}(z_{1},A)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}z_{1})\pi(\mathrm{d}z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{E}P_{t}(z_{1},A)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}z_{1})

\displaystyle=P_{t}(x,A).

Further, since
\pi
is invariant with respect to
(P_{t})
, we have analogously

Q_{x,t}(E\times A)=\int_{E}P_{t}(z_{2},A)\pi(\mathrm{d}z_{2})=\pi(A).

This shows that
Q_{x,t}
is a coupling of
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
\pi
.
Note that by (
1
) we may express the diagonal as
D=\{(x,x)\mid x\in E\}=\{(x,y)\in E\times E\mid f(x,y)<c\}
and, since
f
is measurable,
D\in\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}
.
By
Lemma

13
, it suffices to bound
Q_{x,t}(D^{c})
.
Applying Markov’s inequality, we get

\left\lvert P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\leq Q_{x,t}(D^{c})\leq\frac{1}{c}\int_{E\times E}f(z)Q_{x,t}(\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{1}{c}\int_{E\times E}(Q_{t}f)(z)(\delta_{x}\otimes\pi)(\mathrm{d}z).

Finally, since
(Q_{t}f)(z)\leq\textup{e}^{-\delta t}f(z)
, applying Fubini’s theorem yields

\left\lvert P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\rvert_{\mathrm{tv}}\leq\frac{1}{c}\cdot\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{E\times E}f(z)(\delta_{x}\otimes\pi)(\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{1}{c}\cdot\textup{e}^{-\delta t}\int_{E}f(x,y)\pi(\mathrm{d}y),

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.5.
Markov process associated with a jump kernel

We proceed by discussing in what sense we can associate a stochastic process (more precisely a Markov jump process) on
(E,\mathcal{E})
with a given jump kernel.
By a stochastic process we mean a tuple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}})
where
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space and
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
is a collection of random variables
X_{t}:\Omega\to E
.
Since we will always use
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as the index set for
(X_{t})
, we will drop it from now on to simplify notation (the same will hold for filtrations as introduced below).
For every
\omega\in\Omega
, we call the function
t\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)
the
sample path
of
\omega
.
We call a stochastic process
measurable
if the map
(t,\omega)\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\otimes\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{E}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
is the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

A
filtration
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(X_{t}))
is a sequence of
\sigma
-fields
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
such that
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
s\leq t
.
We call a random variable
T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
a stopping time with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
if, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the event
\{T\leq t\}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.
Given a stopping time
T
with respect to some filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
, we define
\mathcal{F}_{T}\coloneqq\{A\in\mathcal{F}\mid\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:A\cap\{T\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
.
Further, say that a stochastic process is
progressive
with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
if for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
it holds that
(s,\omega)\mapsto X_{s}(\omega)
as a function from
[0,t]\times\Omega
into
E
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{[0,t]}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{E}
.
A particularly important filtration is the
natural filtration

(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
, which is given by
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(X_{s}\mid s\in[0,t])
.

Finally, given a probability measure
\mu
and a Markov transition family
(P_{t})
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
, we say that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(X_{t}))
is a
Markov process
with
initial distribution

\mu
and
transition function

(P_{t})
if:

(1)

for all
A\in\mathcal{E}
we have
\mathbb{P}[X_{0}\in A]=\mu(A)
, and

(2)

for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and all
A\in\mathcal{E}
it holds that
P_{t}(X_{s},A)
is a version of the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}[X_{s+t}\in A\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{s}]
.

Note that, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A\in\mathcal{E}
, it holds that
x\mapsto P_{t}(x,A)
is measurable. Hence, it also holds that
\omega\mapsto P_{t}(X_{s}(\omega),A)
is measurable with respect to
\sigma(X_{s})
and thus

\mathbb{P}[X_{s+t}\in A\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{s}]=\mathbb{P}[X_{s+t}\in A\mid X_{s}]\text{\hskip 30.00005pt $\mathbb{P}$-almost surely.}

This is usually referred to as the Markov property.

It turns out that, given a non-explosive jump kernel
K
and associated transition family
(P_{t})
, we can construct a particularly nice version of the Markov process with transition function
(P_{t})
for any given initial distribution (see for example
[
3
, §1.12]
and
[
22
]
).

Theorem 19
.

Let
K
be a non-explosive jump kernel and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
There is a stochastic process
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu},(X_{t}))
with values in
(E,\mathcal{E})
such that:

(1)

The sample paths of
(X_{t})
are piecewise constant.
That is, for every
\omega\in\Omega
and
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
there is some
h>0
such that the map
t\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)
is constant on
[s,s+h)
.

(2)

For every
A\in\mathcal{E}
,
T_{A}\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid X_{t}\in A\}
is a stopping time with respect to
(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
.

(3)

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu},(X_{t}))
is a Markov process with initial distribution
\mu
and transition function
(P_{t})
.

(4)

The process satisfies the following version of the
strong Markov property
: Suppose
T
is a stopping time with respect to
(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
, then for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
A\in\mathcal{E}

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T<\infty,X_{T+t}\in A\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{T}]=\mathds{1}_{\{T<\infty\}}\cdot P_{t}(X_{T},A)\text{ $\mathbb{P}_{\mu}$-a.s.}

Moreover, on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
we can construct random variables
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with values in
(E,\mathcal{E})
and
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with values in
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
such that

(5)

It holds that
\tau_{0}=0
and
n\mapsto\tau_{n}
is strictly increasing.

(6)

(Z_{n})
is a Markov chain on
(E,\mathcal{E})
with transition kernel
\Pi
(see (
5
)) and initial law
\mu
.

(7)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\tau_{n}\leq t<\tau_{n+1}
it holds that
X_{t}=Z_{n}
.

We will make use of the following two lemmas about hitting times for the process that is given by
Theorem

19
.
The first lemma formalizes the following intuition: if, for two sets
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
, the rate of jumping from outside of
A\cup B
into
B
is
0
, then the process must (almost surely) go through
A
to ever reach
B
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
K
be a non-explosive jump kernel and
\mu
be a probability distribution on
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu},(X_{t}))
be the Markov process associated with
K
and
\mu
as above.
Suppose
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
are such that
\mu(B)=0
and
K(x,B)=0
for all
x\notin A\cup B
, and define
T_{A}\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid X_{t}\in A\}
and
T_{B}\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid X_{t}\in B\}
.
It holds that
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{B}\leq T_{A},T_{B}<\infty]=0
.

Proof.

Note that by
Theorem

19
(
7
) it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{B}\leq T_{A},T_{B}<\infty]

The second lemma states that, if we consider a sequence of regions

Claim 21

Let

be a jump kernel on

for

Proof.

We show that the statement holds for each of the recursively defined sub-Markov families

For

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{(0)}(x,A)+\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\kappa(x)\cdot s}\int_{E}P_{t-s}^{(n)}(x,A)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}s

4.
Constructing spatial birth-death dynamics for Gibbs point processes

In this section we construct the jump process that we will use to prove uniqueness of infinite volume Gibbs measures.
To this end, fix some activity
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
\phi

For every

\displaystyle K_{\Lambda\mid\xi}(\eta,A)\coloneqq\int_{\Lambda}\mathds{1}_{\{A\}}(\eta\setminus\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)+\lambda\int_{\Lambda}\mathds{1}_{\{A\}}(\eta+\delta_{x})\cdot\textup{e}^{-W(x,\eta+\xi_{\Lambda^{c}})}\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

Lemma 24
.

Let

Using the lower bound on

We check that this choice of

Obviously, we have that

Throughout the following subsections, we study two important properties of the transition family

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\leq\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell+1})\in S^{\text{o}}_{\ell+1}(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathbb{P}[T_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell+1}}<T_{\boldsymbol{k}_{\ell}}<\dots<T_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\leq t]

To this end, fix some

By

6.
Discussion

We conclude the paper by comparing our result to the existing literature.
We further give a detailed discussion of our assumptions and potential ways to extend our results in future work.

6.1.
Comparison to existing bounds for absence of phase transitions

To compare our results to the existing literature, it is useful to introduce the inverse temperature parameter
\beta\geq 0
.
For any fixed pair potential
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we denote by
\phi_{\beta}\coloneqq\beta\phi
the pair potential at inverse temperature
\beta
.
Intuitively, changing
\beta
scales the strength of interactions between particles: in the small temperature limit (
\beta\to\infty
) interactions between particles become more relevant for the behavior of the system, whereas in the large temperature limit (
\beta\to 0
) only hard-core interactions (cases where
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)=\infty
) prevail.
We write
\hat{C}_{\beta}\coloneqq\hat{C}_{\phi_{\beta}}
for the associated weak temperedness constant and
C_{\beta}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|1-\textup{e}^{-\beta\phi(x,y)}|\mathrm{d}y
for the associated temperedness constant.
Note that, if
\phi
is (weakly) tempered, the same applies to
\phi_{\beta}
for all
\beta
(see for example
[
20
, Exercise 5.3]
).
Moreover, if
\phi
is locally stable with constant
L
, then
\phi_{\beta}
is locally stable with constant
\beta L
.
Our bound for uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure then translates into
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{\beta L}\hat{C}_{\beta})^{-1}
with the additional assumption that
\phi
has bounded range.

We start by comparing our result with the classical Penrose–Ruelle bound of
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{2\beta S+1}C_{\beta})^{-1}
for stable pair potentials, where
S
is the stability constant of
\phi
, which first appeared in
[
30
,
34
]
.
Within this regime of
\lambda
, uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs measures can be established by considering the Kirkwood–Salsburg equations, which describe the point density functions of any infinite-volume Gibbs point process as a fix point of a linear operator.
Arguing that this linear operator contracts under a suitable metric proves uniqueness of that fixed point and by extension shows that there is at most one Gibbs measure (see
[
20
, Chapters 5.6-5.8]
for a detailed discussion).
In the setting of locally stable potentials, this approach yields uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs measures for
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{\beta L+1}C_{\beta})^{-1}
.
Hence, our result yields an improvement by a factor of
\textup{e}C_{\beta}/\hat{C}_{\beta}
at the cost of relying on a bounded-range assumption.
In particular, this improvement is lower-bounded by a factor of
e
, and if
\phi
has some non-trivial negative part (i.e., attractive interactions), it becomes more substantial for large
\beta
(i.e., low temperature).

The second bound we want to compare our result to is a recently obtained result by Qidong He
[
16
]
.
They showed that for locally stable, tempered, and translation and rotation invariant pair potentials, the (empty-boundary) infinite-volume pressure
\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu(\Lambda_{n})^{-1}\log(\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\boldsymbol{0}}(\lambda))
is an analytic function
5
5

5

Here, the limit is taken along a suitable sequence
\Lambda_{n}\nearrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. See
[
20
]
for a detailed discussion.
for any
\lambda<\textup{e}^{2(1-W(\textup{e}A_{\beta}/C_{\beta}))}(\textup{e}^{\beta L+1}C_{\beta})^{-1}
, where
W
denotes Lambert’s W-function and
A_{\beta}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{\{\phi(0,x)<0\}}\cdot(\textup{e}^{-\beta\phi(0,x)}-1)\mathrm{d}x
.
The result is obtained by adapting a technique introduced by Michelen and Perkins
[
29
]
for repulsive potentials (i.e.,
\phi\geq 0
), which relies on showing contraction of a recursive integral identity for the (generalized complex) point density.
We point out that this result aims at a different notion of absence of a phase transition, and, to our knowledge, does not a priori imply uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure in the DLR sense.
However, we believe that their proof can be modified to show uniqueness up to the same activity threshold via similar arguments as in
[
29
, Section 4]
, while handling the effect of the boundary condition as part of the modulation of the activity function in the sense of
[
16
]
.
With this in mind, we observe that the relation of their bound to ours heavily depends on the inverse temperature
\beta
and the potential in question.
In particular, if the potential is purely repulsive or the temperature is high (i.e., small
\beta
), their bound is up to a factor of
e
larger than ours.
On the other hand, for potentials with a non-trivial attractive parts and low temperature (i.e., large
\beta
) our bound performs better as
W(\textup{e}A_{\beta}/C_{\beta})\in[0,1)
while
C_{\beta}/\hat{C}_{\beta}\to\infty
for
\beta\to\infty
.
Again, this improvement comes at the cost that we require the potential to have bounded range.

Lastly, we compare our result to the bound for analyticity of the infinite-volume pressure (again with empty boundary) by Procacci and Yuhjtman
[
33
]
.
They show that for stable and weakly tempered potentials, the infinite-volume pressure is analytic for all
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{\beta S+1}\hat{C}_{\beta})^{-1}
by proving absolute convergence of the cluster expansion, a series expansion of
\log(\Xi_{\Lambda\mid\boldsymbol{0}}(\lambda))
.
In the setting of locally stable potentials, this translates to a bound of
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{\beta L/2+1}\hat{C}_{\beta})^{-1}
.
Again, this approach aims at a different notion of phase transition.
However, as opposed to the result we discussed before, it is not known whether uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure can be proven for the same activity regime.
On the one hand, the precise relation between conditions for convergence of the cluster expansion and uniqueness of Gibbs measures is elusive (see
[
21
]
for a more comprehensive discussion).
On the other hand, it is not clear whether the activity bound is preserved when accounting for the effect of boundary conditions on the convergence criterion (see
[
13
]
for a similar observation).
If uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure were shown to hold up to the convergence bound from
[
33
]
, then this would give an improvement over our result in the high-temperature regime (
\beta<2/L
), while our result would still be stronger at low temperature.
However, a proof of this fact would be non-trivial, and it is not clear whether it should hold at all.
It would make for an interesting contrast to our previous comparison to
[
16
]
, where our bound was better at low-temperature.

We finish our discussion of related uniqueness results by noting that for the special case of purely non-negative potentials, stronger results are known due to Michelen and Perkins
[
29
,
28
]
, which improve our uniqueness regime by a factor of at least
e
.
It remains open whether such an improvement carries over to potentials with non-trivial negative part.
As discussed before, a promising step towards this was presented in
[
16
]
, which maintains this improvement at small
\beta
.

6.2.
Assumptions and extensions

The first assumption we discuss is that
\phi
needs to have bounded range.
This is in fact only required in the disagreement percolation argument in
Section

4.3
, where we use it to make sure that disagreements between any pair of birth-death processes only spread locally.
In contrast, our second main ingredient, which is that each birth-death process rapidly converges to a finite-volume Gibbs distribution, does not rely on it.
A similar observation is true for the fact that we restrict our main result to Euclidean space.
While the mixing result in
Lemma

18
holds in any complete separable metric space, the disagreement percolation in
Lemma

30
requires additional information on the growth rate of the underlying space.
Both of these assumptions are inherited from the discrete origin of our proof technique
[
10
]
, which is in studying lattice gasses that naturally satisfy such conditions.
However, one could hope to remove the bounded-range assumption by applying a suitable truncation of the pair potential at some range (maybe requiring additional assumptions on the decay rate of the pair potential).

The second main assumption we discuss is local stability.
This assumption is relevant throughout most of our proofs: we use it for proving non-explosiveness of the jump kernel (in
Lemma

24
), to bound the rate at which the Markov transition family converges to its stationary distribution (in
Lemma

18
and
Claim

28
) and to bound the overall rate at which two coupled processes spawn a disagreement (in
Lemma

30
and
Lemma

29
).
All of these applications make use of the fact that local stability permits a bounding of the rate at which new points are generated uniformly over the configurations space of the process.
Interestingly, other approaches to prove uniqueness, such as proving contraction of the Kirkwood–Salsburg operator, encounter similar difficulties; there the problem is solved by observing that stability implies that at least one point in any configuration interacts in a “locally stable manner” with the rest of the configuration (see
[
35
, Chapter 4.2]
for details).
However, it is not clear how a similar strategy can be implemented here.

Finally, we would like to take a more detailed look at our obtained activity bound of
\lambda<(\textup{e}^{L}\hat{C}_{\phi})^{-1}
.
This bound is solely due to the activity regime for which we can prove that each birth-death process converges rapidly to its stationary distribution.
Therefore any improvement on the activity bound for rapid mixing would immediately improve the uniqueness regime.
The main candidate for approaching this is to use a more suitable metric
f
on the configuration space in the proof of
Lemma

18
(see also
Lemma

27
) to show contraction of the coupling.
For the hard-sphere model (i.e.,
\phi(x,y)=\infty\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{d(x,y)<r\}}
for some fixed
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
), this approach was used by Helmuth, Perkins and Petti
[
17
]
to gain an additional factor of
2
on the activity bound.
The intuition behind their metric was to weight pairs of configurations based on how likely they are to spawn disagreements.
In particular, this approach exploits the fact that, in the hard-sphere model, adding a point only decreases this likelihood.
While this idea generalizes nicely for other repulsive potentials (i.e.,
\phi\geq 0
), it becomes much harder to apply with attractive interactions, where adding a point might make it more likely to spawn new disagreements.
However, focusing on more specific models could be a reasonable step towards finding better conditions for rapid convergence of the associated birth-death dynamics.
