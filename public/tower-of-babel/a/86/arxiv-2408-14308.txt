Directional descent
Andrew J. Young

Abstract
We identity the optimal non-infinitesimal direction of descent for a convex function. An algorithm is developed that can theoretically minimize a subset of (non-convex) functions.



1
Introduction

Non-smooth convex functions can be minimized using various algorithms
[
1
]
(more efficiently with additional structure
[
2
,
1
]
).
Non-convex optimization problems are in general NP-hard but, with some restrictions, e.g. strong smoothness properties and requiring only
\varepsilon
-stationary solutions, there are efficient algorithms
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
and complexity bounds
[
6
,
7
]
.
We consider both non-convexity and limited smoothness.

Our algorithm is two-staged: the first stage is finding the minimizer of a constrained optimization problem and the second is an incrementation step.
More specifically, the first stage seeks the minimizer of a function over a closed ball with some positive radius.
The solution of this optimization is the optimal direction of descent for any ball with positive radius (Theorem
5
).
The second stage proceeds from the initial point along the given direction.
If the function is Lipschitz, then given an appropriately close solution in the first stage, e.g. order
\varepsilon
, the second stage provides an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution with iteration complexity
O(1/\varepsilon)
.

This algorithm can be applied to any function that is lower semicontinuous with a bounded effective domain and unique minimizer whose points of convexity in its effective domain have a nonempty interior.

We begin with some auxiliary results related to lower convex envelopes.

2
Lower convex envelope

Let
X
be a normed vector space and
f:X\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
.
The lower convex envelope of
f
is the pointwise supremum of all convex functions lower bounding
f
, i.e.

\sup\{h(x):h\leq f\text{ convex}\},

and, as the pointwise supremum of an arbitrary collection of convex functions is convex, it is convex.
In the sequel, the lower convex envelope of a function
f
is denoted by
\breve{f}
.

We give an explicit characterization of the lower convex envelope.
This representation is well known and can be found in
[
8
]
on page 36.

Lemma 1
.

The lower convex envelope of
f
is

g(x)=\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}f(x_{k}):(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\in C(x,f)\right\},

where

C(x,f):=\left\{(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x}):\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}x_{k}=x,m\in\mathbb{N},\lambda_{k}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\!=\!1,x_{k}\in\mathrm{dom}(f)\right\},

\mathrm{dom}(f)
is the effective domain of
f
and the infimum of the empty set is
\infty
.

f
is
convex at a point

x
, if, for all
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\in C(x,f)
,

f(x)\leq\sum\lambda_{k}f(x_{k}).

This definition includes points
x
outside
\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
which have
f
infinite, empty
C(x,f)
and are thus, trivially, convex.
Moreover, If
x
is an extreme point of
\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
then
C(x,f)=\{(1,x)\}
and thus
x
is a point of convexity for
f
.
Let
A_{f}
be the
set of points where
f
is convex
.
We have the following immediate corollary to Lemma
1
.

Corollary 2
.

A_{f}=\{x:f(x)=\breve{f}(x)\}.

3
Euclidean space

We now specialize to the case where
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
n
-dimensional Euclidean space endowed with the Euclidean norm and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
.

3.1
Sufficiency of the lower convex envelope

Suppose
f
is not convex but has a
unique
minimizer.
Any minimizer of
f
is a minimizer of the lower convex envelope
\breve{f}
, but the converse is not necessarily true.
We give sufficient conditions for
\breve{f}
to have the same unique minimizer.
Necessity of these conditions is also discussed.

Theorem 3
.

If
f
is lower semicontinuous with a bounded effective domain and unique minimizer, then the lower convex envelope of
f
has the same unique minimizer.

Proof.

Let
x^{\ast}
be the unique minimizer of
f
, so that
f(x)>f(x^{\ast})
for all
x\neq x^{\ast}
.
By Caratheodory (Theorem
8
) the sets
C(x,f)
can be restricted to vectors of length
n+1
.
Choose by boundedness any compact set
B
containing
\mathrm{dom}(f)
.
Let
g(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}f(x_{k})
on
[0,1]^{n+1}\times B^{n+1}
with the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
.
Then (by properties of
\liminf
)
g
is lower semicontinuous (lsc).
Let

D_{x}=\left\{(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x}):\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}x_{k}=x,\lambda_{k}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}=1,x_{k}\in B\right\}.

Then
D_{x}
is compact, as
[0,1]^{n+1}\times B^{n+1}
is compact if
B
compact, the functions
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}x_{k}
and
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}
are continuous and the intersection of closed sets, the preimage of
x
and
1
, respectively, with a compact set is compact.
By construction, the infimum in Lemma
1
can be extended to
D_{x}
instead of
C(x,f)
.
As in that lemma, if
D_{x}
is empty
\breve{f}(x)
is infinite.
The infimum of an lsc function on a nonempty compact set
g:D_{x}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is achieve by some
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})
.
Then, for any such
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{k}f(x_{k})=f(x^{\ast})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda_{k}>0\Longrightarrow f(x_{k})=f(x^{\ast})\Longrightarrow x_{k}=x^{\ast}.

Hence
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\in D_{x^{\ast}}
.
∎

Necessity of lower semicontinuity:

f(x)=\begin{cases}0&x=0\\
x-1/2&1/2<x\leq 1\\
\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Necessity of bounded effective domain:

f(x)=\begin{cases}\lvert x\rvert&-1\leq x\leq 1\\
1&\text{else}\end{cases}.

3.2
Existence of subgradients

It is well known that a proper convex function has subgradients at all the interior points of its effective domain (Proposition
9
).
We explore the connection between points of convexity and existence of subgradients.

Lemma 4
.

i)

If
f
has a subgradient at
z
, then
f
is convex at
z
.

ii)

If
\breve{f}
is proper,
z\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
and
f
is convex at
z
, then
f
has a subgradient at
z
.

Proof.

(i)

If
f
has a subgradient at
z
, then there exists
g
such that

f(x)\geq g^{\prime}(x-z)+f(z),

for all
x
.
In particular, if

z=\sum\lambda_{k}z_{k},

then, for all
k
,

f(z_{k})\geq g^{\prime}(z_{k}-z)+f(z).

Summing over
k

\sum\lambda_{k}f(z_{k})\geq\sum\lambda_{k}\left[g^{\prime}(z_{k}-z)+f(z)\right]=g^{\prime}\left(\sum\lambda_{k}z_{k}-z\right)+f(z)=f(z).

(ii)

Let
h
be the lower convex envelope of
f
.
Then by Proposition
9
, there exists a subgradient
h_{z}
for all
z\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(h))
, where
\mathrm{dom}(h)=\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
, such that

h(x)\geq h_{z}^{\prime}(x-z)+h(z)

for all
x
.
By definition of
h
and Corollary
2
, for all such
z
where
f
is convex,

f(x)\geq h(x)\geq h_{z}^{\prime}(x-z)+h(z)=h_{z}^{\prime}(x-z)+f(z)

for all
x
.
∎

3.3
Optimal direction

If
f
have a unique minimizer
x^{\ast}
, then, for all
x_{0}\neq x^{\ast}
and
\delta>0
, there exists
\lVert d\rVert\leq\delta
such that

\inf_{\alpha>0}f(x_{0}+\alpha d)=f(x^{\ast}),

namely
d=\delta d_{0}
with

d_{0}:=\frac{x^{\ast}-x_{0}}{\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\lVert}.

This choice is in fact optimal, a result that forms the theoretical basis for our algorithm.

Theorem 5
.

Let
f
be convex with a unique minimizer
x^{\ast}
.
For all
x_{0}\neq x^{\ast}
in
\mathrm{dom}(f)
and directions
\lVert d\rVert\leq\delta\leq\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert

f(x_{0}+d)\geq f(x_{0}+\delta d_{0}).

If
d\neq\delta d_{0}
, then this inequality is strict.

Proof.

Obvious for
\delta=0
.
Suppose
\delta>0
.
WLOG
x^{\ast}=0
, otherwise, as this property is shift invariant, consider

g(x)=f(x+x^{\ast}),

and
\lVert d\rVert=\delta
, by convexity the minimum over such
d
must occur on the boundary.

Let

f_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}f\left(\lVert x\rVert\frac{x_{0}}{\lVert x_{0}\rVert}\right)&\lVert x\rVert\leq\lVert x_{0}\rVert\\
\infty&\text{else}\end{cases},

which is convex by Lemma
6
.
Given two functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
let
h_{1}\sim h_{2}
if
h_{1},h_{2}
are convex,
h_{1},h_{2}\geq f(0)
and
h_{1}(x)=h_{2}(x)
for all
x=\alpha x_{0}
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, e.g.
f_{0}\sim f
.
The equivalence class
[f]
is partially ordered under the pointwise order, i.e.
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
if
h_{1}(x)\leq h_{2}(x)
for all
x
.
Moreover, if the pointwise limit of proper convex functions is proper, then it is convex.
Therefore, any chain in
[f]
has a lower bound, i.e. the infimum of a nonincreasing net of extended real numbers bounded below converges to a limit.
Thus, by Zorn’s lemma,
[f]
has a minimal element
h
.
By definition
h\leq f
is convex and, as
f_{0}\in[f]
, for all
\lVert y\rVert=\lVert x_{0}\rVert
,
h((1-\lambda)y)\leq h((1-\lambda)x_{0})
.

Let
\lambda=\delta/\lVert x_{0}\rVert
.
For any direction
d
there exists an orthogonal matrix
U
such that

d=-\delta U\frac{x_{0}}{\lVert x_{0}\rVert}=-\lambda Ux_{0}.

Then

(1-\lambda)x_{0}=(1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda(I-U)x_{0}+\lambda(U-I)x_{0}

and

\displaystyle h((1-\lambda)x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)h((1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda(I-U)x_{0})

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda h((1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda(I-U)x_{0}+(U-I)x_{0})

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)h(x_{0}-\lambda Ux_{0})+\lambda h((1-\lambda)Ux_{0})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)h(x_{0}-\lambda Ux_{0})+\lambda h((1-\lambda)x_{0}),

the first inequality uses the convex combination
(1-\lambda)0+\lambda(U-I)x_{0}
and the second is the definition of
h
.
Rearranging, by definition of
h
,

f((1-\lambda)x_{0})=h((1-\lambda)x_{0})\leq h(x_{0}-\lambda Ux_{0})\leq f(x_{0}-\lambda Ux_{0}).

Hence

f(x_{0}+\delta d_{0})=f((1-\lambda)x_{0})\leq f(x_{0}-\lambda Ux_{0})=f(x_{0}+d).

For the second statement, suppose there are two minimizers,
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
of norm
\delta
, with

f(x_{0}+d_{1})=f(x_{0}+d_{2}),

then, for all
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, by convexity,

x_{0}+\beta d_{1}+(1-\beta)d_{2}

is also a minimizer.
However, if
0<\beta<1
, this is an interior point of the ball of radius
\delta
around
x_{0}
, a contradiction.
∎

Along this optimal direction
f
is strictly decreasing.

Lemma 6
.

Let
f
be convex with a unique minimizer
x^{\ast}
,
x_{0}\neq x^{\ast}
in
\mathrm{dom}(f)
and

z_{\alpha}=x_{0}+\alpha(x^{\ast}-x_{0}).

For all
0\leq\alpha<\beta\leq 1
,

f(z_{\beta})<f(z_{\alpha}).

Proof.

z_{\beta}=z_{\alpha}+(\beta-\alpha)(x^{\ast}-x_{0})

and

x^{\ast}-z_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)(x^{\ast}-x_{0}).

Thus, letting
\gamma=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{1-\alpha}
with
0<\gamma\leq 1
,

z_{\beta}=\gamma x^{\ast}+(1-\gamma)z_{\alpha}.

Hence

f(z_{\beta})\leq\gamma f(x^{\ast})+(1-\gamma)f(z_{\alpha})<f(z_{\alpha}).

∎

4
Optimization

We consider the following optimization problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\inf f(x),

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is
K
-Lipschitz over
\mathrm{dom}(f)
with a unique minimizer and
\mathrm{dom}(f)
is compact.

Combing the results of the previous sections, the lower convex envelope
\breve{f}
has the same unique minimizer and any
x\in A_{f}\cap\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
has a subgradient
g_{x}
.

4.1
Algorithm

This section gives a formal statement of our algorithm.

Algorithm 7
.

(Directional descent)

Stage 1:

d^{\ast}=\arg\min_{\lVert d\rVert\leq\delta}f(x_{0}+d),

where
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
and either
x_{0}+\delta d_{0}\in A_{f}
or
\delta\geq\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert
.

Stage 2:

x_{m}=x_{0}+m\alpha\frac{d^{\ast}}{\delta},

for some step size
\alpha>0
.

If the minimizer happens to be in the
\delta
-ball of stage 1, i.e.
\delta\geq\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert
, then stage 2 is unnecessary.
If this is not the case, then, by Theorem
5
,
d^{\ast}=\delta d_{0}
.
While
x_{0}+\delta d_{0}\in A_{f}
is all that is required for this property to hold, it may be more convenient for the entire ball of radius
\delta
around
x_{0}
to be contained in
A_{f}
, providing a convex optimization.
The convergence guarantees of stage 2 follow directly from
f
being
K
-Lipschitz.
For any direction
d^{\ast}=\delta d
, such that
x_{m}
remains in the effective domain,

\displaystyle f(x_{m})-f(x^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq K\lVert x_{m}-x^{\ast}\rVert

\displaystyle=K\lVert m\alpha d-(x^{\ast}-x_{0})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq K\lVert m\alpha d_{0}-(x^{\ast}-x_{0})\rVert+Km\alpha\lVert d-d_{0}\rVert

\displaystyle=K\left\lvert m\alpha-\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert\right\rvert+Km\alpha\lVert d-d_{0}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq K\alpha+K\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert\lVert d-d_{0}\rVert\quad\left(m=\left\lfloor\frac{\lVert x^{\ast}-x_{0}\rVert}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\right).

This gives an
O(1/\varepsilon)
algorithm when
d
is appropriately close to
d_{0}
.
We note that, without some local strong convexity type of structural property, the function may be arbitrarily flat.

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

Let
C(x)=C(x,f)
.
Then
x\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
if and only if
C(x)
is nonempty, and
g(x)=\infty
when
C(x)
is empty.
Moreover, if
x\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
, then
(1,x)\in C(x)
and
g(x)\leq f(x)
.
If
h\leq f
is convex, then, for all
(\underline{\lambda},\underline{x})\in C(x)
,

h(x)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}h(x_{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}f(x_{k}).

Thus
h\leq g
and it suffices to show that
g
is convex.
To that end, let
z=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y
for some
x,y\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{dom}(f))
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
.
Then

C(z)\supset\{(\alpha\underline{\lambda}^{(1)},\underline{x}):(\underline{\lambda}^{(1)},\underline{x})\in C(x)\}\oplus\{((1-\alpha)\underline{\lambda}^{(2)},\underline{y}):(\underline{\lambda}^{(2)},\underline{y})\in C(y)\}.

Thus
g(z)
is upper bounded by

\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\alpha\lambda_{i}^{(1)}f(x_{i})\!+\!\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}(1\!-\!\alpha)\lambda_{j}^{(2)}f(y_{j}):(\underline{\lambda}^{(1)},\underline{x})\in C(x),(\underline{\lambda}^{(2)},\underline{y})\in C(y)\right\}.

Hence

g(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\leq\alpha g(x)+(1-\alpha)g(y).
