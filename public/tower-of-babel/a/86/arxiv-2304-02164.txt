Improved upper bounds on even-cycle creating Hamilton paths
John Byrne, Michael Tait

Abstract
We study the function H n ​ ( C 2 ​ k ) H_{n}(C_{2k}) , the maximum number of Hamilton paths such that the union of any pair of them contains C 2 ​ k C_{2k} as a subgraph. We give upper bounds on this quantity for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , improving results of Harcos and Soltész, and we show that if a conjecture of Ustimenko is true then one additionally obtains improved upper bounds for all k ≥ 6 k\geq 6 . We also give bounds on H n ​ ( K 2 , 3 ) H_{n}(K_{2,3}) and H n ​ ( K 2 , 4 ) H_{n}(K_{2,4}) . In order to prove our results, we extend a theorem of Krivelevich which counts Hamilton cycles in ( n , d , λ ) (n,d,\lambda) -graphs to bipartite or irregular graphs, and then apply these results to generalized polygons and the constructions of Lubotzky-Phillips-Sarnak and Füredi.

Introduction

For a graph
G
, we denote by
H_{n}(G)
the maximum number of Hamilton paths in
K_{n}
the union of any two of which contains
G
as a subgraph. The problem of studying
H_{n}(G)
for various graphs
G
is motivated by the fact that there is a natural correspondence between permutations of
[n]
and directed Hamilton paths in
K_{n}
, and there has been extensive study on finding sets of permutations whose pairs satisfy some property (see for example
[
2
]
or
[
9
]
). This allows problems involving permutations to be studied using graph-theoretic tools. An example of such a connection occurs in
[
6
]
. Cohen, Fachini, and Körner showed there that the maximum number of pairwise reversing permutations of
[n]
is at most
H_{2n}(C_{4})/2^{\lfloor(2n-2)/4\rfloor}
.

The study of
H_{n}(G)
was initiated in
[
18
]
where it was shown that the maximum number of Hamilton paths such that the union of any pair contains an odd cycle (of any length) is exactly
\binom{n}{\lfloor{n/2}\rfloor}
for odd
n
and
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{\lfloor{n/2}\rfloor}
for even
n
. They asked if the same result holds when replacing an odd cycle of arbitrary length by a triangle. This question was answered positively by Kovács and Soltész
[
21
]
, determining
H_{n}(C_{3})
precisely. The authors generalized their work to other odd cycles in
[
22
]
, giving bounds on
H_{n}(C_{2k+1})
. In particular they showed that
H_{n}(C_{2k+1})=2^{(1+o(1))n}
when
k
is a power of
2
, and they conjectured that the same result holds for all
k
.

In the same paper, they showed that the maximum number of Hamilton paths such that the union of any pair contains an even cycle (of any length) is
\Omega\left(n!/n^{2}\right)
. After this, Cohen, Fachini, and Körner
[
6
]
showed that the behavior changes substantially if one replaces any even cycle with
C_{4}
, showing that behavior of the problem is different for even and odd cycles. In particular, they showed that

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!\leq H_{n}(C_{4})\leq n^{\frac{3}{4}n+o(n)},

leaving a superexponential gap in the upper and lower bounds. This work was built on by Soltész
[
33
]
, and finally in
[
15
]
Harcos and Soltész gave the current best-known upper bounds for
H_{n}(C_{2k})
, including determining the asymptotics of
\log H_{n}(C_{4})
.

Theorem 1

(Harcos and Soltész
[
15
]
)
.

We have

n^{\frac{1}{2}n-\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{\log n}-O(1)}\leq H_{n}(C_{4})\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}n+o\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)}.

Moreover, for
k>2
we have

n^{\frac{1}{k}n-o(n)}\leq H_{n}(C_{2k})\leq n^{\left(1-\frac{1}{3k}\right)+o(n)}.

In this paper we will improve the upper bounds in
Theorem 1
and also consider the cases
G=K_{2,3}
and
G=K_{2,4}
. Our main results are the following.

Theorem 2
.

We have

(a)

H_{n}(C_{6})\leq n^{\frac{2}{3}n+o(n)}

(b)

H_{n}(C_{8})\leq n^{\frac{4}{5}n+o(n)}

(c)

For every

H_{n}(C_{2k})\leq n^{\left(1-\frac{2}{3k}\right)n+o(n)}.

We have

H_{n}(K_{2,3})\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}n}2^{-\frac{n}{2}+o(n)}

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with average degree
d
, with minimum and maximum degrees
\delta
and
\Delta
, respectively, all of whose nontrivial eigenvalues are at most
\lambda
in absolute value. If the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\Delta-\delta\leq R\lambda
for some constant
R>0
;

(2)

\frac{d}{\lambda}\gg\log^{2}n
;

(3)

\log d\cdot\log\frac{d}{\lambda}\gg\log n
,

Then the number of Hamilton cycles in
G
is
n!\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}
.

We make some remarks on the conditions (1)-(3). First, it will sometimes be convenient for us to assume that
\lambda=o(d)
and condition (2) guarantees this. With this in mind, condition (1) then guarantees that we have
\frac{\Delta}{\delta}=1+o(1)
. Next, if one uses
\lambda=\max\{\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{n}\}
, then
using condition (1) may be undesirable when the graph is highly pseudo-random and
\lambda
is small. Therefore we note that the condition can be replaced as follows with a condition that only references the degree parameters. Since
\Tr(A^k)
is the number of closed walks of length
k
in
A
which start and end at the same vertex, we have
\Tr(A^2)\geq n\delta
. On the other hand, any eigenvalue of
G
is at most
\Delta

\Tr(A^2)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\Delta^{2}+(n-1)\lambda^{2}.

It follows that
\lambda^{2}\geq\frac{n\delta-\Delta^{2}}{n-1}
. If
\Delta=o(n)
and
\frac{\Delta}{\delta}\to 1
, then the last quantity is
\Delta(1-o(1))
, implying
\lambda=\Omega(\sqrt{\Delta})
. Therefore, the conditions
\Delta=o(n)
and
\Delta-\delta=O(\sqrt{\Delta})
jointly imply (1). This will be strong enough for our purposes, however we make a final remark that if one needed to have a larger difference between maximum and minimum degree, then one could possibly choose
\lambda
to be much larger than
\max\{\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{n}\}
. Conditions (2) and (3) are quite mild, and in the frequently seen case that
d=n^{a}
and
\max\{\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{n}\}<d^{1-b}
, one may choose for example to take
\lambda
to be any power of
d
sufficiently close to
1
, which gives much more freedom for the degree sequence of
G
. One final remark is that the exponent
2
in condition (2) is slightly weaker than the
1+\epsilon
found in
[
23
]
. Because our graphs are not regular it is not as straightforward to obtain a lower bound on the permanent of our adjacency matrix, and this is where we use the stronger condition. We do not know if the
2
can be replaced with
1+\epsilon
.

We remark that
Theorem 6
implies that the Hamilton cycles in a graph satisfying (1)-(3) are ‘evenly distributed’ in the following sense. If (say) a bounded number of edges are deleted from each vertex, then using the Courant-Weyl inequalities one can show that (1)-(3) are still satisfied so the asymptotic formula
n!(d/n)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}
still holds. The allowance in

that the graph be irregular is required for this argument to work, even if the starting graph is regular. We suspect that quantitative results of this type could be proved given more a more careful argument.

3
Hamilton cycles

We now prove
Theorem 6
and
Theorem 7
. Our argument follows
[
23
]
, but we must take into account the fact that the graph is irregular or bipartite. For the upper and lower bounds, we utilize the connection between the permanent of
A
and
2
-factors in the graph. The upper bound follows quickly using
6
. A high-level outline of the proof of the lower bound is as follows:

•

Using
7
and the connection between the permanent of
A
and
2
-factors in the graph, we have a lower bound on a weighted count of the
2
-factors in
G
.

•

We show that the number of
2
-factors with “many” distinct cycles is negligible compared to the total.

•

For each
2
-factor with “few” distinct cycles,
4
or
5
is used to convert it to a Hamilton cycle with few rotations.

•

For any given Hamilton cycle, the number of ways that a
2
-factor can be converted to it by few rotations is bounded above.

•

Combining all of the bounds, we obtain a lower bound on the number of distinct Hamilton cycles in the graph.

We now proceed with the details.

3.1
Irregular case

First note that
per(A)
counts the number of oriented 2-factors of
G
(where an orientation is applied to each cycle), so
per(A)=\sum 2^{c(F)}
with the sum taken over all
2-
factors
F
. To get an upper bound on the number of Hamilton cycles, we apply
6
to the matrix
A
:

\displaystyle h(G)

\displaystyle\leq per(A)\leq(\lceil d\rceil!)^{\frac{n}{\left\lfloor d\right\rfloor}}\leq\left(\lceil d\rceil(\lceil d\rceil-1)\left(\frac{\lceil d\rceil-1}{e}\right)^{\lceil d\rceil-1}\right)^{\frac{n}{d-1}}\leq\left((d+1)^{2}\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^{d}\right)^{\frac{n}{d-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^{n}\left[\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^{\frac{n}{d-1}}(d+1)^{\frac{2n}{d-1}}\right]=\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}.

We now focus on the lower bound. Applying equation (
2
), we have

\left(\frac{\Delta}{e}\right)^{n}e^{-\frac{20n}{\log^{2}\Delta}}\leq per(A)=\sum_{s=1}^{n/2}f(G,s)2^{s}.

(3)

Moving to the second step in the proof, we show that the
2
-factors with too many cycles contribute negligibly to the sum. Set
s^{*}:=20n/\log^{2}\Delta
. Let
s>s^{*}
. Define
s_{1}:=4s/\log\Delta
. If a 2-factor
F
has
s
cycles then the
s_{1}
shortest cycles have total length
t\leq 4n/\log\Delta
. Fix
t\leq\frac{4n}{\log\Delta}
, and let
(k_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,k_{\ell}^{(m_{\ell})})
be a multiset representing the
s_{1}
shortest cycle lengths, listed in increasing order. Thus
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{\ell}=s_{1}
and
k_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+k_{\ell}m_{\ell}=t
. We want to estimate the number
N\left(k_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,k_{\ell}^{(m_{\ell})}\right)
of
2
-factors whose
s_{1}
shortest cycles give this multiset. To count this, first choose a vector of disjoint sets of vertices
(W_{1}\ldots,W_{\ell})
with
|W_{i}|=m_{i}
. Then for each

, for each

, choose a path of length
k_{i}-1
starting at
v
, and close the path to a cycle if possible. Finally cover the remaining
n-t
vertices with a 2-factor. Each 2-factor with
s_{1}
shortest cycle lengths
(k_{1}^{(m_{1})},\cdots,k_{\ell}^{(m_{\ell})})
occurs at least
k_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots k_{\ell}^{m_{\ell}}
times. On the other hand, there are at most
{n\choose m_{1}}\cdots{n\choose m_{\ell}}\Delta^{m_{1}(k_{1}-1)}\cdots\Delta^{m_{\ell}(k_{\ell}-1)}\phi(G,n-t)

\displaystyle N\left(k_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,k_{\ell}^{(m_{\ell})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{n\choose m_{1}}\cdots{n\choose m_{\ell}}}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots k_{\ell}^{m_{\ell}}}\cdot\Delta^{t-s_{1}}\cdot\phi(G,n-t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(en)^{s_{1}}}{(k_{1}m_{1})^{m_{1}}\cdots(k_{\ell}m_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}}}\cdot\Delta^{t-s_{1}}\cdot\phi(G,n-t).

We want to estimate the denominator above. Along the lines of Lemma 11.4 in
[
29
]
, we use the fact that the function
x\log x
on
(0,\infty)
is convex. Let
\gamma=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{k_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{k_{\ell}}}
, and note that since
\frac{1}{k_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{k_{\ell}}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\cdots+\frac{1}{s_{1}}\leq\log(s_1)
, we have
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{\log(s_1)}
. Now we have

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{\gamma}{k_{i}}m_{i}k_{i}\right)\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{\gamma}{k_{i}}m_{i}k_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{\gamma}{k_{i}}m_{i}k_{i}\log(m_ik_i).

Simplifying gives

s_{1}\log(\gamma s_1)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\log\left((m_{i}k_{i})^{m_{i}})\right),

and finally using the lower bound on

, we have

\left(\frac{s_{1}}{\log s_{1}}\right)^{s_{1}}\leq(\gamma s_{1})^{s_{1}}\leq(m_{1}k_{1})^{m_{1}}\cdots(m_{\ell}k_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}}.

This implies that

N\left(k_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,k_{\ell}^{(m_{\ell})}\right)\leq\left(\frac{en\log s_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)^{s_{1}}\cdot\Delta^{t-s_{1}}\cdot\phi(G,n-t).

(4)

Now we estimate the term

of equation (
3

(5)

h(G)\geq\left(\frac{\Delta}{e}\right)^{n}\cdot\frac{1-o(1)}{2^{s^{*}}{n\choose k}\Delta^{2k}}\cdot e^{-o(n)}.

Now since

k=O\left(s^{*}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\frac{d}{\overline{\lambda}}}\right)=O\left(\frac{n\log n}{\log^{2}\Delta\log(d/\ov\lambda)}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{n}{\log\Delta}\right),

for some constant
C
we have

{n\choose k}\leq{n\choose Cn/\log\Delta}\leq\left(\frac{en}{Cn/\log\Delta}\right)^{\frac{Cn}{\log\Delta}}\leq e^{\frac{Cn\log\log\Delta}{\log\Delta}}=e^{o(n)}

and by (3
′
),

\Delta^{2k}\leq e^{O\left(\frac{n}{\log\Delta}\frac{20\log n}{\log\frac{d}{\overline{\lambda}}}\right)}=e^{o(n)}.

Therefore

h(G)\geq\left(\frac{\Delta}{e}\right)^{n}e^{-o(n)}\geq n!\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)^{n}(1-o(1))^{n}.

6
Concluding remarks

In
Theorem 2
and
Theorem 3
we improved the best known upper bounds on

for every

. Here we discuss a possible approach to obtaining a further improvement for every
k\geq 6
. In
Theorem 3
we use the graphs of Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak because they are dense graphs with high girth, but a denser construction is known: these are the graphs
CD(k,q)
of Lazebnik, Ustimenko, and Woldar
[
26
]

there exists a graph

which is
q-
regular and bipartite with
2q^{k-\lfloor\frac{k+2}{4}\rfloor+1}
vertices and with girth at least
k+5
, if
k
is odd. The additional improvement on
H_{n}(C_{k+5})
would follow from the following open conjecture of Ustimenko (for background see
[
32
]
).

Conjecture 1

(Ustimenko)
.

For all
(k,q)
, the nontrivial eigenvalues of
D(k,q)
(and therefore
CD(k,q)
) are at most
2\sqrt{q}
.

Currently this conjecture is known for
k=2
and
k=3
(Li-Lu-Wang
[
28
]
),
k=4
(Moorhouse-Sun-Williford
[
32
]
), and
k=5
(Gupta-Taranchuk
[
13
]
). Moreover
CD(6,q)
is isomorphic to
D(5,q)
and so the bound also holds for
CD(6,q)
.

For any
k
for which
1
holds,
Theorem 7
gives
h(CD(k,q))\geq n^{\frac{1}{k-\lfloor(k+2)/4\rfloor+1}n-o(n)}
and
1
gives
H_{n}(C_{2k})\leq n^{\left(1-\frac{1}{2k-2-\lfloor(2k)/4\rfloor+1}\right)n+o(n)}
which improves the exponent in
Theorem 3
. In light of the known cases of the conjecture mentioned above, the graphs

obtain this bound for

when

. This matches the best known bounds for

and

, but good bounds on the eigenvalues of

would have to be proved for some

is considerably weaker than the conjecture

, a weaker bound such as

Regarding the gap between the lower and upper bounds for

We now consider some possible improvements of our results counting Hamilton cycles, focusing on
Theorem 6
. A common problem in graph theory is to obtain the weakest possible assumptions on a graph that guarantee the existence of a Hamilton cycle. A longstanding conjecture from
[
25
]
is that there is a constant
C
such that any
(n,d,\lambda)-
graph with
d/\lambda>C
is Hamiltonian. Recently, Glock, Correia, and Sudakov in
[
12
]
proved that the conjecture holds for graphs where
d
is polynomial in
n
, and that for general
(n,d,\lambda)-
graphs the condition
d/\lambda\geq C(\log n)^{1/3}
guarantees Hamiltonicity. In
[
12
]
, Lemma
4
(Lemma 3.1 of
[
23
]
) is improved, and it would be interesting to see if combining the methods in this paper with
[
12
]
could give any improvements. When we care about the number of Hamilton cycles, there are several ways one could formulate an analogous question. Perhaps most naturally, we would ask how much we can weaken conditions (1)-(3) such that the formula
h(G)=n!(d/n)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}
still holds, since this represents similar behavior to the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,d/n)
. We suspect that it is possible to decrease the right-hand side of (2) to
(\log n)^{1+\varepsilon}
for an arbitrary constant
\varepsilon>0
, which is the corresponding condition appearing in
[
23
]
. The only place we needed the increased exponent 2 is in equation (
2
) (and there we could have instead used
d/\lambda\gg(\log\Delta)^{2}
).

Another natural question is whether the condition (1) on the degrees in the graph can be relaxed, perhaps to a condition of the form
\Delta/\delta\leq 1+\varepsilon
for some positive
\varepsilon>0
, such that the conclusion
h(G)=n!(d/n)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}
still holds. We believe that the techniques in this paper still can be used to prove nontrivial bounds on
h(G)
under weaker assumptions about the degrees, perhaps using stronger pseudorandomness conditions. However, the resulting lower bound may be less than
n!(d/n)^{n}(1+o(1))^{n}
, and when the degrees may differ by a constant factor, it is unclear what the desired formula for
h(G)
should be. We note some places in our proof where we have used bounds on the degrees:

•

in
2
, to bound the degree variance;

•

in
4
;

•

in the bounds on
g(n/2,z)
in
1
;

•

in equation (
2
);

•

in estimating the average degree of
G[V-V_{0}]
in
section 3
.

For irregular graphs one often considers the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian
L
rather than adjacency matrix. In
[
5

, Butler and Chung built on
[
25

and gave a mild condition on the spectral gap of

[
