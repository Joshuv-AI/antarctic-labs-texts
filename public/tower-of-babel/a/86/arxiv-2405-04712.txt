Tube Formulae for Generalized von Koch Fractals through Scaling Functional Equations
Will Hoffer

Abstract
In this work, we provide a treatment of scaling functional equations in a general setting involving fractals arising from sufficiently nice self-similar systems in order to analyze the tube functions, tube zeta functions, and complex dimensions of relative fractal drums. Namely, we express the volume of a tubular neighborhood in terms of scaled copies of itself and a remainder term and then solve this expression by means of the tube zeta functions. We then apply our methods to analyze these generalized von Koch fractals, which are a class of fractals that allow for different regular polygons and scaling ratios to be used in the construction of the standard von Koch curve and snowflake. In particular, we describe the volume of an inner tubular neighborhoods and the possible complex dimensions of such fractal snowflakes.



1
Introduction

In this work, we establish tube formulae for a class of fractals called generalized von Koch snowflakes (see Definition
2.1
) such as those depicted in Figure
1
. We construct and analyze scaling functional equations in a general setting where a set is partitioned into finitely many scaled copies and a remainder term from the leftover region. Using the theory of tube zeta functions and complex fractal dimensions, we deduce the leading asymptotics of the tube function for such sets based on the order of this remainder term.

Then, as an application of our main result, we deduce explicit tube formulae for the generalized von Koch fractals studied herein. In doing so, we extend the results of Lapidus and Pearse on computing a tube formula for the standard von Koch snowflake in
[
LP06
]
. Additionally, our analysis of scaling functional equations with remainder terms can be applied to many other types of fractals whose tubular neighborhoods are not completely self-similar.

Our work is organized as follows. We begin with a background regarding the origins of these fractals, previous work that we build upon, and then a summary of our main results in Section

4
) and then specializing to tube and zeta functions in Section

1.2
Main Results

Our main results consist of establishing scaling functional equations and analyzing their solutions first in a general setting (Section
4
) and then specifically with relative tube and zeta functions (Section
5
). We conclude by applying our work to generalized von Koch fractals (introduced in Section
2
and analyzed in Section
6

Firstly, Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
4.3
represent our treatment of scaling functional equations of the form

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f(x/\lambda_{i})+R(x),

where we include a remainder term

. Additionally, our treatment of the solution is through multiplicative means, using restricted Mellin transforms, rather than converting to an additive variable and using renewal theory directly. Letting

denote the restricted Mellin transform (c.f. Definition

\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}}(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)),

where
E(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{i}}[f](s)
is an entire function and where
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)
is holomorphic in the right half plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
when

. The function

may be recovered by Mellin inversion and computation (or estimation) of the integrals appearing

; see Theorem
4.3
and the discussion after its proof.

Secondly, in the context of fractal geometry we describe relative fractal drums

that arise from self-similar iterated function systems

“osculating”

V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda_{i})+V_{X,R}(\varepsilon),

where

and where the set

is the multiplicity of each scaling ratio. We then deduce Theorem

that the tube zeta function

(see Definition

) takes the form

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s)=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}}\cdot h(s),

where

is holomorphic in the right half plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
(compare with Equation
5.4
.) Consequently, the complex dimensions of

with real part strictly larger than
\sigma_{0}
are exactly the solutions

1=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\omega}

where also

; see Theorem
5.3
and Corollary
5.3
.

Furthermore, we show that the

V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}^{[k]}(t)=\sum_{\mathclap{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{0})}}\Res\Bigg(\frac{t^{2-s+k}}{(3-s)_{k}}\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+O(t^{2-\alpha+k}),

(s)_{k}:=\Gamma(s+k)/\Gamma(s)=s(s+1)(s+2)\cdots(s+k-1)

Of note, in the case of generalized von Koch fractals we see the appearance of the lattice-non lattice dichotomy: the geometry of the fractal is fundamentally different depending on the arithmetic properties of the scaling ratios. In studying this problem through the lens of fractal zeta functions, we are able to understand this behavior by way of the structure of the complex dimensions of the fractal. See for example Figure
9
, where the standard von Koch snowflake is arithmetic while the “squareflake” and “pentaflake” fractals depicted are non-arithmetic. For the former, the tube function will have an oscillatory leading term, but for the other two the tube formula will have a monotone leading order term and oscillatory effects at lower orders.

2
Generalized von Koch Fractals

What we shall dub generalized von Koch fractals (abbr. GKFs) are fractals in which regular polygons other than triangles are used in the construction of the snowflake and/or those in which the scaling ratio chosen is a factor other than one third. This provides a two-parameter family of fractal curves, where
n\geq 3
is the number of sides of the regular polygon and
0<r<1
is the scaling ratio for the middle segments.

2.1
Generalized von Koch Fractal Curves

To define an
(n,r)
-von Koch curve, we shall employ the notion of an iterated function system, which were introduced by Hutchinson in
[
Hut81
]
. Hutchinson in particular proved that iterated function systems induce a contraction on the space of nonempty, compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
equipped with the Hausdorff metric, whence by the Picard-Banach fixed point theorem there is a unique fixed point of the system (which is nonempty and compact.) This fixed point, or equivalently the attractor of the system, is a convenient way to define many fractals
[
Fal90
,
Bar88
]
.

We shall actually use a slightly more restrictive type of iterated function system, namely a self-similar system, whose definition we provide below.

A self-similar system
\Phi
on a complete metric space
(X,d)
is a finite set of contractive similitudes:

\Phi:=\{\phi_{k}:X\to X\}_{k=1}^{m},

where for each
k=1,...,m
and for every
x,y\in X
,

d(\phi_{k}(x),\phi_{k}(y))=r_{k}d(x,y),

where
r_{k}\in(0,1)
is the scaling ratio of
\phi_{k}
.

Note that an equivalent phrasing of the above statement is that a self-similar system is an iterated function system where each contraction mapping is, a fortiori, a similitude.

Similitudes in Euclidean spaces are compositions of translations, rotations, reflections, and homotheties. In order to explicitly write self-similar systems in what follows, we introduce the following translation, rotation, and homothety/scaling transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\displaystyle T_{(a,b)}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=(x+a,y+b),

\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{2};

(2.1)

\displaystyle R_{\theta}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=(x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta),

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R};

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=(\lambda x,\lambda y),

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Note that the scaling ratio of
S_{\lambda}
is
\lambda
, and that translations and rotations are isometries, viz. their scaling ratios are equal to one.

Lastly, we shall also need some angles related to a regular
n
-gon. Let us define
\theta_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{n}
to be the exterior angle (or equivalently the central angle) and

to be the interior angle of a regular

A set

3
Tubular Neighborhoods and Zeta Functions

Tube zeta functions were introduced in
[
LRŽ17
]
to study fractals in higher dimensions and to extend the theory of complex dimensions (see Definition
3.2
) to such fractals. These tube zeta functions are constructed from tube functions, which are the volumes of tubular neighborhoods of the given set. The tube zeta functions are essentially restricted Mellin transforms of this volume function, and thus, they possess important scaling properties (c.f. Lemma
3.2
.)

3.1
Tube Functions and their Properties

Let
N
denote a given Euclidean dimension. Given a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
X_{\varepsilon}
denote an epsilon neighborhood of
X
, that is,

X_{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\exists x\in X,\,|y-x|<\varepsilon\}.

Note that even if
X
itself is not Lebesgue measurable,
X_{\varepsilon}
is necessarily measurable as it is open. For example, it may be written as a union of open sets
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
for
x\in X
, where
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
is an open ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
x
.

In what follows, we will be considering these tubular neighborhoods relative to some other set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. To that end, we recall the notion of a relative fractal drum
[
LRŽ17
]
.

Let
X,\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and suppose further that
\Omega
is open, has finite Lebesgue measure, and has the property that
\exists\delta>0
such that
\Omega_{\delta}\supset X
. Then the pair
(X,\Omega)
is called a relative fractal drum, or RFD for short.

The tube function of an RFD
(X,\Omega)
shall be the volume of the tubular neighborhood of
X
contained within the set
\Omega
. Notably, such tube functions may be defined for any subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
so long as the set
\Omega
relative to which the volume is computed is Lebesgue measurable.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a Lebesgue measurable set, and denote by
m
the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The tube function
V_{X,\Omega}
of
X
relative to
\Omega
is the function
V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon):=m(X_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega)
, defined for
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

When the set
\Omega
is clear from context or is all of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we may suppress it in the notation. Any such function
V_{X,\Omega}
is continuous and non-decreasing on its domain, and it is finite when
X
or
\Omega
is a bounded set.

Note that the Lebesgue measure
m
has the scaling property that, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
m(\lambda X)=\lambda^{N}m(X)
. When a tubular neighborhood
X_{\varepsilon}
is scaled, the parameter
\varepsilon
defining the new scaled neighborhood will scale linearly. In other words,
\lambda\cdot(X_{\varepsilon})=(\lambda X)_{\lambda\varepsilon}
. Combining these properties, we may deduce how tube functions change under scaling. In the simplest case, where
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{N}
and we omit it, we have that
V_{\lambda X}(\lambda\varepsilon)=\lambda^{N}V_{X}(\varepsilon)
. We state and prove the following property for relative tube functions.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be measurable. For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\lambda>0
, we have that

V_{\lambda X,\lambda\Omega}(\varepsilon)=\lambda^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda).

More generally, if
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a similitude with scaling ratio
\lambda
, then we have the analogous identity:

V_{\phi(X),\phi(\Omega)}(\varepsilon)=\lambda^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda).

Proof.

Let
\phi=\widetilde{\psi}\circ\phi_{\lambda}\circ\psi^{\prime}
, where
\psi^{\prime},\widetilde{\psi}
are compositions of rotations, reflections, and translations and where
\phi_{\lambda}
is a scaling transformation, viz.
\phi_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda x
. Any similitude of scaling ratio
\lambda
, by definition, may be written this way. Note that the first identity is a special case of the second property; merely set
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi^{\prime}=\text{Id}
. For convenience, let us denote
U^{\prime}=\psi^{\prime}(U)
in what follows, and note that
\lambda U=\phi_{\lambda}(U)
.

The maps in question have lots of nice properties. First, we note that
\psi^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{\psi}
are isometries, which is to say that
m(U^{\prime})=m(U)
and
m(\tilde{\psi}(U))=m(U)
for any
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since
\widetilde{\psi},\psi^{\prime},\phi_{\lambda},
and their composition
\phi
are injective, they each have the property that the intersection of the images of two sets is the image of the intersection of those sets.
The same property is true (unconditionally) for the union of images being the image of the union.

This lattermost fact is one way to see that
\widetilde{\psi}(X_{\varepsilon})=(\widetilde{\psi}(X))_{\varepsilon}
. One may write the neighborhood
X_{\varepsilon}
as a union of
\varepsilon
-balls, and then apply the preservation of unions under images to see that this set is exactly the neighborhood of the transformed set
\widetilde{\psi}(X)
.

Using this fact and the above properties, we find that the tube functions are unaffected by an injective isometry such as
\widetilde{\psi}
:

\displaystyle V_{\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda X^{\prime}),\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda\Omega^{\prime})}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=m(\widetilde{\psi}((\lambda X^{\prime})_{\varepsilon})\cap\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda\Omega^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=m(\widetilde{\psi}[(\lambda X^{\prime})_{\varepsilon}\cap\lambda\Omega^{\prime}])=V_{\lambda X^{\prime},\lambda\Omega^{\prime}}(\varepsilon).

As mentioned before, we have that
\phi_{\lambda}(X_{\varepsilon})=(\lambda X)_{\lambda\varepsilon}
. Writing
\varepsilon=\lambda t
, we obtain that:

\displaystyle V_{\lambda X^{\prime},\lambda\Omega^{\prime}}(\lambda t)=m(\phi_{\lambda}(X^{\prime}_{t}\cap\Omega^{\prime}))=\lambda^{N}V_{X^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime}}(t),

where the last step is the scaling property of the Lebesgue measure. We have already shown that for an injective isometry like
\psi^{\prime}
,
V_{\psi^{\prime}(X),\psi^{\prime}(\Omega)}=V_{X,\Omega}
. Thus, the result follows by writing
t=\varepsilon/\lambda
and combining these equalities.
∎

3.2
Tube Zeta Functions and Complex Dimensions

Tube zeta functions were introduced in
[
LRŽ17
]
to study fractals in higher dimensions. They are defined as a restricted Mellin transform of the tube function of the corresponding set, introducing a cutoff value of
\delta>0
to the upper bound of the usual Mellin transform.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum and let
\delta>0
. The relative tube zeta function
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
of
X
relative to
\Omega
is given by

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta):=\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{s-N-1}V_{X,\Omega}(t)\,dt,

for
s\in\mathbb{C}
with sufficiently large real part.

As with the tube functions, when the set
\Omega
is clear from context, we shall sometimes write
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X}=\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
and omit the relative set. We shall also denote by
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
the maximal meromorphic extension of the holomorphic function defined by the integral.

Next, we observe that for any two
\delta_{2}>\delta_{1}>0
, we have that

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta_{2})-\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta_{1})=\int_{\delta_{1}}^{\delta_{2}}t^{s-N-1}V_{X,\Omega}(t)\,dt.

(3.1)

Of note, this difference is a holomorphic function which possesses an entire analytic continuation
[
LRŽ17
]
. As such, the poles of a tube zeta function are not dependent on the choice of
\delta
.

These poles of a tube zeta function are of importance to the geometry of the fractal. They are called complex (fractal) dimensions of the set
X
. Typically, one specifies a subset of the complex plane, or a “window,” which is usually the domain of meromorphicity of the function in
\mathbb{C}
or a half-plane contained therein.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum and let
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
be the relative tube zeta function of
X
.

If

, then the complex dimensions of

relative to
\Omega
contained in the window

, are the poles of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
contained within
W
.

The complex dimensions control the exponents and the form of the tube formula for the set
[
LvF13
,
LRŽ17
]
. See especially chapter five in
[
LRŽ17
]
.

Tube zeta functions are well suited to studying self-similar or nearly self-similar objects due to their scaling properties. These follow from the nature of the zeta function as a (restricted) Mellin transform on the space of positive scaling factors. The scaling relation is complicated only slightly by the truncation of the integral, as the cutoff value will change with the scaling.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let

. Then for all

in its domain, the tube zeta function of

relative to

satisfies the following:

\widetilde{\zeta}_{\lambda X,\lambda\Omega}(s;\delta)=\lambda^{s}\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta/\lambda).

Moreover, if

\widetilde{\zeta}_{\phi(X),\phi(\Omega)}(s;\delta)=\lambda^{s}\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta/\lambda).

We note that this result is essentially just Proposition 4.6.11 in
[
LRŽ17
]

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.1
and a change of variables, we compute that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\zeta}_{\phi(X),\phi(\Omega)}(s;\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{s-N}\lambda^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(t/\lambda)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta/\lambda}(\lambda t)^{s-N}\lambda^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}=\lambda^{s}\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta/\lambda).

∎

Now, from Lemma

\widetilde{\zeta}_{\lambda X,\lambda\Omega}(s;\delta)=\lambda^{s}\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta)+\lambda^{s}\int_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda}t^{s-N-1}V_{X,\Omega}(t)\,dt.

(3.2)

We shall introduce notation for this “partial” tube zeta function, as it encapsulates the effects the truncation on the scaling property of the tube zeta function.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

its relative tube function, and

for all

with sufficiently large real part.

These partial tube zeta functions shall appear in scaling functional equations such as Equation

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\xi}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta_{1},\delta_{2})|\leq\begin{cases}V_{X,\Omega}(\delta_{2})\cfrac{\delta_{2}^{\sigma-N}-\delta_{1}^{\sigma-N}}{\sigma-N},&\sigma\neq N,\\

Note that these bounds are enough to deduce that

extends to an entire function in the complex variable

on the parameter

Let

4
General Scaling Functional Equations

In this section, we study scaling functional equations in a general setting. In particular, we show how they may be solved by means of (truncated) Mellin transforms. The approach is similar in spirit to solving functional equations by means of renewal theory, except in this case we use the Mellin transform which is natural for studying for the positive real line as a group with multiplication. We shall illustrate the ideas up through the use of the transform to create nicely solvable functional equations into the Mellin inversion theorem. Notably, we use a truncation of the Mellin transform as opposed to the standard Mellin transform. This is primarily because in the application to tube functions in Section
5
, these are preferred.

The positive real line
\mathbb{R}^{+}
(as a group with multiplication) may be thought of as the space of scaling factors, and its associated Haar measure
\frac{dt}{t}
is invariant with respect to scaling transformations. We shall first treat scaling functional equations of functions on this space, and later specialize to tube and zeta functions in the next section.

4.1
Truncated Mellin Transforms

Let
C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
denote the space of continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
. The standard Mellin transform of
f
is the integral
\mathcal{M}[f](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}f(x)\,dx.
A truncated or restricted Mellin transform is simply an integral of the same integrand but over an interval which is a subset of
(0,\infty)
.

Let
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
and fix
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
. The truncated Mellin transform of
f
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
, is given by

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s):=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}t^{s}f(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which the integral is convergent. If
\alpha=0
, we write
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}
for the truncated transform and if additionally
\beta=\infty
then
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\infty}
is the standard Mellin transform.

Note that we may equivalently define
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
to be the Mellin transform of
f
times the characteristic function of the interval
(\alpha,\beta)
, viz.

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]=\mathcal{M}[f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}].

This allows us to compare the convergence of the two directly, and it shows that the truncated transform inherits the properties of its counterpart, most notably linearity.

First in our discussion of convergence of such transforms, we note an immediate corollary of the alternate definition is that a restricted transform converges automatically if the Mellin transform converges. It is known (see for example Chapter 6 in
[
Gra10
]
) that
\mathcal{M}[f](s)
is holomorphic in the vertical strip
\sigma_{-}<\Re(s)<\sigma_{+}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{-}:=

\displaystyle\inf\{\sigma:f(x)=O(x^{-\sigma},x\to 0^{+})\}

\displaystyle\sigma_{+}:=

\displaystyle\sup\{\sigma:f(x)=O(x^{-\sigma},x\to\infty)\}.

When
\alpha<\beta<\infty
,
f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}\equiv 0
as
x\to\infty
, whence
\sigma_{+}=\infty
. Similarly, if
0<\alpha<\beta
then
f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}\equiv 0
as
x\to 0^{+}
, whence
\sigma_{-}=-\infty
. So, if
0<\alpha<\beta<\infty
, the restricted transform is automatically entire, i.e. holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}
. If
0=\alpha<\beta<\infty
and
f=O(x^{\sigma_{0}})
, then
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f](s)
is holomorphic in the half plane
\mathbb{H}_{-\sigma_{0}}
, i.e. when
\Re(s)>-\sigma_{0}
.

Notably, the restricted transform may converge even if the full Mellin transform integral diverges. Perhaps the most important class of functions for which this occurs is that of polynomials: if
f(t)=t^{k}
and
\Re(s)>-k
,
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f](s)
converges whilst
\mathcal{M}[f](s)
is divergent for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus, for a general polynomial
p(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}t^{k}
, we have that

\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[p](s)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{s+k}\beta^{s+k},\quad\Re(s)>\max\{-k:a_{k}\neq 0\}.

Negative powers become admissable if the lower bound
\alpha
of the truncation is positive.

4.2

Next, we define scaling operators which act on

. To borrow terminology from statistical mechanics, we shall define pure and mixed scaling operators. A pure scaling operator

, where

M_{\lambda}[f](x):=f(x/\lambda),

where this convention of inverting the scaling factor shall be convenient for our applications. A mixed scaling operator shall be a linear combination of such scaling operators. If we have that

is a (mixed) scaling operator, then it acts by:

L[f](x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f(x/\lambda_{i}).

We shall not require the combination to be convex; later, the only constraint we will add is that the multiplicities be positive and integral. For now, each

.

L
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=L[f](x)+R(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f(x/\lambda_{i})+R(x).

(4.2)

The scaling functional equation is exact if
R\equiv 0
and approximate if not. We generally assume that
R
cannot be written as the image of
f
under some scaling operator
T
(whence the equation is exact with respect to the operator
L+T
,) but do not require it since it may be unknown except perhaps for estimates thereupon.

We now analyze the solutions to scaling functional equations using (truncated) Mellin transforms. The first step is to transform the functional equation into an equation for the truncated Mellin transform.

Let
f,R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and let
L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}M_{\lambda_{i}}
be a scaling operator such that the scaling ratios
\lambda_{i}
are each in
(0,1)
and with positive integer multiplicities
a_{i}
. Suppose also that either
m\geq 2
or that
a_{1}\geq 2
. Additionally, assume that
R(x)=O(x^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
x\to 0^{+}
for some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
f
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f=L[f]+R
, then its truncated Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f]
satisfies the equation:

\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)),

for any
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{L}\cap\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. Here,
E
is the entire function explicitly given by:

E(s):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{i}}[f](s),

and
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
is the half plane
\Re(s)>\sigma_{0}
.

Moreover, if
D
is the unique positive solution to Equation
4.1
, then the function
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f]
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\max(D,\sigma_{0})}
. It is meromorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
, with poles contained within a subset of
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})
.

Proof.

We begin by applying
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}
to the scaling functional equation

f=L[f]+R.

This and a change of variables yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{s}f(t/\lambda_{i})\,\frac{dt}{t}+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}\int_{0}^{\delta/\lambda_{i}}t^{s}f(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s).

By the linearity of integration, we may split the integrals to write that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s).

Provided that
s\notin\mathcal{D}_{L}
, it follows that
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s))
.

We have that the function
E
is entire since it is a linear combination of entire functions
\lambda_{i}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{i}}[f]
and that
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. By Proposition
4.2
,
\zeta_{L}
is holomorphic in the half plane
\mathbb{H}_{D}
and meromorphic in all of
\mathbb{C}
.

So, the functions
\zeta_{L}\cdot\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R]
and
\zeta_{L}\cdot E
are holomorphic when
\Re(s)>\max{D,\sigma_{0}}
and meromorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
with poles at exactly the points
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\Res(\zeta_{L}(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R]))\neq 0.

∎

The solution to this scaling functional equation is then produced by the Mellin inversion theorem. This result is analogous to the Fourier inversion theorem, and provides a means by which one may invert the Mellin transform; see for instance
[
Tit48
]
. We shall make use of the following notation convention:

\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}f(z)\,dz:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{c-iT}^{c+iT}f(z)\,dz,

which may also be considered a principal value integral such as in
[
Gra10
]
.

Let
f,R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, let
L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}M_{\lambda_{i}}
be a scaling operator, and suppose that
f
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f=L[f]+R
. Suppose further that
R(x)=O(x^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
x\to 0^{+}
and that
D
is the unique positive solution to Equation
4.1
.

Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and for any
\delta>x
,
f
is given by:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}[\zeta_{L}\cdot(E+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R])](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}x^{-s}\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s))\,ds,

where
c
is any positive constant greater than both
D
and
\sigma_{0}
and
E
is as in Theorem
4.3
.

Proof.

Theorem
4.3
yields that
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s))
. We know from this theorem and from Proposition
4.2
that the functions
\zeta_{L}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R]
are holomorphic in the half planes
\mathbb{H}_{D}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
, respectively.

Since
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f]=\mathcal{M}[f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[0,\delta]}]
is holomorphic in the half plane
\mathbb{H}_{\max(D,\sigma_{0})}
, we may apply the Mellin inversion theorem to find that for any
c>\max(D,\sigma_{0})
,

f(x)\cdot\mathds{1}_{[0,\delta]}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}x^{-s}\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s)+\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s))\,ds.

Since
\delta>x
, we have that
\mathds{1}_{[0,\delta]}(x)=1,
whence the result follows.
∎

In practice, the next step is to apply the residue theorem and contour deformation in order to obtain the function in terms of the poles of
\zeta_{L}
and integration involving the remainder term. As this has already been done in detail for fractal tube functions (see Chapters 5 and 8 in
[
LvF13
]
in the context of explicit formulae and Chapter 5 of
[
LRŽ17
]
for the treatment of higher dimensional tube formulae,) we shall specialize to the relative tube functions of interest in the next section.

5
Fractal Scaling Functional Equations

In this section, we establish our main results for fractal geometry: analysis of scaling functional equations for tube functions and explicit description of the corresponding tube zeta functions. We shall focus on relative fractal drums which describe attractors of self-similar systems, i.e. fractals arising from iterated function systems in which the mappings are all similitudes. The relevant background information may be found in Section
3
, and we shall use the theorems of Section
4
which have been established in a more general setting.

5.1
Osculant Fractal Drums

Throughout this section, let
\Phi=\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a self-similar system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For convenience, given a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
let us denote
U_{i}=\phi_{i}(U)
for each
i=1,...,m
. Then if
X
is the attractor of
\Phi
, we have that
X=\cup_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(X)
and in this case
X
is said to be self-similar.

We will study iterated function systems satisfying the open set condition, introduced by Moran
[
Mor46
]
for relating similarity and Hausdorff dimensions. See also the work of Hutchinson in
[
Hut81
]
.

An iterated function system
\Phi=\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies the open set condition (OSC) if there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that:

1.

U\supset\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(U)
;

2.

For each
i,j\in\{1,...,m\}
with
i\neq j
,
\phi_{i}(U)\cap\phi_{j}(U)=\emptyset
.

In particular, we will focus on relative fractal drums
(X,\Omega)
such that
X
is the attractor of a self-similar system
\Phi
for which
\Omega
is such an open set.

When
X
is a self-similar set, its tubular neighborhoods
X_{\varepsilon}
may or may not themselves be self-similar in the same sense that it partitions according to a self-similar system. However, with an appropriate relative fractal drum an approximate self-similarity will still lend itself to direct analysis. Namely, if a relative fractal drum
(X,\Omega)
(recall Definition
3.1
) can be written in terms of some similar RFDs
(\lambda_{i}X,\lambda_{i}\Omega)
and a residual RFD
(X,R)
, then the tubular neighborhood can be partitioned according to its overlap with the sets
\lambda_{i}\Omega
and
R
.

To that end, suppose
\Phi
is a self-similar system with attractor
X
, and let
\Omega
be an open set for which
\Phi
satisfies the open set condition and such that
(X,\Omega)
is a relative fractal drum. Write
R=\Omega\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(\Omega))
so that
\Omega
is the disjoint union of each
\phi_{i}(\Omega)
and
R
. There are two points of order. Firstly, we need that
X_{\varepsilon}\cap\phi_{i}(\Omega)
is in fact
(\phi_{i}(X))_{\varepsilon}\cap\phi_{i}(\Omega)
. Equivalently, for every point
y\in\phi_{i}(\Omega)
,
d(y,X)=d(y,\phi_{i}(X))
. This condition will be necessary to relate
V_{X,\phi_{i}(\Omega)}
to
V_{\phi_{i}(X),\phi_{i}(\Omega)}
.

The other is a small technical point:
R
need not be open, so strictly speaking
(X,R)
is not an RFD. If
\partial R
has measure zero, we may safely replace
R
with its interior and
V_{X,R}
shall be unaffected. Even if not, however, the definition of
R
ensures that it is measurable with
m(R)\leq m(\Omega)<\infty
, and thus
V_{X,R}
(and consequently
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,R}
) will still be well-defined as in Definition
3.1
(resp. Definition
3.2
.)

We will conclude this subsection by endowing the relative fractal drums to which our methods apply with a suitable name.

Let
\Phi=\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be an iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
X
be its attractor. An open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to be an osculating set for
\Phi
if the following hold:

1.

\Omega\supset\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(\Omega)
;

2.

For each
i,j\in\{1,...,m\}
with
i\neq j
,
\phi_{i}(\Omega)\cap\phi_{j}(\Omega)=\emptyset
;

3.

\Omega
has finite Lebesgue measure;

4.

\exists\,\delta>0
such that
\Omega_{\delta}\supset X
;

5.

For each
i=1,..,m
, if
y\in\phi_{i}(\Omega)
, then
d(y,X)=d(y,\phi_{i}(X))
.

The pair
(X,\Omega)
is called an osculant fractal drum for
\Phi
.

Note that the first two conditions imply that
\Phi
satisfies the open set condition as per Definition
5.1
. The third and fourth conditions imply that
(X,\Omega)
is a relative fractal drum as per Definition
3.1
. The last condition, which to the best of our knowledge has not been studied before, might be called the “osculating” condition: the points in iterates
\phi_{i}(\Omega)
of
\Omega
stay closest to the corresponding iterate
\phi_{i}(X)
of
X
, rather than to a different iterate
\phi_{j}(X)
.

5.2
Self-Similar Systems Yielding SFEs

Given a self-similar system
\Phi
which satisfies the open set condition, we suppose that it gives rise to an osculant fractal drum
(X,\Omega)
. For this work, our examples shall be focused on (self-avoidant) generalized von Koch snowflakes; see Section
6
. Under the conditions of Definition
5.1
, we deduce a scaling functional equation satisfied by the volume of a tubular neighborhood relative to
\Omega
.

Let
\Phi=\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a self-similar system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
where the scaling ratio of
\phi_{i}
is
\lambda_{i}
for each
i=1,...,m
. Let
X
be the attractor of
\Phi
and suppose that
\Omega
is an osculating set for
\Phi
. Define
R=\Omega\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(\Omega)).

Then the tube function
V_{X,\Omega}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to[0,\infty]
satisfies the following scaling functional equation:

V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)=L[V_{X,\Omega}](\varepsilon)+V_{X,R}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda_{i})+V_{X,R}(\varepsilon),

(5.1)

for all
\varepsilon
. Here,
L:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{N}M_{\lambda_{i}}
is the associated scaling operator.

Note that a scaling ratio may be repeated, in which case
L
may be written in the reduced form

L=\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{j}\lambda_{j}^{N}M_{\lambda_{j}},

where the
\lambda_{j}
’s are distinct,
a_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
is the multiplicity of
\lambda_{j}
, and
m^{\prime}\leq m
.

Proof.

We begin by partitioning
\Omega
according to the iterates and the residual set:
\Omega=\uplus_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(\Omega)\uplus R
. Next, we intersect the sets of the partition with
X_{\varepsilon}
and taking the Lebesgue measure. By disjoint additivity, we obtain that

V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{X,\phi_{i}(\Omega)}(\varepsilon)+V_{X,R}(\varepsilon).

Now, we have that for each
i\in\{1,...,m\}
,
d(y,X)=d(y,\phi_{i}(X))
for all
y\in\phi_{i}(\Omega)
. Consequently,
X_{\varepsilon}\cap\phi_{i}(\Omega)=(\phi_{i}(X))_{\varepsilon}\cap\phi_{i}(\Omega)
, as the epsilon neighborhoods are the preimages of the interval
[0,\varepsilon)
under the respective distance functions
d(\cdot,X)\equiv d(\cdot,\phi_{i}(X))
, equivalent in
\phi_{i}(\Omega)
. Thus,
V_{X,\phi_{i}(\Omega)}=V_{\phi_{i}(X),\phi_{i}(\Omega)}
.

Therefore, we obtain the identity that

V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{\phi_{i}(X),\phi_{i}(\Omega)}(\varepsilon)+V_{X,R}(\varepsilon).

Using Lemma
3.1
, we have that
V_{\phi_{i}(X),\phi_{i}(\Omega)}(t)=\lambda_{i}^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(t/\lambda)
. Equation
5.1
then follows by applying this term-by-term.
∎

In practice, it is generally easiest to ensure that a relative fractal drum is osculant in the construction of the set
\Omega
. In this work, we explicitly apply our results to generalized von Koch fractals in Section
6
.

5.3
Complex Dimensions from SFEs

We first note the following which will serve as a dictionary between truncated Mellin transforms and tube zeta functions.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[t^{-N}V_{X,\Omega}(t)](s).

The main note of interest is that the tube function is scaled by a factor of
t^{-N}
. This in particular is behind the weights of
\lambda_{i}^{N}
in the scaling operator in Theorem
5.2
.

Now, given a scaling functional equation determined by a scaling operator
L
and a remainder term for the tube function of a set
X
, we deduce the possible complex dimensions of
X
up to an order based on the estimates for the remainder.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let
V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)
be the corresponding relative tube function. Suppose that
V_{X,\Omega}
satisfies the scaling functional equation

V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)=L[V_{X,\Omega}](\varepsilon)+R(\varepsilon)
where
L:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{N}M_{\lambda_{i}}
is a scaling operator with distinct ratios
\lambda_{i}\in(0,1)
with positive integral multiplicities
a_{i}
and where
R(\varepsilon)
is a continuous remainder term of order
O(\varepsilon^{N-r})
, where
r\in\mathbb{R}
depends on this remainder quantity.

Then the set of complex fractal dimensions with real part
\sigma>r
, i.e. in the open right half plane
\mathbb{H}_{r}
, is contained in the set:

\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})\subset\Big\{\omega\in\mathbb{H}_{r}:1=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\omega}\Big\}.

(5.2)

If
D=\overline{\dim}_{\text{Mink}}(X,\Omega)
is the relative upper Minkowski dimension of the set
X
, suppose that
r<D
. Then
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{D}
with abscissa of absolute convergence
D(\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega})=\overline{\dim}_{\text{Mink}}(X,\Omega)
.

Explicitly, the functional equation for
V_{X,\Omega}
takes the form:

\displaystyle V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=L[V_{X,\Omega}](\varepsilon)+R(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda_{i})+R(\varepsilon).

(5.3)

The structure of these complex (fractal) dimensions is dependent on the nature of the scaling ratios. In particular, there is a dichotomy between the lattice (or arithmetic) and the non-lattice (or non-arithmetic) cases. We note that the structure results are exactly the same as in Theorem 2.16 of
[
LvF13
]
.

Proof.

First, we note that the function
f(t)=t^{-N}V_{X,\Omega}(t)
satisfies the corresponding scaling functional equation

f(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f(t/\lambda_{i})+t^{-N}R(t),

since
\lambda_{i}^{N}\varepsilon^{-N}V_{X,\Omega}(\varepsilon/\lambda_{i})=f(\varepsilon/\lambda_{i})
. Note that by Proposition
5.3
, the restricted Mellin transform of
f
is exactly the tube zeta function of the RFD
(X,\Omega)
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)=\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta)
. Let us also define
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
by
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[t^{-N}R(t)](s)
.

So, by Theorem
4.3
, we have that the tube zeta function
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
satisfies the functional equation:

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta)=\zeta_{L}(s)(E(s;\delta)+\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)).

(5.4)

Here,
L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}M_{\lambda_{i}}
,
\zeta_{L}
is its associated scaling zeta function, and the remainder term satisfies
t^{-N}R(t)=O(t^{-r})
since
R(t)=O(t^{N-r})
. This formula is valid for all
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{L}\cap\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Additionally, the entire function
E
can be written in terms of partial tube zeta functions, namely:

E(s)=E(s;\delta)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{s}\widetilde{\xi}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta,\delta/\lambda_{i}).

The theorem additionally yields that
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\max(D,r)}
and meromorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
, with
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})\subset\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{L})
. Equation
5.2
follows from this containment and Proposition
4.2
.

The abscissa of absolute convergence is a consequence of Theorem 4.1.7 in
[
LRŽ17
]
, in light of the functional equation relating tube and distance zeta functions. See Equation 2.2.23 and Theorem 5.3.2 in
[
LRŽ17
]
.
∎

Note that Equation
5.4
yields the following additional information about the structure of
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})
. Namely if
\Re(\omega)>r
, then
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})
if and only if
\zeta_{L}
has a pole of higher order than the degree of vanishing of the term
E(s;\delta)+\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)
. So, we obtain the following corollary.

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum as in Theorem
5.3
, in particular whose complex dimensions in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
satisfy Equation
5.2
. Suppose that for each
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{r})
we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\omega}\widetilde{\xi}_{X,\Omega}(\omega;\delta,\delta/\lambda_{i})+\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(\omega;\delta)\neq 0.

Then in fact
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})=\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{r})
, where
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\mathbb{H}_{r})
is the set of complex dimensions of the RFD
(X,\Omega)
in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
, as in Definition
3.2
. In other words, each such
\omega
is a complex dimension of
(X,\Omega)
rather than simply a possible complex dimension.

We conjecture that for every pole of
\zeta_{L}
, there is at least one value of
\delta
for which the factor is nonzero. This claim would be enough to prove that these sets of poles are in fact equal, as the poles of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
are known to be independent of the parameter
\delta
. The integrals defining
\widetilde{\xi}_{X,\Omega}
are notably oscillatory, so one must take care in estimating them and their linear combination. Additionally, this claim is likely dependent on a suitable estimate for
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)
.

Of note, Theorem
5.3
is only as good as the scaling exponent in the known estimate of the remainder term. If the remainder term is merely bounded (viz.
R(t)=O(\varepsilon^{0})
, with
r=N
), then there is little information to be gained. The best results occur if the parameter
r
is small; in Section
6
, our results shall apply for any order
\alpha>0
arbitrarily close to
0
. Informally,
r
corresponds to the (Minkowski) dimension of the remainder quantity: when
R
scales like the volume of an epsilon neighborhood of a point, the exponent is
N
and thus
r=0
; when
R
scales like the volume of a neighborhood of a line, so the exponent is
N-1
and
r=1
; and so forth. In other words, our work determines the complex dimensions with real parts (or amplitudes) up to the real part corresponding to the dimension of (the estimate of) the remainder term.

6
Results on Generalized von Koch Fractals

In this section, we shall apply the results of Section
5
to generalized von Koch fractals (or GKFs.) See Section
2
for the definitions and properties concerning these fractals. In particular, we provide a scaling functional equation for the tubular neighborhood volume and estimates of the error term that allow us to describe the possible complex dimensions of these GKFs.

Notably, a lattice/non-lattice dichotomy arises depending on whether the scaling ratio
r
and its conjugate factor
\ell=\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
are arithmetically related or not. For the standard von Koch snowflake, or the
(3,\frac{1}{3})
-von Koch snowflake to be precise, the ratios are the same:
r=\ell
, and thus the fractal falls under the lattice (aka arithmetic) case. The emergence of the non-lattice (aka non-arithmetic) case is unique to the generalizations of the von Koch snowflake.

,
V_{K}
satisfies the approximate scaling functional equation

V_{K}(\varepsilon)=2\ell^{2}V_{K}(\varepsilon/\ell)+(n-1)r^{2}V_{K}(\varepsilon/r)+R(\varepsilon),

(6.1)

where
\ell=\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
is the conjugate scaling factor to
r
and
R(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{2})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

The remainder satisfies the estimate
R(\varepsilon)\leq(2\cot(\theta_{n}/2)+\theta_{n})\varepsilon^{2}
, where
\theta_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{n}
is the central angle of the regular
n
-gon, for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
K:={K_{n,r}}\cap S
is exactly the
(n,r)
-von Koch curve
{C_{n,r}}
; in general,
K
is isometric to this set. In this setting, we have explicitly the similitudes for the self-similar system
\Phi_{n,r}=\{\phi_{L},\phi_{R},\psi_{k},k=1,...,n-1\}
which defines
{C_{n,r}}
as in Definition
2.1
. (In the general setting, simply compose these maps
\phi_{i}
with the isometry between the sets.)

By definition,
K
is the attractor of the self-similar system
\Phi
in Definition
2.1
. There are two similitudes
\phi_{L}
and
\phi_{R}
with scaling ratios
\ell:=\frac{1}{2}(1-r)\in(0,1)
and
n-1
similitudes
\psi_{k}
with scaling ratios
r\in(0,1)
. So, we define the scaling operator
L=L_{n,r}
by
L:=2M_{\ell}+(n-1)M_{r}.

Let
U=\Omega\cap S
. Then we have that
U
is an osculating set for
\Phi
. Indeed, the collection of sets
\{\phi_{L}(U),\phi_{R}(U),\psi_{k}(U),k=1,...,n-1\}
is readily seen to be pairwise disjoint and each set is contained within
U
itself.
(K,U)
is a clearly a relative fractal drum since
K
and
U
are bounded sets.

The osculating condition may be seen from the symmetry involved in the construction of
K
. In particular, we shall use the property that if
X
and
X^{\prime}
are two sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
related by reflection about the line
L
, then the set of points which are equidistant to
X
and
X^{\prime}
must lie on
L
. We note that by choice of
U
, its images
\psi_{k}(U)
partition a regular
n
-gon scaled by a factor of
r
about the lines of symmetry passing from the center of the shape to its vertices. Because the adjacent images
\psi_{j}(K)
and
\psi_{j+1}(K)
are reflections of each other about these lines, it follows that
d(\cdot,K)=\min_{\phi\in\Phi}d(\cdot,\phi(K))
can only change form across these lines by continuity of the distance functions. In other words, it follows that
d(\cdot,K)\equiv d(\cdot,\psi_{j}(K))
in each
\psi_{j}(U)
,
j=1,...,n-1
.

Now, the other two iterates on the edges are simpler. Indeed, we have that
d(\cdot,K)\equiv d(\cdot,\phi_{L}(K))
in
\phi_{L}(U)
(resp.
d(\cdot,K)\equiv d(\cdot,\phi_{R}(K))
in
\phi_{R}(U)
) is clear from the geometry of
K
since a ball
B_{\varepsilon}(y)
for
y\in\phi_{L}(U)
(resp.
y\in\phi_{R}(U)
) will intersect with
\phi_{L}(K)
(resp.
\phi_{R}(K))
before ever reaching
\psi_{1}(K)
(resp.
\psi_{n-1}(K)
).

So, by Theorem
5.2
, we obtain the following equality:

V_{K}(\varepsilon)=2\ell^{2}V_{K}(\varepsilon/\ell)+(n-1)r^{2}V_{K}(\varepsilon/r)+V_{K,R}(\varepsilon),

where
R=U\setminus(\cup_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi(U))
and
V_{K,R}(\varepsilon)=m(K(\varepsilon)\cap R)
. Now, we note that
K(\varepsilon)\cap R
may be partitioned into two sectors of angle
\theta_{n}
and length
\varepsilon
and four congruent pieces contained in triangles of height
\varepsilon
and width
\varepsilon\cot(\theta_{n}/2)
. See Figure
8
for a depiction of one such triangle. Therefore, we may write that

R(\varepsilon):=V_{K,R}(\varepsilon)\leq\theta_{n}\varepsilon^{2}+2\varepsilon^{2}\cot(\theta_{n}/2).

∎

Figure 8:
A portion of the epsilon neighborhood of the von Koch snowflake
K_{3,1/3}
is depicted. In particular, note the regions contained in the triangles of height
\varepsilon
and width
\varepsilon\cot(\theta_{n}/2)
, where
\theta_{n}=2\pi/n
, on the left and the right.

Note that it follows that
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
satisfies the same functional equation as Equation
6.1
but with remainder
nR(\varepsilon)
. So, Theorem
5.3
will yields a description of the complex dimensions of
{K_{n,r}}
. Additionally, we highlight the description of the tube zeta function in terms of a self-similar zeta function,
\zeta_{L}
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
r\in(0,1)
be such that
{K_{n,r}}
is a simple, closed curve. Let
\tilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s)
be the relative tube zeta function for
{K_{n,r}}
relative to the bounded component
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{K_{n,r}}
. Further, let
\ell=\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
be the conjugate scaling ratio to
r
and
\delta>0
. Then for all
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{L}\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}
,

\tilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s)=\cfrac{1}{1-2\ell^{s}-(n-1)r^{s}}\cdot\Big(E(s;\delta)+\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)\Big),

(6.2)

where
E(s;\delta)
is an entire function,
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta):=n\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{s-3}R(t)\,dt
is holomorphic in the right half plane
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, and
L:=2M_{\ell}+(n-1)M_{r}
is a scaling operator with associated zeta function
\zeta_{L}(s)=\frac{1}{1-2\ell^{s}-(n-1)r^{s}}
and complex dimensions
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{C})=\{\omega\in\mathbb{C}:1=2\ell^{\omega}+(n-1)r^{\omega}\}
.

Consequently, we have that the complex dimensions of
{K_{n,r}}
satisfy the containment:

\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}}}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\subseteq\{\omega\in\mathbb{C}:1-2\ell^{\omega}-(n-1)r^{\omega}=0\}.

\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
is meromorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{0}
with poles at the points in
\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}}}(\mathbb{H}_{0})
and holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{D}
, where
D=\overline{\dim}_{\text{Mink}}({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
.

Proof.

This is a corollary of Theorem
6.1
and Theorem
5.3
. See in particular Equation
5.4
from the proof of the latter. Explicitly, we have that

E(s;\delta)=2\ell^{s}\widetilde{\xi}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta,\delta/\ell)+(n-1)r^{s}\widetilde{\xi}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta,\delta/r)

and that
R(\varepsilon)\leq(\theta_{n}+2\cot(\theta_{n}/2))\varepsilon^{2}
, where
\theta_{n}=2\pi/n
.
∎

6.2
Languidity of Generalized von Koch Fractals

In this section, we provide estimates on the growth of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
known as languidity, see Definitions 5.1.3 and 5.1.4 in
[
LRŽ17
]
. These are primarily necessary in order to deduce tube formulae for
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
. Note that here we use
i
for the imaginary unit, whereas in previous sections we have used it as an indexing variable.

Let
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
be the relative fractal drum of a GKF as in Theorem
6.1
, with tube zeta function
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
. Then
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
is languid with exponent
\kappa=0
. If, a fortiori,
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
is strongly languid as a function (in the sense of Definition
4.2
) then so too is
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
and the RFD
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
.

Proof.

Firstly, we note that by Proposition
4.2
,
\zeta_{L}
is strongly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
. We shall thus need to estimate the terms in the multiplicative factor in order to deduce the languidity of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
. We do have that

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta)|

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta_{L}(s)|(|E(s;\delta)|+|\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)|),

so we may estimate
E(s;\delta)
and
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)
independently.

Let
\alpha\in(0,D)
,
\beta>2\geq D
, and
\delta>0
be fixed. Let us define the screen
S(\tau)
to be the constant function
S(\tau)\equiv\alpha
. Further, suppose that
\{\tau_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of real numbers for which
\zeta_{L}
satisfies the languidity estimates. In particular, it satisfies that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\tau_{m}\to\infty
,
\lim_{m\to-\infty}\tau_{m}=-\infty
, and
\forall m\geq 1
,
\tau_{-m}<0<\tau_{m}
.

To estimate the entire function
E(s;\delta)
, we recall the estimates in Equation
3.3
for partial tube zeta functions. Using these estimates on the function
E(s;\delta)
, when
\sigma=\Re(s)\neq 2
we have that:

\displaystyle|E(s;\delta)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\ell^{\sigma}V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\delta/\ell)\frac{\delta^{\sigma-2}}{\sigma-2}(\ell^{2-\sigma}-1)

\displaystyle+(n-1)r^{\sigma}V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\delta/r)\frac{\delta^{\sigma-2}}{\sigma-2}(r^{2-\sigma}-1)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\delta^{\sigma-2}}{\sigma-2}(\ell^{2}-\ell^{\sigma}+r^{2}-r^{\sigma}).

Additionally,
E(s;\delta)
is bounded in a neighborhood of
\sigma=2
, say
[2-\gamma,2+\gamma]
, by the second estimate of Equation
3.3
and the continuity of
E(\cdot;\delta)
. So,
E(s;\delta)
may be uniformly estimated on the intervals
[\alpha+i\tau_{m},(2-\gamma)+i\tau_{m}]
,
[2-\gamma+i\tau_{m},2+\gamma+i\tau_{m}]
, and
[2+\gamma+i\tau_{m},\beta+i\tau_{m}]
with a constant
C
which does not depend on
\tau_{m}
. Along the screen
S(\tau)\equiv\alpha
, we similarly have that
E(s;\delta)
is uniformly estimated by a constant determined only by
\alpha
and the other fixed parameters (
\delta,n,r
and so forth.) We do note that the estimate involves a factor of the form
B^{\alpha}
for some
B>0
, but otherwise is valid for any
\alpha<D
; thus
E(s;\delta)
may in fact be seen to satisfy the estimates required of strongly languidity.

Next, we estimate the remainder term. By Theorem
6.1
, we have the estimate
nR(t)\leq Ct^{2}
for a constant
C>
depending only on
n
. Therefore, we have that if
\Re(s)=\sigma>0
,

|\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(s;\delta)|\leq C\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{\sigma-1}\,dt\leq C\frac{\delta^{\sigma}}{\sigma}.

Therefore, we have that it is bounded on
\Re(s)=\alpha>0
independently of
\Im(s)
(with bound depending only on
n
and
\alpha
) and on the intervals
[\alpha+i\tau_{m},\beta+i\tau_{m}]
independently of
\tau_{m}
.

Let
C_{L}
be a constant for which the estimates on
\zeta_{L}
hold,
C_{E}
for
E
, and
C_{R}
for
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
. Combining these estimates together with the constant
C=C_{L}(C_{E}+C_{R})
yields the estimates for the function
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
. Thus, the RFD
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
is languid with exponent
\kappa=0
with respect to any screen
S(\tau)\equiv\alpha>0
. Additionally, if
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
satisfies the strong languidity conditions, then so too does
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
since the functions
\zeta_{L}
and
E
both satisfy the necessary estimates.
∎

6.3
Tube Formulae for GKFs

Having proven the necessary growth estimates, we may now deduce explicit tube formulae for the RFDs
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
. We shall focus on pointwise formulae in this paper for simplicity, but the interested reader may also wish to consider the distributional analogues which are simpler in expression but more technical in their interpretation. There is an explicit formula for the tube function
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
which is valid distributionally (Equation
6.4
), and the pointwise formulae of Theorem
6.3
will be valid for the integrated tube functions
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}^{[k]}
for any
k\geq 2
.

In what follows, we shall use the Pochhammer symbol
(s)_{k}
which may be defined as

(s)_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(s+k)}{\Gamma(s)},

which for
k\in\mathbb{N}
takes the form
(s)_{k}=s(s+1)\cdots(s+k-1)
.

Let
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
be the relative fractal drum of a generalized von Koch snowflake relative to its interior component
\Omega
. Let
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
be its tube function and let
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}^{[k]}
be the
k^{\text{th}}
antiderivative of
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
with the constraint that
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}^{[l]}(0)=0
for all
l=1,...,k
.

Let
\delta>0
and
k>1
. Then for any
t\in(0,\delta)
, we have that

\displaystyle V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}^{[k]}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})}}\,\Res\Bigg(\frac{t^{2-s+k}}{(3-s)_{k}}\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+O(t^{2-\alpha+k}),

(6.3)

as
t\to 0^{+}
for any
\alpha>0
for which
\{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{0}):\Re(\omega)=\alpha\}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.2
, the RFD
({K_{n,r}},\Omega)
is languid. Therefore, we may apply Theorem 5.1.11 in
[
LRŽ17
]
to obtain Equation
6.3
. Explicitly, we construct the screen
S(\tau):=\alpha+i\tau
, which by assumption does not intersect any poles of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}}}
. In this case,
\sup S=\alpha
and the error estimate follows from Equation 5.1.33.
∎

Note that if a pole
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{0})
is simple, then we have that

\Res\Bigg(\frac{t^{2-s+k}}{(3-s)_{k}}\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta);\omega\Bigg)=\frac{t^{2-\omega+k}}{(3-\omega)_{k}}\Res(\zeta_{L})(E(\omega;\delta)+\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}(\omega;\delta)),

where
\zeta_{L}
,
E
, and
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
are as in Theorem
6.1
. In this case, the formula is seen to be a sum of terms of the form
\frac{r_{\omega}}{(3-\omega)_{k}}t^{2-\omega+k}
, where
r_{\omega}
is a constant determined from
\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}}}
. Note that this computation of the residue relies on Equation
6.2
and the holomorphicity of
E
and
\widetilde{\zeta}_{R}
.

Also, we note that the Pochhammer symbol has a shift by one since the term in Equation 5.1.30 in
[
LRŽ17
]
is
(N-s+1)_{k}
; the codimension
2-\omega
is still the important term in the denominator. Indeed, by writing the product formula in reverse order and fixing the codimension
2-\omega
in each factor, we remark that it takes the form

(3-\omega)_{k}=(2-\omega+k)(2-\omega+k-1)\cdots(2-\omega+1).

In this form, the correspondence between the exponent of
t
and the factors in the product is clearer.

Lastly, we stress that the sum in Equation
6.3
must be interpreted as an appropriate limit of sums over poles contained in windows of increasing size. More explicitly, it is the limit of the sum of the complex dimensions whose imaginary parts are bounded in magnitude by a fixed upper bound, say
T_{n}
, with the limit taken over the bounding parameter
T_{n}
going to infinity. Equivalently, this may be stated in terms of a limit of sums over truncated windows. See Remark 5.1.12 in
[
LRŽ17
]
for more details.

Now, Equation
6.3
is explicitly for second order and higher antiderivatives of the tube function
V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
. This formula is, however, valid in a distributional sense for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
(and in particular for the tube function itself where
k=0
) without further assumption; see Theorem 5.2.2 in
[
LRŽ17
]
. Interpreting the following equality in the distributional sense, the formula for
k=0
takes the form:

V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(t)=\sum_{\mathclap{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})}}\,\Res(t^{2-s}\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta);\omega)+\mathcal{R}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(t),

(6.4)

where
\mathcal{R}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}
is a distribution whose action on test functions is of asymptotic order
O(t^{2-\alpha})
for small
\alpha>0
, in the sense of Definition 5.2.9 in
[
LRŽ17
]
. If the poles
\omega
are each known to be simple, then Equation
6.4
takes the simpler form

V_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(t)=\sum_{\mathclap{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})}}\,a_{\omega}t^{2-\omega}+\mathcal{R}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(t),

(6.5)

where
a_{\omega}:=\Res(\widetilde{\zeta}_{{K_{n,r}},\Omega}(s;\delta);\omega)
are constants determined only by the residues of the tube zeta function. We refer the interested reader to Section 5.2 of
[
LRŽ17
]
for more detail and most importantly the description of the appropriate space of test functions for which these distributions act. Theorem 5.2.2 in particular leads Equation
6.4
and Equation
6.5
follows from Corollary 5.2.12.

6.4
Lattice/Non-Lattice Dichotomy

The possible complex dimensions
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{0})
, or equivalently the complex solutions to Equation
4.1
with positive real part, have structure heavily dependent on the arithmetic properties of
r
and its conjugate
\ell
. We shall say that
{K_{n,r}}
is arithmetic (or lattice) if
\log r/\log\ell
is rational and non-arithmetic (or non-lattice) otherwise. In the arithmetic case, this implies there exists a (unique) positive number
x
and positive integers
p,q
so that
r=x^{p}
and
\ell=x^{q}
.

Let the scaling ratios
r
and
\ell=\frac{1}{2}(1-r)
be arithmetic, that is, there exist a positive
x
and positive integers
p,q
so that
r=x^{p}
,
\ell=x^{q}
.

Then there exist finitely many poles
D=\omega_{1},\omega_{2},...,\omega_{n}
with real parts
D=\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}\geq...\geq\sigma_{n}
so that the complex dimensions of
{K_{n,r}}
are contained in the set

\Big\{\omega_{k}+\frac{2\pi in}{\log x^{-1}}:n\in\mathbb{Z},k=1,...,n\Big\}.

For each
\omega
with
\Re(\omega)_{k}>0
, these are indeed complex dimensions, and all of the poles with real part
D
are simple.

Proof.

This result is a corollary of Theorem
6.1
and the results in
[
LvF13
, Theorem 2.16]
regarding self-similar zeta functions having the same form as
\zeta_{L}
.
∎

This corollary implies that whenever
{K_{n,r}}
is arithmetic, it is not Minkowski measurable.

K_{3,\frac{1}{3}}

K_{4,\frac{1}{4}}

K_{5,\frac{1}{5}}

Two dimensional complex argument plots of associated scaling zeta functions of the fractals
K_{3,\frac{1}{3}}
(left),
K_{4,\frac{1}{4}}
(middle), and
K_{5,\frac{1}{5}}
(right). The possible complex dimensions of the fractals occur at the poles of these functions, indicated in the plots by the white circles.

Meanwhile, in the non-lattice case, the complex dimensions are no longer distributed periodically along finitely many vertical lines. Instead, there is a unique pole of highest order, the Minkowski dimension
D
of the fractal or equivalently the unique positive real solution to Equation
4.1
specialized to these fractals. Compare the possible complex dimensions of the fractals plotted in Figure
9
: the first example is the lattice case since there is one scaling ratio
r=\frac{1}{3}
. However, in the other cases, the ratios of logarithms of scaling ratios take the form
\log(1/4)/\log(3/8)
for
K_{4,\frac{1}{4}}
and
\log(1/5)/\log(2/5)
for
K_{5,\frac{1}{5}}
.

Describing these poles exactly is in general quite challenging since they are solutions to a transcendental equation without the simplifications possible in the arithmetic case. However, the possible complex dimensions in the non-lattice case have a quasiperiodic structure which in particular may be approximated by sets of lattice complex dimensions. We refer the reader to Chapter 3, and in particular Theorem 3.6 of
[
LvF13
]
for a thorough description of the structure of non-lattice complex dimensions and the proofs of these statements. The theory of approximation of non-lattice complex dimensions may be found in Section 3.4 of
[
LvF13
]
and also in the further study of Lapidus, van Frankenhuijsen, and Voskanian in
[
LvFV21
]
.

7
Conclusion

In summary, we have analyzed fractals arising from self-similar systems by constructing scaling functional equations. These scaling functional equations may be solved by means of truncated Mellin transforms. In the case of tube formulae for relative fractal drums, this process induces a functional equation satisfied by the zeta function. Moreover, the poles of this zeta function are essentially controlled by the self-similar complex dimensions from the scaling functional equation.

We conclude the work by discussing future directions and open problems related to this work.

7.1
Future Directions

Firstly, the methods here may be applied to compute the possible complex dimensions of many other self-similar fractals. All of the necessary framework is present in Sections
4
and
5
, and one primarily needs to verify that an RFD
(X,\Omega)
is osculant and languid in order to deduce an explicit tube formula such as Equation
6.3
for generalized von Koch fractals.

Secondly, it is of interest to more carefully analyze the exactness of complex dimensions obtained via this methodology. Under what conditions are the possible complex dimensions exact, such as when does Corollary
5.3
apply? We conjecture that a much stronger conclusion is possible, namely that Equation
5.2
is in fact an equality with only mild assumptions on the remainder term.

Additionally, the analysis of generalized von Koch fractals has been done in this specific way in order to better understand the connection between geometry and spectrum of fractals in higher dimensions. For one-dimensional fractal strings, the relationship is elegantly given by an identity of geometric and spectral zeta functions using Riemann’s zeta function (see
[
LvF13
]
.) The relationship is being explored in higher dimensions, and while there are known results (see for example Chapter 4, Section 3 in
[
LRŽ17
]
, and explicitly Theorems 4.3.8 and 4.3.11), the general problem is open. See Problem 6.2.32 in
[
LRŽ17
]
(and the references therein) for a more thorough discussion of this problem.

In particular, we have studied geometric properties of generalized von Koch fractals by means of scaling functional equations in order to compare with the work of Michiel van den Berg such as in
[
vdB00
,
vdBG98
,
vdBdH99
]
on the heat equation on such fractals. We plan to further explore this connection in future work.
