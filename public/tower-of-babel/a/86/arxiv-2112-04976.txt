Cutoff and Dynamical Phase Transition for the General Multi-component Ising Model
Seoyeon Yang

Abstract
. We study the multi-component Ising model, which is also known as the block Ising model. In this model, the particles are partitioned into a fixed number of groups with a fixed proportion, and the interaction strength is determined by the group to which each particle belongs. We demonstrate that the Glauber dynamics on our model exhibits the cutoff – metastability phase transition as passing the critical inverse-temperature β c ​ r \beta_{cr} , which is determined by the proportion of the groups and their interaction strengths, regardless of the total number of particles. For β < β c ​ r \beta<\beta_{cr} , the dynamics shows a cutoff at α ​ n ​ log ⁡ n \alpha n\log n with a window size O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) , where α \alpha is a constant independent of n n . For β = β c ​ r \beta=\beta_{cr} , we prove that the mixing time is of order n 3 / 2 n^{3/2} . In particular, we deduce the so-called non-central limit theorem for the block magnetizations to validate the optimal bound at β = β c ​ r \beta=\beta_{cr} . For β > β c ​ r \beta>\beta_{cr} , we examine the metastability, which refers to the exponential mixing time. Our results, based on the position of the employed Ising model on the complete multipartite graph, generalize the results of previous versions of the model.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the general multi-component Curie–Weiss Ising model and demonstrate the cutoff–metastability phase transition including the rigorous power-law mixing at the critical temperature. We aim to deal with the most general case of a multi-component system in the mean-field approximation.

The phase transition between cutoff and metastability is observed universally in many cases of statistical physics, typically when the system is determined by parameters, such as temperature. The
cutoff
phenomenon is an asymptotically abrupt convergence of a system to its equilibrium over negligible time referred to as the
cutoff window
, which is generally considered as the convergence rate, whereas
metastability
corresponds to the phenomenon when the system is trapped in local minima of energy and the exit time from these traps takes exponentially long time. There have been several efforts to associate these two phenomena although they differ in focus. Specifically, cutoff focuses on the distance between measures, whereas metastability examines exit time. After a conjecture that a necessary and sufficient condition for cutoff requires the divergence of the product, as
n
approaches infinity, of the mixing time and spectral gap was proposed in 2004 (
[
27
]
), Barrera, Bertoncini, and Fernández purposed to link the two phenomena in
[
3
,
2
]
, and Hernández1, Kovchegov, and Otto followed suit in
[
15
]
.

Consequently, several rigorous studies have been conducted to prove cutoff–metastability transition. A popular model that exhibits this phase transition is the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model. In
[
20
]
, the total magnetization chain, which is the sum of all spins, was primarily used to prove the full cutoff–metastability phase transition with the classical coupling argument including a contraction in the mean of coupling distances. In the high temperature regime, the case of when the inverse temperature
\beta
satisfies
\beta<1
, the dynamics exhibits a cutoff at
(2(1-\beta))^{-1}n\log n
with a window of order
n
. At a critical temperature, the case of
\beta=1
, the mixing time is of order
n^{3/2}
. In the low temperature regime, the case of
\beta>1
, the dynamics shows metastability, wherein mixing time is exponential in
n
; moreover, the restricted system to the set of non-negative magnetization has a mixing time of order
n\log n
. The metastability is derived from the conductance arguments that a bottleneck exists between states with positive magnetization and states with negative magnetization. If the system is restricted to non-negative magnetization, the bottleneck is removed to accelerate the chain convergence. Detailed characterization and behavior of the mixing time to transition from
O(n\log n)
to
O(n^{3/2})
and ultimately to
O(\exp(n))
is provided in
[
8
]
. In the same aspect, the phase transition of the Ising model on a regular tree was completed in
[
9
]
by verifying polynomial mixing at criticality. Following the in-depth research of the spectral gap on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(except for the criticality), Lubetzky and Sly demonstrated the polynomial upper bound for the critical mixing, accomplishing the full slowdown in
[
23
]
. Furthermore, the universal property that the Ising model in any geometry exhibits cutoff at sufficiently high temperature is proven in
[
24
]
.

There are also several studies on
q
-states Potts model, such as the Curie–Weiss Potts model and the lattice model in the case where
q
is sufficiently large. See
[
7
]
and therein. In
[
7
]
, the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{s}(q)
and the transition from cutoff to metastability passing through polynomial mixing at
\beta_{s}(q)
has been proved. The authors utilized the Markov chain named proportions chain, which is the vector of the proportions of
q
states, and a coupling argument to compute mixing times. The obtained results are similar to the Ising case because the dynamics exhibits a cutoff at
O(n\log n)
with a window of order
n
on
\beta<\beta_{s}(q)
, a polynomial mixing time at
\beta=\beta_{s}(q)
, and mixing time of exponential order on
\beta>\beta_{s}(q)
.

For the Ising model, it is conjectured that a similar cutoff–metastability phase transition phenomenon will occur in the multi-component model (Fig
2
). The multi-component Ising model has been proposed as a better approximation of lattice models than the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model. To illustrate, the model considers the partitioning of the interacting particles into several groups such that the interaction strength depends on the groups to which the particles belong (Fig

). The multi-component model has received considerable attention following the research on the bipartite mean-field in
[
11

17

, as an approximation of lattice models for meta-magnets. Moreover, as Brock proposed a new perspective discrete choice models with interactions for the Curie–Weiss model in
[

, the multi-component model is also considered as a representative approach to describe social phenomena or collective dynamics with social interactions among several groups. See
[
1
,
26
]
and therein for more details. Thereby it has also gained immense attention from various fields besides statistical physics. Owing to increasing interest in block networking systems, multi-component models based on the Ising and Potts model has been substantially studied because of their versatility of applications in many fields including statistical physics, applied mathematics
[
25
]
, economics
[
12

, and biology
[

.

Furthermore, there are diverse mathematical studies establishing limit theorems on the multi-component model as well as the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model. For instance, the authors in
[
19
]
analyzed the multi-component Ising system demonstrating the large deviations principles (LDPs), central limit theorems (CLTs), and non-central limit theorems. Specifically, two methods were used to prove the CLT in the high temperature regime: the Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation, which is one of the most core techniques of showing CLT, and the multivariate version of the exchangeable pair approach in Stein’s method, which is used to determine the convergence rates in the CLT. For more studies on such limit results, see
[
6

5

18
,
22

and therein. There have also been some studies on the cutoff phenomenon in the multi-component model. To the best of our knowledge, the first attempt at a non-classical Curie–Weiss model was established in
[
15
]
; the author employed the aggregate path coupling method by extending the classical path coupling method to verify rapid mixing in the high temperature regime on the bipartite Potts model. Subsequently,
[
16
]
proved cutoff–metastability transition of the Ising model on the complete multipartite graph, for the case of no interaction strength on the same group and homogeneous interaction strength otherwise, using the classical path coupling method.

In this paper, we obtain the critical inverse-temperature
\beta_{cr}
explicitly and employ some techniques including an elaborate coupling argument to demonstrate the cutoff–metastability transition on the general multi-component Ising model. This is the first time full cutoff–metastability phase transition on the multi-component model with a general condition on interaction strength is proven. Moreover, based on the results obtained in this paper, our model generalizes other previous versions of the Ising model on the multipartite graph. Despite the growing interest and significant research on the multi-component model, coupled with the widespread belief that the dynamics can exhibit cutoff–metastability transition, relatively few cases have been introduced to rigorously verify this conjecture as cited in
[
23
]
. As a result, the full cutoff–metastability phase transition on the multi-component model has not been adequately investigated. Although the overall proof flow is a coupling argument, which is the universally accepted method, the detailed content is sufficiently delicate and sophisticated to deal with the general interaction strength.

To begin with, we verify the cutoff in the high temperature regime using the coupling argument. For an upper bound, we show various contraction properties of grand coupling, which is a generalized version of the monotone coupling in the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model, and subsequently construct a suitable coupling to establish proper supermartingale. See Section
4
for the precise definition. For a lower bound, we attain a particular starting state whose distribution of Glauber dynamics differs from the stationary measure. Despite the notable cutoff proofs in
[
16
,
15
]
, the authors assumed a homogeneous interaction strength. In contrast to this homogeneous interaction case, our generalized model is burdensome because both mean-field influence and updating probabilities are different for each group, further complicating the mean-field approximation. This challenge is alleviated by applying the mean value theorem in a novel manner along with intricate matrix computations.

Additionally, we prove power-law mixing at the critical temperature with the general settings on interaction strength. The critical case is the most demanding as observed in other models. This is because one should evince the number of steps it takes to agree in magnetizations; however, the criticality implies neither convergence nor divergence of the total-variation distance. Therefore, the ordinary coupling argument does not apply. In this paper, we observe the square sum of magnetizations and use tower property to compute its expected value to bound the mixing time at the critical temperature. Furthermore, we confirm that this bound is optimal by applying the non-central limit theorem with respect to the magnetization vector, which is also verified in this paper. In many cases, the dynamics at criticality follows a specific distribution under the stationary measure. For example, in the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model, the quantity
n^{-3/4}\sum_{v\in V}\sigma(v)
converges weakly to a non-trivial limit law (
[
28
]
). Generally, it is onerous to ascertain the limit distribution, that is, although the multi-component Ising model offers significant contributions, the results have limitations on bipartite models (
[
22
]
) or the condition of the uniform case (
[
19
]
). In this paper, we achieve a suitable Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation to demonstrate the non-trivial distribution of magnetizations under the stationary measure on the generalized multi-component Ising model. Consequently, we observe a specific starting configuration whose dynamics is different from the stationary one to conclude the optimal polynomial mixing time at the critical temperature.

Lastly, we prove metastability in the low temperature regime. We establish two local minima of free energy by certifying the existence of two solutions of the mean-field equation. By conductance arguments, as shown in
[
7
]
or
[
21
, Chapter 7]
, more than one local minima of free energy function in terms of the magnetization indicate that the mixing time for the Glauber dynamics is exponential in
n
. Two metastability states are derived from the characteristic of the hyperbolic tangent function and the intermediate value theorem. This result coincides with other studies regarding the number of the ground states, such as
[
4
]
.

In conclusion, we complete the full cutoff–metastability transition on the general multi-component Ising model. We explicitly characterize a critical inverse-temperature
\beta_{cr}
and a constant
\alpha
in (
4.9
) that only depend on the proportions of the group size and the interaction strength to describe the phase transition as follows.

(i)

When
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, there is a cutoff at
\alpha n\log n
with a window of order
n
.

(ii)

When
\beta=\beta_{cr}
, the mixing time of order
n^{3/2}
which is the optimal bound.

(iii)

When
\beta>\beta_{cr}
, the dynamics exhibits metastability and the mixing time is exponential in
n
.

2.
Proposed Model

In this section, we describe the multi-component Ising model.

2.1.
Ising model and the Glauber dynamics

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with finite (
|V|=n
) vertices. The state space is defined by
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{V}
, and each element
\sigma\in\Omega
is referred to as
configuration
, i.e., each configuration is equivalent to an assignment of positive or negative spins on the sites in
V
. The nearest-neighbor
Ising model
on
G
with the inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
is defined on
\Omega
by the Hamiltonian function

H(\sigma)=-\sum_{v\sim w}K(v,w)\sigma(v)\sigma(w),

where
x\sim y
indicates that they are neighboring sites, and its corresponding Gibbs measure

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mu(\sigma)=Z_{\beta}^{-1}\exp(-\beta H(\sigma)),

where
Z_{\beta}
is a normalizing constant. We use a notation
\mu_{n}
for the Gibbs measure to indicate
|V|=n
. The parameter
K(v,w)
represents the interaction intensity between vertices
v
and
w
. In this paper, we consider the ferromagnetic interaction that every
K(v,w)
is positive. Notably, the higher the temperature rises, the weaker the influence of spin interaction is.

Throughout this paper, we employ the
Glauber dynamics
, discrete-time Markov chains on the state space
\Omega
, with respect to the Gibbs distribution and denote this by
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. It is equivalent to updating the spins by the following procedure: At each step, choose a site
v
uniformly at random on
V
and create a new configuration according to
\mu
on the feasible set

\Omega^{v}:=\{\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega:\sigma(w)=\sigma^{\prime}(w)\text{ for every }w\neq v\},

which implies that the probability of updating
v
to
\pm 1
is
r_{\pm}(S^{v})
, where

\displaystyle r_{\pm}(s):=\frac{e^{\pm\beta s}}{e^{\beta s}+e^{-\beta s}}=\frac{1\pm\tanh{\beta s}}{2},

and
S^{v}:=\sum_{w:w\sim v}K(v,w){\sigma(w)}
. The variable
S^{v}
is referred to as the mean-field at site
v
. We use the notation of
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
to indicate the expectation operator and probability measure with the starting state
\sigma_{0}:=\sigma
. Note that the Gibbs measure is the stationary measure of the Glauber dynamics.

2.2.
Markov chain mixing time and cutoff

Our main concern is the distance between the distribution of the Markov chain by the Glauber dynamics and stationary distribution at time
t
. For analysis, we use the
total-variance distance

d_{n}(t):=\max_{\sigma\in\Omega}\|\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\sigma_{t}\in\cdot)-\mu_{n}\|_{\textrm{TV}}.

The total-variation
mixing time
is the number of steps until the total-variance distance is at most
\epsilon
, precisely written as

\displaystyle t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon):=\min\{t:d_{n}(t)\leq\epsilon\}.

We work with
t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}:=t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(1/4)
. In Section
5
, we observe a certain phenomenon known as
cutoff
. The sequence of the Markov chain is said to exhibit
cutoff
if for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)}{t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(1-\epsilon)}=1.

This equation implies that the total-variance distance falls drastically at the mixing time
t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}
. In addition, to characterize the time scale the distance drops from near one to near zero, we say that a Markov chain exhibits a cutoff phenomenon with a
window
of size
O(w_{n})
when
w_{n}=o\left(t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}\right)
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}d_{n}(t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}-\gamma w_{n})

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}d_{n}(t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}+\gamma w_{n})

\displaystyle=0.

2.3.
Model

We consider
G
to be the complete graph with
n
vertices: the case of when the interaction intensity is homogeneous such that
K(v,w)=1/n
corresponds to the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model. In this paper, we generalize the classical Curie–Weiss Ising model to the multi-component Ising model, that is, the sites are partitioned into a finite number of groups and their interaction strength depends on the groups to which the sites belong. For some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the
m
number of groups by
G_{1},G_{2},\cdots,G_{m}
with fixed proportion
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{m}
to ensure that
|G_{i}|=np_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, and the interaction is given by

\displaystyle K(v,w)=K_{ij}=k_{ij}/n,\quad\text{if }v\in G_{i},~w\in G_{j}.

We would also call a group a
block
. We consider the ferromagnetic case that we may assume
k_{ij}>0
. The interaction matrix is given by

\mathbf{J}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\begin{pmatrix}k_{11}J_{np_{1},np_{1}}&k_{12}J_{np_{1},np_{2}}&\cdots&k_{1m}J_{np_{1},np_{m}}\\
k_{21}J_{np_{2},np_{1}}&k_{22}J_{np_{2},np_{2}}&\cdots&k_{2m}J_{np_{2},np_{m}}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
k_{m1}J_{np_{m},np_{1}}&k_{m2}J_{np_{m},np_{2}}&\cdots&k_{mm}J_{np_{m},np_{m}}\end{pmatrix},

where
J_{a,b}
is the
a\times b
matrix such that every element equals one. We use the notation on matrices

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{K}&:=(k_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq m},\\
\mathbf{P}&:=\text{diag}(p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{m}),\\
\mathbf{D}&:=\text{diag}(\sqrt{p_{1}},\cdots,\sqrt{p_{m}}),\\
\mathbf{B}&:=(p_{i}k_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}=\mathbf{D}^{2}\mathbf{K}.\end{split}

Henceforth, we would refer to the matrix
\mathbf{K}
as the interaction matrix. We often employ the block
magnetization
that are defined by the sum of the spins in each group, precisely,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&M^{(i)}(\sigma):=\sum_{v\in G_{i}}\sigma(v),~~i=1,\cdots m,\\
&\bm{M}(\sigma):=(M^{(1)}(\sigma),M^{(2)}(\sigma),\cdots,M^{(m)}(\sigma)),\quad\bm{S}(\sigma):=\frac{1}{n}\bm{M}(\sigma),\end{split}

which are Markov chains (proven in Section
4
), and it will be often utilized instead of the configuration chain. This magnetization chain would play a key role in analyzing the mixing time. Moreover, observe that the Gibbs distribution is represented with the magnetization by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mu(\sigma)

\displaystyle=Z_{\beta}^{-1}\exp\left(\beta\sum_{v\sim w}K(v,w)\sigma(v)\sigma(w)\right)={Z^{\prime}}_{\beta}^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\langle\bm{M}(\sigma),\mathbf{K}\bm{M}(\sigma)\rangle\right),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
indicates inner product between two vectors and
Z^{\prime}_{\beta}
is a new normalizing constant, since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\langle\bm{M}(\sigma),\mathbf{K}\bm{M}(\sigma)\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\frac{k_{ij}}{n}M^{(i)}(\sigma)M^{(j)}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\sim w}K(v,w)\sigma(v)\sigma(w)+\sum_{v\in V}{K(v,v)\sigma(v)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\sim w}K(v,w)\sigma(v)\sigma(w)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}{k_{ii}p_{i}},

of which second term is constant.

3.
Main Results

3.1.
Cutoff in the high temperature regime

Our first result shows that the multi-component Ising model exhibits cutoff in the high temperature regime. We remark that the absence of interior interaction strength, i.e.,
k_{ii}=0
is allowed throughout this section. We first introduce the critical inverse-temperature
\beta_{cr}
, which only depends on the proportions of blocks and interaction strength regardless of the total numbers of sites
n
. We demonstrate that the Glauber dynamics for the multi-component Ising model exhibits cutoff at
\alpha n\log n
with a window of size
O(n)
in the high temperature regime
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, where
\alpha
is a constant independent of
n
determined by the inverse temperature
\beta
and
\beta_{cr}
. Both
\beta_{cr}
and
\alpha
are characterized explicitly in (
4.9
) in terms of
k_{ij}
,
p_{i}
, and
\beta
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Glauber dynamics for the multi-component Ising model shows a cutoff phenomenon at
t_{n}:=\alpha n\log n
with window size
n
in the high temperature regime
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, where
\alpha=\alpha(\beta,p_{i},k_{ij})
and
\beta_{cr}=\beta_{cr}(p_{i},k_{ij})
are constants defined in (
4.9
).

Recently, there have been considerable results related to the multi-component Ising model, including various limit theorems. For example, it is known that the sequence

\tilde{\bm{M}}_{n}=\left(\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{v\in G_{i}}\sigma(v)\right)_{i=1,\cdots,m}

satisfies an LDP under the Gibbs measure. See
[
19
]
for a detailed description.
The high temperature regime and low temperature regime in
[
19
]
coincide with ours; hence, it can be applied to our model. In addition,
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
implies the CLT in the high temperature regime that

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{np_{i}}}\sum_{v\in G_{i}}\sigma(v)\right)_{i=1,\cdots,m}

converges to the normal distribution if
\mathbf{K}
is a positive definite matrix. Moreover, according to
[
19
, Theorem 4]
, one can establish convergence rate in the CLT using the exchangeable pair approach of Stein’s method.

3.2.
Power-law at the critical temperature

At the critical temperature, we not only prove that
O(n^{3/2})
is bound on mixing time, but also that it is the optimal bound. In this section, we assume that the interaction matrix
\mathbf{K}
is positive definite to warrant the optimality. Generally, this condition ensures that the interaction within the groups dominates the interaction among the groups.

Theorem 3.2
.

When
\beta=\beta_{cr}
, there exist constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that the mixing time of Glauber dynamics for the multi-component Ising model satisfies

C_{1}n^{3/2}\leq t_{mix}(n)\leq C_{2}n^{3/2}.

We employ a proper coupling argument to demonstrate the upper bound. To verify the lower bound, we prove a limit theorem stated in Proposition
3.3
based on a suitable Hubbard–Stratonovich transform. As described in Section
6
, at the critical temperature
\beta=\beta_{cr}
, the largest eigenvalue of a positive symmetric matrix
\beta\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}
is one. Thus, the spectral theorem implies that it can be decomposed by

\beta\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m-1},1)\mathbf{V}^{\top},

with the eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m-1}<\lambda_{m}=1
and an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{V}
(see Section
6
for details). Define matrices

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{n}&:=\text{diag}(n^{-1/2},n^{-1/2},\cdots,n^{-1/2},n^{-3/4}),\\
\mathbf{C}_{n}&:=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},~\lambda_{2},~\cdots,\lambda_{m-2},~\lambda_{m-1},~n^{1/2}),\\
\bm{U}_{n}(\sigma)&:=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{n}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\bm{M}(\sigma).\end{split}

Then, we obtain the following result with regard to
\bm{U}_{n}
under the Gibbs measure
\mu_{n}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\bm{Y}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{C}_{n}^{-1})
. When
\beta=\beta_{cr}
,
\mu_{n}^{-1}(\bm{U}_{n})+\bm{Y}_{n}
converges in distribution to a probability measure with density

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}}):=\frac{1}{\tilde{Z}}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}{\Big((\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i}^{2})x_{i}^{2}\Big)}-\frac{x_{m}^{4}}{12}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(V_{im})^{4}}{p_{i}}\right)}d{\bm{x}},

where
\tilde{Z}
is a normalizing constant.

Remark 3.4
.

By the previous proposition, the
(m-1)
-dimensional vector
\Big({U}_{n}^{(j)}\Big)_{j=1,\cdots,m-1}
converges to a normal distribution with covariance matrix

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\text{diag}\left((1-\lambda_{1})^{-1},\cdots,(1-\lambda_{m-1})^{-1}\right),

and the random variable
{U}_{n}^{(m)}
converges to a distribution with Lebesgue-density

\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(V_{im})^{4}/p_{i}\right)x^{4}\right)dx,

where
Z
is a normalizing constant, under the stationary measure
\mu_{n}
.

In the classical Curie–Weiss model, there are substantial results concerning the limit theorems, such as CLT as shown in
[
28
]
and
[
14
]
. In particular, it is well-known that
n^{-3/4}\sum_{v\in V}\sigma(v)
converges weakly to a non-trivial measure at the critical temperature of
\beta=1
(see
[
20
]
). The multi-component version of this fact was treated in
[
19
]
and referred to as non-CLT in the restricted condition of the uniform case. Above Proposition
3.3
proposes the generalized version of non-CLT with respect to the magnetization vector.

3.3.
Metastability in the low temperature regime

Finally, we prove the exponential mixing time in the low temperature regime. The entropy of the system is maximum when half of the spins is
+1
and the other half is
-1
. For a sufficiently high temperature, the entropy would dominate the energy barrier, and the average magnetization would be zero. In contrast to this situation, in the low temperature regime, it is difficult to overcome the energy barrier, which mathematically speaking denotes the existence of at least two local minima of free energy. We show the existence of two local minima to conclude exponentially long mixing time.

Theorem 3.5
.

When
\beta>\beta_{cr}
, the Glauber dynamics for the multi-component Ising model has an exponential mixing time.

We remark that this result also coincides with
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
, which characterized the ground states of the multi-component Ising model with two groups of equal size.

4.
Preliminaries

In this section, we provide the prerequisite concepts and properties for our multi-component Ising model to achieve the main results. Before elaborating on the preliminaries, we explain some of the notations used in the paper as follows:

\bullet

We write
f(x)=O(g(x))
if there exists a positive real number
M
and a real number
x_{0}
such that
|f(x)|\leq Mg(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

\bullet

For any square matrix
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{A}^{t}
indicates the power of
t
, not the transpose of the matrix. We use
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
for the transposed matrix.

\bullet

The symbol
\circ
means the Hadamard product, namely, the element-wise product. Precisely,
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}=(A_{ij}B_{ij})_{i,j}
for matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
with the same dimension.

\bullet

Given two
k-
dimensional vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
, we write
\bm{a}\leq\bm{b}
when the inequality holds element-wisely, i.e.,
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Note that we consider the multi-component Ising model on
n
sites, when the sites are partitioned into
m
groups, or blocks,
G_{1},G_{2},\cdots,G_{m}
with the proportion
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{m}
, i.e.,
|G_{i}|=np_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,m
. The set of configurations
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{V}
is the state space and
\mu_{n}
is the Gibbs measure as given in (
2.1
). Additionally,
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
indicate the expectation and probability measure of the Glauber dynamics whose starting state is
\sigma
.

In Subsection
4.1
, we describe a grand coupling known as the
monotone coupling
and its contraction properties. We interpret such properties with the magnetization chains in Subsection
4.2
. Subsection
4.3
is devoted to analyzing variance bounds of the magnetization that are important to bound the order of mixing times. In Subsection
4.4
, we prove that once the magnetizations agree,
O(n\log n)
steps are sufficient to obtain full agreement at all temperatures, which will be primarily used at the critical temperature. Lastly, in Subsection
4.5
, we state a vital lemma about supermartingales that is applied in deriving the upper bound of mixing times.

4.1.
Monotone coupling

We illustrate the
\emph{monotonecoupling}
to construct Markov chains evolving together. Let
I
be a uniform random variable over the sites
V=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, and generate a uniform random variable
U
on
[0,1]
independent of
I
. Suppose
I\in G_{j}
. For each
\sigma\in\Omega
, determine the spin
S^{\sigma}
according to
I
and
U
by

S^{\sigma}=\begin{cases}+1,&0<U\leq r_{+}\left(\frac{\sum_{1\leq l\leq m}{k_{jl}M_{l}}-k_{jj}\sigma(I)}{n}\right),\\
-1,&r_{+}\left(\frac{\sum_{1\leq l\leq m}{k_{jl}M_{l}}-k_{jj}\sigma(I)}{n}\right)<U\leq 1,\end{cases}

and generate the next configuration as follows.

\sigma_{1}(v)=\begin{cases}\sigma(v),&v\neq I,\\
S^{\sigma},&v=I.\end{cases}

For a given pair of configurations
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, we name the two-dimensional grand coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\emph{monotonecoupling}
with starting states
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
. We write
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
to designate the case of
\sigma(v)\leq\sigma^{\prime}(v)
for all sites
v\in G
.

Remark 4.1
.

If the starting states satisfy
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
, then the monotone coupling maintains the monotonicity
\sigma_{t}\leq\sigma_{t}^{\prime}
for every
t\geq 0
by means of the definition.

We measure the distance between two configurations
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
via
Hamming distance
. The
i
th-
\emph{Hammingdistance}
equals to the number of sites where the two configurations disagree on the set
G_{i}
, i.e.,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{(i)},\sigma^{\prime(i)}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in G_{i}}|\sigma(v)-\sigma^{\prime}(v)|.

The monotone coupling shows a contraction of the mean Hamming distance as follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

For the monotone coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma^{\prime}_{t})
starting at
(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{\prime})
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(1)})\\
\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(2)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(m)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(m)})\\
\end{pmatrix}\leq\mathbf{A}^{t}\begin{pmatrix}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{0}^{(1)},\sigma_{0}^{\prime(1)})\\
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{0}^{(2)},\sigma_{0}^{\prime(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{0}^{(m)},\sigma_{0}^{\prime(m)})\\
\end{pmatrix},

where the matrix
\mathbf{A}
is defined by

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\frac{1}{n}+\beta K_{11}(p_{1}-\frac{1}{n})&\beta p_{1}K_{12}&\cdots&\beta p_{1}K_{1m}\\
\beta p_{2}K_{21}&1-\frac{1}{n}+\beta K_{22}(p_{2}-\frac{1}{n})&\cdots&\beta p_{2}K_{2m}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\beta p_{m}K_{m1}&\beta p_{m}K_{m2}&\cdots&1-\frac{1}{n}+\beta K_{mm}(p_{m}-\frac{1}{n})\end{pmatrix}.

With simpler notation, we can write

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{ij})=\left.\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{n}+\beta K_{ii}\left(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\right),&\text{for }i=j\\
\beta p_{i}K_{ij},&\text{for }i\neq j\end{cases}\right\},

and therefore

\mathbf{A}=(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbf{I}_{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\mathbf{B}-\frac{\beta}{n^{2}}\mathbf{C},

where
(\mathbf{B})_{ij}=p_{i}k_{ij}
and
\mathbf{C}:=\text{diag}\left(k_{11},\cdots,k_{mm}\right)
. Since
\mathbf{A}\leq(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbf{I}_{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\mathbf{B}
, we will often employ

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}:=(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbf{I}_{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\mathbf{B}

instead of
\mathbf{A}
in the aforementioned equation.

Proof.

We first show that the Lemma holds when
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma,{\sigma}^{\prime})=1
and at time
t=1
. We may assume
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
with
\sigma(v)=-1
,
\sigma^{\prime}(v)=1
for some vertex
v
, and
\sigma(w)=\sigma^{\prime}(w)
elsewhere
w\neq v
. Then, for
i=1,2,\cdots,m
,

\textnormal{dist}({\sigma_{1}}^{(i)},{\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}^{(i)})\leq\mathbbm{1}_{v\in A_{i}}(1-\mathbbm{1}_{I=v}+\mathbbm{1}_{I\neq v}\mathbbm{1}_{I\in A_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{i}})+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbbm{1}_{v\in A_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{I\in A_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{j}},

where

B_{i}=\left\{r_{+}\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma(w)\right)\leq U<r_{+}\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma^{\prime}(w)\right)\right\}.

Note that for
k=1,2,\cdots,m
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(B_{k}\big|\big\{I\in A_{i}

\displaystyle\big\}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh{\beta\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma^{\prime}(w)\right)}-\tanh{\beta\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma(w)\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh{\beta\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma(w)+2K_{ik}\right)}-\tanh{\beta\left(\sum_{w:w\sim I}K(I,w)\sigma(w)\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tanh{(\beta K_{ik})}\leq\beta K_{ik}.

Thus,

\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}({\sigma_{1}}^{(i)},{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}}^{(i)})\leq\mathbbm{1}_{v\in A_{i}}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n})\beta K_{ii}\right)+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbbm{1}_{v\in A_{j}}p_{i}\beta K_{ij}.

Now consider
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
with
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=k
for some
k>1
. There exist a sequence of configurations
\sigma^{0}:=\sigma,\sigma^{1},\cdots,\sigma^{k}:=\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{i},\sigma^{i+1})
=1. By the triangular inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{1}^{(i)},\sigma_{1}^{\prime(i)})\leq\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{(i)},\sigma^{\prime(i)})\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n})\beta K_{ii}\right)+\sum_{j\neq i}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{(j)},\sigma^{\prime(j)})p_{i}\beta K_{ij}.

This establishes Lemma
4.2
for
t=1
. Iterating completes the proof.
∎

Recall from
(
4.8
)
that
\mathbf{Q}
is a positive matrix. Thus, by the Perron–Frobenius theorem, there exists the largest eigenvalue
\rho_{n}>0
with the left eigenvector
{\bm{a}}^{\top}:=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m})>\mathbf{0}
with
\|{\bm{a}}\|_{1}=1
. Moreover, the left eigenvector of the largest eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}
is also
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
. Therefore,
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
only depends on
p_{i}
s and
k_{ij}
s. Now we define

(4.9)

\displaystyle\alpha:=\frac{1}{2n(1-\rho_{n})},\quad\beta_{cr}:=\frac{1}{\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq m}{a_{i}p_{i}k_{ij}}}.

We prove that
\alpha
and
\beta_{cr}
are independent of
n
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The critical inverse temperature
\beta_{cr}
is determined by the proportion of the groups and their interaction strengths, i.e.,
\beta_{cr}=\beta_{cr}(p_{i},k_{ij})
, and
\alpha
is a constant that depends on
\beta
but independent of
n
, i.e.,
\alpha=\alpha(p_{i},k_{ij},\beta)
. Moreover,
\beta<\beta_{cr}
is equivalent to
\rho_{n}<1
.

Proof.

Considering that
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
is independent of
n
,
\beta_{cr}
is independent of
n
and is a function on
p_{i}

(1\leq i\leq m)
, and
k_{ij}

(1\leq i,j\leq m)
directly follows from the definition. In addition,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2n\alpha}=\rho_{n}=\|\mathbf{Q}^{\top}{\bm{a}}\|_{1}=\left\|\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\mathbf{I}_{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\right){\bm{a}}\right\|_{1}=1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq m}a_{i}p_{i}k_{ij},

to obtain

\alpha=\frac{1}{2\left(1-\beta/\beta_{cr}\right)},

which shows that
\alpha
depends only on
\beta
,
p_{i}
s, and
k_{ij}
s. Finally, by the definition of
\beta_{cr}
,
\beta<\beta_{cr}
is equivalent to
\alpha>0
and
\rho_{n}<1
.
∎

We may denote the maximum and minimum element of
{\bm{a}}
by
a_{\text{max}}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{a_{i}}
and
a_{\text{min}}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq m}{a_{i}}
, that are independent of
n
. The following lemma is required to take advantage of the matrix part of Lemma
4.2
. We write
\bm{e}_{j}
for
j
th unit vector and
\mathbbm{1}
for all-ones vector on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}\|_{1}\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2},\quad\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}\leq\sqrt{p_{j}}\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

By the definition of the matrices, we get

\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\mathbf{I}_{n}+\frac{\beta}{n}\left((\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}k_{ij})_{ij}\right)=:\mathbf{X},

which means that the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is similar to the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{X}
. Since
\mathbf{X}
is symmetric,
\|\mathbf{X}\|_{2}=\rho_{n}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}=\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{X}\|_{2}^{t}\|\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{D}\mathbbm{1}\|_{2}=\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2},

where
\text{Tr}(\cdot)
is the trace of matrix. In a similar manner,

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{D})\leq\|\mathbf{X}^{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{X}\|_{2}^{t}\|\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\|\mathbf{D}^{-1}{\bm{s}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{D}\bm{e}_{j}\|_{2}=\sqrt{p_{j}}\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}.

∎

Combining Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.4
, we bound the expectation of distance with the initial value without the matrix form.

Lemma 4.5
.

For a monotone coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
starting at
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(i)})\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{(i)},\sigma^{\prime(i)}).

In addition,
\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(i)})\leq n\sqrt{p_{i}}\rho_{n}^{t}
.

Proof.

The first equation is directly derived from multiplying
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
on both sides of Lemma
4.2
. Since
\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(i)})\leq np_{i}
for all
i
, by multiplying
\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}
on both sides of Lemma
4.2
, we get

\mathbb{E}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},\sigma_{t}^{\prime(i)})\leq n\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}(p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{m})^{\top}\leq n\sqrt{p_{i}}\rho_{n}^{t}.

The last inequality comes from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

4.2.
Magnetization chain

Recall the definition of the magnetization
{\bm{S}}(\sigma)
from (
2.3
). We use the notation
{\bm{S}}_{t}={\bm{S}}(\sigma_{t})
and
\bm{M}_{t}=\bm{M}(\sigma_{t})
throughout this paper. We first show that the magnetization chain is a Markov chain. Indeed,
{\bm{S}}_{t+1}-{\bm{S}}_{t}
takes values in
\cup_{i=1}^{m}\{-\frac{2}{n}\bm{e}_{i},+\frac{2}{n}\bm{e}_{i},0\}
, where
\bm{e}_{i}
is the
i
th unit vector on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The process
{\bm{S}}_{t}=(S_{t}^{(1)},\cdots,S_{t}^{(m)})
is a Markov chain on the magnetization state space
\mathcal{S}
, where

\mathcal{S}^{(i)}:=\{-p_{i},-p_{i}+2/n,\cdots,p_{i}-2/n,p_{i}\},\quad\mathcal{S}:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{S}^{(i)}.

Proof.

Clearly, each of
S_{t}^{(i)}
can take a value in the set
\{-p_{i},-p_{i}+2/n,\cdots,p_{i}-2/n,p_{i}\}
. Furthermore, it is easy to examine that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{S}}_{t+1}={\bm{S}}_{t}+\frac{2}{n}\bm{e}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{i}\cdot\frac{(|G_{i}|-nS_{t}^{(i)})/2}{|G_{i}|}\cdot r_{+}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}k_{ij}S_{t}^{(j)}+k_{ii}\left(S_{t}^{(i)}+\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

Now, we define

(4.11)

\displaystyle X_{t}^{(i)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}S_{t}^{(j)}.

Then, we can rewrite the aforementioned equation (
4.10
) and obtain the rest of the equations in a similar way,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{S}}_{t+1}={\bm{S}}_{t}\pm\frac{2}{n}\bm{e}_{i}\right)=\frac{p_{i}\mp S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\cdot r_{\pm}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}\pm\frac{k_{ii}}{n}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{S}}_{t+1}={\bm{S}}_{t}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(p_{i}-\frac{p_{i}-S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\cdot r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+\frac{k_{ii}}{n}\right)-\frac{p_{i}+S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\cdot r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-\frac{k_{ii}}{n}\right)\right)}.

∎

Remark 4.7
.

Observing that

\left|X_{t}^{(i)}\pm\frac{k_{ii}}{n}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}+\frac{k_{ii}}{n}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}+\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{k_{ii}}=:C_{k},

and
C_{k}
is a constant independent of
n
, the derivative of
\tanh x
is bounded away from both
0
and
1
for every possible
x
in the state space. Thus, by the mean value theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big({\bm{S}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{S}}_{t}\pm\frac{2}{n}\bm{e}_{i}\Big)=\frac{p_{i}\mp S_{t}^{(i)}}{4}\cdot\left(1\pm\tanh(X_{t}^{(i)})\right)+O(n^{-1}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big({\bm{S}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{S}}_{t}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(p_{i}-\frac{p_{i}-S_{t}^{(i)}}{4}\cdot\left(1+\tanh(X_{t}^{(i)})\right)-\frac{p_{i}+S_{t}^{(i)}}{4}\cdot\left(1-\tanh(X_{t}^{(i)})\right)\right)}+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(\frac{p_{i}}{2}+\frac{S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\tanh(X_{t}^{(i)}))\right)}+O(n^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(\frac{S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\tanh(X_{t}^{(i)}))\right)}+O(n^{-1}).

Thus, we have that the remaining probability is uniformly bounded away from
0
and
1
.

Continuing the remark, we can also observe the drift of
S_{t}^{(i)}
. We define a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
generated by
S_{t}^{(1)},S_{t}^{(2)},\cdots,S_{t}^{(m)}
. By direct calculation, we obtain

(4.12)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}^{(i)}-S_{t}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]&=\frac{2}{n}\cdot\frac{p_{i}-S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\cdot r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii}\right)-\frac{2}{n}\cdot\frac{p_{i}+S_{t}^{(i)}}{2}\cdot r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\left(-S_{t}^{(i)}+f_{n}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})+g_{n}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})\right),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle f_{i}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=p_{i}\left(r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii}\right)-r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}\right)\right),

\displaystyle g_{i}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-S_{t}^{(i)}\left(r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii}\right)+r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}\right)-1\right).

Note that with the similar technique in Remark
4.7
,

(4.13)

\begin{split}r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii}\right)-r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}\right)&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh{\beta(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii})}+\tanh{\beta(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}})\right)\\
&=\tanh{\beta X_{t}^{(i)}}+O(n^{-1}),\\
r_{+}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii}\right)+r_{-}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}\right)&=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh{\beta(X_{t}^{(i)}+K_{ii})}-\tanh{\beta(X_{t}^{(i)}-K_{ii}})\right)\\
&=1+O(n^{-1}),\end{split}

to obtain the approximation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}^{(i)}-S_{t}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\sim\frac{1}{n}\left(-S_{t}^{(i)}+p_{i}\tanh{(\beta X_{t}^{(i)}})\right).

We will scrutinize this drift later.

Now we demonstrate the contraction properties in terms of the magnetization chain.

Lemma 4.8
.

For starting magnetizations
{\bm{s}}:=(s^{(1)},\cdots,s^{(m)})\geq{\bm{s}}^{\prime}:=(s^{\prime(1)},\cdots,s^{\prime(m)})
, the magnetization satisfies

0\leq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(1)}\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(2)}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(m)}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{t}\begin{pmatrix}s^{(1)}-s^{\prime(1)}\\
s^{(2)}-s^{\prime(2)}\\
\vdots\\
s^{(m)}-s^{\prime(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Let
(\sigma_{t},{\sigma^{\prime}}_{t})
be a monotone coupling starting from
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
with
\sigma\geq\sigma^{\prime}
, where the starting magnetizations satisfy
{\bm{S}}(\sigma)={\bm{s}}
and
{\bm{S}}(\sigma^{\prime})={\bm{s}}^{\prime}
. By monotonicity,
\sigma_{t}^{(i)}\geq{\sigma^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}
holds for all
i
and
t
. Therefore,
S_{t}^{(i)}-{S^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}=|S_{t}^{(i)}-{S^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}|=\frac{2}{n}\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},{\sigma^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)})\geq 0
, and by Lemma
4.2
,

0\leq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}S_{t}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(1)}\\
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}S_{t}^{(2)}-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}S_{t}^{(m)}-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}|S_{t}^{(1)}-S_{t}^{\prime(1)}|\\
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}|S_{t}^{(2)}-S_{t}^{\prime(2)}|\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}|S_{t}^{(m)}-S_{t}^{\prime(m)}|\\
\end{pmatrix}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{t}\begin{pmatrix}s^{(1)}-s^{\prime(1)}\\
s^{(2)}-s^{\prime(2)}\\
\vdots\\
s^{(m)}-s^{\prime(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The conclusion follows from Proposition
4.6
that
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}S_{t}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(i)}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(i)}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}S_{t}^{\prime(i)}
.
∎

Recall that the notation
\circ
indicates the Hadamard product, i.e., the element-wise product.

Proposition 4.9
.

For a monotone coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting at
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
with magnetizations
({\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\|{\bm{a}}\circ{{\bm{S}}}_{t}-{\bm{a}}\circ\mathbf{S^{\prime}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\|{\bm{a}}\circ\mathbf{s}-{\bm{a}}\circ\mathbf{s^{\prime}}\|_{1}.

Proof.

For any starting magnetizations
{\bm{s}}
and
{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
, there exists
{\bm{s}}_{0}:={\bm{s}}
,
{\bm{s}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{s}}_{k-1}
,
{\bm{s}}_{k}:={\bm{s}}^{\prime}
such that
{\bm{s}}_{i-1}-{\bm{s}}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}(s^{(i)}-s^{\prime(i)})
for
i=1,2,\cdots,k
. Then, we can consider monotone coupling
\sigma_{0,t},\sigma_{1,t},\cdots,\sigma_{k,t}
with the corresponding magnetization of the starting configuration as
{\bm{s}}_{0},{\bm{s}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{s}}_{k}
. Note that either
{\bm{s}}_{i-1}\leq{\bm{s}}_{i}
or
{\bm{s}}_{i-1}\geq{\bm{s}}_{i}
holds; therefore, we can apply Lemma
4.8
.
Let
{\bm{S}}_{i,t}
denote the magnetization with respect to
\sigma_{i,t}
for
i=0,1,\cdots,k
. Then, with the triangle inequality and Lemma
4.8
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\|{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}_{t}-{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}_{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma_{i-1},\sigma_{i}}\|{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}_{i-1,t}-{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}_{i,t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{{\bm{a}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}\bm{e}_{i}|s^{(i)}-s^{\prime(i)}|}=\rho_{n}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}|s^{(i)}-s^{\prime(i)}|=\rho_{n}^{t}\|{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{s}}-{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

∎

4.3.
Variance Bound

In this subsection, we analyze the variance bound of the magnetization. We start with the following lemma stated in
[
16
, Lemma 3.1.]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(\bm{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov chain taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}
when
\bm{Z}_{0}={\bm{s}}
. Suppose that there exist some
0<\kappa<1
such that for any starting states
{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\|{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\kappa^{t}\|{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

Then, for the
l^{2}
norm variance
v_{t}:=\sup_{{\bm{s}}_{0}}\mathrm{Var}_{{\bm{s}}_{0}}\bm{Z}_{t}
,

v_{t}\leq mv_{1}\cdot\min\{t,(1-\kappa^{2})^{-1}\}.

We employ this lemma to prove the upper bound on the variance of magnetization.

Proposition 4.11
.

When
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, for any starting state
{\bm{s}}
, we have
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}{{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}}(S_{t}^{(i)})}=O(n^{-1})
and
{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}=O(n^{-1})
. When
\beta=\beta_{cr}
, we have
{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}=O(t/n^{2})
.

Proof.

Note that the increments of
S_{1}^{(i)}
from
S^{(i)}
are bounded by
2/n
in absolute value for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}a_{i}S_{1}^{(i)}\leq a_{max}^{2}(2/n)^{2}}.

Recall that
\beta<\beta_{cr}
is equivalent to
\rho_{n}<1
. Thus, by Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
4.10
, we obtain

a_{min}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}(S_{t}^{(i)})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}(a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)})\leq m\frac{4a_{max}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\rho_{n}^{2}}=\frac{8\alpha ma_{max}^{2}}{n(1+\rho_{n})}\leq\frac{8\alpha ma_{max}^{2}}{n},

to have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}(S_{t}^{(i)})}=O(n^{-1})
. In addition,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}|S_{t}^{(i)}|+2\sum_{i<j}\text{Cov}(|S_{t}^{(i)}|,|S_{t}^{(ij}|)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}+2\sum_{i<j}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{s}S_{t}^{(j)}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}+\sum_{i<j}(\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}+\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(j)})=m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}=O(n^{-1}).

Moreover,
\beta=\beta_{cr}
implies
\rho_{n}=1
. Thus, again by Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
4.10
, we obtain

a_{min}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}(S_{t}^{(i)})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}(a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)})\leq m\frac{4a_{max}^{2}}{n^{2}}t=O\left(\frac{t}{n^{2}}\right).

and
\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}=O(t/n^{2})
in a similar way.
∎

Furthermore, we examine the expected number of positive and negative spins on subsets of
G_{i}
in the high temperature regime to establish the cutoff.

Proposition 4.12
.

When
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, for any
B\subseteq G_{i}
, and a chain
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting at
\sigma
, let us define

M_{t}(B):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in B}\sigma_{t}{(v)}.

Then the following hold:

(1)

|\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}M_{t}(B)|\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}
,

(2)

\mathrm{Var}_{\sigma}(M_{t}(B))=O(n)
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|M_{t}(B)|\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}+O(\sqrt{n})
,

(3)

For any starting magnetization
{\bm{s}}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}+O(n^{-1/2}),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\rho_{n}^{t}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{s}}+O(n^{-1/2}),

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad 0\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}S_{i}^{(i)}\leq\sqrt{p_{i}}\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{s^{(i)}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{1}
denote the configuration of all positive spins, and let
(\sigma_{t}^{\mathbf{1}},\sigma_{t}^{\mu})
be the monotone coupling with starting configuration
(\mathbf{1},\mu)
, where
\mu
is the stationary distribution. Then, by Lemma
4.5
and triangular inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}\left[M^{\mathbf{1}}_{t}(G_{i})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{1},\mu)}\left[|M^{\mathbf{1}}_{t}(G_{i})-M^{\mu}_{t}(G_{i})|\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[M^{\mu}_{t}(G_{i})\right]\leq n\sqrt{p_{i}}\rho_{n}^{t},

considering that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[M^{\mu}_{t}(G_{i})\right]=0
. Hence, by symmetry of vertices on set
G_{i}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}\left[M^{\mathbf{1}}_{t}(B)\right]\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}/\sqrt{p_{i}}
. Accordingly, for any starting state
\sigma
, by monotonicity, we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}M_{t}(B)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}M^{\mathbf{1}}_{t}(B)\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}
. Similarly, considering the configuration of all negative spins (denoted by
-\mathbf{1}
), we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}M_{t}(B)\geq-|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}
. Consequently,
|\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}M_{t}(B)|\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}
for any starting configuration
\sigma
.

For part (ii), because the spins are positively correlated (
[
13
]
), from Proposition
4.11
,

\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{v\in B}\sigma_{t}(v))\leq\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{v\in G_{i}}\sigma_{t}(v))\leq n^{2}\mathrm{Var}(S_{t}^{(i)})\leq cn,

to obtain
\mathrm{Var}(M_{t}(B))=O(n)
. Moreover, for the expectation of absolute value,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|M_{t}(B)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[|M_{t}(B)|^{2}\right]}=\sqrt{(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[M_{t}(B)])^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\sigma}(M_{t}(B))}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}M_{t}(B)|+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\sigma}(M_{t}(B))}\leq|B|\rho_{n}^{t}\sqrt{p_{i}}+O(\sqrt{n}).

For part (iii), consider the vector
\bm{x}:=\left(\frac{1-(-1)^{|G_{i}|}}{2}\right)_{i=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which is the least non-negative starting configuration on
G_{i}
and define the starting distribution
\nu
, which yields
\bm{x}
with probability
1/2
and
-{\bm{x}}
with probability
1/2
. For any starting state
{\bm{s}}\geq\mathbf{0}
, we apply
{\bm{s}}
and
\nu
on Lemma
4.8
. Note that
{\bm{s}}\geq{\bm{x}}\geq-{\bm{x}}
and by symmetry,
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}S_{t}^{(i)}=0
. Hence,

(4.14)

\displaystyle 0\leq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}S_{t}^{\prime(1)}\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(2)}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}S_{t}^{\prime(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(m)}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}S_{t}^{\prime(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(1)}\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}S_{t}^{(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{t}({\bm{s}}-{\bm{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{t}({\bm{s}}+{\bm{x}})=\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}.

Therefore,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}S_{t}^{(i)}\leq\|\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}\|_{1}\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}
by Lemma
4.4
. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{|S_{t}^{(i)}|}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{|S_{t}^{(i)}|^{2}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}})^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}}|+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}}+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}{S_{t}^{(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}+O(n^{-1/2}).

Moreover, by multiplying
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
on both sides of (
4.14
), we obtain

(4.15)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{t}\right]\leq\rho_{n}^{t}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{s}}.

Similarly, combining Proposition
4.11
and (
4.15
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}\|{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{|a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}|}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{|a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}})^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}{a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}{a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}}|+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{s}}{a_{i}S_{t}^{(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{t}\right]+O(n^{-1/2})\leq\rho_{n}^{t}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{s}}+O(n^{-1/2}).

Now, by multiplying
\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}
on both sides of (
4.14
), we obtain,

0\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}S_{i}^{(i)}\leq\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{t}{\bm{s}}\leq\sqrt{p_{i}}\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{s^{(i)}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}.

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

4.4.
Coupling after the same magnetization

We claim that once the magnetizations agree,
O(n\log n)
steps are sufficient to obtain full agreement at all temperatures. Note that we only require
O(n)
steps to have full agreement in the high temperature regime
(\beta<\beta_{cr})
which will be proved in Section
5
.

Lemma 4.13
.

For two configurations
\sigma
,
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfying
{\bm{S}}(\sigma)={\bm{S}}(\sigma^{\prime})
, there is a constant
c(\beta)
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau>c(\beta)n\log n)=0,

where
\tau:=\min\{t\geq 0:\sigma_{t}=\sigma^{\prime}_{t}\}
.

Proof.

We construct the coupling as follows. First, we take random variables
I
uniformly on the vertex set
V
and
U
uniformly on
[0,1]
, independently. We then suppose
I=i\in G_{j}
. For each
\sigma
, we define the spin
S^{I}
by

S^{I}=\begin{cases}+1,&0<U\leq r_{+}(\sum_{1\leq l\leq m}{K_{jl}M_{l}}-K_{jj}\sigma(I)),\\
-1,&r_{+}(\sum_{1\leq l\leq m}{K_{jl}M_{l}}-K_{jj}\sigma(I))<U\leq 1,\end{cases}

and determine the next configuration by

\sigma_{1}(v)=\begin{cases}\sigma(v),&v\neq I,\\
S^{I},&v=I,\end{cases}

Now, for
\sigma^{\prime}
, if
\sigma^{\prime}(I)=\sigma(I)
, we determine the configuration by

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}(v)=\begin{cases}\sigma(v),&v\neq I,\\
S^{I},&v=I,\end{cases}

and if
\sigma^{\prime}(I)\neq\sigma(I)
, we choose
I^{\prime}
uniformly on the set
\{i\in G_{j}:\sigma^{\prime}(i)=\sigma(I),~\sigma^{\prime}(i)\neq\sigma(i)\},

and determine the configuration by

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}(v)=\begin{cases}\sigma(v),&v\neq I^{\prime},\\
S^{I},&v=I^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Note that the site is chosen uniformly on
V
, and this is also the Glauber dynamics. Recall the
i
-th Hamming distance from (
4.7
) and define

d^{(i)}_{t}:=\textnormal{dist}(\sigma^{(i)}_{t},\sigma^{\prime(i)}_{t}),\quad d_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{d^{(i)}_{t}}.

As mentioned in Remark
4.7
,
r_{+}(s)
and
r_{-}(s)
are bounded below uniformly for
s
in the domain; hence, we have a constant
c_{1}(\beta)
such that for all possible
s
,
\min\{r_{+}(s),r_{-}(s)\}\geq c_{1}(\beta)
. Because
d^{(i)}
decreases by
-1
if
\sigma^{\prime}(I)\neq\sigma(I)
and
S^{I}=\sigma(I^{\prime})=\sigma^{\prime}(I)
, and otherwise remains the same, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d^{(i)}_{t+1}-d^{(i)}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq-c_{1}(\beta)\frac{d^{(i)}_{t}}{n},

to get
\mathbb{E}[d_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq-\left(1-\frac{c_{1}(\beta)}{n}\right)d_{t}
by summing from
i=1
to
i=m
. Hence
d_{t}\left(1-\frac{c_{1}(\beta)}{n}\right)^{-t}
is a non-negative supermartingale, which indicates that

\mathbb{E}[d_{t}]\leq\mathbb{E}[d_{0}]\left(1-\frac{c_{1}(\beta)}{n}\right)^{t}\leq\mathbb{E}[d_{0}]e^{-c_{1}(\beta)t/n}\leq ne^{-c_{1}(\beta)t/n}.

There exist a sufficiently large constant
c(\beta)
that makes the right-hand side less than
n^{-1}
when
t\geq c(\beta)n\log n
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau>c(\beta)n\log n)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(d_{c(\beta)n\log n}\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(d_{c(\beta)n\log n})<n^{-1}.

∎

4.5.
Upper bound of stopping time

In closing, the following lemma is helpful to show the upper bound of the mixing time in the high temperature regime (
\beta\leq\beta_{cr}
) because certain functions for magnetization satisfies supermartingale property.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a non-negative supermartingale with a stopping time
\tau
satisfying

(1)

W_{0}=k
,

(2)

|W_{t+1}-W_{t}|\leq B<\infty
for some constant
B
,

(3)

\mathrm{Var}(W_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t})\geq\sigma^{2}>0
on the event
\{\tau>t\}
.

Then, for
u>12B^{2}/\sigma^{2}
,
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\tau>u)\leq\frac{4k}{\sigma\sqrt{u}}
holds.

Remark 4.15
.

We apply this lemma when
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is determined by
W_{t}
. In this case, from the definition of conditional variance, the third condition is satisfied when
\mathbb{P}(W_{t+1}\neq W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t})
is uniformly bounded above on the event
\{\tau>t\}
, and
|W_{t+1}-W_{t}|
is bounded away from zero on the event
\{W_{t+1}\neq W_{t}\}
. Precisely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{t+1}\neq W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t})\cdot\min\{(W_{t+1}-W_{t})^{2}:W_{t+1}\neq W_{t}\}\geq\sigma^{2}>0\text{ on the event }\{\tau>t\}.

The proof can be found in
[
21
]
: however, we prove the Lemma for the self-containedness of the article.

Proof.

By Doob’s decomposition, we can write
W_{t}=M_{t}-A_{t}
with martingale
(M_{t})
with
M_{0}=W_{0}
and non-decreasing previsible
(A_{t})
with
A_{0}=0
. Then, the second condition gives

A_{t+1}-A_{t}=-\mathbb{E}[W_{t+1}-W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq B,

which implies that
M_{t+1}-M_{t}\leq 2B
. In addition, by the third condition, since
A_{t}
is previsible, on the event
\{\tau>t\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[M_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(M_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}])^{2}]|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(W_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[W_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}])^{2}]|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\mathrm{Var}[W_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\geq\sigma^{2}>0.

Now, for arbitrary fixed
h>2B
, we define the stopping time
T_{h}:=\min\{t:M_{t}\geq h\}\wedge\tau\wedge u.
Since
T_{h}
is bounded by
u
, we can apply the optional stopping theorem to get

(4.16)

\displaystyle k=\mathbb{E}[M_{T_{h}}]\geq h\mathbb{P}(M_{T_{h}}\geq h).

Now, we define a process by
V_{t}:=M_{t}^{2}-hM_{t}-\sigma^{2}t
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}-V_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[(M_{t+1}-M_{t})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]-\sigma^{2}\geq 0,

which means
V_{t\wedge\tau}
is a submartingale. Additionally, using the optional stopping theorem, we obtain

-kh\leq V_{0}\leq\mathbb{E}[V_{T_{h}}]=\mathbb{E}[M_{T_{h}}(M_{T_{h}}-h))]-\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[T_{h}].

Note that both
W_{t}
and
A_{t}
are non-negative, as well as
M_{t}
, which implies that
M_{T_{h}}(M_{T_{h}}-h)
is positive iff
M_{T_{h}}>h
. Hence, combining the fact that the increment of
M_{t}
is at most
2B
and (
4.16
), we bound the first term of the right-hand side by

\mathbb{E}[M_{T_{h}}(M_{T_{h}}-h))]\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{T_{h}}>h)(h+2B)(2B)\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{T_{h}}>h)\cdot 2h^{2}\leq 2kh,

which concludes that
\mathbb{E}[T_{h}]\leq 3kh/\sigma^{2}
; thus,
\mathbb{P}(T_{h}\geq u)\leq\frac{3kh}{\sigma^{2}u}
. Therefore, combining again with (
4.16
),

\mathbb{P}(\tau>u)\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{T_{h}}\geq h)+\mathbb{P}(T_{h}\geq u)\leq\frac{k}{h}+\frac{3kh}{\sigma^{2}u}.

Finally, we consider
h=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}u/3}
to obtain that

\mathbb{P}(\tau>u)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{3}k}{\sigma\sqrt{u}}<\frac{4k}{\sigma\sqrt{u}}.

Note that we can choose
h>2B
only when
u>12B^{2}/\sigma^{2}
.
∎

5.
Cutoff at High Temperature

In this section, we prove the cutoff phenomenon in the high temperature regime, when
\beta<\beta_{cr}
. Recall from (
4.9
) that
\alpha=\frac{1}{2n(1-\rho_{n})}=\frac{1}{2(1-\beta/\beta_{cr})}
, a constant independent of
n
, and define
t_{n}:=\alpha n\log n
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\cdots\leq p_{m}
.

5.1.
Upper Bound

We may restate the upper bound part of Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

When
\beta<\beta_{cr}
, then

\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d_{n}(t_{n}+\gamma n)=0.

To show the upper bound, we first construct a coupling of the dynamics so that the magnetizations agree after
t_{n}+O(n)
steps.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any two configurations
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, there is a coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma_{t}^{\prime})
with starting states
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
such that, if
\tau_{\text{mag}}:=\min\{t\geq 0:{\bm{S}}_{t}={\bm{S}}_{t}^{\prime}\}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{\text{mag}}>t_{n}+\gamma n)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\gamma}},

holds for sufficiently large
\gamma n
, where
C
is a constant that is independent of
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
and
n
.

Here, we use
“modified” monotone coupling
. To illustrate, we first define
modified matching
. For
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega
and their magnetization
{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, we suppose
s^{(i)}\geq s^{\prime(i)}
for some
1\leq i\leq m
, which means that the number of positive spin in
s^{(i)}
is more than or the same as that of in
s^{\prime(i)}
. We then match each positive spin in
s^{(i)}
with a positive spin in
s^{\prime(i)}
and match the remaining spins arbitrarily. In this manner, we can construct a bijection
f_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
, which is known as modified matching. We update the matched vertices together in the two chains by modified monotone coupling.

Proof.

Our strategy is as follows: initially, we run the monotone coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma^{\prime}_{t})
with starting states
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
until time
t_{n}
. We then define
Y_{t}:=\frac{n}{2}\|{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}_{t}-{\bm{a}}\circ{\bm{S}}^{\prime}_{t}\|_{1}
; hence, we have

(5.17)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}[Y_{t_{n}}]\leq\frac{n}{2}\rho_{n}^{\alpha n\log{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(2a_{i}p_{i})=cn\left(1-\frac{1}{2n\alpha}\right)^{\alpha n\log{n}}\leq cne^{-\frac{\log{n}}{2}}=c\sqrt{n},\end{split}

by Proposition
4.9
. Recall
d_{i,t}:=\textnormal{dist}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},{\sigma^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{n}{2}|S_{t}^{(i)}-{S^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}|
and let us define the stopping time

\tau_{0}:=\min\{t\geq 0:\max_{1\leq i\leq m}d_{i,t}\leq 1\}.

After time
t_{n}
, we will construct a coupling such that
(Y_{t})_{t_{n}\leq t<\tau_{0}}
with the stopping time
\tau_{0}
that satisfies the condition of the Lemma
4.14
. After time
\tau_{0}
, we run the modified monotone coupling.

Then, let us consider the time
t\in[t_{n},\tau_{0})
. We partition the sites by
L_{t}:=\cup_{i:d_{i,t}\leq 1}G_{i}
and
J_{t}:=\cup_{i:d_{i,t}>1}G_{i}
. Note that
J_{t}\neq\emptyset
for
t<\tau_{0}
. We choose a site uniformly over
V=L_{t}\dot{\cup}J_{t}
, where
\dot{\cup}
indicates a disjoint union. If a site is chosen in the set
L_{t}
, we run the modified monotone coupling, otherwise we run two chains independently. This is a coupling of the Glauber dynamics. Note that once
G_{i}
becomes a subset of
L_{t}
, it remains a subset of
L
. In addition, the sign of each
S_{t}^{(i)}-{S^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}
does not change if
t<\tau_{0}
.

Clearly,
Y_{t}
has bounded increments, and from Proposition
4.9
, because
\beta<\beta_{cr}
implies
\rho_{n}<1
, we have
(Y_{t})_{t_{n}\leq t<\tau_{0}}
as non-negative supermartingale. In addition, on
t\in[t_{n},\tau_{0})
,
J_{t}\neq\emptyset
; this implies
|J_{t}|\geq np_{1}
. If
I
, the uniform random variable over
V
, is chosen in the set
J_{t}
,
\sigma_{t+1}(I)
and
\sigma_{t+1}^{\prime}(I)
are chosen independently. Hence, the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}(Y_{t+1}\neq Y_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t})
is bounded away from zero uniformly. Therefore, by Lemma
4.14
, for sufficiently large
\gamma n
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{0}>t_{n}+\gamma n|\sigma_{t_{n}},\sigma_{t_{n}}^{\prime})\leq\tilde{c}\frac{Y_{t_{n}}}{\sqrt{\gamma n}},

for some positive constant
\tilde{c}
not depending on
n
. Taking expectation coupled with (
5.17
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{0}>t_{n}+\gamma n)\leq O(\gamma^{-1/2}).

Since
d_{i,\tau_{0}}
is at most one for all
i
, in this degree
Y_{\tau_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}d_{i,\tau_{0}}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}=1
. As previously mentioned, from time
\tau_{0}
onward, we run the modified monotone coupling to apply the modified version of Proposition
4.9
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{\text{mag}}>\tau_{0}+\gamma^{\prime}n|\sigma_{\tau_{0}},\sigma^{\prime}_{\tau_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left(Y_{\tau_{0}+\gamma^{\prime}n}\geq a_{\text{min}}|\sigma_{\tau_{0}},\sigma^{\prime}_{\tau_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left[Y_{\tau_{0}+\gamma^{\prime}n}|\sigma_{\tau_{0}},\sigma^{\prime}_{\tau_{0}}\right]/a_{\text{min}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}^{\gamma^{\prime}n}\cdot a_{\text{min}}^{-1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2n\alpha}\right)^{\gamma^{\prime}n}\cdot a_{\text{min}}^{-1}\leq e^{-\gamma^{\prime}/(2\alpha)}\cdot a_{\text{min}}^{-1}.

Accordingly,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{\text{mag}}>t_{n}+\gamma n+\gamma^{\prime}n)\leq O(\gamma^{-1/2})+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}/(2\alpha)}\cdot a_{\text{min}}^{-1}.

We conclude that for sufficiently large
\gamma
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{\text{mag}}>t_{n}+\gamma n)\leq O(\gamma^{-1/2}).

∎

We will run the Glauber dynamics starting from the set of “

Lemma 5.3

For any subset
\Omega_{0}\subseteq\Omega
and stationary distribution

\displaystyle d_{n}(t_{0}+t)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\Omega}\|\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\sigma_{t_{0}+t}\in\cdot)-\mu\|_{\textnormal{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma_{0}\in\Omega_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\sigma_{0}}(\sigma_{t}\in\cdot)-\mu\|_{\textnormal{TV}}+\max_{\sigma\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\sigma_{t_{0}}\notin\Omega_{0}).

\Omega_{0}:=\{\sigma\in\Omega:|S^{(i)}(\sigma)|\leq p_{i}/2,\quad i=1,2,\cdots,m\}.

\displaystyle d_{n}(\zeta n+t)\leq\max_{\sigma_{0}\in\Omega_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\sigma_{0}}(\sigma_{t}\in\cdot)-\mu\|_{\textnormal{TV}}+O(n^{-1}).

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Combining Lemma
5.5
and (

), we have

d_{n}(t+\zeta n)\leq\max_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{W}}_{t}\in\cdot)-\nu\|_{\textnormal{TV}}+O(n^{-1}).

For given
2m
-coordinate chains
{\bm{W}}_{t}
and
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}_{t}
starting from
{\bm{w}}
and
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
, we define the stopping times when the

th coordinates and the full chain coincide by

\tau_{i,c}:=\min\{t\geq 0:(U_{t}^{(i)},V_{t}^{(i)})=({U^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)},{V^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)})\},\quad\tau_{c}:=\min\{t\geq 0:{\bm{W}}_{t}={\bm{W}}^{\prime}_{t}\}.

It is well-known that (e.g
[
21
, Section 5.2]
),

\|\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{W}}_{t}\in\cdot)-\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}}^{\prime}}({\bm{W}}^{\prime}_{t}\in\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}},{\bm{w}}^{\prime}}(\tau_{c}>t),

follows; thus we obtain

\max_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{W}}_{t}\in\cdot)-\nu\|_{\textnormal{TV}}\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{C}\\
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{w}},{\bm{w}}^{\prime}}(\tau_{c}>t).

Hence, all we have to do is to bound the right-hand side. We define
t_{n}(\gamma):=t_{n}+\gamma n
and fix a good starting figuration
\bar{\sigma}\in\Omega_{0}
with the corresponding
2m
-coordinate chain
\mathbf{\bar{{\bm{w}}}}=(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{v}_{1},\cdots,\bar{u}_{m},\bar{v}_{m})\in\mathcal{C}
. We will construct a suitable coupling by two steps.

The first step is the magnetization coupling phase. By Lemma
5.2
, there exists a coupling
(\sigma_{t},\sigma_{t}^{\prime})
with starting configuration
(\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime})
such that for
H_{M}:=\{\tau_{\text{mag}}\leq t_{n}(\gamma)\}
,
\mathbb{P}(H_{M}^{c})\leq O(\gamma^{-1/2})
holds.

The next step is the
2m
-coordinate chain coupling phase. On the event

, we run the coupling defined on Lemma
5.6
for

, and on the event

, which is unlikely to occur for large

, we run two chains independently for

. To deal with the situation described on Lemma

, we define

H_{i}(t):=\{\sigma_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{A}_{i},{\sigma^{\prime}}_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\},\quad H_{i}:=\bigcap_{t_{n}(\gamma)\leq t\leq t_{n}(2\gamma)}H_{i}(t),\quad H_{G}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}H_{i},

\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{i}^{c})\leq\gamma O(n^{-1}),\quad i=1,2,\cdots,m.

\mathcal{G}_{i}:=\{v\in G_{i}:\bar{\sigma}(v)=+1\},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,m.

Note that

\displaystyle\{\sigma_{t}^{(i)}\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}\}\subset\{U_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\bar{u}_{i}-U_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16\}

\displaystyle\cup\{V_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16\}\cup\{\bar{v}_{i}-V_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16\},

, we have
\bar{u}_{i}\geq np_{i}/4
; thus,
U_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16

\bar{u}_{i}-U_{t}^{(i)}\geq 3np_{i}/16
. Again, this implies that
|M_{t}(\mathcal{G}_{i})|=\frac{1}{2}|\sum_{v\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\sigma_{t}(v)|=\frac{1}{2}|U_{t}^{(i)}-(\bar{u}_{i}-U_{t}^{(i)})|\geq np_{i}/16
. Similarly, we can obtain that
\bar{u}_{i}-U_{t}^{(i)}<np_{i}/16

|M_{t}(G_{i}/\mathcal{G}_{i})|\geq np_{i}/16
. Now, let us define

B^{*}:=\bigcup_{t_{n}(\gamma)\leq t\leq t_{n}(2\gamma)}\Big\{|M_{t}(\mathcal{G}_{i})|\geq np_{i}/16\Big\},\quad Y^{*}:=\sum_{t_{n}(\gamma)\leq t\leq t_{n}(2\gamma)}\mathbbm{1}_{|M_{t}(\mathcal{G}_{i})|\geq np_{i}/32}.

Since

has increments of 0, 1, or

, we have

. Accordingly, with Markov’s inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(B^{*})\leq\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(Y^{*}\geq np_{i}/32)\leq 32\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}[Y^{*}]/(np_{i}).

, so
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}[Y^{*}]=\gamma O(1)
to get
\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(B^{*})=\gamma O(n^{-1})
. Similar results hold for

and for

instead of

\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{i}^{c})\leq 8\gamma O(n^{-1})=\gamma O(n^{-1}),\quad i=1,2,\cdots,m.

\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{G}^{c})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{i}^{c})\leq\gamma O(n^{-1})

Meanwhile,

, to verify the claim. In conclusion,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{c}>t_{n}(2\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{c}>t_{n}(2\gamma)\cap H_{M}\cap H_{G})+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{M}^{c})+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{G}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau_{i,c}>t_{n}(2\gamma)\cap H_{M}\cap H_{G})+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{M}^{c})+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma^{\prime}}(H_{G}^{c})

\displaystyle=O(\gamma^{-1/2})+O(\gamma^{-1/2})+\gamma O(n^{-1})=O(\gamma^{-1/2})+\gamma O(n^{-1}),

to yield

d_{n}(t_{n}+(2\gamma+\zeta)n)\leq O(\gamma^{-1/2})+\gamma O(n^{-1})+O(n^{-1}).

By taking

and then

, the limit goes to zero.
∎

In order to verify the lower bound in the high temperature regime, we use the following lemma from

.

Lemma 5.7

Let
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

.

Theorem 5.8
.

When
\beta<\beta_{cr}
,

\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d_{n}(t_{n}-\gamma n)=1.

Proof.

Recall the drift of
{\bm{S}}_{t}
from (
4.12
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}^{(i)}-S_{t}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\left(-S_{t}^{(i)}+f_{n}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})+g_{n}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})\right),

and
X_{t}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{K_{ij}M_{t}^{(j)}}
with

\displaystyle f_{i}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})=p_{i}\left(\tanh{\beta X_{t}^{(i)}}+O(n^{-1})\right),\quad g_{i}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})=-S_{t}^{(i)}\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right).

Note that
\tanh{x}\geq x-\frac{x^{2}}{3}
implies
f_{n}({{\bm{S}}}_{t})\geq p_{i}\beta X_{t}^{(i)}-\frac{p_{i}\beta^{2}}{3}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}+O(n^{-1})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}^{(i)}-S_{t}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(-S_{t}^{(i)}+p_{i}\beta X_{t}^{(i)}-\frac{p_{i}\beta^{2}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}}{3}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

to rewrite,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)S_{t}^{(i)}+\frac{p_{i}\beta X_{t}^{(i)}}{n}-\frac{p_{i}\beta^{2}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}}{3n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Equivalently, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bm{S}}}_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{Q}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}+O(n^{-2}),

where
{\bm{x}}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{3n}\Big(p_{1}(X_{t}^{(1)})^{2},\cdots,p_{m}(X_{t}^{(m)})^{2}\Big)^{\top}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{n}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}-{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+O(n^{-2}).

Note that
{\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{3n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}}
. Let
k_{M}:=\max_{i,j}{k_{ij}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{M}|S_{t}^{(j)}|\right)^{2}}=k_{M}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}p_{i}\right)\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2}).

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\geq\rho_{n}{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3n}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2}).

By taking expectation, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t+1}\right]\geq\rho_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}\right]-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3n}\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2}).

By Proposition
4.11
and Proposition
4.12
-(3),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}^{2}=(\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Var}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1}=(\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{S}}_{t}\|_{1})^{2}+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}^{2t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}+2\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}O(n^{-1/2})+O(n^{-1}).

to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\bigg]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3n}\left(\rho_{n}^{2t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}+2\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}O(n^{-1/2})\right)+O(n^{-2}).

Let us define
Z_{t}:=\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{a}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t}}{\rho_{n}^{t}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle Z_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}Z_{t}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3n\rho_{n}^{t+1}}\left(\rho_{n}^{2t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}+2\rho_{n}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}O(n^{-1/2})\right)+\frac{1}{\rho_{n}^{t+1}}O(n^{-2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(n-1/(2\alpha))}\left(\rho_{n}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}+2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\right)^{1/2}O(n^{-1/2})\right)+\frac{1}{\rho_{n}^{t+1}}O(n^{-2}).

Summing up from
0
to
t-1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z_{t}-\mathbb{E}Z_{0}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(n-1/(2\alpha))}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\frac{1-\rho_{n}^{t}}{1-\rho_{n}}+t\cdot O(n^{-1/2})\right)+\frac{\rho_{n}^{-t}-1}{1-\rho_{n}}O(n^{-2}).

Consider the time
t_{*}:=t_{n}-2\gamma\alpha n
. Note that
\rho_{n}^{t_{*}}\geq\frac{e^{\gamma}}{n^{n/(2(n-(2\alpha)^{-1}))}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z_{t_{*}}-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}s_{i}}\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(1-(2\alpha n)^{-1})/(2\alpha)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}(1-\rho_{n}^{t_{*}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(n-(2\alpha)^{-1})}t_{*}\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)+\frac{\rho_{n}^{-t_{*}}-1}{1-g}O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(1-(2\alpha n)^{-1})/(2\alpha)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}\left(1-\frac{e^{\gamma}}{n^{n/2(n-(2\alpha)^{-1})}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3(n-(2\alpha)^{-1})}\left(n\log n-{2\gamma n}\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)+2\alpha\left(\frac{n^{n/(2(n-(2\alpha)^{-1}))}}{e^{\gamma}}-1\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

For every
\gamma
, the right-hand side converges to
-\frac{2\alpha\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let us define
c:=\frac{2\alpha\beta^{2}k_{M}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}p_{i}}}{3}
. We have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}s_{i}-c\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(s^{(i)})^{2}}{p_{i}}>0
for any
0<s^{(i)}<\frac{a_{i}p_{i}}{c}
. Let us fix one of the satisfying starting states
{\bm{s}}
and, for that value, there exists a positive constant
\epsilon
such that
\mathbb{E}Z_{t_{*}}>\epsilon
. Consequently, for sufficiently large
n
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}}({\bm{a}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{t_{*}})>\epsilon\rho_{n}^{t_{*}}\geq\epsilon\frac{e^{\gamma}}{n^{n/(2(n-(2\alpha)^{-1}))}}\geq\frac{\epsilon e^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Since
\mathrm{Var}({{\bm{a}}}^{\top}{{\bm{S}}}_{t_{*}})=O(n^{-1})
by Proposition
4.11
, combining with Lemma
5.7
, there exists some positive constant
c^{\prime}
such that

\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}d_{n}\left(t_{n}-{2\alpha\gamma n}\right)\geq\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}1-\frac{c^{\prime}}{\epsilon^{2}e^{2\gamma}}=1.

∎

6.
Mixing time at Critical Temperature

In this section, we prove
O(n^{3/2})
mixing time at the critical temperature, i.e.,
\beta=\beta_{cr}
. Recall the matrices
\mathbf{K},\mathbf{P},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B}
from (
2.2
). In addition, recall from Proposition
4.3
that
\beta=\beta_{cr}
implies that the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
\beta\mathbf{B}
is equal to 1. Since the matrix
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{D}^{2}\mathbf{K}
is similar to the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}
, the largest eigenvalue of
\beta\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}
is 1 as well. Since
\mathbf{K}
is positive definite, spectral decomposition implies there exists orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{V}
and diagonal matrix
\Lambda:=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{m-1},1)
such that

\displaystyle\beta\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m-1},1)\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\Lambda\mathbf{V}^{\top},

with the eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\lambda_{m-1}<\lambda_{m}=1
.

6.1.
Upper Bound

Theorem 6.1
.

When
\beta=\beta_{cr}
, we have
t_{\text{mix}}=O(n^{3/2})
.

Note that it takes
O(n\log n)
steps for two configurations to coincide after their magnetizations agree by Lemma
4.13
. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that the magnetization coincides in
O(n^{3/2})
steps. We first show that the magnetization on each group almost goes to zero in
O(n^{3/2})
steps.

Lemma 6.2
.

We define
\tau_{0}:=\min\{t\geq 0:\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\{|S_{t}^{(i)}|\}\leq 1/n\}
. Then there exist positive constant
c_{1}
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\tau_{0}>(c_{1}+\gamma)n^{3/2})=O(\gamma^{-1/2}).

Proof.

From now on, we discuss on the event
\{t<\tau_{0}\}
. Recall the drift of
{\bm{S}}_{t}
from (
4.12
) that

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)S_{t}^{(i)}+\frac{p_{i}}{n}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(i)})+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}).

Recall
X_{t}^{(i)}
from (
4.11
) to obtain
\mathrm{Var}[X_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{t+1}^{(i)}-X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=O(n^{-2})
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)X_{t}^{(i)}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})+O(n^{-2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{t+1}^{(i)})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}])^{2}+\mathrm{Var}[X_{t+1}^{(i)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}+\frac{2}{n}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})+O(n^{-2}).

We compute the drift of
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(X_{t}^{(i)}\right)^{2}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})+O(n^{-2}),

(6.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t+1}-\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=-\frac{2}{n}\Big(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big)+O(n^{-2}).

We take conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
on both sides of (
6.1
), and use tower property of conditional expectations to obtain

(6.21)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t+1}^{\top}\mathbf{P}&\mathbf{X}_{t+1}-\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\\
&=-\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]+\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+O(n^{-2}).\end{split}

Meanwhile, since the shift of
X^{(i)}
, i.e.,
X_{t+1}^{(i)}-X_{t}^{(i)}
is
O(n^{-1})
, the conditional variance of both
\mathrm{Var}(|X_{t}^{(i)}||\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
and
\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}||\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)
are
O(n^{-2})
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}{\bm{X}}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(X_{t}^{(i)})^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}||\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)^{2}+O(n^{-2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}||\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}||\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right]\right)^{2}+O(n^{-2})=\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)^{2}+O(n^{-2}),

which implies

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}{\bm{X}}_{t}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)^{2}+O(n^{-2}).

Now, we define

\eta_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)^{2}.

Then, we would observe the drift of
\eta
. Taking expectation on both sides of (
6.21
) by applying (
6.22
), we obtain

(6.23)

\eta_{t+1}-\eta_{t}=-\frac{2}{n}\eta_{t}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]+O(n^{-2}).

Now we estimate the second term of the right-hand side. By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for conditional expectations, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}X_{t}^{(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}^{(i)}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right],

(6.24)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{t}^{(i)}\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}||\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\\
&\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right)^{1/2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+O(n^{-1}).\end{split}

The last equality holds from the similar process of variance bound of
O(n^{-2})
.
By taking expectation on both sides of (
6.24
) with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

(6.25)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\bigg[X_{t}^{(i)}\tanh&(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})\bigg]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[|\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right]+O(n^{-1})\\
&\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[|\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}+O(n^{-1})\\
&=\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\cdot\mathbb{E}[|\tanh(\beta X_{t}^{(j)})|]+O(n^{-1}),\end{split}

and the last equality follows from
O(n^{-2})
variance bound.
We now combine (
6.23
) and the aforementioned estimation (
6.25
), to obtaine

\displaystyle\eta_{t+1}-\eta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2}{n}\eta_{t}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\tanh(|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]+O(n^{-2})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2}{n}\eta_{t}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\tanh\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]\right)+O(n^{-2}).

The last inequality comes from Jensen’s inequality since the
\tanh
function is concave on the positive axis. Now, we estimate the lower bound of the right-hand side. Suppose
\eta_{t}>mp\epsilon^{2}
for some
\epsilon>0
, where
p:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\{p_{i}\}
, which implies
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\geq\epsilon
(wlog, we may assume
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(l)}|\right]\geq\epsilon
); then, there exists a positive constant
c_{\epsilon}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\beta|X_{t}^{(l)}|\right]-\tanh\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\beta|X_{t}^{(l)}|\right]\right)\geq c_{\epsilon}.

Finally, we define a constant
C_{\epsilon}:=\epsilon c_{\epsilon}\cdot\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\{p_{i}^{2}k_{ii}\}.
By direct computation, we get

\displaystyle\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\tanh\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{il}p_{i}p_{l}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot c_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]+k_{ll}p_{l}p_{l}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(l)}|\right]\cdot c_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\beta k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(|X_{t}^{(j)}|)\right]+C_{\epsilon}.

We define the vector of expectation of the absolute value by

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}:=\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)_{1\leq i\leq m},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{t}:=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}.

Then, the right-hand side is

\displaystyle\eta_{t}-\beta\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{2}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}+C_{\epsilon}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{D}(I-\beta\mathbf{D}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D})\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}+C_{\epsilon}=\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\Lambda)\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{t}+C_{\epsilon}\geq C_{\epsilon},

since all diagonal element of
\Lambda
is less than or equal to 1. This means that if
\eta_{t}>\delta
, there exist a constant
C_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle\eta_{t+1}-\eta_{t}\leq-\frac{C_{\delta}}{n}+O(n^{-2}),

which implies that for any constant
\delta>0
, there exists a time
t_{*}=O(n)
such that
\eta_{t}\leq\delta
for all
t\geq t_{*}
. Since
\tanh{x}\leq x-\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{2x^{5}}{15}
for
x>0
, we have
\tanh{x}\leq x-\frac{x^{3}}{4}
when
x\leq 1/4
. Therefore, there exist
t_{*}=O(n)
such that for
t\geq t_{*},

\displaystyle\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\tanh\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{(i)}|\right]\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}[|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|]-\left(\mathbb{E}[|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|]\right)^{3}/4\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\left(\mathbb{E}[|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|]\right)^{3}/4.

We define

\xi:=\beta^{3}\cdot\min_{1\leq i,j\leq m}\{k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\}/4

to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\left(\mathbb{E}[|\beta X_{t}^{(j)}|]\right)^{3}/4\geq\xi\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(j)}|]\right)^{3}

\displaystyle\geq\xi\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(j)}|]\right)^{2}=\xi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}^{(i)}|]\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\xi}{p^{2}}\eta_{t}^{2},

that yields

\displaystyle\eta_{t+1}-\eta_{t}\leq-\frac{2\xi}{np^{2}}\eta_{t}^{2}+O(n^{-2}),

for
t\geq t_{*}
. We may rewrite this formula with a sufficiently large constant
C
and
\theta:=\frac{2\xi}{p^{2}}
as

\eta_{t+1}-\eta_{t}\leq-\frac{\theta}{n}\eta_{t}^{2}+\frac{C}{n^{2}},

which shows that for sufficiently large
n
,
\eta_{t}
is decreasing for
t\geq t_{*}
whereas
\eta_{t}\geq\sqrt{C/\theta}n^{-1/2}
. Consider the decreasing sequence
l_{i}:=\delta/2^{i}

(i=0,1,2,\cdots)
and define the time

\omega_{i}:=\min\{t\geq t_{*}:\eta_{t}\leq l_{i}\}.

Then, for
t\in(\omega_{i},\omega_{i+1}]
, we have
l_{i+1}\leq\eta_{t}<l_{i}
, thus,

\eta_{t+1}\leq\eta_{t}-\frac{\theta l_{i+1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{C}{n^{2}}=\eta_{t}-\frac{\theta l_{i}^{2}}{4n}+\frac{C}{n^{2}}.

Hence, we obtain

\omega_{i+1}-\omega_{i}\leq\frac{l_{i}/2}{\frac{\theta l_{i}^{2}}{4n}-\frac{C}{n^{2}}}=\frac{2n}{\theta l_{i}}\left(1+\frac{4C}{\theta nl_{i}^{2}-4C}\right).

We define

i_{*}:=\min\{i:l_{i}\leq c_{1}n^{-1/2}\},

for some constant
c_{1}\geq 2\sqrt{4C/\theta}
. Then, for sufficiently large
n
and
0\leq i<i_{*}
,

\omega_{i+1}-\omega_{i}\leq\frac{8n}{3\theta l_{i}}.

By summing up from
i=0
to
i=i_{*}-1
, we have

\omega_{i_{*}}-\omega_{0}\leq\frac{8n}{3\theta}\sum_{i=0}^{i_{*}-1}\frac{1}{l_{i}}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{n}{l_{i_{*}}}=O(n^{3/2}).

Therefore, we conclude

\omega_{i_{*}}\leq O(n)+O(n^{3/2})=O(n^{3/2}),

which implies that it takes
O(n^{3/2})
steps to obtain
\eta_{t}\leq c_{1}n^{-1/2}
. In other words, there exists a constant
\tilde{c}
such that
\eta_{t}\leq c_{1}n^{-1/2}
for
t\geq\tilde{c}n^{3/2}=:u_{n}
. Recall from (
6.22
) that
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}{\bm{X}}_{t}]=\eta_{t}+O(n^{-2})
, which implies that
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}{\bm{X}}_{t}]
has the same properties as
\eta_{t}
. Hence,
{\bm{X}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}{\bm{X}}_{t}
is non-negative supermartingale with bounded increment. Moreover, there exists a constant
q>0
such that
r_{+}(s)\wedge r_{-}(s)\geq q
, on the event
\{t<\tau_{0}\}
, there exists a constant
B
such that

\mathbb{E}[(n^{5/4}\mathbf{X}_{t+1}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t+1}-n^{5/4}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{t})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\geq\frac{B}{n^{3/2}}.

Therefore by Lemma
4.14
, if
\gamma
is sufficiently large,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\tau_{0}>u_{n}+\gamma n^{3/2}|\mathcal{F}_{u_{n}})\leq\frac{c_{1}|n^{5/4}\mathbf{X}_{u_{n}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{u_{n}}|}{\sqrt{\gamma n^{3/2}}}.

By taking expectation, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\tau_{0}>u_{n}+\gamma n^{3/2})\leq\frac{c_{1}n^{3/4}}{\sqrt{\gamma}n^{3/4}}=O(\gamma^{-1/2}).

to complete the proof.
∎

Note that at
t=\tau_{0}
, if the size of
G_{i}
is even, the magnetization
S^{(i)}_{t}
is zero, otherwise the magnetization
|S^{(i)}_{t}|=\frac{1}{n}
. Therefore, by running the modified monotone coupling after
\tau_{0}
, we obtain that the probability that two magnetization chains couple in additional
m
steps, i.e., in total
\tau_{0}+m
steps, is uniformly bounded away from zero. Therefore, we have
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\tau_{\text{mag}}\leq\bar{C}n^{3/2})\geq\bar{c}
for some positive constants
\bar{C}
and
\bar{c}
. By combining this with Lemma
4.13
, we conclude that there exist positive constants
C
and
c
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\tau_{c}\leq{C}n^{3/2})\geq{c},

to conclude that
t_{\text{mix}}=O(n^{3/2})
.

[-1,1]

) that the Gibbs measure is proportional to
\exp{\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}{k_{ij}M^{(i)}M^{(j)}}\right)}
. Therefore, by multiplying the entropy term, which is the number of possible states, we deduce that the distribution of
\vec{\chi}
is proportional to

\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\binom{np_{i}}{np_{i}(\chi_{i}+1)/2}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}\exp{\Big(n\beta k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}\Big)}.

Stirling’s formula implies that this is

\displaystyle\sim\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}

\displaystyle\exp{\Big(n\beta k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}\Big)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp{\left(-np_{i}\left(\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}+\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(n\left\{\beta\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}{k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}+\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\right)\right\}\right),

where
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
implies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{\log b_{n}}=1
. We define a function on
\vec{\chi}
by

f(\vec{\chi}):=\beta\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq m}{k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\left(\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{2}+\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\ln\frac{1-\chi_{i}}{2}\right),

which indicates free energy. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(\vec{\chi})}{\partial\chi_{i}}=\beta\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\chi_{j}-\frac{p_{i}}{2}\ln{\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{1-\chi_{i}}}.

We seek for
\vec{\chi}
such that
|\chi_{i}|\leq 1
and
\frac{\partial f(\vec{\chi})}{\partial\chi_{i}}=0
for every
1\leq i\leq m
, equivalently,

\beta\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{ij}p_{j}\chi_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+\chi_{i}}{1-\chi_{i}},

which is known as mean-field equation. We rewrite this equation in matrix form

\beta\vec{\chi}\mathbf{B}=(\tanh^{-1}\chi_{1},\tanh^{-1}\chi_{2},\cdots,\tanh^{-1}\chi_{m}).

Now, we define functions by

\displaystyle F(\vec{\chi}):=\beta\vec{\chi}\mathbf{B}-(\tanh^{-1}\chi_{1},\cdots,\tanh^{-1}\chi_{m}),\quad\tilde{F}(\gamma):=F(\gamma{\bm{a}}^{\top}).

Clearly, the function
F
is continuous on the domain
(-1,1)^{m}
. Note that
{\bm{a}}^{\top}
is the left eigenvector of the matrix
\mathbf{B}
with eigenvalue
1/\beta_{cr}
to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\beta/\beta_{cr}){\bm{a}}^{\top}-\left(\tanh^{-1}(\gamma a_{1}),\cdots,\tanh^{-1}(\gamma a_{m})\right).

Considering that
\beta/\beta_{cr}>1
, there exists a unique positive value
\eta<1
such that

\displaystyle\tanh^{-1}\eta=(\beta/\beta_{cr})\eta,\quad\begin{cases}\tanh^{-1}x<(\beta/\beta_{cr})x&\text{if }x<\eta,\\
\tanh^{-1}x>(\beta/\beta_{cr})x&\text{if }x>\eta,\end{cases}

Therefore, we derive

F\left(\frac{\eta}{a_{\text{max}}}{\bm{a}}^{\top}\right)=\tilde{F}\left(\frac{\eta}{a_{\text{max}}}\right)\geq\mathbf{0}.

Observing that
\lim_{\vec{\chi}\rightarrow\mathbf{1}}F(\vec{\chi})=-\mathbf{\infty}
and
F
is a continuous function, the intermediate value theorem implies that there exists an
m
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{0}<\vec{\chi}_{0}<\mathbf{1}
such that
F(\vec{\chi}_{0})=\mathbf{0}.
Clearly, we also have
F(-\vec{\chi}_{0})=\mathbf{0}.
Therefore, free energy function has two local minima. These metastable states result in exponentially slow mixing.
