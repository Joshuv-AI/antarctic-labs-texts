A CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM FOR COUNTING FUNCTIONS
RELATED TO SYMPLECTIC LATTICES AND BOUNDED SETS

Abstract


†

Keywords:
Space of lattices, symplectic group, exponential mixing

Abstract.
We use a method developed by Björklund and Gorodnik to show a central limit theorem (as
T
tends to
\infty
) for the counting functions
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
where
\Lambda
ranges over the space
{Y_{2d}}
of symplectic lattices in
{\bf R}^{2d}
(
d\geqslant 4
). Here
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Omega_{T}}}\right\}_{T}
is a certain family of bounded domains in
{\bf R}^{2d}
that can be tessellated by means of the action of a diagonal semigroup contained in
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
. In the process we obtain new
L^{p}
bounds on a certain height function on
{Y_{2d}}
originally introduced by Schmidt.

Introduction

The interplay between lattices and subsets of their ambient Euclidean space has always been a central topic in the geometry of numbers. For example, Minkowski’s classic lattice point theorem
[
7
, Theorem III.2.2]
gives conditions under which it is always possible to find non-zero lattice points in convex, centrally symmetric sets, and in the opposite direction the Minkowski-Hlawka theorem
[
7
, Corollary VI.3.2]
gives conditions on a symmetric set ensuring that at least some lattice will have to avoid it.

In 1945, Siegel generalized the Minkowski-Hlawka theorem in his seminal paper
[
17
]
and arguably created a kind of probabilistic geometry of numbers where questions about lattices and Euclidean sets can be studied from the perspective of a
random lattice
. Namely, if
\Omega\subset{\bf R}^{n}
is any measurable set, Siegels mean value theorem
[
17
]
says that on average, a unimodular lattice
\Lambda
of dimension
n
has
\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega)
non-zero points in common with
\Omega
. (Such a statement of course presupposes the existence of a measure on the set
X_{n}
of unimodular lattices in dimension
n
. See
Section 2
for more details.) In the same vein, Rogers
[
14
]
later generalized Siegel’s theorem and proved formulas for the first
k
moments (
k<n
) of the counting functions
\#(\Lambda\cap\Omega)
.

In light of Siegel’s theorem, a natural question is to what extent the function
\#(\Lambda\cap\Omega)
can vary around its mean as
\Omega
varies across some prescribed family of sets. In studying this problem, Schmidt employed Rogers’ moment formula and observed
[
16
]
that for almost all lattices (not necessarily unimodular), the counting functions
\#(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T})
(where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Omega_{T}}}\right\}
is an increasing family of sets whose volumes constitute an unbounded sequence) enjoy rather sharp bounds. Essentially, Schmidt proved
[
16
, Theorem 1]
that for almost all lattices,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)\cdot d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)=\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\frac{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)}{d(\Lambda)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right),\quad T\longrightarrow\infty,

where
d(\Lambda):=\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf R}^{n}/\Lambda}}\right)
denotes the covolume of
\Lambda
. (Schmidt’s result is slightly more general than this, but we simplified it for the sake of the present exposition.)

Continuing with the probabilistic perspective, we note that (
1.1
) can be understood as something between a
law of large numbers
(giving the main term) and a
law of the iterated logarithm
(giving an error term). Indeed, suppose for simplicity that we specialize to unimodular lattices and partition the set
\Omega_{T}
into
N=N(T)
pieces of equal volume
V
:
\Omega_{T}\approx S_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{N}
. By taking indicator functions of these sets, we then expect to have

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)\approx{\sum}_{m=1}^{N}\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap S_{m}}}\right).

If we assume that the individual counting functions
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap S_{m}}}\right)
are ”sufficiently (pairwise) independent” as random variables on the space of lattices, the classic law of large numbers and the law of the iterated logarithm imply that we can expect (almost surely)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{m=1}^{N}\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap S_{m}}}\right)=V+O_{\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}}}\right),\hskip 11.74988ptN\longrightarrow\infty,

and hence

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)={\sum}_{m=1}^{N}\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap S_{m}}}\right)=NV+O_{\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N^{1/2+\varepsilon}}}\right)=\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)+O_{\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)^{1/2+\varepsilon}}}\right)

as
T\longrightarrow\infty
, a statement quite similar to (
1.1
).

The fact that such a probabilistic interpretation of Schmidt’s theorem is possible motivates the question if any other classic probabilistic theorems have equivalents in terms of lattice point counting. This was answered in the affirmative by Björklund and Gorodnik who proved central limit theorems (CLT’s) in the case of unimodular lattices for the sequence
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
; in the first case with the family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Omega_{T}}}\right\}
being defined in terms of certain conditions coming from Diophantine approximation
[
5
]
, and in the second case for a different family of sets defined in terms of products of linear forms
[
6
]
. These results are in accordance with the fact that Schmidt’s result remains valid if one specializes to the nullset of all unimodular lattices, a fact that follows from Schmidt’s original proof.

In a recent paper
[
10
]
, Kelmer and Yu consider the even smaller set
{Y_{2d}}
of all symplectic lattices in
{\bf R}^{2d}
(see
Section 2
for the definition) and prove a symplectic version of (a special case of) the moment formula of Rogers
[
14
]
, proving a mean square bound for the discrepancy and verifying that Schmidt’s metrical bound continues to hold. Before the work of Kelmer–Yu, Athreya and Konstantoulas
[
1
]
also studied the case of symplectic lattices (in fact, the larger class of general symplectic lattices) and proved a Rogers-type second moment formula to obtain both metrical and mean square bounds as well. However, the methods used in
[
1
]
and
[
10
]
are very different.

A notable difference between the symplectic mean square bounds of
[
1
,
10
]
and the analogous bound in the case of unimodular lattices is that, in the case of unimodular lattices, Rogers’ formula gives an exact value for the second moment of the discrepancy. By contrast, an exact value for the second moment of the discrepancy is not known in the symplectic case. This is due to a number of technical obstacles that arise from the symplectic structure and render the moment formulas more complicated and less explicit. On the other hand, Siegel’s mean value theorem still holds on the space
{Y_{2d}}
, so the mean of the random variables
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
over
{Y_{2d}}
is known, and even equals the mean value taken over the larger space of unimodular lattices. In light of the results from
[
5
,
6
]
, these considerations motivate the study of the distribution of the counting functions
\Lambda\longmapsto\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
where
\Lambda
is a symplectic lattice chosen uniformly at random from
{Y_{2d}}
. In this article, our goal is to show that, just as in the special linear case discussed above, even in the symplectic case one has a central limit theorem for the sequence
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Omega_{T}}}\right\}
is a specific family of sets. We now describe this family in more detail.

Our concrete family of sets is given as

\displaystyle\Omega_{T}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\in{\bf R}^{d}\times{\bf R}^{d}:1\leqslant\|\textbf{x}\|\cdot\|\textbf{y}\|\leqslant 2\text{ and }1\leqslant\|\textbf{y}\|<T}}\right\},

where we emphasize that the bounds on
\|\textbf{x}\|\cdot\|\textbf{y}\|
are completely arbitrary and could be replaced with any positive real numbers. We note that ”such sets” appear in a natural way when studying statistical properties of Diophantine approximation, cf.
[
5
]
. However, we make no use of any special properties of this family other than the following facts, valid for every
T>0
:

1)

\Omega_{T}
admits an approximate tessellation of the form
\Omega_{T}\approx a\Omega_{2}\cup a^{2}\Omega_{2}\cup\cdots\cup a^{m(T)}\Omega_{2}
where
a
is a symplectic diagonal matrix, and
m(T)
is a suitable integer depending on
T
. In particular, for
T=2^{N}
(
N\geqslant 1
an integer),
\Omega_{T}
coincides exactly with such a union;

2)

\Omega_{T}
is symmetric in the sense that
-\Omega_{T}=\Omega_{T}
and does not meet arbitrarily small neighbourhoods of
0
. (The symmetry condition facilitates the use of the symplectic version of Rogers’ formula
[
10
, Theorem 1]
.)

Given this family, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
d\geqslant 4
, and let
\Lambda\in{Y_{2d}}
be distributed according to the probability measure
\mu
coming from the restriction of Haar measure on
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
(see
Section 2
). Then, there exists
\sigma\geqslant 0
such that as
T\longrightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T})-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)^{1/2}}\Longrightarrow N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sigma^{2}}}\right).

Remarks.

1) We suspect that this result also holds in the cases
d=2,3
. However, we are unable to prove it due to technical limitations of our methods: In the case
d=2
, the
L^{2}
bound in Lemma
5.6
becomes trivial, and we lose control of the error term in approximating the Siegel transform
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
(see
Section 2
) with a smooth, compactly supported function on
{Y_{2d}}
; in the case
d=3
, the inequality (
5.37
) is not satisfied for
r\geqslant 4
, and we are thus unable to show that the cumulants of order
4
and higher vanish as
T\longrightarrow\infty
, which is required due to the CLT criterion of Fréchet and Shohat (see below). The case
d=1
is obscured by the fact that the counting function
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
is not square integrable in this case.

2) We believe that the limiting variance is positive, but have not been able to prove any such result. This matter is intimately connected with subtle properties of an
L^{2}
-isometry
\iota
that appears in Kelmer and Yu’s symplectic version
[
10
, Thm. 1]
of Rogers’ theorem. See
Section
5 for more details.

3) If
\Lambda
varies over the larger space of
all unimodular lattices
of dimension
d\geqslant 4
, we are able to prove that the sequence
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)
satisfies a CLT with a strictly positive variance. This contrast to the symplectic case has to do with the fact that the
L^{2}
isometry that appears in Rogers’ theorem is very simple and explicit compared to
\iota
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
we follow the arguments in
[
5
]
closely. Our strategy for proving the theorem for a general
T
is to first prove it for
T=2^{N}
and then show that this special case implies the theorem in its full generality. To deal with the special case
T=2^{N}
, we employ the
method of cumulants
(Theorem
2.3
), which is a CLT criterion for sequences of bounded functions due to Fréchet and Shohat. However, we cannot apply Theorem
2.3
directly as the functions

\displaystyle\Lambda\longmapsto\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}})-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)^{1/2}}\quad\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\geqslant 1}}\right)

are unbounded on
{Y_{2d}}
. To remedy this, we exploit the fact that thanks to the tessellation properties of the family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Omega_{T}}}\right\}
, the statement of Theorem
1.1
can be given, for
T=2^{N}
, in terms of
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
-translates of the Siegel transform
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
of the indicator function
\chi_{2}
of
\Omega_{2}
, and the fact that this function can be approximated by a family of smooth and bounded functions on the space
{Y_{2d}}
. Concretely, if
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({Y_{2d}})
and
a\in G
are fixed, and

(1.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{m}:=\phi\circ a^{m}-\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad\quad F_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m},

we first prove that the sequence
\{F_{N}\}
satisfies a central limit theorem. This result is given in Theorem
4.1
, and we prove it in
Section 4
by appealing to Theorem
2.3
as
F_{N}

is
bounded for each
N
. Specifically, we use a quantitative correlation estimate
[
3
, Thm. 1.1]
due to Björklund, Einsiedler, and Gorodnik in combination with a combinatorial technique developed by Björklund and Gorodnik in
[
4
]
in order to analyse the cumulants
\mathrm{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{N}}}\right)
and prove that they vanish when
N\longrightarrow\infty
in accordance with the hypotheses of Theorem
2.3
. In terms of the probabilistic heuristic about Schmidt’s result above, this correlation estimate expresses the ”sufficient independence” required of the counting functions corresponding to different tiles in the tessellation of
\Omega_{2^{N}}
.

In
Section 5
we will then construct a smooth, compactly supported function
\phi
which approximates
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
, which will allow us to extend the central limit theorem proved in
Section 4
to our case of interest. In the case of general unimodular lattices considered in
[
5
]
, this approximation makes use of integrability properties of a certain height function introduced by Schmidt
[
15
]
, namely

\displaystyle\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right):=\sup_{V}\bigg\{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap\Lambda}}\right)^{-1}:V\cap\Lambda\text{ }\mathrm{is}\text{ }\mathrm{a}\text{ }\mathrm{lattice}\text{ }\mathrm{in}\text{ }V\bigg\},

where
V
runs over all non-zero subspaces of the ambient Euclidean space of
\Lambda
. However, at a first glance it is not obvious whether these properties continue to hold when one considers this height function on
{Y_{2d}}
. In order to adapt this method to the symplectic case, we therefore also prove the following theorem which we show is optimal.

Theorem 1.2
.

The function
\alpha
belongs to
L^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{2d}}}\right)
for
p\leqslant d
. Consequently, for any such
p
and
L>0
one has the estimate

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Lambda\in{Y_{2d}}:\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)\geqslant L}}\right\}}}\right)\ll_{p}L^{-p}.

By taking
V
to be a one-dimensional subspace in the definition of
\alpha
, we see that
\alpha(\Lambda)
is at least equal to the inverse length of the shortest non-zero vector in
\Lambda
. Mahler’s compactness theorem
[
7
, Chap. V, Thm. IV]
therefore implies that the preimage of any compact subset of
{Y_{2d}}
under the height function
\alpha
is, itself, compact. The
L^{p}
bounds provided by Theorem
1.2
therefore give us as much control over this function as one could hope for. Since the Siegel transform
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
that we wish to truncate is bounded when
\alpha
is small, the fact that
\alpha
is only large on a small subset of
{Y_{2d}}
is a key ingredient in constructing the approximation
\phi
that allows us to reduce Theorem
1.1
to Theorem
4.1
. We prove Theorem
1.2
in
Section 3
.

Preliminaries

We let
J=J_{2d}
be the standard skew-symmetric matrix

\displaystyle J_{2d}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}0&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0\end{matrix}}}\right)

where
I_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix. Any
2d\times 2d
matrix
A
that satisfies
A^{\intercal}JA=J
is called a
symplectic
matrix. We will denote by
G=\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
the group of all real symplectic matrices of dimension
2d\times 2d
, or the
real symplectic group
. (Some authors use a different skew-symmetric reference matrix than
J
to define symplecticity. The group
G
is independent of this choice.)

In the usual way,
J
gives rise to an anti-symmetric bilinear form on
{\bf R}^{2d}
given by
\omega(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=\textbf{x}^{\intercal}J\textbf{y}
. If
\Lambda\subset{\bf R}^{2d}
is a unimodular lattice, we say
\Lambda
is
symplectic
if the restriction of
\omega
to
\Lambda\times\Lambda
takes values in
{\bf Z}
. Denoting the space of all symplectic
2d
-dimensional lattices by
Y_{2d}
, one can show that the group
G
acts transitively on
Y_{2d}
with stabilizer
\Gamma=\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})
(see Proposition
2.4
). Consequently, the space
Y_{2d}
can be realized as the coset space
G/\Gamma
. As both
G
and
\Gamma
are unimodular groups, the quotient inherits a
G
-invariant measure
\mu
from the Haar measure on
G
. Moreover, this measure is finite (see e.g.
[
12
]
), so by normalizing it to ensure
\mu({Y_{2d}})=1
we can realize the set of symplectic lattices as a probability space.

In the following we take
T=2^{N}
with
N\geqslant 1
an integer. As mentioned in the introduction, and as will be proved in
Section 5
, this comes at no loss of generality. The advantage of specializing to the subsequence
2^{N}
is that by doing so, we can conveniently tessellate the set
\Omega_{T}=\Omega_{2^{N}}
and exploit this to describe the function
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)=\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)
as a sum of
Siegel transforms
.

Reformulation in Terms of the Siegel Transform

We now define the Siegel transform of a compactly supported function on Euclidean space
{\bf R}^{n}
and show that the function
\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)
may be expressed in terms of the Siegel transform
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
of the characteristic function
\chi_{2}
of
\Omega_{2}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f:{\bf R}^{n}\longrightarrow{\bf R}
be a measurable function of compact support. Its Siegel transform is the function
\widehat{f}
on the space
X_{n}
of unimodular lattices in
{\bf R}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}:X_{n}\longrightarrow{\bf R},\quad\quad\widehat{f}(\Lambda)={\sum}_{\textbf{v}\in\Lambda\atop\textbf{v}\neq 0}f(\textbf{v}).

We note that the condition on the support of
f
ensures that the function
\widehat{f}
takes finite values and so is well-defined. However, even if
f
is a bounded function, its Siegel transform
\widehat{f}
is typically not bounded on
X_{n}
. We will return to this matter later.

If
\chi_{T}
denotes the indicator function of the set
\Omega_{T}
, we now have

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)=\widehat{\chi_{2^{N}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right).

Furthermore,
\Omega_{2^{N}}
can be tessellated by means of
G
-translations of the set
\Omega_{2}
. More specifically, for
t>0
we let

\displaystyle a_{t}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}e^{t}I_{d}&0\\
0&e^{-t}I_{d}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in G,

so that with
b=a_{\log 2}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Omega_{2^{N}}={\bigsqcup}_{m=0}^{N-1}b^{-m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2}}}\right).

Taking the Siegel transform of the indicator functions of both sides, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi_{2^{N}}}(\Lambda)={\sum}_{\textbf{v}\in\Lambda\atop\textbf{v}\neq 0}\chi_{2^{N}}(\textbf{v})={\sum}_{\textbf{v}\in\Lambda\atop\textbf{v}\neq 0}{\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\chi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b^{m}(\textbf{v})}}\right)={\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}{\sum}_{\textbf{v}\in\Lambda\atop\textbf{v}\neq 0}\chi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b^{m}(\textbf{v})}}\right)={\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\widehat{\chi_{2}}\circ b^{m}.

By the symplectic version of Siegel’s mean value theorem
[
13
, Theorem 2]
and the
G
-invariance of
\mu
, we now obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)=\int_{{Y_{2d}}}{\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\widehat{\chi_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b^{m}(\Lambda)}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu(\Lambda)={\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2}}}\right)=N\cdot\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2}}}\right).

It follows that, for
T=2^{N}
, the convergence in distribution claimed in Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\widehat{\chi_{2}}\circ b^{m}-N\cdot\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2}}}\right)}}\right)\Longrightarrow N(0,c^{2}\sigma^{2})

for
c=\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2}}}\right)^{1/2}
. Hence, our main objective now is to prove (
2.2
).

Cumulants and the Criterion of Fréchet and Shohat

In order to prove (
2.2
), we use a CLT criterion due to Fréchet and Shohat. First, we need to introduce the notion of a
cumulant.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X,\nu)
be a probability space. For bounded and measurable functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
on
X
, their joint cumulant of order
r
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}}\right)={\sum}_{\mathcal{P}}(-1)^{\#\mathcal{P}-1}(\#\mathcal{P}-1)!\prod_{I\in\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.42271pt\int_{X}\prod_{i\in I}f_{i}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\nu,

where the first sum is taken over the set of all partitions
\mathcal{P}
of the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a partition of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,r}}\right\}
, the conditional joint cumulant of order
r
of the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\mid\mathcal{Q}}}\right)={\sum}_{\mathcal{P}}(-1)^{\#\mathcal{P}-1}(\#\mathcal{P}-1)!\prod_{I\in\mathcal{P}}\prod_{J\in\mathcal{Q}}\hskip 1.42271pt\int_{X}\prod_{i\in I\cap J}f_{i}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\nu

where
\mathcal{P}
ranges over all partitions of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,r}}\right\}
.

Finally, if
f
is a bounded and measurable function on
X
, we let

\displaystyle\operatorname{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)=\operatorname{cum}_{r}(f,\ldots,f).

Remark.
It is not hard to see that
\operatorname{cum}_{r}
is an
r
-linear functional on the space of bounded, measurable functions on
X
. (See e.g.
[
18
, Prop. 4.2]
.)

With this definition in place, we can state the criterion of Fréchet and Shohat.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
(X,\nu)
be a probability space. Let
\{F_{T}\}
be a sequence of real-valued bounded measurable functions on
X
such that

\displaystyle\int_{X}F_{T}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\nu=0,\quad\quad\sigma^{2}:=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{X}F_{T}^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty.

Suppose that for all
r\geqslant 3
,
\operatorname{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{T}}}\right)\longrightarrow 0
as
T\longrightarrow\infty
. Then, for every
\xi\in{\bf R}
,

\displaystyle\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{F_{T}<\xi\}}}\right)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}t

as
T\longrightarrow\infty
.

The Symplectic Group and Symplectic Lattices

In this subsection we study certain Lie theoretic aspects of the group
G
and its relationship to the space of all symplectic lattices. Specifically, in order to be able to introduce an important family of Sobolev norms on the space
C_{c}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({Y_{2d}}}}\right)
of compactly supported, smooth functions on
{Y_{2d}}
in the next subsection, we will examine the Riemannian structure on
G
and the differential action of its Lie algebra on
C^{\infty}(G)
. We begin by proving that the space of symplectic lattices
Y
coincides with the quotient
G/\Gamma
, justifying a claim that was made earlier.

Proposition 2.4
.

The natural action of
G
on
{Y_{2d}}
is transitive with stabilizer
\Gamma
. Consequently,
{Y_{2d}}
may be identified with
G/\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be a symplectic lattice. If
\textbf{v}\in\Lambda
is non-zero, the set
\omega(\textbf{v},\Lambda)\subset{\bf Z}
is a non-zero ideal, hence equal to
n_{\textbf{v}}{\bf Z}
for some positive integer
n_{\textbf{v}}
. Choose now a
\textbf{v}\in\Lambda
that minimizes
n_{\textbf{v}}
. Then, since
\Lambda
is discrete, there is necessarily some
\textbf{w}\in\Lambda
such that
\omega(\textbf{v},\textbf{w})=n_{\textbf{v}}
. By the choice of
v
and
w
, we see that these vectors span the sublattice

\displaystyle\Pi:=\mathrm{span}_{{\bf R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{v},\textbf{w}}}\right\}\cap\Lambda.

Indeed, clearly the integer span of
v
and
w
is a sublattice of
\Pi
. Furthermore, if
\Pi
is not contained in
\mathrm{span}_{{\bf Z}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{v},\textbf{w}}}\right\}
, then
\Pi
must contain a rational, non-integer multiple of either
v
or
w
, meaning that one of
v
and
w
is not a primitive lattice point of
\Lambda
. As this is impossible because of the minimality of
n_{\textbf{v}}
, the claim follows. We now let
\textbf{e}_{1}=\textbf{v}
,
\textbf{f}_{1}=\textbf{w}
, and
n_{1}=n_{\textbf{v}}
. We can then complete
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1}}}\right\}
to a basis
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1},\textbf{g}_{3},\ldots,\textbf{g}_{2n}}}\right\}
of
\Lambda
. It will be clear later that we in fact have
n_{1}=1
, so we will now proceed by ensuring orthogonality between
\textbf{e}_{1}
(resp.
\textbf{f}_{1}
) and the remaining basis vectors. To this end, note that all the integers
\omega(\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{g}_{i})
and
\omega(\textbf{g}_{i},\textbf{f})
(
i=3,\ldots,2n
) have to be divisible by
n_{1}
due to the minimality of
n_{1}
. This means that we can obtain a (possibly) new lattice vector by
replacing
\textbf{g}_{i}
with the integer combination

\displaystyle\textbf{g}_{i}^{\prime}:=\textbf{g}_{i}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\omega(\textbf{g}_{i},\textbf{f}_{1})\textbf{e}_{1}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\omega(\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{g}_{i})\textbf{f}_{1}.

Since the map taking
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1},\textbf{g}_{3},\ldots,\textbf{g}_{2n}}}\right\}
to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1},\textbf{g}^{\prime}_{3},\ldots,\textbf{g}^{\prime}_{2n}}}\right\}
has determinant
1
, we see that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1},\textbf{g}_{3}^{\prime},\ldots,\textbf{g}_{2n}^{\prime}}}\right\}
is a basis for
\Lambda
. Moreover, the construction ensures that
\omega(\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{g}_{i}^{\prime})=\omega(\textbf{g}_{i}^{\prime},\textbf{f}_{1})=0
for all
i
.

Continuing inductively with
\Lambda/\mathrm{span}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\textbf{f}_{1}}}\right\}
in place of
\Lambda
, and so on, we thus obtain a set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{e}_{n},\textbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{f}_{n}}}\right\}
of lattice vectors. The fact that each pair of lattice vectors forms a basis for the sublattice of
\Lambda
containing their integer span means that
\textbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{e}_{n},\textbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{f}_{n}
form a basis for
\Lambda
. We now let

\displaystyle M=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\textbf{e}_{n}&\textbf{f}_{1}&\cdots&\textbf{f}_{n}\end{matrix}}}\right],

which has determinant
\pm 1
. By construction, the matrix
M^{\intercal}JM
has the form
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&N\\
-N&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
with

\displaystyle N=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}n_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&n_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&n_{d}\end{matrix}}}\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\pm 1=\det M=\pm\prod_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}^{2}.

Therefore, as all the
n_{i}
are positive integers, we have
n_{i}=1
for all
i
. Therefore we even have
M^{\intercal}JM=J
, which means that our matrix

is symplectic.

It remains to show that the stabilizer of
{\bf Z}^{2d}
is
\Gamma
. However, since we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{stab}_{\mathrm{SL}(2d,{\bf R})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right)=\mathrm{SL}(2d,{\bf Z}),

we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{stab}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right)=\mathrm{stab}_{\mathrm{SL}(2d,{\bf R})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right)\cap G=\Gamma.

This completes the proof.
∎

The Lie group

carries a right-invariant Riemannian metric that descends onto the quotient space

. Moreover, since

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be the norm on

coming from the Riemannian metric on

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|gXg^{-1}}}\right\|:X\in\mathfrak{g},\,\|X\|=1}}\right\}.

Our operator norm of

is therefore the usual operator norm of the adjoint map
\mathrm{Ad}(g):\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
. It is not hard to see that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is submultiplicative. In what follows, however, we also need to be able to estimate
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
from below.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any non-zero
t\in{\bf R}
there exists
\lambda=\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,d}}\right)>1
such that for any positive integer
m
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a_{t}^{m}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geqslant\lambda^{m}.

Proof.

It is a standard fact that the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
consists of all matrices
X
such that
X^{\intercal}J+JX=0
where
J
is the standard skew-symmetric matrix. Thus, with

\displaystyle E=\begin{pmatrix}0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}0&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&0\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr 0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&0\end{pmatrix},

where all blocks have size
d\times d
, and the upper rightmost block only has non-zero entries in the corners on the antidiagonal, the matrices
E
and
E^{\intercal}
are elements of
\mathfrak{g}
and eigenvectors of
\mathrm{Ad}(a_{t})
with respective eigenvalues
e^{2t}
and
e^{-2t}
. If
t>0
, let
\lambda=e^{2t}>1
and let
F=E/\|E\|
, and if
t<0
, let
\lambda=e^{-2t}>1
and

. Then, if

is any positive integer, we see that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a_{t}^{m}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathrm{Ad}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{t}^{m}}}\right)F}}\right\|=\lambda^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|F}}\right\|=\lambda^{m}>1,

and the claim follows.
∎

There is an action of

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots Y_{r}^{e_{r}}}}\right).\phi:=\mathcal{D}_{Y_{1}}^{e_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{D}_{Y_{r}}^{e_{r}}\phi:=\mathcal{D}_{Y}\phi

Bounds on the Height Function
\alpha

In this second preliminaries section, we will investigate the height function
\alpha
mentioned in the introduction that plays a key role in our construction of a smooth function that approximates the Siegel transform
\widehat{\chi_{2}}
.

As we mentioned earlier, the Siegel transform of a bounded function
f
on
{\bf R}^{2d}
will generally be unbounded on
{Y_{2d}}
. Fortunately, however, if
f
has compact support it is possible to remedy this situation due to an explicit connection between the Siegel transform
\hat{f}
and the following function.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a lattice (not necessarily unimodular) in any number of dimensions and denote by
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)<\infty
the covolume of
\Lambda
. Then we define

\displaystyle\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right):=\sup_{V}\bigg\{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap\Lambda}}\right)^{-1}:V\cap\Lambda\text{ }\mathrm{is}\text{ }\mathrm{a}\text{ }\mathrm{lattice}\text{ }\mathrm{in}\text{ }V\bigg\},

where
V
runs over all non-zero subspaces of the ambient Euclidean space of
\Lambda
.

Note that one always has
1/s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)\leqslant\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)
where
s\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)
denotes the length of the shortest non-zero vector of
\Lambda
. Therefore, since the existence of very short lattice vectors will generally cause Siegel transforms to blow up, one could expect that
\alpha
should more or less determine the growth of Siegel transforms on
X_{n}
. This result, due to Schmidt, is the source of our interest in
\alpha
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
15
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Suppose
f:{\bf R}^{n}\longrightarrow{\bf R}
is a bounded function with compact support. Then for any unimodular lattice
\Lambda\in X_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\hat{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)}}\right|\ll_{\mathrm{supp}\,f}\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right),

where
\widehat{f}
denotes the Siegel transform of
f
.

For the purposes of estimating norms of Siegel transforms on the space of unimodular lattices, Proposition
3.2
is particularly useful since
\alpha\in L^{p}(X_{n})
for
p=1,\ldots,n-1

[
8
, Lemma 3.10]
. The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
, which extends this integrability result to the symplectic case.

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we will need to describe a rather explicit Siegel set containing a fundamental domain of the coset space
G/\Gamma
. To this end, we initially describe three distinguished subgroups of
G
and introduce some notation.

Let
{\bf K}=\text{SO}(2d)\cap G
, and let

\displaystyle{\bf A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}D&0\\
0&D^{-1}\end{matrix}}}\right):D>0\text{ diagonal}}}\right\}.

Moreover, let
\textbf{N}(d)=\textbf{N}
be the subgroup of
(2d)\times(2d)
symplectic matrices given by

\displaystyle\textbf{N}(d):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N&M\\
0&N^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right):N\text{ unipotent, upper-triangular, }\,NM^{\intercal}=MN^{\intercal}}}\right\}.

Finally, for real parameters

, let
{\bf N}_{u}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{A\in{\bf N}:\|A\|_{\infty}\leqslant u}}\right\}
and
{\bf N}_{{\bf Z}}:=\mathrm{GL}(2d,{\bf Z})\cap{\bf N}

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{t}:=\Bigg\{\operatorname{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d},a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{d}^{-1}}}\right):0<a_{i}\leqslant ta_{i+1}\,\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,d-1,\text{ and }0<a_{d}\leqslant t\Bigg\}.

For future reference, we note that for

(3.1)

\displaystyle a_{i}\leqslant t^{d+1-i},\quad\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

We also note the following Iwasawa decomposition of the symplectic group.

Theorem 3.3

(
[

)
.

The Lie group
G
is diffeomorphic to the product
{\bf K}\times{\bf A}\times{\bf N}
.

With these definitions in place, we can describe the Siegel set containing a fundamental domain for
G/\Gamma

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists an explicit

such that, with

\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})={\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}_{u}\cdot\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})

We believe that such a result is known to experts of the field, but we have been unable to find a suitable reference. We therefore postpone the proof of Proposition

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Assuming Proposition
3.4
, we will now prove Theorem
1.2
.

Because of the identification
{Y_{2d}}\simeq G/\Gamma
, we can view
\alpha
as a right-
\Gamma
-invariant function on
G
. We will also denote by
\alpha
the lift of this map to
G
, so that
\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g\Gamma}}\right)=\alpha(g)
. Since the group of diagonal symplectic matrices can be parametrized in a very straightforward manner, making integration over (a subset of) this group relatively simple, we want to prove that up to some constant, for
g=kan\in\textbf{K}\textbf{A}_{t}\textbf{N}_{u}
,
\alpha(g\Gamma)
essentially only depends on
a
. To this end, we will need the following alternative characterization of
\alpha
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given a discrete subgroup
\Delta\leqslant{\bf R}^{2d}
, let
d(\Delta)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\infty}}\right)
denote the covolume of
\Delta
in the subspace
V_{\Delta}\subset{\bf R}^{2d}
spanned by
\Delta
. For
g\in G
, one has

\displaystyle\alpha(g)=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)^{-1}:\Delta\leqslant g{\bf Z}^{2d}\text{ is discrete}}}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
g\in G
. Since
\alpha(g)
is already defined as a supremum over all discrete subgroups
\Delta
of a particular form, namely those satisfying
\Delta=V_{\Delta}\cap g{\bf Z}^{2d}
, we simply have to ensure that the remaining discrete subgroups of
g{\bf Z}^{2d}
have larger covolumes than those considered in the definition of
\alpha
. However, this is clear: If
\Delta\leqslant g{\bf Z}^{2d}
is a discrete subgroup with
\Delta

properly contained
in

, then

\displaystyle d(\Delta)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\Delta}/\Delta}}\right|>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\Delta}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{\Delta}\cap g{\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right)}}\right|=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{\Delta}\cap g{\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right),

so that

.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists
C=C(t,u)>0
such that for any

.

Proof.

From
[
2

with the property that if

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g\Delta}}\right)\leqslant\beta(g)d(\Delta).

Letting

\displaystyle d(\Delta)=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g\Delta^{\prime}}}\right)\leqslant\beta(g)\,d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{\prime}}}\right)=\beta(g)\,d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{-1}\Delta}}\right),

and hence, by exchanging

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g\Delta}}\right)\geqslant\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{-1}}}\right)^{-1}d(\Delta)

Note that for

\displaystyle n=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N&M\\

\displaystyle n^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N^{\prime}&M^{\prime}\\

\displaystyle\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((n^{\prime})^{-1}}}\right)\leqslant\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{-1}}}\right):n\in\textbf{N}_{u}}}\right\}=C<\infty,

which proves the lemma.
∎

we see that

\displaystyle=\int_{\bf A}\int_{\bf N}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(aa_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a_{0}^{-1}na_{0}}}\right]n_{0}}}\right)\rho(a)\,\mathrm{d}n\,\mathrm{d}a

where the third equality is due to the change of variables

Proof of Theorem

.

For

, let us introduce the function

on

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V/(V\cap g{\bf Z}^{2d})}}\right|^{-1}:{\small\begin{array}[]{l}V\cap g{\bf Z}^{2d}\text{ is a lattice in a sub-}\\
\text{space $V\subset{\bf R}^{2d}$, $\text{dim }V=r$}\end{array}}}}\right\}.

To prove the claim, it is enough to show that for arbitrary

, the function

belongs to

for the mentioned values of

. To this end, in view of Lemma

, Lemma
3.7
, and Lemma
3.8
it is enough to show that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf A}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{max}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a_{1}^{\pm p},\ldots,a_{d}^{\pm p}}}\right\}\prod_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2(d-i)+1}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}a_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}a_{d}<\infty,

where

denotes the

’th largest element of the set in question.

Observe that as functions of

(3.4)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{max}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a_{1}^{\pm p},\ldots,a_{d}^{\pm p}}}\right\}<{\sum}a_{1}^{e_{1}p}a_{2}^{e_{2}p}\cdots a_{d}^{e_{d}p}<3^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{max}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a_{1}^{\pm p},\ldots,a_{d}^{\pm p}}}\right\},

the sum extending over all

-tuples

with exactly

be an arbitrary monomial in the above sum and show that

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf A}_{t}}a_{1}^{e_{1}p}a_{2}^{e_{2}p}\cdots a_{d}^{e_{d}p}\prod_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2(d-i)+1}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}a_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}a_{d}<\infty.

, we see that the left-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf A}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{e_{i}p+2(d-i)+1}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}a_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}a_{d}

if we have

. Analogously, if

\displaystyle\ll_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{ta_{d}}\int_{0}^{ta_{d-1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{ta_{4}}a_{3}^{(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3})p+6d-7}\prod_{i=4}^{d}a_{i}^{e_{i}p+2(d-i)+1}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}a_{3}\cdots\mathrm{d}a_{d}.

is non-negative, i.e. if

\displaystyle 2id-i^{2}+i-1+(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i})p\geqslant 0.

) holds for

, then we obtain (

). Note that for any

is such that

\displaystyle p\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|e_{1}+\cdots+e_{i}}}\right|^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2id-i^{2}+i-1}}\right).

∎

Remark.
This result is optimal in the sense that

Theorem

A Central Limit Theorem for Functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({Y_{2d}}}}\right)

The goal of this section is to prove the following intermediate theorem, which states that we do indeed have a central limit theorem for averages
F_{N}
of translations of a smooth and compactly supported function on

as defined in (
1.2

For the remainder of this article, we will use the notation

\displaystyle\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi}}\right):=\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
\phi
is an integrable function on
{Y_{2d}}
. We will also write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Leb}(f):=\int_{{\bf R}^{2d}}f(\textbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}

where

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({Y_{2d}}}}\right)
, let
a=\mathrm{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{t},\ldots,e^{t},e^{-t},\ldots,e^{-t}}}\right)
for some

\displaystyle\psi_{m}:=\phi\circ a^{m}-\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi}}\right),\quad\quad F_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\sum}_{m=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m}.

Then there is
\sigma\geqslant 0
such that as
N\longrightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle F_{N}\Longrightarrow N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sigma^{2}}}\right).

Remark.
In the event that
\sigma=0
, the resulting distribution
N(0,0)
is to be understood as the Dirac distribution at
0
.

It is immediate from the definition of
F_{N}
that it integrates to
0
. Hence, using Theorem
2.3
to show that

, we only need to demonstrate that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int_{{Y_{2d}}}F_{N}^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle<\infty,

and that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{cum}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F_{N}}}\right)

\displaystyle=0

for all
r\geqslant 3
. We note that (
4.1
) can be demonstrated without too much trouble as follows: One has

\displaystyle\int_{{Y_{2d}}}F_{N}^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{m=0\atop n=0}^{N-1}\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\psi_{m-n}\psi_{0}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{\pm s=0}^{N-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|s}}\right|}}\right)\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\psi_{s}\psi_{0}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle={\sum}_{s\in{\bf Z}}\mathbbm{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-N\leqslant s\leqslant N-1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{|s|}{N}}}\right)\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\psi_{s}\psi_{0}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu.

The action

is mixing with an exponential rate, cf.
[
3
, Thm. 1.1]
(see also Theorem

below). Therefore, for any

, the series above is dominated termwise by an absolutely convergent series, in which case the theorem of dominated convergence shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int_{{Y_{2d}}}F_{N}^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu={\sum}_{s\in{\bf Z}}\,\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\psi_{s}\psi_{0}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu={\sum}_{s\in{\bf Z}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\phi\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\circ a^{s}}}\right)-\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi}}\right)^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu}}\right),

which is finite, again according to
[
3
, Thm. 1.1]
. This proves (

The main result of this section is that the function

\displaystyle P_{f}(\textbf{x})=P_{f}(Ka_{y})=\int_{Y^{\prime}}\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)^{2d-1}}{\sum}_{\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\bf 0}}}\right\}}f(Ka_{y}m(A)u_{\textbf{t},s}\textbf{v})\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{t}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}s\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu_{d-1}(A),

where

, and

\displaystyle m(A)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0\\
0&A&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}}}\right),\quad u_{\textbf{t},s}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0\\
\textbf{t}&I&0\\
s&\textbf{t}^{*}&1\end{matrix}}}\right)

\textbf{t}^{*}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{2},\ldots,t_{2d-1}}}\right)^{*}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{2d-1},\ldots,t_{d+1},-t_{d},\ldots,-t_{2}}}\right)

Theorem 5.3

(Corollary to
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Suppose
f,\,g:{\bf R}^{2d}\rightarrow{\bf R}
are even and bounded and have compact support. Then one has the formula

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{2d}}\hat{f}\hat{g}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{1}{\zeta(2d)}{\sum}_{j\geqslant 1}\int_{{\bf R}^{2d}}P_{f}(\textbf{x})g(j\textbf{x})\,\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}.

Proof.

Denote the primitive Siegel transform of a function
h
by
\widetilde{h}
. It follows immediately from
[
10
, Remark 5.14]
and
[
10
, Eq. (5.11)]
that if
f_{k}
and
g_{j}
are even and bounded and have compact support,

(5.17)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{2d}}\widetilde{f_{k}}\widetilde{g_{j}}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{1}{\zeta(2d)}\int_{{\bf R}^{2d}}\mathcal{P}_{f_{k}}g_{j}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{x},

where the operator
\mathcal{P}
maps any bounded, compactly supported, measurable function
h
on
{\bf R}^{2d}
to the function
\mathcal{P}_{h}:{\bf R}^{2d}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\bf 0}}}\right\}\longrightarrow{\bf R}
, whose value at
\textbf{x}=Ka_{y}\textbf{e}_{2d}\in{\bf R}^{2d}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\bf 0}}}\right\}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}(\textbf{x})=\int_{Y^{\prime}}\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)^{2d-1}}\widetilde{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ka_{y}m(A)u_{\textbf{t},s}{\bf Z}^{2d}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{t}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}s\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu_{d-1}(A),

cf.
[
10
, Eq. (2.2)]
and the proof of
[
10
, Prop. 2.2]
. If we now take
f_{k}(\textbf{x})=f(k\textbf{x})
and
g_{j}(\textbf{x})=g(j\textbf{x})
in (
5.17
), the relation

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(\Lambda)={\sum}_{k\geqslant 1}\widetilde{h_{k}}(\Lambda)\hskip 11.74988pt(\text{$h$ even, bounded and with compact support)}

shows that

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{2d}}\widehat{f}\widehat{g}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu={\sum}_{k,j\geqslant 1}\int_{Y_{2d}}\widetilde{f_{k}}\widetilde{g_{j}}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu.

Hence, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle{\sum}_{k\geqslant 1}\mathcal{P}_{f_{k}}=P_{f},

but this is clear from the definition of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

We will also need the following result.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let

\displaystyle P_{f}(\textbf{x})=\int_{{\bf R}^{2d}}f(\textbf{r})\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\hskip 2.84544pt+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\textbf{x}\|^{-1}}}\right),\quad\quad\textbf{x}\in{\bf R}^{2d}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\bf 0}}}\right\}.

Proof.

To ease the notation, we will write

\displaystyle u_{\textbf{t},s}\textbf{v}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}\\
t_{2}v_{1}+v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
t_{2d-1}v_{1}+v_{2d-1}\\

v_{i}\neq 0

where we stress that the bounds of the integral are independent of

cover the real line exactly

times. Therefore, this sum over

equals

(5.20)

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf R}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ka_{y}m(A)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,v_{2},\ldots,v_{2d-1},r}}\right)^{\intercal}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}r.

Next, if both
v_{1}=0

(5.21)

\displaystyle f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ka_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\ldots,0,v_{2d}}}\right)^{\intercal}}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{2d}Ka_{y}\textbf{e}_{2d}}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{2d}\textbf{x}}}\right).

(5.23)

\displaystyle{\sum}_{v_{i}\in{\bf Z}\atop(i\geqslant 2)}\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)^{2d-1}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ka_{y}m(A)u_{\textbf{t},s}\textbf{v}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{t}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}s=\int_{{\bf R}^{2d-1}}f(Ka_{y}m(A)(v_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{2d})^{\intercal})\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{{\bf R}^{2d}}f\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}-\|\textbf{x}\|^{-1}{\sum}_{n\in{\bf Z}}\int_{{\bf R}^{2d-1}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{\textbf{x}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\textbf{x}\|^{-1}n,\textbf{r}}}\right)^{\intercal}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}}}\right|\leqslant\frac{C_{0}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\textbf{x}}}\right\|}+C_{1}

\textbf{x}\neq 0

Lemma 5.7
.

For

Proof.

By definition of the cumulant

\displaystyle\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi_{m_{1}}\cdots\psi_{m_{n}}}}\right|\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu\ll_{n,d,\mathrm{supp}\,f_{\varepsilon}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\phi}}\right\|_{\infty}^{(n-d)^{+}}.

Suppose first that

\displaystyle\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi_{m_{1}}\cdots\psi_{m_{n}}}}\right|\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\psi_{m_{1}}}}\right\|_{d}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\psi_{m_{n}}}}\right\|_{d}\ll_{d,\mathrm{supp}\,f_{\varepsilon}}1.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\psi_{m}}}\right\|_{d}\leqslant\mu(\phi)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{f_{\varepsilon}}}}\right\|_{d}\ll_{d,\mathrm{supp}\,f_{\varepsilon}}\mu(\phi)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\alpha}}\right\|_{d}

\displaystyle\mu(\phi)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{\varepsilon}}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{2}}}\right\|_{1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\chi_{2}}}\right\|_{1}\ll\varepsilon+\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{2})

Next, suppose that

\displaystyle\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi_{m_{1}}\cdots\psi_{m_{n}}}}\right|\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\psi_{m_{d+1}}\cdots\psi_{m_{n}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{{Y_{2d}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi_{m_{1}}\cdots\psi_{m_{d}}}}\right|\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\mu

Estimating the Sobolev Norm

We now proceed by estimating

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime}}\widehat{f_{\varepsilon}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime}}\widehat{f_{\varepsilon}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right)=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{{\sum}}_{\textbf{v}\in g{\bf Z}^{2d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime}}f_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textbf{v}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime}}f_{\varepsilon}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime}}f}}\right)(\textbf{x})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}}\right|_{t=0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{tZ^{\prime}}\textbf{x}}}\right)-f(\textbf{x})}}\right),\quad\quad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf R}^{2d}}}\right),\quad\quad\textbf{x}\in{\bf R}^{2d}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{L}}}\right)=\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Xi*\mathbbm{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha\leqslant L}}\right\}}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}_{Z^{\prime\prime}}\Xi}}\right)*\mathbbm{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha\leqslant L}}\right\}},

Reduction of Theorem
1.1
to the Special Case

We conclude by showing that, in fact, the special case

of Theorem

implies the theorem in its full generality. For the real parameter

, we let
N=N(T)=\lfloor\log_{2}T\rfloor
so that

(5.38)

\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}<2^{N}\leqslant T<2^{N+1}.

If
\Lambda\in{Y_{2d}}
denotes any symplectic lattice, we then have

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)+\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}\setminus\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)}}\right)-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}\setminus\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right),

and hence

\displaystyle X_{T}:=\frac{\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{T}}}\right)-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{T}Z_{T}+\beta_{T}-\gamma_{T},

where we write

\displaystyle\alpha_{T}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)}}}\right)^{1/2},\quad\beta_{T}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}\setminus\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{T}}}\right)^{1/2}},\quad\gamma_{T}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta_{T}}}\right\|_{1},

and

\displaystyle Z_{T}:=\frac{\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Lambda\cap\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)-\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)}{\operatorname{Vol}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{2^{N}}}}\right)^{1/2}}.

So far, we know that as
T\longrightarrow\infty
,
Z_{T}
converges in distribution to
N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sigma^{2}}}\right)
for some
\sigma\geqslant 0
. It will therefore follow by standard methods that
X_{T}\Longrightarrow N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sigma^{2}}}\right)
if we can show that

(5.39)

\displaystyle\alpha_{T}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 1,

(5.40)

\displaystyle\gamma_{T}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0.

To this end, we now compute the volume of
\Omega_{T}
. By changing to spherical coordinates, we see that
\Omega_{T}
is the set

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r,\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d-1};\,s,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d-1}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bf R}_{+}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\pi}}\right]^{d-2}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,2\pi}}\right)}}\right)^{2}:1/s\leqslant r\leqslant 2/s,\,1\leqslant s<T}}\right\}.

\displaystyle c_{d}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{\pi}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d-1}}}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\phi_{d-1},

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\log 2}{\log T}<\frac{N\log 2}{\log T}<1+\frac{\log 2}{\log T},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\log 2}{\log T}}}\right)^{1/2}<\alpha_{T}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{N\log 2}{\log T}}}\right)^{1/2}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\log 2}{\log T}}}\right)^{1/2},

and it follows that

\displaystyle\gamma_{T}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c_{d}^{2}(2^{d}-1)}{d}}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\log T-N\log 2}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log T}}\right)^{1/2}}\ll_{d}\frac{\log 2}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log T}}\right)^{1/2}}\longrightarrow 0.

Appendix A
A Siegel Set for

We now give a proof of the explicit Siegel set decomposition expressed in Proposition

. We follow the arguments in
[
2
, Chap. V.1]
.

Lemma A.1
.

There exists a number
m(d)\geqslant 2
with the property that
{\bf N}={\bf N}_{m(d)}{\bf N}_{{\bf Z}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction in
d
. The case
d=1
is well-known (see
[
2
]
) since
\mathrm{Sp}(2,{\bf R})=\mathrm{SL}(2,{\bf R})
, and in this case we can take
m(1)=2
.

We now let
d\geqslant 2
and assume the claim for
2(d-1)\times 2(d-1)
matrices in
{\bf N}(d-1)
and define
m(d)
recursively. Given any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N&M\\
0&N^{-\intercal}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\in{\bf N}(d)
, we have to prove the existence of a
d\times d
unipotent, upper-triangular, integer matrix
S
and a
d\times d
integer matrix
T
such that

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|NS}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant m(d),

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(NS}}\right)^{-1}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant m(d),

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|NT+MS^{-\intercal}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant m(d),

(A.4)

\displaystyle ST^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=TS^{\intercal},

for some
m(d)
only depending on
d
.

Let us write
N
in the form

\displaystyle N=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}A&\textbf{x}\\
0&1\end{matrix}}}\right),

where
A
is a
(d-1)\times(d-1)
unipotent upper-triangular matrix and
\textbf{x}\in{\bf R}^{d-1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle N^{-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}A^{-1}&-A^{-1}\textbf{x}\\
0&1\end{matrix}}}\right).

Applying the induction hypothesis to the matrix
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&0\\
0&A^{-\intercal}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
, we can find a unipotent, upper-triangular integer matrix
S_{0}
of dimensions
(d-1)\times(d-1)
such that
\|AS_{0}\|_{\infty}\leqslant m(d-1)
and
\|(AS_{0})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant m(d-1)
.

We now augment
S_{0}
to a matrix
S
satisfying (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) as follows. Write first

\displaystyle S=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}S_{0}&\textbf{s}\\
0&1\end{matrix}}}\right)

where
\textbf{s}\in{\bf Z}^{d-1}
. Then one has

\displaystyle NS

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}AS_{0}&A\textbf{s}+\textbf{x}\\
0&1\end{matrix}}}\right),

\displaystyle(NS)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}(AS_{0})^{-1}&-(AS_{0})^{-1}(A\textbf{s}+\textbf{x})\\
0&1\end{matrix}}}\right).

It is now possible to choose the vector

in such a way that
\|A\textbf{s}+\textbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2
. Indeed, this claim is equivalent to the existence of a point of the lattice

in the set
-\textbf{x}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-2,2}}\right]^{d-1}
. This claim, on the other hand, is true since surely
-\textbf{x}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-2,2}}\right]^{d-1}
contains one of the cubes

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\textbf{v}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1}:\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{d-1}}}\right\},

each of which necessarily contains a lattice point from
A{\bf Z}^{d-1}
, as may be seen by solving the system of inequalities

\displaystyle A\textbf{s}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}1&a_{12}&a_{13}&\cdots&a_{1,d-1}\\
0&1&a_{23}&\cdots&a_{2,d-1}\\
0&0&1&\cdots&a_{3,d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}s_{1}\\
s_{2}\\
s_{3}\\
\vdots\\
s_{d-1}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\textbf{v}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]^{d-1}

by starting with

. It follows that we now have
\|AS_{0}\|_{\infty}\leqslant m(d-1)
,
\|(AS_{0})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant m(d-1)
, and
\|A\textbf{s}+\textbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2
. However, these inequalities imply that additionally

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(AS_{0})^{-1}(A\textbf{s}+\textbf{x})}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2(d-1)m(d-1).

We therefore obtain

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|NS}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m(d-1),2}}\right\}=m(d-1)<2(d-1)m(d-1),

since
m(d-1)\geqslant 2
by assumption, and

(A.6)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(NS)^{-1}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2(d-1)m(d-1).

Let
u(d)=2(d-1)m(d-1)
. With
S
as above, we now let
T^{\prime}
be any integer matrix satisfying
S(T^{\prime})^{\intercal}=T^{\prime}S^{\intercal}
so that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N&M\\
0&N^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}S&T^{\prime}\\
0&S^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N^{\prime}&M^{\prime}\\
0&(N^{\prime})^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)

with
\|N^{\prime}\|_{\infty},\,\|(N^{\prime})^{-\intercal}\|_{\infty}\leqslant u(d)
. Thus, if we can find a symmetric integer matrix
T^{\prime\prime}
such that
\|N^{\prime}T^{\prime\prime}+M^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
is bounded, we will be done. Indeed, then we may take
m(d)
as an appropriate multiple of
u(d)
and define
T
by the relation

(A.7)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}S&T\\
0&S^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}S&T^{\prime}\\
0&S^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}I&T^{\prime\prime}\\
0&I\end{matrix}}}\right).

In order to find such a matrix
T^{\prime\prime}
, we note that the condition
N^{\prime}(M^{\prime})^{\intercal}=M^{\prime}(N^{\prime})^{\intercal}
implies that
(N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}
is symmetric. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}}}\right)^{\intercal}=(M^{\prime})^{\intercal}(N^{\prime})^{-\intercal}=(N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}(N^{\prime})^{\intercal}(N^{\prime})^{-\intercal}=(N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}.

Therefore we obtain a symmetric integer matrix by letting

\displaystyle T^{\prime\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{ij}}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{d},\quad\quad t_{ij}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-(N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}}}\right)_{ij}}}\right\rfloor.

For a suitable
d\times d
matrix
U
with
\|U\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, we now have
T^{\prime\prime}=-(N^{\prime})^{-1}M^{\prime}+U
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|N^{\prime}T^{\prime\prime}+M^{\prime}}}\right\|_{\infty}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|N^{\prime}U}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant d\cdot u(d).

Hence we can take
m(d)=d\cdot u(d)
. By (
A.5
) and (
A.6
), the conditions (
A.1
)-(
A.4
) are satisfied with
S
and
T
given by (
A.7
).
∎

For
M
a
(d-1)\times(d-1)
matrix,
a\in{\bf R}
, and
\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\in{\bf R}^{d-1}
, let us introduce the
d\times d
matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a,\textbf{x},\textbf{y},M):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}a&\textbf{x}^{\intercal}\\
\textbf{y}&M\end{matrix}}}\right).

To complete the induction step and obtain a Siegel set for
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})/\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})
, we will rely on the following lemma which allows us to project and lift matrices to and from
\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf R})
.

Lemma A.2
.

For
i=1,2,3
, let
a_{i}\in{\bf R}
and let
{\bf x}_{i}\in{\bf R}^{d-1}
. Moreover, for
i=1,2,3,4
, let
M_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}(d-1,{\bf R})
.

i)
Suppose that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(a_{1},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf 0},M_{1})&\mathcal{M}(a_{2},{\bf x}_{2},{\bf x}_{3},M_{2})\\
0&*\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R}).

Then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}M_{1}&M_{2}\\
0&M_{1}^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf R}).

ii)
Suppose that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}M_{1}&M_{2}\\
M_{3}&M_{4}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf R}).

Then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R}).

Proof.

We first prove claim
i)
. The assumption implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a_{1},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf 0},M_{1})\mathcal{M}(a_{2},{\bf x}_{2},{\bf x}_{3},M_{2})^{\intercal}=\mathcal{M}(a_{2},{\bf x}_{2},{\bf x}_{3},M_{2})\mathcal{M}(a_{1},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf 0},M_{1})^{\intercal}.

By computing the transposes and matrix products on both sides, one immediately obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(*,*,*,M_{1}M_{2}^{\intercal}}}\right)=\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(*,*,*,M_{2}M_{1}^{\intercal}}}\right),

and the claim follows.

We now prove claim
ii)
. The assumption implies that

(A.8)

\displaystyle M_{1}^{\intercal}M_{3}=M_{3}^{\intercal}M_{1},\quad\quad M_{2}^{\intercal}M_{4}=M_{4}^{\intercal}M_{2},\quad\quad M_{1}^{\intercal}M_{4}=I+M_{3}^{\intercal}M_{2}.

Using this, one now computes that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right)^{\intercal}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{matrix}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{3}^{\intercal})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{1}^{\intercal})\\
\mathcal{M}(-1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{4}^{\intercal})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{2}^{\intercal})\end{matrix}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},M_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{3}^{\intercal}M_{1}+M_{1}^{\intercal}M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{3}^{\intercal}M_{2}+M_{1}^{\intercal}M_{4})\\
\mathcal{M}(-1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{4}^{\intercal}M_{1}+M_{2}^{\intercal}M_{3})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-M_{4}^{\intercal}M_{2}+M_{2}^{\intercal}M_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}0&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},I)\\
\mathcal{M}(-1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},-I)&0\end{matrix}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{matrix}}}\right).

This proves the second claim.
∎

Finally, we also need the following lemma.

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose that
g\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
has the Iwasawa decomposition
g=kan
where
k\in{\bf K}
,
a=\mathrm{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots}}\right)\in{\bf A}
, and
n\in{\bf N}
. If
\|g\textbf{e}_{1}\|\leqslant\|g\textbf{v}\|
for any
\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
, then we have
a_{1}/a_{2}\leqslant 2/\sqrt{3}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
[
2
, Lemma V.1.6]
: Instead of ensuring that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|n_{ij}}}\right|\leqslant 1/2
for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant 2d
, we only have to ensure
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|n_{12}}}\right|\leqslant 1/2
. This can be done in the symplectic case with the help of
[
2
, Lemma V.1.5]
applied to the upper leftmost block
N
in the block decomposition
n=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N&M\\
0&N^{-\intercal}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
of
n
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
3.4
with
u=m(d)
, where
m(d)
is defined in Lemma
A.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

If
d=1
, the result follows from
[
2
, Thm. V.1.7]
since then the symplectic group coincides with the special linear group. We now proceed by induction in
d
.

Assume that
d\geqslant 2
and that the claim holds for
\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf R})
. Now, let
g\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf R})
, and let
\textbf{v}_{0}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
be a primitive vector with the property that
g\textbf{v}_{0}
is a shortest non-zero vector in the lattice
g{\bf Z}^{2d}
. Since
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})
acts transitively on the set of primitive vectors in
{\bf Z}^{2d}
(see e.g.
[
13
]
), there is
\gamma\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})
such that
\gamma\textbf{e}_{1}=\textbf{v}_{0}
. Then with
g^{\prime}=g\gamma
, we have

(A.9)

\displaystyle\|g^{\prime}\textbf{e}_{1}\|=\|g\gamma\textbf{e}_{1}\|=\|g\textbf{v}_{0}\|\leqslant\|g\textbf{v}\|,

for any non-zero
\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}
.

We claim that, in order to finish the proof, it is enough to find
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
such that
a^{\prime}n^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}\in{\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}
where
k^{\prime}a^{\prime}n^{\prime}=g^{\prime}
is the Iwasawa decomposition of
g^{\prime}
. Indeed, according to Lemma
A.1
, we will then have

\displaystyle a^{\prime}n^{\prime}\in{\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}_{m(d)}{\bf N}_{{\bf Z}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{\prime}}}\right)^{-1}\subset{\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}_{m(d)}\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z}),

and hence
g=g^{\prime}\gamma^{-1}\in{\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}_{m(d)}\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\bf Z})
.

If
a^{\prime}=\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots)
, then we can write

\displaystyle h=a^{\prime}n^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1},\textbf{x}_{1},{\bf 0},E_{0}}}\right)&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\textbf{y}_{1},\textbf{y}_{2},F_{0}}}\right)\\
0&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1},{\bf 0},\textbf{x}_{2},E_{0}^{-\intercal}}}\right)\end{matrix}}}\right)

where the symplecticity of
h
forces the relations

(A.10)

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=-d_{1}^{-1}E_{0}^{-\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1},

(A.11)

\displaystyle\textbf{y}_{2}

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{0}\textbf{y}_{1}-F_{0}\textbf{x}_{1}}}\right).

It follows from Lemma
A.2
that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}E_{0}&F_{0}\\
0&E_{0}^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf R}).

By the inductive hypothesis, we can therefore find

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\Gamma_{1}&\Gamma_{2}\\
\Gamma_{3}&\Gamma_{4}\end{matrix}}}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(2d-2,{\bf Z})

such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}E_{0}&F_{0}\\
0&E_{0}^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right)\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in{\bf K}{\bf A}_{t}{\bf N}_{m(d-1)}.

We then augment
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
to a
2d\times 2d
integer matrix
\gamma^{\prime}
by letting

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},\Gamma_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},\Gamma_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},\Gamma_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},\Gamma_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right).

By Lemma
A.2
,
\gamma^{\prime}
is symplectic. A computation now shows that

(A.12)

\displaystyle h\gamma^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}\Bigl(d_{1},\Gamma_{1}^{\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1}+\Gamma_{3}^{\intercal}\textbf{y}_{1},{\bf 0},E_{0}\Gamma_{1}+F_{0}\Gamma_{3}\Bigr)&\mathcal{M}\Bigl(\lambda,\Gamma_{2}^{\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1}+\Gamma_{4}^{\intercal}\textbf{y}_{1},\textbf{y}_{2},E_{0}\Gamma_{2}+F_{0}\Gamma_{4}\Bigr)\\
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{3}}}\right)&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1},{\bf 0},\textbf{x}_{2},E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{4}}}\right)\end{matrix}}}\right).

Let us suppose that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}E_{0}&F_{0}\\
0&E_{0}^{-\intercal}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\gamma^{\prime\prime}
has the Iwasawa decomposition
k^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime\prime}n^{\prime\prime}
with

\displaystyle k^{\prime\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}K_{1}&K_{2}\\
K_{3}&K_{4}\end{matrix}}}\right),\quad a^{\prime\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}A&0\\
0&A^{-1}\end{matrix}}}\right),\quad n^{\prime\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}N_{1}&M_{1}\\
0&N_{1}^{-\intercal}\end{matrix}}}\right).

We will now augment
k^{\prime\prime}
,
a^{\prime\prime}
, and
n^{\prime\prime}
to symplectic
(2d)\times(2d)
matrices
\tilde{k}
,
\tilde{a}
, and
\tilde{n}
so that
\tilde{k}\tilde{a}\tilde{n}=h\gamma^{\prime}
is the Iwasawa decomposition of
h\gamma^{\prime}
. To this end, we let

\displaystyle\tilde{k}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},K_{1})&\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},K_{2})\\
\mathcal{M}(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},K_{3})&\mathcal{M}(1,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},K_{4})\end{matrix}}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}(d_{1},{\bf 0},{\bf 0},A)&0\\
0&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1},{\bf 0},{\bf 0},A^{-1}}}\right)\end{matrix}}}\right),

and, for some suitable
\textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{w}_{1}\in{\bf R}^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{n}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\textbf{v}_{1},{\bf 0},N_{1}}}\right)&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1}\lambda,\textbf{w}_{1},N_{1}\textbf{w}_{1}-M_{1}\textbf{v}_{1},M_{1}}}\right)\\
0&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,{\bf 0},-N_{1}^{-\intercal}\textbf{v}_{1},N_{1}^{-\intercal}}}\right)\end{matrix}}}\right).

Here it is clear that
\tilde{a}
is symplectic, and from Lemma
A.2
we see that also
\tilde{k}
is. To see that even
\tilde{n}
is symplectic, we need to verify that the lower rightmost block in
\tilde{n}
is the inverse-transpose of its upper leftmost block, which is clear; and moreover that we have the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\textbf{v}_{1},{\bf 0},N_{1}}}\right)\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1}\lambda,\textbf{w}_{1},N_{1}\textbf{w}_{1}-M_{1}\textbf{v}_{1},M_{1}}}\right)^{\intercal}

(A.13)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1}\lambda,\textbf{w}_{1},N_{1}\textbf{w}_{1}-M_{1}\textbf{v}_{1},M_{1}}}\right)\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\textbf{v}_{1},{\bf 0},N_{1}}}\right)^{\intercal}.

This follows immediately from the fact that
N_{1}M_{1}^{\intercal}=M_{1}N_{1}^{\intercal}
. Using the fact that
\smash{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}E_{0}&F_{0}\\
0&E_{0}^{-\intercal}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\gamma^{\prime\prime}}=k^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime\prime}n^{\prime\prime}
, we then obtain that

\displaystyle\tilde{k}\tilde{a}\tilde{n}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1},d_{1}\textbf{v}_{1},{\bf 0},E_{0}\Gamma_{1}+F_{0}\Gamma_{3}}}\right)&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,d_{1}\textbf{w}_{1},\textbf{u}_{1},E_{0}\Gamma_{2}+F_{0}\Gamma_{4}}}\right)\\
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,{\bf 0},{\bf 0},E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{3}}}\right)&\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{1}^{-1},{\bf 0},\textbf{u}_{2},E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{4}}}\right)\end{matrix}}}\right)

with

(A.14)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=-(E_{0}\Gamma_{2}+F_{0}\Gamma_{4})\textbf{v}_{1}+(E_{0}\Gamma_{1}+F_{0}\Gamma_{3})\textbf{w}_{1},

(A.15)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=-E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{4}\textbf{v}_{1}+E_{0}^{-\intercal}\Gamma_{3}\textbf{w}_{1}.

By (
A.10
), (
A.11
), and (
A.12
) the matrix
\tilde{k}\tilde{a}\tilde{n}
equals
h\gamma^{\prime}
precisely if we have the relations

(A.16)

\displaystyle\textbf{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma_{1}^{\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1}+\Gamma_{3}^{\intercal}\textbf{y}_{1}}}\right),

(A.17)

\displaystyle\textbf{w}_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma_{2}^{\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1}+\Gamma_{4}^{\intercal}\textbf{y}_{1}}}\right),

(A.18)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-F_{0}\textbf{x}_{1}+E_{0}\textbf{y}_{1}}}\right),

(A.19)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=-d_{1}^{-1}E_{0}^{-\intercal}\textbf{x}_{1}.

Since we are free to choose
\textbf{v}_{1}
and
\textbf{w}_{1}
, we simply take (
A.16
) and (
A.17
) as definitions. It then follows from (
A.14
) and (
A.15
) that (
A.18
) and (
A.19
) are satisfied if

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4}\Gamma_{1}^{\intercal}-\Gamma_{3}\Gamma_{2}^{\intercal}=I,\quad\quad\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}^{\intercal}=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{1}^{\intercal},\quad\quad\Gamma_{3}\Gamma_{4}^{\intercal}=\Gamma_{4}\Gamma_{3}^{\intercal}.

These relations follow immediately from the fact that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{\prime\prime}}}\right)^{\intercal}
is symplectic because
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is, and we conclude that with the above choices of
\textbf{v}_{1}
and
\textbf{w}_{1}
,
\tilde{k}\tilde{a}\tilde{n}
is, indeed, the Iwasawa decomposition of
h\gamma^{\prime}
.

It follows from the induction hypothesis that the entries
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d-1}
of the diagonal matrix
A
satisfy the inequality
a_{i}/a_{i+1}\leqslant 2/\sqrt{3}
for
i=1,\ldots,d-2
. Hence, in order to conclude the proof we must show that
d_{1}/a_{1}\leqslant 2/\sqrt{3}
. This can easily be accomplished by appealing to Lemma
A.3
: The matrix
\gamma^{\prime}
fixes
\textbf{e}_{1}
, and therefore, for any
\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|h\gamma^{\prime}\textbf{e}_{1}}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|g^{\prime}\textbf{e}_{1}}}\right\|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|g^{\prime}\textbf{v}}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|h\textbf{v}}}\right\|

due to (
A.9
). Replacing
v
by
\gamma^{\prime}\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|h\gamma^{\prime}\textbf{e}_{1}}}\right\|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|h\gamma^{\prime}\textbf{v}}}\right\|

for any non-zero
\textbf{v}\in{\bf Z}^{2d}
. By Lemma
A.3
, we therefore have
d_{1}/a_{1}\leqslant 2/\sqrt{3}
, and so the proof is concluded.
∎
