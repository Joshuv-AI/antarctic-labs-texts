On a manifold formulation of self-concordant functions
Hiroshi HIRAI

Abstract
In this paper, we address an extension of the theory of self-concordant functions for a manifold. We formulate the self-concordance of a geodesically convex function by a condition of the covariant derivative of its Hessian, and verify that many of the analogous properties, such as the quadratic convergence of Newton’s method and the polynomial iteration complexity of the path-following method, are naturally extended. However it is not known whether a useful class of self-concordant functions/barriers really exists for non-Euclidean manifolds. To this question, we provide a preliminary result that the squared distance function in the hyperbolic space of curvature − κ -\kappa is κ / 2 \sqrt{\kappa}/2 -self-concordant and the associated logarithmic barrier of a ball of radius R R is an O ⁡ ( κ ​ R 2 ) O(\kappa R^{2}) -self-concordant barrier. We also give an application to the minimum enclosing ball in a hyperbolic space.



Keywords: geodesically convex optimziation, interior point method,
self-concordant functions, information geometry

1
Introduction

The present paper addresses the power of
geodesically convex optimization

from computational complexity perspectives.
This is motivated by the recent development
on operator scaling and its generalization
[
2
,
5
,
8
]
, where
several important problems in mathematical science
are shown to be formulated as geodesically convex optimization on Riemannian manifolds.
By first- or second-order methods on the manifolds,
some of them are successfully solved in polynomial time
[
2
,
5
]
.
On the other hand, due to the limitation of current methods
[
7
]
,
many of them still remain to desire a theoretically good algorithm.
In the literature,
the design of optimization methods on manifolds,
which utilizes geodesic convexity and has polynomial iteration complexity,
is an important issue. Particularly, several papers
asks an interior-point polynomial algorithm
for geodesically convex optimization on manifolds;
see
[
5
, p. 99]
and see also
[
6
,
7
]
.

For Euclidean convex optimization, interior-point methods
provide a powerful framework to derive polynomial time complexity.
The underlying mathematical basis is established
by the theory of
self-concordant functions

due to Nesterov and Nemirovskii
[
18
]
.
The aim of the present paper is
to examine an extension of self-concordant functions and
the associated path-following method for a manifold setting.
Actually,
such an attempt has already been addressed by Udriste
[
28
]

and Jiang, Moore, and Ji
[
11
]
.
They considered self-concordant functions on general manifolds,
and claimed several results analogous to the ones for the Euclidean case.
However, we found that the both papers
contain several vague points on treatments of tensor fields and
covariant differentiation
\nabla
.
For example, the both papers assume that trilinear form

(X,Y,Z)\mapsto\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{Z}f
is symmetric.
This is not true,
even if
\nabla
is a “symmetric” connection (
\nabla
is torsion-free);
see Lemma
2.3
.
Although their arguments
may be justified by appropriate modifications and corrections,
we here examine a “faithful adaptation”
of arguments in Nesterov’s books
[
16
,
17
]
for a manifold setting,
by means of the standard language of differential geometry.

The first contribution of this paper is to show
that such an adaptation is actually possible.
A naive definition of the self-concordance (SC) for a Riemannian manifold
M
is
by differentiation via geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
as

|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)|\leq 2\sigma(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)
.
We point out that, due to the asymmetry of
\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{Z}f
,
a lemma of symmetric trilinear forms by Nesterov and Nemirovski
[
18
, Proposition 9.1.1]
is not applicable.
Consequently,
this naive definition is not enough to deriving desirable properties of SC.
We here employ a stronger condition as a definition of SC,
which concerns the covariant derivative of the Hessian
Hf
of
f

and equivalents the original if
M={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then we verify several required properties of SC analogous to the Euclidean case.
Although many of presented arguments may be straightforward adaptations
of those in
[
16
,
17
]
,
they clarify differential geometric nature of SC properties, which are
hidden for the Euclidean case.
Particularly, we incorporate viewpoints of
information geometry

[
3
]

to the quadratic convergence proof of Newton’s method.
We believe that these are insightful even in the original Euclidean setting.

The second contribution of this paper addresses the next question:
For non-Euclidean manifolds,
do nontrivial, or even useful, classes of SC functions really exist ?
We show that
the squared distance function
x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}d(p,x)^{2}

in the hyperbolic space of curvature
-\kappa<0

is
\sqrt{\kappa}/2
-self-concordant.
Together with the logarithmic barrier construction,
any ball
B
of radius
R
admits an
O(\kappa R)
-self concordant barrier.
We exhibit that our results can be applied
to the
minimum enclosing ball problem
in a hyperbolic space
[
15
]
.
This suggests the existence of an SC-barrier for a more general convex set
and its further applications.
On the other hand, since the order of the barrier parameter is tight,
the presented barrier of a ball may be unsatisfactory;
the iteration complexity of the path-following method involving this barrier
depends on radius
R
.
This suggests a further difficulty of constructing a good SC-barrier,
which is not easy already in the Euclidean case.
Nevertheless, we hope that these results will be a springboard for discussion
and stimulate further research in this direction.

Related work.

Optimization on manifolds (Riemannian optimization) is one of active areas
in continuous optimization.
In its modeling flexibility, various problems in machine learning, statistics,
and control theory can be formulated in Riemannian optimization,
and (practically) efficient algorithms have been developed;
see textbooks
[
1
,
4
,
25
]
.
The design of theoretically efficient optimization algorithms,
with basis on geodesic convexity,
is a challenging new direction of research; see
[
4
, Chapter 11]

and references therein.

The
ellipsoid method
is another powerful algorithm to
derive polynomial-time complexity.
Rusciano
[
23
]
introduced a cutting plane method,
similar to the ellipsoid method, for Hadamard manifolds.
He showed the existence of a cutting plane sequence
such that the volume of the domain including an optimum decreases in linear order.
However, the sequence is non-constructive.
Also, as pointed out in
[
7
]
,
the volume of a ball in a hyperbolic space
depends exponentially on its diameter (see e.g.,
[
24
, p. 189]
),
and hence the iteration complexity depends on the diameter of the domain.
So this algorithm, even if it could be implemented,
has difficulty for problems on a large domain.

Already in Euclidean case,
interior-point methods are closely linked to Riemannian geometry.
Nesterov and Todd
[
19
]
regarded the interior of the feasible region of LP
as a Riemannian manifold with Hessian metric induced
by a self-concordant barrier.
They connected the Riemannian length of the central curve
with the complexity of interior-point methods.
Along this line,
Ohara and Tsuchiya
[
21
]
developed
an information geometric framework for LP, and provided
a sharp iteration bound of an interior point method
by means of a differential geometric measure of the central curve;
see
[
12
]
for further generalization.
We are partly inspired by their framework:
Our setting is on a manifold
M
with a connection
\nabla
and
a Riemannian metric
\langle,\rangle_{f}
given by the Hessian of an SC-function
f
.
Here
\nabla
is not necessarily the Levi-Civita connection of
\langle,\rangle_{f}
,
and the
dual connection

\nabla^{*}
,
an information geometric concept, plays a role.

One of important aspects of self-concordant functions is
its relation to the
Fenchel-Legendre duality

in convex analysis; see
[
17
, Section 5.1.5]
.
Our presented framework lacks such an aspect.
Recently, several analogues of Fenchel-Legendre duality
are considered for Hadamard manifolds
[
9
,
27
]
.
It is an interesting direction to incorporate
them into the framework or to take inspiration from them
to introduce a more reasonable definition of self-concordance on manifolds.

Organization.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we give preliminaries
on linear algebra and differential geometry.
In Section
3
, we present the results on self-concordance.
In Section
4
,
we present the results on squared distance functions on hyperbolic spaces.

Preliminaries

2.1
Bilinear forms

In this paper, all vector spaces are finite dimensional and over
{\mathbb{R}}
.
For a vector space
V
, let
V^{*}

denote the dual space of
V
, i.e., the vector space of all linear functions
V\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
A vector
p
in
V^{*}
is also written as
p(\cdot)
; this means that
the dot may be replaced by any vector
v\in V
, such as
p(v)
.

Let
U,V
be vector spaces.
For a linear map
\tau:U\to V
,
let
\tau^{*}:V^{*}\to U^{*}
denote the dual map defined by
p(\cdot)\mapsto p(\tau(\cdot))
.
For a multilinear form
G\in V^{*}\otimes\cdots\otimes V^{*}

on
V
, let
\tau^{*}G\in U^{*}\otimes\cdots\otimes U^{*}

denote the multilinear form on
U
defined by
\tau^{*}G:=G(\tau(\cdot),\cdots,\tau(\cdot))
.

A bilinear form
G\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
is said to be
symmetric
if

G(u,v)=G(v,u)
for
u,v\in V
.
A symmetric bilinear form
G\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
is viewed as
G:V\to V^{*}

by
v\mapsto G(v,\cdot)=G(\cdot,v)
.
Suppose that
G
is nondegenerate.
The inverse map
G^{-1}:V^{*}\to V
is
viewed as a symmetric bilinear form on
V^{*}
by

G^{-1}(p,q):=G(G^{-1}p,G^{-1}q)
.
If

\tau:U\to V
is a linear isomorphism,
then it holds,

(\tau^{*}G)^{-1}=\tau^{-1}G^{-1},

(2.1)

where
(\tau^{-1})^{**}=\tau^{-1}
.
Indeed, it holds
v\mapsto(\tau^{*}G)v=G(\tau(v),\tau(\cdot))=\tau^{*}(G\tau v)
,
and hence
p\mapsto(\tau^{*}G)^{-1}p=\tau^{-1}(G^{-1}(\tau^{*})^{-1}p)=G^{-1}((\tau^{-1})^{*}(p),(\tau^{-1})^{*}(\cdot))
.

For two symmetric bilinear forms
F,G
on
U
,
the notation
F\preceq G
means that
F(u,u)\leq G(u,u)
for all
u\in U
.
If
F\succeq 0
, then
F
is called positive semidefinite.
In addition, if
F
is nondegenerate,
F
is called positive definite.
Let
G
be a positive definite bilinear form on
U
.
Then
G
defines the inner product

\langle,\rangle
on
U
by
\langle u,v\rangle:=G(u,v)
, and the norm
\|u\|:=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}
accordingly.
Let
F:U\to U^{*}
be another symmetric bilinear form.
The linear transformation
G^{-1}F:U\to U
is
symmetric
with respect to
\langle,\rangle
,
i.e.,
\langle u,G^{-1}Fv\rangle=\langle G^{-1}Fu,v\rangle(=F(u,v))
.
In particular,
G^{-1}F
can be diagonalized with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors.
Then

F\preceq G\Leftrightarrow\mbox{each eigenvalue of $G^{-1}F$ is at most $1$}.

(2.2)

Suppose, in addition, that
F
is also positive definite.
Then it holds

F\preceq G\Leftrightarrow F^{-1}\succeq G^{-1}.

(2.3)

These can be seen from
the matrix representation
\tilde{F}=(F(e_{i},e_{j})),\tilde{G}=(G(e_{i},e_{j}))
of
F,G
with respect to an orthonormal basis for
\langle,\rangle
(so that
\tilde{G}=\tilde{G}^{-1}=I
).

Additionally,
G
defines an inner product
\langle,\rangle^{*}
and norm
\|\cdot\|^{*}
of the dual space
U^{*}

by
\langle p,q\rangle^{*}:=G^{-1}(p,q)
and
\|p\|^{*}:=\sqrt{\langle p,p\rangle^{*}}
.
Then the following version of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality holds:

p(u)\leq\|p\|^{*}\|u\|\quad(u\in U,p\in U^{*}).

(2.4)

This follows from
p(u)=(GG^{-1}p)(u)=G(G^{-1}p,u)=\langle G^{-1}p,u\rangle\leq\|G^{-1}p\|\|u\|

and
\|G^{-1}p\|^{2}=G(G^{-1}p,G^{-1}p)=(\|p\|^{*})^{2}
.

2.2
Manifold with an affine connection

We here introduce basic terminologies for smooth manifolds;
our references are
[
4
,
24
]
.
Throughout the paper,
sufficient smoothness is assumed for functions, maps, and vector/tensor fields on manifolds.
Let
M
be an
n
-dimensional manifold.
For
x\in M
, let
T_{x}=T_{x}(M)
denote the tangent space at
x
.
For a vector field
X
on
M
, let
X_{x}\in T_{x}
denote the vector at
x
.
For a map
\varphi:M\to N
and
x\in M
, where
N
is a manifold,
let
D\varphi(x):T_{x}(M)\to T_{\varphi(x)}(N)
denote the differential of
\varphi
at
x
.
For a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
,
let
\dot{\gamma}(t):=D\gamma(t)(d/dt)

be the vector tangent to
\gamma
at
t\in[a,b]
.

Suppose that
M
has an affine connection
\nabla
on tangent bundle
T(M)=\bigcup_{x\in M}T_{x}
.
Given a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
with
x=\gamma(a)
,
the connection
\nabla
determines
the
parallel transport
along
\gamma
,
which is a linear isomorphism
\tau_{\gamma,t}

from
T_{x}
to
T_{\gamma(t)}
for
t\in[a,b]
.
For a vector field
Y
and vector
u\in T_{x}
at
x\in M
,
the
covariant derivative

\nabla_{u}Y
of
Y
by
u

is given by
\nabla_{u}Y=\lim_{t\to 0}(\tau_{\gamma,t}^{-1}Y_{\gamma(t)}-Y_{x})/t
,
where
\gamma:[0,l]\to M
is any smooth curve with
\gamma(0)=x

and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=u
.
The covariant derivative
\nabla_{X}Y
of
Y
by a vector field
X

is a vector field so that
(\nabla_{X}Y)_{x}=\nabla_{X_{x}}Y
.
The
geodesic
is a curve
\gamma:[0,l]\to M

such that
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\tau_{\gamma,t}(\dot{\gamma}(0))
.
A geodesic
\gamma
is a solution of differential equation

\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\gamma(t)=0
, and
it is uniquely determined from initial point
x=\gamma(0)
and direction
u=\dot{\gamma}(0)
.
Particularly, given
x\in M
and
u\in T_{x}
,
there is a geodesic
\gamma:[0,l]\to M
with
x=\gamma(0)
and direction
u=\dot{\gamma}(0)
.
In this case, we write
\gamma(t)=\exp_{x}tu
.
The map
u\mapsto\exp_{x}1u
is defined on
a neighborhood of the origin of
T_{x}
, and
is called the
exponential map
.

The covariant derivative is extended for tensor fields;
see
[
4
, Section 10.7]
and
[
24
, II.1.3]
.
In this paper, we consider it for covariant tensor fields.
For a
(0,k)
-tensor field
G=(G_{x})_{x\in M}
,
where
G_{x}\in T_{x}^{*}\otimes\cdots\otimes T_{x}^{*}
,
the covariant derivative
\nabla_{u}G
by
u\in T_{x}
is defined by

\nabla_{u}G:=\left.\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\tau_{\gamma,t}^{*}G_{x}\right|_{t=0},

(2.5)

where
\gamma:[0,l]\to M
is any curve with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=u
.
The covariant derivative
\nabla_{X}G
by vector field
X
is defined as
a
(0,k)
-tensor field obtained by
\nabla_{X}G=(\nabla_{X_{x}}G)_{x\in M}
.
The covariant derivative
\nabla_{X}G
can also be written as (or defined by)

(\nabla_{X}G)(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})=X(G(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}))-\sum_{i=1}^{k}G(Y_{1},\ldots,\nabla_{X}Y_{i},\ldots,Y_{k}),

(2.6)

where
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
are vector fields.
Then

(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k},X)\mapsto(\nabla_{X}G)(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})
is a
(0,k+1)
-tensor field.

In this paper, we assume that
(M,\nabla)
satisfies the following conditions:

•

\nabla
is torsion-free:

\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X=[X,Y]
holds for vector fields
X,Y
.

•

For each point
x\in M
,
the exponential map
\exp_{x}
is defined on
T_{x}

and is a surjection from
T_{x}
to
M
.

We basically consider the case where
M
is a complete Riemannian manifold
and
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection;
then
(M,\nabla)
satisfies the above conditions; see
[
24
, III.1]
.
However, by its affine invariant nature,
our argument in Section
3

does not use the intrinsic Riemannian metric
\langle,\rangle
of
M
,
and also consider another metric
\langle,\rangle_{f}

induced by a smooth function
f
in question.
So we do not make
\langle,\rangle
explicit.

Let
f:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function.
The differential
Df=(Df_{x})_{x\in M}
of
f
is a
(0,1)
-tensor field, where

Df_{x}=Df(x):T_{x}\to T_{f(x)}({\mathbb{R}})={\mathbb{R}}
is given by

Df_{x}(u)=\left.\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}f(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}\quad(u\in T_{x})

(2.7)

for any curve
\gamma:[0,l]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\dot{\gamma}(0)=u
.
If
f
is viewed as a
(0,0)
-tensor, then

Df(X)
equals
\nabla_{X}f
.
A further differentiation of
Df
yields
a
(0,2)
-tensor field
Hf=(Hf_{x})_{x\in M}
, called the
Hessian
of
f
, as

Hf(X,Y):=(\nabla_{X}Df)(Y)=X(Df(Y))-Df(\nabla_{X}Y),

(2.8)

where
X,Y
are vector fields.
In the case where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection
of a Riemannian manifold, this matches the usual definition
of the Hessian

(X,Y)\mapsto\langle\nabla_{X}\mathop{\rm grad}f,Y\rangle
; see
[
4
, Example 10.78]
.

The bilinear form
Hf_{x}\in T^{*}_{x}\otimes T^{*}_{x}

is called the Hessian of
f
at
x\in M
.
The following is well-known. See
[
4
, Proposition 5.15]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Hf_{x}
is a symmetric bilinear form on
T_{x}
.

Proof.

This follows from
(\nabla_{X}Df)(Y)=X(Df(Y))-Df(\nabla_{X}Y)=XYf-Df(\nabla_{Y}X+[X,Y])=(XY-[X,Y])f-Df(\nabla_{Y}X)=YXf-Df(\nabla_{Y}X)=(\nabla_{Y}Df)(X)
.
∎

The diagonal of the Hessian is given as follows. See
[
4
, Eaxmple 10.81]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
x\in M
and
u\in T_{x}
, we have

Hf_{x}(u,u)=\left.\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}t^{2}}f(\exp_{x}tu)\right|_{t=0}.

Proof.

For
\gamma(t)=\exp_{x}tu
we have
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\tau_{\gamma,t}u
and

Df_{\gamma(t)}(\tau_{\gamma,t}u)=Df_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t))=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}f(\gamma(t))
. Apply (
2.5
).
∎

We consider the covariant derivative of
(0,2)
-tensor field
Hf
, which provides
a
(0,3)
-tensor field
(X,Y,Z)\mapsto(\nabla_{X}Hf)(Y,Z)
.
Accordingly, we have a trilinear form
(u,v,w)\mapsto(\nabla_{u}H)(v,w)
on each
T_{x}
.
In contrast to the Euclidean case,
this tri-linear form is not necessarily symmetric,
due to nonzero curvature; see e.g.,
[
4
, Exercise 10.87]
.
Recall the curvature, which is a
(1,3)
-tensor field
(X,Y,Z)\mapsto R(X,Y)Z
defined by

R(X,Y)Z:=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.

(2.9)

Lemma 2.3
.

(\nabla_{X}Hf)(Y,Z)=(\nabla_{Y}Hf)(X,Z)-(R(X,Y)Z)f.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle(\nabla_{X}Hf)(Y,Z)=X(Hf(Y,Z))-Hf(\nabla_{X}Y,Z)-Hf(Y,\nabla_{X}Z)

\displaystyle=X(YZf-Df(\nabla_{Y}Z))-(\nabla_{X}Y)Df(Z)+Df(\nabla_{\nabla_{X}Y}Z)

\displaystyle\quad-YDf(\nabla_{X}Z)+Df(\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z).

By changing role of
X,Y
, we have the expression of
(\nabla_{Y}Hf)(X,Z)
, and obtain

\displaystyle(\nabla_{X}Hf)(Y,Z)-(\nabla_{Y}Hf)(X,Z)

\displaystyle=([X,Y]-\nabla_{X}Y+\nabla_{Y}X)Zf+\nabla_{\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X}Zf+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Zf-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Zf

\displaystyle=-R(X,Y)Zf.

∎

The diagonal of
\nabla_{X}Hf(Y,Z)
is
given by differentiation along geodesic; see
[
4
, Example 10.81]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For
x\in M
and
u\in T_{x}
, we have

(\nabla_{u}Hf)(u,u)=\left.\frac{{\rm d}^{3}}{{\rm d}t^{3}}f(\exp_{x}tu)\right|_{t=0}.

Proof.

For
\gamma=\exp_{x}tu
, we have

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Hf_{\gamma(t)}(\tau_{\gamma,t}u,\tau_{\gamma,t}u)=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Hf_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))=\frac{\rm d^{3}}{{\rm d}t^{3}}f(\gamma(t))
.
∎

3
Self-concordant functions

Let
M
be a manifold with an affine connection
\nabla
(satisfying the above conditions).
A subset
C\subseteq M
is said to be
convex
(or more precisely
geodesically convex
)
if for every geodesic
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
with
\gamma(a),\gamma(b)\in C

it holds
\gamma(t)\in C
for every
t\in[a,b]
.
Accordingly, a function
f:M\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}

is said to
convex
if for every geodesic
\gamma:[a,b]\to M

the function
f\circ\gamma:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex.

Let
C\subseteq M
be an open convex subset,
which is viewed as a submanifold of
(M,\nabla)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

f:C\to{\mathbb{R}}
is convex if and only if the Hessian
Hf_{x}

is positive semidefinite for all
x\in C
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
2.2
and:

f
is convex
\Leftrightarrow

(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0

for every geodesic
\gamma
.
∎

We say that a convex function
f:C\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
nondegenerate
if
Hf_{x}
is positive definite
for all
x\in C
, and
closed
(or lower semicontinuous) if its epigraph
\{(x,y)\in C\times{\mathbb{R}}\mid y\geq f(x)\}

is a closed set.
We often omit the subscript
x
of tensor
G_{x}
at
x\in M
if
x
is clear.

3.1
Definitions and properties

Let
C\subseteq M
be an open convex set.
A closed and nondegenerate convex function

f:C\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
\sigma
-self-concordant (
\sigma
-SC)
for
\sigma\geq 0

if it satisfies

|(\nabla_{v}Hf)(u,u)|\leq 2\sigma Hf(v,v)^{1/2}Hf(u,u)\quad(u,v\in T_{x},x\in C),

(3.1)

and is called
weakly
\sigma
-self-concordant (
\sigma
-WSC)

if it satisfies

|(\nabla_{u}Hf)(u,u)|\leq 2\sigma Hf(u,u)^{3/2}\quad(u\in T_{x},x\in C).

(3.2)

By Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
,
the latter condition (
3.2
) is equivalent to:

\left|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right|\leq 2\sigma(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)^{3/2}\quad(\gamma:\mbox{geodesic in $C$}).

(3.3)

The condition (

Lemma 3.2
.

For
i=1,2
, let
f_{i}:C_{i}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
\sigma_{i}
-(W)SC. Suppose that
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
has nonempty interior.
For
\alpha,\beta>0
, the function
\alpha f_{1}+\beta f_{2}:C_{1}\cap C_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is

\sigma
-(W)SC for
\sigma:=\max\{\sigma_{1}/\sqrt{\alpha},\sigma_{2}/\sqrt{\beta}\}

For the WSC-case, the proof is also exactly the same as in the Euclidean case, and is omitted.
For the SC-case, it needs a little modification.

Proof.

Let
f:=\alpha f_{1}+\beta f_{2}
.
For
x\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
and
u,v\in T_{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla_{v}Hf(u,u)|}{2Hf(u,u)^{1/2}Hf(v,v)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha|\nabla_{v}Hf_{1}(u,u)|+\beta|\nabla_{v}Hf_{2}(u,u)|}{2\sqrt{\alpha Hf_{1}(v,v)+\beta Hf_{2}(v,v)}(\alpha Hf_{1}(u,u)+\beta Hf_{2}(u,u))}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{1}x_{1}\omega_{1}+\sqrt{\beta}\sigma_{2}x_{2}\omega_{2}}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}(\alpha\omega_{1}+\beta\omega_{2})},

where we let
x_{i}:=\sqrt{\alpha}Hf_{i}(v,v)^{1/2}
and
\omega_{i}:=Hf_{i}(u,u)
for
i=1,2
.
We bound the maximum of this quantity (
3.4
).
Observing invariance under the change
(x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\to(sx_{1},sx_{2},t\omega_{1},t\omega_{2})

for
s,t>0
, we may consider
the following optimization problem:

Max.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{1}\omega_{1}x_{1}+\sqrt{\beta}\sigma_{2}\omega_{2}x_{2}

s.t.

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=1,\alpha\omega_{1}+\beta\omega_{2}=1,

\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\geq 0.

Under fixing
\omega_{i}
, maximize this by
x_{i}
.
This is a linear optimization over unit circle,
and hence the optimum is given by
(x_{1},x_{2})=(\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{1}\omega_{1},\sqrt{\beta}\sigma_{2}\omega_{2})/\sqrt{\alpha\sigma_{1}^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}+\beta\sigma_{2}^{2}\omega_{2}^{2}}
.
The problem reduces to

\mbox{Max.}\sqrt{\alpha\sigma_{1}^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}+\beta\sigma^{2}_{2}\omega^{2}_{2}}\ \ \mbox{s.t.}\ \ \alpha\omega_{1}+\beta\omega_{2}=1,\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\geq 0.

The maximum is attained by
(1/\alpha,0)
or
(0,1/\beta)
, and
(
3.4
)
\leq\max(\sigma_{1}/\sqrt{\alpha},\sigma_{2}/\sqrt{\beta})
, as required.
∎

Remark 3.3

This setting may be discussed from viewpoints

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
f
is
\sigma
-WSC.

(1)

For any
x\in C
, it holds

W^{0}(x;1/\sigma)\subseteq C.

(2)

For a geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to C
,
it holds

\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{f,\gamma(1)}\geq\frac{\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}}{1+\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}}.

(3.6)

In addition, if
\gamma(1)\in W^{0}(\gamma(0);1/\sigma)
, then

\|\dot{\gamma}(1)\|_{f,\gamma(1)}\leq\frac{\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}}{1-\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}}.

(3.7)

Proof.

(1).
Consider a geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma(0)=x
.
Suppose that for
a\in(0,1]
the point
\gamma(a)
is in the boundary of
C
.
Define a univariate function
\phi:[0,a)\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\phi(t):=1/\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{f,\gamma(t)}=Hf(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))^{-1/2}=(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)^{-1/2}.

(3.8)

Then we have

\left|\phi^{\prime}(t)\right|=\frac{\left|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right|}{2(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)^{3/2}}\leq\sigma.

By integrating

from

to

,
we have

\phi(0)-\sigma a\leq\lim_{t\to a}\phi(t)\leq\phi(0)+\sigma a

(3.9)

Since

is lower semicontinuous and

is open, it holds
f(\gamma(t))\to\infty
for

. Then
\lim_{t\to a}\phi(t)=0
.
Hence it must hold

.
If
\phi(0)/\sigma>1\Leftrightarrow\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,x}<1/\sigma
,
then
\gamma(1)
belongs to the domain

of

. This implies (1).

(2). From (

) and

, we have
\phi(1)\leq\phi(0)+\sigma
,
which is equivalent to

.
This is (

The second equation (

) follows similarly from

.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle Df_{\gamma(1)}(\dot{\gamma}(1))-Df_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))\geq\frac{\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|^{2}_{f,\gamma(0)}}{1+\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle f(\gamma(1))\geq f(\gamma(0))+Df_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\omega(\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}),

(3.11)

where

\displaystyle Df_{\gamma(1)}(\dot{\gamma}(1))-Df_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))\leq\frac{\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|^{2}_{f,\gamma(0)}}{1-\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}},

\displaystyle f(\gamma(1))\leq f(\gamma(0))+Df_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\omega_{*}(\sigma\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)}),

Proof.

We only show the latter part; the former is similar.

\displaystyle Df_{\gamma(1)}(\dot{\gamma}(1))-Df_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Df_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t)){\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{1}Hf_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|^{2}_{f,\gamma(t)}{\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|^{2}_{f,\gamma(0)}}{(1-\sigma t\|\dot{\gamma}(0)\|_{f,\gamma(0)})^{2}}{\rm d}t,

where the inequality follows by applying (

By using (

Proof of Theorem

Then

follows from Proposition
3.13
(1) and

\lambda_{f}(x)=\|\overline{u}\|<1/\sigma
.
By Proposition
3.8
(2), we have

\lambda_{f}(x^{+})=\|\overline{u}^{+}\|_{f,x^{+}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\sigma\lambda}\|\delta_{\gamma,1}^{-1}\overline{u}^{+}\|_{f,x}.

(3.27)

Let
K:T_{x}\to T_{x}
denote the symmetric linear transformation
defined by

Ku:=u-\int_{0}^{1}\delta_{\gamma,t}^{-1}\tau_{\gamma,t}u{\rm d}t.

By the above lemma,
it holds
\delta_{\gamma,1}^{-1}\overline{u}^{+}=K\overline{u}
.
By Proposition
3.8
(1) (and

), the absolute value
of each eigenvalue of

(1-\sigma\lambda)^{-1}-1=\sigma\lambda/(1-\sigma\lambda)

\|\delta_{\gamma,1}^{-1}\overline{u}^{+}\|_{f,x}=\|K\overline{u}\|_{f,x}\leq\|K\|_{\rm op}\|\overline{u}\|_{f,x}\leq\frac{\sigma\lambda}{1-\sigma\lambda}\lambda,

(3.28)

where

denotes the operator norm of

. By (
3.27
) and (
3.28
), we have (
3.25
). ∎

Consider a scaled variant (
damped Newton update
) of the Newton update

x\leftarrow x^{+}_{\rm damp}:=\exp_{x}\frac{1}{1+\sigma\lambda_{f}(x)}\overline{u}.

(3.29)

This
guarantees global convergence, as in

, Theorem 4.1.12]
(
[

, Theorem 5.1.14]
).

Proposition 3.11
.

Suppose that
f
is
\sigma
-WSC. It holds

and

(3.30)

Thus, for given

,

x
with
\lambda_{f}(x)<\beta/\sigma
in

Proof.

Let
\lambda:=\lambda_{f}(x)
.
By
\|\overline{u}/(1+\sigma\lambda)\|_{f,x}=\lambda/(1+\sigma\lambda)<1/\sigma
and Proposition
3.4
(1), it holds

. By Proposition

, we have

\displaystyle f(x^{+}_{\rm damp})

\displaystyle\leq

∎

3.3
Self-concordant barrier

For
\nu>0
,
a
\nu
-self-concordant barrier (
\nu
-SCB)

of (the closure of)
C
is a
1
-SC function
F:C\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

\sup_{u\in T_{x}}2DF(u)-HF(u,u)\leq\nu\quad(x\in M).

(3.31)

By nondegeneracy of
F
,
this condition is equivalent to

DF(u)^{2}\leq\nu HF(u,u)\quad(u\in T_{x},x\in M).

(3.32)

The following corresponds to
[
16
, Theorem 4.2.2]
(
[
17
, Theorem 5.3.2]
).

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
F_{i}:C_{i}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\nu_{i}
-SCB for
i=1,2
.
If
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
has nonempty interior, then
F_{1}+F_{2}:C_{1}\cap C_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is a
(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})
-SCB.

The proof is the same and is omitted.
The next corresponds to
[
16
, Theorem 4.2.4.1]
(
[
17
, Theorem 5.3.7.1]
).

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
F:C\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\nu
-SCB. For any geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to C
, it holds

DF_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))<\nu.

(3.33)

Proof.

We can assume that
DF_{\gamma(0)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))>0
.
Define
\phi:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
t\mapsto DF_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t))
.
By (
3.32
), it holds

\phi^{\prime}(t)=HF_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\geq(1/\nu)\phi(t)^{2}.

In particular,
\phi
is monotone increasing, and positive.
Integrating
\phi^{\prime}/\phi^{2}\geq 1/\nu
from
0
to
1
, we have

-1/\phi(1)+1/\phi(0)\geq 1/\nu.

Then
\phi(0)<\nu
, and we have (
3.33
).
∎

Instead of formulating other properties of SCB,
we directly describe the path-following method for
minimization of an affine function on a compact convex set.
Here an
affine function
is a function
\ell
such that
it is an affine function on every geodesic
\gamma
, i.e.,
(\ell\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)=0
.
In a general manifold,
nonconstant affine functions are very rare.
The main situation we think of is the minimization of

(x,y)\mapsto c^{\top}y
over
C\subseteq M\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
; see Example
4.4
.

Let
Q\subseteq M
be a compact convex set such that its interior
Q^{\circ}
is nonempty.
For an affine function
\ell:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
, consider the following optimization problem:

\mbox{Min.}\quad\ell(x)\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad x\in Q.

(3.34)

We can assume that
\ell
is not a constant function; then
D\ell_{x}\neq 0
for all
x\in Q^{\circ}
.
Let
\ell^{*}
denote the minimum of
\ell(x)
over
x\in Q
.

Let
F:Q^{\circ}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\nu
-SCB of
Q
. Consider the parametric problem with
t\geq 0
:

\mbox{Min.}\quad f_{t}(x):=t\ell(x)+F(x)\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad x\in Q^{\circ}.

(3.35)

Let
x^{*}(t)
denote the minimizer of
f_{t}
.
As in Euclidean case,
the curve
t\mapsto x^{*}(t)
is called the
central path
,
and the starting point
x^{*}(0)
, the minimizer of
F
, is called the
analytic center

of
Q
(relative to
F
).
The
path-following method
can be formulated as in
[
16
, Section 4.2.4]
:

Path-following method

Input:

A point
x_{0}\in Q^{\circ}
with
\lambda_{F}(x_{0})\leq\beta
, where
0<\beta<\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{1+\sqrt{\beta}}
; say
\beta:=1/9
.

0:

(x,t)\leftarrow(x_{0},0)
.

1:

t^{+}:=t+\alpha/\|D\ell_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}
,
where
\alpha:=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{1+\sqrt{\beta}}-\beta>0
.

2:

x^{+}:=\exp_{x}\overline{u}
, where
\overline{u}
is the Newton direction of
f_{t^{+}}
at
x
.

3:

(x,t)\leftarrow(x^{+},t^{+})
; go to
1
.

The initial point
x_{0}
can be obtained
by the damped Newton method.
The polynomial iteration complexity is obtained as in the Euclidean case:

Theorem 3.14
.

Suppose that
\|(Df_{t})_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}\leq\beta
. Then it holds

(1)

\|(Df_{t^{+}})_{x^{+}}\|^{*}_{F,x^{+}}\leq\beta
.

In addition, if
t>0
, then it holds:

(2)

\|D\ell_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}\leq\frac{1}{t}(\beta+\sqrt{\nu})
; then
t^{+}\geq\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{\beta+\sqrt{\nu}}\right)t
.

(3)

\ell(x)-\ell^{*}\leq\frac{1}{t}\left(\nu+\frac{(\beta+\sqrt{\nu})\beta}{1-\beta}\right)
.

Thus, given
\epsilon>0
,
the path-following method produces
a point
x\in Q^{\circ}
with
\ell(x)-\ell^{*}\leq\epsilon
in

O(\sqrt{\nu}\log\nu\|D\ell_{x_{0}}\|^{*}_{F,x_{0}}/\epsilon)

iterations.

Proof.

(1). Let
\lambda_{0}:=\|(Df_{t})_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}\leq\beta
,

\lambda_{1}:=\|(Df_{t^{+}})_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}
and
\lambda^{+}:=\|(Df_{t^{+}})_{x^{+}}\|_{F,{x^{+}}}^{*}
.
Then

\lambda_{1}=\|\alpha D\ell_{x}/\|D\ell_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}+(Df_{t})_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}\leq\alpha+\|(Df_{t})_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}\leq\alpha+\beta=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{1+\sqrt{\beta}}<1.

Here
f_{t}
is
1
-SC for
t\geq 0
.
By Theorem
3.9
(with
\|(Df_{t^{+}})_{x}\|^{*}_{f_{t^{+}},x}=\lambda_{1}<1
), it holds

\lambda^{+}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1-\lambda_{1}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\beta+\alpha}{1-\beta-\alpha}\right)^{2}=\beta.

(2).
\|D\ell_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}=\frac{1}{t}\|(Df_{t})_{x}-DF_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}\leq\frac{1}{t}(\|(Df_{t})_{x}\|_{F,x}^{*}+\|DF_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x})\leq\frac{1}{t}(\beta+\sqrt{\nu})
.

(3). Let
x^{*}
be a minimizer of
\ell
over
Q
,
and
x^{*}(t)
be the unique minimizer of
f_{t}
over
Q^{\circ}
.
By the assumption of
(M,\nabla)
,
we can choose a geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to Q
from
x^{*}(t)
to
x^{*}
.
Since
\ell
is an affine function and
(Df_{t})_{x^{*}(t)}=tD\ell_{x^{*}(t)}+DF_{x^{*}(t)}=0
, we have

\ell(x^{*}(t))-\ell(x^{*})=-D\ell_{x^{*}(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))=\frac{1}{t}DF_{x^{*}(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(0))\leq\frac{\nu}{t},

(3.36)

where the last inequality follows from Proposition
3.13
.
Also, for geodesic
c:[0,1]\to Q
connecting
c(0)=x
and
c(1)=x^{*}(t)
, we have

t(\ell(x)-\ell(x^{*}(t)))=-tD\ell_{x}(\dot{c}(0))\leq t\|D\ell_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}\|\dot{c}(0)\|_{F,x}\leq(\beta+\sqrt{\nu})\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}.

(3.37)

For the last inequality, we use
\|\dot{c}(0)\|_{F,x}\leq\beta/(1-\beta)
,
which can be seen as follows. By (
3.10
) with
(Df_{t})_{x^{*}(t)}=0
and Cauchy-Schwarz (
2.4
), it holds

\frac{\|\dot{c}(0)\|^{2}_{F,x}}{1+\|\dot{c}(0)\|_{F,x}}\leq-(Df_{t})_{x}(\dot{c}(0))\leq\|(Df_{t})_{x}\|^{*}_{F,x}\|\dot{c}(0)\|_{F,x}\leq\beta\|\dot{c}(0)\|_{F,x}.

By (
3.36
) and (
3.37
),
we have (1).
∎

We finally examine construction of the logarithmic barrier.
For a convex function
f:M\to{\mathbb{R}}

and
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the level set
L_{f,\beta}\subseteq M
is defined by

L_{f,\beta}:=\{x\in M\mid f(x)\leq\beta\}.

(3.38)

Denote by
L_{f,\beta}^{\circ}
its interior.
Define the logarithmic barrier
F_{\beta}:L_{f,\beta}^{\circ}\to{\mathbb{R}}

by

F_{\beta}(x):=-\log(\beta-f(x))\quad(x\in L_{f,\beta}^{\circ}).

(3.39)

Lemma 3.15
.

F=F_{\beta}
is closed convex, and satisfies

DF(u)^{2}\leq HF(u,u)\quad(u\in T_{x},x\in L_{f,\beta}^{\circ}).

(3.40)

Proof.

Let
\omega:=\beta-f(x)>0
. Then we have

DF(u)=\frac{Df(u)}{\omega},\quad HF(u,u)=\frac{Hf(u,u)}{\omega}+\frac{Df(u)^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(3.41)

Then
HF(u,u)\geq 0
and hence
F
is convex.
Also
HF(u,u)\geq DF(u)^{2}
holds.
∎

If a
\sigma
-SC function
F
for
\sigma>0
satisfies (
3.40
), then
\sigma^{2}F
is a
\sigma^{2}
-SCB.
The following is a correspondent of
[
17
, Theorem 5.1.4]
.

Proposition 3.16
.

Suppose that
f:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
\sigma
-SC.
Then
F_{\beta}:L_{f,\beta}^{\circ}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is
\tilde{\sigma}
-SC for

\tilde{\sigma}:=\sqrt{(\sigma(\beta-f^{*})^{1/2}+1)^{2}+9/4},

where
f^{*}:=\inf_{x\in M}f(x)
. In particular,

L_{f,\beta}
admits an
O(\sigma^{2}(\beta-f^{*}))
-SCB.

In the case of WSC,
the constant
\tilde{\sigma}
can be taken as
\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(\beta-f^{*})+1}
,
since precisely the same proof of
[
17
, Theorem 5.1.4]
works.
For the SC case, as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, a little modification is required,
which causes the increase of the constant.

Proof.

From (
3.41
),
Df(u)^{2}=(Df\otimes Df)(u,u)
,
and
\nabla_{v}(Df\otimes Df)(u,u)=(\nabla_{v}Df\otimes Df+Df\otimes\nabla_{v}Df)(u,u)=2Df(u)Hf(u,v)
(see
[
24
, II. Proposition 1.3 (1)]
),
the covariant derivative of
HF
is given by

\nabla_{v}HF(u,u)=\frac{\nabla_{v}Hf(u,u)}{\omega}+\frac{Df(v)Hf(u,u)}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{2Df(u)Hf(u,v)}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{2Df(v)Df(u)^{2}}{\omega^{3}}.

(3.42)

Hence we have

\displaystyle|\nabla_{v}HF(u,u)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\sigma Hf(v,v)^{1/2}Hf(u,u)}{\omega}+\frac{|Df(v)|Hf(u,u)}{\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2|Df(u)|Hf(v,v)^{1/2}Hf(u,u)^{1/2}}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{2|Df(v)|Df(u)^{2}}{\omega^{3}}.

Define
\tau_{1},\tau,\xi_{1},\xi
by

\tau_{1}:=(Hf(v,v)/\omega)^{1/2},\ \tau:=(Hf(u,u)/\omega)^{1/2},\ \xi_{1}:=|Df(v)|/\omega,\ \xi:=|Df(u)|/\omega.

Then we have

\frac{|\nabla_{v}HF(u,u)|}{2HF(v,v)^{1/2}HF(u,u)}\leq\frac{\sigma\omega^{1/2}\tau_{1}\tau^{2}+(1/2)\xi_{1}\tau^{2}+\xi\tau_{1}\tau+\xi_{1}\xi^{2}}{(\tau_{1}^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2})^{1/2}(\tau^{2}+\xi^{2})}.

(3.43)

We bound the RHS.
By homogeneity,
we may consider the optimization problem:

\mbox{Max.}\quad\sigma\omega^{1/2}\tau_{1}\tau^{2}+(1/2)\xi_{1}\tau^{2}+\xi\tau_{1}\tau+\xi_{1}\xi^{2}\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\tau_{1}^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2}=1,\ \tau^{2}+\xi^{2}=1.

Fixing
(\tau,\xi)
,
optimize
(\tau_{1},\xi_{1})
, which is a linear optimization over the unit circle.
The optimum is
(\sigma\omega^{1/2}\tau^{2}+\xi\tau,(1/2)\tau^{2}+\xi^{2})/\sqrt{(\sigma\omega^{1/2}\tau^{2}+\xi\tau)^{2}+((1/2)\tau^{2}+\xi^{2})^{2}}
.
Then we have

\mbox{Max.}\quad\sqrt{(\sigma\omega^{1/2}\tau^{2}+\xi\tau)^{2}+((1/2)\tau^{2}+\xi^{2})^{2}}\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\tau^{2}+\xi^{2}=1.

Therefore, the RHS of (
3.43
) is at most
\sqrt{(\sigma\omega^{1/2}+1)^{2}+((1/2)+1)^{2}}
.
∎

4
Squared distance function

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold, where the inner product and norm on
T_{x}
are denoted by

\langle,\rangle_{x}
and
\|\cdot\|_{x}
, respectively.
The length of a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is defined
as
\int_{a}^{b}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}dt
.
The distance
d(x,y)
between two points
x,y
is defined as
the infimum of the length of a curve connecting
x,y
.
We consider the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
.
Then a minimum-length curve is a geodesic with respect to
\nabla
.
For a
2
-dimensional subspace
F
in
T_{x}
,
the
sectional curvature
of
F
is defined
as
\langle R(u,v)v,u\rangle_{x}
where
\{u,v\}

is an orthonormal basis of
F
.

Suppose that
M
is an
Hadamard manifold
, i.e., it is complete, simply-connected,
and has nonpositive sectional curvature for every tangent plane at every point.
Then
a geodesic connecting any two points is uniquely determined,
up to the choice of an affine parameter.
The exponential map
u\mapsto\exp_{x}u
gives a diffeomorphism from
T_{x}
to
M
.
The distance
d(x,y)
coincides with the length of the unique geodesic between
x
and
y
.
See
[
24
, VI. Theorem 4.1]
.

Fix an arbitrary point
p\in M
.
Define the distance and squared distance functions
d_{p},f_{p}:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

d_{p}(x):=d(p,x),\quad f_{p}(x):=\frac{1}{2}d(p,x)^{2}\quad(x\in M).

It is known
[
14
, Lemma 12.15]

that
f_{p}
is smooth and
1
-strongly convex
(i.e.,
Hf_{p}(u,u)\geq\|u\|^{2}
for
u\in T_{x},x\in M
), and hence nondegenerate.
Note that
d_{p}
is also convex but nondifferentiable at
p
and degenerate.

For exploring a nontrivial class of SC-functions,
the first trial might be to examine whether
f_{p}
is SC or not.
In this section, we show that
f_{p}
is SC for hyperbolic spaces.
A
hyperbolic space
of curvature
-\kappa<0
is an Hadamard manifold
such that every sectional curvature of every point is constant
-\kappa
.
The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
M
is a hyperbolic space with curvature
-\kappa<0
, and let
p\in M
.

(1)

f_{p}
is
\sqrt{\kappa}/2
-SC, and is not
(\sqrt{\kappa}/2-\epsilon)
-SC for every small

\epsilon>0
.

(2)

f_{p}
is
\sqrt{4\kappa/27}
-WSC, and is not
(\sqrt{4\kappa/27}-\epsilon)
-WSC for every small

\epsilon>0
.

Note that
in the hyperboloid model (
\kappa=1
),
the
\sqrt{4/27}
-WSC property of
f_{p}
is claimed in

[
10
, Lemma 4]
(without proof).
It seems reasonable to conjecture that
f_{p}
is

\sqrt{\kappa}/2
-SC for any Hadamard manifold having
curvature lower bound
-\kappa
; it may be proved via comparison theory.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
3.13
,
any ball in a hyperbolic space admits an SCB.
For
R>0
, let
B(p;R)^{\circ}:=\{x\in M\mid d(p,x)<R\}

be the open ball with center
p
and radius
R
.
let
F_{p,R}:B(p;R)^{\circ}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by

F_{p,R}(x):=-\log\left(R^{2}-d(p,x)^{2}\right)\quad(x\in M).

(4.1)

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
M
is a hyperbolic space with curvature
-\kappa<0
.
Then
F_{p,R}
is
O(\sqrt{\kappa}R)
-SC.
In particular, the open ball
B(p;R)^{\circ}
admits an
O(\kappa R^{2})
-SCB.

We show that the order
O(\sqrt{\kappa}R)
cannot be improved.
Define the function
R\mapsto\sigma_{\kappa}(R)
as
the infimum of
\sigma
for which
F_{p,R}
is
\sigma
-SC.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that
M
is a hyperbolic space with curvature
-\kappa<0
.
Then
\sigma_{\kappa}(R)=\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa}R)
.

This may be a pity result.
In the path-following method,
for guaranteeing the existence of the analytic center,
we need to add a constraint to make the feasible region bounded.
A natural constraint is the distance constraint
d(p,x)\leq R
.
Then, adding the above barrier increases the barrier parameter

\nu
by
\Omega(\kappa R^{2})
, and
the resulting iteration complexity of the path-following method depends on
\sqrt{\kappa}R
.

In the Euclidean case, the logarithmic barrier of
f_{p}
provides a
2
-SCB for a ball of arbitrary diameter.
Further, a general result of Nesterov and Nemirovskii
[
18
]

says that every bounded convex set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
admits an
O(n)
-SCB.
The above result may suggest the nonexistence of such an SCB with constant
independent of the size of diameter.

We provide one example to which the presented results are applicable.

Example 4.4

(Minimum enclosing ball in hyperbolic space
[
15
]
)
.

The
minimum enclosing ball problem (MEB)
on a manifold
M

is:
Given distinct points
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}
in
M
, find the smallest
ball including them.
MEB is a nonsmooth optimization problem:

\mbox{Min.}\quad r\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad(x,r)\in M\times{\mathbb{R}},\ d(p_{i},x)\leq r\ (i=1,2,\ldots,m).

(4.2)

In the case of Euclidean space
M={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
MEB is a well-studied problem in computational geometry, and can be formulated
by second-order cone program to which an interior-point method is applicable; see e.g.,
[
13
]
.

We here assume that
M
is a
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space with
curvature
-\kappa
. This case is addressed by Nielsen and Hadjeres
[
15
]
.
We may further assume
\kappa=1
(see below).
Let
r^{*}
denote the optimal value of MEB.
Let
R:=2\max_{1\leq i<j\leq m}d(p_{i},p_{j})
. Clearly
r^{*}<R
.
So we may add constraint
r^{*}\leq R
to make the feasible region bounded.
For applying our framework, we consider the following equivalent formulation:

\mbox{Min.}\quad s\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad(x,s)\in M\times{\mathbb{R}},\ d(p_{i},x)^{2}\leq s\leq R^{2}\ (i=1,2,\ldots,m).

(4.3)

Consider the following barrier

F(x,s):=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}K^{2}\log(s-d(p_{i},x)^{2})-\log(R^{2}-s),

(4.4)

where
K=\sqrt{2^{-1/2}(R+1)^{2}+9/4}
. By Theorem
4.1
,

(s,x)\mapsto d(p_{i},x)^{2}-s
is (degenerate)
2^{-1/2}
-SC.
The proof of Proposition
3.16
implies that

(s,x)\mapsto\log(s-d(p_{i},x)^{2})
satisfies (
3.1
) with

\sigma=K
if
d(p_{i},x)^{2}\leq s\leq R^{2}
.
The last term of
F
is a well-known
1
-SCB.
With Lemmas
3.2
and
3.12
,
we conclude that
F
is an
O(mR^{2})
-SCB for the problem (
4.3
).

By the damped Newton method with starting point
(p_{i},R^{2}/2)
(say),
we obtain the next starting point
(x_{0},s_{0})
in

O(mR^{2}\log R)
iterations. Given
\epsilon>0
,
by the path-following method with starting point
(x_{0},s_{0})
and accuracy

\epsilon^{\prime}:=\epsilon/\min_{1\leq i<j\leq m}d(p_{i},p_{j})

we obtain an additive
