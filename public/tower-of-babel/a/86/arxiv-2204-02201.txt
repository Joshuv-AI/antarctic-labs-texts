On the size distribution of the fixed-length Levenshtein balls with radius one Thanks: The paper was presented (in part) at The Twelfth International Workshop on Coding and Cryptography (WCC), Rostock, Germany, March 7–11, 2022.
Geyang Wang, Qi Wang

Abstract
. The fixed-length Levenshtein (FLL) distance between two words 𝒙 , 𝒚 ∈ ℤ m n \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n} is the smallest integer t t such that 𝒙 \boldsymbol{x} can be transformed to 𝒚 \boldsymbol{y} by t t insertions and t t deletions. The size of a ball in the FLL metric is a fundamental yet challenging problem. Very recently, Bar-Lev, Etzion, and Yaakobi explicitly determined the minimum, maximum and average sizes of the FLL balls with radius one, respectively. In this paper, based on these results, we further prove that the size of the FLL balls with radius one is highly concentrated around its mean by Azuma’s inequality.

1.
Introduction

The
Levenshtein distance
(also known as
edit distance
) between two words is the smallest number of deletions and insertions needed to transform one word to the other. This is a metric used for codes correcting synchronization errors. The theory of coding with respect to the Levenshtein distance dates back to the 1960s
[
6
]
, but there has been much less progress in comparison with the classical theory of coding with respect to the Hamming distance. As commented by Mitzenmacher
[
7
]
,
“Channels with synchronization errors, including both insertions and deletions as well as more general timing errors, are simply not adequately
understood by current theory. Given the near-complete knowledge we have for channels with erasures and errors …, our lack of understanding about channels with synchronization errors is truly remarkable.”
Indeed, even the fundamental problem of counting the number of words formed by deleting and inserting symbols into a given word remains elusive.

In 1966, Levenshtein
[
6
]
gave the earliest bound on the number of words formed by deleting a constant number of symbols from a word. This bound was later improved by Calabi and Hartnett
[
3
]
; also by Hirschberg and Regnier
[
5
]
. On the other hand, the number of words formed by inserting
r
symbols into
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{x}
itself, and is given by
\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n+r}{i}(m-1)^{i}

[
6
]
. Motivated by
estimating the rate of synchronization error-correction codes, Sala and Dolecek
[
8
]
studied the number of words formed by deleting and inserting a constant number of symbols into a given word. The
fixed-length Levenshtein (FLL) distance
(originally under the name “
ancestor distance
”) between two words
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
is defined as half of the traditional Levenshtein distance, i.e., the smallest
t
such that
\boldsymbol{x}
can be
transformed to
\boldsymbol{y}
by
t
insertions
and

t
deletions. An explicit expression was given on the FLL ball size with radius one (see Lemma
2.5
), and the size of the FLL balls with radius larger than one was upper bounded in
[
8
]
. Very recently, Bar-Lev, Etzion, and Yaakobi
[
2
]
found the explicit expressions for the minimum, the maximum and the average sizes of the FLL balls with radius one, respectively. A natural follow-up question is how the size of the FLL balls with radius one is distributed. In this paper, we prove that the size of the FLL balls with radius one is highly concentrated around its mean by Azuma’s inequality (see Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.5
).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we provide some notations, definitions, and auxiliary results. In Section
3
, we analyze in detail the size distribution of the FLL balls with radius one, and state the main results in Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.5
.
Finally, we conclude this paper in Section
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{m}=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
for an integer
m\geq 2
. We use
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. In this paper, vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
are called
m
-ary sequences (words) with length
n
, and are written as strings for convenience. For a word
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
and
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, the subsequence
x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{j}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{x}_{[i,j]}
. A
run
of
\boldsymbol{x}
is a
maximal
subsequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{[i,j]}
with all symbols from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
identical, i.e.,
x_{i}=x_{i+1}=\dots=x_{j}
,
x_{i-1}\neq x_{i}
for
i\geq 2
and
x_{j+1}\neq x_{j}
for
j\leq n-1
. An
alternating segment
of
\boldsymbol{x}
is a
maximal
subsequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{[i,j]}
with the form
abab\dots ab
or
abab\dots ba,
where
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
a\neq b
. Note that by ‘maximal’ we mean that both
\boldsymbol{x}_{[i-1,j]}
(when
i>1
) and
\boldsymbol{x}_{[i,j+1]}
(when
j<n
) are no longer alternating. The number of runs in
\boldsymbol{x}
is denoted by
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})
, and the number of alternating segments in
\boldsymbol{x}
is denoted by
a(\boldsymbol{x})
. For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, we have
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})+a(\boldsymbol{x})=n+1
. The lengths of the first and the last alternating segments in a word
\boldsymbol{x}
are of particular interest in this work and denoted by
h(\boldsymbol{x})
and
t(\boldsymbol{x})
, respectively.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=001100101
. Then the runs of
\boldsymbol{x}
are
00,11,00,1,0,1
and
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=6
; The alternating segments of
\boldsymbol{x}
are
0,01,10,0101
and
a(\boldsymbol{x})=4
,
h(\boldsymbol{x})=1
,
t(\boldsymbol{x})=4
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n>0
,
m>1
both be integers. If
\boldsymbol{x}
is picked uniformly at random, then we have

(1)

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{1}\right]=2-\frac{1}{m^{n-1}},

(2)

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[t(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[t(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{n}\right]=2-\frac{1}{m^{n-1}}.

Proof.

We prove Eq. (
1
), and Eq. (
2
) then follows by symmetry.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
be an arbitrary sequence.
Define the indicator variable
I_{i}
for
i\in[n]
as follows.

I_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if $x_{i}$ belongs to the first alternating segment},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then we have
h(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}
. Also note that
\Pr(I_{1}=1)=1
, and
\Pr(I_{i}=1)=(m-1)/m^{i-1}
for
i>1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{i}\right]=1+(m-1)\left(\frac{1}{m}+\dots+\frac{1}{m^{n-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=2-\frac{1}{m^{n-1}}.

Intuitively, exposing the first symbol of a random
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
does not provide any information about
h(\boldsymbol{x})
since
h(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 1
for all words.
Thus, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\middle|x_{1}\right]
.
More precisely, we define an equivalence relation “
\approx
” on
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
as follows.
For
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
, we say that
\boldsymbol{x}\approx\boldsymbol{y}
if and only if
x_{i}\equiv y_{i}+k\mod{m}
for each
i\in[n]
and some integer
k
.
Clearly,
h(\boldsymbol{x})=h(\boldsymbol{y})
if
\boldsymbol{x}\approx\boldsymbol{y}
, and
\{a\}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n-1}
contains exactly one element from each equivalence class.
It then follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{1}=a\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{1}=b\right]
.
Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{1}=a\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})|x_{1}=a\right],

and the proof is then completed.

∎

A sequence
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n-t}
with
t\in[n-1]
is called a
t
-subsequence
of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
if
\boldsymbol{y}
is formed by deleting
t
symbols from
\boldsymbol{x}
. In other words,
\boldsymbol{y}=(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\dots,x_{i_{n-t}})
, where
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{n-t}\leq n
. Likewise,
\boldsymbol{x}
is called a
t
-supersequence
of
\boldsymbol{y}
. The set of all
t
-subsequences of
\boldsymbol{x}
is called the
deletion
t
-sphere
centered at
\boldsymbol{x}
, and denoted by
D_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The set of all
t
-supersequences of
\boldsymbol{x}
is called the
insertion
t
-sphere
centered at
\boldsymbol{x}
, and denoted by
I_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The fixed-length Levenshtein distance is formally defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Fixed-length Levenshtein (FLL) distance)
.

The fixed-length Levenshtein (FLL) distance between two words
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
is the smallest
t
such that
D_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\cap D_{t}(\boldsymbol{y})\neq\emptyset
, and is denoted by
d_{l}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
.

It is easy to see that
d_{l}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=t
if and only if
t
is the smallest integer such that
\boldsymbol{x}
can be transformed to
\boldsymbol{y}
by
t
deletions and
t
insertions.

Definition 2.4

(FLL ball)
.

For each word
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
, the
FLL
t
-ball
centered at
\boldsymbol{x}
is defined by

L_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}|\ d_{l}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\leq t\},

and
t
is called the radius.

The following results on the size of the FLL ball were given in
[
8
,
2
]
, and will be useful later.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
8
, Theorem 1]

For all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
,

(3)

|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|=\rho(\boldsymbol{x})(mn-n-1)+2-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x})}s_{i}^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x})}s_{i}-a(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
s_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq a(\boldsymbol{x})
, is the length of the
i
-th alternating segment of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Lemma
2.5
was proved by a careful argument based on the principle of inclusion and exclusion. Moreover, it can be shown that
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})(mn-n-1)+2
is an upper bound of
|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|
, and Eq. (
3
) was obtained by subtracting the number of overcounted sequences.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
2
, Lemma 11, Corollary 8, Lemma 12, and Lemma 16]

Let the notations be the same as above. For integers
m,n>1
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x})}s_{i}\right]=n+(n-2)\cdot\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{m^{2}},

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}[a(\boldsymbol{x})]=1+\frac{(n-2)(m-1)(m-2)}{m^{2}}+\frac{n-1}{m},

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}[\rho(\boldsymbol{x})]=n-\frac{n-1}{m},

(4)

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x})}s_{i}^{2}\right]=\frac{n\left(4m^{2}-3m+2\right)}{m^{2}}+\frac{6m-4}{m^{2}}-4-\frac{2}{m-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{m^{n}}\right)+\frac{2}{m^{n}}.

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}[|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|]=n^{2}\left(m+\frac{1}{m}-2\right)+2-\frac{n}{m}+\frac{m^{n-1}-1}{m^{n-1}(m-1)}.

A
martingale
is a sequence of real random variables
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n}
with finite expectation such that for each
0\leq i<n
,

\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i+1}\middle|Z_{i},Z_{i-1},\dots,Z_{0}\right]=Z_{i}.

A classical martingale named
Doob martingale
(see for example
[
1
]
) will be used in this paper. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be the underlying random variables (not necessarily independent) and
f
be a function over
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. The Doob martingale
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle Z_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\right];

\displaystyle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\middle|X_{1},\dots,X_{i}\right]\text{ for }i\in[n].

In other words,
Z_{i}
is defined by the expected value of
f
after exposing
X_{1},\dots,X_{i}
.
The following classical result
[
1
]
plays a key role in the proof of our main results.

Theorem 2.9

(Azuma’s inequality)
.

Let
Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be a martingale such that for each
1\leq i\leq n
,

|Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}|\leq c_{i}.

Then for every
\lambda>0
, we have

\Pr(Z_{n}-Z_{0}\geq\lambda)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda^{2}}{2(c_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{n}^{2})}\right),

and

\Pr(Z_{n}-Z_{0}\leq-\lambda)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda^{2}}{2(c_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{n}^{2})}\right).

3.
The size distribution of the FLL balls with radius one

In this section, we discuss the size distribution of the FLL balls with radius one. We start with the binary case and then deal with the general
m
-ary case.

3.1.
The binary case

Let
n,n^{\prime}
be positive integers. In order to estimate
|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|
, we define the following notation: for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n^{\prime}}
, define

(6)

f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})=\rho(\boldsymbol{y})n-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{y})}s_{i}^{2},

where
s_{i}
is the length of the
i
-th alternating segment of
\boldsymbol{y}
for
1\leq i\leq a(\boldsymbol{y})
.
Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{y})}s_{i}=n^{\prime}
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n^{\prime}}
, and
\rho(\boldsymbol{y})+a(\boldsymbol{y})=n^{\prime}+1
.
Then by Eq. (
3
), we have
|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{n}{2}+1
for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.
Therefore, it suffices to find the distribution of
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.
To this end, we express
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
by two partial values
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})
and
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n,n^{\prime}
be positive integers.
For each
i\in[n^{\prime}-1]
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n^{\prime}}
, we have

f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})=\begin{cases}f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})-n,&\text{if $y_{i}=y_{i+1}$},\\
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})-t(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})h(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]}),&\text{if $y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

It is important to use the additive behavior of
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
in Eq. (
6
).
The first case follows from the equations of
\rho(\boldsymbol{y})=\rho(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+\rho(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})-1
and
a(\boldsymbol{y})=a(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+a(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})
.

When
y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}
, we have
\rho(\boldsymbol{y})=\rho(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+\rho(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})
. Then the difference of
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
and
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})
is given by

\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(t(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})+h(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})\right)^{2}-t(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})^{2}-h(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]})^{2}\right]=t(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,i]})h(\boldsymbol{y}_{[i+1,n^{\prime}]}).

The proof is then completed.

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
n,n^{\prime}
be positive integers.
For all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n^{\prime}}
, we have

f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})-f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,n^{\prime}-1]})=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2},&\text{if }y_{n^{\prime}-1}=y_{n^{\prime}},\\
n-\frac{1}{2}-t(\boldsymbol{y}_{[1,n^{\prime}-1]}),&\text{if }y_{n^{\prime}-1}\neq y_{n^{\prime}}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that by the definition of
f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})
in Eq. (
6
), we have
f_{n}(y_{n^{\prime}})=n-\frac{1}{2}
. By setting
i=n^{\prime}-1
, the result then follows from Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Now we are ready to discuss the distribution of
|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|
for uniformly distributed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. Since the case that
n\leq 3
is trivial, we now focus on the cases that
n
is large.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n>3
be an integer and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be independent random variables such that
\Pr(x_{i}=0)=\Pr(x_{i}=1)=\frac{1}{2}
for
i\in[n]
. Then for the word
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
, we have

(7)

\Pr\left({|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|-\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right]\geq cn\sqrt{n-1}}\right)\leq e^{-2c^{2}},

and

(8)

\Pr\left({|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|-\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right]\leq-cn\sqrt{n-1}}\right)\leq e^{-2c^{2}},

where
\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right]=\frac{n^{2}}{2}-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}+3
, and
c
is a positive constant.

Proof.

We define the Doob martingale
Z_{0}=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]
, and
Z_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]}\right]
by exposing one
x_{i}
at a time for
i\in[n]
.
Clearly, by Eq. (
6
) and Lemma
2.6
, we have
Z_{n}=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and

\displaystyle Z_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{x})n-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x})}s_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\left(n-\frac{n-1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{n(4\cdot 2^{2}-3\cdot 2+2)}{2^{2}}+\frac{6\cdot 2-4}{2^{2}}-4-2\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)+\frac{2}{2^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}-n+2-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}.

For every
1\leq i<n
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\middle|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\middle|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]},x_{i}=x_{i+1}\right]\Pr(x_{i}=x_{i+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\middle|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]},x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}\right]\Pr(x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}).

Then by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})-n\middle|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]},x_{i}=x_{i+1}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})-t(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})h(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})\middle|\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]},x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})+\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})\right]-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}t(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})\middle|x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}\right].

Note that by Eq. (
6
) and Lemma
2.6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})n-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{a(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})}s_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\cdot\frac{n-i+1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left[3(n-i)-4+\frac{1}{2^{n-i-2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}-\frac{in}{2}+2-\frac{3}{2}(n-i)+\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{n-i-1}},

and by Lemma
2.2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{x}_{[i+1,n]})\middle|x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}\right]=2-\frac{1}{2^{n-i-1}}.

It then follows that

(9)

Z_{i}=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})+\frac{n^{2}}{2}-\frac{in}{2}+2-\frac{3}{2}(n-i)-\frac{1}{2^{n-i-1}}-t(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1,i]})\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n-i}}\right).

Notice that
Z_{n}=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
also satisfies Eq. (
9
). Thus, for
1<i\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}

\displaystyle=

4.
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze the distribution of
|L_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})|
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}
by Azuma’s inequality. The numerical result suggests that

are more concentrated than we expected. Specifically, the gap between the simulation results and the bounds in Theorem

and Theorem

is still large (see Appendix

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{n}

is in general difficult and left open. Very recently, He and Ye

]
considered the case of radius two, and further gave the concentration bound for
