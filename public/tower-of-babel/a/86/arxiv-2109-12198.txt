Wasserstein Contraction Bounds on Closed Convex Domains with Applications to Stochastic Adaptive Control
Tyler Lekang, Andrew Lamperski

Abstract
This paper is motivated by the problem of quantitatively bounding the convergence of adaptive control methods for stochastic systems to a stationary distribution. Such bounds are useful for analyzing statistics of trajectories and determining appropriate step sizes for simulations. To this end, we extend a methodology from (unconstrained) stochastic differential equations (SDEs) which provides contractions in a specially chosen Wasserstein distance. This theory focuses on unconstrained SDEs with fairly restrictive assumptions on the drift terms. Typical adaptive control schemes place constraints on the learned parameters and their update rules violate the drift conditions. To this end, we extend the contraction theory to the case of constrained systems represented by reflected stochastic differential equations and generalize the allowable drifts. We show how the general theory can be used to derive quantitative contraction bounds on a nonlinear stochastic adaptive regulation problem.

III-A

Problem Setup

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will examine contractivity properties of reflected stochastic differential equations of the form:

\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}={H}(\bm{x}_{t})\text{d}t+G\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{v}_{t}\text{d}\bm{\mu}(t),

(2)

where
G
is an invertible
n\times n

\bm{w}_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion, and
\bm{\psi}_{t}=-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{v}_{s}\text{d}\bm{\mu}(s)
is a bounded variation reflection process that enforces that

for all

whenever
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. In this case,

has mean zero and

.

When

is Lipschitz, it can be shown that

is the unique bounded variation process such that for all

and
\bm{v}_{t}\in N_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}_{t})
,
\|\bm{v}_{t}\|\in\{0,1\}
,
\bm{\mu}
is a random measure with
\bm{\mu}([0,t])<\infty
, and the solution has
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. See
[
21
,
22
]
. The Lipschitz condition can be relaxed to
{H}
being locally Lipschitz, provided that the process is non-explosive. See Section 2.4 of
[
23
]
.

Reflected stochastic differential equations can be simulated numerically via a projected Euler method:

\bm{x}_{t+\eta}\approx\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}_{t}+\eta{H}(\bm{x}_{t})+G(\bm{w}_{t+\eta}-\bm{w}_{t})).

See
[
21
,
24
]
. In other words, the effect of
\bm{\psi}_{t}
is the continuous time limit of a projection operation.

III-B

Assumptions

The first requirement is a one-sided growth condition. We assume that there is a function
\kappa(r):(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{1}s\kappa(s)ds<\infty
and a non-negative number
\alpha
such that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
r=\|x-y\|
the following bound holds:

\langle x-y,{H}(x)-{H}(y)\rangle\leq\kappa(r)r^{2}+\alpha r(\|x\|+\|y\|)

(3)

This one-sided growth condition generalizes the one-sided Lipschitz condition from
[
20
]
, which corresponds to the special case with
\alpha=0
. The extra terms are required to handle the application to adaptive control in Section
IV
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the generator associated with the process
\bm{x}_{t}
. Specifically, for any function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

(\mathcal{A}g)(x)=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}h^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{x}_{h})|\bm{x}_{0}=x]-g(x)).

We assume that there is a twice continuously differentiable Lyapunov function
{V}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
and positive numbers
\lambda
and
C
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
t\geq 0
:

(\mathcal{A}{V})(x)\leq C-\lambda{V}(x).

(4)

We assume that
{V}(x)
increases monotonically with
\|x\|
. Specifically, there is a strictly monotonically increasing function
\phi
such that
{V}(x)=\phi(\|x\|)
. We will further assume that
{V}
grows at least linearly with
\|x\|
.

Let
R_{1}
be the diameter of the set
\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}|{V}(x)+{V}(y)\leq 4C/\lambda\}
. Linear growth implies that
R_{1}
is finite. By construction, if
\|x-y\|>R_{1}
, then

(\mathcal{A}{V})(x)+(\mathcal{A}{V})(y)\leq-(\lambda/2)({V}(x)+{V}(y)).

(5)

Let
M
be a positive number such that
M\geq R_{1}
and for all
x
with
\|x\|\geq M
, the following bound holds:

{V}(x)\geq\max\left\{\frac{2}{\lambda}(\alpha\|x\|+C),\frac{4C}{\lambda}\left(2\|x\|+1\right)\right\}.

(6)

Let
R_{2}
be the diameter of
\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}|\|x\|\leq M\textrm{ and }\|y\|\leq M\}
. Note that
R_{2}\leq 2M
by the triangle inequality.

In Section
IV
we take
{V}(x)=\|x\|^{2}+1
, so the growth conditions are automatically satisfied.

III-C

Background on Wasserstein Distances

Our main theory describes convergence in a Wasserstein distance.
To state the result, some basic concepts from optimal transport are required. See
[
25
]
for a general reference. Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures over
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the standard Borel sigma algebra. A measure,
\Gamma
, over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is called a
coupling
of
P
and
Q
if its marginals are
P
and
Q
, respectively. In other words, for any Borel set
S
, we have
\Gamma(S\times\mathcal{X})=P(S)
and
\Gamma(\mathcal{X}\times S)=Q(S)
. Let
{C}(P,Q)
denote the set of all couplings of
P
and
Q
.

If
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is a metric, the induced
q
-Wasserstein distance between
P
and
Q
is defined by:

W^{q}_{\rho}(P,Q)=\inf_{\Gamma\in{C}(P,Q)}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\rho(x,y)^{q}\text{d}\Gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/q}

(7)

For simple notation, we follow the convention that
W_{\rho}:=W_{\rho}^{1}
for general
\rho
and for the norm,
W^{q}:=W^{q}_{\|\cdot\|}
.

III-D

Main Contraction Result

The idea behind
[
20
]
is to construct a new metric for which convergence of
W_{\rho}
can be tractably analyzed, and then use the result to examine more standard measures such as
W^{q}
and the total variation distance. We follow a similar approach, but the metric must be modified to account for the more general growth condition.

Our metric will have the form:

\rho(x,y)=\left[f(\|x-y\|)+\gamma{V}(x)+\gamma{V}(y)+\right.\\
{V}(x)\lor\phi(M)+{V}(y)\lor\phi(M)\left.\right]\mathbb{I}(x\neq y).

(8)

Recall that
\phi
is a function such that
V(x)=\phi(\|x\|)
and the
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function. Here
\gamma
is a positive number defined below.

The function
f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is defined via the following chain of definitions:

\displaystyle h(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r}s\kappa(s)\text{d}s+\alpha Mr\right)

(9a)

\displaystyle\varphi(r)

\displaystyle=e^{-h(r)}

(9b)

\displaystyle\Phi(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\varphi(s)\text{d}s

(9c)

\displaystyle\xi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\varphi(s)^{-1}\text{d}s

(9d)

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R_{2}}(\Phi(s)/\varphi(s))\text{d}s

(9e)

\displaystyle g(r)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\xi}{4}\int_{0}^{r\land R_{1}}\varphi(s)^{-1}\text{d}s-\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{0}^{r\land R_{2}}(\Phi(s)/\varphi(s))\text{d}s

(9f)

\displaystyle f(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r\land R_{2}}\varphi(s)g(s)\text{d}s.

(9g)

Here
\sigma_{\min}(G)>0
is the smallest singular value of
G
.

Additionally, we set

\gamma=\frac{\xi\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}}{4C}.

(10)

The general contraction result is given below. The proof is given in appendix
A
.

Theorem 1

The function
\rho(x,y)

is a metric over
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
be two solutions to (
2
) with respective laws
\mathbb{P}_{t}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
. If the initial laws satisfy
\int_{\mathcal{X}}{V}(x)d\mathbb{P}_{0}(x)<\infty
and
\int_{\mathcal{X}}{V}(x)d\mathbb{Q}_{0}(x)
, then

W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t})\leq e^{-at}W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{Q}_{0}).

where
a=\min\{\lambda,\xi\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2},\beta\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}\}/2
.

The following corollary establishes convergence to a stationary distribution in total variation distance and norm-based
q
-Wassertein distances. It is analogous to Corollary 2.1 and Remark 3.4 of
[
20
]
. The proof is omitted, since the argument from
[
20
]
works with minimal modification.

Corollary 1

The system from (
2
)
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
such that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}{V}(x)d\pi(x)<\infty
.
Let
\bm{x}_{t}
be a solution to (
2
) with law
\mathbb{P}_{t}
such that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}{V}(x)d\mathbb{P}_{0}(x)<\infty
. If
{V}(x)\geq 1
for all

, then
\mathbb{P}_{t}
converges in total variation as:

\|\mathbb{P}_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\gamma^{-1}e^{-at}W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{0},\pi).

Say
q>1
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. If
{V}(x)\geq\|x\|^{q}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathbb{P}_{t}
converges with respect to

W^{q}(\mathbb{P}_{t},\pi)\leq 2^{1/p}\left(\gamma^{-1}W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{0},\pi)\right)^{1/q}e^{-at/q}.

III-E

Discussion

We describe the distinctions between our results and those of
[
20
]
. The most obvious is that ours applies to processes reflected to remain in the set
\mathcal{X}
, while
[
20
]
examines unconstrained SDEs. Additionally, our one-sided growth condition is used instead of a one-sided Lipschitz condition:

\langle x-y,{H}(x)-{H}(y)\rangle\leq\kappa(r)r^{2}.

with the same definition of
\kappa
.
This condition, however, fails in very basic versions of the control problem from Section
IV
, thus necessitating the our condition (
3
).
Our main application utilizes quadratic Lyapunov functions, and so we restrict to the case of Lyapunov functions
{V}(x)
with quadratic growth. This is less general than
[
20
]
, but leads to substantially simpler analysis. Additionally, this leads to a much simpler and more explicit metric.

IV
Application to Adaptive Regulation

IV-A

Problem Setup

We analyze a stochastic variation of a model reference adaptive control problem examined in Chapter 9 of
[
1
]
.

The basic plant model has the form

\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}=[\bar{A}\bm{x}_{t}+B(\bar{\Omega}^{\top}\Psi(\bm{x}_{t})+\bm{u}_{t})]\text{d}t+G_{x}\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}^{x}.

(11)

Here
\bar{A}
is an unknown state matrix,
B
is a known input matrix,
\Psi
is a known vector of feature functions (which could be linear or nonlinear),
\bar{\Omega}
is an unknown matrix of parameters, and
G_{x}
is an unknown matrix scaling the Brownian motion. The state is
\bm{x}_{t}
and the inputs are
\bm{u}_{t}
. The setup in
[
1
]
also includes an unknown scaling factor on
B
, which we have omitted for simplicity.

We focus on the problem of adaptive regulation, while
[
1
]
examines tracking problems. In our setup, the matching assumption is that there is a known Hurwitz matrix,
A
, and an unknown feedback gain,
\bar{K}
, such that

\bar{A}+B\bar{K}=A.

Here
A
is the state matrix for the
reference system
.

It appears to be possible to extend the contraction theory to tracking problems, but this is left for future work.

If we knew
\bar{K}
and
\bar{\Omega}
, we could set
\bm{u}_{t}=\bar{K}\bm{x}_{t}-\bar{\Omega}^{\top}\Psi(\bm{x}_{t})
and render the system stochastically stable:

\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}=A\bm{x}_{t}\text{d}t+G_{x}\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}^{x}.

The challenge is that we do not know
\bar{K}
or
\bar{\Omega}
. So instead, we use
\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{K}_{t}\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{\top}\Psi(\bm{x}_{t})
, where
\bm{K}_{t}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
are estimates. We derive rules for their computation later.

To simplify notation, we set

\bar{\Theta}=\begin{bmatrix}-\bar{K}^{\top}\\
\bar{\Omega}\end{bmatrix},\>{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}-\bm{K}_{t}^{\top}\\
\bm{\Omega}_{t}\end{bmatrix},\>\Lambda(\bm{x}_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{t}\\
\Psi(\bm{x}_{t})\end{bmatrix}.

Then the dynamics of (
11
) with
\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{K}_{t}\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{\top}\Psi(\bm{x}_{t})
can be written as

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}=\big(A\,\bm{x}_{t}+B\,(\bar{\Theta}-{\bm{\Theta}}_{t})^{\top}\Lambda(\bm{x}_{t})\big)\text{d}t+G_{x}\,\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}^{x}.

(12)

where fixed matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
form a controllable pair
and
\bm{w}_{t}^{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is standard Brownian motion with coefficient matrix
G_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, which is fixed and invertible.

We assume that the function
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
is Lipschitz:

\|\Lambda(x)-\Lambda(y)\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{L}\|x-y\|_{2},

for some
\mathcal{L}>0
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm.

Assume that
\bar{\Theta}
and
{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}
are
L\times\ell
matrices, and set
m=L\ell
. Let
S:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times\ell}
be the reshaping function defined by
S(v)_{i,j}=v_{(i-1)\ell+j}

for
i=1,\ldots,L
and
j=1,\ldots,\ell
. Then
S
is an invertible linear function.

Let
\bar{\theta}=S^{-1}(\bar{\Theta})
be the unknown parameters and let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, containing

\bar{\theta}
. We assume that
\mathcal{K}
is known. Let
D
be the diameter of
\mathcal{K}
.

Now let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{K}
be the closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
containing the combined
state
\bm{z}_{t}=[\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\ {\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and assume that
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
has dynamics of the form

\displaystyle\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}={R}(\bm{z}_{t})\,\text{d}t+G_{\theta}\,\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}^{\theta}-\bm{v}_{t}^{\theta}\,\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{\theta}(t)\ ,

(13)

where
{R}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\bm{w}_{t}^{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is standard Brownian motion
with invertible coefficient matrix
G_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, and

\bm{\psi}_{t}^{\theta}=-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{v}_{s}^{\theta}\,\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{\theta}(s)
is a bounded variation reflection process that
enforces
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in\mathcal{K}
for all
t\geq 0
whenever
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
. Here we assume that the reflections are computed with respect to the standard Euclidean inner product for simplicity.

Under any norm of the form
\|z\|^{2}=x^{\top}Px+\theta^{\top}\theta
, the joint dynamics defined by (
12
) and (
13
) define a special case of the reflected stochastic differential equation (
2
). Indeed, if
z^{\top}=[x^{\top},\theta^{\top}]
, then every element of

has the form

, where

and

is defined with respect to the Euclidean inner product.

Remark 1

Our parameter tuning rule from (
13
) differs from typical methods from adaptive control in how it forces
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
to remain in the constraint set,
\mathcal{K}
. Indeed, our method uses reflection processes, which can be approximated in discrete time by convex projections.
For simple sets such as Euclidean balls and boxes, the convex projections have simple analytic formulas. More generally, for any convex set, the convex projection can be computed via optimization. In contrast, the parameters of adaptive control laws are commonly constrained using specialized projection operators that are designed for specific classes of convex sets
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

IV-B

Lyapunov-Based Adaptation

Here we follow a Lyapunov-based design procedure to design the update rule, (
13
), similar to the method from
[
1
]
.
The main differences are that we examine stochastic problems and the constraints are enforced by reflection.

First we construct the Lyapunov function candidate. Fix a positive definite
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then since
A
is Hurwitz, there is a unique positive definite
P
such that

A^{\top}P+P\,A=-Q.

For
z^{\top}=[x^{\top},\theta^{\top}]
,
we define the Lyapunov function candidate by:

{V}(z)=x^{\top}Px+(\theta-\bar{\theta})^{\top}(\theta-\bar{\theta})+1.

(14)

Define a norm over
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
by
\|z\|^{2}=x^{\top}Px+\theta^{\top}\theta
.

Theorem
1

requires that the Lyapunov function be a monotonic function of a norm. This could be attained using the affine coordinate transformation
\hat{z}^{\top}=[x^{\top},(\theta-\bar{\theta})^{\top}]
. In that case we can set
\hat{}{V}(\hat{z})={V}(z)=\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+1
. In the analysis below, we will work in the original coordinates.

To derive the required decrease condition, we use Itô’s formula:

\displaystyle\text{d}{V}(\bm{z}_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}{V}^{\top}\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}(\nabla_{x}^{2}\,{V})\,\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+\nabla_{\theta}{V}^{\top}\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}(\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,{V})\,\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

\displaystyle=2\,\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}P^{\top}\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}+\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}P\,\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+2\,({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bar{\theta})^{\top}\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}+\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}\big(A^{\top}P+PA\big)\,\bm{x}_{t}\,\text{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt-2\,\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}P\,B({\bm{\Theta}}_{t}-\bar{\Theta})^{\top}\Lambda(\bm{x}_{t})\,\text{d}t+2\,\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}P\,G_{x}\,\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}^{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}^{\top}P\,\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}+2\,\text{tr}\big(({\bm{\Theta}}_{t}-\bar{\Theta})^{\top}\text{d}{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\big)+\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}\text{d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

Proof of Theorem

Many of the arguments are similar to those of
[
20
]
. We give terse explanations of these, and then highlight the differences.

The main idea is to define an explicit coupling between
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
and show that
e^{at}\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})
is a supermartingale under this coupling. Then the definition of
W_{\rho}
from (
7
) followed by the supermartingale property show that:

W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t})\leq\mathbb{E}[\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})]\leq e^{-at}\mathbb{E}[\rho(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{y}_{0})].

The final bound from the theorem follows by taking an optimal coupling for
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
. This optimal coupling exists by Theorem 4.1 of
[
25
]
.

Showing That
\rho
is a Metric

First note that

[\gamma V(x)+\gamma V(y)+V(x)\lor\phi(M)+V(y)\lor\phi(M)]\mathbb{I}(x\neq y)

is a metric since
V(x)\geq 0
and
\phi(M)>0
. In particular, it is a weighted version of the Hamming metric.

Now since the sum of metrics is again a metric, it suffices to show that
f(\|x-y\|)
is a metric. This holds provided that
f
is concave, with
f^{\prime}(0)>0
and
f(0)=0
. See

[
20
,
19
]
for details.

To this end, it suffices to show that
f(0)=0
,
f
is monotonically increasing, and
f(a+b)\leq f(a)+f(b)
for any
a,b\geq 0
. The property
f(0)=0
is immediate from (
9g
). Monotonicity follows because
\varphi(r)>0
and
g(r)\geq 1/2
by construction.
In particular, we get concavity since for
r\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{R_{1},R_{2}\}
it holds that

f^{\prime\prime}(r)=-h^{\prime}(r)f^{\prime}(r)-\frac{\xi}{4}\mathbb{I}(r<R_{1})-\frac{\beta}{4}\Phi(r)\mathbb{I}(r<R_{2}).

(20)

In particular
f^{\prime\prime}(r)\leq 0
, so that
f^{\prime}
is monotonically decreasing.
The triangle inequality property then follows because

f(a+b)=f(a)+\int_{a}^{a+b}f^{\prime}(s)\text{d}s\\
\leq f(a)+\int_{0}^{b}f^{\prime}(s)\text{d}s=f(a)+f(b).

(21)

Reflection Coupling

The key approach from
[
20
]
is to create an explicit coupling between
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
and then bound
\mathbb{E}[\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})]
. By the definition of the Wasserstein distance from (
7
),
W_{\rho}(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t})\leq\mathbb{E}[\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})]
.
The specific coupling is known as a
reflection coupling

[
27
]
.

To define the reflection coupling, let
{\bm{\tau}}
be coupling time:

{\bm{\tau}}=\inf\{t|\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t}\}.

Let
\bm{u}_{t}=(\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y}_{t})/\|\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y}_{t}\|
and let
\bm{u}_{t}^{\star}
be its dual vector so that
\|\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}=\bm{u}_{t}^{\star}\bm{u}_{t}
. The reflection coupling is given by the following definitions of
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{x}_{t}

\displaystyle={H}(\bm{x}_{t})\text{d}t+G\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{v}^{x}_{t}\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{x}(t)

\displaystyle\text{d}\bm{y}_{t}

\displaystyle={H}(\bm{y}_{t})\text{d}t+(I_{n}-2\bm{u}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}^{\star}\mathbb{I}(t<{\bm{\tau}}))G\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{v}_{t}^{y}\text{d}\bm{\mu}_{t}^{y}(t).

Here
-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{v}_{s}^{x}\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{x}(s)
and
-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{v}_{s}^{y}\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{y}(s)
are the unique bounded-variation reflection processes that ensure that
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
remain in
\mathcal{X}
.

Note that
\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t}
for all

.

Unfortunately, “reflection” has two unrelated meanings in this setup: 1) reflecting the processes to remain within

and 2) reflecting the Brownian motion via

The Supermartingale Property

Here we show that
e^{at}\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})
is a supermartingale.
This is an exercise in non-smooth Itô calculus for continuous semimartingales. A good reference is Chapter 29 of
[
28
]
.
In the discussion below,
\bm{m}_{t}
will denote a local martingale. We use this notation in several places to denote different processes. The specific value of
\bm{m}_{t}
of will not matter, since it will have zero mean.

Let
\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y}_{t}
and
\bm{r}_{t}=\|\bm{z}_{t}\|
. We will evaluate
d\bm{r}_{t}
via Itô’s formula when
\bm{r}_{t}>0
. (Note that
\bm{r}_{t}>0
if and only if

[
20
]
, but here extra reflection terms,
-\bm{v}^{x}_{t}d\bm{\mu}^{x}(t)
and
-\bm{v}^{y}_{t}d\bm{\mu}^{y}(t)
, appear.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
u=z/\|z\|
. Then we have the Taylor expansion:

\|z+\delta\|=\|z\|+\langle u,\delta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\|z\|}\langle(I_{n}-uu^{\star})\delta,\delta\rangle+o(\|\delta\|^{2}).

Itô’s formula for continuous-semimartingales gives

\text{d}\bm{r}_{t}=\langle\bm{u}_{t},{H}(\bm{x}_{t})-{H}(\bm{y}_{t})\rangle\text{d}t+\langle\bm{u}_{t},\bm{v}_{t}^{y}\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{y}(t)-\bm{v}_{t}^{x}\text{d}\bm{\mu}^{x}(t)\rangle\\
+\frac{1}{2\|\bm{z}_{t}\|}\text{tr}\left((I_{n}-\bm{u}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}^{\star})4\bm{u}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}^{\star}GG^{\star}\bm{u}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}^{\star}\right)\text{d}t+2\langle\bm{u}_{t},G\text{d}\bm{w}_{t}\rangle.

(22)

A significantly simpler upper bound on
\text{d}\bm{r}_{t}
can be obtained.
Recall that

\bm{u}_{t}=(\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y}_{t})/\|\bm{z}_{t}\|
,
\bm{v}_{t}^{x}\in N_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}_{t})
, and
\bm{v}_{t}^{y}\in N_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{y}_{t})
. It follows from the definition of the normal cone that
\langle\bm{u}_{t},\bm{v}_{t}^{y}\rangle\leq 0
and
-\langle\bm{u}_{t},\bm{v}_{t}^{x}\rangle\leq 0
. Since
\bm{\mu}^{x}
and
\bm{\mu}^{y}
are non-negative measures, it follows that the corresponding terms can be bounded above by
0
. Furthermore, the trace term vanishes, thus giving:

d\bm{r}_{t}\leq\langle\bm{u}_{t},{H}(\bm{x}_{t})-{H}(\bm{y}_{t})\rangle\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}.

(23)

Recall that
\bm{m}_{t}
denotes a zero-mean local martingale.

Now we aim to bound
\text{d}f(\bm{r}_{t})
. This part is similar to the analysis from
[
20
]
. The main distinction is that
[
20
]
examines the special case of
G=I_{n}
, while the more general case leads to slightly more complex formulas. We extend
f
to the entire real line by setting
f(r)=r
for
r\leq 0
. Note that
f
is a non-smooth concave function which is left-differentiable everywhere. Denote the left-derivative by
f_{-}^{\prime}(r)
. Let
\bm{L}^{r}_{t}
denote the right-continuous local time of
\bm{r}_{t}
. Then Theorem 29.5 of
[
28
]
states that

f(\bm{r}_{t})-f(\bm{r}_{0})=\int_{0}^{t}f_{-}^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{s})\text{d}\bm{r}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bm{L}^{r}_{t}\text{d}f_{-}^{\prime}(r),

(24)

where the integral on the right is a Stieltjes integral. (Specifically, it is an integral with respect to the signed measure
\nu
defined by
\nu([a,b))=f_{-}^{\prime}(b)-f_{-}^{\prime}(a)
for
a<b

Theorem 29.5 of
[
28
]
also states that for any measurable function

, we have

\int_{0}^{t}b(\bm{r}_{s})\text{d}[\bm{r}]_{s}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b(r)\bm{L}_{t}^{r}\text{d}r,

(25)

almost surely.
Here
[\bm{r}]_{t}=4\int_{0}^{t}\|G^{\star}\bm{u}_{s}\|^{2}\text{d}s
is the quadratic variation. Since
G
is assumed to be invertible, it follows that
4\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}t\leq[\bm{r}]_{t}\leq 4\|G\|_{2}^{2}t
, where
\sigma_{\min}(G)>0
is the smallest singular value of
G
.

Note that
f
is twice continuously differentiable except at
\{R_{1},R_{2}\}
. For compact notation, set
\zeta=4\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}
. Then (
25
) implies that
\bm{r}_{t}
spends zero time at

almost surely since:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{s}\in\{R_{1},R_{2}\})\text{d}s\leq\zeta^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{s}\in\{R_{1},R_{2}\})\text{d}[\bm{r}]_{s}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}(r\in\{R_{1},R_{2}\})\bm{L}_{t}^{r}\text{d}r=0.

(26)

The last equality follows because
\bm{L}_{t}^{r}
is right continuous in
r
. More generally, this argument shows that
\bm{r}_{t}
spends zero time in any finite collection of points.

The local time is non-negative, and the measure defined by
\nu([a,b))=f_{-}^{\prime}(b)-f_{-}^{\prime}(a)
is non-positive. Thus, we get the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bm{L}_{t}^{r}\text{d}f_{-}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}(r\notin\{R_{1},R_{2}\})\bm{L}_{t}^{r}\text{d}f_{-}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}(r\notin\{R_{1},R_{2}\})f^{\prime\prime}(r)\bm{L}_{t}^{r}\text{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{s}\notin\{R_{1},R_{2}\})f^{\prime\prime}(\bm{r}_{s})\text{d}[\bm{r}]_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\bm{r}_{s})\text{d}s.

(27)

The last inequality follows from the bounds on the quadratic variation, non-negativity of
f^{\prime\prime}
and the fact that
\bm{r}_{s}
spends zero time in
\{R_{1},R_{2}\}
almost surely.

The preceding argument then implies that for almost all
t\in[0,{\bm{\tau}})
, the following holds almost surely:

\displaystyle\text{d}f(\bm{r}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq[f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\langle\bm{u}_{t},{H}(\bm{x}_{t})-{H}(\bm{y}_{t})\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\zeta f^{\prime\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})]\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq[f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})(\kappa(r)r+\alpha(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|+\|\bm{y}_{t}\|)+\frac{1}{2}\zeta f^{\prime\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})]\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\left[f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\alpha(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|+\|\bm{y}_{t}\|-2M)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{\xi\zeta}{8}\mathbb{I}(r<R_{1})-\frac{\beta\zeta}{8}f(r)\mathbb{I}(r<R_{2})\right]\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}.

The first inequality combined (
23
), (
24
), and (
27
). The second inequality uses the one-sided growth bound (
3
). The third inequality uses (
20
), combined with the following facts, which hold by construction:

\displaystyle f(r)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(r)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\zeta}\left(r\kappa(r)+2\alpha M\right).

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq\beta\zeta/8=\beta\sigma_{\min}(G)/2.

The fact that
f(r)\leq\Phi(r)
can be deduced from the definitions of
\Phi
and
f
from (
9
), since
g(r)\in[1/2,1]
.

Using the Lyapunov assumption, we have

\displaystyle\text{d}{V}(\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq(C-\lambda{V}(\bm{x}_{t}))\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}

\displaystyle\text{d}{V}(\bm{y}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq(C-\lambda{V}(\bm{y}_{t}))\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}.

Now, we wish to bound
\text{d}({V}(\bm{x}_{t})\lor\phi(M))
. Recall that
{V}(x)=\phi(\|x\|)
and
\phi(r)
is strictly monotonically increasing.
We will follow a similar strategy as used to bound
\text{d}f(\bm{r}_{t})
.
For compact notation, set
\bm{V}_{t}={V}(\bm{x}_{t})
, let
\hat{\bm{L}}_{t}^{r}
be the right-continuous local time of
\bm{V}_{t}
, and let
F(r)=\max\{r,\phi(M)\}
. Note that
F
is convex and smooth for
r\neq\phi(M)
. Furthermore, (
26
) implies that
\bm{r}_{t}
spends zero time at
M
, and thus
\bm{V}_{t}
spends zero time at
\phi(M)
.

Note
that the measure defined by
\hat{\nu}([a,b))=F_{-}^{\prime}(b)-F_{-}^{\prime}(a)
is a Dirac delta centered at
\phi(M)
. Thus, the calculation analogous to (
24
) gives

F(\bm{V}_{t})-F(\bm{V}_{0})=\int_{0}^{t}F_{-}^{\prime}(\bm{V}_{s})\text{d}\bm{V}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{L}}_{t}^{\phi(M)}.

Note that the local time,
\bm{L}_{t}^{\phi(M)}
only changes at the times when
\bm{V}_{t}=\phi(M)
. See
[
28
]
.
Indeed, the local time is defined by:

\hat{\bm{L}}^{\phi(M)}_{t}=|\bm{V}_{t}-\phi(M)|-|\bm{V}_{0}-\phi(M)|\\
-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathbb{I}(\bm{V}_{s}>\phi(M))-\mathbb{I}(\bm{V}_{s}\leq\phi(M))\right)\text{d}\bm{V}_{s}

(28)

Thus, the local time remains unchanged on intervals in which
\bm{V}_{t}>\phi(M)
or
\bm{V}_{t}<\phi(M)
.
Then, since the amount of time
\bm{V}_{t}
spends at
\phi(M)
is zero almost surely, we have that for almost all
t<{\bm{\tau}}
:

\displaystyle\text{d}F(\bm{V}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(\bm{V}_{t}>\phi(M))\text{d}\bm{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)\text{d}{V}(\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)(C-\lambda{V}(\bm{x}_{t}))+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)(\alpha\|\bm{x}_{t}\|+\lambda{V}(\bm{x}_{t})/2)+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}.

The first inequality is due to the Lyapunov assumption, while the second inequality follows from the assumption from (
6
).

Finally, all of the differentials can be combined to give for almost all
t<{\bm{\tau}}
:

d(e^{at}\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t}))\leq e^{at}\Big[af(\bm{r}_{t})-\frac{\beta\zeta}{8}f(\bm{r}_{t})\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{t}<R_{2})+\\
\left.f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\alpha(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|+\|\bm{y}_{t}\|-2M)-\frac{\xi\zeta}{8}\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{t}<R_{1})\right.\\
\left.+\gamma(2C+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{x}_{t})+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{y}_{t}))\right.\\
\left.+\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)((a-\lambda/2){V}(\bm{x}_{t})-\alpha\|\bm{x}_{t}\|)\right.\\
+\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)((a-\lambda/2){V}(\bm{x}_{t})-\alpha\|\bm{x}_{t}\|)\Big]\text{d}t+\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}.

(29)

Now we will explain why all of the positive terms inside the brackets get canceled by an appropriate negative term.

Note that for
\bm{r}_{t}<R_{2}
, the
af(\bm{r}_{t})
term is canceled by
\frac{\beta\zeta}{8}f(\bm{r}_{t})
, since
a\leq\beta\eta/8=\beta\sigma_{\min}(G)/2
. When
\bm{r}_{t}\geq R_{2}
, Lemma 2.1 of
[
20
]
shows that
af(\bm{r}_{t})
is canceled by
\mathcal{A}{V}(\bm{x}_{t})+\mathcal{A}{V}(\bm{y}_{t})
.

In this case, the triangle inequality implies that
2\max\{\|\bm{x}_{t}\|,\|\bm{y}_{t}\|\}\geq\bm{r}_{t}\geq M/2
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\|\bm{x}_{t}\|\geq\|\bm{y}_{t}\|
. Then

a\leq\lambda/2
, combined with (
6
) imply

\displaystyle\gamma(2C+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{x}_{t})+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{y}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\gamma(2C-(\lambda/2){V}(\bm{x}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq-\gamma 4C\|\bm{x}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\xi\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}}{2}\bm{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq-a\bm{r}_{t}

This cancels
af(\bm{r}_{t})
since
f(\bm{r}_{t})\leq\bm{r}_{t}
.

Now consider the case that
f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\alpha(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|+\|\bm{y}_{t}\|-2M)>0
. Then, since
f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\geq 0
, at least one of
\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M
or
\|\bm{y}_{t}\|>M
must hold. If
\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M
, then since
f^{\prime}(\bm{r}_{t})\leq 1
, the corresponding term is canceled by

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}(\|\bm{x}_{t}\|>M)((a-\lambda/2){V}(\bm{x}_{t})-\alpha\|\bm{x}_{t}\|)\leq-\alpha\|\bm{x}_{t}\|.

A similar cancellation occurs if
\|\bm{y}_{t}\|>M
.

The only remaining positive term is now
\gamma 2C=\xi\sigma_{\min}(G)^{2}/2=\xi\zeta/8
. In the case that
\bm{r}_{t}<R_{1}
, this term is canceled by
-(\xi\zeta/8)\mathbb{I}(\bm{r}_{t}<R_{1})
. When
\bm{r}_{t}\geq R_{1}
, the definition of
R_{1}
along with
a\leq\lambda/2
imply that

2C+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{x}_{t})+(a-\lambda){V}(\bm{y}_{t})\\
\leq 2C-(\lambda/2)({V}(\bm{x}_{t})+{V}(\bm{y}_{t}))\leq 0.

(30)

Thus, this term is canceled as well.

We have shown that
\text{d}(e^{at}\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t}))\leq\text{d}\bm{m}_{t}
.

Localization

The last step in proving the theorem requires ruling out the possibility that
\mathbb{E}[e^{at}\rho(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})]=\infty
. This is performed in
[
20
]
by a localization argument using stopping times
{\bm{\tau}}_{k}=\inf\{t|\|\bm{r}_{t}\|\leq 1/k\ \textrm{ or }\max\{\|\bm{x}_{t}\|,\|\bm{y}_{t}\|\}\geq k\}
with
{\bm{\tau}}_{k}\to{\bm{\tau}}
. The argument works without change in this setting.
∎
