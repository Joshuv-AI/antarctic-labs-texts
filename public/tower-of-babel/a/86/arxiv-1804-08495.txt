Correlations for symplectic and orthogonal Schur measures
Dan Betea

Abstract
We show, using either Fock space techniques or Macdonald difference operators, that certain symplectic and orthogonal analogues of Okounkov’s Schur measure are determinantal with kernels given by explicit double contour integrals. We give two applications: one equates certain Toeplitz+Hankel determinants of random matrix theory with appropriate Fredholm determinants and computes Szegő asymptotics for the former; another finds that the simplest examples of said measures exhibit discrete sine kernel asymptotics in the bulk and Airy 2 to 1 kernel—along with a certain dual—asymptotics at the edge. We believe the edge behavior to be universal.

Introduction

The classical Cauchy identities for the symplectic and orthogonal characters, which pair one such function with a Schur polynomial, read as follows—see, e.g.,
[

,
Sun90
]

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}sp_{\lambda}(x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{N}^{\pm 1})s_{\lambda}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(1-y_{i}y_{j})\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\frac{1}{(1-y_{i}x_{j})(1-y_{i}x_{j}^{-1})},

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}(x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{N}^{\pm 1})s_{\lambda}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq N}(1-y_{i}y_{j})\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\frac{1}{(1-y_{i}x_{j})(1-y_{i}x_{j}^{-1})},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}(x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{N}^{\pm 1},1)s_{\lambda}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(1-y_{i}y_{j})\prod_{1\leq i\leq N}(1+y_{i})\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\frac{1}{(1-y_{i}x_{j})(1-y_{i}x_{j}^{-1})}.

They can be used to construct (a priori complex) probability measures on partitions via

Schur, symplectic, orthogonal and free fermions

2.1
Schur functions, symplectic and orthogonal characters

In this section we gather some generalities on Schur functions and on symplectic/orthogonal characters.

A
partition
is a non-increasing sequence of non-negative integers
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots
, called
parts
, only finitely many of which are non-zero. It has
size

|\lambda|=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
and its
length
, denoted
\ell(\lambda)
, is the number of (non-zero) parts. For a partition
\lambda
, its
conjugate
, denoted
\lambda^{\prime}
, is defined by
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=|\{j:\lambda_{j}\leq i\}|
.

We denote by
\Lambda
the algebra of symmetric functions in a countable alphabet
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
. Algebraic bases are given by the elementary and complete homogeneous symmetric functions
e_{k}(Y),h_{k}(Y),k\geq 0
, themselves defined by their generating series

\sum_{i\geq 0}h_{k}(Y)t^{k}=\prod_{i}\frac{1}{1-ty_{i}}:=H(Y;t),\qquad\sum_{i\geq 0}e_{k}(Y)t^{k}=\prod_{i}(1+ty_{i}):=E(Y;t).

(2.1)

Note
H(Y;t)E(Y;-t)=1
. Another useful basis is that of the Newton powersums
p_{k}=\sum_{i}y_{i}^{k},k\geq 1
with generating series

\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}(Y)t^{k}}{k}=H(Y;t).

(2.2)

Given alphabets
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots),Z=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots)
we use the notation

H(Y;Z):=\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-y_{i}z_{j}}=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}(Y)p_{k}(Z)}{k},\ E(Y;Z):=\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-y_{i}z_{j}}=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}p_{k}(Y)p_{k}(Z)}{k}

(2.3)

and we also reserve the following for later purposes:

h_{sp}(Y):=\prod_{i<j}(1-y_{i}y_{j}),\qquad h_{o}(Y):=\prod_{i\leq j}(1-y_{i}y_{j}).

(2.4)

Schur functions

s_{\lambda}(Y)
form yet another linear basis of
\Lambda
and are defined, for now and our purposes, by the Jacobi–Trudi formulae:

s_{\lambda}(Y)=\det[h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}(Y)]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}=\det[e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+j}(Y)]_{1\leq i,j\leq\lambda_{1}}.

(2.5)

Using (
2.5
) one could actually define
skew
Schur functions as follows: for a partition
\mu\subseteq\lambda
,
s_{\lambda/\mu}(Y)=\det[h_{\lambda_{i}-i-(\mu_{j}-j)}(Y)]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}
. When
\mu=0
is the
empty partition
,
s_{\lambda/0}=s_{\lambda}
. For more on symmetric functions, the reader should consult Chapter I of the scriptures
[
Mac95
]
.

Let
X=(x_{1},x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N}^{-1})
be a finite alphabet containing variables and inverses, and let
\tilde{X}
stand for either
X
or
(X,1):=(x_{1},x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N}^{-1},1)
. Given a partition
\lambda
of length
\leq N
, we define the
BC
-symmetric
(symmetric under permuting and inverting variables)
symplectic and orthogonal characters
via the following Jacobi–Trudi formulae:

\begin{split}sp_{\lambda}(X)&=\frac{1}{2}\det[h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}(X)+h_{\lambda_{i}-i-j+2}(X)]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}\\
&=\det[e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+j}(X)-e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i-j}(X)]_{1\leq i,j\leq\lambda_{1}},\\
o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})&=\det[h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}(\tilde{X})-h_{\lambda_{i}-i-j}(\tilde{X})]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\det[e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+j}(\tilde{X})+e_{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i-j+2}(\tilde{X})]_{1\leq i,j\leq\lambda_{1}}.\end{split}

(2.6)

They are the irreducible characters of the symplectic and orthogonal groups where the
x
’s should be viewed as eigenvalues of the corresponding conjugacy classes of matrices
2
2

2

In the case of the orthogonal groups, the situation depends on whether the group has even or odd rank; we use
\tilde{X}
to stand for eigenvalues from both.
. For more on these aspects, we refer the reader to Chapters 24 and onwards of
[
FH91
]
, as well as the review
[
Sun90
]
and references therein.

Yet another way to define
sp
and
o
is in terms of the expansion in
skew
Schur functions. To wit, one has

sp_{\lambda}(X)=\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha|/2}s_{\lambda/\alpha}(X),\qquad o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})=\sum_{\beta}(-1)^{|\beta|/2}s_{\lambda/\beta}(\tilde{X})

(2.7)

where
\alpha
ranges over all partitions having
Frobenius coordinates

\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots|a_{1}+1,a_{2}+1,\dots)
;
\beta
ranges over all partitions having Frobenius coordinates
\beta=(b_{1}+1,b_{2}+1,\dots|b_{1},b_{2},\dots)
; the empty partition is in both sets; and for a partition
\lambda=(a_{1},\dots|b_{1},\dots)
we recall the Frobenius coordinates are
(a_{i},b_{i})=(\max(\lambda_{i}-i,0),\max(\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i,0))
.

The important properties, for probabilistic purposes, that these functions satisfy are the Cauchy and dual Cauchy identities—see, e.g.,
[

,
Sun90
]
:

\begin{split}\sum_{\lambda}sp_{\lambda}(X)s_{\lambda}(Y)=h_{sp}(Y)H(X;Y),\qquad\sum_{\lambda}sp_{\lambda}(X)s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(Y)=h_{o}(Y)E(X;Y),\\
\sum_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})s_{\lambda}(Y)=h_{o}(Y)H(\tilde{X};Y),\qquad\sum_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(Y)=h_{sp}(Y)E(\tilde{X};Y)\\
\end{split}

(2.8)

which are analogues of the well-known

identities for Schur functions:

\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(Y)s_{\lambda}(Z)=H(Y;Z),\qquad\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(Y)s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(Z)=E(Y;Z).

(2.9)

The symplectic and orthogonal characters admit lifts to the algebra of symmetric functions. If
Z
is a normal alphabet in a countable number of variables (i.e., not containing any inverses), then

and

are defined either by equation

by replacing

, or by expanding

and

and picking the coefficients of

. We call these lifts the

. They satisfy the natural property that if one then sets

and
z_{j}=0,j>2N
then

becomes the
BC

and similarly for

.

Finally we can generalize the above a bit further by forgetting the variables
X,Y,Z
and noting these functions could be defined entirely in terms of the
e
’s,
h
’s or
p
’s. To wit, a
specialization

\rho
is an algebra homomorphism
\rho:\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
. For
f\in\Lambda
we write
f(\rho)
en lieu of
\rho(f)
. We note specifying
\rho
is equivalent to specifying either of the three collections
h_{k}(\rho),e_{k}(\rho),p_{n}(\rho),k\geq 0,n\geq 1
(with the convention that
h_{0}(\rho)=e_{0}(\rho)=1
). The
H
and
E
generating series of
\rho
are respectively

H(\rho;t):=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}(\rho)t^{k}=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}(\rho)t^{k}}{k},\qquad E(\rho;t):=\sum_{k\geq 0}e_{k}(\rho)t^{k}=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}p_{k}(\rho)t^{k}}{k}

(2.10)

noting again
H(\rho;t)E(\rho;-t)=1
. Further, if
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
are two specializations, we define

\begin{split}H(\rho;\rho^{\prime})&:=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}(\rho)p_{k}(\rho^{\prime})}{k},\\
h_{sp}(\rho)&:=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{p_{2k}(\rho)}{2k}-\frac{p^{2}_{k}(\rho)}{2k}\right),\qquad h_{o}(\rho):=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(-\frac{p_{2k}(\rho)}{2k}-\frac{p^{2}_{k}(\rho)}{2k}\right).\end{split}

(2.11)

We remark that we think of
\rho
in terms of the numbers

which completely determine it.

The meaning of the
s_{\lambda}(\rho),sp_{\lambda}(\rho),o_{\lambda}(\rho)

2.5
) and (
2.6
) with
\rho

. For

and

). Finally if
\omega:\Lambda\to\Lambda

\omega(p_{n})=(-1)^{n-1}p_{n}
then one has:

\omega(s_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}},\qquad\omega(sp_{\lambda})=o_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

(2.12)

The Cauchy identities (
2.8
) become:

\begin{split}\sum_{\lambda}sp_{\lambda}(\rho^{+})s_{\lambda}(\rho^{-})&=h_{sp}(\rho^{-})H(\rho^{+};\rho^{-})=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{p_{k}(\rho)p_{k}(\rho^{\prime})}{k}+\frac{p_{2k}(\rho)}{2k}-\frac{p^{2}_{k}(\rho)}{2k}\right),\\
\sum_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}(\rho^{+})s_{\lambda}(\rho^{-})&=h_{o}(\rho^{-})H(\rho^{+};\rho^{-})=\exp\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{p_{k}(\rho)p_{k}(\rho^{\prime})}{k}-\frac{p_{2k}(\rho)}{2k}-\frac{p^{2}_{k}(\rho)}{2k}\right).\end{split}

(2.13)

2.2
Free fermions and Fock space

We recall some standard material on the theory of discrete free fermions, useful for the study of
Schur measures
[
Oko01
]
and processes
[
OR03
]
. We follow the exposition of Okounkov
[
Oko01
, Appendix A]
and Baker
[
Bak96
]
for what is needed on symplectic and orthogonal characters and functions—see also
[
MJD00
]
and
[
Kac90
, Chapter14]
for the Schur case.

The
charged fermionic Fock space
, denoted
\mathcal{F}
, is the infinite
dimensional Hilbert space spanned by the orthonormal basis
|S\rangle
, where
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}
with
|S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}_{+}|<\infty
and
|\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}_{-}-S|<\infty
. We call the elements of
S

particles
. We also use the bra–ket notation throughout, denoting dual vectors by

\langle\cdot|
. A basis vector
|S\rangle
could be seen as the semi-infinite wedge product

|S\rangle=\underline{s_{1}}\wedge\underline{s_{2}}\wedge\underline{s_{3}}\wedge\cdots

where
s_{1}>s_{2}>s_{3}>\cdots
are the
particle positions
. States
S
are in bijection with pairs
(\lambda,c)
, where
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
is a partition and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
, via the following
combinatorial boson–fermion
correspondence:

(\lambda,c)\longleftrightarrow S(\lambda,c):=\{\lambda_{i}-i+1/2+c,\ i\geq 1\}.

(2.14)

Using this observation, we see that
\mathcal{F}=\oplus_{c\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{F}_{c}
where
\mathcal{F}_{c}
is spanned by partitions
\lambda
of charge
c
. We denote by
|\lambda,c\rangle
the vector corresponding to the pair
(\lambda,c)
, and by
|\lambda\rangle
the vector in
|\lambda,0\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
. Throughout this note we will only be interested in the charge 0 subspace
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. The vector
|0\rangle
, corresponding to the empty partition (of charge 0), is called the
vacuum
vector (sometimes, the
Fermi sea
).

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}
, define the free fermionic (Clifford) operators

\psi_{k}|S\rangle:=\underline{k}\wedge|S\rangle,\qquad\psi^{*}_{k}|S\rangle:=\begin{cases}(-1)^{i-1}s_{1}\wedge\dots s_{i-1}\wedge s_{i+1}\dots,&\text{if }k=s_{i}\text{ for some }i,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.15)

Informally,
\psi_{k}
tries to add a particle to the configuration
S
, while
\psi^{*}_{k}
tries to remove one (with an appropriate sign in both cases), and both kill the vector if a particle is there at site
k
(for
\psi_{k}
) or is absent (for
\psi^{*}
). Furthermore,
\psi_{k}
and
\psi_{k}^{*}
are adjoint under the inner product
\langle\lambda,c|\mu,d\rangle=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}\delta_{c,d}
and satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relations

\{\psi_{k},\psi_{\ell}^{*}\}=\delta_{k,\ell},\qquad\{\psi_{k},\psi_{\ell}\}=\{\psi_{k}^{*},\psi_{\ell}^{*}\}=0,\qquad k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}.

(2.16)

The generating series of these operators, the so-called
fermionic fields
, are:

\psi(z):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}}\psi_{k}z^{k},\qquad\psi^{*}(w):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}}\psi^{*}_{k}w^{-k}.

(2.17)

Observe that
\psi_{k}|0\rangle=\psi_{-k}^{*}|0\rangle=0
for
k<0
.

The
charge operator
, defined as
C:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\psi_{k}\psi^{*}_{k}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{-}}\psi^{*}_{k}\psi_{k}
acts diagonally on states
C|\lambda,c\rangle=c|\lambda,c\rangle
and has
\mathcal{F}_{0}
as kernel. The
shift operator

R
takes
\mathcal{F}_{c}\to\mathcal{F}_{c+1}
via
R|\lambda,c\rangle=|\lambda,c+1\rangle
.

We construct the

bosonic operators

\alpha_{n}
as follows:

\alpha_{n}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}}\psi_{k-n}\psi_{k}^{*},\qquad n=\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots

(2.18)

and for
n=0
,
\alpha_{0}=C
. We have

\alpha_{n}^{*}=\alpha_{-n}
, and
\alpha_{n}|0\rangle=0,n>0
. The bosons satisfy the Heisenberg commutations

[\alpha_{n},\alpha_{m}]=n\delta_{n,-m}

(2.19)

and commute thusly with the fermionic fields:

[\alpha_{n},\psi(z)]=z^{n}\psi(z),\qquad[\alpha_{n},\psi^{*}(w)]=-w^{n}\psi^{*}(w).

(2.20)

Define the
half-vertex operators

\Gamma_{\pm}(A)
by

\Gamma_{\pm}(A):=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}(A)\alpha_{\pm n}}{n}\right)

(2.21)

where we recall
p_{n}(A)
is the
n
-th Newton powersum in
A=(a_{1},\dots)
.
\Gamma_{-}(A)
is the adjoint of
\Gamma_{+}(A)
. If
A
consists of a single variable
x
, we write
\Gamma_{\pm}(x)
. Observe that
\Gamma_{\pm}(A)=\prod_{a\in A}\Gamma_{\pm}(a)
, a consequence of

\Gamma_{+}(A)\Gamma_{+}(B)=\Gamma_{+}(B)\Gamma_{+}(A).

(2.22)

The half-vertex operators act trivially on the vacuum and co-vacuum as follows:

\Gamma_{+}(A)|0\rangle=|0\rangle,\qquad\langle 0|\Gamma_{-}(A)=\langle 0|.

(2.23)

Finally, implied by (
2.19
), by (
2.20
) and by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, we have the following quasi-commutation relations:

\Gamma_{+}(A)\Gamma_{-}(B)=H(A;B)\Gamma_{-}(B)\Gamma_{+}(A)

(2.24)

and

\Gamma_{\pm}(A)\psi(z)=H(A;z^{\pm 1})\psi(z)\Gamma_{\pm}(A),\qquad\Gamma_{\pm}(A)\psi^{*}(w)=H(A;w^{\pm 1})^{-1}\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{\pm}(A).

(2.25)

Remark 1
.

These relations always make sense at a formal level; analytically (
2.24
) requires
|a_{i}b_{j}|<1
; for (
2.25
) we need factors of the form
1/(1-a\zeta)
have
|a\zeta|<1
to be expandable in power series of
\zeta
where
\zeta\in\{z^{\pm 1},w^{\pm 1}\}.

The half-vertex
\Gamma
operators commute with the charge (
C
) and shift (
R
)
operators. They have skew Schur functions as matrix elements:

\langle\lambda,c|\Gamma_{+}(A)|\mu,d\rangle=\langle\mu,d|\Gamma_{-}(A)|\lambda,c\rangle=s_{\mu/\lambda}(A)\delta_{c,d}

(2.26)

The fermionic operators
can be reconstructed from the half-vertex operators and the charge and
shift operators
C
and
R
(see e.g.
[
Kac90
, Theorem 14.10]
) via the so-called

boson–fermion correspondence
:

\begin{split}\psi(z)=z^{C-\frac{1}{2}}R\,\Gamma_{-}(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}\left(z^{-1}\right),\qquad\psi^{*}(w)=R^{-1}w^{-C+\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{-}^{-1}(w)\Gamma_{+}\left(w^{-1}\right).\end{split}

(2.27)

In what follows,
X=(x_{1},x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N}^{-1}),Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
are alphabets, taken without loss of generality to have the same number of letters. Recall that
\tilde{X}
stands for one of
X
or
(X,1)
. Following Baker
[
Bak96
]
, let

\begin{split}\Gamma_{sp\pm}(X)&=\exp\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\left(p_{n}(X)\alpha_{\pm n}+\frac{\alpha_{\pm 2n}}{2}-\frac{\alpha_{\pm n}^{2}}{2}\right),\\
\Gamma_{o\pm}(\tilde{X})&=\exp\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\left(p_{n}(\tilde{X})\alpha_{\pm n}-\frac{\alpha_{\pm 2n}}{2}-\frac{\alpha_{\pm n}^{2}}{2}\right).\end{split}

(2.28)

\Gamma_{sp+}/\Gamma_{o+}
acts trivially on the vacuum while
\Gamma_{sp-}/\Gamma_{o-}
on the dual vacuum: e.g.,
\Gamma_{sp+}(X)|0\rangle=|0\rangle
and
\langle 0|\Gamma_{o-}(\tilde{X})=\langle 0|
. They satisfy more complicated quasi-commutation relations with
\Gamma_{\pm},\psi,\psi^{*}
, which we now provide along with a short proof for the benefit of the reader:

Proposition 2
.

We have the following quasi-commutation relations:

\begin{split}\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{+}(Y)&=\Gamma_{+}(Y)\Gamma_{sp+}(X),\\
\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(Y)&=H(X;Y)h_{sp}(Y)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(Y)\Gamma_{sp+}(X),\\
\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\psi(z)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)&=H(X;z)\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z),\\
\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)&=(1-w^{2})H(X;w)^{-1}\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{+}(w),\\
\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X})\Gamma_{+}(Y)&=\Gamma_{+}(Y)\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X}),\\
\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X})\Gamma_{-}(Y)&=H(\tilde{X};Y)h_{o}(Y)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(Y)\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X}),\\
\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X})\psi(z)\Gamma_{o+}^{-1}(\tilde{X})&=(1-z^{2})H(\tilde{X};z)\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z),\\
\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X})\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{o+}^{-1}(\tilde{X})&=H(\tilde{X};w)^{-1}\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{+}(w).\end{split}

(2.29)

Proof.

We will prove the first four equations. The last four follow along similar arguments. We also remark that the relations make sense formally; analytically they require similar conditions as in Remark
1
.

The first commutation relation is immediate, as both
\Gamma_{+}
and
\Gamma_{sp+}
only involve exponentials of bosonic operators
\alpha_{n}
with
n>0
and all such operators commute between themselves. The second could be proved from the third as follows: first, via the boson–fermion correspondence (
2.27
) one has
\psi(z)=z^{C-1/2}R\Gamma_{-}(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(1/z)
; second, observing that
R
and
C
commute with everything else below and that again
\Gamma_{+}
and
\Gamma_{sp+}
also commute, the third equation implies

\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(z)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)=H(X;z)\Gamma_{-}(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z)

(2.30)

and so

\begin{split}\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(y_{i})\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}H(X;y_{i})\Gamma_{-}(y_{i})\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(y_{i})\\
&=H(X;Y)h_{sp}(Y)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(Y)\end{split}

(2.31)

where for the last equality we have commuted all
\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(y_{i})
to the right and we have collected the terms at the end.
We now prove the third equation. We can write
\Gamma_{sp+}(X)=\exp(H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{3})
where
H_{1}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}p_{n}(X)n^{-1}\alpha_{n}
,
H_{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}(2n)^{-1}\alpha_{2n}
,
H_{3}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}(2n)^{-1}\alpha_{n}^{2}
. The
H
’s commute among themselves as they only involve positive index bosons. Then equation (
2.20
) implies, via Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff,

e^{H_{1}}\psi(z)e^{-H_{1}}=H(X;z)\psi(z),\qquad e^{H_{2}}\psi(z)e^{-H_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\psi(z).

(2.32)

Finally, for
H_{3}
, notice that
[\alpha_{n}^{2},\psi(z)]=z^{n}\alpha_{n}\psi(z)+z^{n}\psi(z)\alpha_{n}=z^{2n}\psi(z)+2z^{n}\psi(z)\alpha_{n}
where for the first equation we have used
[ab,c]=a[b,c]+[a,c]b
, while for the second we used (
2.20
) to write the
\alpha\psi
term in reverse order. Therefore

[H_{3},\psi(z)]=\psi(z)(a(z)-H_{+}(z))

(2.33)

where
a(z):=\log\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
,
H_{+}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-1}z^{n}\alpha_{n}=\log\Gamma_{+}(z)
. Inductively then we obtain

[H_{3},[H_{3},\dots[H_{3},\psi(z)]\dots]]=\psi(z)(a(z)-H_{+}(z))^{n}

(2.34)

where above there are
n
open commutator brackets. Then, again using Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff, we obtain

e^{H_{3}}\psi(z)e^{-H_{3}}=\psi(z)+[H_{3},\psi(z)]+\frac{1}{2!}[H_{3},[H_{3},\psi(z)]]+\dots=\psi(z)e^{a(z)-H_{+}(z)}=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z).

(2.35)

Putting it all together yields the desired result.
The fourth equation follows along similar computations except we mention that the factor
\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
survives, squared, owing to the commutation
[\alpha_{n},\psi^{*}(w)]=-w^{n}\psi^{*}(w)
. Also the
\Gamma_{+}
operator appearing in the end is not inverted, as
[H_{3},\psi^{*}(w)]=\psi(w)(a(w)+H_{+}(w))
. We leave the details to the reader.
∎

Remark 3
.

Similar quasi-commutations between
\Gamma_{sp-}/\Gamma_{o-}
and
\Gamma_{+},\psi,\psi^{*}
could also be written down.

The important fact about these
twisted
half-vertex operators is that they have symplectic and orthogonal characters as matrix elements
[
Bak96
]
:

sp_{\lambda}(X)=\langle 0|\Gamma_{sp+}(X)|\lambda\rangle=\langle\lambda|\Gamma_{sp-}(X)|0\rangle,\qquad o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})=\langle 0|\Gamma_{o+}(\tilde{X})|\lambda\rangle=\langle\lambda|\Gamma_{o-}(\tilde{X})|0\rangle.

(2.36)

3
Correlation functions

3.1
The symplectic and orthogonal character case

Assume
X=(x_{1},x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N}^{-1})
and
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
satisfy the following inequalities:

1\geq x_{1}\geq x_{2}\dots\geq x_{N}>y_{1}\geq y_{2}\geq\dots\geq y_{N}\geq 0.

(3.1)

and recall that
\tilde{X}
stands for either
X
or
(X,1)
.

Consider the following two measures on partitions (the dependence on parameters
X,Y
assumed implicit):

m_{sp}(\lambda)=\frac{sp_{\lambda}(X)s_{\lambda}(Y)}{H(X;Y)h_{sp}(Y)},\qquad m_{o}(\lambda)=\frac{o_{\lambda}(\tilde{X})s_{\lambda}(Y)}{H(\tilde{X};Y)h_{o}(Y)}.

(3.2)

These are true probability measures in view of the Cauchy identities (
2.8
), of the fact both
s_{\lambda}
(see
[
Mac95
]
) and
sp_{\lambda},o_{\lambda}
(see
[
KT87
,
Sun90
]
) can be written as generating series of semi-standard tableaux with positive coefficients, and of the inequalities (
3.1
).

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}
be particle positions. We are interested in the probability, under
m_{sp/o}
that the particle configuration of
\lambda
has particles at these positions. These are the so-called
n
-point correlation functions
:

\varrho_{sp/o}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}):=m_{sp/o}(\{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\}\subset\{\lambda_{i}-i+1/2\}).

(3.3)

Theorem 4
.

The measures
m_{sp}
and
m_{o}
are determinantal:

\varrho_{sp/o}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})=\det[K_{sp/o}(k_{i},k_{j})]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

(3.4)

with kernels

\begin{split}K_{sp}(a,b)&=\int_{z}\int_{w}\frac{F_{sp}(z)}{F_{sp}(w)}\frac{(1-w^{2})}{(1-wz)(1-wz^{-1})}\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}z^{a+3/2}w^{-b+1/2}},\\
K_{o}(a,b)&=\int_{z}\int_{w}\frac{F_{o}(z)}{F_{o}(w)}\frac{(1-z^{2})}{(1-wz)(1-wz^{-1})}\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}z^{a+3/2}w^{-b+1/2}}\end{split}

(3.5)

where

F_{sp}(z)=\frac{H(X;z)}{H(Y;z)H(Y;z^{-1})},\qquad F_{o}(z)=\frac{H(\tilde{X};z)}{H(Y;z)H(Y;z^{-1})}

(3.6)

where the
z
and
w
contour are simple counterclockwise circles around 0 satisfying
y_{1}<|w|<|z|<x_{N}
.

Proof.

We will prove the
sp
statement, the other being analogous. Throughout we use
F
for
F_{sp}
and
Z
for the partition function
H(X;Y)h_{sp}(Y)
. First notice, using
\langle A\rangle:=\langle 0|A|0\rangle
throughout, that

\begin{split}\varrho_{sp}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})&=\frac{1}{Z}\left\langle\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{k_{i}}\psi^{*}_{k_{i}}\right)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\left[\frac{z_{1}^{k_{1}}\dots z_{n}^{k_{n}}}{w_{1}^{k_{1}}\dots w_{n}^{k_{n}}}\right]\left\langle\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\right)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle\\
\end{split}

(3.7)

since the projector
\psi_{k}\psi_{k}^{*}
picks out only the configurations satisfying
k\in\{\lambda_{i}-i+1/2\}
. Here
[z^{k}]f
denotes the coefficient of
z^{k}
in
f
.

Let us start with the one point correlator:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z}\langle\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\psi(z)\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\rangle&=\frac{1}{Z}\langle\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\psi(z)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{sp+}^{-1}(X)\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\rangle\\
&=(1-w^{2})\frac{H(X;z)}{H(X;w)}\langle\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z)\psi^{*}(w)\Gamma_{+}(w)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\rangle\\
&=(1-w^{2})\frac{H(X;z)H(Y;w)H(Y;w^{-1})}{H(X;w)H(Y;z)H(Y;z^{-1})}\langle\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z)\psi^{*}(w)\rangle\\
&=\frac{F(z)}{F(w)}(1-w^{2})\sqrt{\frac{w}{z}}\frac{1}{(1-wz)(1-wz^{-1})}\end{split}

(3.8)

with

F(z)=\frac{H(X;z)}{H(Y;z)H(Y;z^{-1})}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(1-y_{i}z)(1-y_{i}z^{-1})}{(1-x_{i}z)(1-x^{-1}_{i}z)}

(3.9)

as desired and where in the last equality we have used the boson–fermion correspondence to evaluate
\langle\psi(z)\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z)\psi^{*}(w)\rangle
.

We can now perform a similar computation for the
n
-point correlator. Using the notation
\Ad_{G}(A)=GAG^{-1}
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\left\langle\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\left(\prod_{i}\psi(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\right)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\left\langle\left(\prod_{i}\Ad_{\Gamma_{sp+}(X)}(\psi(z_{i}))\Ad_{\Gamma_{sp+}(X)}(\psi^{*}(w_{i}))\right)\Gamma_{sp+}(X)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}(1-w_{i}^{2})\frac{H(X;z_{i})}{H(X;w_{i})}\left\langle\left(\prod_{i}\psi(z_{i})\Gamma_{+}^{-1}(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\Gamma_{+}(w_{i})\right)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}(1-w_{i}^{2})\frac{H(X;z_{i})H(Y;w_{i})}{H(X;w_{i})H(Y;z_{i})}\prod_{i<j}\frac{H(w_{i};z_{j})}{H(z_{i};z_{j})H(w_{i};w_{j})}\prod_{i\leq j}H(z_{i};w_{j})\left\langle\left(\prod_{i}\psi(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\right)\Gamma_{-}(Y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}(1-w_{i}^{2})\frac{H(X;z_{i})H(Y;w_{i})H(Y;w_{i}^{-1})}{H(X;w_{i})H(Y;z_{i})H(Y;z_{i}^{-1})}\prod_{i<j}(1-z_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}w_{j})\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-z_{i}w_{j}}\left\langle\prod_{i}\psi(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\right\rangle

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-w_{i}^{2})\frac{H(X;z_{i})H(Y;w_{i})H(Y;w_{i}^{-1})}{H(X;w_{i})H(Y;z_{i})H(Y;z_{i}^{-1})}\prod_{i<j}(1-z_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}w_{j})\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-z_{i}w_{j}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sqrt{\prod_{i}\frac{w_{i}}{z_{i}}}\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}^{-1}-z_{j}^{-1})(w_{i}-w_{j})\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-w_{i}z_{j}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\frac{F(z_{i})}{F(w_{i})}(1-w_{i}^{2})\sqrt{\frac{w_{i}}{z_{i}}}\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(w_{i}-w_{j})(1-w_{i}w_{j})\left(1-\frac{1}{z_{i}z_{j}}\right)\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{(1-w_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}z_{j}^{-1})}

\displaystyle=\det\left[(1-w_{i}^{2})\sqrt{\frac{w_{i}}{z_{j}}}\frac{F(z_{j})}{F(w_{i})}\frac{1}{(1-w_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}z_{j}^{-1})}\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

where:

•

the first equality is tautologically true due to cancellations from conjugation;

•

the second equality is true due to the action of
\Gamma_{sp+}
on
\psi(z),\psi^{*}(w)
using Proposition
2
;

•

the third equality follows upon commuting all
\Gamma_{+}^{\pm 1}
’s to the right where they are absorbed by the vacuum;

•

the fourth equality follows upon commuting
\Gamma_{-}(Y)
to the left where it is absorbed by the vacuum;

•

the fifth equality follows from computing
\left\langle\prod_{i}\psi(z_{i})\psi^{*}(w_{i})\right\rangle
using e.g., the boson–fermion correspondence (
2.27
) and the quasi-commutation between
\Gamma_{+}
and
\Gamma_{-}
;

•

the sixth equality uses the definition of
F
from (
3.9
);

•

and the last equality follows from using the following
BC
-type Cauchy determinant:

\det_{i,j}\frac{1}{(1-w_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}z_{j}^{-1})}=\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(w_{i}-w_{j})(1-w_{i}w_{j})\left(1-\frac{1}{z_{i}z_{j}}\right)\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{(1-w_{i}z_{j})(w_{i}z_{j}^{-1})}.

(3.11)

Passing to the correlation functions and using contour integrals to perform the coefficient extraction, we obtain

\begin{split}\varrho_{sp}(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})&=\int_{z_{1}}\int_{w_{1}}\cdots\int_{z_{n}}\int_{w_{n}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{w_{i}^{k_{i}-1}\mathrm{d}z_{i}\mathrm{d}w_{i}}{z_{i}^{k_{i}+1}(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left[\frac{F(z_{j})}{F(w_{i})}\frac{(1-w_{i}^{2})w_{i}^{1/2}z_{j}^{-1/2}}{(1-w_{i}z_{j})(1-w_{i}z_{j}^{-1})}\right]\\
&=\det\left[\int_{z}\int_{w}\frac{F(z)}{F(w)}\frac{(1-w^{2})}{(1-wz)(1-wz^{-1})}\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}z^{k_{i}+3/2}w^{-k_{j}+1/2}}\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\end{split}

(3.12)

where the contours in the first
2n
-fold integral are simple counterclockwise circles centered around the origin satisfying

x_{N}>|z_{1}|>|w_{1}|>\dots>|z_{n}|>|w_{n}|>y_{1}

(3.13)

while in the second equation the
z
and
w
contours satisfy the conditions in the statement of the theorem. The second equation follows from the first by multilinearity of the determinant.
∎

3.2
Another proof of Theorem
4

In this Section we give another proof of Theorem
4
based on Macdonald difference operators. The use of such operators to compute observables for Macdonald measures/processes has been championed by Borodin and Corwin in
[
BC14
]
, but see also
[
BCGS16
]
for a more systematic approach. Using this approach when one specializes to the case of Schur measures, the full determinantal correlations for Okounkov’s
[
Oko01
]
Schur measure and the full pfaffian correlations for the (
one free boundary
) pfaffian Schur measure have been computed by Aggarwal
[
Agg15
]
and Ghosal
[
Gho17
]
respectively. We follow the approach of Ghosal
[
Gho17
]
since his computations, with minor modifications, will give rise to our result. We note that while Ghosal obtains pfaffian processes, we of course are dealing with determinantal ones, while using the same method with almost the same ingredients. This is by no means a coincidence and was probably observed for the first time by Stembridge
[
Ste90
, Section 7]
, and utilized to great success by Baik and Rains
[
BR01a
,
BR01b
]
.

Proof.

(difference operator proof of Theorem
4
)

As usual, we prove the
sp
case, the
o
case following along similar lines. We only sketch the main argument and refer to Section 4 of
[
Gho17
]
for the technical details.

Consider the following difference operator, acting on symmetric functions in the alphabet
Y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
:

D_{q}=\sum_{i}\prod_{j:j\neq i}\frac{qx_{i}-x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}T_{i}

(3.14)

where
T_{i}f(y_{1},\dots,y_{i},\dots,y_{N}):=f(y_{1},\dots,qy_{i},\dots,y_{N})
. This is the
0<q=t<1,r=1
version of the
q,t
Macdonald difference operator
D_{N}^{r}
defined in the scriptures
[
Mac95
, Chapter VI.3]
. It has Schur functions as eigenfunctions:

D_{q}s_{\lambda}(Y)=\left(\sum_{i}q^{\lambda_{i}+N-i}\right)s_{\lambda}(Y).

(3.15)

Theorem 4.1 of
[
Gho17
]
gives the action of
D_{q}
on functions of the form

G(Y)=\prod_{i<j}f(y_{i}y_{j})\prod_{i}g(y_{i})

(3.16)

for “simple”
f,g
. For Ghosal,
f(y)=(1-y)^{-1},g(y)=H(X;y)
. For us,
g
is unchanged (with the remark that

Conclusion and future outlook

In this section we conclude by providing an outlook for where the techniques and results herein announced might be useful.

First, on the Toeplitz+Hankel determinant side, both in
[
BR01a
]
and in
[
BE09
]
there are other such determinants not considered here. In particular the latter discusses constants (in their notation)
EF_{I},EF_{II}
which are simple modifications of
Z_{sp},Z_{o}
from above. One can expand these new constants easily in Schur functions, call the coefficients of
s_{\lambda}
e.g.
sq_{\lambda},sr_{\lambda}
and then
provided one finds an appropriate Jacobi–Trudi identity
, the other results from
[
BE09
]
should follow.

Second, the symplectic Schur measure afore described, specialized in variables, is related, at least in the continuous (exponential) limit, to last passage percolation in the so-called point-to-half-line regime, as one can see in
[
BZ17b
,
BZ17a
]
. Along with the cited authors, we hope to clarify and address this in a future note.

Third, it would be interesting to clarify the so-called integrable hierarchy behind the symplectic and orthogonal Schur functions. Note Baker
[
Bak96
]
already discusses this in a limited yet informative way. We do not know in particular if any interesting (to other branches of mathematics)

Fourth, the symplectic and orthogonal measures from above, specialized with
\rho^{+}=0

[

\pm
sine

Fifth, one can try to generalize our results to the case of a symplectic/orthogonal equivalent of the Schur

[
OR03
]

While the dynamical correlations can be written down naively with minimal effort, the lack of a symplectic/orthogonal branching rule compatible with the corresponding half-vertex operators prevented the author from writing down the result. It is nonetheless possible some traction could be gained here.

Sixth, the results herein obtained along with the work of Bisi–Zygouras
