for multidimensional Kac models

Abstract

We study the approach to equilibrium of systems of gas particles in terms of relative entropy. The systems are modeled by the Kac master equation in arbitrary dimensions. First, we study the Kac system coupled to a thermostat, and secondly connected to a heat reservoir. The use of the Fisher-information allows elementary proofs with weak regularity assumptions. As a result, we obtain for both systems exponential decay rates for the entropy and information that are essentially independent of the size of the systems.

1
Introduction

In the seminal paper
[
9
]
from 1956, Mark Kac suggested a probabilistic model to describe a spatially homogeneous system of gas particles, now called the
Kac Model
. The velocities
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the
N\in\mathbb{N}
particles are combined into a ”master vector”
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
that describes the state of the system. Energy and momentum preserving pair collisions are introduced by the transformation of the master vector. The occurrence of the collisions in time is modeled by a Poisson-like process. This leads to a linear evolution equation for the probability density function describing the system, called the
Kac master equation
. For details about the assumptions of the Kac model, and the derivation of the Kac master equation from them, see
[
9
]
or
[
5
]
[Chapter 2, p. XI-5].

The importance of the Kac model for us is due to the following two reasons: First, the non-linear spatially homogeneous Boltzmann equation can be derived from the Kac model via the notion of propagation of chaos, see
[
9
]
. Second, the Kac model enables the investigation of the approach to equilibrium in relative entropy.

A natural measure for the approach to equilibrium is the relative entropy of the system. The relative entropy is an extensive quantity, i.e. it is proportional to the size of the system. We would like to obtain an exponential decay rate that is essentially independent of the size of the system. However, the results of Cédric Villani and Amit Einav (see
[
12
]
and
[
7
]
) suggest that this is not possible for arbitrary initial conditions. Therefore, it is necessary to consider specific classes of initial conditions.

In this paper, we consider a multidimensional Kac system first coupled to a thermostat, and secondly coupled to a heat reservoir. We obtain exponential decay rates for the entropy and information of the Kac system in both models that are essentially independent of the size of the systems. The proofs for the decay of information are elementary and require only weak assumptions. The obtained decay rates for the information are then transferred to the entropy. This information approach was first used in
[
2
]
for a one-dimensional Kac model coupled to a heat reservoir.

The thermostat is treated as an infinite gas at thermal equilibrium. Thus, it always remains in its equilibrium state. In
[
4
]
, the entropy decay of a one-dimensional Kac model coupled to a thermostat was already derived. However, the proof does not carry over to the multidimensional case due to technical difficulties. The approach via information used in this paper solves that problem.

The heat reservoir is assumed to be much larger than the Kac system and is only initially in a thermal equilibrium. It is therefore a more realistic version of the thermostat. The fact, that one does not know how the heat reservoir evolves in time makes this model more difficult.

2
The Kac system coupled to a thermostat

In this section, we will first lay out the model, apply a ground state transformation, and then state the main results.

2.1
Model

We combine the
d
-dimensional velocities of the
N
particles in the Kac system to a master vector
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
. The state of the Kac system at time
t\geq 0
is described by a probability density function
f_{t}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. The time evolution is governed by the linear Kac master equation

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}f_{t}=\mathcal{L}f_{t}.

(1)

The time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\lambda N(Q-\mathds{1})+\mu N(R-\mathds{1}).

(2)

Hereby, the collision operator
Q
representing the collisions between particles within the Kac system is given by
2
2

2

For
N=1
we set
Q=\mathds{1}
so that
\mathds{1}-Q=0
, and the first part in the master equation vanishes. Note that this follows naturally, if we define the first part of the master equation via
(\mathds{1}-Q)\coloneqq\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\big(\mathds{1}-\tilde{Q}_{i,j}\big)[f]
, where
\tilde{Q}_{i,j}[f](\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f\big(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v},t\big)\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)
.

Q[f](\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\,\textup{d}\rho(\sigma).

(3)

We use the reflection map to model the collisions between two particles
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with scattering angle
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}\mapsto M_{\sigma}\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v-(\sigma[v-w])\sigma\\
w+(\sigma[v-w])\sigma\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{\sigma}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma&\sigma\otimes\sigma\\
\sigma\otimes\sigma&\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

Note that the reflection map only depends on the tensor product
\sigma\otimes\sigma
of the scattering angle.

The kinematic of the collisions is explained in great detail in
[
6
, Appendix A]
. The collision mapping is clearly kinetic energy and momentum conserving. The collision between the
i
-th and
j
-th particle is represented by the collision matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\right)^{N\times N}
defined by

\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0&\cdots&&&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma&\cdots&\sigma\otimes\sigma&&\\
&&\vdots&&\vdots&&\\
&&\sigma\otimes\sigma&\cdots&\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&&&\cdots&0&\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Note that
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}
is the identity matrix except for the four entries
(i,i)
,
(i,j)
,
(j,i)
, and
(j,j)
that are substituted by the entries of
M_{\sigma}
. In other words,
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{i}^{*},...,v_{j}^{*},...,v_{N})
with
(v_{i}^{*},v_{j}^{*})^{T}=M_{\sigma}(v_{i},v_{j})^{T}
.

For each collision, a scattering angle is selected by a probability measure
\rho
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, for which we assume the symmetry condition
3
3

3

In fact, the results in this paper depend only on the symmetry condition and not on the probability measure itself. We thank Felix Otto for this useful remark.

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sigma\otimes\sigma\,\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)=\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{d}.

(6)

The thermostat is assumed to contain infinitly many particles. Therefore, the velocity distribution of the particles in the thermostat is not influenced by the collision process, i.e. the distribution of the particles in the thermostat remains constant over time. This leads to the following operator
R:L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\to L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
representing the interaction with the thermostat

R\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{j},

(7)

with
R_{j}:L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\to L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
defined by

R_{j}[f]\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,g\left(\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\right]_{N+1}\right)f\left(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\right).

(8)

Hereby,
\boldsymbol{P}:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{N},(v_{1},\dots,v_{N},w)\mapsto(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
denotes a projection onto the first
N
variables.

The parameter
\frac{1}{\lambda}
describes the average waiting time for a particle in the Kac system to collide with any particle in the Kac system. Similarly, the parameter
\frac{1}{\mu}
gives the average waiting time for a particle in the Kac system to collide with any particle in the thermostat.

Note that the collision operators
Q
and
R_{j}
only depend on the scattering angle
\sigma
by the integral over a function of the tensor product
\sigma\otimes\sigma
. Therefore, the process describing our model is entirely defined by the symmetry condition (
6
), i.e. all probability measures that fulfill the symmetry condition generate the same process.

Finally, we assume that the velocity vectors of the particles in the thermostat are distributed according to the Maxwell distribution

g(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{dN}{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}.

(9)

Hereby, the mass is assumed to be
m=1
and the inverse temperature is
\beta\coloneqq\frac{1}{kT}
.

2.2
Kinetic energy, entropy and Fisher-information

A first observation is that the time evolution of the total kinetic energy and the total momentum can be computed explicitly, as done in
[
4
, p. 6]
for a one-dimenional model.

Definition
.

For any solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the Kac master equation (
1
) with
f_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, the
total kinetic energy

K

of the Kac system at time

t\in[0,\infty)
is defined by

K(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(10)

The
total momentum

P(t)

of the Kac system
is defined by

P(t)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}v_{i}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(11)

Differentiating under the integral sign, and inserting the master equation (
1
) leads to a first-order linear inhomogeneous ODE, from which we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

The total kinetic energy
K(t)
of the Kac system for any solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the Kac master equation (
1
) with
f_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and finite initial kinetic energy, i.e.
K(0)<\infty
, is given by

K(t)=\left(K(0)-\frac{dN}{\beta}\right)e^{-\frac{\mu}{2d}t}+\frac{dN}{\beta}.

(12)

The total momentum
P(t)
of the Kac system for any solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the Kac master equation (
1
) with
f_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and finite initial momentum, i.e.
P(0)<\infty
, is given by

P(t)=e^{-\frac{\mu}{d}t}P(0).

(13)

Lemma
2.1
shows that the average kinetic energy of a particle in the Kac system tends exponentially towards the average kinetic energy of a particle in the thermostat. It is interesting to note that (
12
) is independent of the rate parameter
\lambda
, i.e. the collisions between particles in the Kac system do not influence the time evolution of the kinetic energy of the Kac system. This explains that the rate of convergence is independent of the number of particles in the Kac system. Similarly, the total momentum tends exponentially to zero, and is not influenced by the collisions between particles in the Kac system.

Definition
.

We define the
relative entropy
of a state
f_{t}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
at time
t\in[0,\infty)

with respect to the equilibrium state

\gamma
as (see
[
4
, p. 4]
)

S(f_{t}|\gamma)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\ln\left(\frac{f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})}{\gamma(\boldsymbol{v})}\right)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(14)

Note that the entropy is defined with the inverted sign.

Before turning to the information, it is convenient to write our model with the constant function
H\equiv 1
as ground state instead of the Gaussian
g
, see
[
4
, p. 14]
. For a solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the master equation (
1
) we define
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
by

h_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\frac{f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}.

(15)

Inserting the transformed PDF into the Kac master equation (
1
) yields the following transformed master equation.

Lemma 2.2
.

The functions
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
form a solution of the Kac master equation (
1
) if and only if
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
defined by (
15
) solves the transformed master equation

\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}[h_{t}]\coloneqq\lambda N(Q-\mathds{1})[h_{t}]+\mu N(T-\mathds{1})[h_{t}],

(16)

where
T\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}
with

T_{j}[h_{t}](\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)^{T}\right).

(17)

Definition
.

We define the Fisher-information with respect to the Gaussian measure
g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}
of a probability density function
h:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\sqrt{h}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
by

\InfTS(h)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla h(\boldsymbol{v})|^{2}}{h(\boldsymbol{v})}\,g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(18)

2.3
Main results

Our first main result is the decay of the information of the Kac system.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of the transformed master equation (
16
) with the symmetry condition (
6
) such that
\sqrt{h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. Then, the information decays exponentially in time with

\InfTS(h_{t})\leq e^{-\frac{1}{d}\mu t}\InfTS(h_{0}).

(19)

The proof is given in section
5.1
.

The connection between information and entropy is established via the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
(P_{s})_{s\geq 0}
(see
[
2
, p.4, eq. (12)]
)

S(f|g)=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\InfTS\left(P_{s}e^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}t}h_{0}\right)\,\textup{d}s.

(20)

Recall the definition of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup, see appendix
B.1
.

This raises the question, if the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup commutes with the time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, since a decay rate for the information

\InfTS(h_{t})\leq c(t)\InfTS(h_{0}),

(21)

would then imply the same decay rate for the entropy:

\displaystyle S(f_{t}|g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\InfTS\left(P_{s}e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}h_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\InfTS\left(e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}P_{s}h_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}c(t)\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\InfTS(P_{s}h_{0})=c(t)S(f_{0}|g).

(22)

For the reflection map, that we use as collision mechanism, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup indeed commutes with the time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup commutes with the time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, i.e. for all
s\geq 0
we have

P_{s}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}P_{s}.

(23)

Hereby, the operators are assumed to act on
L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. The proof is given in section
5.2
.

Therefore, theorem
2.4
implies by inequality (
22
) the following theorem about the transfer of the decay rate from the information to the entropy.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of the Kac master equation (
1
) with the symmetry condition (
6
) such that
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and
K(0)<\infty
. Assume we have for
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by (
15
) an inequality for the information

\InfTS(h_{t})\leq c(t)\InfTS(h_{0}).

(24)

Then, we get an inequality for the relative entropy by

S(f_{t}|g)\leq c(t)S(f_{0}|g).

(25)

Theorem
2.3
together with theorem
2.5
prove the entropy decay as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of the master equation (
1
) with the symmetry condition (
6
) such that
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and
K(0)<\infty
. Then, the relative entropy decays exponentially with

S(f_{t}|g)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{d}\mu t}S(f_{0}|g).

(26)

3
The Kac system coupled to a heat reservoir

In this section, we replace the thermostat by a finite heat reservoir. The main difference is that the number of particles in the heat reservoir
M\in\mathbb{N}
is assumed to be finite. Thus, the state of the heat reservoir changes over time.

3.1
Model

The system is described by a probability distribution
F:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\times\mathbb{R}^{dM}\to\mathbb{R}
of the velocity vectors of all particles, normalized with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We use the notation
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N},w_{N+1},\dots,w_{N+M})
to number the particles from
1
to
N+M
. The particles
1,\dots,N
belong to the Kac system, and the particles
N+1,\dots,N+M
to the heat reservoir. The pair collisions are modeled by the operator
R_{ij}:L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)\to L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
given by

R_{ij}[F](\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,F\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{u}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},

(27)

where we model the collision between the
i
-th and
j
-th particle again by the reflection map, i.e.
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+M)\times(N+M)}
is defined as in (
5
) and
\rho
is a probability measure fulfilling the symmetry condition (
6
). The time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
is given by (compare to
[
2
, p. 2]
)

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{N-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(R_{ij}-\mathds{1})+\frac{\lambda_{R}}{M-1}\sum_{N<i<j\leq N+M}(R_{ij}-\mathds{1})+\frac{\mu}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}(R_{ij}-\mathds{1}).

(28)

The parameter
\frac{1}{\lambda_{S}}
describes the average waiting time for some particle in the Kac system to collide with any other particle in the Kac system. Analogously, the parameter
\lambda_{R}
gives the rate of collisions within the heat reservoir. Finally, the rate at which a particle of the Kac system collides with any particle of the heat reservoir is
\mu
.

The time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
generates the exponential semigroup
\left(e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. The time evolution of the system is given by

F_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}F_{0}.

(29)

This is equivalent to the following abstract Cauchy problem, called the
Kac Master Equation

\frac{\partial F_{t}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}F_{t}.

(30)

Our goal is to investigate the approach to equilibrium of the Kac system. We assume, that the heat reservoir is initially in an equilibrium state. As in
[
2
, p. 2]
, the initial probability distribution of the heat reservoir is therefore assumed to be a Gaussian function. Hence, we can write

F_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=f_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})\underbrace{\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{dM}{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{2}}}_{\eqqcolon g(\boldsymbol{w})}=f_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w}),

(31)

where
\beta
denotes the inverse temperature of the initial state of the heat reservoir.

3.2
Basic notions

We are interested in the time evolution of the probability distribution
f_{t}
describing the Kac system. We define
f_{t}
by the marginal of
F_{t}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure over the particles in the heat reservoir (see
[
2
, eq. (6)]
or
[
1
, eq. (8)]
)

f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}.

(32)

Recall that the relative entropy
S
of a probability density function
f\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
with respect to an equilibrium state
\gamma
is defined by

S(f|\gamma)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}f(\boldsymbol{v})\ln\left(\frac{f(\boldsymbol{v})}{\gamma(\boldsymbol{v})}\right)\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(33)

Note that we use the inverted sign.

In order to define the approach to equilibrium via the entropy of the Kac system, we need to specify the equilibrium state
\gamma
, that we compare the system to. If the Kac system is initially not in a Gaussian distributed state, we can not expect the system to reach a Gaussian distribution over time. However, in
[
3
]
it is shown, that the Kac system coupled to a heat reservoir approximates the Kac system coupled to a thermostat uniformly in time in different norms for
M\to\infty
. In other words, we can expect that the heat reservoir stays close to its initial state over time. Therefore, it is reasonable to use the initial state
g
of the heat reservoir as relative state for the entropy.

As in the case of the thermostat, it is convenient to consider a ground state transformation (see also
[
2
, p. 3]
). We define for
t\in[0,\infty)
the function

h_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\frac{f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}.

(34)

This allows us to write the entropy via

S\left(f_{t}|g\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}h_{t}\ln(h_{t})\,g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(35)

Recall that the information of a probability density function
h
with respect to the Gaussian measure
g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\sqrt{h}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
is defined by

\InfTS(h)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla h(\boldsymbol{v})|^{2}}{h(\boldsymbol{v})}\,g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(36)

Further, we can use the rotational invariance of the Gaussian
g(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})
to get

\displaystyle h_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{EqDefh_t}}{=}\frac{f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}\overset{\eqref{EqDeff_t}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{EqMasterEqHeatRes}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\left[f_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})\right](\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{EqDefh_t}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\frac{1}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\left[h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})\right]\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}e^{\mathcal{L}t}[h_{0}](\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\,g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}[h_{0}](\boldsymbol{v}),

(37)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the marginal with respect to the Gaussian measure
g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}
. Note that we considered the function
h_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\to\mathbb{R}
as a function
\tilde{h}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\tilde{h}_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})
.

3.3
Kinetic Energy

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the inverse temperature
\beta
of a Gaussian distributed system and the kinetic energy

K(\beta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{dN}{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{dN}{2\beta}.

(38)

Thus, it is interesting to investigate the time evolution of the kinetic energy. We consider

•

the total kinetic energy
K
including the Kac system and the heat reservoir,

•

the kinetic energy of the Kac system
K_{S}
, and

•

the kinetic energy of the heat reservoir
K_{R}
.

These are defined by

\displaystyle K_{S}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}u_{i}^{2}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v},

(39)

\displaystyle K_{R}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}u_{i}^{2}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u},

\displaystyle K(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{u})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}=K_{S}(t)+K_{R}(t).

The total kinetic energy stays constant, since the collision mechanism conserves the kinetic energy. We will therefore write
K(t)=K
. The time evolution of the kinetic energy of the Kac system and heat reservoir can be computed by taking the derivative, deriving an ODE, and solving the latter. This is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The total kinetic energy stays constant over time and is given by

K=\frac{dM}{2\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}f_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

(40)

The evolution of the kinetic energy of the Kac system and of the heat reservoir are given by

\displaystyle K_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(K_{S}(0)-\frac{N}{N+M}K\right)e^{-\frac{\mu}{2dM}(N+M)t}+\frac{N}{N+M}K,

(41)

\displaystyle K_{R}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(K_{R}(0)-\frac{M}{N+M}K\right)e^{-\frac{\mu}{2dM}(N+M)t}+\frac{M}{N+M}K.

(42)

Lemma
3.1
shows that the kinetic energy of the Kac system will tend to
\frac{N}{N+M}K
. The decay rate is proportional to the number of particles in the Kac system, and thus much faster as what we will get for the entropy decay rate. Further, in the limit
t\to\infty
, all particles will have on average the same kinetic energy. Since the heat reservoir is assumed to be much larger than the Kac system, it is reasonable to use the inverse temperature
\beta
of the initial state of the heat reservoir for the equilibrium state, that we compare the Kac system to.

3.4
Main results

Our first main result is that the information decays exponentially down to a small number.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
be a solution of the Kac master equation (
30
) with symmetry condition (
6
) that satisfies the initial condition (
31
) with
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. Assume further, that
K(0)<\infty
. Then, the information of
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by (
34
) decays as follows

\InfTS(h_{t})\leq\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{\mu}{d}\frac{N+M}{M}t}\right]\InfTS(h_{0}).

(43)

The proof is given in section
5.3
.

In other words, if
\sqrt{\varphi_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, we obtain under the Kac evolution

\InfTS\left(\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\varphi_{0}\right)\leq\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{\mu}{d}\frac{N+M}{M}t}\right]\InfTS(\varphi_{0}),

(44)

where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the marginal with respect to the Gaussian measure
g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}
, i.e.

[\mathcal{M}\varphi](\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\,g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}.

(45)

In particular, since
\sqrt{P_{s}h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
if
\sqrt{h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, the estimate in theorem
3.2
holds as well for
\varphi_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}h_{0}
.
We will show in the following that the decay rate of the information transfers to the entropy. The connection is established via Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup by the following formula given in
[
2
, p.4, eq. (12)]

S(f_{t}|g)=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\InfTS(P_{s}h_{t})\,\textup{d}s.

(46)

This was similarly done in
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
. The crucial point now is to show that the time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
commutes with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup.

Lemma 3.3
.

The time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
commutes with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup on
L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
, i.e.

[\mathcal{L},P_{s}]=\mathcal{L}P_{s}-P_{s}\mathcal{L}=0,\quad\text{for all }s\geq 0.

(47)

The proof is given in section
5.4
.

The transfer of the decay rate follows as given in the theorem below.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
be a solution of the Kac master equation (
30
) with symmetry condition (
6
) that satisfies the initial condition (
31
) with
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and
K(0)<\infty
. Let further
(f_{t})_{t>0}
and
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be defined by (
32
) and (
34
). If we have an inequality for the information of the form

\InfTS(\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}h_{0})\leq c(t)\InfTS(P_{s}h_{0}),

(48)

then we get an inequality for the entropy by

S(f_{t}|g)\leq c(t)S(f_{0}|g).

(49)

Proof.

We claim that
P_{s}
commutes with the time evolution
\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}
, i.e.

P_{s}\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}h_{0}=\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}h_{0}.

(50)

The theorem follows from this claim by

\displaystyle S(f_{t}|g)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{EqConnEntInfHR}}{=}\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\,\InfTS(P_{s}h_{t})\overset{\eqref{EqHtEvo}}{=}\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\,\InfTS(P_{s}\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}h_{0})\overset{\eqref{EqProofDecayInfClaim}}{=}\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textup{d}s\,\InfTS(\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}h_{0})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{EqTheoEntDecByInfDecAssumpt}}{\leq}\frac{c(t)}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\InfTS(P_{s}h_{0})\,\textup{d}s=c(t)S(f_{0}|g).

(51)

To show the claim (
50
), we will implicitly consider
h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})
to be a function on
\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}
. Let us denote the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup on
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
by
P_{s}^{(1)}
and on
\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}
by
P_{s}^{(2)}
. It immediately follows that
P_{s}^{(2)}h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=P_{s}^{(1)}h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})
. Further, we obtain by definition that

\mathcal{M}P_{s}^{(2)}h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}h(e^{-s}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))e^{-\pi(\boldsymbol{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{y}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}^{2})}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{y}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}.

(52)

The rotation transformation
\boldsymbol{p}\coloneqq e^{-s}\boldsymbol{w}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{q}\coloneqq-\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{w}+e^{-s}\boldsymbol{y}
has Jacobian determinant 1 and fulfills
\boldsymbol{p}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}=\boldsymbol{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{y}^{2}
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}P_{s}^{(2)}h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}h(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{v}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p})e^{-\pi(\boldsymbol{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{p}^{2}+\boldsymbol{q}^{2})}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=P_{s}^{(1)}\mathcal{M}h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(53)

Using lemma
3.3
, this proves the claim (
50
) by

P_{s}^{(1)}\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}h_{0}=\mathcal{M}P_{s}^{(2)}e^{\mathcal{L}t}h_{0}=\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}^{(2)}h_{0}=\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}P_{s}^{(1)}h_{0}.

(54)

∎

Our next main result is now a direct consequence of the theorem
3.2
applied to the function
P_{s}h_{0}
and theorem
3.4
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
be a solution of the Kac master equation (
30
) with the symmetry condition (
6
) that satisfies the initial condition (
31
) with
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and
K(0)<\infty
. Let further
(f_{t})_{t>0}
be defined by (
32
). The entropy of the Kac system decays for
t\geq 0
by

S(f_{t}|g)\leq\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{\mu}{d}\frac{N+M}{M}t}\right]S(f_{0}|g).

(55)

It is interesting to note that for the limit
M\to\infty
, we get the same decay rates for the entropy and information as in the case of the thermostat.

As pointed out in
[
1
, p. 7]
, we can deduce the classic Kac model with
N+M
particles defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\textup{cl}}\coloneqq\frac{2}{N+M-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N+M}(R_{ij}-\mathds{1})

(56)

from the heat reservoir model by setting the parameters

\lambda_{S}\coloneqq\frac{2(N-1)}{N+M-1},\quad\lambda_{R}\coloneqq\frac{2(M-1)}{N+M-1},\quad\mu\coloneqq\frac{2M}{N+M-1}.

(57)

This gives us the following corollary:

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
F_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=f_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})
and

f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}\left[e^{\mathcal{L}_{\textup{cl}}t}F_{0}\right]\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w},\quad h_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\frac{f_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})}{g(\boldsymbol{v})}.

(58)

Assume that
\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
and
K(0)<\infty
. The decay of information and entropy is then given by

\displaystyle\InfTS(h_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{2(N+M)}{N+M-1}t}\right]\InfTS(h_{0}),

(59)

\displaystyle S\left(f_{t}|g\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{2(N+M)}{N+M-1}t}\right]S\left(f_{0}|g\right).

(60)

4
Discussion and future work

The decay rates for the entropy and information for the Kac system coupled to a thermostat or a heat reservoir obtained in the theorems
2.3
,
2.6
,
3.2
and
3.5
are consistent with the decay rates proven in
[
4
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
. It is interesting to note that the decay rates are quite uniform in the sense that they are independent of the collisions within the Kac system, the probability measure used to sample the scattering angle, as well as the inverse temperature of the thermostat. They are also essentially independent of the size of the Kac system.

While the information and entropy of the Kac system coupled to the thermostat tends to zero, the information and entropy of the Kac system coupled to a heat reservoir only tends to a small number. This is due to the fact, that the Kac system in the model with the heat reservoir will in general not tend towards a Gaussian distribution anymore. However, in the limit
M\to\infty
we recover decay rate of the Kac system coupled to a thermostat. Therefore, the estimate is expected to be optimal in some sense.

In comparison to
[
4
]
and
[
1
]
the approach via the information used in this paper and
[
2
]
provides a more natural and elegant method with simpler proofs and weaker assumptions. It also allows to treat multidimensional systems as well as the spherical Kac model (see
[
2
]
for the latter).

For future work, more intricate collision mechanisms could be considered. For example, the swapping map as given in
[
6
, Eq. (A.9)]

(v,w,\sigma)\mapsto\left(\frac{v+w}{2}+\frac{|v-w|}{2}\sigma,\frac{v+w}{2}-\frac{|v-w|}{2}\sigma,\frac{v-w}{|v-w|}\right)

(61)

could be used instead of the reflection map. Since this map is firstly not linear, and secondly only bijective if the scattering angle is included as third argument, it us harder to handle. In particular, it is not immediately clear, how to prove that the time evolution commutes with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup. However, we would still expect exponential decay rates with possibly different constants. Further, the case of hard sphere collisions or Maxwellian molecules could be discussed as suggested in
[
1
, p. 853]
.

In
[
4
, p. 19]
, it was proposed to consider a Kac system coupled to two heat reservoirs at different temperatures. Using the information approach of this paper, it could be possible to obtain results about the decay of entropy for that model. The interesting part would be that the two big heat reservoirs would merge over time into one heat reservoir on a much larger time scale than it takes the smaller Kac system to tend towards the equilibrium state. However, the distribution of the merged reservoirs would not be a Gaussian distribution anymore. If a Gaussian state with averaged inverse temperature is used as relative state, we would expect to obtain an exponential decay for the entropy down to a small number.

Arguably even more interesting is to consider the Kac system coupled to two thermostats at different temperatures. Even after long times, there would always be heat flowing into the Kac system from one thermostat and heat flowing from the Kac system to the other thermostat. Thus, we expect that the Kac system would tend towards a steady state instead of an equilibrium state. Therefore, the Kac system coupled to two thermostats might not be an approximation of the Kac system coupled to two heat reservoirs.

Finally, it is open to our knowledge to rigorously derive the true distributions of the equilibrium or steady states, respectively. For example, we do not know the equilibrium state in the case of two coupled heat reservoirs with different temperatures under the Kac evolution, or the distribution of the steady state occuring in the case of a Kac system coupled to two thermostats.

5
Proofs

5.1
Proof of theorem
2.3

The proof consists of two parts.
First, we estimate how pair collisions decrease the information of the Kac system. That is, we consider the collision of either two particles in the Kac system, or a particle in the Kac system and a particle in the thermostat.

Second, we use the convexity of the information to break down the time evolution into single pair collisions. For this, we repeatedly write the solution
h_{t}
as convex combination of simpler terms, and use Jensen’s inequality.

Lemma 5.1
.

The collisions of the particles in the Kac system with each other do not increase the information of the system. I.e. for all initial conditions
\sqrt{h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
the following estimate holds true:

\InfTS(Qh_{0})\leq\InfTS(h_{0}).

(62)

Proof.

Using the convexity of the information, we get

\displaystyle\InfTS(Qh_{0})

\displaystyle=\InfTS\left(\binom{N}{2}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\binom{N}{2}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\InfTS\left(h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right).

(63)

Since the matrix
M_{\sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma&\sigma\otimes\sigma\\
\sigma\otimes\sigma&\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma\end{pmatrix}
fulfills
M_{\sigma}^{2}=\mathds{1}
, the only possible eigenvalues of
M_{\sigma}
are
\pm 1
. This implies

\left|\left|\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right|\right|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right)}|\lambda|=1.

(64)

This completes the proof by

\InfTS\left(h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right)=\int g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\cdot\nabla h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right|^{2}}{h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)}=\InfTS(h_{0}).

(65)

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

The collision of a particle in the Kac system with the thermostat decreases its information by at least a factor of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}
. The information of the other particles remains unchanged. I.e. for all initial conditions
\sqrt{h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, we have the estimate

\InfTS(T_{j}h_{0})\leq\InfTS(h_{0})-\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\frac{\left|(\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}))_{j}\right|^{2}}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})},

(66)

where
(\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}))_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
j
-th three dimensional block component.

Notation
.

To separate the action on the particles in the Kac system, we write

\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\left(\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{w}_{\sigma}^{(j)},N_{\sigma}w+\sigma\otimes\sigma[v_{j}]\right)

(67)

where

\begin{split}N_{\sigma}&\coloneqq\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma\\
\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(i)}&\coloneqq\diag(\mathds{1},\dots,\mathds{1},\underbrace{\mathds{1}-\sigma\otimes\sigma}_{\text{i-th entry}},\mathds{1},\dots,\mathds{1}),\\
\boldsymbol{w}_{\sigma}^{(i)}&\coloneqq(0,\dots,0,\underbrace{\sigma\otimes\sigma[v_{j}]}_{\text{i-th entry}},0,\dots,0).\end{split}

(68)

Note that
\mathds{1}
is the identity matrix in
d
dimensions and

{\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(i)}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(i)}

(69)

This leads to the representation

R_{j}[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,g\left(N_{\sigma}w+\sigma\otimes\sigma[v_{j}]\right)f_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{w}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\right).

(70)

For shorter notation, we write

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{w}_{\sigma}^{(j)}.

(71)

Proof.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\nabla[T_{j}h_{0}]

\displaystyle=\int g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle|\nabla[T_{j}h_{0}]|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\frac{\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)}{\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)}}\cdot\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)\right|^{2}}{h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right)}\cdot T_{j}h_{0}.

(72)

Further since
{\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\nabla h_{0}^{T}\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}=|\nabla h_{0}|^{2}-|\sigma(\nabla h_{0})_{j}|^{2}.

(73)

Therefore, we get with the transformation

(\boldsymbol{p},q)\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)

that the information decreases by

\displaystyle\InfTS(T_{j}h_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{p})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(q)\textup{d}q\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{N}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})\right|^{2}}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{p})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(q)\textup{d}q\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\frac{|\nabla h_{0}|^{2}-|\sigma(\nabla h_{0})_{j}|^{2}}{h_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\InfTS(h_{0})-\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{p})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\,\frac{|(\nabla h_{0})_{j}|^{2}}{h_{0}}.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

It follows using the convexity of the information from the lemma
5.2
that

\InfTS\left(Th_{0}\right)\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\InfTS(T_{j}h_{0})\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(N\InfTS(h_{0})-\frac{1}{d}\InfTS(h_{0})\right)\\
=\left(1-\frac{1}{dN}\right)\InfTS(h_{0}).

(74)

In other words, the operator
T
decreases the information by a factor of at least
\left(1-\frac{1}{dN}\right)
.

Next, we can prove theorem
2.3
by repeatedly applying the convexity of the information to break down the information into simpler functions.

First, we write the solution
h_{t}
as a convex series

\displaystyle h_{t}=e^{-(\lambda+\mu)Nt}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((\lambda+\mu)Nt)^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}T\right)^{k}h_{0}.

(75)

Hence, we get for the information of
h_{t}
that

\InfTS(h_{t})\leq e^{-(\lambda+\mu)Nt}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((\lambda+\mu)t)^{k}}{k!}\InfTS\left(\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}T\right)^{k}h_{0}\right).

(76)

Noting that
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}T
is also a convex combination, we get using lemma
5.1
and remark
5.3
that

\displaystyle\InfTS\left(\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}T\right)h_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}\InfTS(Qh_{0})+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\InfTS(Th_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu}{dN(\lambda+\mu)}\right)\InfTS(h_{0}).

(77)

The claim now follows from the evaluation of the series

\displaystyle\InfTS(h_{t})\leq e^{-(\lambda+\mu)Nt}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((\lambda+\mu)Nt)^{k}}{k!}\left(1-\frac{\mu}{dN(\lambda+\mu)}\right)^{k}\InfTS(h_{0})=e^{-\frac{1}{d}\mu t}\InfTS(h_{0}).

(78)

5.2
Proof of theorem
2.4

It is sufficient to prove, that the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup commutes with the operators
Q
and
T_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Step 1:

We prove first, that the operator
Q
commutes with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup, i.e. for all
s\geq 0
we have

QP_{s}=P_{s}Q.

(79)

We write the transformation of the collisions using the matrix
\boldsymbol{v}_{i,j}(\sigma)=\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}
as described in
5
. The transformation
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{x}
yields

\displaystyle P_{s}Q[h]

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h\Bigr(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{v}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{x})}_{=e^{-s}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{x}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{y})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{y}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h\left(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{y}\right)

\displaystyle=QP_{s}[h].

(80)

Step 2:

Next, we prove that the operators
T_{j}
commute with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup.

We interpret any function
h^{(1)}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
as a function in two variables

h^{(2)}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},(\boldsymbol{v},w)\mapsto h^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(81)

This allows us to write

T_{j}[h^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h^{(2)}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)\right).

(82)

With the definitions

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\big[h^{(2)}\big](\boldsymbol{v},w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,h^{(2)}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)\right),

(83)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\big[h^{(2)}\big](\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\,h^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{v},w),

(84)

we get

T_{j}\big[h^{(1)}\big]=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{U}\big[h^{(1)}\big].

(85)

Similarly, we denote with
P_{s}^{(1)}
the
dN
-dimensional, and with
P_{s}^{(2)}
the
d(N+1)
-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup. Using
\alpha\coloneqq e^{-s}
and
\delta\coloneqq\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}
for shorter notation, we get

\displaystyle P_{s}^{(2)}h^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h^{(2)}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{v},w)+\delta(\boldsymbol{x},y))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,h^{(1)}(\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\delta\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{s}^{(1)}h^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{v}).

(86)

The commutation relation between the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup and the marginal
\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle P_{s}^{(1)}\mathcal{M}[h^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(p)\textup{d}p\,h^{(2)}(\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\delta\boldsymbol{x},p)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(p)\textup{d}p\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(q)\textup{d}q\,h^{(2)}(\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\delta\boldsymbol{x},p)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h^{(2)}(\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\delta\boldsymbol{x},\alpha w+\delta y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\,P_{s}^{(2)}[h^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{v},w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}P_{s}^{(2)}[h^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{v},w),

(87)

where we used the rotation transformation

\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\delta\\
-\delta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}w\\
y\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, the operator
U
commutes with the
d(N+1)
-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}P_{s}^{(2)}

\displaystyle[h^{(2)}]=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h^{(2)}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\alpha(\boldsymbol{v},w)+\delta(\boldsymbol{x},y)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h^{(2)}\left(\alpha\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)+\delta\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h^{(2)}\left(\alpha\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}(\boldsymbol{v},w)+\delta(\boldsymbol{p},q)\right)

\displaystyle=P_{s}^{(2)}\mathcal{U}[h^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{v},w),

(88)

where we used the transformation

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}\\
q\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\
y\end{pmatrix}.

The claim now follows by

P_{s}^{(1)}T_{j}[h^{(1)}]=P_{s}^{(1)}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{U}[h^{(2)}]=\mathcal{M}P_{s}^{(2)}\mathcal{U}[h^{(2)}]=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{U}P_{s}^{(2)}[h^{(2)}]=T_{j}P_{s}^{(1)}[h^{(1)}].

5.3
Proof of theorem
3.2

In a first remark, we break down the time evolution
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
into a convex series over collision histories, i.e. sequences of collisions. The proof then consists of two parts. First, we use the convexity of the information to break down the information decay of the time evolution into the information decay caused by collision histories. This reduces the problem to the derivation of a sum rule for some explicitly given matrix
K
, which we will derive in the second part.

Remark 5.4

(Representation as collision histories)
.

We can represent the time evolution semigroup
\left(e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
in terms of collision histories, i.e. we can write
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
as convex series over products of pair collisions. For this, we write (see
[
2
, p. 7]
)

\mathcal{L}=\Lambda(Q-\mathds{1})

(89)

with

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{S}N}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{R}M}{2}+\mu N

(90)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\Lambda(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}R_{ij}+\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\Lambda(M-1)}\sum_{N<i<j\leq N+M}R_{ij}+\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}R_{ij}.

(91)

This allows us to write
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
as the convex series

e^{\mathcal{L}t}=e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}Q^{k},

(92)

Next, we want to express the powers
Q^{k}
in terms of collision histories, i.e. by combinations of products of pair collisions. We introduce some notation: The index set
I
denotes the set of collision pairs:

I\coloneqq\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq N+M\}.

(93)

For
\alpha\in I
we define the coefficients

\lambda_{\alpha}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\Lambda(N-1)},&1\leq i<j\leq N,\\
\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\Lambda(M-1)},&N<i<j\leq N+M,\\
\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M},&1\leq i\leq N<j\leq N+M.\end{cases}

(94)

For
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in I^{k}
we set
\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\coloneqq\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}\cdots\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}
and
R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\coloneqq R_{\alpha_{k}}\cdots R_{\alpha_{1}}
. Now, we can write the powers
Q^{k}
by

\displaystyle Q^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

(95)

Note that
\sum_{\alpha\in I}\lambda_{\alpha}=1=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is again a convex combination. Therefore, the semigroup is given by the following convex series of pair collision operators:

e^{\mathcal{L}t}=e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

(96)

Lemma 5.5
.

The information decay is given by

\InfTS(h_{t}|g)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\frac{\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\boldsymbol{K}\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})}

(97)

where

\boldsymbol{K}\coloneqq e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k(d-1)}}\textup{d}\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\boldsymbol{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

(98)

Proof.

Inserting the representation formula (
96
) in equation
37
, we get

\displaystyle h_{t}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}e^{\mathcal{L}t}[h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{P}]=e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathcal{M}R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{P}](\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k(d-1)}}\textup{d}\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\underbrace{\mathcal{M}[h_{0}\circ\boldsymbol{P}]\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}\cdots\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})^{T}\right)}_{\eqqcolon h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{v})},

(99)

where
\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=(\rho(\sigma_{1}),\dots,\rho(\sigma_{k}))
. The convexity of the information implies by Jensen’s inequality that

\mathcal{I}(h_{t}|g)\leq e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k(d-1)}}\textup{d}\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\mathcal{I}\left(h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}|g\right).

(100)

Next, we separate the action of the rotations by denoting

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})&\boldsymbol{D}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}\cdots\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{1}}^{(\alpha_{1})},

(101)

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN},\boldsymbol{B}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dM},\boldsymbol{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{dM\times dN},\boldsymbol{D}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{dM\times dM}
. This allows us to observe, that

\displaystyle h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}\,h_{0}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{w}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dM}}g(\boldsymbol{w})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}\,h_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}\right)

(102)

is independent of
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{k}
. Further, this representation of
h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
allows an easy computation of its gradient. Using Jensen’s inequality in the first step, the information of
h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is given by

\displaystyle\InfTS(h_{k,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}\frac{|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}[h_{0}(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w})]|^{2}}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w})}\,g(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{A}_{k}[\nabla h_{0}](\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w})]|^{2}}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{w})}\,g(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{w})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}\frac{\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}\,g(\boldsymbol{p})g(\boldsymbol{q})\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\nabla h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}{h_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}\,g(\boldsymbol{p})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p},

(103)

where we used the transformation

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}\\
\boldsymbol{q}\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{w}\end{pmatrix}.

(104)

Inserting this formula in the inequality (
100
) yields the claim.
∎

Computing the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
in the following lemma completes the proof.

Lemma 5.6
.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
defined by (
98
) is given by

\boldsymbol{K}=\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{dM}t}\right]\mathds{1}_{dN}.

(105)

Proof.

First, we observe that
\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}
is the top left entry of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(106)

That is, we can write

\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{1}}^{(\alpha_{1})}\dots\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}\dots\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{1}}^{(\alpha_{1})}\boldsymbol{P}^{T}.

(107)

Next, we will iteratively integrate out the factors
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}\begin{pmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma_{k}}^{(\alpha_{k})}
from the inside to the outside. For this, we note that pointwise integration in the matrix yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&m_{2}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{pmatrix}M_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\mathds{1}_{d}-(m_{1}-m_{2})(\sigma\otimes\sigma)&0\\
0&m_{2}\mathds{1}_{d}-(m_{2}-m_{1})(\sigma\otimes\sigma)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\left(m_{1}-\frac{1}{d}(m_{1}-m_{2})\right)\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\left(m_{2}-\frac{1}{d}(m_{2}-m_{1})\right)\mathds{1}_{d}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{m_{1}}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\tilde{m_{2}}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{pmatrix},

(108)

with

\tilde{m}_{1}\coloneqq m_{1}-\frac{1}{d}(m_{1}-m_{2}),\quad\tilde{m}_{2}\coloneqq m_{2}-\frac{1}{d}(m_{2}-m_{1}).

(109)

This proves that the matrix always stays diagonal throughout the process and shows how the coefficients develop. In the following, we compute the sum over all collision pairs and integrals over all scattering angles for this expression with the general collision matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}
. We use the notation

\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\mathds{1}_{dN}&0\\
0&m_{2}\mathds{1}_{dM}\end{pmatrix}.

(110)

Further, we distinguish between the three cases of collisions in the Kac system, in the heat reservoir, and the interaction between the systems:

\sum_{\alpha\in I}\lambda_{\alpha}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\Lambda(N-1)}+\sum_{N<i<j\leq N+M}\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\Lambda(M-1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}.

(111)

Step 1:

The collisions between particles in the Kac system give us

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\Lambda(N-1)}

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\Lambda(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}{\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}}^{2}&0\\
0&m_{2}\mathds{1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{S}N}{2\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2}).

(112)

Step 2:

The collisions between particles in the heat reservoir act similarly on
\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\Lambda(M-1)}

\displaystyle\sum_{N<i<j\leq N+M}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\Lambda(M-1)}\sum_{N<i<j\leq N+M}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&m_{2}{\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{R}M}{2\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2}).

(113)

Step 3:

Using (
5.6
) we can compute the interaction between the two systems by

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}\diag(m_{1}\mathds{1}_{d},\dots,\underbrace{\tilde{m_{1}}\mathds{1}_{d}}_{i-\text{th entry}},\dots,m_{1}\mathds{1}_{d},m_{2}\mathds{1}_{d},\dots,\underbrace{\tilde{m_{2}}\mathds{1}_{d}}_{j-\text{th entry}},\dots,m_{2}\mathds{1}_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}\begin{pmatrix}\left(\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+M}\,(N-1)m_{1}+\tilde{m}_{1}\right)\mathds{1}_{dN}&0\\
0&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,(M-1)m_{2}+\tilde{m}_{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{dM}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\Lambda M}\begin{pmatrix}\left(NMm_{1}+\frac{M}{d}(m_{2}-m_{1})\right)\mathds{1}_{dN}&0\\
0&\left(NMm_{2}+\frac{N}{d}(m_{1}-m_{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{dM}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu N}{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})-\frac{\mu}{d\Lambda M}\boldsymbol{L}\big(M(m_{1}-m_{2}),N(m_{2}-m_{1})\big).

(114)

From Step 1 to 3, we get the sum of the three terms to

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in I}\lambda_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{S}N}{2\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})+\frac{\lambda_{R}M}{2\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})+\frac{\mu N}{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle\,-\frac{\mu}{d\Lambda M}\boldsymbol{L}\big(M(m_{1}-m_{2}),N(m_{2}-m_{1})\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1},m_{2})-\frac{\mu}{d\Lambda M}\boldsymbol{L}(M(m_{1}-m_{2}),N(m_{2}-m_{1}))

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})

(115)

with

\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{\prime}\\
m_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\left(\mathds{1}_{2}-\frac{\mu}{d\Lambda M}\begin{pmatrix}M&-M\\
-N&N\end{pmatrix}\right)}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{P}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{P}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(116)

If we start with
m_{1}=1,m_{2}=0
, and apply
\mathcal{P}
iteratively
k
-times, we get

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k(d-1)}}\textup{d}\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\,\boldsymbol{A}_{k}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\boldsymbol{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\sigma})=e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{L}\left(\mathcal{P}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right)\boldsymbol{P}^{T}.

(117)

To compute this expression, we decompose the start vector into the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{P}
. The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}
are given by
\lambda_{1}=1,\lambda_{2}=1-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{d\Lambda M}
with corresponding eigenvectors
v_{1}=(1,1)^{T},v_{2}=\frac{1}{N+M}(M,-N)^{T}
. Using
(1,0)^{T}=\frac{N}{N+M}v_{1}+v_{2}
, we get

\mathcal{P}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{N}{N+M}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\left(1-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{d\Lambda M}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{N+M}\begin{pmatrix}M\\
-N\end{pmatrix}.

(118)

Taking the first entry, we get

\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{L}\left(\mathcal{P}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right)\boldsymbol{P}^{T}=\left(\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}\left(1-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{d\Lambda M}\right)^{k}\right)\mathds{1}_{dN}.

(119)

This proves the claim as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}\left(1-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{d\Lambda M}\right)^{k}\right)\mathds{1}_{dN}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{N}{N+M}+\frac{M}{N+M}e^{-\frac{\mu(N+M)}{dM}t}\right]\mathds{1}_{dN}.

∎

Proof of lemma
3.3

Recall that for any
F\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)},g(\boldsymbol{u})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{u}\right)
, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is defined by

P_{s}[F](\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}F\left(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{w}\right)\,g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

(120)

Using the change of variables
\boldsymbol{y}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{x}
, the rotational invariance of
g
and the linearity of the reflection map we get

\displaystyle P_{s}R_{ij}[F](\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)F\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\left(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)F\left(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}}g(\boldsymbol{y})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{y}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)F\left(e^{-s}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\boldsymbol{y}\right)

\displaystyle=R_{ij}P_{s}[F](\boldsymbol{u}),

(121)

for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N+M\},i\neq j
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is a linear combination of operators
R_{ij}
, this proves the claim.

Technical remarks

In this chapter, we make some remarks on elementary facts that are either interesting by themselves or used in the main part with reference to this appendix for improved clarity.

Remark A.1

(Collision process)
.

The collision transformation
(v_{i},v_{j})\mapsto M_{\sigma}(v_{i},v_{j})
of two particles within the Kac system is a momentum and kinetic energy conserving rotation. It follows by straight calculation that

M_{\sigma}=M_{\sigma}^{T}=M_{\sigma}^{-1},

(122)

which implies that

\det\left(M_{\sigma}\right)=1,\\
v_{i}+v_{j}=v_{i}^{*}+v_{j}^{*},v_{i}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}=\left(v_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}+\left(v_{j}^{*}\right)^{2},

(123)

where
(v_{i}^{*},v_{j}^{*})^{T}=M_{\sigma}(v_{i},v_{j})^{T}
are the post-collisional velocity vectors. It follows, that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}={\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}}^{T}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}}^{-1},\quad

\displaystyle\det\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\right)

\displaystyle=1,

(124)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\quad

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{2}.

Remark A.2

(Information of the transformed state)
.

For both models, we can relate the information
\InfK(f_{0})
to the information of the transformed state
\InfTS(h_{0})
as follows. If the initial kinetic energy
K(0)<\infty
is finite, we have

\sqrt{f_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sqrt{h_{0}}\in H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right).

(125)

If one of the statements is true, we get in particular that

\InfTS(h_{t})=\InfK(f_{t})+2\beta^{2}\left(K(t)-\frac{dN}{\beta}\right)

(126)

Note that for the Kac model coupled to a thermostat, we get

\lim_{t\to\infty}\InfTS(h_{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\InfK(f_{t})

(127)

since
K(t)\to\frac{dN}{\beta}
by lemma
2.1
.

Proof.

It is clear that
f_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
if and only if
h_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. By the definition, it is also clear that
\nabla f_{0}
exists in a weak sense if and only if
\nabla h_{0}
exists in a weak sense. Therefore, the following computation completes the proof:

\displaystyle\InfTS(h_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla h_{t}|^{2}}{h_{t}}\,g(\boldsymbol{v})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\left|\frac{\nabla f_{t}\cdot g+\beta vf_{t}g}{g^{2}}\right|^{2}\frac{g^{2}}{f_{t}}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla f_{t}+\beta\boldsymbol{v}f_{t}|^{2}}{f_{t}}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla f_{t}|^{2}+2\beta f_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\nabla f_{t}+\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}f_{t}^{2}}{f_{t}}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\InfK(f_{t})+2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}\nabla f_{t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}+\beta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}f_{t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\InfK(f_{t})-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\underbrace{\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}_{=dN}f_{t}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}+2\beta^{2}K(t)

\displaystyle=\InfK(f_{t})+2\beta^{2}\left(K(t)-\frac{dN}{\beta}\right).

(128)

∎

Remark A.3

(Regularity of the Kac system coupled to a thermostat)
.

Considering the Kac system coupled to a thermostat, we show that the operators
Q
and
R
are bounded with
||Q||=1,||R||=1
. This implies that
\mathcal{L}
is bounded with
||\mathcal{L}||\leq 2(\lambda+\mu)N
. Therefore, the time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
is by corollary 1.5 in
[
8
]
the generator of the exponential semigroup
\left(e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. In fact, we show that
\mathcal{L}
generates a contraction semigroup. Further, we show that
f_{0}
is a probability density function if and only if
f_{t}
is a probabilty density function for all
t\geq 0
. This is due to the fact that the operators
Q
and
R
are averages over rotations.

i)

The operator
Q
is well-defined with
||Q||\leq 1
, since for any
f\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, we get with the transformation
\boldsymbol{p}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}
that

\displaystyle||Qf||_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\left|f\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{2}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\textup{d}\boldsymbol{p}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,\left|f\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=||f||_{1}.

(129)

ii)

The operator
R
is well-defined with
||R||\leq 1
, since for any
f\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
, we get with the transformation

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}\\
q\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}

that

\displaystyle||R_{j}f||_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,g\left(\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\right]_{N+1}\right)\left|f\left(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{M}_{\sigma}^{(j,N+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\textup{d}\rho(\sigma)\,g(q)|f(\boldsymbol{p})|

\displaystyle=||f||_{1}.

(130)

iii)

Next, we show that
\mathcal{L}
generates a contraction semigroup. Note that we can write
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
as the convex combination

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{L}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}(\lambda N(Q-\mathds{1})+\mu N)(R-\mathds{1}))^{k}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\lambda+\mu)Nt}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((\lambda+\mu)Nt)^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}R\right)^{k}.

(131)

Since
||Q||\leq 1
and
||R||\leq 1
by the previous parts, we get

\left|\left|\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}Q+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}R\right|\right|\leq\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}||Q||+\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}||R||\leq 1.

(132)

This proves that

\left|\left|e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right|\right|\leq 1.

(133)

iv)

Let
f_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{dN},\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. Then, we get that similar to part
i)
and
ii)
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}Qf_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}R_{j}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(134)

Termwise integration yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}e^{\mathcal{L}t}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}(\lambda N(Q-\mathds{1})+\mu N(R-\mathds{1}))^{k}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}(\lambda N(Q-\mathds{1})+\mu N(R-\mathds{1}))^{0}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}f_{0}\,\textup{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(135)

Note that we can integrate term by term since
\mathcal{L}
is bounded, and therefore the exponential series converges uniformly.

Remark A.4

(Regularity of the Kac system coupled to a heat reservoir)
.

i)

The time evolution operator
\mathcal{L}
generates a contraction semigroup. This is precisely what provides us the following majorant needed to exchange the order of integration and differentiation in the proofs of this chapter. For all
t\geq 0
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(N+M)}
we get

\left|\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}F_{t}(\boldsymbol{u})\right|=\left|\mathcal{L}e^{\mathcal{L}t}F_{0}(\boldsymbol{u})\right|\leq||\mathcal{L}||\,\left|\left|e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right|\right|\,|F_{0}(\boldsymbol{u})|\leq||\mathcal{L}||\,|F_{0}(\boldsymbol{u})|

(136)

The claim follows from the decomposition (

) of

in collision histories. Since
||R_{\alpha}||\leq 1
for all
\alpha\in I
, we get
||R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}||\leq 1
for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}
. This yields

\left|\left|e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right|\right|\leq e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in I^{k}}\lambda^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}||R^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}||\leq 1.

(137)

∎

ii)

For a solution

of the Kac master equation (

is a PDF.

b)

is a PDF for all

.

The equivalence of

the statements

Appendix B
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup

In this chapter, we summarize the relevant properties of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup and provide proofs as well. Hereby, we always consider the Gaussian measure
g(v)\textup{d}v
, where

g(v)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}v^{2}}.

(140)

Definition B.1
.

The
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
is defined by (see
[
10
]
, p. 444)

P_{s}h(v)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h\left(e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}x\right)\,g(x)\textup{d}x,\quad s\in[0,\infty),v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(141)

where
P_{s}
acts on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g(x)\textup{d}x)
.

Lemma B.2
.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is a strongly continuous semigroup.

Proof.

It is clear that
P_{0}=\mathds{1}
, since

P_{0}h(v)=\int h(v)\,g(x)\textup{d}x=h(v).

(142)

Further, we have by the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}P_{s}h(v)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0}\int h\left(e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}x\right)\,g(x)\textup{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\lim_{s\to 0}h\left(e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}x\right)\,g(x)\textup{d}x=h(v).

(143)

This proves, that
\slim_{s\to 0}P_{s}=\mathds{1}
. Finally, we verify that
P_{s+t}=P_{s}P_{t}
for all
s,t\geq 0
. We observe that

\displaystyle P_{s}P_{t}

\displaystyle[h](v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\textup{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h\left(e^{-t}\left(e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}x\right)+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}y\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\textup{d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\textup{d}y\,h\left(e^{-(s+t)}v+e^{-t}\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}y\right).

(144)

Defining

\begin{split}a&\coloneqq\frac{e^{-t}\sqrt{1-e^{2s}}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}},\\
p&\coloneqq ax+by,\end{split}\quad\begin{split}b&\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}},\\
q&\coloneqq-bx+ay.\end{split}

(145)

yields

\begin{split}a^{2}+b^{2}&=\frac{e^{-2t}\left(1-e^{-2s}\right)+1-e^{-2t}}{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}=\frac{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}=1,\\
p^{2}+q^{2}&=\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)=x^{2}+y^{2}.\end{split}

(146)

Therefore, the transformation
\phi(x,y)\coloneqq(p,q)
is a rotation with

\begin{split}\det(\phi^{\prime}(x,y))&=\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix}\right)=a^{2}+b^{2}=1,\\
g(x)g(y)&=g(p)g(q).\end{split}

(147)

Hence, equation (
144
) transforms into

\displaystyle P_{s}P_{t}[h](v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(p)\textup{d}p\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(q)\textup{d}q\,h\left(e^{-(s+t)}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}p\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(p)\textup{d}p\,h\left(e^{-(s+t)}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2(s+t)}}p\right)

\displaystyle=P_{s+t}[h](v).

(148)

∎

Lemma B.3
.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is self-adjoint (see
[
10
]
), i.e.
P_{s}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},g(x)\textup{d}x)
for all
s\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, it follows since
P_{s}[1]=1
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{s}h\,g(x)\textup{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h\,g(x)\textup{d}x.

(149)

Proof.

For all functions
F,G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textup{d}g(v)\textup{d}v)
, we have

\displaystyle\lscal P_{s}F,G\rscal

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}v\,g(v)P_{s}F(v)G(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}v\,\textup{d}w\,g(v)g(w)F\left(e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}w\right)G(v).

(150)

Defining

\begin{split}p&\coloneqq e^{-s}v+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}w\\
q&\coloneqq-\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}u+e^{-s}w\end{split}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\begin{split}v&=e^{-s}p-\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}q\\
w&=\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}p+e^{-s}q,\end{split}

(151)

yields the transformation
\phi(v,w)\coloneqq(p,q)
with

\displaystyle\left(e^{-s}\right)^{2}+\left(\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\det(\phi^{\prime}(u,w))

\displaystyle=1,

(152)

\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}

\displaystyle=v^{2}+w^{2},

\displaystyle g(u)g(w)

\displaystyle=g(p)g(q).

Therefore, the claim follows by

\displaystyle\lscal P_{s}F,G\rscal

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}p\,\textup{d}q\,g(p)g(q)F(p)G\left(e^{-s}p-\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}q\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}p\,\textup{d}q\,g(p)g(-q)F(p)G\left(e^{-s}p+\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}q\right)

\displaystyle=\lscal F,P_{s}G\rscal,

(153)

where we used in the last equality, that the Gaussian
g
is centered, i.e.
g(-q)=g(q)
. ∎

Next, we investigate the generator of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup. In
[
11
, Lemma 13.1.1]
an explicit formula is given for the generator:

Lemma B.4
.

The generator
L
of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup is given by

Lh(v)=\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta h(v)-v\cdot\nabla h(v),

(154)

where we recall that
\beta
is the inverse temperatur of the Gaussian
g
as given in (
140
).

Proof.

The generator
L
is for any test function
h\in D(L)
in its domain given by

L[h]=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{P_{t}h-h}{t}=P_{s}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{P_{t}h-h}{t}\bigg|_{s=0}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{P_{s+t}h-P_{s}h}{t}\bigg|_{s=0}=\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}s}P_{s}h\bigg|_{s=0}.

(155)

Hence, we compute the derivation of
P_{s}h
with respect to
s
. For shorter notation, we define
\alpha(s)\coloneqq e^{-s}
and
\delta(s)\coloneqq\sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}
. Since we do not know the derivation of
h
, but the derivation of
g
, we use the transformation
p\coloneqq\alpha v+\delta w\Leftrightarrow w=\frac{p-\alpha v}{\delta}
to get

P_{s}h(v)=\frac{1}{\delta^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}p\,g\left(\frac{p-\alpha v}{\delta}\right)h(p).

(156)

Now, we can compute

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}s}P_{s}h(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\,g(w)h(\alpha v+\delta w)\left(-d\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta^{2}}-\beta\frac{\alpha}{\delta}v\cdot w+\beta\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta^{2}}w^{2}\right),

(157)

A lengthy computation yields

\nabla_{v}P_{s}h(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textup{d}w\,g(w)h(\alpha v+\delta w)\cdot\beta\frac{\alpha}{\delta}w

(158)

and

\Delta_{v}P_{s}h(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(w)\textup{d}w\,h(\alpha v+\delta w)\left(\beta^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta^{2}}w^{2}-\beta d\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right).

(159)

Combining (
157
), (
158
), and (
159
) we get

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}s}P_{s}h(v)=\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta_{v}P_{s}h(v)-v\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{s}h(v).

(160)

Evaluating at
s=0
completes the proof.
∎
