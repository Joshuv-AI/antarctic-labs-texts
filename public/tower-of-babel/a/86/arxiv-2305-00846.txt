On ordered beta distribution and the generalized incomplete beta function
Mayad Al-Saidi , Alexey Kuznetsov and Mikhail Nediak

Abstract
Motivated by applications in Bayesian analysis we introduce a multidimensional beta distribution in an ordered simplex. We study properties of this distribution and connect them with the generalized incomplete beta function. This function is crucial in applications of multidimensional beta distribution, thus we present two efficient numerical algorithms for computing the generalized incomplete beta function, one based on Taylor series expansion and another based on Chebyshev polynomials.



Keywords
: Beta distribution, beta function, incomplete beta function, ordered simplex, Chebyshev polynomials

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
: Primary 60E05, Secondary 65D15

1
Introduction

Before we introduce the main obect of our study – the ordered beta distribution – let us consider the following motivating example. Let
S
be a random variable with binomial distribution with parameters
N
and
X
, where
N
is a positive integer and
X
is a random variable having
{\textnormal{Beta}}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution. Then it is well-known that the posterior distribution of
X
given
S=m
(where
0\leq m\leq N
) is
{\textnormal{Beta}}(\alpha+m,\beta+N-m)
. The ordered beta distribution arises naturally in the following multidimensional generalization of the above example. Let
\{S_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
be random variables such that
S_{i}
has binomial
(N_{i},X_{i})
distribution, and
\{X_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are random variables such that

0\leq X_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq\dots\leq X_{n}\leq 1

almost surely. What prior distribution can we assign to the vector

{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})

such that the posterior distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}
given

S_{i}=m_{i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,n
has the same form as the prior?

To answer this question, we first define the main object of our investigation.

Definition 1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a_{i}>0
and
b_{i}>0
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
We say that a random vector
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
has ordered beta distribution with parameters

\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
if

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}\in{\textnormal{d}}x_{1},X_{2}\in{\textnormal{d}}x_{2},\dots,X_{n}\in{\textnormal{d}}x_{n})=C^{-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}\leq 1\}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{a_{i}-1}(1-x_{i})^{b_{i}-1}{\textnormal{d}}x_{i}

(1)

where
C
is the normalization constant.

Before we proceed, we would like to explain the notation that will be used everywhere in this paper. We denote random variables by capital letters (such as
X,Y,Z
), real numbers by lower case letters (such as
x,y,z
) and vectors by bold font (such as
{\mathbf{X}}
or
{\mathbf{x}}
).

Definition
2
could be stated in the following equivalent way. Take
n
independent random variables
Y_{i}\sim{\textrm{Beta}}(a_{i},b_{i})
and denote by

\Delta_{*}^{n}
the ordered simplex

\Delta_{*}^{n}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\;:\;0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{n}\leq 1\}.

(2)

Then the ordered beta distribution can be defined as the distribution of the vector
{\mathbf{Y}}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
conditioned on the event that it lies in the ordered simplex
\Delta_{*}^{n}
:

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{X}}\in{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{Y}}\in{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\in\Delta_{*}^{n}).

(3)

The ordered beta distribution was introduced in
[
3
]

(also subsequently used in
[
1
]
) in the study of dynamic pricing and demand learning problems.
Let us summarize the setup of these problems. Consider a retailer who wants to find a price level at which selling a particular product would bring the highest revenue. To solve this problem the retailer needs to know the demand for this product as a function of price. One strategy to learn the demand is to fix a set of
n
ordered prices
p_{n}<p_{n-1}<\dots<p_{2}<p_{1}
and then to estimate the proportion of population that would buy the product at price
p_{j}
(for each
j
). Let
X_{j}\in(0,1)
be the probability that a randomly sampled customer would buy the product at price
p_{j}
. The demand is a decreasing function of price, which implies that the probabilities
X_{j}
must satisfy

0\leq X_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq\dots\leq X_{n}\leq 1
. We observe how many customers made the decision to purchase or not to purchase the product priced at
p_{j}
, and we denote by
m_{j}
the number of successful sales at price
p_{j}
while
k_{j}
is the number of times the product was offered at price
p_{j}
but the customer decided not to purchase it. We assume that the customers make decisions independently of each other, thus the number of customers who decided to purchase the product at price level
p_{j}
had binomial distribution with parameters
m_{j}+k_{j}
and
X_{j}
. The question is how the retailer can use the information contained in vectors

{\mathbf{m}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
and
{\mathbf{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})

to learn more about the probabilities
X_{j}
and to increase the revenue? The answer to this question can be found in
[
3
]
, and it is based on the following easily-verified property: if the prior distribution of purchase probabilities
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is ordered beta with parameters
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
{\mathbf{b}}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
, then the posterior distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}
given
{\mathbf{m}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}
is also ordered beta with parameters
{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{m}}
and
{\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{k}}
. This result can also be found in
[
1
]
[Proposition 1, page 13].

The aim of this paper is to study the ordered beta distribution and to state its properties and also to present several numerical algorithms which will make it easier to use this distribution in applications. The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present various probabilistic properties of ordered beta distribution (such as marginal distributions). To state these properties, we need to introduce a new special function, which we call
the generalized incomplete beta function
. The generalized incomplete beta function is indispensable for applications of the ordered beta distribution, in particular it is important to be able to compute this function numerically. In Section
3
we study analytic properties of the generalized incomplete beta function and in Section
4
we provide two efficient numerical algorithms for its computation. We demonstrate the efficiency of these algorithms by presenting the results of several numerical experiments in Section
5
.

2
Properties of the ordered beta distribution

The following function will be needed for describing the properties of the ordered beta distribution:

Definition 2
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a_{i}>0
,
b_{i}>0
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

The generalized incomplete beta function
(with parameters

\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
) is defined for
z\in[0,1]
as follows:

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=\int\limits{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{n}\leq z\}}\Big[\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{a_{i}-1}(1-x_{i})^{b_{i}-1}\Big]{\textnormal{d}}x_{1}\dots{\textnormal{d}}x_{n}.

(4)

We will call

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big):=\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|1\Big)

the generalized beta function
.

It is clear that the normalization constant in (
1

C=\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big).

When

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1}\\
b_{1}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=\int_{0}^{z}x^{a_{1}-1}(1-x)^{b_{1}-1}{\textnormal{d}}x,

and the classical beta function

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1}\\
b_{1}\end{matrix}\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}x^{a_{1}-1}(1-x)^{b_{1}-1}{\textnormal{d}}x=\frac{\Gamma(a_{1})\Gamma(b_{1})}{\Gamma(a_{1}+b_{1})}.

The above two functions are usually denoted as

\mathrm{B}_{z}(a,b)
and
\mathrm{B}(a,b)
, see
[
5
]
[Section 8.38].

Everywhere in this section we assume that
n\geq 2
and that

{\mathbf{X}}
has ordered beta distribution with parameters

\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
.
To simplify the presentation of results, in the rest of the paper we follow the convention that

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}-\\
-\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=1
for all

Theorem 1
.

(8)

(v)

Properties of the generalized incomplete Beta function

4
Computing the generalized incomplete Beta function

Now we turn our attention to the question of computing the generalized incomplete beta function for arbitrary
z\in(0,1]
.
Our first observation is that it is enough to be able to compute this function for
z\in(0,1/2]
. To show this, we rewrite the identity (
13
) in the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)+\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}b_{n},b_{n-1},\dots,b_{1}\\
a_{n},a_{n-1},\dots,a_{1}\end{matrix}\Big|1-z\Big)

(20)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}b_{n},b_{n-1},\dots,b_{k+1}\\
a_{n},a_{n-1},\dots,a_{k+1}\end{matrix}\Big|1-z\Big).

We see that we can compute the value of

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big)

by setting
z=1/2
in (
20
) and when
z\in(1/2,1)
we can compute

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)
by induction, since all other terms in
(
20
) either depend on
1-z\in(0,1/2)
or have fewer parameters. Thus, one can use identity (
20
) coupled with induction on
n
to compute the value of any generalized incomplete beta function (with arbitrary number of parameters) for any value of
z\in[0,1]
.

Instead of computing the generalized incomplete beta function

B\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)
, it is more convenient to solve an equivalent problem of computing

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)
, as this function is analytic in a wider domain. Next we present two algorithms for computing this function: the first algorithm is based on Taylor series expansion and the second algorithm is based on Chebyshev series.

4.1
The Taylor expansion method

The computation proceeds by iteration of
(
15
).
First of all, we note that when
n=1
we have a series representation

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1}\\
b_{1}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=\int_{0}^{1}y^{a_{1}-1}(1-yz)^{b_{1}-1}{\textnormal{d}}y=\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}\frac{(1-b_{1})_{k}}{(a_{1}+k)k!}z^{k},\;\;\;|z|<1,

(21)

which is easily obtained by integrating the binomial series expansion of the term
(1-yz)^{b_{1}}
. Here
(a)_{k}=a(a+1)\dots(a+k-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol.
Computing the coefficients of Taylor series of
\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)
when
n\geq 2
can be done iteratively with the help of the following result.

Proposition 3
.

For
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
denote by
c_{k}^{(n)}
the coefficients in the Taylor series expansions

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}c^{(n)}_{k}z^{k},\;\;\;|z|<1.

(22)

Then for
n\geq 2
and
k\geq 0

c^{(n)}_{k}=\frac{1}{k+a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}c^{(n-1)}_{k-l}\times\frac{(1-b_{n})_{l}}{l!}.

(23)

Proof.

Using the Binomial series and (
15
) we obtain for
|z|<1

\displaystyle\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}y^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}-1}(1-yz)^{b_{n}-1}\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}\end{matrix}\Big|yz\Big){\textnormal{d}}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}y^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}-1}\Big[\sum\limits_{l\geq 0}\frac{(1-b_{n})_{l}}{l!}(yz)^{l}\Big]\times\Big[\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}c_{k}^{(n-1)}(yz)^{k}\Big]{\textnormal{d}}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}y^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}-1}\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}\Big[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}c^{(n-1)}_{k-l}\times\frac{(1-b_{n})_{l}}{l!}\Big](yz)^{k}{\textnormal{d}}y

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}\frac{z^{k}}{k+a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}}\Big[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}c^{(n-1)}_{k-l}\times\frac{(1-b_{n})_{l}}{l!}\Big].

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Now we present the algorithm for computing

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)

for
z\in(0,1/2]
.

Step 1:
Fix a large integer
N
and compute
\{c_{k}^{(1)}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
via (
21
).

Step 2:
For
m=2,3,\dots,n
, once we have the values of
\{c_{k}^{(m-1)}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
we compute the values of
\{c_{k}^{(m)}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
via
(
23
). This computation involves a convolution of two sequences, thus each step can be made more efficient with the help of the Fast Fourier Transform.

Step 3:

Compute the value of

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)

by truncating the series in (
21
) after
N
terms.

Several remarks are in order. First of all, we need to discuss the error due to truncation of series in (
21
).
Since the function

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)
is analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[1,\infty)
and is continuous as
z\to 1
, for every
n
we have
c_{k}^{(n)}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
. This fact can be seen by indentifying these coefficients with the coefficients of Fourier series of a continuous function

t\mapsto\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|e^{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}t}\Big).

Thus after truncating the series in (
21
) after
N
terms we will have an error of
O(|z|^{N})
, and since
z\in(0,1/2]
this error is less than
C2^{-N}
, for some constant
C
, which may depend on
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
. Second, let us discuss the computational complexity of this algorithm. Using the Fast Fourier Transform, each Step 2 can be computed in
O(N\ln(N))
arithmetic operations.
Thus, the computational complexity of evaluating
\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)

is
O(nN\ln(N))
arithmetic operations.

The previous two comments show that the algorithm based on Taylor series is fast-convergent and very efficient. However, there is one potential problem with this algorithm. When one or more of parameters
a_{i}
(or
b_{i}
) is large, the coefficients

(1-a_{i})_{l}
(or
(1-b_{i})_{l}
) that will appear in (
23
) will also become very large and will have alternating sign, causing lot of cancellation in the sum in (
23
) and resulting in loss of precision. Therefore, when some of the parameters
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
are large one has to be mindful of this potential loss of precision. We resolved this problem by using a multi-precision arithmetic when computing the coefficients
c_{k}^{(n)}
via (
23
).

4.2
The Chebyshev expansion method

Let
\{\xi^{(n)}_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
be the coefficients in the Chebyshev expansion

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\xi^{(n)}_{0}+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\xi^{(n)}_{k}T_{k}(4z-1).

(24)

Note that we use scaled Chebyshev polynomials
T_{k}(4z-1)
, since we are interested in computing the
\beta
-function only for
z\in(0,1/2]
and the function
4z-1
maps the interval
(0,1/2]
onto
(-1,1)
. Below we present an algorithm that computes the coefficients
\{\xi^{(m)}_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
using the previously computed values of

\{\xi^{(m-1)}_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
.

Initial step:
Choose a large integer
N
and set
m=1
. Let
\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{0}=2
and
\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{k}=0
for
1\leq k\leq N
.

Step 1:

For
j=0,1,\dots,N
compute (using the Fast Fourier Transform)

v_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\xi}^{(m-1)}_{0}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\hat{\xi}^{(m-1)}_{k}\cos\Big(\frac{\pi k(j+1/2)}{N+1}\Big).

(25)

Step 2:

For
k=0,1,\dots,N
compute (using the Fast Fourier Transform)

\eta_{k}=\frac{2}{N+1}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}(1-(1+x_{j})/4)^{b_{m}-1}v_{j}\cos\Big(\frac{\pi k(j+1/2)}{N+1}\Big),

(26)

where

x_{j}:=\cos\Big(\frac{\pi(j+1/2)}{N+1}\Big).

Step 3:

We set
\mu_{N}=\mu_{N+1}=0
and
A_{m}:=a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{m}
and compute recursively

\mu_{k-1}=\frac{1}{1+A_{m}/k}\Big(8\eta_{k}-2\mu_{k}-\mu_{k+1}(1-A_{m}/k)\Big),\;\;\;k=N,N-1,\dots,1,

(27)

Step 4:
We compute

\hat{\xi}^{(m)}_{0}=\frac{1}{A_{m}}\Big(\eta_{0}-\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{0}+\mu_{1})\Big)

(28)

and

\hat{\xi}^{(m)}_{k}=\frac{\mu_{k-1}-\mu_{k+1}}{8k},\;\;\;k=1,2,\dots,N.

(29)

After running the above algorithm for
m=1,2,\dots,n
, we obtain the coefficients
\{\hat{\xi}^{(n)}_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
(which also depend on
N
) that serve as approximations to the desired coefficients
\xi^{(n)}_{k}
. It is clear that the computational complexity of this algorithm is also
O(nN\ln(N))
– the same as for Taylor expansion method. The next theorem gives us a bound for the error of the approximation.

Theorem 2
.

There exists a constant
C
(depending on
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
) such that for all
N\geq 1

\max\limits_{0\leq k\leq N}|\hat{\xi}^{(n)}_{k}-\xi^{(n)}_{k}|<C3^{-8N/5}.

(30)

Remark 1
.

The constant in (
30
) can be slightly improved: as will be clear from the proof of Theorem
2
, it is true that

\max\limits_{0\leq k\leq N}|\hat{\xi}^{(n)}_{k}-\xi^{(n)}_{k}|=O(\rho^{-N})

for every
\rho\in(0,3+8\sqrt{8})
, where the constant in the big-O notation depends on
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
and
\rho
. Note that
3^{8/5}=5.7995...
and
3+\sqrt{8}=5.8284...
, thus the bound given in (
30
), while suboptimal, should be good enough for all practical purposes.

The proof of Theorem
2
relies on the following

Fact:

Assume the function
g(z)
is analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[1,\infty)
. Then the coefficients in the Chebyshev expansion

g(z)=w_{0}/2+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}w_{k}T_{k}(4z-1),\;\;\;0\leq z\leq 1/2,

satisfy
w_{k}=O(\rho^{-k})
for any
\rho\in(0,3+\sqrt{8})
.

The above result follows easily from the classical fact that the Chebyshev series expansion of a function
f
converges in the largest Bernstein ellipse in which the function
f
is analytic (see
[
4
]
[Theorem 5.16]). More precisely, the function
f((1+z)/4)
is analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[3,\infty)
, thus it is analytic inside the Bernstein ellipse

E_{\rho}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\frac{\textnormal{Re}(z)^{2}}{a_{\rho}^{2}}+\frac{\textnormal{Im}(z)^{2}}{b_{\rho}^{2}}=1\}

with
a_{\rho}=(\rho+\rho^{-1})/2
and
b_{\rho}=(\rho-\rho^{-1})/2
with
\rho=3+\sqrt{8}
.

Proof of Theorem
2
:

The choice of
\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{k}
in the initial step corresponds to our convention that

\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}-\\
-\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)\equiv 1=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{0}+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{k}T_{k}(4z-1),

thus at this level there is no approximation error:
\xi^{(0)}_{k}=\hat{\xi}^{(0)}_{k}
for all
k
.

Let us now justify Steps 1-4. We denote

f(z):=\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{m-1}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big),\;\;\;F(z):=\beta\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big).

According to (
15
) and (
18
),
these two functions are related by the equation

z^{A_{m}}F(z)=\int_{0}^{z}x^{A_{m}-1}(1-x)^{b_{m}-1}f(x)dx,\;\;\;z\in(0,1),

where
A_{m}:=a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{m}
.
We take derivative of both sides of the above equation and obtain a differential equation

A_{m}F(z)+zF^{\prime}(z)=(1-z)^{b_{m}-1}f(z),\;\;\;z\in(0,1).

(31)

Assume we know the coefficients
\theta_{k}
in the Chebyshev expansion

(1-z)^{b_{m}-1}f(z)=\theta_{0}/2+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\theta_{k}T_{k}(4z-1),\;\;\;0\leq z\leq 1/2.

(32)

Let us denote by
\nu_{k}
the coefficients in the Chebyshev expansion

F^{\prime}(z)=\nu_{0}/2+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\nu_{k}T_{k}(4z-1),\;\;\;0\leq z\leq 1/2.

(33)

Chebyshev polynomials satisfy

\int T_{n}(x)dx=\frac{T_{n+1}(x)}{2(n+1)}-\frac{T_{n-1}(x)}{2(n-1)},

which can be established from formulas 8.941 and 8.949.1 in

[
5
]
.
From here we obtain

\int T_{k}(4z-1)dz=\frac{1}{8}\Big(\frac{T_{k+1}(4z-1)}{k+1}-\frac{T_{k-1}(4z-1)}{k-1}\Big),\;\;\;k\geq 2.

We also check directly (using the facts
T_{1}(x)=x
and
T_{2}(x)=2x^{2}-1
) that

\int 1dz=T_{1}(4z-1)/4,\;\;\;\int T_{1}(4z-1)dz=T_{2}(4z-1)/16.

Combining the above formulas, we obtain, after integrating both sides of (
33
) and rearranging the terms of the series, the following expansion

F(z)=Q+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{\nu_{k-1}-\nu_{k+1}}{8k}T_{k}(4z-1),\;\;\;0\leq z\leq 1/2,

(34)

for some constant
Q
.
Next we use formulas

\displaystyle z=\frac{1}{4}(T_{1}(4z-1)+1),

\displaystyle zT_{k}(4z-1)=\frac{1}{8}(T_{k+1}(4z-1)+2T_{k}(4z-1)+T_{k-1}(4z-1)),\;\;\;k\geq 1,

and equations (
31
), (
32
), (
33
) and (
34
) and conclude that

\displaystyle A_{m}\Big[Q+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{\nu_{k-1}-\nu_{k+1}}{8k}T_{k}(4z-1)\Big]+\frac{\nu_{0}}{8}\Big(T_{1}(4z-1)+1\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{\nu_{k}}{8}\Big(T_{k+1}(4z-1)+2T_{k}(4z-1)+T_{k-1}(4z-1)\Big)=\theta_{0}/2+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\theta_{k}T_{k}(4z-1).

Comparing the coefficients in front of
T_{k}
in the above equation gives us the following identities:

\displaystyle 2A_{m}Q+\frac{1}{4}(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1})=\theta_{0},

(35)

\displaystyle A_{m}\frac{\nu_{k-1}-\nu_{k+1}}{8k}+\frac{1}{8}(\nu_{k-1}+2\nu_{k}+\nu_{k+1})=\theta_{k},\;\;\;k\geq 1.

(36)

The equation (
36
) can be written in an equivalent form

\nu_{k-1}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{A_{m}}{k}}\Big(8\theta_{k}-2\nu_{k}-\nu_{k+1}\Big(1-\frac{A_{m}}{k}\Big)\Big)

(37)

Now we have a preliminary form of the algorithm for computing
\xi_{k}^{(m)}
: First we compute recursively
\nu_{k}
via (
37
)
and then evaluate

\displaystyle\xi_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=2Q=\frac{1}{A_{m}}\Big(\theta_{0}-\frac{1}{4}(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1})\Big),

(38)

\displaystyle\xi_{k}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{k-1}-\nu_{k+1}}{8k},\;\;\;1\leq k\leq N.

(39)

There are two problems that we need to overcome to make this work. First of all, when performing backward iteration in (
37
) we need to start with some values
\nu_{N}
and
\nu_{N+1}
in order to compute
\nu_{k}
for
k=N-1,N-2,\dots,1,0
, however we do not know the values of
\nu_{N}
and
\nu_{N+1}
. Second, we do not know the values of coefficients
\theta_{k}
in the Chebyshev expansion (
32
), thus we will need to approximate them and control the resulting error. Let us first consider the former problem.

We define
\{\hat{\nu}_{k}\}_{0\leq k<N}
to be the values obtained by recursion (
37
) starting from
\hat{\nu}_{N}=\hat{\nu}_{N+1}=0
. Let us estimate the difference
y_{k}=\nu_{k}-\hat{\nu}_{k}
for
0\leq k<N
. Note that
y_{k}
satisfy the homogeneous recurrence equation

y_{k-1}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{A_{m}}{k}}\Big(-2y_{k}-y_{k+1}\Big(1-\frac{A_{m}}{k}\Big)\Big),\;\;\;0\leq k<N

(40)

started with values
y_{N}=\nu_{N}
and
y_{N+1}=\nu_{N+1}
. We denote

{\bf u}_{k}=[y_{k-1},y_{k}]^{T}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
and

Q_{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{2}{1+A_{m}/k}&-\frac{1-A_{m}/k}{1+A_{m}/k}\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Then recursion (
40
) can be rewritten in vector-matrix form as follows

{\bf u}_{k}=Q_{k}{\bf u}_{k+1}

and we find

{\bf u}_{k}=Q_{k}Q_{k+1}\dots Q_{N}{\bf u}_{N+1}.

(41)

Let us also define
R_{k}=V^{-1}Q_{k}V
, where

V:=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\
2&0\end{bmatrix}.

One can check that

R_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&\frac{1}{2}\\
0&-\frac{1-A_{m}/k}{1+A_{m}/k}\end{bmatrix},

so that
R_{k}
belongs to class
{\mathcal{D}}
of matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}-1&\frac{1}{2}\\
0&d_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}

with
|d_{2,2}|<1
. It is easy to show by induction that a product of
l
matrices from class
{\mathcal{D}}
has the form

\begin{bmatrix}(-1)^{l}&d_{1,2}\\
0&d_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}

where
|d_{1,2}|<l/2
and
|d_{2,2}|<1
, thus the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
norm of this matrix is less than
1+l
.
From (
41
) we conclude

{\bf u}_{k}=VR_{k}R_{k+1}\dots R_{N}V^{-1}{\bf u}_{N+1}

so that
\lVert{\bf u}_{k}\rVert_{\infty}<6(2+N-k)\lVert{\bf u}_{N+1}\rVert_{\infty}
for
0\leq k\leq N
(since
\lVert V\rVert_{\infty}=3
and
\lVert V^{-1}\rVert_{\infty}=2
). Since
\nu_{k}
are coefficients in the Chebyshev expansion
(
33
) of the function
F^{\prime}(z)

that is analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,\infty)
, we know that

\nu_{k}=O(\rho^{-k})
for any
\rho\in(0,3+\sqrt{8})
. Thus

\lVert{\bf u}_{N+1}\rVert_{\infty}=O(\rho^{-N})
and
for every
0\leq k\leq N
we have
\lVert{\bf u}_{k}\rVert_{\infty}=O(N\rho^{-N})
for any
\rho\in(0,3+\sqrt{8})
.
Since
3^{8/5}<(3+\sqrt{8})
, for
\rho\in(3^{8/5},3+\sqrt{8})
we have

N\rho^{-N}=O(3^{-8N/5})
and we conclude that
y_{k}=\nu_{k}-\hat{\nu}_{k}=O(3^{-8N/5})
.

Thus we have shown that the backward recursion (
37
) is stable and if we start it from
\nu_{N}=\nu_{N+1}=0
we will contribute an error of size
O(3^{-8N/5})
.

Now we need to address the second issue, namely, that we do not know the coefficients
\theta_{k}
in the Chebyshev expansion (
32
). However, we can approximate
f
by its truncated Chebyshev series, which will give us an approximation

(1-z)^{b_{m}-1}f(z)\approx g(z):=(1-z)^{b_{m}-1}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\xi^{(m-1)}_{0}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\xi^{(m-1)}_{k}T_{k}(4z-1)\Big],

and the difference
(1-z)^{b_{m}-1}f(z)-g(z)
will be bounded by
O(\rho^{-N})
for any
\rho\in(0,3+\sqrt{8})
, uniformly on
0\leq z\leq 1/2
(this follows from the fact that the coefficients
\xi^{(m-1)}_{k}
decay at rate
O(\rho^{-k})
as
k\to+\infty
).
The numbers
\eta_{k}
computed in (
26
) are precisely the Chebyshev coefficients of
g(z)
on
0\leq z\leq 1/2
. Thus
|\eta_{k}-\theta_{k}|=O(\rho^{-N})
for
0\leq k\leq N
and
any
\rho\in(0,3+\sqrt{8})
. Since we have already demonstrated that the backward iteration (
37
) is stable, we see that when we replace
\theta_{k}
by
\eta_{k}
we contribute an overall error of the size
O(3^{-8N/5})
.

Numerical examples

In this section we present several examples that will illustrate the performance of the two algorithms for computing the generalized beta function

\mathrm{B}\Big(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\\

in (

) and it is also used to compute various moments of the generalized beta distribution as in (

). These two computations were instrumental in
[
3
]
and it was the question of efficiently computing the values of the generalized beta function that motivated this research. However, as we explained in section

using a Taylor series expansion approach described in section
