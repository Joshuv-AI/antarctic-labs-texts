PINNSim: A Simulator for Power System Dynamics based on Physics-Informed Neural Networks
Jochen Stiasny, Spyros Chatzivasileiadis, Baosen Zhang

Abstract
The dynamic behaviour of a power system can be described by a system of differential-algebraic equations. Time-domain simulations are used to simulate the evolution of these dynamics. They often require the use of small time step sizes and therefore become computationally expensive. To accelerate these simulations, we propose a simulator – PINNSim – that allows to take significantly larger time steps. It is based on Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) for the solution of the dynamics of single components in the power system. To resolve their interaction we employ a scalable root-finding algorithm. We demonstrate PINNSim on a 9-bus system and show the increased time step size compared to a trapezoidal integration rule. We discuss key characteristics of PINNSim and important steps for developing PINNSim into a fully fledged simulator. As such, it could offer the opportunity for significantly increasing time step sizes and thereby accelerating time-domain simulations.

I
Introduction

Differential equations form a centre piece in the modelling of the dynamic behaviour of power systems. They provide very widely applicable component and system models, however, their solution requires numerical integration methods. These tools for
Time-Domain Simulations (TDSs)
constitute a workhorse of power system analysis and can reliably deliver accurate solutions. The used algorithms are scalable, versatile, and applicable without case-specific modifications, but they incur a high computational burden
[
1
]
. Efforts around improved solvers and parallelisation can reduce this burden, see
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
for an overview. However, a fundamental problem is the small time step size that is required by the schemes to ensure accurate results and numerical stability. Hence many time steps and function evaluations are necessary.

The proposition of
Neural Network (NN)
-based approaches
[
5
]
, and in particular
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

[
6
]
, has very different characteristics. In the training process,
PINNs
learn the solution to the provided differential equations and subsequently provide fast and sufficiently accurate solutions over long time intervals – addressing two main issues of conventional numerical integration methods.
PINNs
have been adopted to power system dynamics in
[
7
]
to predict single machine dynamics. In
[
8
]
, the related methodology of operator learning is also applied to single machine dynamics. The applicability of learning-based methods to multi-machine systems is explored in
[
9
]
and with alternative architectures in
[
10
,
11
]
. The speed advantage persists and the accuracy remains sufficiently high for the trained setup. However, the common challenge lies in achieving generalisation, i.e., being able to apply the same
NN
for an unseen task without retraining. Such flexibility would make
NN
-based
TDS
approaches comparable with conventional numerical integration methods. However, in the case of multi-machine systems, this requires the generalisation to a very large variety of setups. As a consequence, the dimensionality of the associated learning tasks increases dramatically with the system size and thereby becomes very or even too costly to learn
[
12
]
.

To nonetheless harness the advantages of
PINNs
, i.e., fast solutions for large time steps, we propose
PINNSim
. This algorithm allows a modular integration of
PINNs
into
TDSs
for multi-machine systems. We learn the solution of all dynamic components in the system separately, i.e., with entirely independent
PINNs
, and before executing any simulation. Thereby, the learning tasks are of relatively low dimensionality and hence remain tractable. To perform a
TDS
, we solely need to determine the interaction between the components. To this end, we utilise a root-finding algorithm similar to conventional
TDS
algorithms; at this stage, no learning is required. As a result, the scalability and flexibility of PINNSim become decoupled from the difficulties of learning high-dimensional problems. At the same time, we still can benefit from fast and accurate solutions for larger time step sizes due to the use of
PINNs
.

II
Concept

To facilitate the presentation of the methodology in
Section

III
, we want to first describe the conceptual idea behind PINNSim. It originates from the problem that we face when solving
Differential-Algebraic Equations (DAEs)
, a form of differential equations where a set of algebraic equations constraints the differential equations. It formulates as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)

(1a)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\bm{g}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)

(1b)

and we refer to
\bm{x}(t)
as the differential variables and to
\bm{y}(t)
as the algebraic variables. The update function
\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)
and the algebraic relationship
\bm{g}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)
govern the dynamics of the system
1
1

1

For notational clarity, we formulate an autonomous, unforced system. The conceptual idea can also accommodate non-autonomous and forced systems.
. Our interest focuses on the particular form of index-1
DAEs
or semi-analytical
DAEs

[
13
]
. This form implies that
\bm{g}
can be differentiated once with respect to time
t
which is possible when
\frac{\partial\bm{g}{}}{\partial\bm{y}}
is non-singular. Based on
Eq.

1
, we could describe the temporal evolution of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
as

\displaystyle\bm{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\bm{f}\left(\bm{x},\bm{y}\right)d\tau

(2a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{g}^{\prime}(\bm{x})

(2b)

where
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}(t_{0})
represents the initial condition and
\bm{g}^{\prime}(\bm{x})
describes the solution to the algebraic equations given
\bm{x}
. However, usually no analytical expression describes the evolution
\bm{x}(t)
and
\bm{y}(t)
for a given
\bm{x}_{0}
. Hence, we revert to numerical integration methods to obtain an approximate solution.

To resolve the non-trivial integration operation and the implicit relationship
\bm{g}
, numerical schemes often restrict the functional form of
\bm{x}
to a certain approximation
\hat{\bm{x}}
. For instance,
Runge-Kutta (RK)
methods assume a polynomial form of
\hat{\bm{x}}{}(t)=\bm{x}_{0}+a_{1}(t-t_{0})+a_{2}(t-t_{0})^{2}+\dots
as they match the Taylor expansion up to a certain degree by construction. The different
RK
schemes prescribe the order of the scheme and the computation of the coefficients. When algebraic variables are present, they have to be interfaced with the approximation of the differential variable to incorporate their interaction with each other. Simultaneous and partitioned integration methods describe such routines
[
1
]
and are used in many variations. The accuracy of these constructions is dependent on the order of the used integration scheme and potential “interface” errors. These considerations and aspects of numerical stability limit the usable time step size.

By choosing a different functional form for
\hat{\bm{x}}
, like Fourier-series based or around the Adomian decomposition
[
14
]
, the practical time step size might be increased. Due to their high flexibility of their functional form,
PINNs
, and
NNs
in general as suggested in
[
5
]
, can allow significantly larger time steps. In fact, we can even choose a functional form of
\hat{\bm{y}}{}(t)
and approximate
\hat{\bm{x}}{}=\PINN(t,\hat{\bm{y}}{})
in dependence of it. However, this great approximation flexibility of
PINNs
comes with the challenge of generalising well across the entire domain of interest, i.e., being accurate over the entire domain and not only on the training dataset. When the dimensionality of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
in
Eq.

1
increases, the training of a
PINN
to approximate a wide range of solutions becomes increasingly difficult and eventually intractable. With PINNSim, we avoid this problem by exploiting the structure of the power system specific
DAEs
. The structure allows a decomposition of
Eq.

1
into multiple smaller sub-problems which remain tractable from a learning perspective. At the same time, the use of
PINNs
allows for large and accurate time steps of these sub-problems. To simulate the entire system, we need to align the sub-problems’ solutions by enforcing the algebraic relationship
\bm{g}\left(\bm{x}{},\bm{y}{}\right)
.

III-A

Problem formulation and solution approach

The key relation for power system dynamics is described by the current balance, i.e., Kirchhoff’s current law, and it needs to hold at all times
t
and for each of the
n
buses in the network. We distinguish between the complex current injections stemming from the network
\bar{\bm{i}}^{N}{}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and from connected components
\bar{\bm{i}}^{C}{}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The former can be described by the algebraic relationship (neglecting electro-magnetic transients)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{i}}^{N}{}=\bar{\bm{Y}}\bar{\bm{v}}.

(3)

where
\bar{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
represents the complex voltages at the buses and
\bar{\bm{Y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the complex admittance matrix. The component current injections
\bar{i}_{i}^{C}
at bus
i
can stem from a
dynamic component
, i.e., their behaviour is governed by differential equations,

\displaystyle\bar{i}_{i}^{C}

\displaystyle=h_{i}(\bm{x}_{i},\bar{v}_{i})

(4a)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bar{v}_{i},\bm{u}_{i}\right)

(4b)

where the current injection depends on the state vector
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and the voltage
\bar{v}_{i}
of the bus
i
to which the component is connected
2
2

2

For notational ease, we assume that component
i
is connected to bus
i
. If component
i
was connected to bus
j
,
\bar{v}_{i}
would be replaced by
\bar{v}_{j}
. If a component is connected to multiple buses, all of the corresponding voltages will be included in
h_{i}(\cdot)
and
\bm{f}_{i}\left(\cdot\right)
in
Eqs.

4b
and
4a
.
. The update function
\bm{f}{}_{i}
can furthermore depend on control inputs
\bm{u}_{i}
. For
static components
the current injections becomes a function of the local voltage
\bar{i}_{i}^{C}=h_{i}(\bar{v}_{i})
. If no component is connected to bus
i
, then
\bar{i}_{i}^{C}=0
. If multiple components were connected to the same bus, the current injections would be summed up.

\displaystyle\bm{0}=\bar{\bm{i}}^{C}{}(\bm{x}(t),\bar{\bm{v}}(t))-\bar{\bm{i}}^{N}{}(\bar{\bm{v}}(t)),

(5)

and consider the system’s structure, we note that the component currents
\bar{\bm{i}}^{C}
have only a
local
dependency, i.e., to their corresponding state vectors
\bm{x}_{i}
and the local voltage
\bar{v}_{i}

\bar{\bm{i}}^{N}

.

PINNSim exploits this structure as follows: We assume a voltage evolution
\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}{}(t)
– we denote all approximations with the hat symbol
\hat{}
– based on which we evaluate the resulting network currents
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{N}
and the component currents
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{C}
. For the latter, we furthermore require the state evolution
\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}
of each component; we approximate the necessary temporal integration with
PINNs
. When comparing the resulting currents

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{C}{}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}(t),\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}(t)\right)-\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{N}{}\left(\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}(t)\right)

(6)

the result will not equal
\bm{0}
by default, i.e., the current balance
Eq.

5
is not obeyed. However, the mismatch indicates the quality of the approximation. By adjusting the assumed voltage evolution
\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}{}(t)
, we iteratively reduce this mismatch. The resulting voltage
\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}{}(t)
and state
\hat{\bm{x}}{}(t)
evolutions constitute the solution of PINNSim to the system of
DAEs
.

III-B

Parametrisation of the voltage evolution
\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(t)

We describe the evolution of the complex voltage in polar form
\bar{v}_{i}(t)=V_{i}(t)e^{j\theta_{i}(t)}
. To approximate
\bar{v}_{i}(t)
, we express the voltage magnitude
V_{i}
and the voltage angle
\theta_{i}
as a power-series with respect to time up to power
r
as suggested in
[
16
]

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}V_{k,i}(t-t_{0})^{k}\right)e^{j\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}\theta_{k,i}(t-t_{0})^{k}\right)}.

(7)

The coefficients
V_{0,i},V_{1,i},\dots,V_{r,i}
and
\theta_{0,i},\theta_{1,i},\dots,\theta_{r,i}
form the parameters which we will later on update to improve the approximation. The vector
\bm{\Xi}{}_{i}
collects all parameters at bus
i

\displaystyle\bm{\Xi}{}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{0,i}&\theta_{0,i}&\ldots&V_{r,i},\theta_{r,i}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\Xi}{}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(r+1)}.

(8)

This parametisation is repeated for all
n
buses in the system. We concatenate all
\bm{\Xi}{}_{i}
in the vector
\bm{\Xi}{}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(r+1)n}
.

III-C

Solving component dynamics with PINNs

The exact solution for the evolution of the differential variables
\bm{x}_{i}(t)
of a single dynamic component
i
can be obtained by integration of
Eq.

4b

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0,i}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\bm{f}_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{i}(\tau),\bar{v}_{i}(\tau),\bm{u}_{i}\right)d\tau.

(9)

As there usually exists no explicit analytical solution to
9
, we need to approximate the solution. As motivated earlier, using
PINNs
allows us to accurately approximate
9
over large time steps
\Delta t=t-t_{0}
while being fast to evaluate. The approximation will be dependent on the time step size
\Delta t
, the initial state
\bm{x}_{0,i}
, potentially the control input
\bm{u}_{i}
, and the voltage profile
\bar{v}_{i}
. As we cannot train the
PINN
with an arbitrary
\bar{v}_{i}
, we restrict the learning to the same form as in
Eq.

7
, i.e.,
\bar{v}_{i}\approx\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(\Delta t,\bm{\Xi}_{i})
. Hence, the approximation of
Eq.

9
will depend also on
\bm{\Xi}_{i}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)=\PINN(\Delta t,\bm{x}_{0,i},\bm{\Xi}_{i},\bm{u}_{i}).

(10)

The functional form of the
PINN
is a standard feed-forward
NN
with
K
hidden layers parameterised by the weight matrices
\bm{W}^{k}
and bias vectors
\bm{b}^{k}
and a non-linear activation function
\sigma

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=[\Delta t,\bm{x}_{0,i},\bm{\Xi}_{i},\bm{u}_{i}]

(11a)

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\bm{W}^{k+1}\bm{z}_{k}+\bm{b}^{k+1}\right),\,\forall k=0,1,\ldots,K-1

(11b)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0,i}+\Delta t(\bm{W}^{K}\bm{z}_{K}+\bm{b}^{K}).

(11c)

We adjust the last layer to enforce that the initial condition
\bm{x}_{0,i}
is met if
\Delta t=0
, i.e.,
t=t_{0}
. Thereby we ensure numerical consistency and can improve inaccuracies related to recursive application of the
PINN

[
5
]
.
To train a
PINN
, we optimise

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}^{1},\bm{b}^{1},\dots,\bm{W}^{K},\bm{b}^{K}}\quad\mathcal{L}_{x}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{c},

(12)

where
\mathcal{L}_{x}
evaluates the prediction error based on a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of simulated data points

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\mathcal{D}\right\rvert}\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lvert\mathcal{D}\right\rvert}\left\lVert\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(j)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(13)

and a physics-based loss term
\mathcal{L}_{c}
evaluated on
collocation
points which do not require any simulation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left\lvert\mathcal{D}\right\rvert}\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lvert\mathcal{D}_{c}\right\rvert}\left\lVert\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(j)}-\bm{f}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(j)},\bar{v}_{i}^{(j)},\bm{u}_{i}^{(j)}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(14)

The two loss terms are weighted with the hyperparameter
\alpha
. For a more detailed explanation of
PINNs
, we refer to
[
9
]
. Based on the evolution of the state
\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)
and the voltage
\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(t)
, we can calculate the current injection
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}
by evaluating

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}(t)=h_{i}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}(t),\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(t)).

(15)

For each of the
m
dynamic components, we will train a separate
PINN
and all training can be can be performed in advance of executing PINNSim.

III-D

Updating the voltage profile

The approximations
\hat{\bar{v}}_{i}(t)
and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}(t)
are both continuous functions and therefore the same holds for
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}(t)
and
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}(t)
. The current balance
Eq.

5
will only be fulfilled, if
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}(t)=\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}(t)
for the entire time step and at all buses. We express this notion by requiring that the norm
\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert
shall be 0. This norm

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\langle\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N},\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\rangle}

(16)

is induced by the inner product
\langle\bar{a},\bar{b}\rangle
between two complex functions
\bar{a}(t),\bar{b}(t)
over the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta t]

\displaystyle\langle\bar{a},\bar{b}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\bar{a}(t)\,\bar{b}^{*}(t)\,dt.

(17)

As
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}
and
\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}
depend on the parametrised voltages
\hat{\bar{\bm{v}}}{}(t,\bm{\Xi})
, we subsequently aim to find a parametrisation
\bm{\Xi}
that minimises
\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert
. To this end, we formulate the following optimisation problem for the entire system as the sum of the norms (we square
Eq.

16
to avoid the calculation of the square root)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Xi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert^{2}.

(18)

The above expression involves the integration

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\Re\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right)^{2}+\Im\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right)^{2}dt

which we approximate with the Midpoint rule, i.e., we split the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta t]
into
s
equally sized intervals of width
\frac{\Delta t}{s}
and sum the function value at the middle of these intervals

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lVert\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}\right\rVert^{2}\approx\frac{\Delta t}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}&\Re\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}(t_{j})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}(t_{j})\right)^{2}\\
&+\Im\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{C}(t_{j})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{i}^{N}(t_{j})\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(19)

Thereby, we can approximate the optimisation in
Eq.

18
as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Xi}}\frac{\Delta t}{s}\bm{\rho}{}^{\top}\bm{\rho}{}

(20)

where
\bm{\rho}
collects all summands in a vector

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\rho}{}&=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{1}^{C}(t_{1})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{1}^{N}(t_{1})\right)\\
\Im\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{1}^{C}(t_{1})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{1}^{N}(t_{1})\right)\\
\vdots\\
\Re\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{n}^{C}(t_{s})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{n}^{N}(t_{s})\right)\\
\Im\left(\hat{\bar{i}}_{n}^{C}(t_{s})-\hat{\bar{i}}_{n}^{N}(t_{s})\right)\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\rho}{}\in\mathbb{R}^{2ns}\end{split}

(21)

We solve the above non-linear least square problem by iteratively updating the parameters
\bm{\Xi}^{(k+1)}=\bm{\Xi}^{(k)}+\Delta\bm{\Xi}
. To determine
\Delta\bm{\Xi}
, we compute the Jacobian

\displaystyle\bm{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{\rho}{}}{\partial\bm{\Xi}}\Bigr|_{\bm{\Xi}^{(k)}},\quad\bm{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{2ns\times 2(r+1)n}

(22)

at the values
\bm{\Xi}^{(k)}
and solve the linear problem

\displaystyle\left(\bm{J}^{\top}\bm{J}\right)\Delta\bm{\Xi}=-\bm{J}^{\top}\bm{\rho}{}.

(23)

To calculate
\bm{J}
in
Eq.

22
, we use
Automatic Differentiation (AD)

[
17
]
as all computations for
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{C}
and
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{N}
are expressed as explicit functions. While the size of
\bm{J}
can become large, it has a very sparse structure that can be exploited in its construction and when solving
Eq.

23
– herein lies the key to the scalability of PINNSim as it closely resembles the structure of conventional integration schemes such as the trapezoidal method.

V-A

Accuracy of PINNSim for a full TDS

First, we consider a simulation of the described test case over
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{s}

V-C

Computation cost of a time step

The larger time step sizes of PINNSim come at increasd computational cost compared to the trapezoidal rule. Providing a fair comparison based on optimised implementations will be future work, instead, we will here focus on the main cost of PINNSim from a perspective of scalability.

In
Algorithm

1
, the evaluation of the inner loop (lines 2-8) requires the calculation of network and component current injections
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{N}
,
\hat{\bar{\bm{i}}}^{C}
and their sensitivity to
\bm{\Xi}
. For the network currents, this requires merely matrix-vector products and for the component currents the evaluations of the
PINNs
. A single evaluation (pass) of a
PINN
requires on the order of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
, e.g., to compute the current injection, and the sensitivity calculations requires only two additional passes thanks to
AD
. As all
PINNs
could be evaluated in parallel, these computations can easily be scaled to large system. The “expensive” computation arises in the solution of the linear system of equations in line 9 of
Algorithm

1
, but its sparsity and known structure can be exploited. A closely related calculation is required for the trapezoidal rule.

The overall cost then scales linearly with the number of iterations
k
per time step, i.e., line 1-10 in
Algorithm

1
, until convergence is reached. We show in
Fig.

5
the value of the objective
Eq.

19
over the iterations for two time step sizes and different values of
r
. In the left panel, the least square problem is fully determined (
s=r+1
), hence the objective value continues to decrease. In the right panel, the five additional query points render the problem over-determined, hence the algorithm converges at a non-zero objective value. In either case, we observe that larger time step sizes require more iterations, while the order of the voltage profiles primarily affects the magnitude of the objective value and less its convergence.

VI
Discussion

The presented results shall serve as a proof of concept of PINNSim as a novel time stepping simulator. We demonstrated that PINNSim outperforms the trapezoidal rule on the metric of the allowable time step size. The following describes four aspects that are necessary to turn PINNSim from a proof of concept into a fully fledged simulator for power system dynamics and to realise the potential acceleration of
TDSs
.

1.

Speed
: Numerical methods require a high level of optimisation in the implementation to become competitive. For PINNSim this concerns primarily the optimisation of the calculation of the residual
\bm{\rho}
, the Jacobian
\bm{J}
, and the solution of the sparse linear system in
Eq.

23
. The focus should therefore lie on controlling memory allocations, utilising parallelisation, and exploiting sparsity patterns for these computations.

2.

Accuracy
: The accuracy of PINNSim hinges around accurately learned
PINNs
. We envision that this process can be highly standardised, so that
PINNs
can be trained reliably to high accuracy and with desirable error characteristics for a wide range of dynamic components.

3.

Scale
: The analysis of the computational complexity of PINNSim suggests its scalability but it remains to be shown in practice. Furthermore, we need to investigate the accuracy and convergence properties of the voltage update scheme for larger systems.

4.

Applications
: In this work, we have considered the dynamics of classical machine models under a set-point change. Future work should include more detailed dynamical models and apply PINNSim to short-circuits, topology changes, and other numerically more demanding setups.

VII
Conclusion

With PINNSim, we introduced a novel approach for time-domain simulations that allows the integration of
PINNs
. As a result we achieve accurate results with significantly larger time steps than with the common trapezoidal method. Hence, we require fewer time steps which could result in a significant acceleration of time-domain simulations in power systems. By design, we only require the training of
PINNs
for single dynamical components and thereby enable the scalability of PINNSim to large systems. Additionally, all calculations at run-time are highly scalable and parallelisable, necessary requirements to develop PINNSim into a powerful simulator.
