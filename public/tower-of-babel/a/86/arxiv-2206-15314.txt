Equation of State of Neutron-rich Matter in d d -Dimensions Journal: Annals of Physics
Bao-Jun Cai, Bao-An Li

Abstract
Nuclear systems under constraints, with high degrees of symmetries and/or collectivities may be considered as moving effectively in spaces with reduced spatial dimensions. We first derive analytical expressions for the nucleon specific energy E 0 ​ ( ρ ) E_{0}(\rho) , pressure P 0 ​ ( ρ ) P_{0}(\rho) , incompressibility coefficient K 0 ​ ( ρ ) K_{0}(\rho) and skewness coefficient J 0 ​ ( ρ ) J_{0}(\rho) of symmetric nucleonic matter (SNM), the quadratic symmetry energy E sym ​ ( ρ ) E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho) , its slope parameter L ⁡ ( ρ ) L(\rho) and curvature coefficient K sym ​ ( ρ ) K_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho) as well as the fourth-order symmetry energy E sym , 4 ​ ( ρ ) E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho) of neutron-rich matter in general d d spatial dimensions (abbreviated as “ d d D”) in terms of the isoscalar and isovector parts of the isospin-dependent single-nucleon potential according to the generalized Hugenholtz-Van Hove (HVH) theorem. The equation of state (EOS) of nuclear matter in d d D can be linked to that in the conventional 3-dimensional (3D) space by the ϵ \epsilon -expansion which is a perturbative approch successfully used previously in treating second-order phase transitions and related critical phenomena in solid state physics and more recently in studying the EOS of cold atoms. The ϵ \epsilon -expansion of nuclear EOS in d d D based on a reference dimension d f = d − ϵ d_{\mathrm{f}}=d-\epsilon is shown to be effective with − 1 ≲ ϵ ≲ 1 -1\lesssim\epsilon\lesssim 1 starting from 1 ≲ d f ≲ 3 1\lesssim d_{\mathrm{f}}\lesssim 3 in comparison with the exact expressions derived using the HVH theorem. Moreover, the EOS of SNM (with/without considering its potential part) is found to be reduced (enhanced) in lower (higher) dimensions, indicating in particular that the many-nucleon system tends to be more bounded but saturate at higher densities in spaces with lower dimensions. The symmetry energy perturbed from its counterpart in 3D is found to strongly depend on the momentum-dependence of the nucleon isovector potential. Moreover, the specific structure of the fourth-order symmetry energy in d d D is also analyzed generally, and it is found to be naturally small, confirming the parabolic approximation for the EOS of neutron-rich matter from an even wider viewpoint. The links between the EOSs in 3D and d d D spaces from the ϵ \epsilon -expansion provide new perspectives to the EOS of neutron-rich matter. Further studies and potential applications of these links in nuclear physics and/or astrophysics are discussed.



1. Introduction

Dimensionality reflects distinct natures or independent degrees of freedom of a single particle, a many-body physical system or an object. The concept of dimensionality is very general and wide and could even not be limited to conventional physical systems, e.g., the dimensionality of (animal) behavior could be defined as the minimum number of features of the past needed to make the maximally informative predictions about the future

Since ordinary lifes and conventional physical systems live in three dimensions, their spatial degree of freedom are often frozen to three. To introduce the relevant characteristics of ANM EOS, lets first recall here a few definitions and terminologies.
The EOS of ANM at zero temperature can be described by the energy per nucleon (specific energy)
E(\rho,\delta)

with
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{n}}+\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
being the nucleon density and
\delta=(\rho_{\mathrm{n}}-\rho_{\mathrm{p}})/\rho
the isospin asymmetry of the system, here
\rho_{\mathrm{n}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
are the neutron and proton densities, respectively. Preserving the neutron-proton exchange symmetry, it is usually expanded in even powers of
\delta
as
[
60
]

E(\rho,\delta)\approx E_{0}(\rho)+E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta^{2}+E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)\delta^{4}+\cdots,

(1.1)

in terms of the energy per nucleon
E_{0}(\rho)\equiv E(\rho,0)
in symmetric nuclear matter (SNM), the (quadratic) symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\equiv 2^{-1}\partial^{2}E(\rho,\delta)/\partial\delta^{2}|_{\delta=0}
, the fourth-order (quartic) symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)\equiv{24}^{-1}\partial^{4}E(\rho,\delta)/\partial\delta^{4}|_{\delta=0}
, etc.
If we truncate the above expansion to order
\delta^{2}
, the resulting EOS is often called the parabolic approximation
[
79
]
.
Moreover, the EOS of SNM, the symmetry energy as well as the fourth-order symmetry energy could be further expanded around the saturation density
\rho_{0}
as

\displaystyle E_{0}(\rho)\approx

\displaystyle E_{0}(\rho_{0})+\frac{1}{2}K_{0}\chi^{2}+\frac{1}{6}J_{0}\chi^{3}+\cdots,~~

(1.2)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\approx

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})+L\chi+\frac{1}{2}K_{\mathrm{sym}}\chi^{2}+\frac{1}{6}J_{\mathrm{sym}}\chi^{3}+\cdots,

(1.3)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)\approx

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho_{0})+L_{\mathrm{sym,4}}\chi+\frac{1}{2}K_{\mathrm{sym,4}}\chi^{2}+\frac{1}{6}J_{\mathrm{sym,4}}\chi^{3}+\cdots,

(1.4)

defining the incompressibility
K_{0}\equiv 9\rho_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{0}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}
and skewness
J_{0}\equiv 27\rho_{0}^{3}\mathrm{d}^{3}E_{0}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho^{3}|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}
of the SNM, the slope coefficient
L\equiv 3\rho_{0}\mathrm{d}E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}
, the curvature coefficient
K_{\mathrm{sym}}\equiv 9\rho_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}
and the skewness coefficient
J_{\mathrm{sym}}\equiv 27\rho_{0}^{3}\mathrm{d}^{3}E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho^{3}|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}
of the symmetry energy, etc., here the dimensionless quantity
\chi
is defined as
\chi=(\rho-\rho_{0})/3\rho_{0}
.
The EOS of ANM provides an important and basic input for various applications in both nuclear physics and astrophysics

In this work, we explore the EOS of ANM in a space of general dimension

The establishment of the general formalism is the first step to investigate possibly some novel features itself and to explore more interesting problems for the nuclear many-body system in dimensions different from three. The organization and the main conclusions of the work are outlined as follows:

In section

, we establish the general formalism for the EOS of ANM including the characteristics

and

, etc., via the Hugenholtz-Van Hove (HVH) theorem
[

The HVH theorem is very useful for establishing the connection between the EOS of the system (which is a thermodynamic quantity) and the single particle energy (including the potential as well as the kinetic parts)
[

,

,

,

,

The results obtained from the HVH theorem are essentially model independent. Interesting features of the EOS of ANM may naturally emerge as we shift the focus from

to certain other dimensions.

In section
3
, we investigate the EOS of ANM in space with
d=2
and discuss its qualitative properties. Here we find that the parabolic approximation for the kinetic EOS of ANM is exact, meaning all the higher-order kinetic symmetry energies including the quatic term are exactly zero.
The parabolic approximation in spaces with a general dimension
d
is also investigated here and it is found to be naturally good considering the specific structure of the fourth-order symmetry energy.
Moreover, all the characteristic coefficients of the kinetic symmetry energy beyond the slope parameter
L
are also exactly zero, i.e., the curvature
K_{\mathrm{sym}}
as well as the skewness
J_{\mathrm{sym}}
vanish.
The origin of these features could be traced back to the linear relation between the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
and the nucleon density
\rho

Section
4
is denoted to the study on the
\epsilon
-expansion of the EOS of ANM based on the conventional dimension

. While we have no direct information about the EOS in low dimensions (e.g. 2D), the
\epsilon
-expansion gives us some indications about the qualitative characteristics of the 2D EOS. In particular, we find that the EOS of SNM will probably be reduced if
d
is downwardly perturbed, showing that the EOS of SNM is more bounded in 2D. The symmetry energy in 2D, on other hand, strongly depends on the momentum dependence of the symmetry (isovector) potential. Its qualitative tendency in 2D needs more accurate determination of the aforementioned momentum dependence of the nucleon potential and/or higher-order contributions from the
\epsilon
expansion.
In addition, the effectiveness of the
\epsilon
-expansion is demonstrated by comparing the exact kinetic EOS (which is obtained in section
2
) and the perturbative EOS.
Discussions in section
4
show that the 3D EOS actually encapsulates relevant information on the EOS in
d_{\mathrm{f}}
D with

being near 3.

In section

we further investigate the effects generated by varying the dimension

, adopting a toy model for the single-nucleon potential. Here the model nucleon potential is designed to effectively re-produce certain empirical knowledge on the 3D EOS
[

of the SNM, the magnitude

and the slope coefficient

of the symmetry energy at the saturation density

is also required to be consistent with the constraint from analyzing collective flow in relativisitc heavy-ion reactions. Then we find that the EOS of SNM as well as the symmetry energy are generally enhanced (reduced) as the dimension

increases (decreases), from the viewpoint of the
\epsilon
-expansion approach developed in section
4

These results may find relevant applications in both astrophysics and nuclear physics.

In section
6
, we introduce the short-range-correlations (SRC) induced high-momentum-tail (HMT) in the single-nucleon momentum distribution function

,

into the kinetic EOS of ANM in dimension

Although we can only modify the kinetic part at this point, the SRC-induced HMT is obviously an effective representation of the nucleon-nucleon interactions, thus useful information may be obtained by investigating how the kinetic EOS may change as the

The calculations of the relative nucleon momentum fluctuation also confirm this conclusion. This connection may find potential applications in the future to explore the 3D EOS of ANM from the low-dimensional counterpart, providing a possible alternative.

In section

2. General formalism

2.1. Review of the generalized HVH theorem in 3D and the EOS of neutron-rich matter

In this section, we derive the general formalism for the EOS of ANM in
d
D space via the generalized HVH theorem
[
87
]
.
We first briefly review the derivations in 3D and give the necessary ingredients
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
The single-nucleon potential
U_{J}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)
(where
J=\mathrm{n,p}
) could be expanded around
\delta=0
as,

U_{J}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)\approx U_{0}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)+U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\tau_{3}^{J}\delta+U_{\mathrm{sym,2}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\delta^{2}+\cdots,

(2.1)

where
\tau_{3}^{\mathrm{n}}=+1
and
\tau_{3}^{\mathrm{p}}=-1
, and the symmetry potentials are defined as,

U_{\mathrm{sym},\ell}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)=\left.\frac{1}{\ell!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\ell}}{\mathrm{d}\delta^{\ell}}\frac{U_{\mathrm{n}}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)+(-1)^{\ell}U_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)}{2}\right|_{\delta=0}.

(2.2)

With
l
being odd (even), one obtains the isovector (isoscalar) single-nucleon potentials.
If only the first two terms on the right hand side (RHS) of (
2.1
) are kept, the corresponding approximation reduces to the well-known Lane’s potential
[
99
]
.

As mentioned in the
Introduction
, the HVH theorem is independent of the nucleon-nucleon interactions adopted and should be satisfied by all nuclear many-body theories. It expresses the exact relation between the single-particle energy and the thermodynamic quantities in a many-body system.
The HVH theorem in the non-relativistic case for the SNM simply reads

P_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}=\mu_{0}\rho
,
where
P_{0}
is the pressure of the SNM and
\varepsilon_{0}
is the energy density, both are thermodynamic quantities,
\mu_{0}
is the nucleon chemical potential (single particle quantity). For SNM at saturation density
\rho_{0}
where
P_{0}=0
, the HVH theorem is reduced to the familar relation
\mu_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}/\rho_{0}=E_{0}(\rho_{0})
.
The power of the HVH theorem is established via the well-known relation between the Fermi momentum and the nucleon density, i.e.,
k_{\mathrm{F}}=(3\pi^{2}\rho/2)^{1/3}
(in 3D).
According to the thermodynamic definition of the pressure in SNM at an arbitrary density
\rho
, i.e.,
P_{0}=\rho^{2}\partial E_{0}/\partial\rho
,
one could obtain the following nucleon chemical potential

\mu_{0}=\rho\frac{\partial E_{0}}{\partial\rho}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\rho}=\rho\frac{\partial(\varepsilon_{0}/\rho-M)}{\partial\rho}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\rho}=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{0}}{\partial\rho}.

(2.3)

In fact, it is just the definition of the nucleon chemical potential in SNM.

For nucleons in ANM, the non-relativistic nucleon chemical potential
\mu_{J}
with
J
denoting protons or neutrons is written as,

\mu_{J}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})={T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}),

(2.4)

where
T_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})
is the nucleon kinetic energy and
k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}=k_{\mathrm{F}}(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)^{1/3}
is the nucleon Fermi momentum.
Generalizing the relation (
2.3
) to neutrons and protons leads to,

\displaystyle{T}_{\mathrm{n}}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{n}})+U_{\mathrm{n}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{n}})=\frac{\partial[\rho E(\rho,\delta)]}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{n}}},

(2.5)

\displaystyle{T}_{\mathrm{p}}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{p}})+U_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{p}})=\frac{\partial[\rho E(\rho,\delta)]}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}.

(2.6)

Subtracting Eq. (
2.5
) and Eq. (
2.6
) gives,

{[{T}_{\mathrm{n}}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{n}})-{T}_{\mathrm{p}}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{p}})]+[U_{\mathrm{n}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{n}})-U_{\mathrm{p}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{p}})]=\frac{\partial[\rho E(\rho,\delta)]}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{n}}}-\frac{\partial[\rho E(\rho,\delta)]}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}},}

(2.7)

both sides could be expanded according to the isospin asymmetry
\delta
.
Since

\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{J}}=\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\rho_{J}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial\rho_{J}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\frac{2\tau_{3}^{J}\rho_{\overline{J}}}{\rho^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta},

(2.8)

where
\rho_{\overline{\mathrm{p}}}=\rho_{\mathrm{n}}
, etc., one obtains

\frac{\partial(\rho E)}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{n}}}-\frac{\partial(\rho E)}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}=\frac{2}{\rho}\frac{\partial(\rho E)}{\partial\delta}=2\frac{\partial E}{\partial\delta}.

(2.9)

On the other hand, if one writes the EOS of ANM as
E(\rho,\delta)=E_{0}(\rho)+E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta^{2}+E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)\delta^{4}+\cdots=E_{0}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}E_{\mathrm{sym},2i}(\rho)\delta^{2i}
, then we obtain the following series in
\delta
,

\frac{\partial(\rho E)}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{n}}}-\frac{\partial(\rho E)}{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}=\sum_{i=1}4iE_{\mathrm{sym},2i}(\rho)\delta^{2i-1}\approx 4E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta+8E_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)\delta^{3}+\cdots.

(2.10)

The Eq. (
2.7
) can then be rewritten as

\sum_{J=\mathrm{n,p}}\tau_{3}^{J}\left[{T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\right]=\sum_{i=1}4iE_{\mathrm{sym},2i}(\rho)\delta^{2i-1},

(2.11)

where
E_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho)\equiv E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
is the conventional (quadratic) symmetry energy.
It is obvious that in order to obtain the symmetry energy one needs to expand the single-nucleon potential and the kinetic energy as power series in
\delta
. They should be expanded to order
\delta^{3}
if one wants to obtain the fourth-order symmetry energy, etc. In addition, if one subtracts the Eq. (
2.5
) and Eq. (
2.6
), one obtains the following equation determining the slope parameter of the symmetry energy at an arbitrary density
\rho

\sum_{J=\mathrm{n,p}}\left[{T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\right]=2\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left[\rho E_{0}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}\rho E_{\mathrm{sym},2i}(\rho)\delta^{2i}\right].

(2.12)

For example, in order to obtain the slope parameter of the conventional symmetry energy,
L(\rho)\equiv L_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho)
, we need to expand the single-nucleon energy to order
\delta^{2}
. Moreover, the determination of the slope parameter of the fourth-order symmetry energy, i.e.,
L_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)
, needs the expansion of the single-nucleon energy to order
\delta^{4}
, etc.
The zeroth-order of the Eq. (
2.12
) gives the formula for determining the EOS of SNM.
Then, according to the terms proportional to
\delta
in Eq. (
2.11
) and the terms proportional to
\delta^{2}
in Eq. (
2.12
), one obtains the formulae determining the symmetry energy as well as its slope parameter as,

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\times\mbox{coefficient of $\delta$ terms in expanding}\sum_{J=\mathrm{n,p}}\tau_{3}^{J}\left[{T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\right],

(2.13)

\displaystyle L(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\times\mbox{coefficient of $\delta^{2}$ terms in expanding}\sum_{J=\mathrm{n,p}}\left[{T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\right]+3E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho),

(2.14)

where the following relation is used when deriving the second relation,

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}[\rho E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\left[E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta^{2}+\rho\frac{\partial E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\delta^{2}+2\rho E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\delta\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial\rho}\right]=\left[-2E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)+\frac{2}{3}L(\rho)\right]\delta^{2},

(2.15)

similarly the fourth-order symmetry energy is given by

E_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)=\frac{1}{8}\times\mbox{coefficient of $\delta^{3}$ terms in expanding}\sum_{J=\mathrm{n,p}}\tau_{3}^{J}\left[{T}_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\right].

(2.16)

2.2. Generalization to
d
D space

Now consider the EOS of ANM in
d
D space, the total nucleon number
A=N+Z
could be written as,

A=2\cdot 2\cdot\left(\frac{R}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{0}^{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}=4\left(\frac{R}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{0}^{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\frac{d\pi^{d/2}k^{d-1}\mathrm{d}k}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}=\frac{2^{2-d}dR^{d}\pi^{-d/2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{d}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)},

(2.17)

here the pre-factor
4=2\cdot 2
is taken for considering the isospin and spin degeneracy.
By introducing the volume
V_{d}=R^{d}
, one obtains the relation between the nucleon density
\rho
and the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
as,

k_{\mathrm{F}}=\left[\rho 2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\left({d}/{2}+1\right)\right]^{1/d}\sim\rho^{1/d},

(2.18)

here
[\rho]\sim[\mathrm{fm}^{-d}]
.
We use the notation
\rho
as the density in
d
D in situations with no confusion, and when we discuss the perturbative expansion over the dimension
d
as in section
4
, the notation
\rho_{d}
is adopted to denote the density in
d
D and there
\rho
is the conventional 3D density (it should be clear from the context).
The expression of
k_{\mathrm{F}}
provides the mathematical foundations of the effectiveness of the perturbative calculation based on the dimension
d
.
Very similarly, the Fermi momenta of neutrons and protons are given by

k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{n}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}(1+\delta)^{1/d}
and
k_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{p}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}(1-\delta)^{1/d}
,
which could be written in a unified form,

k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}=k_{\mathrm{F}}(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)^{1/d}=\left[\rho 2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\left(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta\right)\right]^{1/d}.

(2.19)

Mathematically if the dimension
d
is very large and the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
is fixed at certain value, then the nucleon density
\rho=k_{\mathrm{F}}^{d}/2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)
decreases as
d
increases and approaches zero if
d\to\infty
.
Moreover, most of the density
\rho
is contained in an annulus of width
k_{\mathrm{F}}(1-1/d)
near the Fermi surface.

For the convenience of later calculations, here we list a few relevant perturbative quantities and relations. For the expansion based on the isospin asymmetry
\delta
, we have

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)^{m/d}\approx

\displaystyle 1+\frac{m\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}+\frac{m}{2d}\left(\frac{m}{d}-1\right)\delta^{2}+\frac{m\tau_{3}^{J}}{6d}\left(\frac{m}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{m}{d}-2\right)\delta^{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{m}{24d}\left(\frac{m}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{m}{d}-2\right)\left(\frac{m}{d}-3\right)\delta^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{5}).

(2.20)

The situation where
m=2
is relevant for computing the kinetic EOS.
This expansion is very intuitive since all even-order terms in
\delta
starting from
\delta^{4}
contain the factor
(m/d-1)(m/d-2)
which is identically zero for
d=1
or
d=2
(if
m=2
is adopted).
This means the kinetic fourth-order symmetry energy and terms beyond in low dimensions are exactly zero.
Similarly for the perturbative expansion around the reference density
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}
, by introducing the small quantity,

\theta_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}{3\rho_{\mathrm{f}}},

(2.21)

one obtains
\theta_{\mathrm{f}}=\theta_{0}\equiv\chi
with
\chi
being the conventional dimensionless quantity
(\rho-\rho_{0})/3\rho_{0}
for
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}=\rho_{0}
.
The Fermi momentum at density
\rho
can be expanded in terms of that at the reference density
{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}
as

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{F}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{F}}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}+3\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}\right)\approx k_{\mathrm{F}}(\rho_{\mathrm{f}})+\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\right|_{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}\cdot 3\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\left.\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}\right|_{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}\cdot 9\rho_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{F}}(\rho_{\mathrm{f}})+\frac{a}{d}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}^{1/d-1}\cdot 3{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{a}{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\rho_{\mathrm{f}}^{1/d-2}\cdot 9\rho_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{F}}(\rho_{\mathrm{f}})\times\left[1+\frac{3}{d}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\frac{9}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{3}),

(2.22)

where,

a=a(d)=\left[2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\right]^{1/d}.

(2.23)

Moreover, one has for the square of the Fermi momentum as,

k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}(\rho)\approx k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\times\left[1+\frac{6}{d}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\frac{9}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{3}).

(2.24)

In the remaining part of this subsection, we give the general expressions for the EOS of SNM
E_{0}(\rho)
, the symmetry energy
E_{\textmd{sym}}(\rho)
, the fourth-order symmetry energy
E_{\textmd{sym,4}}(\rho)
, the slope parameter
L(\rho)
of the symmetry energy, the incompressibility coefficient
K_{0}(\rho)
as well as the skewness
J_{0}(\rho)
of the EOS of SNM as functions of density
\rho
in dimensions
d
, in terms of the single-nucleon potential together with its isospin expansions.
The single-nucleon energy in
d

e_{J}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)={\mathbf{k}^{2}}/{2M}+{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)
,
with the first term the kinetic energy and the second term the potential energy. Considering the free Fermi gas (FFG) model, we have for the kinetic part of the EOS as
[
100
]
,

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,\delta)=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2M}\frac{1}{2}\left[(1+\delta)^{1+2/d}+(1-\delta)^{1+2/d}\right]=\frac{d}{d+2}{E}_{\mathrm{F}}F_{1+2/d}(\delta),

(2.25)

where
{E}_{\mathrm{F}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/2M
is the nucleon Fermi energy, and in addition,

\displaystyle F_{1+2/d}(\delta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[(1+\delta)^{1+2/d}+(1-\delta)^{1+2/d}\right]\approx 1+\frac{d+2}{d^{2}}\delta^{2}+\frac{(d^{2}-4)(d-1)}{6d^{4}}\delta^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{6}).

(2.26)

By expanding the
E^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,\delta)
around
\delta=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,\delta)\approx

\displaystyle\frac{d{E}_{\mathrm{F}}}{d+2}\left[1+\frac{d+2}{d^{2}}\delta^{2}+\frac{(d^{2}-4)(d-1)}{6d^{4}}\delta^{4}\right]=\frac{d{E}_{\mathrm{F}}}{d+2}+\frac{{E}_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\delta^{2}+\frac{d^{2}-3d+2}{6d^{3}}{E}_{\mathrm{F}}\delta^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{6}),

(2.27)

and consequently

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{kin}}_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d+2}{E}_{\mathrm{F}},~~E^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}{E}_{\mathrm{F}},~~E^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)=\frac{d^{2}-3d+2}{6d^{3}}{E}_{\mathrm{F}}.

(2.28)

The contribution from the single-nucleon potential to the EOS of SNM is similarly given as

E^{\mathrm{pot}}_{0}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f,~~k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}=\left[f2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\right]^{1/d},

(2.29)

and by combining the kinetic part and the potential contribution, one has the general expression for the EOS of SNM,

\displaystyle E_{0}(\rho)=\frac{d{E}_{\mathrm{F}}}{d+2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f=\frac{d}{d+2}\frac{1}{2M}\left[\rho 2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\right]^{2/d}+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f.

(2.30)

Next, the perturbative expansions for the kinetic energy and the single-nucleon potential
{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,|\mathbf{k}|)
could be given.
Since our aim is to give the analytic expressions for quantities including the fourth-order symmetry energy, the relevant expansions on
\delta
need to be kept at order
\delta^{3}
.
For the nucleon kinetic energy, one then has

\displaystyle T_{J}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{1}{d}\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta+\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2M}\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{2}{d}-2\right)\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{6M}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}).

(2.31)

The single-nucleon potential also needs to be expanded to order
\delta^{3}
, and when it is taken at the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx

\displaystyle U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+k_{\mathrm{F}}\left[\frac{\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}+\frac{1}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{6d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{d}-2\right)\tau_{3}^{J}\delta^{3}\right]\left.\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\left[\frac{\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}+\frac{1}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\delta^{2}\right]^{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{6}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}\right)^{3}\left.\frac{\partial^{3}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{3}}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}},

(2.32)

and similarly,

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+k_{\mathrm{F}}\left[\frac{\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}+\frac{1}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\delta^{2}\right]\left.\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\left(\frac{\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}{d}\right)^{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}},

(2.33)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym},2}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta,~~U_{\mathrm{sym},3}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx U_{\mathrm{sym},3}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}).

(2.34)

Combining these contributions, one obtains the nucleon potential at the Fermi surface as

\displaystyle{U_{J}}(\rho,\delta,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J})\approx

\displaystyle U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta\left[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\left[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2d^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta^{3}\Bigg[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}{6d^{3}}\frac{\partial^{3}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{3}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2d^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{6d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{d}-2\right)\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2d^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt-\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym},2}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+U_{\mathrm{sym},3}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\Bigg]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}.

(2.35)

According to the formulas derived earlier from the HVH theorem, i.e., relations (
2.13
), (
2.14
) and (
2.16
), we then obtain the expressions for
E_{\textmd{sym}}(\rho),L(\rho)
, and

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2d}\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(2.36)

\displaystyle L(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{d^{2}}\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}+\left[\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2d^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d^{2}}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\left.\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+3U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(2.37)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{24d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{2}{d}-2\right)\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}{24d^{3}}\frac{\partial^{3}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{3}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8d^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{24d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{d}-2\right)\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8d^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{8d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-1\right)\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{4d}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym},2}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{4}U_{\mathrm{sym},3}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}).

(2.38)

The symmetry energy could be cast into the following simple form,

E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2dM_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(2.39)

by introducing the scalar nucleon Landau effective mass
M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}
defined as
[
101
]

M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)=M\left(1+\frac{M}{|\mathbf{k}|}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right)^{-1}.

(2.40)

Taking the momentum at the Fermi surface gives the effective mass as a function of density.

In order to derive the expressions for
K_{0}(\rho)
and
J_{0}(\rho)
,
we need to use the zeroth-order equation of the HVH theorem, i.e.,

P_{0}(\rho)=\rho[\mu_{0}(\rho)-E_{0}(\rho)]
, where

\mu_{0}(\rho)={k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}/{2M}+U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})

is the nucleon chemical potential. Moreover, the
U_{0}
appeared here depends only on the density
\rho
(instead of also on the momentum), the zeroth-order equation could further be written in the following form by recasting the HVH theorem using

P_{0}(\rho)=3^{-1}\rho L_{0}(\rho)

\frac{1}{3}L_{0}(\rho)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2M}+U_{0}(\rho)-E_{0}(\rho),

(2.41)

where
L_{0}(\rho)=3\rho\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho
which is identically zero at
\rho_{0}
(definition of the saturation density
\rho_{0}
).
A few related relations are listed here for convenience,

\displaystyle E_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle=E_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+L_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\frac{1}{2}K_{0}(\rho_{\mathrm{f}})\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2},

(2.42)

\displaystyle L_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle=L_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+\left.3{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\right|_{{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}+\left.\frac{9{\rho^{2}_{\mathrm{f}}}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}L_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}\right|_{{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}}\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2},

(2.43)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho}

\displaystyle=3\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho}+3\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}},~~\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}L_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}=6\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}+3\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}E_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{3}}.

(2.44)

By expanding the equation (
2.41
) around the reference density
{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}L_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+\theta_{\mathrm{f}}\left[L_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+\frac{1}{3}K_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\right]+\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\left[K_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+\frac{1}{6}J_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\right],

(2.45)

\displaystyle\mathrm{RHS}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})}{2M}+U_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})-E_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})+\theta_{\mathrm{f}}\left[\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})}{Md}+\left.3{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\right|_{{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}}-L_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\right]

\displaystyle+\theta_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\left[\frac{9k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})}{2M}\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)+\left.\frac{9{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}\right|_{{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}}-\frac{1}{2}K_{0}({\rho_{\mathrm{f}}})\right],

(2.46)

where the total-derivative of the
U_{0}
with respect to the density
\rho
is understood here as
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\rho=\partial/\partial\rho+(\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}/\mathrm{d}\rho)\cdot\partial/\partial k_{\mathrm{F}}
, by comparing terms at the same order and removing the subscript “f”, we have

\displaystyle K_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{9k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{Md}+9\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}-6L_{0}(\rho),

(2.47)

\displaystyle J_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{27k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{2}{d}-1\right)+27\rho^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-9K_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{54k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}-2\right)+27\rho^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-81\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}+54L_{0}(\rho),

(2.48)

where

\displaystyle L_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle 3\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho}=\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{(d+2)M}+3\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}^{\mathrm{pot}}(\rho)}{\mathrm{d}\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{(d+2)M}-\frac{3}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f+3U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{(d+2)M}-\frac{3}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f+3U_{0}(\rho).

(2.49)

Thus the

and

could also be rewritten in the following forms as (where

),

\displaystyle K_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{d}\frac{d-2}{d+2}\frac{9k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}+9\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}+\frac{18}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f-18U_{0}(\rho),

(2.50)

\displaystyle J_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{d^{2}(d+2)}\frac{54k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{M}+27\rho^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-81\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}-\frac{162}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f+162U_{0}(\rho).

(2.51)

2.3. A relationship between the isovector
U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)
and isoscalar

potentials

Using the HVH theorem as shown above, we have derived separate expressions for the symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
and its slope parameter
L(\rho)
in terms of the isoscalar and isovector components of the single-nucleon potential. On the other hand, the basic definition of

as the density derivative of

also gives an expression for

once the

is obtained from the HVH theorem. Thus, when equating these two expressions for

, an identity follows.
This identity connects the strength of the (isovector) symmetry potential

with that of the (isoscalar) second-order potential
U_{\mathrm{sym},2}
in
d
D as,

U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)}{\partial\rho}-U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)\right)_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}-\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d}\left(\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)}{\partial|{\mathbf{k}}|}-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)}{\partial\rho\partial|{\mathbf{k}}|}\right)_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}.

(2.52)

This relation (
2.52
) holds for any single-nucleon potential at any density, i.e., it is model-independent, which can be used to check the consistency of a given
U_{J}
or act as a guidance in constructing phenomenological single-nucleon potentials.
Using the relation between the density
\rho
and the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
in
d
D, i.e.,
k_{\mathrm{F}}\sim\rho^{1/d}
or
\rho\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}/\mathrm{d}\rho=k_{\mathrm{F}}/d
, we can rewrite (
2.52
) in the following form,

U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})=\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{2d}\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{\mathrm{F}}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}-\frac{d}{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{d}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)}{\partial k_{\mathrm{F}}\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}.

(2.53)

If the single nucleon potential
U_{J}
has no momentum dependence, then (
2.52
) is reduced to

U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}-U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\right).

(2.54)

Interestingly, it is independent of the spacial dimension
d
.

As a basic application of the relation (
2.52
), we now check if the Skyrme pseudo-potential
[
102
]
fulfills the constraint given above. For example, the
D^{[4]}
-term in the single-nucleon potential
[
102
]
reads as

\frac{D^{[4]}}{16}\left[\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}|\mathbf{k}|^{4}}{3\pi^{2}}\cdot(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)+\frac{2k_{\mathrm{F}}^{5}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{3\pi^{2}}\cdot(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)^{5/3}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{7}}{7\pi^{2}}\cdot(1+\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)^{7/3}\right],

(2.55)

which depends on both the density
k_{\mathrm{F}}
and the momentum
|\mathbf{k}|
.
The symmetry potential and the second-order potential could be obtained straightforwardly by expanding the above expression around
\delta=0
. Putting them into the relation (
2.52
) with
d=3
, both sides readily give the result
D^{[4]}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{7}/27\pi^{2}
, i.e.,
U_{\mathrm{sym,2}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})=D^{[4]}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{7}/27\pi^{2}
.
Other momentum- and density-dependent terms of the Skyrme-like potential of Ref.
[
102
]
could be checked similarly. Another potential application of (
2.52
) is to construct consistent single-nucleon potentials.
For instance, consider a simple nucleon potential depending on the density alone as
U_{J}(\rho)=[\alpha+\alpha_{\mathrm{IS}}f(\Gamma_{\vartheta}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)]\widetilde{\rho}^{\vartheta}
where
\widetilde{\rho}=\rho/\rho_{0}
is the reduced density and
\Gamma_{\vartheta}
is a factor needed to fulfill the identity (
2.54
).
When using the relation (
2.54
) the isospin-independent term
\alpha
plays no role and only the isospin-dependent term represented by the
\alpha_{\mathrm{IS}}
is relevant.
Consequently, we have
U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=\alpha_{\mathrm{IS}}\Gamma_{\vartheta}f^{\prime}(0)\widetilde{\rho}^{\vartheta}
where the derivative of
f
is taken with respect to the isospin asymmetry
\delta
, and similarly
U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho)=2^{-1}\alpha_{\mathrm{IS}}\Gamma_{\vartheta}^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)\widetilde{\rho}^{\vartheta}
.
Putting the
U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
and
U_{\mathrm{sym,2}}(\rho)
into (
2.54
) gives the relation,

\Gamma_{\vartheta}=\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}\cdot({\vartheta}-1).

(2.56)

This formula excludes the linear function of
\Gamma_{\vartheta}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta
since the second-order derivative in the denominator is required to be non-zero. For instance, if
f(\Gamma_{\vartheta}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)=\exp(\Gamma_{\vartheta}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta)
, then the
\Gamma_{\vartheta}
is given as
\Gamma_{\vartheta}={\vartheta}-1
, i.e., the single-nucleon potential having the form
[\alpha+\alpha_{\mathrm{IS}}e^{(\vartheta-1)\tau_{3}^{J}\delta}]\widetilde{\rho}^{\vartheta}
fulfills the constraint from the HVH theorem.
Moreover if
f(x)=ax+bx^{2}
with
a,b>0
and
x=\Gamma_{\vartheta}\tau_{3}^{J}\delta
, then the
\Gamma_{\vartheta}
factor is given by
\Gamma_{\vartheta}=a({\vartheta}-1)/2b
, simply using the relation (
2.56
). Similarly, the potential
\sim\widetilde{\rho}^{\vartheta}[1-(\vartheta-1)\tau_{3}^{J}\delta/2]^{-1}
fulfills the requirement of Eq. (
2.54
).
There is no doubt that if the single-nucleon potential is momentum-dependent, the corresponding requirement by the HVH theorem will become more complicated. In these situations, one needs to adopt the more general relation (
2.52
).

3. The two-dimensional nucleonic EOS

In this section, we discuss the EOS of ANM in 2D.
In 2D, the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
is determined via the density
\rho
as
k_{\mathrm{F}}=(\pi\rho)^{1/2}
, see the general formula (
2.18
), thus the dimensions of density and Fermi momentum scale as
[\rho]=[\mathrm{fm}]^{-2}=[\mathrm{MeV}]^{2}
. The corresponding formulas for the EOS of ANM are given as

\displaystyle E_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\rho}{4M}+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f=\frac{\pi\rho}{4M}+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f,~~k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}=(\pi f)^{1/2},

(3.1)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\rho}{4M}+\left.\frac{\sqrt{\pi\rho}}{4}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{64}\left(\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}-k_{\mathrm{F}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{3}\frac{\partial^{3}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{3}}\right)_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{32}\left(\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}+k_{\mathrm{F}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\right)_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{8}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym},2}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{4}U_{\mathrm{sym},3}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle P_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{3\pi\rho^{2}}{4M}-\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f+\rho U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle K_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle 9\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}+\frac{18}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f-18U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle J_{0}(\rho)=

\displaystyle 27\rho^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}-81\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}-\frac{162}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}\left(f,k_{\mathrm{F}}^{f}\right)\mathrm{d}f+162U_{0}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle L(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{3\pi\rho}{4M}+\frac{3\pi\rho}{8}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\rho}}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right]_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\left.\sqrt{\pi\rho}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}})+3U_{\mathrm{sym},2}(\rho,k_{\mathrm{F}}),

(3.7)

\displaystyle K^{\mathrm{SNM}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle 9\frac{\partial P_{0}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}=\frac{9\pi\rho}{2M}+9\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}.

(3.8)

The coefficient
K^{\mathrm{SNM}}(\rho)
defined above is generally different from
K_{0}(\rho)=9\rho^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}E_{0}(\rho)/\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}
, and the relation between them is
K^{\mathrm{SNM}}(\rho)=K_{0}(\rho)+6L_{0}(\rho)
. Thus only at the saturation density
\rho_{0}
these two coefficients are equal
K^{\mathrm{SNM}}(\rho_{0})=K_{0}(\rho_{0})\equiv K_{0}
due to the vanishing of the pressure
P_{0}(\rho_{0})=L_{0}(\rho_{0})\rho_{0}/3
.

All the kinetic parts of the characteristic coefficients beyond the linear terms
L_{0}(\rho)
and
L(\rho)
are zero (including the coefficients
K_{0}(\rho),J_{0}(\rho)
and
K_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
), i.e., the EOS are totally represented by the effective potentials. Furthermore, the higher-order kinetic symmetry energies including the
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
are also zero (the sixth-order term is given by
E_{\mathrm{sym},6}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)=(3d-2)(d-1)(d-2)(2d-1)k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/180d^{5}M
) which vanishes with
d=2
, indicating that the conventional parabolic approximation of the kinetic EOS of ANM is exact in 2D.
Since the potential contribution to the EOS is essentially smaller than its kinetic part (especially at low densities), the absence of the kinetic part in the
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
already indicates the parabolic approximation should be good in 2D.

Since the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
in dimensions 2 scales with the density as
\rho^{1/2}
, the kinetic symmetry energy is linear in density.
If the single-nucleon potential
U_{0}
depending on the momentum
k
via the manner
U_{0}\approx a+bk+ck^{2}+\cdots
and
U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\approx a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}k_{\mathrm{F}}+c^{\prime}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
, then
\partial U_{0}/\partial k\approx b+2ck+\cdots
(and similarly for the symmetry potential), thus the density dependence of the
E_{0}(\rho)
or the symmetry energy is given as

\sim f_{1}\sqrt{\rho}+f_{2}\rho+f_{3}\rho^{3/2}+\cdots,

(3.9)

i.e., the effective expansion of the
E_{0}(\rho)
or the symmetry energy on density is based on
\rho^{1/2}
, which is different from the situation in dimensions 3 where the expansion element is
k_{\mathrm{F}}\sim\rho^{1/3}
.
As an example, assume that the
U_{0}
is a function of density
\rho
alone (without momentum dependence), in the polynomial form as,

U_{0}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}c_{i}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i},~~k_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\pi\rho},

(3.10)

consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\rho}}\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{0}}{\mathrm{d}k_{\mathrm{F}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\rho}}\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\sum_{i=1}c_{i}ik_{\mathrm{F}}^{i}=\frac{1}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}ic_{i}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}U_{0}(f)\mathrm{d}f=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{i=1}c_{i}\pi^{i/2}\int_{0}^{\rho}f^{i/2}\mathrm{d}f=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{i=1}c_{i}\pi^{i/2}\frac{1}{i/2+1}\rho^{i/2+1}=2\sum_{i=1}\frac{c_{i}}{i+2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i}.

(3.12)

The EOS of SNM is then expressed as,

E_{0}(\rho)=\frac{\pi\rho}{4M}+2\sum_{i=1}\frac{c_{i}}{i+2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i},

(3.13)

while the pressure and incompressibility are respectively given by

P_{0}(\rho)=\rho\left(\frac{3\pi\rho}{4M}+\sum_{i=1}\frac{i}{i+2}c_{i}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i}\right),~~K_{0}(\rho)=9\sum_{i=1}\frac{i}{2}\frac{i-2}{i+2}c_{i}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{i}.

(3.14)

If the density expansion contains only two effective terms, i.e.,
c_{1},c_{2}
, we then have

E_{0}(\rho)=\frac{2}{3}c_{1}\sqrt{\pi\rho}+\left(\frac{1}{4M}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}\right)\pi\rho,~~P_{0}(\rho)/\rho=\frac{1}{3}c_{1}\sqrt{\pi\rho}+\left(\frac{3}{4M}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}\right)\pi\rho,~~K_{0}(\rho)=-\frac{3}{2}c_{1}\sqrt{\pi\rho}.

(3.15)

Assuming the nuclear system in dimensions 2 behaves similarly as the one in dimensions 3 (at least qualitatively), i.e., the
E_{0}(\rho)
is negative at low densities and eventually increases to be positive at densities larger than the saturation density
\rho_{0}
(corresponding to the vanishing of the pressure), we than have the condition that

c_{1}<0,

(3.16)

and similarly
1/4M+c_{2}/2>0
as well as
3/4M+c_{2}/2>0
, or

c_{2}>-{1}/{2M}
. The first condition namely
c_{1}<0
could also be obtained from the expression for
K_{0}(\rho)
, since there would be a positive value for the
K_{0}
at the saturation density
\rho_{0}
.
Moreover, a
\sqrt{\rho}
-dependence of the
K_{0}(\rho)
is also predicted under the above assumptions.
Furthermore, if one assumes that the potential
U_{0}
is not always negative as density
\rho
increases, the second-order coefficient
c_{2}
should be greater than zero, i.e.,

c_{2}>0
.
Under this assumption, we can define three densities: The saturation density
\rho_{0}
is defined as the point where the pressure vanishes, i.e.,
P_{0}(\rho_{0})=0
, the density
\rho_{\mathrm{v}}
corresponding to the minimum of the potential
U_{0}
is defined via
\mathrm{d}U_{0}/\mathrm{d}\rho|_{\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{v}}}=0
, and finally the crossing density
\rho_{+}
above which the single-nucleon potential
U_{0}
turns positive,
U_{0}(\rho_{+})=0
. We have the expressions for these three densities as,

\sqrt{\pi\rho_{0}}=-\frac{c_{1}}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{3/4M+c_{2}/2},~~\sqrt{\pi\rho_{\mathrm{v}}}=-\frac{c_{1}}{2c_{2}},~~\sqrt{\pi\rho_{+}}=-\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}.

(3.17)

Consequently, we obtain,

\rho_{+}/\rho_{\mathrm{v}}=4,~~\rho_{+}/\rho_{0}=\frac{9}{4}\left(1+\frac{3}{2Mc_{2}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{9}{4}.

(3.18)

These relations show that at low densities, the potential
U_{0}
is attractive, as the
\rho
increases the potential eventually decrease to reach its minimum at
\rho_{\mathrm{v}}
which could either be larger than
\rho_{0}
or smaller than
\rho_{0}
depending on the coefficient
c_{2}
since
\rho_{\mathrm{v}}/\rho_{0}=(9/16)\cdot(1+3/2Mc_{2})^{2}
, as the density increases even further above
\rho_{\mathrm{v}}
, the potential starts to increases and finally becomes repulsive when crossing
\rho_{+}
.
See the left panel of Fig.
1
for a sketch of the density dependence of
U_{0}
.
On the other hand, if one assumes that
-1/2M<c_{2}<0
, the potential
U_{0}
always decreases as
\rho
increases, i.e., the attractive interaction the nucleons feel becomes deeper and deeper, see the right panel of Fig.
1
.
The density behavior of the skewness
J_{0}
could be analyzed in a similar manner. Further justifications on the
U_{0}
as well as possible constraints on the coefficient
c_{i}
need a detailed model of the nucleon potential.
For example, when the third-order term
c_{3}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}
is included in the potential
U_{0}
, the qualitative analysis should be correspondingly modified.

Fig. 1
:
Sketch of the single-nucleon potential
U_{0}(\rho)
as a function of
\rho
in 2D.

We can also analyze the strength of the symmetry energy and the fourth-order symmetry energy, starting from the formulas (
3.2
) and (
3.3
). Here we assume that the single-nucleon potential has a sizable first-order symmetry potential
U_{\mathrm{sym}}
but all the terms beyond the linear order are small, i.e.,
U_{\mathrm{sym,2}}\approx U_{\mathrm{sym,3}}\approx\cdots\approx 0
. In addition, the momentum-dependence of the isospin-dependent part of the potential is also assumed to be weak, then we can approximate (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) by,

E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)\approx\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{4M}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{4}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}},~~E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)\approx\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{64}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}.

(3.19)

In 3D, the scalar nucleon effective mass is about
M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}(\rho_{0})\gtrsim 0.8M

[
101
]
and the symmetry potential (decreasing with increasing nucleon kinetic energy) has a maximum value of about
U_{\mathrm{sym}}\approx 36.7\,\mathrm{MeV}
at zero nucleon kinetic energy
[
92
,
93
]
, one can estimate the ratio between
E_{\mathrm{sym},4}(\rho)
and
E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
if these two constraints holds approximately in 2D (since
k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/4M\approx 18.4\,\mathrm{MeV}\approx 2^{-1}U_{\mathrm{sym}}
and
M/M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}\approx 5/4
),

\frac{E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho_{0})}{E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})}\approx\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{64}\left(\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}}-\frac{1}{M}\right)\right/\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{4M}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{32}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ast}}-1\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{128},

(3.20)

indicating the parabolic approximation for the EOS of ANM including the potential part may still be reasonable (or even better) in 2D. Of course, as we will discuss in the next section, the quantities in 3D can be perturbed to give estimates of their correspondences in 2D. Indeed, we find qualitatively similar results.

Similarly, for the 1D situation, the symmetry energy and the fourth-order symmetry energy are given via (
2.36
) and (
2.38
) as,

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2M}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}},

(3.21)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}{24}\frac{\partial^{3}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{3}}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\left.\frac{k_{\mathrm{F}}}{4}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym,2}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=k_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{4}U_{\mathrm{sym,3}},

(3.22)

where
k_{\mathrm{F}}=\pi\rho/4
.
Under the similar assumption that higher order symmetry potentials as well as their momentum dependence are weak, now the fourth-order symmetry energy could even be approximated as zero, i.e., the parabolic approximation for the EOS of ANM in 1D behaves even better.

Finally, in the large-
d
limit, due to the pre-factors like
1/24d,1/8d^{2}
and
1/24d^{3}
, etc., as shown from (
2.38
), the fourth-order symmetry energy is still expected to be small, and if the term
2^{-1}U_{\mathrm{sym}}
in the symmetry energy is assumed to be larger than the corresponding term
4^{-1}U_{\mathrm{sym,3}}
in the fourth-order symmetry energy
[
92
]
, i.e., comparing the first-order term with the third-order term of the expansion
U_{J}
over
\delta
, the ratio
{E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho_{0})}/{E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})}
stays small, indicating that the parabolic approximation holds again in the large-
d
limit. In the infinite-
d
limit, we have then
{E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})}/{E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho_{0})}=U_{\mathrm{sym,3}}/2U_{\mathrm{sym}}\ll 1
.
Writing out the EOS of ANM in a general dimension
d
thus gives us extra insight into the strength of different expansion terms, and from this viewpoint, the goodness of the conventional parabolic approximation in 3D has no magic origin
[
100
]
.

4. The
\epsilon
-expansion of the EOS in
d\mathrm{D}
space based on the EOS with
d_{\mathrm{f}}=3

We have in this work encountered up to now two types of perturbative calculations, one is based on the isospin asymmetry
\delta
and the other is based on the expansion around some reference density
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}
through the quantity
\theta_{\mathrm{f}}
(see (
2.21
)).
These two expansions both give us useful/informative information on the EOS of ANM, e.g., as we discussed in the last section, the
\delta^{2}
-expansion generally leads to the parabolic approximation, i.e., the fourth-order symmetry energy and beyond are smaller than the quadratic symmetry energy.
In addition, via the HVH theorem, a novel expansion related to the
\delta
was developed, leading to the useful expressions for the symmetry energy as well as its slope parameter in terms of the single-nucleon potentials, with the latter being extractable from experiments
[
92
,
93
]
.
If one treats the dimension
d
as a continuous variable, one can also develop corresponding perturbation theories based on certain (fixed) dimension
d_{\mathrm{f}}
by introducing the (apparently) small quantity
\epsilon=d-d_{\mathrm{f}}
. This approach is often called the
\epsilon
-expansion theory. Some problems are well defined and easier to analyze in certain dimensions, while they are difficult or even impossible to study in other dimensions.
The
\epsilon
-expansion was invented in the 1970’s to deal with the second-order phase transition problems and related critical phenomena with impressive successes
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
. A recent successful example of the
\epsilon
-expansion is in studying the EOS of a unitary Fermi gas. The latter could simply be written as
\xi\cdot(3E_{\mathrm{F}}/5)
where
E_{\mathrm{F}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/2M
is the Fermi energy with
M
being the mass of the atoms.
Here the universal dimensionless quantity
\xi
characterizes the interacting nature of the unitary gas, and it is often called the Bertsch’s parameter
[
103
,
104
]
.
Due to the infinite scattering length
a_{\mathrm{s}}
near the unitary limit, there are currently no self-consistent theories on the BEC-BCS crossover physics
[
35
,
49
,
105
]
and thus lacks direct theories on the Bertsch’s parameter.
The effective theories based on the
\epsilon
-expansion to order
\epsilon^{5/2}\ln\epsilon

[
50
]
and later to higher orders in
\epsilon
successfully predicted the value of
\xi\approx 0.367

[
106
]
, which was found to be very close to the experimental result of about 0.376
[
107
]
.

In this section, we expand the EOS of ANM near the conventional 3D situation by introducing the perturbative dimension
\epsilon=d-3
and investigate the general features when a nonzero
\epsilon
is applied.
Then we shall show that the
\epsilon
-expansion is effective (e.g., to 2D) at least for the kinetic EOS of ANM by comparing the perturbed kinetic EOS with the exact one given in section
3
.
In the next section, we shall apply the
\epsilon
-expansion method to calculate the EOS of ANM adopting a toy-model nucleon potential and explore how the EOS in
d
D space modifies as the
d
varies from 3 to other values.
In section
6
, we adopt the
\epsilon
-expansion to calculate the kinetic EOS of ANM when the SRC-induced HMT in the single-nucleon momentum distribution is considered. In section
7
, we then perform the expansion to order
\epsilon^{2}
for the symmetry energy, and investigate the corresponding features from the high order corrections.
The discussions of this section and the relevant parts of section
5
and section
6
on the
\epsilon
-expansion are mostly intuitive and probably preliminary. Our main motivation here is to demonstrate the interesting features of the
\epsilon
-expansion method and to stimulate further studies on this topic.

In order to give the EOS of ANM in dimension
d=d_{\mathrm{f}}+\epsilon=3+\epsilon
with
\epsilon
being the perturbative dimension, we need to expand the relevant quantities assuming
\epsilon
is small.
The expansion of the function
a(d)
(defined in (
2.23
)) around
\epsilon=0
is

\displaystyle a(d)=

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,|\mathbf{k}|)+2\epsilon\sigma\left(\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{6}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}}{2}\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{sym}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}-\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}}{6}\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right)_{|\mathbf{k}|=\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}}.

E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{6M_{\mathrm{eff}}}\left.+\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}}{6}\frac{\partial U_{0}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{1}{2}U^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}})=\frac{\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{6M_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{eff}}^{\ast}}+\frac{1}{2}U^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho,\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}),

is the scalar Landau effective mass based on

Interestingly, (

For the 2D situation (
\epsilon=-1

and similarly

The expressions from (

We have discussed the non-trivial contributions caused by the momentum-dependence of the nucleon potential to the EOS of SNM and to the symmetry energy.
On the other hand, if one artificially assumes that the

\displaystyle{E}_{0}(\rho)=

(4.29)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)=

(4.31)

(4.32)

(4.35)

here the partial and total differentials involved are the same, i.e,

It is obvious now that all the

Under such conditions, the linear-

On the other hand, from the expression for the slope parameter

Moreover, we can investigate the features of the
\epsilon
-expansion by making use of the exact expressions for the (kinetic part of the) relevant quantities in the general dimension
d

In doing so, a natural concern is how to determine the nucleon Fermi momentum in
d
D.
Two schemes are possible:

(a)

The Fermi momentum takes the same value irrespective of the dimension, e.g.,
k_{\mathrm{F}}
is always about 200-300 MeV, either in 3D or
d
D.
In this sense, we treat the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
as a fixed quantity, and then one does not need to do perturbative calculations on
k_{\mathrm{F}}

Consequently the factor
\sigma

(b)

On the other hand, we can treat the density

\sigma

Compared with the total EOS, the tendency of the kinetic part when
d

Next, we shall extrapolate the kinetic EOS of SNM and the kinetic symmetry energy to other dimensions and compare them with the corresponding exact results.
For
\epsilon=-1

\displaystyle E_{0}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)\approx

\displaystyle\frac{3\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{10M}\left(1-2\sigma-\frac{2}{15}\right)=\frac{3\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{10M}\left(\frac{13}{15}-2\sigma\right).

26/25\approx 1.04
(based on the assumption that

For the kinetic symmetry energy, we now have

Then for the 2D situation, this gives

If we adopt the assumption that the Fermi momenta in different dimensions are not equal but related via the corresponding relation between the density and the Fermi momentum, then the coefficient
\sigma
should be kept.
For example, the density in dimensions 2 could be estimated via the conventional density
\rho

In addition, for the 4D with
\epsilon=1

\pi^{1/3}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{5/2}\left(\frac{4}{3}-2\sigma\right)\approx 1.09,

(4.39)

As a reference, we also list the relevant results for the 4D and 1D cases: for the 4D situation,
\overline{k}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/6M\cdot(2/3+2\sigma)\approx 14.06\,\mathrm{MeV}

Although the

We make some initial attempts in these directions in this work as we shall discuss later, and in the remaining of the present work, we adopt the scheme (b) above related to the issue of the perturbative treatment on the density.

5.2. Perturbing the EOS of spatial dimension

We now study the EOS of ANM in spaces with dimensions near the reference dimension

based on the
\epsilon
-expansion formalism with the 3D toy model EOS developed in the last subsection.
The EOS of SNM in dimension
d+\epsilon
with
\epsilon
being the perturbative dimension is given by the formula (
4.15
), where the term originated from the momentum-dependence of the single-nucleon potential
U_{0}
(see (
5.2
)) could be written out as,

\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}\left(\left.\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=\overline{k}^{f}_{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot\overline{k}^{f}_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\mathrm{d}f=-\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\frac{3\mu\nu}{\rho_{0}}\int_{0}^{\rho}\mathrm{d}f\frac{f^{2}}{(1+\nu f)^{2}},

(5.34)

where
\nu=3\pi^{2}\theta/2\Lambda^{3}
. The integration in the above equation can be carried out analytically, leading to

\int_{0}^{\rho}\mathrm{d}f\frac{f^{2}}{(1+\nu f)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\nu^{3}}\left[\frac{x(x+2)}{x+1}-\ln(x+1)\right],

(5.35)

where
x=\theta({k_{\mathrm{F}}}/{\Lambda})^{3}
as introduced earlier is a function of density through
k_{\mathrm{F}}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}\left(\left.\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial|\mathbf{k}|}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=\overline{k}^{f}_{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot\overline{k}^{f}_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\mathrm{d}f=

\displaystyle-3\sigma\mu\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left[\frac{x^{2}+2x}{x+1}-2\ln(x+1)\right]

(5.36)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-3\sigma\mu\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{3}x-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{3}{5}x^{3}\right),~~\mbox{for small $x$},

(5.37)

where the second line (approximation) holds for small values of
x
(corresponding to low densities). In fact the limit
x\approx 1
is equivalent to a density about
\rho\approx 2.85\,\mathrm{fm}^{-3}\approx 18\rho_{0}
using
\theta=1.3
and
\Lambda=750\,\mathrm{MeV}
(see Tab.
1
). Moreover, the saturation density
\rho_{0}
corresponds to

about

.
Thus, the above small-

\rho_{0}

This is consistent with the general analysis given in the paragraph after formula (

(e.g., see the right panel of Fig.

In particular, if the

This phenomenon actually can be traced back to a deep principle behind the high-dimensional geometry: According to the relation between the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
and the density
\rho

\rho=k_{\mathrm{F}}^{d}/[{2^{d-2}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)}]

is
(2\pi)^{d}\rho/4
. For a fixed Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
, the volume of the sphere approaches zero as
d\to\infty

[

\rho

k_{\mathrm{F}}
should approach infinity as

dE_{\mathrm{F}}/(d+2)\sim E_{\mathrm{F}}=k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}/2M

\displaystyle{C}_{J}\left({k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}}/{|\mathbf{k}|}\right)^{4},&k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}<|\mathbf{k}|<\phi_{J}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{J}.\end{array}\right.

(6.1)

The three parameters

6.3. Momentum fluctuations of nucleons in symmetric nuclear matter in
d
D spaces

Once the nucleon momentum distribution is specified, one can investigate its moments. In particular, the variance of nucleon momentum
k
namely
\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle^{2}
characterizing the strength of nucleon momentum fluctuations is very informative. We define the (relative) nucleon momentum fluctuation
\Upsilon_{k}
in SNM in
d
D spaces as,

\Upsilon_{k}(d)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle^{2}}{\langle k\rangle^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle}{\langle k\rangle^{2}}-1},

(6.22)

with the moment
\langle k^{\gamma}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{k}^{\gamma}\rangle
being defined with respect to the momentum distribution
n_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}
, i.e.,

\langle\mathbf{k}^{\gamma}\rangle=\left.\int n_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}\mathbf{k}^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}\right/\int n_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}.

(6.23)

For our purposes here,
\gamma=1
and
\gamma=2
are relevant,

\displaystyle\left.\langle k\rangle\right/k_{\mathrm{F}}=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d+1}\left[1+C_{0}\left(\frac{4}{(d-3)(d-4)}+\frac{d+1}{d-3}\phi_{0}^{d-3}-\frac{d}{d-4}\phi_{0}^{d-4}\right)\right],

(6.24)

\displaystyle\left.\langle k^{2}\rangle\right/k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d+2}\left[1+C_{0}\left(\frac{8}{(d-2)(d-4)}+\frac{d+2}{d-2}\phi_{0}^{d-2}-\frac{d}{d-4}\phi_{0}^{d-4}\right)\right],

(6.25)

from which the fluctuation factor
\Upsilon_{k}(d)
in
d
D could be obtained.

For the nucleons in
d=1,2,3,4
with HMT, one has,

\displaystyle\mathrm{1D}:~~\left.\langle k\rangle\right/k_{\mathrm{F}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[1+C_{0}\left(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3\phi_{0}^{3}}-\frac{1}{\phi_{0}^{2}}\right)\right],~~\left.\langle k^{2}\rangle\right/k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\left[1+C_{0}\left(\frac{8}{3}+\frac{1}{3\phi_{0}^{3}}-\frac{3}{\phi_{0}}\right)\right],

(6.26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{2D}:~~\left.\langle k\rangle\right/k_{\mathrm{F}}=

8. Summary and discussions of some unresolved issues for future studies

Freeing up the spatial dimension
d
for neutron-rich matter, we derived the general expression of its EOS in terms of the nucleon isoscalar and isovector potentials based on the generalized HVH theorem. We find that the SNM EOS is more bounded at a higher 3D-equivalent saturation density while the symmetry energy becomes smaller in spaces with lower dimensions compared to the conventional 3D case. While we speculated that there are objects and/or sub-systems of nucleons that may be considered as living in spaces with reduced dimensions, implications of our findings for nuclear physics and astrophysics remain to be studied. In the following we summarize our main results and point out a few unresolved issues deserving further studies.

(a)

Analytical expressions of the nucleon specific energy
E_{0}(\rho)
, pressure
P_{0}(\rho)
, incompressibility coefficient
K_{0}(\rho)
and skewness coefficient
J_{0}(\rho)
of SNM are derived in a general
d
D space. The corresponding expressions for the quadratic symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
, its slope parameter
L(\rho)
and curvature coefficient
K_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
as well as the fourth-order symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
are also given.

(b)

The general features of the EOS of ANM in 2D are analyzed in some details. In particular, we found that the EOS of ANM (including the symmetry energy and the EOS of SNM) could be obtained from an effective expansion based on
\sqrt{\rho}
. Moreover, there are no kinetic terms appearing in the general expressions for
K_{0}(\rho),J_{0}(\rho)
and
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
. Based on this finding, we have shown that the quartic term
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
is generally far smaller than the quadratic symmetry energy
E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho)
in the sense that
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho_{0})/E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})\lesssim 1\%
, validating the conventional parabolic approximation for the EOS of ANM in 2D.
Furthermore, the fourth-order symmetry energy in other dimensions (from 1D to higher dimensions) is also shown to be small due to the specific structure of
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}(\rho)
where the dimension
d
plays an essential role (see the expression (
2.38
)). Thus, from the viewpoint in a
d
D space in which the nucleons live, the effectiveness of the parabolic approximation of its EOS is consequently natural and the conventional 3D case is not unique.

(c)

The EOSs in
d
D spaces are derived from the
\epsilon
-expansion (using an assumed small perturbative dimension
\epsilon=d-d_{\mathrm{f}}
with
d_{\mathrm{f}}
being a reference dimension) based on the EOS in 3D.
In particular, we investigated the kinetic EOS both for the FFG model (section
4
with
d_{\mathrm{f}}=3
to linear order of
\epsilon
) and for the HMT model (section
6
with general
d_{\mathrm{f}}
). We found that starting from the conventional 3D kinetic EOS, one can reasonably approximate the corresponding kinetic EOS of SNM and the kinetic symmetry energy in 2D, see (
4.38
) and (
4.39
). In addition, the
\epsilon
-expansion based on
d_{\mathrm{f}}\approx 1\sim 3
even with the SRC-induced HMT included is effective, see results of Fig.
13
.
Moreover, the tendency of the EOS in a perturbed dimension is analyzed using the relevant analytical expressions.
Specifically, we found that the nucleon specific energy and pressure in SNM will be reduced (enhanced) if the perturbative dimension
\epsilon
is negative (positive) compared with the 3D case, e.g., the EOS of SNM in 2D will be correspondingly softened compared with the 3D EOS of SNM, see the formula (
4.15
). The correction to the symmetry energy from the
\epsilon^{2}
contribution does not qualitatively change the prediction from the linear approximation, as shown in section
7
.
In a nutshell, the 3D EOS of ANM in fact encapsulates the very relevant/useful information on the EOS in
d_{\mathrm{f}}
D with
d_{\mathrm{f}}
being near 3, and the
\epsilon
-expansion provides a useful technique to explore the low-
d
EOS of ANM from its 3D counterpart.

(d)

A toy model for the single-nucleon potential is constructed by enforcing the corresponding EOS of ANM to fulfill all the available empirical constraints, including the one on the pressure in SNM from studying nuclear collective flow data in relativistic heavy-ion collision
[
128
]
, the constraint on the single-nucleon potential in SNM from optical model analyses of nucleon-nucleus scattering data
[
94
]
, the scalar nucleon effective mass around the saturation density
[
101
]
, the causality constraint at densities
\lesssim 6\rho_{0}
, the isobaric analog state constraint on the symmetry energy near the saturation density
[
130
]
as well as the constraint on the EOS of PNM from chiral effective field theories
[
144
]
. The constructed model (which owns certain momentum dependence) is then applied to study the EOS of ANM in
d
D spaces. Specifically, we found that the EOS of SNM is enhanced (reduced) if
d
is upwardly (downward) perturbed with respect to the 3D case, verifying the general conclusion found in section
4
.
This means that the many-nucleon system tends to be more bounded in spaces with reduced dimensions, while it may become completely unbounded in spaces with
\gtrsim 4
. The latter phenomenon in fact reflects some deep principles from the viewpoint of the high-dimensional geometry regarding the
d
-dimensional sphere.
In addition, the symmetry energy in 2D is found to be reduced compared to its correspondence in 3D.

(e)

The kinetic EOS of ANM in the presence of SRC-induced HMT in the single-nucleon momentum distribution function in
d
D is studied. Besides the effectiveness of the
\epsilon
-expansion of the kinetic EOS of SNM, we also found that the first two moments of the nucleon momentum distribution in lower dimensions become smaller in both the FFG and HMT models, while the relative nucleon momentum fluctuation significantly increases as
d
decreases below 2, see Fig.
15
.
More interestingly, the prediction on the relative momentum fluctuation
\Upsilon_{k}
from the HMT and the FFG model becomes closer as
d
decreases, indicating that the many-nucleon system in low dimensions behaves like a free system in terms of their kinetic EOSs. The same phenomenon is also found through the calculations on the isospin-expansion of the kinetic EOS of ANM, i.e., the predictions on them from the FFG and the HMT model become very similar in low dimensions (e.g., in 1D or 2D), as shown in the left panel of Fig.
18
. The nearly free property of the kinetic EOS of ANM in low dimensions may provide a useful tool to explore the EOS in 3D from the corresponding counterpart in 2D or 1D, or vice versa.
Furthermore, the predictions on the kinetic EOS of PNM by combining the
E_{0}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)
,
E_{\mathrm{sym}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)
,
E_{\mathrm{sym,4}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)
and
E_{\mathrm{sym,6}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho)
from the HMT and the FFG models are very close for
d\lesssim 3
, verifying the conventional picture in 3D that there exist very few high-momentum neutrons in PNM.
However, as the dimension
d
increases beyond 3, the above predictions from the HMT and the FFG models tend to deviate, see the right panel of Fig.
18
, indicating that the EOS of PNM in high dimensions may behave very differently from the one in
d\lesssim 3
.

There is currently no systematic study on the EOS of ANM in a space of general dimension
d
, partially due to the experimental limitation, theoretical inability or lack of interest. While our work presented here is exploratory in nature, we found the exploration very interesting and feel confident that the results summarized above are useful. Certainly, much more work is needed to further
investigate many of the issues we have touched on in the present work. There are also many other interesting issues to be studied. To stimulate further studies along this line we list below a few possible such issues:

(a)

We studied in this work the kinetic EOS (both with and without the HMT) in some details in the general dimension
d
, the next important step is to effectively/reasonably incorporate the nucleon-nucleon interactions/potentials in a self-consistent way, either at the phenomenological or at the microscopic levels. For example, one needs to consider how to determine the model parameters in
d
D, and the basic principles and/or symmetries to be considered, etc.
Before detailed schemes (fitting schemes and/or the principles) are available, the
\epsilon
-expansion provides a practical starting point, e.g., one can use (
4.28
) to investigate how the 3D knowledge on the symmetry energy could induce relevant information in dimensions
\epsilon+d_{\mathrm{f}}
.

(b)

Considering the HMT issue, how can one obtain the EOS of ANM based on the HVH theorem in general
d
D, i.e., the close relation between the partial derivative of the energy density with respect to the density and the single energy at
k_{\mathrm{F}}
, since when the momentum distribution has depletion as well as the HMT, one does not have
\partial\varepsilon/\partial\rho=e(k_{\mathrm{F}})
, where
e
is the single-nucleon energy as the sum of the kinetic part and the potential part, and
\varepsilon
is the energy density of the system, i.e.,
e(k_{\mathrm{F}})
is generally not the chemical potential of the nucleons (which however holds for the FFG model). This is in fact a fundamental problem in (nuclear) many-body theories
[
192
]
.

(c)

As discussed in the last section, considering that the many-nucleon system in low dimensions is close to its FFG counterpart, can one rely on it (e.g., via the higher-order
\epsilon
-expansions) to extract useful information for the corresponding EOS in 3D?
A related issue is on the correlation between the symmetry energy and its slope parameter. It is known that the latter (at
\rho_{0}
) in 3D is constrained to be about
L\approx fS
with
f
being about 2-3
[
122
]
and
S\equiv E_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0})
, which is in fact quite close to its FFG prediction
L=3S
.
If the low-dimensional system is close to the free model, can one establish the corresponding correlation (even with the effective potential included) to be near
L\approx 3S
in 2D or
L\approx 6S
in 1D, or more generally
L\approx 6S/d
?

(d)

In this work, we study the EOS of ANM in a space of general dimension
d
totally adopting the non-relativistic calculations. Since the relativistic kinematics, the nucleon-nucleon interactions, and/or the dimensionality together may effectively affect the EOS of ANM
[
100

, it would be extremely interesting to combine the relativistic kinematics and the fruitful nucleon-nucleon interactions (we have already found that the momentum dependence of the single-nucleon potential could effectively influence certain problems) in the general dimension

(e)

The role played by the momentum-dependences of the single-nucleon potentials especially that of the symmetry potential

(f)

The last but not least, it is really important to find applications where the low-dimensional EOS of ANM could be used. As we have speculated in the
