Characterizing Oscillations in Heterogeneous Populations of Coordinators and Anticoordinators
Pouria Ramazi and Mohammad Hossein Roohi

Abstract
Oscillations often take place in populations of decision makers that are either a coordinator , who takes action only if enough others do so, or an anticoordinator , who takes action only if few others do so. Populations consisting of exclusively one of these types are known to reach an equilibrium, where every individual is satisfied with her decision. Yet it remains unknown whether oscillations take place in a population consisting of both types, and if they do, what features they share. We study a well-mixed population of individuals, which are either a coordinator or anticoordinator, each associated with a possibly unique threshold and initialized with the strategy 𝙰 \mathtt{A} or 𝙱 \mathtt{B} . At each time, an agent becomes active to update her strategy based on her threshold: an active coordinator (resp. anticoordinator) updates her strategy to 𝙰 \mathtt{A} (resp. 𝙱 \mathtt{B} ) if the portion of other agents who have chosen 𝙰 \mathtt{A} exceeds (falls short of) her threshold, and updates to 𝙱 \mathtt{B} (resp. 𝙰 \mathtt{A} ) otherwise. We define the state of the population dynamics as the distribution over the thresholds of those who have chosen 𝙰 \mathtt{A} . We show that the population can admit several minimally positively invariant sets, where the solution trajectory oscillates. We explicitly characterize a class of positively invariant sets, prove their invariance, and provide a necessary and sufficient condition for their stability. Our results highlight the possibility of non-trivial, complex oscillations in the absence of noise and population structure and shed light on the reported oscillations in nature and human societies.

Introduction

Oscillatory phenomena are observed in nature and human societies:
periodic patterns in biology

,

,

In most such situations, individuals have to choose between one out of two actions and either pick an action only if enough others do so, which are referred to as
coordinating individuals
or simply
coordinators
, or pick an action only if not many others do so, which are referred to as
anticoordinating individuals
or simply

.
Followers in technology markets avoid risk by producing common products, whereas innovators perceive their benefits in building up a monopoly by developing new or rare products

“Activator” and “repressor” cells in synthetic microbial consortia, respectively increase and decrease gene expression if the transcription is low and high, resulting in a positive and negative feedback loop

.
Populations consisting exclusively of one of these two types of individuals are known to eventually reach an equilibrium state where individuals are satisfied with their decisions
[
8
]
.
Therefore, it is only the coexistence of the two that may explain the observed oscillations.
However, not every coexistence of the two leads to an oscillation, and even if it does, the characteristics of the oscillation is unknown.

Scholars from various disciplines have studied populations of decision-making individuals

Evolutionary game theory

\mathtt{A}

(myopic) best response update rule

The update rule dictates individuals to pick the strategy maximizing their payoffs against their opponents.
Therefore, if an individual’s payoff matrix is in the form of a
coordination game
, where playing the same strategy as that of the opponent results in a higher payoff, she will be a coordinator, and if it is in the form of an
anticoordination game
, where playing the strategy opposite to that of the opponent results in a higher payoff, she will be an anticoordinator.
In a different framework, coordinators (resp. anticoordinators) can be captured by
linear threshold models
where every individual has a threshold and chooses
\mathtt{A}
only if the number of others who have chosen
\mathtt{A}
exceeds (resp. falls short of) her threshold
[
24
]
.
In either of these models, a realistic population is
heterogeneous
in individuals’ thresholds or payoff matrices, rather than
homogeneous
.
In
[
25

26

, networks of all coordinators or all anticoordinators who update their strategies synchronously, that is all at a time, are shown to reach a limit cycle of length at most two.
In
[
8

, any network of all coordinators or all anticoordinators who update their strategies asynchronously, that is one at a time, is guaranteed to equilibrate.
In
[
27
,
28
]
, sufficient conditions for existence of and finite-time convergence to equilibrium in heterogeneous mixed populations of coordinators and anticoordinators are shown.
Although structured populations are often more popular as they model the heterogeneity in neighbors, many real-world populations are well-mixed, where individuals know the total number or ratio of others who have chosen a particular strategy.
In technology markets, for example, firms typically know how many others are producing a particular product, and individuals deciding whether to volunteer for a non-gevernmental organization (NGO) may have access to the total number of existing volunteers.
Note that the exact number/ratio is not required, and just knowing whether it is greater than their thresholds suffices for them to accordingly update their choices.
Well-mixed heterogeneous populations of anticoordinators updating asynchronously are known to equilibrate regardless of the initial condition
[
29
]

The population state either equilibrates or fluctuates between two states in the long-run.
However, the fluctuations are simply because each individual updates based on the total number of

-players other than herself.
.

However, to best of our knowledge, there is no in-depth study on the non-converging and fluctuating behavior of heterogeneous mixed-populations of both coordinators and anticoordinators, despite their inevitable coexistence in many real-world situations

This missing piece in the literature is perhaps key in understanding many oscillatory phenomena.

We study a well-mixed heterogeneous population comprising both coordinators and anticoordinators who play either of the strategies
\mathtt{A}
or
\mathtt{B}
and update asynchronously over time.
We take the distribution of
\mathtt{A}

First, via a numerical example, we show that a single population can possess an equilibrium as well as several non-singleton positively invariant sets where the state undergoes never-ending fluctuations.
Second, for the first time, we explicitly identify a group of positively invariant sets that are either a singleton, resulting in an equilibrium, or non-singleton, where the solution trajectory fluctuates between its states.
Third, we study the notion of stability for the invariant sets and derive a necessary and sufficient stability condition.
Fourth, investigate the synchronous version of the dynamics.
The results shed light on observed oscillations in populations of binary decision-makers, and serve as a stepping stone towards providing incentives or establishing regulatory polices in technology market, social and cultural learning, and health plans

II
Model

Consider a well-mixed population of
n\geq 2
agents who play either of the two strategies
\mathtt{A}
or
\mathtt{B}
, correspondingly earn payoffs, and over a discrete time sequence
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
revise their strategies to maximize their payoffs.
More specifically, the four possible payoffs for an agent
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
against her opponent are summarized by the payoff matrix

\bordermatrix{&\mathtt{A}&\mathtt{B}\cr\mathtt{A}&a_{j}&b_{j}\cr\mathtt{B}&c_{j}&d_{j}},\qquad a_{j},b_{j},c_{j},d_{j}\in\mathbb{R},

where, for example,
b_{j}
is the payoff when agent
j
plays
\mathtt{A}
, and her opponent plays
\mathtt{B}
.
Given agent
j
, let
A_{j}
(resp.
B_{j}
) denote the total number of
\mathtt{A}
-playing (resp.
\mathtt{B}
-playing) agents in the remaining of the population, i.e., excluding agent
j
.
Then the accumulated payoff to agent
j
against the remaining of the population is
a_{j}A_{j}+b_{j}B_{j}
when she plays
\mathtt{A}
and
c_{j}A_{j}+d_{j}B_{j}
otherwise.
At each time step
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, an agent
j
becomes active to update her strategy at time
t+1
based on the
(myopic) best-response update rule
, dictating that she chooses the strategy that maximizes her payoff against the remaining population.
Denote the strategy of agent
j
by
s_{j}\in\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}
.
Then her strategy at time
t+1
would be

s_{j}(t+1)=\begin{cases}\mathtt{A}&a_{j}A_{j}(t)+b_{j}B_{j}(t)\geq c_{j}A_{j}(t)+d_{j}B_{j}(t)\\
\mathtt{B}&a_{j}A_{j}(t)+b_{j}B_{j}(t)<c_{j}A_{j}(t)+d_{j}B_{j}(t)\\
\end{cases}

Knowing
B_{j}=n-A_{j}-1
, we simplify the expressions as

s_{j}(t+1)=\begin{cases}\mathtt{A}&\sigma_{j}A_{j}(t)\geq\gamma_{j}(n-1)\\
\mathtt{B}&\sigma_{j}A_{j}(t)<\gamma_{j}(n-1)\\
\end{cases},

(1)

where
\sigma_{j}\overset{\Delta}{=}a_{j}-c_{j}+d_{j}-b_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}\overset{\Delta}{=}d_{j}-b_{j}
.
For
\sigma_{j}\neq 0
, define the
temper
of agent
j
as
\tau^{j}\overset{\Delta}{=}\gamma_{j}/\sigma_{j}(n-1)
.
Note that this is different from the
threshold
of agent
j
, defined as
\gamma_{j}/\sigma_{j}
, which is often used in the literature
[
34
]
.
Now if
\sigma_{j}>0
, the update rule becomes

s_{j}(t+1)=\begin{cases}\mathtt{A}&A_{j}(t)\geq\tau^{j}\\
\mathtt{B}&A_{j}(t)<\tau^{j}\\
\end{cases}.

(2)

Namely, the agent plays
\mathtt{A}
only if the number of other
\mathtt{A}
-players does not fall short of her temper.
We refer to these agents as
coordinators
.
If
\sigma_{j}<0
, the update rule becomes

s_{j}(t+1)=\begin{cases}\mathtt{A}&A_{j}(t)\leq\tau^{j}\\
\mathtt{B}&A_{j}(t)>\tau^{j}\\
\end{cases}.

(3)

Namely, the agent plays
\mathtt{A}
only if the number of other
\mathtt{A}
-players does not exceed her temper.
We refer to these agents as
anticoordinators
.
For the case when
\tau^{j}\not\in[0,n-1]
or
\sigma_{j}=0
, update rule (
1
) is equivalent to one of the above two cases.
So we assume that every agent in the population has some temper
\tau^{j}\in[0,n-1]
and is either a coordinator or an anticoordinator.
Note that our setup is similar to that in
[
8
]
.

The agents may represent people aiming at establishing an NGO for a social service program.
The individuals know how many others have volunteered (played
\mathtt{A}
) and accordingly decide to also volunteer (play
\mathtt{A}
) or not (play
\mathtt{B}
).
Some may consider the NGO a failure or burdensome if the volunteers are few, and hence, would only join if enough others have joined (coordinators).
Others may find it a duty to join even if no one else does, but they also find it unnecessary for too many to join.
Hence, they would join only if less than enough others have joined (anticoordinators).
Another example is the co-existence of
innovators
and

followers
in the technology market
[
5
]
.
Companies can either take an existing market from an entrenched technology (play
\mathtt{A}
) or take on competitors with any form of innovation (play
\mathtt{B}
).
Some companies are conservative in the sense that enough number of other sectors focusing on a technology convinces them to follow (coordinators).
Others tend to build up a monopoly, so they change their strategy to innovate upon the presence of rivals developing the same technology (anticoordinators).

We categorize all anticoordinators (resp. coordinators) with the same temper as the same
type
, and assume that there are all together
b\geq 1
(resp.
b^{\prime}\geq 1
) different types of anticoordinators (resp. coordinators).
We label the anticoordinator (resp. coordinator) types in the descending (resp. ascending) order of their tempers by
1,2,\ldots,b
(resp.
1,2,\ldots,b^{\prime}
).
So we have a total of
b+b^{\prime}
types of agents.
Denote the temper of an anticoordinator (resp. coordinator) of type
i
by
\tau_{i}
(resp.
\tau^{\prime}_{i})
.
Then we have

\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}>\ldots>\tau_{b},\qquad\tau^{\prime}_{b^{\prime}}>\tau^{\prime}_{b^{\prime}-1}>\ldots>\tau^{\prime}_{1}.

(4)

We take the distribution of the
\mathtt{A}
-players among these types as the
state
of the system:

\bm{x}(t)\overset{\Delta}{=}\left(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{b}(t),x^{\prime}_{b^{\prime}}(t),\ldots,x^{\prime}_{1}(t)\right),

where
x_{i}
(resp.
x^{\prime}_{i}
) denotes the number of
\mathtt{A}
-playing anticoordinators (resp. coordinators) of type
i
.
Clearly,
\bm{x}(t)
lies in the state space

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\overset{\Delta}{=}\Big\{(

\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots,x_{b},x^{\prime}_{b^{\prime}},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{b+b^{\prime}}_{\geq 0}\,\Big|\,

\displaystyle x_{i}\leq n_{i}\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,b\},x^{\prime}_{i}\leq n^{\prime}_{i}\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,b^{\prime}\}\Big\},

where
n_{i}\geq 1
(resp.
n^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1
) is the number of anticoordinators (resp. coordinators) of type
i
.
Let
A(\bm{x})
denote the total number of
\mathtt{A}
-players in the population at state
\bm{x}
.
One can write update rules (
2
) and (
3
) with respect to
A(\bm{x}(t))
and the tempers of the types according to Lemma
20
in the appendix.

The update rules (
2
) and (
3
) together with the activation sequence of the agents govern the dynamics of
\bm{x}(t)
, called the
population dynamics
.
We do not impose any assumption on the activation sequence, although a realistic sequence might be generated by a random process.
A
positively invariant set
under the population dynamics is a set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that if
\bm{x}(0)\in\mathcal{O}
, then
\bm{x}(t)\in\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and under any activation sequence.
Namely, the solution trajectory never leaves the set after entering it.
Our goal is to identify these sets.
If
\mathcal{O}
is a singleton, then it consists of a single equilibrium state, where the solution trajectory settles.
However, if
\mathcal{O}
is a non-singleton and does not include an equilibrium, then the solution trajectory perpetually fluctuates between several states in
\mathcal{O}
, as shown in the following section.

IV-A

Left-to-right and right-to-left activation sequences

The key property of coordinators is their conjoint switching to the same strategy: if a coordinator tends to switch to
\mathtt{A}
(resp.
\mathtt{B}
), so do all other coordinators with a lower (resp. higher) temper.
Consequently, starting from any initial condition, if all coordinators become active in the ascending order of their tempers, we will reach a state with a benchmark temper such that all coordinators with lower tempers play
\mathtt{A}
and all coordinators with higher tempers play
\mathtt{B}
.
Formally, let
\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be the function that maps the state
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}
to the resulting state after we consecutively update first all type-
1
, next all type-
2
,
\ldots
, and finally all type-
b^{\prime}
coordinators.
That is,
\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}(\bm{y})=\bm{x}(t=\sum_{k=1}^{b^{\prime}}n^{\prime}_{k})
when we start from
\bm{x}(0)=\bm{y}
and follow the above activation sequence, which we refer to as the
coordinating right-to-left activation (sequence)
.
The state
\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}(\bm{y})
can be easily shown to take the structure

\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}(\bm{y})=(\!\!\underbrace{*,\ldots,*}_{\text{anticoordinating}}\!\!,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0,n^{\prime}_{i},n^{\prime}_{i-1}\ldots,n^{\prime}_{1}}_{\text{coordinating}}),

where
i=\overleftarrow{c}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(\bm{y})=\overleftarrow{c}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}(\bm{y})-1
.
A similar behavior is seen when the coordinators become active in the descending order of their tempers.
Correspondingly, we define the function
\overrightarrow{\bm{c}}
similar to
\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}
but when first all type-
b^{\prime}
, next all type-
(b^{\prime}-1)
,
\ldots
, and finally all type-
1
coordinators become active, which we refer to as the
coordinating left-to-right activation

The anticoordinators, however, do not exhibit such a conjoint switching to the same strategy.
Let
\overleftarrow{\bm{a}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}

\mathtt{A}

\mathtt{A}
-players after we consecutively update all type-
b
, type-
(b-1)
, …, type-
i

\bm{y}

The following result is straightforward.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}

,

Lemma 2

Let

IV-B

Identifying positively invariant sets

We define the indices
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}
and provide the intuition behind the definitions, but note that the indices are rigorously defined and do not depend on what we claim to be intuitively true. As mentioned in Section

, the indices
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}
are related recursively.
This hinders any of the indices to be defined independently from the others.
The difficulty is due to the fact that index
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
, for example, is the
greatest

\mathtt{A}
-fixed anticoordinating type.
If, instead, we search over every arbitrary (yet possibly close to the greatest)
\mathtt{A}
-fixed anticoordinating type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}

More specifically, given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}\in\{0,\ldots,b\}
and
{\delta}\in\{0,\ldots,b^{\prime}\}
, define the state

\bm{y}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}\overset{\Delta}{=}(\underbrace{n_{1},\ldots,n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}},0,\ldots,0}_{\text{anticoordinating}},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0,n^{\prime}_{{\delta}},\ldots,n^{\prime}_{1}}_{\text{coordinating}}).

We approximate
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}
by
\delta
and later tighten it up.
Representing
\mathtt{A}
-fixed agents, all type-
1

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}

\mathtt{A}

\bm{y}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}

Now, although restrictive, we also force all type-
1

\mathtt{A}

1

\mathtt{A}

\displaystyle\Psi\overset{\Delta}{=}\Big\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta})\,|\,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}\in\{0,\ldots,b\},{\delta}\in\Delta({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}),

\displaystyle{\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}+1}}<A(\overleftarrow{\bm{c}}(\overleftarrow{\bm{a}}(\bm{y}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}})))\leq{\tau_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}}}+1\Big\}.

IV-C

Proving invariance

The following is the main result of the paper.
Recall that
\Psi
is defined in (
5
), based on which
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}
are defined in (
6
) to (
8
).
Correspondingly, the set
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}}
, and hence,
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
is defined in (

).

Theorem 1
.

Given
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta})\in\Psi
,
the set
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
is positively invariant.

In what follows, we provide the necessary lemmas to prove this theorem.
For simplicity, we drop the superscripts
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
from the states, e.g., we use
\bm{y}
instead of
\bm{y}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
.
The main part of the proof is to show that given
\bm{x}(0)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
,

x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{a}}}(t)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},x^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathtt{b}}}(t)\leq{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta},x^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}(t)\leq{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}},x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(t)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}\ \forall t\geq 0,

which we do by Lemmas
7
,
8
,
9
and
10
.
We start by showing
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{a}}}(t)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
, that is, all type-
1
, …, type-
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
anticoordinators will keep playing
\mathtt{A}
, given that we start from the positively invariant set.
The idea is to use induction and show that as long as
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{a}}}(t)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
, the number of
\mathtt{A}
-players does not exceed that in
\tilde{\bm{y}}
as stated in Lemma
6
, which in turn ensures
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{a}}}(t+1)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}
.
First, we show the following result.
In what follows, we assume
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta})\in\Psi
.

Lemma 3
.

Consider
\bm{x}(0)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
.
If for some
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{a}}}(t)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}\quad\forall t\in\{0,\ldots,T\},

(10)

then

x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(t)\geq{\delta}\quad\forall t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}.

(11)

Proof.

First, we show by contradiction that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}\geq{\delta}
.
Assume on the contrary,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}<{\delta}
.
By definition,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}=\overrightarrow{c}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(\hat{\bm{z}})
.
Hence, starting from the state
\hat{\bm{z}}
, under the coordinating left-to-right activation and after all type-
b^{\prime}
, …, type-
(\delta+1)
coordinators are activated,
the total number of
\mathtt{A}
-players becomes insufficient for the
\delta
-type coordinators to keep playing
\mathtt{A}
.
Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\hat{z}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{{\delta}}n^{\prime}_{i}\leq\tau^{\prime}_{{\delta}}.

On the other hand,
\sum_{i=1}^{b}\hat{z}_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{b}y_{i}
, implying

\sum_{i=1}^{b}y_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{{\delta}}n^{\prime}_{i}\leq\tau^{\prime}_{{\delta}},

which contradicts the definition of
{\delta}
, yielding
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}\geq{\delta}
.
On the other hand,
\bm{x}(0)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\delta}}
yields
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(0)\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}}
.
Hence,
x^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{d}}}(0)\geq{\delta}
.
We complete the proof by induction on

.
The inequality in (
11
) holds for
t=0
.
Suppose it holds for

Suppose a type-
p,p\in\{1,\ldots,{\delta}\},

In view of (

A(\bm{x}(k))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}}x_{i}(k)+\sum_{i=1}^{{\delta}}x^{\prime}_{i}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta}}n_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{{\delta}}n^{\prime}_{i}\geq\tau^{\prime}_{{\delta}}+1.

Hence, the active agent keeps playing
\mathtt{A}
at

, completing the proof.
∎

Case 2:

VI
Stability analysis

Consider the quadruple
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}})\in\Omega
such that the set
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
defined in Section
III
is positively invariant.
Note that
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}})
is not necessarily the same as
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\eta^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\zeta})
defined in Section
IV
.
We investigate the notion of stability for the positively invariant set
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
.
That is, we examine whether the solution trajectory in the set
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
remains close to the set under small perturbations.
Unlike in continuous dynamics, here, “small perturbations” and “closeness” are explicitly quantified to one since the smallest nonzero difference between two states is one, i.e.,
\min_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X},\bm{x}\neq\bm{y}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|=1
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the
L^{1}
norm.
The notion of stability, therefore, simplifies to requiring any solution trajectory that is “adjacent” to the positively invariant set to remain adjacent or enter the set.
We provide a formal argument in the following.

Given a state
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, let
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{O}}
denote the distant between
\bm{x}
and
\mathcal{O}
, defined by

\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{O}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\inf_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{O}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|.

A natural extension of the notion of equilibrium (Lyapunov) stability yields the following definition of
(positively invariant) set stability
: A positively invariant set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is stable if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that if
\|\bm{x}(0)\|_{\mathcal{O}}<\delta
then
\|\bm{x}(t)\|_{\mathcal{O}}<\epsilon
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
However, since the state space is discrete in our case, the norm
\|\cdot\|
is confined to non-negative integers.
Thus, we can assume
\epsilon
and
\delta
are natural numbers.
On the other hand,
\epsilon
cannot be one; otherwise, the definition requires the solution trajectory to always stay in the stable set, even at time zero.
Neither does
\delta
equal one; otherwise, the solution trajectory starts from the positively invariant set, and hence, will always remain there, which does not allow the investigation of the dynamics under small perturbations.
Hence, the minimum of
\epsilon
and
\delta
is two.
Moreover, if the stability condition is satisfied for
\epsilon=2
, then it is also satisfied for
\epsilon>2
.
We, therefore, obtain the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A positively invariant set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is stable if under any activation sequence,

\|\bm{x}(0)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 1\Rightarrow\|\bm{x}(t)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 1\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

The following result follows by induction.

Lemma 14
.

A positively invariant set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is stable if and only if under any activation sequence,

\|\bm{x}(0)\|_{\mathcal{O}}=1\Rightarrow\|\bm{x}(1)\|_{\mathcal{O}}\leq 1.

(34)

So for
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
to be stable, it is necessary and sufficient to investigate solution trajectories that start from adjacent states.
Let
\bm{1}_{i}
be the all-zero vector of length
b+b^{\prime}
, whose
i^{\text{th}}
entry is one.
The initial condition
\bm{x}(0)
, adjacent to
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
, is in the form of
\bm{z}\pm\bm{1}_{s}
for some state
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}
and type
s
.
Then the state at the next time step
\bm{x}(1)
will be in the form of

, where

denotes the type of the initially active agent.
Stability of
\mathcal{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}

({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}})\in\Omega

\lfloor\tau_{i}\rfloor<\lfloor\tau_{i-1}\rfloor\quad\forall i\in\{2,\ldots,b\}.

Lemma 15
.

Given the state
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{I}
, if
\mathcal{R}(\bm{z}),\mathcal{L}(\bm{z})\neq\emptyset
, then

\max\mathcal{R}(\bm{z})\leq\min\mathcal{L}(\bm{z})
.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
Then

\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}}-1}n^{\prime}_{k}+\!\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}}n_{k}+\!\!\!\!\sum_{k={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}+1}^{r}\!\!\!\!z_{k}+\!\!\!\sum_{k=r+1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}-1}\!\!n_{k}=\lfloor\tau_{r}\rfloor+1.

Hence, for
i\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}+1,\ldots,r-1\}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}n^{\prime}_{k}+\!\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}}n_{k}+\!\sum_{k=i}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}-1}z_{k}\leq\lfloor\tau_{r}\rfloor+1\overset{\eqref{assumption_tauOrdering}}{<}\lfloor\tau_{i}\rfloor+1.

Thus,
\mathcal{L}\cap({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},r)=\emptyset
.
By setting
r=\max\mathcal{R}
, we obtain
\mathcal{L}\cap({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},\max\mathcal{R})=\emptyset
.
Because of
\mathcal{L}\subseteq({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q})
, it follows that
\mathcal{L}\cap(0,\max\mathcal{R})=\emptyset
.
This completes the proof.
∎

.

Consider the state
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{I}
, where there exist anticoordinating types

and

takes the form

\scalebox{.95}{\text{$(\underbrace{n_{1},\ldots,n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}},z_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}+1},\ldots,z_{s}+1,\ldots,z_{v}-1,\ldots,z_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}-1},0,\ldots,0}_{\text{anticoordinating}})$}}.

For all

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}n^{\prime}_{k}+\!\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}}n_{k}+\!\sum_{k=i}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}-1}x_{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}n^{\prime}_{k}+\!\!\sum_{k=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}}n_{k}+\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=i;\\

where the last inequality follows
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{I}
.
So
\bm{x}
satisfies (

L

).
Similarly,
\bm{x}
satisfies (

R

), leading to the proof.

Lemma 17
.

Consider the state
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{I}
, where there exist anticoordinating types
v
and
s
satisfying
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}<v<s<{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}
,
z_{s}<n_{s}
, and
z_{v}>0
.
Define the state
\bm{x}=\bm{z}+\bm{1}_{s}-\bm{1}_{v}
.
Then

|\mathcal{R}(\bm{z})\cap[v,s)|\times|\mathcal{L}(\bm{z})\cap(v,s]|=0\iff\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}\leq 1,

with equality when exactly one of
\mathcal{R}(\bm{z})\cap[v,s)
or
\mathcal{L}(\bm{z})\cap(v,s]
is empty.

Proof.

The anticoordinating part of
\bm{x}
is of the form

\scalebox{.95}{\text{$(\underbrace{n_{1},\ldots,n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}},z_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}+1},\ldots,z_{v}-1,\ldots,z_{s}+1,\ldots,z_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q}-1},0,\ldots,0}_{\text{anticoordinating}})$}}.

The equation
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}=1
holds if and only if there exits a state
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{I}
, such that
\bm{y}=\bm{x}-\bm{1}_{u}
or
\bm{y}=\bm{x}+\bm{1}_{u}
for some
u\in\{1,\ldots,b+b^{\prime}\}
.
Now, for
l\in\mathcal{L}
,
we observe that if
l\in(v,s]
, then
y_{u}=x_{u}-1
and
u\geq l
.
Similarly, for
r\in\mathcal{R}
,
we observe that if
r\in[v,s)
, then
y_{u}=x_{u}+1
and
u\leq r
.
So in the case where both
\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]
and
\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)
are nonempty, the former requires
y_{u}=x_{u}-1
and the latter requires
y_{u}=x_{u}+1
, which is impossible, proving
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}\geq 2
.
On the other hand, if
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}\geq 2
, then for every state
\bm{y}
satisfying
\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|=1
, it holds that
\bm{y}\not\in\mathcal{I}
.
Hence, by considering
\bm{y}=\bm{x}-\bm{1}_{s}
, since
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q^{\prime}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p^{\prime}}}

L

\bm{y}
violates (

R

).

\mathcal{R}\cap[v,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q})\neq\emptyset
.
Similarly, by considering
\bm{y}=\bm{x}+\bm{1}_{v}

\mathcal{L}\cap({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p},s]\neq\emptyset
.
Hence, in view of Lemma
15
, both
\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]
and
\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)
are nonempty.
Therefore, we proved

\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)\neq\emptyset,\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]\neq\emptyset\iff\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}\geq 2.

(36)

Now consider the case where
\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)=\emptyset
but
\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]\neq\emptyset
.
Then in view of Lemma
15
,
\mathcal{R}\cap[v,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}q})=\emptyset
.
Now let
\bm{y}=\bm{x}-\bm{1}_{s}
.
Then
\bm{y}=\bm{z}-\bm{1}_{v}
, implying that
\bm{y}
satisfies (

L

) for

\bm{y}\in\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)=\emptyset,\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]\neq\emptyset\Rightarrow\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}=1.

Similarly, the case with

\mathcal{R}\cap[v,s)\neq\emptyset,\mathcal{L}\cap(v,s]=\emptyset\Rightarrow\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{I}}=1.

Now if

) and (

(40)

The result follows (

), (

), and (
40
).

VIII
Synchronous updates

The agents in our model update their strategies
asynchronously
; that is, a single agent becomes active at each time step.
For example, individuals may separately decide to join or leave NGOs at different times, based on their availability, and companies may change their strategies separately, based on their financial situation.
Although often less realistic, one may consider the
synchronous
case, where all agents update their strategies simultaneously at every time step
[
35
]
.
This leaves no room for the possible stochasticity in the agents’ activation sequence, resulting in fully deterministic dynamics.

More specifically, given the population state
\bm{x}(t)
, the state at the next time step is obtained by
\bm{x}(t+1)=\bm{\beta}(\bm{x}(t))
, where
\bm{\beta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is the
best-response map
defined by

\beta_{i}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}n_{i}&A(\bm{x})\leq\tau_{i}\\
x_{i}&A(\bm{x})=\lfloor\tau_{i}\rfloor+1\\
0&A(\bm{x})>\tau_{i}+1\end{cases},\beta_{j}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}n^{\prime}_{j}&A(\bm{x})\geq\tau^{\prime}_{j}+1\\
n^{\prime}_{j}-x^{\prime}_{j}&A(\bm{x})=\lceil\tau^{\prime}_{j}\rceil\\
0&A(\bm{x})<\tau^{\prime}_{j}\end{cases}

where
i\in\{1,\ldots,b\}
is an anticoordinating and
j\in\{1,\ldots,b^{\prime}\}
is a coordinating type.
The equilibria of the synchronous and asynchronous dynamics are the same since both require every individual to be satisfied with their strategies.
What about the invariant sets?
Under the synchronous dynamics, same-type agents either all simultaneously or none switch strategies.
Hence, the population state no longer evolves gradually by a distance of one from the previous state, but may undergo large jumps at each time step.
As an immediate result, (

L

) and (

R

) may no longer hold, and the rich asynchronous dynamics may become limited to a small limit cycle.

To analyze the synchronous dynamics, Granovetter
[
24
]
(followed by
[
36
]
) proposed the convenient approach of focusing on the evolution of the number of
\mathtt{A}
-players,
A(t)
, rather than the state
\bm{x}(t)
, and obtain a first-order difference equation
A(t+1)=F(A(t))
for some function
F
.
However, this is not the case with our model, because
A(t+1)
is not a function of
A(t)
only but also
\bm{x}(t)
as seen in the definition of
\bm{\beta}
.
Nevertheless, due to the potential interesting insights of this approach, we consider the simplified population dynamics where
A_{j}
in (
2
) and (
3
) is replaced with
A
; namely, instead of basing her decisions on the number of
\mathtt{A}
-players in the remaining of the population, agent
j
bases her decision on the total number of
\mathtt{A}
-players in the population.
This simplifies
\bm{\beta}
to
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
as follows:

\hat{b}_{i}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}n_{i}&A(\bm{x})\leq\tau_{i}\\
0&A(\bm{x})>\tau_{i}\end{cases},\ \hat{b}_{j}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}n^{\prime}_{j}&A(\bm{x})\geq\tau^{\prime}_{j}\\
0&A(\bm{x})<\tau^{\prime}_{j}\end{cases}
.

Then the number of
\mathtt{A}
-playing coordinators, denoted by
A^{c}
, is governed by
A^{c}(t+1)=F^{c}(A(t))
, where
F^{c}
is the cumulative distribution function of the coordinators’ tempers.
Moreover, the number of
\mathtt{A}
-playing anticoordinators, denoted by
A^{a}
, is governed by
A^{a}(t+1)=F^{a}(A(t))
, where
F^{a}(A)
is the number of anticoordinators whose tempers are non-less than
A
.
Thus, the total number of
\mathtt{A}
-players is governed by

A(t+1)=F(A(t)),

(64)

where
F=F^{c}+F^{a}
, which is plotted in Figure
3
for the population in Example
1
.

Intersections of

As expected, the point

The synchronous dynamics additionally admit two limit cycles, characterized by
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{9,10\}
and

\mathcal{O}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}5},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}3},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}}

One can also easily find the domain of attractions from the graph:
the domain of attraction of

So the dynamics of the number of
\mathtt{A}

For example, unlike the asynchronous case, here the limit cycles are only of length two, and the extremum number of
\mathtt{A}
-players in the limit cycles and invariant sets do not match.
Also, the first limit cycle is stable under (

Moreover, in the first limit cycle

One may also note the somewhat surprising gap in the domain of attraction

The situation is not much different under the original synchronous dynamics governed by

Our simulations show that then

Overall, the exact relationship between the synchronous and asynchronous dynamics remains unknown.

IX
Conclusion

We have studied a well-mixed heterogeneous population of coordinators and anticoordinators.
We have explicitly characterized a class of positively invariant sets under the population dynamics.
If any such set does not include an equilibrium, it contains a minimally positively invariant subset where the solution trajectory will perpetually fluctuate and never equilibrate.
In the context of collective decision-makers such as NGOs and firms, this means that observed oscillations in the decisions are not necessarily caused by errors in the decisions; namely, they are not a matter of chance.
Nor do they imply a complex interaction network between the individuals.
They are likely intrinsic to the dynamics.
Moreover, the oscillations are not unpredictable.
For example, Corollary
1
bounds the number of individuals choosing a certain action over time.
We also know if the oscillations are stable, and hence, persistent under small errors in the individuals’ decisions.
These results pave the path to control the corresponding populations, for example, by providing incentives
[
37
]
, and lead them to a desired outcome where the oscillations are settled or modified.
Designing the control algorithms as well as fitting the dynamics to data remain as future work.
