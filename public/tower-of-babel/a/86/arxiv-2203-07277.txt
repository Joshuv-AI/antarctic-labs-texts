A short note on the appearance of the simplest antilinear ODE in several physical contexts
Dmitry Ponomarev

Abstract
In this short note, we review several one-dimensional problems such as those involving linear Schrödinger equation, variable-coefficient Helmholtz equation, Zakharov-Shabat system and Kubelka-Munk equations. We show that they all can be reduced to solving one simple antilinear ordinary differential equation u ′ ​ ( x ) = f ​ ( x ) ​ u ⁡ ( x ) ¯ u^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{u\left(x\right)} or its nonhomogeneous version u ′ ​ ( x ) = f ⁡ ( x ) ​ u ⁡ ( x ) ¯ + g ⁡ ( x ) u^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{u\left(x\right)}+g\left(x\right) , x ∈ ( 0 , x 0 ) ⊂ ℝ x\in\left(0,x_{0}\right)\subset\mathbb{R} . We point out some of the advantages of the proposed reformulation and call for further investigation of the obtained ODE.

Many physical phenomena can be directly described by or reduced to
systems of differential equations having certain structural properties.
Restricting ourselves here to linear one-dimensional settings, we
are concerned with a pair of first-order ODEs whose matrix is antidiagonal
with complex-conjugate elements. Namely, given
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

and complex-valued function
f\left(x\right)
, we consider the equation

U^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&f\left(x\right)\\
\overline{f\left(x\right)}&0\end{array}\right)U\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(1)

as well as its nonhomogeneous analog

U^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&f\left(x\right)\\
\overline{f\left(x\right)}&0\end{array}\right)U\left(x\right)+G\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right)\text{,}

(2)

where
U\left(x\right)\equiv\left(u_{1}\left(x\right),u_{2}\left(x\right)\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

is an unknown solution-vector,
G\left(x\right)\equiv\left(g_{1}\left(x\right),g_{2}\left(x\right)\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

is a given vector-function, and each of equations (
1
)–(
2
)
is supplemented by the initial condition
U\left(0\right)=U_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Here and onwards, we employ the notation
\overline{\cdot}
to denote
complex conjugation.

Similarly to Hamiltonian, Dirac and more general canonical systems
(see e.g.
[
12
]
), equations (
1
)–(
2
)
constitute an important class of dynamical systems for two reasons.
On the one hand, as we shall further see, formulations of several
important problems are reducible to either (
1
)
or (
2
). On the other hand, these systems
are close to being exactly solvable in the following sense. Let us
focus on (
1
) and consider the more general
system

U^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p\left(x\right)&r\left(x\right)\\
s\left(x\right)&q\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)U\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right).

(3)

We note that the diagonal elements in the matrix of (
3
)
can be removed by the exponential multiplier transform. Namely, by
setting

V\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-\int_{0}^{x}p\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau}&0\\
0&e^{-\int_{0}^{x}q\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau}\end{array}\right)U\left(x\right),

one can observe that
V\left(x\right)
satisfies

V^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&r\left(x\right)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)\\
s\left(x\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\end{array}\right)V\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(4)

with the initial condition
V\left(0\right)=U_{0}
. Now, if the anti-diagonal
elements of the matrix in the right-hand side of (
4
)
are equal, i.e.

r\left(x\right)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)=s\left(x\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)=:c_{1}\left(x\right),

(5)

then the solution can be written explicitly as

V\left(x\right)=\left[\cosh\left(\int_{0}^{x}c_{1}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)I+\sinh\left(\int_{0}^{x}c_{1}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)S\right]U_{0},

where

I:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptS:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right).

(6)

However, condition (
5
), which amounts to the
assumption

s\left(x\right)=r\left(x\right)\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right),

(7)

may be too restrictive. Indeed, in view of multiple possible similarity
transformations allowing to rewrite (
3
) in different
equivalent forms, we want to have a clearly identifiable matrix structure
which should be, on the one hand, immediately recognisable and, on
the other hand, leading to a solution simplification or even an explicit
solution. Such an identifiable structure may be, for example, a pairwise
relation between some of the elements of the matrix in (
3
).
The explicit solvability condition, nevertheless, plays against any
visible structural property of the matrix: even though assumption
(
7
) leads to a closed-form solution, it implies
a rather complicated relation between the matrix elements. Condition
(
7
) is very specific and thus unlikely to be
satisfied for any easily describable class of matrix elements unless,
of course,
p\equiv q
, which would then also entail that
r\equiv s
.
This exactly solvable case with equal diagonal and anti-diagonal elements
in (
3
) may be sometimes valuable but it does not
seem to be the one that covers many fruitful applications.

It turns out that condition (
7
) has an analog
which is less stringent in form of the matrix elements in (
3
),
with more pertinence to important physical contexts, and, at the same
time, it still leads to a significant simplification of the solution
procedure (and, at least in some cases, also to closed-form solutions).
This condition reads

s\left(x\right)=\overline{r\left(x\right)}\exp\left(-2\text{ Re}\int_{0}^{x}\left[p\left(\tau\right)-q\left(\tau\right)\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right).

(8)

Despite the similarity to (
7
), condition (
8
)
is easier to satisfy while preserving a visible matrix structure.
Indeed, if
p\left(x\right)-q\left(x\right)
is a purely imaginary
function (e.g., in particular, when
p\equiv\overline{q}
), the complicated
exponential factor in (
8
) disappears. In this
case, the implied condition
s\left(x\right)=\overline{r\left(x\right)}

is clearly indentifiable but far from being trivial since, as we shall
see, it covers a variety of different practical applications. This
reasoning motivates us to consider (
1
) as well
as its nonhogeneous analog (
2
).

The plan of this note is as follows. Section
2

is dedicated to the transformation of (
1
) and
(
2
) into the homogeneous antilinear ODE

u^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{u\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(9)

and its nonhomogeneous analog

u^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{u\left(x\right)}+g\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(10)

respectively. Next, in Section
3
, we outline some
relevant physical applications, i.e. problems which, upon appropriate
transformations, can be recast as (
1
) or (
2
)
and are thus reducible to formulations involving antilinear ODE (
9
),
or, in one case, its nonhomogeneous version (
10
).
Finally, in Section
4
, we conclude with some remarks
on how antilinear ODEs can be constructively addressed further and
briefly mention a couple of other applications.

2
Transformation of an antidiagonal problem into an antilinear ODE

2.1
Homogeneous case: from (
1
) to (
9
)

We consider (
1
) supplemented with the initial
data
U\left(0\right)=U_{0}\equiv\left(u_{1}^{0},u_{2}^{0}\right)^{T}
,
where
u_{1}^{0}
,
u_{2}^{0}\in\mathbb{C}
are arbitrary constants,
and we devise a transformation that allows construction of the solution
of system (
1
) in terms of the solution of an
antilinear ODE of the form (
9
).

Let us first motivate our approach to construction of such a transformation.
To this effect, given a complex-valued function
f\left(x\right)
,
it is instructive to consider the elementary differential equation
for
v\left(x\right)

v^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)v\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(11)

with the initial condition
v\left(0\right)=1
. On the one hand,
(
11
) is in separable form and hence can be integrated
directly to yield the solution

v\left(x\right)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right).

(12)

On the other hand, rewriting (
11
) in the integral form

v\left(x\right)=1+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)v\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

the Picard iterative process gives

v\left(x\right)=1+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}+\ldots.

(13)

Comparison of (
12
) with (
13
) results
in important identities

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)=1+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}+\ldots,

\sinh\left(\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)=\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}+\ldots,

(14)

\cosh\left(\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)=1+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{4}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{4}}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}\textrm{d}\tau_{4}+\ldots,

(15)

where we used the identity
\exp\left(z\right)=\cosh\left(z\right)+\sinh\left(z\right)
,

z\in\mathbb{C}
, and the parity argument to split the terms:
\sinh

is an odd function and hence (
14
) may contain only
odd number of multiplicative instances of
f
, and similarly, (
15
)
may contain only terms with even number of multiplications by
f

due to
\cosh
being an even function.

Now, similarly to (
14
)–(
15
), let us
consider the following quantities

S_{f}\left(x\right):=\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}+\ldots,

(16)

C_{f}\left(x\right):=1+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{4}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{4}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{3}\right)}\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}\textrm{d}\tau_{4}+\ldots.

(17)

Let us show that (
16
)–(
17
) are inherent
to an algebraic structure underlying (
1
). To
this effect, we rewrite (
1
) in the integral
form

U\left(x\right)=U_{0}+\int_{0}^{x}A\left(\tau\right)U\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptA\left(\tau\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&f\left(\tau\right)\\
\overline{f\left(\tau\right)}&0\end{array}\right),

(18)

and note that

A\left(\tau_{2}\right)A\left(\tau_{1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}&0\\
0&\overline{f\left(\tau_{2}\right)}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptA\left(\tau_{3}\right)A\left(\tau_{2}\right)A\left(\tau_{1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{2}\right)}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\\
\overline{f\left(\tau_{3}\right)}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}&0\end{array}\right),

A\left(\tau_{4}\right)A\left(\tau_{3}\right)A\left(\tau_{2}\right)A\left(\tau_{1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f\left(\tau_{4}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{3}\right)}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}&0\\
0&\overline{f\left(\tau_{4}\right)}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\overline{f\left(\tau_{2}\right)}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\ldots.

Therefore, writing out Picard iterations for solving (
18
),
we obtain

U\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{f}\left(x\right)&S_{f}\left(x\right)\\
S_{\overline{f}}\left(x\right)&C_{\overline{f}}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)U_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{f}\left(x\right)&S_{f}\left(x\right)\\
\overline{S_{f}\left(x\right)}&\overline{C_{f}\left(x\right)}\end{array}\right)U_{0}.

(19)

Furthermore, it is easy to see from (
16
)–(
17
)
that
S_{f}\left(x\right)
,
C_{f}\left(x\right)
obey the following
intertwining relation

C_{f}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{S_{f}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptS_{f}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{C_{f}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(20)

and the conditions
S_{f}\left(0\right)=0
,
C_{f}\left(0\right)=1
.
Introducing another pair of functions

Z_{+}\left(x\right):=C_{f}\left(x\right)+S_{f}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{-}\left(x\right):=C_{f}\left(x\right)-S_{f}\left(x\right),

we decouple (
20
) as

Z_{+}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{Z_{+}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{+}\left(0\right)=1,

(21)

Z_{-}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-f\left(x\right)\overline{Z_{-}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{-}\left(0\right)=1.

(22)

Equations (
21
)–(
22
) are two separate
instances of the initial-value problem featuring the antilinear ODE
given by (
9
). Solution of this ODE would thus
yield the solutions of (
21
)–(
22
)
and, consequently, also of (
20
), providing
S_{f}\left(x\right)
,

C_{f}\left(x\right)
appearing in (
19
) which furnishes
the solution of (
1
).

2.2
Nonhomogeneous case: from (
2
) to (
10
)

Let us now consider (
2
) with
G\left(x\right)\equiv\left(g_{1}\left(x\right),g_{2}\left(x\right)\right)^{T}

and subject to the initial condition
U\left(0\right)=U_{0}\equiv\left(u_{1}^{0},u_{2}^{0}\right)^{T}
.
We are going to show that, in particular case where

g_{2}\left(x\right)=i\,\overline{g_{1}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptu_{2}^{0}=i\,\overline{u_{1}^{0}},

(23)

the solution of (
2
) can be constucted
in terms of solutions of two instances of problem (
10
).
As we shall see in Subsection
3.2
, assumption
(
23
) will be satisfied in at least one important

Similarly to (
16
)–(
17
), let us introduce

\mathrm{S}_{f,h}\left(x\right):=\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)h\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{3}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}f\left(\tau_{1}\right)h\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}+\ldots,

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{f,h}\left(x\right):=

\displaystyle h\left(x\right)+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}h\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}

(25)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{x}f\left(\tau_{4}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{4}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{3}\right)}\int_{0}^{\tau_{3}}f\left(\tau_{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}\overline{f\left(\tau_{1}\right)}h\left(\tau_{1}\right)\textrm{d}\tau_{1}\textrm{d}\tau_{2}\textrm{d}\tau_{3}\textrm{d}\tau_{4}+\ldots.

U\left(x\right)=U_{0}+\int_{0}^{x}G\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{x}A\left(\tau\right)U\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptA\left(\tau\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&f\left(\tau\right)\\
\overline{f\left(\tau\right)}&0\end{array}\right),

(26)

it is straightforward to see that Picard iterations give

U\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{S}_{f,h_{2}}\left(x\right)+\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)\\
\mathrm{S}_{\overline{f},h_{1}}\left(x\right)+\mathrm{C}_{\overline{f},h_{2}}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

where
\mathrm{S}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
,
\mathrm{C}_{f,h}\left(x\right)

are as defined by (
24
)–(
25
), and

h_{1}\left(x\right):=u_{1}^{0}+\int_{0}^{x}g_{1}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pth_{2}\left(x\right):=u_{2}^{0}+\int_{0}^{x}g_{2}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau.

(27)

By means of differentiation of
\mathrm{S}_{f,h_{2}}\left(x\right)

and
\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)
, we obtain the following
intertwining relation

\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=h_{1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)+f\left(x\right)\mathrm{S}_{\overline{f},h_{1}}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{S}_{f,h_{2}}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\mathrm{C}_{\overline{f},h_{2}}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(28)

which is to be supplemented by the conditions
\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(0\right)=h_{1}\left(0\right)
,

\mathrm{S}_{f,h_{2}}\left(0\right)=0
.

Note that, from (
24
)–(
25
),
\mathrm{S}_{\overline{f},h_{1}}\left(x\right)=\overline{\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}\left(x\right)}

and
\mathrm{C}_{\overline{f},h_{2}}\left(x\right)=\overline{\mathrm{C}_{f,\overline{h_{2}}}\left(x\right)}
.
Moreover, assumption (
23
) entails that
h_{2}\left(x\right)=i\,\overline{h_{1}\left(x\right)}
,
and by linearity in
h
of
\mathrm{S}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
,
\mathrm{C}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
,
we have
\mathrm{S}_{f,h_{2}}\left(x\right)=i\,\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}\left(x\right)

and
\overline{\mathrm{C}_{f,\overline{h_{2}}}\left(x\right)}=i\,\overline{\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)}
.
Consequently, relation (
28
) becomes

\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=h_{1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)+f\left(x\right)\overline{\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right).

(29)

Setting

Z_{+}\left(x\right):=\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)+\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{-}\left(x\right):=\mathrm{C}_{f,h_{1}}\left(x\right)-\mathrm{S}_{f,\overline{h_{1}}}\left(x\right),

(30)

we obtain from (

) two decoupled ODE problems

Z_{+}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{Z_{+}\left(x\right)}+g_{1}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{+}\left(0\right)=u_{1}^{0},

(31)

Z_{-}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-f\left(x\right)\overline{Z_{-}\left(x\right)}+g_{1}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{-}\left(0\right)=u_{1}^{0},

(32)

each of them are of the form (

).

3.1
Linear Schrödinger equation

Consider the stationary linear Schrödinger equation in
1D
,
with a potential
a\left(x\right)>0
,

u^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)+a\left(x\right)u\left(x\right)=0,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right).

(33)

We focus here on the initial-value problem, i.e. we supplement (
33
)
with the boundary conditions
u\left(0\right)=u_{0}
,
u^{\prime}\left(0\right)=u_{1}
,
but boundary-value problems on
\left(0,x_{0}\right)
, with
x_{0}

being finite or infinite, could also be treated. We assume
a\in C^{1}\left(\left[0,x_{0}\right]\right)
.

Introducing the vector-function

U\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\left(x\right)\\
\frac{1}{a^{1/2}\left(x\right)}u^{\prime}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

(34)

we observe that
U\left(x\right)
satisfies

U^{\prime}\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u^{\prime}\left(x\right)\\
-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{2a^{3/2}\left(x\right)}u^{\prime}\left(x\right)+\frac{1}{a^{1/2}\left(x\right)}u^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)=A\left(x\right)U\left(x\right),

(35)

with

A\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&a^{1/2}\left(x\right)\\
-a^{1/2}\left(x\right)&-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{2a\left(x\right)}\end{array}\right),

and
U\left(0\right)=\left(u_{0},u_{1}/a^{1/2}\left(0\right)\right)^{T}
.
Here, in the second inequality of (
35
), we used (
33
)
to eliminate
u^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)
.

By writing,

A\left(x\right)=a^{1/2}\left(x\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{2a\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),

we note that the first matrix in the right-hand side is diagonalisable
as follows

P\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)P^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i&0\\
0&-i\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptP:=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i&1\\
1&i\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptP^{-1}=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-i&1\\
1&-i\end{array}\right).

(36)

Consequently, introducing
V\left(x\right):=PU\left(x\right)
, we
multiply the both sides of (
35
) by
P
and thus transform
it into

V^{\prime}\left(x\right)=PA\left(x\right)P^{-1}V\left(x\right)=B\left(x\right)V\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(37)

with

B\left(x\right):=a^{1/2}\left(x\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i&0\\
0&-i\end{array}\right)-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{4a\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-i\\
i&1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b_{0}\left(x\right)&0\\
0&\overline{b_{0}\left(x\right)}\end{array}\right)-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{4a\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right),

b_{0}\left(x\right):=ia^{1/2}\left(x\right)-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{4a\left(x\right)},

(38)

and supplemented by the initial condition

V\left(0\right)=PU\left(0\right)=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}iu_{0}+u_{1}/a^{1/2}\left(0\right)\\
u_{0}+iu_{1}/a^{1/2}\left(0\right)\end{array}\right).

Furthermore, introducing

W\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}b_{0}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\overline{b_{0}\left(\tau\right)}\textrm{d}\tau\right)\end{array}\right)V\left(x\right),

we have

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}b_{0}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\overline{b_{0}\left(\tau\right)}\textrm{d}\tau\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-b_{0}\left(x\right)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}b_{0}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\\
0&-\overline{b_{0}\left(x\right)}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\overline{b_{0}\left(\tau\right)}\textrm{d}\tau\right)\end{array}\right).

Therefore, (
37
) entails

W^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-\frac{a^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{4a\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\left[b_{0}\left(\tau\right)-\overline{b_{0}\left(\tau\right)}\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)\\
i\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}\left[b_{0}\left(\tau\right)-\overline{b_{0}\left(\tau\right)}\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\end{array}\right)W\left(x\right),

which, recalling (
38
), we can rewrite as

W^{\prime}\left(x\right)=C\left(x\right)W\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(39)

with

C\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{0}\left(x\right)\\
\overline{c_{0}\left(x\right)}&0\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptc_{0}\left(x\right):=\frac{ia^{\prime}\left(x\right)}{4a\left(x\right)}\exp\left(-2i\int_{0}^{x}a^{1/2}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right),

and the initial condition

W\left(0\right)=V\left(0\right)=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}iu_{0}+u_{1}/a^{1/2}\left(0\right)\\
u_{0}+iu_{1}/a^{1/2}\left(0\right)\end{array}\right).

The steps described above draw from
[
10
]
(see also
[
4
]
)
and provide one way to rewrite the linear Schrödinger equation
in the form (
1
), but this approach is not the
only one. Alternative reduction procedures may be more cumbersome
but more beneficial in practice, depending on a final goal. For instance,
in
[
2
]
, the initial vectorisation of (
33
)
is different from (
34
) yet other steps of the
transformation are ideologically similar.

3.2
Helmholtz equation

Stationary problems for the wave propagation in heterogeneous media
are described by the Helmholtz equation whose 1
D
version is given
by

\left(\alpha\left(x\right)u^{\prime}\left(x\right)\right)^{\prime}+\beta\left(x\right)u\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right).

(40)

Here,
\alpha\left(x\right)
,
\beta\left(x\right)>0
are material
parameters and
f\left(x\right)
is the source term. As in Subsection

3.1
, we suppose that (
40
) is supplemented
by the initial conditions
u\left(0\right)=u_{0}
,
u^{\prime}\left(0\right)=u_{1}
.
Furthermore, we assume that
f\left(x\right)
,
u_{0}
,
u_{1}

are all real-valued. This assumption does not reduce generality since
(
40
) is linear with real-valued
\alpha
\left(x\right)
,

\beta\left(x\right)
and hence a real-valued problem can be solved
separately for real and imaginary parts of the solution of the original
equation.

Setting

U\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{1/2}\left(x\right)\beta^{1/2}\left(x\right)u\left(x\right)\\
\alpha\left(x\right)u^{\prime}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

we recast (
40
) in the vector form

U^{\prime}\left(x\right)=A\left(x\right)U\left(x\right)+F_{0}\left(x\right),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\left(0,x_{0}\right),

(41)

with

A\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{2\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)}&\left(\frac{\beta\left(x\right)}{\alpha\left(x\right)}\right)^{1/2}\\
-\left(\frac{\beta\left(x\right)}{\alpha\left(x\right)}\right)^{1/2}&0\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptF_{0}\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
f\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

and the initial condition

U\left(0\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{1/2}\left(0\right)\beta^{1/2}\left(0\right)u_{0}\\
\alpha\left(0\right)u_{1}\end{array}\right).

We now follow the reduction steps similar to those in Subsection
3.1
.
We write

A\left(x\right)=\left(\frac{\beta\left(x\right)}{\alpha\left(x\right)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{2\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),

and note that we can diagonalise the first matrix with the help of
the auxiliary constant matrix
P
introduced in (
36
).
Denoting
V\left(x\right):=PU\left(x\right)
, we hence have, from
(
41
),

V^{\prime}\left(x\right)=B\left(x\right)V\left(x\right)+F_{1}\left(x\right),

(42)

with

B\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b_{1}\left(x\right)&0\\
0&\overline{b_{1}\left(x\right)}\end{array}\right)+\frac{\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{4\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&i\\
-i&0\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptF_{1}\left(x\right):=PF\left(x\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{f\left(x\right)}{2^{1/2}}\\
i\frac{f\left(x\right)}{2^{1/2}}\end{array}\right),

b_{1}\left(x\right):=i\left(\frac{\beta\left(x\right)}{\alpha\left(x\right)}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{4\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)},

and the initial condition

V\left(0\right)=PU\left(0\right)=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}i\alpha^{1/2}\left(0\right)\beta^{1/2}\left(0\right)u_{0}+\alpha\left(0\right)u_{1}\\
\alpha^{1/2}\left(0\right)\beta^{1/2}\left(0\right)u_{0}+i\alpha\left(0\right)u_{1}\end{array}\right).

Introducing

W\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}b_{1}\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\overline{b_{1}\left(\tau\right)}\textrm{d}\tau\right)\end{array}\right)V\left(x\right),

equation (
42
) transforms into

W^{\prime}\left(x\right)=C\left(x\right)W\left(x\right)+G\left(x\right),

(43)

where

C\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{1}\left(x\right)\\
\overline{c_{1}\left(x\right)}&0\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptc_{1}\left(x\right):=\frac{i\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{4\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)}\exp\left(-2i\int_{0}^{x}\left(\frac{\beta\left(\tau\right)}{\alpha\left(\tau\right)}\right)^{1/2}\textrm{d}\tau\right),

G\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g_{1}\left(x\right)\\
i\overline{g_{1}\left(x\right)}\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002ptg_{1}\left(x\right):=\frac{f\left(x\right)}{2^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\left[i\left(\frac{\beta\left(\tau\right)}{\alpha\left(\tau\right)}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\left[\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)\right]^{\prime}}{4\alpha\left(x\right)\beta\left(x\right)}\right]\textrm{d}\tau\right),

and the initial condition

W\left(0\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}w_{1}^{0}\\
i\overline{w_{1}^{0}}\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptw_{1}^{0}:=\frac{1}{2^{1/2}}\left(i\alpha^{1/2}\left(0\right)\beta^{1/2}\left(0\right)u_{0}+\alpha\left(0\right)u_{1}\right).

Here, in relating the first and the second components of the vector

G\left(x\right)
, and similarly
W\left(0\right)
, we employed
the real-valuedness of
\alpha\left(x\right)
,
\beta\left(x\right)
,

u_{0}
,
u_{1}
that was discussed in the beginning of this Subsection.

It remains to observe that system (
43
) is such that
the matrix
C\left(x\right)
and the vectors
G\left(x\right)
,

W\left(0\right)
fit the assumptions discussed in Subsection
2.2
.

3.3
Zakharov-Shabat system

It is well-known that solution of a spectral problem with the linear
Schrödinger equation appears as an intermediate step in solving
the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation by using the inverse scattering
transform. Zakharov-Shabat systems play the same role in the integrability
of other nonlinear equations
[
1
, p.10]
. In particular,
the Zakharov-Shabat system

\partial_{x}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\left(x,t\right)\\
v_{2}\left(x,t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-i\xi&q\left(x,t\right)\\
\overline{q\left(x,t\right)}&i\xi\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\left(x,t\right)\\
v_{2}\left(x,t\right)\end{array}\right),

(44)

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
being a spectral parameter, is a linear problem
pertinent to the integration of the defocusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger
(NLS) equation

i\partial_{t}q\left(x,t\right)=\partial_{x}^{2}q\left(x,t\right)-2\left|q\left(x,t\right)\right|^{2}q\left(x,t\right)

subject to the initial data
q\left(x,0\right)=q_{0}\left(x\right)
.
We refer to
[
6
]
for more details on this matter.

We observe that by setting

W\left(x,t\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\xi x}&0\\
0&e^{-i\xi x}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\left(x,t\right)\\
v_{2}\left(x,t\right)\end{array}\right),

Zakharov-Shabat system (
44
) immediately reduces to

\partial_{x}W\left(x,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&q\left(x\right)e^{2i\xi x}\\
\overline{q\left(x\right)}e^{-2i\xi x}&0\end{array}\right)W\left(x,t\right),

which is a system of the form (
1
).

3.4
Kubelka-Munk equations

Kubelka-Munk equations is a simple phenomenological model for computing
reflection and transmission optical fluxes without solution of significantly
more complicated radiative transfer equations
[
9
]
. Due
to their simplicity, Kubelka-Munk equations have been popular in practice
(in paper paint visibility, see e.g.
[
3
]
), they have
been extensively studied from modelling viewpoint and several generalisations
have been proposed
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.

We consider the following model equations

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}F_{+}\left(x\right)\\
F_{-}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-K\left(x\right)-S\left(x\right)&S\left(x\right)\\
-S\left(x\right)&K\left(x\right)+S\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}F_{+}\left(x\right)\\
F_{-}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

(45)

where
F_{+}
,
F_{-}
are fluxes in positive and negative directions,
and
K
and
S
are related to absorption and scattering, respectively.
Note that, unlike in the classical model, we take here
K
,
S

to be dependent on the optical depth
x
rather than simply being
constants. This generalisation is expected to be useful since constant
scattering and absorption coefficients are known to be a considerable
limitation of the Kubelka-Munk model
[
3
, Sect. 4.5]
.

The procedure of reduction of (
45
) to (
1
)
is similar to that performed in Subsection
3.1
.
Therefore, we shall omit any detailed calculation.

Let us write

A\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-K\left(x\right)-S\left(x\right)&S\left(x\right)\\
-S\left(x\right)&K\left(x\right)+S\left(x\right)\end{array}\right)=S\left(x\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right)-\left[K\left(x\right)+S\left(x\right)\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),

and compute

B\left(x\right):=PA\left(x\right)P^{-1}=S\left(x\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i&0\\
0&-i\end{array}\right)-\left[K\left(x\right)+S\left(x\right)\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&i\\
-i&0\end{array}\right)

with
P
defined as in (
36
). Multiplying the both
sides of (
45
) by
P
and introducing

V\left(x\right):=P\left(\begin{array}[]{c}F_{+}\left(x\right)\\
F_{-}\left(x\right)\end{array}\right),

we obtain

V^{\prime}\left(x\right)=B\left(x\right)V\left(x\right).

(46)

Furthermore, setting

W\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{x}S\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(i\int_{0}^{x}S\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right)\end{array}\right)V\left(x\right),

C\left(x\right):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{2}\left(x\right)\\
\overline{c_{2}\left(x\right)}&0\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptc_{2}\left(x\right):=-i\left[K\left(x\right)+S\left(x\right)\right]\exp\left(-2i\int_{0}^{x}S\left(\tau\right)\textrm{d}\tau\right),

we arrive at

W^{\prime}\left(x\right)=C\left(x\right)W\left(x\right),

(47)

which is a system of the form (
1
).

4
Conclusion

We have introduced a new scalar differential equation of the first
order which is curious for two principal reasons. First, it is, in
some sense, the simplest nonlinear ODE (either with or without a non-homogeneous
term), with the nonlinearity being merely the complex conjugation.
Second, this equation emerges, after appropriate reduction steps,
in rather different physical contexts. Certainly, much more application
areas can be identified (e.g. telegrapher’s equations or Goldstein-Taylor
model
[
5
]
), but already the context of the linear Schrödinger
equation alone is a good enough motivation to further study the antilinear
ODE
u^{\prime}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)\overline{u\left(x\right)}
.
For example, reduction of matrix-vector manipulations to those involving
scalar quantities already provides a simplification in tedious constructions
of asymptotic-numerical methods, cf.
[
2
,
8
]
. Therefore,
this new reformulation yields concrete practical advantages. We believe
that theoretical aspects of the mentioned models could benefit from
it, too. This might be achievable, for instance, through newly produced
forms of the Prüfer transformation (which is typically used for
studying Sturm-Liouville problems, see e.g.
[
11
, Sect. 5.2]
).
Furthermore, it is important to identify classes of functions
f

for which the antilinear ODE can be solved in a closed form. Here,
the Kubelka-Munk model context hints on the elementary exponential
class (note that system (
45
) with constant
K
and

S
is solvable explicitly). This can be generalised further since
the form of the antilinear ODE is amenable to a treatment by integral
transform methods (unlike other nonlinearities) typically compatible
with an exponential function and combinations thereof. Finally, the
form of the antilinear ODE calls for study of the possible connection
with
d
-bar problems, see e.g.
[
7
]
. In this case, an
appropriate extension of the equation to the complex plane may yield
a formulation that eventually produces a closed-form solution due
to numerous constructive results on Hilbert and Riemann-Hilbert problems.
