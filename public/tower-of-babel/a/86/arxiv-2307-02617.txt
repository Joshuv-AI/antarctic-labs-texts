The complexity of the Chinese Remainder Theorem
Miguel Campercholi, Diego Castaño, Gonzalo Zigarán

Abstract
The Chinese Remainder Theorem for the integers says that every system of congruence equations is solvable as long as the system satisfies an obvious necessary condition. This statement can be generalized in a natural way to arbitrary algebraic structures using the language of Universal Algebra. In this context, an algebra is a structure of a first-order language with no relation symbols, and a congruence on an algebra is an equivalence relation on its base set compatible with its fundamental operations. A tuple of congruences of an algebra is called a Chinese Remainder tuple if every system involving them is solvable. In this article we study the complexity of deciding whether a tuple of congruences of a finite algebra is a Chinese Remainder tuple. This problem, which we denote CRT , is easily seen to lie in coNP. We prove that it is actually coNP-complete and also show that it is tractable when restricted to several well-known classes of algebras, such as vector spaces and distributive lattices. The polynomial algorithms we exhibit are made possible by purely algebraic characterizations of Chinese Remainder tuples for algebras in these classes, which constitute interesting results in their own right. Among these, an elegant characterization of Chinese Remainder tuples of finite distributive lattices stands out. Finally, we address the restriction of CRT to an arbitrary equational class 𝒱 \mathcal{V} generated by a two-element algebra. Here we establish an (almost) dichotomy by showing that, unless 𝒱 \mathcal{V} is the class of semilattices, the problem is either coNP -complete or tractable.



1
Introduction

A fundamental property of the integers is the so-called Chinese Remainder Theorem, which can be stated as follows.

Chinese Remainder Theorem
.

Let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
be positive integers, and let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
be arbitrary integers such that

a_{i}\equiv a_{j}\mathrel{(\text{\rm mod }g_{ij})}\text{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

where
g_{ij}:=\gcd(m_{i},m_{j})
. Then, there is an integer
x
such that

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv a_{1}\mathrel{(\text{\rm mod }m_{1})},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv a_{2}\mathrel{(\text{\rm mod }m_{2})},

\displaystyle\hskip 4.83694pt\vdots

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv a_{k}\mathrel{(\text{\rm mod }m_{k})}.

This statement can be recast in the language of Universal Algebra. For this, recall that an
algebra
is just a structure of a first-order language with no relation symbols, and a
congruence
on an algebra is an equivalence relation on its base set compatible with its fundamental operations. Since the set of all congruences of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is closed under arbitrary intersections, it can be endowed with a complete lattice structure; the resulting lattice is usually denoted by
\mathop{\mathbf{Con}}\mathbf{A}
. The join
\theta\vee\delta
of two congruences in
\mathop{\mathbf{Con}}\mathbf{A}
is the transitive closure of
\theta\cup\delta
. In this setting, let us say that a tuple of congruences
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a
Chinese Remainder tuple
(CR tuple for short) of
\mathbf{A}
provided that for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
from
\mathbf{A}
such that
\langle a_{i},a_{j}\rangle\in\theta_{i}\vee\theta_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
there is
a
from
\mathbf{A}
with
\langle a,a_{i}\rangle\in\theta_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Thus, considering the integers as an algebra
\mathbf{Z}
in the language
\{+,\cdot,-,0\}
, its congruences are exactly the equivalence relations modulo positive integers, and the original Chinese Remainder Theorem says that every tuple of congruences of
\mathbf{Z}
is a CR tuple. It turns out that this property of the ring of integers is actually captured by two particular conditions on its congruences: they form a distributive lattice, and they permute, that is, the join of any pair of congruences is their relational composition. Any algebra whose congruences enjoy these two properties is called
arithmetic
, and Pixley showed that an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is arithmetic if and only if every tuple of congruences of
\mathbf{A}
is a CR tuple (see
[
10
]
). Since every ring has permutable congruences, it follows that any congruence-distributive ring is arithmetic, thus, Pixley’s theorem yields the Chinese Remainder Theorem for
\mathbf{Z}
. Other examples of arithmetic algebras are Boolean algebras, or more generally any algebra in a discriminator variety
[
4
]
, and residuated lattices
[
7
]
(e.g., Heyting algebras). On the other hand, arithmeticity is a very strong condition on an algebra, and there are many well-known structures that fail to be arithmetic and, thus, have tuples of congruences that are not CR tuples. For instance, in the three-element lattice with
0<\frac{1}{2}<1
the pair of congruences given by the partitions
\{\{0\},\{\frac{1}{2},1\}\}
and
\{\{0,\frac{1}{2}\},\{1\}\}
is not a CR tuple.

A natural computational problem that arises from this discussion is the following.

CRT

Instance
: A finite algebra
\mathbf{A}
and
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
congruences of
\mathbf{A}
.

Question
: Is
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}
?

In this paper we study the computational complexity of this problem and its restrictions to several familiar classes of algebras. We prove that
CRT
is coNP-complete (see Section
3
). We also show that there are polynomial-time algorithms that decide
CRT
for inputs in the following three classes of algebras: vector spaces (see Section
4.1
), distributive nearlattices (see Section
4.2
) and algebras belonging to some dual discriminator variety (see Section
4.3
). In each of the latter two cases the algorithm is powered by a purely algebraic characterization of CR tuples, which constitutes an interesting result in its own right. In particular, we obtain elegant characterizations of CR tuples in finite distributive lattices and Tarski algebras (Corollaries
4.5
and
4.6
). The article concludes (Section
5
) with the study of all varieties (i.e., classes defined by identities) generated by a two-element algebra. We were able to show that the restriction of
CRT
to each of these varieties is either coNP-complete or in P for all cases but for the variety of semilattices.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we fix some notation and terminology. We assume the reader is familiar with the basic concepts of Universal Algebra and Complexity Theory. For undefined notions see, e.g.,
[
4
,
8
]
.

An
algebra
is a model of a first-order language with no relation symbols. Algebras are usually denoted by boldface capital letters such as
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
, etc., whereas their underlying sets are denoted by the corresponding non-bold letters, e.g.,
A
is the universe of
\mathbf{A}
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra. A
congruence
of
\mathbf{A}
is an equivalence relation
\theta
on
A
compatible with the operations of
\mathbf{A}
, that is, for each positive integer
n
, each
n
-ary function symbol
f
and elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in A
such that
\langle a_{1},b_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle a_{n},b_{n}\rangle\in\theta
we have that
\langle f^{\mathbf{A}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),f^{\mathbf{A}}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\rangle\in\theta
. Since the set
\mathop{\mathrm{Con}}\mathbf{A}
of all congruences of
\mathbf{A}
is closed under arbitrary intersections, it has an associated complete lattice denoted by
\mathop{\mathbf{Con}}\mathbf{A}
. The least element in
\mathop{\mathbf{Con}}\mathbf{A}
, is the equality relation and is denoted by
\Delta_{A}
(or just
\Delta
if
A
is clear from the context). The join
\theta\vee\delta
of two congruences in
\mathop{\mathbf{Con}}\mathbf{A}
is the transitive closure of
\theta\cup\delta
.

A
congruence system
on
\mathbf{A}
is a tuple
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
where
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Con}}\mathbf{A}
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
and
\langle a_{i},a_{j}\rangle\in\theta_{i}\vee\theta_{j}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. A
solution
to the congruence system
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
is an element
a\in A
satisfying
\langle a,a_{i}\rangle\in\theta_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. A tuple of congruences
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is said to be a
Chinese Remainder tuple
(CR tuple for short) of
\mathbf{A}
provided that every system
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
has a solution.

In this article we investigate the complexity of the following computational problem.

CRT

Instance
: A finite algebra
\mathbf{A}
and
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
congruences of
\mathbf{A}
.

Question
: Is
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}
?

Throughout this work we assume a fixed encoding of finite algebras and congruences as strings of a finite alphabet. Since any reasonable choice of such an encoding makes no difference in the arguments below, we do not take the trouble to go into the low-level details, and the exposition is entirely presented at an abstract level.

All polynomial reductions in this article are Karp reductions, that is, many-one poly-time computable reductions.

Here is an easy observation.

Lemma 2.1
.

CRT
is in
coNP
.

Proof.

Note that the fact that
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is not a CR tuple of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is witnessed by a tuple
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle\in A^{k}
such that
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
is a system and
a_{1}/\theta_{1}\cap\ldots\cap a_{k}/\theta_{k}=\emptyset
. Since both these tests can be carried out in polynomial time with respect to the input, we are done.
∎

As we shall see in the next section,
CRT
is actually complete for
coNP
. Much of this article is concerned with identifying classes of algebras for which
CRT
is tractable. To this end it is helpful to introduce the following notation. Given a class of algebras
\mathcal{K}
, we write
CRT
|_{\mathcal{K}}
to denote the restriction of
CRT
to instances where the algebra
\mathbf{A}
is in
\mathcal{K}
. A particular type of class is prominent in the sequel. Recall that a
variety
is a class of algebras in the same language axiomatized by identities.

We close this section with a basic fact about CR tuples whose easy proof is left to the reader. Given congruences
\delta\subseteq\theta
of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
, recall that
\theta/\delta:=\{\langle a/\delta,b/\delta\rangle:\langle a,b\rangle\in\theta\}
is a congruence of
\mathbf{A}/\delta
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra, and let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
be congruences of
\mathbf{A}
. Put
\delta:=\theta_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\theta_{k}
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}
;

(b)

\langle\theta_{1}/\delta,\ldots,\theta_{k}/\delta\rangle
is a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}/\delta
.

3
CRT
is coNP-complete

In this section we establish the coNP-hardness of
CRT
by providing a reduction from a special form of
3SAT
to the complement of
CRT
.

Given a propositional formula
\varphi
let
\mathop{\mathrm{var}}(\varphi)
be the set of variables occurring in
\varphi
. If
\varphi
is in CNF, we write
\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)
for the set
\{\mathop{\mathrm{var}}(\gamma):\gamma\text{ is a clause of }\varphi\}
.

Let
3SAT
′
be the restriction of
3SAT
to input formulas
\varphi
satisfying the following:

(C1)

each clause of
\varphi
has three pairwise different variables,

(C2)

|\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)|\geq 5
,

(C3)

each variable of
\varphi
occurs in at least three distinct sets in
\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)
.

It is an easy exercise to show that
3SAT
′
is NP-complete.

We are now ready to present the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

CRT
is
coNP
-complete.

Proof.

We prove that
3SAT
′
is polynomially reducible to the complement of
CRT
.

Given an instance
\varphi
of
3SAT
′
we construct a set
S
and equivalence relations
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
on
S
such that
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a CR tuple if and only if
\varphi
is not satisfiable; more precisely, such that a system
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
is not solvable if and only if
\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
corresponds to an assignment making
\varphi
true.

Put
\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}:=\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)
; note that
k\geq 5
by (C
2
). For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
define

A_{i}:=\{a\in\{0,1\}^{V_{i}}:\gamma(a)=1\text{ for all clauses $\gamma$ in $\varphi$ with $\mathop{\mathrm{var}}(\varphi)=V_{i}$}\},

that is,
A_{i}
is the set of assignments to the variables in
V_{i}
that make all clauses of
\varphi
whose variables are in
V_{i}
true. Next, put

A:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}.

Given
a\in A
we write
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(a)
for the set of variables assigned by
a
; note that
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(a)\in\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)
. For a set
V
of variables and
a,b\in A
we say that
a
and
b
are
V
-compatible
, in symbols
a\uparrow_{V}b
, if they assign the same values to the variables in
V\cap\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(a)\cap\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(b)
. We simply write
a\uparrow b
when
a
and
b
agree on all variables in
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(a)\cap\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(b)
; in this case, we say that
a
and
b
are
compatible
. We are ready to define our base set; let

S:=\{\{a,b\}\subseteq A:a\uparrow b\}.

Observe that
\{a\}\in S
for every
a\in A
. In addition, if
\{a,b\}\in S
and
a\neq b
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(a)\neq\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\nolimits(b)
.

The final phase in the construction is to define the congruences. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
let

\theta_{i}:=\{\langle s,t\rangle\in S^{2}:s\cap t=\{a\}\text{ for some }a\in A_{i}\}\cup\{\langle s,s\rangle:s\in S\}.

(1)

We leave it to the reader to check that
\theta_{i}
is in fact an equivalence relation. This finishes the definition of our reduction. Again we leave to the reader the straightforward verification that the reduction is many-one poly-time.

We prove next that the reduction works. The argument is split into several claims, and the theorem follows from Claims
2
and
5
.

We say that a system
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
is
coherent
provided that
s_{i}\cap A_{i}\neq\emptyset
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
; a system is
incoherent
if it is not coherent.

Claim 1
.

Coherent systems are not solvable.

For the sake of contradiction suppose
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
is a coherent system with a solution
s
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
take
a_{i}
as the (unique) element in
s_{i}\cap A_{i}
, and observe that, since
s\mathrel{\theta_{i}}s_{i}
, it follows by (
1
) that
a_{i}\in s
. But, as the
a_{i}
’s are all different, we have that
|s|\geq k\geq 5
, which is clearly not possible. This finishes the proof of the claim.

Claim 2
.

If
\varphi
is satisfiable, then there is an unsolvable system.

By Claim
1
it suffices to check that, if
a
is an assignment that makes
\varphi
true, then

\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},\{a|_{V_{1}}\},\ldots,\{a|_{V_{k}}\}\rangle,

(2)

where
a|_{V_{i}}
stands for the restriction of
a
to
V_{i}
, is a a coherent system. Observe that for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
we have
a|_{V_{i}}\uparrow a|_{V_{j}}
, and thus

\{a|_{V_{i}}\}\mathrel{\theta_{i}}\{a|_{V_{i}},a|_{V_{j}}\}\mathrel{\theta_{j}}\{a|_{V_{j}}\}.

Hence (
2
) is a system which is clearly coherent.

For the next leg of the proof it is convenient to extend the compatibility relation to
S
. Given
s,t\in S
and a set of variables
V
we write
s\uparrow_{V}t
if
a\uparrow_{V}b
for all
a\in s
and
b\in t
.

Let us record two properties of the joins of the
\theta_{j}
’s needed in the sequel. Both facts follows routinely from the definitions, and we leave their proofs to the reader.

Claim 3
.

Let
s,t\in S
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
; the following holds:

(a)

If
s\cap A_{i}=\emptyset
,
s\neq t
and
s\mathrel{(\theta_{i}\vee\theta_{j})}t
, then
s\cap A_{j}\neq\emptyset
.

(b)

If
s\mathrel{(\theta_{i}\vee\theta_{j})}t
, then
s\uparrow_{V_{i}\cap V_{j}}t
.

Next, we establish the key property of incoherent systems.

Claim 4
.

Incoherent systems are solvable.

Suppose
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
is an incoherent system, that is, there is
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
s_{i}\cap A_{i}=\emptyset
. We may assume without loss that
s_{1}\cap A_{1}=\emptyset
. Note that Claim
3
.(a) implies that
s_{1}\cap A_{\ell}\neq\emptyset
for any
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
s_{\ell}\neq s_{1}
. Thus,

the set
I:=\{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:s_{\ell}\neq s_{1}\}
has at most two elements.

(3)

We prove that
s_{1}
is a solution to the system. Clearly it suffices to show that
s_{1}\mathrel{\theta_{i}}s_{i}
for
i\in I
. For clarity of exposition we provide the proof for the concrete case
I=\{2,3\}
. However, it should be clear that the arguments below apply seamlessly to the general case.

We start by observing that there are
a_{2}\in A_{2}
and
a_{3}\in A_{3}
such that

s_{1}=\{a_{2},a_{3}\}.

(4)

Also, in view of (
3
), the system is as follows:

\langle\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{1},\ldots,s_{1}\rangle.

(5)

So, to prove that
s_{1}
is a solution, it suffices to establish that
s_{1}\mathrel{\theta_{2}}s_{2}
and
s_{1}\mathrel{\theta_{3}}s_{3}
. We first show that
s_{2}\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Aiming for a contradiction suppose this does not hold. Then, invoking Claim
3
.(a), we have
s_{2}\cap A_{\ell}\neq\emptyset
for any
\ell
such that
s_{2}\neq s_{\ell}
. Looking at (
5
) we see that there are at least
k-2
indices
\ell
such that
s_{2}\neq s_{\ell}
. But this is not possible because it would entail that
|s_{2}|\geq 3
since
k\geq 5
. Hence,

there is
b_{2}\in s_{2}\cap A_{2}
.

(6)

We claim that
b_{2}=a_{2}
. In fact, suppose
p
is a variable in
V_{2}
. By the assumption (C3), there is
V_{j}\in\mathop{\mathrm{varsets}}(\varphi)
with
j\neq 2,3
such that
p\in V_{j}
. Now, the system condition says that
s_{1}=s_{j}\mathrel{(\theta_{2}\vee\theta_{j})}s_{2}
. Now, Claim
3
.(b) yields
s_{1}\uparrow_{V_{2}\cap V_{j}}s_{2}
, and as
p\in V_{2}\cap V_{j}
we have
a_{2}(p)=b_{2}(p)
. Since
p
is an arbitrary variable in
V_{2}
, we conclude that
a_{2}=b_{2}
. This fact in combination with (
4
) and (
6
) produces
s_{1}\cap s_{2}=\{a_{2}\}
, which in turn says that
s_{1}\mathrel{\theta_{2}}s_{2}
.

Obviously, the same line of reasoning shows that
s_{1}\mathrel{\theta_{3}}s_{3}
, and hence we conclude that
s_{1}
is in fact a solution to the system.

The remaining task to complete the proof of the theorem is to establish the following.

Claim 5
.

If there is an unsolvable system, then
\varphi
is satisfiable.

Suppose
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle
has no solution. By Claim
4
, the system must be coherent, and thus there is
a_{\ell}\in s_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}
for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Note that Claim
3
.(b) implies
a_{i}\uparrow_{V_{i}\cap V_{j}}a_{j}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, which says that the assignment
a\colon\mathop{\mathrm{var}}(\varphi)\to\{0,1\}
given by

a(p):=\begin{cases}a_{1}(p)&\text{if }p\in V_{1},\\
\quad\vdots\\
a_{k}(p)&\text{if }p\in V_{k},\end{cases}

is well defined. Finally, since for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
we have
a_{\ell}\in A_{\ell}
, the partial assignment
a_{\ell}
makes each clause of
\varphi
with variables in
V_{\ell}
true. Hence, the assignment
a
makes
\varphi
true.
∎

Recall that a
semigroup
is an algebra in the language
\{\cdot\}
satisfying the associativity identity
x\cdot(y\cdot z)\approx(x\cdot y)\cdot z
. Let
\mathcal{SG}
denote the class of semigroups. An immediate consequence of the previous theorem is the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

CRT
|_{\mathcal{SG}}
is
coNP
-complete.

Proof.

Observe that any set can be turned into a semigroup by adding the trivial product
x\cdot y:=x
. This product is preserved by all equivalence relations.
∎

4
Some tractable cases

In this section we show that the restriction of
CRT
to each of the following classes is in P:

•

vector spaces,

•

distributive nearlattices,

•

the class of algebras belonging to some dual discriminator variety.

It is worth mentioning that the provided polynomial algorithms are strongly dependent on the algebraic features of each of these classes, and thus each of them turns out to have a unique character.

4.1
Vector spaces

We present next a poly-time algorithm solving the restriction of
CRT
to vector spaces over finite fields. Our algorithm rides piggy-back on Gaussian elimination and takes advantage of the fact that vector spaces have a dimension.

Given a finite field
\mathbf{F}
, we consider
\mathbf{F}
-vector spaces as algebras in the language
\{+,-,0\}\cup\{\lambda_{r}:r\in F\}
, where each
\lambda_{r}
is a unary symbol for scalar multiplication by
r
. Let
\mathcal{VS}
denote the class of all finite vector spaces over finite fields.

Theorem 4.1
.

CRT
|_{\mathcal{VS}}
is in
P
.

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{VS}
and
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
congruences of
\mathbf{V}
, we can explicitly compute in polynomial time a finite field
\mathbf{F}
, a positive integer
n
, an isomorphim
\gamma\colon\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{F}^{n}
and subsets
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\subseteq F^{n}
such that for
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
we have that
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\in\theta_{i}
iff
\gamma(v_{1}-v_{2})\in W_{i}
. Thus,
\langle\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a CR tuple of
\mathbf{V}
if and only if

(*)

for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in F^{n}
such that
v_{i}-v_{j}\in W_{i}+W_{j}:=\{w+w^{\prime}:w\in W_{i},w^{\prime}\in W_{j}\}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, there is
z\in F^{n}
such that
z-v_{i}\in W_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.

We prove the theorem by showing that
(*)
is decidable in polynomial time from the input
\langle\mathbf{F}^{n},W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\rangle
.

Define

S:=\{\langle z+w_{1},\ldots,z+w_{k}\rangle:z\in F^{n},w_{1}\in W_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in W_{k}\}
,

T:=\{\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\rangle\in(F^{n})^{k}:v_{i}-v_{j}\in W_{i}+W_{j}\text{ for }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\}
.
Note that
S\subseteq T
and both are subuniverses of
(\mathbf{F}^{n})^{k}
. Further note that
S=T
if and only if
(*)
holds. Thus, since
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{T}
are vector spaces over
\mathbf{F}
, it suffices to check whether they have the same dimension.

Since
\dim\mathbf{W}=|W|/|F|
for any finite
\mathbf{F}
-vector space
\mathbf{W}
, the dimension of
\mathbf{S}
is poly-time computable by observing that

\dim\mathbf{S}=n+\dim\mathbf{W}_{1}+\ldots+\dim\mathbf{W}_{k}-\dim(\mathbf{W}_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\mathbf{W}_{k}).

Next, we show that
\dim\mathbf{T}
is also poly-time computable. It is a routine exercise to see that for each pair
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
i<j
, we can construct in polynomial time an
(n-\dim(\mathbf{W}_{i}+\mathbf{W}_{j}))\times n
matrix
A_{ij}
with entries in
F
such that
A_{ij}v=0
iff
v\in W_{i}+W_{j}
.
Next, using the
A_{ij}
’s as blocks, we can construct a matrix
A
such that for
\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\rangle\in(F^{n})^{k}
we have

\begin{bmatrix}\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet\\
\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet\\
\textbullet&\textbullet&A&\textbullet&\textbullet\\
\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet\\
\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet&\textbullet\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{12}(v_{1}-v_{2})\\
A_{13}(v_{1}-v_{3})\\
\vdots\\
A_{1k}(v_{1}-v_{k})\\
A_{23}(v_{2}-v_{3})\\
\vdots\\
A_{2k}(v_{2}-v_{k})\\
\vdots\\
A_{(k-1)k}(v_{k-1}-v_{k})\end{bmatrix}.

Since each of the
A_{ij}
’s is poly-time computable, it is clear we can construct
A
in polynomial time. Finally, observe that
T
agrees with the kernel of
A
, and thus we can compute the dimension of
\mathbf{T}
in polynomial time by applying Gaussian elimination to
A
.
∎

4.2
Distributive nearlattices

We deal now with distributive nearlattices, which constitute a common generalization of distributive lattices and Tarski algebras (see
[
1
]
for a definition). Since distributive nearlattices are unlikely to be familiar to the reader, we recall next their definition and some basic facts.

Let
\mathbf{2}_{\mathcal{N}}:=\langle\{0,1\},\mathsf{n}\rangle
where
\mathsf{n}
is the ternary operation defined by
\mathsf{n}(x,y,z):=(x\wedge y)\vee z
(of course,
\wedge
and
\vee
are the natural meet and join operation on the set
\{0,1\}
). Let
\mathcal{N}
be the variety generated by
\mathbf{2}_{\mathcal{N}}
. The algebras in
\mathcal{N}
are called
distributive nearlattices
.
It is known that this variety is congruence-distributive and
\mathbf{2}_{\mathcal{N}}
is, up to isomorphisms, the only subdirectly irreducible member of
\mathcal{N}
(
[
9
]
).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{N}
. Note that
\mathbf{A}
is a join-semilattice with the operation
x\vee y:=\mathsf{n}\langle x,x,y\rangle
satisfying that for all
a\in A
the up-set
[a)
is a distributive lattice under the order induced by
\vee
. In the sequel we freely refer to this order and we denote it by
\leq
. Also, given
a,b\in A
with a common lower bound we have that the meet
a\wedge b
exists. An element
p\in A
is
meet-irreducible
provided that
p
is not the top element of
\mathbf{A}
and whenever
p=a\wedge b
with
a,b\in A
, we have
p=a
or
p=b
. Replacing
=
by
\geq
in the previous sentence we get the definition of a
meet-prime
element. As for distributive lattices, an element is meet-irreducible if and only if it is meet-prime.

Until further notice we assume
\mathbf{A}
is finite. Denote by
P_{\mathbf{A}}
the set of meet-irreducible elements of
\mathbf{A}
, and let
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}
be the poset obtained by ordering
P_{\mathbf{A}}
by
\leq
. Let
D_{\mathbf{A}}
be the set of down-sets of
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}
. Of course,
D_{\mathbf{A}}
has a natural distributive lattice structure under the inclusion ordering. Hence, the operation
\mathsf{n}(x,y,z):=(x\cap y)\cup z
makes it into a distributive nearlattice, which we denote by
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Given
a\in A
define

\sigma(a):=\{p\in P_{\mathbf{A}}:p\not\geq a\}.

It is not hard to show that the map
a\mapsto\sigma(a)
is a nearlattice embedding from
\mathbf{A}
into
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}}
. We write
A^{\sigma}
for the range of
\sigma
and
\mathbf{A}^{\sigma}
for the corresponding substructure of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}}
. We shall need the following two facts, whose proofs we leave to the reader:

•

\mathbf{A}^{\sigma}
is an up-set of
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}}
;

•

\bigcap A^{\sigma}=\emptyset
.

We next address the congruences of
\mathbf{A}
. Given
p\in P_{\mathbf{A}}
let

\theta_{p}:=\{\langle a,b\rangle\in A^{2}:a\leq p\Leftrightarrow b\leq p\}.

A routine argument shows that
\theta_{p}
is a meet-irreducible congruence of
\mathbf{A}
, and that, furthermore, every meet-irreducible congruence of
\mathbf{A}
is of this form. Now, since
\mathbf{A}
is semisimple and
\mathop{\mathrm{Con}}\mathbf{A}
is distributive, we have a correspondence between congruences of
\mathbf{A}
and sets of meet-irreducible elements of
\mathbf{A}
. Thus, the map

\theta\mapsto F_{\theta}:=\{p\in P_{\mathbf{A}}:\theta\subseteq\theta_{p}\}.

is an order-reversing bijection from the congruence lattice of
\mathbf{A}

onto the powerset of
P
ordered by inclusion. The inverse of this map is given by

F\mapsto\theta_{F}:=\{\langle a,b\rangle\in A^{2}:\sigma(a)\cap F=\sigma(b)\cap F\}.

In particular we have
\theta_{F}\vee\theta_{F^{\prime}}=\theta_{F\cap F^{\prime}}

and
\theta_{F}\cap\theta_{F^{\prime}}=\theta_{F\cup F^{\prime}}
, which entails
the following easy observation:
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle

is a system on
\mathbf{A}
if and only if

\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{i}\cap F_{j}=\sigma(a_{j})\cap F_{i}\cap F_{j}\text{ for }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

(7)

where
F_{i}:=F_{\theta_{i}}
. In addition, an element
a
is a
solution for the system iff

\sigma(a)\cap F_{i}=\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{i}\text{ for }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

(8)

The next lemma characterizes the solvable systems.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite algebra in
\mathcal{N}
, and let
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
be a system on
\mathbf{A}
such that
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}=\Delta
. Then, the set

s:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{\theta_{i}}

is the unique subset

of

satisfying

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Thus, the system is solvable if and
only if

.

Proof.

Put
F_{i}:=F_{\theta_{i}}
for

. Observe that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}F_{i}=P_{\mathbf{A}}
since
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}=\Delta
. We check first that

satisfies
s\cap F_{i}=\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{i}

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and note
that:

\displaystyle s\cap F_{j}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{i}\cap F_{j}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{j})\cap F_{i}\cap F_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma(a_{j})\cap F_{j}.

Next, suppose
x\subseteq P_{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies

for all

. Then,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}x\cap F_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(a_{i})\cap F_{i}
,
which produces

.
∎

Next we single out some special elements in
D_{\mathbf{A}}
(recall that
D_{\mathbf{A}}

stands for the set of down-sets of
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}
). Let

and

, we say that:

•

is a

if

is maximal (with respect to inclusion) in

•

is

-interpolable

if there is

such that

.

It follows from our definitions and the fact that
A^{\sigma}
is an up-set of

that if

is
F
-interpolable
by
\mathbf{A}
and

is such that

, then

is
F
-interpolable by
\mathbf{A}
.

We are now in a position to characterize CR tuples.

4.3
Dual discriminator varieties

The class we consider next, albeit natural, takes some work to define. It is the aggregate of all dual discriminator varieties.

Recall that the
dual discriminator
on a set
A
is the function
q_{A}\colon A^{3}\to A
given by

q_{A}(a,b,c):=\begin{cases}a&\text{ if }a=b,\\
c&\text{ if }a\neq b.\end{cases}

A ternary term is a
dual discriminator term
for an algebra
\mathbf{A}
provided that it interprets as the dual discriminator function on
\mathbf{A}
. A variety
\mathcal{V}
is a
dual discriminator variety
if it is generated as a variety by a subclass
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with a common dual discriminator term. If
\mathcal{V}
is a dual discriminator variety, then
\mathcal{V}
is semisimple and its class of simple members is the largest subclass of
\mathcal{V}
possessing a common dual discriminator term (see
[
6
]
).

A standard example of a dual discriminator
variety is the variety of distributive lattices. In this case we can take
\mathcal{K}
to be the class of two-element lattices, and the term

(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z)
interprets as the dual discriminator on
\mathcal{K}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the class of finite algebras belonging to some dual discriminator variety. In this section we prove that
CRT
|_{\mathcal{D}}
is in P (see Theorem
4.18
). This is accomplished through a deep analysis of congruence systems for algebras in
\mathcal{D}
, which produces a nontrivial characterization of CR tuples for algebras in
\mathcal{D}
(see Theorem
4.16
).

4.3.1
Crosses in products

One of the key features of dual discriminator varieties is that the subalgebras of twofold products of simple algebras come in three flavors: graphs of homomorphisms, subproducts and crosses (see Lemma
4.11
). Our study of congruence systems in
\mathcal{D}
is carried out by a purely combinatorial analysis of the interaction of crosses. This analysis is presented in this section and applied to congruence systems in the next section.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be sets. A subset

is a

(
of

) provided that there are

with

,

and

such that

We denote this cross by
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
;

and

are the
indices
of

.

Two crosses
C
and

are

if they share exactly one index, say

, and the elements corresponding to

in
C
and

are different. For example, the crosses
{{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}
and
{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}
are compatible if and only if

and

.

We define the operation of

between compatible crosses by

{{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}\circ{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}:={{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}.

Given a set of crosses
\mathcal{C}
, let

be

\mathcal{C}\cup\{C\circ D:\text{ compatible }C,D\in\mathcal{C}\},

and define

\bar{\mathcal{C}}:=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{C}^{\circ}\cup\mathcal{C}^{\circ\circ}\cup\ldots

Until further notice we fix sets

with at least two elements and put

.

Lemma 4.7
.

Given a set of crosses
\mathcal{C}
of

This is an easy induction.

If

and

for the twofold projection of

, that is,

.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of crosses of

, and put

Suppose first that neither of the equalities

, which implies that

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of crosses of
P
. A cross

-reducible

Here is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of crosses of
P
, and put
S:=\bigcap\mathcal{C}
. Suppose that for each
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
we have that either
S_{ij}=A_{i}\times A_{j}
or
S_{ij}
is a cross of
A_{i}\times A_{j}
.

1.

For any cross
C
of
P
we have that
S\subseteq C
iff
C\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
.

2.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the set of
\mathcal{C}
-irreducible crosses, and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{C}}
. Then,
\bar{\mathcal{B}}=\bar{\mathcal{C}}
iff
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

We start by defining the directed graph
\mathbf{G}:=\langle G^{+}\cup G^{-},E\cup F\rangle
with

•

G^{+}:=\{ia:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},a\in A_{i}\}
,

•

G^{-}:=\{\neg ia:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},a\in A_{i}\}
,

•

E:=\{\langle ia,\neg ia^{\prime}\rangle:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},a,a^{\prime}\in A_{i},a\neq a^{\prime}\}
,

•

F:=\{\langle\neg ia,jb\rangle:{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{C}\}
.

We also set
G:=G^{+}\cup G^{-}
. Given
g,g^{\prime}\in G
we write
g\to g^{\prime}
if
\langle g,g^{\prime}\rangle\in E\cup F
and
g\rightsquigarrow g^{\prime}
if
g=g^{\prime}
or there is a directed path from
g
to
g^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{G}
. We also put
\neg g:=\neg ia
if
g=ia
and
\neg g:=ia
if
g=\neg ia
.

The proof of the theorem follows from a series of claims. We start with an easy observation.

Claim 6
.

If
g\rightsquigarrow g^{\prime}
, then
\neg g^{\prime}\rightsquigarrow\neg g
.

For a subset
X\subseteq G
let
X^{\rightsquigarrow}:=\{g\in G:\text{ there is }x\in X\text{ with }x\rightsquigarrow g\}
. We say that
X
is
inconsistent
if there is
g\in G
such that
g,\neg g\in X^{\rightsquigarrow}
;
X
is
consistent
if it is not inconsistent.

Next for
\bar{a}\in P
let
X_{\bar{a}}:=\{ia_{i}:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}
.

Claim 7
.

For all
\bar{a}\in P
we have that
\bar{a}\in S
if and only if
X_{\bar{a}}
is consistent.

For the forward implication let
Y:=\{\neg ia^{\prime}:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},a^{\prime}\in A_{i}\setminus\{a_{i}\}\}
. Note that
Y
is the set of nodes that can be reached from
X_{\bar{a}}
in exactly one step. Thus,
X_{\bar{a}}^{\rightsquigarrow}=X_{\bar{a}}\cup Y^{\rightsquigarrow}
. Note that
Y\subseteq G^{-}
, so if
\neg ia^{\prime}\in Y
and
\neg ia^{\prime}\to g
, the edge
\langle\neg ia^{\prime},g\rangle
must belong to
F
. Hence, there is a cross
{{{a^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
g=jb
. Since
\bar{a}\in S\subseteq{{{a^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
and
a_{i}\neq a^{\prime}
, we have that
a_{j}=b
, that is,
g\in X_{\bar{a}}
. This proves that
X_{\bar{a}}^{\rightsquigarrow}=X_{\bar{a}}\cup Y
, so
X_{\bar{a}}
is consistent.

For the converse assume
X_{\bar{a}}
is consistent and let
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{C}
. Suppose
a_{i}\neq a
. Then, we have a path
ia_{i}\to\neg ia\to jb
in
\mathbf{G}
, and thus
a_{j}=b
because otherwise
X_{\bar{a}}
would be inconsistent. This proves that
\bar{a}\in{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
.

Claim 8
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
a\in A_{i}
we have that
\{ia\}
is consistent.

Note that, by hypothesis, the
i
-th canonical projection of
S
is onto
A_{i}
. Thus, there is
\bar{a}\in S
such that
a_{i}=a
, and by Claim
7
,
X_{\bar{a}}
is consistent.

Claim 9
.

If
X\subseteq G^{+}
is inconsistent, there are
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y
and
x\rightsquigarrow\neg y
.

Since
X
is inconsistent, there is
g\in G
such that
g,\neg g\in X^{\rightsquigarrow}
, that is, there are
x,y\in X
with
x\rightsquigarrow g
and
y\rightsquigarrow\neg g
. By Claim
8
,
x\neq y
, and by Claim
6
,
g\rightsquigarrow\neg y
, and so
x\rightsquigarrow\neg y
.

Claim 10
.

If
X\subseteq G^{+}
is consistent, there is
\bar{a}\in S
such that
X\subseteq X_{\bar{a}}
.

Let
X\subseteq G^{+}
be consistent, and suppose
Z
is maximal among the consistent subsets of
G^{+}
containing
X
. Observe that
Z=Z^{\rightsquigarrow}\cap G^{+}
by maximality. Let
I:=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:ia\in Z\text{ for some }a\in A_{i}\}
; we claim that
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose there is
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus I
. Fix
b\in A_{j}
and note that
Z\cup\{jb\}
is inconsistent. By Claim
9
, there must be
ia\in Z
such that
ia\rightsquigarrow\neg jb
. Since the only edges incoming to
\neg jb
are of the form
\langle jb^{\prime},\neg jb\rangle
, there must be
b^{\prime}\in A_{j}
such that
ia\rightsquigarrow jb^{\prime}
. Hence,
jb^{\prime}\in Z^{\rightsquigarrow}\cap G^{+}=Z
, a contradiction.

Finally, note that for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
there is exactly one
a_{i}\in A_{i}
such that
ia_{i}\in Z
, because otherwise
Z
would be inconsistent. Thus,
Z=X_{\bar{a}}
, and by Claim
7
we have
\bar{a}\in S
.

For
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{C}}
define
F_{\mathcal{B}}:=\{\langle\neg ia,jb\rangle:{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{B}\}
. Given
g,g^{\prime}\in G
we say that there is a
\mathcal{B}
-path
from
g
to
g^{\prime}
, and write
g\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\rightsquigarrow}g^{\prime}
, if there is a path in
\mathbf{G}
from
g
to
g^{\prime}
with edges in
E\cup F_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Claim 11
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{C}}
,
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
i\neq j
, and
a\in A_{i}
,
b\in A_{j}
. Then,
\neg ia\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\rightsquigarrow}jb
if and only if
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
.

The direction from right to left is a straightforward induction and therefore left to the reader. We prove the remaining implication. Suppose
\neg ia\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\rightsquigarrow}jb
. We proceed by induction on the length of the
\mathcal{B}
-path from
\neg ia
to
jb
. If this length is one, the claim follows from the definition of
\mathcal{B}
-path. So suppose there are
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, with
k\neq i
, and
c,c^{\prime}\in A_{k}
, with
c\neq c^{\prime}
, such that
\neg ia\to kc\to\neg kc^{\prime}\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\rightsquigarrow}jb
with
\langle\neg ia,kc\rangle\in F_{\mathcal{B}}
. Clearly, we can assume without loss that
kc\neq jb
. Observe that
k\neq j
. Otherwise,
jc\to\neg jc^{\prime}\rightsquigarrow jb\to\neg jc
, and
\{jc\}
would be inconsistent in contradiction with Claim
8
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis, the cross
{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
. Furthermore, since
\langle\neg ia,kc\rangle\in F_{\mathcal{B}}
, we have
{{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}\in\mathcal{B}
. Finally, as these crosses are compatible,
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}={{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}\circ{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
.

Claim 12
.

If
C
is a cross of
P
such that
S\subseteq C
, then
C\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
.

Let
C:={{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
be a cross of
P
such that
S\subseteq C
. Take
a^{\prime}\in A_{i}\setminus\{a\}
and
b^{\prime}\in A_{j}\setminus\{b\}
. Observe that, by Claim
10
, the set
\{ia^{\prime},jb^{\prime}\}
must be inconsistent. Now, by Claim
9
, we have
ia^{\prime}\rightsquigarrow\neg jb^{\prime}
. Thus, there are
a^{\prime\prime}\in A_{i}
and
b^{\prime\prime}\in A_{j}
such that
ia^{\prime}\to\neg ia^{\prime\prime}\rightsquigarrow jb^{\prime\prime}\to\neg jb^{\prime}
. Using Claim
11
, the cross
{{{a^{\prime\prime}}\,{b^{\prime\prime}}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence, by Lemma
4.7
, we have that
S\subseteq{{{a^{\prime\prime}}\,{b^{\prime\prime}}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
, and thus
S\subseteq S^{\prime}:={{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\cap{{{a^{\prime\prime}}\,{b^{\prime\prime}}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
. By Lemma
4.8
,
a=a^{\prime\prime}
and
b=b^{\prime\prime}
.

This takes care of 1. since the above claim is the direction from left to right and the other direction follows from Lemma
4.7
. We complete the proof with the following three claims.

Claim 13
.

There are no directed cycles in
\mathbf{G}
.

For the sake of contradiction suppose there is a directed cycle from
ia
to
ia
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
a\in A_{i}
(the case where the cycle starts and ends in
G^{-}
is symmetrical in view of Claim
6
). By the definition of
\mathbf{G}
there are
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
j\neq i
,
a^{\prime}\in A_{i}
and
b,b^{\prime}\in A_{j}
with
a\neq a^{\prime}
and
b\neq b^{\prime}
such that
ia\to\neg ia^{\prime}\to jb^{\prime}\to\neg jb\rightsquigarrow ia
. Thus, by Claim
11
, we have
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}},{{{a^{\prime}}\,{b^{\prime}}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, and by 1.  this entails
S\subseteq{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\cap{{{a^{\prime}}\,{b^{\prime}}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
. This is a contradiction in view of Lemma
4.8
.

Claim 14
.

\bar{\mathcal{I}}=\bar{\mathcal{C}}
.

The inclusion
\subseteq
is obvious. Take
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, note that
\neg ia\rightsquigarrow jb
. Since
\mathbf{G}
is acyclic, we can take a path
\pi
of maximum length from
\neg ia
to
jb
. Note that
\pi
must be an
\mathcal{I}
-path because otherwise
\pi
could be lengthened. Now, by Claim
11
, we have that
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{I}}
.

Claim 15
.

If
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{C}}
is such that
\bar{\mathcal{B}}=\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, then
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.

Take
C\in\mathcal{I}
. Note that
C\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
and, since
C
is
\mathcal{C}
-irreducible, it cannot belong to
\bar{\mathcal{B}}\setminus\mathcal{B}
.

Finally, observe that the direction from left to right in 2. is Claim
15
and the other direction follows from Claim
14
.
∎

4.3.2
Congruence systems in
\mathcal{D}

Next we apply the combinatorial analysis in the previous section to investigate congruence systems in
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra and let
M(x,y,z)
be a ternary term in the language of
\mathbf{A}
. Recall that
M
is a
majority term
for
\mathbf{A}
provided that
\mathbf{A}\vDash M(x,x,y)=M(x,y,x)=M(y,x,x)=x
. Note also that any dual discriminator term for
\mathbf{A}
is a majority term.

Lemma 4.10

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
, with
n\geq 2
, be algebras with a common majority term, and let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
be finite subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
. Then, if
A_{ij}\subseteq B_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
, we have
A\subseteq B
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a subalgebra of a product
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, and let
\pi_{i}|_{A}\colon A\to A_{i}
be given by
\pi_{i}|_{A}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):=a_{i}
. The algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a
subdirect subalgebra
(of
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
) provided that each
\pi_{i}|_{A}
is onto. In this context, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we write
\rho_{i}
to denote the kernel of
\pi_{i}|_{A}
, that is,

\rho_{i}:=\{\langle\bar{a},\bar{b}\rangle\in A^{2}:a_{i}=b_{i}\}.

A subdirect algebra
\mathbf{A}
of
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
is
irredundant
, in symbols
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, if for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have that
\rho_{i}\subseteq\rho_{j}
implies
i=j
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be algebras with a common dual discriminator term. Let
\mathbf{S}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
. One of the following holds:

1.

S=A\times B
.

2.

S=(\{a\}\times B)\cup(A\times\{b\})
for some
a\in A
and
b\in B
.

Proof.

The result follows from
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
by observing that
S
cannot be the graph of a homomorphism since
\mathbf{S}
is irredundant.
∎

The combination of Lemmas
4.10
and
4.11
provides a powerful tool to handle subproducts in dual discriminator varieties, which, together with Theorem
4.9
, allows us to fully unravel the relationship between subproducts and sets of crosses.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, and suppose there is a common dual discriminator term for
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{A}
be the set of crosses of
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
containing
A
. The following holds:

1.

\bigcap\mathcal{C}_{A}=A
.

2.

\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{A}=\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

3.

For
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{A}
the following are equivalent:

(a)

\bigcap\mathcal{B}=A
.

(b)

\bar{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

(c)

Each
\mathcal{C}_{A}
-irreducible cross is in
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

1. Put
S:=\bigcap\mathcal{C}_{A}
; by Lemma
4.10
, it suffices to show that
S_{ij}=A_{ij}
for all
i,j
. Fix
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
. If
A_{ij}=A_{i}\times A_{j}
, clearly
A_{ij}=S_{ij}
since
A\subseteq S
. Otherwise, by Lemma
4.11
, there are
a\in A_{i}
and
b\in A_{j}
such that
A_{ij}=(\{a\}\times A_{j})\cup(A_{i}\times\{b\})
. Thus,
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
and we are done.

2. This follows from 1. and Lemma
4.7
.

3. (a)
\Rightarrow
(b) Clearly
\bar{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{A}=\mathcal{C}_{A}
. For the other inclusion, take a cross
C\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
. So
\bigcap\mathcal{B}=A\subseteq C
, and by item 1. of Theorem
4.9
with
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}
we have
C\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a) This follows from 1. and Lemma
4.7
.

(b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) Since
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{A}=\mathcal{C}_{A}
, the equivalence follows from item 2. of Theorem
4.9
with
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{A}
.
∎

We now turn to apply the previous results to the study of congruence systems in
\mathcal{D}
. We need the following well-known fact about congruences of semisimple congruence-distributive algebras. We include a sketch of the proof for convenience of the reader.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
be a congruence distributive algebra and suppose
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
are simple. The map

\theta\mapsto F_{\theta}:=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:a_{i}=b_{i}\text{ for all }\langle a,b\rangle\in\theta\}

is a dual lattice-isomorphism from
\mathop{\mathrm{Con}}\mathbf{A}
onto the power set of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
ordered by inclusion, whose inverse is

F\mapsto\theta_{F}:=\{\langle a,b\rangle\in A^{2}:a_{i}=b_{i}\text{ for all }i\in F\}.

Proof.

From
[
5
]
we know that, if
\theta
is a congruence of
\mathbf{A}\leq_{sd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
and
\mathbf{A}
is congruence distributive, then there are unique congruences
\theta_{j}
of
\mathbf{A}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\theta=\langle\theta_{1}\times\ldots\times\theta_{n}\rangle\cap A^{2}
. (Here
\theta_{1}\times\ldots\times\theta_{n}:=\{\langle\bar{a},\bar{b}\rangle\in(A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}\rangle^{2}:\langle a_{i},b_{i}\rangle\in\theta_{i}\text{ for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}
.) Now, as each
\mathbf{A}_{j}
is simple, the only choices for
\theta_{j}
are the diagonal and total congruences of
\mathbf{A}_{j}
. From here the proof of the lemma follows easily.
∎

The notation
\theta_{F},F_{\theta}
is used in the sequel whenever the hypotheses of Lemma
4.13
hold.

Given
A\subseteq A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
and
F\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
we define

A_{F}:=\{b\in A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}:\text{ there is }a\in A\text{ with }a|_{F}=b|_{F}\}.

Also, for
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
let

A_{\mathcal{F}}:=\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}.

Note that
A\subseteq A_{F}
for any
F\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and, if
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
are algebras and
A
is a subuniverse of
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, then
A_{F}
is a subuniverse as well.

Next we present a first characterization of CR tuples for algebras in
\mathcal{D}
in terms of crosses.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, with
n\geq 2
, where
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
have a common dual discriminator term. Let
\mathcal{C}_{A}
be the set of crosses of
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
containing
A
.

1.

For all
F\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
we have

A_{F}\subseteq C\text{ iff both indices of $C$ belong to $F$.}

2.

Let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
be congruences of
\mathbf{A}
such that
\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{k}\theta_{\ell}=\Delta
, and put
\mathcal{F}:=\{F_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,F_{\theta_{k}}\}
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}
,

(b)

A_{\mathcal{F}}=A
.

(c)

For every
\mathcal{C}_{A}
-irreducible cross
C
, there is
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that both indices of
C
belong to
F
.

Proof.

1. Fix
F\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
C:={{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
. The direction from right to left is immediate. For the other direction, we show that if
i\notin F
, a contradiction arises. Note that, since
n\geq 2
and
\mathbf{A}
is irredundant, each of
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
is nontrivial. In particular, since
\mathbf{A}
is subdirect, there is
\bar{a}\in A
with
a_{j}\neq b
. Let
a^{\prime}\in A_{i}\setminus\{a\}
, and let
\bar{c}\in A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
be such that
c_{i}=a^{\prime}
and
c_{t}=a_{t}
for
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i\}
. Note that
\bar{c}\in A_{F}\setminus C
, a contradiction.

2. To improve readability we write
F_{\ell}
instead of
F_{\theta_{\ell}}
for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Note that Lemma
4.13
says that
\bigcup\mathcal{F}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. By the same lemma, for
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in A^{k}
we have that

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{1},

\displaystyle\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle\text{ is a system }

\displaystyle\text{ iff }a_{\ell}|_{F_{\ell}\cap F_{m}}=a_{m}|_{F_{\ell}\cap F_{m}}\text{ for }\ell,m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

(9)

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b) Take

, and observe that, by definition of
A_{\mathcal{F}}
, there are

such that

for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. So
a_{\ell}|_{F_{\ell}\cap F_{m}}=b|_{F_{\ell}\cap F_{m}}=a_{m}|_{F_{\ell}\cap F_{m}}
for
\ell,m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and thus,
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
is a system on
\mathbf{A}
by (
9
). By hypothesis, there is a solution

to this system, which, by Lemma

, implies that

for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Since
\bigcup\mathcal{F}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, this says that

.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a) Suppose
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
is a system on
\mathbf{A}
. By (

) there is (a unique)

such that

for

. Hence,

. Using (

) again we see that
b
is a solution to
\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle
.

To prove the equivalence between (b) and (c) we first establish the following.

Claim 16
.

Let

\mathcal{B}:=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{A}:\text{there is $F\in\mathcal{F}$ such that both indices of $C$ belong to $F$}\}.

We have that
\bigcap\mathcal{B}=A_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Fix
F\in\mathcal{F}
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{F}:=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{A}:\text{both indices of $C$ belong to $F$}\}
. Item 1. says that
\mathcal{B}_{F}
is the set of crosses of
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
that contain
A_{F}
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is an irredundant subdirect product of the
\mathbf{A}_{i}
’s, so is any extension of
\mathbf{A}
in
\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, in particular
\mathbf{A}_{F}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.12
applied to
\mathbf{A}_{F}
, we have
\bigcap\mathcal{B}_{F}=A_{F}
. Hence, since
\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{B}_{F}=\mathcal{B}
, we have
\bigcap\mathcal{B}=A_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Finally, in view of Claim
16
, the equivalence (b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) follows at once from the equivalence (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) of Lemma
4.12
.(3).
∎

The final stretch to Theorem
4.16
is achieved by translating condition
(c)
in Lemma
4.14
into a purely congruential property.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
, and suppose there is a common dual discriminator term for
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{A}
be the set of crosses of
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
containing
A
. The following holds for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
:

1.

There is a cross in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
with indices
i,j
iff
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
do not permute.

2.

Suppose
C\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
is a cross with indices
i,j
, then
C
is
\mathcal{C}_{A}
-reducible iff there is
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}
such that
\rho_{k}\subseteq\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}
.

Proof.

1. Note that, since
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
are maximal congruences of
\mathbf{A}
, we have that
\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}=\rho_{j}\circ\rho_{i}
iff
\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}=\nabla
. Now, it is easy to see that
\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}=\nabla
iff
A_{ij}=A_{i}\times A_{j}
. So, by Lemma
4.11
, we are done.

2. The implication from left to right is an easy exercise; we prove the remaining implication. Suppose there is
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}
such that
\rho_{k}\subseteq\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}
. Note that
\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{k}\subseteq\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}
; thus
\rho_{i}
cannot permute with
\rho_{k}
because otherwise
\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{k}=\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}=\nabla
, and this is contradicts the fact that
A
is contained in
C
. Analogously,
\rho_{k}
and
\rho_{j}
do not permute, and by 1. there are crosses
{{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}
and
{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}
in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
for some
a\in A_{i}
,
b\in A_{j}
,
c,c^{\prime}\in A_{k}
. We claim that if
c=c^{\prime}
, then
A_{ij}=A_{i}\times A_{j}
. In fact, suppose
c=c^{\prime}
and take
x\in A_{i}
,
y\in A_{j}
; so
\langle x,c\rangle\in A_{ik}
and
\langle c,y\rangle\in A_{kj}
. Now, from the fact that
\rho_{k}\subseteq\rho_{i}\circ\rho_{j}
it follow that
\langle x,y\rangle\in A_{ij}
, finishing the proof of the claim. Since by assumption
A_{ij}
is a cross, we must have
c\neq c^{\prime}
. Hence,
{{{a}\,{c}}\choose{{i}\,{k}}}
and
{{{c^{\prime}}\,{b}}\choose{{k}\,{j}}}
are compatible, and their composition
{{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
is in
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{A}
and thus in
\mathcal{C}_{A}
by 2. from Lemma
4.12
. Finally, by Lemma
4.8
, we have that
C={{{a}\,{b}}\choose{{i}\,{j}}}
is
\mathcal{C}_{A}
-reducible.
∎

We are now in a position to present the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra in
\mathcal{D}
, and let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
be congruences of
\mathbf{A}
. Let
\Sigma
be the set of meet irreducible congruences of
\mathbf{A}
that contain
\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}\theta_{j}
. The following are equivalent:

1.

\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a CR tuple of
\mathbf{A}
,

2.

for all
\lambda,\mu\in\Sigma
one of the following conditions holds:

•

\lambda\circ\mu=\mu\circ\lambda
,

•

there is
\sigma\in\Sigma\setminus\{\lambda,\mu\}
with
\sigma\subseteq\lambda\circ\mu
,

•

there is
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
\theta_{j}\subseteq\lambda\cap\mu
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
and the Correspondence Theorem it suffices to show that the theorem holds under the additional assumption
\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{k}\theta_{\ell}=\Delta
. By Birkhoff’s subdirect decomposition theorem we may assume without loss that
\mathbf{A}\leq_{isd}\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathbf{A}_{n}
with
\Sigma=\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
. (The irredundancy follows from the fact that each
\rho_{i}
is a maximal congruence of
\mathbf{A}
.) Let
\mathcal{F}:=\{F_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,F_{\theta_{k}}\}
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{A}
be the set of crosses of
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
containing
A
. Then, by Lemma
4.14
.(2), item (1) is equivalent to

(2^{\prime})

For every
\mathcal{C}_{A}
-irreducible cross
C
, there is
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that both indices of
C
belong to
F
.

Finally,
(2^{\prime})
easily translates to (2) using Lemma
4.15
.
∎

Since finite distributive lattice are included in
\mathcal{D}
, Theorem
4.16
immediately yields a characterization of CR tuples for these structures. An entertaining exercise is to directly derive Corollary
4.5
from Theorem
4.16
.

4.3.3
CRT
|_{\mathcal{D}}
is poly-time computable

We now employ Theorem
4.16
to characterize the complexity of
CRT
|_{\mathcal{D}}
. First, a useful lemma.

Lemma 4.17
.

There is a poly-time algorithm that, given a finite congruence-distributive algebra
\mathbf{A}
as input, returns the set of meet-irreducible congruences of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

We start by observing that the set
J
of join-irreducible congruences
of
\mathbf{A}
can be computed in poly-time. Recall that given
a,b\in A
the
principal congruence

\mathbf{\theta}^{\mathbf{A}}(a,b)
is the last congruence of
\mathbf{A}
containing
\langle a,b\rangle
. We know from
[
3
]
that
\theta^{\mathbf{A}}(a,b)
is poly-time
computable from
\mathbf{A}
. Of course, each join-irreducible congruence
of
\mathbf{A}
is principal, thus to compute
J
, we first compute
the set
P:=\{\theta^{\mathbf{A}}(a,b):a,b\in A\}
, and then check
for each
\theta\in P
whether it is join-irreducible via the test

\theta>\bigvee\{\delta\in P:\delta<\theta\}
. Next, given
\theta\in J

let
\theta^{\prime}:=\bigvee\{\delta\in J:\delta\nleq\theta\}
; clearly
each
\theta^{\prime}
is computable in poly-time. Finally, since
\con\mathbf{A}

is a distributive lattice, we have that
\{\theta^{\prime}:\theta\in J\}

is exactly the set of meet-irreducible congruences of
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Theorem 4.18
.

CRT
|_{\mathcal{D}}
is in
P
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
,
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
be an instance of
CRT
|_{\mathcal{D}}
. By Lemma
4.17
the set
\Sigma
of meet irreducible congruences of
\mathbf{A}
that contain
\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}\theta_{j}
is computable in poly-time. Finally, observe that the conditions stated in 2. of Theorem
4.16
are easily poly-time computable once
\Sigma
is known.
∎

5
(Almost) Dichotomy for Varieties Generated by Two-Element Algebras

In this final section we study the complexity of
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}}
for an arbitrary variety
\mathcal{V}
generated by a two-element algebra. This is possible in part due to a result by Post
[
11
]
, that shows that (up to a term-equivalence) there are countable many of these varieties and provides concrete descriptions for each of them. We fall slightly short of establishing a dichotomy, as we are able to show that each of the cases is either tractable or coNP-complete, with the exception of the case of semilattices, which is left as an open problem. As we shall see, the heavy lifting to obtain the results below is already done, since they follow with little extra effort from the content of the previous sections leveraged by Post’s classification and the concept of interpretation between varieties.

Let
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
be varieties in languages
L,L^{\prime}
, respectively. An
interpretation
of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is a mapping
T
from
L
into
L^{\prime}
-terms satisfying:

•

if
c
in
L
is a constant symbol, then
T(c)
is a unary
L^{\prime}
-term such that
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\vDash T(c)(x)\approx T(c)(y)
;

•

if
f
in
L
is a function symbol of arity
n>0
, then
T(f)
is an
n
-ary
L^{\prime}
-term;

•

for every
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
the algebra
\mathbf{A}^{T}:=\langle A,T(s)^{\mathbf{A}}\rangle_{s\in L}
is in
\mathcal{V}
. (For constant
c\in L
the operation
T(c)^{\mathbf{A}}
is the nullary operation on
A
corresponding to the value of
T(c)
in
\mathbf{A}
).

The varieties
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
are said to be
term-equivalent
if there are interpretations
T
of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and
S
of
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{V}
such that
(\mathbf{A}^{T})^{S}=\mathbf{A}
for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and
(\mathbf{A}^{S})^{T}=\mathbf{A}
for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{V}
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is a variety in the language
L
and
c
is a constant symbol not in
L
, we write
\mathcal{V}(c)
to denote the variety in the language
L\cup\{c\}
where no restrictions are imposed on the new constant.

Given computational problems
X
and
Y
we write
X

\leq_{P}

Y
whenever there is a poly-time many-one reduction from
X
to
Y
.

The relevance of interpretations for our task lies in the fact that they instantly produce polynomial reductions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
be varieties.

(a)

If there is an interpretation of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, then
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}

\leq_{P}

CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}}
.

(b)

CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}}

\leq_{P}

CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}(c)}
.

Proof.

(a). If
T
is an interpretation of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, then the map
\langle\mathbf{A},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle\mapsto\langle\mathbf{A}^{T},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
is a polynomial reduction from
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}
to
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}}
.

(b). The map that takes
\langle\mathbf{A},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
to
\langle\langle\mathbf{A},a\rangle,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
, where
a
is arbitrarily chosen in
A
, is a polynomial reduction from
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}}
to
CRT
|_{\mathcal{V}(c)}
.
∎

Next we introduce the varieties that perform as the main actors in our analysis, by specifying two-element algebras generating them. Let
2:=\{0,1\}
. We write
\neg,\wedge,\vee,+
to denote the usual operations on
2
, and define the following ternary operations on 2:

•

\mathsf{s}(x,y,z):=x+y+z
,

•

\mathsf{n}(x,y,z):=(x\wedge y)\vee z
,

•

\mathsf{m}(x,y,z):=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z)
.

Given an algebra
\mathbf{A}
, let
V(\mathbf{A})
denote the variety generated by
\mathbf{A}
. Define:

•

\mathcal{S}:=V(2)
,

•

\mathcal{U}:=V(\langle 2,\neg,0,1\rangle)
,

•

\mathcal{A}:=V(\langle 2,\mathsf{s}\rangle)
,

•

\mathcal{N}:=V(\langle 2,\mathsf{n}\rangle)
,

•

\mathcal{M}:=V(\langle 2,\mathsf{m}\rangle)
,

•

\mathcal{J}:=V(\langle 2,\vee\rangle)
,
\mathcal{J}_{0}:=V(\langle 2,\vee,0\rangle)
,
\mathcal{J}_{1}:=V(\langle 2,\vee,1\rangle)
,
\mathcal{J}_{01}:=V(\langle 2,\vee,0,1\rangle)
.

Observe that
\mathcal{S}
is the class of all sets.

The choice of this list is explained by the fact that every variety generated by a two-element algebra is either interpretable in or interprets one of the above defined varieties.

Recall that a term-operation of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is just the interpretation in
A
of a term in the language of
\mathbf{A}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety generated by the two-element algebra
\mathbf{A}
. Then, one of the following holds:

(a)

\mathsf{s}(x,y,z):=x+y+z
is a term-operation of
\mathbf{A}
, and thus,
\mathcal{A}
is interpretable in
\mathcal{V}
;

(b)

\mathsf{n}(x,y,z):=(x\wedge y)\vee z
is a term-operation of
\mathbf{A}
, and thus,
\mathcal{N}
is interpretable in
\mathcal{V}
;

(c)

\mathsf{m}(x,y,z):=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z)
is a term-operation of
\mathbf{A}
, and thus,
\mathcal{M}
is interpretable in
\mathcal{V}
;

(d)

\mathcal{V}
is interpretable in
\mathcal{U}
;

(e)

\mathcal{V}
is term-equivalent to
\mathcal{J}
,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
,
\mathcal{J}_{1}
or
\mathcal{J}_{01}
.

Proof.

Given an algebra
\mathbf{A}
let
\mathop{\mathrm{Clo}}\mathbf{A}
denote the set of all term-operations of positive arity of
\mathbf{A}
.
Given algebras
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
with universe
\{0,1\}
we write
\mathbf{A}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{B}
provided that
\mathop{\mathrm{Clo}}\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Clo}}\mathbf{B}
. The relation
\sqsubseteq
is a preorder whose associated equivalence relation we denote by
\equiv
. It is not hard to see that
\mathbf{A}\equiv\mathbf{B}
iff
V(\mathbf{A})
and
V(\mathbf{B})
are term-equivalent. The equivalence classes of
\equiv
ordered by
\sqsubseteq
form a countable lattice, known as Post’s lattice, which is completely described in
[
11
]
. The lemma follows from the above observations and this description.
∎

We already know that
CRT
|_{\mathcal{N}}
is in P (Theorem
4.4
); in the next series of lemmas we consider the remaining varieties in our list.

Lemma 5.3
.

CRT
|_{\mathcal{A}}
is in
P
.

Proof.

Using the shorthand
x+_{w}y:=\mathsf{s}(x,y,w)
, note that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the identities:

•

(x+_{w}y)+_{w}z\approx x+_{w}(y+_{w}z)
,

•

x+_{w}y\approx y+_{w}x
,

•

x+_{w}w\approx x
,

•

x+_{w}x\approx w
.

Hence, given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}
and
e\in A
the algebra
\mathbf{A}_{e}:=\langle A,+_{e},-,e,\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\rangle
, where
-x:=x
,
\lambda_{0}(x):=e
and
\lambda_{1}(x):=x
, is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-vector space. It follows that there is an interpretation of the variety of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-vector spaces in
\mathcal{A}(c)
, where is a new constant symbol. So, by Lemma
5.1
and Theorem
4.1
we are done.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

CRT
|_{\mathcal{U}}
is
coNP
-complete.

Proof.

By the proof of Theorem
3.1
it suffices to show that
CRT
|_{\mathcal{S}}

\leq_{P}

CRT
|_{\mathcal{U}}
.

Given a set
A
and any element
c\in A
define the algebra
\mathbf{A}_{c}:=\langle A\cup A^{\prime},\neg,c,c^{\prime}\rangle
where

•

A^{\prime}:=\{a^{\prime}:a\in A\}
is a set disjoint with
A
such that
|A^{\prime}|=|A|
;

•

\neg a:=a^{\prime}
and
\neg a^{\prime}:=a
for all

.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is axiomatized by the identities
\neg\neg x\approx x
and
\neg 0\approx 1
, it is clear that
\mathbf{A}_{c}\in\mathcal{U}
. Also, given an equivalent relation
\theta
on

let
\tilde{\theta}:=\theta_{i}\cup\{\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle:\langle a,b\rangle\in\theta\}
. We leave it to the reader to check that the transformation taking an instance
\langle A,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\rangle
of

|_{\mathcal{S}}
and an arbitrary

is in fact a polynomial reduction from

|_{\mathcal{S}}
to
CRT
|_{\mathcal{U}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

The restrictions of
CRT
to the varieties
\mathcal{J}
,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
,
\mathcal{J}_{1}
,
\mathcal{J}_{01}

Proof.

Taking into account the obvious interpretaions among these four varieties, by Lemma

.(a), we have that

and

\leq_{P}

\leq_{P}

\leq_{P}

Sadly, we were not able to characterize the complexity of

Question
.

What is the complexity of

|_{\mathcal{J}}
?

With that out of the way, we present now our (almost) dichotomy theorem.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety generated by the two-element algebra
\mathbf{A}
. Suppose

is not term-equivalent to any of

,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
,

,

•

\mathsf{s}
,
\mathsf{n}
or

is a term-operation of

is in

•

\mathcal{V}
is interpretable in

, and

|_{\mathcal{V}}
is

If
\mathsf{s}
,
\mathsf{n}
or
\mathsf{m}
is a term-operation of

, then

satisfies condition (a), (b) or (c) in Lemma

.(a) together with Lemma

, Theorem

, and Theorem

|_{\mathcal{V}}
is in P. (For (c) note that

is the dual discriminator function on 2.)

Otherwise, by Lemma

and our assumption on

, we have that
\mathcal{V}
is intepretable in

, so Lemmas

.(a) and

say that
