A framework for stable spectral methods in d d -dimensional unit balls
Jing Gao, Arieh Iserles

Abstract
The subject of this paper is the design of efficient and stable spectral methods for time-dependent partial differential equations in unit balls. We commence by sketching the desired features of a spectral method, which is defined by a choice of an orthonormal basis acting in the spatial domain. We continue by considering in detail the choice of a W-function basis in a disc in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . This is a nontrivial issue because of a clash between two objectives: skew symmetry of the differentiation matrix (which ensures inter alia that the method is stable) and the correct behaviour at the origin. We resolve it by representing the underlying space as an affine space and splitting the underlying functions. This is generalised to any dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 in a natural manner and the paper is concluded with numerical examples that demonstrate how our choice of basis attains the best outcome out of a number of alternatives.



1
Introduction: spectral methods

We are concerned in this paper with the solution of differential equations of the form

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}u+f(t,\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},u),\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\Omega,\quad t\geq 0,

(1.1)

where
\Omega
is a connected domain with Jordan boundary, given with
\mathrm{L}_{2}
initial conditions in
\Omega
and zero boundary conditions on its boundary. Here
\mathcal{L}
is a linear differential operator that generates a strongly continuous semigroup, while
f
is a Lipschitz function. More specifically, our interest is focused on spectral methods
[
funaro92pad
,
hesthaven07smt
]
in the special setting of
\Omega=\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unit ball,

\mathcal{B}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\|_{2}\leq 1\}.

While our setting is very general, throughout this paper we illustrate our ideas with two paradigmatic equations: the
diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u

and the
linear Schrödinger equation (LSE)

{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\Delta u+V(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})u.

Assuming that the solution of (
1.1
) evolves in the separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
, we let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies zero boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. The solution of (
1.1
) is expanded in the form

u(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}(t)\varphi_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}),

(1.2)

where the functions
\hat{u}_{m}
are determined by the Galerkin conditions

\left\langle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}^{\prime}\varphi_{m}-\mathcal{L}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}\varphi_{m}-f\!\left(t,\,\cdot\,,\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}\varphi_{m}\right)\!,\varphi_{n}\right\rangle=0,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Because of orthonormality of
\Phi
and linearity of
\mathcal{L}
, this reduces to the ODE system

\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle+\left\langle f\!\left(t,\,\cdot\,,\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}\varphi_{m}\right)\!,\varphi_{n}\right\rangle,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(1.3)

Needless to say, in practice both (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are truncated.

Let us assume that the
\varphi_{n}
s are sufficiently regular – in applications they are often analytic functions and this is the setting when spectral methods display their maximal advantage. Since
\Phi
is a basis of
\mathcal{H}
and
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a linear transformation
\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
taking the basis
\Phi
to the partial derivative with respect to
x_{\ell}
,

\frac{\partial\varphi_{m}}{\partial x_{\ell}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}^{\,[\ell]}\varphi_{n},\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

The matrices
\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d
, are called the
differentiation matrices
of
\Phi
.

This is the moment to list the desired features of a spectral method, while bearing in mind that the choice of a spectral method (at least in the current setting, we say nothing here about the solution of the semidiscretised ODE system (
1.3
)) depends wholly on the choice of the orthonormal system
\Phi
. At the first instance, a spectral method (i.e., the choice of
\Phi
) must be assessed with respect to the following five desiderata:

1.

Stability:
This is the
sine qua non
of any numerical method for time-dependent PDEs. By virtue of the Lax equivalence theorem, the solution of (truncated) (
1.3
) converges to
u
as
N\rightarrow\infty
if and only if the solution of

\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\hat{u}_{m}\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle,\qquad n=0,\ldots,N

is uniformly well posed for
N\gg 1
.

2.

Fast convergence:
In classical spectral methods the set
\Phi
consists of orthogonal polynomials or (in the case of periodic boundary conditions) is a Fourier basis. In that case, provided that all functions concerned are analytic, the expansion (
1.2
) converges at least at an exponential speed and we might choose relatively small
N
while attaining high accuracy. This feature makes spectral methods very powerful
whenever they can be applied.
Unfortunately, this caveat typically restricts such methods to fairly simple geometries.

3.

Fast expansion:
A major computational issue is to expand functions in the underlying basis
\Phi
, something that needs be done, often repeatedly, in each step of the solution. A major attraction of spectral methods in the presence of periodic boundary conditions is that Fourier bases lend themselves to fast computation with the Fast Fourier Transform but fast algorithms exist also for expansions in the most popular bases of orthogonal polynomials
[
olver29fau
]
.

4.

Easy algebra:
Numerical mathematics reduces analysis to linear algebra, and this is the case with both the ODEs (
1.3
) and their discretisation. Working at the level of orthogonal expansions, a function
g\in\mathcal{H}
is represented by the coefficients of its orthogonal expansion
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$g$\unboldmath}}=(\hat{g}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that
g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{g}_{n}\varphi_{n}
. We deduce that the representation of
\partial g/\partial x_{\ell}
is
\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$g$\unboldmath}}
and, with greater generality, the action of the differential operator
\mathcal{L}
can be constructed, Lego blocks style, using differentiation matrices. Consequently, the structure of differentiation matrices is fundamental in ensuring that the underlying linear algebra – not just representing the action of
\mathcal{L}
by a matrix but solving linear systems and computing exponentials and other functions involving the matrix in question – can be performed cheaply and effectively.

5.

Preservation of structure:
Diverse PDEs possess structure which is of major importance in both mathematics and their application areas. Thus, the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\Omega)
norm of the solution of the diffusion equation (with zero boundary conditions) should monotonically decrease in
\mathrm{L}_{2}
: this reflects the second law of thermodynamics. In the ‘spectral method world’ and using Cartesian coordinates this means that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}}^{*}\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
should be a negative semidefinite matrix.

Likewise, the solution of LSE is
unitary:
The
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\Omega)
norm stays constant for all
t\geq 0
. This reflects the quantum-mechanical interpretation of LSE: for any measurable subset
\Xi\subseteq\Omega
the integral
\int_{\Xi}|u(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
is the probability of a particle residing in the set
\Xi
at time
t\geq 0
. Thus, unless
\|u(\,\cdot\,,t)\|\equiv 1
, the solution is nonphysical. In the present context this means that
{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}}^{*}\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
should be a skew-Hermitian matrix.

Note that both conditions – negative definiteness and skew-Hermicity – imply stability, thereby fulfilling the first, most important feature of any method for time-dependent PDEs. Note further that, once each
\mbox{\curly D}^{[\ell]}
is skew-Hermitian then
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}}^{*}\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
is negative semidefinite and
{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}}^{*}\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
skew Hermitian. This underlies the
leitmotif
of this paper:
using orthonormal systems with skew-Hermitian differentiation matrices.

There are many other structures of interest, not least Hamiltonians, but they form no part of the work of this paper.

It is important to comment on a critical issue.
{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}}^{*}\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
is skew Hermitian for
any
square matrices
\mbox{\curly D}^{\,[\ell]}
but, in our context, we need each
-{\mbox{\curly D}^{[\ell]}}^{*}
to be a differentiation matrix as well. Therefore, skew Hermicity bridges the strong and the weak form of the PDE and is crucial in approximating the LSE while maintaining the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm and approximating the diffusion equation while dissipating it.

An obvious orthonormal set consists of polynomials: their univariate theory is exceedingly well known
[
chihara78iop
,
ismail05cqo
]
and has been extended to numerous multivariate sets, in particular to discs
[
zernike34bss
]
and simplexes
[
dunkl14ops
,
koornwinder75tva
]
. However, while orthogonal polynomials have been used extensively in spectral methods for boundary-value problems, they are highly problematic insofar as initial-value PDEs (
1.1
) are concerned: their differentiation matrices are lower-triangular. This is inimical to preservation of unitarity and, moreover, leads to instability. While stability can be recovered using in tandem two orthonormal systems
[
townsend15asp
]
, our approach is to abandon polynomial bases altogether.

In
[
iserles19oss
,
iserles20for
,
iserles21fco
,
iserles24ost
]
we have introduced for
d=1
and
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
the concept of
T-functions.
For any Borel measure supported on
\mathbb{R}
we can construct, using a Fourier transform, a corresponding set
\Phi
of functions for which the differentiation matrix
D
is skew-Hermitian and tridiagonal. Skew-Hermicity ensures unitarity (hence also stability) and tridiagonality is a gateway to fast algebra. The univariate setting can be extended to
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
using tensor products. However, T-functions are unsuitable in the presence of finite boundaries. This motivated in
[
iserles24ssm
]
the introduction of
W-functions.

Given an interval
(a,b)
and a
\mathrm{C}^{1}(a,b)
weight function
w(x)\geq 0
,
\int_{a}^{b}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x>0
, we let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be the underlying set of orthonormal polynomials. The
n
th W-function is then
\varphi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{w(x)}p_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and it is easy to see that
\Phi
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
. Moreover, it has been proved in
[
iserles24ssm
]
that the underlying differentiation matrix
D
is skew symmetric if and only if
w(a)=w(b)=0
. Two cases have been worked out in great detail in
[
iserles24ssm
]
:
ultraspherical W-functions,
generated by
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha}
,
x\in(-1,1)
, for
\alpha>0
and
Laguerre W-functions,
generated by
w(x)=x^{\alpha}{\mathrm{e}}^{-x}
,
x\in(0,\infty)
, again for
\alpha>0
. In both instances, while not being tridiagonal,
D
has another welcome feature: it is semi-separable of rank s
[
hackbusch15hma
]
, where
s=1
for Laguerre W-functions and
s=2
for ultraspherical W-functions: every submatrix placed entirely above (or entirely below) the main diagonal is of rank s. This allows for fast algebra. Moreover, judicious choice of
\alpha
leads to exponential convergence.

W-functions can be extended to
d
-dimensional boxes by tensor products. In that instance the differentiation matrix (in the ultraspherical or Laguerre cases) is semi-separable and the advantage of fast algebra is maintained.

A word on the function spaces used in this paper. The least requirement one may impose is that the underlying functions live in a (possibly weighed)
\mathrm{L}_{2}
space in
\mathcal{B}
, but this is way too weak both for meaningful statements on convergence in the context of spectral methods and for the imposition of boundary conditions. In this paper we consider functions analytic in
\mathcal{B}
(considered as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), i.e. whose Taylor expansion about the origin converges in
\mathcal{B}
: we denote this function space by
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
. The intermediate case, the Sobolev space
\mathrm{H}^{p}_{2}(\mathcal{B})
for a suitable
p\geq 1
, follows at once with obvious limitations, e.g. exponential convergence reduces to polynomial one. In addition, we assume that all underlying functions obey zero boundary conditions on the unit sphere.

In Section 2 we focus on the disc,
d=2
. Interestingly, this simplest nontrivial case already displays all the intricacies associated with designing orthonormal systems in any dimension
d\geq 2
. The obvious course of action is to use a scalar inner product in polar coordinates
0\leq r\leq 1
,
-\pi\leq\theta_{1}\leq\pi
,
0\leq\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{d-1}\leq\pi

\langle\!\langle f,g\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{\|\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$\unboldmath}\|\leq 1}f(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})\overline{g(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{\pi}rf(r,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})\overline{g(r,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}\,\mathrm{d}r,

(1.4)

which is the same as standard Cartesian
\mathrm{L}_{2}
inner product for
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1
. This is the standard approach to approximation in a disc and there exists rich theory on polynomial approximation, using Zernike polynomials
[
zernike34bss
]
and their generalisations
[
wunsche05gzd
]
. It might thus seem that all we need is to ‘translate’ polynomials to W-functions
á la

[
iserles24ssm
]
but, unfortunately, this course of action leads to systems whose differentiation matrices depart substantially from skew symmetry.

As an alternative, we are using a Cartesian inner product in the box
\{(r,\theta)\,:\,r\in[0,1],\;\theta_{1}\in[-\pi,\pi],\;\theta_{j}\in[0,\pi],\;j=2,\ldots,d-1\}
,

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{\pi}f(r,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})\overline{g(r,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath}\,\mathrm{d}r,

(1.5)

but this is not problem-free either. The boundary conditions on
\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\|=1
translate seamlessly to
r=1
, while the boundary conditions on
\theta
remain periodic. However, for
r=0
we require natural boundary conditions,
\partial f(0,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})/\partial\theta_{m}\equiv 0
,
m=1,\dots,d-1

[
iserles09fna
, p. 336–7]
. This is inconsistent with our approach to W-functions: if
\varphi_{n,m}(r,\theta)=\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(r){\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}m\theta}
is a W-function then
\partial\varphi_{n,m}(0,\theta)={\mathrm{i}}m\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(0){\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}m\theta}
and setting this to zero for all
n
implies
\varphi_{n,m}(0,\theta)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. This precludes convergence in the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
norm and renders
\mathrm{L}_{2}
convergence exceedingly slow.

Yet, imposing
\varphi_{n,m}(0,\theta)=0
has one beneficial effect: it represents a necessary and sufficient condition, similarly to
[
iserles24ssm
]
, for differentiation matrices to be skew symmetric. The tension between skew symmetry and fast convergence is at the heart of this paper: a course of action, which we advocate in Section 2, is to split
f\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
into a sum of the form
f_{0}+f_{1}
such that
f_{0}
is orthogonal to
f_{1}
and
f_{1}(0,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})=0
– in addition,
f_{0}(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})=f_{1}(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})=0
. In other words, we consider
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
with zero boundary condition for
r=1
as an affine space,
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})=h(r,\theta)\oplus{\mathfrak{A}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}}(\mathcal{B})
, where
h\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
,
h(0,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})=f(0,\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$\unboldmath})
, while
{\mathfrak{A}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}}(\mathcal{B})\subset\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
consists of functions that obey zero boundary conditions at
r=0
. Using a variation upon the theme of Zernike polynomials, in tandem with the W-function approach, we present an orthonormal basis on
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
whose differentiation matrices in
{\mathfrak{A}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}}(\mathcal{B})
are skew symmetric: we say that such differentiation matrices are
essentially skew symmetric.
Since such matrices have at most a finite number of eigenvalues in the complex right-half plane, they allow for the construction of stable semidiscretisations for our two paradigmatic equations.

In Section 2 we develop in detail the theory in the disc,
d=2
, and this is generalised in a straightforward manner to any
d\geq 2
in Section 3. Finally, in Section 4 we subject the same function
f
to five different computational approaches based on the ideas in this paper. Four of these approaches do not tick
all
the boxes and they either lead to slow convergence or to differentiation matrices that depart significantly from skew symmetry, while the last is the one we are advocating and this is confirmed by numerical results.

2
Zernike-type W-functions

2.1
d=2

In this paper we consider W-functions on unit balls
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with zero boundary conditions on
\partial\mathcal{B}
. In the present section we commence with
d=2
, hence, in polar coordinates, a single radial variable
r\in[0,1]
and a single angular variable
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
, imposing zero boundary conditions for
r=1
. The usual
\mathrm{L}_{2}
inner product in polar coordinates has been given in (
1.4
) and it motivates the standard orthogonal system on
\mathcal{B}
in polar coordinates, namely
Zernike polynomials

p_{n,m}(r,\theta)=\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(0,1)}(2r-1){\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}m\theta},\qquad r\in[0,1],\;\theta\in[-\pi,\pi],\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\;m\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.1)

where
\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
is an
n
th-degree Jacobi polynomial
[
zernike34bss
]
(the original system is usually written somewhat differently, but is isomorphic to (
1.3
)). Note that the polynomials
\{\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
are orthogonal in
[-1,1]
with respect to the weight function
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
and this renders Zernike polynomials orthogonal in polar coordinates.

Zernike polynomials been extended to the so-called
disc polynomials
in
[
wunsche05gzd
]
, allowing for more general Jacobi weight functions. However, (
2.1
) and its generalisation to disc polynomials exhibit two critical shortcomings in the present context: firstly, their differentiation matrices are not skew symmetric and, secondly, they fail to obey zero boundary conditions for
r=1
.

With greater generality, the inner product (
1.4
) is highly problematic within our framework: consider just functions of the radial variable
r
and set with greater generality

\langle\!\langle f,g\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}w(r)f(r)\overline{g(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r,

(2.2)

where
w
is a differentiable weight function,
w\not\equiv\mathrm{const}
.

Theorem 1

Let a scalar product be given by (
2.2
) . The conditions

(a)

\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is a real orthogonal basis with respect to the inner product
\langle\!\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle\!\rangle
;

(b)

w(0)=0
, while
w(x)\neq 0
,
x\in(0,1)
;

(c)

q_{n}(1)=0
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
;

(d)

The differentiation matrix

\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}=\langle\!\langle q_{m}^{\prime},q_{n}\rangle\!\rangle,\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

is skew Hermitian

are contradictory.

Proof
Let
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Integrating by parts,

\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}=\langle\!\langle q_{m}^{\prime},q_{n}\rangle\!\rangle=w(r)q_{m}(r)q_{n}(r)\,\rule[-4.0pt]{0.75pt}{18.0pt}_{\,r=0}^{\,r=1}-\int_{0}^{1}w^{\prime}q_{m}q_{n}\,\mathrm{d}r-\int_{0}^{1}wq_{m}q_{n}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r

therefore, because of conditions (b) and (c),

\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}+\mbox{\curly D}_{n,m}=-\int_{0}^{1}w^{\prime}q_{m}q_{n}\,\mathrm{d}r.

It now follows from condition (d) that
\int_{0}^{1}w^{\prime}q_{m}q_{n}\,\mathrm{d}r=0
for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, but this is clearly impossible. Otherwise, since
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is an orthogonal basis and every function in the underlying Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
can be expanded in it, it would have followed that
\langle\!\langle w^{\prime}/w,f\rangle\!\rangle=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{G}
. This is possible only if
w^{\prime}\equiv 0
but this is ruled out by condition (b).

\Box

The theorem is not very surprising. The deep reason skew symmetry matters is its connection to standard
\mathrm{L}_{2}
unitarity: the Lie algebra corresponding to the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(n,\mathbb{C})
is
\mbox{\gothic su}(n,\mathbb{C})
, the set of skew-Hermitian matrices. Yet, the inner product (
1.4
) is not a ‘standard’
\mathrm{L}_{2}

1.4

1.5

2.1
) (or, with greater generality, disc polynomials) to W-functions, letting

\varphi_{n,m}(r,\theta)=\pi^{-1/2}2^{\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)}(1-r)^{\alpha/2}r^{\beta/2}\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(2r-1){\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}m\theta},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\;m\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.3)

where
\alpha,\beta>-1
and

\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)}{\sqrt{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}}},\qquad h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}=\frac{2^{1+\alpha+\beta}\mathrm{\Gamma}(1+\alpha+n)\mathrm{\Gamma}(1+\beta+n)}{n!(1+\alpha+\beta+2n)\mathrm{\Gamma}(1+\alpha+\beta+n)},

are orthonormal Jacobi polynomials
[
rainville60sf
, p. 260]
.

1

The factor

.
Yet, this choice is problematic as well. In order for

to be skew symmetric, a necessary and sufficient condition is
\alpha,\beta>0

[
iserles24ssm

. However

implies that
\varphi_{m,n}(0,\theta)\equiv 0
: this precludes

To avoid this conundrum, we split
f\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
in the form
f=f_{0}+f_{1}
, subject to three conditions:

1.

f_{0}(1)=f_{1}(1)=0
(boundary conditions for

are satisfied);

2.

f_{0}(0)=f(0)
,
f_{1}(0)=0
;

3.

\langle f_{0},f_{1}\rangle=0
, therefore
\|f\|^{2}=\|f_{0}\|^{2}+\|f_{1}\|^{2}
.

Given
f\in\mathrm{C}(\mathcal{B})
, we call a pair
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
of
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
functions such that
f_{0}+f_{1}=f
and conditions 1–3 are satisfied, a
proper orthogonal splitting (POS).
Herewith a constructive means to produce a POS.

Let
\tilde{f}_{0}
and
\tilde{f}_{1}
be two
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
functions that obey the conditions 1–2 and such that
\tilde{f}_{0}+\tilde{f}_{1}=f
. We set, in a single step of the Gram–Schmidt algorithm,

f_{0}(r)=\tilde{f}_{0}(r)-c\tilde{f}_{1}(r),\qquad f_{1}(r)=(1+c)\tilde{f}_{1}(r),\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad c=\frac{\langle\tilde{f}_{0},\tilde{f}_{1}\rangle}{\|\tilde{f}_{1}\|^{2}}.

(2.4)

It is trivial to prove that conditions 1–3 hold and we have a POS.

Reasonable choices of
\tilde{f}_{0}
and
\tilde{f}_{1}
in (
2.4
) are
\tilde{f}_{0}(r)=f(0)(1-r)
or
\tilde{f}_{0}(r)=f(0)\cos(\pi r/2)
, with
\tilde{f}_{1}(r)=f(r)-\tilde{f}_{0}(r)
.

The idea is to expand a general
f\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{B})
in the form

f(r,\theta)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}f^{\circ}_{m}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}m\theta}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n,m}\varphi_{n,m}(r,\theta),

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle f_{m}^{\circ}

Definition 1

We say that a differentiation matrix is essentially skew symmetric if its action on

We deduce that, once acting in Cartesian coordinates, the differentiation matrix

The Laplacian in polar coordinates being

\Delta f=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\theta^{2}},

integration by parts, in tandem with zero boundary conditions for

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\Delta f,f\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}r\!\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\theta^{2}}\right)\!\bar{f}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}r

Let us examine the impact of this observation on the stability in the case of the diffusion equation and the LSE. To this end we consider
compound differentiation matrices

\mbox{\curly E}^{[r]}
and
\mbox{\curly E}^{[\theta]}
. Their first row and column originate in
f_{0}
, while the
(1,1)
principal minor stem from
f_{1}
. Thus,

\mbox{\curly E}^{[r]}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d&\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}^{\top}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$0$\unboldmath}&\mbox{\curly D}^{[r]}\end{array}\right]\!,\qquad\mathrm{Re}\,d=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(0,\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

while
\mbox{\curly E}^{[\theta]}
is skew-Hermitian. It follows that
\mathcal{R}={\mbox{\curly E}^{[r]}}^{\top}\!\mbox{\curly E}^{[r]}
is symmetric and its largest eigenvalue is bounded, while
\Theta={\mbox{\curly E}^{[\theta]}}^{*}\!\mbox{\curly E}^{[\theta]}
is symmetric and negative semidefinite. Therefore
\|\exp(t(\mathcal{R}+\Theta))\|\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{|d|^{2}t}
and the method is stable for the diffusion equation (essentially, because
\mathcal{R}
is symmetric and its spectrum is bounded in the complex right half-plane – in the language of semigroup theory, it is a sectorial operator). Note however that dissipativity might be lost.

Matters are simpler in the case of LSE.
\mathcal{R}+\Theta
being symmetric, it follows that
{\mathrm{i}}(\mathcal{R}+\Theta)
is skew-Hermitian, therefore
\|\exp({\mathrm{i}}t(\mathcal{R}+\Theta))\|\equiv 1
: the method is both stable and preserves unitarity.

We now put together all the ingredients that guarantee an essentially skew-symmetric, semi-separable differentiation matrix and rapid rate of convergence:
