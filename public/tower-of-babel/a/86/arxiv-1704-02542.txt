Differential Geometric Aspects of Causal Structures
Omid MAKHMALI

Abstract
This article is concerned with causal structures, which are defined as a field of tangentially non-degenerate projective hypersurfaces in the projectivized tangent bundle of a manifold. The local equivalence problem of causal structures on manifolds of dimension at least four is solved using Cartan’s method of equivalence, leading to an { e } \{e\} -structure over some principal bundle. It is shown that these structures correspond to parabolic geometries of type ( D n , P 1 , 2 ) (D_{n},P_{1,2}) and ( B n − 1 , P 1 , 2 ) (B_{n-1},P_{1,2}) , when n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 , and ( D 3 , P 1 , 2 , 3 ) (D_{3},P_{1,2,3}) . The essential local invariants are determined and interpreted geometrically. Several special classes of causal structures are considered including those that are a lift of pseudo-conformal structures and those referred to as causal structures with vanishing Wsf curvature. A twistorial construction for causal structures with vanishing Wsf curvature is given.

1.1
Motivation and history

In the general theory of relativity, space-time is represented by a four dimensional manifold
M
equipped with a Lorentzian metric
g
. An important feature of a Lorentzian structure is its associated field of null cones in
TM
given by the set of vectors
v\in TM
satisfying
g(v,v)=0
. As a result, it is possible to define a relation of
causality
between the points of
M
, i.e., a partial ordering
x\prec y
defined by the property that
x
can be joined to
y
by a curve that is either time-like or null. If
x
and
y
represent two events in space-time then
x\prec y
means that the occurrence of
x
has an effect on
y
. In
[
KP-causal
]
Kronheimer and Penrose examined causality in a manifold
M
of dimension
n
on an axiomatic basis. The starting point of their study is a continuous assignment of null cones in the tangent space of each point of
M
so that it is possible to define the set of points that are inside, outside and on the null cone in each tangent space.

The importance of the causal relationship between points of space-time became more significant in the context of the singularity theorems of Penrose and Hawking
[
HE-SpaceTime
,
Penrose-Sing
,

]

[

]

[

]

[

,

[

]

Rather than to investigate causality relations between the points of a manifold with a given causal structure, the main idea of this article is to gain a deeper understanding of the geometry of causal structures through the solution of the Cartan equivalence problem.
This approach enables one to construct a complete set of local invariants and also to obtain some results on symmetries of causal structures.

Two causal structures

In order to describe a causal structure in an open set

The main motivation behind the study of causal structures comes from a variety of perspectives. As a part of the geometric study of differential equations, it was shown by Holland and Sparling
[

A causal structure is given in this setting by a family of curves
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
where
\mathcal{C}_{x}:=\pi^{-1}(x)\subset\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
is a
non-degenerate
curve in the sense that it admits a well-defined projective Frenet frame. This causal structure descends to a conformal Lorentzian structure on

if a certain contact invariant of the third order ODE known as the Wünschmann invariant vanishes. In other words, it is shown in
[
HS2010causal
]
that

is locally isomorphic to
J^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
5
5

5

In this article

. As a result, the vanishing of the Wünschmann invariant implies that the curves

The work of Holland and Sparling is a part of the program of studying geometries arising from differential equations, which has a long history going back to Monge, Jacobi, Lie, Darboux, Goursat, Cartan and others. In the case of third order ODEs under contact transformations, Chern
[
ChernODE

[

[
GNThirdODEs
]

[

to show if

contact projective structure

Main results

In all the statements of this article smoothness of underlying manifolds and maps is always assumed and the dimension of

3.6
.

Given a causal structure with vanishing
Wsf
curvature
(\mathcal{C},M)
, its space of characteristic curves admits a
\beta
-integrable Lie contact structure. Conversely, any
\beta
-integrable Lie contact structure on a manifold
\mathcal{N}
induces a causal structure with vanishing
Wsf
curvature on the space of its corresponding Legendrian submanifolds.

It should be noted that the theorem above is reminiscent of Grossman’s work
[
GrossmanPath
]
on torsion-free path geometries. He showed that given a torsion-free path structure on
N^{n+1}
, the space of paths,
\mathcal{S}^{2n}
, has a Segré structure, i.e., each tangent space is equipped with a Segré cone of type
(2,n)
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{S}
contains an
(n+1)
-parameter family of
n
-dimensional submanifolds such that through each point
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}
there passes a 1-parameter family of them. Grossman showed that at each point
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}
, the Segré cone is ruled by the tangent spaces of the 1-parameter family of submanifolds passing through
\gamma
. Such Segré structures are called
\beta
-integrable.

Furthermore, twistorial constructions such as that of Theorem
3.6
fit exactly into Čap’s theorem of correspondence spaces
[
CapCorr
]
. However, Čap’s theory does not contain the geometric interpretation given here in terms of existence of submanifolds that rule the null cone or the Segré cone. Such interpretations are clear from the constructions presented here.

Appendix
A
contains the details of the equivalence problem calculations. Since causal structures are examples of parabolic geometries of type
(B_{n-1},P_{1,2})
and
(D_{n},P_{1,2})
if
n\geq 4
and
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
, they admit a
regular
and
normal
Cartan connection
[
ANNMonge
,
CS-Parabolic
]
. Using the machinery of Tanaka, the correct change of coframe can be given recursively, in order to introduce a Cartan connection on the structure bundle of causal geometries which is normal in the sense of Tanaka. However, for computational reasons, writing down the precise form of the Cartan connection leads to very long expressions which are not particularly enlightening (see
[
Omid-Thesis
]
). Furthermore, the first order structure equations suffice for the purposes of this article.

2
Local description of causal structures

In this section, after introducing the necessary definitions and notations, the first order structure equations of causal structures are derived. It is assumed that the reader is familiar with Cartan’s method of equivalence for
G
-structures (see
[
Gardner-Book
,
Olver-Equiv-Book
]
for an account). The essential torsion terms appearing in the first structure equations are interpreted geometrically. The derivation of the full structure equations is carried out in Appendix
A
. In Section
2.5
a discussion on the fundamental invariants of a causal structure is given. Finally, the section ends with a few examples of causal structures.

2.1
Definitions and notational conventions

Since the main purpose of this article is the local study of causal geometries, the smoothness of manifolds and maps that are considered is always assumed. In other words, the manifolds considered are assumed to be the maximal open sets over which the necessary smoothness conditions are satisfied.

Let
M
be a smooth
(n+1)
-dimensional (real or complex) manifold,
n\geq 3
and
\mathbb{P}TM
denote its projectivized tangent bundle with projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{P}TM\rightarrow M
, and fibers
\pi^{-1}(x)=\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
. Given a tangent vector
y\in T_{x}M
, its projective equivalence class is denoted by
[y]\in\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
. For
S\subset\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
, the cone over
S
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{S}:=\{y\in T_{x}M\,|\,[y]\in S\}\subset T_{x}M.

Throughout this article, the indices range as

\displaystyle 0\leq i,j,k\leq n,\hskip 16.38895pt1\leq a,b,c\leq n-1,\hskip 16.38895pt0\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n-1,

and are subjected to the summation convention.
When a set of 1-forms is introduced as
\mathcal{I}=I\{\omega^{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
, it is understood that
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal algebraically generated by
\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n}\}
in the exterior algebra of
M
.

The symmetrization and anti-symmetrization operations for tensors are denoted using Penrose’s abstract index notation. For example, consider a tensor with coefficients
A_{abcd}
. The tensor
A_{(ab|c|d)}
is symmetric with respect to all indices except the third, and
A_{[a|bc|d]}
is anti-symmetric in the first and last indices. When dealing with symmetric forms such as
g=2\omega^{0}\circ\omega^{n}-\varepsilon_{ab}\omega^{a}\circ\omega^{b}
, or
F_{abc}\theta^{a}\circ\theta^{b}\circ\theta^{c}
with
F_{abc}=F_{(abc)}
, the symbol
\circ
denotes the symmetric tensor product. Bold-face letters such as
\mathbf{i}
,
\mathbf{j}
,
\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{b}
are used to denote a specific index, e.g., the set
\{\omega^{\mathbf{a}}\}
only contains the 1-form
\omega^{\mathbf{a}}
. The summation convention is not applied to bold-face letters.

At this stage, before stating the definition of a causal structure, a more general geometric structure, namely that of a
cone structure
, is defined
[
HwangSurveyVMRT
]
. The reason for this is that in the process of coframe adaption for cone structures of codimension one, causal structures are distinguished as a sub-class of such geometries via the characterizing property of having
tangentially non-degenerate
fibers (see Section
2.3.3
).

Definition 2.1
.

A
cone structure
of codimension
k\leq n
on
M^{n+1}
is given by an immersion
\iota\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}TM
where
\mathcal{C}
is a connected, smooth manifold of dimension
2n+1-k
with the property that the fibration
\pi\circ\iota\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
is a submersion whose fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}:=(\pi\circ\iota)^{-1}(x)
are mapped, via the immersion
\iota_{x}\colon\mathcal{C}_{x}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
, to connected projective submanifolds of codimension
k
in
\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
.

Two cone structures
\iota\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}TM
and
\iota^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}TM^{\prime}
are locally equivalent around points
x\in M,x^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}
, if there exists a diffeomorphism
\phi\colon U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
where
x\in U\subset M
and
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}\subset M^{\prime}
, such that
x^{\prime}=\phi(x)
and
\phi_{*}\left(\iota(\mathcal{C})\right)=\iota^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\phi(y)})
for all
y\in M
.

In this article, only cone structures of codimension one are considered i.e., the fibers
\iota(\mathcal{C}_{x})\subset\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
are projective hypersurfaces. Since the map
\iota\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}TM
is assumed to be an immersion and this article concerns the local nature of geometric structures, it is safe to drop the symbol
\iota
in
\iota(\mathcal{C})
and
\iota(\mathcal{C}_{x})
and denote them as
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{x}
, respectively. As a result, by restricting to small enough neighborhoods, one can safely assume
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}TM
is a sub-bundle defined in an open set
U\subset\mathbb{P}TM
, where the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
are smooth submanifolds. Consequently, a cone structure of codimension one over a manifold
M
can be represented by
(\mathcal{C},M)
.

In local coordinates, a point of
TM
is represented by
(x;y)
where
x=(x^{0},\dots,x^{n})
are the base coordinates and
y=(y^{0},\dots,y^{n})
are the fiber coordinates. In a sufficiently small neighborhood of a point
p=(x;z)\in\mathcal{C}
, the homogeneous coordinates
[y^{0}:\ldots:y^{n}]
can be chosen so that
z=[0:\ldots:0:1]
and the cone
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{x}}
is tangent to the hyperplane
\{y_{0}=0\}
along
\hat{z}
. The affine coordinates
(y^{0},\ldots,y^{n-1})
obtained from setting
y^{n}=1
, are used to derive the explicit expressions of the invariants. Using these preferred coordinates in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
p
, it is assumed that
\mathcal{C}
is given in terms of homogeneous coordinates as a locus

\displaystyle L\big(x^{0},\ldots,x^{n};y^{0},\ldots,y^{n}\big)=0,

where
L
is homogeneous of degree
r
in
y^{i}
and, in terms of affine coordinates, as a graph

\displaystyle y^{0}=F\big(x^{0},\ldots,x^{n};y^{1},\ldots,y^{n-1}\big).

Example 2.2

A cone structure of codimension one on

Example 2.3

A pseudo-conformal structure, i.e., a conformal class of a pseudo-Riemannian metric

of signature

2.3
The first order structure equations

In this section the first order structure equations for causal geometries are derived via the method of equivalence. The procedure involves successive coframe adaptations, as a result of which various important aspects of causal geometries are unraveled, e.g., the quasi-contact structure of
\mathcal{C}
, the projective invariants of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
, and the
Wsf
curvature. Also, it is noted that a causal structure can be equivalently formulated as a codimension one sub-bundle of
\mathbb{P}T^{*}M
, which is called a dual formulation of causal geometries. Such formulation is traditionally preferred in the context of geometric control theory
[
AZ-JacobiCurves
]
.

The derivation of the first order structure equations should be compared to that of Finsler structures as presented in
[
BryantRemarksFinsler
]
.

2.3.1
The projective Hilbert form

Given a cone structure of codimension one
(\mathcal{C},M)
, the subset
\mathcal{C}_{x}\subset\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
is a projective hypersurface, for all
x\in M
. At a point
y\in\mathcal{C}_{x}
, the affine tangent space
\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
is defined as the hyperplane
T_{z}\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\subset T_{x}M
tangent to the cone
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\subset T_{x}M
, containing
z\in\hat{y}
. Note that this subspace is independent of the choice of
z
. It is important here to distinguish between the affine tangent space
\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}\subset T_{x}M
and the vertical tangent space
\mathrm{V}T_{(x;y)}\mathcal{C}
corresponding to the fibration
\pi\circ\iota\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
.

The 1-form
\omega^{0}\in T^{*}\mathcal{C}
, at
(x;y)\in\mathcal{C}
is defined as a multiple of
(\pi\circ\iota)^{*}(\alpha)
where
\alpha:=\Ann\big(\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}\big)\in T_{x}^{*}M
. By definition,
\omega^{0}
is defined up to a multiplication by a nowhere vanishing smooth function on
\mathcal{C}
and is therefore semi-basic with respect to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
. In this article
\omega^{0}
is referred to as the projective Hilbert form.

2.3.2
The adapted flag

At each point
(x;y)\in\mathcal{C}
, there is a natural basis
\big(\textstyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{0}}},\dots,\textstyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}}\big)
for
T_{x}M
adapted to the flag

\displaystyle\hat{y}\subset\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}\subset T_{x}M,

where
\big\{\textstyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}}\big\}
and
\big\{\textstyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{1}}},\ldots,\textstyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}}\big\}
, respectively, span
\hat{y}
and
\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
.

In terms of the graph (
2.1
), the local expressions are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}=F\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}+y^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{a}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{n}},\hskip 16.38895pt\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}=\partial_{\underline{a}}F\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{a}},\hskip 16.38895pt\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{0}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}.

The dual coframe
\big(\omega^{0},\ldots,\omega^{n}\big)
can be written as

\displaystyle\omega^{n}=\mathrm{d}x^{n},\hskip 16.38895pt\omega^{a}=\mathrm{d}x^{a}-y^{a}\mathrm{d}x^{n},\hskip 16.38895pt\omega^{0}=\mathrm{d}x^{0}-\partial_{{\underline{a}}}F\mathrm{d}x^{a}+(y^{a}\partial_{\underline{a}}F-F)\mathrm{d}x^{n}.

(2.3)

The pull-back of these 1-forms to
\mathcal{C}
spans the semi-basic 1-forms with respect to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
. At each point
p\in\mathcal{C}
, given by
p=(x^{i};y^{i})
, these 1-forms are adapted to the flag

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}T_{p}\mathcal{C}\subset K_{p}\subset H_{p}\subset T_{p}\mathcal{C},

(2.4)

where
\mathrm{V}T_{p}M=\operatorname{Ker}\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n}\}
is the vertical tangent bundle,
K_{p}=\operatorname{Ker}\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1}\}
is the fiber of the tautological vector bundle given by

\displaystyle K_{p}=\{v\in T_{p}\mathcal{C}\,|\,\pi_{*}(p)(v)\in\hat{y}\subset T_{\pi(p)}M\},

where
p=(x;y)
, the line
\hat{y}
corresponds to the projective class of
y
and
H_{p}=\operatorname{Ker}\{\omega^{0}\}
is the quasi-contact distribution (see Section
2.3.5
). To clarify the notation, if
p=(x;y)
then the fiber
\mathrm{V}T_{p}\mathcal{C}
is also denoted by
T_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
and the quasi-contact distribution can be expressed as the splitting
T_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}\oplus\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
which is not unique.

2.3.3
Causal structures and the first coframe adaptation

Recall that a projective hypersurface
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is called
tangentially non-degenerate
if its projective second fundamental form has maximal rank (see
[
AG-Projective
,
SasakiBook
]
).
Now the stage is set to define causal structures.

Definition 2.4
.

A
causal structure
of signature
(p+1,q+1)
on
M^{n+1}
is a cone structure of codimension one,
(M,\mathcal{C})
, where the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
are tangentially non-degenerate projective hypersurfaces and their projective second fundamental form has signature
(p,q)
everywhere.

In Section
2.3.6
it is shown that the tangential non-degeneracy assumption of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
implies that the signature of
h^{ab}
at any point of
\mathcal{C}
determines its the signature of
h^{ab}
everywhere.

Let
\mathrm{d}_{V}
denote the vertical exterior derivative, i.e., the exterior differentiation with respect to fiber variables
y^{i}
. Using the expressions (
2.3
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{V}\omega^{0}\equiv-\theta^{0}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a},\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ I\big\{\omega^{0}\big\},

(2.5)

where
\theta^{0}{}_{a}=\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F\mathrm{d}y^{b}
. As a result of (
2.5
) one obtains

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}=-2\theta^{0}{}_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\theta^{0}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}+T_{0i}\omega^{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{i}+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}T_{ab}\omega^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}+T_{an}\omega^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}.

By setting

\phi_{0}:=\theta^{0}{}_{0}-T_{0i}\omega^{i}
,
\theta_{a}:=\theta^{0}{}_{a}-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}T_{ab}\omega^{b}-T_{an}\omega^{n},

one arrives at

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}=-2\phi_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\theta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a},

(2.6)

which implies that
\omega^{0}
has rank
2n-1
, i.e.,
\omega^{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{0})^{n-1}\neq 0
.

The 1-forms
\theta_{a}
define vertical 1-forms with respect to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
. The 1-forms
\theta_{a}
are linearly independent everywhere when the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
are tangentially non-degenerate at every point, i.e.,
\det(\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F)\neq 0
.

Since the vertical 1-forms
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-1}
are linearly independent, at each point
(x;y)\in\mathcal{C}
, one obtains a coframe
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}:=\{\omega^{0},\ldots,\omega^{n},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-1}\}
for
T_{(x;z)}\mathcal{C}
adapted to the flag

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}T_{p}\mathcal{C}\subset K_{p}\subset H_{p}\subset T_{p}\mathcal{C},

(2.7)

defined previously in (
2.4
). Such a coframe is called
1
-adapted
. For a quadric, this filtration coincides with the one introduced in (
2.2
). In analogy with the flat model, the following algebraic ideals are defined:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}=I\big\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n},\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{n-1}\big\},\hskip 16.38895pt\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bas}}=I\big\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}=I\big\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1},\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{n-1}\big\}.

Using the expressions (
2.3
), equation (
2.6
) gives

\displaystyle\theta_{a}=\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F\mathrm{d}y^{b}+\partial_{0}\partial_{\underline{a}}F\mathrm{d}x^{0}+\partial_{b}\partial_{\underline{a}}F\mathrm{d}x^{b}+(\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{n}F-\partial_{a}F-\partial_{0}F\partial_{\underline{a}}F)\mathrm{d}x^{n}.

(2.8)

From the expression above it follows that the linear independence of
\theta_{a}
’s modulo
\omega^{i}
’s is equivalent to the non-degeneracy of the vertical Hessian
\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F
.

The fact that such a coframing is adapted to the flag (
2.7
) and satisfies (
2.6
) implies that different choices of 1-adapted coframes are related by

\displaystyle\omega^{0}\longmapsto\mathbf{a}^{2}\omega^{0},\hskip 16.38895pt

\displaystyle\omega^{a}\longmapsto\mathbf{E}^{a}\omega^{0}+\mathbf{A}^{a}{}_{b}\omega^{b},

\displaystyle\omega^{n}\longmapsto\mathbf{e}\omega^{0}+\mathbf{B}_{a}\omega^{a}+\mathbf{b}^{2}\omega^{n},\hskip 16.38895pt

\displaystyle\theta_{a}\longmapsto\mathbf{C}_{a}\omega^{0}+\mathbf{S}_{ab}\big(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big)^{b}{}_{c}\omega^{c}+\mathbf{a}^{2}\big(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big)^{b}{}_{a}\theta_{b}.

In the language of
G
-structures, the structure group of the bundle of 1-adapted coframes,
G_{1}
, is of the form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{2}&0&0&0\\
\mathbf{E}&\mathbf{A}&0&0\\
\mathbf{e}&\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{b}^{2}&0\\
\mathbf{C}&{}^{t}\!\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{S}&0&\mathbf{a}^{2}\,{}^{t}\!\mathbf{A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,\vline\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathbf{A}\in\GL_{n-1}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\\
{}^{t}\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathbf{S}\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{S}={}{}^{t}\mathbf{S}\end{array}\right\}.

Remark 2.5
.

The reason for using
\mathbf{a}^{2}
and
\mathbf{b}^{2}
in
G_{1}
is as follows. Let
L(x^{i};y^{i})
be a Lagrangian function for a causal structure, i.e., the vanishing set of
L
defines
\mathcal{C}
. As is conventional in the case of pseudo-conformal structures, it is desirable to restrict to the set of causal transformations preserving the sign of
L
in a neighborhood of the null cones, i.e., restricting to the set of Lagrangians of the form
S^{2}L
for some nowhere vanishing homogeneous function
S\colon TM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Recall that the projective Hilbert form in terms of
L
is given by
\omega^{0}=\partial_{\underline{i}}L\mathrm{d}x^{i}
.
Replacing
L
by
S^{2}L
and restricting to the zero locus of
L
, the projective Hilbert form transforms to
S^{2}(L_{\underline{i}}\mathrm{d}x^{i})=S^{2}\omega^{0}
. This observation justifies the use of
\mathbf{a}^{2}
in
G_{1}
.
Moreover, recall that a line bundle is called orientable if it has a non-vanishing global section. In the case of contact manifolds, a contact distribution is called
co-orientable
or
transversally orientable
if it has a globally defined contact form
[
LMSympl87
]
. Similarly, a quasi-contact structure is called co-orientable if it has a globally defined quasi-contact form
\omega^{0}
. If the quasi-contact distribution on
\mathcal{C}
is given a co-orientation, it is preserved under the action of
G_{1}
. Similarly, if the characteristic field of a quasi-contact structure (see Section
2.3.5
) is given an orientation, then having
\mathbf{b}^{2}
in (
2.3.3
) implies that the structure group
G_{1}
preserves the orientation of the characteristic field. Moreover, the conformal scaling of the quadratic form
g
defined in Section
2.3.11
by the action of
G_{1}
is given by
\mathbf{a}^{2}\mathbf{b}^{2}
. As a result, it is possible to define space-like and time-like horizontal vector fields
v
in
T\mathcal{C}
via the signature of
g(v,v)
.

To implement Cartan’s method of equivalence, one lifts the 1-adapted coframe to the
G_{1}
-bundle in the following way (see
[
Gardner-Book
,
IL-Book
,
Olver-Equiv-Book
]
for the background).
Recall that the set of all coframes on
\mathcal{C}
, defines a
\mathrm{GL}(2n)
-principal bundle
\tilde{\varsigma}\colon\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, with the right action

\displaystyle R_{g}(\vartheta_{p})=g^{-1}\cdot\vartheta_{p},

where
\vartheta_{p}\in\varsigma^{-1}(p)
is a coframe at
p\in\mathcal{C}
,
g\in\mathrm{GL}(2n)
, and the action on the right hand side is the ordinary matrix multiplication.

Among the set of all coframes on
\mathcal{C}
, let
\vartheta
represent 1-adapted coframes
{}^{t}(\omega^{0},\dots,\theta_{n-1})
, which are well-defined up to the action of
G_{1}
. As a result, the 1-adapted coframes are sections of a
G_{1}
-bundle
\varsigma_{1}\colon\mathcal{P}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. On
\mathcal{P}_{1}\cong\mathcal{C}\times G_{1}
one can define a canonical set of 1-forms by setting

\displaystyle\underline{\vartheta}(p,g)={}^{t}\big(\underline{\omega}^{0},\dots,\underline{\theta}_{n-1}\big):=g^{-1}\cdot\vartheta_{p},

where
\vartheta_{p}
is a choice of 1-adapted coframe at
p\in\mathcal{C}
.

The exterior derivatives of the 1-forms in
\underline{\vartheta}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\underline{\vartheta}=\mathrm{d}g^{-1}{\,{\wedge}\;}\vartheta+g^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vartheta=-g^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}g\cdot{\,{\wedge}\;}g^{-1}\vartheta+g^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vartheta=-\alpha{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\vartheta}+T,

(2.12)

where
\alpha(p,g)=g^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}g
is
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-valued 1-form where
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
denotes the Lie algebra of
G_{1}
. The 2-forms
T(p,g)
can be expressed in terms of

\underline{\omega}^{i}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{j}
,
\underline{\theta}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{i}
,\underline{\theta}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\theta}_{b}
. The terms constituting
T
are called the torsion terms. They are semi-basic with respect to the projection
\varsigma_{1}
. Via
\alpha
, one can identify the vertical tangent spaces of the fibers of
\varsigma_{1}\colon\mathcal{P}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
with
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. The
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-valued 1-form
\alpha
is called a
pseudo-connection
on
\mathcal{P}_{1}
which is not yet uniquely defined. Recall that the difference between a
connection
and a pseudo-connection on
G_{1}
-bundle lies in the fact that a connection form
\tilde{\alpha}
satisfies the equivariance condition
R^{*}_{g}\tilde{\alpha}=\mathrm{Ad}(g^{-1})\tilde{\alpha}
, where
g\in G_{1}
, while a pseudo-connection in general does not.

A choice of pseudo-connection in (
2.12
) gives rise to structure equations for the 1-forms
\underline{\vartheta}
. For 1-adapted coframes, the pseudo-connection forms are of the form,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2\phi_{0}&0&0&0\\
\gamma^{a}{}_{0}&\psi^{a}{}_{b}&0&0\\
\gamma^{n}{}_{0}&\gamma^{n}{}_{a}&2\phi_{n}&0\\
\pi_{a}&\pi_{ab}&0&2\phi_{0}\delta^{a}_{b}-\psi^{a}{}_{b}\end{pmatrix},

where
\pi_{ab}=\pi_{ba}
.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\underline{\omega}^{0}=-2\phi_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{0}-\underline{\theta}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{a},

(2.13a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\underline{\omega}^{a}=-\gamma^{a}{}_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{0}-\psi^{a}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{b}-h^{ab}\,\underline{\theta}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{{n}},

(2.13b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\underline{\omega}^{{n}}=-\gamma^{n}{}_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{0}-\gamma^{n}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{a}-2\phi_{{n}}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{{n}},

(2.13c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\underline{\theta}_{a}=-\pi_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{0}-\pi_{ab}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{b}+\psi^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\theta}_{b}-2\phi_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\theta}_{a}+\sigma_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{n}+\sigma^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\theta}_{b},

(2.13d)

where the 1-forms
\sigma_{a}
,
\sigma^{b}{}_{a}
, are the torsion terms and vanish modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}
. Their components are determined in Section
2.3.9
.

The equation of
\mathrm{d}\underline{\omega}^{0}
follows directly from (
2.6
). Moreover, the Pfaffian system
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bas}}
, is completely integrable with the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
being its integral manifolds. It follows that
\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}=\tau^{a}{}_{i}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{i}
, and
\mathrm{d}\omega^{n}=\tau^{n}{}_{i}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{i}
, where
\tau^{a}{}_{i}
,
\tau^{n}{}_{i}\equiv 0
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}
. Using the fact that the action of
G_{1}
preserves
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bas}}
, and that two pseudo-connection differ by a semi-basic
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-valued 1-form, all the 1-forms
\tau^{a}_{i}
,
\tau^{n}{}_{i}
can be absorbed into the pseudo-connection forms. The only torsion term left is
h^{ab}\underline{\theta}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\underline{\omega}^{n}
, for some function
h^{ab}
, which is discussed in Section
2.3.6
. From now on, since structure equations are always written for the lifted coframe
\underline{\vartheta}
, the underline in
\underline{\omega}^{i}
and
\underline{\theta}_{a}
’s will be dropped.

Remark 2.6
.

The oriented projectivized tangent bundle
\mathbb{P}_{+}TM
is defined so that at
x\in M
the fiber
\mathbb{P}_{+}T_{x}M
is isomorphic to the space of
rays
in
T_{x}M
, or, equivalently, the set of oriented real lines in
T_{x}M
passing through the origin. As a result,
\mathbb{P}_{+}T_{x}M
is the nontrivial double cover of
\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
.

The above definition of cone structures, and more specifically causal structures, can be changed so that
\mathcal{C}
is a sub-bundle of
\mathbb{P}_{+}TM
. In the case of causal structures immersed in
\mathbb{P}_{+}TM
, the assumption that the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}=\iota^{-1}(x)
be connected is not desirable. This is due to the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{x}
should be thought of as the light cones which are comprised of the future and past light cones whose projectivizations are disjoint. Thus, one expects
\mathcal{C}_{x}=\mathcal{C}^{+}_{x}\sqcup\mathcal{C}^{-}_{x}
. This way, in analogy with Finsler geometry, a cone structure is called
reversible
if
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=-\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
. Otherwise, it is called
non-reversible
. It should be noted that among oriented causal structures, non-reversible ones can be of interest in certain problems due to possible differences in their future and past light cones. This is similar to the case of non-reversible Finsler structures containing the important class of Randers spaces
[
BRS-Zermelo
]
. In
[
KP-causal
]
, Kronheimer and Penrose use the term
self-dual
for a reversible causal structure. This is due to the fact that they define the dual of a causal structure to be the causal structure obtained from replacing all the causal relations between the points of the space by their inverses, i.e., interchanging the words “future” and “past”.

2.3.4
A dual formulation of causal structures

In the context of geometric control theory, geometric structures arising from distributions are typically formulated in the cotangent bundle
[
AZ-JacobiCurves
]
. Firstly, the definition of a Legendre transformation is recalled.

Definition 2.7
.

Given a sufficiently smooth real-valued function
L
, usually referred to as a Lagrangian, on
TM
, the associated Legendre transformation
\textrm{Leg}\colon TM\rightarrow T^{*}M
, is defined by

\displaystyle\textrm{Leg}=\hat{\pi}\circ\mathrm{d}_{V}L,

where
\mathrm{d}_{V}L
defines a section of the product bundle
TM\times T^{*}M
, with the projection map
\hat{\pi}\colon TM\times T^{*}M\rightarrow T^{*}M
. In local coordinates,
\textrm{Leg}(x^{i};y^{i})=(x^{i};p_{i})
where
p_{i}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{i}}
, and
(p_{0},\dots,p_{n})
are local coordinates for the fibers of
T^{*}M
.

The Legendre transformation is a local diffeomorphism provided that
\textrm{Leg}_{*}=\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}
is non-degenerate everywhere. The inverse Legendre transformation
\textrm{Leg}^{-1}
can be obtained by the same procedure using the Hamiltonian function
Q:=\mathcal{A}\circ\textrm{Leg}^{-1}
where
\mathcal{A}:=\mathrm{d}L(\underline{A})-L
with
\underline{A}
being the Liouville vector field, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}=y^{i}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{i}}-L
in local coordinates. When the degree of homogeneity of
L
is
r\neq 1
, Euler’s theorem gives
Q=(r-1)L\circ\textrm{Leg}^{-1}
.

Changing the defining function to
L^{\prime}(x,y)=S(x,y)L(x,y)
, the function
Leg
remains invariant over
\mathcal{C}
, hence the vanishing set of
Q
in
T^{*}M
remains invariant.
Consequently, one can equivalently study the
dual causal structure

\mathcal{C}^{*}:=\textrm{Leg}(\mathcal{C})\subset\mathbb{P}T^{*}M
whose fibers
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{x}\subset\mathbb{P}T^{*}_{x}M
are the projective dual of the projective hypersurfaces
\mathcal{C}_{x}\subset\mathbb{P}T_{x}M
.

Via the Legendre transformation the projective Hilbert form
\omega^{0}=\partial_{\underline{i}}L\,\mathrm{d}x^{i}
is the pull-back of the canonical contact form
\lambda=p_{i}\mathrm{d}x^{i}
restricted to
\mathcal{C}^{*}\subset\mathbb{P}T^{*}M
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textrm{Leg}^{*}\big(p_{i}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\big)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{i}}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x^{i}.

Consequently, the coframe adaptation can be carried out using
\lambda
for the dual cone structure. Hence, this dual formulation can be taken as the starting point for studying causal structures.

2.3.5
Characteristic curves and a quasi-Legendrian foliation

A causal structure comes with a distinguished set of curves which can be defined using the quasi-contact nature of
\mathcal{C}
. Recall from Example
2.2
that a quasi-contact structure (or even contact structure) on a
2n
-dimensional manifolds was defined locally in terms of a 1-form of rank
2n-1
. As a result, there is a unique direction along which
\omega^{0}
and
\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}
are degenerate, i.e.,
[\mathbf{v}]\subset T\mathbb{P}TM
such that
\mathbf{v}\im\omega^{0}=0
and
\mathbf{v}\im\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}=0
, which is called the
characteristic field
of the quasi-contact structure and its integral curves are called
characteristic curves
(cf.
[
HsuCalculus92
]
). A section of the characteristic field is called a
characteristic vector field
.

In local coordinates using the defining function
F
in (
2.1
), one obtains that the vector field

\displaystyle\textstyle F\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}+y^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{a}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{n}}+A^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{a}},

where

\displaystyle A^{b}\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F=-F\partial_{0}\partial_{\underline{a}}F-y^{b}\partial_{b}\partial_{\underline{a}}F+\partial_{a}F+\partial_{0}F\partial_{\underline{a}}F-\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{n}F

represents a characteristic vector field on
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
M
be a
2n
-dimensional manifold with a quasi-contact 1-form
\omega\in\Gamma(T^{*}M)
. A submanifold
N\subset M
is called
quasi-Legendrian
if
T_{x}N\in\operatorname{Ker}\omega
and
T_{x}N\im\mathrm{d}\omega=0
for all
x\in N
, with the property that
T_{x}N
is transversal to the characteristic field of
\omega
.

The bundle
\mathcal{C}
is
2n
-dimensional and
\omega^{0}\in T^{*}\mathcal{C}
has maximal rank
2n-1
, therefore,
\mathcal{C}
has a quasi-contact structure. The fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
form a quasi-Legendrian foliation of
\mathcal{C}
since the vertical tangent spaces
T_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
are annihilated by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bas}}=\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n}\}
.

Moreover, using (
2.6
), the characteristic field of
\omega^{0}
is the kernel of the Pfaffian system

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}=\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1},\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-1}\}
, which justifies the subscript
\mathsf{char}
.

Remark 2.9
.

Recall that the geodesics of a Finsler structure are defined as the projection of integral curves of the Reeb vector field associated to the contact structure on the indicatrix bundle induced by the Hilbert form
[
BryantRemarksFinsler
]
. Here, the projection of the characteristic curves gives an analogue of null geodesics in pseudo-conformal structures.

2.3.6
Projective second fundamental form and the second coframe adaptation

The next coframe adaptation involves the projective second fundamental form of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}\equiv-h^{ab}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}
modulo
I\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1}\}
. Using the identity
\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega^{0}=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle h^{ab}\theta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}=0,

i.e.,
h^{ab}=h^{ba}
.
Using the local expressions of
\omega^{a}
in (
2.3
), one obtains that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{V}\omega^{a}\equiv-\mathrm{d}y^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ I\big\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1}\big\}.

Recall from (
2.5
) that
\mathrm{d}y^{a}=h^{ab}\theta^{0}{}_{b}
where
(h^{ab})=(\partial_{\underline{a}}\partial_{\underline{b}}F)^{-1}
.

Using the structure equations (
2.13
), the infinitesimal action of the structure group
G_{1}
on
h^{ab}
is obtained from the identity
\mathrm{d}^{2}\omega^{a}=0
, and is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h^{ab}+h^{ac}\psi^{b}{}_{c}+h^{bc}\psi^{a}{}_{c}-2h^{ab}(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}.

(2.14)

The relation above implies that the functions
h^{ab}(p,g_{0})
and
\tilde{h}^{ab}(p,g_{1})
where
g_{1}=gg_{0}
, with
g\in G_{1}
represented by the matrix (
2.3.3
), are related by

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{ab}={\mathbf{a}^{2}\mathbf{b}^{2}}(\mathbf{A})^{-1}{}^{a}{}_{c}(\mathbf{A})^{-1}{}^{b}{}_{d}h^{cd}.

(2.15)

It follows that the bilinear form
\phi_{2}=h^{ab}\theta_{a}\circ\theta_{b}
when pulled-back to the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
coincides with its projective second fundamental form (see
[
AG-Projective
,
SasakiBook
]
).

Note that by definition
2.4
, tangential non-degeneracy of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
implies that
h^{ab}
is non-degenerate everywhere in
\mathcal{C}
. As a result, the signature of
h^{ab}
, as a well-defined tensor over
\mathcal{C}
, remains the same everywhere.

Assuming that
h^{ab}
has signature
(p,q)
, the relation (
2.15
) implies that
\mathbf{A}^{a}{}_{b}
can be chosen so that
h^{ab}
is normalized to
\varepsilon^{ab}
. By restricting the
G_{1}
-bundle to the coframes for which
h^{ab}=\varepsilon^{ab}
, and using the relation (
2.14
), it follows that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{ac}\phi^{b}{}_{c}+\varepsilon^{bc}\phi^{a}{}_{c}\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}},\hskip 16.38895pt\mathrm{where}\hskip 8.19447pt\phi^{a}{}_{b}=\psi^{a}{}_{b}-(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})\delta^{a}{}_{b}.

(2.16)

As a result, for such coframes one can replace
\psi^{a}{}_{b}
by
\phi^{a}{}_{b}+(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})\delta^{a}{}_{b}
where the matrix-valued 1-forms
\phi^{a}{}_{b}
take values in
\mathfrak{o}(p,q)
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}

Coframings with normalized
h^{ab}
are
2
-adapted
, and they are local sections of a
G_{2}
-bundle over
\mathcal{C}
where

\displaystyle G_{2}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{2}&0&0&0\\
\mathbf{E}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{A}&0&0\\
\mathbf{e}&\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{b}^{2}&0\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}&0&\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\,\vline\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{O}(p,q),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\\

2.3.7
The third coframe adaptation

The 1-forms
\phi^{a}{}_{b}
are
\mathfrak{o}(p,q)
-valued, i.e.,
\phi_{ab}+\phi_{ba}\equiv 0

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}

where

(2.18a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}=-\gamma^{a}{}_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\phi^{a}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}-(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n}){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}-\varepsilon^{ab}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{{n}}

(2.18d)

where
\varepsilon^{ab}
is used to raise the indices in

and
F_{a}{}^{c}{}_{b}

\displaystyle\phi^{a}{}_{b}\longmapsto\phi^{a}{}_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\big(E^{a}{}_{bc}\omega^{c}-\varepsilon^{ae}E^{d}{}_{ec}\varepsilon_{da}\omega^{c}+\varepsilon_{cd}E^{c}{}_{ab}\omega^{d}\big),

Differentiating

\tau_{ab}=\tau_{ba}
and
\tau^{a}{}_{a}\equiv 0
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}

\displaystyle\big[\big(\mathrm{d}E_{anb}+2E_{anb}\phi_{n}+2E_{cn(a}\phi^{c}{}_{b)}+\pi_{ab}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}-\mathrm{d}(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})\varepsilon_{ab}\big]{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}=0,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}E_{anb}+2E_{anb}\phi_{n}+2E_{cn(a}\phi^{c}{}_{b)}+\big(\pi_{ab}-\textstyle\frac{1}{n-1}\pi_{cd}\varepsilon^{cd}\varepsilon_{ab}\big)\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}.

(2.19)

2.3.8
Symmetries of
\boldsymbol{F^{abc}}

In order to show

is completely symmetric, one first differentiates equation (

) to obtain

\displaystyle 0\equiv\varepsilon^{ab}\big(\mathrm{d}\theta_{b}-\phi^{c}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{c}+(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})\theta_{b}+F^{cd}{}_{b}\theta_{d}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{c}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \omega^{0},\ldots,\omega^{n-1}.

Differentiating equation (

2.3.9
The fourth coframe adaptation, Fubini cubic form and the

The next coframe adaptation reveals the essential invariants of a causal structure, which are the Fubini cubic forms of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
and the Weyl shadow flag curvature (also referred to as the

Differentiating (
2.18b
) and collecting terms of the form
\tau^{ab}{}_{c}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c}

\tau_{a}{}^{b}{}_{c}=\tau_{c}{}^{b}{}_{a}

\displaystyle 0\equiv\big[\big(\mathrm{d}F^{abc}+F^{abd}\phi^{c}{}_{d}+F^{adc}\phi^{b}{}_{d}+F^{dbc}\phi^{a}{}_{d}-F^{abc}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})

\displaystyle\hphantom{0\equiv}{}-\big(\gamma^{n}{}_{d}\varepsilon^{dc}\varepsilon^{ab}-\gamma^{a}{}_{0}\varepsilon^{bc}\big)\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}-\mathrm{d}(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})\varepsilon^{ac}\big]{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega_{c}\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \omega^{0},\omega^{n}.

(2.20)

Contacting by
\varepsilon_{ab}
, and defining

\displaystyle F^{a}:=\varepsilon_{bc}F^{abc},

one arrives at

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})=\textstyle\frac{1}{n-1}\big(\gamma^{b}{}_{0}-\gamma^{n}{}_{d}\varepsilon^{db}+\mathrm{d}F^{b}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}.

Putting this back in (
2.20
), and contracting with
\varepsilon_{bc}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}F^{a}\equiv-F^{b}\phi^{a}{}_{b}+F^{a}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})-\textstyle\frac{n(n-2)}{n-1}\big(\gamma^{a}{}_{0}-\gamma^{n}{}_{b}\varepsilon^{ba}\big)\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}.

As before, the infinitesimal action above makes it possible to translate
F^{a}
to zero. Therefore, the class of
4
-adapted
coframes can be defined by the
apolarity relation

\displaystyle F^{a}=0.

(2.21)

The resulting reduction in the structure group is realized via the relation
\gamma^{a}{}_{0}\equiv\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma^{n}{}_{b}
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}
.

It can be shown that the cubic form

\displaystyle F_{3}=F^{abc}\theta_{a}\circ\theta_{b}\circ\theta_{c},

when pulled-back to the fiber

(see

It is noted that because the second fundamental form is normalized to

, for any choice of 4-adapted coframe, the Fubini cubic form is well-defined tensor on
\mathcal{C}
.

The structure group
G_{4}
is reduced to the group of matrices of the form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{2}&0&0&0\\
\mathbf{E}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{A}&0&0\\
\mathbf{e}&\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{b}^{2}&0\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{c}{}^{t}\mathbf{A}\varepsilon&0&\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}{}^{t}\!\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\,\vline\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{O}(p,q),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\\

\mathbf{E}=\frac{a}{b}\mathbf{A}\,{}^{t}\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right\},

where

denotes the transpose of

with respect to
\varepsilon
.

By setting
\gamma^{a}{}_{0}-\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma^{n}{}_{b}=K^{a}{}_{i0}\,\omega^{i}+K^{ab}\theta_{b}
, the structure equations after the fourth coframe adaptation are of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}=-2\phi_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\theta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a},

(2.22a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}=-\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\phi^{a}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}-(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n}){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}-\varepsilon^{ab}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}=}{}-K^{a}{}_{i0}\omega^{i}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-K^{ab}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-F^{ab}{}_{c}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c},

(2.22b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{n}=-\gamma^{n}{}_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\gamma_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}-2\phi_{n}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n},

(2.22c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\theta_{a}=-\pi_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\varepsilon_{ab}\pi_{n}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}+\phi^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}-(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n}){\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{a}+A^{b}{}_{a}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{d}\theta_{a}=}{}+W_{anbn}\omega^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{n}+B^{bc}{}_{a}\theta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{c}+E_{an}{}^{c}{}_{b}\theta_{c}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}+W_{anbc}\omega^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c},

(2.22d)

where
\gamma^{n}{}_{a}
is being replaced by
\gamma_{a}
.

Differentiating (
2.22a
) yields

\displaystyle A^{a}{}_{b}{}=0,\hskip 16.38895ptW_{anbn}{}=W_{bnan},\hskip 16.38895ptE_{anbc}{}=E_{ancb},

\displaystyle K^{ab}=K^{ba},\hskip 16.38895ptB^{ab}{}_{c}=0,\hskip 16.38895ptW_{[a|n|bc]}=0.

By making the following changes

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}\longmapsto\gamma_{a}-K_{cn0}\omega^{n}-K_{(ab)0}\omega^{a},\hskip 16.38895pt\phi_{n}\longmapsto\phi_{n}-K_{cn0}\,\omega^{c},

\displaystyle\phi^{a}_{b}\longmapsto\phi^{a}_{b}+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\big(T^{a}{}_{bc}\omega^{c}-\varepsilon^{ae}T^{d}{}_{ec}\varepsilon_{da}\omega^{c}+\varepsilon_{cd}T^{c}{}_{ab}\omega^{d}\big)+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\big(K^{a}{}_{b}-\varepsilon^{ac}K^{d}{}_{c}\varepsilon_{eb}\big)\omega^{0},

where

, and
K_{an0}=\varepsilon_{ab}K^{b}{}_{n0}
, it can be assumed that
K^{a}{}_{i0}
vanishes.

By replacing

\displaystyle\pi_{n}\longmapsto\pi_{n}+\textstyle\frac{1}{n-1}\varepsilon^{ac}E_{anbc}\,\theta^{b}-\textstyle{\frac{1}{n-1}}\varepsilon^{cd}W_{cndn}\,\omega^{n},

By differentiating (
2.22b

, and

\displaystyle E_{anbc}=-f_{abc},

where

along the characteristic curves.

2.3.10
The fifth coframe adaptation

There is one more coframe adaptation that can be imposed on any causal structure, which reduces the structure group by one dimension. First, note that the derivative of (
2.22b
) gives

\displaystyle\big(\big(\mathrm{d}K^{ab}+2K^{c(a}\phi^{b)}{}_{c}-2K^{ab}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})-\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma^{n}{}_{0}-F^{abc}\gamma_{c}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}+\mathrm{d}\gamma^{a}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}\equiv 0

(2.23)

modulo
I\{\omega^{1},\dots,\omega^{n}\}
. To find the infinitesimal group action on
K^{ab}
via the relation above one needs to find the 2-form components
\nu^{ab}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}
of
\mathrm{d}\gamma^{a}
where
\nu^{ab}\not\equiv 0
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}
. Differentiating (
2.22c
), it follows that

\displaystyle\big(\mathrm{d}\gamma_{a}+F^{bc}{}_{a}\gamma_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{c}+\Lambda_{a}\big){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}\equiv 0,

where
\Lambda_{a}
is of the form
\nu^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}
with
\nu^{b}{}_{a}\equiv 0
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}
. Inserting the above expression of
\mathrm{d}\gamma_{a}
into (
2.23
), one sees that the infinitesimal action of the structure group
G_{5}
on
K^{ab}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}K^{ab}+2K^{c(a}\phi^{b)}{}_{c}-2K^{ab}(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n})+\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma^{n}{}_{0}-2F^{abc}\gamma_{c}\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}.

Note that
K^{ab}
is not a tensor as its infinitesimal transformation rule in terms of the structure group involves
F^{ab}{}_{c}
.

Using the apolarity relation (
2.21
), and the fact that
K_{ab}
and
\phi_{ab}
are symmetric and skew-symmetric respectively, the infinitesimal action of the structure group
G_{4}
on
K:=\varepsilon_{ab}K^{ab}
is found to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}K+K(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})+(n-1)\gamma^{n}{}_{0}\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}}.

Now, the
5
-adapted
coframes can be defined by the property that
K=0
. Hence, one obtains

\displaystyle\gamma^{0}{}_{n}=M_{ni}\omega^{i}+L^{a}\theta_{a}.

By replacing

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}\longmapsto\gamma_{a}-M_{na}\,\omega^{0}-\varepsilon_{ab}M_{nn}\,\omega^{b},\hskip 16.38895pt\phi_{n}\longmapsto\phi_{n}-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}M_{nn}\,\omega^{0},

it can be assumed that
M_{ni}=0
.

The 5-adapted coframes are local sections of a
G_{5}
-bundle where
G_{5}
is the matrix group defined by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{2}&0&0&0\\
\mathbf{E}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{A}&0&0\\
\mathbf{e}&\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{b}^{2}&0\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{c}\,{}^{t}\!\mathbf{A}\varepsilon&0&\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}\,{}^{t}\!\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\,\vline\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{O}(p,q),\hskip 8.19447pt{}^{t}\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\\
\mathbf{E}=\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{A}\,{}^{t}\mathbf{B},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbf{e}=\frac{1}{2\mathbf{b}^{2}}\mathbf{B}\,{}^{t}\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right\}.

2.3.11
Invariant conformal quadratic form

According to (
2.3.10
), the structure group
G_{5}
preserves the conformal class of the quadratic form

\displaystyle g:=2\omega^{0}\circ\omega^{n}-\varepsilon_{ab}\omega^{a}\circ\omega^{b},

(2.27)

which is semi-basic with respect to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
. At

the null cone of

in

gives the osculating quadric of second order for
\mathcal{C}_{x}
at

.

As will be shown in Section

, the vanishing of the Fubini form implies that the conformal class of

is well-defined on

and that the null cones of the causal structure coincide with those of

. In that case, the
\mathfrak{o}(p,q)
-valued part of the associated conformal connection is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{n}-\phi_{0}&\theta&0\\
{}^{t}\!\gamma&\phi&{}^{t}\theta\\

where
{}^{t}\!\gamma
and

are column vectors of 1-forms
\gamma^{a}:=\varepsilon^{ab}\gamma_{b}
and

will be used to raise and lower the indices

,
b
,

.

2.3.12
The first order structure equations

Having carried out five coframe adaptations, the first structure equations for causal structures can now be expressed as follows.

The pseudo-connection form for the first order structure group is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2\phi_{0}&0&0&0\\
\gamma^{a}&\phi^{a}{}_{b}+\left(\phi_{0}+\phi_{n}\right)\delta^{a}{}_{b}&0&0\\
0&\gamma_{b}&2\phi_{{n}}&0\\
\pi_{a}&\varepsilon_{ab}\pi_{n}&0&-\phi^{a}{}_{b}+(\phi_{0}-\phi_{{n}})\delta^{a}{}_{b}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.28)

Consequently, the structure equations can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}=-2\phi_{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-{}\theta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a},

(2.29a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}=-\gamma^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\phi^{a}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{b}-(\phi_{0}+\phi_{{n}}){\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}-\theta^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{{n}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{d}\omega^{a} =}{}-K^{a}{}_{b}\theta^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-F^{a}{}_{bc}\theta^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c},

(2.29b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{{n}}=-\gamma_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{a}-2\phi_{{n}}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{{n}}-L^{a}\theta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0},

(2.29c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\theta_{a}=-\pi_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{0}-\pi_{{n}}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega_{a}+\phi^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{b}-\left(\phi_{0}-\phi_{{n}}\right){\,{\wedge}\;}\theta_{a}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{d}\theta_{a} =}{}-f_{abc}\theta^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c}+W_{anbn}\omega^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{{n}}+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}W_{anbc}\omega^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\omega^{c},

(2.29d)

with the following symmetries

\displaystyle F_{abc}=F_{(abc)},\hskip 16.38895ptF^{a}{}_{ab}=0,\hskip 16.38895ptK^{ab}=K^{ba},\hskip 16.38895ptK^{a}{}_{a}=0,\hskip 16.38895ptf_{abc}=F_{abc;n},

\displaystyle W_{anbn}=W_{bnan},\hskip 16.38895ptW^{a}{}_{nan}=0,\hskip 16.38895pt\hskip 8.19447ptW_{anbc}=-W_{0acb},\hskip 16.38895ptW_{[a|n|bc]}=0.

(2.30)

The infinitesimal group action on the torsion coefficients is equivalent to saying that the following 1-forms are semi-basic with respect to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}F_{abc}:=\mathrm{d}F_{abc}-F_{abd}\phi^{d}{}_{c}-F_{adc}\phi^{d}{}_{b}-F_{dbc}\phi^{d}{}_{a}-F_{abc}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n}),

(2.31a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}K_{ab}:=\mathrm{d}K_{ab}-K_{ac}\phi^{c}{}_{b}-K_{bc}\phi^{c}{}_{a}-2K_{ab}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})-2F_{abc}\gamma^{c},

(2.31b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}L_{a}:=\mathrm{d}L_{a}-L_{b}\,\phi^{b}{}_{a}-3L^{a}(\phi_{0}-\phi_{n})+K^{ab}\gamma_{b},

(2.31c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}W_{anbn}:=\mathrm{d}W_{anbn}-W_{ancn}\phi^{c}{}_{b}-W_{bncn}\phi^{c}{}_{a}-4W_{anbn}\phi_{n},

(2.31d)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}W_{anbc}:=\mathrm{d}W_{anbc}-W_{dnbc}\phi^{d}{}_{a}-W_{andc}\phi^{d}{}_{b}-W_{anbd}\phi^{d}{}_{c}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{D}W_{anbc} :=}{}-W_{anbc}(\phi_{0}-3\phi_{n})+(W_{anbn}\varepsilon_{cd}+W_{ancn}\varepsilon_{bd})\gamma^{d}.

(2.31e)

By the infinitesimal actions (
2.31
), it follows that no more reduction of the structure group is possible, unless certain non-vanishing conditions on the torsion coefficients are assumed. Such special cases are considered in subsequent sections.

2.3.13
An
\boldsymbol{\{e\}}
-structure

Using the first order structure equations, an
\{e\}

Theorem 2.10

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\phi+\phi{\,{\wedge}\;}\phi=\begin{pmatrix}-\Phi_{0}-\Phi_{n}&-\Pi_{n}&-\Pi_{b}&-\Pi_{0}&0\\

2.3.14
The case of conformal structures

Using the first order structure equations, an observation is made regarding (pseudo-)conformal structures viewed as causal structures.

A causal structure

of signature

with vanishing Fubini cubic form induces a pseudo-conformal structure of the same signature on

Proof.

To show the first part, note that if the Fubini form vanishes then, by the Bianchi identities (
A.14
), the quantities
K_{ab}
and
L_{a}
vanish as well. The first order structure equations (
2.28
) can be used to show that the Lie derivatives of the quadratic form

, defined in Section

, along vertical vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{n-1}}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}}g=\lambda g,

(2.33)

for some non-vanishing function
\lambda
on
\mathcal{C}
. The vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{i}}
, and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}
are defined as dual to the coframe
\omega^{0},\dots,\theta_{n-1}
. The vertical vector fields are integrable with the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
as their integral manifolds, and
M
as the quotient space. Consequently, the conformal class of the quadratic form

descends to

and because it is non-degenerate and of maximal rank on

-structure is given by the Maurer–Cartan forms of

as well, implying that the resulting pseudo-conformal structure has to be flat too.

The converse part is shown similarly. Given a pseudo-conformal structure of signature
(p+1,q+1)

(\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n})

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
. Using conformal transformations,
\omega^{i}

Because the construction is reversible, the result follows from the first part of the proposition.

The following corollary follows immediately.

If a pseudo-conformal structure lifts to a causal structure with vanishing

By Proposition

2.4.1
Parabolic geometries and Tanaka theory

A parabolic geometry is a Cartan geometry
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
of type
(G,P)
where

is a real or complex semi-simple Lie group and

\omega

The 1-form

1)

is the fundamental field of

3)

(R_{h})^{*}\omega=\mathrm{Ad}(h^{-1})\omega

A canonical way of identifying a geometric structure as a parabolic geometry is by characterizing it in terms of certain

possibly equipped with a tensor field, e.g., conformal structures or semi-Riemannian structures. Recall the following definitions. To any distribution

, one can associate a Pfaffian system

\displaystyle D^{\perp}:=\{\omega\in T^{*}\mathcal{C}\,|\,\omega(u)=0\ \forall\,u\in D\}.

Given a distribution

and its

is defined by
D^{2}=D+[D,D]

means a distribution whose sections are obtained from
\Gamma(D)+[\Gamma(D),\Gamma(D)]

D^{1}=D
. The

\displaystyle D^{k+1}=D^{k}+\big[D^{k},D\big].

One obtains that

\displaystyle\big(D^{k+1}\big)^{\perp}=\big\{\omega\in\big(D^{k}\big)^{\perp}\,\big|\,\mathrm{d}\omega\equiv 0\ \operatorname{mod}\ \big(D^{k}\big)^{\perp}\big\}.

.
More directly, by the discussion in Section
2.4.2
and the first order structure equations, one obtains that
\operatorname{Aut}_{\rm gr}(\mathfrak{g}_{-})
is reduced to
G_{0}
. Thus, by Tanaka prolongation one can view causal structures as regular, normal parabolic geometries of type
(B_{n-1},P_{1,2})
or
(D_{n},P_{1,2})
if
n\geq 4
, or
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
. In Section
2.5.1
it is discussed that when
n\geq 4
, causal structures are canonically isomorphic to regular, normal parabolic geometries of type
(B_{n-1},P_{1,2})
or
(D_{n},P_{1,2})
. When
n=3
, causal structures are
“subgeometries”
of regular, normal parabolic geometries of type
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
, i.e., the integrability of the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{x}
, implies the vanishing of one of the harmonic curvature components of such parabolic geometries.

Knowing the existence of a normal Cartan connection for causal structures, the normality conditions of Tanaka are straightforward to obtain and can be used to recursively define the correct change of basis which transforms the
\{e\}
-structure to a Cartan connection (see
[
Omid-Thesis
]
). Since finding the explicit expressions for the change of basis is very tedious and the result is not particularly illuminating, finding the normal Cartan connection is not carried out here.

2.5
Essential invariants and the Raychaudhuri equation

In this section the essential invariants whose vanishing imply the flatness of the causal structure are introduced. Their geometric interpretation is given. A variational problem is naturally associated to a causal geometry which allows one to define the notion of null Jacobi fields in the causal setting. Finally, the section ends with a discussion on associated Weyl connections of a causal structure and a derivation of the Raychaudhuri equation for causal structures. In this section there are references to Appendix
A
, particularly to the
\{e\}
-structure obtained in Section
A.1.4
.

2.5.1
The harmonic curvature

According to the
\{e\}
-structure obtained in Section
A.1.4
, obstructions to the flatness of a causal structure, as listed in Sections
A.2
and
, are generated by
F_{abc}
and
W_{anbn}
. For instance, differentiating (
2.29d
) gives

\displaystyle W_{anbc}=\tfrac{2}{3}\big(W_{an[b|n;|{\underline{c}}]}+F_{a[c}{}^{d}W_{b]ndn}\big)+\tfrac{4}{3(n+2)}\big(\varepsilon_{a[c|}W^{d}{}_{n|b]n;{\underline{d}}}+\varepsilon_{a[b}F^{de}{}_{c]}W_{dnen}\big).

In Section
A.2
, the quantities
W_{ijkl}
are expressed as the coframe derivatives of
W_{anbn}
with respect to
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{a}}
. As a result, in the case
F_{abc}=0
, the vanishing of
W_{nabn}
implies flatness of the causal structure.

For an understanding of
W_{anbn}
, consider a 2-plane in
T_{x}M
given by
\lambda{\,{\wedge}\;}\mu
where
\lambda=\lambda^{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
,
\mu=\mu^{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\in TM
for some basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\}
. The
sectional conformal curvature
of a pseudo-conformal structure evaluated at
\lambda{\,{\wedge}\;}\mu
is defined by

\displaystyle W(\lambda{\,{\wedge}\;}\mu)=W_{ijkl}\lambda^{i}\mu^{j}\lambda^{k}\mu^{l}.

As a result, at the point
(x,[y])\in\mathcal{C}
, the invariant
W_{nabn}
can be interpreted as the part of the conformal sectional curvature acting on 2-planes
\xi^{a}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}{\,{\wedge}\;}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}
. According to the construction of the coframes in (
2.4
),
\xi
is a subset of
\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}
and contains
\hat{y}
where
[\hat{y}]=y\in\mathcal{C}_{x}
. It follows that
\xi^{a}=\mu^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}y^{i}\partial_{i}
, where
\mu^{a}
is an element of the
shadow space

\displaystyle S_{(x,[y])}:=\widehat{T}_{y}\mathcal{C}_{x}/\hat{y}.

(2.35)

As a result,
W_{anbn}
can be thought of as the restriction of the bilinear form
W_{ijkl}y^{j}y^{l}
to the shadow space. This restriction is well-defined up to scale. Infinitesimally, this scaling action is realized by the 1-form
\phi_{n}
as is apparent from (
2.31d
).

Finally, it can be argued that the Fubini cubic form and the
Wsf
curvature coincide with the harmonic curvatures of the parabolic geometries that correspond to causal structures. By the discussion in Section
2.4.3
, causal structures on an
(n+1)
-dimensional manifold correspond to parabolic geometries of type
(B_{n-1},P_{1,2})
and
(D_{n},P_{1,2})
if
n\geq 4
, and
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
. As discussed in Section
2.4.1
, a grading is induced on the cohomology classes of parabolic geometries. Moreover, the set of essential invariants for a parabolic geometry is given by the harmonic curvature. It turns out that for parabolic geometries of type
(B_{n-1},P_{1,2})
and
(D_{n},P_{1,2})
if
n\geq 4
, the infinitesimal flag structure is the same as causal structures and the harmonic curvature has two irreducible components. Using the grading (
2.34
) of the curvature, one component has homogeneity one whose
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-irreducible representation is given as a totally symmetric and trace-free cubic tensor. The other irreducible component has homogeneity two whose
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-irreducible representation is given as a symmetric and trace-free bilinear form (see
[
ANNMonge
,
KT-gap
,
Yamaguchi-SimpleDiffSystems
]
). The structure equations of causal geometries imply that the essential invariant
F_{abc}
corresponds to a cohomology class of homogeneity one and the
Wsf
curvature corresponds to a cohomology class of homogeneity two. Also, from the infinitesimal group actions (
2.31a
) and (
2.31d
) one obtains that the Fubini cubic form and the
Wsf
curvature are
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-irreducible. Moreover, one can impose the Tanaka normality conditions on the pseudo-connection form of the
\{e\}
-structure to derive a normal Cartan connection (see
[
Omid-Thesis
]
). It turns out that the necessary change of coframe does not affect the Fubini cubic form and the
Wsf
curvature, i.e., the essential invariants of the
\{e\}
-structure coincide with the harmonic curvature.

The situation is different in four dimensions. Four dimensional causal structures are a special class of parabolic geometries of type
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
. The harmonic curvature of these parabolic geometries is five dimensional, three of which have homogeneity one and two have homogeneity two. Consider a four dimensional causal structure of split signature. The Fubini cubic form of projective surfaces and the
Wsf
curvature are comprised of two entries.It turns out that all these four entries are irreducible under the action of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. As a result, four dimensional causal structures are special cases of parabolic geometries of type
(D_{3},P_{1,2,3})
in which one of the harmonic curvatures of homogeneity one is zero. The vanishing of this piece of harmonic curvature amounts to the fibration
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow M
with 2-dimensional fibers.

2.5.3
Null Jacobi fields and the tidal force

As was mentioned in Section
2.3.5
, the characteristic curves of causal structures is intrinsically defined by the quasi-contact structure of
\mathcal{C}
. Nevertheless, one can associate a
variational problem
to causal geometries via the Griffiths formalism
[
Griffiths-EDS-Book
]
(see also
[
HsuCalculus92
]
). As a result, the notion of null Jacobi fields naturally arises. The corresponding Jacobi equations result in another interpretation of the
Wsf
curvature. The fact that Jacobi equations of null Jacobi fields involve the
Wsf
curvature has to do with the second Lie derivative of
g
in (
2.27
), which is referred to as the
tidal force

[
O'neill-Semi-Riemannian
]
.

Recall that for the Pfaffian system
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}=\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1},\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{n-1}\}
, after fixing an interval
I=(a,b)
, one can define the space of smooth immersions of its integral curves as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{U}}(I)=\{\gamma\colon I\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\,|\,\gamma^{*}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{tot}})=0\}.

The space
\widehat{\mathscr{U}}(I)
can be given a Whitney
C^{\infty}
-topology. After making a choice of orientation for the characteristic curves, the space of the characteristic curves of
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathscr{U}(I)
, is defined as
\widehat{\mathscr{U}}(I)/\mathrm{Diff}(I)
, i.e., the space obtained from
\widehat{\mathscr{U}}(I)
by identifying the characteristic curves that are reparametrizations of each other.

Consider the functional
\Phi\colon\mathscr{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which at a point
[\gamma]\in\mathscr{U}(I)
is defined by

\displaystyle\Phi([\gamma])=\int_{I}\gamma^{*}\omega^{0}.

The triple
(\mathcal{C},I,\omega^{0})
is referred to as a
variational problem
on
\mathcal{C}
, and is intrinsic to any causal structure.
Since
\omega^{0}(\dot{\gamma})=0
everywhere, the above definition of
\Phi
is independent of the choice of representative
\gamma\in[\gamma]
.
This is in contrast with Finsler geometry where the geodesics are defined as the extremal curves of the arc-length functional which is always positive and are equipped with a specific parametrization.

Intuitively, the infinitesimal variations of a characteristic curve
\gamma
can be identified with the tangent space
T_{\gamma}\mathscr{U}(I)
which is the space of smooth variational vector fields along
\gamma
. To give a more precise definition consider a parametrization for
I=(a,b)
and a map
\Gamma\colon(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow\mathscr{U}(I)
. As a result of such parametrization, the curves given by
\Gamma(s)
can be parametrized. Using this parametrization, one can define
\widehat{\Gamma}\colon(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\times I\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, as
\widehat{\Gamma}(s,t)=(\Gamma(s))(t)
. The map
\Gamma
is called a compact variation of the curve
\gamma
if the corresponding map
\widehat{\Gamma}
is smooth,
\widehat{\Gamma}(0,t)=\gamma(t)
,
\widehat{\Gamma}^{*}(s_{0},t)(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}})=0
, for any fixed
s_{0}
, and
\widehat{\Gamma}
is compactly supported in
I
, i.e., for all values of
s
, the curves
\widehat{\Gamma}(s,t)
and
\widehat{\Gamma}(0,t)
coincide outside of a compact subset of
I
. Then,
T_{\gamma}\mathscr{U}(I)
is expressed as

\displaystyle T_{\gamma}\mathscr{U}(I):=\left\{\frac{\partial\widehat{\Gamma}}{\partial s}(0,t)\,\vline\,\Gamma\colon(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\rightarrow\mathscr{U}(I)\mathrm{\ is\ a\ compact\ variation\ of\ }\gamma\right\}.

The
variational equations
of a characteristic curve can be thought of as the first order approximation of
T_{\gamma}\mathscr{U}(I)
. A practical derivation of the variational equations can be given in terms of the Pfaffian system
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}
.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}
, and
\Gamma
a variation of the characteristic curve
\gamma
. By the definition of
\widehat{\Gamma}(s,t)
, one has
\widehat{\Gamma}^{*}(s_{0},t)\eta=0
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\partial}{\partial s}}\widehat{\Gamma}^{*}(s,t)\eta\equiv 0
modulo
I\{\mathrm{d}s\}
. Setting
s=0,
the latter expression implies that

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}\big(\hat{J}\im\mathrm{d}\eta+\mathrm{d}(\hat{J}\im\eta)\big)=0,

where
\hat{J}(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\widehat{\Gamma}(0,t)
is the infinitesimal variations associated to
\Gamma(s)
.

In the expression of the variational equations one can drop the pull-back
\gamma^{*}
and write them as

\displaystyle\hat{J}\im\mathrm{d}\eta+\mathrm{d}\big(\hat{J}\im\eta\big)\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}.

The tangential component of the vector field
\hat{J}(t)
along
\gamma(t)
has no effect on the variational equations, hence
\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{n}}
will be dropped from the expressions. Therefore,
\hat{J}
along the curve
\gamma(t)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{J}(t)=V^{a}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}+V^{{\underline{a}}}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{a}}.

The vector field
J=\pi_{*}\hat{J}
is called a
null Jacobi field
along the null geodesics
\pi(\gamma(t))\subset M
.

The variational equations for
\hat{J}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{J}\im\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}+\mathrm{d}\big(\hat{J}\im\omega^{0}\big)=0,

(2.38a)

\displaystyle\hat{J}\im\mathrm{d}\omega^{a}+\mathrm{d}\big(\hat{J}\im\omega^{a}\big)=0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}:=I\big\{\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{n-1},\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-1}\big\}.

(2.38b)

\displaystyle\hat{J}\im\mathrm{d}\theta_{a}+\mathrm{d}\big(\hat{J}\im\theta_{a}\big)=0,

(2.38c)

equation (
2.38a
) gives no new information. The last two equations give

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V^{a}+V^{b}\phi^{a}{}_{b}-V^{\underline{a}}\omega^{n}\equiv 0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V^{\underline{a}}+V^{\underline{b}}\phi^{a}{}_{b}+W^{a}{}_{nbn}V^{b}\omega^{n}\equiv 0\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{char}}.

(2.39)

Using the pseudo-connection from Section
2.3.12
, the covariant derivative along characteristic curves of vector fields lying within the shadow space (
2.35
) is given by

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{v}}\hat{J}\equiv\mathbf{v}\im\left(\mathrm{d}V^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}+V^{b}\phi^{a}{}_{b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}\right)\hskip 16.38895pt\operatorname{mod}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{n-1}},

where
\mathbf{v}
is the characteristic vector field satisfying
\omega^{n}(\mathbf{v})=1
.

Using equations (
2.39
), it follows that

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{v}}D_{\mathbf{v}}\hat{J}\equiv\begin{cases}-W^{a}{}_{nbn}V^{b}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{a}}&\operatorname{mod}\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{1}},\dots,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{n-1}},\\
-\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{W}\hat{J}&\operatorname{mod}\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{1}},\dots,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{n-1}},\end{cases}

(2.40)

where
\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{W}
is viewed as an automorphism of the shadow space. Recall from Section
2.5.1
that
W^{a}{}_{nbn}
is given by
\omega^{a}(W(\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{b}},\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}
. Hence, defining

\displaystyle J^{\prime}=\pi_{*}\big(\mathrm{D}_{\mathbf{v}}\hat{J}\big),

the null Jacobi equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}+W(J,\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}=0,

(2.41)

where
J=\pi_{*}\hat{J}
and
W(J,\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}
denotes
\pi_{*}\big(W(\hat{J},\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\big)
. For convenience, in Section
2.5.4
equation (
2.41
) is expressed as

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}+\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{W}J=0.

(2.42)

In some references
[
O'neill-Semi-Riemannian
]
(cf.
[
HS2011Causal
]
) the automorphism of the shadow space
W^{a}{}_{nba}\colon S_{(x,[y])}\rightarrow S_{(x,[y])}
is called the trace-free part of the
tidal force
in analogy with the terminology from general relativity.
Equation (
2.40
) shows that variational problem for null geodesics only depends on
Wsf
curvature and not the flag curvature.

2.5.4
The Raychaudhuri equation

In this section, a causal analogue of the Raychaudhuri equation along null geodesics is derived. The approach that is taken here is presented in
[
BEE-Global
, Chapter 12]
.

Taking a section of the structure bundle,
s\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
, define the null Jacobi fields
\hat{J}_{1}(t),\dots
,
\hat{J}_{n-1}(t)
along the characteristic curve
\gamma(t)\subset\mathcal{C}
,
t\in[a,b]
by the initial conditions

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{a}(t_{0})=0,\hskip 16.38895pt\mathrm{D}_{\mathbf{v}(t_{0})}\hat{J}_{a}(t_{0})=\frac{\partial}{\partial(s^{*}\omega^{a})},

where
t_{0}\in[a,b]
and
\mathbf{v}(t)
is tangent vector to
\gamma(t)
. Define the
(1,1)
tensor field called the
null Jacobi tensor
as

\displaystyle A(t)=[J_{1}(t),\dots,J_{n-1}(t)],

which is a
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix whose
b
th column is the null Jacobi field
J_{b}(t)=\pi_{*}(\hat{J}_{b}(t))
.

The optical invariants along null geodesics are defined as follows.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
A
be a null Jacobi tensor along a null geodesic and define
B=A^{\prime}A^{-1}
, where
A
is invertible. The
expansion
,
\theta
,
vorticity tensor,

\omega
and
shear tensor
,
\sigma
, are defined as

\displaystyle\theta=B^{a}{}_{a},\hskip 16.38895pt\omega^{a}{}_{b}=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\big(B^{a}{}_{b}-(B^{*})^{a}{}_{b}\big),\hskip 16.38895pt\sigma^{a}{}_{b}=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\big(B^{a}{}_{b}+(B^{*})^{a}{}_{b}\big)-\frac{\theta}{n-1}\delta^{a}{}_{b},

where
B^{*}
denotes the adjoint matrix with respect to the bilinear form
s^{*}g
.

Following the discussion in Section
2.5.3
, after taking a section of the structure bundle one obtains that a null Jacobi field satisfies the equation

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}+\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R}J=0,

rather than equation (
2.42
), where
\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R}
represents the matrix form of the shadow flag curvature
R^{a}{}_{nbn}=W^{a}{}_{nbn}+p_{nn}\delta^{a}{}_{b}
defined in (
2.37
).

It follows that

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}+\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R}A=0.

Moreover, one has

\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\big(A^{\prime}A^{-1}\big)^{\prime}=A^{\prime\prime}A^{-1}-A^{\prime}A^{-1}A^{\prime}A^{-1}=-\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R}-B^{2}.

Since
B=\omega+\sigma+\frac{\theta}{n-1}\mathrm{Id}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}=\operatorname{tr}(B^{\prime})=-\operatorname{tr}(\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R})-\operatorname{tr}(B^{2})=-\operatorname{tr}(\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R})-\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\omega+\sigma+\frac{\theta}{n-1}\mathrm{Id}\right)^{2}\right].

Using the fact that
\operatorname{tr}(\omega)=\operatorname{tr}(\sigma)=\operatorname{tr}(\omega\sigma)=0
and
\operatorname{tr}(\overset{\mathrm{sf}}{R})=(n-1)p_{nn}
one arrives at

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}=-(n-1)p_{nn}-\operatorname{tr}\big(\omega^{2}\big)-\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma^{2}\big)-\frac{\theta^{2}}{n-1},

which is the causal analogue of the Raychaudhuri equation along null geodesics.

2.6
Examples

In this section three examples of causal structures are given. The first one is obtained from the product of two Finsler structures and the second and third ones are given in terms of a defining function.

2.6.1
Product of Finsler structures

A Finsler manifold is denoted by
(N,\Sigma)
, where
N
is
n
-dimensional and
\Sigma\subset TN
is of codimension one whose fibers
\Sigma_{p}\subset T_{p}N
are strictly convex hypersurfaces. The fiber bundle
\Sigma
is often referred to as the indicatrix bundle over which the Finsler metric has unit value. It is known
[
BryantRemarksFinsler
,
CartanFinsler
]
that
\Sigma
is equipped with unique coframing
\eta=\big(\eta^{0},\eta^{1},\dots,\eta^{n-1},\zeta^{1},\dots,\zeta^{n-1}\big)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\eta^{0}=-\zeta_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{a},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\eta^{a}=\phantom{-}\zeta^{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{0}-\psi^{a}{}_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{b}-I^{ab}{}_{c}\zeta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{c},

(2.43)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\zeta_{a}=\phantom{-}\psi^{b}{}_{a}{\,{\wedge}\;}\zeta_{b}+R_{0a0b}\eta^{0}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{b}+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}R_{0abc}\eta^{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{c}-J_{a}{}^{b}{}_{c}\zeta_{b}{\,{\wedge}\;}\eta^{c}

for some invariants
I_{abc}
,
J_{abc}
,
R_{0a0b}
and
R_{0abc}
. The 1-form
\eta^{0}
is called the Hilbert form of the Finsler structure which, at the point
v\in\Sigma_{p}
, is uniquely characterized by the conditions

\displaystyle\eta^{0}(H_{v}\Sigma_{p})=0,\hskip 16.38895pt\eta^{0}(v)=1,

where
H_{v}\Sigma_{p}\subset T_{p}N
is the tangent hyperplane to
\Sigma_{p}
at the point
v
. As a result of the structure equation,
\eta^{0}
induces a contact structure on
\Sigma
whose associated Reeb vector field defines the geodesic flow of the Finsler structure on
\Sigma
. Moreover, the quadratic form
\omega^{0}\circ\omega^{0}+\delta_{ab}\omega^{b}\circ\omega^{c}
is well-defined for the Finsler structure. It turns out that the quantity
I_{abc}=\delta_{ad}\delta_{be}I^{de}{}_{c}
can be used to define the cubic form
I_{abc}\zeta^{a}\circ\zeta^{b}\circ\zeta^{c}
, which, when restricted to a fiber
\Sigma_{p}
, coincides with the centro-affine cubic form of the hypersurface
\Sigma_{p}\subset T_{p}N
. The quantities
J_{abc}
measure the rate of change of
I_{abc}
along the geodesics, i.e.,
J_{abc}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta^{0}}I_{abc}
. The quantity
R_{0a0b}
is symmetric using which the
flag curvature
of the Finsler structure can be represented by

\displaystyle R_{0a0b}\eta^{a}\circ\eta^{b}.

Let
(N,\Sigma)
and
(\tilde{N},\tilde{\Sigma})
be two Finsler manifolds.
Given a strictly positive function
f
on their product space, at a point
p\in M=N\times{}_{f}\tilde{N}
, one can define a projective hypersurface whose associated cone is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{p}=\big\{(v,\tilde{v})\in T_{p}\big(N\times\tilde{N}\big)\,|\,\lambda fv\in\Sigma_{\pi(p)},\lambda\tilde{v}\in\tilde{\Sigma}_{\tilde{\pi}(p)}\mathrm{\ for\ some\ }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

As a result,
M
is equipped with a causal structure of signature
(n,\tilde{n})
where
\tilde{n}=\dim\tilde{N}
. Note that it is assumed that the Finsler structures are reversible.

If
G
and
\tilde{G}
represent the Finsler metrics on
N
and
\tilde{N}

First, a definition of Lie contact structures in terms of a field of Segré cones of type

Let

\displaystyle z^{1}_{\mu}z^{2}_{\nu}-z^{2}_{\mu}z^{1}_{\nu}=0,

has a double ruling. A

-parameter ruling by 2-planes

-planes

is given by the 1-parameter family of
\mathsf{N}
-planes

Proof.

By the matrix form of the structure group

modulo

As a result of the proposition above, the set of tangent vectors

Definition 3.4

A manifold

with a contact distribution

\beta
