Abstract:
We establish high probability estimates on the eigenvalue locations of Brownian motion on the
N
-dimensional unitary group, as well as estimates on the number of eigenvalues lying in any interval on the unit circle. These estimates are optimal up to arbitrarily small polynomial factors in
N
. Our results hold at the spectral edges (showing that the extremal eigenvalues are within
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/3+})
of the edges of the limiting spectral measure), in the spectral bulk, as well as for times near
4
at which point the limiting spectral measure forms a cusp. Our methods are dynamical and are based on analyzing the evolution of the Cauchy transform of the empirical spectral measure along the characteristics of the PDE satisfied by the limiting spectral measure, that of the free unitary Brownian motion.

Introduction and main results

†
†
footnotetext:

In this work we study Brownian motion on the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(N)
of dimension
N
. One can define Brownian motion on
\mathbb{U}(N)
by considering the left-invariant Riemannian metric induced by the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=N\mathrm{tr}(AB^{*})
on the Lie algebra of skew-Hermitian matrices. Unitary Brownian motion is then the Markov diffusion process starting from the identity matrix with generator given by the Laplacian on
\mathbb{U}(N)
associated to this metric.

It will be more convenient for us to consider the following equivalent definition of
U_{t}
as the solution of the Itô stochastic differential equation,

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}=\mathrm{i}U_{t}\mathrm{d}W_{t}-\frac{1}{2}U_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\qquad U_{0}=\bm{1}

where
W_{t}
is a standard complex Hermitian Brownian motion. That is, if
X_{t}
and
X^{\prime}_{t}
are
N\times N
matrices of independent standard Brownian motions, then,

(1.2)

W_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4N}}\left(X_{t}+X_{t}^{T}+\mathrm{i}(X^{\prime}_{t}-(X^{\prime}_{t})^{T})\right).

The process (
1.1
) admits strong solutions by standard results (see, e.g., Theorem 8.3 of
[
34
]
).

Unitary Brownian motion is well-studied in random matrix theory as well as in the context of free probability due to its connection with an object called free unitary Brownian motion. Of particular interest is the empirical spectral measure of
U_{t}
, which is a random, time-dependent measure on the unit circle defined by,

(1.3)

\mathrm{d}\nu_{N,t}(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(t)}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

In the work
[
9
]
, Biane showed that for fixed
t
, the measure
\nu_{N,t}
converges almost surely to a measure on the unit circle which we will denote by
\nu_{t}
. Identifying the unit circle with the angular coordinates
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]
, the measure
\nu_{t}
has a density
\rho_{t}(\theta)
for any
t>0
. For
t<4
the support of
\rho_{t}
is given by,

(1.4)

I_{t}:=[-\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t}],\qquad\Theta_{t}:=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(4-t)t}+2\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{4}}\right),

whereas for
t\geq 4
, the support is the entire unit circle. Moreover, for
t\geq 4
the density is everywhere non-zero unless
t=4
in which case
\rho_{t}
vanishes only at
\pi
. In fact,
\rho_{t}
can be described as the spectral measure of free unitary Brownian motion, an object appearing in free probability. The limit of the
\nu_{N,t}
was also derived independently by Rains in
[
42
]
.

Since Biane’s paper, there have been many works studying the convergence of
\nu_{N,t}
to
\rho_{t}
. Concentration estimates and convergence for the empirical averages
\int f\mathrm{d}\nu_{N,t}
were established by Kemp
[
30
]
for various classes of
f
of low regularity. Meckes and Melcher
[
41
]
established explicit convergence rates in terms of the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein metric. For
0<t<4
, the convergence of the spectral edge of
U_{t}
to
\pm\Theta_{t}
was established by Collins, Dahlqvist and Kemp
[
16
]
. This work also established a multi-time, multi-matrix version of this result. The asymptotic Gaussian fluctuations of the empirical averages of
\int f\mathrm{d}\nu_{N,t}
were established by Lévy and Maida
[
37
]
. Multivariate fluctuations for trace polynomials of a two parameter family of diffusion processes (including unitary Brownian motion as a special case) were studied by Cébron and Kemp
[
12
]
.

The main contribution of the present work is to establish almost-optimal rates (i.e., up to polynomial
N^{\varepsilon}
factors) of convergence of
\nu_{N,t}
to the limiting distribution
\rho_{t}
on the almost-shortest possible scales, as well as almost-optimal estimates on the eigenvalue locations. In the random matrix literature, these estimates are known as
local laws
and
rigidity
estimates, respectively. Our local laws are stated in Theorem
1.2
and in Corollary
1.3
below, showing that the number of eigenvalues in any sub-interval
I
of the unit circle is given by
N\rho_{t}(I)+\mathcal{O}(N^{\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

For times
t<4
we establish almost-optimal rates of convergence of the spectral edge of
U_{t}
to
\pm\Theta_{t}
. That is, the extremal eigenvalues are within distance
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/3+\varepsilon})
of
\pm\Theta_{t}
(what is usually termed
edge rigidity
in the literature). Given the square-root behavior of the spectral measure at the edges, this is expected to be optimal up to the
N^{\varepsilon}
factor. We also derive almost-optimal edge rigidity results up to
t=4-N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
at which point the measure
\rho_{t}
forms a cusp (i.e., vanishes like a cube root) near
\theta=\pm\pi
. This is expected to be optimal as at later times, the natural inter-particle distance at the spectral edges exceeds the distance between
+\Theta_{t}
and
-\Theta_{t}
. Our rigidity estimates are formulated in Corollaries
1.5
and
1.7
below. The scaling behaviors in the various parameter regimes will be given as the results are introduced.

To our knowledge, our results are the strongest available estimates on eigenvalue locations for unitary Brownian motion. Our methods are completely different from prior works on rates of convergence to
\rho_{t}
, relying on the method of characteristics from PDEs. Previous works were based on moment calculations and/or concentration estimates for heat kernels on Lie groups.

1.1
Discussion of methodology

There are many approaches in the literature for proving local laws and rigidity in random matrix theory. For Wigner matrices and related mean-field random matrices
W
, a multi-scale approach to analyzing the resolvent
(W-z)^{-1}
giving estimates down to the optimal scale was developed by Erdős, Schlein and Yau
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. For pedagogical overviews of this strategy see
[
8
,
25
]
. Earlier local laws for random matrices on short scales appeared in
[
27
,
7
]
.

While powerful, the resolvent method makes heavy use of the matrix structure and independence between different entries; while
U_{t}
is associated with a matrix process, the correlation structure between entries is complicated and renders this approach intractable. Instead, the process
U_{t}
somewhat resembles what is known as (Hermitian) Dyson Brownian motion, whose definition we now recall. In the work
[
18
]
, Dyson showed that the eigenvalues of
V+W_{t}
for any Hermitian
V
(and
W_{t}
as in (
1.2
)) obey the system of SDEs,

(1.5)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\beta}}\mathrm{d}B_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{i}\mathrm{d}t,

where
\beta=2
. Moreover, at the level of formal calculation, Dyson showed that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(t)
of the matrix
U_{t}
satisfy the closed system of SDEs,

(1.6)

\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{i}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{d}B_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{d}t,

where the
\{B_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are a family of independent standard Brownian motions. We will not make use of this SDE, but mention that well-definedness of this process was studied in
[
13
]
.

Given the similar form of these two processes, it is therefore useful to recall how rigidity has been established in the study of DBM (
1.5
) for general
\beta
. Given Dyson’s original derivation of (
1.5
) we see that for the special values
\beta=1,2,4
, this eigenvalue process in fact comes from a matrix-valued Brownian motion that can be thought of as an additively deformed Gaussian Orthogonal/Unitary/Symplectic ensemble. In the case that the initial data is the
0
matrix, this is just a scaled Gaussian matrix, and so the local laws and rigidity follow from those for Wigner matrices. For general initial data, local laws and rigidity were developed by Lee and Schnelli
[
36
,
35
]
as well as the second author with Yau
[
32
,
33
]
still using resolvent methods, as the additive structure inherent in DBM allows this approach to work. However, for general
\beta
, the process (
1.5
) is no longer naturally associated with any matrix process and so no resolvent methods can work.

Nonetheless, the second author with Huang
[
29
]
showed that local laws and rigidity in fact hold for DBM with general
\beta
as well as a general potential (the equation (
1.5
) being associated with quadratic
V^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\mu
). This was further developed by the first author with Huang to study the spectral edges
[
1
]
. The method in these works is our starting point and so we review it here.

The works
[
1
,
29
]
were based on a PDE-style approach to studying the Stieltjes transform,

(1.7)

m(z,t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}(t)-z},

of the empirical eigenvalue measure associated to the Hermitian DBM (
1.5
), on short scales
\mathrm{Im}[z]\sim N^{-1}
.
For
\beta=2
, this quantity satisfies,

(1.8)

\mathrm{d}m(z,t)=\left(m(z,t)+\frac{z}{2}\right)\partial_{z}m(z,t)\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}m(z,t)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}N_{t},

for some Martingale
N_{t}
which turns out to be lower-order. This leads to the limiting complex Burger’s equation,

(1.9)

\partial_{t}\tilde{m}(z,t)=\left(\tilde{m}(z,t)+\frac{z}{2}\right)\partial_{z}\tilde{m}(z,t)+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{m}(z,t).

These equations were considered by Rogers and Shi
[
44
]
and general potential analogues by Li, Li and Xie
[
39
,
38
]
. The complex Burger’s equation may be solved by the elementary method of characteristics. The works
[
1
,
29
]
were based on tracking the difference between
\tilde{m}(z_{t},t)
and
m(z_{t},t)
along characteristics
z_{t}
. Note that for any fixed
z
, it is relatively straightforward to see that the limiting
m(z,t)
must satisfy (
1.9
). The main challenge in
[
1
,
29
]
is to extend this to short scales and obtain optimal error estimates.

The natural analogue of the Stieltjes transform
m(z,t)
above for measures supported on the unit circle is the following transform,

(1.10)

f(z,t):=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{U_{t}+z}{U_{t}-z}\right)=\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+z}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-z}\mathrm{d}\nu_{N,t}(\theta).

We will refer to this as the “Cauchy transform” although the Cauchy transform is usually defined slightly differently
[
14
]
(the usual definition differs from ours only by an affine transformation). Our choice of
f
is to match the work of Biane
[
9
,
10
]
discussed in more detail below.

An application of Itô’s formula starting from (
1.1
) shows that
f(z,t)
obeys the SDE,

(1.11)

\mathrm{d}f(z,t)=-\frac{zf(z,t)}{2}\partial_{z}f(z,t)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}M_{t}(z)

where
M_{t}(z)
is a complex-valued Martingale defined via,

(1.12)

\mathrm{d}M_{t}(z)=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}z}{N}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{U_{t}}{(U_{t}-z)^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)=-\frac{2\mathrm{i}z}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left[\frac{U_{t}}{(U_{t}-z)^{2}}\right]_{ij}\mathrm{d}(W_{t})_{ij},

where the second equality explicitly writes out the trace in terms of the matrix elements of
U_{t}(U_{t}-z)^{-2}
and the matrix of stochastic differentials of the Hermitian Brownian motion
W_{t}
.
The covariation process of
M_{t}
is easily calculated,

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{d}M,\mathrm{d}M\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{4z^{2}}{N^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{U_{t}^{2}}{(U_{t}-z)^{4}}\right)\mathrm{d}t

(1.13)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{d}M,\mathrm{d}\bar{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4|z|^{2}}{N^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{|U_{t}|^{2}}{|U_{t}-z|^{4}}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

Based on this, one sees that the limiting Cauchy transform should be the solution to,

(1.14)

\partial_{t}\tilde{f}(z,t)=-\frac{z\tilde{f}(z,t)}{2}\partial_{z}\tilde{f}(z,t),\qquad\tilde{f}(z,0)=\frac{1+z}{1-z},

the analogue of the complex Burger’s equation (
1.9
).
Indeed, these equations were found by Biane
[
10
,
9
]
, and the Cauchy transform
\tilde{f}(z,t)
characterizes the limiting spectral measure
\nu_{t}
. We collect properties of this measure and its density
\rho_{t}(\theta)
in Appendix
A
.

In the present work, we will analyze the equation (
1.11
) through the characteristics associated to (
1.14
). That is, if
z(t)
is a time-dependent curve in
\mathbb{C}
satisfying,

(1.15)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z(t)=\frac{z(t)\tilde{f}(z(t),t)}{2},

then,

(1.16)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tilde{f}(z(t),t)=0.

In fact, Biane’s work
[
10
]
shows that for any
t
the map
z\to z(t)
is a conformal map of some domain onto the complement of the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
, which allows one to construct
\tilde{f}(z,t)
from the characteristics.

At a superficial level, we are translating the methods of
[
29
,
1
]
from the real line to the unit circle. However, the translation is not at all straightforward and there are serious obstacles to be overcome once one moves to our new setting. We briefly mention a few now; they will be presented in more detail below as we discuss our results. First, it is not a-priori clear that this method could even work in the first place. In particular, it is crucial that the Martingale term
M_{t}(z)
above can be controlled by the empirical Cauchy transform
f(z,t)
itself. The precise form of the quadratic variation of
M_{t}
is therefore important. Similar considerations hold for controlling the term
\partial_{z}f(z,t)
by
f(z,t)
. In fact, getting precise constants here is crucial due to the use of Gronwall’s inequality in our proof.

One of the new novelties of our work is to deal with times close to
t\approx 4
where the spectral measure
\rho_{t}(\theta)
forms a cusp singularity near
\theta=\pm\pi
. Local laws near a cusp are in general delicate (see e.g.,
[
5
,
19
,
15
]
) and in the random matrix setting have not been dealt with via the characteristics approach before our work (but see the work
[
28
]
which studies non-intersecting random walks via characteristics in a different context). Secondly, the works
[
1
,
29
]
dealt only with short times
t=o(1)
whereas we are interested in times of order
1
. Coupled with the curvature of the unit circle, this requires a more detailed understanding of the behavior of the characteristics, especially near the spectral edges and cusps than was required before. Here, we partially rely on the semi-explicit form of the spectral measure and Cauchy transform
\tilde{f}(z,t)
. Finally, the results of
[
1
]
make somewhat strong assumptions on the initial data. This was primarily due to the treatment of general potentials in that work, which allowed for an analysis of the movement of the spectral edge. Here, our initial data is a delta function, falling outside the assumptions of
[
1
]
. In particular, our short-time analysis is more involved.

The characteristic approach has appeared in a few other works on the short-scale behavior of eigenvalues. Bourgade used characteristics to analyze a “stochastic advection equation” derived from a certain coupling between DBMs and obtained fine estimates on the local eigenvalue behavior of general Wigner matrices, including universality of the extreme gaps
[
11
]
. Von Soosten and Warzel used random characteristics to prove local laws for Wigner matrices
[
48
]
and to study delocalization in the Rosenzweig–Porter model
[
47
]
.

1.2
Statement of main results

Cauchy transforms of measures
\mu
on the unit circle
f_{\mu}(z)
are traditionally studied for
z
in the open unit disc,
\{|z|<1\}
. Due to the identity,

(1.17)

f_{\mu}(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})=-\widebar{f_{\mu}}(r^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}),

this is equivalent to studying the behavior for
|z|>1
, and it is somewhat conceptually simpler for our techniques to treat
|z|>1
.
Based on this, our first main result is the following, identifying the rate of convergence of the empirical spectral measure of unitary Brownian motion down to the optimal scale (up to polynomial factors). In order to state our results we introduce the notion of
overwhelming probability.

Definition 1.1
.

If
\mathcal{A}_{i}
are events indexed by some set
i\in\mathcal{I}
(and may depend on
N
) then we say that the family of events
\mathcal{A}_{i}
hold with overwhelming probability if for all
D>0
there is a
C>0
so that,

(1.18)

\sup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}]\leq N^{-D},

for all
N\geq C
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
T>0
and
\varepsilon,\delta,\mathfrak{c}>0
. For any
0<t<T
, we define the domain,

(1.19)

\mathcal{B}_{t}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:5>\log|z|>\frac{N^{\delta}}{N|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]|}\vee N^{-\mathfrak{c}}\right\}.

Then, with overwhelming probability we have uniformly for all
0<t<T
and
z\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
that,

(1.20)

|f(z,t)-\tilde{f}(z,t)|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\log|z|}.

Let us now explain the connection between the Cauchy transform and the spectral measure.
For a general probability measure
\mu
on the unit circle, one may recover
\mu
via the weak limits,

(1.21)

(2\pi)\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\left(\mathrm{Re}\left[\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\prime}}+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\prime}}-r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}\theta=-\lim_{r\downarrow 1}\left(\mathrm{Re}\left[\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\prime}}+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta^{\prime}}-r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}\theta.

However, this is not useful in order to obtain effective estimates on, e.g., the number of eigenvalues in an interval. The Helffer-Sjöstrand formula
[
17
]
for measures on
\mathbb{R}
allows one to relate empirical averages of test functions to integrals of the Stieltjes transform over
\mathbb{C}
, turning estimates on the Stieltjes transform into effective estimates on the eigenvalues
[
8
]
. In Section
6.1
we quickly develop a version of the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula for measures on the unit circle (like the usual HS formula, it is a consequence of Green’s theorem). Similar formulas have appeared before in the literature
[
40
]
, but the form given here is well-adapted to our purposes. Using this and the above theorem as input, we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
T>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. With overwhelming probability, the following holds uniformly over all intervals
I\subset(-\pi,\pi]
, and all
0<t<T
,

(1.22)

\left|\left|\{\lambda_{i}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}(t)}:\theta_{i}\in I\}\right|-N\int_{I}\rho_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\right|\leq N^{\varepsilon}+t^{-1/2}N^{-1/\varepsilon},

for
N
large enough.

The above corollary shows that as long as
t\geq N^{-C}
for some
C>0
then the number of eigenvalues in any interval is given by the limiting spectral measure up to an arbitrarily small polynomial error, with very high probability. This is the optimal scaling up to perhaps replacing the
N^{\varepsilon}
error by some sort of logarithmic error.

Theorem
1.2
is proven in Section
2
, and Corollary
1.3
is derived in Section
6.2
. This is the most straightforward of our results as it is the most literal translation of the methods of
[
29
,
1
]
to the unitary setting. Nonetheless, estimates as sharp as those of Corollary
1.3
were not known before this work.

Our method relies on an application of Gronwall’s inequality to the the function
t\to f(z(t),t)-\tilde{f}(z(t),t)
where
z(t)
is a characteristic as above. The most delicate estimates are associated with estimating the martingale term
M_{t}(z(t))
defined above, as well as the argument around (
2
) and (
2.24
). The integral form of Gronwall’s inequality we apply involves an exponential term which this latter argument estimates. Here, we cannot lose any constants or else the error term would be far too large to close our argument.

Recall that for
t<4
the support of
\rho_{t}
is not yet the entire unit circle and is instead the interval
I_{t}:=[-\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t}]
where
\Theta_{t}
is as in (
1.4
). The estimates of Theorem
1.2
are insufficient to address the natural question of whether or not there are eigenvalues outside of
I_{t}
, or their typical distance from the edges of
I_{t}
.

As mentioned above, Collins, Dahlqvist and Kemp
[
16
]
showed that with high probability there are no eigenvalues outside any open set containing
I_{t}
. However, such a statement does not yield the correct order of fluctuations of the extremal eigenvalues of
U_{t}
.

Before stating our results regarding the extremal eigenvalues, let us first ascertain what we expect for the order of magnitude of the fluctuations. For
\delta<t<4-\delta
, we show in Appendix
A
that for
E>0
sufficiently small,

(1.23)

\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-E)=c_{t}E^{1/2}(1+\mathcal{O}(E)).

That is, the spectral measure vanishes like a square-root at the edges of its spectrum.
The square-root behavior near spectral edges is generic in random matrix theory and is associated with limiting Tracy-Widom fluctuations on the order of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/3})
. While not explicitly formulated, we expect that the estimates of
[
16
]
in fact show that the extremal eigenvalues of
U_{t}
are no more than
\mathcal{O}(N^{-c})
from the edges of
I_{t}
for some small, explicit
c>0
. However, the interparticle distance associated with the square-root behavior is
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/3})
and so such an estimate nonetheless falls short.

Our next result shows that with overwhelming probability, the extremal eigenvalues are in fact within
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/3+\varepsilon})
of the edges of the support
I_{t}
. These are analogues of the well-known rigidity results in random matrix theory at the edge (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
for the first such estimates for generalized Wigner matrices). This is also the analogue of the results of
[
1
]
on the Hermitian DBM in the unitary setting.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. With overwhelming probability, the following holds uniformly for all
t
satisfying
\delta<t<4-\delta
,

(1.24)

\left|\left\{i:\lambda_{i}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta},\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]\backslash[-\Theta_{t}-N^{-2/3+\varepsilon},\Theta_{t}+N^{-2/3+\varepsilon}]\right\}\right|=0,

and for all
0\leq t\leq\delta
,

(1.25)

\left|\left\{i:\lambda_{i}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta},\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]\backslash[-\Theta_{t}-N^{-\varepsilon/6},\Theta_{t}+N^{-\varepsilon/6}]\right\}\right|=0.

For short times
t\ll 1
, the measure
\rho_{t}
looks like an approximate semicircle centered at
\theta=0
of width
\sqrt{t}
and
\rho_{t}(0)\asymp t^{-1/2}
. One therefore expects a different scaling of the interparticle distance for short times
t
. The error we obtain is not optimal for short times
t
, but this regime is not the main focus of our work and so we do not try to optimize our approach here.

Together with Corollary
1.3
, we can then deduce the following rigidity estimates. To introduce them, we require some further notation. Note that for all times
t>0
the joint law of the eigenvalues of
U_{t}
has a density with respect to Haar measure, and so for each fixed time
t_{0}>0
, the eigenvalues are almost surely distinct. On the other hand, it follows from
[
13
]
that for distinct initial data, the solution to (
1.6
) exists as a strong solution for all times
t>t_{0}
and moreover the eigenvalues do not intersect. Taking
t_{0}\to 0
it follows that with probability
1
, the eigenvalues are distinct for all times
t>0
. Since the eigenvalue locations are continuous functions of time and all start at the location
z=1
at
t=0
it follows that there is a labelling
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
so that we can write

Compared to the work
[
1
]
, we encounter several new difficulties in our unitary setting in establishing these edge rigidity results. These mostly have to do with the fact that establishing the above results heavily depends on detailed analysis of the behavior of characteristics near the spectral edge. This behavior depends especially on the distance along the unit circle of characteristic from the location
\pm\Theta_{t}
(the angular coordinate) as well as the distance from the unit circle. However, these coordinates introduce curvature whereas in the real line setting these are flat Cartesian coordinates of the real and imaginary part of the characteristic. This is further complicated by the fact that for short times
t
, the spectral measure is very peaked and that for long times
t
, the characteristics will leave the small neighbourhoods of the spectral edges for which we can develop expansions of
\tilde{f}(z,t)
.

We overcome the short-time difficulties mainly by sacrificing obtaining optimal estimates for short times; i.e., for short times we consider only characteristics that are somewhat far from the spectral edge. The second fact we use to overcome the difficulties associated with curvature and long times is monotonicity of the radial coordinate of the characteristics in time. This second fact is lacking for the general potential processes considered in
[
29
,
1
]
, and is one of the sources of the short-time restrictions in those works (“shocks” can develop for these processes). Essentially, monotonicity of the characteristics allows us to split the paths into “short” and “long” time regimes. In the short-time regimes, we can use a combination of analytic approaches to square-root measures and the semi-explicit formulas for
\tilde{f}(z,t)
to control the behavior of characteristics close to spectral edges. In the long time regime, things are far away from the spectrum and so relevant quantities can usually be bounded by constants.

We now turn our attention to times
t\sim 4
. This is a critical time for unitary Brownian motion, as at
t=4
, the two spectral edges
\pm\Theta_{t}
merge, and the support of the density of states becomes the entire unit circle for later times
t
. In fact, as we show in Appendix
A
, the spectral measure at
t=4
has a cusp singularity,

(1.29)

\rho_{4}(\pi+E)=c|E|^{1/3}(1+\mathcal{O}(|E|^{1/3})),

for some constant
c>0
. Moreover, for times
t
near
4
, the spectral measure undergoes a transition where the two edges gradually form a “near-cusp”, become a cusp, and then form a small local minimum as
t
ranges from slightly less than
4
to slightly larger than
4
.

This behavior is identical to that found in the theory of the so-called quadratic vector equation. The quadratic vector equation is a generic equation characterizing the density of states of certain classes of mean field self-adjoint random matrix models. In a series of works
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, Ajanki, Erdős and Krüger carried out a systematic study of the solutions to the quadratic vector equation. In particular, they found that the only possible singularities that may occur are cusps, where the density of states of vanishes like a cube root, and square roots, occurring at either external or internal edges. Moreover, they characterized transitional regimes where intervals of the density of states merge or split. In such regimes, they showed that the leading order of the density of states is always given by universal shape functions arising from Cardano’s formula for the roots of third-degree polynomials. There are two such functions; the first,
\Psi_{e}
corresponds to the case when two separate intervals merge and describe a transition from square-root to cubic behavior. The second,
\Psi_{m}
describes what occurs after the cusp forms, when the density of states has a small minimum.

In fact, in Appendix
A
we show that for times
t<4
that the density of states
\rho_{t}
of unitary Brownian motion is described by
\Psi_{e}
and for times
t>4
by
\Psi_{m}
, the universal shape functions of
[
2
]
. On the one hand, this is remarkable as there is no quadratic equation describing the density of states of unitary Brownian motion and moreover that the eigenvalues are on the unit circle instead of the real line. On the other hand, the universal shape functions arise from expansions of the Cauchy or Stieltjes transform near critical points (i.e., the spectral edges or minima) as soon as one is guaranteed that either the coefficients of the quadratic or cubic terms is non-degenerate and so this behavior is somewhat expected.

and

1.3
Further discussion and motivation

In addition to being an intrinsic question about the nature of the process
U_{t}
, these local law and rigidity estimates have been well studied in the context of Hermitian random matrix theory. There, a primary motivation is the study of the universality of the local eigenvalue statistics: that is, whether or not the limiting local eigenvalue statistics coincide with those of the Gaussian ensembles, which admit exact formulas.

The short scale behaviors of the repulsive interaction terms of the eigenvalue process of the Hermitian and Unitary Brownian motions (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are similar. Based on this and the general belief in the universality of large correlated systems, it is natural to conjecture that the local eigenvalue statistics of (
1.6
) should be given by the same statistics as the GUE in the limit
N\to\infty
.

Indeed, there have been many developments in the universality theory of the local eigenvalue statistics both within the larger context of random matrix theory, as well as that of the Hermitian Dyson Brownian motion started from general initial data. Note that if the initial data of (
1.5
) is not the
0
matrix, then the joint eigenvalue distribution of
X_{t}
is no longer that of a re-scaled GUE. Nonetheless, local scaling limits of DBM with general initial data has been obtained in
[
32
,
31
,
24
,
33
]
.

Universality has been established for wide classes of Hermitian random matrices. We refer the interested reader to, e.g., the book
[
25
]
for an overview of these developments as well as to the seminal papers of Tao and Vu
[
46
,
45
]
, and Erdős, Schlein and Yau
[
23
]
.

Given these advances in the theory of Hermitian random matrices, it is natural to turn to the question of universality of unitary Brownian motion. An important tool in many of the proofs of Hermitian universality are the aforementioned rigidity and local law estimates.

The main contribution of the present work is to establish these results. It is then a subject of current investigation to use these estimates to prove the local universality of unitary Brownian motion: that the local eigenvalues statistics of
U_{t}
are given by the Tracy-Widom, and Pearcey and Sine kernels in the various scaling regimes of interest.

\mathbb{P}\left[\exists k:\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{k}}|\mathcal{E}_{2}(t)|>\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{2N\log|z_{t_{k}}|}\right]\leq C\log(N)\mathrm{e}^{-cN^{\varepsilon_{1}}}.

Let
0<t<\tau
and let
k
be such that
\tau_{k}\leq t<\tau_{k+1}
. On the complement of the event on the LHS of (
2.19
) we have,

(2.20)

|\mathcal{E}_{2}(t)|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{2N\log|z_{t_{k}}|}\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{t}|}

by the choice of the
t_{k}
’s. This completes the proof. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
. Let
z\in\mathcal{B}_{T}
with characteristic
z_{t}
as above. Let
\tau
be the stopping time as defined above. Let
\Upsilon
be the event of Proposition
2.2
and define,

(2.21)

g(s):=\frac{|z_{s}\partial_{z}f(z_{s},s)|}{2}.

By Gronwall’s inequality we have for all
0<t<\tau
on the event
\Upsilon
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle|f(z_{t},t)-\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\exp\left[\int_{s}^{t}g(u)\mathrm{d}u\right]\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{s}|}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{t}|}.

We now estimate the integral of the function
g
that appears above in the argument of the exponential function. Using first (
2.4
) we have,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}g(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\frac{|z_{u}||\partial_{z}f(z_{u},u)|}{2}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\frac{|z_{u}||\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{u},u)]|}{|z_{u}|^{2}-1}\mathrm{d}u

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\frac{|\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{u},u)]|}{2\log|z_{u}|}\mathrm{d}u.

In the last inequality we used the elementary inequality,

(2.24)

\frac{x}{x^{2}-1}\leq\frac{1}{2\log(x)}

which holds for
x>1
(see Lemma
B.1
). Using now (
2.17
) we have,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\frac{|\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{u},u)]|}{2\log|z_{u}|}\mathrm{d}u\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{\log(N)}\right)\int_{s}^{t}\frac{|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z_{u},u)]|}{2\log|z_{u}|}\mathrm{d}u

(2.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{\log(N)}\right)\log\left(\frac{\log|z_{s}|}{\log|z_{t}|}\right)

where we used in the last line that
\partial_{u}\log|z_{u}|=-\frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z_{u},u)]|
. Therefore,

(2.26)

\exp\left[\int_{s}^{t}g(u)\mathrm{d}u\right]\leq C\frac{\log|z_{s}|}{\log|z_{t}|}.

We used the fact that the assumption
C\geq\log|z_{u}|\geq N^{-\mathfrak{c}}
from the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{T}
implies that

(2.27)

\left(\frac{\log|z_{s}|}{\log|z_{t}|}\right)^{1/\log(N)}\leq C.

Substituting (
2.26
) into (
2.22
) yields,

(2.28)

\displaystyle|f(z_{t},t)-\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)|\leq\frac{CN^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{t}|}\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\mathrm{d}s+\frac{N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{t}|}.

From the definition of
g
and (
2.4
) and (
2.17
) we see that,

(2.29)

g(s)\leq C\frac{|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z_{s},s)]|}{\log|z_{s}|}

for
s<\tau
. Therefore,

(2.30)

\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\mathrm{d}s\leq C\log(N)

using that
\log|z_{t}|\geq N^{-\mathfrak{c}}
. We conclude that for all
0<t<\tau
that,

(2.31)

|f(z_{t},t)-\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)|\leq C\frac{\log(N)N^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{N\log|z_{t}|}.

Taking
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon/2
, where
\varepsilon>0
is in the definition of the stopping time, we see that on the event
\Upsilon
, we must have
\tau=T
for all
N
large enough. This proves the estimate of Theorem
1.2
at the final time
T
and at the point
z
. The extension to all points
z\in\mathcal{B}_{T}
may be done by first taking a union bound over
\mathcal{O}(N^{C})
points and then using

(2.32)

|f_{\mu}(z)-f_{\mu}(w)|\leq C|z-w|\left(\frac{1}{\log|z|}+\frac{1}{\log|w|}\right)^{2}

valid for any Cauchy transform and
|z|,|w|
in bounded regions of
\mathbb{C}
. The above estimate is elementary and can be proven directly from the definition

f_{\mu}(z):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+z}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-z}\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)

and the fact that for
|z|>1
we have for the quantity in the denominator
|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-z|\geq|z|-1\geq c\log|z|
.

The extension to all times
t<T
is done in a similar manner; one first proves the estimate for all
z\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
for all times
t
in a well-spaced grid of
[0,T]
of at most size
\mathcal{O}(N^{A})
, some
A>0
to be determined. Then one notes that the proof also gives that the estimate holds along the entire characteristic, and along each characteristic, we have, e.g.,
|z_{t}-z_{s}|\leq CN^{C}|t-s|
, for some
C>0
depending on
\mathfrak{c}>0
. We need only take
A
large enough depending on
C>0
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
. ∎

3

Fix
T<4
. We will need to consider characteristics ending at many times and so we introduce the characteristic map,

(3.1)

\mathcal{C}_{s,t}(z)=z\exp\left[-\frac{(t-s)\tilde{f}(z,t)}{2}\right]

for
0\leq s\leq t
. We will need to establish several properties about the behavior of characteristics near the edge
\Theta_{t}
. The proof of the following lemma, an exercise in calculus, is postponed to Appendix

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\rho
be a measure on
[-\pi,\pi]
such that
\rho(\theta)=\rho(-\theta)
that is supported in
[-E,E]
for
0<E<\pi
. Assume either that
\rho(\theta)\leq M
or
E<\pi/8
. For any
\varepsilon>0
there is a
c_{\varepsilon}>0
, depending only on
E,M
and
\varepsilon>0
so that for
0<r-1<c_{\varepsilon}
and
E<\theta<\pi-\varepsilon
we have,

(3.2)

\mathrm{Im}[f(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})]<\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})]<\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}E})]

where
f(z)
is the Cauchy transform of
\rho
. We also have for
E<\theta\leq\varphi\leq\pi
,

(3.3)

0\leq\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi})]\leq\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})],

with equality occuring only in trivial cases.

We now prove the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T<4
and
z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\kappa)}
for
\kappa>0
. There is a small
c>0
and
d>0
so that,

(3.4)

\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}},t)]\leq-d\sqrt{\kappa}

for
0<t<T
and all
0<r-1<c
and
\kappa<\pi-\Theta_{t}
.

Proof.
By Lemma
B.3
, the desired estimate hold for all
\kappa<\varepsilon
and
0<r-1<\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. In particular,

(3.5)

\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\varepsilon/2)},t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}},t)]\leq-c_{1}

for some
c_{1}>0
. Since for
t<T
the measures are supported away from
\pi
we see that there is a
\delta>0
so that

(3.6)

|\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(-1,t)]|\leq\frac{c_{1}}{2},

for
|z+1|<\delta
. It follows that for all
|z+1|<\delta
,

(3.7)

\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}})]\leq\frac{c_{1}}{2}+\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\varepsilon/2}),t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}})]\leq-\frac{c_{1}}{2}

where we used the second estimate of Lemma
3.1
. This proves the desired estimate for
|z+1|<\delta
after possibly decreasing the value of
d>0
.

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.1
we conclude that there is a
c_{2}>0
so that for
0<r-1<c_{2}
and
\varepsilon/2<\kappa<\pi-\Theta_{t}-\delta/2
that,

(3.8)

\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\kappa)},t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}})]\leq\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\varepsilon/2)},t)]-\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}})]\leq-c_{1}.

This concludes the proof. ∎

The following contains the properties of the characteristics that we will need.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
T<4
. There is a
\mathfrak{a}>0
and
\mathfrak{b}>0
depending on
T
so that the following holds. Let
z_{s}=\mathcal{C}_{s,t}(z)
for any
t<T
, where
z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
satisfies
0<r-1<\mathfrak{a}
and
\Theta_{t}<\theta<\pi
. Denote
z_{s}=r_{s}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{s}+\kappa_{s})}
. Let
s_{*}
be,

(3.9)

s_{*}=\inf\{s<t:(r_{s}-1)<\mathfrak{a}\}.

Then for
s_{*}<s<t
we have,

(3.10)

\sqrt{\kappa_{s}}\geq\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}+\mathfrak{b}(t-s)

and for
s\leq s_{*}
we have
r_{s}\geq 1+\mathfrak{a}
. Furthermore, let
D(s)
be a function that obeys,

(3.11)

\sqrt{D(s)}\leq\sqrt{D(t)}+\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}(t-s),\qquad s<t.

If
\kappa_{t}\geq D(t)
then for
s_{*}<s<t
we have,

(3.12)

\kappa_{s}\geq D(s)+\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}(t-s).

Finally, characteristics do not cross the real axis in the complex plane.

Proof.
We take
\mathfrak{a}
so small so that the conclusion of Lemma
3.2
for
z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
r-1<10\mathfrak{a}
. For
s<s_{*}
we have
r_{s}>\mathfrak{a}
because the radial coordinate is decreasing in time. Let
s_{1}
be,

(3.13)

s_{1}=\inf\{s<t:\kappa_{s}>0\}.

Note that
s_{1}<t
as we assume
\kappa_{t}>0
. We claim that
s_{1}\leq s_{*}
. For
t>s>s_{1}\vee s_{*}
we have, the following calculation,

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\kappa_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z_{s},s)]-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}},s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)]-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}},t)]-\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{\frac{4-s}{s}}-\sqrt{\frac{4-t}{t}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)]-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}},t)]]-c(t-s)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\leq-d\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}-c(t-s)

The second line is from the fact that
\tilde{f}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{t}},t)=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{4t^{-1}-1}
for all
t
and that
\tilde{f}
is constant along characteristics. The third line is straightforward. The last line follows from Lemma
3.2
. In particular, we see that
\kappa_{s}
is a decreasing function for
s>s_{*}\vee s_{1}
. Since
\kappa_{t}>0
it follows that
s_{1}\leq s_{*}
. Therefore, the final inequality in (
3
) holds for all
s_{*}<s<t
. Therefore via integration we obtain,

(3.15)

\kappa_{s}\geq\kappa_{t}+2c_{2}(t-s)\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}+c_{2}(t-s)^{2}

for some small
c_{2}>0
for all
s_{*}<s<t
. This is equivalent to the desired inequality. For the last inequality of the proposition, we have that

(3.16)

\sqrt{\kappa_{s}}\geq\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}+\mathfrak{b}(t-s)\geq\sqrt{D(t)}+\mathfrak{b}(t-s)\geq\sqrt{D(s)}+\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}(t-s).

We can square this to get

\kappa_{s}\geq D(s)+\mathfrak{b}(t-s)\sqrt{D(s)}
as well as
\kappa_{s}\geq\kappa_{t}+2\mathfrak{b}(t-s)\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}
. If
D(s)\geq\kappa_{t}
, we can use the first inequality. Otherwise, we can use the second.

The final claim that characteristics not cross the real axis is due to the fact that the imaginary part of
\tilde{f}(z,t)
vanishes for purely real
z
, due to the symmetry of
\rho_{t}
. ∎

c\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}\leq|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]|\leq C\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}

we also have,

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\delta>0
and assume
\delta<t<4-\delta
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
0\leq k\leq\frac{2}{3}
. Suppose that the estimate,

(3.20)

\left|f(z,t)-\tilde{f}(z,t)\right|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\sqrt{\eta+\kappa}\sqrt{\eta}}

holds for all
z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
for any
r
and
\theta
satisfying,

(3.21)

\Theta_{t}+N^{-2/3+k+5\varepsilon}\leq|\theta|\leq\pi,\qquad N^{-2/3+k/4+\varepsilon}\leq r-1\leq c

where
c>0
is any positive constant. Then there are no eigenvalues
\lambda=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
for
\Theta_{t}+N^{-2/3+k+5\varepsilon}\leq|\theta|\leq\pi
. The same conclusion holds for
t<\delta
if we take
k=2/3-5\varepsilon-\varepsilon/6
.

Proof.
Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue in
f(z,t)
. We will show that if
z
is of the form
r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
r>1
and
|\theta-\arg\lambda|<r-1
, then
\mathrm{Re}[f(z,t)]\geq\frac{1}{N(r-1)}
. By rotational invariance along the unit circle, it suffices to consider
\lambda=1
.

We see by direct calculation that

(3.22)

\mathrm{Re}\left[\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right]=\frac{r^{2}-1}{(r-1)^{2}+2r(1-\cos\theta)}\geq\frac{r^{2}-1}{(r-1)^{2}+r\theta^{2}}\geq\frac{r^{2}-1}{(r-1)^{2}(1+r)}\geq\frac{1}{r-1}.

To get the second inequality, we used
1-\cos\theta\leq\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}
. The third inequality comes from
\theta<r-1
.

Thus, we see that if
z
of the form
r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
r>1
and
|\theta-\arg\lambda|<r-1
, we know that
\mathrm{Re}[f(z,t)]\geq\frac{1}{N(r-1)}
provided that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue appearing in the empirical measure for
f(z,t)
.

Now, consider the point
z=(1+N^{-2/3+k/4+\varepsilon})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, where
\theta
is an angle in the region
|\theta|\in[\Theta_{t}+N^{-2/3+k+5\varepsilon},\pi]
.

Applying the triangle inequality and the estimates (
3.20
) and (

), we see that for large enough

,

(3.23)

|\mathrm{Re}[f(z,t)]|\leq|f(z,t)-\tilde{f}(z,t)|+|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]|<C\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}+\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}<\frac{1}{N\eta}.

\eta
and
\theta
to estimate,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}\leq N^{-1/3-k/4-3/2\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}\leq N^{-1/3-5k/8-2\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\eta}=N^{-1/3-k/4-\varepsilon}.

This shows

and therefore
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
cannot be an eigenvalue of the empirical measure associated to

.

We now consider the case
0<t<\delta
and
k=2/3-5\varepsilon-\varepsilon/6
. We can instead apply the trivial bound

\eta

\kappa

(3.25)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\kappa^{2}}\leq N^{-1/2+\varepsilon/24},

Therefore, for

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(s_{k}\wedge\tau)\mathcal{E}_{2}(s_{k}\wedge\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}-N^{-2/3+k(t)+5\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{s_{*}}^{s_{k}}\frac{|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z_{t},t)]|}{\eta_{t}\left((\kappa_{s_{k}}(t-s_{k})+\eta_{t}^{2}\right)}\mathrm{d}t

In this section we prove Theorem

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a constant
C>0
so that the following holds. For
0<t<\frac{1}{10}
and
z=(1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta_{t}+\kappa)}
with
\eta
and
\kappa
satisfying,

(4.4)

0<\eta<\Delta_{t},\qquad 0<\kappa<\pi-\Theta_{t}

we have that

(4.5)

\frac{1}{C}\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta+\kappa}}\leq\Delta_{t}^{1/6}|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]|\leq C\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta+\kappa}}

Proof.
Denoting
\theta=\kappa+\Theta_{t}
we have,

(4.6)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z,t)]\asymp\eta\int\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\eta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\frac{\theta-x}{2})}\mathrm{d}x.

Due to symmetry of the density,

(4.7)

\int\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\eta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\frac{\theta-x}{2})}\mathrm{d}x\asymp\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\eta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\frac{\theta-x}{2})}\mathrm{d}x.

Changing coordinates and using the fact that
\sin^{2}(x)\asymp x^{2}
for
|x|\leq\pi/2
we have,

(4.8)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\eta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\frac{\theta-x}{2})}\mathrm{d}x\asymp\int_{0}^{\Theta_{t}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

For an upper bound we split the integral into the regions
[0,\Delta_{t}]
and
[\Delta_{t},\Theta_{t}]
. For the latter region we can bound
\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)\leq Cx^{1/3}
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{t}}^{\Theta_{t}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Delta_{t}}^{\Theta_{t}}(\Delta_{t}+x)^{-5/3}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\Delta_{t}^{-2/3}\leq C\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}},

where in the last inequality we used the assumption
\kappa+\eta\leq 2\Delta_{t}
. In the region
[0,\Delta_{t}]
we use
\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)\Delta_{t}^{1/6}\leq Cx^{1/2}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Delta_{t}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\leq

\displaystyle C\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\int_{0}^{\Delta_{t}}\frac{x^{1/2}}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\int_{0}^{\Delta_{t}}(x+\kappa+\eta)^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}.

This completes the proof of the upper bound. For the lower bound,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Theta_{t}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{t}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\Theta_{t}-x)}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq c\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{t}}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq c\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\int_{\frac{\kappa+\eta}{2}}^{\kappa+\eta}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\eta^{2}+(\kappa+x)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

(4.11)

\displaystyle\geq c\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\int_{\frac{\kappa+\eta}{2}}^{\kappa+\eta}(\kappa+\eta)^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x\geq c\Delta_{t}^{-1/6}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}}.

We used above the assumption that
\kappa+\eta\leq 2\Delta_{t}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Recall the convention

. There are
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}>0
so that the following holds. Let
z_{s}=\mathcal{C}_{S,T}(z)

(4.13)

\Delta_{S}^{1/6}\sqrt{\kappa_{S}}>\Delta_{T}^{1/6}\sqrt{\kappa_{T}}+\mathfrak{b}(T-S),

is decreasing.
For

\eta_{S}\geq\eta_{S_{*}}\geq\mathfrak{a}\Delta_{S_{*}}\geq\mathfrak{a}\Delta_{T}

We let
100\mathfrak{a}
be the constant from Proposition

. For

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}S}\kappa_{S}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\kappa_{S}+\eta_{S}}\eta_{S}^{1/4}}\int_{S_{0}}^{S}|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(z_{U},U)]|\eta_{U}^{-5/4}\mathrm{d}U

(4.87)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{S}+\eta_{S}}\eta_{S}^{1/2}}.

In the first inequality we used constancy of
\tilde{f}
along characteristics and Lemma
4.1
. In the second inequality we used Lemma
4.1
again. In the third inequality we used (
4.86
).

Summarizing, we see that on the event
\Upsilon
we have for any
S_{0}<S<\tau\wedge T_{i}
that

(4.88)

|f(z_{S},S)-\tilde{f}(z_{S},S)|\leq C\log(N)N^{\varepsilon_{1}}B(z_{S}).

Taking
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon/100
we see that we must have
\tau>T_{i}
for every
i
. Therefore,
\tau=T
and we conclude the theorem. ∎

Center of mass evolution

In this section we prove Proposition
1.8
. We will not track dependence of constants on the parameter

, as Proposition
1.8
is a statement in soft analysis. The reader should note that we will therefore change notation and consider unitary matrices of size
n
instead of
N
evolving according to (

). We do this in order to emphasize the ineffectiveness of the estimates in the dimension of the system.

Fix some time
t_{0}>0
and assume that there are no eigenvalues in the set
\{z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:\theta\in I_{0}\}
for
I_{0}=[\theta_{0}-L/2,\theta_{0}+L/2]
at time
t_{0}
and
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
. Let
\tau>t_{0}
be the first time an eigenvalue enters this set. Let
\Gamma
be the contour,

\displaystyle\Gamma:=

\displaystyle\{z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:1/2<r<3/2,\theta=\theta_{0}\pm L/4\}

(5.1)

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\{z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:r=1/2,3/2,\theta\notin[\theta_{0}-L/4,\theta_{0}+L/4]\}.

That is, it encloses all of the eigenvalues on the unit circle but avoids the ray
\{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{0}}:r\geq 0\}
. A schematic diagram of the contour
\Gamma
is given in Figure
1
.

We use this contour as we will take a logarithm with branch cut being this ray.
Then for any time
t_{0}<t_{1}<\tau
we have,

(5.2)

\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}(t_{1})-\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g_{\theta_{0}}(z)\left(\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}(t_{1})}-\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}(t_{0})}\right)\mathrm{d}z

where
g_{\theta_{0}}(z)
is the branch of the logarithm holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{0}},r\geq 0\}
. This formula holds due to the fact that no eigenvalue crosses the angle
\theta_{0}
in this time interval, and so for this entire time interval,
\theta_{i}(t)=2\pi k+\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t)
where
k
is a constant integer and
\theta_{0}<\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t)<\theta_{0}+2\pi
.

Introducing,

(5.3)

m(z,t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(t)-z}=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\frac{1}{U_{t}-z}

we therefore have,

(5.4)

\bar{\theta}(t_{1}\wedge\tau)-\bar{\theta}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{-g_{\theta_{0}}(z)}{\mathrm{i}}(m(z,t_{1}\wedge\tau)-m(z,t_{0}))\mathrm{d}z.

For
t_{0}<t<\tau
,
m(z,t)
is well-defined for
z\in\Gamma
and so we may apply the Itô formula. Since,

(5.5)

f(z,t)=1+2zm(z,t)

we have from (
1.11
) that (abbreviating
g=g_{\theta_{0}}
and
m=m(z,t)
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\mathrm{i}g(z)m(z,t)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{-\mathrm{i}g}{2}(1+2zm)(m+zm^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}t

(5.6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g(z)\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{U_{t}}{(U_{t}-z)^{2}}\mathrm{d}W\right)\mathrm{d}z.

Figure 1:
Schematic diagram of contour
\Gamma
defined in (

). Contour in red; unit circle in black. Eigenvalues are the thick black dots enclosed by the red contour.

Observe that by the holomorphic functional calculus,

(5.7)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g(z)\frac{1}{(U_{t}-z)^{2}}\mathrm{d}z=U_{t}^{-1},

and so

(5.8)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g(z)\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{U_{t}}{(U_{t}-z)^{2}}\mathrm{d}W\right)\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{d}W\right).

The first line of (
5
) turns out to vanish identically. To see this, we will evaluate all of the integrals explicitly using the Cauchy integral formula as well as the variants

(5.9)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{F(z)}{(z-a)(z-b)}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{F(b)-F(a)}{b-a}

and

(5.10)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{F(z)}{(z-a)^{2}(z-b)}\mathrm{d}z=-\frac{F^{\prime}(a)}{b-a}+\frac{F(b)-F(a)}{(b-a)^{2}}

valid for
F
analytic in the appropriate domain and
\Gamma
encircling
a,b
. We have,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int g(z)(1+2zm)(m+zm^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int g(z)(m+2zm^{2}+zm^{\prime}+2z^{2}mm^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\mathrm{d}zg(z)\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-z}+2\frac{z}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{j}-z)}+z(2n^{-1}+1)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-z)^{2}}

\displaystyle+2\frac{z^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{j}-z)^{2}}+2\frac{z^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-z)^{3}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}g(\lambda_{i})+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}g(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{j}g(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\frac{2n^{-1}+1}{n}\sum_{i}(g(\lambda_{i})+1)

(5.11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{4\lambda_{j}g(\lambda_{j})+2\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}-\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}g(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{j}^{2}g(\lambda_{j})}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}(2g(\lambda_{i})+3).

Now, note that

(5.12)

\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}g(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{j}^{2}g(\lambda_{j})}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}=0

by symmetry as well as,

(5.13)

-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}g(\lambda_{i})+\frac{2n^{-1}+1}{n}\sum_{i}(g(\lambda_{i})+1)-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}(2g(\lambda_{i})+3)=1-\frac{1}{n}

Finally,

(5.14)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{i}g(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{j}g(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{4\lambda_{j}g(\lambda_{j})+2\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}=-\frac{n(n-1)}{n^{2}}

and so indeed the term on the first line of (
5
) vanishes. It follows that for any
t_{0}<t_{1}\leq\tau
we have

(5.15)

\bar{\theta}(t_{1})-\bar{\theta}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathrm{tr}W_{t_{1}}-\mathrm{tr}W_{t_{0}}\right).

Fix a final time
T>1
and a large integer
m>0
, and intermediate times
t_{i}=iT/m
. Let
\theta_{k}
,
k=1,2,\dots,4n
, be
4n
equally spaced points along the unit circle. For any
\ell>0
define the arcs,

(5.16)

Z_{k,\ell}=\{z=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:|\theta-\theta_{k}|<\ell/(100n)\}.

Throughout the remainder of the proof we will make use of the arcs
\{Z_{k,1}\}_{k}
and
\{Z_{k,1/2}\}_{k}
, i.e., the choices
\ell=1,1/2
.

For each
0\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq k\leq 4n
define the stopping time
\tau_{i,k}
as follows. If at time
t_{i}
there is an eigenvalue in
Z_{k,1}
let
\tau_{i,k}=t_{i}
. Otherwise,
\tau_{i,k}
be the first time
t_{i}<t\leq t_{i+1}
that an eigenvalue hits the set
Z_{k,1/2}
. If this does not occur, let
\tau_{i,k}=\infty
. Then, let
\tau_{i}=\min_{k}\tau_{i,k}
. By the pigeonhole principle we have
\tau_{i}>t_{i}
for all
i
. Finally, let
\tau^{(m)}=\min_{i}\tau_{i}
.

From the above discussion we have on the event
\tau^{(m)}=\infty
and by telescoping that for any
0<t<T
we have

(5.17)

\bar{\theta}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(W_{t}\right).

The proof of Proposition
1.8
will be complete once we prove that

(5.18)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{(m)}=\infty\right]=1.

The remainder of this section is devoted to this. Consider the points,

(5.19)

z_{\pm,k}=(1+\eta)\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\left(\theta_{k}\pm\frac{3}{4}\frac{1}{100n}\right)\right].

Note that for any
k
that if
Z_{k,1}
contains no eigenvalues at time
t_{i}
, then

(5.20)

|\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{\pm,k},t_{i})]|\leq C_{1}n^{2}\eta

for some
C_{1}>0
. On the other hand, if for this
k
we have that
\tau_{i,k}<\infty
then there is some
t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
such that,

(5.21)

|\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{+,k},t)]|+|\mathrm{Re}[f(z_{-,k},t)]|>\frac{c_{1}}{n\eta}.

Choosing
\eta
small enough so that

(5.22)

\frac{c_{1}}{n\eta}>10C_{1}n^{2}\eta

we have that

(5.23)

\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{4n}\left\{\exists t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]:|f(z_{+,k},t)-f(z_{+,k},t_{i})|+|f(z_{-,k},t)-f(z_{-,k},t_{i})|>\frac{c_{2}}{10n\eta}\right\}.

The parameter
\eta>0
is fixed for the remainder of the proof.
We see by (
1.11
) that (abbreviating
z=z_{\pm,k}
)

(5.24)

|f(z,t)-f(z,t_{i})|\leq CT\frac{1}{m\eta^{3}}+(M_{t}-M_{t_{i}}).

By the BDG inequality,

(5.25)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{t_{i}<t<t_{i+1}}|M_{t_{i}}-M_{t}|>s\right]\leq C\exp\left[-csm^{1/2}n^{-2}T^{-1}\right].

So taking

we see that for all

and

such that

(5.26)

\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{i}<\infty\right]\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-m^{1/2}c}.

This completes the proof of Proposition
1.8
. ∎

6.1
Helffer-Sjöstrand formula

This section establishes an analog of the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula for measures on the unit circle. Recall,

(6.1)

\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+\mathrm{i}\partial_{y})

as well as Green’s theorem,

(6.2)

F(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(\partial_{\bar{z}}F)(x,y)}{\lambda-(x+\mathrm{i}y)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

for any
F\in C^{2}
of compact support. In polar coordinates we recall,

(6.3)

\partial_{\bar{z}}F(r,\theta)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{2}\left(\partial_{r}F+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{r}\partial_{\theta}F\right).

For any
F
supported in an annulus,

(6.4)

F(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}F}{2z}\frac{\lambda+z}{\lambda-z}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Let
\varphi:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function on the unit circle that extends to a smooth
2\pi
-periodic function on
\mathbb{R}
. We define the quasi-analytic extension of
\varphi
by,

(6.5)

\tilde{\varphi}(r,\theta):=(\varphi(\theta)-\mathrm{i}\log(r)\varphi^{\prime}(\theta))\chi(r)

where
\chi(r)
is a function that is
1
on
[3/4,4/3]
and
0
outside of
[1/2,2]
. We may also assume that,

(6.6)

\chi(r)=\chi(r^{-1}).

If
\mu
is any measure on the unit circle with Cauchy transform
f_{\mu}(z)
we see that,

\displaystyle\int\varphi(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{\varphi}}{2z}f_{\mu}(z)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int(\varphi(\theta)-\mathrm{i}\log(r)\varphi^{\prime}(\theta))\chi^{\prime}(r)f_{\mu}(z)\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta

(6.7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\log(r)\chi(r)f_{\mu}(z)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta.

This is the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula we require to establish our eigenvalue estimates and will be used in the next subsections.

6.2
Proof of Corollary
1.3

We follow Section 3.3 of

]
very closely, as the estimates of Theorem

and the formula (

. Introduce,

(6.8)

\eta(\theta)=\inf_{\eta>1}\{(\eta-1)|\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{f}(\eta\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})]|\geq N^{\varepsilon-1}\}.

Note that from Lemma

(6.9)

(6.10)

\tilde{\eta}:=\inf_{\eta:\log\eta\geq N^{1-\mathfrak{c}}}\{\eta:\max_{\theta_{0}\leq x\leq\theta_{0}+\eta}\eta(x)\leq\eta\}.

Let
\tilde{\theta}
be

(6.11)

\tilde{\theta}:=\mbox{argmax}_{\theta_{0}-\tilde{\eta}\leq x\leq\theta_{0}}\eta(x)

so that

(6.12)

\eta(\tilde{\theta})=\tilde{\eta}.

We let
\varphi
be a function that is
1
on
[\theta_{0},\pi]
and

for

near
\theta_{0}
.

By (

) we have,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\varphi(\lambda_{i}(t))-\int\varphi(\theta)\rho_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int(|\varphi(\theta)|+|\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)|)|\chi^{\prime}(r)|S(z)|\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta

(6.13)

\displaystyle+\left|\int\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\log(r)\chi(r)S(z)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta\right|.

where

On the event that the estimates of Theorem

(6.15)

\int(|\varphi(\theta)|+|\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)|)|\chi^{\prime}(r)|S(z)|\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta\leq C\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N}.

For the second term, note that the measure
r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r

(6.16)

\left|\int\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\log(r)\chi(r)S(z)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta\right|=2\left|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{r>1}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\log(r)|\mathrm{Re}[S(z)]|\chi(r)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta\right|

Recall that
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=0

|\mathrm{Re}[S(z)]|\leq C\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\log|z|}

\displaystyle\int_{\theta\in[\pi,\pi+N^{2\varepsilon-1}]}\int_{r>1}|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)|\log(r)|\mathrm{Re}[S(z)]|\chi(r)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta

\left|\left\{i:\theta_{i}(t)\in I\right\}\right|\leq N\int\varphi(\theta)\rho_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta+CN^{3\varepsilon}.

Proof of Corollary

It follows from Proposition

that with overwhelming probability,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\bar{\theta}(t)|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Next, for each

(6.26)

\theta_{i}(t)=2\pi n_{i}(t)+\varphi_{i}(t)

for

an integer and

. For any fixed

it follows from the fact that the eigenvalues never cross that the set
\{n_{1}(t),n_{2}(t),\dots,n_{N}(t)\}
contains at most two consecutive integers whose absolute values we will denote by
m(t),m(t)+1
. Let
N_{1}(t)
be the number of eigenvalues s.t.
|n_{i}(t)|=m(t)
and
N_{2}(t)
be the number of eigenvalues s.t.
|n_{i}(t)|=m(t)+1
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[-\pi,\pi]
with a density. We have,

(6.27)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\theta\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mu([t,\pi])\mathrm{d}t,

and since
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\theta\rho_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta=0

(6.28)

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(t)\right|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N}

by Corollary
1.3
. From this it follows that,

(6.29)

(1+m(t))N_{2}(t)+m(t)N_{1}(t)\leq N^{\varepsilon}

with overwhelming probability. In particular,

Appendix A

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Theta}\rho(\Theta-x)\left(\frac{\sin(x)}{4\sin^{2}(x/2)}-\frac{(1+\eta)\sin(x+\kappa)}{\eta^{2}+4(1+\eta)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x+\kappa}{2}\right)}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Theta}\rho(\Theta-x)\sin(x+\kappa)\left(\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x+\kappa}{2}\right)}-\frac{r}{\eta^{2}+4r\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x+\kappa}{2}\right)}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Theta}\rho(\Theta-x)\sin(x+\kappa)\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x+\kappa}{2}\right)\left(\eta^{2}+4r\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x+\kappa}{2}\right)\right)}\right)\mathrm{d}x

(B.42)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\Delta^{-1/6}\int_{\eta}^{2\eta}\sqrt{x}\frac{\eta^{2}}{(x+\kappa)(\eta^{2}+(x+\kappa)^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\geq c\Delta^{-1/6}\eta^{1/2}\geq c\Delta^{-1/6}(\eta+\kappa)^{1/2}.

We have therefore proven that,

(B.43)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+z}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z}\right]-\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}}\right]\right)\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq c_{1}\Delta^{-1/6}(\kappa+\eta)^{1/2}

for some small
c_{1}>0
. On the other hand, using that,

(B.44)

\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+z}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+w}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-w}\right|\leq C|z-w|\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z|^{2}}+\frac{1}{|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-w|^{2}}\right)

for
z,w
in bounded regions of the complex plane, one easily sees that for
|z|\geq 1
in the upper-half plane,

(B.45)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{-\pi}\left(\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+z}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z}\right]-\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}}\right]\right)\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C|z-\Theta|\int_{0}^{\Theta}\frac{\rho(\Theta-x)}{x^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

A routine calculation shows that the integral is
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2/3})
. It follows that there is a small
c_{2}>0
so that for
|z-\Theta|\leq c_{2}\Delta
we have,

(B.46)

\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta})]-\mathrm{Im}[f(z)]\geq\Delta^{-1/6}\frac{c_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}.

From the proof of Lemma
3.1
we see that, since
\rho(x)
is symmetric, that

(B.47)

\theta\to\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})]

is decreasing for
\Theta<\theta<\pi
. Therefore, there is a
c_{3}>0
so that for
c_{2}\Delta/2\leq\kappa\leq\pi-\Theta
we have,

(B.48)

\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta})]-\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta+\kappa)}]\geq c_{3}\Delta^{1/3}.

On the other hand, for
c_{2}\Delta/2\leq\kappa\leq\pi-\Theta
we see from the above argument that,

(B.49)

\left|\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta+\kappa)})]-\mathrm{Im}[f((1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta+\kappa)})]\right|\leq C\eta\Delta^{-2/3}

It follows that there is a
c_{4}>0
so that for
\eta\leq c_{4}\Delta
and
c_{2}\Delta/2\leq\kappa\leq\pi-\Theta
that,

(B.50)

\mathrm{Im}[f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta})]-\mathrm{Im}[f((1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Theta+\kappa)})]\geq c_{4}\Delta^{1/3}.

This completes the proof. ∎

The following proposition is used in the Proof of Theorem

Proposition B.5
.

Let
c<t<4-c
for some
c>0
. Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
. In the domain

(B.51)

\mathcal{D}_{1}:=\{z=(1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:N^{\delta-1}<\eta<\delta^{-1}\}

the estimate

(B.52)

|\tilde{f}(z,t)-f(z,t)|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon}}{N\eta}

holds with overwhelming probability. In the domain,

(B.53)

\mathcal{D}_{2}:=\{z=(1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:N^{\delta-1}<\eta<\delta^{-1},\Theta_{t}+N^{-2/3+\delta}<|\theta|\leq\pi\}

Proof.
Let
\lambda_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}
be the eigenvalues and
\gamma_{i}
be the quantiles of
\rho_{t}(\theta)
. We know that

(B.55)

|\theta_{i}-\gamma_{i}|\leq\frac{N^{\varepsilon/10}}{N^{2/3}\min\{i^{1/3},(N+1-i)^{1/3}\}}

with overwhelming probability. We have,

(B.56)

\displaystyle\left|f(z,t)-\tilde{f}(z,t)\right|\leq 2|z|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\gamma_{i+1}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}}{(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z)(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}-z)}\right|\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Note the identity,

(B.57)

|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}-(1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}|^{2}=\eta^{2}+4(1+\eta)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\varphi-\theta}{2}\right),

which is useful for estimating from below the quantities in the denominator appearing above.
WLOG assume
z
is in the upper half-plane. For the first estimate we break the sum into the terms near
z
and far from
z
. Let
z=(1+\eta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
and let
i_{0}
be the index such that
|\theta_{i_{0}}-\theta|
is minimized. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{|i-i_{0}|<N^{\varepsilon/2}}\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\gamma_{i+1}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}}{(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z)(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}-z)}\right|\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|i-i_{0}|<N^{\varepsilon/2}}\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\gamma_{i+1}}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z|}+\frac{1}{|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}-z|}\right)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(B.58)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CN^{\varepsilon/2}}{N\eta}.

For
|i-i_{0}|>N^{\varepsilon/2}
on the event that (
B.55
) holds we have always that

(B.59)

2|\theta-\theta_{i}|\geq|\theta-x|,\qquad x\in[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}].

Moreover, one can check that (
B.55
) implies,

(B.60)

|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}|\leq C\frac{N^{\varepsilon/10}}{N\rho_{t}(x)},\qquad x\in[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}]

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{|i-i_{0}|>N^{\varepsilon/2}}\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\gamma_{i+1}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}}{(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z)(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}-z)}\right|\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(B.61)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N^{\varepsilon/10}}{N}\sum_{|i-i_{0}|>N^{\varepsilon/2}}\int_{\gamma_{i}}^{\gamma_{i+1}}\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\rho_{t}(x)(\eta^{2}+(\gamma_{i}-\theta)^{2})}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\frac{N^{\varepsilon/10}}{N\eta}

as desired. The second set of estimates is proven similarly. Instead of splitting into two sets of sums, we can directly find for a lower bound for the denominator,

(B.62)

|(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}-z)(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}}-z)|\geq c((\eta+\kappa)^{2}+(x-\theta)^{2}),\qquad x\in[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}].

This yields the claim. ∎

Appendix C
General
\beta

In this section we discuss extensions of our result to general
\beta>0
. In this case, we consider the
N
particles all on
\mathbb{R}
,

(C.1)

\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\beta}}\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j\neq i}\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{i}(t)-\theta_{j}(t)}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t

where
\theta_{i}(0)=0
for all

and
\{W_{i}\}_{i}
are a family of independent standard Brownian motions. We leave aside discussion of the existence of solutions to the above system for now and proceed at a formal level. Setting,
\lambda_{i}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{i}(t)}
, we find

(C.2)

\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}=\mathrm{i}\lambda_{i}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\beta}}\mathrm{d}W_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\lambda_{i}(\beta-2)}{2\beta N}\mathrm{d}t

1.6
) when

. Letting now,

\mathrm{d}f(z,t)=-\frac{zf(z,t)}{2}\partial_{z}f(z,t)+\mathrm{d}M_{t}^{\beta}(z)+\frac{\beta-2}{2\beta N}\left(z\partial_{z}f(z,t)+z^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}f(z,t)\right).

where

(C.5)

\mathrm{d}M^{\beta}_{t}(z)=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{N\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{2\mathrm{i}z\lambda_{i}(t)}{(\lambda_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t)

C.4

f
or not. Recall that our proof strategy is to introduce a stopping time
