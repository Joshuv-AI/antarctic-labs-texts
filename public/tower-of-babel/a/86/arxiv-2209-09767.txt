On additive MDS codes with linear projections
Sam Adriaensen, Simeon Ball

Abstract
We support some evidence that a long additive MDS code over a finite field must be equivalent to a linear code. More precisely, let C C be an 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} -linear ( n , q h ​ k , n − k + 1 ) q h (n,q^{hk},n-k+1)_{q^{h}} MDS code over 𝔽 q h \mathbb{F}_{q^{h}} . If k = 3 k=3 , h ∈ { 2 , 3 } h\in\left\{2,3\right\} , n > max ⁡ { q h − 1 , h ​ q − 1 } + 3 n>\max\left\{q^{h-1},hq-1\right\}+3 , and C C has three coordinates from which its projections are equivalent to linear codes, we prove that C C itself is equivalent to a linear code. If k > 3 k>3 , n > q + k n>q+k , and there are two disjoint subsets of coordinates whose combined size is at most k − 2 k-2 from which the projections of C C are equivalent to linear codes, we prove that C C is equivalent to a code which is linear over a larger field than 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} .

Introduction

MDS codes, which are codes meeting the Singleton bound, are very useful in different applications of coding theory and cryptography, such as error-correcting codes
[
Bal20
, Chapter 6]
, secret sharing
[
PZ03
]
, and distributed storage
[
DBGW+10
]
.
The classical examples are the Reed-Solomon codes.
They are constructed as follows.
Take a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and denote its elements as
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{q}
.
Choose an integer
k\leq q
and for any polynomial

f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]

of degree smaller than
k
, define
f(\infty)=f_{k-1}
.
Then

\left\{(f(\alpha_{1}),\dots,f(\alpha_{q}),f(\infty))\,\,||\,\,f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X],\,\deg(f)<k\right\}

is the
k
-dimensional Reed-Solomon code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This is an MDS code of length
q+1
.
If
k\in\left\{3,q-1\right\}
and
q
is even, this code can be extended to an MDS code of length
q+2
.

It is generally believed that if
C
is an
(n,q^{k},d)_{q}
MDS code with
d\geq 3
and
k<q
, then
n\leq q+1
, with a few known exceptions.
This belief is referred to as the
MDS conjecture
.
For linear MDS codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with
q=p^{h}
and
p
prime, this conjecture is known to hold if one of the following conditions is met:

•

q
is prime,

•

q
is not prime and
k\leq Cp^{\lfloor\frac{h+1}{2}\rfloor}
, for some constant
C
that depends on the parity of
q
and on whether
q
is a square.

For more details, we refer to the recent survey
[
BL19
]
.
The MDS conjecture has been verified for non-linear codes of alphabet size up to 8
[
KKO15
,
KO16
]
.

In this paper, we study additive MDS codes over finite fields.
It was shown in
[
BGL22
]
that these codes are equivalent to certain geometric objects, called pseudo-arcs.
The general link between additive codes over finite fields and certain geometric objects is explained in §
3
.
Pseudo-arcs have been investigated in the context of generalised quadrangles, and we revise the most relevant results for the study of MDS codes in §
4
.
In §
5
, we prove the following theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{3h},n-2)_{q^{h}}
MDS code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Suppose that one of the following holds.

1.

h=2
, and
n\geq 2q+3
.

2.

h=3
, and
n\geq q^{2}+3+\delta_{2,q}
.

If
C
has at least three coordinates from which the projections are equivalent to linear codes, then
C
itself is equivalent to a linear code.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{hk},n-k+1)_{q^{h}}
MDS code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, with
k>3
.
Suppose that there exist two subsets
A,B\subset[1,n]
such that

•

A\cap B=\varnothing
,

•

|A|+|B|\leq k-2
,

•

the projections of
C
from
A
and
B
are equivalent to linear codes.

If
n>q+k
, then
C
is equivalent to an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}}
-linear code for some divisor
s>1
of
h
.
Moreover, if
n>q^{e}+k
, with
e
the largest divisor of
h
, which is strictly smaller than
h
, then
C
is equivalent to a linear code.

For a slightly stronger version of the latter theorem, see Theorem
5.7
.

2
Preliminaries

We will start by revising the basics of coding theory.
For a survey, see e.g.
[
Bal20
]
.
Suppose that
A
is a finite set of size
q
.
A
code
of
length

n
over the
alphabet

A
is a subset
C
of
A^{n}
.
The
(Hamming) distance
between two vectors
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
is given by

d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=|\left\{i\in[1,n]\,\,||\,\,x_{i}\neq y_{i}\right\}|.

The
minimum distance
of
C
is given by

d(C)=\min\left\{d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in C,\,\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\right\}.

If
|C|=M
and
d(C)=d
, we call
C
an
(n,M,d)_{q}
code.

Suppose that the alphabet
A
of
C
is an abelian group.
Then we call
C

additive
if
C
is closed under addition, i.e.

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in C\Longrightarrow(x_{1}+y_{1},\dots,x_{n}+y_{n})\in C.

Moreover, suppose that the alphabet is a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Then
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{n}
is also an
nh
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
If
C
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{n}
we call
C

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-
linear
.
An
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-linear code over the alphabet
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is simply called linear.
Note that if
C
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear, it is also additive.

The
weight
of a codeword
\mathbf{x}
in an additive code is the number of coordinate positions in which
\mathbf{x}
has a non-zero entry, and we denote it by
\textnormal{wt}(\mathbf{x})
.
Note that
d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\textnormal{wt}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
, which implies that

d(C)=\min\left\{\textnormal{wt}(\mathbf{x})\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{x}\in C\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\right\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
C
and
D
be two
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
We call
C
and
D

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-equivalent
(or simply equivalent if
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is clear from context) if one can be obtained from the other by

•

permuting the coordinate positions,

•

in each coordinate position, applying an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear automorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.

We are allowed to apply different
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear automorphisms in the different coordinate positions.

Note that equivalent codes have the same size and minimum distance.

One of the most important bounds on the parameters of a code is the
Singleton bound
.

Theorem 2.2

(Singleton bound)
.

Let
C
be an
(n,M,d)_{q}
code.
Then

M\leq q^{n-d+1}.

Codes attaining equality in the Singleton bound are called
maximum distance separable
codes, or simply
MDS
codes.
The characteristic property of an
(n,q^{k},n-k+1)_{q}
MDS code over alphabet
A
is that if one chooses the entries of a vector of
A^{n}
in
k
positions, the vector can be completed in a unique way to a codeword of
C
.
One of the most central questions in the study of MDS codes is the following.

Question 2.3
.

Given positive integers
q
,
k
, what is the largest number
n
such that an MDS code with parameters
(n,q^{k},n-k+1)_{q}
exists?

Quite some effort has been invested in (partially) answering this question for linear MDS codes, as was mentioned in the introduction.
In this paper, we will investigate
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear MDS codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
On one end of the spectrum, we have the case where
q
is prime, in which case a code is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear if and only if it is additive.
On the other end of the spectrum, we have the case where
q=q^{h}
, in which case a code is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear if and only if it is linear.

3
The geometry behind additive codes

A linear
(n,q^{k},d)_{q}
code can be linked to a point set in
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
and vice versa.
In
[
BGL22
]
, this link was generalised to additive codes over finite fields.
In this section, we will explain this link and prove some extra properties. First we note that an additive code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
must be linear over some subfield, which we fix to be
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Consider an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{k},d)_{q^{h}}
code
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Let
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k\times n}
be a matrix whose rows form an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis for
C
.
We call
G
a
generator matrix
of
C
(over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
).
Choose an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h}
for
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, and write

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\dots&\alpha_{h}\end{pmatrix}^{t}
.
Then the
j
th
column of
G
is of the form
G_{j}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
for some
G_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times h}
.
Let
\pi_{j}
denote the subspace of
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
corresponding to the column space of
G_{j}
.
Note that the (projective) dimension of
\pi_{j}
is at most
h-1
.

Remark 3.1
.

\pi_{j}
is independent of the choice of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Indeed, take another
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{h}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, and write

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}&\dots&\beta_{h}\end{pmatrix}^{t}
.
Then

\boldsymbol{\beta}=M\boldsymbol{\alpha}

for some non-singular
M\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{h\times h}
.
Therefore,

G_{j}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(G_{j}M^{-1})\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Since
M^{-1}
is non-singular,
G_{j}
and
G_{j}M^{-1}
have the same column space.

The non-zero codewords of
C
are the vectors
\mathbf{a}G
,
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
.
Note that
\mathbf{a}G
has a zero in position
j
if and only if
\mathbf{a}G_{j}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=0
.
Since the entries of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, this is equivalent to
\mathbf{a}G_{j}=\mathbf{0}
.
This happens if and only if every column of
G_{j}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{a}
, or equivalently if
\pi_{j}
is contained in the hyperplane
a_{1}X_{1}+\dots+a_{k}X_{k}=0
of
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
.
Therefore,

d=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyperplane }\Pi\\
\text{of }\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)\end{subarray}}|\left\{j\,\,||\,\,\pi_{j}\not\subseteq\Pi\right\}|.

Definition 3.2
.

A
projective
h-(n,k,d)_{q}
system
is a multiset

\left\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\right\}

of subspaces in
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
that have dimension at most
h-1
, span the entire space, and such that

d=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyperplane }\Pi\\
\text{of }\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)\end{subarray}}|\left\{j\,\,||\,\,\pi_{j}\not\subseteq\Pi\right\}|.

We will also refer to it as a projective
h
-system.

As we just saw, an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{k},d)_{q}
code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
gives rise to a projective
h-(n,k,d)_{q}
system.
In fact, it gives rise to several such systems, since we have freedom in choosing the generator matrix
G
.
If
G
is one generator matrix of
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the others are exactly the matrices
MG
,
M\in\text{GL}(k,q)
.
Thus, the set of all projective
h
-systems corresponding to
C
forms an orbit under
\textnormal{PGL}(k,q)
.

Vice versa, given a projective
h-(n,k,d)_{q}
-system, we can construct an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{k},d)_{q^{h}}
code by doing the following.

•

Order the elements of the projective
h
-system, and denote the elements in this ordering as
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}
.

•

For each
\pi_{j}
, choose a
k\times h
matrix
G_{j}
whose column space corresponds to
\pi_{j}
.

•

Choose an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.

•

Construct the matrix

G=\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}&\dots&G_{n}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}.

•

Let
C
be the row space of
G
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We have freedom in choosing the ordering of the subspace, which corresponds to a coordinate permutation in the code.
We are also free to choose any matrix
G_{j}
whose column space corresponds to
\pi_{j}
.
A different choice for
G_{j}
corresponds to applying an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map in the
j
th
coordinate of
C
.

Thus there is an equivalence between:

•

Equivalence classes of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
(where the notion of equivalence is taking from Definition
2.1
).

•

Orbits of projective
h
-systems under
\textnormal{PGL}(k,q)
.

For the case
h=1
, this correspondence is well-known, see e.g.
[
TV91
, §1.1.2]

Definition 3.3
.

A
pseudo-arc
of
(h-1)
-spaces is a set of
(h-1)
-spaces in
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
such that any subset of size
k
spans the entire space.
If
h=1
, this is simply called an
arc
.

Recall that if
G
is a generator matrix of
C
, the codewords of
C
are of the form
\mathbf{a}G
, and
\textnormal{wt}(\mathbf{a}G)
equals the number of subspaces
\pi_{j}
not contained in the hyperplane
a_{1}X_{1}+\dots+a_{k}X_{k}=0
.
This leads to the following result.

Result 3.4

(
[
BGL22
]
)
.

An
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is MDS if and only if its associated projective
h
-system is a pseudo-arc.

We now describe a way to geometrically recognise
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear codes which are
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-equivalent to linear codes.

Definition 3.5
.

An
(h-1)
-spread
of
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
is a set of
(h-1)
-spaces in
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
such that each point is contained in exactly one of these
(h-1)
-spaces.

An
(h-1)
-spread of
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
is equivalent to a set of
h
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{kh}
such that every non-zero vector is contained in exactly one of them.
The classical way to construct such a set is by considering
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
as a
kh
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space.
The
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-dimension 1 have
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-dimension
h
, and every non-zero vector is contained in exactly one of them.
This gives us a so-called
Desarguesian spread
.
In general, the Desarguesian spreads are the spreads which are
PGL
-equivalent to this construction
[
VdV16
, Corollary 3.8]
.
Desarguesian spreads can also be characterised by the fact that the set of all subspaces spanned by some elements of the spread together with the natural incidence gives us
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q^{h})
.
For a survey on Desarguesian spreads, we refer the reader to
[
BL11
]
.

The previous description can be translated into a more algebraic one.
Fix a primitive element
\omega
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Then
1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{h-1}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis for
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
For each element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, define the matrix
M(\alpha)
as the matrix representation of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map

\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}:x\mapsto\alpha x

with respect to the basis
1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{h-1}
.
Alternatively, if the minimal polynomial of
\omega
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is

X^{h}-a_{h-1}X^{h-1}-\ldots-a_{0},

then

M(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&a_{0}\\
1&\ddots&&a_{1}\\
&\ddots&0&\vdots\\
&&1&a_{h-1}\end{pmatrix},

M(\omega^{j})=M(\omega)^{j}
, and
M(0)
is the zero matrix.
Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
,

\left\langle v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}}=\left\langle v,\omega v,\dots,\omega^{h-1}v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}.

If we replace each element of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
by its coordinate vector over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
w.r.t. the basis
1,\dots,\omega^{h-1}
, we see that

\left\{\textnormal{Col}\begin{pmatrix}M(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
M(x_{k})\end{pmatrix}\,\,||\,\,\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q^{h})\right\}

is a Desarguesian spread of
(h-1)
-spaces in
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
, where
\textnormal{Col}(M)
denotes the column space of a matrix
M
.

Lemma 3.6
.

An
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{kh},d)_{q^{h}}
code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-equivalent to a linear code if and only if its associated projective
h
-system consists of
(h-1)
-spaces is contained in a Desarguesian spread.

Proof.

First suppose that
C^{\prime}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, equivalent to the linear code
C
.
It suffices to prove that we can construct a projective
h
-system from
C
contained in a Desarguesian spread.
Take an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-basis
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
of
C
.
Then

G^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}-&g_{1}&-\\
-&\omega g_{1}&-\\
&\vdots&\\
-&\omega^{h-1}g_{1}&-\\
&\vdots&\\
-&g_{k}&-\\
&\vdots\\
-&\omega^{h-1}g_{k}&-\end{pmatrix}

is a generator matrix for
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Write

g_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{i1}&\dots&g_{in}\end{pmatrix}
for
i\in[1,k]
, and define

G_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}M(g_{1j})\\
\vdots\\
M(g_{kj}).\end{pmatrix}

Write

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\omega&\dots&\omega^{h-1}\end{pmatrix}^{t}
.
Then

G=\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}&\dots&G_{n}\boldsymbol{\omega}\end{pmatrix}

is a generator matrix of
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
\textnormal{Col}(G_{1}),\dots,\textnormal{Col}(G_{n})
is contained in a Desarguesian spread.

Vice versa, consider a projective
h
-system of
(h-1)
-spaces
\left\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\right\}
contained in a Desarguesian spread.
Then up to
PGL
-equivalence, there exist a matrix
G=(g_{ij})
such that

\pi_{j}=\textnormal{Col}\begin{pmatrix}M(g_{1j})\\
\vdots\\
M(g_{kj})\end{pmatrix}
.
Reversing the above part of the proof, we see that the codes associated to this projective
h
-system are the codes which are
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-equivalent to the row space of
G
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
∎

Lastly, an important tool in this paper will be projections, both of projective
h
-systems and of codes.
We will give the definitions here, and prove their equivalence.

Definition 3.7
.

The
projection
of an additive
(n,M,d)_{q}
code
C
from position
i
is the code

\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})\,\,||\,\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},0,x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})\in C\right\}.

Remark 3.8
.

The above process is sometimes also referred to as
shortening
.

Let
\pi
be an
(m-1)
-space in
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
and let
U
be the corresponding
m
-dimensional subspace of
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
.
The cosets of
U
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
form a
(k-m)
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which we denote by
V/U
.
For every subspace
W
of
V
, define its
projection
from
U
as the subspace
W+U
in
V/U
.
In the projective space
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)=\textnormal{PG}(V)
, this yields a map from its subspaces to the subspaces of
\textnormal{PG}(k-m-1,q)=\textnormal{PG}(V/U)
.
We call this map the projection from
\pi
.

A more geometric way to describe this projection is as follows.
Take a
(k-m-1)
-space
\Sigma
in
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
, disjoint to
\pi
.
Map a subspace
\rho
of
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
to the subspace
\left\langle\pi,\rho\right\rangle\cap\Sigma
.
For any choice of
\Sigma
, this map is
PGL
-equivalent to the projection from
\pi
as defined above.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\right\}
be a projective
h
-system in
\textnormal{PG}(k-1,q)
.
The
projection
of
\mathcal{A}
from
\pi_{i}
equals the projective
h
-system consisting of the images of the elements of
\mathcal{A}\setminus\left\{\pi_{i}\right\}
under the projection from
\pi_{i}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{k},d)_{q^{h}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, and let
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\right\}
be an associated projective
h
-system (where
\pi_{j}
corresponds to position
j
in the codewords of
C
).
Then the projective
h
-systems associated to the projection of
C
from position

equal (up to
PGL
-equivalence) the projection of
\mathcal{A}
from
\pi_{i}
.

Proof.

We will use vector space notation instead of projective space notation for the projective
h
-systems.
Let
G
be a generator matrix for
C
.
Fix an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Write

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\dots&\alpha_{h}\end{pmatrix}^{t}
.
Suppose that

G=\begin{pmatrix}G_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}&\dots&G_{n}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}.

Denote the columns of
G_{j}
by
G_{j}^{1},\dots,G_{j}^{h}
, and denote the dual space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
by
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k})^{*}
.
Then

\displaystyle C=\left\{\mathbf{a}G\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\right\}=\left\{\Big(\begin{pmatrix}f(G_{j}^{1})&\dots&f(G_{j}^{h})\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\Big)_{j\in[1,n]}\,\,||\,\,f\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k})^{*}\right\}.

Then the projection of
C
from position
i
equals

\displaystyle C^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\{\Big(\begin{pmatrix}f(G_{j}^{1})&\dots&f(G_{j}^{h})\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\Big)_{j\in[1,n]\setminus\left\{i\right\}}\,\,||\,\,f\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k})^{*},\,\textnormal{Col}(G_{i})\in\ker f\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\Big(\begin{pmatrix}f\left(\left\langle G_{j}^{1},\textnormal{Col}(G_{i})\right\rangle\right)&\dots&f\left(\left\langle G_{j}^{h},\textnormal{Col}(G_{i})\right\rangle\right)\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\Big)_{j\in[1,n]\setminus\left\{i\right\}}\,\,||\,\,f\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}/\textnormal{Col}(G_{i}))^{*}\right\}.

So the projective
h
-system associated to
C^{\prime}
in vector space notation equals

\langle\textnormal{Col}(G_{2}),\textnormal{Col}(G_{1})\rangle,\dots,\langle\textnormal{Col}(G_{n}),\textnormal{Col}(G_{1})\rangle

in the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}/\textnormal{Col}(G_{1})
.
In projective terminology, this equals the projection of
\mathcal{A}
from
\pi_{i}
.
∎

These ideas can be generalised.
The projection of
C
from a subset of positions can be obtained by consecutively projecting from each of the positions.
Likewise, the projection of a projective
h
-system can be obtained by consecutively projecting from the elements of this subset.
The above lemma generalises in an obvious way to these more general notions of projection.

4
Pseudo-arcs and translation generalised quadrangles

Definition 4.1
.

A
generalised quadrangle
of order
(s,t)
is a point-line incidence geometry such that

1.

every line is incident with exactly
s+1
points (
s\geq 1
),

2.

every point is incident with exactly
t+1
lines (
t\geq 1
),

3.

given a point
P
and a line
l
not incident with
P
,
P
is collinear with a unique point of
l
.

An
oval
is an arc of size
q+1
in
\textnormal{PG}(2,q)
.
Tits described a way to construct a generalised quadrangle
T_{2}(\mathcal{O})
of order
(q,q)
, from an oval.
A
pseudo-oval
is a pseudo-arc of
(h-1)
-spaces of size
q^{h}+1
in
\textnormal{PG}(3h-1,q)
.
As a generalisation of Tits’ construction, a generalised quadrangle of order
(q^{h},q^{h})
can be constructed from a pseudo-oval.
Generalised quadrangles arising from this construction are characterised by certain properties of their automorphism group, and are called
translation generalised quadrangles
.
We refer to the monograph
[
TTVM06
]
for more information.

Suppose that a pseudo-oval is contained in a Desarguesian spread.
We can interpret the elements of the spread as points in
\textnormal{PG}(2,q^{h})
.
Hence, the pseudo-oval corresponds to an oval in
\textnormal{PG}(2,q^{h})
.
The generalised quadrangle constructed from the pseudo-oval in
\textnormal{PG}(3h-1,q)
will be isomorphic to the generalised quadrangle constructed from the oval in
\textnormal{PG}(2,q^{h})
.
Therefore, people have been looking for pseudo-ovals not contained in Desarguesian spreads, but none have been found so far.
One could wonder whether there even exist pseudo-ovals not contained in Desarguesian spreads.
There is some evidence to the contrary.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a pseudo-arc of
(h-1)
-spaces in
\textnormal{PG}(kh-1,q)
, of size
n
.
One easily verifies that the projection of
\mathcal{A}
from any of its elements is a pseudo-arc of
(h-1)
-spaces of size
n-1
in
\textnormal{PG}((k-1)h-1,q)
.
The projection of a Desarguesian spread from any of its elements is again a Desarguesian spread.
Hence, if a pseudo-arc is contained in a Desarguesian spread, then so are all its projections.
Conversely, can we infer that a pseudo-arc is contained in a Desarguesian spread if some of its projections are?

The following result proved by Penttila and Van de Voorde
[
PVdV13
]
strengthens a result by Casse, Thas, and Wild
[
CTW85
]
.
A
conic
in a projective plane
\textnormal{PG}(2,q)
is a set of points satisfying an irreducible homogeneous quadratic equation.
All conics are projectively equivalent, and Segre
[

]
proved that the largest arcs in
\textnormal{PG}(2,q)

q
odd, are exactly the conics, which have

[

Proof of the main theorem

The characteristic property of an
(n,q^{k},n-k+1)_{q}
MDS code
C
over an alphabet
A
is that if you fix the entries of a vector in
A^{n}
in any
k
positions, there is a unique way to choose the
n-k
remaining entries from
A
which gives you a codeword of
C
.
Thus, an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{kh},n-k+1)_{q^{h}}
MDS code
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is of the form
C=\left\{(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}
,
for some function
f:\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{n-k}
.
Take
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Since
C
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear, the codeword

\alpha(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))+\beta(\mathbf{y},f(\mathbf{y}))=(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y},\alpha f(\mathbf{x})+\beta f(\mathbf{y}))

is also in
C
.
Hence,

(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y},\alpha f(\mathbf{x})+\beta f(\mathbf{y}))=(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y},f(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y})).

This implies that
f
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear.
In other words,
f
is of the form

f:(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto(f_{k+1,1}(x_{1})+\ldots+f_{k+1,k}(x_{k}),\,\dots\,,f_{n,1}(x_{1})+\ldots+f_{n,k}(x_{k})),

where each
f_{i,j}:\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is a linearised polynomial, i.e. it is of the form

f_{i,j}(X)=\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}a_{l}X^{q^{l}},

for some coefficients
a_{l}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
We will use the notation
f_{i,j}
in this way throughout this section, with
(i,j)\in[k+1,n]\times[1,k]
.
The fact that
C
is MDS implies that every
f_{i,j}
is invertible.

The next result applies to a larger class of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear codes than only the MDS codes.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{hk},d)_{q^{h}}
code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
We say that
C
is in
standard form
if

C=\left\{(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}

for some
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map

f:\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{n-k}
satisfying

•

f_{k+1,j}(x_{j})=x_{j}
for all
j\in[1,k]
,

•

f_{i,1}(x_{1})=x_{1}
for all
i\in[k+1,n]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that
C=\left\{(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{hk},d)_{q^{h}}
-code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
in standard form.

C
is equivalent to a linear code if and only if there exist an invertible linearised polynomial
g
, such that

f_{i,j}(x_{j})=g(a_{i,j}g^{-1}(x_{j}))

for all
(i,j)\in[k+2,n]\times[2,k]
, with all
a_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
constants.

Proof.

Let
D
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-linear code equivalent to
C
.
We may suppose without loss of generality that we do not need to permute the coordinate positions of
D
, since such a permutation does not affect the linearity of
D
.
The first
k+1
positions of
C
are a
[k+1,k,2]_{q^{h}}
MDS code.
Therefore, the same holds for the first
k+1
positions of
D
.
Consider a generator matrix
(M\;z\;N)
of
D
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, where
M
is square, and
z
a column vector.
Then
M
must have full rank due to the MDS property.
Therefore,
(I_{k}\;z^{\prime}\;N^{\prime})
, with
z^{\prime}=M^{-1}z
and
N^{\prime}=M^{-1}N
is also a generator matrix for
D
.
Furthermore,
z^{\prime}
cannot have any entries equal to zero, for this would violate the MDS property.

Let
\mathbf{1}
denote the all-one vector, and let
\Delta
denote the diagonal matrix with the entries of
z^{\prime}
on the diagonal.
Consider the
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
-linear code
E
with generator matrix

G=(I_{k}\;\mathbf{1}\;\Delta^{-1}N^{\prime})=\Delta^{-1}(\Delta\;z^{\prime}\;N^{\prime}).

Then
(\Delta\;z^{\prime}\;N^{\prime})
is also a generator matrix for
E
, which shows that
E
is an equivalent code to
D
, without permutation of the coordinate positions.
Thus,
C
can be constructed from
E
by applying an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear permutation
g_{i}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
to each coordinate
i
.
Hence,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(g_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,g_{k}(x_{k}),\sum_{j=1}^{k}g_{k+1}(x_{j}),...,\sum_{j=1}^{k}g_{i}(a_{i,j}x_{j}),\dots\right)\,\,||\,\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}

(1)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{k},y_{1}+\dots+y_{k},\dots,y_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}f_{i,j}(y_{j}),\dots\right)\,\,||\,\,(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\},

(2)

with
(a_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in[k+2,n]\times[1,k]}=\Delta^{-1}N^{\prime}
.

Let
e_{j}
denote the vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
with a 1 in position
j
, and zeros everywhere else.
Since
C
contains the codeword
(e_{1},f(e_{1}))=(e_{1},\mathbf{1})
, this has to equal the codeword

(g_{1}(x_{1}),0,\dots,0,g_{k+1}(x_{1}),\dots,g_{j}(a_{i,1}x_{1}),\dots),

for some
x_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}

Hence, none of the
a_{i,1}
are zero.
We can replace
E
by an equivalent linear code by rescaling the last columns of
G
in such a way that each
a_{i,1}=1
.

Now choose
j\in[1,k]
.
Consider the codewords
(\beta e_{j},f(\beta e_{j}))
of
C
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
.
These correspond to the codewords of
C
in (
1
) with
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=x_{j}e_{j}
.
Looking in positions
j
and
k+1
, we see that
g_{j}(x_{j})=g_{k+1}(x_{j})
for each
x_{j}
.
Write
g=g_{k+1}
.

Now choose
j>k+1
and look at the codeword
(\beta e_{1},f(\beta e_{1}))
in positions 1 and
j
.
Then we see that
g_{1}(x_{1})=g_{j}(x_{1})
for all
x_{1}
.
Thus
g_{i}=g
for all

.
In (
1
), we can replace
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
by
(g^{-1}(x_{1}),\dots,g^{-1}(x_{k}))
, since the latter also runs through all elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}
if we vary
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.
Therefore,

C=\left\{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},x_{1}+\dots+x_{k},\dots,x_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}g(a_{i,j}g^{-1}(x_{j})),\dots\right)\,\,||\,\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\},

as claimed.

Now, conversely, suppose that

is in standard form and that
f_{i,j}(x_{j})=g(a_{i,j}g^{-1}(x_{j}))
for all
(i,j)\in[k+2,n]\times[2,k]
.
Let
D
be the code equivalent to

obtained by applying the linearised polynomial

in every position, i.e.

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(g^{-1}(x_{1}),\dots,g^{-1}(x_{k}),\dots,g^{-1}(x_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{k}a_{i,j}g^{-1}(x_{j}),\dots\right)\,\,||\,\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{k},\dots,y_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}a_{i,j}y_{j},\dots\right)\,\,||\,\,(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{k}\right\}.

Then

is clearly linear.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f
be an invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
, and take

.

is
\mathbb{F}_{q}(a)
-semi-linear.

Proof.

First suppose that
f
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}(a)
-semi-linear, thus there exists a field automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
such that

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(a)

Note that since

-linearised polynomial

Then

Vice versa, suppose that

, and hence also

\left(\begin{array}[]{c c | c c c}1&\mathbf{0}&1&\dots&1\\

Since

\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}}

Then by Lemma

The last part of the theorem follows from inductively applying the first part, relying on the fact that

To make the statement of the proof a bit less technical, one can replace the right-hand sides of the bounds in (

One could wonder how tight the bound on

, and

Write

Note that

.
Take a linear

\mathbb{F}_{q}

of this code in standard form.
Write

&1&&&1&a_{6,2}&\dots&a_{n,2}\\
&&1&&1&a_{6,3}&\dots&a_{n,3}\\
&&&1&1&a_{6,4}&\dots&a_{n,4}\end{pmatrix}

Consider the linear code
C
generated by
G
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
This code is still MDS.
We want to make a slight modification so that the code still has two postions from which its projection is equivalent to a linear code, but so that the code itself is no longer equivalent to a linear code.

Choose
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}\varsubsetneq\mathbb{F}_{q}(\alpha)\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}(\beta)

Note that if we replace

by
\alpha

still generates an MDS code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
We make one more modification.
Choose an invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial

that is not semi-linear over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\alpha)\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}(\beta)
.
Now consider the code

consisting of the codewords

\Big(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4},\underbrace{\dots,x_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{4}a_{i,j}x_{j}\dots}_{i=6,\dots,n-1},x_{1}+a_{n,2}x_{2}+\alpha x_{3}+g(\beta g^{-1}(x_{4}))\Big)

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})

\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{4}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

The projections of
C
from the third and from the fourth coordinate are equivalent to linear codes, but
C
is not equivalent to a linear code.

Proof.

The projection from the fourth coordinate being linear is obvious.
Now project from the third coordinate.
Apply
g^{-1}
in every coordinate position, and write
y_{j}=g^{-1}(x_{j})
.
Then the obtained code is

\Big(y_{1},y_{2},y_{4},y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{4},\dots,y_{1}+\sum_{j=2,4}a_{i,j}y_{j}\dots,y_{1}+a_{n,2}y_{2}+\beta y_{4}\Big)

which is clearly linear.

Now suppose that
C
is equivalent to a linear code.
By Lemma
5.2
, there exists an invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial
f
such that

1.

f(\gamma f^{-1}(X))\equiv\alpha X
, for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
This implies that
f^{-1}(\alpha f(X))\equiv\gamma X
.
Hence,

is
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\alpha)
-semi-linear.

2.

f(\delta f^{-1}(X))\equiv g(\beta g^{-1}(X))
for some
\delta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
This implies that
(f^{-1}\circ g)(\beta(f^{-1}\circ g)(X))\equiv\delta X
.
Hence,
f^{-1}\circ g
is
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\beta)
-semi-linear.

This implies that

is semi-linear over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\alpha)\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}(\beta)

.
∎

The question remains how we must choose
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
g
such that

is an MDS code.
It must hold that if we choose 4 coordinate positions of
C
, and we want the entries of the positions to be zero, this is only possible if all

.
Since
C
is a slight modification of an MDS code, the only thing we need to check is the following.
Choose 4 coordinate positions of
C
, including the last one.
Write down the linear system that says we have zeros in these positions.

\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{i,j}x_{i,j}=0&i=i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\\
x_{1}+a_{n,2}x_{2}+\alpha x_{3}+g(\beta g^{-1}(x_{4}))=0\end{cases}

We only need to prove that if we eliminate the variables
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
from these equations, we are left with an equation that implies that
x_{4}=0
.
Then
x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=0
by the MDS property of our original code.
After elimination, the remaining equation is of the form

g(\beta g^{-1}(x_{4}))=(\lambda_{1}\alpha+\lambda_{2})x_{4}

for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
In other words, if no non-zero

satisfies

, then

is an MDS code.

Although it is not evident to find a general construction for
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
g
, for small values of

, we found examples by computer.

5.2
The case
k=3

The case
k=3
is more difficult, since then there is not enough “overlap” between the different projections.

Definition 5.9
.

Let
f
and
g
be invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
We say that
(f,g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{m})
if there exist triples
(a_{j},b_{j},c_{j})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{*})^{3}
,
1\leq j\leq m
, such that

a_{j}f(b_{j}f^{-1}(X))\equiv g(c_{j}g^{-1}(X))

and for every
i\neq j
, it holds that
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
,
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
, and
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
.

We will always suppose that
a_{1}=b_{1}=c_{1}=1
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose that for a given value of
m
, the only pairs of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomials of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
satisfying property
(Prop_{m})
are monomials.
Then every
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{3h},n-2)_{q^{h}}
code
C
, with
n\geq 3+m
which has at least three projections that are equivalent to linear codes, is itself equivalent to a linear code.

Proof.

Suppose the hypothesis from the lemma holds.
As in the previous proof, we may suppose that
C
is in standard form, and the projections from the first three coordinates are equivalent to linear codes.
Since the projections from the second and third coordinate are equivalent to linear codes, Lemma
5.2

implies that
C
is of the form

\left\{(x,y,z,\dots,x+g_{1}(a_{j}g_{1}^{-1}(y))+g_{2}(b_{j}g_{2}^{-1}(z)),\dots)\,\,||\,\,(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{3}\right\},

with
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomials, and
a_{j}
,
b_{j}
constants.
This code is equivalent to

\displaystyle C^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\left\{(g_{1}^{-1}(x),g_{1}^{-1}(y),g_{1}^{-1}(z),\dots,g_{1}^{-1}[x+g_{1}(a_{j}g_{1}^{-1}(y))+g_{2}(b_{j}g_{2}^{-1}(z))],\dots)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y,z,\dots,x+a_{j}y+g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(b_{j}(g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2})^{-1}(z)),\dots)\right\}.

Now write
g=g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}
.
The fact that the projection from the first coordinate is equivalent to a linear code implies that there exists an invertible
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial
f
such that

a_{j}^{-1}g(b_{j}g^{-1}(z))=f(c_{j}f^{-1}(z))

for some constants
c_{j}
.
The fact that
C
is an MDS code implies that
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
,
b_{i}\neq b_{j}
, and
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
if
i\neq j
.
Thus,
(f,g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{m})
.
Therefore, they are monomials, which implies that
g(b_{j}g^{-1}(X))
is a linear function for every value
b_{j}
.
Hence,

is linear.
∎

Definition 5.11
.

The
Dickson matrix
of an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial

over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
is the matrix

M_{F}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{0}&F_{1}&\dots&F_{h-1}\\
F_{h-1}^{q}&F_{0}^{q}&&F_{h-2}^{q}\\
&&\ddots\\
F_{1}^{q^{h-1}}&F_{2}^{q^{h-1}}&\dots&F_{0}^{q^{h-1}}\end{pmatrix}=\left(F_{j-i}^{q^{i}}\right)_{i,j\in[0,h-1]},

where we take the indices of

modulo
h
.

For more on Dickson matrices, see e.g.
[
WL13
]
.
An important property is that
M_{F\circ G}=M_{F}M_{G}
and hence
M_{F^{-1}}=M_{F}^{-1}
.

Lemma 5.12
.

Suppose that
m>\max\{q^{h-1},hq-1\}
.
If
(f,g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{m})
, then

|\left\{i\,\,||\,\,f_{i}=0\right\}|=|\left\{i\,\,||\,\,g_{i}=0\right\}|\geq 1.

Proof.

By Remark
5.4
, we may suppose w.l.o.g. that
f_{0}\neq 0
and
g_{0}\neq 0
.
Let
(a_{j},b_{j},c_{j})
be as in Definition
5.9
.
Write
B_{i,j}=b_{j}^{q^{i}}-b_{j}
for

.
Then

\displaystyle f(b_{j}f^{-1}(X))\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}f_{i}\left(b_{j}\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\overline{f}_{l}X^{q^{l}}\right)^{q^{i}}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}b_{j}^{q^{i}}f_{i}\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\overline{f}_{l-i}^{q^{i}}X^{q^{l}}\equiv b_{j}F(X)+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}B_{ij}f_{i}\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\overline{f}_{l-i}^{q^{i}}X^{q^{l}}

for some
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearised polynomial

, where we take the indices of
\overline{f}_{i}
modulo
h
.
Since
b_{1}=1
, it holds that
f(b_{1}f^{-1}(X))\equiv X
and all
B_{i,1}=0
, thus

.

Likewise define
C_{ij}=c_{j}^{q^{i}}-c_{j}
.
Then for every

\displaystyle a_{j}\left(b_{j}X+\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}B_{i,j}f_{i}\overline{f}_{l-i}^{q^{i}}\right)X^{q^{l}}\right)\equiv c_{j}X+\sum_{l=0}^{h-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}C_{i,j}g_{i}\overline{g}_{l-i}^{q^{i}}\right)X^{q^{l}}.

(5)

Consider the Dickson matrices
M_{f^{-1}}
and
M_{f}
of
f^{-1}
and
f
.
Since
M_{f}M_{f^{-1}}=I
,

\begin{pmatrix}f_{0}&\cdots&f_{h-1}\end{pmatrix}M_{f^{-1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}

Let
\hat{M}_{f}
denote the submatrix of

obtained by deleting the top row and first column.
By Cramer’s rule,
\displaystyle f_{0}=\frac{\det\hat{M}_{f}}{\det M_{f^{-1}}}
.
Since we assumed
f_{0}\neq 0
, this implies that
\det\hat{M}_{f}\neq 0
.
Define the diagonal matrix

D_{f}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&f_{h-1}\end{pmatrix}

Analogously define
\hat{M}_{g}
and
D_{g}
.
Write
B_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1,j}\\
\vdots\\
B_{h-1,j}\end{pmatrix}
and
C_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{1,j}\\
\vdots\\
C_{h-1,j}\end{pmatrix}
.
Looking in (
5
) at the coefficients of
X^{q},\dots,X^{q^{h-1}}
, we see that for any
j
,

\displaystyle a_{j}\hat{M}_{f}D_{f}B_{j}=\hat{M}_{g}D_{g}C_{j}.

(6)

Take a vector
u
in the left-kernel of
\hat{M}_{f}D_{f}
.
Then for any
j
,
u\hat{M}_{g}D_{g}C_{j}=0
.
Since all entries of
C_{j}
are polynomial in
c_{j}
of degree at most
q^{h-1}
, we find a polynomial equation in
c_{j}
of degree at most
q^{h-1}
.
Since
(f,g)
satisfies
(Prop_{q^{h-1}+1})
, this equation has at least
q^{h-1}+1
roots, and therefore identically equals the zero polynomial.
This implies that
u\hat{M}_{g}D_{g}=0
.
We can repeat this argument with the roles of
f
and
g
reversed.
It follows that
\hat{M}_{f}D_{f}
and
\hat{M}_{g}D_{g}
have the same left kernel.
Since
\hat{M}_{f}
and
\hat{M}_{g}
have full rank, this implies that

and
D_{g}
have the same rank.
Note that the rank defect of
D_{f}
and
D_{g}
equal respectively
|\left\{i\,\,||\,\,f_{i}=0\right\}|
and
|\left\{i\,\,||\,\,g_{i}=0\right\}|
.

What is left to prove, is that
D_{f}
and
D_{g}
do not have full rank.
So suppose the contrary.
Consider the field automorphism
\varphi:x\mapsto x^{q}
.
For a matrix
A
, we let
A^{\varphi}
denote the matrix obtained by applying
\varphi
to all its entries.
Note that

B_{i,j}^{q}=b_{j}^{q+1}-b_{j}^{q}=\begin{cases}B_{i+1,j}-B_{1,j}&\text{if }i<h-1,\\
-B_{1,j}&\text{if }i=h-1.\end{cases}

Define the matrix

L=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
-1&&&1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}

Then

and it is easy to check that

has full rank.
If we apply
\varphi
to (

a_{j}^{q}\hat{M}_{f}^{\varphi}D_{f}^{\varphi}LB_{j}=\hat{M}_{g}^{\varphi}D_{g}^{\varphi}LC_{j}

This implies that

C_{j}=a_{j}D_{g}^{-1}\hat{M}_{g}^{-1}\hat{M}_{f}D_{f}B_{j}=a_{j}^{q}L^{-1}D_{g}^{-\varphi}\hat{M}_{g}^{-\varphi}\hat{M}_{f}^{\varphi}D_{f}^{\varphi}LB_{j}.

Lemma 5.13
.

If
(f,g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{m})
, then so do
(f^{-1},f^{-1}\circ g)
and
(g^{-1},g^{-1}\circ f)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle a_{j}f(b_{j}f^{-1}(X))

\displaystyle\equiv g(c_{j}g^{-1}(X))

\displaystyle\iff f(b_{j}^{-1}f^{-1}(a_{j}^{-1}X))\equiv[a_{j}f(b_{j}f^{-1}(X))]^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv[g(c_{j}g^{-1}(X))]^{-1}\equiv g(c_{j}^{-1}g^{-1}(X))

\displaystyle\iff b_{j}^{-1}f^{-1}(a_{j}^{-1}f(Y))

\displaystyle\equiv f^{-1}\circ g(c_{j}^{-1}g^{-1}\circ f(Y))

The last equality follows from the substitution
X=f(Y)
.
Since
(f^{-1}\circ g)^{-1}=g^{-1}\circ f
, it follows that
(f^{-1},f^{-1}\circ g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{m})
.
The proof for
(g^{-1},g^{-1}\circ f)

∎

Lemma 5.14
.

Suppose that
f
has exactly two non-zero coefficients, and that
f
is not semi-linear over some subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q^{s}}\supset\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Then all coefficients of
f^{-1}
are non-zero.

Proof.

We can again compose
f
with a monomial to make
f_{0}
one of the non-zero coefficients.
Then the other non-zero coefficient is
f_{j}
where
j
is coprime with
h
.

Let
M
be the transposition of the Dickson matrix of
f
, that is

M=M_{f}^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0}&&&&f_{j}^{q^{h-j}}\\
&f_{0}^{q}&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&&f_{j}^{q^{h-1}}\\
&&&\ddots\\
f_{j}\\
&f_{j}^{q}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&f_{j}^{q^{h-j-1}}&&&f_{0}^{q^{h-1}}\end{pmatrix}

Then
MM_{f^{-1}}^{t}=I_{h}
.
Looking at the first column, this implies that

M\begin{pmatrix}\overline{f}_{0}\\
\overline{f}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\overline{f}_{h-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, by Cramer’s rule, we need to prove that if we remove the top row of
M
, then any
(h-1)\times(h-1)
submatrix of has non-zero determinant.
We do this using the following expression for the determinant of a
n\times n
-matrix
A=(a_{il})
.

\det(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{\sigma(i)\,i},

where
S_{n}
denotes the symmetric group on
n
elements and
\text{sgn}(\sigma)
denotes the sign of the permutation
\sigma
.
We will prove for every
(h-1)\times(h-1)
-submatrix described above that when expressing its determinant in this way, the sum has exactly one non-zero term, hence is not zero.

So remove column
k
and the top row from
M
, and index the rows and columns in the corresponding matrix by
[1,h-1]\times[0,h-1]\setminus\left\{k\right\}
.
We will denote the indices modulo
h
.
We need to prove that there is a unique bijection
\sigma:[0,h-1]\setminus\left\{k\right\}\to[1,h-1]
such that
M_{\sigma(i),i}\neq 0
for all
i
.
Equivalently, for every
i
,
\sigma(i)\in\left\{i,i+j\right\}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.
Suppose that
\sigma(i)=i
.
If
i-j\neq k
, then
\sigma(i-j)=i-j
, since
\sigma(i-j)=i
would violate the injectivity of
\sigma
.
Likewise if
\sigma(i)=i+j
and
i+j\neq k
, then
\sigma(i+j)=i+2j
.

Now write
i\prec l
if
i\equiv aj\pmod{h}
and
l\equiv bj\pmod{h}
for some
0\leq a<b<h
.
In other words,
i\prec l
if given the residue classes of
ij^{-1}
and
lj^{-1}
modulo
h
, the former has the smallest representative in
[0,h-1]
.
Note that this ordering is well-defined because
j
is coprime with
h
.

Consider a column
i
.

•

If
i\prec k
, we can walk from column 0 to column
i
with step size
j
without encountering column
k
.
Since
\sigma(0)
must equal
j
, this means that
\sigma(i)
must equal
i+j
.

•

If
i\succ k
, we can walk from column
-j
to column
i
with step size
-j
.
Since
\sigma(-j)
must equal
-j
, this implies that
\sigma(i)
must equal
i
.

Hence, the only possible way to construct
\sigma
is as follows.

\sigma(i)=\begin{cases}i+j&\text{if }i\prec k,\\
i&\text{if }i\succ k.\end{cases}

One can easily check that this gives indeed the desired bijection.
∎

Theorem 5.15
.

Let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
(n,q^{3h},n-2)_{q^{h}}
MDS code over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.
Suppose that one of the following holds.

1.

h=2
, and
n\geq 2q+3
.

2.

h=3
, and
n\geq q^{2}+3+\delta_{2,q}
.

If
C
has at least three coordinates from which the projections are equivalent to linear codes, then
C
itself is equivalent to a linear code.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.10
, it suffices to prove that if
(f,g)
satisfies property
(Prop_{n-3})
, then
f
and
g
are monomials.
By Lemma
5.12
and
5.13
, we know that
f
and
f^{-1}
have at least one coefficient equal to zero.
For
h=2
, this proves that
f
is a monomial.
Now suppose that
h=3

If
f
would have exactly one coefficient equal to zero, then
f^{-1}
would have no coefficients equal to zero by Lemma

.
Thus,
f
has at least two coefficients equal to zero, which implies that
f

Likewise for
g
.
∎
