Superconductivity induced by the inter-valley Coulomb scattering in a few layers of graphene
Tommaso Cea

Abstract
We study the inter-valley scattering induced by the Coulomb repulsion as a purely electronic mechanism for the origin of superconductivity in few layers of graphene. The pairing is strongly favored by the presence of van Hove singularities (VHS’s) in the density of states (DOS). We consider three different hetherostructures: twisted bilayer graphene (TBG), rhombohedral trilayer graphene (RTG) and Bernal bilayer graphene (BBG). We obtain trends and estimates of the superconducting (SC) critical temperature in agreement with the experimental findings, which might identify the inter-valley Coulomb scattering as a universal pairing mechanism in few layers of graphene.

In this article, we study the inter-valley scattering
induced by the Coulomb interaction
as a purely electronic mechanism for the origin
of superconductivity in few layers of graphene.
The resulting Cooper pairs are spin-triplets
with the two electrons in opposite valleys,
K,K^{\prime}
,
featuring

Because the large momentum transfer,
\Delta K\equiv K-K^{\prime}
,
involved in the process makes the interaction strength negligible,
a high DOS is necessary to boost the pairing.
This condition is often realized in few layers of graphene,
where the electronic bands can be flattened
by tuning a number of experimental parameters,
thus giving rise to VHS’s.
At first approximation, we neglect the contribution of the intra-valley Coulomb repulsion,
which is long-ranged,
since it is drastically screened in the van Hove scenario.
We show quantitatively that this assumption is fully justified in the SI
SI
.
Using effective continuum models with realistic parameters,
we characterize the SC transition induced by the
inter-valley Coulomb scattering in
TBG, RTG and BBG, upon varying the relative twist between the layers and/or
the electronic density and/or the displacement field.
We obtain estimates and trends of
T_{c}

in good agreement with the experimental results,
emphasizing the strong enhancement of
T_{c}

by the presence of VHS’s.
Remarkably, our calculations account for the different orders of magnitude
of the critical temperatures observed in different materials.
Considering also the experimental evidence
of spin-triplet superconductivity in these systems,
our study might identify the inter-valley Coulomb scattering as a universal driving mechanism
for the superconductivity observed so far in few layers of graphene.
We also identify a non-trivial structure of the SC order parameter (OP) in real space.

II
The model: effective attraction from the inter-valley scattering

Our theoretical description of the pairing interaction starts from considering the
Coulomb repulsion between the
p_{z}

electrons within the minimal lattice model for a multilayer of graphene:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}}\sum_{ij\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})V^{ij}_{C}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})c_{j\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})c_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R}),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{R}
are the coordinates of the Bravais lattice,
i,j
are the labels of the sub-lattice/layer,

is the the quantum operator for the annihilation
of one electron with spin
\sigma

p_{z}

\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}
,

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}
being the internal coordinate in the unit cell,
and:

\displaystyle V^{ij}_{C}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon\left|\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}\right|}

(2)

is the Coulomb potential, where
e
is the electron charge and

\epsilon
is the dielectric constant of the environment,

\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
in the vacuum.
Next, we consider the continuum limit of the lattice model,
by expanding the operators

\displaystyle c_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})\equiv A_{c}^{1/2}\left[\psi^{K}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})e^{iK\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}+\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})e^{iK^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\right],

(3)

where

K,K^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{r}
,
and represent the valley projections of
c_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})
.
Replacing the Eq. (
3

1

among all the terms one finds the following valley-exchange interaction:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{exc}=A_{c}^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}}\sum_{ij\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\psi^{K,\dagger}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})\psi^{K^{\prime},\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})\psi^{K}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{R})V^{ij}_{C}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})e^{-i\Delta K\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})},

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{exc}

\displaystyle-J\sum_{i\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\int

It’s worth noting that the interaction described by the Eq. (

not only in the space coordinates, but also in the sub-lattice and layer indices.
Because

This kind of interaction belongs to the universality class identified by

As we already mentioned,
we stress that the spin triplet superconductivity is

On the other hand, the valley-exchange from

Roldán et al. 2013

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J}{\beta}\sum_{j}\int\,d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\times

(6)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{K}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};i\omega_{l}\right)\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};-i\omega_{l}\right)\Delta^{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right),

It’s worth noting that:
i)

\epsilon/\epsilon_{0}=4

eVÅ

Wehling et al. 2011

Such a small value requires a large DOS at the Fermi energy,

for making the dimensionless SC coupling,

While the DOS is suppressed in the monolayer graphene close to charge neutrality,
multilayer stacks of graphene offer a way to increase the value of

and hence to strengthen

like eg the relative twist between the layers, the electronic density, the displacement field etc.;
ii) we are not considering the effects of the intra-valley scattering
induced by the Coulomb interaction at small momenta, which are repulsive.
In a van Hove scenario, these terms are suppressed by the strong internal screening.
As we show in the SI

for the case of the RTG,
the strength of the screened intra-valley Coulomb repulsion is orders of magnitude smaller
than

iii) we are not considering the internal screening of

which is supposed to be negligible as it is induced by the particle-hole excitations

These kind of processes are indeed suppressed despite of a large DOS.
This assumption is justified quantitatively in the SI

,
where we show that the screening essentially does not affect the value of

as compared to its bare value.

IV
Conclusions

Our work represents one step further in the study of
the origin of the superconductivity recently observed in few layers of graphene,
that can be explained by a universal purely electronic mechanism,
in which the valley-exchange induced by the Coulomb repulsion
serves as the paring glue in the valley-singlet/spin-triplet Cooper channel.
The pairing is then favored by the proximity of the Fermi level to the VHS’s,
that can be achieved in these materials by tuning a set of experimental parameters.
Exploiting low energy continuum models,
we provide a general method for computing the SC
critical temperature as well as the symmetry of the OP,
and we test it in three representative systems: TBG, RTG and BBG,
obtaining an excellent agreement with the experimental findings.
Beyond the case of the graphene-based heterostructures,
our analysis might be generalized to study the pairing in
the wider class of materials displaying
valley degrees of freedom and VHS’s,
as is the case of the recently discovered
superconductors on Silicon surface
Nakamura et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Ming et al. 2022
.

\Delta K

to assume that the interaction potential of the Eq. (
S4
) is approximately local:

\displaystyle V^{ij}_{C}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})e^{-i\Delta K\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})}\simeq A_{c}^{-1}v^{ij}_{C}\left(\Delta K\right)\delta_{\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}},

(S5)

where:

\displaystyle v^{ij}_{C}\left(\Delta K\right)\equiv A_{c}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}V^{ij}_{C}(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})e^{-i\Delta K\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime})}.

(S6)

Using:
A_{c}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}\simeq\int\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}
within the previous expression,
one obtains:

\displaystyle v^{ij}_{C}\left(\Delta K\right)\simeq\delta^{ij}J\quad,\quad J\equiv\frac{e^{2}}{2\epsilon|\Delta K|},

(S7)

where the elements in which
i,j
correspond to the same layer
but to different sub-lattices vanish exactly by symmetry (see the App.
A
),
while we neglected the elements in which
i,j
correspond to different layers,
that are suppressed by the factor

e^{-\left|\Delta K\right|d_{ij}}
,

d_{ij}>0
being the interlayer distance.
Using the Eqs. (
S5
) and (
S7
) into the Eq. (
S4
) and
taking the continuum limit finally gives:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{exc}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle J\sum_{i\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\int\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\psi^{K,\dagger}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K^{\prime},\dagger}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{r})=

(S8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{i\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\int\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\psi^{K,\dagger}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K^{\prime},\dagger}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}),

where we have permuted two operators in the second line,
picking up the minus sign.
Because
J>0
, the Eq. (
S8
) provides an attractive interaction
favoring the local occupancy of the two valleys and hence
the Cooper pairing with electrons in opposite valleys.
In order to figure out the spin
structure
of the Cooper pairs
allowed by the interaction of the Eq. (
S8
),
it is convenient to define the following two-fermions operators:

\displaystyle\Psi_{1,-1}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})=\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\quad,\quad\Psi_{1,0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r})+\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})}{\sqrt{2}}\quad,\quad\Psi_{1,1}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})=\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r}),

(S9a)

each of those annihilates a spin triplet Cooper pair, and:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r})-\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\uparrow}(\boldsymbol{r})\psi^{K}_{i\downarrow}(\boldsymbol{r})}{\sqrt{2}},

(S9b)

which in turns annihilates a spin singlet Cooper pair.

Then the Eq. (
S8
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{exc}\simeq-J\sum_{i}\int\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\left[\sum_{s=-1,0,1}\Psi_{1,s}^{i,\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})\Psi_{1,s}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})-\Psi_{0,0}^{i,\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})\Psi_{0,0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})\right],

(S10)

which is attractive in the triplet channel and repulsive in the singlet one,
meaning that only the spin triplet Cooper pairs are stable and,
in addition, they are degenerate.
The SC OP is purely local:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})\propto\left\langle\Psi_{1,s}^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})\right\rangle.

(S11)

Within the BCS theory,
the value of
T_{c}
can be obtained by looking
for nonzero solutions of the linearized gap equation:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{J}{\beta}\sum_{j}\int\,d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{G}^{K}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};i\omega_{l}\right)\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};-i\omega_{l}\right)\Delta^{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right),

(S12)

where
\beta=(K_{B}T)^{-1}
is the inverse of the temperature,

\omega_{l}=\pi(2l+1)/(\hbar\beta)
are fermionic Matsubara frequencies
and
\mathcal{G}^{K,K^{\prime}}
are the Green’s function for the
K,K^{\prime}
valleys,
respectively, computed in the normal phase.
The Eq. (
S12
) is written in real space in order to be as general as possible,
holding also for the systems that are not translationally invariant,
as is the case in the presence of a moiré.
In all the cases preserving the translational invariance, then the OP is uniform:

\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})\equiv\Delta^{i}
, and the Eq. (
S12
)
reduces to the familiar expression:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}=\frac{J}{\beta\Omega}\sum_{j}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{G}^{K}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{q},i\omega_{l}\right)\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(-\boldsymbol{q},-i\omega_{l}\right)\Delta^{j},

(S13)

where
\Omega
is the area of the system.

II
The case of the TBG

We consider the continuum model of the TBG
introduced in the Refs.
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
,
that describes the hopping between the electrons in opposite layers
by means of a local potential having the periodicity of the moiré.
This potential breaks the translational invariance within each moiré unit cell and strongly
hybridizes the
p_{z}

orbitals of the constitutive graphene sheets,
generating a number of weakly dispersive minibands at small twist angles.
The Bloch’s eigenfunction corresponding to the band
E^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}
of the
K
-valley
can be written as:

\displaystyle\Phi^{K}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\phi^{K}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G}),

(S14)

where
\boldsymbol{k}
is the wave-vector in the moiré BZ,
the
\boldsymbol{G}
’s are the vectors of the moiré reciprocal lattice
and the
\phi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G})
’s are numeric eigenvectors normalized according to:

\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}i}\phi^{K,*}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G})\phi^{K}_{i,m,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G})=\delta_{nm}
.
The energies and eigenfunctions corresponding to the
K^{\prime}

valley are related to the ones at
K
by the
\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry:

\displaystyle E^{K^{\prime}}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}=E^{K}_{n,-\boldsymbol{k}}\quad,\quad\Phi^{K^{\prime}}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\Phi^{K,*}_{i,n,-\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(S15)

The Green’s functions appearing in the Eq. (
S12
)
are expressed by their spectral decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{K/K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};i\omega_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\Phi^{K/K^{\prime}}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})\Phi^{{K/K^{\prime}},*}_{j,n,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)}{i\hbar\omega_{l}-\xi^{K/K^{\prime}}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}},

(S16)

where:
\xi^{K/K^{\prime}}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}=E^{K/K^{\prime}}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}-\mu
and
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Using the Eq. (
S15
), we can write
\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}

in terms of the eigenfunctions and energies at
K
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};i\omega_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\Phi^{K,*}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})\Phi^{K}_{j,n,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)}{i\hbar\omega_{l}-\xi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}}.

(S17)

Because:

\mathcal{G}^{K/K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime};i\omega_{l}\right)=\mathcal{G}^{K/K^{\prime}}_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{R}_{m},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{R}_{m};i\omega_{l}\right)

for any moiré lattice vector
\boldsymbol{R}_{m}
, the solution of the linearized gap equation
(
S12
) is periodic in the moiré unit cell:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}\Delta^{i}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}.

(S18)

This implies that the Eq. (
S12
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}mn}\frac{f\left(-\xi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}\right)-f\left(\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\right)}{\xi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}+\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}}\Phi^{K}_{i,m,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})\Phi^{K,*}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})\int\,d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\sum_{j}\Phi^{K,*}_{j,m,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\Phi^{K}_{j,n,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\Delta^{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right),

(S19)

where we have performed the Matsubara sum
and introduced the Fermi distribution:
f(x)=\left(e^{\beta x}+1\right)^{-1}
.
We can safely assume that the terms with

m\neq n
barely contribute to the rhs of the Eq. (
S19
),
and write:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle J\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}m}\frac{\tanh\left(\beta\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}/2\right)}{2\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}}\left|\Phi^{K}_{i,m,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2}\int\,d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\sum_{j}\left|\Phi^{K}_{j,m,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right|^{2}\Delta^{j}\left(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}\right).

(S20)

The Eq. (
S20
)
is equivalent to the following equation for the Fourier amplitudes,

\Delta^{i}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)
’s:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)=\sum_{j\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\Gamma^{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}\right)\Delta^{j}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}\right),

(S21)

where:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}\right)\equiv\frac{J}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}m}\frac{\tanh\left(\beta\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}/2\right)}{2\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}}W^{i}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)W^{j,*}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}\right),

(S22)

and:

\displaystyle W^{i}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}\phi^{K,*}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}\right)\phi^{K}_{i,n,\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}\right).

(S23)

The critical temperature,
T_{c}
,
is obtained as the value of
T
such that the largest eigenvalue of
the hermitian kernel
\Gamma
is equal to
1
.
The Fourier transform of the corresponding eigenvector,
\Delta^{i}\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)
,
provides the profile of the SC OP in the real space,
\Delta^{i}(\boldsymbol{r})
.
Because we consider values of the electronic density close to the CNP,
we take into account only the contribution of the two central bands
to the Eq. (
S21
).

shows the values of
T_{c}

\nu
,
obtained for:

, which is close to a magic angle,
and
\epsilon/\epsilon_{0}=4,6,10
, as coded in the inset panel.

IV
Screening of the Coulomb interaction

Here we estimate the screening of the Coulomb interaction by the particle-hole excitations
within the Random Phase Approximation (RPA).
We consider in particular the case of the RTG, which is translationally invariant,
but the arguments can be generalized to the other systems.
We consider both the long- and the short-range parts of the interaction.

The bare long-range interaction is described by the potential:

\displaystyle v_{LR}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{e^{2}}{2\epsilon|\boldsymbol{q}|}\text{ , for }\boldsymbol{q}\simeq 0.

(S29)

Note that
v_{LR}
does not depend on the internal indices of sub-lattice/layer, since the long-range interaction is an effective charge-charge repulsion.
The screening is given by the usual Thomas-Fermi formula:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{LR}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{v^{-1}_{LR}(\boldsymbol{q})-\Pi(\boldsymbol{q})}\simeq-\frac{1}{\Pi(0)},

(S30)

where
\Pi(\boldsymbol{q})
is the charge susceptivity that, at
\boldsymbol{q}=0
, can be written as:

\displaystyle\Pi(0)=\frac{4}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}nm}\left|\boldsymbol{u}^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}\right|^{2}\frac{f\left(\xi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}\right)-f\left(\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}\right)}{\xi^{K}_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}-\xi^{K}_{m,\boldsymbol{k}}},

(S31)

where the factor of
4
comes from the spin/valley degeneracy.
The leading contribution to
\Pi(0)
is the intra-band contribution corresponding to the band crossing the Fermi level,
\bar{n}
,
so we can write:

\displaystyle\Pi(0)\simeq\frac{4}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}f^{\prime}\left(\xi^{K}_{\bar{n},\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\begin{matrix}\\
\to\\
T\to 0\end{matrix}-N_{F}.

(S32)

If the Fermi level matches the VHS, then
N_{F}\to\infty
and hence
\tilde{v}_{LR}(\boldsymbol{q})\to 0
.

To derive the screening of the short-range interaction, namely of
J
,
it is convenient to consider the Hamiltonian of the Eq. (
S8
)
and write the corresponding euclidean action:

\displaystyle S_{exc}=J\sum_{i\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\int\,dx\psi^{K,\dagger}_{i\sigma}(x)\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(x)\psi^{K^{\prime},\dagger}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(x)\psi^{K}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}(x),

(S33)

where
x=(\boldsymbol{r},\tau)
and
\tau
is the imaginary time.
When computing the partition function,

S_{exc}
can be decoupled by introducing a set of complex Hubbard-Stratonovich (HS) fields,

\Phi_{i}
, according to:

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{exc}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}\left[\Phi_{i},\Phi^{*}_{i}\right]\exp\left\{-\frac{\hbar}{J}\sum_{i}\int\,dx\left|\Phi_{i}(x)\right|^{2}+i\sum_{i\sigma}\int\,dx\left[\bar{\psi}^{K}_{i\sigma}(x)\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(x)\Phi_{i}(x)+c.c.\right]\right\}=

(S34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}\left[\Phi_{i},\Phi^{*}_{i}\right]\exp\left\{\frac{\hbar}{J}\sum_{iq}\left|\Phi_{i}(q)\right|^{2}+i\sqrt{\frac{K_{B}T}{\hbar\Omega}}\sum_{i\sigma kk^{\prime}}\left[\bar{\psi}^{K}_{i\sigma}(k)\psi^{K^{\prime}}_{i\sigma}(k^{\prime})\Phi_{i}(k-k^{\prime})+c.c.\right]\right\},

where

\psi,\bar{\psi}
are grassmanian numbers,

q=\left(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{l}\right)
,
\Omega_{l}=2\pi lK_{B}T/\hbar
are bosonic Matsubara frequencies,
and
k=\left(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{l}\right)
.
The Eq. (
S34
) defines the following self-energy:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{ij}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k-k^{\prime}\right)\equiv-i\delta_{ij}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{K_{B}T}{\hbar\Omega}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\Phi_{i}\left(k-k^{\prime}\right)\\
\Phi^{*}_{i}\left(k^{\prime}-k\right)&0\end{pmatrix},

(S35)

where the
2\times 2
matrix acts in the valley space.
Integrating out the Fermionic fields, we obtain the effective action for the HS fields that, at the gaussian level,
is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{eff}\left[\Phi,\Phi^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{J}\sum_{iq}\left|\Phi_{i}(q)\right|^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\sum_{kq}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{G}(k+q)\Sigma(q)\mathcal{G}(k)\Sigma(-q)\right\}=

(S36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\sum_{ijq}\tilde{J}^{-1}_{ij}(q)\Phi^{*}_{i}(q)\Phi_{j}(q),

where:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{-1}_{ij}(q)\equiv\frac{\delta_{ij}}{J}-2\frac{K_{B}T}{\Omega}\sum_{k}\mathcal{G}^{K}_{ij}(k+q)\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}_{ji}(k)

(S37)

is the inverse of the screened
J
. Note that
\tilde{J}
is a matrix in the sub-lattice/layer indices.
This is not surprising, since
J
is a matrix too, but proportional to the identity.
We focus on the
q=0
limit. Using the spectral decomposition of the Green’s functions
\mathcal{G}^{K},\mathcal{G}^{K^{\prime}}

and performing the Matsubara sums,
we finally obtain:
