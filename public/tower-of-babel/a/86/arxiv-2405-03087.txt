1.
Introduction

1.1.
Packing problem in Euclidean space

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a metric space of maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.

For Borel sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{A}
, we define

\displaystyle\Lambda(E):=\bigcup_{f\in\Lambda}f(E)=\{f(x):x\in E,f\in\Lambda\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The packing problem in this setting can be formulated as follows: Is it possible for a set of zero
d-
dimensional Lebesgue measure to contain an image
f(E)
of
E
for every
f\in\Lambda
?

The study of the packing problem has a long history, see for example
[
14
]
,
[
31
]
, and
[
23
]
. For the planar case, lines and circles are special cases of curve-packing problems.
If we consider
E
as a line segment in the plane, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of all rigid motions, then the above problem dates back to the works of Besicovitch
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
in the 1920s. In these papers, he constructed a set named after him which has zero Lebesgue measure and contains a line segment of unit length in every direction. Now let
E=S^{1}
, the unit circle, let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and define for each
\gamma=(z,r)\in\mathcal{A}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\gamma(x)=rx+z.

Then
\Lambda(S^{1})
is the union of all circles centered at
z
, of radius
r
, for
(z,r)\in\Lambda
. In 1968, Besicovitch and Rado
[
3
]
, and Kinney
[
25
]
showed that there exists a set in the plane of Lebesgue measure zero containing a circle of radius
r
for each
r>0
(see also Davies
[
9
]
). Later, in 1980, Talagrand
[
44
]
proved that there exists a set of measure zero containing a circle centered at
x
, for all
x
on a given straight line.
Thus it is natural to ask which conditions will guarantee that the union of lines (circles) has positive measure.

In this paper, we will study the following question, which generalizes the question for lines and circles above: Under which conditions on the dimensions of
E
and
\Lambda
, the Lebesgue measure of
\Lambda(E)
is positive? Note that when this holds, we certainly can not pack
\Lambda(E)
into any set of zero Lebesgue measures. Similarly, we can ask: Given a constant
0<u\leq d
, how large do the Hausdorff dimensions
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E
and
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Lambda
need to be to ensure that

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Lambda(E)\geq u

holds?

For circles in the plane, in 1985, Bourgain
[
4
]
, and independently Marstrand
[
28
]
, demonstrated that if the centers form a set of positive Lebesgue measure, then the union of the circles must have positive Lebesgue measure, which answer a question by Falconer
[
14
]
(see also Bourgain
[
5
]
). Earlier Stein
[
43
]
, as a consequence of his work on the spherical means maximal operator, proved that the same conclusion holds true for spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 3
. The case
d=2
turned out to be much more difficult.
Since the spheres have dimension
d-1
, we can expect that it is enough for the centers of the spheres to form a set of Hausdorff dimension bigger than one to give a positive result. This was shown by Mitsis
[
34
]
for
d\geq 3
and by Wolff
[
49
]
for
d=2
(see also D. Oberlin
[
35
]
). In a paper published in 1997, Wolff
[
47
]
also showed that if the set of centers has Hausdorff dimension
s
,
0<s\leq 1
, the corresponding union of circles has dimension of at least
1+s
. For results in higher dimensions, see D. Oberlin
[
36
]
.

We can get similar results by replacing the circle in the plane with rather general smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
E
is a nondegenerate (i.e., derivatives span the whole space) curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, Ham, Ko, Lee, and Oh
[
17
]
showed that if the set of translations and dilations has dimension
>\alpha
, for
0<\alpha\leq d-1
, then the union of all curves has dimension
\geq\alpha+1
. For results in the plane, see
[
22
]
. Simon and Taylor

[
41
]
,
[
42
]
studied properties of
A+\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a planar curve with at least one point of non-vanishing curvature. For general results on the packing of curves, surfaces, and manifolds, see for example Falconer
[
13
]
, Wisewell
[
46
]
.

Turning to the case of affine hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the Grassmanian manifold of all affine hyperplanes by
\mathcal{G}(d,d-1)
.
Then if the set of hyperplanes has Hausdorff dimension larger than
1
, the union of these hyperplanes has a positive Lebesgue measure. In fact, we can say more, if
0<s<1
, and the dimension of the set of hyperplanes is
s
,
then the union of hyperplanes will have dimension at least
d-1+s
. These results are due to D. Oberlin
[
36
]
. Later, in
[
37
]
, he generalized to affine
k
-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More precisely, he showed that if the set of affine
k
-planes has Hausdorff dimension
\alpha>(k+1)(d-k)-k
, then the union of corresponding
k
-planes has a positive Lebesgue measure. The result is sharp, in the sense that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set of
k
-planes of dimension
(k+1)(d-k)-k-\varepsilon
such that the union of these
k-
planes has zero Lebesgue measure. In 2016, R. Oberlin
[
38
]
conjectured that if a set of lines
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has dimension
\geq 2(k-1)+\beta
, with integer
1\leq k\leq d
, then the union of these lines has dimension at least
k+\beta
. Recently, this conjecture was proved by Zahl
[
50
]
. For the variant of this problem when
L
is replaced by a set of
k-
dimensional affine subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see
[
15
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
19
]
, and
[
16
]
. For results on packing skeletons of polytopes, see
[
24
]
,
[
45
]
, and
[
7
]
.

In this paper, instead of limiting the study to certain smooth objects, we will consider the packing problem for general Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, we are interested in packing problems in the case where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of dilations and translations; rigid transformations; or similarity transformations.

Similar questions for rigid motions in finite field settings have also been studied in
[
39
]
.

1.2.
Packing sets by dilations and translations

In this section, we will discuss the results of packing problems by using dilations and translations.

Let
d\geq 1
, we denote
\Gamma(d)=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
as the set of all translations and dilations. For each
\gamma=(z,r)\in\Gamma(d)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\gamma(x)=rx+z
. For
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Gamma\subset\Gamma(d)
, set

\displaystyle\Gamma(E)=\bigcup_{\gamma=(z,r)\in\Gamma}(rE+z)=\{\gamma(x):\gamma\in\Gamma,x\in E\}.

In other words,
\Gamma(E)
is the union of all dilated and translated copies
\gamma(E)
of
E
, with
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
If
\Gamma
is of the form
Z\times T
, where
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, by denoting
TE=\{rx:x\in E,r\in T\}
, we can write

\displaystyle\Gamma(E)=TE+Z.

We restate the packing problem using dilations and translations as follows: For Borel sets
E
and
\Gamma
, can a set of zero
d-
dimensional Lebesgure measure contains dilated and translated copies
\gamma(E)=rE+z
of
E
for each element
\gamma=(z,r)\in\Gamma
?

Recently, by assuming that the set of dilations and translations has product form
Z\times T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
Hambrook and Taylor
[
18
]

proved the following theorem. Throughout this paper,
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}
denotes the Fourier dimension, see section
2
for details.

Theorem A
.

[
18
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
E,Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, be non-empty compact sets. Let

\delta=\max\{\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(TE)+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z,\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(TE)+\dim_{\mathcal{F}}Z\}.

(i)

If
\delta>d
, then
\mathcal{L}^{d}(TE+Z)>0
.

(ii)

If
\delta\leq d
, then
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(TE+Z)\geq\delta
.

Remark 1.1
.

Theorem
A
is a variant of some classical results for smooth objects. For example, when
E
is the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this theorem recovers results of Wolff
[
47
]
,
[
49
]
and D. Oberlin
[
35
]
in the case the set of centers and radii has product structure, which we mentioned in the introduction. However, Theorem
A
is still not a complete generalization, since the much deeper results
by Wolff and D. Oberlin take the union of spheres over an arbitrary set
\Gamma
of translations and dilations, while Theorem
A
takes the union of spheres over the Cartesian product set
\Gamma=Z\times T
(see also the discussion in
[
18
]
).

Theorem
A
holds in the more general form as follows.
Throughout this paper,
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the Sobolev dimension. We say that a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has Sobolev dimension
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}A\geq s
if
A
carries a Borel probability measure
\mu
such that

\displaystyle\int|\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}(1+|x|)^{s-d}dx<\infty.

Theorem B
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Borel sets. Then

(i)

\dim_{\mathcal{S}}(A+B)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{F}}A+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}B.

(ii)

If
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}A+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}B>d
, then
\mathcal{L}^{d}(A+B)>0.

(iii)

If for
0<u<d
,
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}A+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}B>u
, then
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(A+B)\geq u.

The proof of Theorem
B
is as in
[
18
]
(see Section
2
for details).

While Theorem
A
is sharp (for example, take
E=\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T=\{1\}
and
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\dim_{H}Z=d
, while
\mathcal{L}^{d}(Z)=0
), the role of the Hausdorff dimension of
E
in this problem is not provided. Additionally, finding lower bounds for
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(TE)
in Theorem
A
is important.

Our first main result is the following, which answers the above questions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
E,Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be Borel sets, and put
\Gamma=Z\times T
. Then we have the following:

(i)

\dim_{\mathcal{F}}TE\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T-d
, and

\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\Gamma(E)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T-d.

(ii)

If

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T>2d
,
then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Gamma(E))>0.

(iii)

For
0<u<d
, if

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T>d+u
,
then

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma(E)\geq u.

Remark 1.2
.

(i)

The bounds in Theorem
1.1

(i)
, and
(ii)
are sharp.
Indeed, for part
(ii)
, one can take
E=[0,1]^{d}
,
T=\{0\}
,
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z=d
, and
\mathcal{L}^{d}(Z)=0
. Then
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T=2d
, while
\mathcal{L}^{d}(TE+Z)=\mathcal{L}^{d}(Z)=0
. The sharpness of part
(i)
follows by the sharpness of part
(i)
. For other examples, see
6
.

(ii)

When
d=1
, Theorem
1.1
is the result of Bourgain
[
6
, Theorem 7]
. Also, when
d=1
, part
(ii)
follows from Falconer’s exceptional estimate for projections
[
13
]
and part
(iii)
from a recent result of Ren and Wang
[
40
]
.

To emphasize the importance of the estimates for lower bounds of the Fourier dimension, we give an immediate corollary of Theorem
1.1
to the
k
-fold sum-product set without proof, see
[
31
, Proposition 3.14]
. Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote
A^{(k)}=\underbrace{A+\cdots+A}_{k}
as the
k-
fold sum-set of
A
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
k\geq 1
. Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be Borel sets.

(i)

If
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T>d\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{d}\left((TE)^{(k)}\right)=\mathcal{L}^{d}(TE+\dots+TE)>0.

(ii)

For
0<u<d
, if
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T>d+\frac{u}{k}
, then

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\left((TE)^{(k)}\right)\geq u.

(iii)

If
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}T>d\left(1+\frac{2}{k}\right)
, then
(TE)^{k}
has non empty interior.

Remark 1.3
.

(i)

For
d\geq 1
, we can find set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E=d
,
\mathcal{L}^{d}(E)=0
, while
E^{(k)}=E+\cdots+E
has
\mathcal{L}^{d}(E^{(k)})=0
, for all
k\geq 1
, see Section
6
for details.

(ii)

When
d=1
,
(i),(ii)
follow from a result of Erdoğan, Hart, and Iosevich
[
12
]
.

We also obtain a similar result when
\Gamma
is a general set in
\Gamma(d)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Gamma\subset\Gamma(d)
be Borel sets. We have the following:

(i)

\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\Gamma(E)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma-d.

(ii)

If

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma>2d
,
then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Gamma(E))>0.

(iii)

For
0<u<d
, if

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma>d+u
,
then

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma(E)\geq u.

Remark 1.4
.

(i)

The bounds in Theorem
1.2

(i)
,
(ii)
are sharp by the examples in Remark
1.2
.

(ii)

Theorem
1.2
generalizes Theorem
1.1
, which is restricted to the case
\Gamma
has product structure.

More generally, we also consider the packing problem by multi-parameter dilations and translations as follows. Denote
\tilde{\Gamma}(d)=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
as the set of all multi-parameter dilations and translations. For each
\tilde{\gamma}=(z,r)=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d},r_{1},\dots,r_{d})\in\tilde{\Gamma}(d)
, and for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(x)=(r_{1}x_{1},\dots,r_{d}x_{d})+(z_{1},\dots,z_{d}).

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\tilde{\Gamma}(d)
be Borel sets, set

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(E)=\{\tilde{\gamma}(x):x\in E,\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}\}.

Our next main result is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\tilde{\Gamma}(d)
be Borel sets. We have the following:

(i)

\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\tilde{\Gamma}(E)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{\Gamma}-2d+1.

(ii)

If

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{\Gamma}>3d-1
,
then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(\tilde{\Gamma}(E))>0.

(iii)

For
0<u<d
, if

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{\Gamma}>2d-1+u
,
then

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{\Gamma}(E)\geq u.

Remark 1.5
.

The bounds in Theorem
1.3

(i)
,
(ii)
are sharp. See examples in Section
6
.

1.3.
Packing sets by rigid motions

We shall now investigate the packing problem in Euclidean space by using another class of affine transformations, the set of all rigid motions.

Let
d\geq 2
, we denote
E(d)=O(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the Euclidean group or the set of all rigid motions. For each
\theta=(g,z)\in E(d)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\theta(x)=g(x)+z
. Let
\Theta\subset E(d)
and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle\Theta(E)=\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\theta(E)=\{g(x)+z:(g,z)\in\Theta,x\in E\}.

When
\Theta=G\times Z
, where
G\subset O(d)
and
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote

\displaystyle GE=\{g(x):x\in E,g\in G\}\,\,\text{, and }\,\,GE+Z=\{y+z:y\in GE,z\in Z\}.

From definition,
\Theta(E)
is the union of all rotated and translated copies
\theta(E)=g(E)+z
of
E
, for
\theta=(g,z)\in\Theta
. We will find conditions on the dimensions of
E
and
\Theta
to ensure that the set containing all rotated and translated copies
\theta(E)=g(E)+z
of
E
for each element
\theta=(g,z)\in\Theta
has positive Lebesgue measure.

Before stating the theorem, we will recall the definition of
spherical Fourier dimension
(see
[
29
]
). For a given Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the spherical Fourier dimension of
E
, denoted by
\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E
, is defined by

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E=\sup\{\alpha\leq d:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)\text{ such that }\sigma(\mu)(r)\lesssim r^{-\alpha}\text{ for all }r>0\},

where
\sigma(\mu)(r)
is the
L^{2}
spherical average of the Fourier transform of
\mu
, see (
2.3
).
We have the following lemma, see Section
2
for more details.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. Then one has
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E\geq\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E\geq\dim_{\mathcal{F}}E.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E,\quad\text{ if }\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E\leq(d-1)/2,

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d-1}{d}\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E.

The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
E,Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
G\subset O(d)
be Borel sets such that
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G>\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}
. Then we have the following:

(i)

\dim_{\mathcal{F}}(GE)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G-\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}
,
and

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{S}}(GE+Z)\geq\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z-\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}.

(ii)

If

\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z>\frac{d^{2}+d}{2}
,
then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(GE+Z)>0.

(iii)

For
0<u<d
, if

\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z>\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}+u,

then

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(GE+Z)\geq u.

Recalling Lemma
1.1
we have

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
E,Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
G\subset O(d)
be Borel sets such that
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G>\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}
. Then we have the following:

(i)

If

\frac{d-1}{d}\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z>\frac{d^{2}+d}{2}
,
then

\mathcal{L}^{d}(GE+Z)>0.

(ii)

For
0<u<d
, if

\frac{d-1}{d}\dim_{\mathcal{H}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}G+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z>\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}+u,

then

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(GE+Z)\geq u.

Remark 1.6
.

(i)

The conditions in Theorem
1.4

(i)
,
(ii)
, and Corollary
1.2

(i)
are generally sharp. The sharpness examples are given in Section
6
.

(ii)

Corollary
1.2
also follows from
[
33
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Based on the above theorem, it is plausible to make the following conjecture for packing arbitrary Borel sets in Euclidean space using rigid transformations.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Theta\subset E(d)
be Borel sets. If

\dim_{\mathcal{SF}}E+\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Theta>\frac{d^{2}+d}{2},\quad\text{then}\quad\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Theta(E))>0.

1.4.
Packing sets by similarity transformations

Instead of using rigid motions, we can extend the study of the packing problems to the class of similarity transformations.

Let
d\geq 2
, for each
\omega=(g,z,r)\in\Omega(d)=O(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the set of similarity transformations, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\omega(x)=rg(x)+z
. Let
\Omega\subset\Omega(d)
and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set

\displaystyle\Omega(E):=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}\omega(E)=\{rg(x)+z:(g,z,r)\in\Omega,x\in E\}.

When
\Omega=G\times Z\times T
, where
G\subset O(d)
,
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
T\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we denote

\displaystyle GTE=\{rg(x):(g,r)\in G\times T,x\in E\},\,\,\text{ and }\,\,GTE+Z=\{y+z:y\in GTE,z\in Z\}.

Observe that
\Omega(E)
is the union of all dilated, rotated, and translated copies
\omega(E)
of
E
, for each
\omega\in\Omega
.

Similar to the packing problem using rigid motions, we ask: Under which conditions of the dimensions of
E
and
\Omega
, we can not pack
\Omega(E)
into a set of zero Lebesgue measure in

The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 1.5

Let

2.1.
Frostman’s lemma, dimensions of sets, ball averages, and spherical averages

Lemma 2.1

(Frostman’s lemma, Theorem 2.7,
[
31
]
)
.

Let
0\leq s\leq d
.
For a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
s-
dimensional Hausdorff measure of
E
is positive if and only if there exists a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,t))\lesssim r^{s},\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\,r>0.

In particular, Frostman’s lemma implies that given any exponent
0<s<\dim_{\mathcal{H}}(E)
, there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
E
such that

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,t))\lesssim r^{s},\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},r>0.

(2.1)

A measure satisfying condition (
2.1
) is often called an
s
-dimensional Frostman measure (or
s
-Frostman measure).
The
s
-energy integral of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
[
30
,
31
]
)
is

\displaystyle I_{s}(\mu)=\iint|x-y|^{-s}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=c(n,s)\int|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{s-d}d\xi.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Frostman condition
(
2.1
)
, then
I_{t}(\mu)<\infty
for all
0<t<s
.

We have for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}A>0
, (see Theorem 8.9 in
[
30
]
),

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{H}}A

\displaystyle=\sup\{s\leq d:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\text{ such that }\mu(B(x,r))\leq r^{s}\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},r>0\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{s\leq d:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\text{ such that }I_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}.

The Fourier dimension of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{F}}A=\sup\{s\leq d:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\text{ such that }|\widehat{\mu}(x)|\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-\frac{s}{2}},\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

The Sobolev dimension of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\int|\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}(1+|x|)^{s-d}dx<\infty\}.

We will say that a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has Sobolev dimension
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}A\geq s
if
A
carries a Borel probability measure
\mu
such that
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu\geq s
. The greater the Sobolev dimension is, the smoother the measure is in some sense. We recall the following well-known result, see
[
31
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
31
, Theorem 5.4]
)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(i)

If
0<\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu<d
, then
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu=\sup\{s>0:I_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}
.

(ii)

If
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu>d
, then
\mu\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(iii)

If
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}\mu>2d
, then
\mu
is a continuous function.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an
s
-Frostman measure. We
have the following ball average estimate (see
[
31
, Section 3.8]
),

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,R)}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\lesssim R^{d-s},\quad R>0.

(2.2)

Next, given a Radon measure
\mu
with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, we define the
L^{2}
spherical averages of the Fourier transform of
\mu
by

\displaystyle\sigma(\mu)(r)=\int_{S^{d-1}}|\widehat{\mu}(rv)|^{2}d\sigma(v),\quad r>0,

(2.3)

where
\sigma
is the surface measure on the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
. In
[
29
]
, Mattila developed a method to study Falconer’s distance problem by studying the decay rates of spherical averages of fractal measures, namely, the supremum of the numbers
\beta
for which for all
r>1
, one has

\displaystyle\sigma(\mu)(r)\lesssim r^{-\beta}.

(2.4)

For any
s
-Frostman measure
\mu
we have for all
\varepsilon>0
,
r>1
,

\sigma(\mu)(r)\lesssim\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}r^{-s+\varepsilon},&s\in\big(0,\frac{d-1}{2}\big],\\
r^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+\varepsilon},&s\in\big[\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d}{2}\big],\\
r^{-\frac{(d-1)s}{d}+\varepsilon},&s\in(0,d).\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

The first two estimates were proved in
[
29
]
. The last estimate is the deepest. It is due to Wolff
[
48
]
for
d=2
, to Du-Guth-Ou-Wang-Wilson-Zhang
[
10
]
for
d=3,3/2<s\leq 2
, and to Du-Zhang
[
11
]
for
d=3,2\leq s<3
, and
d\geq 4
. The first estimate is always sharp and for
d=2
they all are sharp.

The lower bounds for the spherical Fourier dimension in Lemma
1.1
follow from the best known decay of spherical averages (
2.5
).

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\textrm{spt }\zeta\subset[1,2]
.
Let
\phi
be a smooth compactly supported function such that
\phi\geq 0

on
\textrm{spt }\mu

\widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\phi\mu}=\widehat{\phi}\ast\widehat{\mu}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\gamma,\zeta}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\iint|\widehat{\phi\mu}(rg^{-1}(\xi))|^{2}\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r)

\displaystyle=\iint\bigg|\int\widehat{\phi}(rg^{-1}(\xi)-x)\widehat{\mu}(x)\,dx\bigg|^{2}\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r).

By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fast decay property of
\widehat{\phi}
, the integral is dominated by

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint\bigg(\int|\widehat{\phi}(rg^{-1}(\xi)-x)|dx\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\int|\widehat{\phi}(rg^{-1}(\xi)-x)||\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}\,dx\bigg)\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r)

\displaystyle\lesssim\iiint_{\{|rg^{-1}(\xi)-x|\leq|\xi|^{\varepsilon}\}}|\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r)\,dx

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\iiint_{\{|\xi|^{\varepsilon j}\leq|rg^{-1}(\xi)-x|\leq|\xi|^{\varepsilon(j+1)}\}}|\widehat{\phi}(rg^{-1}(\xi)-x)||\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r)\,dx.

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\iiint_{\{|\xi|^{\varepsilon j}\leq|rg^{-1}(\xi)-x|\leq|\xi|^{\varepsilon(j+1)}\}}|\xi|^{-N\varepsilon j}\,d\gamma(g)\,d\zeta(r)|\widehat{\mu}(x)|^{2}\,dx

4.
Proofs of Theorems
1.2
and

In this section, we will give proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. First, we will recall a result of D. Oberlin
[
37

on the exceptional estimate for projections.

Let
0\leq l\leq n
be integers. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{l(n-l)}
, we define the projection
P_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
by

\displaystyle P_{x}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{l})+X\cdot(y_{l+1},\dots,y_{n})^{T}=\tilde{y}+X\cdot\bar{y}.

Here
X=(x_{ij})\in M_{l\times(n-l)}
is the matrix identified by

and
\bar{y}=(y_{l+1},\dots,y_{n})
.

With
x
and

as above, for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{l}

T_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-l}
by

\displaystyle T_{x}(\xi)=X^{T}\cdot\xi^{T}.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
37
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a compactly supported nonnegative Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{l(n-l)}
which satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\lambda(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{l(n-l)}:|T_{x}(\xi)-p|\leq\delta\})\leq c\delta^{\beta},

(4.1)

for some
c>0
and all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
with
|\xi|=1
,
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n-l}
, and
\delta>0
. Suppose
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Borel set with Hausdorff dimension at least
\alpha
. Suppose

\displaystyle n-l+\sigma-\alpha<\beta.

(4.2)

Then for
\lambda
-almost all

,
\dim_{\mathcal{S}}P_{x}(E)\geq\sigma
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We will give the proof for part
(i)

and

and Proposition
2.1
.

Let

We will define a family of projections as follows. Put

, and

5.
Union of sets by rigid motions over finite fields

In this section, we will give proof of Theorem
1.6
in dimension two. In higher dimensions, the proofs of Theorem
1.7
and Theorem
1.8
follow by using the same argument and the fact that the stabilizer of a non-zero element in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is about
|O(d-1)|
. We begin by reviewing some basic background on Fourier analysis over finite fields.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
be the
d-
dimensional vector space over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
elements, where
q
is an odd prime power. The Fourier transform of a complex-valued function
f
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with respect to a nontrivial principal additive character
\chi
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(m)=q^{-d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}\chi(-x\cdot m)f(x),

and the Fourier inversion formula takes the form

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}\chi(x\cdot m)\widehat{f}(m).

We also have the Plancherel theorem

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(m)|^{2}=\frac{1}{q^{d}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2},

which can be proved easily by using the following orthogonal property of the canonical additive character

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}\chi(m\cdot x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\neq(0,\dots,0),\\
q^{d}&\text{ if }x=(0,\dots,0).\end{cases}

We will identify the set
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with the characteristic function on the set
E
, and we denote by
|E|
the cardinality of the set
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.

For
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, define

M^{*}(E)=\max_{j\neq 0}\sum_{m\in S_{j}}|\widehat{E}(m)|^{2},~\mbox{and}~M(E)=\max_{j\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{m\in S_{j}}|\widehat{E}(m)|^{2}.

By Plancherel, it is trivial that

M(E),M^{*}(E)\leq\frac{|E|}{q^{d}}.

We recall the following improvements from
[
8
]
and
[
26
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. We have

(1)

If
d=2
, then
M^{*}(E)\ll q^{-3}|E|^{3/2}
.

(2)

If
d\geq 4
even, then
M^{*}(E)\ll\min\left\{\frac{|E|}{q^{d}},~\frac{|E|}{q^{d+1}}+\frac{|E|^{2}}{q^{\frac{3d+1}{2}}}\right\}
.

(3)

If
d\geq 3
odd, then
M(E)\ll\min\left\{\frac{|E|}{q^{d}},~\frac{|E|}{q^{d+1}}+\frac{|E|^{2}}{q^{\frac{3d+1}{2}}}\right\}
.

In some specific dimensions, the same restriction estimate holds for the sphere of radius zero. A detailed proof can be found in
[
21
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Assume
d\equiv 2\mod{4}
and
q\equiv 3\mod{4}
, then we have

\sum_{m\in S_{0}}|\widehat{E}(m)|^{2}\ll\frac{|E|}{q^{d+1}}+\frac{|E|^{2}}{q^{\frac{3d+2}{2}}}.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.6
.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Let
\lambda_{\Theta}
be a function defined by

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}}f(y)\lambda_{\Theta}(y):=\sum_{x\in E}\sum_{(g,z)\in\Theta}f(gx+z)\Theta(g,z),

for all functions
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
.

Let
f
be the characteristic function of the set
\Theta(E)
. Then we have

\sum_{x}f(x)\lambda_{\Theta}(x)=|E||\Theta|.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one has

|\Theta(E)|\geq\frac{|E|^{2}|\Theta|^{2}}{\sum_{x}\lambda_{\Theta}(x)^{2}}.

In the next step, we are going to bound
\sum_{x}\lambda_{\Theta}(x)^{2}
from above. It suffices to prove that

\sum_{x}\lambda_{\Theta}(x)^{2}\ll\frac{|\Theta|^{2}|E|^{2}}{q^{2}}+q|E|^{3/2}|\Theta|.

We have

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda_{\Theta}}(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{g,z,x}\chi(-m\cdot(gx+z))E(x)\Theta(g,z)=\sum_{g,z}\widehat{E}(g^{-1}m)\chi(-m\cdot z)\Theta(g,z)

\displaystyle=q^{2}\sum_{g}\widehat{E}(g^{-1}m)\left(\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{z}\chi(-m\cdot z)\Theta(g,z)\right)=q^{2}\sum_{g}\widehat{E}(g^{-1}m)f_{g}(m),

where

f_{g}(m)=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{z}\chi(-m\cdot z)\Theta(g,z).

For
m=(0,0)
, a direct computation shows that
\widehat{\lambda_{\Theta}}(0,0)=\frac{|E||\Theta|}{q^{2}}
.

For
m\neq(0,0)
, we can apply Cauchy-Schwarz to get

\displaystyle|\widehat{\lambda_{\Theta}}(m)|^{2}\leq q^{4}\sum_{g}|\widehat{E}(gm)|^{2}\sum_{g}|f_{g}(m)|^{2}.

Note that
||m||\neq 0
when
m\neq(0,0)
since
q\equiv 3\mod 4
. In the plane
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, the stabilizer of a non-zero element is of size at most
2
. Thus, given
m
with
||m||=j\neq 0
, by Theorem
5.1
, we have

\sum_{g}|\widehat{E}(gm)|^{2}\ll M^{*}(E)\ll|E|^{3/2}q^{-3}.

So,

|\widehat{\lambda_{\Theta}}(m)|^{2}\ll q^{4}\cdot\frac{|E|^{3/2}}{q^{3}}\cdot\sum_{g}|f_{g}(m)|^{2},

which is bounded further by

\frac{|E|^{3/2}}{q^{3}}\sum_{\theta}\sum_{u,v}\chi((u-v)\cdot m)\Theta(g,u)\Theta(g,v).

Taking the sum over all
m
and use the orthogonality of
\chi
, one has

\sum_{m}|\widehat{\lambda_{\Theta}}(m)|^{2}\ll\frac{|\Theta|^{2}|E|^{2}}{q^{4}}+\frac{|E|^{3/2}|\Theta|}{q}.

Using the fact that

\sum_{x}\lambda_{\Theta}(x)^{2}=q^{2}\sum_{m}|\lambda_{\Theta}(m)|^{2},

we obtain

\sum_{x}\lambda_{\Theta}(x)^{2}\ll\frac{|\Theta|^{2}|E|^{2}}{q^{2}}+q|E|^{3/2}|\Theta|.

This completes the proof of the theorem.

6.

In this section, we will give some examples regarding our results for packing problems using affine transformations.

In the first example, we want to emphasize that our results recover the known results for spheres in a special case.

.

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathcal{H}}Z>1

[

]
,
[
49
]
and Oberlin
[
35
]
in the case spheres with a fixed given radius.

Example 6.2
.

.
