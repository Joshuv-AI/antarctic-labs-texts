Prediction Errors for Penalized Regressions based on Generalized Approximate Message Passing
Ayaka Sakata

Abstract
We discuss the prediction accuracy of assumed statistical models in terms of prediction errors for the generalized linear model and penalized maximum likelihood methods. We derive the forms of estimators for the prediction errors, such as C p C_{p} criterion, information criteria, and leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) error, using the generalized approximate message passing (GAMP) algorithm and replica method. These estimators coincide with each other when the number of model parameters is sufficiently small; however, there is a discrepancy between them in particular in the parameter region where the number of model parameters is larger than the data dimension. In this paper, we review the prediction errors and corresponding estimators, and discuss their differences. In the framework of GAMP, we show that the information criteria can be expressed by using the variance of the estimates. Further, we demonstrate how to approach LOOCV error from the information criteria by utilizing the expression provided by GAMP.

Introduction

Over the last decade, there has been a considerable increase in the use of statistical methods and related machine learning techniques in various research fields.
This trend, which can be called the ‘data science
revolution’
[
1
]
, is driven by qualitative and quantitative
changes in data, increase in computational power,
and development of algorithms.
Further, physics has also been part of this trend; for example, the reconstruction of black hole
images in the event horizon telescope collaboration using sparse estimation is a recent
cooperative achievement between astrophysics and statistics
[
2
]
.

Natural science is the quest for physical laws as the ground truth,
and hence, statistical inference in natural science aims at the inference of the truth.
However, all data do not necessarily possess the ground truth.
After industrialization, data from
non-natural science fields
have become diversified
[
3
]
, and
the statistical inference for such non-nature data can be considered practical science for the human society.
For example, industrial data are used for the reasonable
determination, control, and creation of values.
In such cases, it is irrelevant if there are true physical laws,
or if our statistical models are different from these laws.
The statistical model is considered acceptable if practical operations based on this model do not result in any problems.

Once the purpose of statistical inference becomes people-oriented, the goodness of a statistical
model becomes subjective, as the justification by nature is impossible.
Mathematical
schemes are required for the construction of statistical models to make statistical inference a scientific procedure. The Akaike information
criterion (AIC)
[
4
]
and subsequent cross validation (CV) method
[
5
]

are schemes where the statistical model is
objectively selected using data among the set of models prepared subjectively.
In these methods, the goodness of the model is quantified by its predictive performance,
i.e. based on whether the model built by the given data can describe the unknown data,
which are expected to be observed in future.
The key idea for the mathematical formalism of the prediction is that the predictive
performance of the model is defined by the distributions rather than the value of a certain
statistic.
This idea led to the geometrical understanding of
the maximum likelihood method and associated model selection
in terms of information theory as a projection of the real to the model space

[
6
]
.

Conventional statistics focuses on the construction of statistical models that take into account empirical knowledge on the system, sometimes it requires experts’ wisdom.
In contrast to such approach,
deep learning,
which is a central topic in the recent research on statistics and
associated machine learning,
provides a framework to construct the generative model with less manual tuning.
The development of deep learning does not indicate the decline of the traditional statistical inference.
There have been attempts to understand the properties of deep learning using statistical methodologies such as understanding the double descent
phenomena in an overparametrized region using ridge regression
[
7
]
,
and characterizing the flat minima
[
8
]
in deep neural networks using
information criteria
[
9
]
.
The new paradigm offered by deep learning gives conventional statistics
a role as a simplified system for understanding the picture behind complicated
inference problems. This relationship will resonate with physicists,
who are trying to extract essentials of physical
phenomena using idealized theoretical frameworks or simplified experimental systems.
In addition,
there exists problems wherein it is difficult to acquire large amounts
of datasets because of the operating cost and technical difficulties.
In such cases, conventional
statistical modeling remains an important approach.

In this paper, we review model quantifications in terms of prediction performance,
and explain the physical consideration for statistical methodologies.
We introduce a statistical-physics-based approach for the mathematical
analysis of the prediction accuracy of the model under consideration.
The mathematical formalism for statistical inference has similarities
with statistical physics, in particular with the theory of spin-glass systems,
as the mathematical treatment of the randomness between spins’ interactions can be
regarded as that of data

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Utilizing the similarity, statistical physics-based methods have been applied to
the analyses of the inference problems as a mathematical tool
[
13
,
14
]
.
The statistical physics methods are effective even in parameter regions where
asymptotic normality does not hold; this is key in classical statistics.
This paper indicates the complementarity between traditional statistical
methods and statistical physics methods.

We consider regression problems on the
generalized linear model under penalty functions.
We focus on the estimator of the generalization gap, which is the
difference between the prediction and training errors
[
15
,
16
]
.
As estimators for the prediction errors and corresponding generalization gaps,
we analyze the

[

,

[

or Takeuchi Information Criterion (TIC)
[

We compare these estimators in the same setting of the penalized maximum likelihood method, i.e. the zero-temperature limit of the Bayesian inference in the terminology of the statistical physics.
The generalization gaps are quantified by the generalized message passing (GAMP) algorithm.
We show that the generalization gap obtained using the

This relationship resembles the fluctuation-response relationship in statistical physics,
in the sense that the response of the error

We also show that both

We correct TIC towards CV by removing the assumption

The resultant form reproduces the CV error with high accuracy
even in the non-asymptotic limit in statistics,
and one time estimation is sufficient under full data to obtain the form.

2
Prediction errors and their estimators

We focus on a parametric model
f(z|\bm{\theta})

with parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}

where
\bm{\Theta}
represents a parameter space.
The model parameter
\bm{\theta}
is estimated under the model
f(\cdot|\bm{\theta})

and training
data
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

to describe the data generative process.
Here, we consider regression problems wherein the parameter
\bm{\theta}
is
described by

\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x})=\bm{F}\bm{x}/\penalty\sqrt{N}
,
where
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

are the predictor matrix and the regression coefficient to be estimated,
respectively.
Therefore,
parameter space
\Theta
is specified by the
distributions of the predictors and regression coefficients.
The data set consists of
\bm{y}
and
\bm{F}
, hence
we denote
{\cal D}=\{\bm{y},\bm{F}\}
.
In the following discussion,
we assume that each component of the training data

y_{\mu}~(\mu=1,\cdots,M)
is independently and identically generated according to
the predetermined rule.
For mathematical convenience, we denote the true rule as the distribution

q(y_{\mu}|\bm{F})
.
Furthermore, we consider a case in which
the columns of the predictors are standardized
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{F}}[\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F_{\mu i}^{2}]=M

for any
i
.
Let us prepare a set of candidate models

{\cal M}=\{f_{1}(z|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}_{1})),\cdots,f_{m}(z|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}_{m}))\}

for the true distribution,
where
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{k}({\cal D})

represents the estimated regression coefficient under the
k
-th model
f_{k}

using training data
{\cal D}
.
The model selection is the problem of adopting a model based on a certain criterion
from the set of candidate models.

We consider the penalized maximum likelihood method
to construct the estimate
\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\bm{x}}\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x}),

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x})=\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))-h(\bm{x})

(2.2)

and
f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))
and

h(\bm{x})
are
the likelihood and penalty terms, respectively.
In this paper, we focus on separable likelihoods

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x})),

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{f}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
represents the
\mu
-th row vector of the predictor matrix,
and
\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbf{f}_{\mu}\bm{x}
.
Here,
h(\bm{x})
contains the smoothing or
regularization parameter introduced to avoid overfitting
[
15
,
21
]
.
Models with varying regularization parameter
constitute the model space;
the determination of the regularization parameter
corresponds to a model-selection problem.

In general, a model is constructed to represent the given training data sample
{\cal D}

accurately. The accuracy of representing the training data can
be quantified by the training error defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))).

(2.4)

The adequacy of the models with
the parameter
\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))

is quantified by the prediction error,
which indicates
the goodness of the model
for the description of the test data not used for training.
The high prediction error implies the overfitting,
and therefore, a model with a small prediction error is required in practice.

2.1
Extra-sample and in-sample prediction errors

Figure 1:
Definitions of extra-sample and in-sample prediction errors.
A test sample whose position is not in the training data is used for the evaluation of the extra-sample prediction error.
Test samples whose positions are the same as that of the
training sample are used for the in-sample prediction error.

There are two definitions of the prediction error:
extra-sample prediction error and in-sample prediction error,
which are considering different generative processes of the test data
used for evaluating the prediction error
[
15
]
.
Their definitions are given as follows.

Definition 1

(Extra-sample prediction error)
.

Let us denote

{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}

as a predictor value that is not included in the training sample
{\cal D}
.
The extra-sample prediction error for the penalized maximum likelihood method
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(\mathrm{ex})}({\cal D})=-\mathbb{E}_{z_{\mathrm{new}},{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}}\left[\ln f\left(z_{\mathrm{new}}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})\right)\right],

(2.5)

where
z_{\mathrm{new}}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the test data associated with the predictor

{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}
, and

\mathbb{E}_{z_{\mathrm{new}},{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}}[\cdot]

denotes the expectation with respect to

z_{\mathrm{new}}
and
{\bf f}_{\mathrm{new}}
.

Definition 2

(In-sample prediction error)
.

The in-sample prediction error for the penalized maximum likelihood method is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(\mathrm{in})}({\cal D})=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{z_{\mu}\sim q(z|{\bf f}_{\mu})}[\ln{f(z_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}({\bf f}_{\mu},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))}],

(2.6)

where
z_{\mu}
represents the new response variable (test data) observed at

\mu
-th training point.

Fig.
1
schematically shows the difference between
the test data for
the extra-sample and in-sample prediction errors.
The terms ‘extra’ and ‘in’ indicate
whether the position of the test data overlaps the training set.
The test data is on the location not covered by training samples in the case of the extra-sample error, hence the extra-sample error
describes the prediction error for varying the predictors.
The in-sample prediction error considers the test data generated by
repeated observation on the same position.
The in-sample prediction error sometimes does not match the
actual problem settings; however, it yields a convenient comparison method between models and
leads to an understanding of the model complexity as explained later.
In the parameter region where the conventional statistics focused on,
the tendencies of the two prediction errors
are considered to be similar with each other
[
15
]
.

The exact values of both prediction errors cannot be obtained because we
do not know the true generative process of the data.
Instead, estimators for the prediction errors are
used for evaluating the prediction accuracy.

2.2
Cross-validation error

The cross-validation error is an estimator for the extra-sample prediction error

[
5
]
.

Definition 3

(Leave-one-out cross validation error)
.

Let us denote a leave-one-out (LOO) sample
{\cal D}_{\setminus\mu}

where the
\mu
-th data sample
{\cal D}_{\mu}\equiv\{y_{\mu},\mathbf{f}_{\mu}\}

is discarded from the data set
{\cal D}
.
The estimate under the LOO sample, which is denoted by
\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}_{\setminus\mu})
,
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}_{\setminus\mu})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{x}}\sum_{\nu\in\bm{M}\setminus\mu}\ln f(y_{\nu}|\theta_{\nu}(\mathbf{f}_{\nu},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}_{\nu})))-h(\bm{x}),

(2.7)

where

\bm{M}=\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
and
\bm{M}\setminus\mu

denotes
\bm{M}
except
\mu
.
The leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) error is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{LOOCV}}({\cal D})=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln f\left(y_{\mu}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}{\bf f}_{\mu}\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}_{\setminus\mu})\right).

(2.8)

Intuitively, for a sufficiently large
M
,
the empirical mean with respect to the data sample is assumed to converge to the
expectation value
with respect to the true distribution. Therefore, the LOOCV error is considered an adequate estimator of the extra-sample prediction error.

2.2.1
Special form of LOOCV error for the linear estimation rule

For calculating the LOOCV error,

M

under
M

There is a long history of analytical and numerical

Here, we focus our discussion on the linear estimation rule.

In the linear estimation methods,

2.3
Information criteria as estimators of the in-sample prediction error

Information criteria are known as the
estimators of the in-sample prediction error.
These criteria were originally
introduced to evaluate the quality of the statistical model
constructed with the maximum likelihood method
using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
defined by

\displaystyle{\rm KL}(q:f)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}\left[\ln{q(\bm{z})}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[\ln{f(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\rm ML}({\cal D}))}],

(2.18)

where
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\rm ML}({\cal D})
represents the maximum likelihood estimator
under the training sample
{\cal D}
.
A statistical model with smaller KL divergence to the true distribution is
expected to be appropriate for the expression of given data.
The second term of KL divergence
(
2.18
) corresponds to the in-sample prediction error
under the ML estimator,
and the first term is a constant.
Therefore, the minimization of KL divergence corresponds to that of
the in-sample prediction error.

Takeuchi information criterion (TIC) is an estimator of the in-sample prediction error
and in particular under the maximum likelihood method,
TIC corresponds to an unbiased estimator of the in-sample prediction error

[
19
]
.

Definition 5

(Takeuchi information criterion)
.

The TIC is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{TIC}({\cal D})=2\left\{\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widehat{\cal I}(\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))\widehat{\cal J}^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))\right)\right\},

(2.19)

where
\widehat{\cal I}
and
\widehat{\cal J}
represent the estimator of the
Fisher information matrix

\overline{\cal I}

and the expected Hessian
\overline{\cal J}

given as

\displaystyle\overline{\cal I}(\widehat{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}\left[\frac{\partial\ln f(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))}{\partial\bm{x}}\frac{\partial\ln f(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))}{\partial\bm{x}^{\top}}\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}}\right]

(2.20)

\displaystyle\overline{\cal J}(\widehat{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ln f(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\bm{x}))}{\partial\bm{x}\partial\bm{x}^{\top}}\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}}\right],

(2.21)

respectively.

The factor 2 in the definition of TIC comes from
the convention that TIC for the Gaussian likelihood corresponds to the
estimator for the squared error.
As the estimators of Fisher information matrix and
the expected Hessian,
sample-mean of the (non-centered) covariance of gradient

{\cal I}(\bm{x})

and that of the negative Hessian
{\cal J}(\bm{x})

are used, where

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{ij}(\widehat{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\!\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\!\ln\!f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}}\!\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\!\ln\!f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}},

(2.22)

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{ij}(\widehat{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\bm{F},\bm{x})\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}}.

(2.23)

with the expectation that they converge to
\overline{\cal I}
and
\overline{\cal J}

from the law of large numbers.

For the special case where the model space contains the true model,
where a regression coefficient
\bm{x}^{*}

exists such that
q(\bm{z})=f(\bm{z}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{F}\bm{x}^{*})
,
it is shown that
TIC is reduced to Akaike’s information criterion (AIC)
[
4
]
,
which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{AIC}({\cal D})=2\left(\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+\frac{N}{M}\right).

(2.24)

This is the consequence of

\overline{\cal I}\overline{\cal J}^{-1}=\bm{I}_{N}

that holds in the special case
[
28
]
,
where
\bm{I}_{N}
represents the
N
-dimensional identity matrix.

The difference between the prediction and training error is termed the
generalization gap.
Based on the correspondence between
the generalization gap obtained by AIC and the dimension of the parameters,
the generalization gap is considered for expressing the model complexity.
The model can express various distributions with an increase in the complexity of the model, and therefore, there is a decrease in the training error.
However, the models with large parameters
are prone to overfitting, which hampers the prediction of unknown data,
because these models can fit the randomness in given training data.
The form of AIC is given by the training error
penalized by the number of model parameters.
Therefore, model selection based on the minimization of AIC
represents a tradeoff between fitting accuracy to training data and model complexity.

We note that the meaning of AIC as an unbiased estimator of the
in-sample prediction error
holds in the case where the
the maximum likelihood estimator is considered,
and the model candidates include true distribution.
In the case where the latter condition does not hold,
TIC is theoretically adequate for evaluating the in-sample prediction error.
This does not mean, however, that the AIC should not be used, although
theoretical justification is difficult.
In fact, AIC is, at times, superior to TIC
in terms of low computational cost and the small variance.
To evaluate TIC, we need to compute the inverse of Hessian,
whose computational cost is
O(N^{3})
,
and the fluctuation of the generalization gap
of TIC tends to be large, in particular when
eigenvalues close to zero exist
[
29
]
.

2.5
Mallows’
C_{p}
and model complexity in linear models

The model selection criteria explained thus far are for models constructed
using the maximum likelihood method.
A general expression of the estimator for the in-sample generalization error
is derived from
another model selection criterion, that is, Mallows’
C_{p}

[
17
]
.
We consider a linear estimation rule where

\widehat{\bm{y}}=\bm{H}\bm{y}
holds with a matrix
\bm{H}
,
and Gaussian likelihood with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Definition 6

(
C_{p}
criterion and degrees of freedom)
.

C_{p}
criterion is an estimator for the in-sample prediction error defined by

\displaystyle{c_{p}}(\bm{y})=2\sigma^{2}{\rm err}_{\rm train}(\bm{y})+2{\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{df}.

(2.31)

The term df is called degrees of freedom (DF)
[
31
,
15
]

defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}=\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{H}).

(2.32)

Under the known variance assumption,

C_{p}
criterion divided by
\sigma^{2}

is reduced to AIC.
To demonstrate this, let us apply a singular value decomposition to
\bm{F}

as
\bm{F}=\bm{U}\bm{D}\bm{V}^{\top}
, where

\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

represent the left and right singular vectors, respectively,
and
\bm{D}
represents the
N\times N
dimensional diagonal matrix whose
(i,i)
-component
corresponds to the
i
-th singular value
d_{i}
.
We obtain
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{2}/\penalty d_{i}^{2}=N
,
which indicates the equivalence of the
C_{p}
criterion and AIC.

C_{p}
can be extended to general estimators
\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})

in regression problems.
The following theorem shown in
[
16
]
is the basis for the extension:

Theorem 2
.

Let us consider the case where the following conditions hold:

•

The data generative process is given by Gaussian distribution

\bm{y}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{M})
,
where
\bm{I}_{M}
represents the
M
-dimensional identity matrix.

•

The likelihood is given by Gaussian distribution as

\displaystyle f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{||\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}||_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(2.33)

•

The variance
\sigma^{2}
is known.

The following estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}({\cal D})=2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+\frac{2}{M}\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D}))

(2.34)

is an unbiased estimator of the in-sample prediction error
with the multiplying factor
2\sigma^{2}

when
\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D}))

is an unbiased estimator of the covariance

\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D}))=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim q(\bm{y})}[\bm{y}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D})]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim q(\bm{y})}[\bm{y}]^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim q(\bm{y})}[\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D})]
.

Proof.

In the conditions considered in Theorem
2
,
the in-sample prediction and training errors are respectively given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(\mathrm{in})}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[||\bm{z}-\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))||_{2}^{2}],

(2.35)

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}M}||\bm{y}-\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))||_{2}^{2}.

(2.36)

Using the relationship
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[\bm{z}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim q(\bm{y})}[\bm{y}]
,
the generalization gap is given by the form known as
covariance penalty
[
16
]

\displaystyle\Delta^{(\mathrm{in})}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}M}\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D})\right).

(2.37)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(\mathrm{in})}({\cal D})=2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+\frac{2}{M}\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D})\right),

(2.38)

and this expression indicates that (
2.34
) is
an unbiased estimator of the in-sample prediction error.
∎

For constructing the estimator for the covariance penalty,
the following identity that holds when

\bm{y}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{M})

is convenient, known as Stein’s lemma
[
32
]
;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}M}\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{y},\widehat{\bm{y}}({\cal D})\right)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}}\left[\frac{\partial\widehat{y}_{\mu}({\cal D})}{\partial y_{\mu}}\right].

(2.39)

For the linear estimation rule, we obtain

\partial\widehat{y}_{\mu}({\cal D})/\penalty\partial y_{\mu}=H_{\mu\mu}
;
therefore, DF is an unbiased estimator of the covariance penalty.
We can obtain the estimator of the generalization gap
even when the analytical form of
\widehat{\bm{y}}

cannot be derived using the relationship (
2.39
).
Stein’s unbiased risk estimate (SURE)
of the mean squared error
[
32
]

is an estimator of
2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(\mathrm{in})}
for the
Gaussian likelihood applicable to
general estimators of
\widehat{\bm{y}}
.

Definition 7

(SURE and generalized degrees of freedom)
.

SURE is an estimator of the in-sample prediction error defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SURE}({\cal D})=2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{gdf}({\cal D}),

(2.40)

where
\mathrm{gdf}(\bm{y})
is termed the
generalized degree of freedom (GDF)
[
33
]
,
which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{gdf}({\cal D})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\frac{\partial\widehat{y}_{\mu}({\cal D})}{\partial y_{\mu}}.

(2.41)

GDF coincides with DF for the linear estimator, as we have already observed.
The expression (
2.41

is applicable to various models; however,
the exact correspondence between GDF and the number of
estimated variables in the model does not hold in general.
The functional form of
\widehat{y}_{\mu}({\cal D})

depends on the true generative process of the data,
assumed model, and estimation method; therefore,

2.5.1
Shrinkage estimators reduce model complexity

A well-known tendency of GDF is that
it decreases under shrinking estimators.
As an example, let us consider ridge regression

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\bm{x}}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\Big|\Big|\bm{y}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{F}\bm{x}\Big|\Big|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}||\bm{x}||_{2}^{2}\right\}.

The estimate
\widehat{\bm{x}}
and the

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{F}+\lambda\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{y}

(2.43)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\bm{H}\bm{y}

(2.44)

where

Applying the singular value decomposition

(2.45)

where

represents the
i
-th singular value.
Therefore,

However, in case of the

-penalized maximum likelihood method,

although the estimator is shrinking from ordinary least square estimator

[
34
,
35

36
]

This reason is considered that
the selection of the non-zero components increase model complexity, which is an increase balanced with the decrease of the model complexity attributed to shrinkage.

2.6
Importance sampling cross validation and functional variance

We introduce another expression of the LOOCV error
in the framework of the Bayesian inference; however, we consider the penalized maximum likelihood method in this paper.
In our problem setting, posterior distribution is defined by

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D})=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\exp(\beta\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x})),

(2.46)

where
Z_{\beta}=\int d\bm{x}\exp(\beta\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x}))
and
\beta
is the inverse temperature.
In the usual Bayesian inference,
\beta=1
is considered.
This parameter connects the Bayesian inference and the
penalized maximum likelihood method as

\displaystyle\widehat{x_{i}}({\cal D})=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\int d\bm{x}x_{i}p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D}).

(2.47)

We introduce the importance sampling cross validation (ISCV) error as follows.

Definition 8

(Importance sampling cross-validation error)
.

Under the posterior distribution
p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D})
,
ISCV error is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{ISCV}}({\cal D};\beta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln\int d\bm{x}\frac{1}{f^{\beta}(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}_{\mu}))}p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D}).

(2.48)

Theorem 3
.

ISCV error is equivalent to the LOOCV error
for the Bayesian inference at any
\beta

[
37
,
38
]
.

Proof.

We start with the LOOCV error for the Bayesian inference

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{LOOCV}}({\cal D};\beta)=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln\int d\bm{x}f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}_{\mu}))p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D}_{\setminus\mu}),

(2.49)

which is reduced to (

) at
\beta\to\infty

Setting
\phi(\bm{x})=\exp(-h(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{LOOCV}}({\cal D};\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln\frac{\int d\bm{x}\prod_{\nu\in\bm{M}\setminus\mu}f^{\beta}(y_{\nu}|\theta_{\nu}(\mathbf{f}_{\nu},\bm{x}))\phi^{\beta}(\bm{x})}{\int d\bm{x}\prod_{\nu\in\bm{M}}f^{\beta}(y_{\nu}|\theta_{\nu}(\mathbf{f}_{\nu},\bm{x}))\phi^{\beta}(\bm{x})}

(2.50)

and the R.H.S. corresponds to ISCV error.

For the sake of generality, we define the

\eta

\displaystyle{\cal T}(\eta)=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ln\int d\bm{x}p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D})f^{\eta\beta}(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x})).

(2.51)

The training and ISCV errors correspond to

Applying Maclaurin expansion to

\displaystyle{\cal T}(\eta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\frac{\partial^{k}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{k}}\Big|_{\eta=0}\eta^{k},

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta}\Big|_{\eta=0}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{2}}\Big|_{\alpha=0}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial^{3}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{3}}\Big|_{\alpha=0}+O(\eta^{4})

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{ISCV}}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta}\Big|_{\eta=0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{2}}\Big|_{\alpha=0}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial^{3}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{3}}\Big|_{\alpha=0}+O(\eta^{4}),

(2.54)

where

Thus, the following relationship holds:

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{ISCV}}({\cal D})=\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})+\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal T}(\eta)}{\partial\eta^{2}}\Big|_{\eta=0}+O(\eta^{4}).

(2.55)

The second derivative of
{\cal T}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}{\cal T}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\beta\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\Big[\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\left[\left(\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu})\right)^{2}\exp(\eta\beta\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}))\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\left[\exp(\eta\beta\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}))\right]}

(2.56)

\displaystyle-\left\{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\left[\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu})\exp(\eta\beta\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}))\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\left[\exp(\eta\beta\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}))\right]}\right\}^{2}\Big],

The functional variance (FV) is defined by the variance of the component-wise

\displaystyle\mathrm{FV}({\cal D})=\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\Big\{\int d\bm{x}

(2.58)

should be considered,

\displaystyle\mathrm{FV}({\cal D})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\gamma_{\mu}^{2}}\ln Z_{\beta,\bm{\gamma}}({\cal D})\Big|_{\bm{\gamma}=1},

(2.59)

where

\displaystyle Z_{\beta,\bm{\gamma}}({\cal D})=\int d\bm{x}f^{\beta\gamma_{\mu}}(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\phi^{\beta}(\bm{x})

(2.60)

is the extended partition function with an auxiliary variable

This form is useful for the derivation of FV in the framework of GAMP later.

2.6.1
Equivalence of TIC and WAIC

The derivations of TIC and WAIC from the LOOCV error differ from each other.
TIC is obtained by removing one observation and assuming that the
difference in estimates due to the remove is sufficiently small.
Meanwhile, WAIC considers
component-wise variance of the log-likelihood.
However, it can be shown that TIC and WAIC are equivalent to each other,
as explained in
[
41
]
,
when the Laplace approximation is valid.
By expanding the log-likelihood around the MAP estimate

\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))=\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))

(2.61)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})}(x_{i}-\widehat{x}_{i}({\cal D})).

The function to be minimized is expanded as

\displaystyle\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\varphi({\cal D},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))

(2.62)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\varphi({\cal D},\bm{x})\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})}(x_{i}-\widehat{x}_{i}({\cal D}))(x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}({\cal D})),

where we consider that the gradient at
\widehat{\bm{x}}
is zero.
Hence, we obtain the Laplace approximation of the posterior distribution as

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\beta M}{2}(\bm{x}-\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})){\cal J}^{\varphi}(\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))(\bm{x}-\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))^{\mathrm{T}}\right),

(2.63)

where
{\cal J}^{\varphi}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
is given by (
2.23
).
Using (
2.61
) and (
2.63
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{FV}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\sum_{ij}\int d\bm{x}p_{\beta}(\bm{x}|{\cal D})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})}

(2.64)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\ln f(y_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\mathbf{f}_{\mu},\bm{x}))\Big|_{\bm{x}=\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})}(x_{i}-\widehat{x}_{i}({\cal D}))(x_{j}-\widehat{x}_{j}({\cal D}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Tr}\left({\cal I}(\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})){\cal J}^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))\right),

which corresponds to the bias term of TIC.

3
Our purpose and problem setting

In the conventional problem settings in statistics,

M\to\infty
with sufficiently small
N
are considered,
hence the difference between the in-sample prediction error and
the extra-sample prediction error has not been focused on.
Recently, characteristic phenomena known as double descent phenomena,
coined by
[
42
]
,
wherein the extra-sample prediction error exhibits two minima:
one each in the underparameterized and overparametrized regions,
has been studied
[
7
]
.
These phenomena were reported in

[
43
,
44
,
45
]
for linear models,
and have since been reported
[
46
,
47
]

for neural networks, attracting
considerable attention.
For a broader understanding of these phenomena,
some studies are attempted on the random features model

[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
.
In general, the modern neural network considers the overparametrized region,
and some studies attempted to extend the conventional statistical
methods to the overparametrized region
[
9
]
.
Following such direction,
we focus on the differences between the estimator of the prediction errors,
namely,

C_{p}
, TIC or WAIC, and LOOCV error,
in the parameter region
M\sim O(N)
, including the
regime where the number of parameters is larger than the data dimension.
In the subsequent sections, we derive
their forms using the generalized approximate message passing (GAMP)
algorithm
[
52
,
53
]
,
and discuss their behavior in the
M\sim O(N)
region.

Hereafter, we denote
\alpha=M/\penalty N
.
For the dense regression coefficient,

\alpha
corresponds to the ratio of data dimension to
the number of parameters, and

\alpha\gg 1
is the parameter region where the
conventional studies of statistics focused on.
We focus on the finite
\alpha
region where
\alpha\sim O(1)
.
In this paper, we consider the generalized linear model (GLM),
and demonstrate the results for the penalized linear regression
and penalized logistic regression.
The forms shown in this study can be applied to general
exponential family distributions.

We here summarize our contribution shown in the following sections.

•

We derive the forms of the estimators for the generalization gaps using GAMP,
which is applicable to any
\alpha
region
as long as the local stability condition of GAMP is not violated.

•

We show that GDF and FV are related to the variance of the
estimated parameter in the framework of GAMP.

•

Using the expression by GAMP,
it is shown that the convergence condition of the
series expansion (
2.52
), which is the basis of WAIC,
is related to GDF in case of the Gaussian likelihood.

•

The resulting forms of the estimators for the in-sample prediction error
have differences with the extra-sample prediction error
in the finite
\alpha
region.
We derive the differences in the finite
\alpha
region
using the expression by GAMP.

3.1
Problem setting

We consider GLM as the likelihood where
the assumed model is provided by the natural exponential family
distribution;

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{y}-a(\bm{\theta})+b(\bm{y}),

(3.1)

where
a(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}a(\theta_{\mu})

and
b(\bm{y})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}b(y_{\mu})
.
In the natural exponential family,

\bm{\theta}
relates to the statistics of the distribution as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[y_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}a(\theta_{\mu}),~~~\mathbb{V}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[y_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}\theta_{\mu}}a(\theta_{\mu}),

(3.2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{V}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[\cdot]

denote the mean and variance according to the likelihood
f

with the parameter
\bm{\theta}
.
In GLM, the model parameter and the mean of the distribution are related to each other
through link function
\ell
as

\displaystyle\ell(\mathbb{E}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[y_{\mu}])=\theta_{\mu},~~~\ell^{-1}(\theta_{\mu})=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal L}(\bm{\theta})}[y_{\mu}].

(3.3)

The distribution is specified by the functions
a(\cdot)
and
b(\cdot)
.
For example, the linear regression with Gaussian likelihood
corresponds to

\displaystyle a(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}||\bm{\theta}||_{2}^{2},~~~b(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}||\bm{y}||_{2}^{2},

(3.4)

and the link function is given by the identity function
\ell(\theta)=\theta
.
Other representative GLMs are
presented in
B
.
The generalization gap of the in-sample prediction error
for GLM
is written in the same form as that of (
2.37
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{train}}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M}\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left\{-\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))^{\top}\bm{y}+a(\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))-b(\bm{y})\right\}

\displaystyle{\rm err}_{\rm pre}^{(\mathrm{in})}({\cal D})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[\ln f(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))]

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\Big\{-\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[\bm{z}]+a(\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D})))

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim q(\bm{z})}[b(\bm{z})]\Big\},

hence

\displaystyle\Delta^{(\mathrm{in})}({\cal D})=\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y},|\bm{\theta}(\bm{F},\widehat{\bm{x}}({\cal D}))).

(3.7)

For the penalty term, we restrict our discussion to the separable
penalties, where
h(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(x_{i})
.
We focus on the
elastic net penalty

\displaystyle h(x)=\lambda_{1}|x|+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}x^{2},

(3.8)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are regularization
parameters for controlling the intensity of the penalty.
The elastic net penalty at
\lambda_{1}=0
and
\lambda_{2}=0
correspond to

\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{1}
norm penalty, respectively.

4
GAMP for GLM

Message passing is an algorithm used to approximately calculate the
marginal posterior distribution.
The algorithm is based on the
graphical representation of the
conditional independency of the variables according to the
probability distribution
[
54
,
55
]
.
Graphical models provide visual support to draw inferences and
construct efficient algorithms.
In practice, message passing shows efficient performances
as a decoding algorithm for
low density parity check (LDPC) code
[
56
]
,
code division multiple access (CDMA)
[
57
]
,
and others.

Message passing with a Gaussian approximation is known as
approximate message passing (AMP)
[
58
]
that reduces the
computational cost required for the inference.
The AMP was proposed in the study on compressed sensing
and it was extended to a generalized approximate message passing (GAMP)
[
52
]
to deal with general separable likelihood and prior.
The AMP has a similarity between
the cavity approach
[
59
]
and Thouless–Anderson–Palmer (TAP)
equation
[
60
]
in statistical physics for spin glasses.
This relationship induces a statistical-physics based study on the
information science and machine learning
[
61
]
such as
compressed sensing
[
14
]
,
regression
[
62
]
, and matrix factorization
[
63
]
.
The details of the derivation of the GAMP for the
regression problem of the separable likelihoods and priors are
shown in
C
.

The message passing procedure provides the exact marginal distribution when the
graph consists of tree structures.
Our problem setting corresponds to the case where the graph has loops, hence
the marginal distribution calculated by the message passing is
an approximation.
More precisely, the resulting marginal posterior distributions are
locally consistent marginals, and
do not necessarily constitute an overall posterior distribution
[
61
]
.
Furthermore, it is known that
GAMP with random initialization
only provides an accurate approximation of the posterior distribution
only in the case in which the global minima of the free energy is the closest minima to zero overlap
[
64
,
65
]
.
Although a lack of the exactness and limitations exist in
accurate approximation,
GAMP is useful for calculating the generalization gap.
In the estimation of the in-sample prediction error,
analytical expression of the degrees of freedom
for general regression problems are limited, and
specific expressions are studied for LASSO
[
34
]
,
group lasso, fused lasso
[
66
]
,
and minimax concave penalty
[
67
]
.
Numerically, the bootstrap method is useful for evaluating the generalization gap

[
16
]
;
however, its computational cost is expensive, and it
often leads unstable estimates.
Extension of the path seeking algorithm
[
68
]

is another numerical approach that
is applied for estimating the generalization gap
[
69
]
,
which requires additional matrix operation.
Compared with these numerical approaches,
GAMP has several advantages as an estimation method of
the generalization gap for the in-sample prediction error.
First, GAMP provides a general expression of the
generalization gap for the in-sample prediction error.
This expression leads to a unified interpretation of the
prediction as a
relationship between the fluctuation and response,
as discussed in sec.
4.4
.
Second, GAMP does not require additional computation
for evaluating the generalization gap for the in-sample prediction error,
as the generalization gap can be expressed
using variables that constitute GAMP.

In addition to the advantages of evaluating the in-sample prediction error,
we can obtain LOOCV error using GAMP by
assuming that the cavity distribution in GAMP corresponds to the
posterior distribution under the LOO sample.
While the verification of the assumption is required,
it greatly reduces the computational cost required to estimate the
LOOCV error, as discussed in sec.
4.3.3
.

4.1
General form of message passing algorithm

Figure 3:
Graphical representation of regression problem
and messages defined on the edges

The graphical representation of the regression problems considered here is shown in
Figure
3
.
Factor nodes
\square
, variable nodes
\bigcirc
, and
the edges represent the data
\bm{y}
, regression coefficient
\bm{x}
, and
predictor matrix
\bm{F}
, respectively.
The message passing procedure is
described by following two messages defined on the edges
between the factor nodes and the variable nodes:

•

{\xi}_{i\to\mu}(x_{i})
: Message from the variable
x_{i}
to
data
y_{\mu}
(output message)

•

\widetilde{\xi}_{\mu\to i}(x_{i})
: Message from
data
y_{\mu}
to the variable
x_{i}
(input message)

Intuitively, output and input messages are the
probability distributions before and after the observation of
\mu
-th data.
Hence, in particular, the output message
{\xi}_{i\to\mu}(x_{i})

is considered to describe the probability distribution
under the LOO sample
{\cal D}_{\setminus\mu}

[
70
]
.
We term the graphical model without
\mu
-th data as
\mu
-cavity system.

For general graphs,
these messages need to be recursively updated
to obtain the posterior distribution.
Here, we place the superscript
(t)
on the messages and related quantities
to indicate that their values are the results of the
t
-times update.
The messages at the
t
-th and
(t+1)
-th step are related as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}^{(t)}_{\mu\to i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\int d\bm{x}_{\backslash i}f^{\beta}(y_{\mu}|\bm{\theta})\prod_{j\in\bm{N}\setminus i}{\xi}^{(t)}_{j\to\mu}(x_{j})

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\xi}_{i\to\mu}^{(t+1)}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\phi^{\beta}(x_{i})\prod_{\nu\in\bm{M}\setminus\mu}\widetilde{\xi}_{\nu\to i}^{(t)}(x_{i}),

(4.2)

where
\bm{N}=\{1,\cdots,N\}
,

\bm{M}=\{1,\cdots,M\}
, and

\bm{N}\setminus i
denote
\bm{N}
except
i
.
Using the input messages,
the marginal distribution is given by

\displaystyle p^{(t)}_{\beta,i}(x_{i})\propto\phi^{\beta}(x_{i})\prod_{\nu\in\bm{M}}\widetilde{\xi}^{(t)}_{\nu\to i}(x_{i}),

(4.3)

and the
i
-th component of the penalized maximum likelihood
estimate is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{x}^{(t)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\int d{x_{i}}~x_{i}p_{\beta,i}^{(t)}(x_{i}).

(4.4)

4.2
Approximate message passing

The updating rule (
4.1
) and (
4.2
)
consists of
2MN
messages.
For simplification, we introduce the following assumptions for sufficiently large
M
and
N
.

a1:

All components of the predictor matrix are
O(1)
.

a2:

The correlation between predictors is negligible:
the off-diagonal components of
\frac{1}{N}\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{F}
are
O(N^{-1/\penalty 2})
.

Under assumptions
a1
and
a2
,
the input messages
\{\widetilde{\xi}_{\mu\to i}\}

can be considered Gaussian distributions, as shown in
C
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to consider the means and variances of the messages; we denote them as

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i\to\mu}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\int dx_{i}x_{i}\xi_{i\to\mu}^{(t)}(x_{i})

(4.5)

\displaystyle s_{i\to\mu}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\beta\int dx_{i}(x_{i}-\widehat{x}_{i\to\mu}^{(t)})^{2}\xi_{i\to\mu}^{(t)}(x_{i})

(4.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}_{\mu\to i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\int dx_{i}x_{i}\widehat{\xi}_{\mu\to i}^{(t)}(x_{i})

(4.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{s}_{\mu\to i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\beta\int dx_{i}(x_{i}-\widetilde{x}_{\mu\to i}^{(t)})^{2}\widetilde{\xi}_{\mu\to i}^{(t)}(x_{i}).

(4.8)

An algorithm for updating these means and variances
under the arbitrary likelihood and prior distribution
is called generalized approximate message passing (GAMP).
In GAMP, the marginalized distribution
p_{\beta,i}(x_{i})

is represented by

\displaystyle p_{\beta,i}^{(t)}(x_{i})={\cal P}_{\beta}(x_{i};\Sigma_{i}^{(t)},\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)}),

(4.9)

where
{\cal P}_{\beta}
is the distribution defined by

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\beta}(x;\Sigma,\mathrm{m})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}}\phi^{\beta}(x)\exp\left\{-\frac{\beta(x-\mathrm{m})^{2}}{2\Sigma}\right\},

(4.10)

with the normalization constant
{\cal Z}
.
We define the mean and variance of (
4.10
)
as
\mathbb{M}_{\beta}(\Sigma,\mathrm{m})
and
\beta^{-1}\mathbb{V}_{\beta}(\Sigma,\mathrm{m})
,
respectively, and denote

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\int dx_{i}x_{i}{\cal P}_{\beta}(x;\Sigma_{i}^{(t)},\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)})=\mathbb{M}_{\beta}(\Sigma_{i}^{(t)},\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)})

(4.11)

\displaystyle s_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\beta\int dx_{i}(x_{i}-\widehat{x}_{i}^{(t)})^{2}{\cal P}_{\beta}(x;\Sigma_{i}^{(t)},\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)})=\mathbb{V}_{\beta}(\Sigma_{i}^{(t)},\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)}),

(4.12)

where
\widehat{x}_{i}^{(t)}
corresponds to the penalized maximum likelihood estimate
of step
t
at
\beta\to\infty
,
which limit will be taken later.
Here, the following relationship holds.

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\beta}(\Sigma,\mathrm{m})=\beta^{-1}\Sigma\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrm{m}}\mathbb{M}_{\beta}(\Sigma,\mathrm{m}).

(4.13)

The form of (
4.10
) indicates that
the posterior distribution is given by the prior distribution for
x_{i}

and the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mathrm{m}_{i}
and
variance
\Sigma_{i}
, which represents a contribution from other variables.
Here, we set

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{\mu i}\widehat{x}_{i\to\mu}^{(t)},

(4.14)

\displaystyle{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{\mu i}^{2}s_{i\to\mu}^{(t)},

(4.15)

where (
4.14
) corresponds to the
estimated model parameter in the
\mu
-cavity system.
After some calculations shown in
C
,
we derive
\mathrm{m}_{i}
and
\Sigma_{i}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle={\Sigma}_{i}^{(t)}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}F_{\mu i}\beta^{-1}g_{\mathrm{out}}({s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},y_{\mu})

(4.16)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{1}{N}F_{\mu i}^{2}\widehat{x}_{i}^{(t)}\beta^{-1}\partial_{\widehat{\theta}}g_{\mathrm{out}}({s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},y_{\mu})\Big\}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\Sigma}_{i}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F^{2}_{\mu i}\beta^{-1}\partial_{\widehat{\theta}}g_{\mathrm{out}}({s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},y_{\mu}),

(4.17)

where

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{out}}({s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},y_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\frac{\beta(\theta_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)})}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)}}\Big\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\partial_{\widehat{\theta}}g_{\mathrm{out}}({s_{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu(t)},y_{\mu})

(4.19)

The expectation

(4.45)

for any

For computing the hat matrix in the linear estimator,

4.3.4
Local stability condition of GAMP

The GAMP algorithm sometimes fails to converge
depending on the model parameters
N
,
M
, and regularization parameters.
The instability condition can be derived by
considering the linear stability analysis around the GAMP’s fixed point.

We consider the case that
\widehat{\bm{x}}
is at a fixed point with
the corresponding values of
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
for
\mu\in\bm{M}
.
When
\widehat{\bm{x}}
is slightly deviated from the value at the fixed point,
the deviation propagates to
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
by GAMP,
and back to
\widehat{\bm{x}}
.
If estimates under the small deviance are moved away from the fixed point
by the GAMP update,
the fixed point can be regarded as locally unstable.

We consider the difference in
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}

induced by the small deviance in
\widehat{\bm{x}}
as

\displaystyle\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\equiv\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}(\{\widehat{x}_{i}+\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\})-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}(\{\widehat{x}_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}{\partial\widehat{x}_{i}}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}

(4.46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{\mu i}\delta\widehat{x}_{i},

where
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}(\widehat{\bm{x}})
denotes
the value of
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}

under
\widehat{\bm{x}}
.
We restrict our discussion to the predictors whose
entries are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1.
For such predictors,
the summation of L.H.S. in (
4.46
)
is reduced to zero at sufficiently large
N
, hence
we consider the squared sum as

\displaystyle{\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}^{2}\simeq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}^{2}.

(4.47)

Meanwhile, we consider the deviation of
\widehat{\bm{x}}
attributed to the
slight deviance of
\theta_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
defined by

\displaystyle\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\equiv\widehat{x}_{i}(\{\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}+\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\})-\widehat{x}_{i}(\{\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\frac{\partial\widehat{x}_{i}}{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu},

(4.48)

where
\widehat{x}_{i}(\{\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\})

denotes the estimates under
\{\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\}
.
The deviation of
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
propagates to
\widehat{x}_{i}

through
\mathrm{m}_{i}
, and hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{x}_{i}}{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathrm{m}_{i}}{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}\frac{\partial\widehat{x}_{i}}{\partial\mathrm{m}_{i}}

(4.49)

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{i}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}F_{\mu i}(\check{\partial}_{\hat{\theta}}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}+O(N^{-1})\right\}s_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{-1},

where we use the relationship (
4.13
).
For the random preditors,
the summation of (
4.49
) with
respect to
\mu
is expected to converge to zero for a sufficiently large
M
, and
hence, we consider the squared summation under the assumptions as

\displaystyle\delta\widehat{x}_{i}^{2}\simeq\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F_{\mu i}^{2}(\check{\partial}_{\hat{\theta}}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}^{2}{\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}^{2}\simeq\frac{\alpha s_{i}^{2}}{M}\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}(\check{\partial}_{\hat{\theta}}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\nu}^{2}{\delta\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}^{2}.

(4.50)

Therefore, setting
\overline{\delta\widehat{x}^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}^{2}
,
we obtain the recursive relationship of
\overline{\delta\widehat{x}^{2}}
as

\displaystyle\overline{\delta\widehat{x}^{2}}=\alpha\overline{s^{2}}\overline{(\check{\partial}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})^{2}}\overline{\delta\widehat{x}^{2}},

(4.51)

where
we set
\overline{s^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{2}

and
\overline{(\check{\partial}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}(\check{\partial}_{\hat{\theta}}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\nu}^{2}
.
Thus, the solution
\overline{\delta\widehat{x}^{2}}=0
is unstable when

\displaystyle\alpha\overline{s^{2}}\overline{(\check{\partial}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})^{2}}>1,

(4.52)

which indicates the local instability of the fixed point.
This condition corresponds the instability of the replica symmetric ansatz in the replica analysis
shown in sec.
6.4
.

4.4
Expression of GDF by GAMP

We start with the
form (
4.24
) for the estimated parameter
\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}
.
By definition,

\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
and
s_{\theta_{\mu}}^{\setminus\mu}

are defined in the
\mu
-cavity system,
and hence, their values are independent from
y_{\mu}

when the graphical model does not have any loops including
y_{\mu}
.
In fact, when the graph contains loops,

\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}
and
s_{\theta_{\mu}}^{\setminus\mu}
are dependent on
y_{\mu}
,
but we assume that their dependency on
y_{\mu}
is negligible.
Under this consideration,
the dependency on
y_{\mu}
only appears in the Onsager reaction term,
i.e. the term

, and hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}}{\partial y_{\mu}}={s_{\theta}}_{\mu}\frac{\partial(\check{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}}{\partial y_{\mu}}.

(4.53)

From (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\check{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}}{\partial y_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}\left\{\left\langle(\theta_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu})\!\left(\theta_{\mu}+b^{\prime}(y_{\mu})\right)\right\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}\!\!\!\!-\!\left\langle\theta_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}\right\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}\!\!\!\!\left\langle\theta_{\mu}+b^{\prime}(y_{\mu})\right\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}\!\right\}

(4.54)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu}}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}},

where we define the variance of the model parameter as

\displaystyle{\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu}=\beta\left(\langle\theta_{\mu}^{2}\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}-\langle\theta_{\mu}\rangle_{\theta_{\mu}}^{2}\right).

(4.55)

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}}{\partial y_{\mu}}={\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu},

(4.56)

which holds for the general value of
\beta
, and implies that
the GAMP evaluates GDF and the generalization gap for the in-sample error
as
[
36
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{gdf}^{(\mathrm{AMP})}({\cal D})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}{\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu}

(4.57)

Eq. (
4.57
) indicates the proportionality between the
response of the learning error and the variance of the estimated parameter at equilibrium,
which can be regarded as a fluctuation-response relationship described by GAMP

[
20
]
.

The form of
{\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu}
can be derived at the
\beta\to\infty
limit,
when the following second order approximation is valid.
Expanding the function

around
{\theta}_{\mu}^{*}
up to the second order,
we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\xi}(\theta_{\mu};y_{\mu},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu},{s_{\theta}}_{\mu})

\displaystyle=f_{\xi}(\theta^{*}_{\mu};y_{\mu},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu},{s_{\theta}}_{\mu})

(4.58)

\displaystyle+\frac{(\theta_{\mu}-\theta_{\mu}^{*})^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}f_{\xi}(\theta;y_{\mu},\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu},{s_{\theta}}_{\mu})\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{\mu}^{*}}.

Hence, in case of GLM, we obtain
[
53
]

\displaystyle{\chi_{\theta}}_{\mu}=\frac{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}{1+{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}a^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{\mu}^{*})}.

(4.59)

For the Gaussian likelihood, where

,
GDF is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{gdf}^{(\mathrm{AMP})}({\cal D})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\frac{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}{1+{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}.

(4.60)

Considering the correspondence between the linear estimation rule and GAMP
(
4.45
),
the expression of GDF by GAMP can be regarded as DF
using the extended hat matrix (
4.45
)
for the Gaussian likelihood.

6
Replica method

For a part of predictor matrices,
the prediction error and generalization gap can be analytically
derived by using replica method.
In this section, we show the analysis for the
random predictor matrix
\bm{F}
whose components are
independently and identically distributed according to the Gaussian distribution.
In such cases, it is shown that the
typical trajectory of the time evolution of the GAMP,
termed as state evolution,
corresponds to the saddle point equations of the
replica method

[
14
]
.
The rigorousness of the state evolution for GAMP
has been proved under i.i.d. Gaussian data
[
73
]
,
and correlated Gaussian data
[
51
]
.

In the replica analysis,
we derive the expectation value of the errors
with respect to
{\cal D}=\{\bm{y},\bm{F}\}
,
to understand the typical property
under the given
{\cal D}

[
64
]
.
The basis for the analysis is the free energy density, which is defined as

\displaystyle\Psi=-\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\beta}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal D}}[\ln Z_{\beta}({\cal D})].

(6.1)

The
training error relates to the
free energy density at zero temperature,
and as discussed in sec.
2.6
,
the expression of FV by the replica method
can be derived from the partition function
by introducing auxiliary variables.

In the analysis, we consider the same setting as the numerical simulations
by GAMP summarized in sec.
5
,
where the data
\bm{y}
is generated by
a certain rule governed by the ‘true’ parameter

\bm{\theta}^{(0)}=\bm{F}\bm{x}^{(0)}/\penalty\sqrt{N}
with a
‘true’ regression coefficient
x_{i}^{(0)}
.
Here, we consider that the true regression coefficient is generated by
Bernoulli–Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\phi^{(0)}(x^{(0)})=(1-\rho)\delta(x^{(0)})+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{x}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x^{(0)})^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right),

(6.2)

where
\rho\in[0,1]
is the fraction of nonzero components in the true coefficient.
We assume that the model parameter
is subjected to additive
noise
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
; hence we denote

\bm{y}={\cal G}(\bm{\theta}^{(0)}+\bm{\varepsilon})
,
where
{\cal G}
is an arbitrary function that describe the data generative process.
The expectation of the free energy density
with respect to
\bm{y},\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{F}
and
\bm{x}^{(0)}

is implemented by replica method
based on the following identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ln Z_{\beta}({\cal D})]=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\mathbb{E}_{\cal D}[Z_{\beta}^{n}({\cal D})].

(6.3)

We focus on the
N\to\infty
and
M\to\infty
limit
which keep
M/\penalty N=\alpha\sim O(1)
.
For the sake of convenience, we define free entropy density
for the finite
\beta
and finite
n
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}(n)=\frac{1}{N}\ln\mathbb{E}_{{\cal D}}[Z_{\beta}^{n}({\cal D})].

(6.4)

Assuming
n
is a positive integer, we express the
n
-th power
by introducing the
n
-replicated system as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int d\bm{\varepsilon}P_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\varepsilon})\int d\bm{F}P_{F}(\bm{F})\int d\bm{x}^{(0)}\phi^{(0)}(\bm{x}^{(0)})

(6.5)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{a=1}^{n}\int d\bm{x}^{(a)}f^{\beta}\left({\cal G}(\bm{\theta}^{(0)}+\bm{\varepsilon})|\bm{\theta}^{(a)}\right)\phi^{\beta}(\bm{x}^{(a)})

where we set
\bm{\theta}^{(a)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{Fx}^{(a)}
.
For implementing the integrals,
we introduce the subshells with respect to the macroscopic variables

\{q^{(ab)}\}
and
\{m^{(a)}\}

by inserting trivial identities

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int dq^{(ab)}\delta\left(q^{(ab)}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{(a)}x_{i}^{(b)}\right)

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\int dq^{(ab)}d\widehat{q}^{(ab)}\exp\left(-Nq^{(ab)}\widehat{q}^{(ab)}+\widehat{q}^{(ab)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{(a)}x_{i}^{(b)}\right)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int dm^{(a)}\delta\left(m^{(a)}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{(0)}x_{i}^{(a)}\right)

(6.7)

\displaystyle=\int dm^{(a)}d\widehat{m}^{(a)}\exp\left(-Nm^{(a)}\widehat{m}^{(a)}+\widehat{m}^{(a)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{(0)}x_{i}^{(a)}\right)

for all pairs of
(a,b)~(a,b\in\bm{n})
and
a\in\bm{n}
,
where
\bm{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
and
\{\widehat{q}^{(ab)}\}
and
\{\widehat{m}^{(a)}\}

are conjugate variables for the integral expressions of the delta functions.
The expectation with respect to
\bm{F}
,
whose components are independently and identically
distributed according to Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance 1,
within the subshells lead to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{F}|q^{(ab)}}[\theta^{(a)}_{\mu}\theta^{(b)}_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=q^{(ab)}

(6.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{F}|m^{(a)}}[\theta^{(a)}_{\mu}\theta^{(0)}_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=m^{(a)}

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{F}}[\theta^{(0)}_{\mu}\theta^{(0)}_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\rho\sigma_{x}^{2}.

(6.10)

Thus, in the subshell,
the joint distribution of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mu}\equiv\{\theta_{\mu}^{(0)},\theta_{\mu}^{(1)},\cdots\theta_{\mu}^{(n)}\}
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mu}|{\cal Q})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi|{\cal Q}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mu}^{\top}{\cal Q}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mu}\right)

(6.11)

for any
\mu\in\bm{M}
,
where
{\cal Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}

is given by

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{ab}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c}q^{(ab)}&a\neq 0~\mbox{and}~b\neq 0\\
m^{(a)}&a\neq 0~\mbox{and}~b=0\\
m^{(b)}&b=0~\mbox{and}~b\neq 0\\
\rho\sigma_{x}^{2}&a=b=0\end{array}\right..

Using the joint distribution with respect to
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mu}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}

\displaystyle(n)=\frac{1}{N}\ln\int\prod_{a\leq b}dq^{(ab)}d\widehat{q}^{(ab)}\prod_{a=1}^{n}dm^{(a)}d\widehat{m}^{(a)}

(6.17)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-N\sum_{a\leq b}q^{(ab)}\widehat{q}^{(ab)}-N\sum_{a=1}^{n}m^{(a)}\hat{m}^{(a)}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\int d\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\int d\varepsilon P_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)P_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}|{\cal Q})\prod_{a=1}^{n}f\left({\cal G}(\theta^{(0)}+\varepsilon)|\theta^{(a)}\right)\right]^{M}

\displaystyle\times\left[\int\!\!d\widetilde{\bm{x}}\!\prod_{a=1}^{n}\!\phi(x^{(a)})\phi^{(0)}(x^{(0)})\!\exp\!\!\left(\sum_{a\leq b}\widehat{q}^{(ab)}x^{(a)}x^{(b)}\!+\!\!\sum_{a=1}^{n}\widehat{m}^{(a)}x^{(0)}x^{(a)}\!\!\right)\!\right]^{N},

where
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\{x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\cdots x^{(n)}\}

and

P(\varepsilon)
represents the
distribution of the noise
\varepsilon
.
By applying the saddle point equation,
we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}(n)=\mathop{\mathrm{extr}}_{\{q^{(ab)}\},\{m^{(a)}\},\{\widehat{q}^{(ab)}\},\{\widehat{m}^{(a)}\}}\left\{\Phi_{\beta}^{(C)}(n)+\alpha\Phi^{(E)}_{\beta}(n)+\Phi^{(S)}_{\beta}(n)\right\},

(6.18)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}^{(C)}(n)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a\leq b}q^{(ab)}\widehat{q}^{(ab)}-\sum_{a=1}^{n}m^{(a)}\widehat{m}^{(a)}

(6.19)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}^{(E)}(n)

\displaystyle=\ln\int d\varepsilon P_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)\int d\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}P_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}|{\cal Q})\prod_{a=1}^{n}f^{\beta}\left({\cal G}(\theta^{(0)}+\varepsilon)|\theta^{(a)}\right)

(6.20)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}^{(S)}(n)

\displaystyle=\ln\int d\widetilde{\bm{x}}\phi^{(0)}(x^{(0)})\phi^{\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}^{(a)})

(6.21)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\exp\left(\sum_{a\leq b}\widehat{q}^{(ab)}x^{(a)}x^{(b)}+\sum_{a=1}^{n}\widehat{m}^{(a)}x^{(0)}x^{(a)}\right).

6.1
Replica symmetric assumption

For further calculations, we introduce
replica symmetric (RS) assumption as

\displaystyle(q^{(ab)},\widehat{q}^{(ab)})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(Q,-\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{Q}}{2}\right)&{\rm if}~a=b\\
(q,\widehat{q})&{\rm if}~a\neq b\\
\end{array},\right.

\displaystyle(m^{(a)},\widehat{m}^{(a)})

\displaystyle=(m,\widehat{m})~{\rm for~any}~a.

(6.25)

RS assumption restricts the extremum of
(
6.18
) to be in the space where
(
6.1
) and (
6.25
) hold.
Therefore,
it is always necessary to examine the validity of the solution under the RS
assumptions.
The RS formula at zero-temperature
derived here has been proven to be exact for convex likelihoods in
[
65
]
,
and that for Bayes-optimal inference was proven to be rigorous in
[
74
]
.
The RS assumption is known as to be equivalent with the assumption introduced
in the cavity approach
[
59
]
.

For the integral of
\theta
in (
6.20
) under the RS assumption, it is convenient to
introduce the transformation
using independent Gaussian random variables
w^{(a)}~(a\in\bm{n})
,

z
, and
v

\displaystyle\theta^{(a)}=\sqrt{Q-q}w^{(a)}+\sqrt{q}z,~(a\neq 0)

(6.26)

\displaystyle\theta^{(0)}=\sqrt{\rho\sigma_{x}^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{q}}v+\frac{m}{\sqrt{q}}z,

(6.27)

where this transformation preserves the relationships
(
6.8
)–(
6.10
).
We denote the functions

6.4
Instability of RS solution

The RS solutions discussed so far lose
local stability when subjected to perturbations
that break the symmetry between replicas in a certain parameter region.
This phenomenon is known as de Almeida-Thouless (AT) instability
[
71
]

and occurs when the following conditions hold;

\displaystyle\alpha\int Dzdx^{(0)}\phi^{(0)}(x^{(0)})\left(\frac{\partial x_{\mathrm{RS}}^{*}(z,x^{(0)})}{\partial\sqrt{\widehat{\chi}}z}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int D\nu D\zeta\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\sqrt{Q-\frac{m^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}}\zeta}\left({\cal G}\left(\sigma_{T}\nu\right)-a^{\prime}(u_{\mathrm{RS}}^{*}(\nu,\zeta))\right)\right)^{2}>1.

(6.71)

The derivation of the AT instability condition is explained in
E.3
,
by introducing the solution under the 1 step replica symmetry breaking assumption.
Based on the correspondence between AMP and the RS saddle point
discussed so far and summarized in Table
1
,
the AT instability condition (
6.71
)
is coincident with the local instability condition of
the GAMP fixed point.
Similar correspondence has been shown in the analysis for CDMA
[
57
]
.

Summary and Conclusion

We discussed prediction errors, generalization gaps and their estimators
in GLM.
We derived the form of
C_{p}
and WAIC using GAMP, and we
discussed the relationship between them.
We showed that GDF and FV, which are the estimators of the generalization gap,
can be expressed by the variance of the model parameter in the framework of GAMP.

We focused on the differences between the LOOCV error and WAIC or TIC at the
finite
\alpha
region.
The first-order expansion of the LOOCV error with respect to the
difference between the estimates under the full and LOO samples
is insufficient for describing the LOOCV error
for the finite
\alpha
.
Thus, we derived the difference between the LOOCV error and WAIC
within the framework of GAMP by considering the higher order terms;
we showed that the difference can be
described by the cumulant generating function of the likelihood.
The resultant form can be calculated under full data samples,
without considering LOO samples.
Further, we extended GAMP-based estimators of the prediction errors
to deal with correlated predictors and showed that these estimators exhibit a good agreement with
LOOCV errors calculated by the linear algebraic form or coordinate descent algorithm.

We show that GAMP or replica method describes
the prediction accuracy of the model
by using the variance of the parameter at equilibrium,
which relationship can be regarded as an exemplification of
fluctuation-response relationship
in the prediction based modeling
[
20
]
.
The concept of the prediction-based modeling is
compatible with that in statistical physics.
The derivation of such a relationship in this study
is restricted to the Gaussian predictors,
whose components independently and identically
distributed according to the Gaussian distribution.
However, this setting is not as restrictive as it appears.
In fact, recent studies have shown Gaussian universality for
GLMs with a large class of features,
wherein their asymptotic behaviors can be computed to leading order
under a simpler model with Gaussian features

[
65
,
75
,
76
,
77
]
.
Utilizing this correspondence,
the statistical physics-based method is expected to be
a valuable tool for analyzing the statistical methods to find limitation
in the conventional statistical theory, analyze the
\alpha\sim O(1)
region,
and construct efficient algorithms.

.

Appendix B
Generalized linear models

B.1
Logistic regression with Bernoulli distribution

The logistic regression on the
Bernoulli distribution is defined for binary data
y_{\mu}\in\{0,1\}
as

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\pi})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\left\{y_{\mu}\log\pi_{\mu}+(1-y_{\mu})\log(1-\pi_{\mu})\right\}

(B.1)

where the Bernoulli parameter
\pi_{\mu}
provides the mean of
y_{\mu}
,
and the probability that
y_{\mu}
is 1.
In the logistic regression, the model parameter

\bm{\theta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{F}\bm{x}

is connected to the Bernoulli parameter as

\displaystyle\pi_{\mu}=\ell^{-1}({\theta}_{\mu})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\theta_{\mu})},

(B.2)

using the link function
\ell(\cdot)
. Therefore,

\ell(\pi_{\mu})=\ln\left(\pi_{\mu}/\penalty(1-\pi_{\mu})\right)
.
Using (
B.2
),
the distribution (
B.1
) is transformed into the form
of the exponential family distribution

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{y}-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\log(1+e^{\theta_{\mu}}),

(B.3)

where functions
a
and
b
respectively correspond to

\displaystyle a(\bm{\theta})=\log\prod_{\mu=1}^{M}(1+e^{\theta_{\mu}}),~~~b(\bm{y})=0.

(B.4)

The maximization problem in GAMP (
4.30
)
for the logistic regression is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{\mu}^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta_{\mu}}\left\{-\theta_{\mu}(1-y_{\mu})-\log(1+e^{-\theta_{\mu}})-\frac{(\theta_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu})^{2}}{2{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}\right\},

(B.5)

and the estimate
\theta_{\mu}^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{\mu}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{\setminus\mu}}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}=y_{\mu}-\frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta_{\mu}^{*}}}.

(B.6)

The estimated mean of
y_{\mu}
is given by
\pi_{\mu}^{*}\equiv(1+e^{-\theta_{\mu}^{*}})^{-1}
, and the variance is
\pi_{\mu}^{*}(1-\pi_{\mu}^{*})
; therefore, we get

\displaystyle(\check{g}_{\rm out})_{\mu}

\displaystyle=y_{\mu}-\pi_{\mu}^{*},~~~(\partial_{\widehat{\theta}}\check{g}_{\rm out})_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{\mu}^{*}(1-\pi_{\mu}^{*})}{1+{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}\pi_{\mu}^{*}(1-\pi_{\mu}^{*})}.

(B.7)

B.2
Poisson regression

Poisson distribution is defined for
y_{\mu}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers including zero as

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\left\{y_{\mu}\ln\lambda_{\mu}-\lambda_{\mu}-\log y_{\mu}!\right\},

(B.8)

where
\lambda_{\mu}
denotes the mean of
y_{\mu}
.
In Poisson regression, the model parameter
\bm{\theta}
is
connected to the mean as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mu}=\ell^{-1}(\theta_{\mu})=\exp(\theta_{\mu}),

(B.9)

and hence,
\ell(\lambda_{\mu})=\log(\lambda_{\mu})
.
This link function reduces Poisson distribution to the exponential family’s form as

\displaystyle a(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\exp(\theta_{\mu}),~~~b(\bm{y})=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\log y_{\mu}!.

(B.10)

The maximization problem in GAMP (
4.30
)
for the Poisson regression is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{\mu}^{*}=\max_{\theta}\left\{y_{\mu}\theta_{\mu}-\exp(\theta_{\mu})-\frac{(u_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}^{\setminus\mu}_{\mu})^{2}}{2{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}\right\},

(B.11)

and the solution
\theta^{*}_{\mu}
satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{\mu}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}^{\setminus\mu}_{\mu}}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}=y_{\mu}-\lambda_{\mu}^{*},

(B.12)

where
\lambda_{\mu}^{*}\equiv\exp(u_{\mu}^{*})
is the estimated average.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle(\check{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}

\displaystyle=y_{\mu}-\lambda_{\mu}^{*},~~~(\check{\partial}_{\widehat{\theta}}{g}_{\mathrm{out}})_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\mu}^{*}}{1+{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}^{*}}.

(B.13)

B.3
Exponential regression

Exponential distribution with mean
\lambda_{\mu}

is defined for
y_{\mu}\in[0,\infty)
as

\displaystyle\ln f(\bm{y}|\bm{\eta})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\left\{-\frac{y_{\mu}}{\eta_{\mu}}-\log(\eta_{\mu})\right\}.

(B.14)

In the exponential regression, the model parameter

\bm{\theta}
relates to the mean as

\displaystyle-\eta_{\mu}=\ell^{-1}(\theta_{\mu}),

(B.15)

and hence,
\ell(\theta_{\mu})=-\eta_{\mu}^{-1}
,
where we introduce the factor
-1
for convenience.
Using the parameter
\bm{\theta}
,
the exponential distribution is rewritten as an exponential family
with functions
a
and
b
given by

\displaystyle a(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\log(-\theta_{\mu}),~~~b(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=0.

(B.16)

The maximization problem in GAMP (
4.30
)
for exponential regression is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{\mu}^{*}=\max_{\theta}\left\{y_{\mu}\theta_{\mu}+\ln(-\theta_{\mu})-\frac{(\theta_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}^{\setminus\mu}_{\mu})^{2}}{2{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}\right\},

(B.17)

and the solution satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{\mu}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}^{\setminus\mu}_{\mu}}{{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}}=y_{\mu}+\frac{1}{\theta_{\mu}}.

(B.18)

The estimated mean and variance
corresponds to
\eta_{\mu}^{*}\equiv-(\theta_{\mu}^{*})^{-1}

and

, respectively; hence

\displaystyle(\check{g}_{\rm out})_{\mu}=y_{\mu}-\eta_{\mu}^{*},~~~(\partial_{\widehat{\theta}}\check{g}_{\rm out})_{\mu}=\frac{(\theta_{\mu}^{*})^{-2}}{1+{s_{\theta}}_{\mu}(\theta_{\mu}^{*})^{-2}}.

(B.19)

B.4
Cumulant generating function for exponential family distribution

The moment-generating function for the exponential family distribution is given by

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{{\cal L}(\theta)}(t)

\displaystyle=\int dy\exp(ty)f(y|\theta)

(B.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(a(\theta+t))}{\exp(a(\theta))}\int dy\exp((t+\theta)y-a(t+\theta)+b(y)).

Hence, the cumulant generating function

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{{\cal L}(\theta)}(t)=a(\theta+t)-a(\theta).

(B.21)

The

denoted by
\kappa_{k}[f(\cdot|\theta)]
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{k}[f(\cdot|\theta)]=\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial t^{k}}{\cal C}_{f(\cdot|\theta)}(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial\theta^{k}}a(\theta),

(B.22)

hence the derivative of

corresponds to the cumulant.

Appendix D
Coordinate descent algorithm

Coordinate descent (CD) is an algorithm that can optimise each variable
successively where other variables are fixed.
The CD algorithm is efficient for penalised regression problems, and
partially for non-convex optimization problems
[
78
,
79
]
.

D.1
Gaussian likelihood with penalty

We focus on the variable
x_{i}
and optimise it
under the fixed variables
x_{j}=\widehat{x}_{j}
where
j\in\bm{N}\setminus i
.
For Gaussian likelihood,
we rewrite the function to be minimised as

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\!\left\{-2\frac{y_{\mu}}{\sqrt{N}}F_{\mu i}x_{i}\!+\!\frac{1}{N}F_{\mu i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\!+\!\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}F_{\mu i}x_{i}\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{-i}\!\right\}\!-\!h(x_{i})\!+\!\mathrm{const.},

(D.1)

where we set

\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{-i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j\neq i}F_{\mu j}\widehat{x}_{j}
.
In the case of the elastic net penalty,
the maximiser of (
D.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i}=\frac{b_{i}-\lambda_{1}\mathrm{sgn}(b_{i})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F_{\mu i}^{2}+\lambda_{2}}\mathbb{I}\left(|b_{i}|>\lambda_{1}\right)

(D.2)

where

\displaystyle b_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}(y_{\mu}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mu}^{-i})F_{\mu i}.

(D.3)

One can obtain the estimate by repeating this procedure for
i\in\bm{N}

sufficient time steps.
