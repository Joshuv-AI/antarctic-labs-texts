1.
Introduction

1.1.
Definitions and Background

The analysis of various parameters of random rooted trees is a classic subject if those parameters relate to a near-root part of a tree or to
global tree structure. See
[
11
]
for a comprehensive treatment of these results. A more recent area of interest is the random tree
fringe, that is, the part close to the leaves. A list of articles on this subject can be found in
[
6
]
.

A particularly interesting problem is the enumeration of vertices of a given
rank
.
If
v
is a vertex of a tree
T
, then let the
rank
of
v
be the number of edges in the
shortest path from
v
to a leaf of
T
that is a
descendant
of
v
. So leaves are of rank 0, parents of leaves are of rank 1, and so on.
Let us fix a variety of trees, such as binary search trees, or non-plane binary trees, or increasing trees. Let
n
be a fixed positive integer,
and let us compute the probability that a randomly selected vertex of a randomly selected tree of size
n
is of rank
k
. What can we say about this probability as
n
goes to infinity?

This question for various models of random trees has been the subject of vigorous research in the last few years.
Perhaps the first was a random binary search tree process
\{T_{n}\}
: conditioned on a binary tree
T_{n}
with
n
vertices,
T_{n+1}
is obtained by selecting uniformly at random (u.a.r.) one of
n
available positions for a new leaf and putting the next vertex into it. It was proved in
[
5
]
that for any fixed
k
, the probability in question converges to a rational number
c_{k}
, and
c_{k}
was computed for
k\leq 3
. Then in
[
6
]
, the present authors computed
c_{4}
and
c_{5}
, and proved lower and upper bounds for

c_{k}
in general. They proved that as
k
grows,
c_{k}
exponentially decays. They also showed that the ranks of the uniformly random, fixed size, sample of vertices are asymptotically independent. For increasing non-plane trees where the
next vertex is attached to a previous vertex selected u.a.r. from the existing vertices, known also as recursive trees, ranks were extensively studied by Holmgren and Janson
in
[
16
]
. They also studied ternary trees from this aspect
[
15
]
. In
[
7
]
, the first author and István Mező proved the existence of the limits
c_{k}
for non-plane 1-2 trees and plane 1-2-trees.

A natural tree variety that is missing from the above list is
plane increasing trees
. These are rooted plane trees whose vertices are bijectively labeled with the elements of
[n]
so that each vertex has a

larger
label than its parent, and the children of every parent form an
ordered
set. This tree variety is the subject of our paper. As these are the only kind of trees that will appear in this
work, we will often simply call these rooted plane increasing trees just
trees
. These trees have been studied under various names, such as
plane-oriented trees

[
17
,
21
]
,
PORT
s (see page 14 of
[
9
]
),
or scale free trees
[
1
]
.

Given a tree with
n-1
vertices of
total
degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}
, there are

d_{\text{root}}+1+\sum_{j\in[2,n-1]}d_{j}=2n-3
different ways to attach vertex
n
to a vertex in
[n-1]
.
Indeed, there are
d_{\text{root}}+1
ways to attach
n
to the root, and
d_{j}
ways to attach
n
to any other non-root vertex
j\in[2,n-1]
. So the total number of these trees
is
(2n-3)!!:=1\cdot 3\cdot\cdots\cdot(2n-3)
.
Our tree
T_{n}
is chosen u.a.r. from the set of all
(2n-3)!!
trees. We can view
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
as snapshots of the tree
growing (Markov) process: conditioned on
T_{n-1}
, vertex
n
becomes a child of a vertex
j\in[n-1]
taking up one of

d_{j}

possible positions in the ordered set of the
other children of
j
. So, unlike in recursive trees, a host vertex for the vertex
n
is chosen from existing vertices with probability proportional to total degree of
that
potential host. The second author
[
25
]
proved that
the
height, that is, the
length of the longest path from the root to a leaf of this tree, scaled by
\log n
converges, almost surely, to
1.795560738
, which is the root of the equation
2y/e=\exp(1/2y)
. Later, Drmota
[
8
]
extended this result to a broad variety of increasing
trees introduced by Bergeron, Flajolet, and Salvy
[
2
]
.

In a different direction,
\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a well-known case of the random tree (graph, if cycles allowed) process with “preferential attachment”. See Barabási and Albert
[
1
]
, Móri
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
, Bollobás and Riordan
[
4
]
,
Bollobás
[
3
]
, van der Hofstad
[
14
]
, Frieze and Karoński
[
10
]
, Pittel
[
26
]
.

1.2.
Our Main Results

As we mentioned above, in this paper, the tree variety we study is that of increasing plane trees. In Section
3
, we prove that for this tree variety, the numbers
c_{k}
exist for all
k
, and that
c_{k}
is equal to the value of a certain integral. We provide the explicit value of this integral for
k=0
and
k=1
, and its approximate value (to eleven decimals) for

k=2
and
k=3
.
We also prove a non-obvious result that
\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}=1
, so the numbers
c_{k}
form a probability
distribution.

In order to see that this fact is not obvious and has to be proved, consider the trivial variety of trees in which every non-leaf vertex has exactly one child.
In that case, the ratio of vertices of rank
k
in a tree of size
n
is
c_{k}(n)=1/n
if
k<n
, so
c_{k}=0
for any fixed
k
. That is, for this tree variety,
\sum_{k}c_{k}=0<1
.

For an arbitrary tree variety, only the following holds. As
\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(n)=1
, it follows that
for each fixed bound
B
, we have

\sum_{k\leq B}c_{k}(n)\leq 1
,
and taking limits as
n
goes to infinity,

\sum_{k=0}^{B}c_{k}\leq 1
. Now taking limits as
B
goes to infinity,
we get

\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}\leq 1
. So the sum of the
c_{k}
is never more than 1, i.e.
\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}\in[0,1]
. To show for a particular increasing tree that
\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}=1
, one has to prove tightness condition, namely that
\sum_{k=K}^{n}c_{k}(n)
can be made arbitrarily small for all
n
by selecting a
constant

K
that is sufficiently large. Less formally, one has to show that, as a tree in question grows indefinitely, with high probability no
substantial portion of the vertices will have higher and higher ranks, exceeding any fixed
k
. That’s what we will prove
for the tree growing process studied in this paper.

In fact, we will prove not only that
\sum_{k}c_{k}=1
, but also the stronger statement that

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\leq\tfrac{3^{k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
.
So the tail of the limiting rank distribution is super-exponentially narrow.
The latter property holds uniformly for all finite
n
as well.
These results together imply that as
n
goes to infinity, the limiting probability that a random vertex of a random tree
has rank four or more is approximately 0.0002843360. So about 99.97 percent of all vertices have rank three or less.

Using our results on the expected number of vertices of a given rank, we are able to prove lower and upper bounds on the
highest rank

R_{n}
of a vertex in a tree of size
n
selected
uniformly at random.
First, in Theorem
3.3
, we show that, for each
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality
R_{n}\leq(1.5+\epsilon)\log n/\log\log n
holds with probability
1-n^{-\varepsilon-o(1)}
. (Bollobás and Riordan
[
3
]
proved that with probability
1-o(1)
, the diameter of the preferential attachment tree is asymptotic to
\log n/\log\log n
).
Later, in Theorem
3.11
, focusing on vertices whose descendant tree is a path we prove that if
0<\epsilon<1
, then with probability
1-n^{-\epsilon}
, there exist
\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
vertices with
rank
k=(1-\epsilon)\log n/\log\log n
. (This result obviously implies Bollobás-Riordan’s lower bound for the diameter.) In other words, with probability
1-o(1)
, the highest rank is asymptotic to
\log n/\log\log n
.

In Section
3.2
, we turn to the counting of
r
-tuples of vertices with given ranks. We are able to prove that the ranks of a finite, uniformly random, set of vertices
are asymptotically independent. In other words, if

R_{n}(1),R_{n}(2),\cdots,R_{n}(r)
denote the ranks of
r
vertices, chosen uniformly at random, with order and without replacement, from the
set of
n
vertices of a random tree, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}(R_{n}(i)=k_{i})\right)=(1+O(n^{-1}))\prod_{j=1}^{r}c_{k_{j}}.

2.
Counting vertices by ranks via generating functions

2.1.
Individual vertices and tuples of vertices

The following result is well-known
[
21
]
, and we have proved it in a purely combinatorial way in the introduction. Nevertheless, here we include another proof, to show the method we will use later in more complicated circumstances.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
. Then the number
t_{n}
of all rooted increasing plane trees on vertex set
[n]
is given
by the formula
t(n)=(2n-3)!!
.

Proof.

Note that
t(1)=1
and for
n\geq 2
we have the recurrence

(2.1)

t(n)=\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{s}=n-1}\frac{(n-1)!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{s}!}\prod_{i=1}^{s}t(j_{s}),\quad(t(0):=0),

where
s
is the number of the children of the root. Multiplying both sides by
z^{n-1}/(n-1)!
, summing over
n\geq 2
and adding
z\,t(1)
, we obtain that the power series
T(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{t(n)}{n!}z^{n}
satisfies the equation

(2.2)

T^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}T^{j}(z)=(1-T(z))^{-1},\quad T(0)=0,

if
|T(z)|<1
. The solution of this differential equation is
T(z)=1-\sqrt{1-2z}
, where we use the main branch of the square root,
and
|T(z)|<1
if
|z|<1/2
. It follows that

\frac{t(n)}{n!}=-[z^{n}](1-2z)^{1/2}=\frac{(2n-3)!!}{n!}\longrightarrow t(n)=(2n-3)!!.

∎

Note.

We could have used the symbolic method (Flajolet and Sedgewick
[
11
]
) and obtain (
2.2
) directly, but we decided to present an explicit, admittedly pedestrian, argument where we start with tree-based recurrence for the tree counts in question and then derive recurrences for
generating functions. This short proof will serve as a self-contained template for our considerably more technical proofs coming next, especially when we will turn to counting tuples of vertices of given ranks.

Notation.
Suppose
|z|<1/2
. Let
A_{k}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{k}(n)z^{n}/n!
be the (exponential) generating function for the number of all vertices of rank
k
in all rooted plane trees on vertex set
[n]
.
Let
B_{k}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}b_{k}(n)z^{n}/n!
be the generating function for the number of trees in which
the root
is of rank
k
, so we have
B_{0}(z)=z
.
We will also need

B_{\geq k}(z)
, the generating function for the number of trees in which the root is of rank
\geq k
. So we have
B_{\geq 0}(z)=T(z)
, and

B_{\geq 1}(z)
is the generating function of numbers of trees with at least two vertices, i.e.
B_{\geq 1}(z)=T(z)-z
.
We will use the
inequality

B_{\geq k+1}(z)\leq B_{\geq k}(z)
to express the fact that for all
n
, the coefficient
[z^{n}]B_{\geq k}(z)
decreases as
k
increases.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
k
, the generating functions
A_{k}(z)
and
B_{k}(z)
satisfy

(2.3)

\displaystyle A_{k}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{A_{k}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{2}}

\displaystyle+B_{k}^{\prime}(z),\,\,B_{k}(z)=B_{\geq k}(z)-B_{\geq k+1}(z),\,\,k\geq 0,

\displaystyle B_{\geq k}^{\prime}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{B_{\geq k-1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq k-1}(z)},\,\,k\geq 1,\,\,B_{\geq 0}(z)=T(z).

Proof.

Let
X_{k}(n)
stand for the total number of rank
k
vertices in the uniformly random tree, so that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]=\tfrac{a_{k}(n)}{t(n)}
. Let
p_{k}(n)
, (resp.
p_{\geq k}(n)
) stand for probability that the root
1
is of rank
k
(resp. rank
\geq k
). So, the formulas
b_{k}(n)=t(n)p_{k}(n)
and
b_{\geq k}(n)=t(n)p_{\geq k}(n)
provide the total number of trees on
[n]
with root of rank
k
and of
rank
\geq k
, respectively.
For
k\geq 1
, the root is of rank at least
k
if and only if the root has children and each of them is of rank at least
k-1
. Therefore, for
n\geq 2
,

(2.4)

p_{\geq k}(n)=\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{s}=n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})\,p_{\geq k-1}(j_{r})}{j_{s}!},\quad j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\geq 1.

So,
B_{\geq k}(z)
and
B_{k}(z)
, the exponential generating functions of
\{b_{\geq k}(n)\}
and
\{b_{k}(n)\}
, satisfy

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 1}(B_{\geq k-1}(z))^{s}=\tfrac{B_{\geq k-1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq k-1}(z)},\,\,k\geq 1;\quad B_{\geq 0}(z)=T(z),

\displaystyle B_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=B_{\geq k}(z)-B_{\geq k+1}(z),\quad k\geq 0.

The series
B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(z)
above converges for
|z|<1/2
and
k\geq 1
because

|B_{\geq k-1}(z))|\leq B_{\geq k-1}(|z|)\leq B_{\geq 0}(|z|)=T(|z|)<T(1/2)=1.

Let us turn to
A_{k}(z)
. Now, in the random tree
X_{k}(n)
equals
\mathbb{I}(\text{root is of rank }k)
plus the total sum of all vertices of rank
k
in the subtrees rooted at the children of the root. The probability that the root has
s
children with ordered subtrees of sizes
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}
is

\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})}{j_{r}!}.

So, by linearity of expectation, for
n\geq 2
, the following holds.

(2.5)

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]=p_{k}(n)\\
+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{s}=n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})}{j_{r}!}\biggl(\sum_{r^{\prime}=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(j_{r^{\prime}})]\biggr).

Multiplying both sides by
\tfrac{t(n)z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
, and summing over
n\geq 2
, we obtain

\tfrac{d}{dz}\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{t(n)\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]}{n!}\,z^{n}=\tfrac{d}{dz}\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{t(n)p_{k}(n)}{n!}\,z^{n}\\
+\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{r=1}^{s}\sum_{j_{r}\geq 1}\tfrac{t(j_{r})E[X_{k}(j_{r})]}{j_{r}!}\,z^{j_{r}}\sum_{j_{r^{\prime}}\geq 1,\,r^{\prime}\neq r}\,\,\prod_{r"\neq r}\tfrac{t(j_{r")}}{j_{r"}!}\,z^{j_{r"}},

or equivalently

A^{\prime}_{k}(z)=B^{\prime}_{k}(z)+A_{k}(z)\sum_{s\geq 1}sT^{s-1}(z)=B^{\prime}_{k}(z)+\tfrac{A_{k}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{2}}.

∎

and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{\geq k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}(n)]=\tfrac{b_{\geq k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}(n)}{t(n)}
.

Let us write a recurrence for
\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}}(n)]
. The
r
-long tuples
\mathbb{v}
that contain the root
1
contribute
\sum_{u\in[r]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}\bigr]
to this expectation. (The
u
-th term corresponds to the case when
k_{u}
, i.e. the
u
-th component of
\mathbb{k}
, is the root’s rank.) Consider the tuples
\mathbb{v}
that do not contain the root
1
. Let
s\geq 1
be a generic number of the root’s ordered children, and let
[r]
be a disjoint, union of
s
subsets, some of which may be empty. This ordered partition of
[r]
determines the partition of the ordered tuple
\mathbb{k}
into
s

sub-tuples
\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(s)}
of total length
r
, where each sub-tuple inheriting the order of
{\bf k}
. Conditioned on
s
and the sizes
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}
of the subtrees rooted at
s
children of the root
1
, the subtrees are independent. Therefore, the conditional expected number of
r
-long tuples of rank
\mathbb{k}
, composed of
s
sub-tuples of ranks
\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(s)}
,
is
\prod_{u=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(j_{u})]
. Here
\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(j_{u})]:=1
if
\mathbb{k}^{(u)}=\emptyset
. Therefore, for
n\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}}(n)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in[r]}\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}(n)\bigr]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{\mathbb{k}^{(1)}\!,\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(s)}}\,\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{s}=n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})}{j_{r}!}\prod_{u=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(j_{u})].

Using
t(n)\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathbb{k}}(n)]=a_{\mathbb{k}}(n)
with
a_{\emptyset}(n):=t(n)
and
t(n)\mathbb{E}[Y_{\mathbb{k}}(n)]=b_{\mathbb{k}}(n)
, then multiplying the resulting equation by

\tfrac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
, and summing over
n\geq 2
, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{\mathbb{k}}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in[r]}B^{\prime}_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}(z)+\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(s)}}\prod_{u=1}^{s}A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z),

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in[r]}B^{\prime}_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}(z)+\sum_{\tau\geq 1}(1-T(z))^{-\tau-1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\mathbb{k}^{(1)}\!,\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)}\neq\emptyset}\,\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z).

Explanation: The roots of
\tau
subtrees that contain non-empty sub-tuples of vertices of ranks
\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)}
form
\tau+1

consecutive intervals. Therefore,
(1-T(z))^{-\tau-1}
is the EGF of finite forests of ordered trees whose roots are hosted by these intervals.

Let
\mathbb{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})=:(k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime})
. To handle
B_{\mathbb{k}}(z)
, introduce
B_{\geq k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}(z)
, the generating function of the numbers of occurrences of
\mathbb{v}=(\text{root},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})
=:(\text{root},\mathbb{v}^{\prime})
such that the root is of rank
\geq k_{1}
, and
\mathbb{v}^{\prime}
is of
rank
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
. Clearly
B_{\mathbb{k}}(z)\leq B_{\geq k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}(z)
. Analogously to the case of
A_{\mathbb{k}}(z)
, we obtain

(2.6)

B_{\geq k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}^{\prime}(z)\leq\sum_{\tau\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1-T(z))^{\tau+1}}\biggl(\,\sum_{\cup_{u=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{k}^{(u)}=\mathbb{k}^{\prime};\atop\mathbb{k}^{(u)}\neq\emptyset}\,\,\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z)\biggr).

Here
\tau
is the number of children of the root
1
whose subtrees split among themselves the ordered
(r-1)
-tuple
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
according to a generic ordered partition of
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
into

\tau
non-empty sub-tuples
\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)}
, each ordered in compliance with the ordering of
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
. Note that (
2.6
) is an inequality, because we
neglected the condition that the roots of all the subtrees, rooted at the children of the root
1
, should have ranks
\geq k_{1}-1
as well.
∎

3.
Asymptotics for the uniformly random tree
T_{n}

We start this section by a series of lemmas that will allow us to prove an upper bound for the expected number
of vertices of rank at least
k
in the uniformly random tree in Theorem
3.3
. We then turn to the expected number of vertices of rank exactly
k
, and prove an asymptotic formula for that number in Theorem
3.4
.
That theorem will show that this number is asymptotically equal to
c_{k}n
, for a positive constant
c_{k}
. We compute the values of
c_{k}
for
k\leq 3
, and then, in Theorem
3.5
, we prove an upper bound for

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}
, which proves that the numbers
c_{j}
form a probability distribution.

3.1.
Counting individual vertices by rank in
T_{n}

Recall that
A_{k}(z)
is the (exponential) generating function of all occurrences of vertices of rank
k
in all the trees on
[n]
,

n\geq 1
, and
B_{k}(z)
is the generating function of number of trees with root of rank
k
. We start with a simple, but
instrumental lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For

z\in\mathbb{C}
and
|z|<1/2
, we have

A_{k}(z)=\frac{I_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}},\quad I_{k}(z):=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi,

where the integral is taken over any path from
0
to
z
within the disc
|\xi|<1/2
.

Proof.

According to Proposition
2.2
, we have

A_{k}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{A_{k}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{2}}+B_{k}^{\prime}(z),\quad|z|<1/2.

Since
T^{\prime}(z)=(1-T(z))^{-1}
, we have
(1-T(z))^{-2}=T^{\prime\prime}(z)/T^{\prime}(z)
, whence

A^{\prime}_{k}(z)=\frac{T^{\prime\prime}(z)}{T^{\prime}(z)}A_{k}(z)+B^{\prime}_{k}(z)\Longrightarrow\left(\frac{A_{k}(z)}{T^{\prime}(z)}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{B_{k}^{\prime}(z)}{T^{\prime}(z)}=(1-T(z))B_{k}^{\prime}(z),

where
1-T(z)=\sqrt{1-2z}
. Our claim is now proved by integration.
∎

Let us introduce
A_{\geq k}(z)=\sum_{j\geq k}A_{j}(z)
, the exponential generating function for the number of all vertices of rank
\geq k
in all rooted trees on vertex set
[n]
. By Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

(3.1)

A_{\geq k}(z)=\frac{I_{\geq k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}},\quad I_{\geq k}(z):=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{\geq k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Lemma 3.2
.

For

z\in\mathbb{C}
and
|z|<1/2
and
k>2
, we have
|B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(z)|\leq 1/(k-2)!
. Consequently,

|I_{\geq k}(z)|\leq 1/(k-2)!
.

Proof.

Let

z\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in[0,1/2)
. By Proposition
2.2
, we have: for
\ell\geq 2
,

\displaystyle B_{\geq\ell}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{\geq\ell-1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq\ell-1}(z)}\leq\frac{B_{\geq\ell-1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq 1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{\geq\ell-1}(z)}{1+z-T(z)}\leq 2B_{\geq\ell-1}(z).

In the last step, we used the fact that if
z\in[0,1/2)
, then
z-T(z)\geq-1/2
.

Integrating
both sides of the resulting inequality
B_{\geq\ell}^{\prime}(z)\leq 2B_{\geq\ell-1}(z)
, by induction we obtain

B_{\geq\ell}(z)\leq\frac{2^{\ell-2}z^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}.

Hence, for
k>2
, we have

B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(z)\leq 2B_{\geq k-1}(z)\leq\frac{(2z)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}\leq\frac{1}{(k-2)!}.

So, for

z\in\mathbb{C}
and
|z|<1/2
, we have

|B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(z)|\leq B^{\prime}_{\geq k}(|z|)\leq\frac{1}{(k-2)!},\quad|\sqrt{1-2z}|\leq\sqrt{2}.

It follows that
|I_{\geq k}(z)|\leq 2^{-1/2}/(k-2)!\leq 1/(k-2)!
.
∎

Notation.
To proceed, recall that
X_{k}(n)
and
X_{\geq k}(n)
stand for the total number of vertices of rank
k

and of rank
\geq k
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}
stand for the largest rank of a vertex in
T_{n}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Uniformly for
k\leq n
, we have

\mathbb{E}[X_{\geq k}(n)]=O(n^{3/2}/(k-2)!)
.
Consequently,
\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\mathcal{R}_{n}\leq(1.5+\varepsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\Bigr)=1-O(n^{-\varepsilon+o(1)})
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy integral theorem, for a circular contour
C
of radius
\rho<1/2
centered at zero, we have

\frac{a_{\geq k}(n)}{n!}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{z\in C}\frac{I_{\geq k}(z)}{z^{n+1}\sqrt{1-2z}}\,dz.\\

Let
C^{*}
be the circle
|z|=1/2
.
Then letting
C
tend to
C^{*}
, substituting
z=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}
, and using Lemma
3.2
, we have

\tfrac{a_{\geq k}(n)}{n!}\leq\tfrac{2^{n+2}}{2\pi(k-2)!}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|1-e^{i\theta}|^{-1/2}\,d\theta=\tfrac{2^{n+3/2}}{2\pi(k-2)!}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{-1/2}(\theta/2)\,d\theta\\
\leq\tfrac{2^{n+2}}{2\pi(k-2)!}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\theta^{-1/2}\,d\theta\leq\tfrac{2^{n+1}}{(k-2)!}.

So using
2^{2n}\binom{2n}{n}^{-1}\leq 2n^{1/2}
, we get

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}[X_{\geq k}(n)]=\frac{a_{\geq k}(n)}{(2n-3)!!}\leq\tfrac{2^{n+1}n!}{(2n-3)!!(k-2)!}\leq\tfrac{4n}{(k-2)!}\cdot\frac{2^{2n}}{\binom{2n}{n}}\leq\tfrac{8n^{3/2}}{(k-2)!}.

Since
X_{k}(n)\leq n
, this bound is useless if
k
is fixed, or grows very slowly as
n
grows. However,
setting
k_{n}=\lceil(1.5+\varepsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\rceil
,
\varepsilon>0
being arbitrary, and using
m!\geq(m/e)^{m}
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{n}\geq k_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{\geq k_{n}}(n)]=O\bigl(n^{1.5}(k_{n}/e)^{-k_{n}}\bigr)=O\bigl(n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\bigr),\quad\forall\,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon).

∎

Let us turn now to
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For every fixed
k
, we have

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]=c_{k}n\bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr),\quad c_{k}:=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\!\!\!\!\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, for
|z|<1/2
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n,k}]=\frac{a_{k}(n)}{(2n-3)!!}=\frac{n!}{(2n-3)!!}[z^{n}]A_{k}(z),

\displaystyle A_{k}(z)=\frac{I_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}},\quad I_{k}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\!\!\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Since
B_{0}(z)=z
, the integral
I_{0}(z)
is
\bigl(1-(1-2z)^{3/2}\bigr)/3
, so we view
I_{0}(z)
as being analytically extended to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z\in\mathbb{R}:z\in[1/2,\infty)\}
.

Further, recall that
B_{k}(z)=B_{\geq k}(z)-B_{\geq k+1}(z)
, and

(3.4)

B_{\geq j+1}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{B_{\geq j}(\xi)}{1-B_{\geq j}(\xi)}\,d\xi,\quad|z|<1/2,

and the integral is taken along any path from
0
to
z
in the circle
|z|<1/2
. (Recall that
|B_{j}(z)|<1
for
|z|<1/2
.) Simply by the definition of
B_{\geq 1}(z)
, we have

B_{\geq 1}(z)=B_{\geq 0}(z){\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-z}=\sum_{\nu\geq 2}\tfrac{t(\nu)}{\nu!}z^{\nu}=T(z)-z=1-\sqrt{1-2z}-z,

with
\sqrt{\xi}=|\xi|^{1|/2}e^{i\text{Arg}(\xi)/2}
,
\text{Arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi,\pi)
. The last expression defines an analytic extension of
B_{\geq 1}(z)
to

\mathbb{C}\setminus[1/2,\infty)
. For
|z|<1/2
, the series formula implies that

|B_{\geq 1}(z)|\leq B_{\geq 1}(|z|)\leq B_{\geq 1}(1/2)=T(1/2)-1/2=1/2.

Since
B_{\geq j+1}(z)\leq B_{\geq j}(z)
for those
z
’s, we obtain that
|B_{\geq j}(z)|\leq 1/2
for
j>1
and
|z|<1/2
.

So, by induction on
j
, it follows from (
3.4
) that for
|z|<1/2
and
j\geq 1
the following holds.

(a)

|B_{j}(z)|\leq 1/2
, whence the integral in (
3.4
) is well-defined;

(b)

|B^{\prime}_{\geq j+1}(z)|\leq 1
.

We use (
3.4
) to assert, by induction on
j
, the existence of the analytic extension of each
B_{\geq j}(z)
to the domain
D_{j}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<\rho_{j}\}\setminus[1/2,\rho_{j})
, where
\rho_{1}\geq\rho_{2}\geq\cdots>1/2
, such that—
keeping
B_{j}(z)
as notation for this extension—
|B_{\geq j}(z)|\leq 3/4
for
z\in D_{j}
, and (
3.4
) holds for
z\in D_{j+1}
.
We use
D_{j}
for
fixed

j
only. So, setting

\rho>1/2
equal to the minimum of
\rho_{j}
over the
finitely
many
j
’s in question, we may and will focus on the analytic continuations
B_{\geq}j(z)
to the same
D:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<\rho\}\setminus[1/2,\rho)
.

Consequently, each
B_{k}(z)
analytically extends to
D
.
Keeping
B_{k}(z)
and
I_{k}(z)
to denote the extensions of
B_{k}(z)

and the corresponding
I_{k}(z)
given by (
3.3
), we obtain the following.
Uniformly for
z\in D
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle I_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma_{k}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\lambda_{k,j}(1-2z)^{\tfrac{j+2}{2}},}

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1/2}\!\!\!\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi;

here the integral is taken over any path which is within
D
. Recall that
B_{1}(z)=z
, so in particular,
B_{1}^{\prime}(1/2)
does exist. A bounded factor implicit in the big-O notation here, and in what follows, may depend on
k
.

Let
C_{m}
be a contour that consists of the counter-clockwise circular arc
z=\rho e^{i\theta}
,
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
,
and a detour part formed by two opposite-directed line segments, one from
z=\rho e^{i((2-(1/m)\pi)}
to
z=1/2e^{i((2-(1/m))\pi)}

and another from
z=1/2e^{i(-1/m))}
back to
z=\rho e^{i(1/m)}
.

Now consider the contour
C
that
consists of the counter-clockwise circular arc
z=\rho e^{i\theta}
,
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
,
and a detour part formed by two opposite-directed line segments, one from
z=\rho e^{i(2\pi-0)}
to
z=1/2e^{i(2\pi-0)}

and another from
z=1/2e^{i(+0)}
back to
z=\rho e^{i(+0)}
. Then

C
is obviously a limit of the contours
C_{m}
, which are covered by the Cauchy theorem.
See Figure
for an illustration. (Readers can consult the Transfer theorem in

[
11
]
for more on this technique.)

Figure 1.
The contour
C
.

Applying this theorem and going to the limit,
we have, for
\alpha<1/2
,

[z^{n}]\frac{I_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=\alpha}\frac{I_{k}(z)}{z^{n+1}\sqrt{1-2z}}\,dz\\
=\gamma_{k}[z^{n}](1-2z)^{-1/2}+\lambda_{k,0}[z^{n}](1-2z)^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{z\in C}\frac{O\bigl(|1-2z|\bigr)}{z^{n+1}}\,dz.

Here

\gamma_{k}[z^{n}](1-2z)^{-1/2}+\lambda_{k,0}[z^{n}](1-2z)^{1/2}=\gamma_{k}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{n!}+\lambda_{k,0}\frac{(2n-3)!!}{n!}\\
=\gamma_{k}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{n!}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr)=\Theta\bigl(n^{-1/2}2^{n}\bigr).

and the remainder integral is of order

\int_{1/2}^{\rho}\frac{2\eta-1}{\eta^{n}}\,d\eta+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\leq n^{-2}2^{n}+O(\rho^{-n}).

We conclude that, for every fixed
k
,

(3.6)

[z^{n}]\frac{I_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}}=\gamma_{k}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{n!}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr).

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]=\frac{n!}{(2n-3)!!}\,[z^{n}]\frac{I_{k}(z)}{\sqrt{1-2z}}=(2n-1)\gamma_{k}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr),

implying that
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]=nc_{k}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1})\bigr)
,
c_{k}:=2\gamma_{k}
.
∎

3.1.1.
Computing
c_{k}
for
k\leq 3
.

Recall that
c_{k}=\lim n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]
, where

c_{k}=2\gamma_{k}=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\!\!\!\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,B_{k}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Here
B_{0}(z)=z
, and for
k\geq 1
we have the recurrence

B_{k}(z)=B_{\geq k}(z)-B_{\geq k+1}(z),\quad B^{\prime}_{\geq k}=\frac{B_{\geq k-1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq k-1}(z)},\quad|z|<1/2.

So,

c_{0}=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\!\!\!\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,d\xi=\frac{2}{3}.

Next, we compute

B_{1}^{\prime}(z)=B^{\prime}_{\geq 1}(z)-B^{\prime}_{\geq 2}(z)=\frac{B_{\geq 0}(z)}{1-B_{\geq 0}(z)}-\frac{B_{\geq 1}(z)}{1-B_{\geq 1}(z)};

here
B_{\geq 0}(z)=T(z)=1-\sqrt{1-2z}
, and

(3.7)

\displaystyle B_{\geq 1}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{T(\xi)}{1-T(\xi)}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1-\sqrt{1-2\xi}}{\sqrt{1-2\xi}}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=1-z-\sqrt{1-2z}.

Therefore

B_{1}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-2z}}{\sqrt{1-2z}}-\frac{1-z-\sqrt{1-2z}}{z+\sqrt{1-2z}}=\frac{z}{\sqrt{1-2z}\,(z+\sqrt{1-2z})}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\!\!\!\sqrt{1-2z}\,B_{1}^{\prime}(z)\,dz=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{z}{z+\sqrt{1-2z}}\,dz

\displaystyle=5+4\log 2-6\sqrt{2}\,\text{arctanh}(2^{-1/2})\approx 0.2938858406.

Next,

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{\geq 2}(z)-B^{\prime}_{\geq 3}(z)=\frac{1}{1-B_{\geq 1}(z)}-\frac{1}{1-B_{\geq 2}(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z+\sqrt{1-2z}}-\frac{1}{1-B_{\geq 2}(z)},

where

\displaystyle B_{\geq 2}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}\frac{B_{\geq 1}(y)}{1-B_{\geq 1}(y)}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-z+\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1}{y+\sqrt{1-2y}}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-z+\log(z+\sqrt{1-2z})-2^{1/2}\text{arctanh}\bigl(2^{-1/2}(\sqrt{1-2z}-1)\bigr).

So, using the two last formulas, we obtain

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2z}\,B_{2}^{\prime}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2z}\biggl(\frac{1}{z+\sqrt{1-2z}}-\frac{1}{1-B_{\geq 2}(z)}\biggr)\,dz

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.03589474655.

Let us now turn to

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2z}\,B_{3}^{\prime}(z)\,dz=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2z}\,\bigl(B_{\geq 3}^{\prime}(z)-B_{\geq 4}^{\prime}(z)\bigr)\,dz

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2z}\,\bigl((1-B_{\geq 2}(z))^{-1}-(1-B_{\geq 3}(z))^{-1}\bigr)\,dz.

To compute
c_{3}
, introduce

C_{3}(z)=2\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1-2y}\,\bigl((1-B_{\geq 2}(y))^{-1}-(1-B_{\geq 3}(y))^{-1}\bigr)\,dy.

Then
c_{3}=C_{3}(1/2)
and
C_{3}(z)
,
B_{\geq 3}(z)
satisfy a system of two ODEs,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{3}}{dz}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{1-2z}\,\bigl((1-B_{\geq 2}(z))^{-1}-(1-B_{\geq 3}(z))^{-1}\bigr),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{dB_{\geq 3}}{dz}

\displaystyle=(1-B_{\geq 2}(z))^{-1}-1,

with
C_{3}(0)=B_{\geq 3}(0)=0
, and
B_{\geq 2}(z)
being defined in the computation of
c_{2}
above.

of the integrals on each of intervals forming a partition of

, and used Matlab to show

if each interval has length

. Selecting

. We owe a debt of gratitude to Sara for her generous help.

3.1.2.
The numbers
\{c_{k}\}
form a proper distribution.

As we stated in the introduction, we will prove that
\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}=1
, so the numbers
c_{k}
form
a proper distribution. We saw in Section
1.2
that
\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}\leq 1
, so it suffices to show that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Bigl(1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\Bigr)=0.

We accomplish this by proving the following stronger statement.

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
k
, the inequality

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\leq\tfrac{3^{k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
holds.

Note.
So the limiting expected fraction of vertices having rank at least
4
is
0.0002843360\dots
exactly.

Proof.

(i)
Given fixed
k
, let
a_{\leq k}(n)
and
a_{>k}(n)
stand for the total number of
vertices of rank
\leq k
and
>k
in all trees on
[n]
, and let
X_{\leq k}(n)
,
X_{>k}(n)
stand for the number of vertices of rank
\leq k
and
>k
in the random tree.
So
t(n)\mathbb{E}[X_{\leq k}(n)]=a_{\leq k}(n)
and
t(n)\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]=a_{>k}(n)
. By (
3.1
),

(3.10)

A_{\leq k}(z)=(1-2z)^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1-2x}\,B_{\leq k}^{\prime}(x)\,dx.

The RHS of (
3.10
) is analytic within an open circle of a radius
\rho>1/2
minus a cut
[1/2,\rho)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\tfrac{(2n-3)!!\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\leq k}(n)]}{n!}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))[z^{n}](1-2z)^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2x}\,B_{\leq k}^{\prime}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\tfrac{(2n-1)!!}{n!}\int_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1-2x}\,B_{\leq k}^{\prime}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\tfrac{(2n-1)!!}{n!}\int_{0}^{1/2}T^{\prime}(x)B_{\leq k}(x)\,dx.

Using

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\leq k}(n)]=n-\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)],\quad B_{\leq k}(x)+B_{>k}(x)=T(x),

\displaystyle c_{j}=\lim n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}(n)],\quad 2\int_{0}^{1/2}T^{\prime}(x)T(x)\,dx=1,

we obtain

(3.11)

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\tfrac{B_{>k}(x)}{1-T(x)}\,dx.

Recall that, for
|z|<1/2
, the power series
B_{>k}(z)
is the EGF generating function of
b_{>k}(n)
, where
b_{>k}(n)
is the total number of trees in which the root is of rank
>k
. Clearly we need to find a good bound for
b_{>k}(n)
, or equivalently for
p_{>k}(n)=b_{>k}(n)/t(n)
, the probability that the root of the random tree has rank more than
k
. To get such a bound we need a provably well-performing randomized algorithm which is likely to find a competitively short path from the root to a leaf.

Here is the algorithm. If there are
s\geq 2
children of the root, we keep a single tree, which we select with probability proportional to the total size of the deleted subtrees. For
n\geq 1
,
k\geq-1
, let
\pi_{>k}(n)
denote the probability that edge length of the resulting path strictly exceeds
k
; obviously
p_{>k}(n)\leq\pi_{>k}(n)
. Further,
\pi_{>-1}(n)=1
, and for
n>1
,
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\pi_{>k}(n)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{t(n-1)}{t(n)}\,\pi_{>k-1}(n-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 2}\sum_{r=1}^{s}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{s}=n-1}\!\!\tfrac{\sum_{r^{\prime}\neq r}j_{r^{\prime}}}{(n-1)(s-1)}\,\pi_{>k-1}(j_{r})\prod_{r"=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r"})}{j_{r"}!},

or equivalently

\displaystyle\tfrac{t(n)\pi_{>k}(n)}{(n-2)!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{t(n-1)\pi_{>k-1}(n)}{(n-2)!}

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 2}(s-1)^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{s}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{s}=n-1}\!\!\pi_{>k-1}(j_{r})\sum_{r^{\prime}\neq r}j_{r^{\prime}}\prod_{r"=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r"})}{j_{r"}!}.

Introducing
\mathbb{P}_{>k}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{t(n)\pi_{>k}(n)}{n!}z^{n}
, (so that
\mathbb{P}_{>-1}(z)=T(z)
, and
\mathbb{P}_{>0}(z)=T(z)-z
),
we obtain a
linear
differential recurrence equation

\displaystyle\tfrac{d^{2}\mathbb{P}_{>k}(z)}{dz^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{d\mathbb{P}_{>k-1}(z)}{dz}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{s\geq 2}\biggl(\sum_{j_{1}\geq 1}\tfrac{t(j_{1})\pi_{>k-1}(j_{1})}{j_{1}!}z^{j_{1}}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{j_{2}\geq 1}\tfrac{t(j_{2})}{(j_{2}-1)!}z^{j_{2}-1}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{j_{3}\geq 1}\tfrac{t(j_{3})}{j_{3}!}z^{j_{3}}\biggr)^{s-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{d\mathbb{P}_{>k-1}(z)}{dz}+\mathbb{P}_{>k-1}(z)\cdot T^{\prime}(z)\sum_{t\geq 0}T^{t}(z),

or

\tfrac{d^{2}\mathbb{P}_{>k}(z)}{dz^{2}}=\tfrac{d\mathbb{P}_{>k-1}(z)}{dz}+(1-T(z))^{-2}\mathbb{P}_{>k-1}(z),\quad|z|<1/2.

Furthermore, by (
3.11
), for real-valued
x\in[0,1/2]
,

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\leq 2\int_{0}^{1/2}\tfrac{\mathbb{P}_{>k}(x)}{1-T(x)}\,dx.

Since
(1-T(x))^{-1}=T^{\prime}(x)
, we switch from
x
to
\tau\in[0,1)
such that
x=x(\tau)
is inverse of
T(x)
. So, introducing
\Pi_{>k}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}_{>k}(x(\tau))
, we obtain

(3.13)

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}\Pi_{>k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

And, with a bit of work, the above differential equation becomes

(3.14)

\Pi^{(2)}_{>k}(\tau)=\Pi^{(1)}_{>k-1}(\tau)\bigl(1-\tau-\tfrac{1}{1-\tau}\bigr)+\Pi_{>k-1}(\tau),\quad k\geq 0,\,\,\tau\in[0,1),

subject to the initial conditions
\Pi_{>k}(0)=\Pi^{(1)}_{>k}(0)=0
.

Thus we need to bound the RHS of (
3.1.2
). Introduce

(3.15)

I_{k,t}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{t}\,\Pi_{>k}(\tau)\,d\tau,\quad k\geq-1,\,t\geq 0;

so (
3.13
) becomes

1-\sum_{j\leq k}c_{j}\leq 2I_{k,0}.

To bound
I_{k,0}
, we use (
3.15
) to derive a recurrence inequality for all
t\geq 0
and
k\geq-1
. First note that, for
t\geq 0
,

I_{-1,t}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{t}\,\Pi_{>-1}(\tau)\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{t}\tau\,d\tau=\tfrac{1}{(t+2)_{2}}.\\

Let us turn to
k\geq 0
.
Integrating the RHS of (
3.15
) by parts twice, using the initial conditions, and (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle I_{k,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{(t+2)_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{t+2}\Pi_{>k}^{(2)}(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{(t+2)_{2}}\biggl[\int_{0}^{1}\bigl[(1-\tau)^{t+3}-(1-\tau)^{t+1}\bigr]\Pi_{>k-1}^{(1)}(\tau)\,d\tau+I_{k-1,t+2}\biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{(t+2)_{2}}\Bigl[(t+4)I_{k-1,t+2}-(t+1)I_{k-1,t}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{3}{(t+2)_{2}}\cdot I_{k-1,t+2}.

Here the third line results from yet another integration by parts, and the bottom line results from the observation that
I_{k-1,s}
decreases as
s
increases.
It follows then that

I_{k,0}\leq\tfrac{3^{k}}{(2k)!}\cdot I_{0,2k}\leq\tfrac{3^{k}}{(2k+1)!}.

∎

3.1.3.
How tight is
\{n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]\}_{k\geq 0}
?

It trivially follows from Theorem
3.5
that the sequence of probability distributions
\{n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(n)]\}_{k\geq 0}
is tight, meaning that
\sup_{n}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]\to 0
as

k\to\infty
. The question is how fast. The answer is super-exponentially fast.

Theorem 3.6
.

For every
r\in(0,1)
, we have
\sup_{n}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]=O(r^{k})
, with the coefficient implicit in the big-O notation dependent on
r
only.

Proof.

(a)
Let
\rho>1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Introduce
g_{n}(\rho)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\rho^{k}\pi_{>k}(n)
. We want to prove that
g_{n}(\rho)\leq h_{n}(\rho):=An^{\alpha}
if
A
is sufficiently large. Let
n>1
. The, by (
3.1.2
), we have:

\displaystyle g_{n}(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{t(n-1)}{t(n)}(1+\rho g_{n-1}(\rho))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 2}\tfrac{s}{(n-1)(s-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-s}(1+\rho g_{j}(\rho))\tfrac{t(j)(n-1-j)}{j!}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{s-1}=n-1-j}\prod_{r^{\prime}=1}^{s-1}\tfrac{t(j_{r^{\prime}})}{j_{r^{\prime}}!}.

Since
g_{n}(0)=\pi_{>0}(n)=1
for
n>1
, and the
(s-1)
-fold sum equals
[z^{n-1-j}]\,T(z)^{s-1}
, the equation simplifies to

(3.16)

\displaystyle g_{n}(\rho)

\displaystyle=1+\tfrac{\rho t(n-1)}{t(n)}g_{n-1}(\rho)

(3.17)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\rho\,(n-2)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 2}\tfrac{s}{s-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-s}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)(n-1-j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-1-j}]T(z)^{s-1}.

Changing the order of summation in the double sum, we transform it into

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)(n-1-j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-1-j}]\sum_{s=2}^{n-j}\tfrac{s}{s-1}T(z)^{s-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)(n-1-j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-1-j}]\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\tfrac{s}{s-1}T(z)^{s-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)(n-1-j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-1-j}]\biggl(\tfrac{T(z)}{1-T(z)}+\log\tfrac{1}{1-T(z)}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-2-j}]\bigl(\tfrac{1}{(1-T(z))^{3}}+\tfrac{1}{(1-T(z))^{2}}\bigr);

for the last step we used
\nu[z^{\nu}]F(z)=[z^{\nu-1}]F^{\prime}(z)
and
T^{\prime}(z)=\tfrac{1}{1-T(z)}
. So (
3.16
) becomes

(3.18)

\displaystyle g_{n}(\rho)=1+\tfrac{\rho t(n-1)}{t(n)}g_{n-1}(\rho)

(3.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\tfrac{\rho\,(n-2)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}g_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-2-j}]\bigl(\tfrac{1}{(1-T(z))^{3}}+\tfrac{1}{(1-T(z))^{2}}\bigr).

Our task is to prove that the RHS of (
3.18
) where
g_{j}(\rho)
is replaced with
h_{j}(\rho)
is at most
h_{n}(\rho)
,
provided that

n\geq n(\rho)
and
A\geq A(\rho)
. We will use
the identity

[z^{\nu}](1-T(z))^{-\mu}=\tfrac{(\mu+2\nu-2)!!}{\nu!},

where the double factorial is the product of all positive integers at most
\mu+2\nu-2
, of the same parity as
\mu
. Using the concavity of the function
x^{\alpha}
,
we have:
\sum_{j}p_{j}j^{\alpha}\leq\bigl(\sum_{j}p_{j}j\bigr)^{\alpha}
for
p_{j}\geq 0
,
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
.

To apply this inequality to

\Sigma_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}j^{\alpha}\,\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-2-j}]\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr),

introduce

p_{j}:=\frac{\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-2-j}]\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)}{\sum_{k}\tfrac{t(k)}{k!}\cdot[z^{n-2-k}]\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)}

The denominator equals

[z^{n-2}]T(z)\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)=[z^{n-2}]\,\bigl((1-T)^{-3}-(1-T)^{-1}\bigr).

Therefore, for
n\geq 3
,

\Sigma_{n}\leq\biggl([z^{n-2}]\,\bigl((1-T)^{-3}-(1-T)^{-1}\bigr)\biggr)^{1-\alpha}\\
\times\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\,\tfrac{jt(j)}{j!}\cdot[z^{n-2-j}]\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)\biggr)^{\alpha},

Recalling
T^{\prime}=(1-T)^{-1}
, we transforrn the second line sum into

[z^{n-2}]\biggl(\sum_{j}\tfrac{jt(j)}{j!}z^{j}\biggr)\cdot\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)\\
=[z^{n-2}]\,zT^{\prime}\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)=[z^{n-3}]\,T^{\prime}\bigl((1-T)^{-3}+(1-T)^{-2}\bigr)\\
=[z^{n-3}]\,T^{\prime}\bigl((1-T)^{-4}+(1-T)^{-3}\bigr).

Collecting the pieces, we obtain

\Sigma_{n}\leq\biggl([z^{n-2}]\,\bigl((1-T)^{-3}-(1-T)^{-1}\bigr)\biggr)^{1-\alpha}\\
\times\biggl([z^{n-3}]\bigl((1-T)^{-4}+(1-T)^{-3}\bigr)\biggr)^{\alpha}\\
=\Bigl(\tfrac{2(2n-5)!!}{(n-2)!}\Bigr)^{1-\alpha}\cdot\Bigl(\tfrac{(2n-4)!!+(2n-5)!!}{(n-3)!}\Bigr)^{\alpha}.

So the RHS of (
3.18
) with
h_{j}(\rho)
instead of
g_{j}(\rho)
is at most

1+A\tfrac{\rho}{2n-3}(n-1)^{\alpha}+A\tfrac{\rho(n-2)!}{(2n-3)!!}\Bigl(\tfrac{2(2n-5)!!}{(n-2)!}\Bigr)^{1-\alpha}\cdot\Bigl(\tfrac{(2n-4)!!+(2n-5)!!}{(n-3)!}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\\
=1+A\tfrac{\rho}{2n-3}(n-1)^{\alpha}+A\rho\bigl(\tfrac{2}{2n-3}\bigr)^{1-\alpha}\Bigl((n-2)\bigl(\tfrac{(2n-4)!!}{(2n-3)!!}+\tfrac{1}{2n-3}\bigr)\Bigr)^{\alpha}\\
\leq 1+A\rho n^{-1+\alpha}+2A\rho n^{-1+2\alpha}\leq 1+3A\rho n^{-1+2\alpha}.

The last bound is indeed below
An^{\alpha}
, if
n>n(\rho)
and
A\geq A(\rho,\alpha)
, where

n(\rho):=1+\max\bigl\{3,(3\rho)^{(1-\alpha)^{-1}}\bigr\},\,\,A(\rho,\alpha):=\bigl(n^{\alpha}(\rho)-3\rho n^{2\alpha-1}(\rho)\bigr)^{-1}.

Observe that, very crudely,
g_{n}(\rho)\leq n_{0}\rho^{n_{0}}
if
n\leq n_{0}
. So, for those small values of
n
we have
g_{n}(\rho)<An^{\alpha}
, if
A\geq n_{0}\rho^{n_{0}}
. By induction on
n\geq n_{0}
we obtain that, for all
n
, we have
g_{n}(\rho)\leq A^{*}n^{\alpha}
if
A^{*}:=\max\{A(\rho,\alpha),n_{0}\rho^{n_{0}}\}
. Consequently,
p_{>k}(n)\leq\pi_{>k}(n)\leq A^{*}n^{\alpha}\rho^{-k}
.

(b)
Using
h_{n}(\rho)\leq An^{\alpha}
, we will prove that
\sup_{n\geq 1}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]=O(r^{k})
for every
r<1
. To
begin, analogously to (
2.5
), we have: for
n\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]=p_{>k}(n)+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{s}=n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})}{j_{r}!}\biggl(\sum_{r^{\prime}=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(j_{r^{\prime}})]\biggr).\\

By part
(a)
, for all
\rho
and
\alpha<1
, we have
\sum_{k}p_{>k}(n)\rho^{k}\leq h_{n}(\rho)=An^{\alpha}
, with
A=A(\rho,\alpha)
.
Introduce
H_{n}(\rho)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\rho^{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]
; obviously
H_{n}(\rho)=O(n)
for
\rho\leq 1
. Let us show
that
H_{n}(\rho)=O(n)
for every
\rho>1
as well. Of course, the coefficient hidden in the big-O notation will depend on
\rho
. From the above equation, and part
(a)
, it follows that

H_{n}(\rho)\leq h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{k\geq 0}\rho^{k}\sum_{s\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}+\dots+j_{s}=n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{s}\tfrac{t(j_{r})}{j_{r}!}\biggl(\sum_{r^{\prime}=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(j_{r^{\prime}})]\biggr)\\
=h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\biggl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\rho^{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(j)]\biggr)\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\sum_{s\geq 1}s\sum_{\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{s-1}=n-1-j}\prod_{r=1}^{s-1}\tfrac{t(\ell_{r})}{\ell_{r}!}\\
=h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}H_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}\,[z^{n-1-j}]\sum_{s\geq 1}sT^{s-1}(z)\\
=h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\,H_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{j!}[z^{n-1-j}](1-T(z))^{-2}\\
=h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{2^{n-1}(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}H_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{2^{j}j!},

since
(1-T(z))^{-2}=(1-2z)^{-1}=\sum_{u\geq 0}(2z)^{u}
. Clearly
H_{n}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{H}_{n}(\rho)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\rho)=H_{1}(\rho)=0
and

\mathcal{H}_{n}(\rho)=h_{n}(\rho)+\tfrac{2^{n-1}(n-1)!}{t(n)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{j}(\rho)\tfrac{t(j)}{2^{j}j!},\quad n\geq 2.

It follows that

\tfrac{t(n+1)}{2^{n}n!}\bigl[\mathcal{H}_{n+1}(\rho)-h_{n+1}(\rho)\bigr]-\tfrac{t(n)}{2^{n-1}(n-1)!}\bigl[\mathcal{H}_{n}(\rho)-h_{n}(\rho)\bigr]=\mathcal{H}_{n}(\rho)\tfrac{t(n)}{2^{n}n!},

or equivalently

\tfrac{\mathcal{H}_{n+1}(\rho)}{2n+1}=\tfrac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{2n-1}+D_{n},\quad D_{n}:=\bigl[h_{n+1}(\rho)-\tfrac{2n}{2n-1}h_{n}(\rho)\bigr](2n+1)^{-1}.

Since
h_{n}(\rho)=An^{\alpha}
, we have
D_{n}=O(n^{\alpha-1})
, and the recurrence equation implies that

\mathcal{H}_{n+1}(\rho)=(2n+1)\cdot\biggl(\tfrac{\mathcal{H}_{2}(\rho)}{3}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}D_{n}\biggr)=O(n+n^{\alpha})=O(n).

Therefore, uniformly for
k
, and
n\geq 1
,

n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{>k}(n)]\leq n^{-1}\tfrac{\mathcal{H}_{n}(\rho)}{\rho^{k}}=O(\rho^{-k})

for every
\rho>1
. In other words, the constant hidden in
O(\rho^{-k})
depends only on
\rho
and not on
k
.
∎

3.2.
Counting
r
-tuples of vertices with given ranks in
T_{n}
.

We saw in Proposition
2.4
that

(3.20)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\mathbb{k}}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\leq r}(1-T)^{-\tau-1}\biggl(\,\sum_{\mathbb{k}^{(1)}\!,\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)}\neq\emptyset}\,\,\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z)\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{u\in[r]}B^{\prime}_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}(z),

where
(\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)})
denotes an ordered partition of an
r
-tuple
\mathbb{k}
into
\tau
non-empty sub-tuples, and

(3.21)

where
\mathbb{k}=(k_{1},\mathbb{k}^{\prime})
is an
r
-tuple, and
(\mathbb{k}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{k}^{(\tau)})
denotes an ordered partition of
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
into
\tau
non-empty sub-tuples.
Note that the right-hand side of (

) does not depend on
k_{1}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Given an
r
-tuple
\mathbb{k}
, there exist positive constants

\varepsilon^{(i)}_{\mathbb{k},\ell}
such that we have
[z^{\nu}]A_{\mathbb{k}}(z)\in\bigl[[z^{\nu}]A_{\mathbb{k}}^{(1)}(z),[z^{\nu}]A_{\mathbb{k}}^{(2)}(z)\bigr]
for each
\nu
, (
A_{\mathbb{k}}(z)\in[A_{\mathbb{k}}^{(1)}(z),A_{\mathbb{k}}^{(2)}(z)]
, in short) where

(3.22)

A_{\mathbb{k}}^{(i)}(z)=\frac{(2r-3)!!\,\prod_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{k_{j}}}{(1-T)^{2r-1}}+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\varepsilon^{(i)}_{\mathbb{k},\ell}(1-T)^{-2r+1+\ell},\quad T=T(z).

Proof.

This claim certainly holds for
r=1
; see (
3.5
). Suppose the claim holds for all
r\leq r_{0}
, for some
r_{0}\geq 1
.
Each
\mathbb{k}^{(u)}
on the right-hand side of the inequality (
3.21
) is of cardinality
\leq r-1
. So, applying the inductive hypothesis to each
A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z)
there, we bound the
B^{\prime}
-sum in (
3.20
):

(3.23)

\sum_{u\in[r]}B^{\prime}_{k_{u},(k_{t})_{t\in[r]\setminus\{u\}}}(z)\in\Bigl[\sum_{\ell\leq 2r-1}\delta^{(1)}_{\mathbb{k},\ell}(1-T)^{-\ell},\sum_{\ell\leq 2r-1}\delta^{(2)}_{\mathbb{k},\ell}(1-T)^{-\ell}\Bigr].

Let us turn to the
A
-sum in (
3.20
). For
\tau\in[2,r]
, each
|\mathbb{k}^{(u)}|\leq r-1
, where
|\mathbb{k}^{(u)}|
is
just the number of components
in
\mathbb{k}^{(u)}
.
So, by the inductive hypothesis again, the contribution of all ordered partitions of
[r]
with
\tau\geq 2
parts to this sum is at least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-T)^{\tau+1}}\sum_{\cup_{u=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{k}^{(u)}=\mathbb{k}}\,\,\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}A_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{k_{j}}}{(1-T)^{2r+1}}\sum_{\cup_{u=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{k}^{(u)}=\mathbb{k}}\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}\bigl(2|\mathbb{k}^{(u)}|-3\bigr)!!\cdot\biggl(1+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\varepsilon_{\mathbb{k}^{(u)},\ell}(1-T)^{\ell}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{k_{j}}}{(1-T)^{2r+1}}\sum_{\cup_{u=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{k}^{(u)}=\mathbb{k}}\prod_{u=1}^{\tau}\bigl(2|\mathbb{k}^{(u)}|-3\bigr)!!+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\frac{\Delta^{(1)}_{\mathbb{k},s,\ell}}{(1-T)^{2r+1-\ell}}.

And we have the similar bound from above.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let

3.3.
More on the largest rank

In Section 3.1 we proved that with probability

is

We focus on vertices

to a unique leaf. Therefore
\mathcal{R}_{n}\geq\mathcal{R}^{p}_{n}

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{p}

.

Recall that
A_{k}(z)
is the exponential generating function for the number of all vertices of rank
k
in all rooted
plane trees on vertex set
[n]
, and
B_{k}(z)
is the exponential generating function for the number of trees in which the root is of rank
k
. Furthermore, recall
A_{(k,k)}(z)
is the exponential generating function for the number of

k
each in all trees on
[n]
, and
B_{(k,k)}(z)
is the exponential generating function for the number of ordered pairs

k

is the

of the tree.
We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}(z)
,
\mathcal{A}_{(k,k)}(z)
,
\mathcal{B}_{k}(z)
, and
\mathcal{B}_{(k,k)}(z)

be the counterparts of

,

,

for p-type vertices. Then

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}_{k}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{2}}+\frac{z^{k-1}}{(k-1)!},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{(k,k)}^{\prime}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}_{(k,k)}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{2}}+\frac{2\mathcal{A}_{k}^{2}(z)}{(1-T(z))^{3}}.

The proof is a simpler version of the proofs of Proposition

Note that the part concerning

and

, namely the path

Lemma 3.12

For

\displaystyle\ [z^{n}]\mathcal{A}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n-1)!!}{n!\,(2k+1)!!},

Let us start with

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-2\xi}\,\,\frac{\xi^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}

Therefore, integrating

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-2z)^{-1/2}}{2^{k-1}\,(k-1)!}\sum_{j<k}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+3}\binom{k-1}{j}\bigl[1-(1-2z)^{j+3/2}\bigr]

\displaystyle P_{k}(z)

Here

1

1
