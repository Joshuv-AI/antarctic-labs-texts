Stable limit theorems for additive functionals of one-dimensional diffusion processes
Loïc Béthencourt

Abstract
We consider a positive recurrent one-dimensional diffusion process with continuous coefficients and we establish stable central limit theorems for a certain type of additive functionals of this diffusion. In other words we find some explicit conditions on the additive functional so that its fluctuations behave like some α \alpha -stable process in large time for α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] \alpha\in(0,2] .

Introduction and main result

Consider a one-dimensional diffusion process with continuous coefficients
b
and
\sigma
, i.e. a continuous adapted process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the SDE

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s},

(1)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion. Without loss of generality, we assume
X_{0}=0
, even if it means changing
b
and
\sigma
. If
\sigma
does not vanish, then weak existence and uniqueness in law hold for (
1
), see Kallenberg
[
18
, Chapter 23 Theorem 23.1]
.

Assumption 1
.

The functions
b,\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(x)>0
. Moreover
b
and
\sigma
are such that the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent in the sense of Harris.

We recall that a strong Markov process
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},(X_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\mathbb{P}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}})
valued in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
is said to be recurrent in the sense of Harris if it has a
\sigma
-finite invariant measure
\mu
such that for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

\mu(A)>0\quad\text{implies}\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\bm{1}_{A}(X_{t})=1,\qquad\mathbb{P}_{x}-\text{almost surely for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

The process is then said to be positive recurrent if
\mu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
and null recurrent otherwise. We introduce the scale function
\mathfrak{s}
of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\mathfrak{s}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{v}b(u)\sigma^{-2}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\mathrm{d}v,

(2)

which is a
C^{2}
, strictly increasing function solving
\frac{1}{2}s^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{2}+s^{\prime}b=0
. We also introduce the speed measure density

m(x)=\sigma^{-2}(x)\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{x}b(v)\sigma^{-2}(v)\mathrm{d}v\right)=[\sigma^{2}(x)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)]^{-1}

(3)

Remark 2
.

Assume
b
and
\sigma
are continuous and
\sigma
does not vanish. Then we have the equivalence between the two following propositions, see for instance Revuz-Yor
[
26
, Chapter VII]
.

1.

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent in the sense of Harris.

2.

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\mathfrak{s}(x)=\pm\infty
and
\kappa^{-1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.

Moreover, in this case, the measure
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\kappa m(x)\mathrm{d}x
is the unique invariant probability measure for the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

The ergodic theorem for Harris recurrent processes, see Azema-Duflo-Revuz
[
1
]
or Revuz-Yor
[
26
, Chapter X]
, tells us that for
f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, a.s.

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\underset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu(f),

(4)

where
\mu(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\mu
. The convergence in (
4
) can be seen as a strong law of large numbers for the additive functional
\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s
of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Then it is very natural to study its fluctuations, i.e. to describe the asymptotic behavior of
\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}(f(X_{s})-\mu(f))\mathrm{d}s
. In this paper, we give simple conditions on
f
for these asymptotic fluctuations to be
\alpha
-stable for some
\alpha\in(0,2]
. The conditions on
f
are entirely prescribed by the coefficients
b
and
\sigma
.

We recall that
\ell:[0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
is said to be slowly varying if for every
\lambda>0
,
\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\to 1
as
x
goes to infinity. The use of such functions is justified by the fact that domains of attraction of stable laws involve slowly varying functions.

Assumption 3
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is locally bounded and Borel. Moreover, there exists
\alpha>0
,
(f_{+},f_{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a continuous slowly varying function
\ell:[0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
such that

[\sigma(x)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)]^{-2}|\mathfrak{s}(x)|^{2-1/\alpha}\ell(|\mathfrak{s}(x)|)f(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{\pm},

and
|f_{+}|+|f_{-}|>0
. If
f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, we impose
\mu(f)=0
. Finally, if
\alpha\geq 2
, we impose
f
to be continuous.

This assumption appears naturally in the computations. We refer to Section
4

\alpha=1
, we will add a mild assumption which ensures that the set
\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
is big enough.

Assumption 4
.

There exists
\lambda>0
such that
|\mathfrak{s}|^{\lambda}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
.

Under Assumption
3
, we set
\rho=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x
which can be infinite, and we define the diffusive constant as follows:

•

If
\alpha>2
or
\alpha=2
and
\rho<\infty
,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}=4\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}f(v)[\sigma^{2}(v)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}v\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x
.

•

If
\alpha=2
and
\rho=\infty
,
\sigma_{2}^{2}=4\kappa(f_{+}^{2}+f_{-}^{2})
.

•

If
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}=\frac{\kappa 2^{\alpha-2}\pi}{\alpha\sin(\alpha\pi/2)}\left(\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)^{2}(|f_{+}|^{\alpha}+|f_{-}|^{\alpha})
.

We also introduce, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, the complex numbers

•

z_{\alpha}(\xi)=1-i\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(f_{+})|f_{+}|^{\alpha}+\mathrm{sgn}(f_{-})|f_{-}|^{\alpha}}{|f_{+}|^{\alpha}+|f_{+}|^{\alpha}}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)
if
\alpha\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}

•

z_{1}(\xi)=1+i\frac{f_{+}+f_{-}}{|f_{+}|+|f_{-}|}\frac{2}{\pi}\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)\left[\log\left(\frac{\pi|\xi|}{2(|f_{+}|+|f_{-}|)}\right)+2\gamma+\log(2)+\frac{\log|f_{+}|f_{+}+\log|f_{-}|f_{-}}{f_{+}+f_{-}}\right]
, where
\gamma
is the Euler constant.

Finally, for a family of processes
((Y_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0})_{\epsilon>0}
valued in
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
(Y_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}(Y_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
if for all
n\geq 1
and all
0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
, the vector
(Y_{t_{i}}^{\epsilon})_{1\leq i\leq n}
converges in law to
(Y_{t_{i}}^{0})_{1\leq i\leq n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We are now ready to state our main theorem, which concerns positive recurrent diffusions.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of (
1
),
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a Brownian motion and
(S_{t}^{(\alpha)})_{t\geq 0}
an
\alpha
-stable process such that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\xi S_{t}^{(\alpha)})]=\exp(-t|\xi|^{\alpha}z_{\alpha}(\xi))
.

(i)

If
\alpha>2
, or
\alpha=2
and
\rho<\infty
,

\left(\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}\qquad\hbox{as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

(ii)

If
\alpha=2
and
\rho=\infty
, we set
\rho_{\epsilon}=\int_{1}^{1/\epsilon}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x
and we have

\left(|\epsilon/\rho_{\epsilon}|^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}\left(\sigma_{2}W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}\qquad\hbox{as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

(iii)

If
\alpha\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}
,

\left(\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}S_{t}^{(\alpha)}\right)_{t\geq 0}\qquad\hbox{as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

(iv)

If
\alpha=1
and Assumption
4
holds, there exists a deterministic family
(\xi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
of real numbers such that

\left(\epsilon\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\xi_{\epsilon}t\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}\left(\sigma_{1}S_{t}^{(1)}\right)_{t\geq 0}\qquad\hbox{as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

Moreover
\xi_{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\sim}\kappa(f_{+}+f_{-})\ell(1/\epsilon)\zeta_{\epsilon}
, where
\zeta_{\epsilon}=-\int_{1/\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}
if
f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
and
\zeta_{\epsilon}=\int_{1}^{1/\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}
otherwise.

For the reader more familiar with infinitesimal generators than with characteristic functions, let us mention the following remark.

Remark 6
.

When
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the process
(\sigma_{\alpha}S_{t}^{(\alpha)})_{t\geq 0}
of Theorem
5
is a Lévy process with Lévy measure

\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\left(c_{+}x^{-1-\alpha}\bm{1}_{\{x>0\}}+c_{-}|x|^{-1-\alpha}\bm{1}_{\{x<0\}}\right)\mathrm{d}x,

where

c_{+}=\lambda_{\alpha}\left[\bm{1}_{\{f_{+}>0\}}|f_{+}|^{\alpha}+\bm{1}_{\{f_{-}>0\}}|f_{-}|^{\alpha}\right],\quad c_{-}=\lambda_{\alpha}\left[\bm{1}_{\{f_{+}<0\}}|f_{+}|^{\alpha}+\bm{1}_{\{f_{-}<0\}}|f_{-}|^{\alpha}\right]

and
\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{\kappa 2^{\alpha-2}\pi}{\alpha\sin(\alpha\pi/2)}\left(\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)^{2}
. Its infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}
is such that for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
,

•

\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}[\phi(x+z)-\phi(x)]\nu(dz)
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

•

\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}[\phi(x+z)-\phi(x)-z\phi^{\prime}(x)]\nu(dz)
if
\alpha\in(1,2)
,

•

if
\alpha=1
,
\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}\phi(x)=a\phi^{\prime}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}[\phi(x+z)-\phi(x)-z\bm{1}_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}\phi^{\prime}(x)]\nu(dz)
, where

a=-(f_{+}+f_{-})\left[2\gamma+\log(2)+\kappa\log(\kappa)+\frac{\log|f_{+}|f_{+}+\log|f_{-}|f_{-}}{f_{+}+f_{-}}+\kappa\frac{\pi}{2}A\right],

\gamma
is the Euler constant and
A=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sin(x)-x}{x^{2}}dx+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(x)}{x^{2}}dx
.

One can refer to Bertoin
[
3

to understand how to go from the Lévy-Khintchine triplet to the generator and to Sato

28

Observe that in the Lévy regime
\alpha\in(0,2)
, we have a convergence in finite dimensional distribution of a continuous process towards a discontinuous process (stable processes possess many jumps). Hence we cannot hope to obtain convergence in law as a process, for example for the usual Skorokhod distance. Observe also that if
f_{-}=0
, the stable process has only positive jumps. Thus the limiting process is completely asymmetric although the process
(f(X_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
may visit the whole space infinitely often.

When
f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
and
\mu(f)\neq 0
, we can use Theorem
5
with the function
f-\mu(f)
provided it satisfies Assumption
3
.

When
\alpha\geq 2
, the stable process obtained in the limit is a Brownian motion, which is the only
2
-stable process. A standard strategy to show central limit theorems for the additive functional
\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s
is to solve the Poisson equation
\mathcal{L}g=f
, where
\mathcal{L}
is the infinitesimal generator of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with domain
D_{\mathcal{L}}
. One can refer to Jacod-Shiryaev
[
15
, Chapter VIII]
, Pardoux-Veretennikov
[
25
]
or Cattiaux, Chafaï and Guillin
[
9
]
. Assume that
f
is a continuous function in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)
and define the function

g(x)=2\int_{0}^{x}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)\int_{v}^{\infty}f(u)[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

(5)

Then
g
is a
C^{2}
function solving the Poisson equation
2bg^{\prime}+\sigma^{2}g^{\prime\prime}=-2f
. Hence, applying the Itô formula with
g
, we express the additive functional as a martingale plus some remainder, and hope to obtain the result using a central limit theorem for martingales. This will be the strategy used when
\alpha\geq 2
, and we will actually prove the following more general result. Note that, for
g
to be well defined, it suffices that
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)
since
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\kappa[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}x
, whence
\int_{v}^{\infty}f(u)[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}u=\kappa^{-1}\mu(f\bm{1}_{(v,\infty)})
.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the solution of (
1
). Let also
f
be a continuous function in
\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
such that
\mu(f)=0
and
g
be defined by (
5
). If
g^{\prime}\sigma\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mu)
, then

\left(\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{f.d.}{\longrightarrow}\left(\gamma W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}\qquad\hbox{as $\epsilon\to 0$},

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion and
\gamma^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}[g^{\prime}\sigma]^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu
. Moreover, if
g
is bounded, the finite dimensional convergence can be replaced by a convergence in law of continuous processes, with the topology of uniform convergence on compact time intervals.

Note that in the Lévy regime, we do not require
f
to be regular whereas we need
f
to be continuous in the diffusive regime, so we can apply the Itô formula with
g
.

References

The central limit theorem for Markov processes has a long history which goes back to Langevin
[
19
]
. Roughly, he studied a one-dimensional particle, the velocity of which is subject to random shocks and a restoring force
F(v)=-v
, and showed that the position of the particle behaves like a Brownian motion, when
t
tends to infinity.

Probabilistic techniques to obtain such results can be found in Jacod-Shiryaev
[
15
, Chapter VIII]
: if there exists a solution to the Poisson equation having few properties, then we have a central limit theorem. This strategy is applied in Pardoux-Veretennikov
[
25
]
for very general multidimensional diffusion processes. However,
[
25
]
does not completely include the diffusive regime of Theorem
5
: we have a little less assumptions but our result only holds in dimension one. It seems that the critical diffusive regime
\alpha=2
was not much studied. Finally, one can refer to Cattiaux, Chafaï and Guillin
[
9
]
for a detailed state of the art of the techniques used to show such theorems.

Regarding the Lévy regime, we could not find many results for Markov processes and this does not seem to be well developed. Through the prism of ergodic theory, Gouëzel
[
13
]
proved stable limit theorems for a certain class of maps. Applied to a positive recurrent Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with countable state space, its results tells us that if the function
f
is such that, up to a slowly varying function,

\mathbb{P}_{i}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}f(X_{k})>x\right)\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}c_{+}|x|^{-\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{i}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}f(X_{k})<x\right)\underset{x\to-\infty}{\sim}c_{-}|x|^{-\alpha},

(6)

where
\tau_{i}=\inf\{k>0,X_{k}=i\}
, then we have

\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}S^{\alpha},

where
S^{\alpha}
is a stable random variable. This is more or less obvious in this case since we have a sum of i.i.d blocks in the domain of attraction of stable laws and we can thus apply classical stable central limit theorems.

Similar results with more tangible assumptions were proved by Jara, Komorowski and Olla
[
16
]
for Markov chains with general state space. They assume the function
f
is such that, roughly,
\mu(\{|f|>x\})\simeq|x|^{-\alpha}
, where
\mu
is the invariant measure of the chain and a spectral gap condition, the latter one being more or less equivalent to return times having exponential moments. Since the return times are small, it is reasonable to think that we have
\mathbb{P}_{i}(\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}f(X_{k})>x)\simeq\mu(\{f>x\})
, at least if we consider a countable state space. They actually give two proofs, one using a martingale approximation and a second one with a renewal method involving a coupling argument.

Mellet, Mischler and Mouhot
[
23
]
showed that for a linearized Boltzmann equation with heavy-tailed invariant distribution, the rescaled distribution of the position converges to the solution of the fractional heat equation, i.e. the position process behaves like a stable process. Their work is closely related to
[
16
]
although their proof is entirely analytic.

The method proposed in this article is rather powerful and gives a simple condition on the additive functional for stable limit theorems to occur. The assumption made on the function
f
does not seem to be a time-continuous equivalent of (
6
) and we do not assume anything on the return times, nor on
\mu(\{|f|>x\})
. We will see in Section
4
that, indeed, the index
\alpha
is not always prescribed by
\mu(\{|f|>x\})
.

The strategy is the following: we classically express the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
changed in time and in space. Then we write
\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{\rho_{t}}\phi(W_{s})\mathrm{d}s
for a certain function
\phi
, which roughly looks like
\int_{0}^{\tau_{t}}|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}\mathrm{d}s
(up to asymmetry and principal values issues) in large time, where
(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the generalized inverse of the local time at
0
of
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This process is known to be a stable process, see Itô-McKean
[
14
, page 226]
, Jeulin and Yor
[
17
]
and Biane and Yor
[
5
]
. The computations are thus tractable and the method is very robust.

This method was proposed by Fournier and Tardif
[
12
]
, studying a kinetic model and the purpose of this paper is to extend this method to general one-dimensional diffusions and to more general additive functionals. The model they were studying was the following:

V_{t}=B_{t}-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{V_{s}}{1+V_{s}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s,\qquad X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}\mathrm{d}s,

(7)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion and
\beta>1
. The processes
(V_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are respectively the velocity and the position of a one-dimensional particle subject to the restoring force
F(v)=-\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{v}{1+v^{2}}
and random shocks. Lebeau and Puel
[
20
]
showed that, when
\beta\in(1,5)\setminus\{2,3,4\}
, the rescaled distribution of the position
X_{t}
converges to the solution of the fractional heat equation. Their work is analytical and relies on a deep spectral analysis leading to an impressive result. The diffusive regime (
\beta>5
) and critical diffusive regime (
\beta=5
) are treated by Nasreddine-Puel
[
24
]
and Cattiaux-Nasreddine-Puel
[
10
]
. We stress that all these papers are P.D.E papers.

To summarize, if the restoring force field is strong enough (
\beta\geq 5
), the rescaled position process resembles a Brownian motion and we say that there is a
normal diffusion limit
. On the other hand, when the force is weak (
\beta\in(1,5)
), the position resembles a stable process and there is an
anomalous diffusion limit
(or
fractional diffusion limit
).

Actually, physicists discovered that atoms diffuse anomalously when they are cooled by a laser. See for instance Castin, Dalibard and Cohen-Tannoudji
[
8
]
, Sagi, Brook, Almog and Davidson
[
27
]
and Marksteiner, Ellinger and Zoller
[
22
]
. A theoretical study (see Barkai, Aghion and Kessler
[
2
]
) modeling the motion of atoms precisely by (
7
) proved with quite a high level of rigor the observed phenomenons.

In the Lévy regime, the index
\alpha
is not prescribed by the function
f=\text{id}
and the invariant measure
\mu
. Indeed observe that we have
\alpha=(\beta+1)/3
whereas one can check, see Section
4
, that

\mu(\{|f|>x\})\simeq|x|^{1-\beta}.

In this situation, the return times are large and the dynamics are more complex.

Then, using probabilistic techniques, Fournier and Tardif
[
12
]
treated all cases of (
7
) for a larger class of symmetric forces. Naturally, we recover their result and enlarge it to asymmetrical forces, see Section
4
. In a companion paper
[
11
]
, they also prove the result in any dimension and the proof is much more involved.

We also found a paper of Bertoin and Werner
[
4
]
where they use similar techniques to redemonstrate
Spitzer’s theorem
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Slowly varying functions

In this section, we define slowly varying functions and give the properties of these functions that will be useful to us. One can refer to Bingham, Goldie and Teugels
[
7
]
for more details.

Definition 8
.

A measurable function
\ell:[0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
is said to be slowly varying if for every
\lambda>0
,
\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\longrightarrow 1
as
x\to\infty
.

Famous examples of slowly varying functions are powers of logarithm, iterated logarithms or functions having a strictly positive limit at infinity. It holds that for any
\theta>0
,
x^{\theta}\ell(x)\to\infty
and
x^{-\theta}\ell(x)\to 0
, as
x\to\infty
. We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\ell
be a continuous slowly varying function.

(i)

For each
0<a<b
, we have
\sup_{\lambda\in[a,b]}\left|\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)-1\right|\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1.

(ii)

(Potter’s bound).
For any
\delta>0
and
C>1
, there exists
x_{0}>0
, such that for all
x,y\geq x_{0}
,

\frac{\ell(y)}{\ell(x)}\leq C\left(\Big|\frac{y}{x}\Big|^{\delta}\vee\Big|\frac{x}{y}\Big|^{\delta}\right).

(iii)

The functions
L:x\mapsto\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v\ell(v)}
and

are slowly varying, as well as

Proof.

Items (i) and (ii).
See

Item (iii).
Let us start with the function

2.2
Brownian local times

Local times have been widely studied in Revuz-Yor
[
26
]
to which we refer for much more details. Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion. We introduce the local time of
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
at
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which is the process
(L_{t}^{x})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

L_{t}^{x}=|W_{t}-x|-|x|-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{sgn}(W_{s}-x)\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

The process
(L_{t}^{x})_{t\geq 0}
is continuous and non-decreasing and the random non-negative measure
\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{x}
on
[0,\infty)
is a.s. carried by the set
\{t\geq 0,W_{t}=x\}
, which is a.s. Lebesgue-null. We will heavily use the occupation times formula, see
[
26
, Chapter 6 Corollary 1.6 page 224]
, which tells that a.s., for every
t\geq 0
and for all Borel function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(W_{s})\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)L_{t}^{x}\mathrm{d}x.

We will also use the fact that the map
x\mapsto L_{t}^{x}
is a.s. Hölder of order
\theta
, for
\theta\in(0,1/2)
, uniformly on compact time intervals, see
[
26
, Corollary 1.8 page 226]
, i.e. that

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}|x|^{-\theta}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|<\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(8)

We now introduce
\tau_{t}=\inf\{u\geq 0,L_{u}^{0}>t\}
, the right-continuous generalized inverse of
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
. Some properties of
(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
will be needed. For all
t>0
,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{t_{-}}<\tau_{t})=0
, which means that
(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has no deterministic jump time and a.s., for all
t\geq 0
,
L_{\tau_{t}}^{0}=t
since
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
is continuous. We will also use that a.s., for all
t\geq 0,W_{\tau_{t}}=0
and that a.s.,
\tau_{0}=0
.

Finally, the processes
(\tau_{t},W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
\left(L_{\tau_{t}}^{x}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0}
enjoy the following scaling property: for all
c>0

(\tau_{t},W_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}(c^{-2}\tau_{ct},c^{-1}W_{c^{2}t})_{t\geq 0}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(L_{\tau_{t}}^{x}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}\left(c^{-1}L_{\tau_{ct}}^{xc}\right)_{x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0}

(9)

There is a huge litterature on stable processes and we will mainly refer to Sato

is a
1/2
-stable subordinator. Let us first recall a classic result on stable processes, see Sato
[
28
, Theorem 14.15 page 86 and Definition 14.16 page 87]
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}
and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an
\alpha
-strictly stable process, i.e. a Lévy process enjoying the scaling property
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}(c^{-1/\alpha}X_{ct})_{t\geq 0}
for all
c>0
. Then there exist
\nu\geq 0
and
\beta\in[-1,1]
such that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(i\xi X_{t})\right]=\exp\left(-\nu t|\xi|^{\alpha}\left(1-i\beta\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)\right)\right).

If
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
1
-stable process, i.e. a Lévy process such that for all
c>0
, there exists
k_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
with
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}(c^{-1}X_{ct}+k_{c}t)_{t\geq 0}
, then there exist
\nu\geq 0
,
\beta\in[-1,1]
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(i\xi X_{t})\right]=\exp\left(-\nu t|\xi|\left(1+i\beta\frac{2}{\pi}\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)\log|\xi|\right)+it\tau\xi\right).

In any case, if
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has only positive jumps, i.e. if its Lévy measure is carried by
(0,\infty)
, then
\beta=1
.

We now consider a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, its local time
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
at 0, and the generalized inverse
(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of its local time. Our goal is to build all possible stable processes in terms of this Brownian motion and its inverse local time. This is important in order to adapt the method of Fournier-Tardif
[
12
]
who only treated the symmetric case. Let us recall the notation
\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)=a\bm{1}_{\{x>0\}}+b\bm{1}_{\{x<0\}}
. We have the following result.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and define the process
K_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(W_{s})|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}\mathrm{d}s
. Then
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(K_{\tau_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
is strictly
\alpha
-stable and for all

and all

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\xi S_{t}\right)\right]=\exp\left(-c_{\alpha,a,b}t|\xi|^{\alpha}\left(1-i\beta_{\alpha,a,b}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)\right)\right),

where
c_{\alpha,a,b}=\frac{2^{\alpha-2}\pi}{\alpha\sin(\alpha\pi/2)}\left(\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)^{2}(|a|^{\alpha}+|b|^{\alpha})

.

When

, stable processes have finite variations and no drift part meaning they are pure jump processes and should be seen this way:
S_{t}=\sum_{s\in[0,t]}\int_{\tau_{s-}}^{\tau_{s}}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(W_{u})|W_{u}|^{1/\alpha-2}\mathrm{d}u

and the size of a jump is equal to the integral of the function
\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{1/\alpha-2}
over the corresponding excursion of the Brownian motion.

While

is well-defined when

since
\int_{0}^{T}|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}\mathrm{d}s<\infty
a.s., this is not the case when
\alpha\in[1,2)
and we have to work a little more.

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)

\beta_{\alpha,a,b}=\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(a)|a|^{\alpha}+\mathrm{sgn}(b)|b|^{\alpha}}{|a|^{\alpha}+|b|^{\alpha}}

Let

Remark 14

Let

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|K_{t}^{\alpha}+\frac{(a+b)\alpha}{\alpha-1}L_{t}^{0}-K_{t}\right|\leq

\displaystyle+c\bm{1}_{\{M_{T}\geq 1\}}\int_{1\leq x\leq M_{T}}\left(|x|^{-1}-|x|^{1/\alpha-2}\right)L_{T}^{x}\mathrm{d}x.

Let us call
R_{T}^{1,\alpha}
the first term on the right-hand-side of the above inequality and
R_{T}^{2,\alpha}
the second. Then we have for any
T>0
, a.s.,

R_{T}^{1,\alpha}\leq c\left(\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[-1,1]}|x|^{-1/3}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\right)\int_{|x|\leq 1}\left(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}-|x|^{-1}\right)|x|^{1/3}\mathrm{d}x.

By (
8
) again, the quantity
\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[-1,1]}|x|^{-1/3}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|
is a.s. finite. For any
x\neq 0
, the integrand in the above quantity converges to
0
as
\alpha
decreases to
1
. We can use dominated convergence since for any
\alpha\in[1,4/3)
, for any
x\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
, we have
(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}-|x|^{-1})|x|^{1/3}\leq(|x|^{-5/4}-|x|^{-1})|x|^{1/3}
, which is integrable on
(-1,0)
and
(0,1)
. We conclude that
R_{T}^{1,\alpha}
converges to
0
almost surely as
\alpha\downarrow 1
. Regarding
R_{T}^{2,\alpha}
, we have a.s., for any
T>0
, a.s.

\displaystyle R_{T}^{2,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq c\bm{1}_{\{M_{T}\geq 1\}}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}|L_{t}^{x}|\times\int_{1\leq x\leq M_{T}}\left(|x|^{-1}-|x|^{1/\alpha-2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq c\bm{1}_{\{M_{T}\geq 1\}}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}|L_{t}^{x}|\times\int_{1\leq x\leq M_{T}}|x|^{-1}\mathrm{d}x.

Again, we conclude by dominated convergence that
R_{T}^{2,\alpha}
converges to
0
almost surely as
\alpha\downarrow 1
. All in all, we showed that (
11
) holds.

As a consequence, for all
t\geq 0
and all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\xi S_{t}^{\alpha}\right)\right]e^{i\xi t\frac{(a+b)\alpha}{\alpha-1}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\xi S_{t}\right)\right].

But we know from Lemma
12
that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\xi S_{t}^{\alpha}\right)\right]e^{i\xi t\frac{(a+b)\alpha}{\alpha-1}}=\exp\left(-c_{\alpha,a,b}t|\xi|^{\alpha}+it\xi\left(c_{\alpha,a,b}\beta_{\alpha,a,b}|\xi|^{\alpha-1}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)+\frac{(a+b)\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)\right),

where
c_{\alpha,a,b}\beta_{\alpha,a,b}=\frac{2^{\alpha-2}\pi}{\alpha\sin(\alpha\pi/2)}\left(\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)^{2}(\mathrm{sgn}(a)|a|^{\alpha}+\mathrm{sgn}(b)|b|^{\alpha})\underset{\alpha\downarrow 1}{\to}\frac{\pi}{2}(a+b)
. The function
h_{a,b}(\alpha)=c_{\alpha,a,b}\beta_{\alpha,a,b}
defined for
\alpha>0
is
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
1
and a careful computation shows that

h_{a,b}^{\prime}(1)=\frac{\pi}{2}(a+b)\left[1+\log(2)+2\gamma+\frac{\log|a|a+\log|b|b}{a+b}\right],

where
\gamma
is the Euler constant, which appears from the fact that
\Gamma^{\prime}(1)=-\gamma
. Hence we have
c_{\alpha,a,b}\beta_{\alpha,a,b}\underset{\alpha\downarrow 1}{=}\frac{\pi}{2}(a+b)+h_{a,b}^{\prime}(1)(\alpha-1)+o(\alpha-1)
. Now using that
|\xi|^{\alpha-1}\underset{\alpha\downarrow 1}{=}1+(\alpha-1)\log|\xi|+o(\alpha-1)
and
\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)\underset{\alpha\downarrow 1}{=}\frac{-2}{\pi(\alpha-1)}+o(1)
, it comes that

c_{\alpha,a,b}\beta_{\alpha,a,b}|\xi|^{\alpha-1}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)+\frac{(a+b)\alpha}{\alpha-1}=(a+b)-\frac{2}{\pi}h_{a,b}^{\prime}(1)-(a+b)\log|\xi|+o(1),

and thus we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\xi S_{t}^{\alpha}\right)\right]e^{i\xi t\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\underset{\alpha\downarrow 1}{\to}\exp\left(-c_{a,b}t|\xi|\left(1+i\beta_{a,b}\frac{2}{\pi}\log(|\xi|)\mathrm{sgn}(\xi)\right)+it\tau_{a,b}\xi\right),

where
c_{a,b}=\frac{\pi}{2}(|a|+|b|)
,
\beta_{a,b}=\frac{a+b}{|a|+|b|}
and
\tau_{a,b}=-(a+b)\left[2\gamma+\log(2)+\frac{\log|a|a+\log|b|b}{a+b}\right]
.
∎

2.4
Inverting time-changes

We remind some classical convergence results enabling to treat the convergence of the generalized inverses of time changes.

Lemma 15
.

For all
n\geq 1
, let
(a_{t}^{n})_{t\geq 0}
from
[0,+\infty)
to itself be a continuous, increasing and bijective function for all
n\geq 1
and consider its inverse
(r_{t}^{n})_{t\geq 0}
. Assume
(a_{t}^{n})_{t\geq 0}
simply converges, as
n\to\infty
, to a non-decreasing function
(a_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}a_{t}=\infty
. Consider
r_{t}=\inf\{u\geq 0,a_{u}>t\}
its right-continuous generalized inverse and
J=\{t\geq 0,r_{t_{-}}<r_{t}\}
. For all
t\in[0,+\infty)\setminus J
, we have
\lim_{n\to+\infty}r_{t}^{n}=r_{t}
.

This lemma is classical and we took the above statement in Fournier-Tardif
[

, Lemma 8]
. Actually, the proof of this lemma is not different from that of the pointwise convergence of inverse distribution functions, used to show

3.1
Integrability of
f
and
g^{\prime}\sigma

In this subsection, we characterize with the index
\alpha
the integrability of
f
and
g^{\prime}\sigma
with respect to the measure
\mu
. We first introduce the functions
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\psi=\left(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\right)\times\left(\sigma\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi=(f\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})/\psi^{2}.

(12)

Assumption
3
precisely tells us that
\phi
is a regular varying with index
1/\alpha-2
. Indeed, since
\mathfrak{s}
is an increasing bijection by Assumption
1
and Remark
2
, we have

|x|^{2-1/\alpha}\ell(|x|)\phi(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{\pm}.

(13)

We make the following proposition and let us insist on the fact that from now on, we work under Assumptions
1
and
3
.

Proposition 16
.

f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
is equivalent to
\alpha>1
or
\alpha=1
and
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}<\infty
.

Proof.

If
\alpha>1
, then
2-1/\alpha>1
. Since
\ell
is a slowly varying function, we can find
\delta>0
with
2-1/\alpha-\delta>1
such that
|x|^{\delta}\ell(|x|)\to\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
. Thus we have by (
13
) that

|\phi(x)|\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{=}o\left(|x|^{\delta+1/\alpha-2}\right)

and the function
\phi
is integrable with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. But using the substitution
x=\mathfrak{s}(v)
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi(x)|\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(v)|}{\sigma^{2}(v)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)}\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f|\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty.

(14)

If
\alpha=1
and
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}<\infty
, then (
13
) tells us that
|x|\ell(|x|)\phi(x)\to f_{\pm}
, and thus
\phi
is integrable with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
which tells us by (
14
) that
f
is integrable with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

We can make a similar reasoning to show that
\alpha<1
or
\alpha=1
and
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}=\infty
implies that
f\notin\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
.
∎

We now focus on the function
g
defined by (
5
) when
\alpha\geq 2
and recall that

g(x)=2\int_{0}^{x}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)\int_{v}^{\infty}f(u)[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v=2\kappa^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)\mu(f\bm{1}_{(v,\infty)})\mathrm{d}v.

By Assumption
3
, Proposition
16
and since
\alpha\geq 2
, we have
\mu(f)=0
and thus
\mu(f\bm{1}_{(v,\infty)})=-\mu(f\bm{1}_{(-\infty,v)})
so that

g(x)=-2\int_{0}^{x}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)\int_{-\infty}^{v}f(u)[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

(15)

We now express the function
g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}
, using the substitutions
v=\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y)
and
u=\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z)
:

g(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(x))=2\int_{0}^{x}\int_{y}^{\infty}f(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))[\sigma(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))]^{-2}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y=2\int_{0}^{x}\int_{y}^{\infty}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.

(16)

Using (
15
), we also get

g(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(x))=-2\int_{0}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.

(17)

We are now ready to state the proposition characterizing the integrability of
g^{\prime}\sigma
with respect to
\mu
.

Proposition 17
.

If
\alpha>2
, or
\alpha=2
and
\rho=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, then
g^{\prime}\sigma\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

From (
16
) and (
17
), we get that

\displaystyle(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v=-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v.

(18)

Assume
\alpha>2
, then
2-1/\alpha>3/2
. From Assumption
3
and since
\ell
is a slowly varying function, we can find
\delta>0
such that
2-1/\alpha-\delta>3/2
and

|\phi(x)|\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{=}o\left(|x|^{\delta+1/\alpha-2}\right),

and thus

|(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{=}o\left(|x|^{2(\delta+1/\alpha-1)}\right).

Since
2(\delta+1/\alpha-1)<-1
,
(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
. Now using the fact that
(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}]^{-1}\times g^{\prime}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}
and the substitution
x=\mathfrak{s}(v)
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|g^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(x))|^{2}}{[\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(x))]^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|g^{\prime}(v)|^{2}}{\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)}\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g^{\prime}\sigma|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu,

whence the result. Now assume
\alpha=2
and
\rho=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty
. Assumption
3
tells us that
\phi(x)\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}f_{\pm}\frac{1}{|x|^{3/2}\ell(|x|)}
. Integrating and using (

(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(x)\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}2f_{+}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}.

Hence the fact that
\rho<\infty
tells us precisely that
(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}x)
. Using a similar computation involving (

), we find that
(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathrm{d}x)
. We conclude that
g^{\prime}\sigma\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{2}(\mu)
as when
\alpha>2
.
∎

3.2
Scale function and speed measure

In this subsection, we classically represent the solution
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
) as a function of a time-changed Brownian motion. Roughly, the process
(\mathfrak{s}(X_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous local martingale and the Dubins-Schwarz theorem tells us
(\mathfrak{s}(X_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a time-changed Brownian motion. The next lemma enables us to represent
X_{t/\epsilon}
and
\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s
through the functions
\psi
,
\phi
, defined by (
12
), and
\mathfrak{s}
, generalizing to our context the identity found in Fournier-Tardif
[
12
, Lemma 6]
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
a_{\epsilon}>0
. We consider a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and we define

A_{t}^{\epsilon}=\epsilon a_{\epsilon}^{-2}\int_{0}^{t}\psi^{-2}\left(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}s

and its inverse
(\tau_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
,
which is a.s. a continuous and strictly increasing bijection from
[0,+\infty)
in itself. Let us set

X_{t}^{\epsilon}=\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\left(W_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}/a_{\epsilon}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad F_{t}^{\epsilon}=H_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}\quad\text{where}\quad H_{t}^{\epsilon}=a_{\epsilon}^{-2}\int_{0}^{t}\phi\left(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

For
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the unique solution to (
1
), we have

\left(\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s,X_{t/\epsilon}\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}\left(F_{t}^{\epsilon},X_{t}^{\epsilon}\right)_{t\geq 0}.

We introduce the unusual degree of freedom
a_{\epsilon}
that will allow us to make converge the time-change
A_{t}^{\epsilon}
without normalization.

Proof.

We recall that the function
\psi
and
\phi
are defined by
\psi=\left(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\right)\times\left(\sigma\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}\right)
and
\phi=(f\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})/\psi^{2}
. Let us consider the function
\psi_{\epsilon}(w)=\epsilon^{-1/2}a_{\epsilon}\psi(w/a_{\epsilon})
, so that
A_{t}^{\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{\epsilon}^{-2}(W_{s})\mathrm{d}s
. We set
Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=W_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}
which classicaly solves, see
[
18
, page 452]
,

Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{\epsilon}\left(Y_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{\epsilon}

where
(B_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion. Next, we consider the function
\varphi_{\epsilon}(y)=\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon})
. We can apply Itô’s formula to
X_{t}^{\epsilon}=\varphi_{\epsilon}(Y_{t}^{\epsilon})
since
\mathfrak{s}^{-1}
is
C^{2}
and we get

X_{t}^{\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(Y_{s}^{\epsilon})\psi_{\epsilon}\left(Y_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(Y_{s}^{\epsilon})\psi_{\epsilon}^{2}\left(Y_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

The functions are such that:

\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(y)\psi_{\epsilon}(y)=\frac{1}{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon}))}\times\frac{a_{\epsilon}\psi(y/a_{\epsilon})}{\epsilon^{1/2}}=\frac{\sigma(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon}))}{\epsilon^{1/2}}=\frac{\sigma(\varphi_{\epsilon}(y))}{\epsilon^{1/2}}

and,

\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(y)\psi_{\epsilon}^{2}(y)=\frac{-1}{a_{\epsilon}^{2}}\frac{\mathfrak{s}^{\prime\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon}))}{[\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon}))]^{3}}\times\frac{a_{\epsilon}^{2}\psi^{2}(y/a_{\epsilon})}{\epsilon}=\frac{2b(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(y/a_{\epsilon}))}{\epsilon}=\frac{2b(\varphi_{\epsilon}(y))}{\epsilon}.

Indeed remember that
\mathfrak{s}
satisfies
\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{2}+\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}b=0
. Finally we have

X_{t}^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s+\epsilon^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{\epsilon}

Now taking equation (
1
), we write

X_{t/\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}b(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}\sigma(X_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s/\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s+\epsilon^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s/\epsilon})\mathrm{d}\hat{B}_{s}^{\epsilon},

where
(\hat{B}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}=(\epsilon^{1/2}B_{t/\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion. Thus,
\forall\epsilon>0
, the processes
(X_{t/\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
are solutions of the same SDE, for which we have uniqueness in law, driven by different Brownian motion,
(\hat{B}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and
(B_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
. Thus they are equal in law :
(X_{t/\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
. As
\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s/\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s
, we have

\left(\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s,X_{t/\epsilon}\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s,X_{t}^{\epsilon}\right)_{t\geq 0}.

Using the substitution
u=\tau_{s}^{\epsilon}\Leftrightarrow s=A_{u}^{\epsilon}
, we have
\mathrm{d}s=\psi_{\epsilon}^{-2}(W_{u})\mathrm{d}u
, and

\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}f\circ\varphi_{\epsilon}\left(W_{\tau_{s}^{\epsilon}}\right)\mathrm{d}s=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}\frac{f\circ\varphi_{\epsilon}(W_{u})}{\psi_{\epsilon}^{2}(W_{u})}\mathrm{d}u=a_{\epsilon}^{-2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}\phi(W_{u}/a_{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}u=F_{t}^{\epsilon}

which ends the proof.
∎

We end this section with the following proposition that we will use several times later on.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
a_{\epsilon}>0
such that
a_{\epsilon}\to 0
when
\epsilon\to 0
. Let also
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion and
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
its local time at
0
. For every
\varphi\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, we have a.s., for all
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\kappa a_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}s-\mu(\varphi)L_{t}^{0}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Denote
Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=\kappa a_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}s
. Using the occupation times formula, we get

Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=\kappa a_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(x/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(x/a_{\epsilon})}L_{t}^{x}\mathrm{d}x=\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi(v)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)}{\psi^{2}(\mathfrak{s}(v))}L_{t}^{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)}\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(v)L_{t}^{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)}\mu(\mathrm{d}v),

where we used the substitution
x=a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)
. We recall that
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\kappa[\sigma^{2}(x)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}x
and that
\psi=(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})\times(\sigma\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})
. Hence we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{t}^{\epsilon}-\mu(\varphi)L_{t}^{0}\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi(v)|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\mu(\mathrm{d}v).

But for all
v\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|L_{t}^{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)}-L_{t}^{0}|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0
a.s. (see
[
26
]
Corollary 1.8, page 226), and
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|L_{t}^{a_{\epsilon}\mathfrak{s}(v)}-L_{t}^{0}|\leq 2\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}L_{t}^{x}<\infty
a.s.. We can conclude by dominated convergence since
\varphi\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
by assumption.
∎

3.3
The Lévy regime

In this section, which generalizes
[

, we first show two lemmas, that will enable us to conclude. The first lemma says that if in Lemma

we choose carefully

, the time change

Lemma 20
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion and
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
its local time at 0. For all
\epsilon>0
, we consider the processes

,

(i)

We have a.s., for all

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|A_{t}^{\epsilon}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

(ii)

For all

\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\tau_{t}
, the generalized inverse of
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
.

(iii)

Suppose Assumption

. For any slowly varying function

(iv)

If

\rho=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\infty

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|T_{t}^{\epsilon}-\sigma_{2}^{2}L_{t}^{0}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\text{where}\quad T_{t}^{\epsilon}=\kappa^{2}|\epsilon\rho_{\epsilon}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\chi(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s,

(v)

For all
\varphi\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, for all

\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\mu(\varphi)t.

Proof.

Item (i).
The first point is just the application of Proposition
19
with
\varphi=1
.

Item (ii).
The second point follows immediately from the first and Lemma
15
, since for all
t\geq 0
, as recalled in Subsection

,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{t-}<\tau_{t})=0
.

Item (iii).
Using the same scheme as in the proof of Proposition
19
, we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\gamma(1/\epsilon)\left|A_{t}^{\epsilon}\!-\!L_{t}^{0}\right|\!=\!\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\kappa\gamma(1/\epsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{L_{t}^{\epsilon y/\kappa}-L_{t}^{0}}{\psi^{2}(y)}\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(1/\epsilon)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{\epsilon\mathfrak{s}(v)/\kappa}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\mu(\mathrm{d}v).

Now we use (
8
) with some
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\wedge\lambda)
, where
\lambda
refers to the constant of Assumption
4
, so that
|\mathfrak{s}|^{\lambda}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
. We thus have
\gamma(1/\epsilon)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|L_{t}^{\epsilon\mathfrak{s}(v)/\kappa}-L_{t}^{0}|\leq C|\mathfrak{s}(v)|^{\theta}\gamma(1/\epsilon)\epsilon^{\theta}
, for some random

. Since

\gamma(1/\epsilon)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|L_{t}^{\epsilon\mathfrak{s}(v)/\kappa}-L_{t}^{0}|\longrightarrow 0

, for each fixed

. Moreover

\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\gamma(1/\epsilon)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{\epsilon\mathfrak{s}(v)/\kappa}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\leq C\left(\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\gamma(1/\epsilon)\epsilon^{\theta}\right)|\mathfrak{s}(v)|^{\theta}

and we can conclude by dominated convergence since
|\mathfrak{s}|^{\theta}\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
.

Item (iv).

Step 0 :
We first estimate asymptotically the function
\chi
. Let us recall from (
18
) that

(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(x)=2\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v=-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v.

Let us define the function
h
on
(0,\infty)
:

h(x)=\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{2}.

By (
13
), we have
|x|^{3/2}\ell(|x|)\phi(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{\pm}
and thus

\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{-1}\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{+}

and

\left(\int_{|x|}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v)}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(v)\mathrm{d}v\underset{x\to-\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{-}.

All in all, recalling that
\chi=[(g\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1})^{\prime}]^{2}
, we have
[h(|x|)]^{-1}\chi(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}4f_{\pm}^{2}
from which

\int_{-x}^{x}\chi(z)\mathrm{d}z\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}4(f_{+}^{2}+f_{-}^{2})\int_{1}^{x}h(v)\mathrm{d}v.

(19)

We used that
\rho=\int_{1}^{\infty}h(v)\mathrm{d}v=\infty
by assumption. Moreover there exists
A>0
such that for all
x
, we have
\chi(x)\leq Ah(|x|)
. Now we write for
\delta>0
fixed,
T_{t}^{\epsilon}=D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}+E_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
where

D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}=\int_{0}^{t}\kappa^{2}|\epsilon\rho_{\epsilon}|^{-1}\chi(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon)\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}\mathrm{d}s\>\>\>\text{ and }\>\>\>E_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}=\int_{0}^{t}\kappa^{2}|\epsilon\rho_{\epsilon}|^{-1}\chi(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon)\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|\leq\delta\}}\mathrm{d}s.

Step 1 :
We show that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}|\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. We have
\chi(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon)\leq Ah(\kappa|W_{s}|/\epsilon)
. Now notice that in this case, we have a.s.,

\displaystyle\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}h(\kappa|W_{s}|/\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}\left(\int_{\kappa|W_{s}|/\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}\ell(u)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}\frac{\epsilon}{\kappa|W_{s}|}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(\kappa|W_{s}|v/\epsilon)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}\frac{\epsilon}{\kappa\delta}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(\kappa|W_{s}|v/\epsilon)}\right)^{2}.

We know from Lemma
9
-(i) (with
a=\delta
and
b=\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|W_{s}|
) that the following uniform convergence holds a.s.

\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}\left|\frac{\ell(v/\epsilon)}{\ell(\kappa|W_{s}|v/\epsilon)}-1\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

Hence there exists a random
M>0
such that for any
s\in[0,T]
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
|W_{s}|>\delta
implies that
1/\ell(\kappa|W_{s}|v/\epsilon)\leq M/\ell(v/\epsilon)
. Consequently,

\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}|\chi(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon)|\leq\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\bm{1}_{\{|W_{s}|>\delta\}}Ah(\kappa|W_{s}|/\epsilon)\leq\frac{\epsilon AM^{2}}{\kappa\delta}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v/\epsilon)}\right)^{2}.

We know from Potter’s bound (Lemma
9
-(ii)) that there exists
x_{0}>1
such that for all
x,y\geq x_{0}
,

\frac{\ell(y)}{\ell(x)}\leq 2\left(\Big|\frac{y}{x}\Big|^{1/4}\vee\Big|\frac{x}{y}\Big|^{1/4}\right).

Hence, if
\epsilon\in(0,1/x_{0})
, we have

\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}\ell(v/\epsilon)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\ell^{2}(1/\epsilon)}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|v|^{1/4}\vee|v|^{-1/4}\mathrm{d}v}{v^{3/2}}\right)^{2}.

All in all, there exists a random constant that we name
M
again, such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}|\leq\frac{MT}{\delta\ell^{2}(1/\epsilon)\rho_{\epsilon}}.

It holds that
\ell^{2}(1/\epsilon)\rho_{\epsilon}\longrightarrow\infty
, see the end of the proof of Lemma
9
-(iii) and use that
\rho_{\epsilon}=M(1/\epsilon)
, and thus for each
\delta>0
,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}|\longrightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Step 2 :
We concentrate on
E_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
. Using the occupation times formula, we have

E_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}=\left(\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\frac{\kappa^{2}\chi(\kappa x/\epsilon)\mathrm{d}x}{|\epsilon\rho_{\epsilon}|}\right)L_{t}^{0}+\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\frac{\kappa^{2}\chi(\kappa x/\epsilon)(L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0})\mathrm{d}x}{|\epsilon\rho_{\epsilon}|}=r_{\epsilon,\delta}L_{t}^{0}+R_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta},

the last equality standing for a definition. We have, using a substitution and (
19
),

r_{\epsilon,\delta}=\frac{\kappa}{\rho_{\epsilon}}\int_{-\kappa\delta/\epsilon}^{\kappa\delta/\epsilon}\chi(z)\mathrm{d}z\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\sim}\frac{4\kappa(f_{+}^{2}+f_{-}^{2})}{\rho_{\epsilon}}\int_{1}^{\kappa\delta/\epsilon}h(x)\mathrm{d}x\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\sim}4\kappa(f_{+}^{2}+f_{-}^{2})=\sigma_{2}^{2},

where we used that
\rho_{\epsilon}=\int_{1}^{1/\epsilon}h(x)\mathrm{d}x
and that the function
M(x)=\int_{1}^{x}h(v)\mathrm{d}v
is slowly varying by Lemma
9
-(iii).

Step 3 :
We conclude. Recalling that
T_{t}^{\epsilon}=r_{\epsilon,\delta}L_{t}^{0}+R_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}+D_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
, we proved that a.s., for all
T>0
and all
\delta>0
,

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|T_{t}^{\epsilon}-\sigma_{2}^{2}L_{t}^{0}\right|\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}|.

But

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}|\leq r_{\epsilon,\delta}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[-\delta,\delta]}|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}|,

which implies

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|T_{t}^{\epsilon}-\sigma_{2}^{2}L_{t}^{0}\right|\leq\sigma_{2}^{2}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[-\delta,\delta]}|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}|.

Now, we let
\delta
to
0
which completes the proof.

Item (v).
Using the definition of
X_{t}^{\epsilon}
, and making the substitution
u=\tau_{s}^{\epsilon}\Leftrightarrow s=A_{u}^{\epsilon}
, we have

\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s=\epsilon a_{\epsilon}^{-2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}s=\kappa a_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}s.

Let
T=\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}
which is a.s. finite from the second point. We have

\begin{split}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s-\mu(\varphi)t\right|\leq&\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|\kappa a_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{0}^{u}\frac{\varphi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}{\psi^{2}(W_{s}/a_{\epsilon})}\mathrm{d}s-\mu(\varphi)L_{u}^{0}\right|\\
&+\mu(\varphi)\left|L_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}^{0}-t\right|.\end{split}

The first term on the right hand side goes to
0
by Proposition
19
and the second goes to
0
since
\tau^{\epsilon}_{t}\to\tau_{t}
by Point 2,
L_{\tau_{t}}^{0}=t
a.s., and
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
is continuous.
∎

Let us state two immediate consequences of the previous lemma.

Remark 21
.

Point (5) of the preceding Lemma recovers a weak version of the ergodic theorem for the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Although it is straightforward since the process is Harris recurrent, this provides a self-contained proof of this weak version, which we only need. For all
\varphi\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
and all
t\geq 0
,
\epsilon\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}\varphi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\overset{d}{=}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}s
, hence for all
t\geq 0

\epsilon\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}\varphi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\mu(\varphi)t.

Remark 22
.

Point (4) prepares the critical diffusive regime. Using arguments similar to those in the proof of point (5), we easily see that if
\alpha=2
and
\rho=\infty
, then for every
t\geq 0
, a.s.,
T_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\sigma_{2}^{2}t
. Now one can also see that as
|\epsilon/\rho_{\epsilon}|\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}[g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})]^{2}\mathrm{d}s\overset{d}{=}T_{\tau_{t}^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}
, we have

|\epsilon/\rho_{\epsilon}|\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}[g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})]^{2}\mathrm{d}s\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\sigma_{2}^{2}t.

The next result is the last preliminary before proving the main result in the Lévy case.

Lemma 23
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion, and
(L_{t}^{0})_{t\geq 0}
its local time in 0. Let also
(K_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the process defined in section
2.3
, with
(a,b)=(f_{+},f_{-})
. For all
\epsilon>0
, we consider the process
(H_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
from Lemma
18
with the choice
a_{\epsilon}=\epsilon/\kappa
.

(i)

If
\alpha\neq 1
, then a.s., for all
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\kappa^{1/\alpha}K_{t}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

(ii)

If
\alpha=1
, there exists
(\xi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
such that, a.s., for all
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\epsilon\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\xi_{\epsilon}L_{t}^{0}-\kappa K_{t}\right|\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0,

where
\xi_{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\sim}-\kappa(f_{+}+f_{-})\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{1/\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}
if
f\in\mathrm{\bm{L}}^{1}(\mu)
and
\xi_{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\sim}\kappa(f_{+}+f_{-})\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{1}^{1/\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x\ell(x)}
otherwise.

Proof.

Technical estimates.
We first show some technical estimates that we will use in this proof.

(i)
For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
, it holds (and this is the case for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
) that

\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\kappa^{1/\alpha}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{1/\alpha-2}.

(20)

Indeed we have by (
13
), since
\ell
is slowly varying and since
(a,b)=(f_{+},f_{-})
,

\forall x>0,\quad\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\kappa^{1/\alpha}ax^{1/\alpha-2},

\forall x<0,\quad\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\kappa^{1/\alpha}b|x|^{1/\alpha-2}.

(ii)
For all
\delta>0
, there exist
A>0
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
(and this is the case for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
) such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
,

\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)\leq A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)(|x|^{\delta}+|x|^{-\delta})

(21)

Indeed, we start using (
13
) again and that
\phi
and
\ell
are locally bounded: there exists
A>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\ell(|x|)|\phi(x)|\leq A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)
. Hence we have

\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)|\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)|\leq A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)\frac{\ell(1/\epsilon)}{\ell(\kappa|x|/\epsilon)},

the value of
A
being allowed to change. We know from Potter’s bound (Lemma
9
-(ii)) that there exists
x_{0}
such that for all
x,y\geq x_{0}
,

\frac{\ell(y)}{\ell(x)}\leq 2\left(\Big|\frac{y}{x}\Big|^{\delta}\vee\Big|\frac{x}{y}\Big|^{\delta}\right).

Thus, for
\epsilon\in(0,1/x_{0})
we have

\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)|\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)|\bm{1}_{\{\kappa|x|\geq\epsilon x_{0}\}}\leq A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)(|x|^{\delta}\vee|x|^{-\delta}).

The function
\ell
is strictly positive and bounded from below on every compact set and thus there exists
\ell_{0}>0
such that for all
x\in[0,x_{0}]
,
\ell(x)\geq\ell_{0}
. Now if
\kappa|x|<\epsilon x_{0}
, we have
\ell(\kappa|x|/\epsilon)\geq\ell_{0}
and
1\leq(\kappa|x|/\epsilon x_{0})^{-\delta}
. Hence

\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)|\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)|\bm{1}_{\{\kappa|x|<\epsilon x_{0}\}}\leq A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)|x|^{-\delta}\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\epsilon^{\delta}\ell(1/\epsilon).

We proved (
21
).

Item (i) when
\bm{\alpha\in(0,1)}
. Let us remind that
K_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(W_{s})|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}ds
. We have
\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}=\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{0}^{t}\phi\left(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon\right)\mathrm{d}s
and

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\kappa^{1/\alpha}K_{t}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon\right)-\kappa^{1/\alpha}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(W_{s})|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}\right|\mathrm{d}s

which tends to 0 a.s. by dominated convergence. Indeed, since
\{t\in[0,T],W_{t}=0\}
is a.s. Lebesgue-null, we have by (
20
) that a.s., for a.e.
s\in[0,T]
,

|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon\right)-\kappa^{1/\alpha}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(W_{s})|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}|\to 0

Next we can dominate
\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)|\phi(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon)|
using (
21
) by

A(|W_{s}|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)(|W_{s}|^{\delta}+|W_{s}|^{-\delta})

which is a.s. integrable on
[0,T]
if
\delta>0
is small enough so that
1/\alpha-2-\delta>-1
.

Item (i) when
\bm{\alpha\in(1,2)}
. We notice that
\mu(f)=\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z
, which equals
0
by Assumption
3
. Indeed,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))}{\sigma^{2}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{-1}(z))^{2}}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(v)}{\sigma^{2}(v)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)}\mathrm{d}v=\frac{\mu(f)}{\kappa}.

(22)

We can now use the occupation times formula to write

\begin{split}\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}&=\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{0}^{t}\phi\left(\kappa W_{s}/\epsilon\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)L_{t}^{x}\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)(L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0})\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z=0
as noted above. Thus, recalling that
K_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{1/\alpha-2}(L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0})\mathrm{d}x
in this case, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\epsilon^{1/\alpha}\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\kappa^{1/\alpha}K_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi(\kappa x/\epsilon)-\kappa^{1/\alpha}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{1/\alpha-2}\right|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\mathrm{d}x.

We conclude by dominated convergence. First, by (
20
), the integrand converges to
0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
.

We next use (
21
) to dominate
R_{\epsilon}(x)=|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{1/\alpha-2}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi(\kappa x/\epsilon)|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|
by

A(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)(|x|^{\delta}+|x|^{-\delta})|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\leq M(|x|^{1/\alpha-2}+1)(|x|^{\delta}+|x|^{-\delta})|\min(1,|x|^{\theta}),

for some random constant
M>0
and
\theta\in(0,1/2)
fixed. We used (
8
) and the fact that
\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|<\infty
a.s. This bound is integrable on
\mathbb{R}
if we choose
\delta>0
and
\theta\in(0,1/2)
such that
1/\alpha-2+\delta<-1
and
1/\alpha-2+\theta-\delta>-1
. This is possible because
-3/2<1/\alpha-2<-1
.

Item (ii): Convergence
. Setting
\xi_{\epsilon}=\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\ell(1/\epsilon)\int_{-1}^{1}\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)\mathrm{d}x
and using the occupation times formula, we have

\epsilon\ell(1/\epsilon)H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\xi_{\epsilon}L_{t}^{0}=\int_{|x|\geq 1}\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)L_{t}^{x}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{|x|\leq 1}\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi\left(\kappa x/\epsilon\right)(L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0})\mathrm{d}x

Then, remembering that
K_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{-1}(L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\bm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}})\mathrm{d}x
, we write

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\epsilon H_{t}^{\epsilon}-\xi_{\epsilon}L_{t}^{0}-\kappa K_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leq 1}\left|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi(\kappa x/\epsilon)-\kappa\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{-1}\right|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|L_{t}^{x}-L_{t}^{0}\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{|x|>1}\left|\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\ell(1/\epsilon)\phi(\kappa x/\epsilon)-\kappa\mathrm{sgn}_{a,b}(x)|x|^{-1}\right|L_{T}^{x}\mathrm{d}x,

Again, we conclude by dominated convergence. By (

3.4
The diffusive regime and critical diffusive regime

This case is standard and we use the strategy explained in the introduction. It consists in solving the Poisson equation
\mathcal{L}g=f
, see Jacod-Shiryaev
[
15

. We recall the function

defined by

g(x)=2\int_{0}^{x}\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(v)\int_{v}^{\infty}f(u)[\sigma^{2}(u)\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(u)]^{-1}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

which is a
C^{2}

. Also since

is a regular positive recurrent diffusion by Assumption
1

tends in law to

[

7

Proof of Theorem

.

Using Itô formula with the function

\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}-g(X_{t}).

Thus
\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s=M_{t}^{\epsilon}-Y_{t}^{\epsilon}
where
M_{t}^{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}
is a local martingale and

converges in law as

in probability, for each fixed

. Moreover if

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}Y_{t}^{\epsilon}\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0

We now show that

[

) to show that for each

\langle M^{\epsilon}\rangle_{t}=\epsilon\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}[g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})]^{2}\mathrm{d}s
and the result follows from Remark
21

All in all we have proved that
(\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s)_{t\geq 0}
converges in finite dimensional distributions to

and as a continuous process if

∎

Remark 24
.

We stress that it is plausible we can show the limit theorem in the Lévy regime, at least when
f_{+}=-f_{-}
, using the martingale strategy, i.e. writing
\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s=M_{t}-g(X_{t})
where
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a local martingale with bracket
\int_{0}^{t}[g^{\prime}(X_{s})\sigma(X_{s})]^{2}\mathrm{d}s
. Using similar arguments as in Lemma
23

might behave like a Brownian motion subordinate by a stable subordinator, which is known to be a stable process.

4
Applications

Here we give some examples of application.
Each time, we try to explain intuitively why the resulting limiting stable process is not a Brownian motion.
It can be either because (a)
f
is large near infinity even if the diffusion (
1
) has small
return times to
0
(as studied by Jara-Komorowski-Olla
[
16
]
in the case of Markov chains),
or (b) the diffusion (
1
) has large return times to
0
, meaning that its invariant
distribution has a rather slow decay; (c) or for both reasons.

In case (a), and as we will see only in case (a), one easily determines the index
\alpha

of the limiting stable process
from the behavior of
\mu(\{f>x\})
as
x\to\pm\infty
; namely,
\mu(\{f>x\})\sim|x|^{-\alpha}
,
up to a constant or a slowly varying function.

Diffusions with fast decay invariant measure :
Consider the following stochastic differential equation

X_{t}=-\frac{\theta+1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{sgn}(X_{s})|X_{s}|^{\theta}\mathrm{d}s+B_{t},

(25)

where
\theta>0
and
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion. This model includes the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
\theta=1
). Following equations (
2
) and (
3
), the invariant measure and the scale function are given by

\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\kappa e^{-|x|^{\theta+1}}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathfrak{s}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}e^{|v|^{\theta+1}}\mathrm{d}v.

One can check that
\mathfrak{s}(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\sim}\mathrm{sgn}(x)e^{|x|^{\theta+1}}/[(\theta+1)|x|^{\theta}]
. Hence, as soon as
f
is a continuous function such that there exists
\alpha>0
and
(f_{+},f_{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying

|x|^{(1/\alpha-2)\theta}e^{-|x|^{\theta+1}/\alpha}f(x)\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{\pm},

and
|f_{+}|+|f_{-}|>0
, we can apply Theorem
5
with
\ell\equiv 1
, provided
\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s
is replaced by
\int_{0}^{t/\epsilon}[f(X_{s})-\mu(f)]\mathrm{d}s
when
\alpha>1
. We can also generalize using slowly varying functions.

Roughly, the return times of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
have exponential moments,
because its invariant distribution has an exponential decay. To get an

\alpha
-stable process at the limit with some
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the function
f

really has to be large near infinity. Namely, we need that
there exists a slowly varying function
L
and
c_{+},c_{-}>0
such that

L(x)|x|^{\alpha}\mu(\{f>x\})\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}c_{+}\quad\text{and}\quad L(x)|x|^{\alpha}\mu(\{f<x\})\underset{x\to-\infty}{\longrightarrow}c_{+}.

(26)

A toy kinetic model :
Here we slightly generalize the results of
[
24
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
20
]
and
[
12

. Consider a one-dimensional particle with position

and velocity

subject to random shocks and a force field

\beta>1
and a function

(27)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion modeling the random shocks. The invariant measure and the scale function are given by

\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\kappa[\Theta(x)]^{\beta}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathfrak{s}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}[\Theta(v)]^{-\beta}\mathrm{d}v.

We make the following assumption on the function
\Theta
:

Assumption 25
.

There exists
(c_{-},c_{+})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
such that
|x|\Theta(x)\longrightarrow c_{\pm}
as
x\to\pm\infty
and
c_{+}+c_{-}>0

c_{+}=0
(respectively
c_{-}=0

\Theta^{\prime}(x)<0
(respectively for all
x\leq-A
,
\Theta^{\prime}(x)>0
).

Obviously,
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\mathfrak{s}(x)=\pm\infty
and since
\beta>1
,
\mu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
. Now take
f=\text{id}
, we want to find
\alpha>0
such that Assumption
3
holds. Let us show that, with
\alpha=(\beta+1)/3
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-2}|\mathfrak{s}(x)|^{2-1/\alpha}x\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}(\beta+1)^{1/\alpha-2}c_{+}^{\beta/\alpha},

(28)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-2}|\mathfrak{s}(x)|^{2-1/\alpha}x\underset{x\to-\infty}{\longrightarrow}-(\beta+1)^{1/\alpha-2}c_{-}^{\beta/\alpha}.

(29)

Observe that we have the following estimations

|x|^{\beta}|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-1}\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}c_{\pm}^{\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad|x|^{\beta+1}|\mathfrak{s}(x)|^{-1}\underset{x\to\pm\infty}{\longrightarrow}(\beta+1)c_{\pm}^{\beta}.

(30)

Hence if
c_{+}>0
and
c_{-}>0
, the result is immediate. We consider e.g. the case
c_{+}=0
. First we notice that
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\beta\Theta^{\prime}(x)[\Theta(x)]^{-\beta-1}
and thus Assumption
25

A>0

\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}

0\leq\mathfrak{s}(x)\leq\mathfrak{s}(A)+\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)(x-A)\leq\mathfrak{s}(A)+\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)x,

and thus for all

|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-2}|\mathfrak{s}(x)|^{2-1/\alpha}x\leq|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-2}|\mathfrak{s}(A)+\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)x|^{2-1/\alpha}x\underset{x\to\infty}{\sim}|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-1/\alpha}|x|^{3-1/\alpha}

But since
\alpha=(\beta+1)/3

|\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)|^{-1/\alpha}|x|^{3-1/\alpha}=|x^{\beta}[\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x)]^{-1}|^{1/\alpha}

When

Remark that for this model, the return times do not have exponential moments, because
the invariant distribution has a slow decay.
The exponent
\alpha
is not prescribed by the invariant measure. Indeed we have

|x|^{\beta-1}\mu(\{f>x\})\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\kappa c_{+}^{\beta}/(\beta-1)\quad\text{and}\quad|x|^{\beta-1}\mu(\{f<x\})\underset{x\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\kappa c_{-}^{\beta}/(\beta-1)

\beta\in(1,2)

(\beta+1)/3>\beta-1

\beta>2

(\beta+1)/3<\beta-1

(i)

Now if
