Theory of possible sliding regimes in twisted bilayer WTe 2
Yi-Ming Wu, Chaitanya Murthy, Steven A. Kivelson

Abstract
Inspired by the observation of increasingly one-dimensional (1D) behavior with decreasing temperature in small-angle twisted bilayers of WTe 2 (tWTe 2 ), we theoretically explore the exotic sliding regimes that could be realized in tWTe 2 . At zero displacement field, while hole-doped tWTe 2 can be thought of as an array of weakly coupled conventional two-flavor 1D electron gases (1DEGs), the electron-doped regime is equivalent to coupled four -flavor 1DEGs, due to the presence of an additional “valley” degree of freedom. In the decoupled limit, the electron-doped system can thus realize phases with a range of interesting ordering tendencies, including 4 ​ k F 4k_{F} charge-density-wave and charge- 4 ​ e 4e superconductivity. Dimensional crossovers and cross-wire transport due to inter-wire couplings of various kinds are also discussed. We find that a sliding Luther–Emery liquid with small inter-wire couplings is probably most consistent with current experiments on hole-doped tWTe 2 .

Interwire effects
—
Finally, we address the effect of weak inter-wire couplings.

As discussed in the main text and in the above section that we model the decoupled 1D wire system with multi components labeled by

The spin degeneracy ensures that the spin-up fermions and spin-down fermions have the same Fermi momentum

For simplicity, we also assume that

(29)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle R_{\uparrow u}L_{\uparrow d}R_{\uparrow d}L_{\uparrow u}+sR_{\downarrow d}L_{\downarrow u}R_{\downarrow u}L_{\downarrow d}.

Suppose we evaluate this operator perturbatively in the presence of

to the first order of

Note in the main text we have introduced new definitions for these backscattering terms as follows,

g_{2}:=V_{1,2},~~g_{1}:=V_{1,3},~~g_{3}:=V_{1,4}.

Alternatively, we can use
-V_{1,2}

(32)

where we have used the new convention defined in Eq.(

The field

\displaystyle\text{Spin density fluctuations}:~\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{1}(x),

(34)

\displaystyle\text{Valley density fluctuations}:~\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{2}(x),

\displaystyle\text{Spin-valley density fluctuations}:~\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{3}(x).

It is related to the

Expressing the fermion density operators in terms of

\displaystyle{}^{2}+[\rho_{-,i}(x)]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{[\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{i}(x)-\rho_{0,i}]^{2}+[\Pi_{i}(x)]^{2}}{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\rho_{+,i}(x)\rho_{-,i}(x)+\rho_{-,i}(x)\rho_{+,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{[\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{i}(x)-\rho_{0,i}]^{2}-[\Pi_{i}(x)]^{2}}{2}.

Substituting these relations to Eq.(

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{3}\int dx\left\{\frac{\pi v_{i}K_{i}}{2}\left[\Pi_{i}(x)\right]^{2}+\frac{v_{i}}{2\pi K_{i}}\left[\partial_{x}\phi_{i}(x)\right]^{2}\right\}

(38)

\displaystyle-\frac{4g_{2}}{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}}\int dx\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{3})-\frac{4g_{1}}{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}}\int dx\cos(2\phi_{2})\cos(2\phi_{3})-\frac{4g_{3}}{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}}\int dx\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2}),

Here

\displaystyle V_{0}=V_{4,1}+V_{4,2}+V_{4,3}+V_{4,4}+V_{2,1}-V_{1,1}+V_{2,2}+V_{2,3}+V_{2,4},

(40)

\displaystyle U_{0}=V_{4,1}+V_{4,2}+V_{4,3}+V_{4,4}-V_{2,1}+V_{1,1}-V_{2,2}-V_{2,3}-V_{2,4},

\displaystyle V_{1}=V_{4,1}-V_{4,2}+V_{4,3}-V_{4,4}+V_{2,1}-V_{1,1}-V_{2,2}+V_{2,3}-V_{2,4},

\displaystyle U_{1}=V_{4,1}-V_{4,2}+V_{4,3}-V_{4,4}-V_{2,1}+V_{1,1}+V_{2,2}-V_{2,3}+V_{2,4},

\displaystyle V_{2}=V_{4,1}+V_{4,2}-V_{4,3}-V_{4,4}+V_{2,1}-V_{1,1}+V_{2,2}-V_{2,3}-V_{2,4},

\displaystyle U_{2}=V_{4,1}+V_{4,2}-V_{4,3}-V_{4,4}-V_{2,1}+V_{1,1}-V_{2,2}+V_{2,3}+V_{2,4},

\displaystyle V_{3}=V_{4,1}-V_{4,2}-V_{4,3}+V_{4,4}+V_{2,1}-V_{1,1}-V_{2,2}-V_{2,3}+V_{2,4},

\displaystyle U_{3}=V_{4,1}-V_{4,2}-V_{4,3}+V_{4,4}-V_{2,1}+V_{1,1}+V_{2,2}+V_{2,3}-V_{2,4}.

In a special case when all

\displaystyle G_{0}=4(V_{4}+V_{2}),~~U_{0}=4(V_{4}-V_{2}),

\displaystyle G_{1}=U_{1}=G_{2}=U_{2}=G_{3}=U_{3}=0.

Without the backscattering (cosine) terms, Eq.(

The Hamiltonian in Eq.(

S_{tot}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}S_{i}+S_{I}

where

\displaystyle S_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi K_{i}}\int dxd\tau\left\{\frac{1}{v_{i}}\left(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}+v_{i}\left(\partial_{x}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}\right\}

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi K_{i}}\int dxdy\left\{\tilde{v}_{i}\left(\partial_{x}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{v}_{i}}\left(\partial_{y}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}\right\}

with

and
\tilde{v}_{i}=v_{i}/\bar{v}
with

. The integration limits will be the same in zero temperature limit. Similarly,

S_{I}=-\frac{4}{(2\pi\alpha)^{2}}\int d^{2}\bm{r}\left[\frac{g_{2}}{\bar{v}}\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{3})+\frac{g_{1}}{\bar{v}}\cos(2\phi_{2})\cos(2\phi_{3})+\frac{g_{3}}{\bar{v}}\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2})\right].

(45)

Without the cosine terms, the regularized Green’s function is given by

G(x,t):=\braket{\phi_{j}(x,t)\phi_{j}(0,0)}=-\frac{K_{j}}{4}\ln\left[\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}+a^{2}}{R^{2}}\right]_{y\to iv_{j}t+a}=-\frac{K_{j}}{4}\ln\left[\frac{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right]

(46)

where

and
R\to\infty
are the UV and IR cutoff respectively. According to the T-duality we have

G_{\theta\theta}(x,t):=\braket{\theta_{j}(x,t)\theta_{j}(0,0)}=-\frac{1}{4K_{j}}\ln\left[\frac{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right].

(47)

The Green’s function between
\phi_{j}
field and
\theta_{j}
field is calculated from the original action when both of these two fields are present,

G_{\phi\theta}(x,t):=\braket{\phi_{j}(x,t)\theta_{j}(0,0)}=-\frac{1}{4}\ln\frac{i(v_{j}t+x)+a}{i(v_{j}t-x)+a}.

(48)

On the other hand, if the boson becomes massive, namely

S_{i}=\frac{1}{2\pi K_{i}}\int dxdy\left\{\left(\partial_{x}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\partial_{y}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}+m^{2}\phi_{i}^{2}\right\},

(49)

then the correlation function is given by

\braket{\phi_{i}(\bm{r})\phi_{i}(0)}=\frac{K_{i}}{2}K_{0}(m|\bm{r}|)

(50)

where

is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, which decays exponentially at large distance,

(51)

(52)

Therefore, we can identify the correlation length as

which is finite. The correlation function in space-time coordinates in the massive case is thus

G(x,t)=\frac{K_{j}}{2}K_{0}\left(\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}}{\xi}\right).

(53)

III
III. Order parameters and correlation functions at Gaussian fixed point

To obtain the correlation functions, one can make use of the general formula for the vertex operator correlation functions for a massless boson model (let’s at this point neglect the logarithmic corrections due to finite backscattering in the limit when all
K_{i}\to 1
),

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\beta_{1}\phi(\bm{r}_{1})}...e^{i\beta_{N}\phi(\bm{r}_{N})}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{i>j}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}G(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{r}_{j})\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\beta_{i}^{2}G(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{r}_{i})\right)

(54)

This equation can be proved by using the cumulant expansion formula for Gaussian action.

III.1
Massless boson

If the boson model is massless, we expect that the expectation value of the vertex operator vanishes. This is because

\braket{e^{i\beta\phi_{j}(x)}}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}G(0)\right]=\left(\frac{a}{R}\right)^{\frac{\beta^{2}K_{j}}{4}},

(55)

where
G(0)
is the Green’s function in Eq.(
46
) in the IR limit. Apparently, this results vanishes in the limit when
R\to\infty
. This results is consistent with the fact that in the massless case the expectation value of order paramters remains zero.

For multi-point correlation functions, we use the explicit formula for the Green’s function in Eq.(
46
) in the massless case and obtain

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\beta_{1}\phi(\bm{r}_{1})}...e^{i\beta_{N}\phi(\bm{r}_{N})}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i>j}\left[\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}+a^{2}}{R^{2}}\right]^{\frac{\beta_{i}\beta_{j}K}{4}}\prod_{i}\left[\frac{a^{2}}{R^{2}}\right]^{\frac{\beta_{i}^{2}K}{8}}

(56)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i>j}\left[\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}+a^{2}}{a^{2}}\right]^{\frac{\beta_{i}\beta_{j}K}{4}}\left[\frac{a}{R}\right]^{\frac{K}{8}(\sum_{i}\beta_{i})^{2}}.

The correlation function does not vanish in the limit
R\to\infty
only if
\sum_{i}\beta_{i}=0
. From this general expression for correlations functions, it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\phi_{j}(x,t)-i\phi_{j}(0)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{K_{j}}{4}},

(57)

\displaystyle\braket{\cos\phi_{j}(x,t)\cos\phi_{j}(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\sin\phi_{j}(x,t)\sin\phi_{j}(0,0)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{K_{j}}{4}},

\displaystyle\braket{\cos\phi_{j}(x,t)\sin\phi_{j}(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\sin\phi_{j}(x,t)\cos\phi_{j}(0,0)}=0.

The correlation functions for the
\theta
field can be found by the duality relation in Eq.(
42
).

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\theta_{j}(x,t)-i\theta_{j}(0)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4K_{j}}},

(58)

\displaystyle\braket{\cos\theta_{j}(x,t)\cos\theta_{j}(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\sin\theta_{j}(x,t)\sin\theta_{j}(0,0)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4K_{j}}},

\displaystyle\braket{\cos\theta_{j}(x,t)\sin\theta_{j}(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\sin\theta_{j}(x,t)\cos\theta_{j}(0,0)}=0.

III.2
Massive boson

If we use the massive action with the Green’s function given by Eq.(
53
), and if the expectation value of
\phi_{j}
is
\braket{\phi_{j}}=0
, we obtain for the expectation value

\braket{e^{i\beta\phi_{j}(x)}}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}G(0)\right]=\left(\frac{a}{\xi}\right)^{\frac{\beta^{2}K_{j}}{4}},

(59)

which remains finite as long as
\xi=1/m
is finite.

The two point correlation function
\braket{e^{i\phi_{j}(\bm{r})-i\phi_{j}(0)}}
is of long range, and the fluctuation

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\phi_{j}(\bm{r})-i\phi_{j}(0)}}-\braket{e^{i\phi_{j}(\bm{r})}}\braket{e^{-i\phi_{j}(0)}}

(60)

\displaystyle=e^{G(\bm{r})-G(0)}-e^{-G(0)}\approx e^{-G(0)}G(\bm{r})=\left(\frac{a}{\xi}\right)^{\frac{K_{j}}{2}}\frac{K_{j}}{2}e^{-\frac{r}{\xi}}

has exponential decaying behavior at large distances, due to the presence of the mass term.

When a mass term of
\phi_{j}(x)
field develops, the correlation functions of
e^{i(...)\theta_{j}(x)}
has exponentially decaying behavior. A direct calculation based on the effective
\theta
-only action is difficult, but some arguments based on symmetry and continuity all supports this result
Voit 1998
.

IV. Renormalization group analysis

To proceed with the RG analysis, we reformulate the action as follows

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\int d^{2}\bm{r}\left(\tilde{v}_{i}(\partial_{x}\phi_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{v}_{i}}(\partial_{y}\phi_{i})^{2}\right)

(73)

\displaystyle+\int d^{2}\bm{r}\left[u_{2}\cos(\beta_{1}\phi_{1})\cos(\beta_{3}\phi_{3})+u_{1}\cos(\beta_{2}\phi_{2})\cos(\beta_{3}\phi_{3})+u_{3}\cos(\beta_{1}\phi_{1})\cos(\beta_{2}\phi_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=S_{0}[\phi_{i}]+S_{I}[\phi_{i}]

where we have introduced

u_{i}=\frac{-g_{i}}{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}\bar{v}},~~\beta_{i}=\sqrt{4\pi K_{i}}

(74)

Each of these fields
\phi_{i}(x)
can be separated into slow mode
\phi_{i}^{s}(x)
and fast mode
h_{i}(x)

\phi_{i}(x)=\phi_{i}^{s}(x)+h_{i}(x)

(75)

and the Gaussian part of the action is also separable

S_{0}[\phi_{i}]=S_{0}[\phi_{i}^{s}]+S_{0}[h_{i}].

(76)

The partition function is then

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}[\phi_{i}^{s}]e^{-S_{0}[\phi_{i}^{s}]}\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}[h_{i}]e^{-S_{0}[h_{i}]}e^{-S_{I}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}

(77)

\displaystyle=Z_{h}\int\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}[\phi_{i}^{s}]e^{-S_{0}[\phi_{i}^{s}]}\braket{e^{-S_{I}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}}_{h}

\displaystyle=Z_{h}\int\prod_{i}\mathcal{D}[\phi_{i}^{s}]e^{-S_{\text{eff}}[\phi_{i}^{s}]}

where

S_{\text{eff}}[\phi_{i}^{s}]=S_{0}[\phi_{i}^{s}]+\braket{S_{I}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\braket{S_{I}^{2}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}_{h}-\braket{S_{I}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}_{h}^{2}\right)

(78)

and
Z_{h}
is the partition function coming from fast mode which is unimportant, and
\braket{...}_{h}
means average over all the fast mode under
e^{-S_{0}[h_{i}]}
.

We begin with the first order corrections, i.e. the term
\braket{S_{I}[\phi_{i}^{s}+h_{i}]}_{h}
. As an example, we discuss only one of the three interacting terms,

\braket{u_{2}\int d^{2}\bm{r}\cos[\beta_{1}(\phi_{1}^{s}+h_{1})]\cos[\beta_{3}(\phi_{3}^{s}+h_{3})]}_{h}=u_{2}\int d^{2}\bm{r}\cos(\beta_{1}\phi_{1}^{s})\braket{e^{i\beta_{1}h_{1}}}_{h_{1}}\cos(\beta_{3}\phi_{3}^{s})\braket{e^{i\beta_{3}h_{3}}}_{h_{3}}.

(79)

The average of the vertex
e^{i\beta_{1}h_{1}}
and
e^{i\beta_{3}h_{3}}
is evaluated as follows,

\braket{e^{i\beta_{1}h_{1}}}_{h_{1}}\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}\braket{h_{1}^{2}}_{h_{1}}

(80)

and in the
T\to 0
limit,

\displaystyle\braket{h_{1}^{2}}_{h_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda^{\prime}<\sqrt{k^{2}+(\omega/\bar{v})^{2}}<\Lambda}\frac{dkd\omega}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{v_{1}k^{2}+\omega^{2}/v_{1}}

(81)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda^{\prime}<\sqrt{k^{2}+(\omega/\bar{v})^{2}}<\Lambda}\frac{dkd(\omega/\bar{v})}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{v_{1}}{\bar{v}}\frac{1}{(v_{1}/\bar{v})^{2}k^{2}+(\omega/\bar{v})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda^{\prime}<p<\Lambda}\frac{pdpd\theta}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{v_{1}}{\bar{v}}\frac{1}{(v_{1}/\bar{v})^{2}p^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+p^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}=\frac{dl}{2\pi}\frac{v_{1}}{\bar{v}}\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\frac{1}{(v_{1}/\bar{v})^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta}=\frac{dl}{2\pi}.

where we use
dl=d\Lambda/\Lambda>0
. Note this result is independent of
v_{1}
in the zero temperature limit, so for other sectors it remains the same. Using this result, Eq.(
79
) becomes

u_{2}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}+\beta_{3}^{2}}{4\pi}dl\right)\int d^{2}\bm{r}\cos(\beta_{1}\phi_{1}^{s})\cos(\beta_{3}\phi_{3}^{s})

(82)

and other terms in the first order correction are obtained in exactly the same way.
To compare with the original action, we need to rescale the real-space
\phi_{i}^{s}
back to
\phi
. From the Gaussian level, we see
\phi_{i}^{s}
does not change, but since the small distance cutoff is now
1+dl
larger than the last step, we need to multiply
(1+dl)^{2}\approx(1+2dl)
to Eq.(
82

74

\beta_{j}^{2}/(4\pi)=K_{j}

\displaystyle\frac{du_{2}}{dl}

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{dl}

\displaystyle=\left[2-(K_{2}+K_{3})\right]u_{1},

V. Self-consistent Gaussian approximation for the massive phase

We now discuss when the three double-cosine terms in Eq.(
73
) are relevant, the gap can be determined self-consistently in a variational approach, also known as self-consistent Gaussian approximation. Let’s use the notations formulated in Eq.(
44
) and(
45
).

Deep inside the massive phase, we can approximate the action as some Gaussian form, expecting the soliton modes are excluded in the low energy limit. To this end, we write the variational Gaussian action as (for
i=1,2,3
)

S_{\text{var}}[\phi_{i}]=\frac{T}{2L}\sum_{n,q}\sum_{i,j}G^{-1}_{ij}(q,\omega_{n})\phi_{i}^{*}(q,\omega_{n})\phi_{j}(q,\omega_{n})

(104)

where

G^{-1}(\bm{q})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{v_{1}}{\pi K_{1}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{v_{1}^{2}}+q^{2}+\frac{\Delta_{1}^{2}}{v_{1}^{2}}\right)&\Delta_{12}&\Delta_{13}\\
\Delta_{21}&\frac{v_{2}}{\pi K_{2}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{v_{2}^{2}}+q^{2}+\frac{\Delta_{2}^{2}}{v_{2}^{2}}\right)&\Delta_{23}\\
\Delta_{31}&\Delta_{32}&\frac{v_{3}}{\pi K_{3}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{v_{3}^{2}}+q^{2}+\frac{\Delta_{3}^{2}}{v_{3}^{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix}

(105)

and
\Delta_{i}
is the gap for the
i
-th sector.
We will assume that
\Delta_{ij}=\Delta_{ji}
so
G^{-1}
is a symmetric matrix which can be diagonalize by an orthogonal matrix
U
:

\phi^{\dagger}G^{-1}\phi=\tilde{\phi}^{\dagger}G_{d}^{-1}\tilde{\phi}

(106)

where

\phi=U\tilde{\phi},~~G^{-1}=UG_{d}^{-1}U^{-1}

(107)

and
G_{d}
is now a diagonal matrix.

The variational free energy to be minimized is,

F_{\text{var}}=-T\ln Z_{\text{var}}+T\braket{S-S_{\text{var}}}_{\text{var}}

(108)

where
Z_{\text{var}}
is the partition function evaluated using
S_{\text{var}}
and the original
S
is given in Eqs.(
44
) and (
45
). The first term in Eq.(
108
) can be evaluated directly,

-T\ln Z_{\text{var}}=-T\text{Tr}\ln G(q,\omega_{n}).

(109)

Note in the second term,
\braket{S_{\text{var}}}_{\text{var}}
does not contain dependence on the variational parameter, so it can be dropped.
We are then left with evaluating
\braket{S}_{\text{var}}
, which involves in evaluating the average of the product of two cosine terms
g_{i}\cos(2\phi_{j})\cos(2\phi_{k})
using the Gaussian distribution function. This requires one first identify the classical ground state of these cosine terms. In the case when there is no frustration, i.e. when
\sgn(g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})>0
, the classical ground state is easy to find. When all
g_{i}>0
, all
\phi_{i}
should be around
\phi_{i}=0
. when
g_{i}>0
and
g_{j},g_{k}<0
, we should have
\phi_{j,k}=0
and
\phi_{i}=\pm\pi/2
. Since
\phi_{i}
is around
\pm\pi/2
we can shift
\phi_{i}
by
\pm\pi/2
such that
\cos 2\phi_{i}
picks up a minus sign, which can be absorbed in
g_{j}
and
g_{k}
. Therefore, in the case without frustration, we can use the absolute value of
g_{i,j,k}
and then average all the cosine terms at
\phi_{i,j,k}
around
0
.

\displaystyle\braket{S}_{\text{var}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{v_{i}}{2\pi K_{i}}\sum_{n,q}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}+q^{2}\right)G_{i,i}(q,\omega_{n})

(110)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}\frac{L}{T}\left(|g_{1}|\braket{e^{i2(\phi_{2}+\phi_{3})}+e^{i2(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3})}}_{\text{var}}+|g_{2}|\braket{e^{i2(\phi_{3}+\phi_{1})}+e^{i2(\phi_{3}-\phi_{1})}}_{\text{var}}+|g_{3}|\braket{e^{i2(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})}+e^{i2(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})}}_{\text{var}}\right).

Here we average the
\cos(2\phi_{i})
around
\phi_{i}=0
. Going to the
\tilde{\phi}
basis, we have

\braket{e^{i2(\phi_{2}\pm\phi_{3})}}_{\text{var}}=\braket{e^{i2\sum_{j}(U_{2j}\pm U_{3j})\tilde{\phi}_{j}}}_{\text{var}}=e^{-\frac{2T}{L}\sum_{n,q}\sum_{j}(U_{2j}\pm U_{3j})^{2}G_{d,j}(q,\omega_{n})}.

(111)

Since
G_{d}
is diagonal, and
U^{T}=U^{-1}
, we have

\braket{e^{i2(\phi_{2}\pm\phi_{3})}}_{\text{var}}=e^{-\frac{2T}{L}\sum_{n,q}(G_{22}+G_{33}\pm 2G_{23})}

(112)

and similarly for other two terms in Eq.(
110
).
Therefore

\displaystyle\braket{S}_{\text{var}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{v_{i}}{2\pi K_{i}}\sum_{n,q}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}+q^{2}\right)G_{i,i}(q,\omega_{n})-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}\frac{L}{T}\times

(113)

\displaystyle\times\left(|g_{1}|e^{-\frac{2T}{L}\sum_{n,q}(G_{22}+G_{33})}\cosh[4\text{Tr}G_{23}]+|g_{2}|e^{-\frac{2T}{L}\sum_{n,q}(G_{11}+G_{33})}\cosh[4\text{Tr}G_{13}]+|g_{3}|e^{-\frac{2T}{L}\sum_{n,q}(G_{11}+G_{22})}\cosh[4\text{Tr}G_{12}]\right).

Here
\text{Tr}G_{ij}=\frac{T}{L}\sum_{q,n}G_{ij}(q,\omega_{n})
.
To obtain
G
, we need to inverse the matrix

. In the case when all
\Delta_{ij}
are close to zero, we can approximately have

VI. Transport property

We first show if only the CDW order is considered in the inter-wire coupling, there is no transverse current. To see this, we note the inter-wire coupling Hamiltonian for CDW order is

\displaystyle H_{c}

\displaystyle=J\sum_{\braket{ij}}\int dxO_{\text{CDW}}^{\dagger}(i,x)O_{\text{CDW}}(j,x)+\text{h.c.}

(121)

\displaystyle=J\sum_{\braket{ij},\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\int dx\psi^{\dagger}_{i,-,\lambda}(x)\psi_{i,+,\lambda}(x)\psi^{\dagger}_{j,+,\lambda^{\prime}}(x)\psi_{j,-,\lambda^{\prime}}(x)+(i\leftrightarrow j).

which is the most relevant one when the system is in the massive phase with gaps in all
\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}
sectors and in the presence of strong repulsion.
From this Hamiltonian, one can calculate the current density operator from the continuity equation
\partial_{y}j_{y}=i[\rho(i,x),H_{c}]=0
and find the current vanishes. Note the vanishing is local, i.e. it’s not because two current operators on
i
and
j
cancel each other, but rather the current on each wire vanishes.
Therefore, we see that by only turning on the CDW coupling is not enough for obtaining a finite cross-wire conductivity, we must have other couplings.

The most trivial one, will be the single particle tunneling (keeping only the nearest neighbor),

H_{t}=t_{\bot}\sum_{i,rr^{\prime},\lambda}\int dx\psi_{r,\lambda}^{\dagger}(i,x)\psi_{r^{\prime},\lambda}(i+1,x)+\text{h.c.}

(122)

The tunneling charge current due to the presence of this Hamiltonian is given by the continuity equation, and we obtain

\displaystyle j_{y}(i,x)

\displaystyle=ict_{\bot}\sum_{r,\lambda}\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{r,\lambda}(i,x)\psi_{r,\lambda}(i+1,x)-\psi^{\dagger}_{r,\lambda}(i+1,x)\psi_{r,\lambda}(i,x)\right),

(123)

\displaystyle j_{y}(q_{y},x)

\displaystyle=-2ct_{\bot}\sum_{r,\lambda}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dk_{y}\psi^{\dagger}_{r,\lambda}(k_{y}-\frac{q_{y}}{2},x)\psi_{r,\lambda}(k_{y}+\frac{q_{y}}{2},x)\sin k_{y}e^{i\frac{q_{y}}{2}},

where
c
is the spacing between adjacent wires, and since
j_{y}(i,x)
is Hermitian,
j_{y}(q_{y},x)^{\dagger}=j_{y}(-q_{y},x).

Below, we will evaluate the current-current correlation in the Hamiltonian without
H_{t}
, similar to the approach in Ref.
Moser et al. 1998
. However, we will keep
H_{c}
to the first order as in a perturbation theory. According to Kubo formula, the real part of conductivity is given by

\text{Re}\sigma_{\bot}(\omega)=-\frac{e^{2}}{\omega}\text{Im}\left[\lim_{q_{y}\to 0}\int dx\Pi_{\bot}^{R}(q_{y},x,\omega)\right]

(124)

where
\Pi_{\bot}^{R}(q_{y},x,\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the retarded current-current correlation function, which can be obtained from

\Pi_{\bot}(q_{y},x,i\omega_{n})=\braket{j_{y}(q_{y},x,i\omega_{n})j_{y}(-q_{y},0,-i\omega_{n})}

(125)

by analytic continuation. Here we have assumed translation symmetry. To the first order in
H_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{q_{y}\to 0}\int dx\Pi_{\bot}(q_{y},x,i\omega_{n})=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{ik_{n}}\sum_{r,\lambda}\int\frac{dk_{x}dk_{y}}{(2\pi)^{2}}4c^{2}t_{\bot}^{2}\sin^{2}k_{y}G_{r,\lambda}(k_{x},ik_{n})G_{r,\lambda}(k_{x},ik_{n}+i\omega_{n})

(126)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{J}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{ik_{n},ip_{n}}\sum_{r,\lambda}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}d^{2}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{4}}4c^{2}t_{\bot}^{2}\sin^{2}k_{y}\sin^{2}p_{y}G_{r,\lambda}(k_{x},ik_{n})G_{r,\lambda}(k_{x},ik_{n}+i\omega_{n})G_{-r,\lambda}(p_{x},ip_{n})G_{-r,\lambda}(p_{x},ip_{n}+i\omega_{n}).

Here we used the fact that all the Green’s functions are evaluated in the decoupled Hamiltonian, so these are essentially 1D Hamiltonians and do not depend on
k_{y}
or the wire index. Note in the second line of Eq.(
126
) the difference in the
r
-index between these Green’s functions are due to the nature of the CDW interaction
H_{c}
. Upon expressing the Green’s functions using their spectral representations

G(k_{x},ik_{n})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\varepsilon\frac{A(k_{x},\varepsilon)}{ik_{n}-\varepsilon},~~A(k_{x},\varepsilon)=-2\text{Im}G^{R}(k_{x},\varepsilon)

(127)

and analytically continuing on the real frequency axis by changing
i\omega_{n}
to
\omega+i0^{+}
, we obtain (after summing over
ik_{n}
)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\sigma_{\bot}(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}ct_{\bot}^{2}}{2\pi}\sum_{r,\lambda}\int dx\int d\varepsilon\frac{n_{F}(\varepsilon)-n_{F}(\varepsilon+\omega)}{\omega}A_{r,\lambda}(x,\varepsilon)A_{r,\lambda}(-x,\varepsilon+\omega)

(128)

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{2}Jt_{\bot}^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}\sum_{r,\lambda}\int dxdx^{\prime}P\int d\epsilon d\epsilon^{\prime}\frac{n_{F}(\epsilon)-n_{F}(\epsilon^{\prime})}{\omega+\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}}A_{r,\lambda}(x,\epsilon)A_{r,\lambda}(-x,\epsilon^{\prime})\int d\varepsilon\frac{n_{F}(\varepsilon)-n_{F}(\varepsilon+\omega)}{\omega}A_{-r,\lambda}(x^{\prime},\varepsilon)A_{-r,\lambda}(-x^{\prime},\varepsilon+\omega),

where in the second line we use
P\int
to denote the principle value integral.
To proceed with obtaining the temperature , we need the spectral function for the decoupled 1D system. But here we can estimate the magnitude of
\sigma_{\bot}
from the its prefactors (after restoring
\hbar
everywhere),

\sigma_{\bot}(T)=\frac{e^{2}}{h}4(ack_{F}^{2})\left(\frac{t_{\bot}}{E_{F}}\right)^{2}F\left(\frac{T}{E_{F}}\right)\approx 18\frac{e^{2}}{h}\left(\frac{t_{\bot}}{E_{F}}\right)^{2}F\left(\frac{T}{E_{F}}\right)~~\text{for}~~k_{F}\approx\frac{\pi}{2a}

(129)

where
F
is some dimensionless function that comes from the integral involving the spectral functions.

VI.1
A. Single-particle tunneling in the gapless case

We begin by discussing the conductivity due to the single particle tunneling process in the gapless case, i.e. all the four
\phi_{j}
fields are gapless. As shown above, this requires the calculation of the single particle spectral function.
By definition, the retarded single particle Green’s function is given by

G^{R}_{r,\lambda}(x,t)=-i\Theta(t)\braket{\{\psi_{r,\lambda}(x,t),\psi^{\dagger}_{r,\lambda}(0,0)\}}=-i\Theta(t)(G_{r,\lambda}(x,t)+G_{r,\lambda}(-x,-t))

(130)

where

G_{r,\lambda}(x,t)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{e^{irk_{F}x}}{2\pi\alpha}\braket{e^{i\theta_{\lambda}(x,t)-ir\phi_{\lambda}(x,t)}e^{-i\theta_{\lambda}(0,0)+ir\phi_{\lambda}(0,0)}}

(131)

according to Eq.(
25
). Without loss of generality, we take
r=+
and
\lambda=\uparrow u
. Using Eq.(
27
) we have

G_{r,\lambda}(x,t)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{e^{irk_{F}x}}{2\pi\alpha}\prod_{j=0}^{3}\braket{e^{\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(x,t)-\phi_{j}(x,t)]}e^{-\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(0,0)-\phi_{j}(0,0)]}}_{j}

(132)

For Gaussian models we have
\braket{e^{iA}e^{-iB}}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\braket{A^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\braket{B^{2}}+\braket{AB}\right)
, thus

\braket{e^{\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(x,t)-\phi_{j}(x,t)]}e^{-\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(0,0)-\phi_{j}(0,0)]}}_{j}=\exp\left[\frac{1}{4}\braket{\left(\theta_{j}(x,t)-\phi_{j}(x,t)\right)\left(\theta_{j}(0,0)-\phi_{j}(0,0)\right)}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}\braket{(\theta_{j}-\phi_{j})^{2}}_{j}\right].

(133)

Using Eq.(
46
), (
47
) and (
48
), it is easy to see

\braket{e^{\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(x,t)-\phi_{j}(x,t)]}e^{-\frac{i}{2}[\theta_{j}(0,0)-\phi_{j}(0,0)]}}_{j}=\left(\frac{a}{a+i(v_{j}t-x)}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{K_{j}+\frac{1}{K_{j}}-2}{16}}.

(134)

As a result, we have,

G_{+,\uparrow A}(x,t)=\frac{e^{ik_{F}x}}{2\pi}\prod_{j=0}^{3}\left(\frac{1}{a+i(v_{j}t-x)}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\gamma_{j}/2},~~~\gamma_{j}=\frac{K_{j}+\frac{1}{K_{j}}-2}{8}

(135)

In fact, this form does not depend on
\lambda
. For
r=-
, the only changes is to replace
x
with
-x
in the above expression.

For comparison, we note in the conventional two-component case, we have

G(x,t)\sim\prod_{j=c,s}\left(\frac{1}{a+i(v_{j}t-x)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+(iv_{j}t+a)^{2}}\right)^{\gamma_{j}/2},~~~\gamma_{j}=\frac{K_{j}+\frac{1}{K_{j}}-2}{4}.

(136)

Using this result, and if we neglect the correction due to the CDW order coupling, we have

\displaystyle\text{Re}\sigma_{\bot}(\omega\to 0,T)

\displaystyle\sim\int dx\int d\varepsilon\frac{1}{4T}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\right)A(x,\varepsilon)A(-x,\varepsilon+\omega)

(137)

\displaystyle\sim\int dx\int d\varepsilon\frac{1}{4T}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\right)F(x,\varepsilon)

where
F(x,\varepsilon)
obeys

F\left(ax,\frac{\varepsilon}{a}\right)=\frac{1}{a^{2\gamma}}F(x,\varepsilon),~~~\gamma=\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}.

(138)

The spectral function
A(x,\varepsilon)
has the following properties:

A(x=0,\varepsilon)=N(\varepsilon)\sim|\varepsilon|^{\gamma},~~~A(x\gg 1/\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\sim\frac{\cos{\varepsilon x}}{x^{\gamma}},

(139)

so the function
F(x,\varepsilon)
behaves like

\displaystyle F(x,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon^{2\gamma}~\text{for}~x\ll 1/\varepsilon,

(140)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{x^{2\gamma}}~\text{for}~x\gg 1/\varepsilon.

Therefore

\text{Re}\sigma_{\bot}(\omega\to 0,T)\sim\int d\varepsilon\frac{1}{4T}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}dx\varepsilon^{2\gamma}+\int_{1/\varepsilon}^{+\infty}dx\frac{\cos{2\varepsilon x}}{x^{2\gamma}}\right)\sim T^{2\gamma-1}.

(141)

In the main text, we introduce the scaling dimension of the single particle hopping
\alpha_{e}
, which is related to
\gamma
simply by
\gamma=\alpha_{e}-1
.

VI.2
B. Single-particle tunneling in the gapped case

When there is a gap opening in either one or more of the
\phi_{j}
fields, the total single particle density of states has a gap structure. This is because the total real pace Green’s function is a product of the four component, as shown in Eq.(
135
). Consequently, the spectral function (and hence the density of states), which is the Fourier transform of Eq.(
135
), is a convolution of different components. It is this convolution that leads to a gap in the total spectral function. For two component 1DEG this was demonstrated in Ref.
Voit 1998

If there is a gap in the total density of states, the conductivity becomes zero when
T
is below the gap energy scale. However, when
T
is well above the energy scale set by the gap, Eq.(
141
) still applies.

VI.3
C. Josephson tunneling in the gapped case

Unlike the single particle tunneling, the pair or quadruple hopping process does not depend on the single particle density of states, and can contribute to the cross wire conductivity even in the gapped phase and when
T
not well above the gap energy scale.
Below we discuss this Josephson tunneling due to charge-2e and charge-4e orders.

VI.3.1
charge-2e superconductivity

We take spin-triplet pseudospin-triplet pairing order parameter in Eq.(
68
) as an example. Assuming we are in the partially gapped phase when
\phi_{2,3}
are gapped. The tunneling is governed by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{J}

\displaystyle=J_{s}\sum_{\braket{y,y^{\prime}}}\int dxO_{T_{1},S}^{\dagger}(x,y)O_{T_{1},S}(x,y^{\prime})+\text{h.c.}

(142)

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{s}\sum_{\braket{y,y^{\prime}}}\int dx\cos\left[\theta_{0}(x,y)+\theta_{1}(x,y)-\theta_{0}(x,y^{\prime})-\theta_{1}(x,y^{\prime})\right],

where
\tilde{J}_{s}=J_{s}\delta_{2}^{K_{2}/2}\delta_{3}^{K_{3}/2}
and
J_{s}\sim t_{\bot}^{2}/E_{F}
.
On the other hand, the local charge density operator is given by
\rho_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{0}(x,y)
. Then according to the continuity equation, the transverse current density is found to be

j_{y}(x)=\tilde{J}_{s}c\sin[\theta_{0}(x,y+1)+\theta_{1}(x,y+1)-\theta_{0}(x,y)-\theta_{1}(x,y)]

(143)

which is the Josephson current. To evaluate the current-current correlation in the decoupled 1D system, we note that only the correlation involving two current operators with the same

survives. Thus we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{\bot}(x,y,\tau)

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}\braket{\sin[\theta_{0}(x,y+1)+\theta_{1}(x,y+1)-\theta_{0}(x,y)-\theta_{1}(x,y)]\sin[\theta_{0}(0,0+1)+\theta_{1}(0,0+1)-\theta_{0}(0,0)-\theta_{1}(0,0)]}

(144)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+v_{0}^{2}\tau^{2}+a^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2K_{0}}+\frac{1}{2K_{1}}}\delta_{y,0}.

\Pi_{\bot}(\bm{q}\to 0,\omega)=\int dxdte^{i\omega t}\sum_{y}\Pi_{\bot}(x,y,t)\sim\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}\omega^{1/K_{0}+1/K_{1}-2}

(145)

\text{Re}\sigma(\omega)\sim\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}\omega^{1/K_{0}+1/K_{1}-3}\to\text{Re}\sigma(T)\sim\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}T^{1/K_{0}+1/K_{1}-3}.

(146)

VI.3.2
charge-4e superconductivity

Like the charge-2e superconductivity case, the tunneling current is determined by the coupling Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{J}

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{s}\sum_{\braket{y,y^{\prime}}}\int dx\cos\left[2\theta_{0}(x,y)-2\theta_{0}(x,y^{\prime})\right].

(147)

The resulting current operator is

j_{y}(x)=\tilde{J}_{s}c\sin[2\theta_{0}(x,y+1)-2\theta_{0}(x,y)].

(148)

The calculation of conductivity is exactly the same as the 2e-SC case, with the modification of
\theta_{0}\to 2\theta_{0}
and hence
1/K_{0}\to 4/K_{0}
, and the final result is

\text{Re}\sigma(T)\sim\tilde{J}_{s}^{2}c^{2}T^{4/K_{0}-3}

(149)
