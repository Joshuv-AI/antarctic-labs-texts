Isomorphisms between random d d -hypergraphs
Théo Lenoir

Abstract
. We characterize the size of the largest common induced subgraph of two independent random uniform d d -hypergraphs of different sizes with d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . More precisely, its distribution is asymptotically concentrated on two points, and we obtain as a consequence a phase transition for the inclusion of the smallest hypergraph in the largest one. This generalizes to uniform random d d -hypergraphs the results of Chatterjee and Diaconis [ 7 ] for uniform random graphs. Our proofs rely on the first and second moment methods.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and main result

The question of the size of the largest common induced subgraph of two graphs is a well-studied problem in algorithmics
[
5
,
13
]
. The probabilistic counterpart of this problem is to identify the distribution of the size of the largest common induced subgraph of two random graphs. It was raised by Chatterjee and Diaconis to understand the following seeming paradox. For every
p\in(0,1)
the Erdős-Renyi graph
G(\infty,p)
is almost surely isomorphic to the Rado graph
[
6
]
, thus two
G(\infty,p)
are almost surely isomorphic. However, the probability of having two independent
G(N,1/2)
isomorphic is at most
N!2^{-\binom{N}{2}}=o(1)
indicating a real difference in the behavior of the size of the largest common induced subgraph.

Indeed, in
2023
, Chatterjee and Diaconis proved in
[
7
]
that the typical size of the largest common induced subgraph of two independent uniform random graphs (equivalently two independent
G(N,1/2)
Erdős-Renyi graphs) is logarithmic. More precisely, there exists an explicit sequence of integers
(z_{N})_{N\geq 1}
such that as
N
goes to
\infty
, with probability tending to one, the largest common induced subgraph has size
z_{N}
or
z_{N}+1
. This phenomenon is called two-point concentration for the size of the largest common induced subgraph of two uniform random graphs.

Two-point concentration is a well-known behavior for several characteristics of random graphs. For example, for Erdős-Renyi graphs
G(N,p)
:

•

for the clique number a two-point concentration phenomenon has been proved by Matula
[
12
]
when
p
is constant;

•

for the chromatic number Shamir and Spencer
[
15
]
proved a five-point concentration and Alon and Krivelevich
[
3
]
proved later a two-point concentration when
p=N^{-1/2-\delta}
(see also
[
1
,
2
]
);

•

for the independence number, Bohman and Hofstad
[
4
]
proved a two-point concentration;

•

for the domination number, Glebov and al.
[
10
]
proved a two-point concentration when
p\gg N^{-1/2}
and Bohman and al. extended the result for
p\gg N^{-2/3}
.

This phenomenon also occurs for other models of graphs, see for example
[
14
]
for the chromatic number of a model of random geometric graph.

A natural question is to extend these two-point concentration results to hypergraphs, see
[
8
]
for example for the chromatic number. A
d
-hypergraph
1
1

1

Also called
d
-uniform hypergraph in the litterature, however to avoid confusion with the probability notion of uniformity, we adopt this terminology.

G=(V,E)
is composed of a set of vertices
V
and a set
E
of hyperedges (
i.e.
subsets of
V
) of size
d
. Hypergraphs appear naturally in a variety of contexts: to model satisfiability problems
[
11
]
, databases
[
9
]
, recommendation systems in machine learning
[
17
]
etc. For these reasons they are studied from both algorithmic and probabilistic points of view.

The aim of this paper is to extend the result of Chatterjee-Diaconis to uniform random
d
-hypergraphs of different sizes.

.

Let

The proof of Theorem

, several differences appear. First, in their article they only deal with the cases corresponding to 1 for

1.2.
Notations and proof strategy

We begin by defining the notion of induced subgraph:

Definition 1.2
.

For any
d
-hypergraph
G=(V,E)
, and any
k

of distinct elements of

is an hyperedge of

if and only if

is an hyperedge of
G
.

Here are the different notations that are used throughout the article:

•

The notation

stands for the natural logarithm in base

•

d
is an integer greater or equal to
3
;

•

(N_{2}(N_{1}))_{N_{1}\geq 1}
is a sequence such that for all
N_{1}
,
N_{1}\geq N_{2}(N_{1})
. For convenience, we will write
N_{2}
instead of
N_{2}(N_{1})
;

•

N=\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}}
;

•

\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are two independent uniform random
d
-hypergraphs of respective size
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
: each possible hyperedge is present independently with probability
1/2
;

•

a=\frac{2}{\log 2}d!
;

•

(\beta_{N_{1}})_{N_{1}\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence such that, if we set for every
N_{1}\geq 1
,

(4)

\ell_{N_{1}}:=(a\log N)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}+\beta_{N_{1}},

then
\ell_{N_{1}}
is a positive integer. We will write
\ell
instead of
\ell_{N_{1}}
to lighten the notations;

•

for every positive integers

and

with

,

•

for every positive integers

and

•

the random variable

With the above notations, having a common subgraph of size

is thus equivalent to

. The proof of Theorem

consists in estimating the first and second moments of

for well-chosen values of

In Section
2
, the first moment of

is computed explicitly, and we prove that asymptotically with high probability the largest common induced subgraph is of size at most

with the first moment method.

In Section

, we give an upper bound on the second moment of

for

We conclude by carefully applying both results to the different cases of Theorem

.

2.
First moment

We will start with a technical lemma. Denote by

U_{\alpha,N_{1}}:=2\log N-\frac{(\alpha\ell-1)\dots(\alpha\ell-(d-1))}{d!}\log 2

for
\alpha\in(0,1]
fixed. Since the asymptotic expansion of
U_{\alpha,N}
will be used several times in our estimations, we compute it separately.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we have the following asymptotic expansion as
N_{1}
goes to infinity:

U_{\alpha,N_{1}}=(2-2\alpha^{d-1})\log N+\alpha^{d-2}\frac{d-1}{2}\frac{a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log 2}{d!}(d-2\alpha\beta_{N_{1}})(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}+\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}).

For
\alpha=1
, we get

U_{1,N_{1}}=\frac{d-1}{2}\frac{a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log 2}{d!}(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}+\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}).

Proof.

Note that:

\displaystyle U_{\alpha,N_{1}}

\displaystyle=2\log N-\frac{\alpha^{d-1}\ell^{d-1}}{d!}\log{2}+{d\choose 2}\frac{\alpha^{d-2}\ell^{d-2}}{d!}\log{2}+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{d-3})

\displaystyle=2\log N-a\alpha^{d-1}\log N\frac{\log 2}{d!}-\alpha^{d-1}\frac{\log 2}{d!}(d-1)\beta_{N_{1}}a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\alpha^{d-2}\frac{\log 2}{d!}{d\choose 2}a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}+\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}\right)

by Eq. (
4
) and since
(\beta_{N_{1}})_{N_{1}\geq 1}
is bounded.
Thus, using the value of
a
,

U_{\alpha,N_{1}}=(2-2\alpha^{d-1})\log N+\alpha^{d-2}\frac{d-1}{2}\frac{a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log 2}{d!}(d-2\alpha\beta_{N_{1}})(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}+\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}\right)

concluding the computation.∎

The following lemma provides two asymptotic estimates for
\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]
, both of which will be useful later.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have:

\displaystyle\bullet\text{ if $N_{2}<\ell$,\ \ \ \ }\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\bullet\text{ otherwise, }\ \mathbb{E}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=2^{-\binom{\ell}{d}}N^{2\ell}\frac{(N_{2})_{\ell}}{N_{2}^{\ell}}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\exp\left((d-1)(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})\log N+\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\right)\right).

Proof.

If
N_{2}<\ell
, then
W=0
which implies the first equality. Otherwise assume that
N_{2}\geq\ell
. By linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}_{N_{1},\ell}|\times|\mathcal{A}_{N_{2},\ell}|\mathbb{P}\left(\Gamma_{1,(1,\dots,\ell)}=\Gamma_{2,(1,\dots,\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(N_{1}-i\right)\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(N_{2}-i\right)2^{-\binom{\ell}{d}},

thus

\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]=2^{-\binom{\ell}{d}}N^{2\ell}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{N_{1}}\right)\frac{(N_{2})_{\ell}}{N_{2}^{\ell}}.

Since

\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{N_{1}}\right)=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{i}{N_{1}}\right)\right)=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}}{N_{1}}\right)\right)=\exp\left(o(1)\right),

we have
\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]=(1+o(1))2^{-\binom{\ell}{d}}N^{2\ell}\frac{(N_{2})_{\ell}}{N_{2}^{\ell}}
.

Moreover since
N_{2}\geq\ell
,

\displaystyle 1\geq\frac{(N_{2})_{\ell}}{N_{2}^{\ell}}=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{N_{2}}\right)\geq\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{\ell}\right)=\frac{\ell!}{\ell^{\ell}}=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}(\ell)\right),

therfore

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{(N_{2})_{\ell}}{N_{2}^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}(\ell)\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2\ell\log N+\mathcal{O}(\ell)-\frac{\ell(\ell-1)\dots(\ell-(d-1))}{d!}\log 2\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}(\ell)+\ell U_{1,N_{1}}\right).

Finally from Lemma
2.1
we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}(\ell)+\left((a\log N)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}+\beta_{N_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\frac{a^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log 2}{d!}(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})(\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}+\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}})+(d-1)(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})\frac{a\log 2}{2d!}\log N\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}})+(d-1)(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})\log N\right)\quad\text{as $a=\frac{2d!}{\log 2}$}

concluding the proof. (Note that we used

when the
\mathcal{O}(\ell)=\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\right)
was absorbed by
\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-3}{d-1}}\right)
.)∎

For

3.
Second moment

We now tackle the estimation of the second moment.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exist positive constants
K,K^{\prime}
which do not depend on
N_{1}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]\leq(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]^{2}\left(1+K\exp\left(-K^{\prime}(d-2\beta_{N_{1}})\log N+\mathcal{O}\left((\log N)^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log(\log N)\right)\right)\right).

Proof.

Note that if
\ell>N_{2}
,
W=0
thus
\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]=0=\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]
which implies the lemma. Now assume that
\ell\leq N_{2}
.
First we expand the second moment:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{(A,C)\in\mathcal{A}_{N_{2},\ell}^{2},(B,D)\in\mathcal{A}_{N_{1},\ell}^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(\Gamma_{2,A}=\Gamma_{1,B},\Gamma_{2,C}=\Gamma_{1,D}\right).

We now split the computation according to the size of

has size
m

1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\leq\ell
and
m

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=S_{0}+\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\ell}S_{m}

where

4.
Remaining questions

•

Theorem
1.1
implies, in the case of item 1 and 2a, that for many
N_{1}
’s, there is actually a one-point concentration:
e.g.
for item 1 there are arbitrary large sequences of consecutive
N_{1}
such that
\lfloor x_{N_{1}}+(\log_{2}N_{1})^{-1/d}\rfloor=\lfloor x_{N_{1}}-(\log_{2}N_{1})^{-1/d}\rfloor
. However, there are also arbitrary large sequences of consecutive
N_{1}
such that
\lfloor x_{N_{1}}+(\log_{2}N_{1})^{-1/d}\rfloor=\lfloor x_{N_{1}}-(\log_{2}N_{1})^{-1/d}\rfloor
+1. In this case, it is still open to know if there is concentration in only one of these values. We would need more precise asymptotic estimates for the second moment, or a new method to answer this question.

•

Very recently in
[
16
]
, Surya, Warnke and Zhu provided a generalization of the result of Chatterjee and Diaconis to Erdős-Renyi graphs of parameters different than
\frac{1}{2}
. They show that for
p,q\in(0,1)
the size of the largest common induced subgraph of a
G(N,q)
and a
G(N,p)
Erdős-Renyi graph is logarithmic, and that there is also a two-point concentration phenomenon. This extension is however a lot more involved: to avoid the explosion of the second moment, they consider only pseudorandom graphs in the computation of the second moment.

Investigate whether the approach employed in
[
16
]
can be used to prove that there is a two-point concentration phenomenon for analogous models of random hypergraphs of different size is a natural extension of the present paper.
