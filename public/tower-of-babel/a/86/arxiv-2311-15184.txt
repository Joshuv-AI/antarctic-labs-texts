Factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order k k Journal: (internal report CC23-11)
S. R. Mane

Abstract
The factorial moments of the standard Poisson distribution are well known. The present note presents an explicit combinatorial sum for the factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order k k . Unlike the standard Poisson distribution (the case k = 1 k=1 ), for k > 1 k>1 the structure of the factorial moments is much more complicated. Some properties of the factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order k k are elucidated in this note.

It is well known that for a Poisson distribution with rate parameter
\lambda
, the mean and variance both equal
\lambda

and the
n^{th}
factorial moment is
\lambda^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
The Poisson distribution of order
k

[
1
]
is a variant or extension of the standard Poisson distribution.
For
k=1
it is the standard Poisson distribution.
It is an example of a compound Poisson distribution
[
2
]
.
Its probability mass function is
[
1
]

P_{n}(k,\lambda)=e^{-k\lambda}\sum_{n_{1}+2n_{2}+\dots+kn_{k}=n}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda^{n_{j}}}{n_{j}!}\,.

(1)

For
k=1
this simplifies to standard result
P_{n}(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{n}/n!
.
Philippou
[
3
]
derived the mean and variance

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=(1+2+\dots+k)\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\,\lambda\,,

(2a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle=(1^{2}+2^{2}+\dots+k^{2})\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{k(k+1)(2k+1)}{6}\,\lambda\,.

(2b)

Less is known about its factorial moments.
Let
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
denote the
n^{th}
factorial moment of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.
The author obtained the following combinatorial sum

M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)=n!\sum_{n_{1}+2n_{2}+\dots+kn_{k}=n}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda^{n_{j}}}{n_{j}!}\biggl[\binom{k+1}{j+1}\biggr]^{n_{j}}\,.

(3)

On searching the literature, it was discovered that
Charalambides published the following expression for the factorial moment
(eq. (4.9) in
[
4
]
)

M_{(n)}=n!\sum_{r=0}^{n}\lambda^{r}\,Q(n+r,r,k+1)/r!\,.

(4)

The quantity
Q(n+r,r,k+1)
was given as follows (eq. (3.19) in
[
4
]
)

T_{n,r;k}(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{m})=n!k^{-r}Q(n+r,r,k+1)/r!\,.

(5)

Here (unnumbered before eq. (3.19) in
[
4
]
)

\displaystyle g_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{j!}{k}\binom{k+1}{j+1}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n,r;k}(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{m})\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=k^{-r}t^{-r}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j+1}t^{j+1}\biggr)^{r}\biggr/r!\,.

(6b)

(
Note: this corrects a misprint in
[
4
]
, where
t^{j+1}
was written as
t^{i+1}
.
)
The subscript “
m
” on
g_{m}
is
m=\min\{n,k\}
.
Upon making the relevant substitutions into eq. (
4
),
the result coincides with eq. (
3
).
Hence eq. (
3
) can be considered as a confirmation of the formulas in
[
4
]
.
However
[
4
]
did not display an explicit combinatorial sum
for the factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.

For the standard Poisson distribution (
k=1
),
the sum in eq. (
3
) contains only one summand and the result simplifies to (setting
n_{1}=n
)

M_{(n)}(1,\lambda)=n!\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\biggl[\binom{2}{2}\biggr]^{n}=\lambda^{n}\,.

(7)

This is the well known result for the standard Poisson distribution.
However, for
k>1
, the sum in eq. (
3
) contains many more terms.
At a minimum, it is evident that
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
is a polynomial in
\lambda
of degree
n
.
It is also evident that
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)=1
for
n=0
and has no constant term for
n>0
.
Also note that if
n<k
, then terms where
j>n
do not appear in the sum in eq. (
3
) because we must have
n_{j}=0
if
j>n
.
This tells us the following: for fixed
n\geq 1
, the expression for
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
is the
same
for all
k\geq n
.
For example:

1.

For
n=1
, only
n_{1}
is nonzero and the factorial moment is
M_{(1)}(k,\lambda)=\lambda
for all
k\geq 1
.

2.

For
n=2
, only
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are nonzero. We shall see below that

M_{(2)}(k,\lambda)=\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{2}\lambda^{2}+\frac{(k-1)k(k+1)}{3}\,\lambda\,.

(8)

This is the expression for
M_{(2)}(k,\lambda)
for all
k\geq 2
.
Observe that for
k=1
, the second term (in
\lambda
) vanishes and also
k(k+1)/2=1
so the result is
M_{(2)}(1,\lambda)=\lambda^{2}
.

For brevity of the notation in the formulas below, define the following (where
\kappa_{j}=0
if
j>k
)

\kappa_{j}=\binom{k+1}{j+1}\,.

(9)

Hence
\kappa_{1}=k(k+1)/2
and
\kappa_{2}=(k-1)k(k+1)/6
, etc.
Observe that
\kappa_{j}
is proportional to
g_{j}
in
[
4
]
(see eq. (
6a
)).
Let us study the simplest case, viz.
k=2
.
Set
n_{2}=s
and
n_{1}=n-2s
and note that
0\leq s\leq\lfloor(n/2)\rfloor
to obtain

M_{(n)}(2,\lambda)=\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor(n/2)\rfloor}\frac{n!}{(n-2s)!s!}\kappa_{1}^{n-2s}\kappa_{2}^{s}\,\lambda^{n-s}\,.

(10)

Then for
n=1,2,\dots
(omitting explicit mention of the arguments
(k,\lambda)
)

\displaystyle M_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\lambda\,,

(11a)

\displaystyle M_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}+2\kappa_{2}\lambda\,,

(11b)

\displaystyle M_{(3)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{3}\lambda^{3}+6\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{2}\,,

(11c)

\displaystyle M_{(4)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{4}\lambda^{4}+12\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{3}+12\kappa_{2}^{2}\lambda^{2}\,,

(11d)

\displaystyle M_{(5)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{5}\lambda^{5}+20\kappa_{1}^{3}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{4}+60\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}^{2}\lambda^{3}\,,

(11e)

\displaystyle M_{(6)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{6}\lambda^{6}+30\kappa_{1}^{4}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{5}+180\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{2}^{2}\lambda^{4}+120\kappa_{2}^{3}\lambda^{3}\,.

(11f)

Note the following:

1.

Clearly, for any
k>1
, even for
k=2
, the number of terms in
M_{(n)}
is not bounded as a function of
n
.
For
k=2
, the polynomial
M_{(n)}(2,\lambda)
contains all powers of
\lambda
from
n
down to
\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor
.

2.

More generally, the polynomial
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
contains all powers of
\lambda
from
n
down to
\lfloor(n+k-1)/k\rfloor
.
It is only for
k=1
that the number of terms in
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
is a bounded function of
n
.

3.

Stated another way, the lowest power of
\lambda
in
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
is
\lambda
for
n\in[1,k]

and
\lambda^{2}
for
n\in[k+1,2k]
and in general it is
\lambda^{j}
for
n\in[jk-k+1,jk]
.

Let us write the expressions for
M_{(n)}(k,\lambda)
for low values of
n
and arbitrary
k
.
We again omit explicit mention of the arguments
(k,\lambda)
.

\displaystyle M_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\lambda\,,

(12a)

\displaystyle M_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}+2\kappa_{2}\lambda\,,

(12b)

\displaystyle M_{(3)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{3}\lambda^{3}+6\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{2}+6\kappa_{3}\lambda\,,

(12c)

\displaystyle M_{(4)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{4}\lambda^{4}+12\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{3}+(12\kappa_{2}^{2}+24\kappa_{1}\kappa_{3})\lambda^{2}+24\kappa_{4}\lambda\,,

(12d)

\displaystyle M_{(5)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{5}\lambda^{5}+20\kappa_{1}^{3}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{4}+(60\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}^{2}+60\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{3})\lambda^{3}+(120\kappa_{1}\kappa_{4}+120\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3})\lambda^{2}+120\kappa_{5}\lambda\,,

(12e)

\displaystyle M_{(6)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}^{6}\lambda^{6}+30\kappa_{1}^{4}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{5}+(180\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{2}^{2}+120\kappa_{1}^{3}\kappa_{3})\lambda^{4}+(360\kappa_{1}^{2}\kappa_{4}+120\kappa_{2}^{3}+720\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3})\lambda^{3}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\;\,+(720\kappa_{1}\kappa_{5}+720\kappa_{2}\kappa_{4}+360\kappa_{3}^{2})\lambda^{2}+720\kappa_{6}\lambda\,.

(12f)

We adopt the notation in the textbook by Graham, Knuth and Patashnik
[
5
]

and write
[\lambda^{j}]M_{(n)}
for the coefficient of
\lambda^{j}
in
M_{(n)}
.
Then note the following:

1.

For all
k\geq 1
and
n\geq 1
, the coefficient of the highest power
\lambda^{n}
is

[\lambda^{n}]\,M_{(n)}=\frac{n!}{n!}\kappa_{1}^{n}=\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{n}\,.

(13)

2.

For all
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, the coefficient of the second highest power
\lambda^{n-1}
is

\begin{split}[\lambda^{n-1}]\,M_{(n)}&=\frac{n!}{(n-2)!1!}\kappa_{1}^{n-2}\kappa_{2}\\
&=\frac{n(n-1)}{3}\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{n-1}(k-1)\,.\end{split}

(14)

3.

It is more complicated for the coefficient of the third highest power
\lambda^{n-2}
and lower powers.

4.

For all
k\geq 3
and
n\geq 3
, the coefficient of the third highest power
\lambda^{n-2}
is

\begin{split}[\lambda^{n-2}]\,M_{(n)}&=\frac{n!}{(n-4)!2!}\kappa_{1}^{n-4}\kappa_{2}^{2}+\frac{n!}{(n-3)!1!}\kappa_{1}^{n-3}\kappa_{3}\\
&=\frac{1}{6}\binom{n}{3}\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{n-2}(k-1)((2n-3)k-2n)\,.\end{split}

(15)

5.

For all
k\geq 4
and
n\geq 4
, the coefficient of the fourth highest power
\lambda^{n-3}
is

\begin{split}[\lambda^{n-3}]\,M_{(n)}&=\frac{n!}{(n-6)!3!}\kappa_{1}^{n-6}\kappa_{2}^{3}+\frac{n!}{(n-5)!1!1!}\kappa_{1}^{n-5}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}+\frac{n!}{(n-4)!1!}\kappa_{1}^{n-4}\kappa_{4}\\
&=\frac{2}{135}\binom{n}{4}\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{n-3}(k-1)\biggl[(10n^{2}-45n+47)k^{2}-5(4n^{2}-9n-1)k+2(5n^{2}+1)\biggr]\,.\end{split}

(16)

6.

However, the coefficient of the
lowest
power
\lambda
is simple, for all
k\geq 1
and
n\geq 1
:

[\lambda]\,M_{(n)}=\frac{n!}{1!}\kappa_{n}=n!\binom{k+1}{n+1}=\frac{(k+1)k\dots(k-n+1)}{n+1}\,.

(17)

The value of the coefficient is zero if
k<n
.
For
k\geq 2
and
n=2
, the coefficient of
\lambda
is
(k-1)k(k+1)/3
, as stated in eq. (
8
).

The factorial moments can also be derived from the factorial moment generating function (FMGF).
For the Poisson distribution of order
k
, the FMGF is, omitting the arguments
(k,\lambda)

(
[
2
]
, see also
[
4
]
)

M(t)=e^{-k\lambda}e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}\,.

(18)

The
n^{th}
factorial moment is given by

M_{(n)}=\frac{d^{n}M(t)}{dt^{n}}\biggr|_{t=1}\,.

(19)

The first few factorial moments are as follows.

1.

The first derivative is

\frac{dM(t)}{dt}=\lambda e^{-k\lambda}e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})\,.

(20)

2.

Evaluate at
t=1
to obtain the first factorial moment

M_{(1)}=\lambda(1+2+\dots+k)=\binom{k+1}{2}\lambda=\kappa_{1}\lambda\,.

(21)

3.

The second derivative is

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}M(t)}{dt^{2}}&=\lambda e^{-k\lambda}\,\frac{d\ }{dt}\Bigl[\,e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})\,\Bigr]\\
&=\lambda e^{-k\lambda}\,e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}\biggl[\,\lambda(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})^{2}+2!\sum_{j=2}^{k}\binom{j}{2}t^{j-2}\,\biggr]\,.\end{split}

(22)

4.

Evaluate at
t=1
to obtain the second factorial moment

\begin{split}M_{(2)}&=\lambda^{2}\binom{k+1}{2}^{2}+2!\lambda\binom{k+1}{3}\\
&=\kappa_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}+2\kappa_{2}\lambda\\
&=\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{2}\lambda^{2}+\frac{(k-1)k(k+1)}{3}\lambda\,.\end{split}

(23)

This agrees with eqs. (
8
) and (
12b
).

5.

The third derivative is

\begin{split}\frac{d^{3}M(t)}{dt^{2}}&=\lambda e^{-k\lambda}\,\frac{d\ }{dt}\biggl\{\,e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}\biggl[\,\lambda(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})^{2}+2!\sum_{j=2}^{k}\binom{j}{2}t^{j-2}\,\biggr]\biggr\}\\
&=\lambda e^{-k\lambda}\,e^{\lambda(t+t^{2}+\dots+t^{k})}\biggl\{\,\lambda^{2}(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})^{3}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+3\times 2!\lambda\biggl(\sum_{j=2}^{k}\binom{j}{2}t^{j-2}\biggr)(1+2t+\dots+kt^{k-1})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+3!\sum_{j=2}^{k}\binom{j}{3}t^{j-3}\,\biggr\}\,.\end{split}

(24)

6.

Evaluate at
t=1
to obtain the third factorial moment

\begin{split}M_{(3)}&=\lambda^{3}\binom{k+1}{2}^{3}+6\lambda^{2}\binom{k+1}{2}\binom{k+1}{3}+3!\lambda\binom{k+1}{4}\\
&=\kappa_{1}^{3}\lambda^{3}+6\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda^{2}+6\kappa_{3}\lambda\\
&=\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{3}\lambda^{3}+2\biggl(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\biggr)^{2}(k-1)\lambda^{2}+\frac{(k-2)(k-1)k(k+1)}{4}\lambda\,.\end{split}

(25)

This agrees with eq. (
12c
) and is the same expression for all
k\geq 3
.
For
k=1
the last two terms vanish and for
k=2
the last term vanishes.

,

The present note displays an explicit combinatorial sum for the factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order

,

[
4
]
.
Unlike the standard Poisson distribution (the case
k=1
), for
k>1
the structure of the factorial moments is much more complicated.
Some properties of the factorial moments of the Poisson distribution of order
k
