On the Optimal Control of a Linear Peridynamics Model
Tadele Mengesha and Abner J. Salgado and Joshua M. Siktar

Abstract
. We study a non-local optimal control problem involving a linear, bond-based peridynamics model. In addition to existence and uniqueness of solutions to our problem, we investigate their behavior as the horizon parameter δ \displaystyle\delta , which controls the degree of nonlocality, approaches zero. We then study a finite element-based discretization of this problem, its convergence, and the so-called asymptotic compatibility as the discretization parameter h \displaystyle h and the horizon parameter δ \displaystyle\delta tend to zero simultaneously.

45F15, 49M41, 49M25, 49J21, 65R20, 74P10.

1.
Introduction

This paper focuses on an optimal control problem with a system of constraint equations derived from peridynamics (PD), which is a contemporary non-local model in solid mechanics,
[
46
,
48
]
. PD models do not assume the differentiability (even in the weak sense) of
pertinent forces acting on a body nor on the resulting displacement vector fields, unlike their local counterparts in continuum mechanics. This feature of PD models makes them attractive to analyze certain physical phenomena with inherent discontinuities, such as the formation of cracks
in solids
[
51
,
49
,
50
]
. In this work, we will focus on the bond-based PD model, where particles in a solid are assumed to expend long distance forces on other particles within a certain radius. With this in mind, we will consider the problem of linearly deforming a [possibly heterogeneous] elastic solid occupying a domain
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to achieve a desired deformation state by applying a certain external force. The deformation field given by
\displaystyle v(x):=x+u(x)
, where
\displaystyle u
is the displacement, and the external force
\displaystyle g
are related via the linearized bond-based PD model
[
20
,
35
,
47
]

given by

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{f}_{\delta}(s[u](x,y),y,x)dy=g(x),\quad x\in\Omega,

where the vector-valued pairwise force density function
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{\delta}
along the bond joining material points
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
, and the scalar linearized strain field
\displaystyle s[u]
associated with the displacement
\displaystyle u
are given by

\begin{split}s[u](x,y)&=\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|}\cdot\frac{x-y}{|x-y|};\\
\mathfrak{f}_{\delta}(s[u](x,y),y,x)&=H(x,y)k_{\delta}(|x-y|)s[u](x,y)\frac{y-x}{|x-y|}.\end{split}

In the above,
\displaystyle H(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(h(x)+h(y))
serves as material coefficient for some bounded function
\displaystyle h
. The function
\displaystyle k_{\delta}(|x-y|)
is the interaction kernel that is radial and describes the force strength between material points. The parameter
\displaystyle\delta>0
, in the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
, is called the
horizon
and measures the degree of non-locality, i.e., the radius within which the interaction forces are considered. We assume that
\displaystyle k_{\delta}(|x-y|)=0
if
\displaystyle|x-y|\geq\delta
; additional assumptions on the family
\displaystyle\{k_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
will be given later.

To quantify the desirability of a displacement state
\displaystyle u
subject to the the external force
\displaystyle g
, which will be our control, we introduce an objective functional
\displaystyle I(u,g)
. This functional will be taken to be a sum of two parts: one measures, say, the mismatch of the displacement state
\displaystyle u
and the desired displacement field, say
\displaystyle u_{des}
, and the other penalizes the control
\displaystyle g
and serves as a regularizer.
We will delay the exact form of the objective functional until the next section, but the optimal control problem of interest of the paper can now be stated as

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\min\{I(u,g)\ |\ (u,g)\in X_{ad}\times Z_{ad}\},\\
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u\ =\ g\quad\text{in $\displaystyle\Omega$},\end{cases}

where the
admissible set
\displaystyle X_{ad}\times Z_{ad}
will be specified in the next section. As described above, the state equation, codified by the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
, will be a strongly coupled linear system of integral equations. The definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u
requires the knowledge of the state
\displaystyle u
outside of the domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, up to a boundary layer of thickness
\displaystyle\delta
. Thus, we close the state equation in (
1.1
), by assigning
\displaystyle u
to be a fixed displacement field
\displaystyle u_{0}
in the boundary layer which we call the
nonlocal Dirichlet boundary condition.

In this work, we prove the well-posedness of (
1.1
) for a more general class of objective functionals and a broader class of interaction kernels
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
that include fractional-type kernels. We also study the behavior of the optimal pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g})
as a function of the horizon
\displaystyle\delta
. In fact, we demonstrate that in the vanishing horizon limit
\displaystyle\delta\to 0^{+}
the integral equation-based optimal control problem (
1.1
) converges, in a certain sense, to a differential-equation-based optimal control problem. Well-posedness as well as vanishing nonlocality limit for the state equations have been studied in
[
35
,
45
,
56
]
.
In addition, we consider the numerical approximation of solutions to (
1.1
) via the first-order optimality conditions. The discrete problem will involve two parameters: the discretization parameter
\displaystyle h
and the horizon
\displaystyle\delta
. We will show that we have convergence, not only when
\displaystyle h
tends to zero, but that we also have
asymptotic compatibility
(see
[
53
]
), in the sense that the limit is unique regardless of the path we use to let
\displaystyle h\to 0^{+}
and
\displaystyle\delta\to 0^{+}
.

While literature on optimal control problems is immense, we cite some works that are related to the current study. The optimal control problem when the state equation is a scalar fractional or non-local equation is studied in

[
3
,
4
,
2
,
12
,
17
,
40
]
.
The papers
[
7
,
8
,
1
,
17
,
22
]
study the finite element analysis of optimal control problems of fractional or nonlocal equations.
For our approach of using the first-order optimality conditions in order to approximate the continuous problem with the corresponding discrete problems, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
15
,
14
,
17
,
41
]
for more on this subject matter. To the best of our knowledge the optimal control problem for a strongly coupled system of nonlocal equations of peridynamic-type has not been studied in the literature; the current work makes a contribution in that direction. We also mention that while the present work focuses on the basic linear bond-based peridynamic model, similar analysis can be done on the more general state-based peridynamics
[
48
]
as well as other nonlinear models, like those studied in
[
37
]
. This and other related issues will be addressed in future work.

states the problems to be studied, with all notation made precise. Section

4
. The remaining sections study the relationship between our problems as
\displaystyle\delta
and
\displaystyle h
change: Section
5
considers
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence results as
\displaystyle\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
; Section
6

\displaystyle h\rightarrow 0^{+}
; and Section
7
proves the asymptotic compatibility of limits as
\displaystyle\delta
and
\displaystyle h
both tend to
\displaystyle 0
.

2.
Problem formulation

2.1.
Notation and assumptions

Let us begin by introducing some notation; first, by
\displaystyle A\lesssim B
we mean that there is a nonessential constant
\displaystyle c
, such that
\displaystyle A\leq cB
. In addition,
\displaystyle A\sim B
means
\displaystyle A\lesssim B\lesssim A
. We assume throughout the paper that
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open, bounded domain with a Lipschitz boundary, and denote
\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}:=\Omega\cup\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ \dist(x,\Omega)<\delta\}
, where
\displaystyle\delta>0
is the horizon parameter. By volumetric boundary we mean the boundary layer
\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\Omega
surrounding
\displaystyle\Omega
. For any
\displaystyle r>0
and
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote a ball centered at
\displaystyle x_{0}
with radius
\displaystyle r
by
\displaystyle B_{r}(x_{0})
. Next we provide assumptions on our kernels which are adopted from
[
9
,
40
]
.

Assumption 2.1

(Kernel assumptions)
.

We assume that
\displaystyle\{k_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
is a family of radial, non-negative, kernels in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
supported in
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(0)
such that

(2.1)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}k_{\delta}(|\xi|)d\xi\ &=\ 1,\\
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}k_{\delta}(|\xi|)d\xi\ &=0,\quad\text{for all $\displaystyle\epsilon>0$.}\end{split}

The above two conditions say that the family of
\displaystyle L^{1}
functions
\displaystyle\{k_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
converges to the Dirac measure
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
in the sense of measures.
We also assume that for each
\displaystyle\delta>0,

\displaystyle k_{\delta}(r)r^{-2}
is non-increasing in
\displaystyle r
.

Given any
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-function
\displaystyle k(|\xi|)
supported on the unit ball
\displaystyle B_{1}(0)
, the family
\displaystyle k_{\delta}(|\xi|)=\delta^{-n}k\bigg({|\xi|\over\delta}\bigg)
satisfies (
2.1
); for other nontrivial kernels satisfying the above conditions see
[
9
]
.
To properly define our function spaces and norms, we introduce some additional notation. First, given
\displaystyle u:\Omega_{\delta}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
measurable, we let
\displaystyle Du
represent the
projected difference
defined as

Du(x,y):=(u(x)-u(y))\cdot\frac{(x-y)}{|x-y|}.

This quantity is the trace of
\displaystyle(u(x)-u(y))\otimes\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}
. Notice then that the linearized strain field
\displaystyle s[u](x,y)
is given by
\displaystyle s[u](x,y)=\frac{Du(x,y)}{|x-y|}
. Using these notations, the vector-valued nonlocal operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x)=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}H(x,y)k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\frac{Du(x,y)}{|x-y|}\frac{y-x}{|x-y|}dy

whenever it makes sense. We notice that for
\displaystyle u,v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see
[
23
, Proposition A.5]
,

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(x)\cdot v(x)dx&={1\over 2}\iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}H(x,y)k_{\delta}(x-y)\frac{Du(x,y)}{|x-y|}\frac{Dv(x,y)}{|x-y|}dxdy\\
&=:B_{\delta}(u,v),\end{split}

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\delta}=(\Omega\times\Omega_{\delta})\cup(\Omega_{\delta}\times\Omega).
The latter defines a bi-linear form and we understand the strongly coupled system of nonlocal equations for the state
\displaystyle u
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u=g
, in the weak sense as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the corresponding quadratic potential energy

(2.2)

{1\over 2}\iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}H(x,y)k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|\frac{Du(x,y)}{|x-y|}\right|^{2}dxdy-\int_{\Omega}{g}(x)\cdot{u}(x)dx

defined on an appropriate space of functions with a displacement field on the nonlocal boundary.
Recall that
\displaystyle H(x,y)={1\over 2}(h(x)+h(y))
and that there are positive constants
\displaystyle h_{min}
and
\displaystyle h_{max}

such that
\displaystyle h_{min}\leq h(x)\leq h_{max}
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega_{\delta}
.
With this assumption on
\displaystyle H
and for
\displaystyle g\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the energy in (
2.2
) is finite for
\displaystyle u:\Omega_{\delta}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
measurable such that

u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\text{and }\,\iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|{Du(x,y)\over|y-x|}\right|^{2}dxdy<\infty.

We denote this space of functions by
\displaystyle X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
; i. e.

X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \middle|\ \iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|{Du(x,y)\over|y-x|}\right|^{2}dxdy<\infty\right\}.

We also introduce the corresponding space of functions having a zero nonlocal boundary condition as

X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{u\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \middle|\ u=0\,\,\text{on $\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\Omega$}\right\}.

It is not difficult to show that the spaces
\displaystyle X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
are normed spaces with the norm

(2.3)

\|u\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ :=\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+[u]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}

where
\displaystyle[u]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|{Du(x,y)\over|y-x|}\right|^{2}dxdy.

Notice that the form
\displaystyle B_{\delta}:X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
is a well defined continuous bi-linear form.

One objective of this work is to make connections between the non-local optimal control problem and a local control problem as
\displaystyle\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. As we will show, the corresponding bi-linear form of interest is

(2.4)

B_{0}(u,v)\ :=\ \frac{1}{n(n+2)}\int_{\Omega}h(x)(2\langle\Sym(\nabla u(x)),\Sym(\nabla v(x))\rangle_{F}+\dive(u(x))\dive(v(x)))dx,

where
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
is the Fröbenius inner product on matrices:

\langle A,B\rangle_{F}\ :=\ \sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}a_{i,j}b_{i,j},\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

It turns out that the appropriate energy space for the resulting local problem is the classical space

(2.5)

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ :=\ \{u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ \nabla u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}),\ u\ =\ 0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\},

with the natural norm

(2.6)

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ :=\ \left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+[u]_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and corresponding semi-norm

(2.7)

[u]_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ :=\ \|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}.

Now, to state the optimal control problem of interest precisely, we define the pertinent objective functional. As we mentioned earlier the functional will be taken to be the sum of two terms. The first is a quality functional
\displaystyle Q:X_{ad}\subset X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,\infty)
, that assigns a certain value
\displaystyle Q(u)
to each admissible displacement field depending on a certain criteria. For example, given a desired displacement state
\displaystyle u_{des}
, we may want a state
\displaystyle u
that matches
\displaystyle u_{des}
as closely as possible. In this case we wish to choose
\displaystyle u
that keeps the mismatch between
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle u_{des}
to the minimum. The mismatch may be defined as a weighted squared error
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\gamma(x)|u(x)-u_{des}(x)|^{2}dx
for some
\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Notice that by choosing
\displaystyle\gamma
appropriately, we may seek to match the desired state only on a portion of the domain.
More generally, we would want the quality functional to have the form

Q(u)=\int_{\Omega}F(x,u(x))dx,

where the integrand
\displaystyle F:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
possesses the following properties:

(1)

For all
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the mapping
\displaystyle x\mapsto F(x,v)
is measurable;

(2)

For all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
the mapping
\displaystyle v\mapsto F(x,v)
is continuous and convex;

(3)

There exist constant
\displaystyle c_{1}>0
and
\displaystyle l\in L^{1}(\Omega)
for which

(2.8)

|F(x,v)|\ \leq\ c_{1}|v|^{2}+l(x)

for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
and all
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The second part of the objective functional is a cost functional associated with the external force. We seek a forcing term
\displaystyle g
whose associated displacement has the desired quality while keeping the cost as minimal as possible. Typically, we take this cost functional,
\displaystyle C(g)
, to be a weighted
\displaystyle L^{2}
-norm of
\displaystyle g
of the form

C(g)=\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g(x)|^{2}dx,

for some
\displaystyle 0<\Gamma\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.
To that end, we take the admissible control space to be
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
, a nonempty, closed, convex, and bounded subset of
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and it takes the form

(2.9)

Z_{\text{ad}}=\{z\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ a(x)\preceq z\preceq b(x)\}

for some
\displaystyle a,b\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
\displaystyle a\preceq b
means
\displaystyle[a]_{i}\leq[b]_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Without loss of generality, we shall assume that
\displaystyle 0\in Z_{\text{ad}}
.

In summary, the objective function we will be working with is of the form

(2.10)

I(u,g)\ :=\ \int_{\Omega}F(x,u(x))dx+\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g(x)|^{2}dx

under the above assumptions on
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle\Gamma
.

2.2.
Problem set up

Now that we have specified the different function spaces as well as bi-linear forms of interest, we are now ready to precisely pose the optimal control problems.
The first one is the optimal control problem of the coupled system of nonlocal equations. Given a boundary data
\displaystyle u_{0}\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the problem is
finding a pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
such that

(2.11)

I(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})=\min I(u_{\delta},g_{\delta}),

where the minimization is over pairs
\displaystyle(u_{\delta},g_{\delta})\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
that satisfy

(2.12)

u_{\delta}-u_{0}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,\,\text{and}\,\,B_{\delta}(u_{\delta},v)\ =\ \langle g_{\delta},v\rangle,\,\text{for all }\,v\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Here we use the notation
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-inner product.
We remark that without loss of generality we may assume that
\displaystyle u_{0}=0
in the above formulation. Indeed, if
\displaystyle u_{\delta}
solves (
2.12
) and we set
\displaystyle e_{\delta}:=u_{\delta}-u_{0}
, then
\displaystyle e_{\delta}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

(2.13)

B_{\delta}(e_{\delta},v)\ =\ \langle g_{\delta},v\rangle+B_{\delta}(u_{0},v),\,\,\text{for all $\displaystyle v\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})$}.

After noting that the map
\displaystyle v\to B_{\delta}(u_{0},v)
is a bounded linear functional on
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the right hand side of (
2.13
) can be viewed to define a duality pairing between
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and its dual. The objective functional as a function of
\displaystyle(e_{\delta},g_{\delta})
with still have the form as (
2.10
) with an integrand
\displaystyle\tilde{F}(x,e)=F(x,e+u_{0}(x))
. Notice that
\displaystyle\tilde{F}
the exact same properties as
\displaystyle F
.

With this simplification at hand, we summarize the problem as follows.

Problem 2.2

(Non-local continuous problem)
.

Find a pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
such that

(2.14)

I(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})=\min I(u_{\delta},g_{\delta})

where the minimization is over pairs
\displaystyle(u_{\delta},g_{\delta})\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
that satisfy

(2.15)

B_{\delta}(u_{\delta},v)\ =\ \langle g_{\delta},v\rangle,\,\text{for all }\,v\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

The effective admissible class of pairs for this nonlocal optimal control problem is

(2.16)

\mathcal{A}^{\delta}=\{(w,f)\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}|B_{\delta}(w,v)\ =\ \langle f,v\rangle,\,\text{for all }\,v\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

We are also interested in the behavior of the above nonlocal optimal control problem in the limit of vanishing nonlocality as quantified by
\displaystyle\delta
which turns out to be a local problem.

Problem 2.3

(Local continuous problem)
.

Find a pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g})\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
such that

(2.17)

I(\overline{u},\overline{g})\ =\ \min I(u,g),

where the minimization is over pairs
\displaystyle(u,g)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
that satisfy

(2.18)

B_{0}(u,v)\ =\ \langle g,v\rangle,\ \quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

As before, the effective admissible class of pairs for the control problem is

(2.19)

\mathcal{A}^{\text{loc}}\ :=\ \{(w,f)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}|B_{0}(w,v)\ =\ \langle f,v\rangle\quad\forall\ v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

We now introduce notation for our finite element scheme and discretized problems. The family of meshes
\displaystyle\{\mathscr{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
discretizing
\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}
is assumed to be quasi-uniform and of size
\displaystyle h
. Let
\displaystyle X_{h}\subset X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the space of continuous, piecewise linear, functions subject to the mesh with zero non-local boundary data, i.e.,

(2.20)

X_{h}\ :=\ \{w_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega_{\delta}};\mathbb{R}^{n})\ |\ w_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \forall T\in\mathscr{T}_{h},w_{h}\ =\ 0\ \text{on}\ \Omega_{\delta}\setminus\Omega\},

and
\displaystyle X_{\delta,h}
will denote this same function space, albeit with a different norm. For the local discrete problem, this space will denoted as
\displaystyle X_{h}
and equipped with the norm (
2.6
), and for the non-local discrete problem, this space will instead be denoted as
\displaystyle X_{\delta,h}
and equipped with the norm (
2.3
). Similarly, let
\displaystyle Z_{h}
denote the piecewise constant functions with respect to our mesh, i.e.,

(2.21)

Z_{h}\ :=\ \{z_{h}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{N})\ |\ z_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \forall T\in\mathscr{T}_{h}\}.

Here and henceforth, we denote the space of vector-valued polynomials of degree
\displaystyle m
as

(2.22)

\mathcal{P}_{m}(T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ :=\ \left\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\leq m}v_{\alpha}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\ \middle|\ v_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\intercal}\in T\right\}.

We will use
\displaystyle X_{\delta,h}
and
\displaystyle X_{h}
, as appropriate, to discretize the state space, and
\displaystyle Z_{h}
to discretize the control space. Now we may state our non-local and local discrete problems.

Problem 2.4

(Non-local discrete problem)
.

Find a pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{\delta,h}},\overline{g_{\delta,h}})\in X_{\delta,h}\times Z_{h}
such that

(2.23)

I(\overline{u_{\delta,h}},\overline{g_{\delta,h}})=\min I(u_{\delta,h},\ g_{\delta,h})

where the minimization is over pairs
\displaystyle(u_{\delta,h},g_{\delta,h})\in X_{\delta,h}\times Z_{h}
that satisfy

(2.24)

B_{\delta}(u_{\delta,h},v_{\delta,h})\ =\ \langle g_{\delta,h},v_{\delta,h}\rangle\ \quad\forall v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}.

The effective admissible class of pairs for the above nonlocal discrete problem is

(2.25)

\mathcal{A}^{\delta}_{h}\ :=\ \{(w_{\delta,h},f_{\delta,h})\in X_{\delta,h}\times Z_{h},w_{\delta,h}\ |\ B_{\delta}(w_{\delta,h},v_{\delta,h})\ =\ \langle f_{\delta,h},v_{\delta,h}\rangle\ \quad\forall v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}\,\}

Finally we state the local discrete optimal control problem.

Problem 2.5

(Local discrete problem)
.

Find a pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{h}},\overline{g_{h}})\in X_{h}\times Z_{h}
such that

(2.26)

I(\overline{u_{h}},\overline{g_{h}})\ =\ \min I(u_{h},g_{h}),

where the minimization is over pairs
\displaystyle(u_{h},g_{h})\in X_{h}\times Z_{h}
that satisfy

(2.27)

B_{0}(u_{h},v_{h})\ =\ \langle g_{h},v_{h}\rangle\ \quad\forall v_{h}\in X_{h}.

The effective admissible class of pairs for this local problem is

(2.28)

\mathcal{A}^{\text{loc}}_{h}\ :=\ \{(w_{h},f_{h})\in X_{h}\times Z_{h}\ |\ B_{0}(w_{h},v_{h})\ =\ \langle f_{h},v_{h}\rangle,\ \quad\forall v_{h}\in X_{h}\}.

Note that in each problem, the state equation governs the relationship between the force [control] and the displacement [state] that must take place in any admissible solution.

3.
Properties of function spaces

In this section we state and prove some structural properties of the function spaces

\displaystyle X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined in the previous section. We begin noting that the function spaces
are separable Hilbert spaces with the following inner product defined for
\displaystyle u,v\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

[[u,v]]:=\langle u,v\rangle+[u,v]_{X},

where
\displaystyle[u,v]_{X}=\iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|){Du(x,y)\over|x-y|}{Dv(x,y)\over|x-y|}dydx.
It is obvious that
\displaystyle[u,u]_{X}=[u]_{X}^{2}
, and that, under the working assumption on
\displaystyle H
, we have that
\displaystyle{h_{min}\over 2}[u]_{X}^{2}\leq B_{\delta}(u,u)\leq{h_{max}\over 2}[u]^{2}_{X}.

Moreover, for
\displaystyle u\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

[u]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\iint_{\Omega_{\delta}\times\Omega_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|{Du(x,y)\over|x-y|}\right|^{2}dydx

which we also use as a seminorm. It then follows from
[
39
]
that if
\displaystyle\widetilde{u}
is the zero extension of
\displaystyle u
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
then there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C(\delta,p)>0
such that, for any open set
\displaystyle B
containing
\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}
, we have

(3.1)

[\widetilde{u}]_{X(B;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ C\|u\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

In particular, the constant is independent of
\displaystyle B
, and we may select
\displaystyle B:=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where we define

X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\middle|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|){Du(x,y)\over|x-y|}dydx<\infty\right\}.

We now seek to demonstrate a continuous embedding result for Sobolev spaces into the space
\displaystyle X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. To accomplish this, we need a quantitative version of continuity in the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-norm; a local, scalar-valued analogue is discussed and proven in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Quantitative
\displaystyle L^{2}
-continuity)
.

For any
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, and all
\displaystyle v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

(3.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|(v(y+\xi)-v(y))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|^{2}dy\ \leq\ |\xi|^{2}\|\Sym(\nabla v)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}.

Proof.

We first prove the desired claim in the special case where
\displaystyle v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Fix
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Then by the Chain Rule, the Mean-Value Theorem for integrals, and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, we have

(3.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|(v(y+\xi)-v(y))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|^{2}dy\ =\ \frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int^{1}_{0}\nabla v(y+t\xi)\xi\cdot\xi dt\right|^{2}dy\\
\ \leq\ \frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int^{1}_{0}|\Sym(\nabla v(y+t\xi))\xi\cdot\xi|^{2}dtdy\ \leq\ |\xi|^{2}\|\Sym(\nabla v)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})},

where in the last step we have used invariance of the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-norm under translations, demonstrating the inequality for

\displaystyle v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The general case for
\displaystyle v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
follows by density.
∎

The estimate of Lemma
3.1
will now be used to prove a continuous embedding result.

Lemma 3.2

(Continuous embedding)
.

For all
\displaystyle\delta>0
, we have

(3.4)

\begin{split}[v]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ &\lesssim\ |v|_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).\end{split}

That is,
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the constant is independent of
\displaystyle\delta
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, for any
\displaystyle v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
its extension by zero outside of
\displaystyle\Omega
is in
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
vanishing almost everywhere outside of
\displaystyle\Omega.

Now for any
\displaystyle\delta>0
, we have

(3.5)

\begin{split}[v]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}&=\iint_{\Omega_{\delta}\times\Omega_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(|x-y|)\left|{Du(x,y)\over|x-y|}\right|^{2}dydx\\
&\leq\int_{B(0,\delta)}\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|(v(y+\xi)-v(y))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|^{2}dyd\xi,\end{split}

where we have used that
\displaystyle\supp(k_{\delta})\subset B(0,\delta)
. Now our expression is in a form on which we can use Lemma
3.1
on the inner integral to conclude that

(3.6)

\int_{B(0,\delta)}\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|(v(y+\xi)-v(y))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|^{2}dyd\xi\ \leq\ \|\Sym(\nabla v)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}\leq\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Next we show that compactly supported smooth functions are dense in
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 3.3

(Density)
.

The set

\left\{v\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \middle|\ \exists R>0:\supp(v)\subset B(0,R)\right\}

is dense in
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi\in C_{0}^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
\displaystyle 0\leq\varphi\leq 1
,
\displaystyle\varphi\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle B(0,1)
,
\displaystyle\supp(\varphi)\subset B(0,2)
, and
\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1
. For
\displaystyle R>0
define
\displaystyle\varphi_{R}(x)=\varphi(x/R)
and
\displaystyle\psi_{R}:=1-\varphi_{R}
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and we claim that

(3.7)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}[u-u\varphi_{R}]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\ =\ 0.

To this end, we compute

(3.8)

\displaystyle{}^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|D(u\psi_{R})(x,y)|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle\lesssim\ \iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\psi_{R}(x)-\psi_{R}(y)|^{2}\left|u(x)\cdot\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}\right|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\psi_{R}(y)^{2}\left|(u(x)-u(y))\cdot\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}\right|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy.

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem we deduce

(3.9)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\psi_{R}(y)^{2}\left|(u(x)-u(y))\cdot\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}\right|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy\ =\ 0.

Now, to handle the first integral in (
3.8
) we define

(3.10)

K_{R}(x)\ :=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\psi_{R}(x)-\psi_{R}(y)|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dy.

By using the conditions on
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
and the Lipschitz continuity of
\displaystyle\psi
, the sequence
\displaystyle|K_{R}(x)|
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle R
and in
\displaystyle x
. Further,
\displaystyle K_{R}(x)\rightarrow 0
pointwise on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\displaystyle R\rightarrow\infty
, so we may, once again, use the Dominated Convergence Theorem to conclude that

(3.11)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u(x)|^{2}K_{R}(x)dx\ =\ 0,

proving (
3.7
). Finally, with one more application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we see that

(3.12)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\|u-u\varphi_{R}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ \lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\|u\psi_{R}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ 0,

and this lets us complete the proof, with
\displaystyle\{u\varphi_{R}\}^{\infty}_{R=1}
as our approximating family of functions in
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded support.
∎

The following result is analogous to
[
28
, Proposition 4.1]
and
[
45
, Lemma 5.2]
.

Lemma 3.4

(Mollification)
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a vector field that vanishes outside a compact subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
denote by
\displaystyle\eta_{\epsilon}
a standard mollifier, and
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}=u*\eta_{\epsilon}
. Then, for
\displaystyle\epsilon
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,

(3.13)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}[u-u_{\epsilon}]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ 0.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set so that
\displaystyle\supp(u)\subset K
. Then

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is supported in
\displaystyle K_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \dist(x,K)\leq\epsilon\}
for any
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. Since the mollifier
\displaystyle\eta_{\epsilon}
is even, we may use Hölder’s Inequality, Jensen’s Inequality, and the identity

(3.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\eta_{\epsilon}*\eta_{\epsilon})(z)dz\ =\ \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\epsilon}(z)dz\right)^{2}\ =\ 1

to obtain the estimate

(3.15)

\displaystyle{}^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\iiiint_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}\eta_{\epsilon}(z)\eta_{\epsilon}(z^{\prime})Du(x-z,y-z)Du(x-z^{\prime},y-z^{\prime})\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dz^{\prime}dzdxdy\

\displaystyle=\ \iiiint_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}\eta_{\epsilon}(z)\eta_{\epsilon}(z^{\prime})Du(x+z^{\prime}-z,y+z^{\prime}-z)Du(x,y)\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dz^{\prime}dzdxdy\

\displaystyle=\iiiint_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}\eta_{\epsilon}(z-z^{\prime})\eta_{\epsilon}(z^{\prime})Du(x+z,y+z)Du(x,y)\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dz^{\prime}dzdxdy\ \

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\eta_{\epsilon}*\eta_{\epsilon})(z)Du(x,y)Du(x+z,y+z)dzdxdy\

\displaystyle\leq[u]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\eta_{\epsilon}*\eta_{\epsilon})(z)|Du(x+z,y+z)|dz\right)^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\

\displaystyle\leq[u]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\eta_{\epsilon}*\eta_{\epsilon})(z)\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|Du(x+z,y+z)|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdydz\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ \

\displaystyle=[u]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}

which holds for all
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. As a consequence,
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
.
To proceed further, we define the maps
\displaystyle U,U_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(3.16)

U(x,y)\ =\ Du(x,y)\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}\qquad U_{\epsilon}(x,y)\ =\ Du_{\epsilon}(x,y)\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}},

and these definitions in turn imply that

(3.17)

[u-u_{\epsilon}]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ \iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|D(u-u_{\epsilon})(x,y)|^{2}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy\ =\ \|U-U_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

The proof will be complete once we show that
\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}\rightarrow U
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. As is standard for mollifiers,
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and a.e. pointwise in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both as
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Thus by Fatou’s Lemma, we get the convergence

(3.18)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \geq\ \|U\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})},

while the reverse inequality follows from sending
\displaystyle\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in (
3.15
). This combined with showing
\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup U
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is enough to show the strong convergence in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
that we seek, so we focus on proving this weak convergence. Let
\displaystyle V\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary and define the function

(3.19)

V_{j}(x,y)\ :=\ \begin{cases}V(x,y),|x|,|y|\leq j,|x-y|\geq\frac{1}{j}\\
0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

With this definition in mind, the Dominated Convergence Theorem tells us that
\displaystyle V_{j}\rightarrow V
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\displaystyle j\rightarrow\infty
. We define
\displaystyle h_{1}^{j},h_{2}^{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(3.20)

\displaystyle h_{1}^{j}(x)

\displaystyle:=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}V_{j}(x,y)\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}dy;

\displaystyle h_{2}^{j}(y)

\displaystyle:=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}V_{j}(x,y)\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}dx.

Since
\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}\leq 1+\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}
for all
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can see that these functions have bounded support, and thus belong to
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Then due to the a.e. convergence
\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\rightarrow u
, we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}U_{\epsilon}(x,y)V_{j}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\ \lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}[u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(y)]\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}\cdot V_{j}(x,y)dxdy\

\displaystyle=\ \lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{\epsilon}(x)\cdot h_{1}^{j}(x)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{\epsilon}(y)\cdot h_{2}^{j}(y)dy\right)\

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)\cdot h^{j}_{1}(x)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(y)\cdot h_{2}^{j}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\ \iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}Du(x,y)\sqrt{\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}}V_{j}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\ \iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}U(x,y)V_{j}(x,y)dxdy,

which holds for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Taking a limit supremum in
\displaystyle\epsilon
, the convergence in (
3.21
), and applying Hölder inequality gives

(3.22)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(U_{\epsilon}-U)(x,y)V(x,y)dxdy\right|

\displaystyle=\ \limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(U_{\epsilon}-U)(x,y)(V-V_{j})(x,y)dxdy\right|\

\displaystyle\leq\ \limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|U_{\epsilon}-U\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|V-V_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\ 2\|U\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|V-V_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ ,

which holds for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Finally, due to
\displaystyle V_{j}\rightarrow V
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we obtain the limit

(3.23)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(U_{\epsilon}-U)(x,y)V(x,y)dxdy\ =\ 0,

and from this it follows that
\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup U
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.5

\displaystyle\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}

Proposition 3.6

(Nonlocal Poincaré)
.

There exists a
\displaystyle\delta_{0}>0
and a constant
\displaystyle C(\delta_{0})>0
such that for all
\displaystyle\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
and
\displaystyle u\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

(3.24)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ C(\delta_{0})\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}k_{\delta}(x-y)\frac{|Du(x,y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy.

With the aid of above Poincaré-type inequality we may apply Lax-Milgram to deduce the unique solvability of the state equations of the nonlocal optimal control problem stated in the previous section. We summarize this with the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7

(Well-posedness of state equation)
.

The state equations (
2.15
), (
2.18
), (
2.24
), and (
2.27
) are uniquely solvable in their corresponding energy spaces.

From standard linear theory, we know that the solution operator of the state equations is linear and continuous. One important fact we need to demonstrate the solvability of optimal control problems is the compactness of this solution operator. While for the discrete problems this question is trivial, for the continuous problems it needs a resolution. The compactness of the solution operator is related to the compactness of the image space which, for (
2.15
), is
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
; whereas for (
2.18
) is
\displaystyle H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The compactness of the latter in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is standard.

Below we build a framework needed to ultimately prove the compact embedding os
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This is much akin to the compact embedding results for fractional Sobolev spaces; see, for instance,
[
18
,
19
]
. This will largely be based on the results of
[

]
, see also
[
25
]
, which we extend to vector-valued functions using a weaker norm that only involves a projected difference quotient. To this end we introduce a definition.

Definition 3.8

(Local compactness)
.

If
\displaystyle E
is a normed vector space, we call a continuous linear operator
\displaystyle T:E\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})

locally compact
if the operator
\displaystyle R_{K}T:E\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined via the truncation function
\displaystyle R_{K}u:=\mathds{1}_{K}u
is a compact operator for every compact subset
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following proposition demonstrates that it suffices to show
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a locally compact embedding.

Proposition 3.9

(Compactness)
.

If
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a locally compact embedding, then for every bounded and open
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and every
\displaystyle\delta>0
, the embedding
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is compact.

Proof.

As we remarked earlier, for every
\displaystyle u\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, its extension by zero outside of
\displaystyle\Omega_{\delta}
belongs to
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,
\displaystyle[u]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}=[u]_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Now if the inclusion
\displaystyle\mathfrak{i}:X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is locally compact, then in Definition
3.8
, we can set
\displaystyle K:=\overline{\Omega}
to conclude that
\displaystyle R_{K}\mathfrak{i}:X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is compact. The result now follows easily.
∎

We now prove the local compact embedding of
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the remaining portion of this section. We follow the argument in
[

]
.

Lemma 3.10

(Convolution)
.

Suppose
\displaystyle W\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
is a matrix-valued function with
\displaystyle L^{1}
-entries. Then the corresponding convolution operator
\displaystyle T_{W}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined via

(3.25)

[T_{W}u(x)]_{i}=[(W*u)(x)]_{i}:=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[W(x-y)]_{i,\cdot}\cdot u(y)dy=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[W(x-y)]_{i,j}[u(y)]_{j}dy

for each
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is locally compact.

Proof.

The proof follows from
[
29
, Lemma 3.1]
after noting that for
\displaystyle i=1,2,\dots,n
,
\displaystyle[T_{W}u]_{i}
is a finite sum convolution operators which are locally compact.
∎

Theorem 3.11

(Local compactness)
.

Fix
\displaystyle\delta>0
.
Suppose that
\displaystyle\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then the space

\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is locally compactly embedded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

For
\displaystyle\tau>0
, let
\displaystyle j_{\tau}(\xi):=\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus{B(0,\tau)}}(\xi)
. Then
\displaystyle j_{\tau}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and that, by assumption on
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
, we have that
\displaystyle\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\to\infty
as
\displaystyle\tau\to 0
. We now introduce the matrix-valued function

(3.26)

J_{\tau}(\xi)\ :=c_{n}\ \frac{j_{\tau}}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}.

where
\displaystyle c_{n}
is a normalizing constant that depends only on
\displaystyle n
so that

(3.27)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\tau}(\xi)d\xi\ =\ \mathbb{I}_{n},\quad\text{the identity matrix.}

Let
\displaystyle u\in X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and we claim that

(3.28)

\|u-T_{j_{\tau}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \left(\frac{1}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}[u]_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We prove this via a direct calculation: rewrite
\displaystyle u-T_{j_{\tau}}u
as

(3.29)

u(x)-T_{j_{\tau}}(u)(x)\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\tau}(\xi)(u(x)-u(x+\xi))d\xi.

Now, we calculate the
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
-norm, and estimate it with the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality and the pointwise inequality
\displaystyle j_{\tau}(\xi)\leq\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}
:

(3.30)

\displaystyle\|u-T_{j_{\tau}}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{j_{\tau}(\xi)}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\left((u(x)-u(x+\xi))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}d\xi\right|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\ \frac{1}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}j_{\tau}(\xi)\left|(u(x)-u(x+\xi))\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right|^{2}d\xi dx\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}[u]^{2}_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Taking square roots in (
3.30
) immediately yields (
3.28
).

Now let
\displaystyle M\subset X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a bounded set, and
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be compact; our proof will be complete once we show that
\displaystyle R_{K}(M)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is relatively compact. To this end, let
\displaystyle C:=\sup_{u\in M}\|u\|_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
. Since
\displaystyle\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we may take
\displaystyle\tau>0
to be sufficiently small so that
\displaystyle\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geq\frac{C^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}
.
By Lemma
3.10
, the set
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}:=[R_{K}T_{j_{\tau}}](M)
is relatively compact in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus we may use the estimate (
3.28
) to obtain, for any
\displaystyle u\in M
,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|R_{K}u-[R_{K}T_{j_{\tau}}]u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \|u-T_{j_{\tau}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\

(3.32)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\|j_{\tau}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}[u]_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \frac{\epsilon\|u\|_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{C}\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\ \epsilon.

From this we conclude that
\displaystyle R_{K}(M)
is contained within an
\displaystyle\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}
, and which is relatively compact in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
(since
\displaystyle j_{\tau}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
). Thus,
\displaystyle R_{K}(M)
is totally bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is a sufficient condition for the local compact embedding to hold.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

We make two remarks. First, the assumption
\displaystyle j_{0}={k_{\delta}(\xi)\over|\xi|^{2}}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
cannot be waived. Indeed, otherwise, we have
\displaystyle X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the norm estimate that
\displaystyle[u]_{X(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq 4\|j_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
Second, a similar type of compactness result, with a proof that uses a different approach (see
[
10
]
), is also established in
[
35
]
under the assumption on the kernel
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
that

(3.33)

\lim_{\varrho\to 0}{\varrho^{2}\over\int_{B_{\varrho}(0)}k_{\delta}(\xi)d\xi}\ =\ 0.

On the one hand, if
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
satisfies (
3.33
), then
\displaystyle{k_{\delta}(\xi)\over|\xi|^{2}}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Otherwise, by continuity of the integral,

(3.34)

\lim_{\varrho\to 0}\int_{B_{\varrho}(0)}{k_{\delta}(\xi)\over|\xi|^{2}}d\xi\ =\ 0,

from which it follows that
\displaystyle\lim_{\varrho\to 0}{\varrho^{2}\over\int_{B_{\varrho}(0)}k_{\delta}(\xi)d\xi}=\infty,
contradicting (
3.33
). As such (
3.33
) is a more restrictive assumption on
\displaystyle k_{\delta}.
On the other hand, there are kernels with the property that
\displaystyle{k_{\delta}(\xi)\over|\xi|^{2}}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that fail to satisfy (
3.33
). For example, the kernel
\displaystyle k_{\delta}(\xi)={1\over|\xi|^{n-2}}\chi_{B_{\delta}(0)}(\xi)
has the property that
\displaystyle\lim_{\varrho\to 0}{\varrho^{2}\over\int_{B_{\varrho}(0)}k_{\delta}(\xi)d\xi}>0
, yet
\displaystyle{k_{\delta}(\xi)\over|\xi|^{2}}={1\over|\xi|^{n}}\chi_{B_{\delta}(0)}(\xi)\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

4.
Well-posedness: state system and minimization

In this section we show existence and uniqueness of solutions for each one of the optimal control problems introduced in Section
2
. The approach we use is a reduced formulation where the constrained optimization is reformulated as an unconstrained optimization of the control via the solution operator of the state equation. To facilitate that we begin by proving an abstract well-posedness result that appears in some form in
[
27
,
55
]
; we provide a proof for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 4.1

(Well-posedness)
.

Let
\displaystyle(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
be a real Banach space with
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset Y^{*}
. Suppose also that
\displaystyle S:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow Y
is a compact operator, and
\displaystyle G:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous. For a given
\displaystyle\lambda\geq 0
and
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
a nonempty, closed, bounded, and convex subset of
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define
\displaystyle j:Z_{\text{ad}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(4.1)

j(g)\ :=\ G(Sg)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g(x)|^{2}dx,

for some non-negative
\displaystyle\Gamma\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Then, the optimization problem

(4.2)

\min_{g\in Z_{\text{ad}}}j(g)

has a solution
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}
. Furthermore, if
\displaystyle\lambda>0
,
\displaystyle S
is linear, and
\displaystyle G
is convex, then (
4.2
) has a unique minimizer.
Alternatively, if
\displaystyle\lambda=0
and
\displaystyle G
is strictly convex on its domain (with
\displaystyle S
still being linear), then the minimizer is unique.

Proof.

We use the direct method of calculus of variations to show that (
4.2
) has a solution. First, we note that
\displaystyle j
is bounded from below. Indeed, since the second term is nonnegative for all
\displaystyle g
, it suffices to demonstrate that the first term is bounded from below.
To that end, assume otherwise.
Then there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\{w_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}\subset Z_{\text{ad}}
such that

(4.3)

G(Sw_{m})\ <\ -m

for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. However,
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
is a closed, bounded, convex subset of a Hilbert space, and, by
[
55
, Theorem 2.11]
, it is weakly sequentially compact. It follows that some sub-sequence
\displaystyle\{w_{m_{k}}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
of
\displaystyle\{w_{m}\}^{\infty}_{n=1}
converges weakly to some
\displaystyle\overline{w}\in Z_{\text{ad}}
. Since
\displaystyle S
is a compact operator,
\displaystyle Sw_{m_{k}}\rightarrow Sw
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
strongly in
\displaystyle Y
. Since
\displaystyle G
is lower semi-continuous, we have

(4.4)

\ G(S\overline{w})\leq\ \liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}G(Sw_{m_{k}})\ =-\infty,

which poses a contradiction, since
\displaystyle G
does not assume the value
\displaystyle-\infty
.

We henceforth denote
\displaystyle j_{0}:=\inf_{g\in Z_{\text{ad}}}j(g)
, and the remainder of the existence part of the proof is comprised of finding
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}\in Z_{\text{ad}}
such that
\displaystyle j(\widetilde{g})=j_{0}
. To this end, we identify a sequence
\displaystyle\{g_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}\subset Z_{\text{ad}}
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}j(g_{m})=j_{0}
as
\displaystyle m\rightarrow\infty
. Recalling again
[
55
, Theorem 2.11]
we obtain that some sub-sequence
\displaystyle\{g_{m_{k}}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
of
\displaystyle\{g_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
converges weakly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to some
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}\in Z_{\text{ad}}
. Moreover, since
\displaystyle|\sqrt{\Gamma}(x)g_{m}(x)|\leq|\sqrt{\Gamma}(x)b(x)|
for all
\displaystyle m
, the sequence
\displaystyle\{\sqrt{\Gamma}\,g_{m}\}
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as well. From this we may choose the sub-sequence
\displaystyle\{g_{m_{k}}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
so that
\displaystyle\{\sqrt{\Gamma}\,g_{m_{k}}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
converges weakly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By a density argument, it is easy to show that the weak limit has to be
\displaystyle\sqrt{\Gamma}\,\tilde{g}
.
Since
\displaystyle S
is compact and
\displaystyle G
is lower semi-continuous, we have the inequality chain

(4.5)

\displaystyle j(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\ G(S\widetilde{g})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|\tilde{g}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\ \liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}G(Sg_{m_{k}})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|\tilde{g}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\ \liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}G(Sg_{m_{k}})+\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g_{m_{k}}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\ \liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(G(Sg_{m_{k}})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g_{m_{k}}|^{2}dx\right)\ \leq\ \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}j(g_{m_{k}})\ =\ j_{0}.

Since
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}\in Z_{\text{ad}}
, it follows that
\displaystyle j(\widetilde{g})=j_{0}
, and we have found a minimizer. The proof of uniqueness under the given additional conditions is standard since
\displaystyle j
will automatically become strictly convex.
∎

Corollary 4.2

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

Problems
2.2
,
2.4
,
2.3
, and
2.5
are all well-posed. That is, the objective functional has a [unique] minimizing pair, which in turn solves the corresponding state equation.

Proof.

The well-posedness of the state equation of each problem follows from the Lax-Milgram lemma as done in Corollary
3.7
. Notice that in all cases, the solution space
\displaystyle Y
is compactly embedded into
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For the local problems, the embedding
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\Subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is standard, while for the non-local problems we invoke Theorem
3.11
and Proposition
3.9
. We thus have that the solution mapping
\displaystyle S:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to Y
is compact, and then
we may write the reduced cost functionals for our problems abstractly as

(4.6)

j(g)\ :=\ \int_{\Omega}F(x,Sg(x))dx+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x)|g(x)|^{2}dx.

Note that this functional satisfies all the conditions of Theorem
4.1
, which guarantees existence and uniqueness of a minimizer.
∎

5.
Analysis in vanishing horizon parameter

Having shown that, for every horizon
\displaystyle\delta\geq 0
, the nonlocal optimal control problem
2.2
has a unique solution
\displaystyle(\overline{u}_{\delta},\overline{g}_{\delta})
, we now study the behavior of the pair as
\displaystyle\delta\to 0
. Notice that
\displaystyle\overline{u}_{\delta}
minimizes
the potential energy functional

(5.1)

W_{\delta}(u)\ :=\ B_{\delta}(u,u)-\int_{\Omega}g_{\delta}(x)\cdot u(x)dx

over
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We begin with the following convergence result.

Lemma 5.1

(Compactness of solutions of the control problem)
.

Let
\displaystyle\{(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})\}_{\delta>0}
be the family of optimal state-control pairs solving
2.2
. There exists a
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g})\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}
such that, up to a sub-sequence,
\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\delta}\rightharpoonup\overline{g}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle\overline{u_{\delta}}\rightarrow\overline{u}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\displaystyle\delta\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

Theorem
4.1
gives existence and uniqueness of optimal pairs that minimize the energy
\displaystyle W_{\delta}
defined in (
5.1
).
Moreover, since
\displaystyle 0
is an admissible control, we have that

\displaystyle W_{\delta}(\overline{u_{\delta}})\ \leq\ 0,

and so, after rearranging we get

(5.2)

B_{\delta}(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{u_{\delta}})\ \leq\ \int_{\Omega}\overline{g_{\delta}}(x)\cdot\overline{u_{\delta}}(x)dx.

The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, in conjunction with the nonlocal Poincaré inequality (
3.24
) and the Triangle inequality, gives us

(5.3)

[\overline{u_{\delta}}]^{2}_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ \|\overline{g_{\delta}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\overline{u_{\delta}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\|\overline{g_{\delta}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}[\overline{u_{\delta}}]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Notice that the constant in this estimate, owing to (
3.24
), is independent of
\displaystyle\delta
. Furthermore, since
\displaystyle\{\overline{g_{\delta}}\}_{\delta>0}\subset Z_{\text{ad}}
, it is norm bounded (and therefore has a weak limit, up to a sub-sequence), and as a consequence

(5.4)

\sup_{\delta>0}[\overline{u_{\delta}}]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ C.

Now since
\displaystyle\overline{u}_{\delta}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, after extending by zero to
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
(with
\displaystyle\delta=1
) we have that

\sup_{\delta>0}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}k_{\delta}(x-y){|Du_{\delta}(x,y)|^{2}\over|x-y|^{2}}dydx=\sup_{\delta>0}[\overline{u_{\delta}}]_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\ \leq\ C.

From this, we may use
[
37
, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
33
, Theorem 2.5]
to conclude that the
\displaystyle\{\overline{u_{\delta}}\}_{\delta>0}
is precompact in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and converges strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to some
\displaystyle\overline{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(up to a sub-sequence).
∎

The main question we would like to address in the remaining is whether the limiting pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g})
solves a corresponding limiting optimal problem.
The limiting behavior of the minimizers is closely related to the variational convergence of the above parametrized energy functionals.
The main tool we shall use is
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence (see
[
10
,
13
,
16
,
43
]
for more on properties of
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence;
[
6
,
Bon
,
37
,
38
,
43
]
for examples of proofs of
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence for other peridynamics models.
For convenience, we recall its definition here.

Definition 5.2

(
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence)
.

We say that the sequence
\displaystyle E_{\delta}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

\displaystyle\Gamma
-converges
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\displaystyle E_{0}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
(denoted
\displaystyle E_{\delta}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}E_{0}
) if the following properties hold:

GC1

The liminf property:
Assume
\displaystyle u_{\delta}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then we have the Fatou-type inequality

(5.5)

E_{0}(u)\ \leq\ \liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}E_{\delta}(u_{\delta}).

GC2

Recovery sequence property:
For each
\displaystyle u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
where
\displaystyle u_{\delta}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

5.1.
Vanishing horizon parameter for continuous problem

We will be working on the extended linear peridynamic energy functional we now define.
Let
\displaystyle E_{\delta}:L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow[0,\infty]
denote the energy

(5.7)

E_{\delta}(u)\ :=\ \iint_{\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}H(x,y)k_{\delta}(x-y)\frac{|Du(x,y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dxdy,\quad\text{for $\displaystyle u\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})$}

and
\displaystyle+\infty
otherwise. Similarly, define a limiting energy
\displaystyle E_{0}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow[0,\infty]

(5.8)

E_{0}(u)\ :=\ \frac{1}{n(n+2)}\int_{\Omega}H(x,x)(2\|\Sym(\nabla u(x))\|^{2}_{F}+\dive(u(x))^{2})dx\quad\text{for $\displaystyle u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})$,}

and
\displaystyle+\infty
otherwise.
Note that since, for all
\displaystyle\delta>0
, our energy
\displaystyle E_{\delta}
is quadratic we have

(5.9)

|E_{\delta}(u)-E_{\delta}(v)|\ \leq\ E_{\delta}(u+v)^{\frac{1}{2}}E_{\delta}(u-v)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for all
\displaystyle u,v\in X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 5.3

(Nonlocal to local)
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\Subset\Omega
and
\displaystyle w\in C^{2}(A,\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have that

\begin{split}\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{A}\int_{\Omega}&h(x)k_{\delta}(x-y)\frac{|Dw(x,y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}dydx\ \\
&=\frac{1}{n(n+2)}\int_{A}h(x)(2\|\Sym(\nabla w(x))\|^{2}_{F}+\dive(w(x))^{2})dx.\end{split}

The proof of this can be found in
[

,
35
,
37
]
in some form or another.

We now state the result on the variational convergence of the parameterized energies
\displaystyle E_{\delta}
.

Theorem 5.4

(
\displaystyle E_{\delta}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}E_{0}
)
.

Let
\displaystyle E_{\delta}
and
\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle E_{\delta}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}E_{0}

Proof.

We verify each of the conditions that comprise this definition.

Proof of
GC1
:
Let
\displaystyle u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\{u_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}\subset L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle u_{\delta}\rightarrow u

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle E_{\delta}(u_{\delta})<\infty

(5.10)

\sup_{\delta>0}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{k_{\delta}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}|Du_{\delta}(x,y)|^{2}dxdy\ <\ \infty.

Arguing in the same way as in the proof of

\displaystyle u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle u_{\delta}\rightarrow u

From here we will look to find a variant of
[

, largely repeating the lower semi-continuity part of the proof of

\displaystyle h
is the constant function
\displaystyle h=1

\displaystyle A\Subset\Omega
open, we have the inequality

(5.11)

\displaystyle\epsilon

Next we assume that
\displaystyle h

6.
First order Optimality and discretization

Let us now turn our attention to first-order optimality conditions, which are the gateway to discretizing the nonlocal optimal control problem. From here onward, we assume that our integrand
\displaystyle F
(first introduced in (
2.10
)) is continuously Gâteaux-differentiable in the second argument. The first Gâteaux derivative will be denoted as
\displaystyle F_{u}
. We will also denote by
\displaystyle S_{\delta}
the solution operator corresponding to the state system (
2.15
), and by
\displaystyle S_{\delta}^{*}
the adjoint of
\displaystyle S_{\delta}
in the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-sense. Due to Corollary
4.2
, the operator
\displaystyle S_{\delta}
is well defined. Using the reduced objective functional (
4.6
), we recall that
[
55
, Lemma 2.21]
shows the first order necessary condition

(6.1)

\langle j^{\prime}(\overline{g_{\delta}}),\gamma_{z}-\overline{g_{\delta}}\rangle\ \geq\ 0\ \quad\forall\gamma_{z}\in Z_{\text{ad}},

where
\displaystyle j^{\prime}
represents the derivative of
\displaystyle j
in some appropriate sense.
This functional has two terms that need to be differentiated: for the first term, we use the Fréchet differentiability of
\displaystyle F
and the Chain Rule; the derivative of the second term comes from the Fréchet derivative of
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\Gamma}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
(the weighted
\displaystyle\Gamma
norm). See
[
17
, Lemma 3.5]
for a similar calculation corresponding to the fractional Laplacian.
Inequality (
6.1
) can now be rewritten as

(6.2)

\langle j^{\prime}(\overline{g_{\delta}}),\gamma_{z}-\overline{g_{\delta}}\rangle\ =\ \Big\langle S_{\delta}^{*}F_{u}(\cdot,S_{\delta}\overline{g_{\delta}}(\cdot))+\lambda\Gamma\,\overline{g_{\delta}},\gamma_{z}-\overline{g_{\delta}}\Big\rangle\ \geq\ 0\ \quad\forall\gamma_{z}\in Z_{\text{ad}}.

It is standard to introduce a new notation to rewrite the above as the system

(6.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\langle\overline{p_{\delta}}+\lambda\Gamma\,\overline{g_{\delta}},\gamma_{z}-\overline{g_{\delta}}\rangle\ \geq\ 0,\quad\forall\gamma_{z}\in Z_{\text{ad}}\\
&\overline{p_{\delta}}\ =\ S_{\delta}^{*}F_{u}(\cdot,\overline{u_{\delta}})\\
&\overline{u_{\delta}}\ =\ S_{\delta}\overline{g_{\delta}}.\end{aligned}

Note that
\displaystyle S_{\delta}
is a self-adjoint operator, so
\displaystyle S_{\delta}^{*}F_{u}(\cdot,\overline{u_{\delta}})\ =\ S_{\delta}F_{u}(\cdot,\overline{u_{\delta}})
, and so
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta}}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Furthermore, as a consequence of these conditions, in the event that
\displaystyle\Gamma=1
, we obtain
\displaystyle g_{\delta}
is the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-projection of the adjoint
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta}}
onto the control space
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
, i.e.

(6.4)

\overline{g_{\delta}}(x)\ =\ -\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\overline{p_{\delta}}(x)),

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{E}
denotes the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-projection onto the set
\displaystyle E
. Notice that, owing to the assumption that the objective functional is strictly convex, these first order necessary conditions are also sufficient. We summarize the result as follows.

Proposition 6.1

(Optimality conditions)
.

For every
\displaystyle\delta>0
, the pair
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{ad}
is a solution to Problem
2.2
if and only if (
6.3
) holds.

6.1.
Error analysis for nonlocal problems

With the aid of the optimality system, we are able to perform an error analysis, which we now begin. From here on we assume, for simplicity, that
\displaystyle\Gamma\equiv 1
and that
\displaystyle F(x,v)=\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}
. With this at hand, the optimality conditions for the non-local discrete problem read:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\overline{p_{\delta,h}}+\lambda\overline{g_{\delta,h}},\gamma_{h}-\overline{g_{\delta,h}}\rangle\ &\geq\ 0,\quad\forall\gamma_{h}\in Z_{\text{ad}}\cap Z_{h}\\
\overline{p_{\delta,h}}\ &=\ S_{{\delta,h}}^{*}\overline{u_{\delta,h}},\\
\overline{u_{\delta,h}}\ &=\ S_{{\delta,h}}\overline{g_{\delta,h}},\end{aligned}

where
\displaystyle S_{{\delta,h}}
is the discrete solution operator, and
\displaystyle S_{{\delta,h}}^{*}
is its discrete
\displaystyle L^{2}
adjoint. Note that
\displaystyle S_{{\delta,h}}
is a self-adjoint operator, so
\displaystyle S^{*}_{\delta,h}F_{u}(\cdot,\overline{u_{\delta,h}})=S_{{\delta,h}}F_{u}(\cdot,\overline{u_{\delta,h}})
. Also as with the non-local continuous optimality conditions, it follows that
\displaystyle\overline{g_{\delta,h}}(x)\ =\ -\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{\delta,h}}(x))
, where
\displaystyle\Pi_{0}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow Z_{h}
denotes the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-projection onto
\displaystyle Z_{h}
.

To ease the error analysis, define the intermediary functions
\displaystyle\widehat{u_{\delta}},\widehat{p_{\delta}}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

(6.6)

B_{\delta}(\widehat{u_{\delta}},v_{\delta})\ =\ \langle\overline{g_{\delta,h}},v_{\delta}\rangle\ \quad\forall v_{\delta}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n});

(6.7)

B_{\delta}(v_{\delta},\widehat{p_{\delta}})\ =\ \langle v_{\delta},\overline{u_{\delta,h}}\rangle\ \quad\ \forall v_{\delta}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

The existence and uniqueness of these functions follows from the Lax-Milgram Theorem. More importantly, we observe that the optimal discrete state and adjoint variables are nothing but the Galerkin approximations to
\displaystyle\widehat{u_{\delta}},\widehat{p_{\delta}}
, respectively. From this we immediately obtain, using Céa’s Lemma, that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{u_{\delta}}-\overline{u_{\delta,h}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}}\|\widehat{u_{\delta}}-v_{\delta,h}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})},

\displaystyle\|\widehat{p_{\delta}}-\overline{p_{\delta,h}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{q_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}}\|\widehat{p_{\delta}}-q_{\delta,h}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We now prove error estimates for the state and adjoint.

Theorem 6.2

(State and adjoint error estimates)
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle(\overline{u_{\delta,h}},\overline{g_{\delta,h}})
is the solution to Problem
2.4
;
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta,h}}
solves the discrete adjoint equation in (
6.5
) given
\displaystyle\overline{u_{\delta,h}}
; (
\displaystyle\overline{u_{\delta}},\overline{g_{\delta}})
is the solution to Problem
2.2
; and
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta}}
solves the continuous adjoint equation in (
6.3
) corresponding to the state
\displaystyle\overline{u_{\delta}}
. Then we have these error estimates for the states, and the adjoints:

(6.9)

\|\overline{u_{\delta}}-\overline{u_{\delta,h}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ \inf_{v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}}\|\widehat{u_{\delta}}-v_{\delta,h}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\overline{g_{\delta}}-\overline{g_{\delta,h}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})};

(6.10)

\|\overline{p_{\delta}}-\overline{p_{\delta,h}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ \inf_{v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}}\|\widehat{p_{\delta}}-v_{\delta,h}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\inf_{v_{\delta,h}\in X_{\delta,h}}\|\widehat{u_{\delta}}-v_{\delta,h}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
+\|\overline{g_{\delta}}-\overline{g_{\delta,h}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

We begin by proving (
6.9
). Substitute
\displaystyle v_{\delta}:=\overline{u_{\delta}}-\widehat{u_{\delta}}
in (
2.15
) and (
6.6
), and subtract those two equations to obtain

(6.11)

B_{\delta}(\overline{u_{\delta}}-\widehat{u_{\delta}},\overline{u_{\delta}}-\widehat{u_{\delta}})\ =\ \langle\overline{g_{\delta}}-\overline{g_{\delta,h}},\overline{u_{\delta}}-\widehat{u_{\delta}}\rangle.

Using the definition of
\displaystyle H(x,y)
, Hölder’s inequality, and (
3.6
) gives

(6.12)

\|\overline{u_{\delta}}-\widehat{u_{\delta}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ \|\overline{g_{\delta}}-\overline{g_{\delta,h}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

This, combined with (
6.8
) then yields the result.
The proof of (
6.10
) uses the same procedure, and is thus omitted.
∎

At this stage we must observe that the infima in (
6.9
) and (
6.10
) tend to zero as
\displaystyle h\to 0^{+}
. This is because of density; if a rate of convergence in these terms is desired, then further regularity of
\displaystyle\widehat{u_{\delta}}
and
\displaystyle\widehat{p_{\delta}}
must be studied. For some kernels this could be done, for instance, by exploiting that
\displaystyle\overline{u_{\delta,h}}
belongs to a space that is strictly smaller than the dual of
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
; see, for instance,
[
1
,
26
,
44
]
. Due to the generality we place on our kernel, we do not pursue this. It remains to estimate the difference between continuous and discrete controls, which will now be our focus.

While in general our controls only belong to
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, in the event we have additional regularity, we can quantify our forthcoming estimates even more. Indeed, in the local case, the projection formula
\displaystyle\overline{g}(x)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\overline{p}(x))
combined with the fact that
\displaystyle\overline{p}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
imply further regularity on the control (namely, that
\displaystyle\overline{g}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
). The following lemma provides a sufficient condition on the kernel for this to also be the case for nonlocal problems.

In the following result, we require
\displaystyle s\neq\frac{1}{2}
to be able to use the Hardy-type inequality
[
34
, Theorem 2.3]
. This is essentially a technicality.

Lemma 6.3

(Regularity of control for fractional-type kernels)
.

Suppose that in the definition of
\displaystyle Z_{ad}
, given in (
2.9
), the functions
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
are constants. Suppose also that, in addition to the contents of Assumption
2.1
, we have that

(6.13)

\frac{k_{\delta}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\ \sim\ \frac{1}{|\xi|^{n+2s}}

holds for all
\displaystyle\xi\in B_{\delta}(0)
, for some
\displaystyle s\neq\frac{1}{2}
. Then, necessarily,
\displaystyle\overline{g_{\delta}}\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

We introduce some notation specifically for this proof. As seen in
[
39
]
, we denote by
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
the fractional Sobolev norm on vector fields, and denote by
\displaystyle H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space of vector fields with finite fractional Sobolev norm. It has been shown in
[
39
, Theorem 1.1]
that
the space

\chi^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ :=\ \left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \middle|\ \int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}{|Du(x,y)|^{2}\over|x-y|^{n+2s}}dydx<\infty\right\}

coincides with
\displaystyle H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with comparable norms.
Now since
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta}}\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
satisfies (
6.13
), via direct calculation we have that
\displaystyle\overline{p_{\delta}}\in\chi^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and so it is in
\displaystyle H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
To finish the proof, we recall the component-wise, pointwise formula

(6.14)

\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\overline{p_{\delta}})\ =\ \max\{a,\min\{\overline{p_{\delta}},b\}\},

proven in
[
55
, Theorem 2.28]
, where we use the assumption that the boxing functions in
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
are constants. It is now clear that
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\overline{p_{\delta}})
is in
\displaystyle H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
from directly estimating the max-min expression. Moreover,
\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\overline{p_{\delta}})\|_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\|\overline{p_{\delta}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle g_{\delta}
follows from the formula (

).
∎

Remark 6.4
.

An alternative to estimating
\displaystyle\|\overline{g_{\delta}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
directly is to use interpolation theory; see
[
32
, Chapter 16]
and
[
52
, Chapter 25]
. To see this, it suffices to recall that the
\displaystyle H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
space is an intermediate space between
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Having shown that it is possible for the control to lie in a smoother space than
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can proceed with the error analysis. Again, due to the generality of the kernel we are not very explicit in this. Instead, we introduce
\displaystyle\omega:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which
\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}\omega(h)=0
. This is such that, if
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

(6.15)

\|\Pi_{0}w-w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \omega(h),

where
\displaystyle\Pi_{0}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow Z_{h}
denotes the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-projection onto
\displaystyle Z_{h}
. Clearly,
\displaystyle\omega
depends on the spatial dimension
\displaystyle n
, on the embedding number (or Gelfand width) of the embedding
\displaystyle X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and on the properties of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}
. In the setting of Lemma
6.3
a proper rate of approximation can be established.

Lemma 6.5

(Approximation with smoothness)
.

Assume that
\displaystyle k_{\delta}
satisfies (
6.13
) on
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(0)
for some
\displaystyle s\neq\frac{1}{2}
, then

(6.16)

\|\Pi_{0}w-w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ h^{s}\|w\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})},\qquad\forall\ w\in X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

The proof repeats that of the Fractional Poincaré Inequality
[
22
, Lemma 7.1]
in the vector-valued setting, to obtain the estimate

(6.17)

\|\Pi_{0}w-w\|_{L^{2}(T;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ h^{s}[w]_{H^{s}(T;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

for each
\displaystyle T\in\mathscr{T}_{h}
. Since our mesh is quasi-uniform, from (
6.17
) we may deduce, via a localization argument, that

(6.18)

\|\Pi_{0}w-w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \lesssim\ h^{s}[w]_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Then invoke
[
39
, Theorem 1.1]
to estimate
\displaystyle[w]_{H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
in terms of the
\displaystyle X(\Omega_{\delta};\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm.
∎

We can now obtain an error estimate for the control. In the following result the idea is that, once further regularity of the state/adjoint is known (which can be done for more specific kernels), and a bound on
\displaystyle\omega
like the one in Lemma
6.5
is obtained, the right hand side in the estimate below can be bounded by a power of
\displaystyle h
.

Theorem 6.6

(Convergence of controls)
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\overline{g_{\delta}}
is the optimal control associated with Problem
2.2
, and
\displaystyle\overline{g_{\delta,h}}
is the discrete optimal control associated with Problem
2.4

In

the concept of asymptotically compatible schemes for parameter-dependent linear problems was introduced. The goal of asymptotic compatibility is to guarantee that we reach the same local, continuous solution regardless of whether we send
\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle 0

Our main goal in this section is to extend this notion to nonlocal optimal control problems, and to show that our ensuing numerical schemes are indeed asymptotically compatible.

For each
\displaystyle\delta>0

Proposition 7.2

(Asymptotic structural properties)
.

The following hold:

AC1

The family of spaces
\displaystyle\{X_{\delta,h}\}_{\delta>0,h>0}
is asymptotically dense in
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. That is, given a
\displaystyle v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and some sequences
\displaystyle h_{k},\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
, we can find a sequence
\displaystyle v_{k}\in X_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}
such that
\displaystyle v_{k}\rightarrow v
strongly in
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
.

AC2

For any sequences
\displaystyle\{\delta_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=1},\{h_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
with
\displaystyle\delta_{k},h_{k}\rightarrow 0
and the family of solutions
\displaystyle\{(\overline{u_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}},\overline{g_{\delta,h_{k}}})\}
to Problem
2.4
, there exists a
\displaystyle C>0
so that
\displaystyle\|\overline{u_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C
uniformly in
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

AC3

For each
\displaystyle u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle\delta\geq 0
, we have that
\displaystyle A_{\delta}u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

AC4

For any
\displaystyle u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that
\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|A_{\delta}u-A_{0}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ 0
.

Proof.

The fact that finite element spaces of piecewise linear functions are asymptotically dense in
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is well-known, thus verifying
AC1
. For any
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
, the bound stated in
AC2
follows from a standard a priori estimate and the fact that
\displaystyle Z_{\text{ad}}
is bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Finally, the remaining properties are addressed in
[
53
]
; see also
[
35
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

\displaystyle\overline{u_{k}}\rightarrow u_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
\displaystyle\{(\overline{u_{k}},\overline{g_{k}})\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
are pairs satisfying Problem
2.4
, we have for all
\displaystyle v_{k}\in X_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}
that

(7.3)

B_{\delta_{k}}(\overline{u_{k}},v_{k})\ =\ \langle\overline{g_{k}},v_{k}\rangle.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary, and denote by
\displaystyle I_{k}
the Lagrange nodal interpolant with respect to the mesh of size
\displaystyle h_{k}
. If
\displaystyle w_{k}:=I_{k}\varphi\in X_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}
, then
\displaystyle w_{k}\rightarrow\varphi
in
\displaystyle W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
. This convergence is sufficiently strong to ensure

(7.4)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\langle\overline{g_{k}},w_{k}\rangle\ =\ \langle g_{*},\varphi\rangle.

Now, utilizing the definition (
7.1
), we write

(7.5)

\begin{split}B_{\delta_{k}}(\overline{u_{k}},w_{k})\ &=\ \langle A_{\delta_{k}}\overline{u_{k}},w_{k}\rangle_{X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*},X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \\
&=\langle A_{\delta_{k}}\varphi,\overline{u_{k}}\rangle_{X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*},X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&+\langle A_{\delta_{k}}(w_{k}-\varphi),\overline{u_{k}}\rangle_{X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*},X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \\
&=:\ I_{k}+II_{k}.\end{split}

Due to item
AC3
of Proposition
7.2
, necessarily
\displaystyle A_{\delta_{k}}\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and by item
AC4
, we have that
\displaystyle A_{\delta_{k}}\varphi\rightarrow A_{0}\varphi
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Due to this and
\displaystyle\overline{u_{k}}\rightarrow u_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the term
\displaystyle I
behaves as follows:

(7.6)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\langle A_{\delta_{k}}\varphi,\overline{u_{k}}\rangle_{X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*},X_{0}(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ =\ \langle A_{0}\varphi,u_{*}\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

As for
\displaystyle II_{k}
, we may use the definition (
7.2
) and that
\displaystyle\overline{u_{k}}
is the solution to (
2.15
), along with Hölder to deduce

(7.7)

II_{k}\ =\ B_{\delta_{k}}(\overline{u_{k}},w_{k}-\varphi)\ \lesssim\ \|\overline{u_{k}}\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|w_{k}-\varphi\|_{X(\Omega_{\delta_{k}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Due to item
AC2
the first factor is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle k
, whereas the second factor is controlled up to a constant (uniform in
\displaystyle k
) by
\displaystyle\|w_{k}-\varphi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
, due to Lemma
3.2
. This factor is further bounded from above by
\displaystyle\|w_{k}-\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}
, and then the convergence of
\displaystyle w_{k}\rightarrow\varphi
in
\displaystyle W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
tells us that
\displaystyle II_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
. The result is that

(7.8)

B_{0}(u_{*},\varphi)\ =\ \langle g_{*},\varphi\rangle

for all
\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
; by density, we may then extend (
7.8
) to all
\displaystyle\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Repeating the analysis just used for the sequence of states
\displaystyle\{\overline{u_{k}}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
, we identify a
\displaystyle p_{*}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
so that
\displaystyle\overline{p_{k}}\rightarrow p_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

(7.9)

B_{0}(\varphi,p_{*})\ =\ \langle u_{*},\varphi\rangle

for all
\displaystyle\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Now, we link the states, controls and adjoints, beginning as follows: due to (
6.1
), for each
\displaystyle k
we have that

(7.10)

B_{\delta_{k}}(\overline{p_{k}},v_{k})\ =\ \langle\overline{u_{k}},v_{k}\rangle

for all
\displaystyle v_{k}\in X_{\delta_{k},h_{k}}
, and the identity

(7.11)

\overline{g_{k}}(x)\ =\ -\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{k}}(x)).

The next step is to show that
\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{k}}\rightarrow p_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By the Triangle Inequality and the stability of
\displaystyle\Pi_{0}
, we estimate

(7.12)

\|\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{k}}-p_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \|\overline{p_{k}}-p_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\Pi_{0}p_{*}-p_{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Since
\displaystyle\overline{p_{k}}\rightarrow p_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the first term in (
7.12
) decays to
\displaystyle 0
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
, while the second term vanishes due to (
6.15
).

Now, due to the convergence
\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{k}}\rightarrow p_{*}
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the projection mapping being Lipschitz, we have that
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(\Pi_{0}\overline{p_{k}}(x))\rightarrow-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(p_{*}(x))
; this coupled with the weak convergence
\displaystyle\overline{g_{k}}\rightharpoonup g_{*}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
lets us conclude

(7.13)

g_{*}(x)\ =\ -\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{\text{ad}}}(p_{*}(x)).

Since (
7.8
), (
7.9
), and (
7.13
) all hold, and solutions to the local continuous optimality conditions (
6.48
) are necessarily unique, we have that
\displaystyle g_{*}=\overline{g}
;
\displaystyle p_{*}=\overline{p}
; and
\displaystyle u_{*}=\overline{u}
.
Finally, notice that this limit point
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g},\overline{p})\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times Z_{\text{ad}}\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is independent of the original sub-sequence chosen, which means the entire sequence
\displaystyle\{(\overline{u_{k}},\overline{g_{k}},\overline{p_{k}})\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
converges to
\displaystyle(\overline{u},\overline{g},\overline{p})
in the
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}_{\textit{wk}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
topology, completing the proof.
∎
