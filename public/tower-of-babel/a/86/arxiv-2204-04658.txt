Flag matroids with coefficients
Manoel Jarra, Oliver Lorscheid

Abstract
. This paper is a direct generalization of Baker-Bowler theory to flag matroids, including its moduli interpretation as developed by Baker and the second author for matroids. More explicitly, we extend the notion of flag matroids to flag matroids over any tract, provide cryptomorphic descriptions in terms of basis axioms (Grassmann-Plücker functions), circuit/vector axioms and dual pairs, including additional characterizations in the case of perfect tracts. We establish duality of flag matroids and construct minors. Based on the theory of ordered blue schemes, we introduce flag matroid bundles and construct their moduli space, which leads to algebro-geometric descriptions of duality and minors. Taking rational points recovers flag varieties in several geometric contexts: over (topological) fields, in tropical geometry, and as a generalization of the MacPhersonian.

The moduli space

The theory of moduli spaces of matroids from Baker and the second author’s paper
[

]

An

with

that contains

and partial order

Since

is closed under addition, we lose information about tracts

\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
.

Theorem G
generalizes the fact that the
K
-rational points of a flag variety correspond to flags of
K
-linear subspaces for fields
K
and that the points of the flag Dressian correspond to flags of tropical linear spaces; see
[
BEZ21
]
. In fact,
Theorem G
follows from the stronger property that
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
is the fine moduli space of flag matroid bundles; see
Theorem 3.8
for the precise statement.

The previously discussed constructions for flag
F
-matroids extend to geometric constructions in terms of certain canonical morphisms between flag varieties under the identification of flag
F
-matroids with
F
-rational points of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
in
Theorem G
.

Coordinate projection

Let
{\mathbf{i}}=(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{t})
be a sequence of integers with
1\leqslant i_{1}\leqslant\dotsb\leqslant i_{t}\leqslant s
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}=(r_{i_{1}},\dotsc,r_{i_{t}})
. Then there is a canonical morphism
\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},E)
, which maps a flag
F
-matroid
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
to
{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}=(M_{i_{1}},\dotsc,M_{i_{t}})
. This is
section 3.7
.

Duality

Define
{\mathbf{r}}^{\ast}=(r_{s}^{\ast},\dotsc,r_{1}^{\ast})
with
r_{i}^{\ast}=n-r_{i}
. Then there is a canonical morphism
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\ast},E)
, which maps a flag
F
-matroid
{\mathbf{M}}
to its dual
{\mathbf{M}}^{\ast}
(after composition with the duality involution of
F
). This is
Theorem 3.12
.

Minors

Let
e\in E
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{k}=(r_{1}-1,\dotsc,r_{k}-1,r_{k+1},\dotsc,r_{s})
. Then there exist locally closed subschemes
W_{{\mathbf{r}},k,/e}\hookrightarrow\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
and morphisms
\Psi_{{\mathbf{r}},k,/e}:W_{{\mathbf{r}},k,/e}\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}_{k},E)
such that
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(F)=\mycoprod_{k=1}^{s}W_{{\mathbf{r}},k,/e}(F)
and such that
\Psi_{{\mathbf{r}},k,/e}
maps a flag
F
-matroid
{\mathbf{M}}
to its contraction
{\mathbf{M}}/e
. There are analogous morphisms for the deletion
{\mathbf{M}}\backslash e
. This is
Theorem 3.13
.

Flags of minors

Let
n^{\prime}=n+r_{s}-r_{1}
and
E^{\prime}=\{1,\dotsc,n^{\prime}\}
. Let
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}
be the open locus in
\Gr(r_{s},E^{\prime})
of matroids for which
\{n+1,\dotsc,n^{\prime}\}
is independent and co-independent. Then there is a canonical morphism
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
, which sends an
F
-matroid
M^{\prime}
of rank
r_{s}
on
E^{\prime}
to the flag
F
-matroid
(M^{\prime}\backslash I_{1}/J_{1},\dotsc,M^{\prime}\backslash I_{s}/J_{s})
of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
where
J_{i}=\{n+1,\dotsc,r_{s}-r_{i}\}
and
I_{i}=\{r_{s}-r_{i}+1,\dotsc,n^{\prime}\}
. This is
Theorem 3.15
.

As explained in our initial example, every flag matroid is a sequence of minors, i.e.,
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}({\mathbb{K}})\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
is surjective for all
{\mathbf{r}}
and
E
. This is also true for fields
K
: the map
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}(K)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(K)
is surjective since it is
\GL(E,K)
-equivariant and since
\GL(E,K)
acts transitively on
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(K)
. Initially Las Vergnas expected the same for pairs of oriented matroids, but this was later disproven by Richter-Gebert; see
[
Ric93
, Cor. 3.5]
. This makes us wonder:

Problem
.

For which
{\mathbf{r}}
,
E
and
F
is
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}(F)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(F)
surjective?

Topologies on rational point sets

A topology on
F
induces a topology on
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(F)
, which recovers several known instances of flag varieties. If
F=K
is a topological field, then
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(K)
is the flag variety over
K
together with its strong topology; in particular,
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{R}})
is the manifold of flags of real linear subspaces. Endowing
F={\mathbb{T}}
with the real topology identifies
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
with the flag Dressian from
[
BEZ21
]
as a tropical variety. Endowing the sign hyperfield
{\mathbb{S}}
with the topology generated by the open subsets
\{1\}
and
\{\myepsilon\}
yields a generalization of the MacPhersonian to oriented flag matroids. Endowing the Krasner hyperfield
{\mathbb{K}}
with the topology generated by the open subset
\{1\}
endows
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
with a topology, in which a matroid
M
is in the closure of another matroid
N
precisely if all bases of
M
are bases of
N
. A more detailed discussion of topologies on rational point sets can be found in
section 3.11
.

Relation to combinatorial flag varieties and Tits geometries

Borovik, Gelfand and White introduce in
[
BGW01
]
the
combinatorial flag variety
\Omega_{W}
for the symmetric group
W=S_{n}
as the order complex of the collection of all matroids (of arbitrary rank) on
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, endowed with the partial order
M\leqslant N
if and only if
N\twoheadrightarrow M
. Thus
\Omega_{W}
is a chamber complex, and its chambers are indexed by flag matroids on
E
. The maximal chambers have dimension
n-2
and correspond to flag matroids of rank
(1,\dotsc,n-1)
.

The same authors mention at the end of section 7.14 of their book
[
BGW03
]
that:

Many geometries over fields have formal analogues which can be thought of as geometries over the field of
1
element. For example, the projective plane over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
has
q^{2}+q+1
points and the same number of lines; every line in the plane has
q+1
points. When
q=1
, we have a plane with three points and three lines, i.e., a triangle. The flag complex of the triangle is a thin building of type
A_{2}=Sym_{3}
. In general, the Coxeter complex
{\mathcal{W}}
of a Coxeter group
W
is a thin building of type
W
and behaves like the building of type
W
over the field of
1
element.

However, the Coxeter complex has a relatively poor structure. In many aspects,
\Omega_{W}
and
\Omega_{W}^{\ast}
are more suitable candidates for the role of a “universal” combinatorial geometry of type
W
over the field of
1
element.

For the Coxeter group
W=S_{n}
of type
A_{n-1}
, the combinatorial flag variety
\Omega_{W}
resurfaces as the set of
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational points of the
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-schemes
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
(with varying
{\mathbf{r}}
). More precisely, the chambers of
\Omega_{W}
correspond bijectively to

\mycoprod_{{\mathbf{r}}\in\Theta}\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})\quad\text{where}\quad\Theta\ =\ \big\{\,(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})\,\big|\,s>0,\;0<r_{1}<\dotsb<r_{s}<n\,\big\},

and the chamber of a flag matroid
{\mathbf{N}}
is the face of the chamber of a flag matroid
{\mathbf{M}}
if and only if
{\mathbf{N}}=\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}({\mathbf{M}})
for an appropriate coordinate projection
\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}
(see
Theorem D
).

Moreover, Borovik, Gelfand and White observe in
[
BGW01
]
that the Coxeter complex
{\mathcal{W}}
of
W
appears naturally as the subcomplex of
\Omega_{W}
that consists of flags of matroids with exactly one basis, which correspond to the closed points of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
. This links their idea to Tits’ seminal paper
[
Tit57
]
on
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, where Tits introduces
geometries
, which can be thought of as a predecessor of a building over a finite field, and where he muses over the (lack of a)
field of characteristic one
, which could explain the role of the Coxeter complexes
{\mathcal{W}}
.

In so far, our flag varieties
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
, together with the various coordinate projections
\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}
, can be seen as an enrichment of both Tits’ and Borovik, Gelfand and White’s perspectives on
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry.

1.
Baker-Bowler theory

In this section, we review the theory of matroids over a tract, as developed by Baker and Bowler in
[
BB19
]
.

1.1.
Tracts

A
pointed monoid
is a (multiplicatively written) monoid
F
with neutral element
1
and an
absorbing element
0
(or
zero
for short), which is characterized by the property that
0\cdot a=0
for all
a\in F
. The
unit group of
F
is the submonoid
F^{\times}
of all invertible elements of
F
, which is a group.

A
tract
is a commutative pointed monoid
F
with unit group
F^{\times}=F-\{0\}
together with a subset
N_{F}
of the group semiring
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
, called the
nullset of
F
, which satisfies:

(T0)

The zero element of
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
belongs to
N_{F}
.

(T1)

The multiplicative identity of
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
is not in
N_{F}
.

(T2)

There is a unique element
\myepsilon
in
F^{\times}
with
1+\myepsilon\in N_{F}
.

(T3)

N_{F}
is closed under the natural action of
F^{\times}
on
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
.

Note that the axioms imply that
\myepsilon^{2}=1
and that
a+b\in N_{F}
if and only if
b=\myepsilon a
. A
morphism between tracts
F
and
F^{\prime}
is a multiplicative map
f:F\to F^{\prime}
such that
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
and such that
\sum f(a_{i})\in N_{F^{\prime}}
whenever
\sum a_{i}\in N_{F}
.

Example 1.1

(Fields)
.

A field
K
can be considered as the tract whose multiplicative monoid equals that of
K
and whose nullset is
N_{K}=\{\sum a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[K^{\times}]|\;\sum a_{i}=0\text{ in }K\}
. If the context is clear, we denote the tract by the same symbol
K
, and we say that a tract
K
is a field if it is associated with a field.

More generally, partial fields can be considered as tracts, as explained in
[
BL21
, Thm. 2.21]
. The most relevant example for our purposes is the
regular partial field
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, whose appearance as a tract consists of the multiplicative monoid
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{0,1,\myepsilon\}
and the nullset
N_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{n.1+m.\myepsilon\mid n=m\}
.

Example 1.2

(Hyperfields)
.

A
hyperoperation
on a set
S
is a map
S\times S\rightarrow 2^{S}
. A
hyperfield
is a generalization of a field whose addition is replaced by a hyperoperation
(a,b)\mapsto a{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}b
, which satisfies analogous properties to the addition of a field. We consider a hyperfield
K
as the tract
(K^{\times},N_{K})
with nullset
N_{K}=\big\{\sum a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[K^{\times}]\,\big|\,0\in\hypersum a_{i}\big\}
.

Some particular examples are the following:

•

The
Krasner hyperfield
is the tract
{\mathbb{K}}=\{0,1\}
with nullset
N_{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{N}}-\{1\}
and
\myepsilon=1
. Its hyperaddition is given by
0{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}a=\{a\}
for
a=0,1
and
1{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}1=\{0,1\}
.

•

The
tropical hyperfield
is the tract
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
with nullset

\textstyle N_{\mathbb{T}}\ =\ \big\{\sum a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}]\,\big|\,\text{the maximum appears twice in }\{a_{i}\}\big\}

and
\myepsilon=1
. Its hyperaddition is given by
a{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}b=\big\{\max\{a,b\}\big\}
for
a\neq b
and
a{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}a=[0,a]
.

•

The
sign hyperfield
is the tract
{\mathbb{S}}=\{0,1,-1\}
with nullset

N_{\mathbb{S}}\ =\ \big\{n.1+m.(-1)\,\big|\,n=m=0\text{ or }n\neq 0\neq m\big\}

and
\myepsilon=-1
. Its hyperaddition satisfies
a{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}a=\{a\}
and
a{\,\raisebox{-1.1pt}{\larger[-0]{$\boxplus$}}\,}(-a)=\{0,1,-1\}
for
a=1,-1
.

Note that
{\mathbb{K}}
is a terminal object in the category of tracts: for every tract
F
, the
terminal map

t_{F}:F\to{\mathbb{K}}
that maps
F^{\times}
to
1
is the unique tract morphism from
F
to
{\mathbb{K}}
.

1.2.
Matroids over tracts

Let
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
0\leqslant r\leqslant n
. A
Grassmann–Plücker function of rank
r
on
E
with coefficients in
F
is a function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\rightarrow F
such that:

(GP1)

\myvarphi
is not identically zero;

(GP2)

\myvarphi
is alternating, i.e.,
\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{i},\dotsc,x_{j},\dotsc,x_{r})=\myepsilon\cdot\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{j},\dotsc,x_{i},\dotsc,x_{r})
and
\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})=0
if
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
;

(GP3)

for all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r+1}\in E
, we have

\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+1}{\sum}}\ \myepsilon^{k}\cdot\myvarphi(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r+1})\cdot\myvarphi(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\quad\in\quad N_{F}.

The relations in
(GP3)
are called the
Plücker relations
.

We say that two Grassmann–Plücker functions
\myvarphi_{1}
and
\myvarphi_{2}
are
equivalent
if
\myvarphi_{1}=a\cdot\myvarphi_{2}
for some
a\in F^{\times}
, and define an
F
-matroid (of rank
r
on
E
)
as the equivalence class
M_{\myvarphi}=[\myvarphi]
of a Grassmann–Plücker function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F
.

Remark 1.3
.

These are, in fact, the definition of
strong
Grassmann-Plücker functions and
strong

F
-matroids in
[
BB19
]
. Though weak matroids are important to understand the representations of matroids over fields and other tracts, strong matroids are more suitable to study “cryptomorphic” properties (as in
[
And19
]
) and “algebro-geometric” properties (as in
[
BL21
]
). We will not encounter weak matroids in this text and omit the attribute “strong.”

1.3.
Push-forwards and the underlying matroid

Let
f:F\rightarrow F^{\prime}
be a morphism of tracts and
\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F
a Grassmann-Plücker function. Then
f\circ\myvarphi:E^{r}\rightarrow F^{\prime}
is also a Grassmann-Plücker function. We define the
push-forward of
M_{\myvarphi}
along
f
as
f_{\ast}M_{\myvarphi}=M_{f\circ\myvarphi}
.

Let
M
be an
F
-matroid. The
underlying matroid of
M
is defined as the classical matroid
\underline{M}
whose set of bases is

\mathcal{B}(\underline{M})=\big\{\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\}\subseteq E\,\big|\,\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})\neq 0\big\}.

For a tract morphism
f:F\to F^{\prime}
and an
F
-matroid
M
, we have
\underline{f_{\ast}M}=\underline{M}
. The map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\big\{\text{${\mathbb{K}}$-matroids}\big\}&\longrightarrow&\big\{\text{usual matroids}\big\}\\
M&\longmapsto&\underline{M}\end{array}

is a bijection that identifies classical matroids with
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids.

1.4.
Circuits

For a tuple
X=(X_{i})_{i\in E}
of
F^{E}
, we define the
support of
X
as the set
\underline{X}:=\{i\in E\mid X_{i}\neq 0\}
, and for a subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq F^{E}
, we define the
support of
\mathcal{X}
as
\textup{supp}(\mathcal{X}):=\{\underline{X}\mid X\in\mathcal{X}\}
.

We define the set of
F
-circuits of
M
as follows. Let
\mathcal{C}(\underline{M})
be the set of circuits of
\underline{M}
. For each
C\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{M})
, fix a
y_{0}\in C
and a basis
\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r}\}
of
\underline{M}
containing
C-y_{0}
. We define
X_{C}\in F^{E}
by

\displaystyle X_{C}(y):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\myepsilon^{i}\cdot\myvarphi(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{i}},\dotsc,y_{r})&\text{if }y=y_{i}\text{ for some $i$,}\\
0&\text{if }y\notin\{y_{0},\dotsc,y_{r}\}.\end{cases}

The set of
F
-circuits of
M
is given by
\mathcal{C}(M):=\{a\cdot X_{C}|\;a\in F^{\times},\,C\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{M})\}
. It does not depend on the choice of elements
y_{0}\in C
and bases
\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r}\}
containing
C-y_{0}
. Note that
\textup{supp}\big(\mathcal{C}(M)\big)=\mathcal{C}(\underline{M})
and that
\mathcal{C}(M)
satisfies the following three properties:

(C0)

0\notin\mathcal{C}(M)
.

(C1)

If
X\in\mathcal{C}(M)
and
a\in F^{\times}
, then
a\cdot X\in\mathcal{C}(M)
.

(C2)

If
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}(M)
and
\underline{X}\subseteq\underline{Y}
, then there exists
a\in F^{\times}
such that
X=a\cdot Y
.

Remark 1.4
.

The
F
-circuits satisfy an elimination property, which characterizes together with
(C0)
–
(C2)
the subsets of
F^{E}
that are sets of
F
-circuits of an
F
-matroid
M
. Moreover,
M
is determined by
{\mathcal{C}}(M)
, which yields a cryptomorphic description of
F
-matroids in terms of
F
-circuits (see
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.17]
). We forgo to spell out the elimination axiom, however, since it is somewhat involved and since we do not use it in this text.

1.5.
Duality

An
involution of
F
is a tract morphism
\mytau:F\to F
such that
\mytau^{2}
is the identity on
F
. In the following, we fix an involution
\mytau
and write
\overline{x}=\mytau(x)
.

Fix a total order for
E=\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}
and let
\mysigma
be the unique permutation such that
x_{\mysigma(1)}<\dotsb<x_{\mysigma(n)}
. We consider
\sign(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})=\sign(\mysigma)\in\{\pm 1\}
as an element of
F
by identifying
-1
with
\myepsilon
.

The
dual of
M
is the
F
-matroid
M^{*}=M_{\myvarphi^{*}}
, where
\myvarphi^{*}
is the Grassmann-Plücker function
\myvarphi^{\ast}:E^{n-r}\to F
that is determined by

\myvarphi^{\ast}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n-r})\ =\ \sign(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n-r},x^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,x^{\prime}_{r})\cdot\overline{\myvarphi(x^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,x^{\prime}_{r})}

whenever
E=\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n-r},x^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,x^{\prime}_{r}\}
. The dual of
M
satisfies
M^{**}=M
, and the underlying matroid of
M^{\ast}
is the dual of
\underline{M}
.

1.6.
Orthogonality

Let
X,Y\in F^{E}
. The
inner product of
X
and
Y
is

X\cdot Y\ =\ \sum_{i\in E}X_{i}\cdot\overline{Y_{i}},

considered as an element of
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
. We say that
X
and
Y
are
orthogonal
, and write
X\perp Y
, if
X\cdot Y\in N_{F}
. We say that two subsets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
of
F^{E}
are
orthogonal
, and write
\mathcal{X}\perp\mathcal{Y}
, if
X\perp Y
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

The circuits of
M^{*}
are called the
cocircuits of
M
, and we write
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)=\mathcal{C}(M^{*})
. Circuits and cocircuits are orthogonal:
\mathcal{C}(M)\perp\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
.

We denote by
{\mathcal{X}}^{\perp}=\{X\in F^{E}\mid X\perp{\mathcal{X}}\}
the orthogonal complement of a subset
{\mathcal{X}}
of
F^{E}
. The set
\mathcal{V}(M)=\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)^{\perp}
is called the set of
F
-vectors of
M
, and
\mathcal{V^{*}}(M)=\mathcal{C}(M)^{\perp}
the set of
F
-covectors of
M
. There is a cryptomorphic description of
F
-matroids in terms of their vectors, as explained in
[
And19
]
. Note that, as
\mathcal{C}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(M)
, we have
\mathcal{V}(M)^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{*}(M)
.

Definition 1.5
.

A tract
F
is
perfect
if
\mathcal{V}(M)\perp\mathcal{V}^{*}(M)
for every
F
-matroid
M
.

Note that all fields and partial fields are perfect, and so are the hyperfields
{\mathbb{K}}
,
{\mathbb{S}}
and
{\mathbb{T}}
.

1.7.
Dual pairs

Let
N
be a (classical) matroid on
E
. We call a subset
\mathcal{C}
of
F^{E}
an
F
-signature
of
N
if
\textup{supp}(\mathcal{C})
is the set of circuits of
N
and
\mathcal{C}
satisfies
(C0)
–
(C2)
.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be subsets of
F^{E}
. We say that
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})
is a
dual pair of
F
-signatures of
N
if:

(DP1)

\mathcal{C}
is an
F
-signature of
N
;

(DP2)

\mathcal{D}
is an
F
-signature of
N^{*}
;

(DP3)

\mathcal{C}\perp\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 1.7

(
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.26]
)
.

There is a bijection between
F
-matroids
M
with underlying matroid
\underline{M}=N
and dual pairs of
F
-signatures of
N
, given by

M\longmapsto(\mathcal{C}(M),\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)).

1.8.
Minors

Let
\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F
be a Grassmann–Plücker function with associated matroid
M=M_{\myvarphi}
and
A\subset E
.

(1)

(Contraction) Let
\ell
be the rank of
A
in
\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}
, and let
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dotsc,a_{\ell}\}
be a maximal
\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}
-independent subset of
A
. Define
\myvarphi/A:(E\backslash A)^{r-\ell}\rightarrow F
by

(\myvarphi/A)(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-\ell}):=\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-\ell},a_{1},\dotsc,a_{\ell}).

The
contraction of
M
by
A
is
M/A=M_{\myvarphi/A}
.

(2)

(Deletion) Let
k
be the rank of
E\backslash A
in
\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}
, and choose
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{r-k}\in A
such that
\{a_{1},\dotsc,a_{r-k}\}
is a basis of
\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}/(E\backslash A)
. Define
\myvarphi\backslash A:(E\backslash A)^{k}\rightarrow F
by

(\myvarphi\backslash A)(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}):=\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k},a_{1},\dotsc,a_{r-k}).

The
deletion of
A
from
M
is
M\backslash A=M_{\myvarphi\backslash A}
.

Contractions and deletions are well-defined due to the following fact.

Lemma 1.8

(
[
BB19
, Lemma 4.4]
)
.

(1)

Both
\myvarphi/A
and
\myvarphi\backslash A
are Grassmann–Plücker functions. Their definitions are independent of all choices up to global multiplication by an element of
F^{\times}
.

(2)

\underline{M}_{\myvarphi/A}=\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}/A
and
\underline{M}_{\myvarphi\backslash A}=\underline{M}_{\myvarphi}\backslash A
.

(3)

(\myvarphi\backslash A)^{*}=\myvarphi^{*}/A
and
(\myvarphi/A)^{*}=\myvarphi^{*}\backslash A
.

2.
Flag matroids

2.1.
Definitions

Let us introduce the central notion of this text: flag matroids with coefficients in tracts. Throughout the whole section, we fix
E=\{1,\dots,n\}
and integers
r
and
w
between
0
and
n
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M
and
N
be
F
-matroids of respective ranks
r
and
w
on
E
. We say that
M
is a
quotient
of
N
, and write
N\twoheadrightarrow M
, if every choice of Grassmann-Plücker functions
\mymu
and
\mynu
representing
M
and
N
, respectively, satisfies the
Plücker flag relations

(1)

\underset{k=1}{\overset{w+1}{\sum}}\ \myepsilon^{k}\;\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc y_{w+1})\cdot\mymu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\ \in\ N_{F}

for all
y_{1},\dotsc,y_{w+1},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\in E
.

A
flag
F
-matroid
on
E
is a sequence
\textbf{M}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
of
F
-matroids such that
M_{j}\twoheadrightarrow M_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant s
. The sequence
\rk({\mathbf{M}})=\big(\rk(\underline{M_{1}}),\dotsc,\rk(\underline{M_{s}})\big)
is called the
rank of
{\mathbf{M}}
.

The identification of classical matroids with
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids yields an identification of classical flag matroids with flag
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids. The proof of this fact relies, however, on the circuit-vector characterization of flag matroids. We postpone this discussion to
section 2.4
.

Remark 2.2
.

The following are some immediate observations.

(1)

Since two Grassmann-Plücker functions representing the same
F
-matroid only differ by a non-zero factor, the validity of equation (
1
) does not depend on the choice of Grassmann-Plücker functions.

(2)

Note that for
N=M
, the Plücker flag relations in (
1
) are nothing else than the usual Plücker relations of a Grassmann-Plücker function, see
(GP3)
. Thus one always has
M\twoheadrightarrow M
.

(3)

If
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},...,M_{s})
is a flag
F
-matroid, then
(M_{1},...,\widehat{M_{i}},...,M_{s})
is also a flag
F
-matroid, for all
i
in
[s]
.

2.2.
Functoriality

As for single matroids, we can change the coefficients of flag matroids along tract morphisms.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f:F\to F^{\prime}
be a tract morphism and
N\twoheadrightarrow M
a quotient of
F
-matroids. Then
f_{\ast}N\twoheadrightarrow f_{\ast}M
. Consequently, if
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
is a flag
F
-matroid, then
f_{\ast}{\mathbf{M}}=(f_{\ast}M_{1},\dotsc,f_{\ast}M_{s})
is a flag
F^{\prime}
-matroid.

Proof.

Since the Plücker flag relations for
f_{\ast}N\twoheadrightarrow f_{\ast}M
are indexed by the same tuples of elements
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\rk(M)-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\rk(N)+1}\in E
and are of the same shape, the first assertion follows at once from the definition of a morphism of tracts. The second assertion follows at once from the first and the definition of a flag
F
-matroid.
∎

2.3.
Cryptomorphism

The core result of our theory consists of the following cryptomorphic description of flag
F
-matroids in terms of their cocircuits and covectors.

Theorem 2.4

(Cryptomorphism for flag
F
-matroids)
.

Let
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
be a sequence of
F
-matroids on
E
with respective cocircuit sets
{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}(M_{i})
and covector sets
{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(M_{i})
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
is a flag
F
-matroid;

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{*}(M_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{*}(M_{j})
for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant s
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the following assertions are equivalent for two
F
-matroids
M
and
N
on
E
:

(1)

M
is a quotient of
N
;

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{*}(N)
.

As a first step, we show that (
1
) implies (
2
). Assume that
M
is a quotient of
N
. Fix Grassmann-Plücker functions
\mymu
and
\mynu
that represent
M
and
N
, respectively. Let
Z\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)=\mathcal{C}(M^{*})
. By
[
BB19
, p. 841]
, there exists a
z_{0}\in\underline{Z}
, an
\myalpha\in F^{\times}
and a basis
D=\{z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n-r}\}
of
\underline{M}^{*}
containing
\underline{Z}-z_{0}
such that

Z(z_{i})=\myalpha\cdot\myepsilon^{i}\cdot\mymu^{*}(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{i}}\dotsc,z_{n-r}).

Let
\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}=E\backslash\{z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r}\}
. Similarly, for
Y\in\mathcal{C}(N)
, there exist an
y_{0}\in\underline{Y}
, a
\mylambda\in F^{\times}
and a basis
B=\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{w}\}
of
\underline{N}
containing
\underline{Y}-y_{0}
such that

Y(y_{j})=\mylambda\cdot\myepsilon^{j}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}}\dotsc,y_{w}).

Let
S=(D\cup\{z_{0}\})\cap(B\cup\{y_{0}\})
and

I\ =\ \big\{i\in\{0,\dotsc,n-r\}\,\big|\,z_{i}\in S\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J\ =\ \big\{j\in\{0,\dotsc,w\}\,\big|\,y_{j}\in S\big\}.

There exists a bijection
\mathfrak{b}:J\rightarrow I
such that
y_{j}=z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}
, for all
j\in J
. Note that

\displaystyle(\mylambda\overline{\myalpha})^{-1}\cdot\big(Y\cdot Z\big)=(\mylambda\overline{\myalpha})^{-1}\cdot\underset{e\in E}{\sum}Y(e)\cdot\overline{Z(e)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mylambda\overline{\myalpha})^{-1}\cdot\underset{e\in S}{\sum}\ Y(e)\cdot\overline{Z(e)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mylambda\overline{\myalpha})^{-1}\cdot\underset{j\in J}{\sum}\big(\mylambda\cdot\myepsilon^{j}\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}},\dotsc,y_{w})\big)\cdot\big(\overline{\myalpha\cdot\myepsilon^{\mathfrak{b}(j)}\mymu^{*}(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}}\dotsc,z_{n-r})}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j\in J}{\sum}\ \myepsilon^{\big(j+\mathfrak{b}(j)\big)}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}}\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}},\dotsc,z_{n-r},z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})

\displaystyle\cdot\mymu(z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j\in J}{\sum}\ \myepsilon^{\big(j+\mathfrak{b}(j)\big)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}},\dotsc,z_{n-r},z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}}\dotsc,y_{w})

\displaystyle\cdot\mymu(y_{j},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}),

because
\mymu(y_{j},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})=0
for
j\in\{0,\dotsc,s\}\backslash J
, since in this case
y_{j}\in\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}
. Further we have for all
j\in J
that

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(n-r)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{2(n-r)-\mathfrak{b}(j)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}},\dotsc,z_{n-r},z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{\mathfrak{b}(j)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}},\dotsc,z_{n-r},z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}).

Thus

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(n-r)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot(\mylambda\overline{\myalpha})^{-1}\cdot\big(Y\cdot Z\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j\in J}{\sum}\ \myepsilon^{j}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}}\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\mymu(y_{j},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j=0}{\overset{w}{\sum}}\ \myepsilon^{j}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}}\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\mymu(y_{j},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}).

This implies that
Y\cdot Z\in N_{F}
. Therefore
Z\in\mathcal{V}^{*}(N)
, which shows that (
1
) implies (
2
).

Next we show that (
2
) implies (
1
). Assume that
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
is a subset of
\mathcal{V}^{*}(N)
and let
\{y_{0},\dotsc,y_{w}\}
be an
(w+1)
-subset and
\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}
be an
(r-1)
-subset of
E
.

Case 1.
If there is no
\ell
in
\{0,\dotsc,w\}
such that
\{y_{\ell},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}
is a basis of
\underline{M}
and
\{y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},

\dotsc,y_{w}\}
is a basis of
\underline{N}
, one has that
\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}},\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\mymu(y_{j},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})=0
for all
j\in\{0,\dotsc,w\}
. Thus

\underset{k=0}{\overset{w}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\mymu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\ =\ 0\ \in\ N_{F}.

Case 2.
If there is an
\ell
in
\{0,\dotsc,w\}
such that
\{y_{\ell},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}
is a basis of
\underline{M}
and
\{y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},\dotsc,y_{w}\}
is a basis of
\underline{N}
, then
\{z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n-r}\}=E-\{y_{\ell},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1}\}
is a basis of
\underline{M^{*}}
. Define
z_{0}:=y_{\ell}
. Then

H(z)\ =\ \begin{cases}\myepsilon^{i}\cdot\mymu^{*}(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{i}},\dotsc,z_{n-r})&\text{if}\ z=z_{i}\text{ for some}\ i\in\{0,\dotsc,n-r\},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\

defines a circuit of
M^{*}
and

G(y)\ =\ \begin{cases}\myepsilon^{j}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}},\dotsc,y_{w})&\text{if}\ y=y_{j}\text{ for some}\ j\in\{0,\dotsc,w\},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

defines a circuit of
N
.

Note that

H(z_{i})=\myepsilon^{i}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{i}},\dotsc,z_{n-r},z_{i},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\overline{\mymu(z_{i},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})},

for all
i\in\{0,\dotsc,n-r\}
. Let
S=\{z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r}\}\cap\{y_{0},\dotsc,y_{w}\}
and

I\ =\ \big\{i\in\{0,\dotsc,n-r\}\,\big|\,z_{i}\in S\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J\ =\ \big\{j\in\{0,\dotsc,w\}\,\big|\,y_{j}\in S\big\}.

There exists a bijection
\mathfrak{b}:J\rightarrow I
such that
y_{j}=z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)}
, for all
j\in J
. Since
G\cdot H
is in
N_{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{k=0}{\overset{w}{\sum}}\ \myepsilon^{k}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\overline{\mymu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j\in J}{\sum}\ \myepsilon^{j}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{j}},\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\overline{\mymu(z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(n-r)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\underset{j\in J}{\sum}G(y_{j})\cdot\overline{H(z_{\mathfrak{b}(j)})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(n-r)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\underset{e\in E}{\sum}G(e)\cdot\overline{H(e)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(n-r)}\cdot\sign(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{n-r},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot G\cdot H\ \in\ N_{F},

which concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem 2.4
shows that our definition of quotients extends the concept of quotients of oriented matroids; see
[
BLVS+99
, Def. 7.7.2]
.

The characterization (
2
) of flag matroids can be seen, in fact, as an expansion of the concept of dual pairs of
F
-signatures since
{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}(M_{i})\subset{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(M_{j})
if and only if
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M_{i})\perp\mathcal{C}(M_{j})
. This latter form of condition (
2
) exhibits at once the duality property of flag matroids; see
section 2.5
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
F
be a tract and
M
and
N
two
F
-matroids such that
N\twoheadrightarrow M
. Then
\rk({N})=\rk({M})
implies
N=M
.

Proof.

One has
\underline{N}\twoheadrightarrow\underline{M}
by
section 2.4
and
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{*}(N)
by
Theorem 2.4
. As
\rk(\underline{N})=\rk(\underline{M})
, one also has
\underline{N}=\underline{M}
(see
[
Kun86
, Prop. 8.1.6 and Lemma 8.1.7]
). As the (co)circuit set characterizes the
F
-matroid (see
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.17]
), everything follows if we can show that
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)=\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
.

Let
Z\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
. Then
Z\in\mathcal{V}^{*}(N)
and
\underline{Z}\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(\underline{M})=\mathcal{C}^{*}(\underline{N})=\textup{supp}\big(\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)\big)
. As
\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
is equal to the set of nonzero covectors of minimal support by
[
And19
, Thm. 2.18]
, one has that
Z\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
. Therefore
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
. Then there exists an
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
such that
\underline{X}=\underline{Y}
. As
\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
satisfies
(C2)
, there is an
\myalpha\in F^{\times}
such that
X=\myalpha Y
. As
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
satisfies
(C1)
,
X\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)
. This shows that
\mathcal{C}^{*}(M)=\mathcal{C}^{*}(N)
, which implies
M=N
.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Exterior algebra description of flag matroids)
.

The identification of
F
-matroids with classes of exterior
F
-algebras from the first author’s paper
[
Jar23
]
leads to yet another description of flag matroids. To explain, the exterior algebra
\Lambda F^{E}
is an
F
-module that generalizes exterior algebras of vector spaces and the Giansiracusa exterior algebra from
[
GG18
]
to all idylls
F
, which are tracts with additively closed nullset
N_{F}
; we refer the reader to
[
Jar23
]
for details.

A Grassmann-Plücker function
\mymu:E^{r}\to F
determines an element
\mynu\in\Lambda^{r}F^{E}
with coordinates
\mynu_{\mathbf{I}}=\mymu({\mathbf{I}})
for
{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r}
. This association yields a bijection between
F
-matroids of rank
r
on
E
and
F^{\times}
-classes
[\mynu]
of elements
\mynu\in\Lambda^{r}F
that satisfy the Plücker relations.

Thus a flag
F
-matroid of rank
(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
on
E
corresponds to a tuple
\big([\mynu_{1}],\dotsc,[\mynu_{s}]\big)
of
F^{\times}
-classes
[\mynu_{i}]
of elements
\mynu_{i}\in\Lambda^{r_{i}}F^{E}
that satisfy the Plücker flag relations

0\ \leqslant\ \sum_{k=1}^{r_{j}+1}\myepsilon^{k}\;\mynu_{i,(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}-1})}\mynu_{j,(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r_{j}+1})}

in

for all

and
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r_{j}+1}\in E
.

2.4.
Flag matroids as flag
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids

The realization of matroids as
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids extends to flag matroids as explained in the following.

Let us recall the notion of a flag matroid from
[
BGVW97
]
. Given two matroids
M
and
N
on the same ground set
E
, we say that
M
is a quotient of
N
and write
N\twoheadrightarrow M
if the identity on
E
is a strong map from
N
to
M
, i.e., every flat of
M
is a flat of
N
or, equivalently, every cocircuit of
M
is a union of cocircuits of
N
; see
[
Kun86
, Prop. 8.1.6]
for details. A
flag matroid
is a sequence
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
of matroids such that
M_{i}
is a quotient of
M_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dotsc,s-1
.

Proposition 2.8

(Classical flag matroids)
.

Let
M
and
N
be
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids on
E
. Then
M
is a quotient of
N
if and only if
\underline{M}
is a quotient of
\underline{N}
. In consequence, a sequence
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
of
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids is a flag
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid if and only if
(\underline{M_{1}},\dotsc,\underline{M_{s}})
is a flag matroid.

Proof.

Baker-Bowler theory provides a bijection between
{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}(N)
and the cocircuit set of
\underline{N}
, which sends a cocircuit
C:E\to{\mathbb{K}}
of
N
to its support
\underline{C}
. By
[
And19
, Prop. 5.2]
, this association extends to a bijection between
{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(N)
and unions of cocircuits of
N
. Therefore
\underline{M}
is a quotient of
\underline{N}
, i.e., every cocircuit of
M
is a union of cocircuits of
N
, if and only if
{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}(M)\subset{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(N)
. By
Theorem 2.4
, the latter property is equivalent to
M
being a quotient of
N
, which establishes the first claim of the proposition.

The second claim follows from the analogous definitions of flag
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids and flag matroids, taking into account that strong maps of classical matroids are composable.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

An alternative proof of
section 2.4
is as follows. It is known that
\underline{N}
is a quotient of
\underline{M}
if and only if for every basis
B_{N}
of
\underline{N}
, for every basis
B_{M}
of
\underline{M}
and for every

there is an

such that
B_{N}-e+f
is a basis of
\underline{N}
and

is a basis of
\underline{M}
; cf.
[

]
as well as
[

,
Bou87
,
Bou89
]
. This latter condition is directly equivalent to the Plücker flag relations for

.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
F
be a tract and
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
a flag
F
-matroid. Then
\rk({\mathbf{M}})
is a non-decreasing sequence of natural numbers.

Proof.

Since the rank of an
F
-matroid
M
is equal to the rank of its underlying matroid
\underline{M}
, we need only to show that
N\twoheadrightarrow M
implies
\rk(\underline{N})\geqslant\rk(\underline{M})
. Let
t_{F}:F\to{\mathbb{K}}
be the terminal map. By
section 2.2
, we have
t_{F,\ast}N\twoheadrightarrow t_{F,\ast}M
, and by
section 2.4
, we have
\underline{N}=\underline{t_{F,\ast}N}\twoheadrightarrow\underline{t_{F,\ast}M}=\underline{M}
. By
[
Kun86
, Lemma 8.1.7]
, we have
\rk(\underline{N})\geqslant\rk(\underline{M})
, as desired.
∎

2.5.
Duality

Thanks to the cryptomorphism from
Theorem 2.4
, many standard properties of matroids fall into their places, the first one being duality.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
M
and
N

y_{1},\dotsc,y_{w+1}\in E
there is an
i\in\{1,\dotsc,w+1\}
such that equation (
2
) holds if and only if the maximum of

\big\{\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{i}},\dotsc,y_{w+1})\cdot\mymu(y_{i},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})\,\big|\,i=1,\dotsc,w+1\big\}

is attained at least twice. By the definition of
{\mathbb{T}}
, this happens if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{w+1}\ \myiota\circ\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{i}},\dotsc,y_{w+1})\cdot\myiota\circ\mymu(y_{i},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})\ \ \in\ \ N_{\mathbb{T}}.

Since
\myepsilon=1
in
{\mathbb{T}}
, this is precisely the condition for
\widetilde{M}\twoheadrightarrow\widetilde{N}
if we vary through all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{w+1}\in E
, and thus the result follows.
∎

Remark 2.22
.

Since
{\mathbb{T}}
is perfect (see
[
BB19
, Cor. 3.45]
) and the covector set
{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(M)
of a valuated matroid
M
is a tropical linear space,
Theorem 2.17
identifies flag
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroids
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
with flags
{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(M_{1})\subset\dotsb\subset{\mathcal{V}}^{\ast}(M_{s})
of tropical linear subspaces in
{\mathbb{T}}^{E}
. This recovers
[

, Thm. 4.3.1]
. The Plücker flag relations also show at once that a flag
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroid is the same thing as a point of the flag Dressian

, which recovers
[

section 3.11.2
.

2.9.
Flags of minors

As explained in the first example of the introduction, certain sequences of minors of a matroid
M^{\prime}
on
E^{\prime}
are flag matroids, see
[
Kun77
]
. This generalizes verbatim to flag
F
-matroids over an arbitrary tract
F
.

Theorem 2.23
.

Let
F
be a tract,
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
M^{\prime}
an
F
-matroid on
E^{\prime}=E\sqcup\{n+1,\dotsc,n+p\}
and fix integers
0\leqslant n_{s}\leqslant\dotsc\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant p
. For
i=1,\dotsc,s
, we define
M_{i}=M^{\prime}\backslash I_{i}/J_{i}
where
J_{i}=\{n+1,\dotsc,n+n_{i}\}
and
I_{i}=\{n+(n_{i}+1),\dotsc,n+p\}
. Then
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
is a flag
F
-matroid on
E
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that if
a
and
b
are integers such that
0\leqslant b<a\leqslant p
, then
M^{\prime}\backslash I_{b}/J_{b}\twoheadrightarrow M^{\prime}\backslash I_{a}/J_{a}
, where
J_{c}:=\{n+1,\dotsc,n+c\}
and
I_{c}:=\{n+(c+1),\dotsc,n+p\}
for
c\in\{a,b\}
.

Let
\myvarphi
be a Grassmann-Plücker function such that
M^{\prime}=M_{\myvarphi}
. Let
r:=\rk(M^{\prime})
and
r_{c}:=\rk(M^{\prime}\backslash I_{c}/J_{c})
for
c\in\{a,b\}
. We aim to find suitable sets
\{q_{1},\dotsc,q_{r-r_{b}}\}
and
\{u_{1},\dotsc,u_{r-r_{a}}\}
such that

\myvarphi\backslash I_{b}/J_{b}(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{r_{b}})=\myvarphi(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{r_{b}},q_{1},\dotsc,q_{r-r_{b}})

and

\myvarphi\backslash I_{a}/J_{a}(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{r_{a}})=\myvarphi(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{r_{a}},u_{1},\dotsc,u_{r-r_{a}}).

Let
Y\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}|J_{b})
. Then there are
X\subseteq E
,
V\subseteq J_{a}\backslash J_{b}
and
Z\subseteq I_{a}
such that
Y\sqcup X\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}|E\sqcup J_{b})
,
Y\sqcup X\sqcup V\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}|E\sqcup J_{a})
and
Y\sqcup X\sqcup V\sqcup Z\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime})
. This means that
Z\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}/E\sqcup J_{a})
,
Z\sqcup V\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}/E\sqcup J_{b})
and
Y\sqcup V\in\mathcal{I}(M^{\prime}|J_{a})
. Thus there is
Y^{+}\subseteq J_{a}\backslash(Y\sqcup V)
such that
Y\sqcup Y^{+}\sqcup V\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}|J_{a})
. Let
Y=\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell}\}
,
Y^{+}=\{y_{\ell+1},\dotsc,y_{t}\}
,
V=\{v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m}\}
and
Z=\{z_{1},\dotsc,z_{w}\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\myvarphi\backslash I_{b}/J_{b}(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{r_{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myvarphi\backslash I_{b}(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{r_{b}},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myvarphi(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{r_{b}},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell},z_{1},\dotsc,z_{w},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})

and

\displaystyle\myvarphi\backslash I_{a}/J_{a}(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{r_{a}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myvarphi\backslash I_{a}(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{r_{a}},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1},\dotsc,y_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myvarphi(g_{1},\dotsc,g_{r_{a}},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1},\dotsc,y_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m},z_{1},\dotsc,z_{w}).

To finish the proof, we only need to show that
\myvarphi\backslash I_{b}/J_{b}
and
\myvarphi\backslash I_{a}/J_{a}
satisfy the Plücker flag relations (
1
). Let
\{w_{1},\dotsc,w_{r_{b}+1}\}
and
\{f_{1},\dotsc,f_{r_{a}-1}\}
be subsets of
E
. Define the following two sequences

\displaystyle(w_{r_{b}+2},\dotsc,w_{r+1})=(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell},z_{1},\dotsc,z_{w},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})

\displaystyle\text{and }

\displaystyle(f_{r_{a}},\dotsc,f_{r-1})=(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1},\dotsc,y_{t},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m},z_{1},\dotsc,z_{w}).

Then

\displaystyle\underset{k=1}{\overset{r_{b}+1}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\myvarphi\backslash I_{b}/J_{b}(w_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{w_{k}},\dotsc w_{r_{b}+1})\cdot\myvarphi\backslash I_{a}/J_{a}(w_{k},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{r_{a}-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{k=1}{\overset{r_{b}+1}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\myvarphi(w_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{w_{k}},\dotsc w_{r+1})\cdot\myvarphi(w_{k},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{r-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+1}{\sum}}\,\myepsilon^{k}\myvarphi(w_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{w_{k}},\dotsc w_{r+1})\cdot\myvarphi(w_{k},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{r-1}),

as the terms for
k=r_{b}+2,\dotsc,r+1
are zero, because
\{w_{r_{b}+2},\dotsc,w_{r+1}\}\subseteq\{f_{r_{a}},\dotsc,f_{r-1}\}
. As
\myvarphi
is a Grassmann-Plücker function, the last sum is in
N_{F}
.
∎

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
F
be a perfect tract and
{\mathbf{M}}
a flag
F
-matroid of rank
(r,r+1)
. Let
\widehat{E}=E\sqcup\{e\}
. Then there is an
F
-matroid
\widehat{M}
of rank
r+1
on
\widehat{E}
such that
{\mathbf{M}}=(\widehat{M}/e,\,\widehat{M}\backslash e)
. More precisely, the set of
F
-matroids
\widehat{M}
with
{\mathbf{M}}=(\widehat{M}/e,\,\widehat{M}\backslash e)
stays in bijection with
F^{\times}
.

Proof.

Let
\mymu:E^{r}\rightarrow F
and
\mynu:E^{r+1}\rightarrow F
be Grassmann-Plücker functions such that
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{\mymu},M_{\mynu})
. We define the function
\myvarphi:\widehat{E}^{r+1}\to F
by

\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1})\ =\ \begin{cases}\mynu(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1})&\text{if }e\notin\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1}\};\\
\myepsilon^{r+1-\ell}\mymu(x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell}},\dotsc,x_{r+1})&\text{if }e=x_{\ell}\notin\{x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell}},\dotsc,x_{r+1}\};\\
0&\text{if }e=x_{\ell}=x_{k}\text{ for }\ell\neq k.\end{cases}

We aim to show that
\myvarphi
is a Grassmann-Plücker function and
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{\myvarphi}/e,M_{\myvarphi}\backslash e)
. As
\mynu
is not identically zero,
\myvarphi
satisfies
(GP1)
. Property
(GP2)
is obvious but for the case that
e=x_{i}\notin\{x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{i}},\dotsc,x_{r+1}\}
where we find

\displaystyle\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{i},\dotsc,x_{j},\dotsc,x_{r+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r+1-i}\mymu(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{i-1},\widehat{x_{i}},x_{i+1},\dotsc,x_{j},\dotsc,x_{r+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(r+1-i)+j-(i+1)}\mymu(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{i-1},x_{j},x_{i+1},\dotsc,x_{j-1},\widehat{x_{i}},x_{j+1},\dotsc,x_{r+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon\cdot\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{j},\dotsc,x_{i},\dotsc,x_{r+1}).

Since
F
is perfect, we only need to show that
\myvarphi
satisfies
(GP3)
for subsets
I=\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r+2}\}
and
J=\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\}
of
E
with
\#I\backslash J=3
(see
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.46]
). As
\myvarphi
satisfies
(GP2)
, one can suppose that
a:=y_{1},\,b:=y_{2},\,c:=y_{3}
are the elements of
I\backslash J
,
d=x_{1}
is the unique element in
J\backslash I
and
x_{i}=y_{i+2}
for
i=2,\dotsc,r
. For
w,z\in\{a,b,c,d\}
define
\widehat{\myvarphi}(w,z):=\myvarphi(w,z,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})
. Note that

(3)

\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+2}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\cdot\myvarphi(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\myvarphi(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})

=\;\myepsilon^{1}\cdot\widehat{\myvarphi}(b,c)\cdot\widehat{\myvarphi}(a,d)+\myepsilon^{2}\widehat{\myvarphi}(a,c)\cdot\widehat{\myvarphi}(b,d)+\myepsilon^{3}\cdot\widehat{\myvarphi}(a,b)\cdot\widehat{\myvarphi}(c,d).

We have 4 cases to analyze.

Case 1:
If
e\notin I\cup J
, then equation (
3
) becomes

\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+2}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\cdot\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mynu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}),

which is in
N_{F}
because
\mynu
is a Grassmann-Plücker function.

Case 2:
If
e=y_{\ell}=x_{\ell+2}\in I\cap J
, then we define
(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{r+1})=(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})
and
(u_{1},\dotsc,u_{r-1})=(x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell-2}},\dotsc,x_{r})
. With this, equation (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle\underset{k=1}{\overset{\ell-1}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\cdot\mymu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell-2}},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{\ell}\cdot\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot 0

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underset{k=\ell+1}{\overset{r+2}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k-1}\cdot\mymu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{\ell}},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(y_{k},x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell-2}},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+1}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\cdot\mymu(t_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{t_{k}},\dotsc,t_{r+1})\cdot\mymu(t_{k},u_{1},\dotsc,u_{r-1}),

which is in
N_{F}
because
\mymu
is a Grassmann-Plücker function.

Case 3:
If
e=d
, then equation (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r}\mynu(b,c,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(a,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r+1}\mynu(a,c,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(b,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r+2}\mynu(a,b,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(c,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r-1}\cdot\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+2}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\mynu(y_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(y_{k},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r}),

which is in
N_{F}
because
M_{\mynu}\twoheadrightarrow M_{\mymu}
.

Case 4:
The cases for
e\in\{a,b,c\}
are similar to prove. We demonstrate the case
e=a
, in which equation (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(r+1)+2}\mynu(b,c,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(d,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(r+2)}\mymu(c,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})\cdot\mynu(b,d,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{(r+3)-2}\mymu(b,x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})\cdot\mynu(c,d,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\myepsilon^{r}\cdot\underset{k=1}{\overset{r+2}{\sum}}\myepsilon^{k}\mynu(z_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{z_{k}},\dotsc z_{r+2})\cdot\mymu(z_{k},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r}),

which is in
N_{F}
because
M_{\mynu}\twoheadrightarrow M_{\mymu}
, where
(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{r+2}):=(b,c,d,y_{4},\dotsc,y_{r+2})
.

To conclude, this shows that
\myvarphi
is a Grassmann-Plücker function. Since
\myvarphi/e=\mymu
and
\myvarphi\backslash e=\mynu
, one has
(M_{\myvarphi}/e,M_{\myvarphi}\backslash e)={\mathbf{M}}
.

For the last part of the theorem, it is enough to prove that the set of Grassmann-Plücker functions
\mylambda
with
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{\mylambda}/e,M_{\mylambda}\backslash e)
stays in bijection with
(F^{\times})^{2}
. This follows from the following two facts:

(1)

If
\mylambda
is a Grassmann-Plücker function on
\widehat{E}
satisfying
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{\mylambda}/e,M_{\mylambda}\backslash e)
, then there are
\myalpha
,
\mybeta\in F^{\times}
such that
\mylambda/e=\myalpha\cdot\mymu
and
\mylambda\backslash e=\mybeta\cdot\mynu
.

(2)

Conversely, given
\myalpha
,
\mybeta\in F^{\times}
, by a construction analogue to that of
\myvarphi
above, there is a unique Grassmann-Plücker function
\mylambda
on
\widehat{E}
such that
\mylambda/e=\myalpha\cdot\mymu
and
\mylambda\backslash e=\mybeta\cdot\mynu
, which implies

{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{\mylambda}/e,M_{\mylambda}\backslash e)
.

This concludes the prof of
section 2.9
.
∎

Corollary 2.26
.

Let
F
be a perfect tract. Let
M
and
N
be
F
-matroids with respective Grassmann-Plücker functions
\mymu:E^{r}\to F
and
\mynu:E^{r+1}\to F
. Assume that
(\underline{M},\underline{N})
is a flag matroid. Then
(M,N)
is a flag
F
-matroid if

\mymu(a,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\mynu(b,c,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\ -\ \mymu(b,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\mynu(a,c,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\\
+\ \mymu(c,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\cdot\mynu(a,b,x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1})\quad\in\ N_{F}

for all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1},a,b,c\in E
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{E}=E\sqcup\{e\}
and
\myvarphi:\widehat{E}^{r+1}\to F
be defined by

\myvarphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1})\ =\ \begin{cases}\mynu(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1})&\text{if }e\notin\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r+1}\};\\
\myepsilon^{r+1-\ell}\mymu(x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell}},\dotsc,x_{r+1})&\text{if }e=x_{\ell}\notin\{x_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x_{\ell}},\dotsc,x_{r+1}\};\\
0&\text{if }e=x_{\ell}=x_{k}\text{ for }\ell\neq k.\end{cases}

Following through the steps of the proof of
section 2.9
, we see that the relations in the statement of the corollary are sufficient to prove that
\myvarphi
is a weak Grassmann-Plücker function.

Since
(\underline{M},\underline{N})
is a flag matroid, it is of the form
(\widetilde{M}/e,\widetilde{M}\backslash e)
for a matroid
\widetilde{M}
on
\widehat{E}
by
section 2.9
, which is unique since
{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}=\{1\}
. Since the construction of
\myvarphi
is formally independent of the tract
F
, it is evident that
\myvarphi
represents the matroid
\widetilde{M}
. We conclude that
\myvarphi
defines an
F
-matroid
M_{\myvarphi}=[\myvarphi]
by
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.46]
.

By the construction of
\myvarphi
, we have
M=M_{\myvarphi}/e
and
N=M_{\myvarphi}\backslash e
. Thus it follows from
Theorem 2.23
that
(M,N)=(M_{\myvarphi}/e,M_{\myvarphi}\backslash e)
is a flag
F
-matroid.
∎

3.
The moduli space of flag matroids

In this part, we construct the moduli space
\Fl(r_{1},\dots,r_{s};E)
of flag matroids, which adds the cryptomorphic description of a flag
F
-matroid as an
F
-rational point of
\Fl(r_{1},\dots,r_{s};E)
. This extends results by Baker and the second author in
[
BL21
]
from matroids to flag matroids.

Our construction of
\Fl(r_{1},\dots,r_{s};E)
utilizes ordered blue schemes, a theory that was developed in
[
Lor15
]
and
[
BL21
]
. Since we cannot present the necessary background on this theory in a compact way, we assume that the reader is familiar with the latter paper by Baker and the second author. In particular, we assume that the reader is familiar with the terminologies and notations for ordered blueprints; see
[
BL21
]
for details.

Throughout the section, we fix a ground set
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and a rank
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
with
0\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant\dotsb\leqslant r_{s}\leqslant n
. For
{\mathbf{I}}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})\in E^{r}
and
e\in E
, we use the notations
{\mathbf{I}}e=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r},e)
and
|{\mathbf{I}}|=\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\}
.

3.1.
Idylls and ordered blueprints

We recall from
[
BL21
, Thm. 2.21]
that a tract
F=(F^{\times},N_{F})
can be realized as the ordered blueprint
B=F^{\textup{oblpr}}
with underlying monoid
B^{\bullet}=F
, ambient semiring
B^{+}={\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
and (additive and multiplicative) partial order
{\mathfrak{r}}_{B}\ =\ \langle 0\leqslant\sum a_{i}\mid\sum a_{i}\in N_{F}\rangle
on
B^{+}
. An
idyll
is an ordered blueprint of the form
B=F^{\textup{oblpr}}
for a tract
F
.

The association
F\mapsto F^{\textup{oblpr}}
extends naturally to a functor
(-)^{\textup{oblpr}}:\Tracts\to\OBlpr
from tracts to ordered blueprints that comes with a right adjoint
(-)^{\textup{tract}}:\OBlpr\to\Tracts
. Composing both functors sends a tract
F
to the tract
F^{\prime}=(F^{\textup{oblpr}})^{\textup{tract}}
that equals
F
as a monoid and whose nullset
N_{F^{\prime}}
equals the closure of
N_{F}
under addition. This means that the functors
(-)^{\textup{tract}}
and
(-)^{\textup{oblpr}}
restrict to mutually inverse equivalence of categories between idylls and tracts whose nullset is additively closed.

By abuse of language, we call tracts with additively closed nullset also idylls and we do not make a distinction between the tract and the associated ordered blueprint. In particular, we denote the associated ordered blueprint with the same symbol, which applies, for instance, to the idylls
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
,
{\mathbb{K}}
,
{\mathbb{S}}
and
{\mathbb{T}}
. Note that, more generally, all partial fields and all hyperfields are idylls. Following this logic, we define a
flag
F
-matroid
for an idyll
F
with associated tract
F^{\prime}=F^{\textup{tract}}
as a flag
F^{\prime}
-matroid.

Note that every idyll is an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-algebra in a unique way.

Example 3.1
.

The incarnations of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
and
{\mathbb{K}}
as ordered blueprints are

{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\ =\ \{0,\pm 1\}\!\sslash\!\langle 0\leqslant 1+(-1)\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{K}}\ =\ \{0,1\}\!\sslash\!\langle 0\leqslant 1+1,\ 0\leqslant 1+1+1\rangle.

The associated ordered blueprint
B=K^{\textup{oblpr}}
of a field
K
has underlying monoid
B^{\bullet}=K
, ambient semiring
B^{+}={\mathbb{N}}[K^{\times}]
and partial order

{\mathfrak{r}}_{B}\ =\ \langle 0\leqslant\sum a_{i}\mid\sum a_{i}=0\text{ in $K$}\rangle
.

3.2.
Flag matroid bundles

As a first step towards the moduli space of flag matroids, we generalize the notion of a flag
F
-matroid to that of a flag matroid bundle on an (ordered blue)
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
X
be an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme,
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
. A
flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions (of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
) in
X
are line bundles
{\mathcal{L}}_{1},\dotsc,{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
on
X
and functions

\myvarphi_{i}:\ E^{r_{i}}\ \longrightarrow\ \Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i})

for
i=1,\dotsc,s
such that for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant s
,

(GP1
∗
)

the global sections
\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})
with
{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}

generate
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
, i.e., for every point
x\in X
, there is an
{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}
such that the class of
\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
is a unit;

(GP2
∗
)

\myvarphi_{i}
is
alternating
, i.e.,
\myvarphi_{i}(x_{\mysigma(1)},\dotsc,x_{\mysigma(r_{i})})=\sign(\mysigma)\cdot\myvarphi_{i}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}})
and
\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})=0
if
\#|{\mathbf{I}}|<r_{i}
;

(GP3
∗
)

\myvarphi_{i}
and
\myvarphi_{j}
satisfy the
Plücker flag relations

0\ \leqslant\ \sum_{k=1}^{r_{j}+1}\ \myepsilon^{k}\,\cdot\,\myvarphi_{i}(y_{k},\,x_{1},\dotsc,\,x_{r_{i}-1})\,\cdot\,\myvarphi_{j}(y_{1},\dotsc,\,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,\,y_{r_{j}+1})

as relations in
\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{j})
for all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r_{j}+1}\in E
.

Two flags of Grassmann-Plücker functions

\big(\myvarphi_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i})\big)_{i=1,\dotsc,s}\quad\text{and}\quad\big(\myvarphi^{\prime}_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{i})\big)_{i=1,\dotsc,s}

are
equivalent
if there exists a collection of isomorphisms
\myiota_{i}:{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\to{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\prime}
of line bundles (for
i=1,\dotsc,s
) such that
\myvarphi^{\prime}_{i}=\myiota_{i,X}\circ\myvarphi_{i}
for
i=1,\dotsc,s
where
\myiota_{i,X}:\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i})\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\prime})
is
\myiota_{i}
evaluated on global sections. A
flag matroid bundle (of rank
(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
on
E
) over
X
is the equivalence class of a flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions (of rank
(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
on
E
) in
X
.

Remark 3.3
.

Flag matroid bundles extend the notion of flag matroids in the following sense. Let
F
be an idyll. Then there is a canonical bijection

\big\{\text{flag $F$-matroids}\big\}\ \longrightarrow\ \big\{\text{flag matroid bundles over $\Spec F$}\big\},

which is given as follows. Note that we have a canonical bijection
\myiota:F\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
for
X=\Spec F
. As explained in
[
BL21
, Prop. 5.3]
, we can associate with a Grassmann-Plücker function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F
the Grassmann-Plücker function
\myiota\circ\myvarphi:E^{r}\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
, which yields a (well-defined) bijection
M\mapsto\widetilde{M}
between
F
-matroids and matroid bundles over
\Spec F
.

Unraveling definitions, we see that a sequence
(\myvarphi_{1},\dotsc,\myvarphi_{s})
of Grassmann-Plücker functions
\myvarphi_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to F
satisfies the Plücker flag relations if and only if the associated sequence
(\myiota\circ\myvarphi_{1},\dotsc,\myiota\circ\myvarphi_{s})
satisfies them (considered as Grassmann-Plücker functions in
X
). Taking classes yields the desired bijection
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})\mapsto(\widetilde{M}_{1},\dotsc,\widetilde{M}_{s})=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that a Grassmann-Plücker function of rank
r
on
E
in
[
BL21
]
is defined as a function on the collection
\binom{E}{r}
of
r
-subsets of
E
. This is equivalent with our definition since functions on
\binom{E}{r}
identify with alternating functions on
E^{r}
by choosing a total order on
E
(which we do implicitly by identifying
E
with
\{1,\dotsc,n\}
).

While the Plücker coordinates of Grassmannians are usually indexed by elements of
\binom{E}{r}
, the representation of matroids as (alternating) Grassmann-Plücker functions on
E^{r}
is more natural with relation to several constructions in matroid theory, which is our reason to choose alternating functions over functions on
r
-subsets.

3.3.
The moduli problem

Given an ordered blue
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme
X
, we define
\cFl({\mathbf{r}};E)(X)
as the set of all flag matroid bundles of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
over
X
. A morphism
\myalpha:X\to Y
defines the map

\myalpha^{\ast}:\ \cFl({\mathbf{r}};E)(Y)\ \longrightarrow\ \cFl({\mathbf{r}};E)(X)

by pulling back functions via
\myalpha_{Y}^{\sharp}:\Gamma(Y,{\mathcal{L}})\to\Gamma(X,\myalpha^{\ast}{\mathcal{L}})
: given a flag matroid bundle
{\mathbf{M}}
on
Y
that is represented by a flag
\big(\myvarphi_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(Y,{\mathcal{L}}_{i})\big)_{i=1,\dotsc,s}
, then we define
\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}})
as the flag matroid bundle on
X
that is represented by
\big(\myalpha_{Y}^{\sharp}\circ\myvarphi_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(X,\myalpha^{\ast}{\mathcal{L}}_{i})\big)_{i=1,\dotsc,s}
. We omit the verification that the latter family is indeed a flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions. We pose the following moduli problem:

Is there an ordered blue
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme that represents
\cFl({\mathbf{r}};E)
?

We work in the following sections towards its answer, which is the content of
Theorem 3.8
.

3.4.
Flag varieties

The answer to our moduli problem is given by flag varieties over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which are closed subschemes of a product of Grassmannians that parametrize the matroid bundles of the flag. The locus of the flag variety is given by the Plücker flag relations, which are multi-homogeneous with respect to the Plücker coordinates of each Grassmannian in the product. In order to bypass a rigorous treatment of multi-homogeneous calculus in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry, we give an explicit description of flag varieties in terms of open affine coverings.

We define the flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}};E)
as a closed subscheme of the product space

{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}^{n^{r_{1}}-1}\times\dotsb\times{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}^{n^{r_{s}}-1}\ =\ \prod_{i=1}^{s}\ \Proj\,{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{{\mathbf{I}}}\mid{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}],

which is covered by products
\prod_{i=1}^{s}U_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}
of the canonical open subsets

U_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}\ =\ \Spec\,{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{\mathbf{I}}/T_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}\mid{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}]

of
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
where
{\mathbf{J}}_{i}\in E^{r_{i}}
for
i=1,\dotsc,s
.

Definition 3.5
.

The
flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}};E)
(of type
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
) over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
is the closed subscheme of
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
that is covered by the open subschemes

\textstyle\prod\ U_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}\ =\ \Spec\Big(\big(\bigotimes{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{i,{\mathbf{I}}}/T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}\mid{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}]\big)\!\sslash\!{\mathfrak{r}}\Big)

where
{\mathfrak{r}}
is generated by the following relations for
1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant s
:

\frac{T_{i,(x_{\mysigma(1)},\dotsc,x_{\mysigma(r_{i})})}}{T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}}\ =\ \sign(\mysigma)\ \cdot\ \frac{T_{i,(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}})}}{T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}}

for all
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}})\in E^{r_{i}}
and all permutations
\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r_{i}}
;

\frac{T_{i,{\mathbf{I}}}}{T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}}\ =\ 0

for all
{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}
with
\#|{\mathbf{I}}|<r_{i}
;

0\ \ \leqslant\ \ \sum_{k=1}^{r_{j}+1}\quad\myepsilon^{k}\,\cdot\,\frac{T_{i,(y_{k},\,x_{1},\dotsc,\,x_{r_{i}-1})}}{T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}}\,\cdot\,\frac{T_{j,(y_{1},\dotsc,\,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,\,y_{r_{j}+1})}}{T_{j,{\mathbf{J}}_{j}}}

for all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r_{i}-1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r_{j}+1}\in E
. The
Plücker embedding
is the closed immersion

\pl:\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\ \longrightarrow\ \prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}

as a subscheme.

Note that the intersection
V
of affine opens
\prod U_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}
and
\prod U_{{\mathbf{J}}^{\prime}_{i}}
is affine. Multiplying the defining relations of
\Gamma(\prod U_{{\mathbf{J}}_{i}})
with the invertible section
T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}/T_{i,{\mathbf{J}}^{\prime}_{i}}
on
V
yields the defining relations of
\Gamma(\prod U_{{\mathbf{J}}^{\prime}_{i}})
. This shows that
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
is well defined as a closed subscheme of
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

Note that in the case of
s=1
and
r_{1}=r
, the flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
is nothing else than a Grassmannian
\Gr(r,E)
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which Baker and the second author called the
matroid space
\Mat(r,E)
in
[
BL21
]
to distinct it from other models of Grassmannians in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry (e.g. see
[
LL12
]
). We will drop this distinction in our text to appeal better to the reader’s intuition from algebraic geometry.

For arbitrary
s\geqslant 1
, the Plücker embedding factors into closed immersions

\textstyle\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\ \longrightarrow\ \prod_{i=1}^{s}\Gr(r_{i},E)\ \longrightarrow\ \prod_{i=1}^{s}{\mathbb{P}}^{r_{i}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}},

i.e., the flag variety is a closed subscheme of a product of Grassmannians over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
.

3.5.
The universal flag matroid bundle

The Plücker embedding
\pl:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
endows the flag variety over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
with a flag matroid bundle, which is universal for all matroid bundles (see
Theorem 3.8
) and which is defined as follows.

Let
\pl_{j}=\mypi_{j}\circ\pl\,
be the composition of the Plücker embedding
\pl\,
with the
j
-th coordinate projection
\mypi_{j}:\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{j}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
be the first twisted sheaf on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which is generated by the sections
T_{{\mathbf{I}}}
with
{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}
.

Definition 3.7
.

The
universal flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions (of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
)
is the sequence of Grassmann-Plücker functions
\big(\myvarphi_{i}^{\textup{univ}}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E),{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\textup{univ}})\big)
with line bundles
{\mathcal{L}}^{\textup{univ}}_{i}=\pl_{i}^{\ast}\big({\mathcal{O}}(1)\big)
and with
\myvarphi_{i}^{\textup{univ}}({\mathbf{I}})=\pl_{i}^{\sharp}(T_{{\mathbf{I}}})
. The
universal flag matroid bundle
{\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}}
(of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
)
is the equivalence class of the universal flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions.

Note that the universal flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions is indeed a flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
over
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
since
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\textup{univ}}
is generated by
\{\pl_{i}^{\sharp}(T_{{\mathbf{I}}})\mid{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}\}
, and since the relations
(GP2∗)
and
(GP3∗)
are satisfied by the defining relations (
3.5
)–(
3.5
) of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
. Consequently,
{\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}}
is a flag matroid bundle on
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
.

3.6.
The moduli property

We are prepared to formulate the central result of this section.

Theorem 3.8
.

The flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
is the fine moduli space of flag matroid bundles. More explicitly, the maps

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Phi_{X}:&\Hom(X,\;\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E))&\longrightarrow&\cFl({\mathbf{r}},E)(X)\\
&\myalpha:X\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)&\longmapsto&\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}}),\end{array}

indexed by ordered blue
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-schemes
X
, are functorial bijections.

Proof.

Let us fix an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme
X
. We begin with the construction of the inverse bijection
\Psi_{X}
to
\Phi_{X}
that maps a flag matroid bundle
{\mathbf{M}}
of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
over
X
to a morphism
X\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
.

Let
\big(\myvarphi_{i}:E^{r_{i}}\to\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i})\big)
be a flag of Grassmann-Plücker functions in
X
that represents the flag matroid bundle
{\mathbf{M}}
. Since the global sections in the image of
\myvarphi_{i}
generate the line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
, we can apply
[
BL21
, Thm. 4.20]
, which asserts the existence of a unique morphism
\mypsi_{i}:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
and a unique isomorphism
\myiota_{i}:\myiota^{\ast}({\mathcal{O}}(1))\to{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
such that
\myiota_{i}\big(\mypsi_{i}^{\sharp}(T_{\mathbf{I}})\big)=\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})
where we fix an identification of the homogeneous coordinates of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
with
\{T_{\mathbf{I}}\mid{\mathbf{I}}\in E^{r_{i}}\}
.

Taking the product over all
i=1,\dotsc,s
yields a morphism
\mypsi:X\to\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
. Since the
\myvarphi_{i}
are Grassmann-Plücker functions,
[
BL21
, Thm. 5.5]
applies and shows that the image of
\mypsi
is contained in
\prod\Gr(r_{i},E)
. Since the functions
\myvarphi_{i}
satisfy moreover the Plücker flag relations, the image is, in fact, contained in
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
, i.e.,
\mypsi
factors into a uniquely determined morphism
\myalpha:X\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
followed by the closed immersion
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
. We define
\Psi_{X}({\mathbf{M}})=\myalpha
.

Next we show that
\Phi_{X}\circ\Psi_{X}=\textup{id}
. Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\textup{univ}}=\mypi_{i}^{\ast}({\mathcal{O}}(1))
for the coordinate projection
\mypi_{i}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
, the isomorphism
\myiota_{i}:\myiota^{\ast}({\mathcal{O}}(1))\to{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
becomes an isomorphism
\widetilde{\myiota}_{i}:\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\textup{univ}})\to{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
, and we have
\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})=\widetilde{\myiota}_{i}\big(\myalpha^{\sharp}(T_{\mathbf{I}})\big)
. Thus
\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}})={\mathbf{M}}
, which shows that
\Psi_{X}
is right inverse to
\Phi_{X}
.

Next we show that
\Psi_{X}\circ\Phi_{X}=\textup{id}
. Let
\myalpha:X\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
be a morphism and
{\mathbf{M}}=\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}})
the associated flag matroid bundle over
X
. Then
{\mathbf{M}}
is represented by the flag
(\myvarphi_{1},\dotsc,\myvarphi_{s})
of Grassmann-Plücker functions

\myvarphi_{i}:\ E^{r_{i}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\myvarphi_{i}^{\textup{univ}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \Gamma\big(\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E),{\mathcal{L}}_{i}^{\textup{univ}}\big)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\myalpha^{\sharp}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}}_{i}).

The composition of
\myalpha
with the coordinate projection
\mypi_{i}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
yields the morphism
\myalpha_{i}:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}
, which satisfies
\myalpha_{i}^{\sharp}(T_{\mathbf{I}})=\myvarphi_{i}({\mathbf{I}})
. By the construction of
\Psi_{X}
, we have thus
\Psi_{X}({\mathbf{M}})=\myalpha
, which verifies that
\Psi_{X}
is a left inverse to
\Phi_{X}
.

We are left to show the functoriality of
\Phi_{X}
. Consider a morphism
\mybeta:Y\to X
and
\myalpha:X\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
. Then

\Phi_{Y}\big(\mybeta^{\ast}(\myalpha)\big)\ =\ \Phi_{Y}(\myalpha\circ\mybeta)\ =\ (\myalpha\circ\mybeta)^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}})\ =\ \mybeta^{\ast}\big(\myalpha^{\ast}({\mathbf{M}}^{\textup{univ}})\big)\ =\ \mybeta^{\ast}(\Phi_{X}(\myalpha)\big),

which shows that
\Phi_{X}
is functorial in
X
.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
F
be an idyll. Then there is a canonical bijection between the set
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(F)
of
F
-rational points of the flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
and the set of flag
F
-matroids of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
.

Proof.

This follows at once from
Theorem 3.8
coupled with
section 3.2
.
∎

Definition 3.10
.

Given an idyll
F
and a flag
F
-matroid
{\mathbf{M}}
of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
be the corresponding flag matroid bundle on

. We call the inverse image of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
under
\Phi_{X}
the

3.7.
Projection onto subflags

Let
{\mathbf{i}}=(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{t})
be a sequence of integers with

. Let
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}=(r_{i_{1}},\dotsc,r_{i_{t}})

\widetilde{\mypi}_{\mathbf{i}}:\ \prod_{i=1}^{s}\Gr(r_{i},E)\ \longrightarrow\ \prod_{k=1}^{t}\Gr(r_{i_{k}},E)

Proposition 3.11
.

The morphism
\widetilde{\mypi}_{\mathbf{i}}
restricts to a morphism
\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},E)
. Let
F
be an idyll and
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
a flag
F
-matroid of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
with characteristic morphism
\mychi_{\mathbf{M}}:\Spec F\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)

{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}=(M_{i_{1}},\dotsc,M_{i_{t}})
is

\mychi_{{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}}=\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}\circ\mychi_{\mathbf{M}}:\ \Spec F\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mychi_{\mathbf{M}}\;}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mypi_{\mathbf{i}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},E).

Proof.

This follows at once from the construction of
\Psi_{X}

and the definition of

3.8.
Duality

The duality of flag
F
-matroids extends to a functorial dualization of flag matroid bundles, which is reflected by a canonical isomorphism of flag varieties.

Let
\Gr(r,E)=\Fl((r),E)
be the Grassmannian of rank
r
on
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. Let
r^{\ast}=n-r
. By
[
BL21
, Thm. 5.6]
, there is a canonical isomorphism

is independent and co-independent. In other words, we can consider
\mymu_{{\mathbf{r}},E}
as morphism
\mymu_{{\mathbf{r}},E}:W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
. We omit further details for the sake of a compact presentation and proceed with the central statement about this morphism (without proof).

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
F
be a tract and
M^{\prime}
be an
F
-matroid of rank
r_{s}
on
E^{\prime}
with characteristic morphism
\mychi_{M^{\prime}}:\Spec F\to\Gr(r_{s},E^{\prime})
whose image we assume to be in
W_{{\mathbf{r}},E}
. Let
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
be the flag
F
-matroid of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
with
M_{i}=M^{\prime}\backslash I_{i}/J_{i}
and
\mychi_{\mathbf{M}}:\Spec F\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
its characteristic morphism. Then
\mychi_{\mathbf{M}}=\mymu_{{\mathbf{r}},E}\circ\mychi_{M^{\prime}}
.

3.11.
Rational point sets

A
topological idyll
is an idyll
F
together with a topology such that the multiplication
F\times F\to F
and the inversion
F^{\times}\to F^{\times}
are continuous maps and such that
\{0\}
is closed. Examples of topological idylls are topological fields (considered as idylls with the same topology), the tropical hyperfield with the real topology, the Krasner hyperfield
{\mathbb{K}}
with the topology generated by the open subset
\{1\}
and the sign hyperfield
{\mathbb{S}}
with the topology generated by the open subsets
\{1\}
and
\{-1\}
.

The sets of
F
-rational points
X(F)
for an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme
X
and a topological idyll
F
come with the
fine topology on
X(F)
, which is characterized by the following properties:

(1)

for affine
X=\Spec B
, the fine topology
X(F)=\Hom(B,F)
carries the compact-open topology with respect to the discrete topology for
B
;

(2)

the canonical bijection
F\to{\mathbb{A}}_{F}^{1}(F)
is a homeomorphism;

(3)

the canonical bijection
(X\times Y)(F)\to X(F)\times Y(F)
is a homeomorphism;

(4)

for every morphism
Y\to X
, the canonical map
Y(F)\to X(F)
is continuous;

(5)

for every open / closed immersion
Y\to X
, the canonical inclusion
Y(F)\to X(F)
is an open / closed topological embedding;

(6)

for every covering of
X
by ordered blue open subschemes
U_{i}
, a subset
W
of
X(F)
is open if and only if
W\cap U_{i}(F)
is open in
U_{i}(F)
for every
i
;

(7)

for every continuous morphism
F\to F^{\prime}
, the induced map
X(F)\to X(F^{\prime})
is continuous.

For more details, see
[
Lor15
, section 6]
and
[
BL21
, section 5.10]
.

In the following, we briefly discuss the topological spaces that arise from the topological idylls that we mention above.

3.11.1.
Topological fields

Let
K
be a topological field. Then
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(K)
is canonically homeomorphic to the set of
K
-rational points of the usual flag variety of type
{\mathbf{r}}
flags of subspaces of
K^{E}
, equipped with the strong topology stemming from
K
.

As a particular example, we consider
{\mathbb{R}}
in its incarnation as an idyll. Then
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{R}})
is canonically homeomorphic to the real flag manifold of type
{\mathbf{r}}
flags of linear subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
.

3.11.2.
The tropical hyperfield

The space
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
of tropical points is canonically homeomorphic to the flag Dressian
FlDr(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
of Brandt, Eur and Zhang’s paper
[
BEZ21
]
. They are naturally tropical subvarieties of a product
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r_{i}}-1}({\mathbb{T}})
of projective tropical spaces (in their incarnations as tropical varieties). We regain several insights of
[
BEZ21
]
from our point of view.

By
section 3.6
, a point of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
corresponds to a flag
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroid. Since the covectors of a
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroid, or valuated matroid, form a tropical linear space, a flag
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroid corresponds to a flag of tropical linear spaces in
{\mathbb{T}}^{E}
by
Theorem 2.4
. In conclusion,
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
corresponds bijectively to rank
{\mathbf{r}}
-flags of tropical linear spaces in
{\mathbb{T}}^{E}
.

The fine topology of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
corresponds to the variation of flags of tropical linear space in terms of open neighbourhoods in
{\mathbb{T}}^{E}
of a given flag or, equivalently, to a variation of the coefficients of the defining equations of the tropical linear spaces within an open interval.

We alert the reader that tropical Grassmannians are defined as the tropicalizations of classical Grassmannians in tropical geometry, which are in general proper subvarieties of Dressians. This terminological artifact leads to a slight inconsistency in the notation
\Gr(r,E)
for the (underlying scheme of the) Dressian. Still, the generalization of tropical Grassmannians to flag varieties can also be recovered from our viewpoint: let
K
be a field with a non-archimedean absolute value
v:K\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, which can be seen as an idyll morphism
v:K\to{\mathbb{T}}
, see
[
Lor22
, Thm. 2.2]
. The tropical flag variety of type
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
stemming from
K
is the image of the map
v_{\ast}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(K)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{T}})
.

3.11.3.
The Krasner hyperfield

The natural topology for
{\mathbb{K}}
is the poset topology with
0<1
, which turns
{\mathbb{K}}
into the terminal object in the category of topological tracts. In consequence, the finite set
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
inherits the topology of a poset, which equals the subspace topology of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
considered as a subset of the underlying topological space of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)
together with the Zariski topology. In particular, the closed points of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
correspond to all flag matroids
(M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s})
of rank
{\mathbf{r}}
on
E
for which the matroids
M_{1},\dotsc,M_{s}
have each exactly one basis.

The poset
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
has a unique maximal element
\hat{1}
, or generic point, which is the rank
{\mathbf{r}}
-flag of uniform matroids on
E
. Thus
\{\hat{1}\}
is an open subset of
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
and, in consequence,
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{K}})
can be contracted to the point
\hat{1}
, which generalizes the analogous result of Anderson and Davis for the “hyperfield Grassmannian”
\Gr(e,E)({\mathbb{K}})
; see
[
AD19
, section 6]
.

3.11.4.
The sign hyperfield

Similar to the case of the Krasner hyperfield,
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{S}})
is a finite poset with the order topology. In this case, the topology is highly non-trivial and links to the (disproven) MacPhersonian conjecture in the case of the Grassmannian
\Gr(r,E)({\mathbb{S}})
, see
[
MZ93
,
Liu20
,
AD19
]
. An interesting question is whether the results from
[
AD19
]
generalize to flag varieties. In particular, we pose the following question.

Problem
.

Does the continuous map
\sign_{\ast}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{R}})\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{S}})
induce a surjection in mod
2
cohomology?

3.11.5.
The triangular hyperfield

Yet another interesting example of a topological idyll is Viro’s
triangle hyperfield

{\mathbb{V}}
(see
[
Vir11
,
AD19
]
) whose underlying monoid is
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, whose ambient semiring is
{\mathbb{N}}[{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}]
and whose partial order is generated by the relations
0\leqslant a+b+c
whenever
|a-b|\leqslant c\leqslant a+b
, endowed with the real topology. Alternatively
{\mathbb{V}}
can be described as the hyperfield quotient of
{\mathbb{C}}
by the unit circle
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid|z|=1\}
. The continuous morphisms
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{V}}
induce continuous maps

\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{R}})\ \longrightarrow\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{C}})\ \longrightarrow\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({\mathbb{V}}).

3.11.6.
The regular partial field

The natural choice of topology for the regular partial field is the discrete topology, which retains the position of the regular partial field as an initial object in the category of topological tracts. This topology turns
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}})
into a discrete point set. The unique continuous map
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\to F
into any other topological tract
F
induces a continuous map
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}})\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)(F)
.
