Global existence of high-frequency solutions to a semi-linear wave equation with a null structure
Arthur Touati

Abstract
We study the propagation of a compactly supported high-frequency wave through a semi-linear wave equation with a null structure. We prove that the self-interaction of the wave creates harmonics which remain close to the light-cone in the evolution. By defining a well-chosen ansatz, we describe precisely those harmonics. Moreover, by applying the vector field method to the equation for the remainder in the ansatz, we prove that the solution exists globally. The interaction between the dispersive decay of waves and their high-frequency behaviour is the main difficulty, and the latter is not compensated by smallness of the initial data, allowing us to consider the high-frequency limit where the wavelength tends to 0.



1
Introduction

1.1
Presentation of the result

In this article we are interested in proving the global existence of
high-frequency solutions
to the following semi-linear wave system

\displaystyle\Box\Phi=Q(\partial\Phi,\partial\Phi)

(1.1)

on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
where
\Box=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta
is the standard d’Alembertian operator and
Q
has the null structure (see Definition
2.1
). The initial data we consider are highly oscillatory in the radial direction. This corresponds to a large data regime, which is to be compared with the well-known small data regime, see the discussion below. Let us give a rough version of the theorem proved in this article (see Theorem
3.1
for a precise version):

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider sufficiently regular functions
(F_{0},\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
of size
\varepsilon
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
\lambda>0
. If
\varepsilon
is small enough (independent of
\lambda
), there exists a global solution
\Phi_{\lambda}
to
\Box u=Q(\partial u,\partial u)
of the form:

\Phi_{\lambda}=\varphi+\lambda F\cos\left(\frac{t-r}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda^{2}F_{\lambda}

(1.2)

where

•

\varphi
satisfies
\Box\varphi=Q(\partial\varphi,\partial\varphi)
and
(\varphi,\partial_{t}\varphi)_{|t=0}=(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
,

•

F
satisfies
\left(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)F=F\partial\varphi
and
F_{|t=0}=F_{0}
,

•

F_{\lambda}
satisfies
|F_{\lambda}|\lesssim\varepsilon
.

In the next section we will review in detail our strategy of proof, for now let us just mention that the global existence of solutions to (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3+1}
with any (regular and small enough) initial data is a classical result of Klainerman (see
[
13
]
), which introduced the concept of null quadratic forms precisely for this purpose, and Christodoulou (see
[
6
]
) which used conformal compactification. Our proof relies crucially on the vector field method Klainerman introduced, but our theorem is
not
a consequence of his result for the following reasons:

•

Theorem
1.1
can be summarized as follows: take some oscillating initial data containing terms like
\lambda\cos\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)
, then the unique global solution arrising from those initial data actually presents the same oscillating behaviour. Unlike Klainerman, we not only prove global existence but propagate the shape of the high-frequency ansatz.

•

Another crucial aspects of Theorem
1.1
is that the smallness threshold is independent of
\lambda
. One interesting aspects of high-frequency solutions of the form (
1.2
) is that their
H^{s}
norm is large for any
s>1
, if
\lambda
is close to 0. Indeed, by differentiating (
1.2
) strictly more than once we obtain negative powers of
\lambda
. Therefore, if one wants to apply Klainerman’s result with a fixed but small
\lambda
one needs to counterbalance the size of
\frac{1}{\lambda}
with the smallness of the initial data (concretely by assuming something like
\varepsilon\ll\lambda
), see Remark
3.1
. Therefore, our work gives an example of how one can relax the smallness assumption of Klainerman’s result.

Our motivations for studying solutions of the form (
1.2
) to non-linear wave equations like (
1.1
) come from Burnett’s conjecture in general relativity and are presented at the end of this introduction (see Section
1.3
). However, highly oscillating solution to hyperbolic equations have been intensely studied in the field of
geometric optics
. In 1957, Lax laid the foundations of this field in his article
[
14
]
, where he proved that linear hyperbolic equations admit WKB solutions. Named after the physicists Wentzel, Kramers and Brillouin, WKB solutions were first introduced to understand the behaviour of quantum systems in a semi-classical regime, the main idea being to write some part of the solution as an asymptotic expansion in terms of the small parameter
\hbar
. In geometric optics, the small parameter is a wavelength.

In this article, we prove global existence of a highly oscillating solutions of a
non-linear
wave equation. The construction of high-frequency ansatz for non-linear systems has been first conducted by Choquet-Bruhat in
[
3
]
, where she applies her result to deduce the ill-posedness of the Cauchy problem in
C^{1}
for the systems she considers. In
[
11
]
, Joly and Rauch extend the approximation procedure to higher space dimension. A complete overview of geometric optics can be found in the book of Rauch
[
16
]
.

In the limit
\lambda\to 0
, solutions of the form (
1.2
) explode in any
W^{k,p}
if
k>0
. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
allows us to consider large initial data for which we are still able to prove global existence. In
[
17
]
, Wang and Yu also consider the large data regime by studying the
short pulse
ansatz, inspired by Christodoulou and his study of black holes formation.

1.2
Strategy of proof

Let us present our strategy of proof, which can be viewed as a high-frequency adaptation of Klainerman’s vector field method.

1.2.1
Global existence for non-linear wave equations

In spatial dimension strictly larger than 3, the standard decay of waves is enough to ensure the global existence under the small initial data assumption of solutions to wave equations of the form

\displaystyle\Box u=(\partial u)^{2}.

(1.3)

However, since the counter example of John (see
[
10
]
), it is known that solutions to (
1.3
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3+1}
don’t necessarily exist for all time, even in the small initial data regime. The globality of solutions depends on the actual structure of the quadratic non-linearity. In 1984, Klainerman introduced the famous
null condition
(see
[
13
]
and Definition
2.1
). The null condition can be summarized as follows: in every product
\partial u\partial u
at least one of the derivatives is
tangent to the light cone
, and therefore enjoys better decay. This restriction on the non-linearity in (
1.3
) is sufficient (though not necessary) to obtain the existence of global solutions for small initial data.

In order to prove global existence of a solution, one powerful method is the vector field method, which goes back to Klainerman (see
[
12
]
). Instead of using standard derivatives
\partial_{\alpha}
to derive energy estimates, the heart of the method consists in using special vector fields linked to the symmetries of the Minkowski spacetime (see (
2.3
)). These vector fields are interesting for two reasons: they enjoy nice commutation properties with the wave operator and a more refined version of the classic Sobolev embedding
H^{s}\subset L^{\infty}
for
s>\frac{3}{2}
holds when replacing the standard derivatives in the
H^{s}
norm by the Minkowski vector fields (the so-called Klainerman-Sobolev inequality, see Proposition
2.2
). This refined Sobolev embedding comes with decay rates in the null coordinates
t+r
and
t-r
, which is the key argument to prove global existence.

This method can be improved by using a
weighted
energy estimate with an increasing function of
q=t-r
as a weight. This weight implies the presence of an additional term on the LHS of the usual energy estimates (see Lemma
2.1
), allowing to better control some of the derivatives of the solution (in our case the derivatives tangent to the light cone). This is known as the ghost weight method and was first introduced by Alinhac in
[
1
]
.

1.2.2
The high-frequency hierarchy

The main particularity of our result is the use of an high-frequency ansatz with a precise description of the oscillating terms, displaying what we call a
half-chessboard shape
. Let
\Phi_{\lambda}
be the solution of (
1.1
) admitting the following formal high-frequency expansion

\displaystyle\Phi_{\lambda}(t,x)\sim\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda^{k}\Phi^{(k)}\left(t,x,\frac{t-r}{\lambda}\right).

(1.4)

By half-chessboard shape, we mean that each
\Phi^{(i)}(t,x,\theta)
contains several oscillating terms in the
\theta
variable according to the following pattern:

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)

\displaystyle\Phi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta)+\cos(2\theta)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\Phi^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)+\sin(2\theta)+\cos(3\theta)

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

Remark 1.1
.

In (
1.5
), we only wrote down the oscillating terms, i.e the dependence of
\Phi^{(i)}
on its third variable, and by
\Phi^{(0)}=1
we mean that it does not actually depends on the third variable, i.e it is not oscillating.

The main challenge in defining a pattern of oscillation for each order is to capture the creation of harmonics due to the non-linear part of (
1.1
). It is not trivial that the half-chessboard shape is ”stable” under quadratic non-linearity of the form
(\partial\Phi_{\lambda})^{2}
, i.e that those terms only create harmonics already present in the ansatz for
\Phi_{\lambda}
. This will be rigourously proved in Section
4
, where we present the exact form of the ansatz for
\Phi_{\lambda}
(see (
4.1
)). The non-linear interactions also create non-oscillating terms which can’t be absorbed by the ansatz, unless we add to (
1.5
) some non-oscillating fields. To lighten this introduction we choose not to show them yet.

From a differential point of view, plugging oscillating terms
\lambda^{k}\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F
(with
u
a phase to be chosen later) in the wave operator gives

\displaystyle\Box\left(\lambda^{k}\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{k-2}\left(m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}u\partial_{\beta}u\right)\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F

(1.6)

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda^{k-1}\sin\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)\left(2m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}u\partial_{\beta}+\Box u\right)F

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{k}\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)\Box F

where
m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}u\partial_{\beta}u=-(\partial_{t}u)^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}
. We recover what is called the
geometric optics approximation
: in order to have
\Box\left(\lambda^{k}\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F\right)
at the same order as
\lambda^{k}\cos\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F
we need

•

m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}u\partial_{\beta}u=0
, i.e
u
to be a solution of the eikonal equation, or said differently we need the spacetime gradient of
u
, denoted
\partial u
, to be a null vector field for the Minkowski metric. In the rest of the article we will choose
u=t-r
.

•

\left(2m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}u\partial_{\beta}+\Box u\right)F=0
, i.e
F
to be transported along the rays of
u
. If
u=t-r
, this rewrites

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)F=0.

The fact that
u
satisfies the eikonal equation has another consequence, related to our pattern of oscillation (
1.5
). Indeed since
Q
is a null form and
\partial u
is a null vector the following term vanishes

\displaystyle\left(\sin\left(\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)F^{(1)}\right)^{2}Q(\partial u,\partial u)

(1.7)

where
F^{(1)}
is the coefficient of
\Phi^{(1)}
. The presence of this term would totally break the half-chessboard shape since we would need
\Phi^{(1)}
(as well as
\Phi^{(k)}
for
k\geq 2
because of the triangular structure) to contain all frequencies (see Remark
4.10
).

The conclusion of this discussion is that solving (
1.1
) is equivalent to solving a
triangular
hierarchy of
linear
transport equations along the rays of
u
for each coefficients in the high-frequency expansion of
\Phi_{\lambda}
, as well as a semi-linear wave equation for the remainder
h
, which is added to the formal expansion (
1.4
) to make it an exact solution of (
1.1
). Our ansatz is therefore of the following schematic form

\displaystyle\Phi_{\lambda}(t,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\lambda^{k}\Phi^{(k)}\left(t,x,\frac{t-r}{\lambda}\right)+h_{\lambda}

(1.8)

where
h_{\lambda}=O\left(\lambda^{K}\right)
solves an equation of the form

\displaystyle\Box h_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=Q(\partial h_{\lambda},\partial h_{\lambda})+\lambda^{K}\Phi^{(\cdot)}

(1.9)

where
Q
is the null form and
\lambda^{K}\Phi^{(\cdot)}
denotes terms of orders
\lambda^{K}
depending polynomially on the high-frequency waves
\Phi^{(k)}
and their derivatives. Note that we deliberately forgot a lot of terms in (
1.9
), since from the point of view of the dependence in
\lambda
the quadratic terms in
\partial h_{\lambda}
are the most dangerous.

In (
1.8
), the degree of precision of the expansion defining
\Phi_{\lambda}
is given by the integer
K
. Our work shows that we need
K\geq 4
to close the bootstrap procedure for (
1.9
). This is due to the method used to prove global existence to (
1.9
), i.e the vector field method. Indeed, we want
h_{\lambda}
to satisfy bootstrap assumptions of the form
\left\|\partial Z^{I}h_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{K-|I|}
(where
Z
denotes any Minkowskian vector fields, see (
2.3
)). This mimics a high-frequency behaviour while
h_{\lambda}
is not oscillating. In order to improve these bootstrap assumptions, we will recover decay from the Klainerman-Sobolev inequality. This inequality allows one to bound
|f|
by
\left\|Z^{3}f\right\|_{L^{2}}
with appropriate decay rates, which we omit in this introduction (see Proposition
2.2
). The bootstrap assumptions for
h_{\lambda}
therefore imply that
|\partial h_{\lambda}|\lesssim\lambda^{K-4}
. This explains the lower bound on the degree of precision of the ansatz
K\geq 4
.

As a final remark on our strategy of proof, let us explain how we get decay for the high-frequency waves
\Phi^{(k)}
, which appear as source terms in (
1.9
). As explained above, the high-frequency waves solve a hierarchy of transport equation of the form

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)f=g

(1.10)

with a RHS depending on the lower order high-frequency waves (thus respecting the triangular structure of the system). This equation rewrites
(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r})(rf)=rg
, from which we get
|f|\lesssim r^{-1}
if the RHS satisfies
|g|\lesssim r^{-3}
. See Proposition
5.2
for a precise statement. In the hierarchy of transport equations, the most dangerous term is
\Box\Phi^{(k)}
, i.e comes from the third line in (
1.6
). Schematically if we only consider this term we have

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)\Phi^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\Box\Phi^{(k)}.

Therefore, in order to apply Proposition
5.2
we need
\left|\Box\Phi^{(k)}\right|\lesssim r^{-3}
. We can’t get this decay by commuting
\Box
with the transport operator
\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}
since it would only give
\left|\Box\Phi^{(k)}\right|\lesssim r^{-1}
. Instead we compute and estimate
\Box\Phi^{(k)}
directly from
\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}
in Lemma
A.2
. See Remark
5.2
for a more detailled discussion of this procedure.

1.3
A toy model for general relativity

Here we present our principal motivation for studying the global existence of high-frequency solutions to (
1.1
).

1.3.1
The Einstein vacuum equations in wave coordinates

The Einstein equations are the main equations of the general relativity theory, they spell out the link between the curvature of the spacetime and the matter and energy it contains. On a Lorentzian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
and in the vacuum case they can be written in an elegant and compact form:

\mathrm{Ric}(g)=0,

(1.11)

where
\mathrm{Ric}(g)
is the Ricci tensor of the metric
g
. In order to define a initial value problem for this equation, we need to specify a coordinate system on
\mathcal{M}
. Noticed by Einstein in 1916 and used by Choquet-Bruhat to show local existence of the Einstein system in the 1950’s, the
wave coordinates
define the most common coordinate system used in the study of the Einstein equations. In this coordinate system, the equation (
1.11
) becomes

\Box_{g}g_{\alpha\beta}=P_{\alpha\beta}(\partial g,\partial g),

(1.12)

where
\Box_{g}
is the wave operator associated to
g
and
P_{\alpha\beta}
is a quadratic non-linearity. Thus, the Einstein vacuum equations are recast as a quasi-linear hyperbolic system for the metric coefficients, which allows one to define and study a Cauchy problem (see
[
8
]
for the first proof of local well-posedness using wave coordinates).

One of the major question in general relativity is the question of the
stability
of particular solutions, such as the Minkowski spacetime, which, thanks to (
1.12
) reduces to a hyperbolic long-time existence problem. As it was proved by Choquet-Bruhat in
[
4
]
, the non-linearity in (
1.12
) does not satisfy the null condition presented in Section
1.2
. Note that the Einstein vacuum equations do satisfy a null condition through a dynamical gauge choice, see the seminal work
[
7
]
. However, the Einstein vacuum equations in wave gauge satisfy what Lindblad and Rodnianski call a
weak null condition
, allowing them to prove the non-linear stability of the Minkowski spacetime in this gauge in
[
15
]
.

Therefore, studying the semi-linear equation
\Box u=Q(\partial u,\partial u)
where
Q
is a null form is a toy model for the Einstein vacuum equations: it is a semi-linear equation instead of a quasi-linear equation, with the quadratic part being a null form instead of merely satisfying the weak null condition.

1.3.2
Burnett’s conjecture

The Einstein vacuum equations are highly non-linear, which a priori allows
backreaction
to occur. This phenomenon can be described as follows: if a sequence of metrics
(g_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
converges in some sense to a background metric
g_{0}
, and if for all
\lambda
we have
\mathrm{Ric}(g_{\lambda})=0
, do we have
\mathrm{Ric}(g_{0})=0
? Said differently, can we pass to the limit in the Ricci tensor? Identifying the potential limit matter models occuring because of backreaction is called the Burnett conjecture, and was introduced by Burnett in
[
2
]
.

If the convergence
g_{\lambda}\to g_{0}
happens in a very strong topology then the answer is obviously positive, but if the convergence is weak enough, we might obtain non-zero contributions from quadratic terms like
\partial(g_{\lambda}-g_{0})\partial(g_{\lambda}-g_{0})
. In the framework chosen by Burnett, the convergence is the following:
g_{\lambda}
converges strongly in
L^{\infty}
and
\partial g_{\lambda}
converges weakly in
L^{2}
. High-frequency ansatz of the form (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) precisely enjoy this type of convergence, and have been used to tackle Burnett’s conjecture locally in time by Huneau and Luk in
[
9
]
. This fully motivates the study of high-frequency solutions to (
1.1
).

Remark 1.2
.

Even though the high-frequency solution
\Phi_{\lambda}
converges in the Burnett sense to
\Phi^{(0)}
, no backreaction occurs in the case of the equation (
1.1
), meaning that the background limit solution
\Phi^{(0)}
also satisfy (
1.1
) (see also (
BG
) in the reduced system). As Choquet-Bruhat noticed in
[
4
]
, this is linked to the null condition satisfied by (
1.1
). Indeed, backreaction could only occur through the non-oscillating contributions of the squared sinus in (
1.7
), but as we saw (
1.7
) vanishes because
Q
is a null form.

The absence of backreaction for (
1.1
) takes nothing away from the study of high-frequency solutions to this equation, as it is a first step towards proving the stability of high-frequency perturbation of some background metric
g_{0}
close to Minkowski for the equation (
1.12
), which would require to incorporate the ideas of
[
15
]
to the high-frequency setting presented in this article.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we describe our geometric and analytic setting, and introduce the key estimates used in this article.

2.1
Notations and function spaces

2.1.1
Coordinates and derivatives

We work in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the usual global coordinates system
(t=x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
. We also introduce the null coordinates

s=t+r\quad\text{and}\quad q=r-t.

For
t\geq 0
we use the standard notation
\Sigma_{t}\vcentcolon=\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We also define the following subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
for some
R>0
:

\displaystyle A^{R}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}~\middle|~R^{-1}\leq q\leq R\right\},

\displaystyle B^{R}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}~\middle|~q\leq R\right\},

and we set
A_{0}^{R}\vcentcolon=A^{R}\cap\Sigma_{0}
. If
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
r=|x|
, recall that
\partial_{r}=\sum_{i=1,2,3}\omega_{i}\partial_{i}
where
\omega_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{r}
. If
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a scalar function, then we define its spacetime gradient by the vector
\partial f=(\partial_{t}f,\partial_{1}f,\partial_{2}f,\partial_{3}f)
.
The ”good” derivatives are defined to be

\overline{\partial}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r})\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\partial}_{i}=\partial_{i}-\frac{x_{i}}{r}\partial_{r}.

As we will see, they enjoy better decay than the other derivatives. In this article we consider
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued functions for some
d\geq 1
, i.e vectorial functions of the form
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
. For such a function we define

\displaystyle|\partial u|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=0,\dots,3\\
i=1,\dots,d\end{subarray}}|\partial_{\alpha}u_{i}|\quad\text{and}\quad|\overline{\partial}u|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=0,\dots,3\\
i=1,\dots,d\end{subarray}}|\overline{\partial}_{\alpha}u_{i}|.

2.1.2
Null forms

Now we define the class of quadratic non-linearties we consider in this article, i.e the classical null forms introduced by Klainerman.

Definition 2.1
.

A quadratic form
Q
is said to be a null quadratic form (also called null form in the sequel) if it is a linear combination of
Q_{0}
and
Q_{\alpha\beta}
where

Q_{\alpha\beta}(X,Y)=X_{\alpha}Y_{\beta}-X_{\beta}Y_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{0}(X,Y)=-X_{0}Y_{0}+\delta^{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}

for two vector fields
X,Y
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\alpha,\beta=0,1,2,3
.

One can show that the null forms defined in this way are precisely the quadratic forms on the tangent bundle of Minkowski spacetime vanishing when contracted twice with the same null vector field.

Since the spacetime gradient of a scalar function is a vector field on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, this definition allows us to consider
Q(\partial u,\partial v)
for
u,v
two
scalar
functions. However in this article, we would like to consider
vectorial
functions. Therefore if
u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
and
v:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
with
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d^{\prime}})
, and if
Q_{k\ell}
are null forms, we define

\displaystyle Q(\partial u,\partial v)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\dots,d\\
\ell=1,\dots,d^{\prime}\end{subarray}}Q_{k\ell}(\partial u_{k},\partial v_{\ell}).

(2.1)

With this notation, we can write down properly the system studied in this article. For
d\geq 1
, we want to solve the system

\displaystyle\Box u_{i}=Q_{i}(\partial u,\partial u)

where
u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vectorial function, i.e
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
, and where
Q_{i}
is as in (
2.1
) for
i=1,\dots,d
. Though the Einstein equations motivates the study of a system of wave equations in this article, this plays no particular role here and from now on (with the exception of the statement of the main result) we will drop the indexes relative to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and simply use the condensed notation

\displaystyle\Box u=Q(\partial u,\partial u).

The null forms are in some sense the best quadratic non-linearity we can hope for in the sense that in each product
\partial u\partial u
, at least one of the derivatives is a good one, meaning that:

|Q(\partial u,\partial v)|\lesssim|\partial u||\overline{\partial}v|+|\overline{\partial}u||\partial v|.

(2.2)

2.1.3
Function spaces

In terms of function spaces, we use the usual
L^{p}
,
C^{m}
and Sobolev spaces defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, together with weighted Sobolev spaces, defined by

Definition 2.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
1<p<\infty
,
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
. The weighted Sobolev space
W^{m,p}_{\delta}
is the completion of
C^{\infty}_{0}
under the norm

\|u\|_{W^{m,p}_{\delta}}=\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta+\beta}\nabla^{\beta}u\right\|_{L^{p}}.

We will use the notation
H^{m}_{\delta}=W^{m,2}_{\delta}
. The weighted Hölder space
C^{m}_{\delta}
is the completion of
C^{m}
under the norm

\|u\|_{C^{m}_{\delta}}=\sum_{|\beta|\leq m}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta+\beta}\nabla^{\beta}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Note that we defined scalar functions spaces. However by setting

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{X}=\sum_{i=1,\dots,d}\left\|u_{i}\right\|_{X}

for
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
and
X
any previously defined space, we extend those spaces and the corresponding norms to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued functions. A weighted equivalent of the Sobolev embedding holds for those spaces (see
[
5
]
for a proof):

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
s,m\in\mathbb{N}
. If
s\geq 2
and
\beta\leq\delta+\frac{3}{2}
, then we have the continuous embedding

H^{s+m}_{\delta}\xhookrightarrow{}C^{m}_{\beta}.

2.2
Minkowski vector fields

We consider the set of Minkowski vector fields

\left\{\partial_{\alpha},S=t\partial_{t}+r\partial_{r},\Omega_{\alpha\beta}=x_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}-x_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\right\}

(2.3)

and denote by
Z^{I}
any product of
|I|
elements of this set, where
I
is an 11-dimensional integer. Note that since
x_{0}=-x^{0}
, we have
\Omega_{0i}=-t\partial_{i}-x_{i}\partial_{t}
. These vector fields allow us to recover the usual derivatives
\partial
while gaining a weight, whose nature explain why the derivatives
\overline{\partial}
are called ”good” derivatives:

|\partial u|\lesssim\frac{1}{1+|q|}\sum_{|I|=1}|Z^{I}u|\quad\text{and}\quad|\overline{\partial}u|\lesssim\frac{1}{1+s}\sum_{|I|=1}|Z^{I}u|.

(2.4)

The Minkowski vector fields enjoy good commutativity properties with the wave operator:

[\Box,Z]=0

except for
S
, which satisfies
[\Box,S]=2\Box
. This implies that

|\Box Z^{I}u|\lesssim\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|}|Z^{I^{\prime}}\Box u|.

(2.5)

The Minkowski vector fields behave nicely with the null forms, in the sense that the following holds:

|Z^{I}Q(\partial u,\partial v)|\lesssim\sum_{|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq|I|}|Q(\partial Z^{I_{1}}u,\partial Z^{I_{2}}v)|

(2.6)

2.3
Useful estimates

In this section we collect different estimates crucially used in this paper.

2.3.1
Weighted Klainerman-Sobolev inequality

We start by an estimate which allows us to bound the
L^{\infty}
norm of a function knowing the
L^{2}
norm of some of its derivatives with weights.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider the weight

w(q)\vcentcolon=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}1+(1+|q|)^{-\alpha}\quad\text{for}\quad q<0\\
1+(1+|q|)^{\beta}\quad\text{for}\quad q>0\end{array}\right.

with
1<\beta<3
and
\alpha>0
. Let
f
be compactly supported, we have

|w^{\frac{1}{2}}(q)f|\lesssim\frac{1}{(1+s)\sqrt{1+|q|}}\sum_{|I|\leq 3}\left\|w^{\frac{1}{2}}(q)Z^{I}f\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(2.7)

This is the so-called weighted Klainerman-Sobolev inequality, for which a proof can be found in
[
15
]
. The assumption on
f
in the previous proposition can be relaxed: the proposition holds as long as the RHS of (
2.7
) is finite.

2.3.2
Weighted energy estimate

As is standard, our strategy of proof relies on energy estimates for the wave operator
\Box
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
w(q)
be a non-decreasing weight function. Then

\int_{\Sigma_{t}}|\partial\varphi|^{2}w(q)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}}|\overline{\partial}\varphi|^{2}w^{\prime}(q)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau\lesssim\int_{\Sigma_{0}}|\partial\varphi|^{2}w(q)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}}|\Box\varphi\partial_{t}\varphi|w(q)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau.

This lemma is the semi-linear equivalent of Lemma 6.1 in
[
15
]
. It gives a
weighted
energy estimate, with a weight depending only on
q
. This is called the
ghost weight
method and was first introduced by Alinhac in
[
1
]
. Depending on the choice of the weight, it gives us extra decay for solutions of a wave equation in the region
q>0
, i.e the exterior of the light-cone. The other advantage of this method is the presence of a space-time integral on the LHS involving only the ”good” derivatives of the solution.

The usual energy estimate for the wave operator is obtained when
w(q)\equiv 1
, and one of the standard application of this case is the
finite speed of propagation
for solutions of semi-linear wave equation.

Lemma 2.2
.

We define

C_{t_{0},x_{0}}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}~\middle|~0\leq t\leq t_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad|x-x_{0}|\leq t_{0}-t\right\}

and
B(x_{0},t_{0})
is the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
centered at
x_{0}
of radius
t_{0}
.
Let
P:\mathbb{R}^{3+1}\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function such that

|P(X)|\lesssim|X|+|X|^{2}.

Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
u:\mathbb{R}^{3+1}\to\mathbb{R}
a solution of
\Box u=P(\partial u)
in
C_{t_{0},x_{0}}
. If
(u,\partial_{t}u)
initially vanishes on
B(x_{0},t_{0})
, then
u_{|C_{t_{0},x_{0}}}=0
.

This lemma will be used to localize the different terms in the decomposition of our solution (see (
4.1
)).

3
Main result

We now state the main result of this article.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
N\geq 26
,
R>0
,
-\frac{1}{2}<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
,
F_{0}\in C^{N-4}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in
A_{0}^{R}
and
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\in H^{N+1}_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{N}_{\delta+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\|F_{0}\|_{C^{N-4}}+\left\|\varphi_{0}\right\|_{H^{N+1}_{\delta}}+\left\|\varphi_{1}\right\|_{H^{N}_{\delta+1}}\leq\varepsilon.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(N,R,\delta)>0
such that if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
there exists a unique global solution
\Phi_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Box\Phi_{\lambda,i}&=&Q_{i}(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})\\
\Phi_{\lambda|t=0}&=&\varphi_{0}+\lambda F_{0}\cos\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)\\
\partial_{t}\Phi_{\lambda|t=0}&=&\varphi_{1}+F_{0}\sin\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda\tilde{F}_{0,\lambda}\end{array}\right.

where
Q_{i}
is a null form and
\tilde{F}_{0,\lambda}
is supported in
A^{R}_{0}
depending only on
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},F_{0})
and which satisfies
\left\|\tilde{F}_{0,\lambda}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon
. The function
\Phi_{\lambda}
admits the decomposition

\Phi_{\lambda}=\varphi+\lambda F\cos\left(\frac{t-r}{\lambda}\right)+\lambda^{2}\tilde{F}_{\lambda},

where

•

\varphi\in H^{N+1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies
\Box\varphi_{i}=Q_{i}(\partial\varphi,\partial\varphi)
and
(\varphi,\partial_{t}\varphi)_{|t=0}=(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
,

•

F\in C^{N-4}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is supported in
A^{R}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)F_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,\ell=1,\dots,d}F_{k}Q_{i}(\partial(t-r),\partial\varphi_{\ell})

and
F_{|t=0}=F_{0}
,

•

\tilde{F}_{\lambda}
is supported in
B^{R}
and it is such that
|\tilde{F}_{\lambda}|\lesssim\varepsilon
and
|\partial\tilde{F}_{\lambda}|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The main feature of this theorem is that the smallness constant
\varepsilon_{0}
does not depend on
\lambda
, which is allowed to take any value in
(0,1]
. In addition to the nature of the decomposition of
\Phi_{\lambda}
, this is at the heart of the high-frequency nature of the solution we construct: if we let
\lambda
tends to 0, the high order norms of
\Phi_{\lambda}
(precisely the
W^{\infty,p}
norm for
p>1
) blows up but this is not compensated by letting
\varepsilon_{0}
go to 0 as well. Therefore, the limit of
\Phi_{\lambda}
as
\lambda
tends to 0 is meaningful, and we have:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\to\varphi,\quad\text{uniformly in $L^{\infty}$,}

\displaystyle\partial\Phi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\partial\varphi,\quad\text{weakly in $L^{2}$.}

4
Construction of the ansatz

The heart of the proof of Theorem
3.1
is to construct a high-frequency ansatz for the solution
\Phi_{\lambda}
. The main purpose of this ansatz is to capture the creation of higher-order harmonics through the quadratic non-linearity. Those harmonics may also include non-oscillating terms. As explained in the introduction, we choose an ansatz with a
half-chessboard
shape. More precisely we consider a solution
\Phi_{\lambda}
of the form

\Phi_{\lambda}=\varphi+\sum_{2\leq k\leq K-1}\lambda^{k}\psi^{(k)}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\leq K}\lambda^{k}F^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+h_{\lambda},

(4.1)

where
K\geq 2
is an integer and where we use the following notation for and
k,i\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}=\left\{{\begin{array}[]{cc}\sin\left(\frac{i(t-r)}{\lambda}\right)&\textrm{ if }k+i\textrm{ is odd,}\\
\cos\left(\frac{i(t-r)}{\lambda}\right)&\textrm{ if }k+i\textrm{ is even.}\end{array}}\right.

(4.2)

Besides from being at the very heart of what we call an half-chessboard shaped ansatz, this notation is useful to capture the behaviours of the standard trigonometric functions regarding products and derivation (see Lemma
4.1
). A bit of vocabulary:

•

\varphi
is called the
background
solution,

•

the functions
\psi^{(\ell)}
are called the
non-oscillating
terms of the ansatz, whereas
F^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}
are the
oscillating
terms,

•

the function
h_{\lambda}
is the remainder of the ansatz and is non-oscillating, however it mimics a high-frequency behaviour, i.e it loses one power of
\lambda
for each derivatives, a fact clearly seen on the schematic estimates it will satisfy:

\left\|\partial^{m}h_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{K+1-m}\quad\text{for}\quad m\geq 1.

The degree of precision of the ansatz (
4.1
) is given by the integer
K
. Our work is valid for all values of
K\geq 2
, but shows that global existence as a solution of (
1.1
) requires
K\geq 4
. As explained in the introduction, this is due to the Klainerman-Sobolev inequality.

Remark 4.1
.

Since we adopt the standard convention that sums over empty sets vanish, there are no
\psi^{(\ell)}
functions in the ansatz if
K=2
. However, one major interest of our result is precisely the freedom we have on
K
: even though we are interested in a lower bound on
K
to ensure the global existence of
\Phi_{\lambda}
, our proof also shows that we can describe the propagation of high-frequency waves with an arbitrary large precision.

Remark 4.2
.

In the rest of the article we will use the following convention:
\psi^{(1)}=0
and
F^{(k,i)}=0
if
i>k
or
k=0
. This will be useful in the sequel not to be too bothered with complicated sums.

Our strategy of proof is to derive formally from
\Box\Phi_{\lambda}=Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})
a system of equations for the unknowns
(\varphi,\psi^{(\ell)},F^{(k,i)},h_{\lambda})
and prove global existence for this system, called in the rest of this article the
reduced system
. Let us start by stating a technical lemma summarizing some basic properties of the trigonometric functions
\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}
regarding products, the wave operator and the null forms.

Remark 4.3
.

In the next lemma and in the rest of the article (including the technical next section), we won’t write the numerical constants which appear in the computations, meaning for example that an equality like (
4.3
) has to be understood in the following sense: there exists real constants
A=A(k,\ell,i,j)
and
B=B(k,\ell,i,j)
such that

\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}=A\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell,i+j)}+B\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-\ell,i-j)}.

Though these numerical constants can depend on every indexes appearing in (
4.1
), they are not allowed to depend on
\lambda
, meaning that we will always keep trace of
\lambda
in the formal computations.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k,\ell,i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f
and
g
any function and
Q
any null form. We have
\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}=\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(|k|,|i|)}
and

\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}=\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell,i-j)},

(4.3)

\Box\left(g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\right)=\Box g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathscr{L}g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)},

(4.4)

Q\left(\partial\left(f\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\right),\partial g\right)=Q(\partial f,\partial g)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+\frac{1}{\lambda}f\omega\overline{\partial}g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)},

(4.5)

\displaystyle Q\left(\partial\left(f\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\right),\partial\left(g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=Q(\partial f,\partial g)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda}f\omega\overline{\partial}g\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}+\frac{1}{\lambda}g\omega\overline{\partial}f\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell-1,j)},

where
\mathscr{L}
is the following transport operator

\mathscr{L}\vcentcolon=\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}

and where
\omega\overline{\partial}
stands for a linear combination of
\omega_{i}\overline{\partial}_{\alpha}
for
i=1,2,3
and
\alpha=0,1,2,3
.

Proof.

The proof of (
4.3
) is a direct rewriting with our notation (
4.2
) of the usual linearization formulas

\displaystyle 2\cos(a)\cos(b)

\displaystyle=\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b),

\displaystyle 2\sin(a)\cos(b)

\displaystyle=\sin(a-b)+\sin(a+b),

\displaystyle 2\sin(a)\sin(b)

\displaystyle=\cos(a-b)-\cos(a+b).

The proof of (
4.4
) follows from a direct computation, and even though
\Box
is a second order operator, we don’t lose two powers of
\lambda
since the phase
t-r
satisfies the eikonal equation for the Minkowski metric, i.e

\displaystyle m^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}(t-r)\partial_{\beta}(t-r)=0.

(4.7)

The proof of (
4.5
) follows from the computations

\displaystyle Q_{0}(\partial(t-r),\partial h)

\displaystyle=-2\overline{\partial}_{0}h,

\displaystyle Q_{0i}(\partial(t-r),\partial h)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{i}h+2\omega_{i}\overline{\partial}_{0}h,

\displaystyle Q_{ij}(\partial(t-r),\partial h)

\displaystyle=\omega_{j}\overline{\partial}_{i}h-\omega_{i}\overline{\partial}_{j}h,

(which holds for any scalar function
h
) and the definition of a null form as in Definition
2.1
.

The formula (
4.6
) follows directly from (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Note that even though a null form only involves first order derivatives, we would expect some
\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
terms in (
4.6
) since
Q(\partial u,\partial v)
is a quadratic expression. It is not the case since null forms are precisely the quadratic forms vanishing on null vector fields in Minkowski space, and (
4.7
) precisely means that the space-time gradient of
t-r
is a null vector field.

Remark 4.5
.

Since
|\partial^{|\alpha|}\omega_{i}|\lesssim 1
in the region
\{r\geq\frac{1}{R}\}
for any multi-index
\alpha
and since formulas (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) will be applied with
f
supported in
A^{R}
, the
\omega
factors in (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) are irrelevant in our estimates and we omit them in the sequel.

4.1
High-frequency expansion of the semi-linear wave equation

In this section, we plug the ansatz (
4.1
) into the equation

\displaystyle\Box\Phi_{\lambda}=Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda}).

(4.8)

The computations are rather tedious, and we present them in the form of two lemmas, one for the LHS of (
4.8
) and one for its RHS.

4.1.1
Expansion of the wave operator

We start with the RHS of (
4.8
).

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\Phi_{\lambda}
is defined by (
4.1
) we have

\displaystyle\Box\Phi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq p\leq K}\lambda^{p}\left(\Box\Phi_{\lambda}\right)^{(p)}

with

\displaystyle\left(\Box\Phi_{\lambda}\right)^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\Box\varphi+\mathscr{L}F^{(1,1)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(0,1)}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\left(\Box\Phi_{\lambda}\right)^{(K)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq K}\Box F^{(K,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(K,i)}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{K}}\Box h_{\lambda}

(4.10)

and

\displaystyle\left(\Box\Phi_{\lambda}\right)^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq p+1}\left(\mathscr{L}F^{(p+1,i)}+\Box F^{(p,i)}\right)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i)}+\Box\psi^{(p)}

(4.11)

for
1\leq p\leq K-1
.

The proof of this lemma is left to the reader and is an application of (
4.4
) together with the convention stated in Remark
4.2
.

4.1.2
Expansion of the null form

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\Phi_{\lambda}
is defined by (
4.1
) we have

\displaystyle Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\lambda^{k}Q^{(k)}+\lambda^{K}Q^{(\geq K)}

with

\displaystyle Q^{(0)}

\displaystyle=Q(\partial\varphi,\partial\varphi)+F^{(1,1)}\overline{\partial}\varphi\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(0,1)},

(4.12)

\displaystyle Q^{(\geq K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{K}}\mathcal{N}(h_{\lambda})+Q(\partial F,\partial\varphi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{+}\left(F\overline{\partial}F\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial F,\partial F)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial F,\partial\psi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+F\overline{\partial}\psi\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial\psi,\partial\psi)\right),

and

\displaystyle Q^{(p)}

\displaystyle=Q(\partial\psi^{(p)},\partial\varphi)+\sum_{2\leq k\leq p-2}Q(\partial\psi^{(k)},\partial\psi^{(p-k)})

(4.14)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{1\leq i\leq p+1}\left(F^{(p+1,i)}\overline{\partial}\varphi+Q(\partial F^{(p,i)},\partial\varphi)\right)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i)}

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\leq p\\
1\leq j\leq p-k+1\end{subarray}}\left(Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial F^{(p-k,j)})+F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}F^{(p-k+1,j)}+F^{(p-k+1,j)}\overline{\partial}F^{(k,i)}\right)\left(\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i-j)}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq p-1\\
1\leq i\leq k+1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\left(F^{(k+1,i)}\overline{\partial}\psi^{(p-k)}+Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial\psi^{(p-k)})\right)

for
1\leq p\leq K-1
, and where
\mathcal{N}(h_{\lambda})
is defined in (
4.16
).

Remark 4.6
.

In this lemma, we use the following notations for clarity:

•

F
or
\psi
denotes linear combinations of terms of the families
F^{(k,i)}
or
\psi^{(\ell)}
,

•

\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}
denotes linear combinations of terms of the family
\mathbb{T}^{(k,i)}_{\lambda}
,

•

in front of a linear combination, the symbol
\lambda^{+}
means that the coefficients of the linear combination contains some positive powers of
\lambda
.

Proof.

Using (
4.5
)-(
4.6
), we obtain by direct expansion

\displaystyle Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=Q(\partial\varphi,\partial\varphi)+Q(\partial h_{\lambda},\partial h_{\lambda})+Q(\partial h_{\lambda},\partial\varphi)+\lambda^{+}Q(\partial(F\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}),\partial h_{\lambda})+\lambda^{+}Q(\partial\psi,\partial h_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\leq K}\lambda^{k}Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial\varphi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\leq K}\lambda^{k-1}F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}\varphi\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{2\leq k\leq K-1}\lambda^{k}Q(\partial\psi^{(k)},\partial\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq K\\
1\leq i\leq k\\
1\leq j\leq\ell\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k+\ell-1}\left(F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}F^{(\ell,j)}+F^{(\ell,j)}\overline{\partial}F^{(k,i)}\right)\left(\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell-1,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell-1,i-j)}\right)

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq K\\
1\leq i\leq k\\
1\leq j\leq\ell\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k+\ell}Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial F^{(\ell,j)})\left(\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell,i-j)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\leq K\\
2\leq\ell\leq K-1\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k+\ell}Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial\psi^{(\ell)})\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\leq K\\
2\leq\ell\leq K-1\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k+\ell-1}F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}\psi^{(\ell)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{2\leq k,\ell\leq K-1}\lambda^{k+\ell}Q(\partial\psi^{(k)},\partial\psi^{(\ell)})

Note that in order to simplify the terms
F\overline{\partial}F
we used (
4.3
) and its consequence

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell,j)}=\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(\ell-1,j)}=\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell-1,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k+\ell-1,i-j)}.

Now we want to regroup terms by their
\lambda
powers. For this we use the following facts

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq K}\lambda^{k+\ell}a_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{2\leq p\leq K-1}\lambda^{p}\sum_{1\leq k\leq p-1}a_{k,p-k}+O\left(\lambda^{K}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq K\\
2\leq\ell\leq K-1\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k+\ell}a_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{3\leq p\leq K-1}\lambda^{p}\sum_{1\leq k\leq p-2}a_{k,p-k}+O\left(\lambda^{K}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq k,\ell\leq K-1}\lambda^{k+\ell}a_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{4\leq p\leq K-1}\lambda^{p}\sum_{2\leq k\leq p-2}a_{k,p-k}+O\left(\lambda^{K}\right)

which holds for every families
(a_{k,\ell})_{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}
. Therefore if
1\leq p\leq K-1
we have

\displaystyle Q^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}Q(\partial F^{(p,i)},\partial\varphi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i)}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq p+1}F^{(p+1,i)}\overline{\partial}\varphi\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i)}+Q(\partial\psi^{(p)},\partial\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\leq p-1\\
1\leq j\leq p-k\end{subarray}}Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial F^{(p-k,j)})\left(\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i-j)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\leq p-1}Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial\psi^{(p-k)})\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}+\sum_{2\leq k\leq p-2}Q(\partial\psi^{(k)},\partial\psi^{(p-k)})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\leq p\\
1\leq j\leq p-k+1\end{subarray}}\left(F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}F^{(p-k+1,j)}+F^{(p-k+1,j)}\overline{\partial}F^{(k,i)}\right)\left(\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i+j)}+\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i-j)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k\leq p-1}F^{(k,i)}\overline{\partial}\psi^{(p-k+1)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k-1,i)}

where we used several times our convention on

It remains to compute

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(h_{\lambda})=Q(\partial h_{\lambda},\partial h_{\lambda})+Q(\partial h_{\lambda},\partial\varphi)+\lambda^{+}Q(\partial(F\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}),\partial h_{\lambda})+\lambda^{+}Q(\partial\psi,\partial h_{\lambda}).

(4.16)

4.2
The reduced system

As the two previous lemmas show, solving

\Box\Phi_{\lambda}=Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})

is equivalent to solving the following high-frequency hierarchy:

\displaystyle(\Box\Phi_{\lambda})^{(p)}=Q^{(p)}

(4.17)

for
0\leq p\leq K-1
and

\displaystyle(\Box\Phi_{\lambda})^{(K)}=Q^{(\geq K)}.

(4.18)

Since
h_{\lambda}
is not-oscillating, (

\displaystyle\Box h_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(h_{\lambda})+\lambda^{K}Q(\partial F,\partial\varphi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+\lambda^{K}\Box F\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{K}\lambda^{+}\left(F\overline{\partial}F\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial F,\partial F)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial F,\partial\psi)\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+F\overline{\partial}\psi\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}+Q(\partial\psi,\partial\psi)\right)

For the remaining equations of order
\lambda^{p}
for
0\leq p\leq K-1
, note that both the LHS and RHS contain non-oscillating and oscillating terms, which we have to identify precisely.

For
p=0
it is quite straightforward: the non-oscillating part can be absorbed by the background equation

\displaystyle\Box\varphi=Q(\partial\varphi,\partial\varphi)

and the part oscillating like
\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(0,1)}
is absorbed thanks to a transport equation for

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}F^{(1,1)}=F^{(1,1)}\overline{\partial}\varphi.

If

leads to the creation of non-oscillating terms. This is a consequence of the presence of

in (

is even and

\psi^{(\ell)}
, indeed they are absorbed by

is non-oscillating, we only need
\psi^{(\ell)}
for

\psi^{(\ell)}=0
for

This has the following nice consequence: in the last term of

(for
1\leq p\leq K-1

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq p-1\\
1\leq i\leq k+1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(k,i)}\left(F^{(k+1,i)}\overline{\partial}\psi^{(p-k)}+Q(\partial F^{(k,i)},\partial\psi^{(p-k)})\right)

(since

and

for

\displaystyle Q^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq p+1}Q^{(p,i)}\mathbb{T}_{\lambda}^{(p,i)}+\pi Q^{(p)}

where

\displaystyle Q^{(p,i)}

\displaystyle=F^{(p+1,i)}\overline{\partial}\varphi+Q(\partial F^{(\leq p)},\partial\varphi)+Q(\partial F^{(\leq p)},\partial F^{(\leq p)})

\displaystyle\quad+F^{(\leq p)}\overline{\partial}F^{(\leq p)}+F^{(\leq p)}\overline{\partial}\psi^{(\leq p)}+Q(\partial F^{(\leq p)},\partial\psi^{(\leq p)})

\displaystyle\pi Q^{(p)}=Q(\partial\psi^{(p)},\partial\varphi)+Q(\partial\psi^{(\leq p-2)},\partial\psi^{(\leq p-2)})+Q(\partial F^{(\leq p)},\partial F^{(\leq p)})+F^{(\leq p)}\overline{\partial}F^{(\leq p)}.

Remark 4.10
.

According to Klainerman’s result, considering quadratic non-linearities with a null structure ensures global existence for the non-linear wave equation in space dimension 3. But from a high-frequency perspective, null forms are also of prime interest. Indeed, if
Q(\partial u,\partial v)
were not a null form, the expansion of
Q(\partial(f\mathbb{T}),\partial(g\mathbb{T}))
would contain a term of the form
fgQ(\partial(t-r),\partial(t-r))
. Looking at the decomposition of
Q(\partial\Phi_{\lambda},\partial\Phi_{\lambda})
, we see that
the equation on
\Phi^{(1)}
would be of the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\partial_{\theta}\Phi^{(1)}=\left(\partial_{\theta}\Phi^{(1)}\right)^{2}+\cdots

(4.23)

where
\partial_{\theta}
corresponds to derivatives with respect to the third variable of
\Phi^{(1)}
(see (
1.4
) for the formal definition of
\Phi^{(1)}
). The non-linear equation (
4.23
) requires
\Phi^{(1)}
to be described by a ”full” Fourier series, i.e

\displaystyle\Phi^{(1)}(t,x,\theta)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}F^{(1,\ell)}(t,x)\exp\left(i\ell\theta\right)

with an infinite amount of
F^{(1,\ell)}
non-zero. Though the equations for the higher order terms (i.e
\Phi^{(i)}
for
i\geq 1
) would remain linear equations, the triangular structure and the presence of
\Phi^{(1)}
as source term would also imply that all the
\Phi^{(i)}
are described by full Fourier series. Therefore the null structure allows us to consider a ”simple” ansatz, the so-called half-chessboard ansatz.

\displaystyle\left\|w_{1}\partial Z^{I_{1}}\varphi\overline{\partial}Z^{I_{2}}\varphi\partial_{t}Z^{I}\varphi\right\|_{L^{1}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C_{0}\varepsilon}{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left\|w_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\partial Z^{I_{1}}\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|w_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{t}Z^{I}\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\frac{C_{0}^{3}\varepsilon^{3}}{(1+\tau)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

Therefore, since
t\longmapsto(1+\tau)^{-1-\nu}
and
t\longmapsto(1+\tau)^{-\frac{3}{2}}
are both integrable over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
we obtain for
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq N}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|w_{1}\partial Z^{I_{1}}\varphi\overline{\partial}Z^{I_{2}}\varphi\partial_{t}Z^{I}\varphi\right\|_{L^{1}}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\lesssim C_{0}^{3}\varepsilon^{3}+C_{0}\varepsilon S(t).

Absorbing
C_{0}\varepsilon S(t)
into the LHS of (
5.5
) we have proved that

\displaystyle\left\|w_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\partial Z^{I}\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon+C\sqrt{C_{0}\varepsilon}C_{0}\varepsilon\qquad\text{for}\quad|I|\leq N,

with
C>0
a numerical constant. We now choose
C_{0}
such that
C\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
CC_{0}\varepsilon\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}
and
C\sqrt{C_{0}\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{4}
. Thus, we proved that the inequality (
5.2
) hold with a constant
\frac{C_{0}}{2}
instead of
C_{0}
, which contradict the maximality of

, thus proving that
T=T_{0}
. But if
T=T_{0}
, it implies that the energy is bounded up to the time
T_{0}
, implying that

, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.2

We now choose

Therefore, we improved (

∎

This concludes the induction and thus the proof of Proposition

(L-\eta)\phi=0
which is close to 1.

Definition B.1
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function supported in
\{\frac{1}{2R}\leq r\leq 2R\}
and such that
\chi_{|A_{0}}\equiv 1
. Using polar coordinates we define
\hat{\chi}
a cut-off function around
A^{R}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\hat{\chi}(t,r,\omega)=\chi(r-t,\omega).

Lemma B.1
.

There exists a function
\beta
vanishing outside of
\{\frac{1}{2R}\leq q\leq 2R\}
which satisfies

|\beta|\lesssim\varepsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad L(1+\beta)=(1+\beta)\eta\quad\text{in $A^{R}$}.

Proof.

We simply define
\beta
using polar coordinates

\beta(t,r,\omega)\vcentcolon=\exp\left(\hat{\chi}(t,r,\omega)\int_{0}^{t}\eta(t^{\prime},r-t+t^{\prime},\omega)\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)-1.

Thanks to our assumption on
\eta
,
\beta
satisfies the bound
|\beta|\lesssim\varepsilon
. Thus, if
\varepsilon
is small enough, we can bound
1+\beta
from below by
\frac{1}{2}
, and therefore
\ln(1+\beta)
is well-defined and satisfies clearly
L(\ln(1+\beta))=\eta
in
A^{R}
, which implies
L(1+\beta)=(1+\beta)\eta
.
∎

It is now easy to solve (
B.1
):

Lemma B.2
.

Let
f_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
supported in
A_{0}
and
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
supported in
A^{R}
. There exists a unique global solution
f
of (
B.1
) with initial data
f_{0}
. This solution is supported in
A^{R}
and satisfies

|f|\lesssim\sup_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(|f_{0}(x)|+(1+|x|)^{2}|g(t,x)|\right).

Proof.

The uniqueness follows easily from an energy estimate related to the operator
L+\eta
. To construct a solution, we use polar coordinates and set

\displaystyle f(t,r,\omega)\vcentcolon

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta(t,r,\omega)}\Bigg((1+\beta(0,r-t,\omega))f_{0}(r-t,\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}(1+\beta(s,r-t+s,\omega))g(s,r-t+s,\omega)\mathrm{d}s\Bigg).

By construction, we have
f_{|t=0}=f_{0}
and
L((1+\beta)f)=(1+\beta)g
, and
f
is supported in
A^{R}
. Let
(t,x)\in A^{R}
, since
L(1+\beta)=(1+\beta)\eta
in
A^{R}
we have:

(L+\eta)f(t,x)=\frac{1}{1+\beta(t,x)}L((1+\beta)f)(t,x)=g(t,x).

This proves the existence of a solution. The estimate on
|f|
comes from the expression of
f
, the estimate
\frac{1}{2}\leq 1+\beta\leq 2
and the support property of
g
(note that in
A^{R}
,
r
and
t
are equivalent).
∎

B.2
The transport operator
\mathscr{L}

Using the result of the previous section, we now solve the equation

\mathscr{L}f=f\mu+g

(B.2)

where

(1+r)^{2}|Z^{I}\mu|+(1+r)^{3}|Z^{I}g|\lesssim\varepsilon

(B.3)

for
|I|\leq M
, where
M
is an integer. We consider initial data
f_{0}
supported in
A_{0}
and satisfying
\left\|f_{0}\right\|_{C^{M}}\lesssim\varepsilon
. Our strategy is to first deduce some
a priori
estimates.

We first take advantage of
[\mathscr{L},\Omega]=0
:

Lemma B.3
.

If
f
is a smooth and global in time solution of (
B.2
) initially supported in
A_{0}
, then

|\Omega^{J}f|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1+r}\qquad\text{for}\quad|J|\leq M.

(B.4)

Proof.

We prove this lemma by strong induction on the value of
|J|
. If
|J|=0
, we just rewrite (
B.2
) as

(L-\mu)(rf)=rg

and apply Lemma
B.2
using (
B.3
). Assume now that the estimate (
B.4
) holds for all value of
|J|\leq k
with
k\leq M-1
and let
J
be a multi-index such that
|J|=k+1
. Since
[\mathscr{L},\Omega]=0
, the equation for
\Omega^{J}f
is

\mathscr{L}\Omega^{J}f=\Omega^{J}f\mu+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J_{1}+J_{2}=J\\
|J_{1}|\leq k\end{subarray}}\Omega^{J_{1}}f\Omega^{J_{2}}\mu+\Omega^{J}g

which rewrites

(L-\mu)(r\Omega^{J}f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J_{1}+J_{2}=J\\
|J_{1}|\leq k\end{subarray}}r\Omega^{J_{1}}f\Omega^{J_{2}}\mu+r\Omega^{J}g.

Because of the induction hypothesis and of (
B.3
), the RHS of this equation is bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}
. Thus, we can apply Lemma
B.2
and conclude the proof.
∎

Note that in the previous lemma, in order to apply Lemma
B.2
, we have to prove that the initial data for
r\Omega^{J}f
are bounded. Since the vector fields
\Omega
involve only spatial derivations, this boundedness property follows from
f
being supported in
A_{0}
and smooth. In the next lemma however, we fully use Proposition
A.1
and estimate
\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f
, again with the help of Lemma
B.2
. This requires to bound the initial data for
r\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f
, which may involve some time derivative. To find
\partial^{k}_{t}f_{|t=0}
, we simply use the equation (
B.2
) and
[\mathscr{L},\partial_{t}]=0
to derive the equation for
\partial^{k-1}_{t}f
which gives

\partial^{k}_{t}f=-\partial\partial_{t}^{k-1}f-\frac{\partial_{t}^{k-1}f}{r}+\sum_{i+j=k-1}\partial^{i}_{t}f\partial^{j}_{t}\mu+\partial^{k-1}_{t}g.

Therefore we obtain
\partial^{k}_{t}f_{|t=0}
by induction on
k
, and check easily their initial boundedness. With that in mind, we can estimate
\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f
:

Lemma B.4
.

If
f
is a smooth and global in time solution of (
B.2
) initially supported in
A_{0}
, then

|\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1+r}\qquad\text{for}\quad|I|+|J|\leq M.

(B.5)

Proof.

We prove this lemma by strong induction on the value of
|I|
. The case
|I|=0
is a consequence of Lemma
B.3
. Assume now that the estimate (
B.5
) holds for all value of
|I^{\prime}|\leq k
with
k\leq M-1
and all
J^{\prime}
such that
|I^{\prime}|+|J^{\prime}|\leq M
. Let
I
be a multi-index such that
|I|=k+1
and let
J
be a multi-index such that
|I|+|J|\leq M
. Formally, the equation on
\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f
is

(L-\mu)(r\Omega^{J}Z^{I}f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}+I_{2}=I\\
J_{1}+J_{2}=J\\
|I_{1}|\leq k\\
|J_{1}|\leq|J|-1\end{subarray}}r\Omega^{J_{1}}Z^{I_{1}}f\Omega^{J_{2}}Z^{I_{2}}\mu+r\Omega^{J}Z^{I}g+r[\mathscr{L},\Omega^{J}Z^{I}]f.

(B.6)

Using the induction hypothesis and (
B.3
), the first two terms in the RHS of (
B.6
) are bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}
. It remains to estimate the commutator. Using
[\mathscr{L},\Omega]=0
and Proposition
A.1
we have:

\displaystyle\ |r[\mathscr{L},\Omega^{J}Z^{I}]f|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|r\Omega^{J}\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}\left(\frac{a^{I,\ell}_{I^{\prime}}}{r}\overline{\partial}_{\ell}Z^{I^{\prime}}f+b^{I}_{I^{\prime}}Z^{I^{\prime}}\mathscr{L}f+\frac{c^{I}_{I^{\prime}}}{r^{2}}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}\left|\Omega^{J}\overline{\partial}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right|+\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}r\left|\Omega^{J}Z^{I^{\prime}}\mathscr{L}f\right|+\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}\frac{1}{r}\left|\Omega^{J}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right|

(B.7)

Since
|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1
we can use the induction hypothesis to obtain

\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}\frac{1}{r}\left|\Omega^{J}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}.

To estimate the terms involving
\mathscr{L}f
, we simply use (
B.2
), the induction hypothesis and (
B.3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}r\left|\Omega^{J}Z^{I^{\prime}}\mathscr{L}f\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1\\
I_{1}+I_{2}=I^{\prime}\\
J_{1}+J_{2}=J\end{subarray}}r|\Omega^{J_{1}}Z^{I_{1}}f\Omega^{J_{2}}Z^{I_{2}}\mu|+r|\Omega^{J}Z^{I}g|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}.

It remains to estimate the first terms in (
B.7
). Using
[\Omega,\overline{\partial}]\sim\overline{\partial}
and
\overline{\partial}_{\ell}=\frac{\omega^{k}}{r}\Omega_{\ell k}
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1}\left|\Omega^{J}\overline{\partial}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right|\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1\\
|J^{\prime}|\leq|J|+1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r}\left|\Omega^{J^{\prime}}Z^{I^{\prime}}f\right|

In this last sum, note that
|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1
and
|I^{\prime}|+|J^{\prime}|\leq|I|-1+|J|+1\leq M
. Thus we can use our induction hypothesis to bound this by
\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}
. In conclusion, the RHS of (
B.6
) is bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{(1+r)^{2}}
, and we can apply Lemma
B.2
and conclude the proof.
∎

In the previous lemma, the case
J=0
gives that a global smooth solution of (
B.2
) satisfies

|Z^{I}f|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1+r}\qquad\text{for}\quad|I|\leq M.

This
a priori
estimates allows us easily to prove the existence and uniqueness of a global solution to (
B.2
). We summarize this discussion in the following corollary:

Corollary B.1
.

Let
f_{0}
supported in
A_{0}
and such that and let
\mu
and
g
satisfy (
B.3
). There exists a unique global solution
f\in C^{M}
to (
B.2
) with initial data
f_{0}
. Moreover,
f
is supported in
A^{R}
and satisfies

|Z^{I}f|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1+r}\qquad\text{for}\quad|I|\leq M.
