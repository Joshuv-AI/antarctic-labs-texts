Abstract
. We consider the configuration space of points on the two-dimensional sphere that satisfy a specific system of quadratic equations. We construct periodic orbits in this configuration space using elliptic theta functions and show that they satisfy semi-discrete analogues of mKdV and sine-Gordon equations. The configuration space we investigate corresponds to the state space of a linkage mechanism known as the Kaleidocycle, and the constructed orbits describe the characteristic motion of the Kaleidocycle. Our approach is founded on the relationship between the deformation of spatial curves and integrable systems, offering an intriguing example where an integrable system generates an orbit in the space of real solutions to polynomial equations defined by geometric constraints.

1.
Introduction

Given a simple finite graph
(V,E)
with edge weights

\ell:E\to\mathbb{R}
,
its
d
-dimensional
realisation
is a map

\phi:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies the condition
|\phi(u)-\phi(v)|^{2}=\ell(uv)^{2}
for all edges
uv\in E
.
This mapping embeds the graph into
d
-dimensional Euclidean space while preserving the prescribed edge lengths.
The collection of all possible realisations forms a real algebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{d|V|}
, known as the configuration space of
(V,E,\ell)
. This space has been the subject of extensive research across various domains, including algebraic geometry, robotics, and mechanism theory
[
10
,
21
,
25
]
.
The primary motivation for this work is rooted in the
analysis of the motion of a particular class of linkage mechanisms called
Kaleidocycles

(Fig.
1
(Left)).
These mechanisms consist of
k
identical copies of equifacial tetrahedra connected by their opposing edges as hinge joints, forming a ring.
A state of a Kaleidocycle corresponds to a realisation of the graph formed by the vertices and edges of the tetrahedra (Eq. (
2.1
)), where
\ell
is
determined by the shape of the tetrahedron.
Its motion, disregarding self-collision, is described by a path in the configuration space.
Although this object has been studied by several authors
[
8
,
28
,
30
]
,
even its existence beyond simple classical cases has not been clarified.
Our work addresses this gap by providing an explicit construction of the object and its motion.

Our key observation is to associate each state of a Kaleidocycle with a closed spatial polygonal curve. This curve is formed by connecting the midpoints of the hinge edges.
The angles between the hinge edges are reminiscent of the torsion angle of a curve, and the turning angles are reminiscent of the curvature angle (§
2.1
).
In this geometric formulation using spatial curves,
the motion of a Kaleidocycle is modelled
by a segment-length and torsion-angle preserving deformation of the corresponding closed polygonal curve of a constant torsion angle.
We can then identify the configuration space of a given Kaleidocycle with a subspace of a direct product of two-spheres (
2.2
).
Note that it is not clear if there exists
\ell
(the shape of the tetrahedra) for a given
k
such that the configuration space is non-empty;
that is, if a Kaleidocycle exists.
We construct a one-dimensional subspace of the configuration space using elliptic theta functions, proving that there is a Kaleidocycle for any
k\geq 6
that can move.
The idea is to compare the functional identities of elliptic theta functions with
the compatibility conditions of the framing of a discrete spatial curve.

It was known in
[
19
]
that
the semi-discrete modified KdV (mKdV) and the sine-Gordon equations
[
19
]

are defined on the configuration space (see §
3.2
).
Therefore, a one-dimensional orbit
in the configuration space
is generated through the time evolution defined by
the system of ordinary differential equations.
The main result of this paper can also be viewed as providing a solution to the system through an explicit construction based on the
\tau
functions of the two-component KP hierarchy
[
11
]
.
We show that our construction of the motion of the Kaleidocycle (§
5
) indeed satisfies the system of the ordinary differential equations described in
[
19
]
.
The sweeping surface of the motion
corresponds to a semi-discrete analogue of the
discrete constant negative curvature surface
(
K-surface
)
studied in
[
2
,
16
]
.

Our work connects seemingly distant fields of integrable systems and kinematics of linkage
via an interesting object, Kaleidocycle.
It provides an interesting example, where
a flow generated by an integrable system on a real-algebraic set is explicitly integrated using elliptic theta functions.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
Section
2
introduces the definition of Kaleidocycles. As essential invariants to describe the state of a Kaleidocycle,
the signed curvature angle and torsion angle are defined together with the Frenet frame.
The state of
a Kaleidocycle is identified
with a solution to a system of quadratic equations corresponding to the geometric constraints on the binormal vectors.
Section
3
reviews the connection between the theory of curves and integrable systems.
The construction of discrete spatial curves and their Frenet frames in terms of the
\tau
functions is presented.
A previous result that describes the motion of a Kaleidocycle using semi-discrete integrable equations is recalled.
Section
4
constructs a set of
\tau
functions with three parameters
v,r,y
using elliptic theta functions.
The resulting curve is shown to have a constant torsion angle.
A condition for the resulting curve to be closed is
also discussed; for a given
k
, the appropriate choice of parameters yields a Kaleidocycle.
By introducing an additional parameter, a family of time-parametrised Kaleidocycles is constructed in Section
5
.
We then study the time evolution equations satisfied by the vertex positions and curvature angle of the Kaleidocycles and show connections to previous research.
Section
6
provides examples of Kaleidocycles and corresponding semi-discrete surfaces with figures.
We present two conjectures that are suggested
by numerical experiments.
The Kaleidocycles constructed in this paper seem to be Möbius Kaleidocycles (Conjecture
6.1
).

Most of the proofs of our theorems and propositions are
deferred to the appendix since
they require technically-involved computations
regarding elliptic theta functions.

2.
Algebraic and geometric models of Kaleidocycle

2.1.
Configuration space of Kaleidocycle

A Kaleidocycle consists of (typically six)
identical copies of equifacial tetrahedra
connected by their opposing edges as hinge joints to form a ring.
It can be folded from a sheet of paper and
makes an example of rigid origami; it rotates like a bubble ring while keeping all faces rigid.
The origin of Kaleidocycle is obscure but
one of the first appearances in the literature is in Bricard’s paper
[
3
]
,
where he considers an essentially equivalent closed kinematic chain,
now known as the Bricard 6R.
For nearly a hundred years, the geometry and kinematics of this object have attracted the attention of researchers (see
[
29
]
and the references therein).

We first define a generalisation of Kaleidocycle following
[
18
]
.
We consider
k
-copies of an equifacial tetrahedron in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
connected by opposing edges to form a ring.
Denote the vertex set by
\{0,1,\ldots,2k-1\}
such that the vertices
(2n,2n+1)

form a hinge edge and the vertices
(2n,2n+1,2n+2)
form one of the triangle faces.
Let
l_{01},l_{12},l_{20}
be the lengths of the sides of the triangle face.
A state of a
k
-Kaleidocycles of type

(l_{01},l_{12},l_{20})
is identified by the real solution

(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-1})\in(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{2k}

of the quadratic equations

(2.1)

\displaystyle|x_{2n}-x_{2n+1}|^{2}

\displaystyle=l^{2}_{01},

\displaystyle|x_{2n}-x_{2n+2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|x_{2n+1}-x_{2n+3}|^{2}=l^{2}_{02},

\displaystyle|x_{2n+1}-x_{2n+2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|x_{2n}-x_{2n+3}|^{2}=l^{2}_{12}.

Note that collision between tetrahedra is ignored.
The solution space of (
2.1
) admits an action of the Euclidean transformation group
E(3)
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and the expected dimension of the quotient is
3\cdot 2k-5k-\dim(E(3))=k-6
.
This simple counting estimate is often called
Maxwell’s
degree-of-freedom counting law
[
8
]
.
The Bricard 6R is a case of
k=6
.
Its configuration space has dimension one,
which is greater than the expected dimension of
0
,
giving an example of an
overconstrained

[
8
]
.
When
k\geq 7
, it is numerically observed that the configuration space degenerates to a circle when the angle between adjacent hinge edges is set to a critical value
[
20
]
.
†
†

Some of the findings of
[
20
]
are also published in
[
30
]
.

For
k>7
, this class of Kaleidocycles, named the Möbius Kaleidocycles, provides a rare example of
underconstrained
linkage mechanisms whose configuration space has less dimension than the expected one.

The state of a Kaleidocycle can be equivalently described by a spatial polygon or a
closed discrete spatial curve
.
Let
\gamma_{n}=(x_{2n}+x_{2n+1})/2\ (0\leq n<k-1)
be the mid-point of the hinge edge. We set

\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{0}
.
Since the tetrahedron is equifacial,
\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n}

is orthogonal to both
\widetilde{B}_{n}
and

\widetilde{B}_{n+1}
, where

\widetilde{B}_{n}=\dfrac{x_{2n+1}-x_{2n}}{|x_{2n+1}-x_{2n}|}
is the hinge direction.
By reordering the vertices, we can assume the inner product

\langle\widetilde{B}_{n-1},\widetilde{B}_{n}\rangle
is constant for

1\leq n<k
. Denote this constant by
\cos\lambda

with
0<\lambda<\pi
,
which we call the
torsion angle
.
We have two cases

\langle\widetilde{B}_{k-1},\widetilde{B}_{0}\rangle=\pm\cos\lambda
.
We say the Kaleidocycle is oriented (resp. non-oriented) when the value is
+\cos\lambda
(resp.
-\cos\lambda
).
We set
\widetilde{B}_{k}=\pm\widetilde{B}_{0}
so that

\langle\widetilde{B}_{k-1},\widetilde{B}_{k}\rangle=\cos\lambda
.
Furthermore, since
\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n}
is parallel to
\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n}
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n})=0
.
Conversely, the vertex coordinates
x_{n}
can be reconstructed from
\{\gamma_{n}\}
,
\{\widetilde{B}_{n}\}
and
(l_{01},l_{12},l_{20})
up to translation.
Therefore,

Definition 1
.

The configuration space of the
k
-Kaleidocycles with the torsion angle
\lambda

is the set

\{(\widetilde{B}_{0},\ldots,\widetilde{B}_{k})\in(S^{2})^{k+1}\}
satisfying the following conditions:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{B}_{n-1},\widetilde{B}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\lambda,\quad(1\leq n\leq k)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\pm\widetilde{B}_{0}.

A motion of a Kaleidocycle
is identified with a one-dimensional (time-parametrised) orbit in the space defined by (
2.1
) or
(
2.2
).

(Left) The classical 6-Kaleidocycle. (Right) Kaleidocycle as a discrete curve with its framing.

2.2.
Discrete spatial curve of a constant torsion angle

A discrete spatial curve with non-vanishing torsion angles can be described in terms of binormals.
Let
\widetilde{B}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow S^{2}

satisfy

\langle\widetilde{B}_{n},\widetilde{B}_{n-1}\rangle\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
A discrete spatial curve of unit segment-length
having
\widetilde{B}
as
the binormals can be constructed as follows.
Define the tangent
T:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow S^{2}
and the normal
\widetilde{N}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow S^{2}
by

(2.3)

T_{n}=\frac{\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n}}{\left|\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n}\right|},\quad\widetilde{N}_{n}=\widetilde{B}_{n}\times T_{n}.

Furthermore, given
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
define
\gamma:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

(2.4)

\gamma_{n+1}=\gamma_{n}+T_{n}=\gamma_{n}+\frac{\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n}}{\left|\widetilde{B}_{n+1}\times\widetilde{B}_{n}\right|}.

The
signed curvature angle

\kappa

and the
torsion angle

are defined by

(2.5)

\begin{split}\langle T_{n},T_{n-1}\rangle&=\cos\kappa_{n},\quad-\pi<\kappa_{n}<\pi,\\
\langle\widetilde{B}_{n},\widetilde{B}_{n-1}\rangle&=\cos\lambda_{n},\quad 0<\lambda_{n}<\pi.\end{split}

The
discrete Frenet frame

\Phi_{n}=[T_{n},\widetilde{N}_{n},\widetilde{B}_{n}]\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
satisfies the
discrete Frenet-Serret formula

(2.6)

\Phi_{n+1}=\Phi_{n}L_{n},\quad L_{n}=M_{1}(-\lambda_{n+1})M_{3}(\kappa_{n+1}),

where

(2.7)

M_{1}(\theta)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
0&\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\end{array}\right],\quad M_{3}(\theta)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right].

Remark 2.1
.

Note that our Frenet frame coincides with the usual one only up to sign.
We adopt this convention to avoid discontinuous changes in the framing during the motion of the Kaleidocycle; its motion passes the state when the curvature vanishes.

As in the case of a smooth curve,
a discrete spatial curve of a unit segment length is determined by its torsion and curvature angles.
Therefore, the motion of a discrete curve of a constant torsion angle
is determined by the set of functions
\kappa_{n}(t)
,
which can be freely chosen.
The fact that a Kaleidocycle is
closed
enforces interesting topological constraints.
For example, the self-linking number must be a half-integer, and hence, stay constant during the motion.
By Călugăreanu-Fuller-White’s theorem
[
1
]
,
the writhing number of the curve should stay constant as well.
The self-linking number also tells that the configuration space of a Kaleidocycle is not generally connected.

3.
Curves and integrable systems

There are deep connections between differential geometry and the theory of integrable systems, where
the integrable differential or difference equations arise as compatibility conditions of the geometric objects (see, for example,
[
27
]
). In the context of
the deformation of spatial and plane curves, the Frenet frame and its deformation are described by the system of
linear partial differential equations.
The compatibility condition yields so-called the Ablowitz-Kaup-Newell-Segur (AKNS) hierarchy
for the curvature and the torsion, including the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation as a typical example.
In particular, if the torsion is a constant, the integrable deformation is governed by the mKdV equation and its hierarchy
[
9
,
23
,
27
]
.
The connections are also investigated in a discrete setting, where curves are discretised as piecewise linear (polygonal) curves.
For example, continuous deformations of discrete plane and
spatial curves have been considered in
[
5
]
, where the deformations are governed by the semi-discrete analogue of
the mKdV and the NLS equations. Discrete deformations of the discrete plane and spatial curves have been studied in
[
16
]
.
In principle, for a given solution to the nonlinear differential/difference equation for the curvature and torsion,
one can reconstruct the curve by solving the associated system of linear differential/difference equations for the Frenet frame
and integrating the tangent vector.

The theta functions naturally appear in so-called the quasi-periodic solutions to the nonlinear integrable equations, and the corresponding solutions to the associated linear problem are nothing but the Baker-Akhiezer functions. Reconstruction of the deformation of the spatial curve with constant torsion described by the theta function solutions to the mKdV equation has been studied in this framework in
[
4
,
24
]
. Also, an explicit formula of the spatial curves in terms of the
\tau
functions of the two-component KP hierarchy based on Hirota’s bilinear formalism has been presented in
[
11
]
.

Since the dynamics of the Kaleidocycles are modelled by the integrable deformation of the closed discrete spatial curves with a constant torsion angle which preserves the segment-length and torsion angle
[
19
]
, we study the theta function solutions to the corresponding integrable semi-discrete equations, where the theta functions are nothing but the
\tau
functions. Therefore we construct the explicit formula of the Kaleidocycles based on the method of direct reconstruction of the discrete spatial curves in terms of the
\tau
functions.

Hereafter, a (discrete) curve always indicates a polygonal spatial curve.

3.2.
Motion of Kaleidocycle on the osculating plane

Let
\gamma
be a Kaleidocycle, that is, a closed curve with a constant torsion angle and a unit segment length.
A motion of a Kaleidocycle is identified with a time-parametrised family

\gamma:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

\gamma(t)
is a Kaleidocycle for all
t\geq 0
.
In
[
19
]
, a particular motion of a Kaleidocycle
satisfying the following conditions is considered:

(3.20)

\left\langle\dot{\gamma}_{n},\widetilde{B}_{n}\right\rangle=0,\quad\left\langle\dot{\gamma}_{n},\dot{\gamma}_{n}\right\rangle=\tilde{\rho}^{2},\quad|{\gamma}_{n+1}-{\gamma}_{n}|=1\quad(0\leq\forall n\leq k),

where
\tilde{\rho}>0
is a constant and

\dot{\gamma}_{n}=\dfrac{d\gamma_{n}}{dt}
.
These conditions mean that the velocity of each vertex is restricted within the osculating plane and has a constant magnitude.
It is shown in
[
19
]
that
the Frenet-Serret formula (
2.6
) together with
(
3.20
) demand that there exists a function

\Theta_{n}
, called the
potential function
, with
\kappa_{n}=\dfrac{\Theta_{n+1}-\Theta_{n-1}}{2}
that satisfies either
the
semi-discrete potential mKdV equation
:

(3.21)

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\Theta_{n+1}+\Theta_{n}}{2}\right)=\left(1+\cos\lambda\right)\tilde{\rho}\sin\left(\frac{\Theta_{n+1}-\Theta_{n}}{2}\right),

or the
semi-discrete sine-Gordon equation

(3.22)

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\Theta_{n+1}-\Theta_{n}}{2}\right)=\left(1-\cos\lambda\right)\tilde{\rho}\sin\left(\frac{\Theta_{n+1}+\Theta_{n}}{2}\right).

The motions corresponding to these equations differ in general. However, the explicit construction discussed in §
5
satisfies both equations simultaneously.

Remark 3.2
.

As noted in §
2.1
,
the dimension of the configurations of a Kaleidocycle defined by
(
2.2
) is generally
k-6
, and hence, (
3.20
) may look too restrictive.
However, the Möbius Kaleidocycles, which exhibit critical torsion angles,
the dimension of the configurations is conjectured to be one regardless of
k
.
An extensive numerical simulation suggests that the motion of
the Möbius Kaleidocycle satisfies (
2.2
).
Therefore, (
2.2
) specifies a particular yet essential motion of Kaleidocycles.

4.
An explicit formula of closed discrete curve with a constant torsion angle in terms of elliptic theta functions

4.1.
A family of open curves with a constant torsion angle

Throughout this section,

Fix

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pi iw\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+2\pi i\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)v},

\displaystyle\vartheta_{3}(v;w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\pi iwn^{2}+2\pi inv},

The following observations are useful insights for constructing solutions.
In order to make

4.2.
Sufficient conditions for closedness

The constant torsion-angle and unit segment-length curves given in Theorem

We derive a sufficient condition on the parameters

for the curve
\gamma
to be closed to define a Kaleidocycle.
We say a curve

when
\gamma_{n+k}=\gamma_{n}
for all

Rewriting this condition using the
\tau

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{H_{n+k}}{F_{n+k}}=\frac{H_{n}}{F_{n}},

\displaystyle n+k-2\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log F_{n+k}}{\partial z}=n-2\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log F_{n}}{\partial z}.

It is difficult to give general solutions to (

) to obtain particular solutions.
For a fixed

, choose

and set

. Then, from (

), we see

\displaystyle\frac{H_{n+k}}{F_{n+k}}=\frac{H_{n}}{F_{n}}\left(R_{1}R_{3}\right)^{-k}\exp\left(4mr\pi i\right)(-1)^{m},

\displaystyle F_{n+k}=F_{n}\exp\left(k\frac{\Delta_{3}}{\Delta_{3}-\Delta_{1}}z\right)\exp\left[-\pi i\left\{2m\left(\mu_{n}-\frac{1}{2}iv\right)+m^{2}w\right\}\right],

where

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\exp\left\{\pi im\left(4r+1\right)\right\}\left(R_{1}R_{3}\right)^{-k}-1=0,

\displaystyle\Xi:=\frac{\left(\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{1}\right)k-4\pi im}{\Delta_{3}-\Delta_{1}}=0.

Note that since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\log(\Lambda+1)=4\pi im-k\left(\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{1}\right)=-\left(\Delta_{3}-\Delta_{1}\right)\Xi

holds, (

\Lambda=0,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Lambda=0.
