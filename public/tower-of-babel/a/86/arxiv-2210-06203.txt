Thermodynamics of the Ising model encoded in restricted Boltzmann machines
Jing Gu, Kai Zhang

Abstract
The restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) is a two-layer energy-based model that uses its hidden-visible connections to learn the underlying distribution of visible units, whose interactions are often complicated by high-order correlations. Previous studies on the Ising model of small system sizes have shown that RBMs are able to accurately learn the Boltzmann distribution and reconstruct thermal quantities at temperatures away from the critical point T c T_{c} . How the RBM encodes the Boltzmann distribution and captures the phase transition are, however, not well explained. In this work, we perform RBM learning of the 2 ​ d 2d and 3 ​ d 3d Ising model and carefully examine how the RBM extracts useful probabilistic and physical information from Ising configurations. We find several indicators derived from the weight matrix that could characterize the Ising phase transition. We verify that the hidden encoding of a visible state tends to have an equal number of positive and negative units, whose sequence is randomly assigned during training and can be inferred by analyzing the weight matrix. We also explore the physical meaning of visible energy and loss function (pseudo-likelihood) of the RBM and show that they could be harnessed to predict the critical point or estimate physical quantities such as entropy.



I
Introduction

The tremendous success of deep learning in multiple areas over the last decade has really revived the interplay between physics and machine learning, in particular neural networks
Carleo et al. 2019
. On one hand, (statistical) physics ideas
Bahri et al. 2020
, such as renormalization group (RG)
Lin et al. 2017
, energy landscape
Ballard et al. 2017
, free energy
Zhang et al. 2018
, glassy dynamics
Baity-Jesi et al. 2018
, jamming
Geiger et al. 2019
, Langevin dynamics
Feng and Tu 2021
, and field theory
Roberts et al. 2022
, shed some light on the interpretation of deep learning and statistical inference in general
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. On the other hand, machine learning and deep learning tools are harnessed to solved a wide range of physics problems, such as interaction potential construction
Behler and Parrinello 2007
, phase transition detection
Carrasquilla and Melko 2017
, structure encoding
Bapst et al. 2020
, physical concepts discovery
Iten et al. 2020
, and many others
Bedolla et al. 2020
;
Cichos et al. 2020
.
At the very intersection of these two fields lies the restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM)
Hinton and Salakhutdinov 2006
, which serves as a classical paradigm to investigate how an overarching perspective could benefit both sides.

The RBM uses hidden-visible connections to encode (high-order) correlations between visible units
Smolensky 1986
. Its precursor–the (unrestricted) Boltzmann machine was inspired by spin glasses
Sherrington and Kirkpatrick 1975
;
Ackley et al. 1985
and is often used in the inverse Ising problem to infer physical parameters
Cocco and Monasson 2011
;
Aurell and Ekeberg 2012
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
. The restriction of hidden-hidden and visible-visible connections in RBMs allows for more efficient training algorithms, and therefore leads to recent applications in Monte Carlo simulation acceleration
Huang and Wang 2017
, quantum wavefunction representation
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Melko et al. 2019
, and polymer configuration generation
Yu et al. 2019
.
Deep neural networks formed by stacks of RBMs have been mapped onto the variational RG due to their conceptual similarity
Mehta and Schwab 2014
. RBMs are also shown to be equivalent to tensor network states from quantum many-body physics
Chen et al. 2018
.
As simple as it seems, energy-based models like the RBM could eventually become the building blocks of autonomous machine intelligence
LeCun 2022
.

Besides the above mentioned efforts, the RBM has also been applied extensively in the study of the minimal model for second-order phase transition–the Ising model. For the small systems under investigation, it was found that RBMs with an enough number of hidden units can encode the Boltzmann distribution, reconstruct thermal quantities, and generate new Ising configurations fairly well
Torlai and Melko 2016
;
Morningstar and Melko 2018
;
D’Angelo and Böttcher 2020
. The visible
\to
hidden
\to
visible
\cdots
generating sequence of the RBM can be mapped onto a RG flow in physical temperature (often towards the critical point)
Iso et al. 2018
;
Funai and Giataganas 2020
;
Koch et al. 2020
. But the mechanism and power of the RBM to capture physics concepts and principles have not been fully explored. First, in what way is the Boltzmann distribution of the Ising model learned by the RBM? Second, can the RBM learn and even quantitatively predict the phase transition without extra human knowledge? An affirmative answer to the second question is particularly appealing, because simple unsupervised learning methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) using configuration information alone do not provide quantitative prediction for the transition temperature
Wang 2016
;
Wetzel 2017
and supervised learning with neural networks requires human labeling of the phase type or temperature of a given configuration
Tanaka and Tomiya 2017
;
Kashiwa et al. 2019
.

In this article, we report a detailed numerical study on RBM learning of the Ising model with a system size much larger than those used previously.
The purpose is to thoroughly dissect the various parts of the RBM and reveal how each part contributes to the learning of the Boltzmann distribution of the input Ising configurations. Such understanding allows us to extract several useful machine-learning estimators or predictors for physical quantities, such as entropy and phase transition temperature. Conversely, the analysis of a physical model helps us to obtain important insights about the meaning of RBM parameters and functions, such as weight matrix, visible energy and pseudo-likelihood. Below, we first introduce our Ising datasets, the RBM and its training protocols in Sec.
II
. We then report and discuss the results about model parameters, hidden layers, visible energy and pseudo-likelihood in
Sec.
III
. After the conclusion, more details about the Ising model and the RBM are provided in Appendices. Sample codes of the RBM are shared on the GitHub at
https://github.com/Jing-DS/isingrbm
.

Models and Methods

II.1
Dataset of Ising configurations generated by Monte Carlo simulations

The Hamiltonian of the Ising model with
N=L^{d}
spins in a configuration
{\bf s}=[s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N}]^{T}
on a
d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice of linear dimension

in the absence of magnetic field is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({\bf s})=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}

(1)

where the spin variable

(

means to sum over nearest neighbors

{\bf s}
drawn from the sample space of

states follows the Boltzmann distribution

\displaystyle p_{T}({\bf s})=\frac{e^{-\frac{{\mathcal{H}}({\bf s})}{k_{B}T}}}{Z_{T}}

(2)

where

is the partition function. The Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
is set to unity.

Using single-flip Monte Carlo simulations under periodic boundary conditions

, we generate Ising configurations for two-dimensional (
2d
) systems (
d=2
) of
L=64
(
N=4096
) at
n_{T}=16
temperatures
T=0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0,\cdots,4.0
(in units of
J/k_{B}
) and for three-dimensional (
3d
) systems
(d=3)
of
L=16
(
N=4096
) at
n_{T}=20
temperatures
T=2.5,2.75
,
3.0,3.25
,
3.5,3.75,4.0
,
4.25,4.3,4.4,4.5
,
4.6,4.7,4.75,5.0,5.25
,
5.5,5.75,6.0,6.25
. After fully equilibrated,
M=50000
configurations at each
T
are collected into a dataset
D_{T}
for that

. For
2d
systems, we also use a dataset
D_{\cup T}
consisting of
50000
configurations per temperature from
all

T
’s.

Analytical results about thermal quantities of the

Ising model, such as internal energy
\langle E\rangle
, (physical) entropy

, heat capacity

.

II.2
Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM)

The restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) is a two-layer energy-based model with
n_{h}
hidden units (or neurons)
h_{i}=\pm 1
(
i=1,2,\cdots,n_{h}
) in the hidden layer, whose state vector is
{\bf h}=[h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{n_{h}}]^{T}
, and
n_{v}
visible units
v_{j}=\pm 1
(
j=1,2,\cdots,n_{v}
) in the visible layer, whose state vector is
{\bf v}=[v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n_{v}}]^{T}
(Fig.
1
)
Fischer and Igel 2012
. In this work, the visible layer is just the Ising configuration vector, i.e.
{\bf v}={\bf s}
, with
n_{v}=N
. We choose binary unit
\{-1,+1\}
(instead of
\{0,1\}
) to better align with the definition of Ising spin variable
s_{i}
.

The total energy
E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})
of the RBM is defined as

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})

\displaystyle=-{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}-{\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}-{\bf h}^{T}{\bf W}{\bf v}

(3)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}c_{i}h_{i}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}h_{i}v_{j}

where
{\bf b}=[b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n_{v}}]^{T}
is the visible bias,
{\bf c}=[c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n_{h}}]^{T}
is the hidden bias and

{\bf W}_{n_{h}\times n_{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}-{\bf w}_{1}^{T}-\\
-{\bf w}_{2}^{T}-\\
\vdots\\
-{\bf w}_{n_{h}}^{T}-\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
{\bf w}_{:,1}&{\bf w}_{:,2}&\cdots&{\bf w}_{:,n_{v}}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

(4)

is the interaction weight matrix between visible and hidden units. Under this notation, each row vector
{\bf w}_{i}^{T}
(of dimension
n_{v}
) is a
filter
mapping from the visible state
{\bf v}
to a hidden unit
i
and each column vector
{\bf w}_{:,j}
(of dimension
n_{h}
) is an
inverse filter
mapping from the hidden state
{\bf h}
to a visible unit
j
.
All parameters are collectively written as
{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\bf W},{\bf b},{\bf c}\}
.“Restricted” refers to the lack of interaction between hidden units or between visible units.

Figure 1:
A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) with
n_{h}=6
hidden units and
n_{v}=9
visible units. Model parameters
{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\bf W},{\bf b},{\bf c}\}
are represented by connections. A filter
{\bf w}_{1}^{T}
from visible units to the first hidden unit is highlighted by red (light color) connections.

The joint distribution for an overall state
({\bf v},{\bf h})
is

p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})=\frac{e^{-E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}

(5)

where the partition function of the RBM

Z_{\bm{\theta}}=\sum_{\bf v}\sum_{\bf h}e^{-E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}.

(6)

The learned
model distribution
for visible state
{\bf v}
is from marginalization of
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})
,

p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})=\sum_{\bf h}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})=\frac{1}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})},

(7)

where the
visible energy
–an effective energy for visible state
{\bf v}
(often termed as “free energy” in machine learning literature),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})

\displaystyle=-{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\ln\left(e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}\right)

(8)

is defined according to
e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}=\sum\limits_{\bf h}e^{-E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}
such that

Z_{\bm{\theta}}=\sum\limits_{\bf v}e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}.

See Appendix
B
for a detailed derivation.

The conditional distributions to generate
{\bf h}
from
{\bf v}
,
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})
, and to generate
{\bf v}
from
{\bf h}
,
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}|{\bf h})
, satisfying
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})=p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})=p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}|{\bf h})p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h})
, can be written as products

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}|{\bf v})

(9)

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}|{\bf h})

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n_{v}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}|{\bf h})

because
h_{i}
are independent from each other (at fixed
{\bf v}
) and
v_{j}
are independent from each other (at fixed
{\bf h}
).
It can be shown that

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(2(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v})\right)

(10)

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=-1|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=1-\sigma\left(2(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v})\right)

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}=1|{\bf h})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(2(b_{j}+{\bf h}^{T}{\bf w}_{:,j})\right)

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}=-1|{\bf h})

\displaystyle=1-\sigma\left(2(b_{j}+{\bf h}^{T}{\bf w}_{:,j})\right)

where the sigmoid function
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
(Appendix
B
).

II.3
Loss function and training of RBMs

Given the dataset
D=[{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\cdots,{\bf v}_{M}]^{T}
of
M
samples generated independently from the identical
data distribution

p_{D}({\bf v})
(
{\bf v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}p_{D}({\bf v})
), the goal of RBM learning is to find a model distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
that approximates
p_{D}({\bf v})
. In the context of this work, the data samples
{\bf v}
’s are Ising configurations and the data distribution
p_{D}({\bf v})
is or is related to the Ising Boltzmann distribution
p_{T}({\bf s})
.

Based on maximum likelihood estimation, the optimal parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
can be found by minimize the negative log likelihood

{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})=\langle-\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}=\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}+\ln Z_{\bm{\theta}}

(11)

which serves as the
loss function
of RBM learning. Note that the partition function
Z_{\bm{\theta}}
only depends on the model but not on data. Since the calculation of
Z_{\bm{\theta}}
involves summation over all possible
({\bf v},{\bf h})
states, which is not feasible,
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
can not be evaluated exactly, except for very small systems
Oh et al. 2020
. Approximations have to be made, for example, by mean-field calculations
Huang and Toyoizumi 2015
. An interesting feature of the RBM is that, although the actual loss function
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
is not accessible, its gradient

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})=\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}-\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}

(12)

can be sampled, which enables a gradient descent learning algorithm. From step
t
to step
t+1
, model parameters are updated with learning rate
\eta
as

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}).

(13)

To evaluate the loss function, we use its approximate – the pseudo-(negative log)likelihood
Besag 1975

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})=\left\langle-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{v}}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{i}|v_{j\neq i})\right\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}\approx{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})

(14)

where the notation

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{i}|v_{j\neq i})

\displaystyle=p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{i}|v_{j}~{\rm for}~j\neq i)

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}}{e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}+e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}([v_{1},\cdots,-v_{i},\cdots,v_{n_{v}}])}}

is the conditional probability for component
v_{i}
given that all the other components
v_{j}

(j\neq i)
are fixed.
Practically, to avoid the time-consuming sum over all visible units
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{v}}
, it is suggested to randomly sample one
i_{0}\in\{1,2,\cdots,n_{v}\}
and estimate that

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})\approx\left\langle-n_{v}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{i_{0}}|v_{j\neq i_{0}})\right\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}},

(16)

if all the visible units are on average translation-invariant
LISA 2018
.
To monitor the reconstruction error, we also calculate the cross entropy
{\rm CE}
between the initial configuration
{\bf v}
and the conditional probability
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}^{\prime}|{\bf h})
for reconstruction
{\bf v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}^{\prime}|{\bf h})}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf v}^{\prime}
(See Appendix
C
for definition).

For both
2d
and
3d
Ising systems, we first train single temperature RBMs (
T
-RBM).
M=50000
Ising configurations at each
T
forming a dataset
D_{T}
are used to train one model such that there are
n_{T}

T
-RBMs in total. While
n_{v}=N
, we try various number of hidden units with
n_{h}=400,900,1600,2500
in
2d
and
n_{h}=400,900,1600
in
3d
. For
2d
systems, we also train an all temperature RBM (
\cup T
-RBM) for which
50000
Ising configurations per temperature are drawn to compose a dataset
D_{\cup T}
of
M=50000n_{T}=8\times 10^{5}
samples. The number of hidden units for this
\cup T
-RBM is
n_{h}=400,900,1600.
Weight matrix
{\bf W}
are initialized with Glorot normal
initialization
Glorot and Bengio 2010
(
{\bf b}
and
{\bf c}
are initialized as zero). Parameters are optimized with the stochastic gradient descent algorithm of learning rate
\eta=1.0\times 10^{-4}
and batch size 128. The negative phase (model term) of the gradient
\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}
is calculated using CD-k Gibbs sampling with
k=5
. We stop the training until
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and CE converge, typically at 100-2000 epochs (see Supplemental Material).
Three Nvidia GPU cards (GeForce RTX 3090 and 2070) are used to train the model, which takes about two mins per epoch for a
M=50000
dataset.

III
Results and Discussion

In this section, we investigate how the RBM uses its weight matrix
{\bf W}
and hidden layer
{\bf h}
to encode the Boltzmann distributed states of the Ising model, and what physical information can be extracted from machine learning concepts such as visible energy and loss function.

III.1
Filters and inverse filters

It can be verified that the trained weight matrix elements

(and
{\bf h}^{T}{\bf w}_{:,j}

and

When a hidden layer
{\bf h}
is provided, the RBM reconstructs the visible layer
{\bf v}
by applying the
n_{v}
inverse filters
{\bf w}_{:,j}
(

\sim 60\%

•

or

•

III.3
Visible energy

When a
T
-RBM for temperature
T
is trained, we expect that
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\approx p_{D}({\bf v})\approx p_{T}({\bf s})
– the Boltzmann distribution at that
T
.
Although formally related to the physical energy in the Boltzmann factor (with temperature absorbed), the visible energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
of a RBM should be really considered as the negative log (relative) probability of a visible state
{\bf v}
. For single temperature
T
-RBMs, the mean visible energy
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
increases monotonically with temperature (except for the largest
n_{h}
, which might be due to overfitting) (Fig.
7
a-b). The value of
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
and its trend, however, cannot be used to identify the physical phase transition. In fact,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
can differ from the reduced Hamiltonian
\frac{\mathcal{H}({\bf s})}{k_{B}T}
by an arbitrary (temperature-dependent) constant while still maintaining the Boltzmann distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\approx p_{T}({\bf s})
(if the partition function
Z_{\bm{\theta}}
is calibrated accordingly).

The trend of
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
for
T
-RBMs can be understood by considering following approximate forms. First, due to the symmetry of
+1
and
-1
, the biases
b_{j}
and
c_{i}
are all close to zero. A constrained
T
-RBM with zero bias has a visible energy

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\ln\left(e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}\right)

(17)

that approximates the visible energy of the full
T
-RBM, i.e.
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\approx{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})
.
Next, unless
{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}
is close to zero, one of the two exponential terms in Eq. (
17
) always dominates such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})\approx\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})
where

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\left|{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}\right|=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}\right|.

(18)

Eq. (
18
) can further be approximated by setting
{\bf v}={\bf 1}
with all
v_{j}=+1
, i.e.
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})\approx\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf 1})
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf 1})=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\left|{\rm sum}({\bf w}_{i}^{T})\right|=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}\right|.

(19)

In summary,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})
,
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf v})
and
\widetilde{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{W}}({\bf 1})
are all good approximations to the original
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
(Fig.
7
a). The increase of mean
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
with temperature coincides with the increase of
-\left|{\rm sum}({\bf w}_{i}^{T})\right|
with temperature, which is evident from Fig.
3
b. At fixed temperature, the decrease of
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
with
n_{h}
is a consequence of the sum
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}
in the definition of visible energy.
The variance
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle^{2}
is a useful quantity for phase transition detection, because it reflects the fluctuation of the probability
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
. In both low
T
ferromagnetic and high
T
paramagnetic regimes,
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
is relatively homogeneous among different states. When
T
is close to
T_{c}
, the variance of
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
is expected to peak (Fig.
7
d-e). The abnormal rounded (and even shifted) peaks at large
n_{h}
could be a sign of overfitting.

For the all temperature
\cup T
-RBM, the Ising phase transition can be revealed by either the sharp increase of the mean
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
or the peak of the variance
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle^{2}
(Fig.
7
c,f). However, this apparent detection can be a trivial consequence of the special composition of the dataset
D_{\cup T}
, which contains Ising configurations at different temperatures in equal proportion. Only configurations at a specific
T
are fed into the model to calculate the average quantity at that
T
. Technically, a visible state
{\bf v}
in
D_{\cup T}
is not subject to the Boltzmann distribution at any specific temperature. Instead, the true ensemble of
D_{\cup T}
is a collection of
n_{T}
different Boltzmann distributed subsets. Many replicas of the same or similar ferromagnetic states are in
D_{\cup T}
, giving rise to a large multiplicity, high probability and low visible energy for such states. In comparison, high temperature paramagnetic states are all different from each other, and therefore have low
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
(high
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
) for each one of them. Knowing this caveat, one should be cautious when monitoring the visible energy of a
\cup T
-RBM to detect phase transition, because changing the proportion of Ising configurations at different temperatures in
D_{\cup T}
can modify the relative probability of each state.

III.4
Pseudo-likelihood and entropy estimation

The likelihood
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
defined in Eq. (
11
) is conceptually equivalent to the physical entropy
S
defined by the Gibbs entropy formula, apart from the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
difference (Appendix
A
). However, just as entropy
S
cannot be directly sampled, the exact value of
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
is not accessible. In order to estimate
S
, we calculate the pseudo-likelihood
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
instead, which is based on the mean-field like approximation
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\approx\prod_{i=1}^{n_{v}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{i}|v_{j\neq i})
.
A similar idea to estimate free energy was put forward using variational autoregressive networks
Wu et al. 2019
.
The true and estimated entropy of
2d
and
3d
Ising models using
T
-RBMs with different
n_{h}
are shown in Fig.
8
(a-b).
As a comparison, we also consider a “pseudo-entropy” with the similar approximation

\widetilde{S}=-k_{B}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{T}(s_{i}|s_{j\neq i})\right\rangle_{{\bf s}\sim p_{T}}\approx S

(20)

where the conditional probability

p_{T}(s_{i}|s_{j\neq i})=\frac{e^{-\frac{{\mathcal{H}}({\bf s})}{k_{B}T}}}{e^{-\frac{{\mathcal{H}}({\bf s})}{k_{B}T}}+e^{-\frac{{\mathcal{H}}([s_{1},\cdots,-s_{i},\cdots,s_{N}])}{k_{B}T}}}

(21)

and the ensemble average
\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle_{{\bf s}\sim p_{T}}
is taken over states obtained from Monte Carlo sampling. In both
2d
and
3d
,
\widetilde{S}
is lower than the true
S
, especially at high
T
, because a mean-field treatment tends to underestimate fluctuations.

While increasing model complexity by adding hidden units is usually believed to reduce the reconstruction error, e.g. of energy and heat capacity
Torlai and Melko 2016
;
Morningstar and Melko 2018
(see also Supplemental Material), recent study suggests that a trade-off could exist between the accuracy of different statistical quantities
Yevick and Melko 2021
. Here we find that the pseudo-likelihood of
T
-RBMs with the fewest hidden units in our trials (
n_{h}=400
) appears to provide the best prediction for entropy. Increasing
n_{h}
leads to larger deviations from the true
S
at higher
T
. The decreasing of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
with
n_{h}
at fixed temperature agrees with the trend of the visible energy. A lower
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
corresponds to a higher
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
and thus a lower
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
according to its definition.
The surprisingly good performance of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
in approximating
S
could be due to the fact that visible units
v_{i}
in RBMs are only indirectly correlated through hidden units, which collectively serve as an effective mean-field on each visible unit.
We also calculate
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
with the all temperature
\cup T
-RBM in
2d
(Fig.
8
c). Compared with single temperature
T
-RBMs of the same
n_{h}
(Fig.
8
a), the
\cup T
-RBM predicts higher
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
with considerable deviations even at low
T
. The trend of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
also agrees with that of
\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
(Fig.
7
c).

A knowledge about the entropy allows us to estimate the phase transition point according to the thermodynamic relation
C_{V}=T\frac{dS}{dT}
. We construct this estimated
C_{V}
as a function of temperature using
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
and its numerical fitting, whose peaks are expected to be located at
T_{c}
(Supplemental Material). The predicted
T_{c}
are compared with the results from entropy and pseudo-entropy, as well as the known exact values in Table
2
. It can be seen that single temperature
T
-RBMs capture the transition point fairly well within an error about
1
-
3\%
.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we trained RBMs using equilibrium Ising configurations in
2d
and
3d
collected from Monte Carlo simulations at various temperatures. For single temperature
T
-RBMs, the filters (row vectors) and the inverse filters (column vectors) of the weight matrix exhibit different characteristic patterns and correlations, respectively, below, around and above the phase transition. These metrics, such as filter sum fluctuation and inverse filter correlation, can be used to locate the phase transition point. The hidden layer
{\bf h}
on average contains an equal number of
+1
and
-1
units, whose variance decreases as more hidden units are added. The sign of a particular hidden unit
h_{i}
is determined by the signs of the filter sum
{\rm sum}({\bf w}_{i}^{T})
and the magnetization
m
of the visible pattern. But there is no spatial pattern in the sequence of positive and negative units in a hidden encoding.

The visible energy reflects the relative probability of visible states in the Boltzmann distribution. Although the mean of visible energy is not directly related to the (physical) internal energy and does not reveal a clear transition, its fluctuation which peaks at the critical point can be used to identify the phase transition. The value and trend of the visible energy can be understood from its several approximation forms, in particular, the sum of the absolute value of filter sums. The pseudo-likelihood of RBMs is conceptually related to and can be used to estimate the physical entropy. Numerical differentiation of pseudo-likelihood provides another estimator of the transition temperature because it provides an estimate of the heat capacity. All these predictions about the critical temperature are made by unsupervised RBM learning, for which human labeling of phase types are not needed.

As a comparison, we also trained an all temperature
\cup T
-RBM whose dataset is a mixture of Boltzmann-distributed states over a range of temperatures. Each filter of this
\cup T
-RBM is featured by one large domain in its receptive field. Although the visible energy and pseudo-likelihood of the
\cup T
-RBM show certain signature of the phase transition, one should be cautious that this detection could be an artifact of the composition of the dataset. Changing the proportions of Ising configurations at different temperatures could bias the probability and the transition learned by the
\cup T
-RBM.

By extracting the underlying (Boltzmann) distribution of input data, RBMs capture the rapid (phase) transition of such distribution as the tuning parameter (temperature) is changed, without knowledge of the physical Hamiltonian. Information about the distribution is completely embedded in the configurations and their frequencies in the dateset. It would be interesting to see if such a general scheme of RBM learning can be extended to study other physical models of phase transition.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})

\displaystyle=-\ln\sum_{\bf h}e^{-E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}=-\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})-\ln Z_{\bm{\theta}}=-\ln\left[e^{\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}}\sum\limits_{\bf h}e^{\sum\limits_{i}^{n_{h}}\left(\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}+c_{i}\right)h_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}-\ln\left[\sum\limits_{h_{1}=-1}^{+1}\sum\limits_{h_{2}=-1}^{+1}\cdots\sum\limits_{h_{n_{h}}=-1}^{+1}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}e^{\left(\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}+c_{i}\right)h_{i}}\right]=-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}-\ln\left[\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\sum\limits_{h_{i}=-1,1}e^{\left(\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}+c_{i}\right)h_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}-\ln\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\left(e^{-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}-c_{i}}+e^{\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}+c_{i}}\right)=-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}b_{j}v_{j}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\ln\left(e^{-\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}-c_{i}}+e^{\sum\limits_{j}^{n_{v}}W_{ij}v_{j}+c_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=-{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\ln\left(e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}\right).

The conditional probability

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}=\frac{e^{-E_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})}}{e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}}=\frac{e^{{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}}}{e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}}e^{{\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}+{\bf h}^{T}{\bf W}{\bf v}}

=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}e^{{\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}+{\bf h}^{T}{\bf W}{\bf v}}

where the
{\bf h}
-independent constant
\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})=e^{-{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}=\sum\limits_{\bf h}e^{{\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}+{\bf h}^{T}{\bf W}{\bf v}}
such that
Z_{\bm{\theta}}=\sum\limits_{\bf v}\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})e^{{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}}
. So

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}e^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}c_{i}h_{i}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}h_{i}{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}e^{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}h_{i}\left(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}e^{h_{i}\left(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}\right)}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}|{\bf v})

from which it can be recognized that
p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}|{\bf v})\propto e^{h_{i}\left(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}\right)}
. The single unit conditional probability

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=-1|{\bf v})+p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})}

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}}{e^{-c_{i}-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}+e^{c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v})}}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(2(c_{i}+{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v})\right).

Other relations about
p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=-1|{\bf v})
,
p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}=1|{\bf h})
and
p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}=-1|{\bf h})
can be found similarly.

Appendix C
Maximum likelihood estimation and gradient descent of RBMs

In this appendix, we review the gradient descent algorithm of RBMs derived from maximum likelihood estimation
Murphy 2012
.
The likelihood function for a given dataset
D=[{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\cdots,{\bf v}_{M}]^{T}
is
P_{\bm{\theta}}(D)=\prod\limits_{m=1}^{M}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}_{m})
and maximum likelihood is equivalent to minimum negative log likelihood (or its average)

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\arg\max\limits_{\bm{\theta}}\prod\limits_{m=1}^{M}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}_{m})

(35)

\displaystyle=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\theta}}\left\{-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}_{m})\right\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\theta}}\left\{-\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}_{m})\right\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\theta}}\langle-\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})

where
{\bf v}\sim p_{D}
means to randomly draw
{\bf v}
from
p_{D}
and
\langle\cdots\rangle
is the expectation value (subject to the distribution). Alternatively, this can be considered as to minimize the Kullbach-Leibler (KL) divergence

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(p_{D}|p_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}p_{D}({\bf v}_{m})\ln\frac{p_{D}({\bf v}_{m})}{p_{\theta}({\bf v}_{m})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\ln p_{D}({\bf v})-\ln p_{\theta}({\bf v})\right\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}\geq 0

with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
, where only the second term
\left\langle-\ln p_{\theta}({\bf v})\right\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}
depends on parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
. In this work, we use
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
as the loss function to train RBMs.

It is sometimes useful to directly monitor the reconstruction error by comparing the input (
{\bf v}
) and reconstructed configurations (
{\bf v}^{\prime}
), or more quantitatively, by the (normalized) cross entropy

\displaystyle{\rm CE}

\displaystyle=\left\langle-\frac{1}{n_{v}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{v}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{v_{j}=+1}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}^{\prime}=+1|{\bf h})\right.\right.

(36)

\displaystyle+\left.\left.\mathbbm{1}_{v_{j}=-1}\ln p_{\bm{\theta}}(v_{j}^{\prime}=-1|{\bf h})\right]\right\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}

where the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}_{A}=1
if
A
is true, or
0
if
A
is false.

The gradient of the loss function

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ln Z_{\bm{\theta}}

(37)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ln Z_{\bm{\theta}}

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ln Z_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}Z_{\bm{\theta}}}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}=\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sum\limits_{{\bf v}}e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{{\bf v}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}=-\frac{\sum\limits_{{\bf v}}e^{-{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}{Z_{\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{{\bf v}}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}.

So,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{D}}-\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}

(38)

\displaystyle={\rm positive~phase}+{\rm negative~phase}

\displaystyle={\rm data~term}+{\rm model~term}

In both positive and negative phase,

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left[-{\bf b}^{T}{\bf v}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{h}}\ln\left(e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}\right)\right]

which has components

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}{\partial W_{ij}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{-v_{j}e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+v_{j}e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}{e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}

(39)

\displaystyle=v_{j}\frac{e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}-e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}{e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}=-v_{j}\tanh\left({\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-v_{j}\left[(-1)p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=-1|{\bf v})+(+1)p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})\right]

\displaystyle=-v_{j}\langle h_{i}\rangle_{h_{i}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}|{\bf v})}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}{\partial c_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{-e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}{e^{-{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}-c_{i}}+e^{{\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}}}=-\tanh\left({\bf w}_{i}^{T}{\bf v}+c_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left[(-1)p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=-1|{\bf v})+(+1)p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}=1|{\bf v})\right]

\displaystyle=-\langle h_{i}\rangle_{h_{i}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(h_{i}|{\bf v})}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})}{\partial b_{j}}

\displaystyle=-v_{j}.

To evaluate the expectation value
\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle
, in positive phase
{\bf v}
can be directly drawn from the dataset, while in negative phase
{\bf v}
must be sampled from the model distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
.
In practice, as an approximation, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is used to generate
{\bf v}
states that obey the distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
, such that

\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})\rangle_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}\approx\frac{1}{\rm sample~size}\sum\limits_{{\bf v}\sim p_{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}).

(40)

Using the conditional probability,
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf h}|{\bf v})
and
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v}|{\bf h})
, we can generate a sequence of states

{\bf v}^{(0)}\to{\bf h}^{(0)}\to{\bf v}^{(1)}\to{\bf h}^{(1)}\to\cdots\to{\bf v}^{(t)}\to{\bf h}^{(t)}\to\cdots.

As
t\to\infty
, the MCMC converges with
({\bf v}^{(t)},{\bf h}^{(t)})\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v},{\bf h})
and
{\bf v}^{(t)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf v})
.

Markov chain starting from a random
{\bf v}^{(0)}
takes a lot of steps to equilibrate. There are two ways to speed up the sampling
Hinton 2012

•

k
step contrastive divergence (CD-
k
)

For each parameter update, draw
{\bf v}^{(0)}
(or a minibatch) from the training data
D=[{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\cdots,{\bf v}_{M}]^{T}
and run Gibbs sampling for
k
steps. Even CD-1 can work reasonably well.

•

persistent contrastive divergence (PCD-
k
)

Always keep the same MC during the entire training process. For each parameter update, run this persistent MC for another
k
steps to collect
{\bf v}
states.
