An Asymptotic Theory of Joint Sequential Changepoint Detection and Identification for General Stochastic Models Thanks: The work was supported in part by the Russian Science Foundation under the grant 18-19-00452 at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology.
Alexander G. Tartakovsky

Abstract
The paper addresses a joint sequential changepoint detection and identification/isolation problem for a general stochastic model, assuming that the observed data may be dependent and non-identically distributed, the prior distribution of the change point is arbitrary, and the post-change hypotheses are composite. The developed detection–identification theory generalizes the changepoint detection theory developed by Tartakovsky (2019) to the case of multiple composite post-change hypotheses when one has not only to detect a change as quickly as possible but also to identify (or isolate) the true post-change distribution. We propose a multi-hypothesis change detection–identification rule and show that it is nearly optimal, minimizing moments of the delay to detection as the probability of a false alarm and the probabilities of misidentification go to zero.



I
Introduction

In many applications, one needs not only to detect an abrupt change as quickly as possible but also to provide a detailed diagnosis of the occurred change – to determine which type of change is in effect.
For example, the problem of detection and diagnosis is important for rapid detection and isolation of intrusions in large-scale distributed computer networks, target detection with radar, sonar and optical sensors
in a cluttered environment, detecting terrorists’ malicious activity, fault detection and isolation in dynamic systems and networks, and integrity monitoring of navigation systems, to name a few (see
[
20
, Ch 10]

for an overview and references).
In other words, there are several kinds of changes that can be associated with several different post-change distributions and the goal is to detect the change and to identify which distribution corresponds to the change.
As a result, the problem of changepoint detection and diagnosis is a generalization of the quickest change detection problem
[
15
,
16
,
6
,
13
,
20
]

to the case of
N\geqslant 2
post-change hypotheses,
and it can be formulated as a joint change detection and identification problem. In the literature, this problem is usually called
change detection and isolation
. The detection–isolation problem has been considered in
both Bayesian and minimax settings. In 1995, Nikiforov
[
8
]
suggested a minimax approach to the change detection–isolation problem and showed that the multihypothesis version of the CUSUM rule is
asymptotically optimal when the average run length (ARL) to a false alarm and the mean time to false isolation become large. Several versions of the multihypothesis CUSUM-type and SR-type procedures, which have
minimax optimality properties in the classes of rules with constraints imposed on the ARL to a false alarm and conditional probabilities of false isolation, are proposed by Nikiforov
[
9
,
10
]

and Tartakovsky
[
22
]
. These rules asymptotically minimize maximal expected delays to detection and isolation as the ARL to a false alarm is large and the probabilities of wrong
isolations are small. Dayanik et al.
[
2
]
proposed an asymptotically optimal Bayesian detection–isolation rule assuming that the prior distribution of the change point is geometric.
In all these papers, the optimality results were restricted to the case of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations (in pre- and post-change modes with different distributions) and simple
post-change hypotheses. In many practical applications, the i.i.d. assumption is too restrictive. The observations may be
either non-identically distributed or dependent or both, i.e., non-i.i.d. Also, in a variety of applications, a pre-change distribution is known but the post-change distribution is rarely known completely.
A more realistic situation is parametric uncertainty when the parameter of the post-change distribution is unknown since a putative parameter value is rarely representative.
Lai
[
5
]
provided a certain generalization for the non-i.i.d. case and composite hypotheses for a specific loss function.
See Chapter 10 in Tartakovsky et al.
[
20
]
for a detailed overview.

One of the most challenging and important versions of the change detection–isolation problem is the multidecision and multistream detection
problem when it is necessary not only to detect a change as soon as possible but also to identify the streams where the change happens with given probabilities of misidentification.
Specifically, there are
N
data streams and the change occurs in some of them at an unknown point in time. It is necessary to detect
the change in distribution as soon as possible and indicate which streams are “corrupted.” Both the rates of false alarms and misidentification should be controlled by given (usually low) levels. In the following, we will refer
to this problem as the
Multistream Sequential Change Detection–Identification
problem.

In this paper, we address a simplified multistream detection–identification scenario where change can occur only in a single stream and we need to determine in which stream. We assume that the
observations in streams can have a very general structure, i.e., can be dependent and non-identically distributed. We focus on a semi-Bayesian setting assuming that the change point is random possessing the
(prior) distribution.
However, we do not suppose that there is a prior distribution on post-change hypotheses. We generalize the asymptotic Bayesian theory developed by Tartakovsky
[
23
]
for a single
post-change hypothesis (for a single stream). Specifically, we show that under certain
conditions (related to the law of large numbers for the log-likelihood processes) the proposed multihypothesis detection–identification rule asymptotically minimizes the trade-off between positive moments of the detection
delay and the false alarm/misclassification rates expressed via the weighted probabilities of false alarm and false identification. The key assumption in the general asymptotic theory is the
stability property of the log-likelihood ratio processes in streams between the “change” and “no-change” hypotheses, which can be formulated in terms of the law of large numbers and rates of convergence
of the properly normalized log-likelihood ratios and their adaptive versions in the vicinity of the true parameter values.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we describe the general stochastic model, which is treated in the paper. In Section
III
, we introduce the mixture-based
change detection–identification rule. In Section
IV
, we formulate the asymptotic optimization problems in the class of changepoint detection–identification rules with the constraint imposed
on the probabilities of false alarm and wrong identification. In Section
V
, we obtain upper bounds on the probabilities of false alarms and misidentification as functions of thresholds.
In Section
VI
, we derive asymptotic lower bounds for moments of the detection delay in the class of rules with given probabilities of false alarms and misidentification,
and in Section
VII
, we prove asymptotic optimality of the proposed mixture detection–identification rule as the probabilities of false alarm and misidentification go to zero.
In Section
VIII
, we consider an example that illustrates general results. Section
IX
concludes.

II
The General Stochastic Model

Suppose there are
N
independent data streams
\{X_{n}(i)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, observed sequentially in time subject to a change at an unknown time
\nu\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
,
so that
X_{1}(i),\dots,X_{\nu}(i)
are generated by one stochastic model and
X_{\nu+1}(i),X_{\nu+2}(i),\dots
by another model when the change occurs in the
i
th stream.
We will assume that the change in distributions may happen only in one stream and it is not known which stream is affected, i.e., we are interested in
a “multisample slippage” changepoint model (given
\nu
and that the
i
th stream is affected with the parameter
\theta_{i}
) for which joint density
p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i},\theta_{i})
of the data

{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=({\mathbf{X}}^{n}(1),\dots,{\mathbf{X}}^{n}(N))
,
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}(i)=(X_{1}(i),\dots,X_{n}(i))
observed up to time
n
is of the form

\begin{split}&p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i},\theta_{i})=p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty})\\
&=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}g_{\ell}(X_{t}(\ell)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(\ell))~~\text{for}~~\nu\geqslant n,\\
&p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i},\theta_{i})=\prod_{t=1}^{\nu}g_{i}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))\times\\
&\prod_{t=\nu+1}^{n}f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))\times\\
&\prod_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}}\prod_{t=1}^{n}g_{j}(X_{t}(j)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(j))~~\text{for}~~\nu<n,\end{split}

(1)

where
{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i}
denotes the hypothesis that the change occurs at time
\nu
in the stream
i
,
g_{i}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))
and
f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))
are conditional pre- and post-change densities in the
i
th data stream, respectively (with respect to some sigma-finite measure), and
{\mathcal{N}}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. In other words, all components
X_{t}(\ell)
,
\ell\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
have conditional densities
g_{\ell}(X_{t}(\ell)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(\ell))
before the change occurs and
X_{t}(i)
has conditional density
f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))
after the change occurs in the
i
th stream and the rest of the components

X_{t}(j)
,
j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}
have conditional densities
g_{j}(X_{t}(j)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(j))
. The parameters
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
of the post-change distributions are unknown. The event
\nu=\infty
and the
corresponding hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}:\nu=\infty
mean that there never is a change. Notice that the model (
1
) implies that
X_{\nu+1}(i)
is the first post-change observation under hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i}
.

Regarding the change point
\nu
we assume that it is a random variable independent of the observations with prior distribution

\pi_{k}={\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
with
\pi_{k}>0
for
k\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We will also assume that a change point may take negative values, which means that the change has
occurred by the time the observations became available. However,
the detailed structure of the distribution
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
for
k=-1,-2,\dots
is not important. The only value which matters is the total probability
q={\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant-1)
of the change being in
effect before the observations become available, so we set
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant-1)={\mathsf{P}}(\nu=-1)=\pi_{-1}
,
\pi_{-1}\in[0,1)
. Therefore, in what follows we assume that
\nu\in\{-1,0,1,\dots\}=\mathbb{Z}
and the prior distribution of
the change point is defined on
\mathbb{Z}
.

III
The Detection–Identification Rule

A changepoint detection–identification rule is a pair
\delta=(d,T)
, where
T
is a stopping time (with respect to the filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\sigma({\mathbf{X}}^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
) associated with the time of alarm on change and

d=d_{T}\in{\mathcal{N}}
is a decision on which stream is affected (or which post-change distribution is true) which is made at time
T
.

It follows from (
1
) that for an assumed value of the change point
\nu=k
, stream
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and the post-change parameter value in the
i
th stream
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, the
likelihood ratio (LR)

LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)=p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{k,i},\theta_{i})/p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty})
between the hypotheses
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,i}
and
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}
for observations accumulated by the time
n
has the form

LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(t),\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}},~~n>k

(2)

(
k=-1,0,1,\dots
), where

{\mathcal{L}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(t)=f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))/g_{i}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i)).

We suppose that
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(0)=1
, so that
LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(-1,n)=LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(0,n)
.
Define the average (over the prior
\pi_{k}
) LR statistics

\Lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(n)=\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n),\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

(3)

Let
W_{i}(\theta_{i})
,
\int_{\Theta_{i}}{\rm d}W_{i}(\theta_{i})=1
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
be mixing measures and, for
k<n
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
define the LR-mixtures

LR_{i,W}(k,n)=\int_{\Theta_{i}}LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)\,\mathrm{d}W_{i}(\theta_{i}),

(4)

and the statistics

\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}(n)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}LR_{i,W}(k,n),~~&i\in{\mathcal{N}}\\
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)~~&i=0\end{cases};

(5)

\begin{split}\bar{\Lambda}_{ij}^{\pi,W}(n)&=\frac{\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}(n)}{\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\sup_{\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}}LR_{j,\theta_{j}}(k,n)},\\
&\quad i,j\in{\mathcal{N}},~i\neq j,~~n\geqslant 1;\\
\bar{\Lambda}_{i0}^{\pi,W}(n)&=\frac{\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}(n)}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)},\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}},~~n\geqslant 1,\end{split}

(6)

where in the statistic
\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}(n)
defined in (
5
)
i=0
corresponds to the hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{0}
that there is no change (in the first
n
observations).

Write
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}=\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. For the set of positive thresholds
A=(A_{ij})
,
j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\setminus\{i\}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
the change detection–identification rule
\delta_{A}=(d_{A},T_{A})
is defined as follows:

T_{A}=\min_{\ell\in{\mathcal{N}}}T_{A}^{(\ell)},~\quad d_{A}=i~~\text{if}~~T_{A}=T_{A}^{(i)},

(7)

where the Markov times
T_{A}^{(i)}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
are given by

T_{A}^{(i)}=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:\bar{\Lambda}_{ij}^{\pi,W}(n)\geqslant A_{ij}~\forall~j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\setminus\{i\}\right\}.

(8)

In definitions of stopping times we always set
\inf\{\mathchoice{\mbox{\msbmx\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmx\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmvii\char 63\relax}}{\mbox{\msbmv\char 63\relax}}\}=\infty
, i.e.,
T_{A}^{(i)}=\infty
if there is no such
n
.
If
T_{A}=T_{A}^{(i)}
for several values of
i
then any of them can be taken.

IV
Optimization Problems and Assumptions

Let
{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}
denote expectations under probability measures
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
, respectively, where
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
corresponds to
model (
1
) with an assumed value of the parameter
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, change point
\nu=k
, and the affected stream
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Define the probability measure

{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}({\mathcal{A}}\times\mathcal{K})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\,\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left({\mathcal{A}}\right)
under which the change point
\nu
has distribution
\pi=\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
and the model for the observations

1

{\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}

For
r\geqslant 1
,
\nu=k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
introduce the risk associated with the conditional
r
th moment of the detection delay

{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(\delta)={\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left[(T-k)^{r};d=i\,|\,T>k\right],

(9)

where for
k=-1
we set
T-k=T
, but not
T+1
, as well as the integrated (over prior
\pi
) risk associated with the moments of
delay to detection

\begin{split}{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}^{r}_{i,\theta_{i}}(\delta)&:={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}[(T-\nu)^{r};d=i\,|\,T>\nu]\\
&=\frac{{\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}[(T-\nu)^{r},d=i,T>\nu]}{{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T>\nu)}\\
&=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left[(T-k)^{r},d=i,T>k\right]}{\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(T>k)}\\
&=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}}\pi_{k}{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(\delta){\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(T>k)}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant 0)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)}\\
&=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}}\pi_{k}{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(\delta){\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)}{1-\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta)},\end{split}

(10)

where

\begin{split}\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta)&={\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}(T\leqslant\nu)\\
&=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(T\leqslant k)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)\end{split}

(11)

is the weighted probability of false alarm.
Note that in (
10
) and (
11

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(T\leqslant k)={\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)
since the event
\{T\leqslant k\}
belongs to the sigma-algebra

{\mathcal{F}}_{k}=\sigma({\mathbf{X}}^{k})
and, hence, depends only on the first
k
observations which distribution corresponds to the measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
. This implies, in particular, that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T>\nu)

\displaystyle=1-\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant 0)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k).

Also, introduce

\begin{split}\mathsf{PFA}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta)&={\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{i,\theta_{i}}(T\leqslant\nu;d=i)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(T\leqslant k;d=i)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k;d=i),\end{split}

(12)

the weighted probability of false alarm on the event
\{d=i\}
, i.e., the probability of raising the alarm with the decision
d=i
that there is a change in the
i
th stream when there is no change.

The loss associated with wrong identification is reasonable to measure by the maximal probabilities of wrong decisions (misidentification)

\mathsf{PMI}_{ij}^{\pi}(\delta)=\sup_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d=j;T<\infty|T>\nu),

(13)

i,j=1,\dots,N,~~i\neq j
. Note that

\begin{split}&{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d=j;T<\infty|T>\nu)=\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d=j;\nu<T<\infty)}{{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T>\nu)}\\
&=\frac{\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\,{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}(d=j;k<T<\infty)}{1-\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta)}.\end{split}

Define the class of change detection–identification rules
\delta
with constraints on the probabilities of false alarm
\mathsf{PFA}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta)
and the probabilities of misidentification
\mathsf{PMI}_{ij}^{\pi}(\delta)
:

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{C}}_{\pi}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\{\delta:\mathsf{PFA}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta)\leqslant\alpha_{i},~i\in{\mathcal{N}}~~\text{and}\\
&\quad\mathsf{PMI}_{ij}^{\pi}(\delta)\leqslant\beta_{ij},~i,j\in{\mathcal{N}},i\neq j\},\end{split}

(14)

where
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N})
and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{ij})_{i,j\in{\mathcal{N}},i\neq j}

\beta_{ij}\in(0,1)
.

Ideally, we would be interested in finding an optimal rule
\delta_{\rm opt}=(d_{\rm opt},T_{\rm opt})

{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{r}(\delta_{\rm opt})=\inf_{\delta\in{\mathbb{C}}_{\pi}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}\,{\bar{\mathcal{R}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{r}(\delta)~\forall~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},~i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

However, this problem is intractable for arbitrary values of
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1)
and
\beta_{ij}\in(0,1)
. For this reason, we will

where

\begin{split}&\lim_{\alpha_{\max}\to 0,\beta_{\max}\to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\inf_{\delta\in{\mathbb{C}}_{\pi}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\mathcal{R}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}^{r}(\delta)}{{\mathcal{R}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}^{r}(\delta_{A})}=1\\

, define

\displaystyle p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j})=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left\{\frac{1}{M}\max_{1\leqslant n\leqslant M}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\right\},

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{r}(\varkappa,\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta\in\Gamma_{\varkappa,\theta_{i}}}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)

\displaystyle<I_{i}(\theta_{i})-\varepsilon\Big\},

(19)

where
I_{i0}(\theta_{i},\theta_{0})=I_{i}(\theta_{i})
, so that

\begin{split}&p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i};0,\theta_{0})=p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i})\\
&={\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left\{\frac{1}{M}\max_{1\leqslant n\leqslant M}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)I_{i}(\theta_{i})\right\}.\end{split}

Regarding the model for the observations (
1
), we assume that the following two conditions are satisfied (for local LLRs in data streams):

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
.
There exist positive and finite numbers
I_{i}(\theta_{i})
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and

I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})
,
\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}
,
j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0

\begin{split}&\lim_{M\to\infty}p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j})=0\\
&\text{for all}~k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},~\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j},~j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\setminus\{i\},~i\in{\mathcal{N}}.\end{split}

(20)

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
and some
r\geqslant 1

\begin{split}\lim_{\varkappa\to 0}\Upsilon_{r}(\varkappa,\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i})&<\infty\quad\text{for all}~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},~i\in{\mathcal{N}}.\end{split}

(21)

Note that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds whenever
\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)/n
converges almost surely (a.s.) to
I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
, i.e.,
for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)=I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\right\}=1.

(22)

Regarding the prior distribution
\pi_{k}={\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
we assume that it is fully supported (i.e.,
\pi_{k}>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
0\leqslant\pi_{-1}<1
and
\pi_{\infty}=0
) and
the following two conditions are satisfied:

{\mathbf{CP}}_{1}
.
For some

0\leqslant\mu<\infty
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left|\log\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\right|=\mu.

(23)

{\mathbf{CP}}_{2}
.
If
\mu=0
, then in addition

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}|\log\pi_{k}|^{r}<\infty\quad\text{for some}~r\geqslant 1.

(24)

The class of prior distributions satisfying conditions
{\mathbf{CP}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{CP}}_{2}
will be denoted by
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu)
.

Note that if
\mu>0
, then the prior distribution has an exponential right tail. In this case, condition (
24
) holds automatically.
If
\mu=0
, the distribution has a heavy tail, i.e., belongs to the model with a vanishing hazard rate.
However, we cannot allow this distribution to have a too heavy tail, which
is guaranteed by condition
{\mathbf{CP}}_{2}
.
A typical heavy-tailed prior distribution that satisfies both conditions
{\mathbf{CP}}_{1}
with
\mu=0
and
{\mathbf{CP}}_{2}
for all
r\geqslant 1
is a discrete Weibull-type distribution with the
shape parameter
0<\kappa<1
. Constraint (
24
) is often guaranteed by finiteness of the
r
-th moment,
{\mathsf{E}}[\nu^{r}]<\infty
.

To obtain lower bounds for moments of the detection delay we need only right-tail conditions (
20
). However, to establish the asymptotic optimality property of the rule
\delta_{A}
both
right-tail and left-tail conditions (
20
) and (
21
) are needed.

V
Upper Bounds on Probabilities of False Alarm and Misidentification of the Detection–Identification Rule
\delta_{A}

Let
\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,n}({\mathcal{A}})={\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}({\mathcal{A}},\nu<n)
denote the measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}
on the event
\{\nu<n\}
.
The distribution
\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,n}({\mathbf{X}}^{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n})
has density

\begin{split}f_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,n}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})&=\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\Bigg[\pi_{k}\prod_{t=1}^{k}g_{i}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))\\
&\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))\Bigg]\times\\
&\quad{\mathsf{P}}(\nu<n)\prod_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}}\prod_{t=1}^{n}g_{j}(X_{t}(j)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(j)),\end{split}

where
\prod_{t=1}^{-1}g_{i}(X_{t}(i)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}(i))=1
. Write

f_{i,W}^{\pi,n}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=\int_{\Theta_{i}}f_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,n}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}W_{i}(\theta_{i}).

Next, define the statistic
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i,j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,W}(n)=\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}(n)/\Lambda_{j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi}(n)
and the measure

\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell,W}^{\pi,n}({\mathcal{A}})=\int_{\Theta_{\ell}}\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell,\theta_{\ell}}^{\pi,n}({\mathcal{A}})\mathrm{d}W_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell}).

Denote by
{\mathsf{P}}|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}
the restriction of the measure
{\mathsf{P}}
to the sigma-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Obviously,

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i,j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,W}(n)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{i,W}^{\pi,n}}{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,n}}\Bigg|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}},\quad i\neq j,

and hence, the statistic
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i,j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,W}(n)
is a
(\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,n},{\mathcal{F}}_{n})
-martingale with unit expectation for all
\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}
. Therefore, by the Wald–Doob identity,
for any stopping time
T
and all
\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j},

\begin{split}&\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}\left[\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j,i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,W}(T){\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{{\mathcal{A}},T<\infty\}}}\right]=\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{j,W}^{\pi}\left[{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{{\mathcal{A}},T<\infty\}}}\right]\\
&=\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,W}^{\pi,T}({\mathcal{A}}\cap\{T<\infty\}),\end{split}

(25)

where
\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{j,W}^{\pi}
and
\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi}
stand for the operators of expectation under
\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,W}^{\pi,T}
and
\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,\theta_{j}}^{\pi,T}
, respectively.

The following theorem establishes upper bounds for the PFA and PMI of the proposed detection–identification rule
\delta_{A}
. Note that these bounds are valid in the most general case – neither of the conditions
on the model
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
or on the prior distribution
{\mathbf{CP}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{CP}}_{2}
are required.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\delta_{A}
be the changepoint detection–identification rule defined in (
7
)–(
8
). The following upper bounds for the PFA and PMI of rule
\delta_{A}
hold

\displaystyle\mathsf{PFA}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta_{A})

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+A_{i0})^{-1},\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta_{A})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+A_{i0})^{-1}

(27)

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{PMI}_{ij}^{\pi}(\delta_{A})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1+A_{i0}}{A_{i0}\,A_{ji}},\quad i,j\in{\mathcal{N}},~i\neq j,

(28)

\displaystyle\mathsf{PMI}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta_{A})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1+A_{i0}}{A_{i0}}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}}\frac{1}{A_{ji}},\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

(29)

Thus, if
\alpha_{\max}<1-\pi_{-1}
, then

\begin{split}&A_{i0}=\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}~~\text{and}~~A_{ij}=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha_{j})\beta_{ji}}\\
&\quad\text{imply}~~\delta_{A}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{\pi}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),\end{split}

(30)

and if
A_{i0}=A_{0}
for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
A_{ij}=A_{j}
for
j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}
, then

\begin{split}&A_{0}=\frac{N}{\alpha}(1-\alpha/N)~~\text{and}~~A_{j}=\frac{N-1}{(1-\alpha/N)\bar{\beta}_{j}}\\
&\quad\text{imply}~~\delta_{A}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{\pi}^{\star}(\alpha,\bar{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}).\end{split}

(31)

Proof:

Using the Bayes rule, notation (
2
)–(
6
), and the fact that
LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)=1
for
k\geqslant n
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k|{\mathcal{F}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}LR_{i,W}(k,n)}{\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}\pi_{j}LR_{i,W}(j,n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{k}LR_{i,W}(k,n)}{\sum_{j=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{j}LR_{i,W}(j,n)+{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)},

so that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n|{\mathcal{F}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k|{\mathcal{F}}_{n})=\frac{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}{\Lambda_{i,W}^{\pi}+{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\Lambda}_{i0}^{\pi,W}(n)+1}.

Next, obviously,

\mathsf{PFA}_{i}(\delta_{A})={\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}^{(i)}\leqslant\nu,T_{A}=T_{A}^{(i)})\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}^{(i)}\leqslant\nu).

Therefore, taking into account that
{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}^{(i)}\leqslant\nu)={\mathsf{E}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}[{\mathsf{P}}(T_{A}^{(i)}\leqslant\nu|{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{A}^{(i)}})]

and that
\bar{\Lambda}_{i0}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(i)})\geqslant A_{i0}
on
\{T_{A}^{(i)}<\infty\}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathsf{PFA}_{i}(\delta_{A})&\leqslant{\mathsf{E}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}[(1+\bar{\Lambda}_{i0}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(i)}))^{-1};T_{A}^{(i)}<\infty]\\
&\leqslant 1/(1+A_{i0})\end{split}

and inequalities (
26
) follow. Inequality (
27
) follows immediately from the fact that
\mathsf{PFA}^{\pi}(\delta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{PFA}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta)
.

To prove the upper bound (
28
) note that
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j,i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,W}(n)\geqslant\bar{\Lambda}_{ji}^{\pi,W}(n)
for all
n\geqslant 1
and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
and that

\bar{\Lambda}_{ji}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(j)})\geqslant A_{ji}
on
\{T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\}
and we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d_{A}=j,\nu<T_{A}<\infty)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)},\nu<T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,T_{A}}(T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)},T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}\left[\frac{\bar{\Lambda}_{ji}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(j)})}{\bar{\Lambda}_{ji}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(j)})}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)},T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{A_{ji}}\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}\left[\bar{\Lambda}_{ji}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(j)}){\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)},T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{A_{ji}}\widetilde{{\mathsf{E}}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}\left[\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j,i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi,W}(T_{A}^{(j)}){\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)},T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\}}}\right]~\forall~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},

where, by equality (
25
), the last term is equal to

\frac{1}{A_{ji}}\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,W}^{\pi,T_{A}}(\{T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)}\}\cap\{T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\}).

This yields

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d_{A}=j,\nu<T_{A}<\infty)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{A_{ji}}\widetilde{{\mathsf{P}}}_{j,W}^{\pi,T_{A}}(\{T_{A}=T_{A}^{(j)}\}\cap\{T_{A}^{(j)}<\infty\})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{A_{ji}}~~\text{for all}~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}.

Since
{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d_{A}=j|T_{A}>\nu)={\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(d_{A}=j,\nu<T_{A}<\infty)/{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}>\nu)
and, by (
26
),
{\mathsf{P}}_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\pi}(T_{A}>\nu)\geqslant A_{i}/(1+A_{i})
, the upper bound
(
28
) follows. The upper bound (
29
) follows from (
28
) and the fact that
\mathsf{PMI}_{i}^{\pi}(\delta)=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}}\mathsf{PMI}_{ij}^{\pi}(\delta)
.

Implications (
30
) and (
31
) are obvious.
∎

Remark 1
.

Typically, the upper bounds (
26
)–(
29
) for PFA and PMI are not tight but rather quite conservative, especially when overshoots over thresholds are large (i.e., when the hypotheses

{\mathsf{H}}_{i}
and
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}
are not close). Unfortunately, in the general non-i.i.d. case, the improvement of these bounds is not possible. In the i.i.d. case where observations are independent and identically distributed
with the common pre-change density

and the common post-change density
f_{i}(x)
in the

th stream (i.e., when the post-change hypotheses are simple), it is possible to obtain asymptotically
accurate approximations using the renewal theory similarly to how it was done in
[
20
, Th 7.1.5, p. 327]
for the PFA in the single-stream case.

\displaystyle M_{i}(A)

An Example: Detection of Signals with Unknown Amplitudes

Suppose there is an
N
-channel sensor system and we are able to observe the output vector

X_{n}=(X_{n}(1),\dots,X_{n}(N))
,
n=1,2,\dots
The observations
X_{n}(i)
in the
i
th channel have the form

X_{n}(i)=\theta_{i}S_{n}(i){\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{n>\nu\}}}+\xi_{n}(i),\quad n\geqslant 1,~i=1,\dots,N,

where
\theta_{i}
is an unknown intensity or amplitude (
\theta_{i}>0
) of a deterministic signal
S_{n}(i)
(e.g., the signal
S_{n}(i)=\cos(\omega_{i}n)
) and
\{\xi_{n}(i)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
are
mutually independent noises which are AR
(p)
Gaussian stable processes that obey recursions

\xi_{n}(i)=\sum_{t=1}^{p}\varrho_{i,t}\xi_{n-t}(i)+w_{n}(i),\quad n\geqslant 1.

(56)

Here
\{w_{n}(i)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, are mutually independent i.i.d. Gaussian sequences with mean zero and standard deviation
\sigma>0
.
The coefficients
\varrho_{i,1},\dots,\varrho_{i,p}
and variance
\sigma^{2}
are known.

A signal may appear only in one channel and should be detected and isolated quickly, i.e., the number of a channel where the signal
appears should be identified along with detection.

Define
\widetilde{S}_{i,n}=S_{n}(i)-\sum_{t=1}^{p_{n}}\varrho_{i,t}S_{n-t}(i)
and
\widetilde{X}_{i,n}=X_{n}(i)-\sum_{t=1}^{p_{n}}\varrho_{i,t}X_{n-t}(i)
, where
p_{n}=p
if
n>p
and
p_{n}=n
if
n\leqslant p
.
The LLRs have the form

\begin{split}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)&=\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,t}\widetilde{X}_{i,t}-\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,t}^{2},\\
\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)&=\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)-\lambda_{j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n).\end{split}

Under measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\vartheta}
,
\vartheta\in\Theta_{i}
, the LLR
\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)
is a Gaussian process (with independent non-identically distributed increments)
with mean and variance

\begin{split}&{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\vartheta}[\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)]=\\
&\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left[(2\theta_{i}\vartheta-\theta_{i}^{2})\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,t}^{2}+\theta_{j}^{2}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{j,t}^{2}\right],\\
&\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits_{k,i,\vartheta}[\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)]=\\
&\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\theta_{i}^{2}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,t}^{2}+\theta_{j}^{2}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{j,t}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(57)

Let
\Theta_{i}=(0,\infty)
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and assume that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,t}^{2}=Q_{i},

where
0<Q_{i}<\infty
. This is typically the case in most signal processing applications, e.g., for the sequence of sine pulses
S_{n}(i)=\sin(\omega_{i}n+\phi_{i})
with frequency
\omega_{i}
and phase
\phi_{i}
.
Then for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\theta_{i},\theta_{j}\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i};j,\theta_{j}}(k,k+n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}-\text{a.s.}}\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}+\theta_{j}^{2}Q_{j}}{2\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j}),\quad j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\},~i\in{\mathcal{N}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}-\text{a.s.}}\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=I_{i}(\theta_{i}),\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}},

so that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds. Furthermore, since all moments of the LLR are finite condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
holds for all
r\geqslant 1
. Indeed, using (
57
), we obtain that

I_{i}(\vartheta,\theta_{i})=:\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\vartheta}[\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)]=(\vartheta\theta_{i}-\vartheta^{2}/2)Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}

and for any
\varkappa>0

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{|\vartheta-\theta_{i}|<\varkappa}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)<I_{i}(\theta_{i})-\varepsilon\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\sup_{\vartheta\in[\theta_{i}-\varkappa,\theta_{i}+\varkappa]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)-I_{i}(\vartheta,\theta_{i})\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(|Y_{k,n}(\theta_{i})|>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right),

where

and
\{\eta_{t}(i)\}_{t\geqslant 1}
is the sequence of standard zero-mean normal random variables. Hence
\{Y_{k,n}(\theta_{i})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is the sequence of normal random variables with mean zero and
variance
\sigma_{i,n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sigma^{-2}\theta_{i}^{2}\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}(\widetilde{S}_{i,t})^{2}
, which is asymptotic to
\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}
.
Thus, for a sufficiently large

there exists

such that

, and we obtain that for all large

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{|\vartheta-\theta_{i}|<\varkappa}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)<I_{i}(\theta_{i})-\varepsilon\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}\left(|\hat{\eta}|>\frac{\delta_{0}+\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{0}+\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}\left(|\hat{\eta}|>\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{0}+\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}}\right),

where
\hat{\eta}
is a standard normal random variable. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{r}(\varkappa,\varepsilon;i,\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\inf_{|\vartheta-\theta_{i}|<\varkappa}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)

\displaystyle\quad\quad<I_{i}(\theta_{i})-\varepsilon\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(|\hat{\eta}|>\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\delta_{0}+\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/\sigma^{2}}\right),

where the right-hand side term is finite for all

so that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
holds for all
r\geqslant 1
.

Obviously,
\inf_{\theta_{j}\in(0,\infty)}I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}/(2\sigma^{2})=I_{i}(\theta_{i})>0
.
Therefore, by Theorem
3
, the detection–identification rule
\delta_{A}
is asymptotically first-order optimal with respect to all positive moments of the detection delay and asymptotic formulas

48
) and (
50
) hold with

\inf_{\theta_{j}\in(0,\infty)}I_{ij}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=I_{i}(\theta_{i})=\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

If
\max_{j\neq i}\beta_{ji}\geqslant\alpha_{i}
,
\max_{j\neq i}\bar{\beta}_{j}\geqslant\alpha

\mu=0

Note that by condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
rule
\delta_{A}
is asymptotically optimal for almost arbitrary mixing distributions

. In this example, it is most convenient to
select the conjugate prior,

, where

is a standard normal distribution and

, in which case the decision statistics can be computed explicitly.

It is worth noting that this example arises in certain interesting practical applications, e.g., in multichannel/multisensor surveillance systems such as radars, sonars, and electro-optic/infrared sensor systems, which deal
with detecting moving and maneuvering targets that appear at unknown times, and it is necessary to detect a signal from
a randomly appearing target in clutter and noise with the minimal average detection delay as well as to identify a channel where it appears.
See
[
1
,
14
,
7
,
19
]
.
Another challenging application area where the multichannel model is useful is cyber-security

[
17
,
24
,
21
]
. Malicious intrusion attempts in computer networks (spam campaigns, personal data theft,
worms, distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, etc.) incur significant financial damage and are severe harm to the integrity of personal information.
It is therefore essential to devise automated techniques to detect computer network intrusions as quickly as possible so that an appropriate response can be provided and the
negative consequences for the users are eliminated. In particular, DDoS attacks typically involve many traffic streams resulting in a large number of packets aimed at
congesting the target’s server or network.

Concluding Remarks

1. Since we do not specify a class of models for the observations such as Gaussian, Markov, or HMM and build the decision statistics on the LLR processes, we restrict the behavior of LLRs which is expressed by conditions

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
related to the law of large numbers for the LLR and rates of convergence in the law of large numbers. As the example in Section
VIII
shows,
these conditions hold for the additive changes (in the mean) of the AR(
p
) process governed by
the Gaussian process. These conditions also hold in a variety of non-additive examples (detection of changes in spectrum of time series such as AR(
p
) and ARCH(
p
) processes) as well as for a large class of
homogeneous Markov processes
[
11
,
12
]
,
[
18
, Sec 3.1, Ch 4]
and for hidden Markov models with finite hidden state space
[
4
]
.

2. While we focused on the multistream detection–identification problem (
1
), it should be noted that similar results also hold in the “scalar” detection–isolation problem when the
observations
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
represent either a scalar process or a vector process but
all components of this process change at time
\nu
. Specifically, let
\{f_{\theta}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}),\theta\in\Theta\}
be a parametric family of densities and for
i=1,\dots,N
and
\Theta_{i}\subset\Theta
consider the model

\begin{split}&p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i},\theta)=p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})~\text{for}~\nu\geqslant n,\\
&p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|{\mathsf{H}}_{\nu,i},\theta)=\prod_{t=1}^{\nu}f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\prod_{t=\nu+1}^{n}f_{\theta}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\\
&\quad\text{for}~\nu<n,~\theta\in\Theta_{i},\end{split}

where
\theta_{0}
is the known pre-change parameter and
\theta
is the unknown post-change parameter. In other words, there are
N
types of change and for the
i
th type of change the
value of the post-change parameter
\theta
belongs to a subset
\Theta_{i}
of the parameter space
\Theta
. It is necessary to detect and isolate a change as rapidly as possible, i.e.,
to identify what type of change has occurred. The change detection–identification rule
\delta_{A}=(d_{A},T_{A})
is defined as in (
7
) where the statistics

\bar{\Lambda}_{ij}^{\pi,W}(n)
get modified as follows

\begin{split}\bar{\Lambda}_{ij}^{\pi,W}(n)&=\frac{\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\int_{\Theta_{i}}LR_{\theta}(k,n)\,{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\theta)}{\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{j}}LR_{\theta}(k,n)},~~i,j\in{\mathcal{N}};\\
\bar{\Lambda}_{i0}^{\pi,W}(n)&=\frac{\sum_{k=-1}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\int_{\Theta_{i}}LR_{\theta}(k,n)\,{\mathrm{d}}W_{i}(\theta)}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)},\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}\end{split}

with the likelihood ratio

LR_{\theta}(k,n)=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}\frac{f_{\theta}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})}{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})}.

Write
\lambda_{\theta}(k,k+n)=\log LR_{\theta}(k,n)
and
\lambda_{\theta,\theta^{*}}(k,k+n)=\lambda_{\theta}(k,k+n)-\lambda_{\theta^{*}}(k,k+n)
, where
\lambda_{\theta^{*}}(k,k+n)=0
for

. Conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
also get modified as follows

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
.
There exist positive and finite numbers

,
\theta\in\Theta_{i}
,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
I(\theta,\theta^{*})
,
\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{j}
,

,
\theta\in\Theta_{i}
,

i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\theta\in\Theta_{i}
,
\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{j}
,

,

\lim_{M\to\infty}p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;\theta,\theta^{*})=0.

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
and some

\Upsilon_{r}(\varepsilon;\theta)<\infty\quad\text{for all}~\theta\in\Theta_{i},~i\in{\mathcal{N}},

where

\displaystyle p_{M,k}(\varepsilon;\theta;\theta^{*})=

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\left\{\frac{1}{M}\max_{1\leqslant n\leqslant M}\lambda_{\theta,\theta^{*}}(k,k+n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)I(\theta,\theta^{*})\right\},

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{r}(\varepsilon;\theta)=\lim_{\varkappa\to 0}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\theta}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\{\vartheta\in\Theta\,:\,|\vartheta-\theta|<\varkappa\}}\lambda_{\vartheta}(k,k+n)<I(\theta)-\varepsilon\Bigg\}.

for all

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{|\log\alpha_{i}|}{I(\theta)+\mu},\max_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|\log\beta_{ji}|}{\inf_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta_{j}}I(\theta,\theta^{*})}\right\}^{m},

i.e., the detection–identification rule

is asymptotically optimal to first order.

Note also that, in general, these asymptotics are not reduced to (
53
) even when

3. All previous results can be easily generalized for the case when the change points are different for different streams, i.e., when
\nu=\nu_{i}
with prior distributions

, assuming that condition (

)

6. For practical purposes, it is more reasonable to consider a “frequentist” problem setup that does not use prior distributions of the changepoint
