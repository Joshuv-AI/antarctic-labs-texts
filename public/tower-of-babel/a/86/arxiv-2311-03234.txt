Combinatorics of nondeterministic walks
Élie de Panafieu, Michael Wallner

Abstract
This paper introduces nondeterministic walks , a new variant of one-dimensional discrete walks. The main difference to classical walks is that its nondeterministic steps consist of sets of steps from a predefined set such that all possible extensions are explored in parallel. We discuss in detail the nondeterministic Dyck step set { { − 1 } , { 1 } , { − 1 , 1 } } \{\{-1\},\{1\},\{-1,1\}\} and Motzkin step set { { − 1 } , { 0 } , { 1 } , { − 1 , 0 } , { − 1 , 1 } , { 0 , 1 } , { − 1 , 0 , 1 } } \{\{-1\},\{0\},\{1\},\{-1,0\},\{-1,1\},\{0,1\},\{-1,0,1\}\} , and show that several nondeterministic classes of lattice paths, such as nondeterministic bridges, excursions, and meanders are algebraic. The key concept is the generalization of the ending point of a walk to its reachable points, i.e., a set of ending points. We extend our results to general step sets: We show that nondeterministic bridges and several subclasses of nondeterministic meanders are always algebraic. We conjecture the same is true for nondeterministic excursions, and we present python and Maple packages to support our conjecture. This research is motivated by the study of networks involving encapsulation and decapsulation of protocols. Our results are obtained using generating functions, analytic combinatorics, and additive combinatorics. Keywords. Random walks, analytic combinatorics, generating functions, limit laws, networking, encapsulation.

Introduction

In recent years, lattice paths have received a lot of attention in different fields, such as probability theory, computer science, biology, chemistry, and physics
[
24
,
21
,
12
,
6
]
.
One of their key features is their versatility as models to capture natural phenomena, as in up-to-date models of certain polymers
[
23
]
.
In this paper we continue this success story and present a new model of lattice paths capturing
the en- and decapsulation of protocols over networks.
The key to understand them will be to be able to follow all trajectories in the network
simultaneously
.
For this purpose, we generalize the class of lattice paths to so called
nondeterministic lattice paths
.
In our context, this word does not mean “
random
”.
Instead it is understood in the same sense
as for automata and Turing machines.
A process is nondeterministic
if several branches are explored in parallel,
and the process is said to end in an accepting state
if one of those branches ends in an accepting state.
Before we give a precise definition, we recall the classical model of lattice we build on
[
2
]
.

Classical walks.

Given a finite set
S\subset\mathds{Z}
of integers, called the
steps
, a
walk
of length
n
is a sequence

v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
of steps
v_{i}\in S
. In this paper we will always assume that our walks start at the origin.
Its its
endpoint
is equal to
the sum of its steps
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
.
As illustrated in Figure
1a
,
a walk can be visualized by its
geometric realization
as a sequence of ordinates
(y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
.
Since the walk starts at the origin it starts at
y_{0}=0
,
and after appending
k
steps it has reached
y_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}
.
Thus, the relation
v_{i}=y_{i}-y_{i-1}
directly connects the steps and the ordinates of a walk, and shows their equivalence.

We distinguish four different types of walks:

•

A
bridge
is a walk with endpoint
y_{n}=0
.

•

A
meander
is a walk never crossing the
y
-axis,
i.e.,

y_{j}\geq 0
for all
j=0,\ldots,n
.

•

An
excursion
is a meander with endpoint
y_{n}=0
.

Nondeterministic walks.

We are now ready to generalize classical walks to nondeterministic walks.
The only difference to above is that steps are now sets of integers.

Definition 1.1

(Nondeterministic walks)
.

An
N-step

s\subset\mathds{Z}
is a non-empty finite set of integers.
Given an N-step set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
of N-steps,
an
N-walk
of length
n
is a sequence
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
of N-steps
s_{i}\in S
.

In addition, as for classical walks, we always assume that they start at the origin and we distinguish different families.

Definition 1.2

(Families of N-walks)
.

An N-walk
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
and a classical walk
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})

are
compatible
if they have the same length
n
, the same starting point,
and for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
the
i^{\text{th}}
step is included in the
i^{\text{th}}
N-step,
i.e.,

v_{i}\in w_{i}
.
An
N-bridge
(
resp.

N-meander
,
resp.

N-excursion
)
is an N-walk compatible with at least one bridge
(
resp.
meander,
resp.
excursion).
Thus, N-excursions are particular cases of N-meanders.

The endpoints of classical walks are central to the analysis.
We define their nondeterministic analogues.

Definition 1.3

(Reachable points)
.

The
reachable points
of an N-walk (resp. N-meanders) are the endpoints of all compatible walks (resp. meanders).
The minimum (
resp.
maximum) reachable point of an N-walk
w
is denoted by
\min(w)
(
resp.

\max(w)
).
The minimum (
resp.
maximum) reachable point of an N-meander
w
is denoted by
\min^{+}(w)
(
resp.

\max^{+}(w)
).

Note that the reachable endpoints of an N-meander are nonnegative.
The geometric realization of an N-walk
is the sequence of its reachable points after
j
steps for
j=0,\dots,n
, as shown in Figure
1
.

(b)

Moreover, the steps of a walks or N-walk can be weighted.
Then, each step is associated with a weight
p_{i}
and the weight of the N-walk is the product of the weights associated to its steps.
In many cases, these weights
p_{i}
will represent probabilities,
i.e.,

p_{i}\geq 0
and
p_{1}+\dots+p_{m}=1
.
We talk about the

when all weights are equal to one:
p_{i}=1
.

1.2
Motivation and related work

Our nondeterministic model of lattice paths has its roots in networking.
Let us first give a vivid description of the underlying mechanisms using Russian dolls.

Russian dolls.

Suppose we have a set of
n+1
people arranged in a line. There are three kinds of people. A person of the first kind is only able to put a received doll in a bigger one. A person of the second kind is only able to extract a smaller doll (if any) from a bigger one. If she receives the smallest doll, then she throws it away. Finally, a person of the third kind can either put a doll in a bigger one or extract a smaller doll if any.
We want to know if it is possible for the last person to receive the smallest doll after it has been given to the first person and then, consecutively, handed from person to person while performing their respective operations.
This is equivalent to asking if a given N-walk with each N-step
\in\left\{\{1\},\{-1\},\{-1,1\}\right\}
is an N-excursion,
i.e.,
if the N-walk is compatible with at least one excursion. The probabilistic version of this question is: what is the probability that the last person can receive the smallest doll according to some distribution on the set of people over the three kinds?

Networks and encapsulations.

The original motivation of this work comes from networking. In a network, some nodes are able to encapsulate protocols (put a packet of a protocol inside a packet of another one), decapsulate protocols (extract a nested packet from another one), or perform any of these two operations (albeit most nodes are only able to transmit packets as they receive them). Typically, a tunnel is a subpath starting with an encapsulation and ending with the corresponding decapsulation. Tunnels are very useful for achieving several goals in networking (
e.g.,
interoperability: connecting IPv6 networks across IPv4 ones
[
33
]
; security and privacy: securing IP connections
[
30
]
, establishing Virtual Private Networks
[
29
]
, etc.). Moreover, tunnels can be nested to achieve several goals. Replacing the Russian dolls by packets, it is easy to see that an encapsulation can be modeled by a
\{1\}
-step and a decapsulation by a
\{-1\}
-step, while a passive transmission of a packet is modeled by a
\{0\}
-step.

Given a network with some nodes that are able to encapsulate or decapsulate protocols, a path from a sender to a receiver is
feasible
if it allows the latter to retrieve a packet exactly as dispatched by the sender.
Computing the shortest feasible path between two nodes is polynomial
[
25
]
if cycles are allowed without restriction. In contrast, the problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if cycles are forbidden or arbitrarily limited. In
[
25
]
, the algorithms are compared through worst-case complexity analysis and simulation. The simulation methodology for a fixed network topology is to make encapsulation (
resp.
decapsulation) capabilities available with some probability
p
and observe the processing time of the different algorithms. It would be interesting, for simulation purposes, to generate random networks with a given probability of existence of a feasible path between two nodes. This work is the first step towards achieving this goal, since our results give the probability that any path is feasible (
i.e.,
is a N-excursion) according to a probability distribution of encapsulation and decapsulation capabilities over the nodes.

Lattice paths.

Nondeterministic walks naturally connect between lattice paths and branching processes.
This is underlined by our usage of many well-established analytic and algebraic tools previously used to study lattice paths.
In particular, those are the robustness of D-finite functions
with respect to the Hadamard product,
and the kernel method
[
19
,
11
,
2
,
7
,
3
]
.

The N-walks are nondeterministic one-dimensional discrete walks.
We will see that their generating functions require three variables:
one marking the lowest point
\min(w)
that can be reached by the N-walk
w
,
another one marking the highest point
\max(w)
,
and the last one marking its length.
For this reason they are also closely related to two-dimensional lattice paths,
when interpreting
\left(\min(w),\max(w)\right)
as coordinates in the plane.

Nondeterminism and context-free grammars.

Nondeterministic walks are very closely linked to nondeterministic finite automata and context free grammars.
In particular,
for any finite set of N-steps the class of N-excursions, N-meanders, and N-bridges can be encoded by a context-free grammar.
To see this, note that
languages generated by context-free grammars are equivalently recognized by pushdown automata with a single stack.
These automata are by definition nondeterministic, hence they allow epsilon transitions which we use to follow all trajectories in the N-walk simultaneously.
Then the stack is used to keep track of the distance of the current trajectory to the
x
-axis, thereby distinguishing between different types of N-walks.

If a context-free language admits an unambiguous grammar, the generating function of the number of its words of length
n
is algebraic.
These words of length
n
are exactly the N-walks of length
n
, and hence, this gives a strong hint that the N-walks we analyze in this paper are algebraic.
However, it is not obvious how to find a non-ambiguous grammar directly, and even if it is found, how to derive the characterizing algebraic equations.
Moreover, the associated system of equation is already for small examples nearly impossible to solve (resultants of large degree).
For this reason, we show in this article an alternative approach for the enumeration and asymptotics building on the “kernel method”.
It has the advantage that it captures the algebraic nature of the generating functions and allows to compute the asymptotics via singularity analysis.

We leave as an interesting open problem
to determine which N-step sets
S

correspond to non-ambiguous context free languages
for N-bridges or N-excursions.

2
Dyck N-walks

One of the most fundamental families of classical lattice paths are Dyck paths, which are excursions associated to the step set
\{-1,1\}
.
They are enumerated by the ubiquitous Catalan numbers, which also enumerate many other combinatorial objects.
In this section we study their corresponding N-walks.
A
Dyck N-walks
is an N-walk associated with the N-step set

S_{D}=\Big\{\{-1\},\{1\},\{-1,1\}\Big\}.

Additionally, every step is associated with a weight

p_{-1},p_{1}
, and
p_{-1,1}
, respectively.
In the following we will talk of Dyck N-meanders, Dyck N-bridges, and Dyck N-excursions if the walks use the above N-step
S_{D}
and are N-meanders, N-bridges, or N-excursions, respectively.

2.1
Dyck N-walks and N-bridges

In order to enumerate any class of Dyck N-walks we need to study their reachable points.

Lemma 2.1
.

The reachable points of a Dyck N-walk
w
are

\left\{\min(w)+2i\ |\ i\in\left\{0,\dots,\frac{\max(w)-\min(w)}{2}\right\}\right\}.

The same result holds for Dyck N-meanders,
with
\min(w)
and
\max(w)
replaced by
\min^{+}(w)
and
\max^{+}(w)
; see Definition
1.3
.

Proof.

We perform an induction on the number of steps.
A walk starts at
0
.
After appending a new N-step the reachable points are either shifted down or up by one unit by
\{-1\}
or
\{1\}
, respectively, or they are the sum of both shifts by
\{-1,1\}
.
Hence, there is always a distance
2
between the reachable points.
∎

By Lemma
2.1
the reachable points are fully determined by their minimum and maximum.
Therefore, we define the generating functions
D(x,y;t)
and
D^{+}(x,y;t)
,
of Dyck N-walks and Dyck N-meanders, respectively, as

\displaystyle D(x,y;t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\text{Dyck N-walk $w$}}\bigg(\prod_{s\in w}p_{s}\bigg)x^{\min(w)}y^{\max(w)}t^{|w|},

\displaystyle D^{+}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\text{Dyck N-meander $w$}}\bigg(\prod_{s\in w}p_{s}\bigg)x^{\min^{+}(w)}y^{\max^{+}(w)}t^{|w|}.

Note that by construction these are power series in
t
with Laurent polynomials in
x
and
y
, as each of the finitely many N-walks of length
n
has a finite minimum and maximum reachable point.

Remark 2.2
.

The variables
x
and
y
are called
catalytic variables
, as they facilitate the enumeration of such paths by length but are not part of the initial enumeration question.
The reachable points used here are a generalization of the coordinate of the end point that is used for classical lattice paths; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
10
]
.

As a direct corollary of Lemma
2.1
,
all N-bridges and N-excursions have even length.
The number of Dyck N-bridges and Dyck N-excursions are then, respectively, given by

\displaystyle[x^{\leq 0}y^{\geq 0}t^{2n}]D(x,y;t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad[t^{2n}]D^{+}(0,1;t),

(1)

where
the nonpositive part extraction operator
[x^{\leq 0}]

[x^{\leq 0}]\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{k}x^{k}:=\sum_{k\leq 0}f_{k}x^{k}

(and analogously for
[y^{\geq 0}]

In the following two subsections we derive closed-form expressions for these quantities.
The generating function of Dyck N-walks has a particularly simple shape.

Proposition 2.3
.

The generating function of Dyck N-walks is given by

\displaystyle D(x,y;t)=\frac{1}{1-t\left(\frac{p_{-1}}{xy}+p_{1}xy+p_{-1,1}\frac{y}{x}\right)}.

(2)

The only N-walk of length

We obtain each non-empty N-walk, by appending an N-step to another N-walk.

2.2
Dyck N-meanders and N-excursions

We continue by deriving a functional equation for Dyck N-meanders using a recursive step-by-step decomposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

The generating function of Dyck N-meanders is characterized by the relation

\displaystyle D^{+}(x,y;t)=1

\displaystyle+t\left(p_{-1}x^{-1}y^{-1}+p_{1}xy+p_{-1,1}x^{-1}y\right)\left(D^{+}(x,y;t)-D^{+}(0,y;t)\right)

\displaystyle+t\left(p_{-1}xy^{-1}+(p_{1}+p_{-1,1})xy\right)\left(D^{+}(0,y;t)-D^{+}(0,0;t)\right)

\displaystyle+t\left(p_{1}+p_{-1,1}\right)xyD^{+}(0,0;t).

Proof.

Applying the symbolic method (see
[

),
we translate the following combinatorial characterization
of N-meanders into the claimed equation.
An N-meander is either of length
0
,
or it can be uniquely decomposed into an N-meander

followed by an N-step.

If
\min^{+}(w)>0
then any N-step can be appended.
The generating function of N-meanders
with positive minimum reachable point
is
D^{+}(x,y;t)-D^{+}(0,y;t)

If
\min^{+}(w)=0
but
\max^{+}(w)>0
, which corresponds to the generating function
D^{+}(0,y;t)-D^{+}(0,0;t)

then an additional N-step

increases

(the path ending at

becomes the minimum) and decreases
\max^{+}(w)

while an additional N-step

or

and
\max^{+}(w)

Finally, if

which corresponds to the generating function

then the N-step

is forbidden,
and the two other available N-steps both increase
\min^{+}(w)
and
\max^{+}(w)
.

The representation (

However, for the problem of a changing step sets depending on the current spatial position not much is known. Recently, the problem where the position depends on a nondeterministic automaton was treated for some special cases in

Let us introduce the

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S(x,y)&:=\frac{p_{-1}}{xy}+p_{1}xy+p_{-1,1}\frac{y}{x},\\

\displaystyle D(x,y;t)=\frac{1}{1-tS(x,y)}.

Remark 2.6

(N-walks for general step sets)

Note that for any finite N-step set

\displaystyle S(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S}p_{s}\,x^{\min(s)}y^{\max(s)}.

Then, equation (

A key role in the following result on the closed form of Dyck N-meanders
is played by

Remark 2.12

Let us answer the question of how many classical excursions are contained in an N-excursion in the unweighted model, i.e.,

2.3
Limit laws of N-excursion

In order to better understand the influence of the probabilities of the N-steps, we will analyze some parameters in N-excursions.
In particular, we will derive the limit law of the maximal reachable point and the number of returns to zero.

The following result will depend on the relative change in the minimal and maximal reachable point. We define the

\delta_{x}=\mathbb{E}(x)
and the

-drift
\delta_{y}=\mathbb{E}(y)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta_{x}&=p_{1}-p_{-1,1}-p_{-1}=2p_{1}-1,\\
\delta_{y}&=p_{1}+p_{-1,1}-p_{-1}=1-2p_{-1}.\end{aligned}

Proof.

After this detailed discussion of nondeterministic walks derived from Dyck paths, we turn to the probably next most classical lattice paths: Motzkin paths.

3
Motzkin N-walks

The step set of classical Motzkin paths is
\{-1,0,1\}
.
The N-step set of all non-empty subsets is

S_{M}=\Big\{\{-1\},\{0\},\{1\},\{-1,0\},\{-1,1\},\{0,1\},\{-1,0,1\}\Big\},

and we call the corresponding N-walks (N-meanders, N-excursions, …)
Motzkin N-walks
(
Motzkin N-meanders
,
Motzkin N-excursions
, …).
Note that we will perform the subsequent analysis only in the unweighted case, however, as shown in the previous section on Dyck N-walks, it is straightforward to generalize the results to the weighted case.

3.1
Motzkin N-walks and N-bridges

As for Dyck N-walks, we start with a description of the reachable points of Motzkin N-walks.
Here, we need to distinguish two cases.

Definition 3.1

(Types of reachable points)
.

A Motzkin N-walk
w
is

•

of
type
\operatorname{{I}}
if
\operatorname{reach}(w)=\left\{\min(w)+i~:~i=0,1,\dots,\|w\|\right\}
and
\|w\|\geq 1
.

•

of
type
\operatorname{{II}}
if
\operatorname{reach}(w)=\left\{\min(w)+2i~:~i=0,1,\dots,\frac{\|w\|}{2}\right\}
,

In other words, the sets of reachable points are either
1
- or
2
-periodic finite subsets of integers.
The condition
\|w\|\geq 1
guarantees that the types are disjoint.
For Dyck N-walks we did not need to distinguish different types, as they where always of type
\operatorname{{II}}
.
Now, the following proposition will explain how these two types suffice to characterize the structure of Motzkin N-walks.

Proposition 3.2
.

A Motzkin N-walk
w
is of type
\operatorname{{II}}
if and only if
it is constructed only from the N-steps
\{-1\}
,
\{0\}
,
\{1\}
, and
\{-1,1\}
.
Otherwise, it is of type
\operatorname{{I}}
; see Figure
7
.

Proof.

We use induction on the number of N-steps.
If the Motzkin N-walk is empty, its set of reachable points
is
\{0\}
, which is of type
\operatorname{{II}}
by definition.
Now consider a Motzkin N-walk
w

of reachable points
\bm{r}
,
a Motzkin N-step
\bm{s}
,
and the set of reachable points of
w\cdot\bm{s}
,
given by
\bm{r}+\bm{s}
.
Let us assume the proposition holds for
w

and prove it for
w\cdot\bm{s}
.

If
\bm{s}
has size
1
, like
\{-1\}
,
\{0\}
or
\{1\}
,

\bm{r}+\bm{s}
is just a translation of
\bm{r}
,
so the type is unchanged.

If
\bm{r}
is of type
\operatorname{{I}}
,
it is an integer interval of size
\ell\geq 2
,
so there exists
m\in\mathds{Z}
such that

\bm{r}=[m,m+\ell]
.
Then for any Motzkin N-step
\bm{s}
,

\bm{r}+\bm{s}
is also an integer interval,
of size
\ell+\max(\bm{s})-\min(\bm{s})
.

If
\bm{r}
is of type
\operatorname{{II}}

and
\bm{s}=\{-1,1\}
, then we saw in Lemma
2.1

that
\bm{r}+\bm{s}
has type
\operatorname{{II}}
.
The last case is when
\bm{r}
has type
\operatorname{{II}}
,
say
\bm{r}=\{m,m+2,\ldots,m+2\ell\}
,
and
\bm{s}
is equal to
\{-1,0\}
,
\{0,1\}
, or
\{-1,0,1\}
.
The value of
\bm{r}+\bm{s}
, in each case,
is
[m-1,m+2\ell]
,
[m,m+2\ell+1]
or
[m-1,m+2\ell+1]
.
It is always an interval of length at least
2
,
so it is of type
\operatorname{{I}}
.
∎

\operatorname{{I}}
(

(

\displaystyle M_{\operatorname{{I}}}(x,y;t)

Theorem 3.3
.

The generating functions of Motzkin N-walks of type
\operatorname{{I}}
and
\operatorname{{II}}
, as well as for all Motzkin N-walks are rational.

Proof.

This result is a direct consequence of the analysis of the interactions of types and N-steps in the proof of Proposition
3.2
; see also Figure

.
Translating the change in the minimal and maximal reachable points for each step into polynomials, we directly get

\displaystyle M_{\operatorname{{II}}}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=1+t\left(\frac{1}{xy}+1+xy+\frac{y}{x}\right)M_{\operatorname{{II}}}(x,y;t),

\displaystyle M_{\operatorname{{I}}}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=t\left(\frac{1}{x}+y+\frac{y}{x}\right)M_{\operatorname{{II}}}(x,y;t)+t\left(\frac{1}{xy}+1+xy+\frac{1}{x}+y+2\frac{y}{x}\right)M_{\operatorname{{I}}}(x,y;t).

Alternatively, note that by Remark

is given by (

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

The generating function of Motzkin N-bridges is algebraic.

Proof.

An N-bridge
w
of type
\operatorname{{II}}
is an
M_{\operatorname{{II}}}
N-walk that satisfies
\min(w)\leq 0
,
\max(w)\geq 0
, and
\min(w)
even.
An N-bridge
w
of type
\operatorname{{I}}
is an
M_{\operatorname{{I}}}
N-walk that satisfies
\min(w)\leq 0
and
\max(w)\geq 0
.
Thus, the generating function of Motzkin N-bridges is equal to

Since the generating functions of

and

are rational, the generating function of N-bridges is D-finite; see
[

[

Next, we rearrange this equation into

On the left hand side, we identify a factorization involving the explicit factor

If this system would consist of only a single equation, we could apply the classical kernel method; see,

However, for this system we need to generalize this approach to matrix equations; see

Our idea is to choose specific values of

becomes singular, and then left-multiply by an element from its kernel to deduce a new identity.
Due to the triangular nature of

\displaystyle 1-tA_{0,0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 1-tA_{1,1}(x,y)

This gives two candidates

\displaystyle X_{1}(y,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-t-\sqrt{1-4t^{2}y^{2}-3t^{2}-2t}}{2ty},

\displaystyle X_{2}(y,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-t(y+1)-\sqrt{1-7t^{2}y^{2}-2t^{2}y-3t^{2}-2ty-2t}}{2ty}.

We then define the row vectors

\displaystyle\bm{u_{1}}

\displaystyle=(1,0),

\displaystyle\bm{u_{2}}(y,t)

\displaystyle=(1,0),

\displaystyle=\left(\operatorname{Id}-tA(x,y)\right)^{-1}(\bm{e_{1}}-t\left(A(x,y)-B(x,y)\right)\bm{M^{+}}(0,y;t)

\displaystyle\quad-t\left(B(x,y)-C(x,y)\right)\bm{M^{+}}(0,0;t)).

3.3
Bijections with two-dimensional lattice paths

Any N-step set can be interpreted as a two-dimensional lattice path problem.
The minimum reachable point is mapped to the
x
-coordinate and the maximum reachable point to the
y
-coordinate:
We define the mapping
\varphi
from N-steps to two-dimensional steps as

\displaystyle\varphi(s)=\left(\min(s),\max(s)\right).

This generalizes the previously discussed mapping (
3
).
For Motzkin N-steps
S_{M}
this gives the following mapping to nearest neighbor steps:

\displaystyle\{-1\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1,-1),

\displaystyle\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(0,1),

\displaystyle\{-1,1\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1,1)_{1},

\displaystyle\{0\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(0,0),

\displaystyle\{-1,0\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1,0),

\displaystyle\{-1,0,1\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-1,1)_{2},

\displaystyle\{1\}

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,1).

Note that the step
(-1,1)
comes in two colors, depending on whether it corresponds to
\{-1,1\}
or
\{-1,0,1\}
.
We denote the two colors by
(-1,1)_{1}
and
(-1,1)_{2}
.
Moreover, by construction the steps
(1,0)
,
(1,-1)
, and
(0,-1)
will never be used.
This played a crucial role in proving the algebraicity of N-bridges in Theorems
2.4
and
3.4
as it led to Equation (
4
).
In the following section, we will show that this fact holds for arbitrary finite N-step sets, and also allows to prove the algebraicity of general N-bridges.

Now we use
\varphi
to give bijections between different families of Motzkin N-walks and two-dimensional walks.
In general the steps above are used, just on the axes we might need to modify the steps; compare with Figure
3
.

1.

N-walks are in bijection with unconstrained two-dimensional walks.

2.

N-bridges are in bijection with two-dimensional walks ending in the second quadrant, where if only steps
\{(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1),(-1,1)_{1}\}
are used it has to be of even length.

3.

N-meanders are in bijection with two-dimensional walks confined to the first quadrant, where the positive
y
-axis and the origin use different steps that depend on the possible types.

4.

N-excursions are in bijection with two-dimensional walks associated with N-meanders that end on the
y
-axis.

4
N-bridges with general N-steps

We have already seen in (
7
) that the generating function of N-walks is always rational.
In this section we treat the generating function of N-bridges for arbitrary finite N-step set.
The following main result generalizes Theorems
2.4
and
3.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any finite N-step set
S
, the generating function
B(x,y;t)
of N-bridges with respect to length, minimal, and maximal reachable point is algebraic.

A method for computing this generating function is provided by the proof,
in Subsection
4.2
.
In order to establish this result,
we first establish the required algebraic setting.

4.1
Sumset monoid

This subsection contains the algebraic definitions
needed to describe sets of reachable points
of N-walks on a given N-step set.

Finite integer sets.

We consider the set
\mathcal{F}
containing
all finite sets of integers

\mathcal{F}=\{\bm{s}\mid\bm{s}\subset\mathds{Z},\ |\bm{s}|<\infty\}.

They will represent N-steps as well as
sets of reachable points.

Sum.

It is equipped with a sum,
known as the
sumset
or
Minkowski sum
,
and defined by

\bm{s}+\bm{t}=\{a+b\mid a\in\bm{s},\ b\in\bm{t}\}.

This is a central operation in additive combinatorics

[
32
]
.
Together,
\mathcal{F}
and the sumset operation form
a commutative monoid with neutral element
\{0\}
.
Observe that for any
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{F}
,
we have
\bm{s}+\emptyset=\emptyset
.
Furthermore, shifting a set
\bm{s}
by
c\in\mathds{Z}

corresponds to a sumset with the set
\{c\}

\bm{s}+\{c\}=\{a+c\mid a\in\bm{s}\}.

The link with N-walks is that the set of reachable points
\bm{r}

of any N-walk
w=\bm{s}_{1}\cdots\bm{s}_{n}

is the sumset of its N-steps:
\bm{r}=\bm{s}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{s}_{n}
.

Product.

Given a nonnegative integer
n
and
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{F}
,
we define
n\times\bm{s}
as the
n
-fold sumset of
\bm{s}
with itself

n\times\bm{s}=\bm{s}+\cdots+\bm{s}=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\mid a_{i}\in\bm{s}\}.

For example, we have

n\times\{0,m\}=\{k\,m\mid 0\leq k\leq n\}

and
0\times\bm{s}=\{0\}
by convention.

Norm.

The
norm
of any
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\|\bm{s}\|=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\bm{s}=\emptyset,\\
\max(\bm{s})-\min(\bm{s})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Bottom pruning.

Let
\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathcal{F}
be two sets of integers.
Then
bottom pruning
is defined as

\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{t}=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{if }\bm{s}=\emptyset,\\
\bm{s}\setminus(\{\min(\bm{s})\}+\bm{t})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It corresponds to shifting
\bm{t}
to the bottom of
\bm{s}
,
and then removing the corresponding elements from
\bm{s}
.
In other words,
\bm{t}
accounts for the elements relative to
\min(\bm{s})
.
Note that negative elements in
\bm{t}
will always lie outside the range after shifting.
For example, removing the smallest element of
\bm{s}

corresponds to removing
\{0\}
, and

\{3,4,6,7,8\}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\{-2,1,3,5\}=\{3,4,6,7,8\}\setminus\{1,4,6,8\}=\{3,7\}.

Top pruning.

Let
\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathcal{F}
be two sets of integers.
Then
top pruning
is defined as

\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{t}=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{if }\bm{s}=\emptyset,\\
\bm{s}\setminus(\{\max(\bm{s})\}-\bm{t})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Top pruning corresponds to removing from the top,
where
\bm{t}
accounts for the elements relative to
\max(\bm{s})

from top to bottom.
Again, negative elements in
\bm{t}
will lie outside the range after shifting.
For example, removing the largest element of
\bm{s}

corresponds to a top pruning by
\{0\}
, and

\{0,1,2,3,4\}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\{1,2\}=\{0,1,4\}.

Compare the previous example to the bottom pruning

\{0,1,2,3,4\}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\{1,2\}=\{0,3,4\}.

Equivalence.

Two integer sets
\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathcal{F}

are
equivalent
, denoted by
\bm{s}\sim\bm{t}
,
if there exists an integer
m
such that

\bm{s}=\bm{t}+\{m\}
.
In other words, one can be obtained from the other by a shift.

Conjugate.

The
conjugate
of an element of
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\overline{\bm{s}}=\{\min(\bm{s})+\max(\bm{s})-a\mid a\in\bm{s}\}.

For example, the conjugate of
\{1,3,4\}
is
\{1,2,4\}
.
Furthermore, bottom and top pruning are linked by conjugation

\overline{\bm{s}}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{t}\sim\overline{\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{t}}.

Type.

For two nonnegative integers
n
,
k

and
\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}\in\mathcal{F}
,
we define
\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
as the following subset of
\mathcal{F}
:

\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\left\{j\times\{0,n\}+\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}+\{m\}\mid m\in\mathds{Z},\ j\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq k}\right\}.

For example,

\operatorname{type}(3,1,\emptyset,\{0,1\},\{1,2,3\})=\left\{\{0,4\},\{0,1,3,7\},\{0,1,3,4,6,10\},\{0,1,3,4,6,7,9,13\},\dots\right\}.

Provided
n\geq 1
and
\bm{b}\neq\emptyset
,

\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
contains elements of arbitrarily large norm.
However, the key property is that each
\bm{t}\in\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
is uniquely characterized
by
\min(\bm{t})
and
\max(\bm{t})
.
We will prove in Lemma
4.2

that there are finitely many
possible types for the reachable points
of an N-walk on a given N-step set.
In our generating functions, we will keep track
of the minimal and maximal reachable points,
as well as the type of the reachable points.
This information will be enough
to reconstruct the complete set of reachable points.
Below are a few examples of relations between types:

\displaystyle\operatorname{type}(0,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\{\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}+\{m\}\mid m\in\mathds{Z}\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\emptyset,\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\{\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{type}(1,0,\emptyset,\{0\},\emptyset)

\displaystyle=\{[u,v]\mid v\geq u\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b}+\{m\},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\{\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}\mid\bm{s}\in\operatorname{type}(n,k,\emptyset,\bm{b},\emptyset)\}.

Proper type.

A type
(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
is
proper

if either

•

n=0
, then
k=0
,
\bm{a}=\bm{c}=\emptyset
and
\min(\bm{b})=0
, or

•

n>0
, then
\min(\bm{a})\geq 0
,
\min(\bm{c})\geq 0
,
\min(\bm{b})=0
,
\bm{b}\subset[0,n-1]
,
and
kn>\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{c})
.

The condition on
\bm{b}
guarantees that in the sumset of
j\times\{0,n\}
and
\bm{b}
there are no overlaps.
Therefore, all
\bm{s}
in a proper type are, except for the beginning and the end, constructed using a periodic repetition of a pattern defined by
\bm{b}
of size
n
.

Since for any
m\in\mathds{Z}
and type
(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
,
we have

\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(n,k,\bm{a}\cap\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0},\bm{b}+\{m\},\bm{c}\cap\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}),

and

\operatorname{type}(0,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(0,0,\emptyset,\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c},\emptyset),

any type with
n=0
is equal to a proper type,
and we will prove in Lemma
4.6

that for any type
(n,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
with
n>0
,
there exists
k^{\prime}
large enough such that
the type
(n,k^{\prime},\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
is equal to a proper type.

4.2
Automaton for N-bridges

For N-walks and in particular N-bridges, we will show that finitely many types suffice to capture all possible sets of reachable points (of which in general, however, infinitely many will exist).

Proposition 4.2
.

For any finite subset
S\subset\mathcal{F}
,
there is a finite set of types
(n_{i},k_{i},\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{i},\bm{c}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}

such that any element generated by
(S,+)

belongs to
\operatorname{type}(n_{i},k_{i},\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{i},\bm{c}_{i})
for some
1\leq i\leq k
.

The last proposition is almost enough to prove
that general N-bridges have algebraic generating functions.
In fact, we rely on the following, more effective, generalization.

Proposition 4.3
.

For any finite subset
S\subset\mathcal{F}
of N-steps,
there is a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}
on the alphabet
S

where each state corresponds to a proper type,
such that for any N-walk
w=(\bm{s}_{1},\ldots,\bm{s}_{m})\in S^{m}
,
the sumset
\bm{s}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{s}_{m}

belongs to the type corresponding to the state that

\mathcal{A}
reaches after reading
w
.
Furthermore, if loops are removed,
the directed graph underlying this automaton is acyclic.

Before proving this proposition,
we show how it implies the proof of the main result of this section, Theorem
4.1
,
showing that for any finite N-step set
S
,
the generating function of N-bridges is algebraic.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We start with the automaton from Proposition
4.3
.
By construction, each N-walk, read step-by-step, corresponds to a walk on this automaton such that the current state corresponds to the current type of the reachable points.
Now, we associate to each state
q
a generating function

F_{q}(x,y;t)=\sum_{n,i,j\geq 0}f^{(q)}_{i,j,n}x^{i}y^{j}t^{n},

where
f^{(q)}_{i,j,n}
is the number of N-walks of length
n
whose reachable points have type associated to
q
, minimum
i
, and maximum
j
.
As in the previous proofs of Theorems
2.4
and
3.4
for the Dyck and Motzkin cases, it is now easy to build a characterizing system of equations for
F_{q}(x,y;t)
.
This system is finite and therefore each generating function is rational.

It remains to extract N-bridges, i.e., N-walks
w
such that
0\in\operatorname{reach}(w)
.
We can do this now for each type individually.
For all types, it is necessary that
i\leq 0
and
j\geq 0
.
In terms of generating functions, this translates to

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{q}(x,y;t):=F_{q}(x,y;t)-[x^{>0}]F_{q}(x,y;t)-[y^{<0}]F_{q}(x,y;t).

Indeed, the key observation is as before that
[x^{>0}y^{<0}]F_{q}(x,y;t)=0
, since the maximum can never by smaller than the minimum.
Recall that the positive (and negative) part of a rational generating function is algebraic
[
20
, Section 6]
.
Moreover, for
0\in\operatorname{reach}(w)
and a given type
\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})
,
we additionally require
i\in-\bm{b}\mod g
,
i\notin-\bm{a}
, and
j\notin\bm{c}
.
First, we extract the arithmetic progressions of the minimum associated with
\bm{b}
. This is a standard method, which we recall for completeness:
Let
\omega
be a
g
th root of unity. Then,

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{q}(x,y;t):=\sum_{b\in\bm{b}}\frac{1}{g}\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}\omega^{i-b}\tilde{B}(\omega^{i}x,y;t)

Second, it remains to exclude the finite amount of values determined by
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
to get the generating function
B_{q}(x,y;t)
of bridges associated with state
q
as

\displaystyle B_{q}(x,y;t)=\hat{B}(x,y;t)-\sum_{a\in\bm{a}}[x^{-a}]\hat{B}(x,y;t)-\sum_{c\in\bm{c}}[y^{c}]\hat{B}(x,y;t).

Finally, the generating function of bridges is the sum of the generating functions
B_{q}(x,y;t)
over all states
q
.
Algebraic functions are closed under a range of operations, including finite sums, derivatives, and substitutions of constants, among others; see
[
19
]
.
∎

The rest of this section is dedicated
to the proof of Proposition
4.3
.
Let
\sum_{\geq m}S
denote the family
of sumsets containing at least
k
occurrences
of each N-step from
S

\sum_{\geq m}S\subseteq\Big\{\sum_{\bm{s}\in S}k_{\bm{s}}\times\bm{s}\mid\ k_{\bm{s}}\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq k}\Big\}.

The following lemma is the first key result to prove that finitely many types are sufficient.
It shows that the reachable points of any N-walk after sufficiently many steps are, except for the start and the end, equivalent to an integer interval.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any N-step set
S
,
there is an integer
m

and a type
(g,0,\bm{a},\{0\},\bm{c})
such that

\sum_{\geq m}S\subseteq\operatorname{type}(g,0,\bm{a},\{0\},\bm{c}).

Proof.

If
S
is empty, we fix
m=0
and have

\sum_{\geq m}S=\{\{0\}\},

as the empty sumset is equal
to the neutral element
\{0\}
.
In that case, we choose

g=0
and
\bm{a}=\bm{c}=\emptyset
.
If
S
contains the N-step
\emptyset
, then
\sum_{\geq m}S=\{\emptyset\}
for all
m\geq 1
.
Hence, we may choose
m=1
, and again
g=0
and
\bm{a}=\bm{c}=\emptyset
.

Let us now assume
S
does not contain
\emptyset
.
Let
S^{\prime}
denote the set of N-steps from
S

of size at least
2
, shifted so that their minimum is
0

S^{\prime}=\{\{x-\min(\bm{s})\mid x\in\bm{s}\}\mid\bm{s}\in S,\ |\bm{s}|\geq 2\}.

Any sumset of N-steps from
S

can be rewritten as a shifted version
of a sumset of N-steps from
S^{\prime}
.
Indeed, consider such a sumset where

(\bm{s}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
denote the N-steps of size at least
2
,
and
(\{c_{i}\})_{1\leq i\leq m}
denote the N-steps of size
1
.
Then

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{s}_{n}+\{c_{1}\}+\cdots+\{c_{m}\}=\

\displaystyle\{x-\min(\bm{s}_{1})\mid x\in\bm{s}_{1}\}+\cdots+\{x-\min(\bm{s}_{n})\mid x\in\bm{s}_{n}\}

\displaystyle+\{c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}+\min(\bm{s}_{1})+\cdots+\min(\bm{s}_{n})\}.

Since by definition types are stable by shift,
it is sufficient to prove the lemma for
S^{\prime}
instead of
S
.
Let
g
denote the greatest common divisor of the union of the N-steps from
S^{\prime}
.
We define another set
S^{\prime\prime}
as the set of N-steps contracted by
g
:

S^{\prime\prime}=\left\{\left\{\frac{x}{g}\mid x\in\bm{s}\right\}\mid\bm{s}\in S^{\prime}\right\}.

Any sumset of N-steps from
S^{\prime}
is obtained
from the corresponding sumset of N-steps from
S^{\prime\prime}

by dilation by
g
.
Thus, if we prove the lemma for
S^{\prime\prime}
,
obtaining a type
(1,k,\bm{a},\{0\},\bm{c})
,
then the lemma holds for
S^{\prime}
with type

(g,k,\{gx\mid x\in\bm{a}\},\{0\},\{gx\mid x\in\bm{c}\})
.

Let
\bm{a}
denote the points that cannot be obtained
as nonnegative linear combinations of elements from the N-steps

\bm{a}=\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus\bigg\{\sum_{t\,\in\!\!\bigcup\limits_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\!\bm{s}}k_{t}\,t\mid k_{t}\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}\bigg\}.

According to Schur’s Theorem, for any set
T

of positive integers of greatest common divisor
1
, there is a smallest number
F_{T}\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
called the
Frobenius number
,
such that for any
n>F_{T}
,
there exist nonnegative integers
(k_{t})_{t\in T}
such that

n=\sum_{t\in T}k_{t}\,t.

Let
F
denote the Frobenius number
for
\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}
.
Schur’s Theorem implies that
\bm{a}
is a finite set,
with maximum
F
.
Let also
\overline{S^{\prime\prime}}
denote the family
of conjugate N-steps from
S^{\prime\prime}

\overline{S^{\prime\prime}}=\{\overline{\bm{s}}\mid\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}\},

and
\overline{F}
denote the Frobenius numbers for

\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\overline{\bm{s}}
.
The integer set
\bm{c}
is defined like
\bm{a}
, but for
\overline{S^{\prime\prime}}

instead of
S^{\prime\prime}
.
The maximum of
\bm{c}
is equal to
\overline{F}
.
Moreover, let
M
and
m_{0}
denote the integers

M=\max_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\max_{x\in\bm{s}}x\quad\text{ and }\quad m_{0}=F+\overline{F}+M.

The reason for the definition of
M

is that for any
n\geq M
, any interval
[a,b]
of length
n
,
and any N-step
\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}
, it holds that
[a,b]+\bm{s}
is still an interval now
of length
n+\|\bm{s}\|
.
By definition of
\bm{a}
,
the integers from
[0,F+M]
that belong
to a sumset of N-steps from
S^{\prime\prime}
are

[0,F+M]\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}.

Those points
y
are characterized by the existence of
nonnegative integers
(k_{t})_{t\in\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}}

such that

y=\sum_{t\in\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}}k_{t}\,t.

We have

m_{0}\geq y\geq\sum_{t\in\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}}k_{t}.

Now, note that as all N-steps in
S^{\prime\prime}
contain
0
, it holds that once
y\in\bm{r}
we have
y\in\bm{r}+\bm{s}
for all
\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}
.
Thus,
y
is in particular reached by
m_{0}\times\sum_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}
.
We conclude that the bottom of any element
\bm{r}\in\sum_{\geq m_{0}}S^{\prime\prime}

contains all integers, except the elements from
\bm{a}
:

\bm{r}\cap[0,F+M]=[0,F+M]\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}.

Looking at the conjugates, we obtain

\bm{r}\cap[\max(\bm{r})-\overline{F}-M,\max(\bm{r})]=\left([\max(\bm{r})-\overline{F}-M,\max(\bm{r})]\right)\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}.

Since
\max(\bm{a})<F
and
\max(\bm{c})<\overline{F}
,
the integer set
\bm{r}=m_{0}\times\sum_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}

contains the following two (possibly overlapping) intervals
of length
M
:

[F+1,F+M]

and

[\max(\bm{r})-\overline{F}-M,\max(\bm{r})-\overline{F}-1]
.
Note that if they overlap, then the claim follows.
Otherwise, we show now how to extend these intervals until they overlap.

Let
\bm{t}_{1},\bm{t}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
be two integer sets.
If
\min(\bm{t}_{1})=0
and
\bm{t}_{2}
contains
an interval
[u,v]
with
v-u\geq\max(\bm{t}_{2})
,
then
\bm{t}_{1}+\bm{t}_{2}
contains the interval
[u,v+\max(\bm{t}_{2})]
.
By our choice of
M
, this holds for the previous two intervals.
Recursively, we deduce that
for any
\bm{s}_{1},\ldots,\bm{s}_{n}\in S^{\prime\prime}
,
the sumset
\bm{r}+\bm{s}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{s}_{n}
contains
the intervals

[F+1,F+M+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(\bm{s}_{i})]

and

[\max(\bm{r})-\overline{F}-M,\max(\bm{r})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(\bm{s}_{i})-\overline{F}-1]
.
When
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(\bm{s}_{i})
is large enough,
those two intervals overlap.
Specifically, consider the sumset

\bm{r}=m_{0}\times\sum_{\bm{s}\in S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{s}

and set
m=m_{0}+\max(\bm{r})
.
For any
\bm{t}\in\sum_{\geq m}S^{\prime\prime}
, we have

\bm{t}=\bm{r}+\bm{s}
for some
\bm{s}\in\sum_{\geq\max(\bm{r})}S^{\prime\prime}
,
thus satisfying
\max(\bm{s})\geq\max(\bm{r})
.
Therefore,
\bm{t}
contains the full interval

[F+1,\max(\bm{t})-\overline{F}-1].

and
\bm{t}
has type
(1,0,\bm{a},{\{0\}},\bm{c})
,
concluding the proof.
∎

Next we show that types are closed under sumsets with finite integer sets.
For this purpose, we extend the sumset to sets of sets as follows:
For
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{F}
we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}+\bm{s}:=\{\bm{r}+\bm{s}\mid\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any integer
g
and finite integer sets
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{c}
,
\bm{s}
,
there exist an integer
k
and a type
(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime})

such that

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})+\bm{s}=\operatorname{type}(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime}).

Proof.

If
g=0
, then for
\bm{r}=\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}
we have

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\{\bm{r}+\{m\}\mid m\in\mathds{Z}\}.

We then choose
k=0
and the type

(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime})=(0,0,\emptyset,\bm{r}+\bm{s},\emptyset).

Let us now assume
g>0
.
Both sides of the equality of the lemma
are stable by shift,
so we assume without loss of generality

\min(\bm{s})=0
and
\min(\bm{b})=0
.
We say that an integer set
\bm{t}
is
g
-periodic
on an interval
[\alpha,\beta]

if for all
x,y\in[\alpha,\beta]

with
x-y\equiv 0\mod g
, we have

x\in\bm{t}
if and only if
y\in\bm{t}
.
Let
k
be an integer satisfying

kg\geq 2+\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{c})+\max(\bm{s}).

Let
\bm{r}_{j}
denote the integer set

\bm{r}_{j}=j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c},

so that

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\{\bm{r}_{j}+\{m\}\mid j\geq k,\ m\in\mathds{Z}\}.

The integer set

j\times\{0,g\}
is
g
-periodic
on the interval
[0,jg]
.
Note in general that for any
\bm{t}
and
\bm{b}
with
\min(\bm{b})=0
, to decide whether an integer
x\in\bm{t}+\bm{b}
,
one only needs to consider if

x\in(\bm{t}\cap[x-\max(\bm{b}),x])+\bm{b}.

Thus,
j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}

is
g
-periodic on the interval
[\max(\bm{b}),jg]
.
Observe that on the interval

[\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{b})+1,jg-\max(\bm{c})-1]
,
the integer sets
r_{j}
and
j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}
are equal,
so
r_{j}
is
g
-periodic on this interval.

Applying the same reasoning,

\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s}

is
g
-periodic on the interval
I_{j}
defined as

I_{j}=[\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{s})+1,jg-\max(\bm{c})-1].

The integer
k
has been chosen to ensure
this interval is non-empty whenever
j\geq k
.
On this interval,
\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s}
is equal to

I_{j}\cap(\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s})=I_{j}\cap(j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}+\bm{s})

because the bottom pruning by
\bm{a}

and the top pruning by
\bm{c}
do not overlap.
For any integer sets
\bm{t}\subseteq\bm{r}
, we have

\bm{t}+\bm{s}\subseteq\bm{r}+\bm{s}.

This implies

\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s}\subseteq j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}+\bm{s}.

Let
I_{\operatorname{bottom}}
and
I_{j,\operatorname{top}}

denote the intervals

\displaystyle I_{\operatorname{bottom}}

\displaystyle=[0,\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{s})],

\displaystyle I_{j,\operatorname{top}}

\displaystyle=[jg-\max(\bm{c}),jg+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{s})],

so that
I_{\operatorname{bottom}}
,
I_{j}
, and
I_{j,\operatorname{top}}
form a partition
of the interval
[0,\max(\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s})]
.
There exists

\bm{a}^{\prime}\subseteq[0,\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{s})]
,
independent of
j
, such that

I_{\operatorname{bottom}}\cap(\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s})=I_{\operatorname{bottom}}\cap(j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}+\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}^{\prime}).

Similarly, working on the conjugates,
there exists
\bm{c}^{\prime}\subseteq[0,\max(\bm{c})+\max(\bm{b})+\max(\bm{s})]

independent of
j
such that

I_{j,\operatorname{top}}\cap(\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s})=I_{j,\operatorname{top}}\cap(j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}+\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}^{\prime}).

Collecting the results about
I_{\operatorname{bottom}}
,
I_{j}
, and
I_{j,\operatorname{top}}
,
we obtain

\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s}=j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}+\bm{s}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}^{\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}^{\prime}.

Thus

\{\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{s}\mid j\geq k\}=\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}+\bm{s},\bm{c}^{\prime}).\qed

We need one last technical lemma, stating that for
k
large enough, each type can be chosen to be proper.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
g
,
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{c}
,
there exist an integer
k

and a proper type
(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime})
such that

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime}).

For any
j\geq k
, there exists
j^{\prime}
such that

\operatorname{type}(g,j,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(g,j^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Since for any
m\in\mathds{Z}
,
we have

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a}\cap\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0},\bm{b}+\{m\},\bm{c}\cap\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}),

we can assume without loss of generality
\min(\bm{b})=0
,

\min(\bm{a})\geq 0
, and
\min(\bm{c})\geq 0
.
If
g=0
or
\max(\bm{b})<g
, then the type is already proper.
Otherwise, let
g\mathds{Z}
denote the set
\{gm\mid m\in\mathds{Z}\}
.
Let

\bm{b}^{\prime}=(g\mathds{Z}+\bm{b})\cap[0,g-1].

By periodicity, we have

g\mathds{Z}+\bm{b}=g\mathds{Z}+\bm{b}^{\prime}.

Thus, for all
j\geq 0
,

j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}\subseteq g\mathds{Z}+\bm{b}=g\mathds{Z}+\bm{b}^{\prime},

and, more precisely,

j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}\subseteq(j+\lceil\max(\bm{b})/g\rceil)\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}^{\prime}.

We fix
k
large enough
to ensure both
k>\lceil\max(\bm{b})/g\rceil

and
\max(\bm{a})+\max(\bm{c})<kg
.
The first constraint ensures
that for any
j\geq k
,
we have

(j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b})\cap[0,\max(\bm{b})]=(k\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b})\cap[0,\max(\bm{b})],

because all the points from
\{jg\}+\bm{b}

are greater than
\max(\bm{b})
.
The second constraint ensures that
the bottom and top pruning do not overlap.
We also define

\bm{a}^{\prime\prime}=\left((k\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}^{\prime})\setminus(k\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b})\right)\cap[0,\max(\bm{b})]

and the same on the conjugate for
\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}
.
This ensures for all
j\geq k

j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}=(j+\max(\bm{b})/g)\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}^{\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}^{\prime\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}.

Since the bottom and top pruning do not overlap,
for all
j\geq k

j\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}=(j+\max(\bm{b})/g)\times\{0,g\}+\bm{b}^{\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}^{\prime\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise 5.8125pt\hbox to0.0pt{\begin{turn}$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\end{turn}\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{a}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c}^{\prime\prime}\smash{\mskip 4.0mu\raise-2.15277pt\hbox to0.0pt{$\hat{\phantom{-}}$\hss}-}\mskip 4.0mu{\vphantom{\widehat{-}}}\bm{c},

so, combining the two bottom prunings into one,
and the two top prunings into one,
there exist
\bm{a}^{\prime}
and
\bm{c}^{\prime}

such that

\operatorname{type}(g,k,\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})=\operatorname{type}(g,k^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b}^{\prime},\bm{c}^{\prime}).\qed

After these technical lemmas, we are now ready to prove our main result.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Consider a finite family
S=\{\bm{s}_{1},\ldots,\bm{s}_{n}\}
of N-steps,
and let
S^{\prime}
denote the subset containing
all N-steps of size at least
2
.
We first build an automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

like in the proposition
except that the types associated to the states need not be proper,
and that
S
is replaced by
S^{\prime}
.
Then we will build from it an automaton
\mathcal{A}
with proper types,
and finally add the N-steps of size
0
and
1
.

We will first build the states of the automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
To each state
q
, we associate a subset
T_{q}
of
S^{\prime}
,
a type
(g_{q},k_{q},\bm{a}_{q},\bm{b}_{q},\bm{c}_{q})

and integers
(u_{q,\bm{t}})_{\bm{t}\in S^{\prime}\setminus T_{q}}

indexed by
S^{\prime}\setminus T_{q}
.
Informally, the idea is that any sumset
\bm{s}
of N-steps from
S^{\prime}

where each N-step from
T_{q}
appears a
“
large enough number of times
”,
and each N-step

5
N-meanders and N-excursions with general N-steps

In this section we will show that the counting generating function (keeping track of the length and the maximal reachable point) of N-meanders is always algebraic.
We conjecture that the same is true for N-excursions, which we show in several further examples.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let an N-step set
S
be given and define the multiset
S_{T}=\{\max(s)|s\in S\}
.

•

The generating function
D^{+}(1,y;t)
of N-meanders with N-step set
S
is equal to the generating function of classical meanders with step set
S_{T}
and therefore algebraic.

•

The generating function
D^{+}(0,0;t)
of N-meanders with N-step set
S
and ending with reachable point set
\{0\}
is equal to the generating function of classical excursions with step set
S_{T}
and therefore algebraic.

Proof.

Observe that an N-meander can be continued as long as the set of reachable points is non-empty.
This is equivalent to the fact that the top-path in the N-meander, i.e., the one the furthest away from the
x
-axis, can be continued.
Thus, it suffices to count the number of top paths, which are classical lattice paths.
Its step set consists of the maxima of each N-step, which is therefore
S_{T}
.
Note that the multiset can be converted into an ordinary set, by introducing colors for steps that appear multiple times.

Then, the number of N-meanders is the number of classical meanders with step set
S_{T}
.
Moreover, the number of N-meanders with reachable point set
\{0\}
is equal to the number of classical excursions with step set
S_{T}
.
Hence, by the theory of Banderier and Flajolet
[
2
, Theorem 2]
we know that its generating function is algebraic and can be computed explicitly.
∎

Remark 5.2

(Step set
S_{T}
with colors)
.

We can interpret the step set
S_{T}
also as ordinary set
\widehat{S_{T}}
, after attaching “colors” to the steps.
For this purpose, recall that each step
s\in S
is associated with a weight
p_{s}
.
Then, by the previous result, each step
j\in\widehat{S_{T}}
gets a weight

q_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(s)=j\\
s\in S\end{subarray}}p_{s}.

This interpretation directly shows that the generating functions
D^{+}(1,y;t)
and
D^{+}(0,0;t)
depend only on these weights
q_{j}
, which generalizes the result of Corollary
2.10
.

It remains to treat N-excursions.
First, let us consider the following special case.
An N-step set
S
is
symmetric
, if for all
s\in S
also
-s\in S
.
The following result generalizes Corollary
2.9

Theorem 5.3
.

For a symmetric N-step set
S
, the generating function
D^{+}(0,1;t)
of N-excursions is symmetric in
p_{s}
and
p_{-s}
for all
s\in S
.

Proof.

We define an involution
\Phi:S\to S
by mapping each N-step to symmetric counter part

\displaystyle\Phi(s)=-s.

As in Corollary
2.9
, this extends to an involution between N-excursions:

\displaystyle E=(e_{0},\dots,e_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto E^{\prime}=(e^{\prime}_{0},\dots,e^{\prime}_{n}),\quad\text{ where }e^{\prime}_{i}=\Phi(e_{n-i}).

The key observation is that if the classical excursion
e
is compatible with
E
, then the time-reversed classical excursion
e^{\prime}
is compatible with
E^{\prime}
; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
for such operations on lattice paths.
By the symmetry of the N-step set
S
, the N-steps of
E^{\prime}
all lie in
S
.
∎

Finally, under certain conditions on the N-steps and, most importantly, under the assumption that there are only finitely many types, we are able to prove that N-excursions are algebraic.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let an N-step set
S
be given, such that
\max\min_{s\in S}s\leq 0
and the reachable points of N-meanders are described by finitely many types.
Then, the generating function of N-excursions is algebraic.

Proof.

Let
d=\max\min_{s\in S}
be the maximal nonpositive jump in all N-steps.
The assumption
d\leq 0
guarantees that the minimum of all reachable points can never be larger than
d
.
Then, we build a deterministic pushdown automaton as follows:
First, we create
d+1
states for each type, where each of them corresponds to a different value of the minimum.
Second, we add the transitions according to the N-steps.
The types are chosen in such a way that all but finitely many of its reachable points lead to the same type after appending an N-step.
Recall that in each type each reachable point is uniquely identified by its minimum and maximum.
As the minimum is known, it suffices to know the maximum, which we encode in the stack of a pushdown automaton.
Hence, N-excursions can be encoded by a context-free grammar
[
4
]
, and therefore they have an algebraic generating function.
∎

The simplicity of
D^{+}(0,1;t)
for N-excursions in Corrolary
2.11
quite remarkable.
An alternative proof idea for its algebraicity might be to prove that the language of N-excursions admits an unambiguous context free grammar.
Then the Chomsky–Schützenberger enumeration theorem would imply that the generating function for the number of words (
i.e.,
, N-excursions), is algebraic.

Of course, not all algebraic generating functions correspond to context-free grammars, however, for lattice path problems this strategy normally works
[
4
,
18
,
27
,
12
]
.

Furthermore, note that context-free grammars are equivalent to languages accepted by pushdown automata.
While it is easy to build a pushdown automaton using the nondeterministic character to follow all trajectories simultaneously,
the associated grammar is ambiguous.
Now, a deterministic pushdown automaton would correspond to an unambiguous grammar, however, we were not able to build one and it seems surprisingly difficult.
The main difficulty arises from the observation that you seem to have to keep track of the minimum and maximum at the same time, but you only have one stack.
We leave it as an open problem, to explain these difficulties.

The solved cases of Dyck and Motzkin N-steps, as well as solved special cases, lead us to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.5

The generating function of N-excursions is algebraic.

6
Conclusion

In this paper we introduced nondeterministic lattice paths and solved the asymptotic counting problem for such walks of the Dyck and Motzkin type.
The strength of our approach relies on the methods of analytic combinatorics, which allowed us to derive not only the asymptotic main terms but also lower order terms (to any order if needed).
Furthermore, we showed that for a general N-step set the generating function of N-bridges is algebraic.
The method of choice is the well-established kernel method.
We extended it to a two-phase approach in order to deal with two catalytic variables.

Additionally to the mathematically interesting model, our nondeterminstic lattice paths have applications in the encapsulation and decapsulation of protocols over networks.

A.2

5.1
