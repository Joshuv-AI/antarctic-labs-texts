Abstract

If n k + 2 ​ r n d ⁡ ( k + 1 ) → ∞ n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to\infty , the scaled persistence diagram converges to a deterministic Radon measure almost surely in the vague metric. Introduction

The main theme of this paper is persistent homology and its diagrams associated with random geometric complexes. In applied topology, persistent homology is one of the tools most ubiquitously used to analyze data in a way robust to various deformations. In recent times, persistent homology has demonstrated its applicability in areas as diverse as sensor networks

First, we present one illustrative example, which helps capture the essence of persistent homology.
A more rigorous description of persistent homology can be found in

In applied topology, we frequently place special emphasis on the combinatorial nature of geometric objects by means of a higher-dimensional notion of graphs, called the
geometric complex
. Among many candidates of geometric complexes (see
[
19
]
), we especially focus on the
Čech complex
.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a set
{\mathcal{X}}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a positive number
r>0
, the Čech complex
\check{C}({\mathcal{X}},r)
is defined as follows.

(1)

The
0
-simplices are the points in
{\mathcal{X}}
.

(2)

For each
m\geq 1
,
[x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{m}}]\subset{\mathcal{X}}
forms an
m
-simplex if
\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}B(x_{i_{j}},r/2)\neq\emptyset
.

The main advantage of the Čech complex is its homotopy equivalence to the union of balls
U(r/2)
. This fact is known as the Nerve lemma (e.g., Theorem 10.7 in
[
4
]
).

In the present work, we are interested in the asymptotic behavior of persistence diagrams associated with random Čech complexes. More specifically, let
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be an iid random sample on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with common density
f
. Then, we define the
Čech filtration
by

(1.1)

\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n}):=\big(\check{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n},t),\,t\geq 0\big)=\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t),\,t\geq 0\big),

over a “scaled” random sample
r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n}:=\{r_{n}^{-1}X_{1},\dots,r_{n}^{-1}X_{n}\}
, such that
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Note that (
1.1
) represents a nested sequence of Čech complexes, satisfying monotonicity property

\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}s)\subset\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\ \ \text{for all }0<s\leq t.

In many of the studies on the stochastic topology of geometric complexes, (persistent) Betti numbers have been employed as a good quantifier of topological complexity
[
26
,
28
,
47
,
48
,
5
,
23
,
31
]
.
Given an integer
k\geq 0
, let
Z_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)=\text{ker }\partial_{k}
be the
k
th cycle group of the Čech complex in (
1.1
), where
\partial_{k}
is a boundary homomorphism. Additionally, denote by
B_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)=\text{im }\partial_{k+1}
the
k
th boundary group of the same complex. Then, the
k
th homology group
H_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big):=Z_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)/B_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)
,
is defined as the quotient group, representing the elements of (non-trivial)
k
-dimensional cycles as a boundary of a
(k+1)
-dimensional body. Hereinafter, we call it a “
k
-cycle” for short. The
k
th Betti number
, denoted by

(1.2)

\beta_{k,n}(t):=\beta_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big),

is the rank of
H_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)
, representing the number of
k
-cycles in the Čech complex.
More generally, the
k
th
persistent Betti number
of
\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n})
is defined as

(1.3)

\beta_{k,n}(s,t):=\beta_{k}^{s,t}\big(\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n})\big)=\text{dim}\frac{Z_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}s)\big)}{Z_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}s)\big)\cap B_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)},\ \ 0\leq s\leq t<\infty.

More intuitively,
\beta_{k,n}(s,t)
represents the number of
k
-cycles that appear in
\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n})
before time
s
and remain alive at time
t
. In particular, if
s=t
, then
\beta_{k,n}(t,t)

reduces to the usual Betti number in (
1.2
). A more rigorous coverage of these algebraic topological notions can be found in
[
35
,
21
,
16
]
.

The earliest results of examining persistence diagrams from a probabilistic viewpoint were presented in
[
34
]
,
[
46
]
, the authors of which developed the definition of probability measures that support expectations, variances, and conditional probabilities. In more recent years, in
[
8
]
it was shown that, for a wide class of filtrations, the expected persistence diagram as a Radon point measure has a density
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
From the viewpoints of stochastic topology, the studies most relevant to this paper are described in
[
23
,
45
,
14
]
, in which the authors considered persistence diagrams as a point process and related them to the persistent Betti numbers.
In particular,
[
23
]
provided a strong law of large numbers (SLLN) for the persistence diagram generated by a stationary point process in the so-called
critical (or thermodynamic) regime
. In our notation, the critical regime is understood as the condition
nr_{n}^{d}\to c
as
n\to\infty
for some constant
c\in(0,\infty)
. In this setting, Čech complexes become highly connected, forming many large components of cycles of various dimensions
[
48
,
37
,
20
]
.

In contrast to the previous works cited in the last paragraph, the main focus of this paper is the
sparse regime
:
nr_{n}^{d}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, the spatial distribution of Čech complexes is more sparse and less complicated than the critical regime because of a faster decay of
r_{n}
as a result of
nr_{n}^{d}\to 0
.
The main point of the current paper is that, for every
k\geq 1
,
the behavior of the persistence diagram, associated with the
k
-cycles in the Čech filtration, splits into three different regimes:

(1.4)

(i)\ n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to\infty,\ \ (ii)\ n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to c\in(0,\infty),\ \ (iii)\ n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to 0.

More specifically, the rate of the sequence
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}
determines various phase transitions of the
k
th homology group of the Čech complex
\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)
. For instance, in view of the expectation of
\beta_{k,n}(t)
, it holds that

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}\big[\beta_{k,n}(t)\big]\sim Cn^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)},\ \ \text{as }n\to\infty,

for some
C>0
(see
[
26
,
28
]
).

In particular, if
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to\infty
, as in case
(i)
, the central limit theorem holds for
\beta_{k,n}(t)
; that is,

\frac{\beta_{k,n}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\beta_{k,n}(t)]}{\sqrt{n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}}}

converges weakly to a centered Gaussian process
[
28
,
37
]
.
For the asymptotics of persistence diagram, Theorem
3.2
below claims that if the persistence diagram is scaled by the sequence
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}
, it converges to a deterministic Radon measure almost surely in the vague metric. The obtained result can be viewed as a SLLN for persistence diagrams. This also implies that the number of birth-death pairs grows at the rate of
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}
, so that the limiting persistence diagram consists of infinitely many birth-death pairs as
n\to\infty
. As for the proof techniques, our approach relies on a fundamental relation between a persistence diagram and the persistent Betti numbers
\big(\beta_{k,n}(s,t),0\leq s\leq t\leq\infty\big)
in (
1.3
); that is, the former can be expressed as a simple linear combination of the latter (see (
2.2
) for an explicit expression). Owing to this relation, along with technical results for an underlying vague convergence (e.g., Corollary A.3 and Proposition 3.4 in
[
23
]
), the SLLNs for a persistence diagram can be obtained from those for persistent Betti numbers. The main difficulty here is that the scaler for
\beta_{k,n}(s,t)
may grow very slowly (e.g., logarithmically); in such a case, a direct use of the Borel–Cantelli lemma, together with the lower-order moment calculations, does not help to establish the required SLLN. To overcome this issue, we employ the concentration inequalities in
[
3
]
, which themselves were developed for analyzing Poisson
U
-statistics of the geometric configuration of a point cloud. For the application of these concentration bounds, one needs to detect appropriate subsequential upper and lower bounds for the quantities that are used to approximate persistent Betti numbers. This approach is a higher-dimensional version of a standard technique for the theory of geometric graphs; see, e.g., Chapter 3 of the monograph
[
38
]
.

Suppose next that
r_{n}
decays faster, so that
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to c\in(0,\infty)
, as in case
(ii)
of (
1.4
). Then,
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{k,n}(t)]
is asymptotically a positive constant. As a result,
\beta_{k,n}(t)
converges weakly
to the difference of two dependent Poisson processes
[
28
,
37
]
. Theorem
3.1
below reveals that the persistence diagram weakly converges to a limiting point process without normalization. As expected, the limiting point process possesses a Poissonian structure (see (
3.1
)). This implies that the number of cycles is not large, and asymptotically, all the
k
-cycles affecting the limiting persistence diagram are necessarily formed by components of size
k+2
(i.e., components of the smallest size). Our proof techniques for Theorem
3.1
are closely related to those in Theorem 5.1 of
[
37
]
. By virtue of an aforementioned linear relation between a persistence diagram and the persistent Betti number
\beta_{k,n}(s,t)
, we need to demonstrate the Poisson limit theorem for
\beta_{k,n}(s,t)
. This will follow from weak convergence of a point process induced by the persistent Betti number (see (
4.13
)). For the proof, we show directly a set of sufficient conditions provided in Theorem 3.1 of
[
12
]
.

Finally, if
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to 0
as in case
(iii)
of (
1.4
), the occurrence of
k
-cycles becomes even rarer, in the sense of
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{k,n}(t)]\to 0
,
n\to\infty
.
Accordingly, the persistence diagram converges to the null measure (i.e., the measure assigning zeros to all measurable sets in the diagram).
In this case, the sequence of probability distributions of a persistence diagram has to be normalized.
The problem is however that the probability distribution of a persistence diagram is defined in the space of Radon point measures. Since this space is not locally compact, the corresponding convergence cannot be treated in vague topology. Alternatively, we employ the
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-topology, a standard topology that has been used for the study of regular variation of point processes and stochastic processes
[
25
,
32
,
43
]
. Theorem
3.3
below gives a more precise statement. In particular, Section
4.3
proves sufficient conditions that are based on Theorem A.2 in
[
25
]
, for the required convergence.

For easy reference later on, we refer to case
(i)
of (
1.4
) as the
divergence regime
, since (
1.5
) indicates that
\mathbb{E}\big[\beta_{k,n}(t)\big]
diverges as
n\to\infty
. We also refer to case
(ii)
as the
Poisson regime
, in that the weak limit of
\beta_{k,n}(t)
is Poisson distributed. Finally, case
(iii)
is called the
vanishing regime
, for which
\beta_{k,n}(t)
vanishes in the sense of an expectation.

The potential applications of the current study are in topological data analysis (TDA). Denote by
D_{k,n}
the
k
th persistence diagram of Čech filtration (
1.1
).
A common practice in TDA is to transform persistence diagrams into the representation

(1.6)

\sum_{(b_{i},d_{i})\in D_{k,n}}\phi(b_{i},d_{i}),

where
(b_{i},d_{i})
is the list of the
k
th persistence birth-death pairs (see Section
2
for a formal definition) and
\phi
is a real-valued function.
One example of such functions is the
\alpha
th total persistence, given by
\phi(x,y)=(y-x)^{\alpha}
(see
[
14
]
). In the special case
\alpha=1
, it reduces to the sum of persistence barcodes
[
18
,
7
]
.
One of the primary benefits of our results is that one may give a probabilistic foundation to the functional in (
1.6
). Indeed, when the asymptotic theory for persistence diagrams has been completed, the standard machinery via the continuous mapping theorem for different modes of convergence (see
[
41
,
30
,
24
]
) may yield a variety of limit theorems for (
1.6
).
This will be technically challenging, since many of the required functionals are not continuous, especially in the region close to the diagonal line. Nonetheless, it will be possible to overcome this difficulty by means of a well established approximation scheme that was recently developed by the authors of
[
14
]
(see also
[
13
]
). This line of research remains a future topic of our research.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, Section
2
provides a formal definition of a persistence diagram as a point process, expressing it as a function of persistent Betti numbers. All the main results on the limiting persistence diagram are presented in Section
3
. This section is divided into three parts, corresponding to each of the regimes in (
1.4
). All the proofs are deferred to Section
4
.

Before concluding the Introduction, let us add a few more comments on our setup and assumptions. First, we assume that the density
f
of a random sample
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
is a.e. continuous and bounded. Although it seems possible to obtain the same results under a weaker assumption that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)^{2k+4}dx<\infty
, we decided to rely on stronger assumptions. By doing so, we can avoid technical arguments based on the Lebesgue differentiation theorem. Second, we consider only persistence diagrams associated with Čech filtration. However, the proposed methods seem to be applicable to other geometric complexes. In particular, in the case of a Vietoris–Rips filtration, all the results obtained can be carried over by a simple replacement of the scaler
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}
in (
1.4
) with an appropriate one.

2.
Setup

We start with a formal definition of the space for persistence diagrams. First, let
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty]
be a product space endowed with the product topology. Define an infinite triangle in the first quadrant by

\Delta:=\big\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq y<\infty\big\}\cup\big\{(x,\infty):0\leq x<\infty\big\},

and equip
\Delta
with the relative topology of
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty]
. Let
L=\big\{(x,x):0\leq x<\infty\big\}
be the diagonal line in the first quadrant. Throughout the paper, we take
\Delta_{L}:=\Delta\setminus L=\big\{(x,y):0\leq x<y\leq\infty\big\}
as an underlying space for persistence diagrams.

Let

\mathcal{A}=\big\{(s,t]\times(u,v],\,[0,t]\times(u,v],\,0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq v\leq\infty,\,t<\infty\big\},

and denote an element of
\mathcal{A}
by
R_{s,t,u,v}
(if
s=0
, it represents either
(0,t]\times(u,v]
or
[0,t]\times(u,v]
).
Next, we define

D_{k,n}=D_{k}\big(\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n})\big)=\big\{(b_{i},d_{i})\in\Delta:i=1,\dots,n_{k}\big\}

to be the
k
th persistence diagram associated with the Čech filtration (
1.1
). Here,
\big((b_{i},d_{i})\big)_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
is the list of the
k
th persistence birth-death pairs, representing the time at which each
k
-cycle first appears in
\mathcal{C}(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{n})
and the time at which it is terminated (or filled in), respectively. We then define
D_{k,n}
as a point process,

(2.1)

\xi_{k,n}:=\sum_{(b_{i},d_{i})\in D_{k,n}}\delta_{(b_{i},d_{i})},

where
\delta_{(x,y)}
is the Dirac measure at
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. A key relation between (
2.1
) and the persistent Betti number (
1.3
) is that, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq v\leq\infty
,

(2.2)

\xi_{k,n}(R_{s,t,u,v})=\beta_{k,n}(t,u)-\beta_{k,n}(t,v)-\beta_{k,n}(s,u)+\beta_{k,n}(s,v).

Finally, we introduce a certain indicator function that is used for characterizing the limiting objects for each of the regimes in (
1.4
). For
r>0
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}
, define

(2.3)

\displaystyle h_{r}(x_{1},\dots,

\displaystyle x_{k+2}):={\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\beta_{k}\big(\check{C}(\big\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2}\big\},r)\big)=1\Big\}

\displaystyle={\mathbbm{1}}\,\bigg\{\Big\{\bigcap_{j=1,\,j\neq j_{0}}^{k+2}B(x_{j},r/2)\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }j_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k+2\}\Big\}\cap\Big\{\bigcap_{j=1}^{k+2}B(x_{j},r/2)=\emptyset\Big\}\bigg\},

where
{\mathbbm{1}}\{\cdot\}
is an indicator function. This indicator function requires that a set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2}\}
of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
forms a single
k
-cycle with connectivity radius
r
.

3.
Main results

In this section, we present the limit theorems for persistence diagrams. For ease of discussion, we first treat the Poisson regime and then move on to the other two cases. Recall that
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
represents iid random variables on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with common density
f
. We assume that
f
is a.e. continuous and bounded, that is,
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\text{esssup}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)<\infty
. Moreover, we take an integer
k\geq 1
, which remains fixed in the following. Since our main focus is the sparse regime, we assume throughout the paper that
nr_{n}^{d}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

3.1.
Poisson regime

Assume that
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to c
as
n\to\infty
for some constant
c\in(0,\infty)
. Write
\lambda
for the
d(k+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and
C_{k}:=((k+2)!)^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)^{k+2}dx
.
Let
M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
be the space of Random measures on
\Delta_{L}
, which is equipped with
vague topology
(see
[
30
,
41
]
).
Additionally, denote by
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
the subset in
M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
of all Radon point measures. Note that
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
is a closed subset of
M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
in vague topology (see Proposition 3.14 in
[
41
]
).
Next, for
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}
we define

H_{s,t,u,v}({\bf x}):=h_{t}({\bf x})h_{u}({\bf x})-h_{t}({\bf x})h_{v}({\bf x})-h_{s}({\bf x})h_{u}({\bf x})+h_{s}({\bf x})h_{v}({\bf x}),\ \ 0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq v\leq\infty,

where the indicators on the right hand side are defined in (
2.3
).

Before stating the main result, we need to define the limiting point process of (
2.1
). First, we note that
\mathcal{A}
forms a semi-ring of subsets of
\Delta_{L}
. Indeed, one can check that:
(i)\ \emptyset\in\mathcal{A}
;
(ii)
if
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, then
A\cap B\in\mathcal{A}
; and
(iii)
if
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, then
A\setminus B=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}
for some finite disjoint sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}
has the covering property; i.e., for any open
G\subset\Delta_{L}
, there exists
(A_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{A}
so that
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}
. In particular,
\mathcal{A}
generates a Borel
\sigma
-field on
\Delta_{L}
.
It then follows from Proposition 9.2. III. in
[
10
]
that one can define a point process
\zeta_{k}
, whose probability distribution on
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
is uniquely determined by the finite-dimensional distributions

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\zeta_{k}(R_{s_{i},t_{i},u_{i},v_{i}})=m_{i},\ i=1,\dots,d\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}H_{s_{i},t_{i},u_{i},v_{i}}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})M_{k}(d{\bf y})=m_{i},\ i=1,\dots,d\Big)

for
d\geq 1
, and
m_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
0\leq s_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq\infty
,
i=1,\dots,d
. Here,
M_{k}
denotes the Poisson random measure on
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}
with mean measure
C_{k}\lambda
. Namely, the distribution of

is defined as

M_{k}(A)\sim\text{Poi}\big(C_{k}\lambda(A)\big)

for all measurable

(“Poi” stands for a Poisson distribution). Furthermore, if
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
are disjoint subsets in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}
, then
M_{k}(A_{1}),\dots,M_{k}(A_{m})
are independent.

As defined, the marginal distribution of
\zeta_{k}
depends on a linear combination of the indicators in (
2.3
). This implies that the
k
-cycles affecting
\zeta_{k}
must always be formed by connected components on
k+2
points (i.e., components of the smallest size).
It is also easy to check that
\zeta_{k}(R_{s,t,u,v})
has a Poisson law with mean
C_{k}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}H_{s,t,u,v}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})d{\bf y}
. Further characterization of (
3.1
) (as a random field) is provided in
[
36
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to c\in(0,\infty)
as
n\to\infty
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

(3.2)

\xi_{k,n}\Rightarrow c\zeta_{k}\ \ \text{in }M_{p}(\Delta_{L}),

where
\Rightarrow
denotes weak convergence.

3.2.
Divergence regime

Next, we turn to the case for which
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. In this case, there appear infinitely many
k
-cycles as
n\to\infty
(see (
1.5
)), and accordingly, the limiting persistence diagram of
\xi_{k,n}
consists of infinitely many persistence birth-death pairs, so that
\xi_{k,n}(R_{s,t,u,v})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq v\leq\infty
. Hence, in order to obtain a non-degenerate limit of
\xi_{k,n}
, the process itself must be normalized by a growing sequence.
Let
C_{K}^{+}(\Delta_{L})
be a collection of non-negative and continuous functions on
\Delta_{L}
with compact support. Recall that a sequence of Radon measures
(\nu_{n})\subset M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
is said to converge vaguely to
\nu\in M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
, denoted by
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\to}}\nu
in
M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
, if
\int_{\Delta_{L}}f(x)\nu_{n}(dx)\to\int_{\Delta_{L}}f(x)\nu(dx)
for all
f\in C_{K}^{+}(\Delta_{L})
.

For the theorem below, we need to
apply a mild condition to the decay rate of
r_{n}
. More precisely, we assume that it is a regularly varying sequence (at infinity) with exponent
\rho<0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{\lfloor an\rfloor}}{r_{n}}=a^{\rho}\ \ \text{ for all }a>0.

Finally, for two sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, write
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a_{n}/b_{n}\geq C
for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Assume that
(r_{n})
is a regularly varying sequence with exponent
\rho<0
, such that

(3.3)

n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}=\Omega\big((\log n)^{\eta}\big)

for some
\eta>0
.
Then, there exists a unique Radon measure
\mu_{k}\in M_{+}(\Delta_{L})
such that

(3.4)

\frac{\xi_{k,n}}{n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\to}}\mu_{k},\ \ \text{almost surely in }M_{+}(\Delta_{L}),

and

(3.5)

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k,n}]}{n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\to}}\mu_{k},\ \ \text{in }M_{+}(\Delta_{L}),

where
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k,n}]
denotes the mean measure of
\xi_{k,n}
, which itself is a Radon measure on
\Delta_{L}
. The measure
\mu_{k}
satisfies

(3.6)

\mu_{k}(R_{s,t,u,v})=C_{k}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}H_{s,t,u,v}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})d{\bf y},

except for at most countably many
R_{s,t,u,v}\in\mathcal{A}
.

In spite of the significant difference in the proof techniques, the results above more or less parallel Theorem 1.5 in
[
23
]
(see also Theorem 1.1 in
[
45
]
), in which the authors obtained the SLLN for persistence diagrams in the critical regime. In the critical regime, Čech complexes are highly connected, so that they form many large components of cycles of any dimension. As a consequence, the limiting measures in
[
23
,
45
]
are so complicated that they do not have an explicit representation, as in (
3.6
).

3.3.
Vanishing regime

Once again, we return to (
1.4
) and consider case
(iii)
for which
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
In this case, it holds that
\mathbb{E}\big[\beta_{k,n}(t)\big]\sim Cn^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This indicates that all the
k
-cycles vanish in the limit, and accordingly,
\xi_{k,n}
converges to the null measure
\emptyset\in M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
(i.e., the measure assigning zeros to all measurable sets in
\Delta_{L}
). In particular, we have, for all
m=1,2,\dots
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\xi_{k,n}(R_{s,t,u,v})=m\big)=0,\ \ 0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq v\leq\infty.

In the stochastic topology literature, not necessarily related to random geometric complexes, a fundamental interest lies in how rapidly each homology group appears and disappears
[
6
,
17
,
27
,
29
,
44
]
. In the same spirit, we are naturally interested in the decay rate of the sequence
(\mathbb{P}\circ\xi_{k,n}^{-1})
of probability measures on
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
. However, the space
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
is not locally compact, and thus, the resulting convergence can no longer be treated under vague topology, as in the last section. To overcome this difficulty, we adopt a notion of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-topology
. This notion was first developed by the authors of
[
24
]
. Since then, it has been intensively used, especially in extreme value theory, for the study of the regular variation of point processes and stochastic processes
[
25
,
32
,
43
]
.
The main benefit of employing
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-topology is that it requires only that the underlying space be complete and separable. Since
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
is complete and separable (see Proposition 3.17 in
[
41
]
), we can utilize
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-topology as an appropriate topology for the convergence below.

Let
B_{\emptyset,r}
denote an open ball of radius
r>0
centered at the null measure
\emptyset\in M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
in terms of the metric induced by vague topology. Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{0}\big(M_{p}(\Delta_{L})\big)
the space of Borel measures on
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
, the restriction of which to

M_{p}(\Delta_{L})\setminus\ B_{\emptyset,r}
is finite for all
r>0
. Moreover, define
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{C}_{0}\big(M_{p}(\Delta_{L})\big)
to
be the space of continuous and bounded real-valued functions on
M_{p}(\Delta_{L})
that vanish in the neighborhood of
\emptyset
. Finally, given
m_{n},m\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
, the convergence
m_{n}\to m
in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is defined as
\int_{M_{p}(\Delta_{L})}f(x)m_{n}(dx)\to\int_{M_{p}(\Delta_{L})}f(x)m(dx)
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k,n}\in\cdot)\to C_{k}\lambda\Big\{{\bf y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}:\delta_{(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y}))}\in\cdot\Big\}\ \ \text{in }\mathcal{M}_{0},

where
{\bf y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}
and for
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}
,

\displaystyle b({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:h_{t}({\bf x})=1\big\},

\displaystyle d({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{t\geq b({\bf x}):h_{t}({\bf x})=0\big\}

(by convention, we take
\inf\emptyset\equiv\infty
).

Remark 3.4
.

We here provide a more detailed structure of the limiting measure

\eta(\cdot):=C_{k}\lambda\Big\{{\bf y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}:\delta_{(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y}))}\in\cdot\Big\}.

For
m\geq 1
and
0\leq s_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq\infty
,
i=1,\dots,m
, we define
a map
T:M_{p}(\Delta_{L})\to[0,\infty)^{m}
by
T(\xi)=\big(\xi(R_{s_{i},t_{i},u_{i},v_{i}})\big)_{i=1}^{m}
. It is then straightforward to check that

\eta\circ T^{-1}(\cdot)=C_{k}\lambda\Big\{{\bf y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}:\Big({\mathbbm{1}}\big\{(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y}))\in R_{s_{i},t_{i},u_{i},v_{i}}\big\}\Big)_{i=1}^{m}\in\cdot\Big\}.

Observe also that, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
\big(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y})\big)\in R_{s_{i},t_{i},u_{i},v_{i}}
holds if and only if

(3.7)

h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})=0,\ \ h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})=h_{u_{i}}(0,{\bf y})=1,\ \ h_{v_{i}}(0,{\bf y})=0.

In particular, (
3.7
) requires that a point set
\{0,{\bf y}\}=\{0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1}\}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}
forms a single
k
-cycle between times
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
, such that this cycle disappears between times
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
.
Combining these observations, we finally obtain that

\eta\circ T^{-1}(\cdot)=C_{k}\lambda\Big\{{\bf y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}:\Big(\big(1-h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\big)h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{u_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\big(1-h_{v_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\big)\Big)_{i=1}^{m}\in\cdot\Big\}.

Lemma 4.1

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}g_{r_{n}s,r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n})\leq\beta_{k,n}(s,t)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\

Moreover, we have for all

(4.2)

(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\mathbb{E}[L_{r_{n}t}]\to 0,\ \ \ n\to\infty.

Proof.

We observe that the leftmost term in (

k

r_{n}s
and still alive at time
r_{n}t

counts more
k

For the remaining inequality in (

{\mathcal{Y}}
of

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\mathcal{Z}\supset{\mathcal{Y}}

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

Note that

and

are identically zero for all
j\geq 2
, since it is impossible to form multiple
k
-cycles from
k+2
points.
With these indicators available,
\beta_{k,n}(t)
can be represented as

(4.3)

\beta_{k,n}(t)=\beta_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{n},r_{n}t)\big)=\sum_{i=k+2}^{n}\sum_{j>0}j\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=i\end{subarray}}g^{(i,j)}_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n}).

Using this representation, we claim that

(4.4)

\beta_{k,n}(s,t)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}g_{r_{n}s,r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n})+\sum_{i=k+3}^{n}\sum_{j>0}j\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=i\end{subarray}}g^{(i,j)}_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n}).

In fact, by (

) the right hand side of (

) is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}g_{r_{n}s,r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n})+\beta_{k,n}(t)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}g_{r_{n}t}^{(k+2,1)}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\beta_{k,n}(t)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\

Here,

represents the number of

and

By (

(4.5)

To prove this, rewrite the left hand side of (

(4.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k+3}^{n}\sum_{j>0}j\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=i\end{subarray}}g^{(i,j)}_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k+3}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\

Here, we recall that

-simplices on

(with connectivity radius

\displaystyle\beta_{k}\big(\check{C}({\mathcal{Y}},r_{n}t)\big)\leq f_{k,r_{n}t}({\mathcal{Y}})\leq\binom{k+3}{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Z}\subset{\mathcal{Y}},\\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k+3}^{n}\sum_{j>0}j\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{r_{n}t}]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k+3}r_{n}^{d(k+2)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\check{C}(\{0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+2}\},t)\text{ is connected}\big\}

Since

and

(4.9)

\partial R_{s,t,u,v}\subset\big(\{s\}\times[u,v]\big)\cup\big(\{t\}\times[u,v]\big)\cup\big([s,t]\times\{u\}\big)\cup\big([s,t]\times\{v\}\big).

Now, we show that

(4.10)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\zeta_{k}\big(\{s\}\times[u,v]\big)=0\Big)=1,\ \ 0\leq s\leq u\leq v\leq\infty.

We check only the case
s>0
. Writing

\{s\}\times[u,v]=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v},

and appealing to Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\zeta_{k}\big(\{s\}\times[u,v]\big)\geq 1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\zeta_{k}\Big(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v}\Big)\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\lim_{n\to\infty}\zeta_{k}\big(R_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v}\big)\Big].

Applying Fatou’s lemma together with (
3.1
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\zeta_{k}\big(\{s\}\times[u,v]\big)\geq 1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\zeta_{k}\big(R_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}H_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y})\Big]

\displaystyle=C_{k}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}H_{s-n^{-1},s+n^{-1},u-n^{-1},v}(0,{\bf y})d{\bf y}.

By the dominated convergence theorem, the last term is equal to
0
, and hence, we have obtained (
4.10
). Repeating the same arguments for each of the terms in the right hand side of (
4.9
), we obtain (
4.7
), as desired.

Now, we proceed to show the weak convergence in (
4.8
). Our proof techniques are related to those in Theorem 5.1 of
[
37

. Because of (
2.2
) and (

), it suffices to verify that, as

,

(4.11)

\big(\beta_{k,n}(s_{i},t_{i}),\,i=1,\dots,m\big)\Rightarrow\Big(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y}),\,i=1,\dots,m\Big)

for all
m\geq 1
and
0\leq s_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq\infty
,
i=1,\dots,m
, where
{\bf y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}
.
In the following, we first demonstrate that

(4.12)

\big(G_{k,n}(s_{i},t_{i}),\,i=1,\dots,m\big):=\Big(\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}}),\,i=1,\dots,m\Big)

) and the persistent Betti numbers in (

) vanishes in probability as

Now, we claim that for every
m\geq 1
and
0\leq s_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq\infty
,
a_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,\dots,m
,

(4.13)

\eta_{k,n}:=\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2}\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})}\Rightarrow\eta_{k}\ \ \text{in }M_{p}\big((0,\infty]\big).

Here,

is the Poisson random measure on
(0,\infty]
with finite mean measure

, where

\tau_{k}(\cdot)=\lambda\Big\{{\bf y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\in\cdot\Big\}.

Notice that

can be represented as

Now, we prove (

). After establishing it, we will show the weak convergence of (

By virtue of Theorem 3.1 in

]
, the following two conditions suffice for (

\sup_{A}\big|\,\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k,n}(A)]-\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}(A)]\,\big|\to 0,\ \ \ n\to\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all Borel sets in

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k+1}n^{2k+4-\ell}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2k+4-\ell}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\neq 0\Big\}

\displaystyle\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell},x_{k+3},\dots,x_{2k+4-\ell})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell},x_{k+3},\dots,x_{2k+4-\ell})\neq 0\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{i=1}^{2k+4-\ell}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}\to 0,\ \ \ n\to\infty.

For (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k,n}(A)]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k+2}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\in A\Big\}\prod_{i=1}^{k+2}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\in A\Big\}

(with

On the other hand, one can rewrite

\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}(A)]=\frac{1}{(k+2)!}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)^{k+2}dx\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\in A\Big\}d{\bf y}.

a.e. Moreover,

For the proof of (
4.15
),
the change of variables

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k+1}n^{2k+4-\ell}r_{n}^{d(2k+3-\ell)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2k+3-\ell}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})h_{t_{i}}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})\neq 0\Big\}

\displaystyle\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell-1},y_{k+2},\dots,y_{2k+3-\ell})h_{t_{i}}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell-1},y_{k+2},\dots,y_{2k+3-\ell})\neq 0\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times f(x)\prod_{i=1}^{2k+3-\ell}f(x+r_{n}y_{i})d{\bf y}dx

, because the integral is finite and

\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k+1}n^{2k+4-\ell}r_{n}^{d(2k+3-\ell)}=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k+1}(nr_{n}^{d})^{k+2-\ell}\to 0,\ \ \ n\to\infty.

Now, we obtain (
4.13

Subsequently, we claim that, as
n\to\infty
,

(4.17)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}G_{k,n}(s_{i},t_{i})\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y}).

Since the choice of
a_{i}
’s is arbitrary, (
4.17
) is equivalent to

(4.18)

\big(G_{k,n}(s_{i},t_{i}),\,i=1,\dots,m\big)\Rightarrow\Big(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y}),\,i=1,\dots,m\Big).

For the proof of (
4.17
), let
\gamma>0
and define a continuous map
\widehat{T}_{\gamma}:M_{p}\big((0,\infty]\big)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by
\widehat{T}_{\gamma}\big(\sum_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}\big)=\sum_{j}x_{j}{\mathbbm{1}}\{x_{j}\geq\gamma\}
(the continuity of
\widehat{T}_{\gamma}
is proven in Section 7.2.3 of
[
42
]
). Applying the continuous mapping theorem to (
4.13
), we have

\widehat{T}_{\gamma}(\eta_{k,n})\Rightarrow\widehat{T}_{\gamma}(\eta_{k})\ \ \text{as }n\to\infty.

Clearly, as
\gamma\downarrow 0
,

\widehat{T}_{\gamma}(\eta_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}\{Y_{i}\geq\gamma\}\to\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i},\ \ \ \text{a.s.}

Furthermore,

\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y}).

One can prove this by computing the Laplace transforms of both sides. On the one hand, Theorem 5.1 in
[
42
]
demonstrates that, for every
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big\{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})M_{k}(d{\bf y})\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\exp\Big\{-C_{k}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}\Big(1-e^{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})}\Big)d{\bf y}\Big\}.

On the other hand, it is a simple exercise to check that the Laplace transform of
\sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_{i}
is equal to (
4.19
).

Now, (
4.17

\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})\,{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})\leq\gamma\Big\}\geq\epsilon\bigg)=0.

By Markov’s inequality and the customary change of variables as in (
4.16
), as well as

, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\\
|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2\end{subarray}}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})\,{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}({\mathcal{Y}})\leq\gamma\Big\}\geq\epsilon\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\binom{n}{k+2}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{r_{n}s_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})h_{r_{n}t_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\leq\gamma\Big\}\prod_{i=1}^{k+2}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}^{k+1}}{\epsilon(k+2)!}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y}){\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}h_{s_{i}}(0,{\bf y})h_{t_{i}}(0,{\bf y})\leq\gamma\Big\}d{\bf y}.

The last term converges to
0
as
\gamma\downarrow 0
by the dominated convergence theorem, and we have obtained (
4.18
).

From the argument thus far, the entire proof can be completed, provided that, for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq\infty

\beta_{k,n}(s,t)-G_{k,n}(s,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}0,\ \ \ n\to\infty.

\displaystyle\big|\,\beta_{k,n}(s,t)-G_{k,n}(s,t)\,\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2}\hskip-10.0pt{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\check{C}({\mathcal{Y}},r_{n}t)\text{ is connected}\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\|y-z\|\leq r_{n}t\text{ for some }y\in{\mathcal{Y}}\text{ and }z\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\setminus{\mathcal{Y}}\big\}+\binom{k+3}{k+1}L_{r_{n}t}

By (

as

In the course of our proof,

For the brevity of the proof, some of the technical results and arguments have been moved to the Appendix.

It is known from Corollary A.3 in

\displaystyle G_{k,n}(s,t)

Similarly,
G_{k,n}(s,t)

in the Appendix, there exists

\sum_{(b_{i},d_{i})\in D_{k,n},\,b_{i}\leq T}\delta_{(b_{i},d_{i})}=\delta_{(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}))},

and hence, for each
\ell=1,2

\sum_{(b_{i},d_{i})\in D_{k,n}}H_{\ell}(b_{i},d_{i})=H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big).

This in turn implies that

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{U_{n}\cap\big\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})=1,\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{{\bf j}})=0\text{ for all }{\bf j}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}\text{ with }{\bf j}\neq{\bf i}\big\}\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{U_{n}\cap\big\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})=1\big\}\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle-(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{U_{n}\cap\bigcup_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2},\,{\bf j}\neq{\bf i}}\big\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{{\bf j}})=1\big\}\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle=:G_{n}-H_{n}.

Of the last two terms,
H_{n}
is negligible as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, repeating the same argument as that for proving
D_{n}\to 0
can yield that

H_{n}\leq(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k+1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2},\,|{\bf i}\cap{\bf j}|=\ell}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf j})=1\Big)\to 0,\ \ \ n\to\infty.

Subsequently, note that, if
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})=0
for some
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}
, then
b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\geq T
, and therefore,
H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)=0
for
\ell=1,2
. This implies that

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big){\mathbbm{1}}_{U_{n}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle-(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\Big(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\Big){\mathbbm{1}}_{U_{n}^{c}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=:I_{n}-J_{n}.

Then,

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{H_{1}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\big)\geq\epsilon_{1}\big\}\times{\mathbbm{1}}_{U_{n}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\,{\mathbbm{1}}_{U_{n}^{c}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k+2}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+2}}\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{I}_{k+3},\,|{\bf i}\cap{\bf j}|=\ell}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf i})\,{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\check{C}({\mathcal{X}}_{\bf j},r_{n}T)\text{ is connected}\big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\binom{n}{k+2}\binom{n-k-2}{k+3}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k+2})\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathbb{P}\Big(\check{C}\big(\{X_{1},\dots,X_{k+3}\},r_{n}T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big)

\displaystyle+(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k+2}\binom{n}{k+2}\binom{k+2}{\ell}\binom{n-k-2}{k+3-\ell}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k+2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\check{C}\big(\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell},X_{k+3},\dots,X_{2k+5-\ell}\},r_{n}T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}n^{2k+5}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})\prod_{i=1}^{k+2}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+3}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\check{C}\big(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k+3}\},r_{n}T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big\}\prod_{i=1}^{k+3}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k+2}(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}n^{2k+5-\ell}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2k+5-\ell}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{r_{n}t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\check{C}\big(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell},x_{k+3},\dots,x_{2k+5-\ell}\},r_{n}T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big\}\prod_{i=1}^{2k+5-\ell}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=:K_{n}+L_{n}.

By the customary change of variables as before, we have as
n\to\infty

K_{n}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}^{2k+3}n^{k+3}r_{n}^{d(k+2)}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{t}(0,{\bf y})d{\bf y}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\check{C}\big(\{0,{\bf y}\},T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big\}d{\bf y}\to 0,

and

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k+2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2k+4-\ell}(nr_{n}^{d})^{k+3-\ell}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2k+4-\ell}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}h_{t}(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathbbm{1}}\Big\{\check{C}\big(\{0,y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell-1},y_{k+2},\dots,y_{2k+4-\ell}\},T\big)\text{ is connected}\Big\}d{\bf y}\to 0.

Thus, it follows that
J_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

From all of the arguments thus far, we conclude that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(F_{H_{1},H_{2},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}})=I_{n}+o(1)

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\binom{n}{k+2}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\bigg(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(r_{n}^{-1}{\bf x}),d(r_{n}^{-1}{\bf x})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{i=1}^{k+2}f(x_{i})d{\bf x}+o(1)

\displaystyle=(n^{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)})^{-1}\binom{n}{k+2}r_{n}^{d(k+1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\bigg(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times f(x)\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}f(x+r_{n}y_{i})d{\bf y}dx+o(1)

\displaystyle\to C_{k}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+1}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{2}\bigg(1-\exp\Big\{-\Big(H_{\ell}\big(b(0,{\bf y}),d(0,{\bf y})\big)-\epsilon_{\ell}\Big)_{+}\Big\}\bigg)d{\bf y}=\eta(F_{H_{1},H_{2},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}),

where the last convergence is due to the continuity of
f
and the dominated convergence theorem.

One of the key ingredients for our proof is the concentration bound for a Poisson
U
-statistics (
[
3
]
). Let us first rephrase the setup and assumptions given in
[

in a way suitable for the current study.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a Poisson point process on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite intensity measure and
s:({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{i}\to\{0,1\}
be a symmetric indicator function that satisfies the following.

(i)
There exists
c_{1}>0
such that

(5.1)

s(x_{1},\dots,x_{i})=1\ \ \text{whenever diam}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i})<c_{1},

where diam
(x_{1},\dots,x_{i})=\max_{1\leq p,q\leq i}\|x_{p}-x_{q}\|
.

(ii)
There exists
c_{2}>c_{1}
such that

(5.2)

s(x_{1},\dots,x_{i})=0\ \ \text{whenever diam}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i})>c_{2}.

We then define a
Poisson
U
-statistics
or order
i
by

(5.3)

F(\mathcal{P}):=\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathcal{P},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=i}s({\mathcal{Y}}).

Proposition 5.1

(Theorem 3.1 in
[
3
]
)
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
i
,
d
,
c_{1}
, and
c_{2}
, such that, for all
r>0
,

(5.4)

\mathbb{P}\Big(F(\mathcal{P})\geq\mathbb{E}\big[F(\mathcal{P})\big]+r\Big)\leq\exp\Big\{-C\Big[\big(\mathbb{E}[F(\mathcal{P})]+r\big)^{1/(2i)}-\big(\mathbb{E}[F(\mathcal{P})]\big)^{1/(2i)}\Big]^{2}\Big\}

and

(5.5)

\mathbb{P}\Big(F(\mathcal{P})\leq\mathbb{E}\big[F(\mathcal{P})\big]-r\Big)\leq\exp\Big\{-\frac{Cr^{2}}{\text{Var}\big(F(\mathcal{P})\big)}\Big\}.

For the next lemma, recall the sequences
(p_{m})
and
(q_{m})
in (
4.26
), and
v_{m}=\lfloor e^{m^{\gamma}}\rfloor
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u_{m}
and
w_{m}
be any of the sequences
p_{m}
,
q_{m}
,
v_{m}
, and
v_{m+1}
. Assume that
(r_{n})
is a regularly varying sequence with exponent
\rho<0
. Then,

\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\to 1,\ \ \ m\to\infty,

and

\frac{r_{u_{m}}}{r_{w_{m}}}\to 1,\ \ \ m\to\infty.

Proof.

By the definition of these sequences, it is clear that

\frac{v_{m}}{v_{m+1}}\leq\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\leq\frac{v_{m+1}}{v_{m}}.

We see that

1\leq\frac{v_{m+1}}{v_{m}}\leq\frac{e^{(m+1)^{\gamma}-m^{\gamma}}}{1-e^{-m^{\gamma}}}=\frac{e^{m^{\gamma-1}(\gamma+o(1))}}{1-e^{-m^{\gamma}}},\ \ \ m\to\infty.

As
0<\gamma<1
, the rightmost term goes to
1
as
m\to\infty
.

For the proof of the second statement, we write

\frac{r_{u_{m}}}{r_{w_{m}}}=\frac{r_{\lfloor w_{m}\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\rfloor}}{r_{w_{m}}}-\Big(\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\Big)^{\rho}+\Big(\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\Big)^{\rho}.

As shown above,
u_{m}/w_{m}\to 1
as
m\to\infty
, and therefore,
u_{m}/w_{m}\in[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
for sufficiently large
m
. Hence, as
m\to\infty
,

\Big|\,\frac{r_{\lfloor w_{m}\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\rfloor}}{r_{w_{m}}}-\Big(\frac{u_{m}}{w_{m}}\Big)^{\rho}\,\Big|\leq\sup_{\frac{1}{2}\leq a\leq\frac{3}{2}}\Big|\,\frac{r_{\lfloor w_{m}a\rfloor}}{r_{w_{m}}}-a^{\rho}\,\Big|\to 0,

by the uniform convergence of regularly varying sequences (see Proposition 2.4 in
[
42
]
).
∎

The following lemma gives the asymptotic moments of various quantities used for the proof of Theorem

Lemma 5.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

\mathbb{E}\big[T_{m}^{\uparrow}(s,t)\big]\sim A_{k}(s,t)v_{m}^{k+2}r_{q_{m}}^{d(k+1)},

(5.7)

\mathbb{E}\big[T_{m}^{\downarrow}(s,t)\big]\sim A_{k}(s,t)v_{m+1}^{k+2}r_{p_{m}}^{d(k+1)},

(5.8)

\text{Var}\big(T_{m}^{\downarrow}(s,t)\big)\sim A_{k}(s,t)v_{m+1}^{k+2}r_{p_{m}}^{d(k+1)},

(5.9)

\mathbb{E}\big[V_{m}(t)\big]\sim\frac{v_{m}^{k+3}r_{q_{m}}^{d(k+2)}}{(k+3)!}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)^{k+3}dx\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k+2}}{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\check{C}\big(\{0,{\bf y}\},t\big)\text{ is connected}\big\}d{\bf y},

The proofs of (

Lemma 5.4
.

\big(v_{m}^{k+2}r_{q_{m}}^{d(k+1)}\big)^{-1}\Big[\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{v_{m+1}},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2}h_{r_{p_{m}}s}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{p_{m}}t}({\mathcal{Y}})-T_{m}^{\uparrow}(s,t)\Big]\to 0,\ \ \text{a.s.},

|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+3\end{subarray}}{\mathbbm{1}}\big\{\check{C}({\mathcal{Y}},r_{p_{m}}t)\text{ is connected}\big\}-V_{m}(t)\Big]\to 0,\ \ \text{a.s.}

The proofs of these statements are essentially the same; therefore, we prove only (
5.10

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{v_{m}^{k+2}r_{q_{m}}^{d(k+1)}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{v_{m+1}},\,|{\mathcal{Y}}|=k+2}h_{r_{p_{m}}s}({\mathcal{Y}})h_{r_{p_{m}}t}({\mathcal{Y}})-T_{m}^{\uparrow}(s,t)\Big|\Big]<\infty.
