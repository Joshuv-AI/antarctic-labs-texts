1.
Introduction

1.1.

A result due to Atzmon and Olevskii
[
AO96
]
asserts that
for every
p>2
, there is a function
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})

whose translates by the positive integers

\{f(x-n)\}
,
n=1,2,\dots
,
span the whole space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
, that is,
these translates are complete in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
.
There are now several known approaches to this result,
see
[
Nik99
]
,
[
FOSZ14
]
.

In the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, on the other hand,
integer translates can never be complete.
In this connection, it was conjectured (see
[
RS95
]
)
that no system

\{f(x-\lambda)\}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
can be complete in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
if the set
\Lambda

is
uniformly discrete
, i.e. satisfies the condition

|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda|\geqslant\delta(\Lambda)>0

(1.1)

for any two distinct points
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda
.
However, this is not the case. It was proved by Olevskii

[
Ole97
]
that for any “small perturbation” of the integers,

\lambda_{n}=n+\alpha_{n},\quad 0\neq\alpha_{n}\to 0\quad(|n|\to+\infty)

(1.2)

there exists
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

such that the system

\{f(x-\lambda_{n})\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
is complete in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It was moreover
shown in
[
OU04
]

that if the perturbations are exponentially small, i.e.

0<|\alpha_{n}|<Cr^{|n|},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.3)

for some
0<r<1
and
C>0
,
then
f
can be chosen in the Schwartz class.

More recently, by a development of the approach
from
[
OU04
]
, the latter result was
extended to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
spaces
[
OU18a
]
, namely,
it was proved that there is a function

f
in the Schwartz class, such that if the sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
satisfies (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)
then the system
\{f(x-\lambda_{n})\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
is complete in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for every
p>1
.

In fact the latter result holds for a wide class of
separable Banach function spaces on
\mathbb{R}

[
OU18b
]
. On the other hand, in the space

L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
no system of uniformly discrete
translates can be complete, see
[
BOU06
]
.

1.2.

The goal of the present note is to provide a different approach
to the construction of a function
f
which spans the
space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
p>1
, by a uniformly discrete system of translates.
In fact, in our construction we shall use only positive translates, and
moreover the completeness remains true for any subsystem
obtained by the removal of a finite number of elements.

The result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a real sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

\lambda_{n}=n+o(1)
, and there is
a Schwartz function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
,
such that for any
N
the system

\{f(x-\lambda_{n})\},\;n>N,

(1.4)

is complete in the space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for every
p>1
.

Moreover, our approach allows us to extend the result to the wider
class of Banach function spaces on
\mathbb{R}
considered in
[
OU18b
]
,
see Section
5
.

The approach is inspired by the papers
[
Lan64
]
and
[
AAG08
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we briefly recall some necessary background
in the theory of Schwartz distributions on
\mathbb{R}

(see
[
Rud91
]
for more details).

The
Schwartz space

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
consists of all infinitely smooth
functions
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
such that for each
n,k\geqslant 0
, the seminorm

\|\varphi\|_{n,k}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}(1+|x|)^{n}|\varphi^{(k)}(x)|

(2.1)

is finite. It is a topological linear space whose topology is
induced by the metric

d(\varphi,\psi):=\sum_{n,k\geqslant 0}2^{-(n+k)}\frac{\|\varphi-\psi\|_{n,k}}{1+\|\varphi-\psi\|_{n,k}}

(2.2)

which also makes
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
a complete, separable metric space.

A
temperate distribution
on
\mathbb{R}
is a
linear functional on the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})

which is continuous with respect to the
metric (
2.2
).
We use
\alpha(\varphi)
to denote the action of
a temperate distribution
\alpha
on a
Schwartz function
\varphi
.

If
\varphi
is a Schwartz function then its Fourier transform
is defined by

\widehat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)e^{-2\pi ixt}dt.

(2.3)

If
\alpha
is a temperate distribution then
its Fourier transform is defined by

\widehat{\alpha}(\varphi)=\alpha(\widehat{\varphi})
.

We denote by
\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
the closed support of a
temperate distribution
\alpha
.

If
\alpha
is a temperate distribution with compact
support, then
\alpha(\varphi)
is well defined
for every smooth function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
.
In this case
\widehat{\alpha}

is an infinitely smooth function given by

\widehat{\alpha}(x)=\alpha(e_{-x})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
where here we denote

e_{x}(t):=e^{2\pi ixt}
.

If
\alpha
is
a temperate distribution and if

\varphi
is a
Schwartz function, then
the product
\alpha\cdot\varphi
is
a temperate distribution defined
by
(\alpha\cdot\varphi)(\psi)=\alpha(\varphi\cdot\psi)
,

\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.
In this case we have

\operatorname{supp}(\alpha\cdot\varphi)\subset\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\cap\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)
.

The convolution

\alpha\ast\varphi

of a temperate distribution

\alpha
and
a Schwartz function
\varphi
is
an infinitely smooth function
which is also
a temperate distribution, and
whose Fourier transform is
\widehat{\alpha}\cdot\widehat{\varphi}
.
If
\varphi
has compact support, then

\operatorname{supp}(\alpha\ast\varphi)
is contained in the
Minkowski sum

\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)+\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)
.

3.
Landau’s complete system of exponentials

The starting point for our approach
is H. Landau’s paper
[
Lan64
]
.
The paper is concerned with
the completeness of a system of exponential functions
in the space

L^{p}(\Omega)
,
1\leqslant p<\infty
, or
C(\Omega)
,
where
\Omega
is an interval, or a finite union of intervals,
in
\mathbb{R}
.

By classical theory it is known that if
\{\lambda_{n}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
is a “regular” sequence, i.e.

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\lambda_{n}-n|<+\infty,

(3.1)

then the system of exponential functions

\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
is complete on any interval of length
<1
,
while it is incomplete on any interval of length
>1

(see e.g.
[
OU16
, Section 4.7]
). These results are
based on the theory of entire functions of exponential type.

It was discovered by Landau that
the situation is quite different if the single interval is
replaced by a finite union of intervals.
The following result was proved in
[
Lan64
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Lan64
]
)
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
there is a real sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with

|\lambda_{n}-n|<\varepsilon
, such that
for any finite union of intervals of the form

\Omega_{h,K}:=\bigcup_{|k|\leqslant K}[k+h,k+1-h],\qquad\text{($K$ finite, $0<h<1/2$)}

(3.2)

and for any
N
, the system

\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}\}
,
n>N
,
is complete in the space
C(\Omega_{h,K})
.

We note that a set of the form (
3.2
) can have arbitrarily large measure.

The sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in Theorem
3.1

is constructed as follows. First, the positive integers are partitioned
into pairwise disjoint subsets
\{S_{r}\}_{r=1}^{\infty}

such that

\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\#(S_{r}\cap[1,k])}{k}=1,\quad r=1,2,\dots.

(3.3)

Now, let
\{\theta_{r}\}_{r=1}^{\infty}
be real numbers
satisfying
|\theta_{r}|<\varepsilon
, and for each positive integer
n
, define

\lambda_{n}:=n+\theta_{r}
if
n\in S_{r}
.
It is shown in
[
Lan64
]
that there is
a choice of the numbers
\{\theta_{r}\}
, such that the sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

thus obtained satisfies the conclusion in
Theorem
3.1
.
(In fact, it suffices that the numbers
\{\theta_{r}\}

be distinct modulo
1
).

By duality, Landau’s result can be equivalently stated as a uniqueness
result for (complex) measures
\mu
whose support lies in a set
of the form (
3.2
) and whose Fourier transform

\widehat{\mu}
vanishes on the set
\{-\lambda_{n}\}
,
n>N
.
We will need the following
slightly more general version of the result,
where “measure” is replaced by “distribution”.

Corollary 3.2
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, let

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

be the sequence from Theorem
3.1
.
Let
\alpha
be a temperate
distribution on
\mathbb{R}
whose support
lies in a set
\Omega_{h,K}
of the form (
3.2
),
and assume that there is
N
such that

\widehat{\alpha}(-\lambda_{n})=0
,
n>N
.
Then
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

Let
\chi
be a smooth nonnegative function,

\operatorname{supp}(\chi)\subset[-1,1]
,

\int\chi=1
, and define
\chi_{\delta}(t):=\delta^{-1}\chi(\delta^{-1}t)
.
The convolution
\alpha_{\delta}:=\alpha\ast\chi_{\delta}
is
a measure (in fact, a smooth function)
supported on the set
\Omega_{h-\delta,K}

and annihilating the system

\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}\}
,
n>N
.
By the completeness of the system in the
space
C(\Omega_{h-\delta,K})
it follows that

\alpha_{\delta}=0
for
0<\delta<h
.
Letting
\delta\to 0
, we obtain
\alpha=0

(see
[
Rud91
, Theorem 6.32]
).
∎

4.
Completeness of weighted exponentials

4.1.

The next step in our approach is
to construct a system of

weighted exponential functions

with “almost integer” frequencies,
which is complete in a certain
space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}

that will be now introduced.

Definition 4.1
.

The space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is defined to be
the closed subspace
of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, that consists
of the functions
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying

f^{(j)}(n)=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad j=0,1,2,\dots.

(4.1)

The space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is then a
complete, separable metric space
with the metric inherited from the Schwartz space.

We will use Landau’s Theorem
3.1
in order
to prove the following result:

Theorem 4.2
.

There is a real sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with

\lambda_{n}=n+o(1)
, and there is a function
\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
such that for any
N
the system

\{\varphi(t)e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}\},\;n>N,

(4.2)

is complete in the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

The proof will be done in several steps.

4.2.

We use
J_{0}(\mathbb{R})
to denote the linear space consisting of
all the smooth, compactly supported functions on
\mathbb{R}

which vanish in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

J_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is a dense subspace of
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We choose a smooth, compactly supported
function
\chi
on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\chi(t)=1
on
[-1,1]
, and define

\chi_{r}(t):=\chi(rt)
. First,
assume that a smooth, compactly supported
function
f
has a zero of infinite order
at the origin. It is then straightforward to check
that
\|f\cdot\chi_{r}\|_{n,k}\to 0

as
r\to+\infty
for every
n,k
,
hence
f\cdot(1-\chi_{r})\to f

as
r\to+\infty
in the Schwartz space metric.
This shows that

f
is a limit of smooth, compactly supported
functions vanishing in a neighborhood of the origin.
By a similar argument, any compactly supported
function
f\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is a limit
of functions belonging to
J_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
So to conclude the proof, it would suffice
to show that the
compactly supported functions in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})

form a dense set in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed,
if
f\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
then

f\cdot\chi_{r}
is
compactly supported, belongs to
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})

and
f\cdot\chi_{r}\to f
as
r\to 0

(see
[
Rud91
, Theorem 7.10]
).
∎

4.3.

We will next prove the following
version of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, let

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

be the sequence from Theorem
3.1
.
Then there is a function
\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
such that for any
N
the system
(
4.2
)
is complete in the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover the set of such functions
\varphi

is residual in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

We note that the sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in this result
is uniformly discrete, but does not satisfy the condition

\lambda_{n}=n+o(1)
, so Theorem
4.2

does not follow directly.

The proof below is based on the method from
[
AAG08
, Section 2]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

For each
\chi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and each
N
and
\rho>0
,
let
G(\chi,\rho,N)
be the set of all functions

\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that there is a polynomial

P(t)=\sum_{n>N}c_{n}e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}

(4.3)

satisfying
d(\varphi\cdot P,\chi)<\rho
.
We claim that
G(\chi,\rho,N)
is an open, dense
set in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

To see that
G(\chi,\rho,N)
is an open set,
we observe that
multiplication by a polynomial
P
is a continuous mapping

I_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to I_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, hence

\{\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{T}):d(\varphi\cdot P,\chi)<\rho\}

is an open set for every polynomial
P
.
It follows that
G(\chi,\rho,N)
, being a union of sets of this form
as
P
goes through all polynomials (
4.3
),
is an open set.

Next we show that the set

G(\chi,\rho,N)
is dense
in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. To establish this we
fix
f\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and
\delta>0
, and show
that there is
\varphi\in G(\chi,\rho,N)
such that

d(f,\varphi)<\delta
.

Since
J_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is a dense subspace of
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we can find
two smooth functions

\chi_{0}
and
f_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}
,
both with compact support and
vanishing in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{Z}
, such that

d(\chi,\chi_{0})<\rho/2
and

d(f,f_{0})<\delta/2
.
We choose
R
sufficiently large and

h>0
sufficiently small, such that

\chi_{0}
and
f_{0}
are both supported on
[-R,R]

and both vanish on the set
\mathbb{Z}+(-2h,2h)
.
We also choose
a smooth, compactly supported function
\eta
on
\mathbb{R}

vanishing on the set
\mathbb{Z}+(-h,h)
and
such that
\eta(t)=1
on
[-R,R]\setminus(\mathbb{Z}+(-2h,2h))
.

We claim that there is a Schwartz function
q

such that

d(f_{0},q\cdot\eta)<\delta/2

and moreover,
q
has no zeros on
[-R,R]
.
Indeed, the fact that
\eta(t)=1

on
\operatorname{supp}(f_{0})
implies that

f_{0}=f_{0}\cdot\eta
, hence
by approximating
f_{0}
in the Schwartz space
by a nonzero smooth function
g
such that

\widehat{g}
has compact support, we obtain

d(f_{0},g\cdot\eta)<\delta/2
.
Observe that the function
g
admits a holomorphic
extension to the complex plane, given by

g(z):=\int\widehat{g}(u)e^{2\pi iuz}du,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

(4.4)

and
g
being nonzero has only finitely many zeros
in the closed rectangle given by
|\operatorname{Re}(z)|\leqslant R
and

|\operatorname{Im}(z)|\leqslant 1
. We then
define
g_{\sigma}(t):=g(t+i\sigma)

and observe that

g_{\sigma}\to g
as
\sigma\to 0
in the Schwartz space,
so the function
q:=g_{\sigma}

satisfies the required properties
provided that
\sigma>0
is small enough.

Let
\varphi:=q\cdot\eta
, then

\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
(in fact,
\varphi\in J_{0}(\mathbb{R})
) and

d(f,\varphi)\leqslant d(f,f_{0})+d(f_{0},q\cdot\eta)<\delta/2+\delta/2=\delta.

(4.5)

We claim that

\varphi\in G(\chi,\rho,N)
. Indeed,
suppose that
\alpha
is a temperate distribution on
\mathbb{R}
which
annihilates the system (
4.2
).
This means that the distribution

\alpha\cdot\varphi
satisfies

(\alpha\cdot\varphi)^{\wedge}(-\lambda_{n})=0

for all
n>N
.
Since
\varphi
is compactly supported and vanishes
on
\mathbb{Z}+(-h,h)
, the distribution

\alpha\cdot\varphi
thus satisfies the assumptions
in Corollary
3.2
. It therefore
follows from Corollary
3.2
that

\alpha\cdot\varphi=0
. Since

\varphi
has no zeros on
\operatorname{supp}(\chi_{0})
, we can write

\chi_{0}=\varphi\cdot g
where
g
is a smooth
function of compact support. Hence

\alpha(\chi_{0})=\alpha(\varphi\cdot g)=(\alpha\cdot\varphi)(g)=0.

(4.6)

We conclude (see
[
Rud91
, Theorem 3.5]
) that the function

\chi_{0}
must lie in the closed linear subspace of
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})

spanned by the system (
4.2
),
so there is a polynomial
(
4.3
)
satisfying
d(\varphi\cdot P,\chi_{0})<\rho/2
.
In turn this implies that

d(\varphi\cdot P,\chi)\leqslant d(\varphi\cdot P,\chi_{0})+d(\chi,\chi_{0})<\rho/2+\rho/2=\rho,

(4.7)

so that
\varphi\in G(\chi,\rho,N)
.
This establishes that

G(\chi,\rho,N)
is a dense set in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

Finally, we choose a sequence
\{\chi_{j}\}

dense in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, and consider the set

\bigcap_{j,k,N}^{\infty}G(\chi_{j},k^{-1},N).

(4.8)

It is a residual set in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, being
the countable intersection of open, dense sets,
and any function
\varphi

from the set (
4.8
)
satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
4.4
.
∎

4.4.

Now we use Theorem
4.4
in order to
establish Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Let
\{\chi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence
which is dense in the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
We then construct by induction functions
\varphi_{k}\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
an increasing sequence of positive integers
\{N_{k}\}
,
and trigonometric polynomials

P_{k}(t)=\sum_{N_{k}<n<N_{k+1}}c_{n}e^{2\pi i\lambda^{(k)}_{n}t}

(4.9)

in the following way. We let
\varphi_{0}:=0
and
N_{0}:=0
.

At the
k
’th step of the induction, we invoke
Theorem
3.1
with
\varepsilon=k^{-1}

and obtain a real sequence

\{\lambda^{(k)}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

with

|\lambda^{(k)}_{n}-n|<k^{-1}
, for all
n
.
By Theorem
4.4
, there exists
in the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
a dense (in fact, residual)
set of functions
\varphi
,
such that for any
N
the system

\{\varphi(t)e^{2\pi i\lambda^{(k)}_{n}t}\}
,
n>N
,
is complete in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. This implies that we can
choose
\varphi_{k}\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and
a polynomial
P_{k}
as in (
4.9
), such that

d(\varphi_{k}\cdot P_{k},\chi_{k})<k^{-1}

and

d(\varphi_{k},\varphi_{k-1})\leqslant 2^{-k},\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant k-1}d(\varphi_{k}\cdot P_{l},\varphi_{k-1}\cdot P_{l})\leqslant k^{-1}2^{-k}.

(4.10)

The sequence

\{\varphi_{k}\}
then
converges in the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
to some

\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, and

d(\varphi\cdot P_{k},\chi_{k})\leqslant d(\varphi_{k}\cdot P_{k},\chi_{k})+\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}d(\varphi_{l}\cdot P_{k},\varphi_{l-1}\cdot P_{k})<2k^{-1}

(4.11)

for every
k
, due to (
4.10
).

The intervals
(N_{k},N_{k+1})
,
k=1,2,\dots
, are pairwise
disjoint, so each positive integer
n
may belong to at most
one of these intervals. For each
n\in(N_{k},N_{k+1})\cap\mathbb{Z}

we define
\lambda_{n}:=\lambda^{(k)}_{n}
, and if
n
does not
lie in any of the intervals
(N_{k},N_{k+1})
,
then we set
\lambda_{n}:=n
. It follows that

|\lambda_{n}-n|<k^{-1}
for
n>N_{k}
,
which implies that
\lambda_{n}=n+o(1)
.

Now given any
N
, we choose a sufficiently large

k_{0}
such that
N_{k_{0}}>N
. Then the sequence

\{\chi_{k}\}
,
k>k_{0}
, is also dense in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
Since we have

d(\varphi\cdot P_{k},\chi_{k})<2k^{-1}

according to (
4.11
),
we conclude that the sequence

\{\varphi\cdot P_{k}\}
,
k>k_{0}
,
is dense in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
as well. But notice that

\varphi\cdot P_{k}
belongs to the linear
span of the system (
4.2
)
if
k>k_{0}
, due to
(
4.9
).
This implies that the system (
4.2
)
is complete in
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

5.
Completeness of translates

Now we can prove
Theorem
1.1
. Moreover,
we will prove an extension of this theorem to the wider
class of Banach function spaces on
\mathbb{R}
considered in
[
OU18b
]
.

5.1.

Let
X
be a Banach function space on
\mathbb{R}
.
Following
[
OU18b
]
we assume that

(i)

The Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})

is continuously and densely embedded in
X
.

In this case every element
\alpha

of the dual space
X^{*}
defines a continuous
linear functional on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, so
\alpha

is a temperate distribution on
\mathbb{R}
.
We suppose further that

(ii)

If a temperate distribution
\alpha\in X^{*}
satisfies

\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{\alpha})\subset\mathbb{Z}
,
then
\alpha=0
.

We can now establish the following result
(compare with
[
OU18b
, Theorem 1]
).

Theorem 5.1
.

There is a real sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

\lambda_{n}=n+o(1)
, and there is
a Schwartz function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
,
such that for any
N
the system

\{f(x-\lambda_{n})\},\;n>N,

(5.1)

is complete in every Banach function space
X

satisfying
(i)
and
(ii)
above.

Proof.

Consider the sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

and the function
\varphi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
from
Theorem
4.2
.
Let
f:=\widehat{\varphi}
which
is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then the Fourier transform maps the
weighted exponential system

\{\varphi(t)e^{2\pi i\lambda_{n}t}\}

onto the system of translates

\{f(x-\lambda_{n})\}
.

Let now
X
be a Banach function space
satisfying
(i)
and
(ii)
.
The Fourier transform is an isomorphism of
the Schwartz space (as a topological linear space)
onto itself, so it
follows from
(i)
that the Fourier transform
embeds
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
continuously into
X
.
Hence, to prove that the system (
5.1
)
is complete in
X
,
it would suffice to show that the image

\widehat{I}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
of the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})

under the Fourier transform, is a dense subspace of
X
.

Indeed, let
\alpha\in X^{*}
be
a temperate distribution which annihilates

\widehat{I}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, that is,

\alpha(\widehat{\psi})=0
for every
\psi\in I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
This means that the Fourier transform

\widehat{\alpha}
annihilates the space
I_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
which is possible only if
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{\alpha})\subset\mathbb{Z}
.
It then follows from
(ii)
that
\alpha=0
.
So this establishes that

\widehat{I}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is a dense subspace of
X
,
and proves the theorem.
∎

5.2.

Finally,
Theorem
1.1
follows as a special case of
Theorem
5.1
. Indeed, we have

Proposition 5.2
.

The space
X=L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
p>1
,
satisfies conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
above.

This is proved in
[
OU18b
, Section 3]
as a consequence
of a more general result.
Below we give a short, self-contained proof of this fact.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Condition
(i)
follows from the inequality

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{p}\|f\|_{1,0}

which holds for every Schwartz function
f
,
where
C_{p}=(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|x|)^{-p}dx)^{1/p}

is a finite constant that depends
on
p
but does not depend on
f
.

To establish condition
(ii)
, we recall that
a temperate distribution
\alpha\in(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))^{*}

is an element of the space
L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
,
q=p/(p-1)
.
Assume that
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{\alpha})\subset\mathbb{Z}
,
and let
\chi
be a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}
with

\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{\chi})\subset[-\frac{2}{3},\frac{2}{3}]
and

\widehat{\chi}(t)=1
on
[-\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}]
.
The distribution
\widehat{\alpha}\cdot\widehat{\chi}
is then
supported at the origin and
coincides with
\widehat{\alpha}
in a neighborhood of the origin.
It is well known that a distribution supported at
the origin is a finite linear combination of derivatives
of Dirac’s measure at the origin
(see
[
Rud91
, Theorem 6.25]
). In turn this
implies that
\alpha\ast\chi
must be a polynomial.
But
\alpha\ast\chi\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
, so this is possible only if

\alpha\ast\chi=0
. We conclude that
\widehat{\alpha}

vanishes in a neighborhood of the origin.
By a similar argument,
\widehat{\alpha}
must
vanish also in a neighborhood of any integer other
than the origin.
Hence
\widehat{\alpha}
, and thus also
\alpha
, is zero,
which establishes the condition
(ii)
.
∎

We refer the reader to
[
OU18b
, Section 3]

where other examples of Banach function spaces on
\mathbb{R}

satisfying the conditions
(i)
and
(ii)

are given.
