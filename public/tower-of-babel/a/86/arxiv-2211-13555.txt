Error estimates for the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) scheme to the Cahn-Hilliard equation
Shu Ma, Weifeng Qiu, Xiaofeng Yang

Abstract
The optimal error estimate that depending only on the polynomial degree of ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} is established for the temporal semi-discrete scheme of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, which is based on the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) formulation. The key to our analysis is to convert the structure of the SAV time-stepping scheme back to a form compatible with the original format of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, which makes it feasible to use spectral estimates to handle the nonlinear term. Based on the transformation of the SAV numerical scheme, the optimal error estimate for the temporal semi-discrete scheme which depends only on the low polynomial order of ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} instead of the exponential order, is derived by using mathematical induction, spectral arguments, and the superconvergence properties of some nonlinear terms. Numerical examples are provided to illustrate the discrete energy decay property and validate our theoretical convergence analysis. Key words: Cahn-Hilliard equation, SAV formulation, energy decay, spectral estimates, polynomial order, error estimates.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider the initial boundary value problem for the Cahn-Hilliard (CH) phase field equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta\big(-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{n}}u

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{n}}\big(-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u)\big)=0

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{on}\,\,\,\partial{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

(1.1b)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0},

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega},

(1.1c)

where
{\varOmega}\subset R^{d}
,
d=2,3
is a bounded domain,
{\bm{n}}
is the
outward normal,
\varepsilon
is a small parameter, and
f
is the derivative of a non-negative potential function
F
with two local minima, i.e.,
f=F^{\prime}
. For instance, the Ginzburg-Landau energy function

F(v)=\frac{1}{4}(v^{2}-1)^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f(v)=v^{3}-v.

In view of its wide application as a phase field model
[
10
,
39
]
, many numerical methods and analyses have been developed for approximating the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
1.1
). On the one hand, most of these works have been performed for the Cahn-Hilliard equation with fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
As pointed out in
[
4
,
15
,
17
,
18
]
, error estimates using the direct Gronwall inequality argument yield a constant factor
e^{T/\varepsilon}
, and as a result the error grows exponentially as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Such an estimate is clearly not useful for very small
\varepsilon
, especially in solving the problem of whether the computed numerical interface converges to the original sharp interface of the Hele-Shaw problem when
\varepsilon\to 0
, see
[
14
,
28
]
for details.
To overcome this difficulty, Feng and Prohl
[
25
]
first established a priori error estimates with polynomial dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
for the time-discrete format of the Cahn-Hilliard equation.
Then, Feng and Wu obtained a posteriori error estimates with polynomial-order of
\varepsilon^{-1}
in

[
26
]
for the same time-discrete methods.
The main idea of the polynomial-order error estimates of
\varepsilon^{-1}
is to use the spectral estimates given by Alikakos and Fusco
[
3
]
and Chen
[
13
]
for the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator to handle the nonlinear term in the error analysis.
After this, spectral estimates were frequently used to eliminate the exponential dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
in the error analyses of other numerical methods for the Cahn-Hilliard equations (see
[
6
,
19
,
21
,
29
,
34
]
and references therein) and the related phase field equations, including the Allen-Cahn equations in
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
20
,
23
,
24
,
27
]
, the Ginzburg-Landau equations in
[
5
]
, and the phase field models with nonlinear constitutive laws in
[
16
]
.

On the other hand, it is wellknow that the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
1.1
) is the
H^{-1}
-gradient flow of the energy functional

\displaystyle E[u]:=\int_{{\varOmega}}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(u)\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

(1.2)

As a result, the solution of the Cahn-Hilliard equation has decaying energy. Indeed, testing (
1.1
)
by
-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u)
yields

\displaystyle E[u(\cdot,t_{2})]-E[u(\cdot,t_{1})]=-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq 0,

(1.3)

where
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
and
\|\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{-1}}=\|\nabla w\|
with
w=-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u)
.

Accordingly, great efforts have been devoted to the construction of efficient and accurate numerical methods that preserve the energy decay properties at the discrete level. In particular, for those widely used linear time-stepping methods with energy decay, including stabilized semi-implicit schemes
[
11
,
12
,
33
]
, invariant energy quadratization (IEQ) methods
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
, and the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) approach in
[
30
,
31
]
and
[
2
]
.
Optimal error estimates of these time-stepping schemes for the Cahn-Hilliard equations with fixed
\varepsilon^{-1}
are now well developed. As far as the error analysis is concerned, the main difficulty that remains with these methods is how to establish error bounds that depend only on the polynomial order of
\varepsilon^{-1}
rather than the exponential order for small
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This is due to the fact that compared to Feng’s previous work
[
19
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
29
]
, the numerical methods based on the IEQ/SAV formulation break the standard structure of the nonlinear term of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, which is crucial to the utilization of the spectral arguments. This makes the spectral estimates ineffective in estimating errors for the IEQ/SAV approach.
To the best of our knowledge, the optimal error estimates depending only on the polynomial order of
\varepsilon^{-1}
for the IEQ/SAV methods to the Cahn-Hilliard equation remains open.

The objective of this paper is to establish error bounds which depend on
\varepsilon^{-1}
only in low polynomial order for a semi-discrete methods based on the SAV formulation of the Cahn-Hilliard equation. The SAV reformulation of the Cahn-Hilliard equation was introduced in
[
31
,
32
]
as an enhanced version of the invariant energy quadratization (IEQ) approach
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
, for developing energy-decay methods at the discrete level. By reconstructing the system based on the SAV reformulation, we obtain a time semi-discrete scheme, which is linear and easy-to-implement.
The SAV formulation introduces new difficulties to the error analysis for the Cahn-Hilliard equation due to the presence of a new scalar
r
in the nonlinear part (see equation (
2.8b
)), which alters the structure of the original Cahn-Hilliard equation and makes the spectral argument not directly applicable.
To improve the current error analysis, Zhang and Yang
[
40
]
have recently made a breakthrough in the estimates of the IEQ method for the Allen-Cahn equation. They established optimal error bounds on the polynomial dependence of
\varepsilon^{-1}
for the IEQ-based numerical schemes.
Inspired by
[
40
]
, we rewrite the format of the SAV scheme using the new scalar variable into a form compatible with the original format of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, which makes it feasible to use spectral estimates in the error analysis.
However, this structural transformation will accordingly introduces a strong perturbation term that needs to be delicately controlled. Furthermore, unlike the Allen-Cahn equation which is a gradient flow in
L^{2}({\varOmega})
, the Cahn-Hilliard equation is a gradient flow in
H^{-1}({\varOmega})
, which makes the analysis for the Cahn-Hilliard equation in this paper more delicate and complicated than that for the Allen-Cahn equation. In our analysis, these difficulties are overcome by combining the following techniques:

(1)

To use the spectral estimates of the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator to deal with the nonlinear potential term in the error analysis, we reconvert the structure of the SAV scheme into a form compatible with the original Cahn-Hilliard equation (
1.1
).

(2)

An inductive argument is used to deal with the difficulties caused by the strong perturbation term that appear in the structural transformation (see equation (
4.50
)). In particular, error bounds of
\|\nabla e^{i-1}-\nabla e^{i-2}\|
and
\|e^{i-1}-e^{i-2}\|_{H^{-1}}
for
i\leq n
need to be established. By integrating them into the estimates of the perturbation terms, the super-convergence characteristics of some of their resulting nonlinear terms will complete the mathematical induction method.

(3)

Given that the solution of the Cahn-Hilliard problem (
1.1
) preserves the total mass property (i.e.
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{{\varOmega}}u(x,t)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0
), which is not possessed by the corresponding Allen-Cahn problem, we can establish its optimal error bounds in the
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{-1}({\varOmega}))
-norm with polynomial dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
, with the help of an error estimate in the
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\varOmega}))
-norm. This is different from the error estimates in the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\varOmega}))
-norm of the Allen-Cahn equation given in
[
40
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first error estimate of the polynomial dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
for the IEQ/SAV-type schemes of the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
1.1
).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we present the SAV reformulation of the Cahn-Hilliard equation and introduce an equivalent transformation of the SAV time-stepping scheme.
In Section
3
, we show the properties of energy decay and derive the consistency estimates for the proposed method.
In Section
4
, we present an error estimate of the semi-discrete SAV scheme to derive a convergence rate that does not depend on
\varepsilon^{-1}
exponentially. The spectrum estimate plays a crucial role in the proof. Finally, in Section
5
, we present a few numerical experiments to validate the theoretical results.

2
Formulation of the Semi-discrete SAV scheme

In this section, we construct a backward Euler implicit-explicit type temporal semi-discrete numerical scheme based on the SAV reformulation of the CH equation (
1.1

2.2
The SAV reformulation

The SAV formulation of the CH equation (cf.
[
30
,
31
]
) introduces a scalar auxiliary variable

\displaystyle r=\sqrt{\mbox{$\int_{\varOmega}$}F(u){\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad g(u)=\frac{f(u)}{\sqrt{\mbox{$\int_{\varOmega}$}F(u){\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}},

with a positive

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta w

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}rg(u)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}r}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u),\partial_{t}u\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

(2.8c)

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{n}}u

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{n}}w=0

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{on}\,\,\,\partial{\varOmega}\times(0,T],

(2.8d)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{in}\,\,\,{\varOmega},

\displaystyle r(0)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mbox{$\int_{\varOmega}$}F(u_{0}){\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}.

(2.8f)

We define an energy functional with respect to

and

\displaystyle E(u,r)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{2},

(2.9)

and taking the

) with

)
with

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}E(u,r)=-\|\nabla w\|^{2}=-\|\partial_{t}u\|_{-1}^{2}\leq 0.

2.3
The equivalent formulation of the SAV scheme

Let
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N+1}
be a uniform partition of
[0,T]
with the time step size
\tau
, where

is a positive integer and hence
\tau=\frac{T}{N+1}
.
We consider the following temporal semi-discrete SAV scheme for solving the system (

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}&=\Delta w^{n+1},\\
w^{n+1}&=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n+1}g(u^{n}),\\
r^{n+1}-r^{n}&=\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big),\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}u^{n+1}|_{\partial{\varOmega}}&=\partial_{\bm{n}}w^{n+1}|_{\partial{\varOmega}}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.11)

with
u^{0}=u_{0}
and
r^{0}=r(0)
for
n=0,1,\dots,N
.

By taking the
L^{2}
inner product of the first equation in (
2.11
) with
v=1
, we have
the following conservation property, which is important to the error estimates.

Lemma 2.1

The numerical solution of (
2.11
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\varOmega}|}\int_{{\varOmega}}u^{n}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{1}{|{\varOmega}|}\int_{{\varOmega}}u^{0}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad n=1,\dots,N,

(2.12)

and the error function
e^{n}:=u(t_{n})-u^{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{{\varOmega}}e^{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0,\qquad\qquad n=1,\dots,N.

(2.13)

Because of (
2.12
)-(
2.13
),
u^{n+1}-u^{n}
and

belong to
L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
such that we can define their

Also, the semi-discrete SAV scheme (
2.11
) can be written as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta^{-1}\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}&=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n+1}g(u^{n}),\\
r^{n+1}-r^{n}&=\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big),\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.14)

In order to avoid the exponentially dependence of the error bound on
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
induced by using the Gronwall inequality, we need to use a spectral estimate of the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator, which is given in
[
3
,
13
,
25
]
and will be described in Section

However, compared with the previous work
[
25
]
, the SAV method (
2.11
) alters the structure of the CH equation such that the spectral argument can not be applied directly. To achieve the ideal error bound, we need to transform the structure into a form compatible with the CH equation (
1.1
) so that the spectral estimate of the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator can be used.
To this end, we define
A(v)=\sqrt{\int_{{\varOmega}}F(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}
, the Gateaux derivatives of
A(v)
can be
defined as follows:

\displaystyle DA(v,w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{f(v)}{\sqrt{\int_{{\varOmega}}F(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}},w\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(g(v),w\big),

(2.15)

\displaystyle D^{2}A(v,w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{{\varOmega}}f^{\prime}(v)w^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x}{\sqrt{\int_{{\varOmega}}F(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\big(\int_{{\varOmega}}f(v)w\,{\mathrm{d}}x\big)^{2}}{\Big(\int_{{\varOmega}}F(v)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(2.16)

By Taylor expansion, we derive

\displaystyle A(u^{i})=A(u^{i-1})+\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u^{i-1}),u^{i}-u^{i-1}\big)+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1}),

(2.17)

where
\xi_{i}=\theta u^{i}+(1-\theta)u^{i-1}
with
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Thus we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u^{i-1}),u^{i}-u^{i-1}\big)=A(u^{i})-A(u^{i-1})-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1}),

which together with the second equation in (
2.14
) implies

\displaystyle r^{i}-r^{i-1}=A(u^{i})-A(u^{i-1})-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1}).

After summing up the above equation from
i=1
to
n
, we derive

\displaystyle r^{n}-r^{0}=A(u^{n})-A(u^{0})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1}).

Since
r^{0}=A(u^{0})
, we obtain

\displaystyle r^{n}=A(u^{n})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1}).

(2.18)

Then the SAV scheme (
2.14
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}=\,

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta u^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n}g(u^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(u^{n})(r^{n+1}-r^{n})

(2.19)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta u^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n}g(u^{n})+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{n})\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big),

which together with (
2.18
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}=\,

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta u^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u^{n})-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1})

(2.20)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{n})\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big).

The above equation (
2.20
) provides an equivalent formulation of the semi-discrete SAV scheme (
2.11
), which will be frequently used in the subsequent error analysis.

3
Energy decay and consistency analysis

In this section, we present several inequalities related to the proposed numerical method.

3.1
Assumption and regularity

Before presenting the detailed numerical analysis, we first make some assumptions. The quartic growth of the Ginzburg-Landau energy function
F(u)=\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-1)^{2}
at infinity poses various technical difficulties for the analysis and approximation of CH equations.
Although the CH equation does not satisfy the maximum principle, if the maximum norm of the initial condition
u^{0}
is bounded, it has been shown in
[
9
]
that the maximum norm of the solution of the CH equation for the truncation potential
F(u)
with quadratic growth rate at infinity is bounded. Therefore, it has been a common practice (cf.
[
33
]
) to consider the CH equations with a truncated
F(u)
.

Assumption 3.1

We assume that the potential function
F(u)
whose derivative
f(u)=F^{\prime}(u)
satisfies the following condition:

(i)

F\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R})
,
F(\pm 1)=0
, and
F>0
elsewhere.

(ii)

f(\pm 1)=0
,
f^{\prime}(\pm 1)>0
, and there exists a non-negative constant

such that

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}}|f(v)|\leq L,\quad\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{\prime}(v)|\leq L\quad\mbox{and}\quad\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{\prime\prime}(v)|\leq L.

(3.21)

In order to trace the dependence of the solution on the small parameter
\varepsilon>0
, we assume that the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies the following conditions:

Assumption 3.2

\varepsilon

for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\varOmega}|}\int_{{\varOmega}}u\,{\mathrm{d}}x=m_{0}\in(-1,1),

\displaystyle\esssup_{t\in[0,\infty]}\Big\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{{\varOmega}}F(u)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\Big\}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\|u_{t}\|_{-1}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\rho_{1}},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\|_{-1}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\rho_{3}}.

Remark 3.1

(3.23)

where

\displaystyle D^{2}A(v;w)\lesssim\big(\|f^{\prime}(v)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|f(v)\|^{2}\big)\|w\|^{2}\lesssim\|w\|^{2},

(3.25)

which will be frequently used to control the difficult term in the error analysis.

(c) Assumption
3.2
can be achieved in many cases. For example,
suppose that
f
satisfies Assumption
3.1
,
\partial{\varOmega}
is of
class
C^{2,1}
,
u^{0}\in H^{3}({\varOmega})
, and there exist positive
\varepsilon
-independent constants
\sigma_{j}
for
j=1,2,3
such that

\displaystyle E[u^{0}]=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla u^{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{{\varOmega}}F(u^{0})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2\sigma_{1}},

(3.26a)

\displaystyle\|-\varepsilon\Delta u^{0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u^{0})\|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2\sigma_{2}},

(3.26b)

\displaystyle\|-\varepsilon\Delta u^{0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u^{0})\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2\sigma_{3}}.

(3.26c)

Then the estimates (
3.22a
)–(
3.22d
) can be derived
by standard test function techniques and satisfy:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}=2\sigma_{1},\quad\rho_{2}=\max\{2\sigma_{1}+2,2\sigma_{2}-1\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\rho_{3}=\max\{2\sigma_{1}+4,2\sigma_{2}+1,2\sigma_{3}-1\}.

We refer to
[
22
,
25
]
for their detailed proof.

3.2
Energy decay structure

In this subsection, we prove the following energy decay property of the numerical solution, which comprise of the first theorem of this paper.

Theorem 3.1

(energy decay)
The scheme (
2.11
) is unconditionally energy stable in the sense that

\displaystyle E(u^{n+1},r^{n+1})-E(u^{n},r^{n})\leq-\frac{1}{\tau}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}\leq 0\quad\mbox{for }n\geq 1.

(3.27)

Proof.

Taking the inner product of the first equation in (
2.11
) with
-\Delta^{-1}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
, and of the second equation with
u^{n+1}-u^{n}
, and multiplying the third equation in (
2.11
) by
\frac{2}{\varepsilon}r^{n+1}
, we derive that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big(\|\nabla u^{n+1}\|^{2}-\|\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}+\|\nabla u^{n+1}-\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}\Big)

(3.28)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n+1}\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big)=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big((r^{n+1})^{2}-(r^{n})^{2}+(r^{n+1}-r^{n})^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{n+1}\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big).

(3.29)

Taking the summation of the above equations, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big(\|\nabla u^{n+1}\|^{2}-\|\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}+\|\nabla u^{n+1}-\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}\Big)

(3.30)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big((r^{n+1})^{2}-(r^{n})^{2}+(r^{n+1}-r^{n})^{2}\Big)=0.

which gives (
3.27
).

\square

Remark 3.2

After summing up (
3.30
) from
n=0
to
N
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla u^{N+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(r^{N+1})^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|\nabla u^{n+1}-\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}

(3.31)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(r^{n+1}-r^{n})^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla u^{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(r^{0})^{2}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\rho_{1}}.

It follows from
u^{n+1}-u^{n}\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
and (
2.6
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}\leq\,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}\|\nabla u^{n+1}-\nabla u^{n}\|

(3.32)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|\nabla u^{n+1}-\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-(\rho_{1}+\frac{1}{2})}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}.

3.3
Consistency

To derive the error estimates of the equivalent transformation (
2.20
) of the semi-discrete SAV scheme (
2.11
), we reformulate the CH equation (
1.1
) as the truncated form

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})}{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta u(t_{n+1})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u(t_{n}))+\mathcal{R}^{n+1},

(3.33)

where the truncation error
\mathcal{R}^{n+1}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{n+1}:=\Big[\Delta^{-1}\frac{u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})}{\tau}-\Delta^{-1}\partial_{t}u(t_{n+1})\Big]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[f(u(t_{n+1}))-f(u(t_{n}))\Big].

(3.34)

Lemma 3.1

(consistency estimate)
Suppose that assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold, then we have the following consistency estimate:

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|\mathcal{R}^{n+1}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{-\max\{\rho_{2}+2,\,\rho_{3}\}}\tau^{2}.

(3.35)

Proof.

For any
\varphi\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
, there holds
\varphi_{1}=-\Delta^{-1}\varphi\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})\cap H^{1}({\varOmega})
with
\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi_{1}=0
on
\partial{\varOmega}
and
\|\varphi\|_{-1}=\|\nabla\varphi_{1}\|
, which gives

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\,\in H^{1}({\varOmega})}\frac{(\varphi,v)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}}=\sup_{v\,\in H^{1}({\varOmega})}\frac{\big(-\Delta(-\Delta^{-1})\varphi,v\big)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}}=\sup_{v\,\in H^{1}({\varOmega})}\frac{\big(\nabla\varphi_{1},\nabla v\big)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}}

(3.36)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\varphi_{1}\|=\|\varphi\|_{-1}.

By performing standard calculations, it follows from
\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
that

\displaystyle\Big\|\Delta^{-1}\frac{u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})}{\tau}-\Delta^{-1}\partial_{t}u(t_{n+1})\Big\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}=\,

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(s-t_{n})\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\Big\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}

(3.37)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\tau\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\tau\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\|_{-1}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

and

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u(t_{n+1}))-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u(t_{n}))\Big\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}=\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\sup_{v\,\in H^{1}({\varOmega})}\frac{\big(f(u(t_{n+1}))-f(u(t_{n})),v\big)^{2}}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}^{2}}

(3.38)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\sup_{v\,\in H^{1}({\varOmega})}\frac{\|f^{\prime}(\xi^{n})\|_{L^{3}}^{2}\|u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\|^{2}\|v\|_{L^{6}}^{2}}{\|v\|_{H^{1}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\|u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\tau\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|u_{t}\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

where
\xi^{n}
is between
u(t_{n})
and
u(t_{n+1})
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|\mathcal{R}^{n+1}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\Delta^{-1}u_{tt}\|_{-1}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{t}\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s

(3.39)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\rho_{3}}\tau^{2}+\varepsilon^{-(\rho_{2}+2)}\tau^{2}.

The proof is completed.

\square

4
Error estimates

In this section, we will derive the error bound of the semi-discrete scheme (
2.11
), in which the focus is to obtain the polynomial type dependence of the error bound on
\varepsilon^{-1}
.
If we use the usual error estimate of the SAV numerical scheme (
2.11
), the error growth depends on
\varepsilon^{-1}
exponentially. To avoid the exponential dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
induced by using the Gronwall inequality, we need to use a spectral estimate of the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator, which is given in
[
3
,
13
,
25
]
.

Lemma 4.1

(spectral estimate)
Suppose that Assumption
3.1
holds. Then there exist
0<\varepsilon_{0}<<1
and a positive constant
\lambda_{0}
such that
the principle eigenvalue of the linearized Cahn-Hilliard operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{CH}:=\Delta(\varepsilon\Delta-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}(u)I)

satisfies for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\lambda_{CH}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\neq v\in H^{1}({\varOmega})\\
\Delta w=v\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon\|\nabla v\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(f^{\prime}(u(\cdot,t)v,v)\big)}{\|\nabla w\|^{2}}\geq-\lambda_{0},

(4.40)

for
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, where
I
denotes the identity operator and
u
is the solution of the Cahn-Hilliard problem (
1.1
).

We will now prove the following error estimates for the semi-discrete numerical scheme, which is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 4.1

(error estimate)
We assume that assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
hold and that

\tau\leq\tilde{C}\varepsilon^{\beta_{0}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\beta_{0}=\frac{4\alpha_{0}+32+4d}{4-d},

(4.41)

then the discrete solution given by (
2.11
) satisfies the following error estimate for
e^{n}=u(t_{n})-u^{n}
:

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq n\leq m}\|e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\|e^{n}-e^{n-1}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{4}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\tau\|\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+\max_{1\leq n\leq m}\tau\varepsilon^{4}\|\nabla e^{m}\|^{2}

(4.42)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{4}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\tau\|\nabla e^{n}-\nabla e^{n-1}\|^{2}\leq\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{0}}\tau^{2}.

where
\alpha_{0}:=\max\{\rho_{1}+3,\,2\rho_{2}+4,\,\rho_{2}+6,\,\rho_{3}+4\}
, and the constants
\tilde{C}
and
\kappa_{0}
are given in the proof.

Proof.

We use the mathematical induction as follows. The proof is split into four steps. The first step gives the error estimate for the first step
t=t^{1}
. Steps two and three use the spectral estimate (
4.40
) to avoid exponential blow-up in
\varepsilon^{-1}
of the error constants. In the last step, an inductive argument is used to conclude the proof.

Step 1: Estimation of
\|e^{1}\|_{-1}^{2}+\tau\varepsilon\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}
.
For
n=0
in (
2.14
), we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta^{-1}\frac{u^{1}-u^{0}}{\tau}&=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}r^{1}g(u^{0})\\
r^{1}&=r^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\big(g(u^{0}),u^{1}-u^{0}\big)\end{aligned}\right.

(4.43)

After plugging the second equation into the first equation, we get

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{u^{1}-u^{0}}{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(u^{0})+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{0})\big(g(u^{0}),u^{1}-u^{0}\big).

(4.44)

Subtracting (
4.44
) from (
3.33
), we get the corresponding error equation

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{e^{1}}{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta e^{1}+\mathcal{R}^{1}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{0})\big(g(u^{0}),u^{1}-u^{0}\big).

(4.45)

Taking the
L^{2}
inner product of the above equation with
e^{1}\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
, we have

\displaystyle\|e^{1}\|_{-1}^{2}+\tau\varepsilon\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}=-\tau(\mathcal{R}^{1},e^{1})+\frac{\tau}{2\varepsilon}\big(g(u^{0}),u^{1}-u^{0}\big)\big(g(u^{0}),e^{1}\big).

(4.46)

Using Poincaré’s inequality for
e^{1}\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
and Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{R}^{1},e^{1})\leq\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{4}\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-1}\tau\|\mathcal{R}^{1}\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{4}\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-\max\{\rho_{2}+3,\,\rho_{3}+1\}}\tau^{2}.

(4.47)

From (
3.32
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2\varepsilon}\big(g(u^{0}),u^{1}-u^{0}\big)\big(g(u^{0}),e^{1}\big)\leq\,

\displaystyle C\tau\varepsilon^{-1}\|g(u^{0})\|^{2}\|u^{1}-u^{0}\|\|e^{1}\|

(4.48)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\tau\varepsilon^{-1}\|u^{1}-u^{0}\|\|e^{1}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e^{1}\|_{-1}\|\nabla e^{1}\|+C\tau^{\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{5}{2}}\|u^{1}-u^{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|e^{1}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{4}\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-(\rho_{1}+3)}\tau^{2}

Thus, there exists a positive constant
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\|e^{1}\|_{-1}^{2}+\tau\varepsilon\|\nabla e^{1}\|^{2}\leq\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\varepsilon^{-\max\{\rho_{1}+3,\,\rho_{2}+3,\,\rho_{3}+1\}}\tau^{2}.

(4.49)

We use the mathematical induction as follows.
For
m=1
, (
4.42
) holds from (
4.49
).
We suppose (
4.42
) holds for
m=1,2,\dots,N
, and show that (
4.42
) is also valid for
m=N+1
.

Subtracting (
2.20
) from (
3.33
), by denoting
e^{n}=u(t_{n})-u^{n}
, we get the error equation

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}\frac{e^{n+1}-e^{n}}{\tau}=\,

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta e^{n+1}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[f(u(t_{n}))-f(u^{n})\Big]

(4.50)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}g(u^{n})\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big)+\mathcal{R}^{n+1}.

Step 2: Estimation of
\displaystyle\|\nabla e^{N+1}\|^{2}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}
.

By taking the
L^{2}
inner product of (
4.50
) with
e^{n+1}-e^{n}\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|^{2}-\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\Big\|\frac{e^{n+1}-e^{n}}{\tau}\Big\|_{-1}^{2}

(4.51)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(f(u(t_{n}))-f(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(\mathcal{R}^{n+1},e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)

\displaystyle=:\,

\displaystyle K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}+K_{4}.

It follows from (
2.6
) that

\displaystyle K_{1}=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(f(u(t_{n}))-f(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\Big)

(4.52)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\|f^{\prime}(w^{n})\|_{L^{\infty}}\|e^{n}\|\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}\|\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n}\|\|e^{n}\|_{-1}+\gamma_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\tau\|\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-4}\|e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\tau^{-1}\varepsilon^{-4}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}

where
w_{n}=\theta u^{n}+(1-\theta)u(t_{n})
with
\theta\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma_{0}
is a sufficiently small constant such that the two terms involving
\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}
and
\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}
can be absorbed by the left hand side in the Step 4.

By using
D^{2}A(v;w)\lesssim\big(\|f^{\prime}(v)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|f(v)\|^{2}\big)\|w\|^{2}
, the term
K_{2}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle K_{2}=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}A(\xi_{i}\,;u^{i}-u^{i-1})

(4.53)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big|\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|u^{i}-u^{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\big|\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e^{i}-e^{i-1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\big|\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|u(t_{i})-u(t_{i-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=:\,

\displaystyle K_{21}+K_{22}.

From the assumptions of induction, we have

\displaystyle\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\int_{{\varOmega}}F(u^{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}x+c_{0}}}\big(f(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)

(4.54)

\displaystyle\leq C\|f(u^{n})\|\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e^{i}-e^{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e^{i}-e^{i-1}\|_{-1}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla e^{i}-\nabla e^{i-1}\|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.55)

\displaystyle\lesssim\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{-(\alpha_{0}+2)}\tau^{\frac{3}{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|u(t_{i})-u(t_{i-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\|u_{t}\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\lesssim\tau\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\|u_{t}\|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s

(4.56)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\rho_{2}}\tau.

Using the estimates above,
we have

\displaystyle K_{21}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{-(\alpha_{0}+2)}\tau^{\frac{3}{2}}

(4.57)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}+C\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\kappa_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{-(2\alpha_{0}+6)}\tau^{\frac{7}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\tau^{-1}\varepsilon^{-4}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+C\kappa_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{-(2\alpha_{0}+6)}\tau^{\frac{7}{2}}.

and

\displaystyle K_{22}\leq\,

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\rho_{2}}\tau

(4.58)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}+C\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\varepsilon^{-(2\rho_{2}+2)}\tau^{\frac{5}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\tau^{-1}\varepsilon^{-4}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-(2\rho_{2}+2)}\tau^{\frac{5}{2}}.

In addition, we divided the term
K_{3}
into two parts as

\displaystyle K_{3}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big(g(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\big)

(4.59)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big(g(u^{n}),u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=:\,

\displaystyle K_{31}+K_{32},

where

\displaystyle K_{31}=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)

(4.60)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}\|g(u^{n})\|^{2}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\varepsilon^{-4}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{2},

and

\displaystyle K_{32}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\big(g(u^{n}),e^{n+1}-e^{n}\big)\big(g(u^{n}),u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\big)

(4.61)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{-2}\|e^{n+1}-e^{n}\|_{-1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}-\nabla e^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Phi_{+}^{(i)}(M)=F^{(i)}(M)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{-}^{(i)}(-M)=F^{(i)}(-M)\qquad\mbox{for}\,\,\,i=0,1,2,3,4,\\
&\Phi_{+}(2M)=\Phi_{-}(-2M)=\frac{1}{4}((2M)^{2}-1)^{2},\\
&\Phi_{+}^{(1)}(2M)=\Phi_{-}^{(1)}(-2M)=(2M)^{3}-2M,\\
&\Phi_{+}^{(i)}(2M)=\Phi_{-}^{(i)}(-2M)=0\qquad\mbox{for}\,\,\,i=2,3,4.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.88)

Note that the truncation point
M=2
used here are for convenience only.
For simplicity, we still denote the modified function
\hat{F}(u)
by
F(u)
.

The spatial discretization is done by using the Galerkin finite element method. Let
S_{h}
denotes the
P_{s}
conforming finite element space defined by

\displaystyle S_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in C(\bar{\varOmega});\,\,v_{h}|_{K}\in P_{s}(K),\,\,\,\forall\,K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

where
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a quasi-uniform triangulation of
{\varOmega}
.
We introduce space notation
S_{h}^{\circ}:=\{v_{h}\in S_{h};\,\,(v_{h},1)=0\}
, and define the discrete inverse Laplace operator
-\Delta_{h}^{-1}:L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})\to S_{h}^{\circ}
such that

\displaystyle\big(\nabla(-\Delta_{h}^{-1})v,\nabla\eta_{h}\big)=(v,\eta_{h})\qquad\forall\,\,\,\eta_{h}\in S_{h}.

(5.89)

Since the exact solution of the considered problem is not known, we compute the orders of convergence by the formula

\mbox{order of convergence}=\log\Bigg(\frac{\|u_{N}^{(\tau)}-u_{N}^{(\tau/2)}\|_{h,-1}}{\|u_{N}^{(\tau/2)}-u_{N}^{(\tau/4)}\|_{h,-1}}\Bigg)/\log(2)

based on the finest three meshes, where
u_{N}^{(\tau)}
denotes the numerical solution at
t_{N}=T
computed by using a stepsize
\tau
, and

\|v\|_{h,-1}:=\sqrt{(v,-\Delta_{h}^{-1}v)}
for
v\in L_{0}^{2}({\varOmega})
.
