Light bulb smoothing for topological surfaces in 4-manifolds
Jae Choon Cha, Byeorhi Kim

Abstract
. We present new smoothing techniques for topologically embedded surfaces in smooth 4-manifolds, which give topological isotopy to a smooth surface. As applications, we prove “topological = smooth” results in dimension 4 for certain disks and spheres modulo isotopy. A key step in our approach is to link Quinn’s smoothing theory with ideas in Gabai’s 4-dimensional light bulb theorem and succeeding developments of Schneiderman-Teichner and Kosanović-Teichner. As another application of our smoothing technique, we obtain a topological version of the Dax invariant which gives topological isotopy obstructions for topological disks in 4-manifolds.



1.
Introduction and main results

A major challenge in 4-dimensional topology is to understand how the topological and smooth categories are similar and different.
There have been enormous advances since the work of Freedman and Donaldson, and especially it has been a very successful direction to find smoothly distinct but topologically equivalent objects.
Recall that the special sophistication of dimension 4 is from the difficulty of finding embedded disks, most importantly for Whitney moves.
Known differences between the topological and smooth categories can be understood as a failure of smoothing topological disks in 4-manifolds.
Topological disks from Freedman’s work and subsequent developments eventually lead us to fundamental topological results such as the classification of simply connected 4-manifolds, while modern smooth techniques detect a surprisingly large extent of the failure of smoothing for many of those topological disks.

On the other hand, it is much less understood when the smooth and topological cases behave in the same way in dimension 4.
From the viewpoint of disks, the question is: when are topologically embedded disks smoothable?
Results in this direction could potentially bring us closer to affirmative answers to major open questions about the equivalence of topological and smooth categories in specific cases of interest, such as the smooth 4-dimensional Poincaré conjecture and (non-)existence questions for exotic smooth structures on other small 4-manifolds.

In this paper, we develop new smoothing techniques for topologically embedded disks and more generally surfaces of arbitrary genus in smooth 4-manifolds.
We focus on smoothing by topological isotopy.
That is, we study when a topologically embedded surface in a smooth 4-manifold is topologically isotopic to a smooth embedding.
Our main smoothing results are Theorems
C
and
D
below in this introduction.

As an application, we prove a “topological = smooth” result for the isotopy classification of certain disks.
Throughout this paper, surfaces in a 4-manifold are properly embedded, and their isotopy is rel
\partial
, i.e., the boundary is fixed pointwise.
Topological embeddings and isotopy are locally flat.

The following additional conventions apply throughout this paper.

1.1.
Light bulb smoothing for surfaces

A key idea in our approach is to link Quinn’s smoothing theory for 4-manifolds
[
Qui82
,
Qui84
,
FQ90
]
, which is built on Freedman’s work
[
Fre82
,
Fre84
,
FQ90
]
, with ideas in Gabai’s recent work on the 4-dimensional light bulb theorem
[
Gab20
]
.
Specifically, it leads us to the following main result.

Theorem C

(Light bulb smoothing for surfaces)
.

If a topological surface
R
(with possibly empty boundary) in a smooth 4-manifold
M
has a smooth geometric dual
G
, then
R
is topologically isotopic to a smooth surface in
M
, via a topological isotopy supported in an arbitrary neighborhood of
R\cup G
.

As in Corollary
A
, the geometric dual condition in this statement can be reformulated to an algebraic condition: the following is equivalent to the above statement.

An alternative form of Theorem
C
.

Let
R
be a topological surface in a smooth 4-manifold
M
.
If a boundary component
K
of
R
has a meridian null-homotopic in
\partial M\smallsetminus K
, then
R
is topologically isotopic to a smooth surface in
M
, via a topological isotopy supported in an arbitrary neighborhood of
R\cup\partial M
.

Note that the surjectivity in Corollaries
A
and
B
follows immediately from Theorem
C
.
We also use Theorem
C
to develop ingredients of the proof of the injectivity.

1.2.
Smoothing surfaces in a stabilization

As another application of Theorem
C
, we obtain a smoothing result for arbitrary topological surfaces without the need for a dual, at the cost of
(S^{2}\times S^{2})
-stabilizations of the ambient 4-manifold.

Theorem D

(Stable smoothing for surfaces)
.

A topological surface
R
in a smooth 4-manifold
M
is topologically isotopic to a smooth embedding in
M\#k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
for some
k
.

Here, the connected sum with
k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
is understood as taken away from
R
so that
R
can be regarded as a topological surface in
M\#k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
.

Theorem
D
also holds when we allow
R
to have multi-components.
See Section
3
.

The proof of Theorem
D
relies on the Light Bulb Smoothing Theorem
C
and a controlled version of Quinn’s smoothing theory.
Briefly speaking, Quinn showed that a topological surface can be changed to a smooth immersion by controlled topological finger moves, i.e., finger moves with arbitrarily small diameter.
We show that when the diameter is small enough, the topological Whitney disks introduced by the finger moves are
isotopic
to smooth Whitney disk after stabilization, using the Light Bulb Smoothing Theorem
C
.
This enables us to smoothly undo the topological finger moves.
Details are in Section
3
.

Stabilization of the ambient 4-manifold in Theorem
D
is necessary since there exist non-smoothable topological surfaces in smooth 4-manifolds.
For example, it is well known that there are knots
K
in
S^{3}
which are topologically slice but not smoothly slice.
That is,
K
bounds a topological disk in
D^{4}
but bounds no smooth disk in
D^{4}
.
By Schneiderman
[
Sch10
]
, if a knot
K
has vanishing Arf invariant,
K
is smoothly slice in
D^{4}\#k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
for some
k
.
In particular, when
K
bounds a topological slicing disk in
D^{4}
,
K
bounds a smooth slicing disk in
D^{4}\#k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
, but this is not isotopic to a given topological slicing disk in general.
Theorem
D
readily gives the following:

Corollary E
.

Let
K
be a topologically slice knot in
S^{3}
.
Then any topological slicing disk for
K
in
D^{4}
is topologically isotopic to a smooth slicing disk in
D^{4}\#k(S^{2}\times S^{2})
for some
k
.

Motivated by Theorem
D
and Corollary
E
, it appears to be intriguing to study the minimal number
k
of ambient 4-manifold stabilization required to smoothen a given topological disk or more generally a surface.

1.3.
Topological applications of surface smoothing

For topological manifolds, the seminal work of Kirby and Siemenmann
[
KS77
]
establishes fundamental tools such as transversality and handle decomposition in high dimensions by developing a smoothing theory that allows us to import theorems for smooth manifolds to the topological case.
Dimension 4 is more sophisticated, but based on Freedman’s work and subsequent developments, the same approach shows that fundamental theorems work, to a large extent, for topological 4-manifolds
[
FQ90
]
.

Our smoothing technique enables us to employ a similar approach for topological surfaces in 4-manifolds: Theorems
C
and
D
isotope topological surfaces to smooth surfaces, to which we can apply smooth techniques, to import smooth results into topological cases.
It leads us to several applications.
We discuss some of them below.

Topological Dax invariant

Recently, an invariant of Dax in
[
Dax72
]
has received significant attention, owing to work of Gabai
[
Gab21
]
, Kosanović-Teichner
[
KT21
,
KT22
]
and Schwartz
[
Sch21
]
.
They used the Dax invariant of smooth disks to study 4-dimensional light bulb theorems and generalizations.
While the Dax invariant in these earlier works requires smoothness, our smoothing technique provides a convenient method to generalize it to topological disks.
This topological version of the Dax invariant appears to be interesting on its own, and it is also an ingredient of the proof of Corollary
A
.

Theorem F
.

Let
M
be a topological 4-manifold and
K
be a circle embedded in
\partial M
.
Fix an arbitrary almost smooth structure of
M
.
Then there is a function

\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}\colon\Big\{(D_{0},D_{1})\,\Big|\,\begin{tabular}[]{@{}c@{}}$D_{0}$, $D_{1}$ are topological disks in $M$\\
bounded by $K$, homotopic rel $\partial$\end{tabular}\Big\}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}M\smallsetminus 1]^{\sigma}/d(\pi_{3}(M))

satisfying the following.

(1)

and

.
If

and

are topologically isotopic,
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})=0

(2)

Here, an almost smooth structure of

It is known that every topological 4-manifold admits an almost smooth structure
[

When we say that a disk is smooth with respect to an almost smooth structure, we implicitly assume that the disk does not contain the singular point

Briefly speaking, we define

is smoothable in

As another application of Theorem

, we prove that there exist topologically knotted smooth 3-balls in a 4-manifold.

2.
Light bulb smoothing: proof of Theorem
C

Let
R
be a topological surface in a smooth 4-manifold
M
, which has a smooth geometric dual
G
.
Our goal is to show that
R
is topologically isotopic to a smooth surface in
M
, by a topological isotopy supported in a neighborhood of
R\cup G
.

By smooth approximation,
R
is always homotopic rel
\partial
to a smooth immersion.
But, without assuming the existence of a geometric dual,
R
is not topologically isotopic to a smooth embedding in general.
The following deep result of Quinn
[
FQ90
,
Qui82
,
Qui84
]
shows how far one can proceed toward a smooth embedding in general.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
FQ90
, 8.1A]
)
.

Let
V
and
M
be smooth 4-manifolds and
h\colon V\hookrightarrow M
be a topological embedding that is smooth on the boundary.
Let
R\subset V
be a smooth surface.
Then
R
can be changed to a smoothly immersed surface
R^{\prime}\subset V
by smooth finger moves and smooth isotopy in
V
, so that there is a topological isotopy
\{h_{t}\colon V\hookrightarrow M\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
of
h=h_{0}
such that
h_{t}(V)=h(V)
for all
t
and
h_{1}\colon V\hookrightarrow M
restricts to a smooth embedding on a neighborhood of
R^{\prime}
.

In our case, there exists a topological normal bundle
V
for the given topological surface
R
in
M
by
[
FQ90
, 9.3]
.
It means that
V
is a neighborhood of
R
, which has a 2-dimensional vector bundle structure over
R
such that
R\subset V
is identified with the zero section.
(A topological normal bundle is also required to be extendable
[
FQ80
, 9.3, p. 137]
, but we do not explicitly use it.)
The topological normal bundle
V
will always be equipped with the smooth structure induced by the vector bundle structure and the (unique) smooth structure of
R
.
Note that the inclusion
h\colon V\hookrightarrow M
is a topological embedding but not necessarily smooth.
When
\partial R
is nonempty,
\partial V
is a normal bundle of the 1-manifold
\partial R
in the 3-manifold
\partial M
, which can always be assumed to be smooth.

Quinn’s Theorem
2.1
applies directly to our inclusion
h
and
R\subset V
, to give a smoothly immersed surface
R^{\prime}\subset V
and a topological isotopy
\{h_{t}\}
of
h=h_{0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The smooth finger moves in Theorem
2.1
introduces smooth Whitney disks in
V
which pair up double points of
R^{\prime}
.
Applying the isotoped embedding
h_{1}
, topological finger moves in
M
changes
h_{1}(R)
to
h_{1}(R^{\prime})
and introduces topological Whitney disks in
M
which pair up double points of
h_{1}(R^{\prime})
.
Although
h_{1}(R^{\prime})
is smooth in
M
, the Whitney disks are not smooth in
M
.
Furthermore, in general, the Whitney disks are not smoothable in
M
, i.e., not topologically isotopic to a smooth disk.
If they were smoothable, we could use Whitney moves to undo the topological finger moves smoothly in
M
, so that we could change the smoothly immersed surface
h_{1}(R^{\prime})
to a smoothing of
R
in
M
.
It would contradict the existence of non-smoothable surfaces in smooth 4-manifolds.

In our case, the existence of a geometric dual
G
for
R
is essential in avoiding the issue discussed in Remark
2.2
.
We will use
G
together with the topological Whitney disks to construct a topological isotopy of
R
in
M
.
(Another approach will be developed in Section
3
.)

For this purpose, we first need to arrange the isotopy
\{h_{t}\}
slightly more carefully.
Specifically, in addition to the properties of
R^{\prime}
and
\{h_{t}\}
in Quinn’s Theorem
2.1
, we can also assume the following.
Let
\nu(G)\subset M
be a normal bundle of

.

Claim
.

We may assume that the isotopy
\{h_{t}\}
is satisfies
h_{t}=h
on
\nu(G)\cap V

.

Briefly, the claim holds because
R\subset M
is already smooth near

so that

does not have to be modified or isotoped near

.
A proof can be given by applying Theorem
2.1
in a modified way.
For concreteness, we provide technical details below.

Proof of Claim.

Write

Now

Let

The only if direction is obvious.

3.1.
Double point loops of an approximation of an embedding

We begin by recalling the definition of a double point loop.
For an arbitrary map
g\colon K\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}V
, a
double point
means a point
p\in g(K)
such that
p=g(x)=g(y)
for some
x
,
y\in K
,
x\neq y
.
If
\gamma
is a path from
x
to
y
in
K
, the loop
g(\gamma)
on

is called a
double point loop
for
p
.
In this paper,
K
and
V
will always be manifolds, but the map
g
does not have to be generic to define a double point.

The following simple principle turns out to be very useful for our purpose:
“an approximation close to an embedding has null-homotopic double point loops.”
This means that if a map
g
approximates an embedding
f
sufficiently closely, then double points of
g
have double point loops which are sufficiently short, and thus null-homotopic in a manifold.
The lemma below is a formal statement that we will use.
It is slightly more general than what we need in this section, for later use in this paper.
For brevity, for two maps
f
,
g\colon K\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}V
to a metric space
(V,d)
, we say that
g
is
\epsilon
-close
to
f
if
d(g(x),f(x))<\epsilon
for all
x\in K
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
K
be a compact connected smooth manifold and
V
be a topological manifold equipped with a metric
d
.
The manifolds
K
and
V
are allowed to have nonempty boundary.
Let
f\colon K\hookrightarrow V
be a topological embedding.
Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that if a map
g\colon K\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}V
is
\epsilon
-closed to
f
, every double point of
g
has a double point loop which is null-homotopic in
V
.

Remark 3.3
.

In addition, if
K
is simply connected and
g
is an immersion, then it follows that
the image

is
\pi_{1}
-null in
M
, i.e.,
\pi_{1}(g(K))\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\pi_{1}(M)
is trivial for any choice of a basepoint.

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is elementary.
For the reader’s convenience, we spell out details below.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Fix a metric
\rho\colon K\times K\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}[0,\infty)
induced by a Riemannian metric on

In

and

, denote the metric ball with center

Use the compactness of

to choose constants

and

such that

(i)

is contained in an contractible open set in

(ii)

there is a short geodesic between

and
y
in

\rho(x,y)<\delta
.

In addition, since

is continuous, we may assume that

(3)

if
\rho(x,y)<\delta
.

Use the continuity of

to choose

\rho(x,y)<\delta

Let

Suppose

If

There is a short geodesic

For any

That is,

By

.
∎

Remark 4.7

(Dax for disks with geometric dual)
.

Suppose that two topological disks
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
are homotopic rel
\partial
in a smooth 4-manifold
M
and suppose that each
D_{i}
has a smooth geometric dual in
M
.
Especially, it is the case if a meridian of the common boundary
K=\partial D_{0}=\partial D_{1}
is trivial in
\pi_{1}(\partial M\smallsetminus K)
, by Lemma
2.7
.
Then, we do not need to stabilize
M
to define
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})
, since
D_{i}
has a smoothing
D_{i}^{\prime}
in
M
by the Light Bulb Smoothing Theorem
C
.

For later use, we state basic properties of
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}
below.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
D_{0}
,
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are topological disks in a smooth 4-manifold
M
which are mutually homotopic rel
\partial
.
Then the following hold.

(1)

\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{2})=\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})+\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{1},D_{2})
.

(2)

\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})=-\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{1},D_{0})

(3)

If the disks
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
are smooth,
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})=\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{sm}}(D_{0},D_{1})
.

Proof.

(1)
and
(2)
follow readily from the additivity and inversion formulas for
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{sm}}
in Section
4.1
.

(3)
is obvious from Definition
4.5
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

If two topological disks
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
in a smooth 4-manifold
M
are topologically isotopic, then
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})=0
.
More generally,
\operatorname{Dax}^{\mathrm{top}}(D_{0},D_{1})

and

Let
h_{i}
be a topological isotopy from

to a smoothing

in

as in Definition
4.5
.

and

are topologically isotopic in

to
D_{1}^{\prime}

.

\operatorname{Dax}^{\smash{\mathrm{top}}}(D_{0},D_{1})=\operatorname{Dax}(H^{\prime})=0

4.4
.
From this and Lemma

below, the first sentence of Theorem

The second sentence follows from Theorem
4.8

(1)
and the first sentence.
∎

More applications in the topological category

In this section, we discuss more applications in the topological category, which generalize previously known smooth category results in the literature.

The following theorem states a topological category version of Gabai’s smooth 4D light bulb theorem

Theorem 7.1

(Gabai’s light bulb theorem in the topological category)

If

Let

\pi_{0}(\operatorname{Homeo}_{0}(S^{2}\times D^{2}))=0

Remark 7.3

Remark 7.7

It seems hard, if possible at all, to develop the Dax invariant for disks in topological 4-manifolds by an alternative approach similar to Remark

Appendix Appendix
High-dimensional smoothing, immersion and topological Freedman-Quinn invariant

In the smooth category, Schneiderman and Teichner provided a detailed treatment of the Freedman-Quinn invariant
[
FQ90
,
Sto93
]
, including its definition and basic properties in
[
ST22
, Section 4]
.
See also
[
KM21
,
KM22
]
.
The definition of the invariant is based on counting self-intersections of maps of

S^{2}\times I
and
S^{3}
into certain 6-manifolds, after perturbing involved maps to self-transverse maps.
The proof of the well-definedness depends on a similar perturbation to generic maps.
These arguments rely on standard results of transversality and singularity theory for smooth maps.

The main goal of this appendix is to provide a concrete treatment in the topological category.
It appears to be known to experts that the Freedman-Quinn invariant can also be defined in the topological category, but no details were given in the literature.
To fill in this omission, we describe technical details, especially how one can perturb involved maps to generic maps in the topological category, using topological immersion theory of Lees
[
Lee69
]
and high dimensional smoothing theory of Kirby and Siebenmann
[
KS77
]
.

In this appendix, manifolds are topological unless stated otherwise.

A.1.
Generic maps and Freedman-Quinn invariant

We begin with the notion of a generic immersion.
Let
P
be a 6-manifold and
Y
be either
S^{3}
or
S^{2}\times I
.
We say that a map
f\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
is a
generic immersion
if
f
embeds
\partial Y
into
\partial P
and
f
is a local embedding which fails to be one-to-one only at finitely many transverse double points.
When
P
is smooth, a
smooth generic immersion

Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
is a local smooth embedding which is a topological generic immersion.
(The 3-manifold
Y
is equipped with its unique smooth structure.)
It is known that if a smooth map
f\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
is a smooth embedding on
\partial Y
, then
f
is homotopic rel
\partial Y
to a smooth generic immersion (e.g. see
[
Hae61
, Theorem 2.5]
).
The following is a topological analog for our case.

Lemma A.1
.

A map
f\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
that embeds
\partial Y
into
\partial P
is homotopic rel
\partial Y
to a generic immersion
g\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
.
In fact, there is a smooth structure on a neighborhood
W
of
g(Y)
in
P
such that
g\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}W
is a smooth generic immersion.
If
f
is based, the homotopy can be assumed to be based.

Raising the dimensions by one, consider a map
F\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Q
, where
Q
is a 7-manifold and
X=S^{3}\times I
or
S^{2}\times I^{2}
(i.e.
X=Y\times I
where
Y
is as above).
Similarly to the above, if
Q
is smooth and
F\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Q
is a smooth map that restricts to a smooth generic immersion
\partial X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\partial Q
, then
F
is homotopic rel
\partial X
to a smooth generic map (e.g. see
[
Hae61
, Theorem 2.5]
).
Here,
F\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
is a
smooth generic map
if
F
is a smooth immersion except at a finite set
S\subset\operatorname{int}(X)
of cusp singularities,
F
has no triple points, double points of
F
are not in
F(S)
, and
F
is self-transverse at each double point.
Lemma
A.2
below is a topological analog.
In the topological category, we say that
F\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Q
is a
generic map
if
F
satisfies the local properties of a smooth generic map.

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
F\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Q
is a map such that
F|_{\partial X}\colon\partial X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\partial Q
is a generic immersion.
Then
F
is homotopic rel
\partial X
to a generic map
G\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}Q
.
In fact, there is a smooth structure on a neighborhood
W
of
G(X)
in
Q
such that
G\colon X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}W
is a smooth generic immersion.

The proofs of Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
are given in Section
A.2
of this appendix.

Once we have Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
, we can carry out the smooth category development of the Freedman-Quinn invariant
[
ST22
, Section 4]
, without essential changes, in the topological category.
For the reader’s convenience, we outline it below.

Let
Y=S^{3}
or
S^{2}\times I
and
P
be a topological 6-manifold, as above.
Fix basepoints, and let
f\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
be a based map that embeds
\partial Y
into
\partial P
.
By Lemma
A.1
,
f
is based homotopic rel
\partial Y
to a based generic immersion
f^{\prime}\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
.
For a double point
p
of
f^{\prime}
, the sign
\epsilon(p)\in\{1,-1\}
and the
\pi_{1}
element
g(p)\in\pi=\pi_{1}(P)
is defined as usual.
Switching the involved sheets changes
\epsilon(p)g(p)
to
-\epsilon(p)g(p)^{-1}
, so the self-intersection invariant is defined as follows:

\mu(f)=\sum_{p}\epsilon(p)g(p)\in\mathbb{Z}[\pi]/\langle 1,g+g^{-1}\rangle.

In the smooth category, it is known that
\mu(f)
is invariant under based homotopy of
f
rel
\partial Y
.
More generally,
\mu(f_{0})=\mu(f_{1})
if
f_{0}
,
f_{1}\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
are smooth generic immersions into a smooth 6-manifold
P
and if there is a map
F\colon Y\times I\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P\times I
such that
F|_{Y\times i}=f_{i}\times i
(
i=0,1
) and
F|_{\partial Y\times I}=f_{0}|_{\partial Y}\times\mathrm{id}
.
A sketch of a proof is as follows.
We may assume that
F
is a smooth generic map.
Then double points of
F
form a 1-manifold bounded by double points of
F|_{\partial(Y\times I)}
and cusp singular points of
F
, so these points are paired up by arc components.
If an arc component
\beta
joins two double points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
,
\epsilon(p_{1})g(p_{1})=\epsilon(p_{2})g(p_{2})
.
If
\beta
joins a double point
p
and a cusp,
g(p)
vanishes since a double point loop near a cusp is null-homotopic.
It follows that
\mu(f_{1})-\mu(f_{0})=\mu(F|_{\partial(Y\times I)})=0
in
\mathbb{Z}[\pi]/\langle 1,g+g^{-1}\rangle
.

In the topological category, Lemma
A.2
ensures that
\mu(f)
is invariant under based homotopy of
f
.
For a topological 6-manifold
P
, a map
F\colon Y\times I\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P\times I
as above is homotopic to a (topological) generic map by Lemma
A.2
, so that the above argument can be applied.

Similarly, for two maps
f_{0}
,
f_{1}\colon Y\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
which embeds
\partial Y
into
\partial P
, the intersection invariant
\lambda(f_{0},f_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}[\pi]
can defined in the topological category.
Homotope
f_{i}
to a generic immersion rel
\partial
using Lemma
A.1
, isotope
f_{i}
to assume that double points of
f_{i}
are not in
f_{1-i}(Y)
, and apply topological transversality
[
FQ90
, 9.5]
to the submanifolds
f_{i}(Y)\smallsetminus\{
double points
\}
to make
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
transverse.
Count transverse intersections
p
of
f_{0}(Y)
and
f_{1}(Y)
with signs
\epsilon(p)
and associated
\pi_{1}
elements
g(p)
, to define
\lambda(f_{0},f_{1})=\sum_{p}\epsilon(p)g(p)\in\mathbb{Z}[\pi]
.
The above equating argument that uses Lemma
A.2
shows that
\lambda(f_{0},f_{1})
is invariant under homotopy of
f_{i}
rel
\partial Y
in the topological category.

When
P=M\times\mathbb{R}\times I
where
M
is a 4-manifold, the value of
\mu(f)
lies in the subgroup
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}T_{M}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}(M)]/\langle 1,g+g^{-1}\rangle
, where
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}T_{M}
is the vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
generated by the set

of 2-torsion elements in
\pi_{1}(M)
, by
[
ST22
, Lemma 4.1]
.
Especially, when
Y=S^{3}
, a self-intersection invariant
\mu\colon\pi_{3}(M)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{F}}_{2}T_{M}
is defined by
\mu([f])=\mu(f)
where
f\colon S^{3}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}M\times\mathbb{R}\times I
is a based generic immersion representative of a given element
[f]\in\pi_{3}(M)
.
By
[
ST22
, Lemma 4.1]
,
\mu
is a homomorphism.

Let
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
be homotopic spheres embedded in a 4-manifold
M
, which have a common geometric dual

.
Suppose
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
agree near

.
Let
H\colon S^{2}\times I\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}M\times\mathbb{R}\times I
be a map which restricts to an embedding

onto

.
(For instance, the track of a homotopy

is such a map.)
By the above,

is invariant under homotopy of

\partial
.
Now, the

for

is defined by
\operatorname{FQ}(R_{0},R_{1})=\mu(H)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}T_{M}/\mu(\pi_{3}(M))
.
It is well-defined, independent of the choice of

, by the arguments in
[
ST22
, Lemma 4.5]
,
[
KM21
, Proposition 4.9]
and
[
KM22
, Proposition 3.3]
.

We remark that the topological version of the intersection invariant
\lambda

[

in the topological category.

When
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
are concordant, we can compute

using a concordance between

and

, which is an embedding

Obviously, an embedding has vanishing

.
Therefore
\operatorname{FQ}(R_{0},R_{1})=\mu(H)=0
if

and

are concordant.
It is readily seen from the definition that the additivity

Thus, it follows that

is determined by the concordance classes of

and

This shows that Theorem

in the body of this paper holds.

The proofs of Lemmas

For our purpose, it suffices to use the following consequence of the immersion theorem.
For a map

between manifolds, denote by

Theorem A.3

(A consequence of

)

Let

-manifolds without boundary,

If there is a bundle map
\phi\colon\tau_{V}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\tau_{P}

g_{1}\colon V\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}P
such that

(Consequently,

is homotopic to the map

We will also use the following high-dimensional smoothing theorem of Kirby and Siebenmann
[

]

For a map

to a (topological) manifold

, we say that

is

of

in

such that

is a smooth immersion and

is smoothly self-transverse.

Theorem A.4

(
[

)

Let

and

be

-manifolds,

is proper, and

a closed subset on which

is weakly smoothly self-transverse.
Suppose

and

.
Then there is an arbitrarily small regular homotopy of

to

such that
g_{1}
is weakly smoothly self-transverse on

.

Proof of Lemma
A.1

Identifying collars of
\partial Y
and
\partial P
with
\partial Y\times I
and
\partial P\times I
, we may assume that the given

on the collar.

Claim

There is a smooth 3-dimensional vector bundle
