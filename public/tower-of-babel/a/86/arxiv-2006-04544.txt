Optimal Work Extraction and the Minimum Description Length Principle
Léo Touzo, Matteo Marsili, Neri Merhav, Édgar Roldán

Abstract
We discuss work extraction from classical information engines (e.g., Szilárd) with N N -particles, q q partitions, and initial arbitrary non-equilibrium states. In particular, we focus on their optimal behaviour, which includes the measurement of a set of quantities 𝚽 \boldsymbol{\Phi} with a feedback protocol that extracts the maximal average amount of work. We show that the optimal non-equilibrium state to which the engine should be driven before the measurement is given by the normalised maximum-likelihood probability distribution of a statistical model that admits 𝚽 \boldsymbol{\Phi} as sufficient statistics. Furthermore, we show that the minimax universal code redundancy ℛ ⋆ \mathcal{R}^{\star} associated to this model, provides an upper bound to the work that the demon can extract on average from the cycle, in units of k B ​ T k_{\rm B}T . We also find that, in the limit of N N large, the maximum average extracted work cannot exceed H ⁡ [ 𝚽 ] / 2 H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]/2 , i.e. one half times the Shannon entropy of the measurement. Our results establish a connection between optimal work extraction in stochastic thermodynamics and optimal universal data compression, providing design principles for optimal information engines. In particular, they suggest that: (i) optimal coding is thermodynamically efficient, and (ii) it is essential to drive the system into a critical state in order to achieve optimal performance.

2.3
Relation to the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle

Equations (
10
) and (
11
) are
intimately related to the solution of an apparently very different problem,
the problem of optimal universal coding, which sets the stage to the MDL principle.
We limit our discussion to a brief account and refer to Ref.
[
49
]
for a more detailed discussion.

Let
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})
be a message generated by an
information source that we wish to compress as much as possible.
This entails to translating
\mathbf{s}
into a sequence of nats
7
7

7

For later convenience,
we measure information in nats and take logarithms in the natural basis.
. A code is a rule that assigns to each

\mathbf{s}
a sequence of nats by a one–to–one mapping.
The
description length
,
E(\mathbf{s})
, is the number of nats of
the compressed sequence. The key to achieve efficient codes is to assign short codewords to
frequent patterns. This has to be done satisfying Kraft-McMillan inequality

\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{-E(\mathbf{s})}\leq 1,

(18)

which imposes a fundamental limit to uniquely decodable codes
[
59
]
.

Let us consider messages
\mathbf{s}
that are generated by drawing at random and independently each character
s_{i}

from a probability distribution,
f({s_{i}}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
, that depends
on a vector of
M\geq 1
parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M})
.
If the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are known, the optimal description length that can be
achieved (ignoring integer length constraints) is
[
59
]

E(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\ln P(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\equiv-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln f(s_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(19)

MDL deals with optimal coding in the case where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are unknown. Then,
for a given message
\mathbf{s}
, the optimal compression is achieved minimising the
description length over
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, i.e.
E(\mathbf{s}|\hat{}\boldsymbol{\theta})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{-\ln P(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
.
A code with description length
E(\mathbf{s})
entails to an excess code length for this sample, of

\mathcal{R}(E,\mathbf{s})=E(\mathbf{s})-E(\mathbf{s}|\hat{}\boldsymbol{\theta})
nats,
which is called the
redundancy
8
8

8

\mathcal{R}
is also called
regret
in the
literature of predictive learning theory, in the context of learning with expert advice.
.
The MDL identifies optimal codes with those achieving the minimal redundancy, for the worst possible sample
[
49
]

\mathcal{R}^{\star}=\min_{E}\max_{\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{R}(E,\mathbf{s}),

(20)

where the minimum is taken over all
E(\mathbf{s})

satisfying the Kraft-McMillan inequality (
18
).
A code that solves this problem
is associated with the length function,
E^{*}(\mathbf{s})=-\ln P^{*}(\mathbf{s})
, where

P^{*}(\mathbf{s})=e^{-\mathcal{R}^{\star}}P(\mathbf{s}|\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{s})),

(21)

which is called the normalised maximum likelihood (NML) probability
distribution and

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\sum_{\mathbf{s}}P(\mathbf{s}|\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{s}))

(22)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{M}{2}\ln\frac{N}{2\pi}+\int\text{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\sqrt{{\rm det}\,{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(23)

minimax universal code redundancy

[
60
]

Equation (
23

N
leading behaviour
[
60
]
, where
M

\boldsymbol{\theta}
) and
{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the Fisher information matrix. The latter is a
M\times M
matrix with elements

J_{a,b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}P(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\ln P(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\ln P(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\right].

(24)

Notice
that, a sample
\mathbf{s}
of length
N
allows to estimate each parameter
to a precision of
{\delta\theta_{a}}\sim 1/\sqrt{N}
, and hence it provides

\frac{1}{2}\ln N
nats of information for each parameter, in agreement with
the first term of Eq. (
23
).
In a loose sense,

quantifies the amount of information that the sequence
\mathbf{s}

provides on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of the model from which it has been drawn.
9
9

9

This statement can be made more precise
within a Bayesian framework, where Eq. (
23
) is the asymptotic value
of the mutual information
I(\mathbf{s};\theta)
between a sample and the
parameters, under Jeffrey’s prior.

The NML provides a
universal code
because it achieves a compression per data point
which is as good as the compression that would be achieved asymptotically as
N\to\infty
with the optimal choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

[
61

\mathcal{R}^{\star}

\ln N

23

{\mathcal{R}^{\star}}/N\to 0
as
N\to\infty

10
10

10

Moreover, the convergence of

possible rate when
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is unknown (in a very strong sense)
[
61
]
.
. Note that the minimax redundancy,
{\mathcal{R}^{\star}}

\mathbf{s}

3.1
A physical realization of the
N
-particle Szilárd engine

We now discuss, in the light of stochastic thermodynamics, a physical analog of a Szilárd engine which enlightens extensions of our theory to more general scenarios. The model consists of
N
classical non-interacting particles in
q
energy levels
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{q})
immersed in a thermal bath with
k_{\rm B}T=1
. We let
s_{i}
denote the energy level occupied by the
i^{\rm th}
particle and
n_{s}
be occupation number of the
s^{\rm th}
energy level. Each level can be occupied by a maximum of
N
particles, with the constraint
\sum_{s=1}^{q}n_{s}=N

The equilibrium probability of a configuration

(25)

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{s_{i}}=\sum_{s=1}^{q}n_{s}\epsilon_{s}

Z(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\left(\sum_{s=1}^{q}e^{-\epsilon_{s}}\right)^{N}
the partition function, and
\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=-\ln Z(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
the equilibrium free energy. Setting

\ell_{s}=\frac{e^{-\epsilon_{s}}}{\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{q}e^{-\epsilon_{s^{\prime}}}}

(26)

for all
s=1,\dots,q

25
) becomes identical to Eq. (
2

\ell_{s}
is the probability that a particle is found in partition
s
of the Szilárd engine. Hence the system of particles described by Eq. (
25

in the

e^{-\epsilon_{s}}
.
Note that

can be enforced by an appropriate shift of all energy levels and that would imply
\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=0

3.2
Optimal work extraction measuring sufficient statistics: theory

We now ask the question: how do these results generalise when the measured quantities
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
are different from
\mathbf{n}
?
In order to address this question, we consider a model where the energy levels

\epsilon_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\epsilon_{s}^{0}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)=\epsilon_{s}^{0}-\sum_{m=1}^{M}\theta_{m}\phi_{m}(s),

(35)

depend on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M})
through the
M
functions
\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)=(\phi_{1}(s),\ldots,\phi_{M}(s))
.
Here the dot denotes the scalar product and
\epsilon_{s}^{0}
specifies the energy of level
s
when it is unoccupied (
n_{s}=0
).
For a microscopic state
s
, the Hamiltonian now takes the value

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbf{s})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}),

(36)

with

\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{s_{i}}^{0}=\sum_{s=1}^{q}n_{s}\epsilon_{s}^{0},

(37)

and the vector

\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})=\left(\sum_{s=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}(s)n_{s},\dots,\sum_{s=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{M}(s)n_{s}\right).

(38)

The equilibrium distribution becomes

P_{\rm eq}(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=e^{-\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathbf{s})+\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{s})+\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(39)

with the corresponding equilibrium free energy given by

\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\ln\left[\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{-\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathbf{s})+\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{s})}\right].

(40)

From the viewpoint of mathematical statistics, the parametric probability distribution (
39
) belongs to an
exponential family
. Exponential families have special properties in that they exhibit explicitly the sufficient statistics, i.e., those functions of the data whose knowledge is sufficient to estimate the parameters from a sample. In the present context, notice that
P_{\rm eq}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}f(s_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the probability of a sequence
s
of
N
independent draws
s_{i}
from the distribution
f(s|\boldsymbol{\theta})=e^{-\epsilon_{s}^{0}+\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s)-\varphi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
, with
\varphi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/N
. For this distribution,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})
is a sufficient statistics, in the sense that it contains all necessary information about
\boldsymbol{\theta}

[
59
]
, i.e. the mutual information between
s
and the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
equals that between
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
:

I(\mbox{\boldmath$s$};\boldsymbol{\theta})=I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\Phi}).

(41)

Exponential families are unique, in that the Pitman-Koopman-Darmois theorem
[
62
]
ensures that the set of sufficient statistics of a parametric model is independent of
N
only if the model has the exponential form of Eq. (
39
). As we shall see, this is a crucial step in the work extraction cycle from measurements of sufficient statistics.

An information engine based on the model (
39
) performs a cycle analogous to that shown in Fig.
2
, with the sole exceptions that here the parameters of the model are
\boldsymbol{\theta}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\ell}
and the values of the statistics
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{s})
are measured instead of
\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{s})
. Furthermore, work is extracted by adjusting the conjugate parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
. The work extracted in a cycle can be calculated in exactly the same way as in the discussion leading to Eq. (
30
). Note that, by Eqs. (
36
,
39
),
W_{\rm ext}
depend on
s
only through the measured quantity
\Phi
.
The work extracted is maximal for

{\hat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s})={\rm arg}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\left[\ln P_{\rm eq}(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\right]={\rm arg}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})-\mathcal{F}_{\rm eq}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]

(42)

which is again the MLE.
The crucial point is that the measured quantity
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
should provide sufficient information to compute the values
\hat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}
of the optimal parameters in the work extraction phase. Therefore
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
must be a sufficient statistics of model Eq. (
39
) and, by the Pitman-Koopman-Darmois theorem
[
62
]
,
P_{\rm eq}(\mathbf{s}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
needs to have the exponential form of Eq. (
39
), for this to be possible for all
N
. Taking the expected value of the optimal extracted work, following the same steps of the previous section, we arrive at

\langle W_{\rm ext}\rangle\leq\mathcal{R}^{\star}-D[P_{\rm neq}(\mathbf{s})||P^{\star}(\mathbf{s})],

(43)

where
P^{*}
is the NML distribution (
21
) and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
is the minimax redundancy of Eq. (
22
).
This shows that the maximal work that can be extracted from a cycle, on average, equals the minimax redundancy
\mathcal{R}^{*}
, and that the non-equilibrium state that achieves this bound is the NML, i.e
P_{\rm neq}=P^{*}
. The only change, with respect to the previous section, is that the re-adjustment of energy levels
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is constrained by the functional form of Eq. (
35
), to those that can be achieved by fine-tuning the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, conjugate to the measured quantity.

Also in this case, this result is independent of the equilibrium parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in which the engine is initialised and the optimal work extracted grows asymptotically as
\frac{M}{2}\ln N
with the number of particles, for
N\gg 1
(see Eq. (
23
)).

Let us now see how close the maximal extracted work approaches the fundamental limit imposed by the second law of thermodynamics,
\langle W_{\rm ext}\rangle\leq k_{B}TH[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]

[
7
]
, where
H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]
is the entropy of the measurement. In the limit of large
N\gg M
, to leading order, we find (see Appendix
A
)

H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]\simeq M\ln N+\ln\int\!d\boldsymbol{\theta}\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\boldsymbol{\theta})}+\frac{\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\ln\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{\int\!d\boldsymbol{\theta}\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}.

(44)

This confirms that, as in Eq. (
17
),
\mathcal{R}^{*}/H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]\to 1/2
as
N\to\infty
. The intuition at the basis of this result is the same as in Section
2.2
: The total reduction in the uncertainty on
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
brough about by the measurement is
H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]\simeq M\ln N
. A part of this is lost when the walls are removed, and the system relaxes to state E. In state E, the relative fluctuations of each component of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
are of order
1/\sqrt{N}
, hence the loss amounts to
\frac{1}{2}\ln N
nats per parameter. This result tallies with the findings of Ref.
[

4
Discussion

The result of this paper lends itself to a number of considerations:

4.1
Efficient communication as ideal information engines

The process of measuring a quantity
\Phi
can be seen as a communication problem between two parties, Alice and Bob. In order to focus on thermodynamic efficiency, we model the channel over which Alice and Bob communicate, as an information engine. The messages that Alice and Bob exchange are a sequence
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})
of symbols from a finite alphabet, where
s_{i}
are drawn i.i.d. from a certain distribution. The exchange is realized by an information engine that operates at a constant temperature. Let
\Phi(\mathbf{s})
be the value of the quantity that is measured. Alice and Bob both have a device, that is initially in state
P_{0}(\mathbf{s})
. They can choose to code their messages with a distribution
P_{\rm neq}(\mathbf{s})
. This requires Alice to spend
\Delta F=D[P_{\rm neq}(\mathbf{s})||P_{0}(\mathbf{s})]
units of
k_{B}T
to generate messages, but it allows Bob to harvest work
-W(\Phi)
from the measurement of
\Phi(\mathbf{s})
. If Alice and Bob exchange messages both ways it makes sense for them to agree on a design
that minimises the average energetic cost
\Delta F+\langle W\rangle
of the communication, over many cycles. Our result implies that (i)
P_{0}
should be of the form Eq. (
39
); (ii)
P_{\rm neq}=P^{*}
should be the corresponding NML, and (iii) some work can be extracted for each message, that on average cannot exceed the minimax redundancy
\mathcal{R}^{\star}
times
k_{B}T
. The channel should work as follows: A value of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is drawn at random from
P^{*}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
. Knowing
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, Alice can prepare the engine with partitions of sizes dictated by
\hat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
and distribute the
N
particles in such a way that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})
takes the desired value
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. Then she can operate a quasistatic transformation to bring back the partitions to the sizes that correspond to the equilibrium values
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, spending
\Delta F
units of
k_{B}T
. Then Alice can pass the information engine to Bob, who would measure
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and extract work from the isothermal expansion of the partitions.

Interestingly, the costs and benefits across the communication channel are shared unequally in this optimal protocol.
This argument neglects the cost needed to erase the memory in which
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is stored, which is at least equal to the Shannon entropy
H[\boldsymbol{\Phi}]
of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. This makes sense in a communication setting, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is valuable information that does not need to be erased. In addition, we show that this communication protocol provides an energy budget that can cover half of the memory cost, in the limit of long messages.

4.2
The importance of being critical

The NML codes have been recently shown to be “critical” in a very precise sense. First, Ref.
[
50
]
has shown that, a typical sample drawn from
P^{*}
exhibits
statistical criticality

[
63
]
, i.e. the distribution of
n_{s}
is very broad. Second, studying large deviations of the in-sample description length per character,
\hat{H}[s]=-\sum_{s=1}^{q}\hat{\ell}_{s}\ln\hat{\ell}_{s}
, for samples generated from
P^{*}
, Ref.
[
50
]
has shown that
P^{*}
sits exactly at a critical point. This is because there are no codes that can achieve a better compression than
P^{*}
. It is interesting that extracting the maximal amount of work from the cycle of an information engine, requires driving the system to a critical state. This provides hints for possible experimental protocols towards more efficient information engines.

4.3
Which observables should be measured?

The above discussion suggests that most efficient communication is achieved for sufficient statistics
\Phi
such that
\mathcal{R}^{\star}
is largest. This result provides further insights with respect to recent work
[
53
]
which pointed to the relevance of the existence of sufficient statistics in information-thermodynamic efficiency. We have shown that, for fixed number of particles
N
and parameters
M
, the maximal work extracted by a broad class of information engines depends on the value
c=\int\text{d}\theta\sqrt{\det{\mathbf{J}(\theta)}}
of the constant term in Eq. (
23
), which is reminiscent of the so-called ”thermodynamic length” introduced in Refs.
[
35
,
64
,
65
]
. Observables for which the corresponding value of
c
is larger should allow for more energy efficient measurement devices. For example Ref.
[
58
]
has studied the term
c
for spin models with interactions of arbitrary order and found that pairwise models achieve large values of
c
.

4.4
Experimental applications

Our results shed light on potential future experimental applications in stochastic thermodynamics. Until now, experimental realizations of one-particle Szilárd engines have been achieved using e.g. colloidal particles trapped with optical tweezers
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
and feedback traps
[
24
]
, DNA hairpins
[
28
]
, and single-electron transistors
[
23
]
. Most of these experiments rely on the accurate control of a single degree of freedom, e.g. the position of a colloid along the
x
axis, the energy level occupied by one extra electron in a single-electron transistor, etc. This leads to optimal work extractions of the order of
k_{\rm B}T
by means of current experimental techniques.

We have shown that Szilárd engines extracting work above the ”
k_{\rm B}T
limit” can be realized physically, e.g. with
N
-particle systems in
q
energy levels. Our formalism provides exact formulas for the non-equilibrium state at which an
N
-particle information engine should be driven and how much work can be extracted from the measurement of an arbitrary coarse-grained physical quantity. The potential theoretical extensions to bosonic systems and experimental realizations of these results with e.g. polaritons or photons trapped in optical cavity networks
[
66
,
67
]
, are two interesting avenues of future research.
