Idempotents in the group algebra of the infinite dihedral group
Ivan Dimitrov, Charles Paquette, David Wehlau, Tianyuan Xu

Abstract
. We prove that over an algebraically closed field 𝕂 \mathbb{K} of characteristic different from 2 2 , the group algebra R = 𝕂 ​ D ∞ R=\mathbb{K}D_{\infty} of the infinite dihedral group D ∞ D_{\infty} has exactly six conjugacy classes of involutions (equivalently, of idempotents). This allows us to recover the fact that R R admits exactly four non-isomorphic indecomposable projective modules of the form e ​ R eR where e e is an idempotent, a result that was first established by Berman and Buzási.

1.
Introduction

In this short note, we consider an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic different from

2
and study the involutions of the group algebra
R=\mathbb{K}D_{\infty}
where
D_{\infty}
is the infinite
dihedral group
D_{\infty}=\langle a,b\mid a^{2}=b^{2}=1\rangle
. Our goal is to give an elementary,
self-contained classification of conjugacy classes of involutions in
R
. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

The algebra
R
has exactly six conjugacy classes of involutions, namely, those of the elements
1,-1,\frac{1+a}{2},\frac{1-a}{2},\frac{1+b}{2}
and
\frac{1-b}{2}
.

The group
D_{\infty}
and the algebra
R
are interesting for many reasons, some of which have a
representation-theoretic nature. For example, the group
D_{\infty}
admits every group presented by
two involutions as a quotient, including every finite dihedral group and the infinite cyclic group,
therefore the representation theory of
R
is closely related to that of such groups. Also, the
algebra
R
is isomorphic to the free product
\mathbb{K}C_{2}*\mathbb{K}C_{2}
of two copies of the semisimple algebra
\mathbb{K}C_{2}
,
the group algebra
of the cyclic
C_{2}
of order 2. It is interesting to note that among all free
products of group algebras of finite cyclic groups,
R
stands out as the only algebra
with infinite representation type that has a global bound on the dimensions of the finite
dimensional simple modules; see
[
DPWX22
, Proposition 5.4]
. More generally, among all free
products of finitely many nontrivial semisimple
\mathbb{K}
-algebras,
R
also stands out as the only such
algebra of tame representation type; see
[
BDG+24
, Theorem 7.3]
. Furthermore, the category of finite
dimensional modules of
R
can be realized as a subcategory of the category of regular modules over
the
4
-subspace quiver
\tilde{D}_{4}
, so that the finite dimensional indecomposable modules are all
uniserial and the collection of simple modules consists of four one-dimensional modules together with a
one-parameter family of simple modules of dimension 2; see
[
BDG+24
, Example 7.2]
.

Finitely generated modules of the algebra
R
have also been studied extensively. In
[
BB81
]
, Berman and Buzási
consider the infinite cyclic subgroup
C
of
D_{\infty}
generated by the element
ab
and restrict a
given
\mathbb{K}D_{\infty}
-module to a
\mathbb{K}C
-module, allowing them to exploit the well-understood
representation theory of
\mathbb{K}C
. Among other things, this fruitful approach yields the following
facts. Up to isomorphism, there are only four indecomposable infinite dimensional finitely generated
(right)
R
-modules, namely, those of the form
eR
where
e
is one of the four idempotents

(1-a)/2,(1+a)/2,(1-b)/2,(1+b)/2
. Moreover, these modules are all projective, and they are the only indecomposable modules having no
\mathbb{K}C
-torsion.

The results mentioned above immediately imply that
R
has at least four conjugacy classes of primitive idempotents, because if two
idempotents
e
and
e^{\prime}\in R
are conjugate then the modules
eR
and
eR^{\prime}
are isomorphic.
However, an isomorphism
eR\cong eR^{\prime}
for idempotents
e,e^{\prime}
does not guarantee
e
and
e^{\prime}
are
conjugate (see
[
KLN18
, Lemma 2.2]
), so it is not obvious whether
R
has exactly four conjugacy
classes of primitive idempotents. Theorem
1
answers this question affirmatively. Combining Proposition
4
with Theorem
1
, we prove the fact that
R
has exactly four isomorphism classes of indecomposable projective modules of the form
eR
where
e
is an idempotent. We note that this fact follows from Theorem 6 of
[
BB81
]
, whose proof involves careful analysis of restrictions of
\mathbb{K}D_{\infty}
-modules to
\mathbb{K}C
, but the proof given here is shorter and more elementary.

2.
Preliminaries and main result

Let
R=\mathbb{K}\langle a,b\,|\,a^{2}=b^{2}=1\rangle
. We want to classify the involutions of

R
up to conjugacy. Doing so is equivalent to classifying the idempotents of

R
up to conjugacy, because an element
r\in R
is an idempotent if and only
if
2r-1
is an involution, and two elements
r,r^{\prime}\in R
are conjugate if and
only if
2r-1
and
2r^{\prime}-1
are conjugate.

Let
s=a,t=ab
and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{K}[t,t^{-1}]
be the corresponding ring of Laurent polynomials. Observe that
R
can also be
presented as
R=\mathbb{K}\langle s,t:s^{2}=1,ts=st^{-1}\rangle
. In particular,
R
is spanned by elements of form
t^{n}

and
st^{n}
where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, so every element
u\in R
can be written in the
form
f(t)+sg(t)
for some
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
. It is straightforward to check that
R
embeds into
M_{2\times 2}(\mathcal{A})

via the injective homomorphism

(1)

\iota:R\rightarrow M_{2\times 2}(\mathcal{A}),\quad f(t)+sg(t)\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}f(z)&g^{*}(z)\\
g(z)&f^{*}(z)\end{bmatrix}

where
*:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
is the involutive map defined by
h^{*}(z)=h(1/z)
for
all
h\in\mathcal{A}
. We will henceforth often identify each

u\in R
with its image
\iota(u)\in M_{2\times 2}(\mathcal{A})
.

With the identification provided by (
1
), Theorem
1
can now be rephrased as follows.

Theorem 2
.

The involutions of
R
fall into six conjugacy classes. These
classes are represented by the elements

1=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},s=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{bmatrix},st=\begin{bmatrix}0&1/t\\
t&0\end{bmatrix},

and their additive inverses
-1,-s,-st
; in other words, the classes are represented by the elements
1,-1,a,-a,b,-b
.

Corresponding to the non-trivial involutions we have the following four
non-trivial idempotents:

e=\frac{1-a}{2},\quad e^{\prime}=\frac{1-b}{2},\quad 1-e=\frac{1+a}{2},\quad 1-e^{\prime}=\frac{1+b}{2}\ .

We will use this notation for these idempotents for the remainder of the paper.

By the idempotent-involution correspondence, the following is now immediate.

Corollary 3
.

There are precisely four conjugacy classes of primitive idempotents in
R
, namely, the classes represented by
e,1-e,e^{\prime},1-e^{\prime}
.

Next we study the projective modules of the form
xR
where
x
is an idempotent.

Proposition 4
.

There are exactly four pairwise non-isomorphic indecomposable projective modules of the form
xR
where
x
is an idempotent. They are the modules
eR,(1-e)R,e^{\prime}R
and
(1-e^{\prime})R
where
e
and
e^{\prime}
are as in Corollary 3.

Proof.

For
\mu,\nu\in\{-1,1\}
, we can define a simple
R
module
S_{\mu,\nu}
of dimension one for which
a
acts by multiplication by
\mu
and
b
acts by multiplication by
\nu
. The following hold.

(1)

\Hom_{R}(eR,-)
vanishes on
S_{1,1}
and
S_{1,-1}
;

(2)

\Hom_{R}((1-e)R,-)
vanishes on
S_{-1,1}
and
S_{-1,-1}
;

(3)

\Hom_{R}(e^{\prime}R,-)
vanishes on
S_{1,1}
and
S_{-1,1}
; and

(4)

\Hom_{R}((1-e^{\prime})R,-)
vanishes on
S_{1,-1}
and
S_{-1,-1}
.

These facts immediately imply the desired result.
∎

Notice that from the equality

(2)

eR\oplus(1-e)R=e^{\prime}R\oplus(1-e^{\prime})R,

we have two distinct decompositions of the right
R
-module
R
into a direct sum of indecomposable modules. In particular, the category of finitely generated (projective) right
R
-modules does not satisfy the Krull-Schmidt decomposition theorem. The relation (
2
) appears in
[
BB81
, Remark 2]
. Moreover,
[
BB81
, Theorem 4]

states that the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(R)
of
R
is a free abelian group of rank 3,
and (
2
) is the only relation among its generators.

3.
Proof of the theorem

Throughout this section, we use the embedding
\iota
from (
1
) to identify
R
with its image in
M_{2\times 2}(\mathcal{A})
. Let

(3)

u_{\varepsilon,\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\varepsilon t^{-\theta}\\
\varepsilon t^{\theta}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The set
\{u_{\varepsilon,\theta}:\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\},\theta\in\{0,1\}\}
coincides with the set

\{s,-s,st,-st\}
. To prove Theorem
2
, it suffices to prove that every element
u
of
R
such that

u^{2}=1
and
u\neq\pm 1

is conjugate to exactly one element of the set
\{u_{\varepsilon,\theta}:\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\},\theta\in\{0,1\}\}
.

Let
u\in R
be as above. Since
u^{2}=1
, the minimal polynomial
m_{x}(u)
of
u

divides
x^{2}-1=(x-1)(x+1)
, therefore
m_{x}(u)
equals
x-1,x+1
or
x^{2}-1
. In
the first two cases we have
u=1
and
u=-1
, respectively, so it suffices to
assume
m_{x}(u)=x^{2}-1
and show that
u
is conjugate to one of the four elements
\pm s,\pm st
.

Let
Q=\mathbb{K}(t)
denote the field of fractions of
\mathcal{A}
.
Since
u\in M_{2\times 2}(Q)
and
m_{x}(u)=x^{2}-1
, the characteristic polynomial of
u
equals
x^{2}-1
,
therefore
u
has trace 0 and determinant
-1
.
It follows that
u
is of the form

u=\begin{bmatrix}f&g^{*}\\
g&f^{*}\end{bmatrix}

where
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
,
f^{*}=-f
and
ff^{*}-gg^{*}=-1
.

Recall that the ring
\mathcal{A}
is
a unique factorization domain with units given by
\mathcal{A}^{\times}=\{\lambda t^{m}:\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Consider the unique prime factorization of
1+f
given by

(4)

1+f=\delta t^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i},

where
\delta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
p_{i}
is a prime element in
\mathcal{A}

for each
i\in[n]
.
Then as
f^{*}=-f
, we have

(5)

1-f=1+f^{*}=(1+f)^{*}=\delta t^{-m}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{*}

and hence

(6)

1+ff^{*}=1-f^{2}=(1+f)(1-f)=\delta^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}p_{i}^{*}.

Since
gg^{*}=1+ff^{*}
, it further follows that there is a subset
I
of

[n]
such that

(7)

g=\gamma t^{l}\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}p_{i}^{*},\quad g^{*}=\gamma t^{-l}\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}^{*}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}p_{i}

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},l\in\mathbb{Z}
and
I^{c}=[n]\setminus I
.
Note that

\delta^{2}=\gamma^{2}
by equations (
6
) and (
7
), so

\gamma=\varepsilon\delta
and
\delta=\varepsilon\gamma
with

\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
. Let
\theta
be the unique integer in
\{0,1\}
with the same parity as

l+m
. Note that both the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\theta
are uniquely
determined by
f
and
g
and hence by
u
.

Next we show that
u
is conjugate to
u_{\varepsilon,\theta}
by
constructing
\nu\in R^{\times}
such that
\nu^{-1}u\nu=u_{\varepsilon,\theta}
. To define
\nu
, let

(8)

g_{1}=\varepsilon t^{\frac{l+m+\theta}{2}}\prod_{i\in I}p_{i},\quad g_{2}=\delta t^{\frac{l-m-\theta}{2}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}p_{i}^{*},

let

(9)

a=\dfrac{\varepsilon t^{\theta}g_{2}+g_{1}}{2g},\quad b=\dfrac{\varepsilon t^{\theta}g_{2}-g_{1}}{2g},

and let

(10)

\nu=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon(a+bf)&bt^{-\theta}+at^{-\theta}f\\
\varepsilon bg&at^{-\theta}g\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2\times 2}(Q).

With these, we can now establish the following.

Proposition 5
.

The element
\nu\in M_{2\times 2}(Q)
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

\nu\in\iota(R)
.

(2)

\nu
is invertible in
M_{2\times 2}(Q)
, and its inverse

\nu^{-1}
in
M_{2\times 2}(Q)
is also in

\iota(R)
.

(3)

\nu^{-1}u\nu=u_{\varepsilon,\theta}
.

Proof.

(1) To prove
\nu\in\iota(R)
it suffices to show that
at^{-\theta}g\in\mathcal{A},(at^{-\theta}g)^{*}=\varepsilon(a+bf),\varepsilon bg\in\mathcal{A}

and

(\varepsilon bg)^{*}=bt^{-\theta}+at^{-\theta}f
.
Note that it follows from Equation (
9
) that

at^{-\theta}g=\frac{\varepsilon g_{2}+t^{-\theta}g_{1}}{2},

therefore
at^{-\theta}g\in\mathcal{A}

and

(at^{-\theta}g)^{*}=\frac{\varepsilon g_{2}^{*}+t^{\theta}g_{1}^{*}}{2}.

Also, from equations (
4
) and (
5
), it follows that

(11)

\frac{1+f}{g_{1}}=\gamma t^{\frac{m-l-\theta}{2}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}p_{i}=\varepsilon t^{-\theta}g_{2}^{*},\quad\frac{1-f}{g_{2}}=t^{\frac{-l-m+\theta}{2}}\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}^{*}=\varepsilon t^{\theta}g_{1}^{*}.

Combining (
9
), (
11
), and the fact that
g_{1}g_{2}=g
, we get

\varepsilon(a+bf)=\frac{t^{\theta}g_{2}+\varepsilon g_{1}+t^{\theta}g_{2}f-\varepsilon g_{1}f}{2g}=\dfrac{t^{\theta}\frac{1+f}{g_{1}}+\varepsilon\frac{1-f}{g_{2}}}{2}=\frac{\varepsilon g_{2}^{*}+t^{\theta}g_{1}^{*}}{2}.

Therefore, we have

(at^{-\theta}g)^{*}=\varepsilon(a+bf).

Using (
9
) again yields

\varepsilon bg=\dfrac{t^{\theta}g_{2}-\varepsilon g_{1}}{2}\in\mathcal{A}.

and using (
9
) with (
11
) yields

\displaystyle bt^{-\theta}+at^{-\theta}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varepsilon g_{2}-t^{-\theta}g_{1}+\varepsilon fg_{2}+t^{-\theta}fg_{1}}{2g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varepsilon g_{2}(\frac{1+f}{g})-t^{-\theta}g_{1}(\frac{1-f}{g})}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\varepsilon(\frac{1+f}{g_{1}})-t^{-\theta}(\frac{1-f}{g_{2}})}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{t^{-\theta}g_{2}^{*}-\varepsilon g_{1}^{*}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\varepsilon bg)^{*},

which completes our proof of the fact that
\nu\in\iota(R)
.

(2) A straightforward calculation shows that

\det\nu=\varepsilon t^{-\theta}g(a^{2}-b^{2})=\varepsilon t^{-\theta}g(a+b)(a-b).

Since
g_{1}g_{2}=g
, it follows from (
9
) that

a+b=\varepsilon t^{\theta}g_{1}^{-1},\quad a-b=g_{2}^{-1}

and hence
\det\nu=1
. It further follows
that the inverse
\nu^{-1}
of
\nu
in
M_{2\times 2}(Q)
is given by

\nu^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\nu}\begin{bmatrix}at^{-\theta}g&-(bt^{-\theta}+at^{-\theta}f)\\
-\varepsilon bg&\varepsilon(a+bf)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}at^{-\theta}g&-(bt^{-\theta}+at^{-\theta}f)\\
-\varepsilon bg&\varepsilon(a+bf)\end{bmatrix}

from which it is clear that
\nu^{-1}\in\iota(R)
, because
\nu\in\iota(R)
.

(3) Using the facts that
f^{*}=-f
and
ff^{*}-gg^{*}=-1
, it is easy to check that
u\nu=\nu u_{\varepsilon,\theta}
, therefore
\nu^{-1}u\nu=u_{\varepsilon,\theta}
.
∎

By Part (3) of Proposition
5
, we may now conclude that
\{\pm 1,\pm s,\pm st\}
forms a complete list of representatives of conjugacy classes of
involutions in
R
. Equivalently, the corresponding four primitive idempotents
e,1-e,e^{\prime},1-e^{\prime}
form a complete list of representatives of conjugacy classes of primitive idempotents in
R
. As observed in Proposition
4
, the corresponding projective modules are pairwise non-isomorphic, therefore these four idempotents are pairwise non-conjugate. This completes the proof.
