Smoothing the Edges: Smooth Optimization for Sparse Regularization using Hadamard Overparametrization
Chris Kolb chris.kolb@stat.uni-muenchen.de, Christian L. Müller cmueller@flatironinstitute.org, Bernd Bischl bernd.bischl@stat.uni-muenchen.de

Abstract
We present a framework for smooth optimization of explicitly regularized objectives for (structured) sparsity. These non-smooth and possibly non-convex problems typically rely on solvers tailored to specific models and regularizers. In contrast, our method enables fully differentiable and approximation-free optimization and is thus compatible with the ubiquitous gradient descent paradigm in deep learning. The proposed optimization transfer comprises an overparameterization of selected parameters and a change of penalties. In the overparametrized problem, smooth surrogate regularization induces non-smooth, sparse regularization in the base parametrization. We prove that the surrogate objective is equivalent in the sense that it not only has identical global minima but also matching local minima, thereby avoiding the introduction of spurious solutions. Additionally, our theory establishes results of independent interest regarding matching local minima for arbitrary, potentially unregularized, objectives. We comprehensively review sparsity-inducing parametrizations across different fields that are covered by our general theory, extend their scope, and propose improvements in several aspects. Numerical experiments further demonstrate the correctness and effectiveness of our approach on several sparse learning problems ranging from high-dimensional regression to sparse neural network training.

Introduction and Background

As a result of the proliferation of high-dimensional and unstructured data in recent years, methods for sparse (vectors) and low-rank (matrices and tensors) representations have received increasing attention in fields such as machine learning, statistics, and signal processing. Parsimonious models are commonly used to incorporate prior knowledge about the complexity of the underlying phenomenon or to obtain interpretable sparse approximations of non-sparse ground truths
(
Bach et al. 2012
)
. They are also employed to regularize otherwise intractable inverse problems
(
Benning and Burger 2018
)
and reduce the computational burden of expensive large-scale optimization or inference, e.g., in deep learning (DL) models
(
Blalock et al. 2020
;
Hoefler et al. 2021
)
. Structured or group sparsity naturally generalizes the notion of unstructured sparsity to enable encoding
structural
prior information about model complexity into the optimization problem
(
Huang et al. 2009
;
Jenatton et al. 2011
)
.

\bm{\ell_{0}}
and
\bm{\ell_{1}}
regularization
In sparse estimation problems of a parameter vector
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
given a smooth objective function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
, the classical optimization problem using explicit regularization is

\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}(\bm{\beta})\,,

with regularization or penalty function
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
to
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})
, whose strength is controlled by
\lambda\geq 0
.
A natural choice for the regularizer is
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\beta})=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
, i.e., the cardinality of the support of
\bm{\beta}
counting its non-zero entries. However, this best-subset approach is infeasible due to its non-convex, and non-continuous NP-hard nature
(
Natarajan 1995
;
Chen et al. 2017
)
. To overcome these difficulties, convex relaxations of
\ell_{0}
regularization have been proposed that enable optimization via, e.g., coordinate descent or projected gradient methods
(
Tropp 2006
;
Schmidt et al. 2007
)
. The tightest convex relaxation of
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
is given by its convex envelope
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
, resulting in

\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\,.

(1)

This formulation is known as
\ell_{1}
regularization today. In the context of linear models, it has been introduced as the lasso to the statistics community
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
and as Basis Pursuit Denoising in signal processing
(
Chen and Donoho 1994
;
Chen et al. 2001
)
.
For convex
\mathcal{L}
, such as in linear regression, the well-developed machinery of convex optimization can be utilized to solve (
1
).
\ell_{1}
regularization has also been shown to have some favorable theoretical properties, such as consistent recovery of the true support of
\bm{\beta}
under restricted conditions
(
Donoho and Elad 2003
;
Zhao and Yu 2006
;
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
)
. However, using
\ell_{1}
regularization to achieve sparsity also comes with a disadvantage: whereas
\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{0}
is constant on the support of
\bm{\beta}
, the
\ell_{1}
penalty increases linearly in the magnitude of its components. This leads to estimation bias for large parameters
(
Zhang and Huang 2008
)
and inconsistent support recovery
(
Chartrand 2007
;
Xu et al. 2012
)
.
To mitigate the challenges posed by
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{0}
regularization, the seminal work of
Fan and Li 2001
proposed smoothly clipped absolute deviations (SCAD), one of the earliest examples of non-convex regularizers. Another popular non-convex penalty that enables feature selection and nearly unbiased estimation is the minimax concave penalty (MCP) introduced by
Zhang 2010
.

\bm{\ell_{p,q}}
regularization
In this work, however, we focus on a generalization of the
\ell_{1}
penalty based on the
\ell_{q}
quasi-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{q}
, for
0<q\leq 1
. This approach was initially described by
Frank and Friedman 1993
and subsequently popularized as the bridge penalty by
Fu 1998
. Non-convex bridge regularization is defined by a regularization term of the form
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\beta})=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{q}^{q}
for
0<q<1
. For the case of structured sparsity,
\ell_{q}
regularization can be straightforwardly extended to mixed-norm
\ell_{p,q}
regularization for
0<q<p\leq 2
, studied, e.g., in
Hu et al. 2017
. A number of important desirable theoretical results have been established for non-convex
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularization, such as requiring fewer linear measurements for support recovery and permitting sparser solutions compared to convex
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2,1}
(group-wise) regularization
(
Fu 1998
;
Fu and Knight 2000
;
Chartrand 2007
;
Xu et al. 2012
)
. Moreover, the regularity conditions required for consistent recovery are weaker than typically required for
\ell_{1}

(
Chartrand and Staneva 2008
;
Loh and Wainwright 2017
)
or
\ell_{2,1}
penalties
(
Hu et al. 2017
)
.

Optimization using
\ell_{q}
regularization, however, poses a non-smooth and non-convex problem for
0<q<1
and is thus difficult to solve efficiently.
Ge et al. 2011
show that identification of the global minimum is strongly NP-hard. Still, computing local minima of the non-convex regularization problem usually performs better compared to convex regularization approaches
(
Xu et al. 2010
;
Xu et al. 2012
;
Lyu et al. 2013
;
Wen et al. 2018
)
. A variety of optimization techniques such as the local quadratic approximation or majorization-minimization algorithms
(
Lange et al. 2000
;
Hunter and Li 2005
)
, and various flavors of coordinate or subgradient descent methods have been discussed in the literature
(see
Wen et al. 2018
, for a survey of optimization methods for non-convex regularized problems)
.

The need for specialized optimization routines for non-smooth and non-convex regularized optimization problems has arguably hindered the widespread use of
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularization, despite their favorable theoretical properties and the limitations of convex regularizers
(
Freijeiro-González et al. 2022
, see, e.g.,)
. In contrast, smooth first-order methods have become the go-to optimization tool for many researchers and practitioners, not limited to the field of DL anymore. This can be attributed to their applicability to a vast class of problems using automatic differentiation, their scalability to large data sets, and their surprising effectiveness despite using only cheaply computed gradient information.
While in practice, popular DL platforms offer implementations of
\ell_{1}
regularization, this essentially reduces to applying stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to a non-differentiable problem. Unsurprisingly, this mismatch typically results in oscillating parameter updates, slow convergence, and a failure of parameter iterates to approach zero values (see Figure
6
).

1.2
Our Contributions

To overcome the obstacles and complexities of using optimization routines tailored for specific non-smooth and potentially non-convex regularized problems, we apply a smooth variational form (SVF) that allows expressing the non-smooth regularizer as the constrained minimum of a smooth surrogate regularizer, where the constraint involves an overparametrization of model parameters. In our framework, we construct a general template for exact smooth surrogate optimization of non-smooth and potentially non-convex regularized problems. This optimization transfer is based on finding SVFs of the respective regularizers, which entail a smooth parametrization map together with a smooth surrogate regularizer. Combined, an equivalent smooth surrogate objective can be constructed. Specifically, we

•

provide a comprehensive
review of the loosely connected works on Hadamard parametrizations, relating literature across DL, statistics, and optimization.

•

introduce a smooth surrogate optimization framework for non-smooth and non-convex regularization of arbitrary parameters, including a matching local minima property. While previous works often exploit properties particular to their setting, our main results (Thm.
2.10
and Lemma
2.4
-
2.9
) are stated broadly and hold for arbitrary losses, learning models, and regularizers given our assumptions.

•

apply our template method to a wide array of (group-)sparse
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularized problems, expanding the collection of sparsity-inducing parametrizations to Hadamard powers and shared parameters.

•

present different SVFs with variable amounts of overparametrization for the same induced regularizer, highlighting that it is not overparametrization
per se
inducing sparsity, but rather its effect on the curvature of the loss landscape.

•

identify parametrizations with specific neural network structures, generalizing previous findings on linear models to modular components within arbitrary networks. This enables the integration of sparse regularization into the prevalent SGD-based optimization paradigm in DL using sparse “drop-in” replacements.

•

evaluate our smooth optimization transfer approach on various sparse learning applications and demonstrate its correctness and practical feasibility.

1.3
Related Work

In recent years, parametrizations based on Hadamard products have attracted considerable interest in several fields, including DL, statistics, signal processing, and optimization.

DL literature
In the theoretical DL community, the surge in activity can be ascribed to the correspondence of Hadamard product-based parametrizations of linear models and simple, easy-to-analyze network architectures with linear activations
(
Tibshirani 2021
;
Dai et al. 2021
)
, predominantly studied under the name of diagonal linear networks
(
Gunasekar et al. 2018
;
Gissin et al. 2019
;
Pesme et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Even et al. 2023
;
Wang and Jacot 2023
)
, as well as similar stylized architecture for structured sparsity
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
. These networks are primarily analyzed in the context of implicit regularization effects and the representation cost of neural networks. The first phenomenon studies initialization and trajectory-based regularization effects of (S)GD without any explicit regularization term
(
Vaskevicius et al. 2019
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
)
, whereas the latter is concerned with measuring the cost that is required for a DNN to represent particular functions in terms of norms of network weights
(
Dai et al. 2021
;
Jacot et al. 2022
)
. Implicit regularization through Hadamard product-based overparametrization of linear models was further extended to robust and sparse linear regression in
Ma and Fattahi 2022
using subgradient descent. In the absence of explicit regularization,
Chou et al. 2023
study the implicit regularization of two variants of Hadamard parametrizations on gradient flow under vanishing initialization, obtaining improved sample complexity for compressed sensing problems. The “rich” gradient dynamics
(
Woodworth et al. 2020
)
caused by identical small initialization are further developed for overparametrized non-negative least squares problems in
Chou et al. 2022
. Contrasting our explicit surrogate regularization, an important shortcoming of implicit regularization approaches is that it is limited to convex
\ell_{q}
norms for
q\geq 1
for common losses such as the square loss, ruling out non-convex
\ell_{q}
regularization for
q<1

(
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Nacson et al. 2022
)
.

Statistics literature
The implicit
\ell_{1}
regularization effect of applying a simple Hadamard product parametrization to the parameters of a linear model under vanishing initialization and GD was studied by
Zhao et al. 2022
. Under the name “neuronized priors”,
Shin and Liu 2022
study similar parameter factorizations in a Bayesian modeling framework.

Signal processing literature

Li et al. 2023a
recently applied the HPP to solve the tail-
\ell_{1}
problem in compressed sensing. In a more general setting,
Yang et al. 2022
, and subsequently
Parhi and Nowak 2023
, analyze the sparse functional representations learned by
\ell_{2}
regularized DNNs with homogeneous activation functions from a signal processing perspective, employing a similar line of reasoning to our work to show the equivalence of group sparse
\ell_{2,1}
and surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization using their Neural Balance Theorem. Similarly, starting with
Neyshabur et al. 2015a
;
Neyshabur et al. 2015b
, several works advanced the understanding of
\ell_{2}
regularized networks and the inductive biases in the learned representations
(
Pilanci and Ergen 2020
;
Ergen and Pilanci 2021a
;
Ergen and Pilanci 2021b
;
Jagadeesan et al. 2022
, see, e.g.)
.

Optimization literature

Micchelli et al. 2013
study the optimization of convex regularizers such as the
\ell_{1}
penalty by smoothly approximating the absolute value using a quadratic variational formulation of the regularizer involving an additional surrogate parameter
\bm{\eta}
. This concept is similarly discussed in
Bach et al. 2012
under the umbrella term sub-quadratic norms. Formal connections between the so-called
\bm{\eta}
-trick and the Hadamard product (over)parametrizations studied in
Hoff 2017
are established for convex and lower semicontinuous proper loss functions in
Poon and Peyré 2021
, who subsequently leverage Hadamard parametrizations to smooth bilevel programming
(
Poon and Peyré 2023
)
. Recently,
Ouyang et al. 2024
study smooth
\ell_{1}
regularization using Hadamard parametrizations, and derive the surrogate Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz exponent at second-order stationary points from that of the original objective. Another branch of literature in optimization that is related to our approach is the perspective functions framework, a versatile tool for constructing proximal methods
(
Combettes and Müller 2018
;
Combettes and Müller 2020
)
.

Explicit regularization and our work
We extend the literature on approximation-free, differentiable optimization for sparse regularization using a combination of overparametrization and surrogate regularization. An early connection between
\ell_{1}
and an adaptive variant of
\ell_{2}
regularization was first observed by
Grandvalet 1998
. In statistics, the basic idea was re-discovered for a restricted problem class by
Hoff 2017
, however, without noting its compatibility with simple SGD or considering additional parametrizations. Their suggested optimization routine employing alternating ridge regressions thus prevents its application beyond linear models.

Tibshirani 2021
studies similar parametrizations in linear models and finds that they have identical global minima to certain weight-decayed network architectures (cf. Figure
3
). Notably, a simple weight-decayed diagonal linear network with one hidden layer has the same global minimum as the lasso, which, in turn, is equivalent to applying the Hadamard product parametrization and
\ell_{2}
regularization. This observation can also be implicitly inferred from the representation cost analysis of the same architecture presented in
Dai et al. 2021
. Building on the results of
Hoff 2017
,
Ziyin and Wang 2023
propose smooth surrogate
\ell_{1}
regularization for DNN weights, obtaining feature selection and network compression using SGD.

Previous works, however, exhibit limitations, including focusing only on single parametrizations (cf. Table
2
), applying the parametrization to all model parameters instead of arbitrary subsets, or considering only linear models.
Further, we place particular emphasis on ensuring matching local minima as a crucial property to preserve structure in the overparametrized problem, which aligns with the work of
Levin 2020
and
Nouiehed and Razaviyayn 2022
.

Set-Up for Transfer and Theoretical Results

Notation
We represent vectors using bold lowercase letters and bold capital letters for matrices. We use
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the parameter vector which is subject to regularization, and
\bm{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}
for the remaining parameters, so that all model parameters are collected in
(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
. We make this notational distinction to emphasize that our approach can be applied to arbitrary subsets of parameters of an optimization problem, irrespective of the presence of other parameters or the structure of the main objective
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, sparse regularization using our smooth optimization transfer framework can be applied to, e.g., specific layers of a neural network. Further, let
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{q}\triangleq(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\beta_{j}|^{q})^{1/q}

denote the
\ell_{q}
norm
\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},q\in(0,\infty)
. Note that for
0<q<1
, only a quasi-norm is defined as the subadditivity does not hold. For
q=0
, the
\ell_{0}
“norm” penalty
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
counts the number of non-zero elements in
\bm{\beta}
. Given a partition
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
of
[d]\triangleq\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the
\ell_{p,q}
group (quasi-)norm is defined as
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{p,q}\triangleq(\sum_{j=1}^{L}(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{j}}\left|\beta_{i}\right|^{p})^{q/p})^{1/q}=(\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{p}^{q})^{1/q}\,\forall\,\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,p,q>0
, where
\bm{\beta}_{j}
contains the components corresponding to
{\mathcal{G}_{j}}
. The regularization term for an
\ell_{p,q}
penalty is given by the
q
-th power of the
\ell_{p,q}
mixed-norm,
\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{p,q}^{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{p}^{q}
.
Further, we use various notations to define Hadamard product-like operations, introduced in the following. Let
\odot:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the classical Hadamard product, defined as
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\mapsto(u_{1}v_{1},\ldots,u_{d}v_{d})^{\top}
,
and
\bigodot_{l=1}^{k}\bm{u}_{l}
the Hadamard product of
k
vectors, for which we also use the shorthand notation
\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}
. For parameter vectors with more than one index, e.g.,
\bm{u}_{jl}^{\odot k}
, the Hadamard product is always taken over the second index. The self-Hadamard product
\bm{u}\odot\bm{u}
is simply written as
\bm{u}^{2}
. A generalization of the self-Hadamard product to non-integer exponents
k>0
, i.e., element-wise raising the entries of
\bm{u}
to the
k
-th power, is denoted as
\bm{u}^{\circ k}
. Given a partition
\mathcal{G}
of
[d]
into
L\leq d
subsets, we define the group Hadamard product
\odotg
of two vectors
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
as
\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}\triangleq(\bm{u}_{j}\nu_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{G}}
, or more explicitly as

\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{u}_{L}\end{pmatrix}\odot\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{1}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{1}|}\\
\vdots\\
\nu_{L}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{L}|}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
denotes the
1
-vector of size
|\mathcal{G}_{j}|
. To make the distinction between vectors of size
d
and
L
more clear where necessary, we denote vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\bm{v}
, and alternatively, use
\bm{\nu}
for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{L}
. In case
\bm{v}=(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{L})^{\top}
is constant within groups
{\mathcal{G}_{j}}
, both are related as
\bm{v}_{j}=\nu_{j}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
for
j=1,\ldots,L
, and
\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
equals the group Hadamard product
\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}
. Further,
\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta},\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is used to denote an open ball with radius
\varepsilon
centered at
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for a Euclidean space endowed with the standard topology induced by the Euclidean metric. Given a differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},\,\bm{a}\mapsto f(\bm{a})
, the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{a}}f(\bm{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
f
at
\bm{a}
contains partial derivatives
\partial f(\bm{a})/\partial a_{j}
for
j\in[m]
. The Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{f}(\bm{a})
of
f
at
\bm{a}
is the
m\times m
matrix containing second partial derivatives
(\mathcal{H}_{f}(\bm{a}))_{ij}\triangleq\partial^{2}f/\partial a_{i}\partial a_{j}
.
For vector-valued differentiable maps
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{a}\mapsto\bm{f}(\bm{a})
, the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{a})
of
\bm{f}
at
\bm{a}
is the
n\times m
matrix containing partial derivatives
(\mathcal{J}_{\bm{f}}(\bm{a}))_{ij}\triangleq\partial\bm{f}_{i}/\partial a_{j}
. Local solutions to an optimization problem over
(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
are denoted by
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
. Finally, missing proofs are deferred to the appendix.

Set-up

Before discussing the applications of our proposed framework to specific sparse regularizers, we first provide a number of general results on the equivalence of (regularized) optimization problems under smooth parametrization and a change of penalties, which will be applied throughout the paper. Let

\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\,(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})

(2)

denote the regularized objective function in its base parametrization
(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\bm{\beta}
is an arbitrary subset of all model parameters, and
\bm{\psi}
comprises the complementary components. Further, let
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
be a smooth and potentially non-convex objective. Although this definition comprises a wide range of widely used loss functions, we assume a smooth
\mathcal{L}
mainly to emphasize the transfer from a non-smooth
\mathcal{P}
to an equivalent smooth problem that becomes amenable to smooth first-order optimization methods.

\mathcal{D}\triangleq\left\{\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}

(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Here,

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\,,\,\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
denote generic feature and label spaces,
\mathscr{L}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is an arbitrary loss contribution, and the (parametric) model
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is parametrized by
(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
. The non-smooth and potentially non-convex regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0},\,\bm{\beta}\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\,,

(3)

with
\lambda\geq 0
controlling the amount of regularization. To be precise, given a partition
\mathcal{G}
of parameter indices
[d]
into
L
groups, we here consider regularizers
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
of the form

{\textstyle\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\in\Big\{\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\,,\bm{\beta}\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{L}\omega_{j}\left\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\right\rVert_{p}^{q}\;\big|\;0<q\leq p\leq 2,\;\omega_{j}>0\;\forall\,j\Big\}}\,.

(4)

Note that setting

\omega_{j}=1\,\forall\,j
reduces the expression to the familiar
\ell_{q}

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{q}^{q}

\omega_{j}=1

\ell_{p,q}

\omega_{j}=|\mathcal{G}_{j}|\in\mathbb{N},p=2,q=1

Smooth optimization transfer
To transfer the non-smooth optimization of
\mathcal{P}
to a smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
, first consider a parametrization of
\bm{\beta}
defined by
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}
for
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
. As the parametrizations considered in this paper are based on Hadamard products and variations thereof, they can be described by the following set of assumptions:

Assumption 1

(Parametrization map
\mathcal{K}
)
.

The parametrization map
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\xi}\mapsto\bm{\beta}
is a smooth surjection with the following properties:

a)

\mathcal{K}
is block-separable, i.e., for a partition of
[d]
into
L\leq d
groups of size
|{\mathcal{G}_{j}}|,j\in[L]
, the corresponding
\bm{\beta}_{j}
are parametrized by disjoint subsets
\bm{\xi}_{j}
of the entries of
\bm{\xi}
. That is,
\mathcal{K}
is the Cartesian function product
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=(\mathcal{K}_{1}(\bm{\xi}_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{L}(\bm{\xi}_{L}))
of block-wise parametrizations
\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})=\bm{\beta}_{j}
.

b)

All
\bm{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
are regular values of
\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
for each
j\in[L]
, i.e., the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}_{j}}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
has full rank
|\mathcal{G}_{j}|
for all
\bm{\xi}_{j}\in\mathcal{K}_{j}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
.

c)

\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
is positively homogeneous of degree (here “depth”)
k>1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{j}(c\,\bm{\xi}_{j})=c^{k}\,\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
for all
\bm{\xi}_{j}
and
c>0
, ensuring the fibers of
\mathcal{K}
are scaled versions of each other at regular values
\bm{\beta}_{j}
.

d)

Each
\bm{\xi}_{j}
is a block vector containing
k
Hadamard factors
\bm{\xi}_{jl},l\in[k]
, so that
\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j1},\ldots,\bm{\xi}_{jk})
has the zero-product property, i.e.,
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{0}
if and only if
\bm{\xi}_{jl}=\bm{0}
for at least one
l\in[k]
.

Note that the degree of homogeneity
k
mentioned in c) coincides with the number of Hadamard factors
k
in d) for all (monomial) parametrizations that only contain Hadamard factors with unit exponents, comprising all parametrizations considered here except those employing parameter sharing or Hadamard powers.
Moreover, we define a smooth and strongly convex surrogate regularization function
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0},\bm{\xi}\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
. Throughout this work, each surrogate regularizer is taken to be a weighted sum of
\ell_{2}
penalties of components of
\bm{\xi}
, i.e., is of the form

{\textstyle\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\in\Big\{\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\xi}}}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\;\bm{\xi}\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{{d_{\xi}}}\tilde{\omega}_{j}\;\xi_{j}^{2}\;\big|\;\tilde{\omega}_{j}>0\;\forall\,j\Big\}}\,.

(5)

Together, these define our proposed optimization transfer approach to construct an equivalent smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
from the non-smooth objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
:

Definition 2.1

(Smooth optimization transfer)
.

We define a smooth optimization transfer for an objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
as in (
2
), with non-smooth regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
as in (
4
), as the tuple
(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}})
, where
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is a smooth and surjective parametrization of
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
is a smooth surrogate penalty term imposed on
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
, as defined in (
5
). The smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
can then be constructed from
(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}})
by first applying the parametrization
\bm{\beta}=\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
to obtain the “lifted” objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
, and further substituting
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\,(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\,.

(6)

The next definition explicitly states our notion of equivalence between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
:

Definition 2.2

(Equivalence of optimization problems)
.

We say the two optimization problems

\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\beta}}{\text{minimize}}\;\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\quad\;\text{and}\;\quad\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\xi}}{\text{minimize}}\;\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})\,,

as defined in (
2
) and (
6
), are equivalent if the following conditions hold:

a)

\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\beta}}{\inf}\;\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\xi}}{\inf}\,\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
, i.e., their globally optimal values coincide.

b)

If
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
, then there is a local minimizer
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})
of
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
with
\hat{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\hat{\bm{\beta}})
and
\mathcal{Q}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})=\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
.

c)

If
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
, then
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
with
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
and
\mathcal{Q}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})=\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
.

Equivalence of local minima is particularly important for non-convex regularization, encompassed by the second and third conditions, as finding global minima in non-convex optimization is challenging and for the most part intractable. Moreover, in non-convex regularization, local minima have been observed to generalize similarly or even better than global minima on test data
(
Chartrand and Yin 2008
;
Chartrand 2007
;
Olsson et al. 2017
)
.

This so-called matching of local minima
(
Levin 2020
)
ensures that the transfer from the non-smooth objective
\mathcal{P}
to a smooth objective
\mathcal{Q}
preserves such properties. By focusing only on global minima, important information about the structure of problem structure is neglected. Importantly, we do not introduce spurious local minima in
\mathcal{Q}
, which would artificially increase the difficulty of the optimization problem. Through the matching property, we can further use the surjection
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
to reconstruct all (local) minimizers of
\mathcal{P}
from local minimizers of
\mathcal{Q}
as
\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}})=\hat{\bm{\beta}}
. To guarantee this matching of local minima property for
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
under the parametrization
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
, local openness of the parametrization mapping
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
at all local minimizers
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
is a crucial property
(
Nouiehed and Razaviyayn 2022
)
.
Levin et al. 2024
show that it is both a necessary and sufficient condition for the preservation of local minima under smooth parametrization.

Local openness is closely related to, but distinct from the notion of continuity, which is defined as
\forall\,\varepsilon>0\,\exists\,\delta>0:\,\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B(\bm{\xi})},\delta)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}),\varepsilon)
using the same notation.

Definition 2.3

(Local openness)
.

A mapping
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\bm{\xi}\mapsto\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is locally open at
\bm{\xi}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
we can find
\delta>0
such that
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}),\delta)\subseteq\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{B}(\bm{\xi},\varepsilon))
. Further, the map
\mathcal{K}
is called globally open if it is locally open at all
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
.

General theoretical results

Using the function characterizations encapsulated in Definition
2.1
, we can now prove the following results. Note that these hold for any continuous, potentially unregularized objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
under reparametrization:

Lemma 2.4
.

If
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
, and
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is a continuous surjection, then all
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})
such that
\hat{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\hat{\bm{\beta}})
are local minimizers of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
with
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}))
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
, and the continuous surjection
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is locally open at
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
, then
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}))=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
with
\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=\mathcal{P}(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}))
.

Their proofs are given in Appendices
A.1
and
A.2
. Together, both results show that the set of local minima of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
and
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
are equal if
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is locally open at all local minimizers of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
, and the local minimizers are related via
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\mathcal{K}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}))=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
. Still, for non-smooth regularizers, smoothly parametrizing
\bm{\beta}
will not result in a smooth optimization problem. However, the results using local openness of the map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
can be applied to guarantee matching local minima under smooth and surjective parametrizations in general problems, e.g., for parametrizations used in the implicit regularization literature.

In our smooth optimization transfer approach, however, we further replace the parametrized regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
by the surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
to obtain the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
. The smooth surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}
and the non-smooth regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}
are related through an SVF, defined as follows:

Definition 2.6

(Smooth variational form)
.

A smooth variational form is an expression of a function
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
as the minimum of a smooth surrogate
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
over a feasible set given by the fiber
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\bm{\beta})
of a surjective parametrization map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
at
\bm{\beta}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=\min_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(7)

We further require stability of the solutions
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})
to the SVF with respect to the constraint parameter
\bm{\beta}
, i.e., continuous dependence of the minimizers
\hat{\bm{\xi}}\in\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
on
\bm{\beta}
, formalized through upper hemicontinuity of the set-valued solution mapping:

Definition 2.7

(Upper hemicontinuity)
.

A set-valued map
\hat{\bm{\xi}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}},\,\bm{\beta}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})
is said to be upper hemicontinuous (u.h.c.) at
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
\forall\,\varepsilon>0\,\exists\,\delta>0:\,\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta},\delta))\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}),\varepsilon)
. Equivalently, for all
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta},\delta)
, it holds that all
\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\in\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})
belong to
\mathcal{B}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}),\varepsilon)
. Note that an
\varepsilon
-ball
\mathcal{B}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}),\varepsilon)
around a set
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
is defined as
\bigcup_{\bm{\xi}\in\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})}\mathcal{B}(\bm{\xi},\varepsilon)
.

This allows us to formulate the assumptions of our optimization transfer approach regarding the SVF. Note that finding the appropriate SVF is non-trivial and will be derived for each presented regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}
in the respective section.

Assumption 2

(Smooth variational form with continuous solution)
.

a)

The minimum of the surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
over the the fiber of the smooth surjection
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
at
\bm{\beta}
, is given by

\min_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thus,
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
and
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
define a smooth variational form for the non-smooth regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
according in Definition
2.6
.

b)

The smooth surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
majorizes the parametrized non-smooth regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\geq\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))\forall\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
.

c)

The set-valued solution map
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}},\,\bm{\beta}\mapsto\underset{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
is u.h.c.

Instead of requiring local openness, as previously for smooth parametrizations without a change of regularizers, we use the upper hemicontinuity of the solution map
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})
in Assumption
2
, a property that is easily obtained as a by-product in the construction of our smooth variational forms. For details on set-valued analysis, we refer to
Aubin and Frankowska 2009

\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
and
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})

If
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})

Theorem 2.10

(Smooth optimization transfer for sparse regularization)
.

Let the non-smooth objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
and its smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
be defined as in Equations (

1
and
2

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\beta}}{\text{minimize}}\;\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\underset{\bm{\psi},\,\bm{\xi}}{\text{minimize}}\;\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})

Proof.

, we show that the infima of both
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
and
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
coincide. Because
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))

\mathcal{K}

\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})=\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi}}\big\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\big\}=\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta}}\big\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\inf_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\}\big\}

By Assumption
2

\inf_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})=\min_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
, and thus

\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})=\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta}}\left\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\right\}=\inf_{\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\,.

This shows the first point. For the second and third points of Definition
2.2

2.9

In this section, we introduce two smooth surrogate approaches for sparsity-inducing
\ell_{1}

3.1
Hadamard Product Parametrization

We first present a canonical example of our optimization transfer framework based on the so-called Hadamard product parametrization
(
Hoff 2017
)
. This approach enables smooth optimization of
\ell_{1}
regularized objectives by applying an overparametrization
\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
and imposing
\ell_{2}
regularization on the surrogate parameters. As the prototype case of our framework, this connection between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
regularization under reparametrization will be briefly re-derived in the following. Assume a non-smooth
\ell_{1}
regularized objective
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=2\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
and consider the following overparametrized smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\,(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+2\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+2\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\beta_{j}\right|}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\psi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0},\,(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{v})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}\big)}\,.

(9)

In (
9
), the HPP map is defined as

\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u},\bm{v})\mapsto\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}=\bm{\beta}\,,

(10)

while the surrogate penalty is the plain
\ell_{2}
regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
with
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{u},\bm{v})^{\top}
. Our goal is to show that the minimization of (
8
) and (
9
) is equivalent according to Definition
2.2
. In our smooth optimization transfer framework, the main assumption of Theorem
2.10
requires that the HPP
\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
and the surrogate regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}
together define an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=2\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
(cf. Definition
2.6
). The inequality of arithmetic and geometric means (AM-GM) provides a simple but powerful tool for the construction of SVFs using
\ell_{2}
regularization as the surrogate penalty and is repeatedly applied throughout the paper. The AM-GM inequality states that, given a list of
n\in\mathbb{N}
non-negative numbers
x_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n
, it holds that
\frac{x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}}{n}\geq\sqrt[n]{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}}
with equality if and only if
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{n}
.

In the case of the HPP, it allows us to determine the minimum of the surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}
under the constraint
\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}=\bm{\beta}
for any
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given the parametrization map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
, the minimum of surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
subject to
\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}=\bm{\beta}
constitutes an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=2\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
in (
8
) and is given by

\min_{\bm{u},\bm{v}:\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}=\bm{\beta}}\quad\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}=2\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}\;\,\,\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Because the HPP defines element-wise multiplication, we can minimize
u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}
such that
u_{j}v_{j}=\beta_{j}
for some
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
j=1,\ldots,d
. Using the AM-GM inequality for
n=2
and the non-negative numbers
u_{j}^{2}
and
v_{j}^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}{2}\geq\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}v_{j}^{2}}=\sqrt{(u_{j}v_{j})^{2}}=\sqrt{\beta_{j}^{2}}=|\beta_{j}|\,,

which reduces to equality if and only if
u_{j}^{2}=v_{j}^{2}
, yielding a minimum value of
u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}=2|\beta_{j}|
. Repeating this procedure for all
j=1,\ldots,d
shows that the constrained minimum of the surrogate penalty is indeed equal to
2\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
for all
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

The optimality conditions
u_{j}^{2}=v_{j}^{2}=|\beta_{j}|
further ensure that we can derive continuous solutions
(\hat{u}_{j},\hat{v}_{j})
as functions of
\beta_{j}=u_{j}v_{j}
. Analytically,
(\hat{u}_{j},\hat{v}_{j})
are of the form

\underset{(u_{j},v_{j}):u_{j}v_{j}=\beta_{j}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}=\begin{cases}\left(\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|}\right)\;\text{and}\;\left(-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|}\right)&\text{for $\beta_{j}>0$}\\
(0,0)&\text{for $\beta_{j}=0$}\\
\left(\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|}\right)\;\text{and}\;\left(-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|}\right)&\text{for $\beta_{j}<0$}\,.\end{cases}

Further, we can determine the number of equivalent solutions in the surrogate problem, corresponding to a specific solution in the original problem, using the AM-GM inequality. Due to this duplicity for each
j=1,\ldots,d
, there are a total of
2^{s}
equivalent local minimizers
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}},\hat{\bm{v}})
of
\mathcal{Q}
for each local minimizer
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
of
\mathcal{P}
, where
s=||\hat{\bm{\beta}}||_{0}
.
Moreover, we can establish the stability of a solution mapping in a more general setting for solutions that are characterized by necessary optimality conditions similar to the above, obtained from applying the AM-GM inequality to the squared surrogate parameters
u_{j}^{2}
and
v_{j}^{2}
for
j\in[d]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}},\,\bm{\beta}\mapsto\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
, be a set-valued solution mapping, where the components of
\bm{\beta}
can be separated into
j=1,\ldots,L\leq d
groups with independent corresponding solution parameters. Within each group vector
\bm{\beta}_{j}
, the solution parameter vectors in
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
can be further grouped into factors
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{jl}
for
l=1,\ldots,k
. Assume we are given necessary optimality conditions
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})\subseteq\{\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}:\,\|\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j1}\|_{2}^{2}=\ldots=\|\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{jk}\|_{2}^{2}=T(\bm{\beta}_{j})\}
for all
j=1,\ldots,L
, where
T(\bm{\beta}_{j})
is a continuous scalar-valued function that is
0
if and only if
\bm{\beta}_{j}=0
and strictly increasing in
\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}
. Then the solution map is upper hemicontinuous at all
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Note that for the simple HPP, we have
L=d
and
k=2
, so that the optimality conditions simplify to
u_{j}^{2}=v_{j}^{2}=T(\beta_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, where
T(\beta_{j})=|\beta_{j}|
.

Corollary 3.3
.

The optimization of
\mathcal{P}
in (
8
) is equivalent to the optimization of the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
in (
9
) by Definition
2.2
, and solutions to the base problem can be constructed as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}}\odot\hat{\bm{v}})
.

Proof.

The result directly follows by combining Lemma
3.1
with Theorem
2.10
and observing that
\mathcal{K}
conforms to the requirements on a parametrization map.
∎

For brevity, this proof will be omitted from now. Inspired by the implicitly regularized elastic net
Zhao et al. 2022
, we propose the following explicitly regularized smooth variant:

Remark 3.4
.

(Smooth elastic net formulation via HPP)
We can readily extend the HPP optimization transfer for
\ell_{1}
regularization to the Elastic Net penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq(1-\alpha)\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}+\alpha\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, as introduced in
Zou and Hastie 2005
. To do this, we merely redefine
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})\triangleq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\alpha\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
\lambda\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\lambda(1-\alpha)\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
. Applying the HPP, we minimize

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})+\lambda\alpha\left\lVert\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda(1-\alpha)}{2}(\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})

over
(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{v})
instead of
(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
.
Solutions to the Elastic Net-regularized problem can be reconstructed after optimization of the smooth surrogate as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}}\odot\hat{\bm{v}})
.

Further, for general smoothly parametrized objectives
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))
using the HPP, one requirement for equivalence to
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
by Lemma
2.5
is that the parametrization
\mathcal{K}
is locally open at the local minimizers
(\hat{\bm{u}},\hat{\bm{v}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))
. In fact, the Hadamard product of two
d
-dimensional real-valued vectors is a (uniformly) open map everywhere
(
Balcerzak et al. 2016
)
, meaning that under
\mathcal{K}
, the image of any open ball around
(\bm{u},\bm{v})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
contains an open ball around
(\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
(cf. Def.
2.3
).

3.2
The Hadamard Difference Parametrization

An alternative smooth optimization transfer approach for
\ell_{1}
regularization is based on the Hadamard difference parametrization (HDP), which is defined as

\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})\mapsto\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}\odot\bm{\delta}=\bm{\beta}\,.

(11)

This variant of the HPP is often employed in studying the implicit regularization effects of GD in linear neural networks and is sometimes referred to as an “unbiased” diagonal network of depth
2

(
Woodworth et al. 2020
)
. This description is used because the HDP allows for a
balanced
non-zero initialization of
(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})
with weights of equal magnitude, e.g.,
(\bm{\gamma}_{0},\bm{\delta}_{0})=(\alpha\mathds{1}_{d},\alpha\mathds{1}_{d})
for some small constant
\alpha>0
, that results in a zero initialization of
\bm{\beta}
. In contrast, for the HPP, a zero initialization of
\bm{\beta}
either requires an unbalanced initialization
\bm{u}_{0}\neq\bm{v}_{0}
or setting
(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{v}_{0})=(\bm{0},\bm{0})
, which initializes the optimization process exactly at a saddle point, resulting in GD to get stuck as gradient updates are zero for all iterations. This can be circumvented using the HDP, facilitating an easier theoretical analysis by enabling a balanced zero initialization
(
Vaskevicius et al. 2019
;
Vivien et al. 2022
, see, e.g.,)
.
3
3

3

On the other hand,
Even et al. 2023
show that using the HPP with initialization
(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{v}_{0})=(\sqrt{2}\alpha\mathds{1}_{d},\bm{0})
yields identical optimization trajectories to the HDP initialization described above.
In our framework, applying the HDP and imposing explicit surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization on
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\delta}
corresponds to
\ell_{1}
regularization of
\bm{\beta}
, albeit without the scaling factor of
2
present in the HPP. To establish a connection between the HDP and the HPP, let
\bm{\gamma}=\frac{\bm{u}+\bm{v}}{2}
and
\bm{\delta}=\frac{\bm{v}-\bm{u}}{2}
, or equivalently,
\bm{u}=\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{\gamma}+\bm{\delta}
. Then it is easy to confirm that
\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}\odot\bm{\delta}=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
.

For an objective
\mathcal{P}
with
\ell_{1}
regularization of
\bm{\beta}
, we can construct a smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
applying the HDP and surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization. Both objectives can be written as

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\beta_{j}\right|}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\gamma}^{2}-\bm{\delta}^{2})+\lambda(\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\gamma}^{2}-\bm{\delta}^{2})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\gamma_{j}^{2}+\delta_{j}^{2}\big)}.

(13)

To show equivalence of the smooth surrogate, we first establish that
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})=\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
together define an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given the parametrization map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})=\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}\odot\bm{\delta}
as defined in Equation (
11
), the minimum of the surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta}))=\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
subject to
\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}\odot\bm{\delta}=\bm{\beta}
constitutes an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{1}
in (
12
) and is given by

\min_{\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta}:\bm{\gamma}^{2}-\bm{\delta}^{2}=\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(14)

For each
\beta_{j}
in
\bm{\beta}
, either
\gamma_{j}^{2}
or
\delta_{j}^{2}
must equal zero at the minimum, depending on the sign of
\beta_{j}
, with the square of the second parameter being equal to
|\beta_{j}|
. The minimizers
(\hat{\gamma}_{j},\hat{\delta}_{j})
hence form a continuous set-valued function of
\beta_{j}
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Optimization of
\mathcal{P}
(
12
) is equivalent to optimization of the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
(
13
), and solutions to the
\mathcal{P}
can be constructed as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{2}-\hat{\bm{\delta}}^{2})
.

For the preservation of local minima in general, potentially unregularized objectives
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
under a smooth parametrization using the HDP, Lemma
2.5
again requires local openness of the HDP at local minimizers
(\hat{\gamma},\hat{\delta})
of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta}))
. Recall that rotating a point
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
45^{\circ}
clockwise about the origin defines the transformation
(\gamma,\delta)\triangleq(\frac{u+v}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{2}})
. Evaluating the HDP at the rotated point yields
\gamma^{2}-\delta^{2}=2uv
, showing that the HDP constitutes a rotation of the HPP scaled by a factor of
2
, with both actions preserving the openness. Details on the difference between the HPP and HDP can be found in Appendix
C.1
.

regularized linear regression under smooth
\ell_{2}
regularization of the weights. Figure
3
shows two such linear networks, with the diagonal network corresponding to the HPP on the left, and the diagonal network corresponding to the HDP in the middle. This correspondence, however, is not limited to overparametrized linear models. For example, we can “stretch out” a network architecture by inserting additional diagonal layers at certain locations, promoting localized sparse representations. More generally, we can overparametrize any layer of a DNN by replacing its weights
\bm{\beta}
by
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
. Imposing suitable surrogate regularization on the weights
\bm{\xi}
of the parametrized layer then induces sparse regularization of the original layer in the base parametrization.

Geometric intuition

A graphical analysis of our optimization transfer approach for
\ell_{1}
regularization using the HPP provides additional insights into the underlying geometry. Figure

\ell_{2}
penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}
over
\{(\bm{u},\bm{v}):\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}=\bm{\beta}\}
equals
2\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
.
The setting in Figure

shows the HPP
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
(blue/green), the majorizing surrogate
\ell_{2}
penalty (orange), as well as the feasible set defined by the fiber
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\bm{\beta})
(black hyperbola). In this example, we set
d=1
and fix
\beta=10
. Alternatively, we can interpret the plot as an illustration for only a single entry
\beta_{j}=10
,
j\in[d]
. The shape of
\mathcal{K}(u_{j},v_{j})=u_{j}v_{j}
is a hyperbolic paraboloid, and the fiber
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\beta_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
for
\beta_{j}=10
is obtained by intersecting
\mathcal{K}
with the horizontal plane
\beta_{j}=10
. The geometric shape of the resulting set is a rectangular hyperbola defined by
u_{j}(v_{j})=\frac{\beta_{j}}{v_{j}}
. This forms an unbounded feasible set in the constrained minimization problem stated in Lemma
3.1
.
Since the surrogate regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
defines an elliptic paraboloid for each
j\in[d]
, the constrained minimization problem is solved by searching the entry-wise feasible sets with respect to the smallest surrogate penalty, i.e., the smallest distance to the center of the paraboloid at level
\beta_{j}
,
(0,0,\beta_{j})
.
For a hyperbola defined by
u_{j}(v_{j})=\frac{\beta_{j}}{v_{j}},\beta_{j}>0
,
this is achieved at the vertices
(\sqrt{\beta_{j}},\sqrt{\beta_{j}})
and
(-\sqrt{\beta_{j}},-\sqrt{\beta_{j}})
, with a minimal distance of
\sqrt{2\beta_{j}}
. Similarly, for
\beta_{j}<0
, minimal distance of
\sqrt{2|\beta_{j}|}
is attained at
(-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|})
and
(\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|},-\sqrt{|\beta_{j}|})
. For
\beta_{j}=0
, the fiber
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(0)
contains all points on the coordinate axes, with 0 minimal distance at
(0,0)
.

The majorization property is visualized in Figure

. We further demonstrate how the proposed optimization transfer to an equivalent smooth surrogate transforms the loss landscape using a simple toy objective in Figure

and a more detailed visualization in Figure

.

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}

\ell_{1}
regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))=2\|\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}\|_{1}
, with equality of both regularizers achieved at the two vertices of the hyperbolas defined by the fibers
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\beta_{j})
for all non-zero values of
\beta_{j}
.

4
Hadamard Group Lasso for Structured Sparsity

In many applications, we have additional
a priori
structural information on the parameters, e.g., that certain gene pathways can only be jointly relevant or that a set of dummy-coded features representing a categorical variable should either be included in the model or fully selected out. To obtain structured sparsity, we make use of parametrization maps “tying together” groups of parameters through shared factors, with the property that adding smooth
\ell_{2}
regularization on the surrogate parameters induces an
\ell_{2,1}
(group lasso) penalty
2\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}
in the base parametrization
\bm{\beta}
.

Set-up for structured sparsity regularization

Let
[d]
denote the index set corresponding to the entries of
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define
\mathcal{G}_{j}=\big\{{j}_{1},\ldots,{j}_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}\big\}\subseteq[d]
to be the subsets of indices corresponding to groups
j=1,\ldots,L
. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{L}\}
form a partition of
[d]
, i.e.
\cup_{j=1}^{L}\mathcal{G}_{j}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}\cap\mathcal{G}_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
, so that
|\mathcal{G}_{1}|+\ldots+|\mathcal{G}_{L}|=d
. The parameter vector
\bm{\beta}
contains the group-wise vectors
\bm{\beta}_{j}
, i.e.,
\bm{\beta}=\left(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{L}\right)^{\top}
, where
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\big(\beta_{j_{1},\ldots,\beta_{j_{|{\mathcal{G}_{j}}|}}}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
for
j\in[L]
.

4.1
Group Hadamard Product Parametrization

For the group Hadamard product parametrization (GHPP), we again use the parametrization structure
\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
, but now with the elements of
\bm{v}
(and thus also
\bm{\beta}
) constrained to reflect the group membership. Noting that
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{1}|+\ldots+|\mathcal{G}_{L}|}
, the Hadamard factors are

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\left(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{L}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\,\bm{v}=\left(\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{L}\right)^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{1}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{1}|}\\
\vdots\\
\nu_{L}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{L}|}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\;.

Then we have
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{u}_{j}\odot\bm{v}_{j}=\nu_{j}\cdot(u_{{j}_{1}},\ldots,u_{{j}_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{G}_{j}}|}
for
j\in[L]
.

Note that in this parametrization, the second Hadamard factor
\bm{v}
is a
d
-dimensional vector containing values
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{L}
, where each
\nu_{j}
is repeated
|\mathcal{G}_{j}|
times in
\bm{v}
. Comparing this to the Hadamard factor
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})^{\top}
in the HPP, the
d
distinct entries of
\bm{v}
are replaced by entries that are constant within groups
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{L}
, thereby “tying” together the parameters in each
{\mathcal{G}_{j}}
. The first Hadamard factor
\bm{u}
remains unconstrained as in the HPP, i.e.,
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Letting
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
denote
(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{L})^{\top}
, the GHPP map is defined as:

\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{u}_{L}\end{pmatrix}\odot\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{1}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{1}|}\\
\vdots\\
\nu_{L}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{L}|}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\beta}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\beta}_{L}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{\beta}\,,

(15)

where we use the notation
\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}\triangleq(\bm{u}_{j}\nu_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{G}}
. Given an objective
\mathcal{P}
with non-smooth regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})=2\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{2,1}
, defining the surrogate regularization as
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})\triangleq\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\nu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
provides a smooth optimization transfer
(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}})
, from which we construct the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+2\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2,1}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+2\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{v})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})+\lambda(\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\nu}\|_{2}^{2})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{L}\big(\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}\big)}\,.

(17)

The functions
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}
are chosen so that we obtain an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}
:

Lemma 4.1
.

Given the parametrization map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}
, the minimum of the surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\nu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
subject to
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\bm{\beta}
constitutes an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
in (
16
) and is

\min_{\bm{u}_{j},\nu_{j}:\bm{\beta}_{j}=\nu_{j}\bm{u}_{j}}{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}=2\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}}\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

(18)

According to Lemma
3.2
, the optimality conditions
\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\nu_{j}^{2}=\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}
of the AM-GM inequality in the proof allow us to derive the minimizers
(\hat{\bm{u}}_{j},\hat{\nu}_{j})
as an upper hemicontinuous function of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
for all
\bm{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
:

\arg\hskip-1.42271pt\min_{\hskip-11.38092pt\begin{subarray}{c}(\bm{u}_{j},\nu_{j}):\\
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\nu_{j}\bm{u}_{j}\end{subarray}}\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\nu_{j}^{2}=\begin{cases}\pm\left(\bm{\beta}_{j}/\sqrt{\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}},\sqrt{\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}}\right),&\hskip-7.11317pt\text{$\left\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}>0$}\\
(\bm{0},0),&\hskip-7.11317pt\text{$\left\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}=0$}\\
\end{cases}

(19)

The tuple
(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}})
is thus a valid optimization transfer for
\ell_{2,1}
group sparsity:

Corollary 4.2
.

The optimization of
\mathcal{P}
in (
16
) is equivalent to the optimization of the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
in (
17
) by Definition
2.2
, and solutions to the base problem can be obtained as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}}\odotg\hat{\bm{\nu}})
.

Note that there are only two equivalent minimizers for each
\bm{\beta}_{j}
given
\left\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}>0
, as the sign of
\hat{\nu}_{j}
uniquely determines the sign of all
\hat{u}_{j_{i}}
in
\hat{\bm{u}}_{j}
for
i=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{G}_{j}|
. Thus, for each minimizer
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})
of
\mathcal{P}
, there will be
2^{s}
equivalent corresponding solutions
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}},\hat{\bm{\nu}})
to
\mathcal{Q}
, where
s
is the number of groups
\mathcal{G}_{j}
with
\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\|_{2}>0
.

For linear predictors, structure-inducing overparametrization was also studied in
Tibshirani 2021
and
Dai et al. 2021
, however, without proving the matching local minima property or going beyond linearity. Similar to the HPP approach to smooth
\ell_{1}
regularization, the GHPP corresponds to a particular network structure with linear activations and a grouping layer when applied to a linear model, as shown in Figure
3c
. The
\ell_{2}
regularized network then corresponds to a linear model with an
\ell_{2,1}
penalty.

Considering the preservation of local minima in a general objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
under smooth parametrization of
\bm{\beta}
using the GHPP, local openness of
\mathcal{K}
at the local solutions to
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
is a crucial requirement for Lemma
2.5
. This assumption, however, is not straightforward for the GHPP. While the Banach open mapping theorem states that every continuous linear surjection between Banach spaces is globally open, it is known that this openness principle can not be extended to
bilinear
continuous surjections
(
Horowitz 1975
;
Balcerzak et al. 2013
)
. A widely used counterexample of a bilinear continuous surjection that is not open everywhere is given, e.g., in
Rudin 1991
, corresponding to the GHPP for
L=1
and
d=2
. Therefore, as opposed to the HPP, the GHPP is not globally open in general. To establish matching local minima, we thus need to ensure that local solutions
(\hat{\bm{u}},\hat{\bm{\nu}})
to
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu}))
are indeed points of local openness:

Lemma 4.3

(Local openness of the GHPP)
.

The parametrization map defined by
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{L},\,(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})\mapsto\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}
is locally open at
(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})
, with
\bm{u}=(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{L})^{\top}
and
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{L})^{\top}
, if the
(\bm{u}_{j},\nu_{j})
are such that
\nu_{j}=0
implies
\left\lVert\bm{u}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}=0
for all
j\in[L]
.

Note that all minimizers of
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})
are of the form stated in (
19
), i.e., either
(\hat{\bm{u}}_{j},\hat{\nu}_{j})=(\bm{0},0)
, or the
(\hat{\bm{u}}_{j},\hat{\nu}_{j})
are such that
\left\lVert\hat{\bm{u}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}>0
and
|\hat{\nu}_{j}|>0
for all
j\in[L]
. Then, Lemma
4.3
shows that
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})
is locally open at all local solutions to
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})
.

4.2
Adjusting the GHPP for Variable Group Sizes

The well-known group lasso, initially proposed by
Yuan and Lin 2006
, does not employ plain
\ell_{2,1}
regularization, but includes additional weights accounting for the variable group sizes
|\mathcal{G}_{j}|,j\in[L]
. With this modification, we can define the non-smooth penalty as
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sqrt{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}\left\lVert\bm{\beta}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}
. Interestingly, this regularizer can be obtained as a simple extension to the previous approach by introducing a scaling factor in the surrogate penalty. The derivation is deferred to Appendix
A.9
. This results in the following smooth objective
\mathcal{Q}
and corresponding equivalent group lasso regularized objective
\mathcal{P}
:

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+2\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sqrt{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}}\,,

(20)

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{L}\big(\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+|\mathcal{G}_{j}|\nu_{j}^{2}\big)}.

(21)

Going Deeper: Non-Convex Regularization with Hadamard Product Parametrizations of Depth
k

The Hadamard product parametrizations factorizing
\bm{\beta}
using two factors
\bm{u},\bm{v}
can be naturally extended to deeper factorizations of depth
k>2,\,k\in\mathbb{N}
. For a suitable surrogate penalty, these parametrizations induce (a restricted class) of non-convex
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularizers for
0<q<1
and
0<q<p\leq 2
in the base parametrization
\bm{\beta}
.

5.1
Hadamard Product Parametrization of Depth
k

First, consider a multilinear extension of the bilinear HPP termed the
\text{HPP}_{k}
,

{\textstyle\mathcal{K}:\prod_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})\mapsto\bigodot_{l=1}^{k}\bm{u}_{l}=\bm{\beta}\,,}

(22)

where
\prod_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
k
th Cartesian power of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k>2
. The depth two case recovers the simple HPP (
10
). Each
\beta_{j},\,j\in[d]
, is parametrized as the product
\prod_{l=1}^{k}u_{jl}
, where each factor
u_{jl}
is taken from a different
\bm{u}_{l}
. Further, we define
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})\triangleq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\left\lVert\bm{u}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Then, minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})
subject to the constraint imposed by the parametrization map
\mathcal{K}
yields an SVF for non-convex
\ell_{q}
regularization with
q=2/k
:

Lemma 5.1
.

Given the parametrization map
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})=\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}
, the minimum surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\left\lVert\bm{u}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
subject to
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})=\bm{\beta}
constitutes an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq k\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{2/k}^{2/k}
and is given by

\min_{\bm{u}_{l}:\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}}{\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}}\|\bm{u}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}=k\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k}^{2/k}\,\,\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A visualization of the
\text{HPP}_{k}
for
k=3
can be found in Appendix
C.2
, illustrating the shape of the fibers of
\mathcal{K}
and the majorization of the non-smooth
\ell_{2/3}
penalty by the smooth surrogate
\ell_{2}
penalty. Given an objective
\mathcal{P}
with smooth
\mathcal{L}
and non-convex
\ell_{2/k}
regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
, applying the optimization transfer defined by
(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}})
yields the corresponding
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle{\textstyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda k\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k}^{2/k}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda k\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\beta_{j}\right|^{2/k}}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt{\textstyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})=\mathcal{L}\big(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}\big)+\lambda\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|\bm{u}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{L}\big(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}\big)+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}u_{jl}^{2}}\,.

(24)

The optimality conditions of the AM-GM inequality ensure upper hemicontinuity of the solution map in Lemma
5.1
by Lemma
3.2
, implying equivalence of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
using Thm.
2.10
:

Corollary 5.2
.

The optimization of
\mathcal{P}
(
23
) is equivalent to optimization of the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
(
24
) by Def.
2.2
, and solutions to
\mathcal{P}
can be constructed as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\odot k})
.

Extending the HPP, the
\text{HPP}_{k}
also corresponds to a horizontally “stretched” diagonal network structure with increased depth, as shown in Figure
5a
. The relation of parametrization and corresponding network structure for linear models was also studied in simpler settings and without proof of our general result
(

)
. Besides these works in explicit regularization, a strand of literature in DL uses diagonal linear networks to study the implicit regularization of GD
(
Gunasekar et al. 2018
;
Gissin et al. 2019
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Moroshko et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
)
.

\mathcal{K}

.

The map
\mathcal{K}:\prod_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})\mapsto\bigodot_{l=1}^{k}\bm{u}_{l}

2.5
, smoothly parametrizing any continuous objective using the

preserves the local minima of
\mathcal{P}
.

5.2
Group Hadamard Product Parametrizations of Depth
k

The smooth optimization transfer for
\ell_{2,1}
regularized problems can be naturally extended to structured sparsity with non-convex
\ell_{2,2/k}
regularization. We start with the same set-up as in Section
4
, but now consider deeper factorizations of
\bm{\beta}
. Recall that the GHPP is defined as
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}=\bm{\beta}
. Further factorizing the grouping parameter
\bm{\nu}
into
k-1
Hadamard factors, i.e.,
\bm{\nu}=\bigodot_{r=1}^{k-1}\bm{\nu}_{r}
, defines the
\text{GHPP}_{k}
map:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\mathcal{K}:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u},\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{k-1})\mapsto\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}_{r}^{\odot(k-1)}={\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{1}\\
\vdots\\

5.4
Parametrizations with Parameter Sharing

Parameter or weight sharing enables interesting modifications of the previously presented parametrizations, as the parameter redundancy caused by overparametrization can be greatly reduced by allowing for shared parameters between the Hadamard factors. Parameter sharing can be defined as identifying two or more parameters of an objective function as a single parameter, i.e., interpreting them as identical. For example, the group structure-inducing GHPP,
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}
, is essentially the HPP
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}
, but with shared parameters
\bm{v}_{j}=\nu_{j}\mathds{1}_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
within groups
j\in[L]
, collapsed into the scalar
\nu_{j}
. Despite requiring many fewer additional parameters, these parametrizations still define a valid SVF
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}
like their fully overparametrized counterparts.

Deep HPP with shared parameters

Consider the parametrization map for the
\text{HPP}_{k}
, defined as
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})=\bigodot_{l=1}^{k}\bm{u}_{l}
. By introducing parameter sharing between
(k-1)
Hadamard factors, i.e., replacing the Hadamard product of
k-1

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the first Hadamard factor, and
\bm{v}^{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the other factors that are collapsed into a single shared vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The following defines the
\text{HPP}_{k}^{shared}

\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u},\bm{v})\mapsto\bm{u}\odot\left(\bm{v}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{v}\right)=\bm{u}\odot{\textstyle(\bigodot_{l=1}^{k-1}}\bm{v})=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}^{k-1}=\bm{\beta}\,.

(33)

The suitable surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}
to obtain an SVF is a re-weighted
\ell_{2}
penalty accounting for the increased contribution of the shared parameter to the parametrization. More precisely, the shared parameter
\bm{v}
is counted
(k-1)
times, providing the appropriate re-weighting for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}
to define an SVF for non-convex
\ell_{2/k}

Lemma 5.9
.

Given the parametrization
\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\bm{u}\odot\bm{v}^{k-1}
, the minimum surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\triangleq\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+(k-1)\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
subject to

constitutes an SVF for
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq k\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert_{2/k}^{2/k}

However, despite constituting a valid SVF with less overparametrization, parameter sharing breaks the balance and symmetry in the parametrization, with unclear consequences for the optimization. Yet, we can relate the GD optimization dynamics for the
\text{HPP}_{k}^{shared}

\text{HPP}_{k}

A.16

Moreover, initializing

\text{HPP}_{k}

\beta_{j}

(

HDP of depth

6.2
Invertible Reparametrization with Hadamard Powers

In
Schwarz et al. 2021
, a differentiable sparsity-promoting parametrization termed Powerpropagation was introduced, aligning with discussions of related approaches in mathematical optimization
(
Ramlau and Zarzer 2012
)
. The underlying motivation is to artificially increase the curvature of the loss landscape, which induces optimization- and initialization-dependent “rich get richer” dynamics for sparse training of DNNs: the key idea is that applying a power parametrization makes the gradient with respect to the surrogate parameters critically depend on their current values (cf. Figure
17a

Intuitively, this promotes the accumulation of weights either close to or far away from zero, however,
Schwarz et al. 2021
did not realize the induced sparse regularization in the base parametrization under explicit
\ell_{2}
regularization. Being bijective, Powerpropagation is not an over- but rather a reparametrization given by

\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{v}\odot|\bm{v}|^{\circ(k-1)}=\bm{\beta}\,.

(37)

For this parametrization, the feasible set of the problem
\min_{\bm{v}:\bm{v}\odot|\bm{v}|^{\circ(k-1)}=\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}
is a singleton containing
\hat{\bm{v}}
such that
\hat{v}_{j}=\sqrt[k]{|\beta_{j}|}
for
\beta_{j}\geq 0
and
\hat{v}_{j}=-\sqrt[k]{|\beta_{j}|}
for
\beta_{j}<0
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. Hence,
\|\hat{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}
contains
d
summands
\hat{v}_{j}^{2}=|\beta_{j}|^{2/k}
, and we conclude
\min_{\bm{v}:\bm{v}\odot|\bm{v}|^{\circ(k-1)}=\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k}^{2/k}
.
Since the solution map is continuous in
\bm{\beta}
, Assumption
2
holds. Thus, for an
\ell_{2/k}
regularized objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
with real-valued
k>1
, we can construct an equivalent smooth
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{v})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k}^{2/k},

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\big(\bm{\psi},\bm{v}\odot|\bm{v}|^{\circ(k-1)}\big)+\lambda\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(39)

Corollary 6.3
.

The optimization of
\mathcal{P}
in (
38
) is equivalent to the optimization of the smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
in (
39
) by Definition
2.2
, and solutions to
\mathcal{P}
can be constructed as
(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}})=(\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{v}}\odot|\hat{\bm{v}}|^{\circ(k-1)})
.

This result shows that it is the functional shape of the parametrization and its warping effect on the loss surface that induces sparsity, not overparametrization
per se
.

Hadamard Group Powers (GHPowP)

We can naturally extend the Hadamard power parametrization presented in

to structured sparsity, thereby obtaining a more flexible choice of the hyperparameters

and

in

{\scriptsize\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},(\bm{u},\bm{\nu})\mapsto\bm{u}\odotg|\bm{\nu}|^{\circ(k-1)}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{1}\\

On the group level, we have
\bm{\beta}_{{j}}=|\nu_{j}|^{k-1}(u_{j_{1}},\ldots,u_{j_{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}})^{\top}\,\forall j\in[L]

\bm{u}=\left(\bm{u}_{{1}},\ldots,\bm{u}_{{L}}\right)^{\top}
and

Lemma 6.4
.

Given the parametrization map

, the minimum of the surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularizer

\min_{\bm{u},\bm{\nu}:\bm{u}\odotg|\bm{\nu}|^{\circ(k-1)}=\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+(k-1)\|\bm{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}=k\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2,2/k}^{2/k}\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(41)

Using Lemma

, the optimality conditions provided in the proof ensure upper hemicontinuity of the solution map. Therefore, Assumption

holds and we can construct an equivalent smooth surrogate

to an

(42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\mathcal{L}\big(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odotg|\bm{\nu}|^{\circ(k-1)}\big)+\lambda\big(\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+(k-1)\|\bm{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\big).

(43)

.

The optimization of

in (

6.4
Mixed Norm Regularization with Hadamard Group Powers

Analogous to the previous subsection, we can further apply Hadamard powers to induce

\bm{\beta}=\left(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{L}\right)^{\top}

\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}\odotg|\bm{\nu}|^{\circ k_{2}}

k_{2}>0
, with
\bm{u}=\left(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{L}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{L})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}

\bm{u}=\bm{\mu}\odot|\bm{\mu}|^{\circ(k_{1}-1)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,

For a parametrization

7
Optimization Details

In this section, we discuss optimization details of our smooth optimization transfer approach and provide some guidance regarding practical implementations.

Iterative optimization using (S)GD

A considerable body of literature has established desirable convergence properties of (S)GD that hold in overparametrized non-convex settings, such as provably almost always escaping (strict) saddle points under random initialization and mild regularity conditions
(
Lee et al. 2016
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
. For full-batch GD, however,
Du et al. 2017
show that it might take exponentially long to escape saddle points. This can be reduced to polynomial time in the presence of sufficient perturbation in the gradient updates
(
Ge et al. 2015
;
Jin et al. 2017
)
, emphasizing the benefit of SGD in efficiently optimizing non-convex problems. The effect of applying a smooth parametrization on the optimization landscape is not only to transfer the problem to a more curved space, but it also impacts the optimization geometry of (S)GD in a way that has been termed the “rich get richer” effect in the literature. This effect hinges on the multiplicative structure of the parametrizations
\mathcal{K}
, leading to additional multiplicative dependence of the gradient updates of one Hadamard factor on the current parameter values of the remaining factors parametrizing the same base parameter. This also shows in the gradient of the smooth surrogate (
47
) where the product-structured Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})
essentially acts as a parameter-dependent preconditioner leading to adaptive step sizes and momentum
(
Arora et al. 2019
)
.

Besides overparametrization, our approach also imposes differentiable surrogate regularization, inducing additional regularizing effects that differentiate our approach from the mere (unpenalized) overparametrization in implicit regularization. In contrast, our method does not require impractically small initializations to induce regularization
(
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Vaskevicius et al. 2019
)
because the actual solutions of the regularized smooth surrogate reflect the induced sparse regularization, regardless of how this solution is reached. Likewise, early stopping is not strictly required as in implicit regularization
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, since the equivalence holds on the solution level independent of some optimization trajectory window.

Critical points

Due to the results obtained in Lemma
2.8
and Lemma
2.9
, any local minimum of the surrogate optimization problem corresponds to a local minimum in the base parametrization. As a result, if the base optimization problem
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})

is convex, e.g., for a convex
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})
with
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{2,1}
regularization, every local minimum of the surrogate problem
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\xi})
is necessarily global. For non-convex base problems, our approach ensures no spurious minima are created in the optimization transfer.

However, such a matching property does not necessarily hold for critical points of the surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
, owed to the zero-product property of the parametrizations
\mathcal{K}
. Without loss of generality, consider a non-smooth regularized objective
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
with smooth loss
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})
and no additional unregularized parameters
\bm{\psi}
. Applying the proposed smooth optimization transfer, we construct the surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
using a smooth parametrization
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
and further imposing surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization on
\bm{\xi}
. The gradient of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
\bm{\xi}
is then given by

\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}^{\top}(\bm{\xi})\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\,,

(47)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})
is the
d\times{d_{\xi}}
-dimensional Jacobian of
\mathcal{K}
at
\bm{\xi}
, and the gradients
\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
and
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
are
d
- and
{d_{\xi}}
-dimensional vectors, respectively. For the parametrizations we consider, the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{0})
at
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
is the null matrix. As
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
is a type of
\ell_{2}
penalty, we have
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
, and it follows
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
. Therefore,
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
is a critical point of
\mathcal{Q}
, irrespective of the gradient
\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}))
of
\mathcal{L}
in the base objective. A derivation of the Hessian of
\mathcal{Q}
is given in Appendix
D
.

Regarding the nature of potentially spurious critical points, it is known that parametrizations of depth
k=2
, such as the HPP or HDP, only induce strict saddle points at
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
, since their Hessian evaluated at the origin
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{0})
contains parameter-independent non-zero constants that ensure a strictly negative eigenvalue
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
.
4
4

4

A strict or ridable saddle point is a saddle at which the Hessian has at least one strictly negative eigenvalue, i.e., there is a direction of descent.
Through construction of a counterexample,
Kawaguchi 2016
shows that the strict saddle property does not necessarily hold for deep factorizations with depth
k>2
. In our framework, this corresponds to those parametrizations
\mathcal{K}
that induce non-convex
\ell_{q}
or
\ell_{p,q}
regularization in the base objective under surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization. For this class of non-convex regularizers with unbounded derivatives approaching the origin,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\bm{0}
is always a local minimizer in the base problem
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})
, regardless of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})

(
Loh and Wainwright 2015
)
. In the constructed smooth surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})
, this is reflected in the Hessian
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})
. For
k>2
and
\lambda=0
, the Hessian at
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
degenerates to a null matrix,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
, inducing a higher-order saddle point. For the regularized problems we are interested in, the strong convexity of
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
guarantees that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{0})
has only positive eigenvalues. Thus,
\hat{\bm{\xi}}=\bm{0}
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{Q}
, corresponding to the local minimizer
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\bm{0}
in
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})
that is induced by the non-convex regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
. Hence, the additional
\ell_{2}
regularization in our smooth surrogate avoids problematic spurious non-strict saddle point at
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
induced by
\mathcal{K}
, even for non-convex regularization. Importantly,

\hat{\bm{\xi}}=\bm{0}
being a local minimizer of the surrogate
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})
for non-convex
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularization in the base problem
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})
is not a property of our proposed method, but of the non-convex regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta})
.

Initialization
Another relevant question concerns finding effective and well-founded initializations for the surrogate parameters, and how they relate to an appropriate initialization of the base parameter
\bm{\beta}^{t}
at
t=0
.
A natural approach would be to initialize the surrogate parameters functionally equivalent to a standard initialization scheme for the base parameter
\bm{\beta}^{0}
. However, in the case of overparametrization, there are many such options, and it is
a priori
unclear how to optimally select among feasible initializations of
\bm{\beta}^{0}
. It seems natural to initialize the surrogate parameters
\bm{\xi}
according to the optimality conditions provided by the implemented SVF, i.e.,
\bm{\xi}^{0}=\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta}^{0})
, where
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})
is the set-valued solution mapping of the SVF, and
\bm{\beta}^{0}
is obtained from a standard initialization scheme for the base parameters. This ensures that the optimization is initialized at a minimizer of the surrogate penalty
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
over
\{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}^{0}\}
.

To provide two examples, consider a parametrization of
\bm{\beta}
using
\text{HPP}_{k}
, i.e.,
\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}
with surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization. One approach then entails initializing the surrogate factors
\bm{u}_{l}
identically as
\bm{u}_{l}^{0}=\sqrt[k]{|\bm{\beta}^{0}|}
, and subsequently multiplying one (arbitrary) factor
\bm{u}_{l}^{0}
by the respective signs of
\bm{\beta}^{0}
.
5
5

5

Applying any sign pattern to the
\bm{u}_{l}
that respects the signs of
\bm{\beta}^{0}
under the parametrization
\mathcal{K}
is valid.
For structured sparsity using the GHPP,
\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}\odotg\bm{\nu}
, the surrogate parameters are initialized as
\nu_{j}^{0}=\sqrt{\|\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0}\|_{2}}
and
\bm{u}_{j}^{0}=\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0}/\sqrt{\|\bm{\beta}_{j}^{0}\|_{2}}
, again equivalently for sign patterns
\pm(\bm{u}_{j}^{0},\nu_{j}^{0})
. Another option would be to randomly initialize all factors, but increase the initialization scale so that the product is initialized at a desired scale.

Effects on Optimization Landscape

The parametrizations
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\beta}
considered in this work (cf. Assumption
1
) are based on Hadamard products and powers. This has a notable effect on the loss landscape, primarily due to a modification of curvature induced by the multiplicative nature of the parametrizations. For the bijective Powerpropagation (
37
), the warping effect of the reparametrization takes place in the base parameters and can thus be disentangled from overparametrization. Appendix
C.3
contains more details.

Practical implementation

It is important to consider the case when the surrogate parameters
\bm{\xi}_{j}
corresponding to some base parameter
\beta_{j}
are randomly initialized in an orthant that maps to an incorrect sign under the multiplicative parametrization
\mathcal{K}
compared to the solution
\hat{\beta}_{j}
. In these cases, it is crucial to use large learning rates during early iterations, as previously suggested by, e.g.,
Li et al. 2023b
. Otherwise, the respective parameter iterates will gradually approach zero from the side of the initial orthant. This occurs due to the “rich get richer” effect, resulting in diminishing gradient magnitudes as the parameter approaches zero, making it difficult to “step over” the zero boundary. Although for most DNNs the sign pattern is not identified, large step sizes in DL have been found to drive SGD toward simpler structures
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
and balanced Hadamard factors
(
Ziyin 2023
)
, thus facilitating sparse optimization.
Besides initially large learning rates, we further emphasize the importance of the commonplace recommendation of using either small batch sizes in SGD or perturbing the gradient updates via additional noise injection for faster convergence and the improved ability to escape saddles and local minima
(
Jin et al. 2017
)
.
Further, note that using (S)GD to optimize the differentiable surrogate does not have an inherent proximal step. Consequently, the iterates do not produce theoretically zero parameter values in a finite number of steps. However, with sufficient training time, floating point representations that are numerically zero can be obtained. For resource-efficient optimization, we recommend early stopping and post-hoc thresholding.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present experimental findings supporting our theoretical results and demonstrating the generality of our method by applying it to various learning problems ranging from non-convex regularized linear regression to enhanced DNN pruning and filter-sparse convolutional neural networks (CNNs). The main goal of these experiments is not to establish the superiority of our method over other approaches but rather to demonstrate the practical feasibility and competitiveness of using SGD to solve non-smooth regularization.
6
6

6

We stress that the proposed method offers a differentiable formulation of sparse regularizers, thus inherently tying its performance to that of the induced regularizer.
Details on optimization settings and architectures can be found in Appendix
B
.

8.1
Failure of (Sub)GD to Solve Sparse Regularization

First, we illustrate the failure of directly applying GD to solve both unstructured and structured sparsity regularization, even in the case of a convex (group) lasso objective with linear predictor and independent features. In DL libraries, the gradient at non-differentiable points is typically assigned zero in the GD update, effectively constituting subgradient descent. To this end, we draw
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{1000\times 100}
,
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{100}
, and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{1000}
from independent Gaussians and compose the noisy outcome as
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\varepsilon}
. For the group lasso, the parameters are partitioned into
L=20
groups. The objectives in the base parametrization for both regularizers are

\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{1}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2,1}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{j}\|_{2}
, and we compare three optimization approaches: directly applying GD to the non-smooth objective, GD under smooth optimization transfer using the (G)HPP, and a highly efficient specialized combination of non-smooth methods, implemented in
glmnet

(
Friedman et al. 2010
)
and
SGL

(
Simon et al. 2013
)
. The equivalent differentiable objectives of the second approach are defined as
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell_{1}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}(\bm{u}\odot\bm{v})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2})
and

Next, we investigate the behavior of our smooth optimization method for

The
\ell_{q}

\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k}^{2/k}

\mathcal{Q}(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k})={\textstyle\frac{1}{n}}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}\|_{2}^{2}+{\textstyle\frac{\lambda}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\left\lVert\bm{u}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\ell_{1}

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}/\|\bm{\beta}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}

\sqrt{n^{-1}\|\bm{Y}-\hat{\bm{Y}}\|_{2}^{2}}

The next experiment applies the structured Hadamard power parametrization from Section

Although structured sparsity in DL generally leads to poorer performance, its capacity to jointly remove whole model components permits a much greater reduction in computational footprint and is thus of particular interest for practical applications. Writing the regularized CNN training objective for filter sparsity as
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{\beta})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2,2/k}^{2/k}

\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u},\bm{\nu})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\psi},\bm{u}\odotg|\bm{\nu}|^{\circ(k-1)})+\frac{\lambda}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{L}(\|\bm{u}_{{j}}\|_{2}^{2}+(k-1)\nu_{j}^{2})

k
, resulting in minimal overparametrization (
99,370

\ell_{2}

8.5
Computational Complexity

An important question is how the overparametrization in our method affects the runtime complexity of DNN training using SGD. Since the networks are reduced to their base parametrization after training and sparse components are removed, the inference time complexity is reduced by the extent of the achieved sparsity. During training, the overparametrization increases both model size and computational complexity which is heavily dependent on the architecture, hardware, and specific choice of
\mathcal{K}
.

9
Summary and Discussion

In this work, we propose a general framework for smooth optimization of objectives that involve non-smooth and potentially non-convex sparse regularization of parameter subsets. Being model- and loss-agnostic, our approach is applicable to a wide range of scenarios. The key idea underlying our method is to find a smooth variational form of the non-smooth sparse regularizer. Applying a smooth parametrization map and a change of regularizers enables the construction of an equivalent smooth surrogate objective, eliminating the need for specialized optimization routines for non-smooth and non-convex problems. Moreover, our framework can be easily integrated into existing differentiable structures such as DNNs. Our general template is applied to the smooth optimization of a broad range of non-smooth
\ell_{q}
and
\ell_{p,q}
regularized optimization problems for (structured) sparsity. Numerical experiments demonstrate the practical feasibility and effectiveness of our method in various sparse learning problems and in comparison with other methods.

Our approach also presents certain limitations that merit discussion. One limitation pertains to the initialization of the surrogate parameters, where an optimal choice is not straightforward.
In addition, while our approach enables efficient optimization using SGD, obtaining (numerically) exact zeros is not guaranteed for small
\lambda
. This is a characteristic of SGD and not a limitation of the optimization transfer
per se
.
For variable selection, we recommend a post-thresholding step.
It is worth emphasizing that these challenges do not inherently limit the potential of our approach; instead, they underline key areas where additional research is needed.

There are several promising avenues for future research. Notably, our approach offers the flexibility to construct reparametrized, sparse “drop-in” replacements for network components, allowing for modular sparse regularization in differentiable network structures. This makes our method especially suited for exploring applications in various domains such as input-sparse DNNs.
Although a heuristic initialization performed well in our experiments, there is further great interest in understanding how to construct initialization schemes tailored to the surrogate parameters. Lastly, there is also an opportunity to investigate the relationship between our smooth optimization transfer approach and implicit regularization methods in the DL literature. Our approach enforces a balanced parameter norm condition through surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization, which bears similarities to the balanced weight conditions employed in implicit regularization techniques. Investigating this relationship could reveal valuable insights and potential synergies between the two approaches.

\bm{\beta}

this property extends to

. By the previous result,
\hat{\bm{\xi}}\in\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\hat{\bm{\beta}}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})

\forall\varepsilon>0\exists\delta>0:(\tilde{\bm{\psi}},\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{B}((\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}),\delta)\implies\exists(\tilde{\bm{\psi}},\tilde{\bm{\xi}})\in\mathcal{B}((\hat{\bm{\psi}},\hat{\bm{\xi}}),\varepsilon):\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\in\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\bm{\xi}:\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})=\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi}).

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
for

\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
, first consider

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{0})

\bm{\beta}_{j}^{n}
that converges to
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{0}

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j}^{n})
that converges to
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{0})=\{\bm{0}\}

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
at
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{0}

Now, consider
\bm{\beta}_{j}\neq\bm{0}

\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\delta)
, we have
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j}),\varepsilon)
. For an arbitrary
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\delta)
, consider the set of perturbed solutions
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
by scaling the vectors
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{jl}

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
and
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})

\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{j}
satisfies
the optimality conditions associated with
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}

\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{j}
in
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}
in the original set
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})

\|\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{jl}-\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{jl}\|_{2}^{2}=\textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|(s_{j}-1)\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{jl}\|_{2}^{2}=k\cdot|s_{j}-1|^{2}\cdot T(\bm{\beta}_{j})\,.

\bm{\beta}_{j}\neq\bm{0}

\bm{\beta}_{j}\neq\bm{0}

\forall\,\varepsilon>0\,\exists\,\delta>0:\big|\sqrt{T(\bm{\beta}_{j})}-\sqrt{T(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})}\big|<\varepsilon/\sqrt{k}
for all
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\delta)

\|\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{j}-\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}\|_{2}=\sqrt{k}\cdot\big|\sqrt{T(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})}-\sqrt{T(\bm{\beta}_{j})}\big|<\varepsilon

\tilde{\bm{\xi}}_{j}\in\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j})
and corresponding
\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}\in\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})

Therefore, for each
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{\beta}_{j},\delta)

\mathcal{B}(\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j}),\varepsilon)

\bm{\beta}_{j}\neq 0

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
at all
\bm{\beta}_{j}
follows for
j=1,\ldots,L

under Assumption

\hat{\bm{\xi}}_{j}(\bm{\beta}_{j})

\bm{\beta}_{j}
implies upper hemicontinuity of
\hat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\beta})
at
\bm{\beta}=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{L})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

∎

\gamma_{j}
and
\delta_{j}

(Prop. 1 in

Let

\displaystyle\exists\tilde{\bm{M}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times z}:\tilde{\bm{M}}_{1}\bm{M}_{2}=\bm{0}_{m\times n}\,

\displaystyle\land\,\tilde{\bm{M}}_{1}+\bm{M}_{1}\,\text{is full row-rank}\,\,\,\textbf{or}\,

\displaystyle\exists\tilde{\bm{M}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{z\times n}:\bm{M}_{1}\tilde{\bm{M}}_{2}=\bm{0}_{m\times n}\,

\displaystyle\land\,\tilde{\bm{M}}_{2}+\bm{M}_{2}\,\text{is full column-rank}\,.

Letting

In (S)GD, dynamics with shared Hadamard factors can be related to their fully overparametrized counterparts through identical initialization of the to-be-shared parameters. We define a differentiable surrogate
\mathcal{Q}
based on the
\text{HPP}_{k}

\bm{\beta}=\bm{u}_{l}^{\odot k}

Consider an updating scheme for the
\bm{u}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{k_{1}\|\bm{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+k_{2}|\nu_{j}|^{2}}{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{k_{1}\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2/k_{1}}^{2/k_{1}}+k_{2}|\nu_{j}|^{2}}{k}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\left(\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2/k_{1}}^{2/k_{1}}\right)^{k_{1}}\cdot\left(|\nu_{j}|^{2}\right)^{k_{2}}\right)^{1/(k_{1}+k_{2})}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\Big|\|\bm{u}_{j}\|_{2/k_{1}}\cdot|\nu_{j}|^{k_{2}}\Big|\right)^{2/k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{u}_{j}\cdot|\nu_{j}|^{k_{2}}\|_{2/k_{1}}^{2/k}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\|\bm{\beta}_{{j}}\|_{2/k_{1}}^{2/k}=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2/k_{1},2/k}^{2/k}\,,

with equality holding if and only if
\|\bm{\mu}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=|\nu_{j}|^{2}=\|\bm{\beta}_{{j}}\|_{2/k_{1}}^{2/k}\;\forall j=1,\ldots,L
.
∎

A.20
Proof of (local) connectedness of fibers of parametrization map

Let
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a parameter that can be partitioned into
(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{L})
for
j\in[L]
,
L\leq d
, so that
\bm{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
and
|\mathcal{G}_{1}|+\ldots+|\mathcal{G}_{L}|=d
. Further, let
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth surjective parametrization of
\bm{\beta}
as in Assumption
1
.

Lemma A.5

(Local Connectedness of the Fibers of
\mathcal{K}
)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the fi- bers of
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are (locally) connected sets at every
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Further, the fibers of
\mathcal{K}
are smooth manifolds whenever
\bm{\beta}
is a regular value of
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

We use the following well-known result in differential geometry, which can be derived from the inverse function theorem and is also known as the regular value or regular level set theorem
(
Lee 2012
, e.g., Corollary 5.14 in)
:

Proposition A.6

(Preimage Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\bm{\xi}\mapsto\bm{\beta}
, be a smooth map and let
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a regular value of
\mathcal{K}
. Then the fiber
\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\bm{\beta})
is a
({d_{\xi}}-d)
-dimensional smooth manifold.

By Assumption
1
,
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})
is block-separable into parametrizations
\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
, for each of which the
|\mathcal{G}_{j}|\times{d_{\xi}}_{j}
-dimensional Jacobian has full rank if
\bm{\xi}_{jl}\neq\bm{0}
for all
l\in[k]
. Therefore, the fibers of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
at non-zero points
\bm{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|}
are smooth manifolds, and thus (locally) connected sets by Proposition
A.6
. Further,
\mathcal{K}_{j}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
maps to the non-regular value
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{0}
if at least one surrogate parameter factor
\bm{\xi}_{jl}
of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
is zero. Thus, the fiber
\mathcal{K}_{j}^{-1}(\bm{0})
contains all
\bm{\xi}_{j1},\ldots,\bm{\xi}_{jl}
such that at least one
\bm{\xi}_{jl}=\bm{0}
. This fiber, while not a manifold, is thus a connected set, since all of the contained hyperplanes intersect at
\bm{\xi}_{j1}=\ldots=\bm{\xi}_{jk}=\bm{0}
.

Considering the separable Cartesian product structure of
\mathcal{K}
, the fiber of
\mathcal{K}
at
\bm{\beta}
is a Cartesian product of the fibers of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
at their respective values
\bm{\beta}_{j}
. Each of these fibers is either locally connected (for regular values of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
) or connected (for
\bm{\beta}_{j}=\bm{0}
), and thus the fiber of
\mathcal{K}
at
\bm{\beta}
is also locally connected.
∎

Remark A.7

(Fibers of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
at regular values
\bm{\beta}_{j}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi_{j}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}_{j}|},\,\bm{\xi}_{j}\mapsto\bm{\beta}_{j}
, be a
k
-positively homogeneous function as defined in Assumption
1
. In the context of the fibers of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
, the positive homogeneity implies scale-invariance of the shape of each individual fiber at the regular values
\bm{\beta}_{j}
. That is, a fiber associated with a regular value
\bm{\beta}_{j}
has the same “shape” as the fiber of any scaled version of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
. Moreover, continuity of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
ensures that the fibers
\mathcal{K}_{j}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
are closed sets. However, they are not compact in the case of overparametrization, since the product structure of the
\mathcal{K}_{j}
implies unbounded fibers that extend to infinity.

Moreover, by Proposition
A.6
, the fiber of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
at a regular value
\bm{\beta}_{j}
forms a
(d_{\xi_{j}}-|\mathcal{G}_{j}|)
-dimensional smooth manifold. This manifold consists of disjoint connected components, each embedded within an orthant of suitable sign configuration. For any non-zero
\bm{\beta}_{j}
, the fiber
\mathcal{K}_{j}^{-1}(\bm{\beta}_{j})
contains a unique minimal-norm point in each connected component. By the AM-GM inequality, this point is attained when the Euclidean norms of the surrogate factors
\bm{\xi}_{jl}
are balanced, i.e.,
\|\bm{\xi}_{j1}\|_{2}=\ldots=\|\bm{\xi}_{jk}\|_{2}
.

As we traverse the fiber away from the minimal-norm points, i.e., the more unbalanced the factorization of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
becomes, the fiber exhibits increasing curvature. Figure
16c

\text{HPP}_{k}

k=3

C.1
Difference between HPP and HDP:
2\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
vs
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}

Figure 15:
Left
: HPP (blue/green) and the surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization
u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}
(orange).
Right
: HDP (blue/green) and surrogate
\ell_{2}
regularization.

Both parametrizations define hyperbolic paraboloids for each
j=1,\ldots,d
that are equivalent bar rotation and scaling. Differences in the minimum of the SVF for the HPP (
2\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
) and HDP (
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
) arise because the HDP defines hyperbolas that are not only rotated by
-45^{\circ}
, relative to the ones defined by the HPP, but also have their arguments scaled by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
. Without scaling, including only the rotation, the HDP could be written as
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\delta})=\frac{\bm{\gamma}\odot\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\delta}\odot\bm{\delta}}{2}
. To see this, consider
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The
-45^{\circ}
rotated coordinates of any point
(u,v)
on the Cartesian plane are given by
(\frac{u+v}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{2}})\triangleq(\gamma,\delta)
. Computing the value of the HDP at the rotated point, we find
\gamma^{2}-\delta^{2}=2uv
, i.e., the HDP is twice the value of the HPP. Note that, as opposed to the clockwise
45^{\circ}
rotation, the coordinate change for the HDP was defined as
(\gamma,\delta)=(u+v,v-u)
. This additional scaling of the arguments by
\sqrt{2}
in the HDP compared to the rotation mapping cancels out the denominator, effectively resulting in an increase in the HDP by a factor of
2
. Assuming
\beta>0
and ignoring the rotation, switching from the HPP to the HDP shrinks the gap between
\mathcal{K}
and the surrogate regularization
\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
(cf. Figure
4a
), such that
\mathcal{K}
evaluated at the vertices of all level-set hyperbolas is also contained in the graph of
\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
. As a result, there is one parabola contained in each elliptic paraboloid
\gamma_{j}^{2}+\delta_{j}^{2}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, that is exactly identical to one parabola contained in the hyperbolic paraboloid
\gamma_{j}^{2}-\delta_{j}^{2}
. Thus, the absolute value of the HDP evaluated at the optimal points with minimum
\ell_{2}
norm is identical to the surrogate regularizer. If
\beta<0
, the statements hold true for the negative mirror image of the surrogate penalty, i.e., the elliptic paraboloid
-(\gamma_{j}^{2}+\delta_{j}^{2})
. For a visualization of the difference between the HPP and HDP, see Figure
15
.

Appendix D
Derivation of Gradient and Hessian of Smooth Surrogate
\mathcal{Q}

For simplicity, we assume no unregularized parameters
\bm{\psi}
so that the objective function
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is defined as
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
,
where
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth functions,
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is a strongly convex
\ell_{2}
regularization term, and
\lambda\geq 0
a scalar. The gradient of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
\bm{\xi}
is given by

\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}^{\top}(\bm{\xi})\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\lambda\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})\,,

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})
is the
d\times{d_{\xi}}
-dimensional Jacobian of
\mathcal{K}
at
\bm{\xi}
, and the gradients
\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))
and
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{\xi})
are vectors with
d
and
{d_{\xi}}
entries. The Hessian of
\mathcal{Q}
at
\bm{\xi}
is then obtained as

\mathcal{H}_{Q(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})\nabla_{\mathcal{K}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))+\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}^{\top}(\bm{\xi})\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))}(\bm{\xi})\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})+\lambda\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{\xi})\,,

where
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})
is a third-order
{d_{\xi}}\times{d_{\xi}}\times d
-dimensional tensor, and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi}))}(\bm{\xi})
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{\xi})
are Hessians of dimensions
d\times d
and
{d_{\xi}}\times{d_{\xi}}
. From this representation, we can see that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}(\bm{\xi})}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{0}
imply
