Saecular persistence
Robert Ghrist, Gregory Henselman-Petrusek

Abstract
A persistence module is a functor f : 𝐈 → 𝖤 f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E} , where 𝐈 \mathbf{I} is the poset category of a totally ordered set. This work introduces saecular decomposition : a categorically natural method to decompose f f into simple parts, called interval modules. Saecular decomposition exists under generic conditions, e.g., when 𝐈 \mathbf{I} is well ordered and 𝖤 \mathsf{E} is a category of modules or groups. This represents a substantial generalization of existing factorizations of 1-parameter persistence modules, leading to, among other things, persistence diagrams not only in homology, but in homotopy. Applications of saecular decomposition include inverse and extension problems involving filtered topological spaces, the 1-parameter generalized persistence diagram, and the Leray-Serre spectral sequence. Several examples – including cycle representatives for generalized barcodes – hold special significance for scientific applications. The key tools in this approach are modular and distributive order lattices, combined with Puppe exact categories. Keywords: persistence diagram, barcode, order lattice, exact category, topological data analysis



1
Framework

sæculum

(sæculī, n.)
: generation; a period of long duration; the longest fixed interval.

Advances in topological data analysis have generated growing attention to functors of the form
f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the poset category of a totally ordered set. As the order-theoretic term for a totally ordered set is a
chain
, we will call such objects
chain diagrams
or
chain functors
. For
\mathsf{E}
the category of vector spaces and linear transformations, chain functors are called
persistence modules
.

Topological persistence began with a set of independent works getting at parametrized topological features
[
21
,
22
,
37
,
38
]
and crystallized in the emergence of a persistence diagram (PD) for the homologies of filtered simplicial complexes
[
18
,
19
]
. This is not a functor, but a function from the plane to the non-negative integers encoding birth and death of homology classes.

The first revolution in persistence came from a change of perspective, that the values of the PD correspond to multiplicities of indecomposable factors in a Krull-Schmidt decomposition of a persistence module
[
14
,
13
]
. From this foundation, richer aspects of persistence were appended, including the representation-theoretic interpretation, with relations to quivers and Gabriel’s Theorem
[
11
]
providing both clarity and extension.

The persistence module perspective was transformative for several reasons. For scientific and industrial applications, it solved the fundamental problem of mapping persistent features back to data, thus making individual points in the PD and bars in the barcode interpretable. For theorists, the module perspective provided the formal basis on which duality, stability, and other concepts later grew or expanded. In the process, the module perspective also expanded the domain where persistence was defined, and made the persistence diagram itself more readily computed. Thus our understanding of persistence advanced in two stages: first with the definition of the persistence diagram, and second with the decomposition of an underlying algebraic object.

In 2016, Patel launched a new iteration of this two-stage framework for persistence. In
[
34
]
, he introduced the notion of a
generalized
persistence diagram, which is well-defined for tame chain functors in any abelian or essentially small symmetric monoidal category.
As the name suggests, the generalized PD represents a substantial advance over the classic persistence diagram of
[
19
]
, which requires field coefficients. However, this new formulation of the generalized persistence diagram also provides no decomposition of the underlying functor itself.

The purpose of the present work is to complement the cycle launched by Patel by providing the desired decomposition of chain functors. We do so for chain functors valued in abelian categories – as well as Puppe exact categories and
\mathsf{Group}
, the category of groups and group homomorphisms. In so doing, we provide new tools for mapping features back to data; for structural investigation of the persistence module; and for algebraic connections with mathematics more broadly.
In particular, we provide the first notion of a cycle representative for a generalized persistent feature, and the first definition of a barcode for persistent homotopy. We also formulate an enumeration theorem that relates the generalized persistence diagram to the Leray-Serre spectral sequence. A second enumeration theorem relates the saecular decomposition to the generalized PD. This relationship is less rigid than the one that relates Krull-Schmidt decomposition of linear-coefficient persistence modules with classical PD’s, but similarly informative.

The essential ingredients in this approach are Puppe exact (p-exact) categories and order lattices. P-exact categories give a lightweight axiomatic framework for homological algebra which brings much of the essential structure into focus. Many of the ideas in this work came directly or indirectly from existing work on p-exact categories, particularly
[
24
]
. Order lattices are deeply entwined in theory of exact categories, and provide much of the technical heavy lifting in the present work. At the same time, they help to bring the main ideas into sharp relief. Order lattices were also essential to extending the basic ideas of this work from p-exact categories to the (not p-exact) category of
groups
. The details of this extension are surprising, and relate to a remarkable structure theorem concerning half-normal bifiltrations.

To begin, fix a functor

f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}

where
\mathsf{E}
is a category of (left or right)
R
-modules, and
\mathbf{I}
is the posetal category of a well ordered set. We will consider more general types of source and target categories throughout; however, restricting to well-ordered sets and
R
-modules will simplify the language needed for a general summary.

Recall that an
interval
in a poset
\mathrm{P}
is a subset
{I}\subseteq\mathrm{P}
such that
b\in{I}
whenever
{I}
contains an upper bound of
b
and a lower bound of
b
. This notion coincides with the usual notion of interval, when
\mathbf{I}
is the real line. A functor
g:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
is an
interval diagram of support type
{I}
if
f_{a}=0
for
a\notin{I}
, and the restricted functor
f|_{{I}}
carries each arrow to an isomorphism. The family of
nonempty
intervals in
\mathbf{I}
is denoted
{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
. The family of interval diagrams with support type
{I}
is denoted
{\mathrm{Int}}({I})
.

2.1
Saecular homomorphisms and interval decomposition

Our first contribution is a formal definition of what it means to decompose the functor
f
into a family of interval diagrams. We do this with the aid of a uniquely defined lattice homomorphism
{\Omega}
, which we call the
saecular homomorphism
, as per the following.

•

Domain:
Write
{\mathbb{A}(\mathrm{P})}
for the family of down-closed subsets of any poset
\mathrm{P}
. We regard
{\mathbb{A}(\mathrm{P})}
as an order lattice, under inclusion.
Given
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
and
S,S^{\prime},T,T^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{I})}
, let us define
{I}\sim(S,T)\iff{I}=T-S
, and define
(S,T)\leq(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\iff S\subseteq S^{\prime}
and
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
. We can then endow
{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
with a unique partial order such that
{I}\leq{J}\iff{I}\sim(S,T)\leq(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\sim{J}
for some
S,S^{\prime},T,T^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{I})}
. The domain of
{\Omega}
is
\mathbb{A}{{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}:={\mathbb{A}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))}
.

•

Codomain:
Recall that the category
[\mathbf{I},\mathsf{E}]
of functors
\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
is abelian. The functor
f
is an object in
[\mathbf{I},\mathsf{E}]
, and the family of subobjects of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{Sub}(f)
or
\mathrm{Sub}_{f}
, is a complete order lattice under inclusion. In particular, every subset of
\mathrm{Sub}_{f}
has a meet and a join. The codomain of
{\Omega}
is
\mathrm{Sub}(f)
.

Recall that a homomorphism of order lattices is
complete
if it preserves arbitrary meets and joins, and, in addition, its domain and codomain are complete lattices. To streamline the definition of
{\Omega}
, we denote downsets as
\;{\downarrow}\;{I}=\{{J}:{J}\leq{I}\}
and
\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}=\{{J}:{J}<{I}\}
(for nonstrict and strict downsets respectively).

Theorem 1
.

There exists a unique complete lattice homomorphism
{\Omega}:\mathbb{A}{{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}\to\mathrm{Sub}(f)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}\;}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}\;}\in{\mathrm{Int}}({I})

(1)

for each
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
.

Definition 1
.

The
saecular CDI homomorphism
is the complete lattice homomorphism defined in Theorem
1
. We say that
{\Omega}
decomposes
f
into the indexed family of interval functors defined in Equation (
1
).

The abbreviation
CDI
stands for
completely distributive interval
and stems from
{\Omega}
being a free completely distributive homomorphism whose domain is an Alexandrov topology on a poset of intervals. The need for delineating various types of saecular lattice homomorphisms will arise later in §
8
.

2.2
Saecular functors

The saecular CDI homomorphism can be extended to a more informative object, called the
saecular CDI functor
. To construct this functor, first define the
locally closed
category,
\mathrm{Lcl}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))
, as follows.
Objects are sets of form
S-T
, where
S,T\in\mathbb{A}{{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}
; equivalently, objects are locally closed sets, with respect to the Alexandrov topology on the poset
{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
. An arrow
X\to Y
is a subset
S\subseteq X\cap Y
such that
S
is up-closed in
X
and down-closed in
Y
. Composition of arrows
X\xrightarrow{S}Y\xrightarrow{T}Z
is intersection:
T\circ S=T\cap S
.
It can be shown that
\mathrm{Lcl}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))
has a zero object, kernels, and cokernels.
A functor
\mathsf{F}
from
\mathrm{Lcl}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))
to any other category with zeros, kernels, and cokernels is
exact
it if preserves these universal objects.

Theorem 2
.

There exists an exact functor
{\Omega}:\mathrm{Lcl}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))\to[\mathbf{I},\mathsf{E}]
such that

{\Omega}\{{I}\}\in{\mathrm{Int}}({I})

for each nonempty interval
{I}
.
This functor is unique up to unique isomorphism, if we require
{\Omega}
to restrict to a complete lattice homomorphism
\mathbb{A}{{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}\to\mathrm{Sub}(f)
. In this case
{\Omega}|_{\mathbb{A}{{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}}
is the saecular CDI homomorphism.

Definition 2
.

The
saecular CDI functor
of
f
is the unique (up to unique isomorphism) functor defined in Theorem
2
.

Remark 1
.

When
\mathbf{I}
is finite, we can replace
\mathsf{E}
with any Puppe exact category in Theorems
1
and
2
. Puppe exact categories include all abelian categories, as well as many non-additive categories. If we require
\mathrm{Sub}(f_{a})
to have finite height for each
a\in\mathbf{I}
, then
\mathbf{I}
can be any totally ordered set. Several other relaxations are available, in addition.

2.3
Saecular functors for nonabelian groups

If
\mathsf{E}
were the category
\mathsf{Group}
, rather than a category of
R
modules, then there would be at least two reasonable ways to interpret a fraction
N/M
for any nested pair of subdiagrams
M\subseteq N\subseteq f
.

In the first interpretation,
N/M
is the

of the inclusion
M\subseteq N
. This is the sense in which we write the fraction
\frac{|{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}|}{|{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}|}

In the second interpretation,

is a diagram of pointed sets, where the distinguished point is the 0-coset. For concreteness, we sometimes write this as

for all

, in which case
\mathrm{Cst}(N/M)=\mathrm{Cok}(N/M)

Remarkably, Theorem

Theorem 3
.

Theorem
1
remains true when
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{Group}
, if we interpret
\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}\;}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}\;}
as
\mathrm{Cst}\left(\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}\;}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}\;}\right)
.
Moreover, if
{\Omega}
is the unique homomorphism that satisfies Theorem
1
under this interpretation, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cok}\left(\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}}\right)\in{\mathrm{Int}}({I})

(2)

for each interval
{I}
.

Definition 3
.

We call
\mathrm{Cok}\left(\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}}\right)
and
\mathrm{Cst}\left(\frac{{\Omega}\;{\downarrow}\;{I}}{{\Omega}\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I}}\right)
the
{I}^{th}
saecular factor
of
f
and the
{I}^{th}
saecular
coset

Remark 2
.

Theorem
2

3.1
Barcodes, persistence diagrams, and inverse problems

The
barcode
of a chain diagram
g:\mathbf{R}\to\mathsf{E}
is a multiset of intervals, i.e. elements of
{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{R})
.
It is well defined whenever
\mathsf{E}={\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
, the category of finite dimensional
{\mathds{k}}
-linear vector spaces, for some field
{\mathds{k}}
. We denote the barcode
\mathrm{BC}_{g}
.

The associated
persistence diagram
(PD)
[
19
]
is the function
\mathrm{PD}_{g}:\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{Z}
sending
(p,q)
to the multiplicity of interval
[p,q)
in
\mathrm{BC}_{g}
. Barcodes and persistence diagrams carry identical information when
\mathrm{BC}_{g}
contains only left-closed, right-open intervals, but each introduces minor impositions in formal arguments; multisets entail a variety of semantic subtleties, and the notion of a persistence diagram fails to capture information about intervals of form
(a,b),(a,b]
, and
[a,b]
.
Rather than introduce a third term, we break from convention, and use both persistence diagram and barcode to refer to the function
{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{R})\to\mathbf{Z}
sending
{I}
to the number of copies of
{I}
in
\mathrm{BC}_{g}
. It has been shown
[
23
,
43
,
6
]
that the barcode uniquely determines the isomorphism class of
g
; indeed, one can generalize this result to chain functors with any totally ordered index category.

The notion of a
generalized persistence diagram

[
34
]
is a natural abstraction of the persistence diagram, which is suitable for a wider range of target categories
\mathsf{E}
. Generalized PD’s come in two varieties. A type-
\mathsf{A}
generalized PD is a function
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{A}}_{g}:\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{Z}^{W}
, where
W
is the set of object isomorphism classes in
\mathsf{E}
. Type-
\mathsf{A}
PD’s are well defined for any tame (meaning constructible,
cf.

[
34
]
) functor
g
from
\mathbf{R}
to an essentially small symmetric monoidal category with images. A type-
\mathsf{B}
generalized PD is a function
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}_{g}:\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{Z}^{V}_{\geq 0}
, where
V
is the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathsf{E}
(meaning objects with no proper nonzero subobjects), and
\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
is the set of nonnegative integers. Type-
\mathsf{B}
PD’s are well defined for any constructible functor from
\mathbf{R}
to an abelian category.

The
saecular persistence diagram
, or the
saecular barcode
, is a fourth notion, distinct from
\mathrm{PD},\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{A}}
, and
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}
. It is well defined for any chain functor
f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
where
\mathbf{I}
is well ordered and
\mathsf{E}\in\{R\mathsf{Mod},\mathsf{Mod}R,\mathsf{Group}\}
; in fact, it can be defined much more generally.

Definition 4
.

The
saecular barcode
(or
saecular persistence diagram
) of
f
, is the function
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}
that sends an interval
{I}
to the
{I}^{th}
saecular factor of
f
. The saecular
coset
barcode is defined similarly, when
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{Group}
.

The saecular barcode
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{g}
agrees with the classical
\mathrm{PD}_{g}
in the following sense:

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbf{J}
be totally ordered set and
g:\mathbf{J}\to{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
be a functor. Then
\mathrm{PD}_{g}
and
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{g}
are well defined, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{PD}_{g}({I})=\dim(\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{g}({I}))

for each
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{J})
, where
\dim(h):=\max_{a\in\mathbf{I}}\dim(h_{a})
.

The saecular barcode
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{g}
also agrees with
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}_{g}
in a natural sense (Theorem
5
).
To streamline the statement of this result, recall that the
length
of a module – or, more generally, the length of an object

in an abelian category – is the supremum of all
n
such that
A
admits a strictly increasing sequence of subobjects
0<N_{1}<\ldots<N_{n}=A
.
When
A
has finite length, we may define the Jordan-Hölder vector of
A
to be unique element
\mathrm{JH}(A)\in\mathbf{Z}^{V}
such that
\mathrm{JH}(A)_{C}=\#\{i:N_{i}/N_{i-1}\cong C\}
for each
C\in V
, where
N
is any maximal-with-respect-to-inclusion chain of subobjects. It can be shown that
\mathrm{JH}(A)
does not depend on the choice of
N

[
24
, Theorem 1.3.5]
.

Given an interval functor
g
with support type
{I}
, we write
\mathrm{JH}(g)
for
the Jordan-Hölder vector of any object
g_{a}
, where
a\in{I}
. This vector does not depend on the choice of
a
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
g:\mathbf{R}\to\mathsf{E}
be a constructible functor in the sense of
[
34
]
. If
\mathsf{E}
is abelian and
\mathrm{Sub}(f_{a})
has finite length for each
a\in\mathbf{R}
, then
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}_{f}
and
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}
are well defined, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}_{f}({I})=\mathrm{JH}(\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}({I}))

for each
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
.

Theorems
4
and
5
illustrate the overlap that exists in information captured by
\mathrm{PD},\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{A}},\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}
, and
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}
. However, the saecular approach complements the preceding notions in several important ways:

Inverse problems and cycle representatives.
The classical persistence diagram, PD, plays a preeminent role in modern topological data analysis. It is used when data takes the form of a filtered topological space
X_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq X_{n}
, from which one can derive a chain functor
f:[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}\to{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin},\;p\mapsto\mathsf{H}_{m}(X_{p};{\mathds{k}})
. A
basis of cycle representatives
for the functor
f
consists of two parts: (i) an internal direct sum decomposition
f=\bigoplus_{k}f^{k}
, where each
f^{k}
is a dimension-1 interval functor, and (ii) for each
k
, a cycle
z^{k}\in\mathsf{Z}_{m}(X_{n};{\mathds{k}})
that generates that summand
f^{k}
.

Cycle representatives have played a formative role in the development of homological persistence, because they allow one to map bars in the barcode back to data; concretely, one can localize the
k
th bar at the support of
z^{k}
,
\mathrm{supp}(z^{k})\subseteq X
. However, the notion of a persistent cycle representative is only defined for chain functors
f:\mathbf{I}\to{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
, at present, because chain functors valued in other categories generally cannot be expressed as direct sums of interval diagrams.

The saecular framework bridges this gap. In particular, we can define a
generating set
for interval
{I}
to be a family of cycles
S\subseteq\mathsf{Z}_{m}(X_{n})
that collectively generate the interval functor
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}({I})
, in the natural sense. Indeed, we can also define generators for barcodes in persistent
homotopy
,
cf.
§
3.4
. This is one of several concrete benefits of functoriality.

Vanishing of free components.

In generalized persistence, the torsion-free components of abelian groups either vanish (type-
\mathsf{B}
diagrams) or require the introduction of
sign conventions
(type-
\mathsf{A}
diagrams). The saecular barcode naturally accommodates the torsion-free component of abelian groups without the need for sign conventions.

Constraints on
f
and
\mathsf{E}
.
The generalized persistence diagrams
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}
are defined only for chain functors
f:\mathbf{R}\to\mathsf{E}
that meet certain criteria. For example,
\mathsf{E}
must be abelian and
f
must be
constructible
. Constructible functors resemble finite collections of constant functors, in the sense that every constructible functor
f
admits a finite collection of disjoint intervals
{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{m}
such that
\mathbf{R}={I}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{I}_{m}
and
f(a\leq b)
is an isomorphism whenever there exists an interval
{I}_{k}
such that
a,b\in{I}_{k}
.
The saecular persistence diagram exists under substantially looser conditions.

Extension problems.
As formalized in Theorem
5
, the saecular PD solves a basic extension problem posed by the type-
\mathsf{B}
generalized PD. In particular, if
\mathsf{E}\in\{R\mathsf{Mod},\mathsf{Mod}R\}
, the functor
f
is constructible, and each
f_{a}
has finite length as a module, then
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}_{f}({I})
represents the family of composition factors of the nonzero objects
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}({I})
.

Functoriality and uniqueness.
The saecular PD sits within a lager categorical framework. In particular, Theorem
2
implies that
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathrm{sae}}_{f}
is essentially the restriction of the saecular functor
{\Omega}
to the (full) subcategory of simple objects (i.e. singletons) in
\mathrm{Lcl}({\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I}))
. This fact has diverse structural implications, many of which will be expanded below. Moreover, while a given chain functor
f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
could admit many Krull-Schmidt decompositions in general, it admits at most
one
saecular homomorphism, and, up to canonical isomorphism, only one saecular functor and saecular PD.

There exists, moreover, a productive interaction between the formalisms of generalized and saecular persistence.
The authors of the present work might never have considered persistence outside
{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
were it not for the introduction of generalized persistence modules by
[
34
]
– and had not the author of that work posed the problem of how to compute
\mathrm{PD}^{\mathsf{B}}
, algorithmically, as an open problem. Conversely, the machinery of saecular persistence served as inspiration for important theoretical results concerning generalized persistence, e.g.
[
30
]
.

3.2
Series

The notion of a series has many useful realizations in abstract algebra – subnormal series, central series, composition series, etc. Saecular decomposition can also be repackaged as a series.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{I}
is well ordered and
\mathsf{E}\in\{R\mathsf{Mod},\mathsf{Mod}R,\mathsf{Group}\}
. For each linearization
\sigma
of

\forall
-complete lattice homomorphism
\Sigma:{\mathbb{A}(\sigma)}\to\mathrm{Sub}_{f}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma\;{\downarrow}\;_{\sigma}({I})}{\Sigma\;\mathring{\downarrow}_{\sigma}\;({I})}\in{\mathrm{Int}}({I})

(3)

for each
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
. To wit,

\Sigma={\Omega}|_{{\mathbb{A}(\sigma)}}

where
{\Omega}
is the saecular CDI homomorphism.

Definition 5
.

We call
\Sigma
the
subsaecular series
of
f
subordinate to
\sigma
. The fraction

\frac{\Sigma\;{\downarrow}\;_{\sigma}({I})}{\Sigma\;\mathring{\downarrow}_{\sigma}\;({I})}

is the
{I}^{th}
subsaecular factor
of
\sigma
. When
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{Group}
, the
{I}^{th}

, the associated factors are uniquely determined, up to isomorphism.

Theorem 7
.

The
{I}^{th}

{\Omega}(\;{\downarrow}\;{I})/{\Omega}(\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;{I})

Remark 3
.

If

means a totally ordered subset of
X
. The notions of boundedness and completeness which we have introduced for posets and lattices are also suitable for every chain, when regarded as an order lattice. Thus a
\mathring{\forall}
-complete chain in
\mathbf{L}
is a
\mathring{\forall}
-complete sublattice of
\mathbf{L}
that is totally ordered as a poset, a
bounded chain
in
\mathrm{P}
is a totally ordered subset containing maximum and minimum, etc.

Chain functors.

A
chain functor
in a category
\mathsf{E}
is a functor of form
f:\mathbf{I}\to\mathsf{E}
for some chain
\mathbf{I}
.
When
\mathsf{E}
has a zero object, the
support
of
f
can be defined

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(f):=\{i\in\mathbf{I}:f_{i}\neq 0\}.

A functor has
support type
S
if
\mathrm{supp}(f)\in\{S,\emptyset\}
. In particular, the 0 functor has every support type.

A chain functor is
interval
if

\displaystyle f(i\leq j)\quad\text{is}\quad\begin{cases}\text{invertible}&i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(f)\\
\text{zero}&else.\end{cases}

The support of every interval chain functor is an interval of
\mathbf{I}
. The
object type
of a nonzero interval functor is the isomorphism class
f_{i}
, where
i
is any element of
\mathrm{supp}(f)
. The object type of the zero functor is the isomorphism class of the zero object.

Categories.
We write
\mathsf{CD}
for the category of completely distributive lattices and complete lattice homomorphisms, and
\mathsf{P}
for the category of partially ordered sets and order-preserving functions. The category of sets and functions is
\mathsf{Set}
, and the category of pointed sets is
\mathsf{SetP}
. The category of groups and group homomorphisms is
\mathsf{Group}
. The category of vector spaces and finite-dimensional vector spaces over
{\mathds{k}}
are denoted
{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{Vect}
and
{\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
, respectively.

7
P-exact categories

Puppe exact categories are generalizations of abelian categories that may or may not be additive. Formally, a well-powered category
\mathsf{C}
is
Puppe exact
(or p-exact) if (i) it has a zero object, kernels, and cokernels, (ii) every mono is a kernel and every epi is a cokernel, (iii) every morphism has an epi-mono factorization. Here we review the definitions and prove some basic structural results used in later sections. The reader is referred to
[
24
]
for further details.

\displaystyle f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }:\mathrm{Sub}(A)\to\mathrm{Sub}(B)

Lemma 10
.

The direct image operator of an arrow

\mathrm{Sub}(A)
containing the kernel of

. The dual statement holds for inverse image operator.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a
\forall
-complete CD sublattice of
\mathrm{Sub}(A)
that contains

, and fix

. Complete distributivity then provides the third of the following identities

\textstyle f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(\bigwedge S)=f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(f^{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }\bigwedge S)=f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }((\bigwedge S)\vee k)=f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(\bigwedge\{s\vee k:s\in S\})=\\
\textstyle f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(\bigwedge\{f^{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }s:s\in S\})=f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(f^{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }\bigwedge\{f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }s:s\in S\})=\bigwedge f_{\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ }(S).

This establishes the claim for the direct image operator, since

preserves existing joins. The claim for inverse image is equivalent, by duality. If we assume, instead, that

is distributive, then similar argument shows the direct and inverse image operators preserve binary meets and joins.
∎

Given any index category
\mathsf{I}
, we denote the category of
\mathsf{I}

by

. Diagrams make new p-exact categories from old.

Lemma 11

(P-exact diagrams)
.

Let
\mathsf{I}
be a small category.

1.

The category of diagrams
[\mathsf{I},\mathsf{E}]
is p-exact.

2.

Kernels and cokernels obtain object-wise. Concretely,

is exact in

if and only if

is exact in
\mathsf{E}
, for each
i
.

3.

An arrow

in

is mono iff

is mono for each
i
. Likewise for epis.

4.

If
(B^{j})_{j\in J}
is an indexed family of subdiagrams of
A
and
\bigvee_{j}B^{j}_{i}
exists for each

, then
\bigvee B
exists and obtains object-wise. The dual statement holds for meets.

Proof.

Claim 1 is a routine exercise, as observed in
[
24
, p. 48]
. Coincidence of exact and object-wise exact sequences follows from the universal property of (co)kernels. Claim 3 follows Claim 2, since p-exact categories have normal epis and monos

24

. Claim 4 follows Claim 3.
∎

7.2
Regular and canonical induction

If
\mathsf{C}
is any category with subobjects and

such that
\mathrm{Grs}(\mathrm{P}(A))=\{(M,N)\in\mathrm{Sub}(A)^{2}:M\leq N\leq A\}

and

,
cf.

[
24
]
. The following lemma can be proved directly using the axioms of kernels and cokernels.

Lemma 12
.

Regularly induced maps are closed under composition. More precisely, for each object
A
in
\mathsf{E}

there exists a functor
\mathsf{F}:\mathrm{P}(A)\to\mathsf{E}
sending
{(M,N)}
to
N/M
and sending each relation
(M,N)\leq(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
to the regularly induced map
N/M\to N^{\prime}/M^{\prime}
.

Following
[
24
]

on the family of subquotients of

such that

\displaystyle N/M\;\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}\;N^{\prime}/M^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle M\vee N^{\prime}=N\vee M^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad M\wedge N^{\prime}=M^{\prime}\wedge N.

(21)

Remark 5
.

Grandis defines this relation,

, in a rather more general context than that which we require here. Equivalence between (

) and this more abstract formulation appears in expression (4.3.2) of
[
24
]
, and a more accessible interpretation appears in the proof of Theorem 4.3.5.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
M\subseteq N
and
M^{\prime}\subseteq N^{\prime}
are elements of the lattice
\mathbf{L}
of closed sets in a semitopology. Then

\displaystyle N/M\;\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}\;N^{\prime}/M^{\prime}\quad\iff\quad N-M=N^{\prime}-M^{\prime}

If

N\vee N^{\prime}=N\vee M^{\prime}=M\vee N^{\prime}
and
M\wedge M^{\prime}=M\wedge N^{\prime}=M^{\prime}\wedge N

[
24
, p. 23]

∎

\mathbf{L}

The main result of this section is Theorem

.

9.3
Classification of point-wise finite dimensional chain diagrams, redux

The existence of Krull-Schmidt decomposition for arbitrary chain functors

was first shown by Botnan and Crawley-Boevey

. The saecular framework provides a short new proof – in addition to a structural connection with saecular functors.

Theorem 29
.

If
\mathbf{I}
is a totally ordered set and
\mathsf{E}={\mathds{k}}\mathsf{VectFin}
, then every functor

admits a saecular CDI functor

. Moreover,

\displaystyle f\cong\bigoplus_{{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})}{\Omega}\{{I}\}.

Proof.

In this case
f
satisfies the well ordered criterion of Theorem

, so the saecular CDF functor exists. For each
{I}\in{\mathbb{I}}(\mathbf{I})
fix an indexed family of sets
(B_{i}^{I})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
such that

and

is a basis for
{\Omega}(\{{I}\})_{i}
for each

, where

is the quotient map

{\Omega}(\downarrow{I}\twoheadrightarrow\{{I}\})

We call each

an
{I}
-prebasis
in

. We claim that one can choose

such that

. If

is finite-dimensional, the indexed family of inverse images

, by fixing a linearization

is a prebasis for

.
∎

As in the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle V=\bigsqcup_{U\in\mathrm{Succ}(C)}(\Lambda\;{\downarrow}\;\xi_{U})-(\Lambda\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;\xi_{U})=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathrm{Im}(\xi)}(\Lambda\;{\downarrow}\;a)-(\Lambda\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;a)=\bigsqcup_{a\in\sigma}(\Lambda\;{\downarrow}\;a)-(\Lambda\;{\mathring{\downarrow}}\;a)

10
Saecular persistence in
\mathsf{Group}

Most of the results presented for p-exact categories thus far have natural analogs for chain diagrams in
\mathsf{Group}
. In general, however, these results require independent proofs. A fundamental point of disconnect between the two domains is the following structural distinction: in a p-exact category, every subobject of form
\mathrm{K}_{f}(a)
or
\hat{\mathrm{K}}_{f}(a)
is
normal
, meaning that it is the kernel of some arrow
f\to g
in
[\mathbf{I},\mathsf{E}]
. Such is not the case for chain diagrams in
\mathsf{Group}
. In fact,
g
is normal in
f
iff
g_{a}\unlhd f_{a}
for all
a\in\mathbf{I}
. This becomes relevant for the following reasons:

•

The lattice of normal subgroups, like the lattice of normal objects in any p-exact category, is modular; however, subgroup lattices are not normal in general. Since we relied heavily on modularity to prove existence of the saecular BD homomorphism for p-exact categories, we therefore need a new proof of existence of SBD homomorphisms in
\mathsf{Group}
.

•

While
\mathsf{Group}
has kernels and cokernels, a monomorphism
g:H\hookrightarrow L
satisfies
g=\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{coker}(g))
iff
g
is normal. In particular, even if
H,L
lie in the image of a lattice homomorphism,
\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{coker}(g))
may not.

In light of these and other hurdles, it is surprising that the machinery built for p-exact generally categories carries over to
\mathsf{Group}
in any way. The successful transfer of ideas from one domain to the other therefore speaks both to the power of p-exact categories as a source of inspiration, and to the power of order lattices to bridge disciplines.

, as well as the saecular CDH, CDIH, BDH, BDIH, and corresponding saecular functors, are defined exactly as in §
9
. As per convention, we write

for the value taken by one of these maps on a locally closed set

.

Let us say that a subdiagram

is
normal
, written

10.4
Non-normal subdiagrams

In light of Theorem
46
, it is natural to ask whether some version of the saecular functor can still be constructed, even when there exists a map

whose image is not normal in
f_{b}
. Remarkably, this can be done; the only essential change is that we must work with cosets, rather than quotient groups.

The overall strategy can be summarized as follows. We will define a relation
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{Gp}}
on coset families, which is similar to the relation
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}
which exists for subquotients in a p-exact category. Like
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}
, the relation
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{Gp}}
is an equivalence relation. Also like
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}
, the statement
(N/M)\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{Gp}}(N^{\prime}/M^{\prime})
will turn out to imply that
N/M
is Noether isomorphic to
N^{\prime}/M^{\prime}
– not in the category of groups or even the category of sets equipped with group actions, but in the category
\mathsf{SetP}
of pointed sets.

The relation
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{Gp}}
will allow us to define quasi-regular induction for
\mathsf{Group}
in much the same way that we defined quasi-regular induction for p-exact categories,
cf.
diagram (
71
). We will then complete the construction using a cohesive family of isomorphisms related to
\Phi_{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{Gp}}
; the result is a close analogue of the saecular functor.
