3.

In this section, we study ring extensions in terms of

Lemma 3.1
.

[
4

Let
R
be a ring. Then
\delta(R[[x]])\subseteq\delta(R)+\langle x\rangle
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring. If
R[[x]]
is a
\delta
-reversible ring, Then

-reversible ring.

Proof.

The proof is clear via Lemma

Recall that a
Morita context
is a 4-tuple
\begin{bmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}
where
A,B
are rings,
{}_{A}M_{B}
and
{}_{B}N_{A}
are bimodules and there exist context products
M\times N\rightarrow A
and
N\times M\rightarrow B
written multiplicatively as
(w,z)\mapsto wz
and
(z,w)\mapsto zw
such that
\begin{bmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}
is an associative ring with the obvious matrix operations.

Morita contexts were introduced by Morita in
[
7
]
. A Morita context
\begin{bmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}
is called
trivial
if the context products are trivial, i.e.
MN=0
and
NM=0
. This is also called
null context
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}
is a trivial Morita context. Then
\delta(R)\subseteq\begin{bmatrix}\delta(A)&M\\
N&\delta(B)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Let
r=\begin{bmatrix}a&m\\
n&b\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\delta(R)
. To see that
a\in\delta(A)
, let
aA+I=A
where
I
is a right ideal of
A
. It is enough to show that
I
is a direct summand of
A
. Since
rR+\begin{bmatrix}I&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}=R
and
r\in\delta(R)
,
\begin{bmatrix}I&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}
is a direct summand of
R
. Hence there exists
\Big(\begin{bmatrix}f&m\\
n&g\\
\end{bmatrix}\Big)^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}f&m\\
n&g\\
\end{bmatrix}\in R
such that
\begin{bmatrix}I&M\\
N&B\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f&m\\
n&g\\
\end{bmatrix}R
. Thus we have
f^{2}=f\in A
and
I=fA
which shows that
I
is a direct summand of
A
. So
a\in\delta(A)
. Similarly, it can be shown that
b\in\delta(B)
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}A&M\\

\end{bmatrix}

-reversible, then

Proof.

Assume that

\delta

2.15
,

and

are
\delta
-reversible.
∎

The rings
H_{(s,t)}(R)
:
Let
R
be a ring and
s,t\in C(R)\cap U(R)
. Set

H_{(s,t)}(R)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&0&0\\
c&d&e\\
0&0&f\end{bmatrix}\in M_{3}(R)\mid a,c,d,e,f\in R,a-d=sc,d-f=te\right\}.

Then
H_{(s,t)}(R)
is a subring of
M_{3}(R)
.
Now we give an important result which is proved in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then

\delta(H_{(s,t)}(R))=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&0&0\\
c&d&e\\
0&0&f\end{bmatrix}\in M_{2}(R):a,d,f\in\delta(R)\right\}.

Now we have the following.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then
R
is
\delta
-reversible if and only if
H_{(s,t)}(R)
is
\delta
-reversible.

Proof.

Let
R
be a
\delta
-reversible ring. Assume that
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&0&0\\
c_{1}&d_{1}&e_{1}\\
0&0&f_{1}\end{bmatrix}
,
B=\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}&0&0\\
c_{2}&d_{2}&e_{2}\\
0&0&f_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in H_{(s,t)}(R)
such that
AB=0
. With a basic computation we handle
a_{1}a_{2}=d_{1}d_{2}=f_{1}f_{2}=0
. Since
R
is
\delta
-reversible,
a_{2}a_{1},d_{2}d_{1},f_{2}f_{1}\in\delta(R)
. So
BA=\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}a_{1}&0&0\\
c_{2}a_{1}+d_{2}c_{1}&d_{2}d_{1}&d_{2}e_{1}+e_{2}f_{1}\\
0&0&f_{2}f_{1}\end{bmatrix}\in\delta(H_{(s,t)}(R))
by Lemma
3.8
. The converse is clear.
∎

Generalized matrix rings:
Let
R
be a ring and
s
a central element of
R

\begin{bmatrix}R&R\\
R&R\end{bmatrix}
becomes a ring denoted by
K_{s}(R)
with addition defined componentwise and multiplication defined in
[
6
]

\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&x_{1}\\
y_{1}&b_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}&x_{2}\\
y_{2}&b_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}+sx_{1}y_{2}&a_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}b_{2}\\
y_{1}a_{2}+b_{1}y_{2}&sy_{1}x_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

In
[
6
]
,
K_{s}(R)
is called a
generalized matrix ring
over
R
.
Now we give an important result.

Lemma 3.12
.

\delta(K_{0}(R))=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in K_{0}(R)\mid a,d\in\delta(R)\right\}
.

Proof.

\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in K_{0}(R)\mid a,d\in\delta(R)\right\}\subseteq\delta(K_{0}(R))
by
[
5
]
.
\delta(K_{0}(R))\subseteq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in K_{0}(R)\mid a,d\in\delta(R)\right\}
can be shown similarly to the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then
R
is
\delta
-reversible if and only if
K_{0}(R)
is
\delta
-reversible.

Proof.

Let
R
be a
\delta
reversible ring. Assume that
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
,
B=\begin{bmatrix}x&y\\
z&t\end{bmatrix}
and
AB=0
. So
ax=dt=0
. Since
R
is
\delta
- reversible,
xa
,
td\in\delta(R)
. A basic computation shows that
BA\in\delta(R)
. By Lemma
3.12
, converse is clear.
∎
