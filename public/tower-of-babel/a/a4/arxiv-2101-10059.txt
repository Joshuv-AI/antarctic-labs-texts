Membranes with thin and heavy inclusions: asymptotics of spectra
Yuriy Golovaty

Abstract
. We study the asymptotic behaviour of eigenvalues of 2D vibrating systems with mass density perturbed in a vicinity of closed curves. The threshold case in which the resonance frequencies of the membrane and the frequencies of thin inclusion coincide is investigated. The perturbed eigenvalue problem can be realized as a family of self-adjoint operators acting on varying Hilbert spaces. However the so-called limit operator is non-self-adjoint and possesses the Jordan chains of length 2 2 . Apart from the lack of self-adjointness, the operator has non-compact resolvent. As a consequence, its spectrum has a complicated structure, for instance, the spectrum contains a countable set of eigenvalues with infinite multiplicity. The complete asymptotic analysis of eigenvalues has been carried out.

1.
Introduction and Statement of Problem

The mechanical systems with strongly inhomogeneous mass distributions have become the subject of intensive experimental and theoretical studies
since the time of Poisson and Bessel
[
1
, Ch.2]
, and a lot of research has been devoted to the analysis of vibrating systems with so-called added masses.
Historically, the ﬁrst relevant mathematical models in classical mechanics go back to the first half of the 20th century (see e.g.
[
2
]
and the references given there). Many authors have investigated properties of strings and rods with the mass densities perturbed by finite or infinite
sums
\sum M_{k}\delta(x-x_{k})
, where
\delta
is Dirac’s delta-function and
M_{k}
is an added mass at the point
x_{k}
.
Recently, such models in dimensions two and three with heavy inclusions of a different geometry are widely used not only in mechanics, but in various fields of science and technology such as physics of liquid crystals, physical chemistry of polymers, micelles and microemulsions, molecular theory, cell membrane theory
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. For instance, cell membranes are known to contain embedded proteins and various colloidal particles
[
6
]
.

In higher dimensions, the perturbation of mass densities by the
\delta
-functions often leads to incorrect mathematical models, because the formal differential equations which appear have no mathematical meaning. As an example of such ill-posed problem we can consider the eigenvalue problem for the Laplace operator

-\Delta u=\lambda(1+M\delta(x))u\text{ \ in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\text{ \ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing the origin. The equation has no non-trivial solution, because any such solution
u
has a singularity at
x=0
and therefore the product
\delta(x)u(x)=u(0)\delta(x)
is not defined. The new and at the same time obvious idea was instead to replace the
\delta
-function with its regularization
\varepsilon^{-3}q(x/\varepsilon)
, where
q
is a function of compact support, and study the asymptotic behaviour of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The problem was first investigated by E. Sánchez-Palencia
[
7
,
8
]
, who proved the existence of the so-called local eigenvibrations: the eigenfunctions are significant in a small neighborhood of the origin only.

The model in which the density is perturbed by
\sum M_{k}\delta(x-x_{k})
is not adequate even in the one-dimensional case, when dealing with the large
masses
M_{k}
. The very heavy inclusions cause a strong local reaction of vibrating system, but this phenomenon can not be described on the discrete set which is a support of the sum of Dirac’s functions. The geometry of small domains where the large masses are loaded should also have an effect on the form of eigenvibrations. In
[
9
]
, asymptotic analysis was applied to a spectral problem for the Sturm-Liouville operator with weight function of the form

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\rho(x)+\varepsilon^{-m}q(x/\varepsilon)
,
where
q
is a function of compact support and
m\in\mathbb{R}
. For the case
m=1
the perturbation is a
\delta
-like sequence, but the most interesting cases of the limit behaviour of eigenfunctions as
\varepsilon\to 0
are those when the power
m
is greater than
1
.

These advanced models have attracted considerable attention in the mathematical literature over the last three decades (see e.g.
[
10
]
for a review).
The spectral properties of differential operators with weight functions having the form

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\rho(x)+\sum\varepsilon^{-m_{k}}q_{k,\varepsilon}(x),

where

are compactly supported in vicinity of different sets,
have been investigated in numerous articles. We mention here

[

]
for the Laplace operator in dimension
3
,
[
12
,
13
]
for ordinary differential operators of the fourth order and the biharmonic operator,
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
for boundary value problems on junctions of a very complicated geometry,
[
17
,
18
]
for the Sturm-Liouville operators on metric graphs.
The spectral properties of strings with rapidly oscillating and periodic densities have been treated in
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
in the framework of homogenization theory. Another model in which the heavy inclusions were regarded as rigid ones has been studied in
[

Since the

s of the last century, a series of papers was published concerning 2D and 3D elastic systems with many concentrated masses near the boundary

[

(1.1)

Let us consider the spectral problem

-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+au_{\varepsilon}=\lambda^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\ell u_{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

This paper is a continuation of

Also, it has been shown that there exist five different limit behaviours for the spectrum and eigenspaces depending on

,

,

and

From the mathematical viewpoint, the difference between the cases is that the spectral parameter in the limit eigenvalue problems appears alternately both in a differential equation on
\Omega
and in an equation on the strip
\gamma\times(-1,1)
, which is a dilatation of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, and even in coupling conditions on
\gamma
.

The threshold case

is the most difficult and interesting one to analyse, because the spectral parameter is included simultaneously in two differential equations, and the limit spectral problem is associated with a non-self-adjoint operator.
The main insight of the present work is to exhibit this non-self-adjoint operator, its spectrum and generalized eigenspaces.

To better understand what happens in dimension
2
, we briefly discuss the similar model for a string. Recently, we have revised some results from
[
9
]
concerning the case
m=2
. In
[
38
]
, we have studied the limiting behavior, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, of eigenvalues
and eigenfunctions of the problem

\displaystyle-y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}+Qy_{\varepsilon}=\lambda^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\varepsilon}y_{\varepsilon},\quad x\in(a,b),

(1.3)

\displaystyle y_{\varepsilon}(a)\cos\alpha+y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(a)\sin\alpha=0,\quad y_{\varepsilon}(b)\cos\beta+y_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(b)\sin\beta=0

(1.4)

with the weight function
\rho_{\varepsilon}
given by (
1.1
), where
\Omega=(a,b)
is an interval containing the origin, and
\omega_{\varepsilon}=(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. For each real
\alpha
and
\beta
the problem can be associated with a family of self-adjoint operators
S_{\varepsilon}
in the weighted space

. The operators
S_{\varepsilon}
are defined by
S_{\varepsilon}\phi=\rho_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(-\phi^{\prime\prime}+Q\phi)
on functions

obeying boundary conditions (

are real, discrete and simple.
It is worth noting that the study of operator families acting on varying spaces entails some mathematical difficulties. First of all, the question arises how to understand the convergence of such families. Next, if these operators do converge in some sense, does this convergence implies the convergence of their spectra (see, e.g.,
[
19
, III.1]
,
[
39
,
40
]
).
By abandoning the self-adjointness, we have realized (
1.3
), (
1.4
) as a family of non-self-adjoint matrix operators
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
acting on the fixed Hilbert space
L_{2}(\rho,(a,b))\times L_{2}(q,(-1,1))
(see details in
[
38
]
). The operators are certainly similar to self-adjoint ones and their resolvents are compact. Moreover the spectra of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
and

coincide and the corresponding eigenspaces are isomorphic. It has been proved that
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
converge in the norm resolvent sense (the resolvents of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
converge in the uniform norm as

) to the matrix operator
\mathcal{A}
associated with the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle-v^{\prime\prime}+Qv=\lambda\rho v\;\;\text{in }(a,0)\cup(0,b),\quad-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda qw\;\;\text{in }(-1,1),

\displaystyle\quad w^{\prime}(-1)=0,\quad w^{\prime}(1)=0,\quad v(-0)=w(-1),\quad v(+0)=w(1),

\displaystyle\quad v(a)\cos\alpha+v^{\prime}(a)\sin\alpha=0,\quad v(b)\cos\beta+v^{\prime}(b)\sin\beta=0.

Surprisingly enough,

is not similar to a self-adjoint one, because it possesses multiple eigenvalues with the Jordan chains of length
2
. In view of
[
41
, Ch.1]
,
[
42
]
, the norm resolvent convergence

implies the “number-by-number” convergence of the corresponding eigenvalues and some results on the convergence of eigenspaces.

Problem (
1.2
) can also be associated with a family of matrix operators acting on the same Hilbert space. However, in two dimensions there is no uniform convergence of resolvents, because the limit operator
\mathcal{P}
which can be obtained in the strong resolvent topology has non-compact resolvent
[
43
, Theorem IV.2.26]
. It is known that the strong resolvent convergence of operators does not imply the convergence of their spectra. For this reason, we study the convergence of eigenvalues of (
1.2
) without touching on the convergence of corresponding operators. The operator
\mathcal{P}
is non-self-adjoint and possesses nontrivial Jordan chains. In Sec.
3
, we describe the spectrum of
\mathcal{P}
and the generalized eigenspaces. The spectrum is discrete and real; it consists of eigenvalues of three types:
(a)
eigenvalues of finite multiplicity with generalized eigenspaces generated by eigenvectors only;
(b)
infinite-fold eigenvalues with generalized eigenspaces generated by eigenvectors only;
(c)
infinite-fold eigenvalues with generalized eigenspaces containing both eigenvectors and generalized eigenvectors.
In fact, this spectrum is a union of spectra of two operators that correspond to two different parts of the vibrating system, namely, the membrane clamped along the curve
\gamma
and the thin heavy inclusion. Using the method of quasimodes we describe the asymptotic behaviour of eigenvalues of (
1.2
) as perturbation of
\sigma(\mathcal{P})
. In Sec.
4
and
5
, we prove the existence of a countable number of eigenvalues with the asymptotics
\lambda_{\varepsilon,k}=\lambda+\varepsilon\lambda_{1}^{(k)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of type
(b)
or
(c)
. The set of correctors
\{\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a spectrum of some pseudodifferential operator
N_{\lambda}
acting in
L_{2}(\gamma)
. In addition, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of type (c), there also exists
2d
eigenvalues with the half-integer power asymptotics
\mu_{\varepsilon,j}^{\pm}=\lambda\pm\varepsilon^{1/2}\lambda_{1/2,j}+\varepsilon\lambda_{1,j}^{\pm}+O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where

is a number of Jordan chains of length

corresponding to
\lambda
. In Sec.
6
we prove that there exist

eigenvalues of (
1.2
) with the asymptotics
\lambda_{\varepsilon,k}=\lambda+\varepsilon\lambda_{1}^{(k)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
, where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of type
(a)
with multiplicity

2.
Formal construction of the limit operator

It will be convenient to parameterize the curve
\gamma

.
Let

The domain

In the sequel, the coordinate

For any

We introduce operator

Obviously, the spectrum of

We look for the approximation to the eigenvalue
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
and the corresponding eigenfunction

of (
1.2
) in the form

\lambda^{\varepsilon}\sim\lambda+\cdots,\qquad u_{\varepsilon}(x)\sim\begin{cases}v(x)+\cdots&\text{if }x\in\Omega\setminus\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
w(s,\textstyle\frac{r}{\varepsilon})+\cdots&\text{if }x\in\omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Since

, the function

-\Delta v+av=\lambda\rho v

\ell v=0

. Of course,

To find these conditions, we must examine more closely the equation in (

) in a vicinity of

Returning to the local coordinates

,
we see that the vectors

\alpha=(\dot{\alpha}_{1},\dot{\alpha}_{2})

The Jacobian of transformation

We see that

In addition, the Laplace-Beltrami operator becomes

\Delta\phi=J^{-1}\left(\partial_{s}(J^{-1}\partial_{s}\phi)+\partial_{r}(J\partial_{r}\phi)\right).

In the local coordinates

\Delta=(1-\varepsilon n\varkappa)^{-1}\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\partial_{n}(1-\varepsilon n\varkappa)\partial_{n}+\partial_{s}\big((1-\varepsilon n\varkappa)^{-1}\partial_{s}\big)\right).

From this we readily deduce the representation

\Delta=\varepsilon^{-2}\partial^{2}_{n}-\varepsilon^{-1}\varkappa\partial_{n}-n\varkappa^{2}\partial_{n}+\partial^{2}_{s}+\varepsilon(2n\varkappa\partial^{2}_{s}+n\varkappa^{\prime}\partial_{s}-n^{2}\varkappa^{3}\partial_{n})+\varepsilon^{2}P_{\varepsilon},

(2.2)

where

is a PDE of the second order on

and the first one on

whose coefficients are uniformly bounded in
\omega
with respect to
\varepsilon
.
Then using this representation and (

) we obtain the equation
-\partial^{2}_{n}w=\lambda qw
in
\omega
.
Next, the eigenfunction

as an element of
\mathop{\rm dom}T_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the conditions

[u_{\varepsilon}]_{-\varepsilon}=0,\quad[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\varepsilon}=0,\quad[\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}]_{-\varepsilon}=0,\quad[\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}]_{\varepsilon}=0,

(2.3)

where
[\,\cdot\,]_{t}
stands for the jump of a function across

. These conditions imply

and

. Combining the latter equalities, we can formally deduce that the pair

must be an eigenvector of the spectral problem

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta v+av=\lambda\rho v\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\qquad\ell v=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(2.4)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{n}w=\lambda qw\>\text{ in }\omega,\quad\partial_{n}w(s,-1)=0,\;\>\partial_{n}w(s,1)=0,

(2.5)

\displaystyle v^{-}=w(s,-1),\quad v^{+}=w(s,1),\quad s\in S

We will show that the problem can be associated with a non-self-adjoint matrix operator. Moreover this operator is not similar to a self-adjoint one, because it possesses generalized eigenvectors. It means that the limit problem admits no self-adjoint realization. It it worth mentioning that the similar non-self-adjoint problem appeared in

give us nontrivial examples of self-adjoint operators acting on varying Hilbert spaces that converge in some sense to a non-self-adjoint operator.

3.
Properties of the limit operator

We use the following notation. The spectrum and resolvent set of a linear operator
T
are
denoted by
\sigma(T)
and
\varrho(T)
, respectively. Let
T^{*}
denote the adjoint operator of
T
. For any
z\in\varrho(T)
,
\mathrm{R}_{z}(T)=(T-zI)^{-1}
is the resolvent operator. Here and subsequently,
I
is an identity operator.

3.1.
Spectrum of the limit operator

We introduce the operators

\displaystyle\mathring{A}=\rho^{-1}(-\Delta+a)\quad\text{in }L_{2}(\rho,\Omega),\quad\mathop{\rm dom}\mathring{A}=\{f\in W_{2}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\gamma)\colon\ell f=0\;\;\text{on }\partial\Omega\},

\displaystyle B=-q^{-1}\partial^{2}_{n}\quad\text{in }L_{2}(q,\omega),\quad\mathop{\rm dom}B=\left\{g\in W_{2}^{2,0}(\omega)\colon\partial_{n}g(\,\cdot\,,-1)=\partial_{n}g(\,\cdot\,,1)=0\right\},

where
W_{2}^{2,0}(\omega)
is the anisotropic Sobolev space

W_{2}^{2,0}(\omega)=\left\{g\in L_{2}(\omega)\colon\partial^{k}_{n}g\in L_{2}(\omega)\text{ for }k=1,2\right\}.

In the space
\mathcal{L}=L_{2}(\rho,\Omega)\times L_{2}(q,\omega)
we consider the matrix operator

\mathcal{P}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathring{A}&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{P}=\bigl\{(f,g)\in\mathop{\rm dom}\mathring{A}\times\mathop{\rm dom}B\colon\\
f^{-}=g(\,\cdot\,,-1),\>\;f^{+}=g(\,\cdot\,,1)\bigr\}.

Now (
2.4
)–(
2.6
) can be written as
\mathcal{P}u=\lambda u
with the notation
u=(v,w)^{\top}
. For simplicity of notation we often write
(v,w)
instead of
(v,w)^{\top}
.
Note that the operator
\mathcal{P}
is non-self-adjoint. Direct computations show that

\mathcal{P}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mathring{B}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{P}^{*}=\bigl\{(f,g)\in\mathop{\rm dom}A\times W_{2}^{2,0}(\omega)\colon\\
\partial_{r}f^{-}=-\partial_{n}g(\,\cdot\,,-1),\>\;\partial_{r}f^{+}=\partial_{n}g(\,\cdot\,,1)\bigr\},

where
A
is the restrictions of
\mathring{A}
to
\mathop{\rm dom}A=\{f\in\mathop{\rm dom}\mathring{A}\colon f=0\;\text{ on }\gamma\}

and
\mathring{B}
is the extension of
B
to the whole space
W_{2}^{2,0}(\omega)
. Here
\partial_{r}f^{\pm}
stand for the one-side traces of the normal derivative of
f
on
\gamma
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The spectrum of
B
consists of a countable set of real eigenvalues of infinite multiplicity. Moreover,
\lambda
belongs to
\sigma(B)
if and only if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the Sturm-Liouville problem

y^{\prime\prime}+\lambda q(n)y=0,\;\;n\in(-1,1),\quad y^{\prime}(-1)=0,\;\;y^{\prime}(1)=0.

(3.1)

Proof.

Obviously, the operator
B
is self-adjoint.
For given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
g\in L_{2}(q,\omega)
, the equation
(B-\lambda I)\phi=g
can be treated as the boundary value problem for the ordinary differential equation

-(\partial^{2}_{n}+\lambda q(n))\phi=q(n)g(s,n)\;\text{ in }\omega,\quad\partial_{n}\phi(s,-1)=0,\;\>\partial_{n}\phi(s,1)=0

with the parameter
s\in S
. If
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of (
3.1
), then the problem has a unique solution
\phi(s,\,\cdot\,)
for each
g(s,\,\cdot\,)\in L_{2}(-1,1)
and almost all
s\in S
. Moreover
\phi
belongs to
L_{2}(q,\omega)
in view of its integral representation via the Green function.
Otherwise, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of (
3.1
) with eigenfunction
y
, the problem is unsolvable for some right-hand sides
g
, because the corresponding homogeneous problem has infinitely many linearly independent solutions of the form
b(s)y(n)
, where
b\in L_{2}(\gamma)
.
Therefore the spectrum of
B
consists of all eigenvalues of (
3.1
), and the corresponding eigenspaces are infinite-dimensional.

Theorem 3.2
.

The operator
\mathcal{P}
has real discrete spectrum. Moreover

\sigma(\mathcal{P})=\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(B).

Proof.

Let us construct the resolvent of
\mathcal{P}
in an explicit form. Given
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,
f\in L_{2}(\rho,\Omega)
and

(\mathring{A}-\mu I)v=f
,
(B-\mu I)w=g
.
The second equation admits a unique solution

if
\mu\in\varrho(B)
, and then

v
is a solution of the problem

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta v+av-\mu\rho v=\rho f\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(3.2)

\displaystyle v^{-}=w(\,\cdot\,,-1),\quad v^{+}=w(\,\cdot\,,1).

Suppose the operator

T(\mu)\colon W_{2}^{2}(\omega)\to L_{2}(\rho,\Omega)
solves the problem

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta\phi+a\phi-\mu\rho\phi=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell\phi=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\phi^{-}=\psi(\,\cdot\,,-1),\quad\phi^{+}=\psi(\,\cdot\,,1)

for a given function
\psi\in W_{2}^{2}(\omega)
. If
\mu\in\varrho(A)
, then the problem has a unique solution
\phi=T(\mu)\psi
and hence
T(\mu)
is bounded.
Next,
v
can be represented as the sum of a solution of (
3.2
) subject to the homogeneous boundary conditions on
\gamma_{\pm}
and a solution of (
3.3
) with
\psi=w
.
Therefore

v=R_{\mu}(A)f+T(\mu)w=R_{\mu}(A)f+T(\mu)R_{\mu}(B)g,

provided
\mu\in\varrho(A)\cap\varrho(B)
.
Then the resolvent of
\mathcal{P}
can be written in the form

R_{\mu}(\mathcal{P})=\begin{pmatrix}R_{\mu}(A)&T(\mu)R_{\mu}(B)\\
0&R_{\mu}(B)\end{pmatrix}.

The equality
\sigma(\mathcal{P})=\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(B)
follows directly from this representation and the fact that
T(\mu)
is bounded for
\mu\in\varrho(A)
. Obviously,
\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(B)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{P})
. Suppose, contrary to our claim, that
\sigma(\mathcal{P})
is not contained in
\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(B)
. Then there exists
\mu\in\sigma(\mathcal{P})
such that
\mu\in\rho(A)\cap\rho(B)
. Hence, the operators
R_{\mu}(A)
,
R_{\mu}(B)
and

are bounded, and therefore, the operator
R_{\mu}(\mathcal{P})
is also bounded, a contradiction. Clearly,
\sigma(\mathcal{P})
is real and discrete, because
A
and
B
are self-adjoint operators with discrete spectra.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The operator
\mathcal{P}
has non-compact resolvent.

Proof.

The entry
R_{\mu}(B)
of the matrix
R_{\mu}(\mathcal{P})
is a non-compact operator, which is clear from Lemma
3.1
. The main reason for which
R_{\mu}(B)
is not compact is that the second derivative
\partial^{2}_{n}
is a non-elliptic PDE in the two-dimensional domain
\omega
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The operator
A
can be represent as the direct sum
A_{-}\oplus A_{+}
, where

\displaystyle A_{-}=\rho^{-1}(-\Delta+a),\quad\mathop{\rm dom}A_{-}=\{f\in W_{2}^{2}(\Omega_{-})\colon\ell f=0\;\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\;f=0\;\text{ on }\gamma\},

\displaystyle A_{+}=\rho^{-1}(-\Delta+a),\quad\mathop{\rm dom}A_{+}=\{f\in W_{2}^{2}(\Omega_{+})\colon f=0\text{ on }\gamma\}.

Hence

\sigma(\mathcal{P})=\sigma(A_{-})\cup\sigma(A_{+})\cup\sigma(B)
.

3.2.
Structure of generalized eigenspaces

Let
X_{\mu}
be the generalized eigenspace corresponding to
\mu\in\sigma(\mathcal{P})
, i.e.,
X_{\mu}=\{h\in\mathcal{L}\colon h\in\ker(\mathcal{P}-\mu I)^{k}\text{ for some }k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. If a vector
h
belongs to
\ker(\mathcal{P}-\mu I)^{j}
and
(\mathcal{P}-\mu I)^{j-1}h\neq 0
, one says that
h
is a generalized eigenvector of rank
j
. The eigenspace
X_{\mu}^{0}=\ker(\mathcal{P}-\mu I)
is a subspace of
X_{\mu}
; eigenvectors are precisely the generalized eigenvectors of rank
1
.

3.2.1.
Case
\lambda\in\sigma(A)\setminus\sigma(B)
.

We look first for non-trivial solutions
u=(v,w)
of
\mathcal{P}u=\lambda u
.
Since
\lambda
does not belong to
\sigma(B)
, we have
w=0
.
This in turn implies
v|_{\gamma}=0
, by (
2.6
). Therefore
v
must be a eigenvector of
A
corresponding to
\lambda
. All eigenvalues of
A
have finite multiplicities. Assume that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
K
and
V_{1}
,…,
V_{K}
are the eigenfunctions of
A
such that

\int_{\Omega}\rho V_{i}V_{j}\,dx=\delta_{i,j},\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,K.

(3.4)

Here
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker symbol. Then
X_{\lambda}^{0}
is spanned by

u_{1}=(V_{1},0),\;\;u_{2}=(V_{2},0),\;\;\dots\;\;,u_{K}=(V_{K},0).

(3.5)

The generalized eigenvectors of rank
2
satisfy the equation

(\mathcal{P}-\lambda I)u_{*}=u
, where
u
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{P}
. There is no such vectors
u_{*}=(v_{*},w_{*})
in this case, because the second components of all the eigenvectors
u_{k}
are zero. In fact,
Bw_{*}=\lambda w_{*}
implies
w_{*}=0
. Therefore
(A-\lambda I)v_{*}=v
, where
v
is a linear combination of
V_{j}
. The last equation is unsolvable, since
A
is self-adjoint.

3.2.2.
Case
\lambda\in\sigma(B)\setminus\sigma(A)
.

Suppose that
y
is an eigenfunction of (
3.1
) corresponding to
\lambda
. Then
B
has a countable collection of linearly independent eigenfunctions

\big\{b_{j}(s)y(n)\big\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, where
\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a basis in
L_{2}(\gamma)
consisting of smooth functions. On the other hand, the problem

-\Delta v+av=\lambda\rho v\>\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v=0\>\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\quad v^{-}=y(-1)b,\quad v^{+}=y(1)b

(3.6)

is uniquely solvable for any
b\in C^{\infty}(\gamma)
, since
\lambda
does not belong to
\sigma(A)
. Therefore

\mathcal{P}
possesses the countable set of linearly independent eigenvectors

u_{1}=(v_{1},b_{1}y),\;u_{2}=(v_{2},b_{2}y),\;\dots\;,u_{j}=(v_{j},b_{j}y),\;\dots,

(3.7)

where
v_{j}
is a solution of (
3.6
) with
b_{j}
in place of
b
. Note that the values
y(\pm 1)
are different from zero and hence all
v_{j}
are non-trivial solutions.
In this case, finding generalized eigenvectors of rank
2
leads to the equation
(B-\lambda I)w_{*}=by
, which is unsolvable for
b\neq 0
. Hence
X_{\lambda}=X^{0}_{\lambda}
and
\dim X_{\lambda}=\infty
.

3.2.3.
Case
\lambda\in\sigma(A)\cap\sigma(B)
.

Since
\lambda
belongs to
\sigma(B)
, any eigenvector of
\mathcal{P}
has the form
(v(x),b(s)y(n))
, where
v
is a solution of (
3.6
). But now we can not solve problem (
3.6
) for any function
b
, since
\lambda
is a point of
\sigma(A)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\in L_{2}(\Omega)
and
b_{\pm}\in W_{2}^{3/2}(\gamma)
.
Assume that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
of multiplicity
K
and the corresponding eigenfunctions
V_{1},\dots,V_{K}
satisfy (
3.4
). Then the problem

-\Delta v+av=\lambda\rho v+f\>\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v=0\>\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\quad v^{-}=b_{-},\quad v^{+}=b_{+}

(3.8)

admits a solution
v\in W_{2}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\gamma)
if and only if

\int_{\gamma}\big(b_{+}\partial_{r}V_{k}^{+}-b_{-}\partial_{r}V_{k}^{-}\big)\,d\gamma+\int_{\Omega}fV_{k}\,dx=0

(3.9)

for all
k=1,\dots,K
.

Proof.

This statement is a simple consequence of the Fredholm alternative for the self-adjoint operator
A
with compact resolvent.
Conditions (
3.9
) can be obtained
by multiplying the equation in (
3.8
) by
V_{1},\dots,V_{K}
in turn and then integrating by parts
twice in view of the boundary conditions.
∎

In view of Proposition
3.5
, problem (
3.6
) is solvable if and only if
(b,\Psi_{k})_{L_{2}(\gamma)}=0

for all
k=1,\dots,K
, where

\Psi_{k}=y(1)\,\partial_{r}V_{k}^{+}-y(-1)\,\partial_{r}V_{k}^{-}.

(3.10)

Let

be the subspace in

spanned by
\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{K}
. Hence the solvability of (

) is equivalent to the orthogonality of

to

.

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A=A_{-}\oplus A_{+}
and
k_{\pm}
is the multiplicity of
\lambda
in the spectrum of
A_{\pm}
. Then
\dim H_{\lambda}\geq\max(k_{+},k_{-})
.

Proof.

Since the operator
A
is a direct sum of
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
, we can choose a basis in the eigenspace of
A
such that
V_{1},\dots,V_{k_{+}}
are identically equal to zero in
\Omega_{-}
and the rest of
k_{-}
eigenfunctions
V_{k_{+}+1},\dots,V_{K}
are identically equal to zero in
\Omega_{+}
. Note that
k_{+}+k_{-}=K
.
Then
H_{\lambda}
is the linear span of

\partial_{r}V_{1}^{+},\,\ldots\,,\partial_{r}V_{k_{+}}^{+},\,\partial_{r}V_{k_{+}+1}^{-},\,\ldots\,,\partial_{r}V_{K}^{-}.

(3.11)

In general, these normal derivatives are linearly dependent in
L_{2}(\gamma)
, but the first of
k_{+}
derivatives are always linearly independent as well as the last of
k_{-}
ones.
Suppose, contrary to our claim, that the functions
\partial_{r}V_{1}^{+},\,\ldots\,,\partial_{r}V_{k_{+}}^{+}
are linearly dependent in
L_{2}(\gamma)
. Then there exists an eigenfunction
v
of the Dirichlet type problem
-\Delta v+av=\lambda\rho v
in
\Omega_{+}
,
v=0
on
\partial\Omega_{+}
such that
\partial_{r}v=0
on the whole boundary of
\Omega_{+}
, but this is impossible. The same conclusion can be drawn for the second part of the normal derivatives.
∎

Assume
\dim H_{\lambda}=d
and choose a basis
\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
L_{2}(\gamma)
such that
b_{1}
,…,
b_{d}
belong to
H_{\lambda}
, while
b_{k}
for

are elements of
H^{\perp}_{\lambda}
, and
b_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\gamma)
.
Then

(V_{1},0),\,\dots,\,(V_{K},0),\,(v_{d+1},b_{d+1}y),\,(v_{d+2},b_{d+2}y),\dots

(3.12)

is a countable set of linearly independent eigenvectors of
\mathcal{P}
.
Here
v_{j}
is a solution of (
3.6
) for
b=b_{j}
which is orthogonal to the span of
V_{1},\dots,V_{K}
in
L_{2}(\rho,\Omega)
. So
X_{\lambda}^{0}
is infinite-dimensional.

In this case, we can also find the generalized eigenvectors
u_{*}=(v_{*},w_{*})
of rank
2
. They satisfy the equation

(\mathcal{P}-\lambda)u_{*}=u
, where
u=(v,w)
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{P}
. Hence
v_{*}
and
w_{*}
solve the problem

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta v_{*}+av_{*}=\lambda\rho v_{*}+\rho v\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v_{*}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(3.13)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{n}w_{*}=\lambda qw_{*}+qw\quad\text{in }\omega,\qquad\partial_{n}w_{*}(\,\cdot\,,-1)=0,\quad\partial_{n}w_{*}(\,\cdot\,,1)=0,

(3.14)

\displaystyle v_{*}^{-}=w_{*}(\,\cdot\,,-1),\quad v_{*}^{+}=w_{*}(\,\cdot\,,1).

(3.15)

It is easily seen that (
3.13
)–(
3.15
) is unsolvable if

.
Therefore the generalized eigenvectors can be associated with some non-trivial linear combinations of the eigenvectors
(V_{1},0),\dots,(V_{K},0)
only.
We set

and
v=c_{1}V_{1}+\cdots+c_{K}V_{K}
. Then
w_{*}(s,n)=b_{*}(s)y(n)
and

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta v_{*}+av_{*}=\lambda\rho v_{*}+\rho\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}V_{j}\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v_{*}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(3.16)

\displaystyle v_{*}^{-}=y(-1)\,b_{*},\quad v_{*}^{+}=y(1)\,b_{*}.

(3.17)

By Proposition
3.5
and (
3.4
), the problem admits a solution if and only if

c_{1}=-(b_{*},\Psi_{1})_{L_{2}(\gamma)},\,\dots\,,c_{K}=-(b_{*},\Psi_{K})_{L_{2}(\gamma)}.

Since
v
is non-zero,
b_{*}
must have a non-trivial projection onto the subspace
H_{\lambda}
. But
b_{1}
,…,
b_{d}
have this property.
Putting
b_{*}=b_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
into (
3.16
), (
3.17
), we obtain the Jordan chains of length
2

(V^{(j)},0)\;\;\mapsto\;\;(v^{(j)}_{*},b_{j}y),\quad j=1,\ldots,d,

where
V^{(j)}=c_{1}^{(j)}V_{1}+\cdots+c_{K}^{(j)}V_{j}
with
c_{i}^{(j)}=-(b_{j},\Psi_{i})_{L_{2}(\gamma)}
.

There are no generalized eigenvectors of rank
3
, because all the eigenvectors of rank
2
have nonzero second components and the equation
(B-\lambda I)y_{*}=b_{j}y
is unsolvable for
b_{j}\neq 0
. Hence the space
X_{\lambda}
has the basis consisting of:

\circ

d
Jordan chains of length
2

(V^{(1)},0)\longmapsto(v^{(1)}_{*},b_{1}y),\,\ldots\,,(V^{(d)},0)\longmapsto(v^{(d)}_{*},b_{d}y),

(3.18)

\circ

the series of eigenvectors of the form

(v_{d+1},b_{d+1}y),\,(v_{d+2},b_{d+2}y),\,\dots\,,(v_{j},b_{j}y),\,\dots\,,

(3.19)

\circ

K-d
eigenvectors of the form

(V^{(d+1)},0),\,\dots\,,(V^{(K)},0).

(3.20)

Here
V^{(1)},\dots,V^{(K)}
are linearly independent eigenfunctions of
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda
.

We summarize the information about the spectrum and generalized eigenspaces of the limit operator
\mathcal{P}
that we have obtained.

.

Let

be the generalized eigenspace corresponding to

and

be the eigenspace.

(i) If

Remark 3.8
.

The operator

has always the eigenvalue

of infinite multiplicity, since

.
The zero eigenvalue is the smallest infinite-fold one, because

is non-negative. All negative eigenvalues, if they exist, have finite multiplicities.

4.
Asymptotics of eigenvalues in the case
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(B)\setminus\sigma(A)

We will first focus our attention on perturbations of infinite-fold eigenvalues. In this section, we construct the asymptotics of countable set of eigenvalues
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of (
1.2
) that converge to an infinite-fold eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\mathcal{P}
when
\lambda_{0}\not\in\sigma(A)
. We look for the asymptotics in the form

\lambda^{\varepsilon}\sim\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\varepsilon+\cdots,\quad u_{\varepsilon}(x)\sim\begin{cases}v_{0}(x)+\varepsilon v_{1}(x)+\cdots&\text{if }x\in\Omega\setminus\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
w_{0}(s,\textstyle\frac{r}{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon w_{1}(s,\textstyle\frac{r}{\varepsilon})+\cdots&\text{if }x\in\omega_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
(v_{0},w_{0})
is an non-zero element of
X_{\lambda_{0}}
.
To match the expansions on
\gamma_{\pm\varepsilon}
, we write
v_{k}
in the local coordinates

. Then (

) becomes

\displaystyle w_{0}(s,\pm 1)+\varepsilon w_{1}(s,\pm 1)-v_{0}(s,\pm\varepsilon)-\varepsilon v_{1}(s,\pm\varepsilon)+\cdots\sim 0,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{n}w_{0}(s,\pm 1)+\partial_{n}w_{1}(s,\pm 1)-\partial_{r}v_{0}(s,\pm\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\partial_{r}v_{1}(s,\pm\varepsilon)+\cdots\sim 0.

After expanding
v_{k}(s,\pm\varepsilon)
and their derivatives into the Taylor series about

for fixed

, we in particular derive
w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,\pm 1)=v_{1}^{\pm}\pm\partial_{r}v_{0}^{\pm}
and
\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,\pm 1)=\partial_{r}v_{0}^{\pm}
.
Of course,
w_{0}(\,\cdot\,,\pm 1)=v_{0}^{\pm}
and
\partial_{n}w_{0}(\,\cdot\,,\pm 1)=0
, since
(v_{0},w_{0})\in\mathop{\rm dom}\mathcal{P}
.

Substituting (
4.1
) into (
1.2
), and taking into account representation (
2.2
), we obtain that the pair
(v_{1},w_{1})
solves the problem

\displaystyle-\Delta v_{1}+av_{1}=\lambda_{0}\rho v_{1}+\lambda_{1}\rho v_{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\qquad\ell v_{1}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(4.2)

\displaystyle-\partial^{2}_{n}w_{1}=\lambda_{0}qw_{1}-\varkappa\partial_{n}w_{0}+\lambda_{1}qw_{0}\quad\text{in }\omega,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\phantom{-}\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,-1)=\partial_{r}v_{0}^{-},\quad\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,1)=\partial_{r}v_{0}^{+},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\phantom{-}v_{1}^{-}=w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,-1)+\partial_{r}v_{0}^{-},\quad v_{1}^{+}=w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,1)-\partial_{r}v_{0}^{+}.

(4.5)

The compatibility condition for (
4.3
), (
4.4
) reads

y(-1)\,\partial_{r}v_{0}^{-}-y(1)\,\partial_{r}v_{0}^{+}+\varkappa\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{n}w_{0}\,y\,dn=\lambda_{1}\int_{-1}^{1}qw_{0}y\,dn\quad\text{on }\gamma,

(4.6)

where
y
is an eigenfunction of (
3.1
) corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
.
We have applied the Fredholm alternative for the operator with compact resolvent associated with the Sturm-Liouville problem (
3.1
). The relation (
4.6
) can be treated as a spectral equation on
\lambda_{1}
for a pseudodifferential operator on
\gamma
.
We introduce the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps
N^{\pm}_{\lambda}
in
L_{2}(\gamma)
as follows. Let
z
be the solution of problem

-\Delta z+az=\lambda\rho z\;\;\text{in }\Omega_{-},\quad z=\varphi\quad\text{on }\gamma,\;\;\ell z=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega

(4.7)

for given
\varphi
. Set
N^{-}_{\lambda}\varphi=\partial_{r}z|_{\gamma}
. We follow
[
44
]
in assuming that

\mathop{\rm dom}N^{-}_{\lambda}=\big\{\varphi\in L_{2}(\gamma)\colon z\in W_{2}^{1}(\Omega_{-})\text{ and }\partial_{r}z|_{\gamma}\in L_{2}(\gamma)\big\}.

Likewise, we set
N^{+}_{\lambda}\varphi=-\partial_{r}z|_{\gamma}
, where
z
is a solution of the problem

-\Delta z+az=\lambda\rho z\;\;\text{in }\Omega_{+},\quad z=\varphi\quad\text{on }\gamma,

(4.8)

and
\mathop{\rm dom}N^{+}_{\lambda}=\big\{\varphi\in L_{2}(\gamma)\colon z\in W_{2}^{1}(\Omega_{+})\text{ and }\partial_{r}z|_{\gamma}\in L_{2}(\gamma)\big\}
.
The operators
N^{\pm}_{\lambda}
transform the Dirichlet data on

for solutions of the corresponding boundary value problems into the Neumann ones. Both operators are well-defined if
\lambda\not\in\sigma(A)
.
The minus sing in definition of

indicates that the direction of axis

coincides with the inward normal on

\gamma=\partial\Omega_{+}
.

It follows from (

) that
w_{0}(s,n)=b_{0}(s)y(n)
and
v_{0}
is a solution of (

) for

.
Then
\partial_{r}v_{0}^{-}=y(-1)\,N^{-}_{\lambda_{0}}b_{0}
and

. Consequently, condition (

y^{2}(-1)\,N^{-}_{\lambda_{0}}b_{0}+y^{2}(1)\,N^{+}_{\lambda_{0}}b_{0}+\varkappa b_{0}\int_{-1}^{1}yy^{\prime}\,dn=\lambda_{1}b_{0}\int_{-1}^{1}qy^{2}\,dn.

Suppose
\|y\|_{L_{2}(q,(-1,1))}=1

and write
\theta_{\lambda_{0}}^{\pm}=y^{2}(\pm 1)
.
Since

\int_{-1}^{1}yy^{\prime}\,dn=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(y^{2})^{\prime}\,dn=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\theta_{\lambda_{0}}^{+}-\theta_{\lambda_{0}}^{-}\right),

we can finally rewrite (

N_{\lambda_{0}}b_{0}=\lambda_{1}b_{0}
,
where

N_{\lambda}=\theta_{\lambda}^{-}N^{-}_{\lambda}+\theta_{\lambda}^{+}N^{+}_{\lambda}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\theta_{\lambda}^{+}-\theta_{\lambda}^{-})\varkappa.

(4.9)

Note that the values

Proposition 4.1
.

The operator

is self-adjoint, bounded below and has compact resolvent for all

.

Proof.

The operators
N^{-}_{\lambda}
and
N^{+}_{\lambda}
are self-adjoint in

, bounded below and have compact

[

, Th.3.1]
. The linear combination

has the same properties, since
\theta_{\lambda}^{-}
and
\theta_{\lambda}^{+}
are positive.
Finally,
N_{\lambda}
is a perturbation of this linear combination by the operator of multiplication by the bounded function
\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\theta_{\lambda}^{+}-\theta_{\lambda}^{-})\varkappa(s)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Denote by
\{\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

in asymptotics (

Let

.

Let us fix the corrector

\mathring{W}^{1}_{2}(\Omega)=\{v\in W^{1}_{2}(\Omega)\colon u_{\varepsilon}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}
. Due to the second representation theorem
[
43
, Theorem VI.2.23]
,
A_{\varepsilon}
is defined by the identity
\mathfrak{a}(A_{\varepsilon}u,\phi)=(\rho_{\varepsilon}u,\phi)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}
for all
\phi\in\mathring{W}^{1}_{2}(\Omega)
, because the Dirichlet form
\mathfrak{a}(u,\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\,dx
is a scalar product in
\mathring{W}^{1}_{2}(\Omega)
. Problem (
5.30
) can be written in the form
A_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{-1}u_{\varepsilon}
. For
m>1
the operators
A_{\varepsilon}
diverge as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and it has been shown that
\|A_{\varepsilon}\|\leq c\varepsilon^{1-m}
with a constant
c
being independent of
\varepsilon
.

The complete asymptotic analysis of eigenvalues
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of (
5.30
) has been carried out for
m=3
. We have proved that the spectrum consists of a countable number of infinite series of eigenvalues with the asymptotics

\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{kj}=\mu_{k}\varepsilon+\nu_{kj}\varepsilon^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2})\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,\quad k,j\in\mathbb{N},

(5.31)

where
\mu_{k}
is an infinite-fold eigenvalue of
B
and
\{\nu_{kj}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a spectrum of pseudodifferential operator
N_{\mu_{k}}
acting in
L_{2}(\gamma)
(see formula (
4.9
)). The operator
N_{\mu_{k}}
is a self-adjoint realization of a boundary value problem for the Laplace operator in
\Omega
with coupling conditions on
\gamma
.

Since the first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
of
B
is equal to zero, the low-lying eigenvalues admit the asymptotics
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1j}=\varepsilon^{2}(\nu_{1j}+o(1))
,
\varepsilon\to 0
. In this case,
\{\nu_{1j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are eigenvalues of the spectral problem
-\Delta u=\nu q_{0}\delta_{\gamma}u
in
\Omega
,
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
,
where
q_{0}=\int_{-1}^{1}q(r)\,dr
and
\delta_{\gamma}
is a distribution in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
acting as
\langle\delta_{\gamma},\phi\rangle=\int_{\gamma}\phi\,d\gamma
for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
The problem describes the eigenvibrations of a membrane with the mass density
\rho_{0}(x)=q_{0}\delta_{\gamma}(x)
, i.e., the whole mass of the membrane is supported on
\gamma
and the rest part of vibrating system is massless. Such series of infinitesimal eigenvalues with the asymptotics
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1j}=\varepsilon^{m-1}(\nu_{1j}+o(1))
,
\varepsilon\to 0
, exists for any
m>1
. Hence, the estimate
\|A_{\varepsilon}\|\leq c\varepsilon^{1-m}
is sharp. The similar result is obtained in Theorems
4.4
and
5.4
for problem (
1.2
) when
m=2
(see Corollary
5.5
).

In
[
37
]
some results on the convergence of eigenfunctions have been also obtained. Interestingly enough, the complete asymptotic description of
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
and
u_{\varepsilon}
in (
5.30
) includes not only the operator
B
but also the operator
A
which is associated with the problem
-\Delta u=\lambda u
in
\Omega\setminus\gamma
,
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\gamma
in this case. In view of (
5.31
), any point of the positive spectral half-line is an accumulation point for eigenvalues
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
, i.e., for each
\lambda>0
there exists a sequence
\{\lambda^{\varepsilon_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of eigenvalues such that
\lambda^{\varepsilon_{i}}\to\lambda
as
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
. We have proved that only the points of
\sigma(A)
can be approximated by eigenvalues
\lambda^{\varepsilon_{i}}
so that the corresponding eigenfunctions

converge to nontrivial limits in

. These limits are eigenfunctions of
A
corresponding to
\lambda
.

6.
Asymptotics of eigenvalues in the case
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(A)\setminus\sigma(B)

For the sake of completeness, we briefly discuss the perturbation of eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\mathcal{P}
which does not belong to
\sigma(B)
.
In view of part
(i)
of Theorem
3.7
,
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue of finite multiplicity. Also, we have
v_{0}=\alpha_{0}\cdot\mathbf{V}
and
w_{0}=0
in asymptotics (
4.1
), where
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) imply

-\partial^{2}_{n}w_{1}=\lambda_{0}qw_{1}\;\;\text{in }\omega,\quad\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,-1)=\alpha_{0}\cdot\partial_{r}\mathbf{V}^{-},\;\;\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,1)=\alpha_{0}\cdot\partial_{r}\mathbf{V}^{+}.

Since
\lambda_{0}\not\in\sigma(B)
, there exists a unique solution of the problem. Suppose the functions
W_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,K
, solve the problems

-\partial^{2}_{n}w=\lambda_{0}qw\;\;\text{in }\omega,\quad\partial_{n}w(\,\cdot\,,-1)=\partial_{r}V_{k}^{-},\;\;\partial_{n}w_{1}(\,\cdot\,,1)=\partial_{r}V_{k}^{+},

and
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{K})
. Thus we have
w_{1}=\alpha_{0}\cdot\mathbf{W}
. Next, we can rewrite (
4.2
) and (
4.5
) in the form

\displaystyle-\Delta v_{1}+av_{1}=\lambda_{0}\rho v_{1}+\lambda_{1}\rho\,\alpha_{0}\cdot\mathbf{V}\;\;\text{in }\Omega\setminus\gamma,\quad\ell v_{1}=0\;\;\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(6.1)

\displaystyle\quad v_{1}^{-}=\alpha_{0}\cdot(\mathbf{W}(\,\cdot\,,-1)+\partial_{r}\mathbf{V}^{-}),\quad v_{1}^{+}=\alpha_{0}\cdot(\mathbf{W}(\,\cdot\,,1)-\partial_{r}\mathbf{V}^{+}).

(6.2)

In general, the problem is unsolvable, because
\lambda_{0}
belongs to the spectrum of
A
which is the direct sum of

. To achieve the solvability one needs to choose

along with the parameter

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that

is an eigenvalue of the limit operator
\mathcal{P}
