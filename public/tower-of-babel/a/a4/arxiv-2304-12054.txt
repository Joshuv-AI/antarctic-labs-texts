Differential equations for Gaussian statistical models with rational maximum likelihood estimator
Carlos Améndola, Lukas Gustafsson, Kathlén Kohn, Orlando Marigliano, Anna Seigal

Abstract
. We study multivariate Gaussian statistical models whose maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is a rational function of the observed data. We establish a one-to-one correspondence between such models and the solutions to a nonlinear first-order partial differential equation (PDE). Using our correspondence, we reinterpret familiar classes of models with rational MLE, such as directed (and decomposable undirected) Gaussian graphical models. We also find new models with rational MLE. For linear concentration models with rational MLE, we show that homaloidal polynomials from birational geometry lead to solutions to the PDE. We thus shed light on the problem of classifying Gaussian models with rational MLE by relating it to the open problem in birational geometry of classifying homaloidal polynomials.



Keywords:

maximum likelihood degree, multivariate Gaussian, homaloidal polynomial

MSC codes:

62R01, 62F10, 62H22, 14E05, 35C11, 35F20

1.
Introduction

The
maximum likelihood degree
(ML degree) of a statistical model
is the number of complex critical points of the likelihood function given general data. It
was introduced in
[
CHKS06
]
for discrete statistical models and in
[
SU10
]
for Gaussian models.
For models with ML degree one, the likelihood function for general data has a unique critical point, which is the maximum likelihood estimate given that data. The
maximum likelihood estimator
(MLE) is the function that maps data to its maximum likelihood estimate. A model has ML degree one if and only if the MLE is a rational function of the data.

A classification of discrete statistical models of ML degree one was obtained in
[
Huh14(1)
,
DMS21(1)
]
.
The classification follows a two step procedure.
The first step shows that discrete models of ML degree one are the solutions to a system of partial differential equations (PDEs)
[
Huh14(1)
, Lemma 15]
.
The second step uses Horn uniformization to parametrize all solutions to the PDEs
[
Huh14(1)
, Lemma 16]
.

This article pursues the analogous classification for Gaussian models of ML degree one.
We show that varieties of Gaussian ML degree one are in bijection with solutions to a nonlinear first-order PDE.
This is analogous to the first step of
[
Huh14(1)
]
.
For the second step, we show that parametrizing the solutions to our PDE specializes to an open problem in classical algebraic geometry concerning
homaloidal polynomials
, which have been extensively studied
[
Dol00
,
DP03
,
Huh14(2)
,
SST21
]
.
We leave it as an open problem for future work to parametrize all solutions to our
homaloidal PDE
.

Other related work.

The concept of ML degree fits into the perspective of
likelihood geometry
, introduced by Huh and Sturmfels
[
huh2014likelihood
]
and part of the field of algebraic statistics
[
sullivant2018algebraic
]
.
In the discrete setting, Huh
[
Huh13
]
studied the ML degree of a very affine variety and showed that, for smooth varieties, the ML degree is equal to the topological Euler characteristic. ML degrees have been studied for toric varieties, which in statistics correspond to discrete exponential families
[
ABBGHHNRID19
]
. In the Gaussian setting, the focus has largely been on linear concentration models; i.e., models where the inverse covariance matrices lie on a linear space of symmetric matrices
[
AGKMS21
,
amendola2021likelihood
,
boege2021reciprocal
,
dye2021maximum
,
davies2021coloured
,
eur2021reciprocal
,
jiang2021linear
,
MMMSV20
,
MMW21
]
. Further work relates ML degrees to Euler characteristics in the Gaussian setting
[
DRGS22
]
. The ML degree was also studied for
exponential varieties

[
MSUZ16
]
.

We now introduce our main concepts. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a semi-algebraic subset of the cone of real positive definite symmetric
m\times m
matrices. We regard
\mathcal{M}
as a set of concentration (inverse covariance) matrices parametrizing a mean-centered Gaussian statistical model. We typically assume that
\mathcal{M}
is
scaling-invariant
; that is,
K\in\mathcal{M}
implies

\lambda K\in\mathcal{M}
for all positive scalars
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
That is, rescaling of measurements does not affect membership in the model. Given data
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Gaussian
log-likelihood function
is

(1)

\ell_{S}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R},\quad K\mapsto\frac{n}{2}(\log\det(K)-\mathrm{tr}(KS)-m\log(2\pi)).

where
S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}
is the sample
covariance
matrix.
In contrast,
the points in the model are parametrized by
concentration
matrices.
We choose this parametrization to make the trace term linear in both arguments:
the trace term is a canonical pairing of a vector space with its dual. We will find this perspective helpful later.

We pass from statistical models to algebraic varieties.
We denote the Zariski closure of the complexification of
\mathcal{M}
by
X
.
The log-likelihood function extends to a function
\ell_{S}
on
X
.
The ML degree of
X
is the number of complex critical points of this function for a general complex symmetric
m\times m
matrix
S\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We reinterpret the Gaussian log-likelihood function in a coordinate-free context, using projective varieties and homogeneous polynomials in Section
2
. We introduce the homaloidal PDE in Section
3
.
Our main result is a one-to-one correspondence between varieties of Gaussian ML degree one and solutions to the PDE (Theorem
3.5
). Equally important is a parallel result on another PDE which is uniquely satisfied by the MLEs of such varieties (Theorem
3.1
).
We study solutions to the homaloidal PDE coming from linear spaces in Section
4
and find that the linear spaces of ML degree one correspond to homaloidal polynomials.
We revisit known families of Gaussian graphical models with ML degree one in Section
5
, where we also formulate our main result in terms of usual Gaussian statistical models (Corollary
5.3
). We take first steps towards parametrizing the solutions to the PDE in Section
6
, and produce new ML degree one varieties in Section
7
. Code for our computational examples can be found at the MathRepo repository
https://mathrepo.mis.mpg.de/GaussianMLDeg1
.

2.
A coordinate-free Gaussian likelihood function

This section gives a coordinate-free definition of the Gaussian ML degree of an embedded variety, extending ideas from
[
MSUZ16
,
DRGS22
]
.
In this paper,
\mathcal{L}
denotes a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space.
Before focusing on the ML degree, we recall that the Jacobian of a rational map
\varphi:X\dashrightarrow Y
between algebraic varieties
X
and
Y
is a rational map

J\varphi:X\dashrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(TX,TY),\quad p\mapsto J_{p}\varphi:T_{p}X\to T_{p}Y.

If
X=\mathcal{L}
, we identify the tangent space
T_{p}\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L}
. In this case, the Jacobian of
\varphi:\mathcal{L}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the gradient

\nabla\varphi:\mathcal{L}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\ast},\quad p\mapsto\nabla_{p}\varphi.

We give a coordinate-free definition of the Gaussian ML degree of a variety
X
.
We assume throughout that
X
is irreducible, since the ML degree of a reducible variety is the sum of the ML degrees of its irreducible components. The coordinate-free definition replaces the space of symmetric matrices by an arbitrary ambient linear space
\mathcal{L}
.
This offers a simplification when
X
lies in a low-dimensional affine subspace of the space of symmetric matrices.
We show that the coordinate-free Gaussian ML degree is equivalent to the usual Gaussian ML degree in Proposition
2.3
.

In the definition below, we count the critical points of a function
\ell
on
X
. Note that
\ell
can only be differentiated along
X
at its smooth locus
X^{\mathrm{sm}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
finite-dimensional

\mathbb{C}
-vector space, let
X\subseteq\mathcal{L}
be an affine variety, and fix a polynomial
F
on
\mathcal{L}
. For
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
and
p\in\mathcal{L}\setminus V(F)
, the
(Gaussian) log-likelihood
is

\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(p)\coloneqq\log(F(p))-{\boldsymbol{u}}(p).

The
(Gaussian) ML degree
of
X
with respect to
F
, denoted
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)
, is the number of critical points of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
over the domain
{X^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)}
for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

To define the log-likelihood
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
as a function on
\mathcal{L}\setminus V(F)
we must choose a local branch of the logarithm around each
p\in\mathcal{L}\setminus V(F)
. The gradient
\nabla_{p}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
does not depend on this choice. The number
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)
is the cardinality of the set

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{p\in X^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)\mid T_{p}X\subseteq\ker(\nabla_{p}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}})\}

for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}
. This cardinality does not depend on (general)
{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Moreover,

\mathcal{C}
is reduced as a scheme, cf.
[
DRGS22
, Lemma 2.5]
.

We now prove the equivalence of the usual and coordinate-free
ML degrees.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\det
denote the determinant on
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.

(a)

Let
X
be a subvariety of
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.
Then
\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(X)
is the usual Gaussian ML degree of
X
.

(b)

Conversely, let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite-dimensional

\mathbb{C}
-vector space,
X\subseteq\mathcal{L}
a variety, and
F
a polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. Then there exists an affine-linear embedding
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
such that
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(\mathcal{A}(X))
.

Proof.

Set
F=\det
in
Definition
2.1
.
A generic linear form
{\boldsymbol{u}}
can be written as

{\boldsymbol{u}}(K)=\mathrm{tr}(KS)
, for
S\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
generic, since the trace induces an isomorphism of
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
with its dual. This proves (a).

For (b), let
k=\dim(\mathcal{L})
. There exist symmetric matrices
A_{0},\dotsc,A_{k}
, such that

(2)

\displaystyle F(x)=\det A(x),\quad\text{where}\quad A(x)\coloneqq A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}A_{i},

by
[
AW21
, Theorem 13]
.
Let
m
be the size of the
A_{i}
.
If the
A_{1},\dotsc,A_{k}
are linearly independent, then sending a basis of
\mathcal{L}
to
(A_{0}+A_{1},\dotsc,A_{0}+A_{k})
gives an affine-linear embedding
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
with
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)=\operatorname{MLD}_{\det}(\mathcal{A}(X))
. Otherwise, let
\{A_{r},\dotsc,A_{k}\}
be a maximal linearly independent subset of
\{A_{1},\dotsc,A_{k}\}
, possibly after reordering. If
r\leq m
, define

B\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}D&A(x)\\
A(x)&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{where }D\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r-1},0,\dotsc,0)\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m}).

Then

\det B=\det A(x)^{2}=F^{2}
.
Furthermore, we have
B(x)=B_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}B_{i}
with linearly independent
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k}
. Define the embedding
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2m})
by sending a basis of
\mathcal{L}
to
(B_{0}+B_{1},\dotsc,B_{0}+B_{k})
. Observe that

(3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)=\operatorname{MLD}_{\lambda F^{n}}(X)

for any nonzero scalar
\lambda
and positive integer
n
, since
\nabla_{p}\ell_{\lambda F^{n},{\boldsymbol{u}}}=n\nabla_{p}\log F-{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Hence
\operatorname{MLD}_{\det}(\mathcal{A}(X))=\operatorname{MLD}_{F^{2}}(X)=\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)
and we are done.

If
r>m
, we increase the size of
A(x)
by taking the direct sum with a suitably large identity matrix. We then apply the same argument to the resulting matrix
B
.
∎

We give a hands-on example of the construction in the previous proof when the matrices
A_{i}
are linearly dependent.

Example 2.4
.

Let
F(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x_{1}+x_{3})(x_{2}+x_{3})
. Then
F(x)=\det A(x)
, where

A(x)=x_{1}A_{1}+x_{2}A_{2}+x_{3}A_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}+x_{3}&0\\
0&x_{2}+x_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

However, the matrices
A_{i}
relate via
A_{1}=A_{3}-A_{2}
. Using the proof of Proposition
2.3
(b) with
r=m=2
, we write
F^{2}
as the determinant of the matrix

\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}B_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&x_{1}+x_{3}&0\\
0&0&0&x_{2}+x_{3}\\
x_{1}+x_{3}&0&0&0\\
0&x_{2}+x_{3}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Here, the
B_{i}
are linearly independent.

To apply Proposition
2.3
to a Gaussian statistical model
\mathcal{M}
in the cone of positive-definite real symmetric
m\times m
matrices, take the Zariski closure
X
of
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
and let
\mathcal{A}
be its affine span.
The restriction
F\coloneqq\det|_{\mathcal{A}}
of the determinant to
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(X),

which is the usual Gaussian ML degree of
\mathcal{M}
. The log-likelihood
\ell_{S}
for a covariance matrix
S
becomes the function
\ell_{\det,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
from Definition
2.1
with
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\mathrm{tr}(S-)
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be the set of diagonal
positive definite real symmetric
3\times 3

matrices and let
X\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}
be its complex Zariski closure.
The determinant restricts to the linear space
\mathcal{A}=X
to give the cubic
F(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
.
Then

\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(X)=\mathrm{MLD}_{x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}}(\mathbb{C}^{3})=1,

where the latter equality can be verified as follows.
For a generic linear form
{\boldsymbol{u}}=u_{1}x_{1}+u_{2}x_{2}+u_{3}x_{3}
,
the log-likelihood

\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(x)=\log(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})-{\boldsymbol{u}}(x)

has a unique critical point, namely the unique solution
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(\nicefrac{{1}}{{u_{1}}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{u_{2}}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{u_{3}}})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
to the equation

\nabla\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{x_{1}}}-u_{1}\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{x_{2}}}-u_{2}\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{x_{3}}}-u_{3}\end{pmatrix}=0.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite-dimensional
real
vector space,
X\subseteq\mathcal{L}
a variety, and
F
a polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. Then it is still true that there exists an affine-linear embedding
A:\mathcal{L}\to\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
F
becomes the restriction of the determinant. However, it is not always possible to find an embedding
A
that intersects the positive definite cone. Thus
A
need not yield a statistical model. When such an
A
exists, the polynomial
F
is said to possess a
definite symmetric determinantal representation
; i.e., it can be written as in (
2
) with a
positive definite
matrix
A_{0}
.
The problem of computing a definite symmetric determinantal representation of a real polynomial is well-studied in convex algebraic geometry, due to its connections to semidefinite programming and the generalized Lax conjecture
[
blekherman2012semidefinite
,
CD20
,
D20
]
.

From now on, we consider
projective
varieties
X
, reflecting the assumption that our statistical models are scaling-invariant. In this case, the affine span of the affine cone
C_{X}
over
X
is a linear space and restricting the determinant to that linear subspace yields a homogeneous polynomial. We thus assume that
F
is homogeneous and set

\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)\coloneqq\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(C_{X}).

We now make a connection between the coordinate-free ML degree of a variety
X
and the Euclidean distance degree
\mathrm{EDD}(X)
, which counts the critical points of the Euclidean distance function to
X
from a general point
[
euclidean-distance
]
.
This builds on the perspective in
[
MRW21
, Corollary 3.3]
and
[
DRGS22
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
Q=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
be the Fermat quadric on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a projective variety.
Then the Euclidean distance degree of
X
and the ML degree of
X
with respect to
Q
coincide; i.e.,

\mathrm{EDD}(X)=\mathrm{MLD}_{Q}(X).

Proof.

We identify
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
and
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\ast}
via
Q
and thus regard
x
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}
as points of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.
We find a bijection between the critical points of
\ell_{Q,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and the critical points of the Euclidean distance function
d_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}(x)\coloneqq Q(x-{\boldsymbol{u}})
.
That is, between the sets

\mathcal{C}(d^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{u}}})\coloneqq\{x\mid x-{\boldsymbol{u}}\perp T_{x}C_{X}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{u}})\coloneqq\{x\mid\frac{2x}{Q(x)}-{\boldsymbol{u}}\perp T_{x}C_{X}\}.

Taking scalar products with
x\in T_{x}C_{X}
we deduce that
Q(x)-\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},x\rangle=0
for
x\in C(d^{2}_{\boldsymbol{u}})
and
2-\langle{\boldsymbol{u}},x\rangle=0
for
x\in C(\ell_{\boldsymbol{u}})
.
The required bijection is then given in both directions by the rational involution
\mu:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
defined by
\mu(x)=\nabla\log Q=2x/Q(x)
.
∎

The goal of this paper is to characterize irreducible projective varieties with ML degree one.
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
be a projective variety and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
.
We denote by
C_{X}
the affine cone over
X
.
If
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
, there is a map

(4)

\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow C_{X}^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)

that takes a general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
to the unique critical point of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
along
C_{X}
.
If
X
is the Zariski closure of a statistical model in
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
and
F=\det
, the map
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
is the
maximum likelihood estimator
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The map
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
is rational and dominant.
Furthermore, if
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W})
for some subspace
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and
\pi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{W}^{\ast}
is the restriction to
\mathcal{W}
, then

{\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}}={\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{W},F|_{\mathcal{W}}}}\circ\pi.

Proof.

Consider the incidence variety
\mathfrak{X}\subseteq(C_{X}^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F))\times\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
of all pairs
(p,{\boldsymbol{u}})
such that
p
is a critical point of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
. Let
\pi_{i}
be the projections from
\mathfrak{X}
onto its
i
th factor.
By assumption,
\pi_{2}
is birational. For a general
p\in C^{\mathrm{sm}}_{X}\setminus V(F)
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}\coloneqq\nabla_{p}\log F
, we have
\nabla_{p}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\nabla_{p}\log F-{\boldsymbol{u}}=0
.
Thus,
p
is a critical point of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
, which shows that
\pi_{1}
is dominant.
Hence, the map

\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}=\pi_{1}\circ\pi_{2}^{-1}
is rational and dominant.
The second statement holds since
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\ell_{F|_{\mathcal{W}},{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{\mathcal{W}}}
on
C_{X}^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

As seen in the proof of Proposition
2.8
, the map
p\mapsto\nabla_{p}\log F
sends points in
C_{X}^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)
to linear forms
{\boldsymbol{u}}
such that
(p,{\boldsymbol{u}})
is a critical pair. This is analogous to the fact that a statistical model can be viewed as a set of empirical probability distributions such that every model point is its own maximum likelihood estimate.

A projective variety of ML degree one has rational maximum likelihood estimator,
by Proposition
2.8
.
We show the converse.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
be a projective variety and

\Psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow C_{X}^{\mathrm{sm}}\setminus V(F)

a dominant rational map such that, for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
, the point
\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
is a critical point of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
along
C_{X}
. Then
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and
\Psi=\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{X}
be the incidence variety from the proof of Proposition
2.8
. Suppose
(p,{\boldsymbol{u}})
and
(p,{\boldsymbol{v}})
lie in
\mathfrak{X}
for some
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
. Then
the linear forms
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
are the same when restricted to
T_{p}C_{X}
.
Thus
\pi_{1}:\mathfrak{X}\to C^{\mathrm{sm}}_{X}\setminus V(F)
makes
\mathfrak{X}
into a vector bundle whose fiber at a base point
p
is isomorphic to the co-normal space
N^{\ast}_{p}C_{X}
. Hence
\dim(\mathfrak{X})=\dim(\mathcal{L}^{\ast})
. The variety
X
is irreducible since
\Psi
is dominant, hence so is
\mathfrak{X}
. Define
\sigma:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow\mathfrak{X}
by
\sigma({\boldsymbol{u}})=(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{u}})
. Then
\sigma
is a right-inverse of
\pi_{2}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, thus an injective rational map between irreducible varieties of the same dimension, thus birational with inverse
\pi_{2}
. The map
\pi_{2}
has degree one, thus
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and
\Psi=\pi_{1}\circ\pi_{2}^{-1}=\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
.
∎

3.
The homaloidal PDE

In this section, we state and prove our main results. We characterize the map
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
as the solution to a PDE in Theorem
3.1
. We then introduce the
homaloidal PDE
and show that its solutions are precisely projective varieties of Gaussian ML degree one in Theorem
3.5
.
Our justification for the adjective
homaloidal
is explained after Corollary
4.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
be an irreducible projective variety and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. Then
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
if and only if there exists a dominant rational map
\Psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow C_{X}

that satisfies

(a)

\Psi(t{\boldsymbol{u}})=t^{-1}\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
for all
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

(b)

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\log(F\circ\Psi)=-\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})

for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.

In this case,

\Psi
is the function
\mathrm{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
.

Before the proof, recall that Euler’s homogeneous function theorem says that a homogeneous function
f
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of degree
d
satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\cdot x_{i}=d\cdot f(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Translated to a coordinate-free formulation for a vector space and its dual, the result says that if

\varphi:\mathcal{L}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a homogeneous rational function of degree
d
with
p\in\mathcal{L}
then

(\nabla_{p}\varphi)(p)=d\cdot\varphi(p).

Similarly, given
\psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, the result says that
{\boldsymbol{u}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\psi)=d\cdot\psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
d\coloneqq\deg(F)
. We first show that if
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
then
the map

\Psi\coloneqq\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}

satisfies (a) and (b).
This map is rational and dominant,
by Proposition
2.8
.
For (a), let
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
p\coloneqq\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
.
Then

\nabla_{t^{-1}p}\ell_{F,t{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\frac{\nabla_{t^{-1}p}\,F}{F(t^{-1}p)}-t{\boldsymbol{u}}=\frac{(t^{-1})^{d-1}\,\nabla_{p}F}{(t^{-1})^{d}F(p)}-t{\boldsymbol{u}}=t\,\nabla_{p}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}.

Since
C_{X}
is a cone,
T_{p}C_{X}
and
T_{t^{-1}p}C_{X}
are equal as linear subspaces of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus

T_{t^{-1}p}C_{X}=T_{p}C_{X}\subseteq\ker(\nabla_{p}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}})=\ker(\nabla_{t^{-1}p}\ell_{F,t{\boldsymbol{u}}}).

Since
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
, we have
t^{-1}p=\Psi(t{\boldsymbol{u}})
.
Thus,
\Psi
is homogeneous of degree
-1
.
For (b), we have

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\log(F\circ\Psi)=(\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}\log F)\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi={\boldsymbol{u}}\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi

because
\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
is a critical point of
\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\operatorname{Im}(J_{u}\Psi)\subseteq T_{\Psi(u)}C_{X}
.
Furthermore, since
C_{X}
is a cone we have
\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})\in T_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}C_{X}
, which yields

0=(\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}\ell_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}})(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))=\frac{(\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}F)(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))}{F(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))}-{\boldsymbol{u}}(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))=d-{\boldsymbol{u}}(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})),

where the last equality follows from Euler’s homogeneous function theorem.
Now let
\beta:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\times\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{C}
be the canonical pairing. Then

\nabla_{({\boldsymbol{u}},p)}\beta(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}},\dot{p})=\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(p)+{\boldsymbol{u}}(\dot{p})
for all
{\boldsymbol{u}},\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
and all
p,\dot{p}\in\mathcal{L}
and so
\nabla_{({\boldsymbol{u}},p)}\beta=\beta(-,p)+\beta({\boldsymbol{u}},-)
. Consider the function
\varphi\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{u}}(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))=\beta\circ(\operatorname{id},\Psi):\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\to\mathbb{C}
. The above computation shows that
\varphi
is a constant function equal to
d
. Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\varphi=(\nabla_{({\boldsymbol{u}},\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))}\beta)\circ(\operatorname{id},J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi)

\displaystyle=(\beta(-,\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))+\beta({\boldsymbol{u}},-))\circ(\operatorname{id},J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi)=\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})+{\boldsymbol{u}}\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi.

Hence
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\log F\circ\Psi)={\boldsymbol{u}}\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi=-\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
.

For the converse statement,
let
\Psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow C_{X}
be a dominant rational map satisfying (a) and (b), and take a general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.
The map
F\circ\Psi
is homogeneous of degree
-d
.
By Euler’s homogeneous function theorem,
{\boldsymbol{u}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(F\circ\Psi))=-d\cdot F(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))
. By (b),

{\boldsymbol{u}}(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))={\boldsymbol{u}}(-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\log F\circ\Psi))=-\frac{{\boldsymbol{u}}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}(F\circ\Psi))}{F(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))}=d.

Thus
0=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\varphi=\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})+{\boldsymbol{u}}\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi
as before, so
(\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}\log F-{\boldsymbol{u}})\circ J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi=0
by (b) again. Since
\Psi
is dominant and
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is general,
J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi
maps surjectively onto
T_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}C_{X}
. Hence,
T_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}C_{X}\subseteq\ker\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}\ell_{{\boldsymbol{u}},F}
, so
\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})
is a critical point of
\ell_{{\boldsymbol{u}},F}
. In other words,
\Psi
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
2.10
. Thus,
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and
\Psi=\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
.
∎

The next proposition shows that
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
can be recovered from its projectivization.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
be a projective variety and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
. Fix
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
and let
p\in C_{X}
be any representative of the image of
[{\boldsymbol{u}}]
under the map

\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}):\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}^{\ast})\dashrightarrow X.

Then

\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{\deg(F)}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p)}\,p.

Proof.

Let
q\coloneqq\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}({\boldsymbol{u}})
. Then
{\boldsymbol{u}}(q)=\deg F
, as shown in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Since
q=\lambda p
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
q/{\boldsymbol{u}}(q)=p/{\boldsymbol{u}}(p)
, thus

q=\frac{\deg(F)}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(q)}q=\frac{\deg(F)}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p)}p.\qed

The next definition will be used throughout the rest of the paper.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
finite-dimensional

\mathbb{C}
-vector space and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. The
homaloidal PDE
is the nonlinear first-order PDE:

(5)

\Phi=F\circ(-\nabla\log\Phi),\quad\Phi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\textup{ rational and homogeneous.}

Remark 3.4
.

(a)

Every solution
\Phi
to (
5
) is homogeneous of degree
-\deg(F)
, since
\nabla\log\Phi
is homogeneous of degree
-1
.

(b)

If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\Phi
is a solution for
(\mathcal{L},F)
, then
\lambda\Phi
is a solution for
(\mathcal{L},\lambda F)
.

We now establish the promised one-to-one correspondence between solutions to the homaloidal PDE and projective varieties of ML degree one. We consider two rational functions to be equal if they agree on a dense open set.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. There is a bijection between the projective varieties
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
with
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and the solutions
\Phi
to the homaloidal PDE.
The bijection sends a variety
X
to the function

\Phi\coloneqq F\circ\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F},

and a function
\Phi
to the variety

X\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\overline{\operatorname{Im}(-\nabla\log\Phi)}).

A variety
X
with
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and its corresponding solution
\Phi
are related by

(6)

\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}=-\nabla\log\Phi.

Proof.

If
\Phi
is a solution of the homaloidal PDE (
5
) then
\Psi\coloneqq-\nabla\log\Phi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow C_{X}
is rational, homogeneous of degree
-1
, and satisfies

\nabla\log(F\circ\Psi)=\nabla\log(F\circ(-\nabla\log\Phi))=\nabla\log\Phi=-\Psi.

Let
X\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\overline{\operatorname{Im}(\Psi)})
. We have
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}=\Psi
,
by Theorem
3.1
. In particular, (
6
) holds.

Conversely, let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
satisfy
\mathrm{MLD}_{F}(X)=1
and
\Psi\coloneqq\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
. By Theorem
3.1
(a),
\Psi
is homogenous of degree
-1
. Define
\Phi
to be the restriction of
F\circ\Psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}
to the Zariski-dense open set

\{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\mid\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}F\text{ and }J_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Psi\text{ have full rank}\}.

Then
\Phi
is a smooth rational homogeneous function. By Theorem
3.1
(b),

-\nabla\log\Phi=-\nabla\log(F\circ\Psi)=\Psi,

thus (
6
) holds.
Applying
F
to both sides, we get
F(-\nabla\log\Phi)=F\circ\Psi=\Phi
, hence
\Phi
is a solution of (
5
).

To show that the above constructions are mutually inverse, start with (
6
). Apply
F
to both sides for one direction and
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\operatorname{Im}(-)})
for the other direction.
∎

One upshot of Theorem
3.5
is that the maximum likelihood estimator
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
can be expressed purely in terms of the scalar-valued function
\Phi_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
, the solution to the homaloidal PDE associated to
(X,\mathcal{L},F)
.
Since the map
\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
behaves well when restricting to linear subspaces, by Proposition
2.8
, so does the function
\Phi_{X,\mathcal{L},F}=F\circ\operatorname{MLE}_{X,\mathcal{L},F}
. More precisely,
if
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W})
for some subspace
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and
\pi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{W}^{\ast}
is the restriction to
\mathcal{W}
then

{\Phi_{X,\mathcal{L},F}}={\Phi_{X,\mathcal{W},F|_{\mathcal{W}}}}\circ\pi.

4.
Linear spaces and homaloidal polynomials

This section studies the case
X=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
. We seek the homogeneous polynomials
F
on
\mathcal{L}
such that

\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1.

This offers a change of perspective from the usual study of the ML degree: rather than, for fixed
F
, finding the linear spaces
\mathcal{L}
of ML degree one, we instead fix
\mathcal{L}
and find the polynomials
F
with respect to which
\mathcal{L}
has ML degree one. As it turns out, this class of polynomials is already present in the literature.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
finite-dimensional

\mathbb{C}
-vector space. A homogeneous polynomial
F
on
\mathcal{L}
is
homaloidal
if the rational map
\nabla\log F:\mathcal{L}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
is birational.

The above definition follows Dolgachev
[
Dol00
, Section 2]
. For more recent results on homaloidal polynomials we refer the reader to
[
Huh14(2)
]
and
[
SST21
]
. Our interest in these polynomials comes from the following fact.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F
be a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. Then:

(a)

\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=\deg(\nabla\log F)

(b)

\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
F
is homaloidal. In this case,
\operatorname{MLE}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}),\mathcal{L},F}
is the rational inverse of
\nabla\log F
.

Proof.

The degree of
\nabla\log F
is the number of solutions
p\in\mathcal{L}
to

\frac{\nabla_{p}F}{F(p)}-{\boldsymbol{u}}=0

for generic
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, which is exactly
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})).
Thus,
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
\nabla\log F
is birational. In this case, its inverse
\Psi
satisfies

\frac{\nabla_{\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})}F}{F(\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}}))}-{\boldsymbol{u}}=0,

for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, thus it equals
\operatorname{MLE}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}),\mathcal{L},F}
.
∎

Part (a) of Proposition
4.2

is used as a definition of the ML degree in
[
DMS21(2)
, Definition 2.3.1]
. Part (b) allows us to establish a close connection between homaloidal polynomials and solutions to the homaloidal PDE.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
F
be homaloidal and
\Psi=(\nabla\log F)^{-1}
. Then
F\circ\Psi
satisfies the homaloidal PDE.
Conversely, if
\Phi
is a solution to the homaloidal PDE and if

X\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\overline{\operatorname{Im}(-\nabla\log\Phi)})
is equal to
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
,
then
F
is homaloidal.

Proof.

Both statements follow from Proposition
4.2
and Theorem
3.5
. For the converse statement, the birational inverse of
\nabla\log F
is given by
-\nabla\log\Phi
.
∎

Our motivation for calling (
5
) the homaloidal PDE comes from the fact that if
\Phi
is a solution to (
5
) for an arbitrary subvariety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
, then
\Psi\coloneqq-\nabla\log\Phi
satisfies
\Psi\circ(\nabla\log F)=\operatorname{id}
on
C_{X}
, giving a birational inverse of

\nabla\log F
on
C_{X}
. This extends Definition
4.1
to instances beyond the linear space
\mathcal{L}
.

In light of Proposition
4.2
(b), we can restate two results from the literature on homaloidal polynomials as results on linear models of ML degree one, as follows.

Theorem 4.4
.

[
Dol00
, Theorem 4]
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}
and let
F
be a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
without repeated factors. Then
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if the projective variety
V(F)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
is one of the following plane curves:

(a)

A smooth conic.

(b)

The union of three nonconcurrent lines.

(c)

The union of a smooth conic and a tangent line. ∎

Theorem 4.5
.

[
Huh14(2)
, Theorem 4]
.
Let
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{C}^{m}
where
m\geq 4
and
F
a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
. Suppose that all singularities of the projective variety
V(F)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})
are isolated. Then
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
V(F)
is a smooth quadric. ∎

Remark 4.6
.

When
F
is the Fermat quadric, Theorems
4.5
and
4.4
together with Proposition
2.7
yield a classification of linear spaces of Euclidean distance degree one. For instance, a linear subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, where
m\geq 4
, has ED degree one if and only if it intersects the Fermat quadric hypersurface transversally.

We solve the homaloidal PDE when
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, without the assumptions on
F
in the previous two theorems.
We use the following statement.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis of a

\mathbb{C}
-vector space
\mathcal{L}
and
(\ell_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
the associated dual basis of
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
. Let
F\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}^{a_{i}}
, with
a_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
. Then
F
is homaloidal if and only if all
a_{i}\geq 1
. In this case, the inverse
\Psi:\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{L}
of
\nabla\log F
is

\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p_{i})}\,p_{i}.

Proof.

For
p\in\mathcal{L}
we have

\nabla_{p}\log F=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{\ell_{i}(p)}\,\ell_{i},

thus
\operatorname{Im}(\nabla\log F)\subseteq\operatorname{Span}(\ell_{i})_{a_{i}\neq 0}
. So, if
\nabla\log F
is birational then all
a_{i}\geq 1
. Conversely, if all
a_{i}\geq 1
then the map
\Psi
of the statement is a birational inverse of
\nabla\log F
.
Indeed, by substituting
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\nabla_{p}\log F
in the expression for
\Psi
we obtain

(\Psi\circ\nabla\log F)(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}\,\ell_{i}(p)}{a_{i}}p_{i}=p.\qed

Remark 4.8
.

Bruno
[
B07
, Theorem B]
showed the more general result that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}^{a_{i}}

as in Lemma
4.7
,
with
a_{i}\geq 1
, is homaloidal if and only if the
\ell_{i}
form a basis of
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
F
be a homogeneous polynomial on
\mathcal{L}
.

(a)

If
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{C}
then
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
F
is not constant. In this case,

\operatorname{MLE}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}),\mathcal{L},F}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{\deg(F)}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p)}\,p\quad\text{ for general }{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast},

where
p
is any generator of
\mathcal{L}
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
then
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
F=\lambda\ell_{1}^{a_{1}}\ell_{2}^{a_{2}}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 1
, and linearly independent
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
. In this case,

\operatorname{MLE}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}),\mathcal{L},F}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{a_{1}}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p_{1})}\,p_{1}+\frac{a_{2}}{{\boldsymbol{u}}(p_{2})}\,p_{2}\quad\text{ for general }{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast},

where
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
form a basis dual to
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
.

Proof.

Part (a) is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.7
. For (b),
decompose
F
as a product of powers of
k
distinct linear forms and use Remark
4.8
to conclude that
F=\lambda\ell_{1}^{a_{1}}\ell_{2}^{a_{2}}
with
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
linearly independent. The rest follows from Lemma
4.7
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

We comment on a possible way to extend Proposition
4.9
beyond the bivariate case.
Let
D_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}\coloneqq V(F)\cup V({\boldsymbol{u}})
and let
\chi_{\mathrm{top}}
denote the topological Euler characteristic. By
[
DP03
, Theorem 1]
,

(7)

\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=(-1)\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})\setminus D_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}})\quad\text{ for general }{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}.

When
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
, the Euler characteristic on the right hand side of (
7
) can be computed as follows.
Consider the factorization of
F
as a product of powers of
k
distinct linear forms.
Then
k=\#V(F)
and, for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, the space
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L})\setminus D_{F,{\boldsymbol{u}}}
is obtained by removing
k+1
distinct points from a
2
-sphere. It follows that
\operatorname{MLD}_{F}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}))=1
if and only if
k=2
, as seen in Proposition
4.9
.
For higher-dimensional
\mathcal{L}
, the Euler characteristic becomes more difficult to compute.

Remark 4.11
.

Whether
F
is homaloidal depends only on its radical, thus we may discard repeated factors of
F
. Indeed, the right-hand side of (
7
) only depends on the topology of
V(F)
, so we may replace
F
with
\operatorname{rad}F
.
In particular, the restriction in Theorem
4.4
that
F
has no repeated factors is not necessary.

Returning to the case
F=\det
and
\mathcal{L}
embedded into
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, the upshot of the results in this section is the following.

Corollary 4.12
.

A linear space
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
of dimension
k\leq 3
has ML degree one if and only if the variety
\mathbb{P}(V(\det)\cap\mathcal{L})
is

(
k
=1)

Empty.

(
k
=2)

Two distinct points.

(
k
=3)

A smooth conic, the union of three nonconcurrent lines, or
the union of a smooth conic and a tangent line.

Proof.

For
k=1
and
k=2
, the statement follows from Proposition
4.9
after restricting the determinant to
\mathcal{L}
. The case
k=3
is derived similarily using Theorem
4.4
.
∎

Proposition
4.2
relates Gaussian models of the simplest type, linear models, to homaloidal polynomials. Such a model has ML degree one if and only if the determinant restricted to it is a homaloidal polynomial. Varying the dimension of the ambient space
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, this produces every homogeneous polynomial, by Proposition
2.3
. Hence classifying all linear models of ML degree one independently of the ambient space is equivalent to classifying all homaloidal polynomials. The latter is a long-standing open problem in birational geometry. For a discussion on the difficulty of this problem, we refer to
[
homaloidal-hypersurfaces
]
. Even if we fix the ambient space
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, homaloidal polynomials arising as the determinant of a linear space are ill-understood
[
homaloidal-determinants
]
.

In Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
, we used the literature on homaloidal polynomials to find Gaussian models of ML degree one.
One could continue in this fashion, turning results on homaloidal polynomials, such as those in
[
homaloidal-hypersurfaces
,
homaloidal-determinants
]
, into results on varieties of ML degree one. This would strengthen the bridge between birational geometry and algebraic statistics.

5.
Graphical models

In this section, we illustrate our results by applying them to Gaussian graphical models, both directed and undirected.
We use the homaloidal PDE (
5
) to find maximum likelihood estimators, which agree with formulae from
[
Lau96
]
. We construct a maximum likelihood estimator from products of determinants of positive definite symmetric matrices
and compute its image, the corresponding model. Two determinant equations are relevant here: a product of determinants for a chordal undirected graph, from
[
Lau96
, Lemma 5.5]

and its directed analogue, which we derive in Lemma
5.14
.
We show that these two determinant equations lead to solutions to the homaloidal PDE. We first explain how to formulate Theorems
3.1
and
3.5
for
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, without passing to its dual vector space, to simplify our later computations.

5.1.
Coordinate formulation of main results

The bilinear trace pairing
(A,B)\mapsto\mathrm{tr}(AB)
on
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
restricts to an inner product on
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Using that pairing, we identify
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
with its dual vector space via
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})\ni A\mapsto\mathrm{tr}(A\bullet)
.
Under this identification, the gradient
\nabla_{S}\Phi
of a scalar-valued function
\Phi
on
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
is a symmetric matrix with entries

(8)

(\nabla_{S}\Phi)_{ij}=\frac{1}{2-\delta_{ij}}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s_{ij}},

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta and the
s_{ij}
are the coordinates on
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
. Jacobi’s formula gives
\nabla_{S}\log\det=S^{-1}
.

Example 5.1
.

When
m=2
we have

\det(S)=\det\begin{bmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}\\
s_{12}&s_{22}\end{bmatrix}=s_{11}s_{22}-s_{12}^{2},\qquad\nabla_{S}\log\det=\frac{1}{\det(S)}\begin{bmatrix}s_{22}&-s_{12}\\
-s_{12}&s_{11}\end{bmatrix}.

The off-diagonal entries in the second equation are
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\log\det}{\partial s_{12}}=-s_{12}
.

Maximum likelihood estimation for graphical models fits the setting described in Definition
2.1
by specializing to
F=\det
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\mathrm{tr}(S\bullet)
.
We reformulate Theorems
3.1
and
3.5
for that special case.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m}))
be a irreducible projective variety. We have
\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(X)=1
if and only if there exists a dominant rational map
\Psi:\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})\dashrightarrow C_{X}

that satisfies

(a)

\Psi(tS)=t^{-1}\Psi(S)
for all
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

(b)

\nabla_{S}(\log\det\Psi)=-\Psi(S)

for general
S\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.

The map

\Psi
is the maximum likelihood estimator.

Corollary 5.3
.

There is a bijection between the
projective varieties
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m}))
with
\mathrm{MLD}_{\det}(X)=1
and the
solutions
\Phi
to

(9)

\Phi=\det(-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi),\quad\Phi:\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\textup{ rational and homogeneous.}

The bijection sends a function
\Phi
to the variety

\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathrm{Im}(-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi)})
.

The functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
from Corollaries
5.2
and
5.3
relate via
\Phi=\det\Psi
.

Example 5.4
.

Let the model be the full positive definite cone.
Its Zariski closure is, as a projective variety,
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m}))
.
Recall from (
1
) that the log-likelihood
\ell_{S}(K)
is, up to additive and multiplicative constants, equal to

\log\det(K)-\mathrm{tr}(KS)
.
Then

\nabla_{K}\log\det K=K^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{K}\mathrm{tr}(KS)=S,

where the first equation comes from
the Jacobi formula, see Example
5.1
.
Hence the relation

\nabla\ell_{S}(K)=0
is equivalent to
K^{-1}-S=0
, leading to the solution
\hat{K}\coloneqq S^{-1}
.
Thus,
if the sample covariance matrix
S
has full rank, then
S^{-1}
is the global maximum and unique critical point of the log-likelihood.
Hence the maximum likelihood estimator is
\Psi(S)=S^{-1}
and,
moreover, we have
\Phi(S)={\det(S)}^{-1}
.

We check that
\Psi
satisfies the conditions of Corollary
5.2
, as follows.
The map
\Psi(S)=S^{-1}
is a dominant rational map and
\Psi(tS)=(tS)^{-1}=t^{-1}S^{-1}=t^{-1}\Psi(S)
. In addition,

(10)

\nabla_{S}(\log\det\Psi)=\nabla_{S}\log\det(S^{-1})=-\nabla_{S}\log\det S=-S^{-1}=-\Psi(S).

Next we show that
\Phi=\det\Psi
satisfies the conditions of Corollary
5.3
, as follows.
Taking determinants on both sides of (
10
) and setting
\Phi=\det\Psi
gives

\det(-\nabla_{S}(\log\Phi))=\Phi
.

5.2.
Undirected graphical models

Definition 5.5
.

Fix an undirected
graph
G=(V,E)
. The undirected Gaussian graphical model

\mathcal{M}(G)

consists of (positive definite) concentration matrices
K
with
K_{ij}=0
if
(i,j)\notin E
.
Its Zariski closure is the linear space

\mathcal{L}_{G}\subseteq\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{V})
.

Example 5.6
.

Let
G
be the complete graph on
m
nodes. The associated graphical model is the full cone of symmetric positive definite matrices. Its Zariski closure is
\mathcal{L}_{G}=\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
. Hence this is Example
5.4
.

A fundamental class of undirected graphical models is given by
chordal
graphs, also known as
triangulated
or
decomposable
graphs.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph. A
weak decomposition
of
G
is a triple
A,B,C\subseteq V
such that

(1)

A,B
and
C
are pairwise disjoint with union
V
,

(2)

C
separates
A
from
B
,

(3)

C
induces a complete graph.

We denote such a decomposition by
G=A\amalg_{C}B
, after identifying a vertex set with its induced subgraph. The notion of a
chordal
graph can be defined recursively by saying that complete graphs are chordal and that a graph
G
is chordal if it has a weak decomposition
G=A\amalg_{C}B
where
A
and
B
are chordal.

An alternative definition of a graph being chordal is that it has no
n
-cycle for
n\geq 4
as an induced subgraph. See
[
Lau96
, Sec 2.1]
for the equivalence of these definitions.

Chordal graphs have ML degree one
[
SU10
]
. We give an alternative proof of this result using the homaloidal PDE.
Given a matrix
S\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{V})
we denote by
S_{A}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{A})
its submatrix indexed by
A\subseteq V
.
Dually, given a matrix
M\in\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{C}^{A})
, we pad it with zeros at positions in
V\backslash A
to obtain a
(|V|\times|V|)
-matrix denoted by
[M]^{V}
.
Define
[S^{-1}_{A}]^{V}\coloneqq[(S_{A})^{-1}]^{V}
; i.e., it is the matrix
S_{A}
, inverted, then padded with zeros.

The following lemma finds the determinant formula we use for finding solutions to the homaloidal PDE for chordal graphical models.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
A\cup B\cup C
be a partition of
\{1,\dotsc,m\}
. Let
S
be an
m\times m
general positive definite symmetric matrix. Let
\hat{K}\coloneqq[S^{-1}_{A\cup C}]^{V}+[S^{-1}_{B\cup C}]^{V}-[S^{-1}_{C}]^{V}
. Then

\det\hat{K}=\frac{\det(S_{C})}{\det(S_{A\cup C})\det(S_{B\cup C})}.

Proof.

Let
f
be the probability density associated to
\hat{K}^{-1}
and
g
the density associated to
S
. Denote by
g_{A\cup C}
the marginal density associated to
A\cup C
, and likewise define
g_{B\cup C}
and
g_{C}
. We compute

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-m/2}(\det\hat{K})^{1/2}\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}S^{-1}_{A\cup C}y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}S^{-1}_{B\cup C}y)}{\exp(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}S^{-1}_{C}y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\det\hat{K})^{1/2}(\det S_{C}^{-1})^{1/2}}{(\det S^{-1}_{A\cup C})^{1/2}(\det S^{-1}_{B\cup C})^{1/2}}\,\frac{g_{A\cup C}(y)g_{B\cup C}(y)}{g_{C}(y)}.

It remains to show that
g_{A\cup C}g_{B\cup C}/g_{C}
is a probability distribution. We integrate over
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, to obtain

\int_{C}\int_{B}\int_{A}\frac{g_{A\cup C}(y)g_{B\cup C}(y)}{g_{C}(y)}=\int_{C}\frac{1}{g_{C}(y)}(\int_{A}g_{A\cup C}(y))(\int_{B}g_{B\cup C}(y))=\int_{C}\frac{g_{C}(y)^{2}}{g_{C}(y)}=1.\qed

We proved Lemma
5.8
in the real positive definite setting; a proof in the complex setting is outlined in
[
Lau96
, Lemma 5.5]
. We now describe the solution to the homaloidal PDE for chordal graphical models and the associated MLE map. In particular, we show that such models have ML degree one.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a chordal graph. The function
\Phi_{G}
defined recursively by

\displaystyle\Phi_{G}(S)=\begin{cases}\det(S^{-1})&G\text{ complete,}\\
\dfrac{\Phi_{A\cup C}(S_{A\cup C})\,\Phi_{B\cup C}(S_{B\cup C})}{\Phi_{C}(S_{C})}&G=A\amalg_{C}B\\
\end{cases}

satisfies the homaloidal PDE. The corresponding MLE
\Psi_{G}\coloneqq-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi_{G}(S)
satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi_{G}(S)=\begin{cases}S^{-1}&G\text{ complete,}\\
[\Psi_{A\cup C}(S_{A\cup C})]^{V}+[\Psi_{B\cup C}(S_{B\cup C})]^{V}-[\Psi_{C}(S_{C})]^{V}&G=A\amalg_{C}B.\end{cases}

The function
\Psi_{G}
maps surjectively to
\mathcal{M}(G)
.

Proof.

The recursion in the statement terminates if and only if
G
is chordal. If
G
is complete, the statement holds by Example
5.4
. We induct on the structure of
G
. If
G
is chordal but not necessarily complete, let
G=A\amalg_{C}B
be a non-trivial weak decomposition. Graphs
A
,
B
, and
C
are chordal, thus by the induction hypothesis
\Phi_{C}
,
\Phi_{A\cup C}
, and
\Phi_{B\cup C}
satisfy the homaloidal PDE and
the corresponding MLEs
\Psi_{C}
,
\Psi_{A\cup C}
, and
\Psi_{B\cup C}
exist and map surjectively onto
\mathcal{M}(C)
,
\mathcal{M}(A\cup C)
and
\mathcal{M}(B\cup C)
, respectively.
Using the definition of
\Phi_{G}
, we compute

\displaystyle\Psi_{G}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi_{G}(S)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi_{A\cup C}(S)-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi_{B\cup C}(S)+\nabla_{S}\log\Phi_{C}(S)

\displaystyle=[\Psi_{A\cup C}(S_{A\cup C})]^{V}+[\Psi_{B\cup C}(S_{B\cup C})]^{V}-[\Psi_{C}(S_{C})]^{V}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
5.8
,

\det\Psi_{G}=\frac{(\det\Psi_{C}^{-1})}{(\det\Psi_{A\cup C}^{-1})(\det\Psi_{B\cup C}^{-1})}=\frac{\Phi_{A\cup C}\Phi_{B\cup C}}{\Phi_{C}}=\Phi_{G}.

Thus
\Phi_{G}
satisfies the homaloidal PDE.
Finally,
let
K\in\mathcal{M}(G)
. Then the submatrices
K_{A\cup C}
,
K_{B\cup C}
and
K_{C}
are elements of
\mathcal{M}(A\cup C)
,
\mathcal{M}(B\cup C)
and
\mathcal{M}(C)
, respectively. Since
K_{ij}=0
whenever
i\in A
and
j\in B
, we have
K=[K_{A\cup C}]^{V}+[K_{B\cup C}]^{V}-[K_{C}]^{V}.
Using the induction hypothesis, we see that
K
is in the image of
\Psi_{G}
. Hence,
\Psi_{G}
maps surjectively onto
\mathcal{M}(G)
.
∎

\Phi
, verify the homaloidal PDE, and then compute the log-derivative to get an MLE map, whose image is the corresponding model. We explore some examples.

5.3.
Directed graphical models

Definition 5.13
.

Fix a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G=(V,E)
. A
directed Gaussian graphical model
on
G
consists of concentration matrices
K=(I-A)^{\top}\Omega(I-A)
, where
\Omega
is diagonal positive definite and
A_{ij}=0
unless
j\to i
is in
E
.

We prove that directed Gaussian graphical models have ML degree one by providing a solution to the homaloidal PDE,
in Proposition
5.15
.
We begin with the determinant formula that is analogous to Lemma
5.8
.
The formula makes use of the
parents
of a node
v\in V
; i.e.,
\operatorname{pa}(v)\coloneqq\{i\mid i\to v~\textnormal{in}~E\}
.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a DAG and
S
a general positive definite symmetric
|V|\times|V|
matrix. Define

(11)

\hat{K}\coloneqq\sum_{v\in V}K_{[v|\operatorname{pa}(v)]},\quad\text{where}\quad K_{[v|\operatorname{pa}(v)]}\coloneqq[S^{-1}_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)}]^{V}-[S^{-1}_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}]^{V}.

Then

\det\hat{K}=\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\det S_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}}{\det S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)}}.

Proof.

The density function associated to
\hat{K}^{-1}
is

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-m/2}(\det\hat{K})^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}\hat{K}y\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-m/2}(\det\hat{K})^{1/2}\prod_{v\in V}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}K_{[v|\operatorname{pa}(v)]}y\right).

We obtain a second description of
f(y)
. We have

f(y_{v}|y_{\operatorname{pa}(v)})=\frac{f(y_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)})}{f(y_{\operatorname{pa}(v)})}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\frac{\det(S_{\operatorname{pa}(v)})^{1/2}}{\det(S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)})^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}K_{[v|\operatorname{pa}(v)]}y\right).

Thus,

f(y)=(\det\hat{K})^{1/2}\bigg(\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\det S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)}}{\det{S_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\prod_{v\in V}f(y_{v}|y_{\operatorname{pa}(v)})\bigg).

According to the factorization property of
G
, the rightmost factor of the above expression is a probability distribution. Integrating over
y
we obtain the desired result.
∎

While we prove Lemma
5.14
in the real positive definite setting, a proof can also be found in the general complex setting, by following the structural equations of the graph

with complex variables.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let

be a DAG and
\mathcal{M}
its associated Gaussian graphical model. The function

\Phi(S)\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\det(S_{\operatorname{pa}(v)})}{\det(S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)})}

\Psi
sends

to
\hat{K}
and maps surjectively to
\mathcal{M}
.
In particular, every DAG model has ML degree one.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle-\nabla_{S}\log\Phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\big(\nabla_{S}\log\det(S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)})-\nabla_{S}\log\det(S_{[\operatorname{pa}(v)]})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\big([S_{v\cup\operatorname{pa}(v)}^{-1}]^{V}-[S_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}^{-1}]^{V}\big)=\hat{K}

and
\det(-\nabla\log\Phi)=\Phi
, by Lemma
5.14
. In the proof of the lemma, we also see that

since the distribution associated to
\hat{K}
factorizes according to
G
.
∎

6.
Solutions to the homaloidal PDE

We saw instances where linear ML degree one varieties can be characterized, via connections to homaloidal polynomials, in Section
4
.
In this section, we
present steps towards parametrizing the solutions to the homaloidal PDE when
X
is not necessarily linear.
We study factorization properties of solutions to the homaloidal PDE in Proposition
6.1
, inspired by the proof of
[
Huh14(1)
, Lemma 16]
.
We solve the PDE when the polynomial
F
is linear in Theorem
6.2
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix a basis
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\dim\mathcal{L}}
for
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
. Let
\Psi
be the MLE map of some variety with ML degree one. Consider prime decompositions
of the coordinates of
\Psi
,

\psi_{i}=c_{i}\prod_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f^{\alpha_{i,f}}

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and

\mathcal{F}
is the set of all prime factors that appear in some coordinate.
The prime decompositions satisfy:

(a)

The factors in the denominators are linear; i.e.,
\alpha_{i,f}\geq-1
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\dim\mathcal{L}\}
.

(b)

If
f
appears in some denominator, then
\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{j}}\neq 0
if and only if
\alpha_{j,f}=-1
. That is,
f
only depends on the
u_{j}
for which
f
is a factor of the denominator of
\psi_{j}
.

Proof.

There is a rational function
\Phi
such that
\Psi=-\nabla\log\Phi
,
by Theorem
3.5
.
We decompose
\Phi=\prod_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}^{\beta_{k}}
into prime factors for some integer
N
. Fixing a basis, let
\mathcal{G}_{i}\coloneqq\{k:\,\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}\neq 0\}
denote the set of indices
k
such that
g_{k}
depends on
u_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{i}=-\frac{\partial\log\Phi}{\partial u_{i}}=-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\frac{\beta_{k}}{g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}=-\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{k\}}g_{j})\beta_{k}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}}{\prod_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}g_{k}}.

Then (a) follows, because no exponent in the denominator of any
\psi_{i}
can be greater than one after simplifying the expression above. We next show that there are no common prime factors of the numerator and denominator of the derived expression for
\psi_{i}
. For each index
l\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
we may write

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{k\}}g_{j}\right)\beta_{k}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}=\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{l\}}g_{j}\right)b_{l}\frac{\partial g_{l}}{\partial u_{i}}+g_{l}\cdot\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{l\}}\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{k,l\}}g_{j}\right)\beta_{k}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}\right).

From this we deduce that

\displaystyle g_{l}\Big|\sum_{k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{k\}}g_{j}\right)\beta_{k}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial u_{i}}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad g_{l}\Big|\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{l\}}g_{j}\right)b_{l}\frac{\partial g_{l}}{\partial u_{i}}.

However,
g_{l}\nmid(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{l\}}g_{j})b_{l}\frac{\partial g_{l}}{\partial u_{i}}
because all
g_{k}
are distinct primes and a polynomial cannot be a factor of its own derivative. Thus the expression for
\psi_{i}
has no common prime factor in the numerator and denominator. From this we conclude that
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}
and that these factors
g_{k}
are the only ones that occur in the denominator of
\psi_{i}
. Moreover,
g_{k}
will appear exactly in the denominators of
\psi_{i}
with
k\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
.
∎

We can observe the described properties of the map
\Psi
in all examples of ML degree one varieties presented throughout this paper. We conclude this section by solving the PDE when the polynomial
F
is linear.
If
Y\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a hypersurface and
p\in Y
a singular point with multiplicity
\deg(Y)-1
, we define a retraction
r_{p}:\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow Y
that sends a point
q
to the unique point in
Y
on the line through
p
and
q
. The map
r_{p}
is rational.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
finite-dimensional

\mathbb{C}
-vector space and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.
Let
\Phi=\nicefrac{{f}}{{g}}
be a rational function on
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, homogeneous of degree
-1
. Then
\Phi
satisfies the homaloidal PDE with respect to
\ell
if and only if
\ell
is a point of
V(g)
of multiplicity
\deg g-1
and
f=\ell(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}g)
.

In this case, the map
\Psi\coloneqq-\nabla\log\Phi
satisfies
[\Psi({\boldsymbol{u}})]=[\nabla_{r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}})}g]
for general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
, where
r_{\ell}
is the retraction to
V(g)
. The variety of ML degree one, parametrized by
\Psi
, is the dual variety
\mathbb{P}(V(g))^{\vee}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\overline{\operatorname{Im}(\nabla g|_{V(g)}}).

Proof.

The function
\Phi
satisfies the homaloidal PDE if and only if

(13)

1=\frac{\ell(-\nabla\log\Phi)}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{u}})}=-\frac{\ell(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Phi)}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{u}})^{2}}=\left[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{u}}+t\ell)}\right]_{t=0}.

Pick a basis
(\ell_{1},\dotsc,\ell_{n})
of
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
such that
\ell=\ell_{1}
. In this basis, elements of
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
are written as
{\boldsymbol{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\ell_{i}
and (
13
) is equivalent to

\frac{d}{du_{1}}\frac{1}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{u}})}=1.

This equation is satisfied if and only if

\frac{1}{\Phi(u)}=u_{1}+\frac{h_{2}}{h_{1}}=\frac{u_{1}h_{1}+h_{2}}{h_{1}},

where
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[u_{2},\dotsc,u_{n}]
and
\deg h_{2}=\deg h_{1}+1
.
The latter is equivalent to

(14)

f\in\mathbb{C}[u_{2},\dotsc,u_{n}]\quad\text{and}\quad g=u_{1}f+h\quad\text{for some}\quad h\in\mathbb{C}[u_{2},\dotsc,u_{n}]_{\deg f+1}.

Since
\ell
is the origin in the affine chart
u_{1}=1
, (
14
) holds if and only if

\ell
is a point of
V(g)
of multiplicity
\deg g-1
with
f=\ell(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}g)
.

Let
\Phi
as above satisfy the homaloidal PDE. Use the same basis as above, so that
\ell=\ell_{1}
and
g=u_{1}f+h
where
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[u_{2},\dotsc,u_{n}]
. Then

\displaystyle[\Psi]

\displaystyle=[\nabla\Phi]=[\nabla\log f-\nabla\log(u_{1}f+h)]

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{h\nabla f-f^{2}\nabla u_{1}-f\nabla h}{f(u_{1}f+h)}\right]=[h\nabla f-f^{2}\nabla u_{1}-f\nabla h].

The right-hand side does not depend on
u_{1}
. Hence the function
[\Psi]
is constant along the line through
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\ell
. Moreover,

[\Psi]=\left[\frac{g\nabla f-f\nabla g}{g^{2}}\right]=[g\nabla f-f\nabla g],

thus
[\Psi]
is equivalent to a rational function that is defined on
V(g)
. For general
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
we have
g(r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}}))=0
and
f(r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}}))\neq 0
, thus

[(g\nabla f-f\nabla g)(r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}}))]=[f(r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}}))\nabla_{r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}})}g]=[\nabla_{r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}})}g].

Since
r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}})
is on the line through
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\ell
, we conclude that
[\Phi({\boldsymbol{u}})]=[\nabla_{r_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{u}})}g]
.
∎

The next three examples involve a smooth quadric
Q

For vectors

The dual quadric

{\boldsymbol{u}}

Note that

\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}Q^{\vee}=2A^{-1}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}

The points on

and

(15)

Q(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}Q^{\vee})=4Q^{\vee}({\boldsymbol{u}}).

The gradients of

(16)

(\nabla_{x}Q)(y)=2A(x,y)\qquad\boldsymbol{v}(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}Q)=2A^{-1}(\boldsymbol{v},{\boldsymbol{u}}).

The ambient vector space and its dual relate via

and

{\boldsymbol{u}}x

We have the following biduality. Take

Define

at

We can compute

cuts out the tangent hyperplane of

at

.

The solutions to the homaloidal PDE for ML degree one hyperplanes in

are the rational functions

\displaystyle\Phi(S)=\frac{\det\left((gSg^{\top})_{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}\right)}{\det\left((gSg^{\top})_{[n]\setminus\{1\}}\right)\det\left((gSg^{\top})_{[n]\setminus\{2\}}\right)},

gAg^{\top}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\begin{matrix}0&1\\
