Optimal Transport between Gaussian random fields
Mattia Zorzi

Abstract
We consider the optimal transport problem between zero mean Gaussian stationary random fields both in the aperiodic and periodic case. We show that the solution corresponds to a weighted Hellinger distance between the multivariate and multidimensional power spectral densities of the random fields. Then, we show that such a distance defines a geodesic, which depends on the weight function, on the manifold of the multivariate and multidimensional power spectral densities.

II
OTP between random fields

Consider two jointly Gaussian stationary random fields
\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
and
\mathbf{y}=\{\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}},\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
having zero mean and taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. It is worth noting that the index
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots t_{d})
has dimension
d
. These random fields are completely characterized by the finite dimensional probability density functions

p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}),\;\;p_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{s}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s})

with
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, while the corresponding joint random field is completely characterized by the finite dimensional probability density

p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

with
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We consider the following optimal transport problem

\displaystyle d(p_{\mathbf{x}},p_{\mathbf{y}})^{2}=\underset{p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{P}}{\inf}\{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\|^{2}]\hbox{ s.t. (\ref{cond_marg1})-(\ref{cond_marg2}) hold}\}

(1)

where

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}),\;\;\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}),\;\;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(3)

and
\mathcal{P}
is the set of Gaussian joint probability densities
p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
. In plain words, the above problem represents the optimal transport between Gaussian random fields
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
and the transportation cost is the variance of
\mathbf{e}:=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
which can be understood as the discrepancy random field.

Since the joint random field is Gaussian, it is completely characterized by its covariance field

R_{\mathbf{t}}=R_{-\mathbf{t}}^{T}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}}\\
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}}^{T}&\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{s}}^{T}\end{array}\right]\right],\;\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

or, equivalently, by its discrete-time multidimensional Fourier transform

\displaystyle\Phi(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}}):=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}R_{\mathbf{t}}e^{-j\langle\bm{\vartheta},\mathbf{t}\rangle}

(4)

where
\bm{\vartheta}=(\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2},\ldots,\vartheta_{d})\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and it represents the power spectral density of the joint process. Partitioning (
4
) in a conformable way with respect to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, we obtain:

\Phi(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})&\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\\
\Phi_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})&\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\end{array}\right]

where
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}
are the power spectral densities of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively.

Since
p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
and
\Phi
represent two equivalent descriptions of the joint process, we want to rewrite (
1
) in terms of
\Phi
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\tr\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}]

\displaystyle=\tr\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}+\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}-\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}-\Phi_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}\mu

(5)

where

\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{\vartheta})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{k}.

Then conditions (
II
) and (
II
) imposes that
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}
are fixed. Accordingly, we obtain the optimal transport problem

\displaystyle d(p_{\mathbf{x}},p_{\mathbf{y}})^{2}=

\displaystyle\underset{\Phi_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}{\inf}\tr\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}+\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}-\Phi_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\Phi_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\hbox{ s.t. }\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}&\Phi_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\\
\Phi_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}}&\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}\end{array}\right]\geq 0.

In what follows, we assume that

III
OTP between periodic random fields

Consider two jointly Gaussian stationary periodic random fields
\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
and
\mathbf{y}=\{\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}},\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
having zero mean, taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and with period
\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{d})
. This means that for any

\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbf{y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{l-1},t_{l}+N_{l},t_{l+1},\ldots t_{d})

\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\mathbf{N}}:=\left\{\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d}),\;\;0\leq t_{l}\leq N_{l}-1,\;\;l=1\ldots d\right\}.

The corresponding joint random field is completely characterized by

p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

with
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{N}

We consider the following optimal transport problem

\displaystyle d(p_{\mathbf{x}},p_{\mathbf{y}})^{2}=\underset{p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{P}}{\inf}\{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\|^{2}]\hbox{ s.t. (\ref{cond_marg1p})-(\ref{cond_marg2p}) hold}\}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}),\;\;\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbf{N}}^{d}

(15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{v}};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}),\;\;\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\mathbf{N}}

(16)

and
\mathcal{P}
is the set of Gaussian joint probability densities
p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}

Since the joint random field is Gaussian, it is completely characterized by its covariance field

R_{\mathbf{t}}=R_{-\mathbf{t}}^{T}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}}\\
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}}^{T}&\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{s}}^{T}\end{array}\right]\right],\;\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

which is also periodic, that is

R_{\mathbf{t}}=R_{(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{l-1},t_{l}+N_{l},t_{l+1},\ldots,t_{d})}

for any

. Accordingly, its power spectral density is

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}}):=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\mathbf{N}}}R_{\mathbf{t}}\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}}^{-\mathbf{t}}\;\;

(17)

where
\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta_{\ell_{1}},\zeta_{\ell_{2}},\ldots,\zeta_{\ell_{d}})
,
\bm{\ell}=(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbf{N}}^{d}

\xi_{\ell_{i}}=e^{\frac{2\pi}{N_{i}}\ell_{i}}

d

\Phi(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})
according to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
:

\Phi(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})&\Phi_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})\\
\Phi_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})&\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{\ell}})\end{array}\right]

and
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\Phi_{\mathbf{y}}
are the power spectral densities of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle[\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\|^{2}]=\tr\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}+\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}}^{T}]

(18)

where

\displaystyle(p_{\mathbf{x}},p_{\mathbf{y}})^{2}

IV
Spectral morphing

Consider a zero mean Gaussian (aperiodic) random field whose description slowly varies over time. Moreover, suppose that in a sufficiently small time interval
[k-\sigma,k+\sigma]
, for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
, the random field can be considered to be stationary. Therefore, at time
k
it can be approximately described by a power spectral density, say
\Phi_{k}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})
. It is then natural to wonder how to construct a smooth interpolation between nearby power spectral densities, e.g.
\Phi_{k-1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})
and
\Phi_{k}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})
. The latter task is referred to as spectral morphing. A possible smooth interpolation is given by the geodesic defined by the weighted Hellinger distance (
2

k=0

k=1

\displaystyle d_{\Omega}

\displaystyle(\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\tr\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\Omega\Phi_{0}+\Phi_{1}-2(\Phi_{0}^{1/2}\Omega\Phi_{1}\Omega\Phi_{0}^{1/2})^{1/2})\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\tr\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|\Phi_{0}^{1/2}-\Phi_{1}^{1/2}U_{\Omega}\|^{2}_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle U_{\Omega}

\displaystyle(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})=\Phi_{1}^{-1/2}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Omega^{-1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Phi_{0}^{-1/2}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})

\displaystyle\times(\Phi_{0}^{1/2}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Omega(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Phi_{1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Omega(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})\Phi_{0}^{1/2}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}}))^{1/2}

U_{\Omega}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})U_{\Omega}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})^{*}=I

\Phi_{1}^{1/2}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})U_{\Omega}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})

\Phi_{0}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})

\Phi_{1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})

\displaystyle\Phi_{\tau}

\displaystyle(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})=[(1-\tau)\Phi_{0}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})^{1/2}+\tau\Phi_{1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})^{1/2}U_{\Omega}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})]

\displaystyle\times[(1-\tau)\Phi_{0}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})^{1/2}+\tau\Phi_{1}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})^{1/2}U_{\Omega}(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})]^{*}

(24)

with
\tau\in[0,1]
.

\Omega(e^{j\bm{\vartheta}})=I

(25)

which is the one considered in

VI
The general case

The OTP’s analyzed before consider
\mathcal{P}

as the set of Gaussian joint probability densities. This hypothesis, however, can be weakened. Notice that a Gaussian process is a particular elliptical process. More precisely,
we can take
\mathcal{P}
as the set of the joint probability densities such that
[\,\mathbf{x}^{T}\;\mathbf{y}^{T}\,]^{T}
is an elliptical stationary process having zero mean and with joint power spectral density bounded and coercive. Accordingly,
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are elliptical processes with zero mean. We conclude that the same reasoning and thus same results hold also in this case.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper we have introduced the optimal transport problem between Gaussian aperiodic/periodic Gaussian random fields. The solution to these problems leads to a weighted Hellinger distance between multivariate and multidimensional power spectral densities. Such a distance can be characterized in terms of spectral factors. In the unidimensional case, the Hellinger distance can be defined in such a way to have the freedom in choosing one of these two spectral factors, see
[
8
]
; in particular, it is always possible to choose a rational spectral factor if the corresponding spectral density is rational. It is worth stressing that this last fact in the multidimensional case, however, is no longer true in general,

[
33
,
34
]
.

Finally, we have shown that the weighted Hellinger distance defines a geodesic, depending on the weight function, on the manifold of the multivariate and multidimensional spectral densities.
