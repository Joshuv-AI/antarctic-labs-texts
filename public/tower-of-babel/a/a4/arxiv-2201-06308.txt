Preferred basis of states derived from eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
Hua Yan, Jiaozi Wang, Wen-ge Wang

Abstract
We study the long-time average of the reduced density matrix (RDM) of a central system that is locally coupled to a large environment, under a Schrödinger evolution of the total system. We consider a class of interaction Hamiltonian, whose environmental part satisfies the so-called eigenstate thermalization hypothesis ansatz with a constant diagonal part in the energy region concerned. Relations among elements of the averaged RDM are derived. When steady states of the central system exist, these relations imply the existence of a preferred basis, which is given by the eigenbasis of a renormalized self-Hamiltonian that includes certain averaged impact of the system-environment interaction. Numerical simulations performed for a qubit coupled to a defect Ising chain confirm the analytical predictions.



I
Introduction

Properties of small open quantum systems, which are coupled to large quantum environments,
have attracted significant attention and been studied extensively in recent decades in various
fields of physics
Leggett et al. 1987
;
Breuer et al. 2002
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007
;
Breuer et al. 2016
;
De Vega and Alonso 2017
.
Such a central system is described by its reduced density matrix (RDM) and may approach a steady
state in many situations. For example, it is now well known that the phenomenon of decoherence,
due to interactions with huge quantum environments,
may happen in such a way that a RDM becomes approximately diagonal on a so-called preferred (pointer) basis
of states (PBS)

Zurek 1981
;
Zurek 2003
;
Schlosshauer 2005
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Paz and Zurek 1999
;
Joos et al. 2013
.
Under pure-dephasing interactions, decoherence has been studied well,
with PBS given by eigenbases of self-Hamiltonians

Gorin et al. 2004
;
Albash and Lidar 2015
;
Weiss 2012
.
However, under strong interactions and complex environments, with the self-Hamiltonians negligible,
PBS may be given by eigenbases of the interaction Hamiltonians

Zurek 1981
;
Gorin et al. 2004
.

The situation is much more complicated with a generic dissipative interaction,
whose Hamiltonian is not commutable with the central system’s self-Hamiltonian,
due to the interplay of decoherence and relaxation.
In this generic case, knowledge about PBS is still far from being complete.
Under a sufficiently weak interaction and by a first-order
perturbation theory, it was found that the system’s eigenbasis is approximately a PBS
under a quantum chaotic environment
Wang et al. 2008
.
When the total system’s eigenfunctions possess certain special randomness,
a PBS (if existing) is given by the eigenbasis of a renormalized self-Hamiltonian

He and Wang 2014
.
These results are in agreement with a generic expectation for Markovian processes described by Lindblad master
equations, as exemplified in solvable models
Breuer et al. 2002
;
Alicki and Lendi 2007
;
Albash and Lidar 2015
.
While, when non-Markovian effects due to dynamics of the total system are taken into account,
nonnegligible off-diagonal elements of RDM have been found at long times in various spin-boson models
on the spin’s energy basis

Lee et al. 2012
;
Addis et al. 2014
;
Roszak et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2015
;
Guarnieri et al. 2018
.

In this paper, we go further in the study of properties of steady states of small open systems,
by directly computing their long-time averaged RDM under overall Schrödinger evolutions of total systems.
A key point of our approach is to consider those environments, for which
the environmental parts of the interaction Hamiltonians satisfy
the so-called eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) ansatz

Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Srednicki 1999
;
Rigol et al. 2008
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018

and their diagonal elements in the ansatz may be treated as constants within the energy regions of relevance.
We are to derive
\frac{1}{2}(m-1)(m+2)
relations among elements of such an averaged RDM
for a central system with a number of
m
levels.
When steady states exist, these relations imply that the central system should have a PBS,
which is given by the eigenbasis of a renormalized self-Hamiltonian
that includes certain impact of the system-environment interaction.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we specify the systems to be studied.
In Sec.
III
, we derive the above-mentioned relations.
Some further discussions are given in Sec.
IV
.
Numerical simulations are presented in Sec.
V
,
to illustrate validity of the analytical predictions for a qubit as the central system
and a defect Ising chain as the environment.
Finally, conclusions and discussions are given in Sec.
VI
.

III
Main result

In this section, we derive the main result of this paper,
as relations among elements of the long-time averaged RDM.
Specifically, we give some formal discussions in Sec.
III.1
,
then, in Sec.
III.2
, derive an upper bound to the environmental energy region,
which is of relevance effectively to the wave function at all times.
The main result is derived in Sec.

III.1
Preliminary discussions

The total system undergoes a Schrödinger evolution,

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iHt/\hbar}|\Psi(0)\rangle.

(15)

We write
|\Psi(t)\rangle
in the following expansion with respect to the central system’s states
|\alpha\rangle
,

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}|\alpha\rangle|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle,

(16)

and call
|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
the
environmental branches
of
|\Psi(t)\rangle
.
These branches, as vectors in the environmental Hilbert space, are written as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=\langle\alpha|\Psi(t)\rangle,

(17)

and are usually not normalized.
Under the initial condition in Eq.(
11
), it is direct to find that

|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=\sum_{\beta}c_{0\beta}\langle\alpha|e^{-iHt/\hbar}|\beta\rangle|\mathcal{E}_{0}\rangle,

(18)

and

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=H_{\alpha\alpha}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}H_{\alpha\beta}|\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)\rangle,

(19)

where
H_{\alpha\beta}
indicate operators that act on the Hilbert space
of the environment, as defined below,

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}:=\langle\alpha|H|\beta\rangle.

(20)

By definition, the RDM of the system
S
, denoted by
\rho^{S}(t)
,
is given by
\rho^{S}(t)=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{E}}\rho(t)
, where
\rho(t)=|\Psi(t)\rangle\langle\Psi(t)|
.
It is easy to check that elements of the RDM on the basis
\{|\alpha\rangle\}
, namely

\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)=\langle\alpha|\rho^{S}(t)|\beta\rangle
,
have the following expression,

\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(21)

Making use of Eq.(
19
), after some deviation,
one finds that the elements
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)
satisfy the following equation
(see Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)}{dt}=W_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}+\lambda W_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)},

(22)

where

\displaystyle W_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e_{\alpha}^{S}-e_{\beta}^{S})\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t),

(23a)

\displaystyle W_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{m}H_{\alpha\gamma}^{IS}F_{\beta\gamma}(t)-\sum_{\gamma=1}^{m}H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}F_{\gamma\alpha}(t).

(23b)

Here,
F_{\alpha\beta}(t)
indicate
c
-number quantities defined below,

F_{\alpha\beta}(t):=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)|H^{I\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)\rangle,

(24)

and, from them, we define the following operator,

\displaystyle F(t):=\sum_{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}(t)|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|.

(25)

It is easy to check that
W_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}
and
W_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}
have the following concise expressions,

\displaystyle W_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}=\langle\alpha|[H^{S},\rho^{S}(t)]|\beta\rangle,

(26a)

\displaystyle W_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}=\langle\alpha|[H^{IS},F^{T}(t)]|\beta\rangle,

(26b)

where
{F}^{T}
indicates the transposition operator of
{F}
, which is defined on the eigenbasis of
H^{S}
.

We use an overline to indicate the long-time average of a term.
For example, the long-time average of the RDM is written as
\overline{\rho}^{S}
,

\overline{\rho}^{S}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\rho^{S}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(27)

Clearly, in the case that a steady state of the RDM exists, it is given by
\overline{\rho}^{S}
.
Since the elements
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)
have bounded values,
the long-time average of
d\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)/{dt}
must be zero, i.e.,

\overline{{d\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)}/{dt}}=0
.
Then, Eq.(
22
) gives that

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}+\lambda\overline{W}_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}=0.

(28)

Substituting the explicit expressions of
{W}_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}

and
{W}_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}
in Eq.(
23
) into Eq.(
28
), one finds
the following formal relation for the long-time averaged RDM:

\displaystyle(e_{\alpha}^{S}-e_{\beta}^{S})\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}+\lambda\sum_{\gamma=1}^{m}\Big[H_{\alpha\gamma}^{IS}\overline{F}_{\beta\gamma}-H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}\overline{F}_{\gamma\alpha}\Big]=0.

(29)

It is straightforward to check that a concise form of Eq.(
29
) is written as

\displaystyle[H^{S},\overline{\rho}^{S}]+\lambda[H^{IS},\overline{F}^{T}]=0.

(30)

III.2
Effective environmental energy region

In this section, we discuss an environmental energy region,
within which all the branches
|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
effectively lie for all the times
t
, and indicate it by
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
.
We do not need to find the smallest one of this type of region.
Instead, we consider a region that has the following simple form,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}=[e_{0}-\delta e/2,e_{0}+\delta e/2],

(31)

centered at the initial center
e_{0}
and with a width
\delta e
.

Below, we derive an expression for
\delta e
, as an upper bound to the width of the energy
region that effectively contains all
|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
.
For this purpose, we need to analyze the components
\langle i|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
,

\displaystyle\langle i|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=\sum_{\beta,n}\sum_{e_{j}\in\Gamma_{0}^{\mathcal{E}}}c_{0\beta}c_{0j}C^{n*}_{\beta j}C^{n}_{\alpha i}e^{-iE_{n}t},

(32)

which is directly obtained by making use of Eqs.(
11
)-(
13
) and (
15
)-(
17
).
Initially, with
e^{-iE_{n}t}=1
at
t=0
,
due to correlations among the terms of
(C^{n*}_{\beta j}C^{n}_{\alpha i})
of different indices
n
,
which originate from the completeness of the states
|n\rangle
as a basis in the total Hilbert space,
nonzero values of the rhs of Eq.(
32
) are restricted within the initial energy region
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
.
With increase of the time
t
, the phases
e^{-iE_{n}t}
gradually destroy the above-mentioned correlations
and, as a result, the energy region that is effectively occupied by
|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
expands.
Cutting all the correlations by taking an absolute value for each summed term on the rhs of Eq.(
32
),
one gets an upper bound to
|\langle i|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle|
:

\displaystyle|\langle i|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle|\leq\sum_{e_{j}\in\Gamma_{0}^{\mathcal{E}}}\sum_{\beta,n}\left|c_{0\beta}c_{0j}C^{n*}_{\beta j}C^{n}_{\alpha i}\right|.

(33)

When using the rhs of Eq.(
33
) to get an upper bound
to the environmental energy region that effectively contains

|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
, exact values of the nonzero coefficients
c_{0\beta}
and
c_{0j}
are not important.
Hence, we may focus on the values of
C^{n*}_{\beta j}C^{n}_{\alpha i}
.
In particular, for the eigenfunction (EF)
C^{n}_{\alpha i}
of each state
|n\rangle
,
what is of relevance is its main-body region,
within which the main population lie up to a small error indicated by
\epsilon
.
Energetically, such a main-body region consists of those uncoupled states
|\alpha i\rangle
, whose energies
E_{\alpha i}

are around the exact energy
E_{n}
within a scope which we indicate by
w^{\epsilon}_{n}
.
More exactly, the set of the indices of these uncoupled states, indicated by
\Omega^{\epsilon}_{n}
,
is written as

\displaystyle\Omega^{\epsilon}_{n}=\left\{(\alpha,i):|E_{\alpha i}-E_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{2}w^{\epsilon}_{n}\right\}.

(34)

Then, the main-body region of the EF
C^{n}_{\alpha i}
satisfies the following requirement,

\displaystyle\sum_{(\alpha,i)\in\Omega^{\epsilon}_{n}}\left|C^{n}_{\alpha i}\right|^{2}\doteq 1-\epsilon\quad(\epsilon\ll 1),

(35)

where “
\doteq
” means that the set
\Omega^{\epsilon}_{n}

is chosen such that the left hand side of Eq.(
35
) is the closest to its right hand side.

\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
.

For a product
C^{n*}_{\beta j}C^{n}_{\alpha i}
to give a nonnegligible contribution

both of the two basis states

As as result, the expansion from

We use

to indicate the maximum value of

for those states

that are of relevance to the time evolution of the initial state.
Then, noting that

in Eq.(
4
) gives the maximum of

,
we find the following expression of

\displaystyle\delta e=\delta e_{0}+2\Delta_{S}+w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max},

as illustrated in Fig.

Two remarks are in order: (1) At

, the real width is just

(2) When the states

III.3
Relations among elements of averaged RDM

In this section, we derive the main result, as relations that the elements of
\overline{\rho}^{S}
satisfy.
To this end, let us expand the environmental branches
|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle
as,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=\sum_{i}f_{\alpha i}(t)|i\rangle

(37)

with expansion coefficients
f_{\alpha i}(t)
.
Substituting Eq.(
37
) into Eqs.(
21
) and (
24
),
taking the long-time average, and making use of the fact that
all the environmental branches effectively lie within the energy region
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
,
one finds the following expressions of
\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}

and
\overline{F}_{\beta\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}=\sum_{i}\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha i}}\simeq\sum_{e_{i}\in\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}}\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha i}},

(38)

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\beta\alpha}=\sum_{i,j}\overline{f_{\beta j}^{*}f_{\alpha i}}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}\simeq\sum_{e_{i},e_{j}\in\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}}\overline{f_{\beta j}^{*}f_{\alpha i}}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ji}.

(39)

Substituting the ETH ansatz (
10
) into Eq.(
39
), one gets that

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\beta\alpha}\simeq\sum_{e_{i}\in{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}}h(e_{i})\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha i}}+\Delta_{\alpha\beta},

(40)

where
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}
indicates a fluctuation term, given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{i,j}\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}e^{-S(e)/2}g(e,\omega)R_{ij}.

(41)

Generically, the two operators
\overline{F}
and
\overline{\rho}^{S}
do not have a simple relationship.
One key observation made here is that they may possess a simple relationship, if
the function
h(e)
is approximately a nonzero constant within the
energy shell
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
.
More exactly, the condition is that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{h_{0}}\Delta h\right|\leq\epsilon_{h}\quad\text{with $\epsilon_{h}\ll 1$},

(42)

where
\epsilon_{h}
is a parameter much smaller than
1
,

h_{0}\equiv h(e_{0})
with
h_{0}\neq 0
(even in the limit of
N\to\infty
),
and
\Delta h
indicates the maximum difference between
h_{0}
and
h(e_{i})
within
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
:

\displaystyle\Delta h=\max_{e_{i}\in{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}}|h(e_{i})-h_{0}|.

(43)

To show the above-mentioned relationship,
we note that, when the condition in Eq.(
42
) is satisfied, Eqs.(
38
)
and (
40
) imply that

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\beta\alpha}\simeq h_{0}\overline{\rho}^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+\Delta_{\alpha\beta},

(44)

or, equivalently,

\overline{F}^{T}\simeq h_{0}\overline{\rho}^{S}+\Delta,

(45)

where
\Delta
is a fluctuation operator,

\displaystyle\Delta:=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|.

(46)

From the rhs of Eq.(
41
), one sees three factors that influence the
N
-dependence of
\Delta
.
The first one is the exponential decay of
e^{-S(e)/2}
, with
S(e)\sim N
.
The second factor is given by the unknown ETH-ansatz function
g(e,\omega)
,
for which numerical simulations show a polynomial increase of
N^{\gamma}
, with
\gamma=1/2

in some (diffusive one-dimensional) systems

Luitz and Lev 2016
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
.
The third factor lies in the summation over the indices
i
and
j
and
the long-time average term
\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}
.
As shown in Appendix
B
, due to the randomness of
R_{ij}
,
contribution from the third factor is negligible compared with the first factor.
(See Eq.(
97
) for an upper bound to the norm of
\Delta
.)
Therefore, the
N
-scaling behavior of the fluctuation operator
\Delta

is dominated by the exponential decay term
e^{-S(e)/2}
.
Due to this exponential decay, as well as
the fact that
h_{0}\neq 0
in the limit of large
N
and that each RDM has a unit trace,
one gets that

\displaystyle\|\Delta\|\ll\|h_{0}\overline{\rho}^{S}\|\quad\text{at sufficiently large $N$}.

(47)

Then, Eq.(
45
) gives the following relation between the two operators of
\overline{F}
and
\overline{\rho}
:

\displaystyle\overline{F}^{T}\simeq h_{0}\overline{\rho}^{S}\quad\text{at sufficiently large $N$.}

(48)

The main result of this paper is obtained by substituting Eq.(
48
) into Eq.(
30
),

\displaystyle[\widetilde{H}^{S},\overline{\rho}^{S}]\simeq 0,

(49)

which holds under the condition of Eq.(
42
) and at sufficiently large
N
.
Here,
\widetilde{H}^{S}
is a renormalized self-Hamiltonian of the central system, defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{S}:=H^{S}+\lambda h_{0}H^{IS},

(50)

which includes certain averaged impact of the system-environment interaction.
From Eq.(
49
), one sees that, if a PBS exists, it should be
given by the eigenbasis of the renormalized self-Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{S}
.
Writing Eq.(
49
) explicitly, one gets that

\displaystyle(e_{\alpha}^{S}-e_{\beta}^{S})\overline{\rho}^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+\lambda h_{0}\sum_{\gamma}[H^{IS}_{\alpha\gamma}\overline{\rho}^{S}_{\gamma\beta}-\overline{\rho}^{S}_{\alpha\gamma}H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}]\simeq 0.

(51)

This gives
m(m-1)/2
relations among elements of the averaged RDM
for
\alpha\neq\beta
and
(m-1)
relations for
\alpha=\beta
.

As an illustration of the above result, let us consider a nondegenerate two-level system (TLS), with
e_{2}^{S}\neq e_{1}^{S}
.
From Eq.(
51
) with
\alpha\neq\beta
, one gets that

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}^{S}_{12}\simeq\frac{\lambda\eta_{r}h_{0}}{1-\lambda\eta_{d}h_{0}}(\overline{\rho}^{S}_{22}-\overline{\rho}^{S}_{11}),

(52)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{d}=\frac{H_{11}^{IS}-H_{22}^{IS}}{e_{2}^{S}-e_{1}^{S}},\quad\eta_{r}=\frac{H_{12}^{IS}}{e_{2}^{S}-e_{1}^{S}}.

(53)

The quantity
\eta_{d}
gives a relative measure for the strength of dephasing,
while,
\eta_{r}
gives a relative measure for the strength of relaxation (dissipation).
Meanwhile, in the case of
\alpha=\beta
, one gets that

H^{IS}_{12}\overline{\rho}^{S}_{21}-\overline{\rho}^{S}_{12}H^{IS}_{21}\simeq 0,

(54)

which implies approximate realness of the product
H^{IS}_{12}\overline{\rho}^{S}_{21}
.

III.4
N
- and
\lambda
-relevance to the condition (
42
)

In this section, we discuss relevance of the particle number
N
to Eq.(
42
),
a main prerequisite for the above-derived main result, as well as relevance of the interaction strength
\lambda
.

6
6

6

One may note that Eq.(
42
) is always satisfied,
in the case that EFs of the quantum chaotic environment
may be effectively described by the random matrix theory (RMT).
In fact, in this case,
h(e)
is a constant, given by
h(e)=\text{tr}(H^{I\mathcal{E}})/d_{\mathcal{E}}

D’Alessio et al. 2016
.

Basically, Eq.(
42
) requires that the environmental energy shell
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}

should be “sufficiently narrow”, such that the function
h(e)
may be approximately taken as a constant within it,
compared with its nonzero central value
h_{0}
.
Below, we give a detailed discussion of the exact meaning of “being sufficiently narrow”.

III.4.1
Relevance of the particle number N

Relevance of
N
to Eq.(
42
) comes mainly from two aspects: the width
\delta e
of
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}

in Eq.(
36
) and the ETH-ansatz function
h(e)
.
The width
\delta e=\delta e_{0}+2\Delta_{S}+w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
contains three terms.
Clearly,
\Delta_{S}
, the central system’s energy scope, is
N
-independent.
The
N
-dependence of
\delta e_{0}
is usually determined according to the problem at hand,
particularly, to quantities of final interest;
e.g., it may be taken as a constant, or as some polynomial function of
N
.

The situation with
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
, the maximum width of relevant EFs of the total system
on the uncoupled energy basis, is more complicated.
In fact, presently, still not much is known analytically about widths of the EFs.
It seems reasonable to assume that
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}\sim N^{\mu}
with some parameter

\mu
the value of which may be model-dependent.
By a first-order perturbation-theory treatment to long tails of EFs in certain model,
it was found that
\mu<0

Wang 2012
;
while, a study of higher-order contributions is still under investigation
Wang et al.

by making use of a semiperturbative theory
Wang and Wang 2019
;
Wang et al. 1998
;
Wang 2000
;
Wang 2002
.

The ETH ansatz does not assume any specific form of the function
h(e)
.
According to numerical simulations with the help of some analytical analysis

Kim et al. 2014
;
Brenes et al. 2020a
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Brenes et al. 2020b
,

h(e)
was found approximately a function of per-site energy,

\displaystyle h(e_{i})\approx\widetilde{h}(e_{i}/N),

(55)

where
\widetilde{h}(x)
is some smooth function of
x
, independent of
N
.
Then, Taylor’s expansion gives that

\displaystyle h(e_{i})-h(e_{j})=\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(e_{i}/N)\frac{e_{i}-e_{j}}{N}+O_{2}(\frac{e_{i}-e_{j}}{N}),

(56)

where
\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(x)
indicates the derivative of
\widetilde{h}(x)
and

O_{2}
represents the second and higher order terms of the expansion.

To be specific, let us discuss a case,
in which the initial width
\delta e_{0}
increases slower than
N
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\delta e_{0}}{N}=0.

(57)

This case may be met quite often practically.
Note that Eq.(
57
) does not really require narrowness of the initial shell
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
;
e.g., it holds for
\delta e_{0}\sim N^{b}
with a parameter
b<1
.
Then, as long as
\mu<1
for
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}\sim N^{\mu}
,
Eq.(
57
) implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\delta e}{N}=0.

(58)

This implies that the ratio
|e_{i}-e_{j}|/{N}
should approach zero in the limit of large
N

for
e_{i},e_{j}\in\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
.
If
\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\neq 0
, then, according to Eq.(
56
),
the difference
[h(e_{i})-h(e_{j})]
is approximately given by the first-order term at sufficiently large
N
.
As a consequence,
h(e)
is approximately a linear function
within
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
and
\Delta h
in Eq.(
43
) is written as

\displaystyle\Delta h\approx\frac{\delta e}{2N}\left|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\left(\frac{e_{0}}{N}\right)\right|.

(59)

One sees that, as long as
\left|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\left({e_{0}}/N\right)\right|
has a finite upper bound,

\Delta h/h_{0}\to 0
in the limit of
N\to\infty
.
Otherwise, i.e., if
\widetilde{h}^{\prime}=0
, one may consider the second-order term (if nonzero) in Eq.(
56
)
and, following arguments similar to those given above, reach the same conclusion.
Similar arguments also apply, when higher-order terms dominate.
Therefore, for systems with
\mu<1
, under an initial condition satisfying Eq.(
57
),
the condition (
42
) is usually fulfilled at sufficiently large
N
.

III.4.2
Relevance of the interaction strength

Among the three terms of
\Delta_{S}
,
\delta e_{0}
, and
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
in
\delta e
,
only the EF width
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
depends on the interaction strength
\lambda
.
As is well known, usually,
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
increases with increasing
\lambda
,
when other parameters in the total Hamiltonian are fixed.
It is reasonable to expect that dependence of
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
on the pair of
(N,\lambda)

may behave in a quite complicated way.
A full understanding of this behavior is beyond the scope of this investigation.
Below, for the sake of clearness in discussion,
we usually consider a fixed value of
N
when discussing influence of
\lambda
.

To study influence of the interaction strength
\lambda
on the condition in Eq.(
42
),
let us consider a case in which Eq.(
42
) is satisfied at
\lambda=0
with
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}=0
.
For example, one has such a case, if the initial shell
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is sufficiently narrow and the value of

\Delta_{S}
is sufficiently small.
With increase of
\lambda
from
0
, the value of
\delta e
increases due to the increase of
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
.
At a small
\lambda
, the width
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
is still small and, as a result,
Eq.(
42
) is also satisfied.

When the value of
\lambda
increases beyond some regime,
usually, it is possible for
\Delta h
to become
sufficiently large such that Eq.(
42
) gradually becomes invalid.
Note that the width
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
has no upper bound,
because it should increase (approximately) linearly with
\lambda

when the interaction Hamiltonian dominates in the total Hamiltonian.
To be quantitative, related to breakdown of Eq.(
42
),
one may consider a value of
\lambda
, indicated as
\lambda_{h}
,
at which the value of
|\Delta h/h_{0}|
first reaches
\epsilon_{h}
when
\lambda
increases from
0
.
Making use of Eqs.(
59
) and (
36
), from Eq.(
42
) one gets that

\displaystyle\delta e_{0}+2\Delta_{S}+w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}(\lambda_{h})\approx 2N\epsilon_{h}\left|\frac{h_{0}}{\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\left(\frac{e_{0}}{N}\right)}\right|.

(60)

Two properties are seen from Eq.(
60
):
(1) Since the width
w^{\epsilon}_{\rm max}
usually increases with increasing
\lambda
,
for systems with
\mu<1
, the value of
\lambda_{h}
may increase with increasing
N
;
and, (2)
\lambda_{h}
should increase with decreasing
\Delta_{S}
,
if other parameters are fixed.

IV
Further discussions

In this section, we discuss two situations, in which
some modified versions of the RDM relations given in the main result still hold when
some restrictions used above are loosened.
In Sec.
IV.1
, we derive RDM relations in the weak coupling limit,
without the restriction of Eq.(
42
).
In Sec.
IV.2
, we show that the main result may be generalized to
a generic local interaction Hamiltonian.

IV.1
Offdiagonal elements at very weak couplings

In this section, in the weak coupling limit of
\lambda
, without using the condition in Eq.(
42
),
we derive an expression for offdiagonal elements of the averaged RDM of nondegenerate levels,
by employing a first-order perturbation treatment.
In this limit, diagonal elements of RDM keep approximately constants, directly given by the initial condition:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}\simeq|c_{0\alpha}|^{2}\qquad\text{for nondegenerate levels $\alpha$}.

(61)

To be specific, below, we consider two arbitrary nondegenerate levels of the central system
S
,
indicated by
\alpha
and
\beta
with
e_{\beta}^{S}\neq e_{\alpha}^{S}
.
The zeroth-order branches, denoted by

Noting Eq.(

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}^{\rm 0th}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha|e^{-iH^{0}t}|\Psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{0\alpha}e^{-ie^{S}_{\alpha}t}\sum_{e_{j}\in\Gamma^{\cal E}_{0}}e^{-ie_{j}t}c_{0j}|j\rangle.

Substituting Eq.(

e_{\beta}^{S}\neq e_{\alpha}^{S}
,
one sees that
\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}
has a vanishing zeroth-order term.

The zeroth-order term of
\overline{F}_{\alpha\beta}
, indicated as
\overline{F}_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm 0th}
,
is computed by substituting Eq.(
62
) into Eq.(
24
) and taking the long-time average.
Noting that the chaotic environment
\mathcal{E}
has a nondegenerate spectrum,
direct computation gives that

7
7

7

For an environment that possesses a degenerate spectrum,
one may divide the set of those labels

We denote the subsets by
{\mathcal{D}}_{q}
with a label
q
, such that
e_{i}=e_{j}
for all

.
Then, it is easy to find that

\displaystyle h_{1}=\sum_{q}\sum_{i,j\in{\mathcal{D}}_{q}}c_{0i}^{*}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ij}c_{0j}.

(63)

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm 0th}=|c_{0\alpha}|^{2}h_{1}\delta_{\alpha\beta},

(64)

where

\displaystyle h_{1}=\sum_{e_{i}\in\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}|c_{0i}|^{2}H^{I\mathcal{E}}_{ii}.

(65)

Now, we compute the first-order term of
\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}

For this purpose, let us rewrite Eq.(

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{\alpha\beta}^{S}=\frac{\lambda}{e_{\beta}^{S}-e_{\alpha}^{S}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{m}\Big[H_{\alpha\gamma}^{IS}\overline{F}_{\beta\gamma}-H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}\overline{F}_{\gamma\alpha}\Big].

(67)

The two results were gotten under different conditions:

IV.2
A generic interaction

In this section, we give a brief discussion for a generic local interaction Hamiltonian
H^{I}
,
which is written as a sum of direct-product terms.
Suppose that there are
M_{\rm LIT}
such terms, with the subscript “LIT” standing for “local interaction terms”.
Then,
H^{I}
is written as

H^{I}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M_{\rm LIT}}\lambda_{\nu}H^{IS,\nu}\otimes H^{I\mathcal{E},\nu},

(69)

where
\lambda_{\nu}
are parameters and
H^{I\mathcal{E},\nu}
are local operators of the environment.
The operators
H^{I\mathcal{E},\nu}
are assumed to satisfy the ETH ansatz, with functions
h^{\nu}(e)
, respectively.
For such a generic
H^{I}
, the operator
F(t)
in Eq.(
25
) is written as

\displaystyle F(t)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M_{\rm LIT}}F_{\alpha\beta}^{\nu}(t)|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|,

(70)

where

F_{\alpha\beta}^{\nu}(t)=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)|H^{I\mathcal{E},\nu}|\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)\rangle.

(71)

Following arguments similar to those given in Sec.
III
,
with appropriate generalizations,
one may study the long-time average of this generic operator

and get similar results.
More exactly, the main generalization is that Eq.(
42
) is now written as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{h_{0}^{\nu}}\Delta h^{\nu}\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon_{h}}{M_{\rm LIT}}\quad\text{with $\epsilon_{h}\ll 1$}\quad(\forall\nu),

(72)

where
h_{0}^{\nu}=h^{\nu}(e_{0})
and

\displaystyle\Delta h^{\nu}=\max_{e_{i}\in{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{E}}}|{h^{\nu}(e_{i})-h_{0}^{\nu}}|.

(73)

The final result is that, at a sufficiently large
N
,

\displaystyle[\widetilde{H}^{S},\overline{\rho}^{S}]\simeq 0,

(74)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{S}=H^{S}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{M_{\rm LIT}}\lambda_{\nu}h_{0}^{\nu}H^{IS,\nu}.

(75)

VI
Conclusions and discussions

In this paper, the long-time averaged RDM has been studied for a generic small central system
S
with
m
levels,
which is locally coupled to a large many-body chaotic environment
\mathcal{E}
,
with the total system undergoing a Schrödinger evolution.
Beside largeness of the particle number of
\mathcal{E}
, the only restriction is that
the environmental part of the interaction Hamiltonian
satisfies the ETH ansatz, with the diagonal term in the ansatz [namely the function
h(e)
] approximately a constant
within the energy region of relevance.
For such a total system, on the eigenbasis of the central system,

\frac{1}{2}(m-1)(m+2)
approximate relations have be derived among elements of its steady states (if existing).

The above-discussed relations imply that the steady RDM should be commutable
with a renormalized Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{S}
of the central system,
which includes certain averaged impact of the system-environment interaction.
As a consequence, decoherence happens on the eigenbasis of the renormalized Hamiltonian,
even under a system-environment interaction that is dissipative for the original Hamiltonian
H^{S}
,
and leads to a PBS given by the eigenbasis of
\widetilde{H}^{S}
.
This enriches analytical knowledge about PBS for systems under nonweak and dissipative
system-environment interactions, which had been previously observed numerically in some specific models
(see, e.g., Ref.
Wang et al. 2012
).

8
8

8

We would note a difference between the type of models studied in this paper and
the spin-boson models used in
Refs.
Lee et al. 2012
;
Addis et al. 2014
;
Roszak et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2015
;
Guarnieri et al. 2018
.
That is, in a spin-boson model, the environment (the bosons) is not a quantum
chaotic system and the ETH ansatz is usually inapplicable.
Due to this difference, even if a PBS may exist in a spin-boson model,
the mechanism should be quite different from that discussed in this paper.

Moreover, results of this paper give an explicit way of constructing renormalized Hamiltonian for PBS.

In fact, renormalized Hamiltonian is also used in a standard master-equation approach to RDM.
There, at an initial stage before the derivation begins,
the self-Hamiltonian of the central system is taken as certain renormalized Hamiltonian,
which we indicate as
H^{S}_{\rm mas}
with “mas” standing for “master equation”, given by

H^{S}_{\rm mas}=H^{S}+\lambda H^{IS}\text{tr}(H^{I\mathcal{E}}\rho^{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm th})
, where
\rho^{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm th}

denotes a thermal state of the environment.
Under the ETH ansatz and the condition in Eq.(
42
),

H^{S}_{\rm mas}
has almost the same expression as
\widetilde{H}^{S}
in Eq.(
50
),
if the state
\rho^{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm th}
lies effectively within the energy shell
\Gamma^{\mathcal{E}}
.

However, there is a big difference between the physical meanings of
H^{S}_{\rm mas}
and
\widetilde{H}^{S}
.
In fact, in our approach, the operator
\widetilde{H}^{S}
is derived by faithfully
taking the long-time average over the overall Schrödinger evolution;
and it indicates the existence of a PBS, if the RDM may approach a steady state.
While, in the master-equation approach,
the operator
H^{S}_{\rm mas}
is mainly employed for the sake of convenience in derivation,
though with deep physical intuition lying behind it.
Only after a certain type of analytical solution to a derived master equation is found,
which is usually a hard task except in some special models,
could it become clear whether
H^{S}_{\rm mas}
may indeed be of relevance to a PBS.
Moreover, as an approach based a perturbative treatment,
validity of the master-equation approach at long times is a subtle issue.

Finally, we would mention that,
beside the field of decoherence, results of this paper may also be useful in other fields
in which properties of steady states of small and open quantum systems are of relevance,
such as quantum thermodynamics

Binder et al. 2019
;
Goold et al. 2016
;
Wang 2012
;
Wang 2018
.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq.(
22
)

In this appendix, we derive Eq.(
22
).
Using Eq.(
21
), the time evolution of the RDM is written as

i\hbar\frac{d\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)}{dt}=i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=A_{1}+A_{2},

(80)

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=i\hbar\left(\frac{d}{dt}\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|\right)|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle,

(81a)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=i\hbar\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|\left(\frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle\right).

(81b)

Making use of Eq.(
19
), one finds that

\displaystyle A_{1}=-\sum_{\gamma}\langle\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}(t)|H_{\gamma\beta}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(82)

From Eqs.(
7
) and (
20
), one gets that

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}=\langle\alpha|H|\beta\rangle=e^{S}_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\lambda H^{IS}_{\alpha\beta}H^{I\mathcal{E}}+H^{\mathcal{E}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(83)

Then, we write Eq.(
82
) as,

\displaystyle A_{1}=

\displaystyle-e_{\beta}^{S}\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle-\lambda\sum_{\gamma}H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}\langle\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}(t)|H^{I\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|H^{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(84)

Noting Eqs.(
21
) and (
24
), the above equality gives that

\displaystyle A_{1}=-e_{\beta}^{S}\rho^{S}_{\alpha\beta}(t)-\lambda\sum_{\gamma}H^{IS}_{\gamma\beta}F_{\gamma\alpha}(t)-\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|H^{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(85)

Similarly, one finds

A_{2}=e_{\alpha}^{S}\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{S}(t)+\lambda\sum_{\gamma}H^{IS}_{\alpha\gamma}F_{\beta\gamma}(t)+\langle\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(t)|H^{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(86)

Putting the above results together, one gets Eq.(
22
).

Appendix B
Scaling of the fluctuation operator

In this appendix, we show that the main
N
-scaling behavior of the fluctuation operator
\Delta
,

\Delta=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|,

(87)

is an exponential decay with increasing
N
.
For this purpose, let us compute the Frobenius norm of
\Delta
,

\displaystyle||\Delta||_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}.

(88)

Making use of Eq.(
41
), direct derivation shows that

\displaystyle||\Delta||_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\left|\sum_{ij}g(e,\omega)e^{-S(e)/2}\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}R_{ij}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{iji^{\prime}j^{\prime}}g(e,\omega)e^{-S(e)/2}\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}R_{ij}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times{g}^{*}(e^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})e^{-S(e^{\prime})/2}\overline{f_{\beta i^{\prime}}f_{\alpha j^{\prime}}^{*}}\ {R}^{*}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}.

(89)

Note that
g(e,\omega)={g}^{*}(e,-\omega)
and
R_{ij}={R}^{*}_{ji}
.
To proceed, let us discuss the statistical average of
||\Delta||_{F}^{2}
,
taken over the random variables
R_{ij}
, which is indicate by

\langle\cdot\rangle
.
This averaging procedure results in that
Murthy and Srednicki 2019
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016

\displaystyle\langle R_{ij}{R}^{*}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}+\delta_{ij^{\prime}}\delta_{i^{\prime}j},

(90)

and, as a consequence,

\displaystyle\langle||\Delta||_{F}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}|g(e,\omega)|^{2}e^{-S(e)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\left(\sum_{\alpha\beta}\big|\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\big|^{2}+\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\cdot\overline{f_{\beta j}f_{\alpha i}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{ij}|g(e,\omega)|^{2}e^{-S(e)}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\big|\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{ij}(|g(e,\omega)|^{2}e^{-S(e)})\sum_{ij}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\big|\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\big|^{2}.

(91)

To compute
\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}
, we make use of the fact that

f_{\alpha j}=\langle\alpha j|\Psi(t)\rangle
.
This gives that

\displaystyle\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}=\overline{\langle\Psi(t)|\beta i\rangle\langle\alpha j|\Psi(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{mn}\langle\Psi(0)|n\rangle\langle n|\beta i\rangle\overline{e^{i(E_{n}-E_{m})t}}\langle\alpha j|m\rangle\langle m|\Psi(0)\rangle.

(92)

Note that the environment
\mathcal{E}
, as a quantum chaotic system, has a nondegenerate spectrum.
Under a generic system-environment interaction, the spectrum of the total system is nondegenerate, too.
Then, one has
\overline{e^{i(E_{n}-E_{m})t}}=\delta_{mn}
and, as a result,

\displaystyle\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}=\sum_{n}\langle\Psi(0)|n\rangle\langle n|\beta i\rangle\langle\alpha j|n\rangle\langle n|\Psi(0)\rangle.

(93)

This gives that

\displaystyle\left|\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{nm}\langle\Psi(0)|n\rangle\langle n|\beta i\rangle\langle\beta i|m\rangle\langle m|\Psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\times\langle\Psi(0)|m\rangle\langle m|\alpha j\rangle\langle\alpha j|n\rangle\langle n|\Psi(0)\rangle.

(94)

Then, making use of the completeness of the basis of
|\alpha\rangle
and that of
|i\rangle
, one gets that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta ij}\left|\overline{f_{\beta i}^{*}f_{\alpha j}}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{L_{0}},

(95)

where
L_{0}
is the so-called participation function of the initial state
|\Psi(0)\rangle
, defined by

\displaystyle L_{0}=\frac{1}{\sum_{n}\left|\langle\Psi(0)|n\rangle\right|^{4}}.

(96)

As is known,
L_{0}
gives a measure to the localization length, i.e., to the number of those levels
E_{n}

that are effectively occupied by the state
|\Psi(0)\rangle
.
For a large environment and an initial shell not extremely narrow, the value of
L_{0}
is large.

Substituting Eq.(
95
) into Eq.(
91
),
we get an upper bound to the averaged norm
\langle||\Delta||_{F}^{2}\rangle
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle||\Delta||_{F}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{L_{0}}\max_{ij}|g(e,\omega)|^{2}e^{-S(e)}\sim\frac{2N^{2\gamma}}{L_{0}}e^{-S(e)}.

(97)

Since the averaging procedure does not change the
N
-scaling behavior of the norm
||\Delta||_{F}^{2}

and the exponential-decay term
e^{-S(e)/2}
already exists in the
exact expression of
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}
in Eq.(
41
), from Eq.(
97
)
one sees that the
N
-scaling behavior of fluctuation operator
\Delta
should be
dominated by the exponential decay
e^{-S(e)/2}
.
