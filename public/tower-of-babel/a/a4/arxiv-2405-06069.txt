Sufficient conditions for total positivity, compounds, and Dodgson condensation
Shaun Fallat, Himanshu Gupta, Charles R. Johnson

Abstract
. A n n -by- n n matrix is called totally positive ( T ​ P TP ) if all its minors are positive and T ​ P k TP_{k} if all of its k k -by- k k submatrices are T ​ P TP . For an arbitrary totally positive matrix or T ​ P k TP_{k} matrix, we investigate if the r r th compound ( 1 < r < n 1<r<n ) is in turn T ​ P TP or T ​ P k TP_{k} , and demonstrate a strong negative resolution in general. Focus is then shifted to Dodgson’s algorithm for calculating the determinant of a generic matrix, and we analyze whether the associated condensed matrices are possibly totally positive or T ​ P k TP_{k} . We also show that all condensed matrices associated with a T ​ P TP Hankel matrix are T ​ P TP .

. Observe that

Let

.

If

, the

By definition if

.

Let

Another important set of matrices associated with a matrix are its Dodgson condensations. The Dodgson condensation is a method developed by
C. Dodgson (aka. Lewis Carol)
[
7
]
to compute the determinant of a square matrix. The set of matrices derived from this method are called Dodgson condensation matrices. A key ingredient of this technique is the well-known Sylvester’s determinantal identity.

Theorem 1.3

(Sylvester’s determinantal identity
[
15
]
)
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\alpha\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and suppose
|\alpha|=k
. Define the
(n-k)
-by-
(n-k)

matrix

, with

b_{i,j}={\rm det}A[\alpha\cup\{i\},\alpha\cup\{j\}]
, for every
i,j\in\alpha^{c}
. Then, for each

\delta,\gamma\subset\alpha^{c}
, with
|\delta|=|\gamma|=l
,

(1.1)

{\rm det}B[\delta,\gamma]=({\rm det}A[\alpha])^{l-1}{\rm det}A[\alpha\cup\delta,\alpha\cup\gamma].

Observe that a special case of (
1.1
) is that
{\rm det}B=({\rm det}A[\alpha])^{n-k-1}{\rm det}A
. Another very useful special case is the following. Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the partitioned matrix

\displaystyle A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{11}&a_{12}^{T}&a_{13}\\
a_{21}&A_{22}&a_{23}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}^{T}&a_{33}\end{array}\right],

where
A_{22}\in M_{n-2}(\mathbb{R})
,
a_{12},a_{21},a_{23},a_{32}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, and

a_{11},a_{13},a_{31},a_{33}
are scalars. Define the matrices

\displaystyle B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}^{T}\\
a_{21}&A_{22}\end{array}\right],\ C=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{12}^{T}&a_{13}\\
A_{22}&a_{23}\end{array}\right],\ D=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{21}&A_{22}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}^{T}\end{array}\right],\text{ and }E=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{22}&a_{23}\\
a_{32}^{T}&a_{33}\end{array}\right].

If we let
\tilde{b}={\rm det}B
,
\tilde{c}={\rm det}C
,

\tilde{d}={\rm det}D
, and
\tilde{e}={\rm det}E
, then by
(
1.1
) it follows that

{\rm det}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{b}&\tilde{c}\\
\tilde{d}&\tilde{e}\end{array}\right]={\rm det}A_{22}{\rm det}A.

Hence, provided
{\rm det}A_{22}\neq 0
, we have

{\rm det}A=\frac{{\rm det}B{\rm det}E-{\rm det}C{\rm det}D}{{\rm det}A_{22}}.

Dodgson’s condensation produces a sequence of matrices
D_{i}(A)\in M_{n-i}(\mathbb{R})
(
1\leq i\leq n-1
) from a given matrix
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
to which a recursive step is implemented resulting in a scalar whose value equal to
\det A
(see
[
7
]
). This algorithm can be described as follows (see also
[
1
]
): Given
A=[a_{i,j}]\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,

Step 1:

Construct a matrix
D_{1}(A)=[M^{(1)}_{i,j}]\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
, where for

i,j=1,2,\ldots,n-1
,

M^{(1)}_{i,j}=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i,j}&a_{i,j+1}\\
a_{i+1,j}&a_{i+1,j+1}\end{array}\right];

Step 2:

Using both
A
and
D_{1}(A)
, construct another matrix
D_{2}(A)=[M^{(2)}_{i,j}]\in M_{n-2}(A)
, where for

i,j=1,2,\ldots,n-2
,

M^{(2)}_{i,j}=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M^{(1)}_{i,j}&M^{(1)}_{i,j+1}\\
M^{(1)}_{i+1,j}&M^{(1)}_{i+1,j+1}\end{array}\right]/a_{i+1,j+1};

Step 3:

Replace
A
with
D_{1}(A)
,
D_{1}(A)
with
D_{2}(A)
, and repeat Step 2, until a scalar is produced.

The above algorithm is a simple mechanism for computing the determinant of a matrix. We refer to a matrix
D_{k}(A)
as a
Dodgson condensation matrix
. Applying Sylvester’s identity (Theorem
1.3
) and following the discussion afterwards, it can be seen that the entries of
D_{2}(A)
are in fact equal to

M_{i,j}^{(2)}=\det A[\{i,i+1,i+2\},\{j,j+1,j+2\}].

In other words, the entries of
D_{2}(A)
are precisely all of the contiguous minors of order
3
of
A
. In fact, by using the Sylvester’s identity repeatedly, the above claim holds more generally as follows.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and let
D_{k}(A)=[M_{i,j}^{(k)}]
. Then for any
i,j=1,2,\ldots,n-k
we have

M_{i,j}^{(k)}=\det A[\{i,\ldots,i+k\},\{j,\ldots,j+k\}].

Hence, the matrix
D_{k}(A)
consists of all contiguous minors of order
k+1
of
A
. So if
A
is
TP_{k+1}
, then
D_{k}(A)
is
TP_{1}
for any
1\leq k\leq n-1
. In fact, we can say more about the total positivity of a Dodgson condensation matrix. That is our second main result.

Theorem B
.

Let
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Then the following statements hold:

(1)

If
A
is
TP_{k+2}
, then
D_{k}(A)
is
TP_{2}
.

(2)

If
A
is
TP_{k+3}
, then
D_{k}(A)
is
TP_{3}
.

We demonstrate that the previous result can not be improved for others values of

(see Example

). However, we prove a much broader result for a class of matrices known as Hankel matrices. For a given sequence of scalars
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{2n}
, the

(n+1)

\displaystyle A=(a_{i+j})_{i,j=0}^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&\ldots&a_{n}\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{n+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

Theorem C
.

Let
A
be a Hankel matrix based on the sequence of scalars

. If

is
TP
, then

is a Hankel and

An important goal in the study of totally positive matrices is to find out sufficient conditions to verify the total positivity of a matrix. A matrix

We desire to determine if we can learn anything about whether a matrix

is totally positive by looking at the total positivity of compounds or Dodgson condensation matrices. Suppose a matrix

. If

Let

be

for some

Since

Corollary 1.6

Let

be

for some

is

, then

TP_{k+2}
.

\displaystyle=-g-j-\ell<0,

\displaystyle\det(S[\{1,2,3\},\{2,3,4\}])

\displaystyle=-gh-gk-jk<0,

\displaystyle\det(S[\{1,2,4\},\{1,3,4\}])

\displaystyle=i>0,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\det(S[\{1,2,4\},\{2,3,4\}])

\displaystyle=ik>0,

and

\displaystyle\det(S[\{1,3,4\},\{1,2,3\}])

\displaystyle=-a-c-f<0,

\displaystyle\det(S[\{2,3,4\},\{1,2,3\}])

\displaystyle=-bc-bf-ef<0,

\displaystyle\det(S[\{1,3,4\},\{1,2,4\}])

\displaystyle=d>0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\det(S[\{2,3,4\},\{1,2,4\}])

\displaystyle=bd>0.

This implies that
S
is not
TP_{3}
, and hence,
C_{n-2}(A)
is not
TP_{3}
. Now, we prove the statement for
2\leq k\leq n-3
. So assume
A
is
TP_{k+2}
and consider any submatrix

of
A
of order
k+2
. Notice that,
B
is
TP
, and
C_{k}(B)
is a submatrix of
C_{k}(A)
. By the previous case
C_{k}(B)
is not
TP_{3}
, and hence
C_{k}(A)
is not
TP_{3}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Recall that in the definition of compound matrix
C_{k}(A)
we use lexicographic ordering of subsets. One may wonder whether using a different ordering of subsets could alter the result of Theorem
A
. Unfortunately, that is not the case, since in any ordering at least one of the minors from (
3.1
) is negative.

Indeed, any permutation of rows and columns, simultaneously, of the matrix
C_{n-2}(A)
induces a permutation on the rows and columns of the matrix
S
. Considering all
4!
permutations and using the fact that an odd permutation changes the sign of a determinant while an even permutation does not, we can conclude that in any permutation at least one of the minors from (
3.1
) is negative.

We provide an example which shows that the result of Theorem

The following theorem, which can be found in

[

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

be a
TP

A^{\prime}
is also a

Hankel matrix (we use the same notation as defined in Theorem

, the statement follows for

. Next, we assume

. It is easy to check that
