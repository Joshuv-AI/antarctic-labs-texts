REPETITIONS OF MULTINOMIAL COEFFICIENTS AND A GENERALIZATION OF SINGMASTER’S CONJECTURE
Jean-Marie de Koninck

Abstract




Abstract

Given two integers
k\geq 2
and
a>1
, let
N_{k}(a)
stand for the number of multinomial coefficients, with
k
terms, equal to
a
.
We
study the behavior of
N_{k}(a)
and show that its average and normal orders are equal to
k(k-1)
. We also prove that
N_{k}(a)=O\left((\log a/\log\log a)^{k-1}\right)
and make several propositions about extreme results regarding large values of
N_{k}(a)
.

1
Introduction

Let
N(a)
denote the number of times the integer
a>1
occurs as a binomial coefficient, that is,
\displaystyle N(a)=\#\left\{(n,r)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}:\binom{n}{r}=a\right\}
. Singmaster
[
7
]
conjectured that
N(a)=O(1)
and proved that
N(a)=O(\log a)
. Abbott, Erdős, and Hanson
[
1
]
later showed that
\displaystyle N(a)=O\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)
and that its average and normal orders are 2. Finally, Kane
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
improved the bounds to
\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\log a\log\log\log a}{(\log\log a)^{2}}\right)
and
\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\log a\log\log\log a}{(\log\log a)^{3}}\right)
, respectively.

Considering the properties of Pascal’s triangle, one can reformulate the problem as follows. Is there an upper bound for the number of times a given integer appears in Pascal’s triangle?

Despite the fact that infinitely many positive integers appear at least six times in Pascal’s triangle
[
8
]
, only nine such integers, namely

1,120,210,1540,3003,7140,11\,628,24\,310\text{ and }61218182743304701891431482520,

have been found to satisfy this property up to
10^{60}

[
6
]
. It is worth noting that
N(3003)=8
, and that this is the highest known value of
N(a)
. Singmaster
[
8
]
actually conjectured that its maximal value might be
8,10
or
12
.

In what follows, we study repetitions of multinomial coefficients, that is, for a fixed integer
k\geq 2
, those numbers

\displaystyle\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}=\frac{m!}{m_{1}!\cdots m_{k}!}
, where
\displaystyle m=\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}
.

Given an integer
k\geq 2
, let
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
, and let

N_{k}(a)=\#\left\{\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}:\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}=a\right\}

denote the number of times the integer
a>1
occurs as a multinomial coefficient. As such,
N_{k}(a)
is a generalized version of
N(a)
where we consider multinomial coefficients of
k
terms equal to
a
, and in particular,
N(a)=N_{2}(a)
.

If one considers Pascal’s
k
-simplices as a generalization of Pascal’s triangle in dimension
k
, where the entries of the simplex are given by multinomial coefficients of
k
terms, then the problem is equivalent to asking whether there is an upper bound on the number of times an integer appears in Pascal’s
k
-simplex for a given
k\geq 2
.

A simple program tells us that in the first
1000
lines of Pascal’s triangle,
494
integers appear once,
248\,861
twice,
5
three times,
63
four times and
3
six times. In comparison, Pascal’s
3
-simplex, often called Pascal’s pyramid, has
445\,666
distinct integers in its first
250
layers, of which
429\,135
appear exactly six times. These results were to be expected, as we will see.

Obviously, it may be the case that for any fixed value of
k\geq 2
,
N_{k}(a)
is bounded. We will also pursue this investigation for large values of
k
.

2
Main results

We start by showing that the average and normal orders of
N_{k}(a)
are both
k(k-1)
. Intuitively, the average order of
N_{k}(a)
is another arithmetic function which takes the same values on average, while its normal order is an arithmetic function which is close to
N_{k}(a)
for almost all values of
a
. Consequently, our first theorem implies that for any given integer
a>1
, there are almost always
k(k-1)
multinomial coefficients with
k
terms equal to
a
. An immediate corollary is that almost always one can find any given integer a total of
k(k-1)
times in Pascal’s
k
-simplex.

Remark 1
.

A multinomial coefficient of the form
\displaystyle\binom{a}{a-1,1,0,\ldots,0}
is always equal to
a
. As such, one gets
\displaystyle N_{k}(a)\geq k(k-1)
for all
k\geq 2
. Now notice that
a=1
and
a=2
are the only exceptions to this, and while
1
appears infinitely many times, 2 occurs
\displaystyle\binom{k}{2}
times as a multinomial coefficient, for it must be of the form
\displaystyle\binom{2}{1,1,0,\ldots,0}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer and
N_{k}(a)
be defined as above. Then, the average and normal orders of
N_{k}(a)
are both
k(k-1)
.

Proof.

Let
M
be a positive integer. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(k,M)

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{m}:2<\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\leq M\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,M)

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}(k,M):m_{i}=m-1\text{ for some }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(k,M)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(k,M)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,M).

Following Remark
1
, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{1<a\leq M}N_{k}(a)

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{2}\>+\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}(k,M)}1

(1)

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{2}\>+\>\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,M)}1\quad+\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(k,M)}1.

Now, since

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,M)}1

\displaystyle=k(k-1)\sum_{2<\binom{m}{1,0,\ldots,0,m-1}\leq M}1

(2)

\displaystyle=k(k-1)\sum_{2<m\leq M}1

\displaystyle=k(k-1)(M-2),

it only remains to consider
\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(k,M)}1
.

If
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(k,M)
, then

\displaystyle 2<\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\leq M
and
m_{i}<m-1
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Assume, without loss of generality, that
m_{i}\leq m_{i+1}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k-1
.
Then we have the inequalities

M\geq\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\geq\frac{m!}{m_{j}!(m-m_{j})!}\geq\binom{2m_{j}}{m_{j}}\geq 2^{m_{j}}\quad(j=1,2,\ldots,k-1)

(3)

and

M\geq\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\geq\frac{m!}{m_{k}!(m-m_{k})!}\geq\binom{m}{2}=\frac{m(m-1)}{2},

which implies that
m_{j}=O(\log M)
and
m=O(M^{1/2})
, respectively.

Since the value of
m_{k}
is entirely determined by that of
m,m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k-1}
, we get

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(k,M)}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2<\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\leq M\\
0\leq m_{i}<m-1\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{i}\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\sum_{m}1=O\left(M^{1/2}\left(\log M\right)^{k-1}\right).

(4)

Using (
2
) and (
4
) in (
1
), we obtain

\sum_{1<a\leq M}N_{k}(a)=\binom{k}{2}+k(k-1)(M-2)+O\left(M^{1/2}\left(\log M\right)^{k-1}\right),

(5)

and therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{M}

\displaystyle\sum_{1<a\leq M}N_{k}(a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{M}\left(\binom{k}{2}+k(k-1)(M-2)+O(M^{1/2}(\log M)^{k-1})\right)

\displaystyle=k(k-1),

which provides the average order of
N_{k}(a)
.

For the normal order, let

\displaystyle f_{k}(M)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{1<a\leq M:N_{k}(a)<k(k-1)\right\},

\displaystyle g_{k}(M)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{1<a\leq M:N_{k}(a)=k(k-1)\right\},

\displaystyle h_{k}(M)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{1<a\leq M:N_{k}(a)>k(k-1)\right\}.

We wish to prove that
\displaystyle N_{k}(a)-k(k-1)\leq\varepsilon k(k-1)
for all
\varepsilon>0
and for almost all
a
. It should be clear that this inequality holds only if
N_{k}(a)=k(k-1)
, for
N_{k}(a)
only takes integral values. As such, it suffices to show that
f_{k}(M)+h_{k}(M)=o(M)
as
M\to\infty
.

Now Remark
1
implies that
f_{k}(M)=1
. Also,
g_{k}(M)+h_{k}(M)=M-2
since they only exclude
a=1
and
a=2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{1<a\leq M}N_{k}(a)

\displaystyle\geq f_{k}(M)+k(k-1)g_{k}(M)+(k(k-1)+1)h_{k}(M)

\displaystyle=1+k(k-1)(g_{k}(M)+h_{k}(M))+h_{k}(M)

\displaystyle=1+k(k-1)(M-2)+h_{k}(M).

(6)

Comparing (
6
) with (
5
), we get that
h_{k}(M)=O\left(M^{1/2}(\log M)^{k-1}\right)
, which means that
f_{k}(M)+h_{k}(M)=o(M)
as
M\to\infty
and in turn implies that the normal order of
N_{k}(a)
is indeed
k(k-1)
. ∎

We also state an additional result, even though it is weaker than our claim

N_{k}(a)=O\left(\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)^{k-1}\right),

be it simply for the fact that our proof is both more general and simpler than that of Singmaster in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Then
\displaystyle N_{k}(a)=O\left(\log^{k-1}a\right).

Proof.

Since

\frac{\binom{m+1}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{j}+1,\ldots,m_{k}}}{\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}=\frac{\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}{\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}=\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\geq 1,

the multinomial coefficients are strictly increasing in
m_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, which implies
that if we fix
k-1
parameters in the multinomial coefficient, say all but
m_{i}
, then

\#\left\{m_{i}:\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}=a\right\}\leq 1.

(7)

The proof is based on the observation that, for all
1\leq i\leq k
,

N_{k}(a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{j}\\
j\>\in\>\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus\{i\}\end{subarray}}\#\left\{m_{i}:\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}=a\right\},

so that in particular,

N_{k}(a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{j}\\
1\leq j\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\#\left\{m_{k}:\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}=a\right\}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{j}\\
1\leq j\leq k-1\end{subarray}}1=O(\log^{k-1}a),

where we used (
7
) and (
3
).
∎

Remark 2
.

One may be tempted to write
N_{k}(a)
as a sum over less than
k-1
fixed parameters, but then it becomes quite a tedious task to determine the number of multinomial coefficients equal to
a
. This is why we should be satisfied with this bound for now.

Next, we generalize a result of Abbott, Erdős, and Hanson
[
1
]
, obtaining a better upper bound for
N_{k}(a)
. We will need two lemmas.

Lemma 1

(Baker, Harman, and Pintz
[
2
]
)
.

There exists a real number
x_{0}
such that the interval
\displaystyle\left[x,x+x^{0.525}\right]
contains at least one prime number for all
x>x_{0}
.

Lemma 2
.

For each
j=1,2,\ldots,k
, we have
\displaystyle\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}\geq\left(\frac{m}{m_{j}}\right)^{m_{j}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m_{j}
.
At first, if
m_{j}=1
, then,

\displaystyle\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}

\displaystyle=\binom{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{j-1}+1+m_{j+1}+\cdots+m_{k}}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1},1,m_{j+1}\ldots,m_{k}}

\displaystyle=(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{j-1}+1+m_{j+1}+\cdots+m_{k})\binom{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{j-1}+m_{j+1}+\cdots+m_{k}}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{j-1},m_{j+1},\ldots,m_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{j-1}+1+m_{j+1}+\cdots+m_{k}).

Now,

\displaystyle\binom{m+1}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{j}+1,\ldots,m_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\binom{m}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\left(\frac{m}{m_{j}}\right)^{m_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\left(\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\right)^{m_{j}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m+1}{m_{j}+1}\right)^{m_{j}+1},

where the second to last inequality holds since
m\geq m_{j}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Then,
\displaystyle N_{k}(a)=O\left(\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)^{k-1}\right).

Proof.

We let
m_{i}\leq m_{i+1}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k-1
without loss of generality.

Let

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in N_{k}(a):m>\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a\right\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in N_{k}(a):m\leq\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a\right\},

so that
N_{k}(a)=\#A+\#B
.
Hence, it is sufficient to show that
\#A
and
\#B
are both

\displaystyle O\left(\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)^{k-1}\right).

First, assume
\mathbf{m}
is in
A
, so that

m>\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a.

(8)

Notice that in (
3
), we have not used the fact that
m_{i}<m-1
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Thus, we can write

m_{i}\leq\frac{\log a}{\log 2}\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,k-1).

(9)

Using Lemma
2
, (
8
) and (
9
), we get

m_{i}\leq\frac{\log a}{\log\frac{m}{m_{i}}}<\frac{\log a}{\log\frac{\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a}{\log a/\log 2}}=O\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,k-1).

By monotonicity, for fixed
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k-1}
, there is only one
m_{k}
that yields a multinomial coefficient equal to
a
. Hence,

\#A=O\left(\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)^{k-1}\right).

(10)

Secondly, assume
\mathbf{m}
is in
B
. Let
M=M_{1}+M_{2}+\cdots+M_{k}
be the greatest element for which the associated
\mathbf{M}=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{k})
lies in
B
. We also define
S=M-M_{1}
.
Then,

a=\frac{M!}{M_{1}!\cdots M_{k}!}<\frac{M!}{(M-S)!}=M(M-1)\cdots(M-S+1)<M^{S}.

From
\displaystyle M\leq\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a
and
a<M^{S}
, we get

M<(S\log M)^{\frac{22}{21}}.

Note that

\log^{\frac{22}{21}}M\leq S^{\frac{18}{21}}\text{ for all }M\geq 2.

Since
k\geq 2
and
M_{1}
is maximal when all parameters are equal, it is sufficient to show that the result holds for
M_{1}=M/2
. It is easy to verify that
\log^{\frac{22}{21}}M\leq(M/2)^{\frac{18}{21}}
for all
M\geq 2
.

Therefore,
\displaystyle M\leq S^{22/21}S^{18/21}<S^{40/21}+S
or, equivalently,
\displaystyle(M-S)^{21/40}<S
.
Adding
M-S
on both sides, we finally have

\displaystyle(M-S)^{21/40}+(M-S)<M,

that is,

M_{1}+M_{1}^{21/40}<M.

(11)

Using Lemma
1
, there exists a prime in
\displaystyle\left[M_{1},M_{1}+M_{1}^{\frac{21}{40}}\right]
. Combining the latter with (
11
), there is a largest prime P satisfying

M_{1}\leq P<M,

which tells us that
P
divides our fixed
a
, so the same can be said of any
m
for which the associated
\mathbf{m}
is in
B
. Thus,
P\leq m\leq M
whenever
m\leq\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a
.
As such, there are at most
M-P
elements in
B
.

Next, let
x=P
in Lemma
1
and let
Q
be the largest prime in the interval
\displaystyle\left[P,P+P^{\frac{21}{40}}\right]
. Then, since
P
is the largest prime less than
M
, we must have

P<M\leq Q\leq P+P^{21/40},

and so it follows that

\#B\leq M-P\leq P^{\frac{21}{40}}<M^{\frac{21}{40}}\leq(\log^{\frac{22}{21}}a)^{\frac{21}{40}}=\log^{\frac{11}{20}}a=O\left(\left(\frac{\log a}{\log\log a}\right)^{k-1}\right),

which, combined with (
10
), yields the desired result.
∎

We end this paper by stating some partial results regarding large values of
N_{k}(a)
. We will see that we can do much better than the naive bound
N_{k}(a)\geq k(k-1)
for all
k\geq 2
.

Proposition 2
.

For any integer
k>2
,

\#\{a\leq x:N_{k}(a)\geq k!\}\gg x^{\frac{2}{(k-1)(k-2)}}.

Proof.

We will force
a
to take the form of a multinomial coefficient by counting the integers
a
that can be written as

a=\frac{(m+(k-1)(k-2)/2)!}{0!1!\cdots(k-2)!m!}\leq x,

(12)

which obviously has at least
k!
representations as a multinomial coefficient once
x
is big enough, by permuting the terms in the denominator.
We have

\frac{(m+(k-1)(k-2)/2)!}{0!1!\cdots(k-2)!m!}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}\frac{1}{j!}\prod_{j=1}^{(k-1)(k-2)/2}(m+j)\sim C_{k}m^{\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}}

as
m\to\infty
,
where

C_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}\frac{1}{j!}.

It follows that for
x
large enough, (
12
) holds as long as

(1+\varepsilon)C_{k}m^{(k-1)(k-2)/2}\leq x

for any given
\varepsilon>0
, that is

m\leq\frac{1}{((1+\varepsilon)C_{k})^{\frac{2}{(k-1)(k-2)}}}x^{\frac{2}{(k-1)(k-2)}},

which proves our claim.
∎

Proposition 3
.

For each
k\geq 4
,

\#\{a:N_{k}(a)\geq 2\cdot k!+k(k-1)\}=\infty.

Note that this proposition also works for
k=2
since infinitely many positive integers appear at least six times in Pascal’s triangle.

Proof.

Our proof follows from the simple identity

12!2!1!=11!4!0!,

(13)

from which we have, for any
m\geq 15
,

a(m):=\frac{m!}{12!2!1!(m-15)!}=\frac{m!}{11!4!0!(m-15)!}.

If
m\geq 28
, all the integers appearing in the denominator of the above equation are distinct, thus providing
2\cdot 4!=48
distinct vectors. To obtain the remaining
4\cdot 3=12
vectors, we just have to consider the expression

\frac{a(m)!}{(a(m)-1)!1!0!0!},

thus completing the proof of the proposition when
k=4
.
This idea can easily be generalized for
k>4
by observing that

\frac{(m+m_{5}+m_{6}+\cdots+m_{k})!}{12!2!1!(m-15)!m_{5}!m_{6}!\cdots m_{k}!}=\frac{(m+m_{5}+m_{6}+\cdots m_{k})!}{11!4!0!(m-15)!m_{5}!m_{6}!\cdots m_{k}!}.\qed

Proposition 4
.

For each
k\geq 7
,

\#\{a:N_{k}(a)\geq 7\cdot k!/2+k(k-1)\}=\infty.

Proof.

First observe the identity

24!5!3!1!=23!6!4!0!.

(14)

From equations (
13
) and (
14
), we have

24!5!3!1!12!2!=23!6!4!0!12!2!=24!5!3!11!4!0!=23!6!4!0!11!4!0!,

from which we can deduce that for any
m\geq 47
,

\displaystyle\frac{m!}{24!12!5!3!2!1!(m-47)!}=\frac{m!}{23!12!6!4!2!0!(m-47)!}=\frac{m!}{24!11!5!4!3!0!(m-47)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{m!}{23!6!4!0!11!4!0!(m-47)!}.

By choosing
m\geq 72
, we ensure that all the integers appearing in the denominator are distinct, except
0
in the last denominator, which provides
7\cdot 7!/2=17\,640
solutions. To obtain the
7\cdot 6=42
remaining solutions, we set

a(m)=\frac{m!}{24!12!5!3!2!1!(m-47)!}

and consider the quantity

\frac{a(m)!}{(a(m)-1)!1!0!0!0!0!0!}.

This argument can be generalized for
k>7
, by observing that

\displaystyle\frac{(m+m_{8}+m_{9}+\cdots+m_{k})!}{24!12!5!3!2!1!(m-47)!m_{8}!m_{9}!\cdots m_{k}!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(m+m_{8}+m_{9}+\cdots+m_{k})!}{23!12!6!4!2!0!(m-47)!m_{8}!m_{9}!\cdots m_{k}!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(m+m_{8}+m+m_{9}+\cdots+m_{k})!}{24!11!5!4!3!0!(m-47)!m_{8}!m_{9}!\cdots m_{k}!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(m+m_{8}+m+m_{9}+\cdots+m_{k})!}{23!6!4!0!11!4!0!(m-47)!m_{8}!m_{9}!\cdots m_{k}!}.

From this we have that for
k\geq 7
,

\#\{a:N_{k}(a)\geq 7\cdot k!/2+k(k-1)\}=\infty.\qed

We also looked numerically for large values of
N_{3}(a)
and
N_{4}(a)
. With
a=2671465728531600
, we found that
N_{3}(a)\geq 30
and
N_{4}(a)\geq 180
. Recall that we expect
N_{3}(a)=6
and
N_{4}(a)=12
. The bound
N_{3}(a)\geq 30
comes from the fact that

a=\frac{37!}{7!11!9!}=\frac{38!}{19!11!8!}=\frac{39!}{19!14!6!}=\frac{40!}{19!16!5!}=\frac{a!}{(a-1)!1!0!}.

To prove the bound
N_{4}(a)\geq 180
, it suffices to observe that

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{37!}{17!11!9!0!}=\frac{38!}{19!11!8!0!}=\frac{39!}{19!14!6!0!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{40!}{19!16!5!0!}=\frac{40!}{20!16!3!1!}=\frac{39!}{21!13!4!1!}=\frac{38!}{22!10!4!2!}=\frac{a!}{(a-1)!1!0!0!}.

3
Concluding remarks

At this point, it seems natural to ask whether
N_{k}(a)=O(1)
for all
k\geq 2
. However, the problem seems just as hard as in the basic case of binomial coefficients. Obviously, as multinomial coefficients can be rewritten as a product of binomial coefficients, it would be particularly interesting to show that proving Singmaster’s conjecture is equivalent to proving its generalization in any dimension
k\geq 2

Our final remark is that there is still a lot we don’t know about repetitions of multinomial coefficients. For instance, whether
N_{2}(a)=5
or
N_{2}(a)=7
is even possible.
Furthermore, Singmaster

\binom{F_{2i+2}F_{2i+3}}{F_{2i}F_{2i+3}}=\binom{F_{2i+2}F_{2i+3}-1}{F_{2i}F_{2i+3}+1},

where
F_{n}

n

5
]
. This implies that infinitely many integers are going to appear at least six times in Pascal’s triangle, as stated in the Introduction.
