Large deviation principle for generalised multiple intersection local times of multidimensional Brownian motion
Andrey A. Dorogovtsev a and Naoufel Salhi b

Abstract
In this paper we consider examples of positive generalised Wiener functions and we establish a large deviation principle for the generalised multiple intersection local time of the multidimensional Brownian motion.



keywords

Brownian motion; Wiener space; Sobolev space; intersection local time; positive generalised Wiener function; large deviation principle

Introduction

In the recent work
[
8
]
the authors considered the double self-intersection local time of a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
w(t)=\big(w_{1}(t),\cdots,w_{d}(t)\big),\;0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,
in the case
d\geqslant 4
. Formally written as

\rho_{2}(u)=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t)-w(s)-u\big)dsdt

(1)

where
\Delta_{2}=\{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2}\,;\,s<t\}
,
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
\delta_{0}
denotes the Dirac delta function, the
2
-fold self-intersection local time is defined by

\rho_{2}(u)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Delta_{2}}p^{d}_{\varepsilon}\big(w(t)-w(s)-u\big)dsdt

where

p^{d}_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{d/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-\|z\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right),\;z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\varepsilon>0

(
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) and the limit is understood in the sense of the Sobolev space
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
where
\gamma<2-\frac{d}{2}
. We recall that, for any real number
\gamma
, the space
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
is defined as the completion of

\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n}I_{k}(f_{k})\in L^{2}\big(\Omega,\sigma(w),\mathbb{P}\big),\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}

with respect to the norm

\Big\|\sum_{k=0}^{n}I_{k}(f_{k})\Big\|_{2,\gamma}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)^{\gamma}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[I_{k}(f_{k})^{2}\big]\,.

Here
\sigma(w)
denotes the
\sigma
-field generated by the Brownian motion and
I_{k}(f_{k})
denotes the
k
-multiple Itô stochastic integral of the deterministic, symmetric and square integrable kernel
f_{k}:[0,1]^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
(see e.g.
[
11
]
). When the ”differentiablity index”
\gamma
is negative the Sobolev space
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
contains strictly the space
L^{2}\big(\Omega,\sigma(w),\mathbb{P}\big)
of square integrable Wiener random variables and its elements are called generalised Wiener functions. For more details about the Sobolev spaces
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
and the generalised Wiener functions we refer to
[
15
,
17
]
. Particularly,
\rho_{2}(u)
is a generalised Wiener function. Moreover, it is positive. We recall that a generalised Wiener function
\eta\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2,-\gamma}
(for some
\gamma>0
) is positive if for any Wiener test function
F\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
(we recall also that
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,-\gamma}
is the dual space of
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
) we have

F(\omega)\geqslant 0\;\mu-\text{a.e.}\,\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\;\Rightarrow\;(\eta,F)\geqslant 0.

Here
W_{0}^{d}=\left\{\omega:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\omega\text{ is continuous},\,\omega(0)=0\right\}
denotes the Wiener space which is equipped with the sigma-field
\mathcal{B}(W_{0}^{d})
generated by the supremum norm and by the Wiener measure
\mu
. The
2
-fold self-intersection local time
\rho_{2}(u)
is represented by a finite positive measure
\theta_{u}
on the classical Wiener space
(W_{0}^{d},\mathcal{B}(W_{0}^{d}),\mu)
, see Theorem
4.1
in Appendix.

In
[
9
]
(see also
[
2
]
) the authors considered the same formal expression (
1
) but at the origin, i.e.
u=0
, and for any dimension
d
, in the framework of white noise analysis. They proved that if
\rho_{2}(0)
is suitably renormalised then it can be considered as a generalised white noise functional. To do this the authors first introduce the Hilbert space

\big(L^{2}\big)=L^{2}\Big(S^{*}\big({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big),\mu_{d}\Big)

where
S^{*}\big({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)
is the topological dual of the Schwartz space
S\big({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)
and
\mu_{d}
is the white noise measure, see
[
13
]
.
Denote by
\Gamma(A)
the second quantization operator (see e.g.
[
5
]
), acting on
\big(L^{2}\big)
, of

(Af)_{i}(t)=\Big(-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+t^{2}+1\Big)f_{i}(t),\quad i=1,\cdots,d,\;(f_{1},\cdots,f_{d})\in S\big({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big).

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, denote by
(\mathcal{S})_{k}
the domain of
\Gamma\big(A^{k}\big)
. Now if we denote by
\big(\mathcal{S}\big)
the projective limit of the sequence of Hibert spaces
\{\big(\mathcal{S}\big)_{k}\}
then its topological dual
\big(\mathcal{S}\big)^{*}
is the space of generalised white noise functionals and we have a Gelfand triple

\big(\mathcal{S}\big)\subset\big(L^{2}\big)\subset\big(\mathcal{S}\big)^{*}.

The authors proved (see
[
9
, Proposition 1]
) that whenever
s\neq t
, the expression
\delta_{0}\big(w(t)-w(s)\big)
is a generalised white noise functional. Next they proved (see
[
9
, Theorem 2]
) that for any integer
N
such that
2N>d-2
the Bochner integral

L^{(2N)}=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}^{(2N)}(w(t)-w(s))dsdt,

where
\delta_{0}^{(2N)}(w(t)-w(s))
is the projection of
\delta_{0}(w(t)-w(s))
on the chaos of order
k\geqslant 2N
, is a generalised white noise functional.
L^{(2N)}
is then the renormalised 2-fold self intersection local time of the Brownian motion. But the renormalisation here leads to the loss of positivity and consequently to the loss of the representation by a positive measure.

Our aim is to establish a large deviation principle (LDP) for the measure
\theta_{u}
. For more details about large deviation theory we refer for example to
[
4
,
3
]
. In
[
3
]
, a large deviation result for the
k
-fold self intersection local time of
1
-dimensional Brownian motion, formally defined by

T_{k}=\int_{\Delta_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{0}(w(t_{j+1})-w(t_{j}))dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}\,.

(2)

was proved (here
k\geqslant 2
and
\Delta_{k}=\{(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k})\in[0,1]^{k}\,;\,t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}\}
). Precisely, if we denote by
L(1,x)
the local time of the Brownian motion, i.e. the density of the occupation measure defined on the real line by

\nu(A)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}_{A}(w(t))dt,\;A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}),

then
T_{k}
has the following representation :

T_{k}=\dfrac{1}{k!}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}L^{k}(1,x)dx.

In
[
3
, Theorem 4.2.1.]
the author proved that for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}},k\geqslant 2
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-\frac{2}{k-1}}\log{\mathbb{P}}\left\{k!T_{k}\geqslant t\right\}=-\dfrac{1}{4(k-1)}\left(\dfrac{k+1}{2}\right)^{\frac{3-k}{k-1}}B\left(\dfrac{1}{k-1},\dfrac{1}{2}\right),

where
B(.,.)
denotes the Beta function.

In the case of planar Brownian motion, a LDP was also established for the renormalised
2
-fold self intersection local time formally denoted

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t)-w(s)\big)dsdt-{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t)-w(s)\big)dsdt

(3)

Precisely,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be defined through ”triangular approximation” :

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(\beta(A_{k,n})-{\mathbb{E}}\beta(A_{k,n})\big)\Big)

where the series converges in
L^{2}
-sense and

A_{k,n}=\Big[\dfrac{2k}{2^{n+1}},\dfrac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}\Big]\times\Big[\dfrac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}},\dfrac{2k+2}{2^{n+1}}\Big]\quad\forall\;k,n\in{\mathbb{N}},

\beta(A)=L^{2}\text{-}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon}^{2}(w(t)-w(s))dsdt\quad\forall\;A\subset\Delta_{2}.

In
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.1.]
the author proved that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\log{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\mathcal{T}_{2}\geqslant t\right\}=-c,

where
c>0
is the best constant of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality :

\|f\|_{4}\leqslant c\sqrt{\|\nabla f\|_{2}}\sqrt{\|f\|_{2}},\quad f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}),\,\nabla f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}).

Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{4}
denote respectively the norms in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and
L^{4}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.

In both the two previous results, the LDP is established for ”usual” random variables, in contrast with the case of a Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
where
d\geqslant 4
. As we mentioned in the beginning of this introduction, the intersection local time
\rho_{2}(u)
becomes a positive generalised Wiener function and the representation by a measure will be the key result to investiagte a LDP.

Let us recall some terminology related to large deviation theory.

Definition 1.1
.

[
4
, Chapter 1.]

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a topological space and
\mathcal{B}
its Borel
\sigma
-field. A rate function is a lower semi-continuous mapping
I:\mathcal{X}\to[0,+\infty]
(such that for all
\alpha\in[0,+\infty)
the level set
\{x\,:\,I(x)\leqslant\alpha\}
is a closed subset of
\mathcal{X}
). A good rate function is a rate function for which all level sets are compact subsets of
\mathcal{X}
. The domain of
I
is the set of points in
\mathcal{X}
with finite rate
\mathcal{D}_{I}=\{x\,:\,I(x)<\infty\}
.

Definition 1.2
.

[
4
, Chapter 1.]

A family
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}
of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
satisfies a large deviation principle (LDP) with a rate function
I
if, for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
,

-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}I(x)\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(A)\leqslant\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(A)\leqslant-\inf_{x\in\overline{A}}I(x).

Here
A^{\circ}
and
\overline{A}
denote respectively the interior and the closure of
A
.

Roughly speaking this means that, for small
\varepsilon
,
\mu_{\varepsilon}(A)
”behaves like”
e^{-\frac{\inf_{x\in A}I(x)}{\varepsilon}}
.

The definition
1.2
is equivalent to the following statements :

1.

(Upper bound) For any closed set
F\subset\mathcal{X}

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(F)\leqslant-\inf_{x\in F}I(x).

2.

(Lower bound) For any open set
G\subset\mathcal{X}

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(G)\geqslant-\inf_{x\in G}I(x).

The paper is organised as follows. In section
2
we start by considering some examples of positive generalised Wiener functions. Some of such examples can be encountered in physics. Then we focus on two particular examples, the first one – denoted
\rho_{k}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1})\,
– is a generalisation of the Wiener function
\rho(u)
discussed in
[
8
]
, and the second one – denoted
\eta\,
– is related to winding number of Brownian motion. We prove that these two examples are indeed positive generalised Wiener functions, and consequently they can be represented by finite positive measures on the Wiener space. In order to achieve these results we prove that positive generalised Wiener functions can be obtained as products of other ”elementary” positive generalised Wiener functions. These elementary bricks are obtained as Dirac Delta functions of increments of the Brownian motion, i.e. of the form
\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))
. In Proposition
2.3
we show that these simple expressions represent some generalised Wiener functions. Then, in Proposition
2.4
we prove that the product of two generalised Wiener functions constructed from orthogonal subspaces of the underlying Hilbert space over which the noise is constructed is again a generalised Wiener function. These two results are the main results of section
2
. In addition to some other results related to the measures associated to
\rho_{k}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1})
and
\eta
, the last part of section
2
is also devoted to explicit expressions for the dual pairing between the generalised Wiener function
\eta
and some specific kinds of Wiener test functions. In section
3
we establish a LDP for the measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
associated to the positive generalised Wiener function
\rho_{k}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1})
. To do this we first proceed by establishing an integral expression of
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
in which the Wiener measure appears in a conditional form. This result is stated in Lemma
3.3
. Once this result is proved we are able to apply LDP for the Wiener measure, i.e. Schilder theorem. This enables us to prove a large deviation upper bound for the measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
in Theorem
3.5
. But we fail to obtain a lower bound when we replace closed sets by open sets. In this context our results are comparable to those of V.A. Kuznetsov concerning winding angle of planar Brownian motion, see
[
14
]
. Section
4
is an Appendix. In subsection
4.1
we recall some results about the construction of the measure
\theta_{u}
, its support and an associated integral formula. In section
2
, inspired from that case, we obtain similar results for the measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
(Theorems
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.8
). In subsection
4.2
, we recall the LDP for the Wiener measure i.e. Schilder theorem. Finally, subsection
4.3
contains the definition of finite absolute continuity of measures in Banach spaces and a result related to the measures associated with positive generalised Wiener functions. This notion is important to describe the relation between the measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
and the Wiener measure (Proposition
3.4
).

2
Examples of positive generalised Wiener functions

The self-intersection local time
\rho_{2}(u)
formally given by (
1
) is our first example of positive generalised Wiener functions. In polymer models it plays an important role. A polymer is a long molecule consisting of small blocks called monomers which are tied with chemical bonds. Examples of polymers include -but are not limited to- proteins, DNA, RNA which are natural polymers and polyethylene which is a synthetic polymer. The Edwards model represents polymers by trajectories of the Brownian motion. The Hamiltonian of this model is proportional to the self intersection local time
\rho_{2}(0)
:

H=-\beta\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t)-w(s)\big)dsdt.

Here the constant
\beta>0
is called
the strength of self repellence
, see
[
10
, Chapter 3.]
. Moreover, the positive generalised Wiener function
\rho_{2}(u)
can be encountered in constructive quantum field theory, see e.g.
[
18
]
.

In addition to (
1
) one can define several similar positive generalised Wiener functions. In
[
7
]
, the authors defined the following intersection local time

I^{x}=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(x_{2}(t_{2})-x_{1}(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}:=L^{2}\text{-}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Delta_{2}}p_{\varepsilon}^{2}\big(x_{2}(t_{2})-x_{1}(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(4)

where
x(t)=\big(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t)\big),\,t\geqslant 0
is a diffusion process in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
(
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are the projections of
x
on the plane) with generator of the form
L=\nabla\cdot(A\nabla)
. Here
A:{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{4\times 4}
is a matrix-valued function which is symmetric, smooth with bounded derivatives of all orders and such that there exists a constant
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for any
y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
,

\lambda\|y\|^{2}\leqslant\big(A(z)y,y\big)\leqslant\dfrac{\|y\|^{2}}{\lambda}\,.

The integral in (
4
) measures the intersections of the two
2
-dimensional curves
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Therefore, it can be associated with the winding number (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 3.]
) of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. The winding number of two planar curves is the number of times that one curve winds around the other. It can be seen as the linking number (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 3.]
) of the two
3
-dimensional curves
t\mapsto\big(x_{j}(t),t\big),\,j=1,2
. The linking number of two
3
-dimensional disjoint closed curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
can be defined by the Gauss integral (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 3.]
)

lk\big(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\big)=\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\big(\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t_{1}),\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(t_{2}),\gamma_{1}(t_{1})-\gamma_{2}(t_{2})\big)}{\|\gamma_{2}(t_{2})-\gamma_{1}(t_{1})\|^{3}}dt_{1}dt_{2}

(5)

where
(u,v,w)
denotes the determinant constructed from the three vectors
u,v,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The linking number is intimitely related to the helicity of a vector field, see
[
1
, Theorem 4.4]
. Therefore, linking number is a key notion in hydrodynamics, meteorology and electromagnetics, see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 1,$ 5 ]
.

Positive Wiener functions can also appear in the form

\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t_{2})-w(t_{1})\big)f\big(\beta_{1}(t_{1}),\beta_{2}(t_{2})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(6)

where
\beta_{1}(t),\,\beta_{2}(t)
are two independent one-dimensional Brownian motions, independent from the
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
w(t)
, and
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a non-negative measurable map. A particular case of (
6
) is the following

\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{0}\big(w(t_{2})-w(t_{1})\big)\mathbf{1}_{(0,+\infty)}\big(\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(7)

The last expression may appear, for example, when we consider a
(d+1)
-dimensional Brownian motion
B(t)=(w(t),\beta(t))
where
w
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion independent from the one-dimensional Brownian motion
\beta
. If
w
intersects itself, i.e.
w(t_{2})=w(t_{1})
for some
t_{1}<t_{2}
, then this means that there are only two possible relative positions for the two points
B(t_{1}),\,B(t_{2})
and these two positions are related to
\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})
being either positive or negative. The integral (
7
) can be related to the winding number of Brownian motion. And it can be defined by

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Delta_{2}}p_{\varepsilon}^{d}\big(w(t_{2})-w(t_{1})\big)\mathbf{1}_{(0,+\infty)}\big(\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(8)

or, more generally, for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Delta_{2}}p_{\varepsilon}^{d}\big(w(t_{2})-w(t_{1})-u\big)\mathbf{1}_{(0,+\infty)}\big(\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(9)

In this section we will focus on the following particular examples of positive generalised Wiener functions.

Example 2.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N},\,k\geqslant 2
and
u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
where
d\geqslant 4
. Define

\rho_{k}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1})=\int_{\Delta_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{u_{j}}\big(w(t_{j+1})-w(t_{j})\big)dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}

(10)

where
w
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and, for every
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\delta_{u}
denotes the Dirac delta function at
u
which can be defined by

\delta_{u}(\cdot)=\delta_{0}(\cdot-u).

This example is more general than the example defined by (
1
) and which was considered in
[
8
]
. For the moment,
\rho_{k}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1})
is a formal notation. A rigorous meaning of (
10
) will be obtained further in this section.

Example 2.2
.

Define

\eta=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\delta_{u}\big(w(t_{2})-w(t_{1})\big)f\big(\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})\big)dt_{1}dt_{2}

(11)

where
w
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion independent from the one-dimensional Brownian motion
\beta
, and
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. For the moment, the function
f
is such that
f\big(\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1})\big)
is a positive square integrable random variable for any
t_{1}<t_{2}
. We will try first to give an appropriate definition of
\eta
and prove that it is indeed a positive generalised Wiener function and then establish some related properties. In the end of this section we will consider the case when the Brownian motions
w
and
\beta
are not independent.

In order to deal with the examples
2.1
and
2.2
we need some preliminary results.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
0<s<t<1
. Then the limit

\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p^{d}_{\varepsilon}\big(w(t)-w(s)-u)

is a generalised Wiener function that belongs to any Sobolev space
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma}
such that
\gamma<-d/2
. Moreover,

\big\|\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))\big\|_{2,\gamma}\leqslant Cp^{d}_{t-s}(cu)

where
C>0,\,c>0
are constants that depend only on
\gamma
.

Proof.

For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
denote by
H_{n}
the
n
-th Hermite polynomial defined by

H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{(n)}e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2}},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

From
[
11
]
we have the following Itô-Wiener expansion

\displaystyle\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}=k}\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\left\{\dfrac{1}{n_{j}!}\,H_{n_{j}}\Big(\dfrac{w_{j}(t)-w_{j}(s)}{\sqrt{t-s}}\Big)\,H_{n_{j}}\Big(\dfrac{u_{j}}{\sqrt{t-s}}\Big)\right\}\,p^{d}_{t-s}(u)

from which we deduce

\displaystyle\Big\|\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))\Big\|_{2,\gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{\gamma}\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}=k}\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\left\{\dfrac{1}{n_{j}!}\,H_{n_{j}}^{2}\Big(\dfrac{u_{j}}{\sqrt{t-s}}\Big)\right\}\,\Big(p^{d}_{t-s}(u)\Big)^{2}.

Let
\alpha\in\big]1/4,1/2\big[
.

From
[
11
]
we get the following estimations :

\displaystyle\exists\,c_{1}=c_{1}(\alpha)\,;\;\forall\;n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\;;\dfrac{|H_{n}(x)|}{\sqrt{n!}}\leqslant c_{1}(n\vee 1)^{-\frac{8\alpha-1}{12}}e^{\alpha x^{2}},\quad(n\vee 1=\max\{n,1\}),

\displaystyle\forall\;\beta\in]0,1[\;\exists\,c_{2}=c_{2}(\beta)\,;\;\forall\;k\in{\mathbb{N}}\,;\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}=k}\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}(n_{j}\vee 1)^{-\beta}\leqslant c_{2}(k\vee 1)^{d(1-\beta)-1}\,.

Using these inequalities we deduce the existence of constants
c_{3}=c_{3}(\alpha),c_{4}=c_{4}(\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\Big\|\delta_{u}(w(t)-w(s))\Big\|_{2,\gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{3}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{\gamma}(k\vee 1)^{d\big(1-\frac{8\alpha-1}{6}\big)-1}\exp\Big(\dfrac{2\alpha\|u\|^{2}}{t-s}\Big)\Big(p^{d}_{t-s}(u)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{\gamma}(k\vee 1)^{d\big(1-\frac{8\alpha-1}{6}\big)-1}\quad\dfrac{\exp\Big(\frac{(2\alpha-1)\|u\|^{2}}{t-s}\Big)}{(t-s)^{d}}\,,

which is finite provided that
\gamma+d\big(1-\frac{8\alpha-1}{6}\big)<0
, which leads, after letting
\alpha\to 1/2
, to the condition
\gamma<-d/2
and finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider two subspaces
H_{1},\,H_{2}
of the Hilbert space
L_{2}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and suppose they are orthogonal. Associate to them the following
\sigma
-fields

\mathcal{A}_{j}=\sigma\Big(\Big\{\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(t)dw_{i}(t),\,i=1,\cdots,d,\,h=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{d})\in H_{j}\Big\}\Big),\,j=1,2.

Let
\eta_{j}\in{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma_{j}},\,j=1,2,
be two generalised Wiener functions (so
\gamma_{j}<0,\;j=1,2
) such that
\eta_{j}
is
\mathcal{A}_{j}
-measurable,
j=1,2
. Then their Wick product
:\eta_{1}\;\eta_{2}:
is a generalised Wiener function that belongs to the Sobolev space
{\mathbb{D}}^{2,\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}
. Moreover,

\big\|:\eta_{1}\;\eta_{2}:\big\|_{2,\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\leqslant\big\|\eta_{1}\big\|_{2,\gamma_{1}}\big\|\eta_{2}\big\|_{2,\gamma_{2}}\,.

Proof.

Consider the Itô-Wiener expansion of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
:

\eta_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}I_{k},\quad\eta_{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}J_{k}

where for each
k
,
I_{k}
and
J_{k}
are
k
-multiple stochastic integrals. Due to the specific construction of the
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}_{j},\,j=1,2,
each product
I_{i}J_{j}
represents an
(i+j)
-multiple stochastic integral. Consequently one can consider the following series of multiple stochastic integrals :

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i+j=k}I_{i}J_{j}\,.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[\Big(\sum_{i+j=k}I_{i}J_{j}\Big)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\sum_{i+j=k}{\mathbb{E}}\big(I_{i}^{2}\big){\mathbb{E}}\big(J_{j}^{2}\big)\quad(\text{using the independance of }\mathcal{A}_{1},\,\mathcal{A}_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i+j=k}(i+1)^{\gamma_{1}}{\mathbb{E}}\big(I_{i}^{2}\big)(j+1)^{\gamma_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big(J_{j}^{2}\big)\quad(\text{because }\gamma_{1}<0,\,\gamma_{2}<0)

\displaystyle=\big\|\eta_{1}\big\|_{2,\gamma_{1}}^{2}\big\|\eta_{2}\big\|_{2,\gamma_{2}}^{2}\,.

This proves the result.
∎

C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}=\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,(\omega(t_{1}),\cdots,\omega(t_{n}))\in A\}.

Then

\theta_{u}\big(C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}\big)=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,(\omega(t_{1}),\cdots,\omega(t_{n}))\in A,\,\omega(s_{2})-\omega(s_{1})\in B(u,\varepsilon)\big\}}{\lambda(B(u,\varepsilon))}ds_{1}ds_{2}

where
B(u,\varepsilon)
is the ball, in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with center
u
and radius
\varepsilon
and
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure. The limit inside the integral coincides with the density of the measure
\nu_{s_{1},s_{2},t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}
defined on
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

\displaystyle\nu_{s_{1},s_{2},t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}(M)

\displaystyle=\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,(\omega(t_{1}),\cdots,\omega(t_{n}))\in A,\,\omega(s_{2})-\omega(s_{1})\in M\big\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big((w(t_{1}),\cdots,w(t_{n}))\in A,\,w(s_{2})-w(s_{1})\in M\big).

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
and
\phi_{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{nd})
such that

\mathbf{1}_{A}\leqslant\phi_{\delta}\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}

where
A_{\delta}=\cup_{x\in A}B_{(x,\delta)}
is the
\delta
-neighborhood of
A
. Then, let
F_{\delta}(\omega)=\phi_{\delta}(\omega(t_{1}),\cdots,\omega(t_{n}))
. It follows that

\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}\leqslant F_{\delta}\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A_{\delta}}}.

Consequently, the family
\big(F_{\delta}\big)_{\delta>0}
converges pointwise, as
\delta\to 0
, to
\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}
. Thus, using the dominated convergence theorem and Theorem
4.3
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\theta_{u}(C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A})

\displaystyle=\int_{W_{0}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}(\omega)\theta_{u}(d\omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{W_{0}^{d}}F_{\delta}(\omega)\theta_{u}(d\omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Delta_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big(F_{\delta}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)p_{t-s}^{d}(u)dsdt\,.

Following
[
8
]
, the conditional expectations
{\mathbb{E}}\big(F_{\delta}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)
and
{\mathbb{E}}\big(\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)
possess continuous versions. Using again the dominated convergence theorem we deduce

{\mathbb{E}}\big(F_{\delta}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big).

For
(s,t)\in\Delta_{2}
define a measure
\nu_{W_{0}^{d},s,t}
on
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

\nu_{W_{0}^{d},s,t}(M)=\int_{M}p_{t-s}^{d}(x)dx.

Now, with a last use of the dominated convergence theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{u}(C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}(w)\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)p_{t-s}^{d}(u)dsdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\dfrac{d\nu_{s,t,t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}{d\nu_{W_{0}^{d},s,t}}(u)p_{t-s}^{d}(u)dsdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\dfrac{d\nu_{s,t,t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A}}{d\lambda}(u)dsdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,(\omega(t_{1}),\cdots,\omega(t_{n}))\in A,\,\omega(t)-\omega(s)\in B(u,\varepsilon)\big\}}{\lambda(B(u,\varepsilon))}dsdt

which proves the result.
∎

The following lemma extends the previous lemma to another family of closed subsets of the Wiener space.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a closed subset of
[0,1]
and
A
a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Consider the closed subset of
W_{0}^{d}
defined by

C_{\mathbb{T},A}=\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T}\}.

Then

\theta_{u}\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\big)=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,\omega(s_{2})-\omega(s_{1})\in B(u,\varepsilon)\big\}}{\lambda(B(u,\varepsilon))}ds_{1}ds_{2}.

The limit inside the integral coincides with the density of the measure
\nu_{s_{1},s_{2},\mathbb{T},A}
defined on
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

\displaystyle\nu_{s_{1},s_{2},\mathbb{T},A}(M)

\displaystyle=\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,\omega(s_{2})-\omega(s_{1})\in M\big\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big(w(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,w(s_{2})-w(s_{1})\in M\big).

Proof.

Let
\{t_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\}
be a countable dense subset of
\mathbb{T}
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
define a closed cylindrical subset

C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A^{n}}=\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,\omega(t_{j})\in A\;\forall\;j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\}.

Following the proof of Lemma
3.1
, for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
there exists
F_{n}\in\mathcal{FC}_{b}^{\infty}\big(W_{0}^{d}\big)
such that

\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A^{n}}}(\omega)\leqslant F_{n}(\omega)\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},(A_{1/n})^{n}}}(\omega)\quad\forall\;\omega\in W_{0}^{d}.

Using the continuity of the elements
\omega
of
W_{0}^{d}
one can check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},A^{n}}}(\omega)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{C_{t_{1},\cdots,t_{n},(A_{1/n})^{n}}}(\omega)=\mathbf{1}_{C_{\mathbb{T},A}}(\omega)\quad\forall\;\omega\in W_{0}^{d}.

Consequently, the sequence
(F_{n})
converges pointwise, as
n\to\infty
, to
\mathbf{1}_{C_{\mathbb{T},A}}
. Then we proceed in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Following the proof of Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, one can prove the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a closed subset of
[0,1]
and
A
a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Consider the closed subset of
W_{0}^{d}
defined by

C_{\mathbb{T},A}=\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,;\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T}\}.

Then

\displaystyle\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\big)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{k}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,\omega(t_{j+1})-\omega(t_{j})\in B(u_{j},\varepsilon),j=1,\cdots,k-1\big\}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\lambda(B(u_{j},\varepsilon))}dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}\,.

Remark 4
.

From Lemma
3.3
on can deduce that for any closed subset
\mathbb{T}
of
[0,1]
and every closed
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have

\mu\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\big)=0\Rightarrow\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\big)=0.

But
\theta_{u}
is not absolutely continuous with respect to Wiener mesure. Instead it is finitely absolutely continuous. The definition of finite absolute continuity and the main result regarding our considerations are presented in subsection
4.3
of Appendix.

The following proposition is an immediate consequence of Theorems
2.5
,
2.6
and
4.6
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N},\,k\geqslant 2
and
u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, the measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
is finitely absolutely continuous with respect to the Wiener mesure
\mu
.

Remark 5
.

We claim that the limit inside the integrals in Lemma
3.3
can be written as
\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,\omega(t)\in A\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,\omega(t_{j+1})-\omega(t_{j})=u_{j},\,j=1,\cdots,k-1\big\}.

For
0\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}\leqslant 1
define

E_{u,t_{1},\cdots,t_{k}}=\left\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\;:\;\omega(t_{j+1})-\omega(t_{j})=u_{j},\,j=1,\cdots,k-1\right\}.

Remark that

\begin{cases}E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}=\left\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\;:\;\exists\;0\leqslant t_{1},\cdots,t_{k}\leqslant 1,\,\omega(t_{j+1})-\omega(t_{j})=u_{j},\,j=1,\cdots,k-1\right\}\\
\phantom{E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}}=\cup_{(t_{1},\cdots,t_{k})\in\Delta_{k}}E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1},t_{1},\cdots,t_{k}},\\
tE_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1},t_{1},\cdots,t_{k}}=E_{tu_{1},\cdots,tu_{k-1},t_{1},\cdots,t_{k}}\;\forall\;t>0,.\end{cases}

Remark also that for sets of the form
C_{\mathbb{T},A}
and
t>0
we have
tC_{\mathbb{T},A}=C_{\mathbb{T},tA}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

The measure
\theta_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
yields the following large deviation upper bound. If
\mathbb{T}
is a closed subset of
[0,1]
and
A
a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
then

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\log\theta_{tu_{1},\cdots,tu_{k-1}}\big(tC_{\mathbb{T},A}\big)\leqslant-\inf_{\phi\in C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}}I(\phi)

where
I
is the rate function associated with the Wiener measure.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the result when
k=2
. Applying Lemma
3.1
one can obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{tu}(tC_{\mathbb{T},A})

\displaystyle=\theta_{tu}(C_{\mathbb{T},tA})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\mu\big\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\,:\,\omega(t)\in tA\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{T},\,\omega(s_{2})-\omega(s_{1})=tu\big\}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\mu\big(tC_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{tu,s_{1},s_{2}}\big)ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\mu\big(t\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}\big)\big)ds_{1}ds_{2}\,.

According to Theorem
4.4

\forall\;r>0\;\exists\;M>0\;;\;\forall t>M,\;\mu\big(t\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}\big)\big)<e^{-t^{2}\big(\inf_{\phi\in C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}}I(\phi)-r\big)}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\theta_{tu}(tA)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta_{2}}\mu\big(t\big(C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}\big)\big)ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Delta_{2}}e^{-t^{2}\big(\inf_{\phi\in C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}}I(\phi)-r\big)}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Delta_{2}}e^{-t^{2}\big(\inf_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\Delta_{2}}\inf_{\phi\in C_{\mathbb{T},A}\cap E_{u,s_{1},s_{2}}}I(\phi)-r\big)}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}e^{-t^{2}\big(\inf_{\phi\in A\cap E_{u}}I(\phi)-r\big)}\,.

Taking
r
to
0
yields the result.
∎

Remark 6
.

Remark that

\inf_{\phi\in E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}}I(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|u_{j}\|\Big)^{2}.

In order to prove it, let
\phi\in E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}\cap H_{1}
. Then, there exists a square integrable function
h:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h(s)ds\;\forall\;t\in[0,1]
and there exist real numbers
0\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}\leqslant 1
such that
\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}h(s)ds=u_{j},\,j=1,\cdots,k-1
. Consequently,

\displaystyle I(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\|h(s)\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\|h(s)\|ds\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\|h(s)\|ds\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\Big\|\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}h(s)ds\Big\|\Big)^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|u_{j}\|\Big)^{2}.

Moreover, for a specific choice of
0=t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{k}=1
, one can construct a piecewise linear function
\phi:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\phi(t_{1})=0,\;\phi(t_{j+1})-\phi(t_{j})=u_{j},\,j=1,\cdots,k-1,
and

I(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|u_{j}\|\Big)^{2}.

In particular, for any closed
C\subset W_{0}^{d}
,

\inf_{\phi\in C\cap E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}}I(\phi)\geqslant\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\|u_{j}\|\Big)^{2}\,.

Remark 7
.

From Theorem
3.5
one can see that the rate function
\widetilde{I}
of the measure
\theta_{u}
can be expressed in terms of the good rate function
I
of the Wiener measure as

\widetilde{I}(\phi)=\begin{cases}I(\phi),&\phi\in E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}\\
+\infty,&\phi\notin E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}.\end{cases}

It follows that the level sets are given by

\{\widetilde{I}\leqslant\lambda\}=\{I\leqslant\lambda\}\cap E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}.

And since
E_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{k-1}}
is a closed set and the level sets of the rate function
I
are compact (according to Theorem (
4.4
)) then the level sets of
\widetilde{I}
are also compact. Therefore, according to Definition (
1.1
),
\widetilde{I}
is a good rate function.

4.1
Some results about the measure
\theta_{u}

In
[
8
]
the authors used a representation of positive generalised Wiener functions by measures on the Wiener space (see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 4.1.]
) in order to prove that the
2
-fold self-intersection local time
\rho_{2}(u)
can be represented by a finite positive measure
\theta_{u}
on the classical Wiener space
(W_{0}^{d},\mathcal{B}(W_{0}^{d}),\mu)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
8
, Theorem 4]
Let
d\geqslant 4
and
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Then,

\forall\;F\in\mathcal{FC}_{b}^{\infty}\big(W_{0}^{d}\big),\quad(\rho_{2}(u),F)=\int_{W_{0}^{d}}F(\omega)\,\theta_{u}(d\omega).

Here
\mathcal{FC}_{b}^{\infty}\big(W_{0}^{d}\big)
denotes the set of Wiener test functions of the form

F(\omega)=f(\ell_{1}(\omega),\cdots,\ell_{n}(\omega))

where
f\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
: the space of all bounded and infinitely differentiable functions from
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
with all their derivatives being bounded, and
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{n}\in\big(W_{0}^{d}\big)^{*}
: the topological dual of the Wiener space.

Moreover, the following theorems were proved.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
8
, Theorem 5]

Let
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,u\neq 0
. The support of the measure
\theta_{u}
is included in the closed set :

E_{u}=\left\{\omega\in W_{0}^{d}\;;\;\exists\;0\leqslant s<t\leqslant 1,\,\omega(t)-\omega(s)=u\right\}.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
8
, Theorem 7]

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{FC}_{b}^{\infty}\big(W_{0}^{d}\big)
. Then, for every
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,

\int_{W_{0}^{d}}\eta(\omega)\theta_{u}(d\omega)=\int_{\Delta_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta\big|w(t)-w(s)=u\big)p_{t-s}^{d}(u)dsdt

(12)

Moreover, both sides in (
12
) are continuous with respect to the variable
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

We recall also that

\theta_{u}\big(W_{0}^{d}\big)=\int_{\Delta_{2}}p_{t-s}^{d}(u)dsdt=:m(u,d)

and that
\widetilde{\theta_{u}}=\dfrac{\theta_{u}}{m(u,d)}
is a probability measure.

4.2
LDP for the Wiener measure

The following is Schilder theorem which states a LDP for the Wiener measure.

Theorem 4.4
.

(Schilder)
[
4
, Theorem 5.2.3]

Consider the Wiener measure
\mu
and for
\varepsilon>0
let
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\;.\Big)
. Then,
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}
satisfies, in the Wiener space
W_{0}^{d}
, a LDP with good rate function

I(\phi)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\|\phi^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt,&\phi\in H_{1}\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here
H_{1}
is the space of all absolutely continuous functions with value
0
at
0
that possess a square integrable derivative and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

4.3
Finite absolute continuity

Finite absolute continuity was first introduced by A.A. Dorogovstev in
[
6
]
in order to compare Gaussian measures in Banach spaces.

Definition 4.5
.

[
6
, Definition 2]

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
be two probability measures on a Banach space
X
, which have finite weak moments of any order. We say that
\nu_{1}
is finitely absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu_{2}
if for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a constant
c_{n}>0
such that for any polynomial
P:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with degree at most
n
and any
\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{n}\in X^{*}
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{X}P(\ell_{1}(\omega),\cdots,\ell_{n}(\omega))d\nu_{1}(\omega)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{n}\Big(\int_{X}P^{2}(\ell_{1}(\omega),\cdots,\ell_{n}(\omega))d\nu_{2}(\omega)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Theorem 4.6
.

[
16
, Proposition 1]

Let
\nu
be the measure on the Wiener space associated to some positive generalised Wiener function. Then
\nu
is finitely absolutely continuous with respect to the Wiener measure.
