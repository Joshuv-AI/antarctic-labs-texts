Uniform resonance free regions for convex cocompact hyperbolic surfaces and expanders
Louis Soares

Abstract
. We prove that every family of coverings of any infinite-area, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface has uniform spectral gap, provided that the associated Schreier graphs form a family of two-sided expanders. This extends the results of Brooks [ 14 ] , Burger [ 15 , 16 ] , and Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak [ 6 ] to a setting where the Laplacian has no L 2 L^{2} -eigenvalues. In particular, the notion of spectral gap needs to be redefined in terms of the resonances of the Laplacian. As an immediate corollary, we obtain uniform spectral gap for congruence covers of convex cocompact surfaces, a result previously established by Oh–Winter [ 50 ] and Bourgain–Kontorovich–Magee [ 31 ] . Moreover, given any convex cocompact hyperbolic surface X X , we provide a new “universal” resonance-free region for X X , by which we mean a region in the complex plane that contains no resonances for any finite cover of X X . This enlarges the universal resonance-free region given by Magee–Naud [ 48 ] . Our methods rely on the thermodynamic formalism for twisted Selberg zeta functions.

Spectral gap and expanders

Let

42
,
59
,
40

•

•

a “two-sided

A family of

Let us now describe the graphs of interest to us. Fix a Riemannian manifold

are the left cosets of

(of which there are finitely many) and two vertices of

are joined by an edge if and only if the corresponding cosets differ by left multiplication by an element in

. The aforementioned results of Brooks

Here “uniform spectral gap” means that there exists some constant

extended this result to all geometrically finite hyperbolic quotients

whose critical exponent

is an

Let us first review some standard facts about the spectral theory of infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces, referring the reader to Borthwick’s book
[
3
]
for a comprehensive discussion. Every hyperbolic surface
X
can be realized as a quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, where
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is the hyperbolic plane and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a Fuchsian group, i.e., a discrete group of Möbius transformations. For the rest of this introduction we assume that
\Gamma
is

•

finitely generated
, which is equivalent to
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
being geometrically and topologically finite, and

•

non-cofinite
, which is equivalent with
X
having infinite area.

The limit set of
X
, denoted by
\Lambda
, is defined as the set of accumulation points of all orbits of the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Under the above conditions,
\Lambda
is a Cantor-like fractal subset of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
. The Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda
, denoted by
\delta
, is equal to the exponent of convergence of the Poincaré series for
\Gamma
and is sometimes referred to as the “critical exponent” of
X
.

The
L^{2}
-spectrum of the positive Laplacian
\Delta_{X}
on
X
is rather sparse and has been described completely by Lax–Phillips
[
41
]
as follows:

•

The continuous spectrum of
\Delta_{X}
coincides with the interval
[1/4,\infty)
. It does not contain any embedded
L^{2}
-eigenvalues.

•

For
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
the pure point spectrum is a finite set of points in the interval
\left[\frac{1}{4},\delta(1-\delta)\right].

•

For
\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
the pure point spectrum is empty.

This description of the spectrum shows that the resolvent operator

R_{X}(s)=\left(\Delta_{X}-s(1-s)\right)^{-1}:L^{2}(X)\rightarrow L^{2}(X)

is well-defined and analytic on the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
, except at the finite set of poles corresponding to the pure point spectrum of
\Delta_{X}
. In the infinite-area case, the main spectral data of interest are the
resonances
of
X
. These are defined as the poles of the meromorphic continuation of

(1)

R_{X}(s):C_{0}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X)

to the whole complex plane. The continuation of (
1
) can be deduced from the analytic Fredholm theorem using a suitable parametrix provided by Guillopé–Zworski
[
25
]
. In the sequel, we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{X}
the multiset of resonances of
X
; the multiplicity of a resonance
\zeta
is defined as the rank of the residual operator of
R_{X}(s)
at
s=\zeta
. By a a result of Patterson
[
52
]
,
R_{X}(s)
has a simple pole at
s=\delta
and no other poles in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\delta
. In other words, the point
s=\delta
is the resonance for
X
with the largest real part.

Naud
[
45
]
(see also Bourgain–Dyatlov
[
4
]
) proved that every non-elementary, geometrically finite hyperbolic surface
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
has a “spectral gap” in the following sense: there is some
\eta>0
so that

(2)

\mathcal{R}_{X}\cap\{\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\delta-\eta\}=\{\delta\},

or equivalently,

(3)

\mathrm{gap}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\text{$\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{X}\setminus\{\delta\}$}}(\delta-\mathrm{Re}(\zeta))\geqslant\eta.

When
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
, this is an easy consequence of the following facts: every resonance
s
in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
is a real number in the interval
[\frac{1}{2},\delta]
, it corresponds to an
L^{2}
-eigenvalue
\lambda=s(1-s)
, and there are only finitely many such eigenvalues. In the alternative case
\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, Naud’s result is far less trivial because there are infinitely many resonances in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)<\frac{1}{2}
(as established in
[
25
]
), none of which is connected to an
L^{2}
-eigenvalue.

Spectral gaps have a long history and numerous applications. In the context of infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces, these applications include precise asymptotics of the length spectrum
[
46
]
, asymptotic of waves
[
23
]
, as well as arithmetic applications in conjunction with the affine sieve
[
6
]
.

1.2.
Statement of results

Let
X^{\prime}
be a finite cover of
X
, i.e., a finite-degree or finite-sheeted cover. Any resonance for
X
is also one for
X^{\prime}
. This follows directly from the fact that resonances occur as zeros of the Selberg zeta function and from the Venkov–Zograf formula, see §
2.3
. Moreover, the limit sets of
X^{\prime}
and
X
have equal Hausdorff dimensions. In particular, the critical exponent

is a common resonance for all finite covers of
X
and it is the one with the largest real part. Following Magee–Naud
[
48
]
, we say that a resonance for
X^{\prime}
is “new” with respect to the base surface

if it occurs with greater multiplicity than in
X
. In this view, we can define the

spectral gap of a covering
X^{\prime}\to X
by

(4)

\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime},X)=\inf_{\text{$\zeta$ new}}(\delta-\mathrm{Re}(\zeta)),

where the infimum is taken over the new resonances of
X^{\prime}
with respect to
X
. Clearly, we have

\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime})=\min\{\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime},X),\mathrm{gap}(X)\}.

We now focus on the case where

is an infinite-area, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface. Thanks to a result of Button
[
17
]
, any such surface is isometric to a quotient

where

is a so-called
Schottky group
. Schottky groups stand out, among other Fuchsian groups, by their simple geometric construction, which we recall below in §
2.5
. For the rest of this introduction,

•

\Gamma
is a non-elementary
2
2

2

this is equivalent with
\Gamma
having at least
m\geqslant 2
Schottky generators
Schottky group with Schottky generating set
S=\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}\}
,

•

(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in I}
,

is a family of finite-index subgroups of
\Gamma
,

•

we put
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
X_{n}=\Gamma_{n}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and

•

we let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in I}
be the associated family of Schreier coset graphs with respect to the generating set
S
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let notations and assumptions be as above. Suppose the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{n}
form a family of two-sided
\epsilon
-expanders for some
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists some
\eta=\eta(\epsilon,\Gamma)>0
such that

\inf_{n\in I}\mathrm{gap}(X_{n},X)\geqslant\eta.

Moreover, we may take
\eta=\exp(-c\epsilon^{-1})
for some
c=c(\Gamma)>0.

A few remarks are in order.

Remark 1.2
.

If
X
has critical exponent
\delta
larger than
\frac{1}{2}
, Theorem
1.1
is easily deduced from Theorem 1.2 of Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak
[

(albeit without the dependence of

\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
since resonances in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
are not connected to eigenvalues. Moreover, the methods in
[
6
]
do not easily generalize to our setting and we are forced to devise our own proof strategy.

Remark 1.3
.

We know from Beardon
[

2

that the condition
\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
excludes cusps. In other words, any hyperbolic surface with
\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}

Remark 1.4
.

Theorem

\Gamma
is elementary, i.e., when

. In this case
\Gamma
is generated by a single hyperbolic element
\gamma
and

is a hyperbolic cylinder. The critical exponent of

is equal to zero and

is equal to the half-lattice

A quotient

It is well-known that a connected, regular graph is bipartite if and only if its normalized adjacency matrix has

Let notations and assumptions be as above. Assume additionally that

This corollary can also be rephrased in terms of Property (

1.3.
Congruence subgroups

Theorem
1.1
may be used to establish a uniform resonance-free strip for the family of congruence covers. When
\Gamma
is a subgroup in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
, the (principal) congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
of level
q
is defined by

(7)

\Gamma(q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\text{$\gamma\in\Gamma$ : $\gamma\equiv I$ mod $q$}\right\}.

Equivalently,
\Gamma(q)
is the kernel of the reduction map modulo
q
,

\pi_{q}\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}),\quad\gamma\mapsto\text{$\gamma$ mod $q$.}

We denote the associated congruence cover by
X(q)=\Gamma(q)\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}.
When
X
is convex cocompact, the existence of a uniform spectral gap for
X(q)
was first proven by Oh–Winter
[
50
]
for
q
running through the positive square-free integers, and later by Bourgain–Kontorovich–Magee
[
31
]
for
q
running over all positive integers with no small prime divisors.

In both papers
[
50
,
31
]
, one of the main ingredients is the deep result of Bourgain–Varjú
[
8
]
, which implies that there is some
\epsilon=\epsilon(\Gamma)>0
with following property: for every
q\in\mathbb{N},
the Cayley graph

\mathcal{G}_{q}=\mathrm{Cay}(G_{q},\pi_{q}(S)),\quad G_{q}=\pi_{q}(\Gamma),

is an
\epsilon
-expander. Moreover, there exists an integer
q_{0}=q_{0}(\Gamma)
such that for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
co-prime to
q_{0}
we have
G_{q}=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}).
Combining this result with our main Theorem
1.1

immediately
yields the following:

Theorem 1.10

(Resonance-free strip for
X(q)
)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be a non-elementary, convex cocompact group. Then there exists
\eta>0
and
q_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
(q,q_{0})=1
the congruence covers
X(q)=\Gamma(q)\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
have no new resonances in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\delta-\eta.

Special cases of Theorem
1.10
with explicit spectral gaps have been known for a long time. Selberg’s famous

[

]
says that for the modular group

(in which case

) we can take

. This is equivalent with the first non-zero eigenvalue of

being bounded from below by
\lambda_{1}(X(q))\geqslant\frac{3}{16}
for any

. Selberg himself conjectured that the same should hold true with
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
(which is equivalent with saying that
\lambda_{1}(X(q))\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
). Although this conjecture remains open to date, several quantitative improvements have been made
[
29
,
44
,
30
,
35
,
36
]
. We refer to the expository articles of Sarnak
[

,
60
]
. The first spectral gap in the infinite-area setting was established by Gamburd
[

, who proved that for all finitely generated
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\delta>\frac{5}{6}
and for any sufficiently large prime

\eta=\delta-\frac{5}{6}
in Theorem

Apart from the intrinsic interest of this problem, finding explicit spectral gaps for congruence covers has many applications to concrete arithmetic questions, see for instance
[
6
,
37
,
7
,

,

. Selberg’s conjecture (when extended to the infinite-area setting) suggests that the optimal gap should be

, but this seems out of reach given the current state of the art. Good bounds for

are difficult to obtain, mainly due to the reliance on the expander theory developed by Bourgain–Varjú
[

]
and Bourgain–Gamburd
[

]
among others. These papers do not provide explicit bounds for the expansion coefficients of the Cayley graphs

It should be noted that Kowalski

Another source of non-explicitness in Theorem

It is worth noting that the proofs of Oh–Winter
[
50
]
and Bourgain–Kontorovich–Magee
[

rely on two fundamental ingredients. Firstly, for sufficiently large primes

\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
is at least
\frac{p-1}{2}
, which is large when compared to the group order
|\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})|\asymp p^{3}
. In the terminology introduced by Gowers
[
22
]
, the latter means that
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
is a highly
quasirandom
group. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
(and hence Theorem
1.10
) does not require such specific information on the covering group. In fact, Theorem
1.1
does not even require the fundamental group of the coverings to be normal subgroups of
\Gamma
. Nonetheless, our proofs bears certain resemblances to
[
50
,
31
]
, such as the usage of the thermodynamic formalism.

We also point out that Theorem
1.10
can be extended (in slightly weaker form) to congruence groups coming from arbitrary number fields. This follows directly from Theorem
1.1
and the result of Varjú
[
62
]
, which says that for the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}
of an arbitrary number field, the Cayley graphs of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}/\mathcal{I})
, with respect to the projections of
S
, form an expander family when
\mathcal{I}
ranges over square-free ideals of
\mathcal{O}
. (A careful inspection of
[
62
]
reveals that this is actually a family of two-sided expanders.) Thus we have the following:

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the ring of integers in a totally real number field and let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
be a convex cocompact Fuchsian group. Given an ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
let
\Gamma(\mathfrak{q})
be the principal congruence subgroup defined analogously to (
7
). Then there exists a constant
\eta=\eta(\Gamma)>0
such that
X(\mathfrak{q})=\Gamma(\mathfrak{q})\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
has no new resonances in
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\delta-\eta
, where
\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
ranges over square-free ideals of
\mathcal{O}
with no small prime factors.

The restriction to square-free ideals comes from the main theorem in
[
62
]
. The author is not aware whether this restriction can be removed or not.

At this point the reader may wonder if convex cocompact Fuchsian groups
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
where
\mathcal{O}
is an in Theorem
1.11
actually exist (because if not, Theorem
1.11
is just a vacuous statement). Fortunately, such groups are actually ubiquitous, as observed in
[
31
]
: for any pair of hyperbolic elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
with no common fixed points on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
,
\gamma_{1}^{k},\gamma_{2}^{k}
forms a pair of Schottky generators for all

sufficiently large. In particular,
\Gamma=\langle\gamma_{1}^{k},\gamma_{2}^{k}\rangle
is a convex cocompact subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
.

1.4.
Further explicit constructions

We now give more examples of families of covers with uniform spectral gap other than those coming from congruence subgroups. Consider the following recipe:

(1)

Let
G
be a finite group and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
be elements that generate
G
.

(2)

Let
\pi\colon\Gamma\to G
be the homomorphism defined by
\pi(\gamma_{i})=g_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Note that
\pi\colon\Gamma\to G
is surjective since we assume that
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
generate the whole group
G
.

(3)

Finally, let
\Gamma^{\prime}=\ker(\pi)
be the kernel of
\pi\colon\Gamma\to G
. By construction,
X^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is a
G
-cover
of
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, that is, a finite regular cover whose covering group is isomorphic to
\Gamma^{\prime}/\Gamma\cong G
. Moreover, the Schreier coset graph associated to this covering is the Cayley graph

\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{g_{1}^{\pm},\dots,g_{m}^{\pm}\}).

Thanks to Theorem
1.1
, if
\mathcal{G}
is a two-sided
\epsilon
-expander for some
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta=\eta(\epsilon,X)>0
for which
\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime},X)\geqslant\eta
, where
\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime},X)
is the relative spectral gap defined in (
4
).

Hence, by this recipe, in order to manufacture coverings with uniform spectral gap, it suffices to find families of finite groups
G
and corresponding generating elements
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\}\subset G
such that the resulting Cayley graphs
\mathcal{G}
are two-sided
\epsilon
-expanders for some uniform
\epsilon>0.
It turns out that examples for such families are easy to find in the literature. Let us briefly mention a few of these examples along with their respective implications:

(1)

By
[
33
,
13
]
we know that for every finite simple group
G
and for every
m\geqslant m_{0}
there are elements
g_{1},\dots g_{m}
in
G
with the property that the Cayley graph
\mathcal{G}
is a two-sided
\epsilon
-expander, with
m_{0}
and
\epsilon>0
independent of the group. Note that the number
m
of Schottky generators for
X
is related to Euler characteristic by
\chi(X)=1-m
. Hence, by Theorem
1.1
it follows that every convex cocompact hyperbolic surface
X
with negative enough Euler characteristic and for every finite simple group
G
, there exists a
G
-cover
X^{\prime}
of
X
for which
\mathrm{gap}(X^{\prime},X)\geqslant\eta
, with
\eta>0
depending only on
X
.

(2)

By the main result in
[
12
]
(see also references therein) every
simple group of Lie type

G
has the property that if we choose elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
independently and uniformly at random, then, with probability tending to
1
as

, these elements generate the whole group

and the Cayley graph
\mathcal{G}
is a two-sided
\epsilon
-expander for some fixed
\epsilon
. Combining this with Theorem

it follows that for every convex cocompact hyperbolic surface

, almost every

, which is the same statement with “

(3)

By
[

the statement of the previous item also holds true for direct products

, where

(4)

By
[
11
]
there exists a density one subset
\mathcal{P}
of the primes with the following property: for every generating set of

with

the corresponding Cayley graph is a two-sided

-covering

1.5.
Outline of the proofs

Let us now outline the general strategy leading to the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.8
. Throughout, we take advantage of the following two facts. First, the resonances of the surface
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
correspond to the zeros of the corresponding Selberg zeta function, denoted by
Z_{\Gamma}(s)
. Secondly, given a finite cover
X^{\prime}\to X
we can assume that the fundamental group
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
is a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma
. In this case, the resonances of
X^{\prime}
occur as zeros of the “twisted” Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}})
, where
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
is the induced representation of the identity on
\Gamma^{\prime}
to
\Gamma
, see §
2.3
. To make the connection with expanders we notice first that the adjacency operator of the Schreier coset graph can be expressed easily in terms of
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
, see §
2.4
. Being an expander graph thus becomes a property of the induced representation. Therefore, it is natural to reformulate the problem more generally in terms of the zeros of twisted Selberg zeta functions
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
for general finite-dimensional representations
\rho
of
\Gamma
. In this view,

(1)

our main Theorem
1.1
is a special case of Theorem
5.1
and

(2)

Theorem
1.8
is a special case of Theorem
3.1
.

We also exploit the fact that for Schottky groups
\Gamma
,
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
can be realized as the Fredholm determinant of a family of
transfer operators

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
,
s\in\mathbb{C}
, acting on a functional space consisting of
V
-valued functions on a neighbourhood
I\subset\mathbb{R}
of the limit set
\Lambda
. More details will be given in §
2.6
. Any zero
s=\sigma+it
of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
thus corresponds to a
1
-eigenfunction of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
, i.e., there exists some non-zero
F\colon I\to V
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}^{N}F=F.

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. This can be restated as follows: there exists some non-zero
f\colon I\to V
satisfying

(8)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f=e^{-NP(\sigma)}f,

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}
denotes the
normalized
transfer operator, and
P
is the
topological pressure
associated to the natural dynamical system induced by the action of
\Gamma
on the union of Schottky intervals
I
. In §
2.8
, details pertaining to the thermodynamic formalism, including the topological pressure, will be provided. The normalized transfer operator will be introduced in §
3.1
.

1.5.1.
Outline of proof of Theorem
1.8

Let us first describe the proof of Theorem
1.8
. Assume that
s=\sigma+it
is a resonance for a finite cover of
X^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Hence,
s=\sigma+it
must be a zero of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
where
\rho
is the induced representation of
\Gamma^{\prime}/\Gamma
and consequently, there exists a non-zero
f\colon I\to V
satisfying (
8
). When
|t|
is large we use a high-frequency norm estimate (Proposition
3.6
). This estimate, which follows essentially from Magee–Naud
[
48
]
, tells us that for all
|t|\gg 1
and for
N\approx\log|t|

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant\frac{1}{|t|}\|f\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

Combining this with (
8
) yields the desired contradiction, provided
|t|
is large enough and
\sigma
is sufficiently close to
\delta
. Now we assume alternatively that
|t|\ll 1
is bounded from above. In this case we apply the
tensor power trick
(see §
3.2
), which can be described informally as follows: for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
h=f^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-th tensor power of
f
and let
\nu=\rho^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-th tensor power representation of
\rho
. Then, letting
x\in I
be a maximizer of the function
x\mapsto\|f(x)\|_{V}
, one can show that

(9)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{N}h(x)\approx e^{-NP(\sigma)}h(x).

Here, the symbol
\approx
means that the difference of both sides is small measured in the norm of
V^{\otimes k}
, provided
\sigma
is close to
\delta
and
k
is fixed. But now (assuming
t\neq 0
) we take
k
so that
k|t|\gg 1
is large enough. We can once again apply the high-frequency estimate. This allows us to obtain a contradiction to (
9
), provided
\sigma
is sufficiently close to
\delta
in terms of
t
. Pursuing this analysis carefully yields a contradiction for all
\sigma\geqslant\delta-\eta(t)
, where
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the function given in Theorem
1.8
.

1.5.2.
Outline of proof of main theorem

Let us now turn our attention to Theorem
1.1
. Some new notations are needed. The group
\Gamma
is freely generated by the Schottky generating set
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}
and their inverses, which we denote by

\gamma_{m+1}=\gamma_{1}^{-1}
,
\gamma_{m+2}=\gamma_{2}^{-1},
… ,
\gamma_{2m}=\gamma_{m}^{-1}
.

Thus the elements of
\Gamma
can be indexed by reduced words
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}
with letters
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}
in the alphabet
[2m]=\{1,\dots,2m\}
. Given such a word we write

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\gamma_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\gamma_{a_{N}}.

We denote by
W_{N}^{j}
the set of reduced words of length
N
not ending with the letter
j.

Now fix a subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
and assume that the associated Schreier graph is a two-sided
\epsilon
-expander for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
s=\sigma+it
be a
new
resonance for
X^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Then
s=\sigma+it
is a zero of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
where
\rho
is the induced representation of
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}

minus
the identity. Once again, this zero corresponds to a solution
f
of equation (
8
). Suppose that
\sigma\geqslant\delta-\eta
. We seek to obtain a contradiction when
\eta>0
is sufficiently small in terms of
\epsilon
. Let
x\in I
be a maximizer of
x\mapsto\|f(x)\|_{V}
. Using Proposition
3.3
, which is essentially a strict convexity argument, we deduce that there exists some
j\in[2m]
such that for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
,

(10)

f(x)\approx\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x)).

Here we write
\approx
to mean that the difference of both sides (measured in the
V
-norm) is small as
\eta\to 0^{+}
. Now we invoke Corollary
3.2
(a quantitative version of Theorem
1.8
). It says that if
s=\sigma+it
is a resonance for
X^{\prime}
with real part
\sigma
close enough to
\delta
, then we must have
t\approx 0
. It follows that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\approx 1
, so (
10
) becomes

(11)

f(x)\approx\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x)).

Now we take the average of this equation over all words
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
:

f(x)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right).

Finally, we apply the estimate from Proposition
4.3
, which is precisely where we appeal to the expansion property. Roughly speaking, this estimate tells us that

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right)\right\|\ll e^{-c\epsilon N}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Combining this with (
11
) yields

\|f\|_{\infty}=\|f(x)\|_{V}\ll e^{-c\epsilon N}\|f\|_{\infty}

and we now obtain the desired contradiction once
N
is large enough.

It should be noted that the actual proofs deviate considerably from the description given here. Let us mention two aspects omitted from this exposition. First, we clearly need to replace the ambiguous symbol
\approx
by actual estimates. Second, we need to control the size of the derivative
f^{\prime}
in terms of the size of
f
. This is dealt with in Lemma
3.9
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperbolic geometry

We start by reviewing some basic facts about hyperbolic geometry, referring the reader to Borthwick’s book
[
3
]
for in-depth account of the material presented here. One of the standard models for the hyperbolic plane is the Poincaré half-plane

\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ y>0\},

endowed with its standard metric of constant curvature
-1
,

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

The group of orientation-preserving isometries of
(\mathbb{H}^{2},ds)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which acts on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by Möbius transformations

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad z\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\Longrightarrow\gamma(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

This action extends naturally to the extended complex plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. An element
\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is called

•

“hyperbolic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|>2
, which implies that
\gamma
has two distinct fixed points on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

•

“parabolic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|=2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point on
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and

•

“elliptic” if
|\tr(\gamma)|<2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Discrete subgroups of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
are called “Fuchsian” groups. A Fuchsian group is “torsion-free” if it contains no elliptic elements. It is called “convex cocompact” if it is finitely generated and if it contains neither elliptic nor parabolic elements. This is equivalent to the “convex core” of
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
being compact. Every infinite-area, convex cocompact surface is isometric to a quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
with
\Gamma
a so-called “Schottky group”, which we will define in §
2.5
below.

2.2.
Twisted Selberg zeta functions

Given a finitely generated Fuchsian group
\Gamma<\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the set of prime periodic geodesics on
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is bijective to the set
[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}
of
\Gamma
-conjugacy classes of the primitive hyperbolic elements in
\Gamma
(see for instance
[
3
, Proposition 2.25]
). Let
\ell(\gamma)
denote the length of the geodesic corresponding to the conjugacy class
[\gamma]\in[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}
.

The Selberg zeta function is defined for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\delta
by the infinite product

(12)

Z_{\Gamma}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\prod_{[\gamma]\in[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}}\left(1-e^{-(s+k)\ell(\gamma)}\right).

It admits a meromorphic continuation to
s\in\mathbb{C}.
By Patterson–Perry
[
53
]
the zero set of
Z_{\Gamma}(s)
consists of so-called “topological zeros” at
s=-k
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and the set of resonances, repeated according to multiplicity. Therefore, any problem about resonances can be rephrased as a question about the distribution of the zeros of the Selberg zeta function.

In this paper, we are interested in “twisted” Selberg zeta functions. They are defined for any finite-dimensional, unitary representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
by

(13)

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\prod_{[\gamma]\in[\Gamma]_{\mathsf{prim}}}\det\left(I_{V}-\rho(\gamma)e^{-(s+k)\ell(\gamma)}\right).

Note that (
13
) reduces to the classical Selberg zeta function (
12
) when
\rho
is taken to be the trivial one-dimensional representation of
\Gamma.
Moreover, it follows directly from this product definition that we can factorize

(14)

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2})=Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho_{1})Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho_{2}),

where
\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2}
denotes the orthogonal direct sum of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
.

2.3.
Finite covers and Venkov–Zograf formula

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma
and let
X^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
be the corresponding cover of
X
. Let

(15)

\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Ind}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}^{\Gamma}(\textbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\prime}})

be the induced representation of the trivial one-dimensional representation
\textbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
on
\Gamma^{\prime}
to the larger group
\Gamma.
The
Venkov–Zograf (induction) formula
asserts that

(16)

Z_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(s)=Z_{\Gamma}(s,\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}).

This was proven by Venkov–Zograf
[
64
]
in the case where
\Gamma
is a cofinite Fuchsian group (see also
[
63
]
). For an extension of this formula to the non-cofinite case we refer to
[
20
]
.

Let
n=[\Gamma:\Gamma^{\prime}]
be the index of
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
be a full set of representatives in
\Gamma
of the left cosets in
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
. The induced representation can be thought of as acting on the space

(17)

V_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}g_{i}:\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\right\},

To describe the action, note that for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
and for each
i\in[n]
there exists
\sigma(i)\in[n]
and
\tilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\gamma g_{i}=g_{\sigma(i)}\tilde{\gamma}.
The action of
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
is then given by

\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}(\gamma)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}g_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}g_{\sigma(i)}.

In fact,
\sigma\in S_{n}
is a permutation of
[n]
. With respect to the basis
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, the element
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}(\gamma)
acts on
V_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
by the permutation matrix associated to
\sigma.
Moreover, the induced representation splits as an orthogonal direct sum

\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}=\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}\oplus\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0},

where
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0}
is representation acting on the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace given by

(18)

V_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}g_{i}\in V:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=0\right\}.

From (
14
) we deduce that the Selberg zeta function of the subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
can be written as

(19)

Z_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(s)=Z_{\Gamma}(s)Z_{\Gamma}(s,\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0}).

In light of this factorization we obtain the following crucial fact:

Every new resonance for
X^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
with respect to
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is a zero of the twisted Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0}).

2.4.
Schreier graphs and Property
(\tau)

A graph is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where
\mathcal{V}
is a set, called the vertex set of
\mathcal{G}
, and
\mathcal{E}
is a collection of unordered pairs
\{a,b\}
of distinct elements
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
, called the edge set of
\mathcal{G}
. For technical reasons, we allow
\mathcal{E}
to contain repeated edges (also called parallel edges). The graph
\mathcal{G}
is called “
k
-regular” if each vertex is contained in exactly
k
edges, where edges are counted with repetitions. The adjacency operator
A_{\mathcal{G}}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{V})
is defined for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{V})
and
a\in\mathcal{V}
as

(A_{\mathcal{G}}f)(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathcal{V}\\
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}f(b).

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is
k
-regular. The operator
A_{\mathcal{G}}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathcal{V})
and its operator norm is bounded from above by
k
. Hence, by the spectral theorem, all the eigenvalues of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
lie in the interval
[-k,k].
Moreover, the largest eigenvalue of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
equals
k
as
A_{\mathcal{G}}\textbf{1}=k\textbf{1}
, where
1
is the constant one function.

Now recall that given a group
\Gamma
, a finite-index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
, and a generating set
S=\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}\}
of
\Gamma
, the associated Schreier coset graph
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},S)
is constructed in the following way: the vertices are the left cosets in
\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}
and the edges are given by
\{x\Gamma^{\prime},sx\Gamma^{\prime}\}
with
s\in S\cup S^{-1}.
Note that this graph has repeated edges if and only if
s_{1}x\Gamma^{\prime}=s_{2}x\Gamma^{\prime}
for some
x\in\Gamma
and for two distinct elements
s_{1},s_{2}\in S\cup S^{-1}
. By construction,
\mathcal{G}
is a
2m
-regular graph where
m=|S|
with adjacency operator

(A_{\mathcal{G}}f)(x\Gamma^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(f(\gamma_{j}x\Gamma^{\prime})+f(\gamma_{j}^{-1}x\Gamma^{\prime})\right).

Recall from the introduction that
\mathcal{G}
is called

•

an “
\epsilon
-expander” if all the eigenvalues of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
, other than the largest eigenvalue
2m
, lie in
[-2m,2m(1-\epsilon)]
, and

•

a “two-sided
\epsilon
-expander” if all the eigenvalues of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
, other than
2m
, lie in the interval
[-2m(1-\epsilon),2m(1-\epsilon)]
.

We now define the following operator for any representation
(\rho,V)
of
\Gamma
:

(20)

T(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho(\gamma_{j})+\rho(\gamma_{j})^{-1}\right)\in\mathrm{End}(V).

We will henceforth assume that
\rho
is unitary, which is to say that
\rho(\gamma)^{\ast}=\rho(\gamma)^{-1}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. It then follows that
T(\rho)
is self-adjoint, that is,
T(\rho)^{\ast}=T(\rho)
; moreover, since
\|T(\rho)\|\leqslant 1
, all the eigenvalues of
T(\rho)
lie inside
[-1,1].

Let
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
be the induced representation described in
2.3
. Observe that
T(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}})
is equal to the normalized adjacency matrix of the Schreier coset graph
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},S)
, i.e., if we identify the representation space
V_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}
with
L^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime})
we have
T(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2m}A_{\mathcal{G}}
. As a consequence we obtain

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},S)
be the Schreier graph as above. Then the following hold true:

(1)

The graph
\mathcal{G}
is an
\epsilon
-expander if and only if all the eigenvalues of
T(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0})
are contained inside the interval
[-1,1-\epsilon]
, or equivalently, if

\inf_{v\in V,\|v\|=1}\langle(I-T(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0}))v,v\rangle_{V}\geqslant\epsilon.

(2)

The graph
\mathcal{G}
a two-sided
\epsilon
-expander if and only if all the eigenvalues of
T(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}}^{0})
are contained in
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon].

We define the
Kazhdan distance
between a unitary representation
(\rho,V)
of
\Gamma
and the identity
\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}
by

(21)

\kappa_{S}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\max_{\gamma\in S\cup S^{-1}}\|\rho(\gamma)v-v\|.

We say that
\Gamma
has
Property (
\tau
)
with respect to a family of finite-index subgroups
(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in I}
of
\Gamma
if there exists some

such that
\kappa_{S}(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{n}}^{0},\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})\geqslant\epsilon
for all
n\in I.
Property (
\tau
) does not depend on the choice of a set of generators. In fact, one can show easily that if
S
and
S^{\prime}
are finite sets of generators of
\Gamma
, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

c_{1}\kappa_{S}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})\leqslant\kappa_{S^{\prime}}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})\leqslant c_{2}\kappa_{S}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}).

Property
(\tau)
is an important group-theoretic concept and was first defined by Lubotzky and Zimmer
[
43
]
. It can be seen as a weaker variant of Kazhdan’s Property (T), introduced in
[
34
]
, and is exactly what is needed to make the Cayley graphs

expanders:

Lemma 2.2
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\Gamma
has Property
(\tau)
with respect to
(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in I}

(ii)

For any generating subset
S
of
\Gamma
, the Cayley graphs
\mathrm{Cay}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{n},S)
form a family of expanders.

Proof.

A simple calculation shows that for every unitary representation
(\rho,V)
of
\Gamma
and for every

we have

(22)

\frac{1}{4m}\sum_{\gamma\in S\cup S^{-1}}\|v-\rho(\gamma)v\|^{2}=\langle(I-T(\rho))v,v\rangle_{V}.

It follows that

\frac{1}{4m}\kappa_{S}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})^{2}\leqslant\inf_{v\in V,\|v\|=1}\langle(I-T(\rho))v,v\rangle_{V}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{S}(\rho,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma})^{2}.

Lemma

now follows directly from Lemma

.

2.6.
Transfer operator

In what follows, let
V
be a finite-dimensional complex vector space with hermitian inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
and induced norm
\|v\|_{V}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{V}}.
Let
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
be a unitary representation. Recall that “unitary” means that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and for all
v,w\in V
we have
\langle\rho(\gamma)v,\rho(\gamma)w\rangle_{V}=\langle v,w\rangle_{V}
and in particular
\|\rho(\gamma)v\|_{V}=\|v\|_{V}
. Let
H^{2}(D,V)
be the Hilbert space of
V
-valued, square-integrable, holomorphic functions on
D=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{2m}D_{j}
:

(23)

H^{2}(D,V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\text{$F\colon D\to V$ holomorphic}\left|\;\left\|F\right\|<\infty\right.\right\}

with
L^{2}
-norm given by

\|F\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{D}\|F(z)\|_{V}^{2}\dvol(z).

Here,
\vol
denotes the Lebesgue measure on the complex plane and
\|\cdot\|_{V}
is the norm of the representation space
V
. We now define the holomorphic family of operators, parametrized by
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

(24)

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}\colon H^{2}(D,V)\to H^{2}(D,V)

by the formula

(25)

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}F(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{2m}\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(z)^{s}\rho(\gamma_{i})^{-1}F(\gamma_{i}(z))\ \mathrm{if}\ z\in D_{j}

for all
F\in H^{2}(D,V)
. Given
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
z\in D
, the composition
\rho(\gamma)^{-1}F(z)
is the result of applying the unitary endomorphism
\rho(\gamma)^{-1}\in\mathrm{U}(V)
to the vector
F(z)\in V
.

In the one-dimensional case
V=\mathbb{C}
, the functional space (
23
) reduces to the classical Bergman space
H^{2}(D)
and the operator (
24
) reduces to the classical transfer operator for Schottky groups which can be found for instance in Borthwick’s book
[
3
, Chapter 15]
. The next result relates the transfer operator to the Selberg zeta function:

Proposition 2.3

(Fredholm determinant identity)
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{C}
the operator (
24
) is trace class and we have the identity

(26)

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)=\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}).

For the trivial representation
\rho=\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}
, identities in the spirit of (
26
) are well-known in
thermodynamic formalism
, a subject going back to Ruelle
[
57
]
. The relation between the Selberg zeta function and transfer operators has been studied by a number of different authors. For the convex cocompact setting (no cusps) we refer to
[
55
,
56
,
24
]
. The extension to non-trivial twists
\rho
can be found in the more recent papers
[
20
,
54
,
49
,
48
]
. A proof of Proposition
2.3
is given in
[
32
]
.

Proposition
2.3
has the following remarkable corollary: since
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
depends holomorphically on the variable
s
it follows directly that
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
extends to an entire function. This is far from obvious from the definition of the Selberg zeta function in (
13
) as an infinite product over conjugacy classes.

2.7.
Reduced words and bounds for derivatives

We need some notations for indexing elements of the Schottky group
\Gamma
. Given a finite word

\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}

with letters
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}
in the alphabet
[2m]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{1,\dots,2m\}
, we set

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\gamma_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\gamma_{a_{N}}\in\Gamma.

Recall that
\Gamma
is freely generated by the elements
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2m}
, which is equivalent with the generators
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2m}
having no relations other than the trivial ones
\gamma_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}^{-1}=e
. Recall also that we write
\gamma_{i}^{-1}=\gamma_{i+m}
where the indices are defined modulo
2m
. We call the word
a
“reduced” if the corresponding element
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}
is reduced when viewed as a word in the alphabet
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2m}\}
. This is the case if and only if
\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}
satisfies
a_{i}\neq a_{i+1}+m\;(\mathrm{mod}\;2m)
for all
i=1,\dots,N-1
.

We denote by
W_{N}
the set of reduced words of length
N
:

(27)

W_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}:\text{ $a_{i}\neq a_{i+1}+m\;(\mathrm{mod}\;2m)$ for all $i=1,\dots,N-1$}\}.

For
j\in[2m]
we denote by
W_{N}^{j}
the set of reduced words of length
N
not ending with the letter
j
:

W_{N}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}\in W_{N}:a_{N}\neq j\}.

With these notations in place, one verifies inductively that for all
j\in[2m]
and all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(28)

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}^{N}F(z)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)^{s}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}F(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(z))\text{ if $z\in D_{j}$}.

We record the following crucial distortion estimates, referring to Naud
[
47
]
for the proofs:

Proposition 2.4

(Distortion estimates)
.

The following estimates hold true:

(1)

(Uniform hyperbolicity) There are
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
0<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<1
such that for all
N
, all
j\in[2m]
and all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
, we have

c_{1}\theta_{1}^{N}<\sup_{z\in D_{j}}|\gamma^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}(z)|<c_{2}\theta_{2}^{N}.

(2)

(Bounded distortion 1) There exists
c_{3}>0
such that for all
N
, all
j\in[2m]
, all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
and all
z\in D_{j}

\sup_{z\in D_{j}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z)}\right|\leqslant c_{3}.

(3)

(Bounded distortion 2)
There exists
c_{4}>0
such that for all words
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
and all pair of points

\left|\frac{\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z_{1})}{\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(z_{2})}\right|\leqslant c_{4}.

All the constants depend solely upon
\Gamma
.

2.8.
Topological pressure and Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius theorem

Notations being as in §
2.5
, consider the map

T\colon I\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{2m}I_{j}\rightarrow I,\quad T(x)=\gamma_{j}(x)\ \mathrm{if}\ x\in I_{j}.

This map, sometimes called the “Bowen–Series map”, encodes the dynamics of the group
\Gamma
. The origins of this type of coding go back to the work of Bowen–Series
[
10
]
. The limit set
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
can be re-interpreted as the non-wandering set of
T
:

(29)

\Lambda=\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}T^{-N}(I).

For a continuous function
\varphi\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the “topological pressure” in terms of weighted sums over periodic orbits through the formula

(30)

P(\varphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\sum_{T^{N}x=x}e^{\varphi^{(n)}(x)}\right),

where

(31)

\varphi^{(n)}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\varphi(x)+\varphi(Tx)+\ldots+\varphi(T^{n-1}x).

By the variational formula it follows that

(32)

P(\varphi)=\sup_{\mu}\left(h_{\mu}(T)-\int_{\Lambda}\varphi d\mu\right),

where
\mu
ranges over the set of
T
-invariant probability measures and
h_{\mu}(T)
is the measure theoretic entropy. We refer the reader to
[
51
]
for more background on thermodynamic formalism, including facts and properties of the topological pressure. Important for us is Bowen’s celebrated result
[
9
]
, which says that the map

(33)

P\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\sigma\mapsto P(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}P(-\sigma\log|T^{\prime}|)

is convex, strictly decreasing and vanishes precisely at
\sigma=\delta
, the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set. The relevance of the topological pressure stems from the Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius theorem:

Proposition 2.5

(Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius)
.

Set
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma,\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}}
where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
is real and
\textbf{1}_{\Gamma}
is the one-dimensional trivial representation. Then the following statements hold true:

(1)

The spectral radius of
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
on
C^{1}(I)
is equal to
e^{P(\sigma)}
which is a simple eigenvalue associated to a strictly positive
eigenfunction
\varphi_{\sigma}>0
in
C^{1}(I)
.

(2)

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
on
C^{1}(I)
is quasi-compact with essential spectral radius smaller than
\kappa(\sigma)e^{P(\sigma)}
for some

\kappa(\sigma)<1
.

(3)

There are no other eigenvalues on
|z|=e^{P(\sigma)}
.

(4)

Moreover, the spectral projector
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
on
\{e^{P(\sigma)}\}
is given by

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(f)=\varphi_{\sigma}\int_{\Lambda}fd\mu_{\sigma},

where
\mu_{\sigma}
is the unique
T
-invariant probability measure on
\Lambda
that satisfies

\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}^{*}(\mu_{\sigma})=e^{P(\sigma)}\mu_{\sigma}.

For a proof, we refer to
[
51

. An important consequence of the Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theorem is the following estimate, which is due to Naud

,

(37)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f(x)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x)),

where

(38)

w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\varphi_{\sigma}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{\sigma}}{\varphi_{\sigma}(x)e^{NP(\sigma)}}>0.

We call the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}
“normalized” because

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma,\id}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},

where
1
is the constant one function on
I.
This is equivalent with saying that for all
x\in I
and
j\in[2m]
with
x\in I_{j}
,

(39)

\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)=1.

Now, for each
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
, we define the vector

(40)

v_{\textbf{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\in V,

so that

(41)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f(x)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)v_{\textbf{a}}.

It follows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f(x)
, when regarded as a vector in
V
, lies in the convex hull spanned by the vectors
v_{\textbf{a}}\in V
. The following result is a consequence of the fact that the unit sphere in
V
is strictly convex.

Proposition 3.3

(Strict convexity)
.

Let
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
, let
(\rho,V)
be a finite-dimensional, unitary representation of
\Gamma
, and suppose
F\in H^{2}(D,V)
is a 1-eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
. Set
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F
. Choose
j\in[2m]
and
x\in I_{j}
such that

\|f(x)\|_{V}=\|f\|_{\infty}.

Then, notations being as above, we have

(42)

\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}\leqslant\sqrt{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and for all words
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
,

(43)

\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}\leqslant\sqrt{(\delta-\sigma)}e^{C^{\prime}N}\|f\|_{\infty}.

The constants
C>0
and
C^{\prime}>0
depend only on
\Gamma.

Proof.

Let
F\in H^{2}(D,V)
,
j\in[2m]
and
x\in I_{j}
be as in the statement. Then for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(44)

F=\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}^{N}F.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}
be the normalized operator in (
35
) and let
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F
. Then, equation (
44
) becomes

e^{-NP(\sigma)}f(x)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f(x).

In particular, this gives

e^{-NP(\sigma)}\|f(x)\|_{V}^{2}=\mathrm{Re}\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f(x),f(x)\right\rangle_{V}

Using (
41
) we can expand the right-hand side to obtain

(45)

e^{-NP(\sigma)}\|f(x)\|_{V}^{2}=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\mathrm{Re}\left\langle v_{\textbf{a}},f(x)\right\rangle_{V}.

Using the identity

\mathrm{Re}\left\langle v_{\textbf{a}},f(x)\right\rangle_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}+\|f(x)\|_{V}^{2}-\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}\right)

together with the bound
\|v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}
for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
yields

\mathrm{Re}\left\langle v_{\textbf{a}},f(x)\right\rangle_{V}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}.

Inserting this into (
45
) gives

\displaystyle e^{-NP(\sigma)}\|f(x)\|_{V}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\left(\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}.

Recall that
\|f(x)\|_{V}=\|f\|_{\infty}
by assumption. We can thus rearrange the previous inequality to give

(46)

\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}\leqslant 2\left(1-e^{-NP(\sigma)}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Continuity of the topological pressure
\sigma\mapsto P(\sigma)
together with the fact that
P(\delta)=0
yields for all
\sigma\leqslant\delta
,

(47)

1-e^{-NP(\sigma)}=e^{-NP(\delta)}-e^{-NP(\sigma)}\leqslant CN(\delta-\sigma).

for some
C=C(\Gamma)>0.
Inserting this into (
46
) we obtain

\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}\leqslant 2CN(\delta-\sigma)\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{2CN(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty},

which proves (
42
). In particular, this inequality clearly implies that for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
,

\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{CN(\delta-\sigma)}}{w_{\textbf{a}}(x)}\|f\|_{\infty}.

It remains to bound the weights from below. From their definition in (
38
) and from the uniform hyperbolicity property in Proposition
2.4
it follows that

(48)

w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\geqslant e^{-C^{\prime}N}

for some
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\Gamma)>0
. Inserting this into (
48
) yields

\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|_{V}\leqslant\sqrt{(\delta-\sigma)}e^{C^{\prime\prime}N}\|f\|_{\infty},

for some suitable constant
C^{\prime\prime}=C^{\prime\prime}(\Gamma)>0
, completing the proof.
∎

3.2.
Tensor power trick

Given a unitary representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-th tensor power

\rho^{\otimes k}=\underbrace{\rho\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho}_{\text{$k$ times}}

yields a new representation of
\Gamma
with representation space

V^{\otimes k}=\underbrace{V\otimes\cdots\otimes V}_{\text{$k$ times}},

by defining

\rho^{\otimes k}(\gamma)(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\rho(\gamma)v_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes(\rho(\gamma)v_{k}),\quad\gamma\in\Gamma

for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V
, and extending by linearity. We turn
V^{\otimes k}
into an inner product space by defining

\langle v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k},w_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{k}\rangle_{V^{\otimes k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\langle v_{j},w_{j}\rangle_{V}.

for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{k},w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in V
, and again extending by linearity. The representation
\rho^{\otimes k}
is unitary with respect to this inner product. Furthermore, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{V^{\otimes k}}
induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V^{\otimes k}}
satisfies

(49)

\|v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k}\|_{V^{\otimes k}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\|v_{j}\|_{V}.

We will drop the subscripts from the inner product and from the norm whenever it is clear which space is being referred to.

Lemma 3.4

(Tensor power trick)
.

Let
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
and suppose that
F\in H^{2}(D,V)
is a 1-eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
. Set
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F
. Choose
j\in[2m]
and
x\in I_{j}
such that

\|f(x)\|_{V}=\|f\|_{\infty}.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\nu,W)=(\rho^{\otimes k},V^{\otimes k})
be the
k
-th tensor power representation and put
h=f^{\otimes k}
. Then we have

(50)

\left\|h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\sigma+itk}^{N}h(x)\right\|_{W}\leqslant k\sqrt{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k},

where the constant
C>0
depends only on
\Gamma
.

We will use the following elementary estimate.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
v,w\in V
we have

\|v^{\otimes k}-w^{\otimes k}\|\leqslant k(\max\{\|v\|,\|w\|\})^{k-1}\|v-w\|.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\|v\|\leqslant 1
and
\|w\|\leqslant 1
. For
k=1
the inequality is trivial. For
k\geqslant 2
we can write

v^{\otimes k}-w^{\otimes k}=v^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes(v-w)+\left(v^{\otimes(k-1)}-w^{\otimes(k-1)}\right)\otimes w.

Using the triangle inequality and (
49
) gives

\displaystyle\|v^{\otimes k}-w^{\otimes k}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|v\|^{k-1}\|v-w\|+\|v^{\otimes(k-1)}-w^{\otimes(k-1)}\|\|w\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|v-w\|+\|v^{\otimes(k-1)}-w^{\otimes(k-1)}\|.

The result now follows by induction.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

We claim that

(51)

\left\|h(x)-\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{itk}\nu(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right\|\leqslant k\|f\|_{\infty}^{k-1}\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|

for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
. This can be derived by applying Lemma
3.5
to

w=f(x)
and
v=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))
,

and observing that

\text{$w^{\otimes k}=h(x)$ and $v^{\otimes k}=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{itk}\nu(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))$}.

Now note that we can write

h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\sigma+itk}^{N}h(x)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\left(h(x)-\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{itk}\nu(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right).

Thus, from (
51
) it follows that

\displaystyle\left\|h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\sigma+itk}^{N}h(x)\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\left\|h(x)-\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{itk}\nu(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant k\|f\|_{\infty}^{k-1}\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|f(x)-v_{\textbf{a}}\|.

By Proposition
3.3
, the sum on the last line is bounded by
\sqrt{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}
, so we obtain

\left\|h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\sigma+itk}^{N}h(x)\right\|\leqslant k\sqrt{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k},

as claimed.
∎

3.3.
High–Frequency Norm Estimate

We wish to estimate the
C^{1}
-norm of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta+it,\nu}^{N}
when
|t|
is large. Throughout this section
(\nu,W)
is a finite-dimensional, unitary representation of
\Gamma.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{W}
be the inner product of
W
with respect to which
\nu
is unitary, and let
\|v\|_{W}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{W}}
be the induced norm. Let
C^{1}(I,W)
be the set of all
f\colon I\to W
that are continuously differentiable. We endow this space with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{1}(I)}=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where the maximum norm is given as usual by
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in I}\|f(x)\|_{W}.
To define the derivative of
f\in C^{1}(I,W)
, fix an orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
of
W
, and write

f=\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}e_{k}

where
f_{k}(\cdot)=\langle f(\cdot),e_{k}\rangle_{W}\in C^{1}(I).
The derivative is then given by

f^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}^{\prime}e_{k}.

One easily verifies that
f^{\prime}
(and hence
\|f\|_{C^{1}}
) does not depend on the chosen basis.

The main result of this subsection is the following:

Proposition 3.6

(High-frequency norm estimate)
.

There are positive constants
c,\tilde{\eta},T_{0}
, depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma>\delta-\tilde{\eta}
and
|t|\geqslant T_{0}
, the following inequality holds for
N(t)=\lceil c\log|t|\rceil
:

(52)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N(t)}h\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant|t|^{-1}\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

We write
\lceil x\rceil
for the least integer greater than or equal to
x
.

We stress that the constants in Proposition
3.6
are independent of the representation
(\nu,W)
.

The main technical ingredient in the proof of this proposition is a so-called “uniform Dolgopyat” estimate, by reference to Dolgopyat’s work on Anosov flows
[
18
]
. Here, “uniformity” is meant with respect to the representation
(\nu,W)
. To state this estimate, we introduce the following warped norm on
C^{1}(I,W)
:

(53)

\|f\|_{(t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\|f\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{|t|}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Proposition 3.7

(Uniform Dolgopyat estimate, Proposition 7.3 in
[
48
]
)
.

There are positive constants
\beta,c,\eta_{0},C,T_{0},
depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
f\in C^{1}(I,W)
,
|t|\geqslant T_{0}
, and
\sigma>\delta-\eta_{0}
, the following inequality holds with
N=\lceil c\log|t|\rceil
:

(54)

\int_{\Lambda}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}f(x)\|_{W}^{2}d\mu_{\delta}(x)\leqslant C|t|^{-\beta}\|f\|_{(t)}^{2}.

Here,
\mu_{\delta}
is the Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius measure from Proposition
2.5
.

We should note that Proposition
3.7
, which we will use as a black box, was stated in
[
48
]
for
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma+it,\nu}
rather than
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}
. However, one easily verifies that the estimate for
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}
follows from the one for
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma+it,\nu}
, up to adjusting the constant
C.
Proposition
3.7
is a consequence of a a uniform non-integrability result and a deep estimate of Bourgain–Dyatlov
[
4
]
on oscillatory integrals over
\Lambda
.

While the paper of Magee–Naud
[
48
]
does not explicitly state Proposition
3.6
, it can be derived from Proposition
3.7
together with some additional estimates provided in their work. We record the argument in this section for the reader’s convenience. First we need some standard estimates.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every finite-dimensional, unitary representation
\nu\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(W)
, for every function
h\in C^{1}(I,W)
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the following estimates hold:

(1)

(Lasota-Yorke estimate)

\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll(1+|t|)\|h\|_{\infty}+e^{-cN}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

(2)

(Bound for maximum norm)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h\|_{\infty}\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}\|h(x)\|_{W}d\mu_{\delta}(x)+e^{-cN}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

The implied constants as well as the constants
C>0
and
c>0
depend only on
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Part (
1
) is well-know in the thermodynamic literature, at least for the trivial twist
\nu=\textbf{1}
. For general
\nu
, we refer to
[
48
, Lemma 7.2]
. The proof can be sketched as follows. Using (
36
) we can write down the formula for
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h
. We then differentiate this formula using the usual product and chain rules and apply the triangle inequality. The resulting terms can be estimated using the definition of the weights (
38
) together with the bounded distortion and uniform contraction properties. Uniformity with respect to
(\nu,W)
comes from the unitarity of representation
\nu
, which says that
\|\nu(\gamma)\|=1
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma.

We now concentrate on Part (
2
). The triangle inequality and the unitarity of
\nu
yield

(55)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h(x)\|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x),

where

u(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\|h(x)\|_{W}.

Given any index
j\in[2m]
and any two points
x,y\in I_{j}
, write

(56)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)=\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(y))+\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\left(u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))-u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right).

From the mean-value theorem it follows that

(57)

\|u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))-u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(y))\|\ll\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}\|_{I_{j},\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Using the definition of the weights
w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}
in (
38
) together with the distortion estimates from Proposition
2.4
, we obtain

(58)

w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{\delta}\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(y)^{\delta},

where both
C>0
and the implied constants depend only on
\Gamma.
Inserting the previous two bounds into (
56
) yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)

\displaystyle\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(y)^{\delta}u(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(y))+\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}\|_{I_{j},\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{N}u(y)+\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}w_{\textbf{a},\sigma}(x)\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}\|_{I_{j},\infty}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

To estimate the sum on the right, recall that uniform hyperbolicity implies that for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
we have
\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}\|_{I_{j},\infty}\ll e^{-cN}
for some
c=c(\Gamma)>0
, whence

(59)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{N}u(y)+e^{-cN}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Integrating both sides of this bound over
I_{j}
with respect to the
y
-variable against the measure
\mu_{\delta}
yields

\displaystyle\mu_{\delta}(I_{j})\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)

\displaystyle\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{I_{j}}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{N}u(y)d\mu_{\delta}(y)+e^{-cN}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}u(y)d\mu_{\delta}(y)+e^{-cN}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where for the last equality we used the defining property of
\mu_{\delta}
from Proposition
2.5
. By basic properties of
\mu_{\delta}
it follows that
\mu_{\delta}(I_{j})>0
for every
j\in[2m],
so after dividing by
\mu_{\delta}(I_{j})
we obtain

(60)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}u(y)d\mu_{\delta}(y)+e^{-cN}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Recalling that
u(x)=\|h(x)\|_{W}
we have

2u^{\prime}(x)u(x)=\frac{d}{dx}u(x)^{2}=2\mathrm{Re}\langle h(x),h^{\prime}(x)\rangle_{W}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
W
gives
\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.
Hence, it follows from (
55
) and (
60
) that

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta+it}^{N}h(x)\|_{W}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma}^{N}u(x)\ll e^{CN(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}\|h\|_{W}(y)d\mu_{\delta}(y)+e^{-cN}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

As
x\in I
was chosen arbitrarily, we can substitute the leftmost side with
\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta+it}^{N}h\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in I}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta+it}^{N}h(x)\|_{W}
, thereby completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

For future reference, note that we have the following a priori bounds:

(61)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h\|_{\infty}\ll\|h\|_{\infty}

and

(62)

\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll|t|\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

Now let
N\approx\log|t|
be as in Proposition
3.7
and let
M
be some positive integer to be chosen further below. Using the maximum norm bound in Lemma
3.8
together with (
62
) yields

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll e^{CM(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h)(x)\|d\mu_{\delta}(x)+e^{-cM}\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll e^{CM(\delta-\sigma)}\int_{\Lambda}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h(x)\|d\mu_{\delta}(x)+e^{-cM}|t|\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

(63)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}\ll e^{CM(\delta-\sigma)}\left(\int_{\Lambda}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{N}h(x)\|^{2}d\mu_{\delta}(x)\right)^{1/2}+e^{-cM}|t|\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

By Proposition
3.7
it follows that for all
\sigma<\delta
sufficiently close to
\delta
,

(64)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}\ll e^{CM(\delta-\sigma)}|t|^{-\beta/2}\|h\|_{(t)}+e^{-cM}|t|\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

Hence, for any
\kappa>0
large enough we can find some
\eta^{\prime}>0
with the following property: for
M=\lceil\kappa\log|t|\rceil
and
\sigma>\delta-\eta^{\prime}
,

(65)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}\ll|t|^{-\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}.

Applying the Lasota–Yorke estimate in Lemma
3.8
and then (
62
) leads to

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h))^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll|t|\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}+e^{-c(N+M)}\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll|t|\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}+e^{-c(N+M)}|t|^{2}\|h\|_{(t)}.

Inserting (
65
) into the previous line and increasing
\kappa
if necessary, this gives

(66)

\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll|t|^{1-\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}.

Using (
65
) and (
66
), as well as the a priori estimate (
61
), it follows that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h\|_{(t)}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{|t|}\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{M+N}h\|_{\infty}+|t|^{-\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}

\displaystyle\ll|t|^{-\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}.

To summarize, we have shown so far that

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2(M+N)}h\|_{(t)}\ll|t|^{-\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}.

We can apply this bound repeatedly
k
times for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2k(M+N)}h\|_{(t)}\ll_{k}|t|^{-k\beta/3}\|h\|_{(t)}.

But now, using the trivial estimate

(67)

\|g\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant|t|\|g\|_{(t)}\leqslant|t|\|g\|_{C^{1}(I)},

valid for any
g\in C^{1}(I,W)
, we get

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2k(M+N)}h\|_{C^{1}(I)}

\displaystyle\leqslant|t|\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2k(M+N)}h\|_{(t)}

\displaystyle\ll_{k}|t|^{-k\beta/3+1}\|h\|_{(t)}

\displaystyle\leqslant|t|^{-k\beta/3+1}\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

Taking
k=\lceil 6\beta^{-1}\rceil
gives

(68)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+it,\nu}^{2k(M+N)}h\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant|t|^{-1}\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)},

completing the proof of Proposition
3.6
.
∎

3.4.
Basic a priori estimate

We continue with a basic estimate. It controls the maximum norm of
f^{\prime}
in terms of the maximum norm of
f
, where
f
is the normalized 1-eigenfunction of the twisted transfer operator.

Lemma 3.9

(A priori estimate)
.

Let
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma\in[0,\delta]
, let
(\nu,W)
be a finite-dimensional, unitary representation of
\Gamma
, and suppose that
F\in H^{2}(D,W)
is a 1-eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}_{s,\nu}
. Set
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F.
Then there are constants
A=A(\Gamma)>0
and
C=C(\Gamma)>0
such that

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{A}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Consider a 1-eigenfunction
F
of
\mathcal{L}_{s,\nu}
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathcal{L}_{s,\nu}^{N}F=F.

Upon normalizing this can be expressed equivalently as

e^{-NP(\sigma)}f=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\nu}f.

Using the Lasota-Yorke estimate (Part
1
of Lemma
3.8
) we get

e^{-NP(\sigma)}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C^{\prime}(1+|t|)\left(\|f\|_{\infty}+e^{-(c+P(\sigma))N}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)

for some constants
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\Gamma)>0
and
c=c(\Gamma)>0
, or equivalently,

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C^{\prime}(1+|t|)\left(e^{NP(\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}+e^{-cN}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right).

Taking
N=\lceil C^{\prime\prime}\log(1+|t|)\rceil
with
C^{\prime\prime}=C^{\prime\prime}(\Gamma)>0
so large that

C^{\prime}(1+|t|)e^{-cN}<\frac{1}{2}

holds, we obtain

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C^{\prime}(1+|t|)e^{NP(\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

This can be rearranged to give

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2C^{\prime}(1+|t|)e^{NP(\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant C_{0}(1+|t|)^{A}\|f\|_{\infty},

for some
A=A(\Gamma)>0
, as claimed.
∎

3.5.
Finishing the proof of Theorem
3.1

We now finish the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Fix a unitary representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
and suppose that
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
vanishes at
s=\sigma+it
. By Proposition
2.3
and standard properties of Fredholm determinants, zeros of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
correspond to non-trivial 1-eigenfunctions of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
, i.e., there is some non-zero
F\in C^{1}(I,V)
satisfying

(69)

\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}^{N}F=F

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Writing
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F
and letting
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}
denote the normalized operator as in §
3.1
, equation (
69
) becomes

(70)

e^{-NP(\sigma)}f=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N}f.

We may assume without loss of generality that
t\neq 0.
Let
T_{0}
be the constant from Proposition
3.6
. We may assume for the rest of this proof that
T_{0}>1.
Let us first deal with the case
|t|\geqslant T_{0}>1
Then, by Proposition
3.6
, there are
\tilde{\eta}>0
and
c>0
, depending only on
\Gamma
, such that for all
\sigma>\delta-\tilde{\eta}
the following inequality holds with
N_{0}=\lceil c\log|t|\rceil
:

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{s,\rho}^{N_{0}}f\|_{C^{1}(I,V)}\leqslant|t|^{-1}\|f\|_{C^{1}(I,V)}.

Combining this with (
70
) and using the fact that
f\neq 0
it follows that
e^{-N_{0}P(\sigma)}\leqslant|t|^{-1}
. Upon rearranging, this yields

(71)

1\leqslant|t|^{-1+cP(\sigma)},

which implies

(72)

P(\sigma)\geqslant c^{-1}.

Since
\sigma\mapsto P(\sigma)
is strictly decreasing with a zero at
\sigma=\delta
, this forces

(73)

\sigma\leqslant\delta-\eta_{1}

for some constant
\eta_{1}=\eta_{1}(\Gamma)>0
.

Now assume alternatively that
|t|<T_{0}.
In this case we apply the tensor power trick in §
3.2
. Put

k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\lceil\frac{2T_{0}}{|t|}\right\rceil\in\mathbb{N}

and define the
k
-th tensor powers

(74)

(\nu,W)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\rho^{\otimes k},V^{\otimes k})
and
h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}f^{\otimes k}\in C^{1}(I,W)
.

Clearly, we have
\frac{2T_{0}}{|t|}\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{2T_{0}}{|t|}+1
, so
|t|<T_{0}
implies

2<2T_{0}\leqslant k|t|\leqslant 3T_{0}.

Hence, by Proposition
3.6
, there are
N_{0}=O(1)
and
\tilde{\eta}>0
with the following property: for all
\sigma>\delta-\tilde{\eta}
and all
g\in C^{1}(I,W)
,

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{N_{0}}g\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant(k|t|)^{-1}\|g\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_{C^{1}}.

Applying this repeatedly
l
times for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
it follows that

(75)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{lN_{0}}g\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant 2^{-l}\|g\|_{C^{1}(I)}.

To proceed, let us estimate the
C^{1}
-norm of
h
. Given vector spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, observe that by the definition of the tensor product, the following holds for all
f_{1}\in C(I,V_{1})
and
f_{2}\in C(I,V_{2})
:

(76)

\|f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\|_{\infty}=\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}.

By repeatedly applying this rule to
h=f^{\otimes k}
we obtain

(77)

\|h\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

Moreover, the derivative of
h
is equal to

h^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i},\quad h_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\underbrace{f\otimes\cdots\otimes f^{\prime}\otimes\cdots\otimes f}_{\text{derivative at $i$-th place}}.

This follows from applying the product rule to the following identity: for any
\textbf{v}=v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k}
and
\textbf{w}=w_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{k}
, the inner product
\langle h(x)\textbf{v},\textbf{w}\rangle
can be expressed as the product of individual inner products
\langle f(x)v_{i},w_{i}\rangle
:

\langle h(x)\textbf{v},\textbf{w}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\langle f(x)v_{i},w_{i}\rangle.

Using (
76
) once again it follows that
\|h_{i}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k-1}
and so the triangle inequality yields

(78)

\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|h_{i}\|_{\infty}\leqslant k\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k-1}.

By Lemma
3.9
we have

\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll\|f\|_{\infty},

which when inserted into (
78
) yields

\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll k\|f\|^{k}.

We conclude that the
C^{1}
-norm of
h
is bounded by

(79)

\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}=\|h\|_{\infty}+\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll k\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

Next, using this bound and applying (
75
) to
h
yields

(80)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{lN_{0}}h\|_{C^{1}(I)}\ll 2^{-l}\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}\ll k2^{-l}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

We may therefore choose some
l\in\mathbb{N}
of size at most

l=O(\log k)=O(\log(T_{0}|t|^{-1}))

such that

(81)

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{lN_{0}}h\|_{C^{1}(I)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

Now choose
x\in I
for which
\|f(x)\|_{V}=\|f\|_{\infty}
. This choice also gives

\left\|h(x)\right\|_{W}=\left\|f(x)\right\|_{V}^{k}=\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

The triangle inequality and (
81
) yield

\displaystyle\left\|h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{N_{0}l}h(x)\right\|_{W}

\displaystyle\geqslant\|h(x)\|_{W}-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{lN_{0}}h(x)\|_{W}

\displaystyle\geqslant\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{lN_{0}}h(x)\|_{W}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

On the other hand, Lemma
3.4
tells us that

\left\|h(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\sigma+itk,\nu}^{N_{0}l}h(x)\right\|_{W}\leqslant k\sqrt{CN_{0}l(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

Combining the previous two estimates, we arrive at

(82)

\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}\leqslant k\sqrt{CN_{0}l(\delta-\sigma)}\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}.

Dividing both sides by
\|f\|_{\infty}^{k}\neq 0
yields

(83)

\frac{1}{2}\leqslant k\sqrt{CN_{0}l(\delta-\sigma)},

or equivalently,

(84)

\sigma\leqslant\delta-\frac{1}{4Ck^{2}N_{0}l}.

Finally, by our choices of
k
and
l
we obtain

\frac{1}{4Ck^{2}N_{0}l}=O_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{|t|^{2}}{\log|t|^{-1}}\right)

as
|t|\to 0
. Combining (
73
) and (
84
), and taking
\eta_{\infty}=\min\{\eta_{1},\tilde{\eta}\}
, we conclude that

\sigma\leqslant\delta-\eta(t),

for some function
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties:

•

\eta(0)=0
and
\eta(t)>0
for all
t\neq 0
,

•

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\eta(t)=\eta_{\infty}
, and

•

\eta(t)=O_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{|t|^{2}}{\log|t|^{-1}}\right)
as
|t|\to 0
.

For definiteness, we may take

\eta(t)=\eta_{\infty}\frac{\xi(t)}{1+\xi(t)}

with
\xi(t)=\frac{|t|^{2}}{\log\left(C+\frac{1}{|t|}\right)}
for some suitably large constant
C>1
depending only upon
\Gamma.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
is complete.

4.
Exploiting the expansion property

Recall the notation from §
2
. Let
\Gamma
is a non-elementary Schottky group with generators
S=\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}\}
. Let us recall some of the notation. We put
I_{j}=D_{j}\cap\mathbb{R}
where
D_{1},\dots,D_{2m}
are the Schottky disks in the construction of
\Gamma
and
I=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{2m}I_{j}
. The non-identity elements of
\Gamma
are in bijection to set of reduced words
W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigsqcup_{N=0}^{\infty}W_{N}
in the alphabet
[2m]
, where
W_{N}
is the set of
reduced
words of length
N
,

W_{N}=\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}:\text{ $a_{i}\neq a_{i+1}+m\;(\mathrm{mod}\;2m)$ for all $i=1,\dots,N-1$}\},

We denote by
W_{N}^{j}\subseteq W^{N}
the subset of those words not ending with the letter
j
. Moreover, we use
\mathbb{E}_{a\in A}f(a)
as a shorthand for the average

\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}f(a).

The goal of this section is to prove estimates for averages of the type

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right),

where
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
is a finite-dimensional unitary representation and
h\in C^{1}(I,V)
. These estimates will be crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4.1.
Representations and sums over words

Given a finite-dimensional unitary representation
(\rho,V)
of
\Gamma
and a finite set of reduced words
Z\subset W
, put

Z[\rho]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\textbf{a}\in Z}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}).

Note that
Z[\rho]
is an element of the endomorphism ring
\mathrm{End}(V)
. Applying the triangle inequality yields

\|Z[\rho]\|\leqslant|Z|,

where we write
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{V\to V}
for the usual operator norm.

Proposition 4.1

(Norm estimate for
W_{N}
)
.

Assume that the self-adjoint operator

T(\rho)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{2m}\rho(\gamma_{j})=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\rho(\gamma_{j})+\rho(\gamma_{j})^{-1}\right)\in\mathrm{End}(V)

has all its eigenvalues inside
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
. Then the operator norm of
W_{N}[\rho]
is bounded by

\|W_{N}[\rho]\|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}N}|W_{N}|

with implied constant depending on
m
and
\epsilon
but not on
N.

Proof.

Clearly
W_{1}[\rho]=2m\,T(\rho).
Thus, by assumption, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}
of
W_{1}[\rho]
satisfy the bound

(85)

\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\lambda_{k}|\leqslant 2m(1-\epsilon),

which we will use later in the proof.

Consider the following formal power series with coefficients in the endomorphism ring
\mathrm{End}(V):

(86)

G_{\rho}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}W_{N}[\rho]z^{N},

where
W_{0}[\rho]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}I_{V}
is the identity operator. An elementary argument shows that

|W_{N}|=2m(2m-1)^{N-1}\asymp(2m-1)^{N}.

Together with the trivial bound

\|W_{N}[\rho]\|\leqslant|W_{N}|,

it follows that the series in (
86
) is absolutely convergent with respect to the operator norm whenever
|z|<\frac{1}{2m-1}
.

Next we derive a closed-form expression for
G_{\rho}(z)
. To that effect we use the elementary but essential recursion formulas
3
3

3

These recursion formulas were used by Ihara
[
28
]
in his derivation of a closed-form expression of what came to be known as the Ihara zeta function
:

(87)

W_{1}[\rho]^{2}=W_{2}[\rho]+2m\cdot I_{V}

and for all
N\geqslant 2
,

(88)

W_{1}[\rho]W_{N}[\rho]=W_{N+1}[\rho]+(2m-1)W_{N-1}[\rho].

Multiplying
G_{\rho}(z)
on the right by
zW_{1}[\rho]
and applying (
87
) and (
88
) gives us

(89)

\displaystyle zW_{1}[\rho]G_{\rho}(z)

\displaystyle=W_{1}[\rho]z+W_{1}[\rho]^{2}z^{2}+\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}W_{1}[\rho]W_{N}[\rho]z^{N+1}

(90)

\displaystyle=W_{1}[\rho]z+(W_{2}[\rho]+2m\cdot I_{V})z^{2}

(91)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}W_{N+1}[\rho]z^{N+1}+(2m-1)\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}W_{N-1}[\rho]z^{N+1}.

Note that

\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}W_{N+1}[\rho]z^{N+1}=G_{\rho}(z)-W_{2}[\rho]z^{2}-W_{1}[\rho]z-I_{V}

and

\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}W_{N-1}[\rho]z^{N+1}=z^{2}\left(G_{\rho}(z)-I_{V}\right).

Inserting these two equations back into (
91
) we arrive at

(92)

zW_{1}[\rho]G_{\rho}(z)=(z^{2}-1)I_{V}+G_{\rho}(z)\left(1+(2m-1)z^{2}\right),

which, upon rearranging, gives the closed-form expression

G_{\rho}(z)=(1-z^{2})\left(I_{V}-W_{1}[\rho]z+(2m-1)z^{2}\right)^{-1}.

Since
W_{1}[\rho]
is a self-adjoint operator there exists a basis of
V
with respect to which it acts by the diagonal matrix

W_{1}[\rho]=\diag(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}).

With respect to this basis we have the expression

(93)

G_{\rho}(z)=\diag\left(\frac{1-z^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}z+(2m-1)z^{2}}\right)_{k=1}^{d}.

For each
k\in[d]
let
\omega^{\pm}_{k}
be the pair of complex numbers satisfying

1-\lambda_{k}z+(2m-1)z^{2}=(1-\omega_{k}^{+}z)(1-\omega_{k}^{-}z).

We can then expand each diagonal entry of (
93
) as a power series in
z
as follows:

\frac{1-z^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}z+(2m-1)z^{2}}=\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1-\omega_{k}^{+}z)(1-\omega_{k}^{-}z)}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}(\xi_{k,N}-\xi_{k,N-2})z^{N},

where
\xi_{k,-1}=\xi_{k,-2}=0
and

(94)

\xi_{k,N}=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\left(\omega_{k}^{+}\right)^{l}\left(\omega_{k}^{-}\right)^{N-l}

for all non-negative integers
N
. Comparing coefficients in (
93
) and (
86
) yields

W_{N}[\rho]=\diag\left(\xi_{k,N}-\xi_{k,N-2}\right)_{k=1}^{d}.

Since the operator norm of a diagonal matrix is equal to the largest absolute values of its entries we obtain

(95)

\|W_{N}[\rho]\|=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\xi_{k,N}-\xi_{k,N-2}|.

A direct computation shows that

(96)

\omega_{k}^{\pm}=\frac{\lambda_{k}\pm\sqrt{\lambda_{k}^{2}-4(2m-1)}}{2}.

If
|\lambda_{k}|\leqslant 2\sqrt{2m-1}
it follows directly from this formula that

|\omega_{k}^{\pm}|=\sqrt{2m-1}.

Now suppose alternatively that
|\lambda_{j}|>2\sqrt{2m-1}
. In this case we simply recall from (
85
) that

|\lambda_{j}|\leqslant 2m(1-\epsilon),

which implies that

|\omega_{k}^{\pm}|\leqslant\frac{|\lambda_{k}|+\sqrt{(2m)^{2}-4(2m-1)}}{2}\leqslant(2m-1)\left(1-\frac{m}{2m-1}\epsilon\right).

In any case we have

(97)

|\omega_{k}^{\pm}|\leqslant(2m-1)(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3})<(2m-1)e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{3}}.

Therefore, by (
94
),

\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\xi_{k,N}|\leqslant N\left(\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\omega_{k}^{\pm}|\right)^{N}\leqslant Ne^{-\frac{\epsilon}{3}N}(2m-1)^{N}\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}N}|W_{N}|.

Returning to (
95
) we therefore end up with

\|W_{N}[\rho]\|\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\xi_{k,N}|+\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}|\xi_{k,N-2}|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}N}|W_{N}|,

which completes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
∎

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
and for every pair
(i,j)\in[2m]^{2}
consider the set of reduced words of length
N
starting with
i
and ending with
j
:

(98)

A_{N}^{i,j}=\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}\in W_{N}:a_{1}=i,a_{N}=j\}.

It turns out that in our application it is more useful to have an upper bound for the norm of
A_{N}^{i,j}[\rho]
rather than one for
W_{N}[\rho]
.

Corollary 4.2

(Norm estimate for
A_{N}^{i,j}
)
.

Let
(\rho,V)
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and assume that
T(\rho)
has all its eigenvalues in
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
. For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
(i,j)\in[2m]^{2}
the operator norm of
A_{N}^{i,j}[\rho]
is bounded by

\|A_{N}^{i,j}[\rho]\|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N}|W_{N}|,

where the implied constant depends only on
m
and
\epsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[2m]
and consider the set of reduced words of length
N
starting with the letter
i,

B_{N}^{i}=\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}\in W_{N}:a_{1}=i\}.

Observe that

B_{N}^{i}[\rho]=\rho(\gamma_{i})\left(W_{N-1}[\rho]-B_{N-1}^{i+m}[\rho]\right).

Applying the triangle inequality and using the fact that
\rho
is unitary yields

(99)

\|B_{N}^{i}[\rho]\|\leqslant\|W_{N-1}[\rho]\|+\|B_{N-1}^{i+m}[\rho]\|.

Hence, setting

(100)

\mathfrak{b}_{N}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 2m}\|B_{N}^{i}[\rho]\|,

gives

\mathfrak{b}_{N}(\rho)\leqslant\|W_{N-1}[\rho]\|+\mathfrak{b}_{N-1}(\rho).

Applying this inequality repeatedly yields

(101)

\mathfrak{b}_{N}(\rho)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|W_{k}[\rho]\|.

By Proposition
4.1
,

\|W_{k}[\rho]\|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}k}|W_{k}|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}k}(2m-1)^{k},

where the implied constant does not depend on
k
. Inserting this into (
101
), and using a geometric series summation together with
|W_{N}|\asymp(2m-1)^{N}
, yields

(102)

\mathfrak{b}_{N}(\rho)\ll\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{4}k}(2m-1)^{k}\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{5}N}(2m-1)^{N}\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{5}N}|W_{N}|.

Now fix
(i,j)\in[2m]^{2}
, let
A_{N}^{i,j}
be as in (
98
), and observe that

(103)

A_{N}^{i,j}[\rho]=\left(B_{N-1}^{i}[\rho]-A_{N}^{i,j+m}[\rho]\right)\rho(\gamma_{j}).

Thus, setting

\mathfrak{a}_{N}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 2m}\|A_{N}^{i,j}[\rho]\|,

it follows from (
103
) that

\mathfrak{a}_{N}(\rho)\leqslant\mathfrak{b}_{N-1}(\rho)+\mathfrak{a}_{N-1}(\rho).

Applying this bound repeatedly gives

\mathfrak{a}_{N}(\rho)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\rho).

Using (
102
) and arguing as above it follows that

\mathfrak{a}_{N}(\rho)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{5}k}|W_{k}|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N}|W_{N}|,

concluding the proof of Corollary
4.2
.
∎

4.2.
Exponential decay for twisted averages

We now state and prove the key result of this section.

Proposition 4.3

(Exponential decay for twisted averages)
.

Let
(\rho,V)
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
. Suppose that the operator
T(\rho)
has all of its eigenvalues within the interval
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
. Then, for all
h\in C^{1}(I,V)
, for all
x\in I
and for all
j\in[2m]
such that
x\in I_{j}
, the following inequality holds:

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}\ll e^{-c\epsilon N}\|h\|_{C^{1}}.

The implied constant and
c>0
depend solely on
\Gamma.

Proof.

We define for each pair of indices
(l,j)\in[2m]^{2}
the following set:

Z_{N}^{l,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\textbf{a}=a_{1}\cdots a_{N}\in W_{N}:\text{$a_{1}=l+m$ and $a_{N}\neq j$}\},

This set comprises all words
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}
such that
\gamma_{\textbf{a}}
maps
D_{j}
into the interior of
D_{l}
. We can re-express it as

Z_{N}^{l,j}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j^{\prime}\leqslant 2m\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}A_{N}^{l+m,j^{\prime}},

where
A_{N}^{i,j}
are the sets that we have introduced in (
98
). Thus, by Corollary
4.2
,

\left\|Z_{N}^{l,j}[\rho]\right\|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j^{\prime}\leqslant 2m\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}\left\|A_{N}^{l+m,j^{\prime}}[\rho]\right\|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N}|W_{N}|.

One easily verifies that
Z_{N}^{l,j}
and
W_{N}^{j}
are of comparable size, i.e.,
|Z_{N}^{l,j}|\asymp(2m-1)^{N}\asymp|W_{N}^{j}|
with implied constants independent of
N
. It follows that

(104)

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in Z_{N}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})\right)\right\|=\frac{1}{|Z_{N}^{l,j}|}\left\|Z_{N}^{l,j}[\rho]\right\|\ll\frac{1}{|W_{N}^{j}|}\left\|Z_{N}^{l,j}[\rho]\right\|\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N}.

We will use this estimate further below.

To proceed we use a decoupling argument similar to the one contained in
[
6
, Section 8]
. Write
N=N_{1}+N_{2},
where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
will be chosen further below, and observe that for every
j\in[2m]
and
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
there are unique
l\in[2m]
and
\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}
and
\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}
such that
\textbf{a}=\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}
, where
\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}
stands for the concatenation of
\textbf{a}_{2}
and
\textbf{a}_{2}
. Therefore, for all
x\in I
,

\sum_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))=\sum_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\sum_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x)).

Note that

|W_{N}^{j}|\asymp|W_{N_{1}}^{l}||Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}|

with implied constants depending only on
m,
so we obtain

(105)

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}\ll\sum_{l=1}^{2m}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Sigma_{l}}.

It suffices to establish the bound

\Sigma_{l}\ll e^{-c\epsilon N}\|h\|_{C^{1}(I)}

for some constant
c=c(\Gamma)>0.
We rewrite

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))\right)

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left[\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\left(h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)

Applying the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}\leqslant

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left\|h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right\|_{V}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}.

Thus we have

\Sigma_{l}\leqslant\Sigma_{l}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{l}^{(2)},

where

\Sigma_{l}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left\|h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right\|_{V}

and

\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}.

First we consider
\Sigma_{l}^{(1)}
. From the definition of
Z_{n}^{l,j}
it follows that for all
\textbf{a}_{2},\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{n_{2}}^{l,j}
, both
\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}}(x)
and
\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{2}}(y)
belong to the same interval
I_{l}
. For any pair of points
x_{1},x_{2}
in same interval, the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

\|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\|_{V}=\left\|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}h^{\prime}(u)du\right\|_{V}\leqslant|x_{1}-x_{2}|\cdot\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Therefore, given
\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{n_{1}}^{l}
and
\textbf{a}_{2},\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{n_{2}}^{l,j}
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}}(x)))-h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{2}}(x)))\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))-\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))|\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}}(x)-\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{2}}(x)|\sup_{z\in D_{l}}|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}(z)|\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ll\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,I_{l}}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{l}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left\|h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{a}_{2}}(x))-h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left\|h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}}(x)))-h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}(\gamma_{\textbf{b}_{2}}(x)))\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\ll\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,I_{l}}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\|\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty,I_{l}}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Using the pressure estimate (Lemma
2.6
) we obtain

(106)

S_{l}^{(1)}\ll\frac{e^{N_{1}P(1)}}{|W_{N_{1}}^{l}|}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll e^{-c^{\prime}N_{1}}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

with
c^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-P(1)+P(0)>0.

Let us now concentrate on
\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}.
Note that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\right)\cdot\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}})^{-1}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}.

The triangle inequality implies that

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right\|_{V}\leqslant\|h\|_{\infty}.

Since
\rho
is unitary we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\right)\right\|\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{b}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}h(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{1}\textbf{b}_{2}}(x))\right\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\right)\right\|\|h\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{1}\in W_{N_{1}}^{l}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})\right)\right\|\|h\|_{\infty}.

In the last line we used that for any
\textbf{A}\in\mathrm{End}(W)
the operator norm satisfies
\|\textbf{A}^{\ast}\|=\|\textbf{A}\|
, where
\textbf{A}^{\ast}
denotes the adjoint of
A
, as well as

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{-1}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})^{\ast}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}_{2}\in Z_{N_{2}}^{l,j}}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}_{2}})\right)^{\ast},

which is once again a consequence of unitarity of
\rho.
Hence, invoking the estimate (
104
), we obtain

(107)

\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N_{2}}\|h\|_{\infty}.

Putting together (
106
) and (
107
) yields

(108)

\Sigma_{l}\leqslant\Sigma_{l}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{l}^{(2)}\ll e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{6}N_{2}}\|h\|_{\infty}+e^{-c^{\prime}N_{1}}\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Finally, taking
N_{1}=\lceil\frac{\epsilon}{6c^{\prime}+\epsilon}N\rceil
and
N_{2}=N-N_{1},
gives

(109)

\Sigma_{l}\ll e^{-\frac{c^{\prime}}{6c^{\prime}+\epsilon}\epsilon N}\left(\|h\|_{\infty}+\|h^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)=e^{-\frac{c^{\prime}}{6c^{\prime}+1}\epsilon N}\|h\|_{C^{1}}

for all
l\in[2m]
, completing the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We are now prepared to prove our main theorem. Fix a non-elementary Schottky group
\Gamma
with Schottky generating set
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}
as in §
2.5
, fix a finite-dimensional, unitary representation
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V),
and put

T(\rho)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{2m}\left(\rho(\gamma_{j})+\rho(\gamma_{j})^{-1}\right).

In view of the Venkov–Zograf induction formula (
16
) and Lemma
2.1
, Theorem
1.1
is a direct consequence of the following more general result:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let notations and assumptions be as above. Assume that all the eigenvalues of
T(\rho)
are inside the interval
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
for some
\epsilon>0
. Then,
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
has no zeros in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geqslant\delta-\exp(-c\epsilon^{-1}),
where the constant
c>0
depends only on
\Gamma.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and a finite-dimensional unitary representation
(\rho,V)
of
\Gamma
such that all the eigenvalues of
T(\rho)
are inside
[-1+\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
. Suppose that
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
has a complex zero
s=\sigma+it
satisfying
\sigma\geqslant\delta-\eta
. Our goal is to arrive at a contradiction with
\eta=\exp(-c\epsilon^{-1})
for some positive constant
c=c(\Gamma)>0
. Throughout the proof, all implied constants depend solely upon
\Gamma
.

Let
\eta_{0}>0
be the constant from Corollary
3.2
. We may assume that
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0}),
in which case

(110)

|t|=O((\delta-\sigma)^{1/3})=O(\eta^{1/3}).

(The exponent
1/3
may be replaced by any exponent strictly smaller than
1/2
, but
1/3
is enough for our purposes.)

Now we argue as in §
3.5
. If
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)=0
then
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}
has some non-trivial 1-eigenfunction, that is, there exists some
0\neq F\in H^{2}(D,V)
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{s,\rho}F=F
. Put
f=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}F
, let
x\in I=\bigsqcup_{j\in[2m]}I_{j}
be a maximizer of the function
x\mapsto\|f(x)\|_{V}
, and let
j\in[2m]
be the index for which
x\in I_{j}.
By Proposition
3.3
, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
, the norm of

f(x)-\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))

is bounded from above by

O(e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\|f\|_{\infty}),

where the implied constant depends only on
\Gamma
. We simply write this as

(111)

f(x)=\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))+O(e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\|f\|_{\infty}).

The uniform hyperbolicity property in Proposition
2.4
implies that for all
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j},

|\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}-1|=|e^{it\log\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)}-1|\ll|t||\log\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)|\ll N|t|.

Combining this with (
110
) it follows that

\gamma_{\textbf{a}}^{\prime}(x)^{it}=1+O(N|t|)=1+O\left(N\eta^{1/3}\right),

which when inserted into (
111
) gives

f(x)=\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))+O\left(\left(N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\right).

Now we average this over all words
\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}
:

(112)

f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right)+O\left(\left(N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\right).

Taking the norm of
V
on both sides leads to

\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{a}\in W_{N}^{j}}\left(\rho(\gamma_{\textbf{a}})^{-1}f(\gamma_{\textbf{a}}(x))\right)\right\|_{V}+O\left(\left(N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\right).

Invoking Proposition
4.3
gives

(113)

\|f\|_{\infty}=O\left(e^{-c\epsilon N}\|f\|_{C^{1}}+\left(N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\right).

By Lemma
3.9
we have

\|f\|_{C^{1}}=O(\|f\|_{\infty}),

which when inserted into (
113
) yields

\|f\|_{\infty}=O\left(\left(e^{-c\epsilon N}+N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\right).

Finally, as
f\neq 0
, we can divide both sides by
\|f\|_{\infty}\neq 0
which gives

1\leqslant C\left(e^{-c\epsilon N}+N\eta^{1/3}+e^{C_{0}N}\sqrt{\eta}\right)

for some constant
C=C(\Gamma)>0
. To arrive at the desired contradiction we can now choose
N=\lceil c_{0}\epsilon^{-1}\rceil
and
\eta=\exp(-c_{1}\epsilon^{-1})
with some sufficiently large constants
c_{0}>0
and
c_{1}>0
depending only on
C
and
C_{0}
. The proof of Theorem
5.1
is complete.
∎
