Irreversible and dissipative systems
J. Beck, W.W.L. Chen, Y. Yang

Abstract
. We study some new dynamical systems where the corresponding piecewise linear flow is neither time reversible nor measure preserving. We create a dissipative system by starting with a finite polysquare translation surface, and then modifying it by including a one-sided barrier on a common vertical edge of two adjacent atomic squares, in the form of a union of finitely many intervals. The line flow in this system partitions the system into a transient set and a recurrent set. We are interested in the geometry of these two sets.

11K38, 37E35

1.
Introduction

We continue our research into non-integrable systems concerning billiard flow in polygons and solids
as well as the related problem of geodesic flow on flat surfaces and in flat manifolds.
In the cases that we have studied thus far, the flow is piecewise linear, time reversible and measure preserving.
In particular, the last property permits the application of the Birkhoff ergodic theorem, and this leads to uniformity
of the relevant orbits inside flow-invariant sets.

In this paper, we study some new dynamical systems where the corresponding piecewise linear flow is
neither time reversible nor measure preserving.
Here the work of Veech
[
9
]
concerning the
2
-circle problem serves as our motivation.
Related to the
2
-circle problem is a polysquare translation surface comprising
2
atomic squares,
modified by the introduction of some partial barriers on some edges of the squares.
The question then arises as to what may happen if these barriers are one-sided, permitting flow in one direction
but not the other.
We show that these one-sided barriers are responsible for the violation of
both the time reversible and the measure preserving properties.

Dynamical systems with such long term behaviour are said to be
dissipative
.
Intuitively, it means that the time evolution of the flow contracts the space-volume.

The
Lorentz attractor
, motivated by weather prediction and claiming that the time evolution contracts
the space-volume exponentially fast, is perhaps famous and notorious with equal measure.
It is the quintessential mathematical model of
chaos theory
which has a huge literature.
Thus it is somewhat unsatisfactory that little has been proved with any mathematical rigour concerning the Lorentz attractor.

Our flat dissipative systems here are quite different, and exhibit slow contraction of the space-volume,
so we may use the term
slow chaos
.
However, we are able to carry out a rigorous mathematical discussion on these systems with precise proofs.

Time irreversibility is a very interesting property that brings one to the well known
Loschmidt paradox
,
or reversibility-irreversibility paradox, which serves as a criticism of the so-called H-theorem of Boltzmann.
Here a fundamental problem of the
proof
of Boltzmann is
circular reasoning
and concerns an element of time asymmetry
artifically injected into an argument to establish time asymmetry.

The Loschmidt paradox is still fresh after
150
years, perhaps due to the lack of any time irreversible
mathematical model with a detailed theory providing theorems and proofs.
We hope our work here will bring a little insight into this very intriguing question.

We introduce a dissipative system
\mathcal{P}
by starting first with a finite polysquare translation surface,
and then modifying it by including a one-sided barrier
B
on a common vertical edge of two adjacent atomic squares,
in the form of a union of finitely many vertical intervals, and where a set
A
corresponding to
B
is chosen
on a different vertical edge on the same horizontal street that contains
B
.
We consider a flow moving from left to right.
When a flow line with irrational slope hits the barrier
B
from the left, it continues from the corresponding point of the set
A
.
We then observe that there are points on
\mathcal{P}
that stay away from the flow line, apart possibly from the early stages,
and there are points on
\mathcal{P}
with arbitrarily small neighbourhoods that are visited by the flow line infinitely often
as time goes to infinity.
The system
\mathcal{P}
is thus divided into two subsets, and we are interested in the structure of these two subsets
which we call respectively the
transient
set and the
recurrent
set.

We begin our investigation by considering in Section
2
the special case of the
2
-square torus,
modified by the inclusion of a one-sided barrier on the common vertical edge.
We show that there is a simple reverse flow recipe which helps us investigate this question,
and that both the transient set and the recurrent set are finite unions of polygons with total area
1

and with boundary edges that are either vertical or parallel to the direction of the flow.
We also explain how this problem also possesses a form of
cyclic symmetry
.

We then proceed in Section
3
to investigate the corresponding problem with the
n
-square torus
modified in a similar way.
Here we may lose the cyclic symmetry which is crucial in the earlier investigation.
Nevertheless we are able to establish some partial results.
Then in Section
4
, we consider the more general problem of starting with a polysquare translation surface,
and use simple surfaces related to the L-surface to illustrate some properties of the transient sets and the recurrent sets.
We show that each can take up a very large proportion of the system or a very small proportion of the system,
and that the recurrent set can be split into disjoint components.
We then make a thorough study of the case of the modified L-surface.

In Section
5
, we study the problem of modified polysquare translation surfaces in greater detail.
Here we develop an extension process which is iterative and helps us study more closely the recurrent set.
We also use some important results of Kakutani and Kac.
However, the main question is whether this extension process is finite, in which case we can conclude that
both the transient set and the recurrent set are finite unions of polygons
and with boundary edges that are either vertical or parallel to the direction of the flow.

Section
6
is devoted to the study of this question.
We begin this investigation by studying two special cases of the problem,
one by making some assumptions on the one-sided barrier and the other by making some assumption on the recurrent set.
We conclude our investigation by finally showing that the extension process is indeed always finite,
and this leads to the very nice description of the transient sets and the recurrent sets.

, where

Convention
.

Henceforth we consider
dissipative systems
involving
1
-direction
flow from left to right and with irrational slope
\alpha>0
.
When the
dissipative flow line
hits a one-sided barrier, it then continues from the corresponding point on the left vertical edge
of the system and in the same direction.

Consider the dissipative system
\mathcal{P}
obtained from the
2
-square torus modified by the inclusion of a one-sided barrier
which may include both, one or neither of the endpoints
(1,a)
and
(1,b)
, as shown in Figure 5.
We see that the two distinct flow line segments below the dotted line both continue along the bold
flow line segment.
Thus any measure preserving property of the flow is violated by a factor of
2
, and so we cannot directly apply ergodic theory.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-5.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 5: lack of measure preserving property}\end{array}

For the system
\mathcal{P}
shown in Figure 5, the dissipative flow of fixed irrational slope
\alpha>0

can be described in terms of an operator
\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}
, where
t\geqslant 0
denotes time.
Roughly speaking, this operator denotes advancing geodesic flow of the fixed slope
\alpha
by time
t
.
More precisely, for any dissipative flow line
\mathcal{L}(t)
,
t\geqslant 0
, of the fixed slope
\alpha
, we write

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(\mathcal{L}(t_{1}))=\mathcal{L}(t_{1}+t),\quad t_{1}\geqslant 0.

In other words, the operator
\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}
advances the point
\mathcal{L}(t_{1})
on the flow line to the point
\mathcal{L}(t_{1}+t)

after a further time
t
has elapsed.

Using this operator
\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}
, we can partition
\mathcal{P}
into two subsets.
To define these two subsets, we use some concepts introduced by Birkhoff
[
2
]

for a very general class of dynamical systems in 1927.

We say that a point
P\in\mathcal{P}
is a
wandering point
of
\mathcal{P}
if there exists an open neighbourhood
D=D(P)
of the point
P

and a finite threshold
t_{0}\geqslant 0
such that the intersection

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

D\cap\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(D)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{for every $t>t_{0}$};

(2.1)

so that a dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
and starting from
P
is bounded away from
P
after time
t_{0}
.
Furthermore, we say that a point
P\in\mathcal{P}
is a
non-wandering point
of
\mathcal{P}
if
it is not a wandering point of
\mathcal{P}
.

The set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
of all wandering points of
\mathcal{P}
is called the
transient set
of
\mathcal{P}
,
and the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
of all non-wandering points of
\mathcal{P}
is called the
recurrent set
or
attractor
of
\mathcal{P}
.
These two subsets of
\mathcal{P}
are complements of each other, and give a partition of
\mathcal{P}
.

It follows easily from the definition that the transient set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is open
and that the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is closed and invariant under dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
.
These two subsets are illustrated in Figure 6 in the case of the modified
2
-square torus in Figure 5.
We emphasize in particular their dependence on the fixed slope
\alpha
of the flow under consideration.
It is also worth noting that the definition of wandering points and non-wandering points is given in terms of open neighbourhoods
of the points and their images under the operator
\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}
, and not in terms of the points and dissipative flow lines
that start from them.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-6.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 6: transient set and recurrent set}\end{array}

Figure 7 illustrates that any dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
that starts from a point
P\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)

remains in the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
, so that
the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is invariant under dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-7.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 7: invariance of the recurrent set under dissipative flow}\end{array}

Meanwhile, Figure 8 illustrates that any dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
that starts from a point
P\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)

eventually moves into the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
Once there, it clearly never returns to the transient set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-8.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 8: leaving the transient set to join the recurrent set}\end{array}

We comment here that the above definitions for
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)

remain valid for any system
\mathcal{P}
that arises when a finite polysquare translation surface
is modified by the addition of a one-sided barrier on the common vertical edge of
2
atomic squares.
However, we need to pay some attention to the existence of
pathological points
.
These are points
P\in\mathcal{P}
where a dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
that starts from the point
P

hits a singularity of
\mathcal{P}
and becomes undefined.
For the modified
2
-square torus in Figures 5 and 6, provided that we clearly
indicate whether the one-sided barrier includes either endpoint, then while these endpoints are singularities,
they are not pathological points because we have clear indication on how the flow line continues
after hitting either of them.
Pathological points do not affect our argument, as they form a set of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
, where
\alpha>0
is irrational, on a system
\mathcal{P}
such as those
shown in Figures 4–8,
where the
2
-square torus has been modified with the inclusion of a one-sided barrier on the common vertical edge
of the atomic squares, in the form of a union of finitely many vertical intervals.
For the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
and the transient set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
, the following hold:

(i)

Both sets are finite unions of polygons with total area
1
and with boundary edges that are vertical or of slope
\alpha
.

(ii)

The recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is invariant under dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
, and
every half-infinite dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
in the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is uniformly distributed in this set.

(iii)

Every half-infinite dissipative flow line of slope
\alpha
that starts from a point in the transient set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)

eventually leaves the set, moves to the set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
and is uniformly distributed there.

Proof.

We proceed in a number of steps.

Step 1
.

The pointwise definition of the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
does not provide any hint on how we may explicitly
construct and describe the set.
Indeed, in the general situation, this is a rather difficult problem.
However, for the modified
2
-square torus under consideration, we consider a no-go zone
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)

illustrated in Figure 9.
This is an open subset of
\mathcal{P}
obtained by sweeping the barrier
B
without its endpoints by the dissipative flow of slope
\alpha

towards the right vertical edge of
\mathcal{P}
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-9.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 9: the $2$-square torus modified with one-sided barriers}\end{array}

We claim that
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)\subset\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
;
in other words, that every point of the set
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is a wandering point of
\mathcal{P}
.
Since
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is open, every point of
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is contained in an open subset
D\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
To justify this claim, it suffices to show that for every open subset
D\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
,
there exists
t_{0}>0
such that (
2.1
) holds.
Now let
D^{\prime}
denote the image of
D
on the right side of the one-sided barrier
B
under projection in the direction
(-1,-\alpha)
,
as illustrated in the picture on the right in Figure 9.
Then
D^{\prime}
is an open subinterval of
B
and

D^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(D)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{for every $t>0$},

so that for every
t_{0}
satisfying
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant(1+\alpha^{2})^{1/2}
,

\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t_{0})}(D^{\prime})\cap\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(D)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{for every $t>(1+\alpha^{2})^{1/2}$}.

It is not difficult to see that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

D\subset\bigcup_{0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant(1+\alpha^{2})^{1/2}}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t_{0})}(D^{\prime}).

It then follows that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

D\cap\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(D)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{for every $t>(1+\alpha^{2})^{1/2}$},

(2.2)

justifying our claim.

Step 2
.

We define the open set
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
to be the largest possible extension of the no-go zone
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
To find this set, let
A
be an interval on the left vertical edge of
\mathcal{P}
, identical to the interval
B
that represents
the one-sided barrier, as shown in Figure 6.
Consistent with the fact that the transient set
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is open, we assume that the interval
B
is open.
This assumption is valid, since any deviation from it affects only sets of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

Reverse flow recipe
.

A given point
P\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies
P\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
if and only if starting from
P
,
an ordinary geodesic on the
2
-square torus in the direction
(-1,-\alpha)
hits the open interval
B

before hitting the corresponding interval
A
on the left vertical edge of
\mathcal{P}
.

Using time-quantitative equidistribution of geodesic flow on the
2
-square torus
in the form of the Koksma–Erdős–Turán version of the Weyl criterion,
it follows that for any given irrational number
\alpha>0
, there is a finite threshold
T_{0}(\alpha)
such that
starting from any point
P
and moving in the direction
(-1,-\alpha)
, this geodesic segment hits
B
or
A

in time less than
T_{0}(\alpha)
.
Hence the set
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
is a finite union of polygons with boundary edges
that are vertical or of slope
\alpha
.
We shall comment further on the finiteness of

in the Remark after the completion of the proof of Theorem
1
.

We claim that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)\subset\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha);

(2.3)

in other words, that every point of the set
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
is a wandering point of
\mathcal{P}
.
Since
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
is open, every point of
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
is contained in an open subset
D\subset\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
.
To justify this claim, it suffices to show that for every open subset
D\subset\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
,
there exists
t_{0}>0
such that (
2.1
) holds.
An argument similar to that in Part 1 leads to an analogue of (
2.2
), in the form

D\cap\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{(t)}(D)=\emptyset\quad\mbox{for every $t>T_{0}(\alpha)$},

justifying the assertion (
2.3
).

Let
\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
denote the set of all points
P\in\mathcal{P}

that are not in
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
.
Then

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)\subset\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha).

(2.4)

The
2
-cyclic symmetry of the system
\mathcal{P}
then ensures that the sets
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
and
\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)

have essentially the same projection on the unit torus

.
It follows that both sets have
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
1
.

Step 3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(t)
,
t\geqslant 0
, denote any half-infinite dissipative flow line on
\mathcal{P}
.
We say that a point
P\in\mathcal{P}
is an

of
\mathcal{L}
if there exists a sequence of

such that

and
\mathcal{L}(t_{i})\to P
as

.
Furthermore, we say that the collection of all infinite-time limit points of
\mathcal{L}
is
the infinite-time limit set of
\mathcal{L}
.
By definition, this set is invariant under dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
and so has
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure

0
,
1
or
2
.
This cannot be equal to
0
, since its projection to the unit torus
[0,1)^{2}
has
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
1
.
This cannot be equal to
2
, since it does not contain points of
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
Hence it has
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
1
.

Suppose now that
\mathcal{L}(t)
,

, denotes any such half-infinite dissipative flow line on

that starts
from a point in the set
\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
, so that
\mathcal{L}(0)\in\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)

We claim that the entire dissipative flow line is disjoint from the set

Then it follows from the reverse flow recipe that there exists

\mathcal{L}(t_{0})\in B

There are two possibilities:

The essentially

-cyclic symmetry of the system

(2.5)

.
The inclusion (

\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{P};A;\alpha)
must have

This means that

\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{P};A;\alpha)=\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha),

(2.6)

apart possibly from an exceptional set of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

The measure preserving aspect then allows us to use the well known Poincare recurrence theorem,
and conclude that apart possibly from an exceptional set of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure

every point in
\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{P};A;\alpha)
is a non-wandering point and so lies in
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
Combining this observation with (
2.4
) and (

), we conclude that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathfrak{R}(\mathcal{P};A;\alpha)=\mathfrak{M}^{c}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha),

apart possibly from exceptional sets of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

On the other hand, the dissipative flow of slope
\alpha
moves
B
first to the gray part in Figure 6
which is
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
.
When it reaches the gray part immediately to the left of
B
or the gray part
immediately to the left of
A
on right vertical edge, it then jumps to
A
.
Thus every point of
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
is eventually moved to
A
by the flow.
Since both sets
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
and
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)
have
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
1
,
it follows that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)=\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha),

apart possibly from an exceptional set of
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

Step 5
.

Uniformity in the recurrent set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is now established by projecting to the unit torus
[0,1)^{2}

and applying the results such as the Birkhoff ergodic theorem and also unique ergodicity.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark
.

The finiteness of
T_{0}(\alpha)
in Step 2 of the preceding proof follows from the fact that
we are essentially considering geodesic flow in the unit torus
[0,1)^{2}
in the direction
(-1,-\alpha)
,
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{P};\alpha)
is extended to
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{P};B;\alpha)

in a finite number of zig-zaggings;
i.e.
a finite number of traverses between the opposite vertical edges
of atomic squares.
We also use the following fact from diophantine approximation.

Irrational rotation lemma
.

Consider the irrational rotation sequence

s_{0}+n\alpha,\quad n=0,1,2,3,\ldots,

in the unit torus
[0,1)
, where
\alpha
is irrational.
For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, there is a finite threshold
N=N(\alpha;\varepsilon)
such that the union

\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1}(s_{0}+n\alpha-\varepsilon,s_{0}+n\alpha+\varepsilon)=[0,1)

for any starting point
s_{0}
.

Proof.

A fundamental result in diophantine approximation says that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right|<\frac{1}{q_{k}q_{k+1}},

where
p_{k}/q_{k}
,
k=0,1,2,3,\ldots,
is the sequence of convergents to
\alpha
.
Then

\left|\ell\alpha-\frac{\ell p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right|<\frac{\ell}{q_{k}q_{k+1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{q_{k+1}}<\frac{1}{q_{k}},\quad\ell=0,1,2,3,\ldots,q_{k}.

(2.7)

Furthermore,
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
are coprime, so that as
\ell
runs through the numbers
1,\ldots,q_{k}
,

\ell p_{k}
runs through a complete set of residues modulo
q_{k}
.
Choosing a positive integer
k
such that the denominator
q_{k}>1/\varepsilon
,
we see that the threshold
N=N(\alpha;\varepsilon)=q_{k}
satisfies the requirements.
∎

Proof.

For a one-sided vertical barrier

Let

For every integer

5.
Extension process and polygons

We make use of two well known results in ergodic theory.
Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a probability space, so that
\mu(X)=1
, and
\mathbf{T}
is a measure preserving transformation on
X
.
For any measurable subset
S\subset X
with positive measure
\mu(S)>0
,
since the Poincare recurrence theorem guarantees infinite recurrence for
\mu
-almost every
x\in S
to the set
S
,
it is meaningful to define the
first return time
for any such
x\in S
.
This is given by the integer

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

n_{S}(x)=\inf\{n\geqslant 1:\mathbf{T}^{n}(x)\in S\},

and clearly it is finite for
\mu
-almost every
x\in S
.

The first result is due to Kakutani
[
5
]
in 1943, and concerns an induced map of
\mathbf{T}
on
S
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a probability space, so that
\mu(X)=1
,
that
\mathbf{T}
is an invertible measure preserving transformation on
X
,
and that
S\subset X
is a measurable subset with positive measure
\mu(S)>0
.
Then the induced map

\mathbf{T}|_{S}:S\to S:x\mapsto\mathbf{T}^{n_{S}(x)}(x),

valid for
\mu
-almost every
x\in S
, is a measure preserving transformation on the induced measure space
(S,\mathcal{B}\cap S,\mu_{S})
.

The second result is due to Kac
[
4
]
in 1947, and concerns the
average
value of the first return time to
S
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a probability space, so that
\mu(X)=1
,
that
\mathbf{T}
is a measure preserving transformation on
X
,
and that
S\subset X
is a measurable subset with positive measure
\mu(S)>0
.
Suppose further that
\mathbf{T}
is ergodic in the space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
.
Then

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\int_{S}n_{S}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=1.

(5.1)

In other words, the average value of the first return time to the set
S
is equal to the reciprocal of the measure
\mu(S)
of
S
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Note first of all that both
n_{S}:S\to\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{T}|_{S}:S\to S
are well defined and measurable mappings.
For every integer
n\geqslant 1
, let

S_{n}=\{x\in S:n_{S}(x)=n\}

(5.2)

denote the collection of points
x\in S
that return to
S
for the first time at the
n
-th application of the transformation
\mathbf{T}
.
By definition, the sets
S_{n}
,
n=1,2,3,\ldots,
are pairwise disjoint.
Furthermore, we have

S_{1}=S\cap\mathbf{T}^{-1}S,\quad S_{2}=(S\cap\mathbf{T}^{-2}S)\setminus S_{1},\quad S_{3}=(S\cap\mathbf{T}^{-3}S)\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2}),

and in general

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

S_{n}=(S\cap\mathbf{T}^{-n}S)\setminus\bigcup_{1\leqslant m<n}S_{m},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots.

Any measurable subset
A\subset S
can be expressed as a disjoint union in the form

A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}(A\cap S_{n}),

so it follows immediately that

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mu(A)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A\cap S_{n}).

(5.3)

On the other hand, since
\mathbf{T}
is invertible and so
1
-to-
1
, the image
\mathbf{T}|_{S}A
can be expressed as a disjoint union in the form

\mathbf{T}|_{S}A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{T}|_{S}(A\cap S_{n}),

so it follows immediately that

\mu(\mathbf{T}|_{S}A)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(\mathbf{T}|_{S}(A\cap S_{n}))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(\mathbf{T}^{n}(A\cap S_{n})),

(5.4)

where in the last step, we use the key fact that for every fixed
n=1,2,3,\ldots,

if a subset
B\subset S_{n}
is measurable, then
\mathbf{T}|_{S}B=\mathbf{T}^{n}B
.
Finally, since
\mathbf{T}
is invertible and preserves
\mu
, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(\mathbf{T}^{n}(A\cap S_{n}))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A\cap S_{n}).

(5.5)

It now follows on combining (
5.3
)–(
5.5
) that
\mu(\mathbf{T}|_{S}A)=\mu(A)
, and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

For every
n=1,2,3,\ldots,
the set
S_{n}
defined by (
5.2
) clearly satisfies

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

S_{n}=S_{n,0}=S\cap\mathbf{T}^{-1}S^{c}\cap\ldots\cap\mathbf{T}^{-(n-1)}S^{c}\cap\mathbf{T}^{-n}S,

where
S^{c}=X\setminus S
denotes the complement of the set
S
in
X
.
Furthermore, for every
n=1,2,3,\ldots
and every
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, write

S_{n,k}=\mathbf{T}^{k}S_{n}=\mathbf{T}^{k}S\cap\mathbf{T}^{k-1}S^{c}\cap\ldots\cap\mathbf{T}^{k-n+1}S^{c}\cap\mathbf{T}^{k-n}S.

(5.6)

We first show that the sets

S_{n,k},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1,

(5.7)

are pairwise disjoint.
To justify this claim, consider first the sets
S_{n,k^{\prime}}
and
S_{n,k^{\prime\prime}}
, where
0\leqslant k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}\leqslant n-1
.
Then it follows from (
5.6
) that

S_{n,k^{\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{n,k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S^{c},

and so

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

S_{n,k^{\prime}}\cap S_{n,k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S\cap\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S^{c}=\emptyset.

Suppose next that
n^{\prime}<n^{\prime\prime}
,
k^{\prime}=0,1,\ldots,n^{\prime}-1
and
k^{\prime\prime}=0,1,\ldots,n^{\prime\prime}-1
.
Then it follows from (
5.6
) that

S_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S\cap\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}-n^{\prime}}S\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{n^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}S^{c}\cap\ldots\cap\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime\prime}-n^{\prime\prime}+1}S^{c}.

If
k^{\prime}\leqslant k^{\prime\prime}-1
, then clearly
S_{n^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S^{c}
, and so

S_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\cap S_{n^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S\cap\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}}S^{c}=\emptyset.

On the other hand, if
k^{\prime}>k^{\prime\prime}-1
, then
-k^{\prime}\leqslant-k^{\prime\prime}
.
Since
n^{\prime}<n^{\prime\prime}
, it follows that
n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}<n^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime\prime}
, and so
k^{\prime}-n^{\prime}\geqslant k^{\prime\prime}-n^{\prime\prime}+1
.
Then clearly
S_{n^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}-n^{\prime}}S^{c}
, and so

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

S_{n^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\cap S_{n^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\subset\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}-n^{\prime}}S\cap\mathbf{T}^{k^{\prime}-n^{\prime}}S^{c}=\emptyset.

Thus the sets (
5.7
) are pairwise disjoint as claimed.
We therefore have

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{n.k}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu(S_{n,k}).

(5.8)

Since
\mathbf{T}
is measure preserving, it follows from (
5.6
) that
\mu(S_{n,k})=\mu(S_{n})

for every

.
Combining this with (

), we deduce that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu(S_{n,k})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\mu(S_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S_{n}}n_{S}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

(5.9)

The Poincare recurrence theorem now gives

\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{n}=S.

Since this is clearly a pairwise disjoint union, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S_{n}}n_{S}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{S}n_{S}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

(5.10)

Finally, the pairwise disjoint union

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{n.k}

-invariant, so it follows from the ergodicity of

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{n.k}\right)=\mu(X)=1.

(5.11)

The desired identity (

) now follows on combining (
5.8
)–(
5.11
).

Proof of Theorem

Consider the induced map

In Case 2, there must be a non-empty subinterval of

More on the extension process

We continue our investigation of dissipative systems

In Case 1, the extension process does not start at all, so is clearly finite.

7.
Further comments

Here we have studied dynamical systems where the underlying domain is a finite polysquare translation surface,
and the one-sided barriers are located on the vertical sides of some atomic squares.
For such
nice
systems, we have shown that the attractor is a union of finitely many polygons,
and that this set can be determined by a finite process.

If we leave the class of finite polysquare translation surfaces, the structure of the attractor can be significantly different.
The following example, considered by Boshernitzan and Kornfeld
[
3
]
in 1995, provides an excellent illustration.

Let
\alpha=0.311\ldots
be the unique root of the polynomial
p(x)=x^{3}-x^{2}-3x+1
in the interval
(0,1)
.
Consider dissipative flow from left to right with slope
\alpha
on the unit torus
[0,1)^{2}
modified by the inclusion of
a one-sided barrier as shown in Figure 39.
When the flow encounters the bold barrier, it continues in the same direction from the corresponding point on the left edge.
For convenience, assume that the top barrier includes the bottom endpoint and the bottom barrier excludes the top endpoint.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale={0.8}]{figure-39.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 39: a dissipative system with $1$-direction flow}\end{array}

This dissipative flow is described by Boshernitzan and Kornfeld in terms of an
interval translation mapping

which is a generalization of the concept of an interval exchange transformation.
Here the interval
[0,1)
denotes the left vertical edge.
Then the interval translation mapping is given by
\mathbf{T}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
, where

\mathbf{T}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x+\alpha,&\mbox{if $x\in[0,1-\alpha)$},\\
x+\alpha^{2},&\mbox{if $x\in[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})$},\\
x+\alpha^{2}-1,&\mbox{if $x\in[1-\alpha^{2},1)$}.\end{array}\right.

(7.1)

The flow that starts from any point
x
on the left vertical edge returns to the left vertical edge at the point
\mathbf{T}(x)
.

Let
I_{1}=[1-\alpha,1)
.
To study the first return map
\mathbf{T}^{*}_{1}:I_{1}\to I_{1}
, we consider the partition

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

I_{1}=[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})\cup[1-\alpha^{2},1-\alpha^{3})\cup[1-\alpha^{3},1),

in the same ratio as the partition
[0,1)=[0,1-\alpha)\cup[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})\cup[1-\alpha^{2},1)
.
If
y\in[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})
, then

\mathbf{T}(y)=y+\alpha^{2}\in[1-\alpha+\alpha^{2},1)\subset[1-\alpha,1)=I_{1},

so that
\mathbf{T}^{*}_{1}(y)=y+\alpha^{2}
.
If
y\in[1-\alpha^{2},1-\alpha^{3})\subset[1-\alpha^{2},1)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1\in[0,\alpha^{2}-\alpha^{3})=[0,1-3\alpha)\subset[0,1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{2}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1+\alpha\in[\alpha,1-2\alpha)\subset[0,1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{3}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1+2\alpha\in[2\alpha,1-\alpha)\subset[0,1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{4}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1+3\alpha=y+\alpha^{3}\in[3\alpha,1)\subset[1-\alpha,1)=I_{1},

so that
\mathbf{T}^{*}_{1}(y)=y+\alpha^{3}

If

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1\in[\alpha^{2}-\alpha^{3},\alpha^{2})=[1-3\alpha,\alpha^{2})\subset[0,1-\alpha),

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1+\alpha\in[1-2\alpha,\alpha^{2}+\alpha)\subset[0,1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{3}(y)

\displaystyle=y+\alpha^{2}-1+2\alpha=y+\alpha^{3}-\alpha\in[1-\alpha,\alpha^{2}+2\alpha)\subset[1-\alpha,1)=I_{1},

so that

Summarizing, we have

\mathbf{T}^{*}_{1}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y+\alpha^{2},&\mbox{if $y\in[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})$},\\

y+\alpha^{3}-\alpha,&\mbox{if $y\in[1-\alpha^{3},1)$}.\end{array}\right.

(7.2)

Consider next the linear mapping from

to

(7.3)

Using (

1-\alpha+(x+\alpha^{2})\alpha=y+\alpha^{3},&\mbox{if $x\in[1-\alpha,1-\alpha^{2})$},\\

Comparing this with (

This can be interpreted as

Elaborating on this idea, Boshernitzan and Kornfeld can show that the attractor in this example
exhibits Cantor type self similarity and is an uncountable nowhere dense compact set
with measure zero.

Skripchenko and Troubetskoy

have made a study of these systems after
the initial work of Boshernitzan and Kornfeld.
They can show that by varying the position and length of the one-sided barrier and the slope of the flow,
one can construct uncountably many similar systems where the Hausdorff dimension of the closure
of the attractor is zero.
They also study the question of entropy which is beyond the scope of our study here.
