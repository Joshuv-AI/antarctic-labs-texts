Stability Analysis of A Single-Species Model with Distributed Delay
Isam Al-Darabsah

Abstract
The logistic equation has many applications and is used frequently in different fields, such as biology, medicine, and economics. In this paper, we study the stability of a single-species logistic model with a general distribution delay kernel and an inflow of nutritional resources at a constant rate. In particular, we provide precise conditions for the linear stability of the positive equilibrium and the occurrence of Hopf bifurcation. We apply the results to three delay distribution kernels: Uniform, Dirac-delta, and gamma distributions. Without an inflow, we show that the positive equilibrium is stable for a relatively small delay and then loses its stability through the Hopf bifurcation when the mean delay τ m \tau_{\rm m} increases with the three distributions. In the presence of an inflow, the model dynamics depend on the delay distribution kernel. In the uniform and Dirac-delta distributions cases, we find that the dynamics are similar to the absence of a nutrient influx. In contrast, the dynamics depend on the delay order p p when considering the gamma distribution. For p = 1 p=1 , the positive equilibrium is always stable. While for p = 2 p=2 and p = 3 p=3 , we find stability switching of the positive equilibrium resulting from the increase of the value of τ m \tau_{\rm m} , where the positive equilibrium is stable for a relatively short period; then, it loses stability via Hopf bifurcation as τ m \tau_{\rm m} increases; after then, it stabilizes again with an increase in τ m \tau_{\rm m} . The main difference between the delay orders p = 2 p=2 and p = 3 p=3 is that for relatively large τ m \tau_{\rm m} and intrinsic growth rate, the positive equilibrium can be stable when p = 2 p=2 , but it will be unstable when p = 3 p=3 . keywords : Single-species model ⋅ \cdot Stability ⋅ \cdot Distributed delay Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 34K20 ⋅ \cdot 34K18

2
Mathematical model and stability analysis

Let
n(t)
be the density of an organism s population at time
t
. Assume that the organism grows in an environment with constant nutritional resources.
Then, the dynamics of
n(t)
can be represented by
a single-species model with logistic growth
[
16
]

\cfrac{{\rm d}n(t)}{{\rm d}t}=r\,n(t)\,\left[1-\cfrac{1}{K}\int_{-\infty}^{t}n(s)\,g(t-s)\,{\rm d}s\right].

(1)

where
r
is the intrinsic growth rate, and
K
is the carrying capacity of the resources.
The function
g(\cdot)
is the kernel of the delay distribution with compact support, that is,

g(s)\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s){\rm d}s=1.

We calculate the
mean delay
as

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm m}=\int_{0}^{\infty}sg(s)ds.

The function
g
states that the population growth will be proportionate to the size of the population in the past and will solely depend on individuals who can survive the delay.

Assume there is an inflow of more nutritional resources at a constant rate of
D
. Then, model (
1
) has the form
[
23
]

\cfrac{{\rm d}n(t)}{{\rm d}t}=r\,n(t)\,\left[1-\cfrac{1}{K}\int_{-\infty}^{t}n(s)\,g(t-s)\,{\rm d}s\right]+D.

(2)

By setting
\bar{s}=t-s
, model (
2
) can be written, after dropping the bars, as

\cfrac{{\rm d}n(t)}{{\rm d}t}=r\,n(t)\,\left[1-\cfrac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{\infty}n(t-s)\,g(s)\,{\rm d}s\right]+D.

(3)

In the rest of the manuscripts, we study the stability and the existence of Hopf bifurcation of the model (
3
) with a general distribution kernel.

When
D\geq 0
, model (
3
) has only one positive equilibrium

n^{*}=\cfrac{\left(1+\sqrt{1+\cfrac{4D}{rK}}\right)K}{2}.

Notice that when
D=0
, then
n^{*}=K
. Moreover, in this case, the trivial equilibrium
n=0
exists, and it is unstable due to the positive eigenvalue
\lambda=r
.

Define
\tilde{n}=n-n^{*}
. Then the linearization of (
3
) at
n^{*}
is

\cfrac{{\rm d}\tilde{n}(t)}{{\rm d}t}=r\left(\cfrac{K-n^{*}}{K}\right)\tilde{n}(t)-\cfrac{rn^{*}}{K}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{n}(t-s)\,g(s)\,{\rm d}s.

(4)

For the zero delay case
\tau_{\rm m}=0
, i.e.,
{g}(s)=\delta_{0}(s)
, system (
4
) becomes

\cfrac{{\rm d}\tilde{n}(t)}{{\rm d}t}=-r\left(\cfrac{2n^{*}-K}{K}\right)\tilde{n}(t)

and the characteristic equation is
\lambda+r(2n^{*}/K-1)=0
. Hence,
the equilibrium
n^{*}
is locally asymptotically stable
due to
0<K\leq n^{*}
and
D\geq 0
.

Let
\tau_{\rm m}>0
.
To study the dependence of the linear stability of
n^{*}
on the mean delay
\tau_{\rm m}
, we rescale
the dimensional variables as
\bar{t}=t/\tau_{\rm m}
and
\bar{s}=s/\tau_{\rm m}
.
After dropping the bars, model (
4
) becomes

\cfrac{{\rm d}\tilde{n}(t)}{{\rm d}t}=\tau_{\rm m}r\left(\cfrac{K-n^{*}}{K}\right)\tilde{n}(t)-\tau_{\rm m}\cfrac{rn^{*}}{K}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{n}(t-s)\,\widehat{g}(s)\,{\rm d}s.

(5)

where
\widehat{g}(s)=\tau_{\rm m}\,g(\tau_{\rm m}s)
.
By applying the Laplace transform to (
5
) (with zero initial condition), the characteristic equation can be written as

\Delta(\lambda)=\lambda+\tau_{\rm m}r\left(\cfrac{n^{*}-K}{K}\right)+\tau_{\rm m}\cfrac{rn^{*}}{K}\,\widehat{G}(\lambda)=0

(6)

where

\widehat{G}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\lambda s}\,\widehat{g}(s)\,{\rm d}s

is the Laplace transform of
\widehat{g}
.

Recall that
n^{*}
is locally asymptotically stable when
\tau_{\rm m}=0
.
We seek conditions on
\tau_{\rm m}
such that
{\rm Re}(\lambda)
changes its sign as
\tau_{\rm m}
increases.
In other words, the characteristic equation
(
6
) must have a pair of pure imaginary eigenvalues.

It is clear that
\lambda=0
is not an eigenvalue value because
\Delta(0)=r(2n^{*}/K-1)>0
.
To determine the existence of a pair of pure imaginary eigenvalues, we substitute
\lambda=i\omega
(
\omega>0
and
i=\sqrt{-1}
) in
\Delta(\lambda)
defined in (
6
). Consequently, we have

\Delta(i\omega)=i\omega+\tau_{\rm m}r\left(\cfrac{n^{*}-K}{K}\right)+\tau_{\rm m}\cfrac{rn^{*}}{K}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-i\omega s}\,\,\widehat{g}(s)\,{\rm d}s=0

Separating the real and imaginary parts results in

\cfrac{K-n^{*}}{K}=\cfrac{n^{*}}{K}\,C(\omega)\quad\text{and}\quad\omega=\tau_{\rm m}\cfrac{rn^{*}}{K}\,S(\omega),

(7)

where
\widehat{G}(i\omega)=C(i\omega)-i\,S(i\omega)
with

C(\omega):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\!\left(\omega s\right)\widehat{g}\!\left(s\right){\rm d}s\quad\text{and}\quad S(\omega):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin\!\left(\omega s\right)\widehat{g}\!\left(s\right){\rm d}s.

(8)

Now we study the influence of varying the mean delay
\tau_{\rm m}
on the stability of the positive equilibrium
n^{*}
.

2.1
The case of
D=0
.

Recall that when
D=0
, the positive equilibrium
n^{*}=K
. Hence, the characteristic equation (
6
) becomes

\Delta(\lambda)=\lambda+\tau_{\rm m}r\,\widehat{G}(\lambda)=0

(9)

and
equation (
7
) implies

C(\omega)=0\quad\text{and}\quad S(\omega)=\cfrac{\omega}{\tau_{\rm m}r}.

(10)

To determine how the sign of eigenvalues changes as
\tau_{\rm m}
increases,
we calculate the rate of change of the real part of
\lambda
with respect to
\tau_{\rm m}
. To this end, firstly, notice that

\widehat{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}s{\rm e}^{-\lambda s}\,\widehat{g}(s)\,{\rm d}s

Hence,

\widehat{G}^{\prime}(i\,\omega)=-S^{\prime}(\omega)-i\,C^{\prime}(\omega).

From (
9
), we obtain that

1+r\left(\tau_{\rm m}\widehat{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)+\widehat{G}(\lambda)\cfrac{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}{{\rm d}\lambda}\right)\,=0.

Now we have

\displaystyle\left.\left(\cfrac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}\right)^{-1}\right|_{\lambda=i\omega}

\displaystyle=-\left.\left(\cfrac{1+r\,\tau_{\rm m}\,\widehat{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)}{r\,\widehat{G}(\lambda)}\right)\right|_{\lambda=i\omega}

\displaystyle=-\cfrac{1-r\,\tau_{\rm m}\,(S^{\prime}(\omega)+i\,C^{\prime}(\omega))}{r\,(C(\omega)-i\,S(\omega))}

Using (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\left.{\rm Re}\left(\cfrac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}\right)^{-1}\right|_{\lambda=i\omega}

\displaystyle=-\cfrac{C^{\prime}(\omega)}{r\,S(\omega)}=-\cfrac{\tau_{\rm m}}{\omega}C^{\prime}(\omega).

Thus crossing the imaginary axis through the solution of (
10
) depends on the sign of
C^{\prime}(\omega)
.
Therefore, when crossing the imaginary axis,
{\rm Re}(\lambda)
changes from negative to positive (resp. positive to negative) when
C^{\prime}(\omega)<0
(resp.
C^{\prime}(\omega)>0
). Hence, there exists
\tau_{\rm m}^{*}>0
such that a Hopf bifurcation occurs at
\tau_{\rm m}^{*}
.

Recall that
n^{*}=K
is locally asymptotically stable when
\tau_{\rm m}=0
. Consequently, we have the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\omega_{0}
be the smallest solution of (
10
) such that

. Then, a Hopf bifurcation occurs at
\tau_{\rm m}^{*}=\omega_{0}/(r\,S(\omega_{0}))
.
Consequently,
n^{*}=K
is locally asymptotically stable for

0<\tau_{\rm m}<\cfrac{\omega_{0}}{r\,S(\omega_{0})}

(11)

and it is unstable when
\tau_{\rm m}>\frac{\omega_{0}}{r\,S(\omega_{0})}
.

The case of
D>0
.

Since

, equation (

) gives

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle=-\,{\rm Re}\left(\cfrac{K+rn^{*}\tau_{\rm m}\,\widehat{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)}{r(n^{*}-K)+r\,n^{*}\,\widehat{G}(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\,{\rm Re}\left(\cfrac{K-rn^{*}\tau_{\rm m}\,(S^{\prime}(\lambda)+i\,C^{\prime}(\lambda))}{r(n^{*}-K)+r\,n^{*}\,\,(C(\lambda)-i\,S(\lambda))}\right)

.
Using
{\tau_{\rm m}r\,n^{*}}/{K}=\omega/S(\omega)
in (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left.{\rm Re}\left(\cfrac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}\right)^{-1}\right|_{\lambda=i\omega}&=\cfrac{r(n^{*}-K)}{q^{2}(\omega)}\left(r\tau_{\rm m}n^{*}S^{\prime}(\omega)-K+\cfrac{K\omega}{\tau_{\rm m}}\,\cfrac{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}{{\rm d}\omega}\right).\end{split}

(13)

Thus crossing the imaginary axis through the solution of (

) depends on the sign of
S^{\prime}(\omega)
and
\cfrac{{\rm d}\tau_{\rm m}}{{\rm d}\omega}
.
Therefore, if

\end{array}K,

(14)

then

Application 3: Gamma distribution kernel

The gamma distribution kernel (Fig.

~\qquad g(s)={g^{\rm\gamma}_{p}}(s):=\cfrac{\gamma^{p}s^{p-1}e^{-\gamma s}}{(p-1)!},\qquad\gamma\geq 0\text{ and }p\in\mathbb{N}.

(26)

The parameter
p
is the order of the delay kernel, and
{1}/{\gamma}
is the scale parameter.
The mean time delay in this case is
\tau_{\rm m}={p}/{\gamma}
.
When
g(s)={g^{\rm\gamma}_{p}}(s)
, model (
3
) reduces to an integro-differential equation (IDE) of the form

\cfrac{{\rm d}n(t)}{{\rm d}t}=r\,n(t)\,\left[1-\cfrac{\gamma^{p}}{K\,(p-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}n(t-s)\,s^{p-1}e^{-\gamma s}\,{\rm d}s\right]+D.

(27)

Using the linear chain trick
[
25
]
model (
27
) can be transformed to ODEs system of dimension
p+1
of the from

\displaystyle\cfrac{{\rm d}n(t)}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=r\,n(t)\,\left[1-\cfrac{x_{p}(t)}{K}\right]+D.

\displaystyle\cfrac{{\rm d}x_{i}(t)}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,(x_{i-1}(t)-x_{i}(t)),\qquad i=1,2,\dots,p

where
x_{0}(t)=n(t)
and

x_{i}(t)=\cfrac{\gamma^{i}}{(i-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}n(t-s)\,s^{i-1}e^{-\gamma s}\,{\rm d}s.

When
p=1
, the kernel
g(s)
is called

or

From a biological perspective, it shows that the maximum weighted response of population density comes from the present population density.
While

Biologically, it means that the maximum influence on the population density at any time

The normalized gamma distribution has the form

\Delta(\lambda)=\lambda+\cfrac{p\,r}{\gamma}\left(\cfrac{n^{*}-K}{K}\right)+\cfrac{p\,r\,n^{*}}{\gamma\,K}\left(\cfrac{p}{\lambda+p}\right)^{p}=0.

(28)

Following
[
5
]
we have

\displaystyle C(\omega)

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\left[\frac{p^{p}}{(p-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{p-1}{\rm e}^{-(p+i\omega)s}{\rm d}s\right]=\left(1+\cfrac{\omega^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)^{-p}{\rm Re}\left(1-\cfrac{i\omega}{p}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\cfrac{\omega^{2}}{p^{2}}\right)^{-p}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor}\binom{p}{2j}\frac{(-1)^{j}{\omega}^{2j}}{{p}^{2j}}

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we have studied the stability of a time-delayed single-species logistic model with a general distribution delay kernel and an inflow of nutritional resources at a constant rate
D\geq 0
.
By normalizing the time delay, we have studied the stability of the positive equilibrium point and provided precise conditions for the linear stability and the occurrence of Hopf bifurcation.
To study the influence of population density at any previous time on its density at present, we have applied the general results to three delay distribution kernels: Uniform, Dirac-delta, and gamma, where each distribution has its distinctive biological interpretation.

In the case of zero delay, we have proved that the positive equilibrium is always stable with
D\geq 0
.
For Uniform and Dirac-delta distributions, we have found the dynamics are similar when
D=0
or
D>0
, where the positive equilibrium is stable for a relatively small delay and then loses its stability through the Hopf bifurcation when the mean delay increases.
On the other hand, we have shown that the dynamics are different for the gamma distribution when
D>0
, which is affected by the delay order.
We have proved that the positive equilibrium is always stable when the delay order is one (weak gamma distribution or exponential distribution).
When the order of delay is two or three, we have shown that there is stability switching of the positive equilibrium resulting from the increase of the value of
\tau_{\rm m}
, in the sense that the positive equilibrium is stable for a relatively short period. Then, it loses stability via Hopf bifurcation as
\tau_{\rm m}
increases. After then, it stabilizes again with an increase in
\tau_{\rm m}
.
The main difference is that for relatively large
\tau_{\rm m}
and intrinsic growth rate, the positive equilibrium can be stable when delay order is two, but it will be unstable when delay order is three.
We have found that the results are consistent with the parts in the literature
[
23
,
16
]
.

For future work, we will consider a multi-patch time delayed logistic equation with migration
[
26
]
where each patch follows a different distributed delay kernel. Then, study the influence of delay in the density of population in each patch in the presence of migration.
