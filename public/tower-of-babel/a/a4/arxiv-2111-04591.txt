A note on the representations of SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1)


Abstract
SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1) is the isometry group of ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -dimensional de Sitter spacetime ( dS d + 1 \text{dS}_{d+1} ) and the conformal group of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . This note gives a pedagogical introduction to the representation theory of SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1) , from the perspective of de Sitter quantum field theory and using tools from conformal field theory. Topics include (1) the construction and classification of all unitary irreducible representations (UIRs) of SO ​ ( 1 , 2 ) {\text{SO}}(1,2) and SL ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) {\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R}) , (2) the construction and classification of all UIRs of SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1) that describe integer-spin fields in dS d + 1 \text{dS}_{d+1} , (3) a physical framework for understanding these UIRs, (4) the definition and derivation of Harish-Chandra group characters of SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1) , and (5) a comparison between UIRs of SO ​ ( 1 , d + 1 ) {\text{SO}}(1,d+1) and SO ​ ( 2 , d ) {\text{SO}}(2,d) .

Introduction

According to Wigner’s principle of classification
[
1
]
, particles in
\text{dS}_{d+1}
are in one-to-one correspondence with UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)

{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)

{\text{SO}}(1,3)
, can be dated back to Dirac
[
2
]
, who inspired three independent work classifying (unitary) irreducible representations of
{\text{SO}}(1,3)

[

[

{\text{SO}}(1,2)
(and its double covering
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})

{\text{SO}}(1,4)
was solved in
[

7

Due to the dual role of

{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)

{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)

[

The main goal of this note is to provide a pedagogical introduction to the UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
, their Harish-Chandra characters and their relations with quantum fields in de Sitter spacetime. For this purpose, we will show explicitly the construction of these UIRs from scratch, by using a CFT-type language that is more familiar to physicists rather than the standard induced representation approach in math literature. Building on the construction, we will be able to compute Harish-Chandra characters for these UIRs, without turning to advanced tools like Bernstein-Gel’fand-Gel’fand resolutions. By quantizing fields of different masses and spins in
\text{dS}_{d+1}
, we will also identify their single-particle Hilbert spaces as UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
.

Although we have tried to make this note as self-contained as possible, we are not going to present all the mathematical proofs, in particular those technical and lengthy ones that are not directly relevant to our purpose. Our strategy is that we will refer the readers to literature that contains the details of the proofs and then illustrate the underlying ideas of the proofs by working out some examples. For instance, the irreducibility of
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
(c.f. section
4

[
9
]
. Instead of repeating his steps, we give a simpler and more straightforward proof for the scalar case, i.e.
s=0
(c.f. appendix
E

Preliminaries and conventions

(d+1)
-dimensional de Sitter spacetime can be represented as a hypersurface in a
(d+2)
-dimensional embedding space

\displaystyle\text{dS}_{d+1}:\,\,\,\,\,-X_{0}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{d+1}^{2}=1

(2.1)

where the de Sitter radius is chosen to be 1. Its isometry group is
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
, isomorphic to the conformal conformal group of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A standard basis for the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,d+1)
is
L_{AB}=-L_{BA},A=0,1,\cdots d+1
, with commutation relations

\displaystyle[L_{AB},L_{CD}]=\eta_{BC}L_{AD}-\eta_{AC}L_{BD}+\eta_{AD}L_{BC}-\eta_{BD}L_{AC}

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{AB}=\text{diag}(-1,1,\cdots,1)

(2.3)

The isomorphism between
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,d+1)
and the
d
-dimensional Euclidean conformal algebra is realized as

\displaystyle L_{ij}=M_{ij},\,\,\,\,\,L_{0,d+1}=D,\,\,\,\,\,L_{d+1,i}=\frac{1}{2}(P_{i}+K_{i}),\,\,\,\,\,L_{0,i}=\frac{1}{2}(P_{i}-K_{i})

(2.4)

where
D
is the dilatation,
P_{i}
(
i=1,2,\cdots d
) are translations,
K_{i}
are special conformal transformations and
M_{ij}=-M_{ji}
are spatial rotations.
The commutation relations of conformal algebra following from (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) are

\displaystyle[D,P_{i}]=P_{i},\,\,\,\,\,[D,K_{i}]=-K_{i},\,\,\,\,\,[K_{i},P_{j}]=2\delta_{ij}D-2M_{ij}

\displaystyle[M_{ij},P_{k}]=\delta_{jk}P_{i}-\delta_{ik}P_{j},\,\,\,\,\,[M_{ij},K_{k}]=\delta_{jk}K_{i}-\delta_{ik}K_{j}

\displaystyle[M_{ij},M_{k\ell}]=\delta_{jk}M_{i\ell}-\delta_{ik}M_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}M_{jk}-\delta_{j\ell}M_{ik}

(2.5)

The generators
L_{AB}
exponentiate to group elements in
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)

\displaystyle U(\theta)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\theta^{AB}L_{AB}\right)

(2.6)

where
\theta^{AB}=-\theta^{BA}
are real parameters. Some important subgroups of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
that will be used later are

\displaystyle\text{K}=\,{\text{SO}}(d+1),\,\,\,\,\,\text{M}=\left\{e^{\frac{1}{2}\omega^{ij}M_{ij}},\omega^{ij}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}={\text{SO}}(d)

\displaystyle\text{N}=\left\{e^{b\cdot K},b^{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\,\,\,\,\,\widetilde{\text{N}}=\left\{e^{x\cdot P},x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\,\,\,\,\,\text{A}=\left\{e^{\lambda D},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

(2.7)

where
K
is the
maximal compact
subgroup of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
.

To get a unitary representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
, the Lie algebra generators must be realized
as
anti-hermitian
operators on some Hilbert space, i.e.

\displaystyle L_{AB}^{\dagger}=-L_{AB}

(2.8)

This is the reality condition relevant to
(d+1)
-dimensional unitary quantum field theories on a fixed dS background. Notice it is different from the reality conditions relevant to
d
-dimensional unitary CFTs or
(d+1)
-dimensional unitary quantum field theories on a fixed (E)AdS background. The latter corresponds to the reality condition of the
{\mathfrak{so}}(2,d)
algebra obtained
by Wick-rotating the
X^{d+1}
direction. This gives for example
P_{i}^{\dagger}=K_{i}
whereas for
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,d+1)
we have
P_{i}^{\dagger}=-P_{i}
. Throughout the paper, we will call (
2.8
) the
dS reality
.

The quadratic Casimir, which commutes with all
L_{AB}
, is chosen to be

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{AB}L^{AB}=-D^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(P_{i}+K_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}(P_{i}-K_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}M_{ij}M^{ij}

\displaystyle=-D^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(P_{i}K_{i}+K_{i}P_{i})+\frac{1}{2}M_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=D(d-D)+P_{i}K_{i}+\frac{1}{2}M_{ij}^{2}

(2.9)

Here
\frac{1}{2}M_{ij}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2}M_{ij}M^{ij}
is the quadratic Casimir of
{\text{SO}}(d)
and it is negative-definite for a unitary representation since
M_{ij}
are anti-hermitian. For example, for a spin-
s
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d)
, it takes the value of
-s(s+d-2)
.

3.1
A direct construction

Define
L_{0}=iL_{12}=-\frac{i}{2}(P+K)
to be the
Hermitian
operator that generates the maximal compact subgroup
{\text{SO}}(2)
. It takes value in integers for an
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
group representation since
e^{2\pi iL_{0}}
is the identity operator in
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
. As in the
SU(2)
case, we define ladder operators

\displaystyle L_{\pm}=-\frac{i}{2}(P-K)\mp D

(3.2)

and they satisfy

\displaystyle[L_{0},L_{\pm}]=\pm L_{\pm},\,\,\,\,\,[L_{-},L_{+}]=2L_{0},\,\,\,\,\,L_{-}^{\dagger}=L_{+}

(3.3)

Thus
L_{+}
raises the eigenvalue of
L_{0}
by 1 while
L_{-}
lowers it by 1. In terms of the basis
\{L_{0},L_{\pm}\}
, the quadratic Casimir operator is expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{2}=L_{+}L_{-}+L_{0}(1-L_{0})

(3.4)

To construct the whole representation space of certain UIR
R
of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
, we start from an eigenstate of
L_{0}
in
R
, i.e. a state
|N\rangle
satisfying
L_{0}|N\rangle=N|N\rangle
where
N\in\mathbb{Z}
. By acting
L_{\pm}
on
|N\rangle
repeatedly, we obtain a tower of states:
\{L_{+}^{p_{+}}|N\rangle,|N\rangle,L_{-}^{p_{-}}|N\rangle\}_{p_{\pm}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. There exist three different scenarios: (i) the
L_{-}
action truncates, (ii) the
L_{+}
action truncates, and (iii) neither
L_{+}
or
L_{-}
truncates. In scenarios (i),
L_{0}
is bounded from below. Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}
be the minimal eigenvalue of
L_{0}
. The spectrum of
L_{0}
is
\{p,p+1,p+2,\cdots\}
and the quadratic Casimir is equal to
p(1-p)
. In scenarios (ii),
L_{0}
is bounded from above. Let
q-1\in\mathbb{Z}
be the maximal eigenvalue of
L_{0}
. The spectrum of
L_{0}
is
\{q-1,q-2,q-3,\cdots\}
and the quadratic Casimir is equal to
q(1-q)
. In scenario (iii),
L_{0}
takes value in all integers. The three scenarios can be described in a uniform way as follows. First, we fix the quadratic Casimir
{\cal C}_{2}=\Delta(1-\Delta)
, where
\Delta
is an arbitrary complex number. Then by choosing proper normalization for the eigenstates of
L_{0}
, we also fix the action of
L_{-}

\displaystyle L_{-}|n\rangle=(n-\Delta)|n-1\rangle

(3.5)

where
|n\rangle
is the eigenstate of
L_{0}
with eigenvalue
n
. The action of
L_{+}
follows from (
3.5
) and the Casimir condition, i.e.
{\cal C}_{2}|n\rangle=\Delta(1-\Delta)|n\rangle

\displaystyle L_{+}|n\rangle=(n+\Delta)|n+1\rangle

(3.6)

It is clear that the truncations of
L_{\pm}
discussed above happen when
\Delta
hits integers.

With the action being known, we proceed to analyze the constraint of unitarity, which consists of two parts: (a) the positivity of
\langle n|n\rangle
, and (b) the reality condition
L^{\dagger}_{+}=L_{-}
, i.e.
\langle n|L_{-}|n+1\rangle=\langle n+1|L_{+}|n\rangle^{*}
.
The latter implies

\displaystyle\frac{\langle n+1|n+1\rangle}{\langle n|n\rangle}=\frac{n+\bar{\Delta}}{n+\Delta^{*}}\equiv\lambda_{n}

(3.7)

where
\bar{\Delta}=1-\Delta
and
\Delta^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\Delta
. The reality of
\lambda_{n}
yields two possible families of solutions for
\Delta
:

\displaystyle(i)\,\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu,\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,(ii)\,\Delta\in\mathbb{R}

(3.8)

The positivity of
\lambda_{n}
requires separate discussions for different values of
\Delta

•

3.2
A CFT-type construction

In this section, we will show that all the UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
constructed above can be realized in certain function spaces by borrowing ideas from conformal field theory. Compared to the previous construction, this method admits a straightforward generalization to the higher dimensions.

3.2.1
Representation spaces

We start with a
primary state of scaling dimension

\bar{\Delta}=1-\Delta
, i.e. a state
|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle
satisfying:

\displaystyle K|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle=0,\,\,\,\,\,D|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle=\bar{\Delta}|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle

(3.14)

However, unlike in usual unitary CFT, we do not require this state to be normalizable (indeed, as we shall see, it is not and hence does not belong to the Hilbert spaces we will construct). Acting by translations on this state produces a continuous family of states

\displaystyle|\bar{\Delta},x\rangle=e^{xP}|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle

(3.15)

Using this definition and the commutation relations (
3.1
), we obtain (dropping the label
\bar{\Delta}
for
|\bar{\Delta},x\rangle
)

\displaystyle P|x\rangle=\partial_{x}|x\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,D|x\rangle=(x\partial_{x}+\bar{\Delta})|x\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,K|x\rangle=(x^{2}\partial_{x}+2\bar{\Delta}x)|x\rangle

(3.16)

Then a general state
|\psi\rangle
can be expressed as a linear combination

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dx\,\psi(x)|x\rangle

(3.17)

At this point, we allow
\psi(x)
to be any smooth function. The action of the conformal generators on the wavefunctions
\psi(x)
is obtained from (
3.16
) and integrating by part. For example,
P|\psi\rangle=\int dx\,\psi(x)\partial_{x}|x\rangle=\int dx\,(-\partial_{x}\psi(x))|x\rangle
. This gives

\displaystyle P\psi(x)=-\partial_{x}\psi(x),\,\,\,\,\,D\psi(x)=-(x\partial_{x}+\Delta)\psi(x),\,\,\,\,\,K\psi(x)=-(x^{2}\partial_{x}+2\Delta x)\psi(x)

(3.18)

The requirement of lifting the Lie algebra action (
3.18
) to a group action imposes constraints on the wavefunctions. In particular, exponentiating the special conformal transformation yields

\displaystyle\left(e^{bK}\psi\right)(x)=\left(\frac{1}{1+bx}\right)^{2\Delta}\psi\left(\frac{x}{1+bx}\right)

(3.19)

By taking the limit
x\to-\frac{1}{b}
, we can read off the asymptotic behavior of
\psi(x)

\displaystyle\psi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|x|\to\infty}}{{\approx}}\frac{1}{|x|^{2\Delta}}\left(c_{\psi}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right)

(3.20)

where
c_{\psi}
is some constant. Let
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
be the complex vector space of infinitely differentiable functions satisfying the asymptotic boundary condition (
3.20
) and it furnishes a representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
whose infinitesimal version is given by (
3.18
).

3.2.2
Consequences of unitarity

Given the set of states/wavefunctions, we should define a positive definite inner product
\langle x|y\rangle
that respects the dS reality condition. First notice that
P^{\dagger}=-P
leads to the relation
\partial_{y}\langle x|y\rangle=\langle x|P|y\rangle=-\partial_{x}\langle x|y\rangle
, so
(\partial_{x}+\partial_{y})\langle x|y\rangle=0
, i.e.
\langle x|y\rangle=f(x-y)
for some function
f(x)
. Likewise the reality condition of dilatation and special conformal transformation requires

\displaystyle(x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+\bar{\Delta}+\bar{\Delta}^{*})f(x-y)=0

(3.21)

\displaystyle(x^{2}\partial_{x}+y^{2}\partial_{y}+2\bar{\Delta}y+2\bar{\Delta}^{*}x)f(x-y)=0

(3.22)

where the first equation fixes the scaling property of
f(x)
as
(x\partial_{x}+\bar{\Delta}+\bar{\Delta}^{*})f(x)=0
, which together with the second equation implies

\displaystyle(\Delta-\Delta^{*})xf(x)=0

(3.23)

The eq. (
3.23
) holds when either
\Delta
is real or
f(x)\propto\delta(x)
. The former further implies
f(x)\propto\frac{1}{|x|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}
while the latter implies
\Delta+\bar{\Delta}=1
. Thus we arrive at the conclusion that the dS reality condition allows two qualitatively different branches of
\Delta

\displaystyle\text{I}:\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu,\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,\langle x|y\rangle=c\,\delta(x-y)

\displaystyle\text{II}:\Delta\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\langle x|y\rangle=c\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(x-y)^{2\bar{\Delta}}}

(3.24)

where
c
is some constant that may depend on
\Delta
. Correspondingly the inner product of two states of the form (
3.17
) equals

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\begin{cases}c\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,\psi_{1}^{*}(x)\psi_{2}(x)\,\,\,\,\,&\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu\\
2^{\bar{\Delta}}c\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}dx\,dy\,\frac{\psi_{1}^{*}(x)\psi_{2}(y)}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}&\Delta\in\mathbb{R}\end{cases}

(3.25)

In case I, the inner product (
3.25
) defines the usual
L^{2}
-norm for wavefunctions in the space
{\cal F}_{\frac{1}{2}+i\mu}
with
c
chosen to be 1. This norm is clearly well-defined because for any
\psi\in{\cal F}_{\frac{1}{2}+i\mu}
,
|\psi|^{2}
decays as
\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}
near infinity. Therefore,
\psi\in{\cal F}_{\frac{1}{2}+i\mu}
equipped with the
L^{2}
-norm carries a unitary irreducible representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
, which as we shall see is isomorphic to
{\cal P}_{\frac{1}{2}+i\mu}
. To analyze the positivity of (
3.25
) in case II, it is more convenient to rewrite the kernel
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)\equiv\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\,c}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}
in momentum space

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dx\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\,c}{|x|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}e^{ipx}=\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\,c}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t}\,t^{\bar{\Delta}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-tx^{2}+ipx}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\,\sqrt{\pi}c}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t}t^{\bar{\Delta}-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{p^{2}}{4t}}=\frac{c^{\prime}}{p^{2\Delta-1}},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,c^{\prime}=2^{\Delta}\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta-\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}c

(3.26)

As long as we choose
c
properly, the kernel
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)
is a positive function of
p
. However, the positivity of
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)
as a function does
not
necessarily imply that the inner product on
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
defined by
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)
is positive definite. We have to check that all the wavefunctions in
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
are normalizable with respect to this inner product, otherwise regularization can destroy the positivity. Since
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
is infinitely differentiable, its Fourier transformation decays rapidly as
|p|\to\infty
. It suffices to check the normalizability for small
p
. Let
\psi(p)
be the Fourier transformation of
\psi(x)
. Its small
p
behavior has two types of leading fall-offs:
c_{1}\,p^{0}
and
c_{2}\,p^{2\Delta-1}
where
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants. The former comes from the regularity of
\psi(x)
for finite
x

4
4

4

For example, for
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{x^{2\Delta}}
, which is singular at the origin, we do not have the
p^{0}
behavior.
and the latter arises from the large
x
asymptotic behavior of
\psi(x)
. As an explicit example, let’s take
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{\Delta}}
, which clearly lives in the space
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
. Its Fourier transformation can be performed analytically
\psi(p)\propto p^{\Delta-\frac{1}{2}}K_{\frac{1}{2}-\Delta}(p)
, where
K_{\frac{1}{2}-\Delta}(p)
is a Bessel-
K
function of order
\frac{1}{2}-\Delta
. Then the two leading fall-offs of
\psi(p)
are a straightforward result of the following asymptotic property of Bessel
K
-function

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\to 0}}{{\approx}}z^{\alpha}\left(2^{-1-\alpha}\Gamma(-\alpha)+{\cal O}(z^{2})\right)+z^{-\alpha}\left(2^{-1+\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)+{\cal O}(z^{2})\right)

(3.27)

Plugging
\psi(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p\to 0}}{{\approx}}c_{1}+c_{2}p^{2\Delta-1}
into the inner product
\int\frac{dp}{2\pi}{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)|\psi(p)|^{2}
where
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(p)=\frac{c^{\prime}}{p^{2\Delta-1}}
, we conclude that
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
is normalizable if and only if
2\Delta-1>-1
and
1-2\Delta>-1
, i.e.

\displaystyle 0<\Delta<1

(3.28)

which is exactly the range of
\Delta
for complementary series.

3.2.3
A discrete basis of
{\cal F}_{\Delta}

Thus far we have shown that the function spaces
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
furnish unitary irreducible representations of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
when
\Delta\in\frac{1}{2}+i\mathbb{R}
and
0<\Delta<1
. Indeed they are the same as
{\cal P}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal C}_{\Delta}
respectively. To prove this claim, we first find the eigenbasis of
L_{0}=i\left(\frac{1+x^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}+\Delta x\right)

\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{1-ix}{1+ix}\right)^{n}\frac{2^{\Delta}}{(1+x^{2})^{\Delta}}

(3.29)

In addition, it is straightforward to check

\displaystyle L_{+}\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}=(n+\Delta)\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n+1},\,\,\,\,\,L_{-}\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}=(n-\Delta)\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n-1}

(3.30)

which take the same form as the action of

, c.f. (
3.5

For
\boxed{\Delta\in\frac{1}{2}+i\mathbb{R}}
, the

inner product yields

\displaystyle(\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n},\psi^{(\Delta)}_{m})=c\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dx\,\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}(x)^{*}\psi^{(\Delta)}_{m}(x)=c\,\delta_{nm}

(3.31)

By choosing
c=1
, we get to identify
\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}(x)
as the state
|n\rangle
in
{\cal P}_{\Delta}
.

For
\boxed{0<\Delta<1}
, the inner product can be computed by expanding the kernel
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x_{1}\!-\!x_{2})
in terms of
\psi^{(\bar{\Delta})}_{n}
. Define
x_{i}=\tan\frac{\theta_{i}}{2}
and we rewrite
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x_{1}\!-\!x_{2})
as

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x_{12})

\displaystyle=c\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1+x_{1}^{2})^{\bar{\Delta}}}\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1+x^{2}_{2})^{\bar{\Delta}}}\frac{1}{(1-\cos\theta_{12})^{\bar{\Delta}}}

(3.32)

where
x_{12}=x_{1}-x_{2}
and
\theta_{12}\equiv\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
. The desired expansion of
{\cal K}_{\Delta}
is equivalent to the harmonic expansion of
\frac{1}{(1-\cos\theta)^{\bar{\Delta}}}
, which can be done as follows. First use the Schwinger’s trick to write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\cos\theta)^{\bar{\Delta}}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s}\,s^{\bar{\Delta}}e^{-s+s\cos\theta}

(3.33)

Then use the plane wave harmonic expansion

\displaystyle e^{s\cos\theta}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}(s)\,e^{in\theta}

(3.34)

where the coefficients are modified Bessel functions.
Using this in the above Schwinger integral we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\cos\theta)^{\bar{\Delta}}}=\frac{1}{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta-\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+n)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)}\,e^{in\theta}

(3.35)

To be precise, convergence of Schwinger integral requires
\frac{1}{2}<\Delta<1
. For more general
\Delta
, the above result can be viewed as the integral renormalized by analytic continuation. Plugging (
3.35
) into (
3.32
), we obtain the following expansion of
{\cal K}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x_{12})

\displaystyle=\frac{c\,\Gamma(\Delta-\frac{1}{2})}{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1+x_{1}^{2})^{\bar{\Delta}}}\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1+x^{2}_{2})^{\bar{\Delta}}}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+n)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)}\,e^{in\theta_{12}}

\displaystyle=2^{\Delta}\sqrt{\pi}\,c\frac{\Gamma(\Delta-\frac{1}{2})}{\,\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+n)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)}\,\psi^{(\bar{\Delta})}_{n}(x_{1})^{*}\psi^{(\bar{\Delta})}_{n}(x_{2})

(3.36)

where in the last line we have used
e^{-i\theta_{i}}=\frac{1-ix_{i}}{1+ix_{i}}
. By choosing
c=\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}{2^{\Delta}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\Delta-\frac{1}{2})}
, the basis
\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}
is normalized as

\displaystyle\left(\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n},\psi^{(\Delta)}_{m}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}(x_{1})^{*}{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x_{12})\psi^{(\Delta)}_{m}(x_{2})=\delta_{nm}\,\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+n)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)}

(3.37)

and hence we get to identify
\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}
as
|n\rangle
in
{\cal C}_{\Delta}
.

{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}
is

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\psi)(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2N-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}+i\epsilon}\frac{dy}{2\pi i}\,{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)\psi(y),\,\,\,\,\,{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(z)=z^{2(N-1)}\log(z)

(3.42)

where the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
is understood and the branch cut of
\log z
is chosen to be the
z<0
line. When acting
\partial_{x}^{2N-1}
on
({\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\psi)(x)
, the kernel
{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)
becomes
\frac{\Gamma(2N-1)}{x-y}
and we can close the contour in the lower half plane, picking up the pole at
y=x
since
\psi(y)
is holomorphic in the lower half plane and decays fast enough at
\infty
. Alternatively, we can compute the action of
{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}
on the basis
\psi^{(N)}_{n}
directly, which is left to appendix
B

\displaystyle\left({\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\psi^{(N)}_{n}\right)(x)=i(-)^{N+1}\frac{\Gamma(n-N+1)}{\Gamma(N+n)}\psi^{(1-N)}_{n}(x)+\text{Pol}_{N,n}(x)

(3.43)

where
\text{Pol}_{N,n}(x)
is a polynomial annihilated by
\partial_{x}^{2N-1}
. Altogether, the inner product on the space
{\cal F}_{N}^{+}
induced by (
3.40
) and (
3.42
) is

\displaystyle(\psi,\psi)_{{\cal F}^{+}_{N}}=({\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\psi,{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\psi)_{1}=\frac{(-)^{N}c_{N}}{2\pi\Gamma(2N-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\int_{\mathbb{R}+i\epsilon}dy\,\psi(x)^{*}{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x\!-\!y)\psi(y)

(3.44)

In particular, choosing
c_{N}=1
, we get

\displaystyle(\psi^{(N)}_{m},\psi^{(N)}_{n})_{{\cal F}^{+}_{N}}=\frac{\Gamma(n-N+1)}{\Gamma(N+n)}\delta_{mn}

(3.45)

consistent with the inner product (
A.7
) for discrete series
{\cal D}^{+}_{N}
.

Before moving to the next section, we want to make some remarks about the kernel
{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x,y)
.

Remark 3.1
.

For complementary series of scaling dimension
\Delta
, the inner product is defined through the kernel
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)\sim\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}
. As a function of
\Delta
,
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)
is well-defined on the whole complex plane as long as
x\not=y
. Taylor expansion of
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)
around
\Delta=N
yields

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta\to N}}{{\sim}}|x-y|^{2(N-1)}+2(\Delta-N)|x-y|^{2(N-1)}\log(x-y)+\cdots

(3.46)

The first term on the R.H.S is
{\cal K}_{N}(x,y)
and we have argued that it leads to a trivial inner product for
{\cal F}^{+}_{N}
. The second term, with the numerical factor stripped off, is exactly the kernel
{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)
that defines the inner product for discrete series (up to a contour prescription).

Remark 3.2
.

Unlike
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)
,
{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)
is not
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
-invariant. For example, under scaling transformation

\displaystyle e^{\lambda D}:{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)\to e^{2\lambda(1-N)}{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(e^{\lambda}x-e^{\lambda}y)={\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)+\lambda(x-y)^{2(N-1)}

(3.47)

and under special conformal transformation

\displaystyle e^{bK}:{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)

\displaystyle\to{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}\left(\frac{x}{1-bx}-\frac{y}{1-by}\right)(1-bx)^{2(N-1)}(1-by)^{2(N-1)}

\displaystyle\to{\mathfrak{L}}_{N}(x-y)+(x-y)^{2(N-1)}(\log(1-bx)+\log(1-by))

(3.48)

However, these extra terms do not contribute to the inner product (
3.44
).

3.2.5
Shadow transformations

In the eq. (
3.13
), we define the so-called shadow transformation as an intertwining map between the two representations with scaling dimension
\Delta
and
\bar{\Delta}
respectively. In the wavefunction picture, we want to realize
{\cal S}_{\Delta}
as a linear operator acting on any
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
by using a kernel function
S_{\Delta}(x,y)

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\Delta}:\,\,\psi(x)\longmapsto\int\,dyS_{\Delta}(x,y)\psi(y)

(3.49)

The intertwining condition imposes nontrivial constrains on
S_{\Delta}(x,y)
. These constrains turn out to be nothing but conformal Ward identities associated to the conformal group
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
. Thus
S_{\Delta}(x,y)
is the two-point function of a scalar primary operator with scaling dimension
\bar{\Delta}

\displaystyle S_{\Delta}(x,y)=\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}N_{\Delta}}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}},\,\,\,\,\,N_{\Delta}=\frac{1}{2^{\Delta}\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\right)}

(3.50)

where the normalization constant
N_{\Delta}
is chosen such that
\psi_{n}^{(\Delta)}
is mapped to
\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta}+n)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)}\psi^{(\bar{\Delta})}_{n}
as in (
3.13
) with
s_{\Delta}\equiv 1
. For
\Delta\in\frac{1}{2}+i\mu
or
0<\Delta<1
, the shadow transformation
{\cal S}_{\Delta}
is an isomorphism between
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal F}_{\bar{\Delta}}
because it has a well-defined inverse given by
{\cal S}_{\bar{\Delta}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dz\,\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}\,N_{\Delta}}{|x-z|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}\frac{2^{\Delta}\,N_{\bar{\Delta}}}{|z-y|^{2\Delta}}=\delta(x-y)

(3.51)

This equation can be easily checked by Fourier transformation. Using eq. (
3.2.2
) and (
3.50
), it is clear that the shadow transformation
{\cal S}_{\Delta}
amounts to a rescaling in momentum space, namely
{\cal S}_{\Delta}:\psi(p)\longmapsto p^{2\bar{\Delta}-1}\psi(p)
.

Remark 3.3
.

For
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
, the shadow transformation is not an isomorphism between
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal F}_{\bar{\Delta}}
. For example, when
\Delta=1-N
is a nonpositive integer,
\psi^{(1-N)}_{n}
with
n\leq N-1
are annihilated by
{\cal S}_{1-N}
. Indeed, in this case,
{\cal S}_{1-N}
is equivalent to the differential operator
\partial^{2N-1}_{x}
up to normalization.

3.2.6
Summary

Given a complex constant
\Delta
which we call
scaling dimension
, the
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
generators act on the wavefunction space
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
spanned by
\left\{\psi^{(\Delta)}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(\frac{1-ix}{1+ix})^{n}\frac{2^{\Delta}}{(1+x^{2})^{\Delta}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
as follows

\displaystyle P\psi(x)=-\partial_{x}\psi(x),\,\,\,\,\,D\psi(x)=-(x\partial_{x}+\Delta)\psi(x),\,\,\,\,\,K\psi(x)=-(x^{2}\partial_{x}+2\Delta x)\psi(x)

(3.52)

The quadratic Casimir takes the value
\Delta(1-\Delta)
acting on
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
. All unitary irreducible representations of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
can be realized as either
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
or its invariant subspace for certain values of
\Delta
:

•

Case I
:
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu,\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
inner product
(\psi,\psi)=\int dx\psi^{*}(x)\psi(x)
for any
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
. The representations of
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\pm i\mu
are equivalent. This is the
principal series
.

•

Case II
:
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+\nu,\,\,-\frac{1}{2}<\nu<\frac{1}{2}
with inner product
(\psi,\psi)=\int dxdy\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}N_{\Delta}}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}\psi(x)^{*}\psi(y)
or equivalently in momentum space
(\psi,\psi)=\int\frac{dp}{2\pi}\,p^{1-2\Delta}|\psi(p)|^{2}
for any
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
, where
N_{\Delta}
is defined in (
3.50
). The representations of
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\pm\nu
are equivalent. This is the
complementary series
.

•

Case III
:
\Delta=N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with inner product

\displaystyle(\psi,\psi)_{{\cal F}^{\pm}_{N}}=\frac{(-)^{N}}{2\pi\Gamma(2N-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\int_{\mathbb{R}\pm i\epsilon}dy\,\psi(x)^{*}(x-y)^{2(N-1)}\log(x-y)\psi(y)

(3.53)

where the contour
\mathbb{R}+i\epsilon
works for
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}^{+}_{N}
which is spanned by
\{\psi^{(N)}_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq N}
and the contour
\mathbb{R}-i\epsilon
works for
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}^{-}_{N}
which is spanned by
\{\psi^{(N)}_{n}(x)\}_{n\leq-N}
. This is the
discrete series
.

3.3
Understand the two constructions from QFT

In this subsection, we provide a physical picture to understand both constructions for the UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
and their relation.
For this purpose, let’s consider a scalar field
\phi
in
\text{dS}_{2}
of a positive mass
m
. Parameterize the mass by
m^{2}=\Delta(1-\Delta)
, with
\Delta\in(0,1)
for a light scalar, i.e.
0<m<\frac{1}{2}
, and
\Delta\in\frac{1}{2}+i\mathbb{R}
for a heavy scalar, i.e.
m\geq\frac{1}{2}
. In the following discussion, we will focus on
heavy

\phi
with
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu
and prove that its single-particle Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
furnishes the principal series of scaling dimension
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu
. A similar computation can be found in
[
59
,
22
,
60
]
. In addition, we will also illustrate how to realize the discrete basis
|n\rangle
, c.f. eq. (
3.5
), and the continuous basis
|x\rangle
, c.f. eq. (
3.16
), on the Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
. A similar story for light scalars is left to appendix
C
. The QFT picture for
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
discrete series is still an open question.

Figure 3.6
:
The Penrose diagram of
\text{dS}_{2}
. The two sides of the Penrose diagram are identified and hence every constant time slice is a circle. The Wick rotation of the
t\leq 0
part of
\text{dS}_{2}
is represented by the region bounded by dashed lines. The Euclidean vacuum
|E\rangle
can be computed by Euclidean path integral in this region.

3.3.1
Mode expansions

We work in global conformal time coordinates
(t,\varphi)
of
\text{dS}_{2}
, related to the embedding space coordinates by

\displaystyle X^{0}=\tan t,\,\,\,\,\,X^{1}=\frac{\cos\varphi}{\cos t},\,\,\,\,\,X^{2}=\frac{\sin\varphi}{\cos t},\,\,\,\,\,-\frac{\pi}{2}<t<\frac{\pi}{2}

(3.54)

in terms of which, the
\text{dS}_{2}
metric is
ds^{2}=\frac{-dt^{2}+d\varphi^{2}}{\cos^{2}t}
, conformally equivalent to a flat cylinder. The future boundary is at
t\to\frac{\pi}{2}
and the past boundary is at
t\to-\frac{\pi}{2}
. The Wick rotation to
S^{2}
is realized by
t\to-i\tau
, with
\tau\to-\infty
being the southern pole of
S^{2}
. The Penrose diagram of
\text{dS}_{2}
is given by fig. (
3.6
). Consider solutions to the wave equation
(\nabla^{2}-m^{2})\phi=0
of the form
\phi(t,\varphi)=f(t)e^{-in\varphi},n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
n
labels angular momentum along the spatial circle. The equation for
f(t)
is

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(t)+n^{2}f(t)+\frac{m^{2}}{\cos^{2}t}f(t)=0

(3.55)

The general solution to this ODE is
f(t)=c\,g_{n}(t)+c^{*}\,g_{n}(t)^{*}
, where

\displaystyle g_{n}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\bar{\Delta})}{\sqrt{2}}\,e^{-in\,t}\,\mathbf{F}\left(\Delta,\bar{\Delta},n+1,\frac{1}{1+e^{2it}}\right),\,\,\,\,\,\bar{\Delta}=1-\Delta

(3.56)

Here we have used the regularized hypergeometric function
\mathbf{F}(a,b,c,z)\equiv\frac{1}{\Gamma(c)}F(a,b,c,z)
because it is well-defined when
c
is a nonpositive integer.

The Klein-Gordon(KG) inner product is defined as

\displaystyle(\phi,\phi^{\prime})_{\text{KG}}=i\int_{0}^{2\pi}\,d\varphi\,\left(\phi^{*}\,\partial_{t}\phi^{\prime}-\partial_{t}\phi^{*}\,\phi^{\prime}\right)

(3.57)

For the modes
\phi_{n}=g_{n}(t)\frac{e^{-in\varphi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
, the KG inner product becomes
(\phi_{n},\phi_{m})_{\text{KG}}=\delta_{nm}
. A canonically normalized mode expansion of
\phi
is thus

\displaystyle\phi(t,\varphi)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{n}\,a_{n}+\phi_{n}^{*}\,a_{n}^{\dagger}

(3.58)

The vacuum
|E\rangle
of the theory is annihilated by all
a_{n}
. It is the so-called Euclidean vacuum because
\phi_{n}
is regular on the lower hemisphere of the Wick rotated
\text{dS}_{2}

[
61
]
. The states
|n\rangle_{E}=a_{n}^{\dagger}|E\rangle
, satisfying
{}_{E}\langle m|n\rangle_{E}=\delta_{mn}
, form a normalized orthonormal basis of global single-particle Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
. Next, we will show that
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
is isomorphic to the principal series representation
{\cal P}_{\Delta}
.

3.3.2
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,2)
action on
{\cal H}_{\Delta}

Define the action of
L_{AB}
on the quantum field
\phi(t,\varphi)
to be

\displaystyle[L_{AB},\phi(t,\varphi)]\equiv-{\cal L}_{AB}\phi(t,\varphi),\,\,\,\,\,{\cal L}_{AB}\equiv X_{A}\partial_{B}-X_{B}\partial_{A}

(3.59)

where
{\cal L}_{AB}
are Killing vectors of
\text{dS}_{2}
in embedding space coordinates and an extra minus is introduced such that
L_{AB}
satisfy the commutation relations (
2.2
). In conformal global coordinates (
3.54
), these Killing vectors are

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{01}=-\cos t\cos\varphi\partial_{t}+\sin t\,\sin\varphi\partial_{\varphi},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal L}_{02}=-\cos t\sin\varphi\partial_{t}-\sin t\,\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal L}_{12}=\partial_{\varphi}

(3.60)

and hence the differential operator realization of
\{L_{0},L_{\pm}\}
defined in subsection
3.1
becomes

\displaystyle L_{0}\Leftrightarrow-{\cal L}_{0}=-i\partial_{\varphi},\,\,\,\,\,L_{\pm}\Leftrightarrow-{\cal L}_{\pm}=-ie^{\mp i\varphi}(\cos\partial_{t}\mp i\sin t\,\partial_{\varphi})

(3.61)

Using the following recurrence relations of regularized hypergeometric functions

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\left(z^{c-1}\mathbf{F}(a,b,c,z)\right)=z^{c-2}\mathbf{F}(a,b,c-1,z)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\left((1-z)^{a+b-c}\mathbf{F}(a,b,c,z)\right)=(c-a)(c-b)(1-z)^{a+b-c-1}\mathbf{F}(a,b,c+1,z)

(3.62)

one can check

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\pm}\phi_{n}=(n\pm\Delta)\phi_{n\pm 1},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal L}_{\pm}\phi_{n}^{*}=-(n\mp\bar{\Delta})\phi^{*}_{n\mp 1}

(3.63)

Plug (
3.63
) into (
3.59
) and with some reshuffle of indices, we obtain

\displaystyle[L_{\pm},a_{n}]=-(n\mp\bar{\Delta})a_{n\mp 1},\,\,\,\,\,[L_{\pm},a^{\dagger}_{n}]=(n\pm\Delta)a^{\dagger}_{n\pm 1}

(3.64)

These commutation relations have some important implications. First, they are consistent with the expected reality condition
L^{\dagger}_{\pm}=L_{\mp}
. Second, together will the almost obvious commutation relation
[L_{0},a_{n}^{\dagger}]=na^{\dagger}_{n}
, they yield the
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,2)
action on
{\cal H}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle L_{\pm}|n\rangle_{E}=(n\pm\Delta)|n\pm 1\rangle_{E},\,\,\,\,\,L_{0}|n\rangle_{E}=n|n\rangle_{E}

(3.65)

With the inner product
{}_{E}\langle m|n\rangle_{E}=\delta_{mn}
and the
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,2)
action (
3.65
), we are able to identify the single-particle Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
as the principal series representation
{\cal P}_{\Delta}
constructed in section
3.1
.

3.3.3
The continuous basis

In the CFT-type construction, we have used the basis
\{P,D,K\}
of
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,2)
. Let
{\cal P},{\cal D},{\cal K}
be their differential operator realizations respectively and they take the following form

\displaystyle{\cal P}=-\cos t\cos\varphi\partial_{t}+(\sin t\,\sin\varphi-1)\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle{\cal K}=\cos t\cos\varphi\partial_{t}-(\sin t\sin\varphi+1)\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle{\cal D}=-\cos t\sin\varphi\partial_{t}-\sin t\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi}

(3.66)

Next we push the scalar field
\phi
to the future boundary and only keep track of the leading fall-offs. More precisely, put
t=\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta
and expand
\phi
in the limit
\delta\to 0
, up to
(1+{\cal O}(\delta^{2}))
-corrections

\displaystyle\phi\left(t=\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\varphi\right)\overset{\delta\to 0}{\approx}C_{\Delta}\,\delta^{\Delta}{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)+C_{\bar{\Delta}}\,\delta^{\bar{\Delta}}{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)

(3.67)

where

\displaystyle C_{\Delta}=2^{i\mu}\frac{\Gamma(-2i\mu)}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})},\,\,\,\,\,\,C_{\bar{\Delta}}=C_{\Delta}^{*}=2^{-i\mu}\frac{\Gamma(2i\mu)}{\Gamma(\Delta)}

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle(-i)^{n+\Delta}\frac{e^{-in\varphi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}a_{n}+i^{n+\Delta}\frac{\Gamma(n+\Delta)}{\Gamma(n+\bar{\Delta})}\frac{e^{in\varphi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}a^{\dagger}_{n},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)={\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)^{*}

(3.68)

Combining (
3.59
), (
3.3.3
) and (
3.67
), we obtain how the conformal algebra
\{P,K,D\}
acts on the two boundary operators
{\cal O}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}

\displaystyle[P,{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)]=\left((1-\sin\varphi)\partial_{\varphi}-\Delta\cos\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi),\,\,\,\,\,\,[P,{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)]=\left((1-\sin\varphi)\partial_{\varphi}-\bar{\Delta}\cos\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle[K,{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)]=\left((1+\sin\varphi)\partial_{\varphi}+\Delta\cos\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi),\,\,\,\,\,[K,{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)]=\left((1+\sin\varphi)\partial_{\varphi}+\bar{\Delta}\cos\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle[D,{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)]=\left(\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi}-\Delta\sin\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,[D,{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)]=\left(\cos\varphi\partial_{\varphi}-\bar{\Delta}\sin\varphi\right){\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)

(3.69)

which implies that
{\cal O}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}
are two primary operators of scaling dimension
\Delta
and
\bar{\Delta}
respectively. In particular, their two-point functions with respect to the Euclidean vacuum are

\displaystyle\langle E|{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi_{1}){\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi_{2})|E\rangle=\frac{N_{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1-\cos\varphi_{12})^{\Delta}},\,\,\,\,\,\langle E|{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi_{1}){\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi_{2})|E\rangle=\frac{N_{\Delta}}{(1-\cos\varphi_{12})^{\bar{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle\langle E|{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi_{1}){\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi_{2})|E\rangle=e^{\pi\mu}\delta(\varphi_{12}),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\langle E|{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi_{1}){\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi_{2})|E\rangle=e^{-\pi\mu}\delta(\varphi_{12})

(3.70)

where the normalization constant
N_{\Delta}
is defined in eq. (
3.50
). Given these two different primary operators
{\cal O}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}
, one would naively expect them to generate two UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
at the boundary circle of
\text{dS}_{2}
.
However, this is
not
the case. The obvious reason is that they are both linear combinations of the annihilation operators
a_{n}
and the creation operators
a_{n}^{\dagger}
. Although different as operators, when acting with them on the Euclidean vacuum, the annihilation part drops out, so the resulting single-particle excitations are nothing but the Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
. To make this argument more precisely, consider the following two families of states generated by
{\cal O}_{\Delta}
and
{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}
respectively

\displaystyle|\Delta,\varphi\rangle\equiv{\cal O}_{\Delta}(\varphi)|E\rangle=\sum_{n}i^{n+\Delta}\frac{\Gamma(n+\Delta)}{\Gamma(n+\bar{\Delta})}\frac{e^{in\varphi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}|n\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle|\bar{\Delta},\varphi\rangle\equiv{\cal O}_{\bar{\Delta}}(\varphi)|E\rangle=\sum_{n}i^{n+\bar{\Delta}}\frac{e^{in\varphi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}|n\rangle_{E}

(3.71)

They are both complete basis of the single-particle Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{{\cal H}_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n}|n\rangle_{E\,E}\langle n|

\displaystyle=e^{\pi\mu}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi|\Delta,\varphi\rangle\langle\Delta,\varphi|=e^{-\pi\mu}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi|\bar{\Delta},\varphi\rangle\langle\bar{\Delta},\varphi|

(3.72)

Using the completeness condition (
3.3.3
), we can relate the two basis as follows

\displaystyle|\Delta,\varphi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-\pi\mu}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{2}\langle\bar{\Delta},\varphi_{2}|\Delta,\varphi_{1}\rangle|\bar{\Delta},\varphi_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-\pi\mu}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{2}\frac{N_{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1-\cos\varphi_{12})^{\Delta}}|\bar{\Delta},\varphi_{2}\rangle

(3.73)

where the kernel function
\frac{N_{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1-\cos\varphi_{12})^{\Delta}}
is the
S^{1}
counterpart of the kernel
S_{\bar{\Delta}}(x_{12})
.
Altogether, we have only one UIR at the boundary even though there exist two boundary primary operators, and we are free to choose either
|\Delta,\varphi\rangle
or
|\bar{\Delta},\varphi\rangle
as a (continuous) basis of
{\cal H}_{\Delta}
.

Figure 3.7
:
The stereographic map between
S^{1}
and
\mathbb{R}
.

To make the connection with the
|x\rangle
basis more explicit, let’s switch to the stereographic coordinate of
S^{1}
, namely
\cos\varphi=\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}},\sin\varphi=\frac{x^{2}-1}{x^{2}+1}
(see fig. (
3.7
) for an intuitive geometric picture of this coordinate transformation) and rescale the
|\bar{\Delta},\varphi\rangle
state by a Weyl factor associated to the map
x\to\varphi
:

\displaystyle|\bar{\Delta},x\rangle=\left(\frac{2}{1+x^{2}}\right)^{\bar{\Delta}}|\bar{\Delta},\varphi(x)\rangle

(3.74)

Using the commutation relations in (
3.3.3
), it is straightforward to check that (dropping the label
\bar{\Delta}
for
|\bar{\Delta},x\rangle
)

\displaystyle P|x\rangle=\partial_{x}|x\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,K|x\rangle=\left(x^{2}\partial_{x}+2\bar{\Delta}x\right)|x\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,D|x\rangle=(x\partial_{x}+\bar{\Delta})|x\rangle

(3.75)

which is nothing but eq. (
3.16
) in the previous CFT-type construction. In addition, the inner product of the
|x\rangle
-basis can be derived from (
3.3.3
)

\displaystyle\langle x|y\rangle=\frac{2\,e^{\pi\mu}}{(1+x^{2})}\delta(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))=e^{\pi\mu}\delta(x-y)

(3.76)

which is consistent with eq. (
3.2.2
) for
c=e^{\pi\mu}
.

3.3.4
Summary

The physical picture discussed above can be summarized as a dictionary between representation theory of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
and local quantum field theory in
\text{dS}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\text{Principal/Complementary Series}\,\,{\cal F}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\text{The single-Hilbert space}\,\,{\cal H}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle{\text{SO}}(1,2)\text{-invariant inner product of}\,\,{\cal F}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\text{The standard QFT inner product on}\,\,{\cal H}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\text{The discrete}\,\,|n\rangle\,\,\text{basis}

4.3
(Nonexceptional) unitary scalar representations

Recall that a wavefunction
\psi\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
defines a ket
|\psi\rangle=\int d^{d}x\,\psi(x)|x\rangle
. So an inner product on
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
is fixed by defining a pairing
\langle x|y\rangle
:

\displaystyle(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\equiv\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}y\,\psi_{1}(x)^{*}{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)\psi_{2}(y),\,\,\,\,\,{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)=\langle x|y\rangle

(4.41)

Imposing the dS reality condition
L_{AB}^{\dagger}=-L_{AB}
as in section
3.2.2
, we find that the function
{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)
exists only in the following two cases

\displaystyle\text{I}:\Delta=\frac{d}{2}+i\mu,\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)=\,\delta^{d}(x-y)

\displaystyle\text{II}:\Delta\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)=\frac{2^{\bar{\Delta}}c_{\Delta}}{|x-y|^{2\bar{\Delta}}}

(4.42)

where
c_{\Delta}
is a normalization constant.
In case (I), (
4.41
) is the standard
L^{2}
inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is positive definite and normalizable since
\psi(x)\in{\cal F}_{\Delta}
falls off as
\frac{1}{|x|^{2\Delta}}
for large
x
. Therefore, the function space
{\cal F}_{\frac{d}{2}+i\mu}
equipped with the standard
L^{2}
inner product furnishes a unitary irreducible representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
, which is known as the (unitary)
scalar principal series representation
of scaling dimension
\Delta=\frac{d}{2}+i\mu
. In the case (II), we choose
c_{\Delta}=N^{+}_{\Delta}=\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}{2^{\Delta}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-\bar{\Delta})}
, so that
\boxed{{\cal K}_{\Delta}(x,y)=S^{+}_{\Delta}(x,y)}
, i.e. the kernel of shadow transformation
{\cal S}^{+}_{\Delta}:{\cal F}_{\Delta}\to{\cal F}_{\bar{\Delta}}
. Using the Fourier transformation of
S^{+}_{\Delta}(x)
, c.f. eq.(
4.2
), we rewrite the inner product (
4.41
) in momentum space as

\displaystyle(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\,d^{d}p\,p^{2\bar{\Delta}-d}\psi_{1}(p)^{*}\psi_{2}(p)

(4.43)

where
\psi(p)\equiv\int\,d^{d}x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\psi(x)
is the Fourier transformation of
\psi(x)
. This inner product is positive definite as long as it is convergent. Due to the smoothness of
\psi(x)
, its Fourier transformation
\psi(p)
decays exponentially for large
p
. So the
p
-integral in (
4.43
) is convergent around
p\to\infty
. For small
p
, as we have argued in the
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
case,
\psi(p)
has two types of leading fall-offs:
p^{0}
and
p^{2\Delta-d}
. Then the requirement of convergence near
p\to 0
yields
\boxed{0<\Delta<d}
. Therefore, the representations
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
with
0<\Delta<d
are unitary, known as the (unitary)
scalar complementary series
.

Before moving to spinning representations, we want to present a different way to expand the kernel
{\cal K}_{\Delta}
based on our discussion in the section
4.1
. It yields the same constraint on the scaling dimension. Using the Weyl transformation
\psi(x)=\left(\frac{2}{1+x^{2}}\right)^{\Delta}\hat{\psi}(x)
, where
\hat{\psi}(x)
is a function on
S^{d}
, we rewrite the inner product (
4.41
) as

\displaystyle(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=N^{+}_{\Delta}\int\,\frac{d^{d}x}{\left(\frac{1+x^{2}}{2}\right)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}y}{\left(\frac{1+y^{2}}{2}\right)^{d}}\left(\frac{(1+x^{2})(1+y^{2})}{2(x-y)^{2}}\right)^{\bar{\Delta}}\hat{\psi}(x)^{*}\hat{\psi}(y)

(4.44)

Switch to spherical coordinates
\Omega^{i}=(\sin\theta\omega^{i},\cos\theta),\omega^{i}\in S^{d-1}
which is related the stereographic coordinates
x^{i}
by
x^{i}=\cot\frac{\theta}{2}\omega^{i}
and eq. (
4.44
) becomes an integral on
S^{d}\times S^{d}

\displaystyle(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=\int d^{d}\Omega_{1}\,d^{d}\Omega_{2}\,\hat{\cal K}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})\hat{\psi}(\Omega_{1})^{*}\hat{\psi}_{2}(\Omega_{2}),\,\,\,\,\,\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\frac{N^{+}_{\Delta}}{(1-\Omega_{1}\cdot\Omega_{2})^{\bar{\Delta}}}

(4.45)

Now we need to perform a harmonic expansion for the new kernel
\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}
, along the lines of (
3.35
). First, write it as an integral using Schwinger’s trick

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\frac{N^{+}_{\Delta}}{\Gamma(\bar{\Delta})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s}\,s^{\bar{\Delta}}e^{-s+s\Omega_{1}\cdot\Omega_{2}}

(4.46)

Then plug it in the expansion of plane waves in Gegenbauer polynomials
[
64
]

\displaystyle e^{s\Omega_{1}\cdot\Omega_{2}}=\Gamma(\alpha)\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{-\alpha}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(\alpha+\ell)I_{\alpha+\ell}(s)C^{\alpha}_{\ell}(\Omega_{1}\cdot\Omega_{2}),\,\,\,\,\,\alpha=\frac{d-1}{2}

(4.47)

Using the addition theorem, Gegenbauer polynomials can thus be expanded in spherical harmonics on
S^{d}

[
65
]

\displaystyle C^{\alpha}_{\ell}(\Omega_{1}\cdot\Omega_{2})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha)(\ell+\alpha)}\sum_{\bm{m}}Y_{\ell\bm{m}}(\Omega_{1})Y_{\ell\bm{m}}(\Omega_{2})^{*}

(4.48)

Performing the
s
-integral, we obtain the desired expansion of the kernel
\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}
in spherical harmonics

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{\bm{m}}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma(\ell+\Delta)}Y_{\ell\bm{m}}(\Omega_{1})Y_{\ell\bm{m}}(\Omega_{2})^{*}

(4.49)

where we have plugged in the explicit form of
N^{+}_{\Delta}
.
For
\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}
to be positive definite, the sign of
\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma(\ell+\Delta)}
should
not
oscillate with
\ell
, which requires
\frac{\ell+\bar{\Delta}}{\ell+\Delta}>0
for any
\ell\geq 0
. The most stringent constraint comes from
\ell=0
and it is
\boxed{0<\Delta<d}
, in agreement with what we have found in momentum space. With this constraint satisfied,
\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma(\ell+\Delta)}
stays positive for all
\ell
and hence
\hat{\cal K}_{\Delta}
is positive definite.

Remark 4.2
.

When
\Delta
is a nonpositive integer,
\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma(\ell+\Delta)}
vanishes for
0\leq\ell\leq-\Delta
. Thus the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell\bm{m}}
with
0\leq\ell\leq-\Delta
are null states. They furnish an irreducible representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
, which is checked explicitly in appendix
E
. When
\bar{\Delta}
is a nonpositive integer, we choose
c_{\Delta}=N^{-}_{\Delta}
. It amounts to replacing
\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\bar{\Delta})}{\Gamma(\ell+\Delta)}
by
\frac{(\bar{\Delta})_{\ell}}{(\Delta)_{\ell}}
in the expansion (
4.49
).
In this case, all
Y_{\ell\bm{m}}
with
\ell\geq 1-\bar{\Delta}
are null and carry an irreducible representation, which is again checked in the appendix
E
.

4.4
(Nonexceptional) unitary spinning representations

For a spinning representation
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
, inner product is defined by the kernel

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},x_{2})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s},j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}\equiv\,_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}\langle x_{1}|x_{2}\rangle_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}

(4.50)

In the index free formalism, we contract the
i
-indices with a null vector
z
and contract the
j
-indices with a different null vector
w

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},z;x_{2},w)\equiv\,{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},x_{2})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s},j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}\,z^{i_{1}}\cdots z^{i_{s}}w^{j_{1}}\cdots w^{j_{s}}

(4.51)

With the de Sitter reality condition imposed,
{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},z;x_{2},w)
exists only for two cases (I):
\Delta=\frac{d}{2}+i\mu
and (II):
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
. In the first case,
{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}
is simply a
\delta
-function in both spacetime coordinate and spin indices, i.e.

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},z;x_{2},w)=\delta^{d}(x_{1},x_{2})(z\cdot w)^{s}

(4.52)

and hence the inner product defined by this
{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}
becomes the
L^{2}
-inner product for spin-
s
tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\boxed{(\psi,\varphi)_{\cal P}\equiv\int d^{d}x\,\psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{*}(x)\,\varphi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}(x)}

(4.53)

The normalizability of this inner product on
{\cal F}_{\frac{d}{2}+i\mu,s}
is guaranteed by the asymptotic behavior (
4.12
). Therefore,
{\cal F}_{\frac{d}{2}+i\mu,s}
is a unitary irreducible representation, belonging to the so-called (unitary)
spinning principal series
. In the second case, the reality condition is actually equivalent to conformal Ward identities for two-point functions. Therefore
{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}
is the same as
S_{\Delta,s}
up to normalization, i.e.

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}(x_{1},z;x_{2},w)=c_{\Delta,s}\frac{(-z\cdot R(x_{12})\cdot w)^{s}}{\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{2}_{12}\right)^{\bar{\Delta}}}

(4.54)

For a generic
\Delta
, we choose
c_{\Delta,s}=N^{+}_{\Delta,s}
and hence
{\cal K}_{\Delta,s}=S^{+}_{\Delta,s}
, which in momentum space is (c.f. eq. (
4.2
))

\displaystyle S^{+}_{\Delta,s}(p;z,w)=p^{2\bar{\Delta}-d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}\kappa_{s\ell}(\Delta)\Pi^{s\ell}(\hat{p};z,w),\,\,\,\,\,\kappa_{s\ell}(\Delta)=\frac{(\Delta+\ell-1)_{s-\ell}}{(\bar{\Delta}+\ell-1)_{s-\ell}}

(4.55)

As in the scalar case, the inner product defined by
S^{+}_{\Delta,s}
is normalizable when
0<\Delta<d
. Additionally, for
S^{+}_{\Delta,s}
to be positive, we need all
\kappa_{s\ell}(\Delta)
to have a fixed sign (insert an overall minus sign if negative) which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{s,\ell+1}(\Delta)}{\kappa_{s,\ell}(\Delta)}=\frac{\bar{\Delta}+\ell-1}{\Delta+\ell-1}>0

(4.56)

The eq. (
4.56
) holds for all
\ell
if and only if
\boxed{1<\Delta<d-1}
. It is also straightforward to check that all
\kappa_{s\ell}(\Delta)
are indeed positive for
\Delta
in this range. Altogether,
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
with the following inner product

\displaystyle\boxed{(\psi,\varphi)_{\cal C}=\int\,d^{d}x_{1}\,d^{d}x_{2}\,\psi^{*}(x_{1})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}\,S^{+}_{\Delta,s}(x_{12})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s},j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}\varphi(x_{2})_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}}

(4.57)

is a unitary irreducible representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
when
1<\Delta<d-1
. It belongs to the so-called (unitary)
spinning complementary series
.

We have studied the constraint of unitarity on

4.8
Comments on the physical picture

The dictionary between UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
and QFT in

is provided above. In this subsection, we’d like to give a more elaborate and detailed discussion about the realization of the representations in a physical context. We will use massive and massless spin-1 fields to illustrate the basis idea of the realization, and the discussion can be straightforwardly generalized to higher spin fields.

First, consider a massive spin-1 field
A_{\mu}
of mass

in the future planar patch of
\text{dS}_{d+1}

\displaystyle X^{0}=-\frac{1+x^{2}-\eta^{2}}{2\eta},\,\,\,\,\,X^{i}=-\frac{x^{i}}{\eta},\,\,\,\,\,X^{d+1}=\frac{1-x^{2}+\eta^{2}}{2\eta}

corresponds to the future boundary of

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{-d\eta^{2}+dx^{2}}{\eta^{2}}

(4.78)

In the planar patch, Killing vector fields associated to the conformal generators

are

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{i}=-\partial_{i},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal K}_{i}=(x^{2}-\eta^{2})\partial_{i}-2x_{i}(x\cdot\partial_{x}+\eta\partial_{\eta})

\displaystyle{\cal D}=-(x\cdot\partial_{x}+\eta\partial_{\eta}),\,\,\,\,\,{\cal M}_{ij}=x_{i}\partial_{j}-x_{j}\partial_{i}

The mass

of

and its scaling dimension

(4.80)

where the operators
\alpha_{i}
and

are different linear combinations of creation and annihilation operators. Eq. (
4.80

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0\Leftrightarrow\partial_{i}A_{i}=\frac{1}{\eta}(\eta\partial_{\eta}+1-d)A_{\eta}

When

\displaystyle A_{\eta}(\eta,x)

\displaystyle=(\eta\partial_{\eta}+1-d)^{-1}\left(\eta\,\partial_{i}A_{i}(\eta,x)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\bar{\Delta}-1}(-\eta)^{\Delta}\partial_{i}\alpha_{i}(x)+\frac{1}{\Delta-1}(-\eta)^{\bar{\Delta}}\partial_{i}\beta_{i}(x),\,\,\,\,\,\eta\to 0^{-}

5
Harish-Chandra characters

5.1
General theory

With the UIRs constructed, the next step is to compute their group characters which collect the information about the representations in a simple function. The characters associated to finite dimensional representations can be defined unambiguously. For example, given a finite dimensional representation
\rho
of a group
G
, the corresponding group character associated to certain element
g\in G
is defined as a trace of
\rho(g)
over the representation space
V_{\rho}
, i.e.
\Theta_{\rho}(g)\equiv\Tr_{V_{\rho}}\rho(g)
. However, such a trace does not necessarily make sense for an infinite dimensional representation like
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
. To tell when a “trace” can be defined in the infinite dimensional case, we need some deep notions and theorems in representation theory
[
53
]
:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
G
be a connected reductive Lie group and let
K
be a maximal compact subgroup. A representation
\pi
of
G
on a Hilbert space
V
is called
admissible
if
\pi|_{{\text{K}}}
is unitary and if each unitary irreducible representation
\tau
of
K
occurs with only finite multiplicity in
\pi|_{{\text{K}}}
.

In particular,
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
is a connected reductive Lie group and all
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
(including their unitary irreducible subrepresentations) are admissible.

Definition 5.2
.

We say an admissible representation
\pi
of a linear connected reductive group
G
has a
Harish-Chandra character
(or
global character
)
\Theta_{\pi}
if
\pi(\varphi)
is of trace class for any compact supported function
\varphi
on
G
and if
\varphi\to\Tr\pi(\varphi)\equiv\Theta_{\pi}(\varphi)
is a distribution. In this case, the character
\Theta_{\pi}
is clearly conjugation invariant.

Then the following theorem tells us when an admissible representation
\pi
has a Harish-Chandra character

Theorem 5.3
.

Every admissible representation
\pi
of a linear connected reductive group
G
whose decomposition
\pi|_{{\text{K}}}=\bigoplus_{\tau}n_{\tau}\,\tau
satisfies
n_{\tau}\leq C\dim\tau
has a Harish-Chandra character.

Since each
{\text{SO}}(d+1)
content of
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
has multiplicity 1,
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
and its irreducible components admit a Harish-Chandra character. We shall also use the heuristic notations

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(g)\equiv\Tr_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}g

(5.1)

which only exist in the distribution sense. In this section, we will mainly focus on
g=q^{D}
, namely
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q)\equiv\Theta_{\Delta,s}(q^{D})
. The more general case, with
q^{D}
being decorated by angular momenta, i.e.
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q,\bm{u})\equiv\Theta_{\Delta,s}(q^{D}e^{u_{1}M_{12}}e^{u_{2}M_{34}}\cdots)
, will be discussed in appendix
G
.

5.2
Compute
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q)

In this subsection, we show how to compute the character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q)
. Let’s start from the
s=0
case. By definition, one would find an orthonormal basis of
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
and compute the matrix element of
D
with respect to this basis (which is actually done in the appendix
E
with the basis being spherical harmonics). Then exponentiate the infinite dimensional matrix
D
and compute the trace. However, we can make life much easier by directly using the ket basis
|x\rangle
. The action of
q^{D}
on
|x\rangle
is

\displaystyle q^{D}|x\rangle=q^{\bar{\Delta}}\left|qx\right\rangle=\int d^{d}y\,q^{\bar{\Delta}}\delta^{d}(qx-y)|y\rangle

(5.2)

from which we can directly read off the matrix elements of
q^{D}
with respect to the basis
|x\rangle
. The integral of the diagonal entries of
q^{D}
is localized on the fixed points of
q^{D}
, i.e.
0
and
\infty
or equivalently the southern and northern poles when conformally mapped to
S^{d}
. But the
x^{i}
coordinate system only covers the
x=0
point and hence we would not be able to correctly capture the contribution from
\infty
if we naively computed
\int\,d^{d}x\,q^{\Delta}\delta^{d}(qx-x)
. We can bypass this problem by conjugating
q^{D}
with special conformal transformations, i.e.
q^{D}\to e^{-b\cdot K}q^{D}e^{b\cdot K}
. This conjugation maps the two fixed points
0
and
\infty
to
0
and
\frac{b^{i}}{b^{2}}
respectively. More explicitly, the action of
e^{-b\cdot K}q^{D}e^{b\cdot K}
on
|x\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle e^{-b\cdot K}q^{D}e^{b\cdot K}|x\rangle=\Omega(q,x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}|\tilde{x}(q,x,b)\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,\tilde{x}^{i}=q\frac{x^{i}+\left(q-1\right)x^{2}b^{i}}{1+2(q-1)\,x\cdot b+\left(q-1\right)^{2}b^{2}x^{2}}

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle\Omega(q,x,b)=\frac{q}{1+2(q-1)\,x\cdot b+\left(q-1\right)^{2}b^{2}x^{2}}

(5.4)

Therefore the character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)
should be

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)=\int d^{d}x\,\Omega(q,x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}\delta^{d}(\tilde{x}-x)

(5.5)

This integral is localized at
x^{i}=0
and
x^{i}=\frac{b^{i}}{b^{2}}
.
At these points, the scaling factor
\Omega(q,x,b)
becomes

\displaystyle\Omega(q,0,b)=q,\,\,\,\,\,\Omega(q,b^{i}/b^{2},b)=q^{-1}

(5.6)

and the Jacobian associated to the map
x^{i}\to\tilde{x}^{i}-x^{i}
becomes
|q-1|^{d}
and
|1-q^{-1}|^{d}
respectively. Altogether, the integral in eq. (
5.5
) yields

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)=\frac{q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{|q-1|^{d}}+\frac{q^{-\bar{\Delta}}}{|1-q^{-1}|^{d}}=\frac{q^{\Delta}+q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{|1-q|^{d}}

(5.7)

where the
b
dependence drops out explicitly as expected from the conjugation invariance.

Remark 5.4
.

The character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)
given by eq. (
5.7
) is symmetric under
q\to q^{-1}
. This property, which holds for all
{\cal F}_{\Delta,\bm{s}}
, is a result of the conjugation invariance of
\Theta
since
D=L_{0,d+1}
is mapped to
-D
by the conjugation of
e^{i\pi L_{d+1,i}}
. In view of this, we assume
\boxed{0<q<1}
in the remaining part of this section, without loss of generality.

The generalization of our computation to the spinning case is almost straightforward. Let’s write the ket basis of
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
as
|x\rangle_{\alpha}
where the index
\alpha
carries the spin-
s
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d)
. The action of
e^{-b\cdot K}e^{tD}e^{b\cdot K}
on this basis can be schematically expressed as

\displaystyle e^{-b\cdot K}q^{D}e^{b\cdot K}|x\rangle_{\alpha}={\cal O}_{\alpha\beta}(q,x,b)\Omega(q,x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}|\tilde{x}(q,x,b)\rangle_{\beta}

(5.8)

where
{\cal O}_{\alpha\beta}(q,x,b)
is an
{\text{SO}}(d)
rotation matrix in the spin-
s
representation. In general,
{\cal O}_{\alpha\beta}(q,x,b)
takes a very sophisticated form but while evaluated at the two fixed points, i.e.
x^{i}=0
and
x^{i}=\frac{b^{i}}{b^{2}}
, it becomes an identity matrix. Altogether, we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\int d^{d}x\,{\cal O}_{\alpha\alpha}(q,x,b)\Omega(q,x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}\delta^{d}(\tilde{x}-x)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)\times\left(\sum_{\alpha}\,1\right)=D^{d}_{s}\,\frac{q^{\Delta}+q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1-q)^{d}}

(5.9)

where
D^{d}_{s}
is the dimension of the spin-
s
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d)
. Thus
D^{d}_{s}\,\frac{q^{\Delta}+q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{(1-q)^{d}}
is the Harish-Chandra character for both principal and complementary series.

5.3
Harish-Chandra characters of exceptional series

Now let’s proceed to compute the Harish-Chandra characters of exceptional series
{\cal V}_{s,0}
and
{\cal U}_{s,t}
. For
{\cal V}_{s,0}
, recall that
{\cal V}_{s,t}\equiv{\rm Im\,}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t}\cong{\cal F}_{1-s,t}/\ker(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t}
, which yields

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal V}_{s,0}}(q)=\Theta_{{\cal F}_{1-s}}(q)-\Theta_{\ker(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s}}(q)

(5.10)

Since
\ker(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s}
carries the
{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1}
of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
,
\Theta_{\ker(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s}}(q)
is nothing but the usual
{\text{SO}}(d+2)
character corresponding to the highest weight representation
{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1}
, denoted by
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1}}(q)
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal V}_{s,0}}(q)=\frac{q^{1-s}+q^{d+s-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}-\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1}}(q)

(5.11)

Similarly for
{\cal U}_{s,t}
, using the isomorphism
{\cal U}_{s,t}\cong{\cal F}_{1-t,s}/{\cal V}_{s,t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal U}_{s,t}}(q)=D^{d}_{s}\frac{q^{1-t}+q^{d+t-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}-D^{d}_{t}\frac{q^{1-s}+q^{d+s-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}+\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1,t}}(q)

(5.12)

In general, one can write out the characters like
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1,t}}(q)
explicitly by using Weyl character formula
[
53
]
. However, in order to compare with the paper
[
28
]
, we derive a slightly different expression (c.f. eq. (
H.3
)) for
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s-1,t}}(q)
in appendix

. Plugging this new expression into eq. (
5.11
) and eq. (
5.12
) yields

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal V}_{s,0}}(q)=\left[\frac{q^{1-s}+q^{d+s-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}\right]_{+},\,\,\,\,\,\Theta_{{\cal U}_{s,t}}(q)=\left[D^{d}_{s}\frac{q^{1-t}+q^{d+t-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}-D^{d}_{t}\frac{q^{1-s}+q^{d+s-1}}{(1-q)^{d}}\right]_{+}

(5.13)

where the “flipping” operator
[\,\,\,]_{+}
acts on an arbitrary Laurent series
\sum_{k}c_{k}q^{k}
as

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{k}c_{k}q^{k}\right]_{+}=\sum_{k<0}(-c_{k})q^{-k}+\sum_{k>0}c_{k}q^{k}

(5.14)

We have checked that Harish-Chandra characters
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}
,
\Theta_{{\cal V}_{s,0}}
and

agree with the results in literature

\displaystyle d=4:\,\,\Theta_{{\cal U}_{s,t}}(q)=2(s-t)(s+t+2)\frac{q^{2}}{(1-q)^{4}}=\left(D^{d}_{s,t+1}+D^{d}_{s,-(t+1)}\right)\frac{q^{2}}{(1-q)^{4}}

(5.15)

where

for

6
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
vs.

In this section, we will comment on distinctions between UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(2,d)
and
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
.
{\text{SO}}(2,d)
is the isometry group of
(d+1)
dimensional AdS and the conformal group of
\mathbb{R}^{1,d-1}
. It shares the same Lie algebra as
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
but with
different reality conditions (see, for example
[
76
]
)

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}=D,\,\,\,\,\,P_{i}^{\dagger}=K_{i},\,\,\,\,\,M_{ij}^{\dagger}=-M_{ij}

(6.1)

D
generates time translation in the global coordinates of AdS, playing the role of a Hamiltonian. So the physically relevant representations of
{\text{SO}}(2,d)
are the lowest-weight ones, where the Hamiltonian
D
is bounded from below. These representations are fixed by a primary state and the corresponding representation spaces are spanned by primary states together with their descendants. In contrast, primary states in the dS sense (e.g.
|\bar{\Delta},0\rangle_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}
in e.q. (
4
)) are not normalizable and do not belong to the representation spaces
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
. The
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
UIRs given by the list in section
4.7
are not lowest-weight representations.
We also want to mention that
{\text{SO}}(2,d)
does have UIRs that are not lowest-weight representations, for example principal series representations, which describe continuous-spin fields
[
77
,
78
]
.

Since
D
is bounded from below, the most natural way to define an
{\mathfrak{so}}(2,d)
character is
[
79
,
39
]
12
12

12

One can also turn on the
{\text{SO}}(d)
generators, i.e.
\chi_{R}(q,\bm{x})=\Tr_{R}q^{D}x_{1}^{L_{12}}x_{2}^{L_{34}}\cdots

\displaystyle\chi_{R}(q)\equiv\Tr_{R}q^{D},\,\,\,\,\,0<|q|<1

(6.2)

where

is a lowest-weight representation of
{\text{SO}}(2,d)
. Compared to the Harish-Chandra character of

, the trace in (

) makes perfect sense given

but

fails to be a

for

Appendix A
UIRs of
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})

In this appendix, we review the UIRs of
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
, following the constructions in section
3.1
. The group
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
consists of real
2\times 2
matrices with determinant 1. Its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
is isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{so}}(1,2)
. The latter is spanned by three generators
\{P,K,D\}
, satisfying the commutation relation (
3.1
). We will also use them as a basis of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. In the defining representation of
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
, the generators
\{P,K,D\}
can be realized as the following
2\times 2
matrices

\displaystyle P=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,K=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,D=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(A.1)

With this choice,
L_{0}=-\frac{i}{2}(P+K)=-\frac{\sigma_{2}}{2}
, where
\sigma_{2}
is the second Pauli matrix.

For
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
,
e^{2\pi iL_{0}}
is the identity element and hence the spectrum of
L_{0}
must be integers in any
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
representation. In contrast,
e^{2\pi iL_{0}}=-\small\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
in
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
. As an involutory central element,
e^{2\pi iL_{0}}
is either 1 (even “parity”) or
-1
(odd “parity”) in an irreducible representation of
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
. The UIRs of
{\text{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
with an even parity are equivalent to UIRs of
{\text{SO}}(1,2)
. Since the latter have been analyzed extensively in section
3
, we will focus on parity odd UIRs in this appendix. In these representations, the eigenvalues of
L_{0}
are half-integers. Denote the eigenstates of
L_{0}
by
|r\rangle
, i.e.
L_{0}|r\rangle=r|r\rangle
, where
r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
. The action of
L_{\pm}
on these states are given by (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), with
n
replaced by
r
:

\displaystyle L_{\pm}|r\rangle=(r\pm\Delta)|r\pm 1\rangle

(A.2)

The reality condition
L^{\dagger}_{\pm}=L_{\mp}
implies

\displaystyle\frac{\langle r+1|r+1\rangle}{\langle r|r\rangle}=\frac{r+\bar{\Delta}}{r+\Delta^{*}}

(A.3)

For
\lambda_{r}\equiv\frac{r+\bar{\Delta}}{r+\Delta^{*}}
to be real,
\Delta
lies on either the real line or
\frac{1}{2}+i\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

\displaystyle(i)\,\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu,\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\,\,(ii)\,\Delta\in\mathbb{R}

(A.4)

The positivity of
\lambda_{r}
requires separate discussions for different values of
\Delta
:

•

\lambda_{r}
is identically equal to 1 when
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}+i\mu,\mu\not=0
. We can consistently choose
\langle r|r\rangle=1
for all

and hence the resulting representation, denoted by
{\cal P}^{-}_{\Delta}
, is unitary.
{\cal P}^{-}_{\Delta}
is a parity odd principal series representation. The irreducibility of
{\cal P}^{-}_{\Delta}
is shown pictorially in fig. (

).

and

are equivalent since we can easily generalize the shadow transformation, c.f. (

on all the spherical harmonics. This computation can be done by using the following recurrence relations of Gegenbauer polynomials

\displaystyle\partial_{x}C^{\lambda}_{n}(x)=2\lambda C^{\lambda+1}_{n-1}(x),\,\,\,\,\,x\,C^{\lambda}_{n}(x)=\frac{n+1}{2(n+\lambda)}C^{\lambda}_{n+1}(x)+\frac{n-1+2\lambda}{2(n+\lambda)}C^{\lambda}_{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle(1-x)^{2}C^{\lambda+1}_{n-1}(x)=\frac{n+2\lambda}{2\lambda}xC^{\lambda}_{n}(x)-\frac{(n+1)}{2\lambda}C^{\lambda}_{n+1}(x)

(E.5)

which yield

\displaystyle\boxed{DY_{n\ell\bm{m}}=(\Delta+n)\,\sqrt{\frac{(n+1-\ell)(n+\ell+d-1)}{(2n+d-1)(2n+d+1)}}Y_{n+1,\ell\bm{m}}+(1-\bar{\Delta}-n)\,\sqrt{\frac{(n-\ell)(n+\ell+d-2)}{(2n+d-3)(2n+d-1)}}Y_{n-1,\ell\bm{m}}}

(E.6)

The coefficients of
Y_{n\pm 1,\ell\bm{m}}
are nonvanishing except
\Delta\in\{d,d+1,d+2,\cdots\}\cup\{0,-1,-2,\cdots\}
. Due to the reasoning given at the beginning of this appendix, the representation
{\cal F}_{\Delta}
is irreducible when
\Delta
is away form these integers. For
\Delta\in\{d,d+1,d+2,\cdots\}
, the subspace with
{\text{SO}}(d+1)
content
\bigoplus_{n\geq 1-\bar{\Delta}}{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}
is mapped to itself by
D
and this claim also holds for any
L_{0,i}
, because
L_{0,i}
can be related to
D=L_{0,d+1}
by
{\text{SO}}(d+1)
conjugations. Therefore, this subspace is irreducible with respect to
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
. Using the same argument, we can claim that when
\Delta\in\{0,-1,-2,\cdots\}
, the finite dimensional subspace
\bigoplus_{0\leq n\leq-\Delta}{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}
is irreducible, carrying the spin
(-\Delta)
representation of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
. These finite dimensional representations are nonunitary except the
\Delta=0
one which is the trivial representation.

Appendix F
Normalizability of the inner product on
{\cal U}_{s,t}

In the subsection
4.5
, we introduced an inner product for the exceptional series representation
{\cal U}_{s,t}={\cal F}_{1-t,s}/{\rm Im\,}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t}
:

\displaystyle(\psi,\varphi)_{{\cal U}{s,t}}=\int\,d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}\psi(x_{1})^{*}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}S^{+}_{1-t,s}(x_{12})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{2},j_{1}\cdots j_{2}}\varphi(x_{2})_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}

(F.1)

where
\psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}},\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}\in{\cal F}_{1-t,s}
and
S^{+}_{1-t,s}
defines the shadow transformation
{\cal S}^{+}_{1-t,s}
from
{\cal F}_{1-t,s}
to
{\cal F}_{d+t-1,s}
, whose Fourier transformation is given by eq. (
4.2
) in the index-free formalism:

\displaystyle S^{+}_{1-t,s}(p;z,w)=p^{d+2t-2}\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{s}\frac{(\ell-t)_{s-\ell}}{(d+t+\ell-2)_{s-\ell}}\Pi^{s\ell}(\hat{p};z,w)

(F.2)

However, we did not justify the normalizability of this inner product. In this appendix, we will prove that the inner product (
F.1
) is normalizable, by a detailed study of the asymptotic behaviors of wavefunctions in
{\cal F}_{1-t,s}
.

By definition, the large
x
behavior of
\psi(x,z)\in{\cal F}_{1-t,s}
is

\displaystyle\psi(x,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to\infty}}{{\approx}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{2(t-1)}C_{k}\left(\tilde{x}^{i},R(x)^{i}_{\,\,j}z^{j}\right)

(F.3)

where
C_{k}(u,v)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
in the first argument and degree
s
in the second. With some simple power counting, one can learn that the first
t
polynomials, i.e.
\{C_{0},C_{1},\cdots C_{t-1}\}
, might lead to divergence at small
p
in (
F.1
). We will show this does not happen because all
x^{2(t-1)}C_{k}
with
0\leq k\leq t-1
lie in the image of
(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t}
, which is annihilated by
{\cal S}^{+}_{1-t,s}
. In particular, it suffices to show the following proposition:

Proposition F.1
.

Let
P_{k}(x^{i})
be an arbitrary degree-
k
homogeneous polynomial in
x^{i}
and define
\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)=x^{2(t-1-k)}P_{k}(x^{i})v^{i_{1}}\cdots v^{i_{s}}
, where
t\in\{0,1,\cdots s-1\}
and
v^{i}=R(x)^{i}_{\,\,j}z^{j}=\frac{2x\cdot z}{x^{2}}x^{i}-z^{i}
. When
k=0,1,\cdots t-1
,
\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)
can be expressed as
\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)=(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t}Q_{k}(x,v)
for some polynomial
Q_{k}(x,v)
.

In order to prove the proposition
F.1
, we need some lemmas:

Lemma F.2
.

\displaystyle(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n}\frac{x^{i_{1}}\cdots x^{i_{n}}}{x^{2}}=\frac{(-)^{n}\,n!}{x^{2}}v^{i_{1}}\cdots v^{i_{n}}

(F.4)

Proof
: Since the L.H.S is a totally symmetric tensor, it suffices to compute
(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n}\frac{(x\cdot\xi)^{n}}{x^{2}}
, where
\xi_{i}
is an auxiliary vector:

\displaystyle(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n}\frac{(x\cdot\xi)^{n}}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n-\ell}(x\cdot\xi)^{n}\,(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{\ell}\frac{1}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{x^{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}(z\cdot\xi)^{n-\ell}\left(\frac{-2x\cdot z\,x\cdot\xi}{x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}=\frac{(-)^{n}\,n!}{x^{2}}(v\cdot\xi)^{n}

(F.5)

Stripping off
\xi_{i}
, we recover eq. (
F.4
).

Lemma F.3
.

For a degree-
k
polynomial
P_{k}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle P_{k}(x)(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n+k}g(x)=(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n}\left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-)^{\ell}\binom{n+\ell-1}{\ell}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{\ell}P_{k}(x)\,(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{k-\ell}g(x)\right\}

(F.6)

Proof
: Define

\displaystyle A(\bm{a})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}a_{\ell}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{\ell}P_{k}(x)\,(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{k-\ell}g(x)

(F.7)

We want to find
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})
such that
A(\bm{a})
is mapped to
P_{k}(x)(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n+k}g(x)
under
(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{n}
. Let’s first act
z\cdot\partial_{x}
on
A(\bm{a})
and expand the result as follows

\displaystyle z\cdot\partial_{x}A(\bm{a})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}a^{(1)}_{\ell}(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{\ell}P_{k}(x)\,(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{k-\ell+1}g(x)

(F.8)

where
a^{(1)}_{\ell}=a_{\ell}+a_{\ell-1}
for
1\leq\ell\leq k
and
a_{0}^{(1)}=a_{0}
. The relation between
\bm{a}
and
\bm{a}^{(1)}
can be captured by a
(k+1)\times(k+1)
matrix
\Lambda
, i.e.
\bm{a}^{(1)}=(\mathbb{1}+\Lambda)\,\bm{a}
, where

\displaystyle\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&0\end{pmatrix}

(F.9)

Then finding the coefficients
(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots a_{k})
becomes a pure linear algebra problem

\displaystyle(1+\Lambda)^{n}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
\cdots\\
a_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\cdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(F.10)

Since
(1+\Lambda)
is invertible, the desired
\bm{a}
exists and is given by the first column of
(1+\Lambda)^{-n}
. The matrix
(1+\Lambda)^{-n}
can be computed by a formal Taylor expansion and the Taylor series truncates because
\Lambda^{k+1}=0
:

\displaystyle(1+\Lambda)^{-n}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-)^{\ell}\binom{n+\ell-1}{\ell}\Lambda^{\ell}

(F.11)

Combining (
F.11
) and
(\Lambda^{\ell})_{i,1}=\delta_{i,\ell+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{\ell}=(-)^{\ell}\binom{n+\ell-1}{\ell}

(F.12)

and this finishes our proof of (
F.6
).

Now we start proving the proposition
F.1
. Using lemma
F.2
, we rewrite
\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)
as

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)=\frac{(-)^{s-t+k}}{(s-t+k)!}P_{k}(x)(x^{2}v^{i_{1}})\cdots(x^{2}v^{i_{t-k}})(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t+k}\frac{x^{i_{t+1-k}}\cdots x^{i_{s}}}{x^{2}}

(F.13)

where
x^{2}v^{i_{j}}
can be moved to the right of
(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t+k}
since
z\cdot\partial_{x}(x^{2}v^{i})=0

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{k}(x,v)=P_{k}(x)(z\cdot\partial_{x})^{s-t+k}g(x,v),\,\,\,\,\,g(x,v)=\frac{(-)^{s-t+k}}{(s-t+k)!}x^{2(t-k-1)}v^{i_{1}}v^{i_{t-k}}x^{i_{t+1-k}}\cdots x^{i_{s}}

(F.14)

Then proposition
F.1
immediately follows from lemma
F.3
.

Appendix G
Computation of the decorated Harish-Chandra characters

In section
5.2
, we have computed the Harish-Chandra characters
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(g)=\Tr_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}g
for
g
sitting on the one-dimensional subgroup generated by the boost
D
. In this appendix, we will consider more general
g
by also turning on angular momenta in the trace
\Tr_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q,\bm{u})=\Tr_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}\left(q^{D}\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{u_{i}J_{i}}\right)

(G.1)

where
r=\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor
, and
J_{i}=M_{2i-1,i}
are generators of the Cartan subalgebra of
{\text{SO}}(d)
.

Let’s start from the
s=0
case. Following the derivation in section
5.2
, we first conjugate
q^{D}\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{u_{i}J_{i}}
by a special conformal transformation

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q,\bm{u})=\Tr_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}\left(e^{-b\cdot K}g_{0}e^{b\cdot K}\right),\,\,\,\,\,g_{0}\equiv q^{D}\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{u_{i}J_{i}}

(G.2)

where
b^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an arbitrary auxiliary vector. Then we need to compute the action of
e^{-b\cdot K}g_{0}e^{b\cdot K}
on
|x\rangle
. With some straightforward but tedious computations, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-b\cdot K}g_{0}e^{b\cdot K}|x\rangle=\Omega(q,\bm{u},x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}|\tilde{x}(q,\bm{u},x,b)\rangle

(G.3)

where

\displaystyle\Omega(q,\bm{u},x,b)=\frac{q}{1-2\,x\cdot b+b^{2}x^{2}+2\,q(b\cdot x_{\bm{u}}-b\cdot b_{\bm{u}}x^{2})+b^{2}q^{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{i}(q,\bm{u},x,b)=\Omega(q,\bm{u},x,b)(x^{i}_{\bm{u}}+x^{2}(q\,b^{i}-b^{i}_{\bm{u}}))

(G.4)

Here
x_{\bm{u}}^{i}
(
b^{i}_{\bm{u}}
) is related to
x^{i}
(
b^{i}
) by a rotation associated to
\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{u_{i}J_{i}}
. More explicitly,
x_{\bm{u}}^{i}=R(\bm{u})^{i}_{\,\,j}x^{j}
where
R(\bm{u})
is a block diagonal matrix

\displaystyle R(\bm{u})=\begin{cases}\text{diag}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{1}&\sin u_{1}\\
-\sin u_{1}&\cos u_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{2}&\sin u_{2}\\
-\sin u_{2}&\cos u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{r}&\sin u_{r}\\
-\sin u_{r}&\cos u_{r}\end{pmatrix}\right\},&d=2r\\
\text{diag}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{1}&\sin u_{1}\\
-\sin u_{1}&\cos u_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{2}&\sin u_{2}\\
-\sin u_{2}&\cos u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}\cos u_{r}&\sin u_{r}\\
-\sin u_{r}&\cos u_{r}\end{pmatrix},1\right\},&d=2r+1\end{cases}

(G.5)

Given the action (
G.3
), the character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q,\bm{u})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q,\bm{u})=\int d^{d}x\,\Omega(q,\bm{u},x,b)^{\bar{\Delta}}\delta^{d}(\tilde{x}(q,\bm{u},x,b)-x)

(G.6)

This integral is localized to the two fixed points of the map
x\to\tilde{x}(q,\bm{u},x,b)
, namely
x^{i}=0
and
x^{i}=\frac{b^{i}}{b^{2}}
. At these points, the scaling factor
\Omega(q,\bm{u},x,b)
becomes

\displaystyle\Omega(q,\bm{u},0,b)=q,\,\,\,\,\,\Omega(q,\bm{u},b^{i}/b^{2},b)=q^{-1}

(G.7)

The remaining task is to figure out the Jacobian determinant associated to the map
x^{i}\to\tilde{x}^{i}-x^{i}
at these fixed points. At
x^{i}=0
, the Jacobian matrix (denoted by
J_{1}
) is simply

and hence the corresponding Jacobian is

\displaystyle|\det J_{1}|=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-2q\cos u_{i}+q^{2})\times\begin{cases}1,&d=2r\\
|1-q|,&d=2r+1\end{cases}

(G.8)

The Jacobian matrix at
x^{i}=\frac{b^{i}}{b^{2}}
(denoted by
J_{2}
) takes a more complicated form

\displaystyle J_{2}=\frac{1}{q}(1-2B)R(\bm{u})(1-2B)-\mathbb{1}_{d}

(G.9)

where
\mathbb{1}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix and
B^{i}_{\,\,j}=\frac{1}{b^{2}}b^{i}b_{j}
. Although
J_{2}
depends on
b^{i}
explicitly, its determinant does not. Using
B^{2}=B

1-2B

\displaystyle|\det J_{2}|=|\det(q^{-1}R(\bm{u})-\mathbb{1}_{d})|=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-2q^{-1}\cos u_{i}+q^{-2})\times\begin{cases}1,&d=2r\\
|1-q^{-1}|,&d=2r+1\end{cases}

(G.10)

Altogether, the decorated character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q,\bm{u})
is

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q,\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{|\det J_{1}|}+\frac{q^{-\bar{\Delta}}}{|\det J_{2}|}=\frac{q^{\Delta}+q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{P_{d}(q,\bm{u})}

(G.11)

where

\displaystyle P_{d}(q,\bm{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-e^{iu_{i}}q)(1-e^{-iu_{i}}q)\times\begin{cases}1,&d=2r\\
|1-q|,&d=2r+1\end{cases}

(G.12)

Compared to the undecorated character
\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta}}(q)
, c.f. eq. (
5.7
), the only difference is that the denominator
|1-q|^{d}
gets replaced by
P_{d}(q,\bm{u})
. This result agrees with
[
39
]
.

The derivation above can be straightforwardly generalized to spinning representations
{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}
. We simply present the result here

\displaystyle\Theta_{{\cal F}_{\Delta,s}}(q,\bm{u})=\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s}}(\bm{u})\frac{q^{\Delta}+q^{\bar{\Delta}}}{P_{d}(q,\bm{u})}

(G.13)

where
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s}}(\bm{u})
is the
{\text{SO}}(d)
character corresponding to the spin-
s
representation, defined as the trace of
\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{u_{i}J_{i}}
over the spin-
s
representation space (which, for example, is carried by
|0\rangle_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}
in our settings). Some lower
d
examples of
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s}}(\bm{u})
are

\displaystyle\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(3)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s}}(u_{1})=\sum_{n=-s}^{s}e^{inu_{1}}=\frac{\sin((s+\frac{1}{2})u_{1})}{\sin(\frac{1}{2}u_{1})}

(G.14)

\displaystyle\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(4)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{s}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\frac{\cos((s+1)u_{1})-\cos((s+1)u_{2})}{\cos(u_{1})-\cos(u_{2})}

(G.15)

Appendix H
A new expression for certain
\text{SO}(d+2)
characters

In this appendix, we show an interesting property of the
{\text{SO}}(d+2)
characters
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}}(x)
corresponding to two-row representations
{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}
. Let’s first give the statement.

Claim 1
.

The character
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}}(x)
has the same polar part (i.e. all terms of negative powers in
x
) and the same constant term as the following function

\displaystyle\boxed{P^{d}_{n,s}(x)\equiv\frac{D^{d}_{s}\,x^{-n}-D^{d}_{n+1}\,x^{1-s}}{(1-x)^{d}}}

(H.1)

Since
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}}(x)
is symmetric under
x\leftrightarrow x^{-1}
, it is then completely encoded in the function
P^{d}_{n,s}(x)
. In particular, when
s=0
, i.e. spin-
n
representation, the character
\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}}(x)
is encoded in

\displaystyle\boxed{Q^{d}_{n}(x)\equiv\frac{x^{-n}}{(1-x)^{d}}}

(H.2)

More explicitly, let
f(x)
be a function with well-defined Laurent expansion around
x=0
. Denote the polar part of
f(x)
by
[f(x)]_{-}
and denote the polar part together with the constant term of
f(x)
by
[f(x)]_{0}
. With this notations, the claim implies that

\displaystyle\boxed{\Theta^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}}(x)=\left[P^{d}_{n,s}(x)\right]_{0}+\left(\left.\left[P^{d}_{n,s}(x)\right]_{-}\right|_{x\to x^{-1}}\right)}

(H.3)

The claim can be proved rigorously by induction, using branching rules. Here we want to present a more intriguing but less rigorous argument, that shows the similarity and difference between the
{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d+2)
and the infinite dimensional lowest-weight representations of
{\text{SO}}(1,d+1)
. In view of this, we need to rewrite the
{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}
representation in a CFT-style language as follows.
Denote the (antihermitian) generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(d+2)
by
L_{AB},0\leq A,B\leq d+1
which satisfy commutation relations

\displaystyle[L_{AB},L_{CD}]=\delta_{BC}L_{AD}+\text{permutations}

(H.4)

In the differential operator realization,
L_{AB}=X_{A}\partial_{B}-X_{B}\partial_{A}
where
X_{A}
are coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
.
Mimicking the conformal algebra, we define the following basis

\displaystyle M_{ij}=L_{ij},\,\,\,\,\,H=iL_{0,d+1},\,\,\,\,\,P_{i}=L_{d+1,i}+iL_{0i},\,\,\,\,\,K_{i}=-L_{d+1,i}+iL_{0i},

(H.5)

where
1\leq i\leq d
. The new basis leads to some interesting commutators

\displaystyle[M_{ij},H]=0,\,\,\,\,\,[H,P_{i}]=P_{i},\,\,\,\,[H,K_{i}]=-K_{i}\,\,\,\,\,[K_{i},P_{j}]=2M_{ij}-2\delta_{ij}H

(H.6)

In particular,
P_{i}
raises the eigenvalue of
H
by 1 while
K_{i}
lowers the eigenvalue of
H
by 1. Define complex (lightcone) coordinate
z=X_{d+1}+iX_{0},\bar{z}=X_{d+1}-iX_{0}
and then the differential operator realization of
H,P_{i},K_{i}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle H=z\partial_{z}-\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}},\,\,\,\,\,P_{i}=z\partial_{i}-2\,X_{i}\partial_{\bar{z}},\,\,\,\,\,K_{i}=-\bar{z}\partial_{i}+2\,X_{i}\partial_{z}

(H.7)

First, let’s consider spin-
n
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d+2)
generated by the lowest weight state
|lw\rangle
that satisfies

\displaystyle H|lw\rangle=-n|lw\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,K_{i}|lw\rangle=0,\,\,\,\,\ M_{ij}|lw\rangle=0

(H.8)

A generic state in the Verma module generated by
|lw\rangle
is a linear combination of the descendants
P_{i_{1}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}|lw\rangle
. However, some of the
{\mathfrak{so}}(d+2)
descendants of
|lw\rangle
are linearly dependent. To see this more explicitly, let’s switch to the wavefunction picture, where the lowest weight state
|lw\rangle
corresponds to
\psi_{lw}(z,\bar{z})=\bar{z}^{n}
. One crucial observation is that all of the states
\psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}\equiv P_{i_{1}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}\psi_{lw}
with
k\leq n
are linearly independent. To show this, it suffices to notice that the top component of
\psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}
in
X_{i}
is
(-2)^{k}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}\bar{z}^{n-k}
for
k\leq n
which arises from the
-2X_{i}\partial_{\bar{z}}
part of each
P_{i}
. It is clear that
X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}\bar{z}^{n-k}
are nonvanishing and linearly independent, and hence all
\psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}
with
k\leq n
are linearly independent. Then it is almost trivial to construct the polar part and the constant term of the character
\Theta_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}}^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}(x)\equiv\Tr_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}}x^{H}

\displaystyle\left[\Theta_{{\mathbb{Y}}n}^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}(x)\right]_{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{d+k-1}{d-1}x^{k-n}=\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{d+k-1}{d-1}x^{k-n}\right]_{0}=\left[Q^{d}_{n}(x)\right]_{0}

(H.9)

This is exactly the single-row case of the claim of this appendix. Then the full character can be recovered by using the
x\leftrightarrow x^{-1}
symmetry. For example, due to this symmetry the coefficient of
x
in
\Theta_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}}^{{\text{SO}}(d+2)}(x)
should be
\binom{d+n-2}{d-1}
rather than the native counting
\binom{d+n}{d-1}
. Even more explicitly, we can find what are these
\binom{d+n-2}{d-1}
states. The
H=0
eigenspace
{\cal H}^{(n)}_{0}
is spanned by all
X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{n}}
. Acting
P_{j}
on them yields basis of the
H=1
eigenspace
{\cal H}^{(n)}_{1}

\displaystyle P_{j}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{n}}=z\delta_{j(i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{n})}

(H.10)

Therefore
{\cal H}^{(n)}_{1}
is spanned by
z\,X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{n-1}}
and has dimension
\binom{d+n-2}{d-1}
.

Next, we consider more complicated representations like
{\mathbb{Y}}_{n,s}
, for which we have a lowest weight state
|lw\rangle_{{i_{1}}\cdots i_{s}}
that carries a spin-
s
representation of
{\text{SO}}(d)
. As before,
|lw\rangle_{{i_{1}}\cdots i_{s}}
has quantum number
-n
under
H
and is annihilated by
K_{i}

The claim
