Transition of the semiclassical resonance widths across a tangential crossing energy-level
Marouane Assal, Setsuro Fujiie, Kenta Higuchi

Abstract
. We consider a 1D 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix-valued operator ( 1.1 ) with two semiclassical Schrödinger operators on the diagonal entries and small interactions on the off-diagonal ones. When the two potentials cross at a turning point with contact order n n , the corresponding two classical trajectories at the crossing level intersect at one point in the phase space with contact order 2 ​ n 2n . We compute the transfer matrix at this point between the incoming and outgoing microlocal solutions and apply it to the semiclassical distribution of resonances at the energy crossing level. It is described in terms of a generalized Airy function. This result generalizes [ 5 ] to the tangential crossing and [ 3 ] to the crossing at a turning point. Keywords: Resonances; Matrix Schrödinger operators; Energy-level crossing.



1.
Introduction

We study the asymptotic distribution of resonances for the matrix Schrödinger operator

(1.1)

P:=\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}&hU\\
hU^{*}&P_{2}\end{pmatrix},

with a small positive parameter
h
.
The diagonal part consists of scalar Schrödinger operators

P_{j}:=(hD_{x})^{2}+V_{j}(x)\quad(j=1,2),\;\;\;\;D_{x}:=-i\frac{d}{dx},

with real-valued potentials
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
, and the anti-diagonal part describing the interaction between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
consists of (
h
times) a first order semiclassical differential operator
U
with real-valued coefficients on
\mathbb{R}

U=U(x,hD_{x}):=r_{0}(x)+ir_{1}(x)hD_{x},

and its adjoint
U^{*}
. Such operators arise as important models in molecular physics and quantum chemistry for instance in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation of molecular dynamics (see e.g.,
[
9
]
). Our goal is to provide a precise asymptotics on the imaginary parts (widths) of the resonances which correspond to the inverse of the lifetime of the molecule.

Let
p_{j}(x,\xi):=\xi^{2}+V_{j}(x)
be the classical Hamiltonian associated with
P_{j}

(j=1,2)
.
We consider the situation where, at an energy-level
E_{0}
(we set
E_{0}=0
), the classical trajectory on
\Gamma_{1}:=p_{1}^{-1}(0)
is a simple closed curve, while that on
\Gamma_{2}:=p_{2}^{-1}(0)
is non-trapping. In this situation, the scalar operator
P_{1}
has eigenvalues near
E=0
subject to the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rule (
2.4
), but because of the
interaction with
P_{2}
, these eigenvalues may be shifted to resonances of the matrix operator
P
in the lower half plane (Fermi’s golden rule).

The semiclassical asymptotics of the imaginary parts of the resonances in this setting has first been studied in
[
13
,
14
,
4
,
2
]
in the case where
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are disjoint. They proved the exponential decay estimate of the resonance widths.
Recently,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
3
]
studied the case where
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
cross each other and proved the polynomial asymptotics of the resonance widths.
More precisely,
[
5
,
6
]
considered the resonances near a crossing level using a construction of exact solutions to the system
Pu=Eu
.
On the other hand,
[
7
,
3
]
studied the resonances above the crossing level
using a microlocal argument.
The microlocal argument reduces the study of resonances to a microlocal transfer matrix at the crossing point, which relates the microlocal data on the two incoming trajectories to the two outgoing ones.
The recent work
[
3
]
generalized the microlocal transfer matrix at a transversal crossing point of
[
7
]
to a tangential one, by making use of the exact solutions constructed in
[
5
,
6
]
.

In the present article, we study the resonances at the crossing level, which was excluded in
[
3
]
, and generalize
[
5
,
6
]
to the tangential crossing.
A remarkable feature at the crossing level is the transition of the intersection between
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
.
Below this level, they have no intersection, which results in the exponentially small width of resonances. Above the crossing level, on the contrary, they intersect transversally at two points, which implies the width of polynomial order as proved in
[
3
]
.
Our result Theorem
1
asserts that the resonance widths are of order
h^{1+\frac{2}{2n+1}}
, where
n
is the contact order of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, and the coefficient of the leading term is explicitly given in terms of a generalization of the
Airy function. Notice that the contact order of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
is
m=2n
and the order
h^{1+\frac{2}{2n+1}}=h^{1+\frac{2}{m+1}}
of the resonance widths coincides with that ‘above’ an order
m
crossing obtained in
[
3
]
. The generalized Airy function
A_{n}(\lambda)
, which decays exponentially as
\lambda\to+\infty
and oscillates as
\lambda\to-\infty
, describes the transition.

The proof of Theorem
1
is reduced to the asymptotic formula of the microlocal transfer matrix at the crossing point (Theorem
2
). In particular, the exponent of the order in
h
of the resonance width is one plus twice the exponent of the subprincipal order of the transfer matrix, which is
1/(2n+1)
in our case.
This reduction argument is exactly the same as in
[
3
]
, and is omitted in this article.

Theorem
2
is the linear relation between the incoming microlocal data and the outgoing one near a crossing point. Because of the tangential crossing, we cannot reduce it to a normal form. Instead, we construct local exact solutions by a convergent successive approximation. More precisely, let the crossing point be
(x,\xi)=(0,0)
. We construct a basis of exact solutions
(w_{1}^{\flat},w_{2}^{\flat},w_{1}^{\sharp},w_{2}^{\sharp})
near
x=0
and a pair of exact solutions
(w_{1}^{-},w_{2}^{-})
to the equation
(P-E)w=0
, which is a basis of microlocal solutions near
(0,0)
(this means that
w_{1}^{-}
and
w_{2}^{-}
are bounded as
h\to+0
both on the left and right of
x=0
). We express
(w_{1}^{-},w_{2}^{-})
in linear combination (
3.13
) of the basis
(w_{1}^{\flat},w_{2}^{\flat},w_{1}^{\sharp},w_{2}^{\sharp})
. Then we can deduce the transfer matrix from this
4\times 2
matrix since we know in fact the microlocal behavior of
(w_{1}^{\flat},w_{2}^{\flat},w_{1}^{\sharp},w_{2}^{\sharp})
on the incoming and outgoing trajectories.
Thus we obtain two term asymptotics of the transfer matrix. In particular, the subprincipal term, which will give the resonance width, is reduced to an integral (
3.25
) of the product of two WKB solutions associated with
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. This oscillatory integral has its degenerate critical point of order
n
at the crossing point, i.e. the derivative of the phase vanishes at order
n
.
Contrary to
[
3
]
, this critical point is close to the turning points.
The two turning points are of order
E
, and their difference is of order
E^{n}
. Hence if
E
is sufficiently small (
{\rm Re}\hskip 1.0ptE=o(h^{4/(2n+1)})
, see Remark
2.4
and Section
4
) and
n\geq 2
, roughly speaking, the phase behaves like

x^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
and the amplitude like
x^{-\frac{1}{2}}
near the crossing-turning point
x=0
. Then after the change of variable
x=z^{2}
,
the phase becomes like
z^{2n+1}
and the singularity of the amplitude dissappears. The degenerate stationary phase in the variable
z
gives the asymptotics of order
h^{1/(2n+1)}
of the integral. For the maximal energy range
{\rm Re}\hskip 1.0ptE={\mathcal{O}}(h^{2/(2n+1)})
, the asymptotic behavior of this integral is given by a generalization of the Airy function. This describes the transition of the order of the integral from
h^{1/(n+1)}
to
h^{1/(2n+1)}
due to the confluence of crossing points.

This article is organized as follows. The main results (Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
) are stated in Section
2
. Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2
. In section
4
, we restrict the real part of the resonances in a smaller interval to obtain a constant coefficient of the leading term and a better error estimate for the resonance asymptotics.
In Appendix
A
, we note some fundamental properties of Airy functions and the generalized Airy function. Finally in Appendix
B
, we
review various solutions to the single Schrödinger equation near a simple turning point that we use in the construction of the exact solutions to our system.

Under Assumption

3.
Proof of the microlocal connection formulae

We first review the construction of exact solutions due to
[

.
If

is a pair of independent solutions to the scalar homogeneous equation

for any

with arbitrary constants

. Here
{\mathcal{W}}(u,u^{\dagger}):={\rm det}(u,u^{\dagger})
stands for the Wronskian of

and
u^{\dagger}
.

In the following we use the notation
U_{1}:=U
and
U_{2}:=U^{*}

(u_{j},u^{\dagger}_{j})
be a pair of independent solutions to

and

have a compact support near

\widetilde{K}_{j}
is applied to smooth functions without compact support.

Remark that the function

and its derivative take at

the following values:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j}[f](0)=C_{j}(u_{j},u^{\dagger}_{j};s)[f]u_{j}(0)+C_{j}(u^{\dagger}_{j},u_{j};t)[f]u^{\dagger}_{j}(0),

Notice that

If the infinite sums

Appendix A
Airy functions

We review some known facts on the usual Airy functions and define a generalized Airy function used to describe our main result.

The Airy functions are defined by the integrals

(A.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ai}(y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(i\left(\frac{\eta^{3}}{3}+y\eta\right)\right)d\eta,

(A.2)

\displaystyle{\rm Bi}(y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{3}}{3}+y\eta\right)+\sin\left(\frac{\eta^{3}}{3}+y\eta\right)\right]d\eta.

They are solutions to the Airy differential equation:

(A.3)

-v^{\prime\prime}(y)+yv(y)=0.

They are characterized by their asymptotic behavior as
y\to-\infty
:

(A.4)

{\rm Ai}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}(-y)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sin\left(\frac{2}{3}(-y)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}(|y|^{-\frac{7}{4}})\quad\text{as }y\to-\infty,

(A.5)

{\rm Bi}(y)=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{\pi}}(-y)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sin\left(\frac{2}{3}(-y)^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}(|y|^{-\frac{7}{4}})\quad\text{as }y\to-\infty.

As
y\to+\infty
,
{\rm Ai}
is exponentially decaying whereas
{\rm Bi}
is exponentially growing:

(A.6)

{\rm Ai}(y)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}y^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2}{3}y^{\frac{3}{2}}}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-\frac{3}{2}}))\quad\text{as }y\to+\infty,

(A.7)

{\rm Bi}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}y^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{2}{3}y^{\frac{3}{2}}}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-\frac{3}{2}}))\quad\text{as }y\to+\infty.

We introduce another linearly independent pair
(\operatorname{Ci},\operatorname{Ci}^{*})
defined by

(A.8)

\operatorname{Ci}:=e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\operatorname{Ai}+e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}\operatorname{Bi},\quad\operatorname{Ci}^{*}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}\operatorname{Ai}+e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\operatorname{Bi}.

Then
\operatorname{Ci}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\operatorname{Ci}
, and we have the following asymptotic formula

(A.9)

\operatorname{Ci}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}(-y)^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{i{\frac{2}{3}}(-y)^{\frac{3}{2}}}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-\frac{3}{2}}))\quad\text{as }y\to-\infty.

We call
{\rm Ci}
an
outgoing
solution and
{\rm Ci}^{*}
an
incoming
solution.

Lemma A.1
.

For real numbers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
with
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}\neq 0
, we have

(A.10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda_{1}(x-\mu_{1}))\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda_{2}(x-\mu_{2}))dx=\frac{1}{(\lambda_{2}^{3}-\lambda_{1}^{3})^{1/3}}\operatorname{Ai}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\left(\lambda_{1}^{-3}-\lambda_{2}^{-3}\right)^{1/3}}\right).

Here we define
r^{1/3}=\operatorname{sgn}(r)\left|r\right|^{1/3}
for
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Recall the definition (
A.1
) of the Airy function, which is a Fourier transform of the exponential function of a cubic function, more precisely,
\operatorname{Ai}(y)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{F}(e^{-i\frac{\bullet^{3}}{3}})(y)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}(e^{i\frac{\bullet^{3}}{3}})(y)
. The Fourier transform and its inverse are defined by

\mathcal{F}u(y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ixy}u(x)dx,\quad\mathcal{F}^{-1}u(y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ixy}u(x)dx,\quad u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

Then we have

\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda_{j}(x-\mu_{j}))=\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{F}(e^{i\mu_{j}\bullet}e^{-\frac{i}{3}(\frac{\bullet}{\lambda_{j}})^{3}})(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}(e^{-i\mu_{j}\bullet}e^{\frac{i}{3}(\frac{\bullet}{\lambda_{j}})^{3}})(x).

We then apply the standard formula

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{F}f)(x)(\mathcal{F}^{-1}g)(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)g(y)dy

of Fourier transformation to the integral (
A.10
), and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda_{1}(x-\mu_{1}))\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda_{2}(x-\mu_{2}))dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})y}e^{-\frac{iy^{3}}{3}(\lambda_{1}^{-3}-\lambda_{2}^{-3})}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(\lambda_{2}^{3}-\lambda_{1}^{3})^{1/3}}\mathcal{F}(e^{-\frac{i\bullet^{3}}{3}})\left(\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\left(\lambda_{1}^{-3}-\lambda_{2}^{-3}\right)^{1/3}}\right)

∎

y<0
. Then

A_{n}(y)=\frac{h^{-\frac{1}{2n+1}}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\psi(x)/h}dx

The critical points of the phase function
\psi
are
x=\pm 1
, and near each point, it behaves like

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n}-\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}(1-x)^{n+1}+{\mathcal{O}}((x-1)^{n+2})\,\,\text{ near }x=1,

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=-c_{n}+\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}(1+x)^{n+1}+{\mathcal{O}}((x+1)^{n+2})\,\,\text{ near }x=-1.

Then we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\psi(x)/h}dx=e^{\frac{i}{h}c_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{h}\frac{2^{n}z^{n+1}}{n+1}\right)dz+e^{-\frac{i}{h}c_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{i}{h}\frac{2^{n}z^{n+1}}{n+1}\right)dz+{\mathcal{O}}(h^{\frac{2}{n+1}}).

The asymptotics of
A_{n}(y)
as
y\to-\infty
follows from (
A.14
).
∎

Appendix B
Solutions to a scalar Schrödinger equation near a simple turning point

We define local solutions to the scalar Schrödinger equation

(B.1)

P_{j}u:=\left(-h^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{j}(x)\right)u=Eu,

for complex
E
near
0
. We omit the index
j
in this section.
Recall from Assumption
A′
that
V
extends to a holomorphic function in a small complex neighborhood of
x=0
, and
V(0)=0
and
V^{\prime}(0)>0
.

It is known (see e.g.,
[
15
,
16
,
5
]
) that there exists a pair of solutions
(u^{\sharp},u^{\flat})
to (
B.1
) which satisfies
{\mathcal{W}}(u^{\sharp},u^{\flat})=2ih^{-1}
and

(B.2)

\displaystyle u^{\sharp}(x)=\sqrt{\pi}h^{-1/6}\xi^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}\operatorname{Ci}(h^{-2/3}\xi(x))(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h))

\displaystyle u^{\flat}(x)=\sqrt{\pi}h^{-1/6}\xi^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}\operatorname{Ci}^{*}(h^{-2/3}\xi(x))(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h))

as
h\to 0^{+}
uniformly in a neighborhood of
x=0
.
Here the function
\xi(x)
, depending on
E
, is initially defined for real
E
near
0
by

(B.3)

\xi(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\left(\frac{3}{2}\int_{x}^{a(E)}\sqrt{E-V(t)}\,dt\right)^{2/3}\quad\text{for }\ x\leq a(E),\\
&\left(\frac{3}{2}\int_{a(E)}^{x}\sqrt{V(t)-E}\,dt\right)^{2/3}\quad\text{for }\ x>a(E),\end{aligned}\right.\quad

where
a(E)
is the unique root of
V(x)=E
near
x=0
. In particular,
a(E)=\frac{E}{V^{\prime}(0)}+\mathcal{O}(E^{2})
depends analytically on
E
near
E=0
. Then,
\xi
can be extended to an analytic function of
(x,E)
in a small complex neighborhood of
(x,E)=(0,0)
. We can check that this function satisfies the following identity:

(B.4)

\xi(x)\xi^{\prime}(x)^{2}=V(x)-E.

There exists
t=t(h)
such that

t=e^{-i\pi/4}+{\mathcal{O}}(h)\quad\text{as }h\to 0^{+},

and that the pair of solutions
(u^{-},u^{+})
defined by

(B.5)

(u^{-},u^{+}):=(u^{\sharp},u^{\flat})\left[\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}t&\bar{t}\\
\bar{t}&t\end{pmatrix}\right]

satisfies
{\mathcal{W}}(u^{-},u^{+})=h^{-1}
and

(B.6)

\displaystyle u^{-}=\sqrt{\pi}h^{-1/6}\xi^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}\operatorname{Ai}(h^{-2/3}\xi(x))(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h)),

(B.7)

\displaystyle u^{+}=\sqrt{\pi}h^{-1/6}\xi^{\prime}(x)^{-1/2}\operatorname{Bi}(h^{-2/3}\xi(x))(1+{\mathcal{O}}(h)),

as
h\to 0^{+}
uniformly in a neighborhood of
x=0
.

Away from the turning point, the above solutions behave like WKB solutions :

(B.8)

\displaystyle u^{-}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt[4]{V(x)-E}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{h}\int_{a(E)}^{x}\sqrt{V(t)-E}\,dt\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{h^{2/3}}{x-a(E)}\right)\right),

\displaystyle u^{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{V(x)-E}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{h}\int_{a(E)}^{x}\sqrt{V(t)-E}\,dt\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{h^{2/3}}{x-a(E)}\right)\right),

for
x-a(E)\gg h^{2/3}
, and

(B.9)

\displaystyle u^{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{E-V(x)}}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{h}\int_{a(E)}^{x}\sqrt{E-V(t)}\,dt\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{h^{2/3}}{a(E)-x}\right)\right),

\displaystyle u^{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{E-V(x)}}\exp\left(\frac{i}{h}\int_{a(E)}^{x}\sqrt{E-V(t)}\,dt\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{h^{2/3}}{a(E)-x}\right)\right),

for
x-a(E)\ll-h^{2/3}
.
