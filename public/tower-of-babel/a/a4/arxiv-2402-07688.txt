CyberMetric: A Benchmark Dataset for Evaluating Large Language Models Knowledge in Cybersecurity
Norbert Tihanyi, Mohamed Amine Ferrag, Ridhi Jain, Merouane Debbah

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) excel across various domains, from computer vision to medical diagnostics. However, understanding the diverse landscape of cybersecurity, encompassing cryptography, reverse engineering, and managerial facets like risk assessment, presents a challenge, even for human experts. In this paper, we introduce CyberMetric, a benchmark dataset comprising 10,000 questions sourced from standards, certifications, research papers, books, and other publications in the cybersecurity domain. The questions are created through a collaborative process, i.e., merging expert knowledge with LLMs, including GPT-3.5 and Falcon-180B. Human experts spent over 200 hours verifying their accuracy and relevance. Beyond assessing LLMs’ knowledge, the dataset’s main goal is to facilitate a fair comparison between humans and different LLMs in cybersecurity. To achieve this, we carefully selected 80 questions covering a wide range of topics within cybersecurity and involved 30 participants of diverse expertise levels, facilitating a comprehensive comparison between human and machine intelligence in this area. The findings revealed that LLMs outperformed humans in almost every aspect of cybersecurity.

III
Methodology

Our methodology features a thoughtfully constructed Framework that harnesses the synergy of human experts and LLMs for creating cybersecurity-related questions. The framework for generating and evaluating the
CyberMetric
dataset is illustrated in Figure
2
. The Framework comprises five crucial phases: ➀ Data Collection, ➁ Question Generation, ➂ Question Post-processing, ➃ Question Validation Phase, and ➄ Reference Dataset Creation Phase. Human involvement is integral in three key stages: during question generation to eliminate invalid and irrelevant questions; in the test phase, where cybersecurity experts with over 10 years of experience validate and test question relevance and accuracy; and in the survey phase, where 30 participants are recruited to answer 80 questions, enabling comparison between different LLMs and human intelligence. Moreover, human participants’ feedback serves as reference points in the analysis; for instance, if an LLM like GPT-3.5 achieves 87% accuracy on the 80 validated dataset but only 50% on the
10,000
dataset, it indicates potential issues with either the questions or the solutions. Before delving into the analysis of the results, we outline the significant phases within the framework in this section.

III-A

➀Data Collection

The questions are generated from popular cybersecurity documents and references available over the internet. The collected documents include open standards, RFCs, publications, and guidelines from NIST and other organizations. In total, we gathered 580 documents spanning more than
100,000
pages. All of the collected documents were in PDF format. Therefore, we have utilized
pdfminer
1
1

1

\url
https://pypi.org/project/pdfminer/
to handle the files and extract the text from them, which can then be fed into an LLM for further processing. During this stage, we eliminated any irrelevant sections of the text that were unrelated to cybersecurity (e.g., table of contents, prefaces, acknowledgments, pictures, references, and appendices).

III-B

➁Question Generation

During this stage, the extracted raw text is segmented into chunks to ensure it can be effectively fed into an LLM (with each chunk being smaller than the input token size of the LLM). Initially, we utilized the GPT-3.5 API to generate ten questions for every
8,000
tokens. This approach aims to maintain a balanced representation of each publication. Creating
1,000
questions from a ten-page document might introduce bias to the dataset. By employing this method, we ensure that the distribution and representation of questions align proportionately with the length of each topic. The generated questions are reviewed by Falcon-180B to identify and correct grammatical and semantic errors. We refer to this module as the
FALCON validator
, which also filters out questions irrelevant to the topic. By applying semantic analysis
[
34
]
to the text, we excluded questions unrelated to cybersecurity. For instance, the question
”Which is the correct spelling: ’cybersecurity’ or ’cyber-security’?”
primarily focuses on linguistic matters rather than cybersecurity, despite containing the word cybersecurity. Throughout this stage, non-security human validators prioritize evaluating the relevance of the questions and ensuring correct English grammar while reviewing the questions to ensure they are easily understandable for humans. In this phase, a total of
11,000
questions are generated by GPT-3.5. This extra
10\%
margin allows for the removal of unnecessary or irrelevant questions. During this phase, the
FALCON validator
eliminated 1.7% of the questions, while human experts dedicated over
30
hours to remove 2.3% of all irrelevant and unrelated to cybersecurity questions. These questions comprise the
intermediate database
, which consists of grammatically correct cybersecurity-related questions. However, at this stage, neither LLMs nor human experts have verified the accuracy of the solutions. Currently, we have
10,560
questions awaiting validation by human experts to identify and remove any questions featuring multiple or incorrect solutions. All these questions will undergo additional analysis involving various models and human experts.

III-C

➂Question Post-processing

First, we employ the cutting-edge T5-base model from Google, which is trained for grammar correction and can identify inaccuracies more efficiently than human experts. This model identified 230 questions where the English could be improved. For instance, the model identified inaccuracies such as
”Which of the following elements do not apply to privacy?”
. The sentence contains a grammatical inconsistency related to subject-verb agreement. The subject ”which of the following elements” is singular because it refers to one element among several. Therefore, the verb should also be singular to match the subject, so the correct sentence would be:
”Which of the following elements does not apply to privacy?”
After conducting a grammatical error check, we have again utilized the
FALCON validator
. However, this time, instead of focusing on semantic checking, we aimed to eliminate non-contextual questions that cannot be understood without external reference. Questions generated during the creation phase, such as ”According to Figure 1” or ”As seen in Table 6,” are nonsensical without relevant content. A thorough understanding of English is essential to filter out these inappropriate questions. This is where Falcon-180B comes into play, enabling the systematic identification and correction of such issues.
After fixing and removing such issues, we have used GPT-4.0 to analyze all the remaining
10470
questions and requested it to highlight those questions where it is very confident that the answer is incorrect. GPT-4.0 attained an accuracy 85% on the dataset and identified around 1620 questions where it believes the answer is incorrect.

III-D

➃Test Phase

We have reached a critical stage in our analysis. All the questions highlighted by GPT-4 need to be reviewed by human experts to determine whether the question is correct, as GPT-4 lacks the answer, or if GPT-4 was accurate in flagging it as incorrect. Upon thoroughly examining the questions that GPT-4 couldn’t answer correctly (despite providing correct reasoning in most cases), we discovered that many questions were inaccurate or imprecise. We categorize these issues in the following five categories:

1.

Multiple Correct Answers:
Some of the curated questions in the dataset had more than one correct answer; however, we devised our experiments to have only one correct answer out of the provided four options.

2.

Time Relevant Questions:
Since the questions and their answers are extracted from resources from as long as ten years ago, the specified information may not hold in the present. For instance, the response to this question,
”Which web server dominates the market according to web surveys?”
was Apache in 2015 (as per the source document’s publication date), but in 2023 it would be Nginx.

3.

Incomplete Context:
Some questions were partially formed and offered only incomplete information, resulting in multiple potential solutions or no solution.

4.

Incorrect Information in the Source:
To our surprise, some selected sources contained incorrect information despite being very reputed and widely followed. We verified such incorrect statements with multiple field experts.

5.

Missing References:
Some generated questions were deriving references from the origin document, such as ”As per table 2” or “According to Figure 1”. These questions cannot be answered without the referred content. These questions should have been eliminated already, but we still found statements like
”In chapter 4”
or
”In the author’s opinion”
.

After thoroughly reviewing and resolving question-related issues, we have
10,170
questions remaining. Our team dedicated over 200 expert hours during the manual review process to rectify inaccuracies and ensure the elimination of any false positives. The remaining 170 questions were removed from the database through similarity checking using FALCON-180B. Questions that asked the same or similar concepts were minimized in the database. For instance, only one question remains from the pair
”What is the fundamental goal of information security, according to the CIA triad?”
and
”What is the primary objective of information security, as outlined by the CIA triad?”
, as they are similar. In the last phase, an automated statistical analysis is carried out.
This is to confirm that all answers are consistent within the A, B, C, and D options. Additionally, we have taken steps to ensure that the answers are evenly distributed among the options. These thoroughly reviewed and finalized questions establish the core of the
CyberMetric
dataset containing exactly
10.000
questions. In Table

, one can observe the final distribution of questions.

III-E

➂Reference Dataset Creation

The primary aim of this study is to conduct a comprehensive assessment of LLMs by comparing their performance to human responses when addressing security-related queries. Asking
10,000
questions would be logistically impractical for human participants. While randomly selecting a subset, such as ten questions, could serve a purpose, it introduces the risk of bias as some questions may not adequately represent the entire spectrum of security inquiries. To mitigate this challenge, a panel of experts in various cybersecurity domains, each with a minimum of 10 years of experience and holding internationally recognized certifications like CISSP, CISM, OSCP, OSEP, and ISO 27001LA, collaboratively selected 80 questions. We refer to this dataset as
CyberMetric-80
, which will be the foundation for comparing human and machine intelligence in cybersecurity. These questions have been carefully chosen to facilitate a fair and meaningful comparison between LLMs and human respondents. We recruited 30 survey participants through our social networks and contacted individuals from universities, research institutions, and Big4 consulting companies to volunteer. We have prepared a Google Forms survey containing inquiries related to gender, age, years of experience, and the highest level of education and requested volunteers, spanning from beginners to experts, to complete the questionnaire. The comprehensive analysis of the accuracy of the 30 participants and the LLMs will be discussed in Section
IV
. Moreover, the
CyberMetric-80
dataset can be used as a reference point. If there’s a notable difference in LLM’s accuracy between
CyberMetric
and the fully validated
CyberMetric-80
dataset (e.g., 30%), it indicates that the larger dataset lacks precision, or the solutions are wrong.

IV
Experimental Results

The experiments can be classified into two main groups: the first involves conducting thorough testing of LLMs, and the second focuses on evaluating human performance. These two sets of results are then merged to gauge the performance of humans versus machines in the field of cybersecurity precisely.

IV-A

LLMs Performance on CyberMetric-80

Throughout the assessment, we test the most relevant LLM models. In our testing configuration, each model undergoes execution and evaluation on an AWS
[
37
]
ml.p4d.24xlarge instance equipped with 8x NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPU cards, complemented by 382 GB of RAM, and running on Ubuntu 22.04.
For large models like Falcon-180B or Mixtral-8x7B, the minimum requirement to run on Amazon SageMaker is the ml.p4d.24xlarge instance, which costs approximately $
40
per hour, resulting in a monthly cost of $
28,800
USD. Due to variations in how these models handle inputs, a dedicated framework was designed to enhance query processing efficiency. Additionally, the choice of prompts played a significant role in achieving the highest accuracy. For instance, in a 7B model, the formulation of the prompt is critical. In contrast, in a larger and more complex model like Falcon-180B, prompts become less relevant as the model can comprehend most questions without further instructions. A simple ”Please choose the best answer” suffices in such cases. The default temperature is configured at 1.0, along with a
top\_p
parameter set to
0.9
and
top\_k
set to
50
. We conducted four separate runs for each LLM model to assess potential response inconsistencies. To evaluate variability, we calculated both the standard deviation and mean values. Standard deviation is a critical statistical tool used to measure the dispersion or variability within a dataset. It is denoted as
\sigma
and is calculated as:

\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\mu)^{2}}

where
N
is the number of observations,
x_{i}
is each observation, and
\mu
is the mean of these observations (in our specific scenario, N=4, and the
x_{i}
values represent the individual runs).

Table
II
displays the varying outcomes of the 14 tested LLMs, including the four separate runs, standard deviation, and mean values.
Standard deviation calculation is key in enabling fair comparisons among different LLMs. It can reveal the ”stability” of an LLM model. A smaller standard deviation might indicate higher confidence in the model’s responses, suggesting consistency across different iterations. Conversely, a large standard deviation implies that the model’s answers vary significantly in different runs, indicating less output reliability.
It is important to mention that a lower temperature setting, intended to introduce more freedom and randomness in the model’s output, can substantially elevate the standard deviation. The most accurate model we tested was
GPT-4
with mean accuracy
95.63
% (c.f Table
II
). The two most effective models available as open source are Mistral AI’s
Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct
and TII’s
Falcon-180B
. Among these, the top-performing model in terms of model size is HuggingFace’s fine-tuned Transformer architecture known as
Zephyr-7B-beta
. This model comprises a modest 7 billion parameters yet achieves an impressive mean accuracy of
80.94\%
. A thorough examination of incorrect responses, weaknesses, and strengths will be discussed in Section
V
.

We must first evaluate human accuracy to draw meaningful conclusions from the LLMs’ accuracy. Responding to all 80 questions typically takes an expert around 40 to 60 minutes; the considerable time investment indicates a significant degree of determination and commitment. While a short questionnaire comprising 4-5 questions might often be answered hastily or even randomly, the extensive nature of an 80-question survey naturally serves as a deterrent to casual or inattentive responses. The fact that participants devoted enough time and effort to thoroughly answer such a large number of questions strongly suggests that they were highly engaged and committed to the task. This participation level indicates their determination to complete the survey with due diligence. The participants also acknowledged this fact after the survey’s completion. Altogether,

V-C

Most Difficult Questions for LLMs

Which domain poses the greatest challenges for LLMs? This serves as a critical metric for conducting a meaningful comparison. Do humans encounter the same difficulties, or are these issues entirely distinct? Here, we present some fascinating findings, highlighting the most problematic domain for LLMs to tackle in answering questions.

V-C
1
Latest research direction

LLMs may encounter difficulties in providing accurate responses to questions rooted in the most recent research findings, especially when their training data is not up-to-date. Consider a simple example:
”What is the recommended interval for changing passwords?”
Previously, many guidelines stipulated a mandatory 30-day password change interval for all organizations. Most models undergo training with extensive data, and many standards and documents adhere to this guideline, usually endorsing 30 days as the appropriate answer. However, according to the latest guidelines, NIST SP 800-63B Section 5.1.1.2 paragraph 9 advises:
“Verifiers SHOULD NOT require memorized secrets to be changed arbitrarily (e.g., periodically). However, verifiers SHALL force a change if there is evidence of compromise of the authenticator.”
. The official explanation, while simple, is not entirely straightforward as it challenges the established research paradigm of password behaviors that has prevailed for the past 30 years. Recent research indicates that users tend to select weaker passwords when they know they must change them soon. When making these changes, they often create a new password similar to their old one by applying common modifications, like incrementing a number in the password. Since most documents do not align with these updated guidelines, it is anticipated that all LLMs will recommend users to change their passwords.
CyberMetric
includes questions from the most recent recommendations, such as the latest NIST recommendations, posing a significant challenge, even for security experts. Only a limited number of models, such as GPT-4, can answer this question. They may either possess internet access or utilize the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) technique to retrieve pertinent new information from the internet. Another question that advanced language models and humans often find challenging concerns the latest Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) recommendations. In January 2023, the BSI released the
”Cryptographic Mechanisms: Recommendations and Key Lengths (BSI TR-02102-1)”
guideline. This guideline specifies that for the Discrete Logarithm Integrated Encryption Scheme (DLIES), the prime number
p
should be no less than 3000 bits, and the prime number
q
should be a minimum of 250 bits. The choice of
3000
bits for
p
, which is not a power of two, and the requirement for
q
to be
256
bits results in what appears to be an unbalanced prime scheme. Even expert security analysts often find this question challenging, and many language models struggle to provide correct answers when dealing with questions. In our analysis, there was no occurrence where all the previously tested models failed to answer this question in at least one of the four runs.

can be challenging. While a model like GPT-4.0 can easily handle this with an embedded Python tool to ensure accurate results, models like
Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct

VI
Conclusion

In this research, we introduced the CyberMetric dataset, designed to evaluate the knowledge of large language models (LLMs) within the cybersecurity domain. Our study includes an in-depth comparison of LLM capabilities versus human intelligence, involving 30 participants. The evaluation underscores the strengths and limitations of LLMs in responding to security-centric questions. These models demonstrate a remarkable ability to navigate and articulate responses across a wide array of security scenarios and technical details. Although LLMs offer unparalleled speed and breadth in processing information, their reliance on historical data and the potential for embedded biases necessitate careful validation of their outputs. Findings reveal that modern LLMs frequently surpass cybersecurity experts in answering questions across various areas, showcasing their advanced analytical prowess.
