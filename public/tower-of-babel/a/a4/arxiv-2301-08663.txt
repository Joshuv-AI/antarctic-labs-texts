Uniqueness of the inverse conductivity problem once-differentiable complex conductivities in three dimensions
Ivan Pombo

Abstract
We prove uniqueness of the inverse conductivity problem in three dimensions for complex conductivities in W 1 , ∞ W^{1,\infty} . We apply quaternionic analysis to transform the inverse problem into an inverse Dirac scattering problem, as established in two dimensions by Brown and Uhlmann. This is a novel methodology that allows to extend the uniqueness result from once-differentiable real conductivities to complex ones.

Introduction

Let
\gamma\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
be the complex-valued conductivity defined in a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and given by
\gamma=\sigma+i\omega\epsilon
where
\sigma
is the electrical conductivity and satisfies
\sigma\geq c>0
,
\epsilon
is the electrical permittivity and
\omega
is the current frequency.

Given a boundary value
f\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
we can determine the respective electrical potential
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
by uniquely solving

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u)=0,\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\left.u\right|_{\partial\Omega}=f.\end{cases}

(1)

This is the so-called conductivity equation which describes the behavior of the electrical potential,
u
, in a conductive body when a voltage potential is applied on the boundary,
f
.

In 1980, A.P. Calderón,
[
11
]
introduced the problem of whether one can recover uniquely a conductivity

from the boundary measurements, i.e.,
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}:\,H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega),

(2)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\sigma\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\right|_{\partial\Omega}

which connects the voltage and electrical current at the boundary. The normal derivative exists as an element of

by

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda_{\sigma}f,g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx

(3)

where

with

solves (

2
Minimalistic lesson of Quaternions

We present the basis of the quaternionic framework we will use for our work. Let
\mathbb{R}_{(2)}
be the real universal Clifford Algebra over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. By definition, it is generated as an algebra over
\mathbb{R}
by the elements
\{e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}\}
, where
e_{1},e_{2}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}=-2\delta_{ij}
, for
i,\,j=1,\,2
and
e_{0}=1
is the identity and commutes with the basis elements. This algebra has dimension 4 and is identified with the algebra of the quaternions,
\mathbb{H}
. As such it holds
e_{3}=e_{1}e_{2}
.
In the following, we refer to this algebra as the quaternions. An element of the quaternions can be written as:

\displaystyle x=x_{0}+x_{1}e_{1}+x_{2}e_{2}+x_{3}e_{3},

(4)

where
x_{j},\,j=0,...,3
are real. We define the quaternionic conjugate
\bar{x}
of an element
x
as

\displaystyle\bar{x}=x_{0}-x_{1}e_{1}-x_{2}e_{2}-x_{3}e_{3}.

(5)

Let
x,\,y\in\mathbb{H}
, we write
xy
for the resulting quaternionic product. The product
\bar{x}y
defines a Clifford valued inner product on
\mathbb{H}
. Further, we have
\overline{xy}=\bar{y}\bar{x}
and the conjugate of the conjugate of quaternion is that same quaternion. Let
x\in\mathbb{H}
then
\text{Sc}\,x=x_{0}
denotes the scalar of
x
and
\text{Vec}\,x=x-\text{Sc}\,x
. The scalar of a Clifford inner product
\text{Sc}(\bar{x}y)
is the usual inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
for
x,\,y
identified as vectors.

With this inner product
\mathbb{H}
is an Hilbert space and the resulting norm is the usual Euclidean norm.

In order to introduce some of the concepts we also extend the real quaternions to complex quaternions
\mathbb{C}_{2}=\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{H}
. Here, we use the same generators
(e_{1},e_{2})
as above, with the same multiplication rules, however, the coefficients of the quaternion can be complex-valued. That is,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{H}
may be written as

\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}e_{1}+\lambda_{2}e_{2}+\lambda_{3}e_{3},\quad\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C},\,j=0,...,3

(6)

or still as

\displaystyle\lambda=x+iy,\quad x,\,y\in\mathbb{H}.

(7)

Due to the complexification we can still take another conjugation, to which we define has Hermitian conjugation and denote it by
\overline{\cdot}^{\dagger}
. Explicitly, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{H}
one has

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\lambda_{0}^{c}-\lambda_{1}^{c}e_{1}-\lambda_{2}^{c}e_{2}-\lambda_{3}^{c}e_{3},

(8)

where
\cdot^{c}
denotes complex conjugation, or

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\bar{x}-i\bar{y}.

(9)

Similarly, one can introduce an associated inner product and norm in
\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathbb{H}
by means of this conjugation:

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle=\text{Sc}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{{\dagger}}\mu\right);\quad|\lambda|_{\mathbb{C}_{2}}=\sqrt{\text{Sc}\left(\bar{\lambda}^{{\dagger}}\lambda\right)}.

(10)

For ease of notation, we also define for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
the complex conjugation as

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{c}=\lambda_{0}^{c}+\lambda_{1}^{c}e_{1}+\lambda_{2}^{c}e_{2}+\lambda_{3}^{c}e_{3}.

(11)

Now, we can also introduce Quaternion-valued functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{2}
written as
f=f_{0}+f_{1}e_{1}+f_{2}e_{2}+f_{3}e_{3},
where
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

The Banach spaces
L^{p},\,W^{n,p}
of
C_{2}
-valued functions are defines by requiring that each component is in such space. On
L^{2}(\Omega)
we introduce the
C_{2}
-valued inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\bar{f}^{\dagger}(x)g(x)\,dx.

(12)

Analogously to the Wirtinger derivatives in complex analysis, we have the Cauchy-Riemann operators under
(x_{0},\,x_{1},\,x_{2})
coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\displaystyle D=\partial_{0}+e_{1}\partial_{1}+e_{2}\partial_{2},

(13)

where
\partial_{j}
is the derivative with respect to the
x_{j},\,j=0,1,2
variable; and

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\partial_{0}-e_{1}\partial_{1}-e_{2}\partial_{2}.

(14)

The vector part of the Cauchy-Riemann operator is designated as Dirac operator.
It holds that
D\bar{D}=\Delta
where
\Delta
is the Laplacian.

We designate any function
f
fulfilling
Df=0
as a monogenic function, analogous to the holomorphic functions in complex analysis.

2.1
A bit of Operator Theory

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{2}
. All the results in this subsection were taken out from the classical book on quaternionic analysis of Gürlebeck and Sprössig
[
16
]

The Cauchy-Riemann operator has a right-inverse in the form

\displaystyle\left(Tf\right)(x)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\overline{y-x}}{|y-x|^{3}}f(y)\,dy,\text{ for }x\in\Omega,

(15)

where
E(x,y)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\frac{\overline{y-x}}{|y-x|^{3}}
is the generalized Cauchy kernel and
\omega=4\pi
stands for the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3},
that is,
DTf=f.
This operator acts from
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
to
W^{k+1,p}(\Omega)
with
1<p<\infty
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Furthermore, we introduce the boundary integral operator
for
x\notin\partial\Omega

\displaystyle\left(F_{\partial\Omega}f\right)(x)=\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\overline{y-x}}{|y-x|^{3}}\alpha(y)f(y)\,dS(y),

(16)

where
\alpha(y)
is the outward pointing normal unit vector to
\partial\Omega
at
y
. We get the well-known Borel-Pompeiu formula

\displaystyle\left(F_{\partial\Omega}f\right)(x)+\left(TDf\right)(x)=f(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

Obviously,
DF_{\partial\Omega}=0
holds through this formula it it holds that
F_{\partial\Omega}
acts from
W^{k-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)
into
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<p<\infty
.

One of the other well-known results we will need for our work is the Plemelj-Sokhotzki formula is obtaining by taking the trace of the boundary integral operator.

First we introduce an operator over the boundary of
\Omega
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
f\in W^{k,p}(\partial\Omega)
, then there exists the integral

\displaystyle\left(S_{\partial\Omega}f\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\overline{y-x}}{|y-x|^{3}}\alpha(y)f(y)\,dS(y)

(17)

for all points
x\in\Omega
in the sense of Cauchy principal value.

Furthermore, the operator
S_{\partial\Omega}
is continuous in
W^{k,p}(\partial\Omega)
, for
1<p<\infty,\,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

From this the Plemelj-Sokhotzki formula is given as:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f\in W^{k,p}(\partial\Omega)
where by taking the non-tangential limit we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\rightarrow x_{0},\\
x\in\Omega,\,x_{0}\in\partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\left(F_{\partial\Omega}f\right)(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(x_{0})+\left(S_{\partial\Omega}f\right)(x_{0})\right).

One of the corollaries concerns the limit to the boundary acting as a projector. That is,

Corollary 2.3
.

The operator
P_{\partial\Omega}
denoting the projection onto the space of all
\mathbb{H}-
valued functions which may be monogenicaly extended into the domain
\Omega
.

Then, this projection may be represented as

P_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I+S_{\partial\Omega}\right).

The proofs of this results and others to follow in our proofs may be found in
[
16
]
.

Now we are ready to start constructing our work on the inverse conductivity problem.

3
Inverse Dirac scattering problem

Transforming our conductivity equation into another type of equation also changes the inverse problem we are concerned. We transform it into a system of equations based on the Cauchy-Riemann operator
D
(also called Dirac operator in some contexts) and thus we need to solve the inverse Dirac scattering problem first and only afterwards we care about the inverse conductivity problem.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1
) for some boundary function. We define

\displaystyle\phi=\gamma^{1/2}\left(\,\bar{D}u,Du\right)^{T},

remark that
\gamma^{1/2}
is well-defined since it is contained in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Then,
\phi
solves the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}D\phi_{1}&=\phi_{2}q_{1},\\
\bar{D}\phi_{2}&=\phi_{1}q_{2},\end{cases}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(18)

where
q_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\bar{D}\gamma}{\gamma}
and
q_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{D\gamma}{\gamma}
.

This transformation arises as follows:

\displaystyle D\phi_{1}

\displaystyle=D\left(\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u\right)=D\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u+\gamma^{1/2}\Delta u

\displaystyle=D\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u-\gamma^{-1/2}\nabla\gamma\cdot\nabla u

\displaystyle=D\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{-1/2}\left(D\gamma\bar{D}u+\text{D}u\bar{D}\gamma\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{1/2}Du\right)\frac{\bar{D}\gamma}{\gamma}=\phi_{2}q_{1}

Carefully, we can extend our potential to the outside by setting
\gamma\equiv 1
outside of
\Omega
, which lead us to treat the study the equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

3.1
Exponentially Growing Solutions

We devise new exponentially growing solutions from the classical ones used in three dimensions. In most literature works, the exponential behavior is defined through the function
e^{x\cdot\zeta},
with
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
fulfilling
\zeta\cdot\zeta=0
. However, in our scenario this function does not fulfill
De^{ix\cdot\zeta}=0
, which brings the simplicity in all of the literature works.

Since we know that it is harmonic we can generate a monogenic function through it. Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
such that
\zeta\cdot\zeta:=\zeta_{0}^{2}+\zeta_{1}^{2}+\zeta_{2}^{2}=0
, then it holds

\displaystyle\Delta e^{x\cdot\zeta}=0\Leftrightarrow D\left(\bar{D}e^{x\cdot\zeta}\right)=0\equiv D\left(e^{x\cdot\zeta}\,\bar{\zeta}\right)

where now
\zeta
is also defined as a quaternion through
\zeta=\zeta_{0}+e_{1}\zeta_{1}+e_{2}\zeta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}.
Thus the function
E(x,\zeta)=e^{x\cdot\zeta}\,\bar{\zeta}
is monogenic. This also arises from the choice of
\zeta
, since
\zeta\,\bar{\zeta}=\zeta_{0}^{2}+\zeta_{1}^{2}+\zeta_{2}^{2}=0
.

We make a clear statement of when
\zeta
is a complex-quaternion or complex-a vector, but in most cases it is clear from context: it is a vector if it is in the exponent and a quaternion otherwise.

We assume the following asymptotic behaviour for
\phi
:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=e^{x\cdot\zeta}\bar{\zeta}\mu_{1},

(19)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}=e^{x\cdot\bar{\zeta}^{c}}\bar{\zeta}^{c}\mu_{2}

(20)

Setting
\tilde{\mu}_{1}=\bar{\zeta}\mu_{1}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{2}=\bar{\zeta}^{c}\mu_{2}
we have the equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}D\tilde{\mu}_{1}&=e^{-x\cdot\left(\zeta-\,\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{2}q_{1}\\
\bar{D}\tilde{\mu}_{2}&=e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\,\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{1}q_{2}\\
\end{cases}

(21)

Further, we assume
\tilde{\mu}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty.
These system of equations will lead us to an integral equation from which we can extract interesting behaviour for
\zeta\rightarrow\infty
.

The main point of this subsection is to demonstrate how we can obtain the system of integral equations related with (
21
). Here, the approach is similar to
[
21
]
, but we need to be careful due to the non-commutative nature of quaternions.

Recall, that
DT=\bar{D}\,\bar{T}=I
(in appropriate spaces). Hence, applying this to (
21
) it holds:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\mu}_{1}=1+T\left[e^{-x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{2}q_{1}\right]\\
\\
\tilde{\mu}_{2}=\overline{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{1}q_{2}\right]\end{cases}

Thus, we can obtain two integral equations with respect to their function:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\mu}_{1}=1+T\left[e^{-x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\bar{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{1}q_{2}\right]q_{1}\right]\\
\\
\tilde{\mu}_{2}=\bar{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}q_{2}\right]+\bar{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}T\left[e^{-x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{c}\right)}\tilde{\mu}_{2}q_{1}\right]q_{2}\right]\end{cases}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\mu}_{1}=1+M^{1}\tilde{\mu}_{1}\\
\tilde{\mu}_{2}=\overline{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\overline{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)}q_{2}\right]+M^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{2}\end{cases}\hskip-11.38092pt\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}[I-M^{1}](\tilde{\mu}_{1}-1)=M^{1}1\\
[I-M^{2}](\tilde{\mu}_{2})=\bar{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)}q_{2}\right]\end{cases}

(22)

Our objective now is to study the uniqueness and existence of this equations, we approach this task by proving that
M^{j},\,j=1,\,2
are contractions.

Instead of working with all possible
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}_{(2)}
fulfilling
\zeta\bar{\zeta}=0
, we choose them for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

\zeta=k^{\perp}+i\frac{k}{2},\quad k^{\perp}\cdot k=0

and
k^{\perp}
can be algorithmically found.

We now describe our space of functions in terms of the space variable and
k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

\displaystyle S=L^{\infty}_{x}(L^{p}_{k}(|k|>R))

(23)

where
R>0
is a constant. In this space we prove that the operators
M^{1},\,M^{2}
are indeed contractions:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p>2
. Then

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\|M^{j}\|_{S}=0.

To further study the system (
22
), we also need to show that the right-hand side is in
S
for an
R
large enough:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p>2
. Then there exists
R>0
such that

\displaystyle M^{1}1\in S,

(24)

\displaystyle\bar{T}\left[e^{x\cdot\left(\zeta-\bar{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)}q_{2}\right]\in S

(25)

The above Lemmas imply the existence and uniqueness of
(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\tilde{\mu}_{2})
solving the system (
22
) with respect to the potential
q
. This is essential for the reconstruction procedure we show up next.

3.2
Reconstruction from scattering data

In this section, we are mixing ideas from
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
with quaternionic theory to obtain the potential from the scattering data.

Starting from Clifford-Green theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[g(x)\left(\bar{D}f(x)\right)+\left(g(x)\bar{D}\right)f(x)\right]\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x)\overline{\eta(x)}f(x)\,dS_{x}

and using
g(x;i\xi+\zeta)=(i\xi+\zeta)e^{-x\cdot(i\xi+\zeta)}
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
(i\xi+\zeta)\cdot(i\xi+\zeta)=0
. This implies that
g\bar{D}=0.
Thus we define the scattering data as:

\displaystyle h(\xi,\zeta)=\left(i\xi+\zeta\right)\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{-x\cdot(i\xi+\zeta)}\overline{\eta(x)}\phi_{2}(x,\zeta)\,dx

(26)

Applying now Clifford-Green theorem we obtain another form for the scattering data:

\displaystyle h(\xi,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\left(i\xi+\zeta\right)\int_{\Omega}e^{-x\cdot(i\xi+\zeta)}\bar{D}\phi_{2}(x,\zeta)\,dx

\displaystyle=(i\xi+\zeta)\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left(e^{-x\cdot\zeta}\phi_{1}(x,\zeta)\right)q_{2}(x)\,dx,\quad\text{by }\overline{\text{D}}\phi_{2}=\phi_{1}q_{2}

\displaystyle=(i\xi+\zeta)\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left(\zeta\mu_{1}(x,\zeta)\right)q_{2}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=i\xi\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta)q_{2}(x)\,dx,\quad\textnormal{since }\bar{\zeta}\zeta=0

\displaystyle=i\xi\hat{q}_{2}({\xi})+i\xi\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta)-1\right]q_{2}(x)\,dx.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{2}(\xi)=\frac{h(\xi,\zeta)}{i\xi}-\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta)-1\right]q_{2}(x)\,dx

(27)

This is yet not enough to reconstruct the potential, since the integral acts as a residual in the reconstruction and requires data that we technically do not have. Therefore, we integrate everything over an annulus in
k

\displaystyle\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{\hat{q}_{2}(\xi)}{|k|^{3}}\,dk

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\xi}\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{h(\xi,\zeta(k))}{|k|^{3}}\,dk

\displaystyle-\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right]q_{2}(x)\,dx,

(28)

since the potential does not depend on
k
it can be taken out of the integral and taking the limit as
R\rightarrow\infty
leads the second integral on the right to decay to zero, obtaining a reconstruction formula.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
a bounded Lipschitz domain,
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
be a complex-valued potential obtained through a conductivity
\gamma\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega),\,\text{Re }\gamma\geq c>0.
Then, we can reconstruct the potential from

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{2}(\xi)=\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C}{i\xi}\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{h(\xi,\zeta(k))}{|k|^{3}}\,dk,

(29)

where
C=\frac{1}{4\pi\ln(2)}
.

Proof.

The scattering data is defined from the solutions of the Dirac system (
22
) and therefore it holds that
\tilde{\mu}_{1}-1\in S
. Starting from (
3.2
) we obtain by integrating the right-hand side for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle 4\pi\ln 2\,\hat{q}_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\xi}\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{h(\xi,\zeta(k))}{|k|^{3}}\,dk

\displaystyle-\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right]q_{2}(x)\,dx

Let
p>2
and
1/p+1/q=1
. We estimate the last integral:

\displaystyle\Bigg|

\displaystyle\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right]q_{2}(x)\,dx\Bigg|\leq

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3}}\int_{\Omega}\left|e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\left[\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right]q_{2}(x)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Omega}\|q\|_{\infty}\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3}}\sup_{x}\left|\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right|\,dk

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Omega}\|q\|_{\infty}\left[\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3q}}\,dk\right]^{1/q}\left[\int_{R<|k|<2R}\sup_{x}\left|\tilde{\mu}_{1}(x,\zeta(k))-1\right|^{p}\,dk\right]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Omega}\|q\|_{\infty}\|\tilde{\mu}_{1}-1\|_{S}\left[\int_{R<|k|<2R}\frac{1}{|k|^{3q}}\,dk\right]^{1/q}

Taking the limit as
R\rightarrow 0
the integral that is left goes to zero which implies the desired decay to zero and leaves us with our reconstruction formula.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let

Let us recall the main theorem, that we are now able to prove with all these pieces we assembled.

Theorem 1.1

Let

, for example.

For such, let us start with two conductivities

for
\Omega
a bounded domain. By hypothesis
\Lambda_{\gamma_{1}}=\Lambda_{\gamma_{2}}
and thus by
[

\left.\gamma_{1}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\gamma_{2}\right|_{\partial\Omega}

Further, we can extend
\gamma_{j},\,j=1,2

\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
and
\gamma_{j}-1\in W^{1,\infty}_{\text{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle M^{1}f(x)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

If
h
is a scalar-valued and harmonic function that fulfills

\textnormal{Vec}(S_{\partial\Omega}h)=0,

then
\left.h\right|_{\partial\Omega}
is constant.

Proof.

First, note that
I+S_{\partial\Omega}=P_{\partial\Omega}
is a projector and by Proposition 2.5.12 and Corollary 2.5.15 of
[
16
]
it holds that
P_{\partial\Omega}h
is the boundary value of a monogenic function in
\Omega
.

Since
h
is a scalar-valued function it holds that

\displaystyle P_{\partial\Omega}h

\displaystyle=\text{Sc}(P_{\partial\Omega}h)+\text{Vec}(P_{\partial\Omega}h)

\displaystyle=(h+\text{Sc}_{\partial\Omega}h)+\text{Vec}(S_{\partial\Omega}h).

Let
w=(h+\text{Sc}_{\partial\Omega}h)
and
v=\text{Vec}(S_{\partial\Omega}h)
. Now, we denote
f
as the monogenic extension of
P_{\partial\Omega}h
in
\Omega
, as such, the boundary values of
f
fulfill
\text{tr}f=w+v
. Note that by hypothesis we have that
\left.v\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

Hence,
f
is also an harmonic function, which implies that the scalar and vector components are harmonic.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta(\text{Vec}f)=0,\\
\left.\text{Vec\,}f\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{cases}

By a mean value theorem or a maximum principle it holds that
\text{Vec}f=0
. Due to this and

being monogenic we obtain that

. Thus,
\text{Re}f=c
since

Consequently, the boundary values are also constant, which means that

\text{Sc}(S_{\partial\Omega}h)

∎

Proof of Proposition 3.4

Suppose that
(u,v)
are solutions to the following equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bar{D}u=\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1}\\
Dv=\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2}.\end{cases}

From applying the operator
D
and
\bar{D}
to the first and second equation respectively, we obtain from
\phi_{2}=\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}
and
q_{2}=\overline{q}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}
the following:

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=D(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1})=D(\gamma^{-1/2})\phi_{1}+\gamma^{-1/2}D\phi_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{-3/2}(D\gamma\phi_{1})+\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2}q_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[q_{2}\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}q_{1}\right]=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[\overline{q}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}\phi_{1}+\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}q_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\text{Sc }(\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}q_{1}).

and

\displaystyle\Delta v

\displaystyle=\bar{D}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2})=\bar{D}(\gamma^{-1/2})\phi_{2}+\gamma^{-1/2}\bar{D}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{-3/2}(\bar{D}\gamma)\phi_{2}+\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1}q_{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[q_{1}\phi_{2}+\phi_{1}q_{2}\right]=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[q_{1}\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}+\phi_{1}\overline{q}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\text{Sc }(\phi_{1}\overline{q}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}).

The first thing to notice is that both equations imply that
u
and
v
must be scalar-valued functions.

Further, notice that

\displaystyle\Delta(u-v)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[\text{Sc }(\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}q_{1})-\text{Sc }(\phi_{1}\overline{q}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}})\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1/2}\left[\text{Sc }(\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}q_{1})-\text{Sc }(\overline{q_{1}\overline{\phi}_{1}^{\mathbb{H}}})\right]=0.

Therefore,
h=u-v
is an harmonic function. Our objective is to show that
h\equiv 0
, thus showing that
u=v
.

For such, let us consider the theory of integral transforms in quaternionic analysis. We have

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\bar{T}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1})+\overline{F}_{\partial\Omega}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1})\text{ and }

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\bar{T}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1})+\overline{F}_{\partial\Omega}(u),

which implies that

\overline{F}_{\partial\Omega}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1})=\overline{F}_{\partial\Omega}u.

Analogously, we obtain

F_{\partial\Omega}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2})=F_{\partial\Omega}v.

Here, we can extrapolate from the first equation and from
u
being scalar-valued that

\displaystyle\overline{\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1}}F_{\partial\Omega}=F_{\partial\Omega}u

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2}F_{\partial\Omega}=F_{\partial\Omega}u.

Applying the operator
F_{\partial\Omega}
on the other side, we obtain:

\displaystyle F_{\partial\Omega}^{2}u

\displaystyle=F_{\partial\Omega}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2})F_{\partial\Omega}\text{ and }

\displaystyle F_{\partial\Omega}^{2}v

\displaystyle=F_{\partial\Omega}(\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{2})F_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow F_{\partial\Omega}^{2}h=F_{\partial\Omega}^{2}(u-v)=0.

If we take the trace on both sides, the operator becomes a projector thus we obtain
\text{tr }F_{\partial\Omega}h=0
.

Now, through the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula we obtain:

\displaystyle\text{tr }F_{\partial\Omega}h=\left.h\right|_{\partial\Omega}+S_{\partial\Omega}h=0,\text{ at }\partial\Omega.

Since
h
is a scalar-valued function that we decompose this formulation with the scalar and vector part to obtain two conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&h+\text{Sc}(S_{\partial\Omega}h)=0\\
&\text{Vec}(S_{\partial\Omega}h)=0.\end{cases}

Through the second condition and Lemma
4.1
we have that
h
is constant over
\partial\Omega
.

Now, given that
h
is a scalar constant, the first condition reduces to:

\displaystyle h(1+\text{Sc}(S_{\partial\Omega}1))=0

By
[
16
]
we obtain that
1+\text{Sc}(S_{\partial\Omega}1)=1/2
in
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, we conclude that
h\equiv 0
in
\partial\Omega
. Given that
h
is harmonic, this immediately implies that
h=0
in
\Omega
.

Therefore, we obtain
u=v
, and therefore there exists a unique solution to the initial system through the
T
and
F_{\partial\Omega}
operators in
\Omega
.

To finalize, we only need to show that
u
fulfills the conductivity equation in
\Omega
.

Bringing the first equation to light

\bar{D}u=\gamma^{-1/2}\phi_{1},

changing the side of the conductivity we get
\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u=\phi_{1}
and applying the
D
operator to both sides now brings

\displaystyle D\left(\gamma^{1/2}\bar{D}u\right)=D\phi_{1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle D\left(\gamma^{1/2}\right)\bar{D}u+\gamma^{1/2}\Delta u=\phi_{2}q_{1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle D\left(\gamma^{1/2}\right)\bar{D}u+\gamma^{1/2}\Delta u=\gamma^{-1/2}Du\frac{1}{2}\frac{\bar{D}\gamma}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{1/2}D\gamma\bar{D}u+\gamma^{1/2}\Delta u+\frac{1}{2}Du\frac{\bar{D}\gamma}{\gamma^{1/2}}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\nabla\gamma\cdot\nabla u+\gamma\Delta u=0\Leftrightarrow\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla u\right)=0

∎

As such, we conclude our proof of uniqueness for complex-conductivities in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\gamma}
. Notice that (
