Fringe trees of Patricia tries, compressed binary search trees, and three other random full binary trees Thanks: Supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation and the Swedish Research Council
Svante Janson

Abstract
. We study the distribution of fringe trees in Patricia tries (extending earlier results by Ischebeck (2025)) and compressed binary search trees; both cases are random binary trees that have been compressed by deleting nodes of outdegree 1 so that they are random full binary trees. The main results are central limit theorems for the number of fringe trees of a given type, which imply quenched and annealed limit results for the fringe tree distribution; for Patricia tries, this is complicated by periodic oscillations in the usual manner. We also consider extended fringe trees. The results are derived from earlier results for uncompressed tries and binary search trees. In the case of compressed binary search trees, it seems difficult to give a closed formula for the asymptotic fringe tree distribution, but we provide a recursion and give examples. For comparison, we give also results, simpler and partly known, for three other models of random full binary trees: the extended binary search tree, the critical beta-spltting random tree, and the uniform random full binary tree.

Introduction

In this paper, we study and compare fringe tree distributions for some
different types of random full binary trees.
The paper is inspired by the work by
Aldous
[
3
;
4
;
5
;
7
]

on random models for
phylogenetic trees, more precisely
cladograms
.
(These are for our purposes essentially the same as full binary trees;
see Remark
1.2
.)
Aldous
[
3
]
introduced the
beta-splitting model

of a random full binary tree with a given number
n
of leaves.
The model has a parameter
\beta\in[-2,\infty]
, and Aldous noted

[
3
, Section 4.1]

that three special cases
give models that have appeared in many other applications (in, for example,
computer science); they are, with the notations used in the present paper
(see Section

\beta=-3/2

the uniform random full binary tree
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}

\beta=0
:

the extended binary search tree
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n-1}

\beta=\infty
:

the random Patricia trie
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
(in the symmetric case
p=1/2

A fourth special case,
studied in, for example,
[
4
;
5
;
7

is

\beta=-1

the critical beta-splitting random tree

[

Fringe trees for several of these models have been studied before,
see the references given in Section

.
We give in the present paper detailed results for these four models,
partly collected or adapted from earlier work,
and we complement these results with
corresponding results for
the compressed binary search tree

We consider asymptotics as the size of the random tree tends to
infinity.
For
a binary search tree (BST)
\mathcal{B}_{n}
,
or a
random trie

More precise result including asymptotic normality of

[

[

]

[

[

]

These results can be used to derive corresponding results for
Patricia tries
[
28
]

and extended or compressed binary search trees,

5

In particular, in these cases the fringe tree counts are asymptotically normal.

For the critical beta-splitting random tree
\mathcal{D}_{n}

the asymptotic fringe tree distribution is found in
[
5

,
see also
[
4
]
.
We improve earlier results and show convergence in probability of

For the uniform random full binary tree

[

Also in this case, the fringe tree counts are asymptotically normal

For Patricia tries

For the extended binary search tree

We leave as an open problem to find a formula for the asymptotic

In Section

1.1.
The studied trees

We use the following (common, but not universal) notation.
(See Section
2.1
for further notation.)
A
binary tree
is a rooted tree such that each child of a node is
labelled either
left
or
right
, and each node has at most one
left and at most one right child.
A
full binary tree
is a binary tree where each node has outdegree 0
or 2. (In the latter case, it thus has one left and one right child.)
A
leaf
is a node with no children (i.e., outdegree 0).
The
size

|T|
of a tree
T
is its number of nodes,
and the
leaf size

|T|_{\mathsf{e}}
is its number of leaves.
All trees in the paper are non-empty, finite, and binary (and thus rooted),
except when we explicitly say otherwise.
We will mainly consider full binary trees, but note that binary trees are
not assumed to be full unless we say so.

It is well-known that a full binary tree has odd size, and that there is a
bijection between general binary trees of size
n\geq 1
and full binary trees
of size
2n+1
defined as follows:
Given a binary tree
T
,
its
extension

{\overline{T}}

is the full binary tree obtained by
adding new leaves
(often called
external nodes
)
at all
possible places, i.e., we add one new leaf to each node of outdegree 1,
and two new leaves to each node of outdegree 0.
Conversely, the inverse map is:
given a full binary tree, delete all its leaves.
For many purposes, full and general binary trees are
thus equivalent, but both types are important, and there are several reasons
for studying properties of both classes of binary trees.

In the present paper we will also
study another relation between general binary trees and
full binary trees.
Given a binary tree
T
, its
compression

{\widehat{T}}
is the full binary
tree obtained by deleting all nodes of outdegree 1 (and connecting the
remaining nodes in the obvious way).
This is obviously not a bijection; there is no way to reconstruct the binary
tree without further information. Note also that the
size of the compressed tree typically is smaller;
we have
1\leq|{\widehat{T}}|\leq|T|
, and every size in this range is possible;
however,
{\widehat{T}}
and
T
have the same number of leaves.

Remark 1.2
.

A
cladogram
is a full binary tree with labelled leaves,
where we do not
care about the orientations, i.e., we do not
distinguish between left and right. (Formally, we may see a cladogram as an
equivalence classe of leaf-labelled full binary trees.)
In particular, the random full binary trees studied here may be regarded as
random cladograms by
labelling the leaves (randomly, say) and
forgetting all orientations.
Conversely, any cladogram may be regarded as a random leaf-labelled
full binary tree by randomly assigning orientations at all internal nodes.
It will be convenient for us to study full binary trees, but
we can thus regard them as random cladograms, and mathematically this is
essentially equivalent. (At least in symmetric cases; for Patricia tries
with
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
, the orientation is important.)
In particular, the results below on fringe trees yield as corollaries
corresponding results for fringe trees of the corresponding random
cladograms
(these fringe trees are themselves random cladograms); we omit the
details. (See also Remark
8.1
.)

1.1.3.
Uniform random full binary trees

The

1.1.4.
Beta-splitting random trees

The general definition of the
beta-splitting random tree
with
n
leaves

[
3
]
is that (if

)
the leaves are split randomly between the left

subtree has
i

\displaystyle c(n;\beta)\frac{\Gamma(\beta+i+1)\Gamma(\beta+n-i+1)}{\Gamma(i+1)\Gamma(n-i+1)},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-1,

(1.1)

for a normalizing constant
c(n;\beta)
;
the construction proceeds then recursively in each subtree.
It can be checked that the cases
\beta=-3/2,0,\infty
(the latter
interpreted in a limiting sense) yield the random full binary trees

{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
,
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n-1}
,
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
(with
p=\frac{1}{2}
) above

[
3
, Section 4.1]
.
We consider here also
the case
\beta=-1
, known as the
critical beta-splitting random tree
,
which we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{n}

(it is denoted
\mathrm{DTCS}(n)
in
[
4
;
5
]
);
it is thus defined with the splitting probabilities
(
1.1
) being

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2h_{n-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{i(n-i)}=\frac{1}{2h_{n-1}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{n-i}\Bigr),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-1,

(1.2)

where we use the harmonic numbers

\displaystyle h_{n}:=\sum_{1}^{n}\frac{1}{i}.

(1.3)

1.2.
Fringe trees

In this paper we focus on fringe trees of the various random trees.
For a rooted tree
T
and a node
v\in T
,
the
fringe tree

T^{v}

is the subtree of
T
consisting of
v
and all its descendants;
the fringe tree
T^{v}
is
a rooted tree with root
v
, and if
T
is a binary tree or a full
binary tree, then so is each of its fringe trees.
If
T
and
t
are two binary trees,
let
N_{t}(T)
be the number of fringe trees of
T
that are equal to

t
(in the sense of isomorphic as binary trees), i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{t}(T):=\bigl\lvert\{v\in T:T^{v}=t\}\bigr\rvert.

(1.4)

Here
t
will always be a fixed tree (think of it as small),
while
T
usually will be a (big) random tree; then
N_{t}(T)
is a
random variable.
We consider also the random fringe tree
T^{*}
defined as
T^{v}
for a
node
v\in T
chosen uniformly at random.
(If
T
is random, we first condition on
T
and then choose a node
v

in it.)
Thus, for every fixed binary tree
t
and a
non-random binary tree
T
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=t)=\frac{N_{t}(T)}{|T|}.

(1.5)

For a random binary tree
T
, (
1.5
) holds conditionally on
T
,
and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=t)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\frac{N_{t}(T)}{|T|}.

(1.6)

See
[
1
]
for a general study of fringe tree distributions,
including explicit results for several classes of random trees.

In this paper we discuss results for the random fringe tree
T^{*}_{n}
of a
sequence of random trees
T_{n}
.
We state results both conditioned on the tree
T_{n}

and unconditioned, and
we use the standard terminology and call
such results
quenched
and
annealed
, repectively.

Remark 1.3
.

We may also care only about the leaf size
of the fringe trees, and define, for
m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle N_{m}(T):=\bigl\lvert\{v\in T:|T^{v}|_{\mathsf{e}}=m\}\bigr\rvert=\sum_{t:|t|_{\mathsf{e}}=m}N_{t}(T).

(1.7)

Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
denote one of the random trees
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n},\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n-1},\mathcal{D}_{n}

studied here (which all have
n
leaves).
Then
the recursive construction of
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
implies that conditioned on some fringe
tree
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}^{v}
having
m
leaves, that fringe tree has the same distribution as
the random tree
{\mathcal{X}}_{m}
of the same type with
m
leaves.
The same holds evidently also for
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}={\mathcal{U}}_{n}
.
In particular, we have for any full binary tree
t
with
m
leaves

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[N_{t}({\mathcal{X}}_{n})]=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T_{m}=t)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[N_{m}({\mathcal{X}}_{n})].

(1.8)

2.1.
Notation

(See also Section
1.1
; we allow some repetitions below for clarity.)
For a rooted tree
T
, the set of leaves of
T
is denoted
{\mathcal{L}}(T)
.
The leaves are also called

and the other nodes, i.e.,

>0

.
The number of nodes of
T
is denoted by
|T|

(the
size
of

),
the number of leaves
(external nodes) by

|T|_{\mathsf{e}}:=|{\mathcal{L}}(T)|

(the

of
T
),
and the number of internal nodes by

|T|_{\mathsf{i}}:=|T|-|T|_{\mathsf{e}}
.
Recall that in a binary tree
T
,
the number of nodes of outdegree 2 is
|T|_{\mathsf{e}}-1
, and thus
the number of nodes of outdegree 1 is
|T|-2|T|_{\mathsf{e}}+1
.
Hence, if
T
is a full binary tree, then

|T|_{\mathsf{i}}=|T|_{\mathsf{e}}-1
and

\displaystyle|T|=2|T|_{\mathsf{e}}-1.

(2.1)

The
root degree

\rho(T)
is the (out)degree of the root
o\in T
.

We let
\bullet
denote the tree consisting of a root only, so

|\bullet|=|\bullet|_{\mathsf{e}}=1
.

Let

{\mathfrak{T}}
be the set of all binary trees, and

{\widehat{\mathfrak{T}}}
the subset of all full binary trees, and

\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}
the set of all binary trees such that no leaf has a parent of
outdegree 1;
thus
{\widehat{\mathfrak{T}}}\subset\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}\subset{\mathfrak{T}}
.
Furthermore, for any set
S\subseteq\{0,1,2\}
, let

\mathfrak{T}^{S}:=\{T\in{\mathfrak{T}}:\rho(T)\in S\}

be the set of all binary trees with root degree in
S
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{T}^{\{0\}}=\{\bullet\}
.

If
t
is a full binary tree, let

\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
be the set of all binary trees that can be obtained from
t
by
subdividing the edges, i.e., by
replacing every edge by a path of
\ell\geq 1
edges;
each such path thus contains
\ell-1
new nodes of outdegree 1.
Note
that each new node has its (only) child as either a left or a right child.
Let further
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}

be the set of all binary trees that compress to
t
;
note that this is larger than
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
since

\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}
allows also adding a path from the root to a new
root, but
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
does not; in fact,
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}=\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{T}^{\{0,2\}}
.
(If
t\neq\bullet
, then
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}=\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{T}^{\{2\}}
.)

If
v
is a node in a binary tree
T
, then the
left depth

d_{\mathsf{L}}(v)

is the number of edges
that go from some node to its left child
in the path from the root to
v
.
The
right depth

d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)
is defined similarly.
Note that
d_{\mathsf{L}}(v)+d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)=d(v)
, the depth of
v
.
When necessary, we write
d_{T}(v)
for the depth in
T
.

We define the
left external path length

\mathsf{LPL}(T)

and
right external path length

\mathsf{RPL}(T)
by

\displaystyle\mathsf{LPL}(T)=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{L}}(T)}d_{\mathsf{L}}(v),\qquad\mathsf{RPL}(T)=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{L}}(T)}d_{\mathsf{R}}(v).

(2.2)

Thus the sum
\mathsf{LPL}(T)+\mathsf{RPL}(T)
equals the total external path length

\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{L}}(T)}d(v)
.

The fringe tree
T^{v}
and the fringe tree counts
N_{t}(T)

are defined in the introduction.

We use
\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}
and
\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}
to denote convergence in

We further say that
X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}Y

if
X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}Y

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[X_{n}^{r}]\to\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[Y^{r}]

.
We let
o_{\mathrm{p}}(1)
denote any sequence of random variables

such that

X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0
.

\mathfrak{L}(X)
denotes the distribution of a random variable
X
.

N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance

\sigma^{2}\geq 0
.

\operatorname{Po}(\lambda)
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda\geq 0
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Po}(\lambda;n):=\operatorname{Po}(\lambda)(n)=\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}e^{-\lambda},\qquad n=0,1,\dots

(2.3)

We may sometimes abbreviate “uniformly random” to “random”.

\log
denotes natural logarithms.
Unspecified limits are as
{n\to\infty}
.

2.2.
Tries

A
trie

(see e.g.
[
34
, Section 6.3]
and
[
14
, Section 1.4.4]
)
is a rooted tree constructed from a set of
n\geq 1
distinct
strings
\Xi^{(1)},\Xi^{(2)},\dots,\allowbreak\Xi^{(n)}

in some alphabet
\mathcal{A}
;
we consider here only the case
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
, and then
the trie will be a binary tree.
(Fringe trees for tries defined by arbitrary finite alphabets are treated by

Ischebeck [28]
.)
We assume for convenience that the strings are infinite;
thus
\Xi^{(i)}=\xi^{(i)}_{1}\xi^{(i)}_{2}\dotsm\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
for every
i
.
The trie is constructed recursively.
If
n=0
, then the trie is the empty tree
\emptyset
.
Otherwise,
we begin with a root, and put every string in the root.
If
n=1
, then we stop there, so the trie equals
\bullet
.
Otherwise, i.e., if
n\geq 2
, we pass all strings
to new nodes;
all strings beginning with 0 (if any) are passed to a left child of the root
and
all strings beginning with 1 (if any) are passed to a right child of the root.
We continue recursively, the next time partitioning the strings according to
the second letter, and so on, always looking at the first letter not yet
inspected.
At the end there is a tree with
n
leaves, each containing one string.
Equivalently, each string
\Xi^{(i)}
defines an infinite path
\Gamma_{i}

from the root
in the infinite binary tree, by processing the letters in the string in
order and going to a left child for every 0 and a right child for every 1.
We then stop each path at first node that it does not share with any other
of the paths
\Gamma_{j}
; these nodes are the leaves of the trie, and the
trie is the union of the
n
stopped paths.
Note that in a trie, a parent of a leaf must have outdegree 2, i.e., a trie
belongs to the set
\mathfrak{T}^{\prime}
.

We will consider the random trie
\Upsilon_{n}
defined by
n
random strings

\Xi^{(1)},\dots,\Xi^{(n)}
where we assume that the strings are
independent, and furthermore in each string the letters are independent
and identically distributed with distribution
\operatorname{Be}(p)
for some
p\in(0,1)
,
i.e., each letter
\xi^{(i)}_{k}
has
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\xi^{(i)}_{k}=1)=p
and

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\xi^{(i)}_{k}=0)=q:=1-p
.
We omit the parameter
p
from the notation, but it is implicit when we
discuss tries; we use always the notations
p
and
q=1-p
in the sense above.

It is well-known that many results for tries show (typically small) periodic
oscillations instead of limits as
{n\to\infty}
,
see e.g.

[
34
;
36
;
17
;
20
;
30
;
21
;
29
;
33
]
.
More precisely, if
\log p/\log q
is irrational, then such oscillations do
not occur, but if
\log p/\log q
is rational they typically do.
We call the case when
\log p/\log q\in\mathbb{Q}

periodic
;
otherwise we have the
aperiodic
case.
In the periodic case, if
\log p/\log q
equals
a/b
in lowest terms
(with
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
), then define

\displaystyle d=d_{p}:=\frac{-\log p}{a}=\frac{-\log q}{b}>0,

(2.4)

the greatest
common divisor of
-\log p
and
-\log q
.

The Patricia trie
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
is obtained by compressing
\Upsilon_{n}
.
Note that the trie
\Upsilon_{n}
and the Patricia tree
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
both have exactly

n
leaves:

\displaystyle|\Upsilon_{n}|_{\mathsf{e}}=|\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}|_{\mathsf{e}}=n.

(2.5)

The Patricia tree
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
thus has
n-1
internal nodes, while
the number of internal nodes in
\Upsilon_{n}
is random.

2.3.
Binary search trees

Binary search trees may be constructed in different (but equivalent) ways;
we will use the following, closely connected to the sorting algorithm

Quicksort

[

and

[

Consider a set of
n
distinct items,
which we may assume are real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.
If
n=0
, the BST
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is the empty tree
\emptyset
, and if
n=1

\mathcal{B}_{1}:=\bullet
.
If

2.4.
Fringe trees of compressed trees

Let

{\widehat{T}}
its compression.
Note first that the leaves of

T
,
while the roots may differ. (In general, there may in
T

be a path of nodes of outdegree 1 from the root of
T
to the root of

{\widehat{T}}
.) Thus,
|{\widehat{T}}|_{\mathsf{e}}=|T|_{\mathsf{e}}
.

Let

be a full binary tree and consider the nodes
v\in{\widehat{T}}
such that

{\widehat{T}}^{v}=t
. We consider two cases separately:

(i)

If
t=\bullet
, then:

v\in{\widehat{T}}
and
{\widehat{T}}^{v}=t

\iff

is a leaf in
{\widehat{T}}

\iff

v
is a leaf in
T

\iff

v\in T
and
T^{v}=t
.

(ii)

If
|t|>1
, then
the root of
t
has outdegree 2, and:

v\in{\widehat{T}}
and
{\widehat{T}}^{v}=t

\iff

v
has outdegree 2 in
T
and

is the contraction of
T^{v}
.

Consequently, in both cases,

\displaystyle v\in{\widehat{T}}\text{ and }{\widehat{T}}^{v}=t\iff v\in T\text{ and }T^{v}\in\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t},

(2.9)

where we recall that

\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
is the set of all binary trees that can be obtained from

by
subdividing the edges.

Define the functional
\varphi
on binary trees by

\displaystyle\varphi(T):=\boldsymbol{1}\{T\in\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}\},

(2.10)

and define the corresponding additive functional

\displaystyle\Phi(T):=\sum_{v\in T}\varphi(T^{v}).

(2.11)

Then, by (

),

\displaystyle N_{t}({\widehat{T}})=\sum_{v\in{\widehat{T}}}\boldsymbol{1}\{{\widehat{T}}^{v}=t\}=\sum_{v\in T}\boldsymbol{1}\{T^{v}\in\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}\}=\sum_{v\in T}\varphi(T^{v})=\Phi(T).

(2.12)

2.5.
Extended fringe trees

Let
T
be a rooted tree.
The fringe tree
T^{v}
defined in the introduction consists of a node
v
and
all its descendants.

Aldous [1]
introduces also the
extended fringe tree
by
also going up from the chosen node
v
to ancestors and then taking
descendants, thus including siblings,
cousins, and so on; we may formally
define the extended fringe tree as the nested sequence
of fringe trees
(T^{v_{i}})_{i=0}^{d(v)}
where
v_{i}
is the
i
th ancestor of
v
.
(See
[
1
, Section 4]
for details.)
It is shown in
[
1
, Proposition 11]
that if the
random fringe trees of some sequence of random trees
T_{n}
converge in
distribution,
then so do the random extended fringe trees.
The limit then is an infinite nested sequence of random trees

(T_{\infty}^{(i)})_{i\geq 0}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Assume that these limits

T_{\infty}^{(i)}

exist and
also the technical condition (satisfied for the random trees
studied here) that the depth of a random node in
T_{n}
tends to infinity
in probability.
Then the random limit sequence

(T_{\infty}^{(i)})_{i\geq 0}

can be combined to a random infinite tree,
called
sin-tree
,
with a single infinite path from
o
consisting of
the roots of the trees
T_{\infty}^{(i)}
, see
[
1
]
.
The results below can be translated to this, more intuitive, description of
the limit of the extended fringe tree.

\triangle

We consider in the present paper, as in
[
4
;
5
]
,
the extended fringe tree

{T}^{**}=({T}^{**(i)})_{i}

of a uniformly random
leaf
in the tree
T

(instead of a random node as for
T^{*}
);
this also converges in
distribution when the random fringe tree does, since it can be regarded as
the random extended fringe tree conditioned on the fringe tree

T^{*}={T}^{**(0)}

being
\bullet
.

Remark 2.2
.

{T}^{**(i)}
is undefined for
i
greater than the depth of the chosen random
leaf; this is no problem for us since for the random trees studied here,
for every fixed
i
, the probability that the depth of a random leaf is
at most
i

tends to 0; hence
{T}^{**(i)}
is defined with probability tending to 1, which
is all we need.
Cf. Remark
2.1
.

\triangle

It is easy to see that
if
T
is any (deterministic or random) tree,

(t,\ell)
is a pair of a tree
t
and a marked leaf
\ell\in t
,

i\geq 0
,
and
o
is the root of
{T}^{**}
(defined as the root of
{T}^{**(0)}
), then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(({T}^{**(i)},o)=(t,\ell)\bigr)=q(T;t,\ell)\boldsymbol{1}\{i=d_{t}(\ell)\},

(2.13)

where, letting
L
be a uniformly random leaf in
T
,

\displaystyle q(T;t,\ell):=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\exists w\in T:L\in T^{w}\text{ and }(T^{w},L)\text{ is isomorphic to }(t,\ell)\bigr).

(2.14)

We define also the simpler

\displaystyle q(T;t):=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\text{a random leaf $v$ lies in some fringe tree
$T^{w}$ isomorphic to $t$}\bigr).

(2.15)

Note that, if
|T|>1
, any leaf lies in several fringe trees of different
sizes; hence
\sum_{t}q(T;t)>1

so
q(T;\cdot)
is not a probability distribution on trees.
In fact, trivially
q(T;\bullet)=1
for every tree
T
.

For a random tree
T
we define also
q(T;t,\ell\mid T)

and
q(T;t\mid T)

by (
2.14
) and (
2.15
)
conditioned on
T
.
Thus
q(T;t\mid T)
is a functional of
T
, and thus a random variable,
while
q(T;t)
is a number depending on the distribution of
T
only,
and similarly for
q(T;t,\ell)
.
We have, as always for conditional expectations,

\displaystyle q(T;t,\ell)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[q(T;t,\ell\mid T)],\qquad q(T;t)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[q(T;t\mid T)].

(2.16)

Let
t
be a fixed tree with a marked leaf
\ell
, and let
T
be a
(deterministic or random) tree.
For every fringe tree
T^{w}
that is isomorphic to
t
,
there is exactly one leaf
L
in
T
such that
L\in T^{w}
and

(T^{w},L)=(t,\ell)
.
Since all copies of
t
in
T
are disjoint, these leaves
L
are distinct
for different fringe trees
T^{w}=t
, and thus (for a random tree
T
)

\displaystyle q(T;t,\ell\mid T)=\frac{N_{t}(T)}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}

(2.17)

and hence

\displaystyle q(T;t,\ell)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\frac{N_{t}(T)}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}.

(2.18)

Consequently, recalling (
2.13
), the distribution of the random
extended fringe tree
{T}^{**}
is determined by the fringe subtree counts

N_{t}(T)
, and conversely.
More precisely, the quenched (i.e., conditional) distribution of
{T}^{**}
is
determined by the (random) counts
N_{t}(T)
, and the annealed distribution
by their expectations.)
Furthermore, again since the copies of
t
in
T
are disjoint, the
number of leaves that lie in some copy of
t
equals
N_{t}(T)|t|_{\mathsf{e}}
,
and hence,
for any (random) tree
T
,

\displaystyle q(T;t\mid T)=\frac{N_{t}(T)|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}

(2.19)

and

\displaystyle q(T;t)=|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\frac{N_{t}(T)}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}.

(2.20)

Consequently, for every leaf
\ell\in t
,

\displaystyle q(T;t,\ell\mid T)=\frac{1}{|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}q(T;t\mid T),\qquad q(T;t,\ell)=\frac{1}{|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}q(T,t).

(2.21)

(This follows also directly from (
2.14
)–(
2.15
), noting that
by symmetry
q(T;t,\ell)
does not depend on the leaf
\ell\in T
.)
Hence, it suffices to study the simpler
q(T;t\mid T)
and
q(T;t)

without marked leaves.

For a sequence of (deterministic or random) trees
T_{n}
,
it follows from (
2.13
) and (
2.21
) that
the extended fringe trees
{T}^{**}_{n}=({T}^{**(i)}_{n})_{i}
converge in distribution, to
some sequence of (random) trees
{T}^{**}_{\infty}=({T}^{**(i)}_{\infty})_{i\geq 0}
,
if and only if
q(T_{n};t)
converges for every fixed tree
t
,
and in this case the limit distribution is determined by the limits

\displaystyle q({T}^{**}_{\infty};t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}q(T_{n};t).

(2.22)

More precisely, if these limits exist, then
it follows from (
2.13
) and (
2.21
) that
if
o
is the root of
{T}^{**}_{\infty}
(i.e., the root of
{T}^{**(0)}_{\infty}
),
then
for every tree
t
and leaf
\ell\in t
with
d_{t}(\ell)=i
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(({T}^{**(i)}_{\infty},o)=(t,\ell)\bigr)=\frac{1}{|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}\lim_{n\to\infty}q(T_{n};t)=\frac{1}{|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}q({T}^{**}_{\infty};t).

(2.23)

Note also that for any (deterministic) tree
T
, (
2.19
) and (
1.5
)
yield

\displaystyle q(T;t)=\frac{|t|_{\mathsf{e}}|T|\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=t)}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}=|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\frac{|T|}{|T|_{\mathsf{e}}}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=t)=|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=t)}{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}=\bullet)}.

(2.24)

This leads to the following version of the result by

Aldous [1, Proposition 11]
that,
as said above,
if a sequence of random fringe trees converge in distribution,
then so do the extended fringe trees.
(Note that we here, unlike
[
1
]
, consider extended fringe
trees rooted at a random leaf; the fringe trees
T^{*}_{n}
are (of course)
rooted at a random node.)
We also obtain a formula for the limits (
2.22
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence of random trees such that, as
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{n}=t\mid T_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=t)

(2.25)

for every fixed tree
t
and
some random tree
T^{*}_{\infty}

(a quenched limiting fringe tree).
Then

\displaystyle q(T_{n};t\mid T_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}q({T}^{**}_{\infty};t)=\kappa|t|_{\mathsf{e}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=t)},

(2.26)

where (with the limit in probability, in general)

\displaystyle\kappa:=\frac{1}{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=\bullet)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|T_{n}|}{|T_{n}|_{\mathsf{e}}}.

(2.27)

In other words, the (quenched) distribution of the extended fringe tree
converges in probability:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}({T}^{**}_{n}\mid T_{n})=\mathfrak{L}(({T}^{**(i)}_{n})_{i\geq 0}\mid T_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\mathfrak{L}({T}^{**}_{\infty})=\mathfrak{L}(({T}^{**(i)}_{\infty})_{i\geq 0}),

(2.28)

where the distribution of the limiting extended fringe tree
{T}^{**}_{\infty}
is
given by
q({T}^{**}_{\infty};t)
in (
2.26
).

Conversely, if the extended fringe tree distribution converges
in the sense (
2.28
), and the limit
\kappa
in (
2.27
) exists,
then the fringe tree distribution converges in the sense (
2.25
),
and (
2.26
) holds.

For full binary trees with
|T_{n}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\infty
,
we always have (
2.27
) with
\kappa=2
.

Proof.

We have by (
2.24
) and the assumption (
2.25
)

\displaystyle q(T_{n};t\mid T_{n})=|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{n}=t\mid T_{n})}{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{n}=\bullet\mid T_{n})}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=t)}{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=\bullet)},

(2.29)

which can be written as (
2.26
)–(
2.27
),
since (
2.25
) as a special case also yields

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{n}|_{\mathsf{e}}}{|T_{n}|}=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{n}=\bullet\mid T_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(T^{*}_{\infty}=\bullet).

(2.30)

Furthermore, (
2.26
) is equivalent to (
2.28
)
by the discussion before (
2.22
).

The converse follows in the same way.

For full binary trees with
|T_{n}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\infty
,
(
2.1
) yields
\kappa=2
.
∎

Results for extended fringe trees thus follow rather trivially from results

3.
Patricia tries

Recall that the random trie
\Upsilon_{n}

p\in(0,1)

is omitted from the notation, see Section

We fix a full binary tree
t
and consider

Let

t
.
The case

As shown by

We first introduce some notation.

\displaystyle\pi_{t}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\Upsilon_{m}\in\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}).

Theorem 3.1

(Partly

)
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})}}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1),

(3.9)

with convergence of all moments.
We have
\operatorname{Var}N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})=\Theta(n)
as
{n\to\infty}
, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})

\displaystyle=nH^{-1}\psi_{\mathsf{E}}(\log n)+o(n),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}N_{t}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n})

\displaystyle=n\bigl(H^{-1}\psi_{\mathsf{V}}(\log n)-H^{-2}\psi_{\mathsf{C}}(\log n)^{2}+o(1)\bigr),

, and thus

The asymtotics (

It remains to find the Mellin transforms

) that

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{E}}(\lambda):=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda}\in\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t})=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(N_{\lambda}=m)\pi_{t}=\operatorname{Po}(\lambda;m)\pi_{t}=\pi_{t}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}e^{-\lambda}.

(3.17)

This, or simpler

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\mathsf{E}}(\lambda)\lambda^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=\frac{\Gamma(m+s)}{m!}\pi_{t},

(3.18)

verifying (

By

(We also obtain

.)

To find the more complicated

, note first that if
\varphi(T)=1
, i.e.,

, then no fringe tree

except

(where

. Hence, if

, then

, and consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda}),\Phi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Phi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda}).

(3.19)

Similarly,

,
and thus (
3.3
) yields, using (

),

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{V}}(\lambda)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})-2\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Phi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})+(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda}))^{2}.

(3.20)

Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}:=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{n}
be the set of finite strings of
\{0,1\}
.
If

as the probability that the random

where

is the length of

We may regard the strings

as the nodes in the infinite binary

\varphi(\bullet)=0
,
it follows (see
[
33
, (2.25)]
) that,
using also (
3.17
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Phi(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\varphi\bigl(\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{P(\alpha)\lambda}\bigr)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}f_{\mathsf{E}}\bigl(P(\alpha)\lambda\bigr)=\pi_{t}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\operatorname{Po}\bigl(P(\alpha)\lambda;m\bigr).

(3.22)

Let
\sum_{\alpha}^{\prime}
denote the sum over all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
with

i.e., over all strings

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{V}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\pi_{t}\operatorname{Po}(\lambda;m)-2\pi_{t}^{2}\operatorname{Po}(\lambda;m)\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\operatorname{Po}\bigl(P(\alpha)\lambda;m\bigr)+\pi_{t}^{2}\operatorname{Po}(\lambda;m)^{2}

\displaystyle=\pi_{t}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}e^{-\lambda}-2\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{m!^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}P(\alpha)^{m}\lambda^{2m}e^{-(1+P(\alpha))\lambda}+\pi_{t}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{2m}}{m!^{2}}e^{-2\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi_{t}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}e^{-\lambda}-2\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{m!^{2}}{\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m}\lambda^{2m}e^{-(1+P(\alpha))\lambda}-\pi_{t}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{2m}}{m!^{2}}e^{-2\lambda}.

(3.23)

The Mellin transform is thus, for
\operatorname{Re}s>-m
,
by a simple calculation,

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{V}}^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{t}}{m!}\Gamma(m+s)-2\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{m!^{2}}{\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m}(1+P(\alpha))^{-2m-s}\Gamma(2m+s)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{m!^{2}}2^{-2m-s}\Gamma(2m+s).

By a binomial expansion we have, with absolutely convergent sums, e.g.
by (
3.26
) below,

\displaystyle{\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m}(1+P(\alpha))^{-2m-s}

\displaystyle={\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{\Gamma(2m+s+k)}{\Gamma(2m+s)}P(\alpha)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{\Gamma(2m+s+k)}{\Gamma(2m+s)}{\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m+k}.

(3.25)

For any exponent

and

we have from the definition (

Hence, summing over

\displaystyle{\sum_{\alpha}}^{\prime}P(\alpha)^{m}(1+P(\alpha))^{-2m-s}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{\Gamma(2m+s+k)}{\Gamma(2m+s)}\frac{p^{m+k}+q^{m+k}}{1-(p^{m+k}+q^{m+k})}.

Remark 3.9
.

Corollaries
3.6
and
3.7
show that in the periodic case there is
oscillation and no limit distribution, although suitable subsequences
converge in distribution.
It is well-known that for some related functionals for tries, the
oscillations are numerically very small; this is true here too when
m
is
small, but not for large
m
.
Consider the symmetric case
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
; then

d_{p}=\log 2
.
(In other periodic cases,
d_{p}
is smaller and the oscillations are
substantially smaller than in the symmetric case, but they still become
large for large
m
.)
In the Fourier series (
3.7
) for
f_{\mathsf{E}}
, we have
by (
3.12
) the constant term
f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1)=\pi_{t}/m(m-1)
, and
if we normalize by this term,
for the term
k=1
we have

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1-\frac{2\pi}{\log 2}\mathrm{i})}{f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1)}=\frac{\Gamma(m-1-\frac{2\pi}{\log 2}\mathrm{i})}{\Gamma(m-1)}.

(3.42)

For
m=2
, this ratio has absolute value

|\Gamma(1-\frac{2\pi}{\log 2}\mathrm{i})|\doteq 4.9\cdot 10^{-6}
,
and higher Fourier coefficients are much smaller.
Hence, the oscillations are in this case hardly of practical importance.
However, the absolute value of the ratio increases as
m
increases:
for
m=3
it is
\doteq 4.5\cdot 10^{-5}
, for
m=4
it is

\doteq 2.1\cdot 10^{-4}
and for
m=100
it is
\doteq 0.66
;
in fact, the absolute value of the ratio
converges to 1 as
{m\to\infty}
(see

[
37
, 5.11.12]
), and the same holds for every Fourier coefficient.
Hence we cannot always ignore the oscillations.

More precisely, still taking
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
,
the normalized
(\log 2)
-periodic
function
\psi_{0}(x):=\psi_{\mathsf{E}}(x)/f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1)
is non-negative by
(
3.10
) and has by (
3.7
) and (
3.18
) Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{0}(k)=\frac{f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1-k\frac{2\pi}{\log 2}\mathrm{i})}{f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1)}=\frac{\Gamma(m-1-\frac{2\pi k}{\log 2}\mathrm{i})}{\Gamma(m-1)}.

(3.43)

Let
\{x\}:=x-\lfloor x\rfloor

denote the fractional part of a real number
x
, and
suppose that
m\to\infty
along a subsequence such that

\{\lg m\}=\{(\log m)/d\}\to u\in[0,1]
.
(Recall that
d=\log 2
.)
It then follows from (
3.43
) and

[
37
, 5.11.12]
that, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{0}(k)\sim m^{-(2\pi k/\log 2)\mathrm{i}}=e^{-2\pi k(\lg m)\mathrm{i}}\to e^{-2\pi ku\mathrm{i}}=\widehat{\delta_{du}}(k),

(3.44)

where
\delta_{du}
is a point mass at
du
.
Hence, by the continuity theorem,
the function
\psi_{0}(x)
converges weakly
(as a measure on the circle
\mathbb{R}/d\mathbb{Z}
) to
\delta_{du}
,
which roughly means that for large
m
,
\psi_{0}(x)
is concentrated
at
x
close to
du\approx d\{\lg m\}
.
Hence, still roughly,
\psi_{E}(\log n)
in (
3.10
)
is large when
\{\lg n\}=\{\log n/d\}\approx\{\lg m\}
, but small otherwise.
This should not be surprising.
For a large
n
, in the first generations of the construction of the trie
from
n
strings in Section
2.2
, by the law of large numbers
almost exatly half of the strings are passed to the left child and half to
the right child.
Consequently, there will be many fringe trees in
\Upsilon_{n}
, and thus in
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
,
of leaf size
\approx 2^{-j}n
for integers
j
, but few for intermediate sizes,
until we get down to small sizes
m
. Thus, for a fixed large
m
, we expect
many fringe trees of leaf size
m
when
n/m
is close to a power of 2, which is
the same as
\{\lg n\}\approx\{\lg m\}
.

\triangle

4.
Extended binary search trees

In this section we study fringe trees of the extended BST
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
;
in the next section we study the more complicated case of the
compressed BST
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
.

As said in the introduction,
Aldous [1]
shows that the random
fringe trees
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{n}
converge in distribution to some limiting random
fringe tree
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}
as
{n\to\infty}
, and

Devroye [12]
;
Devroye [13]
show asymptotic normality of the subtree
counts
N_{t}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
.
Furthermore,
it is shown in

[
1
]

(and in
[
13
, Theorem 2]
) that
the distribution of the limiting random fringe tree
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}

equals the mixture
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\mathfrak{L}(\mathcal{B}_{k})
,
i.e.,
for any set
{\mathfrak{S}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{T}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}\in{\mathfrak{S}}\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{k}\in{\mathfrak{S}}\bigr).

(4.1)

It is straightforward to use this to obtain the corresponding results for
the extended BST.
For a full binary tree
t
with
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}>1
, and thus
|t|_{\mathsf{i}}>0
,
let
t^{\circ}
denote the subtree ot
t
consisting of the internal
nodes. Note that then
t=\overline{t^{\circ}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The extended BST has a limiting random fringe tree
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}

with distribution given by,
for every full binary tree
t
with
|t|_{\mathsf{i}}=k>0
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}=t\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}=t^{\circ})=\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k}=t^{\circ}),

(4.2)

and (for the trivial case
|t|_{\mathsf{i}}=0
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}=\bullet\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}.

(4.3)

Moreover,
for
t=\bullet
we trivially have
N_{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})=n+1
,
and
for any full binary tree
t
with
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}>1
,

N_{t}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})
is asymptotically normal:
there exist constants
\bar{\beta}_{t}>0
and
\bar{\gamma}_{t}>0
such that, as
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})-n\bar{\beta}_{t}}{\sqrt{n}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\bar{\gamma}^{2}_{t}),

(4.4)

with convergence of mean and variance,
where

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}_{t}=2\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}=t\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}=t^{\circ}\bigr)=\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k}=t^{\circ}).

(4.5)

In particular, for every full binary tree
t
(with
\bar{\beta}_{\bullet}:=1
),

\displaystyle N_{t}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})/n\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\bar{\beta}_{t}.

(4.6)

Consequently, the fringe tree distribution converges to the limit
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}

both in quenched and annealed sense: For every full binary tree
t
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{n}=t\mid\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{t}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})}{|\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}|}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}=t),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{n}=t)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\frac{N_{t}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n})}{|\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}|}\to\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\infty}=t).

(4.8)

Proof.

First,
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
has
n
internal nodes and
n+1
leaves,

5.1.

We define a generating function for binary trees and sets of binary trees as
follows.
For a binary tree
T
, let

\displaystyle p_{T}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{|T|}=T),

(5.19)

\displaystyle F_{T}(x)

\displaystyle:=p_{T}x^{|T|}.

(5.20)

For any set
{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}
of binary trees, let

\displaystyle F_{{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}}(x):=\sum_{T\in{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}}F_{T}(x).

(5.21)

These generating functions will help us to compute fringe tree probabilities
by the following simple formula for the BST.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}
is a set of binary trees, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}\in{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}})=2\int_{0}^{1}F_{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}(x)(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.22)

Proof.

By (
5.21
) and linearity, it suffices to consider the case when

{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}=\{T\}
for a single binary tree
T
.
Let
k:=|T|
.
Then, by (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{\infty}=T)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k}=T)=2p_{T}\int_{0}^{1}x^{k}(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}F_{T}(x)(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(5.23)

which shows (
5.22
).
∎

Let
t
be a full binary tree, and recall that
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}
is the set of all binary
trees that contract to
t
, and
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
the subset of all such
trees where the root has degree 2 or 0.
In other words,
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}
is the set of all
binary trees that can be obtained from

by replacing any edge by a

and
\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}
is the set of all binary trees
that can be obtained from these
by adding a path of length
\ell\geq 0

Define the corresponding generating functions

\displaystyle G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=F_{\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}^{+}}(x),

(5.24)

\displaystyle H_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=F_{\widecheck{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{t}}(x).

(5.25)

We state a series of lemmas to help us compute these generating functions.

Lemma 5.7
.

If
T
is a path with
l\geq 1
nodes, then

\displaystyle F_{T}(x)=\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}.

(5.26)

Proof.

By the construction of the BST,

\displaystyle p_{T}=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{\ell}=T)=\frac{1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell-1}\dotsm\frac{1}{1}=\frac{1}{\ell!},

(5.27)

since a given path
T
is obtained by exactly one choice of pivot each time.
Hence, (
5.26
) follows by the definition (
5.20
).
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

If
{{\mathfrak{T}}_{P}}
is the set consisting of all paths with any number
\ell\geq 1

of nodes, then

\displaystyle F_{{\mathfrak{T}}_{P}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e^{2x}-1\bigr).

(5.28)

Proof.

There are
2^{\ell-1}
different paths with
\ell
nodes.
Thus Lemma
5.7
yields,
letting
P_{\ell}
denote any path with

\displaystyle F_{{\mathfrak{T}}_{P}}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}2^{\ell-1}F_{P_{\ell}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2x)^{\ell}}{\ell!}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(e^{2x}-1\bigr).

(5.29)

∎

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
T
be a binary tree and let
T_{1}
be a tree obtained by adding a path
with
\ell\geq 1
nodes to the root of
T
. Then

\displaystyle F_{T_{1}}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}F_{T}(y)\frac{(x-y)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

Let
|T|=k

|T_{1}|=k+\ell
.
In analogy with (
5.27

\displaystyle p_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k+\ell}=T_{1})=\frac{1}{k+\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{k+\ell-1}\dotsm\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k}=T)

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{(k+\ell)!}p_{T},

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}F_{T}(y)(x-y)^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=p_{T}\int_{0}^{x}y^{k}(x-y)^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{d}y=p_{T}x^{k+\ell}\int_{0}^{1}z^{k}(1-z)^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=p_{T}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(\ell)}{\Gamma(k+1+\ell)}x^{k+\ell}=p_{T}\frac{k!\,(\ell-1)!}{(k+\ell)!}x^{k+\ell}.

By (

{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}

\displaystyle F_{{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}}(x)=\sum_{T\in{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}2^{\ell}\int_{0}^{x}F_{T}(y)\frac{(x-y)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}y=2\int_{0}^{x}F_{{{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}}}(y)e^{2(x-y)}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(5.34)

∎

Lemma 5.11

Let

\displaystyle p_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k_{\mathsf{L}}+k_{\mathsf{R}}+\ell}=T_{1})=\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}+k_{\mathsf{R}}+\ell}\dotsm\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}+k_{\mathsf{R}}+1}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k_{\mathsf{L}}}=T_{\mathsf{L}})\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{B}_{k_{\mathsf{R}}}=T_{\mathsf{R}})

since if the first

Lemma 5.12

Proof.

Proof.

Next, (

Remark 6.2
.

By a summation by parts.

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{h_{m-1}}{m(m-1)}=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}h_{m-1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{m-1}-\frac{1}{m}\Bigr)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(h_{m}-h_{m-1})\frac{1}{m}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

Consequently, (

) really defines a probability distribution on the

t
.

\triangle

We have no explicit formula for the probabilities

in

The limit (

We show that the corresponding quenched result holds too.

Theorem 6.3
.

For any full binary tree
t
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{D}^{*}_{n}=t\mid\mathcal{D}^{*}_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\infty}=t)

(6.6)

given in (

).
Hence,

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{D}_{n};t\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}q({\mathcal{D}}^{**}_{\infty};t)

(6.7)

given in (
6.4
).
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})}{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}q({\mathcal{D}}^{**}_{\infty};t).

(6.8)

Proof.

Fix
t
. We may assume
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}\geq 2
, since the results are trivial
otherwise. Let

\displaystyle\mu(n):=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})].

(6.9)

Theorem
6.1
shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\to\mu:=q({\mathcal{D}}^{**}_{\infty};t).

(6.10)

Fix also a sequence of constants
b_{n}\to\infty
such that

b_{n}=o(n)
. Consider only
n
such that
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}<b_{n}<n
.

We use the terminology of
[
4
;
5
]

and call the sets of leaves used in the recursive construction in
Section
1.1.4

clades
.
Thus, every node
v\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
corresponds to a clade, which equals the set
of leaves in the fringe tree
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{v}
.
The construction starts at the root with the clade
[n]
,
which is recursively split (randomly) into subclades.

We now construct the tree
\mathcal{D}_{n}
by recursive splits
of the clade
[n]
as
in Section
1.1.4
, but stop at each node where the corresponding clade
has size
<b_{n}
. Put a mark at these nodes, and then continue the
construction of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.
This gives a set
v_{1},\dots,v_{\nu}
of marked nodes (with
\nu
random),
where each marked node
v_{i}
corresponds to a clade
Z_{i}
;

Z_{i}
is just the set of leaves in the fringe tree
D_{n}^{v_{i}}
rooted at
v_{i}
.
By construction,
\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\nu}\}
is a partition of
[n]
,
with
|Z_{i}|<b_{n}
for every
i
.

Conditioned on
\nu
and the sizes
\zeta_{i}:=|Z_{i}|
of the clades
Z_{i}
,
the fringe trees
T_{i}:=\mathcal{D}_{n}^{v_{i}}
are independent with

T_{i}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\mathcal{D}_{\zeta_{i}}
. Since we assume
b_{n}>|t|_{\mathsf{e}}
, every fringe tree
in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
that is a copy of
t
is a fringe tree of one of the
T_{i}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}N_{t}(T_{i}).

(6.11)

It follows that, conditioned on
\nu
and the clade sizes

\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{\nu}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\mid\nu,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{\nu}]=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[N_{t}(T_{i})\mid\zeta_{i}]=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mu(\zeta_{i}).

(6.12)

Similarly, for the conditional variance,
using the trivial estimate
\operatorname{Var}[N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{m})]\leq m^{2}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Bigl[\Bigl(N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mu(\zeta_{i})\Bigr)^{2}\mid\nu,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{\nu}\Bigr]=\operatorname{Var}[N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\mid\nu,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{\nu}]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\operatorname{Var}[N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{\zeta_{i}})\mid\zeta_{i}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\zeta_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}b_{n}\zeta_{i}=b_{n}n=o(n^{2}).

(6.13)

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mu(\zeta_{i})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(6.14)

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By (
6.10
) there exists
M=M_{\varepsilon}
such that if
n\geq M
, then

|\mu(n)-n\mu|<n\varepsilon
. Hence,
noting that
0\leq N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{m})\leq m
for
m\geq 1
,
and thus
\mu(m)\leq m
and
\mu\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mu(\zeta_{i})-n\mu\right\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\bigl(\mu(\zeta_{i})-\zeta_{i}\mu\bigr)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\zeta_{i}\geq M}\zeta_{i}\varepsilon+\sum_{\zeta_{i}<M}\zeta_{i}\leq n\varepsilon+\sum_{\zeta_{i}<M}M.

(6.15)

With the notation in Lemma
6.4
below, we have

\sum_{\zeta_{i}<M}M=M\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}X_{j}

and thus
Lemma
6.4
implies that with high probability
(i.e., with probability
1-o(1)
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta_{i}<M}M\leq\varepsilon n.

(6.16)

It follows from (
6.15
) and (
6.16
), since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mu(\zeta_{i})-n\mu\right\rvert\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0,

(6.17)

which together with (
6.14
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n})}{n}-\mu\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(6.18)

This is, by (
6.10
), the same as (
6.8
).

Next, (
6.8
), (
1.6
), and (
6.3
) yield (
6.6
).
Finally, (
6.7
) follows by Lemma
2.3

or directly from (
2.24
), using (
6.3
)–(
6.4
).
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let, as in the proof of Theorem
6.3
,
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{\nu}
be the sizes
of the clades obtained by stopping the construction of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
at
clades of size less than
b_{n}
, for a given sequence
b_{n}\to\infty
.
For
j\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle Y_{j}:=\bigl\lvert\{i:\zeta_{i}=j\}\bigr\rvert,

(6.19)

the number of these clades that have size
j
.
Then, as
{n\to\infty}
, for each fixed
j
,

\displaystyle Y_{j}/n\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(6.20)

Proof.

We continue to use the notation of the proof of Theorem
6.3
.
We assume, as we may,
j<b_{n}<n
.

Let
L^{*}
be a uniformly random leaf in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.
Let
\pi_{j}
be the probability that
L^{*}
belongs to
one of the marked clades of size
j
.
The total number of leaves in these clades is
jY_{j}
, and thus

\displaystyle\pi_{j}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\frac{jY_{j}}{n}=j\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}Y_{j}}{n}.

(6.21)

Let
X_{0}=n,X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be the decreasing sequence of sizes of the
clades that contain
L^{*}
. This is a Markov chain, stopped
when it reaches 1; it is called the
harmonic descent (HD)
chain

[
4
;
5
;
6
;
27
]
,
and has the transition
probabilities, as a simple consequence of (
1.2
),

\displaystyle p(i,j)=\frac{1}{h_{i-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{i-j},\qquad i>j\geq 1.

(6.22)

Let

a(n,i):=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\text{the chain started at state $n$ is ever in state $i$}\bigr).

(6.23)

Since the chosen leaf
L^{*}
belongs to a marked clade if and only if the HD
chain makes a jump from some
X_{i}\geq b_{n}
to
j
(and this can happen at
most once), we have

\displaystyle\pi_{j}=\sum_{k=b_{n}}^{n}a(n,k)p(k,j)=\sum_{k=b_{n}}^{n}a(n,k)\frac{1}{h_{k-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{k-j}.

(6.24)

A lot is known about the occupancy measure
a(n,k)
, see again

[
4
;
5
;
6
;
27
]
,
but here we only need the trivial observation that the right-hand side of (
6.24
)
is increasing in
j<b_{n}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\pi_{j}\leq\pi_{\ell},\qquad 1\leq j\leq\ell<b_{n}.

(6.25)

Since every leaf belongs to exactly one marked clade, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{b_{n}-1}\pi_{j}=1
, and thus (
6.25
) implies

\displaystyle\pi_{j}\leq\frac{1}{b_{n}-j},\qquad 1\leq j<b_{n}.

(6.26)

Consequently, for every fixed
j
,
\pi_{j}\to 0
as
{n\to\infty}
,
and thus by (
6.21
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[Y_{j}/n]\to 0,

(6.27)

which implies (
6.20
).
∎

For comparison, we give also the corresponding values for
the uniform random full binary tree

with
n

Note that this random tree is quite different from the other trees studied

for example, as is well-known, typically

has height of order

, while
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{n}
,
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
have heights of order

, and

have height of order
\log^{2}n

[
7
]
,
[
4
, Section 3.13]
.

The random full binary tree

can be regarded as a conditioned

with critical offspring distribution
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\xi=0)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\xi=2)=\frac{1}{2}
,
see e.g.
[
2
]
,
and thus it follows by
Aldous [1, Lemma 9]

(see also
[
32
]
)
that the asymptotic
fringe tree distribution is the corresponding (unconditioned)

this case simply means that for every full binary tree
t
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}({\mathcal{U}}^{*}_{n}=t)\to 2^{-|t|}=2^{1-2|t|_{\mathsf{e}}}.

(7.1)

We have also the quenched version
and asymptotic normality of the fringe tree counts:

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
t
be a full binary tree with
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}=m\geq 1
.
Then, as
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}({\mathcal{U}}^{*}_{n}=t\mid{\mathcal{U}}_{n})=\frac{N_{t}({\mathcal{U}}_{n})}{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}2^{-|t|}=2^{1-2m}.

(7.2)

and consequently

\displaystyle q({\mathcal{U}}_{n};t\mid{\mathcal{U}}_{n})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\longrightarrow}|t|_{\mathsf{e}}2^{1-|t|}=m2^{2-2m}

(7.3)

Furthermore, if
|t|_{\mathsf{e}}=m>1
, then

N_{t}({\mathcal{U}}_{n})
is asymptotically normal as
{n\to\infty}
:

\displaystyle\frac{N_{t}({\mathcal{U}}_{n})-n2^{-|t|}}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\gamma^{2}_{U}),

(7.4)

with convergence of mean and variance,
where

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}_{U}=2^{1-2m}-(2m-1)2^{3-4m}>0.

(7.5)

Proof.

The case
m=1
is trivial, with
N_{\bullet}({\mathcal{U}}_{n})=n
and
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|=2n-1
.

For
m>1
,
we have (
7.2
) by (

) and
[

, Theorem 7.12]
,
and then (
7.3
) follows by Lemma
2.3
.
The asymptotic normality (

)–(

)
follows by
[

, Corollary 1.8]

∎

For simplicity, we state only the annealed versions of the results; note that
the (stronger) quenched results too hold by the results above.
Also for simplicity, for Patricia tries, we do not show the oscillations in
that appear in the periodic case (in particular in the symmetric case

p=q=\frac{1}{2}
); we give only the constant terms coming from the constant
term
f_{\mathsf{E}}^{*}(-1)
in (
3.7
) and ignore the oscillating terms there
(which is the same as averaging
\psi_{\mathsf{E}}
over a period). We denote this by

\approx
below; note that in the aperiodic case,
\approx
thus means
\to
.
We use freely notation from the previous sections, and omit the simple
calculations.
