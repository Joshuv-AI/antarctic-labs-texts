Anisotropic curvature measures and volume preserving flows
Ben Andrews, Yitao Lei, Yong Wei, Changwei Xiong

Abstract
. In the first part of this paper, we develop the theory of anisotropic curvature measures for convex bodies in the Euclidean space. It is proved that any convex body whose boundary anisotropic curvature measure equals a linear combination of other lower order anisotropic curvature measures with nonnegative coefficients is a scaled Wulff shape. This generalizes the classical results by Schneider [Comment. Math. Helv. 54 (1979), 42–60] and by Kohlmann [Arch. Math. (Basel) 70 (1998), 250–256] to the anisotropic setting. The main ingredients in the proof are the generalized anisotropic Minkowski formulas and an inequality of Heintze–Karcher type for convex bodies. In the second part, we consider the volume preserving flow of smooth closed convex hypersurfaces in the Euclidean space with speed given by a positive power α \alpha of the k k th anisotropic mean curvature plus a global term chosen to preserve the enclosed volume of the evolving hypersurfaces. We prove that if the initial hypersurface is strictly convex, then the solution of the flow exists for all time and converges to the Wulff shape in the Hausdorff sense. The characterization theorem for Wulff shapes via the anisotropic curvature measures will be used crucially in the proof of the convergence result. Moreover, in the cases k = 1 k=1 , n n or α ≥ k \alpha\geq k , we can further improve the Hausdorff convergence to the smooth and exponential convergence.

1.
Introduction

The curvature measures for sets of positive reach (i.e., those sets admitting a locally unique metric projection; see, e.g.,
[
23
, Def. 1.1]
) in the Euclidean space have been extensively studied since they were introduced by Federer
[
13
]
in 1959. Schneider
[
31
]
in 1978 developed the theory of curvature measures for convex bodies, an important subclass of sets with positive reach. Here by convex body we mean compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with nonempty interior. Given a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\varepsilon>0
and any Borel set
\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, the local
\varepsilon
-parallel set of
K
is defined by

A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:~0<d(K,x)\leq\varepsilon,~p(K,x)\in\beta\}

which is the set of all points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that the distance
0<d(K,x)\leq\varepsilon
and the nearest point
p(K,x)
to
x
on
K
belongs to
\beta
. The volume of
A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta)
is a polynomial of degree
n+1
in the parameter
\varepsilon
(see
[
33
, §4.2]
):

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n+1}(A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n+1-k}{\binom{n+1}{k}}\mathcal{C}_{k}(K,\beta)

for
\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
and
\varepsilon>0
. The equation (
1.1
) can be viewed as a localized version of the classical Steiner formula. The coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{0}(K,\cdot),\dots,\mathcal{C}_{n}(K,\cdot)
of (
1.1
) are called the
curvature measures
of the convex body
K
, which are Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. A special one is
\mathcal{C}_{n}
which is the boundary area measure given by
\mathcal{C}_{n}(K,\beta)=~\mathcal{H}^{n}(\beta\cap\partial K)
for
\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
.

The curvature measures, as a measure theoretical replacement of the mean curvatures
E_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq n
) in the smooth differential geometry, have many applications in the non-smooth case. In 1979, Schneider
[
32
]
applied the curvature measures to characterize the Euclidean ball, and proved the following generalization of the classical Alexandrov Theorem in the differential geometry.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 8.5.7 in
[
33
]
)
.

Let
m\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. If
K
is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and its curvature measure
\mathcal{C}_{m}(K,\cdot)
satisfies

(1.2)

\mathcal{C}_{m}(K,\beta)=~c~\mathcal{C}_{n}(K,\beta)

for any Borel set
\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
and a constant
c>0
, then
K
is a ball.

The classification result in Theorem
1.1
was generalized later by Kohlmann
[
25
]
to the case where a linear combination of curvature measures with nonnegative coefficients equals the boundary area measure. If
K
is a smooth convex body, then the curvature measures can be given as the following integrals

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}(K,\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\beta\cap\partial K}E_{n-k}(\lambda)d\mathcal{H}^{n},\quad k=0,\dots,n

for any Borel set
\beta\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
, where
E_{n-k}(\lambda)
is the
(n-k)
th mean curvature of the hypersurface
\partial K
and is defined as the normalized
(n-k)
th elementary symmetric polynomial of the principal curvatures
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of
\partial K
. The equation (
1.2
) for a smooth convex body
K
is equivalent to that
E_{n-m}(\lambda)
is a constant on the boundary
\partial K
, which implies that
K
is a Euclidean ball by the classical Alexandrov theorem.

The curvature measures are useful in the study of the geometry of hypersurfaces. Recently, the first and the third authors
[
6
]
developed a new application of the curvature measures in the study of volume preserving curvature flows. The classification result in Theorem
1.1
was used crucially in
[
6
]
to establish the Hausdorff convergence of a class of volume preserving curvature flows in the Euclidean space by positive powers of higher order mean curvatures. The main objective of this paper is to establish the theory of curvature measures in the anisotropic geometry, and then to develop their applications in the study of volume preserving anisotropic curvature flows.

1.1.
Anisotropic curvature measures

In this paper, we first develop the analogues of the curvature measures by incorporating some explicit anisotropy, which we call the anisotropic curvature measures. The concept of anisotropy originates from crystallography and dates back to Wulff
[
42
]
. Let
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
be a smooth positive function such that the square of its positively
1
-homogeneous extension on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
is uniformly convex. Consider the anisotropic surface energy functional

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}(K)=\int_{\partial^{*}K}\gamma(\nu)d\mathcal{H}^{n}

among sets of finite perimeter, where
\partial^{*}K
denotes the reduced boundary of
K
and
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal vector field. The isoperimetric problem for this energy functional is uniquely solved by a scaled translated Wulff shape
W
, which is the set

W=\bigcap_{\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x\cdot\nu\leq\gamma(\nu)\right\}

determined by the function
\gamma
, see
[
10
,
16
,
35
]
. A sharp quantitative version of the anisotropic isoperimetric problem was established by Figalli, Maggi and Pratelli
[
15
]
, corresponding to the stability for the Wulff shape of a given surface energy (
1.4
). The function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
is called the support function of the Wulff shape
W
. The assumption on
\gamma
ensures that
W
is a smooth uniformly convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and its boundary
\Sigma=\partial W
is also called the Wulff shape determined by the support function
\gamma
. See §
2.1
for details.

The anisotropy is considered in the relative (or Minkowski) differential geometry as well, where the Wulff shape plays the similar role as the unit sphere plays in the Euclidean geometry; see
[
9
]
and references therein. In fact, the Wulff shape allows us to define the anisotropic normal of any smooth orientable hypersurface
M^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by a map
\nu_{\gamma}:M\to\Sigma
, which takes each point
x\in M
to the point in
\Sigma
with the same oriented tangent hyperplane. The anisotropic Weingarten map
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
is the derivative of the anisotropic normal
\nu_{\gamma}
, which is a linear map from
T_{x}M
to itself at each point. The eigenvalues
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})
of
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
are called the anisotropic principal curvatures. We define the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature of
M
as the normalized
k
th elementary symmetric function
E_{k}
of the anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa
:

(1.5)

{E}_{k}=E_{k}(\kappa)={\binom{n}{k}}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\kappa_{i_{1}}\cdots\kappa_{i_{k}},\quad k=1,\dots,n.

It was proved in
[
22
]
that any smooth embedded closed hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with constant
k
th anisotropic mean curvature for some
1\leq k\leq n
is a scaled Wulff shape. Note that when the Wulff shape is the unit sphere, the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature defined in (
1.5
) is just the
k
th mean curvature of the hypersurface in the Euclidean space, and the result in
[
22
]
reduces to the classical Alexandrov theorem.

To introduce the anisotropic curvature measures, we need a notion of local “
\varepsilon
-parallel set” of a convex body in the anisotropic setting. This will be defined via the following relative distance function. Given a Wulff shape
W
, we define a (non-symmetric) distance function in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
relative to
W
, by setting
d_{W}(x,y)=\inf\{r>0:\ y\in x+rW\}
. Then the relative distance of a point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
from a set
K
is defined as

d_{W}(K,y)=\inf\{d_{W}(x,y):\ x\in K\}.

If
K
is a convex body, then the infimum in the above definition is attained at a single point which we denote by
f_{K}(y)\in\partial K
. For any open set
\beta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we define the local
\varepsilon
-parallel set of
K
as follows

A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\ 0<d_{W}(K,x)\leq\varepsilon,\ f_{K}(x)\in\beta\}.

The anisotropic curvature measures can be formally defined as the coefficients of the polynomial

(1.6)

{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}(A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta))=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n+1-r}{n+1\choose r}\Phi_{r}(K;\beta),

which is called the Steiner formula for the volume of the anisotropic local parallel set of
K
. Strictly speaking, as
A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta)
and
\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)
depend on the Wulff shape
W
and it would be better to use the notation
A_{\varepsilon,W}(K,\beta)
and
\Phi_{r,W}(K;\beta)
, but we will omit the subscript
W
for the simplicity of the notation. We will discuss in §
3
the derivation of the formula (
1.6
), and prove that
\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)
indeed defines a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and has the weak continuity property with respect to the Hausdorff distance. As a key ingredient, we introduce the generalized anisotropic principal curvatures of a convex body
K
living on the anisotropic normal bundle of
K
. In §
4
, using the anisotropic curvature measures we prove a generalized version of the Minkowski formulas, which hold for any convex body in the Euclidean space. Moreover, the anisotropic curvature measures allow us to derive in §
5
a representation formula for the volume of a convex body involving the anisotropic interior reach, which leads to an inequality of Heintze–Karcher type. Combining the anisotropic Minkowski formulas and the inequality of Heintze–Karcher type, we are able to prove the following theorem on the anisotropic curvature measures, which is the content of §
6
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For a convex body
K
and some constants
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}\geq 0
, assume that the anisotropic curvature measures of
K
satisfy

(1.7)

\Phi_{n}(K;\cdot)=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}\Phi_{r}(K;\cdot).

Then
K
is a scaled Wulff shape.

Theorem
1.2
is the anisotropic analogue of the results proved by Schneider
[
32
]
and Kohlmann
[
25
]
. In particular, any convex body with anisotropic curvature measures satisfying

(1.8)

\Phi_{n}(K;\cdot)=c\Phi_{n-k}(K;\cdot)

for some
k=1,\dots,n
and some constant
c>0
must be a scaled Wulff shape. When the Wulff shape
W
is the unit Euclidean ball, Theorem
1.2
with anisotropic curvature measures satisfying (
1.8
) reduces to Theorem
1.1
by Schneider
[
32
]
. When the convex body
K
is smooth, Equation (
1.8
) implies that the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature of
\partial K
is a constant. Then in this case the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
reduces to the result proved by He, Li, Ma, and Ge
[
22
]
for hypersurfaces with constant
k
th anisotropic mean curvature.

1.2.
Volume preserving anisotropic curvature flows

In the second part of this paper, we will apply Theorem
1.2
to study the anisotropic analogue of the volume preserving curvature flow. Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and let
X_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a smooth embedding such that
M_{0}=X_{0}(M)
is a strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
enclosing a smooth convex body
K_{0}
. We consider the smooth family of embeddings
X:M^{n}\times[0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
satisfying

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(x,t)=&~(\phi(t)-{E}_{k}^{{\alpha}/k}(\kappa))\nu_{\gamma}(x,t),\\
X(\cdot,0)=&~X_{0}(\cdot),\end{aligned}\right.

where
\alpha>0
,
E_{k}(\kappa)
is the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature defined in (
1.5
) and
\nu_{\gamma}(x,t)
is the anisotropic normal of the evolving hypersurface
M_{t}=X(M,t)
.
The global term
\phi(t)
in the flow (
1.9
) is chosen as

(1.10)

\displaystyle\phi(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}^{{\alpha}/k}(\kappa)d\mu_{\gamma}

to preserve the volume of the closure
K_{t}
of the domain enclosed by
M_{t}
, where
d\mu_{\gamma}=\gamma(\nu)d\mu
is the anisotropic area form on
M_{t}
and
|M_{t}|_{\gamma}
is the anisotropic area of
M_{t}
(i.e., the anisotropic perimeter defined in (
1.4
) for smooth convex bodies).

We will prove the following convergence result for the flow (
1.9
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the smooth support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and
X_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a smooth embedding such that
M_{0}=X_{0}(M)
is a closed strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
enclosing a convex body
K_{0}
.

(1)

For any
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\alpha>0
, the volume preserving flow (
1.9
) has a smooth strictly convex solution
M_{t}
for all time
t\in[0,\infty)
, and
M_{t}
converges as
t\to\infty
to a scaled Wulff shape
M_{\infty}=\bar{r}\Sigma
in the Hausdorff sense, where
\bar{r}
is the radius such that
\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})=\bar{r}^{n+1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)
.

(2)

For (i)
k=1
and
\alpha>0
, or (ii)
k=n
and
\alpha>0
, or (iii)
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
, we can improve the Hausdorff convergence in (1) to the smooth and exponential convergence.

Anisotropic analogue of mean curvature flow has been studied as models of crystal growth
[
36
]
and other phenomena involving the motion of interfaces
[
7
,
8
]
. The graphical anisotropic mean curvature flow has been studied by the first author and Clutterbuck
[
5
,
11
]
, but the convergence result of the anisotropic mean curvature flow for smooth, closed and convex hypersurfaces (anisotropic analogue of Huisken’s result
[
18
]
in 1984) has not yet been proved. The difficulty lies in the increased complexity of the evolution equations of geometric quantities due to the presence of the anisotropy and thus it is hard to obtain a priori curvature pinching ratio estimates. In 2001, the first author
[
2
]
considered the anisotropic analogue of Huisken’s volume-preserving mean curvature flow
[
19
]
(corresponding to the case
k=1
and
\alpha=1
of the flow (
1.9
)), and proved that the solutions converge to the Wulff shape corresponding to the anisotropy. Though the curvature pinching is still not available, the convergence result was proved by exploring the monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio. Our Theorem
1.3
is a generalization of the main result in
[
2
]
to the flow by arbitrary positive powers of higher order anisotropic mean curvatures.

In the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we exploit the isoperimetric bounds on the evolving domains. For a smooth convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the mixed volume relative to
W
is defined as

\displaystyle V_{n+1}(K,W)=(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K),\qquad V_{0}(K,W)=(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(W),

\displaystyle V_{n+1-k}(K,W)=\int_{\partial K}{E}_{k-1}d\mu_{\gamma},\quad k=1,\dots,n,

where
{E}_{k-1}
is the
(k-1)
st anisotropic mean curvature of
\partial K
relative to the Wulff shape
W
. We consider the following isoperimetric ratio

(1.11)

{\mathcal{I}}_{\ell}(K,W)=\frac{V_{\ell}(K,W)^{n+1}}{V_{n+1}(K,W)^{\ell}~V_{0}(K,W)^{n+1-\ell}},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq n,

for the convex body
K
. We will prove that
\mathcal{I}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is monotone non-increasing in time along the flow (
1.9
), and thus is bounded from above by its initial value. On the other hand, the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality (
2.16
) implies

{\mathcal{I}}_{\ell}(K,W)\geq 1,\quad 1\leq\ell\leq n,

with the equality if and only if
K
is homothetic to
W
. These can be used to control the geometry of the evolving hypersurface
M_{t}
, including the
n
-dimensional anisotropic area, the anisotropic inner radius and outer radius.

To study the long-time behavior of the flow (
1.9
), we employ the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization which we will review in §
8
and we rewrite the flow as a scalar parabolic equation of the “anisotropic support function
s
” on the Wulff shape
\Sigma
. This allows us to use the inner radius bound and Tso’s technique
[
38
]
to give an upper bound on the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature and the two-sided positive bounds on the global term
\phi(t)
. We can also obtain the convexity estimate for the evolving hypersurfaces by estimating an upper bound on the anisotropic principal radii
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})
, which are eigenvalues of the matrix

\tau_{ij}[s]=\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}s+\bar{g}_{ij}s-\frac{1}{2}Q_{ijk}\bar{\nabla}_{k}s.

Here
Q_{ijk}
comes from the anisotropy which creates lots of extra terms in the evolution equation of
\tau_{ij}
. Thus the maximum principle for tensors may not be applied directly to the evolution of
\tau_{ij}
to deduce its upper bound. To overcome this problem, we notice that the evolution equation of
s
has some terms which could be used to control the bad terms in the evolution of
\tau_{ij}
, and we can combine them together to get a time dependent upper bound on the largest anisotropic principal radius. Then a standard contradiction argument can be used to conclude the long-time existence of the flow.

From the monotonicity of
\mathcal{I}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
and the long-time existence of the flow, we show that the anisotropic curvature measures of the limit convex body satisfy the equation (
1.8
) and thus the limit convex body is a scaled Wulff shape by our Theorem
1.2
. This proves the Hausdorff convergence of
K_{t}
to a scaled Wulff shape. Then we can apply the argument in
[
6
, §7]
(a variation of Smoczyk’s method
[
34
]
) to conclude a uniform lower bound on the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature. This allows us to obtain uniform regularity estimates for the flow (
1.9
) in the cases
k=1
,
\alpha>0
and
k=n
,
\alpha>0
. The smooth convergence of the flow then follows in these cases.

To deal with the case
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
, we need to further explore the Hausdorff convergence of the solution. We first improve the
C^{0}
and
C^{1}
estimates and show that the anisotropic support function is close to a constant (with respect to some moving center) and its gradient is almost zero. Since
\alpha\geq k
, by the Haussdorff convergence and Hölder inequality we also show that the global term
\phi(t)
satisfies the property

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\phi(t)\geq\bar{r}^{-\alpha},

where
\bar{r}
is the anisotropic radius of the limit Wulff shape. To derive the
C^{2}
estimate, we again employ the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization. As we already have the
C^{1}
estimate, the
C^{2}
estimate is equivalent to the bounds on the anisotropic principal curvatures. Equivalently, it suffices to estimate the upper bound on the anisotropic principal radii of curvature
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}
. For this purpose, we apply the maximum principle to the evolution equation of an auxiliary function involving
\tau_{ij}[s]
, the anisotropic support function
s
and its gradient, and use the improved
C^{0}
,
C^{1}
estimates of
s
and the above-mentioned property of
\phi(t)
. This is the most technical part in the proof, where an observation on smooth symmetric functions due to Guan, Shi, and Sui
[
17
]
will be used. Finally, we show that the smooth convergence is in the exponential rate by studying the linearization of the flow around the Wulff shape.

Remark 1.1
.

We remark that the smooth convergence in Theorem
1.3
holds for all
\alpha>0
and all
k=1,\cdots,n
in the isotropic case (i.e., in the case that the Wulff shape
W
is the unit Euclidean ball), see Theorem 1.1 in
[
6
]
. It’s a natural question whether this is still true in the general anisotropic setting for
0<\alpha<k
,
k=2,\cdots,n-1
.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In §
2
we review some basic facts on the Wulff shape, the anisotropic geometry, the mixed volumes and the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities. §§
3
–
6
comprise the first part of the paper. In §
3
we introduce the anisotropic curvature measures and study their basic properties. In §
4
we prove the generalized anisotropic Minkowski integral formulas. Next in §
5
we discuss the volume representation of convex bodies in terms of the anisotropic interior reach. Finally in §
6
we first use the volume representation in §
5
to establish an inequality of Heintze–Karcher type, and then combine this inequality with the Minkowski formulas in §
4
to complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
. After §
6
we come to the second part of the paper, which consists of §§
7
–
11
. In §
7
we review the variation equations along a general anisotropic curvature flow and study basic properties of our volume preserving anisotropic curvature flow. In §
8
we introduce the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization of convex hypersurfaces and derive evolution equations for various geometric quantities under this parametrization. Next in §
9
we show the long-time existence of our anisotropic curvature flow by investigating the upper bound for the anisotropic principal radii of curvature of
the evolving hypersurfaces. Then in §
10
we use the characterization of Wulff shapes via anisotropic curvature measures in Theorem

to obtain the Hausdorff convergence of the flow. This could be used to improve the estimate on the global term

. By the Hausdorff convergence and a variant of Smoczyk’s method

]
, in §

we also prove a uniform positive lower bound on the

th anisotropic mean curvature. In the last section §

we show the smooth and exponential convergence of the flow for some special cases as mentioned above, and so finish the proof of Theorem

. Throughout the paper the Einstein convention on the summation for indices is used unless otherwise indicated.

2.
Preliminaries on anisotropic geometry

In this section, we briefly review the anisotropic geometry and mixed volumes in convex geometry. We refer the readers to
[
2
,
33
,
43
]
for more details.

2.1.
The Wulff shape

Let
\gamma
be a smooth positive function on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that the matrix

(2.1)

A_{\gamma}(x)~=~\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}{\gamma}(x)+{\gamma}(x)g_{\mathbb{S}^{n}},\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}

is positive definite, where
\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}
denotes the covariant derivative on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Then there exists a unique smooth strictly convex hypersurface
\Sigma
given by

\Sigma=\left\{\phi(x)|\phi(x):={\gamma}(x)x+\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}{\gamma}(x),~x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\right\}

whose support function is given by
{\gamma}
. Denote the closure of the enclosed domain of
\Sigma
by
W
. We call
W
(and
\Sigma
) the Wulff shape determined by the function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. When
\gamma
is a constant, the Wulff shape is just a round sphere.

The smooth function
{\gamma}
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
can be extended homogeneously to a
1
-homogeneous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by

{\gamma}(x)=|x|{\gamma}({x}/{|x|}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}

and setting
{\gamma}(0)=0
. Then it is easy to show that
\phi(x)=D\gamma(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where
D
denotes the gradient on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The homogeneous extension
{\gamma}
defines a Minkowski norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, that is,
\gamma
is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
D^{2}(\gamma^{2})
is uniformly positive definite in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
. We can define a dual Minkowski norm
{\gamma}^{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by

(2.2)

{\gamma}^{0}(z):=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{\langle x,z\rangle}{{\gamma}(x)},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Then the Wulff shape
W
can be written as

W=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:{\gamma}^{0}(z)\leq 1\right\},

and
\Sigma=\partial W=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\gamma^{0}(z)=1\}
.

2.2.
Anisotropic geometry

Let
M
be a smooth orientable hypersurface in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with a unit normal vector field
\nu
, and
\Sigma
be the Wulff shape defined in §
2.1
. We define the anisotropic normal of
M
as the map
\nu_{\gamma}:M\to\Sigma
given by

\nu_{\gamma}(x)~=~\phi(\nu(x))={\gamma}(\nu(x))\nu(x)+\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}{\gamma}(\nu(x))~\in~\Sigma

for any point
x\in M
. It follows from the
1
-homogeneity of
\gamma
that
\nu_{\gamma}(x)=D\gamma(\nu(x))
. The anisotropic Weingarten map is a linear map

\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}=d\nu_{\gamma}:T_{x}M\to T_{\nu_{\gamma}(x)}\Sigma.

Note that
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}=A_{\gamma}\circ\mathcal{W}
, where
\mathcal{W}=d\nu=(h_{i}^{j})
is the Weingarten map of the hypersurface
M
. Recall that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}
are the principal curvatures
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
. The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}
are called the anisotropic principal curvatures of
M
, and we denote them by
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})
. If we write
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}=(\hat{h}_{i}^{j})
in local coordinates, then

(2.3)

\hat{h}_{i}^{j}(x)=(A_{\gamma}(\nu(x)))_{i}^{k}h_{k}^{j}(x).

In particular, it follows from (
2.3
) that
E_{n}(\kappa)=\det(A_{\gamma}(\nu))E_{n}(\lambda)
.

For any smooth orientable hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we define the anisotropic area functional as

|M|_{\gamma}:=\int_{M}\gamma(\nu)d\mu,

where
\nu
is a fixed unit normal vector field of
M
and
d\mu
is the area form of the induced metric on
M
from the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We set
d\mu_{\gamma}=\gamma(\nu)d\mu
and call it the anisotropic area form of
M
.

There is another formulation of the anisotropic principal curvatures, which was introduced by the first author in
[
2
]
and reformulated by Xia in
[
43
]
. The idea is to define a new metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
using the second derivatives of the dual function
{\gamma}^{0}
of the homogeneous extension of
\gamma
. This new metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is defined as

(2.4)

G(z)(U,V):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\frac{\partial^{2}({\gamma}^{0})^{2}(z)}{\partial z^{i}\partial z^{j}}U^{i}V^{j}

for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
and
U,V\in T_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The third derivatives of
{\gamma}^{0}
can be used to define

(2.5)

Q(z)(U,V,Y):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n+1}\frac{\partial^{3}({\gamma}^{0})^{2}(z)}{\partial z^{i}\partial z^{j}\partial z^{k}}U^{i}V^{j}Y^{k}

for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
and
U,V,Y\in T_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The
1
-homogeneity of
{\gamma}^{0}
implies that

\displaystyle G(z)(z,z)=

\displaystyle 1,\quad G(z)(z,V)=0,\quad\mathrm{for}\quad z\in\Sigma~\mathrm{and}~V\in T_{z}\Sigma,

\displaystyle Q(z)(z,U,V)=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\mathrm{for}\quad z\in\Sigma~\mathrm{and}~U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

For a smooth hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the anisotropic normal
\nu_{\gamma}
lies in
\Sigma
. Then

\displaystyle G(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},\nu_{\gamma})=

\displaystyle 1,\quad G(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},U)=0,\quad\mathrm{for}~U\in TM,

\displaystyle Q(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},U,V)=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\mathrm{for}~U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Thus
\nu_{\gamma}
is perpendicular to
TM
with respect to the metric
G(\nu_{\gamma})
. This induces a Riemannian metric
\hat{g}
on
M
from
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},G)
by

(2.6)

\hat{g}(x):=G(\nu_{\gamma}(x))\big|_{T_{x}M},\quad x\in M.

The second fundamental form of
(M,\hat{g})\subset(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},G)
is defined by

(2.7)

\hat{h}_{ij}=-G(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},\partial_{i}\partial_{j}X),

where
X
is the position vector of the hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Then the anisotropic Weingarten map has the expression
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}=(\hat{h}_{i}^{j})=(\sum_{k}\hat{g}^{jk}\hat{h}_{ik})
. The anisotropic Weingarten formula says that

(2.8)

\partial_{i}\nu_{F}=\hat{h}_{i}^{k}\partial_{k}X.

We can state the anisotropic analogue of the Gauss equation and Codazzi equation:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{ijk\ell}=

\displaystyle~\hat{h}_{ik}\hat{h}_{j\ell}-\hat{h}_{i\ell}\hat{h}_{jk}+\hat{\nabla}_{\ell}A_{jki}-\hat{\nabla}_{k}A_{j\ell i}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\qquad+\hat{g}^{pm}A_{jkp}A_{m\ell i}-\hat{g}^{pm}A_{j\ell p}A_{mki},

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{k}\hat{h}_{ij}+

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{j}^{\ell}{A}_{\ell ki}~=~\hat{\nabla}_{j}\hat{h}_{ik}+\hat{h}_{k}^{\ell}{A}_{\ell ji},

where
\hat{\nabla}
and
\hat{R}
are the Levi-Civita connection and Riemannian curvature tensor of
\hat{g}
respectively, and
A
is a
3
-tensor

{A}_{ijk}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{h}_{i}^{\ell}Q_{jk\ell}+\hat{h}_{j}^{\ell}Q_{i\ell k}-\hat{h}_{k}^{\ell}Q_{ij\ell}\right),

with
Q_{ijk}=Q(\nu_{\gamma})(\partial_{i}X,\partial_{j}X,\partial_{k}X)
. Note that by definition (
2.5
),
Q
is totally symmetric in all three indices. Hence the tensor
{A}_{ijk}
is symmetric for the first two indices.

When the hypersurface
M
is the Wulff shape
\Sigma
, the anisotropic normal
\nu_{\gamma}
is just the position vector and the anisotropic principal curvatures are all equal to
1
. Then
A_{ijk}=-Q_{ijk}/2
which is symmetric in all indices. We use the notations
\bar{g}
,
\bar{\nabla}
and
\bar{R}
to denote the induced metric on the Wulff shape
\Sigma
from
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},G)
, and its Levi-Civita connection and curvature tensor, respectively. Then
\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}Q_{ijk}
is totally symmetric in four indices (Prop. 2.2 in
[
44
]
). The Gauss equation (
2.2
) is simplified as

(2.10)

\bar{R}_{ijk\ell}=\bar{g}_{ik}\bar{g}_{j\ell}-\bar{g}_{i\ell}\bar{g}_{jk}+\frac{1}{4}\bar{g}^{pq}Q_{i\ell p}Q_{qjk}-\frac{1}{4}\bar{g}^{pq}Q_{ikp}Q_{j\ell q}.

2.3.
Mixed volumes and Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities

Let
W
be the Wulff shape defined in §
2.1
, which is a smooth strictly convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with boundary
\Sigma=\partial W
and with the support function given by a smooth function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. Associated with
W
, the anisotropic distance in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is defined by

(2.11)

d_{W}(x,y)={\gamma}^{0}(y-x),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

where
\gamma^{0}
is the dual norm defined in (
2.2
). Equivalently, we have

d_{W}(x,y)=\inf\left\{r>0:y\in x+rW\right\}.

This distance is not symmetric (unless
W
is centrosymmetric), but satisfies the triangle inequality

d_{W}(x,z)\leq d_{W}(x,y)+d_{W}(y,z)

with the equality if and only if
x,y,z
are collinear in that order.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we define the anisotropic distance of
y
to
K
by

(2.12)

d_{W}(K,y)=\inf\left\{d_{W}(x,y):x\in K\right\}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the
\varepsilon
-parallel set of
K
by

K^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}~|~d_{W}(K,x)\leq\varepsilon\right\}

which is also equal to the Minkowski sum of the two convex bodies
K
and
W

K^{\varepsilon}=K+\varepsilon W=\left\{x+\varepsilon y~|~x\in K,y\in W\right\}.

The mixed volumes of a convex body
K
relative to
W
are defined as the coefficients of the volume of the above
\varepsilon
-parallel set of
K
, which is a polynomial of
\varepsilon
:

(2.13)

\mathrm{Vol}(K^{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\varepsilon^{n+1-k}\binom{n+1}{k}V_{k}(K,W).

Here the left-hand side of (
2.13
) is the volume of
K^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the Euclidean metric, i.e., the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure (or
(n+1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure) of
K^{\varepsilon}
. The formula (
2.13
) is called the Steiner formula for the convex body
K
. In particular,

\displaystyle V_{n+1}(K,W)=

\displaystyle(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K),

\displaystyle V_{0}(K,W)=

\displaystyle(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(W).

If
\partial K
is smooth, we can express the mixed volumes in terms of the anisotropic curvature integrals

(2.14)

V_{n+1-k}(K,W)=\int_{\partial K}{E}_{k-1}d\mu_{\gamma},\quad k=1,\dots,n,

where
{E}_{k-1}
is the
(k-1)
st anisotropic mean curvature of
\partial K
relative to
W
and
d\mu_{\gamma}={\gamma}(\nu)d\mu
denotes the anisotropic area form.

Moreover, the mixed volumes satisfy the following Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities:

Theorem 2.1

(see
[
33
, §7]
)
.

For any convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, there holds

(2.15)

V_{n+1-j}^{k-i}(K,W)~\geq~V_{n+1-i}^{k-j}(K,W)V_{n+1-k}^{j-i}(K,W)

for all
0\leq i<j<k\leq n+1
. In particular, for
k=n+1
, (
2.15
) reduces to

(2.16)

V_{n+1-j}^{n+1-i}(K,W)~\geq~V_{n+1-i}^{n+1-j}(K,W)V_{0}(K,W)^{j-i}

for
0\leq i<j<n+1
. Furthermore, the equality of (
2.16
) holds if and only if
K
is a scaled translate of
W
.

We note that the inequality (
2.16
) implies that the isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(K,W)
defined in (
1.11
) satisfies

(2.17)

\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(K,W)\geq 1,\quad 1\leq\ell\leq n,

with the equality holding if and only if
K
is homothetic to
W
.

3.
Anisotropic curvature measures

In this section, we introduce the anisotropic curvature measures for convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For that purpose, we first define the generalized anisotropic principal curvatures on the unit anisotropic normal bundle of a convex body
K
. Then we calculate the volume of the local
\varepsilon
-parallel set of
K
, and express it as a polynomial of degree
n+1
in
\varepsilon
. The anisotropic curvature measures will be defined as the coefficients of the expression. At the end of this section, we will prove that such defined anisotropic curvature measures are weakly continuous with respect to the Hausdorff distance.

3.1.

Let
W

.

(3.3)

dv_{\partial K^{t}}\big|_{\sigma(t)}\big(\left(1+(t-\varepsilon)\kappa_{i}(p)\right)e_{i}\big)=\kappa_{i}(p)e_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
\{e_{i},i=1,\dots,n\}
denotes an orthonormal basis of the anisotropic Weingarten map at
p\in\partial K^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the induced metric from
G
. The equation (
3.3
) means that the parallel displacement of
e_{i}
along
\sigma(t)
yields an orthonormal basis of the anisotropic Weingarten map of
\partial K^{t}
at
\sigma(t)
for
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
, and the corresponding eigenvalues at
\sigma(t)
are related to
\kappa_{i}(p)
by

(3.4)

\kappa_{i}(\sigma(t))=\frac{\kappa_{i}(p)}{1+(t-\varepsilon)\kappa_{i}(p)}.

If
\kappa_{i}(p)\neq 1/{\varepsilon}
, the equation is equivalent to

(3.5)

\frac{\kappa_{i}(\sigma(t))}{1-t\kappa_{i}(\sigma(t))}=\frac{\kappa_{i}(p)}{1-\varepsilon\kappa_{i}(p)}.

Since both sides of (

) do not depend on

, we can define their value as
\kappa_{i}(x,v)
for
(x,v)\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
, which we call the generalized anisotropic principal curvatures of
K
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We define the generalized anisotropic principal curvatures of

at
(x,v)\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\subset\mathcal{N}_{K}
by

\kappa_{i}(x,v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\dfrac{\kappa_{i}(p)}{1-\varepsilon\kappa_{i}(p)},&\mathrm{if}~\kappa_{i}(p)\neq 1/\varepsilon,\\
+\infty,&\mathrm{if}~\kappa_{i}(p)=1/\varepsilon,\end{array}\right.

where

) does not depend on
\varepsilon
.

Remark 3.2
.

Alternatively, we have

\kappa_{i}(x,v):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}\kappa_{i}(\sigma(t))\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\},

and write

instead of

for the simplicity of the notation. If the base point of

.

Relabel
\kappa_{i}(v)
such that

Proposition 3.6
.

Let

and

.

(2)

For any

(3)

Using Proposition

Proposition 3.7
.

Define the following two sets

\widetilde{\partial K}:=\{x\in\partial K:\partial K\text{ is twice differentiable at }x\},

and

\widehat{\partial K}:=\{x\in\partial K:\text{the unit anisotropic normal is unique at }x\}.

Then
\widetilde{\partial K}\subset\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})\subset\widehat{\partial K}
and

Proof.

First, for any

3.2.
Volumes of anisotropic parallel sets

3.3.
Anisotropic curvature measures

The formula (
3.2
) allows us to define the anisotropic curvature measures of a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Let

\displaystyle\psi(v):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\kappa_{i}^{2}(v))^{-1/2}

and
E_{r}(v)
be the normalized
r
th elementary symmetric function of
\kappa_{i}(v)
. The product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+t\kappa_{i}(v))
can be expanded as

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+t\kappa_{i}(v))=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}E_{n-r}(v)t^{n-r}.

Therefore by (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta))=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v\right)t^{n-r}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1-r}}{n+1-r}\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n+1-r}\binom{n+1}{r}\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v.

Remark 3.3
.

As in
[
23
]
, the product
\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)
needs to be understood algebraically. In other words, if
\kappa_{i}(v)=+\infty
for some
1\leq i\leq n
, we replace all such
\kappa_{i}(v)
in
\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)
by a finite

and then take
c\rightarrow+\infty
.

If we denote

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v,\quad 0\leq r\leq n,

we arrive at the following local Steiner type formula

(3.14)

\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta))=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n+1-r}\binom{n+1}{r}\Phi_{r}(K;\beta).

By setting

\Phi_{n+1}(K;\beta)=(n+1)\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K\cap\beta),

the formula (
3.14
) is equivalent to

(3.15)

\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(f_{K}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K^{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n+1}\varepsilon^{n+1-r}\binom{n+1}{r}\Phi_{r}(K;\beta).

Since the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure coincides with the
(n+1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the formula (
1.6
) is equivalent to (
3.14
).

Definition 3.8
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. We call
\Phi_{r}(K;\cdot)
(

) the

of the convex body

(with respect to the Wulff shape

).

Remark 3.4
.

(i)

Comparing (
3.15
) with (
2.13
) we can see that

\Phi_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=V_{r}(K,W).

That is, the anisotropic curvature measures of a convex body is the localized version of the mixed volumes introduced in §
2.3
.

(ii)

When
\partial K
is
C^{2}
, then
\mathcal{N}_{K}=\{(p,v(p)):p\in\partial K\}
,
\kappa_{i}(v)=\kappa_{i}(p)
and the Jacobi determinant of the map (with the definition naturally extended to
\partial K

is

\psi(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v=d\mu_{\gamma}(p)=\gamma(\nu)d\mu(p)

and

\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)=\int_{\partial K\cap\beta}E_{n-r}(p)\gamma(\nu)\,d\mu(p).

This implies the same formula as in (i),

\Phi_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=\int_{\partial K}E_{n-r}\gamma(\nu)d\mu=V_{r}(K,W)

by (

K

and

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in

with the support function

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(K;\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\pi(\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\mathcal{N}_{K})}d\mu_{\gamma},

(3.18)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(K;\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\partial K}\cap\beta}{\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p),

where
\pi:\mathcal{N}_{K}\to\Sigma=\partial W
is the projection of
\mathcal{N}_{K}
to the vector part given by
\pi(x,v)=v
and

is given in Proposition

Proof.

For

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(K;\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

3.4.
Weak continuity of the anisotropic curvature measures

As in the classical setting of curvature measures for convex bodies in the Euclidean space, the anisotropic curvature measures introduced here are also weakly continuous in the Hausdorff topology.

For any two convex bodies
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the anisotropic Hausdorff distance between them is defined as

d^{\mathcal{H}}_{W}(K,L)=\inf_{r>0}\{r:K+rW\subset L\text{ and }L+rW\subset K\}.

Note that the anisotropic and isotropic distance functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
have the relation

\frac{1}{C}d(x,y)\leq d_{W}(x,y)\leq Cd(x,y)

for some positive constant
C
depending only on the Wulff shape
W
. So the anisotropic Hausdorff distance
d^{\mathcal{H}}_{W}
and the isotropic Hausdorff distance
d^{\mathcal{H}}
for sets are equivalent.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and
\{K_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
K_{j}\to K
as
j\to\infty
in the Hausdorff topology. Then for every
r=0,\dots,n
, the anisotropic curvature measure
\Phi_{r}(K_{j};\cdot)
converges weakly in the sense of measure to the anisotropic curvature measure
\Phi_{r}(K;\cdot)
.

According to the Steiner type formula (
3.14
), Theorem
3.10
is a direct consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and let
K
and
K_{i}
,
i\geq 1
be convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Suppose that

\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{W}^{\mathcal{H}}(K_{i},K)=0.

Define
\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon}(\beta):=\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(f_{K}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K^{\varepsilon})
for Borel set
\beta
, and similarly
\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(\beta):=\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(f_{K_{i}}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K_{i}^{\varepsilon})
. Then

\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon}

as
i\to\infty
.

Proof.

The sets
f_{K}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K^{\varepsilon}
and
f_{K_{i}}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
are measurable since
f_{K}
and
f_{K_{i}}
are continuous. Then it suffices to prove:

(1)

for an open set
\beta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon}(\beta)\leq\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(\beta)
, and

(2)

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
.

To prove (1), let
p\in\mathring{K}^{\varepsilon}
, the interior of
K^{\varepsilon}
, with
f_{K}(p)\in\beta
. First we claim
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}f_{K_{i}}(p)=f_{K}(p)
. Otherwise assume that a subsequence (still denoted by
f_{K_{i}}(p)
) converges to a point
p^{*}
which is not
f_{K}(p)
. Note that

d_{W}(f_{K_{i}}(p),p)=d_{W}(K_{i},p)\leq d_{W}(K_{i},K)+d_{W}(K,p),

which implies

p\in(d_{W}(K_{i},K)+d_{W}(K,p))W+f_{K_{i}}(p).

Letting
i\rightarrow\infty
yields
p\in d_{W}(K,p)W+p^{*}
. On the other hand, since
f_{K_{i}}(p)\in K_{i}
and
d_{W}(K_{i},K)\rightarrow 0
, we know
p^{*}\in K
. So
d_{W}(K,p)
is attained at two distinct points
f_{K}(p)
and
p^{*}
, which is a contradiction. Since
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}f_{K_{i}}(p)=f_{K}(p)
and
\beta
is open, we have
f_{K_{i}}(p)\in\beta
for any fixed
q\in B\cap\partial K
and any fixed
p\in f_{K}^{-1}(q)\cap\mathring{K}^{\varepsilon}
and any large
i
. So we have proved the claim. It follows that

f_{K}^{-1}(\beta)\cap\mathring{K}^{\varepsilon}\subset\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\bigcap_{m\geq i}f_{K_{m}}^{-1}(\beta)\cap K_{m}^{\varepsilon}.

Therefore,

\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon}(\beta)\leq\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(\beta),

which completes the proof of (1).

To prove (2), note that

\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon-d_{W}^{\mathcal{H}}(K_{i},K)}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\leq\mu_{K_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\leq\mu_{K}^{\varepsilon+d_{W}^{\mathcal{H}}(K_{i},K)}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

for
i
large enough. So (2) follows immediately.
∎

4.
Minkowski integral formulas

The Minkowski integral formulas are important tools in the study of the differential geometry. For smooth closed hypersurfaces, the following anisotropic Minkowski integral formulas are proved in
[
21
]
by He and Li.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For any smooth closed orientable hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with a unit isotropic normal
\nu
and any integer
1\leq r\leq n
, we have

(4.1)

\int_{M}(E_{r-1}(p)-S(p)E_{r}(p)){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)=0,

where
E_{r}
denotes the anisotropic
r
th mean curvature of
M
with respect to the Wulff shape
W
, and
S(p)=G(v)(v,p)
is the anisotropic support function on
M
with
v=D\gamma(\nu)
denoting the unit anisotropic normal of
M
.

In this section, we generalize Theorem
4.1
to nonsmooth setting by adapting the idea in Kohlmann’s work
[
24
]
for the isotropic case.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For any convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
1\leq r\leq n
, we have

(4.2)

\int_{\mathcal{N}_{K}}\psi(v)(E_{r-1}(v)-S(v)E_{r}(v))d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v=0,

where
S(v)
is the anisotropic support function defined on
\mathcal{N}_{K}
by

S(v):=G(v)(v,\Pi(v))

for any
v\in\mathcal{N}_{K}
, and
E_{r}(v)
is the generalized
r
th mean curvature of the convex body
K
.

Note that our formulas live on the unit anisotropic normal bundle
\mathcal{N}_{K}
rather than on the boundary
\partial K
itself. Though the generalized principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}(v)
are defined only at those points in
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
, we can still write our formulas in the form (
4.2
) as we have
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\mathcal{N}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K})=0
and the integrand on this part can be assigned any finite value. Restricted to the smooth case,
S(v)=G(v)(v(p),p)=S(p)
for
p\in\partial K
is just the anisotropic support function defined on the boundary
\partial K
that is used in Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

For the proof we use the fact that any convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
can be approximated by a sequence of smooth convex bodies
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
with respect to the Hausdorff distance (cf.
[
9
,
33
]
). Then by (
4.1
) we have

(4.3)

\int_{\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(E_{r-1}(p)-S(p)E_{r}(p)){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)=0

for small
\varepsilon>0
, where
K_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\{x:~d_{W}(K_{i},x)\leq\varepsilon\}
is the anisotropic parallel body of
K_{i}
with the anisotropic distance
\varepsilon
. Choose
K_{i}
such that
d_{W}^{\mathcal{H}}(K_{i},K)<{\varepsilon}/{i}
. Then

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

d^{\mathcal{H}}_{W}({K^{\varepsilon}_{i}},{K^{\varepsilon}})<\frac{\varepsilon}{i}.

Proof.

Since
K
and
K_{i}
are symmetric in the statement, it suffices to prove that for any
p\in{K^{\varepsilon}}
we can find
q\in{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}
such that
d_{W}(q,p)<\varepsilon/i
. To this end, first we can find
p^{\prime}\in K
with
d_{W}(p^{\prime},p)\leq\varepsilon
. Then we can choose
q^{\prime}\in K_{i}
such that
d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})<\varepsilon/i
. Therefore by the triangle inequality we get
d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)<\varepsilon(1+i^{-1})
.

Now consider two cases: (1) If
d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)\leq d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})
, then we choose
q=q^{\prime}
(
\in K_{i}\subset{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}
). So we have

d_{W}(q,p)=d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)\leq d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})<\frac{\varepsilon}{i}.

(2) If
d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)>d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})
, we can choose
q
on the segment
\overline{q^{\prime}p}
such that
d_{W}(q^{\prime},q)=d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)-d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})
(
\leq d_{W}(p^{\prime},p)\leq\varepsilon
). Then we have

d_{W}(q,p)=d_{W}(q^{\prime},p)-d_{W}(q^{\prime},q)=d_{W}(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})<\frac{\varepsilon}{i}.

Since
d_{W}(q^{\prime},q)\leq\varepsilon
, we have
q\in K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

It follows from Lemma
4.3
that

K^{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}\subset K^{\varepsilon}_{i}\subset K^{\varepsilon(1+\frac{1}{i})}.

For any
p\in\partial K^{\varepsilon}
, we consider the half-line
f_{K}(p)p
starting from
f_{K}(p)
. Since
K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is convex, there exists a unique point
p_{i}(p)\in\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
on the half-line
f_{K}(p)p
. Denote by
v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i})
the unit outward anisotropic normal of
\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
at
p_{i}
. Then we have the following result.

Lemma 4.4
.

In
T\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we have for fixed
p\in\partial K^{\varepsilon}
,

(p_{i}(p),v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}(p)))\longrightarrow(p,v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p)),\text{ as }i\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

First, since
K^{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}\subset K^{\varepsilon}_{i}\subset K^{\varepsilon(1+\frac{1}{i})}
, we have

\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})\leq d_{W}(f_{K}(p),p_{i}(p))\leq\varepsilon(1+\frac{1}{i}).

Letting
i\rightarrow\infty
yields
d_{W}(f_{K}(p),p_{i}(p))\rightarrow\varepsilon
. So
p_{i}(p)\rightarrow p
as
i\rightarrow\infty
.

Second, to show the convergence of the unit anisotropic normals, we consider the translated scaled Wulff shape

W_{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}(f_{K}(p)):=\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})W+f_{K}(p),

and the cone
C_{i}
generated by it with vertex
p_{i}(p)

C_{i}=\left\{t\>\overrightarrow{p_{i}(p)q}:t\geq 0,\quad q\in W_{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}(f_{K}(p))\right\}.

As
i\rightarrow\infty
, the Wulff shape
W_{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}(f_{K}(p))
converges to
W_{\varepsilon}(f_{K}(p))
which is contained in
K^{\varepsilon}
and is tangential to
\partial K^{\varepsilon}
. Noting that
p_{i}(p)\rightarrow p
, we conclude that
C_{i}
converges to
T
, one of the half-spaces bounded by
T_{p}K_{\varepsilon}
.

On the other hand, since
W_{\varepsilon(1-\frac{1}{i})}(f_{K}(p))
is contained in the convex body
K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
, the cone
C_{i}
is contained in
T_{i}
, one of the half-spaces determined by
T_{p_{i}(p)}\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
. Taking
i\rightarrow\infty
, we can see that
T_{i}
converges to a half-space and the limit half-space is
T
. Then we conclude that
v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}(p))\rightarrow v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p)
, as
i\rightarrow\infty
. So we finish the proof.

∎

Letting
i\rightarrow\infty
in (
4.3
) and using the convergence result in Lemma
4.4
, we can prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have

(4.4)

\int_{\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}})}\biggl(S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p))E_{r}(p)-E_{r-1}(p)\biggr)\gamma(\nu_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\mu(p)=0,

where
v_{K^{\varepsilon}}
denotes the unit anisotropic normal of
\partial K^{\varepsilon}
which exists uniquely for any point of
\partial K^{\varepsilon}
, and
\nu_{K^{\varepsilon}}
is the corresponding isotropic normal determined by the identity (
3.7
).

Proof.

For any point
p\in\partial K^{\varepsilon}
, let
p_{i}=p_{i}(p)\in\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
as before. Since
\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is smooth, the anisotropic support function satisfies
S(p_{i})=S(v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}))
. Let

(4.5)

\lambda_{i}:=\int_{\partial K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}(p_{i}))E_{r}(p_{i}){\gamma}(\nu_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}))d\mu(p_{i}),

where
\nu_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i})
is the unit isotropic normal of
\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}
at the point
p_{i}
which is determined by the anisotropic normal
v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i})
via the identity (
3.7
). Then by the equation (
4.3
) and the fact
S(p_{i})=S(v_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}))
, we have

(4.6)

\lambda_{i}=\int_{\partial K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}E_{r-1}(p_{i}){\gamma}(\nu_{K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(p_{i}))d\mu(p_{i}).

We compute the limit
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}
using both expressions of
\lambda_{i}
. First, by (
4.6
) and using the anisotropic curvature measure and its weak continuity with respect to the anisotropic Hausdorff distance, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}d\Phi_{n-r+1}(K_{i}^{\varepsilon};p_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K^{\varepsilon}}d\Phi_{n-r+1}(K^{\varepsilon};p)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}}}\psi(v_{K^{\varepsilon}})E_{r-1}(v_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v_{K^{\varepsilon}}.

Since
\partial K^{\varepsilon}
has only regular points and the anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}(p)
are well defined on
\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}})
, we can rewrite (
4
) as

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}})}E_{r-1}(p)\gamma(\nu_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\mu(p).

On the other hand, since
S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}(p_{i}))
is a bounded function, by (
4.5
) and the weak continuity of anisotropic curvature measures, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial K_{i}^{\varepsilon}}S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}(p_{i}))d\Phi_{n-r}(K_{i}^{\varepsilon};p_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}}}\psi(v_{K^{\varepsilon}})S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p))E_{r}(v_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v_{K^{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}})}S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p))E_{r}(p)\gamma(\nu_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\mu(p),

where we used Lemma
4.4
to show the convergence of
S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}_{i}}(p_{i}))
to
S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p))
as
i\to\infty
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Put
v=v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p)
. Then the map
F_{K}:\partial K^{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{N}_{K}
maps any point
p\in\partial K^{\varepsilon}
to
(p-\varepsilon v,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{K}
. For any point
(p,v)\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K^{\varepsilon}}
, we also have
(p-\varepsilon v,v)\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
. Now we can transform the integral (
4.4
) on
\Pi(\tilde{N}_{K^{\varepsilon}})\subset\partial K^{\varepsilon}
to the integral on
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
or
\mathcal{N}_{K}
as follows:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi(\tilde{N}_{K^{\varepsilon}})}\biggl(S(v_{K^{\varepsilon}}(p))E_{r}(p)-E_{r-1}(p)\biggr)\gamma(\nu_{K^{\varepsilon}})d\mu(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{N}_{K}}\biggl(S(v)E_{r}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}(v)}{1+\varepsilon\kappa_{i}(v)}\right)-E_{r-1}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}(v)}{1+\varepsilon\kappa_{i}(v)}\right)\biggr)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+\varepsilon\kappa_{i}(v)}{(1+\kappa_{i}(v)^{2})^{1/2}}d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v.

Comparing the coefficients of
\varepsilon^{m}
and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we arrive at the equation (
4.2
) and finish the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
∎

5.
Volume representation involving anisotropic interior reach

In this section, we derive a representation for the volume of a convex body involving the anisotropic interior reach. Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. Given a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the anisotropic inner radius
r(K)
of
K
is the radius of the largest Wulff shape contained in
K
, i.e.,

r(K)=\sup\{\lambda>0:\lambda W+x\subset K~\mathrm{for}~\mathrm{some}~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}.

The anisotropic interior reach of
K
at the boundary point
p\in\partial K
is defined by

r(K,p)=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0:p\in\lambda W+x\subset K\text{ for some }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}.

The main result of this section is stated as follows.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For any convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we have the following representation for the volume of
K
:

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\int_{\partial K}r(K,p)^{n+1-i}d\Phi_{i}(K;p),

where
r(K,p)
is the anisotropic interior reach of
K
, and
d\Phi_{i}(K;p)
is the anisotropic curvature measure element.

The proof of (
5.1
) uses inner parallel bodies and is inspired by the argument in Sangwine-Yager’s
[
30
]
for the isotropic case. For
0\leq\lambda\leq r(K)
, the inner parallel body with respect to
W
at a distance
\lambda
from
\partial K
is defined by

K_{-\lambda}=\{x:\lambda W+x\subset K\}.

Then
K_{-\lambda}
is also a convex body. The outer parallel body at the distance
\lambda\geq 0
is defined as
K_{\lambda}=K+\lambda W
. Since the set of boundary points with positive anisotropic interior reach is a subset of the set of boundary points with unique anisotropic normals, we have the following lemma which can be verified straightforwardly.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K
be a convex body with a positive anisotropic inner radius
r(K)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. If
p\in\partial K_{-\lambda}
,
0\leq\lambda<r(K)
, with
r(K_{-\lambda},p)=\varepsilon>0
, then for all
t>-\varepsilon
, we have
p+tv(p)\in\partial K_{-\lambda+t}
and

r(K_{-\lambda+t},p+tv(p))=\varepsilon+t,

where
v(p)
denotes the unique unit anisotropic normal at the point
p
.

In fact, for
p\in\partial K_{-\lambda}
with
r(K_{-\lambda},p)=\varepsilon>0
, the unique outward unit anisotropic normal
v(p)
is well-defined. So
\varepsilon W+(p-\varepsilon v(p))\subset K_{-\lambda}
, and for all
t>-\varepsilon
,
(t+\varepsilon)W+(p-\varepsilon v(p))
is the largest interior Wulff shape contained in
K_{-\lambda+t}
and touching at the boundary point
p+tv(p)
.

For any convex body
K
with a positive anisotropic inner radius, let
\mathscr{Z}(K)=\{p\in\partial K:r(K,p)=0\}
. We now define the interior skeleton of
K
by

\mathscr{S}(K)=\bigcup_{0<\lambda\leq r(K)}\mathscr{Z}(K_{-\lambda}).

The following lemma shows that the interior skeleton is of zero measure.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
K
is a convex body with a positive anisotropic inner radius
r(K)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then

\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(\mathscr{S}(K))=0.

Proof.

We first observe that
\Phi_{n}(K;\mathscr{Z}(K))=0
. In fact, recall that
\widetilde{\partial K}
consists of the boundary points which have a unique anisotropic normal. From the definition of
\mathscr{Z}(K)
and the fact that the set of boundary points with positive anisotropic interior reach is a subset of
\widetilde{\partial K}
, we have
\widetilde{\partial K}\cap\mathscr{Z}(K)=\emptyset
. This together with (
3.18
) implies that

\Phi_{n}(K;\mathscr{Z}(K))=\int_{\widetilde{\partial K}\cap\mathscr{Z}(K)}{\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)=0.

Given a positive integer
m
, let
0=r_{0}<r_{1}<\cdots<r_{m-1}<r_{m}=r(K)
be a partition of the interval
[0,r(K)]
. For
j=1,\dots,m-1
, let

\beta_{j}=\partial K_{-r_{j}}\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K_{-r_{j}})\text{ and }A_{j}=A_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(K_{-r_{j}},\beta_{j}),

where the notation
A_{\varepsilon}(K,\beta)
denotes the local
\varepsilon
-parallel set of a convex body
K
defined as in (
3.11
).
Then any point
p
in
A_{j}
lies on the boundary of some inner parallel body, which implies
K\setminus\mathscr{S}(K)\supset\bigcup_{j=1}^{m-1}A_{j}
. Therefore, using the local Steiner’s formula (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K\setminus\mathscr{S}(K))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(K_{-r_{j}},\beta_{j}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(r_{j}-r_{j-1})^{n+1-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K_{-r_{j}};\beta_{j})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(r_{j}-r_{j-1})\Phi_{n}(K_{-r_{j}};\beta_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(r_{j}-r_{j-1})\Phi_{n}(K_{-r_{j}};\partial K_{-r_{j}}).

Taking the supremum of the right-hand side over all partitions of the interval
[0,r]
yields

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K\setminus\mathscr{S}(K))

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{r(K)}\Phi_{n}(K_{-\lambda};\partial K_{-\lambda})d\lambda.

On the other hand, we can apply Federer’s coarea formula
[
13
]
for Lipschitz functions and the formula (
3.18
) to show that the right-hand side of (
5.2
) is equal to
\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K)
. In fact, since
\partial K_{-\lambda}
is the set of points in
K
having the anisotropic distance
\lambda
to the boundary
\partial K
, we can write

\partial K_{-\lambda}=\{x\in K|~u(x):=d_{W}(x,\partial K)=\lambda\},

i.e.,
\partial K_{-\lambda}
is the level set
u^{-1}(\lambda)
of
u
. We claim that
u:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function. Note that the projection map from
x\in K
to the nearest points on the boundary
\partial K
is different from the map
f_{K}
in §
3.1
(e.g., it may be multi-valued). So we cannot use the property for
f_{K}
. To prove the claim, let
x,y\in K
satisfy
0\leq u(x)<u(y)\leq r(K)
. Choose a point
y_{0}\in\{y+u(y)W\}\cap\partial K
. Then there exists a point
y_{1}
on the segment
yy_{0}
such that
u(y_{1})=u(x)
and we can derive

\displaystyle|u(y)-u(x)|

\displaystyle=d_{W}(y,y_{0})-d_{W}(y_{1},y_{0})=d_{W}(y,y_{1})=d_{W}(\partial K_{-u(y)},y_{1})

\displaystyle\leq d_{W}(\partial K_{-u(y)},x)\leq d_{W}(y,x)\leq C|y-x|.

So we obtain the claim.

In view of the claim, by Federer’s coarea formula
[
13
]
for Lipschitz functions, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r(K)}\int_{u^{-1}(\lambda)}\frac{1}{|Du|}d\mu d\lambda.

Arguing as in §
3.2
, we see that

|Du|\gamma(\nu)~=~1

at any point
x\in u^{-1}(\lambda)
, where
\nu
is the isotropic unit normal vector. Then applying (
3.18
) to (
5.3
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r(K)}\int_{u^{-1}(\lambda)}\gamma(\nu)d\mu d\lambda

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r(K)}\Phi_{n}(K_{-\lambda};\partial K_{-\lambda})d\lambda.

This combined with (
5.2
) completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
K
be a convex body with a positive anisotropic inner radius
r(K)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. If
0<\lambda<\lambda+\varepsilon\leq r(K)
and

\beta=\{p\in\partial K_{-\lambda}:0<r(K_{-\lambda},p)\leq\varepsilon\},

then

\Phi_{i}(K_{-\lambda}+\lambda W;\beta+\lambda W)=\Phi_{i}(K;\beta+\lambda W),\qquad i=0,\dots,n.

Proof.

The idea is to show that the set
A_{t}(K_{-\lambda}+\lambda W,\beta+\lambda W)
coincides with
A_{t}(K,\beta+\lambda W)
for all
t>0
and to use the local Steiner’s formula (
3.14
). This follows from the following observation

(5.5)

(\beta+\lambda W)\cap\partial K=(\beta+\lambda W)\cap\partial(K_{-\lambda}+\lambda W)=\{p+\lambda v(p):~p\in\beta\},

where
v(p)
denotes the unit outward anisotropic normal. The proof of (
5.5
) uses the result in Lemma
5.2
repeatedly and is similar to that for Lemma 3 of
[
30
]
. We omit it here.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
K
be a convex body and
\beta\subset\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)
. Then for
t\geq 0
and
0\leq i\leq n
,

(5.6)

\Phi_{i}(K;\beta)=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{i}{j}(-t)^{i-j}\Phi_{j}(K+tW;\beta+tW).

Proof.

First we note that

(5.7)

\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{s}(K+tW,\beta+tW))=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{n}s^{n+1-r}\binom{n+1}{r}\Phi_{r}(K+tW;\beta+tW),

where
A_{s}(K+tW,\beta+tW)
is defined by (
3.11
). On the other hand, since

A_{t}(K,\beta)\cup A_{s}(K+tW,\beta+tW)=A_{s+t}(K,\beta)

for
s,t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n+1}

\displaystyle(A_{s}(K+tW,\beta+tW))=\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{s+t}(K,\beta))-\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{t}(K,\beta))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(s+t)^{n+1-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta)-\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{t}(K,\beta))

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{i}\sum_{j=0}^{n+1-i}\binom{n+1-i}{j}t^{n+1-i-j}s^{j}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta)-\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{t}(K,\beta)).

Comparing the coefficients of
s^{n+1-r}
on the right-hand side of the equations (
5.7
) and (
5
), we have

\displaystyle\binom{n+1}{r}\Phi_{r}(K+tW;\beta+tW)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n+1}{i}\binom{n+1-i}{n+1-r}t^{r-i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n+1}{r}\binom{r}{i}t^{r-i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta).

It follows that

\Phi_{r}(K+tW;\beta+tW)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{r}{i}t^{r-i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta).

This is a linear system of equations, and can be solved to get
\Phi_{i}(K;\beta)
as in (
5.6
). The verification that (
5.6
) is the solution to this system relies on the fact

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{r}{i}t^{r-i}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{i}{j}(-t)^{i-j}a_{j}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r}t^{r-j}a_{j}\sum_{i=j}^{r}(-1)^{i-j}\binom{r}{i}\binom{i}{j}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r}t^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}a_{j}\sum_{i=j}^{r}(-1)^{i-j}\binom{r-j}{i-j}=a_{r},

where
\{a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{r}
is any sequence.
∎

Let
K
and
\beta
in Lemma
5.5
be the
K_{-\lambda}
and
\beta
in Lemma
5.4
. As an immediate consequence of Lemma
5.5
we can derive the following formula

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{n+1-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K_{-\lambda};\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{n+1-i}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K_{-\lambda}+\lambda W;\beta+\lambda W)

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{n+1-i}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta+\lambda W),

where in the first equality we used the exchange of the summations as in (
5
).

Now we prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Given any positive integer
m
, let
0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)
be a partition of the interval
[0,r(K)]
. For
j=1,\dots,m-1
, let

\displaystyle\beta_{j}=\{p:p\in

\displaystyle\partial K_{-r_{j}},\>0<r(K_{-r_{j}},p)\leq r_{j+1}-r_{j}\},

\displaystyle\quad A_{j}=A_{r_{j}}(K_{-r_{j}},\beta_{j}).

Note that
A_{j}\cap\partial K
consists of all points
x
with
r_{j}<r(K,x)\leq r_{j+1}
.

On the other hand, any
y\in K\setminus(\mathscr{S}(K)\cup\partial K)
must lie on the boundary of some parallel body
K_{-\lambda}
, and the set
(\lambda W+y)\cap\partial K
consists of only one point
x
. Thus for sufficiently large
m
, we can choose some partition of the interval
[0,r(K)]
, such that for some
1\leq j\leq m-1
,

\lambda<r_{j}<r(K,x)\leq r_{j+1}.

It follows that
y\in A_{j}
. Thus
\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K\setminus(\mathscr{S}(K)\cup\partial K))
is the supremum of the sum of
\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{j})
over all partitions of the interval
[0,r(K)]
. Since the interior skeleton is of zero measure by Lemma
5.3
, this also gives the volume of
K
. As a consequence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K\setminus(\mathscr{S}(K)\cup\partial K))=\sup_{0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)}\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(\bigcup_{j}A_{j})

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)}\sum_{j}\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(A_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sup_{0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)}\sum_{j}\sum_{r=0}^{n}(r_{j})^{n+1-r}\binom{n+1}{r}\Phi_{r}(K_{-r_{j}};\beta_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sup_{0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)}\sum_{j}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(r_{j})^{n+1-i}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta_{j}+r_{j}W)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\left(\sup_{0<r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=r(K)}\sum_{j}(r_{j})^{n+1-i}\Phi_{i}(K;\beta_{j}+r_{j}W)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\int_{\partial K}r(K,p)^{n+1-i}d\Phi_{i}(K;p),

where we used (
5
) in the fourth equality. This completes the proof of Proposition
5.1
.
∎

6.
Characterization of the Wulff shape

In this section, we will characterize the Wulff shape using an equation involving linear combinations of curvature measures with positive coefficients. We first prove an inequality of Heintze–Karcher type.

6.1.
An inequality of Heintze–Karcher type

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For any convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we have

(6.1)

(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K)\leq\int_{\partial K}\frac{{\gamma}(\nu)}{E_{1}}d\mu(p),

where
\mathrm{Vol}(K)=\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(K)
denotes the volume of
K
and
E_{1}=E_{1}(\kappa)
is the anisotropic mean curvature of
\partial K
defined for almost every point of
\partial K
. Moreover, the equality holds in (
6.1
) if and only if the anisotropic principal curvatures satisfy
\kappa_{1}(p)=\cdots=\kappa_{n}(p)=r(K,p)^{-1}>0
almost everywhere.

Remark 6.1
.

On the right-hand side of the inequality (
6.1
), the integrand
\gamma(\nu)/E_{1}
is well defined on
\widetilde{\partial K}
, the set of twice differentiable points; while on
\partial K\setminus\widetilde{\partial K}
which has zero
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
\gamma(\nu)/E_{1}
can be assigned any finite value. The inequality (
6.1
) for a smooth
K
was first proved in
[
22
]
(see also
[
12
,
28
,
45
]
for alternative proofs).

Proof.

Recall that the set
\mathscr{Z}(K)=\{p\in\partial K:r(K,p)=0\}
consists of the points on
\partial K
with zero anisotropic interior reach. So by the representation (
5.1
) for the volume of
K
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\int_{\partial K}r(K,p)^{n+1-i}d\Phi_{i}(K;p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n+1}{i}\int_{\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)}r(K,p)^{n+1-i}d\Phi_{i}(K;p).

On the other hand, since on
\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)
the anisotropic unit normal is unique, we obtain that

d\Phi_{i}(K;p)=\psi(v)E_{n-i}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v=E_{n-i}(v(p)){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)

holds on
(\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K))\cap\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K})
. In addition, by Lemma
5.2
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
p\in(\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K))\cap\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K})
, the parallel body
K^{\varepsilon}
has an interior touching Wulff shape with radius
r(K,p)+\varepsilon
at
p+\varepsilon v(p)
, which implies

r(K,p)+\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{i}(p+\varepsilon v(p))},\>i=1,\dots,n.

Sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+
, by the definition of the anisotropic principal curvatures on
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
we get

(6.2)

r(K,p)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{i}(v(p))},\>i=1,\dots,n.

Hence
r(K,p)\leq 1/{E_{1}(v(p))}
. From the inequality above we also know that all
\kappa_{i}(v(p))
are finite for
p\in(\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K))\cap\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K})
. In addition, by the arithmetic–geometric means inequality,

(6.3)

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\kappa_{i}(v)t)\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\kappa_{i}(v)t)\right)^{n}=(1-E_{1}(v)t)^{n},\quad 0\leq t\leq r(K,p).

Combining these facts together and noting that
\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathscr{Z}(K))=0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)}\int_{0}^{r(K,p)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-t)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}E_{n-i}(v(p)){\gamma}(\nu)dtd\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)}\int_{0}^{r(K,p)}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(1-\kappa_{i}(v(p))t){\gamma}(\nu)dtd\mu(p)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)}\int_{0}^{1/E_{1}(v(p))}(1-E_{1}(v(p))t)^{n}{\gamma}(\nu)dtd\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K\setminus\mathscr{Z}(K)}\frac{{\gamma}(\nu)}{E_{1}(v(p))}d\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}\frac{{\gamma}(\nu)}{E_{1}(v(p))}d\mu(p).

This proves the inequality (
6.1
). The equality case follows from the two inequalities (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) becoming equalities.
∎

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Now we prove Theorem
1.2
, which is recalled here.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape with the support function
{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
. For a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, assume that the anisotropic curvature measures satisfy

(6.4)

\Phi_{n}(K;\cdot)=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}\Phi_{r}(K;\cdot)

for some constants
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}\geq 0
. Then
K
is a scaled Wulff shape.

Proof.

First we apply the scaling property of the anisotropic curvature measures to get

\Phi_{n}(\alpha K;\cdot)=\alpha^{n}\Phi_{n}(K;\cdot{})=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}\alpha^{n-r}\Phi_{r}(\alpha K;\cdot)

for
\alpha>0
. Since
\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}\neq 0
, we can assume

(6.5)

\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}=1.

Recall the definition (
3.13
) of the anisotropic curvature measure

(6.6)

\displaystyle\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)=\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

for
0\leq r\leq n
and a Borel set
\beta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. At any point
v\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0}
, we can write the anisotropic principal curvature
\kappa_{i}(v)
as
\kappa_{i}(p)
with
p=\Pi(v)\in\partial K
, and
v
is the unique anisotropic unit normal of
\partial K
at the point
p
. Since the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\partial K\setminus\widetilde{\partial K}
is zero and by Proposition
3.7
the set
\widetilde{\partial K}
is contained in
\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})
, we have the following decomposition

\displaystyle\Phi_{r}(K;\beta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v+\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\beta\cap\Pi(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}E_{n-r}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)+\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

(6.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\beta\cap\partial K}E_{n-r}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)+\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

for
0\leq r\leq n
. Let us define the singular part

a_{r}(K;\beta):=\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v.

Similarly, we define

b_{r}(K;\beta):=\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\beta\cap\partial K)\cap(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0})}\psi(v)S(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v.

Then the Minkowski formula (
4.2
) can be written as

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}E_{r-1}(p)\gamma(\nu)d\mu(p)+a_{n-r+1}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}S(p)E_{r}(p)\gamma(\nu)d\mu(p)+b_{n-r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

for any integer
1\leq r\leq n
, where
a_{n-r+1}
and
b_{n-r}
are singular parts of the formula. We write the regular parts as integrations over
\partial K
, but we need to keep in mind that the integrands are only defined in the smooth part of
\partial K
and that we have set the integrands to be any finite value on
\partial K\setminus\widetilde{\partial K}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For a convex body
K
, an integer
r\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and a constant
\lambda>0
, assume that

\Phi_{r}(K;\cdot)\leq\lambda\Phi_{n}(K;\cdot).

Then
a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0
and we can choose the origin such that
b_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0
.

Proof.

For the first assertion, to get a contradiction, let us assume

\displaystyle a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v>0.

Then there exists a subset
B\subset\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K,0}
with
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(B)>0
such that
\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)>0
on
B
. Then we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\int_{B}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Pi^{-1}(\Pi(B))\cap\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{K}}\psi(v)E_{n-r}(v)d\tilde{\mathcal{H}}^{n}v

\displaystyle=\Phi_{r}(K;\Pi(B))\leq\lambda\Phi_{n}(K;\Pi(B)).

By (
3.18
), the last term is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(K;\Pi(B))=\int_{\Pi(B)\cap\widetilde{\partial K}}{\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p).

However, the set
\Pi(B)\cap\widetilde{\partial K}=\emptyset
by Proposition
3.7
. So
\Phi_{n}(K;\Pi(B))=0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore we have
a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0
.

For the second assertion, by translating the origin, we may assume that there exist
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
such that

r_{1}W\subset K\subset r_{2}W.

It follows that
r_{1}\leq S(v)\leq r_{2}
for
\forall v\in\mathcal{N}_{K}
. So by the definitions of
a_{r}
and
b_{r}
we have

0=r_{1}\>a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\leq b_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\leq r_{2}\>a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0,

which implies
b_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0
.
∎

For
\lambda_{r}>0
, by Lemma
6.3
we have
a_{r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=0
. It follows from (
6.4
) that

(6.9)

1=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}E_{r}(p),

almost everywhere on
\partial K
. Then

1\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}E_{1}^{r}(p),

which implies
E_{1}(p)\geq 1>0
a.e. on
\partial K
in view of (
6.5
).

Similarly, by Lemma
6.3
the singular part
b_{n-r}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
in the Minkowski formula (
6.8
) also vanishes for
\lambda_{n-r}>0
after choosing a suitable origin. Meanwhile, using the generalized divergence formula
[
14
, Thm. 4.5.6]
and the relationship between the anisotropic support function
S(p)
and the isotropic support function
\langle p,\nu\rangle
, we get

\displaystyle(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}\langle p,\nu\rangle d\mu(p)=\int_{\partial K}\frac{\langle p,\nu\rangle}{\gamma(\nu)}\gamma(\nu)d\mu(p)=\int_{\partial K}S(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p).

Then by (
6.9
) and the Minkowski formula (
6.8
) we derive

\displaystyle(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}\int_{\partial K}S(p)E_{r}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}\left(\int_{\partial K}E_{r-1}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)+a_{n-r+1}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}\int_{\partial K}E_{r-1}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p).

On the other hand, by the Heintze–Karcher type inequality (
6.1
),

\displaystyle(n+1)\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial K}\frac{{\gamma}(\nu)}{E_{1}(p)}d\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}\frac{{\gamma}(\nu)}{E_{1}(p)}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}E_{r}(p)\right)d\mu(p)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_{n-r}\int_{\partial K}E_{r-1}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p),

where we used the inequality
E_{r}(p)\leq E_{r-1}(p)E_{1}(p)
. So the equality must hold, and
\kappa_{1}(p)=\cdots=\kappa_{n}(p)=r(K,p)^{-1}=1
almost everywhere on
\partial K
. This implies that the anisotropic area of the boundary
\partial K
satisfies

\displaystyle|\partial K|_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}{\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)=\int_{\partial K}E_{n}(p){\gamma}(\nu)d\mu(p)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})-a_{0}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{0}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n+1})=|\partial W|_{\gamma},

where in the third equality we used the decomposition (
6.2
) and in the last equality we used the fact (
3.19
). Since the anisotropic reach of
K
is 1 almost everywhere,
K
contains a Wulff shape of radius
1
and we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(K)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Vol}(W).

Combining the above two inequalities gives rise to

\left(\frac{|\partial K|_{\gamma}}{|\partial W|_{\gamma}}\right)^{n+1}\leq\left(\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(K)}{\mathrm{Vol}(W)}\right)^{n},

which together with the equality case of the anisotropic isoperimetric inequality (
2.17
) (with
\ell=n
) implies that
K
is a scaled Wulff shape. This completes the proof of Theorem
6.2
.
∎

7.2.
Monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio

We next show that the flow (
1.9
) decreases the
(n+1-k)

{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)=\frac{V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)^{n+1}}{V_{n+1}(K_{t},W)^{n+1-k}V_{0}(K_{t},W)^{k}}

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
M_{t}=X(M,t)=\partial K_{t}
be a smooth strictly convex solution of the flow (
1.9
) on
[0,T)
with the global term
\phi(t)
given by (
1.10

V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is non-increasing in

{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is monotone non-increasing in

.

Proof.

By the definition (

) of

, the volume of the enclosed

remains to be a constant. Since

is smooth and convex, we can write the mixed volume

as in (

), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-k+1)}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)=~\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}(\phi(t)-{E}_{k}^{{\alpha}/k})d\mu_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}d\mu_{\gamma}\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}d\mu_{\gamma}-\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}^{\frac{\alpha}{k}+1}d\mu_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{M_{t}}\left({E}_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right){E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}d\mu_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{M_{t}}\left({E}_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)\left({E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)d\mu_{\gamma}

(7.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~0,

where

denotes the average integral of

. This says that

is non-increasing in time. Since

are constants, we conclude that the isoperimetric ratio

is non-increasing in

.
∎

The monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio together with the isoperimetric inequality (
2.17
) implies that

1\leq{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)\leq{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{0},W)

along the flow (
1.9
). This can be used to control the anisotropic area and shape of the evolving hypersurfaces
M_{t}
. Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with boundary

. The anisotropic outer radius of
K
(or
M
) relative to
W
is defined as

R(K):=\inf\{\rho>0:~K\subset\rho W+p~\mathrm{for}~\mathrm{some}~\mathrm{point}~p\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}.

The anisotropic inner radius of
K
relative to
W
is defined as

r(K):=\sup\{\rho>0:~\rho W+p\subset K~\mathrm{for}~\mathrm{some}~\mathrm{point}~p\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}.

We denote by
r(t)
and
R(t)
the anisotropic inner radius and outer radius of the evolving set
K_{t}
under the flow (
1.9
), respectively. As a consequence of Proposition
7.3
, we have the following estimate on

and
R(t)
.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
M_{t}
be a smooth convex solution of the flow (
1.9
) on
[0,T)
with the global term
\phi(t)
given by (
1.10
). Then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},R_{1},R_{2}
depending only on

and

such that

(7.9)

0<c_{1}\leq|M_{t}|_{\gamma}\leq c_{2}

and

(7.10)

0<R_{1}\leq r(t)\leq R(t)\leq R_{2}

for all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

First, the isoperimetric inequality (
2.17
) and the preserving of the enclosed volume imply that

\displaystyle|M_{t}|_{\gamma}^{n+1}=

\displaystyle~V_{n}(K_{t},W)^{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~V_{n+1}(K_{t},W)^{n}V_{0}(K_{t},W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(n+1)^{n+1}\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})^{n}\mathrm{Vol}(W),

which gives the lower bound of
|M_{t}|_{\gamma}
. For the upper bound of
|M_{t}|_{\gamma}
: if

in the flow equation (
1.9
), the monotonicity of

gives the upper bound

; if

\displaystyle V_{n+1-k}^{n}(K_{0},W)\geq

\displaystyle~V_{n+1-k}^{n}(K_{t},W)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~V_{n}^{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)V_{0}^{k-1}(K_{t},W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~|M_{t}|_{\gamma}^{n+1-k}(n+1)^{k-1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)^{k-1},

which gives the upper bound of

. This proves the inequality (
7.9
).

In Proposition 5.1 of
[
2
]
, the first author proved that for any convex body

, there holds

(7.11)

\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(K)}{|M|_{\gamma}}~\leq~r(K)\leq R(K)\leq~\frac{|M|_{\gamma}^{n}}{(n+1)^{n-1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)\mathrm{Vol}(K)^{n-1}}.

Then the estimate (

) follows from the estimate (

) and the inequality (

∎

8.
Anisotropic Gauss map parametrization

In this section, we rewrite the flow (
1.9
) as a scalar parabolic equation on the Wulff shape
\Sigma=\partial W
of the anisotropic support function of the evolving hypersurface, and derive some evolution equations in this parametrization.

8.1.
Anisotropic Gauss map parametrization

We first review the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization of a smooth strictly convex hypersurface. Given a smooth strictly convex hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we assume that the origin lies inside
M
. The Gauss map is defined as
\nu:M\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
which maps the point
x\in M
to the outward unit normal
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
at this point. The Gauss map of a strictly convex hypersurface is a nondegenerate diffeomorphism between
M
and
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Since the Wulff shape
W
is also strictly convex, we can define a map from
\mathbb{S}^{n}
to
\Sigma=\partial W
which maps
\nu
to
\nu_{\gamma}=D{\gamma}(\nu)
. Then the anisotropic Gauss map is defined as
\nu_{\gamma}:M\to\Sigma
satisfying
\nu_{\gamma}(x)=D{\gamma}(\nu(x))
for any point
x\in M
.

The anisotropic Gauss map is a nondegenerate diffeomorphism and can be used to reparametrize a strictly convex hypersurface
M
(see
[
43
]
):

X:\Sigma\to M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\quad X(z)=X(\nu_{\gamma}^{-1}(z)),\quad z\in\Sigma.

The anisotropic support function of
M
is then defined as a function on
\Sigma
by

(8.1)

s(z)=\sup_{y\in M}G(z)(z,y)=G(z)(z,X(z))

for
z\in\Sigma
, where
G
is the metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
defined in (
2.4
). Let
\bar{g}
and
\bar{\nabla}
denote the induced metric and its Levi-Civita connection on
\Sigma
from
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},G)
respectively. Taking the first covariant derivative of
s
on
\Sigma
, we have

(8.2)

\bar{\nabla}_{i}s(z)=G(z)(\partial_{i}z,X(z))

in a local orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\},i=1,\dots,n
of
T_{z}\Sigma=T_{X(z)}M
. Taking the second covariant derivatives of
s
, Xia
[
43
]
obtained that the anisotropic principal radii of
M
are given by the eigenvalues
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})
of

(8.3)

\tau_{ij}=\tau_{ij}[s]=\bar{\nabla}_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{j}s+\bar{g}_{ij}s-\frac{1}{2}Q_{ijk}\bar{\nabla}_{k}s.

The anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})
are related to the anisotropic principal radii
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})
by

\kappa_{i}=\frac{1}{\tau_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

Moreover, any smooth function
s
on
\Sigma
satisfying
(\tau_{ij})>0
is an anisotropic support function of a smooth, closed and strictly convex hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

We have the following Codazzi and Simons type equations for

The proof of (

8.2.
Properties of symmetric functions

To derive the evolution equation of the anisotropic support function along the flow (
1.9
), we require some properties on smooth symmetric functions which we recall here. The readers can refer to
[
4
]
for more details.

For a smooth symmetric function
F(A)=f(\kappa(A))
, where
A=(A_{ij})\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is a symmetric matrix and
\kappa(A)=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})
give the eigenvalues of
A
, we denote by
\dot{F}^{ij}(A)
and
\ddot{F}^{ij,kl}(A)
the first and second derivatives of
F
with respect to the components of its argument. We also denote by
\dot{f}^{i}(\kappa)
and
\ddot{f}^{ij}(\kappa)
the first and second derivatives of
f
with respect to
\kappa
, respectively. At any diagonal
A
with distinct eigenvalues
\kappa=\kappa(A)
, the first derivative of
F
satisfies

\dot{F}^{ij}(A)~=~\dot{f}^{i}(\kappa)\delta_{ij}

and the second derivative of
F
in the direction
B\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is given in terms of
\dot{f}
and
\ddot{f}
by (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
):

(8.6)

\ddot{F}^{ij,k\ell}(A)B_{ij}B_{k\ell}=\sum_{i,j}\ddot{f}^{ij}(\kappa)B_{ii}B_{jj}+2\sum_{i>j}\frac{\dot{f}^{i}(\kappa)-\dot{f}^{j}(\kappa)}{\kappa_{i}-\kappa_{j}}B_{ij}^{2}.

This formula makes sense as a limit in the case of any repeated values of
\kappa_{i}
.

We say that
f
is concave if its Hessian is non-positive definite. From the equation (
8.6
), we know that
F
is concave at
A
if and only if
f
is concave at
\kappa
and

\left(\dot{f}^{k}-\dot{f}^{\ell}\right)(\kappa_{k}-\kappa_{\ell})~\leq~0,\quad\forall~k\neq\ell.

Moreover, if
f
is concave, homogeneous of degree one and is normalized such that
f(1,\dots,1)=1
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{f}^{i}\geq 1
.

We say that
f
is
inverse-concave
, if the dual function

f_{*}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=f(x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{n}^{-1})^{-1}

is concave. Note that the dual function
f_{*}
is only defined on the positive definite cone
\Gamma_{+}

\Gamma_{+}=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ ~x_{i}>0,~i=1,\dots,n\}.

The functions

E_{k}^{1/k},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad(E_{k}/{E_{\ell}})^{1/{(k-\ell)}},~k>\ell

are important examples of concave and inverse-concave symmetric functions.

8.3.

The anisotropic support function introduced in §
8.1
allows the degenerate parabolic system of Equation (

) to be written as a parabolic scalar equation (see, e.g.,
[
39
,
44
]
). Let
F=E_{k}^{1/k}
and define its dual function

by

\displaystyle F_{*}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=

\displaystyle F(x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{n}^{-1})^{-1}=~\left(\frac{E_{n}(x)}{E_{n-k}(x)}\right)^{1/k}

for

is concave in its arguments.

Lemma 8.2
.

The solution of the flow (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bar{X}(z,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial X}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial(\nu_{\gamma}^{-1})^{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{X}(\nu_{\gamma}^{-1}(z,t),t),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s(z,t)=

\displaystyle G(z)(z,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bar{X}(z,t))

\displaystyle G(z)(z,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{X}(\nu_{\gamma}^{-1}(z,t),t))

\displaystyle\phi(t)-E_{k}^{\alpha/k}(\kappa),

Applying the implicit function theorem to (

of
M_{t}
with respect to
p_{t}
satisfies

(9.1)

R_{1}\leq s(z,t)\leq R_{2},

where
R_{1},R_{2}
are positive constants depending only on
n,k,{\gamma},\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})
and
V_{n+1-k}(K_{0},W)
. The

estimate (

together with the strict convexity of

also implies a bound on the gradient of the anisotropic support function
s

|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}(z,t)\leq C(R_{2}-R_{1})

for all

. The

estimate (

) in terms of the

estimate does not depend on the flow and holds for any strictly convex hypersurface.

Lemma 9.1
.

Assume that
M
is a strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the anisotropic support function
s(z)
with respect to some point
o
satisfying

(9.3)

R_{1}\leq s(z)\leq R_{2},\quad\forall~z\in\Sigma

for some positive constants
R_{1},R_{2}
. Then the gradient of
s
satisfies

(9.4)

|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}(z)\leq C(R_{2}-R_{1})

for all
z\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

This estimate (
9.4
) follows from the estimate (
9.3
) and the equations (
8.1
) and (
8.2
) for strictly convex hypersurfaces directly. Alternatively, we consider the function (cf.
[
44
, Proposition 4.3]
)

\omega(z)=\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}

on
\Sigma
, where
\delta>0
is an arbitrary constant. Suppose that the maximum of
\omega(z)
is achieved at
z_{0}\in\Sigma
. Then at the point
z_{0}
, there holds

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\bar{\nabla}_{i}s\bar{\nabla}_{i}\omega=\frac{2s_{i}s_{k}s_{ki}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}+\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|^{4}}{(R_{2}+\delta-s)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2s_{i}s_{k}\tau_{ki}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}-\frac{2s|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}+\frac{Q_{kip}s_{i}s_{k}s_{p}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}+\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|^{4}}{(R_{2}+\delta-s)^{2}}

(9.5)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}}{(R_{2}+\delta-s)^{2}}\left(|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}-(2s+C|\bar{\nabla}s|)(R_{2}+\delta-s)\right),

where
C
is a constant depending on
Q
, the notation
s_{i},s_{ki}
denotes the covariant derivatives of
s
on
\Sigma
with respect to the metric
\bar{g}
, and we used (
8.3
) in the third equality. It follows from (
9
) that

\omega(z_{0})=\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}}{R_{2}+\delta-s}\leq 2s+C|\bar{\nabla}s|.

If
2s\geq C|\bar{\nabla}s|
at
z_{0}
, then

\omega(z_{0})\leq 4s\leq 4R_{2}.

If
2s\leq C|\bar{\nabla}s|
at
z_{0}
, then

\frac{|\bar{\nabla}s|}{R_{2}+\delta-s}\leq 2C,

and

\omega(z_{0})\leq(2C)^{2}(R_{2}+\delta-s(z_{0}))\leq 4C^{2}(R_{2}+\delta-R_{1}).

Hence, there exists a constant
C=C(Q,R_{1},R_{2})
such that
\omega(z)\leq C
for all
z\in\Sigma
. This implies that

|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}=(R_{2}+\delta-s(z))\omega(z)\leq C(R_{2}+\delta-R_{1})

for all
z\in\Sigma
. Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, the estimate (
9.4
) follows.
∎

The estimate (
9.1
) depends on the origin
p_{t}
we choose. The following lemma shows the existence of a ball with a fixed center enclosed by our flow hypersurfaces on a suitable fixed time interval.

Lemma 9.2
.

For any time
t_{0}\in[0,T)
, let
p_{0}
be the center such that the estimate (
9.1
) holds on
M_{t_{0}}
. Then there exists some positive
\tau
depending only on
n,k,{\gamma},\alpha,\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})
and
V_{n+1-k}(K_{0},W)
such that the anisotropic support function
s(z,t)
of
M_{t}
with respect to the center
p_{0}
satisfies

(9.6)

s(z,t)\geq R_{1}/2,\qquad\forall~t\in[t_{0},\min\{T,t_{0}+\tau\}).

Proof.

Let
\underline{s}(t)=\min_{\Sigma}s(\cdot,t)=s(z_{t},t)
. At the point
z_{t}
, we have

\tau_{ij}[s(z_{t},t)]\geq\underline{s}(t)\bar{g}_{ij}.

Then the evolution equation (
8.7
) of
s(z,t)
implies that

\frac{d}{dt}\underline{s}(t)\geq-\underline{s}(t)^{-\alpha}+\phi(t)\geq-\underline{s}(t)^{-\alpha}.

By the initial condition
\underline{s}(t_{0})\geq R_{1}
, we solve the above inequality as follows

\underline{s}(t)\geq\left(R_{1}^{1+\alpha}-(\alpha+1)(t-t_{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\geq R_{1}/2,

provided that

9.6
) follows immediately.
∎

We now estimate the upper bound on the speed of the flow.

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
M_{t}
be a smooth convex solution of the flow (
1.9
) on
[0,T)
with the global term

\max_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}\leq~C\left(1+t^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right)

n,k,{\gamma},\alpha,\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})

The proof is by applying the technique of Tso
[
38
]
. For any given time

9.6
) implies that the anisotropic support function
s(z,t)
with respect to the center
p_{0}

in the time interval

9.6
) together with (

with respect to the center

) and (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Z-

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}Z-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}Z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F_{*}^{-\alpha}-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}F_{*}^{-\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}F_{*}^{-\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{Z}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}s\right)\right)+2\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\frac{\bar{\nabla}_{k}Z\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\frac{\bar{\nabla}_{k}Z\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}+\frac{1}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}(F_{*}^{-\alpha}-\phi(t))\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}

\displaystyle-\frac{Z}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}\left(\phi(t)+(1+\alpha)\Psi+s\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}\right)

(9.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\frac{\bar{\nabla}_{k}Z\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s}{s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1}}-\frac{R_{1}Z}{4(s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1})}\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}+(1+\alpha)Z^{2}-\phi(t)\frac{\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}+Z}{s-\frac{1}{2}R_{1}}.

The second term on the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\frac{R_{1}Z}{4(s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1})}\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha R_{1}Z}{4(s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1})}F_{*}^{-\alpha-1}\dot{F}_{*}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha R_{1}Z}{4(s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1})}F_{*}^{-\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha R_{1}}{4}Z^{2+\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s-\frac{1}{4}R_{1})^{1/{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\frac{R_{1}}{4}\right)^{1+1/{\alpha}}Z^{2+\frac{1}{\alpha}},

where we used the fact
\dot{F}_{*}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}=\sum_{i}\dot{f}^{i}_{*}\geq 1
due to the concavity of
F_{*}
. Let
\tilde{Z}(t)=\sup Z(\cdot,t)
. Since

, Equation (
9
) implies that (in the form given in
[
20
, Lemma 3.5]
)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{Z}\leq

\displaystyle-\alpha\left(\frac{R_{1}}{4}\right)^{1+1/{\alpha}}\tilde{Z}^{2+\frac{1}{\alpha}}+(1+\alpha)\tilde{Z}^{2}.

Let

\tilde{Z}_{0}(\alpha,R_{1})=\left(\frac{4}{R_{1}}\right)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha},

which is a positive constant depending only on
\alpha
and

. Whenever

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{Z}\leq

\displaystyle-(1+\alpha)\tilde{Z}^{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left(\frac{4}{R_{1}}\right)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha},\left(\tilde{Z}(t_{0})^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}}(t-t_{0})\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C\left(1+(t-t_{0})^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\right)

for all
t\in(t_{0},\min\{T,t_{0}+\tau\})
, where

depends only on
n,k,{\gamma},\alpha,\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})
and
V_{n+1-k}(K_{0},W)
. The upper bound of

follows from the bound on

and the fact

. Proposition
9.3
follows since

is arbitrary.
∎

Let

0<R_{1}/2\leq s(z,t)\leq 2R_{2}
for all
t\in[t_{0},\min\{T,t_{0}+\tau\})
, this also implies that the largest anisotropic principal radius
\tau_{1}
is bounded by a constant depending on its value at the time
t_{0}
and on the parameter
\tau
. If the maximal existence time
T
is finite, after a finite number of iterations, we conclude that the largest anisotropic principal radius
\tau_{1}
is bounded from above by a positive constant
C
depending on the maximal existence time
T
. This completes the proof of Proposition
9.5
.
∎

Now we can prove the long time existence of the flow (
1.9
), i.e., the maximum existence time
T=\infty
.

Theorem 9.6
.

Let
W
be a Wulff shape in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the smooth support function
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and
X_{0}:M^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a smooth embedding such that
M_{0}=X_{0}(M)
is a closed strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
enclosing a convex body
K_{0}
. Then for
any
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\alpha>0
, the volume preserving flow (
1.9
) with the global term
\phi(t)
given by (
1.10
) has a smooth strictly convex solution
M_{t}
for all time
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Suppose that the maximal existence time
T
is finite. By Proposition
9.3
and Proposition
9.5
, there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
M_{0},{\gamma}
and
T
such that the anisotropic principal curvatures satisfy

0<\frac{1}{C}\leq\kappa_{i}\leq C,\qquad i=1,\cdots,n

on
M_{t}
for
t\in[0,T)
. This together with
C^{0}
and
C^{1}
estimates implies the
C^{2}
estimate of the evolving hypersurface
M_{t}
. To derive the higher regularity estimate, we note that the flow (
1.9
) is equivalent to the equation (
8.7
) for the anisotropic support function
s(z,t)
. Since
F_{*}
is concave and
\alpha>0
, the right-hand side of (
8.7
) is concave with respect to the spatial second derivatives of
s(z,t)
. As the global term
\phi(t)
is bounded, Theorem 1.1 of
[
37
]
can be applied to derive the
C^{2,\beta}
estimate of
s
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. The higher order regularity estimate of
M_{t}
follows from the standard parabolic Schauder theory
[
26
]
. Thus
M_{t}
converges to a hypersurface
M_{T}
smoothly as
t\to T
. The short time existence theorem yields that the flow (
1.9
) can be extended past
T
for a short time interval, contradicting the maximality of
T
.
∎

10.
Hausdorff convergence

We showed in the previous section that the smooth solution
M_{t}
of the flow (
1.9
) exists for all time
t\in[0,\infty)
. In this section, using Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
3.10
for the anisotropic curvature measures and applying the argument as in
[
6
, §6]
, we show that the solution
M_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape as
t\to\infty
in the Hausdorff sense. As applications, we further improve the estimate on the global term
\phi(t)
and obtain the uniform positive lower bound on the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature
E_{k}
.

10.1.
Hausdorff convergence to the Wulff shape

Let

\bar{E}_{k}=~\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}d\mu_{\gamma}=~\frac{V_{n-k}(K_{t},W)}{V_{n}(K_{t},W)}

denote the average integral of
E_{k}
. By Proposition
7.3
, the mixed volume
V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is monotone non-increasing in time. Since
V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is bounded from below, by the long-time existence of the flow, the equation (
7.2
) implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{M_{t}}\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)\left(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)d\mu_{\gamma}~\leq~V_{n+1-k}(K_{0},W)<\infty.

Therefore there exists a sequence of times
t_{i}\to\infty
such that

(10.1)

\displaystyle\int_{M_{t_{i}}}\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)\left(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)d\mu_{\gamma}~\to 0.

If
0<\alpha<k
, we have

\displaystyle\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)\left(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-s)\bar{E}_{k}+sE_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\cdot(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\left(\sup_{M_{t}}E_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

by the upper bound on
E_{k}
that proved in Proposition
9.3
. On the other hand, for
\alpha>k
we have

\displaystyle\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)\left(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-s)\bar{E}_{k}+sE_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\cdot(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k-1}(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})^{2}

by Corollary
9.4
. Therefore, (
10.1
) implies that

(10.2)

\displaystyle\int_{M_{t_{i}}}\left({E}_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}\right)^{2}d\mu_{\gamma}~\to

\displaystyle~0,\quad\mathrm{as}~i\to\infty

for a sequence of times
t_{i}\to\infty
.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
M_{t}
be the smooth solution to the flow (
1.9
) with the global term
\phi(t)
given by (
1.10
). Denote the closure of the enclosed domain of
M_{t}
by
K_{t}
. Then
K_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
in the Hausdorff sense as
t\to\infty
, where
\bar{r}
is the radius such that
\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})=\bar{r}^{n+1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to Lemma 6.3 in
[
6
]
. We include it here for completeness. By the estimate (
7.10
) on the anisotropic outer radius of
K_{t}
, the Blaschke selection theorem (see Theorem 1.8.7 of
[
33
]
) implies that there exists a subsequence of times
t_{i}
and a convex body
\hat{K}
such that
K_{t_{i}}
converges to
\hat{K}
in the Hausdorff sense as
t_{i}\to\infty
. As each
K_{t_{i}}
has the anisotropic inner radius
r(K_{t_{i}})\geq R_{1}
, the limit convex body
\hat{K}
has a positive anisotropic inner radius. Without loss of generality, we may assume that the sequence
t_{i}
is the same sequence such that (
10.2
) holds.

We will show that the limit convex body
\hat{K}
satisfies
\Phi_{n-k}(\hat{K};.)=c\,\Phi_{n}(\hat{K};.)
where
c=V_{n-k}(\hat{K},W)/V_{n}(\hat{K},W)
. The weak continuity of the anisotropic curvature measure
\Phi_{m}
proved in Theorem
3.10
is equivalent to the statement that
\int fd\Phi_{m}(K_{i})
converges to
\int fd\Phi_{m}(K)
whenever
f
is a bounded continuous function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
and
K_{i}
is a sequence of convex sets converging to
K
in the Hausdorff distance. In particular we get that
\int fd\Phi_{n-k}(K_{t_{i}})
converges to
\int fd\Phi_{n-k}(\hat{K})
, and
\int fd\Phi_{n}(K_{t_{i}})
converges to
\int fd\Phi_{n}(\hat{K})
, as
i\to\infty
. Since
K_{t_{i}}
is smooth and uniformly convex, by (
3.16
) we obtain for any bounded continuous
f
,

\displaystyle\left|\int fd\Phi_{n-k}(K_{t_{i}})-c\int fd\Phi_{n}(K_{t_{i}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{M_{t_{i}}}fE_{k}\gamma(\nu)d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}-\int_{M_{t_{i}}}fc\gamma(\nu)d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup|f|\int_{M_{t_{i}}}|E_{k}-c|\gamma(\nu)d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sup|f|\int_{M_{t_{i}}}|E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k}|\gamma(\nu)d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}

\displaystyle\quad\hbox{}+\sup|f|V_{n}(K_{t_{i}},W)\left|\frac{V_{n-k}(K_{t_{i}},W)}{V_{n}(K_{t_{i}},W)}-\frac{V_{n-k}(\hat{K},W)}{V_{n}(\hat{K},W)}\right|.

The left-hand side converges to
\left|\int fd\Phi_{n-k}(\hat{K})-c\int fd\Phi_{n}(\hat{K})\right|
by the weak continuity of the anisotropic curvature measures, while the first term on the right-hand side converges to zero by (
10.2
), and the second also does by the continuity of the mixed volumes with respect to the Hausdorff distance. It follows that

\int fd\Phi_{n-k}(\hat{K})=c\int fd\Phi_{n}(\hat{K})

for all bounded continuous functions
f
, and therefore that
\Phi_{n-k}(\hat{K};.)=c\,\Phi_{n}(\hat{K};.)
as claimed. By Theorem
1.2
, the convex body
\hat{K}
is a scaled Wulff shape.

By Proposition
7.3
,
{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is non-increasing in time. Since
{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t_{i}},W)\to 1
as
i\to\infty
for some subsequence of times
t_{i}\to\infty
, we conclude that
{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)\to 1
as
t\to\infty
. It follows from the stability estimate (7.124) in
[
33
]
that the whole family of
K_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
as
t\to\infty
, where
\bar{r}
is the radius such that
\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})=\bar{r}^{n+1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)
. This completes the proof of Proposition
10.1
.
∎

10.2.
Improved estimate on the global term
\phi(t)

The Hausdorff convergence of the solution has the following consequence on the global term
\phi(t)
in the flow equation (
1.9
).

Corollary 10.2
.

If
\alpha\geq k
in the flow equation (
1.9
), the global term
\phi(t)
given by (
1.10
) satisfies

(10.3)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\phi(t)\geq\bar{r}^{-\alpha},

where
\bar{r}
is the radius such that
\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})=\bar{r}^{n+1}\mathrm{Vol}(W)
. In general, for any
\alpha>0
, the global term
\phi(t)
satisfies

(10.4)

\int_{t}^{t+d}\biggl|\phi(s)-\frac{1}{\bar{r}^{\alpha}}\biggr|ds~\to~0

as
t\to\infty
for any fixed constant
d>0
.

Proof.

If
\alpha\geq k
, then by the Hölder inequality we get

\phi(t)=\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}d\mu_{\gamma}\geq\left(\frac{\int_{M_{t}}{E}_{k}d\mu_{\gamma}}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\right)^{\alpha/k}=\left(\frac{V_{n-k}(K_{t},W)}{V_{n}(K_{t},W)}\right)^{\alpha/k}.

Since
K_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
in the Hausdorff sense as
t\to\infty
, the continuity of the mixed volumes implies the estimate (
10.3
).

To show (
10.4
) for a general
\alpha>0
, as at the beginning of this section we note that

\displaystyle\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-s)\bar{E}_{k}+sE_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\cdot(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right).

If
\alpha>k
,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-s)\bar{E}_{k}+sE_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\left(\sup_{M_{t}}E_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}.

If
0<\alpha<k
,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-s)\bar{E}_{k}+sE_{k}\right)^{\alpha/k-1}ds\leq

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k-1}\frac{\alpha}{k}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{\alpha/k-1}ds.

In both cases, we obtain

(10.5)

\displaystyle\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C(E_{k}-\bar{E}_{k})\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right),

where
C=C(n,k,\alpha,\gamma,K_{0})
comes from the bounds on
E_{k},\bar{E}_{k}
in Proposition
9.3
and Corollary
9.4
. Since
V_{n+1-k}(K_{t},W)
is non-increasing and converges to the value
V_{n+1-k}(\bar{r}W,W)
, for any small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a large time
N_{0}
such that for all times
t_{1}>t_{2}>N_{0}
,

V_{n+1-k}(K_{t_{2}},W)-V_{n+1-k}(K_{t_{1}},W)\leq\varepsilon.

In particular, for any fixed constant
d>0
the estimates (
7.2
) and (
10.5
) imply that

(10.6)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+d}\int_{M_{s}}\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)^{2}d\mu_{\gamma}ds\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon

for all
t>N_{0}
, where
C
depends on
n,k,\alpha,\gamma,K_{0}
. Applying the Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\biggl|\phi(t)-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\biggr|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\biggl|\int_{M_{t}}\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)d\mu_{\gamma}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|M_{t}|_{\gamma}}\int_{M_{t}}\left(E_{k}^{\alpha/k}-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\right)^{2}d\mu_{\gamma}\right)^{1/2}

and the uniform bounds on
|M_{t}|_{\gamma}
, we have

(10.7)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+d}\biggl|\phi(s)-\bar{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}\biggr|ds\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{1/2}.

By Proposition
10.1
,
K_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
as
t\to\infty
. The continuity of the mixed volume
V_{n-k}(\cdot,W)
with respect to the Hausdorff distance implies that

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{k}=

\displaystyle~\frac{V_{n-k}(K_{t},W)}{V_{n}(K_{t},W)}\to~\frac{V_{n-k}(\bar{r}W,W)}{V_{n}(\bar{r}W,W)}=\bar{r}^{-k},\quad\mathrm{as}~t\to\infty.

Then we conclude from (
10.7
) that for any fixed constant
d>0
and any small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a large
N_{0}
such that

(10.8)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+d}\biggl|\phi(s)-\bar{r}^{-\alpha}\biggr|ds\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{1/2}

for all
t>N_{0}
, where
C
is a uniform constant depending only on
n,k,\alpha,\gamma
and
K_{0}
.
∎

10.3.
Lower bound on
{E}_{k}

In this subsection, we prove a uniform positive lower bound on the
k
th anisotropic mean curvature
E_{k}
. The key is the Hausdorff convergence of
K_{t}
to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
as
t\to\infty
. We also use the uniform two-sided positive bounds on the global term
\phi(t)
.

Proposition 10.3
.

Let
M_{t}
,
t\in[0,\infty),
be a smooth solution of the flow (
1.9
). Then there exists a positive constant
C=C(n,k,\alpha,\gamma,K_{0})
such that
{E}_{k}\geq C
on
M_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to control
{E}_{k}
from below for sufficiently large times. By Proposition
10.1
,
K_{t}
converges to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}W
as
t\to\infty
in the Hausdorff sense. Then for any small
\epsilon>0
, there exists a sufficiently large time
N_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq N_{0}
, the anisotropic outer radius
R(t)
and anisotropic inner radius
r(t)
of
K_{t}
relative to
W
satisfy

(10.9)

(1-\epsilon)\bar{r}~\leq~r(t)\leq~R(t)\leq~(1+\epsilon)\bar{r}.

In the following, we only consider times
t\geq N_{0}
.

Let
t_{1}>N_{0}
and set

\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)=\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\phi(s)ds.

We use the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization described in §
8
. Choose the origin to be the center of the inner Wulff shape such that
r(t_{1})W\subset K_{t_{1}}
.
Then

s(z,t_{1})\geq~s(z_{1},t_{1})=r(t_{1})\geq~(1-\epsilon)\bar{r},

where
z_{1}
is the minimum point of
s(z,t_{1})
. Define a function
f(z,t)
on
\Sigma=\partial W
by

f(z,t)~=~-(1+\alpha)(t-t_{1})\Psi(z,t)-(1+\alpha)\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)+s(z,t)

for
t\geq t_{1}
. Combining (
8.7
) and (
8.11
) gives the evolution equation of
f(z,t)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(z,t)=

\displaystyle~\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}(z,t)\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}f(z,t)-\frac{1}{2}\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}(z,t)Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}f-\alpha\phi(t)

(10.10)

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl(s(z,t)-(1+\alpha)(t-t_{1})(\Psi+\phi(t))\biggr)\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}.

We now show that the last term on the right-hand side of (
10.3
) is nonnegative for a short time after time
t_{1}
. By Theorem
9.3
,
\Psi=-{E}_{k}^{\alpha/k}
is bounded below by a uniform negative constant
-c_{+}
. On the other hand, by Corollary
9.4
the function
\phi(t)
is bounded from above and below by positive constants:

0<\phi_{-}\leq\phi(t)\leq~\phi_{+},

where
\phi_{-},\phi_{+}
depend only on
n,k,{\gamma},\alpha
and
K_{0}
. Then integrating the evolution equation (
8.7
) of
s(z,t)
gives that

\displaystyle s(z,t)\geq

\displaystyle~s(z,t_{1})+(\phi_{-}-c_{+})(t-t_{1})\geq(1-\epsilon)\bar{r}+(\phi_{-}-c_{+})(t-t_{1}).

Let
t_{2}>t_{1}
be the time such that

(10.11)

t_{2}-t_{1}=~\frac{(1-\epsilon)\bar{r}}{c_{+}-\phi_{-}+(1+\alpha)\phi_{+}},

which is positive and independent of the time
t_{1}
. Then for any
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, we have

\displaystyle s(z,t)-(1+\alpha)(t-t_{1})(\Psi(z,t)+\phi(t))

(10.12)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\epsilon)\bar{r}+(\phi_{-}-c_{+})(t-t_{1})-(1+\alpha)(t-t_{1})\phi_{+}\geq~0.

Since
\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}\geq 0
, combining (
10.3
) with (
10.3
) gives that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(z,t)\geq

\displaystyle~\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}(z,t)\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}f(z,t)-\frac{1}{2}\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}(z,t)Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}f-\alpha\phi(t)

for all time
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
. The maximum principle implies that

\displaystyle f(z,t)\geq

\displaystyle\min_{M_{t_{1}}}f(z,t_{1})-\alpha\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)=~s(z_{1},t_{1})-\alpha\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)

for all time
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, which implies that

(10.13)

-(1+\alpha)\Psi(z,t)\geq~\frac{\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)-s(z,t)+s(z_{1},t_{1})}{t-t_{1}}

for all
t\in(t_{1},t_{2}]
with
t_{2}
defined in (
10.11
).

To estimate the lower bound of
-\Psi
, it remains to estimate the right-hand side of (
10.13
). Recall that the origin is chosen such that
r(t_{1})W\subset K_{t_{1}}
. Then by
[
2
, Lem. 7.2]
, we have

(10.14)

s(z,t_{1})\leq R(t_{1})+C(R(t_{1})-r(t_{1}))\leq(1+(1+2C)\epsilon)\bar{r},\quad\forall~z\in\Sigma,

where
C
is a constant only depending on
\gamma
.

From the flow equation (
8.7
), we deduce that the spatial maximum
\bar{s}(t)=\max_{\Sigma}s(\cdot,t)
of the anisotropic support function
s
satisfies

(10.15)

\frac{d}{dt}\bar{s}(t)=~-F_{*}({\tau}_{ij})^{-\alpha}+\phi(t)\leq~\phi_{+},

which implies that

\displaystyle\bar{s}(t)\leq

\displaystyle~\bar{s}(t_{1})+\phi_{+}(t-t_{1})~\leq~2\bar{s}(t_{1}),\quad\forall~t\in[t_{1},t_{3}],

where
t_{3}
is given by
t_{3}-t_{1}=\phi_{+}^{-1}{\bar{s}}(t_{1})
. By the definition of
\tau_{ij}
, at the maximum point of
s(\cdot,t)
, we have
{\tau}_{ij}\leq\bar{g}_{ij}s
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)-\bar{s}(t)+s(z_{1},t_{1})\big)=

\displaystyle F_{*}({\tau}_{ij})^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\bar{s}(t)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~2^{-\alpha}\bar{s}(t_{1})^{-\alpha},\quad\forall~t\in[t_{1},t_{3}],

which implies that

\displaystyle\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)-\bar{s}(t)+s(z_{1},t_{1})\geq

\displaystyle~-\bar{s}(t_{1})+s(z_{1},t_{1})+2^{-\alpha}\bar{s}(t_{1})^{-\alpha}(t-t_{1})

(10.16)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~2^{-1-\alpha}\bar{s}(t_{1})^{-\alpha}(t-t_{1}),

provided that

t\geq~t_{1}+2^{1+\alpha}r_{+}^{\alpha}(\bar{s}(t_{1})-s(z_{1},t_{1}))~=:t_{4}.

By (
10.9
) and (
10.14
), we know that

\bar{s}(t_{1})-s(z_{1},t_{1})\leq~2(1+C)\epsilon\bar{r}

can be arbitrarily small by assuming
N_{0}
is sufficiently large. Then the waiting time
t_{4}-t_{1}
can be made small such that
t_{4}<\min\{t_{2},t_{3}\}
by choosing
N_{0}
sufficiently large. Combining (
10.13
) and (
10.3
) yields that

\displaystyle-(1+\alpha)\Psi(z,t)\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{\varphi_{t_{1}}(t)-s(z,t)+s(z_{1},t_{1})}{t-t_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~2^{-1-\alpha}\bar{s}(t_{1})^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~2^{-1-\alpha}(1+(1+2C)\epsilon)^{-\alpha}\bar{r}^{-\alpha}

for all time
t\in[t_{4},\min\{t_{2},t_{3}\}]
. This gives the uniform positive lower bound on
{E}_{k}
.
∎

11.
Smooth convergence

In this section, we prove the smooth convergence of the flow (
1.9
) and complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We will consider the three cases (i)
k=1
,
\alpha>0
; (ii)
k=n
,
\alpha>0
and (iii)
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
separately.

11.1.
Smooth convergence for
k=1
,
\alpha>0

For
k=1
, from Proposition
9.3
we see that
nE_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}\leq C
for some constant
C>0
. Since
M_{t}
is convex for each
t>0
by Proposition
9.5
, we get that the anisotropic principal curvatures are bounded

0<\kappa_{i}\leq C,\quad i=1,\cdots,n

along the flow (
1.9
). This is equivalent to the
C^{2}
estimate of
M_{t}
. In the following lemma, we derive the higher regularity of
M_{t}
.

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
M_{t}
be a smooth solution to the flow (
1.9
) with
k=1
,
\alpha>0
. Then for any integer
\ell\geq 2
, there exists a constant
C_{\ell}
depending only on
n,\alpha,\ell,\gamma
such that

|\hat{\nabla}^{\ell}\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}|\leq C_{\ell}

for all
t>0
, where
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}
is the anisotropic Weingarten map of
M_{t}
.

Proof.

As in
[
2
, §8]
, we write the evolving hypersurface locally as a graph
x(y^{1},\dots,y^{n},t)=y^{i}e_{i}+u(y^{1},\dots,y^{n},t)e_{0}
, where
\{e_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
is the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The tangent space of the graph is spanned by
\partial_{i}=e_{i}+u_{i}e_{0}
, where
u_{i}=\partial u/\partial y^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. The isotropic normal vector field is

\nu=(1+|Du|^{2})^{-1/2}(Du,-1).

Let
\gamma
be the
1
-homogenous extension of the support function of the Wulff shape
\Sigma
. Then
\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})
. Define a function
\hat{\gamma}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

(11.1)

\hat{\gamma}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})={\gamma}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n},-1).

Then
\hat{\gamma}(Du)=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}{\gamma}(\nu)
. Let
\omega=(Du,-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. By the
1
-homogeneity of
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}(Du)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{i}\gamma\big|_{\omega}D_{i}u-D^{0}\gamma\big|_{\omega}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{i}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}D_{i}u-D^{0}\gamma\big|_{\omega}.

Then the anisotropic unit normal
\nu_{\gamma}=D\gamma(\nu)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\nu_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{i}\gamma\big|_{\omega}e_{i}+D^{0}\gamma\big|_{\omega}e_{0}

(11.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{i}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}e_{i}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}uD^{i}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}-\hat{\gamma}(Du)\right)e_{0}.

Differentiate (
11.1
) with respect to
y^{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\ell}\nu_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle u_{\ell i}D^{i}D^{k}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}e_{k}+\left(u_{\ell i}D^{i}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}+u_{k}u_{\ell i}D^{i}D^{k}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}-u_{\ell i}D^{i}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}\right)e_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\ell i}D^{i}D^{k}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}(e_{k}+u_{k}e_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\ell i}D^{i}D^{k}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}\partial_{k}x.

By the anisotropic Weingarten formula (
2.8
), we obtain

(11.3)

\hat{h}_{\ell}^{k}=u_{\ell i}D^{i}D^{k}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}.

Moreover, by (
2.7
) and (
11.1
), the anisotropic second fundamental form
\hat{h}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{ij}=

\displaystyle-G(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},\partial_{i}\partial_{j}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G(\nu_{\gamma})(\nu_{\gamma},u_{ij}e_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u_{ij}G(\nu_{\gamma})\left(\nu_{\gamma},-\frac{\nu_{\gamma}}{\hat{\gamma}(Du)}+\frac{D^{i}\hat{\gamma}\big|_{Du}}{\hat{\gamma}(Du)}\partial_{i}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{u_{ij}}{\hat{\gamma}(Du)}.

Then the metric
\hat{g}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{ij}=\hat{\gamma}(Du)D^{i}D^{j}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}.

The equation (
11.3
) also implies that the normalized anisotropic mean curvature is given by

E_{1}(\kappa)=\frac{1}{n}\hat{g}^{ij}\hat{h}_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}u_{ij}D^{i}D^{j}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}.

Now we derive the evolution of the function
u
along the flow (
1.9
). Write the position vector field of the evolving hypersurface near a point
(x_{0},t_{0})
as

X(p,t)~=~y^{i}(p,t)e_{i}+u(y(p,t),t)e_{0},

where
y(p,t)
gives a time-dependent diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the graphical representation is preserved along the flow. Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(p,t)~=~\frac{\partial}{\partial t}y^{i}(p,t)e_{i}+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(y(p,t),t)+D_{i}u\frac{\partial}{\partial t}y^{i}(p,t)\right)e_{0}.

Taking the expression (
11.1
) of
\nu_{\gamma}
into account, from the flow equation (
1.9
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}y^{i}(p,t)

\displaystyle=(\phi(t)-E_{1}^{\alpha})D^{i}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(y(p,t),t)+D_{i}u\frac{\partial}{\partial t}y^{i}(p,t)

\displaystyle=(\phi(t)-E_{1}^{\alpha})\left(D_{i}uD^{i}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}-\hat{\gamma}(Du)\right).

Combining the above two equations, we see that the flow (
1.9
) is equivalent to the following scalar parabolic equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u=

\displaystyle~\hat{\gamma}(Du)(E_{1}^{\alpha}-\phi(t))

(11.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\hat{\gamma}(Du)\left(\frac{1}{n}D^{i}D^{j}\hat{\gamma}|_{Du}u_{ij}\right)^{\alpha}-\hat{\gamma}(Du)\phi(t)

of the function
u
locally on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The bound on the anisotropic principal curvatures and the convexity of
M_{t}
implies that
|Du|+|D^{2}u|\leq C
for some uniform constant on a uniform space-time neighbourhood of any
(y_{0},t_{0})
chosen such that
u(y_{0},t_{0})=0
and
Du|_{(y_{0},t_{0})}=0
(see, e.g., the argument in
[
2
, §9]
). Since
E_{1}
is linear in
D^{2}u
, by the uniform bound

0<1/C\leq{E}_{1}\leq C

on
E_{1}
and the bound on
|Du|
, the equation (
11.4
) is uniformly parabolic. Since
\alpha>0
and
E_{1}
is linear with respect to
D^{2}u
, we can apply Theorem 6 of
[
3
]
to derive the uniform
C^{2,\beta}
estimate of
u
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
in a uniform space-time neighbourhood of
(y_{0},t_{0})
. The parabolic Schauder theory
[
26
]
then implies the
C^{\ell,\beta}
estimate of
u
for all
\ell\geq 2
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

By the Hausdorff convergence of
M_{t}
to a scaled Wulff shape
W
, we conclude that
M_{t}
converges smoothly to a scaled Wulff shape
M_{\infty}=\bar{r}\Sigma
up to a translation.

11.2.
Smooth convergence for
k=n
,
\alpha>0

As mentioned in §
2.2
, in the case
k=n
, we have
E_{n}(\kappa)=\det(A_{\gamma}(\nu))E_{n}(\lambda)
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
denote the isotropic principal curvatures of
M_{t}
. Since the anisotropic normal vector field is given by
\nu_{\gamma}=\gamma(\nu)\nu+\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}\nu
, the flow (
1.9
) is equivalent to

(11.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X=\left(\phi(t)-\det(A_{\gamma}(\nu))^{\alpha/n}E_{n}(\lambda)^{\alpha/n}\right)\gamma(\nu)\nu

up to a tangential diffeomorphism. If we define the isotropic support function
h(z,t):\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of
M_{t}=\partial K_{t}
by

h(z,t):=\sup\{\langle x,z\rangle:x\in K_{t}\},

we can recover
M_{t}
via an embedding
\bar{X}(z,t)
:
\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
:

\bar{X}(z,t)=h(z,t)z+\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{i}h(z,t)\sigma^{ij}\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{j}z,

where we set
g_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}=(\sigma_{ij})
. Denote by
\mathfrak{r}=(\mathfrak{r}_{ij})
the matrix

\mathfrak{r}_{ij}=\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{i}\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{j}h+h\sigma_{ij},

whose eigenvalues are the principal radii of curvature
\mathfrak{r}_{i}=1/{\lambda_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,n
of
M_{t}
. Then it is well known that we can rewrite (
11.5
) as a scalar parabolic PDE of the isotropic support function
h(z,t)
on the unit sphere:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}h(z,t)=

\displaystyle\left(\phi(t)-\det(A_{\gamma}(z))^{\alpha/n}E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-\alpha/n}\right)\gamma(z)

(11.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(t)\gamma(z)-\rho(z)E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-\alpha/n},

where we set
\rho(z)=\det(A_{\gamma}(z))^{\alpha/n}\gamma(z)
for simplicity of the notation. Now the equation (
11.2
) differs from
[
1
, Equ. (13)]
only by the term
\phi(t)\gamma(z)
.

Since the matrix
A_{\gamma}(z)
is uniformly positive definite, the two-sided positive bound on
E_{n}(\kappa)
is equivalent to

(11.7)

0<C_{1}\leq E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})\leq C_{2}

for some positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Then we can apply the argument in Theorem 10 of
[
1
]
to conclude that the principal radii
\mathfrak{r}_{i}
are uniformly bounded from above. In fact, noting that the term
\phi(t)\gamma(z)
in (
11.2
) only contributes some lower order terms in the evolution of
\mathfrak{r}_{ij}
, which are uniformly controlled, we can apply the calculation in Theorem 10 of
[
1
]
to obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathfrak{r}_{ij}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{n}\rho(z)E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-(1+\frac{\alpha}{n})}\dot{E}_{n}^{k\ell}\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{k}\nabla^{\mathbb{S}}_{\ell}\mathfrak{r}_{ij}-\frac{\alpha}{n}\rho(z)E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-(1+\frac{\alpha}{n})}\dot{E}_{n}^{k\ell}(\mathfrak{r})\sigma_{k\ell}\mathfrak{r}_{ij}

\displaystyle+CE_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-\alpha/n}\sigma_{ij}+\phi(t)A_{\gamma}(z)_{ij},

where
C
depends on
\gamma
and
\alpha
. The second term here is crucial. By the generalized Newton’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{n}^{k\ell}(\mathfrak{r})\sigma_{k\ell}=

\displaystyle nE_{n-1}(\mathfrak{r})\geq nE_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{\frac{n-2}{n-1}}E_{1}(\mathfrak{r})^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\geq CE_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{\frac{n-2}{n-1}}\mathfrak{r}_{\max}^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.

Now work at a space-time point where a maximum eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{r}_{ij}
is attained. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathfrak{r}_{\max}\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{n}\rho(z)E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-(\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{\alpha}{n})}\mathfrak{r}_{\max}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}+C\left(E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})^{-\alpha/n}+\phi(t)\right)

for a constant
C
depending only on
\alpha
and
\gamma
. Given the bound (
11.7
) on
E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})
and Corollary
9.4
on
\phi(t)
, the parabolic maximum principle implies that

\mathfrak{r}_{\max}\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{t^{n-1}}\right)

for some positive constant
C
depending only on
C_{1},C_{2}
and
n,\alpha,k,\gamma,\mathrm{Vol}(K_{0})
and
V_{n+1-k}(K_{0})
.

The upper bound on
\mathfrak{r}_{i}
together with
E_{n}(\mathfrak{r})\geq C_{1}
also implies the uniform positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{r}_{i}
. Therefore the equation (
11.2
) is uniformly parabolic. The estimate on
\mathfrak{r}_{i}
also implies the
C^{2}
estimate of the isotropic support function
h(z,t)
. Since (
11.2
) is concave with respect to the second spatial derivatives of
h
, we apply Theorem 1.1 of
[
37
]
to derive the
C^{2,\beta}
estimate of
h(z,t)
. By the parabolic Schauder theory
[
26
]
, we conclude that the
C^{\ell,\beta}
norm of
h
is uniformly bounded for any
\ell\geq 2
. This is equivalent to the uniform
C^{\ell,\beta}
regularity estimate of the evolving hypersurfaces
M_{t}
. This together with the Hausdorff convergence proved in Proposition
10.1
implies that
M_{t}
converges to a translated scaled Wulff shape in the
C^{\infty}
topology as
t\to\infty
.

11.3.
Smooth convergence for
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n

We first prove the improved
C^{0}
and
C^{1}
estimates.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
M_{t}
,
t\in[0,\infty)
be a smooth solution to the flow (
1.9
) with
k=1,\dots,n
and
\alpha>0
. For any small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a large time
N_{0}
and a fixed constant
d>0
such that for all
t_{0}>N_{0}
, if the origin
o
is chosen with
|s(z,t_{0})-\bar{r}|\leq\varepsilon\bar{r}
, then

(11.8)

|s(z,t)-\bar{r}|\leq C\varepsilon\bar{r},\quad\forall~z\in\Sigma

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
, where
s(z,t)
is the anisotropic support function and
C
depends on
d,\bar{r}
but not on
t_{0}
. This combined with the
C^{1}
estimate (
9.2
) implies that

(11.9)

|\bar{\nabla}s(z,t)|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\bar{r}

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
10.1
and Corollary
10.2
, for any given
0<\varepsilon<1/4
there exists
N_{0}
such that for all time
t>N_{0}
, the anisotropic Hausdorff distance of
M_{t}
to a scaled Wulff shape
\bar{r}\Sigma
is less than
\varepsilon
, and

\int_{t}^{t+d}\bigg|\phi(x)-\frac{1}{\bar{r}^{\alpha}}\bigg|dx\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\bar{r}^{\alpha}}

for any fixed constant
d>0
. For each
t_{0}>N_{0}
, we choose the origin
o
such that the anisotropic support function of
M_{t_{0}}
with respect to
o
satisfies

|s(z,t_{0})-\bar{r}|\leq\varepsilon\bar{r}.

Since
s(z,t)
satisfies the parabolic equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s=-F_{*}(\tau_{ij})^{-\alpha}+\phi(t),

by adjusting
d
if necessary, the uniform bounds on
F_{*}
and
\phi(t)
imply that

(11.10)

|s(z,t)-\bar{r}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}

for all time
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
, where
d
is independent of
t_{0}
.
Let
\underline{s}(t)=\min_{z\in\Sigma}s(z,t)
. We have

(11.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\underline{s}(t)\geq

\displaystyle-\underline{s}(t)^{-\alpha}+\phi(t).

We claim that
\underline{s}(t)-\bar{r}\geq-C\varepsilon\bar{r}
on
[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
for a suitable
d
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\underline{s}(t)<\bar{r}
. Integrating (
11.11
) implies that

\displaystyle\underline{s}(t)-\underline{s}(t_{0})\geq

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(\phi(x)-\underline{s}(x)^{-\alpha})dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left((1-\varepsilon)\bar{r}^{-\alpha}-\underline{s}(x)^{-\alpha}\right)dx

(11.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\bar{r}^{\alpha}\underline{s}(x)^{\alpha}}\left(\underline{s}(x)^{\alpha}-\bar{r}^{\alpha}\right)dx-\varepsilon\bar{r}^{-\alpha}(t-t_{0}).

Note that

\displaystyle\underline{s}(t)^{\alpha}-\bar{r}^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-x)\bar{r}+x\underline{s}(t)\right)^{\alpha-1}dx~(\underline{s}(t)-\bar{r})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C\bar{r}^{\alpha-1}(\underline{s}(t)-\bar{r}),

where we used (
11.10
). We have

\displaystyle\underline{s}(t)-\bar{r}=

\displaystyle~\underline{s}(t)-\underline{s}(t_{0})+\underline{s}(t_{0})-\bar{r}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~C\bar{r}^{-\alpha-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(\underline{s}(x)-\bar{r}\right)dx-\varepsilon\bar{r}^{-\alpha}(t-t_{0})+\underline{s}(t_{0})-\bar{r}

(11.13)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~C\bar{r}^{-\alpha-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(\underline{s}(x)-\bar{r}\right)dx-\varepsilon\bar{r}^{-\alpha}(t-t_{0})-\varepsilon\bar{r},

where we used the fact that the origin
o
is chosen such that
\underline{s}(t_{0})-\bar{r}\geq-\varepsilon\bar{r}
. Then the integral form of Gronwall’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\underline{s}(t)-\bar{r}\geq

\displaystyle~-\varepsilon\left(\bar{r}^{-\alpha}(t-t_{0})+\bar{r}\right)\left(1+C\bar{r}^{-\alpha-1}(t-t_{0})e^{C\bar{r}^{-\alpha-1}(t-t_{0})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-C\varepsilon\bar{r},

where
C
depends only on
\bar{r}
and
t-t_{0}
. So we obtain the claim.

A similar argument gives the upper bound on
\bar{s}(t)=\max_{\Sigma}s(\cdot,t)
:

\displaystyle\bar{s}(t)-\bar{r}\leq

\displaystyle~C\varepsilon\bar{r}.

So we have the improved
C^{0}
estimate (
11.8
). The improved
C^{1}
estimate (
11.9
) follows from (
11.8
) and (
9.2
).
∎

In the following, we focus on the case
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
and show that the anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}
of
M_{t}
have a uniform positive lower bound.

Lemma 11.3
.

Along the flow (
1.9
) with
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
, the anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}
of the solution
M_{t}
have a uniform positive lower bound for
t>0
.

Proof.

We have proved in Proposition
9.5
that
\kappa_{i}
are positive on any finite time interval. However, the lower bound on
\kappa_{i}
may degenerate as the time approaches the infinity. To prove that
\kappa_{i}
has a uniform positive lower bound for all time, it suffices to show this for sufficiently large time.

Recall that in the case
\alpha\geq k=1,\dots,n
, the global term
\phi(t)
satisfies (
10.3
). We employ the anisotropic Gauss map parametrization in §
8
. We assume that
N_{0}>0
is sufficiently large such that the anisotropic Hausdorff distance of
M_{t}
to
\bar{r}\Sigma
is less than
\varepsilon
, and

(11.14)

\phi(t)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\bar{r}^{-\alpha}

for all time
t\in[N_{0},\infty)
. By choosing
N_{0}
sufficiently large, the constant
\varepsilon
in (
11.14
) can be any small positive constant. For each fixed
t_{0}>N_{0}
, we choose the origin
o
such that the anisotropic support function of
M_{t_{0}}
with respect to
o
satisfies

|s(z,t_{0})-\bar{r}|\leq\bar{r}\varepsilon,\qquad\forall~z\in\Sigma.

By Lemma
11.2
, there exists a constant
d>0
and
C=C(d,\bar{r})
independent of
t_{0}
such that (
11.8
) and (
11.9
) hold for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
.

In the following, we only focus on the time
t\geq N_{0}
. To estimate the lower bound on the anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}
, we can equivalently estimate the upper bound on the anisotropic principal radii of curvature
\tau_{i}
, which are eigenvalues of
(\tau_{ij})
defined in (
8.3
). We define

\zeta=\sup\{\tau_{ij}\xi^{i}\xi^{j}:~|\xi|=1\}

and for any
t_{0}>N_{0}
we consider the function

(11.15)

\omega(z,t)=\log\zeta(z,t)-\mu_{1}s(z,t)+\mu_{2}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}(z,t)

on the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+d]
, where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
are positive constants to be determined later. We consider a point
(z_{1},t_{1})
where a new maximum of the function
\omega
is achieved. By rotation of the local orthonormal frame, we assume that
\xi=e_{1}
and
(\tau_{ij})=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})
is diagonal at
(z_{1},t_{1})
. First, at
(z_{1},t_{1})
we have
\zeta=\tau_{11}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{11}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tau_{11}-\mu_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s+\mu_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2},

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\omega=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{11}}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\tau_{11}-\mu_{1}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s+\mu_{2}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2},

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\omega=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{11}}\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\tau_{11}-\mu_{1}\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s+\mu_{2}\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}-\frac{1}{(\tau_{11})^{2}}\bar{\nabla}_{k}\tau_{11}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\tau_{11}.

Combining these equations with (
8.9
), (
8.4
) and (
8.5
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\omega-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\omega-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{11}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tau_{11}-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\tau_{11}-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}\tau_{11}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\mu_{1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}s-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}s\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\mu_{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}-\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\left(\bar{\nabla}_{k}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}Q_{k\ell p}\bar{\nabla}_{p}|\bar{\nabla}s|^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(\tau_{11})^{2}}\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{\nabla}_{k}\tau_{11}\bar{\nabla}_{\ell}\tau_{11}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{11}}\left(\ddot{\Psi}^{k\ell,pq}\bar{\nabla}_{1}\tau_{k\ell}\bar{\nabla}_{1}\tau_{pq}+(1-\alpha)\Psi+\phi(t)+C\dot{\Psi}^{k\ell}\bar{g}_{k\ell}\tau_{11}\right)

11.4.
Exponential convergence

The stronger convergence results can be obtained by studying the linearization of the flow around the Wulff shape (cf.
[
2

). The goal is to prove that the linearization of the flow about the Wulff shape
\Sigma
is strictly stable. Without loss of generality, we assume that the hypersurfaces near

can be written as graphs of smooth functions over
\Sigma

M_{t}=\{r(z,t)z:z\in\Sigma\},

where

) is equivalent to the following parabolic equation for the function

(11.29)

\frac{\partial r}{\partial t}=(\phi(t)-E_{k}^{\alpha/k}(\kappa))\left(G(\nu_{\gamma},z)-\frac{1}{r}G^{ij}G(\nu_{\gamma},\partial_{i}{z})\frac{\partial r}{\partial z^{j}}\right),

described in (

) and

stands for the inverse of the matrix

\Sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{n}\left(-\bar{\Delta}\eta-{A}_{pi}{}^{i}\bar{\nabla}^{p}\eta-n\eta\right),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\phi(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Sigma|_{\gamma}}\int_{\Sigma}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}E_{k}^{\alpha/k}(\kappa)d\mu_{\gamma}+E_{k}^{\alpha/k}(I)\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}d\mu_{\gamma}\right)

is non-positive and self-adjoint with respect to the inner product
