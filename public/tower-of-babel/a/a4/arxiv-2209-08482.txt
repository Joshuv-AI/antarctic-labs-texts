Abstract
. We propose an approach for the simultaneous reconstruction of the electromagnetic and acoustic material parameters, in the given medium Ω \Omega where to image, using the photo-acoustic pressure, measured on a single point of the boundary of Ω \Omega , generated by plasmonic nanoparticles. We prove that the generated pressure, that we denote by p ⋆ ​ ( x , s , ω ) p^{\star}\left(x,s,\omega\right) , depending on only one fixed point x ∈ ∂ Ω x\in\partial\Omega , the time variable s s , in a large enough interval, and the incidence frequency ω \omega , in a large enough band, is enough to reconstruct both the sound speed, the mass density and the permittivity inside Ω \Omega . Indeed, from the behavior of the measured pressure in terms of time, we can estimate the travel time of the pressure, for arriving points inside Ω \Omega , then using the eikonal equation we reconstruct the acoustic speed of propagation. In addition, we reconstruct the internal values of the acoustic Green’s function. From the singularity analysis of this Green’s function, we extract the integrals along the geodesics, for internal arriving points, of the logarithmic-gradient of the mass density. Solving this (internal) integral geometric problem provides us with the values of the mass density function inside Ω \Omega . Finally, from the behavior of p ⋆ ​ ( x , s , ω ) p^{\star}\left(x,s,\omega\right) with respect to the frequency ω \omega , we detect the generated plasmonic resonances from which we reconstruct the permittivity inside Ω \Omega .

1.1.
Introduction of the mathematical model

We deal with the photo-acoustic imaging modality. In this technique, we use a laser to excite a medium to image. As a consequence, the medium is heated and produces an elastic expansion that in turn generates a sound waves. This sound wave can be measured on the boundary, or in the exterior, of the medium. The goal of this technique is to reconstruct optical, and eventually acoustical, properties of the medium. Recently there has been much interest in this imaging technique which finds several applications in medical imaging. Without being exhaustive, we refer the reader to the following literature

In the current work, we inject nanoparticles into the tissue that can create the heat while excited by lasers.
Therefore, this technique has the potential to be applied also for tissues having very less absorption as themselves they create the needed contrast of absorption, i.e. they play the role of contrast agents. These nanoparticles can be either plasmonics or dielectrics. Both of them have small absorption (as compared to their diffusion character), i.e. the ratio between the imaginary part and the real part of the permittivity is small. However, if we excite them with incident frequencies close to certain critical frequencies, called plasmonic or dielectric resonances, then the amount of electric (or magnetic) field generated will be enhanced and this will compensate the weakness of the absorption. As the source of the heat is generated by the product of the (imaginary part) of the permittivity and the square of the modulus of the electric field, see below, then the mentioned compensation makes sense. This is, in short, the principle of the Photo-Acoustic effect using resonating nano-particles.

3.1.
Well-posedeness for the wave equation with
L^{2}
-source terms

We start by recalling the acoustic model given by
(\ref{pressurwaveequa})
,

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\partial^{2}_{t}p(x,t)-c^{2}(x)\underset{x}{\Delta}p(x,t)&=&-c^{2}(x)\,\nabla\log(\rho(x))\cdot\underset{x}{\nabla}p(x,t)\qquad in\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
\partial_{t}p(x,0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad p(x,0)&=&\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(x)\;|E|^{2}(x),\qquad in\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{array}\right.

We denote by
H(\cdot,\cdot)
, the Green’s kernel solution, in the distributional sense, of

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\partial^{2}_{t}H(x,t,y,s)-c^{2}(x)\underset{x}{\Delta}H(x,t,y,s)&=&\underset{y}{\delta}(x)\,\underset{s}{\delta}(t)\qquad in\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
\partial_{t}H(x,0)=H(x,0)&=&0\quad in\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{array}\right.

By applying the Laplace transform to the equations
(\ref{Equa1App})
and
(\ref{Equa2App})
, we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\underset{x}{\Delta}\,\mathcal{L}ap(p)(x,s)+\dfrac{(i\,s)^{2}}{c^{2}(x)}\,\mathcal{L}ap(p)(x,s)&=&\nabla\log(\rho(x))\cdot\underset{x}{\nabla}\mathcal{L}ap(p)(x,s)-\dfrac{s}{c^{2}(x)}\,\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(x)\;|E|^{2}(x),\\
\underset{x}{\Delta}\mathcal{L}ap(H)(x,s)+\dfrac{(i\,s)^{2}}{c^{2}(x)}\,\mathcal{L}ap(H)(x,s)&=&\dfrac{-1}{c^{2}(x)}\,\underset{y}{\delta}(x)=\dfrac{-1}{c^{2}(y)}\,\underset{y}{\delta}(x).\end{array}\right.

Hence, we deduce that
\mathcal{L}ap(p)
satisfies a Helmholtz equation with variable wave number and
\mathcal{L}ap(H)
, clearly, is its associated Green’s kernel. Thanks to Green’s formula we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}ap(p)(x,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s\;\mathcal{L}ap\left(\int_{\Omega}H(x,t,y)\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(y)\;|E|^{2}(y)\,dy\right)(s)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}ap(H)(x,s,y)\,c^{2}(y)\,\nabla\log(\rho(y))\cdot\underset{y}{\nabla}\mathcal{L}ap(p)(y,s)\,dy.

By taking the Laplace inverse transform, we deduce that

(3.3)

\displaystyle p(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\;\int_{\Omega}H(x,t,y)\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(y)\;|E|^{2}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\,\int_{0}^{t}\,H(x,t-h,y)\,c^{2}(y)\,\nabla\log(\rho(y))\cdot\underset{y}{\nabla}p(y,h)\,dh\,dy.

Next, we set
\bm{K}_{1}(\cdot)
to be the operator defined by

\bm{K}_{1}(p(\cdot,t))(x):=\int_{\Omega}\,\int_{0}^{t}\,H(x,t-h,y)\,c^{2}(y)\,\nabla\log(\rho(y))\cdot\underset{y}{\nabla}p(y,h)\,dh\,dy.

Then,

\left\|\bm{K}_{1}(p(\cdot,t))\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left|\Omega\right|\,t\,\int_{0}^{t}\,\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\,\,\left|H(x,t-h,y)\,c^{2}(y)\,\nabla\log(\rho(y))\cdot\underset{y}{\nabla}p(y,h)\right|^{2}\,dy\,dx\,dh,

and using the continuity of the convolution operator, with respect to the Helmholtz kernel
H(\cdot,t-h,\cdot)
, from
\mathbb{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
to
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, we obtain:

\left\|\bm{K}_{1}(p(\cdot,t))\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left|\Omega\right|\,t\,\left\|c^{2}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\log(\rho)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\int_{0}^{t}\,\,\left\|\nabla p(\cdot,h)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}\,\,dh,

hence, using the continuity of the gradient operator from
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
to
\mathbb{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
, we obtain:

(3.4)

\left\|\bm{K}_{1}(p(\cdot,t))\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left|\Omega\right|\,t\,\left\|c^{2}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\log(\rho)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}}\,\int_{0}^{t}\,\,\left\|p(\cdot,h)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\,\,dh.

Now, by taking the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-norm in both sides of
(\ref{pressure=term+K})
and gathering with
(\ref{equap})
we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\|p(\cdot,t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\,\left\|\partial_{t}\;\int_{\Omega}H(\cdot,t,y)\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(y)\;|E|^{2}(y)\,dy\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\,\left|\Omega\right|\,t\,\left\|c^{2}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\log(\rho)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}}\,\int_{0}^{t}\,\,\left\|p(\cdot,h)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\,\,dh.

Thanks to the integral form of Grönwall Lemma, we deduce

\left\|p(\cdot,t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\bm{\alpha}\,\left\|\partial_{t}\;\int_{\Omega}H(\cdot,t,y)\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\;{\mathrm{Im\,}}(\varepsilon)(y)\;|E|^{2}(y)\,dy\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

where

\bm{\alpha}=2+4\,\left|\Omega\right|\,t\,\left\|c^{2}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\log(\rho)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\left\|\nabla\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}}\,e^{t}.

By referring to
[
27
, Theorem 7.1, formula 25]
, we know
that the operator
\bm{K}_{2}
, defined by the expression

\bm{K}_{2}\left(f\right)(x,t):=\partial_{t}\;\int_{\Omega}H(x,t,y)\,f(y)\,dy,

is a continuous operator from
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
to
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\lambda_{2}

3.4.
Regularity of the electric field
u_{1}:=E

The goal of this subsection is to justify the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})-
integrability of the source term given by
\dfrac{\omega\,\beta_{0}}{c_{p}}\,Im\left(\varepsilon\right)\,\left|E\right|^{2}
. Thanks to the fact that
Im\left(\varepsilon\right)
is smooth function and vanishing one outside
\Omega
, we reduce the computations check to the
\mathbb{L}^{4}(\Omega)-
integrability of the electric field
u_{1}
solution of the following Lippmann-Schwinger equation (L.S.E),

u_{1}(x)+\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D}\Pi(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}\right)\,dy=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\Pi(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Green tensor of the Maxwell problem with variable permittivity function.
In Theorem 2.1 of
[
15
]
, the previous integral representation was given sense. In addition, the coming decomposition of
\Pi(\cdot,\cdot)
is proved

(3.7)

\Pi(x,y)=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\epsilon_{0}(y)}\underset{y}{\nabla}\underset{y}{\nabla}\Phi_{0}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)\right)^{2}}\underset{x}{\nabla}\underset{x}{\nabla}M\left(\Phi_{0}(\cdot,y)\nabla\epsilon_{0}(y)\right)(x)+R(x,y),\quad x\neq y,

where, for arbitrary small
\delta>0
, we have
R(\cdot,y)\in\mathbb{L}^{3-\delta}(D)
. Here
\Phi_{0}(x,y)
is fundamental solution of the pure Laplacian,
\Phi_{0}(x,y):=\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}
.

Remark 3.1
.

In
(\ref{KernelDecomposition})
, from singularity analysis point of view, we can approximate the second kernel, as

\underset{x}{\nabla}\underset{x}{\nabla}M\left(\Phi_{0}(\cdot,y)\nabla\epsilon_{0}(y)\right)(x)\simeq\underset{x}{\nabla}\left(\Phi_{0}(x,y)\nabla\epsilon_{0}(y)\right),

and the third kernel as

R(x,y)\simeq\Phi_{0}(x,y).

Now, using the decomposition
(\ref{KernelDecomposition})
the L.S.E becomes,

(3.8)

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\underset{x}{\nabla}\int_{D}\underset{y}{\nabla}\Phi_{0}(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\frac{\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}\right)}{\epsilon_{0}(y)}\,dy

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\,\int_{D}\underset{x}{\nabla}\underset{x}{\nabla}M\left(\Phi_{0}(\cdot,y)\nabla\epsilon_{0}(y)\right)(x)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,\frac{\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}\right)}{\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)\right)^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\,\mu\,\int_{D}R(x,y)\cdot u_{1}(y)\,\left(\epsilon_{0}(y)-\epsilon_{p}\right)\,dy=u_{0}(x),

and thanks to Remark
3.1
, we can approximate the study of
(\ref{LSEE})
by the following simplest equation

(3.9)

u_{1}(x)-\nabla M\left(u_{1}\frac{\left(\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{p}\right)}{\epsilon_{0}}\right)(x)-\nabla N\left(\nabla\epsilon_{0}\cdot u_{1}\frac{\left(\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{p}\right)}{(\epsilon_{0})^{2}}\right)(x)+\omega^{2}\,\mu\,N\left(u_{1}\left(\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{p}\right)\right)(x)=u_{0}(x),

where
N(\cdot)
is the Newtonian operator defined from
\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)
to
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
by:

N(f)(x):=\int_{D}\Phi_{0}(x,y)\,f(y)\,d\,y=\int_{D}\frac{1}{\left|x-y\right|}\,f(y)\,d\,y.

To justify that
u_{1}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}(\Omega)
, we split our computation into two steps. First, we prove that
u_{1}\in\mathbb{L}^{4}(D)
. After restricting the equation
(\ref{eq:electromagnetic_scattering})
into
D
, of course using also the fact that
\varepsilon(\cdot)_{|_{D}}:=\epsilon_{p}
, see
(\ref{permittivityfct})
, we deduce that
\mathrm{div}\,(u_{1})=0
