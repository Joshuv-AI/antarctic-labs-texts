City products of right-angled buildings and their universal groups
Jens Bossaert, Tom De Medts

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of city products of right-angled buildings that produces a new right-angled building out of smaller ones. More precisely, if M M is a right-angled Coxeter diagram of rank n n and Δ 1 , … , Δ n \Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n} are right-angled buildings, then we construct a new right-angled building Δ := ⋈ M ⁡ ( Δ 1 , … , Δ n ) \Delta:=\operatorname{\rotatebox[x,y]{90.0}{$\bowtie$}}_{M}(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}) . We can recover the buildings Δ 1 , … , Δ n \Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n} as residues of Δ \Delta , but we can also construct a skeletal building of type M M from Δ \Delta that captures the large-scale geometry of Δ \Delta . We then proceed to study universal groups for city products of right-angled buildings, and we show that the universal group of Δ \Delta can be expressed in terms of the universal groups for the buildings Δ 1 , … , Δ n \Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n} and the structure of M M . As an application, we show the existence of many examples of pairs of different buildings of the same type that admit (topologically) isomorphic universal groups, thereby vastly generalizing a recent example by Lara Beßmann.

1.
Introduction

A building is called
right-angled
if its Coxeter group is right-angled, which means that the only values occurring in its Coxeter matrix are
1
,
2
and
\infty
.
The prototypical example is the case where the Coxeter matrix has rank
2
with a label
\infty
, in which case the building is a tree. In general, the behavior of right-angled buildings is somewhat comparable to that of trees, but in a combinatorially much more complicated (and therefore much more interesting) way.

The first systematic study of right-angled buildings is by Frédéric Haglund and Frédéric Paulin
[
haglundpaulin
]
, who showed the existence and uniqueness of right-angled buildings for any set of parameters (see
Theorem

2.17
below).
Later, right-angled buildings have been used to construct interesting examples of
lattices
, as in the work of Angela Kubena, Anne Thomas and Kevin Wortman
[
thomas1
,
thomas3
,
thomas2
]
.

Our motivation for studying right-angled buildings, initiated by Pierre-Emmanuel Caprace in
[
caprace2014
]
, is the connection with totally disconnected locally compact groups. More precisely, the automorphism group of a right-angled building is always totally disconnected with respect to the permutation topology, and if the building is locally finite, then the automorphism group is also locally compact. This is not true in general, but these automorphism groups contain lots of interesting subgroups, namely so-called
universal groups
, that can still be locally compact even if the building is not locally finite.

These universal groups were first introduced and studied for trees by Marc Burger and Shahar Mozes in their seminal paper
[
burgermozes
]
.
This concept has been generalized to right-angled buildings by the second author in joint work with Ana C. Silva and Koen Struyve in
[
silva1
,
silva2
]
in the locally finite case, and has been further generalized and studied without this assumption in our paper
[
bossaert
]
, focussing on topological properties.

For some right-angled buildings, the large-scale geometry looks like a tree; see, for instance,
Fig.

1
below. This raises the question whether it is possible, in these cases, to somehow reverse the process, i.e., whether we can start from a tree and obtain a more complicated right-angled building by “inserting” more complicated blocks at each vertex of the tree.

This idea gave rise to the construction that we introduce and study in this paper. We call it the
city product
of buildings, as it is a way to construct larger objects out of a given number of buildings, guided by the rough structure of yet another right-angled diagram.
More precisely, if
M
is a right-angled Coxeter diagram of rank
n
and
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
are right-angled buildings, then we construct a new right-angled building
\Delta:=\operatorname{\rotatebox[x,y]{90.0}{$\bowtie$}}_{M}(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n})
. We can recover the buildings
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
as residues of
\Delta
, but we can also construct a
skeletal building

\Phi
of type
M
from
\Delta
that captures the large-scale geometry of
\Delta
.
Constructing this building
\Phi
is not difficult, but it turns out to be far from trivial to show that it is indeed a building. This is the content of
, which relies on the new notions of
weak homotopies
and
parkour maps
that we have introduced for this purpose.

It turns out that the universal groups for these city products can be described as the universal group of this skeletal building
\Phi
with respect to universal groups for each of the smaller buildings
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
; this is the content of
.

In a recent preprint
[
bessmann
]
, Lara Beßmann has shown the existence of pairs of different right-angled buildings, both of type

\infty
\infty
,
admitting universal groups that are topologically isomorphic.
Her method relies on the notion of tree-wall trees from
[
silva1
]
and only works for star-shaped diagrams (see
).
We show that this can be interpreted in terms of city products, which allows us to produce many more examples of such pairs. This is the content of
.

2.1.
Coxeter systems

Definition 2.1
.

(1)

Let
I
be any index set and

M\colon I\times I\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\colon(i,j)\mapsto m_{ij}

, and

W=\bigl\langle s_{i}\mathrel{\bigm|}\text{$(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1$ for all $i,j\in I$}\bigr\rangle.

When

are involutions. Additionally, note that when

and

commute.

Together with the generating set

, the pair

is called the

. The

of
(W,S)
is the cardinality of

.

We can represent

by means of its

(m_{ij})
, or more commonly its

: the nodes of the diagram are the elements of

(sometimes with explicit labels), and two nodes are connected by a decorated edge according to the following rules:

i

m_{ij}=2

i

m_{ij}=3

i
j
m_{ij}=4

i

m_{ij}
m_{ij}\geq 5

(2)

We call a Coxeter system

if the underlying graph of its Coxeter diagram is connected, and

otherwise.

(3)

We call a Coxeter system
(W,S)

if

.

In general, non-isomorphic Coxeter systems may have isomorphic Coxeter groups, but this cannot occur for right-angled Coxeter systems:

.

If a right-angled Coxeter group

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system over some index set
I
.

(1)

We will write

for the free monoid over

. The elements of

.

(2)

There is a natural surjective

\epsilon\colon I^{*}\to W\colon i\mapsto s_{i}.

Definition 2.4
.

For every

such that
m_{ij}
is finite, define in

the word

p(i,j)=\begin{cases}(ij)^{k}&\text{if $m_{ij}=2k$ is even,}\\
j(ij)^{k}&\text{if $m_{ij}=2k+1$ is odd.}\end{cases}

In other words,

is the word with

alternating letters

and

, ending in

. When

Definition 2.5
.

Let

and
w_{1},w_{2}\in I^{*}
.

(1)

An

(or also a

) is a transformation of a word

into the word
w_{1}\,p(j,i)\,w_{2}
.

(2)

Two words

are

can be transformed into

by a sequence of elementary homotopies; we denote this by

. Clearly, homotopy is an equivalence relation and preserves the length of the words.

(3)

An
elementary contraction
is a transformation of a word
w_{1}\,ii\,w_{2}
into the word
w_{1}\,w_{2}
.

(4)

An
elementary expansion
is a transformation of a word
w_{1}\,w_{2}
into a word
w_{1}\,ii\,w_{2}
.

(5)

A word is called

if it is not homotopic to a word of the form

(for some

).

(6)

Two words
w
and
w^{\prime}
are called

w
can be transformed into
w^{\prime}
by a sequence of elementary homotopies, contractions, and expansions. Clearly, every equivalence class contains some reduced word.

.

(1)

Two words

(2)

(3)

. If

2.2.
Chamber systems

Our approach is based on
[

]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
I
be any index set. A

is a set
\Delta
together with, for every

, an equivalence relation called
i

. The elements of

are called

are

, or simply

if we do not want to stress the adjacency type. The cardinality

is called the

of
\Delta

We will usually say that “
\Delta
is a chamber system” when the equivalence relations on
\Delta
are clear from the context.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\Delta
be a chamber system over
I
.
A
gallery
\gamma
in
\Delta
is a finite sequence of pairwise adjacent chambers

c_{0}\sim_{i_{1}}c_{1}\sim_{i_{2}}{\cdots}\sim_{i_{n}}c_{n}

for certain
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in I
. We call the word
i_{1}\mathbin{\cdots}i_{n}\in I^{*}
the
type
of
\gamma
, and the integer

length
of
\gamma
. If there is no strictly shorter gallery from
c_{0}
to

, then we call
\gamma
a
minimal
gallery.

Chamber systems come equipped with a natural metric

\dist\colon\Delta\times\Delta\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

defined by declaring
\dist(c,d)
to be the minimal length of all galleries joining
c
and
d
(or
\infty
if there is no such gallery). It is clear that this distance function is positive-definite, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
J\subseteq I
. A subset
C\subseteq\Delta
is called
J
-connected
if any two chambers in
C
can be joined by a gallery of type in
J^{*}
. A
residue of type
J
, or simply a
J
-residue
, is a
J
-connected component of
\Delta
. A
panel of type
j
, or simply a
j
-panel
, is a residue of type
\{j\}
. The set of all
J
-residues of the chamber system

will be denoted by
\Res_{J}(\Delta)
.

Note that each
J
-residue is, in its own right, a connected chamber system over the index set
J
.

Definition 2.10
.

A chamber system is called
thin
if every panel contains exactly two chambers, and
thick
if every panel contains at least three chambers. (Panels containing only a single chamber are degenerate cases that should not occur in any reasonable application.)

Note that a chamber system might be neither thin nor thick.

Definition 2.11
.

A map
\varphi\colon\Delta_{1}\to\Delta_{2}
between two chamber systems is a
morphism
if
\varphi(c)\sim\varphi(d)
in
\Delta_{2}
whenever
c\sim d
in
\Delta_{1}
. As usual, an
isomorphism
is a bijective morphism, and an
automorphism
is an isomorphism to the same chamber system. Assuming that
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
have the same index set, a morphism is
type-preserving
if
\varphi(c)\sim_{i}\varphi(d)
whenever
c\sim_{i}d
. In this paper, we shall always assume morphisms to be type-preserving.

The set of all automorphisms of a chamber system

forms a group, denoted by

.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system of type
M
over
I
. Define a chamber system over
I
with the elements of
W
as chambers, and declare two group elements

-adjacent if and only if

Coxeter complexes are always connected and thin: every chamber is
i

.

Observe that the Coxeter complex associated to a Coxeter system

is nothing more than the (undirected) Cayley graph of

.

Theorem 2.13

Definition 2.14
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system of type

Definition 2.15

A building

Definition 2.16

A building

over

For any choice of (possibly infinite) cardinal numbers

\Delta

3.1.
Weak homotopies

We start with some combinatorics, the goal of which will become clear later on.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
i,j\in I
with
m_{ij}=2
and define the set

P(i,j)=\bigl\{w\in\{i,j\}^{*}\bigm|\text{$w$ contains at least one $i$ and at least one $j$}\bigr\}.

A
weak homotopy
is a transformation of a word
w_{1}\,p\,w_{2}
into a word
w_{1}\,p^{\prime}\,w_{2}
where
w_{1},w_{2}\in I^{*}
and
p,p^{\prime}\in P(i,j)
. Two words

and
w^{\prime}
are
weakly homotopic
if
w
can be transformed into
w^{\prime}
by a sequence of weak homotopies.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\prec
be a total order on
I
. Endow
I^{*}
with the induced lexicographical order. Then every word
w\in I^{*}
is homotopic to a unique lexicographically minimal word that we call the
normal form
of
w
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system over
I
and let
\prec
be a total order on
I
.

(1)

If two words are homotopic, then their normal forms are equal.

(2)

A word is reduced if and only if its normal form contains no consecutive duplicate letters.

(3)

The normal forms of weakly homotopic words are equal up to consecutive duplicate letters.

Proof.

Claim (i) follows immediately from the definitions. For (ii), let
w\simeq w_{1}\,ii\,w_{2}
and assume that the normal form contains no subword
ii
. Mark the two letters
i
in
w_{1}\,ii\,w_{2}
and write the normal form as
n_{1}\,i\,n_{2}\,i\,n_{3}
(where the two letters
i
are the marked ones). Then
n_{2}
is not the empty word, so let
k
be its first letter; by assumption,
k\neq i
. By homotopy, all letters in
n_{2}
are contained in
\{i\}\cup\{i\}^{\perp}
, where
\{i\}^{\perp}:=\{j\in I\mid m_{ij}=2\}
. It follows that the normal form cannot be lexicographically minimal: if
i\prec k
, then the homotopic word
n_{1}\,ii\,n_{2}\,n_{3}
is lexicographically smaller, and if
i\succ k
, then
n_{1}\,n_{2}\,ii\,n_{3}
is smaller. Claim (ii) follows. For claim (iii), it suffices to note that the effect of a weak homotopy of a word on its normal form is that a subword

with
m\geq 1
,

, is replaced by another such word.
∎

Let
(W,S)

I
and let

be a total order on

If two reduced words

Letting
