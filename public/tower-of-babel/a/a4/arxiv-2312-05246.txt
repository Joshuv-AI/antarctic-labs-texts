SUPER and subpicosecond coherent control of an optical qubit in a tin-vacancy color center in diamond
Cem Güney Torun, Mustafa Gökçe, Thomas K. Bracht, Mariano Isaza Monsalve, Sarah Benbouabdellah, Özgün Ozan Nacitarhan, Marco E. Stucki, Matthew L. Markham, Gregor Pieplow, Tommaso Pregnolato, Joseph H. D. Munns, Doris E. Reiter, Tim Schröder

Abstract
The coherent excitation of an optically active spin system is one of the key elements in the engineering of a spin-photon interface. In this work, we use the novel SUPER scheme, employing nonresonant ultrashort optical pulses, to coherently control the main optical transition of a tin-vacancy color center in diamond, a promising emitter that can both be utilized as a quantum memory and a single-photon source. Furthermore, we implement a subpicosecond control scheme using resonant pulses for achieving record short quantum gates applied to diamond color centers. The employed ultrafast quantum gates open up a new regime of quantum information processing with solid-state color centers, eventually enabling multi-gate operations with the optical qubit and efficient spectral filtering of the excitation laser from deterministically prepared coherent photons.



I
Introduction

The deterministic and coherent creation of single photons is a core element of many quantum-based technologies such as photonic quantum computation
Wang et al. 2020
and entanglement-based quantum networking
Wei et al. 2022
.
To these ends, the negatively charged tin-vacancy color center in diamond (SnV) has attracted increasing attention in recent years. This interest stems from many of its desirable qualities, such as first-order resistance to spectral diffusion
Narita et al. 2023
;
de Santis et al. 2021
;
Aghaeimeibodi et al. 2021

and extended coherence times at elevated temperatures compared to other group-IV color centers in diamond
Guo et al. 2023
. Using these properties a plethora of applications have already been demonstrated, such as sensors
Alkahtani et al. 2018
, quantum memories
Rosenthal et al. 2023
;
Debroux et al. 2021
, and coherent single-photon sources
Arjona Martínez et al. 2022
.

Resonant excitation methods, although they are an effective means to coherently prepare an excited state, come with a very fundamental drawback: filtering the excitation light from the subsequently emitted single photons. Since both fields will have the same carrier frequency, it is not possible to use spectral filtering to separate them. The most established method to circumvent this issue is using cross-polarization filtering
Bernien et al. 2013
. At best, there is a loss of half of the photons that are emitted.

A novel nonresonant method called swing-up of the quantum emitter population (SUPER)
Bracht et al. 2021
has been proposed to remedy the reduced single photon creation efficiency of resonant schemes. By variation of both the energy and central frequency of two detuned excitation pulses, the ground, and excited state population can be coherently inverted. Here, we demonstrate the application of the SUPER scheme
Karli et al. 2022
;
Boos et al. 2022
;
Joos et al. 2023
for the first time with color centers in diamond, and add SUPER to the quantum toolbox of optically accessible solid-state spin qubits.

In this work, we first start by introducing the experimental setup in section
II
. Next, we characterize the coherence properties of the emitter in section
III
by measuring the lifetime and resolving temporal Rabi oscillations under quasi-continuous driving. Then, in section
IV
, we show that we can create ultrafast control gates operating on the optical qubit of the SnV using picosecond and subpicosecond long resonant pulses. Finally, in section
V
, we show the SUPER scheme can be employed using color centers in diamond. We compare our experimental data with theoretical predictions.

II
Experimental Details

The core element of our work for implementing various coherent control schemes
Takou and Economou 2021
is the pulse carver (Fig.
1
a), which is modified from a commercial pulse slicer (APE F50) that enables us to turn a spectrally wide (i.e. temporally short) pulse into one with an arbitrarily shaped spectrum within the original pulse shape (see supplementary materials). For example, we can create broad-subpicosecond pulses, narrow-temporally longer pulses, or a pulse composed of several spectral bands. To do so, a
{\sim 150}\,
fs pulse with a bandwidth of
3.6\,
nm is spatially dispersed via a reflective diffraction grating. A cylindrical lens focuses individual frequency bands onto the pixels of a spatial light modulator (SLM).
By programming the SLM, we apply voltages to individual pixels such that the polarization of selected frequency bands are altered.
The altered frequency bands are later filtered out using a polarizer, which results in a pulse with the desired spectral shape.

The shaped pulses are then directed with the use of a dichroic mirror into a confocal microscope, which is coupled into a helium cryostat operating at
4.5\,
K (see supplementary materials). The cryostat cools SnVs embedded into diamond nanopillars
Rugar et al. 2019
(see supplementary materials) that enhance the photon collection efficiency. After the excitation, the control pulses are spectrally filtered, and photons from the phonon sideband are collected.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the SnV has four distinct energy levels (Fig.
1
b), two of which make up the ground state manifold, and two, the excited state manifold. All levels are two-fold spin degenerate
Trusheim et al. 2020
. There are four optical transitions connecting these levels.
Here, we focus on the optical transition between levels
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|3\right\rangle
, conventionally named as C transition.
The spin states associated with the long-lived ground state
\left|1\right\rangle
are the most viable for use as a qubit
Sukachev et al. 2017
;
Senkalla et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2023
. Therefore, the C transition is the most desirable to work for extracting coherent photons.

The optical coherence of a transition is of great importance when devising an emitter-photon interface. We therefore first quantize the coherence of the optical qubit composed of the states
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|3\right\rangle
.

We start by estimating the lifetime of the optical qubit
T_{\rm 1}
, which corresponds to the inverse of the spontaneous emission rate, and can be obtained by analyzing the fluorescence response after being excited by a pulse much shorter than
T_{\rm 1}
(Suppl Fig.
a).
We record the time it takes for a photon to be registered on an avalanche photodiode detector after excitation.
From the arrival statistics, we find the exponential decay of the excited state population (Fig.
2
a).
After deconvolving the data and instrument response time function, we extract a lifetime of
T_{1}
=16.2 (6) ns, approximately two times longer than previously measured values for SnVs
Tchernij et al. 2017
;
Görlitz et al. 2020
;
Rugar et al. 2021
.
The lifetime is an upper limit for the coherence of the optical qubit.

The second figure of merit we investigate is the optical dephasing
T_{2}^{*}
time.
A quasi-continuous resonant driving of the C transition of the SnV is used for this measurement.
Using a continuous wave (CW) resonant laser, and utilizing an acousto-optic modulator to switch the laser on demand, we drive the qubit and record the arrival of the photons with respect to the starting time of the CW laser.
Fig.
2
b plots the histogram of the detected photons as a function of time. A temporal oscillation of the fluorescence signal is clearly visible.
These oscillations can be interpreted as coherent Rabi rotations between levels
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|3\right\rangle
.

We estimate an overall optical dephasing from the decay of the Rabi rotations by fitting the data with the time evolution of a two-level system in the presence of spontaneous decay of the excited state and pure dephasing (see supplementary materials) and extract
T_{\rm 2}^{*}
=10.9 (4) ns.
We note that we cannot distinguish individual dephasing mechanisms, such as intrinsic dephasing, spectral diffusion, and laser-induced dephasing separately and can only provide an overall rate.
These distinctions can be extracted using a Ramsey method in combination with a linewidth measurement
Arjona Martínez et al. 2022
;
Brevoord et al. 2023
.
Our experimental setup does not allow for a tunable delay for performing a Ramsey-type experiment yet. Although the dephasing time depends on the experimental details such as the applied laser’s power and phase stability, we indeed observe a partially coherent evolution of the optical qubit.

IV
Resonant coherent control

The conventional method of coherently driving a two-level system with an optical transition uses a resonant electromagnetic field. Such an application results in Rabi oscillations where the population is periodically transferred between the ground and excited states. Switching the field off at the desired state, i.e. generating a pulse, allows the preparation of a desired state. Quantum gates can therefore be implemented by tuning the pulse duration or power
Vajner et al.
;
Sbresny et al. 2022
.
So far, spin-photon entanglement has traditionally been created by carving optical pulses with electro-optical modulators. Such pulses generally have a duration around
\sim
2 ns and result in spin-photon fidelities ranging from 77%
Arjona Martínez et al. 2022
to 95%
Humphreys et al. 2018
. Here, we use ultrashort pulses from a mode-locked laser
Becker et al. 2016
to achieve state rotations and realize a subpicosecond quantum control scheme.

The first control experiment uses resonant pulses of 42.3 (2) GHz spectral width. This bandwidth corresponds to the narrowest pulse that can be generated with minimal loss of power and that, at the same time, allows for a high signal-to-background ratio. We estimate a pulse duration of
\sim
15 ps using an autocorrelator (see supplementary materials). The measured pulse duration is
\sim
50% longer than 10.4 ps, the duration expected from a Gaussian time-bandwith product limited pulse. The reason for this discrepancy is the dispersion introduced in the setup.

We demonstrate Rabi rotations up to
7\pi
as can be seen in Fig.
2
c. The observed oscillations in the fluorescence response of the emitter during a variation of the pulse’s energy constitute evidence for coherent control. A direct estimation of the gate fidelities for a
\pi
-pulse is not possible since there is no available method to probe the absolute excited state population.
Instead, we rely on an indirect method and fit the observed oscillations to a model based on a damped oscillator with a linear background contribution. The fidelity is then estimated by using the decay of the signal contrast. We note that the chosen model fails to replicate the experimental observations at higher values of pulse energy and we decide to discard those data points (grey in Fig. c) from the fit.
We are currently attempting to identify the physical mechanisms responsible for the distinct dynamics observed above 1
\sqrt{\rm pJ}
, which will be incorporated into our model.
The cut-off value is selected by monitoring the goodness-of-fit value. We find a population inversion of 98 (7)%. Such a value, however, is the result of multiple physical processes (e.g. ionization) and should only be treated as an estimate. We discuss these statistical effects and provide the details of the data analysis in the supplementary materials.

Finally, we characterize the photon purity, which is an additional figure of merit characterizing the emission. We implement a Hanbury Brown-Twiss interferometry experiment and find
g^{(2)}(0)
=0.1 (1) (Fig.
2
d), which shows clear antibunching (see supplementary materials) and single photon emission from the ultrashort pulse driven emitter.

Next, we investigate the shortest pulse duration which can still coherently excite the emitter.
Using an autocorrelation measurement, we identify pulses with subpicosecond durations (Fig.
2
e, inset). In the subpicosecond regime, the pulses have a bandwidth of
\sim
1400 GHz. The spectra of the wide bandwidth pulses are no longer Gaussian, but rather present a quadrilateral shape, due to the way they are sliced with the SLM.
Since the closest optical transition (i.e. the D transition between
\left|2\right\rangle
and
\left|3\right\rangle
) is about 830 GHz detuned, the selected bandwidth is at the limit for avoiding cross-coupling.
By varying the pulse amplitude of the broadband pulses, we achieve a
6\pi
rotation as shown in Fig.
2
e.

We quantify the visibility
{V=\frac{I(\pi)}{I(2\pi)}}
of rotations by dividing the experimental counts
I
at the
\pi
and
2\pi
rotations for narrowband and subpicosecond pulses. We find
{V_{\rm subpicosecond}=1.38\,(1)}
and
{V_{\rm narrowband}=46\,(5)}
.
The increased pulse energy requirements for subpicosecond control in comparison with narrowband (e.g.
\sim
40 times for a
\pi
-pulse) results in an increased background, which in turn reduces
V
and increases
g^{2}(0)
to 0.6 (1.1) (Fig.
2
f). Quantifying the background contribution, we compare
g^{2}(0)
values at
\pi
,
3\pi
, and
5\pi
rotations with subpicosecond pulses and again find an increasing background with increasing pulse energies (see supplementary materials).
Under these considerations, we associate the reduced contrast with the induced background and estimate that we can provide subpicosecond
\pi
-pulses but at the cost of a reduced single-photon purity.

Enabling coherent operations on this time scale is especially beneficial for analyzing ultrafast processes induced by light-matter interaction
Miloshevsky 2022
. Fast coherent optical control could also be relevant in the presence of large Purcell enhancement, which reduces the lifetime of emitters significantly and therefore makes faster gates a necessity, particularly for generating single photons in high repetition or for the creation of, cluster states
Tiurev et al. 2021
. Ultrashort pulses can also enable applications requiring multiple gate operations on the optical qubit in a limited time window
Weinzetl et al. 2019
. For example, a Hahn-echo type, phase-sensitive measurements, could extend the SnVs’ sensing capabilities
Degen et al. 2017
.

V
Nonresonant swing-up control: SUPER

In this section, we present the experimental implementation of the SUPER scheme. This method achieves coherent excitation by relying on highly detuned pulses, which by themselves can not significantly transfer the population to the excited state. The pulse configuration provides an advantage compared to the resonant scheme because the photons from the laser pulses can be spectrally distinguished from the signal. This benefit makes separation of excitation light and the emitted photons, while maintaining their purity and indistinguishability
Heinisch et al. 2023
, much more feasible.

For a two-level system driven by alternating laser pulses of two different laser frequencies, a coherent population inversion can be achieved if the pulses’ durations are chosen appropriately such that the two off-axis rotations transport the ground state to the excited state
Bracht et al. 2021
. The same coherent state transfer works for a combination of two nonresonant laser pulses with appropriate parameters such as amplitude, detuning, and bandwidth, even though the trajectory on the Bloch sphere no longer follows the idealized picture
Bracht et al. 2023a
.

Experimentally, we find the right configuration of pulses by fixing one pulse at a set frequency, bandwidth, and amplitude; and then performing a scan over detuning and amplitude of the second pulse.
Our fixed pulse has pulse area/amplitude that would result in a 7
\pi
rotation if applied resonantly with 116.6 (2) GHz detuning from the central frequency of the emission. After sweeping the second pulse’s amplitude and detuning, we observe fluorescence occurring in the region of 308 (11) GHz detuning at our maximum achievable power (Fig.
3
a). We estimate the maximum inversion by comparing the counts with a resonant
\pi
-pulse and estimate 55 (1)%. For verification, we apply the same measurement with either one of the pulses in isolation. In both cases, a negligible emission is observed (see supplementary materials). We can therefore confirm that the emission indeed comes from the collective action of both pulses.

We compare the measured fluorescence with a simulation of the coherent excitation using a theoretical model (see supplementary materials) conducted in the experimental scan range and respective pulse parameters. The simulation results are shown in Fig.
3
b. They show excellent qualitative agreement, in terms of the position of the resonance at the maximal power (experiment: 308 (11) GHz, simulation: 298 GHz) and inversion (experiment: 55 (1)%, simulation: 54%).

We also conduct an autocorrelation measurement of the emission to estimate the photon purity (Fig.
3
c). We obtain
g^{(2)}(0)
=0.1 (4) providing convincing evidence for single-photon emission.

Both the experimental and theoretical results confirm that with our current setup, we can achieve a
\pi/2
rotation with the generated SUPER pulses.
Since the non-unity inversion fidelity is a result of the achievable pulse powers, we extend the simulation to higher powers. We find that it is theoretically possible to increase the inversion fidelity up to 99.8% by simply increasing the power. Therefore, we conclude the implemented method is capable of achieving full inversion and facilitating deterministic photon extraction (see supplementary saterials).

VI
Conclusion and Outlook

We have implemented the SUPER scheme using two detuned pulses for the very first time with a color center in diamond and demonstrated nonresonant optical
\pi/2
gates. By implementing this method, we introduce less demanding spectral filtering methods for isolating the zero-phonon line emission of color centers in diamond.

Furthermore, we demonstrate the first ultrafast quantum control of an SnV color center, a core step for further developing building blocks of quantum information processing.
We also limit the resonant control scheme under one picosecond, a record time scale for color centers in diamond, paving the way for multi-gate coherent control of emitters in resonators with short lifetimes.

Extending on the demonstrated quantum control over a single axis and unlocking the full potential of the SnV’s optical qubit requires multi-axis control over the Bloch sphere. In principle, the rotation axis can be manipulated by modulating the relative phase of the pulses.

The demonstrated coherent control schemes of the optical qubit can be integrated with spin qubit control of the SnV. Combined, it will be possible to utilize control over both degrees of freedom for spin-photon entanglement experiments
Bracht et al. 2023b
and pave the way for complex quantum information applications.
