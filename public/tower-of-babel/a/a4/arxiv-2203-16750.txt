Abstract
. The study of torus orbit closures in the (complete) flag variety was initiated by Klyachko and Gelfand–Serganova in the mid-1980s. In this chapter, we present some of the work by Klyachko and Gelfand–Serganova and our recent work on the topology, geometry, and combinatorics of torus orbit closures in the flag variety.

Let

\flag(n)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(\{0\}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n})\mid\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{i}=i\>\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n\},

where each

is a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group on
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. An element of
S_{n}
determines a permutation flag. The natural action of the general linear group
{\rm GL}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
induces an action of
{\rm GL}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
on
\flag(n)
. Let
T
be the maximal torus of
{\rm GL}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
consisting of diagonal matrices, which is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
where
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
. Then the set of permutation flags is exactly the
T
-fixed point set
\flag(n)^{T}
in
\flag(n)
and we identify
\flag(n)^{T}
with
S_{n}
. A key tool to study the
T
-action on
\flag(n)
is a moment map

\mu\colon\flag(n)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

which satisfies the following properties:

\bullet

\mu(w)=(w^{-1}(1),\dots,w^{-1}(n))
for
w\in\flag(n)^{T}=S_{n}
,

\bullet

\mu(\flag(n))
is the convex hull of
n!
points
\mu(\flag(n)^{T})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, that is, the permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
.

We will explore
T
-varieties (i.e., an algebraic variety with an algebraic
T
-action) in
\flag(n)
with respect to the action of the maxima torus
T
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rm GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})}
.
With this understanding, we will explain the content of each section.

The closure of a

Y

Since scalar matrices in

(Q1)

Characterize the polytopes which arise as
\mu(Y)

T

(Q2)

Describe the fan of

Gelfand–Serganova

must be parallel to an edge of the permutohedron

. Since the edges of

-polytope

is a

-polytope. Unfortunately, the converse is not true and the complete solution to
(Q1)

We note that the notion of a

[
77

[
33
]
, and they are also called

[

]
), and polymatroids (see
[
81
]

\Phi

which satisfies the Maximality (or Minimality) Condition (see Definition

). The fixed point set

regarded as a subset of
S_{n}
is a Coxeter matroid. One can define a retraction

\mathscr{R}^{m}_{\mathscr{M}}\colon S_{n}\to\mathscr{M}\subset S_{n}

for a Coxeter matroid

and describe the fan of

(see Corollary

Here, the suffix

shows that this retraction is defined using the matroid structure on

, so that one can distinguish it from the two more retractions introduced later (see Subsection

The retraction

). It also has the following interesting property. The
1

is a graph with

is a Coxeter matroid, then for each

Note that

We study the case when

is a toric variety with the

is not necessarily smooth while every toric Schubert variety

for the pairs

2.
Torus orbit closures (general)

In this section, we review the torus actions on flag varieties and the closure of a torus orbit. Moreover, we consider properties of the moment map images of torus orbit closures. Indeed, the fixed point set of a torus orbit closure becomes a Coxeter matroid.
Finally, we discuss how to describe the fan of a torus orbit closure.

2.1.
Torus actions on flag varieties

Let
G
be the general linear group
{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
over
{\mathbb{C}}
,
B\subset G
the set of upper triangular matrices, and
T\subset G
the set of diagonal matrices. Let
B^{-}\subset G
be the set of lower triangular matrices.
We denote by
S_{n}
the symmetric group on
[n]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then
T=B\cap B^{-}\cong({{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}})^{n}
and
B^{-}=w_{0}Bw_{0}
, where
w_{0}
is the longest element
[n,n-1,\dots,1]\in S_{n}
.
The homogeneous space
G/B
can be identified with the
flag variety

\flag(n)
defined by

\flag(n)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(\{0\}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n})\mid\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{i}=i\>\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n\},

where
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is considered as the complex vector space consisting of
column

For

\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{n}
be the column vectors of the matrix

g=[\mathbf{c}_{1}\ \cdots\ \mathbf{c}_{n}]
. Then

For an element

.
The left multiplication by
T

Lemma 2.1

(
[
42

The set of

2.4.
Coxeter matroids and Gelfand–Serganova Theorem

To study the combinatorial properties of torus orbit closures, Gelfand and Serganova introduced the notion of Coxeter matroids.
In this subsection, we recall the definition of Coxeter matroids and the characterization of Coxeter matroids in terms of polytopes by Gelfand–Serganova. It turns out that the permutations indexing the
T
-fixed points of a
T
-orbit closure in the flag variety
G/B
is a Coxeter matroid.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group, so generators of
W
are prescribed. The symmetric group
S_{n}
on
[n]
with adjacent transpositions as generators is a typical example of a Coxeter group.
For
u\in W
, let
\leq^{u}
denote the
u
-shifted order
, that is,
v\leq^{u}w
means
u^{-1}v\leq u^{-1}w
in the Bruhat order on
W
. Note that
\leq^{u}
is a partial order on
W
with
u
as the smallest element.

Definition 2.11
.

A subset
\mathscr{M}
of a finite Coxeter group
W
is called a
Coxeter matroid
if it satisfies the
Maximality Property
:
for any
u\in W
, there is a unique element
v\in\mathscr{M}
such that
w\leq^{u}v
for all
w\in\mathscr{M}
.

We note that for a finite Coxeter group
W
in type
A
, Coxeter matroids are also called
flag matroids
. See
[
89
]
.
For more details on flag matroids and flag matroid polytopes, we refer the reader to
[
6
,
23
,
12
]
.

Remark 2.12
.

(1)

Since the multiplication by the longest element of
W
reverses the Bruhat order on
W
,
the Maximality Property is equivalent to the
Minimality Property
: for any
u\in W
, there exists a unique element
v\in\mathscr{M}
such that
v\leq^{u}w
for all
w\in\mathscr{M}
.

(2)

In fact, a Coxeter matroid is defined more generally in
[
17
]
. Let
W_{P}
be a parabolic subgroup of a finite Coxeter group
W
. Then the Bruhat order on
W
induces a partial order on
W/W_{P}
and a subset
\mathscr{M}
of
W/W_{P}
is called a Coxeter matroid if it satisfies the Maximality Property above. A Coxeter matroid in Definition
2.11
is the case where
W_{P}
is the identity subgroup and an ordinary matroid can be regarded as the case where
W=S_{n}
and
W_{P}
is a maximal parabolic subgroup (see
[
17
, Theorem 1.3.1]
).

81
]
).

Choose a point
\nu\in V
which is not fixed by any reflection in
W
.
For a subset
\mathscr{M}
of
W
, we define
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
to be the convex hull of the
\mathscr{M}
-orbit
\{w\cdot\nu\mid w\in\mathscr{M}\}
of the point
\nu
in
V
. When
\mathscr{M}=W
,
\Delta_{W}
is called the

-permutohedron
(see
[
39
, Section 2.4]
,
[

]
, and
[

]
). Two vertices

and

of

are joined by an edge of
\Delta_{W}
if and only if
v^{-1}w
is a generator of

(see
[

, Lemma 2.13]
). Thus
any edge of
\Delta_{W}
is parallel to a root and vice versa. Therefore, the

-permutohedron

is a
\Phi
-polytope and we may say that a convex polytope
\Delta
in
V
is a
\Phi
-polytope if every edge of
\Delta
is parallel to an edge of
\Delta_{W}
.

However,
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
is not necessarily a

-polytope unless

. The following is a part of the Gelfand–Serganova Theorem

]
(also see

).

Theorem 2.15

(Gelfand–Serganova Theorem)
.

A subset
\mathscr{M}
of a finite Coxeter group
W
is a Coxeter matroid if and only if
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
is a
\Phi
-polytope. Therefore, the permutations indexing the
T
-fixed points of a
T
-orbit closure in the flag variety
G/B
is a Coxeter matroid by Theorem
2.9
.

We provide the proof for the case
W=S_{n}
. We refer the reader to
[
17
]
for more details.
Before giving it, we prepare two lemmas.
Suppose that

V=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}=0\}

and
S_{n}
acts on
V
as permuting coordinates, i.e.,
u\cdot(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=(a_{u^{-1}(1)},\dots,a_{u^{-1}(n)})
, in other words,

u\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbf{e}_{u(i)}.

We define the ordering
\preceq^{u}
on
V
by putting
x\preceq^{u}y
for
x,y\in V
if there exists non-negative constants
c_{i}\geq 0
such that

y-x=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{u(i)}-\mathbf{e}_{u(i+1)}),

which is equivalent to saying
u^{-1}\cdot y-u^{-1}\cdot x=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1})
with the same coefficients
c_{i}
. This means

(2.9)

x\preceq^{u}y\iff u^{-1}\cdot x\preceq^{e}u^{-1}\cdot y.

Lemma 2.16

(
[
17
, Lemma 6.2.3]
)
.

Suppose that
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{n})\in V
satisfies
\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}<\dots<\nu_{n}
.
If
v\leq^{u}w
, then
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{u}w\cdot\nu
.

Proof.

Note that
v\leq^{u}w
if and only if
u^{-1}v\leq u^{-1}w
, and
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{u}w\cdot\nu
if and only if
u^{-1}v\cdot\nu\preceq^{e}u^{-1}w\cdot\nu
(see (
2.9
)). Hence it suffices to prove the statement in the case
u=e
.
Suppose
v\leq w
. Then we have
v^{-1}\leq w^{-1}
and hence

\{v^{-1}(1),\dots,v^{-1}(d)\}\!\!\uparrow\>\leq\{w^{-1}(1),\dots,w^{-1}(d)\}\!\!\uparrow
for
1\leq d\leq n
(cf.
[
14
, §3.2]
).
This implies
\sum_{i=1}^{d}v^{-1}(i)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}w^{-1}(i)
for
1\leq d\leq n
,
so we get

(2.10)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{v^{-1}(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{w^{-1}(i)}\quad\text{ for }1\leq d\leq n.

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}w\cdot\nu-v\cdot\nu&=(\nu_{w^{-1}(1)},\dots,\nu_{w^{-1}(n)})-(\nu_{v^{-1}(1)},\dots,\nu_{v^{-1}(n)})\\
&=\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{w^{-1}(i)}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{v^{-1}(i)}\right)(\mathbf{e}_{d}-\mathbf{e}_{d+1}).\end{split}

Accordingly, by (
2.10
), we obtain
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{e}w\cdot\nu
and we are done.
∎

The converse in Lemma
2.16
does not hold in general. For instance, if
v=1432
and
w=4123
, then
v\not\leq w
but since

\begin{split}w\cdot\nu-v\cdot\nu&=(\nu_{2},\nu_{3},\nu_{4},\nu_{1})-(\nu_{1},\nu_{4},\nu_{3},\nu_{2})\\
&=(\nu_{2}-\nu_{1})(\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{2})+(\nu_{2}+\nu_{3}-\nu_{1}-\nu_{4})(\mathbf{e}_{2}-\mathbf{e}_{3})+(\nu_{2}-\nu_{1})(\mathbf{e}_{3}-\mathbf{e}_{4}),\end{split}

we have
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{e}w\cdot\nu
if
\nu_{2}+\nu_{3}\geq 0
(note that
\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\nu_{3}+\nu_{4}=0
because
\nu\in V
).

However, the converse holds in the following special case.

Lemma 2.17

(
[
17
, Lemma 6.2.5]
)
.

Let
\nu
be as in Lemma
2.16
.
Suppose that
w\cdot\nu-v\cdot\nu
is parellel to a root in
\Phi
. Then
v\leq^{u}w
if
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{u}w\cdot\nu
.

Proof.

As noted in the proof of Lemma
2.16
, we may assume
u=e
. If
v\cdot\nu\preceq^{e}w\cdot\nu
, then

w\cdot\nu-v\cdot\nu=c(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j})

for some
c>0
and
i<j
. This implies that
w^{-1}=v^{-1}t_{i,j}
and
v^{-1}(i)<v^{-1}(j)
since
c>0
, where
t_{i,j}
denotes the transposition interchanging
i
and
j
. Therefore
v^{-1}\leq w^{-1}
and hence

.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.15
in type A.

We take
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{n})\in V
which satisfies
\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}<\dots<\nu_{n}
.
We will prove that if
\mathscr{M}\subset S_{n}
is a Coxeter matroid, then
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
is a
\Phi
-polytope.
Assume on the contrary that
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
is not a
\Phi
-polytope.
Then there exists an edge

with vertices
v_{1}\cdot\nu
and
v_{2}\cdot\nu
that is not parallel to any root. Consider a linear function

that is constant on

and takes smaller values on the other points of
\Delta_{\mathscr{M}}
.

Since the edge

is not parallel to any root, we may assume that

is not vanishing on any root in
\Phi
. Accordingly, there is a unique simple system of roots
\tilde{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\alpha}_{n-1}
such that

for

.
Moreover, since the group
S_{n}
acts transitively on the set of all simple root systems, there exists an element
u\in S_{n}
which sends
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1}\mid i=1,\dots,n-1\}
to
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\alpha}_{n-1}\}
.

For any

we have

and the vector

has at least one negative coefficient with respect to

.
Therefore, we have

and this implies

.
Accordingly, by the Maximality Property,

must be the maximal element with respect to the
u
-shifted order. However, by the similar argument, one can show that
v_{2}
is also the maximal element with respect to the

-shifted order. This contradicts the Maximality Property.

Now we prove that if

is a
\Phi

and

with respect to

for some

. Let

and

the vertices adjacent to the vertex

. Set

\beta_{p}=x_{p}\cdot\nu-v_{1}\cdot\nu

(p=1,\dots,\ell)
and

.

Since

is a

, and therefore either

or

.

Suppose that

for all

. Then

.
Therefore

Then

and

. The same argument works when

, so we deduce a contradiction in any case. Thus

is a Coxeter matroid.
∎

2.5.
Description of the fan of a torus orbit closure

For a point
x\in G/B
, the closure
Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\overline{T\cdot x}
of its

-orbit

is a (possibly non-normal) toric variety in
G/B
.
Although Proposition
2.6
provides how to find the fixed point set
Y^{T}
and the convexity theorem leads us to the moment polytope
\mu(Y)
, it is hard to describe the fan of
Y
from
\mu(Y)
.
In this subsection and the next subsection, we identify
(G/B)^{T}=W
for simplicity, so
Y^{T}\subset W
.
We define a retraction
\mathscr{R}^{g}_{Y}
(called geometric retraction) of the Weyl group
W
of
G
onto the
T
-fixed point set

of

by using the Orbit-Cone correspondence in toric variety and describe the fan of

using the retraction.

We think of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
of the maximal compact torus of
T
as
\Hom({\mathbb{C}}^{\ast},T)\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
and the lattice
\mathfrak{t}_{\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathfrak{t}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
as
\Hom({\mathbb{C}}^{\ast},T)
, where
\Hom({\mathbb{C}}^{*},T)
denotes the group of algebraic homomorphisms from

to

, i.e., the one parameter subgroup of

(see
[
28
, §1]
). The vector space
\mathfrak{t}_{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ast}
dual to
\mathfrak{t}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
can be thought of as
\Hom(T,{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast})\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
and the set
\Phi
of roots of

is a finite subset of
\Hom(T,{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast})=\mathfrak{t}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\ast}
. The Weyl group
W
of

acts on

as the adjoint action and on its dual space
\mathfrak{t}_{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ast}
as the coadjoint action, i.e.,

(w\cdot f)(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}f(\Ad_{w^{-1}}(x))
for

,

and

.

The Borel subgroup
B
determines the set
\Phi^{+}
of positive roots and the tangent space

of

-fixed point

In this section, we observe that generic torus orbit closures in the flag variety
\flag(n)
are in fact permutohedral varieties and then discuss their topology. Their Poincaré polynomials turn out to be Eulerian polynomials. We also discuss Klyachko’s theorem which describes the restriction image of the cohomology of
\flag(n)
to that of the generic torus orbit closure in
\flag(n)
. Finally we discuss a generalization of Klyachko’s theorem to Hessenberg varieties.

The image of the moment map

\mu\colon\flag(n)=G/B\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

defined in (
2.6
) is the permutohedron

\Pi_{n}=\mathrm{Conv}\{(w(1),\dots,w(n))\mid w\in S_{n}\}.

Note that
\Pi_{n}
is the
S_{n}
-permutohedron in Subsection
2.4
.
The permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
lies on the hyperplane

(3.1)

\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}=n(n+1)/2\}

and one can easily see that
\Pi_{n}
is of dimension
n-1
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a
T
-orbit
O
in
\flag(n)
is
generic
if
\mu(\overline{O})=\Pi_{n}
, in other words if
\overline{O}^{T}=S_{n}
.

3.1.
Faces of the permutohedron
\Pi_{n}

We recall some facts on the faces of the permutohedron
\Pi_{n}

The permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
is contained in the half space of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by

\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\middle|\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\right\}\quad(k=1,\dots,n-1)

and the intersection of its boundary with
\Pi_{n}
, that is,

(3.2)

\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\Pi_{n}\,\,\middle|\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\right\}\quad(k=1,\dots,n-1)

is a facet of
\Pi_{n}
that contains the vertex
(1,\dots,n)
. This provides all the facets of
\Pi_{n}
which meet at the vertex
(1,\dots,n)
. Since
\Pi_{n}
is invariant under permutations of the coordinates of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it follows that
\Pi_{n}
is a simple polytope of dimension

and the facets of
\Pi_{n}
meeting at the vertex
(w(1),\dots,w(n))
of
\Pi_{n}

(w\in S_{n})

(3.3)

\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\Pi_{n}\,\,\middle|\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{w^{-1}(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\right\}\quad(k=1,\dots,n-1).

The facet in (
3.3

\{w^{-1}(1),\dots,w^{-1}(k)\}
of
[n]
and the observation above shows that there is a bijective correspondence between non-empty proper subsets of

and facets of
\Pi_{n}
. Indeed, the facet of
\Pi_{n}

The following lemma can easily be proved.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be non-empty proper subsets of
[n]
. Then
F(A)\cap F(B)\not=\emptyset
if and only if
A\subset B
or
B\subset A
. Therefore, a codimension
k
face of
\Pi_{n}
is
\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}F(A_{i})
with
A_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{k}
.

Since
\Pi_{n}
lies on the hyperplane in (
3.1
) which is perpendicular to the vector
(1,\dots,1)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the normal fan
\Sigma(\Pi_{n})
of
\Pi_{n}
is defined in the quotient space
N_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{R}}(1,\dots,1)
, where the lattice
N
of
N_{\mathbb{R}}
is the one induced from the standard lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. By (
3.4
), the primitive (inward) normal vector
\delta_{A}
to the hyperplane in (
3.4
) defining the facet
F(A)
is the
(0,1)
-vector with
1
in the
a
th coordinate for each
a\in A
and
0
otherwise. Therefore we have the following.

Lemma 3.3
.

The quotient image of the vector
\delta_{A}
on
N_{\mathbb{R}}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{R}}(1,\dots,1)
is the primitive ray vector in the normal fan
\Sigma(\Pi_{n})
corresponding to the facet
F(A)
.

The maximal cone in the fan
\Sigma(\Pi_{n})
corresponding to the vertex
(1,\dots,n)
is spanned by the ray vectors corresponding to the
n-1
facets in (
3.2
) and those ray vectors are the projection image of the following
n-1
vectors on
N_{\mathbb{R}}
:

(1,0,\dots,0),\quad(1,1,0,\dots,0),\quad\dots,\quad(1,\dots,1,0).

The other maximal cones in
\Sigma(\Pi_{n})
are obtained by permuting the above cone. This shows that the collection of maximal cones coincides with the (closures of) Weyl chambers in type
A
.
The maximal cones determine the fan
\Sigma(\Pi_{n})
and the permutohedral variety
{\Perm}_{n}
is the toric variety whose fan has the (closure of) Weyl chambers as maximal cones. Therefore, we obtain the following.

Proposition 3.4
.

The maximal cones in the fan of a generic torus orbit closure in the flag variety
\flag(n)
are the
(
closures of
)
Weyl chambers. Therefore, the closure of a generic torus orbit in the flag variety
\flag(n)
is isomorphic to the permutohedral variety
{\Perm}_{n}
.

The cohomology ring of a compact smooth toric variety is explicitly described in terms of the associated fan. Applying the general result to our setting together with Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

The cohomology ring of the permutohedral variety
{\Perm}_{n}
has the following presentation:

H^{*}(\Perm_{n};{\mathbb{Z}})={\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{A}\mid\emptyset\subsetneq A\subsetneq[n]]/\mathcal{I}

where
\deg\tau_{A}=2
and
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal generated by the following two types of elements:

(1)

\tau_{A}\tau_{B}
for
A\not\subset B
or
B\not\subset A
,

(2)

\sum_{p\in A}\tau_{A}-\sum_{q\in B}\tau_{B}
for
p,q\in[n]
.

Example 3.6
.

Take
n=3
. Then
H^{*}({\Perm}_{3};{\mathbb{Z}})
is generated by six elements

\tau_{\{1\}},\ \tau_{\{2\}},\ \tau_{\{3\}},\ \tau_{\{1,2\}},\ \tau_{\{1,3\}},\ \tau_{\{2,3\}}

with relations

(1)

\tau_{\{1\}}\tau_{\{2\}}=\tau_{\{1\}}\tau_{\{3\}}=\tau_{\{2\}}\tau_{\{3\}}=\tau_{\{1\}}\tau_{\{2,3\}}=\tau_{\{2\}}\tau_{\{1,3\}}=\tau_{\{3\}}\tau_{\{1,2\}}

\quad=\tau_{\{1,2\}}\tau_{\{1,3\}}=\tau_{\{1,3\}}\tau_{\{2,3\}}=\tau_{\{1,2\}}\tau_{\{2,3\}}=0
,

(2)

\tau_{\{1\}}+\tau_{\{1,2\}}+\tau_{\{1,3\}}=\tau_{\{2\}}+\tau_{\{1,2\}}+\tau_{\{2,3\}}=\tau_{\{3\}}+\tau_{\{1,3\}}+\tau_{\{2,3\}}
.

It follows that
H^{2}({\Perm}_{3})
is freely generated by four classes, say

\tau_{\{1\}},\ \tau_{\{2\}},\ \tau_{\{1,2\}},\ \tau_{\{2,3\}}
,
and
H^{4}({\Perm_{3}})
is freely generated by one class, say
\tau_{\{1\}}\tau_{\{1,2\}}
. Therefore, the Poincaré polynomial of
{\Perm}_{3}
is given by

(3.5)

{{\mathscr{P}\textup{oin}}}({\Perm}_{3},t)=1+4t^{2}+t^{4}.

In fact, since the fan of
{\Perm}_{3}
is isomorphic to the fan of the toric variety obtained by blowing up
{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}
with the standard
T^{2}
-action at the three fixed points,
{\Perm}_{3}
is isomorphic to

{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}\#3\overline{{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}}
, the equivariant connected sum of
{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}
with three copies of
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}}
, where
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}}
stands for
{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}
with the opposite orientation.

3.2.
Eulerian polynomial

The
set of

ascents

{\rm asc}(w)
of
w\in S_{n}
is defined by

(3.6)

{\rm asc}(w)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\{i\in[n-1]\mid w(i)<w(i+1)\}

and the Eulerian polynomial
A_{n}(t)
is defined by

A_{n}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{w\in S_{n}}t^{{\rm asc}(w)}.

Here, for a set
A
, we denote by
\#A
the cardinality of
A
.
Note that
0\leq{\mathrm{asc}}(w)\leq n-1
for any
w\in S_{n}
, where the former equality is attained only when
w=[n,n-1,\dots,1]
and the latter equality is attained only when
w
is the identity. Therefore, the constant term of
A_{n}(t)
is
1
and the highest degree term is
t^{n-1}
. Eulerian polynomials
A_{n}(t)
for
n\leq 5
are given as follows:

(3.7)

\begin{split}&A_{1}(t)=1,\quad A_{2}(t)=1+t,\quad A_{3}(t)=1+4t+t^{2},\\
&A_{4}(t)=1+11t+11t^{2}+t^{3},\quad A_{5}(t)=1+26t+66t^{2}+26t^{3}+t^{4}.\end{split}

Remark 3.7
.

(1)

The
descent

{\rm des}(w)
of
w\in S_{n}
is defined by

{\rm des}(w)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\{i\in[n-1]\mid w(i)>w(i+1)\}

and it holds that

\sum_{w\in S_{n}}t^{{\rm asc}(w)}=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}t^{{\rm des}(w)}.

Therefore, we may define the Eulerian polynomial
A_{n}(t)
using the descents.

(2)

The Eulerian polynomial
A_{n}(t)
was originally defined by Euler as the polynomial which appears in the numerator of the following formula:

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{n}t^{k}=\frac{tA_{n}(t)}{(1-t)^{n+1}}.

As easily checked, the Eulerian polynomials defined this way have the following recurrence relation

A_{n+1}(t)=(nt+1)A_{n}(t)-t(t-1)\frac{dA_{n}(t)}{dt}

while one can see that the Eulerian polynomials defined in (
3.6
) satisfy the same recurrence relation as above. Since
A_{1}(t)=1
in both definitions, they define the same family of polynomials.

It follows from (
3.5
) and (
3.7
) that
{{\mathscr{P}\textup{oin}}}(\Perm_{3},t)=A_{3}(t^{2})
. This is not a coincidence.

Theorem 3.8

(
[
57
, Theorem 2]
)
.

{{\mathscr{P}\textup{oin}}}(\Perm_{n},t)=A_{n}(t^{2})
for any
n\geq 1
.

Remark 3.9
.

The polynomials
A_{n}(t)
for
n\leq 5
in (
3.7
) are palindromic and unimodal. Indeed, this holds for any
A_{n}(t)

since the Poincaré duality implies the palindromicity and the hard Lefschetz theorem implies the unimodality because
\Perm_{n}
is a smooth projective toric variety.

In order to give a proof of Theorem
3.8
, we recall a fact on edges of the permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Two vertices
(u(1),\dots,u(n))
and
(v(1),\dots,v(n))
of
\Pi_{n}

(u,v\in S_{n})
are joined by an edge of
\Pi_{n}
if and only if there is an adjacent transposition
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
for
i\in[n-1]
such that
v=s_{i}u
.

Proof.

We note that

(3.8)

(v(1),\dots,v(n))-(1,\dots,n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i}v(k)-\sum_{k=1}^{i}k\right)(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1})

where
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n}
denote the standard basis vectors of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The coefficient of
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1}
in (
3.8
) is non-negative for any
i
and
v\in S_{n}
. Moreover, when
v=s_{i}
, the right hand side at (
3.8
) is
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1}
. This implies the lemma when
u
is the identity. The general case follows from this special case and the invariance of
\Pi_{n}
under permutations of the coordinates of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

Equation (
3.8

(3.9)

(v(1),\dots,v(n))-(u(1),\dots,u(n))=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i}v(k)-\sum_{k=1}^{i}u(k)\right)(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1}).

By Lemma
3.10
, the endpoints of the edges emanating from the vertex

are
(s_{i}u(1),\dots,s_{i}u(n))
for
i\in[n-1]
. Set
p=u^{-1}(i)
and

and

(3.10)

(s_{i}u(1),\dots,s_{i}u(n))-(u(1),\dots,u(n))=\mathbf{e}_{p}-\mathbf{e}_{q}=\mathbf{e}_{u^{-1}(i)}-\mathbf{e}_{u^{-1}(i+1)}.

Take real numbers
a_{1}>\cdots>a_{n}
and consider a linear function

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}.

It follows from (

f(s_{i}u(1),\dots,s_{i}u(n))-f(u(1),\dots,u(n))=a_{u^{-1}(i)}-a_{u^{-1}(i+1)}\begin{cases}>0\ \ &\text{ if }u^{-1}(i)<u^{-1}(i+1),\\
<0\ \ &\text{ if }u^{-1}(i)>u^{-1}(i+1).\end{cases}

Recall that

for the moment map
\mu\colon\flag(n)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and we labeled the vertex

Then, the observation above shows that

Therefore, discrete Morse theory applied to the function

3.3.
Klyachko’s result

Since

\flag(n)=\{V_{\bullet}=(\{0\}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n})\mid\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{i}=i\>\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n\},

there is a sequence of tautological complex vector bundles on
\flag(n)
:

E_{1}\subset E_{2}\subset\cdots\subset E_{n}=\flag(n)\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

where

E_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(V_{\bullet},v)\in\flag(n)\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\mid v\in V_{i}\}\qquad(i=1,\dots,n).

We define

x_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}c_{1}(E_{i}/E_{i-1})\qquad\text{for $i=1,\dots,n$,}

where
E_{0}=\flag(n)
, i.e., the
0
-dimensional vector bundle over
\flag(n)
and
c_{1}(E)
denotes the first Chern class of a vector bundle
E
.
Since

E_{n}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}/E_{i-1}

and
E_{n}
is the trivial vector bundle
\flag(n)\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

1=c(E_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(E_{i}/E_{i-1})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x_{i}).

Here,
c(E)
denotes the total Chern class of a vector bundle
E
.
Therefore, the
i
th elementary symmetric polynomial
e_{i}(x)
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
vanishes in
H^{*}(\flag(n);{\mathbb{Z}})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. The following theorem shows that
H^{*}(\flag(n);{\mathbb{Z}})
is generated by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
as a ring and any polynomial in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
which vanishes in
H^{*}(\flag(n);{\mathbb{Z}})
is generated by
e_{i}(x)
’s.

Theorem 3.12

(Borel
[
16
]
)
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be as above. Then

H^{*}(\flag(n);{\mathbb{Z}})={\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/(e_{1}(x),\dots,e_{n}(x))\quad\text{as a ring}.

We denote by
\Perm_{n}
a generic torus orbit closure in the flag variety
\flag(n)
because it is isomorphic to the permutohedral variety
\Perm_{n}
. The natural action of
S_{n}
on the fan of
\Perm_{n}
induces an action of
S_{n}
on
\Perm_{n}
and hence on its cohomology ring.

Theorem 3.13

(Klyachko
[
57
]
)
.

Let
\iota\colon\Perm_{n}\to\flag(n)
be the inclusion map. Then the image of the restriction map

\iota^{*}\colon H^{*}(\flag(n);\mathbb{Q})\to H^{*}(\Perm_{n};\mathbb{Q})

agrees with the ring of
S_{n}
-invariants
H^{*}(\Perm_{n};\mathbb{Q})^{S_{n}}
and

(3.11)

H^{*}(\Perm_{n};\mathbb{Q})^{S_{n}}=\mathbb{Q}[\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1}]/(\omega_{i}(\omega_{i-1}-2\omega_{i}+\omega_{i+1})\mid i=1,\dots,n-1)

where
\omega_{i}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{i}
and
\omega_{0}=\omega_{n}=0
.

Remark 3.14
.

(1)

The cohomology ring of
\Perm_{n}
is explicitly described in Theorem
3.5
. The
S_{n}
-orbit of
\tau_{A}
with
|A|=i
is
\sum_{|B|=i}\tau_{B}
which is equal to
x_{i}-x_{i+1}
.

(2)

If we set
\alpha_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{i}-x_{i+1}
, then
\omega_{i-1}-2\omega_{i}+\omega_{i+1}=-\alpha_{i}
. Therefore, we may express the ideal in (
3.11
) as
(\omega_{i}\alpha_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,n-1)
. Note that the
\omega_{i}
’s correspond to the fundamental weights while
\alpha_{i}
’s correspond to the roots in type
A
.

3.4.
Relation to Hessenberg varieties

Theorem
3.13
is generalized to the setting of Hessenberg varieties. Given a square matrix
A
of size
n
with complex entries and a function
h\colon[n]\to[n]
(called a
Hessenberg function
) satisfying

h(1)\leq h(2)\leq\cdots\leq h(n)\quad\text{and}\quad h(j)\geq j\quad\text{for all }j\in[n],

the Hessenberg variety
{\rm Hess}(A,h)
is defined as

{\rm Hess}(A,h)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{V_{\bullet}\in\flag(n)\mid A(V_{j})\subset V_{h(j)}\>\text{ for all }j\in[n]\},

where the matrix
A
is regarded as a linear transformation on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
We notice that
{\rm Hess}(A,h)
is isomorphic to
{\rm Hess}(gAg^{-1},h)
via the action of
G
on
\flag(n)
. Accordingly, for a fixed Hessenberg function
h
, the geometry of Hessenberg variety
{\rm Hess}(A,h)
depends only on the conjugacy class of
A
.
We often express the Hessenberg function
h
as a vector
(h(1),\dots,h(n))
by listing the values of
h
. When
h=(n,\dots,n)
,
it is obvious from the definition that
{\rm Hess}(A,h)
is the flag variety
\flag(n)
regardless of
A
.

The Hessenberg variety
{\rm Hess}(S,h)
for a square matrix
S
of size
n
with distinct eigenvalues is called
regular semisimple
.
It is known that

(1)

{\rm Hess}(S,h)
is smooth,

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}{\rm Hess}(S,h)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(h(j)-j)
,

(3)

{\rm Hess}(S,h)
is connected if and only if
h(j)\geq j+1
for all
j\in[n-1]
.

The topology of
{\rm Hess}(S,h)
is independent of the choice of
S
and we take
S

4.2.
Generalized Eulerian polynomials

In this subsection, we introduce a polynomial

\mu\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}Y_{w}\to{\mathsf{Q}}_{w}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

4.3.
Toric Schubert varieties

The complexity of a
T
-variety
X
is the codimension of a maximal dimensional
T
-orbit in
X
. A toric variety is of complexity
0
and in this case there is a nice relation between geometry and combinatorics (see Appendix
A.1
). Indeed, a toric variety has a fruitful symmetry given by the
T
-action and we can extract the essential geometric information (e.g., smoothness) from the corresponding combinatorial object. One can expect a similar nice relation when the complexity is small enough. In this section, we present such kind of results for Schubert varieties of complexity
0
or
1
.

Definition 4.19
.

For
w\in S_{n}
, we define

c(w)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X_{w}-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Y_{w}=\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}X_{w}-\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{w}=\ell(w)-\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{w},

and call

the
complexity
of the Schubert variety
X_{w}
(or the
complexity
of
w
).

When
c(w)=0
, we have
X_{w}=Y_{w}
; so the Schubert variety
X_{w}
is a toric variety.
Let
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{m}}
be a reduced decomposition of
w
.
Then it is known from
[
37
,
56
]
that the Schubert variety
X_{w}
is a toric variety if and only if
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}
are distinct. Moreover, all toric Schubert varieties are Bott manifolds, which are smooth projective toric varieties whose moment map images are combinatorially equivalent to cubes.
We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for more details on Bott manifolds. Furthermore, all toric Schubert varieties are Bott–Samelson varieties which are smooth projective varieties. We will explain them in detail in Section
4.4
.

There are several equivalent conditions characterizing toric Schubert varieties as follows.

Theorem 4.20
.

[

,

,

,

(0)

is a toric variety
(

(1)

X_{w}
is a smooth toric variety.

(2)

w
avoids the patterns

and
3412
.

(3)

A reduced decomposition of

consists of distinct letters.

(4)

is isomorphic to a Bott–Samelson variety.

(5)

The Bruhat interval

is isomorphic to the Boolean algebra

of rank

as posets.

(6)

{\mathsf{Q}}_{w}
is combinatorially equivalent to the cube of dimension

.

Here, we say that a poset

The fan of a toric Schubert variety

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{m}}
. For

, we set

a_{k}=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}(i_{k}),\quad b_{k}=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}(i_{k}+1)

where we understand

and

. Then we have

s_{i_{m}}\cdots\hat{s}_{i_{k}}\cdots s_{i_{1}}=w^{-1}t_{a_{k},b_{k}}.

Proof.

For any

and transposition

, we have

(4.3)

vt_{p,q}v^{-1}=t_{v(p),v(q)}.

Noting that

and

, we apply (

s_{i_{m}}\cdots\hat{s}_{i_{k}}\cdots s_{i_{1}}=w^{-1}s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k-1}}\cdots s_{i_{2}}s_{i_{1}}.

Then the lemma immediately follows.

The

Theorem 4.22
.

Let

be a reduced decomposition of

Assume that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc}1&0&\cdots&0&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0&&-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&a_{j,k}&\ddots&\\

where

for

(

Proof.

Since the fan of

and

as below.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n}

and we set

(4.5)

\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1}\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n-1.

spanned by

is the quotient space

\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\ast}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbf{e}_{i}\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n-1

dual to (

) through the standard scalar product on

At the vertex
\mathsf{e}

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccc}w^{-1}t_{a_{k},b_{k}}=&w^{-1}(1)&w^{-1}(2)&\cdots&w^{-1}(b_{k})&\cdots&w^{-1}(a_{k})&\cdots&w^{-1}(n),\\

Moreover, we get

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}(\mathbf{v}_{i_{k}})

\begin{split}\mathbf{w}_{k}&=-s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{k-1}}(\mathbf{v}_{i_{k}})\\

The facet normal vectors at

5.
Generic torus orbit closures in Richardson varieties

In this section, we consider generic torus orbit closures in Richardson varieties. The topology and geometry of generic torus orbit closures in Richardson varieties are related to the combinatorics of Bruhat interval polytopes. Studying the combinatorial properties of Bruhat interval polytopes, we show that every smooth toric Richardson variety is a Bott manifold.
We also give a sufficient condition for a pair
(v,w)\in S_{n}\times S_{n}
to give rise to a
smooth
toric Richardson variety
X^{v}_{w}
. Motivated by this sufficient condition, we define a toric variety of Catalan type and then show that the number of isomorphism classes of smooth toric Richardson varieties of Catalan type is the Wedderburn–Etherington number.

5.4.

and

Now we find a sufficient condition for a Bruhat interval polytope

to be a cube.

It is shown in

that

(3) motivate us to study the following case:

are distinct.

(
[
66
, Proposition 7.1]
)
.

Suppose that

or

are distinct if and only if

is toric.

and

for

For

A.2.
Fano Bott manifolds

One of the interesting families of smooth toric varieties is the family of Bott manifolds. In this subsection, we first recall the definition of a Bott manifold, and then we characterize Fano Bott manifolds and classify them.

Each Bott manifold is a smooth projective toric variety associated with a smooth lattice polytope combinatorially equivalent to a cube, and the converse also holds.

Proposition A.10

(
[
72
, Corollary 3.5]
)
.

If a smooth lattice polytope
\mathsf{P}
is combinatorially equivalent to a cube, then the toric variety
X_{\mathsf{P}}
is a Bott manifold. Indeed, the family of Bott manifolds is

\{X_{\mathsf{P}}\mid\mathsf{P}\text{ is a smooth lattice polytope that is combinatorially equivalent to a cube}\}.

Let
B_{n}
be a Bott manifold. Then there is a smooth lattice polytope
\mathsf{P}
combinatorially equivalent to a cube of dimension
n
such that
B_{n}=X_{\mathsf{P}}
. The polytope
\mathsf{P}
has
2n
facets and there are
n
pairs of facets not intersecting with each other. Let
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n},\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n}\}
be the ray generators of the normal fan
\Sigma_{P}
of
\mathsf{P}
such that
\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}_{i}
do not form a cone in
\Sigma_{P}
for each
i\in[n]
. That is, each primitive collection of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{P}}
corresponds to the set
\{\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{i}\}
. By Proposition
A.7
, we obtain

(A.2)

B_{n}
is Fano if and only if
\mathbf{v}_{i}+\mathbf{w}_{i}
is either
\mathbf{v}_{j}
or
\mathbf{w}_{j}
for some
j
unless
\mathbf{v}_{i}+\mathbf{w}_{i}=\bm{0}
.

Using (
A.2
), to a Fano Bott manifold

we associate a signed rooted forest with the vertex set
[n]

•

•

i

We can also construct a Fano Bott manifold from a signed rooted forest up to isomorphism.
