Worry No More, The Hubble Tension is Relieved: A Truly Direct Measurement of the Hubble Constant from Mooniversal Expansion
Gagandeep S. Anand, Zachary R. Claytor, Ryan Dungee

Abstract
Using sedimentary and eclipse-based measurements of the lunar recession velocity, we derive a new local-Universe measurement of the Hubble constant ( H 0 H_{0} ) from the recession rate of Earth’s Moon. Taking into account the effects of tides, we find a value of H 0 = H_{0}= 63.01 ± \pm 1.79 km s -1 Mpc -1 , which is in approximate agreement with the Planck space mission’s measurement using the cosmic microwave background (CMB) and base Λ \Lambda CDM. Our new measurement represents the first ever model-independent, single-step measurement of the Universe’s current expansion rate. This is also the first major local Universe measurement of H 0 H_{0} which is below the measurement from Planck . Importantly, it is robust to the systematic errors that may be present in other H 0 H_{0} measurements using other cosmological probes such as type Ia supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations, or lensed quasars. Our work provides key evidence towards the notion that the existing Hubble tension may indeed be a result of systematic uncertainties in the local distance ladder.



I
Motivation

Cosmology today is at an important juncture. There is a large amount of discussion on the value of the Hubble Constant (
H_{0}
), which denotes the present expansion rate of the Universe. Measurements of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) from the Planck Satellite provide an extremely precise value of 67.4
\pm
0.5 km/s/Mpc
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. On the other hand, the latest results from the most prominent “local” measure of the Hubble constant is from the SH0ES team, who use observations of Cepheids in 42 nearby galaxies to derive of value of
H_{0}
= 73.0
\pm
1.0 km/s/Mpc
(
Riess et al. 2021
)
. These two measurements are in 5
\sigma
disagreement with each other, a fact which has sparked an incredible amount of interest within the community
(
Schöneberg et al. 2021
)
to come up with a possible solution to this Hubble “Tension”.

Other local Universe measurements also support values of
H_{0}
which are higher than that obtained from Planck, with many different techniques being explored to provide a cross-check on the Cepheid results (although some of these measurements are somewhat consistent with Planck, such as those from the Tip of the Red Giant Branch). These techniques include Mira variables
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
, Surface Brightness Fluctuations
(
Blakeslee et al. 2021
)
, the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB,
Freedman et al. 2019
;
Anand et al. 2021
), and the Tully-Fisher relation
(
Kourkchi et al. 2020
)
, among many others (see
Di Valentino et al. 2021
for a more complete list).

It has been previously pointed out that the Moon’s recessional velocity is on the same order as the implied Hubble flow expansion in the Earth-Moon system
(
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2021
)
, although the origin of this coincidence is unclear. In this work, we combine this suggestion with improved measurements on the Moon’s recessional velocity and make the assertion that the origin of the Moon’s recessional velocity (after discounting the known effects of tides) is from the Hubble expansion. In doing so, we provide a precise local measurement of the Hubble constant from the first
truly
local measurement of
H_{0}
.

II
Method and Data

We begin with the Hubble law and solve for the Hubble constant:

H_{0}=\frac{v}{d},

where
v
is the recession velocity, and
d
is the distance from Earth. Scaling by some typical values, we obtain the scaling relation

H_{0}=70~\mathrm{km/s/Mpc}\left(\frac{v}{2.755~\mathrm{cm/yr}}\right)\left(\frac{d}{385,000~\mathrm{km}}\right)^{-1}.

The question, then, is which are the appropriate values of
v
and
d
for an estimate of the Hubble constant?

II.1
Lunar Distance

Because of the Moon’s elliptical orbit, the Earth-Moon distance varies from 363,300 km at perigee to 405,500 km at apogee
(
NASA 2022
)
. Table
1
lists important distance values from the Moon’s orbit. Calculating a true cosmic expansion rate from the Moon’s recession would require a series of precise, instantaneous measurements of both the Earth-Moon distance and the lunar recession velocity, spanning many orbits. In the absence of that, we will use our best guess. Since we are looking at the Moon’s recession over time, we adopt the time-averaged distance of 385,000 km
\pm
1 mm as the nominal Earth-Moon distance.

II.2
Lunar Recession Velocity

The value of the lunar recession velocity is the subject of some debate
(
Poliakow 2005
;
Riofrio 2012
;
Maeder & Gueorguiev 2021
)
. Laser measurements suggest an instantaneous, present-day recession velocity of 3.8 cm/yr, but this has been regarded by some as anomalously high
(
Riofrio 2012
)
. Sediment data and eclipse data suggest slower recession speeds of
2.9\pm 0.6
and
2.82\pm 0.08
cm/yr, respectively, and these are supported by detailed numerical simulations
(
Poliakow 2005
)
. We favor the sediment and eclipse values due to their robustness and agreement with simulations, and we adopt the weighted average of the two values,
v=2.82\pm 0.08
cm/yr.

II.3
Correction for Tides

Tides have long been known to influence the Earth-Moon system
(
Darwin 1878
)
, and any measure of the lunar recession must account for this effect. To do this we assume the two effects are entirely independent and each contribute to the total recession linearly. Thus, the cosmological lunar recession
v_{\mathrm{cosmo}}
is given simply by:
v_{\mathrm{total}}=v_{\mathrm{tides}}+v_{\mathrm{cosmo}}
.
v_{\mathrm{tides}}
varies over time due to a variety of effects and
Poliakow 2005
provide a table containing historical values dating back 600 Myr. Taking the
v_{\mathrm{tides}}
for the approximate age of the sedimentary data gives
v_{\mathrm{tides}}=0.34
cm/yr. This then yields a
v_{\mathrm{cosmo}}
value of
2.48
cm/yr.

III
Results and Discussion

Evaluating the Hubble law at the distance and recession velocity of the Moon (after taking into account the effect of tides), we obtain
H_{0}=
63.01
\pm
1.79 km s
-1
Mpc
-1
, a 2.8% determination of the Hubble constant. Figure
1
shows our measurement along with several others from the recent literature. We see that previous results which are from the “late” Universe (e.g. Cepheids) are consistently higher than those from the “early” Universe (e.g. Planck). Our measurement is important not only because it is the only measurement of the Hubble Constant which is both model-independent and performed in a single step, but because it is the first local measurement of
H_{0}
whose value is *below* the value measured by
Planck
.

IV
Summary and Future Outlook

In this letter, we examine the dynamics of the Earth-Moon system to provide the first truly local measurement of the Hubble constant of
H_{0}
= 63.01
\pm
1.79 km s
-1
Mpc
-1
. This is somewhat
lower
than the value measured by the
Planck
satellite, though it is roughly consistent within the errors (see Figure
1
). We emphasize that our measurement of
H_{0}
is the first which is both model-independent and is done within a single step (i.e. does not rely on a multi-step distance ladder). Our work is an important result, as it suggests that other local Universe results whose measurements are higher than those of
Planck
may be biased by systematic errors.

In the near future, we hope to improve the precision on our lunar-based measurement of
H_{0}
by both 1) collecting more geological samples (to improve the sedimentary data), and 2) observing in detail more eclipses (to improve the eclipse-based measurements). Both of these avenues will allow us to decrease the uncertainty on our value of
H_{0}
and solve once and for all this cosmological crisis.

The idea for this paper came in 2019 out of late night desperation of three grad students trying to procrastinate real work. Nearly 3 PhDs and some unknown fraction of a pandemic later, we are enthralled to report this very significant result, which is definitely not a joke.
