Concentration in an advection-diffusion model with diffusion coefficient depending on the past trajectory
Cosmin Burtea, Nicolas Meunier, Clément Mouhot

Abstract
. We consider a drift-diffusion model, with an unknown function depending on the spatial variable and an additional structural variable, the amount of ingested lipid. The diffusion coefficient depends on this additional variable. The drift acts on this additional variable, with a power-law coefficient of the additional variable and a localization function in space. It models the dynamics of a population of macrophage cells. Lipids are located in a given region of space; when cells pass through this region, they internalize some lipids. This leads to a problem whose mathematical novelty is the dependence of the diffusion coefficient on the past trajectory. We discuss global existence and blow-up of the solution.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The problem at hand

Atherosclerosis is a major cause of death in industrialized
societies since it is the primary cause of heart attack (acute
myocardial infarction) and stroke (cerebrovascular accident). It
is now accepted that atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory
disease which starts within the intima, the innermost layer of an
artery. It is driven by the accumulation of macrophage cells
within the intima and promoted by modified low density
lipoprotein (LDL) particles
[
6
,
7
]
. Inflammation
occurs at sites within the arterial wall where modified
low-density lipoproteins (LDL) accumulate after penetrating the
wall from the bloodstream. The immune response attracts
circulating monocytes to these sites.

Mathematical modeling of atherosclerosis has recently gained
interest because of the variety of behavior of macrophages
depending on the amount of lipid ingested
[
8
]
. Many
mathematical models divide the macrophage population into
“macrophages” and “foam cells”. This is true in models based
on ordinary differential equations
[
12
]
, spatially
resolved partial differential equations
models
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
10
,
11
]
and other
computational models
[
17
]
. However, it is now clear that
there is a continuous distribution of lipid loads in macrophage
populations, ranging from monocytes with low lipid loads to
macrophages that can be labeled as foam cells in
atherosclerosis. The variation in lipid load within a macrophage
population suggests that it may be instructive to develop
structured models in which macrophages are characterized by their
intracellular lipid load, similar to adipocytes
[
18
]
, in
the spirit of
[
9
,
19
]
.

We consider here the case where the arterial wall is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

d\geq 1
and lipids are localized in a prescribed region defined
by
\operatorname*{Supp}\psi
where
\psi
is a given
non-negative function of space. We describe the dynamics of
macrophages in the arterial wall by a statistical (kinetic)
description of the evolution of their density with respect to
the spatial and lipid variables:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\Delta_{x}u+\partial_{a}\left\{\left[\psi(x)f(a)-\lambda a(A^{*}-a)\right]u\right\},&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},a\geq 0,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},a\geq 0,\end{cases}

where
A^{*}>0
,
\lambda\geq 0
and
f
is a given function.

In (
1.1
),
u(t,x,a)
is the density of cells
(macrophages) located at time
t
in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and having with
an amount
a
of ingested lipids. The amount of lipids in the
cell affects its spatial dynamics by changing its size for
example. This effect is modeled by a diffusion coefficient
depending on the variable
a
. The term
\lambda a(A^{*}-a)
is a
logistic (dumping) term that describes the recovery effect,
i.e. the tendency of a cell to return to its normal state as it
moves away from the hot spot.

We address the question of whether or not this
advection-diffusion equation can lead to concentration and
eventually blow-up in finite time, if diffusion is not strong
enough to prevent cells from being trapped in lipid dense
regions. More precisely, our purposes here are to investigate the
influence of the nonlinearity
f
, the parameter
\lambda
and
the size of the support of
\psi
on the local or global
well-posedness of problem (
1.1
). To this end we
will consider functions
f
which typically grow like a power,
namely

f(a)=\pm a^{\gamma}\textrm{ with }\gamma>0,

and three different cases for the function
\psi
:

(1)

\psi=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}}
,

(2)

\operatorname*{Supp}\psi\left(x\right)\subset B(0,1)

with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi\left(x\right)\diff\!x=1
,

(3)

\psi=\delta_{x=0}
is the Dirac mass in dimension
d=1
,
which was treated in
[
5
]
when
\lambda=0
.

1.2.
The origin of the model

Let us briefly detail the origin of equation
(
1.1
) in the case where
d=1
, see
[
2
,
5
,
4
]

for more details. Consider a cell, described as a Brownian
particle, with position
X_{t}
, whose diffusion coefficient,

A_{t}
, is modified at each passage in the lipid-rich zone and
which tends to recover a normal diffusion coefficient, see
Figure
1
. The couple
(X_{t},A_{t})
satisfies the
system of stochastic differential equations

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\diff\!X_{t}&=\sqrt{2A_{t}}\diff\!W_{t},\\[5.69054pt]
\diff\!A_{t}&=-\psi(X_{t})f(A_{t})\diff\!t+\ \lambda A_{t}\left(A^{*}-A_{t}\right)\diff\!t,\end{cases}

with initial condition
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
A_{0}>0
, where

(W_{t},t\geq 0)
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion.

Figure 1.
a) Sketch of the different stages of atherosclerosis
plaque formation: (1) rapid diffusion of a “free” macrophage
cell; (2) upon entering a localized lipid-enriched region,
the macrophage accumulates lipids, and thereby grows and
becomes less mobile; and (3) after many crossings of the
lipid-enriched region, the macrophage eventually gets
trapped, resulting in the formation of an atherosclerotic
plaque. (b) Sketch of a one-dimensional particle trajectory
of the model of locally decelerated random walk.

Intuitively,
\int_{0}^{t}\psi(X_{s})\diff\!s
represents the time spent
by
X
in the lipid-rich region during
[0,t]
. Hence, when

f\geq 0
, the time spent in this region by the macrophage cell
tends to decrease its diffusion coefficient
(A_{t},t\geq 0)
, or
equivalently to decelerate the cell. Such a decrease in local
mobility is due to the internalization and accumulation of lipid
particles by macrophages. It can be related to several phenomena:
increase in volume or change in cell fate depending on the amount
of low-density lipoproteins (LDL) ingested.

The parameter
\gamma
describes the intensity of the
internalization: the higher
\gamma
is, the lower the
internalization is. This parameter is thus related to both the
amount of lipids located in the arterial wall hotspot and the
capacity of macrophages to internalize lipids. The case

\gamma=0
corresponds to a very large amount of lipids and a
very large capacity of macrophages to internalize lipids. In
summary, the parameter
\gamma
is related to the inflammatory
state. In contrast, the term

\lambda A_{t}\left(A^{*}-A_{t}\right)\diff\!t
describes the loss of
lipids by the macrophage and its tendency to recover its natural
state. The parameters
\lambda
and
A^{*}
model the natural
immune defenses or medical treatment.

System (
1.2
) models the effects of lipid accumulation on
macrophage dynamics. The novelty of (
1.2
), compared with
the related model in
[
2
,
5
]
, is to include different
geometries for lipid source locations (from the Dirac case to the
whole real line) and to include the lipid unloading through the
logistic dumping term. Non-trivial behaviors of the solution of
(
1.2
) are expected: a dynamic transition to an absorbing
state can occur for a sufficiently strong deceleration, and the
particle can be trapped in finite time; whereas if the
deceleration process is sufficiently weak, the particle is never
trapped.

1.3.
The main results

Since the couple
(X_{t},A_{t})
is Markovian, in order to make the
previous intuition rigorous, we study the partial differential
equation (
1.1
) and the general questions we are
concerned with are the following. By studying the regularity of
the solution to (
1.1
), do we recover the results
expected by the probabilistic description, in particular by the
simplified differential equations
A^{\prime}(t)=-f(A(t))
? In
particular, if
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
, can we
prove global existence as expected from the previous differential
equation? In the case
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
can we prove that the solution to (
1.1
) becomes
unbounded in finite time in any
L^{p}
space? In order to ease the
presentation, we will discuss the particular case of space
dimension 1. However, our results remain true in dimension 2. In
Section
4.3
we explain the changes in order to recover
the blow-up for
\gamma\in[0,1)
in the the two-dimensional
case.

Let us build some intuition on (
1.1
) in the case
where
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
,
\psi=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}}
,
\lambda=0
and

d=1
. Multiplying (
1.1
) with
a^{\gamma}
and
denoting
m(t,x,a)=a^{\gamma}u(t,x,a)
, we obtain:

(1.3)

\partial_{t}m(t,x,a)=a\partial_{xx}m(t,x,a)+a^{\gamma}\partial_{a}m(t,x,a)\,.

Let us assume that
\partial_{x}m(t,x,a)
vanishes at

x\sim\pm\infty
and let us integrate
equation (
1.3
) on
\mathbb{R}
:

(1.4)

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(t,x,a)\diff\!x-a^{\gamma}\partial_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(t,x,a)\diff\!x=0\,.

We then write the characteristics
a(t)
of the previous system,
solution to the equation:

(1.5)

\begin{cases}a^{\prime}(t)&=-a^{\gamma}(t)\\
a(0)&=a_{0}.\end{cases}

The equation (
1.5
) shows how the diffusion
coefficient evolves (note that it changes in the same way for all
individuals) namely we identify two different regimes:

•

if
\gamma\in[0,1)
the diffusion coefficient vanishes in
finite time,

•

if
\gamma\geq 1
the diffusion coefficient is positive for
all time provided the initial datum
a_{0}>0
, and vanishes
asymptotically in time as
t\to+\infty
.

We are now in the position of stating our main results. We begin
with the case where the acceleration-deceleration zone is the
whole real axis:

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}u+\partial_{a}\left(f(a)u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0.\end{cases}

We consider the three following cases:

(a)

acceleration:
f(a)=-a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 0
,

(b)

subcritical deceleration:
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with

\gamma\geq 1
,

(c)

supercritical deceleration:
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with

\gamma\in[0,1)
.

In particular, if
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
, by studying the regularity of
the solution in a
L^{p}
framework, we obtain the following
dichotomy: global existence if
\gamma\geq 1
, while, in the case

\gamma<1
, the solution becomes unbounded in finite time
(so-called blow-up).

This is a linear equation on
u=u(t,x,a)
defined on
t\geq 0
,

x\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\geq 0
.

The first main result reads :

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the initial datum
u_{0}
belongs to
L^{p}\cap L^{1}
,

p\geq 1
. Assume in addition that

\mbox{supp}(u_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]
for some
A_{0}>0
,
then

(a)

in the acceleration case
f(a)=-a^{\gamma}
with

\gamma\geq 0
, there exists a unique global weak solution
to (
1.6
) that satisfies

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(t,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(0,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a,

(b)

in the subcritical deceleration case

f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
, there exists a unique
global weak solution to (
1.6
) that satisfies

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(t,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq e^{\gamma(1-p)A_{0}^{\gamma-1}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(0,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a,

(c)

in the supercritical deceleration case

f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in[0,1)
, any non-zero weak
solution to (
1.6
) blows-up in finite time
with the time of blow-up

T_{b}:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-\gamma}u(0,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a}{(1-\gamma)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u(0,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a\right)}\in(0,+\infty)

for which all the mass is concentrated at
a=0
.

Our second result concerns the case where the
acceleration-deceleration zone is localized:

(1.7)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}u+\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(f(a)u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\end{cases}

with a localization function that satisfies

\operatorname*{Supp}\psi\left(x\right)\subset\left[-1,1\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi\left(x\right)\diff\!x=1.

We will consider the three regimes described above.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given
u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}
,
p\geq 1
, with
u_{0}\geq 0
and

\supp u_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]
,
A_{0}>0
, we have:

(a)

In the acceleration case,
f(a)=-a^{\gamma}
with

\gamma>0
, there exists a unique weak solution
to (
3.5
) that satisfies

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(t,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(0,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a,.

(b)

In the subcritical deceleration case

f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
, there exists a unique
weak solution to (
3.5
) that satisfies

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(t,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq e^{\frac{\gamma(p-1)A_{0}^{\gamma-1}}{2\delta}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(0,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Moreover, when
\gamma>3/2
, the following bound (uniform in

\delta
) holds

\forall\,t\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(t,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq e^{\gamma^{2}(p-1)t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u(0,x,a)|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a.

(c)

in the supercritical deceleration case
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in[0,1)
, provided that
a_{0}
is small, there exists arbitrarily small non-negative initial data

0\leq u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}),\quad\operatorname*{Supp}u_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}\times[a_{0},+\infty)

so that the corresponding unique weak solution

0\leq u\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}
to (
1.7
) blows-up
in finite time
T^{\ast}\in(0,+\infty)
, in the sense

(1.8)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}}\left\|u\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}=+\infty.

Moreover this time of explosion can be as small as wanted provided that
a_{0}
is small enough.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In
Section
2
we study the case where the diffusion
coefficient changes on the whole real line and
\lambda=0
. If

f(a)=a^{\gamma}
, we show that
\gamma=1
plays a critical role
in the problem. In Section

we consider the

) in three directions: (1) with a

.

2.
Acceleration-deceleration on
\mathbb{R}

In this section we prove Theorem
1.6
regarding
the case where the acceleration-deceleration zone is the whole
real axis:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}u+\partial_{a}\left(f(a)u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0.\end{cases}

We start by defining weak solutions to (
1.6
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A_{0},T>0
and

u_{0}\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\cap L^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)
such that

\operatorname*{Supp}u_{0}\left(x,a\right)\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]\text{ and
}u_{0}\geq 0.

A function

u\in C(\mathbb{[}0,T\mathbb{)};L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C(\mathbb{[}0,T\mathbb{)};L_{\mathrm{weak}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
such
that

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{(}0,T\mathbb{)};L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),a\partial_{x}u^{\frac{p}{2}}\in L^{2}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Supp}u\left(t,x,a\right)\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]\text{ and }u\left(t,x,a\right)\geq 0\text{,}

verifying (
1.6
) in the sense of distributions
is called a weak solution to (
1.6
) on

\left(0,T\right)
. A function

u\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C(\mathbb{R}_{+};L_{\mathrm{weak}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
is called a global weak
solution to (
1.6
) if for any
T>0
its
restriction to
\left(0,T\right)
is a weak solution
to (
1.6
) on
\left(0,T\right)
.

Weak solutions in the sense of Definition
2.1
are
mass-preserving:

M=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u_{0}(x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Let us first prove that non-negativity is preserved.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution to (
1.6
)
with initial datum
u_{0}
non-negative almost everywhere. Then

|u(t,\cdot)|=u(t,\cdot)
almost everywhere for later times.

Proof.

If
u
is solution in
L^{1}
then
|u|
is subsolution in
L^{1}

since
\mbox{sgn}(u)\partial_{xx}^{2}u\leq\partial_{xx}^{2}|u|

and

\mbox{sgn}(u)\partial_{a}(f(a)\,u)=\partial_{a}(f(a)\,|u|)
. Hence
|u|-u
is a subsolution, and we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|u|-u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 0,

which proves the claimed conclusion.
∎

We then use the orientation of the drift to obtain estimates on
the
(x,a)
-support of the solution.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume
u
is a weak solution to (
1.6
) with

u_{0}\geq 0
. Assume in addition that
f(a)\geq 0

(deceleration) and
\mbox{supp}(u_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]

for some
A_{0}>0
, then

\mbox{supp}(u_{t})\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,A_{0}]
up to the existence
time. Similarly, in the case
f(a)\leq 0
(acceleration) and

\mbox{supp}(u_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}\times[A_{0},+\infty)
for some

A_{0}>0
, then
\mbox{supp}(u_{t})\subset\mathbb{R}\times[A_{0},+\infty)

for some
A_{0}>0
up to the existence time.

Proof.

Note first that
u_{0}\geq 0
implies
u\geq 0
by standard
arguments. Consider then any non-negative non-decreasing function

\varphi=\varphi(a)
smooth on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with support included in

(A_{0},+\infty)
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u(t,x,a)\varphi(a)\diff\!x\diff\!a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f(a)u(t,x,a)\varphi^{\prime}(a)\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 0,

which proves that
u_{t}\varphi=0
for later times. This proves
that
u_{t}=0
on
\mathbb{R}\times[A_{0},+\infty)
for all times.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

This is a standard drift-diffusion equation and we omit the
details regarding the existence of solutions, see for
instance
[
16
,
1
]
.

In the cases (a) and (b), we prove the propagation of
L^{p}

bounds for all times, which is the crucial a priori
estimate. To prove that solutions blow-up in finite time in the
supercritical case (c), we show that for an appropriate value
of
m>0
, the moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{m}u(t,x,a)\diff\!a
becomes
infinite in finite time.

Case (a).
Given
p\in[1,+\infty)
, we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq

\displaystyle-p(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}u\right|^{2}|u|^{p-2}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle-\gamma(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 0,

which proves that
L^{p}
norms remains finite for all times
provided they are finite initially, and there is no blow-up in
finite time. By applying the same argument to the modulus of
the difference of two solutions one proves similarly uniqueness
in
L^{p}
.

Case (b).
Given
p\in[1,+\infty)
, we
calculate

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq-p(p-1)\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}u\right|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a+\gamma(p-1)\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Since
\gamma\geq 1
, the weight
a^{\gamma-1}
is bounded on

[0,A_{0}]
, using Lemma
2.3
, and we deduce

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\gamma(p-1)A_{0}^{\gamma-1}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a,

which implies the propagation of
L^{p}
norms by Gronwall lemma
and the bound in the statement.

Case (c).
The conclusion follows from

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-\gamma}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-\gamma}\partial_{a}\left(a^{\gamma}u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=-(1-\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=-(1-\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u(0,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a,

where we have used the conservation of mass

\iint u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a=\iint u_{0}(x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a
.
∎

3.
Acceleration-deceleration on an interval

In this section we study, for
f
locally Lipschitz on

(0,\infty)
,

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}u+\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(f(a)u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\end{cases}

with a localization function that satisfies

\operatorname*{Supp}\psi\left(x\right)\subset\left[-1,1\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi\left(x\right)\diff\!x=1.

3.1.
Comparison with the acceleration-deceleration at a
point

Formally, solutions to (
1.7
), for a family of
functions
\psi_{n}
that approximates the Dirac mass at zero,
converge to the model introduced in
[
5
]
which reads

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}u+\delta_{x=0}\partial_{a}\left(f(a)u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\end{cases}

However, the method of
[
5
]
does not apply. Consider for
instance the case
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In

[
5
]
, it was shown that any solution
u
to (
3.2
)
satisfies

(3.3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\frac{1}{2}}u\left(t,0,a\right)\diff\!a<+\infty

for all times, and that for any
m\in(0,1/2)
there is

T^{\ast}_{m}\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}_{m}}\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{m}u\left(t,0,a\right)\diff\!a=+\infty.

Looking now at the equation (
1.7
), the
equivalent of the quantity (
3.3
) is

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a,

and, when
m=1/2
, we are able to find a bound similar
to (
3.3
):

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution to (
1.7
)
with
f(a)=a^{\gamma}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
(in the sense of
Definition
2.1
), then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{\frac{1}{2}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a<\infty.

Proof.

Using a Duhamel formulation along the heat flow, we
have

(3.4)

u\left(t,\cdot,a\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{ta}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4ta}}\ast_{x}u_{0}\left(\cdot,a\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\tau a}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}\ast_{x}\partial_{a}\left(\psi a^{\gamma}u)\right),

which implies, after integration against
a^{m}\psi
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{ta}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4ta}}\ast_{x}u_{0}\left(\cdot,a\right)\right)\left(x\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\tau a}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}\ast_{x}\partial_{a}\left(\psi a^{\gamma}u)\right)\right)(x)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Observe that the first term in the previous right hand side is
bounded for
u_{0}\in L_{x}^{\infty}L_{a}^{1}(a^{m})
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{ta}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4ta}}\ast_{x}u_{0}\left(\cdot,a\right)\right)\left(x\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}a^{m}\left\|u_{0}\left(\cdot,a\right)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\diff\!a<\infty.

Next, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\tau a}}e^{\frac{-\left|\cdot\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}\ast_{x}\partial_{a}\left(\psi a^{\gamma}u\right)\right)(x)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{-\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\partial_{a}\left[\psi\left(y\right)a^{\gamma}u\left(\tau,y,a\right)\right]\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a\diff\!y

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-m\right)\sqrt{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}a^{\gamma+m-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{-\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\psi\left(x\right)\psi\left(y\right)u\left(\tau,y,a\right)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a\diff\!y

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}a^{\gamma+m-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left(\frac{e^{\frac{-\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\right)\psi\left(x\right)\psi\left(y\right)u\left(\tau,y,a\right)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a\diff\!y.

The last term in the previous equality can be written

-\sqrt{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{\gamma+m-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{-\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4\tau a}}}{\sqrt{4\tau}}\frac{\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4\tau a^{2}}\psi\left(y\right)u\left(\tau,y,a\right)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a\diff\!y\leq 0.

Thus, for
m=\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{\frac{1}{2}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}a^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\left(\cdot,a\right)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\diff\!a<\infty

which concludes the proof.
∎

However, unlike for equation (
3.2
), one cannot hope
that the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

becomes infinite for
m\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
since, if

u
is compactly supported in
a
initially, say
u_{0}(x,a)=0
for

a>A_{0}
with
A_{0}>0
, it is controlled by the mass:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi\left(x\right)a^{m}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}A_{0}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Of course, this estimate degenerates for
\psi_{n}
converging to a
Dirac mass at
x=0
, since
\|\psi_{n}\|_{\infty}\to\infty
.

3.2.
The cases without finite time singularity

Denote

\psi_{\delta}(x):=(2\delta)^{-1}{\mathds{1}}_{x\in[-\delta,\delta]}
and consider

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial^{2}_{xx}u+\psi_{\delta}\partial_{a}\left(f(a)\,u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geq 0,\ t>0,\\[5.69054pt]

Proof of Theorem
1.2
for the cases
(a) and (b).

We prove the a priori estimate, then briefly explain how to
construct solutions.

Case (a).
The estimate follows from

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq

\displaystyle-p(p-1)\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}u\right|^{2}|u|^{p-2}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(p-1)}{2\delta}\int_{[-\delta,\delta]}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 0.

Case (b).
We calculate

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq

\displaystyle-p(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}u\right|^{2}|u|^{p-2}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma(p-1)}{2\delta}\int_{x\in[-\delta,\delta]}\int_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Since
\gamma\geq 1
, the weight
a^{\gamma-1}
is bounded on

[0,A_{0}]
by using Lemma
2.3
, and we
deduce

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\frac{\gamma(p-1)A_{0}^{\gamma-1}}{2\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a,

which proves the estimate by the Gronwall lemma. When
p>1
, we
further calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\diff\!}{\diff\!t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a=

\displaystyle-\frac{4(p-1)}{p}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}\left(u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right|^{2}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma(p-1)-m}{2\delta}\int_{x\in[\delta,\delta]}\int_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a

and we control, with
I_{\varepsilon}:=[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]
and

,

\displaystyle|v(t,x,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|v(t,x,a)-\frac{1}{|I_{\varepsilon}|}\int_{y\in I_{\varepsilon}}v(t,y,a)\right|+\left|\frac{1}{|I_{\varepsilon}|}\int_{y\in I_{\varepsilon}}v(t,y,a)\diff\!y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{|I_{\varepsilon}|}\int_{y\in I_{\varepsilon}}\Big(v(t,x,a)-v(t,y,a)\Big)\diff\!y\right|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}}\|v(t,\cdot,a)\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\int_{x}^{y}\partial_{x}v(z,a)\diff\!y\diff\!z\right|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}}\|v(t,\cdot,a)\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\left\|\partial_{x}v(\cdot,a)\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R})}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}}\|v(\cdot,a)\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R})}

and we deduce (note that the previous bound is pointwise in
a
)

\displaystyle\int_{x\in[\delta,\delta]}\int_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}v^{2}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 2\left\|\varepsilon^{1/2}a^{(\gamma-1)/2}\partial_{x}v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,a}}+\left\|\varepsilon^{-1/2}a^{(\gamma-1)/2}v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,a}}.

We set
\varepsilon=\eta a^{(\gamma-1)}
and, for
\gamma\geq 3/2

we have
2(\gamma-1)\geq m+1
so

\displaystyle\int_{x\in[\delta,\delta]}\int_{a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{\gamma-1}v^{2}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq 2\delta\eta\left\|a^{1/2}\partial_{x}v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,a}}+\frac{2\delta}{\eta}\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,a}}

and finally, with
\eta:=\gamma^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\diff\!}{\diff\!t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)(\eta\gamma-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a\left|\partial_{x}\left(u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\right|^{2}\diff\!x\diff\!a+\frac{\gamma(p-1)}{\eta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|u|^{p}\diff\!x\diff\!a

which proves the estimate by the Gronwall lemma.

To construct solutions, one can for instance use the following
iterative scheme, for some
\epsilon>0
:

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}-(\epsilon+\varphi\left(a\right))\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\partial_{a}\left(\chi\left(a\right)\psi\left(x\right)\tilde{u}\right)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
\tilde{u}_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,\end{cases}

where
\varphi
,
\chi
are defined by

(3.7)

\varphi\left(a\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }a\leq 0,\\
a&\text{ if }a\in[0,A_{0}],\\
0&\text{ if }a\geq 2A_{0},\end{cases}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\chi\left(a\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }a\leq 0,\\
f(a)&\text{ if }a\in[0,A_{0}],\\
f(A_{0})&\text{ if }a\geq A_{0}.\end{cases}

Note that

\chi^{\prime}\left(a\right)\in L^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\cap L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
with the weak
derivative

(3.8)

|\chi^{\prime}\left(a\right)|=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }a\leq 0,\\
\gamma a^{\gamma-1}&\text{ if }a\in(0,A_{0}),\\

We then further approximate by the standard Galerkin
method
[
3
]
:

(3.9)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}-\mathbb{P}_{n}\left\{(\varepsilon+\varphi\left(a\right))\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\partial_{a}\left(\chi\left(a\right)\psi\left(x\right)\tilde{u}\right)\right\}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
\tilde{u}_{|t=0}=\mathbb{P}_{n}u_{0},\end{cases}

with

\mathbb{P}_{n}\tilde{u}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathds{1}_{\left[-n,n\right]\times\left[-n,n\right]}\left(x,a\right)\mathcal{F}\tilde{u}\right)

and
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform in
(x,a)
. We
use, as usual in these methods, Bernstein’s lemma
see
[
3
, Lemma 2.1, p.69]
-
[
3
, p.191]
to
control derivative thanks to the Fourier restriction, and the
Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem theorem shows global
existence-uniqueness in

\mathbf{E}_{n}:=\left\{\tilde{u}\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right):\operatorname*{Supp}\mathcal{F}\tilde{u}\subset\left[-n,n\right]\times\left[-n,n\right]\right\}.

for each

. It is then straightforward to check that

, for this approximate problem, and therefore allow to

∎

3.3.
The case of deceleration with

\gamma\in\left(0,1\right)

We study the local well-posedness for strong solution with a
support condition, then for weak solutions with a support
condition, and finally we prove finite-time singularity and
ill-posedness for weak solutions in
L^{1}\cap L^{p}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(-1,1\right)
with
1
1

1

This
assumption is not crucial, but it simplifies some technical
aspects of the proof.

0\leq\psi\left(x\right)\leq 1
,
and
0\leq u_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}
with

\operatorname*{Supp}u_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}\times[a_{0},A_{0}],

for some
0<a_{0}<A_{0}
. Then there exists a time

and a unique non-negative

1.7

with

and

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

As long as the support stays away from
a=0
, the construction
of the smooth non-negative mass-preserving is done by standard
approximation proceedures as before. Let us give the a priori
estimate on the support. Let us compute

\displaystyle\frac{\diff\!}{\diff\!t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a(t)}^{+\infty}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a(t)}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\diff\!a+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x,a(t))a(t)^{\gamma}\diff\!x

since

∎

As an immediate consequence of
Proposition
3.2
we have the following

Proposition 3.3
.

Consider
u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

The proof follows by regularization of the initial data and the
coefficients. When the support is bounded away from

, the

Proof of Theorem
1.2

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u_{0}(x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}4\left(x^{2}-3\right)a^{1-m}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

(3.10)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{m}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m+1-\gamma}}\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Owing to the fact that

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m+1-\gamma}}\diff\!x\diff\!a\right)^{\frac{m}{m+1-\gamma}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\right)^{\frac{1-\gamma}{m+1-\gamma}}

y\left(t\right)\geq y\left(0\right)-12A_{0}^{1-m}t\mathcal{M}(u_{0})+\frac{m}{2}\mathcal{M}(u_{0})^{-\frac{1-\gamma}{m}}\int_{0}^{t}y\left(\tau\right)^{\frac{m+1-\gamma}{m}}\diff\!\tau.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},\theta>0
and

be a continuous function

T\leq T(\alpha):=\alpha^{-\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}}\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\theta}}

y(t)\geq y(0)\left(\frac{1}{1-t\frac{\alpha\theta}{y(0)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}.

The proof of this lemma is straightforward by observing that

is propagated in time, and thus
y(t)\geq y(0)
on the time of

y(t)^{-1-\theta}y^{\prime}(t)\geq\alpha y(0)^{-1-\theta}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u_{0}(x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a\geq\left(\frac{(12+\tilde{\alpha})A^{1-m}}{m}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+1-\gamma}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}\left(x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

This is clear since the integrand in the left hand side has a

in the variable

. Moreover this is an
homogeneous equation in

so the size of
u
does not
matter. By concentrating the initial data near zero at a distance

a_{0}
with
a_{0}
small, we can satisfy the inequality with

\tilde{\alpha}
as large as wanted, and therefore with a time of
explosion as small as wanted. This ends the proof of
Theorem
1.2
.
∎

3.4.
A remark on the blow-up times

Consider
u_{0}
smooth and a corresponding weak solution
to (
1.7
) that blows-up at a
T^{*}
. The
existence time of a classical solution to (
1.7
)
is then strictly shorter than
T^{*}
.
Indeed,if the solution remains regular on
(0,T^{*})
then

\forall\,t\in[0,T^{*}),\quad\lim_{a\rightarrow 0}a^{\gamma}u\left(t,x,a\right)=0.

But then, we can use the maximum principle on the equation
satisfied by
a^{\gamma}u
to get

(3.14)

\forall\,t\in[0,T^{*}),\quad\sup_{x,a}a^{\gamma}u\left(t,x,a\right)\leq\sup_{x,a}a^{\gamma}u_{0}\left(x,a\right).

The Theorem
1.2
(case (c)) for

m\in(0,1-\gamma)
is in contradiction
with (
3.14
) since then

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a\leq\left(\sup_{x,a}a^{\gamma}u_{0}(x,a)\right)\left(\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{A_{0}}\frac{da}{a^{m+\gamma}}\right)

which remains finite. Note moreover than since the time of
explosion can be as small as wanted, we easily deduced that the
equation is ill-posed both for regular and weak solutions, when
one removes the support condition.

4.
Variants and extensions

4.1.
Nonlinear diffusion

The argument we used above is robust enough to deal with
nonlinear diffusions. Consider, for
q\in[1,1+1/\gamma)
,
the equation

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}^{2}(u^{q})+\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}(a^{\gamma}u),&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,\end{cases}

One can extend Theorem
1.2
to the weak
L^{1}\cap L^{p}
solutions to (
4.1
).
The proof is similar. The only difference is in estimating the
first term in (
3.3
), which now becomes

(4.2)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}4\left(x^{2}-3\right)a^{1-m}u^{q}(t,x,a)\diff\!\tau\diff\!x\diff\!a.

To control it we use an additional a priori estimate:
integrate (
4.1
) against

p\left(a^{\gamma}u\right)^{p-1}
to bound

\sup_{t\geq 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}a^{(p-1)\gamma}u^{q}\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

This last quantity controls (
4.2
) if
m
is
chosen such that
m\in(0,1-\left(q-1\right)\gamma)
(the
interval is not empty by assumption). Then one constructs
u_{0}

such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a
blows-up in finite time as above.

4.2.
Logistic memory effect

Consider, for some
\lambda>0
and
A^{*}>0
and

\gamma\in(0,1)
, the equation

(4.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=a\partial_{xx}u-\lambda\partial_{a}\left(g\left(a\right)u\right)+\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(a^{\gamma}u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R},a\geq 0,\end{cases}

where
\psi
is as before, localized in
[-1,1]
and with mass
1
, and

(4.4)

g\left(a\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }a\leq 0,\\
a(A^{\ast}-a)&\text{ if }a\in[0,A^{\ast}],\\
0&\text{ if }a\geq A^{\ast}.\end{cases}

We consider
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and initial data whose support is
included in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,A^{\ast})
. It is easy to check that
this support condition is propagated by the equation. Define the
function

(4.5)

h:[0,A^{\ast}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad a\mapsto\lambda(A^{\ast}-a)a-a^{\gamma}.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
h
has a root
a^{*}
in
(0,A^{*})
, and if the initial data
satisfies

\operatorname*{Supp}u_{0}\subset[a^{*},A^{\ast}]\times\mathbb{R}

then this support condition is preserved and the unique weak

L^{1}\cap L^{p}
solution to (
4.3
) with (
4.4
)
(
p>1
) exists globally in time.

But in any case, for
a_{0}>0
small enough, there are arbitrarily small non-negative initial data

u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}),\quad\operatorname*{Supp}u_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}\times[a_{0},A_{0}],

so that the corresponding unique weak solution

0\leq u\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}
to (
4.3
)-(
4.4
)
blows-up in finite time
T^{\ast}\in(0,+\infty)
, in the
sense

(4.6)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}}\left\|u\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}=+\infty.

Moreover this time of explosion can be as small as wanted
provided that
a_{0}
is small enough.

Note that the existence of a positive root
a^{*}\in(0,A^{*})

always exist for
\lambda
large enough. So large memory erasing
effects can prevent the blow-up mechanism, as expected.

Proof.

The construction of weak solutions is done as before. To prove
blow-up, we argue as in Theorem
1.2
and
integrate the equation against

\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{[-1,1]}\left(x\right)a^{-m}
to get

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a-\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u_{0}(x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}4\left(x^{2}-3\right)a^{1-m}u\left(s,x,a\right)\diff\!s\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{m}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(s,x,a)}{a^{m+1-\gamma}}\diff\!s\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\qquad-m\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\lambda(A^{\ast}-a)\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(s,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!s\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Then the function

y(t):=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a
defined as before
satisfies

y\left(t\right)\geq y\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(C_{2}y^{1+\theta}\left(\tau\right)-C_{1}-C_{3}y\left(\tau\right)\right)\diff\!\tau,

with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
as in the case
\lambda=0
. Using
again Young’s inequality we deduce

y\left(t\right)\geq y\left(0\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\tilde{C}_{2}y^{1+\theta}\left(\tau\right)-2\tilde{C}_{1}\right)\diff\!\tau,

for
\tilde{C}_{1}\tilde{C}_{2}>0
depending on
C_{1},C_{2}

and
C_{3}
. The blow-up argument can then be applied as
before.

To prove that when a positive root
a^{*}\in(0,A^{*})
of
h

exists, one can propagate a support condition in
(a^{*},A^{*})
,
use the following a priori (for smooth solutions)

\displaystyle\frac{\diff\!}{\diff\!t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(h\left(a\right)u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}(1-\psi\left(x\right))\partial_{a}\left(\lambda a\left(A^{\ast}-a\right)u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}\psi\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(h\left(a\right)u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}(1-\psi\left(x\right))\partial_{a}\left(\lambda a\left(A^{\ast}-a\right)u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\psi\left(x\right))\lambda a^{*}(A^{\ast}-a^{*})u\left(t,x,a^{*}\right)\diff\!x\geq 0.

Thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{a^{*}}^{A^{\ast}}u_{0}\left(x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a,

which, given the non negativity of
u
implies that the support
of
u
is included in
\mathbb{R}\times\left[a^{*},A^{\ast}\right]
. It is then easy to deduce that the solution is global.
∎

4.3.
The two-dimensional case

We consider the equation

(4.7)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-a\Delta_{x}u=\mathds{1}_{B\left(0,1\right)}\left(x\right)\partial_{a}\left(a^{\gamma}u\right),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},a\geq 0,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
u_{|t=0}=u_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},a\geq 0.\end{cases}

We focus on the proof of the occurrence of a blow-up. Indeed, the
proof of the existence and uniqueness of the solution is similar
to the case of dimension
1
in Section
3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
\gamma\in\left(0,1\right)
. For any

m\in\left(0,\gamma\right)
there exists arbitrarily small
non-negative, compactly supported initial data

0\leq u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
so that the
corresponding unique weak
L^{1}\cap L^{p}
solution
to (
4.7
) blows-up in finite time
T^{*}>0
,
i.e.

(4.8)

\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{*}}\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}u(t,x,a)}{a^{m}}\diff\!x\diff\!a=\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\limsup_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}}\left\|u\left(t\right)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}=+\infty.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Consider
m\in(0,\gamma)
and calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\diff\!}{\diff\!t}\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a=

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}a\Delta_{x}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}\partial_{a}\left(a^{\gamma}u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Regarding the last term of the right hand side, by
nonnegativity of
u
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}\partial_{a}\left(a^{\gamma}u\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a=

\displaystyle\left[\int_{B(0,1)}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}a^{\gamma}u(t,x,a)\diff\!x\right]_{a=0}^{a=+\infty}

\displaystyle-\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(-m)\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m+1}}a^{\gamma}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle m\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m+1-\gamma}}u\left(t,x,a\right)\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Regarding the first term of the right hand side
of (
4.9
), we integrate by parts to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}a\Delta_{x}u\diff\!x\diff\!a=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}a\nabla_{x}u\frac{x}{\left|x\right|}\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle-\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}\nabla_{x}\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\nabla_{x}u\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Using that

\nabla_{x}\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}=2\left(\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right)\nabla_{x}\left|x\right|^{2}=4\left(\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right)x,

we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}\nabla\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\nabla_{x}u\diff\!x\diff\!a=

\displaystyle 4\int_{\partial B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}u\left(\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right)\left|x\right|

\displaystyle-\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}\Delta_{x}\left(\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\right)u\diff\!x\diff\!a.

Moreover, since

\Delta_{x}\left(\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\right)=4\nabla_{x}\cdot\left[\left(\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right)x\right]=8\left(2\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right),

we get

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}{a^{m}}a\Delta_{x}u\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}\Delta_{x}\left\{\left(1-\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\right\}u\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle=8\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}\left(2\left|x\right|^{2}-1\right)u\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\geq-8\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}a^{1-m}u\diff\!x\diff\!a

\displaystyle\geq-8A_{0}^{1-m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u_{0}\diff\!x\diff\!a

and the rest of the argument is similar to the case of
dimension
1
.
∎
