Automatic balancing parameter selection for Tikhonov-TV regularization
Ali Gholami, Silvia Gazzola

Abstract
This paper considers large-scale linear ill-posed inverse problems whose solutions can be represented as sums of smooth and piecewise constant components. To solve such problems we consider regularizers consisting of two terms that must be balanced. Namely, a Tikhonov term guarantees the smoothness of the smooth solution component, while a total-variation (TV) regularizer promotes blockiness of the non-smooth solution component. A scalar parameter allows to balance between these two terms and, hence, to appropriately separate and regularize the smooth and non-smooth components of the solution. This paper proposes an efficient algorithm to solve this regularization problem by the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM). Furthermore, a novel algorithm for automatic choice of the balancing parameter is introduced, using robust statistics. The proposed approach is supported by some theoretical analysis, and numerical experiments concerned with different inverse problems are presented to validate the choice of the balancing parameter.



1
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the solution of ill-conditioned linear systems of equations of the form

\mathbb{d=Gm+e},

(1)

arising from the discretization of continuous inverse problems in different fields of applied sciences and engineering; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
25
]
. Here
\mathbb{G}
is an
M\times N
real matrix representing a forward operator,
\mathbb{d}
and
\mathbb{e}
are real vectors of lengths
M
representing known measured data and unknown Gaussian white noise, respectively, and the unknown
\mathbb{m}
is a real vector of length
N
representing a quantity of interest.
The singular values of
\mathbb{G}
may concentrate close to the origin and decay to zero, making the system ill-conditioned.
Both one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) problems are considered in this paper; in the 2D case, the model parameters
\mathbb{m}
are obtained by stacking the columns of a rectangular
N_{z}\times N_{x}
array, so that
N=N_{z}\cdot N_{x}
.
Due to the presence of noise and the ill-conditioning of the system, a naive (e.g., least squares) solution of (
1
) possibly gives a meaningless estimate of the unknown
\mathbb{m}
. Thus we seek to determine an approximation of
\mathbb{m}
by regularizing problem (
1
), i.e., by incorporating some a priori information about
\mathbb{m}
into the problem formulation; see

[
14
]
.

In this paper, we assume that the solution to the problem (
1
) is piecewise smooth, e.g., it consists of a smooth background while containing regions with discontinuities. This allows us to decompose
\mathbb{m}
as the sum of two components
\mathbb{m}_{1}
and
\mathbb{m}_{2}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{m}=\mathbb{m}_{1}+\mathbb{m}_{2},

(2)

where
\mathbb{m}_{1}
is the piecewise constant component of
\mathbb{m}
and
\mathbb{m}_{2}
is the smooth component of
\mathbb{m}
. Following
[
12
]
, we incorporate regularizarization by considering a Tikhonov regularization term for the smooth component and a(n anisotropic) total variation (TV) term for the piecewise constant component.
A scalar balancing parameter
\beta>0
is included to control the amount of regularization associated to each term and, hence, to properly separate the two complementary components of the solution.
Assuming that the value
\varepsilon=\|\mathbb{e}\|_{2}^{2}
is available, the pair (
\mathbb{m}_{1},\mathbb{m}_{2}
) is obtained by solving the following Tikhonov-TV constrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\mathbb{m}_{1},\mathbb{m}_{2}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\mathbb{m}_{1},\mathbb{m}_{2}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\mathbb{D}_{2}\mathbb{m}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(3)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbb{m}_{1},\mathbb{m}_{2}}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{m}_{1}+\mathbb{m}_{2})-\mathbb{d}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon,

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
,
p=1,2
, denotes the vector
p
-norm, and

\mathbb{D}_{1}
and
\mathbb{D}_{2}
are scaled finite difference discretizations of the gradient and partial second derivative operators, respectively.

The Tikhonov-TV regularization method (
3
) has gained considerable attention in scientific applications, for example: image denoising, inpainting and deblurring
[
12
]
, signal decomposition
[
15
]
, optical flow computation
[
17
]
, signal filtering
[
22
]
, seismic travel time tomography
[
3
]
, and seismic full waveform inversion
[
2
]
.
This paper proposes an alternative algorithm to the one in
[
12
]
, featuring a fully automatic balancing parameter choice strategy, which can be reliably used to solve large scale problems arising in the applications mentioned above. Note that, in
[
12
]
,
\xi=\beta^{-1}
was used as balancing parameter; using
\beta
is more convenient for the approach presented in this paper.

In this paper we solve (
3
) using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
11
,
8
]
. This method has proved to be particularly advantageous for dealing with large scale problems such as those arising in geophysics and other scientific applications
[
6
]
.
Specifically, ADMM splits the original optimization problem into reduced subproblems that are solved in sequence and whose solution is efficient and simple to implement. Furthermore, ADMM naturally handles non-smooth terms (such as the
1
-norm term in (
3
)) by employing proximal methods. Very recent research has focussed on applying ADMM to nonlinear, nonconvex equality-constrained optimization problems, similar to problem formulation (
3
). For instance,
[
27
]
analyzes the convergence of a nonlinear objective function subject to a 2-norm squared equality constraint; because of our approach in handling each subproblem (see Section
2
), such analysis can be adapted to our problem formulation (
3
).

The value of the parameter
\beta
has a great impact on the
quality of the regularized solution for the problem at hand. The authors of
[
12
]

proposed to determine
\beta
via L-curve analysis, still assuming that an estimate of the error bound
\varepsilon
is known a priori. Specifically, an unconstrained counterpart of (
3
) is considered, which involves two parameters (one for weighting the fit-to-data and the regularization terms, and one for balancing the two regularization terms); the problem is solved by applying both the discrepancy principle and the L-curve (taking a range of values assigned to
\beta
), requiring the solution of many instances of the considered optimization problem (one for each value of
\beta
) to generate the L-curve.
Recently, the authors of
[
15
]

presented a procedure for tuning
\beta
in image decomposition problems, i.e., problems like (
3
) where
\mathbb{G}
is the identity matrix. However, their method assumes that a good estimate of the minimum non-zero gradient norm of the blocky component
\mathbb{m}_{1}
is known a priori.
Therefore, their method is limited to problems for which this quantity can be estimated directly from the input data.

In this paper, we present an extremely simple and efficient strategy for automatic selection of
\beta
using robust statistics. Our basic idea is that, since the desired model
\mathbb{m}
consists of the blocky component
\mathbb{m}_{1}
and the smooth component
\mathbb{m}_{2}
, from a statistical point of view the model gradient

\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}

is from a mixture of non-Gaussian and Gaussian distributions. Specifically, the entries of
\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{1}
are considered as anomalies/outliers in the model gradient and therefore statistical anomaly detection tools are used to identify them. The robust z-score
[
16
]
allows us to optimally determine the lower limit of the anomalous entries (or the upper limit of normal/Gaussian distributed entries) in the gradient vector. In particular, the optimum
\beta
is determined such that
\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{2}\|_{\infty}

is equal to the minimum value of the anomalies (or, equivalently, the maximum value of the normal entries) determined by the z-score.
A simple algorithm is presented to achieve this, which updates the value of
\beta
at each ADMM iteration, leading to an optimal separation of the model components. Extensive numerical examples from imaging, tomography and geophysical inverse problems are presented to show the excellent performance of the proposed method for solving ill-posed inverse problems with piecewise smooth solutions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present more details about the formulation and ADMM implementation of the Tikhonov-TV regularization method (
3
). In Section
3
, we introduce the new technique for the selection of the balancing parameter
\beta
.
Experimental results are displayed in Section
4
. Finally, some concluding remarks are proposed in Section
5
.

2
Solving the Tikhonov-TV regularized problem using ADMM

In this section we provide some details about the formulation and the solution of problem (
3
), where the parameter
\beta
is fixed. We start by precisely defining the matrices
\mathbb{D}_{1}
and
\mathbb{D}_{2}
introduced in Section
. For problems (
1
) formulated in 1D, we take

\mathbb{D}_{1}:=\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}:=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&&&\\
&-1&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&-1&1\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

(4)

and

\mathbb{D}_{2}:=\mathbb{D}_{2,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}:=\underbrace{(\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)})}_{=:\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}\underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}}_{=\mathbb{D}_{1}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrrrr}1&-2&1&&&\\
&1&-2&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&-2&1\\
&&&&1&-1\\
&&&&&0\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

(5)

where the alternative notations
\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}
and
\mathbb{D}_{2,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}
highlight the differentiation order, the dimensionality of the problem, and the size of the matrices. Although, for the 1D case,
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}=\mathbb{D}_{1}=\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N)}
, we keep these notations to better match the 2D case (see (
7
)). Note that
\mathbb{D}_{1}
and
\mathbb{D}_{2}
as expressed above are associated to a rescaled forward first order and second order finite difference scheme, respectively. For problems (
1
) formulated in 2D, denoting Kronecker products by
\otimes
and identity matrices of size
N
by
\mathbb{I}_{N}
, we take

\mathbb{D}_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{x})}\;&\!\!\!\otimes\!\!\!\!&\mathbb{I}_{N_{z}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{x}}&\!\!\otimes\!\!\!&\,\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{z})}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N},

(6)

which represents the discrete partial first order derivatives in the
x
(or horizontal) direction (matrix on the top) and in the
z
(or vertical) direction (matrix on the bottom). Similarly to the 1D case, we take

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}(\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{x})})\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{x})}&\!\!\otimes\!\!&\mathbb{I}_{N_{z}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{x}}&\!\!\otimes\!\!&(\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{z})})\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{z})}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\;=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}(\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{x})})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{z}}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{I}_{N_{x}}\otimes(\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{z})})\end{pmatrix}}_{=:\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{x})}&\!\!\otimes\!\!&\mathbb{I}_{N_{z}}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N_{x}}&\!\!\otimes\!\!&\mathbb{D}_{1,\text{\scriptsize{1D}}}^{(N_{z})}\end{pmatrix}}_{=\mathbb{D}_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N}.

(7)

Note that the one adopted above is not the only way of defining discrete differential operators of the first and second order. Indeed, one may consider alternative operators obtained varying boundary conditions and/or varying the way first-order operators are combined
[
14
]
.

In order to compute the regularized solution
\mathbb{m}
, one can solve problem (
3
) for
\mathbb{m}_{1}
and
\mathbb{m}_{2}
separately, or reformulate the problem to be solved directly for
\mathbb{m}
.
Note that, in general, the two components
\mathbb{m}_{1}
and
\mathbb{m}_{2}
are not unique: indeed, for any constant signal
\mathbb{c}
, we have that
\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{c}=\mathbb{0}
and
\mathbb{D}_{2}\mathbb{c}=\mathbb{0}
, so that, if the pair (
\mathbb{m}_{1}
,
\mathbb{m}_{2}
) is a solution of (
3
), then
(\mathbb{m}_{1}\pm\mathbb{c},\mathbb{m}_{2}\mp\mathbb{c})
is also a solution of (
3
). However, the sum
\mathbb{m}=\mathbb{m}_{1}+\mathbb{m}_{2}
is unique; see
[
26
]
.
Because of this, in this paper, we solve directly for
\mathbb{m}
.

Let us introduce the auxiliary variables

\mathbb{e}=\mathbb{d-Gm},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{g}_{1}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{g}_{2}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{2}\,,

(8)

so that problem (
3
) can be written in the following equivalent form

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\mathbb{m},\mathbb{g}_{1},\mathbb{g}_{2},\mathbb{e}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle\mathbb{m},\mathbb{g}_{1},\mathbb{g}_{2},\mathbb{e}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\|\mathbb{g}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\mathbb{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(9)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbb{m},\mathbb{g}_{1},\mathbb{g}_{2},\mathbb{e}}}{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{g}_{1}+\mathbb{g}_{2},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}+\mathbb{e}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{d},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{e}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon.

The augmented Lagrangian function associated with problem (
9
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{m},\mathbb{g}_{1},\mathbb{g}_{2},\mathbb{e},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2},\widehat{{\lambda}}_{3})=

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{g}_{1}\|_{1}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\mathbb{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}-\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\mathbb{g}_{2}\rangle-\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2},\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}+\mathbb{e}-\mathbb{d}\rangle-\langle\widehat{\lambda}_{3},\|\mathbb{e}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\mathbb{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}+\mathbb{e}-\mathbb{d}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{3}}{2}(\|\mathbb{e}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon)^{2},

(10)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the canonical inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=1,M,2N
),

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2},\,\widehat{\lambda}_{3}

\mu_{1},\,\mu_{2},\,\mu_{3}>0

[
18
]
.

The

th ADMM iteration has the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{m}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{m}}\>\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{m},\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k-1},\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1},\mathbb{e}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}),

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{g}_{1}}\>\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{m}^{k},\mathbb{g}_{1},\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1},\mathbb{e}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}),

(11b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{g}_{2}}\>\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{m}^{k},\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k},\mathbb{g}_{2},\mathbb{e}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}),

(11c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{e}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{e}}\>\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{m}^{k},\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k},\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k},\mathbb{e},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}),

(11d)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{k-1}-\mu_{1}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathbb{m}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}),

(11e)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}^{k-1}-\mu_{2}(\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}^{k}+\mathbb{e}^{k}-\mathbb{d}).

(11f)

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}-\mu_{3}(\|\mathbb{e}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon)\,.

(11g)

By combining the linear and quadratic terms in the augmented Lagrangian function (
2
) as

-\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i},\mathbb{x}\rangle+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\|\mathbb{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\|\mathbb{x}-(\frac{1}{\mu_{i}})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

(12)

and by making a change of variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}=(1/\mu_{i})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i},i=1,2,3
,
the ADMM iteration in (
11a

) can be written in the simpler scaled form

\displaystyle\mathbb{m}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{m}}\{\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k-1}-\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|\mathbb{Gm}+\mathbb{e}^{k-1}-\mathbb{d}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\},

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{g}_{1}}\{\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbb{g}_{1}\|_{1}\},

(13b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{g}_{2}}\{\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\mathbb{g}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\},

(13c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{e}^{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbb{e}}\{\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\mathbb{e}-\mathbb{d}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{3}}{2}\left(\|\mathbb{e}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon-\lambda_{3}^{k-1}\right)^{2}\},

(13d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}+\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}+\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}-\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}^{k},

(13e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}+\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{e}^{k}-\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}^{k},

(13f)

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{3}^{k}

\displaystyle={\lambda}_{3}^{k-1}+\varepsilon-\|\mathbb{e}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(13g)

In the following, we explain how the subproblems (
13a
)-(
13d
) can be solved efficiently.

Regarding
\mathbb{m}
, since the associated subproblem (
13a
) is a differential Tikhonov regularization problem in standard form, the optimality conditions for (
13a
) lead to

\mathbb{m}^{k}\!=\!\left(\mu_{1}\mathbb{D}_{1}^{T}\mathbb{D}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbb{G}^{T}\mathbb{G}\right)^{-1}\!\!\!\left(\mu_{1}\mathbb{D}_{1}^{T}(\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k-1}\!\!+\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1})\!+\mu_{2}\mathbb{G}^{T}(\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{e}^{k-1}\!\!+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1})\right)\!.

(14)

Although
\mathbb{m}^{k}
has the closed form expression given above, in practice and in a large-scale setting, depending on the properties of
\mathbb{G}
, it may be necessary to apply an iterative solver (such as CG or CGLS) to compute
\mathbb{m}^{k}
; additional details about this will be provided in Section
4
.

Regarding
\mathbb{g}_{1}
, since the associated subploblem (
13b
) is, by definition, the proximal operator associated with the
\ell_{1}
- norm penalty, this can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}=\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}(\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}),

(15)

where the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}
is the soft-thresholding operator defined component-wise as:

[\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}(\mathbb{x})]_{i}=x_{i}\penalty\ \max(1-\frac{1}{\mu_{1}|x_{i}|},0).

(16)

Regarding
\mathbb{g}_{2}
, again the associated subproblem (
13c
) is differentiable, and the optimality condition for (
13c
) leads to

\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}=\left(\mathbb{I}+(\beta/\mu_{1})\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}^{T}\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\right)^{-1}\left({\mathbb{D}_{1}}\mathbb{m}^{k}-\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}\right).

(17)

This is a first-order Tikhonov filter and can be calculated efficiently by different methods including those based on the fast Fourier transform or the discrete cosine transform; see
[
24
]
and the references therein.

Regarding
\mathbb{e}
, the associated subproblem (
13d
) is differentiable but
nonconvex
, and the optimality condition of (
13d
) guarantees that
\mathbb{e}^{k}
satisfies

\left(1+\frac{2\mu_{3}}{\mu_{2}}(\|\mathbb{e}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon-\lambda_{3}^{k-1})\right)\mathbb{e}^{k}=\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\,.

(18)

The solution to this system of equations may not be unique. However, we claim that

\mathbb{e}^{k}=\gamma_{k}(\mathbb{e}^{k})(\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1})\,,

(19)

where the scale parameter
\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{k}(\mathbb{e}^{k})
is the maximum real root of the depressed cubic equation (see
[
7
]
)

\gamma^{3}\,+\,\frac{\mu_{2}-2\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})}{2\mu_{3}\|\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\gamma\,+\,\frac{-\mu_{2}}{2\mu_{3}\|\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}}=0\,,

(20)

satisfies (
18
) and is also the global minimizer of (
13d
). Indeed, expression (
19
) comes from the fact that
\mathbb{e}^{k}
on the left-hand side of (
18
) is premultiplied by a real scalar dependent on
\|\mathbb{e}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}
. By substituting
\mathbb{e}^{k}
expressed as in (
19
) into (
18
) leads to the determination of
\gamma_{k}
as a root of the depressed cubic equation (
20
). Furthermore, plugging
\mathbb{e}^{k}
from (
19
) into the objective function (
13d
) (here denoted by
f(\gamma_{k})
), and letting

E_{k}=\|\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2},\quad p_{k}=\frac{\mu_{2}-2\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})}{E_{k}},\quad q_{k}=-\frac{\mu_{2}}{E_{k}}

to simplify the notations, we get that

\displaystyle f(\gamma_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\gamma_{k}(\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1})-\mathbb{d}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{3}}{2}\left(\|\gamma_{k}(\mathbb{d}-\mathbb{Gm}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1})\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon-\lambda_{3}^{k-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{2}E_{k}}{2}(\gamma_{k}-1)^{2}+\frac{\mu_{3}}{2}(E_{k}\gamma_{k}^{2}-\varepsilon-\lambda_{3}^{k-1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}E_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}^{3}+\frac{\mu_{2}-2\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})}{2\mu_{3}E_{k}}\gamma_{k}-\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{3}E_{k}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{2}E_{k}+\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})^{2})

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}E_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}^{3}+\gamma_{k}^{3}+p_{k}\gamma_{k}+q_{k}-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2\mu_{3}E_{k}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{2}E_{k}+\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})^{2})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{3}E_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}^{3}+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2\mu_{3}E_{k}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{2}E_{k}+\mu_{3}(\varepsilon+\lambda_{3}^{k-1})^{2})\,,

(21)

where we have used the fact that
\gamma_{k}
solves (
20
). It follows that
f(\gamma_{k})
is minimized when evaluated at the largest root of (
20
).

3
Balancing Parameter Selection by Robust Statistics

The Tikhonov-TV regularized problem (
3
) introduced in Section
stems from the statistical assumption that the model gradient
\mathbb{g}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}
is a mixture of two components resulting from different distributions: a non-Gaussian distributed (sparse) component
\mathbb{g}_{1}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{1}
and a Gaussian distributed (non-sparse) component
\mathbb{g}_{2}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}_{2}
. We determine the balancing parameter
\beta
by using statistical tools that allow to optimally separate these two components of the gradient. Specifically, we assume that the non-zero entries of
\mathbb{g}_{1}
, which are associated with jumps in the regularized solution, can be considered as anomalies (or outliers) in the gradient vector
\mathbb{g}
. The lower value of these anomalous entries can be optimally determined by robust statistics
[
20
]
.

According to the classical z-score statistic, an element of a data set is considered an anomaly if it falls outside a distance (e.g., 2.5 standard deviations) from the mean. A smaller distance (e.g., 2.0 standard deviations) can be used if the model size is small and a larger distance (e.g., 3.0 or 3.5 standard deviations) can be used if the model size is large. Therefore, its value can be determined according to the user’s perspective; through this paper we denote this threshold distance by
{\tau_{\tiny{\text{nrm}}}}
and, unless otherwise stated, we set its value to 2.5 for all numerical examples.
A main difficulty in anomaly detection is that the z-score is sensitive to extreme values because the mean and standard deviation are sensitive to extreme values.

The robust z-score
[
16
]
is based on robust estimators rather than the mean and the standard deviation.
A robust measure of mean is the median. For a given vector
\mathbb{g}
, the median of
\mathbb{g}
, denoted by
\text{median}(\mathbb{g})
, is the value such that at least half of the entries in
\mathbb{g}
are smaller than or equal to
\text{median}(\mathbb{g})
, and that at least half of the entries in
\mathbb{g}
are larger than or equal to
\text{median}(\mathbb{g})
.
Thus, if
\mathbb{g}
has an odd number of sorted elements, then
\text{median}(\mathbb{g})
is the middle value; otherwise it can be the average of the two middle values.
A robust measure of standard deviation is known as the median absolute deviation (MAD). For a given vector
\mathbb{g}
, the MAD of
\mathbb{g}
, denoted by
\text{MAD}(\mathbb{g})
, is given by

\text{MAD}(\mathbb{g})=1.4826\penalty\ \text{median}(\mathbb{g}-\text{median}(\mathbb{g}))\,,

(22)

where the constant value 1.4826 is used to make the estimator consistent for Gaussian distributions; see
[
19
]
.
Other alternatives to the MAD can also be used for estimation of the standard deviation; see, again,
[
19
]
.
Using these robust measures of the mean and standard deviation, a robust z-score is assigned to each gradient sample, calculated as

z_{i}=\frac{[\mathbb{g}]_{i}-\text{median}(\mathbb{g})}{\text{MAD}(\mathbb{g})}\,,

(23)

and every element for which
|z_{i}|
is larger than the threshold value
{\tau_{\tiny{\text{nrm}}}}
is considered as anomaly. Consequently, we define
\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g})
as the vector comprising the ‘normal’ (i.e., the ‘not-anomalous’) entries of
\mathbb{g}
, i.e.,

\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g})=\{[\mathbb{g}]_{i}:\penalty\ |z_{i}|\leq{\tau_{\tiny{\text{nrm}}}}\}.

(24)

According to the insight given at the beginning of this section, the optimal balancing parameter to be used in (
3
) should be such that the smooth components
\mathbb{g}_{2}
of the computed solution gradient
\mathbb{g}
are classified as ‘normal’ (
24
) according to the robust z-score (
23
). We impose this condition by defining
\beta_{\text{\scriptsize{opt}}}
as a root of the equation

0=\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}-\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}=:\phi(\beta)\,,\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad\phi(\beta_{\text{\scriptsize{opt}}})=0\,.

(25)

In the above equation, the notations
\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)
and
\mathbb{g}(\beta)
have been employed to highlight the dependence of the vectors
\mathbb{g}_{2}
and
\mathbb{g}
on
\beta
; in the following, both these notations will be freely used depending on the context. In (
25
), the vectors
\mathbb{g}_{2}
and
\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g})
are evaluated in the max norm to enforce that the value of the maximum entry in the smooth and normal components of
\mathbb{g}
coincide.

Proposition 3.1
.

The function
\phi(\beta)
defined in (
25
) has at least one positive root.

Proof.

Once the ADMM iterations (
13a
)-(
13g
) have converged, we can write the computed
\mathbb{g}_{1}
and
\mathbb{g}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}(\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{I}+(\beta/\mu_{1})\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}^{T}\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\right)^{-1}\left({\mathbb{D}_{1}}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\right)

(27)

where the updates rules (
15
) and (
17
) have been exploited.
From the above equalities it can be clearly seen that, when
\beta=0
,
\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)=\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
, so that

\mathbb{g}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}(\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{2}-\mathbf{\lambda}_{1})=\mathcal{T}_{\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}}(\mathbb{g}_{1})\,,

which holds if and only if
\mathbb{g}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. As a consequence,
\mathbb{g}(0)=\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)
. After defining the diagonal matrix
\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{g}_{2})
whose diagonal entries are

[\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{g}_{2})]_{i,i}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $[\mathbb{g}_{2}]_{i}\in\text{norm}(\mathbb{g})$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,

and using standard norm inequalities, we get

\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(0))\|_{\infty}=\|\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{g}_{2})\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{g}_{2})\|_{\infty}\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)\|_{\infty}=\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)\|_{\infty}\,,

so that

\phi(0)=\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(0)\|_{\infty}-\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}_{2}(0))\|_{\infty}\geq 0\,.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{v}_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
the eigenvectors and nonnegative eigenvalues of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}^{T}\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
, respectively. It follows that
\mathbb{g}_{2}
can be expressed as

\mathbb{g}_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\mu_{1}}\sigma_{i}\right)^{-1}(\mathbb{v}_{i}^{T}(\mathbb{D}_{1}\mathbb{m}-\mathbb{g}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}))\mathbb{v}_{i},

so that, when
\beta\rightarrow+\infty
,
\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
and
\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
. As a consequence,

\phi(\beta)=\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}-\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}\rightarrow-\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}\leq 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad\beta\rightarrow+\infty\,.

The result follows from the continuity of
\phi(\beta)
, applying the intermediate value theorem.
∎

Under the reasonable assumption that
\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}
is an increasing function of
\beta
, it is possible to prove that

defined in (

and, therefore, the zero of

is unique; see the arguments in Appendix

for more details.

In order to solve (

\beta\left(\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta))\|_{\infty}+\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}\right)=\beta\left(2\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta)\|_{\infty}\right)\,.

(28)

Then, starting from an initial value

\beta^{j+1}=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{j}+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\left(\frac{4\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta^{j})\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathbb{g}_{2}(\beta^{j})\|_{\infty}+\|\text{nrm}(\mathbb{g}(\beta^{j}))\|_{\infty}}-1\right)\beta^{j}}_{=:\psi(\beta^{j})},\quad j=0,1,\dots\,.

Since the above update rule can be regarded as an averaged iteration algorithm and since, under mild assumptions, it can be proved that the function

is not expansive (see Appendix

the iteration (

,

,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{k-1}
;
\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}

\mathbb{m}^{k}

\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k-1}
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{k-1}
;
\mathbb{g}_{2}^{k}

\mathbb{m}^{k}

\mathbb{g}_{1}^{k}

4.2
Inverse problems in Geophysics

We apply the new automated Tikhonov-TV regularization method for subsurface interval-velocity estimation from root-mean-square (RMS) velocities.
For a horizontally layered earth model, the RMS velocity
V(t)
is a continuous function of time defined as

V(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}[v(\tau)]^{2}d\tau},

(30)

where
v(t)
is the instantaneous/interval velocity function and
t
is the wave propagation time; see
[
9
]
.
In practice, the velocity analysis of common-depth-point (CDP) gathers gives an estimate of the RMS velocity; when both the RMS velocity
V(t)
and the interval velocity
v(t)
are needed, the estimation of the latter from the RMS velocity is a severely ill-conditioned problem, thus proper regularization is required to stabilize the solution. Upon discretization, equation (
30
) reads

V_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}v^{2}_{j}},

(31)

where
i=1,...,N
is the sample number and
N
is the number of earth layers.
Squaring both sides of equation (
31
) results in a system of linear equations of the form
\mathbb{d}=\mathbb{G}\mathbb{m}
,
where
d_{i}=iV_{i}^{2}
,
m_{j}=v_{j}^{2}
and the discrete forward operator
\mathbb{G}
is a causal integration matrix, i.e., a lower triangular matrix of ones

\mathbb{G}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&\\
1&1&&&\\
1&1&1&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
1&1&\cdots&1&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

(32)

We simulate a 1D RMS velocity vector from a velocity log of the 2004 BP model
[
5
]
. The interval and RMS velocities are shown in Figs.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a method for selecting the optimal balancing parameter in Tikhonov-TV regularization for the solution of discrete ill-posed inverse problems with piecewise smooth solutions. Namely, the solution of the inverse problem is split into the sum of a smooth and a piecewise constant component, which are separately regularized by a Tikhonov and a TV term that must be balanced.
We have used robust statistical methods for determining the optimal balance of these terms, motivated by the fact that the gradient entries of the piecewise constant component at jump locations can be considered as anomalies/outliers in comparison to the other gradient entries constituting the smooth background. This led to the characterization of the best balancing parameter as a root of a scalar function.
Finally, an extremely simple update scheme was proposed for determining the balancing parameter, which can be naturally coupled with ADMM to solve the Tikhonov-TV regularized problem.
Extensive numerical experiments on different inverse problems demonstrate that high quality reconstructions can be obtained by applying the proposed algorithm, and validate the robustness of the proposed selection strategy of balancing parameter.
