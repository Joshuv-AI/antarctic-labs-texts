A note on the width of sparse random graphs
Tuan Anh Do, Joshua Erde, and Mihyun Kang

Abstract
. In this note, we consider the width of a supercritical random graph according to some commonly studied width measures. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

The
tree-width
of a graph
G
, which we denote by
\textrm{tw}(G)
, is a parameter which broadly measures how similar the global structure of
G
is to that of a tree, via the existence of a
tree-decomposition
, which displays the structure of
G
in a tree-like fashion. Tree-decompositions were originally studied by Halin
[
13
]
, and later independently by Robertson and Seymour as an integral tool in their proof of Wagner’s conjecture
[
34
]
, and have since then been the subject of much study.

Beyond their usefulness as a tool in structural graph theory, tree-decompositions have also turned out to be important in the field of computational complexity. Many problems which are computationally hard on general graphs can be solved in polynomial time on graphs of bounded tree-width (see Bodlaender
[
3
]
). As the subject developed, many other notions of ‘width’ for graphs, and other combinatorial structures, have been considered, each with their own applications to structural problems, as well as computational problems. One particular example that we will consider in this note is the
rank-width
of a graph
G
, which we denote by
\textrm{rw}(G)
. Roughly, if low tree-width implies that a graph has a ‘tree-like’ decomposition over small vertex cuts, where small here is measured in terms of their cardinality, low rank-width implies that the graph has a ‘tree-like’ decomposition over
simple
edge cuts, where the simplicity of these edge cuts is not measured in terms of just the number of edges crossing them, but rather in terms of an algebraic measure more akin to the complexity of the edge cut. In particular, both very sparse and very dense edge cuts are simple in this way, and so, unlike the case of tree-width, there can be quite dense graphs with low rank-width. In this way, the graphs of low rank-width are a broader class of ‘low-complexity’ graphs than the graphs of low tree-width, and in particular, Oum
[
30
]
showed that the rank-width of a graph is always at most one more than the tree-width. We will give precise definitions of the relevant terms later in Section
2
; for more background on tree-width and other width parameters see the survey of Hliněnỳ, Oum, Seese and Gottlob
[
15
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the width of
random graphs
. The
binomial random graph model

G(n,p)
, introduced by Gilbert
[
12
]
, is a random variable distributed on the subgraphs of the complete graph
K_{n}
where we retain each edge independently with probability
p
. The tree-width of

was first considered by Kloks
[
19
]
, who showed that if

for

, then with probability tending to one as

(whp) the tree-width of
G(n,p)
is at least
rn

. Furthermore, he showed that one can take

as

, which can be seen to be optimal, as the tree-width of a graph on

[

improved the lower bound on

This is a natural question to ask, due to the

This question of Gao

1.2.
Main results

Using a technique of Luczak and McDiarmid
[
26
]
, we are able to give short direct proofs of these results which avoid using Theorem

for

for any

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\epsilon>0

\textrm{rw}\left(G\left(n,p\right)\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{3}}\right)n.

From this it is possible to give a corresponding lower bound on the tree-width of
G(n,p)
, however with a more direct argument we can remove some of the polylogarithmic factors from the result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small and let
p=\frac{1+\epsilon}{n}
. Then whp

\textrm{tw}\left(G\left(n,p\right)\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}}\right)n.

Since we are interested in the behaviour of these parameters as functions of
\epsilon
as
\epsilon\to 0
, we have stated the above results only for the
barely supercritical regime
. However, we note that, since tree-width is an increasing graph parameter, Theorem

clearly implies Theorem

. In the case of Theorem

in Theorem

. In fact, it can be seen that both of these proofs are even effective all the way to the critical window, holding in the

However, neither Theorem

nor

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\epsilon=\epsilon(n)>0
be such that
\epsilon=o(1)
and
\epsilon^{3}n\to\infty

\textrm{rw}\left(G\left(n,p\right)\right)=\Theta\left(\epsilon^{3}n\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\textrm{tw}\left(G\left(n,p\right)\right)=\Theta\left(\epsilon^{3}n\right).

We note that the upper bounds in Theorem

follow quite easily from relatively standard facts about

\Theta(\epsilon^{3}n)

1.3.
Techniques and outline of the paper

A well-known result in structural graph theory says that the tree-width of a graph
G
can be bounded from below by the size of the smallest
balanced separation
, a partition of
V(G)
into three pieces
(A,S,B)
with
\min|A|,|B|\geq\frac{|V(G)\setminus S|}{3}
and
e(A,B)=0
, i.e., there are no edges between
A
and
B
. The
size
of such a separation is the order
|S|
of the separator. The idea of the proofs of Kloks
[
19
]
and of Gao
[
11
]
is to bound the tree-width by showing the likely non-existence of small separators using a union bound, which turns out to only be effective when
\epsilon=np-1
is sufficiently large, due to the large number of possible separators.

A useful trick that can reduce the possible number of separators to consider, following a technique of Luczak and McDiarmid
[
26
]
, is to first find a spanning tree of the graph, and since tree-width is decreasing under taking subgraphs, it suffices to first find a large tree in the random graph. Now, classical results on the phase transition already imply that whp a supercritical random graph contains a tree
T
on
\Omega(\epsilon n)
vertices, and with a bit of care it is possible to control the maximum degree of this tree (as a function of
\epsilon
).

Restricting our attention to the vertex set
V(T)
, we can show with a sprinkling argument that it is very unlikely that any fixed partition of
V(T)
forms a balanced separation. Unfortunately, since each subset
S\subseteq V(T)
could potentially be the separator in many balanced separations, we cannot naively complete the argument with a union bound.

However, since
T
spans
V(T)
, any subset
S\subseteq V(T)
splits
T
into at most
\Delta(T)|S|
many components, and hence there are at most
2^{\Delta(T)|S|}
separations whose separator is
S
. This rather simple observation turns out to be powerful enough to use a union bound to show the likely non-existence of small balanced separators.

We note that, in a similar manner, the existence of such a tree reduces the possible number of small balanced edge cuts of
V(T)
, an idea already used by Spencer and Tóth
[
35
]
in their work on the crossing number of random graphs. This in turn, in a similar manner as for tree-width, can be used to give a lower bound for the rank-width of
G(n,p)[V(T)]
, allowing us to use this technique to also prove Theorem
1.4
.

For the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we follow a similar approach to that of Lee, Lee and Oum
[
23
]
, and of Perarnau and Serra
[
32
]
, and consider the expansion properties of the giant component of
G(n,p)
. However, unlike in the supercritical regime, it is not the case that the giant component typically contains a large expanding subgraph in this regime of
p
. Instead, using standard estimates on the degree distribution of the kernel of
G(n,p)
, we show that whp the kernel contains as an
induced topological minor
a large subgraph
H
which is distributed as a random
3
-regular graph, which is known to whp have good expansion properties. This implies that
H
does not contain any small balanced edge cuts, which, together with the fact that
\Delta(H)=3
, is sufficient to bound the rank-width of
H
from below. Finally, since rank-width can be shown to be decreasing under taking induced topological minors, we can conclude that the rank-width of
G(n,p)
is at least the rank-width of
H
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we give preliminary definitions and results. We give our proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
in Section
3
and then prove Theorem
1.6
in Section

5.
Discussion

As mentioned in the introduction, it is relatively easy to see that the proof of Theorem
1.4
also works without changes in the weakly supercritical regime. However, for the proof of Theorem
1.5
we used Theorem
3.1
, which was only stated for
\epsilon>0
constant. Nevertheless, we note that a similar result can be seen to hold in the weakly supercritical regime, although since the bounds in Theorem
1.5
are superseded by the bounds in Theorem
1.6
in this regime, and, whilst the idea is simple, the technical overhead of implementing it is high, we will just give a short sketch of the idea below. Using such a result it can again be seen that the proof of Theorem
1.5
also works in the weakly supercritical regime.

Indeed, standard results on the distribution of the kernel and
2
-core of the largest component of
G(n,p)
when

with
\delta^{3}n\to\infty
and
\delta=o(1)
imply that whp there is an almost spanning connected subgraph

of the
2
-core, which has order
\Omega(\delta^{2}n)
and maximum degree three.

Furthermore, if we let

be the giant component of

and
C
be the
2
-core of

, then it follows from work of Ding, Kim, Lubetzky and Peres

that the graph

is contiguous to a graph built by choosing, for each vertex

independently, a tree

which is distributed as a Poisson

is the smallest solution to

, and satisfies

. Hence, standard bounds on the distribution of Galton-Watson trees imply that with a positive probability the order of

is

, and whp the maximum degree in

is

, and so whp a positive proportion of the trees

will satisfy these two properties. Taking the union of these trees together with

itself will lead to the claimed subgraph.

Whilst Theorem

is optimal in terms of its dependence on

and

, it seems unlikely that Theorems

and

are. In fact, it seems reasonable to expect that the methods we use to prove Theorem

should extend to prove a similar bound in the barely supercritical regime. Indeed, it is likely that the methods of Łuczak
[

can give bounds of a similar nature to Lemma

. It would then follow by the same arguments as in Section

that whp

. However, since the technical details in

are quite involved, we leave this as an open question, and note that perhaps the main interest in Theorems

and

lies in the simplicity and directness of the proofs, rather than their asymptotic dependence on

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Let
G:=G(n,p)
and let

. The basic idea is to analyse a restricted breadth first search process on the graph, where we limit each vertex to have at most
K

2.8
will imply that we expect each vertex to have more than one neighbour, and so we should expect this process to grow to a large size. In practice, we have to first build a large tree ‘by hand’, so as to guarantee that the correct growth happens with high probability. This strategy to build a large bounded degree tree already appears in the work of Erde, Kang and Krivelevich
[
8
]
, where they use similar arguments to build such a tree with large excess in an arbitrary random subgraph.

Throughout the process we will maintain a set of vertices

which will include the vertices of the tree we are building, together with a small set of vertices which we have discarded during an initial step. We will work under the assumption that

, and later verify that this holds throughout our process. Let us write

.

In the initial step, we will build a

of order

via a greedy process. By a partial binary tree we mean a rooted tree, rooted at a vertex
v
of degree two, in which all other vertices have degree three or one, such that there is some integer
\ell
such that every leaf is at distance
\ell
or
\ell-1
from
v
. Note that, since there are at least
2^{\ell-1}
leaves in such a tree, and at most

vertices, there will be at least
N_{2}:=\log\log\log n
leaves in such a partial binary tree of order
N_{1}
.

To build our partial binary tree of order

, we will make a series of

. In the
i

of a tree

and adding it to
X
, and we grow

U
of some leaf

w
has at least two neighbours, we choose two arbitrarily and add them to

. If
w

and we choose an arbitrary set of size

Whenever we expose the neighbours of a vertex

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)n}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)np(1-p)^{\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)n-1}
