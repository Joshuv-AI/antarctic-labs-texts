The weakness of the Erdős-Moser theorem under arithmetic reductions
Ludovic Levy Patey, Ahmed Mimouni

Abstract
The Erdős-Moser theorem ( 𝖤𝖬 \mathsf{EM} ) says that every infinite tournament admits an infinite transitive subtournament. We study the computational behavior of the Erdős-Moser theorem with respect to the arithmetic hierarchy, and prove that Δ n 0 \Delta^{0}_{n} instances of 𝖤𝖬 \mathsf{EM} admit low n+1 solutions for every n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , and that if a set B B is not arithmetical, then every instance of 𝖤𝖬 \mathsf{EM} admits a solution relative to which B B is still not arithmetical. We also provide a level-wise refinement of this theorem. These results are part of a larger program of computational study of combinatorial theorems in Reverse Mathematics.

Introduction

We conduct a computational study of the Erdős-Moser theorem, an infinitary statement from graph theory.
A

. A subtournament of

to a subdomain

. We identify subtournaments with their domains.
The following statement is known as the Erdős-Moser theorem, and is an infinitary version of some theorem by Erdős and Moser
[
6
]
.

Statement 1.1

(Erdős-Moser theorem)
.

\mathsf{EM}
is the statement “Every infinite tournament admits an infinite transitive subtournament.”

The Erdős-Moser theorem easily follows from the celebrated Ramsey theorem for pairs.
Given a set

and some integer

, we let

denote the set of all unordered
n
-tuples over

.
Given a coloring

is
f
-homogeneous (for color

)
if

Statement 1.2

(Ramsey’s theorem)
.

Given

,

is the statement “Every coloring

admits an infinite

-homogeneous set”.

There exists a one-to-one correspondence between a tournament

and a coloring

f(\{x,y\})=1
if
(R(x,y)\leftrightarrow x<_{\mathbb{N}}y)
.
\mathsf{EM}
can the restated as “for every coloring

, there exists an infinite

-transitive

, that is, for every

Since any

-transitive, the Erdős-Moser theorem can be considered as a particular case of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs.

1.1
\mathsf{EM}
and
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
in Reverse Mathematics

Both the Erdős-Moser theorem and Ramsey’s theorem for pairs have been extensively studied in Reverse Mathematics, both from a computational and a proof-theoretic viewpoint. See Hirschfeldt
[
5
]
for an introduction to the reverse mathematics of combinatorial principles.

From many perspectives,
\mathsf{EM}
is very close to
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. The combinatorics are very similar, and the Erdős-Mőser theorem can be considered as a disjunction-free version of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs. These similarities in combinatorics have many consequences in Reverse Mathematics. Jockusch
[
9
]
proved that every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
admits a
\Pi^{0}_{2}
solution, while there exists a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
solution. These bounds are the same for the Erdős-Moser theorem.
On the proof-theoretic side, the first-order part of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs and the Erdős-Moser theorem are known to coincide
[
2
]
. More generally, most of the known statements implied by
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
are already known to follow from
\mathsf{EM}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, the base theory of Reverse Mathematics. Whether

implies
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
was open for a long time, before Lerman, Solomon and Towsner
[
11
]
answered it negatively.

When considering non-computable instances, the behaviors of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
and
\mathsf{EM}
turn out to be very dramatically different. For every function

, there exists a coloring

such that every infinite

-homogeneous set computes a function

. Indeed, simply take

. Thus, by a theorem of Slaman and Groszek
[
7
]
, there exists a (non-computable) instance of

such that every solution computes every hyperarithmetic (or equivalently
\Delta^{1}_{1}
) set. On the other hand, Patey and Wang (both unpublished) independently proved that for every non-computable set

and every instance of
\mathsf{EM}
, there exists a solution which does not compute
B
. This property of
\mathsf{EM}
is shared with the infinite pigeonhole principle (
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
). Indeed, Dzhafarov and Jockusch
[
4
]
proved that for every non-computable set

and every set

, there is an infinite subset

of

or
\overline{A}
which does not compute
B
.

\mathsf{EM}
and

In this article, we prove the following three theorems:

Main Theorem 1.3

The generalization to the hyperarithmetic hierarchy is not proven, but the authors believe that it holds with the same proof

Fix

2
The big picture

The techniques used in this article are rather sophisticated with many technical subtleties, and it may be quite hard to have the big picture. In this section, we describe the general forcing argument used to prove our main theorems, and highlight a few technical difficulties justifying the design choice of our notion of forcing.

2.1
Forcing question

The three main theorems are related, in that they involve very similar techniques of iterated jump control. Indeed, in each case, it consists of constructing a solution whose
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
properties resemble the ones of the ground model. For this, one tries to translate
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G)
formulas relative to the generic object constructed
G
into absolute
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formulas. In set-theoretic forcing, this is achieved through the forcing relation, whose definition must be sufficiently simple (in terms of definitional complexity) to make the new model inherit properties of the ground model. In computability theory, the situation is slightly different, and the simplicity of the forcing relation is less important than the one of the so-called
forcing question
.

In what follows, a
notion of forcing
is a partial order
(\mathbb{P},\leq)

such that every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
induces a set
G_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
Every notion of forcing is equipped with a forcing relation, written
\Vdash
, between the set of conditions
\mathbb{P}
and the set of arithmetic formulas
\text{Form}[G]
with a set parameter
G
denoting the generic object constructed.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix a notion of forcing
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
.
A
forcing question
is a relation
\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}
over
\mathbb{P}\times\text{Form}[G]
such that, for every
c\in\mathbb{P}
and
\varphi(G)\in\text{Form}[G]
,

(1)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G)
, then there is an extension
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash\varphi(G)
;

(2)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\varphi(G)
, then there is an extension
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash\neg\varphi(G)
;

The notion of forcing question is not canonical, and a single notion of forcing might have many candidate forcing questions. On the other hand, many computational properties of the generic object
G
might be directly derived from the existence of a forcing question with sufficiently nice definitional properties.
Consider for example the following property:

Definition 2.2
.

Fix a notion of forcing
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
and some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A forcing question
\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}
is
uniformly
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving
if for every
c\in\mathbb{P}

and every uniform sequence of
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formulas
\varphi_{0}(G),\varphi_{1}(G),\dots
, the sequence
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi_{0}(G),c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi_{1}(G),\dots
is uniformly
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
.

The following proposition is at the heart of our forcing construction. It was used by Wang
[
19
, Proposition 3.1, Proposition 3.4, Theorem 3.6]
, where the author showed for each notion of forcing the existence of a uniformly
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving forcing question, without naming explicitly this concept.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
be a notion of forcing with a uniformly
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving forcing question.
Then for every non-
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
set

and every sufficiently generic set

for this notion of forcing,

is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G)
.

Proof.

Fix a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}

with one free first-order variable

be the set of conditions

such that either

, or
c\Vdash\neg\varphi(G,a)
for some

is dense.
Given a condition

, let

-preserving, then the set

is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, hence

•

If

, then by definition,
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G,a)
, so by property (1) of the forcing question, there is an extension
d\leq c
such that

.

•

If
a\in C\setminus W
, then by definition,

c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\varphi(G,a)

In both cases,

If

\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G)

2.2
Combinatorics of
\mathsf{EM}

In order to understand the design of the notion of forcing for this article, it is important to get familiar with the combinatorics of the Erdős-Moser theorem. Lerman, Solomon and Towsner
[
11
]
analyzed the basic combinatorial ideas essential to the computable study of the theorem.

A transitive tournament
T
over a domain

can be seen as a linear order
(A,\leq_{T})

defined by

iff
a=b

Definition 2.4

(
[

]
)
.

Fix a tournament

over a domain

(1)

The

(a,b)
between

is the set of points

such that

and

holds.

(2)

Given a finite

-transitive subset

and

, the interval
(a,b)
is
minimal
in

if
(a,b)\cap F=\emptyset
.

Fix a tournament

over
\omega

-transitive set

is not necessarily extendible into an infinite solution. Indeed, maybe there exist some

both hold for cofinitely many

where

is a

is extendible into an infinite solution. This yields the following definition (due to Patey

Definition 2.5

(
[
17
]
)
.

An

-condition

such that

2.

Actually, the second property can be obtained from the first one by a simple application of the infinite pigeonhole principle. Indeed, there are only finitely many minimal

To simplify notation, given two disjoint sets

One essential feature in the understanding of the computational content of a theorem is to understand the combinatorics necessary to extend a partial solution with an arbitrarily large number of elements in one block. In the case of the Erdős-Moser theorem, the following lemma contains its core combinatorics.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
17
]
)
.

Fix an
\mathsf{EM}
-condition

for a tournament

(\sigma\cup\rho,Y)
is a valid extension of

.

Suppose one wants to design a good forcing question for deciding
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
formulas with this notion of forcing. To simplify the situation, assume first that the tournament
T
is
stable

(\forall^{\infty}b)T(a,b)
holds, or
(\forall^{\infty}b)T(b,a)
holds. In other words, each element admits a limit behavior with respect to

Let

be the limit behavior of

, that is,
f(a)=0
iff
\forall^{\infty}bT(a,b)
and
f(a)=1

Definition 2.7
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X)
be an EM-condition,
n
be an integer, and
e

hold

.

By

, and otherwise, there exists an extension forcing
\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\uparrow
. From a definitional viewpoint, the previous relation is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(X\oplus T\oplus f)
. However, the tournament

and its limit behavior

Definition 2.8
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X)
be an EM-condition,

be an integer, and

R
and every function
g:\mathbb{N}\to 2
, there is a finite
g
-homogeneous
R
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X
such that
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\downarrow
.

At first sight, an overapproximation yields a forcing question with even worse definitional properties since it contains a universal second-order quantification. However, thanks to compactness, the forcing question is actually
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(X)
, as it is equivalent to the following definition:

c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow

if there exists some threshold
t
such that for every tournament
R
over
\{0,\dots,t\}
and every function
g:\{0,\dots,t\}\to 2
, there is a finite
g
-homogeneous
R
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X
such that
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\downarrow
.

If the forcing question holds, then by letting
R=T
and
g=f
, it is clear that there exists an extension forcing
\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
. On the other hand, if the forcing question does not hold, then the witness of failure might be some tournaments
R
and some colorings
f
which are unrelated to
T
and
g
. This is where the combinatorics of Ramsey theory comes into play.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X)
be an
\mathsf{EM}
-condition,
n
be an integer and
e
be a Turing index.

(1)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
.

(2)

Else, if
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\uparrow
.

Proof in the stable case.

We prove each point :

(1)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
, letting
R=T
and
g=f
, there exists a finite
f
-homogeneous
T
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X
such that
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\downarrow
. By choice of
f
, there exists some
t\in\omega
such that
\tau\to_{T}X\setminus\{0,\dots,t\}
or
X\setminus\{0,\dots,t\}\to_{T}\tau
. Thus, by

Lemma

2.6
, the pair
d:=(\sigma\cup\tau,X\setminus\{0,\dots,t\})
is an EM-condition. Note that
d\leq c
and that
d\Vdash\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
.

(2)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
, then there exists a coloring
h:\mathbb{N}\to 2
and a tournament
R
such that for all finite
h
-homogeneous and
R
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X
,
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\uparrow
. By the pigeonhole principle and the Erdős-Moser theorem restricted to
X
, there exists an infinite subset
Y\subseteq X
which is both
h
-homogeneous and
R
-transitive. The pair
d:=(\sigma,Y)
is a valid EM-condition such that
d\Vdash\Phi_{e}^{G}(e)\uparrow
.

∎

Whenever the tournament is not stable, the situation seems more complicated as there is no clear choice of
f
. Surprisingly, the previous forcing question still holds, but with a more subtle proof in the first case. The idea is the following: in the first case, by compactness, the finite extension candidate is bounded by a threshold. One can then restrict the reservoir, so that every element below the threshold has a limit behavior with respect to the new reservoir, and then act like in the stable case.

Proof in the general case.

We only prove the first case, as the second case did not involve stability of the tournament.

(1)

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
, by compactness, there exists some threshold
t
such that for every tournament
R
over
\{0,\dots,t\}
and every function
g:\{0,\dots,t\}\to 2
, there is a finite
g
-homogeneous
R
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X
such that
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\downarrow
.

For every element
y\in X\setminus\{0,\dots,t\}
, one can associate a function
g_{y}:\{0,\dots,t\}\to 2
defined by
g_{y}(x)=1
iff
T(x,y)=1
.
Since there are
2^{t}
many such functions, then the function
y\mapsto g_{y}
is a finite coloring of the reservoir, so by the infinite pigeonhole principle, there exists an infinite subset
Y\subseteq X\setminus\{0,\dots,t\}
which is homogeneous for some color
g:\{0,\dots,t\}\to 2
. In other words, for every
g
-homogeneous set
\tau\subseteq\{0,\dots,t\}
, either
\tau\to_{T}Y
, or
Y\to_{T}\tau
.

Letting
R=T
, there is a finite
g
-homogeneous
T
-transitive set
\tau\subseteq X\cap\{0,\dots,t\}
such that
\Phi_{e}^{\sigma\cup\tau}(n)\downarrow
. Thus, by

Lemma

2.6
, the pair
d:=(\sigma\cup\tau,Y)
is an EM-condition. Note that
d\leq c
and that
d\Vdash\Phi_{e}^{G}(n)\downarrow
.

∎

Together with the general discussion of
Section

2.1
about forcing questions,
this section constitutes a proof that
\mathsf{EM}
admits strong cone avoidance.

Remark 2.10
.

The bottom line of this section is the following: The combinatorics of the Erdős-Moser theorem involve the pigeonhole principle, in that in order to ensure the extendibility of a finite
T
-transitive set, one needs to ensure that it is homogeneous for the appropriate instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
. This 2-coloring represents the limit behavior of the tournament. Whenever the tournament is not stable, the choice of the 2-coloring is not clear ahead of time, and the colorings must be universally quantified.

Last, note that the use of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
in the proof of
\mathsf{EM}
is not overkill, in that given a 2-coloring
f:\mathbb{N}\to 2
, one can define a tournament
T
by
T(x,y)
iff
[x<y\leftrightarrow f(x)=f(y)]
. Then any infinite transitive subtournament is, up to finite changes,
f
-homogeneous.

2.3
Iterated jump control of EM forcing

In computability-theoretic forcing, one usually forces a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}/\Pi^{0}_{1}
property in a strong sense: if
c\Vdash\varphi(G)
for
\varphi\in\{\Sigma^{0}_{1},\Pi^{0}_{1}\}
, then
\varphi(G_{\mathcal{F}})
actually holds for every filter
\mathcal{F}
containing
c
. The situation becomes significantly more complicated when considering
\Sigma^{0}_{2}/\Pi^{0}_{2}
formulas.

A
\Pi^{0}_{2}
formula
(\forall x)(\exists y)\varphi(G,x,y)
can be considered as a countable collection of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
formulas
\{(\exists y)\varphi(G,n,y):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Such a formula cannot usually be forced in a strong sense. The relation
c\Vdash(\forall x)(\exists y)\varphi(G,x,y)
holds iff for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of conditions forcing
(\exists y)\varphi(G,x,y)
is dense below
c
. This way, every sufficiently generic filter containing
c
will also contain a condition forcing
(\exists y)\varphi(G,x,y)
for each
x\in\mathbb{N}
, thus the property
(\forall x)(\exists y)\varphi(G_{\mathcal{F}},x,y)
holds for every sufficiently generic filter containing
c
.

Stating the density of a collection of conditions can be a definitionally complex statement, depending on the complexity of the notion of forcing. In some simple cases, such as Cohen forcing, the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{2}(G)
formulas is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
, yielding a good forcing question.

Variants of Mathias forcing do not behave that well. Indeed, the statement of density requires universal and existential quantification on the conditions, hence on the reservoirs, which are second-order objects. Actually, the approach of Mathias forcing provably fails:

Lemma 2.11

(Folklore)
.

The set
\emptyset^{\prime\prime}
is
\Pi^{0}_{2}(G_{\mathcal{F}})
for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
for Mathias forcing with computable reservoirs.

Proof.

By Martin’s domination theorem, a set is of high degree iff it computes a function eventually dominating every total computable function. Given a computable function
f
and a computable Mathias condition
(\sigma,X)
, there exists a computable Mathias extension
(\sigma,Y)
such that the principal function of
Y
(the function which to
n
associates the
n
th element of
Y
) dominates
f
. Thus, for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
, the principal function of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
will eventually dominate every total computable function, hence be of high degree.
∎

The same argument holds for computable EM forcing.
Intuitively, the reason of failure of Mathias forcing and its variants, is because of the sparsity of its reservoirs. One way to circumvent the problem is to restrict the class possible reservoirs with a third-order object, which will play the role of a “reservoir of reservoirs”: this meta-reservoir is a class of infinite sets
\mathcal{L}
which cannot contain arbitrarily sparse objects. Our goal is to work with EM conditions
(\sigma,X)
such that
X\in\mathcal{L}
. One however requires the class
\mathcal{L}
to be closed under certain operations which are needed for using the combinatorics of
Section

2.2
. Analyzing the operations made over the reservoirs, they are of three kinds:

1.

truncation of a reservoir from a finite number of elements (case 1 of the forcing question)

2.

splitting of a reservoir based on an instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
(case 2 of the forcing question)

3.

choice of an
R
-transitive subtournament for an instance
R
of
\mathsf{EM}
(case 2 of the forcing question)

Assuming
\mathcal{L}
contains only infinite sets, the truncation operation is a consequence of finitely many applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
. Unfortunately, contrary to the pigeonhole principle, the classes which are closed under applications of
\mathsf{EM}
do not have nice combinatorial properties. Therefore, in Case 2 of
Lemma

2.9
, instead of applying the Erdős-Moser theorem restricted to
X
to obtain an infinite
R
-transitive subtournament
Y\subseteq X
, we shall simply add
R
to the list of tournaments we commit to be transitive for. The benefit of it is that the only remaining operation done on our reservoirs is the application of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
. The counterpart of postponing our application of
\mathsf{EM}
is that our forcing conditions will now be made of triples
(\vec{R},\sigma,X)
, where
\vec{R}
is a finite sequence of tournaments, and such that
(\sigma,X)
is an EM condition for every
R\in\vec{R}
. This list
\vec{R}
can grow with condition extension.

The reservoir
X
will therefore belong to a class
\mathcal{L}
which is closed under applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
. This notion of closure is called
partition regularity
. We shall introduce this concept and its main properties in
Section

3
.
The restriction of the reservoirs to those which belong to a partition regular class dramatically decreases the definitional complexity of the forcing question, as instead of asking whether for every infinite set
Y\subseteq X
, there is an infinite set
Z\subseteq Y
satisfying some property, one can ask whether the collection of all
Z
satisfying the property is partition regular. Based on the complexity of the property, the question will not be definitionally too complex.

3
Partition regularity and largeness

The notion of partition regularity comes from combinatorics and is widely used in Ramsey theory. It therefore naturally occurred in the computability-theoretic analysis of combinatorial theorems.

Definition 3.1
.

A class
\mathcal{L}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is
partition regular
if :

•

\mathcal{L}
is non-empty,

•

for all
X\in\mathcal{L}
, if
X\subseteq Y
, then
Y\in\mathcal{L}
,

•

for every integer
k
, for every
X\in\mathcal{L}
, for every
k
-cover
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{k}
of
X
, there exists
i\leq k
such that
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{L}.

Dorais
[
3
]
was the first to use variants of Mathias forcing in which the reservoirs belong to partition regular classes, to produce generic sets of non-high degree. Since then, Monin and Patey
[
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
successfully used this variant to control iterated jump of solutions to the infinite pigeonhole principle. Monin and Patey
[
15
, Section 2]
contains all the computability-theoretic analysis of partition regularity used in this article.
In this section, we therefore simply state the relevant definitions and theorems for the sake of completeness.

Partition regularity enjoys nice closure properties, but is not a notion of largeness per se, in that a superclass of a partition regular class is not necessarily partition regular itself. Throughout the article, given a property
\varphi(X)
, we will ask whether the class
\{X:\varphi(X)\}
is large, in the sense that it contains a partition regular subclass. This yields the following definition, which is often more convenient to manipulate than partition regularity.

Definition 3.2
.

A class
\mathcal{L}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is
large
if :

•

for all
X\in\mathcal{L}
, if
X\subseteq Y
, then
Y\in\mathcal{L}
,

•

for every integer
k
, for every
k
-cover
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{k}
of
\omega
, there exists
i\leq k
such that
Y\in\mathcal{L}.

The large classes are exactly those which contain a partition regular subclass. To avoid degenerate behaviors, in this article, we shall restrict ourselves to large classes which contain only infinite sets.

Definition 3.3
.

A large class is
non-trivial
if it contains only infinite sets.

In particular, if a partition regular class
\mathcal{P}
is non-trivial, then it is closed under finite changes. Indeed, if
X\in\mathcal{P}
and
Y=^{*}X
as witnessed by a finite set
F
, then
Y,F
form a 2-cover of
X
, so either
Y
or
F
must belong to
\mathcal{P}
, and by non-triviality,
F\not\in\mathcal{P}
.

One of the core properties of large classes is the following lemma, which plays an essential role in the computability-theoretic analysis of large classes. For example, by contraposition, if an
F_{\sigma}
class is not large, then it is included into a non-large open class.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
be a decreasing sequence of large classes. Their intersection
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{P}_{n}
is again large.

The above lemma also holds if one replaces largeness by partition regularity. Moreover, a union of partition regular classes is still partition regular. Therefore, every large class contains a largest (for inclusion) partition regular subclass, which justifies the following definition.

Definition 3.5
.

For every large class
\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
denote the largest partition regular subclass of
\mathcal{P}
.

By the infinite pigeonhole principle, the class of all infinite sets is partition regular. This naturally generalizes to a whole family of partition regular classes:

Definition 3.6
.

For every set
X\subseteq\omega
, let
\mathcal{L}_{X}:=\{E\subseteq\omega:|E\cap X|=\infty\}
.

For every infinite set
X
, the class
\mathcal{L}_{X}
is partition regular. This class plays an essential role in ensuring that a set belongs to all partition regular subclasses. Indeed, if
\mathcal{P}
is a partition regular subclass of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
, then
X\in\mathcal{P}
, as otherwise, since
X,\overline{X}
forms a 2-cover of
\omega
, we would have
\overline{X}\in\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
, contradiction.

3.1
\Pi^{0}_{2}
large classes

By Monin and Patey
[
16
, Proposition 2.15]
, there are no non-trivial
\mathbf{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}
large classes. The first interesting example of such classes are
\Pi^{0}_{2}
.
Along this article, we will only be interested in
F_{\sigma}
classes, and more precisely intersections of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M})
classes, for some Scott set
\mathcal{M}
(recall that a formula is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M})
if it is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(P)
for some
P\in\mathcal{M}
). Fix a Scott set
\mathcal{M}
encoded by a set
M\subseteq\omega
, i.e.,
M=\bigoplus_{n\in\omega}X_{n}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{X_{n}:n\in\omega\}
. One can code such classes by sets
C\subseteq\omega^{2}
as follows:

Definition 3.7
.

Fix a set
P
. For every
e\in\omega
, let
\mathcal{U}^{P}_{e}=\{Z\in 2^{\omega}:\exists\sigma\in W^{P}_{e}:\sigma\subseteq Z\}
.
For every
C\subseteq\omega^{2}
, let
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}=\bigcap_{(e,i)\in C}\mathcal{U}^{X_{i}}_{e}
.

The following lemma is a core lemma in the analysis of the definitional complexity of the statement “
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is large”, thanks to
Lemma

3.4
.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
class. The sentence “
\mathcal{A}
is large” is
\Pi_{2}^{0}
.

By
Lemma

3.4
, a class
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is large iff
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{F}
is large for every finite set
F\subseteq C
. The class
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{F}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M})
uniformly in
F
, hence by a relativization of
Lemma

3.8
, the statement “
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{F}
is large” is
\Pi^{0}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
uniformly in
F
. The overall statement “
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is large” is therefore
\Pi^{0}_{1}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
, where
M
is the set coding
\mathcal{M}
.

The following lemma shows that instead of working with large classes of the form
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
,
one can work with partition regular classes without extra definitional complexity.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
15
]
)
.

For every set
C\subseteq\omega^{2}
, there exists
D\leq_{T}C
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{D}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C})
.

3.2
\mathcal{M}
-minimal and
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive classes

When two classes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are large, their intersection
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{Q}
is not necessarily large. For example, letting
X
be a bi-infinite set, both the classes
\mathcal{L}_{X}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
are large, but their intersection is not, as witnessed by the 2-cover
X,\overline{X}
of
\omega
.
During the forcing construction, one will consider multiple properties to be forced, and therefore will need to ensure that not only the corresponding classes are large, but so are their intersection. One natural approach consists in creating a large class which will be minimal for inclusion, in the following sense:

Definition 3.10
.

A class
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathcal{M}
-minimal
if for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
e\in\omega
, either
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}
or
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}
is not large.

Then, in order to decide whether two
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M})
properties
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are large, one can ask independently whether
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{Q}
are large. If both are, then by
\mathcal{M}
-minimality of
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, hence
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{Q}
is large as well.
Eventhough the notion of minimality was defined for largeness, partition regularity comes for free for an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large class.

Lemma 3.11

(
[
15
]
)
.

Every
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large class
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is partition regular.

There exists
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large classes of the form
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
. However, the index set
C
is computationally too complex, as it is only
M^{\prime\prime}
-computable. Indeed, in order to create the set
C
by finite approximations
C_{0}\subseteq C_{1}\subseteq\dots
, one needs to successively ask whether
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C_{s}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}
is large, which is a
\Pi^{0}_{2}(\mathcal{M})
question. Thankfully, one can consider a weaker notion with better computational properties, which still satisfies the compatibility requirements.

Definition 3.12
.

A class
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive
if for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
, either
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
or
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
.

Every
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive. Moreover, one can compute the index set
C
of an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
in any PA degree over
M^{\prime}
. Indeed, instead of deciding whether
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C_{s}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{X}
is large or not, one needs to pick a true statement among “
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C_{s}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{X}
is large” and “
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C_{s}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
is large”. Choosing a true
\Pi^{0}_{2}(M)
sentence among two such sentences, known that one of them is true, can be computed by any PA degree over
M^{\prime}
(see Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
1
, Lemma 4.2]
for a proof).

Lemma 3.13

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
be an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class. Let
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
be such that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
are both large. Then so is
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

In general, given a large class
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
, the index set
C
can be completed into an index set
D\supseteq C
in multiple ways to form an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large subclass
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{D}
, depending on the order in which the questions are asked. However, whenever
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive, then by
Lemma

3.13
, the order of the questions does not matter, thus it contains a unique
\mathcal{M}
-minimal subclass, which can be explicitly defined as follows:

Lemma 3.14
.

Given an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive large class
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
, the collection of sets

\langle\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\rangle:=\bigcap_{e\in\omega,X\in\mathcal{M}}\{\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}:\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}\text{ is a large }\}

is an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large class contained in
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

3.3
The
(\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}})_{n\in\omega}
sequence

Monin and Patey
[
15
, Section 2.5]
defined an infinite hierarchy of Scott sets together with a decreasing sequence of minimal classes for these Scott sets, playing a central role in the definition of the notion of forcing. The
n
th level of this hierarchy is responsible for having a good
(n+1)
st jump control.

The following first proposition is an easy consequence of the uniform low basis theorem for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes, due to Lawton (see Hirschfeldt and al.
[
8
, Theorem 4.1]
):

Proposition 3.15

(
[
15
]
)
.

There exists a sequence of sets
({M}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
such that :

•

M_{n}
codes for a countable Scott set
\mathcal{M}_{n}
,

•

\emptyset^{(n)}
is uniformly coded by an element of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
,

•

Each
M_{n}^{\prime}
is uniformly computable in
\emptyset^{(n+1)}
.

Then, the next proposition follows from our remark on the complexity of the construction
of an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive large class. Indeed, since
\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
contains
M^{\prime}_{n}
and
\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
is a Scott set, then it contains a PA degre over

. Moreover, this construction is uniform.

Proposition 3.16

(
[
15
]
)
.

There exists a sequence of sets
({C}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
such that :

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}
is an
\mathcal{M}_{n}
-cohesive large class,

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{n+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n+1}}\subseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
,

•

Each
C_{n}
is coded by an element of
\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
uniformly in
n
and
M_{n+1}
.

4
Forcing framework

We now develop the general framework for iterated jump control of solutions to the Erdős-Moser theorem through forcing. This framework will be applied in sections

6

4.1
Forcing conditions

In order to obtain a layerwise version of strong cone avoidance of
\mathsf{EM}

4.2
Forcing question

In this section, we design a forcing question as explained in
Section

2.1
. The general idea is the following: given a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
formula
(\exists x)\varphi(G,x)
and a condition
(\vec{R},\sigma,X)
, one would like to ask whether there exists some
x\in\omega
and a finite set
\tau\subseteq X
satisfying some good combinatorial properties, such that
\varphi(\sigma\cup\tau,x)
holds. This naive question is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\vec{R}\oplus X)
. As explained in
Section

2.2
, one can get rid of the parameter
\vec{R}
by universally quantifying over all
m
-tuples of tournaments, where
m=|\vec{R}|
. By compactness, the question becomes
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(X)
, which is not enough, since
X
can be computationally very complex. The same overapproximation trick cannot be applied for
X
, since the class of all infinite sets is closed, but not compact. One must therefore use a second trick: consider the class
\mathcal{L}
of all reservoirs
Y
such that this property holds, that is, of all
Y
such that there exists some
x\in\omega
and a finite set
\tau\subseteq X
satisfying some good combinatorial properties, such that
\varphi(\sigma\cup\tau,x)
holds. Then, ask whether
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}_{C_{0}}
is large. If it does, then by
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-minimality of
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, and since
X\in\mathcal{P}
, the property holds for
X
in particular.

Before giving the actual definition of the forcing question, let us introduce a very convenient piece of notation. As explained, given a condition
(\vec{R},\sigma,X)
, since no effectiveness constraint is imposed on
\vec{R}
, one will often resort to an over-approximation of
\vec{R}
. This over-approximation
\mathcal{C}
has two essential properties: (1) it must contain
\vec{R}
, and (2) it must be
X
-effectively compact. One can exploit the
\Pi^{0}_{1}
constraints in the definition of a condition to obtain a finer over-approximation:
X
is included in a minimal interval of
\sigma
and
\sigma\cup\{y\}
is
\vec{R}
-transitive for every
y\in X
. This yields the following definition:

Definition 4.4
.

Let
(\sigma,X)
be a Mathias condition.
For every
m\in\omega
,
\mathcal{C}_{m}(\sigma,X)
is the class of all
m
-tuples
\vec{R}
of tournaments such that for all
y\in X
,
\sigma\cup\{y\}
is
\vec{R}
-transitive, and such that
X
is included in a minimal
\vec{R}
-interval of
\sigma

In particular, for every condition
(\vec{R},\sigma,X)
, letting
m=|\vec{R}|
,
\vec{R}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}(\sigma,X)

\mathcal{C}_{m}(\sigma,X)
is an over-approximation of
\vec{R}
. On the other direction, if
(\sigma,X)
is a Mathias condition such that

, then for all

and for all
\vec{R}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}(\sigma,X)

is a condition in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{P}}
. The following lemma shows that the over-approximation

is

Lemma 4.5

For every Mathias condition
(\sigma,X)
and every

,

\mathcal{C}_{m}(\sigma,X)
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}(X)
.

Proof.

Immediate. Both properties are

6
Layerwise strong cone avoidance

In this section, we are going to twist the previous notion of forcing to obtain a layerwise version of
1.3
. More precisely, the goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Main Theorem
1.4
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. If
B
is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, then for every tournament
T
, there is an infinite transitive subtournament
H
such that
B
is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(H)
.

As explained in
Section

2.1
, the proof of such theorems is closely related to the existence of a uniformly
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving forcing question, that is, a forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G)
formulas which is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
uniformly in its parameters.

Unfortunately, by

4.8
, forcing a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G)
formula is
\Pi^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n})
, which is not the desired definitional complexity. We are going to use the same trick as Monin and Patey
[
15
]
and define a twisted notion of forcing “on the top”, that is, leaving the lower levels unchanged, we are going to replace the Scott set
\mathcal{M}_{n}
by another Scott set
\mathcal{N}_{n}
with more suited properties, and define a different forcing question on the top level.

Intuitively, the bad complexity of the forcing question comes from the fact that, since there is no effectiveness restriction on the reservoir
X
, the only way to decide properties is to check whether the class of sets satisfying this property is large. Largeness of a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
property is
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
. Therefore, at the top level, the forcing question will have to directly involve the reservoir. The counterpart is that the forcing conditions will need to impose effectiveness restrictions on the reservoirs, which will raise a few technical difficulties.

\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n})

about diagonalization to work, one needs

not to be
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n})
. We will therefore replace the Scott set
\mathcal{M}_{n}
with another Scott set
\mathcal{N}_{n}
coded by a set

with the following two properties:

•

\emptyset^{(n)}
is coded by an element of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
;

•

is not
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{n})

The second fact replaces the previous assumption that

is computable in

.
The existence of such a Scott set follows from the following proposition by Wang

6.2
Top forcing conditions

The notion of forcing for layerwise cone avoidance resembles the previous notion of forcing, with a few distinctive features.

•

First, since one needs to control only
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}(G)
properties, the partition generic class
\mathcal{P}
will only need to be included in a minimal class of a finite level of the hierarchy of Scott sets. We will actually choose
\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle

•

Second, since the forcing question on the top will depend on the reservoir
X
, one must require that

, in order to obtain a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{n})
forcing question. Since
B
is not
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{n})
, the diagonalization lemma will hold.

•

Last, as explained above, since the proof of

4.12
splits the reservoir based on 2-partitions computable in the tournaments, one must require that
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is closed under this operation. By construction of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
, the closure is ensured for the original tournament

. However, in the proof of

, new tournaments will be added to the condition. Thankfully, all the new tournaments can be chosen as members of

classes, and since

is a Scott set, one can require that

. The new notion of condition will therefore distinguish between the original tournament

which can be of arbitrary strength, and the new tournaments

added along the construction of a generic filter, and which will belong to

.

.

.

Lemma 7.4
