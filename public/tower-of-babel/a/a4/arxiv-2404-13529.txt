Towards Parameter-free Distributed Optimization: a Port-Hamiltonian Approach
Rodrigo Aldana-López, Alessandro Macchelli, Giuseppe Notarstefano

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel distributed optimization technique for networked systems, which removes the dependency on specific parameter choices, notably the learning rate. Traditional parameter selection strategies in distributed optimization often lead to conservative performance, characterized by slow convergence or even divergence if parameters are not properly chosen. In this work, we propose a systems theory tool based on the port-Hamiltonian formalism to design algorithms for consensus optimization programs. Moreover, we propose the Mixed Implicit Discretization (MID), which transforms the continuous-time port-Hamiltonian system into a discrete time one, maintaining the same convergence properties regardless of the step size parameter. The consensus optimization algorithm enhances the convergence speed without worrying about the relationship between parameters and stability. Numerical experiments demonstrate the method’s superior performance in convergence speed, outperforming other methods, especially in scenarios where conventional methods fail due to step size parameter limitations.

Introduction

Current technological trends lean towards the extended use of machine learning algorithms with increasing complexity and computing power requirements. Fortunately, computation and communication capabilities are readily available in modern devices. For this reason, distributed implementations have emerged as scalable and robust alternatives to deploy these technologies. Since many tasks of importance, e.g., the training of neural networks can be posed as optimization programs, distributed optimization has become a persistently active area of research in the last years (see
[
1
,
2
]
). The problem of consensus optimization is particularly important, where the unconstrained cost is comprised of the sum of local costs that can be accessed at each device in the network separately
[
1
]
.

In the following, we review the literature on algorithms developed for distributed optimization problems. First, we examine algorithms formulated in continuous time, leveraging tools from systems theory to guarantee stability. Subsequently, we discuss methodologies that operate in discrete time, reflecting more practical communication scenarios. Finally, we mention works using the Port-Hamiltonian System (PHS) formalism in multi-agent contexts and parameter-free discretization.

Many algorithms for addressing distributed optimization problems through continuous-time systems exist in the literature. For instance, a method for consensus optimization over weighted digraphs was introduced in
[
3
]
. A different approach, employing a proportional-integral protocol to tackle constrained distributed optimization, was proposed in
[
4
]
. The strategy in
[
5
]
aims to resolve constrained distributed convex optimization problems via non-smooth analysis. Meanwhile,
[
6
]
presented a protocol with finite-time convergence in solving constrained distributed optimization issues. The use of adaptive control techniques for unconstrained distributed optimization was explored in
[
7
]
. A passivity-based methodology for designing and analyzing distributed protocol algorithms in the unconstrained scenario was used in
[
8
]
, with a similar approach adopted for balanced digraphs in
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, a distributed protocol leveraging Newton-Raphson iterations was detailed in
[
10
]
. These contributions underscore the benefits of modeling distributed optimization algorithms in continuous time, allowing for the application of systems theory tools, such as Lyapunov theory or passivity. However, given that communication among agents in digital platforms occurs in discrete time, research has pivoted towards considering discrete time interactions. A leading methodology for consensus optimization in discrete time, known as the gradient tracking method, was introduced in
[
11
]
, incorporating a dynamic average consensus component to track the gradient of the objective function. This method has spawned various adaptations, including those tailored for nonconvex programs
[
12
]
and protocols integrating Newton-Raphson iterations
[
13
]
. Extensions to accommodate time-varying and stochastic networks were proposed in
[
11
,
14
]
, respectively. Efforts to enhance convergence speed are used in
[
15
,
16
]
. Techniques such as push-sum and push-pull protocols for message passing, aimed at addressing digraphs, were applied in
[
17
,
18
]
. An adaptive momentum term to boost convergence rates was introduced in
[
19
]
. Using a systems structure perspective, most of these variations can be understood using a general structural framework as in
[
20
]
. Despite their generally effective performance, these algorithms constrain specific parameters, like the learning rate. Tuning such parameters is critical, especially in multi-agent settings where the constraints depend on global information such as the network topology. For instance, a large learning rate may result in instability, and a small learning rate may lead to slow convergence.

An alternative line of research for incorporating discrete time communication involves employing continuous-time systems with event-triggered communication. One of the first works in this context is
[
21
]
. Similar settings, including quantized information, were explored in
[
22
]
. Extensions for quadratic optimization programs with constraints were considered in
[
23
]
. Event-triggered mechanisms for second-order dynamics agents were investigated in
[
24
]
. The works
[
25
,
26
]
explored edge-based approaches for the design of the triggering conditions. A noteworthy contribution in this domain is
[
27
]
, which extends the continuous-time version of the gradient tracking algorithm to include event-triggered communication. While adopting many advantages from continuous-time methodologies, these approaches also need an upper limit on the interval between communications, as indicated in
[
21
]
. Additionally, they require the implementation of a discrete time variant for the continuous-time algorithm, exemplified by
[
27
]
, where the event-triggering condition is evaluated at each timestep during experiments. Hence, parameter constraints cannot be avoided either in this case.

Henceforth, this motivates the need to develop distributed optimization methods that are not limited by design parameter constraints. An important breakthrough for parameter-free optimization was presented in
[
28
]
where the Port-Hamiltonian System (PHS) formalism was used to design a continuous-time gradient flow for unconstrained optimization in a single-agent setting. This formalism allowed the authors to propose a parameter-free symplectic discretization. Generally, a PHS is a class of nonlinear systems describing a wide range of systems of interest, particularly suitable for power-conserving interconnections between subsystems
[
29
]
. This formalism has been proved useful to describe and analyze multi-agent algorithms for consensus
[
30
]
and distributed control in
[
31
]
. Moreover, geometric integration using the discrete gradient has been shown to preserve the PHS structure
[
32
,
33
]
, with a precise parallelism between continuous and discrete time. In addition, under suitable conditions, this type of discretization can ensure convergence regardless of the step size parameter. For instance, the discrete gradient method was used in
[
34
]
to develop a parameter-free optimization algorithm. However, despite the advantages of PHS and geometric integration methods, these tools have yet to be used to solve distributed optimization problems.

The contributions of this work are twofold. First, we propose a PHS framework for the first time to desig and analyze distributed optimization algorithms. In particular, we apply it to solve an unconstrained consensus optimization program, where the convergence proofs are based on PHS tools. Second, our proposal allows the design in continuous time with a novel parameter-free discretization, referred to here as Mixed Implicit Discretization (MID), that enables the preservation of the PHS structure. In this sense, all agents in the network choose a step size parameter without worrying about the instability of the overall system. In addition, we discuss how having the step size as a free design parameter can be further used to accelerate convergence.

This manuscript is organized as follows. First, in Section
2
, we provide a problem statement and some preliminaries on PHS. Section
3
presents our PHS-based framework for consensus optimization in continuous time, including some concrete examples. Section
4
discusses parameter-free discretization. In Section
5
, we provide several numerical examples, comparing the proposal’s performance against other methods in the literature. Conclusions are drawn in Section
6
, and auxiliary results and proofs are placed in the appendices.

2
Poblem statement and preliminaries

2.1
Problem statement

Consider a group of
{\textsf{N}}\in\mathbb{N}
agents labeled using an index set
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,{\textsf{N}}\}
, whose goal is to solve a consensus optimization program of the form:

\bm{\theta}^{*}=\arg\!\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\textsf{N}}f_{i}(\bm{\theta})

(1)

where each
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function which can only be accessed at agent
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex with constant
\mu>0
(See Appendix
A
), and has Lipschitz continuous gradient with constant
L>0
.

Note that Assumption
1
ensures that the program in (
1
) has a unique optimal solution
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Moreover, to obtain such optimal solution, agents cooperate by exchanging information with their neighbors. The communication network is modeled by a fixed undirected connected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{E})
. Here,
\mathcal{I}
is the vertex set, where each
i\in\mathcal{E}
represents a concrete agent, and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}
is the edge set where
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
when agents
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
can communicate. Moreover, we denote with
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
the neighbor set for agent
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Often, an algorithm to solve the program in (
1
) in a distributed fashion often involves a set of design parameters. The algorithm is parameter-free if the constraints for the parameters do not require prior information of the network or the cost function constants
(\mu,L)
to ensure convergence towards the optimal solution. This work proposes a parameter-free solution to solve the program (
1
) based on the PHS structure.

2.2
Port-Hamiltonian systems

A PHS is a particular class of dynamical system that can be described through the interaction of ports with power-conserving features. In particular, it has the form:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\nabla H(\mathbf{x})+\bm{B}_{I}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}_{I}+\bm{B}_{C}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}_{C}

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state variable. The system in (
2
) has four classes of ports. First, a storage port
(-\dot{\mathbf{x}},\nabla H(\mathbf{x}))
with scalar storage function
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
also called the Hamiltonian energy. Second, a resistive structure associated with the symmetric part of
\mathbf{F}
, which satisfies
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
. Third, an interaction port with
(\mathbf{u}_{I},\mathbf{y}_{I})
. This port usually represents the interaction of (
2
) with other systems. Fourth, a control port
(\mathbf{u}_{C},\mathbf{y}_{C})
. This port is used for feedback control.

It can be verified that (
2
) is passive in the sense that:

\dot{H}(\mathbf{x})\leq\mathbf{y}_{I}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{I}+\mathbf{y}_{C}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{C}

with outputs

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{I}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{I}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\nabla H(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{C}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\nabla H(\mathbf{x}).

As will become evident in subsequent sections, the passivity property helps design control feedback laws and obtain Lyapunov functions based on
H
. We refer the reader to
[
29
]
for more details on PHS.

3
PHS-based optimization in continuous time

3.1
System proposal

To go towards a parameter-free discrete time algorithm to solve (
1
), we start by considering a continuous-time formulation based on a PHS. Assume that each agent
i\in\mathcal{I}
stores a state variable
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The proposed dynamics for
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)
are given as:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{i}\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\bm{\phi}_{i}(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{M}\left(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\nabla H_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)

(3)

Where
\mathbf{F}_{i},\mathbf{M}
are constant matrices satisfiying
\mathbf{F}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
. Moreover,
H_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the storage function at agent
i
and
\bm{\phi}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonlinear vector field. Time dependence for the state was omitted in (
3
) for readability. For the same reason, we will also omit state dependence on
H_{i}
when there is no ambiguity.

Note that the system in (
3
) is a PHS since the symmetric part of
\mathbf{F}_{i}+|\mathcal{N}_{i}|\mathbf{M}
is negative semi-definite. Additionally, it includes
|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
interaction ports, each connected to one of the neighbors of the agent with input
\nabla H_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
and input matrix
-\mathbf{M}
. Moreover, it features a local feedback control port with input
\bm{\phi}_{i}(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))
and input matrix
\mathbf{I}_{n}
. Note that the terms of the form
\nabla H_{i}-\nabla H_{j}
in (
3
) define an agreement error between neighboring agents and promote consensus.

The remainder of this section aims to show that the PHS structure in (
3
) naturally ensures convergence towards the equilibrium. Hence, given that stability is provided, the remaining degrees of freedom, namely
\mathbf{F}_{i},\mathbf{M},\bm{\phi}_{i},H_{i}
can be used to design equilibrium to coincide with the solution of an optimization program of interest. To formalize these ideas, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(Well posedness)
.

Let
H_{i},\mathbf{F}_{i},\mathbf{M},{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
be such that:

1.

The PHS in (
3
) has a unique solution for all
t\geq 0,\mathbf{x}_{i}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in\mathcal{I}
.

2.

There exist unique constant vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{i}\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*})+\bm{\phi}(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}))+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{M}\left(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*})-\nabla H_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{*})\right)

(4)

To show convergence towards the unique equilibrium point satisfying (
4
), we write the joint dynamics of all agents incorporating (
3
) for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Let the joint storage function

H(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

the joint output
\mathbf{y}=[\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})]_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and the joint input
\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{y})=[\bm{\phi}_{i}(\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))]_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
with joint state
\mathbf{x}=[\mathbf{x}_{i}]_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
. Moreover, let
\mathbf{F}=\text{blockdiag}(\mathbf{F}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{F}_{\textsf{N}})
. Hence, (
3
) can be written in compact form as follows.

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{M})\nabla H(\mathbf{x})+\bm{\phi}(\nabla H(\mathbf{x}))

(5)

where
\mathbf{L}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{A}\mathds{1})-\mathbf{A}
is the standard Laplacian matrix of the graph
\mathcal{G}
associated with the
0-1
adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
.

One of the most important features of (
5
) inherited from the PHS structure is its passivity property concerning the input-output pair
(\bm{\phi}(\nabla H(\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y})
.

Proposition 1
.

The trajectories of (
5
) satisfy

\dot{H}(\mathbf{x})\leq\mathbf{y}^{\top}\bm{\phi}(\nabla H(\mathbf{x}))

Proof.

Take arbitrary
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\textsf{N}}m}
with
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Recall
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
by assumption. Define
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{E}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{v}
where
\mathbf{E}
is an incidence matrix of
\mathcal{G}
satisfying
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{M})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{M})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\mathbf{E}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})(\mathbf{I}\otimes\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{E}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}\otimes\mathbf{M})\mathbf{w}\leq 0

Therefore,
\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{M}
has symmetric part negative semi-definite. Hence, the proposition follows by taking
\dot{H}=\nabla H^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{x}}
along (
5
).
∎

As discussed in Section
2.2
, passivity can be used to construct a Lyapunov function, ensuring convergence to the equilibrium. This idea is formalized in the following.

Corollary 1
.

Let Assumption
2
and consider (
5
). Moreover, assume that
H(\mathbf{x})
is strongly convex. Finally, assume that
\bm{\phi}_{i}
satisfy the incremental passivity property:

(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\bm{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{u})-\bm{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{v}))\leq 0

(6)

for arbitrary
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. with equality only when
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
. Then, the unique equilibrium of (
5
) is globally asymptotically stable.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}
denote the equilibrium of (
5
). To simplify the notation, let
\nabla H^{*}=\nabla H(\mathbf{x}^{*})
.
Set

V_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})=H(\mathbf{x})-H(\mathbf{x}^{*})-\nabla H(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

(7)

as a Lyapunov function candidate. Note that
V_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})
satisfy the required conditions for this purpose. First,
V_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0
. Second, since
H
is strongly convex, then Lemma
1
in Appendix
A
is used to conclude that
V_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})
is always positive and radially unbounded. Denote with
\mathbf{S}=(\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{M})
. Then,

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}=\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)^{\top}(\dot{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{S}\nabla H^{*}-\bm{\phi}(\nabla H^{*}))

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)^{\top}(\mathbf{S}\nabla H+\bm{\phi}(\nabla H)-\mathbf{S}\nabla H^{*}-\bm{\phi}(\nabla H^{*}))

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{S}\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\nabla H-\nabla H^{*}\right)^{\top}(\bm{\phi}(\nabla H)-\bm{\phi}(\nabla H^{*}))\leq 0

where we used the fact that
\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}^{\top}\preceq\mathbf{0}
from the proof of Proposition
1
. Moreover, we used the incremental passivity property from (
6
) in the last step. Furtheremore, note that
\dot{V}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}=0
only when
\nabla H(\mathbf{x})=\nabla H(\mathbf{x}^{*})
, which occurs only at the equilibrium point. Henceforth,
\dot{V}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}<0
for all
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium.
∎

3.2
Matching the equilibrium with the solution of an optimization program

Given that convergence of the PHS system in (
3
) is ensured by Proposition
1
, it only remains to match the equilibrium equations (
4
) with the solution of the consensus optimization program in (
1
). This can be performed by an appropriate design of the degrees of freedom we have in
\mathbf{F}_{i},\mathbf{M},\bm{\phi}_{i},H_{i}
. To provide intuition, we start with the centralized setting.

Example 1

(Centralized optimization)
.

Consider (
1
) with
{\textsf{N}}=1
and
f:=f_{1}
,
\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{x}_{1}
. For an equilibrium
\mathbf{x}^{*}
of (
5
) to coincide with
\bm{\theta^{*}}
, the first order optimality constraints must be met, namely,

\mathbf{0}=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
.
Comparing with the equilibrium conditions (
4
), this condition can be achieved with either one of the following choices:

a)

H_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}/2
,
\mathbf{F}_{1}=\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
.

b)

H_{1}(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{F}_{1}=-\mathbf{I}
,
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
.

In both cases, the same system results from (
3
), which coincides with the standard gradient flow,

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}).

(8)

Note that strong convexity of
f
imply that the incremental passivity property (
6
) is satisfied for
\mathbf{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
due to Proposition
2
in Appendix
A
. Thus, convergence of trajectories of the standard gradient flow (
8
) towards
\mathbf{x}^{*}
follows from Corollary
1
.

Now, we provide an example aiming to solve (
1
).

Example 2

(Unconstrained distributed optimization)
.

Consider (
1
) with arbitrary
{\textsf{N}}\in\mathbb{N}
and connected
\mathcal{G}
. The first order optimimality conditions for the global optimum of the program (
1
) are
\mathbf{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\nabla f_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
. However, this condition can be expressed in a more appropriate distributed fashion as:

\mathbf{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i}^{*}),\ \ \mathbf{q}_{1}^{*}=\dots=\mathbf{q}_{\textsf{N}}^{*}

(9)

where we introduce auxiliary variables
\mathbf{q}_{i}^{*}=\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. These include a condition for the gradients and a condition for consensus. Setting
\mathbf{q}^{*}=[\mathbf{q}_{i}^{*}]_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, the conditions in (
9
) at the equilibrium can be written as:

\mathbf{0}=(\mathds{1}^{\top}_{\textsf{N}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\nabla f(\mathbf{q}^{*}),\ \ \mathbf{0}=(\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{q}^{*}

(10)

which implies
\mathbf{q}^{*}=(\mathds{1}_{\textsf{N}}\otimes\bm{\theta}^{*})
and with

. Having two sets of constraints motivates the addition of a second set of auxiliary variables
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and partition
\mathbf{x}_{i}=[\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2m
. The PHS equilibrium equations in (
4
) for each partition will be used to match either one of the two conditions (
10
). This can be performed by setting

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{i}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i})\\
\mathbf{0}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m}

Henceforth, from (
4
), the equilibrium for the partitioned states can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{q}^{*}-(\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{p}^{*}-\nabla{f}(\mathbf{q}^{*})

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{q}^{*}

from which the optimally conditions in (
10
) follow by multiplying (
12
) with
(\mathds{1}^{\top}_{\textsf{N}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{m})
from the left and using
\mathds{1}^{\top}_{\textsf{N}}\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{0}
. The resulting system at each agent from (
3
) is

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{i})-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{i})-\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i})

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{i})

Remark 1
.

Note that in all previous examples, global convergence is ensured by Corollary
1
. The only condition to be verified is existence and uniqueness of solutions required in Assumption
2
. Fortunately, this is easily verified, since Assumption
1
for the functions
f_{i}
ensure the Lipschitz property of the right-hand sides of (
8
) and (
13

PHS-based optimization in discrete time

4.1
Discrete gradient-based PHS discretization

The continuous time system in (
3
) can be implemented in discrete time by taking advantage of the PHS structure one more time. For instance, we make the following changes:

\displaystyle i)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\to\frac{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\tau}

(14)

\displaystyle ii)

\displaystyle\nabla H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\to\overline{\nabla}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+})

where we denote with
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}[k]
at an arbitrary time step

and
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}=\mathbf{x}_{i}[k+1]
the updated state at the next time step, and the object
\overline{\nabla}H_{i}
is defined later. Note that the change (

i)
corresponds to the finite difference approximation of the derivative
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
between two time instants

and

In order to circumvent any constraint to

)-

denotes the discrete gradient defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

[

]

Let

be a scalar differentiable function. Hence,
\overline{\nabla}g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}g(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})=g(\mathbf{u})-g(\mathbf{v})

(15)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{u}}\overline{\nabla}g(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\nabla g(\mathbf{u})

While there are many possible ways to construct a discrete gradient, in this work, we consider

\overline{\nabla}g(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla g((1-s)\mathbf{u}+s\mathbf{v})\text{d}s

which for the case of quadratic functions

\overline{\nabla}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+})=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}}{2}

With these changes, the discrete time equivalent of the PHS (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{i}\overline{\nabla}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+})+\bm{\phi}_{i}(\overline{\nabla}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}))

(16)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{M}\left(\overline{\nabla}H_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+})-\overline{\nabla}H_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{+})\right)

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}

which, similar to

\bm{\phi}_{i},\overline{\nabla}H_{i}
Lipschitz:

Assumption 3

4.2
The MID method: fully-distributed PHS in discrete time

In the following, we show a simple modification of (
16

Aiming to obtain a solution of the program in (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{M}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}}{2}-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{j}+\mathbf{x}_{j}^{+}}{2}\right)+\bm{\phi}_{i}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\tau}

\mathbf{x}_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{q}_{i}^{+}-\mathbf{q}_{i}}{\tau}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}^{+}-\mathbf{p}_{i}}{\tau}

6
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a novel distributed optimization approach that achieves parameter-free convergence, a significant departure from traditional methods reliant on precise parameter selection, such as the learning rate. Using the Mixed Implicit Discretization (MID) of a continuous-time port-Hamiltonian system, we demonstrated how this system-based strategy enhances convergence speeds without worrying about the relationship between parameters and stability. Our method leveraged the inherent structure of the port-Hamiltonian system to ensure convergence by aligning the system’s equilibrium with the optimization problem’s solution, even when transitioning from continuous to discrete time. Through the MID scheme, we enabled local resolution of nonlinear implicit equations by network agents, formally proving the algorithm’s parameter-free convergence capability. Extensive numerical analyses highlighted our approach’s superiority in scenarios where traditional methods fail due to parameter constraints, thereby significantly accelerating convergence speeds.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}/2

replacing these expressions in (

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}/\tau+{\mathbf{Q}}&{\mathbf{Q}}\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{p}(\tau)=\begin{bmatrix}-\bm{\Lambda}(\tau)^{-1}(\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{I}+{\mathbf{Q}})\mathbf{L}_{m}&-\frac{1}{\tau}\bm{\Lambda}(\tau)^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{m}\\
