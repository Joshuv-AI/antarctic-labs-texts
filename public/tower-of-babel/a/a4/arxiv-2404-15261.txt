Resistance Distance and Linearized Optimal Transport on Graphs
Sawyer Jack Robertson, Zhengchao Wan, Alexander Cloninger

Abstract
We study the linearization of a discrete transportation distance between probability distributions on finite weighted graphs originally due to Maas (“Gradient flows of the entropy for finite Markov chains,” J. Funct. Anal. 261(8), 2011) which demonstrates various connections to the underlying combinatorial structure of the graph. For a connected graph and a reference density μ \mu on its vertices, our main result is a nonasymptotic local linearization theorem showing that if ν \nu is a small additive perturbation of μ \mu then their squared discrete transportation distance is controlled above and below by the quadratic form of the pseudoinverse of a re-weighted graph Laplacian matrix. When the reference measure is stationary for the simple random walk on the graph, the weights agree with the original graph and this yields the quadratic form ( μ − ν ) ⊤ ​ L w † ​ ( μ − ν ) (\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu) , which can be viewed as a form of resistance distance between probability measures. This distance has a number of combinatorial and variational characterizations, including Beckmann and Benamou–Brenier formulas, a dual homogeneous Sobolev norm formula, a spanning 2 2 -forest formula, and a representation through random walk hitting times. Finally, we show that on the resulting “resistance manifold,” the gradient flow of the χ 2 \chi^{2} functional is the continuous-time random walk and that its geodesic strong convexity modulus equals the spectral gap of the normalized Laplacian. From this geometric vantage point, one recovers the classical fact that the spectral gap of the normalized Laplacian controls the exponential convergence rate of the random walk to stationarity.

Introduction

Recently many researchers have investigated
linearization
as a means to circumvent computational bottlenecks associated with optimal transportation problems in large-scale data science applications. A number of methods have been developed along this line, generally according to the following rough template: on a metric space
\mathcal{X}
, equip a suitable class of probability measures
\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})
with a transportation metric
\mathcal{T}
so that
(\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{T})
has a (formal) Riemannian structure, then linearize
\mathcal{T}
at a
reference measure

\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})
by identifying the Riemannian inner product on the tangent space at
\mu
and using its induced norm distance as a surrogate. The quality of this linearization is usually governed by local geometry (e.g., curvature) of
(\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{T})
near
\mu
.

As an example, consider the
Otto calculus

[
54
]
(the nonasymptotic bounds given below were proved in
[
57
]
; see also
[
59
, Sec. 5.5.2]
). Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded convex domain, let
dx
denote the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
, and let
h\in L^{1}(\Omega)
satisfy
\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,dx=0
. For
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small, assume the perturbed measure
dx+\varepsilon h(x)\,dx
has density bounded from above and below, respectively, by constants
0<a<b<\infty
, i.e.,

\displaystyle a\leq 1+\varepsilon h(x)\leq b\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

Then the
2
-Wasserstein distance, denoted
\mathcal{W}_{2}
, satisfies

\displaystyle b^{-1/2}\|\varepsilon h\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(dx,\,dx+\varepsilon h(x)\,dx)\leq a^{-1/2}\|\varepsilon h\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}.

(1)

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}
is the dual norm of the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
of mean-zero Lebesgue measurable functions with square-integrable weak gradients, equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx,\quad f\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(dx,\,dx+\varepsilon h(x)\,dx)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\varepsilon|^{2}\,\|h\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+o(|\varepsilon|^{2}).

(2)

In the linearization framework above,
Eq.

1
says that, after formally identifying tangent vectors at
dx
with mean-zero perturbations
h
, the Riemannian metric tensor of
\mathcal{W}_{2}
at
dx
is the
\dot{H}^{-1}
inner product. Hence, for small
|\varepsilon|
,
\mathcal{W}_{2}(dx,\,dx+\varepsilon h\,dx)
is approximated by
|\varepsilon|\,\|h\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}
.

We develop an analogous program on weighted graphs, establishing connections between a discrete transportation metric, its linearization, and various combinatorial properties of the underlying graph. We consider graphs of the form
G=(V,E,w)
, where
V=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{n}\}
is a finite set of vertices,
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
is a collection of
m\geq 1
undirected edges, and
w=(w_{xy})_{\{x,y\}\in E}
is an assignment of positive edge weights. We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{V}
the linear space of functions from
V
to
\mathbb{R}
identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and by
\mathscr{P}(V)
the simplex of probability density functions on
V
.

In our setting, the aforementioned linearization template is difficult to reconcile, mostly because of the metric choice. For distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, linearization approaches are often designed around the Monge formulation (see
1.1
Related work
), but on discrete spaces, Monge transport is generally ill-posed. Its relaxation, the
p
-Wasserstein metric (see
Definition

3.3
), still exhibits geometric degeneracies: optimal couplings are typically non-unique, so the map
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is only piecewise smooth on the simplex, with kinks when the combinatorial type of an optimal plan changes. Geodesic behavior is also problematic. While it is true that
\mathcal{W}_{1}
geodesics always exist for Polish metric spaces
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
(see also
[
36
]
),
\mathcal{W}_{p}
geodesics do not exist in general for
p>1
, in particular, Maas
[
46
]
showed that even on a two-vertex graph,
\mathcal{W}_{2}
may fail to admit unit-speed geodesics between given endpoint measures.

Motivated by these geometric considerations, and inspired by the well known Benamou–Brenier formula for
\mathcal{W}_{2}
(see
[
7
]
), Maas
[
46
]
introduced a “Wasserstein-like” transportation metric under which the interior of the probability simplex demonstrates a number of desirable geometric properties. We recall its definition after introducing some preliminary notation.

We denote by
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
the interior of
\mathscr{P}(V)
(i.e., the set of everywhere positive densities). We denote by
A_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
the (weighted)
adjacency matrix
of
G
, and for a vertex
x\in V
, we define its
degree

\mathrm{d}_{x}
to be the sum of the weights of edges incident to
x
, i.e.,
\mathrm{d}_{x}:=\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}
. The
degree matrix

D_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is the diagonal matrix of vertex degrees, and the
Laplacian matrix

L_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is defined via the formula
L_{w}:=D_{w}-A_{w}
. Recall that the
simple random walk on
V
is the Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with transition probability matrix
P=D_{w}^{-1}A_{w}
. We recall that if
G
is connected then
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible and has a unique stationary distribution
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(V)
, and if
G
is additionally not bipartite, then
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is also aperiodic and hence ergodic.

For scalars
a,b\geq 0
, we define their
logarithmic mean

\theta(a,b)
via the formula
\theta(a,b)=\int_{0}^{1}a^{1-\lambda}b^{\lambda}d\lambda
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\,\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V))
denote the set of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
functions taking values in
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, and let
\mathcal{B}([0,1];\,\mathbb{R}^{V})
denote the set of Borel measurable functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}^{V}
.

Definition 1.1

(Discrete transportation distance
[
46
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
denote the corresponding stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
. For any
(\psi_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1];\,\mathbb{R}^{V})
and any
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\,\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V))
, define the
action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}w_{xy}\,(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y))^{2}\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)\,dt.

(3)

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, their
discrete transportation distance

\mathcal{M}(\mu,\nu)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\nu)^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigl\{\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))\;:\;(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\,\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)),\;(\psi_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1];\,\mathbb{R}^{V}),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)+\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\,(\psi_{t}(y)-\psi_{t}(x))\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\rho_{0}=\mu,\ \rho_{1}=\nu\Bigr\}.

(4)

Regarding
Definition

1.1
, we remark that in
[
46
]
the author considered a broader class of means
\theta(\cdot,\cdot)
; we specialize to the logarithmic mean for simplicity and concreteness. Our conventions for density and action functional normalizations also differ slightly: the former amounts to a diagonal change of variables, and the latter is absorbed into constants as needed.

Maas
[
46
, Theorem 1.1]
proves that
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, and moreover that
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),\mathcal{M})
defines a Riemannian manifold. Additionally, Maas shows that the gradient flow of the entropy functional on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
with respect to the Riemannian metric tensor induced by
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
coincides with the associated simple random walk diffusion. To our knowledge, however, no work has yet investigated the properties of linearized
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
or the geometry of the resulting flat metric.

Our main contribution.

Our main result, which can be viewed as an analogue of
Eq.

1
, asserts that near a fixed reference measure
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, the local geometry of the discrete transportation distance is governed by a Laplacian associated with a
\mu
-dependent reweighting of the graph.

For a matrix
X
, we denote by
X^{\dagger}
its Moore–Penrose inverse. We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{V}
consisting of mean zero functions.

Theorem 1.2

(Linearization of discrete transportation distance near a fixed measure)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
denote the stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
. Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
be a fixed reference measure and define
w^{(\mu)}=(w^{(\mu)}_{xy})_{\{x,y\}\in E}
according to the formula

\displaystyle w^{(\mu)}_{xy}:=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad\{x,y\}\in E,

with associated Laplacian matrix
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
be fixed with
\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
. Then there exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
G
and
\mu
, such that for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small (depending only on
G
and
\mu
), it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h\leq\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-C|\varepsilon|}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\varepsilon|^{2}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h+o(|\varepsilon|^{2}).

We prove
Theorem

1.2
as
Theorem

2.1
in
Section

2
. It is not difficult to obtain an asymptotic version of
Theorem

1.2
using known facts about
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
and its metric tensor, indeed, results reminiscent of this are already known in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
44
]
; we also discuss this in greater detail at the beginning of
Section

2
). Thus our contribution is primarily the development of a
nonasymptotic
version with explicit constants, which is arguably more suitable for applications. We state below a straightforward corollary in the simplified case of a stationary reference point.

Corollary 1.3

(Stationary reference measure)
.

Consider the setting described in
Theorem

1.2
. If
\mu=\pi
, then
w^{(\pi)}=w
and hence
L_{w^{(\pi)}}=L_{w}
. In this case,
Theorem

1.2
specializes to the stationary linearization

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi,\pi+\varepsilon h)^{2}=|\varepsilon|^{2}\,h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h+o(|\varepsilon|^{2}),

(5)

with constants depending only on
G
.

We remark that the leading-order term in
Eq.

5
can be considered a graph analogue of the dual homogeneous Sobolev norm, in which case
Eq.

5
parallels the continuum
\dot{H}^{-1}
linearization in
Eq.

2
(see
Theorem

3.6
and the surrounding section for a more detailed discussion of this viewpoint).

At a high level,
Theorem

1.2
asserts that, in a neighborhood of a reference point
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, the tangent space to
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
and that locally the metric tensor is given by the Hilbertian inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle:=f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}g,\quad f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

(6)

In particular, in the stationary case
\mu=\pi
one has
w^{(\pi)}=w
and hence
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{w^{(\pi)}}^{\dagger}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{w}^{\dagger}}
. The induced norm distance
\|f-g\|_{L_{w}^{\dagger}}^{2}=(f-g)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(f-g)
is actually well known when evaluated on Dirac masses: for
x,y\in V
, their
effective resistance
, or simply resistance distance, is given by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y}).

(7)

Here and throughout the paper,
\mathbf{1}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
denotes the indicator vector of the vertex
x\in V
, while
\mathbf{1}
denotes the all ones vector. Thus
Eq.

6
can be seen as a straightforward extension of resistance distance from
vertices
to general probability densities. An extension of this sort appears to have been first mentioned in passing in
[
37
]
but to the best of these authors’ knowledge has not attracted deeper scrutiny in the literature.

Definition 1.4

(Resistance distance between measures)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph. For
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu):=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu).

We remark that in
Definition

1.4
, the term “distance” is used with slight abuse. It is well known that both
(x,y)\mapsto\mathcal{R}(x,y)
and
(x,y)\mapsto\mathcal{R}(x,y)^{1/2}
define metrics on
V
. However,
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
fails to satisfy the triangle inequality in general, and thus
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
defines only a semimetric on
\mathscr{P}(V)
(it is nonnegative, symmetric, and vanishes if and only if
\mu=\nu
). We remark that the weaker bound
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\mathcal{R}(\mu,\tau)+2\mathcal{R}(\tau,\nu)
does hold for
\mu,\nu,\tau\in\mathscr{P}(V)
and that, as in the vertex case,
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)^{1/2}
defines a true metric on
\mathscr{P}(V)
.

We thusly investigate what we term the
resistance manifold
, which is the metric space
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
, where
d_{\mathcal{R}}(\mu,\nu):=\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)^{1/2}
for each
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
.

Our second contribution.

Upon viewing
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
as a flat Riemannian manifold, our second main contribution is to characterize the resulting geodesic distance in a number of ways. Notably, effective resistance is related to a number of
combinatorial
properties of the underlying graph, including the spanning trees and
2
-forests of
G

[
11
,
6
]
, the eigenvalues of
L_{w}
and its normalized counterpart
[
13
]
, and hitting times associated with the simple random walk on
V

[
64
,
45
]
. Thus the geodesic distance
d_{\mathcal{R}}
can be viewed through this lens as well.

We define the
volume
of
G
as the sum of its vertex degrees:
\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)=\sum_{x\in V}\mathrm{d}_{x}
. If
F\subseteq E
, we define the weight of
F
, denoted
w_{F}
, by

\displaystyle w_{F}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{e\in F}w_{e}.

Theorem 1.5

(Characterizations of resistance distance between measures)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then we have the following characterizations of
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
:

1.

(Beckmann formula, see
Theorem

3.4
)
Let
E^{\prime}
denote an orientation of
E
, and let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E^{\prime}}
denote the corresponding oriented incidence matrix. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\sum_{e=(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\;:\;J\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}},\,BJ=\mu-\nu\right\}.

2.

(Benamou–Brenier formula, see
Theorem

3.5
)
Let
E^{\prime}
denote an orientation of
E
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{V})\\
J_{t}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}})\end{subarray}}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J_{t}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,dt\,:\,\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}+BJ_{t}=0,\quad\rho_{0}=\mu,\quad\rho_{1}=\nu\right\}.

3.

(Negative homogeneous Sobolev distance, see
Theorem

3.6
)

\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)}^{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)}
is the norm dual to

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}w_{xy}|f(x)-f(y)|^{2},\quad f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

4.

(Spanning
2
-forest formula, see
Theorem

3.13
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}\,(\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1}))^{2},

(8)

where
\mathcal{T}(G)
denotes the sum of all weights
w_{T}
of spanning trees
T
of
G
, and the sum on the right-hand side of
Eq.

8
runs over the set of spanning
2
-forests
F
of
G
, denoted
\mathcal{F}(G)
, with components
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

5.

(Hitting time formula, see
Theorem

3.10
)
For
x,y\in V
, let
\mathrm{H}(x,y)
denote the expected number of steps for the simple random walk on
V
to reach
y
having started at
x
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))(\mu(y)-\nu(y))\mathrm{H}(x,y).

Theorem

1.5
is proved in
Section

3
.

We explore more connections between the combinatorial structure of the underlying graph
G
, the discrete transportation metric
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and its linearization by considering the concrete setting of bipartite graphs in
Subsection

2.3
. In this environment we can provide quantitative descriptions of both
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
for a one-parameter family of perturbations of the stationary distribution of the simple random walk.

Our third contribution.

We further investigate the geometry of the resistance manifold through the lens of gradient flows. Inspired by Maas’ result that entropy gradient flow on the probability simplex recovers the diffusion generated by the underlying random walk, we show that the
\chi^{2}
functional on the flat manifold
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
has gradient flow given by the continuous time random walk. Relatedly, we are able to control the relaxation rate of this gradient flow by the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian matrix of
G
, thereby recovering a classical result from spectral graph theory. Taken together, these observations constitute our third contribution, stated in full below.

If
G
is connected and
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(V)
denotes the stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
, then we define the (normalized)
\chi^{2}
functional
on
\mathscr{P}(V)
according to the formula

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}\frac{(\mu(x)-\pi(x))^{2}}{\pi(x)},\quad\mu\in\mathscr{P}(V).

(9)

Theorem 1.6

(Gradient flows of the
\chi^{2}
functional)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(V)
denote the stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
. Then, on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, the gradient flow of
\chi^{2}
with respect to the Riemannian metric tensor induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{w}^{\dagger}}
coincides with the continuous time evolution of the random walk.

Theorem

1.6
is proved in
Section

4
as
Corollary

4.3
.

To state our final result, we recall the notion of geodesic strong convexity. If
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a Riemannian manifold, a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functional
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
geodesic
\lambda
-strongly convex
if, for every pair
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
and every constant-speed geodesic
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
joining them, one has:

\displaystyle F(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)F(\rho_{0})+tF(\rho_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{2}t(1-t)d(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}

(10)

for each
0\leq t\leq 1
. The greatest such
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for which
Eq.

10
holds is known as the
geodesic strong convexity modulus
of the functional
F
.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{w}=D_{w}^{-1/2}L_{w}D_{w}^{-1/2}
the
normalized Laplacian matrix
of
G
, and recall that
\mathcal{L}_{w}
is symmetric positive semidefinite with nullspace
\mathbb{R}D_{w}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
. The least strictly positive eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{w}
is denoted
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
and is called the
spectral gap
of
\mathcal{L}_{w}
.

Theorem 1.7

(Geodesic strong convexity of the
\chi^{2}
functional)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi
denote the stationary distribution of the associated simple random walk on
V
. On the Riemannian manifold
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
, the functional
\chi^{2}
has geodesic strong convexity modulus equal to the spectral gap
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
of
\mathcal{L}_{w}
.

Theorem

1.7
is proved in
Section

4
as
Theorem

4.5
.

We remark that geodesic
\lambda
-strong convexity yields exponential relaxation of the associated gradient flow, with
d(\rho_{t},\rho_{\star})\lesssim e^{-\lambda t}
and
|F(\rho_{t})-F(\rho_{\star})|\lesssim e^{-2\lambda t}
for a minimizer
\rho_{\star}
(see, e.g.,
[
2
, Sec. 11.2]
). In our setting,
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.7
therefore recover the classical result concerning
\chi^{2}
-relaxation of the random walk diffusion process at a rate governed by the spectral gap
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
(see, e.g.,
[
18
]
).

From a computational perspective, our results suggest that the discrete transportation metric may be well approximated in certain regimes by the surrogate
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
whose computation amounts to basic linear algebra and which is substantially more tractable than solving the full dynamic transport problem. While we do not develop algorithms directly here, our results develop a geometric framework that naturally supports scalable approximations, local embeddings, and various numerical schemes for graph transport. We feel these topics comprise promising directions for future investigation.

Related work

Research concerning optimal transport on graphs can be modeled as having developed along two related but distinct directions. One such thread considers classical Wasserstein transportation problems using the ambient graph metric, and includes topics such as explicit formulas on trees, algorithmic approximations and regularization, and scalable computation on mesh discretizations
[

,
47
,
61
,
29
]
. Other directions include Sobolev transport on graphs

2.1
Linearization of the transportation metric in asymptotic terms

Letting
G=(V,E,w)
be a fixed connected graph, we recall the definition of the probability simplex on
V
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(V)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{V}\,:\,\mu(x)\geq 0\text{ for each }x\in V,\,\sum_{x\in V}\mu(x)=1\right\}.

Once again we write
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
to denote the interior of
\mathscr{P}(V)
. Letting
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
be a fixed reference measure, it is straightforward to see that the embedded tangent space to
\mathscr{P}(V)
at
\mu
is given by a copy of the hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. On the other hand, Maas
[
46
]
shows that if
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, then by defining the edge weights

\displaystyle w^{(\mu)}_{xy}:=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad\{x,y\}\in E,

and the associated Laplacian matrix
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
, then the intrinsic tangent space to
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),\mathcal{M})
at
\mu
can be identified with the set of discrete gradients

\displaystyle\{\nabla\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}\,:\,\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{V},\,\nabla\psi(x,y)=\psi(y)-\psi(x)\}.

It is straightforward to verify that the map
\nabla\psi\mapsto L_{w^{(\mu)}}\psi
defines a linear isomorphism from the intrinsic tangent space of
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),\mathcal{M})
at
\mu
to the embedded tangent space
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Moreover, since the Riemannian metric on
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),\mathcal{M})
at
\mu
can be expressed in terms of
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
via the formula

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\psi,\nabla\phi\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\psi^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}\phi,

it follows that the linearization of the discrete transportation distance at
\mu
is given by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
as defined in
Eq.

6
. Therefore if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{2}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon);\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V))
is any curve satisfying
\rho_{0}=\mu
and
\frac{d}{dt}\rho|_{t=0}=:h
, then since
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}(\mu,\rho_{t})^{2}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
in a neighborhood of
t=0
(see, e.g.,
[
42
, Lemma 6.8]
), it follows from Taylor’s theorem that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\rho_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=t^{2}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h+o(t^{2}),\quad t\to 0.

(11)

2.2
Linearization of the transportation metric in nonasymptotic terms

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
denote the stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
, and let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
be fixed. Define edge weights
w^{(\mu)}=(w^{(\mu)}_{xy})_{\{x,y\}\in E}
by

\displaystyle w^{(\mu)}_{xy}:=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad\{x,y\}\in E,

and let
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
be the corresponding Laplacian. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
be fixed with
\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
. Let
\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})
denote the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
, and define constants
0<C\leq C^{\prime}
via

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=\left(\min_{x\in V}\mu(x)\right)^{-1},\quad C^{\prime}:=C\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}}.

(12)

Then for any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small (depending only on
G
and
\mu
), it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h\leq\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-C|\varepsilon|}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h.

(13)

In particular,
\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}=|\varepsilon|^{2}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h+o(|\varepsilon|^{2})
.

Proof.

First we will exhibit an admissible pair
(\rho_{t}),(\psi_{t})
as in
4
to obtain the upper bound in
Eq.

13
. To this end let

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=t\,(\mu+\varepsilon h)+(1-t)\,\mu,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Then if
|\varepsilon|
is chosen to be small enough,
(\rho_{t})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\,\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V))
. Clearly
\rho_{0}=\mu,\,\rho_{1}=\mu+\varepsilon h
. The flux constraint in
4
requires of
\psi_{t}
that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon h(x)=-\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\,(\psi_{t}(y)-\psi_{t}(x))\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad x\in V,\,0\leq t\leq 1.

(14)

Write

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}^{(t)}_{xy}

\displaystyle=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad\{x,y\}\in E,

so that
Eq.

14
becomes

\displaystyle\varepsilon\,h

\displaystyle=L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

(15)

Note that since
\rho_{t}\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
holds for all
t
and since
\theta(a,b)>0
whenever
a,b>0
, the weights
\widetilde{w}^{(t)}_{xy}
remain strictly positive for all
0\leq t\leq 1
. In particular, the operator
L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}
remains the Laplacian of a connected weighted graph and is therefore positive definite on the subspace
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
which includes
h
. We may therefore pick, for each
0\leq t\leq 1
, a unique representative
\psi_{t}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
satisfying
Eq.

15
and given by

\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}h.

Then, using
L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}=L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}
, the action functional evaluates to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}^{\ast}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\psi_{t}^{\ast})^{\top}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t}^{\ast}\,dt=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{1}h^{\top}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}h\,dt

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\,h^{\top}\left(\int_{0}^{1}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}\,dt\right)h.

(16)

To bound
16
from above we first observe that, for each
x\in V
and
0\leq t\leq 1
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(x)+t\varepsilon h(x)}{\pi(x)}=\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}+t\varepsilon\frac{h(x)}{\pi(x)}.

Using
|h(x)|\leq\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|t\varepsilon\frac{h(x)}{\pi(x)}\,\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\varepsilon|C,

where
C
is taken as in
Eq.

12
. Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\left(1-|\varepsilon|C\right)\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}\leq\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\leq\left(1+|\varepsilon|C\right)\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\quad x\in V,\,0\leq t\leq 1.

(17)

Since
\theta
is increasing in each argument and homogeneous of degree one,
Eq.

17
yields

\displaystyle\left(1-|\varepsilon|C\right)\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)\leq\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)\leq\left(1+|\varepsilon|C\right)\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),

and therefore, for each
\{x,y\}\in E
and
0\leq t\leq 1
, it holds

\displaystyle(1-|\varepsilon|C)\,w^{(\mu)}_{xy}\leq\widetilde{w}^{(t)}_{xy}\leq(1+|\varepsilon|C)\,w^{(\mu)}_{xy}.

Now let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
be arbitrary. It follows that

\displaystyle(1-|\varepsilon|C)\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}f

\displaystyle\leq f^{\top}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}f\leq(1+|\varepsilon|C)\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}f,

and in particular this bound holds for
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Since
L_{w^{(\mu)}}
and
L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}
are positive definite on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
, the inverse inequality holds for their Moore–Penrose inverses:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+|\varepsilon|C}\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}f

\displaystyle\leq f^{\top}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}f\leq\frac{1}{1-|\varepsilon|C}\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}f,\quad f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

(18)

Since
Eq.

18
holds for all
0\leq t\leq 1
, we may integrate all sides to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+|\varepsilon|C}\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}f

\displaystyle\leq f^{\top}\left(\int_{0}^{1}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}\,dt\right)f\leq\frac{1}{1-|\varepsilon|C}\,f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}f,

thus, with
f=h
and via
16
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}^{\ast}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\,h^{\top}\left(\int_{0}^{1}L_{\widetilde{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}\,dt\right)h\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-|\varepsilon|C}h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h.

(19)

Next we prove the lower bound. By
Eq.

19
and taking
|\varepsilon|C\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon\,h)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h

Since
\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
, we have
h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h\leq\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})^{-1}\|h\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})^{-1}
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon\,h)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}\varepsilon^{2}.

Now let
\eta>0
be fixed, and let
(\rho_{t}),(\psi_{t})
be any admissible pair of curves satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}+\eta\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}\varepsilon^{2}+\eta.

We emphasize that
(\rho_{t}),(\psi_{t})
depend on the choice of
\eta
, but we suppress this in the notation. Then for any
x\in V
and
0\leq t\leq 1
, since
(\rho_{t})
is piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, one has that

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x)-\rho_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\rho_{s}(x)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)\,ds,

where
L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}
is the Laplacian with edge weights

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{s}(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\frac{\rho_{s}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with
\rho_{0}=\mu
,

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}(x)-\mu(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{t}L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)\,ds\right|^{2}\leq t\int_{0}^{t}|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{1}|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}\,ds.

(20)

Let
x\in V
be fixed. Then, once again by Cauchy–Schwarz and the definition of
\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}
, we have

\displaystyle|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}(\psi_{s}(y)-\psi_{s}(x))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}\right)\left(\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}(\psi_{s}(y)-\psi_{s}(x))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{s}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{s}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)\right)\left(\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}(\psi_{s}(y)-\psi_{s}(x))^{2}\right).

Define
r_{z}:=\rho_{s}(z)/\pi(z)
for
z\in V
. Using the bound
\theta(a,b)\leq(a+b)/2
for
a,b\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\,\theta(r_{x},r_{y})\leq\frac{1}{2}r_{x}\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}r_{y}.

Since
\pi(x)=\mathrm{d}_{x}/\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)
, the first term satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}r_{x}\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}=\frac{1}{2}r_{x}\mathrm{d}_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\rho_{s}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G).

For the second term, using
w_{xy}\leq\mathrm{d}_{y}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}r_{y}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{w_{xy}}{\mathrm{d}_{y}}\rho_{s}(y)\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\sum_{y\in V}\rho_{s}(y)=\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G).

Therefore, for each
x\in V
and
0\leq s\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G).

Plugging this into the bound for
|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{xy}(\psi_{s}(y)-\psi_{s}(x))^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\widehat{w}^{(s)}_{uv}(\psi_{s}(u)-\psi_{s}(v))^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t})).

(21)

Recalling
Eq.

20
and using
21
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\rho_{t}(x)-\mu(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|L_{\widehat{w}^{(s)}}\psi_{s}(x)|^{2}\,ds\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}\varepsilon^{2}+\eta\right),

and in particular it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\mu(x)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{\mu(x)}\sqrt{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\left(\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}\varepsilon^{2}+\eta\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left(\min_{u\in V}\mu(u)\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}}\,|\varepsilon|+\sqrt{\eta}\right).

(22)

Equivalently, defining

\displaystyle C^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\left(\min_{u\in V}\mu(u)\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})}},

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}

\displaystyle:=\left(\min_{u\in V}\mu(u)\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sqrt{\eta},

we may write
22
in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\mu(x)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}.

Thus, for each
\{x,y\}\in E
, we have

\displaystyle\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\mu(x)},\,\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\mu(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\theta\left(\left(1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}\right)\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\left(1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}\right)\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}\right)\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\,\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),

where the last step uses the fact that
\theta
is 1-homogeneous. We thus have the bound, for each
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
and
0\leq t\leq 1
,

\displaystyle f^{\top}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}f

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}\right)f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}f,

and inversely so for
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
:

\displaystyle f^{\top}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}f

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}f^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}f.

(23)

Let
0\leq t\leq 1
be fixed. The flux constraint requires that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t},

and we may freely assume that
\psi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
since adding a constant to
\psi_{t}
does not change the action or violate the flux constraint. Thus, since
L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}
is positive definite on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
,
\psi_{t}
is uniquely determined via

\displaystyle\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}\rho_{t}^{\prime},

(24)

where
\rho_{t}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}
for brevity. We then have that, since
L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}=L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}
and since
\rho_{t}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
for all
t
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{t}^{\top}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\rho_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{\top}L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}^{\dagger}\left(\rho_{t}^{\prime}\right)dt\geq\frac{1}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}\int_{0}^{1}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})dt,

where the last step follows from
Eq.

23
. Separately, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}^{\prime}\,dt\right)^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}^{\prime}\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}(L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger})^{1/2}\rho_{t}^{\prime}\,dt\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|(L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger})^{1/2}\rho_{t}^{\prime}\|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})\,dt,

so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}\int_{0}^{1}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}^{\prime}dt\right)^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}^{\prime}dt\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h.

Since
\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}\leq\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))\leq\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}+\eta
, the previous inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon\,h)^{2}+\eta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|+C^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}}h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h.

Letting
\eta\to 0
gives the desired lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon\,h)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+C^{\prime}|\varepsilon|}h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h,

and the theorem is proved.
∎

To conclude this subsection we briefly discuss the sharpness of the constants
C,C^{\prime}
appearing in the statement of
Theorem

2.1
. For the upper bound, our proof only uses the pointwise estimate

\displaystyle(1-|\varepsilon|C)\,\mu(x)\leq\rho_{t}(x)\leq(1+|\varepsilon|C)\,\mu(x),\qquad\rho_{t}:=\mu+t\varepsilon h,

which comes from the crude bound
|h(x)|\leq\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
. Thus, for a fixed direction
h
, one may replace

\displaystyle C=\left(\min_{x\in V}\mu(x)\right)^{-1}

by the sharper
direction dependent
quantity

\displaystyle C_{h}:=\Bigl\|\frac{h}{\mu}\Bigr\|_{\ell^{\infty}(V)}=\max_{x\in V}\frac{|h(x)|}{\mu(x)}.

In particular, if
h
is supported on vertices where
\mu
is relatively large, then the upper bound improves accordingly. On the other hand, if one wants a statement uniform over all
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
with
\|h\|_{2}\leq 1
, then some dependence on
(\min_{x}\mu(x))^{-1}
is unavoidable, since positivity of
\mu+\varepsilon h
already forces
|\varepsilon|
to be at most of order
\min_{x}\mu(x)
in the worst case.

Similarly, for the lower bound, our proof first inserts the coarse estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\mu,\mu+\varepsilon h)^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h\leq\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})},

and then converts this into a uniform control on the deviation of an almost minimizing curve away from
\mu
. If one keeps the factor
h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h
instead of bounding it by
\lambda_{2}(L_{w^{(\mu)}})^{-1}
, the same argument yields the sharper direction dependent constant

\displaystyle C_{h}^{\prime}:=\left(\min_{x\in V}\mu(x)\right)^{-1}\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\,h^{\top}L_{w^{(\mu)}}^{\dagger}h}.

Finally, the order of the error term can improve substantially for structured perturbations. The estimate in
Theorem

2.1
only gives a relative error of order
O(|\varepsilon|)
, because it tracks the first order variation of the metric tensor along an arbitrary affine path. In special cases this can be improved; this is the focus of the subsection which follows.

2.3
Example: Bipartite graphs

In general, the discrete transportation metric
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be difficult to compute and describe in explicit terms. This makes a quantitative assessment of the quality of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
as a surrogate for
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
difficult to assess in general. However, certain settings are more amenable to such an analysis, and the focus of this section is to describe one such example in detail.

Specifically, in the setting of a connected bipartite graph
G
, there exists a one parameter family of perturbations of the corresponding stationary distribution
\pi
for which exact quantitative guarantees on the linearization of
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
near
\pi
can be provided. Moreover, the resulting approximation is stronger than the general bound provided by
Theorem

2.1
, in the sense that
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
captures
\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot)^{2}
up to third order as the perturbation size tends to zero.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
denote a fixed connected bipartite graph with a corresponding bipartition
V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
. We define the
sign function

\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
according to

\displaystyle\zeta(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x\in V_{1}\\
-1&\text{ if }x\in V_{2}\end{cases},\quad x\in V.

We consider the following single parameter family of perturbations of
\pi
:

\displaystyle\pi^{(s)}

\displaystyle:=\pi\,\odot\,(1+s\zeta),\quad-1<s<1,

(25)

where
\odot
denotes componentwise multiplication of vectors. In plain terms,
\pi^{(s)}
describes a perturbation of the stationary distribution in which the mass on
V_{1}
is multiplied by a factor of
1+s
and the mass on
V_{2}
is multiplied by a factor of
1-s
. We illustrate this setup in
Figure

1
.

\pi
.

:

\pi^{(s)}=\pi\odot(1+s\zeta)
from

. In (a), node color opacity at vertex
x\in V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
is proportional to the stationary distribution
\pi(x)
, equivalently to degree since
\pi(x)=\mathrm{d}_{x}/\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)
. In (b) and (c), the same graph is shown with color opacity proportional to
\pi^{(3/4)}(x)
and

, respectively, illustrating how the perturbation shifts mass toward
V_{1}

.

Recall that at a fixed reference measure
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, the discrete transportation distance metric tensor is determined by the edge weights

\displaystyle w_{xy}^{(\mu)}=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\right),\quad\{x,y\}\in E.

In the case of
Eq.

25
, this reads

\displaystyle w_{xy}^{(\pi\odot(1+s\zeta))}=w_{xy}\,\theta(1+s,1-s)

for each
\{x,y\}\in E
. In other words, the weights underwriting the corresponding metric tensor change only by a global factor
\theta(1+s,1-s)
as
s
varies.

The main result of this section is given below.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected bipartite graph and let
\pi
denote the corresponding stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
. Let
\pi^{(s)}
be as in
Eq.

25
. Then for
-1<a<b<1
, as
(a,b)\to(0,0)
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})+\frac{(b-a)^{2}(a^{2}+ab+b^{2})}{18\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}+O((|a|+|b|)^{6}).

In particular, the resistance distance
\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})
captures
\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})^{2}
up to third order as
(a,b)\to(0,0)
.

Below we state and prove
Proposition

2.3
and
Proposition

2.4
, which compute, respectively,
\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})^{2}
and
\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})
in exact terms. With these in hand,
Theorem

2.2
follows from Taylor’s theorem and a short computation; we supply the details at the end of this section for completeness.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
G=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E,w)
be a connected bipartite graph. Let
\pi
denote the corresponding stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
and let
\pi^{(s)}
be as in
Eq.

25
. For brevity, if
-1<u<1
, write
\theta(u):=\theta(1+u,1-u)
. Let
-1<a<b<1
be fixed. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\,\right|.

(26)

Proof.

First we prove the upper bound in
Eq.

26
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\,\right|.

(27)

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];(-1,1))
satisfy
\varphi(0)=a
and
\varphi(1)=b
. Define, for
0\leq t\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{(\varphi(t))},\quad\psi_{t}:=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\theta(\varphi(t))}\zeta.

We claim that
(\rho_{t},\psi_{t})
is feasible for
\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})
as in
4
. Clearly
(\rho_{t})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V))
,
\rho_{0}=\pi^{(a)}
,
\rho_{1}=\pi^{(b)}
, and
(\psi_{t})\in\mathcal{B}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{V})
by construction. For the continuity equation, first observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\pi^{(\varphi(t))}=\varphi^{\prime}(t)\pi\,\odot\,\zeta.

In the notation of
Theorem

2.1
, for
-1<s<1
, the weights
w_{xy}^{\pi^{(s)}}=:w_{xy}^{(s)}
can be computed as

\displaystyle w_{xy}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=w_{xy}\,\theta\left(\frac{\pi^{(s)}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\pi^{(s)}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)=w_{xy}\,\theta(1+s,1-s),

for each
\{x,y\}\in E
. For brevity, write
\theta(s):=\theta(1+s,1-s)
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle L_{w^{(\varphi(t))}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\theta(\varphi(t))}L_{w^{(\varphi(t))}}\zeta.

(28)

We compute, for
x\in V_{1}
,

\displaystyle L_{w^{(\varphi(t))}}\zeta(x)

\displaystyle=\theta(\varphi(t))L_{w}\zeta(x)=\theta(\varphi(t))\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}(\zeta(x)-\zeta(y))=2\theta(\varphi(t))\mathrm{d}_{x}\zeta(x),

and similarly for
x\in V_{2}
. Thus
Eq.

28
becomes

\displaystyle L_{w^{(\varphi(t))}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\pi\odot\zeta=\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t},

as desired. We compute the action functional as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{t}^{\top}L_{w^{(\varphi(t))}}\psi_{t}\,dt=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}}{\theta(\varphi(t))}\zeta^{\top}(\pi\odot\zeta)\,dt

We observe that
\zeta^{\top}(\pi\odot\zeta)=1
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}}{\theta(\varphi(t))}\,dt.

It remains to choose
\varphi
judiciously. To this end, write

\displaystyle\Phi(s)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}},\quad-1<s<1.

Then
\Phi(s)
is strictly increasing in
s
and admits an inverse on its image. Put

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle:=\Phi^{-1}((1-t)\Phi(a)+t\Phi(b)),\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Then

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\Phi(\varphi(t))

\displaystyle=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a),

and separately,

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\Phi(\varphi(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{\theta(\varphi(t))}},

so that
\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{\theta(\varphi(t))}}=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)
for all
0\leq t\leq 1
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(t)^{2}}{\theta(\varphi(t))}\,dt=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,(\Phi(b)-\Phi(a))^{2}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\right|^{2}.

Thus
Eq.

27
is proved. Next we prove the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\,\right|.

(29)

To this end, let
((\rho_{t}),(\psi_{t}))
be a feasible pair for
\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})
in the sense of
4
. Define the function
\sigma(t)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])
via

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)\rho_{t}(x)\,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Then we have that, by feasibility of
\psi_{t}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t}(x),

where we define the edge weights

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{(t)}_{xy}

\displaystyle:=w_{xy}\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right).

We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)L_{\widehat{w}^{(t)}}\psi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V_{1}}\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y))-\sum_{x\in V_{2}}\sum_{y\sim x}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}2\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y)),

where
E^{\prime}
is the orientation of
E
consisting of edges directed from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\sigma(t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}(\psi_{t}(x)-\psi_{t}(y))^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}\right)

(30)

We estimate the rightmost factor as follows. First recall that
(s,t)\mapsto\theta(s,t)
is jointly concave in
s,t
on
(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
. Therefore by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\frac{w_{xy}}{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\theta\left(\frac{\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}w_{xy}\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}}{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)},\frac{\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}w_{xy}\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}}{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(2\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\frac{w_{xy}}{\mathrm{d}_{x}}\rho_{t}(x),2\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\frac{w_{xy}}{\mathrm{d}_{y}}\rho_{t}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(2\sum_{x\in V_{1}}\rho_{t}(x),2\sum_{y\in V_{2}}\rho_{t}(y)\right)=\theta(\sigma(t)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\widehat{w}_{xy}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{2}\,\theta(\sigma(t)).

Combining the preceding with
Eq.

30
and integrating, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}((\psi_{t}),(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\sigma^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}{\theta(\sigma(t))}dt.

Since
\Phi^{\prime}(s)=\theta(s)^{-1/2}
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\sigma(t))=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{\theta(\sigma(t))}}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\sigma^{\prime}(t)|^{2}}{\theta(\sigma(t))}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\sigma(t))\right|^{2}dt\geq\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\sigma(t))dt\right|^{2}=|\Phi(\sigma(1))-\Phi(\sigma(0))|^{2},

since
\Phi
is strictly increasing. Finally, since
\sigma(0)=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)\pi^{(a)}(x)=a
and
\sigma(1)=\sum_{x\in V}\zeta(x)\pi^{(b)}(x)=b
, we have

\displaystyle|\Phi(\sigma(1))-\Phi(\sigma(0))|^{2}=|\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)|^{2}=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\right|^{2}.

Taking the infimum over all feasible pairs
((\rho_{t}),(\psi_{t}))
yields the lower bound
Eq.

29
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
G=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E,w)
be a connected bipartite graph. Let
\pi
denote the corresponding stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
and let
\pi^{(s)}
be as in
Eq.

25
. Let
-1<a<b<1
be fixed. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle=\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}.

(31)

Proof.

Write

\displaystyle h:=\pi\odot\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{V}.

Since
\pi(V_{1})=\pi(V_{2})=\frac{1}{2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V}h(x)=\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\zeta(x)=\pi(V_{1})-\pi(V_{2})=0,

and hence
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. By definition of
\pi^{(s)}
,

\displaystyle\pi^{(a)}-\pi^{(b)}=(a-b)\,h.

(32)

As in the proof of
Proposition

2.3
, it holds

\displaystyle(L_{w}\zeta)(x)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\,h(x).

(33)

Using
Definitions

1.4
and
32
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle=(\pi^{(a)}-\pi^{(b)})^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\pi^{(a)}-\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle=(a-b)^{2}h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h.

It therefore remains to evaluate
h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h
. By
Eq.

33
,

\displaystyle h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}L_{w}\zeta.

Since
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
and
L_{w}L_{w}^{\dagger}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}L_{w}\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,h^{\top}\zeta=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}(\zeta\,\odot\,\pi)^{\top}\zeta=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})

\displaystyle=\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)},

as claimed.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

Once again we write
\theta(u):=\theta(1-u,1+u)
for
-1<u<1
. One computes the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\theta(u)=\theta(1+u,1-u)=\frac{u}{\operatorname{arctanh}(u)}=1-\frac{u^{2}}{3}-\frac{4u^{4}}{45}+O(u^{6}),

which is valid as
u\to 0
(in particular the Taylor series has an interval of convergence
-1<u<1
). By the binomial power series we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{u^{2}}{6}+\frac{31u^{4}}{360}+O(u^{6}).

Thus, by
Proposition

2.3
and by
Proposition

2.4
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\theta(u)}}\,\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\left|\int_{a}^{b}\left(1+\frac{u^{2}}{6}+\frac{31u^{4}}{360}+O(u^{6})\right)du\,\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\left((b-a)+\frac{b^{3}-a^{3}}{18}+O((|a|+|b|)^{5})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}+\frac{(b-a)^{2}(a^{2}+ab+b^{2})}{18\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}+O((|a|+|b|)^{6})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(\pi^{(a)},\pi^{(b)})+\frac{(b-a)^{2}(a^{2}+ab+b^{2})}{18\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}+O((|a|+|b|)^{6}),

again as
(a,b)\to 0
.
∎

3
Characterizations of resistance distance

In
Section

2
, we showed that near a reference measure, the discrete transportation distance admits a second-order expansion governed by a Laplacian pseudoinverse, and that in the stationary case
\mu=\pi
the corresponding inner product is exactly
\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{w}^{\dagger}}=f^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}g
on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. In light of this, it is natural to regard the interior of the probability simplex as a flat Riemannian manifold whose metric tensor is induced by
L_{w}^{\dagger}
, and, in doing so, to explore the dynamical and variational statements that become available in the resulting geometry. The goal of this section is to formalize this viewpoint, and then develop a number of equivalent characterizations of the resulting geodesic distance.

We begin by restating the definition of the so-called resistance manifold.

Definition 3.1

(Resistance manifold)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph, and for
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, write

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{R}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)}=\sqrt{(\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)}.

(34)

The
resistance manifold
is the metric space
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
.

We remark that
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
is naturally a Riemannian manifold:
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
is an open subset of the affine hyperplane
\mathcal{H}=\{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{V}:\sum_{x\in V}\rho(x)=1\}
which can be equipped with the Riemannian metric tensor
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{R}}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\rho}(f,g):=\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{w}^{\dagger}},\qquad\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),\ \ f,g\in T_{\rho}\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)\cong\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0},

(35)

and the Riemannian distance induced by
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{R}}
coincides with
d_{\mathcal{R}}
. Moreover, since
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{R}}_{\rho}
does not depend on
\rho
, the manifold is flat, and its geodesics are precisely the Euclidean line segments in
\mathcal{H}
. We state this as a lemma below without proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, and set
\rho_{t}:=(1-t)\rho_{0}+t\rho_{1}
. Then
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\subseteq\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
is the unique constant-speed geodesic joining
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
in the resistance manifold.

Thus, in the remaining two subsections, we aim to provide several characterizations the metric
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
and in the process prove
Theorem

1.5
. Many of these are based on classical results for resistance distance between vertices, and their proofs amount to generalizations in this regard. In doing so we also highlight some related facts which may be of interest to the reader.
Subsection

3.1
focuses on variational characterizations of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
, while
Subsection

3.2
focuses on combinatorial characterization of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

In the interest of consistency with the results in
Section

2
as well as
Section

4
to follow, in the definition of the resistance manifold we consider only measures in
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
. However, the characterizations of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
which follow remain valid if one or more entries of the measure(s) under consideration vanish, so these are stated on
\mathscr{P}(V)
to achieve fuller generality.

3.1
Variational characterizations of resistance distance

In this subsection we prove the first half of
Theorem

1.5
, which consists of various variational descriptions of resistance distance between measures.

First we recall some background concerning so-called
Beckmann problems
, beginning with the
p
-Wasserstein metric.

Definition 3.3

(
p
-Wasserstein metric)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
and
p\geq 1
be fixed. We define their
p
-Wasserstein distance
, denoted
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
, according to the linear program

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)^{p}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{x,y\in V}\Delta_{w}(x,y)^{p}\,\gamma(x,y),

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\left\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}:\,\gamma(x,y)\geq 0,\,\sum_{y\in V}\gamma(x,y)=\mu(x),\,\sum_{x\in V}\gamma(x,y)=\nu(y),\,x,y\in V\right\},

and where, for
x,y\in V
,
\Delta_{w}(x,y)
is the weighted shortest path distance between
x
and
y
.

In this subsection, the graph metric in
Definition

3.3
is understood as the shortest path metric with edge lengths given by the values
w_{xy}
.

In the setting
p=1
, the Wasserstein distance admits several equivalent characterizations, including the so-called
Beckmann formulation
, which takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{J\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}}\left\{\sum_{(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}w_{xy}|J(x,y)|:\,BJ=\mu-\nu\right\}.

(36)

Here,
E^{\prime}
is an orientation of
E
, and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E^{\prime}}
is the associated
oriented incidence matrix
with entries given by

\displaystyle B_{x,(u,v)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&x=u,\\
-1,&x=v,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The equivalence exhibited in
Eq.

36
can be proved by two applications of strong duality for linear programs; we refer the reader to
[
56
, Ch. 6]
for a proof. In the case of resistance distance
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
,
Eq.

36
admits a natural extension.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
. Let
E^{\prime}
denote an orientation of
E
, and let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E^{\prime}}
denote the associated oriented incidence matrix. Then the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{J\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}}\left\{\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}:\,BJ=\mu-\nu\right\}.

(37)

Proof.

Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}\times E^{\prime}}
denote the diagonal matrix with entries
W_{e,e}=w_{e}
for
e\in E^{\prime}
. Then
Eq.

37
amounts to a linearly constrained least squares problem: minimize
\|W^{-1/2}J\|_{2}^{2}
subject to
BJ=\mu-\nu
. By changing variables we can recast this as minimizing
\|Z\|_{2}^{2}
subject to
BW^{1/2}Z=\mu-\nu
. The Moore–Penrose inverse of the matrix
(BW^{1/2})\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E^{\prime}}
recovers this solution; therefore

\displaystyle\inf_{J\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}}\left\{\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}:\,BJ=\mu-\nu\right\}

\displaystyle=\|(BW^{1/2})^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}((BW^{1/2})^{\dagger})^{\top}(BW^{1/2})^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)

\displaystyle=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}((BW^{1/2})(BW^{1/2})^{\top})^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)

\displaystyle=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)=\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu).

∎

Our second variational characterization of resistance distance between measures is a version of the well known Benamou–Brenier formula for
2
-Wasserstein distance (see
[
7
]
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
. Let
E^{\prime}
denote an orientation of
E
, and let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times E^{\prime}}
denote the associated oriented incidence matrix. Then it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J_{t}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,dt\,:\,\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{V}),J_{t}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}),

(38)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}+BJ_{t}=0,\quad\rho_{0}=\mu,\quad\rho_{1}=\nu\Bigr\}.

(39)

Proof.

We denote by
\mathcal{I}
the infimum on the right-hand side of
Eq.

38
. For the upper bound
\mathcal{I}\leq\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
, let
J^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}
be the unique minimizer of the Beckmann problem as in
Theorem

3.4
:

\displaystyle J^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\arg\min\left\{\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,:\,BJ=\mu-\nu\right\}.

We construct a feasible path by taking the linear interpolation
\rho_{t}=(1-t)\mu+t\nu
,
0\leq t\leq 1
. Then
\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{V})
,
\rho_{0}=\mu
, and
\rho_{1}=\nu
. For this path, we have
\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=\nu-\mu
. Consequently, the constraint
\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}+BJ_{t}=0
becomes

\displaystyle\nu-\mu+BJ_{t}

\displaystyle=0,

which gives
BJ_{t}=\mu-\nu
for all
t\in[0,1]
. To satisfy the continuity equation, we take the constant curve
J_{t}=J^{*}
(independent of
t
). With this choice, the objective functional evaluates to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J_{t}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J^{\ast}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,dt=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J^{\ast}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}=\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu).

This shows that
\mathcal{I}\leq\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
. For the lower bound
\mathcal{I}\geq\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
, let
(\rho_{t},J_{t})
be a given admissible pair. We have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}+BJ_{t}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\rho_{0}=\mu,\quad\rho_{1}=\nu.

Integrating the continuity equation from
t=0
to
t=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nu-\mu+\int_{0}^{1}BJ_{t}\,dt

\displaystyle=0,

so

\displaystyle B\left(\int_{0}^{1}J_{t}\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=\mu-\nu.

Let
\overline{J}:=\int_{0}^{1}J_{t}\,dt
. Then
\overline{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{E^{\prime}}
satisfies the constraint
B\overline{J}=\mu-\nu
that appears in the Beckmann problem. By
Theorem

3.4
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|\overline{J}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{\left|\int_{0}^{1}J_{t}(e)\,dt\right|^{2}}{w_{e}}\leq\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}|J_{t}(e)|^{2}\,dt}{w_{e}}=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|J_{t}(e)|^{2}}{w_{e}}\,dt.

Since this holds for every feasible pair
(\rho_{t},J_{t})
, taking the infimum over all such pairs yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}.

The claim is proved.
∎

The variational formula
Eq.

38
can also be viewed as a relaxation of that in the definition of the discrete transportation distance (cf.
4
), wherein the weights
\theta\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)},\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)
appearing in the action functional are replaced by constant unit weights.

The third and final variational characterization of resistance distance between measures is obtained by recasting
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
as a negative homogeneous Sobolev norm distance interpreted in the graph setting. This is accomplished as follows. First, on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
, we define the following inner product:

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}w_{xy}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y)),\quad f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

We remark that
\langle f,g\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}
is obviously bilinear and symmetric, and from

\displaystyle\langle f,f\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}w_{xy}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}=f^{\top}L_{w}f,

(40)

it follows that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}
is positive definite on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Thus,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}
defines an inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. The notation
\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)
is taken from
Eq.

40
, by viewing
f^{\top}L_{w}f=\|W^{1/2}B^{\top}f\|_{2}^{2}
as a (weighted) norm of the gradient of
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
.

By passing over to the formal dual of the norm induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}
, we obtain a negative homogeneous Sobolev-type norm
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)}
as adapted to the graph setting. The following theorem asserts that the norm distance between measures in this space recovers resistance distance.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)}^{2}:=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\langle f,\mu-\nu\rangle^{2}}{\langle f,f\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(V,w)}}.

Proof.

By the definition of the
\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)
norm and
Eq.

40
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(V,w)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(f^{\top}(\mu-\nu))^{2}}{f^{\top}L_{w}f}=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(f^{\top}(\mu-\nu))^{2}}{(L_{w}^{1/2}f)^{\top}(L_{w}^{1/2}f)}.

On
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
, the matrix
L_{w}
and its square root are positive definite. Therefore we may apply a change of variables, writing
g=L_{w}^{1/2}f
, so that
f=L_{w}^{\dagger/2}g
on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(f^{\top}(\mu-\nu))^{2}}{(L_{w}^{1/2}f)^{\top}(L_{w}^{1/2}f)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left((L_{w}^{\dagger/2}g)^{\top}(\mu-\nu)\right)^{2}}{g^{\top}g}=\sup_{g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left(g^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger/2}(\mu-\nu)\right)^{2}}{g^{\top}g}.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the last expression equals

\displaystyle\|L_{w}^{\dagger/2}(\mu-\nu)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)=\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu),

and the theorem is proved.
∎

3.2
Combinatorial characterizations of resistance distance

In this subsection we prove the second half of
Theorem

1.5
, which consists of two combinatorial formulations of resistance distance between measures.

The first such formulation concerns expected vertex hitting times. To this end, let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the simple random walk on
V
. For
y\in V
, we define the
hitting time
to reach
y
, denoted
T_{y}
, as the random variable

\displaystyle T_{y}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{t}=y\right\}.

For
x,y\in V
, the
expected hitting time
from
x
to
y
, denoted
\mathrm{H}(x,y)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left({T_{y}\,|\,X_{0}=x}\right).

We recall the following classical fact concerning the relationship between vertex effective resistance and so-called
commute times
, namely, that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{H}(x,y)+\mathrm{H}(y,x)}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}.

(41)

We refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
64
,
65
]
for proofs of this fact. It is therefore natural to wonder to what extent
Eq.

41
extends to the case of measure effective resistance and, more generally, how to interpret
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
in terms of vertex hitting times or other related quantities. Naïvely, any such extension must address how to interpret a hitting time between probability measures in the graph setting. This is facilitated by optimal stopping rules.

Definition 3.7

(Stopping rule)
.

A
stopping rule
is a map
S
that associates to each finite path
\tau=(X_{0},X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})
on
G
a number
S(\tau)
in
[0,1]
.
S(\tau)
is understood to be the probability that we continue a random walk given that
\tau
is the walk thus far observed.

With slight abuse, we write
\mathbb{E}\left({S|X_{0}\sim\mu}\right)
to denote the expected length of the corresponding walk conditioned on an initial distribution
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
. If
\mathbb{E}\left({S|X_{0}\sim\mu}\right)<\infty
, then the walk stops almost surely in finite time, so we define
X_{S}
to be the position of the random walk at the stopping time. This leads to a notion of the generalized hitting time between
\mu,\nu
.

Definition 3.8

(Access time)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
. The
access time

\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\mathbb{E}\left({S\,|\,X_{0}\sim\mu}\right):\mathbb{E}\left({S|X_{0}\sim\mu}\right)<\infty,X_{S}\sim\nu\right\},

(42)

where, for any random variable
Y
on
V
, we say
Y\sim\mu
if
\mathbb{P}\left({Y=x}\right)=\mu(x)
for each
x\in V
. In other words,
\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
is the minimum mean length of walks that originate with distribution
\mu
and terminate according to a stopping rule that achieves distribution
\nu
at stopping time. If
S^{\ast}
achieves the infimum in
\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
, then
S^{\ast}
is said to be an
optimal stopping rule
.

We use the overloaded notation
\mathrm{H}(x,y)
and
\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
without risk of confusion since in the case of Dirac measures, the access time recovers the expected hitting time between the corresponding vertices. The set of feasible stopping rules in
Definition

3.8
is nonempty in general provided
G
is connected. For example, one has the
naïve stopping rule

S_{n}
, obtained as follows: at time
t=0
, perform a sample
x\sim\nu
. Then run
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
conditioned on
X_{0}\sim\mu
, and stop the walk when
X_{S_{n}}=x
. It follows that
X_{S_{n}}\sim\nu
, and moreover, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu(x)\,\nu(y)\,\mathrm{H}(x,y)<\infty.

(43)

Several examples of optimal stopping rules for general distributions are given in
[
45
]
. The authors also show that
\mu,\nu
always admit an optimal stopping rule achieving the infimum in
Eq.

42
as long as
G
is connected. The access time
\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
has several useful properties, which we summarize in the proposition below (for proofs, we refer the reader to
[
45
]
).

Proposition 3.9

(Properties of access times,
[
45
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be connected and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then the following facts hold:

(i)

If
\nu=\mathbf{1}_{y}
for some
y\in V
, then
\mathrm{H}(\mu,\mathbf{1}_{y})=\sum_{x\in V}\mu(x)\mathrm{H}(x,y)
,

(ii)

\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{y\in V}\sum_{x\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\mathrm{H}(x,y)
,

(iii)

\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)
is (separately) convex in its arguments; namely if
\mu,\mu^{\prime},\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}(V)
are given and
0\leq c\leq 1
is fixed, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(c\mu+(1-c)\mu^{\prime},\nu)

\displaystyle\leq c\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)+(1-c)\mathrm{H}(\mu^{\prime},\nu),\text{ and }

(44)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(\mu,c\nu+(1-c)\nu^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq c\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu)+(1-c)\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu^{\prime}).

(45)

The next result generalizes
Eq.

41
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))(\mu(y)-\nu(y))\mathrm{H}(x,y).

(46)

Proof.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
denote the matrix with entries
\mathrm{H}(x,y)
. Then
H-H^{\top}
is skew-symmetric, so for any function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, it holds that

\displaystyle f^{\top}(H-H^{\top})f

\displaystyle=0.

In particular,

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)H(x,y)f(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)(H(x,y)+H(y,x))f(y).

Using
Eq.

41
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)(\mathrm{H}(x,y)+\mathrm{H}(y,x))f(y)=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)\mathcal{R}(x,y)f(y).

Expanding
\mathcal{R}(x,y)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})=(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{xx}+(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{yy}-2(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{xy}.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}

\displaystyle f(x)\mathcal{R}(x,y)f(y)=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)((L_{w}^{\dagger})_{xx}+(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{yy}-2(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{xy})f(y).

(47)

With
f=\mu-\nu
, we have
\sum_{x\in V}f(x)=0
, and thus the diagonal terms in the summand on the right-hand side of
Eq.

47
vanish, leaving

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\mathrm{H}(x,y)(\mu(y)-\nu(y))

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\sum_{x,y\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))(L_{w}^{\dagger})_{xy}(\mu(y)-\nu(y))

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu),

by definition. The claim is proved.
∎

We remark that
Theorem

3.10
is slightly more involved than the comparatively simpler commute time formula for vertex effective resistance. However, it is still possible to bound
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
in terms of access times. This is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
satisfies the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\max\{\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu),\mathrm{H}(\nu,\mu)\}

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}(\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{H}_{n}(\nu,\mu))

(49)

where
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{E}\left({S_{n}}\right)
is the expected duration of the naïve stopping rule as in
Eq.

43
.

Proof.

For the first inequality, we use
Theorem

3.10
and Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))(\mu(y)-\nu(y))\mathrm{H}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{y\in V}(\nu(y)-\mu(y))\sum_{x\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\mathrm{H}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}\left\|\sum_{x\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\mathrm{H}(x,y)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\left\|\sum_{x\in V}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\mathrm{H}(x,y)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\max\{\mathrm{H}(\mu,\nu),\mathrm{H}(\nu,\mu)\},

where the final line follows from
Proposition

3.9
(ii). For the second inequality, we use the vertex commute time formula
Eq.

41
. Since
\mu-\nu=\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu(x)\nu(y)(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})
and
z\mapsto z^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}z
is convex, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=(\mu-\nu)^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mu-\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu(x)\nu(y)(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\mathbf{1}_{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu(x)\nu(y)\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu(x)\nu(y)(\mathrm{H}(x,y)+\mathrm{H}(y,x))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}(\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mu,\nu)+\mathrm{H}_{n}(\nu,\mu)),

as claimed.
∎

Our second combinatorial characterization of resistance distance between measures is related to the spanning trees and spanning
2
-forests of the underlying graph. We are primarily motivated by the following result, originally described in
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
,
17
]
).

We denote by
\mathcal{T}(G)
the weighted number of spanning trees of
G
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathscr{T}(G)}w_{T}

where
\mathscr{T}(G)\subseteq 2^{E}
is the set of spanning trees of
G
, each viewed as a subset of edges. Similarly, if
x,y\in V
are fixed, we let
\mathcal{F}(x,y)
denote the weighted number of spanning
2
-forests of
G
in which
x
and
y
belong to different components, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(x,y)}w_{F},

where
\mathcal{F}(x,y)
is the set of spanning
2
-forests of
G
in which
x
and
y
belong to different components.

Theorem 3.12

(Resistance distance via separating spanning forests,
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
x,y\in V
be fixed. Then the effective resistance between
x
and
y
admits the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}(x,y)}{\mathcal{T}(G)}.

We now extend
Theorem

3.12
to the setting of probability measures on
V
. For
A\subseteq V
and
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
, we write
\mu(A)=\sum_{x\in A}\mu(x)
. For
F\subseteq E
, we write

\displaystyle\mu(F):=\mu(\{x\in V\,:\,\{x,y\}\in F\text{ for some }y\sim x\}).

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}(\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1}))^{2},

where the sum runs over all spanning
2
-forests
F
of
G
, denoted
\mathcal{F}(G)
, with components
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

Proof.

We recall from the proof of
Theorem

3.10
, specifically the argument following
Eq.

47
, that for any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
with
\sum_{x\in V}f(x)=0
, one has

\displaystyle f^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}f

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}f(x)\mathcal{R}(x,y)f(y).

(50)

By
Theorem

3.12
, we have the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}\Big(\mathbf{1}\{x\in T_{1},y\in T_{2}\}+\mathbf{1}\{x\in T_{2},y\in T_{1}\}\Big).

where the sum on the right-hand side runs over all spanning
2
-forests of
G
with components
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Applying
Eq.

50
with
f=\mu-\nu
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{x,y\in V}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{w_{F}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))(\mu(y)-\nu(y))\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\times\,\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{x\in T_{1},y\in T_{2}\}+\mathbf{1}\{x\in T_{2},y\in T_{1}\}\bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}\sum_{x\in T_{1}}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))\sum_{y\in T_{2}}(\mu(y)-\nu(y))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}(\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1}))(\mu(T_{2})-\nu(T_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(G)\\
F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\end{subarray}}w_{F}(\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1}))^{2},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\mu(T_{1})+\mu(T_{2})=\nu(T_{1})+\nu(T_{2})=1
, so
\mu(T_{2})-\nu(T_{2})=-(\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1}))
. The claim is proved.
∎

We state as a corollary the special case of
Theorem

3.13
in the case where the graph
G
is a tree.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a tree, let
E^{\prime}
be an orientation of
E
, and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
be fixed. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(x,y)\in E^{\prime}}\frac{|\mu(T_{e})-\nu(T_{e})|^{2}}{w_{e}},

where
T_{e}\subseteq V
denotes the vertices of the component of
G\setminus e
that contains the tail of
e
.

Remark 3.15
.

Theorem

3.13
admits the following probabilistic interpretation. Let
\mathbb{F}
denote the law on
\mathcal{F}(G)
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(F)

\displaystyle\propto w_{F},\quad F\in\mathcal{F}(G).

For
F\in\mathcal{F}(G)
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(V)
fixed, define the imbalance variable
Z
according to

\displaystyle Z(F=T_{1}\cup T_{2})

\displaystyle=\mu(T_{1})-\nu(T_{1})=\nu(T_{2})-\mu(T_{2}).

Then if
F\sim\mathbb{F}
,
Z
defines a random variable, and in turn,
\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)
is proportional to its second moment:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{E}_{F\sim\mathbb{F}}\left({|Z|^{2}}\right),

where the constant of proportionality depends only on
G
.

4
Gradient flows and convexity on the resistance manifold

In
Section

3
, we introduced the resistance manifold
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
and provided a number of characterizations of
\mathcal{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
, equivalently, the corresponding geodesic distance
d_{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. The purpose of this section is to analyze gradient flows of various functionals on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
with respect to the resistance metric, and in particular to prove
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.7
.

In this setting, if
F:\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, then the Riemannian gradient vector
\nabla F(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
is uniquely determined by

\displaystyle\partial F(\rho)[f]=f^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}\nabla F(\rho),\qquad f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0},

where
\partial F(\rho)[f]
denotes the directional derivative of
F
at
\rho
in direction
f
. Given an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
is called a
gradient flow trajectory of
F
if it solves

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=-\nabla F(\rho_{t}),\qquad t\in I.

We now define a class of functionals on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
given by differentiable
f
-divergences relative to the stationary distribution.

Definition 4.1

(
f
-Divergence)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph, and let
\pi
denote its stationary distribution. Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable. The (normalized)
f
-divergence functional
on
\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi):=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\,f\!\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\pi(x)}\right),\qquad\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V).

(51)

The prefactor
1/\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)
is included for scaling convenience in our analysis of the associated gradient flow. Our next result characterizes the gradient flow of
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
on the resistance manifold.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
denote the stationary distribution of the simple random walk with transition matrix
P=D_{w}^{-1}A_{w}
. Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, and consider the functional
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
defined in
Eq.

51
. Then its gradient is given by

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\rho/\pi\right),\quad\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),

(52)

where
f^{\prime}(\rho/\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
denotes the vector with entries
x\mapsto f^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)
. Consequently, for any interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, a smooth curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
is a gradient flow trajectory of
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
if and only if it satisfies the evolution equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\rho_{t}/\pi\right)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,D_{w}\,(P-I)\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\rho_{t}/\pi\right),

(53)

or, equivalently, for each
x\in V
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\left(f^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}\right)-f^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\right).

(54)

Proof.

Fix
\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
and a tangent direction
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Differentiating
Eq.

51
at
\rho
in direction
h
, and using the chain rule, yields

\displaystyle\partial\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)[h]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\frac{h(x)}{\pi(x)}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}h(x)\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,\Bigl(f^{\prime}(\rho/\pi)\Bigr)^{\top}h.

(55)

Let
\Pi_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Since
h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
,
55
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)[h]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,h^{\top}\Pi_{0}f^{\prime}(\rho/\pi).

By definition of the resistance gradient,
g:=\nabla\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
must satisfy

\displaystyle h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}g=\partial\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)[h],\qquad\forall\,h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

(56)

Using
L_{w}^{\dagger}L_{w}=L_{w}L_{w}^{\dagger}=\Pi_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{V}
, the identities above imply

\displaystyle g=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\,\Pi_{0}f^{\prime}\!\left(\rho/\pi\right)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\,f^{\prime}\!\left(\rho/\pi\right),

where the last identity uses that
L_{w}
annihilates constants. This proves
Eq.

52
. Substituting this gradient into the defining identity for gradient flow trajectories gives
Eq.

53
, and conversely any smooth curve satisfying
Eq.

53
is a gradient flow trajectory by definition. Expanding
L_{w}
as
D_{w}-A_{w}
yields
Eq.

54
.
∎

Theorem

4.2
admits two natural corollaries which amount to specializations to the cases of the
\chi^{2}
and entropy functionals obtained by different choices of
f
. We state them below without proof.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(r)=\frac{1}{2}(r-1)^{2}
. Then, for each
\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)=\frac{1}{2\,\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}\frac{(\rho(x)-\pi(x))^{2}}{\pi(x)}=:\chi^{2}(\rho),

and the gradient flow of
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
on
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\right)=(P^{\top}-I)\rho_{t}.

In particular, this is the continuous time random walk forward equation in column vector form.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(r)=r\log r
. Then
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
recovers the (normalized) relative entropy functional; specifically, for each
\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\rho\,\|\,\pi)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{x\in V}\rho(x)\log\frac{\rho(x)}{\pi(x)}.

Moreover, the gradient flow of
\mathsf{Div}_{f}(\cdot\,\|\,\pi)
on
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\,L_{w}\,\log\left(\rho_{t}/\pi\right),

or, equivalently, for each
x\in V
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)}\sum_{y\sim x}w_{xy}\left(\log\frac{\rho_{t}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}\right).

We now prove a second result identifying the geodesic strong convexity modulus of
\chi^{2}
in the resistance manifold with the spectral gap of the normalized Laplacian. Recall that
\mathcal{L}_{w}=D_{w}^{-1/2}L_{w}D_{w}^{-1/2}
is symmetric positive semidefinite and if
G
is connected,
\mathcal{L}_{w}
has kernel spanned by
D_{w}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
. We write
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
for its smallest strictly positive eigenvalue.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a connected graph and let
\pi
be the stationary distribution of the simple random walk on
V
. On the resistance manifold
(\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V),d_{\mathcal{R}})
, the functional
\chi^{2}
defined in
Eq.

9
has geodesic strong convexity modulus (in the sense of
Eq.

10
) equal to the spectral gap
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.2
, every constant-speed geodesic in the resistance manifold is a line segment in
\mathcal{H}
. Fix
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{\star}(V)
, and set
\rho_{t}=(1-t)\rho_{0}+t\rho_{1}
. Let
h:=\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. Then
using
\pi(x)=\mathrm{d}_{x}/\mathrm{Vol}_{w}(G)
,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\pi)^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}(\rho-\pi).

Also,
\rho_{t}-\pi=(\rho_{0}-\pi)+th
, and expanding the quadratic along this segment gives the identity

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\rho_{t})=(1-t)\chi^{2}(\rho_{0})+t\chi^{2}(\rho_{1})-\frac{t(1-t)}{2}\,h^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}h.

By
Lemma

3.2
, the squared geodesic distance on the resistance manifold satisfies
d_{\mathcal{R}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}=h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h
, so
\chi^{2}
is geodesic
\lambda
-strongly convex if and only if

\displaystyle h^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}h\geq\lambda\,h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h,\qquad\forall\,h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}.

Consequently, the largest admissible
\lambda
is

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}:=\inf_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{h^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}h}{h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h}.

(57)

It remains to identify
\lambda_{\star}
with
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
. Since
G
is connected,
\ker(L_{w})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
and hence
L_{w}
is positive definite on
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. In particular, the map
h\mapsto L_{w}^{\dagger/2}h
is a bijection from
\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}
. With the change of variables
z=L_{w}^{\dagger/2}h
, we have

\displaystyle h^{\top}L_{w}^{\dagger}h

\displaystyle=\|L_{w}^{\dagger/2}h\|_{2}^{2}=\|z\|_{2}^{2}=z^{\top}z,

\displaystyle h^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}h

\displaystyle=(L_{w}^{1/2}z)^{\top}D_{w}^{-1}(L_{w}^{1/2}z)=z^{\top}\bigl(L_{w}^{1/2}D_{w}^{-1}L_{w}^{1/2}\bigr)z.

Therefore
Eq.

57
becomes

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}=\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{V}_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{z^{\top}\bigl(L_{w}^{1/2}D_{w}^{-1}L_{w}^{1/2}\bigr)z}{z^{\top}z},

(58)

i.e.,
\lambda_{\star}
is the smallest strictly positive eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix
L_{w}^{1/2}D_{w}^{-1}L_{w}^{1/2}
. Now define
S:=D_{w}^{-1/2}L_{w}^{1/2}
. Then

\displaystyle S^{\top}S=L_{w}^{1/2}D_{w}^{-1}L_{w}^{1/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad SS^{\top}=D_{w}^{-1/2}L_{w}D_{w}^{-1/2}=\mathcal{L}_{w}.

The matrices
S^{\top}S
and
SS^{\top}
have the same multiset of nonzero eigenvalues. Since
G
is connected, both have a one-dimensional kernel (spanned by
\mathbf{1}
for
S^{\top}S
and by
D_{w}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
for
\mathcal{L}_{w}
), and hence their smallest strictly positive eigenvalues agree:

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}\bigl(S^{\top}S\bigr)=\lambda_{2}(SS^{\top})=\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w}).

Combining this with
Eq.

58
yields
\lambda_{\star}=\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{w})
, as claimed.
∎
