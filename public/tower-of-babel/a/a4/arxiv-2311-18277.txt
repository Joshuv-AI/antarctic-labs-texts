Revisiting the two-sample location shift model with a log-concavity assumption
F.Riddhiman Saha, S.Priyam Das, T.Nilanjana Laha

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the two-sample location shift model, a classic semiparametric model introduced by Stein 1956 . This model is known for its adaptive nature, enabling nonparametric estimation with full parametric efficiency. Existing nonparametric estimators of the location shift often depend on external tuning parameters, which restricts their practical applicability ( Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021 ) . We demonstrate that introducing an additional assumption of log-concavity on the underlying density can alleviate the need for tuning parameters. We propose a one step estimator for location shift estimation, utilizing log-concave density estimation techniques to facilitate tuning-free estimation of the efficient influence function. While we employ a truncated version of the one step estimator for theoretical adaptivity, our simulations indicate that the one step estimators perform best with zero truncation, eliminating the need for tuning during practical implementation.

Introduction

This paper focuses on the two-sample semiparametric location model, a widely recognized semiparametric model introduced by
Stein 1956
. Suppose
X
and
Y
are two independent random variables. The two-sample semiparametric location can be described as follows:

X=e_{1}+\mu,\quad Y=e_{2}+\mu+\Delta,

(1.1)

where
\mu
and
\Delta
are real numbers and
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are mean-zero independent random variables with density
g
and corresponding distribution function
G
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
denote the class of all densities on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. We assume that
g
belongs to the class

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0}=\bigg\{g\in\mathcal{P}\ :\ \int xg(x)dx=0,\ \mathcal{I}_{g}<\infty\bigg\}.

(1.2)

Here
\mathcal{I}_{g}
is the Fisher information for location, which is finite if and only if
g
is an absolutely continuous density satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\dfrac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)}\bigg)^{2}g(x)dx<\infty,

(1.3)

where
g^{\prime}
is a weak derivative of
g

(
Huber 1964
, cf. Theorem
3
of)
. In this case
\mathcal{I}_{g}
takes the form

\mathcal{I}_{g}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\dfrac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)}\bigg)^{2}g(x)dx.

The constraint
\int xg(x)dx=0
ensures the identifiability of
\mu
and
g
in model (
1.1
). Suppose we observe
m
and
n
many i.i.d. copies of
X
and
Y
, respectively. The goal is to estimate
\Delta
in the presence of the nuisance parameters
\mu
and
g
.

One specialty of the two-sample location shift model is that it is an adaptive model in the sense of
Bickel et al. 1998
. To elaborate, even when
g
is unknown, it is possible to estimate
\Delta
with the same asymptotic variance as the scenario when
g
is known.
Thus, it is possible to obtain asymptotically efficient estimators of
\Delta
that “adapt" to the unknown infinite dimensional parameter
g

1
1

1

Here and afterwards, an asymptotically efficient estimator is to be understood in the sense of
Van der Vaart 1998
, page 367. In particular, it attains the lowest possible asymptotic variance among the regular estimators; see Section
3.1.1
for more details.
. Ever since
Stein 1956
introduced the idea of adaptation in this model, it has spurred a series of subsequent investigations
(
Bhattacharya 1967
,
Beran 1974
,
Van Eeden 1970
)
. The location-scale version of the model has been explored by
Park 1990
,
Hsieh 1995
,
Weiss and Wolfowitz 1970
, where, in addition to the location shift
\Delta
,
Y
also undergoes a scale shift.

There remain two mainstream approaches for estimating
\Delta
in the semiparametric model (
1.1
). The first approach is based on rank-based estimators such as the Hodges-Lehmann rank estimator or
Kraft and Van Eeden 1970
’s rank estimator
(
Beran 1974
,
Van Eeden 1970
,
Weiss and Wolfowitz 1970
,
Bhattacharya 1967
)
. The rank-based approach prevailed in early research on this problem until the advent of semiparametric efficiency theory.
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
)
. The second approach, which is based on one step estimators, utilizes modern semiparametric efficiency theory
(
Begun et al. 1983
,
Bickel 1982
,
Park 1990
)
.

Although the aforementioned approaches offer an adaptive estimator
\widehat{\Delta}
of
\Delta
in the model (
1.1
), they share a common drawback: a heavy reliance on external tuning parameters
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
,
Hogg 1974
,
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
)
. The primary source of these external tuning parameters is the estimation procedure for
g
and its functionals. Specifically, both the one step estimators and the rank-based estimators depend on estimating the score function
-g^{\prime}/g
. The existing approaches using the one step estimators and the rank-based estimators typically involve smoothing parameters for estimating
g
or its functionals. However, the estimation of
\Delta
is reported to be sensitive to such smoothing parameters
(
Park 1990
,
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
)
. Some of the aforementioned approaches require additional tuning parameters for estimating
g
or its functionals. For instance,
Beran 1974
’s approach relies on a Fourier basis expansion to estimate the scores
-g^{\prime}/g
, necessitating a tuning parameter for choosing the number of Fourier basis functions. The score-estimation approach in
Van Eeden 1970
, which requires grid-based discretization of functions, introduces tuning parameters for selecting the grid points.

Moreover, the estimation of
g
or its functionals is not the sole source of tuning parameters in the above line of work. Some of the aforementioned approaches involve
strategies such as sample splitting
(
Van Eeden 1970
)
and truncation
(
Park 1990
)
to improve the estimation performance. These strategies also introduce additional tuning parameters.
In an attempt to potentially alleviate the burden of tuning parameters,
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
and
Hsieh 1995
proposed an alternative approach based on
Parzen 1979
’s (
Parzen 1979
, Section 10) formulation of the two-sample location-scale problem. This formulation transforms the problem into a generalized linear model using sample quantiles, eliminating the need to estimate the score
-g^{\prime}/g
. However, this approach does not completely eliminate the tuning parameter issue, as it still requires estimating
g
for constructing confidence intervals, introducing tuning parameters. Additionally, the discretization of the quantile function introduces further tuning parameters.

The existing literature lacks a solid methodology for optimally choosing these tuning parameters
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
)
. In their 2021 memoir of Stein’s paper
(
Stein 1956
)
,
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
remarks that the practical implementation of such semiparametric estimators remains “as tricky as in the 1950s" due to the intricacies associated with these tuning parameters. Among the previously mentioned works, only
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
and
Park 1990
discuss data-dependent procedures for tuning parameter selection.
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
requires tuning parameters for estimating
g
and choosing grid points to discretize a quantile function. As stated in their paper, while their tuning procedure prioritizes simplicity and computational feasibility, it does not target the optimal tuning parameters and it is likely to incur a loss of efficiency.

On the other hand,
Park 1990
aims to identify the optimal smoothing parameter for estimating
g
by minimizing the estimated Mean Squared Error (MSE) of
\widehat{\Delta}
through a grid search.
Park 1990
uses bootstrapping to estimate the MSE of
\widehat{\Delta}
for each value of the smoothing parameter. Currently, there are no consistency guarantees for this bootstrap estimator.
However, in the context of nonparametric regression and density estimation, it has been established that bootstrap estimators of the MSE of smoothed estimators can have non-negligible bias
(
Hall 1990
,
Hall and Horowitz 2013
,
Härdle and Bowman 1988
)
. Furthermore, there is no guarantee that tuning parameters minimizing the MSE would be optimal in terms of maximizing the asymptotic efficiency. Our numerical experiments in Section
5
reveal that the MSE-based procedure may result in suboptimal choices of smoothing parameters. In fact, in our simulation study, the asymptotic efficiency and the MSE of the resulting estimators were comparable to our baseline estimator, i.e., the difference of sample means, which is not asymptotically efficient for the two-sample location shift model.

Some of the tuning-related difficulties may be unavoidable since external tuning parameters are essential for estimating
g
in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. This necessity stems from the fact that the search space for
g
, namely the set
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, is too large, lacking enough structure.
If one aims to estimate
g
or its functionals without relying on tuning parameters, additional structural constraints on
\mathcal{P}_{0}
might be necessary. This naturally raises the question:
what restriction on
\mathcal{P}_{0}
would provide sufficient structure for achieving tuning-free estimation of
g
?
In this paper, we impose the shape restriction of log-concavity on
g
to facilitate tuning-free estimation of this density and its functionals.

Log-concavity:
A density is called log-concave if its logarithm is a concave function. We propose this restriction for four main reasons, which are described below.

Tuning-free density estimation
: The class of log-concave densities is structurally rich enough to admit a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Pal, Woodroofe and Meyer 2007
)
. The MLE among the class of all log-concave densities can be efficiently computed using existing R packages in a tuning-free way
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2011
)
.
The log-concavity restriction on
\mathcal{P}_{0}
enables us to leverage this MLE for constructing tuning-free estimators of
g

(
Walther 2009
,
Chen and Samworth 2013
,
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
,
Xu and Samworth 2019
,
Kim, Guntuboyina and Samworth 2016
,
Barber and Samworth 2020
)
.

Versatility of log-concave density class
: The class of log-concave densities is an important subclass of unimodal densities, containing a list of well-known and well-studied commonly used unimodal sub-exponential densities, such as, normal, Laplace, logistic, gamma (for shape parameter greater than one), and beta (for parameters greater than one). A more exhaustive list can be found in
Bagnoli and Bergstrom 2005
. Additionally, this class is closed under convolution.

Availabilty of tests to check log-concavity
: While visually verifying log-concavity from the data can be challenging, several tests are available in the literature to check log concavity
(
Gangrade, Rinaldo and Ramdas 2023
,
Asmussen and Lehtomaa 2017
,
Dunn et al. 2021
)
. If there are grounds to believe that the underlying density is unimodal but not heavy-tailed, then log-concavity is a very plausible assumption
(
Laha et al. 2022
,
Walther 2009
)
.

Application in semiparametric models:
Shape constraints related to convexity have already been considered in other semiparametric models such as the one-sample location model
(
Laha 2021
, log-concavity,)
, the single index model
(
Kuchibhotla and Patra 2016
, convexity,)
, and the mixture model
(
Walther 2002
,
Walther 2009
, log-concavity, )
.
The assumption of log-concavity has also been applied to our two-sample location shift model.
Van Eeden 1970
, who developed an adaptive estimator of
\Delta
in this model, assumed the function
u\mapsto-g^{\prime}(G^{-1}(u))/g(G^{-1}(u))
to be non-increasing, which is equivalent to
g
being log-concave
(
Bobkov 1996
, Proposition A.1)
. However,
Van Eeden 1970
did not use the term "log-concave" because the literature on log-concave density estimation had not yet developed by their time. Therefore,
Van Eeden 1970
’s approach fundamentally differs from our approach, which leverages modern tools for log-concave density estimation.

1.1
Main contribution

In this paper, the proposed estimator of
\Delta
is built upon the one step estimator advocated by
Park 1990
and
Bickel 1982
. As mentioned earlier, a common critique of this estimator has been reliant on the estimation of
g^{\prime}

(
Potgieter and Lombard 2012
,
Hsieh 1995
)
. However, this concern is alleviated under the log-concavity assumption on
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, since log-concave MLE-based estimators of
g
readily provide an estimator of
g^{\prime}

(
Laha 2021
)
. We adopt the smoothed log-concave MLE technique proposed by
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Chen and Samworth 2016
. This technique yields smooth estimators of
g
and
g^{\prime}
without requiring any external tuning parameter for smoothing.

In order to establish the asymptotic efficiency, we use a truncated version of the one step estimator. It is established that the truncated one step estimator is adaptive, provided the truncation level decays to zero (Theorem
4.1
in Section
4
). This implies that after centering and scaling, the aforementioned estimator is asymptotically normal with variance
\mathcal{I}^{-1}
. Much of the theoretical analysis in this paper is dedicated to proving the adaptivity of the truncated one step estimator. This proof necessitates a sharp theoretical analysis of the performance of the one step estimators, with intricacies relying on the log-concave projection theory and empirical processes
(
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
,
Barber and Samworth 2020
,
Samworth 2017
)
. Although it seems that the truncation level of the truncated one step estimators is an external tuning parameter, we emphasize that the truncation is introduced primarily for technical reasons in the proof. Our simulations demonstrate that the one step estimators exhibit maximum efficiency and minimum MSE when the truncation level
\eta_{m,n}
is set to zero. Therefore, for practical implementation of our proposed estimators, we recommend a truncation level of zero. Hence,
no tuning is required
for practical implementation of our estimators. The proposed estimator can be easily implemented using the provided R package TSL.logconcave, made available on Github
(
Saha and Laha 2023
)
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
presents the mathematical formulation. In Section
3
, we delve into the derivation of the one step estimator for the proposed problem, and we provide an outline of the estimation procedure relying on the log-concave MLE. Section
4
establishes the asymptotic efficiency of the truncated versions of the one step estimator. Section
5
conducts extensive simulations to compare our one step estimators (both truncated and untruncated) with existing semiparametric estimators. The detailed proof of Theorem
4.1
is provided in the Appendix.

Method

The one step estimator is derived from the efficient influence function, which is a key concept in semiparametric efficiency theory. This function characterizes the sensitivity of an estimator to small perturbations in the data. As mentioned in Section
1
, the two-sample location shift model is a well-studied semiparametric model
(
Begun et al. 1983
,
Bickel 1982
,
Park 1990
)
.

Park 1990
,
Bickel 1982
,
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
explore the efficient influence function in the balanced case, i.e., when
m=n
.
The balanced case automatically fits into the mold of the one-sample semiparametric theory since, in this case, the problem can be represented as a one-sample problem with bivariate data. We consider the potentially unbalanced case, which demands extra care because, unlike the balanced case, this scenario does not automatically fit the mold of one-sample semiparametric theory.

We derive The efficient influence function and the one step estimator are derived for the unbalanced case in Section
3.1
. The efficient influence function for estimating
\Delta
can be derived working along the lines of
Begun et al. 1983
. Although our approach shares similarities with
Begun et al. 1983
, we introduce essential minor deviations to ensure its alignment with contemporary semiparametric literature such as
Park 1990
and
Van der Vaart and Wellner 2021
. While
Begun et al. 1983
used Hellinger differentiability to define scores, our definitions adhere to the quadratic-mean-differentiability-based convention
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, page 362)
, ensuring consistency with modern semiparametric approaches. Also, the underlying Hilbert space considered in our analysis is
L_{2}(\mathbb{\mathbb{P}})
, which differs from
Begun et al. 1983
, where
L_{2}
-spaces of Lebesgue-like dominating measures were considered. If preferred, readers may choose to skip the derivation in Section
3.1
and proceed directly to Section
3.2
. Section

uses the derived formula of the one step estimator (as described in Section
3.1
) to construct an estimator of

utilizing the additional log-concavity assumption.

3.1.1
Preliminary background

In this section, we provide a concise overview of some key concepts in semiparametric efficiency theory, which play a crucial role in the subsequent derivations. Readers who are already familiar with this topic may choose to skip this section and proceed to Section
3.1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an infinite dimensional set and let
k
be a positive integer.
Consider the problem of estimating a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, in the presence of a finite-dimensional nuisance parameter
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and an infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter
\zeta\in\mathcal{V}
. We assume that the underlying probability distribution
\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
is entirely determined by
\theta
,
v
, and
\zeta
. Furthermore, suppose
\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
is a probability measure defined on a measurable space
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{U}
is an Euclidean space, and
\mathcal{B}
is the corresponding Borel sigma field. To formalize, we let
\mathcal{S}
be the semiparametric model parameterized by
(\theta,v,\zeta)\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}
represents the class of distributions of the form
\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
. Finally, let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote a sequence of
n
i.i.d. random realizations taking value in
\mathcal{U}
from the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta}\in\mathcal{S}
.

The score functions corresponding to
\theta
,
v
, and
\zeta
are defined following
Van der Vaart 1998
, page 372, which we elaborate below. The score functions for the finite-dimensional parameters
\theta
and
v
are represented by the usual partial derivatives of the log-likelihood function and are denoted as
l_{\theta}
and
l_{v}
, respectively. However, to define the nuisance score corresponding to
\zeta
, we require the proper concept of perturbation for the infinite dimensional parameter
\zeta
. For this purpose, we introduce the tangent space of
\mathcal{V}
at
\zeta
, denoted as
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\zeta)
. The tangent space
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\zeta)
is the closed linear span of the tangent set of
\mathcal{V}
at
\zeta
. This tangent set is the set of perturbations
b
by which
\zeta
can be approximated within
\mathcal{V}
. Each perturbation
b
represents a finite-dimensional submodel
t\mapsto\zeta_{t}
of
\mathcal{V}
, taking the form
\zeta_{t}=(1+tb)\zeta
for small values of
t
. These submodels pass through
\zeta
at
t=0
. See page 362 of
Van der Vaart 1998
for more details on these finite dimensional submodels.

Let
\omega(x;\theta,v,\zeta)
be the density of
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
at
x
. Then for any
b\in\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\zeta)
,
the score corresponding to the perturbation
t\mapsto\zeta_{t}
at
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
is the function
B_{b}:\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle B_{b}(u;\theta,v,\zeta)=\partialderivative{\log\nf(x;\theta,v,\zeta_t)}{t}\bigg|_{t=0}\quad\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{U}.

(3.1)

By
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
, we denote the closed linear span of all such scores
B_{b}
’s when
b
ranges across the tangent space
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\zeta)
. The space
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
represents the score space associated with the infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter
\zeta
. Importantly, this space becomes a Hilbert space when equipped with the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
inner product
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, cf. pp. 372 of)
.

The nuisance score space is the closed linear span of all nuisance scores.
The efficient score function of
\theta
in the model
\mathcal{S}
is the orthogonal projection of its score function
l_{\theta}
onto the orthocomplement of the nuisance score space. Thus, for fixed values of
\theta
,
v
, and
\zeta
, the efficient score function
l_{\theta,\text{eff}}(\cdot\mid v,\zeta)
is a function from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathbb{R}
. The efficient score function is an important quantity for us because it is closely related to the efficient influence function.

Begun et al. 1983
gives a general recipe for deriving the efficient score function of
\theta

(
Bickel et al. 1998
, see also pp. 74 of)
. We first project
l_{\theta}
, the score function of
\theta
, onto the orthocomplement of the closed linear span of the score function of
v
, denoted as
l_{v}
. We will refer to the resulting projection as
l_{\theta\mid v}
, and it would represent the efficient score of estimating
\theta
, had
\zeta
been known.
To obtain the efficient score function for estimating
\theta
in
\mathcal{S}
, we need to find the orthogonal projection of
l_{\theta\mid v}
onto the orthocomplement of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
, the nuisance score space for
\zeta
. For all projection-related operations, we take the underlying Hilbert space to be
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
.

The Fisher information
I(\theta\mid v,\zeta)
for estimating
\theta
in
\mathcal{S}
is formally defined as the squared expectation of the efficient score function, which can be expressed as
I(\theta\mid v,\zeta)=\mathbb{E}[l_{\theta,\text{eff}}(U\mid v,\zeta)^{2}]
, with the expectation taken under the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta}
. In semiparametric efficiency theory, the Fisher information plays a crucial role because its inverse serves as the lower bound on the asymptotic variance of any regular estimator of
\theta

(
Van der Vaart 1998
, Theorem 25.21,)
. An estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
of
\theta
in
\mathcal{S}
will be called asymptotically efficient if it is regular and
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})
is asymptotically normal with asymptotic variance
I(\theta\mid v,\zeta)^{-1}

(
Van der Vaart 1998
, pp. 367)
.

In a special case where the score
l_{\theta\mid v}
is orthogonal to
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,\zeta})
, the efficient score
l_{\theta,\text{eff}}(\cdot\mid v,\zeta)
becomes equal to
l_{\theta\mid v}
. In this scenario, the Fisher information for estimating
\theta
in
\mathcal{S}
equals
\mathbb{E}[l_{\theta|v}(U)^{2}]
, which is the Fisher information for estimating
\theta
in the parametric submodel of
\mathcal{S}
where
\zeta
is known. This phenomenon is referred to as adaptation
(
Begun et al. 1983
,
Van der Vaart 1998
,
Bickel 1982
)
.
Consequently, in adaptive models, asymptotically efficient estimators of
\Delta
attain the parametric Cramer Rao lower bound, which is the lowest possible asymptotic variance among the regular estimators in the corresponding parametric model. This implies that in adaptive semiparametric models, not having the knowledge of
\zeta
does not result in any loss of information when estimating
\theta
.

The efficient influence function is the scaled version of the efficient score and is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{l}_{\theta}(u\mid v,\zeta)=\frac{l_{\theta,\text{eff}}(u\mid v,\zeta)}{I(\theta\mid v,\zeta)}\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal{U}.

(3.2)

The efficient influence function plays a key role in the theory of asymptotaically efficient estimators because any estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
of
\theta

is asymptotically efficient if and only if it admits the following asymptotic expansion
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, see page 367,)
:

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\theta+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{l}_{\theta}(U_{i}\mid v,\zeta)+o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(3.3)

3.1.2
Estimation of
\Delta
in the two-sample location shift model

As mentioned earlier, when the sample sizes are unequal, two-sample models do not directly fit into the framework of the conventional one-sample-based semiparametric framework elucidated above. Therefore, to fit the model in (
1.1
) into a one-sample mold, following
Begun et al. 1983
, we introduce an indicator variable
Z
, which indicates the source sample for each observation. We assume that we observe
m+n
independent replicates of
(U,Z)
, where
Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\lambda)
. The random variable
U
is distributed as
X
when
Z=1
, and it is distributed as
Y
when
Z=0
. Consequently, conditional on
Z=z
, the random variable
U
has the density

\displaystyle\omega(u\mid z)=\begin{cases}g(u-\mu)&\ \text{if }z=0\\
g(u-\mu-\Delta)&\text{ if }z=1,\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

where
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Letting
N=m+n
, we can present our observed data as

\displaystyle(Z_{i},U_{i})=\begin{cases}(1,X_{i})&\text{if }i=1,\ldots,m\\
(0,Y_{i-m})&\text{if }i=m+1,\ldots,N.\end{cases}

The joint density of
(Z,U)
writes as

\omega(z,u;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g)\equiv\omega(z,u)=\Big(\lambda g(u-\mu)\Big)^{z}\Big((1-\lambda)g(u-\mu-\Delta)\Big)^{1-z}

for all
z\in\{0,1\}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that, now this model fits into the framework elucidated in Section
3.1.1
. In this context, the finite-dimensional parameter of interest, i.e.,
\theta
in Section
3.1.1
, corresponds to
\Delta
. The finite-dimensional nuisance parameters, denoted as
v
in Section
3.1.1
, correspond to
(\mu,\lambda)
, while the infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter
\zeta
corresponds to
g
.

Letting
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}
denote
the probability measure associated with the density
\omega(\cdot;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g)
,
we formally define our model
\mathcal{S}
by

\mathcal{S}:=\bigg\{\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}:\Delta,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lambda\in(0,1),\quad g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\bigg\}.

Observe that
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}
corresponds to the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,v,g}
in Section
3.1.1
.
Thereafter, the score functions corresponding to
\Delta
,
\mu
, and
\lambda
are denoted by
l_{\Delta}
,
\lambda_{\mu}
, and
l_{\lambda}
, respectively.

Calculation of the scores
:

To compute the efficient influence function for estimating
\Delta
, the first step is to calculate the score functions. Let
g^{\prime}
be a weak derivative of
g
, which exists because
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
is absolutely continuous (see equation
1.3
).
Denoting
\psi=g^{\prime}/g
, we calculate the score functions for
\Delta
,
\mu
, and
\lambda
as follows:

\displaystyle l_{\Delta}(z,u;\mu,\lambda,g):=\partialderivative{\log\nf(z,u;\mu,\lambda,g)}{\Delta}=-(1-z)\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu-\Delta),

\displaystyle l_{\mu}(z,u;\Delta,\lambda,g)=\partialderivative{\log\nf(z,u;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g)}{\mu}=-z\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu)-(1-z)\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu-\Delta),

\displaystyle l_{\lambda}(z,u;\Delta,\mu,g)=\partialderivative{\log\nf(z,u;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g)}{z}=\frac{z}{\lambda}-\frac{1-z}{1-\lambda}.

The Fisher information matrix corresponding to the parameters
(\Delta,\mu,\lambda)

(
Bickel 1982
, cf. page 13 of)
takes the form

\displaystyle I\equiv I(\Delta,\mu,\lambda,f)=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}&(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}&0\\
(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}&\mathcal{I}_{g}&0\\
0&0&\lambda^{-1}+(1-\lambda)^{-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(3.4)

As mentioned in Section
3.1.1
, to compute the score corresponding to the infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we first need to obtain the tangent space of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
at
g
. From
Begun et al. 1983
, it follows that for any
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, this tangent space has the form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(g)=\bigg\{b\in L_{2}(G):\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b(x)g(x)dx=0,\ \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xb(x)g(x)dx=0\bigg\}.

(3.5)

Equation
3.1
shows that for any
b\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(g)
, the score corresponding to the perturbation
g_{t}:t\mapsto(1+tb)g
at
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}
is the function
B_{b}:\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. In our case,
B_{b}
takes the form

\displaystyle B_{b}(z,u;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g)=\partialderivative{\log\nf(z,u;\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g_t)}{t}\bigg|_{t=0}=zb(u-\mu)+(1-z)b(u-\mu-\Delta).

(3.6)

Hence, the score-space corresponding to the nuisance parameter
g
at
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}
is the closed linear span of all functions of the form
B_{b}
, where
b
ranges over
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(g)
. We denote this space as
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
.

The efficient influence function
:

Now we proceed to calculate the efficient score function for estimating
\Delta
in the model
\mathcal{S}
.
Let us define

I_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{22}&I_{23}\\
I_{32}&I_{33}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{1.2}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{12}&I_{13}\end{bmatrix},

where
I
is as defined in (
3.4
).
The
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
projection of
l_{\Delta}
onto the orthocomplement of the closed linear span of
l_{\mu}
and
l_{\lambda}
is given by
(see Proposition 1 of
Bickel 1982
, page 30)

\displaystyle l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}(z,u;g)

\displaystyle=l_{\Delta}(z,u)-I_{2}^{-1}I_{1.2}\begin{bmatrix}l_{\mu}(z,u)\\
l_{\lambda}(z,u)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=z(1-\lambda)\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu)-\lambda(1-z)\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu-\Delta).

The score
l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}
is the efficient score in the submodel of
\mathcal{S}
where
g
is known. The latter submodel is a parametric model because
\mu
and
\lambda
are the only nuisance parameters in this model.
Our discussion in Section
3.1.1
implies that the efficient score for estimating
\Delta
in
\mathcal{S}
is the orthogonal projection of
l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}
onto the orthocomplement of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
, i.e., the score space of
g
.
However, for all scores
B_{b}\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
, we have:

\displaystyle\langle B_{b},l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})}=E_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\Big(Zb(U-\mu)+(1-Z)b(U-\mu-\Delta)\Big)l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}(Z,U)\Big]=0,

where for any probability measure
P
,
E_{P}
denotes the expectation with respect to
P
.
Thus
l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}
is orthogonal to the score-space
\dot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
corresponding to
g
. Therefore, our discussion in Section
3.1.1
implies that the model
\mathcal{S}
is adaptive and the efficient score function
l_{\Delta,\text{eff}}
equals
l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}
.
This result indicates that the Fisher information of estimating
\Delta
in
\mathcal{S}
equals
I(\Delta\mid\mu,\lambda,g)=\mathbb{E}[l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}(Z,U;g)^{2}]
. Straightforward algebra shows that this expectation equals
\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}
.
Then (
3.2
) implies that the efficient influence function
\tilde{l}_{\Delta}(z,u\mid\mu,\lambda,g)
for estimating
\Delta
in
\mathcal{S}
takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{l}_{\Delta}(z,u\mid\mu,\lambda,g)=\frac{l_{\Delta|\mu,\lambda}(z,u;g)}{\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}}=\frac{z\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu)}{\lambda\mathcal{I}_{g}}-\frac{(1-z)\psi^{\prime}(u-\mu-\Delta)}{(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}}.

(3.7)

Since the Fisher information for estimating
\Delta
in
\mathcal{S}
equals
\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}
, a regular estimator
\widehat{\Delta}
of
\Delta
is asymptotically efficient if

\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta)\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\mathcal{I}_{g}^{-1}}{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\right).

Note that
m/N=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{Z_{i}}/N\to_{p}\lambda
and
n/N=1-m/N\to_{p}1-\lambda
. Therefore, by Slutskey’s theorem, asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\Delta}
is equivalent to

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}(\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta)\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathcal{I}_{g}^{-1}\right).

(3.8)

Remark 1
.

Suppose, instead of
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we consider
g\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}_{0}
in (
1.1
). This leads to the model
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, which can be represented as
\{\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}:\Delta,\mu\in\mathbb{R},\lambda\in(0,1),g\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}
. In an extreme case where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{g_{0}\}
is a singleton set,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
becomes a parametric model. The Fisher information for estimating
\Delta
in this parametric model is easily checked to be
\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
. Generally, since
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}_{0}
, the finite-dimensional submodels of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
passing through
g
will also lie in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Thus, if
b
is a perturbation at
g
in the submodel
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, it is also a perturbation with respect to the bigger model
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Therefore, the tangent space resulting from such perturbations will be a subset of
\dot{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
, and the corresponding score space
\dot{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})\subset\dot{S}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
. Consequently, if
l_{\Delta\mid\mu,\lambda}
is orthogonal to the larger score space
\dot{S}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
, it is also orthogonal to the subspace
\dot{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g})
. Hence, adaptation in the larger model
\mathcal{S}
implies adaptation in all the submodels of the form
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Therefore, the Fisher information for estimating
\Delta
in any submodel of the form
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is also
\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}
at
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta,\mu,\lambda,g}
. The efficient influence function for estimating
\Delta
also remains the same across all subsets of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, given by (
3.7
). In particular, in the special case when
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{LC}\cap\mathcal{P}_{0}
, the efficient Fisher information for estimating
\Delta
in the corresponding submodel
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
remains
\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g}
, and
\tilde{l}_{\Delta}
remains the efficient influence function. Therefore, having prior knowledge of log-concavity does not improve the Fisher information for estimating
\Delta
.

3.1.3
One step estimator

In this section, we derive the one step estimator for estimating
\Delta
in
\mathcal{S}
when
\Delta=\Delta_{0}
,
\mu=\mu_{0}
, and
g=g_{0}
.
Since the efficient influence function for estimating
\Delta_{0}
has the form in (
3.7
), by equation
3.3
, an estimator of
\Delta_{0}
is asymptotically efficient if and only if it admits the asymptotic expansion

\Delta_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{Z_{i}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(U_{i}-\mu_{0})}{\lambda\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}}-\frac{(1-Z_{i})\psi_{0}^{\prime}(U_{i}-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})}{(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}}\bigg)+o_{p}(N^{-1/2}).

(3.9)

Suppose we have at our disposal
\sqrt{N}
-consistent preliminary estimators
\overline{\Delta}
and
\overline{\mu}
of
\Delta_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
, respectively. Further, suppose we have reasonable preliminary estimators of
g_{0}
and
\psi_{0}^{\prime}
, denoted by
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
and
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}
, respectively.
Then an estimator of
\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
is readily given by:

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)^{2}\widehat{g}_{m,n}(x)dx.

(3.10)

Finally, we estimate
\lambda
by its maximum likelihood estimator
m/N
. Then using the asymptotic expansion of (
3.9
), we can define an estimator of
\Delta_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}=\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{Z_{i}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(U_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})}{m\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}-\frac{(1-Z_{i})\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(U_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{n\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}\bigg).

(3.11)

The estimator in (
3.11
) is known as the one step estimator (see page 43 of
Bickel 1982
,
Bickel 1982
or page 72 of
Van der Vaart 1998
,
Van der Vaart 1998
).
Replacing the
U_{i}
’s in the definition of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
by
X_{i}
’s and
Y_{i}
’s, we rewrite (
3.11
) as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}=

\displaystyle\ \overline{\Delta}_{m,n}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})}{m\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(Y_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{n\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}

(3.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \overline{\Delta}_{m,n}+\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}d{\mathbb{F}}_{m}(x)-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(y-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}d\mathbb{H}_{n}(y).

The estimator in (
3.12
) is motivated by the asymptotic expansion in (
3.9
) and the semiparametric theory behind it. However, we want to highlight a subtle difference. The one step estimator
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
in (
3.12
) can be well-defined even when the asymptotic expansion in (
3.9
) is not applicable. The semiparametric model
\mathcal{S}
, and consequently the expansion in (
3.9
), requires
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Note that
\lambda
is interpreted as the limit of
m/N
under the model
\mathcal{S}
. However,
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
remains a valid estimator even when
m/N\to 0
or one. In fact, the definition of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
in (
3.9
) does not require
m/N
to have a limit at all. Section
4
will show that we only require
\min(m,n)\to\infty
to establish the adaptivity of the proposed one step estimators. This aligns with the asymptotic behavior observed in
Van Eeden 1970
and
Beran 1974
’s estimators.

3.2
Shape-constrained estimation of
\Delta_{0}

In Section
3.1
, we derived the traditional one step estimator of
\Delta_{0}
, which is given by (
3.12
). However, for the sake of theoretical analysis, we use a truncated variant of this one step estimator. Section
3.2.1
discusses the construction of this one step estimator. All one step estimators require preliminary estimators of
\Delta_{0}
,
\mu_{0}
,
g_{0}
, and its log-derivative
\psi_{0}^{\prime}
.
Section
3.2.2
elaborates on the utilization of log-concave density estimators to compute the one step estimators.

3.2.1
Truncated one step estimator

The asymptotic analysis of the one step estimator in (
3.12
) poses challenges. To elaborate, during the performance analysis of the one step estimator,
some form of uniform bound is required on the growth rate of
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
to apply empirical process tools and convergence results. However, since we impose minimal restriction on
\psi_{0}
apart from concavity, we have limited control on the asymptotic behaviour of
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
at the tails, irrespective of the chosen estimator. Thus, obtaining uniform rate results on
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
can be challenging.
In the literature on one step estimators, a commonly adopted strategy to tackle such challenges is the trimming of extreme observations during the construction of the one step estimator. This practice results in what is commonly known as the truncated one step estimator
(
Laha 2021
,
Stone 1975
,
Park 1990
)
. Hence, for the theoretical analysis presented in this paper, we confine our focus to the truncated one step estimator. However, we include the estimator in (
3.12
) in our numerical experiments in Section
5
, and henceforth, we refer to it as the untruncated one step estimator.

We trim
\eta_{m,n}
-proportion of the data, where we let
\eta_{m,n}
tend to
0
.
For
\eta_{m,n}\in(0,1/2)
, we use the notations
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
, respectively, to represent the
\eta_{m,n}
-th and
1-\eta_{m,n}
-th quantiles of the distribution
G_{0}
.
Suppose
\widehat{G}_{m,n}
is the distribution function of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
. Then
\widehat{\xi}_{1}=\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{-1}(\eta_{m,n})
and
\widehat{\xi}_{2}=\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{-1}(1-\eta_{m,n})
are reasonable estimators of
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
, respectively. Although it would be ideal for the notation of
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}
,
\widehat{\xi}_{1}
, and
\widehat{\xi}_{2}
to explicitly depend on
m
and
n
, we omit this explicit dependency on sample sizes to steamline notation. Now we are ready to introduce our truncated one step estimator, which is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}=

\displaystyle\ \overline{\Delta}_{m,n}+\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}}\dfrac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)

\displaystyle\ -\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}}\dfrac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(y-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}d\mathbb{H}_{n}(y),

(3.13)

where

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})=\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)^{2}\widehat{g}_{m,n}(x)dx

(3.14)

is the truncated estimator of the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
.

We want to clarify a point here. Apparently truncation introduces the tuning parameter
\eta_{m,n}
, which represents the level of truncation. However, our numerical experiments in Section
5
show that when we consider the shape-constrained version of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
(to be discussed in Section
3.2
), its variance monotonically decreases as
\eta_{m,n}
approaches zero. In these experiments, the untruncated one step estimator in (
3.12
) consistently demonstrates the lowest variance and the lowest MSE among the shape-constrained one step estimators. Consequently, we recommend the latter estimator for practical implementations. In Section
3.2
, we will see that the shape-constrained version of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
requires no additional tuning parameter other than
\eta_{m,n}
, and the shape-constrained version of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
is tuning free. Thus, although the theoretical analyses of this paper require truncation for technical reasons, the proposed estimator of this paper is tuning-free. This distinguishes our approach from existing nonparametric methods, where optimal values of tuning parameters are typically unknown, and extensive tuning procedures are often required to identify optimal ranges for these parameters.

3.2.2
Preliminary estimators of
\Delta_{0}
,
\mu_{0}
, and
g_{0}

The current section discusses our choices of
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
,
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
, and
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
. Our focus is primarily on the construction of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
, which heavily relies on the maximum likelihood estimator of log-concave densities.

Preliminary estimators of
\Delta_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
:

In general, one step estimators require the preliminary estimators of the finite dimensional parameters to be
\sqrt{n}
-consistent for achieving asymptotic efficiency (see page 72 of
Van der Vaart 1998
; see also
Park 1990
,
Beran 1974
).
Our theoretical analysis also suggests that
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
have to be
\sqrt{N}
-consistent for
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
to be asymptotically efficient. Under the model defined in equation (
1.1
), we have
\mu_{0}=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\Delta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[X]
. Therefore, if
g_{0}
possesses a finite second moment,
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
are
\sqrt{n}
-consistent estimators for
\mu_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
, respectively. It is worth noting that we can substitute the sample means in the above formulas with other
\sqrt{n}
-consistent estimators of location, such as the sample median
(
Laha 2021
, particularly in the log-concave case,)
, or the Z-estimator for the shift in the logistic shift model
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, see Example 5.40 and Theorem 5.23 of)
. However, simulations showed that the sample-mean-based preliminary estimators tend to exhibit the best overall performance in terms of efficiency. Therefore, for the purpose of this paper, we take
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
to be
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
, respectively.

Preliminary estimator of
g_{0}
:

As mentioned in Section
1
, we take our estimator of
g_{0}
to be log-concave. A vast literature exists on the estimation of log-concave densities
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Chen and Samworth 2016
,
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
)
. Notably, the MLE within the class of log-concave densities does exist and is computationally tractable
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Pal, Woodroofe and Meyer 2007
)
. However, this MLE cannot be used directly to estimate
g_{0}
since our observations, i.e., the
X_{i}
’s and the
Y_{j}
’s, are not sampled from
g_{0}
. Nevertheless,
X_{i}-\mu_{0}
and
Y_{j}-\Delta_{0}-\mu_{0}
have density
g_{0}
. Therefore, we first construct the pseudo-observations
X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s and
Y_{j}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s for
i=1,\ldots,m
and
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then we calculate the log-concave MLE based on these
m+n
pseudo-observations. Given that this density estimator is formed by merging pseudo-observations from both the
X
and
Y
samples, we refer to it as the pooled estimator for
g_{0}
and denote it by
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
.

However,
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
is non-smooth due to the non-smooth form of the log-concave MLE. The lack of smoothness is a common feature of the MLEs of shape-constrained classes
(
Grenander 1956
,
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Balabdaoui and Wellner 2007
)
. In particular, the logarithm of the log-concave MLE is piecewise affine, with knots belonging to the underlying sample
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
)
. However, the lack of smoothness can potentially lead to suboptimal performance in small samples
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Chen and Samworth 2013
,
Samworth 2017
)
. To address this issue, smoothing techniques utilizing a Gaussian kernel can be employed to smooth the log-concave MLE
(
Chen and Samworth 2013
)
. The smoothed version of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
takes the form

\hat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}(x)=\frac{1}{\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi\left(\frac{x-t}{\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}}\right)\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(t)dt,

(3.15)

where
\varphi
is the standard Gaussian density and
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
is the smoothing parameter. Although it might seem that
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
is an external tuning parameter, that is not the case, as
Chen and Samworth 2013
provides a data-dependent closed formula for computing
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
.

For our problem, this formula yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{2}=\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz-\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz\bigg)^{2}

(3.16)

is the variance of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
, and
\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}
is the sample variance of the combined sample.
Given that the overall mean of the combined sample of
X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s and
Y_{j}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
’s is zero, the sample variance
\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}
is given by

\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})^{2}}{N-1}.

The difference
\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}
in (
3.16
) is positive by (2.1) of
Chen and Samworth 2013
. Consequently, the
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
in (
3.16
) is well-defined.

We will refer to the estimator
\hat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}

as the “pooled-smoothed estimator", which signifies that it is derived through a two-step process. This process involves data pooling, where pseudo-observations are combined from both the
X
-sample and the
Y
-sample, followed by a smoothing step.
The pooled-smoothed estimator
\hat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
will serve as our preliminary estimator
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
. This density estimator is smooth and supported on the entire real line
\mathbb{R}
, ensuring that
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}=\log\widehat{g}_{m,n}
and its derivative are well-defined at all points on
\mathbb{R}
.
Additionally, it can be shown that
\int\hat{x}g_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}(x)dx=0
, indicating that this density estimator is centered, similar to the density
g_{0}
.

Other possible choices for
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
:

One might come up with other potential choices for
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
. For instance, instead of combining the
X
-sample and the
Y
-sample, one might opt to estimate
g_{0}
separately from both samples. A weighted average of the resulting estimators could yield an improved density estimator.
A smooth
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
can be obtained by computing smoothed log-concave MLEs
(
Chen and Samworth 2013
)
from both samples during the above construction. However, averaging destroys the log-concavity structure, making the weighted estimators described above non-log-concave. The pooled estimator
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
, as discussed earlier, is another potential choice for
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
. However, while this estimator is log-concave, it is non-smooth.
While these estimators could theoretically lead to asymptotically efficient
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
, simulations have revealed that the
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
derived from our pooled-smoothed estimator outperforms those obtained from these other estimators. As a result, we have excluded the weighted estimators and the non-smooth pooled estimator from further consideration in this paper.

Remark 2
.

Unimodality is a more naturally occurring shape constraint compared to log-concavity, and the class of unimodal densities is a strict superset of the class of log-concave densities
(
Walther 2009
)
. Therefore, unimodality may seem like an alternative choice for the shape restriction on
g
. However, the class of unimodal densities is too large to admit an MLE when the mode is unknown
(
Birgé 1997
)
. Therefore, the unimodality constraint does not offer the computational advantages of the log-concavity constraint
(
Laha 2021
)
. Hence, we opt for log-concavity instead of unimodality as the shape restriction.

4
Asymptotic properties

In this section, we establish the asymptotic efficiency of the truncated one step estimator
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
provided
\eta_{m,n}\to 0
at a sufficiently slow rate. In what follows, we will assume that
g_{0}\in\mathcal{LC}\cap\mathcal{P}_{0}
.
First we present a technical assumption on
\psi_{0}
, which requires
\psi_{0}^{\prime}
to be globally Lipschitz on
\text{int}(\text{dom}(\psi_{0}))
.

Assumption A
.

There exists
\kappa>0
so that

|\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x)-\psi^{\prime}_{0}(y)|\leq\kappa|x-y|\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in\text{int}(\text{dom}(\psi_{0})),

where
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x)
(or
\psi_{0}^{\prime}(y)
) can be either the left or the right derivative of
\psi_{0}
at
x
(or
y
).

When
g_{0}
is log-concave,
\psi_{0}
is concave, and hence the left and right derivative of
\psi_{0}
exist on
\text{int}(\text{dom}(\psi_{0}))

(
Hiriart-Urruty and Lemaréchal 2004
, see page 163)
. When
\psi_{0}
is twice differentible, Assumption
A
reads as
|\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}|\leq\kappa
.
Beran 1974
, who also considered adaptive estimation in the two-sample location shift problem, imposed a similar restriction on a related function
t\mapsto\psi_{0}(G_{0}^{-1}(t))
. Their Assumption B implies that the second derivative of the latter function is continuous on
[0,1]
. Since
[0,1]
is compact, this assumption implies
\psi_{0}\circ G_{0}^{-1}
has a bounded second derivative on
[0,1]
.
Boundedness assumptions on the second derivative is also quite common in the shape-constrained litertaure
(
Laha 2021
,
Kuchibhotla and Patra 2016
,
Mukherjee and Sen 2018
)
.
We also want to clarify that Assumption
A
is required for technical reasons in the proof, and it may not be necessary. As we will see, the Gamma distribution in our simulation setting 4 in Section
5
violates Assumption
A
because
\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-3/x^{2}
in this case. However, our simulations in Section
5
suggest that
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
remains asymptotically efficient under this setting.

Now we are ready to present the main theorem of this section, which establishes the asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
as defined by (
3.8
). Its proof is provided in the Appendix.

Theorem 4.1.

Suppose
X
and
Y
are as in (
1.1
) and
g_{0}\in\mathcal{LC}\cap\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Let
\eta_{m,n}=CN^{-2\varrho/5}
where
C>0
is a constant and
\varrho\in(0,1/4]
. Further suppose
\min(m,n)\to\infty
. Then, under Assumption
A
, the truncated one step estimator
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
with preliminary estimators
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
,
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
, and
\widehat{g}_{m,n}=\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
satisfies

\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-\Delta_{0})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}^{-1}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
is the Fisher information corresponding to
g_{0}
.

The rate of decay of the truncation parameter
\eta_{m,n}
in Theorem
4.1
merits some discussion. Proposition
1
in the Appendix shows that the Hellinger distance between
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
and
g_{0}

is of the order
O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
. Theorem
4.1
requires
\eta_{m,n}
to decay to zero at a slower rate than the latter. Thus Theorem
4.1
calibrates
\eta_{m,n}
’s decay rate by the Hellinger error rate of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
.
To demonstrate that the Hellinger error of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
is
O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
, we utilize the Hellinger continuity theory of log-concave projection developed by
Barber and Samworth 2020
. However, given the Hellinger error of the log-concave MLE is of the order
O_{p}(N^{-2/5})

(
Doss and Wellner 2016
, cf.)
, the actual rate of Hellinger error decay for
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
may be faster than
O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
.
Although a sharper rate of Hellinger error decay would allow for a smaller truncation level
\eta_{m,n}
in Theorem
4.1
, its impact in this work will be nominal. This is because the theoretical truncation levels will not be used during practical implementations.

Although we present Theorem
4.1
for our specific preliminary estimators, the assertion actually holds for a broader class of preliminary estimators. In fact, we only require
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
to be
\sqrt{N}
-consistent estimators of
\mu_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}
, respectively. Additionally,
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
needs to satisfy some consistency conditions including those related to the Hellinger error. A more generalized version of Theorem
4.1
can be found in Appendix
A
.

As previously mentioned, our empirical study indicates that the untruncated one step estimator performs as well as, or even better than, the truncated one step estimators. While we currently lack theoretical results regarding the asymptotic distribution of the untruncated one step estimator, our simulations lead us to conjecture that Theorem
4.1
holds for this estimator as well.

Proof outline:

Although the detailed proof of Theorem
4.1
is provided in the Appendix, we offer a concise overview of the proof here to provide some intuition.
A pivotal and challenging aspect of our proof involves demonstrating that
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)
does not grow faster than a polynomial rate when
x\in[\widehat{\xi}_{1},\widehat{\xi}_{2}]
.
Achieving such bounds on
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
necessitates a degree of control over the Hellinger error of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
.

To this end, we show that
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}},g_{0})=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
, as mentioned previously. Proving this Hellinger error bound is a major part of the proof, which also yields that
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})\to_{p}\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
. Our proof technique is tailored specifically to the truncated one step estimator, as extending it to the untruncated version would require a uniform bound on
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
—a challenge that currently remains unresolved.

The rest of the proof hinges on the following decomposition of
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}}\dfrac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)}_{A}-\underbrace{\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}}\dfrac{\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(y-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}d\mathbb{H}_{n}(y)}_{B}.

(4.1)

Using
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})\to_{p}\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
and the abovementioned bound on
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
on
[\widehat{\xi}_{1},\widehat{\xi}_{2}]
,
A
can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle A\ \approx\ \parbox{50.00008pt}{empirical process\\
term}\ +\ \text{CLT term}_{A}\ +\ \parbox{50.00008pt}{bias due to \\
estimating $g_{0}$}+\underbrace{\mu_{0}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}}_{\text{bias due to estimating }\mathbb{E}[X]}+o_{p}(N^{-1/2}).

(4.2)

The central limit theorem (CLT) term is an important term in the above expansion since it contributes to the desired asymptotic normality. It is the average of some independent random variables based on the
X
sample, and hence amenable to the central limit theorem.
The empirical process term is asymptotically negligible, and we use Donsker’s theorem to establish its asymptotic negligibility. The goal of the asymptotic expansion in (
4.2
) is to separate the CLT term and the bias terms from the other asymptotically negligible terms.

Similarly,
B
’s asymptotic expansion writes as

\displaystyle B\ \approx\ \parbox{50.00008pt}{empirical process\\
term}\ +\ \text{CLT term}_{B}\ +\ \parbox{50.00008pt}{bias due to\\
estimating $g_{0}$}\ +\ \underbrace{\mu_{0}+\Delta_{0}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}}_{\text{bias due to estimating }\mathbb{E}[Y]}\ +\ o_{p}(N^{-1/2}),

(4.3)

where as before, the CLT term contributes to the final asymptotic normality and the empirical process term is asymptotically negligible. The CLT term from
B
, being an average based on the
Y
sample, is independent of the CLT term resulting from the expansion of
A
.

The key point here is that the bias due to the estimation of
g_{0}
in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) are identical, and they cancel each other in the expression of
A-B
in (
4.1
). Subtracting the CLT term of
B
from that of
A
and rearranging (
4.1
) lead to the desired asymptotic normality in Theorem
4.1
.

Comparison with the one sample case:

Laha 2021
developed a one step estimator of the location in the one-sample symmetric location model using log-concavity assumptions similar to ours. They demonstrated that their truncated one step estimator is adaptive when the log-concavity assumption holds. The main distinction between
Laha 2021
’s setting and ours is that the underlying density
g_{0}
is symmetric in
Laha 2021
’s one-sample location model. Therefore,
Laha 2021
’s estimators of
g_{0}
are different from ours. While
Laha 2021
included a smooth density estimator among their density estimators, this density estimator was non-log-concave and substantially different from our
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
. Consequently, the Hellinger rate analysis of our smooth density estimator, a crucial component in proving Theorem
4.1
, differs from
Laha 2021
. Additionally, the overall calculation in our case is more technically involved than
Laha 2021
’s one-sample case due to additional complexities arising from dealing with two samples.

5
Simulation Study

In this section, we compare the performance of the proposed estimator with that of some other asymptotically efficient estimators available in the literature. We particularly choose the estimators of
Park 1990
and
Beran 1974
as comparators since they involve fewer tuning parameters than most existing semiparametric estimators of
\Delta_{0}
. Additionally, we compute the parametric Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) to serve as a benchmark for evaluating the performance of the semiparametric estimators.

5.1
Simulation design

First, we describe the common simulation setup. We consider the following four schemes for data generation:

1.

g_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
\mu=0
,
\Delta=1
.

2.

g_{0}
is the standard logistic density,
\mu=0
, and
\Delta=1
.

3.

g_{0}
is the standard Laplace density,
\mu=0
and
\Delta=1
.

4.

g_{0}=f_{0}(\cdot+2)
where
f_{0}\sim\text{Gamma}(4,0.5)
. Here, for
a,b>0
, we take
\text{Gamma}(a,b)
to be the Gamma distribution with shape
a
and scale
b
. Since the expectation of
\text{Gamma}(4,0.5)
is
2
, it follows that
g_{0}
is centered. We generate the
X
-observations from
\text{Gamma}(4,0.5)
, which means
\mu=2
. As before, we take
\Delta=1
.

In all the scenarios mentioned above,
g_{0}
is log-concave.
The Fisher information for settings 1, 2, 3, and 4 are 1, 1/3, 1, and 2, respectively.
We take
m=n
, with
n
taking on values from the set
\{40,100,200,500\}
. This results in four configurations for each data generating scheme. For each sample size and each data-generating scheme, we generated 4000 Monte Carlo samples.

For our proposed one step estimators, we choose the preliminary estimators as discussed in Section
3.2.2
. Specifically, we set
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
,
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
, and take the preliminary estimator
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
of
g_{0}
to be the pooled-smoothed estimator
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}}
. We consider four choices for the truncation levels, given by,
\eta=0,0.01,0.001
, and
0.0001
. Note that the level
\eta=0
corresponds to the untruncated one step estimator in (
3.12
). Theorem
4.1
implies that when
m=n
, a 95% confidence interval of
\Delta_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})},\ \widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}+z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}\right),

(5.1)

where
z_{.025}
is the
0.975
th quantile of the standard Gaussian distribution. For the untruncated one step estimator
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}
, we consider the following confidence interval:

\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}-z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}},\ \widehat{\Delta}_{m,n,0}+z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}}}\right),

where
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}
is the untruncated Fisher information estimator in (
3.10
).
Finally, to distinguish our one step estimator from that proposed by
Park 1990
, we refer to these one step estimators as shape-constrained one step estimators.

5.1.1
Comparators

Both of the considered comparators require
\sqrt{n}
-consistent preliminary estimators of
\Delta_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
. We choose
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
as
\overline{X}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
as
\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
to maintain consistency with the shape-constrained one step estimators. Further details on these estimators are provided below.

Park 1990
’s estimator
:

Park 1990
considers the joint estimation of location and scale shifts in the two-sample location-scale model and provides a truncated one step estimator for the location shift.
Since our model does not involve scale-shift, we take the scale-shift to be one while computing the above estimator.
From
Park 1990
, it follows that the resulting estimator of location shift is asymptotically efficient under the model in (
1.1
). The main differences between
Park 1990
’s one step estimator and our
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
are as follows: (a) they take
\widehat{g}_{m,n}
to be a kernel density estimator based on the logistic kernel and (b) they employ a smooth truncation where the influence function estimators gradually decrease to zero outside a compact interval. This procedure necessitates two tuning parameters: an external smoothing parameter
b_{m,n}
for selecting the kernel bandwidth and a truncation parameter
c_{m,n}
.
According to
Park 1990
, the one step estimator is not sensitive to the level of truncation. In line with
Park 1990
, we set
c_{m,n}=8
. However,
Park 1990
reports their one step estimator to be sensitive to the choice of the smoothing parameter
b_{m,n}
and proposes a tuning scheme for it.
Their tuning scheme searches for the
b_{m,n}
that minimizes a bootstrap estimator of the MSE of the one step estimator in the interval
[0.0005,1.0]
. We follow this tuning scheme to select
b_{m,n}
.
We calculate a 95% confidence interval for
Park 1990
’s estimator similarly to (
5.1
).
In this case, we use
Park 1990
’s estimator of
\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
. Their estimator is similar to our truncated Fisher information estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})
except that the score estimates are derived from the kernel density estimator of
g_{0}
and the truncation follows the scheme outlined in their paper.

Beran 1974
’s estimator
:

Beran 1974
uses a rank-based estimator developed by
Kraft and Van Eeden 1970
for estimating
\Delta_{0}
. Their method relies on preliminary estimators and the estimators of the scores and the Fisher information. The scores are computed using a Fourier series expansion, and the Fisher information is estimated using the estimated Fourier coefficients. We calculate a 95% confidence similarly to (
5.1
), where we incorporate the aforementioned estimator of the Fisher information.

Beran 1974
’s estimator involves two tuning parameters: the number of Fourier basis functions
M_{b}
and a smoothing parameter
\vartheta
. The smoothing parameter is required for nonparametrically estimating the Fourier series coefficients.

Beran 1974
does not provide guidance on the selection of these tuning parameters. We considered 75 pairs of
(M_{b},\vartheta)
over the two-dimensional grid
\{10,20,30,40,50\}

\times

\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,1.5\}
. For each pair and each simulation scheme, we estimated the MSE and coverage of
Beran 1974
’s estimator using 400 Monte Carlo samples. For each scheme, two pairs of
(M_{b},\vartheta)
were chosen:
one minimizing the estimated MSE and the other maximizing the estimated coverage of the confidence intervals. We refer to the corresponding estimators as the MSE-tuned
Beran 1974
’s estimator and the coverage-tuned
Beran 1974
’s estimator, respectively.
While our tuning procedure for
Beran 1974
’s estimator requires knowledge of the data generating mechanism, we adopt this tuning scheme to ensure that the resulting estimators offer a competitive benchmark for evaluating our method.

To benchmark the performance of our the semiparametric estimators, we also use
\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{MLE}}
, the parametric MLE of
\Delta_{0}
. It is an oracle estimator in the sense that it assumes the knowledge of the density
g_{0}
. The asymptotic variance of the parametric MLE is
2\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}^{-1}/n
, which leads to the following 95% confidence interval for
\Delta_{0}
based on
\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{MLE}}
:

\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{MLE}}-z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}},\ \widehat{\Delta}_{\text{{MLE}}}+z_{0.025}\sqrt{2n^{-1}/\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}}\right).

(5.2)

In settings 1 and 3, the parametric MLE corresponds to the difference between the means and the medians of the two samples, respectively. For the logistic and gamma densities in settings 3 and 4, the parametric MLE of
\Delta_{0}
is numerically obtained by maximizing the respective log-likelihood functions. In addition to these estimators, the preliminary estimator
\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=\overline{Y}-\overline{X}
is included as a baseline for comparison. To construct an asymptotic confidence interval for the preliminary estimator, we apply the central limit theorem and estimate the corresponding variance term using the method of moments estimator:

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(X_{i}-\overline{x})^{2}}{m+n-2}.

5.1.2
Performance measures

The primary performance measure of interest is the efficiency. We define the efficiency of an estimator
\widehat{\Delta}
of
\Delta_{0}
as follows:

\text{Efficiency}(\widehat{\Delta})=\frac{\text{Var}(\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{MLE}})}{\text{Var}(\widehat{\Delta})},

(5.3)

where
\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{MLE}}
is the parametric MLE. In large samples, it is expected that the efficiency of all estimators would be smaller than or equal to one. Higher values of the efficiency are preferred. To compute the efficiency, we estimate the variance of each estimator, including the parametric MLE, using the 4000 Monte Carlo samples. Figure
1(a)
illustrates the efficiency of various estimators. Figure
1(b)
plots the scaled (by
mn/(m+n)
) MSE. In addition to efficiency, we also provide estimates of the coverage of the confidence intervals using the 4000 Monte Carlo samples. We also investigate the width of the confidence intervals, an important aspect of our comparison study. Assuming that the coverage of an estimator is satisfactory, narrower confidence intervals are preferred. Figures
2(a)
and
2(b)
display the coverage and widths of the confidence intervals, respectively.

5.2
Results

First, we analyze the performance of the shape-constrained estimators. Figures
1
and
2
reveal that the shape-constrained one step estimators with truncation level
\eta\leq 0.001
consistently exhibit high efficiency, high coverage, and low MSE across all experimental settings. In terms of efficiency and MLE, these estimators either outperform or perform comparably to
Park 1990
and
Beran 1974
’s estimators, with a minor exception in the Gaussian case, where they exhibit slightly lower efficiency and higher MSE.
However, even in the Gaussian case, their efficiency remains close to 0.90 in large sample sizes at smaller truncation levels. The shape-constrained estimators achieve coverage rates that are highly competitive with the parametric MLE and noticeably higher than those of

To summarize, when the shape constraints are satisfied, the shape-constrained one step estimators exhibit high coverage and efficiency across all settings. 6
Discussion

In this paper, we illustrate how introducing an additional assumption of log-concavity effectively addresses the challenge of external tuning parameters in the two-sample location shift problem. We establish that the truncated one step estimators are adaptive when the truncation level decays to zero sufficiently slowly. Simulation experiments conducted across a range of scenarios provide evidence suggesting that the optimal truncation level is zero. Based on this insight, we recommend the untruncated one step estimator as the preferred estimator for this problem. This finding aligns with a broader trend in nonparametric problems, encompassing density estimation, regression, location estimation, and Hellinger distance estimation, where introducing shape restrictions has been shown to alleviate the tuning parameter burden
(
Dümbgen and Rufibach 2009
,
Kuchibhotla and Patra 2016
,
Laha 2021
,
Laha et al. 2022
,
Samworth 2017
,
Walther 2009
)
. Our research contributes to this growing body of research, highlighting the benefits of employing shape constraints for tuning parameter-free inference. We discuss two intriguing avenues for future research below.

Extension to higher dimension:
In this case,
X
and
Y
can be
d
-dimensional vectors, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The techniques for obtaining the efficient influence function can be generalized to this multidimensional scenario as long as
d
is fixed. However, the algorithm for computing the smoothed log-concave MLE, proposed by
Chen and Samworth 2013
, tends to be slow in higher dimensions
(
Xu and Samworth 2019
,
Samworth 2017
)
. Nevertheless, recent algorithmic advancements by
Chen, Mazumder and Samworth 2021
in computing high-dimensional log-concave MLE hold promise for extending log-concavity-based inference to higher dimensions.

Misspecified case (non-log-concave
g_{0}
):
Using the log-concave projection theory developed by
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
, one may show that our proposed estimator of
g_{0}
would consistently estimate the log-concave projection of
g_{0}
onto the class
\mathcal{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{LC}
when
g_{0}
is non-log-concave, provided some weak moment conditions hold. In light of
Laha 2019
’s findings in the symmetric location model, we anticipate
\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}
to remain a consistent estimator of
\Delta_{0}
even in the misspecified case. However, it might no longer be asymptotically efficient.

B.1
Step 1: proving
\|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}-g_{0}\|_{1}\to_{a.s.}0

In order to establish the convergence
\|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}-g_{0}\|_{1}\to_{a.s.}0
, we will employ the projection theory framework developed by
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
. Let us denote the empirical distribution function of the combined sample comprising the
X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s and the
Y_{j}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}
’s as
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
. Recall that
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
is the log-concave MLE based on this combined sample. As per the insights of
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
,
\widehat{G}_{m.n}^{\text{pool}}
, the distribution function of
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
, is the projection of
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
onto the class of the distribution functions with logconcave densities. This projection is with respect to the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence; for an in-depth exposition, please refer to
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
. This projection operator adheres to a continuity property concerning Wasserstein distance. Specifically, we can draw upon Theorem 2.15 of
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
to deduce that, for any sequence of distributions
F_{n}
, the convergence
d_{W}(F_{n},G_{0})\to_{a.s.}0
implies the convergence
d_{TV}(F_{n},G_{0})\to_{a.s.}0
, provided that
G_{0}
is non-degenerate with finite first moment. Here
d_{W}
denotes the Wasserstein distance and
d_{TV}
denotes the total variation distance. In our case, the non-degeneracy of
G_{0}
is immediate because it is a distribution function with continuous density. Also, all moments of
G_{0}
is finite by Fact
1
. Consequently, to establish the convergence
|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}-g_{0}|_{1}\to_{a.s.}0
, it is sufficient to show that
d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n},G_{0})\to_{a.s.}0
.

To show the convergence

, it is enough to show that
(

\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
weakly converges to
G_{0}
almost surely and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)\to_{a.s.}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|dG_{0}(x).

(B.1)

We will show the weak convergence first. To that end, we show a stronger result which implies weak convergence. In particular, we show that
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
uniformly converges to
G_{0}
with probability one.
Note that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)=\frac{m\mathbb{F}_{m}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})+n\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})}{m+n},

(B.2)

which implies for any

\absolutevalue{\Fp(x)-G_0(x)}\leq\absolutevalue{\Fm(x+\bmu)-G_0(x)}+\absolutevalue{\mathbb H_n(x+\bmu+\bDelta)-G_0(x)}.

Hence to show
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
converges uniformly to
G_{0}
, it suffices to show that with probability one,
\mathbb{F}_{m}(\cdot+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})
and
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\cdot+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})
uniformly converges
to
G_{0}
with probability one. To prove
\mathbb{F}_{m}(\cdot+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})
uniformly converges to
G_{0}
almost surely, note that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\absolutevalue{\Fm(x+\bmu)-G_0(x)}\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\absolutevalue{\Fm(x+\bmu)-F_0(x+\bmu)}

\displaystyle\ +\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\absolutevalue{F_0(x+\bmu)-G_0(x)},

(B.3)

whose first term converges to zero almost surely by the Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem because
F_{0}
is continuous
(
Van der Vaart 1998
, cf. Theorem 2.1 of)
. For the second term, note that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{0}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-F_{0}(x+\mu_{0})|\leq|\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}|\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f_{0}(x)

which converges to zero almost surely because (a)
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f_{0}(x)<\infty
by Fact
1
and (b)
|\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}|\to_{a.s.}0
since
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
is a strongly consistent estimator of
\mu_{0}
. Since
G_{0}(x)=F_{0}(x+\mu_{0})
, it follows that
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\absolutevalue{F_0(x+\bmu)-G_0(x)}\to_{a.s.}0
. Therefore, (
B.1
) implies that
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\absolutevalue{\Fm(x+\bmu)-G_0(x)}\to_{a.s.}0
.
Similarly, we can prove that with probability one,
\mathbb{H}_{n}(\cdot+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})
uniformly converges to
G_{0}
almost surely. Hence the almost sure weak convergence of
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
to
G_{0}
follows from (
B.2
).

To show
d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n},G_{0})\to_{a.s.}0
, it remains to prove (
B.1
). To show (
B.1
), we first note that (
B.2
) implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)-G_{0}(x))dx=\frac{m}{m+n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d(\mathbb{F}_{m}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-G_{0}(x))dx

\displaystyle\ +\frac{n}{m+n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{n})-G_{0}(x))dx.

(B.4)

Since we assume
m/(m+n),n/(m+n)\leq 1
, it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d(\mathbb{F}_{m}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-G_{0}(x))dx\to_{a.s.}0\quad

(B.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{n})-G_{0}(x))dx\to_{a.s.}0.

(B.6)

We will prove the first convergence only because the second convergence will hold similarly.
To that end, observe that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\edint|x|d\Fm(x+\bmu)-\edint|x|dG_0(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \absolutevalue{\edint|x-\bmu|d\Fm(x)-\edint|x-\kmu|dF_0(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \absolutevalue{\edint\slb|x-\bmu|-|x-\kmu|\srb d\Fm(x)+\edint|x-\kmu|d(\Fm-F_0)(x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \absolutevalue{\edint\slb|x-\bmu|-|x-\kmu|\srb d\Fm(x)}+\absolutevalue{\edint|x-\kmu|d(\Fm-F_0)(x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{ |x-\bmu|-|x-\kmu|}d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)+\absolutevalue{\edint|x-\kmu|d(\Fm-F_0)(x)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\ \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{ \bmu-\kmu}d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)+\absolutevalue{\edint|x-\kmu|d(\Fm-F_0)(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \absolutevalue{\bmu-\kmu}+\absolutevalue{\edint|x-\kmu|d(\Fm-F_0)(x)}

where (a) follows from the triangle inequality.
Since we take
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
, by the strong law of large numbers,

\overline{\mu}_{m,n}\to_{a.s.}\mu_{0}
. The strong law also implies that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x-\mu_{0}|d(\mathbb{F}_{m}-F_{0})(x)\to_{a.s.}0.

Therefore

\absolutevalue{\edint|x|d\Fm(x+\bmu)-\edint|x|dG_0(x)}\to_{a.s.}0.

We can similarly show that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}d\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{n})-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{x}dG_{0}(x)\to_{a.s.}0.

Thus (
B.1
) is proved. Hence, we have shown that
d_{W}(\widehat{G}_{m.n}^{\text{pool}},G_{0})\to_{a.s.}0
, which completes the proof of
\|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}-g_{0}\|_{1}\to_{a.s.}0
.

B.2
Step 2: proving that
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
satisfies Condition
2
with
p=1/4

Suppose
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}
is the pooled sample of the
X_{i}-\mu_{0}
’s and the
Y_{j}-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0}
’s, that is
Z_{i}=X_{i}-\mu_{0}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
and
Z_{i}=Y_{i}-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0}
for
i=m+1,\ldots,N
. Note that
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}G_{0}
.
Let us denote the empirical distribution function of the
Z_{i}
’s by
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
.
Therefore

\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)=\frac{m\mathbb{F}_{m}(x+\mu_{0})+n\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\mu_{0}+\Delta_{0})}{m+n}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Denote by
\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
the log-concave MLE based on the sample
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\}
.

Using triangle inequality on Hellinger distance,
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\leq\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0})+\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0},g_{0})
. Since
\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
is the log-concave MLE based on an i.i.d. sample with log-concave density
g_{0}
, the rate of decay of
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0},g_{0})
can be obtained using existing results on log-concave MLEs. In particular,
Doss and Wellner 2016
implies that
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0},g_{0})=O_{p}(N^{-2/5})
. Hence, the proof will follow if we can show that
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0})=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
. To this end, we will use Theorem 2 of
Barber and Samworth 2020
.

Note that
\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
is the density of the log-concave projection of
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
in the sense of
Dümbgen, Samworth and Schuhmacher 2011
, as explained in Section
B.1
.
In contrast,
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
is the density of the log-concave projection of
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
defined in (
B.2
). Theorem 2 of
Barber and Samworth 2020
bounds
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0})
in terms of the Wasserstein distance between
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
. This theorem states that if
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
are non-degenerate distributions with finite first moment, then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
so that

\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0})\leq C\left(\frac{d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0},\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n})}{\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}}\right)^{1/2}

where for any distribution function
F
,
\epsilon_{F}
is defined as
\epsilon_{F}=E_{F}\left[\absolutevalue{U-E_F[U]}\right]
, with
U
being a random variable with distribution function
F
.

To show
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},\widehat{g}^{\text{pool}}_{0})=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
, it suffices to show that
\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}
is bounded away from zero with probability one and that
d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0},\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n})=O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
. We will first show that
\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}
is bounded away from zero. To this end, note that by triangle inequality,

\absolutevalue{\e_F-E_F[|U|]}=\absolutevalue{E_F[\abs{U-E_F[U]}]-E_F[|U|]}\leq|E_{F}(U)|.

Thus
\epsilon_{F}\geq\mathbb{E}_{F}[|U|]-|E_{F}[U]|
. Hence,

\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[|U|]-|E_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[U]|.

Note that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[|U|]=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}|Z_{i}|}{N}\to_{a.s.}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|g_{0}(x)dx

by the strong law of large numbers because
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}
are i.i.d. with common density
g_{0}
, which has finite first moment because of log-concavity (see Fact
1
). The integral
\int|x|g_{0}(x)dx=0
only if
g_{0}
is identically
0
almost everywhere. However, in that case
g_{0}
can not be an absolutely continuous density function, which is required for the Fisher information to be finite (see the discussion preceeding (
1.3
)). Therefore,
\int|x|g_{0}(x)dx>0
. On the other hand,

E_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[U]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}}{N}\to_{a.s.}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xg_{0}(x)dx,

by another application of the strong law of large numbers. Now
g_{0}
is centered because it is a member of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
defined in (
1.2
), which indicates that
\int xg_{0}(x)dx=0
. Thus, we have shown that

\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[|U|]-|E_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}[U]|\to_{a.s.}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|g_{0}(x)dx>0

which establishes that
\epsilon_{\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}}
is bounded away from zero with probability one. Thus it only remains to show that
d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0},\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n})=O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
.

To show
d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0},\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n})=O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
, we will use an alternative definition of
d_{W}
which is due to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality Theorem

(
Bobkov and Ledoux 2019
, cf. theorem 2.5 of)
. By this theorem the Wasserstein distance between two distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
is

d_{W}(F_{1},F_{2})=\sup_{h\in\text{Lip}_{1}}\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d(F_{1}-F_{2})\bigg|,

where
\text{Lip}_{1}
is the class of all real-valued functions with Lipschitz constant one. Therefore,

d_{W}(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0},\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n})=\sup_{h\in\text{Lip}_{1}}\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)-\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x))\bigg|.

Note that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{m\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x+\mu_{0})+n\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{H}_{n}(x+\mu_{0}+\Delta_{0})}{m+n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{m\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)+n\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})d\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)}{m+n}.

Similarly, we can show that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)=\frac{m\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)+n\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})d\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)}{m+n}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)\bigg|\leq\frac{m}{m+n}\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(h(x-\mu_{0})-h(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})\Big)d\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\ +\frac{n}{m+n}\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(h(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})-h(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})\Big)d\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)\bigg|.

If
h
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant one, then it follows that
|h(x-\mu_{0})-h(x-\overline{\mu}_{m,n})|\leq|\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}|
. Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\text{Lip}_{1}}\bigg|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(x)-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{0}(x)\bigg|\leq\frac{m}{m+n}|\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}|

\displaystyle\ +\frac{n}{m+n}|\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0}|

which is
O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
by central limit theorem because
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}=\overline{X}
and
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}=\overline{Y}
. Hence, the proof of this step follows.

Lemma B.1.

Under the setup of Theorem

, the density estimator

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}},g_{0})\leq d_{TV}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool,sm}},g_{0})

whose first term is bounded by

, and the second term can be bounded as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{\edint\varphi(t/\hatlambda)\dint_{x-t}^x g_0'(z)dzdt }dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x-t}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdt+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{x-t}^{x}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdt\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+t}\varphi(-t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdt+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{x-t}^{x}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdt\bigg)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{x-|t|}^{x+|t|}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdtdx

\displaystyle\ \widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{x-|t|}^{x+|t|}\absolutevalue{g_0'(z)}dzdxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{-1}\varphi(t/\widehat{\beta}_{m,n})\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|g_{0}^{\prime}(z)|dzdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 4\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}\mathbb{E}[|\mathbb{Z}|]\cdot\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|g_{0}^{\prime}(z)|dz,

where
\mathbb{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The proof follows noting

and
\displaystyle\int|g_{0}^{\prime}(z)|dz<\infty
because

which is finite since

Next we will find the rate of decay of the smoothing parameter
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
defined in (
3.16
).

Lemma B.2.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, the
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
defined in (
3.16
) satisfies
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
.

The definition of
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}
in (
3.16
) implies
\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{2}=\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
. Here

\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz-\left(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz\right)^{2}

and
\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}
is the sample variance of the combined sample of the
X_{i}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s and the
Y_{j}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\overline{\mu}_{m,n}
’s.
Recalling from Section
B.1
that the empirical distribution function of this combined sample was denoted to be
\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}
, we obtain that

\widehat{s}_{n}^{2}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)-\left(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}zd\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)\right)^{2}.

Since the combined sample has sample-mean zero, it follows that
\int zd\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)=0
.
Also, we constructed
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
so that
\int z\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz=0
. Hence,

\begin{split}\widehat{\beta}_{m,n}^{2}&=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)\\
&=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{pool}(z)-\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)\right)\\
&\leq\underbrace{\left\lvert\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{pool}(z)-G_{0}(z)\right)\right\rvert}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\left\lvert\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)-G_{0}(z)\right)\right\rvert}_{T_{2}}.\end{split}

(B.7)

Using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in the second step, we obtain that

\begin{split}T_{1}=&\left\lvert\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{pool}(z)-G_{0}(z)\right)\right\rvert\\
=&\left|\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}\left(\sqrt{\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}}(z)-\sqrt{g_{0}}(z)\right)\left(\sqrt{\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}}(z)+\sqrt{g_{0}}(z)\right)dz\right|\\
\leq&\left\{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{4}\left(\sqrt{\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}}(z)+\sqrt{g_{0}}(z)\right)^{2}dz\right\}^{1/2}\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\\
\leq&\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\cdot\left\{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2z^{4}\left(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)+g_{0}(z)\right)dz\right\}^{1/2}\\
=&\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\cdot\left\{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2z^{4}\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)dz+\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2z^{4}g_{0}(z)dz\right\}^{1/2}\\
\leq&\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\cdot\left\{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2z^{4}\left|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)-g_{0}(z)\right|dz+\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}4z^{4}g_{0}(z)dz\right\}^{1/2}.\end{split}

Since
g_{0}
is a log-concave density, it has finite moments by Fact
1
, which implies
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{4}g_{0}(z)dz<\infty
. On the other hand,

\begin{split}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{4}\left|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)-g_{0}(z)\right|dz\leq\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2|z|}\left|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)-g_{0}(z)\right|dz.\end{split}

Now note that Step 1 implies
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
converges to
g_{0}
in total variation norm almost surely. Since convergence in total variation norm ensures weak convergence, the distribution corresponding to
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
converges to
g_{0}
weakly almost surely. Since
\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}
’s are logconcave, the latter implies
(
Cule and Samworth 2010
, Proposition 2)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2|z|}\left|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)-g_{0}(z)\right|dz\to_{a.s.}0.

Therefore
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{4}\left|\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}}(z)-g_{0}(z)\right|\to_{a.s.}0
, and hence

\left\lvert\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\widehat{G}_{m,n}^{pool}(z)-G_{0}(z)\right)\right\rvert=O_{p}\left(\mathcal{H}(\widehat{g}_{m,n}^{\text{pool}},g_{0})\right)=O_{p}(N^{-1/4}).

Therefore, we have shown that
T_{1}=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
.
Now we focus on the second term
T_{2}
in (
B.7
), which equals

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\mathbb{G}^{\text{pool}}_{m,n}(z)-G_{0}(z)\right)=\frac{m}{m+n}\underbrace{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\mathbb{F}_{m}(z+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-G_{0}(z)\right)}_{T_{21}}

\displaystyle\ +\frac{n}{m+n}\underbrace{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(z+\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})-G_{0}(z)\right)}_{T_{22}}.

It suffices to show that
T_{21}=O_{p}(m^{-1/2})
and
T_{22}=O_{p}(m^{-1/2})
. We will show that
T_{21}=O_{p}(N^{-1/4})
because the proof of
T_{22}
will follow similarly. Straightforward algebra shows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\left(\mathbb{F}_{m}(z+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-G_{0}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}d\mathbb{F}_{m}(z+\overline{\mu}_{m,n})-\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}dF_{0}(z+\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=

. Fact
10
implies that given any
\epsilon>0
, we can find a
\sigma>0
so small such that

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\psi_{0}^{\prime 2}(x-\mu_{0})f_{0}(x)dx<\epsilon
for any
P_{0}
-measurable set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}
provided
P_{0}(\mathcal{B})<\sigma
.
Thus it is enough to show that
\int_{\mathcal{C}_{m,n}}f_{0}(x)dx=o_{p}(1)
.

Suppose
G_{0}^{-1}(0)>-\infty
and
G_{0}^{-1}(1)<\infty
.
Since
\overline{\mu}_{m,n}\to_{p}\mu_{0}
,
\widehat{\xi}_{1}\to_{p}G_{0}^{-1}(0)
and
\widehat{\xi}_{2}\to_{p}G_{0}^{-1}(1)
by Lemma
D.2
, and
F_{0}
is continuous, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{C_{m,n}}f_{0}(x)dx=

\displaystyle\ 1-F_{0}(\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\widehat{\xi}_{2})+F_{0}(\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\widehat{\xi}_{1})

\displaystyle\to_{p}

\displaystyle\ 1-F_{0}(\mu_{0}+G_{0}^{-1}(1))+F_{0}(\mu_{0}+G_{0}^{-1}(0))

by continuous mapping theorem. Since
\mu_{0}+G_{0}^{-1}(1)=F_{0}^{-1}(1)

and
\mu_{0}+G_{0}^{-1}(0)=F_{0}^{-1}(0)
, it is clear that
\int_{\mathcal{C}_{m,n}}f_{0}(x)dx=o_{p}(1)
in this case. If
G_{0}^{-1}(0)=-\infty
or
G_{0}^{-1}(1)=\infty
, then continuous mapping theorem may not be applicable. However, in these cases,
\widehat{\xi}_{1}\to-\infty
or
\widehat{\xi}_{2}\to\infty
, respectively, with probability approaching one. Using the above, we can show that
\int_{\mathcal{C}_{m,n}}f_{0}(x)dx=o_{p}(1)
still holds in these cases. Therefore, we have shown that

T_{3N}=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)+o_{p}(1),

where
\displaystyle\int\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}})
by the central limit theorem.

Fourth step: combining
A
and
B

Combining the last three steps with (
C
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}A\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})=T_{1N}+T_{2N}+T_{3N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0})\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})+\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)g_{0}(x)dx

\displaystyle\ +\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)+o_{p}(1).

Since
\sqrt{m}\int\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}})
, the random variable
\sqrt{m}\int\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)
is
O_{p}(1)
.
Also noting
\sqrt{n/N}=\sqrt{1-\lambda}+o(1)
, we deduce that

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)=\sqrt{(1-\lambda)m}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d\mathbb{G}_{m}(x)+o_{p}(1)

Recalling
\mathbb{G}_{m}=\sqrt{m}(\mathbb{F}_{m}-F_{0})
, we thus obtain that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}A\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})=

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\mu}_{m,n}-\mu_{0})\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})+\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)g_{0}(x)dx

\displaystyle\ +\sqrt{(1-\lambda)m}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d(\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)-F_{0}(x))+o_{p}(1)

By symmetry, the term
B
defined in (
C
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}B\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})=\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\mu}_{m,n}+\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})

\displaystyle\ +\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\xi}_{1}}^{\widehat{\xi}_{2}}\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)g_{0}(x)dx+\sqrt{\lambda n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)-H_{0}(x))+o_{p}(1).

Note that

\sqrt{n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)-H_{0}(x))\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}).

Thus

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})(A-B)=\sqrt{(1-\lambda)m}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d(\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)-F_{0}(x))

\displaystyle\ -\sqrt{\lambda n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)-H_{0}(x))-\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\Delta_{0})\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})+o_{p}(1).

Let us denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}=

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{(1-\lambda)m}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0})d(\mathbb{F}_{m}(x)-F_{0}(x))

\displaystyle\ -\sqrt{\lambda n}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x-\mu_{0}-\Delta_{0})d(\mathbb{H}_{n}(x)-H_{0}(x)).

Since the
X
-sample and
Y
-sample are independent, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, it follows that

\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}).

Thus we have obtained that

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}(A-B)=\frac{\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}-\sqrt{\dfrac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\Delta_{0})+o_{p}(1)

From (
C
) we obtain that

(\sqrt{mn/N})(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})=(\sqrt{mn/N})(A-B),

leading to

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-\overline{\Delta}_{m,n})=\frac{\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}-\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}(\overline{\Delta}_{m,n}-\Delta_{0})+o_{p}(1).

A change of sides yield

\sqrt{\frac{mn}{N}}(\widehat{\Delta}_{m,n}-\Delta_{0})=\frac{\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}}{\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})}+o_{p}(1).

Since Lemma
D.11
implies
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})\to_{d}\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}
, we obtain
\mathbb{Z}_{m,n}/\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{m,n}(\eta_{m,n})\to_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}^{-1})
by Slutskey’s Lemma. Therefore, the proof follows.

\Box

D
Auxilliary lemmas for proving Theorem
A.1

In this section, we will prove some auxiliary lemmas required for proving Theorem
A.1
.
We will assume that
g_{0}
satisfies Assumption
A
in all the lemmas below.

Some of the lemmas in this section will rely on quantile functions. We will repeatedly use some common results regarding the quantile function, which is the genralized of a distribution function. We list these results below for the sake of convenience. First, we state a Fact collecting some common properties of the quantile function.

Fact 2
.

Suppose
F
is a distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
t\in(0,1)
. Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

F(F^{-1}(t))=t
if
F
is continuous.

(ii)

F^{-1}(F(x))\leq x
if and only if
x\geq F^{-1}(0)
.

(iii)

F^{-1}(F(x))=x
if
F
is strictly increasing in a neighborhood of
x
.

(iv)

F^{-1}
is strictly increasing on
(0,1)
if and only if
F
is continuous.

For a proof of Fact
2
, see Lemma A.3 of
Bobkov and Ledoux 2019
. The next fact pertains to the diffrentiability of the quantile function
F^{-1}
.

Fact 3

(Proposition A.18 of
Bobkov and Ledoux 2019
)
.

Consider a density
f
supported on an (potentially unbounded) interval and let
F
be the corresponding distribution function. Then
F^{-1}
is strictly increasing, and
F^{-1}(q_{2})-F^{-1}(q_{1})=\int_{q_{1}}^{q_{2}}dt/f(F^{-1}(t))
for all
0<q_{1}<q_{2}<1
.

Suppose
f
is a log-concave density with distribution function
F
. Since
F
is continuous,
F^{-1}
is strictly increasing on
(0,1)
and
F(F^{-1}(t))=t
for any
t\in(0,1)
by Fact
2
. Note that
\text{supp}(f)=\text{dom}(\log f)
. Since
\log f
is concave, its domain is an interval, which implies
\text{supp}(f)
is also an interval
(
Hiriart-Urruty and Lemaréchal 2004
, cf. pp. 74 of)
. Then
f>0
on
J(F)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:0<F(x)<1\}

(
Dümbgen, Kolesnyk and Wilke 2017
, cf. Theorem 1(iv) of)
. Therefore,
J(F)=\text{int}(\text{supp}(f))=\text{int}(\text{dom}(\psi_{0}))
, and
F
is strictly increasing on
J(F)
. Hence, for any
x\in J(F)
,
F^{-1}(F(x))=x
by Fact
2
(iii).
Since the density
f
is supported on an interval, by
Fact
3
,
F^{-1}
is strictly increasing and differentiable with derivative
1/f(F^{-1}(t))
on
(0,1)
.

0\geq\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(a)\geq\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(b)
),
\inf_{[a,b]}|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)|
is attained at
b
(or
a
). Now we consider two scenarios:

1.

\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{1}\right)\geq 0
:
Because
\widehat{\xi}_{1}=\tilde{G}_{m,n}^{-1}(\eta_{m,n}/2)
, it follows that
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{G}_{m,n}^{-1}(\eta_{m,n}/4)\right)\geq\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{1}\right)\geq 0
and

\inf_{\eta\in[\frac{q}{2},q]}\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(\tilde{G}^{-1}_{m,n}(\eta))\right|=\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})\right|,

and hence
\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(\tilde{\xi}_{1})\right|
is
O_{p}(N^{p/5})
.

2.

\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{1}\right)<0
:
Then
0>\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{1}\right)\geq\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(G_{m,n}^{-1}(3q/2)\right)
and hence

\inf_{\eta\in[q,\frac{3q}{2}]}\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(\tilde{G}^{-1}_{m,n}(\eta))\right|=\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi_{1}}\right)\right|=O_{p}(N^{p/5}).

Combining the cases, we get
\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi_{1}}\right)\right|=O_{p}(N^{p/5})
. Similarly one can also find
\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi_{2}}\right)\right|=O_{p}(N^{p/5})
. Since
\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}
is non-increasing, its supremum or infrimum over an interval is attained at one of the end-points. Therefore
\sup_{x\in[\tilde{\xi}_{1},\tilde{\xi}_{2}]}|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}(x)|=\max\left\{\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{1}\right)\right|,\left|\widehat{\psi}_{m,n}^{\prime}\left(\tilde{\xi}_{2}\right)\right|\right\}=O_{p}(N^{p/5})
.
The rest of the proof follows from Lemma
D.4
.
∎

Technical facts

Below we list some facts which have been used repeatedly in our proofs. We start with some facts on the Hellinger distance.

Fact 4
.

Let
F
and
G
be two distribution functions with densities
f
and
g
, respectively. Then
d_{TV}(F,G)\leq\sqrt{2}\mathcal{H}(f,g)
and
\mathcal{H}^{2}(f,g)\leq d_{TV}(F,G)
.

Fact 5

(Fact 15 of
Laha 2021
)
.

For a log-concave density
g_{0}
with
\mathcal{I}_{g_{0}}<\infty
,
\mathcal{H}(g_{0}(\mathord{\cdot}+y),g_{0})=O(|y|)
.

The next facts pertain to the convergence of random variables.

Fact 6

(Theorem 5.7 (ii) of
Shorack 2000
)
.

Consider a random sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and a random variable
X
. If
X_{n}\to_{p}X
, then there exists a subsequence
n_{k}
for which
X_{n_{k}}\to_{a.s.}X
.

Fact 7

(Theorem 5.7 (vii) of
Shorack 2000
)
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and a random variable
X
. Then
X_{n}\to_{p}X
if and only if for every subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, there is a further subsequence
\{n_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
such that
X_{n_{r}}\to_{a.s.}X
.

Fact 8
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of distribution functions and
F
be distribution function such that
\|F_{n}-F\|_{\infty}\to 0
. Let
F
have a density, denoted by
f
. Then for
t\in\text{int}(\text{supp}(f))
, we have
|F_{n}^{-1}(t)-F^{-1}(t)|\to 0
.

The proof follows from Lemma A.5 of
Bobkov and Ledoux 2019
.
∎

The next fact is an empirical process result.

Fact 9
.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a class of measurable functions
h
with
\int h^{2}dP_{0}<\epsilon^{2}
where
\|h\|_{\infty}\leq M
for some positive constant
M
. Then

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\lesssim J_{[\ ]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P_{0}))\bigg(1+\frac{MJ_{[\ ]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P_{0}))}{\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{n}}\bigg),

where

J_{[\ ]}(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P_{0}))=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\sqrt{1+\log N_{[\ ]}(\epsilon^{\prime},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P_{0}))}d\epsilon^{\prime}.

The proof follows from Lemma 3.4.2 of
Van der Vaart 1998
.
∎

The following is a property of integrable functions.

Fact 10

(Exercise 16.18, pp. 223 of
Billingsley 1986
)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|h|dP<\infty
for a measurable function
h
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\sigma>0
such that
\int_{\mathcal{B}}|h|dP<\epsilon
for any
\mathbb{P}
-measurable set
\mathcal{B}
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B})<\sigma
.
