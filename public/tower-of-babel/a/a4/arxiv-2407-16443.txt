Bounds and Algorithms for Alphabetic Codes and Binary Search Trees
R. Bruno, R. De Prisco, A. De Santis, and U. Vaccaro

Abstract
Alphabetic codes and binary search trees are combinatorial structures that abstract search procedures in ordered sets endowed with probability distributions. In this paper, we design new linear-time algorithms to construct alphabetic codes, and we show that the obtained codes are not too far from being optimal. Moreover, we exploit our results on alphabetic codes to provide new bounds on the average cost of optimal binary search trees. Our results improve on the best-known bounds on the average cost of optimal binary search trees present in the literature.



I
Introduction

The Theory of Variable-Length Codes and Search Theory are
strongly intertwined. In fact,
any
search procedure
that identifies objects in a given space, by sequentially
executing appropriate tests naturally defines a variable-length
encoding of elements in the space. To wit,
one can encode each possible test outcome with
a different symbol of a
finite alphabet, and the concatenation of
the (encoded) test outcomes, leading to the identification
of an object
x
, gives a
bona fide
encoding of

x
, for any
x
in the search space.

More specifically, binary alphabetic codes and binary search trees are basic combinatorial
structures that naturally arise in Search Theory
[
2
,
3
,
15
]
.
In fact, alphabetic codes are
equivalent
to search procedures (in ordered sets) that operate
through comparison queries with binary results,
while binary search trees correspond
to search procedures that use comparison queries whose outcome is ternary.

Alphabetical codes have been extensively studied in the Information Theory
community (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
25
,
28
,
29
]
and references therein quoted). Equally, binary search trees have been
studied in the Computer Science community, because of the important application scenarios where they appear (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
26
]
and references therein quoted).
The two survey papers
[
1
,
24
]
contain many more references.

It is well known that optimal binary alphabetic codes (i.e., with minimum average length)
can be constructed in time
O(m\log m)

[
13
]
, where
m
is the number of codewords,
and that optimal binary search trees (again, with minimum possible average path length)
can be constructed in time
O(n^{2})

[
18
]
, where
n
is the number of nodes
in the tree. Most of the research activity
that followed
[
13
]
and
[
18
]
has concentrated on the following issues: constructing close-to-optimal binary alphabetic codes (equivalently, close-to-optimal binary search trees)
via
linear-time
algorithms, and providing explicit upper bounds on the cost (average
path length) of the constructed objects. In this paper,
we provide novel upper bounds on the cost of
optimal binary alphabetic codes and optimal binary search trees.
Our bounds improve on the best bounds known in the literature.
Our new results are obtained
by accurately analyzing novel linear-time construction algorithms.

II
Preliminaries

Let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
be a set of symbols,
ordered according to a given total order relation
\prec
, that is, for which it holds that

s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}
.
A binary alphabetical code is an order-preserving
mapping
w:\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}\mapsto\{0,1\}^{+}
,
where the order relation on the set of all
binary strings
\{0,1\}^{+}

is the standard alphabetical order. We denote by
C
the set of
codewords
C=\{w(s):s\in S\}
, and by

\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m})
the probability distribution
on the symbols
in
S
, that is,
\phi_{i}
is the probability of the
i^{th}
symbol
s_{i}
.
For each
s_{i}\in S
we denote by

w(s_{i})
the codeword associated to
s_{i}
, and by

\ell(w(s_{i}))
, or simply by
\ell_{i}
, the length of
w(s_{i})
.

Given an alphabetic code
C
, an order-preserving mapping

w:s\in\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}\mapsto w(s)\in\{0,1\}^{+}
, and
a probability distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m})
on
S
,
we denote the average code length of the code
C
by

\mathbb{E}[C]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\,\ell_{i}.

In the following sections, we will occasionally see an alphabetic code as a binary tree
T
where each edge is labeled either by bit 0 or 1,
and each leaf
of the tree corresponds to a different symbol
s\in S
.
Sometimes, it will be convenient to identify an arbitrary leaf
s_{i}

of the tree
T
with
the probability
\phi_{i}
of the symbol
s_{i}\in S
that is associated to the
leaf.
The codeword
w(s)
for the symbol
s\in S
is equal to
the concatenation of all the 0’s and 1’s
in the path from the root of
T
to the leaf associated with
s
. The order-preserving property
of alphabetic codes implies that the leaves of
T
, read from the leftmost
leaf to
the rightmost one, appear in the order
s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}
.
As said before, alphabetic codes, in their binary tree representation,
naturally correspond to a search procedure that makes use of comparison
queries with binary outcomes.

Binary search trees represent, in a sense, a generalization of binary
trees associated to alphabetic codes, since the former can take into account
both successful and unsuccessful searches. More precisely,
any comparison-based search algorithm on an ordered list of elements
x_{1}\prec\dots\prec x_{n}
can be represented as a binary search tree, with an internal node
associated to each element
x_{i}
and a leaf associated to each of the
n+1
gaps
(-\infty,x_{1}),(x_{1},x_{2}),\dots,(x_{n-1},x_{n}),(x_{n},+\infty)

among the
x_{i}
’s. Let
x
be an element that we want to seek for in the list.
Each internal node represents a comparison operation between
x
and
the content
x_{i}
of the node. The outcome of
the comparison can be either
x\prec x_{i}
,
x=x_{i}
or
x\succ x_{i}

(see Section 6.2 of
[
18
]
). Thus, the internal nodes correspond to successful searches, and the leaves to the unsuccessful ones.

The cost of a binary search tree
T
is defined as the average number of questions needed to identify an arbitrary input element
x
. More precisely,
given a probability distribution
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2\,n+1})=(p_{0},q_{1},p_{2},\dots,q_{n},p_{n})
, where the probabilities of successful
searches are
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
, and
p_{0},\dots,p_{n}
are
the probabilities
of unsuccessful searches, the cost of the binary search tree
T
, for the probability distribution
\sigma
, is equal to

\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\,\ell(q_{i})+\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}\,\ell(p_{i}),

(1)

where
\ell(q_{i})
denotes the level of
q_{i}
in the tree, i.e., the number of nodes from the root of
T
to
q_{i}
, whereas
\ell(p_{i})
is the level of the parent of
p_{i}
in the tree
[
18
]
. We stipulate that the level of the root of
T
is equal to 1.

Note that when the probabilities of successful searches,
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
, are
all 0’s, the problem of finding an optimal binary search tree for the probability distribution
\sigma
is equivalent to the problem of finding an optimal alphabetic code for the probability distribution
\phi=(p_{0},\dots,p_{n})
.

III
Our contributions in perspective

Alphabetic codes have been extensively
studied. In this section, we limit ourselves to discussing
the work that is most closely related to our findings.
We will present our results
in Section
IV
and Section
V
.

In their classic paper
[
9
]
,
Gilbert and Moore designed a

O(m^{3})
time dynamic programming algorithm for the construction of optimal alphabetic codes (that is, of
minimum average length), where
m
is the number of symbols.
Hu and Tucker
[
13
]
improved Gilbert and Moore’s
result by providing an algorithm of time complexity
O(m\log m)
. Subsequent
studies have mostly concentrated on
the problem of giving linear time algorithms for the construction
of almost optimal alphabetic codes and
in providing explicit upper bounds on the average length of
the constructed codes. It is clear that the derived bounds are
upper bounds on the length of optimal alphabetic codes, as well.

Gilbert and Moore
[
9
]
gave a linear time algorithm
to construct an alphabetic code for a set
of symbols
S
, with associated probability distribution
\phi
, whose average length is less than

H(\phi)+2
. Here,
H(\phi)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\log\phi_{i}
is the
Shannon entropy of the distribution
\phi
. Since the
average length of any alphabetic code is lower bounded by
H(\phi)
,
one gets that
Gilbert and Moore’s
codes are at most two bits away from the optimum. We note that Gilbert and Moore’s upper bound cannot be improved, unless one has some additional information on the probability distribution
\phi
.
In fact, one can see that
the average length of the best alphabetical code
for a set of three symbols
s_{1}\prec s_{2}\prec s_{3}
, with probability distribution

\phi=(\epsilon,1-2\epsilon,\epsilon)
, is equal to
2-\epsilon
. On the other hand,

H(\phi)
can be arbitrarily small.
Remarkably, the average length of the
Huffman code for the same
\phi=(\epsilon,1-2\epsilon,\epsilon)

is equal to
1+2\epsilon
, thus there can be a difference of
(approximatively)
1 bit between the average length of the best alphabetical code and that of
the Huffman code, for the same
\phi
.
The general characterization of the “shape” of worst-case
probability distributions for alphabetical codes is given
in the paper
[
16
]
.

Horibe
[
12
]
provided a better
upper bound than the
H(\phi)+2
bound
of Gilbert and Moore, by giving an algorithm to construct
alphabetic codes of average length less than

H(\phi)+2-(m+2)\phi_{\min},

(2)

where
\phi_{\min}
is the
smallest probability of
\phi
.

Nakatsu
[
25
]
claimed a different upper bound
on the minimum average
length of alphabetical codes.
Successively, Sheinwald
[
28
]
pointed out
a gap in the analysis carried out in
[
25
]
.

Yeung
[
29
]
also improved the

H(\phi)+2
bound
of Gilbert and Moore, by
proving the following upper bound on the average length
L_{\min}
of
optimal binary alphabetic codes:

\displaystyle L_{\min}

\displaystyle\leq H(\phi)+2-\phi_{1}\left(2-\log\phi_{1}-\lceil-\log\phi_{1}\rceil\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\phi_{m}\left(2-\log\phi_{m}-\lceil-\log\phi_{m}\rceil\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\leq H(\phi)+2-\phi_{1}-\phi_{m},

(4)

where
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{m}
are the probabilities of the first and the last symbol of the ordered set of symbols
S
, respectively.
In general, the bounds (
2
) and (
3
) are not
comparable, in the sense that there are
probability distributions
\phi
for which (
2
) is better than
(
3
), and probability distributions
\phi
for which
the opposite holds.

A new upper bound on the average length of optimal alphabetic codes was recently given by Dagan
et al.

[
4
]
.
Their bound claims that

\displaystyle L_{\min}

\displaystyle\leq H(\phi)+2-\left(\phi_{1}+\phi_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\min(\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1})\right)

(5)

\displaystyle=H(\phi)+1-\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{m}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|\phi_{i}-\phi_{i+1}|.

(6)

While the upper bound (
5
) is correct, in Remark
1

(which will be given in Section
IV
)
we point out
an unresolved issue in the analysis given in
[
4
]
that leads to (
5
).

Our results for alphabetic codes consist of a series of improvements to the upper bound (
5
) of Dagan
et al.
on the average length
of optimal alphabetic codes
L_{\min}
.
For instance,
we will prove that

\displaystyle L_{\min}

\displaystyle\leq H(\phi)+2-\phi_{1}\,(2-\log_{2}\phi_{1}-\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{1}\rceil)

\displaystyle\quad-\phi_{m}\,(2-\log_{2}\phi_{m}-\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{m}\rceil)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\min(\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1}).

(7)

More importantly,
we design a
O(m)
time algorithm to construct alphabetic
codes of average length at most the right-hand side of (
7
).
The
O(m)
time algorithm that we design will be instrumental in deriving
the results on binary search trees of Section
V
.
We derive further improvements to (
5
)
in Section
IV
.

In Section
V
we turn our attention to binary search trees.
Binary search trees have been extensively
investigated (e.g.,
[
5
,
21
,
22
,
26
]
).
Knuth
[
17
]
gave an
O(n^{2})
time and
O(n^{2})
space complexity
algorithm to construct an optimum binary
search tree, i.e., a binary search tree with minimum cost, as defined in (
1
).
The quadratic complexity of the algorithm designed in

[
17
]
may be prohibitive in many
applications, as Mehlhorn remarked in
[
21
]
.
Therefore,
Mehlhorn
[
21
]
proposed a linear time algorithm to construct a binary search tree for an arbitrary probability distribution

\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{2n+1})=(p_{0},q_{1},p_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n},p_{n}),

where the
p_{i}
’s are probabilities of unsuccessful searches
and the
q_{i}
’s are probabilities of successful searches,
whose cost is not greater than

H(\sigma)+1+\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}.

(8)

De Prisco and De Santis
[
5
]
proposed a different linear time algorithm for
constructing binary search trees and they claimed an upper bound
on their cost given by

H(\sigma)+1-p_{0}-p_{n}+p_{\max},

(9)

where
p_{\max}
is the maximum value among
p_{0},\dots,p_{n}
.
Unfortunately, the bound (
9
) does not hold in general, as pointed out in
[
4
,
19
]
.

In Section
V
we correct the analysis
of the algorithm derived in
[
5
]
, and we explicitly
estimate the cost of the produced binary search trees.
The simplest of our bounds states
that the cost
\mathbb{E}[T]
of the tree
T
constructed by the
algorithm of
[
5
]
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]

\displaystyle\leq H(\sigma)+1+\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}-\Biggl(p_{0}+p_{n}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\min(p_{i},q_{i+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(q_{i},p_{i})+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\min(p_{i},p_{i+1})\Biggr)

\displaystyle=H(\sigma)+1+\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}-\Biggl(1-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{|p_{i}-q_{i+1}|}{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|q_{i}-p_{i}|}{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{|p_{i}-p_{i+1}|}{2}\Biggr).

(10)

It is apparent that (
10
) significantly improves
the Melhorn bound
(
8
). We derive additional and better upper bounds than (
10
)
in Section
V
. Clearly, our results also
represent upper limits on the cost of optimal
binary search trees. The new bounds on the
cost of binary search trees are crucially dependent on the new results on alphabetic codes
that we derive in Section
IV
.

IV
On Alphabetic codes

We first develop some useful machinery.
Given a probability distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
, let us denote with

\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{2\,m-1})=(\phi_{1},0,\phi_{2},\dots,0,\phi_{m})

its
partially extended
distribution, and with

\nu\,^{\prime}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{2\,m+1})=(0,\phi_{1},0,\phi_{2},\dots,0,\phi_{m},0)

its
fully extended
distribution.

We also need some auxiliary tools and results from Nakatsu
[
25
]
.

Definition 1
.

[
25
]

Let
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\rangle
be a list of positive integers.
For a binary fraction
x
and integer
i\geq 1
,
let the function
trunc
be defined as

\mbox{\tt trunc}(i,x)=\frac{\lfloor 2^{i}\,x\rfloor}{2^{i}}

(11)

that is,
\mbox{\tt trunc}(i,x)
is the fraction obtained by considering only the first

i
bits in the binary representation of
x
.

Let
\alpha_{i}=\min(\ell_{i-1},\ell_{i})
, for
i=2,\dots,m
.
The recursive function
sum
is defined as

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)=\begin{cases}\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-\alpha_{i}}&\text{if }i\geq 2,\\
0&\text{if }i=1.\end{cases}

(12)

To better comprehend the functions
trunc
and
sum
defined above, let us
work out a small numerical example.
Let
L=\{4,2,3,3\}
be a list of integers. We have
\alpha_{2}=\min(4,2)=2
,
\alpha_{3}=\min(2,3)=2
and
\alpha_{4}=\min(3,3)=3
.
Now, we can compute the value of
sum
for
L
. By definition
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1)=0
. It holds that

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{2},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(2,0)+2^{-2}=2^{-2},

since
\mbox{\tt trunc}(2,0)
takes the value
whose binary expansion is the same of the first 2 bits of the binary representation of
0
(that are
all
0
’s). Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L,3)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{3},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2))+2^{-\alpha_{3}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(2,2^{-2})+2^{-2}=2^{-2}+2^{-2}=2^{-1},

since
\mbox{\tt trunc}(2,2^{-2})
takes the value whose binary expansion is the same of the first 2 bits of the binary representation of
2^{-2}=\frac{1}{4}
,
which correspond to the same value
2^{-2}
since the binary representation of
\frac{1}{4}
is
0\,\frac{1}{2}+1\,\frac{1}{4}
.
Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L,4)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{4},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,3))+2^{-\alpha_{4}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(3,2^{-1})+2^{-3}=2^{-1}+2^{-3}.

Intuitively, when
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)<1
, one can see that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)

enjoys the property of being the smallest value strictly greater than
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)
that differs from
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)
on
at least the
\alpha_{i}^{th}

bit of their binary expansion, where
\alpha_{i}=\min(\ell_{i-1},\ell_{i})
. Thus, by denoting with
w_{i}
the first
\ell_{i}
bits of
the binary expansion of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)
and with
w_{i-1}
the first
\ell_{i-1}
bits of
the binary expansion of

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)
, the property ensures that
w_{i-1}

comes
before
w_{i}
in the
standard alphabetic order among binary sequences.
Moreover, neither
w_{i}
is a prefix of
w_{i-1}

nor
w_{i-1}
is a prefix of
w_{i}
.

Nakatsu
[
25
]
proved the following important result
(an equivalent result was independently given by Yeung
[
29
]
).

Theorem 1
.

[
25
]

Let
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\rangle
be
a list of integers, associated with the ordered
symbols
s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}
.
There exists an alphabetical code for which the
codeword assigned to symbol
s_{i}
has length
\ell_{i}
,
for each
i=1,\ldots,m
,
if and only if
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)<1
.

Nakatsu proved the sufficient part of Theorem
1
by showing that the
first
\ell_{i}
digits of the binary representation of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)
,
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, (under the hypothesis that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)<1
)
give the alphabetic code
for the ordered
set of
symbols
s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}
.

In the following Lemma
2
we show that an alphabetic code,
whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem
1
, can be constructed
in time
O(m)
.
We first need the following technical result, whose proof is given in Appendix A.

Lemma 1
.

Let
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\rangle
be a list of integers
such that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)<1
, let

i
,
j
be integers
such that
1\leq i<j\leq m
, and let
\{\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i),\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i+1),\dots,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,j)\}
be a subset of consecutive elements of
\{\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1),\dots,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)\}
.
For each
k=i,\dots,j-1
,
let
t_{k}
be the
smallest
integer
s

for which the binary expansion of

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,k)
differs from the binary
expansion of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,k+1)
on the
s^{th}
bit, and
define
t_{ij}=\min_{i\leq k<j}t_{k}
. Then, it holds that:

1.

t_{ij}=\lceil-\log_{2}(\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)\oplus\mbox{\tt sum}(L,j))\rceil,

(13)

where
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)\oplus\mbox{\tt sum}(L,j)
is the
numerical
value
whose binary expansion is the result of the XOR operation between the binary expansions of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)
and
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,j)
, and

2.

there exists an index
z\in\{i,\ldots,j-1\}
such that

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,z)<\mbox{\tt trunc}(t_{ij},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-t_{ij}}

and

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,z+1)\geq\mbox{\tt trunc}(t_{ij},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-t_{ij}}.

For completeness, we recall that the XOR
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)\oplus\mbox{\tt sum}(L,j)

is easily computable, in most programming languages, with a single built-in instruction.
Armed with Lemma
1
, we now prove our constructive result.
To avoid overburdening the notation, when the pair of integers
(i,j)

in (
13
)
is clear from the context, we will simply denote

t_{ij}
with
t
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\rangle
be a list of integers, associated with the ordered symbols
s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}
. If
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)<1
, then one can construct in
O(m)
time an alphabetic code
C
for which the codeword assigned to symbol
s_{i}
has length at most
\min(\ell_{i},m-1)
, for each
i=1,\dots,m.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we design a
O(m)
time algorithm to
construct the binary tree associated with the code

C
, that is, the binary tree whose root-to-leaves paths give
the codewords of
C
.

It is useful to recall that Nakatsu
[
25
, Theorem 1]
showed that the code constructed by taking the first
\ell_{i}
bits of the binary expansion of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)
as the codeword associated to the symbol
s_{i}
, for each
i=1,\dots,m
, is alphabetic.
Our algorithm constructs the binary code tree
by using a

subset
of the first
\ell_{i}
bits of the binary expansion of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)
, for each
i=1,\dots,m
.
The algorithm builds the desired tree
in a top-down fashion, proceeding by iterated bisections of the set

{\cal S}=\{\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1),\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2),\ldots,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m)\}.

(14)

Initially, the root of the tree is associated to the
whole set

{\cal S}
.
To perform the first bisection, we proceed as follows.

For each
a=1,\dots,m-1
,
let
t_{a}
be the smallest integer for which the binary expansions of the pair
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,a)
and
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,a+1)
differ on the
t_{a}^{th}
bit, and let
t=t_{1m}=\min_{1\leq a<m}t_{a}
. From Lemma
1
, we have that
t=\lceil-\log_{2}(\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1)\oplus\mbox{\tt sum}(L,m))\rceil
.
We now compute the index
k\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
for which it holds that

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,k)<\mbox{\tt trunc}(t,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-t}

(15)

and

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,k+1)\geq\mbox{\tt trunc}(t,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-t}.

(16)

We associate the left child of the root to
the subset

The procedure presented above for the construction of the binary tree can be implemented to work in

time and space, by exploiting a technique
devised in
[

.
In fact, the
crucial operation in Line 9 of Algorithm

can be implemented in
O(\log\min(s,j-s))
time, where
s=k-i+1
, by first checking
if
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,r)<\mbox{\tt trunc}(t,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-t}
with

to discover whether the index

or to

. Successively,
we use an exponential search to find the interval in which the value

and, finally, a binary search in such interval to find

.
More precisely, we proceed in the following way: if
k\in\{i,\dots,r-1\}
, we check the validity of the inequality

\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i+2^{v})<\mbox{\tt trunc}(t,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-t}
in Line 9 for increasing

Remark 1

In the proof of the upper bound (

Successively, the authors prune the binary tree representing the alphabetical code

The codeword lengths associated with the null

by a numeric example. Successively,

is equal to 3.

with lengths of the codewords

for

We now present our new results for alphabetic codes. We will divide
our analysis into two cases: The first is about dyadic distributions,
the second case is for everything else.
We recall that a dyadic distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
is a
probability distribution where each
\phi_{i}
is equal to
2^{-k_{i}}
, for suitable integers

Theorem 2
.

For any dyadic probability distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})

on the symbols
s_{1}\prec\dots\prec s_{m}

construct in linear time an alphabetic code
C

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C]{\leq}H(\phi)+1-\phi_{1}-\phi_{m}.

(20)

Proof.

The idea of the proof is simple and is based on
Yeung’s analysis
[
29
, Thm. 5]
. Yeung proved that, given a probability distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
, there exists
an alphabetic code
C
for
\phi
with lengths of the codewords
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\rangle
equal to:

\ell_{i}=\begin{cases}\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{i}\rceil&\text{ if either }i=1\text{ or }i=m,\\
\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{i}\rceil+1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

From Lemma
2
, the code
C
can be constructed in
O(m)
time.

Using the additional hypothesis that
\phi

\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{i}\rceil=-\log_{2}\phi_{i}
for each

with lengths

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C]

\displaystyle{\leq}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\,\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}\,(-\log_{2}\phi_{1})+\phi_{m}\,(-\log_{2}\phi_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\phi_{i}\,(-\log_{2}\phi_{i}+1)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\,\log_{2}\phi_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=H(\phi)+1-\phi_{1}-\phi_{m}.

∎

\displaystyle=H(\phi)+2-2\phi_{1}-2\phi_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\min(\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1}).

. For a given dyadic probability distribution

, let us define

Corollary 4.2
.

For any
probability distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
on the symbols

, with
{\phi_{1}},\phi_{2},\phi_{m-1},{\phi_{m}}>0

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq}H(\phi)+2-\phi_{1}(2-\log_{2}\phi_{1}-\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{1}\rceil)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\min(\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1})

\displaystyle-\phi_{m}\max(0,\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{m}\rceil-\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{m-1}\rceil-2).

Proof.

Assume first that
\phi
is non dyadic.
We consider the same code
C
(equivalently, tree) constructed in Theorem
3
,
and we take advantage of the knowledge of the lengths of the codewords of the first two symbols and of the last two symbols. In fact, such knowledge in these two particular cases allows us to state more precisely how many levels we bump up the first and last symbol during the construction of the code
C
.

Let
\ell_{1}=\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{1}\rceil
and
\ell_{2}=\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{2}\rceil+1
be the lengths of the codewords associated to symbols
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
,
according to (
25
).
When the level of the codeword associated to
s_{1}

than one

constructed through Lemma

is full in its tree representation. The elimination of such nodes bumps up the symbol

V
On Binary search trees

In this section, we exploit the results on alphabetic codes
we have derived in Section
IV
to provide new bounds
on the average cost of optimal binary search trees. We recall that
Knuth’s algorithm
[
17
]
to construct an optimum binary
search tree has quadratic time and space complexity; here we provide
a linear-time algorithm and give upper bounds on the cost of
the trees our algorithm produces. It is clear that our
upper bounds apply to optimal trees as well; we will also
see that our bounds improve on the best previous results.
Since our algorithms to construct almost-optimal search trees
take in input almost-optimal alphabetic trees, in order
to get linear time complexity it is essential that we can
construct almost-optimal alphabetic trees in linear time as well.
This is one of the main reasons why we have stressed
this feature of the algorithms presented in Section
IV
.

We start this section by amending the analysis of
the algorithm presented by De Prisco and De Santis
[
5
]

(actually, our presentation is slightly more
general than that of
[
5
]
).

Theorem 5
.

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2\,n+1})=(p_{0},q_{1},p_{1},\dots,q_{n},p_{n})
be a probability distribution that contains
the probabilities
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
and

for successful and unsuccessful searches, respectively,

\sigma

\mathbb{E}[T]\leq\mathbb{E}[C]-\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\min(p_{i},p_{i+1}).

Let us start from the binary tree
B
corresponding to

.
The tree

To that purpose, let us observe that the search property holds

This concludes the proof of the theorem.

Appendix B

Lemma 4
.

For any non-increasing dyadic distribution
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})
, let
\nu\,^{\prime}=(0,\phi_{1},0,\dots,0,\phi_{m},0)
be its fully extended distribution, then there does not exist any alphabetic code with lengths
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{2\,m+1}\rangle
defined as follows

\ell_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{ if }\nu\,^{\prime}_{i}=0,\\
\lceil-\log_{2}\nu\,^{\prime}_{i}\rceil+1&\text{ if }\nu\,^{\prime}_{i}>0,\\
\end{cases}

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
are arbitrarily chosen values.

Proof.

Without loss of generality let us assume that
\phi_{m}>0
. Otherwise, we can simply show that the lemma holds for
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m-1})
and hence for
\phi
.

Let
L=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{2\,m+1}\rangle
be the
list of lengths as defined in the statement of the Lemma.
We will consider two cases. The first
corresponds to the case in which,
for each
i\in\{1,3,\dots,2\,m-1\}
, we have
x_{i}=\ell_{i}\geq\ell_{i+1}
and
x_{2\,m+1}=\ell_{2\,m+1}\geq\ell_{2\,m}
;
the second case is for all other possible choices of the
x_{i}
.

Case 1
:
Recall that, for
i\in\{2,\dots,m\}
, we have that
\alpha_{i}=\min(\ell_{i-1},\ell_{i})
. Since
\phi
is non-increasing, for each
i\in\{2,4,\dots,2\,m-2\}
, we have that
\ell_{i}\leq\ell_{i+2}
. Thereby, for each
i\in\{2,\dots,2\,m\}
, it holds that
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{i+1}
. In particular, for each
i\in\{2,4,\dots,2\,m\}
, we have that

\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i+1}=\lceil-\log_{2}\phi_{\frac{i}{2}}\rceil+1=-\log_{2}\phi_{\frac{i}{2}}+1.

Putting it all together we obtain that

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)+2^{-\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)+2^{-\left(-\log_{2}\phi_{\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\rfloor}+1\right)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)+\frac{\phi_{\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\rfloor}}{2},

where the second equality holds because the
\alpha_{i}
are non-decreasing,
and therefore the binary representation of
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1)
contains bits equal to 1 only in
the positions less than or equal to
\alpha_{i}
.

Since we add up each
\phi_{i}
exactly once without any truncation,
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m+1)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}=1
and by Theorem
1
there does not exist any alphabetic code for the lengths
L
.

Case 2
: In this case, there is at least one
\ell_{i}=x_{i}
that does not satisfy the condition of Case 1. Let us suppose that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m+1)<1
and show that this leads to a contradiction.

Consider the sub-case in which we increase by 1
x_{i}
, with
1<i<2m+1
.
Let
L^{\prime}=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{i-1},\ell_{i}+1,\ell_{i+1},\dots,\ell_{2\,m+1}\rangle
be the
list of lengths after increasing such
x_{i}
by 1. Let us show that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},2\,m+1)
can only decrease
with respect to
\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m+1)
. Since
x_{i}
can only affect
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
, we have four cases:

•

\alpha_{i}
increases by 1 and
\alpha_{i+1}
does not change:

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},i)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}

\displaystyle+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i).

•

\alpha_{i}
does not change and
\alpha_{i+1}
increases by 1:

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},i+1)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))

\displaystyle+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))

\displaystyle+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-\alpha_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i+1).

•

Both
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
increase by 1: then
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i+1}
and

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},i+1)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},i))

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1}+1,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)})

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}+2^{-(\alpha_{i+1}+1)}

\displaystyle\quad{\mbox{(since $\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i+1}$})}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-\alpha_{i}}+2^{-(\alpha_{i}+1)}

\displaystyle<\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-\alpha_{i}}+2^{-\alpha_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i-1))+2^{-\alpha_{i}})

\displaystyle\quad+2^{-\alpha_{i+1}}

\displaystyle={\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{i+1},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i))+2^{-\alpha_{i+1}}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,i+1).

•

Both
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
do not change: the final sum remains the same.

Therefore, we have that
\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},2\,m+1)\leq\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m+1)
.

Consider now the sub-cases in which we increase by 1
x_{1}
or
x_{2\,m+1}
. In such sub-cases, only
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{2\,m+1}

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},2)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{{2}}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-\alpha_{{2}}-1}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{{2}},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-\alpha_{{2}}-1}

\displaystyle<\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{{2}},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,1))+2^{-\alpha_{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2)

•

\alpha_{2}
does not change: then
\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},2)=\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2)
.

A similar reasoning holds for

L^{\prime}=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{2\,m+1}+1\rangle

•

\alpha_{2\,m+1}
increases by 1:

\displaystyle\mbox{\tt sum}(L^{\prime},2\,m+1)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{2\,m+1}+1,\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m))

\displaystyle+2^{-(\alpha_{2\,m+1}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{2\,m+1},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m))

\displaystyle+2^{-(\alpha_{2\,m+1}+1)}+2^{-(\alpha_{2\,m+1}+1)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt trunc}(\alpha_{2\,m+1},\mbox{\tt sum}(L,2\,m))

\displaystyle+2^{-\alpha_{2\,m+1}}

•

\alpha_{2\,m+1}

Therefore, in such cases too, we have that
