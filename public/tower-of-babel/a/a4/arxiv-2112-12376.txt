Revisiting and Advancing Fast Adversarial Training Through the Lens of Bi-Level Optimization
Yihua Zhang, Guanhua Zhang, Prashant Khanduri, Mingyi Hong, Shiyu Chang, Sijia Liu

Abstract
Adversarial training ( AT ) is a widely recognized defense mechanism to gain the robustness of deep neural networks against adversarial attacks. It is built on min-max optimization (MMO), where the minimizer ( i.e. , defender) seeks a robust model to minimize the worst-case training loss in the presence of adversarial examples crafted by the maximizer ( i.e. , attacker). However, the conventional MMO method makes AT hard to scale. Thus, Fast-AT ( Wong et al. 2020 ) and other recent algorithms attempt to simplify MMO by replacing its maximization step with the single gradient sign-based attack generation step. Although easy to implement, Fast-AT lacks theoretical guarantees, and its empirical performance is unsatisfactory due to the issue of robust catastrophic overfitting when training with strong adversaries. In this paper, we advance Fast-AT from the fresh perspective of bi-level optimization (BLO). We first show that the commonly-used Fast-AT is equivalent to using a stochastic gradient algorithm to solve a linearized BLO problem involving a sign operation. However, the discrete nature of the sign operation makes it difficult to understand the algorithm performance. Inspired by BLO, we design and analyze a new set of robust training algorithms termed Fast B i-level AT ( Fast-BAT ), which effectively defends sign-based projected gradient descent (PGD) attacks without using any gradient sign method or explicit robust regularization. In practice, we show our method yields substantial robustness improvements over baselines across multiple models and datasets. Codes are available at https://github.com/OPTML-Group/Fast-BAT .

Introduction

Given the fact that machine learning (ML) models can be easily fooled by tiny adversarial perturbations (also known as adversarial attacks) on the input
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
;
Carlini & Wagner 2017
;
Papernot et al. 2016
)
, training robust deep neural networks is now a major focus in research.
Nearly all existing effective defense mechanisms
(
Madry et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019b
;
Shafahi et al. 2019
;
Wong et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2019a
;
Athalye et al. 2018a
)
are built on the adversarial training (
AT
) recipe, introduced by
(
Szegedy et al. 2014
)
and later formalized in
(
Madry et al. 2018
)
using
min-max optimization
(MMO),
where a minimizer (
i.e.
defender) seeks to update model parameters against a maximizer (
i.e.
attacker) that aims to increase the training loss by perturbing the training data.

Despite the effectiveness of the
AT
-type defenses in various application domains, the min-max nature makes them difficult to scale, because of the
multiple
maximization steps required by the iterative attack generator at each training step.
The resulting prohibitive computation cost makes
AT
not suitable in practical settings.
For example,
(
Xie et al. 2019
)
used
128
GPUs to run
AT
on ImageNet.
Thereby, how to speed up
AT
without losing accuracy and robustness is now a
grand challenge
for adversarial defense
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
.

Recently,
(
Wong et al. 2020
;
Shafahi et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2019a
;
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
)
attempted
to develop computationally-efficient alternatives of
AT
, that is, the ‘fast’ versions of
AT
.
To the best of our knowledge,
Fast-AT

(
Wong et al. 2020
)
and
Fast-AT
with gradient alignment (GA) regularization, termed
Fast-AT-GA

(
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
)
, are the two state-of-the-art (SOTA) ‘fast’ versions of
AT
, since they achieve the most significant reduction in computational complexity and preserve accuracy and robustness to a large extent.
In particular, the inner-maximization in
Fast-AT

(
Wong et al. 2020
)
only calls for single-step attack generation. However, different from the direct application of fast gradient sign method
(
Goodfellow et al. 2015
)
, the empirical success of
Fast-AT
also relies on a series of heuristics-based strategies,
e.g.
, using large attack step size, cyclic learning rate schedule, and mixed-precision training.
Yet,
Fast-AT
suffers from two main issues:
(i)
lack of stability,
i.e.
, it has a large variance in performance
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, and
(ii)
catastrophic overfitting,
i.e.
, when training with strong adversaries, the robustness of the resulting model can drop significantly.
To alleviate these problems,
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
proposed
Fast-AT-GA
by penalizing
Fast-AT
using an explicit GA regularization. However, we will show that
Fast-AT-GA
encounters another problem:
(iii)
It improves robust accuracy (RA) at the cost of a sharp drop in standard accuracy (SA), leading to a poor accuracy-robustness tradeoff for large attack budget (
e.g.
,
\epsilon=16/255
in Table
1
). Moreover,
(iv)
there has been no theoretical guarantee for the optimization algorithms used in
Fast-AT
and
Fast-AT-GA
.
Given the limitations
(i)
-

(iv)
, we ask:

How to design a
‘fast’
AT
with improved stability, mitigated catastrophic overfitting, enhanced RA-SA tradeoff, and some theoretical guarantees?

To address the above question, we formulate the
AT
problem as a unified
bi-level optimization (BLO)
problem
(
Dempe 2002
)
.
In the new formulation, the attack generation is cast as a constrained
lower-level
optimization problem, while the defense serves as the
upper-level
optimization problem. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that makes a rigorous connection between adversarial defense and BLO.
Technically, we show that
Fast-AT
can be
interpreted
as BLO with linearized lower-level problems. Delving into the linearization of BLO, we propose a novel, theoretically-grounded ‘fast’ AT framework, termed
fast

b
i-level
AT
(
Fast-BAT
). Practically,

1

Fast-AT
and

When a stronger train-time attack (
i.e.
,
\epsilon=16/255
) is adopted,
Fast-AT
suffers from a large degradation of RA and SA, together with a very high variances. Although
Fast-AT-GA
outperforms
Fast-AT
, it still incurs a significant SA loss at
\epsilon=16/255
. In contrast,
Fast-BAT
produces the best robustness with a more graceful SA-RA tradeoff: As
Fast-BAT
brings an improvement of over
1.5\%
on RA in all experimental settings, its SA still remains at a high level,
e.g.
a significant improvement on SA by
9.9\%
and
6.7\%
at
\epsilon=16/255
for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100.

Contributions.

We summarize our contributions below.

①(Formulation-wise) We propose a unified BLO-based formulation for the robust training problem.
Within this formulation, we show the conventional
Fast-AT
method is solving a low-level linearized BLO problem, rather than the vanilla min-max problem.
This key observation not only provides a new interpretation of
Fast-AT
, but most importantly, also explains why
Fast-AT
is difficult to possess strong theoretical guarantees.

②(Methodology-wise) We propose the new
Fast-BAT
algorithm based on our new understanding of
Fast-AT
. The key enabling technique is to introduce a new
smooth
lower-level objective of BLO for robust training. In contrast to MMO, BLO adopts a different optimization routine that requires implicit gradient (IG) computation. We derive the closed-form of IG for
Fast-BAT
.

③(Experiment-wise) We made a comprehensive experimental study to demonstrate the effectiveness of
Fast-BAT
over SOTA baselines across datasets and model types. Besides its merit in robustness enhancement, we also show its improved stability, lifted accuracy-robustness trade-off, and mitigated catastrophic overfitting.

2
Related work

Adversarial attack.

Adversarial attacks are techniques to generate malicious perturbations that are imperceptible to humans but can mislead the ML models
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
;
Carlini & Wagner 2017
;
Croce & Hein 2020
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Athalye et al. 2018b
)
.
The adversarial attack has become a major approach to evaluate the robustness of deep neural networks and thus, help build safe artificial intelligence in many high-stakes applications such as autonomous driving
(
Deng et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2020
)
and surveillance
(
Thys et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2020
)
.

Adversarial defense and robust training at scale.

Our work falls into the category of robust training, which was
mostly built on MMO.
For example,
(
Madry et al. 2018
)
established the framework of
AT
for the first time, always recognized as one of the most powerful defenses
(
Athalye et al. 2018a
)
.
Extended from
AT
, TRADES
(
Zhang et al. 2019b
)
sought the optimal balance between robustness and generalization ability. Further,
AT
-type defense has been generalized to the semi-/self-supervised settings

(
Carmon et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020b
)

and integrated
3
with certified defense techniques such as randomized smoothing
(
Salman et al. 2019
)
.

Despite the effectiveness of
AT
and its variants,
how to speed up
AT
without losing performance remains an open question.
Some recent works attempted to impose algorithmic simplifications to
AT
, leading to

fast but approximate

AT
algorithms, such as ‘free’ AT
(
Shafahi et al. 2019
)
, you only propagate once (YOPO)
(
Zhang et al. 2019a
)
,
Fast-AT

(
Wong et al. 2020
)
, and
Fast-AT
regularized by gradient alignment (termed
Fast-AT-GA
)
(
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
)
.
In particular,
Fast-AT
and
Fast-AT-GA
are the baselines most relevant to ours due to their low computational complexity. However, their defense performance is still unsatisfactory. For example,
Fast-AT
has poor training stability
(
Li et al. 2020
)
and suffers catastrophic overfitting when facing strong attacks
(
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
)
. In contrast,
Fast-AT-GA
yields improved robustness but has a poor accuracy-robustness tradeoff (see Table
1
).

Bi-level optimization (BLO).

BLO is a unified hierarchical learning framework, where the objective and variables of the upper-level problem depend on the lower-level problems. The BLO problem in its most generic form is very challenging, and thus, the design of algorithms and theory for BLO focuses on special cases

(
Vicente et al. 1994
;
White & Anandalingam 1993
;
Gould et al. 2016
;
Ghadimi & Wang 2018
;
Khanduri et al. 2021
;
Ji et al. 2020
;
Hong et al. 2020
)
.
In practice, BLO has been successfully applied to meta-learning
(
Rajeswaran et al. 2019
)
, data poisoning attack design
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
.
Recently, references
(
Zuo et al. 2021
)
formulated the AT training problem using a Stackelberg Game, which is a special case of bi-level optimization. Different from ours, the goal of
(
Zuo et al. 2021
)
is to improve model generalization in natural language processing tasks, rather than improving model robustness in vision tasks. Further, note that in our formulation, we use a generic lower-level problem to model the adversarial learning problem, while in
(
Zuo et al. 2021
)
a multi-step unrolling of certain update mapping is used for this purpose. Importantly, with our formulation, it is possible to provide theoretical analysis for the resulting algorithms, under some conditions about the lower-level problem.

As will be evident later, the existing BLO approach is not directly applied to adversarial defense due to the presence of the
constrained
non-convex lower-level problem (for attack generation).
To the best of our knowledge, our work makes a rigorous connection between adversarial defense and BLO for the first time.

3
A Bi-Level Optimization View on
Fast-AT

Preliminaries on
AT
and
Fast-AT
.

Let us consider the following standard min-max formulation for the robust adversarial training problem
(
Madry et al. 2018
)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{\theta}}~\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}}\left[\maximize_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta},y)\right],\end{array}

where
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes model parameters;
\mathcal{D}
is the training set consisting (a finite number) of labeled data pairs with feature
\mathbf{x}
and label
y
;
\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents adversarial perturbations subject to the perturbation constraint
\mathcal{C}
,
e.g.
,
\mathcal{C}=\{\bm{\delta}\,|\,\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\bm{\delta}\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]\}
for
\epsilon
-toleration
\ell_{\infty}
-norm constrained attack (normalized to
[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]
);
\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\cdot)
is the training loss;

(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta})
represents an adversarial example.

The standard solver for problem (
3
) is known as
AT

(
Madry et al. 2018
)
. However, it has to call an
iterative
optimization method (
e.g.
,
K
-step PGD attack) to solve the inner maximization problem of (
3
), which is computationally expensive.
To improve its scalability, the
Fast-AT
algorithm
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
was proposed to take a
single-step
PGD attack for inner maximization. The algorithm backbone of
Fast-AT
is summarized below.

Fast-AT
algorithm

Let
\bm{\theta}_{t}
be parameters at iteration
t
. The
(t+1)
th iteration becomes
(
Wong et al. 2020
)
:

\bullet

Inner maximization by
1
-step PGD:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}\xleftarrow{}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bm{\delta}_{0}+\alpha\cdot\mathrm{sign}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta}_{0},y)\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{a})
denotes the projection operation that projects the point
\mathbf{a}
onto
\mathcal{C}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{z})=\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}
,
\bm{\delta}_{0}
is a random uniform initialization within
[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]
,
\alpha>0
is a proper learning rate (
e.g.
,
1.25\epsilon
), and
\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)
is the element-wise sign operation.

\bullet

Outer minimization for model training:
This can be conducted by any standard optimizer,
e.g.
,

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t+1}\xleftarrow{}\bm{\theta}_{t}-\beta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta},y)

where
\beta>0
is a proper learning rate (
e.g.
, cyclic learning rate), and
\bm{\delta}
is provided from the inner maximization step.

A few remarks about
Fast-AT
are given below.

①Roughly speaking,
Fast-AT
is a simplification of
AT
using
1
-step PGD for inner maximization. However, it is not entirely clear which problem
Fast-AT
is actually solving. If we take a closer look at the algorithm, we will see that the inner update only changes to the
initial

\bm{\delta}_{0}
, not the most recent
\bm{\delta}
. Clearly, this scheme is fundamentally
different
from the classical gradient descent-ascent algorithm for min-max optimization
(
Razaviyayn et al. 2020
)
, which alternatively updates the inner and outer variables.

Therefore, it remains elusive if
Fast-AT
is solving the original problem (
3
)
.

②
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
demonstrated that
Fast-AT
could lead to catastrophic overfitting
when using strong adversaries for training. However, there was no grounded theory to justify the pros and cons of
Fast-AT
. We will show that BLO provides a promising solution.

BLO: Towards a unified formulation of robust training.

BLO (bi-level optimization) is an optimization problem that involves two levels of optimization tasks, where the
lower-level
task is
nested inside the
upper-level
task. More precisely, it has the following generic form:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}&\hskip 9.24994ptf({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}^{*}({\mathbf{u}}))\\
\text{s.t.}&\hskip 9.24994pt{\mathbf{v}}^{*}({\mathbf{u}})=\arg\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}}\;g(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})\end{array}

(2)

where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are the feasible sets for the variables
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, respectively;
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are the upper- and the lower-level objective functions, respectively.
Intuitively, the BLO (
2
) can be used to formulate the adversarial training problem as the latter also involves two problems, one nested in the other. Importantly, it is more powerful than the min-max formulation (
3
) as it allows the two problems to have
different
objective functions. This flexibility provided by BLO is the key to the generality of our proposed framework.

To make the above intuition precise,
we use the upper-level problem to model the training loss minimization, while the lower-level problem to model the attack generation process, and consider the following BLO problem:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}&\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}}[\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x},y);y)]\\
\text{s.t.}&\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x},y)=\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta};\mathbf{x},y),\end{array}

where the training loss function
\ell_{\rm tr}(\cdot)
has been defined in (
3
), and
\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\cdot)
denotes an attack objective.
For the notation simplicity, in the subsequent discussion, we will not indicate the dependency of the functions
\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}
,
\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}
, and
\bm{\delta}^{*}
with respect to (w.r.t.) the data samples
(\mathbf{x},y)
.
The formulation (
3
) has two key differences from (
3
):

①When we choose
\ell_{\rm atk}=-\ell_{\rm tr}
, problem (
3
) becomes equivalent to the min-max formulation (
3
). It follows that the BLO is suitable to formulate the adversarial training problem. Moreover, we will see shortly that the flexibility provided by choosing the lower-level objective
independently
of the upper-level one enables us to interpret
Fast-AT
as solving a certain special form of the BLO problem. Note that prior to our work, it was not entirely clear what is the problem that
Fast-AT
is trying to solve.

②BLO calls a different optimization routine from those to solve the original min-max problem (
3
). As will be evident later, even if we set
\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}=-\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}
in (
3
), the BLO-enabled solver does not simplify to
Fast-AT
(see more details in Appendix
B
). This is because for a given data sample
(\mathbf{x},y)
, the gradient for the upper-level problem of (
3
) yields:

\displaystyle\resizebox{9496125}{}{$\text{\resizebox{2939400}{}{$\hskip-9.95845pt\frac{d\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))}{d\bm{\theta}}$}}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))+\underbrace{\frac{{d}\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{{d}\bm{\theta}}}_{\text{{{\text{IG}}}}}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))$},\hskip-8.53581pt

(5)

where the superscript
\top
denotes the transpose operation,
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))
denotes the partial derivative w.r.t. the first input argument
\bm{\theta}
; and
\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, if exists, is referred to as
implicit gradient (
IG
)
because it is defined through an implicitly constrained optimization problem
\min_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}
.
The dependence on
IG
is a ‘fingerprint’ of BLO (
3
) in contrast to
AT
or
Fast-AT
.

BLO-enabled interpretation of
Fast-AT
.

Next, we demonstrate how
Fast-AT
relates to BLO.
Fast-AT
can be interpreted as an approximated stochastic gradient algorithm for solving the following
lower-level linearized BLO
. That is to say,
Fast-AT
is not solving the original min-max problem (
3
), but its linearized version below:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\hskip-14.22636pt\min_{\bm{\theta}}&\hskip-5.69054pt\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}}[\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))]\\
\hskip-14.22636pt\text{s.t.}&\hskip-8.53581pt\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})\hskip-2.84526pt=\hskip-2.84526pt\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}~[(\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z})^{\top}\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}))+(\lambda/2)\|\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}],\end{array}

where
\mathbf{z}=\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
\lambda=1/\alpha
. Our justification for the above interpretation is elaborated on below.

①The simplified lower-level problem of (
3
) leads to the
closed-form
solution:

\begin{aligned} \hskip-9.95845pt\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})=&\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}\,(\lambda/2)\|\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z}+(1/\lambda)\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}))\|_{2}^{2}\\
=&\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\mathbf{z}-(1/\lambda)\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}))\right),\hskip-14.22636pt\end{aligned}

(8)

which is exactly given by the
1
-step PGD attack with initialization
\mathbf{z}
and learning rate
(1/\lambda)
.
In the linearization used in (
3
), a quadratic regularization term (with regularization parameter
\lambda
) is introduced to ensure the strong convexity of the lower-level objective within the constraint set
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}
so as to achieve the unique minimizer (
8
).
Note that imposing such a strongly convex regularizer is also commonly used to stabilize the convergence of MMO (min-max optimization) and BLO
(
Qian et al. 2019
;
Hong et al. 2020
)
.
If we set
\mathbf{z}=\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
\lambda=1/\alpha
, (
8
) precisely depicts the inner maximization step used in
Fast-AT
.

②By substituting (
8
)
into the upper-level problem of (
3
),
we can then use (
5
) to compute the stochastic gradients of the upper-level problem. If the stochastic gradient can be precisely computed, we can update the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
using
SGD based on (
5
). That is,

\scriptstyle{\bm{\theta}\xleftarrow{}\bm{\theta}-\beta}\textstyle{\frac{d\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))}{d\bm{\theta}}}
(with learning rate
\beta
).
However, generally speaking, the
IG
function

\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}
involved in (
5
)
may not be differentiable, and even it is, the computation may not be easy. For our case,
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
expressed in (
8
) involves both a projection and a sign operation, which can be particularly difficult to compute. To proceed,
let us make the following approximations.
We assume that the chain rule of the derivative of
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
holds w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}
, implying the differentiability of the projection operation and the sign operation.
Then, based on the closed-form of
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
in (
8
),
IG
is approximately equal to
\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}\scriptstyle=\mathbf{0}
, where we use two facts:
(1) The linearization point
\mathbf{z}
is independent of
\bm{\theta}
,
i.e.

\mathbf{z}=\bm{\delta}_{0}
; And (2)
\frac{d\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)}{d\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}
holds almost everywhere.
Clearly, the use of the gradient sign method simplifies the
IG
computation. Further,
the upper-level gradient is approximated by

\frac{d\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))}{d\bm{\theta}}\scriptstyle\approx\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(\bm{\theta})
, and the upper-level optimization of problem (
3
) becomes

\scriptstyle\bm{\theta}\xleftarrow{}\bm{\theta}-\beta\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(\bm{\theta})
, which is the same as the outer minimization step in
Fast-AT
.
In a nutshell, the BLO solver of problem (
3
), which calls the IG computation based on (
8
), eventually reduces to the
Fast-AT
algorithm.

The aforementioned analysis shows that
Fast-AT
can be viewed as using an approximated stochastic gradient algorithm to solve the linearized BLO (
3
), with the linearization point
\mathbf{z}
and the regularization parameter
\lambda
set as
\mathbf{z}=\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
\lambda=1/\alpha
. However, since a series of approximations have been used when arriving at the approximated gradient
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}(\bm{\theta})
used by
Fast-AT
, it is no longer clear if the resulting algorithm can still sufficiently reduce the objective function of the upper-level problem. Additionally, based on the fact that the lower-level problem of (
3
) involves the
discrete
sign operator, it is unlikely (if not impossible) that any approximated stochastic gradient-based algorithms developed for it can possess any strong theoretical guarantees.

4
Fast-BAT
: Advancing
Fast-AT
by BLO

Fast-BAT
and rationale.

The key take-away
from (
3
)
is that the conventional
Fast-AT
adopts
the
sign of input gradient
to linearize the lower-level attack objective.
However, a more natural choice is to use the first-order Taylor expansion for linearization.
By doing so, problem (
3
) can be modified to the following form:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\hskip-11.38109pt\min_{\bm{\theta}}&\hskip-5.69054pt\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}}[\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))]\\
\hskip-14.22636pt\text{s.t.}&\hskip-8.53581pt\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})\hskip-2.84526pt=\hskip-2.84526pt\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}}~[(\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}))+(\lambda/2)\|\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}],\end{array}

Similar to (
8
), problem (
4
) can be solved as:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\mathbf{z}-(1/\lambda)\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta})\right).

(11)

In contrast to (
8
), the
IG
associated with (
4
) cannot be approximated by zeros since the gradient sign operation is not present in
(
11
).
To compute
IG
, the auto-differentiation (the chain rule)
can be applied to the closed-form of
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
. This is also what has been used in
(
Zuo et al. 2021
)
that calls projected gradient descent unrolling to solve BLO problems.
However, this will not give us an accurate and generalizable
IG
solution since the projection operation
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
is
not
smooth everywhere and thus, the use of chain rule does not yield a rigorous derivation. In the following subsection, we address the

IG
challenge
in a theoretically-grounded manner.

IG
theory for
Fast-BAT
.

The problem of
Fast-BAT

(
4
)
falls into a class of very challenging BLO problems, which requires
constrained
lower-level optimization.
The unconstrained case is easier to handle since one can apply the implicit function theory to the stationary condition of the lower-level problem to obtain
IG

(
Hong et al. 2020
)
. Yet, in our case with
constrained
problems, a stationary point could violate the constraints, and thus, the stationary condition becomes non-applicable.
Fortunately, in problem
(
4
), we are dealing with a special class of lower-level constraints –
linear constraints
. Let us rewrite the constraints below:

\begin{split}\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\bm{\delta}\in[\mathbf{-x},\mathbf{1-x}]\Longleftrightarrow{}\mathbf{B}\bm{\delta}\leq\mathbf{b},\end{split}

(12)

where
\scriptstyle{\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\begin{bmatrix}\scriptstyle{\mathbf{I}}\\
\scriptstyle{-\mathbf{I}}\end{bmatrix},~\scriptstyle{\mathbf{b}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\begin{bmatrix}\scriptstyle{\min\{\epsilon\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x}\}}\\
\scriptstyle{-\max\{-\epsilon\mathbf{1},-\mathbf{x}\}}\end{bmatrix}
.
By exploiting the above linearly constrained problem structure, we show that the
IG
challenge associated with (
4
) can be addressed via
Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT)
conditions. We present our main theoretical results (Proposition
1
and Corollary
1
) below and refer readers to Appendix
A
for detailed derivation.

Proposition 1

For the BLO problem (
4
), let
g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})
denote its lower-level objective function
evaluated at
\bm{\delta}^{*}
given
\bm{\theta}
, then the analytical form of
IG
(implicit gradient) is given by

\begin{array}[]{cc}&\hskip-142.26378pt\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})^{-1}\\
&\hskip 0.0pt+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}]^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})^{-1},\end{array}

where

\textstyle\bm{\delta}^{*}

is given by (
11
) (the dependence on

\textstyle\bm{\theta}

is omitted for ease of notation),

\textstyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

denotes the second-order partial derivatives evaluated at

\textstyle(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})
,
and

\textstyle\mathbf{B}_{0}

denotes the sub-matrix of
\textstyle\mathbf{B}

that only corresponds to the active constraints at

\textstyle\bm{\delta}^{\ast}
,
i.e., those in

\textstyle\mathbf{B}\bm{\delta}^{\ast}\leq\mathbf{b}

satisfied with equality.

It is clear from (
1
) that the computation of IG requires the second-order derivatives as well as matrix inversion. This is computationally intensive.
Recall from (
4
) that the Hessian matrix
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g
of
the lower-level objective function is given by

\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})=\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
. This inspires us to impose the Hessian-free approximation,
i.e.
,
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}=\mathbf{0}
.
The rationale behind the Hessian-free assumption is that
ReLU-based
neural networks commonly
lead to a piece-wise linear decision boundary w.r.t.
the inputs
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2019
;
Alfarra et al. 2020
)
, and thus,
its second-order derivative (Hessian)
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}
is close to zero.
In Sec.
5.2
and Appendix
D
, we will empirically show that the Hessian-free assumption is reasonable for both ReLU and non-ReLU neural networks.
Thus, the Hessian matrix is approximated as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))=\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{0}+\lambda\mathbf{I}.

(15)

With (
15
), we can simplify closed-form of IG as below:

Corollary 1

With the Hessian-free assumption, namely

\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}=\mathbf{0}
,
the
IG
(implicit gradient) of (
4
) is

{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}=-(1/\lambda)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}},

(16)

where
\scriptstyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{bmatrix}\scriptstyle 1_{p_{1}<\delta_{1}^{*}<q_{1}}\mathbf{e}_{1}\cdots 1_{p_{1}<\delta_{d}^{*}<q_{d}}\mathbf{e}_{d}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and the function

1_{p_{i}<\delta_{i}^{*}<q_{i}}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the indicator over the constraint of
\{\delta_{i}\,|\,p_{i}<\delta_{i}^{*}<q_{i}\}
, which returns
1
if the constraint is satisfied,
\delta_{i}^{*}
denotes the
i
th entry of
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
,

p_{i}=\max\{-\epsilon,-x_{i}\}
and

q_{i}=\min\{\epsilon,1-x_{i}\}
characterize the boundary of the linear constraint (
12
) for the variable
\delta_{i}
,
and
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the basis vector with the
i
th entry being
1
and others being
0
.

Fast-BAT
algorithm and implementation.

Similar to
Fast-AT
or
AT
, the
Fast-BAT
algorithm also follows the principle of alternating optimization.
Specifically, it consists of
the
IG
-based
upper-level gradient descent (
5
), interlaced with the lower-level optimal attack (
11
).
We summarize the
Fast-BAT
algorithm below.

\bullet

Lower-level solution
: Obtain
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t})
from (
11
);

\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\mathbf{z}-(1/\lambda)\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta})\right).

\bullet

Upper-level model training
: Integrating the
IG
(
16
) into (
5
), call SGD to update the model parameters as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}&-\alpha_{1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})\\
&-\alpha_{2}(-\frac{1}{\lambda})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\delta}^{\ast})\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}),\end{array}

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
are learning rates associated with the model gradient and the
IG
-augmented descent direction.

It is clear from (
4
) that to train a robust model,
Fast-BAT
can be decomposed into the regular
Fast-AT
update
(
i.e.
, the term multiplied by
\alpha_{1}
)
and the additional update that involves
IG
, (
i.e.
, the term multiplied by
\alpha_{2}
).
To implement
Fast-BAT
, we highlight some key hyper-parameter setups that are different from
Fast-AT

(
Wong et al. 2020
)
and

Fast-AT-GA

(
Andriushchenko & Flammarion 2020
)
.

Remark on implementation details

Next, we discuss the
practical setups
used in Fast-BAT (more details in Appendix
C
).
First,
1/\lambda
serves as the attack step size as shown in (
11
). We refer readers to Table
7
for a sensitivity analysis of
\lambda
.
In Fast-BAT, the hyperparameter
\alpha_{2}
is newly introduced, and is set differently from
\alpha_{1}
so as to control the descent error associated with the coupled second-order/first-order stochastic derivatives,
i.e.
, the
\alpha_{2}
-term in (
4
).
In Sec.
7
, we show that a proper
\alpha_{2}
helps mitigate catastrophic overfitting. In practice, we set
\alpha_{2}/\lambda=0.1\alpha_{1}
.
To specify the linearization point
\mathbf{z}
in (
4
),
we investigate two types of linearization schemes: (1) the random constant linearization (random uniform and random corner linearization) and (2) the
1
-step perturbation warm-up-based linearization (
1
-step sign-based and
1
-step w/o sign PGD).
These linearization schemes have computational complexities up to the one-step attack generation.
Empirically, we find that
Fast-BAT
using “
1
-step PGD w/o sign" leads to the best defensive performance (see Table
9
).
We follow this experiment setup in the sequel.

Remark on convergence analysis

In Appendix
E
, we prove that under some assumptions on the gradient bias, Fast-BAT converges to a first-order stationary point or its small neighborhood in the rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
, where
T
is the iteration number of model updates.
The main analysis difficulty lies in the
last term of the model updating rule (
4
), which involves two coupled derivatives built upon the same mini-batch.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a novel bi-level optimization-based fast adversarial training framework, termed
Fast-BAT
.
The rationale behind designing
Fast-BAT

lies in two aspects.
First, from the perspective of implicit gradients, we show that the existing
Fast-AT
framework is equivalent to the lower-level linearized BLO along the sign direction of the input gradient.
Second, we show that
Fast-BAT

is able to achieve improved stability of performance, mitigated catastrophic overfitting, and enhanced accuracy-robustness trade-off. To the best of our knowledge, we for the first time establish the theory and the algorithmic foundation of BLO for adversarial training.
Extensive experiments are provided to demonstrate the superiority of our method to state-of-the-art accelerated
AT
baselines.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
1
and Corollary
1

Proof
:
Upon defining
g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta})=(\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}=\mathbf{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta})+(\lambda/2)\|\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}
, we repeat (
4
) as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{\theta}}&\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}}[\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))]\\
\st&\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})=\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{B}\bm{\delta}\leq\mathbf{b}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}),\end{array}

where we have used the expression of linear constraints in (
12
).

Our goal is to derive the
IG

\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}
shown in (
5
).
To this end, we first build implicit functions by leveraging KKT conditions of the lower-level problem
of (
A
).
We say
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
(Lagrangian multipliers) satisfy the KKT conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Stationarity:}\qquad&\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))+\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0},\\
\text{Complementary slackness :
}\qquad&\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})\cdot(\mathbf{B}\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})-\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{0}\\
\text{Dual feasibility:}\qquad&\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})\geq\mathbf{0}\end{split}

(21)

where
\cdot
denotes the elementwise product.

Active constraints & definition of
\mathbf{B}_{0}
:
Let
\mathbf{B}_{0}
denote the sub-matrix of
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{b}_{0}
the sub-vector of
\mathbf{b}
, which consists of only the
active constraints
at
\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})
,
i.e.
, those satisfied with the equality
\mathbf{B}_{0}\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{b}_{0}
(corresponding to
nonzero
dual variables). The determination of active constraints is done given
\bm{\theta}
at each iteration.

With the aid of
(\mathbf{B}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})
, KKT (
21
) becomes

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))+\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0},~~\text{and}~~\mathbf{B}_{0}\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})-\mathbf{b}_{0}=\mathbf{0},

(22)

where the nonzero
\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})
only corresponds to the active constraints.
We take derivatives w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}
of (
22
), and thus obtain the following

\displaystyle\frac{d\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))+\underbrace{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}_{\text{IG}}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{0},

(23)

and

\displaystyle~\underbrace{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}_{\text{IG}}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}=\mathbf{0},

(24)

where
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\bm{\theta}|\times|\bm{\delta}|}
denotes second-order partial derivatives (recall that
|\bm{\theta}|=n
and
|\bm{\delta}|=d
).
According to (
23
), we have

\displaystyle{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}=-[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}]\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}.

(25)

Substituting the above into (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}=\mathbf{0},

(26)

which yields:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}]^{-1},

(27)

and thus,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}]^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}.

(28)

Substituting (
28
) into (
25
), we obtain the IG

\displaystyle{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}]^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}.

(29)

The above completes the proof of Proposition
1
.

To further compute (
29
), the Hessian matrix
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}
is needed. Recall from the definition of the lower-level objective that the Hessian matrix is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))=\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{0}+\lambda\mathbf{I}.

(30)

Here we used the assumption that

\nabla_{\bm{\delta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}=\mathbf{0}
. The rationale behind that is
neural networks commonly
leads to a piece-wise linear decision boundary w.r.t.
the inputs
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2019

Alfarra et al. 2020
)
, and thus,
its second-order derivative (Hessian)

is close to zero.

Based on the simplification (
30
), we have

\displaystyle{\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{d\bm{\theta}}}=

\displaystyle-(1/\lambda)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}]^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{0}\right)}_{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}}

\displaystyle-(1/\lambda)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{\ast}(\bm{\theta}))\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}},

where we have used the fact that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}{{g}}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}
.

What is
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
in (

)?

Since
\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
, we can obtain that
\mathbf{B}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}
is a sparse diagonal matrix with diagonal entries being
0
or
1
. Thus,

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
can be first simplified to

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{0}.

(32)

Clearly,
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
is also a diagonal matrix with either
0
or
1
diagonal entries. The
1
-valued diagonal entry of
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
corresponds to the
inactive constraints
in

\mathbf{B}\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})<\mathbf{b}
,
i.e.
, those satisfied with
strict inequalities
in
\{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\mathbf{0}\leq\bm{\delta}\leq\mathbf{1}\}
. This
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{bmatrix}1_{p_{1}\leq\delta_{1}^{*}\leq q_{1}}\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,1_{p_{1}\leq\delta_{d}^{*}\leq q_{d}}\mathbf{e}_{d}\end{bmatrix}

where

and returns

\delta_{i}^{*}
denotes the
i
th entry of
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
,

p_{i}=\max\{-\epsilon,-x_{i}\}
and

, and
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

1

s.

Based on the definition of

, (

Appendix B
Discussion on case
\ell_{\mathrm{atk}}=-\ell_{\mathrm{tr}}

We provide an in-depth explanation on the fact that even if we set
\ell_{\text{atk}}=-\ell_{\text{tr}}
, the
optimization routine
given by (
5
) to solve problem (
3
) does not reduce to the ordinary IG-absent gradient descent to solve
problem (
3
) because of the presence of lower-level constraints.

•

In the
absence
of the constraint
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}
, if we set
\ell_{\text{atk}}=-\ell_{\text{tr}}
, then solving problem (
3
) via
IG-involved descent method (
5
)
will reduce to the ordinary IG-absent method that solves problem (
3
).

This is a known BLO result (
e.g.

(
Ghadimi & Wang 2018
)
) and can be readily proven using the stationary condition. To be specific, based on the stationary condition of unconstrained lower-level optimization, we have
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})=0
. Since
\ell_{\text{atk}}=-\ell_{\text{tr}}
, we have
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})=0
. As a result, the second term in (
5
) becomes
\mathbf{0}
and solving problem (
3
) becomes identical to solving the min-max problem (
3
).

•

In the
presence
of the constraint
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}
, the stationary condition cannot be applied since the stationary point may not be a feasible point in the constraint. In other words,
\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}\ell_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*})=0
does not hold in the case of
\ell_{\text{atk}}=-\ell_{\text{tr}}
. As a matter of fact, one has to resort to KKT conditions instead of the stationary condition for a constrained lower-level problem. Similar to our proof in Proposition
1
, the implicit gradient (and thus the second term of (
5
)) cannot be omitted in general. This makes the optimization routine to solve problem (
3
) different from solving problem (
3
).

The validity of the Hessian-free assumption on non-ReLU based neural networks.

The Hessian-free assumption is based on the fact that the commonly used ReLU activation function is piece-wise linear
w.r.t.
input. We further conduct experiments to verify the feasibility of such an assumption on models with non-ReLU activation functions. We choose two commonly used activation functions, Swish
(
Ramachandran et al. 2017
)
and Softplus, as alternatives for the non-smooth ReLU function. We compare the results both calculating Hessian as well as the Hessian-free version to see if the Hessian-free assumption still holds for the non-ReLU neural network. The results are shown in Table
11
. As we can see, the use of Hessian does not affect performance much. A similar phenomenon can be observed across different
\epsilon
and different model activation functions (ReLU, Softplus, and Swish). However, the introduction of Hessian leads to an increase in time consumption by more than 30%. Therefore, we can draw the conclusion that the Hessian-free assumption is reasonable across different activation function choices.

Appendix E
Convergence Analysis

Let us consider we have data samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
N
denotes the number of the training data. Then the goal of
Fast-BAT
algorithm is to solve:

\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{i}^{*}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i})\\
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})\in&\argmin_{\bm{\delta}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}L_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{i}^{*}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i})\end{split}

(34)

Let us denote the batch size as
b
. Then the problem can be reformulated as :

\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{1}{b_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{i}}\ell_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{i}^{*}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i})\\
\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})\in&\argmin_{\bm{\delta}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}L_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))=\underbrace{\frac{1}{b_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{b_{t}}\ell_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{i}^{*}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i})}_{L_{\mathrm{atk}}^{b_{t}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}_{i}(\bm{\theta}))}\end{split}

(35)

The way to computes the gradient (
5
) is equal to the case where
b=N
.

\displaystyle\frac{dL^{N}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))}{d\bm{\theta}}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{N}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))+\frac{{d}\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}}{{d}\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}L^{N}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))

(36)

If we approximate the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\text{tr}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))}
using a batch size of
b
, then we have the following assumptions:

•

Bias assumption:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))]=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))+\beta(b),

(37)

where
\beta(b)
is the bias and the expectation is taken
w.r.t.
batches. Note that
\beta(b)=0
for
b=N
.

•

Variance assumption:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))-\beta(b)\|^{2}=\sigma^{2}[1+\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))\|^{2}],

(38)

Note that the variance equals 0 for
b=N
.

Now the
Fast-BAT
algorithm with batch size
b
can be stated as follows:

•

Initialization:
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, step size
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, batch size
\{\alpha_{b}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
.

•

for
t=1
to
T
:

–

Choose batsh size
b_{t}
at each iteration
t\in[T]
.

–

L_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))\approx L^{b_{t}}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))
,
L_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))\approx L^{b_{t}}_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))

–

Update:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\alpha_{t-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{\delta}_{t-1}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}))

(39)

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\text{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{\delta}_{t-1}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}))=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}))+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{\delta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})).

(40)

Note
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})
is computed using (
29
) by replacing:

*

L_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))
with
L^{b_{t}}_{\text{atk}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\delta}^{*}(\bm{\theta}))

*

B_{0}
with
B_{0}
computed with (
35
).

Assuming smoothness of
L_{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\theta})}

w.r.t.

\bm{\theta}
, we get:

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\alpha_{t}\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\right\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}L}{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L^{b_{t}}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}

(41)

Taking expectation
w.r.t.
random samples, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})]-\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]-\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}),\beta(b_{t})\right\rangle]+\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}L\mathbb{E}[1+\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}]

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt+2\alpha_{t}^{2}L\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}]+2\alpha_{t}^{2}L\mathbb{E}[\|\beta(b_{t})\|^{2}_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\left[\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}L\right]\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\left[\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}+2\alpha_{t}^{2}L\right]\|\beta(b_{t})\|^{2}_{2}+\alpha^{2}_{t}L\sigma^{2}\right]

Using
\alpha_{t}\leq\frac{1}{4L(2+\sigma^{2})}
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{4}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}+\alpha_{t}\|\beta(b_{t})\|^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2}L\sigma^{2}\right].

(42)

Finally, after rearranging the terms, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{t}}{4}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})-L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}))+\alpha_{t}\|\beta(b_{t})\|^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2}L\sigma^{2}]

(43)

Summing over
t=1
to
T
and multiplying by
1/T
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{t}}{4}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})}{T}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\|\beta(b_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}.

(44)

Taking
\alpha_{t}=\alpha
and
b_{t}=b
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\frac{4[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]}{\alpha T}+4\|\beta(b)\|^{2}+4\alpha\sigma^{2}.

(45)

Recall if
b=N
, we have
\sigma^{2}=0
and
\beta(b)=0
, we can further get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{4[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]}{\alpha T}.

(46)

Case I: We can choose
\alpha=\frac{1}{8L}
and get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{T}).

(47)

Case II: We can choose
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}\|^{2}_{2})]\leq\frac{4[L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})-L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})]}{\sqrt{T}}+\underbrace{\frac{4\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}}_{\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})}+\underbrace{4\|\beta(b)\|^{2}}_{\mathcal{O}(1)}

Now if
\|\beta(b)\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}),

(48)

otherwise we get in the worst case:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})+\mathcal{O}(1).

(49)
