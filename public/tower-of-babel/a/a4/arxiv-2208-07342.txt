Abstract
. We establish the equivalence between the regularity (rectifiability) of sets and suitable estimates on the oscillation of the gradient for smooth non-local distance functions. A prototypical example of such a distance was introduced, as part of a larger PDE theory, by David, Feneuil, and Mayboroda in [ DFM18 ] . The results apply to all dimensions and co-dimensions, require no underlying topological assumptions, and provide a surprisingly rich class of analytic characterizations of rectifiability.

1.
Introduction

Some of the major efforts at the interface between geometric measure theory, analysis, and partial differential equations in the past 20-30 years have been devoted to the characterization of regularity of sets in terms of their PDE and analytic properties. This program, starting with the work of F. and M. Riesz in the plane
[
RR20
]
in 1916, has recently achieved the characterization of uniform rectifiability in terms of the absolute continuity of harmonic measure
[
AHM+20
]
and the solution of the David-Semmes conjecture, a characterization of uniform rectifiability in terms of the Riesz transform
[
NTV14
,
MMV96
]
. One of the key achievements on this path was undoubtedly the introduction of the “correct” notion of regularity, uniform rectifiability, amenable to such scale-invariant characterizations, by David and Semmes in the early 90s, along with their first characterization, the so-called Uniform Square Function Estimate (USFE) in
[
DS93
]
. All in all such characterizations are still scarce, notoriously challenging, and many are compelling open problems.

Aside from the Riesz transform, very few singular integral operators are known to characterize uniform rectifiability of
(n-1)
-dimensional sets (see, e.g.
[
JM22
]
for a non-perturbative example in the plane and
[
PPT21
]
for some perturbative results). The counterexamples are rare as well, but it is known that for some Calderón-Zygmund kernels the existence of principle values or the
L^{2}
-boundedness of the associated operator does not characterize uniform rectifiability (cf.
[
Huo01
]
,
[
Dav01
]
, and
[
Mat97
]
). No characterization of uniform rectifiability using square functions pertaining to different kernels are available to date, though there are some results on the boundedness of square functions for other kernels assuming uniform rectifiability (e.g.
[
HMMM14
]
and
[
MM16
]
).

Moreover, in the context of lower dimensional sets, these questions are of great interest and almost completely open. On the singular integral side, Jaye and Nazarov have introduced a beautiful approach via reflectionless measures, aiming to extend the singular integral characterizations to any dimension and co-dimension (see, e.g.
[
JN18
,
JN19
]
), but unfortunately, checking the initial condition of their theory even for the Riesz transform is still unattainable. As a result, the Riesz transform characterization of
d
-rectifiability,
1<d<n-1
, remains one of the outstanding open problems, and the straightforward analogue of the USFE is known to fail
[
DS93
]
. The analogue of the harmonic measure characterization for
d<n-1
fails too, and in fact, the only known PDE characterization of uniform rectifiability of lower dimensional sets is the recently obtained estimate in terms of the appropriate Green functions in
[
DM20b
]
.

The goal of the present paper is a new characterization of uniform rectifiability in terms of the generalized regularized distance function. On one hand, it could be seen as parallel to the Riesz transform characterization in
[
NTV14
]
or even in
[
Tol08
]
, since a non-tangential limit of the regularized distance function formally, for some values of parameters, looks like the Riesz transform, although in the full generality it is non-linear, non-local, and not a traditional singular integral operator. On the other hand, the theory developed here is parallel to the USFE, although once again, the resemblance is formal. This theory also, quite magically, connects to PDEs: the regularized distance function is the Green function with a pole at infinity for a certain special degenerate differential operator
[
DEM21
]
, not to mention its major role in the newly emerged elliptic theory for domains with lower dimensional boundaries (see, e.g.,
[
DFM18
,
DM20a
,
DM20b
]
). One could also say that it is an alternative characterization altogether: the oscillations of the gradient of the regularized distance are more reminiscent of curvature than any of the above. Most importantly, it applies to all dimensions and co-dimensions.

The first step in this direction was taken by the first and the third author together with Guy David in 2018
[
DEM21
]
, where the appropriate characterizations in terms of

(1.1)

D_{\mu,\alpha}(x)\equiv\left(\int\dfrac{1}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu(y)\right)^{-1/\alpha},\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

were achieved. The notion of
D_{\mu,\alpha}
itself was first introduced in
[
DFM18
]
by David, Feneuil, and the third author. If
\mu
is
d
-Ahlfors regular (cf. (
1.3
)), then
D_{\mu,\alpha}
is smooth away from
E
but also acts like a distance to the support of
\mu
in the sense that
D_{\mu,\alpha}(x)\simeq\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathrm{spt}\,\mu)
. As we alluded to above, in
[
DEM21
]
it was also shown that for a special “magic” value of
\alpha
, the distance given by (
1.1
) is in fact the Green function with pole at infinity for
-\text{div}\left(D_{\mu,\alpha}^{-n+d+1}\nabla\;\cdot\right)
, outside of
any
Ahlfors regular set. The consequences of this fact are numerous and powerful and still being explored (see, e.g.
[
Fen21
,
Per22
]
). Relatedly, applications of these regularized distances to free boundary problems have recently been discovered in
[
DEGT22
]
.

The present paper has started with the natural question for which other kernels
K
one can characterize rectifiability of a
d
-dimensional set,
d\leq n-1
, using a generalized distance

(1.2)

D_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)\equiv R_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)^{1/\alpha}\equiv\left(\int\dfrac{K(x-y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu(y)\right)^{-1/\alpha}.

However, as we hopefully described above, such questions are far from innocent. The exact properties of the kernel needed for a
characterization
are extremely delicate, which is why very few substitutes for the classical Riesz transform are known (e.g.
[
JM22
,
PPT21
]
), and none of them applies to lower dimensional sets. This is also why it is so hard to check that a given operator satisfies the reflectionless condition of Jaye and Nazarov. Moreover, we cannot even draw an analogy with these few “good” singular integral operators as the cancellations of the kernel responsible for quantifying the geometry of the sets by singular integrals are very different from the behavior our kernels in our distance functions, which are necessarily non-degenerate, hence, emphatically avoiding cancellations. Yet, the present paper achieves a rich and comprehensive theory.

In this paper, to our surprise, and in contrast to the situation for Calderón-Zygmund kernels, in every dimension and co-dimension we produce examples of kernels
K
, which are not perturbations of constants, such that the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu,\alpha}|
characterizes uniform rectifiability. More precisely, using a novel functional-analytic argument, we are able to give examples of distance functions that are equal to the regularized distance
D_{\mu,\alpha}
whenever
\mu
is flat, but which a priori may act very differently outside of general measures, see Theorem
1.5
below. In addition, we establish a perturbative theory, showing that if
K
is close to
K^{\prime}
, a kernel with good behavior outside of flat sets, then the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
also characterizes geometric regularity. A complete description of the kernels for which the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterizes geometric regularity is complicated by the aforementioned construction of a large family of “good” kernels
K^{\prime}
(see, e.g. Theorem
3.3
). Nonetheless, in the radial setting we are indeed able to establish a description of
all
kernels that characterize rectifiability – once again, note the difference with the singular integral operator results where only a few examples and counterexamples are available.

To more precisely discuss our work, we now introduce some definitions.

In what follows, we always take
\mu
to be a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0<d<n
, not necessarily an integer. That is,
\mu
is a measure for which there is a uniform constant
C>0
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle C^{-1}R^{d}

\displaystyle\leq\mu(B(Q,R))\leq CR^{d},

for each
Q\in\text{spt }\mu
and every
R>0
. Given such a
\mu
, a number
\alpha>0
and a function
K\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
we define the regularized distance to
\mathrm{spt}\,\mu=:E
according to formula (
1.2
) above. In particular,

(1.4)

R_{K}(x)=R_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)=\int\dfrac{K(x-y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu(y),

so that
D_{K,\mu,\alpha}=R_{K,\mu,\alpha}^{-1/\alpha}
. When
K\equiv 1
these are the regularized distances (
1.1
) introduced in
[
DFM18
]
and further studied in
[
DEM21
]
. In the latter work it was important that
D_{1,\mu,\alpha}(x)\simeq\mathrm{dist}(x,E)
and that
D
was smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash E
with appropriate estimates. To guarantee that those properties also hold for
D_{K,\mu,\alpha}
we impose the following conditions on the kernel
K
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a positive function
K\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
is a distance-standard kernel if

\|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}\|_{\infty}<\infty,\quad\mbox{for}\quad m=0,1,2,

and

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}K(x)>0.

For such functions, we say that the distance-standard constant associated to
K
is

\displaystyle\max\{\|K\|_{\infty},\|\nabla K(x)|x|\|_{\infty},\|\nabla^{2}K(x)|x|^{2}\|_{\infty},\|1/K\|_{\infty}\}.

Using a dyadic shells argument one can see that
D_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)\simeq\mathrm{dist}(x,E)
with constants depending on
n,d,\alpha
, the Ahlfors regularity constant of
\mu
and the distance-standard constant of
K
. Furthermore, one can differentiate under the integral to show that
D_{K,\alpha,\mu}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash E)
. To simplify notation we write
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
and denote by
\delta_{E}(x)=\text{dist}(x,E)
the Euclidean distance to the set
E
. Also, we often drop the dependence of
D_{K,\mu,\alpha}
on
\mu
and
\alpha
when clear from context, and instead write
D_{K}
or
D_{K,\mu}
. Respectively, we often denote the original regularized distance
D_{\mu,\alpha}
by
D_{1}
or
D_{1,\mu}
. Whenever
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
d
-plane, we take
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E}
unless otherwise specified.

As mentioned above, we hope to characterize geometric regularity by the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu,\alpha}|
. Following
[
DEM21
]
we measure this oscillation in two ways. The first one pertains to the existence of non-tangential limits.

Definition 1.2
.

For
Q\in E,R>0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
we let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R,\eta}(Q):=\{x\in\Omega\cap B(Q,R)\;;\;\text{dist}(x,E)\geq\eta|x-Q|\}.

We say that
f
has a non-tangential limit,
L
, at
Q\in E
if there is some
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\downarrow 0}\sup_{x\in\Gamma_{R,\eta}(Q)}|f(x)-L|=0.

In this setting, we write
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}f(x)=L
.

To address the second one, we introduce

(1.5)

\displaystyle F_{K}(x)\equiv F_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle:=\delta_{E}(x)\left|\nabla\left|\nabla D_{K}(x)\right|^{2}\right|,\;x\in\Omega.

The quantity
F_{K}
measures the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
in a scale-invariant way. For a general
d
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
,
F_{K}
is merely a bounded continuous function. One of the main results of this paper is that regularity (uniform rectifiability) of the boundary of
\Omega
is equivalent to an enhanced estimate controlling oscillations of
|\nabla D_{K}|
through the following Carleson measure condition on
F_{K}
:

(1.6)

\sup_{Q\in E}\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{B(Q,r)\cap\Omega}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\,dx<\infty.

Following
[
DEM21
]
and inspired by
[
DS93
]
we refer to condition (
1.6
) as the USFE (usual square function estimate).

Let us now carefully define rectifiability and uniform rectifiability.

Definition 1.3
.

A set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
d
-rectifiable for
d\in\mathbb{N}
if there exist countably many Lipschitz maps
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}\Bigl(E\setminus\bigcup_{j}f_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=0.

If
\mu
is a Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we say that
\mu
is
d
-rectifiable if
\mu\ll\mathcal{H}^{d}
and there is a
d
-rectifiable Borel subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E)=0
.

Definition 1.4
.

A
d
-Ahlfors regular set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
d
-uniformly rectifiable for
d\in\mathbb{N}
if there exist uniform constants
M,\theta>0
such that for each
Q\in E
and each
R>0
there is a Lipschitz map
f:B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Lipschitz norm
\leq M
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{d}(E\cap B(Q,R)\cap f(B(0,R)))\geq\theta R^{d}.

If
\mu
is
d
-Ahlfors regular measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we say that
\mu
is
d
-uniformly rectifiable if its support is
d
-uniformly rectifiable.

Theorem 1.5 in
[
DEM21
]
says that a set
E
is rectifiable if and only if
|\nabla D_{1,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}|
has non-tangential limits almost-everywhere on
E
. One could draw the aforementioned parallels between this result and the Riesz transform characterization, in particular,
[
Tol08
]
, as

\nabla D_{1,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int\dfrac{1}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu(y)\right)^{-1/\alpha-1}\int\dfrac{x-y}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha+2}}\;d\mu(y).

Setting formally
\alpha=-1
above and properly re-interpreting the integrals would transform the latter term into the classical Riesz transform. However, our
\alpha
is always a positive number, so that the resultant expression, while analogous, is actually a quite surprising extension of the concept of the Riesz transform (note that the expression for
\alpha>0
is nonlinear, and does not represent a Calderón-Zygmund singular integral). In a similar vein, inspired by work of David and Semmes
[
DS93
]
on square functions,
[
DEM21
, Theorem 1.4]
states that a set
E
is uniformly rectifiable if and only if
F_{1,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}
satisfies a Carleson measure estimate outside of
E
.

Here, we ask for which
K
do the following hold:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mu\text{ is }d\text{-rectifiable}\text{ if and only if the non-tangential limits of }|\nabla D_{K}|\text{ exist $\mu$ a.e. in }E,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mu\text{ is }d\text{-uniformly rectifiable}\text{ if and only if }D_{K}\text{ satisfies the USFE on }\Omega.

We first show in Section
2
that the answers to (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are both yes whenever

(1.9)

D_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}(x)=c\delta_{E}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash E,\qquad\forall E\in G(n,d).

Here, and throughout the paper, we use
G(n,d)
to denote the Grassmannian of
d
-dimensional planes through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use
A(n,d)
to denote
d
-dimensional affine sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Our main result is that there is a large family of distance standard kernels which satisfy the above relation:

Theorem 1.5

(Main Theorem)
.

For each pair of integers
d<n
, and every
\alpha>0
there exists a non-constant smooth distance-standard kernel,
K
, which satisfies (
1.9
). In particular, the characterizations of rectifiability by (
1.7
) and the characterization of uniform rectifiability by (
1.8
) are both valid for such
K
.

Furthermore,
K
may be chosen to be far from being constant in the sense that
K_{\lambda}(x):=K(\lambda x)
converges to a non-constant kernel in
C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash 0)
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.

On the other hand if
K
is invariant under rotations then
K
satisfies (
1.9
) if and only if
K
is a constant.

We are then able to show that the answers to questions (
1.7
), (
1.8
) are yes if and only if
D_{K}
is “close to” a

which satisfies (
1.9
). In the radial setting, where the only kernels which satisfy (
1.9
) are constants, measuring “closeness” is relatively straightfoward:

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that
K
is radial and distance-standard. Then for any
d
-Ahlfors regular
\mu
which is
d
-rectifiable in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha>0
the non-tangential limits of

exist for

a.e. in
E=\text{spt }\mu
(for cones of every aperture) if and only if
K_{\lambda}=K(\lambda\,\cdot)\rightarrow c
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
for some constant

.

Conversely, if for any
d
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
, the non-tangential limits of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu,\alpha}|
exist for
\mu
a.e. in

(for every aperture), then

must be
d
-rectifiable and

must satisfy
K_{\lambda}=K(\lambda\,\cdot)\rightarrow c
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
as

for some constant

.

For kernels which are not rotationally invariant the analogue of Theorem
1.6
is less clean due to the fact that
K(\lambda_{j}\,\cdot)
could approach different kernels
K^{\prime}
satisfying (
1.9
) along different sequences

. In fact we construct such an example in Theorem

.

Pertaining to question (
1.8
), we work with a Dini-type condition:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
0<d<n
not necessarily an integer, and let
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
K\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
is radial, distance-standard, and
\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If

satisfies the USFE, then

is an integer and

is
d
-uniformly rectifiable.

Conversely, if we assume in addition that
K
is such that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{0}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{\infty}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}<\infty

for some constants
K_{0},K_{\infty}>0
and for

, then

satisfies the USFE in

-uniformly rectifiable measure

.

In the setting of uniform rectifiability, we are unable to prove that the integral condition (
1.10
) on

is sharp. This is due to our inability to quantify the non-existence of other radial kernels satisfying (
1.9
) in Theorem

. However, we are able to show that

must converge to appropriate constants near zero and infinity (see Theorem

). We also have results in the perturbative regime for non-radial

Let us conclude by outlining the remaining sections of the paper. In Section
2
, we study properties of distance-exact kernels (i.e. kernels satisfying (
1.9
)). In particular we show that for these kernels the answers to (

) and (

) are yes and we prove Theorem
1.5
. The existence portion of Theorem
1.5
is proven using a functional analytic approach followed by a smoothing argument. The construction of distance exact kernels which are far from being constant is proven using a scaling construction. Finally, we show that distance-exact radial kernels must be constants using Wiener-Tauberian theory.

In Section
3
we attempt to answer (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) using a perturbative analysis. In terms of non-tangential limits, (
1.7
), we obtain a complete answer in Theorems
3.2
and

. This is done using blowup arguments, specifically tangent measures. We also show by construction that our results are sharp without radial symmetry, see Theorem
3.3
.

We then continue on to (

), and develop sufficient conditions on

to guarantee that

satisfies the USFE for all planes, and from there, for all

-uniformly rectifiable measures. Our main result is Theorem

, which identifies a uniform condition on the growth of

for the distance to satisfy the USFE outside of uniformly-rectifiable sets. This condition seems painful to check in practice but we give other simpler conditions on

satisfies the uniform growth condition, for example, Lemma

(in contrast with

-planes

Finally, we conclude Section

2.
Distance-exact kernels

Recall from
[
DEM21
]
that a fundamental property of the regularized distance functions functions,
D_{1,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}
, is that they are equal to (a multiple of) Euclidean distance when
E
is affine. Let us generalize this notion for general kernels
K
:

Definition 2.1
.

If
K\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a function such that for each
d
-plane
E
, there is a constant
c_{E}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

(2.1)

\displaystyle D_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}(x)\equiv c_{E}\delta_{E}(x),

then we say that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact. If the constants
c_{E}\equiv 0
for each
E
, we say that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Finally, if (
2.1
) holds for a single
d
-plane
E
, then we say that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact for
E
.

Analogously to
[
DEM21
]
we start by showing that if a smooth enough
K
is distance exact, then the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterizes the regularity of
E
. In particular the following theorems hold:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
,
\mu
be a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure and let
\alpha>0
. If
K
is distance-standard and
(d,\alpha
)-distance-exact, then
\mu
is
d
-rectifiable if and only if for each
\eta\in(0,1)
,
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K,\mu,\alpha}(x)|
exists for
\mu
almost all
Q\in\text{spt }\mu
(See Definition
1.2
for the precise definition of non-tangential limits).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
,
\mu
be a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure and let
\alpha>0
. If
K\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact and
\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
D_{K,\mu,\alpha}
satisfies the USFE if and only if
\mu
is
d
-uniformly rectifiable.

We present the proofs of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
in the Appendix
A
.

In view of the above, to find kernels whose associated regularized distance characterizes geometric regularity, it suffices to understand distance-exact kernels. In what follows we first make some observations regarding distance-exact kernels with extra symmetries (i.e. radial or spherical invariance); this is the content of Section
2.1
. Further explicit computations for zero-homogeneous kernels are left to Appendix
B
, since the discussion is slightly tangential to the current one. The main result of this section (proven in Section
2.2
) is that there exist “far from constant” distance-exact kernels which can be taken to be arbitrarily smooth (Theorem
2.7
).

Before moving on we record the following useful Lemma:

Lemma 2.4
.

A function
K\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance exact with constants
c_{E}\equiv c
independent of the plane
E
if and only if there some constant
\tilde{c}
so that
K-\tilde{c}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance orthogonal.

Proof.

Recall that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact with constants
c_{E}\equiv c
independent of
E
if and only if for every
E\in G(n,d)
and every
x\not\in E
, we have

\displaystyle R_{K,E,\alpha}(x)\equiv c^{-\alpha}\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha}.

Given a
c
there exists a
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that if
\tilde{K}(x)\equiv c_{1}
then
R_{\tilde{K},E,\alpha}=c^{-\alpha}\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha}
for any affine
E
. The result follows from the linearity of
R_{K,E,\alpha}(x)
in
K
(for
E,x
and
\alpha
fixed).
∎

2.1.
Distance-exact kernels with additional symmetries

We briefly investigate distance-exact kernels with additional symmetry: either 0-homogeneity or rotational invariance. In particular, we first show that rotationally invariant (i.e. depending only on the radial variable) distance-exact kernels must be constant (this is Theorem
2.5
). On the other hand we show that all
0
-homogeneous kernels whose associated distance characterizes good geometry must be distance exact. We leave to Appendix
B
the existence and non-existence of non-constant distance-exact
0
-homogeneous kernels in various settings.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
K\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact and radial (i.e.,
K(x)=\tilde{K}(|x|)
for
\tilde{K}\in C(0,\infty)\cap L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
). Then
K
is constant.

Proof.

Since
K
is invariant under rotations, the constants
c_{E}
in the definition of distance-exactness are independent of
E\in G(n,d)
. In particular, Lemma
2.4
shows that
K-c
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal for an appropriate constant
c
. In light of this, it suffices to prove that if
K
is radial and
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal, then
K\equiv 0
. In what follows, we abuse notation and use
K
both for the function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and for the function
\tilde{K}
defined on
(0,\infty)
for which
K(x)=\tilde{K}(|x|)
.

Let
E
be a
d
-plane (equipped with the Hausdorff measure), and let
x\not\in E
. Writing
R_{K}(x)
as an integral in polar coordinates about the point
P_{E}(x)\in E
, one can compute

\displaystyle R_{K}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{K(\sqrt{\delta_{E}(x)^{2}+s^{2}})}{\bigl(\delta_{E}(x)^{2}+s^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d+\alpha}{2}}}\,s^{d-1}\;ds

\displaystyle=c_{d}\int_{\delta_{E}(x)}^{\infty}\dfrac{K(t)}{t^{d+\alpha-1}}\bigl(t^{2}-\delta_{E}(x)^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\;ds.

It follows that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal if and only if for each
r>0
, one has

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{\infty}\dfrac{K(t)}{t^{d+\alpha-1}}\bigl(t^{2}-r^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\;dt=0.

When
d=2
, the fundamental theorem of calculus implies that
K\equiv 0
for any
\alpha>0
. When
d=2j>2
, one may differentiate the integral to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{\infty}\dfrac{K(t)}{t^{d+\alpha-1}}\bigl(t^{2}-r^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\;dt=0.

So if
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance orthogonal then it is also
(d-2,\alpha+2)
-distance orthogonal. Repeating this process a total of
j-1
times yields
K\equiv 0
. This completes the proof when
d
is even.

For odd
d
arguing as above reduces to the case when
d=1
. To prove that the only
(1,\alpha)
-distance orthogonal radial kernel is trivial, we use Weiner-Tauberian theory. For each
r>0
define the function

\displaystyle f_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\chi_{(r,\infty)}(t)\dfrac{1}{t^{\alpha}\sqrt{t^{2}-r^{2}}}.

Let
W=\overline{\text{span}\{f_{r}(t)\;:r>0\}}\subset L^{1}(0,\infty)
, and note that
W
is closed under dilations, since for
\lambda>0

\displaystyle f_{r}(\lambda t)

\displaystyle=\chi_{(r,\infty)}(\lambda t)\dfrac{1}{(\lambda t)^{\alpha}\sqrt{(\lambda t)^{2}-r^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-\alpha-1}\chi_{(r/\lambda,\infty)}(t)\dfrac{1}{t^{\alpha}\sqrt{t^{2}-(r/\lambda)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-\alpha-1}f_{r/\lambda}(t)\in W.

By definition, if
K
is distance-orthogonal then
K\in W^{\perp}\subset L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
. Thus to show
K\equiv 0
it suffices to show
W=L^{1}(0,\infty)
. To this end, we consider the linear isomorphism

T:L^{1}(0,\infty)\rightarrow L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

that maps
f(x)
to
e^{x}f(e^{x})
. Note that that
T(W)\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is a closed subspace of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that is also closed under translations, to wit,

\displaystyle(Tf)(x+a)

\displaystyle=e^{a}e^{x}f(e^{a}e^{x})=e^{a}Tg(x)

for
g(x):=f(e^{a}x)\in W
.

The Wiener Theorem (cf. Theorem 9.3 in
[
Rud91
]
) implies that
T(W)=L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
(and thus
K\equiv 0
) if

\displaystyle Z(T(W))

\displaystyle\equiv\bigcap\limits_{g\in T(W)}\{s\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;\hat{g}(s)=0\}=\emptyset.

This is a direct computation; we show that for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{x}f_{1}(e^{x})e^{-2\pi ixs}\;dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{x(1-\alpha)}}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}}e^{-2\pi ixs}\;dx\neq 0.

For
s=0
, this is obvious since
e^{x}f_{r}(e^{x})\geq 0
. Furthermore the case
s>0
and
s<0
are identical up to a change of sign, so we may assume
s>0
. Observe that,
\dfrac{e^{x(1-\alpha)}}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}}
is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, and thus

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{x(1-\alpha)}}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}}\sin(2\pi xs)\;dx>0,

as desired.
∎

The next natural symmetry class to consider is homogeneous of degree
0
kernels, i.e.
K
such that
K(\lambda x)=K(x)
for every
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our first observation is that for kernels that are homogeneous of degree zero, being distance-exact is a necessary requirement in order for
D_{K}
to satisfy the USFE outside of each
E\in G(n,d)
:

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\alpha>0
and let
K
be a distance-standard, homogeneous of degree zero kernel with the property that for each
E\in G(n,d)
,
D_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}
satisfies the USFE. Then necessarily
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact.

Proof.

Suppose that
E\in G(n,d)
is fixed, and
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E}
. Since
K
is zero-homogeneous, it is easy to see that
R_{K}
is homogeneous of degree
-\alpha
, and thus
D_{K}
is homogeneous of degree
1
, and
F_{K}
is homogeneous of degree zero. Moreover, since
D_{K}
is translation invariant with respect to
E
(because
E
is a plane), we have that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,R)}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\;dx\geq cR^{d}\int_{E^{\perp}\cap B(0,cR)}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(x),

where
E^{\perp}\in G(n,n-d)
is the orthogonal complement to
E
and
0<c<1
is a dimensional constant so that
(B(0,cR)\cap E)+(B(0,cR))\cap E^{\perp}\subset B(0,R)
. Since
D_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E},\alpha}
satisfies the USFE, we readily see that taking
R\rightarrow\infty
in (
2.3
) yields

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{E^{\perp}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(x)<\infty.

We show this implies
F_{K}\equiv 0
.

By the coarea formula with the Lipschitz function
\delta_{E}(x)
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{E^{\perp}\cap B(0,R)}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\;

\displaystyle d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\left(\int_{E^{\perp}\cap\{\delta_{E}(x)=t\}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d-1}(x)\right)\;dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}t^{-n+d}\left(\int_{E^{\perp}\cap\{\delta_{E}(x)=t\}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d-1}(x)\right)\;dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}t^{-1}\left(\int_{E^{\perp}\cap\{\delta_{E}(x)=1\}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d-1}(x)\right)\;dt

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{E^{\perp}\cap\{\delta_{E}(x)=1\}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d-1}(x)\right)\int_{0}^{R}\dfrac{dt}{t}.

Combining this with (
2.4
), we see that

(2.5)

\int_{E^{\perp}\cap\{\delta_{E}(x)=1\}}F_{K}(x)^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d-1}(x)=0.

Since
F_{K}
is scale-invariant, and
F_{K}(x)=F_{K}(x+v)
for any
v\in E
, we have that
F_{K}
is constant on the set
\{\delta_{E}(x)=1\}
, and if (
2.5
) holds,
F_{K}\equiv 0
. From here it follows that
|\nabla D_{K}|
is constant on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash E
and thus Theorem 3.1 in
[
DEM21
]
implies that
D_{K}\equiv c\delta_{E}(x)
for some
c=c_{E}>0
. This shows that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact.
∎

In contrast to the radial case, we can construct many examples of
0
-homogeneous non-constant
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact kernels (and even guarantee that the constant
c_{E}
is independent of
E\in G(n,d)
). We leave such computations to Appendix
B
, but want to draw attention to Corollaries
B.3
and
B.4
, which depending on the choice of
n,d,\alpha
, show that continuous examples of 0-homogeneous
(d,\alpha)
-distance exact kernels exist and or do not exist respectively.

2.2.
Existence of non-trivial distance-exact kernels

The goal of this subsection is to prove the first part of our main Theorem
1.5
:

Theorem 2.7
.

For each choice of
n,d,m\in\mathbb{N},
with
1\leq d<n
and
\alpha>0
, there exists a non-constant
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact kernel
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
so that
|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,
K
can be constructed so that the constant
c_{E}
in (
2.1
) is independent of
E
.

In light of Corollary
B.4
some of these kernels are not zero-homogeneous. Additionally, in contrast with Corollary
B.3
, we can guarantee the existence of said kernels for any
\alpha>0
and in any co-dimension.

By Lemma
2.4
we can consider distance-orthogonal kernels. Our first step is to construct a non-zero, distance-orthogonal kernel using functional analytic methods which may or may not have the desired smoothness. In the following lemma, we denote by
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
the closure, under the sup norm, of continuous functions with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. In particular if
f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
then
f(x)\rightarrow 0
as
|x|
goes to
\infty
and
0
.

Theorem 2.8
.

For each choice of
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
, and
\alpha>0
, there exists
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
K\not\equiv 0
, but
R_{K,E,\alpha}\equiv 0
outside of
E
for each
d
-plane
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Take
X=C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. As
X
is a closed subset of
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it is a Banach space (when endowed with the supremum norm). Note that for
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that
R_{K,E,\alpha}\equiv 0
for each
d
-plane
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{E}\dfrac{K(z)}{|z|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(z)=0

for each
E\in A(n,d)
not containing the origin. This is because the affine change of variables
z=x-y
, which maps
E
to the plane
x-E
, preserves
\mathcal{H}^{d}
measure. Hence, a kernel
K
satisfies the conclusion of the Theorem if and only if
K
is orthogonal to each measure of the form
d\mu(x)=|x|^{-d-\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E}(x)
where
E\in A(n,d)
does not contain the origin. For the sake of convenience, denote each such measure by
\mu_{E}
. Define
M\subset X^{*}
to be the weak-star closure of the subspace

\displaystyle\text{span}\{\mu_{E}\;:\;E\in A(n,d)\text{ with }0\not\in E\},

where we view each measure
\mu_{E}
as an element in
X^{*}
. Then
R_{K,E,\alpha}\equiv 0
for each
E\in A(n,d)
if and only if
K\in{}^{\bot}M
, where

\displaystyle{}^{\bot}M\equiv\{f\in X\;:\;\Lambda(f)=0\text{ for all }\Lambda\in M\}.

By the Hahn-Banach separation theorem, the existence of such a
K
is equivalent to the existence of an
x\neq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\delta_{x}\not\in M
. Here,
\delta_{x}\in M
is the functional
\delta_{x}(K^{\prime})=K^{\prime}(x)
. We will actually show something stronger, that
\delta_{x}\notin M
for all
0\neq x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
x_{0}\neq 0
be given, and suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\delta_{x_{0}}\in M
. By definition there exist complex measures of the form
\nu_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}a_{j}^{i}\mu_{E_{j}^{i}}
where
a_{j}^{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
0\not\in E_{j}^{i}\in A(n,d)
are distinct such that
\nu_{i}\rightharpoonup\delta_{x_{0}}
. That is, for each
f\in X
,
\int f\;d\nu_{i}\rightarrow f(x_{0})
. Define
T_{i}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by
T_{i}(f)=\int f\;d\nu_{i}
. The
T_{i}
are bounded linear functionals on
X
, and moreover,

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}|T_{i}(f)|<\infty

since
T_{i}(f)\rightarrow f(x_{0})
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. By the Uniform Boundedness Principle,
\sup_{i}\|T_{i}\|=B<\infty
. One can check that
\|T_{i}\|=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}|a_{j}^{i}|\;\mu_{E_{j}^{i}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since the
E_{j}^{i}
are distinct, and since distinct
E_{j}^{i}
intersect in affine sets of zero
\mathcal{H}^{d}
measure.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given so that
\epsilon<|x_{0}|
, and choose
\phi_{\epsilon}\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
so that
0\leq\phi_{\epsilon}\leq 1
,
\phi_{\epsilon}\equiv 1
on
B(x_{0},\epsilon/2)
, and so that
\text{supp }\phi_{\epsilon}\subset B(x_{0},\epsilon)
. Fix some
f\in X
with
f(x_{0})\neq 0
. A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle\delta_{x_{0}}(f)

\displaystyle=\delta_{x_{0}}(f\phi_{\epsilon})=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f\phi_{\epsilon}\;d\nu_{i}.

Note though that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\phi_{\epsilon}\;d\nu_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}|\nu_{i}|(B(x_{0},\epsilon)).

If we can show that

(2.6)

|\nu_{i}|(B(x_{0},\epsilon))\downarrow 0\text{ uniformly in }i\text{ as }\epsilon\downarrow 0,

then we will have obtained the contradiction with
\delta_{x_{0}}(f)=0
, and conclude that
\delta_{x_{0}}\not\in M
.

For each
\epsilon
, we set

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon}\equiv\sup_{0\not\in E\in A(n,d)}\dfrac{\mu_{E}(B(x_{0},\epsilon))}{\mu_{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1.

Since
\mu_{E}(B(x_{0},\epsilon))\leq C_{\epsilon}\mu_{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})<\infty
, for any
E\in A(n,d)
not containing the origin, we have that

\displaystyle|\nu_{i}|(B(x_{0},\epsilon))\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}|a_{j}^{i}|\mu_{E_{j}^{i}}(B(x_{0},\epsilon))\leq C_{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}|a_{j}^{i}|\mu_{E_{j}^{i}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=C_{\epsilon}\|T_{i}\|\leq C_{\epsilon}B.

In particular, it suffices to show
C_{\epsilon}\downarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Let
\epsilon\ll|x_{0}|
and
E
be an arbitrary
d
-affine plane not containing the origin. We consider two cases; first, if
\mathrm{dist}(0,E)>2|x_{0}|
, then
B(x_{0},\epsilon)\cap E=\emptyset
and
\mu_{E}(B(x_{0},\epsilon))/\mu_{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=0
. On the other hand if
\mathrm{dist}(0,E)<2|x_{0}|
, then we have the lower bound
\mu_{E}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\gtrsim\mathrm{dist}(0,E)^{-\alpha}\gtrsim|x_{0}|^{-\alpha}
. It is then easy to estimate

\mu_{E}(B(x_{0},\epsilon))\lesssim\frac{\epsilon^{d}}{(|x_{0}|-\epsilon)^{d+\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{\epsilon^{d}}{|x_{0}|^{d+\alpha}},

where in the last inequality we used that
\epsilon\ll|x_{0}|
. Putting all this together we get that

C_{\epsilon}\lesssim|x_{0}|^{\alpha}\frac{\epsilon^{d}}{|x_{0}|^{d+\alpha}}=\left(\frac{\epsilon}{|x_{0}|}\right)^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon\downarrow 0}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

∎

We cannot naïvely adapt the above argument to guarantee that the kernel we obtain is smooth (in particular, distance-standard). This is because we do not have the crucial equality
\|T_{i}\|=\sum_{j}^{i}|a_{j}^{i}|\mu_{E_{j}^{i}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
when we consider the norm in
(C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}))^{*}
. Instead, we smooth out the
K
obtained above, first along each ray from the origin and then along each spherical shell.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Let
\phi\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, and suppose that
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Then the kernel defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}K(tx)\phi(t)\;dt

is also
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Moreover, if we assume that
\phi\in C^{k}(0,\infty)
with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{m}|\phi^{(m)}(t)|\;dt<\infty

for
m=1,2,\dotsc,k
, then
f_{x}(t)=\tilde{K}(xt)
is
C^{k}(0,\infty)
in
t
for each
x\neq 0
, and

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}(f_{x}(t))t^{m}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}\leq M_{m}<\infty

for
m=0,1,\dotsc,k
. Here the
M_{m}
are independent of
x
.

Proof.

Observe that since
K
is bounded,
\tilde{K}
is also bounded, with
\|\tilde{K}\|_{\infty}\leq\|K\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{1}
, and thus
R_{\tilde{K}}
is well-defined.

Note that since
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal, the kernel
K_{R}(x)\equiv K(Rx)
also is. Indeed, for each
d
-plane
E
through the origin and each
x\not\in E
, we have

\displaystyle R_{K_{R},E}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\dfrac{K(R(x-y))}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)=R^{\alpha}\int_{E}\dfrac{K(Rx-w)}{|Rx-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)=R^{\alpha}R_{K,E}(Rx).

As such, if
R_{K,E}\equiv 0
, then
R_{K_{R},E}\equiv 0
as well.

Now fix
E\in A(n,d)
. By Fubini’s theorem, we compute
R_{\tilde{K}}(x)
for
x\not\in E
:

\displaystyle R_{\tilde{K}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\dfrac{\tilde{K}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\dfrac{1}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(t(x-y))\phi(t)\;dt\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t)R_{K_{t},E}(x)\;dt\equiv 0,

so that
R_{\tilde{K}}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. This proves the first claim.

Now let us suppose that
\phi\in C^{k}(0,\infty)
as above. Then we remark that for
x\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(xt)\phi(t)\;dt=\dfrac{1}{|x|}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(xs/|x|)\phi(s/|x|)\;ds.

It follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\lambda x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda|x|}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(xs/|x|)\phi(s/(\lambda|x|))\;ds.

The right-hand side is differentiable in
\lambda
with derivative

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\tilde{K}(\lambda x)=\dfrac{-1}{\lambda^{2}|x|}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K(xs/|x|)\phi(s/(\lambda|x|))\;ds-\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{3}|x|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}sK(xs/|x|)\phi^{\prime}(s/(\lambda|x|))\;ds

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-1}{\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}K(\lambda xt)\phi(t)\;dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}K(\lambda xt)t\phi^{\prime}(t)\;dt\right)\

which is bounded in absolute value by
\lambda^{-1}\|K\|_{\infty}\left(\|\phi\|_{1}+\|t\phi^{\prime}(t)\|_{1}\right)
. This proves the claim on the first derivative of
\lambda\rightarrow K(\lambda x)
, and the arguments for the higher derivatives of this function follow in the same fashion.
∎

The following lemma uses the rotation invariance of
\delta_{E}
to smooth out
K
in the tangential directions while preserving the distance-orthogonality.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Denote by
X
the special orthogonal group
SO(n)
, and
\nu
its Haar measure. Let
\phi\in L^{1}(X,\nu)
, and suppose that
K
is bounded and
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Then the kernel defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}K(Ax)\phi(A)\;d\nu(A)

is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Moreover,
\tilde{K}
satisfies the two following smoothness conditions:

(1)

If
\phi\in C^{k}(X)
, then for each
x\neq 0,
we have that
A\rightarrow K(Ax)
is in
C^{k}(X)
with uniformly bounded Lie derivatives.

(2)

If for
x\neq 0
, the map
f_{x}(\lambda)=K(\lambda x)
is in
C^{k}(0,\infty)
, then so is the map
\tilde{f}_{x}(\lambda)=\tilde{K}(\lambda x)
. Moreover
|\tilde{f}^{(m)}_{x}(\lambda)|\lesssim\sup_{|y|=|x|}|f^{(m)}_{y}(\lambda)|
for
m=1,2,\dotsc,k
with constant depending only on
n,d
and
\|\phi\|_{1}
.

Proof.

With
\tilde{K}
as above, we have that
\tilde{K}
is bounded, since
\|\tilde{K}\|_{\infty}\leq\|K\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{1}
, and thus
R_{\tilde{K}}
is well-defined.

The first part of the proof is quite similar to the argument of Lemma
2.9
. Indeed, for each
d
-plane
E
through the origin and each
x\not\in E
, we have

\displaystyle R_{K_{A},E}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\dfrac{K(A(x-y))}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)=\int_{AE}\dfrac{K(Ax-w)}{|Ax-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)=R_{K,AE}(Ax).

Here we have used the fact that
A
preserves Euclidean distance. Thus
K_{A}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance orthogonal whenever
K
is.

Now fix
E\in A(n,d)
. Invoking Fubini’s theorem, we compute
R_{\tilde{K}}(x)
for
x\not\in E
:

\displaystyle R_{\tilde{K},E}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\dfrac{\tilde{K}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)=\int_{E}\dfrac{1}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\int_{X}K(A(x-y))\phi(A)d\nu(A)\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\phi(A)\int_{E}\dfrac{K(A(x-y))}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)\;d\nu(A)=\int_{X}\phi(A)R_{K_{A},E}(x)\;d\nu(A)\equiv 0,

so that
R_{\tilde{K}}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. This proves the first claim.

Now let us suppose that
\phi\in C^{k}(X)
. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},x\neq 0
. Since
\nu
is a Haar measure, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(Bx)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}K(ABx)\phi(A)\;d\nu(A)=\int_{X}K(Ax)\phi(AB^{-1})\;d\nu(A).

Since
\phi\in C^{k}(X)
, it is easy to see that the map
B\rightarrow\int_{X}K(Ax)\phi(AB^{-1})\;d\nu(A)
also is, which gives the desired smoothness.

To verify that
\tilde{K}
stays smooth in the radial direction (i.e. statement (II)), we compute

\displaystyle h^{-1}\left(\tilde{f}_{x}(\lambda+h)-\tilde{f}_{x}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}h^{-1}\left(K((\lambda+h)Ax)-K(\lambda Ax)\right)\phi(A)\;d\nu(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}h^{-1}\left(f_{Ax}(\lambda+h)-f_{Ax}(\lambda)\right)\phi(A)\;d\nu(A),

whence

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{x}^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}f_{Ax}^{\prime}(\lambda)\phi(A)d\nu(A).

Since
|Ax|=|x|
for
A\in X
, we also conclude the estimate on
|\tilde{f}_{x}^{\prime}(\lambda)|
. The same argument is used to prove the statement for the higher order derivatives of
\tilde{f}
, and thus (II) is proved.
∎

Using these two smoothing lemmas we are ready prove Theorem
2.7
:

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

In view of Lemma
2.4
, it suffices to show that one can construct a non-zero, smooth
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal kernel with the same smoothness.
This is essentially a combination of the two Lemmas above.

By Theorem
2.8
, we may choose a
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal kernel
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that for some
x_{0}\neq 0
,
K(x_{0})\neq 0
. Choose
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
so that

\int_{0}^{\infty}K(tx_{0})\phi(t)\;dt\neq 0.

By Lemma
2.9
(since
\phi
is smooth with compact support in
(0,\infty)
) the kernel
K_{1}(x)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}K(tx)\phi(t)\;dt
satisfies the following:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\text{for each $x\neq$ 0},t\rightarrow K_{1}(tx)\in C^{\infty}(0,\infty),\\
&\sup_{x\neq 0,t\in(0,\infty)}\left|t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}K_{1}(tx)\right|<\infty\text{ for }m\in\mathbb{N},\\
&K_{1}(x_{0})\neq 0,\\
&K_{1}\text{ is }(d,\alpha)$-distance-orthogonal$.\end{cases}

Denote by
X
the special orthogonal group
SO(n)
, and
\nu
its Haar measure. Next, choose
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
so that
\int_{X}K_{1}(Ax_{0})\psi(A)\;d\nu(A)\neq 0
. Then by Lemma
2.10
, the kernel
K_{2}\equiv\int_{X}K_{1}(Ax)\phi(A)\;d\nu(A)
satisfies

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\text{for each $x\neq$ 0},A\rightarrow K_{2}(Ax)\in C^{\infty}(X),\\
&\text{for each $x\neq$ 0},t\rightarrow K_{2}(tx)\in C^{\infty}(0,\infty),\\
&\sup_{x\neq 0,t\in(0,\infty)}\left|t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}K_{2}(tx)\right|<\infty\text{ for }m\in\mathbb{N},\\
&K_{2}(x_{0})\neq 0,\\
&K_{2}\text{ is }(d,\alpha)$-distance-orthogonal$.\end{cases}

Since
K_{2}
is smooth in the radial and tangential directions, we can conclude
K_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
. The bounds on the radial and tangential derivatives of
K_{2}
coming from Lemmas
2.9
and
2.10
give us the required bounds on
|\nabla^{m}K_{2}(x)|\,|x|^{m}
.
∎

The next part of Theorem
1.5
asks that the distance-exact kernels we construct be “far” from constant at the origin. We do this by employing a scaling argument to show that the kernels we constructed above can be taken not to be “close” to constant.

We begin with the observation that the kernels constructed as in the proof of Theorem
2.7
decay at
0
and
\infty
together with their properly normalized derivatives.

Lemma 2.11
.

For each choice of
n\in\mathbb{N},d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and
\alpha>0
, there exists a
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal kernel
0\neq K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
so that
|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
m\geq 0
and so that

(2.10)

\limsup_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{p}|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}|=0=\limsup_{|x|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{m=0}^{p}|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}|.

for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.7
, we start with a
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and construct
K_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(tx)\phi(t)\,dt
where
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,\infty))
. Defining, as above,
f_{x}(\lambda)=K_{1}(\lambda x)
we will show that for each
m\geq 0

\limsup_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\left|\left(\frac{d^{m}f_{x}(\lambda)}{d\lambda^{m}}\right)(1)\right|=0=\limsup_{|x|\rightarrow 0}\left|\left(\frac{d^{m}f_{x}(\lambda)}{d\lambda^{m}}\right)(1)\right|.

The result follows by continuing the construction as in the proof of Theorem
2.7
and the estimate above.

We do the case when
m=1
, the others follow similarly. We recall from (
2.7
) above that

\left|\lambda\frac{d}{d\lambda}f_{x}(\lambda)\right|\leq\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}K(\lambda xt)\phi(t)\;dt\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}K(\lambda xt)t\phi^{\prime}(t)\;dt\right|.

Fixing
\lambda=1
we notice that if
|x|\rightarrow 0,+\infty
but
t\in\mathrm{spt}\phi
then
\lambda t|x|\rightarrow 0,+\infty
and indeed does so uniformly in all
t\in\mathrm{spt}\phi
. Since
K\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
this implies that
K(\lambda xt)\rightarrow 0
and thus both integrals converge to zero in the limit, so we are done.
∎

We are now ready to address the second part of Theorem
1.5
:

Theorem 2.12
.

For each choice of
n\in\mathbb{N},d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and
\alpha>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal kernel
0\neq K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
so that
|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
0\leq m\leq p
and, furthermore,

\displaystyle\limsup_{|x|\rightarrow 0}|K(x)|>0.

We observe that adding such a kernel
K
to any large enough constant gives the desired
(d,\alpha)
-distance exact kernel in Theorem
1.5
.

Proof.

Construct
K_{0}
a non-zero
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal kernel, as in Theorem
2.7
which satisfies the estimate

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq 0}|\nabla^{m}K_{0}(x)|\,|x|^{m}=M_{m}<\infty

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Define
g_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by
g_{K}(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p+1}|\nabla^{\ell}K(x)|\,|x|^{\ell}
. By Lemma
2.11
we may assume

\limsup_{|x|\downarrow 0}g_{K_{0}}(x)=0=\limsup_{|x|\uparrow\infty}g_{K_{0}}(x).

Furthermore, upon a harmless dilation and scalar multiplication (which both preserve distance orthogonality) we can assume that there is
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that
K_{0}(x_{0})=1
.

Our
K
will be the limit of
K_{j}
which are constructed iteratively. Let
\epsilon>0
be small. Choose a sequence
a_{\ell}
decreasing monotonically to zero and
b_{\ell}
increasing monotonically such that
a_{\ell}<1<b_{\ell}
and
a_{\ell+1}\leq a_{\ell}^{2}/b_{\ell}
and, finally, if
|x|\not\in(a_{\ell},b_{\ell})
, then
g_{K_{0}}(x)\leq\epsilon 2^{-\ell}
. Define

\tilde{K}_{\ell}(x)=K_{0}\left(\frac{b_{\ell}}{a_{\ell}}x\right),

and note, by scale invariance that

(2.12)

g_{\tilde{K}_{\ell}}(x)\leq\epsilon 2^{-\ell}\text{ for all }x\neq 0,|x|\not\in(a_{\ell}^{2}/b_{\ell},a_{\ell})\equiv I_{\ell}.

We now define
K_{j}
by

(2.13)

K_{j}(x)=K_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{j}\tilde{K}_{k}(x).

Note that
K_{j}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance orthogonal as it is the sum of distance orthogonal kernels.

We want to show the following:

(2.14)

\displaystyle g_{K_{j}}(x)\leq

\displaystyle 2\sup g_{K_{0}}+\epsilon,

\displaystyle K_{j}\left(\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}x_{0}\right)\geq

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon,\qquad\forall\ell\leq j.

To give an upper bound on the estimate of
g_{K_{j}}
we use the upper bound in (
2.12
), the triangle inequality, the disjointness of the
I_{\ell}
and the scale invariance of the definition of
g
to say

\displaystyle g_{K_{j}}(x)\leq

\displaystyle g_{K_{0}}(x)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}g_{\tilde{K}_{\ell}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{K_{0}}(x)+\max_{k}g_{\tilde{K}_{k}}(x)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\epsilon 2^{-\ell}\leq 2\sup g_{K_{0}}+\epsilon.

To get the lower bound on
K_{j}
at the sequence of points
x_{\ell}:=\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}x_{0}
we observe that
\tilde{K}_{k}(x)\leq g_{\tilde{K}_{k}}(x)
(and similarly for
K_{0}
) and that if
k\neq\ell
then
x_{\ell}\notin I_{k}
to conclude that

\displaystyle K_{j}\left(x_{\ell}\right)\geq

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{\ell}(x_{\ell})-\sum_{k\neq\ell}|\tilde{K}_{k}(x_{\ell})|-|K_{0}(x_{\ell})|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle K_{0}(x_{0})-\sum_{k\neq\ell}g_{\tilde{K}_{k}}(x_{\ell})-g_{K_{0}}(x_{\ell})\geq K_{0}(x_{0})-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon 2^{-k}=1-\epsilon.

Having proven the two conditions in (
2.14
) we invoke Arzela-Ascoli and a standard diagonalization argument to say that from
K_{j}
we may extract a subsequence
K_{j_{\ell}}
and a limiting function
K\in C^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for which
K_{j_{\ell}}\rightarrow K
in
C^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}|\nabla^{\ell}K(x)|\,|x|^{\ell}\leq 2\sup g_{K_{0}}(y)+\epsilon.

One may apply Lemma
3.1
and the fact that each
K_{j_{\ell}}
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal to deduce that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-orthogonal. Moreover, (
2.14
) implies that the limiting kernel
K
has
K(x_{k})\geq 1-\epsilon
, for each
k
. Since
|x_{k}|\rightarrow 0
, we have that
K
is our desired kernel.
∎

3.
A perturbation theory for regularized distance kernels

In this section we ask the perturbation question: if
K
is “close” to a distance-exact kernel does the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterize good geometry
and, vice versa, if the oscillation of
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterizes good geometry must it be that
K
is close to being distance exact? Interestingly, using Theorem
2.12
we show that just because
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterizes good geometry does not mean it is a perturbation of a single distance exact kernel (cf. Theorem
3.3
below).

On the other hand, under the additional assumption of radial symmetry, which by Theorem
2.5
simplifies the space of distance exact kernels, we are able to show that the oscillations of
|\nabla D_{K}|
characterize the geometry of
E
when
K
is a perturbation of a constant; what we mean by perturbation depends on the context and we make it precise below. We also establish some weaker results in the absence of radial symmetry.

Finally, as alluded to above, the direction “good control on
|\nabla D_{K}|
implies good geometry of
E
” holds for essentially all distance standard kernels
K
. This is because good control on
|\nabla D_{K}|
actually implies that
K
is close to being distance exact (cf. Corollaries
3.5
and
3.15
).

3.1.
Non-tangential limits and rectifiability.

Let us first address the question of rectifiability of
\mu
in terms of non-tangential limits of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu}|
. We want to use compactness techniques so we first establish that if
K_{i}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in the appropriate sense, then
R_{K_{i}}\rightarrow R_{K_{\infty}}
and
D_{K_{i}}\rightarrow D_{K_{\infty}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
\mu_{i}
are a sequence of uniformly
d
-Ahlfors regular measures with supports
E_{i}
such that
\mu_{i}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\infty}
. Let
E_{\infty}
be the support of
\mu_{\infty}
. Suppose in addition that
K_{i}\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
with
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sup_{i}\|\nabla^{j}K_{i}(x)|x|^{j}\|_{\infty}=:M<\infty,

and such that
K_{i}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in
C^{k}_{loc}(R^{n}\setminus\{0\})
. It follows then that

R_{i}(z)\equiv R_{K_{i},\mu_{i},\alpha}:=\int_{E_{i}}\dfrac{K_{i}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{i}(w)

converges to

R_{\infty}(z)\equiv R_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty},\alpha}:=\int_{E_{\infty}}\dfrac{K_{\infty}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{\infty}(w)

in
C^{k}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash E_{\infty})
. The same holds true for
D_{i}=R_{i}^{-1/\alpha}
and
D_{\infty}=R_{\infty}^{-1/\alpha}
.

Proof.

We prove only that
R_{i}\rightarrow R_{\infty}
in
C_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, since the argument for
\nabla^{j}R_{i}
and the
D_{i}
is essentially the same. Let
\epsilon>0
, and fix
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E_{\infty}
compact. By uniform Ahlfors regularity, choose
\rho\gg 1
large enough so that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(0,\rho-1)}\dfrac{M}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{i}(w)<\epsilon

holds for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, for
i=\infty
, and for all
z\in A
. The existence of such a
\rho
follows from a standard argument using dyadic shells. Since
K_{i}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, for
i
sufficiently large we have

\left|\int_{B(0,\rho)}\dfrac{K_{i}(z-w)-K_{\infty}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}d\mu_{i}(w)\right|<\epsilon,\qquad\forall z\in A.

It thus suffices to show that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B(0,\rho)}\dfrac{K_{\infty}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;(d\mu_{i}-d\mu_{\infty})(w)\right|\leq\epsilon,\qquad\forall z\in A,

provided that
i
is large enough. To obtain this, choose
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\phi\equiv 1
on
B(0,\rho-1)
,
0\leq\phi\leq 1
, and supp
(\phi)\subset B(0,\rho)
. Then since
\mu_{i}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\infty}
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\rho)}\dfrac{\phi(w)K_{\infty}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{i}(w)\rightarrow\int_{B(0,\rho)}\dfrac{\phi(w)K_{\infty}(z-w)}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{\infty}(w)

as
i\rightarrow\infty
. The last terms that need to be estimated can be bound from above by

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\rho)\setminus B(0,\rho-1)}\dfrac{M}{|z-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;(d\mu_{i}+d\mu_{\infty})(w)<2\epsilon.

This finishes the proof of convergence in
C_{loc}
. ∎

Let us now preface our rectifiability results with some basic blow-up calculations. Assume that
E
is a
d
-Ahlfors regular set equipped with the measure
\mu
. If
Q\in E,r_{i}>0
and
x_{i}\in\Omega
, let
X_{i}=(x_{i}-Q)/r_{i}
. In addition, we consider the rescaled kernels
K_{i}(\cdot)=K(r_{i}\,\cdot)
and the rescaled measures

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(A)

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{\mu(r_{i}A+Q)}{r_{i}^{d}}.

One easily checks that
\mu_{i}
are uniformly
d
-Ahlfors regular (with constants only depending on the constants of
\mu
) with supports
E_{i}\equiv\dfrac{E-Q}{r_{i}}
, and that
K_{i}
are distance-standard kernels with the same constants as
K
. Moreover, a simple change of variables yields

\displaystyle D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(X_{i})^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{i}}\dfrac{K_{i}(X_{i}-w)}{|X_{i}-w|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{i}(w)

\displaystyle=r_{i}^{d+\alpha}\int_{E_{i}}\dfrac{K(x_{i}-(r_{i}w+Q))}{|x_{i}-(r_{i}w+Q)|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mu_{i}(w)=r_{i}^{\alpha}D_{K,\mu}(x_{i})^{-\alpha}.

It follows then that

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\nabla D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(X_{i})|

\displaystyle=|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x_{i})|.

We recall some notation for non-tangential limits from Definition
1.2
.
With this language, we can now characterize rectifiability of
\mu
in terms of non-tangential limits of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu}|
, provided that
K
behaves like a constant near zero.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Let
K
be a distance-standard, radial kernel. Then if
\mu
is any
d
-Ahlfors regular measure with
d
-rectifiable support
E
, we have that the non-tangential limit
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K}(x)|
exists for every
\eta\in(0,1)
at almost every
Q\in E
, if and only if
K(\lambda\,\cdot)\rightarrow c_{\infty}
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
for some constant
c_{\infty}>0
.

Proof.

Let
0<\eta<1
,
r_{i}\downarrow 0
, and
x_{i}\in\Gamma_{1,\eta}(Q)
such that
|x_{i}-Q|\downarrow 0
. Passing to a subsequence (which we relabel for convenience) we may assume that
K(r_{i}\,\cdot)\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in
C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
. Since
E
is rectifiable, at almost every
Q\in E
there is a unique tangent measure, which is flat (this measure may, of course, depend on
Q
, cf.
[
Mat95
, Theorem 16.7]
). As such we may assume that
\mu_{i}\rightharpoonup c\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{V}
(where
\mu_{i}
is as above with respect to
r_{i}\downarrow 0
) and that the plane
V
and constant
c
are independent of the sequence
r_{i}\downarrow 0
.

Let
X_{i}=r_{i}^{-1}(x_{i}-Q)
and since
\text{dist}(x_{i},E)\geq\eta|x_{i}-Q|
, we have that
\text{dist}(X_{i},\frac{E-Q_{i}}{r_{i}})\geq\eta|X_{i}|
. If
|x_{i}-Q|\simeq r_{i}
we have that (perhaps passing to a subsequence)
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash V
.

By Lemma
3.1
, the assumption that
K(r_{i}\,\cdot)\rightarrow K_{\infty}
, and the previous calculations on the blowup of
D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}
, we have that,

(3.2)

\displaystyle|\nabla{D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}}(X_{\infty})|=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|\nabla{D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}}(X_{i})|=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x_{i})|.

That the non-tangential limit of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu}|
exists at
Q
means that the limit in (
3.2
) is independent of
\Gamma_{1,\eta}(Q)\ni x_{i}\rightarrow Q
. Fixing
r_{i}
but adjusting
\eta
and
x_{i}
we can get every point
X_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash V
. So the non-tangential limit exists if and only if
K_{\infty}=\lim_{r_{i}\downarrow 0}K(r_{i}\,\cdot)
is a kernel for which
|\nabla D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}|
is constant outside
V
. By Corollary 3.2 in
[
DEM21
]
, since
D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}\simeq\delta_{V}
, the only such functions are of the form
a\delta_{V}
for some
a>0
. Thus we see that the non-tangential limits exists if and only if
K_{\infty}
is distance-exact, for each
r_{i}\downarrow 0
with a constant of exactness independent of the sequence
r_{i}\downarrow 0
(recall that
\mu_{\infty}
is independent of the sequence
r_{i}\downarrow 0
). By Theorem
2.5
, the only distance-exact radial kernels are constants
c_{\infty}
and the constant of exactness changes with
c_{\infty}
. So we conclude that the non-tangential limit exists if and only if
K(r\,\cdot)\rightarrow c_{\infty}
for
r\downarrow 0
and we are done.
∎

Without the assumption of radial symmetry we can only conclude that
D_{K_{\infty}}
is distance-exact. Moreover, we have examples showing that it is possible to obtain a continuum of different
K_{\infty}
:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Let
K
be a distance-standard kernel. Then if
\mu
is any
d
-Ahlfors regular measure with
d
-rectifiable support
E
, we have that the non-tangential limit
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K}(x)|
exists for every
\eta\in(0,1)
at almost every
Q\in E
, if and only if for every plane
V\in G(n,d)
there exists a
c_{V}
such that
D_{K(\lambda\,\cdot)}\rightarrow c_{V}\delta_{V}
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus V)
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.

Furthermore, we can construct a distance-standard kernel
K
such that non-tangential limits of
|\nabla D_{K}|
exist at almost-every point of any plane
V\in G(n,d)
but such that the limit of
K(r_{i}\,\cdot)
as
r_{i}\downarrow 0
depends on the sequence
r_{i}
.

Proof.

The first part of the Theorem follows by arguing exactly as in Theorem
3.2
. Indeed, in the proof of Theorem
3.2
we only invoke radial symmetry once we have concluded that
|\nabla D_{K_{\infty}}|
must be a constant outside of each plane and that constant must not depend on the sequence
r_{i}\downarrow 0
.

Let
\tilde{K}
be a smooth distance-orthogonal kernel satisfying the usual estimate

\nabla^{m}\tilde{K}(x)|x|^{m}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
which is not identically equal to zero but such that
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow 0}\tilde{K}=0=\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{K}
(guaranteed to exist by Lemma
2.11
). Let
1=a_{0}>a_{1}>a_{2}>\ldots>a_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that
a_{i+1}/a_{i}\downarrow 0
monotonically. Let
\phi_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(a_{i+1}/2,2a_{i})
be such that
0\leq\phi_{i}\leq 1
,
\sum\phi_{i}\equiv 1
on
(0,1)
and so that
\phi_{i}\equiv 1
on
(2a_{i+1},a_{i}/2)
. Despite these constraints, a scaling arguments shows that we can still guarantee that
\||x|^{m}\nabla^{m}\phi_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{m}
for some
C_{m}>0
independent of
i
. Define for
M>\|\tilde{K}\|_{\infty}

K(x):=M+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\phi_{i}(|x|)\tilde{K}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}\right).

First, we note that
K
is distance standard, by the fact that
\tilde{K}
is smooth,
M
is large and the estimates on the derivatives of the
\phi_{i}
and
\tilde{K}
. We want to show that for every
\lambda_{i}\downarrow 0
there exists a
\lambda_{i_{j}}
such that
K(\lambda_{i_{j}}\,\cdot)\rightarrow K_{\infty}
where
K_{\infty}
is distance exact. Note by passing to a subsequence and relabeling, we may assume that
a_{i+1}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq a_{i}
.

We have two cases:

Case 1:
Here we assume

0<\liminf_{i}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}\leq\limsup\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}<\infty.

In this case, passing further to a subsequence we may assume that
\lim_{i}\lambda_{i}/\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}=\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. Fix
\mathcal{K}\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. For all
i
large enough and all
x\in\mathcal{K}
we have that
\lambda_{i}|x|\in(a_{j+1},a_{j})
if any only if
i=j
. On the other hand if
i
is large enough (depending only on
\mathcal{K}
), we have that
\lambda_{i}|x|\in(2a_{i+1},a_{i}/2)
for all
x\in\mathcal{K}
(this is because
a_{i+1}/\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}\rightarrow 0
and
a_{i}/\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}\rightarrow+\infty
). Thus

K(\lambda_{i}\,\cdot)-(M+\phi_{i}(\lambda_{i}x)\tilde{K}(\lambda_{i}x/\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}))\rightarrow 0\mbox{ in $C^{1}(\mathcal{K})$}

and

M+\phi_{i}(\lambda_{i}x)\tilde{K}(\lambda_{i}x/\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}})\rightarrow M+\tilde{K}(\alpha x)\mbox{ in $C^{1}(\mathcal{K})$.}

We know
M+\tilde{K}(\alpha x)
is distance exact so we are done in this case.

Case 2:
We assume that either the
\liminf=0
or
\limsup=+\infty
above. The arguments for the two cases are similar so let us just do the case when
\liminf_{i}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}=0
. Relabeling we may assume that
\lim_{i}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}=0
. However, we still have
\lambda_{i}\in(a_{i+1},a_{i})
. Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
be a compact set and observe for any
x\in\mathcal{K}
we have

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{j}|\tilde{K}\left(\lambda_{i}x/\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}\right)|=0.

Indeed this follows from the fact that
\tilde{K}
goes to zero at zero and infinity, that
\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}}\rightarrow 0
and that if
i\neq j
but
i
is large enough we have
\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}}
either blows up or goes to zero.

As such
K(\lambda_{i}\,\cdot)\rightarrow M
in
C^{1}(\mathcal{K})
in this case. Of course, constant kernels are distance exact.

Finally, we see that by letting
\lambda_{i}=\sqrt{a_{i}a_{i+1}}
or
\lambda_{i}=a_{i}
we get that
K(\lambda_{i}\,\cdot)
converges in
C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
to
M+\tilde{K}
or
M
respectively. Since these two kernels are different we are done.
∎

We end by observing that even without any symmetry assumptions on
K
the existence of non-tangential limits of
|\nabla D_{K,\mu}|
implies the rectifiability of
\mu
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
0<d<n
not necessarily an integer and let
\alpha>0
. Let
K
be a distance-standard kernel. Suppose that
\mu
is a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure with support
E
such that
\mu
-almost everywhere,
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K}(x)|
exists each
\eta\in(0,1)
. Then
d
is an integer and
\mu
is
d
-rectifiable.

Proof.

We show that
\mu
is rectifiable by showing that almost everywhere in its support, all of its tangent measures are flat. Since
\mu
has positive lower density and finite upper density (by Ahlfors regularity), Theorem 16.5 in
[
Mat95
]
shows that these are equivalent conditions.

Let
Q\in E
be a point of
E
so that
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x)|
exists for each
\eta\in(0,1)
. Let
r_{i}\downarrow 0
, and define
K_{i},\mu_{i},E_{i}
as above. Up to a subsequence, we may as well assume the convergence of
\mu_{i}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\infty}
and
E_{i}\rightarrow E_{\infty}
. Moreover, since
K
is distance-standard, we may also assume that up to a subsequence,
K_{i}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for some kernel
K_{\infty}
that is strictly positive and satisfies
\nabla K_{\infty}(x)|x|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By Lemma
3.1
, we may assume up to a subsequence that
D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}
converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E_{\infty}
to
D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}
.

Let
Z\in E_{\infty}
and observe that if
\eta_{Z}:=\text{dist}(Z,E_{\infty})/(2|Z|)\in(0,1)
, then the points
x_{i}=Q+r_{i}Z
satisfy
x_{i}\in\Omega
for all
i
sufficiently large with

\displaystyle\text{dist}(x_{i},E)

\displaystyle=r_{i}\text{dist}(Z,E_{i})\geq(r_{i}/2)\text{dist}(Z,E_{\infty})=\eta_{Z}|Z|r_{i}=\eta_{Z}|x_{i}-Q|.

In particular then, for all
i
sufficiently large we have
x_{i}\in\Gamma_{|Z|r_{i},\eta_{Z}}(Q)
, and thus we have by assumption that

\displaystyle\text{n.t.}\lim\nolimits_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta_{Z}}|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x_{i})|

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|\nabla D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(Z)|=|\nabla D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}(Z)|.

Since
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta_{Z}}|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x_{i})|
is independent of
Z
, we have that
|\nabla D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}|
is constant on
\Omega_{\infty}
. This constant cannot be zero, since
D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}
is comparable to
\delta_{E_{i}}
(with constants independent of
i
), and thus
D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}
is comparable to
\delta_{E_{\infty}}
. By
[
DEM21
, Corollary 3.2]
, we have that
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
E_{\infty}
is a
d
-plane, and
\mu_{\infty}
is a constant multiple of
\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E_{\infty}}
. This shows that at this point
Q
, all tangent measures are flat, and thus the claim is proved.
∎

A curious corollary of the above results for any
d
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
, is that the existence of non-tangential limits
|\nabla D_{K}|
for
\mu
-almost every
Q
implies structure on
K
:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
0<d<n
not necessarily an integer and let
\alpha>0
. Let
K
be a distance-standard kernel. Suppose that
\mu
is a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure with support
E
such that
\mu
-almost everywhere,
\text{n.t.}\lim_{x\rightarrow Q}^{\eta}|\nabla D_{K}(x)|
exists each
\eta\in(0,1)
. Then for every plane
V\in G(d,n)
there exists a
c_{V}
such that
D_{K(\lambda\,\cdot)}\rightarrow c_{V}\delta_{V}
in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus V)
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.

3.2.
The USFE for non-exact kernels

Our aim in this section is to develop necessary and sufficient conditions on a distance-standard kernel
K
so that
D_{K}
satisfies the USFE outside of all
d
-uniformly rectifiable measures. The key idea is to measure how close
D_{K}
is to behaving like the Euclidean distance outside of affine spaces, in a way which is uniform over scales and affine spaces. This is the purpose of the
\gamma
function defined below in Definition
3.6
.

Before we define the
\gamma
function, let us remember that for any distance-standard kernel
K
, outside of any affine space
E\in G(n,d)
the functions
R_{K}
(and thus
D_{K},F_{K}
) are invariant in directions parallel to
E
. In particular, assume that
0\in V\in G(n,d)
and that
Q(0,r)
is a cube of side length
r
centered at
0
. Then

\int_{Q(0,r)}F^{2}_{K,E}(x)\,\delta_{E}(x)^{-n+d}\,dx=r^{d}\int_{Q(0,r)\cap V^{\perp}}F^{2}_{K,E}(y)\,\delta_{E}(y)^{-n+d}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(y),

where we abused notation and identified a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with its restriction
f:\mathbb{R}^{n-d}\cong V^{\perp}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
f(y):=f(0+y)
. We will continue this abuse of notation throughout the section, hoping that it does not cause too much confusion.

Definition 3.6
.

Whenever
K
is distance-standard,
\lambda>1
and
\alpha>0
, we write for
r>0
,
W_{\lambda}(r)=B(0,r)\setminus B(0,r/\lambda)
and

\displaystyle\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}(r)^{2}\equiv\sup_{V\in G(d,n)}\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\,\int\limits_{V^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(r)}|\delta_{V}(z)^{\alpha+m}\nabla^{m}(R_{{K},V,\alpha}(z)-

\displaystyle c\delta_{V}(z)^{-\alpha})|^{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\times\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z).

We say that
K
is uniformly good for distances (with exponent
\alpha>0)
if

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}<\infty

for some
\lambda>1
.

Remark 3.7
.

It is straight-forward to verify that if
1<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
, then the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,\lambda_{1},\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,\lambda_{2},\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}\leq C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,\lambda_{1},\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}

holds. Hence
K
is uniformly good for distances if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,2,\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}<\infty.

The key estimate is to bound the integral of
F
on
W_{\lambda}(r)
by the
\gamma
functional.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that
K
is a distance-standard kernel. There is a constant
C
depending only on
n,d,\alpha
and the distance-standard constant for
K
so that the following estimate holds for any
\lambda>1
and any
V\in G(n,d)
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{V^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(r)}F_{K,V,\alpha}(z)^{2}\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)\leq C\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}(r)^{2}.

Proof.

Since (
3.3
) is a supremum over the Grassmanian and since the rotation of a distance standard kernel is distance standard (with the same constants), (
3.5
) is rotation invariant. So we can assume that
V=E\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Denote by
R_{c}(x),D_{c}(x),F_{c}(x)
the corresponding functions with constant kernels that give
R_{c}(x)=c\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha}
where
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall that since
E
is a
d
-plane, we have that
F_{c}(x)\equiv 0
. We first calculate for
j>d
,

\displaystyle\partial_{j}D_{K}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{-1}{\alpha}\right)R_{K}(x)^{-1/\alpha-1}\partial_{j}R_{K}(x),

\displaystyle\left|\nabla D_{K}(x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\right)R_{K}(x)^{-2/\alpha-2}\left(\sum_{j>d}|\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)|^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\left|\nabla D_{K}(x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\right)(-2/\alpha-2)R_{K}(x)^{-2/\alpha-3}\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)\left(\sum_{i>d}|\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{2}{\alpha^{2}}R_{K}(x)^{-2/\alpha-2}\left(\sum_{i>d}\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)\partial_{j}\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)\right).

Recall that
K
is distance standard so for
m=0,1,2
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|\|_{\infty}<\infty

and straightforward estimates give

(3.7)

\displaystyle\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha}\lesssim

\displaystyle\,R_{K}(x)\lesssim\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\,\delta_{E}(x)^{-1-\alpha},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{j}\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\,\delta_{E}(x)^{-2-\alpha}.

Putting (
3.7
) together with the computation of
|\partial_{j}|\nabla D_{K}|^{2}|
we get

(3.10)

\displaystyle\delta_{E}(x)^{2+3\alpha}B_{K}^{j}(x)\lesssim\left|\partial_{j}\left|\nabla D_{K}(x)\right|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta_{E}(x)^{2+3\alpha}B_{K}^{j}(x)

where

\displaystyle B_{K}^{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|(-1/\alpha-1)\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)\left(\sum_{i>d}|\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)|^{2}\right)+R_{K}(x)\left(\sum_{i>d}\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)\partial_{j}\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)\right)\right|

Remark that
B_{c}^{j}(x)\equiv 0
necessarily, since otherwise
F_{c}(x)\neq 0
. We are now in the position to establish (
3.5
).

We choose a constant
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{2}\int_{E^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(r)}|\delta_{E}(z)^{\alpha+m}\nabla^{m}(R_{K}(z)-c_{0}\delta_{E}(z)^{-\alpha})|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{K,\lambda}(r)^{2}.

Recalling that
B^{j}_{c_{0}}\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle|B_{K}^{j}(x)|

\displaystyle=|B_{K}^{j}(x)-B_{c_{0}}^{j}(x)|,

and each of the terms in the latter absolute value can bounded from above by

\displaystyle C\sum_{m=0}^{2}|\nabla^{m}(R_{K}(x)-c_{0}\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha})|\delta_{E}(x)^{-2\alpha-3+m},

where
C
depends on
n,d,\alpha
and the distance-standard constant of
K
. Let us show part of this computation, as each term can be handled essentially the same way. To estimate the first terms appearing in
B_{K}^{j}-B_{c_{0}}^{j}
, we use (
3.8
) to obtain

|\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)^{2}-\partial_{j}R_{c_{0}}(x)\partial_{i}R_{c_{0}}(x)^{2}|\leq|\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)|^{2}|\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{j}R_{c_{0}}(x)|\\
+|\partial_{j}R_{c_{0}}(x)\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)||\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{i}R_{c_{0}}(x)|+|\partial_{j}R_{c_{0}}(x)^{2}||\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{i}R_{c_{0}}(x)|\\
\lesssim\delta_{E}(x)^{-2-2\alpha}(|\partial_{j}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{j}R_{c_{0}}(x)|+|\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{i}R_{c_{0}}(x)|+|\partial_{i}R_{K}(x)-\partial_{i}R_{c_{0}}(x)|)\\
\lesssim|\nabla(R_{K}(x)-c_{0}\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha})|\delta_{E}(x)^{-2\alpha-2}.

Combining this with (
3.10
) we obtain the pointwise estimate

\displaystyle F_{K}(x)\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{2}|\nabla^{m}(R_{K}(x)-c_{0}\delta_{E}(x)^{-\alpha})|\delta_{E}(x)^{\alpha+m}.

From here we readily see that

\displaystyle\int_{E^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(r)}F_{K}(z)^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{2}\int_{E^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(r)}|\delta_{E}(z)^{\alpha+m}\nabla^{m}(R_{K}(z)-c_{0}\delta_{E}(z)^{-\alpha})|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}(r)^{2},

by the choice of
c_{0}
, completing the proof of (
3.5
).
∎

One can pass from estimates out of flat sets to estimates outside of uniformly rectifiable sets following
[
DEM21
, Theorem 2.1]
. However, there will be extra, complicating terms in the analysis, caused by the fact that
F_{K}
may not be identically equal to zero outside of flat sets. We estimate those extra terms using the
\gamma
functional and Lemma
3.8
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d<n
, and let
\beta>0
. Suppose that
K
is a distance-standard kernel that is uniformly good for distances, i.e., (
3.4
) holds for some
\lambda>0
with exponent
\beta
. If in addition

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then
D_{K,\mu,\beta}
satisfies the USFE for each
d
-uniformly rectifiable measure
\mu
.

Proof.

Fix
\beta>0
(which we shall omit in notation of
F_{K}
and
D_{K}
), and fix
\mu
some
d
-uniformly-rectifiable measure with support
E
. Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{d}
be the set of flat measures, and let
\mathcal{D}_{x,r}(\mu,\nu)
denote the Wasserstein distance

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{x,r}(\mu,\nu)=r^{-d-1}\sup_{f\in\Lambda(x,r)}\left|\int_{B(x,r)}f(d\mu-d\nu)\right|

where
\Lambda(x,r)
is the set of functions
f
that are
1
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and vanish on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(x,r)
. With this distance, assign the definition

\displaystyle\alpha(x,r)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{D}_{x,r}(\mu,\nu)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
r>0
is such that
B(x,r)\cap E\neq\emptyset
. These so called “
\alpha
-numbers” are useful in the context of quantitative rectifiability, since any uniformly rectifiable set
E
satisfies Carleson measure estimates on
\alpha(x,r)^{2}
. In particular, since
\mu
is
d
-uniformly rectifiable we have that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{B(Q,R)\cap E}\int_{0}^{R}\alpha(y,r)^{2}\dfrac{d\mu(y)\;dr}{r}\leq CR^{d},

as in Lemma 5.9 in
[
DFM19
]
(see also
[
Tol09
]
where the
\alpha
numbers are introduced).

The key estimate we wish to show is the following: for
x\in\Omega=E\setminus\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
r_{0}=\delta_{E}(x)
, and
k\geq 0
, we let
r_{k}=2^{k}r_{0}
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, we show that

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)-\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\right|+\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\right|

(3.13)

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{E}(x)^{-1}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-(\beta+1)l}\alpha(y,2^{8}r_{0})+\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\right|

for
y\in E\cap B(x,16\delta_{E}(x))
, and where
\nu=\nu(x)
is a well-chosen flat measure. Assuming that (
3.13
) holds, let us prove the result.

Let
a(y,r)
denote the function defining the sum on the right-hand side of (
3.13
). That is,
a(y,r)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}2^{-(\beta+1)\ell}\alpha(y,2^{l}r)
. From (
3.13
), we have that for
Q\in E
and
R>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{B(Q,R)}F_{K}^{2}(X)\delta_{E}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B(Q,R)}\fint_{B(X,16\delta_{E}(X))\cap E}a(y,2^{8}\delta_{E}(X))^{2}\;d\mu(y)\delta_{E}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{B(Q,R)}\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu(X)}(X)\right|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}\delta_{E}(X)^{-n+d+2}\;dX.

(3.14)

\displaystyle\equiv I+II.

We first bound
I
from above by
CR^{d}
. To do this, decompose
B(Q,R)
into a disjoint union of Whitney cubes. Switching the order of integration, and summing over the cubes yields

\int_{B(Q,R)}\fint_{B(X,16\delta_{E}(X))\cap E}a(y,2^{8}\delta_{E}(X))^{2}\;d\mu(y)\delta_{E}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX\\
\leq C\int_{B(Q,R)\cap E}\int_{0}^{R}a(y,2^{8}r)^{2}\;\dfrac{d\mu(y)dr}{r}.

One argues as in Lemma 5.89 in
[
DFM19
]
to then show that

\displaystyle\int_{B(Q,R)\cap E}\int_{0}^{R}a(y,2^{8}r)^{2}\;\dfrac{d\mu(y)dr}{r}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B(Q,R)\cap E}\int_{0}^{R}\alpha(y,2^{8}r)^{2}\;\dfrac{d\mu(y)dr}{r}

and so the Carleson measure estimate (
3.12
) implies
I\leq CR^{d}
.

As for the term
II
in (
3.14
), we require more precise control on
\nu(x)
; we will choose them so that
\nu(x)
are constant on certain Whitney regions outside

as follows. Let

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}

\displaystyle=\{x\in B(Q,R)\;:\;2^{-j-1}R<\delta_{E}(x)\leq 2^{-j}R\}

for
j\in\mathbb{Z},j\geq 0
. Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be sufficiently small and fixed (to be determined below), and suppose that

are a countable collection of balls covering
\Omega_{j}
with bounded overlap. That is, we have that
\sum_{i}\chi_{B_{i}^{j}}\leq M
on
\Omega_{j}
. Such a cover exists by the Besicovitch Covering Theorem and, if we take
\eta
sufficiently small we may assume that
B_{i}^{j}\subset\Omega_{j-1}\cup\Omega_{j}\cup\Omega_{j+1}
for each

. We now need to pick the
\nu(x)
more carefully.

Claim:
We claim, and will prove below, that in each
B_{i}^{j}
we can choose a flat measure
\nu_{i}^{j}
, supported on
V_{i}^{j}
, such that (
3.13
) holds for all

with the measure
\nu_{i}^{j}
. Further assume that
\nu_{i}^{j}(y)=a_{i}^{j}\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{V_{i}^{j}}(y)
with
C^{-1}\leq a_{i}^{j}\leq C
, and such that
V_{i}^{j}\in A(n,d)
with
\delta_{V_{i}^{j}}(x_{i}^{j})\simeq\delta_{E}(x_{i}^{j})
for each
i
and
j
. Of course if
\gamma
is chosen sufficiently small, then this implies also that
\delta_{E}\simeq\delta_{V_{i}^{j}}
on
B_{i}^{j}
.

The
d
-Ahlfors regularity assumption on

implies that
|\Omega_{j}|\leq|\{x\in B(Q,R)\;:\;\delta_{E}(x)\leq 2^{j}R\}|\leq CR^{d}(2^{-j}R)^{n-d}
. Thus, there is a constant
C>0
(independent of

\#\{B_{i}^{j}\}\leq C2^{jd}
. This allows us to estimate
B
brutally by

\displaystyle\int_{B(Q,R)}

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{m}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu(X)}(X)\right|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}\delta_{E}(X)^{-n+d+2}\;dX

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{B_{i}^{j}}F_{K,\nu_{i,j}}^{2}\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j\geq 0}2^{jd}\sup_{i}\int_{B_{i}^{j}}F_{K,\nu_{i,j}}^{2}\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX.

We recall that
F_{K,\nu_{i}^{j}}
and
\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}
are invariant in directions parallel to
V^{i}_{j}
, so, arguing as above, there is a Whitney-type cube
\tilde{B}_{i}^{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-d}\cong(V^{i}_{j})^{\perp}
(which contains the projection of
B_{i}^{j}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
) such that

\int_{B_{i}^{j}}F_{K,\nu_{i,j}}^{2}(x)\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}(X)^{-n+d}\;dX\leq C_{\eta}(2^{-j}R)^{d}\int_{\tilde{B}_{i}^{j}}F_{K,\nu_{i,j}}^{2}(y)\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}(y)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(y).

Letting
\lambda>1
be large enough (depending only on
n,d,\eta
not on

) we can assume that
\tilde{B}_{i}^{j}\subset W_{\lambda}(2^{-j}R)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
.

Putting everything together (and overestimating the integral on
\tilde{B}_{i}^{j}
by the integral on
W_{\lambda}(2^{-j}R)
) we get that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{B(Q,R)}\left|\partial_{m}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu(X)}\right|^{2}\right)\right|^{2}\delta_{E}^{-n+d+2}\;dX

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{d}\sum_{j}\int_{(V_{i}^{j})^{\perp}\cap W_{\lambda}(2^{-j}R)}F_{K,\nu_{i,j}}^{2}\delta_{V^{i}_{j}}^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}

\displaystyle\leq CR^{d}\sum_{j}\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}^{2}(2^{-j}R)\leq CR^{d},

where the penultimate inequality follows from Lemma
3.8
and the final inequality follows from bounding the dyadic sum by the scale invariant integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,\lambda,\alpha}^{2}(r)\,dr/r
. Summing over
1\leq m\leq n
, we get
II\leq CR^{d}
. In summary, we have bound both terms,
I,II
in (
3.14
), and thus established the USFE, provided that
\nu_{i}^{j}
can be chosen as in the claim above.

To prove the claim, we can compute explicitly
\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)-\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(x)\right|^{2}\right)\right|
as a sum, apply the triangle inequality, and simply estimate each term of the form
|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\mu}(x)-\nabla^{j}R_{K,\nu}(x)|
where
\nabla^{j}
is an iterated derivative. Let us consider the simple case, which is when
j=0
.

Let
x\in\Omega
,
r_{0}=r_{0}(x)=\delta_{E}(x)
, and
r_{k}=r_{k}(x)=2^{k}r_{0}
for
k\geq 0
. Let
\phi
be a fixed smooth bump function so that
0\leq\phi\leq 1
,
\phi
is radial, and
\phi\equiv 1
on
B(0,8r_{0})
, and
\phi\equiv 0
outside
B(0,16r_{0})
. Define
\phi_{0}=\phi
, and
\phi_{k}(x)=\phi(2^{-k}x)-\phi(2^{-k+1}x)
for
k\geq 1
. Note that
\phi_{k}
is supported in
A_{k}\equiv\overline{B}(0,2^{k+4}r_{0})\setminus B(0,2^{k+2}r_{0})
, and
\sum_{k\geq 0}\phi_{k}=1
.

Arguing as in
[
DEM21
]
we may choose flat measures
\nu_{k}=\lambda_{k}\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{P_{k}}
where
\lambda_{k}>0
and
P_{k}\in A(n,d)
so that the following hold:

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{x,64r_{k}}(\mu,\nu_{k})\leq C\alpha(x,64r_{k})

with constant depending only on
n,d
and the Ahlfors regularity constant for
\mu
. Moreover, we may choose such measures so that
C^{-1}\leq\lambda_{k}\leq C
and so that
P_{k}\cap B(x,(3/2)r_{k})\neq\emptyset
. Remark also, that if
|x-z|\leq\gamma\delta_{E}(x)
for
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
sufficiently small and fixed, then we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{5+k}\delta_{E}(z)}(\mu,\nu_{k})\leq C\mathcal{D}_{x,2^{6+k}\delta_{E}(x)}(\mu,\nu_{k})\leq C\alpha(x,2^{k+6}\delta_{E}(x))\leq C\alpha(z,2^{k+7}\delta_{E}(z)).

If
\gamma
is small enough, we can also guarantee that
P_{k}\cap B(z,2\delta_{E}(z))\neq\emptyset
for such
z
. In particular, we can choose the flat measures
\nu_{k}
to be constant on
B(x,\gamma\delta_{E}(x))
and maintain

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z,32r_{k}}(\mu,\nu_{k})\leq C\alpha(z,128r_{k}),\;\;P_{k}\cap B(z,2\delta_{E}(z))\neq\emptyset

for
z\in B(x,\gamma\delta_{E}(x))
.

Set
\nu=\nu(x)=\nu_{0}
,
P=P(x)=P_{0}
, and let
z\in B(x,\gamma\delta_{E}(x))
. We use the
\nu_{k}
to estimate
|R_{K,\mu}(z)-R_{K,\nu}(z)|
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
0\in P\cap B(x,2r_{0})
. A direct computation yields

\displaystyle\left|R_{K,\mu}(z)-R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k\geq 0}\int_{A_{k}}\phi_{k}(y)\dfrac{K(z-y)}{|z-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;(d\mu-d\nu)(y)\right|.

Since
K
is distance-standard, we have that
\phi_{k}(y)K(z-y)|z-y|^{d+\alpha}
is Lipschitz in
y
with constant at most
Cr_{k}^{-d-\beta-1}
(here, we are using that
\nabla K(w)|w|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
). Moreover, this function vanishes outside of
B(0,2^{k+4}r_{0})\subset B(0,2^{k+5}r_{0})
. The definition of
\mathcal{D}
thus gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{A_{k}}\phi_{k}(y)\dfrac{K(z-y)}{|z-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;(d\mu-d\nu)(y)\right|\leq Cr_{k}^{-\beta}\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{k+5}r_{0}}(\mu,\nu).

By the triangle inequality for
\mathcal{D}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{k+5}r_{0}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{k+5}r_{0}}(\mu,\nu_{k})+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{k+5}r_{0}}(\nu_{l},\nu_{l-1}).

One argues as in the proof of the equation (5.83) in
[
DFM19
]
to obtain
\mathcal{D}_{z,2^{k+5}r_{0}}(\nu_{l},\nu_{l-1})\leq C\alpha(z,2^{l+7}r_{0})
since the measures
\nu_{i}
are flat, pass near
z
, and approximate
\mu
well in
B(z,2^{l+7}r_{0})
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left|R_{K,\mu}(z)-R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\geq 0}r_{k}^{-\beta}\sum_{0\leq l\leq k}\alpha(z,2^{l+7}r_{0})\leq C\sum_{l\geq 0}r_{l}^{-\beta}\alpha(z,2^{l+7}r_{0}).

If
y\in B(z,16\delta_{E}(z))
, then since
\alpha(z,2^{l+7}r_{0})\leq C\alpha(y,2^{l+8}\delta_{E}(z))
, we have that for all such
y
,

\displaystyle\left|R_{K,\mu}(z)-R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{l\geq 0}r_{l}^{-\beta}\alpha(z,2^{l+7}r_{0})

\displaystyle=Cr_{0}^{-\beta}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-\beta l}\alpha(y,2^{l+8}r_{0})=C\delta_{E}(x)^{-\beta}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-\beta l}\alpha(y,2^{l+8}r_{0}).

This is the desired estimate for
R_{K,\mu}
, but using the same methods as above, we can show that

\displaystyle\left|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\mu}(z)-\nabla^{j}R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-(\beta+j)l}\alpha(y,2^{l+8}r_{0})

for each iterated integral with
j=1,2
, and for
y\in B(z,16\delta_{E}(z))
. Remark as well that since
K
is distance-standard, we know that
\left|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\mu}(z)\right|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}
for
j=0,1,2
, just as in the proof of Lemma
3.8
. We can argue as in
[
DEM21
]
to show that
\left|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}
as well, due to the fact that
|y-z|\geq r_{0}/2
for
y\in P_{0}
.

From here, our estimate follows from the usual process of estimating the terms in

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(z)\right|^{2}\right)-\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(z)\right|^{2}\right)\right|

by brute force i.e. using the bounds
|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\mu}(z)|\lesssim\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}
and
|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\nu}(z)|\lesssim\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}
, along with the estimates
\left|\nabla^{j}R_{K,\mu}(z)-\nabla^{j}R_{K,\nu}(z)\right|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)^{-\beta-j}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-(\beta+j)l}\alpha(y,2^{l+8}r_{0})

to show

\left|\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\mu}(z)\right|^{2}\right)-\partial_{i}\left(\left|\nabla D_{K,\nu}(z)\right|^{2}\right)\right|\leq C\delta_{E}(x)^{-1}\sum_{l\geq 0}2^{-(\beta+1)l}\alpha(y,2^{8}r_{0}),

with the
\nu
chosen as in the claim above (this argument works exactly as in the proof of Lemma
3.8
). That concludes our proof of the claim, i.e. that (
3.13
) holds with the special choices of
\nu
described above. The theorem follows.
∎

Given Theorem
3.9
, it is natural to search for a sufficient condition on
K
which implies that
D_{K}
satisfies (
3.4
) and is easier to verify in practice. In the radial setting this condition is captured by a Dini-type closeness. Recall that this is half of Theorem
1.7
which we restate here for convenience:

Theorem 3.10
.

Suppose that
K\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
is radial, distance-standard, and
\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Further assume that

(3.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{0}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{\infty}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}<\infty

for some constants
K_{0},K_{\infty}>0
and for
m=0,1,2
. Then
D_{K}
satisfies the USFE in
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\text{spt }\mu
for any
d
-uniformly rectifiable measure
\mu
.

Theorem
3.10
follows from the subsequent lemma and Theorem
3.9
:

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose that
K
is a distance-standard radial kernel, and
K_{0},K_{\infty}
are positive constants so that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{0}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(t^{m}\dfrac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\left(K(t)-K_{\infty}\right)\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}\equiv M_{m}<\infty

for
m=0,1,2
. Then there is a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
n,d,\alpha>0
and the distance-standard constants of
K
so that for any
\alpha>0
,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,2,\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}\leq C_{\alpha}(1+M_{0}+M_{1}+M_{2}).

In particular,
K
is uniformly good for distances for any exponent.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
. As is often the case we omit in the notation of
R_{K,V,\alpha}
the dependence on
V
and
\alpha
because they are fixed. Furthermore, since
K
is radial we know that
R_{K,V,\alpha}(x)
depends only on the
\delta_{V}(x)
and, in particular, not on the plane
V\in G(n,d)
. Putting all this together it suffices to estimate:

(3.21)

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\int_{V^{\perp}\cap W_{4}(2^{j})}\left|\delta_{V}(x)^{\alpha+m}\nabla^{m}\left(R_{K}(x)-c\delta_{V}(x)^{-\alpha}\right)\right|^{2}\,\delta_{V}(x)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z).

We will do the
m=0
case, since the other cases follow in the same way. Since
K_{0},K_{\infty}
are constants, we know that there are constants
c_{0},c_{\infty}>0
so that
R_{K_{0}}\equiv c_{0}\delta_{V}^{-\alpha}
and
R_{K_{\infty}}=c_{\infty}\delta_{V}^{-\alpha}
. Applying Jensen’s inequality and rotational invariance,

\displaystyle\int_{V^{\perp}\cap B(0,1)}\left|R_{K}(z)-c_{0}\delta_{V}(z)^{-\alpha}\right|^{2}\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d+2\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{V^{\perp}\cap B(0,1)}\left|R_{K}(z)-R_{K_{0}}(z)\right|^{2}\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d+2\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{V^{\perp}\cap B(0,1)}\left|\int_{V}\dfrac{K(z-y)-K_{0}}{|z-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)\right|^{2}\;\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d+2\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{V^{\perp}\cap B(0,1)}\left(\int_{V}\dfrac{|K(z-y)-K_{0}|}{|z-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)\right)^{2}\;\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d+2\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{1}\int_{V}\dfrac{(K(z-y)-K_{0})^{2}}{|z-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)\delta_{V}(z)^{\alpha-1}\;d\delta_{V}(z)

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\rho}^{\infty}(K(t)-K_{0})^{2}t^{-d-\alpha+1}\bigl(t^{2}-\rho^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\,dt\rho^{\alpha-1}\,d\rho

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\rho}^{1}(K(t)-K_{0})^{2}t^{-d-\alpha+1}\bigl(t^{2}-\rho^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\,dt\rho^{\alpha-1}\,d\rho+C\|K-K_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}.

Apply Fubini and a change of variables to get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\rho}^{1}(K(t)-K_{0})^{2}t^{-d-\alpha+1}\bigl(t^{2}-\rho^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\,dt\rho^{\alpha-1}\,d\rho

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{1}(K(t)-K_{0})^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(1-\left(\frac{\rho}{t}\right)^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\left(\frac{\rho}{t}\right)^{\alpha}\,\frac{d\rho}{\rho}\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{1}(K(t)-K_{0})^{2}\,\frac{dt}{t},

since the interior integral in the second line converges for all
d,\alpha>0
.

We can estimate

\int_{V^{\perp}\cap B(0,1)^{c}}\left|R_{K}(z)-c_{\infty}\delta_{V}(z)^{-\alpha}\right|^{2}\delta_{V}(z)^{-n+d+2\alpha}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

the same way, and putting all these estimates together we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{V^{\perp}\cap W_{4}(2^{j})}\left|\delta_{V}(x)^{\alpha}\left(R_{K}(x)-c\delta_{V}(x)^{-\alpha}\right)\right|^{2}\,\delta_{V}(x)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(K(t)-K_{0}\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(K(t)-K_{\infty}\right)^{2}\;\dfrac{dt}{t}+\|K-K_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|K-K_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\right),

for any choice of
K_{0},K_{\infty}
. Notice that each of the integrals above converge by assumption. Moreover, for the integrals to converge it must be the case that
K_{0},K_{\infty}\leq 4\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}
and so we can bound
\|K-K_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}},\|K-K_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}}
by the distance-standard constants of
K
. Thus we have completed our proof when
m=0
and, as mentioned above, the rest of the argument follows similarly.
∎

As was the case for non-tangential limits, our condition for general kernels

is less clean, due to the richness of the family of distance-exact kernels. The following Lemma is proven is much the same way as above, so we omit the argument.

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose that
K
is distance-standard, and
K_{0},K_{\infty}
are
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact kernels. Then there is a constant
C>0
depending only on

and the distance-standard constants for

and

so that

(3.22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{K,2,\alpha}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}\leq C\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}\theta_{K,K_{0}}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\theta_{K,K_{\infty}}(r)^{2}\;\dfrac{dr}{r})\right)

where for
r\in(0,1)
, we define

\displaystyle\theta_{K,K_{0}}(r)^{2}\equiv\sup_{V\in G(n,d)}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\int_{(V^{\perp}\cap W_{2}(r))\times(B(0,1)\cap V)}\left||x|^{m}\nabla^{m}(K(x)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-(K_{0})(x))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times|x|^{-d-\alpha}\delta_{V}(x)^{-n+d+\alpha}\;dx

and for
r>1
we define

\displaystyle\theta_{K,K_{\infty}}(r)^{2}\equiv\sup_{V\in G(n,d)}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\int_{(V^{\perp}\cap W_{2}(r))\times V}\left||x|^{m}\nabla^{m}(K(x)\right.

\displaystyle-\left.(K_{\infty})(x))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times|x|^{-d-\alpha}\delta_{V}(x)^{-n+d+\alpha}\;dx.

In particular, if
\int_{0}^{1}\theta_{K,K_{0}}(r)^{2}dr/r+\int_{1}^{\infty}\theta_{K,K_{\infty}}(r)^{2}dr/r<\infty
, then

is uniformly good for distances for any exponent
\alpha>0
.

As a final goal of this subsection, we now investigate to what extent the sufficient conditions of Theorems
3.9
,
3.10
and Lemma
3.12
are sharp. Due to our inability to make the results of Theorem
2.5
quantitative; that is to say, our lack of a theorem which says “If
D_{K}
is quantitatively close to being the distance than
K
is quantitatively close to being a distance standard kernel”, there is little hope for us to find necessary quantitative conditions on the kernels
K
, even in the radial setting. However, we are able to find some necessary conditions on
D_{K}
for the USFE to hold outside of planes (and thus uniformly rectifiable sets).

We begin with a simple computation which shows that a condition not so far from being uniformly good for distances (e.g. Definition
3.6
) is necessary for satisfying the USFE outside of all planes:

Lemma 3.13
.

If
K
is a distance standard kernel such that
F_{K}
satisfies the USFE outside of all
d
-planes then for each
E\in G(n,d)
there are constants
c_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}|(z)-c_{k}|\nabla\delta_{E}(z)||^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty,

where
A_{k}=E^{\perp}\cap\left(B(0,2^{k})\setminus B(0,2^{k-1})\right)
.

Above we have suggestively written
|\nabla\delta_{E}(z)|
instead of 1 with an eye towards future applications in the radial setting. Before we prove the lemma let’s make some remarks:

Remark 3.14
.

The condition in Lemma
3.13
differs from Definition
3.6
in two main ways. First, it gives no control on the second or 0th order derivatives of
D_{K}
. While the USFE itself is control on
\nabla|\nabla D_{K}|
(i.e. part of the second derivative) and similar arguments to the ones below give a notion of control on the 0th derivative, we were not able to show that control on all the second derivatives of
D_{K}
is necessary for the USFE outside of planes.

Secondly, Definition
3.6
requires control which is uniform over planes whereas Lemma
3.13
shows only non-uniform control is necessary. To get uniform control for each kernel
K
one would need to build a set
E
which is flat in the “worst possible” direction for a Carleson prevalent set of Whitney-type regions.
Whether such a set exists for every kernel is an interesting question, but outside the scope of this article.

Proof of Lemma
3.13
.

Let
E\in G(n,d)
. Since
F_{K}
satisfies the USFE on
\Omega
, and since
F_{K}
and
\delta_{E}
are translation invariant with respect to
E
, then necessarily

\displaystyle\int_{E^{\perp}}F_{K}(z)^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty.

With
A_{k}=B(0,2^{k+1})\setminus B(0,2^{k})\cap E^{\perp}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}F_{K}(z)^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}|\nabla|\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d+2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty.

With
r_{k}=2^{-k}
and
c_{k}=\int_{A_{k}}|\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)/|A_{k}|
,

\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)=\int_{A_{1}}||\nabla D_{K}(r_{k}z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)\\
\leq C(n,d)\int_{A_{1}}||\nabla D_{K}(r_{k}z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)\leq C(n,d)\int_{A_{1}}|\nabla_{z}|\nabla D_{K}(r_{k}z)|^{2}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)\\
=C(n,d)r_{k}^{2}\int_{A_{1}}|\nabla|\nabla D_{K}(r_{k}z)|^{2}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)\\
\leq C(n,d)\int_{A_{k}}|\nabla|\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d+2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z),

where we have employed the Poincaré inequality in the second line. This shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty,

as desired.
∎

Since radial kernels do not depend on the direction (nor the plane itself), we can strengthen Lemma
3.13
in the radial setting:

Corollary 3.15
.

If
K
is a radial, distance-standard kernel so that
D_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E}}
satisfies the USFE for each
E\in G(n,d)
, then necessarily we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}|\nabla D_{K}(z)-\nabla c_{k}\delta_{E}(z)|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty,

where
A_{k}=E^{\perp}\cap\left(B(0,2^{k})\setminus B(0,2^{k-1})\right)
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.13
shows that for each
E
if
F_{K}
satisfies the USFE outside of
E
we have constants
c_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty

Since
D_{K}
is radial, we know that
\nabla D_{K}(x)
points the same direction as
\nabla\delta_{E}(x)
, and that the
c_{k}
do not depend on
E
, giving the desired result.
∎

Theorem 3.16

(A partial converse)
.

Suppose
E\in G(n,d)
and
K
is a distance-standard kernel such that
F_{K,\mathcal{H}^{d}|_{E}}
satisfies the USFE. Then necessarily there are constants
a_{(*)}>0
so that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow(*),\;x\not\in E}|D_{K}(x)-a_{(*)}\delta(x)|\delta(x)^{-1}\rightarrow 0.

where
(*)
stands for
0
or
\infty
.

Furthermore if
\nabla^{m}K(x)|x|^{m}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
0\leq m\leq k
we can ensure, for
0\leq m\leq k-1
, that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow(*),\;x\not\in E}|\nabla^{m}D_{K}(x)-a_{(*)}\nabla^{m}\delta_{E}(x)|\delta_{E}(x)^{1-m}\rightarrow 0.

Finally, if
K
is radial, then we also have that
K_{\lambda}\rightarrow c_{(*)}
in
C^{k-1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
as
\lambda\rightarrow(*)
for
(*)=0,\infty
and some positive constants
c_{0},c_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Define the functions
f_{N}(x):E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
f_{N}(z)=D_{K}(2^{-N}z)2^{N}
. Since
K
is a distance-standard kernel, we have that

(1)

|\nabla f_{N}(z)|\leq C
for all
z\in(B(0,1)\cap E^{\perp})\setminus\{0\}
,

(2)

f_{N}(0)=0
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3)

|f_{N}(z)|
is uniformly bounded on
B(0,1)\cap E^{\perp}
.

In particular, by Arzela-Ascoli, we may choose a subsequence (still labeled
f_{N}
) such that
f_{N}\rightarrow f
uniformly on
B(0,1)\cap E^{\perp}
. Now let
E_{k}=B(0,2^{k})\setminus B(0,2^{-k})\cap E^{\perp}
. Note that for each fixed
k
, we may choose a constant
C
so that for each

and for any

,

(1)

|f_{N}(z)|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)\leq C2^{k}
,

(2)

|\nabla f_{N}(z)|\leq C
,

(3)

|\nabla^{2}f_{N}(z)|\leq C\delta_{E}(z)^{-1}\leq C2^{k}
.

This allows us to extract a subsequence of
f_{N}
for which
f_{N}\rightarrow g_{k}
in
C^{1}(E_{k})
. By a diagonalization argument, we may then as well assume that
f_{N}\rightarrow g
in
C^{1}_{loc}(E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})
for some function
g\in C^{1}(E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})
.

We now show that
|\nabla g|
is constant. For each fixed

, note that

\displaystyle\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla f_{N}(z)|^{2}-c_{k-N}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}(2^{-N}z)|^{2}-c_{k-N}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{N(n-d)}\int_{A_{k-N}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k-N}|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C(k,n,d)\int_{A_{k-N}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k-N}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z).

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla D_{K}(z)|^{2}-c_{k}|^{2}\delta_{E}(z)^{-n+d}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-d}(z)<\infty,

sending
N\rightarrow\infty
, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int_{A_{k}}||\nabla f_{N}(z)|^{2}-c_{k-N}|^{2}\;dz=0.

Finally, using that
f_{N}\rightarrow g
in
C^{1}_{loc}(E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})
, we see that this implies both that
\tilde{c}=\lim_{k\rightarrow-\infty}c_{k}
exists, and that
|\nabla g(z)|=\tilde{c}
on
A_{k}
. Since

is arbitrary we see that
|\nabla g(z)|=\tilde{c}
on
E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
.

Recall now that
(1/C)\delta_{E}(z)\leq f_{N}(z)\leq C\delta_{E}(z)
, so that in particular,
(1/C)\delta_{E}(z)\leq g(z)\leq C\delta_{E}(z)
. It follows that,
g(0)=0
,
g(z)>0
for each
z\in E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\tilde{c}\neq 0
. Since
g\in C^{1}(E^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})
, Section 3 in
[
DEM21
]
implies that necessarily,
g(z)=a_{0}\delta_{E}(z)
for some constant
a_{0}>0
.

Now since
f_{N}\rightarrow g
uniformly on
B(0,1)\cap E^{\perp}
, we see that this implies that for each
\epsilon>0
, if
N
is large enough, then for any
|z|\leq 1
,
z\in E^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle|f_{N}(z)-a_{0}\delta_{E}(z)|<\epsilon,

i.e.,

\displaystyle|D_{K}(2^{-N}z)2^{N}-a_{0}\delta_{E}(z)|

\displaystyle=2^{N}|D_{K}(y)-a_{0}\delta_{E}(y)|<\epsilon

where
y=2^{-N}z
. It follows that
\lim_{|z|\rightarrow 0}\delta_{E}(z)^{-1}|D_{K}(z)-a_{0}\delta_{E}(z)|=0
. This proves the claim for
(*)=0
, but the proof for
(*)=\infty
is essentially the same.

To prove convergence for the higher derivatives we again restrict ourselves to
(*)=0
, since the argument for
(*)=\infty
is almost identical. Let
\lambda_{i}>0
be any sequence converging to zero. For
\lambda>0
, define
K_{\lambda}(x)=K(\lambda x)
. Now define the sequence of functions
g_{i}(x)=D_{K_{\lambda_{i}}}(x)
for

. By Arzela-Ascoli, we may assume that up to a subsequence,
K_{\lambda_{i}}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in
C^{k-1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for some function
K_{\infty}\in C^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
which is distance standard. By Lemma
3.1
, it follows that
D_{K_{i}}\rightarrow D_{K_{\infty}}
in
C^{k-1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E)
. In particular, we have that
D_{K_{\lambda_{i}}}\rightarrow D_{K_{\infty}}
uniformly in
C^{k-1}(E^{\perp}\cap\overline{(B(0,2)\setminus B(0,1))})
.

As in the computations following Lemma
3.1
, we have that

\displaystyle D_{K_{\lambda_{i}}}(x)

\displaystyle=D_{K}(\lambda_{i}x)/\lambda_{i}

and thus

\displaystyle\nabla^{m}D_{K_{\lambda_{i}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{m}D_{K}(\lambda_{i}x)\lambda_{i}^{m-1}

for

. When

, we know by above that the right-hand side necessarily converges to

as

uniformly for

. It follows that

on

. Thus we have that

\displaystyle\nabla^{m}D_{K}(\lambda_{i}x)\lambda_{i}^{m-1}\rightarrow\nabla^{m}a_{0}\delta(x)

uniformly in

as

. Since

was arbitrary, then necessarily we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\sup_{x\in E^{\perp}\cap\overline{(B(0,2)\setminus B(0,1))}}|\nabla^{m}D_{K}(\lambda x)\lambda^{m-1}-\nabla^{m}a_{0}\delta_{E}(x)|=0.

Unraveling the definition of the limit above gives the desired result, since

and

are translation invariant with respect to

.

To prove the last claim, remark that we have showed that for any sequence
\lambda_{i}\rightarrow(*)
, there is a subsequence
\lambda_{i_{j}}
so that

in

and

is such that

. Since

is radial, then so is

. But then Theorem

implies that

is a positive constant (and the constant is determined by

). Hence the claim is proved.
∎

3.3.
USFE imply uniform rectifiability

The goal of this subsection is to understand for which distance-standard kernels
K
, the USFE for
D_{K,\mu}
implies that
\mu
is uniformly rectifiable. As with non-tangential limits this will be true for essentially all distance-standard kernels. We follow the techniques from
[
DEM21
]
, but first let us introduce some notation.

Definition 3.17
.

If for each
\epsilon>0
, the set

\displaystyle Z(\epsilon)=\{x\in\Omega\;;\;F_{K}(x)>\epsilon\}

is a Carleson set, then we say that
F_{K}
satisfies the weak USFE.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, it is not hard to see that the USFE imply weak USFE. Instead of proving uniform rectifiability directly, we will show
that the weak USFE imply that
E
satisfies the Bilateral Weak Geometric Lemma (BWGL). Along with Ahlfors regularity, this condition characterizes uniform rectifiability
[
DS93
]
.

In what follows, there is one additional assumption needed on
K
to ensure the blow-ups of
F_{K}
are well-behaved. Namely, in addition to assuming that
K
is distance-standard, we assume throughout the entirety of this section that
\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}
is bounded in order to have control on the blowups of
F_{K}
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
n\geq 2
, let
d<n
, and assume
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
supports a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu
. Also, assume that
K
is a distance-standard kernel with the additional assumption that
K\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
with
\|\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\|_{\infty}<\infty
. If
M\geq 1
and
x\in\Omega
, define

\displaystyle W(x)=W_{M}(x)=\{y\in\Omega\cap B(x,M\delta(x))\;;\;\delta(y)\geq M^{-1}\delta(x)\}.

Define the bad sets
\mathcal{B}(\eta)=\mathcal{B}_{M}(\eta)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{M}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\Omega\;;\;F_{K}(y)\geq\eta\text{ for some }y\in W_{M}(x)\}.

If
Z(\epsilon)
is a Carleson set, then for each
M
sufficiently large,
\mathcal{B}_{M}(3\epsilon)
is a Carleson set as well.

Proof.

In
[
DEM21
]
this lemma is proven for
F_{1}
. But the only property of
F
used there is the following continuity assumption: for any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
\tau>0
small enough so that

(3.23)

\displaystyle|F(x)-F(x^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon\text{ whenever }x,x^{\prime}\in\Omega\text{ with }|x-x^{\prime}|\leq 4\tau\delta(x).

It thus suffices to check this condition, which we do by contradiction.

Suppose that there is an
\epsilon_{0}>0
and a sequence
\tau_{i}\downarrow 0
so that for each
i
, one can find points
x_{i},y_{i}\in\Omega
so that
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq 4\tau_{i}\min\{\delta(x_{i}),\delta(y_{i})\}
, but
|F_{K,\mu}(x_{i})-F_{K,\mu}(y_{i})|\geq\epsilon_{0}
. Let
Q_{i}\in E
be such that
|x_{i}-Q_{i}|=\delta(x_{i})\equiv R_{i}
, let
X_{i}=(x_{i}-Q_{i})/R_{i}
, and let
Y_{i}=(y_{i}-Q_{i})/R_{i}
. Define
E_{i}=(E-Q_{i})/R_{i}
, and
\mu_{i}
as in the remarks following Lemma
3.1
. Up to a subsequence, we may as well assume that
\mu_{i}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\infty}
and
E_{i}\rightarrow E_{\infty}
locally in the Hausdorff metric. Since
|X_{i}|=1
for each
i
, we may also assume
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{\infty}
for some
|X_{\infty}|=1
.

Next, we set
K_{i}(x)=K(R_{i}x)
. Remark that since
K
is distance-standard, we know that
K_{i},\nabla K_{i}
are equicontinuous and uniformly bounded on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
\nabla^{3}K
exists and
\|\nabla^{3}K(x)|x|^{3}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, it follows that
\nabla^{2}K_{i}
are also equicontinuous and uniformly bounded on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. By Arzela-Ascoli, we may assume up to a subsequence that
K_{i}\rightarrow K_{\infty}
in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
for some distance-standard kernel
K_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
. Using (
3.1
) we have

\displaystyle F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(X_{i})=F_{K,\mu}(x_{i})\quad\mbox{and}\quad F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(Y_{i})=F_{K,\mu}(y_{i}).

In view of Lemma
3.1
, we know that
D_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}\rightarrow D_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}
in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, and thus
F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}\rightarrow F_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Recall now that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}

\displaystyle\leq|F_{K,\mu}(x_{i})-F_{K,\mu}(y_{i})|=|F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(X_{i})-F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(Y_{i})|.

Since
|X_{i}-Y_{i}|=R_{i}^{-1}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq 4\tau_{i}
, and
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{\infty}
, we have that
Y_{i}\rightarrow X_{\infty}
as well. Since
F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}\rightarrow F_{K_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we see that

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}|F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(X_{i})-F_{K_{i},\mu_{i}}(Y_{i})|=0,

a contradiction. It follows that the function
F_{K}
satisfies (
3.23
), and so one may argue exactly as in
[
DEM21
]
to prove the lemma.
∎

We may now follow the blowup argument to obtain the main lemma in this section.

Lemma 3.19
.

Assume
K
is as in Lemma
3.18
. For each choice of
0<d<n
,
\alpha>0
, an Ahlfors regularity constant
C_{0}
, and constants
\eta>0
and
N\geq 1
, we can find
M\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
such that if
\mu
is Ahlfors regular (of dimension
d
, constant
C_{0}
, and support
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
), and if
x\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}_{M}(3\epsilon)
, then
d
is an integer and there is a
d
-plane
P
such that
d_{x,N\delta(x)}(E,P)\leq\eta
.

Proof.

Again, following
[
DEM21
]
, we argue by contradiction. That is, assume that there is such a choice of
0<d<n
,
\alpha>0
, Ahlfors regular constant
C_{0}
and parameters
\eta>0
and
N\geq 1
so that for each
M_{i}=2^{i}
and
\epsilon_{i}=2^{-i}
, there is a
d
-Ahlfors regular measure
\mu_{i}
(with constant at most
C_{0}
) with support
E_{i}
and point
x_{i}\in\Omega_{i}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{M_{i}}(3\epsilon_{i})
that violate the hypotheses of the lemma. We now proceed with a blow-up argument as in Lemma
3.18
.

Let
Q_{i}
be a point that attains
\delta_{E_{i}}(x_{i})
, and define
R_{i}\equiv\delta_{E_{i}}(x_{i})
with
X_{i}\equiv(x_{i}-Q_{i})/R_{i}
. Arguing as in the previous lemma, define
\tilde{E_{i}}\equiv(E_{i}-Q_{i})/R_{i}
, with supporting
d
-Ahlfors measure
\tilde{\mu}_{i}(A)\equiv\mu_{i}(R_{i}A+Q)/R_{i}^{d}
which has Ahlfors regularity constant bounded by some constant time
C_{0}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\tilde{\mu_{i}}\rightharpoonup\mu_{\infty}
, and
\tilde{E}_{i}\rightarrow E_{\infty}
locally in the Hausdorff metric. For the rescaled kernels

, we have up to a subsequence that

in

. By Lemma
3.1
, we thus have that

uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega_{\infty}
.

Recall that
x_{i}\not\in\mathcal{B}_{M_{i}}(3\epsilon_{i})
, and thus for each

,

\displaystyle F_{K,\mu_{i}}(y)\leq 3(2^{-i})\text{ for all }y\in\Omega_{i}\cap B(x_{i},2^{i}\delta_{E_{i}}(x_{i}))\text{ with }\delta_{E_{i}}(y)\geq 2^{-i}\delta_{E_{i}}(x_{i}).

and

\displaystyle F_{K_{i},\tilde{\mu}_{i}}(y)=F_{K,\mu_{i}}(R_{i}y+Q_{i}).

Recalling that

, we see that for

. Moreover, since

. In particular, for all

\displaystyle F_{K_{i},\tilde{\mu}_{i}}(y)

\displaystyle=F_{K,\mu_{i}}(R_{i}y+Q_{i})\leq 3(2^{-i}).

for each

is constant on each connected component of

.

Computations for radially-invariant distance exact kernels

and to the kernel’s restriction to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
(which, by homogeneity, completely determines the function).

For a
d
-plane
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
P_{E}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow E
be the orthogonal projection onto
E
. In addition, for
x\not\in E
, we define the half
d
-arc induced by
E
and
x
to be the subset
H^{d}(E,x)\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
defined by

\displaystyle H^{d}(E,x)

\displaystyle=\{y/|y|\;:\;y\in E-x\}.

Geometrically,
H^{d}(E,x)
is where the vectors
y-x
for
y\in E
intersect
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Lemma B.1

(A useful change of variables)
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq d<n
and let
\alpha>0
. Let
E\in G(n,d)
and suppose
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. If
\delta_{E}(x_{0})=1
, then

\displaystyle\int_{E}\dfrac{K((x_{0}-y)/|x_{0}-y|)}{|x_{0}-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0})^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

where
H^{d}(E,x_{0})\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is the half
d
-arc induced by
E
and
x_{0}
as above, and

w_{0}=w_{0}(x_{0})=(P_{E}(x_{0})-x_{0})/|P_{E}(x_{0})-x_{0}|.

Proof.

By translation invariance, assume that
x_{0}=0
. After pre-composing with a harmless rotation we may also assume that
E
is given by the plane
\{(y_{1},y_{2},\dotsc,y_{d},0,0,\dotsc,0,-1)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider the map
\Psi
from the open
d
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle U^{d}

\displaystyle=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{d},0)\;:\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|<1\}

to the plane
E
given by

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{d},0)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\dfrac{\tilde{x}}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=(x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{d},0,\dotsc,0,-\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}).

Notice that
\tilde{x}\in H^{d}(E,x)
since
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\lambda\tilde{x}+x\in E
for
\lambda=\left(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}\right)^{-1}
. Moreover, it is easy to check that this map
\Psi
is a bijective mapping from
U^{d}
to
E
. Viewing
E
as a parametrized
d
-manifold, we can compute
R_{K}(0)
as an integral over
U^{d}
. To do this though, we need to compute

\displaystyle V(D\Psi(x))

\displaystyle\equiv\left|\det\left(D\Psi(x)^{T}D\Psi(x)\right)\right|^{1/2}.

One can easily check that
V(D\Psi(x))
is identically equal to
|\det A(x)|
for the
d\times d
matrix
A=(a_{ij}(x))
given by

\displaystyle a_{ij}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{(1-|x|^{2})^{1/2}}+\dfrac{x_{i}x_{j}}{(1-|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(1-|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\left(\delta_{ij}(1-|x|^{2})+x_{i}x_{j}\right)\equiv\dfrac{1}{(1-|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\,b_{ij}(x).

In addition,
B(x)=(b_{ij}(x))
is diagonalizable with eigenvalue
1
with multiplicity
1
, and eigenvalue
(1-|x|^{2})
with multiplicity
d-1
. Hence

\displaystyle V(D\Psi(x))

\displaystyle=\left|\det A(x)\right|=\left|\det\dfrac{1}{(1-|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\,B(x)\right|

\displaystyle=(1-|x|^{2})^{-d(3/2)}(1-|x|^{2})^{d-1}=(1-|x|^{2})^{-d/2-1}.

Note if
\gamma
is the angle between
\tilde{x}
and
-e_{n}
, then
\cos(\gamma)=\tilde{x}\cdot(-e_{n})=\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}
. Thus, viewing
E
as a parametrized manifold, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{E}\dfrac{K(0-y)}{|0-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{d}}K(-\tilde{x})\left(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}\right)^{d+\alpha}V(D\Psi(x))\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{d}}K(-\tilde{x})\left(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}\right)^{\alpha-2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{d}}K(-\tilde{x})(\tilde{x}\cdot(-e_{n}))^{\alpha-2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x).

Finally, we rewrite this last integral as the integral over the parametrized manifold
H^{d}(E,0)
. Recall that in this case the map
\Phi:U^{d}\rightarrow H^{d}(E,x)
that takes
x\rightarrow\tilde{x}
has

\displaystyle V(D\Phi(x))=1/\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}=(\tilde{x}\cdot(-e_{n}))^{-1}.

Altogether we have that

\displaystyle\int_{E}\dfrac{K(0-y)}{|0-y|^{d+\alpha}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{d}}K(-\tilde{x})(\tilde{x}\cdot(-e_{n}))^{\alpha-2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{d}}K(-\tilde{x})(\tilde{x}\cdot(-e_{n}))^{\alpha-1}V(D\Phi(x))\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{d}(E,x)}K(-z)\left(z\cdot(-e_{n})\right)^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(z).

∎

With this change of variables, we obtain a more concrete characterization of
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact kernels in the scale-invariant setting. Geometrically, the following statement says that the distance-exact kernels (outside
E
) are precisely those functions on the sphere whose weighted average over a certain collection of half
d
-arcs is constant.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N},\alpha>0
with
d<n
, and let
E\in G(n,d)
. Then a kernel
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact for
E
if and only if

(B.1)

\displaystyle\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-Aw)(w\cdot w_{0})^{\alpha-1}d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)=\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0})^{\alpha-1}d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

for any
x_{0}\not\in E
with
\delta_{E}(x_{0})=1
, and every orthogonal transformation
A\in O^{n}
such that
A(E)=E
. Here, as above,
w_{0}=(x_{0}-P_{E}(x_{0}))/|x_{0}-P_{E}(x_{0})|
.

Proof.

Fix the parameters
n,d,\alpha,E,
as in the statement of the theorem and let
x_{0}\not\in E
be a point with
\delta_{E}(x_{0})=1
. Recall that since
E
is flat we have
D_{K,E}
is
1
-homogeneous. In particular, this implies that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact for
E
if and only if for any point
x\not\in E
with
\delta_{E}(x)=\delta_{E}(x_{0})
, we have
D_{K,E}(x)=D_{K,E}(x_{0})
, or equivalently,
R_{K,E}(x)=R_{K,E}(x_{0})
. By virtue of Lemma
B.1
, this is true if and only if

(B.2)

\displaystyle\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0}(x_{0}))^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{d}(E,x)}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0}(x))^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

for every such
x
.

Now, since
E
is flat,
R_{K,E}
is translation invariant with respect to vectors parallel to
E
. Hence we need only consider such
x
with
\delta_{E}(x)=\delta_{E}(x_{0})
and such that there is an orthogonal transformation
A
for which
Ax_{0}=x
and
A(E)=E
. Since
A_{\sharp}H^{d}(E,x_{0})=H^{d}(E,x)
, changing variables in the right-hand side of (
B.2
) (and using that
A
is orthogonal), we see that
K
is
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact for
E
if and only if

\displaystyle\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-Aw)(w\cdot w_{0}(x_{0}))^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{d}(E,x_{0})}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0}(x_{0}))^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)

for any orthogonal transformation
A
that fixes
E
.
∎

We end with several observations on the (non-)existence of homogeneous distance exact kernels in higher co-dimension. First we observe that there are many of them in co-dimension 1.

Corollary B.3
.

All even kernels give rise to
(n-1,\alpha)
-distance exact kernels for any
\alpha>0
. Furthermore, if
\alpha=1
the constant
c_{E}
can be taken independent of the plane
E
.

Proof.

When
d=n-1
, the condition (
B.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{H^{n-1}(E,x_{0})}K(-w)(w\cdot w_{0})^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{n-1}(E,x_{0})}K(w)(w\cdot w_{0})^{\alpha-1}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(w).

In particular, any
even
kernel
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
satisfies this condition, and thus any such kernel will be
(d,\alpha)
-distance-exact. Moreover, in the case
\alpha=1
, the integral is independent of the particular
E
chosen.
∎

On the other hand, in higher co-dimension there do not exist non-trivial distance-orthogonal kernels which are
0
-homogeneous. The authors would like to thank Dmitriy Bilyk for pointing out the connection of the integral conditions on distance-exact kernels on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
to the Funk Transform, which gives rise to the following result.

Corollary B.4
.

In
n=3
, every
0
-homogeneous
(1,1)
-distance orthogonal kernel is identically equal to zero.

Proof.

Suppose that
K\in C(\mathbb{S}^{2}),\alpha=1
, and
K
is
(1,1)
-distance-orthogonal. Then Theorem
B.2
implies that

(B.3)

\displaystyle\int_{H^{1}(E,x_{0})}K(w)\;d\mathcal{H}^{1}(w)=0

for each half
1
-arc
H^{1}(E,x_{0})\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
, from which one can deduce that
K
is necessarily even. To see this, simply restrict the half
1
-arcs we consider to be contained in the same great circle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then abusing notation and writing
K
for the restriction of
K
to this circle in polar coordinates, we obtain that for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\theta}^{\pi+\theta}K(\omega)d\omega=0.

It then follows that (again, abusing notation),
K(\theta)=K(\pi+\theta)
for each
\theta
. Lifting this to our original kernel implies that
K
is even.

Now from here, using (
B.3
) applied to the half
1
-arcs
H^{1}(E,x_{0})
and
-H^{1}(E,x_{0})
implies

\displaystyle\int_{C}K(w)\;d\mathcal{H}^{d}(w)=0

for each great circle
C\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
. If
\mathcal{F}
is the Funk transform on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then this says exactly that
\mathcal{F}(K)\equiv 0
. Since this transform is invertible on even continuous functions (see for example, Chapter 2 in
[
Hel99
]
for references and the definition of the Funk transform), we therefore conclude that
K\equiv 0
.
∎
