Abstract

The Cahn–Hilliard equation with anisotropic energy contributions frequently appears in many physical systems. Systematic analytical results for the case with the relevant logarithmic free energy have been missing so far. We close this gap and show existence, uniqueness, regularity, and separation properties of weak solutions to the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation with logarithmic free energy. Since firstly, the equation becomes highly non-linear, and secondly, the relevant anisotropies are non-smooth, the analysis becomes quite involved. In particular, new regularity results for quasilinear elliptic equations of second order need to be shown.

Keywords:
Cahn–Hilliard equation, anisotropy, weak solutions, regularity, separation property.

35K55, 35K61, 74E10, 35Q99.

1  Introduction

The classical Cahn–Hilliard equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\big(M(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega_{T}$},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega_{T}$},

(1.1b)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}

is a bounded domain,
T
is a positive final time and

\Omega_{T}=(0,T)\times\Omega
, can be considered as an
H^{-1}
-gradient flow
of the
Ginzburg–Landau energy

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{iso}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}F(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

see
[
13
,
16
]
.
Here,
\varepsilon>0
is a small parameter related to the width of a diffuse
interface separating two different phases, and
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a free energy density. The function
F

has two global minima that correspond to these two phases, and the
functional
E_{\mathrm{iso}}
describes the energy associated with the diffuse interface. This interfacial energy, which is given by the
Ginzburg–Landau functional, is isotropic. This means that the
energy does not depend on the local orientation of the interface. In many
applications, in particular when crystalline materials are to be described, anisotropic
energies are more realistic, see, e.g.,
[
27
]
. In anisotropic materials, the energy
density actually depends on the local orientation of the interface. In fact, in
a sharp-interface description, the energy is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\nu})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1},

(1.2)

where
\Sigma
is a hypersurface representing the interface,

\boldsymbol{\nu}
stands for the unit normal on
\Sigma
,

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
refers to integration with respect to the

(d-1)
-dimensional surface measure, and

\displaystyle\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)

is an anisotropic density function. The energy density in (
1.2
) now depends via
the normal
\boldsymbol{\nu}
on the local orientation of the
interface. In this context, the function
\gamma
is assumed to be convex and to fulfill

\displaystyle\gamma(\lambda\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\lambda\gamma(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}$ and all $\lambda>0$},

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}$}.

(1.3b)

The property (
1.3a
) says that
\gamma
is positively
one-homogeneous and implies that an anisotropic
\gamma
is
necessarily not differentiable in
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}
.
On the diffuse-interface level, the energy is then given by

\displaystyle E:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptE(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon A(\nabla\varphi)+\varepsilon^{-1}F(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(1.4)

where
A
is given by

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{p})=\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(\mathbf{p})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

It can be shown that the diffuse-interface energy
E
introduced in (
1.4
) converges to the sharp-interface energy (
1.2
) up to a multiplicative constant in the sense of
\Gamma
-limits, see, e.g.,
[
19
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
25
]
.
For the forthcoming mathematical analysis, it is important that
A
is
positively two-homogeneous. In particular, this already entails that an anisotropic
A
necessarily
cannot be twice differentiable. This will make the regularity theory,
which will be discussed later, quite involved.

As discussed for example in
[
18
]
, one can also consider the

H^{-1}
-gradient flow of the anisotropic energy
E
introduced in (
1.4
).
In fact, a weighted
H^{-1}
-gradient flow approach was used in
[
18
]
to study the following general version of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\big(M(\nabla\varphi,\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega_{T}$},

(1.5a)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\nabla\cdot A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega_{T}$},

(1.5b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma_{T}$},

(1.5c)

\displaystyle\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma_{T}$},

(1.5d)

\displaystyle\varphi|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},

(1.5e)

where
\Gamma_{T}=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
, and
\mathbf{n}
is the outer unit normal on
\partial\Omega
.
From now on, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary.
Moreover, the function
M:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
representing the mobility of the mixture is also allowed to be anisotropic.

Especially in applications related to materials science, the physically relevant choice of
F
in the anisotropic energy
E
is the
logarithmic potential

\displaystyle F(s)=\frac{\theta}{2}\big[(1+s)\,\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\,\ln(1-s)\big]+\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(1-s^{2}),

(1.6)

for all
s\in(-1,1)
, which is also referred to as the
Flory–Huggins potential
. Here,
{\theta>0}
is the absolute temperature of the mixture, and
\theta_{c}
is a critical temperature such that phase separation occurs if
0<\theta<\theta_{c}
. The logarithmic potential is classified as a singular potential as its derivative
F^{\prime}
diverges to
\pm\infty
when its argument approaches
\pm 1
. It is often approximated by the
polynomial double-well potential

\displaystyle F(s)=\frac{\alpha}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in(-1,1)$},

(1.7)

where
\alpha>0
is a suitable constant. In the following, we will simply set
\alpha=1
for simplicity.
Another common singular potential is the
double-obstacle potential
, which is given by

\displaystyle F(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(1-s^{2})&\text{if $|s|\leq 1$,}\\
+\infty&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(1.8)

Although originally introduced to model spinodal decomposition in binary alloys, the Cahn–Hilliard equation found many new applications in the recent past.
The anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation has also been used to describe anisotropic solidification processes. In particular, it is capable of describing snow crystal growth as well as the solidification of metals, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
]
. Another important application of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation is to model the growth of thin solid films, which play an important role in the self-organization of nanostructures, see, e.g.,

[
26
,
10
,
28
,
18
]
. In this context, also works on the anisotropic Allen–Cahn equation
[
11
,
1
,
21
,
22
]
and higher order models
[
9
]
are relevant. For more information
on regularity theory and separation properties of the isotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation with logarithmic potential, we recommend the paper
[
14
]
as well as the book
[
24
]
.

The goal of the present paper is to show the existence of weak solutions to the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation with logarithmic potential, to prove a uniqueness result, to show regularity results, and to establish a separation property
under suitable assumptions on the initial data.
It states that for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, there exists
\delta(t)\in(0,1]
such that

\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1-\delta(t).

Here, the term “separation property” means that the solution stays away from the pure states, which are represented by
\varphi=\pm 1
.
In the case
d=2
, we are even able to establish a separation property that is uniform in time. More precisely, there exists a constant
\delta^{*}\in(0,1]
such that for all
(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}
,

\left|\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\right|\leq 1-\delta^{*}.

These results are already well-known in the case of an isotropic energy (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
24
]
), but become non-trivial in the case of anisotropic energies. This is firstly because the resulting equations are much more non-linear and secondly because the anisotropic potential
A
is not smooth. The latter already follows from the requirement that
A
is positively two-homogeneous, but can also be traced back to the usage of non-smooth anisotropies
\gamma
. It is therefore surprising that we can still prove
H^{2}
-regularity with respect to the spatial variables, and that we are also able to show that
\partial_{t}\varphi
lies in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
. To establish separation properties, typically a quite high regularity of the solutions is required. We are able to derive certain estimates for
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
, which at least allows us to prove a separation property for almost all times.

The outline of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce the notation used in this paper, present the general assumptions and state the main results. In Section
3
, we prove the existence result for the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation with a logarithmic potential. Section
4
is devoted to the uniqueness of weak solutions and in Section
5
, we present regularity results for a general class of quasilinear second-order elliptic equations under minimal assumptions on the data. These results are then used in Section
6
to prove higher regularity of weak solutions to the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation, which can in turn be used to establish a separation property for almost all times.

2  Preliminaries and main results

2.1  Notation

We first introduce some notation that is supposed to hold throughout this paper.

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
k\geq 0
, we use the standard notation
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
for Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on any open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\in\mathbb{N}
). The corresponding norms are denoted by
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}
.
In the case
p=2
, these spaces are Hilbert spaces and we write
H^{k}(\Omega)=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. As usual, we identify
H^{0}(\Omega)
with
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

For any Banach space
X
, its dual space is denoted by
X^{\prime}
, and the associated duality pairing between elements
y\in X^{\prime}
and
x\in X
is denoted by
\langle y,x\rangle_{X}
. In the case
X=H^{1}(\Omega)
, we will simply write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
instead of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
.
If
X
is a Hilbert space, we denote its inner product by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
. Moreover, we write

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\langle f,1\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }f\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}

to denote the generalized spatial mean of
f
. Here,
\left|\Omega\right|
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
.
With the usual identification
L^{1}(\Omega)\subset H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}
it holds that

\langle f\rangle_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\int_{\Omega}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
if

f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.
In addition, we introduce

\displaystyle H^{1}_{(m)}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\langle u\rangle_{\Omega}=m\big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for any $m\in\mathbb{R}$},

\displaystyle H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{f\in\big(H^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}\,\big|\,\langle f\rangle_{\Omega}=0\big\}.

We point out that for every
m\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{1}_{(m)}(\Omega)
is an affine subspace of the Hilbert space
H^{1}(\Omega)
. In the case
m=0
, it is even a closed linear subspace, meaning that
H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
is also a Hilbert space.

Let us now assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain. It is well-known that there exists a solution operator

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{N})^{-1}:H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\to H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptf\mapsto u_{f}

mapping any functional
f\in H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
onto its corresponding solution
u_{f}\in H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
of the Poisson–Neumann problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{f}=f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla u_{f}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma=\partial\Omega$}.

Then, the bilinear form

\displaystyle\left(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\right)_{-1}:H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\left(f,g\right)_{-1}:=\left(\nabla(-\Delta_{N})^{-1}f,\nabla(-\Delta_{N})^{-1}g\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

defines an inner product on
H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
, and the function

\displaystyle\|\,\cdot\,\|_{-1}:H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pt\|f\|_{-1}:=\sqrt{\left(f,f\right)_{-1}}

defines the induced norm on
H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
. Note that
H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\subset H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
, and the functions
\left(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\right)_{-1}
and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{-1}
also define an inner product and a norm, respectively, on the space
H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
.

2.2  General assumptions

We make the following general assumptions.

(\mathbf{A1})

The set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with outer unit normal vector field
\mathbf{n}
.
Moreover, the final time
T
and the interface parameter
\varepsilon
are arbitrary positive constants.
For simplicity, we set
\varepsilon=1
as the choice of this parameter does not have any impact on the mathematical analysis.

(\mathbf{A2})

The function
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable, positive on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and positively two-homogeneous, i.e.,

\displaystyle A(\lambda\mathbf{p})=\lambda^{2}A(\mathbf{p})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\lambda>0$ and $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

As a consequence, there exist constants
A_{0},A_{1},a_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<A_{0}\leq A_{1}
and
a_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle A_{0}\left|\mathbf{p}\right|^{2}\leq A(\mathbf{p})\leq A_{1}\left|\mathbf{p}\right|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\left|A^{\prime}(\mathbf{p})\right|\leq a_{1}\left|\mathbf{p}\right|\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

The gradient
A^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be strongly monotone, i.e., there exists a constant
a_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\big(A^{\prime}(\mathbf{p})-A^{\prime}(\mathbf{q})\big)\cdot(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\geq a_{0}\left|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

This directly implies that
A
is strongly convex and thus strictly convex.

(\mathbf{A3})

The function
M:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and there exist constants
M_{0},M_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<M_{0}\leq M_{1}
such that

\displaystyle M_{0}\leq M(\mathbf{p},s)\leq M_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $s\in\mathbb{R}$}.

2.3  Weak solutions for smooth potentials

The anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) endowed with a regular potential
F
was investigated in
[
18
]
. In this subsection, we recall the most important results.

We consider potentials
F
satisfying the following assumption:

(\mathbf{F1})

The potential
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable.
Moreover, there exists an exponent
p\in[2,6)
as well as
non-negative constants
B_{F}
,
C_{F}
and
C_{F^{\prime}}
such that

\displaystyle-B_{F}\leq F(s)\leq C_{F}(1+\left|s\right|^{p})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\left|F^{\prime}(s)\right|\leq C_{F^{\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{p-1})

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

The polynomial double-well potential introduced in (
1.7
) naturally fulfills (

F1

) with
p=4
.
However, both the logarithmic potential (see (
1.6
)) and the double-obstacle potential (see (
1.8
)) do not satisfy this assumption.

A weak solution of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A3

) and (

F1

) are fulfilled, and let
{\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)}
be any initial datum.
Then, the pair
(\varphi,\mu)
is called a weak solution of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) if the following conditions are fulfilled:

(i)

The functions
\varphi
,
\mu
and
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
have the regularity

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\in C\big([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\big)\cap L^{\infty}\big(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)\big)\cap H^{1}\big(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}\big),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\in L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)\big),

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\in L^{2}\big(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\big).

(ii)

The pair
(\varphi,\mu)
satisfies the weak formulations

\displaystyle\big<\partial_{t}\varphi,\zeta\big>_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}M(\nabla\varphi,\varphi)\,\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu\,\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)\cdot\nabla\eta+F^{\prime}(\varphi)\,\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(2.1b)

a.e. in
[0,T]
for all test functions
\zeta,\eta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Moreover,
\varphi
satisfies the initial condition

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\Omega$.}

(2.2)

(iii)

The pair
(\varphi,\mu)
satisfies the weak energy dissipation law

\displaystyle E\big(\varphi(t)\big)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}M(\nabla\varphi,\varphi)\,\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq E(\varphi_{0})

(2.3)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

The existence of such a weak solution was established in
[
18
]
. We restate the existence result as the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A3

) and (

F1

) are fulfilled, and let
{\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)}
be any initial datum. Then, there exists a weak solution
(\varphi,\mu)
to the system (
1.5
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

We point out that since the final time
T
can be chosen arbitrarily (see (

A1

)), the weak solution provided by Proposition
2.2
actually exists globally in time, i.e., on the whole interval
[0,\infty)
.
To obtain an additional uniform estimate for
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
, we need a stronger assumption on the potential
F
:

(\mathbf{F2})

The potential
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is twice continuously differentiable, non-negative,
and there exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle-c_{0}\leq F^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq c_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(2.4)

We point out that (

F2

) implies that (

F1

) holds with
p=2
, which means that the potential
F
has at most quadratic growth.
Provided that
F
satisfies (

F2

), we obtain the following uniform estimate on
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A3

)
and (

F2

) are fulfilled. Let
\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be any initial datum satisfying

\left|\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}\right|\leq 1-\kappa
for some
\kappa\in(0,1]
,
and let
(\varphi,\mu)
be a corresponding weak solution. Then, there exists a constant
c>0
depending only on
\varphi_{0}
,
E(\varphi_{0})
,
c_{0}
and the constants in (

A1

)–(

A3

), but not on
c_{1}
, such that for almost all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime}\big(\varphi(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{c}{\kappa^{2}}\Big[1

\displaystyle+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}([-R,R])}^{2}+\|\nabla\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big],

(2.5)

where
R:=\left|\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}\right|+\frac{\kappa}{2}<1
.

A proof of this result can be found in
[
18
]
. We point out that the inequality (
2.5
) was not explicitly stated there but can clearly be extracted from the proof of
[
18
, Corollary 4.4]
.

The connection between (
2.5
) and the uniform estimate stated in
[
18
, Corollary 4.4]
is as follows:
Due to (

A3

), (

F2

) and the weak energy dissipation law (
2.3
), we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M_{0}\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq E(\varphi_{0}).

(2.6)

We now integrate (
2.5
) with respect to time from
0
to
T
. Employing (
2.6
), we conclude

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime}\big(\varphi\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq\frac{c^{*}}{\kappa^{2}}\Big[1+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}([-R,R])}^{2}\Big],

(2.7)

for some constant
c^{*}>0
depending only on
\varphi_{0}
,
E(\varphi_{0})
,
c_{0}
and the constants in (

A1

)–(

A3

), which is exactly the estimate stated in
[
18
, Corollary 4.4]
.

We point out that the more precise estimate (
2.5
) will be needed in the context of Theorem
2.9
, where
F^{\prime}(\varphi)\in L^{\infty}\big(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\big)
will be shown under suitable assumptions.

2.4  Weak solutions for the logarithmic potential

In this subsection, we choose
F
as the logarithmic potential that was introduced in (
1.6
). It can be decomposed as
F(s)=F_{1}(s)+F_{2}(s)
,
where

\displaystyle F_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{2}\big[(1+s)\,\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\,\ln(1-s)\big],

(2.8a)

\displaystyle F_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(1-s^{2})

(2.8b)

for all
s\in(-1,1)
. We point out that
F_{1}
is convex whereas
F_{2}
is concave. Consequently, the derivative
F_{1}^{\prime}
is monotonically increasing. It is worth mentioning that the function
F_{1}
(and thus also
F
) possesses a continuous extension onto the interval
[-1,1]
. However, this does not hold for
F_{1}^{\prime}
as this function becomes singular at
\pm 1
.

In this situation, a weak solution of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) is defined as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A3

) are fulfilled, let
F
be the logarithmic potential given by (
1.6
), and let
{\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)}
be any initial datum with
\left|\varphi_{0}\right|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
as well as
\left|\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle\right|<1
.
Then, the pair
(\varphi,\mu)
is called a weak solution of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\in L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)\big),

3  Existence of weak solutions of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation with logarithmic potential

In this section, we prove the existence of a weak solution to the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
), where
F
is chosen as the logarithmic potential (see (
1.6
)).

We split the proof into three steps.

Step 1: Approximation of the logarithmic potential by smooth potentials.

The idea of this proof is to approximate the logarithmic potential
F
by a suitable sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of smooth potentials.

Therefore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we approximate the function

H:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptH(s)=s\,\ln(s)

by its second-order Taylor polynomial at the point
\frac{1}{n}
, which is given by

\displaystyle H_{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=H\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\big)+H^{\prime}\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\left(s-\tfrac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{1}{2}H^{\prime\prime}\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\left(s-\tfrac{1}{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{:}=-\tfrac{1}{2n}-s\ln(n)+\tfrac{n}{2}s^{2}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now define

\displaystyle F_{1,n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\theta}{2}\begin{cases}(1-s)\ln(1-s)+H_{n}(1+s)&\text{if }s<-1+\frac{1}{n},\\
(1-s)\ln(1-s)+(1+s)\ln(1+s)&\text{if }|s|\leq 1-\frac{1}{n},\\
H_{n}(1-s)+(1+s)\ln(1+s)&\text{if }s>1-\frac{1}{n},\end{cases}

\displaystyle G_{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=F_{1,n}(s)+F_{2}(s)=F_{1,n}(s)+\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(1-s^{2}).

It is straightforward to check that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
F_{1,n}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with

\displaystyle F_{1,n}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{2}\begin{cases}-\ln(1-s)+n(s+1)-1-\ln(n)&\quad\text{if }s<-1+\frac{1}{n},\\
\ln(1+s)-\ln(1-s)&\quad\text{if }|s|\leq 1-\frac{1}{n},\\
\ln(1+s)+n(s-1)+1+\ln(n)&\quad\text{if }s>1-\frac{1}{n},\end{cases}

\displaystyle F_{1,n}^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{2}\begin{cases}(1-s)^{-1}+n&\quad\text{if }s<-1+\frac{1}{n},\\
2(1-s^{2})^{-1}&\quad\text{if }|s|\leq 1-\frac{1}{n},\\
(1+s)^{-1}+n&\quad\text{if }s>1-\frac{1}{n},\end{cases}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we further introduce the approximate energy functional

\displaystyle E_{n}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptE_{n}(\varphi):=\int_{\Omega}A(\nabla\varphi)+G_{n}(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

(3.1)

Defining

\displaystyle N:=\min\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}:m>\exp\left(\frac{2\theta_{c}}{\theta}\right)\right\},

(3.2)

we now show that the sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has the following properties:

(i)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in(-1,1)
, it holds
G_{n}(s)\leq F(s)
.

(ii)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq N
,
G_{n}
satisfies (

F2

) with
c_{0}=\theta_{c}
and
c_{1}=\theta n-\theta_{c}
.

(iii)

G_{n}\to F
in
C^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((-1,1))
as
n\to\infty
.

To prove (i), we fix an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us first consider the case
s\in\big(1-\frac{1}{n},1\big)
.
Recalling that
H^{\prime\prime}
is monotonically decreasing, we use Taylor’s formula to obtain

\displaystyle F(s)-G_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\big(H(1-s)-H_{n}(1-s)\big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\theta\big(H^{\prime\prime}(1-\xi)-H^{\prime\prime}(1-\tfrac{1}{n})\big)\big(s-\tfrac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\geq 0

for some
\xi\in\big[0,\tfrac{1}{n}\big]
. Hence
F\geq G_{n}
on
\big(1-\frac{1}{n},1\big)
. Proceeding similarly, we show that
F\geq G_{n}
on the interval
\big(-1,-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)
. As obviously
F=G_{n}
on
\big[-1+\frac{1}{n},1-\frac{1}{n}\big]
, this verifies (i).

We further notice that
F_{1,n}^{\prime\prime}>0
and thus,
F_{1,n}
is strictly convex. Since
F_{1,n}
is continuous, it attains its minimum on the compact interval
[-1,1]
. As
F_{1,n}^{\prime}(0)=0
, we infer from the strict convexity of
F_{1,n}
that this minimum is attained at
s=0
.
We thus have

\displaystyle G_{n}(s)\geq F_{1,n}(s)\geq F_{1,n}(0)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in[-1,1]$}.

For
N
as defined in (
3.2
), we now consider
n\geq N
which implies
\tfrac{1}{2}\theta n>\theta_{c}
. Then, for all
s\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\big[\ln(1+s)+n(s-1)+1+\ln(n)\big]-\theta_{c}s

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\big[\ln(1+s)-n+1+\ln(n)\big]+\big[\tfrac{1}{2}\theta n-\theta_{c}\big]s

\displaystyle\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\big[\ln(n)-n\big]+\big[\tfrac{1}{2}\theta n-\theta_{c}\big]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\ln(n)-\theta_{c}\;>0.

In a similar fashion, we show that
G_{n}^{\prime}(s)<0
for all
s\leq 1
. In summary, we conclude that
G_{n}
is non-negative for all
n\geq N
.
Moreover, it is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle-\theta_{c}\leq G_{n}^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq\theta n-\theta_{c}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This means that (ii) is established.

Let now
K
be an arbitrary compact subset of
(-1,1)
. Then, there exists
n_{K}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq n_{K}
, it holds
K\subset\big(-1+\tfrac{1}{n},1-\tfrac{1}{n}\big)
. This implies
G_{n}=F
on
K
, which proves (iii).

Step 2: A priori estimates for the sequence of approximate solutions.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq N
, according to
Proposition
2.2
, there exists a weak solution
(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n})
to the potential
G_{n}
and the initial datum
\varphi_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.1
. In the remainder of this proof, the letter
C
will denote generic positive constants depending only on
F
,
\varphi_{0}
and the quantities introduced in (

A1

)–(

A3

), but not on the approximation index
n
.

In the following, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq N
be arbitrary. We now recall that the weak solution
(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n})
satisfies the weak energy dissipation law (cf. Definition
2.1
(iii)
) written for the energy functional
E_{n}
that was introduced in (
3.1
). Invoking the properties (i) and (ii) from Step 1, we thus derive the estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\varphi_{n}(t)\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}M_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mu_{n}(s)\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq E_{n}\big(\varphi_{n}(t)\big)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}M\big(\nabla\varphi_{n}(s),\varphi_{n}(s)\big)\left|\nabla\mu_{n}(s)\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq E_{n}(\varphi_{0})\leq E(\varphi_{0})\leq C

(3.3)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Testing the weak formulation (
2.1a
) written for
(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n})
with
\zeta\equiv 1
, we infer that
\langle\varphi_{n}(t)\rangle=\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Hence, employing Poincaré’s inequality, we conclude

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C.

(3.4)

From the weak formulation (
2.1b
) written for
(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n})
, we directly infer

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq C.

Defining
\kappa:=1-\left|\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle\right|\in(0,1]
and recalling that
G_{n}
satisfies (

F2

), we use Corollary
2.3
along with (
3.4
) and property (i) of Step 1 to obtain

\displaystyle\big\|G_{n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{c}{\kappa^{2}}\Big[C+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}([-1,1])}^{2}+\|\nabla\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big].

(3.5)

Integrating this inequality with respect to time from
0
to
T
, we deduce

\displaystyle\big\|G_{n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\leq C\big(C+\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\big)\leq C

(3.6)

by means of (
3.4
).
After testing the weak formulation (
2.1b
) written for
(\varphi_{n},\mu_{n})
with
\eta\equiv 1
and taking the modulus of the resulting equation, we use Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\mu_{n}(t)\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|\leq\int_{\Omega}\big|G_{n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}(t)\big)\big|\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq C.

Moreover, since
F_{2}^{\prime}(s)=-\theta_{c}s
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we further have

\displaystyle\big\|F_{1,n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\big\|G_{n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\theta_{c}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C.

Overall, we have thus shown

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\|\mu_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\big\|F_{1,n}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{n}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C.

(3.7)

Step 3: Convergence to a weak solution.

In view of the uniform estimate (
3
), we now use the Banach–Alaoglu theorem as well as the Aubin–Lions–Simon lemma to conclude that there exist functions
\varphi
,
\mu
and
f
such that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle\to\partial_{t}\varphi

weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle\to\varphi

weakly-
∗
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
,

strongly in
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle\to\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))$},

(3.10)

\displaystyle F_{1,n}^{\prime}(\varphi_{n})

\displaystyle\to f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in $L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))$},

(3.11)

as
n\to\infty
along a non-relabeled subsequence.
As the functions
\big|F_{1,n}^{\prime}(\varphi_{n})\big|
are measurable and non-negative, Fatou’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\;\left|F_{1,n}^{\prime}(\varphi_{n})\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(t,\mathbf{x})\leq\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\;\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left|F_{1,n}^{\prime}(\varphi_{n})\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(t,\mathbf{x})\leq C.

(3.12)

We now fix arbitrary representatives of the functions
\varphi
and
\varphi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of their respective equivalence classes in order to make a pointwise evaluation possible.
Due to (
3
), there exists a null set
\mathcal{M}\subset\Omega_{T}
such that
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
pointwise on
\Omega_{T}\setminus\mathcal{M}
.
We now define the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{-}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}\setminus\mathcal{M}\;\big|\;\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\leq-1\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}\,

\displaystyle:=\big\{(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}\setminus\mathcal{M}\;\big|\;\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\in(-1,1)\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{+}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}\setminus\mathcal{M}\;\big|\;\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\geq 1\big\},

which entails that
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{-}\cup\mathcal{M}_{0}\cup\mathcal{M}_{+}
.

Let now
(t,\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
be arbitrary.
As
\varphi_{n}(t,\mathbf{x})\to\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\geq 1
as
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t,\mathbf{x})>

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if $n$ is sufficiently large},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\min\big\{\varphi_{n}(t,\mathbf{x}),1-\tfrac{1}{n}\big\}\to

\displaystyle\;1

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

4  Uniqueness of the weak solution

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem
2.7
, which states (under suitable assumptions on the potential
F
) that weak solutions of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation are unique provided that the mobility function
M
is constant.

As the mobility function
M
is assumed to be constant, we simply set
M\equiv 1
without loss of generality.

Let
(\varphi,\mu)
be a weak solution given by Proposition
2.2
if
F
is a regular potential or given by Theorem
2.5
if
F
is the logarithmic potential.
We now assume that
(\varphi_{*},\mu_{*})
is a further weak solution of the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) to the same initial datum
\varphi_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.1
if
F
is a regular potential or in the sense of Definition
2.4
if
F
is the logarithmic potential. In the following, we use the notation

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi}:=\varphi-\varphi_{*}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mu}:=\mu-\mu_{*}.

We point out that

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi}(0)=0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{\varphi}(t)\in H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)\;\;\text{for almost all $t\in[0,T]$}.

In order to prove uniqueness, we intend to show that
\|\overline{\varphi}(t)\|_{-1}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
From the weak formulations written for
(\varphi,\mu)
and
(\varphi_{*},\mu_{*})
, we deduce

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\overline{\varphi}(s),\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\overline{\mu}(s)\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\overline{\mu}(s)\,\eta\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[A^{\prime}\big(\nabla\varphi(s)\big)-A^{\prime}\big(\nabla\varphi_{*}(s)\big)\big]\cdot\nabla\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{\Omega}\big[F^{\prime}\big(\varphi(s)\big)-F^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{*}(s)\big)\big]\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(4.1b)

for all test functions
\zeta,\eta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and almost all
s\in[0,T]
.
Testing (
4.1a
) with
\zeta\equiv 1
, we immediately observe that
\partial_{t}\overline{\varphi}(s)\in H^{-1}_{(0)}(\Omega)
for almost all
s\in[0,T]
.

Let now
t\in[0,T]
be arbitrary. In the following, we use the notation
\Omega_{t}:=(0,t)\times\Omega
.
For almost all
s\in[0,t]
, we now test (
4.1
) with
\eta:=\overline{\varphi}(s)\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and integrate the resulting equation with respect to
s
from
0
to
t
. Recalling the monotonicity of
A^{\prime}
and
F_{1}^{\prime}
as well as the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{2}^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{t}}\overline{\mu}\,\overline{\varphi}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega_{t}}(A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)-A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi_{*}))\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{\Omega_{t}}\big[F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi)-F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{*})\big]\overline{\varphi}+\big[F_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi)-F_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi_{*})\big]\overline{\varphi}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\geq a_{0}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\nabla\overline{\varphi}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})-L\int_{\Omega_{t}}\left|\overline{\varphi}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x}),

(4.2)

where
L
denotes the minimal Lipschitz constant of
F_{2}^{\prime}
.
Invoking the definition of
(-\Delta_{N})^{-1}
(see Subsection
2.1
),
we derive the inequality

\displaystyle\|\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla(-\Delta_{N})^{-1}\overline{\varphi}(s)\cdot\nabla\overline{\varphi}(s)\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a_{0}}{2}\|\nabla\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2a_{0}}\|\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{-1}^{2}

for almost all
s\in[0,T]
. Using this estimate to bound the last line of (
4
) from below, we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{t}}\overline{\mu}\,\overline{\varphi}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})\geq\frac{a_{0}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s-C\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{-1}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s,

(4.3)

where
C
is a positive constant depending on
L
and
a_{0}
.
By proceeding exactly as for the isotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation,
the integral on the right-hand side can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{t}}\overline{\varphi}\,\overline{\mu}\;\mathrm{d}(s,\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{\varphi}(t)\|_{-1}^{2}.

(4.4)

(For more details about the derivation of this identity, we refer to
[
15
]
or
[
17
]
.)
Combining (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we thus arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{\varphi}(t)\|_{-1}^{2}+\frac{a_{0}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\|\overline{\varphi}(s)\|_{-1}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Hence, applying Gronwall’s lemma, we eventually conclude

\displaystyle\|\overline{\varphi}(t)\|_{-1}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$}.

This directly implies
\overline{\varphi}=0
a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
. As a consequence, the right-hand side of (
4.1
) vanishes and hence, we also have
\overline{\mu}=0
a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
.

This proves that
(\varphi,\mu)
is the unique weak solution and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

5  Regularity theory for a class of quasilinear elliptic PDEs

In this section, we present regularity results for a class of quasilinear elliptic PDEs. They will be applied in the proof of Theorem
2.8
in order to improve the spatial regularity of the phase-field component of a weak solution to the anisotropic Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
).

We make the following assumptions.

(\mathbf{R1})

The set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Lipschitz boundary and outer unit normal vector field
\mathbf{n}
.

(\mathbf{R2})

The function
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\mapsto a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
is

(\mathbf{R2.1})

Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbf{p}
, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{L}\geq 0
such that

\big|a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})-a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{q})\big|\leq C_{L}\left|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right|

for all
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\mathbf{R2.2})

strongly monotone in
\mathbf{p}
, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{M}>0
such that

\big(a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})-a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{q})\big)\cdot(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\geq C_{M}\left|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}

for all
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\mathbf{R2.3})

quasi-Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbf{x}
, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{Q}\geq 0
such that

\big|a(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})-a(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{p})\big|\leq C_{Q}\big(\left|\mathbf{p}\right|+1\big)\left|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right|

for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(\mathbf{R3})

The function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
belongs to
L^{2}(U)
.

We now establish a regularity theory for weak solutions of the following quasi-linear elliptic PDE:

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot a(\cdot,\nabla u)

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $U$},

(5.1a)

\displaystyle a(\cdot,\nabla u)\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\partial U$}.

(5.1b)

A weak solution of this boundary value problem is defined as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Under the assumptions (

R1

)–(

R3

), a function
u\in H^{1}(U)
is called a weak solution of the boundary value problem (
5.1
)
if
u
satisfies the weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{U}a(\cdot,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{U}fv\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $v\in H^{1}(U)$}.

(5.2)

To prove additional regularity of such weak solutions, we first recall the notion of difference quotients.
Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any locally integrable function
w\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
and any open subset
V\subset\joinrel\subset U
, for
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, the
k
-th difference quotient of step size
h
is defined by

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}^{h}w(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{w(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{e}_{k})-w(\mathbf{x})}{h}

for
\mathbf{x}\in V
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<|h|<\textnormal{dist\,}(V,\partial U)
. Here,
\mathbf{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the
k
-th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For vector-valued functions the difference quotient is to be understood componentwise.

5.1  Regularity in the interior

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

R1

)–(

R3

) are fulfilled and let
u\in H^{1}(U)
be a weak solution of (
5.1
) in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(U),

and for every open subset
V\subset\joinrel\subset U
, there exists a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
U
,
V
and
a
such that the estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{2}(V)}\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(U)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(U)}+1\big)

(5.3)

holds.

To prove Theorem
5.2
, we recall the following properties of difference quotients.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
V\subset\joinrel\subset U
be any open subset. Then, it holds:

(a)

If
w_{1},w_{2}\in L^{2}(U)
are functions with
\textnormal{supp\,}w_{i}\subset V
for
i=1
or
i=2
, then the identity

\displaystyle\int_{U}w_{1}\nabla_{k}^{-h}w_{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\int_{U}\nabla_{k}^{h}w_{1}w_{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

holds for all
0<\left|h\right|<\textnormal{dist\,}(V,\partial U)
and all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

(b)

Suppose
w\in H^{1}(U)
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then, for every open set
U^{\prime}\subset\joinrel\subset U
, the difference quotient
\nabla_{k}^{h}w
belongs to
L^{2}(U^{\prime})
for all
0<\left|h\right|<\textnormal{dist\,}(U^{\prime},\partial U)
, and it satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{k}^{h}w\big\|_{L^{2}(U^{\prime})}\leq\|\partial_{k}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}.

(c)

Let
w\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a locally integrable function with
w\in L^{2}(V)
and let
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be arbitrary. Moreover, assume that there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that
\nabla_{k}^{h}w\in L^{2}(V)
and
\|\nabla_{k}^{h}w\|_{L^{2}(V)}\leq C
for all
0<\left|h\right|<\textnormal{dist\,}(V,\partial U)
. Then, the weak partial derivative
\partial_{k}w
exists and it holds

\displaystyle\partial_{k}w\in L^{2}(V)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\|\partial_{k}w\|_{L^{2}(V)}\leq C.

The result
(a)
follows directly by a change of variables. A proof of
(b)
can be found, e.g., in
[
20
, Lemma 7.23]
. Moreover,
(c)
can be established as in
[
12
, §5.8.2 Theorem 3]
.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of Theorem
5.2
.

The idea of this proof is to use Lemma
5.3
(c)
and therefore, to bound every difference quotient of
\nabla u

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{V}a_{l}(\cdot,\nabla u)\partial_{k}v\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg|\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(W)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(W)}+1\big)\|v\|_{L^{2}(V)}.

(5.14)

Using this estimate as well as the weak formulation (

), we infer

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{V}a_{n}(\cdot,\nabla u)\partial_{n}v\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{V}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}(\cdot,\nabla u)\partial_{k}v-fv\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(W)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(W)}+1\big)\|v\|_{L^{2}(V)}.

(5.15)

We now fix an arbitrary

, and we define the linear functional

\displaystyle T_{k}:C_{c}^{\infty}(V)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptT_{k}(v):=\int_{V}a_{n}(\cdot,\nabla u)\partial_{k}v\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

By the estimates (
5.14
) and (
5.2
), we see that for all functions

, it holds

\displaystyle|T_{k}(v)|\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(W)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(W)}+1\big)\|v\|_{L^{2}(V)}.

(5.16)

In the following, since we intend to obtain this estimate also for all functions in
L^{2}(V)
, let

be arbitrary. We proceed as in
[
2
, Theorem 4.15(3)]
to approximate

as follows. For any function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\textnormal{supp\,}\varphi\subset B_{1}(0)
,
\varphi\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=1
, we construct a standard Dirac sequence
(\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by defining
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=i^{n}\varphi(i\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every

, we further introduce the set

V^{(i)}:=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in V\,|\,\textnormal{dist\,}(\mathbf{x},\partial V)>\tfrac{1}{i}\big\}\cap B_{i}(\mathbf{0}).

It clearly holds
v_{i}:=\big(\mathds{1}_{V^{(i)}}v\big)\ast\varphi_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(V)
for all

and
according to
[
2
, Theorem 4.15]
, we also have

\displaystyle v_{i}\to v\text{ in }L^{2}(V)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as}\hskip 10.00002pti\to\infty.

Due to (
5.16
) and (
5.17

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(W)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(W)}+1\big)\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|_{L^{2}(V)}\to 0

Let

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt+\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+1\big).

. For any

and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we further define

\displaystyle\eta_{k}:=\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if $q>2$},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\eta_{k}:=F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if $q=2$}.

Hence, we have
\eta_{k}(t)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
and the gradient is given by

\displaystyle\nabla\eta_{k}:=(q-1)\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\varphi_{k}.

Let now
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
and
q\geq 2
be arbitrary.
Testing the weak formulation (
2.1b
) or (
2.9b
), respectively, with
\eta_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle(q-1)\int_{\Omega}A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}\,\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi_{k})\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\int_{\Omega}F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi)\,F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\,\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}\mu\,F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\,\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Omega}F_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi)\,F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\,\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(6.22)

a.e. in
[0,T]
. By the definition of
\varphi_{k}
and the monotonicity of
A^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}=A^{\prime}(\nabla\varphi_{k})\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}\geq a_{0}\left|\nabla\varphi_{k}\right|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\Omega_{T}$}.

As we further know
F_{1}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
(since
F_{1}
is convex), we infer that the first summand on the left-hand side of (
6
) is non-negative. Since
k\geq 2
and
F_{1}^{\prime}
is monotonically increasing, we further have

\displaystyle F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})^{2}\leq F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi)\,F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\Omega_{T}$},

which can be used to estimate the second summand on the left-hand side of (
6
) from below. In view of (
6
) and the definition of
F_{2}
, we thus deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}\big(\left|\mu\right|+c_{*}\left|\varphi\right|\big)\,\big|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\big|^{q-1}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(6.23)

for some constant
c_{*}>0
depending only on
F
.
By means of Hölder’s inequality, we infer

\displaystyle\|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\leq\big(\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+c_{*}\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\big)\,\|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q-1}

(6.24)

a.e. in
[0,T]
.
This directly implies

\displaystyle\|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\leq(1+\left|\Omega\right|)\big(\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+c_{*}\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big)\,\|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q-1}

(6.25)

a.e. in
[0,T]
.
Since
\varphi,\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
and
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists a null set
\mathcal{N}\subset[0,T]
such that for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
,

\displaystyle c(t)

\displaystyle:=(1+\left|\Omega\right|)\big(\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+c_{*}\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{:}\leq C\big(\|\mu(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+c_{*}\|\varphi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\big)<\infty.

(6.26)

Using this estimate to bound the right-hand side of (
6.25
), we conclude

\displaystyle\big\|F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq c(t)<\infty

(6.27)

for all
q\geq 4
and all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
, after possibly replacing
\mathcal{N}
by a larger null set. As
c(t)
is independent of
q
, this directly entails

\displaystyle\big\|F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c(t)<\infty

(6.28)

for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
.

As for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k}(t))
is measurable and non-negative, we apply Fatou’s lemma to derive the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\;\big|F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\;\int_{\Omega}\big|F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\left|\Omega\right|c(t).

(6.29)

Moreover, it is straightforward to check that
\varphi_{k}(t)\to\varphi(t)
a.e. in
\Omega
for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
, after possibly replacing
\mathcal{N}
by a larger null set.

Let now
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
be arbitrary.
Since
\left|\varphi_{k}(t)\right|<1
a.e. in
\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, this already implies that
\varphi(t)\in[-1,1]
a.e. in
\Omega
.
However, if
\left|\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\right|=1
for some
x\in\Omega
, we have

\left|\varphi_{k}(t,\mathbf{x})\right|\to 1
and thus
\left|F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k}(t,\mathbf{x}))\right|\to+\infty
as
k\to\infty
. We thus conclude that the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}:=\big\{x\in\Omega\,\big|\,\left|\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\right|=1\big\}

has Lebesgue measure zero as otherwise, this would contradict (
6.29
). Consequently, we have
\varphi(t)\in(-1,1)
a.e. in
\Omega
and

\displaystyle F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)\to F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi(t)\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\Omega$ as $k\to\infty$}.

(6.30)

In combination with (
6.28
), this proves

\displaystyle\big\|F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c(t)<\infty

(6.31)

for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
. We now recall (
6
) as well as the regularities
\varphi,\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
. Taking the square on both sides of (
6.31
) and integrating with respect to time from
0
to
T
, we eventually conclude
F_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
. As a direct consequence, we have
F^{\prime}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
.

Let us now consider the case where
F
is a regular potential. After fixing representatives
\varphi
and
\mu
of their equivalence class, we introduce the truncation

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{k}(s):=\begin{cases}k&\text{if $s\geq k$},\\
s&\text{if $\left|s\right|<k$},\\
-k&\text{if $s\leq-k$},\end{cases}

and we set
\varphi_{k}:=\sigma_{k}\circ\varphi
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Proceeding exactly as for the logarithmic potential, we show that
F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi_{k}(t)\big)
satisfies (
6.28
) for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}\subset[0,T]
is some null set and
c(t)
is the constant defined in (
6
). As the regular potential is assumed to fulfill (

F3

is continuous. Hence, since
\varphi_{k}(t)\to\varphi(t)
a.e. in
\Omega
for all

, we deduce the convergence (
6.30
). This entails (

) and by proceeding as above, we conclude the claim.

Step 5:
From now on, let
F
be the logarithmic potential.
We now show that the strict separation property (
2.15
) holds for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

As a consequence of (
6.31
), there exist null sets
\mathcal{N}\subset[0,T]
and
\mathcal{O}\subset\Omega
such that for all
(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}\setminus(\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{O})
,
it holds
\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\in(-1,1)
and

\displaystyle\big|\ln\big(1+\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\big)-\ln\big(1-\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\big)\big|=\Big|\frac{2}{\theta}\,F_{1}^{\prime}\big(\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\big)\Big|\leq\frac{2c(t)}{\theta}.

(6.32)

Let now
(t,\mathbf{x})\in\Omega_{T}\setminus(\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{O})
be arbitrary.

If
\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,1)
, we have
\ln\big(1+\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\big)\geq 0
and

. Hence,

\displaystyle-\ln\big(1-\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\big)\leq\frac{2c(t)}{\theta}.

By a straightforward computation, we thus infer

\displaystyle 0\leq\varphi(t,\mathbf{x})\leq 1-\delta(t),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\delta(t):=\exp\left(-\frac{2c(t)}{\theta}\right)\in(0,1].

If

.

In summary, we have thus shown that

\displaystyle\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1-\delta(t)

for all

. This verifies the strict separation property (
2.15
) for almost all

Recalling the definition of
