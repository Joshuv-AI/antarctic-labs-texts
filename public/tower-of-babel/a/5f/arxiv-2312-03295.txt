Abstract
This research explores neural network-based numerical approximation of two-dimensional convection-dominated singularly perturbed problems on square, circular, and elliptic domains. Singularly perturbed boundary value problems pose significant challenges due to sharp boundary layers in their solutions. Additionally, the characteristic points of these domains give rise to degenerate boundary layer problems. The stiffness of these problems, caused by sharp singular layers, can lead to substantial computational errors if not properly addressed. Conventional neural network-based approaches often fail to capture these sharp transitions accurately, highlighting a critical flaw in machine learning methods. To address these issues, we conduct a thorough boundary layer analysis to enhance our understanding of sharp transitions within the boundary layers, guiding the application of numerical methods. Specifically, we employ physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to better handle these boundary layer problems. However, PINNs may struggle with rapidly varying singularly perturbed solutions in small domain regions, leading to inaccurate or unstable results. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a semi-analytic method that augments PINNs with singular layers or corrector functions. Our numerical experiments demonstrate significant improvements in both accuracy and stability, showcasing the effectiveness of our proposed approach.

Our approach can derive explicit boundary layer profile functions that are theoretically guaranteed, which are then essentially used in our new PINN scheme. Building on techniques from boundary layer analysis, our work addresses multi-dimensional problems with complex domains.

A singularly perturbed boundary value problem, such as the one presented in equations (
1.1
), is widely recognized for inducing a thin layer near the boundary, commonly referred to as a boundary layer. Within this boundary layer, the solution undergoes a rapid transition. Extensive scholarly research has been conducted on the mathematical theory of singular perturbations and boundary layers. The numerical approximation of singular perturbation problems often faces significant computational errors near the boundary due to the stiffness of the solution within the boundary layer. To achieve accurate approximations, traditional methods usually require substantial mesh refinement near the boundary. However, instead of relying on extensive mesh refinements, incorporating a global basis function known as the corrector has proven highly effective. The corrector captures the singular behavior of the solution within the boundary layers, offering a more efficient and accurate approach to solving these problems. In this paper, we deliver the challenge posed by boundary layers by performing a boundary layer analysis for each singular perturbation problem. In particular, we provide a complete singular perturbation analysis for the elliptical domain case, which constitutes a new analytical result. Through this analysis, we identify the corrector function, which accurately captures the singular behavior within the boundary layer. Subsequently, we develop a new semi-analytic approach by constructing neural networks enriched with the corrector function, embedded within the structure of a two-layer PINN with hard constraints. We refer to this approach as a
singular layer PINN (SL-PINN)
. To validate the effectiveness of our proposed method, we conduct numerical simulations for each example presented. The results confirm that our SL-PINNs naturally capture the singular behavior of boundary layers and provide highly accurate approximations for the singularly perturbed boundary value problems discussed in this article. This paper primarily focuses on studying the two-dimensional convection-diffusion equations in various domains, such as a unit square, circle, and ellipse, denoted by
\Omega

\begin{split}L_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}:=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}&=f,\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\varepsilon}&=0,\quad\text{ at }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

(1.1)

The objective of this study is to develop semi-analytical neural networks that can effectively incorporate physics-based information and improve their performance by integrating corrector functions. Our approach to constructing a simple two-layer neural network shares similarities with PINNs but emphasizes the inclusion of hard constraints to enforce boundary conditions. We utilize a straightforward neural network,
\hat{u}

to satisfy the boundary condition as follows:

\overline{u}({x,y};\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=g({x,y})\,\hat{u}({{{x,y}}};\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(1.2)

We define a two-layer neural network

\hat{u}({{{x,y}}};\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}\sigma(w_{1j}x+w_{2j}y+b_{j}),

(1.3)

The network parameters are denoted by

The PINNs with hard constraints described in (

Our approach employs a two-layer neural network, which is not only computationally efficient but also sufficient for achieving accurate numerical approximations. Additionally, by incorporating symbolic computation, our methodology can be conveniently extended to an

2.1
Boundary Layer Analysis

\begin{split}-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}&=f,\quad\text{ in }\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1),\\

\begin{split}\varphi^{0}_{\bar{y}\bar{y}}+\varphi^{0}_{\bar{y}}&=0\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\varphi^{0}&=-u^{0}(x,0)\text{ at }\bar{y}=0,\\
\varphi^{0}&\text{ is periodic in }x.\end{split}

(2.3)

Circular Domain

In this section, we investigate the boundary layer behavior of the singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problem (
1.1
) in a circular domain, as well as in time-dependent and nonlinear cases. As
\varepsilon
becomes small, the solution to these equations exhibits rapid transitions near the boundary
\Gamma_{-}:=\{(x,y)\mid x^{2}+y^{2}=1,y\leq 0\}

(\pm 1,0)
where the upper semi-circle and lower semi-circle meet. To address these issues, we introduce a compatibility condition and employ a corrector through boundary layer analysis to validate our neural network-based approach. We present a series of numerical simulations as numerical evidence, demonstrating the robustness of our method even in non-compatible cases.

3.1
Boundary Layer Analysis

We shift our focus to the convection-diffusion equation within a circular domain. We start by examining the time-independent problem and then extend our study to the time-dependent equations.
We consider the convection-diffusion equation in a circular domain such that

\begin{split}L_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}{:=}-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}&=f,\quad\text{ in }\Omega=\{(x,y)|x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}\\
u^{\varepsilon}&=0,\quad\text{ at }\partial\Omega.\end{split}

(3.1)

We begin by introducing the expansion
{u^{\varepsilon}\approx}u^{0}+\varphi^{0}
, where
u^{0}
represents the outer expansion for the smooth part, and
\varphi^{0}
represents the inner expansion that captures the boundary layer.
To derive the corrector function from (
3.1

\varepsilon
to

\begin{split}L_{0}u^{0}:=-u^{0}_{y}&=f\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{0}&=0\text{ at }\Gamma_{+},\end{split}

(3.2)

where
\Gamma_{+}:=\{(x,y)|x^{2}+y^{2}=1,y>0\}
is the upper semi-circle.
Note that the boundary condition in (
3.2

\Gamma_{-}:=\{(x,y)|x^{2}+y^{2}=1,y\leq 0\}

The singularly perturbed problem described by equation (
3.1

[

]

To develop the boundary layer analysis, we use boundary-fitted coordinates defined by

and

0\leq\eta<1

\tau\in[0,2\pi]

\eta
represents the normal component and
\tau
represents the tangential component.
By setting
u^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=v^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\tau)
, we derive that

\begin{split}P_{\varepsilon}v^{\varepsilon}&:=\frac{1}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\left(-\varepsilon\left(v^{\varepsilon}_{\tau\tau}-(1-\eta)v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}+(1-\eta)^{2}v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta\eta}\right)+(1-\eta)^{2}\sin\tau v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}-(1-\eta)\cos\tau v^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}\right)\\
&=f,\quad\text{in }D,\\
v^{\varepsilon}(0,\tau)&=0,\quad\text{at }0\leq\tau\leq 2\pi,\end{split}

(3.3)

where
D:=\{(\eta,\tau)|0<\eta<1,0\leq\tau\leq 2\pi\}
.
Performing the boundary layer analysis as in
[
14
]
, we are led to the following equation for the corrector equation

\begin{split}-\varepsilon\varphi^{0}_{\eta\eta}+(\sin\tau)\varphi^{0}_{\eta}&=0,\quad\text{ for }0<\eta<1,\quad\pi<\tau<2\pi,\\
\varphi^{0}&=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau),\quad{\text{at }\eta=0},\\
\varphi^{0}&\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }\eta\rightarrow 1.\end{split}

(3.4)

Hence, we can find an explicit solution of the correction equation (
3.4

\varphi^{0}=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau),

(3.5)

where

To match the boundary condition in our numerical scheme, we introduce a cut-off function to derive an approximate form

\bar{\varphi}^{0}=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)\delta(\eta),

(3.6)

where
\delta(\eta)
is a smooth cut-off function such that

\eta\in[0,1/2]

3.2
Numerical Simulations for Time-indepedent problem

We now establish the semi-analytic SL-PINN method as

\widetilde{v}(\eta,\tau;\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=(\hat{v}(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}){\hat{\varphi}^{0}})C(\eta,\tau),

where
\hat{\varphi}^{0}
is given by

\hat{\varphi}^{0}=\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)\delta(\eta),

and

\begin{split}C(\eta,\tau)=\begin{cases}1-(1-\eta)^{3},\text{ if }0<\tau<\pi,\\

and
\hat{v}(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\hat{u}(x,y,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

&\quad+\left(3\varepsilon+\frac{6\varepsilon}{(1-\eta)}+6\varepsilon(1-\eta)\sin(\tau)+6(1-\eta)^{2}\sin(\tau)\right)\hat{v}(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\\
&\quad-\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\Big[\varepsilon\left(C(\eta,\tau)\delta_{\eta\eta}-\left((1-\eta)C(\eta,\tau)-2C_{\eta}(\eta,\tau)\right)\delta_{\eta}\right.\quad\left.-\left((1-\eta)C_{\eta}(\eta,\tau)-C_{\eta\eta}(\eta,\tau)\right)\delta\right)\\
&\quad-C(\eta,\tau)\sin(\tau)\delta_{\eta}+C_{\eta}(\eta,\tau)\sin(\tau)\delta\Big]\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{\delta}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\Big[(\varepsilon\hat{v}_{\tau\tau}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+1+\eta)\cos(\tau)\hat{v}_{\tau}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\eta\sin(\tau)\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))C(\eta,\tau)\\
&\quad+\left(2\varepsilon\hat{v}_{\tau}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+(1+\eta))\cos(\tau)\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)C_{\tau}(\eta,\tau)+\varepsilon\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})C_{\tau\tau}(\eta,\tau)\Big]\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\\
&\quad+C(\eta,\tau)\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\frac{\delta(\eta)}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\left(\frac{\eta\cos^{2}(\tau)}{\varepsilon}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)-f,\end{split}

(3.14)

where
0\leq\eta<1
and
\pi\leq\tau\leq 2\pi

To make the computation in (

) feasible, we extract the largest order term in
\varepsilon
, which includes
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
such that

\psi(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=(1-(1-\eta)^{3}-(((1-\eta)\sin\tau)^{3})\hat{v}(0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\frac{\delta(\eta)}{(1-\eta)^{2}}\left(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\cos^{2}\tau\right)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right){\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)}.

(3.15)

When

) becomes, by the triangular inequality,

(3.16)

The rightmost term does not include a large order term such as
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
(where
\alpha<0

\psi
contains large terms like
\varepsilon^{-1}
. Hence, this part can be computed using conventional

or

loss.
Assume that
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is fixed and that we select a sufficiently large number of sampling points
N
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left|P_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{v}(\eta_{i},\tau_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi(\eta_{i},\tau_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})-f\right|\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\left|P_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{v}(\eta,\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi(\eta,\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta})-f\right|\,d\eta\,d\tau<\infty.

(3.17)

Given that
\psi
includes
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})

loss with the bound:

\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right){\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)}\right|_{L^{1}(D)}<C,

where

N
such that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}|\psi(\eta_{i},\tau_{i},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{\pi}\int^{1}_{0}|\psi(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|d\eta d\tau<C.\end{split}

(3.18)

Hence, we obtain that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{\pi}\int^{1}_{0}|\psi(\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|d\eta d\tau&<C+C\varepsilon e^{-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}}.\end{split}

(3.19)

Therefore, the loss (
3.12
) becomes nearly constant due to (
3.19
) and (
3.17
) when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. This simplification makes our computations feasible.

3.3
Numerical Simulations for Time-depedent problem

We extend our approach to a time dependent problem:

\begin{split}L_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}:u^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}&=f,\quad\text{ for }(x,y)\in\Omega,\quad t\in(0,T),\\
u^{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)&=0,\quad\text{ for }(x,y)\in\partial\Omega,\quad t\in(0,T),\\
u^{\varepsilon}(x,y,0)&=0,\quad\text{ for }(x,y)\in\Omega,\quad\text{ at }t=0.\\
\end{split}

(3.21)

The analysis for time-dependent problems is similar to that for time-independent problems. For more detailed theoretical results, refer to the work by
[
32
]
.
To facilitate the development of the boundary layer analysis, we employ boundary-fitted coordinates expressed as
x=(1-\eta)\cos\tau
and
y=(1-\eta)\sin\tau
, as in the time-independent case.
By introducing the transformation
u^{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)=v^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\tau,t)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon}v^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\ v^{\varepsilon}_{t}+\frac{1}{(1-\eta)^{2}}(-\varepsilon(v^{\varepsilon}_{\tau\tau}-(1-\eta)v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}+(1-\eta)^{2}v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta\eta})

(3.22)

\displaystyle+(1-\eta)^{2}(\sin\tau)v^{\varepsilon}_{\eta}-(1-\eta)(\cos\tau)v^{\varepsilon}_{\tau})

\displaystyle=f,\quad\text{in }(\eta,\tau)\in D,\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle v^{\varepsilon}(0,\tau,t)

\displaystyle=\ 0,\quad\text{at }0\leq\tau\leq 2\pi,\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle v^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\tau,0)

\displaystyle=\ 0,\quad\text{in }(\eta,\tau)\in D.

As stated in the boundary layer analysis conducted by
[
15
]
, the sharp transition occurs solely in the spatial direction. Consequently, the solution does not exhibit a boundary layer in the temporal direction. Therefore, the time-independent problem yields the same form of the corrector equation as in the case without time dependence. Thus, we can derive the corrector equation as described by
[
15
]
:
For
t>0
,

\begin{split}-\varepsilon\varphi^{0}_{\eta\eta}+(\sin\tau)\varphi^{0}_{\eta}&=0,\quad{\text{ for }0<\eta<1,\quad\pi<\tau<2\pi,}\\
\varphi^{0}&={-u^{0}(t,\cos\tau,\sin\tau)},\quad{\text{ at }\eta=0},\\
\varphi^{0}&\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }\eta\rightarrow 1.\end{split}

(3.23)

Thus, we can find an explicit solution to the corrector equation:

\varphi^{0}=-u^{0}(t,\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau),

(3.24)

where
\chi
stands for the characteristic function.
For our numerical scheme, we introduce an approximate form,

\bar{\varphi}^{0}=-u^{0}(t,\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)\delta(\eta),

(3.25)

where
\delta(\eta)
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\delta(\eta)=1
for
\eta\in[0,1/2]
and
=0
for
\eta\in[3/4,1]
.

We establish the
SL-PINN
based on boundary layer analysis, incorporating the profile of the corrector such that

\widetilde{v}(t,\eta,\tau;\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=(e^{t}-1)\left(\hat{v}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\hat{v}(t,0,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}){\hat{\varphi}^{0}(\eta,\tau)}\right)C(\eta,\tau),

(3.26)

where
\hat{\varphi}^{0}
is given by

\hat{\varphi}^{0}=\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)\delta(\eta),

(3.27)

and

\begin{split}C(\eta,\tau)=\begin{cases}1-(1-\eta)^{3},\text{ if }0\leq\tau\leq\pi\\
1-(1-\eta)^{3}-((1-\eta)\sin\tau)^{3},\text{ if }\pi<\tau<2\pi,\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.28)

and
\hat{v}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\hat{u}(t,x,y,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=W_{4}{\sigma}(W_{1}x+W_{2}y+W_{3}t+b)
.
Then, the residual loss is defined by

\begin{split}Loss=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}|P_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{v}((t_{i},\eta_{i},\tau_{i},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))-f|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\quad\text{ for }(t_{i},\eta_{i},\tau_{i})\in[0,T]\times D,\end{split}

(3.29)

where
p=1,2
.
Due to the boundary layer behavior near the lower semi-circle, we split the residual loss (
3.29
) into two sections:
0<\tau<\pi
and
\pi\leq\tau\leq 2\pi
. For the
0<\tau<\pi

\begin{split}&P_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{v}((t,1-\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))-f\\
&=\left(e^{t}\hat{v}+(e^{t}-1)\hat{v}_{t}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)(1-(1-\eta)^{3})\\
&\quad+(e^{t}-1)\left(-\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{(1-\eta)^{2}}-(1-\eta)\right)\hat{v}_{\tau\tau}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\left((1-\eta)^{2}-\frac{1}{(1-\eta)}\right)\cos(\tau)\hat{v}_{\tau}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right.\\
&\quad-\varepsilon(1-(1-\eta)^{3})\hat{v}_{\eta\eta}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\left(6\varepsilon+\varepsilon(1-\eta)^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\eta)}-\sin(\tau)(1-(1-\eta)^{3})\right)\hat{v}_{\eta}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\\
&\quad+\left.\left(3\varepsilon+\frac{6\varepsilon}{(1-\eta)}+3(1-\eta)^{2}\sin(\tau)\right)\hat{v}(t,\eta,\tau,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)-f\end{split}

(3.30)

for
0\leq\eta<1,\quad 0<\tau<\pi
and
\quad 0\leq t\leq T
.
Introducing the boundary layer element in equation (
3.26

ranges from
\pi

2\pi

(3.31)

where
0\leq\eta<1

\begin{split}L_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}{:=}-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}+(u^{\varepsilon})^{3}&=f,\quad\text{ in }\Omega=\{(x,y)|x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}\\

(3.39)

As in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, we employ boundary-fitted coordinates
x=(1-\eta)\cos\tau
and

(3.40)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\varphi^{0}_{\eta\eta}+(\sin\tau)\varphi^{0}_{\eta}

\displaystyle=0,\quad{\text{ for }0<\eta<1,\quad\pi<\tau<2\pi,}

(3.41)

\displaystyle\varphi^{0}

\displaystyle=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau),\quad{\text{ at }\eta=0},

\displaystyle\varphi^{0}

\displaystyle\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }\eta\rightarrow 1.

The explicit solution of (
3.41
) is found as

\varphi^{0}=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau),

(3.42)

where
\chi
stands for the characteristic function.
To match the boundary condition in our numerical scheme, we introduce a cut-off function to derive an approximate form

\bar{\varphi}^{0}=-u^{0}(\cos\tau,\sin\tau)\exp\left(\frac{\sin\tau}{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\chi_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\tau)\delta(\eta),

(3.43)

where
\delta(\eta)
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\delta(\eta)=1
for

In this section, we investigate the boundary layer behavior of the singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problem (

4.1
Boundary Layer Analysis

We consider the convection-diffusion equations on an elliptical domain, which represents a natural yet non-trivial extension of the problem:

\begin{split}L_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}:=-\varepsilon\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}&=f,\quad\text{ in }\Omega=\left\{(x,y)|{\frac{x^{2}}{A^{2}}}+\frac{y^{2}}{B^{2}}<1\right\}\\

(4.1)

Within the elliptical domain, we utilize the elliptic coordinate system

A>B

We start by introducing the expansion

\begin{split}L_{0}u^{0}:=-u^{0}_{y}&=f\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\

(4.2)

5
Conclusion

In this study, we have introduced a semi-analytic approach to enhance the numerical performance of PINNs in addressing a range of singularly perturbed boundary value problems and convection-dominated equations on rectangular, circular, and elliptical domains. For each singular perturbation problem examined, we derived an analytic approximation known as the corrector function, which captures the behavior of the fast (stiff) component of the solution within the boundary layer. Additionally, we conducted a detailed boundary layer analysis to better understand the dynamics within these regions.
By integrating these corrector functions into a 2-layer PINN framework with hard constraints, we successfully addressed the stiffness inherent in the approximate solutions, resulting in our novel semi-analytic SL-PINNs enriched with the correctors. Through the incorporation of these corrector functions, we effectively overcame the stiffness challenge associated with approximate solutions, leading to the development of our improved SL-PINNs.

For future work, we aim to extend our approach to more complex geometries, which present significant challenges. Addressing these complexities may require a greater reliance on machine learning predictions to accurately capture the intricate boundary layer dynamics. Additionally, we plan to apply our semi-analytic SL-PINN methodology to Burgers’ equations in one and two dimensions. This extension will allow us to further test and refine our approach, demonstrating its versatility and robustness in handling a broader range of nonlinear convection-diffusion problems.
