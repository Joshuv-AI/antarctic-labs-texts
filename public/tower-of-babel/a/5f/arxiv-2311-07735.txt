Assessing the potential impact of environmental land management schemes on emergent infection disease risks
Christopher J. Banks, Katherine Simpson, Nicholas Hanley, Rowland R. Kao

Abstract
Financial incentives are provided by governments to encourage the plantation of new woodland to increase habitat, biodiversity, carbon sequestration, and other economic benefits for landowners. Whilst these are largely positive effects, it is worth considering that greater biodiversity and presence of wildlife species in proximity to agricultural holdings may pose a risk of disease transmission between wildlife and livestock. Wildlife transmission and the provision of a reservoir for infectious disease is particularly important in the transmission dynamics of bovine tuberculosis. In this paper we develop an economic model for changing land use due to forestry subsidies. We use this asses the impact on wild deer populations in the newly created woodland areas and the emergent infectious disease risk arising from the proximity of new and existing wild deer populations and existing cattle holdings. We consider an area in the South-West of Scotland, having existing woodland, deer populations, and extensive and diverse cattle farm holdings. In this area we find that, with a varying level of subsidy and plausible new woodland creation, the contact risk between areas of wild deer and cattle increases between 26% and 35% over the contact risk present with zero subsidy. This model provides a foundation for extending to larger regions and for examining potential risk mitigation strategies, for example the targeting of subsidy in low risk areas or provisioning for buffer zones between woodland and agricultural holdings.



1
Introduction

Economic incentives are used to encourage private landholders to undertake costly actions that benefit biodiversity
[
1
]
. These schemes, such as the Scottish Government’s Forestry Grant Scheme
[
2
]
, encourage landholders to change their land management to encourage the plantation of new woodland. This has a positive benefit for the creation of new habitat, increasing biodiversity, and improving carbon sequestration, as well as economic benefits for the land owner. However, one important consideration is that the resultant increase in both the density and variety of wildlife will change the patterns of their contact with livestock and humans, potentially increasing disease risks to both. In particular, plantation subsidy provided to agricultural land owners potentially brings woodland diversity closer to agricultural holdings. There is increasing concern that changes in land use to respond to climate and biodiversity crises could lead to emergent infectious disease problems.

Emerging infectious disease risks present both an economic burden to livestock keepers and are a potential threat to human and animal health and welfare. As the threat of infectious diseases may in turn influence the choices that land managers will make in the future and the determination of the socially-optimal pattern of land use, understanding how land management changes changes those contact patterns may have implications for both disease and land management. It is a critical step in quantifying the changes in those disease risks, and tracking them over time.

One source of disease concern in many countries around the world is the impact of wild deer, with recent examples that under appropriate conditions, deer species have proven capable hosts for the emergence of Chronic Wasting Disease
[
3
]
, contributing to the maintenance of bovine Tuberculosis
[
4
,
5
]
, and spreading of SARS-CoV-2
[
6
]
. As deer numbers and density are likely to increase in northern countries with the increase in forests due to climate change policies and biodiversity target-setting, their potential role to contribute to disease threats may be considerable.

In this analysis, we concentrate on the problem as it relates to cattle-deer interactions, as this is a concern for bovine tuberculosis maintenance due to increased potential for transmission between wild deer and cattle. A critical question is that, while increases in woodland planting may increase the density of deer, it is the geographical distribution of those plantings which determines the extent to which deer and cattle are likely to increase contact as well, and this is the focus of our analysis. We shall build upon a prior work
[
7
,
8
]
evaluating land cover changes under agri-environment scheme incentives to encourage woodland planting, to characterise potential land use change under various levels of incentives. We then use previous estimates of deer density dependence on land cover usage to assess the likely distribution of deer in relation to existing and new woodland plantation. We then consider the deer populations in relation to the location of cattle, and how contact between deer and cattle are likely to change with new plantation. Thus we identify what is the likely impact of subsidy for new plantation on new infection risk, using the adjacency of deer and cattle populations as an indicator of inter-species disease transmission risk.

Methods and data

To implement a model, data is needed on livestock density and current distribution, deer densities and distributions, and economic returns from alternative land uses.

2.2
Model

2.2.1
Land use change

In order to model the potential for land use change due to woodland subsidy provision, the above data are first aggregated into a single dataset. For the set of land parcels in the study area we record the current land use, cattle density, and deer density. Land use types are aggregated into one of: woodland, pasture, arable, or other. Pasture is assigned a cattle density and woodland is assigned a deer density. Pasture and arable land are assigned current gross margin values, according to crop type or the presence of cattle. Figure
2
(left) shows the initial land types assigned in the model.

An agent-based model, based on Simpson et al.
[
8
]
, underlies the land use allocation process. Land use change is decided based upon whether the level of the economic incentive offered for woodland planting is greater than the current agricultural gross margin of the land parcel (or the landholders opportunity cost). For land parcels of pasture or arable type if the offered subsidy is greater than landholders opportunity cost then the expectation is the landholder will sign up to the scheme and plant new woodland on the enrolled land parcel. We model this process as occurring instantaneously.

To assess the sensitivity of land use change to subsidy, we model land manager choices across a range of subsidy levels. Subsidy levels from zero up to double the original subsidy level, in eleven steps, were considered initially. In order to define broader classes of subsidy level for comparison, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 multipliers were used to correspond to low, mid, and high subsidy (where mid is the original fixed subsidy level).

2.2.2
Deer distribution

For land parcels with pre-existing woodland, deer populations remain the same after land use change. We then distribute new deer populations to any land parcels with newly-created woodland. New deer populations are distributed in the model according to an approximation of the pre-existing distribution of deer in deciduous woodland land parcels. Figure
1
shows the distribution of the existing deer population in deciduous woodland areas compared to a Gamma distribution with shape parameter 0.69. Deer population in new woodland land parcels is distributed by drawing from that distribution.

Figure 1:
Distribution of the existing deer population per deciduous woodland land parcel fit to a Gamma distribution with shape parameter 0.69

2.2.3
Animal adjacency networks

We assess the risk model for disease transmission between wild (deer) and agricultural (cattle) species, and within species, by considering changes in the contact patterns amongst and between land parcels holding these two species, under the assumption that this network of contacts is important for establishing the risk of spread. We thus construct a two-layer network. In both networks the vertices are land parcels in the study area. In the first network (all-species network) edges exist between all spatially adjacent land parcels that contain either cattle or deer with a population estimate greater than 0.5. In the second network (inter-species network) edges exist only between pairs of spatially adjacent land parcels where one contains cattle and the other contains a deer population estimate greater than 0.5. The all-species network represents a mapping of all land parcels that contain animals that can potentially interact and affect disease transmission; whereas the inter-species network represents a mapping of the land parcels where there is an interface between species affecting only inter-species disease transmission.

4
Discussion

In this paper we have quantified the effect of subsidy for new plantation of woodland on the risk of disease transmission between cattle and wild deer populations, in terms of the changes in the between-land parcel connections amongst cattle and deer. Our model accounts for potential land use change by adopting an agent-based model of land management decision-making in relation to the profitability of agricultural land types and the subsidy offered for their transformation into new woodland. We then model a plausible distribution of wild deer in new woodland areas and their relation to existing cattle holdings. The adjacency relationship between deer and cattle populations provides us with an estimate of risk.

In the area of study we find that the contact risk between cattle and deer increases between 26% and 35% depending on the level of subsidy provided (and therefore the amount of new woodland created).

The area of this study is relatively small, covering an area of 600km
2
, but it is an area covered by a large number of cattle farms of various sizes. It is also the case that in our economic model for land use change, we only consider average values for land profitability, and we do not consider any non-economic factors in decision making. Therefore, the results can only be considered to be an indicator of possible disease risk increase in a moderately sized region of Scotland. However, the region has a large number of cattle farms and potentially diverse ownership and land management, changes in land use are likely to occur at a scale substantially above the size of one farm. Therefore, there is a sufficiently large population of farms (and therefore diversity of management) for decision-making homogeneity to be a reasonable assumption at this scale.

As an exemplar for a Scottish lowland cattle farming area, and thus relevant to similar areas of cattle farming density and woodland distribution, we believe this study area serves well as a guide to the potential increase in emergent infectious disease risk from new woodland creation. In general, by evaluating the adjacency between land parcels with animal species, we can assess whether fragmentation levels promote increased wildlife density, increased contact between wildlife and agriculture, and whether it has any impact on emergent infectious disease risk. Economic incentive changes each of these variables in a spatially interconnected and hard to predict manner, and our model addresses these complexities.

Future work in this area should concentrate on expanding the model to cover wider and more heterogeneous areas of land, and also include elements of non-financial decision-making in the land use change process. Most importantly, however, consideration should be given to balance the need for new woodland creation (in relation to habitat formation, biodiversity, and decarbonisation) against emergent infectious disease risk. We have seen that, at least in this study area, the level of subsidy does not have a large effect, as much as the binary provision of subsidy or not. Our model provides the foundation for investigating strategies for, say, targeting woodland types, existing land types, discouraging plantation of areas adjacent to agriculture, or providing buffer zones between.
