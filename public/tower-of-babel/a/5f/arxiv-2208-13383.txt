Cutoff profile of the Metropolis biased card shuffling
Lingfu Zhang

Abstract
We consider the Metropolis biased card shuffling (also called the multi-species ASEP on a finite interval or the random Metropolis scan). Its convergence to stationary was believed to exhibit a total-variation cutoff, and that was proved a few years ago by Labbé and Lacoin [ 26 ] . In this paper, we prove that (for N N cards) the cutoff window is in the order of N 1 / 3 N^{1/3} , and the cutoff profile is given by the GOE Tracy-Widom distribution function. This confirms a conjecture by Bufetov and Nejjar [ 15 ] . Our approach is different from [ 26 ] , by comparing the card shuffling with the multi-species ASEP on ℤ \mathbb{Z} , and using Hecke algebra and recent ASEP shift-invariance and convergence results. Our result can also be viewed as a generalization of the Oriented Swap Process finishing time convergence [ 14 ] , which is the TASEP version (of our result).



1
Introduction

In this paper we study the
(Metropolis) biased card shuffling
of size
N
, which can be viewed as a continuous time Markov chain with state space being the permutation group
S_{N}
.
This Markov chain can be described as follows.
Let
[N]
denote the set
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and let elements in
S_{N}
be represented by bijections from
[N]
to itself.
Suppose that the current state is
\lambda:[N]\to[N]
, then for each
i\in[N-1]
independently, if
\lambda(i)<\lambda(i+1)
, with rate
1
we swap
\lambda(i)
and
\lambda(i+1)
; otherwise, with rate
q
we swap
\lambda(i)
and
\lambda(i+1)
.
Here
q\in[0,1)
is a constant.
This Markov chain is known to have a stationary measure,
\mathcal{M}_{N}
, which is also called the
Mallows measure
of size
N
.
We let
\mathcal{W}_{N,t}^{\lambda}
be the law of this chain at time
t
, starting from a deterministic state
\lambda
.
Then
\mathcal{W}_{N,t}^{\lambda}\to\mathcal{M}_{N}
as
t\to\infty
.
Our main result concerns the convergence of
\mathcal{W}_{N,t}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{M}_{N}
in the total-variation distance, which is defined as

\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q}\|_{\TV}=\max_{A\subset S_{N}}|\mathcal{P}(A)-\mathcal{Q}(A)|

for any two probability measures
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
of
S_{N}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{\lambda\in S_{N}}\|\mathcal{W}_{N,2(1-q)^{-1}(N+\tau N^{1/3})}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{M}_{N}\|_{\TV}=1-F_{\GOE}(2^{2/3}\tau),

where
F_{\GOE}
is the distribution function of the Tracy-Widom GOE distribution.

The Tracy-Widom GOE distribution was introduced in
[
44
]
, where it was shown to govern the fluctuation of the largest eigenvalue in the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) random matrices.
It can be defined as

F_{\GOE}(s)=e^{-2^{-1}\int_{s}^{\infty}q(x)+(x-s)^{2}q(x)^{2}dx},

(1.1)

where
p(x)
is the Hastings-McLeod solution of the second Plainlevé equation
p^{\prime\prime}(x)=2p(x)^{3}+xp(x)
, with boundary condition
p(x)\sim\Ai(x)
as
x\to\infty
for
\Ai
being the Airy function.

Our main result (Theorem
1.1
) basically says that there is a cutoff phenomenon in the total-variation convergence of the biased card shuffling, with cutoff window in the order of
N^{1/3}
, and the cutoff profile is GOE Tracy-Widom.

1.1
Background and related works

The word ‘cutoff’ was first used by Aldous and Diaconis
[
4
]
to describe the phenomenon, where (usually in terms of the total-variation distance) a Markov chain stays away from its stationary distribution for some time, but then converges abruptly;
see the review articles
[
17
,
41
]
and the textbook
[
30
]
.
To rigorously establish cutoff often requires rather careful understandings of the particular Markov chains, as achieved in many instances in the past decades, see e.g.
[
19
,
3
,
7
,
33
,
20
,
34
,
32
,
26
,
39
]
where cutoff was proved for various (families of) Markov chains using a variety of methods, and many of them were on different card shuffling processes.

The ‘cutoff window’ refers to the small period when the total-variation decays from near
1
to near
0
, and the ‘cutoff profile’ describes the shape of the decay within the window.
These are refined information of Markov chains, and are not available even for most Markov chains where cutoff have been established.
Among those cases where cutoff profiles are known, many of them are given by the distribution function of Gaussian random variables, see e.g.
[
27
,
32
,
8
]
(and also
[
16
]
where cutoff is in a slightly different setting).
Some other types of cutoff profiles have also been discovered; see e.g.
[
42
,
36
,
15
]
.

The biased card shuffling, the Markov chain we analyze in this paper, was first studied by Diaconis and Ram in
[
18
]
under the name ‘random Metropolis scan’, as it is an instance of the Metropolis algorithm that samples biased random permutations.
The mixing of this Markov chain was later studied in
[
9
]
, whose main result states that the mixing time is at most linear in the size
N
.
A simpler proof of this result later appeared in
[
24
]
.
Since then, whether cutoff happens for the biased card shuffling remained an open problem.
This was answered affirmatively by Labbé and Lacoin
[
26
]
, who proved that the total-variation cutoff happens at time
2(1-q)^{-1}N
. In the same paper the spectral gap was also computed.

Simple Exclusion Process.
A closely related Markov chain is the
Asymmetric Simple Exclusion Process
(ASEP), which has been used as a key tool in studying the biased card shuffling.
It is a classical interacting particle system, and has been intensively studied in the literature (back to e.g.
[
31
]
).
It can be formulated as follows.
For a (finite or infinite) sequence of sites (indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
or a finite discrete interval), each site may be occupied by a particle or be empty (later we shall represent this by a function from
\mathbb{Z}
or the finite discrete interval to
\{0,1\}
, with
1
denoting a particle, and
0
denoting a hole).
For each particle, if the site next to it in the right is empty, with rate
1
it jumps to the right; and if the site next to it in the left is empty, with rate
q
it jumps to the left.
Such jumps happen independently for each particle in each direction.
The ASEP is an important model in the so-called Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class, and has exact-solvable structures.
The hydrodynamics are well understood, and its diffusive scaling limit has been identified with the KPZ equation
[
10
]
.
However, the scaling invariant and long time limit of the ASEP should be obtained under the KPZ scaling instead. That limit is termed the
KPZ fixed point
, and was first constructed in
[
35
]
as the KPZ scaling limit in the
totally asymmetric
case (i.e. when
q=0
), and later proved for the general
q\in[0,1)
case in
[
38
]
.

The ASEP on the finite discrete interval
[N]
can be easily connected to the biased card shuffling of size
N
.
Namely, we can get the ASEP on
[N]
with
k
particles, if in the biased card shuffling we replace each number
\leq k
by a particle and each number
>k
by a hole (see Figure
1
).
In other words, we can project the biased card shuffling of size
N
into the ASEP, for each integer
k
between
0
and
N
. It is worth mentioning that given all such projections (for all
k
), we can reconstruct the biased card shuffling (see Section
2.2
below for the details).
For this reason we also refer to the biased card shuffling of size
N
as ‘the multi-species ASEP’ or ‘the colored ASEP’ on
[N]
.
As the ordinary single-species ASEP, the multi-species ASEP can also be defined on
\mathbb{Z}
or any finite discrete interval (not just
[N]
).
More precisely, we define the multi-species ASEP as an evolving bijection from
\mathbb{Z}
or a finite discrete interval to itself, such that for any pair of nearest neighbors, if the left number is smaller than the right one, they swap with rate
1
; otherwise they swap with rate
q
; and such swaps happen independently for all pairs of nearest neighbors.

The mixing of the single-species ASEP on a finite interval was also studied in
[
9
]
, where a ‘pre-cutoff’ was proven.
Namely, it was shown that the total-variation distance decays from
1
to
0
in a certain time scale, but not necessarily in a small time window.
The cutoff problem (of the single-species ASEP) was settled in
[
26
]
(together the biased card shuffling cutoff), using hydrodynamics of the ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
.
In
[
26
]
Labbé and Lacoin expected that the cutoff window should be in the order of
N^{1/3}
with the cutoff profile related to the so-called Airy
2
process.
These were later established by Bufetov and Nejjar in
[
15
]
. They verified the
N^{1/3}
order of cutoff window and showed that the cutoff profile is given by the GUE Tracy-Widom distribution (which is the one point distribution of the Airy
2
process, and was introduced by Tracy and Widom in
[
43
]
as the scaling limit of the fluctuation of the largest eigenvalue of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) matrices).
As noted in
[
15
, Section 1.2]
, while proving cutoff for the biased card shuffling and the single-species ASEP follow similar and largely related arguments (in
[
26
]
), it is ‘significantly more delicate’ to study the cutoff profile of the biased card shuffling than the single-species ASEP. Due to reasons to be explained shortly, in
[
15
]
they also conjectured the
N^{1/3}
cutoff window and the Tracy-Widom GOE cutoff profile for the biased card shuffling, and this conjecture is verified by our Theorem
1.1
.

We would like to mention that, these mixing time problems in the slightly different symmetric setting, i.e. where
q=1
rather than
q\in[0,1)
, have also been studied.
The behavior of these chains (single-species/multi-species simple exclusion process in a finite interval) in this symmetric setting is quite different.
For both chains the mixing time is in the order of
N^{2}\log(N)

[
46
]
, and cutoff has been proven in
[
28
]
; and for the single-species ASEP on a circle the cutoff window is proven to be in the order of
N^{2}
, with Gaussian cutoff profile
[
27
]
.
See also e.g.
[
22
,
23
,
40
]
, and the survey
[
29
]
and the references therein, for some recent developments in this direction.

The totally asymmetric case.

For the biased card shuffling, the special case where
q=0
has been studied in the literature, first in
[
6
]
, under the name ‘Oriented Swap Process’ (OSP) and as a type of random sorting algorithm.
Note that in this case, there is a unique absorbing state
\lambda\in S_{N}
where
\lambda(x)=N+1-x
for each
x\in[N]
, and mixing of this Markov chain degenerates into the ‘absorbing time’, i.e. the time of reaching this absorbing state.
In
[
6
]
, the authors studied various aspects of the OSP, including the trajectories of the numbers, and various aspects of the absorbing time.
In particular, they showed that the absorbing time divided by
N
converges to
2
in probability. They also studied the finishing time of each number, i.e. the time a number
k\in[N]
makes its last move.
This can be viewed as the finishing time (in a segment) of the
totally asymmetric simple exclusion process
(TASEP), which is the degeneration of the (single-species) ASEP to the
q=0
case.
In
[
6
]
the authors showed that the one number finishing time has fluctuation in the order of
N^{1/3}
with GUE Tracy-Widom scaling limit, using TASEP convergence results from
[
25
]
.

A question asked in
[
6
]
is to figure out the order and distribution of the absorbing time (which can be viewed as the maximum of the finishing times over all
k\in[N]
).
This was settled in
[
14
]
, which proved that the absorbing time fluctuation is in the order of
N^{1/3}
with GOE Tracy-Widom scaling limit, using recently proved symmetries of the six-vertex model
[
13
]
. (This is also the reason why the biased card shuffling cutoff profile is conjectured to be GOE Tracy-Widom in
[
15
]
.)
Another direction pointed out in
[
6
]
is to generate the results of OSP to the partially asymmetric (i.e.
q>0
) case.
Our Theorem
1.1
can be viewed as such a generalization of
[
14
]
.
We would also like to mention a recent interesting development
[
2
]
, which likely leads to generating the results on trajectories in
[
6
]
to the
q>0
case.

1.2
Our contribution and main tools

We next describe our strategy and the used tools, in comparison with related previous works (in particular,
[
15
]
and
[
14
]
).
As discussed above,
[
15
]
studies the general
q\in[0,1)
problem, but at the level of a single-species ASEP projection; on the other hand,
[
14
]
studies the multi-species model, but in the special
q=0
case.

Our proofs use ingredients from both papers.
As
[
14
]
, we can use a shift-invariance property of the multi-species ASEP (from
[
13
,
21
]
), to reduce problems on the multi-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
to problems on the single-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
with step initial configuration.
In
[
14
]
classical convergence results on the TASEP were then used to get the GOE Tracy-Widom limit, while here we instead use the more recently proved ASEP convergence to the KPZ fixed point
[
38
]
.
Then the main task is to connect the cutoff profile problem in a finite interval, with the distribution of the multi-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
.

In the
q=0
case, due to that swaps happen only in one direction, there is a natural coupling between the multi-species TASEP on a finite interval and on
\mathbb{Z}
, using some truncation operators, as first described in
[
6
]
.
Such relation is not available when
q>0
.
Besides, the total-variation distance (in the
q>0
case) is also much less tractable than the absorbing time (in the
q=0
case).

Our main contribution is to develop new ideas to overcome these difficulties, and build the connection in the above ‘main task’.
We use the height function representation of the single-species ASEP, and Hecke algebra identities; and the core arguments are given in Section
2.3
and Section
3
below.
In more details, we couple the biased card shuffling (with a deterministic initial configuration) with the stationary one, so that the swaps are synchronized. This is the
basic coupling
to be defined shortly in Section
2.2
.
Then the mixing time is reduced to the stopping time when they are equal for the first time.
By considering the single-species ASEP projections and using ordering properties of the ASEP height function, this problem is eventually reduced to comparing two (single-species) ASEPs on
\mathbb{Z}
with different initial configurations.

For two (single-species) ASEPs under the basic coupling, such that the set of particle locations of one of them contains the set of particle locations of the other one, they are equivalent to the ASEP with
second-class particles
.
A second-class particle is a particle which can swap with a hole next to it in the right, or a particle next to it in the left, with rate
1
respectively.
It can also swap with a hole next to it in the left, or a particle next to it in the right, with rate
q
respectively.
It is straightforward to check that, by replacing each second-class particle with a hole, or replacing each second-class particle with a particle, one can get two (single-species) ASEPs which are under the basic coupling.
Thus to study the difference of these two (single-species) ASEPs, one just need to track the locations of the second-class particles.
For this we use Hecke algebra identities, reducing it to events under the Mallows measure, which is well-understood with explicit distribution functions (e.g. (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) below).

Some of these ingredients, such as the basic coupling and Hecke algebra, have also appeared in
[
15
]
.
However, there are several key differences between the arguments in this paper and
[
15
]
.
The most significant aspect is in our usages of Hecke algebra.
In
[
15
]
Hecke algebra was used to translate events on certain single-species ASEPs to events on certain ASEPs with second-class particles, which were analyzed directly.
In this paper, Hecke algebra is used in the opposite direction.
Namely, we introduce second-class particles for we wish to compare two (single-species) ASEPs; and the Hecke algebra is used to reduce events on the ASEP with second-class particles to events on another (single-species) ASEP without second-class particles.
Besides, while ordering of particle configurations was also used in
[
15
]
, it is more extensively exploited in this paper, with the help of the more intuitive height function.
Using these we can directly compare ASEPs on a finite interval and
\mathbb{Z}
, and such comparison was not obtained in
[
15
]
.
Finally, we mention that our proof can be easily adapted to the single-species ASEP setting, to get an alternative proof of the main result of
[
15
]

(see Remark
2.6
below).

Notations and terminologies

Some basic notations are used throughout this paper (and some of them have appeared already).
For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
we let
x\wedge y=\min\{x,y\}
and
x\vee y=\max\{x,y\}
, and let
\llbracket x,y\rrbracket
be the discrete interval, containing all integers
\geq x
and
\leq y
. We also write
[N]=\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Below
q
always denotes the rate of reversed jumps or swaps, and we always assume that
q\in[0,1)
.

Organization of the remaining text

The remaining text mainly focuses on the proofs.
In Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, we set up some notations and provide some preliminary lemmas; then in Section
2.3
we state the main steps of our arguments, as three propositions, and prove Theorem

2
Preliminaries and main steps

2.1
The stationary Mallows measure

We start by giving the formal definition of the Mallows measures, which are stationary measures of the biased card shuffling or the multi-species ASEP on a finite interval.
For the convenience of later arguments, we consider the interval
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
for integers
m\leq n
.
As in
[
15
]
, we let
S_{m,n}
denote the set of all bijections between
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
and itself,
and let
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
denote the Mallows measure on
S_{m,n}
, such that for any
w\in S_{m,n}
,

\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(w)=q^{\kappa(w)}Z_{m,n},

(2.1)

where (and also for the rest of this paper) we denote
\kappa(w)=\sum_{m\leq i<j\leq n}\mathds{1}[w(i)<w(j)]
as the ‘energy’ for any
w\in S_{m,n}
, and

Z_{m,n}=\frac{1}{\sum_{w\in S_{m,n}}q^{\kappa(w)}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-m+1}\frac{1-q}{1-q^{i}}.

(2.2)

Note that there is a unique
w\in S_{m,n}
with
\kappa(w)=0
, i.e.
w:i=\mapsto m+n-i
; and that is the ‘ground state’.
It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
is the stationary measure of the biased card shuffling on
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
.

For each
k\in\llbracket 0,n-m+1\rrbracket
, we let
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}
be the measure on
\{0,1\}^{\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}
, obtained from
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
under the projection where each
w\in S_{m,n}
is mapped to
x\mapsto\mathds{1}[w(x)\leq m+k-1]
.
In words, under
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}
there are
k
particles and
n-m+1-k
holes, if we view each
1
as a particle and each
0
as a hole.
One can readily check that, for any
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}
,

\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}(\omega)=\mathds{1}\big[\sum_{i\in\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}\omega(i)=k\big]q^{\kappa(\omega)}Z_{m,n}^{k},

(2.3)

where (as a slight misuse of notations, and also for the rest of this paper)
\kappa(\omega)=\sum_{m\leq i<j\leq n}\mathds{1}[\omega(i)=1]\mathds{1}[\omega(j)=0]
is the ‘energy’ for any
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}
, and

Z_{m,n}^{k}=\frac{1}{\sum_{w\in\{0,1\}^{\llbracket m,n\rrbracket},\sum_{i\in\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}\omega(i)=k}q^{\kappa(w)}}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-q^{i})\prod_{i=1}^{n-m+1-k}(1-q^{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n-m+1}(1-q^{i})}.

Note that here the ‘ground state’ is given by
\omega:i\mapsto\mathds{1}[i\geq n-k+1]
.
From this definition of
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}
as a projection of
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
, it is stationary under the (single-species) ASEP evolution on
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
.

We will frequently use the following estimate of the ‘energy’ under
\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

There is a constant
C>0
depending only on
q
, such that for any integers
m\leq n
,
k\in\llbracket 0,n-m+1\rrbracket
, and any
a\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{P}_{m,n}^{k}\big(\{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\llbracket m,n\rrbracket}:\kappa(\omega)>a\}\big)<Cq^{a/2}.

(2.4)

Proof.

Let
\sum_{0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}z^{i}
be the Taylor series for
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{i})^{-1}
.
By (
2.3
), and noting that
Z_{m,n}^{k}\leq 1
, the left-hand side of (
2.4
) is at most

\sum_{i=a+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}q^{i}<q^{(a+1)/2}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i/2})^{-1}.

By taking
C=q^{1/2}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i/2})^{-1}
the conclusion follows.
∎

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, below we also write
S_{N}=S_{1,N}
,
\mathcal{M}_{N}=\mathcal{M}_{1,N}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{k}=\mathcal{P}_{1,N}^{k}
, for simplicity of notations.

Setup for several processes

In this subsection we define some processes to be used in the proof of Theorem

, and discuss some basic properties of them.

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\zeta_{t}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
.
Let the initial configuration
\zeta_{0}
be the identity map of
\mathbb{Z}
.

In defining the following processes and for the rest of this paper, we take
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

We let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the biased card shuffling of size

, with

•

Basic coupling.

For the multi-species or single-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
or a discrete interval, the evolution can be generated by two independent Poisson point processes on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, with rates
1

Projections.
For any

or

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

,
h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}\leq h\{\zeta_{t}^{k}\}

We first consider the case where

We have

h\{\xi_{0}^{k}\}(N)=h\{\lambda_{0}^{k}\}(N)=h\{\zeta_{0}^{k}\}(N)=h\{\overline{\xi}_{0}^{k}\}(N)=N-2k,

and

.

2.3
Proof of the main result

With the processes defined in the previous subsection, we need (1) to upper bound the first time
T
when
\lambda_{T}=\overline{\xi}_{T}
; (2) to lower bound the first time
T
when
\overline{\xi}_{T}=\xi_{T}
, and show that
\overline{\xi}_{t}
and

are ‘quite different’ for

between

and

.
We now give the main steps to accomplish these tasks.

We start by explaining how the Tracy-Widom GOE distribution is involved.
This distribution appears in scaling limits of the single-species ASEP, as we will later see using the single-species ASEP convergence results from
[
38
]
.
From these results and using an ASEP shift-invariance properties proved in
[
13
,
21
]
, we can deduce convergence results for the multi-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
, as follows.

For
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{D}_{N,t}^{b}
denote the event where
h\{\zeta_{t}^{k}\}(N-k)>N-k+b
for any

.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, and any real number sequence
\{b_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1/3}b_{N}\to 0
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{D}_{N,2(1-q)^{-1}(N+\tau N^{1/3})}^{b_{N}}]=F_{\GOE}(2^{2/3}\tau).

The proof of this proposition will be given in Section
5
.

In the TASEP setting (i.e. when

), the multi-species process on a finite interval can be obtained from the multi-species process on
\mathbb{Z}
, using truncation operators (see e.g.
[
6
]
). Thus Proposition
2.3
directly implies that the absorbing time has GOE Tracy-Widom distribution asymptotically; and that is the proof in
[

]
.

For the general

A more important difference is that, there is no such truncation operators and no exact coupling structure between the process on
\mathbb{Z}
and the process on a finite interval.
Thus we instead show that, under the basic coupling, the processes on
\mathbb{Z}
and a finite interval are ‘close to each other’ in terms of the height functions of their projections, in the following sense.

For
a\in\mathbb{N}
,

,
k\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, and
t\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b}
denote the event where
h\{\zeta_{t}^{k}\}(N-k)>N-k+b
, and

\text{either }\;h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}(N-k-a)<N-k-a\;\text{ or }\;h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}(N-k+a)<N-k-a.

Proposition 2.4
.

There is a constant
C>0
depending only on
q
, such that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b}]<Cq^{a/2}
when

.

We will prove this proposition in Section

, using Hecke algebra techniques.

In analyzing the first time
T
when

, a key ingredient we need is that, if
\lambda_{t}
and
\overline{\xi}_{t}
are ‘close to each other’ at some time

, they will be the same in a short amount of time. We state this result as follows.

For

,

,

, and

, let

for each

(in other words,
\xi_{t+s}^{k}
is not at the ‘ground state’ for each

, since

is equivalent to that

.

There is a constant

, such that

.

\kappa(\overline{\xi}_{2(1-q)^{-1}(N+\tau N^{1/3})}^{k})\geq\lfloor\log(N)^{2}/2\rfloor,

where we recall (from Section
2.1
) that
\kappa(\overline{\xi}_{2(1-q)^{-1}(N+\tau N^{1/3})}^{k})
is the ‘energy’.
By Lemma
2.1
, the probability of this event is bounded by
Cq^{\log(N)^{2}/4}
, for some
C>0
depending only on
q
.
Then by taking a union bound over
k
we have that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}]=0
, and we finish this proof.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Using Propositions
2.4
and
2.5
for a single
k
, and known one-point distribution convergence of ASEP to the GUE Tracy-Widom distribution (e.g.
[

, Theorem 3]
) instead of Proposition
2.3
, one can recover the main result of
[
15
]
, via the same arguments as the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Our Proposition
2.5
plays a similar role as the arguments in Section 3 of
[
15
]
, and both use a hitting time bound from
[
9
]
.
Our Proposition
2.4
, on the other hand, plays a similar role as Section 4 of
[
15
]
, but its proof (to be given in the next section) is quite different. As discussed above, while both our Proposition

and

]
use Hecke algebra as the main tool, it is used in quite different ways with different points of view, enabling us to analyze this more delicate biased care shuffling.

The remaining three sections are devoted to proving the three propositions.

3
ASEP on the line and an interval: Hecke algebra

In this section we prove Proposition
2.4
.
We can write
\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b}=\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}\cup\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,-}
, where

\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,-}
:

h\{\zeta_{t}^{k}\}(N-k)>N-k+b
and

h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}(N-k-a)<N-k-a
,

\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}
:

h\{\zeta_{t}^{k}\}(N-k)>N-k+b
and

h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}(N-k+a)<N-k-a
.

By symmetry, below we just bound the probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}]
.

3.1
Hecke algebra and basic properties

We start by formally introducing Hecke algebra, the main tool of this section. Several notations below are from
[
15
]
.

For any integers
m<n
, we can think of
S_{m,n}
as the permutation group of
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
, such that for any
w,v\in S_{m,n}
we let
wv=v\circ w
.
The Hecke algebra
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}
is the algebra with basis
\{T_{w}\}_{w\in S_{m,n}}
, and the rules

T_{s}T_{w}=\begin{cases}T_{sw},&\text{if }\kappa(sw)=\kappa(w)-1,\\
(1-q)T_{w}+qT_{sw},&\text{if }\kappa(sw)=\kappa(w)+1,\end{cases}

(3.1)

for any
w\in S_{m,n}
and
s
being any nearest neighbor transposition in
S_{m,n}
(i.e. there is some
i\in\llbracket m,n-1\rrbracket
such that
s(i)=i+1
,
s(i+1)=i
, and
s(j)=j
for any
j\in\llbracket m,n\rrbracket\setminus\{i,i+1\}
).
Here we recall (from Section
2.1
) that
\kappa(w)=\sum_{m\leq i<j\leq n}\mathds{1}[w(i)<w(j)]
is the ‘energy’ for any
w\in S_{m,n}
.

We let
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}^{\pr}\subset\mathcal{H}_{m,n}
be the
probability sub-algebra
, containing all
\sum_{w\in S_{m,n}}p_{w}T_{w}
with
\sum_{w\in S_{m,n}}p_{w}=1
and each
p_{w}\geq 0
.
Then any element in
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}^{\pr}
corresponds to a probability measure of
S_{m,n}
.

Note that for any
m^{\prime}\leq m\leq n\leq n^{\prime}
,
S_{m,n}
is naturally embedded into
S_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
.
This gives a natural embedding of
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}
(resp.
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}^{\pr}
) into
\mathcal{H}_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
(resp.
\mathcal{H}_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{\pr}
).

Biased card shuffling.
From (
3.1
), it is not difficult to see that the evolution of the biased card shuffling can be written as multiplying a uniformly chosen random transposition, at rate
n-m
.
Namely, we denote
\hat{T}_{m,n}=(n-m)^{-1}\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}T_{s_{i}}
, with
s_{i}
being the transposition between
i
and
i+1
. Let

W_{m,n}(t)=e^{-(n-m)t}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{((n-m)t)^{i}}{i!}\hat{T}_{m,n}^{i},

where
\hat{T}_{m,n}^{i}
is the product of
i
copies of
\hat{T}_{m,n}
when
i\geq 1
, and
\hat{T}_{m,n}^{0}=T_{id}
for
id\in S_{m,n}
being the identity element (i.e. the identity map of
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
).
Then
W_{m,n}(t)
is in
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}^{\pr}
, and its corresponding probability measure is just the time
t
distribution of the ASEP on
\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
, starting from the identity element.

Mallows element.
A particularly useful object is the
Mallows element

M_{m,n}
of
\mathcal{H}_{m,n}^{\pr}
, which is defined as

M_{m,n}=\sum_{w\in S_{m,n}}\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(w)T_{w}=\sum_{w\in S_{m,n}}q^{\kappa(w)}Z_{m,n}T_{w},

where
Z_{m,n}
is defined in
2.2
.
Then the probability measure given by
M_{m,n}
is just the Mallows measure
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
.

Note that since
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
is the stationary measure of the biased card shuffling, we must have that
W_{m,n}(t)\to M_{m,n}
as
t\to\infty
.

Involution.

Let
\mathfrak{i}:\mathcal{H}_{m,n}\to\mathcal{H}_{m,n}
be the linear map where
\mathfrak{i}(T_{w})=T_{w^{-1}}
for any
w\in S_{m,n}
.
It is straightforward to check that
\mathfrak{i}
is an involutive anti-homomorphism; namely, for any
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,n}
we have
\mathfrak{i}(T_{1}T_{2})=\mathfrak{i}(T_{2})\mathfrak{i}(T_{1})
, and
\mathfrak{i}(\mathfrak{i}(T_{1}))=T_{1}
(see e.g.
[
15
, Proposition 5.1]
).

We also have that
\mathfrak{i}(W_{m,n}(t))=W_{m,n}(t)
, since
s=s^{-1}
for any nearest neighbor transposition
s
in
S_{m,n}
.
We note that this equality can be interpreted as the ASEP ‘color-to-position symmetry’, as proved in
[
5
,
6
,
11
]
.
By sending
t\to\infty
we further have
\mathfrak{i}(M_{m,n})=M_{m,n}
.

,

).

For technical reasons we need to slightly extend the definition of the height function.
For any integers
m<n
and
\omega:\llbracket m,n\rrbracket\to\{0,1\}
, we define
h^{\prime}\{\omega\}=h\{\omega^{\prime}\}
, where
\omega^{\prime}:\mathbb{Z}\to\{0,1\}
is the function satisfying
\omega^{\prime}(x)=\omega^{\prime}(x)
for

, and
\omega^{\prime}(x)=1
for
x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\llbracket m,n\rrbracket
.
Note that compared with

, the only difference is that we let the slope in

be

rather than

.

We then have the following ordering property (see Figure
3
).

Lemma 3.1
.

We have
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{t}^{-1+k-N}\}\leq h\{\xi_{t}^{k}\}
and
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{t}^{-1+k-N}\}\leq h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{t}^{-X+k-1}\}=h\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{-X+k-1}\}\leq h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{k}\}
for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

We first check that

h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}\leq h\{\xi_{0}^{k}\}.

(3.2)

Note that for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we always have that
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(x)\leq x\wedge(2N-2k-x)
, since (by definition) we have
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(-X)=-X
, and
\sum_{x\in\llbracket-X,X\rrbracket}h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(x)=X-N+k
thus
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(X)=2N-2k-X
, and that
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz.
From the definition of
\ddot{\zeta}_{0}
, for each
x\in\llbracket-X,-X+k-1\rrbracket
, we can find
y\in\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket
with
\ddot{\zeta}_{0}(y)=x
.
This implies that

\sum_{x\in\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket}h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(x)\geq k,

so we have
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(k)\leq-k
.
Then for each
x\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
there is
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(x)\leq-x
, and for each
x\in\llbracket k+1,N\rrbracket
there is
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}(x)\leq x-2k
(since
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz).
Putting all the bounds together, we get (
3.2
),
since
h\{\xi_{0}^{k}\}(x)=x\wedge(2N-2k-x)
for
x\in\llbracket-\infty,0\rrbracket\cup\llbracket N,\infty\rrbracket
,
h\{\xi_{0}^{k}\}(x)=-x
for
x\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
, and
h\{\xi_{0}^{k}\}(x)=x-2k
for
x\in\llbracket k,N\rrbracket
.

We next check that

h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}\}\leq h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}=h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}\leq h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{k}\}.

(3.3)

The first inequality holds since
\sum_{y\in\llbracket-X,x\rrbracket}\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-1+k-N}(y)\geq\sum_{y\in\llbracket-X,x\rrbracket}\ddot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}(y)
, for any
x\in\llbracket-X,X\rrbracket
.
The equality is by the second point above.

For the second inequality, by the second point above we have that

\sum_{x\in\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket}h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(x)=k,

so we have
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(k)=-k
.
Then for each
x\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
there is
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(x)\leq-x
, and for each
x\in\llbracket k+1,X\rrbracket
there is
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(x)\leq x-2k
(since
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz).
We also have that
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(x)\leq x\wedge(2X-2k-x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, since
\sum_{x\in\llbracket-X,X\rrbracket}h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(x)=k
(thus
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}(X)=X-2k
), and that
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{-X+k-1}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz.
Putting all the bounds together, we get the second inequality in (
3.2
),
since
h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{k}\}(x)=-x
for
x\in\llbracket-\infty,k\rrbracket
,
h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{k}\}(x)=x-2k
for
x\in\llbracket k+1,X\rrbracket
, and
h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{0}^{k}\}(x)=2X-2k-x
for
x\in\llbracket X,\infty\rrbracket
.

Finally, using arguments similar to those in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, ordering of these height functions is preserved under the basic coupling, so the conclusion follows.
∎

We now define the events

\dot{\mathcal{E}}
:

h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{k}\}(N-k)>N-k+b
and
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{-X+k-1}\}(N-k+a)<N-k+a
.

\ddot{\mathcal{E}}
:

h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{t}^{-X+k-1}\}(N-k+a)=N-k+a
and
h\{\ddot{\zeta}_{t}^{-1+k-N}\}(N-k+a)<N-k-a

By Lemma

, and the fact that the

\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{k}
is

, we have that the

\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}
.

Thus it suffices to bound the probabilities of the events
\dot{\mathcal{E}}
and
\ddot{\mathcal{E}}
.
For this, note that using above stated properties of the involution
\mathfrak{i}
, we have

W_{-X,X}(t)M_{-X,k}=\mathfrak{i}(W_{-X,X}(t))\mathfrak{i}(M_{-X,k})=\mathfrak{i}(M_{-X,k}W_{-X,X}(t)),

(3.4)

and

W_{-X,X}(t)M_{-X,k}M_{-X+k,X}=\mathfrak{i}(W_{-X,X}(t))\mathfrak{i}(M_{-X,k})\mathfrak{i}(M_{-X+k,X})=\mathfrak{i}(M_{-X+k,X}M_{-X,k}W_{-X,X}(t)).

(3.5)

These two identities can be interpreted as distributional identities, and are key inputs in bounding
\mathbb{P}[\dot{\mathcal{E}}]
and
\mathbb{P}[\ddot{\mathcal{E}}]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
\mathbb{P}[\dot{\mathcal{E}}]\leq Cq^{b/4-a/2}
where
C
is a constant depending only on
q
.

Proof.

Assuming
\dot{\mathcal{E}}
, we must have that
h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{k}\}(N-k+a)>N-k+b-a
(since
h^{\prime}\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{k}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz) and
h\{\dot{\zeta}_{t}^{-X+k-1}\}(N-k+a)<N-k+a
. In other words, we have

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:N-k+a+1\leq x\leq X,\dot{\zeta}_{t}(x)\leq k\}|>k-a+b/2,

and

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:N-k+a+1\leq x\leq X,\dot{\zeta}_{t}(x)\leq-X+k-1\}|<k.

Now we use the distributional identity implied by (
3.4
). Let
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
be the inverse of
\dot{\zeta}_{t}
(as a group element in
S_{-X,X}
).
Then the above two inequalities are equivalent to

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)\geq N-k+a+1,-X\leq x\leq k\}|>k-a+b/2,

(3.6)

and

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)\geq N-k+a+1,-X\leq x\leq-X+k-1\}|<k,

(3.7)

respectively. Note that these events only concern
x\mapsto\mathds{1}[\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)\leq N-k+a]
, which is a random configuration in
\{0,1\}^{\llbracket-X,X\rrbracket}
. We next analyze its distribution.

By (
3.4
), the law of
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
is given by
M_{-X,k}W_{-X,X}(t)
, meaning that we can first sample
w
from the law given by
W_{-X,X}(t)
, then sample
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
from the law given by
M_{-X,k}T_{w}
.
Equivalently,
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
is the same as
w
on
\llbracket k+1,X\rrbracket
; and on
\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket
, the law of
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
(conditioned on
w
) is the weak limit of running the (multi-species) ASEP from
w
to time
\infty
.

Let
\phi:\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket\to\{w(x):x\in\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket\}
be the unique increasing bijection determined by
w
in
\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket
.
Then
\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}
in
\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket
can be written as
\phi\circ v
, for
v
sampled from
\mathcal{M}_{-X,k}
.
Now the event in (
3.6
) is equivalent to that

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\phi(x)\geq N-k+a+1,-X\leq x\leq k\}|>k-a+b/2.

Since
\phi
is increasing, this is further equivalent to that

\phi(-\lfloor-a+b/2\rfloor)\geq N-k+a+1.

The event in (
3.7
) is equivalent to that

\exists x\in\llbracket-X,-X+k-1\rrbracket,\quad\underline{\dot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)=\phi(v(x))\leq N-k+a.

So (since
\phi
is increasing) these two events together imply that
v(x)<a-b/2
for some
x\in\llbracket-X,-X+k-1\rrbracket
, which further implies that
\sum_{-X\leq i<j\leq k}\mathds{1}[v(i)<a-b/2,v(j)\geq a-b/2]>b/2-a+1
.
Note that
x\mapsto\mathds{1}[v(x)<a-b/2]
has distribution
\mathcal{P}_{-X,k}^{\lceil a-b/2\rceil+X}
, so by Lemma
2.1
, the probability of this event is at most
Cq^{b/4-a/2}
, for some
C>0
depending only on
q
.
Thus the conclusion follows.
∎

The bound for
\mathbb{P}[\ddot{\mathcal{E}}]
is proved using a similar strategy.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
\mathbb{P}[\ddot{\mathcal{E}}]\leq Cq^{a/2}
where
C
is a constant depending only on
q
.

Proof.

Assuming
\ddot{\mathcal{E}}
, we must have

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:N-k+a+1\leq x\leq X,\ddot{\zeta}_{t}(x)\leq-X+k-1\}|=k,

and

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:N-k+a+1\leq x\leq X,\ddot{\zeta}_{t}(x)\leq-1+k-N\}|<X-N+k-a.

We let
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
be the inverse of
\ddot{\zeta}_{t}
(as a group element in
S_{-X,X}
).
Then the above two relations are equivalent to

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)\geq N-k+a+1,-X\leq x\leq-X+k-1\}|=k,

(3.8)

and

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)\geq N-k+a+1,-X\leq x\leq-1+k-N\}|<X-N+k-a,

(3.9)

respectively. By (
3.5
), the law of
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
is given by
M_{-X+k,X}M_{-X,k}W_{-X,X}(t)
, meaning that we can first sample
w
from the law given by
W_{-X,X}(t)
, and then sample
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
from the law given by
M_{-X+k,X}M_{-X,k}T_{w}
.
Equivalently,
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
can be obtained from
w
, by first running the (multi-species) ASEP on
\llbracket-X,k\rrbracket
to time
\infty
to get
w^{\prime}
, then running the (multi-species) ASEP on
\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket
(from
w^{\prime}
) to time
\infty
to get
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
.

From this construction of
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
, we have that
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
and
w^{\prime}
are the same in
\llbracket-X,-X+k-1\rrbracket
. Then the event in (
3.8
) is equivalent to that

\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)=w^{\prime}(x)\geq N-k+a+1,\quad\forall x\in\llbracket-X,-X+k-1\rrbracket,

and this is further equivalent to that

|\{x\in\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket:w^{\prime}(x)\leq N-k+a\}|=N-k+a+X+1.

(3.10)

Let
\phi:\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket\to\{w^{\prime}(x):x\in\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket\}
be the unique increasing bijection determined by
w^{\prime}
in
\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket
.
Then
\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}
in
\llbracket-X+k,X\rrbracket
can be written as
\phi\circ v
, for
v
sampled from
\mathcal{M}_{-X+k,X}
.
Under the event in (
3.10
), we have that
\phi(N+a)\leq N-k+a
since
\phi
is increasing.
The event in (
3.9
) is equivalent to that

\exists x\in\llbracket k-N,X\rrbracket,\quad\underline{\ddot{\zeta}}_{t}(x)=\phi(v(x))\geq N-k+a+1.

Then under the events in (
3.10
) and (
3.9
), there exists
x\in\llbracket k-N,X\rrbracket
such that
v(x)\geq N+a+1
.
This further implies that

\sum_{-X+k\leq i<j\leq X}\mathds{1}[v(i)\leq N+a,v(j)\geq N+a+1]\geq\sum_{i\in\llbracket-X+k,k-N-1\rrbracket}\mathds{1}[v(i)\leq N+a]\geq a+1.

Note that
x\mapsto\mathds{1}[v(x)\leq N+a]
has distribution
\mathcal{P}_{-X+k,X}^{N+a+X-k+1}
, so by Lemma
2.1
the probability of this event is at most
Cq^{a/2}
, for some
C>0
depending only on
q
.
Thus the conclusion follows.
∎

Putting all these estimates together we can now upper bound
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b}]
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

From the definition of
\dot{\mathcal{E}}
and
\ddot{\mathcal{E}}
, we have that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}]\leq\limsup_{X\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[\dot{\mathcal{E}}\cup\ddot{\mathcal{E}}]\leq\limsup_{X\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[\dot{\mathcal{E}}]+\mathbb{P}[\ddot{\mathcal{E}}]
.
Then by Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,+}]\leq Cq^{(b/4-a/2)\wedge a/2}
, for
C
depending only on
q
.
By symmetry the same upper bound holds for
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{N,k,t}^{a,b,-}]
.
Thus the conclusion follows.
∎

4
Hitting time bound

In this section we prove Proposition
2.5
, for which we need the following known estimate on the ‘ground state hitting time’ of the single-species ASEP.

For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n,m}=(\eta^{n,m}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the (single-species) ASEP, with initial condition given by

\eta^{n,m}_{0}(x)=1
for
x\in\llbracket n-m+1,n\rrbracket
or
x\in\llbracket n+m+1,\infty\rrbracket
, and
\eta^{n,m}_{0}(x)=0
for
x\in\llbracket-\infty,n-m\rrbracket
or
x\in\llbracket n+1,n+m\rrbracket
.

The following estimate is implied by
[
9
, Theorem 1.9]
, which is also used in
[
15
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a constant
C_{*}>0
depending only on
q
, such that the following is true.
Let
H>0
be the smallest number with
\eta^{n,m}_{H}(x)=\mathds{1}[x\geq n+1]
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then
\mathbb{P}[H>C_{*}m]<1/m
.

Below we also assume that
C_{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
, since otherwise we can replace it by
\lceil C_{*}\rceil
.

Recall the setting of Proposition
2.5
and the parameters
N,k,t,a
.
The idea of proving Proposition
2.5
is to split the time interval
[0,\log(N)^{4}]
into segments each with length
C_{*}a
, and use Lemma
4.1
to conclude that the probability of not reaching the ‘ground state’ in each interval is at most
1/a
.
Then we can multiply these probabilities for all the intervals, since that the ASEP is Markovian.

For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
be the event where

h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}\}(N-k-a)=h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}\}(N-k+a)=N-k-a,

and
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
be the event where

h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a+s}^{k}\}(N-k)<N-k,\quad\forall s\in[0,C_{*}a],

i.e.
\xi_{t+iC_{*}a+s}^{k}
is not at the ‘ground state’ for any
s\in[0,C_{*}a]
.
Here
C_{*}
is the constant in Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

We have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}\mid\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}]\leq 1/a
for any
\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}
under the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
.

Proof.

Note that
h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{0}\}(N-k-a)=h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{0}\}(N-k+a)=N-k-a
, and
h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{0}\}
has slope
1
in
\llbracket-\infty,N-k-a\rrbracket\cup\llbracket N-k,N-k+a\rrbracket
, and has slope
-1
in
\llbracket N-k-a,N-k\rrbracket\cup\llbracket N-k+a,\infty\rrbracket
.
Thus assuming
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, we must have
h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{0}\}\leq h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}\}
since
h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a}^{k}\}
is
1
-Lipschitz.

We couple
(\xi_{t+iC_{*}a+s}^{k})_{s\geq 0}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{N-k,a}
under the basic coupling.
Then
h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{s}\}\leq h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a+s}^{k}\}
for any
s\geq 0
, since (by arguments similar to those in the proof of Lemma
2.2
) ordering of these height functions is preserved under the basic coupling.

Now by Lemma
4.1
, with probability
\geq 1-1/a
we have that
\eta^{N-k,a}_{s}(x)=\mathds{1}[x\geq N-k+1]
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, thus
h\{\eta^{N-k,a}_{s}\}(N-k)=N-k
, for some
s\in[0,C_{*}a]
. This implies that we must have
h\{\xi_{t+iC_{*}a+s}^{k}\}(N-k)=N-k
, so
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
does not hold.
Thus the conclusion follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Under the event
\mathcal{B}_{N,k,t}^{a,\log(N)^{4}}
, we must have that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
and
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
hold, for each
i\in\llbracket 0,\log(N)^{4}/(C_{*}a)-1\rrbracket
.
By Lemma
4.2
and that the ASEP is Makovian, we must have that

\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}\mid\bigcap_{0\leq j\leq i}\mathcal{E}_{i}\cap\bigcap_{0\leq j<i}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}\Big]\leq 1/a.

Thus by taking the product of this over
i\in\llbracket 0,\log(N)^{4}/(C_{*}a)-1\rrbracket
, and
using that
2\leq a\leq\log(N)^{2}
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}_{N,k,t}^{a,\log(N)^{4}}]<a^{-\lfloor\log(N)^{4}/(C_{*}a)\rfloor}<e^{-\log(2)\lfloor\log(N)^{2}/C_{*}\rfloor}
. So the conclusion follows.
∎

5
ASEP shift-invariance and convergence to the KPZ fixed point

In this section we deduce Proposition
2.3
.

The first input we need is the shift-invariance property of the multi-specie ASEP, which is degenerated from the shift-invariance property of the colored six-vertex model from
[
13
,
21
]
.
Namely, we obtain the following result.
Recall
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
(the multi-species ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
) and its projections from Section
2.2
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\geq 0
, the event

h\{\zeta^{k}_{t}\}(N-k)>N-k+b,\quad\forall k\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket,

has the same probability as the event

h\{\zeta^{0}_{t}\}(N-2k)>N+b,\quad\forall k\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket.

This is deduced directly from a special case of
[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
or
[
21
, Theorem 1.6]
, using the limit transition from the colored six-vertex model to the multi-species ASEP. Such transition was first observed in
[
12
]
, and then proved in
[
1
]
in the colorless setting; the colored version follows the same proof.
See also
[
47
, Section 3.1]
.
We omit the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Now we just need to analyze the process
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{0}=(\zeta^{0}_{t})_{\geq 0}
, which is the single-species ASEP with step initial condition.
For this, we use the convergence of the single-species ASEP to the KPZ fixed point, which is a random process
\mathfrak{h}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and can be described as a continuous time Markov chain
\mathfrak{h}(t,\cdot)
with explicit transition probabilities.
The state space of this Markov chain is the upper semi-continuous (UC) space, containing all upper semi-continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
with
f(x)<C(|x|+1)
for some
C<\infty
and
f(x)>-\infty
for some
x
.
We refer the readers to
[
35
, Definition 3.12]
for the precise definition of the KPZ fixed point.

We need the following convergence of the single-species ASEP to the KPZ fixed point.

Proposition 5.2

(
[
38
, Theorem 2.2(2)]
)
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, let
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\epsilon}=(\eta^{\epsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the (single-species) ASEP with deterministic initial configuration
\eta^{\epsilon}_{0}
, such that
\eta^{\epsilon}_{0}(x)
stabilizes as
x\to-\infty
(so the its height function is well-defined); and let
g_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathfrak{h}_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous with
|\mathfrak{h}_{0}(x)|<C(1+|x|^{1/2})
for some
C<\infty
.
Suppose that

x\mapsto-\epsilon^{1/2}h\{\eta^{\epsilon}_{0}\}(2\epsilon^{-1}x)+g_{\epsilon}

converges to
\mathfrak{h}_{0}
, uniformly on compact sets. Then for any
t>0
,

x\mapsto-\epsilon^{1/2}h\{\eta^{\epsilon}_{2\epsilon^{-3/2}t}\}(2\epsilon^{-1}x)+g_{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{-1}(1-q)t

converges to
\mathfrak{h}((1-q)t,\cdot)
in distribution, in the uniform on compact sets topology, with
\mathfrak{h}
being the KPZ fixed point with initial data
\mathfrak{h}(0,\cdot)=\mathfrak{h}_{0}
.
Here we regard
h\{\eta^{\epsilon}_{0}\}
and
h\{\eta^{\epsilon}_{2\epsilon^{-3/2}t}\}
as functions on
\mathbb{R}
, by linearly interpolating between integers.

Using Propositions
5.1
and
