Novel Approaches to Solve Simple Harmonic Motion
Zhiwei Chong

Abstract
This paper presents two novel approaches to solve the classic simple harmonic motion. In one approach, the distance between the equilibrium position and the maximal displacement is divided into N N equal segments. In each segment, the mass moves with constant acceleration under the average of two forces at the ends of the segment. Summing up the time covering each segment and taking the large- N N limit reproduce one quarter of the period for simple harmonic motion. In the other approach, the time moving from the maximal displacement to the equilibrium position is divided into N N equal intervals. A recurrence relation for the displacement is obtained. The large- N N limit of its solution results in the same solution as that obtained from solving differential equation.

2
Preliminary Consideration

Suppose we have not learned calculus, in particular differential equations, but we still want to obtain the period for the SHM with whatever mathematics we have at hand. We do not expect too much, and we persuade ourselves to be satisfied with an approximate solution for the moment. But we aim to obtain a value that is as accurate as we can. Note this is not a numerical method paper, and we will develop this approach systematically to obtain the exact solution.

A spring-mass system consists of a mass
m
and a massless spring with spring constant
k
. One end of the spring is fixed and the other is attached with a mass
m
which lies on a frictionless horizontal surface, as is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Figure 1:
Setup of the Problem.

It is stretched to the right by distance
A
. It is then released from rest. We focus on the time for the motion from its initial position back to equilibrium, that is, the first quarter of a complete cycle. For later comparisons, we first give the exact value of one quarter of a period

\displaystyle T_{1/4}\,=\,{\pi\over 2}\sqrt{{m\over k}},

(1)

where the subscript indicates one quarter of a complete cycle.
In Sec.
3
we will rediscover this value from our approach.

The first exercise is to find the time for the mass to move from the maximum displacement
x=A
back to its equilibrium at
x=0
under the
average
of two forces exerted by the spring at these two positions. It will soon be seen that the notion of average, though seems primitive, may become quite powerful if executed appropriately.
The average force
\overline{F}
and the average acceleration
\overline{a}
are

\displaystyle\overline{F}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,A,\quad\overline{a}\,=\,{\overline{F}\over m}\,=\,{k\,A\over 2\,m}.

(2)

The kinematic formula for a motion with constant acceleration
a
, which relates the displacement
x
, the initial velocity
v_{0}
, and the time
t
, is

\displaystyle x\,=\,v_{0}\,t\,+\,{1\over 2}\,a\,t^{2}.

(3)

For our problem, the initial velocity is zero, the displacement is
A
, and the acceleration is
\overline{a}
. Applying Eq. (
3
) gives the time, denoted as
t_{1/4}^{(1)}
, to move from the maximal displacement back to equilibrium, that is,

\displaystyle t_{1/4}^{(1)}\,=\,\sqrt{{2\,A\over\overline{a}}}\,=\,2\sqrt{{m\over k}}.

(4)

It is larger than one quarter of the exact period
T_{1/4}
in Eq. (
1
). The ratio between them is

\displaystyle r_{1}\,\equiv\,{t_{1/4}^{(1)}\over T_{1/4}}\,=\,{2\over\pi/2}\,=\,{4\over\pi}\,\approx\,1.27.

(5)

Now we divide the maximum displacement into two equal segments and find the sum of time to cover each segment under the corresponding average force. We will find that the obtained approximate value is closer to the exact value
T_{1/4}
in Eq. (
1
).
In the first segment, the average force
\overline{F}_{1}
and the average acceleration
\overline{a}_{1}
are

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{1}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,(k\,A\,+\,{1\over 2}\,k\,A)\,=\,{3\over 4}\,k\,A,\quad\overline{a}_{1}\,=\,{3\,k\,A\over 4\,m}.

(6)

Applying the kinematic formula in Eq. (
3
) gives the time
t_{1}
to cover the first segment

\displaystyle t_{1}\,=\,\sqrt{{2\,\times\,{1\over 2}\,A\over\overline{a}_{1}}}\,=\,{2\over\sqrt{3}}\,\sqrt{{m\over k}}.

(7)

The velocity
v_{1}
at time
t_{1}
will be used to calculate the time for the second segment. It is obtained as

\displaystyle v_{1}\,=\,\overline{a}_{1}\,t_{1}\,=\,{\sqrt{3}\over 2}\,A\,\sqrt{{m\over k}}.

(8)

In the second segment, the average force
\overline{F}_{2}
and the corresponding average acceleration
\overline{a}_{2}
are

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{2}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,\times\,{1\over 2}\,k\,A\,=\,{1\over 4}\,k\,A,\quad\overline{a}_{2}\,=\,{k\,A\over 4\,m}.

(9)

Applying the formula in Eq. (
3
) yields the equation determining the time
t_{2}
covering the second segment, that is,

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\,A\,=\,v_{1}\,t_{2}\,+{1\over 2}\,\overline{a}_{2}\,t_{2}^{2},

(10)

where
v_{1}
is given in Eq. (
8
).
The solution for
t_{2}
is

\displaystyle t_{2}\,=\,2\,(2\,-\,\sqrt{3}\,)\sqrt{{m\over k}}.

(11)

The total time
t_{1/4}^{(2)}
to cover these two segments is

\displaystyle t_{1/4}^{(2)}\,=\,t_{1}\,+\,t_{2}\,=\,{2\over\sqrt{3}}\,\sqrt{{m\over k}}\,+\,2\,(2\,-\,\sqrt{3}\,)\sqrt{{m\over k}}\,=\,{4\over 3}(3-\sqrt{3})\sqrt{{m\over k}},

(12)

where the superscript indicates that the whole displacement
A
is divided into two equal segments.
The ratio between it and the exact value in Eq. (
1
) is

\displaystyle r_{2}\,\equiv\,{t_{1/4}^{(2)}\over T_{1/4}}\,=\,{4\,(3-\sqrt{3})/3\over\pi/2}\,=\,1.08\,<\,1.27\,=\,r_{1}.

(13)

It is closer to one quarter of the exact period. We expect that the difference from the exact value will decrease as the number of segments increases. In fact, as the number of segments goes to infinity, the exact value can be recovered. A full development along this direction is presented in the following section.

3
Divide the Distance into Equal Segments: Riemann Sum

The mass is released from rest at its maximal displacement. The goal is to find its position as a function of time
t
, that is,
x(t)
. Moreover, we denote the time for the mass to move from
x
back to its equilibrium as
\tau
. Clearly, the sum of
t
and
\tau
is equal to one quarter of a complete cycle denoted as
T^{\prime}_{1/4}
, that is,

\displaystyle t+\tau\,=\,T^{\prime}_{1/4}.

(14)

The value of
T^{\prime}_{1/4}
is to be solved from our approach. In the end, we will find that
T^{\prime}_{1/4}
is indeed equal to
T_{1/4}
given in Eq. (
1
).
Figure 2 helps to illustrate the idea.

Figure 2:
Illustration of the idea.

The following is the key to our approach: The distance
x
covered by the mass during the time interval
\tau
is divided into
N
equal segments. See Fig.
3
for details. The motion in each segment is considered to have constant acceleration, under the
average
of two forces exerted by the spring at the two ends of the segment under consideration. The quantity
\tau
can be obtained by calculating and summing over the time covering each segment. The sum appears to be the Riemann sum of a familiar integral which can be readily integrated, and the result is the same as that obtained from solving differential equation.

Figure 3:
Divide the distance into
N
equal segments.

The size of each segment is denoted as
\Delta x=x/N
. The equilibrium is denoted as
x_{0}=0
and the position of the right end of the
j
-th segment is
x_{j}=j\,\Delta x
. In particular,
x_{N}=x
. The time for the mass to cover the
j
-th segment is denoted as
\tau_{j}
and the total time
\tau
to cover the distance
x
is
\tau=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{j}
.

By mechanical energy conservation, the
speed

v_{j}
at position
x_{j}
satisfies

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\,m\,v_{j}^{2}\,+\,{1\over 2}\,k\,x_{j}^{2}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,A^{2},\quad j=0,1,\dots,N.

(15)

Then
v_{j}
is obtained as

\displaystyle v_{j}=\omega A\left(1-\delta^{2}\,j^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}},\quad\omega^{2}\equiv k/m,\quad\delta\equiv\Delta x/A.

(16)

Recursively,

\displaystyle v_{j-1}=\omega A\left(1-\delta^{2}\,(j-1)^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}.

(17)

Both
v_{j}
and
v_{j-1}
together with the average acceleration found below will be used to find the time to cover the
j
-th segment.

In the
j
-th segment, the mass moves with an average acceleration under the average force on it. Applying Newton’s second law and recalling
x_{j}=j\,\Delta x,\Delta x=x/N
and
\delta=\Delta x/A
we have

\displaystyle m\overline{a}_{j}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,(x_{j-1}+x_{j})\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,\Delta x\,(2j-1)\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,A\,\delta\,(2j-1),

obtaining

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{j}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,\omega^{2}\,A\,\delta\,(2j-1).

(18)

Note that
\omega\,A
in Eq. (
16
) and
\omega^{2}\,A
in Eq. (
18
) are actually the maximum speed and acceleration for SHM, respectively.

With Eqs. (
16
), (
17
), and (
18
), we are ready to calculate the time for the mass to cover the
j
-th segment. Note that
v_{j-1}>v_{j}
, and
\tau_{j}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\tau_{j}={v_{j-1}-v_{j}\over\overline{a}_{j}}={2\over\omega\,\delta}\,{\left(1-\delta^{2}\,(j-1)^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}-\left(1-\delta^{2}\,j^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}\over 2j-1}

\displaystyle={2\,\delta\over\omega}\,{1\over\left(1-\delta^{2}\,(j-1)^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}+\left(1-\delta^{2}\,j^{2}\right)^{{1\over 2}}}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\omega\,A\,\tau_{j}\,=\,{2\,\Delta x\over\left[1-(x_{j-1}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}+\left[1-(x_{j}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}}.

(19)

Note the fact that
x_{j-1}<x_{j}
(see Fig.
3
).
Then Eq. (
19
) gives

\displaystyle{\Delta x\over\left[1-(x_{j-1}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}}<\omega\,A\,\tau_{j}<{\Delta x\over\left[1-(x_{j}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}}.

(20)

Summing up
j
from 1 to
N
and recalling
\tau=\sum_{N=1}^{N}\tau_{j}
give

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\Delta x\over\left[1-(x_{j-1}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}}<\omega\,A\,\tau<\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\Delta x\over\left[1-(x_{j}/A)^{2}\right]^{{1\over 2}}}.

(21)

The left sum is the so-called lower sum and the right the upper sum in Riemann integral
[
9
,
21
]
. Then, as
N
goes to infinity, we have

\displaystyle\omega\,A\,\tau=\int_{0}^{x}{dx\over\sqrt{1-(x/A)^{2}}}=A\,\arcsin(x/A).

(22)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle x=A\sin\omega\tau.

(23)

Now we are ready to determine
T^{\prime}_{1/4}
in Eq. (
14
). When
x=A
, the time for the mass to arrive at its equilibrium is just
T^{\prime}_{1/4}
. Plugging them into Eq. (
23
) gives

\displaystyle\sin\omega\,T^{\prime}_{1/4}\,=\,1,\quad\omega\,T^{\prime}_{1/4}\,=\,\pi/2,\quad T^{\prime}_{1/4}\,=\,{\pi\over 2\,\omega}\,=\,{\pi\over 2}\,\sqrt{{m\over k}},

(24)

which is indeed
T_{1/4}
in Eq. (
1
).

According to Eq. (
14
),
\tau=T^{\prime}_{1/4}-t
, and Eq. (
23
) becomes

\displaystyle x=A\sin\omega\,(T^{\prime}_{1/4}-t)=A\,\sin\omega\,\left({\pi\over 2\,\omega}-t\right)=A\,\cos\omega\,t,

(25)

which is exactly the same as that obtained from solving the differential equation for SHM
[
7
,
9
]
.

4
Divide the Time into Equal Intervals: Recurrence Relation

Another approach is to divide the time
t
after the mass is released into
N
equal intervals. See Fig.
4
for details.
A recurrence relation for the position of the mass will be obtained. In the end, the large-
N
limit of its solution results in the same one as obtained from solving differential equations.

Figure 4:
Divide the time into
N
equal intervals.

To be specific, let
x_{i}
and
v_{i}
denote the position and speed of the mass at the end of the
i
-th time interval, respectively. The size of each time interval is
\Delta t=t/N
, and the distance covered in the
i
-th time interval is denoted as
\Delta x_{i}=x_{i-1}-x_{i}
, where
x_{i}
is the position of the mass at the end of the
i
-th time interval. Note that
x_{0}=A
,
x_{i}<x_{i-1}
, and
v_{i}>v_{i-1}
. The average speed during the
i
-th interval is

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\,(v_{i}+v_{i-1})={\Delta x_{i}\over\Delta t}.

(26)

The key step in our approach is the following. Within each time interval, the motion is considered to have constant acceleration, under the average of two elastic forces with spring extensions corresponding to the two ends of the time interval, that is,

\displaystyle v_{i}-v_{i-1}=\overline{a}_{i}\,\Delta t,

(27)

where
\overline{a}_{i}
is the average acceleration in the
i
-th time interval. It is obtained by applying Newton’s second law, that is,

\displaystyle m\overline{a}_{i}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,k\,(x_{i}+x_{i-1}),\quad\overline{a}_{i}={1\over 2}\,\omega^{2}\,(x_{i}+x_{i-1}),\quad\omega^{2}\equiv k/m.

(28)

Solving
v_{i}
and
v_{i-1}
from Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) gives

\displaystyle 2v_{i}=2\,{\Delta x_{i}\over\Delta t}+\overline{a}_{i}\,\Delta t,

(29)

\displaystyle 2v_{i-1}=2\,{\Delta x_{i}\over\Delta t}-\overline{a}_{i}\,\Delta t.

(30)

Recursively, from Eq. (
30
) we have an alternative expression for
v_{i}
, that is,

\displaystyle 2v_{i}=2\,{\Delta x_{i+1}\over\Delta t}-\overline{a}_{i+1}\,\Delta t.

(31)

Equating Eq. (
29
) with Eq. (
31
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta x_{i+1}-\Delta x_{i}={1\over 2}\,(\overline{a}_{i}+\overline{a}_{i+1})\,\Delta t^{2}.

(32)

With the expression for
\overline{a}_{i}
in Eq. (
28
), a recurrence relation for
x_{i}
is obtained as

\displaystyle(1+\alpha^{2})\,x_{i+1}-2\,(1-\alpha^{2})\,x_{i}+(1+\alpha^{2})\,x_{i-1}=0,\quad\alpha\equiv{1\over 2}\,\omega\Delta t.

(33)

To solve the recurrence relation in Eq. (
33
), we need two initial conditions. One is obviously the initial position of the mass, that is,
x_{0}=A
. The other can be found as follows. The distance
x_{1}
covered in the first time interval is

\displaystyle A-x_{1}={1\over 2}\,\overline{a}_{1}\,\Delta t^{2}={1\over 4}\,\omega^{2}(A+x_{1})\,\Delta t^{2}=\alpha^{2}\,(A+x_{1}),

(34)

where the second equality follows from Eq. (
28
) and the third from the definition of
\alpha
in Eq. (
33
). Then the two initial conditions are

\displaystyle x_{0}=A,\quad x_{1}={1-\alpha^{2}\over 1+\alpha^{2}}\,A.

(35)

Equation (
33
) with initial conditions in Eq. (
35
) can be solved by writing down
the characteristic equation
[
23
]
, that is,

\displaystyle(1+\alpha^{2})\,r^{2}-2\,(1-\alpha^{2})\,r+(1+\alpha^{2})=0.

(36)

Its two roots are

\displaystyle r_{+}={1+i\,\alpha\over 1-i\,\alpha},\quad r_{-}={1-i\,\alpha\over 1+i\,\alpha},\quad i^{2}=-1.

(37)

The general solution to the recurrence relation is

\displaystyle x_{k}=\lambda_{+}\,r_{+}^{k}\,+\,\lambda_{-}\,r_{-}^{k},\quad k=0,1,\cdots,N,

(38)

where
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
are constants to be determined by initial conditions in Eq. (
35
).
Substituting initial conditions in Eq. (
35
) into Eq. (
38
) gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}\,=\,\lambda_{-}\,=\,{1\over 2}\,A.

(39)

Finally, we obtain the solution to the recurrence relation in Eq. (
33
) with initial conditions in Eq. (
35
) as

\displaystyle x_{k}={1\over 2}\,A\,(r_{+}^{k}\,+\,r_{-}^{k}),\quad k=0,\cdots,N.

(40)

In particular, the position of the mass at time
t
, or equivalently, at the end of the
N
-th time interval is

\displaystyle x_{N}={1\over 2}\,A\,(r_{+}^{N}\,+\,r_{-}^{N}).

(41)

The solution to the SHM can be obtained by taking the large-
N
limit of Eq. (
41
). For this purpose, we write

\displaystyle r_{+}^{N}=\left({1+i\,\alpha\over 1-i\,\alpha}\right)^{N}={\left(1+{1\over 2}\,i\,\omega\,\Delta t\right)^{N}\over\left(1-{1\over 2}\,i\,\omega\,\Delta t\right)^{N}}={\left(1+{1\over 2}\,i\,\omega\,t\,{1\over N}\right)^{N}\over\left(1-{1\over 2}\,i\,\omega\,t\,{1\over N}\right)^{N}}.

(42)

In the large-
N
limit, the numerator becomes
e^{i\,\omega\,t/2}
and the denominator
e^{-i\,\omega\,t/2}
. Thereby, the limit for
r_{+}^{N}
is
\lim_{N\to\infty}r_{+}^{N}=e^{i\,\omega\,t}
and similarly
\lim_{N\to\infty}r_{-}^{N}=e^{-i\,\omega\,t}
.
Finally, the large-
N
limit of Eq. (
41
) is

\displaystyle x(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}x_{N}={1\over 2}\,A\,\left(e^{i\,\omega\,t}\,+\,e^{-i\,\omega\,t}\right)=A\,\cos\omega\,t,

(43)

which is exactly the same as that obtained from solving differential equation for SHM
[
7
,
9
]
.

5
Conclusion and Discussion

This paper presents two novel approaches to solve the classic problem of SHM.
The key idea is dividing the distance or the time into
N
equal segments or intervals, and the motion is approximated as one with constant acceleration. The large-
N
limit is taken to obtain exactly the same result as that from solving differential equations.

The value of our approaches lies in two aspects. On the one hand, they enable students to attack a problem at an early stage using elementary physics and mathematics rather than after they have learned enough calculus or even the theory of differential equation at a much later stage. On the other hand, even this paper is presented to students after they are able to solve differential equations, they can still learn quite some mathematics and problem solving skills from this paper. More importantly, by comparing various approaches, they have a good chance to further appreciate the power, simplicity, and elegance of calculus.
