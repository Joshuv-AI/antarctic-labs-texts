Well-balanced adaptive compact approximate Taylor methods for systems of balance laws Journal: Journal of L a T e X Templates
H. Carrillo a {}^{\hskip 1.42262pt\textrm{a}} , E. Macca b {}^{\hskip 1.42262pt\textrm{b}} , Carlos Parés c {}^{\hskip 1.42262pt\textrm{c}} , G. Russo d {}^{\hskip 1.42262pt\textrm{d}}

Abstract
Compact Approximate Taylor (CAT) methods for systems of conservation laws were introduced by Carrillo and Parés in 2019. These methods, based on a strategy that allows one to extend high-order Lax-Wendroff methods to nonlinear systems without using the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya procedure, have arbitrary even order of accuracy 2 ​ p 2p and use ( 2 ​ p + 1 ) (2p+1) -point stencils, where p p is an arbitrary positive integer. More recently in 2021 Carrillo, Macca, Parés, Russo and Zorío introduced a strategy to get rid of the spurious oscillations close to discontinuities produced by CAT methods. This strategy led to the so-called Adaptive CAT (ACAT) methods, in which the order of accuracy – and thus the width of the stencils – is adapted to the local smoothness of the solution. The goal of this paper is to extend CAT and ACAT methods to systems of balance laws. To do this, the source term is written as the derivative of its indefinite integral that is formally treated as a flux function. The well-balanced property of the methods is discussed and a variant that allows in principle to preserve any stationary solution is presented. The resulting methods are then applied to a number of systems going from a linear scalar conservation law to the 2D Euler equations with gravity, passing by the Burgers equations with source term and the 1D shallow water equations: the order and well-balanced properties are checked in several numerical tests.



Keywords:
High order fully-discrete schemes; High order reconstruction for systems of balance laws; Finite difference schemes; Well-balanced methods.

1
Introduction.

This paper deals with the design of high-order well-balanced methods for hyperbolic quasi-linear systems of balance laws

U_{t}+F(U)_{x}=S(U)H_{x},

(1.1)

with initial condition
U(x,0)=U_{0}(x)
, where
U:\mathbb{R}\times[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown vector field;
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the flux function;
S:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the source term; and
H:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a known function. PDE systems of this form appear in many fluid models in different contexts: shallow water models, multiphase flow models, gas dynamic, elastic wave equations, etc.

More precisely, we focus on the extension of high-order Lax-Wendroff methods to systems (
1.1
). For linear systems of conservation laws, these methods are based on Taylor expansions in time in which the time derivatives are transformed into spatial derivatives using the governing equations
[
22
,
32
,
15
]
. The discretization of the spatial derivatives by means of centered high-order differentiation formulas leads then to numerical methods with arbitrary order of accuracy.

The main difficulty to extend Lax-Wendroff methods to nonlinear problems comes from the transformation of time derivatives into spatial derivatives: the use of the equations through the Cauchy-Kovalesky (CK) procedure may be impractical from the computational point of view because it often requires extended symbolic calculus, ended up with inefficient codes. In the context of ADER methods introduced by Toro and collaborators (see
[
23
,
31
,
29
]
), this difficulty has been circumvented by replacing the CK procedure by local space-time problems that are solved with a Galerkin method: see
[
12
]
,
[
11
]
.

For systems of conservation laws

U_{t}+F(U)_{x}=0,

(1.2)

the CK procedure was avoided in
[
36
]
by computing time derivatives in a recursive way using high-order centered differentiation formulas combined with Taylor expansions in time. Nevertheless, if
(2p+1)
-point differentiation formulas are used to compute spatial and temporal derivatives, the resulting method use
(4p+1)
-point stencils while Lax-Wendroff methods for linear systems use
(2p+1)
-point ones.
In
[
4
]
a variant of these methods that use
(2p+1)
-point stencils, the so-called Compact Approximated Taylor methods (CAT), was introduced. CAT methods were shown to reduce to the standard high-order Lax-Wendroff methods when applied to linear problems. The technique used to reduce the length of the stencils increases the computational cost of a time step compared to the methods introduced in
[
36
]
: the Taylor expansions are computed locally, so that the total number of expansions needed to update the numerical solution is multiplied by
(2p+1)
. Nevertheless, CAT methods have better stability properties allowing larger time steps, thus compensating the extra cost per time iteration:
see
[
4
]
.

As it happens with high-order Lax-Wendroff methods for linear systems, their extensions to nonlinear problems produce spurious oscillations close to discontinuities and a strategy is needed to get rid of them. In
[
36
]
they were combined with WENO reconstructions to compute the first time derivatives. CAT methods were also combined with WENO in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
to avoid oscillations near discontinuities. Nevertheless this combination was not optimal: while the best CAT methods are those of even order, WENO methods have odd accuracy order. Moreover, the restriction on the time step imposed by WENO methods may spoil the advantages of the better stability property of CAT methods. To avoid this, in
[
3
]
a new version of CAT methods, the so-called Adaptive CAT (ACAT) methods, was introduced in which the oscillations near discontinuities are cured by adapting the order of accuracy – and thus the width of the stencils – to the smoothness of the solution. To do this, a class of smoothness indicators was introduced.

The main goal of this article is to extend ACAT methods to balance laws (
1.1
) and to its 2D counterpart. To do this, we follow the strategy in
[
14
]
(see also
[
10
]
) that consists in writing the source term as the derivative of the indefinite integral

\int_{-\infty}^{x}S(U(\sigma,t))H_{x}(\sigma)\,d\sigma,

that is then formally treated as a new flux function. Please note that, although this technique is applied here to derive ACAT methods for (
1.1
), it can be applied in principle to other extensions of high-order Lax-Wendroff methods to nonlinear problems using any technique to remove the spurious oscillations.

Systems of balance laws (
1.1
) have non-trivial stationary solutions that satisfy the ODE system

F(U)_{x}=S(U)H_{x}.

(1.3)

The objective of
well balanced schemes
is to preserve exactly or with enhanced accuracy some of these steady state solutions. In the context of shallow water
equations, Bermúdez and Vázquez-Cendón introduced in

[
1
]
the condition called
C-property
: a
scheme is said to satisfy this condition if it preserves the water at rest solutions.
Since then, many different numerical methods that satisfy this property have been introduced in the literature: see
[
2
]
,
[
33
]
and their references.
In the framework of finite difference methods, high-order schemes that satisfy the C-property were introduced in

[
6
]
and
[
35
]
: while the former was based on the formal writing of the system in conservative form based on the above mentioned technique, the latter relied on the expression of the source term as a function of variables that are constants for the stationary solutions to be preserved: see
[
34
]
.
A similar technique based on the reconstruction of equilibrium variables has been proposed in

[
28
]
.
In
[
24
]
a general technique to derive high-order well-balanced finite-difference methods for systems of balance-laws was introduced. The strategy, inspired on the general technique for finite volume methods discussed in
[
8
]
, was as follows: let
U_{i}
be the numerical approximation of the solution
U(x_{i},t)
at the node

x_{i}
at time
t
and let
U^{*}_{i}
be the stationary solution satisfying the Cauchy problem:

\begin{cases}&\displaystyle F(U^{*}_{i})_{x}=S(U^{*}_{i})H_{x},\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle U^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=U_{i}.\end{cases}

(1.4)

Then, if
U^{*}_{i}
can be found, one has trivially

S(U_{i})H_{x}(x_{i})=S(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i}))H_{x}(x_{i})=F(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i}))_{x}.

(1.5)

Therefore, locally the system of balance laws can be written in conservation form as follows

U_{t}+(F(U)-F(U^{*}_{i}(x)))_{x}=0.

The numerical method is obtained then by discretizating this conservative form by means of high-order WENO reconstruction of this extended ’flux’ function. We will follow this strategy here to derive well-balanced ACAT methods.

This paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, CAT methods for systems of conservation laws (
1.2
) are recalled. In Section 3, these methods are extended to systems of balance laws (
1.1
): after obtaining a first high-order version of the methods for systems of balance laws, the well-balanced property is discussed and a second version is introduced that preserves in principle any stationary solution. In Section 4 the technique introduced in
[
3
]

based on the order-adaption of the methods is recalled and it is applied to the 2 versions of CAT methods derived in Section 3, which leads to ACAT and well-balanced ACAT methods for systems of balance laws. Section 5 is devoted to the extension of the methods to 2D problems. In Section 6 the methods are applied to a number of problems: a 1D scalar balance law, Burgers equation with source term, the 1D shallow water model, and 2D Euler equations with gravitational potential. Finally, in the last section, we draw some conclusions.

2
Compact Approximate Taylor Methods for systems of conservation laws

First of all, let us recap the expression of CAT methods for a 1D system of conservation laws (
1.2
) with initial condition
U(x,0)=U_{0}(x)
. In the CAT
2P
method, a Taylor expansion in time is used to update the numerical solution:

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{(\Delta t)^{k}}{k!}U^{(k)}_{i},

(2.1)

where
\{x_{i}\}
are the nodes of a uniform mesh of step
\Delta x;

U_{i}^{n}
is an approximation of the value of the exact solution
U(x_{i},t_{n})
at time
t_{n}=n\Delta t
at
x_{i}
; and
U_{i}^{(k)}
is an approximation of
\partial^{k}_{t}U(x_{i},t_{n})
. Following the strategy introduced in
[
36
]
to derive Approximate Taylor methods, the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya procedure is avoided using the equality

\partial_{t}^{k}U=-\partial_{x}\partial_{t}^{k-1}F(U),

(2.2)

satisfied by smooth enough solutions.

In the Approximate Taylor methods introduced in
[
36
]
, global approximations that have to be computed only once at every node were used. Although the use of local approximations may increase the number of calculations, it allows us to prevent the increase of the stencil observed in the methods introduced in this reference: indeed, CAT
2P
methods are written in conservative form

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Bigl(F_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-F_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}\Bigr),

(2.3)

and only the values

U_{i-P+1}^{n},\dots,U_{i+P}^{n}

at the stencil
\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i+1/2}

\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i+1/2}=\{x_{i-P+1},\dots,x_{i+P}\},

are used to compute the numerical flux
F_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}
, so that the stencil used to update the solution at the
i
th node is
\{x_{i-P},\dots,x_{i+P}\}
, while in
[
36
]
the stencil for the same order of accuracy required
4P
points. More precisely, the time derivatives of the solution will be approximated by applying a formula of numerical differentiation for first order spatial derivatives to some approximations

{F}^{(k-1)}_{i,j}\approx\partial_{t}^{k-1}F(U)(x_{i+j},t_{n}),\quad j=-P+1,\dots,P

(2.4)

that will be computed using recursively Taylor expansions in time. In this notation,
P
is an arbitrary positive integer and
j
is a local coordinate in the stencil
\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i+1/2}\equiv\{-P+1,\dots,P\}

i.e.
F^{(k)}_{i,j}
is the approximation of
\partial_{t}^{k}F(U)
at time
t_{n}
at the node of local coordinate
j
of the stencil
\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i+1/2}
which is

x_{i+j}
. These approximations are local in the following sense: let us suppose that
i_{1}+j_{1}=i_{2}+j_{2}=l
,
i.e.
x_{l}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i_{1}+1/2}
and
\mathcal{S}^{P}_{i_{2}+1/2}
with local coordinates
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
respectively. Then
F^{(k)}_{i_{1},j_{1}}
and
F^{(k)}_{i_{2},j_{2}}
are, in general, two
different
approximations of
\partial_{t}^{k}F(U)(x_{l},t_{n})
.
Moreover, in
[
4
]
it has been shown that CAT
2P
methods reduce to the
2P
-order Lax-Wendroff method for linear systems. This implies the linear stability for these methods under the usual CFL requirement (which is not the case for the methods introduced in
[
36
]
).

Since numerical differentiation plays a fundamental role in the algorithm, before giving the expression of the numerical flux, let us introduce the notation to describe the formulas that will be used. Given two positive integers
p
,
k
, an index
i
, and a real number
q
, we will represent by

f^{(k)}(x_{i}+q\Delta x)\approx A^{k,q}_{p}(f_{i},\Delta x)=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{k}}\sum_{j=-p+1}^{p}\gamma^{k,q}_{p,j}f(x_{i+j}),\quad\quad k=0,\ldots,2p-1,

(2.5)

the interpolatory formula that approximates the
k
-th derivative of a function
f
at the point
x_{i}+q\Delta x
using its values at the
2p
points
x_{i-p+1},\dots,x_{i+p}
. For
k=0
,

f(x_{i}+q\Delta x,t_{n})=A^{0,q}_{p}(f_{i},\Delta x)=\sum_{j=-p+1}^{p}\gamma^{0,q}_{p,j}f(x_{i+j}),

represents the value at
x_{i}+q\Delta x
of the Lagrange polynomial that interpolates the values of
f
at the points
x_{i-p+1},\dots,x_{i+p}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The coefficients
\gamma^{k,q}_{p,j}
of the differentiation formulas can be recursively computed using the algorithm introduced in
[
13
]
. See also
[
4
]
.

The following notation

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{k}f(x_{i}+q\Delta x,t_{n})\approx A^{k,q}_{p}(f_{i,{*}}^{n},\Delta x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{k}}\sum_{j=-p+1}^{p}\gamma^{k,q}_{p,j}f_{i+j},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{k}f(x_{i},t_{n})\approx A^{k,0}_{p}(f^{n,{*}}_{i},\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{k}}\sum_{j=-p+1}^{p}\gamma^{k,q}_{p,j}f_{i+j},

(2.7)

will be used to indicate that the formula is applied to some approximations

of

and not to its exact point values

.
In cases where there are two or more indices, the symbol

we avoid the extra index

) and (

). For instance, the following approximations will be used in the algorithm adopted to compute the numerical fluxes:

\displaystyle\partial^{k}_{t}U(x_{i+j},t_{n})\approx-A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(F_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{1,j}_{P,l}F_{i,l}^{(k)},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\partial^{k}_{t}F(U)(x_{i+j},t_{n})\approx A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{k}}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{k,0}_{P,r}F_{i,j}^{k,n+r},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\partial^{k}_{t}F(U)\left(x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2},t_{n}\right)\approx A^{0,1/2}_{P}\left({F}_{i,{*}}^{(k)},\Delta x\right)=\sum_{j=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{0,1/2}_{P,j}{F}_{i,j}^{(k)}.

(2.10)

In (

from the local approximations

In (

Using this notation, the expression of the numerical flux is as follows:

F_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{(\Delta t)^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}({F}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x)=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{(\Delta t)^{k-1}}{k!}\sum_{j=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,j}^{0,\frac{1}{2}}F_{i,j}^{(k-1)},

1.

2.

(a)

(b)

where

is the

U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}=U_{i+j}^{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(\Delta t)^{m}}{m!}U_{i,j}^{(m)}.

(c)

Compute for all

F_{i,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr).

.

Observe that the computation of the numerical flux

For

Compact Approximate methods for systems of balance laws

3.1
CAT2P methods

The goal of this section is to extend CAT
2P
methods to systems of balance laws (
1.1
)
with initial condition
U(x,0)=U_{0}(x)
. Many authors have treated the source term as divergence of flux introducing an elliptic equation, see
[
26
,
27
]
. To avoid the introduction of a new equation we follow the strategy in
[
14
]
(see also
[
10
]
) in which the sistem (
1.1
) is first written in conservative form through the definition of a ‘combined flux’ formed by the sum of flux function
F
and the indefinite integral of the source term: more precisely, let us introduce the function
\mathcal{F}
given by

\mathcal{F}(U)(x,t)=F(U(x,t))-\int_{-\infty}^{x}S(U(\sigma,t))H_{x}(\sigma)\,d\sigma,

(3.1)

assuming that the integral is finite. Then, the equality

\mathcal{F}(U)_{x}=F(U)_{x}-S(U)H_{x},

allows one to write the system of balance laws (
1.1
) in the form

U_{t}+\mathcal{F}(U)_{x}=0.

(3.2)

Then, the formal expression of CAT
2P
is given by:

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Big(\mathfrak{F}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-\mathfrak{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}\Big),

(3.3)

where

\mathfrak{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A_{P}^{0,1/2}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)}\Big).

(3.4)

Here,
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{i,j}
are local approximation of
\partial_{t}^{(k)}\mathcal{F}(U)(x_{i+j},t_{n})
that are computed by adapting the algorithm described in Section
2
. Formally, the algorithm is as follows:

1.

Define

\displaystyle F_{i,j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle F(U_{i+j}^{n}),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle I_{i,j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i+j}}S(U(x,t_{n}))H_{x}(x)\,dx,\quad j=-P+1,\dots,P.

2.

For
k=1,\ldots,2P-1:

(a)

Compute for all
j=-P+1,\ldots,P

U_{i,j}^{(k)}=-A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(F_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)+A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr).

(b)

Define for all
j,r=-P+1,\ldots,P

\displaystyle I_{i,j}^{n+r}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i+j}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle F_{i,j}^{k,n+r}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle F\Bigl(U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}\Bigr),

where
U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}
is the
approximation of
U(x_{i+j},t_{n+r})
given by the Taylor expansion in time:

U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}=U_{i+j}^{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(\Delta t)^{m}}{m!}U_{i,j}^{(m)}.

(c)

Compute for all
j=-P+1,\ldots,P

F_{i,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),\quad I_{i,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(I_{i,j}^{{*}},\Delta t\Bigr).

The ’numerical fluxes’ are then defined by:

\mathfrak{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}=F^{P}_{i+1/2}-I^{P}_{i+1/2}

(3.5)

where

\displaystyle F^{P}_{i+1/2}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(F_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x),

(3.6)

\displaystyle I^{P}_{i+1/2}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(I_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x).

(3.7)

This algorithm is formal, since it requires the computation of integrals that depend on the exact solution in intervals of the form
(-\infty,x_{i+j}]
. In order to be computationally implementable, let us first rewrite it using only integrals in bounded intervals. To do that, the key point is the following chain of equalities:

\displaystyle A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}I_{i,s}^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}A_{P}^{k-1,0}\Bigl(I_{i,s}^{{*}},\Delta t\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta t^{k-1}}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}I_{i,s}^{k,n+r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta t^{k-1}}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i+s}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta t^{k-1}}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i+s}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i-P+1}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta t^{k-1}}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}\int_{x_{i-P+1}}^{x_{i+s}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx

where the identity

\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{1,j}_{P,s}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P,

has been used: remember that an interpolatory formula of numerical differentiation
that uses
2P
points is exact at least for polynomials of degree
2P-1
and thus it is exact for constant polynomials. Therefore, if the formula is applied to the constant polynomial
p\equiv 1
, we have

0=p^{\prime}(x_{i+j})=A^{1,j}_{P}(p_{i,{*}},\Delta x)=\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{1,j}_{P,s}p(x_{i+s})=\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma^{1,j}_{P,s}.

By introducing the notation

\displaystyle I_{i,j,l}^{m}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i+j}}^{x_{i+l}}S(U(x,t_{m}))H_{x}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle I_{i,j,l}^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle A_{P}^{k-1,0}\Bigl(I_{i,j,l}^{{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),

we obtain

\displaystyle A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta t^{k-1}}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,s}^{1,j}I_{i,-P+1,s}^{n+r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x}\sum_{r=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,r}^{k-1,0}I_{i,-P+1,s}^{(k-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i,-P+1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr),

where only integrals in intervals of the form
[x_{i-P+1},x_{i+s}]
appear.
Observe that
I_{i-P+1,i-P+1}^{n+r}=0
for all
k
.

Concerning the expression of the numerical method, observe that:

\displaystyle I^{P}_{i+1/2}-I^{P}_{i-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}\left(A^{0,1/2}_{P}(I_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x)-A^{0,1/2}_{P}(I_{i-1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}\sum_{j=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,j}^{0,1/2}\left(I_{i,j}^{(k-1)}-I_{i-1,j}^{(k-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}\sum_{j=-P+1}^{P}\gamma_{P,j}^{0,1/2}A_{P}^{k-1,0}\left(I_{i,j}^{{*}}-I_{i-1,j}^{{*}},\Delta t\right).

Since

I_{i,j}^{n+r}-I_{i-1,j}^{n+r}=I_{i,j-1,j}^{n+r}=\int_{x_{i+j-1}}^{x_{i+j}}S(U(x,t_{n+r}))H_{x}(x)\,dx,

(3.8)

if we define

\mathcal{I}_{i,j}^{(k-1)}=A_{P}^{k-1,0}\Bigl({I}_{i,j-1,j}^{{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),

(3.9)

we have

I^{P}_{i+1/2}-I^{P}_{i-1/2}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(\mathcal{I}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x),

so that (
3.3
) can be written in equivalent form

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Big(F_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}+S_{i}^{P}\Big),

(3.10)

where

S_{i}^{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(\mathcal{I}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x).

(3.11)

Observe that only integrals (
3.8
) in intervals of length
\Delta x
appear in the expression of the numerical source term.

Finally, in order to have an implementable algorithm, all the integrals appearing in it are approximated using quadrature formulas combined with the approximations
U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}
of the exact solution that are available at every stage. To do this, given
i
and
j=-P+2,\dots,P
, we consider at
[x_{i+j-1},x_{i+j}]
the interpolatory quadrature formula

\int_{x_{i+j-1}}^{x_{i+j}}f(x)\,dx\approx\Delta x\sum_{s=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,s}f(x_{i+s})

whose nodes are
x_{i+s}
,
s=-P+1,\dots,P
.
This formula will be used to approximate the integrals appearing at the
k
-th stage of the algorithm as follows: given two indices
j_{1}<j_{2}

\displaystyle I_{i,j_{1},j_{2}}^{m}\approx\widetilde{I}^{k,m}_{i,j_{1},j_{2}}:=\Delta x\sum_{s=j_{1}+1}^{j_{2}}\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,s}_{P,l}S(U^{k,m}_{i,l})H_{x}(x_{i+l}).

Taking into account these approximations of the integral terms, the algorithm is finally as follows, see Figure
2.1
for the case
P=2:

1.

Compute

\displaystyle F_{i,j}^{(0)}=F(U_{i+j}^{n}),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i,j-1,j}^{(0)}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,l}S(U^{n}_{i+l})H_{x}(x_{i+l}),\quad j=-P+2,\dots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i,-P+1,-P+1}^{(0)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i,-P+1,j}^{(0)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}_{i,s-1,s}^{(0)},\quad j=-P+2,\dots,P.

2.

For
k=1,\ldots,2P-1:

(a)

Compute for all
j=-P+1,\ldots,P

U_{i,j}^{(k)}=-\mathcal{A}_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(F_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)+\mathcal{A}_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i,-P+1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr).

(b)

Compute for all
j,r=-P+1,\dots,P

U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}=U_{i+j}^{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(r\Delta t)^{m}}{m!}U_{i,j}^{(m)}.

(c)

Compute for all
j,r=-P+1,\ldots,P

F_{i,j}^{k,n+r}=F\Bigl(U_{i,j}^{k,n+r}\Bigr),

(d)

Compute for all
r=-P+1,\dots,P
,
j=-P+2,\dots,P

\widetilde{I}^{k,n+r}_{i,j-1,j}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,l}S(U^{k,n+r}_{i,l})H_{x}(x_{i+l}).

(e)

Compute for all
j=-P+2,\dots,P

\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i,j-1,j}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(\widetilde{I}_{i,j-1,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr).

(f)

Compute

\displaystyle F_{i,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i,-P+1,-P+1}^{(k)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i,-P+1,j}^{(k)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i,s-1,s}\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P.

Once the algorithm has been executed, the integrals already computed can be used to approximate the source term as follows:

1.

For
k=1,\dots,2P
define

\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i,j}^{(k-1)}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{I}^{(k-1)}_{i-1,j,j+1}&\text{if $j=-P+1,\ldots,0$;}\\
&\\
\widetilde{I}^{(k-1)}_{i,j-1,j}&\text{if $j=1,\dots,P$. }\end{cases}

2.

Compute

\widetilde{S}_{i}^{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x).

Observe that the first
P
integral terms
\mathcal{I}_{i,j}^{(k-1)}
appearing in the expression of the numerical source term (
3.11
) are approximated with the values
I_{i-1,j,j+1}^{(k-1)}
, used to compute the flux at the intercell
i-1/2
, and the
P
last ones by
I_{i,j-1,j}^{(k-1)}
, used to compute the flux at the intercell

i+1/2
.

The final expression of the numerical method is then

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Big(F_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}+\widetilde{S}_{i}^{P}\Big),

(3.12)

where
{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}
is given by (
3.6
).

3.2
CAT2 for system of balance laws

Let us illustrate the above numerical method in the easiest case
P=1
. In this case, the quadrature formula used to compute integrals in intervals of length
\Delta x
is the trapezoidal rule:

\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}f(x)\,dx\approx\frac{\Delta x}{2}\Bigl(f(x_{i})+f(x_{i+1})\Bigr).

The numerical method is then as follows: for every
i

1.

Compute

U_{i,j}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(F(U_{i+1}^{n})-F(U_{i}^{n})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(S(U_{i}^{n})H_{x}(x_{i})+S(U_{i+1}^{n})H_{x}(x_{i+1})\right),\quad j=0,1.

Observe that, there is no dependence on
j
in the right term because the derivative are computed in the same way.

2.

Compute

U_{i,j}^{1,n+1}=U_{i+j}^{n}+\Delta t\;U_{i,j}^{(1)},\quad j=0,1.

Then, define

{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{1}:=\frac{1}{4}\left(F(U_{i}^{n})+F(U_{i+1}^{n})+F(U_{i,0}^{1,n+1})+F(U_{i,1}^{1,n+1})\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta x}{8}\Bigl((S(U_{i-1}^{n})+S(U_{i-1,0}^{1,n+1}))H_{x}(x_{i-1})+(S(U_{i}^{n})+S(U_{i-1,1}^{1,n+1}))H_{x}(x_{i})

(3.14)

\displaystyle+(S(U_{i}^{n})+S(U_{i,0}^{1,n+1}))H_{x}(x_{i})+(S(U_{i+1}^{n})+S(U_{i,1}^{1,n+1}))H_{x}(x_{i+1})\Bigr).

The numerical method is then (
3.12
) with

.

The CAT procedure applied to systems of balance law introduces spurious oscillations near discontinuity points. In Section

4
Well-balanced CAT2P for systems of balance laws

4.1
WBCAT2P methods

The goal of this section is to derive a well-balanced version of the CAT
2P
methods introduced in the previous section. The idea is as follows: let us suppose that the initial condition is given by

U(x,0)=U^{*}(x),

were
U^{*}
is a stationary solution of (
1.1
). Let us introduce then
the function
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(U)(x,t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(U)(x,t))-\mathcal{F}(U^{*})(x)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(U(x,t))-F(U^{*}(x))-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\Bigl(S(U(\sigma,t))-S(U^{*}(\sigma))\Bigr)H_{x}(\sigma)d\sigma.

(4.1)

Hence, observing that

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(U)_{x}=F(U)_{x}-F(U^{*})_{x}-(S(U)-S(U^{*}))H_{x}=F(U)_{x}-S(U)H_{x},

the system of balance laws (
1.1
) can be formally written in the form

U_{t}+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(U)_{x}=0.

(4.2)

Obviously
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(U^{*})=0
, therefore a numerical method based on the discretization of this conservative form
is expected to preserve
U^{*}
exactly.

In practice, this strategy is applied as follows: once the approximation
U_{i}^{n}
has been obtained, we consider the stationary solution
U^{*}_{i}
that satisfies

U^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=U_{i}^{n},

i.e.
U^{*}_{i}
solves the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}F(U)_{x}=S(U)H_{x}\\
U(x_{i})=U_{i}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

(4.3)

Let us assume for simplicity that this Cauchy problem has a unique solution that is explicitly known. Then, the system of balance laws is rewritten in the form

U_{t}+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(U)_{x}=0.

(4.4)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(U)(x,t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(U)(x,t))-\mathcal{F}(U^{*}_{i})(x)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(U(x,t))-F(U^{*}_{i}(x))-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\Bigl(S(U(\sigma,t))-S(U^{*}_{i}(\sigma))\Bigr)H_{x}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

(4.5)

and the CAT
2P
method is then applied:

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Big(\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i,i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i,i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}\Big),

(4.6)

where,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i,i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A_{P}^{0,1/2}\Big(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i;i,{*}}^{(k-1)}\Big),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i,i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A_{P}^{0,1/2}\Big(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i;i-1,{*}}^{(k-1)}\Big).

(4.8)

Here
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{(k)}_{i;l,j}
is an approximation of
\partial_{t}^{(k)}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(U)(x_{l+j},t_{n}).
Observe that, in this case, two numerical fluxes have to be computed at every inter-cell
x_{i+1/2}
,
\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i,i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}
and

\mathfrak{\tilde{F}}_{i+1,i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}
, whose computation are based respectively on the stationary solutions
U^{*}_{i}

(that satisfies
U^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=U_{i}^{n}
) and
U^{*}_{i+1}
(that satisfies
U^{*}_{i+1}(x_{i+1})=U_{i+1}^{n}
).

Observe that, if the initial condition
U_{0}
is a stationary solution, then at time
t=0
,

U^{*}_{i}=U_{0}
for all
i
, so that

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(U_{0})=\mathcal{F}(U_{0})-\mathcal{F}(U^{*}_{i})=\mathcal{F}(U_{0})-\mathcal{F}(U_{0})=0,\quad\forall i,

and the numerical method is expected to preserve the initial condition.

The algorithm is then as follows: for every
i

1.

Compute the solution
U^{*}_{i}(x)
of the Cauchy problem (
4.3
).

2.

Compute

\displaystyle F_{i;i,j}^{(0)}=F(U_{i+j}^{n})-F(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i+j})),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle F_{i;i-1,j}^{(0)}=F(U_{i-1+j}^{n})-F(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i-1+j})),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,j-1,j}^{(0)}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,l}\left(S(U^{n}_{i+l})-S(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i+l}))\right)H_{x}(x_{i+l}),\quad j=-P+2,\dots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,-P+1}^{(0)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,j}^{(0)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}_{i;i,s-1,s}^{(0)},\quad j=-P+3,\dots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}^{(0)}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i-1,j}_{P,l}\left(S(U^{n}_{i-1+l})-S(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i-1+l}))\right)H_{x}(x_{i-1+l}),\quad j=-P+3,\dots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,-P+1}^{(0)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,j}^{(0)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,s-1,s}^{(0)},\quad j=-P+2,\dots,P.

3.

For
k=1,\ldots,2P-1:

(a)

Compute for all
j=-P+1,\ldots,P

\displaystyle U_{i;i,j}^{(k)}=-A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(F_{i;i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)+A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i;i,-P+1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr);

\displaystyle U_{i;i-1,j}^{(k)}=-A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(F_{i;i-1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr)+A_{P}^{1,j}\Bigl(I_{i;i-1,-P+1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x\Bigr).

(b)

Compute for all
j,r=-P+1,\dots,P

\displaystyle U_{i;i,j}^{k,n+r}=U_{i+j}^{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(\Delta t)^{m}}{m!}U_{i;i,j}^{(m)},

\displaystyle U_{i;i-1,j}^{k,n+r}=U_{i+j-1}^{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(\Delta t)^{m}}{m!}U_{i;i-1,j}^{(m)}.

(c)

Compute for all
j,r=-P+1,\ldots,P

F_{i;i,j}^{k,n+r}=F\Bigl(U_{i;i,j}^{k,n+r}\Bigr),\quad F_{i;i-1,j}^{k,n+r}=F\Bigl(U_{i;i-1,j}^{k,n+r}\Bigr).

(d)

Compute for all
r=-P+1,\dots,P
,
j=-P+2,\dots,P

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}^{k,n+r}_{i;i,j-1,j}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,l}S(U^{k,n+r}_{i;i,l})H_{x}(x_{i+l}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}^{k,n+r}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i-1,j}_{P,l}S(U^{k,n+r}_{i;i-1,l})H_{x}(x_{i-1+l}).

(e)

Compute for all
j=-P+2,\dots,P

\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i;i,j-1,j}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(\widetilde{I}_{i;i,j-1,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),\quad\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr).

(f)

Compute

\displaystyle F_{i;i,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i;i,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,-P+1}^{(k)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,j}^{(k)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i;i,s-1,s}\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle F_{i;i-1,j}^{(k)}=A_{P}^{k,0}\Bigl(F_{i;i-1,j}^{k,{*}},\Delta t\Bigr),\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,-P+1}^{(k)}=0;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,j}^{(k)}=\sum_{s=-P+2}^{j}\widetilde{I}^{(k)}_{i;i-1,s-1,s}\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P.

Once the algorithm has been executed, the integrals already computed can be used to approximate the source term as follows:

1.

For
k=1,\dots,2P
define

\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i,j}^{(k-1)}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{I}^{(k-1)}_{i;i-1,j,j+1}&\text{if $j=-P+1,\ldots,0$;}\\
&\\
\widetilde{I}^{(k-1)}_{i;i,j-1,j}&\text{if $j=1,\dots,P$. }\end{cases}

2.

Compute

\widetilde{S}_{i}^{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x).

(4.9)

The final expression of the numerical method is then

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Big(F_{i;i-\frac{1}{2}}^{P}-{F}_{i;i+\frac{1}{2}}^{P}+\widetilde{S}_{i}^{P}\Big),

(4.10)

where
{F}_{i,i\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{P}
are given by

\displaystyle F^{P}_{i;i+1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(F_{i;i,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x),

(4.11)

\displaystyle F^{P}_{i;i-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2P}\frac{\Delta t^{k-1}}{k!}A^{0,1/2}_{P}(F_{i;i-1,{*}}^{(k-1)},\Delta x).

(4.12)

Remark 4.1
.

Apparently scheme
4.10
is not exactly conservative because the fluxes at the two sides of a cell edge may be different, since they depend on the local reconstruction. However, the schemes are exactly well balanced (see Theorem
4.1
below), and as a consequence, in case the source is identically zero, the numerical fluxes at the two sides of a cell edges are equal, and the scheme is conservative.

Remark 4.2
.

Observe that this algorithm can be used to update the solution at the point
x_{i}
at time
t_{n}
only if the Cauchy problem (
4.3
) has a solution that is defined in the cells of the stencils
S^{P}_{i\pm 1/2}
whose analytic expression is known.
Therefore:

1.

If (
4.3
) has no solution, the CAT
2P
method will be used instead. Please note that this choice does not spoil the well-balanced character of the numerical method: in this case, the cell values in the stencil cannot be the point values of a stationary solution (otherwise there would be at least one solution of (
4.3
)) and thus there is no local equilibrium to preserve.

2.

If (
4.3
) has more than one solution, a criterion is needed to select one of them: this is the case for the shallow water system that will be discussed in Section
4.4
.

3.

If (
4.3
) has a solution defined in the stencils but it is not possible to find its expression by analytic procedures, it is possible to apply an ODE solver to approximate it, like it has been done in
[
9
]
for finite-volume methods. In all the problems considered in Section
7
the analytic expression of the stationary solutions is available either in explicit or implicit form.

4.2
Well-balanced property

Numerical method (
4.10
) is fully well-balanced in the following sense:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
U^{*}
be a continuous stationary solution of (
1.1
). Then, if numerical method (
4.10
) is applied to the initial condition

U^{0}_{i}=U^{*}(x_{i}),\quad\forall i,

then

U_{i}^{n}=U_{i}^{0},\quad\forall i,n.

Proof:
The proof is based on induction on the order of the proximate time derivative. Observe first that
U^{*}
solves any Cauchy problem (
4.3
) for
n=0
. Therefore, at the first step the solution of (
4.3
) is given by

U^{*}_{i}=U^{*},\quad\forall i.

Therefore, for every
i
:

\displaystyle F_{i;i,j}^{(0)}=F_{i;i-1,j}^{(0)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,j-1,j}^{(0)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}^{(0)}=0,\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,j}^{(0)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,j}^{(0)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\dots,P;

and thus

U_{i;i,j}^{(1)}=U_{i;i-1,j}^{(1)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P.

As a consequence:

\displaystyle U_{i;i,j}^{1,r}=U_{i+j}^{0},\quad F_{i;i,j}^{1,r}=F\Bigl(U_{i+j}^{0}\Bigr),\quad j,r=-P+1,\dots,P;

\displaystyle U_{i;i-1,j}^{1,r}=U_{i-1+j}^{0},\quad F_{i;i-1,j}^{1,r}=F\Bigl(U_{i-1+j}^{0}\Bigr),\quad j,r=-P+1,\dots,P

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}^{1,r}_{i;i,j-1,j}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i,j}_{P,l}S(U^{0}_{i+l})H_{x}(x_{i+l}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}^{1,r}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}=\Delta x\sum_{l=-P+1}^{P}a^{i-1,j}_{P,l}S(U^{0}_{i-1+l})H_{x}(x_{i-1+l}).

Notice that the values of all these quantities do not depend on
r
. Therefore, when numerical differentiation in time is applied we obtain:

\displaystyle F_{i;i,j}^{(1)}=F_{i;i-1,j}^{(1)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,j-1,j}^{(1)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}^{(1)}=0,\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,j}^{(1)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,j}^{(1)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P.

Therefore

U_{i;i,j}^{(2)}=U_{i;i-1,j}^{(2)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P.

Repeating the reasoning we obtain

\displaystyle F_{i;i,j}^{(k)}=F_{i;i-1,j}^{(k)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P,\quad k=0,\dots,2P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,j-1,j}^{(k)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,j-1,j}^{(k)}=0,\quad j=-P+2,\ldots,P,\quad k=0,\dots,2P;

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{i;i,-P+1,j}^{(1)}=\widetilde{I}_{i;i-1,-P+1,j}^{(1)}=0,\quad j=-P+1,\ldots,P,\quad k=0,\dots,2P.

Therefore

F^{P}_{i;i+1/2}=F^{P}_{i,i-1/2}=S^{P}_{i+1/2}=0,

and we obtain

U^{1}_{i}=U^{0}_{i},\quad\forall i,

as we wanted to prove.

4.4
CAT2P methods that preserve a family of stationary solutions

The strategy described in Subsection
4.1
can be easily adapted to obtain schemes that only preserve a prescribed set of stationary solutions: this would be the case if, for instance, the set to be preserved is a
k
-parameter family of stationary solutions,

U^{*}(x;C_{1},\dots,C_{k}),

with
k<d
where
d
is the number of variables. If it is the case, instead of looking for a solution of (
4.3
), one looks for a solution of the following nonlinear system:

Find
C^{i}_{1},\dots,C^{i}_{k}
such that:

u_{j_{l}}^{*}(x_{i};C^{i}_{1},\dots,C^{i}_{k})=u_{i,j_{l}},\quad l=1,\dots,k,

(4.16)

where
u^{*}_{j}
,
u_{i,j}
denote respectively the
j
-th component of
U^{*}
and
U_{i}
and
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\}
is a set of
k
indices that is
predetermined in order to have the same number of unknowns and equations in (
4.16
).
These indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
are chosen so that systems of equations (
4.16
) have a unique solution, if it is possible.
Once the problem has been solved, the numerical fluxes and source terms are computed as in Section
4.1
with the choice

U^{*}_{i}(x)=U^{*}(x,C^{i}_{1},\dots,C^{i}_{k}).

5
Adaptive CAT2P for systems of balance law

Despite the fact that Compact Approximate Taylor (CAT) schemes are linearly stable in the
L^{2}
sense under the usual CFL condition, spurious oscillations may appear next to a discontinuity of the solution, as it happens for the Lax-Wendroff method: see
[
4
]
. In order to get rid of them, we consider here the shock-capturing technique introduced in
[
3
]
based on a family of high-order smoothness indicators. The idea is as follows: once the approximations at time
t^{n}
have been computed, the candidate stencils
to compute
\mathfrak{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
are

\mathcal{S}^{p}_{i+1/2}=\{x_{i-p+1},\ldots,x_{i+p}\},\quad p=1,\ldots,P.

The selected stencil is the one with maximal length among those in which the solution at time
t^{n}
is smooth, according to some smoothness indicators
\psi^{p}_{i+1/2}
for
p=1,\dots,P
.
If a discontinuity is detected in the stencil
\mathcal{S}^{1}_{i+1/2}
a robust first-order numerical method is used. The ingredients of this strategy are described below:

5.1
First-order numerical method

As first-order robust scheme to be combined with CAT2P methods for balance laws, we select the Lax-Friedrichs method applied to (
3.2
) which leads to the formal expression:

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Bigl(F^{LF}_{i-1/2}-F^{LF}_{i+1/2}+\widetilde{S}^{LF}_{i}\Bigr)

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle F^{LF}_{i+1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(F(U^{n}_{i})+F(U^{n}_{i+1})\right)-\frac{\Delta x}{2\Delta t}\left(U^{n}_{i+1}-U^{n}_{i}\right),

(5.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}^{LF}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta xS(U_{i}^{n})H_{x}(x_{i}),

(5.3)

where the mid-point rule has been used to approximate the integral corresponding to the source term.

In the case of the WBCAT2
P
methods, the Lax-Friedrichs method is formally applied to (
4.4
) and the mid-point formula is again used to compute the integral terms, which leads again to a method of the form (
5.1
) where
F^{LF}_{i+1/2}
is again the standard Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux (
5.2
) but now

\widetilde{S}^{LF}_{i}={F}^{LF,*}_{i;i+1/2}-{F}^{LF,*}_{i;i-1/2},

(5.4)

where

{F}^{LF,*}_{i;i+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(F(U_{i}^{*}(x_{i}))+F(U_{i}^{*}(x_{i+1}))\right)-\frac{\Delta x}{2\Delta t}\left(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i+1})-U^{*}_{i}(x_{i})\right)

(5.5)

and
U^{*}_{i}
is the stationary solution that satisfies

U^{*}_{i}(x_{i})=U_{i}^{n}.

(
5.4
) is a consistent approximation of the integral of the source term, since

{F}^{LF,*}_{i;i+1/2}-{F}^{LF,*}_{i;i-1/2}\approx\Delta xF(U^{*})_{x}(x_{i})=\Delta xS(U^{*}_{i}(x_{i}))H_{x}(x_{i})=\Delta xS(U^{n}_{i})H_{x}(x_{i}).

On the other hand, the well-balanced property of the method can be trivially checked.

5.2
ACAT2 and WBACAT2 methods

The expression of the ACAT2 numerical method is based on a flux limiter (see
[
21
,
22
,
32
]
). Its expression is as follows:

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Bigl(F^{*}_{i-1/2}-F^{*}_{i+1/2}+\widetilde{S}^{*}_{i}\Bigr)

(5.6)

where

\displaystyle{F}^{*}_{i\pm 1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi^{1}_{i}\,{F}^{1}_{i\pm 1/2}+(1-\varphi^{1}_{i})\,{F}^{LF}_{i\pm 1/2},

(5.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi^{1}_{i}\widetilde{S}^{1}_{i}+(1-\varphi^{1}_{i})\widetilde{S}^{LF}_{i},

(5.8)

where
{F}^{1}_{i\pm 1/2}
and
\widetilde{S}^{1}
are given by (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) respectively;
F^{LF}_{i\pm 1/2}
and
\widetilde{S}^{LF}_{i}
are given by (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) respectively;

\varphi^{1}_{i}=\min(\varphi^{1}_{i-1/2},\varphi^{1}_{i+1/2}),

where
\varphi^{1}_{i+1/2}
is computed by a flux limiter, and has the property

\varphi^{1}_{i+1/2}\approx\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\{U_{i-1}^{n},\dots,U_{i+2}^{n}\}$ are 'smooth';}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.9)

For scalar problems,
standard flux limiter functions
\varphi^{1}(r)
, such as minmod, superbee, van Leer
[
30
,
19
]
, may be used:

\varphi_{i+1/2}^{1}=\varphi^{1}(r_{i+1/2}),

(5.10)

where

r_{i+1/2}=\frac{\Delta_{\rm upw}}{\Delta_{\rm loc}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle r^{-}_{i+1/2}:=\frac{u^{n}_{i}-u^{n}_{i-1}}{u^{n}_{i+1}-u^{n}_{i}}&\mbox{if }a_{i+1/2}>0,\\
\displaystyle r^{+}_{i+1/2}:=\frac{u^{n}_{i+2}-u^{n}_{i+1}}{u^{n}_{i+1}-u^{n}_{i}}&\mbox{if }a_{i+1/2}\leq 0;\end{array}\right.

(5.11)

and
a_{i+1/2}
is an estimate of the wave speed such as for instance Roe’s intermediate speed:

a_{i+1/2}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{f(u^{n}_{i+1})-f(u^{n}_{i})}{u^{n}_{i+1}-u^{n}_{i}}&\text{ if $|u^{n}_{i}-u^{n}_{i+1}|>{\rm tol};$}\\
f^{\prime}(u^{n}_{i})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

where tol is a given small tolerance.
An alternative that avoids the computation of an intermediate speed was introduced in
[
32
]
and it consists on defining

\varphi^{1}_{i+1/2}=\min(\varphi^{1}(r_{i+1/2}^{+}),\varphi^{1}(r^{-}_{i+1/2})).

(5.12)

This strategy can be easily extended to systems by computing the flux limiter component by component.

The well-balanced version of the ACAT2 method has the form

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Bigl(F^{*}_{i;i-1/2}-F^{*}_{i;i+1/2}+\widetilde{S}^{*}_{i}\Bigr)

(5.13)

where

\displaystyle{F}^{*}_{i;i\pm 1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi^{1}_{i}\,{F}^{1}_{i;i\pm 1/2}+(1-\varphi^{1}_{i})\,{F}^{LF}_{i;i\pm 1/2},

(5.14)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi^{1}_{i}\widetilde{S}^{1}_{i}+(1-\varphi^{1}_{i})\widetilde{S}^{LF,*}_{i}=\varphi^{1}_{i}\widetilde{S}^{1}_{i}-(1-\varphi^{1}_{i})\left(F^{LF,*}_{i;i+\frac{1}{2}}-F^{LF,*}_{i;i-\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(5.15)

where
{F}^{1}_{i;i\pm 1/2}
are given by (
4
)-(
4
);
F^{LF,*}_{i;i\pm 1/2}
is given by (
5.5
); and
\widetilde{S}_{i}^{1}
is given by (
4.15
).

5.3
Smoothness Indicators

The smoothness indicators introduced in
[
3
]
will be used here: given a set of the point values
f_{j}
of a function

f
in the nodes of the stencil
S^{p}_{i+1/2}
,
p\geq 2
we define
\psi_{i+1/2}^{p}
as follows: first define the lateral weights
I_{p,L}
and
I_{p,R}
as

I_{p,L}:=\sum_{j=-p+1}^{-1}(f_{i+1+j}-f_{i+j})^{2}+\varepsilon,\quad I_{p,R}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(f_{i+1+j}-f_{i+j})^{2}+\varepsilon,

(5.16)

where
\varepsilon
is a small quantity that is added to prevent the lateral weights to vanish when the function is constant. Next, compute the half harmonic mean

I_{p}=\frac{I_{p,L}I_{p,R}}{I_{p,L}+I_{p,R}}.

(5.17)

Figure 5.1:
Left and right part to compute the high order smoothness indicators where

Finally, we define the high order smoothness indicator of the stencil
S_{p}
by

\psi_{i+1/2}^{p}:=\left(\frac{I_{p}}{I_{p}+\tau_{p}}\right),

(5.18)

where

\displaystyle\tau_{p}:=

\displaystyle\left(\Delta^{2p-1}_{i-p+1}f\right)^{2}.

(5.19)

The script
\Delta^{2p-1}_{i-p+1}f
represents the undivided difference of

\{f_{i-p+1},\dots,f_{i+p}\}
:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2p-1}_{i-p+1}f=

\displaystyle(2p-1)!\sum^{p}_{j=-p+1}\,\gamma^{2p-1,1/2}_{p,j}\,f^{n}_{i+j}.

(5.20)

These indicators are such that

\psi^{p}_{i+1/2}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{ if $\{f_{j}\}$ are 'smooth' in $\mathcal{S}^{p}_{i}$;}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise;}\end{array}\right.

(5.21)

see
[
3
]
for a precise statement of this property and its proof.

5.4
ACAT2P and WBACAT2P methods

Using all these ingredients, the final expression of the ACAT2

U_{i}^{n+1}=U_{i}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Bigl({F}^{\mathcal{A}_{i}}_{i;i-\frac{1}{2}}-{F}^{\mathcal{A}_{i}}_{i;i+\frac{1}{2}}+\widetilde{S}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\Bigr),

\widetilde{S}^{\mathcal{A}_{i}}_{i}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{S}^{*}_{i}&\text{if $\mathcal{A}_{i}=\emptyset$;}\\

(5.24)

Here,

is the set of indices given by

\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{p\in\{2,\dots,P\}\ s.t.\ \psi^{p}_{i-1/2}\approx 1\text{ and }\psi^{p}_{i+1/2}\approx 1\};

6
Two-dimensional problems

In this section we will focus on the extension of ACAT methods to non-linear two-dimensional systems of hyperbolic balance laws

{U}_{t}+{F}({U})_{x}+{G}({U})_{y}=S_{1}(U)H_{x}+S_{2}(U)H_{y}.

(6.1)

As we did for the 1D systems of balance laws (
1.1
), let us introduce the functions
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(U)((x,y),t)

\displaystyle=F(U(x,y,t))-\int_{-\infty}^{x}S_{1}(U)H_{\sigma}d\sigma;

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(U)((x,y),t)

\displaystyle=G(U(x,y,t))-\int_{-\infty}^{y}S_{2}(U)H_{\tau}d\tau,

(6.3)

assuming that the integrals are finite. Then, the identities

\mathcal{F}(U)_{x}=F(U(x,y,t))_{x}-S_{1}(U)H_{x}

and

\mathcal{G}(U)_{y}=G(U(x,y,t))_{y}-S_{2}(U)H_{y}

allows one to write the 2D system of balance laws (
6.1
) in the equivalent conservative form

U_{t}+\mathcal{F}(U)_{x}+\mathcal{G}(U)_{y}=0.

(6.4)

The idea is now extend the ACAT
2P
schemes to 2D systems written in the form (
6.4
).

The following multi-index notation will be used:

\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z},

and

\textbf{0}=(0,0)\quad\mathbf{1}=(1,1)\quad\mathbf{1/2}=(1/2,1/2),\quad\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0),\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1).

We consider Cartesian meshes with nodes

\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}=(i_{1}\Delta x,i_{2}\Delta y).

Using this notation, CAT
2P
methods can be extended as follows:

U_{\mathbf{i}}^{n+1}=U_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{F}_{\mathbf{i}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{P}-{F}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{P}+\widetilde{S}^{P}_{1,\mathbf{i}}\right]+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left[{G}_{\mathbf{i}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}}^{P}-{G}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}}^{P}+\widetilde{S}^{P}_{2,\mathbf{i}}\right]

(6.5)

where the numerical fluxes
{F}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{P}
,
{G}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}}^{P}
and the source terms

\widetilde{S}^{P}_{j,\mathbf{i}}
,
j=1,2
are computed using the values of the numerical solution
U_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}
in the
P^{2}-
point stencil centered at
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{1/2}}=((i_{1}+\frac{1}{2})\Delta x,(i_{2}+\frac{1}{2})\Delta y)

\mathcal{S}^{P}_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{1/2}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}},\quad\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{I}_{P}\},

where

\mathfrak{I}_{P}=\{\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},j_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z},\quad-P+1\leq j_{k}\leq P,\quad k=1,2\}.

See Figure
6.1
for an example.

Figure 6.1:
Stencil
S_{2}
centered in
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{1/2}}=(0.5\Delta x,0.5\Delta y
). The rectangular black grid represents the space dimension while the blue line means the time direction of Taylor approximation.

For the sake of simplicity let us introduce index sets which will be used in the approximation of integrals of the source term

\mathfrak{M}_{P}=\{\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{I}_{P}\quad{\rm such\;that}\quad-P+2\leq j_{1}\leq P\}

and

\mathfrak{N}_{P}=\{\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{I}_{P}\quad{\rm such\;that}\quad-P+2\leq j_{2}\leq P\}.

The following algorithm will be used to compute the numerical fluxes
{F}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{P}
,
{G}_{\mathbf{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}}^{P}
and the source terms

\widetilde{S}^{P}_{j,\mathbf{i}}
,
j=1,2
of 2D CAT
2P
method:

1.

Define

\displaystyle F_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}^{(0)}:=F(U_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}}^{n}),\quad\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{I}_{P};

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}^{(0)}:=G(U_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}}^{n}),\quad\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{I}_{P};

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}-\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{j}}^{(0)}:=\Delta x\sum_{q=-P+1}^{P}a_{P,q}^{i_{1},j_{1}}S_{1}(U^{n}_{\mathbf{i}+q\mathbf{e}_{1}})H_{x}(x_{\mathbf{i}+q\mathbf{e}_{1}}),\quad\mathbf{j}\in\mathfrak{M}_{P};

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{i},(-P+1,j_{2}),(-P+1,j_{2})}^{(0)}=0,\quad j_{2}=-P+1,\dots,P;

7.3
Shallow water model

In this section we will focus on the one-dimensional hyperbolic shallow water model

\begin{cases}h_{t}+q_{x}=0\\
\displaystyle q_{t}+\left(\frac{q^{2}}{h}+\frac{g}{2}h^{2}\right)_{x}=ghH_{x},\end{cases}

(7.8)

that can be written in the form (
1.1
) with

U=\begin{bmatrix}h\\
q\end{bmatrix},\quad F(U)=\begin{bmatrix}q\\
\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}}{h}+\frac{g}{2}h^{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad S(U)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
gh\end{bmatrix}.

The variable
x
refers to the axis of the channel and
t
is time;
q(x,t)
and
h(x,t)
represent the discharge and the water thickness;
g,
the acceleration due to gravity;
H(x),
the depth measured from a fixed level of reference; furthermore, the following relation is verified
q(x,t)=h(x,t)u(x,t),
with
u
the depth average horizontal velocity. The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix
J(U)
of the flux
F(U)
are

\lambda_{1}=u-\sqrt{gh}\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\lambda_{2}=u+\sqrt{gh}.

The local Froude number is defined by

Fr=\frac{|u|}{\sqrt{gh}}.

The flow is said to be supercritical if
Fr>1
for all
x\in[a,b],
critical if
Fr=1
for all
x\in[a,b]
and subcritical if
Fr<1
for all
x\in[a,b].

The stationary solution of the shallow water system (
7.8
) are implicitly given by

q=Q\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\frac{q^{2}}{h^{2}}+gh-gH=C,

(7.9)

where
Q
and
C
are arbitrary constants
[
7
]
. In order to implement the well-balanced methods, given
U^{n}_{i}=[h^{n}_{i},q^{n}_{i}]^{T}
one has to find the stationary solution
U^{*}_{i}=[q^{*}_{i},h^{*}_{i}(x)]^{T}
that solves (
4.3
): it is implicitly given by

q^{*}_{i}(x)=q^{n}_{i},\quad\frac{1}{2}\frac{(q^{n}_{i})^{2}}{{h^{*}_{i}(x)}^{2}}+gh^{*}_{i}(x)-gH=C_{i},\quad\forall x,

with

C_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(q^{n}_{i})^{2}}{(h_{i}^{n})^{2}}+gh^{n}_{i}-gH(x_{i}).

Therefore, at a point
x_{j}
of the stencil, one has
q_{i}^{*}(x_{j})=q^{n}_{i}
and
h_{i}^{*}(x_{j})
has to be a positive root of the polynomial:

P_{i,j}(h)=h^{3}-\left(\frac{C_{i}}{g}+H(x_{j})\right)h^{2}+\frac{1}{2g}(q_{i}^{n})^{2}.

This polynomial can have two, one, or zero positive roots. In the first case, one of the roots corresponds to a supercritical state and the other one to a subcritical state: a criterion is necessary to select one root or the other. We follow here a criterion similar to the one chosen in
[
7
]
in the context of finite volume methods: the solution whose regime (sub or supercritical) is the same as the one of
U^{n}_{i}
is selected. A careful implementation is needed to capture transcritical stationary solutions: see for instance the discussion in
[
7
]
or
[
24
]
.

7.4
2D Euler system with gravity

As last test, let us consider the 2D system of compressible Euler equations with a gravity

\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+(\rho u)_{x}+(\rho v)_{y}=0,\\
(\rho u)_{t}+(\rho u^{2}+p)_{x}+(\rho uv)_{y}=-\rho H_{x},\\
(\rho v)_{t}+(\rho uv)_{x}+(\rho v^{2}+p)_{y}=-\rho H_{y},\\
E_{t}+(u(E+p))_{x}+(v(E+p))_{y}=-\rho uH_{x}-\rho vH_{y}.\end{cases}

(7.12)

Here,
\rho
is the density;
u
, the velocity in
x-
direction;
v
, the velocity in
y-
direction;
p
, the pressure;
E
, the energy per unit volume excluding the gravitational energy; and
H(x,y)
, the gravitational potential
[
20
]
. The pressure is supposed to satisfy the equation of state

p=(\gamma-1)\Bigl(E-\frac{1}{2}\rho(u^{2}+v^{2})\Bigr),

where
\gamma
is the ratio between specific heats at constant pressure and volume, which is taken to be constant. System (
7.12
) can be written in the form (
6.1
) with

U=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
\rho v\\
E\end{bmatrix},\quad F(U)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
\rho uv\\
u(E+p)\end{bmatrix},\quad G(U)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho v\\
\rho uv\\
\rho v^{2}+p\\
v(E+p)\end{bmatrix},\quad S(U)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\rho H_{x}\\
-\rho H_{y}\\
-\rho uH_{x}-\rho vH_{y}\end{bmatrix}.

Hydrostatic stationary solutions satisfy

u=0,\quad v=0,\quad\nabla p=-\rho\nabla H.

A two-parameter family of isothermal stationary solution is given by

\rho^{*}(\mathbf{x})=C_{1}e^{-H(\mathbf{x})}\geq 0;\quad p^{*}(\mathbf{x})=C_{2}\rho^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0;\quad u^{*}=v^{*}=0;\quad E^{*}(x)=\frac{p^{*}(x)}{\gamma-1}.

(7.13)

Given

U_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}=[\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{n},\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}u_{\mathbf{i}}^{n},\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}v_{\mathbf{i}}^{n},E_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}]^{T},

the stationary solution
U^{*}_{\mathbf{i}}
selected by applying the technique described in Subsection
4.4
is then

\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}e^{-(H(\mathbf{x})-H(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}))};\;p^{*}_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{x})=\rho_{\mathbf{i}}^{n}e^{-(H(\mathbf{x})-H(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{i}}))};\;u_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}=v_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}=0;\;E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(x)=\frac{p^{*}_{\mathbf{i}}(x)}{\gamma-1}.

(7.14)

8
Conclusion

This paper deals with the construction, analysis, implementation and testing of well-balanced

ACAT2
P
methods to the treatment of hyperbolic systems of balance laws. The starting point is to re-write the systems as conservation laws, by subtracting to the flux a primitive of the the source term. Meanwhile, the well-balanced property has been obtained rewriting the systems as conservation laws, by subtracting to the flux the flux of the stationary solution, and adding to the primitive of the source the source computed at stationary solution.
The methods are developed for systems in one and two space dimensions, and can be extended to 3D.
In principle the procedure allows the construction of well-balanced schemes of arbitrary order, although the computational complexity quickly increases with the order of accuracy.
We prove that the constructed schemes are exactly well-balanced.

The use of suitable limiters allow an effective treatment of discontinuous solutions. Several test cases have been performed for the scalar equation and for systems in one and two space dimensions. In all cases we observe that stationary solutions are preserved within machine precision, allowing very accurate results when the solution is a small deviation from equilibrium.

The main advantage of the method consists in its generality: it allows the automatic construction of very high order well-balanced schemes.

There are still a few things that require improvement and generalization.
First, a careful complexity analysis of the methods is needed, together with an improvement of the computational efficiency, possibly by parallel computing techniques, which should be possible thanks to the local computations required by these methods.

Second, we shall explore new limiting strategies, which will allow more accurate results when the solution is smooth.

An open problem is how to couple ACAT methodology with IMEX method for the treatment of problems with stiff source.

All these issues are subject of current investigation.
