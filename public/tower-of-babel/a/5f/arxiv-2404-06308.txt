A generalization of concise words
Costantino Delizia, Michele Gaeta, Carmine Monetta

Abstract
. The study of verbal subgroups within a group is well-known for being an effective tool to obtain structural information about a group. Therefore, conditions that allow the classification of words in a free group are of paramount importance. One of the most studied problems is to establish which words are concise, where a word w w is said to be concise if the verbal subgroup w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) is finite in each group G G in which w w takes only a finite number of values. The purpose of this article is to present some results, in which a hierarchy among words is introduced, generalizing the concept of concise word.

1.
Introduction

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a group-word in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. For any group
G
and arbitrary
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, the elements
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
are called the
w
-values in
G
. We write
G_{w}
to denote the set of all
w
-values in
G
and
w(G)
to denote the verbal subgroup of
G
corresponding to
w
, which is the subgroup generated by all
w
-values.
For a subset
S
of a group
G
we denote
S^{*}=S\cup S^{-1}
. Note that if
S
is a normal set then
S^{*}
is normal too.
Clearly, any conjugate of a
w
-value is again a
w
-value, and so
G_{w}
and
G_{w}^{*}
are normal sets.

A word
w
is called concise if the verbal subgroup
w(G)
is finite in each group
G

such that
G_{w}
is finite. One of the most studied problems is to establish which words are concise. In the sixties, P. Hall conjectured that every word is concise,
but his conjecture was refuted in 1989 by S. Ivanov
[
7
]
. However,
many words of common use are known to be concise (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
).

A word is called semiconcise if the subgroup
[w(G),G]
is finite in each group
G

such that
G_{w}
is finite. Of course concise words are semiconcise. In
[
3
]
they proved that if
w
is a semiconcise word and
z
is any variable not appearing in
w
, then the word
[w,z]
is also semiconcise. It is not known if there exists a semiconcise word which is not concise, however in
[
3
]
they proved that there exist a word which is not semiconcise.

We consider a generalization of semiconcise words.
Let
w
be a group-word. The word
w
is
\frac{1}{n}
-concise if the finiteness of
G_{w}
for any group
G
implies that the subgroup

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{n-1\mathrm{\;times}}]

is finite.
The word
w
is
0
-concise if the finiteness of
G_{w}
for any group
G
implies that there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
(depending on the group
G
) such that the subgroup

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{n-1\mathrm{\;times}}]

is finite.
Obviously every
\frac{1}{n}
-concise word is a
\frac{1}{m}
-concise word for every
m\geq n
, and every
\frac{1}{n}
-concise word is a
0
-concise word, so this introduces what we can call a hierarchy on words. It is unknown if there are
\frac{1}{m}
-concise word that are not
\frac{1}{n}
-concise for some
m>n
. However, in a similar way as in
[
3
]
, we can see that there exist a word which is not
\frac{1}{n}
-concise for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Further information on the subgroup

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{n-1\mathrm{\;times}}]

when
w
is
\frac{1}{n}
-concise can be obtained using a verbal generalization of
\mathrm{FC}
-groups. For subsets
X
and
Y
of a group
G
we write
X^{Y}
to denote the set of conjugates
\{x^{y}|x\in X,y\in Y\}
.
Let
G
be a group and let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. The subgroup
H
is said to be
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
if
x^{H}
is finite for all
x\in G
. The subgroup
H
is said to be
\mathrm{BFC}
-embedded in
G
if
x^{H}
is finite for all
x\in G
and the number of elements in
x^{H}
is bounded by a constant that does not depend on the choice of
x
.
Let
w
be a group-word and let
G
be a group. The group
G
is an
\mathrm{FC}(w)
-group if the set of conjugates
x^{G_{w}}
is finite for all
x\in G
. The group
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group if
x^{G_{w}}
is finite for all
x\in G
and the number of elements in
x^{G_{w}}
is bounded by a constant that does not depend on the choice of
x
.

In this paper we generalize the results in
[
3
]
. More precisely, we prove the following theorems.

Theorem A
.

Let
w
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be an
\mathrm{FC}(w)
-group. Then

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
.

Theorem B
.

Let
w
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be an
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group. Then

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{BFC}
-embedded in
G
.

We also show that, for a certain word
w
, there is an
example of a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group
G
such that

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{n-1\mathrm{\;times}}]

is not
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
.
Additionally, as a consequence, we have an example of a word which is not
0
-concise.

2.
Unbounded case

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a group-word and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

Let
y\in G_{v}
. Then, there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in G_{w}^{*}
such that
y=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
, with
k\leq 2^{m}
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
m
. The case
m=1
was proven in
[
3
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Assume that the assertion is true in case
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[w(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),g_{n+1},\dots,g_{n+m},g_{n+m+1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[[w(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),g_{n+1},\dots,g_{n+m}],g_{n+m+1}\right].

By induction hypothesis there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in G_{w}^{*}
such that
y=[w_{1}\cdots w_{k},g_{n+m+1}]
, with
k\leq 2^{m}
. So we have

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[w_{1}\cdots w_{k},g_{n+m+1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(w_{1}\cdots w_{k})^{-1}(w_{1}\cdots w_{k})^{g_{n+m+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{k}^{-1}\cdots w_{1}^{-1}w_{1}^{g_{n+m+1}}\cdots w_{k}^{g_{n+m+1}}.

Note that
w_{i}^{j}\in G_{w}^{*}
, with
i\in\{1,\dots,k\},j\in\{-1,g_{n+m+1}\}
. So there exist
w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\in G_{w}^{*}
such that
y=w^{\prime}_{1}\cdots w^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
, with
k^{\prime}\leq 2k\leq 2^{m+1}
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a group-word and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

If
G
is an
\mathrm{FC}(w)
-group, then it is an
\mathrm{FC}(v)
-group.

Proof.

Let
x\in G
and
y\in G_{v}
. By Lemma
2.1
there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in G_{w}^{*}
such that
y=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
, with
k\leq 2^{m}
. By
[
2
, Proposition 2.9(i)]

G
is an
\mathrm{FC}(w^{-1})
-group. Note that
G_{w^{-1}}={G_{w}}^{-1}
, so it follows that
x^{G_{w}^{*}}
is finite. Let
x^{G_{w}^{*}}=\{x^{b_{1}},\dots,x^{b_{s}}\}
and put
A=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}
. By
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
we have that
x^{y}=x^{w_{1}\cdots w_{k}}=x^{a_{1}\cdots a_{k}}
, with
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
. Therefore
|x^{G_{v}}|\leq|A|^{2^{m}}
and so it is finite.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

Let
G
be an
\mathrm{FC}(w)
-group and
B
a finite subset of
G_{v}^{*}
. Then, for any
x\in G
, there exists a positive integer
e
such that
b^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(\langle x,B\rangle)
for all
b\in B
.

Proof.

Let
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\}
and
x\in G
. For any
b_{i}\in B
there exist elements
g_{i_{1}},\dots,g_{i_{n+m}}\in G
such that

b_{i}=[w(g_{i_{1}},\dots,g_{i_{n}}),g_{i_{n+1}},\dots,g_{i_{n+m}}]^{\varepsilon_{i}},

with
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
.
Put

J=\langle x,g_{i_{j}}|1\leq i\leq r,1\leq j\leq n+m\rangle.

By
[
2
, Lemma 2.7(i)]
the set
(J/\mathrm{Z}(J))_{w}
is finite. As
w
is
(\frac{1}{m+1})
-concise, the subgroup

[w(J/\mathrm{Z}(J)),\underbrace{J/\mathrm{Z}(J),\dots,J/\mathrm{Z}(J)}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]=[w(J),\underbrace{J,\dots,J}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]\mathrm{Z}(J)/\mathrm{Z}(J)

is finite. Thus
v(J)
has finite order modulo
\mathrm{Z}(J)
, say
e
. Since
B\subseteq v(J)
, it follows that
b_{i}^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(J)
for all
i
. As
\langle x,B\rangle\leq J
, the result follows.
∎

Theorem A
.

Let
w
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be an
\mathrm{FC}(w)
-group. Then

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
.

Proof.

Set
v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}]
. Then
G
is an
\mathrm{FC}(v)
-group by Lemma
2.2
. Let
x\in G
. By
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
, we can choose
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\in G_{v}^{*}
such that
x^{G_{v}^{*}}=\{x^{b_{1}},\dots,x^{b_{r}}\}
. Write
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\}
. Define the order
<
on the set of all (formal) products of the form
b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}
, with
1\leq i_{k}\leq r
and
j\geq 1
, as follows. Put

b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}<b_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots b_{i^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}}

if and only if one of the following conditions is satisfied:
j<j^{\prime}
or
j=j^{\prime}
and there is a positive integer
l\leq j
such that
i_{l}<i^{\prime}_{l}
and
i_{k}=i^{\prime}_{k}
for all
k>l
. Let
y
be an arbitrary element of
v(G)
. Then
y=y_{1}\cdots y_{j}
, where each
y_{i}\in G_{v}^{*}
. By
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,j\}
, there exist an integer
i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
such that
x^{y}=x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}}
. Clearly, we can choose
b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}
to be the smallest (respect to
<
) product of elements from
B
such that
x^{y}=x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}}
. Now we show that
i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq\dots\geq i_{j}
. Suppose to the contrary that
i_{k}<i_{k+1}
for some
k
. Then

\displaystyle x^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{k-1}}b_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+1}}b_{i_{k+2}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{k-1}}cb_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+2}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}},

where
c=b_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+1}}b_{i_{k}}^{-1}\in G_{v}^{*}
. In view of
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
, we have

x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{k-1}}c}=x^{b_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots b_{i^{\prime}_{k-1}}b_{i^{\prime}_{k+1}}}

for some
1\leq i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{k-1},i^{\prime}_{k+1}\leq r
so that

x^{y}=x^{b_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots b_{i^{\prime}_{k-1}}b_{i^{\prime}_{k+1}}b_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+2}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}}.

This contradicts the choice of the product
b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}
because

b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{k-1}}b_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+1}}b_{i_{k+2}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}>b_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots b_{i^{\prime}_{k-1}}b_{i^{\prime}_{k+1}}b_{i_{k}}b_{i_{k+2}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}.

Thus
x^{y}=x^{b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}}
with
i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq\dots\geq i_{j}
. Equivalently we can write

x^{y}=x^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

for some non-negative integers
e_{r},\dots,e_{1}
.
By Lemma
2.3
, there exists a positive integer
e
such that
b_{i}^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(\langle x,B\rangle)
for all
i
. Hence we may assume that
e_{i}<e
for all
i
, because if we write
x^{y}=x^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{e+t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}
, with
t
non-negative integer, we have

\displaystyle x^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{e+t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{e}b_{j}^{t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{j}^{e}b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{(x^{b_{j}^{e}})}^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{b_{r}^{e_{r}}\cdots b_{j}^{t}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}},

by the fact that
b_{j}^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(\langle x,B\rangle)
.
So
|x^{v(G)}|<e^{r}.
Thus
x^{v(G)}
is finite for all
x\in G
. We conclude therefore that

v(G)=[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
.
∎

3.
Bounded case

Recall that for a non-empty set
I
, a filter over
I
is a set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(I)
, where
\mathcal{P}(I)
denotes the set of all subsets of
I
, satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

\varnothing\not\in\mathcal{F}
,
I\in\mathcal{F}
;

(ii)

if
X,Y\in\mathcal{F}
, then
X\cap Y\in\mathcal{F}
;

(iii)

if
X\in\mathcal{F}
and
X\subseteq Y\subseteq I
, then
Y\in\mathcal{F}
.

The filter
\mathcal{F}
is principal if there exists a non-empty set
Y\subseteq I
such that

\mathcal{F}=\{X\subseteq I|Y\subseteq X\},

and non-principal otherwise. An example of a non-principal filter over an (infinite) set
I
is the so-called cofinite filter

\mathcal{F}=\{X\subseteq I|I\smallsetminus X\mbox{ is finite}\}.

A filter
\mathcal{U}
over
I
is called an ultrafilter if, for every
X\subseteq I
, either
X\in\mathcal{U}
or
I\smallsetminus X\in\mathcal{U}
. This is equivalent to saying that
\mathcal{U}
is a maximal filter over
I
. Also,
\mathcal{U}
is a non-principal ultrafilter if and only if it contains the cofinite filter (see
[
5
, Proposition 1.4]
). Given an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
over
I
and a family
\{G_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of groups, the ultraproduct modulo
\mathcal{U}
is the quotient set of the Cartesian product
\Pi_{i\in I}G_{i}
with respect to the equivalence relation defined as follows: the tuples
(g_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(h_{i})_{i\in I}
of the Cartesian product are equivalent modulo
\mathcal{U}
if and only if
\{i\in I|g_{i}=h_{i}\}\in\mathcal{U}
. Thus the ultraproduct modulo
\mathcal{U}
can be seen as the quotient of the unrestricted direct product of groups
G_{i}
by the subgroup consisting of all tuples
(g_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
\{i\in I|g_{i}=1\}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Recall that the width of a group-word
w
in a group
G
is the supremum of the minimum length of all decompositions of an element
g
in
w(G)
as a product of elements of
G_{w}^{*}
as
g
ranges over
w(G)
. Clearly a word
w
has finite width at most
k\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if any product of
k+1
elements or more of
G_{w}^{*}
can be expressed as a product of at most
k
elements of
G_{w}^{*}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
r\geq 1
. Suppose that
w
is a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
G
is a group in which
w
takes precisely
r
values. Then the order of

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\{m,r\}
-bounded.

Proof.

Assuming
w
involves
n
variables, write
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, and set

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

Then

v(G)=[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}].

By way of contradiction, suppose there exists a family of groups
\mathcal{G}=\{G_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with the property that
|(G_{i})_{w}|\leq r
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
but

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|v(G_{i})|=\infty.

Consider a non-principal ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
over
\mathbb{N}
, and let
Q
be the ultraproduct modulo
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{G}
. Then, by the fact that for a given integer
r
, the property that a given word takes at most
r
values in a group can be expressed as a sentence in the first-order language of groups and
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have
|Q_{w}|\leq r
, because
\mathbb{N}\in\mathcal{U}
. As
w
is
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise, it follows that
v(Q)
is finite. In particular,
v
has finite width, say
k
, in
Q
. Now the fact that for a given positive integer
k
, the property that a given word has finite width at most
k
in a group can be expressed as a sentence in the first-order language of groups and
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
yield that there exist
X\in\mathcal{U}
such that
v
has finite width at most
k
in
G_{i}
for all
i\in X
. Hence every element of
v(G_{i})
can be written as a product of at most
k
elements of
(G_{i})_{v}^{*}
. Moreover from
|(G_{i})_{w}|\leq r
, we get
|(G_{i})_{v}|\leq(2r)^{2^{m}}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, by Lemma
2.1
. Therefore,
|v(G_{i})|\leq(2r)^{2^{m}k}
for all
i\in X
. As noted above,
\mathcal{U}
contains the cofinite filter over
\mathbb{N}
, so
X\cap Y\in\mathcal{U}
for every cofinite subset
Y
of
\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
X\cap Y
is non-empty. Therefore every cofinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
contains some element
i
for which
|v(G_{i})|\leq(2r)^{2^{m}k}
. This is incompatible with the assumption that
|v(G_{i})|
goes to infinity because
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|v(G_{i})|=\infty
implies that for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist an index
\nu_{M}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|v(G_{i})|>M
for every
i>\nu_{M}
, but if we choose
M=(2r)^{2^{m}k}
and consider the set
L=\{1,2,\dots,\nu_{M}\}
we have that
X=\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus L
is a cofinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
and therefore it contains some element
j
, which is greater than
\nu_{M}
, for which
|v(G_{j})|\leq(2r)^{2^{m}k}
, a contraddiction.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a group-word and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

If
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group such that
|x^{G_{w}}|\leq r
for all
x\in G
, then
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(v)
-group and
x^{G_{v}}
has
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded order for all
x\in G
.

Proof.

Let
x\in G
and
y\in G_{v}
. By Lemma
2.1
there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in G_{w}^{*}
such that
y=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
, with
k\leq 2^{m}
. By
[
2
, Proposition 2.9(ii)]

G
is an
\mathrm{BFC}(w^{-1})
-group and
x^{G_{w^{-1}}}
has
\{n,r\}
-bounded order. Note that
G_{w^{-1}}={G_{w}}^{-1}
, so it follows that
x^{G_{w}^{*}}
is finite with
\{n,r\}
-bounded order. Let
x^{G_{w}^{*}}=\{x^{b_{1}},\dots,x^{b_{s}}\}
and put
A=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}
. Note that
s
is an
\{n,r\}
-bounded integer. By
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
we have that
x^{y}=x^{w_{1}\cdots w_{k}}=x^{a_{1}\cdots a_{k}}
, with
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
. Therefore
|x^{G_{v}}|\leq|A|^{2^{m}}=s^{2^{m}}
. Therefore
x^{G_{v}}
has
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded order
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}].

Let
G
be an
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group such that
|x^{G_{w}}|\leq r
for all
x\in G
, and let
B
be a finite subset of
G_{v}^{*}
. Then, for any
x\in G
, there exists an
\{m,n,r,|B|\}
-bounded positive integer
e
such that
b^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(\langle x,b\rangle)
for all
b\in B
.

Proof.

Following the proof of Lemma
2.3
, by
[
2
, Lemma 2.7(ii)]
the set
(J/\mathrm{Z}(J))_{w}
is finite of
\{m,n,r,|B|\}
-bounded order and, by Proposition
3.1
, the number
e
is
\{m,n,r,|B|\}
-bounded.
∎

Theorem B
.

Let
w
be a
\left(\frac{1}{m+1}\right)
-concise word, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be an
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group. Then

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{BFC}
-embedded in
G
.

Proof.

Set
v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}]
. We have that
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group, therefore it exists a positive integer
r
such that
|x^{G_{w}}|\leq r
for any
x\in G
. Then, by Lemma
3.2
,
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(v)
-group and
x^{G_{v}}
has
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded order for any
x\in G
. Let
x\in G
and choose
b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\in G_{v}^{*}
such that
x^{G_{v}^{*}}=\{x^{b_{1}},\dots,x^{b_{s}}\}
. Write
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}
. Define the order
<
on the set of all (formal) products of the form
b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{j}}
, with
1\leq i_{k}\leq s
and
j\geq 1
, as in the proof of Theorem
Theorem A
.

Let
y\in v(G)
. As in the proof of Theorem
Theorem A
, we can write

x^{y}=x^{b_{s}^{e_{s}}\cdots b_{1}^{e_{1}}}

for some non-negative integers
e_{s},\dots,e_{1}
. Since
s
is
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded by Lemma
3.2
, it follows, from
Lemma
3.3
, that there exists an
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded positive integer
e
such that
b_{i}^{e}\in\mathrm{Z}(\langle x,B\rangle)
for all
i
. Hence we may assume that
e_{i}<e
for all
i
, by the same argument shown in the proof of Theorem
Theorem A
, and so
|x^{v(G)}|<e^{s}.
Thus
x^{v(G)}
is finite of
\{m,n,r\}
-bounded order for all
x\in G
. We conclude therefore that

v(G)=[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is
\mathrm{BFC}
-embedded in
G
.
∎

4.
Examples

Every
\frac{1}{n}
-concise word is a
\frac{1}{m}
-concise word for every
m\geq n
. The next result shows another way to obtain
\frac{1}{m}
-concise words.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a group-word, and set

v=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1}].

If
w
is
\frac{1}{m+1}
-concise, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then
v
is
\frac{1}{m+1}
-concise.

Proof.

Let
G
be a group and let’s assume that
G_{v}
is finite.
Since
v(G)^{\prime}
is finite (see
[
6
, Proposition 1]
), we may assume that
v(G)
is abelian. It follows that every subgroup of
v(G)
is finitely generated. Let
K=\langle k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\rangle
be a finitely generated subgroup of
G
such that
v(G)=v(K)
. Consider now a generator of the group

[v(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}].

It is of the form
[y,g_{1},\dots,g_{m}]
, with
y\in v(G)
and
g_{i}\in G
. In particular it lies in the subgroup

[v(G),\langle g_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle g_{m}\rangle].

Put
g_{i}=k_{\ell+i}
and
H=\langle k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell},k_{\ell+1},\dots,k_{\ell+m}\rangle
. Note that
v(H)=v(G)
. Also we have that
|H:C_{H}(v(H))|
is finite, because every
h\in H_{v}
has only finitely many conjugates in
H
(that’s because
H_{v}
is a normal set) and
|H:C_{H}(v(H))|=|H:\bigcap_{h\in H_{v}}C_{H}(h)|\leq\Pi_{h\in H_{v}}|H:C_{H}(h)|
. So from the fact that

C_{H}(v(H))\subseteq C_{H}([v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}])

we have that

|H:C_{H}([v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}])|

is also finite. We claim that

|[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]:[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]\cap\mathrm{Z}(H)|

is also finite. In fact note that the set

\{[x,k_{i}]|x\in H_{w},i=1,\dots,\ell+m\}

is finite because it is a subset of
G_{v}
, and therefore, by
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
,
H_{w}
is contained in finitely many right cosets of
w(H)\cap\mathrm{Z}(H)
. Hence
(H/w(H)\cap\mathrm{Z}(H))_{w}
is finite and so
(H/\mathrm{Z}(H))_{w}
is finite. Since
w
is
\frac{1}{m+1}
-concise, we obtain that

[w(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]\mathrm{Z}(H)/\mathrm{Z}(H)\cong[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]/[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]\cap\mathrm{Z}(H)

is finite.
From this it follows that

[[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}],H]=[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is finite by
[
8
, Corollary p. 103]
. In fact to apply the Corollary we consider the four subgroups

H,\quad[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}],\quad[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]\cap\mathrm{Z}(H),\quad C_{H}([v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}])

of the group
H
. The subgroup

[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]

is normal in
H
because it is a subgroup of
v(H)
which is abelian and fully-invariant in
H
. The subgroup

C_{H}([v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}])

is normal in
H
because it is a centralizer of a normal subgroup and

[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]\cap\mathrm{Z}(H)

is normal in
H
because it is an intersection of normal subgroups. Moreover

[H,[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}]\cap\mathrm{Z}(H)]=1=[[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}],C_{H}([v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m-1\mathrm{\;times}}])]

trivially.
So we have that

[v(H),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]=[v(G),\underbrace{H,\dots,H}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is finite. In particular,
[v(G),\langle g_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle g_{m}\rangle]
is finite. Thus
[y,g_{1},\dots,g_{m}]
is periodic. Therefore

[v(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is a finitely generated periodic abelian group, and so it is finite. That proves that
v
is
\frac{1}{m+1}
-concise.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a group and let
y,b\in G
such that
b^{2}=1
and
[y,y^{b}]=1
. Then for every
m\geq 0
the following equality holds

[y,\prescript{}{m+1}{b}]=y^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}b}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
m
. Let
m=0
, then
[y,b]=y^{-1}byb=y^{-1}y^{b}=y^{b}y^{-1}
.
Assume the assertion true for
m\geq 0
. Then we have

\displaystyle[y,\prescript{}{m+2}{b}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[[y,\prescript{}{m+1}{b}],b]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[y^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}b}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}},b]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{(-1)^{m+2}2^{m}}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b}by^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}b}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}}b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{(-1)^{m+2}2^{m}}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b}y^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{(-1)^{m+2}2^{m+1}}y^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m+1}b}

∎

According to
[
7
]
, for any odd integer
n>10^{10}
and any prime number
p>5000
, the word
v(x,y)=[[x^{pn},y^{pn}]^{n},y^{pn}]^{n}
is not concise. Indeed, Ivanov constructed a
2
-generator torsion-free group
A
whose center is cyclic and
A/\mathrm{Z}(A)
is infinite of exponent
p^{2}n
, such that
v
takes only two values in
A
and the nontrivial value is a generator of
\mathrm{Z}(A)
. In
[
1
, Section 4]
, the authors considered a modification of Ivanov’s example, namely the wreath product

(1)

\displaystyle G=A\mathrm{wr}B,

where
B=\langle b\rangle
is a cyclic group of order 2. Taking

(2)

\displaystyle w(x,y)=v(x^{2},y^{2}),

they showed that
|G_{w}|\leq 4
and
b^{w(G)}
is infinite. In
[
3
, Section 4]
they showed that
b^{[w(G),G]}
is also infinite. In a similar way we now prove that
b^{[w(G),G,\dots,G]}
, where
G
is repeated
m
times, is also infinite for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that
w
is not
\frac{1}{m}
-concise for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exist a group-word
w
and a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group
G
such that

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is not
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
G
and
w
be as in (
1
) and (
2
), respectively. Then, by
[
1
, Proposition 4.1]
,
G
is a
\mathrm{BFC}(w)
-group. Denote by
K=A\times A^{b}
the base group of
G
. For any odd integer
t\geq 1
, let
N=\langle v_{0}^{t},(v_{0}^{b})^{t}\rangle
, where
v_{0}\in A
is the nontrivial value of
v(x,y)
in
A
. Notice that
N
is central in
K
and closed under conjugation by
b
so that
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
. Also, since
K/N
has odd exponent
p^{2}tn
and
|G/K|=2
, we have

K/N=\{g^{2}N|g\in G\}

and consequently
(K/N)_{v}=(G/N)_{w}
. Hence
v_{0}N\in(G/N)_{w}
, and therefore
v_{0}^{k}N\in w(G/N)
for any integer
k
. It follows that

b^{[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m}{b}]}N\in(bN)^{[w(G/N),G/N,\dots,G/N]},

where
G/N
is repeated exactly
m
times, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now
b^{[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m}{b}]}N=b[b,[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m}{b}]]N=b[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m+1}{b}]^{-1}N
. By Lemma
4.2
we have

\displaystyle b[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m+1}{b}]^{-1}N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle bv_{0}^{k(-1)^{m}2^{m}}v_{0}^{k(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b}N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b(v_{0}^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}}v_{0}^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b})^{k}N,

where
v_{0}^{(-1)^{m}2^{m}}v_{0}^{(-1)^{m+1}2^{m}b}N
has order
t
in
G/N
. Thus

|\{b^{[v_{0}^{k},\prescript{}{m}{b}]}N|k\in\mathbb{Z}\}|=t

and so

|(bN)^{[w(G/N),G/N,\dots,G/N]}|\geq t.

In particular
|b^{[w(G),G,\dots,G]}|\geq t
. Since
t
is an arbitrary odd positive integer, we conclude that
b^{[w(G),G,\dots,G]}
is infinite. Therefore

[w(G),\underbrace{G,\dots,G}_{m\mathrm{\;times}}]

is not
\mathrm{FC}
-embedded in
G
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

There exist a group-word
w
which is not
\frac{1}{m}
-concise for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and neither
0
-concise.
