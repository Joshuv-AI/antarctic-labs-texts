Semantics for a Turing-complete Reversible Programming Language with Inductive Types
Kostia Chardonnet, Louis Lemonnier, Benoît Valiron

Abstract
This paper is concerned with the expressivity and denotational semantics of a functional higher-order reversible programming language based on Theseus. In this language, pattern-matching is used to ensure the reversibility of functions. We show how one can encode any Reversible Turing Machine in said language. We then build a sound and adequate categorical semantics based on join inverse categories, with additional structures to capture pattern-matching and to interpret inductive types and recursion. We then derive a notion of completeness in the sense that any computable, partial, first-order injective function is the image of a term in the language.

Introduction

Originally, reversible computation has emerged as an energy-preserving
model of computation in which no data is ever erased. This comes from
Laundauer’s principle which states that the erasure of information is
linked to the dissipation of energy as heat
[
33
,
6
]
. In reversible computation, given some
process
f
, there always exists an inverse process
f^{-1}
such that
their composition is equal to the identity: it is always possible to
“
go back in time
” and recover the input of your
computation. Although this can be seen as very restrictive, non-reversible computation
can be emulated in a reversible setting by keeping track of
intermediate results. As discussed in
[
5
]
, the
simulation of standard computation with reversible computation can be
understood as a notion of
Turing completeness
—provided we
accept that the final result comes together with auxiliary,
intermediate computation.

Reversible computation has since been shown to be a versatile
model. In the realm of quantum computation, reversible computing is at
the root of the construction of
oracles
, subroutines describing
problem instances in quantum algorithms
[
38
]
. Most
of the research in reversible circuit design can then been repurposed
to design efficient quantum circuits. On the theoretical side,
reversible computing serves the main ingredient in several operational
models of linear logic, whether through token-based Geometry of
Interaction
[
36
]

or through the Curry-Howard correspondence for

\mu
MALL
[
9
,
7
]
.

Reversible programming has been approached in two different ways. The
first one, based on Janus and later R-CORE and
R-WHILE
[
35
,
44
,
17
,
43
]
, considers imperative
and flow-chart languages. The other one follows a functional
approach
[
42
,
41
,
23
,
22
,
40
,
9
]
: a function
A\to B
in the language represents a
function —a bijection— between values of type
A
and values of
type
B
. In this approach, types are typically structured, and
functional reversible languages usually feature pattern-matching to
discriminate on values.

One of the issues reversible programming has to deal with is

non-termination
: in general, a reversible program computes a

partial injective map
. This intuition can be formalised with
the concept of
inverse
categories

[
30
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
:
categories in which every morphism comes with a partial inverse, for which the
category
\mathbf{PInj}
of sets and partial injective maps is the emblematic
concrete instance.
This categorical setting has been successfully used in the study of
reversible programming semantics, whether based on
flow-charts
[
16
,
25
]
,
with
recursion
[
2
,
27
,
26
,
29
]
,
with side effects
[
20
,
19
]
,

etc
.

Although much work has been dedicated to the categorical analysis of
reversible computation, the
adequacy
of the developed
categorical constructs with reversible functional programming
languages has only recently been under scrutiny, either in

concrete
categories of partial
isomorphisms
[
29
,
28
]
, or for
simple,
non Turing-complete
languages
[
8
]
. A
formal, categorical analysis of a (reversible) Turing-complete, reversible language
is still missing.

Contributions.
In this paper, we aim at closing this gap: we
propose a Turing-complete (understood as in the reversible setting),
reversible language, together with a
categorical semantics. In particular, the contributions of this paper
are as follows.

•

A (reversible) Turing-complete, higher-order reversible language with
inductive types. Building on the Theseus-based family of languages studied
in
[
40
,
8
,
9
,
7
,
34
]
, we consider an extension with
inductive
types
, general
recursion

and
higher-order
functions.

•

Sound and adequate categorical semantics. We show how the
language can be interpreted in join inverse rig categories. The
result relies on the
\mathbf{DCPO}
-enrichments of join inverse rig
categories.

•

A notion of completeness. We finally discuss how the
interpretation of the language in the category
\mathbf{PInj}
is complete
in the sense that any
first-order
computable, partial
injective function on the images of types is realisable within
the language.

While

Operational semantics.
The language comes
equipped with a rewriting system
\to
on terms, defined
in
Table

4
. As usual, we write
\to^{*}
for the
reflexive transitive closure of
\to
. The evaluation contexts

C_{\to}
are defined by the grammar
[~]~\mid~\mathtt{inj}_{\ell}{\;C_{\to}}~\mid~\mathtt{inj}_{r}{\;C_{\to}}~\mid~\{v_{1}\leftrightarrow e_{1}\mid\dots\mid v_{n}\leftrightarrow e_{n}\}~C_{\to}~\mid~{\mathtt{let}}\,{p}={C_{\to}}~{\mathtt{in}}~{t}~\mid~\langle C_{\to},v\rangle~\mid~\langle v,C_{\to}\rangle~\mid~C_{\to}~t~\mid~\mathtt{fold}{\;C_{\to}}
.
Note how the rewriting system follows a
call-by-value
strategy
on terms and values, requiring that the argument of an iso be fully
evaluated to a value before firing the substitution. On the contrary,
we follow a
call-by-name
strategy to simplify the manipulation
of the fixpoint. Note that unlike
[
9
,
40
]
, we do not require any form of termination and
isos are not required to be exhaustive: the rewriting system can
diverge or be stuck.
The evaluation of an iso applied to a value is dealt with by pattern-matching:
the input value will try to match one of the values from the clauses and
potentially create a substitution if the two values match, giving the
corresponding expression as an output under that substitution.
A substitution
\sigma
is a mapping from a set of variables to terms.
The substitution of
\sigma
on an expression
t
, written

\sigma(t)
, is defined in the usual way by
\sigma(())=()
;

\sigma(x)=v
if
\{x\mapsto v\}\subseteq\sigma
;

\sigma(\mathtt{inj}_{r}{\;(t)})=\mathtt{inj}_{r}{\;(\sigma(t))}
;
\sigma(\mathtt{inj}_{\ell}{\;(t)})=\mathtt{inj}_{\ell}{\;(\sigma(t))}
;
\sigma(\mathtt{fold}{\;(t)})=\mathtt{fold}{\;(\sigma(t))}

\sigma(\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle)=\langle\sigma(t),\sigma(t^{\prime})\rangle
;
\sigma(\omega~t)=\omega~\sigma(t)
and
\sigma({\mathtt{let}}\,{p}={t_{1}}~{\mathtt{in}}~{t_{2}})=({\mathtt{let}}\,{p}={\sigma(t_{1})}~{\mathtt{in}}~{\sigma(t_{2})})
.
The support of a substitution, written

\text{supp}(\sigma)
, is defined as
\{x\mid(x\mapsto v)\in\sigma\}
.

Lemma 2

(Subject Reduction)
.

If

\Psi;\Delta\vdash t\colon A
and
t\rightarrow t^{\prime}
, then

\Psi;\Delta\vdash t^{\prime}\colon A
.∎

The proof is similar to what has been done in
[
9
]
. As
the rewriting system is deterministic, confluence is direct; meanwhile, as we
are concerned with partial functions, progress is not guaranteed: a term can be
stuck, for example,
\{\mathtt{inj}_{\ell}{\;(x)}\leftrightarrow e\}~\mathtt{inj}_{r}{\;(v)}
does not reduce.

Inversion.

Finally, any iso
\omega\colon T
can be inverted into an iso

\omega^{-1}\colon T^{-1}
, such that their composition makes up the
identity. Intuitively, if
\omega
is of type
A\leftrightarrow B
, then
\omega^{-1}

will be of type
B\leftrightarrow A
. Inversion is defined as follows.
Given an iso-type
T
, we define its inverse
T^{-1}
as:
(A\leftrightarrow B)^{-1}=B\leftrightarrow A
and
(T_{1}\to T_{2})^{-1}=T_{1}^{-1}\to T_{2}^{-1}
.
Given an iso
\omega
, we define its dual
\omega^{-1}
as:
\phi^{-1}=\phi
;
(\mathtt{fix}~\phi.\omega)^{-1}=\mathtt{fix}~\phi.\omega^{-1}
;

(\omega_{1}~\omega_{2})^{-1}=(\omega_{1})^{-1}(\omega_{2})^{-1}
;
(\lambda\phi.\omega)^{-1}=\lambda\phi.(\omega)^{-1}
and
\{(v_{i}\leftrightarrow e_{i})_{i\in I}\}^{-1}=\{((v_{i}\leftrightarrow e_{i})^{-1})_{i\in I}\}
and

\left(\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}c@{~~~}l}v_{1}&{\leftrightarrow}&{\mathtt{let}}\,{p_{1}}={\omega_{1}~p_{1}^{\prime}}~{\mathtt{in}}\\
&&\cdots\\
&&{\mathtt{let}}\,{p_{n}}={\omega_{n}~p^{\prime}_{n}}~{\mathtt{in}}~{v^{\prime}_{1}}\end{array}\right)^{-1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}c@{~~~}l}v^{\prime}_{1}&{\leftrightarrow}&{\mathtt{let}}\,{p^{\prime}_{n}}={\omega_{n}^{-1}~p_{n}}~{\mathtt{in}}\\
&&\cdots\\
&&{\mathtt{let}}\,{p^{\prime}_{1}}={\omega_{1}^{-1}~p_{1}}~{\mathtt{in}}~{v_{1}}\end{array}\right).

Property 3

(Inversion is an involution)
.

For any well-typed iso
\omega
, we have
(\omega^{-1})^{-1}=\omega
.

Proof.

By a straightforward induction on
\omega
, notice that if
\omega=\{v_{1}\leftrightarrow e_{1}\mid\dots\mid v_{n}\leftrightarrow e_{n}\}
then by definition we swap twice the order of the

\mathtt{let}
construction, hence recovering the original term.
∎

Lemma 4

(Inversion is well-typed)
.

If
\phi_{1}\colon A_{1}\leftrightarrow B_{1}\ldots\phi_{n}\colon A_{n}\leftrightarrow B_{n}\vdash_{\omega}\omega\colon T
, then

\phi_{1}\colon B_{1}\leftrightarrow A_{1}\ldots\phi_{n}\colon B_{n}\leftrightarrow A_{n}\vdash_{\omega}\omega^{-1}\colon T^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 5

(Inversion is preserved by evaluation)
.

If
\omega\to\omega^{\prime}
then
\omega^{-1}\to\omega^{\prime-1}
.∎

Theorem 6

(Semantics of isos and their inversions
[
9
]
)
.

For all well-typed isos
\vdash_{\omega}\omega\colon A\leftrightarrow B
, and for all
well-typed values
\vdash v\colon A
, if
(\omega~(\omega^{-1}~v))\rightarrow^{*}v^{\prime}
then
v=v^{\prime}
.∎

Example 7
.

Remember that
[A]=\mu X.\mathds{1}\oplus(A\otimes X)
. One can
define the

(A\leftrightarrow B)\to[A]\leftrightarrow[B]
, defined as

Its inverse

If we consider the inverse of the term

Example 8

(Cantor Pairing)
.

One can encode the Cantor Pairing between
\mathbb{N}\otimes\mathbb{N}\leftrightarrow\mathbb{N}

First recall that the type of natural number
\mathtt{nat}
is given by
\mu X.\mathds{1}\oplus X

3.3

A RTM configuration is a set-based construction that we can model
using the type constructors available in our language. Because the
transition relation
\delta
is backward and forward deterministic, it
can be encoded as an iso. Several issues need to be dealt with; we
discuss them in this section.

Encoding configurations.

The set of states

is modeled with the type

Q^{T}=\mathds{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathds{1}
(
n

The set

, and the encoding of the tape symbol

We then define the type of configurations in the obvious manner: a

corresponds to a closed value

\operatorname{isos}(C)
of type

Q^{T}\otimes([\Sigma^{T}]\otimes\Sigma^{T}\otimes[\Sigma^{T}])

4
Categorical Background

We aim at providing a denotational semantics for the programming language
introduced above, meaning a mathematical interpretation abstract to the syntax.
Our approach is categorical, in the spirit of many others before us. Programs
are compositional by design, making it natural to interpret in a
framework ruled by compositionality. Types are usually interpreted as objects
in a category
\mathcal{C}
, and terms as morphisms in this category. We have seen that
the main feature of our programming language is reversibility and its terms can
be seen as partial isomorphisms, or partial injections. We want this property
to be carried on the interpretation, and we present in this section the proper
categories to do so. The category of sets and partial injective functions,
written
\mathbf{PInj}
, will be the recurring example throughout this section to help
the intuition.

4.1
Join inverse rig category

The axiomatisation of join inverse rig categories gives the conditions
for the morphisms of a category to be
partial injections
.
First, the notion of restriction allows to capture the
actual

domain of a morphism through a partial identity function.
Historically,
inverse
categories
[
30
]
were
introduced before
restriction
categories, but the latter are
more convenient to introduce the subject.

Definition 20

(Restriction
[
10
]
)
.

A restriction structure is an operator that
maps each morphism
f:A\rightarrow B
to a morphism
\overline{f}:A\rightarrow A
such that for all
g
and
h
such that the
domain of
g
is
A
and the domain of
h
is
B
we have

f\circ\overline{f}=f
,
\overline{f}\circ\overline{g}=\overline{g}\circ\overline{f}
,
\overline{f\circ\overline{g}}=\overline{f}\circ\overline{g}
and
\overline{h}\circ f=f\circ\overline{h\circ f}
.
A morphism
f
is said to be
total
if
\overline{f}=1_{A}
.
A category with a restriction structure is called a

restriction category
.
A functor
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
is a

restriction functor
if
\overline{F(f)}=F\left(\overline{f}\right)
for all
morphism
f
of
\mathcal{C}
. The definition is canonically
extended to bifunctors.
When unambiguous, we write
gf
for the
composition
g\circ f
.

Example 21
.

Given sets
A,B
and a partial function
f:A\to B
defined on

A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and undefined on
A\setminus A^{\prime}
, the restriction of

f
is
\overline{f}:A\to A
, the identity on
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
undefined on
A\setminus A^{\prime}
. This example shows that
\mathbf{PInj}
is a
restriction category.

To interpret reversibility, we need to introduce a notion of
reversed process, a process that exactly reverses another process.
This is given by a generalised notion of inverse.

Definition 22

(Inverse category
[
27
]
)
.

An
inverse category
is a restriction category where all morphisms
are partial isomorphisms; meaning that for
f:A\rightarrow B
, there
exists a unique
f^{\circ}\colon B\rightarrow A
such that

f^{\circ}\circ f=\overline{f}
and
f\circ f^{\circ}=\overline{f^{\circ}}
.

Example 23
.

In
\mathbf{PInj}
, let us consider the partial function
f\colon\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}

as
f(0)=1
and undefined on
1
. Its restriction
\overline{f}

is undefined on
1
also but
\overline{f}(0)=0
. Its
inverse

f^{\circ}
is undefined on
0
and such that
f^{\circ}(1)=0
.

The example above generalises and
\mathbf{PInj}
is an actual inverse category. Even
more, it is
the
inverse category:
[
30
]
proves that
every locally small inverse category is isomorphic to a subcategory of
\mathbf{PInj}
.

Definition 24

(Restriction compatible
[
27
]
)
.

Two morphisms
f,g:A\to B
in a restriction category
\mathcal{C}

are restriction compatible if
f\overline{g}=g\overline{f}
. The relation is
written
f\smile g
. If
\mathcal{C}
is an inverse category, they
are inverse compatible if
f\smile g
and

f^{\circ}\smile g^{\circ}
, noted
f\asymp g
.
A set
S
of morphisms of the same type
A\to B
is restriction compatible
(
resp.
inverse compatible) if all elements of
S
are
pairwise restriction compatible (
resp.
inverse compatible).

Definition 25

(Partial order
[
10
]
)
.

Let
f,g:A\to B
be two morphisms in a restriction category. We
then define
f\leq g
as
g\overline{f}=f
.

Definition 26

(Joins
[
18
]
)
.

A restriction category
\mathcal{C}
is equipped with joins if for
all restriction compatible sets
S
of morphisms
A\to B
, there
exists

\bigvee_{s\in S}s:A\to B
morphism of
\mathcal{C}
such
that, whenever
t\colon A\to B
and whenever for all
s\in S
,
s\leq t
,

s\leq\bigvee_{s\in S}s
,

\bigvee_{s\in S}s\leq t
,

\overline{\bigvee_{s\in S}s}=\bigvee_{s\in S}\overline{s}
,

f\circ\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}s\right)=\bigvee_{s\in S}fs
,

\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}s\right)\circ g=\bigvee_{s\in S}sg
.
Such a category is called a
join restriction category
. An
inverse category with joins is called a
join inverse
category
.

Building up from Definition
20
, a
join restriction
functor
is a restriction functor that preserves all thus constructed joins.

Definition 27

(Zero
[
27
]
)
.

Since
\emptyset\subseteq\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,B)
, and since
all of its elements are restriction compatible, there exists a
morphism
0_{A,B}\doteq\bigvee_{s\in\emptyset}s
, called

zero
.
It satisfies the following equations:

f0=0
,

0g=0
,

0_{A,B}^{\circ}=0_{B,A}
,

\overline{0_{A,B}}=0_{A,A}
.

Definition 28

(Restriction Zero)
.

A restriction category
\mathcal{C}
has a
restriction zero
object 0
iff for all objects
A
and
B
, there exists a unique morphism
0_{A,B}\colon A\to B
that factors through
0
and satisfies
\overline{0_{A,B}}=0_{A,A}
.

Definition 29

(Disjointness tensor
[
14
]
)
.

An inverse category
\mathcal{C}
is said to have a
disjointness
tensor
if it is equipped with a symmetric monoidal restriction bifunctor

.\oplus.:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, with as unit
a restriction zero
0
and morphisms
\iota_{l}:A\rightarrow A\oplus B
and

\iota_{r}:B\rightarrow A\oplus B
that are total, jointly epic, and such that
their inverses are jointly monic and
\overline{\iota_{l}^{\circ}}~\overline{\iota_{r}^{\circ}}=0_{A\oplus B}
.

Definition 30

(
[
28
]
)
.

Let us consider a join inverse category equipped with a symmetric monoidal
tensor product
(\otimes,1)
and a disjointness tensor
(\oplus,0)
that are
join preserving, and such that there are isomorphisms
\delta_{A,B,C}\colon A\otimes(B\oplus C)\rightarrow(A\otimes B)\oplus(A\otimes C)
and
\nu_{A}\colon A\otimes 0\rightarrow 0
. This is called a
join inverse rig
category
.

4.2
\mathbf{DCPO}
-category

We use the vocabulary of enriched category theory to shorten the discussion in
this section. The notions of enrichment required to understand the semantics
later is basic and should not frighten the reader. Categories in computer
science are usually
locally small
, meaning that given two objects
A
and

B
, there is a
set
of morphisms
A\to B
. Enrichment is the study of the
structure of those sets of morphisms, which could be vector spaces or
topological spaces for example, more details can be found in
[
31
,
32
,
37
]
. It turns out that homsets in join inverse rig
categories are dcpos – directed-complete partial orders, i.e. a partial-ordered
set with all directed joins. This allows us to consider fixpoints in
homsets.
\mathbf{DCPO}
is the category of directed complete partial orders and
Scott-continuous functions – monotone functions preserving joins. Dcpos are
often used for the denotational interpretation of different sorts of

\lambda
-calculi, and more generally, to interpret recursive functions or
indefinite loops.

Definition 31

(
[
13
]
)
.

A category enriched over
\mathbf{DCPO}
, also called a
\mathbf{DCPO}
-category, is a
locally small category whose hom-sets are directly partial ordered and where
composition is a continuous operation (
i.e.
a morphism in
\mathbf{DCPO}
).

It is proven in
[
27
]
that a join inverse category
can be considered enriched in
\mathbf{DCPO}
without loss of generality.

Lemma 32
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a join inverse rig category. The functors:

-\otimes-\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
,

-\oplus-\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
,

-^{\circ}\colon\mathcal{C}^{op}\to\mathcal{C}

are
\mathbf{DCPO}
-functors, meaning that they preserve the dcpo structure
of homsets.

4.3
Compactness

Inductive data types are written in the syntax as some least fixed point. As
said earlier, types are represented as objects in the category, and thus a type
judgement is an object mapping, or rather an endofunctor. Here, we show how to
consider fixed points of endofunctors in our categorical setting.

Definition 33

(Initial Algebra)
.

Given an endofunctor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
, an
F
-algebra is a pair
of an object
A
and a morphism
f\colon FA\to A
.
F
-algebras form
a category with
F
-algebras homomorphisms. An initial
F
-algebra is
an initial object in the category of
F
-algebras.

Theorem 34

(Lambek’s theorem)
.

Given an endofunctor
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
and an
F
-initial algebra

(X,\alpha\colon FX\to X)
,
\alpha
is an isomorphism.∎

With Lambek’s theorem, we know that an initial algebra provides an object
X

such that
X\cong FX
;
X
is a fixed point of the endofunctor
F
, as
requested. The existence of such fixed points is given by the next theorem

[
13
, Corollary 7.2.4]
.

Definition 35

(Ep-pair)
.

Given a
\mathbf{DCPO}
-category
\mathcal{C}
, a morphism

in
\mathcal{C}
is
called an

if there exists a morphism

such
that
p\circ e=\mathrm{id}_{X}
and
e\circ p\leq\mathrm{id}_{Y}
. The morphisms
e
and

p
form an
embedding-projection pair

(e,p)
, also called

ep-pair
.

We recall that an

[
13
, Definition 7.1.1]
,
is an initial object such that every morphism with it as source is an embedding,
and is also a terminal object such that every morphism with it as target is a
projection.

Theorem 36
.

A
\mathbf{DCPO}
-category with an ep-zero and colimits of
\omega
-chains
of embeddings is parametrised
\mathbf{DCPO}
-algebraically
\omega
-compact;
meaning that for every
\mathbf{DCPO}
-functor

F\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
, there is a pair consisting of
a
\mathbf{DCPO}
-functor
F^{\text{\char 5\relax}}\!\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
and an indexed family

\alpha^{F}=\{\alpha^{F}_{A}\colon F(A,F^{\text{\char 5\relax}}\!A)\to F^{\text{\char 5\relax}}\!A\}
of
initial
F(A,-)
-algebras.
This pair is called a
parametrised
initial algebra.
∎

The hypotheses of the theorem above are verified by the categories we want to work with, without loss of generality.

Proposition 37

(
[
27
]
)
.

Any join inverse rig category can be faithfully embedded in a rig join
inverse category with colimits of
\omega
-chains of embeddings. ∎

Adequacy

We show a strong relationship between the operational semantics and the
denotational semantics of the language.
First, we fix a mathematical interpretation
\left\llbracket-\right\rrbracket
in a
join inverse rig category

Theorem 41

.

Given

\left\llbracket\vdash t\colon A\right\rrbracket\neq 0_{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket}

Soundness.

We start by showing the simple implication in

amount to soundness: the denotational
semantics is stable w.r.t. computation.

Proposition 42

(Soundness)
.

Given a valid term judgement

,
provided that

, then we have

\left\llbracket\vdash t\colon A\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\vdash t^{\prime}\colon A\right\rrbracket.
∎

We can conclude that if

with

\left\llbracket\vdash t\colon A\right\rrbracket\neq 0_{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket}

[

]
.

Proof of Adequacy.

Our proof of adequacy involves a finitary sublanguage, where the
number of recursive calls is controlled syntactically: instead of
general fixpoints, we introduce a family of finitary fixpoints

empty iso

We show the adequacy result for the finitary terms thanks to strong

Definition 52

Given a non-empty finite set of states

We then directly get a one-to-one correspondence between elements of

Q^{T}
and
\Sigma^{T}

(resp.

(resp.

Property 53

Given two different states (resp. two letter)

we get

s_{1}^{T}\bot s_{2}^{T}
.∎

We then define the type of configuration in the obvious manner: a

(q,(l,s,r))
will be encoded as an element of

. We follow the
intuition of Zippers
[
21

: a single list (here, the
tape of the RTM) is represented by a pair of lists
(l_{1},l_{2})
where

is in reverse order.

Definition 54

(Encoding of Configurations)
.

We define the type
of configurations as
C^{T}=(Q^{T}\otimes([\Sigma^{T}]\otimes\Sigma\otimes[\Sigma^{T}]))
. Given a configuration
C=(q,((\epsilon,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),a,(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{m}^{\prime},\epsilon)))

as
\operatorname{isos}(C)=(q^{T},([a_{n}^{T},\dots,a_{1}^{T}],a^{T},[a_{1}^{\prime T},\dots,a_{m}^{\prime T}]))

is represented as

\operatorname{isos}(C)=(q_{s}^{T},([],b^{T},[a_{1}^{T},\dots,a_{n}^{T}]))
.

The problem with our current language is that we do not allow infinite
data structures. While the type of list is infinite, its instances are
finite. Implicitly we would like to have the equation
[]=[b]=[b,b]=[b,b,b]=\dots
but this is not possible in our current
system. Instead, in order to simulate this infinite amount of blank
symbol on both sides of the tape during the evaluation, we artificially
grow the size of the two tapes on both ends by blank symbols between each
transition step. In order to do that, we need the following:

Lemma 55

(Existence of “snoc” iso)
.

There exists an iso
\mathtt{snoc}\colon[A]\otimes A\leftrightarrow[A]\otimes A

such that
\mathtt{snoc}~(\langle[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}],a\rangle)\to^{*}\langle[a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a],a\rangle
. ∎

Proof.

In order to implement
\mathtt{snoc}
properly we need the two
following isos :

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textit{len}:[A]\leftrightarrow[A]\otimes\mathtt{nat}\\
=\mathtt{fix}~\phi.\left\{\begin{array}[]{l@{~}c@{~}l}[~]&{\leftrightarrow}&([~],0)\\
h::t&{\leftrightarrow}&{\mathtt{let}}\,{(t^{\prime},n)}={\phi~t}~{\mathtt{in}}\\
&&(h::t^{\prime},S(n))\\
\end{array}\right\}\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textit{snoc'}\colon[A]\otimes A\otimes\mathtt{nat}\leftrightarrow[A]\otimes A\otimes\mathtt{nat}\\

&&([x_{1}],x_{2},0)\\
(h::t,x,S(n))&{\leftrightarrow}&{\mathtt{let}}\,{(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},n^{\prime})}={\phi(t,x,n)}~{\mathtt{in}}\\
(h::t^{\prime},x^{\prime},S(n^{\prime}))\end{array}\right\}\end{array}

The fact that both isos are well-typed is trivial, the non-overlapping

We have

If

•

Case

•

Appendix E
Proof of Adequacy

Our proof of adequacy involves a finitary sublanguage, where the
number of recursive calls is controlled syntactically. We show the
adequacy result for the finitary terms thanks to strong normalisation,
and then show that it implies adequacy for the whole language; this is
achieved by observing that a normalising finitary term is also
normalising in its non-finitary form.

Finitary sublanguage.

We introduce the syntax for finitary terms, where the number of possible
reductions is limited by the syntax itself. The grammar of finitary isos
is given by:

\omega~::=~\{v_{i}\leftrightarrow e_{i}\}_{i\in I}~\mid~\lambda\phi.\omega~\mid~\phi~\mid~\omega\omega~\mid~\mathtt{fix}^{n}~\phi.\omega

above. We introduce syntactic sugar,
