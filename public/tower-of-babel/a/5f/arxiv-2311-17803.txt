On the root system of a Kac-Moody superalgebra
Maria Gorelik, Shay Kinamon Kerbis

Abstract
. In this paper we extend several results about root systems of Kac-Moody algebras to superalgebra context. In particular, we describe the root bases and the sets of imaginary roots.

0.
Introduction

In this paper we study the root systems of Kac-Moody superalgebras.
The root systems of Kac-Moody algebras were studied by Vinberg, Kac, Moody, Peterson and others.

A contragredient Lie algebra is a Lie algebra constructed from a complex
square matrix by a
standard procedure described in
[
K4
]
, Chapter I.
Kac-Moody algebras correspond to the matrices satisfying the following conditions
for all indices
i,j
:

1.

a_{ij}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ a_{ji}=0
;

2.

a_{ii}\not=0
;

3.

\frac{2a_{ij}}{a_{ii}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
for
j\not=i
.

A similar procedure assigns a certain Lie superalgebra to any complex
n\times n
-matrix
A

and a parity function
p:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, where
X:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
We call
(A,p)
the
Cartan datum
. Kac-Moody superalgebras
correspond to the Cartan data satisfying certain conditions
(the condition (1) for all
i,j
, the condition (2) for all
i
with
p(i)=\overline{0}
, and a certain modification of
the condition (3))
which, unlike the Lie algebra
case, should be imposed not only on the Cartan datum
(A,p)
but
on all Cartan data obtained from
(A,p)
by so-called
”isotropic reflexions”.

A big part of the theory described in the Kac’s book “Infinite-dimensional Lie Algebras”
can be developed in the context of Kac-Moody superalgebras.
The construction (Chapter I) was developed in
[
S2
]
,
[
GHS1
]
.
The results of Chapter II (“Invariant bilinear form”) and many results of Chapter III
can be extended straightforwardly. Chapter IV is devoted to classification results; for
Kac-Moody superalgebras
the corresponding classification was obtained by I. van de Leur, C. Hoyt and V. Serganova (
[
vdL
]
,
[
H
]
,
[
S2
]
), see
0.3
below.
In this paper we extend most of results of Chapter V (“Real and Imaginary Roots”).
Some of these results can be found in
[
S2
]
, we give precise references in the text.

Different manifestations of the Weyl group in the context of Kac-Moody algebras lead
to three different notions for superalgebras: the skeleton (an analogue of the Cayley
graph of the Weyl group), the group
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)

and the Weyl group. In this paper we study these objects and
establish a decomposition of the group
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
analogous
to
[
K4
]
, Proposition 6.5. A version of Matsumoto Theorem for skeleta in recently proven
in
[
GHS2
]
.

0.1.
Skeleton

By construction, a Kac-Moody superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
possesses a triangular decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+}
where the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}

is a commutative even Lie algebra which acts diagonally in the adjoint representation.
The subalgebras
\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}
are generated by the root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
where
\alpha

runs through the set of simple roots
\Sigma
.

Starting from the original triangular decomposition, one can construct many more, using “reflexions”
r_{x}
numbered by the indices
x\in X
.
We call such triangular decompositions “attainable”.
The attainable triangular decompositions are parametrized by a regular graph, which we call
a
skeleton
: its vertices
correpond to different triangular decompositions
and the edges are marked by
r_{x}
with
x\in X
.
Each chain
of indices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\in X
represents a path between the corresponding triangular decompositions.
We denote the skeleton by
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
(where the vertex
v
indicates the original triangular decomposition).
For each vertex
u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)
we denote by
\operatorname{Sp}(u)
the full subgraph corresponding
to the triangular decompositions which induce the same triangular decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
.
By
[
S2
]
,
\operatorname{Sp}(u)
is a connected subgraph of
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
.
Similar objects appear in the Weyl groupoids
introduced in
[
HY
]
and developed in
[
HS
]
.

The Weyl group
W(\mathfrak{g})
acts faithfully on the set of vertices of the skeleton;
this action preserves the edges and the markings of the edges.
In the case of Kac-Moody algebras, this action is transitive:
the skeleton is naturally isomorphic to the Cayley graph of

W(\mathfrak{g})
and the spine is a trivial graph
\operatorname{Sp}(v)=\{v\}
.
In general, the spine is a connected subgraph of
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
and
for any vertex
u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)
there exists a unique
w\in W(\mathfrak{g})
and
v^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Sp}(v)
such
that
u=wv^{\prime}
.
The Weyl group can be naturally embedded in another group,

\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
, which also acts faithfully on the set of vertices of the skeleton,
preserving the edges and the marks on the edges.
The elements of
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
map each triangular
decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+}
to an isomorphic triangular decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})^{+}
.

0.2.
Root systems

We denote by
Q_{v}
the root lattice (=
\mathbb{Z}
-span of
\Sigma
).
For each vertex
u
in the skeleton we denote by
\Sigma_{u}

the corresponding set of simple roots and by
Q^{+}_{u}
the semilattice spanned by
\Sigma_{u}
.

Let
\Delta
be the root system of
\mathfrak{g}
(i.e.,
\Delta\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is the set of non-zero eginevalues
of
\mathfrak{h}
in the adjoint representation). Then
\Delta
spans
Q_{v}
and
for each
u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)
one has

\Delta=-\Delta^{+}_{u}\coprod\Delta^{+}_{u}
where
\Delta^{+}_{u}=\Delta\cap Q^{+}_{u}
. Moreover,

\Sigma_{wu}=w\Sigma_{u}
for each
w\in\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
(in particular, for
w\in W(\mathfrak{g})
).

We call a root
\alpha\in\Delta

real

if
\alpha\in\Sigma_{u}
for some
u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)
and

imaginary
if
\alpha
is not proportional to a real root.
We denote by
\pi
the set of even roots
\alpha
with the property
that
\alpha
or
\alpha/2
is a simple root at some vertex
u\in\operatorname{Sp}(v)
.
For each
\alpha\in\pi

one defines a coroot
\alpha^{\vee}
in the usual way.
The Weyl group is a Coxeter group generated by the reflections with
respect to the roots in
\pi
.

For the Kac-Moody algebras
\operatorname{Sp}(v)=\{v\}
and
\Sigma_{v}=\pi
.
For the finite dimensional Kac-Moody superalgebras
\pi
is the set of simple roots for
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
.

0.3.
Classification

For a set
S\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
we denote by
C(S)
the real convex cone
generated by
S
.

We divide indecomposable Kac-Moody superalgebras into three classes:

(Fin)

\mathfrak{g}
is finite-dimenional;

(Aff)

\mathfrak{g}
is infinite-dimensional with a finite Gelfand-Kirillov dimension;

(Ind)

\mathfrak{g}
is of infinite Gelfand-Kirillov dimension.

In the Lie algebra case the corresponding classification of the Cartan matrices is given by Theorem 4.3
in
[
K4
]
. In general, this classification can be described in terms of the
totally positive cone

Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}:=\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}C(\Sigma_{u}).

(denoted as
D^{+}_{\Pi}
in
[
S2
]
, Section 4).
Let
r
be the (real) dimension of the convex cone

Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
By
[
GHS1
]
we have
r=0
for (Fin),
r=1
for (Aff) and
r>1
for (Ind).

The finite-dimensional Kac-Moody superalgebras (type (Fin)) were described by V. Kac in
[
K1
]
.
The symmetrizable Kac-Moody superalgebras of type (Aff) were described by I. van de Leur in
[
vdL
]
.
The non-symmetrizable Kac-Moody superalgebras of type (Aff) and the Kac-Moody superalgebras of type (Ind)
were described by C. Hoyt and V. Serganova in
[
H
]
,
[
S2
]
. These results
imply that in the case (Aff) the Gelfand-Kirillov dimension equals to one.

0.4.
Root systems of Kac-Moody superalgebras

In the first part of our paper we study root systems of Kac-Moody superalgebras.
We extend the main results of Chapter V in
[
K4
]
to the Kac-Moody superalgebras.
For the Kac-Moody algebras the closure of the Tits
cone is the set
\{h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}|\ \langle h,\alpha\rangle\geq 0\ \ \text{ for all }\alpha\in Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}\}
(see
[
K4
]
, Proposition 5.8).
In this paper we do not introduce
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
and the Tits cone
(since Cartan matrices of Kac-Moody superalgebras may have non-real entries), but
work with
Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}
instead. We do not dicuss the results
for symmetrizable Kac-Moody algebras which appear in Chapter V in
[
K4
]
;
it is easy to see that most of these results do not hold in superalgebra context.
Another result which does not hold for superalgebras is
Corollary 5.10 in
[
K4
]
describing the group of automorphisms of the root lattice
preserving the root system. It would be interesting to have such a descrption for Kac-Moody superalgebras.

0.4.1.
Root bases

A linearly independent subset
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Delta
is called
a
root basis
if for
each
\alpha\in\Delta
either
\alpha
or
-\alpha
is a non-negative integral linear combination
of
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Each set of simple roots
is a root basis. By
[
K2
]
for an indecomposable Kac-Moody algebra each root basis
\Sigma^{\prime}

is
W
-conjugated to

\Sigma
or to
-\Sigma
(see
[
K4
]
, Proposition 5.9).
We denote by “
\operatorname{Root\ Bases}
” the set of root bases.

We call
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}

generic
if
\operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,h\rangle\not=0
for all
\alpha\in\Delta
.
For a generic
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
we set

\Delta_{>0}(h):=\{\alpha\in\Delta|\ \operatorname{Re}\langle\alpha,h\rangle>0\}

and denote by
\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}
the set of indecomposable elements in
\Delta_{>0}(h)
.

The following theorem is a slight modification of Theorem 8.3 in
[
S2
]
; it
extends Proposition 5.9 in
[
K4
]
to Kac-Moody superalgebras.

0.4.2.

Theorem.

(i)

For a Kac-Moody component of type (Fin) we have

\{\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}=\operatorname{Root\ Bases}=\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\}_{h\text{ is generic in }\mathfrak{h}}.

(ii)

For an indecomposable Kac-Moody component of type (Aff) we have

\{\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}\coprod\{-\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}=\operatorname{Root\ Bases}=\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\}_{h\text{ is generic in }\mathfrak{h}}.

0.4.4.
Imaginary roots

Set

Q^{++}:=Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}\cap Q_{v}.

Let
(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}
be the set of positive imaginary roots.
We prove that

(1)

(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}=\{\mu\in Q^{++}|\ \ \operatorname{supp}\mu\ \text{ is connected}\}

where for
\mu=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma}k_{\alpha}\alpha

we set
\operatorname{supp}\mu:=\{\alpha\in\Sigma|\ k_{\alpha}\not=0\}
and
\operatorname{supp}\mu

is connected if the corresponding Dynkin diagram is connected.
Combining the above formula with Theorem
0.4.3
(ii),
we obtain a generalization of
[
K4
]
, Proposition 5.2 (a) and Theorem 5.4.

As in the case of Kac-Moody algebras, this implies that
for the Cartan datum of a Kac-Moody superalgebra, all “root superalgebras”
have the same set of imaginary roots (the multiplicities may vary);
this extends Corollary 5.12 in
[
K4
]
to Kac-Moody superalgebras.

In the case when
\Delta
is not “purely anisotropic” (this means that

\Sigma
contains an isotropic root) we have

(2)

(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}=Q^{++}.

0.4.5.
Algebra
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})

By
[
S2
]
, Lemma 3.7, Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
contains a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
which can be seen as
“the best approximation to the largest almost contragredient Lie subalgebra”.
The corresponding contragredient Lie algebra has the Cartan matrix
B_{\pi}

with the entries
(\langle\beta,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle)_{\alpha,\beta\in\pi}
. For example, if

\mathfrak{g}
is finite-dimensional, then
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})=[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]
.
The Weyl group
W(\mathfrak{g})
is naturally isomorphic to the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
.

Denote by
\tilde{\pi}
the set of simple roots for
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
.
We have a linear map which sends
\tilde{\pi}

to
\pi
and the root system of
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
to
\Delta_{\overline{0}}

(this map is injective in the types (Fin) and (Ind)). Denote by
\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi}

the image of the set of imaginary roots of
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
.
Then
\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi}\subset\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\overline{0}}
.
Since
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
is a Kac-Moody algebra,
\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi}
is given
by the formula (
1
).
Our proofs of Theorem
0.4.2
(iii) and the formula (
1
) are based on
this description of
\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi}
and
the Hoyt’s classification
of the Kac-Moody superalgebras of type (Ind).

Our results imply the following formula for an indecomposable Kac-Moody superalgebra

\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}=\{\alpha\in Q_{v}|\ 2\alpha\in\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi}\},\ \ \ \ Q^{++}=\{\alpha\in Q_{v}|\ 2\alpha\in Q^{++}_{\pi}\}.

0.5.
Group
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)

As in the case of Kac-Moody algebras, part of the theory can be naturally developed in a greater generality. In this paper we use the framework of root groupoids developed
in
[
S2
]
,
[
GHS1
]
. Lie superalgebras in this approach are described by
certain connected components of so-called
root groupoid

\mathcal{R}
.
The root groupoid has a distinguished collection of generators, similarly
to the standard generators of a Coxeter group.
In
[
GHS1
]
it was shown that the group
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{R}}(v)
, the automorphism group of the vertex
v
of
the root groupoid, contains two commuting normal subgroups: the Weyl group
W
and the group
of “irrelevant automorphisms”. Any element in
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{R}}(v)
lifts to an automorphism
of the corresponding Kac-Moody superalgebra and this automorphism preserves
\mathfrak{h}
.
Irrelevant automorphisms preserve all root spaces and
the quotient
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{R}}(v)/K(v)
identifies with
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
.

The group
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
can be seen as
a subgroup of the group of automorphisms of the root lattice preserving the root system.
Denote by
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
the stabilizer of the spine
\operatorname{Sp}(v)
under the action of
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
(induced by the action on the root lattice). One has
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)=W\rtimes\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
; moreover,

\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{R}}(v)/(W\times K(v))
identifies with
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
.

The group
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
stabilizes the set
\pi
. This defines a group homomorphism from

\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
to the automorphisms of the Dynkin diagram of
\pi
.
This map is injective if
\pi
spans the root lattice (in particular, for the Kac-Moody superalgebras of
the type II).

0.5.1.
The group
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
for the Kac-Moody superalgebras

Combining the results
of
[
GHS1
]
,
[
Ker
]
and this paper we obtain the following description
of the group
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
for the indecomposable Kac-Moody superalgebras:

\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\text{ for }A(n|n),\ q_{2n}^{(2)}\\
\mathbb{Z}&\text{ for }C(n+1)^{(1)},\ A(m|n)^{(1)}\ (m\not=n),\\
\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\text{ for }A(n|n)^{(1)},\\
1&\text{ for other indecomposable Kac-Moody superalgebras}.\end{array}\right.

In all cases, except
A(n|n)
and
A(n|n)^{(1)}
,
the image of
\operatorname{Sp}^{D}(v)
in the automorphism group of the Dynkin diagram of
\pi

is trivial; for
A(n|n)
and
A(n|n)^{(1)}

the image contains the identity and the involution
which switches two connected components of the Dynkin diagram of
\pi
.

In this paper we introduce
\operatorname{Sk}^{D}(v)
as

Using the formula (

0.6.
Content of the paper

In Section 1 we present a preliminary information on root groupoids, mostly borrowed from
[
GHS1
]
.

In Section 2 we discuss root bases in the general setup and compare root systems of type I and type II;
in particular, we prove Proposition
2.2.3
. In addition, we
prove Theorems
0.4.2
and
0.4.3

for the types (Fin) and (Aff).

In Section 3 we verify that

is a Cartan matrix and study the algebra

;

[
S2
]
, but we give the proofs for the sake

In Section 4 we prove Theorem
0.4.2
(iii). The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem 8.3 in
[
S2
]
;
we present it for the sake of completness.

In Section 5 we study

,

For this case

,

In Section 6 we complete the proof of Theorem

In Section 7 we study the group

Preliminaries

We work over the field
\mathbb{C}
. We fix a finite set

1.1.
Main definitions

Recall that a groupoid is a small category in which every arrow is invertible.
In this section we recall a construction of the root groupoid given in Section 2 of
[
GHS1
]
.

1.1.1.

Definition.

Let
V
be a complex vector space. We call a map
\phi:X\rightarrow V

injective
if the induced linear map
\phi:\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)\rightarrow V
is injective.

For a finite set
X
, a root datum of
X
is,
by definition, a quadruple
(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
where
\mathfrak{h}
is a finite dimensional complex vector space,
a:X\rightarrow\mathfrak{h},b:X\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{*}
are injective maps and
p:X\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

The cardinality of
X
is called the
rank
of the root datum.

1.1.2.

Definition.

For a root datum

v=(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
the
Cartan matrix

A^{v}
is given by
A^{v}=(a^{v}_{x,y})_{x,y\in X}
where

a^{v}_{x,y}:=\langle a(x),b(y)\rangle
. The pair
(A^{v},p^{v})
is called the
Cartan datum
at
v
.
Two Cartan data
(A,p)
and
(A^{\prime},p^{\prime})
are called
D
-equivalent
if
p=p^{\prime}

and there exist an invertible diagonal matrix
D
such that
A=DA^{\prime}
.

For
v=(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
a root datum of
X
and
x\in X
we call
b(x)
a
simple root at
v
.

1.1.3.

Definition.

We call
x\in X

reflectable
at
(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
if one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

a_{xx}=0
and
p(x)=1
.

(ii)

a_{xx}\neq 0,\;p(x)=0
and
\frac{2a_{xy}}{a_{xx}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
for every
y\neq x\in X
.

(iii)

a_{xx}\neq 0,\;p(x)=1
and
\frac{a_{xy}}{a_{xx}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
for every
y\neq x\in X
.

If
x\in X
is reflectable and
a_{xx}\neq 0
we define

r_{x}:(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h},a^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime})
by
p=p^{\prime}
and

a^{\prime}(y)\coloneqq a(y)-2\frac{a_{yx}}{a_{xx}}a(x),\;b^{\prime}(y)\coloneqq b(y)-2\frac{a_{xy}}{a_{xx}}b(x);

we call

r_{x}

anisotropic reflexion
.

If
x\in X
is reflectable and
a_{xx}=0
we define

r_{x}:(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h},a^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime})
by

(a^{\prime}(y),b^{\prime}(y),p^{\prime}(y))\coloneqq\begin{cases}(-a(y),-b(y),p(y))&\;\text{if }x=y\\
(a(y),b(y),p(y))&\;\text{if }x\neq y,\;a_{xy}=0\\
(a(y)+\frac{a_{yx}}{a_{xy}}a(x),b(y)+b(x),p(y)+1)&\;\text{if }x\neq y,\;a_{xy}\neq 0;\\
\end{cases}

we call

r_{x}

isotropic reflexion
.

1.1.4.

Let us now define the root groupoid
\mathcal{R}
.

Fix a finite set
X
. The objects of
\mathcal{R}
are all root data of
X
.
The arrows of
\mathcal{R}
will be defined by generators and relations. The generators are arrows of the following types:

(i)

Reflexions
. For
x\in X
reflectable at
(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)

we have the arrow
r_{x}:(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h},a^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime})
defined in
1.1.3
).

(ii)

Tautological arrows
. If
\theta:\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}

is an isomorphism of vector spaces, then
\theta
gives rise to the following arrow

t_{\theta}:(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime},a^{\prime},b^{\prime},p)

where
a^{\prime}\coloneqq\theta\circ a
and
b^{\prime}\coloneqq(\theta^{*})^{-1}\circ b
.

(iii)

Homotheties
. Let
\lambda:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\lambda

gives rise to the following arrow
h_{\lambda}:(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)\rightarrow(\mathfrak{h},a^{\prime},b,p)
where

a^{\prime}(x)=\lambda(x)a(x)
.

We temporarily denote by
\mathcal{F}
the free category whose objects are the objects of

\mathcal{R}
and whose arrows are generated by the three types of arrows described above.

Let
\mathfrak{h}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathsf{Vect}
be the forgetful functor from

\mathcal{F}
to the category of finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{C}
,
sending
(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
to
\mathfrak{h}
. Notice that this functor sends reflexions and homothety arrows to the
identity map and tautological arrows to the isomorphism that is used to define them.
We now define an equivalence relation on arrows of
\mathcal{F}
:
two arrows from
v=(\mathfrak{h},a,b,p)
to
v^{\prime}=(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime},a^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime})
are equivalent if they induce the same isomorphism from
\mathfrak{h}
to
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
.

The arrows of
\mathcal{R}
are defined as the equivalence class of the arrows of the category
\mathcal{F}
.
By Proposition 2.3 of
[
GHS1
]
, the category
\mathcal{R}
is a groupoid.

Let

1.2.
Root (super)algebras

The root groupoid
\mathcal{R}
consists of connected
components some of which will lead to
“root superalgebras” introduced in Section 3 of
[
GHS1
]
. Root superalgebras
are Lie superalgebras constructed in the following manner.
For each
v\in\mathcal{R}
one defines a “half-baked” Lie superalgebra
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v)

in a classical way, see
[
K4
]
, Chapter I:
this is a Lie superalgebra generated by
\mathfrak{h}

and the elements
\tilde{e}_{\pm b(x)}
,
x\in X
with the parity

p(\tilde{e}_{\pm b(x)}):=p(x)

the relations are

(4)

[\tilde{e}_{b(x)},\tilde{e}_{-b(y)}]=\delta_{x,y}a(x),\ \ \ [h,\tilde{e}_{\pm b(x)}]=\pm\langle h,b(x)\rangle\ \ \text{ for all }x,y\in X,\ h\in\mathfrak{h}.

For an arrow
\gamma:v\to v^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{R}
the
isomorphism

\mathfrak{h}(\gamma):\mathfrak{h}(v)\to\mathfrak{h}(v^{\prime})
does not always extend to a homomorphism of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v)\to\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v^{\prime})
.
A root algebra supported on a component
\mathcal{R}_{0}
of
\mathcal{R}
is a collection of
quotients
\mathfrak{g}(v)
of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v)
such that for any
\gamma:v\to v^{\prime}
the isomorphism

\mathfrak{h}(\gamma)
extends to an isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}(v)\to\mathfrak{g}(v^{\prime})
.

A connected component
\mathcal{R}_{0}
of
\mathcal{R}
is called
admissible
if it admits a root algebra.
In this paper we consider only admissible components. By Theorem 3.3.1 in
[
GHS1
]
,

\mathcal{R}_{0}
, the connected component is admissible if and only if for each
v\in\mathcal{R}_{0}
the Cartan datum

(A_{v},p_{v})
is
weakly symmetrizable
which means

a^{v}_{xy}=0\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ a^{v}_{yx}=0\ \text{ for all $x$ reflectable at $v$.}

For an admissible component
\mathcal{R}_{0}
the category of root algebras
has an initial object, a root algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{U}}
which is called
universal
,
and a final object, a root algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{C}}
is called
contragredient
.
The algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{C}}
is
the quotient of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v)
by the maximal
ideal having zero intersection with
\mathfrak{h}
.
The universal root algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{U}}
is obtained by imposing on
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}(v)
reflected Chevalley relations — so generalizing the classical Serre relations.

1.2.1.

Let
\mathfrak{g}(v)
be a root algebra.
Assume that

is reflectable at
v
and
a_{xx}\not=0

The root spaces
\mathfrak{g}(v)_{\pm\alpha}
act locally nilpotently in the adjoint representation and

if

Note that

1.2.2.

If two vertices

have

1.5.

The notion of the Weyl group can be naturally extended to the case of admissible

For

The Weyl group acts on

Remark

Let

to

1.7.
Indecomposable components

We call a Cartan matrix

indecomposable
if

or
the elements of

can be ordered in such a way
that

a_{x_{j}x_{j+1}}\not=0

We call an admissible connected component

otherwise. Note that

A contragredient superalgebra

if for any ideal

of

is simple if and only if it is quasisimple with

(this implies

).
One readily sees that

corresponding to an admissible Cartan datum
is quasisimple if
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
is an indecomposable component.

]

For a Kac-Moody component we have

For a purely non-isotropic Kac-Moody component we have

Take
\gamma\in\Delta_{\operatorname{an}}
. By
[
GHS1
]
Remark 4.3.13,

\gamma=w\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\Sigma_{pr}
and
w\in W
; moreover,

w=s_{\alpha_{i_{1}}}\ldots s_{\alpha_{i_{m}}}
for some
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in I
.
Choose
w
of the minimal length (i.e.
m
is minimal) and set
\Sigma^{\prime}:=\{\alpha\}\cup\{\alpha_{i_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
.
Denote by
B^{\prime}_{\operatorname{pr}}
the submatrix of
B_{\operatorname{pr}}
which corresponds
to
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Then
B^{\prime}_{\operatorname{pr}}
is a generalized Cartan matrix (of finite size)
and we can construct a Kac-Moody algebra for this Cartan matrix. Let
W^{\prime}
be the corresponding Weyl group. Clearly,
w\in W^{\prime}
. Then

W^{\prime}\Sigma^{\prime}
is the set of real roots for this Kac-Moody Lie algebra, so

\gamma=w\alpha=\sum_{\beta\in\Sigma^{\prime}}m_{\beta}\beta

for some coefficients
m_{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}
and all
m_{\beta}

are either non-positive or non-negative. Since
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Delta^{+}_{\operatorname{an}}
,
the coefficients are non-negative. Hence any
\gamma\in\Delta^{+}_{\operatorname{an}}
can be written as
a linear combination of finitely many elements in
\Sigma_{\operatorname{pr}}
with a non-negative coefficients.
∎

3.5.
The algebra
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be any root algebra for an admissible component
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
.
We denote by
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
the Kac-Moody algebra corresponding to the matrix
B_{\pi}
.
Denote by
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}

which is generated by
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\pi
.

The following construction is similar to one appeared in Lemma 3.7 in
[
S2
]
.

3.5.1.

Let
(\tilde{\mathfrak{h}},\tilde{a},\tilde{b})
be a realization of
B_{\pi}
. This means that
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}

contains linearly independent set
\{\tilde{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\pi}
and

\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
contains linearly independent set
\{h_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\pi}

such that

\langle h_{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}\rangle=\langle\alpha^{\vee},\beta\rangle\ \ \text{ for all }\alpha,\beta\in\pi.

We choose
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
of the minimal possible dimension
(i.e.,
\dim\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}=|\Sigma_{\operatorname{pr}}|+\operatorname{corank}B_{\pi}
, see
[
K4
]
Proposition 1.1). Let

\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})=\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}\oplus\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}

be the half-baked algebra for
B_{\pi}
: this is a Lie algebra generated by
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}

and the even elements
\tilde{e}_{\pm\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\pi
subject to the relations
(
4
).
One has

[\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]=\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}\oplus\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}\oplus\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}

where
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
is spanned by
h_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\pi
.
By
[
K4
]
, Theorem 1.2,
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
(resp.,
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}
) is a
free Lie algebra generated by
\tilde{e}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\pi
(resp.,
\tilde{e}_{-\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\pi
).
Using this result, it is not hard to show that
[\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]
is generated by

h_{\alpha},\tilde{e}_{\pm\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\pi
subject to the relations (
4
) for
h\in\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}

(the freeness result implies that there are no additional relations).
Combining Lemmatta
3.2
and
3.3
we conclude
that there exists a surjective homomorphism

s:\ [\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}

which maps
\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{\pm}_{\pm\alpha}
to
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}

for all
\alpha\in\pi
. Since
\Sigma_{\operatorname{pr}}\subset\Delta^{+}

there exists
h\in\mathfrak{h}
such that
\langle h,\alpha\rangle\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

for all
\alpha\in\pi
. Take
\tilde{h}\in\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
such that

\langle\tilde{h},\alpha\rangle=\langle h,\alpha\rangle
.
The actions of
h
and
\tilde{h}
define
\mathbb{R}
-gradings
on
[\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]
and on

\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
. Then
s
is the homomorphism of the graded algebras, so
\operatorname{Ker}s
is a graded ideal. Therefore

\operatorname{Ker}s=\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+}

where
\mathfrak{m}_{0}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{\pm}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{n}}^{\pm}
.
For any
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\operatorname{pr}}
we have

\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{\pm\alpha}\not\in\operatorname{Ker}s
, so

[\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{\alpha},\mathfrak{m}_{0}]=0,\ \ \ [\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{-\alpha},\mathfrak{m}^{+}]\subset\mathfrak{m}^{+},\ \ [\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{\alpha},\mathfrak{m}^{-}]\subset\mathfrak{m}^{-}.

Therefore
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
lies
in the centre of
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})
and

\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+}
is an ideal of
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})
.
Let

q_{\operatorname{U}}:\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})\to\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{U}}(B_{\pi}),\ \ \ \ \ q_{\operatorname{C}}:\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})\to\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{C}}(B_{\pi})=\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})

be the canonical epimorphisms.
Recall that
\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{C}}
is the maximal ideal having zero intersection with
\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}
;
by above,
\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+}\subset\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{C}}
.
On the other hand,
\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{U}}
is the minimal ideal
I
with property that
for each
\alpha\in\pi
the root space

\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{\pm\alpha}
does not lie in
I
and
acts locally nilpotently
on the quotient
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})_{\pm\alpha}/I
.
Since
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
acts locally nipotently on
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
we conclude that

\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{U}}
lie in
\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+}
, Hence

\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{U}}\subset(\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+})\subset\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{C}}.

This gives a surjective map

(18)

s^{\prime}:\ \mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\twoheadrightarrow[{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]/\mathfrak{c},

where
\mathfrak{c}
, which is the image of
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
, is
a subalgebra of the centre of
{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})
.

3.5.2.
Remark

It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{c}=0
if and only if
\pi

is linearly independent.

3.5.3.
Remark

The above construction does not work
if we substitute
\pi
by
\Sigma_{\operatorname{pr}}
. For example,
\mathfrak{g}:=\mathfrak{osp}(3|2)
does not contain a subalgebra
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\operatorname{pr}})/\mathfrak{c}
.

3.5.4.

Consider the case when
B_{\pi}
is symmetrizable. By
[
GabK
]
symmetrizability of

implies that for any finite subset

\pi^{\prime}\subset\pi
the canonical map

\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{U}}(B_{\pi^{\prime}})\to\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi^{\prime}})
is bijective.
This gives
\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{U}}(B_{\pi})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathfrak{g}^{\operatorname{C}}(B_{\pi})
, so

\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{U}}=\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{C}}
. Then

\mathfrak{m}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{+}=\operatorname{Ker}q_{\operatorname{U}}
and

induces an algebra isomorphism

[{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi}),{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})]/\mathfrak{c}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
.

3.5.5.
Remark

By construction,
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\subset[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]
. We have
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]

in the type (Fin). The following example demonstrates that

can be a proper subalgebra of
[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)^{(1)}
. In this case
{\mathfrak{g}}(B_{\pi})=\mathfrak{sl}_{m}^{(1)}\times\mathfrak{sl}_{n}^{(1)}
, so

[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]_{\delta}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\delta}=m+n-1,\ \ \ \dim{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}_{\delta}=m+n-2.

Other examples include

, see

below.

3.6.
Applications to
\Delta

The construction described in
3.5.1
is very useful for a description of
\Delta
.

Recall that
\tilde{\pi}:=\{\tilde{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\pi}\subset\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
form a set of simple roots for
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
.
We denote by
\tilde{Q}^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}
the totally positive cone for
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
.

For each

\tilde{\beta}:=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\pi}k_{\alpha}\tilde{\alpha}\in\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}

we assign the element
\beta:=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\pi}k_{\alpha}{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.
The Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
is the Coxeter group generated by
s_{\tilde{\alpha}}
,
\alpha\in\pi
.
This group is isomorphic to
W
and for any
w\in W
one has
\widetilde{w\beta}=w\tilde{\beta}
.
Since
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
is a Kac-Moody algebra, the totally positive cone is given

\tilde{Q}^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigcap_{w\in W}C(w\tilde{\pi}).

We set

\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta^{+}_{\pi}:=\{\beta|\ \tilde{\beta}\ \text{ is a positive root of }\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})\},\\
(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}_{\pi}:=\{\beta|\ \tilde{\beta}\ \text{ is a positive imaginary root of }\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})\},\\

The construction described in
3.5.1
implies

\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\geq\dim\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})_{\tilde{\beta}}
. In particular,

\Delta^{+}_{\pi}\subset\Delta^{+}_{\overline{0}}.

3.6.1.

Corollary.

One has

(i)

\tilde{\beta}
is a real root of

\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
if and only if either
\beta\in(\Delta_{\operatorname{an}}\cap\Delta_{\overline{0}})
or
\frac{\beta}{2}\in(\Delta_{\operatorname{an}}\cap\Delta_{\overline{1}})

(ii)

(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}_{\pi}\subset(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}})^{+}_{\overline{0}}
;

(iii)

Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}\subset Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Now (i) follows from the fact that
the real roots of
\mathfrak{g}(B_{\pi})
are
W
-conjugated to simple roots.
By (i),
\tilde{\beta}
is real if
\beta
is not imaginary. This gives (ii).
For (iii) recall that

Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}C(\Sigma_{u})=\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Sp}(v),w\in W}C(w\Sigma_{u}).

Since
\pi\subset\Delta^{+}_{u}
for any

one has

.
∎

3.6.2.
Remark

The converse of (ii) does not hold:
for

.

Let

If

imply the following

In this section we prove Theorem

4.1.

Proposition.

For an indecomposable Kac-Moody component of type (Ind) we have

\{\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}\coprod\{-\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}=\operatorname{Root\ Bases}\subsetneq\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\}_{h\text{ is generic in }\mathfrak{h}}.

4.2.
Proof

Let
\operatorname{Sk}(v)
be of type (Ind). Recall that

contains two non-proportional vectors

\nu_{1}
and

. Take a generic

such that

\langle\nu_{2},h\rangle>0>\langle\nu_{1},h\rangle

\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\not=\Sigma_{u}
and

\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\not=-\Sigma_{u}

\{\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}\coprod\{-\Sigma_{u}\}_{u\in\operatorname{Sk}(v)}\not=\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{h}\}_{h\text{ is generic in }\mathfrak{h}}.

It remains to verify that any root basis
\Sigma^{\prime}

is of the form

or

4.2.1.
Notation

We fix a root basis

such that

(19)

(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi})^{+}\cap\Delta^{+}(\Sigma^{\prime})\not=\emptyset

and fix

such that

for

.
We set

Let

be the
\mathbb{R}
-span of
\Sigma
.
Consider the unit spere

S:=\{\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{\prime}}c_{\alpha}\alpha\in V|\ \sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{\prime}}c^{2}_{\alpha}=1\}.

For any non-zero

denote by

the intersection
of the ray

with the unit spere.
We set

\begin{array}[]{l}K:=\{\mu\in V|\ \ \langle\mu,\beta^{\vee}\rangle\leq 0\ \text{ for all }\beta\in\pi\},\\

Recall that

is an indecomposable matrix of type (IND). Using

4.2.2.

Lemma.

The set

\{z_{\mu}|\ \mu\in Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}\}
is the closure of the set
\{z_{\alpha}|\alpha\in(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi})^{+}\}

in the metric topology.

Proof.

Recall that
(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi})^{+}\subset Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}
.
Observe that

Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}=\bigcap\limits_{w\in W}C(w\pi)

is a
W
-invariant closed convex cone (since

is a closed convex cone).
The set
\{z_{\mu}|\ \mu\in Q(\pi)\}
is dense in

, so the set

\{z_{\mu}|\ \mu\in Q(\pi)\cap Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}\}

is dense in

.

We introduce a partial order on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by setting
\mu\leq\nu
if
\nu-\mu\in Q^{+}(\pi)
.
Note that any subset in

contains a minimal element with respect to this order.
Since
(\Delta^{\operatorname{im}}_{\pi})^{+}
is
W
-invariant, it is enough to verify that for

\mu\in Q(\pi)\cap Q^{++}_{\mathbb{R},\pi}

is a limit point for the set

∎

4.2.3.

Lemma.

∎

4.2.4.

In light of Corollary

for

and

. By

is the Coxeter group
generated by
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\pi

,
\alpha\in\pi

This gives

where

Therefore

Since

between
(\Delta^{\mathit{re}}_{\pi})^{+}

\langle s_{\beta}\nu_{0},h^{\prime}\rangle>0

Since
\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
for any

and

,
we have
m_{\alpha}\leq-1

A(m-1|n-1)

The skeleta is naturally isomorphic to the Cayley graph

8.1.2.
Type
D(m|n)

In this case the vertices in the spine can be encoded by the words consisting of
m
letters

\varepsilon
and
n
letters
\delta
with additional sign
+
or
-

if the last letter is
\delta
(there are
\binom{m+n}{m}+\binom{m+n-1}{m}
vertices). In this case the Weyl group is
W(D_{m})\times W(C_{n})
and has the order

2^{m+n-1}m!n!
, so the number of vertices in the skeleton is equal to

2^{m+n-1}m!n!\bigl(\binom{m+n}{m}+\binom{m+n-1}{m}\bigr)=2^{m+n-1}(m+2n)(m+n-1)!.

The edges in the spine correspond to permutations of the neighbouring
\varepsilon
and
\delta
with the following
“sign rule”: there are no edges between vertices with different signs.

For example,
for
D(2|1)
the spine contains
4
vertices
v_{1}=\delta\varepsilon\varepsilon
,

v_{2}=+\varepsilon\varepsilon\delta
,
v_{3}=-\varepsilon\varepsilon\delta
and
v_{4}=\varepsilon\delta\varepsilon

with
3
edges
v_{i}\overset{r_{i}}{\longleftrightarrow}v_{4}
for
i=1,2,3
;
for
D(1|2)=C(3)
the spine is of the form

+\varepsilon\varepsilon\delta\overset{r_{1}}{\longleftrightarrow}+\delta\varepsilon\delta\overset{r_{2}}{\longleftrightarrow}\delta\delta\varepsilon\overset{r_{3}}{\longleftrightarrow}-\delta\varepsilon\delta\overset{r_{1}}{\longleftrightarrow}-\varepsilon\varepsilon\delta

(see
8.2.2
for
\Sigma_{u}
).

The marking on the edges is the following: an edge

v\overset{r_{i}}{\longleftrightarrow}u
corresponds to the case when
the word of
u
is obtained from the word of
v
by permuting the letter number
i
with the letter number
i+1
, except for the case when one of the words
u,v
has sign
-
and another does not have
sign (this means that we permute
\epsilon
and
\delta
which occupied the last two positions);
in the latter case the edge is marked by
r_{x_{m+n}}
, see the above examples.

Note that the pair
A(m-1|n-1)
,
D(m|n)
is an example of the situation
described in
7.6
. In this case
\operatorname{Sp}(v^{\prime})

(the spine of
A(m-1|n-1)
)
is a proper subgraph of
\operatorname{Sp}(v)
.

The spine admits an involutive automorphism which acts in the following way:

—

preserves the vertices encoded by unsigned words and interchanges
the vertices encoded by the same word with different signs;

—

interchanges the edges marked by
r_{m+n}
with the edges marked by
r_{m+n-1}

and preserves the markings
r_{i}
for
i<m+n-1
.

The image of a vertex
u
has a set of simple roots equal to
s_{\varepsilon_{n}}\Sigma_{u}

up to a permutation of elements. Each orbit of this involutive automorphism
contains a unique representative in
\operatorname{Sp}(v^{\prime})
(the spine of
A(m-1|n-1)
).

By
[
Ker
]
, Section 10
the skeleton for
D(m|n)
is not the Cayley graph of a Coxeter group except
for the case
D(1|1)=C(2)=A(1|0)
.
For example, for
D(2|1)
the graph of the skeleton is isomorphic to a so-called camfered cube
(vertices and edges in the skeleton correspond to vertices and edges in the camfered cube).

G(3)^{(1)}
appears twise: as
G(3)^{(1)}

G(3)^{(2)}

\sigma

[

\sigma

the inner automorphism
\exp(h)

\langle h,\alpha_{2}\rangle=\langle h,\alpha_{3}\rangle=0

Using a well-known presentation of the root system of
