On Constructing Spanners from Random Gaussian Projections
Sepehr Assadi, Michael Kapralov, Huacheng Yu

Abstract
Graph sketching is a powerful paradigm for analyzing graph structure via linear measurements introduced by Ahn, Guha, and McGregor (SODA’12) that has since found numerous applications in streaming, distributed computing, and massively parallel algorithms, among others. Graph sketching has proven to be quite successful for various problems such as connectivity, minimum spanning trees, edge or vertex connectivity, and cut or spectral sparsifiers. Yet, the problem of approximating shortest path metric of a graph, and specifically computing a spanner, is notably missing from the list of successes. This has turned the status of this fundamental problem into one of the most longstanding open questions in this area. We present a partial explanation of this lack of success by proving a strong lower bound for a large family of graph sketching algorithms that encompasses prior work on spanners and many (but importantly not also all) related cut-based problems mentioned above. Our lower bound matches the algorithmic bounds of the recent result of Filtser, Kapralov, and Nouri (SODA’21), up to lower order terms, for constructing spanners via the same graph sketching family. This establishes near-optimality of these bounds, at least restricted to this family of graph sketching techniques, and makes progress on a conjecture posed in this latter work.

We prove

Random Gaussian sketches.

To date, the main success of graph sketching has been for
cut-based
problems
[
AGM12a
,
AGM12b
,
KLM+14
,
GMT15
]
. These sketches all work by encoding a graph
G
as its
{n\choose 2}\times n

signed edge-incidence matrix

B(G)
(see
Section

3.1
) and then apply a sketching matrix
A
with few rows on the left to obtain the sketch
A\cdot B(G)
. The power of these sketches comes from surprisingly powerful cancellations that the use of the signed edge incidence matrix enables. In addition, the sketching matrix
A
of in these approaches implements
a
sparse recovery
scheme on carefully chosen random subgraphs of the input graphs (e.g. uniformly random subgraphs of the input graph in the case of connectivity
[
AGM12a
]
, cut sparsifiers
[
AGM12b
]
, or spectral sparsifiers
[
KLM+14
]
, and sampled vertex induced subgraphs in the case of spanners
[
FKN21
]
).

To give a concrete example, let us consider the AGM sketches
[
AGM12a
]
for finding spanning forests. For any graph
G=(V,E)
and any set of vertices
S\subseteq V
, adding up the columns of
B(G)
corresponding to vertices in
S
, i.e.,
\sum_{v\in S}B(G)^{v}
gives
us a vector with non-zero entries corresponding to edges of the cut
(S,V\setminus S)
. The linearity of matrix
A
then allows us to obtain

A\cdot\left(\sum_{v\in S}B(G)^{v}\right)=\sum_{v\in S}A\cdot B(G)^{v},

for a cut
S
specified in the recovery phase. The sketching matrix
A
itself is an
\ell_{0}
-sampler sketch that samples a non-zero entry of a vector
v
given
A\cdot v
(see
[
JST11
,
KNP+17
]
).
An
\ell_{0}
-sampler sketch is typically implemented
via a simple sparse recovery sketch combined with a sampling matrix that samples the edges of the graph at
O(\log{n})
geometrically decreasing rates. Combined with the above approach, we can thus sample an edge from any cut of the graph specified in
the recovery phase. The algorithm of
[
AGM12a
]
heavily builds on this subroutine by implementing Borůvka’s algorithm for growing connected components via using these sketches to find an outgoing edge
from each component in each step.

In this paper, we focus on this family of sketches where the sparse recovery scheme is implemented using
random Gaussian projections
. This means that each
row

of the sketching matrix is of the type
g\cdot S
where
S
is an
{{n}\choose{2}}\times{{n}\choose{2}}
-dimensional diagonal sampling matrix—where
S_{(u,v),(u,v)}=1
iff

is sampled—and

is an
{{n}\choose{2}}
-dimensional vector of independent Gaussian variables:

\begin{bmatrix}&&&&g&&&&\\
\end{bmatrix}_{1\times{{n}\choose{2}}}\times\begin{bmatrix}&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&\\
&&&S&&\\
&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&\end{bmatrix}_{{{n}\choose{2}}\times{{n}\choose{2}}}\times\begin{bmatrix}&&\\
&&\\
&B(G)&\\
&&\\
&&\end{bmatrix}_{{{n}\choose{2}}\times n}=\begin{bmatrix}&g\cdot S\cdot B(G)&\\
\end{bmatrix}_{1\times{n}}.

The entire sketch is obtained by taking
s
such rows where sampling matrices can be correlated but Gaussian vectors are independent. The recovery algorithm is given sampling matrices and the sketch but
not
Gaussian variables.
We refer to
s
as the
dimension
of the sketch (thus size of the sketch is
O(s\cdot n)
). See
Section

3.1
for formal definitions.

Consider recovering the induced subgraph of the input on the first

Our result.

We prove a near-optimal lower bound on the dimension of random Gaussian sketches for constructing spanners, or even returning the distance of two fixed vertices (see also
Theorem

1
).

Result 1
.

Any random Gaussian sketch for constructing a
d
-spanner with constant probability of success requires dimension
\Omega(n^{1-o(1)}/d^{3/2})
, or put differently, any random Gaussian sketch of dimension
s

can only achieve a stretch of
\Omega((n/s)^{2/3-o(1)})
. The lower bound applies even to the problem of approximating the distance of two fixed vertices.

Our lower bounds in
1
matches algorithmic bounds of
[
FKN21
]
up to the
n^{o(1)}
term for computing spanners via graph sketching (whose sketches fit
the framework of random Gaussian sketches) for all stretch
d
. This establishes the optimality of these bounds at least among this popular family of graph sketching algorithms. We note that
[
FKN21
]
conjectured optimality of their algorithmic
bounds among
all
graph sketching techniques; our bounds in
1
makes partial progress towards settling this conjecture.

Before moving on, we note that for the case when dimension
s=\text{poly}\!\log\!{(n)}
, corresponding
to sketches of size
\widetilde{O}(n)
,
1
implies a lower bound of
n^{2/3-o(1)}
on the stretch; this should be contrasted with the
O(\log{n})
bound of existential results on the stretch of spanners with
O(n)
edges, suggesting that computing spanner
is much harder using graph sketching (specifically via random Gaussian sketches) compared to existential bounds and arbitrary algorithms. Finally, the lower bound holds even for the algorithmically easier problem
of simply estimating distance of two fixed vertices in the graph, as opposed to recovering the entire shortest path metric via a spanner.

Our techniques.

We consider a family of hard instances that form a random chain of cliques of size
(n/d)
with diameter
d
, and a single edge
e^{*}
that connects two vertices
at distance
\Theta(d)
together (see
Figure

1
). It is easy to see that such
e^{*}
should belong to every
o(d)
-spanner of the graph and we prove that no random Gaussian sketch of
“small” dimension can recover
e^{*}
. The proof is through analyzing how much a
single
random Gaussian projection can reveal information about
e^{*}
,
or a bit more formally, the KL-divergence between the resulting sketches of two neighboring graphs that only differ on
e^{*}
. The rest follows by summing up this information across the
s
projections.

To prove the bound for a single projection, we use properties of Gaussian variables and KL-divergence to bound the information revealed about the edge
e^{*}
by the
effective resistance
of the
sampled
subgraph of the input after applying the sampling matrix. We prove that the distribution of our input, combined with a
hierarchical expander decomposition
of all edges of sampling matrix, implies that the sampled subgraph of the input form a chain of
expanders
(with proper lower bounds on both expansion and minimum degree). This step requires analyzing expansion of vertex-sampled subgraphs of an expander which can be of independent interest. Lastly, we bound the effective resistance of the edge
e^{*}
in
this chain of expanders by exhibiting a proper
electrical flow
in the graph using properties of expanders.

Related work.

In a recent independent work Chen, Khanna and Li
[
CKL
]
showed, similarly to our work, a lower bound matching the sketching dimension of
[
FKN21
]
for linear sketches that can support
continuous
weight updates (as opposed to sketches that are only required to work for unweighted graphs). Thus, from the perspective of the ultimate result, the lower bound of
[

]
is incomparable to ours. Their lower bound works for more general sketches than ours (although still not universal), but assumes that these sketches work in the continuous weight update model; our lower bound assumes a special sketch structure, but works in the mode standard setting of unweighted graphs. There is quite a bit of overlap in techniques: both papers use expander decompositions and prove that expanders are preserved under vertex sampling (but the actual proofs of the corresponding lemmas are different).

3.1
Random Gaussian Projections and Sketches

For an
n
-vertex graph
G=(V,E)
, its
signed edge-incidence
matrix is an
{{n}\choose{2}}\times n
-dimensional matrix
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}(G)
defined as follows:

•

Each column corresponds to a vertex
v
and each row corresponds to a pair of vertices
(u,w)
;

•

The entry
\mathbf{B}_{(u,w),v}
is either
+1
if
(u,w)
is an edge in
G
and
v=u
,
-1
if
(u,w)
is an edge in
G
and
v=w
, and
0
otherwise.

Note that for any edge
e=(u,v)
of
G
, the corresponding row
(u,v)
in
\mathbf{B}
has exactly one
+1
at column
u
, one
-1
at column
v
, and is otherwise
0
. A row
(u,v)
of
\mathbf{B}
which does not have a corresponding edge in
G
is the all-
0

Our sketches are based on taking random Gaussian projections of matrix

, which roughly speaking correspond to sampling edge of
G
(using any sampling scheme oblivious to the graph), and multiply a Gaussian vector
with signed edge-incidence matrix of the resulting graph. Formally,

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex graph and consider the following:

(i)

Sampling matrix
: Let
\mathbf{S}
be a
{{n}\choose{2}}\times{{n}\choose{2}}
-dimensional

matrix with
0
-

Notice that the matrix
\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G)
is the edge-incidence matrix of the subgraph of
G
obtained by picking only those edges of
G
that their corresponding (diagonal) value in
\mathbf{S}
is
1
.

(ii)

Gaussian projection
: Let
\mathbf{g}
be a
{{n}\choose{2}}
-dimensional vector of Gaussian random variables, where each entry is sampled independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

A
random Gaussian projection
of

with respect to
\mathbf{S}
is an
n
-dimensional vector obtained by sampling
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{{n}\choose{2}}
,
and returning
\bm{p}:=\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G)
.

Using
Definition

3.1
, we can define the sketches we focus on as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Pi
be a problem defined on
n
-vertex graphs

. A

for

is defined via the following pair:

(i)

: A distribution

(ii)

: An algorithm that given

.

We refer to

as the

).

A random Gaussian sketch for a graph

from

for any constant

In particular,

implies that to obtain a

. This makes progress on a conjecture of
[

]

Finally, we also mention that

works even for the problem wherein we are given two vertices

in the graph using the sketches. This problem is algorithmically easier than finding a spanner of the graph in that firstly, we do not need to pick subset of edges of the graph

and secondly that we only need to maintain the distance between two vertices and not all pairs. Yet, effectively the entirety of our effort is to prove the result for spanners already and we get this stronger lower bound almost for free using standard ideas.

In the rest of this section, we first present a hard input distribution used to establish

1
. We then state our main technical lemma that bounds the information revealed by a single random Gaussian projection
on the graphs sampled from this distribution and show how this lemma easily implies the theorem. The next subsection then includes the proof outline of this technical lemma, whose main ingredients are postponed to the next sections.

KL Divergence Between Sketches of Neighboring Graphs

In this section, we prove

3.5
.
Since the LHS of the inequality is at most one, it suffices to prove the KL-divergence is bounded by the RHS when the effective resistance of

Thus,

is an immediate corollary of the following lemma by setting

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
e=(u,v)
be an edge in
G
with effective resistance at most
1/2
.
Then we have

\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{g}\cdot(\mathbf{B}(G)-\mathbf{B}(e))\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G))\leq\frac{1}{4}\cdot R_{\textnormal{eff}}^{G}(u,v),

where
\mathbf{g}
has independent standard Gaussian coordinates.

Now, observe that both
\mathbf{g}\cdot(\mathbf{B}(G)-\mathbf{B}(e))
and
\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G)
are high-dimensional Gaussian distributions.
To prove
Lemma

4.1
, we will need the following claim on the KL-divergence between two Gaussian distributions.

Claim 4.2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be
n
-dimensional Gaussian distributions with zero mean and covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
respectively.
If
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})
, which have dimension
k
, then

\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\ln\det\left((\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{A}^{+}\right)-k+\text{tr}\left((\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{A}^{+}\right)\right],

where
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{A}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is the pseudoinverse of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
.

Proof.

We view both
P
and
Q
as random
n
-dimensional column vectors.
We first apply the linear transformation
(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}
to both
P
and
Q
.
This does not change the KL-divergence between
P
and
Q
, as it preserves
\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}Q}
.
Note that the covariance matrix
(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}Q
is

\Exp\left[(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}Q\left((\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}Q\right)^{\top}\right]=(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}\mathbf{A}^{+}=(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{+}=\mathbf{I}_{k},

where the last equality uses the fact that
\mathbf{A}
has linearly independent rows, hence
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{+}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
.
Similarly, the covariance matrix of
(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}P
is
\mathbf{\Sigma}=(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{A}^{+}
.
Since
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is invertible.

For simplicity of notations, it suffices to consider
\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)
for covariance matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{I}_{k}
respectively.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}\left[\ln\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}x}/\sqrt{(2\pi)^{k}\det\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)}}{e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}x}/\sqrt{(2\pi)^{k}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}\left[-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}x-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right].

For the first term, since
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}x
is a standard Gaussian,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}\left[x^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}x\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}\left[\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}x\|_{2}^{2}\right]=k
.
For the third term, we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim P}\left[\|x\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{i}{\mbox{Var}}\left[x_{i}\right]=\text{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
.
We have

\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\ln\det\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)-k+\text{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right).

This proves the claim.
∎

In the later proof, we will need the following claim to bound the logarithm of the determinant term from the previous claim.

Claim 4.3
.

For a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
with
\left\|\mathbf{A}\right\|_{2}\leq 1/2
one has
\ln\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A})\geq\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})-\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\ln\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\ln\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\lambda_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(1+\lambda_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i}^{2})=\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})-\text{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{2}),

where the inequality uses the fact that
\ln(1+x)\geq x-x^{2}
for all
\left|x\right|\leq 1/2
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Lemma

4.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

We apply
4.2

and

\mathbf{B}(G)^{\top}\mathbf{B}(G)-b_{e}b_{e}^{\top}=\mathbf{L}-b_{e}b_{e}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{B}(G)^{\top}\mathbf{B}(G)=\mathbf{L}

We get by

\begin{split}&\quad\,\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{g}\cdot(\mathbf{B}(G)-\mathbf{B}_{u,v})\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\ln\det((\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}(L-b_{e}b_{e}^{\top})\mathbf{A}^{+})-k+\text{tr}((\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\top}(L-b_{e}b_{e}^{\top})\mathbf{A}^{+})\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\ln\det(\mathbf{I}-ww^{\top})-k+\text{tr}(\mathbf{I}-ww^{\top}))\right],\end{split}

(4)

where

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{g}\cdot(\mathbf{B}(G)-\mathbf{B}_{u,v})\,\,||\,\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{B}(G))&=\frac{1}{2}\left[-\ln\det(\mathbf{I}-ww^{\top})-k+\text{tr}(\mathbf{I}-ww^{\top}))\right]\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[\|w\|_{2}^{2}+\|w\|_{2}^{4}-k+(k-\|w\|_{2}^{2})\right]\\
&=\|w\|_{2}^{4}/2\\
&=R_{\textnormal{eff}}^{G}(u,v)^{2}/2\\
&\leq R_{\textnormal{eff}}^{G}(u,v)/4.\end{split}

∎

Without loss of generality assume that
j=i+1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(S\cap U_{j})

\displaystyle\geq 2\left|E(S\cap V_{j},S\cap V_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(S\cap U_{i})-2\left|E(S\cap V_{i},S\cap V_{i})\right|-2\left|E(S\cap V_{i},S\cap V_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{8}\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(U_{i})-2\left|E(V_{i},V_{i})\right|-2\left|E(V_{i},V_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{8}\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(U_{i})-\left(\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(U_{i})-2\left|E(V_{j},V_{j})\right|\right)

\displaystyle=2\left|E(V_{j},V_{j})\right|-\frac{1}{8}\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(U_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\text{vol}_{H_{i}}(U_{i}),

where the last inequality uses property
\bf(3)
of balanced path of expanders.

Then by property
\bf(2)
, we have
\frac{1}{24}\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(U_{j})\leq\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(S\cap U_{j})\leq\frac{7}{8}\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(U_{j})
, and by the expansion property in
H_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\,\,|E(S,V\setminus S)|

\displaystyle\geq|E_{j}(S\cap U_{j},U_{j}\setminus S)|

\displaystyle\geq\varphi\cdot\min\{\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(S),\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(U_{j}\setminus S)\}

\displaystyle\geq\varphi\cdot(1/24)\text{vol}_{H_{j}}(U_{j})

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(\varphi)\text{vol}(U_{1}).

This proves the lemma.
∎

We are ready to prove
Lemma

5.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Fix a pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V
. Let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be the unit electrical flow from
u
to
v
, and let
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
be the corresponding vector of potentials.
Recall that the flow on an edge
(a,b)
satisfies
f_{ab}=\phi_{a}-\phi_{b}
, and that the effective resistance between
u
and
v
satisfies
R_{\textnormal{eff}}(u,v)=\phi_{u}-\phi_{v}
.

We define a sequence of thresholds
\theta_{j},j\geq 0
and define

L_{j}=\{w\in V:\phi_{w}\geq\theta_{j}\}.

Let
\theta_{0}=\phi_{u}
, so that
L_{0}
contains
u
. Fix
j
such that
v\notin L_{j}
. Note that since
u\in L_{j},v\in V\setminus L_{j}
, we have that the total electrical flow across the cut
(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})
is one. Since for every edge
(a,b)
the flow on
(a,b)
is
\phi_{a}-\phi_{b}
, we get

\sum_{(a,b)\in E:a\in L_{j},b\in V\setminus L_{j}}(\phi_{a}-\phi_{b})=1.

(6)

By the definition of
L_{j}
, we must have
\phi_{a}>\phi_{b}
for all
(a,b)\in E
and
a\in L_{j},b\in V\setminus L_{j}
.
Thus, at most
\frac{1}{2}|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|
such edges
(a,b)
can have
\phi_{a}-\phi_{b}\geq\frac{2}{|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|}
.

This allows us to define

\theta_{j+1}=\theta_{j}-\frac{2}{|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|}.

Then for all edges
(a,b)\in E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})
such that
\phi_{a}-\phi_{b}<\frac{2}{|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|}
,
b
must be in
L_{j+1}
.
Therefore,

\text{vol}(L_{j+1})\geq\text{vol}(L_{j})+\frac{1}{2}|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|.

We now lower bound
|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|
. We have by Claim
5.6
that

|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|\geq\Omega(\varphi)\cdot\min\{\text{vol}(L_{j}),\text{vol}(U_{1})\},

and therefore

\text{vol}(L_{j+1})\geq\text{vol}(L_{j})+\Omega(\varphi)\cdot\min\{\text{vol}(L_{j}),\text{vol}(U_{1})\}.

Let
j^{*}
be the smallest such that
\text{vol}(L_{j^{*}})>\text{vol}(U_{1})
. For all
j\leq j^{*}
we have

\begin{split}\text{vol}(L_{j})&\geq\text{vol}(L_{j-1})+\Omega(\varphi)\cdot\text{vol}(L_{j-1})\\
&=(1+\Omega(\varphi))\text{vol}(L_{j-1})\\
&\geq(1+\Omega(\varphi))^{j}\text{vol}(L_{0})\\
&\geq(1+\Omega(\varphi))^{j}d_{\min}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}|E(L_{j},V\setminus L_{j})|&\geq\Omega(\varphi)\text{vol}(L_{j})\\
&\geq\Omega(\varphi)(1+\Omega(\varphi))^{j}d_{\min}.\end{split}

This means in particular that

\begin{split}\theta_{j^{*}}-\theta_{0}&\leq\sum_{j<j^{*}}\frac{2}{\Omega(\varphi)\text{vol}(L_{j})}\\
&\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{O(1)}{\varphi d_{\min}}(1+\Omega(\varphi))^{-j}\\
&=\frac{O(1)}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}.\end{split}

Let
J
be the smallest such that
\text{vol}(L_{j})>\frac{1}{2}\text{vol}(V)
.
We now consider
j^{*}<j\leq J
. Here we have

|E(L_{j-1},V\setminus L_{j-1})|\geq\Omega(\varphi)\text{vol}(U_{1}).

Since

\text{vol}(L_{j})\geq\text{vol}(L_{j-1})+\frac{1}{2}|E(L_{j-1},V\setminus L_{j-1})|\geq\text{vol}(L_{j})+\Omega(\varphi)\cdot\text{vol}(U_{1}),

we must have
J=O(\text{vol}(V)/(\varphi\cdot\text{vol}(U_{1})))=O(d/\varphi)
(recall that
d
is the number of layers). Recalling that

\theta_{j}=\theta_{j-1}+\frac{2}{|E(L_{j-1},V\setminus L_{j-1})|},

we now get that

\theta_{J}-\theta_{j^{*}}\leq O(d/\varphi)\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi\cdot\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right)=O\left(\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right).

Putting the above bounds together, we have

\theta_{J}-\theta_{0}\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right).

That is, by the definition of
J
, the volume of all vertices
w
with

\phi_{u}-\phi_{w}\leq c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right)

(for a sufficiently large constant
c
) is more than
\frac{1}{2}\text{vol}(V)
.

By a symmetric argument from vertex
v
, we get that the volume of all vertices
w
with

\phi_{w}-\phi_{v}\leq c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right)

(for a sufficiently large constant
c
) is also more than
\frac{1}{2}\text{vol}(V)
.
In particular, there exists one
w
such that both
\phi_{u}-\phi_{w}
and
\phi_{w}-\phi_{v}
are at most
c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right)
, implying that

\phi_{u}-\phi_{v}\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right).

Hence,

R_{\textnormal{eff}}(u,v)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{d}{\varphi^{2}\text{vol}(U_{1})}\right),

as required.
∎

6
Vertex-Sample Expanders

In this section, we prove
Lemma

5.4
.
To prove that the subsampled graph
H_{\mathrm{sp}}
is also an expander, instead of working with conductance, we will apply Cheeger’s inequality (
Proposition

B.5
), and work with the spectral gap.

More specifically, for expander
H
, we first focus on the matrix
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
.
From the spectral gap of
H
and the properties of the normalized Laplacian matrix, we know that the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{M}
is
1
, and all other eigenvalues are nonnegative and bounded away from
1
.
To prove that
H_{\mathrm{sp}}
is also an expander, we will study the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}
defined similarly.
The idea is to prove that for some large integer
t
,
\text{tr}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})
is close to
1
with high probability.
This gives an estimate on the sum of the
t
-th power of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}
.
In particular, since its largest eigenvalue is still
1
, the
t
-th power of the second largest eigenvalue must be very small, implying a non-trival spectral gap.

The key lemma in this argument is
Lemma

6.1
, which proves an upper bound on the
q
-th moment of
\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})
(
\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}
is an approximation of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}
that is easier to work with).
By applying the standard argument for obtaining concentration from moment bounds, we prove that
\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})
(and hence,
\text{tr}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})
) is small with very high probability.

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

Since
H
is an
\varepsilon
-expander, by

B.5
, the spectral gap of
H
is at least
\varepsilon^{2}/2
.
That is, let
\mathbf{A}
be the adjacency matrix of
H
and
\mathbf{D}
be its degree matrix, the top eigenvalue of the matrix

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\right)

is equal to
1
, and all other eigenvalues are between
0
and
1-\varepsilon^{2}/4
.

Let
S
be the set of vertices that are present in
H_{\mathrm{sp}}
, and let
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{sp}}
be the
n\times n
matrix obtained by zeroing all columns and rows of
\mathbf{A}
corresponding to
\overline{S}
.
Equivalently,
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{sp}}
is the adjacency matrix of

embedded in an

Let
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sp}}
be its degree matrix.
It suffices to upper bound the second largest eigenvalue of

\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}_{S}+\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{-1/2}\right),

where
\mathbf{I}_{S}

To this end, we will first focus on the matrix

\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}_{S}+p^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\right),

where we replaced the degree matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sp}}
by the expected degree
p\cdot\mathbf{D}
, then show that it is close to
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}}
with high probability.

To bound the second largest eigenvalue of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, we analyze the trace of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t}
for some integer

, and apply the fact that the trace is equal to the sum of eigenvalues.
We have the following moment bound for the trace.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any

,
t^{4}\leq p\cdot d_{\min}
,
q\geq 1
and

, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})}^{q}\right]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})}^{q-1}\right]\cdot\left(\text{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{t})+\frac{(q+4)t^{3}}{p\cdot d_{\min}}\right).

Recall that in Lemma
6.1
above
\delta>0
is a parameter such that
d_{\min}\cdot p\geq n^{\delta}
. We defer the proof of Lemma
6.1
to the end of this section.
Now fix
t
to be any integer between
\frac{256\ln n}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}
and
(p\cdot d_{\min})^{1/4}

Since
\text{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{t})\leq 1+n\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{2}/2)^{t}\leq 1+n^{-32}

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})}^{q}\right]\leq\left(1+n^{-32}+(q+4)\cdot n^{-\delta/4}\right)^{q}\leq e^{2q^{2}\cdot n^{-\delta/4}},

for any

By setting

, and by Markov’s inequality, we have

\Pr\left[\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})^{q}>e^{4q^{2}\cdot n^{-\delta/4}}\right]<n^{-\omega(1)}.

It implies that

\Pr\left[\text{tr}(\overline{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{t})\geq 1+8n^{-\delta/8}\cdot\log n\right]\leq n^{-\omega(1)}.

Since
H
has minimum degree

, by Chernoff bound, all vertices

in

have degree at least

This implies that for all edges

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sp}})_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{sp},u}^{1/2}\cdot d_{\mathrm{sp},v}^{1/2}}

To prove Lemma

Lemma 6.2

\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{M}^{k}\right)_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{u}^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{v}

Appendix B
Background

This appendix includes a summary of basic tools from probability and information theory, and spectral graph theory that we use in our paper.

B.1
Background in Probability and Information Theory

The proof of basic facts included in this part can be found in
[
CT06
]
.

KL-divergence.

For continuous distributions
P
and
Q
, the
Kullback–Leibler divergence
(KL-divergence) of
P
from
Q
is

\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)={\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim P}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathbf{d}P(X)}{\mathbf{d}Q(X)}\right)\right].

We may abuse the notation
\mathbb{D}(X\,\,||\,\,Y)
for random variables
X
and
Y
to denote the KL-divergence of the distribution of
X
from the distribution of
Y
.

Fact B.1

(Chain rule of KL-divergence)
.

Let
P(X,Y)
and
Q(X,Y)
be two distributions for a pair of random variables
X
and
Y
. Then,

\mathbb{D}(P(X,Y)\,\,||\,\,Q(X,Y))=\mathbb{D}(P(X)\,\,||\,\,Q(X))+\Exp_{x\sim P}\left[\mathbb{D}(P(Y\mid X=x)\,\,||\,\,Q(Y\mid X=x))\right].

In particular, if
X\perp Y
in both distributions
P
and
Q
, then,

\mathbb{D}(P(X,Y)\,\,||\,\,Q(X,Y))=\mathbb{D}(P(X)\,\,||\,\,Q(X))+\mathbb{D}(P(Y)\,\,||\,\,Q(Y)).

Total variation distance.

Similarly, for continuous distributions
P
and
Q
over the same sample space
\Omega
, the
total variation distance
(TVD) between
P
and
Q
is

\left\lVert P-Q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}=\sup_{\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega}\left|{P(\Omega^{\prime})-Q(\Omega^{\prime})}\right|.

Fact B.2
.

Suppose we are given a single sample
s
chosen uniformly at random from either distribution
P
or
Q
. The best probability of success in determining the source of
s
is

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert P-Q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}.

Fact B.3
.

Let
P(X,Y)
and
Q(X,Y)
be two distributions for a pair of random variables
X
and
Y
. Then,

\left\lVert P(X,Y)-Q(X,Y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}\leq\left\lVert P(X)-Q(X)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}+\Exp_{x\sim P}\left[\left\lVert P(Y\mid X=x)-Q(Y\mid X=x)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}\right].

In particular, if
X
has the same marginal distribution in
P
and
Q
, then

\left\lVert P(X,Y)-Q(X,Y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}\leq\Exp_{x\sim P}\left\lVert P(Y\mid X=x)-Q(Y\mid X=x)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}.

Pinsker’s inequality relates KL-divergence to total variation distance.

Fact B.4

(Pinsker’s inequality)
.

For any pairs of distributions
P
and
Q
over the same domain,

\left\lVert P-Q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{tvd}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{D}(P\,\,||\,\,Q)}.

B.2
Background in Spectral Graph Theory

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph on
n
vertices.
Let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
be the degrees.
For a vertex set
S\subseteq V
, its
volume
in
G
is

\text{vol}_{G}(S):=\sum_{u\in S}d_{u}.

When there is no ambiguity, we may omit the subscript
G
, and denote it by
\text{vol}(S)
.

For
S,T\subseteq V
,
E(S,T)
is the set of edges between
S
and
T
, i.e.,
E(S,T):=\{(u,v)\in E:u\in S,v\in T\}
.
The
conductance
of
G
is

\varphi(G):=\min_{S\subseteq V}\frac{\left|E(S,V\setminus S)\right|}{\min\{\text{vol}(S),\text{vol}(V\setminus S)\}}.

We say that
G
is a
\varphi
-expander
if its conductance is at least
\varphi
.

We associate the following matrices with a graph
G=(V,E)
:

•

The
adjacency matrix

\mathbf{A}
of
G
the
n\times n
matrix such that
\mathbf{A}_{u,v}=1
iff
(u,v)
is an edge in
E
.

•

The
degree diagonal matrix

\mathbf{D}
of
G
is the
n\times n
matrix such that
\mathbf{D}_{v,v}=d_{v}
and
0
elsewhere.

•

The
signed edge-incidence matrix

\mathbf{B}
of
G
is the
{{n}\choose{2}}\times n
matrix such that
\mathbf{B}_{(u,v),w}
is
1
if
w=u
and
(u,v)
is an edge in
E
,
-1
if
w=v
and
(u,v)
is an edge in
E
, and
0
otherwise.

•

The
Laplacian matrix

\mathbf{L}
is the
n\times n
matrix

\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}b_{e}b_{e}^{\top},

where
b_{e}
is the vector with value
1
in coordinate
u
,
-1
in coordinate
v
, and
0
in all other coordinates.

•

The
normalized Laplacian matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
is the
n\times n
matrix such that

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&i=j,\\
-\frac{1}{(d_{i}d_{j})^{1/2}}&(i,j)\in E,\\
0&\textrm{o.w.}\end{cases}

Equivalently,

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}.

Spectral gap:

For any graph
G
, both
\mathbf{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
are positive semidefinite.
For
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
, the smallest eigenvalue is zero, with eigenvector
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the all-one vector.
Its largest eigenvalue is at most
2
.
The
spectral gap
of
G
is the second smallest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}
.
Cheeger’s inequality relates the conductance and the spectral gap.

Proposition B.5

(Cheeger’s inequality,
[
Chu96
]
Section 3)
.

For any graph
G
, let
\lambda
be its spectral gap, and
\varphi
be its conductance, we have

2\varphi\geq\lambda\geq\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}.

Effective resistance.

By treating each edge of
G
as a resistor with unit resistance, we denote the
effective resistance
between
u,v
by
R_{\textnormal{eff}}^{G}(u,v)
.
For any pair of vertices
(u,v)
, we have

R_{\textnormal{eff}}^{G}(u,v)=b_{(u,v)}^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{+}b_{(u,v)},

where
\mathbf{L}^{+}
is the
pseudoinverse
of
\mathbf{L}
.

Expander decomposition.

We also use the following (variant of) expander decomposition that bounds the minimum degree of resulting expanders. The proof is a simple modification of standard decompositions, e.g. in
[
KVV00
,
SW19
]
, and is provided only for completeness.

Proposition

(Restatement of
Proposition

2.1
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be any arbitrary graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
d_{\min}\geq 1
be parameters. The vertices of
G
can be partitioned into subgraphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
such that:

(
i)

Each
H_{i}
is an
\varepsilon
-expander with minimum degree
d_{\min}
;

(
ii)

At most
8\varepsilon\cdot m\log{n}+n\cdot d_{\min}
edges
E_{0}
of
G
do not belong to any subgraph
\{H_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}
.

Proof.

We use a standard sparse cut pruning (contributing the first term in extra edges) plus low degree pruning for vertices with degree
<d_{\min}
(contributing the second term).

Firstly, remove all
vertices
with degree
<d_{\min}
from
G
and include their edges in
E_{0}
.
If
G
at this point is an
\varepsilon
-expander, we terminate.
Otherwise find a set
S\subseteq V
such that
\text{vol}(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{vol}(V)
and
\left|E(S,V\setminus S)\right|<\varepsilon\cdot\text{vol}(S)
.
Partition the graph along the cut
(S,V\setminus S)
and insert all edges in
E(S,V\setminus S)
to
E_{0}
.
Recursively repeat both steps on the subgraphs induced by
S
and
V\setminus S
respectively. At the end, return the resulting expanders as the collection
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
in
part
(i)
and the removed edges
E_{0}
as the extra edges in part
(ii)
.

Firstly, we will only include at most
n\cdot d_{\min}
edges in
E_{0}
when removing vertices with degree
<d_{\min}
in total. Moreover, whenever we remove edges
E(S,V\setminus S)
, we can “charge”
the removed edges to the edges with both endpoints in
S
.
Since
\left|E(S,V\setminus S)\right|<\varepsilon\cdot\text{vol}(S)
, every edge in
S
is charged at most
2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)\leq 4\varepsilon
.
Since
\text{vol}(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{vol}(V)
, which is the smaller side of the cut, each edge can be charged at most
\log\!{{n}\choose{2}}<2\log n
times, summing up to at most
8\varepsilon\log n
.
This proves the proposition.
∎
