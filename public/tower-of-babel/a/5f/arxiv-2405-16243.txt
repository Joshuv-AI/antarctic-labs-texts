A Complete Invariant for Shift Equivalence for Boolean Matrices and Finite Relations
Ethan Akin, Marian Mrozek, Mateusz Przybylski, Jim Wiseman

Abstract
. We give a complete invariant for shift equivalence for Boolean matrices (equivalently finite relations), in terms of the period, the induced partial order on recurrent components, and the cohomology class of the relation on those components.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a finite set. A relation
R
on
X
is a subset of
X\times X
and associated to it is a subshift of finite type,
an important and well-studied dynamical system. The relation can equivalently be described by a Boolean matrix (a
(0,1)
-matrix for which
matrix addition and multiplication are induced by Boolean algebra, where
1+1=0+1=1
).
For such a relation (or matrix)
shift equivalence
is a natural equivalence relation with important dynamical significance.
It is strictly weaker than conjugacy, and corresponds roughly to “eventual conjugacy” (
[
9
]
).
It is useful in the classification of such subshifts and it arises in
defining a Conley index for computational approximations of dynamical systems. Classifications of shift
equivalence are given in
[
3
,
4
,
10
]
. Here, we give a complete invariant in terms of the period,
the induced partial order on recurrent components, and the cohomology class of the relation on those components.

Our main theorem is as follows. (We define the relevant terms more precisely in
sections
2
and
3
.) There exists a least integer

p\geq 1

such that there exists an integer
N>0
such that
R^{n+p}=R^{n}
for all
n\geq N
; we call
p
the

period
of
R
. Additionally,
R
induces a partial order
R_{\geq}
on the strongly connected components,
determined by which components map to which. Finally, a choice of representatives of the strongly connected components
induces a cocycle
\xi:R_{\geq}\to\mathcal{L}_{p}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is the
additive monoid of non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
;
we denote the cohomology class of
\xi
by
[\xi]
.

Theorem
.

For a finite relation
R
, the triple
(R_{\geq}
,
p
,
[\xi])
is a complete invariant of shift equivalence.

2.
Background and definitions

Let
X
and
Y
be sets. A
relation
from
X
to
Y

is a subset
R\subseteq X\times Y
. If
X=Y
we call
R
a

relation on

X
, and denote the pair by
(X,R)
. For
x\in X
and
A\subseteq X
we define

R(x):=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in R\},

R(A):=\bigcup\{R(x):x\in A\}.

Given a relation
R\subseteq X\times Y
, the
reverse relation

R^{-1}\subseteq Y\times X
is defined by
R^{-1}:=\{(y,x):(x,y)\in R\}
.

Given another relation
S\subseteq Y\times Z
we define the
composition
of
S
with
R
as the relation

S\circ R:=\{(x,z)\in X\times Z:\text{ $(x,y)\in R$ and $(y,z)\in S$ for some $y\in Y$}\}.

As with functions, composition of relations is associative.

The identity relation on
X
is
\operatorname{id}_{X}=\{(x,x):x\in X\}
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
the
n
th power
of a relation
R
in
X
is given recursively by

R^{n}:=\begin{cases}\operatorname{id}_{X}&\text{ for $n=0$,}\\
R\circ R^{n-1}&\text{ for $n>0$.}\\
\end{cases}

From associativity it follows that
R^{i}\circ R^{j}=R^{i+j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the set of nonnegative integers.

Now let
X
be a finite set. We can think of a relation on
X
in various ways: as a Boolean matrix, a directed graph,
a multi-valued map, or a morphism in an appropriately defined category. We discuss each briefly.

Boolean matrix

If
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, then we can identify a relation
R
on
X
with the
n\times n
Boolean
matrix
A
with
i,j
entry given by 1 if
x_{i}\in R(x_{j})
and 0 otherwise. It is easy to check
that
A^{n}
(where matrix multiplication is Boolean) is the matrix for the relation
R^{n}
. Thus
any statement about finite relations has a corresponding linear algebraic statement about Boolean
matrices.
Furthermore, Boolean matrices describe the structure of nonnegative matrices,
since the map sending positive numbers to 1 and 0 to 0 is a homomorphism. See
[
8
]
for details, and
[
1
]
for applications.

Directed graph

We can identify a finite relation
(X,R)
with the directed graph
whose vertices are the elements of
X
and with an edge from
x
to
y
if and only
if
y\in R(x)
. Thus, there is a path in the graph of length
n
from
x
to
y
if and only if
y\in R^{n}(x)
.
It is often convenient to think of
(X,R)
in this way and to use graph-theoretic terminology.
The Boolean matrix corresponds to the adjacency matrix for the directed graph.

Multi-valued map

We can think of a relation as a multi-valued map, where the image of a
point
x
is the set
R(x)
. (In general, the image could be empty, but when
considering shift equivalence, we can assume that it contains at least one
point (
[
10
]
Proposition 13). This is equivalent to the condition that
every vertex in the directed graph have at least one outgoing edge, or the
condition that every column of the Boolean matrix have at least one 1.)
From a dynamical systems point of view, the powers
R^{n}
tell us about the
long-term behavior of the map under iteration. In this context, finite relations
can arise from ordinary (single-valued) maps in the form of numerical approximations or reconstructions from sampled data.

The category
\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}}
.

The objects in the category
\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}}
are finite sets, and the morphisms
from set
X
to set
Y
are
the relations from
X
to
Y
. Composition of
morphisms is composition of relations, and the identity morphism is the identity relation.
This is the viewpoint we will adopt when we consider the Szymczak functor and shift equivalence below.

The category
\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
.

This is the category of endomorphisms of
\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}}
.
The objects of
\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
are pairs
(X,R)
, where
X
is a finite set
(an object in
\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}}
) and
R
is a relation on
X
(a morphism from
X
to
X
).
A morphism
A
from
(X,R)
to
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
(written
A:(X,R)\to(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
) is a relation
A

from
X
to
X^{\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}\circ A=A\circ R
.

Definition 2.1
.

Two finite relations
(X,R)
and
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
are
shift equivalent

if there exist morphisms
A:(X,R)\to(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
and
B:(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})\to(X,R)
such that
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
B\circ A=R^{\ell}
and
A\circ B=(R^{\prime})^{\ell}
.

If we think of
R
and
R^{\prime}
as Boolean matrices, then this is
just the usual notion of shift equivalence for Boolean matrices (
[
3
]
).
Working from
\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
we define a category for which shift equivalence is the isomorphism concept.

The category
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
.

We can now define the Szymczak category
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
(cf.
[
11
]
).
The objects of
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
are the objects of
\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
.
Given objects
(X,R)
and
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
in
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
, we consider the equivalence relation
given by
(A_{1},m_{1})\equiv(A_{2},m_{2})
, for
A_{1},A_{2}:(X,R)\to(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
and
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, if and only if there exists a

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
A_{1}\circ R^{m_{2}+k}=A_{2}\circ R^{m_{1}+k}
, or equivalently
(R^{\prime})^{m_{2}+k}\circ A_{1}=(R^{\prime})^{m_{1}+k}\circ A_{2}
.
The morphisms in
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
from
(X,R)
to
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
are the equivalence classes of
\equiv
.
Composition is given by
[A^{\prime},m^{\prime}]\circ[A,m]=[A^{\prime}\circ A,m+m^{\prime}]
and
[\operatorname{id}_{X},0]
is the identity morphism on
(X,R)
.

For example, for any
(X,R)\in\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
R^{m}:(X,R)\to(X,R)
is a morphism with
[R^{m},m]=[id_{X},0]
.

The functor
\text{\sc Szym}:\End(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})\to\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
fixes objects and sends a morphism
A
to
[A,0]
.

As shown in
[
2
]
Proposition 8.1,

(X,R)
and
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
are shift equivalent if and only if they are isomorphic in the Szymczak category. Notice that if

[A,m]:(X,R)\to(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
and
[B,n]:(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})\to(X,R)
are inverse isomorphisms in
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
,
then for some
k,\ BAR^{k}=R^{m+n+k}
and
AR^{k}B=AB(R^{\prime})^{k}=(R^{\prime})^{m+n+k}
. So

A\circ R^{k}:(X,R)\to(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
and
B:(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})\to(X,R)
yield the shift equivalence with
\ell=m+n+k
.

Shift equivalence (Szymczak isomorphism) is an important notion dynamically, when
we consider the long-term behavior of a relation. Very roughly speaking, it
ignores transient behavior and focuses on the dynamics within and between recurrent components.
The same is true when we consider powers of graphs. In
[
3
,
4
]
, Kim and Roush consider shift
equivalence of Boolean matrices in part as a step toward disproving the Williams Conjecture from
symbolic dynamics (
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
). They provide the following algebraic classification of Boolean matrices up to shift equivalence.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
4
]
Proposition 3.5)
.

Every matrix is shift equivalent to a periodic power of itself. Two periodic
matrices are shift equivalent if and only if after deletion of dependent rows and columns, they are conjugate by a permutation matrix.

Another important application is in defining the discrete Conley index
for continuous self-maps
[
11
]
, which provides information about invariant sets.
The form of the index relies on choices made in its construction, and it is defined exactly up to shift equivalence.
In this context it is useful to have a dynamical, rather than algebraic, classification of finite relations up to shift equivalence. Such a classification was given in
[
10
]
as a step toward defining a Conley
index for such relations, particularly those approximating physical systems.

To state the result from
[
10
]
on shift equivalence, and to describe the complete invariant in the next section, we need a few more definitions.

For a relation
R
on
X
, we write
x\to_{R}y
if there is a path from
x
to
y
in the graph corresponding to
R
, or,
equivalently, if
y\in R^{n}(x)
for some

n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of positive integers. We say that
x
is
recurrent

if
x\to_{R}x
, and denote by
X_{R}
the set of recurrent points. We write
x\leftrightarrow_{R}y
if
y\to_{R}x
and
x\to_{R}y
. For the
purposes of shift equivalence, we can assume that every point is recurrent (
[
10
]
Theorem 11),
so the relation
\leftrightarrow_{R}
is an equivalence relation; in accordance with graph theory
usage, we call the equivalence classes the
strongly connected components
.
For a recurrent point
x
, we denote the strongly connected component to which it belongs by
[x]_{R}
,
and the quotient
X/\leftrightarrow_{R}
(the set of strongly connected components) by
\overline{X}
.

Observe that
\to_{R}
induces a partial order on
\overline{X}
as follows:
[x]_{R}\geq[y]_{R}
if
x\to_{R}y
, which is
clearly independent of the choice of representatives of
[x]_{R}
and
[y]_{R}
. For notational clarity, we
will somewhat redundantly denote the relationship
\geq
by
R_{\geq}
, that is,
([x]_{R},[y]_{R})\in R_{\geq}
if and only if
[x]_{R}\geq[y]_{R}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a finite set. A relation
R
on
X
is in
canonical form
if

(1)

every point of
X
is recurrent,

(2)

R
is a bijection (and hence cyclic) on each strongly connected component, and

(3)

R
is periodic, that is, there exists a least integer
p\geq 1
, called the period of
R
, such that
R^{p+1}=R
.

Shift equivalence can be characterized as follows.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
10
]
Theorems 12, 13)
.

Let
(X,R)
be a finite relation. Then
(X,R)
is shift equivalent to a finite relation in canonical form.
Furthermore, two relations
(X,R)
and
(X^{\prime},R^{\prime})
in canonical form are shift equivalent if and only if
they are isomorphic, that is, there is a bijection
h:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
h\circ R=R^{\prime}\circ h
.

The reduction to canonical form is constructive. We sketch the steps.

Periodicity

Since the infinite sequence
\{R^{n}\}
consists of subsets of a finite set, it follows that the set

(1)

\Per(R)=\{q\in\mathbb{N}:R^{q+N(q)}=R^{N(q)}\ \text{for some}\ N(q)\in\mathbb{N}\}

is nonempty. Clearly, if
n\geq N(q)
, then
R^{q+n}=R^{n}
.

Definition 2.5
.

An
eventual period
for
R
is
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that
R^{q}\circ R^{q}=R^{q}
and so
R^{q+n}=R^{n}
for all
n\geq q
.
In particular,
R^{nq}=R^{q}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

An eventual period
q
is an element of
\Per(R)
. On the other hand, if
q\in\Per(R)
and
Nq\geq N(q)
, then
Nq
is an eventual period.

Proposition 2.6
.

(a)

There exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Per(R)=p\mathbb{N}
. In particular, if
q
is an eventual period, then
p|q
.
We call
p
the
period
of
R
(despite the fact that it need not be an eventual period).

(b)

If
q_{1},q_{2}
are eventual periods, then
R^{q_{1}}=R^{q_{2}}
.

(c)

If
q
is an eventual period and
p
is the period, then
R^{q+p}=R^{q}
.

(d)

Define
\bar{R}=R^{q+1}
where
q
is an eventual period. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2)

\bar{R}^{n}=R^{q+n}=\bar{R}^{p+n}

In particular,
\bar{R}^{np}=R^{q}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(e)

The period
p
is the minimum element of the set
\{p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}:R^{q+p^{\prime}}=R^{q}\}
.

Proof.

(a) For
q_{1},q_{2}\in\Per(R),
if
M\geq N(q_{1}),N(q_{2})
, then
R^{q_{1}+q_{2}+M}=R^{q_{2}+M}=R^{M}.
Thus,
\Per(R)
is an additive sub-semigroup
of
\mathbb{N}
. Let
p
be the gcd of
\Per(R)
. It follows that for sufficiently large
N
,
Np\in\Per(R)
. For a proof of this classical result
see, e.g.
[
10
]
Lemma 1. It follows that
p|q
for all
q\in\Per(R)
. Let
N
be large enough that
Np
and
(N+1)p
are in
\Per(R)
. If

M\geq N(Np),N((N+1)p)
, then
R^{p+M}=R^{Np+p+M}=R^{(N+1)p+M}=R^{M}.
Thus,
p\in\Per(R)
and so is every multiple of
p
.

(b) Assume
q_{1}\leq q_{2}
. Since
q_{1}
is an eventual period,
R^{q_{1}+q_{2}}=R^{q_{2}}
. Choose
N
so that
Nq_{1}\geq q_{2}
. Since

q_{1}
is an eventual period
R^{Nq_{1}}=R^{q_{1}}
. Hence, because
q_{2}
is an eventual period,
R^{q_{1}}=R^{Nq_{1}}=R^{Nq_{1}+q_{2}}=R^{q_{1}+q_{2}}
.

(c) Since
p\in\Per(R)
, for large enough
N
,
R^{p+q}=R^{p+Nq}=R^{Nq}=R^{q}
, where again we use
R^{Nq}=R^{q}
.

(d)
\bar{R}^{n}=R^{(q+1)n}=R^{nq+n}=R^{q+n}
since
R^{nq}=R^{q}
.
In particular,
\bar{R}^{p+n}=R^{q+p+n}=R^{q+n}
. Furthermore,

\bar{R}^{np}=R^{q+np}=R^{q}
by induction on
n
.

(e) If
R^{q+p^{\prime}}=R^{q}
, then
p^{\prime}\in\Per(R)
and so
p|p^{\prime}
.
∎

We now fix an eventual period
q
.

It easily follows that
[R^{q},q]:(X,R)\to(X,\bar{R})
and
[R^{q},q]:(X,\bar{R})\to(X,R)
are inverse isomorphisms in
\text{\sc Szym}(\text{\sc Rel}_{\mathrm{f}})
. For details
see
[
10
]
Theorem 9.

Recurrence

Recall that
x
is recurrent for
R
if
x\rightarrow_{R}x
or, equivalently,
x\in R^{n}(x)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It then follows that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in R^{kn}(x)
and so
x
is recurrent for
R^{k}
. Thus,
x
is recurrent for
R
if and only if
it is recurrent for
R^{k}
. In particular,
x
is recurrent for
R
if and only if for
R^{q}
and so if and only if for some
n
,

x\in R^{nq}(x)=R^{q}(x)
. Thus
X_{R}
, the set of recurrent points, equals
\{x:x\in R^{q}(x)=\bar{R}^{p}(x)\}
.

In general,
x\rightarrow_{R}y
if
y\in R^{n}(x)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,
x\rightarrow_{\bar{R}}y

if
y\in\bar{R}^{n}(x)=R^{q+n}(x)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
y\in R^{n}(x)
and
x\in X_{R}
, then
x\in R^{q}(x)
and so
y\in R^{q+n}(x)
.
Similarly, if
y\in X_{R}
, then
y\in R^{q+n}(x)
. Thus, if
x
or
y
is recurrent, then
x\rightarrow_{R}y
if and only if
x\rightarrow_{\bar{R}}y
.
In particular, if
x\leftrightarrow_{R}y
then both
x
and
y
are recurrent and so
x\leftrightarrow_{\bar{R}}y
.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
x\in X_{R}
, then
\Per(x)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x\in\bar{R}^{n}(x)=R^{q+n}(x)\}
is nonempty and there
exists
p^{\prime}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Per(x)=p^{\prime}(x)\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
p^{\prime}(x)|p
. Furthermore,
p
is the least common
multiple of the set
\{p^{\prime}(x):x\in X_{R}\}
.

In addition, if
x\leftrightarrow_{R}y
then
p^{\prime}(x)=p^{\prime}(y)
.

Proof.

If
x\in\bar{R}^{n}(x)
, then
\bar{R}^{m}(x)\subset\bar{R}^{n+m}(x)
. So if
x\in\bar{R}^{m}(x)
as well, then

x\in\bar{R}^{n+m}(x)
. That is,
\Per(x)
is a sub-semigroup of
\mathbb{N}
. Let
p^{\prime}(x)
be the gcd. Since
x\in\bar{R}^{p}(x)
,
it follows that
p^{\prime}(x)|p
. As before, large enough multiples of
p^{\prime}=p^{\prime}(x)
are contained in
\Per(x)
. Hence, for large enough
N
,

Np+p^{\prime}\in\Per(x)
. Hence,
x\in\bar{R}^{Np+p^{\prime}}(x)=\bar{R}^{p^{\prime}}(x)
. Thus,
p^{\prime}\in\Per(x)
and so
\Per(x)=p^{\prime}\mathbb{N}
.
If
\ell
is the lcm of the
p^{\prime}(x)
’s, then
\ell
divides
p
since each
p^{\prime}(x)
does. On the other hand, if

y\in R^{q}(x)
, then
y\in R^{q+np^{\prime}(x)}(x)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and so
y\in R^{q+\ell}(x)
. Conversely, if

y\in R^{q+\ell}(x)
, then
y\in R^{q+\ell+np^{\prime}(x)}(x)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We can choose
n
so that
\ell+np^{\prime}(x)=q
.
Thus,
y\in R^{2q}(x)=R^{q}(x)
. We have shown that
R^{q+\ell}=R^{q}
. From Proposition
2.6
(e) it follows that

p\leq\ell
.

If
y\leftrightarrow_{\bar{R}}x
, then for some
i
,
y\in\bar{R}^{i}(x)
. Since
x\in\bar{R}^{np^{\prime}(x)}(x)
,
y\in R^{i+np^{\prime}(x)}(x)
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand, for some
j
,
x\in\bar{R}^{j}(y)
and so
y\in R^{i+j+np^{\prime}(x)}(y)
for all
n
. Therefore,

p^{\prime}(y)|(i+j+np^{\prime}(x))
for all
n
and so
p^{\prime}(y)|p^{\prime}(x)
. Similarly,
p^{\prime}(x)|p^{\prime}(y)
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

If
y\in R^{q+i+j}(x)=\bar{R}^{i+j}(x)
for some
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists
z\in X_{R}

such that
z\in R^{q+i}(x)=\bar{R}^{i}(x)
and
y\in R^{q+j}(z)=\bar{R}^{j}(z)
.

Proof.

By reducing mod
q
we may assume
i,j\leq q
. Since
R^{Nq+i+j}=R^{q+i+j}
,
we can choose
N
so that
(N-2)q
is greater than the cardinality of
X
and obtain a
path
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{Nq+i+j}=y
. It follows that the portion of the path from
x_{q}
to
x_{(N-1)q+i+j}
contains a loop and
so contains a recurrent point. Insert a cycle of length
q
beginning and ending at this point. Of course all of the points of
this cycle are recurrent. Renumbering, we have
a path
x=x_{0},\dots,x_{(N+1)q+i+j}=y
. We can choose a point
z=x_{t}
on the cycle so that
t=M_{1}q+i
for some
M_{1}\geq 1
and so
that
((N+1)q+i+j)-t=M_{2}q+j
for some
M_{2}\geq 1
. Thus,
z\in R^{M_{1}q+i}(x)=R^{q+i}(x)
and
y\in R^{M_{2}q+j}(z)=R^{q+j}(z)
.
∎

Let
\bar{R}^{n}|X_{R}=(\bar{R}^{n})\cap(X_{R}\times X_{R})
. Using the Lemma and induction on
n
we obtain

(3)

\bar{R}^{n}|X_{R}=(\bar{R}|X_{R})^{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Furthermore, if we define
T=R^{q}\cap(X\times X_{R})
and

3.
The main result

Intuitively, if a relation
R
on
X
is in canonical form, with
p
as its period and
R_{\geq}
as
the partial order on the strongly connected components, we might like to think of
R
as a product

R_{\geq}\times\tau_{p}
on
\overline{X}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, where
\tau_{p}
is the shift on
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, that is,

\tau_{p}(t)=t+1\mod p
. However, it is more complicated than that, for two reasons. First,
for a given strongly connected component, the period of the cycle may be a proper divisor of
p
,
and different components may have different periods. Second, several points of one component
may be related to a single point of another component, or vice versa. We can address both of these issues as follows.

In the simplest case, when these issues do not arise,
(X,R)
is isomorphic to

R_{\geq}\times\tau_{p}
on
\overline{X}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
: we can identify a point
x\in X
with a point

(a,t)\in\overline{X}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and the image
R(x)
with the set
\{(b,t+1):(a,b)\in R_{\geq}\}
.
Otherwise, for each point
(a,t)\in\overline{X}\times\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, we need to keep track of which other
points are in the image, that is, for each
b\in R_{\geq}(a)
, the set of integers
k\mod p
such that
(b,t+1+k)
is in the image of
(a,t)
.

More formally, let
\mathcal{L}_{p}
be the collection of nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.
For
F_{1}
,
F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, define
F_{1}+F_{2}:=\{t_{1}+t_{2}:t_{1}\in F_{1},t_{2}\in F_{2}\}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a commutative monoid with identity
\{0\}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A map
\xi:R_{\geq}\to\mathcal{L}_{p}
is a
cocycle
for the partial order
R_{\geq}
if it satisfies the condition:

(5)

(a,b),(b,c)\in R_{\geq}\Rightarrow\xi(a,b)+\xi(b,c)\subseteq\xi(a,c).

(Note that
(a,b),(b,c)\in R_{\geq}
implies that
(a,c)\in R_{\geq}
.) If
\xi
,
\xi^{\prime}
are cocycles,
then
\xi+\xi^{\prime}
is a cocycle, and so the cocycles form a monoid with identity
\xi_{0}
defined by
\xi_{0}(a,b)=\{0\}
for all
(a,b)\in R_{\geq}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\xi
be a cocycle for
R_{\geq}
.
If
(a,b)\in R_{\geq}
, then
\xi(a,a)
and
\xi(b,b)
are subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and

(6)

\xi(a,a)+\xi(a,b)=\xi(a,b)=\xi(a,b)+\xi(b,b).

Proof.

For
F\in\mathcal{L}_{p}
, we claim that
F+F\subseteq F
if and only if
F
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.
Clearly, if
F
is a subgroup, then
0\in F
and
F+F=F
. Conversely, if
F+F\subseteq F
, then
F
is
closed under addition, and so if
a
is in
F
, then so are
2a,3a,\ldots
.
In particular,
0=pa\in F
and
-a=(p-1)a\in F
. So
F
is a subgroup.

By the definition of cocycle, we have
\xi(a,a)+\xi(a,b)\subset\xi(a,b)
. Since
0\in\xi(a,a)
, equality holds.
The proof that
\xi(a,b)=\xi(a,b)+\xi(b,b)
is similar.
∎

For a cocycle
\xi:R_{\geq}\to\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
a\in\overline{X}
, let
q_{a}
and
p_{a}
be the order and index, respectively, of the
subgroup
\xi(a,a)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\zeta=\bigcap\{\xi(a,a):a\in\overline{X}\}
. Let
q_{*}
and
p_{*}

be the order and index, respectively, of the subgroup
\zeta\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 3.3
.

A cocycle
\xi:R_{\geq}\to\mathcal{L}_{p}
is called
irreducible
when it satisfies the following equivalent conditions:

•

The subgroup
\zeta
is trivial, i.e.
\bigcap\{\xi(a,a):a\in\overline{X}\}=\{0\}
.

•

The least common multiple of the set
\{p_{a}:a\in\overline{X}\}
equals
p
.

•

The greatest common divisor of the set
\{q_{a}:a\in\overline{X}\}
equals
1
.

Otherwise,
\xi
is called
reducible
.

In general we have:

\displaystyle p_{*}:=\text{the index of}

\displaystyle\ \ \zeta=lcm\{p_{a}:a\in\overline{X}\}.

\displaystyle q_{*}:=\text{the order of}

\displaystyle\ \ \zeta=gcd\{q_{a}:a\in\overline{X}\}.

Proof.

Using the quotient map
\mu_{p}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
,
\mu_{p}^{-1}(\xi(a,a))=p_{a}\mathbb{Z}
for some
p_{a}\in\mathbb{N}
. The
quotient map
\mu_{p_{a}}
factors to define a surjection from
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/p_{a}\mathbb{Z}
with kernel
\xi(a,a)
. Hence,

p_{a}
is the index of
\xi(a,a)
in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore,
\mu_{p}^{-1}(\zeta)=p_{*}\mathbb{Z}
where
p_{*}
is the lcm of the
p_{a}
’s.
Hence,
p_{*}
is the index of
\zeta
.

Now we use the number theory fact that if
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
all divide
p
and
p_{*}
is the lcm, then

p/p_{*}\ =\ gcd(p/p_{1},\dots,p/p_{n}).

This is trivial for
n=1
and is an easy exercise for
n=2
. The general result then follows by induction.

It then follows that the order of
\zeta
, which equals
\ p/p_{*}
, is the gcd of the orders
p/p_{a}
.

The equivalences in the above definition are now clear.
∎

Observe that if

\overline{R}_{\xi}^{i}\circ\overline{R}_{\xi}^{j}=\overline{R}_{\xi}^{i+j}

, there exists an isomorphism

are cohomologous.

Proof.

First, assume that they are cohomologous:
\xi^{\prime}(a,b)=\xi(a,b)+\{\theta(b)-\theta(a)\}

is an isomorphism

Next, assume that the isomorphism

Theorem 3.15

Proof.

For a general relation
