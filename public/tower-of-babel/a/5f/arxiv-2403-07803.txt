Abstract
. We prove the convergence of a modified Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme to a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation in Ω ⋐ ℝ d \Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d} with general, positive and temporally constant, Dirichlet boundary conditions. We work under mild assumptions on the domain, the drift, and the initial datum. In the special case where Ω \Omega is an interval in ℝ 1 \mathbb{R}^{1} , we prove that such a solution is a gradient flow—curve of maximal slope—within a suitable space of measures, endowed with a modified Wasserstein distance. Our discrete scheme and modified distance draw inspiration from contributions by A. Figalli and N. Gigli [J. Math. Pures Appl. 94, (2010), pp. 107–130], and J. Morales [J. Math. Pures Appl. 112, (2018), pp. 41–88] on an optimal-transport approach to evolution equations with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Similarly to these works, we allow the mass to flow from/to the boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega throughout the evolution. However, our leading idea is to also keep track of the mass at the boundary by working with measures defined on the whole closure Ω ¯ {\overline{\Omega}} . The driving functional is a modification of the classical relative entropy that also makes use of the information at the boundary. As an intermediate result, when Ω \Omega is an interval in ℝ 1 \mathbb{R}^{1} , we find a formula for the descending slope of this geodesically nonconvex functional.

1.
Introduction

The subject of this paper is the linear Fokker–Planck equation

(1.1)

\frac{\dif}{\dif t}\rho_{t}=\dive\left(\nabla\rho_{t}+\rho_{t}\nabla V\right)

on a bounded Euclidean domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
combined with general, positive and constant in time, Dirichlet boundary conditions. We want to approach this problem by applying the theory of
optimal transport
, which, since the seminal works of R. Jordan, D. Kinderlehrer, and F. Otto
[
14
,
19
,
20
]
, has proven effective in the study of a number of evolution equations.

Existence, uniqueness, and appropriate estimates are often consequence of a peculiar structure of the problem. Important instances are those PDEs which can be seen as
gradient flows
. In fact, it has been proven that several equations, including Fokker–Planck on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, are gradient flows in a space of probability measures endowed with the
2
-Wasserstein distance

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma}\sqrt{\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\gamma(x,y)}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

where the infimum is taken among all couplings
\gamma
between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e., measures with marginals
\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma=\mu
and
\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma=\nu
.
For such PDEs, existence can be deduced from the convergence of the discrete-time approximations given by the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto variational scheme (also known, in a more general metric setting, as De Giorgi’s minimizing movement scheme
[
10
]
)

(1.2)

\rho_{(n+1)\tau}^{\tau}\dif x\in{\arg\min}_{\mu}\left(\mathrm{Ent}(\mu)+\frac{W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\rho_{n\tau}^{\tau}\dif x)}{2\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

where
\mathrm{Ent}
is an entropy functional that depends on the equation, and
\tau>0
is the time step.

When applied on a bounded Euclidean domain, this approach produces solutions
with Neumann boundary conditions
. This fact is inseparable from the choice of the metric space (probability measures with the distance
W_{2}
) in which the flow evolves. Intuitively, Neumann boundary conditions are natural because a curve of probability measures, by definition, conserves the total mass; see also the discussion in
[
22
]
.

In order to deal with Dirichlet boundary conditions, A. Figalli and N. Gigli defined in
[
13
]
a modified Wasserstein distance
Wb_{2}
that gives a special role to the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Despite being a distance between nonnegative measures on
\Omega
, the metric
Wb_{2}
is defined as an infimum over measures
\gamma
on the product of the topological closures
{\overline{\Omega}}\times{\overline{\Omega}}
, and only the restrictions of the marginals
\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma
and
\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma
to
\Omega
are prescribed (see the original paper
[
13
]
or
Section

3.6
below). In this sense, the boundary
\partial\Omega
can be interpreted as an infinite reservoir, where mass can be taken and deposited freely. The main result in
[
13
]
is the convergence of the scheme

\rho^{\tau}_{(n+1)\tau}\in{\arg\min}_{\rho}\left(\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\rho\log\rho-\rho+1\bigr)\dif x+\frac{Wb_{2}^{2}(\rho\dif x,\rho^{\tau}_{n\tau}\dif x)}{2\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

as
\tau\downarrow 0
, to a solution to the heat equation with the
constant
Dirichlet boundary condition
\rho|_{\partial\Omega}=1
. More generally, it was observed in
[
13
, Section 4]
that the same scheme with a suitably modified entropy converges to solutions to the linear Fokker–Planck equation (
1.1
) with the boundary condition
\rho|_{\partial\Omega}=e^{-V}
. In particular, this theory covers the heat equation with
any constant and strictly positive
Dirichlet boundary condition.

In a more recent contribution, J. Morales
[
18
]
proved convergence of a similar discrete scheme for a family of reaction-diffusion equations with drift, subject to rather
general
Dirichlet boundary conditions. In this scheme, the distance between measures is replaced by
\tau
-dependent transportation costs. Morales’ work, together with
[
13
]
, is the starting point of the present paper.

We conclude this brief literature review by mentioning four further works on this subject.
The case of the heat flow with
vanishing
Dirichlet boundary conditions was studied by A. Profeta and K.-T. Sturm in
[
21
]
. They defined ‘charged probabilities’ and a suitable distance on them. This metric is built upon the idea that mass can touch the boundary and be reflected, as with the classical Wasserstein distance, but possibly changing the charge (positive to negative or vice versa). One of their results is the
Evolution Variational Inequality
(see
[
3
]
) for such a heat flow.

D. Kim, D. Koo, and G. Seo
[
15
]
adapted the setting of
[
13
]
to porous medium equations with nonnegative
constant
boundary conditions. J.-B. Casteras, L. Monsaingeon, and F. Santambrogio
[
8
]
found the Wasserstein gradient-flow structure for the equation arising from the so-called Sticky Brownian Motion, i.e., the Fokker–Planck equation together with boundary conditions of Dirichlet type that also evolve in time subject to diffusion and drift on the boundary. The authors of
[
15
]
and
[
8
]
further established Energy Dissipation Inequalities for the respective problems. However, they also pointed out that characterizations as
curves of maximal slope
(cf.
[
3
, Definition 1.3.2]
and
Definition

3.5
) are still missing. From
[
15
]
: «
Question: Does
\rho(t)
satisfy the concept of curve of maximal slope? […] As far as we know, it remains open whether the answer to the question is ‘yes’.
» From
[
8
]
: «
It is worth stressing that something is still missing in order to obtain a rigorous metric gradient flow.
» Later in the introduction, we will comment further on this problem.

The author has also been informed of a work by M. Erbar and G. Meglioli
[
12
]
, in preparation at the same time as this one. The two papers are independent and focus on different questions. The first part of
[

concerns the description of absolutely continuous curves for the distance

Our contribution

In this work, we present two novel results:

(1)

We prove convergence of a modified Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme to a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation with general Dirichlet boundary conditions under mild regularity assumptions. To do this, we adopt a
different point of view
compared to
[
13
,
18
,
15
]
: our scheme is defined on a subset
\mathscr{S}
of the signed measures
on the closure
{\overline{\Omega}}
, rather than on measures on
\Omega
.

(2)

In dimension

, we determine that this solution is also a
curve of maximal slope
for an entropy
\mathcal{H}
in an appropriate metric space
(\mathscr{S},{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}})
.

Let us now explain in detail the extent of these contributions and provide precise statements.

Convergence of a modified JKO scheme

We look at the boundary-value problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif t}\rho_{t}=\dive\left(\nabla\rho_{t}+\rho_{t}\nabla V\right)&\text{in }\Omega\,\mathrm{,}\\
\rho_{t}|_{\partial\Omega}=e^{\Psi-V}&\text{on }\partial\Omega\,\mathrm{,}\\
\rho_{t=0}=\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{.}\end{cases}

Here,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
bounded
open set and
\rho_{0},\Psi,V
are given functions, with
\rho_{0}\geq 0
. The function
\Psi
can be tuned to obtain the desired boundary condition.

We introduce the set
\mathscr{S}
of all signed measures on
{\overline{\Omega}}
with

(1.4)

\mu|_{\Omega}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mu({\overline{\Omega}})=0\,\mathrm{.}

We also define

(1.5)

\mathcal{E}(\rho)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\rho\log\rho+(V-1)\rho+1\bigr)\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad\rho\in L^{1}_{+}(\Omega)\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and, for
\mu\in\mathscr{S}
,

(1.6)

\mathcal{H}(\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)+\int\Psi\dif\mu|_{\partial\Omega}&\text{if }\mu|_{\Omega}=\rho\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In
Section

3.7
, we will define a transportation-cost functional
\mathcal{T}
on
\mathscr{S}
. With it, we can consider the scheme

(1.7)

\mu_{(n+1)\tau}^{\tau}\in\argmin_{\mu\in\mathscr{S}}\,\left(\mathcal{H}(\mu)+\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu,\mu_{n\tau}^{\tau})}{2\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\tau>0\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

starting from some
\mu_{0}^{\tau}=\mu_{0}\in\mathscr{S}
, independent of
\tau
, such that the restriction
\mu_{0}|_{\Omega}
is absolutely continuous with density
\rho_{0}
. These sequences are extended to maps
t\mapsto\mu^{\tau}_{t}
, constant on the intervals
\bigl[n\tau,(n+1)\tau\bigr)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

.

2.
Formal derivation

Let us work on a completely formal level and postulate that a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation (
1.3
) is the “Wasserstein-like” gradient flow of some unknown entropy functional
\mathrm{Ent}
. By this we mean the following:

(1)

the motion of
\rho_{t}
in
\Omega
is governed by the continuity equation

(2.1)

\frac{\dif}{\dif t}\rho_{t}=-\dive(\rho_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t})\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

for some velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
,

(2)

the time-derivative of
\rho_{t}
equals the inverse of the Wasserstein gradient of
\mathrm{Ent}
at
\rho_{t}
for every
t
, in the sense that for every sufficiently nice curve
s\mapsto f_{s}
of functions on
\Omega
starting at
f_{0}=\rho_{t}
we have

(2.2)

\frac{\dif}{\dif s}\mathrm{Ent}(f_{s}\dif x)\Big|_{s=0}=-\int_{\Omega}\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{t},\nabla\psi\rangle\rho_{t}\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\quad\text{where }\frac{\dif}{\dif s}f_{s}\Big|_{s=0}=-\dive(\rho_{t}\nabla\psi)\,\mathrm{.}

We are searching for the right functional
\mathrm{Ent}
and, since we want to retrieve the Fokker–Planck equation, we should try with

(2.3)

\mathrm{Ent}_{0}(\rho\dif x)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\rho\log\rho+(V-1)\rho+1\bigr)\dif x\,\mathrm{.}

Then, for a fixed
t\geq 0
and a curve
s\mapsto f_{s}
, we have

\frac{\dif}{\dif s}\mathrm{Ent}_{0}(f_{s}\dif x)=\int_{\Omega}(V+\log f_{s})\frac{\dif}{\dif s}f_{s}\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif s}\mathrm{Ent}_{0}(f_{s}\dif x)\Big|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(V+\log\rho_{t})\dive(\rho_{t}\nabla\psi)\dif x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle(\nabla V+\nabla\log\rho_{t}),\nabla\psi\rangle\rho_{t}\dif x-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Psi\rho_{t}\langle\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\dif\mathscr{H}^{d-1}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

where, in the last identity, we used the boundary conditions in (
1.3
). Let us choose

\boldsymbol{v}_{t}\coloneqq-\nabla V-\nabla\log\rho_{t}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

which makes the continuity equation (
2.1
) true, since
\rho_{t}
solves (
1.3
). Then,

\frac{\dif}{\dif s}\mathrm{Ent}_{0}(f_{s})\Big|_{s=0}=-\int_{\Omega}\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{t},\nabla\psi\rangle\rho_{t}\dif x-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Psi\rho_{t}\langle\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\dif\mathscr{H}^{d-1}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and we see that
\mathrm{Ent}_{0}
is not the right functional, just because of the integral on the boundary. The measure
\langle\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\rho_{t}\mathscr{H}^{d-1}
on
\partial\Omega
can be seen as the flux of mass (coming from
f_{0}=\rho_{t}
) that is moving away from
\Omega
along the flow
s\mapsto f_{s}
at
s=0
. Thus, if we let this mass settle on the boundary,
\langle\nabla\psi,\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\rho_{t}\mathscr{H}^{d-1}
is the time-derivative of the mass on
\partial\Omega
. For this reason, it makes sense to consider not just measures on
\Omega
, but rather on the closure
{\overline{\Omega}}
, and to define the entropy as

\mathrm{Ent}(\mu)\coloneqq\mathrm{Ent}_{0}(\mu|_{\Omega})+\int\Psi\dif\mu|_{\partial\Omega}\,\mathrm{.}

Our entropy functional
\mathcal{H}
is defined precisely like this, and, as we will see in
Section

3
, the transportation functionals
\mathcal{T}
and
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
are extensions of
Wb_{2}
to the subset
\mathscr{S}
of the signed measures on
{\overline{\Omega}}
, constructed so as to encode the idea that mass leaks from
\Omega
settle on
\partial\Omega
(and vice versa).

This argument is simple, but we should also emphasize the hidden difficulties:

•

we assume low regularity on
\partial\Omega
and on the functions
\rho_{0}
and
V
;

•

the transportation-cost functionals
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
and
\mathcal{T}
will not be, in general, distances;

•

the functional
\mathcal{H}
is not bounded from below on

In fact,

Lemma 4.1

Let

be finite nonnegative Borel measures on

with

(4.2)

\displaystyle Wb_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tilde{\nu}\in\mathscr{S}}\mathinner{\left\{{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\tilde{\nu}_{\Omega}=\nu\right\}}=\inf_{\tilde{\nu}\in\mathscr{S}}\mathinner{\left\{\mathcal{T}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\tilde{\nu}_{\Omega}=\nu\right\}}\,\mathrm{.}

Proof.

In light of (

\inf_{\tilde{\nu}\in\mathscr{S}}\mathinner{\left\{\mathcal{T}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\tilde{\nu}_{\Omega}=\nu\right\}}\leq Wb_{2}(\mu,\nu)\,\mathrm{.}

It is easy to check that

Proposition 4.4

Lemma 4.5

In particular, for any

Proof of

Let us denote by

the density of

and choose a
\mathcal{T}
-optimal transport plan

for every

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V_{k}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}
for every

that the integral

(1)

\mu^{k}_{\partial\Omega}\to_{k}\eta
for some

(2)

\rho^{k}\rightharpoonup_{k}\rho
weakly in

,

(3)

\mu^{k}\to_{k}\mu=\rho\dif x+\eta
weakly in duality with
C({\overline{\Omega}})
,

(4)

\gamma^{k}\to_{k}\gamma
weakly in duality with

for some
\gamma
, and

As already observed,

is lower semicontinuous w.r.t. weak

\mathcal{H}(\mu)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\mu^{k})\,\mathrm{.}

We will see in Step 3 that

Step 1 (Sobolev bound for the approximants).
Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{\infty}
-regular vector field with compact support and, for
\epsilon>0
, let
R_{\epsilon}(x)\coloneqq x+\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}(x)
. We set
\mu^{k,\epsilon}\coloneqq(R_{\epsilon})_{\#}\mu^{k}
and notice that, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small,
R_{\epsilon}
is a diffeomorphism from
\Omega
to itself, and
\mu^{k,\epsilon}\in\mathscr{S}
. The minimality of
\mu^{k}
implies

\int\bigl(\log\rho^{k}-\log(\rho^{k,\epsilon}\circ R_{\epsilon})+V_{k}-V_{k}\circ R_{\epsilon}\bigr)\dif\mu_{\Omega}^{k}\leq\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k,\epsilon},\bar{\mu})-\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{2\tau}\,\mathrm{.}

It can be easily checked that the density
\rho^{k,\epsilon}
of
\mu^{k,\epsilon}_{\Omega}
satisfies

\rho^{k,\epsilon}\circ R_{\epsilon}=\frac{\rho^{k}}{\det\nabla R_{\epsilon}}\quad\mathscr{L}^{d}\text{-a.e. on }\Omega\,\mathrm{;}\;\,

hence we have

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\log\det\nabla R_{\epsilon}+V_{k}-V_{k}\circ R_{\epsilon}}{\epsilon}\rho^{k}\dif x\leq\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k,\epsilon},\bar{\mu})-\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{2\epsilon\tau}\,\mathrm{.}

Passing to the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the dominated convergence theorem (
V_{k}
is smooth),
Lemma

4.19
, and Hölder’s inequality give

(5.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\dive\boldsymbol{w}-\langle\nabla V_{k},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\bigr)\rho^{k}\dif x

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\tau}\int\langle\boldsymbol{w}(x),y-x\rangle\dif\gamma^{k}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\rho^{k})}\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{\tau}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

By the Riesz representation theorem, this means that there exists a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{2}(\rho^{k};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(5.18)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\rho^{k})}\leq\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{\tau}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and

\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\dive\boldsymbol{w}-\langle\nabla V_{k},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\bigr)\rho^{k}\dif x=\int_{\Omega}\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\rho^{k}\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

for all smooth and compactly supported vector fields
\boldsymbol{w}
.
In other words,
-\rho^{k}(\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla V_{k})
is the distributional gradient of
\rho^{k}
. Since
\rho^{k}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, the function
V_{k}
is Lipschitz continuous, and by (
5.18
), we now know that
\rho^{k}\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
. Hence, for every smooth
\boldsymbol{w}
that is compactly supported,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}\dive\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\dif x=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}+\epsilon}\dive\boldsymbol{w}\dif x=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}{2\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}+\epsilon}}\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\dif x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\rho^{k})}}{2}\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sqrt{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho^{k}e^{2V_{k}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rvert}^{2}}{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}+\epsilon}\dif x}=\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\rho^{k})}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(e^{V_{k}})}}{2}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

where, for the second equality, we used a standard property of composition of Sobolev functions (cf.
[
7
, Proposition 9.5]
) and, in the last one, the monotone convergence theorem. With a similar argument as before, we infer that
\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
with

(5.19)

\int_{\Omega}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\nabla\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}\right\rvert}^{2}e^{-V_{k}}\dif x\leq\left(\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\rho^{k})}}{2}\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:boundu}}}{{\leq}}\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{4\tau^{2}}\,\mathrm{.}

The number at the right-hand side of the latter is bounded as

. Indeed, by minimality of

,

\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})-\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})}{2\tau}\leq\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mu)-\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mu^{k})=\mathcal{H}(\mu)-\mathcal{H}(\mu^{k})+\int_{\Omega}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert V-V_{k}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\rho-\rho^{k}\right\rvert}\dif x\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

from which, owing to (
5.16

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V_{k}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}
, we find

(5.20)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})+\limsup_{k\to\infty}2\tau\int_{\Omega}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert V-V_{k}\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\rho-\rho_{k}\right\rvert}\dif x

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})+8\tau\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{k}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho^{k}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\,\mathrm{.}

We infer that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sobBound}}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert V\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\right)\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}^{2}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{4\tau^{2}}<\infty\,\mathrm{.}

In particular, we can extract a further (not relabeled) subsequence such that

(5.21)

\sqrt{\rho^{k}e^{V_{k}}}\rightharpoonup_{k}f\quad\text{weakly in }W^{1,2}(\Omega)\text{ with }\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}\leq\mathfrak{c}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu^{k},\bar{\mu})}{\tau}

for some function

. In the next Step, we show that

.

Step 2 (improved convergence).
Although

is not regular enough to apply directly the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem

Further, we note the following facts:

•

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mu+\epsilon S_{\#}\mu_{A}-\epsilon\mu_{A}\in\mathscr{S}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 6.2
.

In the current setting, i.e.,
\Omega=(0,1)
and
V\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, the function

is Hölder continuous; thus it extends to the boundary
\partial\Omega=\mathinner{\left\{0,1\right\}}
. When

, the function

belongs to
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, is continuous, and extends to the boundary as well.

Remark 6.3
.

The functional

W^{1,2}(\Omega)\ni f\longmapsto\begin{cases}\displaystyle 4\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{x}f\right)^{2}e^{-V}\dif x&\text{if }f-e^{\Psi/2}\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\,\mathrm{,}\\
\infty&\text{if }f-e^{\Psi/2}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\setminus W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\,\mathrm{.}\end{cases}

is particularly well-behaved: it is convex, strongly continuous, weakly lower semicontinuous, and has weakly compact sublevels.

While the formula (
6.1
) reminds the classical slope of the relative entropy (i.e., the relative Fisher information), the crucial difference is in the role of the boundary condition: if
\rho
does not satisfy the correct one, the slope is infinite.

We are going to prove the two opposite inequalities in (
6.1
) separately. To prove
\geq
is easier: for the case where
\sqrt{\rho e^{V}}-e^{\Psi/2}\in W^{1,2}_{0}
, it amounts to taking small variations of
\mu
in an arbitrary direction (as in
Proposition

5.9
, Step 1); for the other case, it suffices to find appropriate sequences that make the difference quotient tend to infinity. To handle the other inequality, we have to bound
\bigl(\mathcal{H}(\mu)-\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mu})\bigr)_{+}
from above for every sufficiently close measure
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathscr{S}
. Classical proofs (e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 15.25]
or
[
3
, Theorem 10.4.6]
) take advantage of geodesic convexity of the functional, which we do not to have, see
Section

A.3
. One of the perks of geodesic convexity is that it automatically ensures lower semicontinuity of the descending slope, which in turn allows to make further regularity assumptions on
\mu
and then argue by approximation. To overcome this problem, we combine different ideas on different parts of
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
. Away from the boundary
\partial\Omega=\mathinner{\left\{0,1\right\}}
, the transport plans move absolutely continuous measures to absolutely continuous measures. The Jacobian equation (change of variables formula) relates the two densities and makes the computations rather easy. The contribution of the part of
\mu
closest to the boundary (and the corresponding portion of

) is, instead,
negligible
. The proof of this fact is more technical: we exploit the boundary condition and the Sobolev regularity of the functions
\rho
,
\log\rho
, and

to obtain appropriate estimates. Note, indeed, that since the boundary condition is positive, also
\log\rho
has a square-integrable derivative in a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
.

To be in dimension

is necessary for

to be a distance, but is also extremely useful because optimal transport maps are monotone. For this reason, it seems difficult (but maybe still possible) to adapt our proof of

for an analogue of

We first provide a variant of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem that is needed for the subsequent proof of

6.1

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(\gamma^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of nonnegative Borel measures on
\Omega\times{\overline{\Omega}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}(\gamma^{n})=0
.
Further assume that
\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma^{n}
is absolutely continuous for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with a density that is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, for every
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

(6.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}\bigr(f(z)-f(x)\bigr)\dif z\right)^{2}\dif\,\gamma^{n}(x,y)=0\,\mathrm{.}

Proof.

Denote by
\rho^{n}
the density of
\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma^{n}
. Let
g\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz continuous. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}\bigr(f(z)-f(x)\bigr)\dif z\right)^{2}\dif\gamma^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 3\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}(f-g)\dif z\right)^{2}\dif\gamma^{n}+3\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}g\dif z-g(x)\right)^{2}\dif\gamma^{n}

\displaystyle\quad+3\int_{\Omega}(g-f)^{2}\rho^{n}\dif x\,\mathrm{.}

Consider the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function of (the extension to
\mathbb{R}
of)
f-g
, that is,

(f-g)^{*}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{\max\mathinner{\left\{x-r,0\right\}}}^{\min\mathinner{\left\{x+r,1\right\}}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f-g\right\rvert}\,\dif z\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\,\mathrm{.}

By the (strong) Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality,

\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}(f-g)\dif z\right)^{2}\dif\,\gamma^{n}\leq 4\int\bigl((f-g)^{*}(x)\bigr)^{2}\dif\,\gamma^{n}=4\int_{\Omega}\bigl((f-g)^{*}\bigr)^{2}\rho^{n}\dif x\\
\leq 4\sup_{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho^{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(f-g)^{*}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}\sup_{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho^{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{.}

The Lipschitz-continuity of
g
gives

\int\left(\fint_{x}^{y}g\dif z-g(x)\right)^{2}\dif\,\gamma^{n}\leq(\Lip g)^{2}\int(x-y)^{2}\dif\,\gamma^{n}\leq(\Lip g)^{2}\mathcal{C}(\gamma^{n})\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and, moreover, we have

\int_{\Omega}(g-f)^{2}\rho^{n}\dif x\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho^{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert}_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{.}

In conclusion,

I_{n}\leq\mathfrak{c}\sup_{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho^{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{\infty}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}}+3(\Lip g)^{2}\mathcal{C}(\gamma^{n})\,\mathrm{.}

After passing to the limit superior in
n
, we conclude by arbitrariness of
g
.
∎

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\mu^{\tau}_{a},\mu^{\tau}_{b})\leq\int_{\lfloor\frac{a}{\tau}\rfloor\tau}^{\lfloor\frac{b}{\tau}\rfloor\tau+\tau}D_{\tau}(r)\dif r\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\qquad 0\leq a<b\,\mathrm{.}

.
Now consider the subsequence

) yield

(7.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{s})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:convH}}}{{=}}\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{s}^{\tau_{k}})\\

Step 2.
By (

\mathscr{L}^{1}_{(0,\infty)}

\liminf_{\tau\to 0}\bigl\lvert\Dif^{-}_{\,}\mathcal{H}\bigr\rvert^{2}(\tilde{\mu}_{r}^{\tau})\leq\liminf_{\tau\to\infty}G_{\tau}^{2}(r)<\infty\,\mathrm{.}

Let us fix one such

(possibly depending on

(7.14)

\mathcal{H}(\mu_{s})\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\dot{\mu}_{r}\right\rvert}^{2}\dif r+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\bigl\lvert\Dif^{-}_{\,}\mathcal{H}\bigr\rvert^{2}(\mu_{r})\dif r+\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{,}\;\,\\

At this point, we can define

and check that it fulfills the properties required by

•

Monotonicity follows directly from the definition.

•

•

Appendix A
Additional properties of
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}

A.1.
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
is not a distance when
d\geq 2

We are going to prove that, when
d\geq 2
, the property

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\mu,\nu)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu=\nu

in general breaks down. In fact, when applying
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
to two measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{S}
the information about
\mu_{\partial\Omega}
and
\nu_{\partial\Omega}
is completely lost, as soon as
\partial\Omega
is connected and “not too irregular”. A similar result is
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
by E. Mainini.

Proposition A.1
.

If
\alpha\colon[0,1]\to\partial\Omega
be
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)
-Hölder continuous for some
\epsilon>0
, then

(A.1)

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}\bigl(\delta_{\alpha(0)}-\delta_{\alpha(1)},0\bigr)=0\,\mathrm{.}

Consequently: Assume that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+}
-path-connected, meaning that for every pair of points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
there exist
\epsilon>0
and a
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)
-Hölder curve
\alpha\colon[0,1]\to\partial\Omega
with
\alpha(0)=x
and
\alpha(1)=y
; then

(A.2)

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\mu,\nu)=Wb_{2}(\mu_{\Omega},\nu_{\Omega})

for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Step 1.
Let
\alpha\colon[0,1]\to\partial\Omega
be
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)
-Hölder continuous for some
\epsilon>0
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
, consider the points

x_{i}\coloneqq\alpha(i/n),\qquad i\in\mathinner{\left\{0,1,\dotsc,n\right\}}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

and the measure

\gamma^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\,\mathrm{.}

It is easy to check that
\gamma^{n}\in\Adm_{{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}}\bigl(\delta_{\alpha(0)}-\delta_{\alpha(1)},0\bigr)
; moreover,

\mathcal{C}(\gamma^{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x_{i}-x_{i+1}\right\rvert}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{\alpha}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}n^{-1-2\epsilon}=\mathfrak{c}_{\alpha}n^{-2\epsilon}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

where the inequality follows from the Hölder-continuity of
\alpha
. We conclude (
A.1
) by letting
n\to\infty
.

Step 2.
Assume now that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+}
-path-connected. Fix a finite signed Borel measure
\eta
on
\partial\Omega
with
\eta(\partial\Omega)=0
, that is,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\eta_{+}\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\eta_{-}\right\rVert}\eqqcolon\lambda
. We shall prove that
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\eta,0)=0
.
Fix
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
and let
X=\mathinner{\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{N}\right\}}\subseteq\partial\Omega
be a
\epsilon_{1}
-covering for
\partial\Omega
, meaning that there exists a function
P\colon\partial\Omega\to X
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-P(x)\right\rvert}\leq\epsilon_{1}
for every
x\in\partial\Omega
. We pick one such
P
that is also Borel-measurable (we can by
[
1
, Theorem 18.19]
). From the previous Step, for every
i,j\in\mathinner{\left\{1,2,\dotsc,N\right\}}
, we get
\gamma_{i,j}
(nonnegative and concentrated on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
) such that

\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma_{i,j}-\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma_{i,j}=\delta_{x_{i}}-\delta_{x_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i,j})\leq\epsilon_{2}\,\mathrm{.}

We define

\gamma\coloneqq(\Id,P)_{\#}\eta_{+}+(P,\Id)_{\#}\eta_{-}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\eta_{+}\bigl(P^{-1}(x_{i})\bigr)\eta_{-}\bigl(P^{-1}(x_{j})\bigr)\gamma_{i,j}\,\mathrm{.}

The
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
-admissibility of
\gamma
, i.e.,
\gamma\in\Adm_{{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}}(\eta,0)
is straightforward. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Id-P\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\,(\eta_{+}+\eta_{-})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\eta_{+}(P^{-1}(x_{i}))\eta_{-}(P^{-1}(x_{j}))\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i,j})

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda(\epsilon_{1})^{2}+\lambda\epsilon_{2}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

which brings us to the conclusion that
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}(\eta,0)=0
by arbitrariness of
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
.

Step 3.
Let us assume again that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+}
-path-connected, and fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{S}
and
\epsilon_{3}>0
. Let
\gamma
be a
Wb_{2}
-optimal transport plan between
\mu_{\Omega}
and
\nu_{\Omega}
, and set
\tilde{\mu}\coloneqq\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma+(\nu-\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma)_{\partial\Omega}
. It is easy to check that
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathscr{S}
and that
\mu_{\Omega}=\tilde{\mu}_{\Omega}
. Therefore, the previous Step is applicable to
\eta\coloneqq\mu_{\partial\Omega}-\tilde{\mu}_{\partial\Omega}
, and produces
\gamma_{\eta}
on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
such that

\pi^{1}_{\#}\gamma_{\eta}-\pi^{2}_{\#}\gamma_{\eta}=\eta\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{\eta})\leq\epsilon_{3}\,\mathrm{.}

The measure
\gamma^{\prime}\coloneqq\gamma+\gamma_{\eta}
is
{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}
-admissible between
\mu
and
\nu
. Therefore,

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathcal{C}(\gamma^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{C}(\gamma)+\epsilon_{3}=Wb_{2}^{2}(\mu_{\Omega},\nu_{\Omega})+\epsilon_{3}\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

which yields one of the two inequalities in (
A.2
) by arbitrariness of
\epsilon_{3}
. The other inequality is (
4.1
).
∎

A.2.
(Lack of) completeness

We prove here two claims from

: in the setting where

complete, but the sublevels of

Proposition A.2
.

Assume that

and that
\Omega
is a finite union of intervals. Then the metric space
(\mathscr{S},{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}})

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(0,1)
is a connected component of
\Omega
, i.e.,
(0,1)\subseteq\Omega
and
\mathinner{\left\{0,1\right\}}\subseteq\partial\Omega

Consider the sequence

For every

Assume now that
\mu^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}}}{{\to}}_{n}\mu
for some
\mu\in\mathscr{S}
and, for every

{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}^{2}(\mu^{n},\mu)=\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\tilde{\gamma}^{n}(x,y)\geq\epsilon^{2}\tilde{\gamma}^{n}\bigl([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\times\partial\Omega\bigr)\,\mathrm{,}\;\,

,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{\Omega}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{\gamma}^{n}\bigl([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\times\Omega\bigr)=\mu^{n}\bigl([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\bigr)-\tilde{\gamma}^{n}\bigl([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\times\partial\Omega\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mu^{n}\bigl([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\bigr)-\frac{{\widetilde{Wb}_{2}}^{2}(\mu^{n},\mu)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\mathrm{.}

Passing to the limit

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{\Omega}\right\rVert}\geq\int_{\epsilon}^{1-\epsilon}\frac{1}{x}\dif x

∎

Proposition A.5
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)
. The functional
\mathcal{H}
is

Proof.

Consider the curve

Clearly,

for every

\mathcal{H}(\mu_{0})<\infty
