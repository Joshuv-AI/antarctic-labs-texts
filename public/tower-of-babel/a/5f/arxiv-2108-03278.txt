Asymptotic Dependence of In- and Out-Degrees in a Preferential Attachment Model with Reciprocity
Tiandong Wang, Sidney I. Resnick

Abstract
Reciprocity characterizes the information exchange between users in a network, and some empirical studies have revealed that social networks have a high proportion of reciprocal edges. Classical directed preferential attachment (PA) models, though generating scale-free networks, may give networks with low reciprocity. This points out one potential problem of fitting a classical PA model to a given network dataset with high reciprocity, and indicates alternative models need to be considered. We give one possible modification of the classical PA model by including another parameter which controls the probability of adding a reciprocated edge at each step. Asymptotic analyses suggest that large in- and out-degrees become fully dependent in this modified model, as a result of the additional reciprocated edges.

Introduction

The
reciprocity
coefficient, which is classically defined as the proportion of reciprocated edges (cf.
Newman et al. 2002
;
Wasserman and Faust 1994
), characterizes the mutual exchange of information between users in a social network.
For instance, on Twitter, users share links or pass on information to
a target user through sending direct @-messages. When interacting with peers on Twitter, the direct @-messages will be exchanged between users in both directions (cf.
Cheng et al. 2011
).
In addition, a study on eight different types of networks in
Jiang et al. 2015
shows online social networks (e.g.
Viswanath et al. 2009
;
Cha et al. 2009
;
Java et al. 2007
;
Magno et al. 2012
;
Mislove et al. 2007
) tend to have a higher proportion of reciprocal edges, compared to other types of networks such as biological networks, communication networks, software call graphs and P2P networks.

To model the evolution of social networks, one appealing approach is the directed preferential attachment (PA) model (cf.
Bollobás et al. 2003
;
Krapivsky and Redner 2001
), which captures the scale-free property, i.e. both in- and out-degree distributions have Pareto-like tails
(cf.
Samorodnitsky et al. 2016
;
Resnick and Samorodnitsky 2015
;
Wan et al. 2017
;
Wang and Resnick 2020
).
Asymptotic behaviors for the reciprocity coefficient in a directed PA model have recently been developed in
Wang and Resnick 2021
.
We see from the theoretical results in
Wang and Resnick 2021
that for certain choice of parameters, the classical PA model will generate networks with proportion of reciprocal edges close to 0, which deviates from the empirical findings for social networks described in
Jiang et al. 2015
.
This motivates us to think of variants of the classical PA model that are able to generate a high proportion of reciprocated edges.

In
Das and Resnick 2017
, the authors analyze the Facebook wall post dataset available at

http://konect.cc/networks/facebook-wosn-wall/
, and find that for the subset of nodes with large in- and out-degrees, the in- and out-degree pairs are modeled as
highly dependent.
Taking a closer look at the data, we see more than 60% of the edges in the Facebook wall post network are reciprocal. These facts lead to the conjecture that
nodes with large in- and out-degrees are likely to have the in- and out-degrees
highly dependent in a network with high reciprocity.
With this conjecture in mind, we extend the directed PA model by introducing another parameter,
\rho\in(0,1)
, which
controls the probability of adding a reciprocated edge to a newly created edge following the PA rule.
We then study the theoretical properties of this modified PA model
with emphasis on the asymptotic dependence structure of large in- and out-degrees.
Theoretical results are obtained through extending the embedding techniques in
Wang and Resnick 2019
;
Wang and Resnick 2020
to continuous-time multitype branching processes with immigration.
Empirical frequencies of nodes with in-degree
i
and out-degree
j

converge to a limiting regularly varying distribution
p_{ij}
, and unlike the
traditional PA model where the mass of the limit distribution is
spread out in the first quadrant, for the model with reciprocity
we find that large in- and out-degrees become asymptotically fully dependent.

In Section
1.1
, we give a detailed description of our modified model with reciprocity probability
\rho\in(0,1)
. We then present important preliminaries in Section
2
, including background knowledge on continuous-time multitype branching processes and multivariate regular variation.
Section
3
contains three theoretical properties for the modified PA model: (1) the growth of in- and out-degrees for a fixed node; (2) the asymptotic limit for the joint in- and out-degree distribution; and (3) the asymptotic dependence structure for large in- and out-degrees.
We highlight some future research directions in Section
4
,
and technical proofs are given in Section
5
.

1.1
Model Setup

Initialize the model with graph
G(0)
, which consists of one node (labeled as Node 1) and a self-loop.
Let
G(n)
denote the graph after
n
steps and

V(n)
be the set of nodes in
G(n)
with
V(0)=\{1\}
and
|V(0)|={1}
.
Denote the set of directed edges in
G(n)
by
E(n)
such that
an ordered pair
(w_{1},w_{2})\in E(n)
,
w_{1},w_{2}\in V(n)
,
represents a directed edge
w_{1}\mapsto w_{2}
.
When
n=0
, we have
E(0)=\{(1,1)\}
.

Set
\bigl(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n),D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)\bigr)
to
be the in- and out-degrees of node
w\in V(n)
.
We use the convention that
D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)=D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)=0
if
w\notin V(n)
.
From
G(n)
to
G(n+1)
, one of the following scenarios happens:

(i)

With probability
\alpha
,
we add a new node
|V(n)|+1
with a directed edge
(|V(n)|+1,w)
, where
w\in V(n)
is chosen with probability

\frac{D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)+\delta}{\sum_{w\in V(n)}(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)+\delta)}=\frac{D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)+\delta}{|E(n)|+\delta|V(n)|},

(1.1)

and update the node set
V(n+1)=V(n)\cup\{|V(n)|+1\}
.
If, with probability
\rho\in(0,1)
, a reciprocal edge
(w,|V(n)|+1)
is added, we
update the edge set as

E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(|V(n)|+1,w),(w,|V(n)|+1)\}
.
If the reciprocal edge is not created, set
E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(|V(n)|+1,w)\}
.

(ii)

With probability
\gamma
,
we add a new node
|V(n)|+1
with a directed edge
(w,|V(n)|+1)
, where
w\in V(n)
is chosen with probability

\frac{D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)+\delta}{\sum_{w\in V(n)}(D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)+\delta)}=\frac{D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)+\delta}{|E(n)|+\delta|V(n)|},

(1.2)

and update the node set
V(n+1)=V(n)\cup\{|V(n)|+1\}
.
If, with probability
\rho\in(0,1)
, a reciprocal edge
(|V(n)|+1,w)
is added, we
update the edge set as
E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(|V(n)|+1,w),(w,|V(n)|+1)\}
. If the reciprocal edge is not created, set
E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(w,|V(n)|+1)\}
.

Here we assume the offset parameter
\delta\geq 0
, and takes the same value for both in- and out-degrees.
From the description above, we see that
|V(n)|=n+1
, and
as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{|E(n)|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}1+\rho.

2
Preliminaries

We start by summarizing important embedding techniques that lay the foundation for theoretical results in Section
3
. Also, to prepare for analyses on the asymptotic structure, we provide useful definitions related to multivariate regular variation.

2.1
Embedding techniques

In this section, we first provide important background knowledge on continuous-time multitype
branching processes with immigration. Then we give the embedding framework for the
in- and out-degree sequences in our modified PA model with reciprocity. These are the key ingredients to prove the asymptotic results in Section
3
.

2.1.1
Continuous-Time Multitype Branching Process

We start this section with the general setup of continuous-time multitype branching processes as introduced in
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
, Chapter V)
.
Consider a set of particles of
K
types, and a type
i
particle has an exponential lifetime with rate
a_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,K
. Upon its death, this type
i
particle produces
Y^{(i)}_{j}
copies of type
j
particles for
j=1,\ldots,K
. Here
(Y^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,Y^{(i)}_{K})\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
is a random vector
with finite expectations,
m_{i,j}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl(Y^{(i)}_{j}\bigr)
,
i,j=1,\ldots,K
, and
with joint distribution

p^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{r}):=\mathbb{P}\left(Y^{(i)}_{j}=r_{j},j=1,\ldots,K\right),

and generating function

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{s}):=(f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{s}),\ldots,f^{(K)}(\boldsymbol{s})),

where
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}
, and

\displaystyle f^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{s})=\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}s_{j}^{Y^{(i)}_{j}}\right)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{K}}p^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{r})s_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots s_{K}^{r_{K}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,K.

All particles live and produce independently from each other, and of the past.

Let
\psi^{(i)}(t)
denote the number of type
i
particles at time
t
, and set

\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t):=(\psi^{(1)}(t),\ldots,\psi^{(K)}(t)),\qquad t\geq 0.

We assume that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(0)
is a
K
-dimensional random vector with distribution

p_{0}(\boldsymbol{r})
,
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
.
Following Chapter V.7.2 in
Athreya and Ney 1972
,
we define a matrix

A:=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,K}
with

\displaystyle a_{i,j}=a_{j}(m_{i,j}-\boldsymbol{1}_{\{i=j\}}).

(2.1)

Suppose that all entries of
e^{t_{0}A}
are positive for some
t_{0}>0
, then
by the Perron–Frobenius theorem (cf.
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
, Theorem V.2.1)
),
the matrix
A
has a largest positive eigenvalue,
\lambda_{1}>0
, with multiplicity 1.
Let
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}
be the left and right eigenvectors of
\lambda_{1}
respectively, with all coordinates strictly positive, and
\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}=1
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}=1
. Then Theorem 2 in
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
, Chapter V.7.5)
gives that there exists some non-negative random variable
W
such that

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}W\boldsymbol{v}.

(2.2)

Also,
\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
if
\mathbb{E}\bigl(Y^{(i)}_{j}\log Y^{(i)}_{j}\bigr)<\infty
for all
i,j=1,2,\ldots,K
.

In
Rabehasaina and Woo 2021
, the multitype branching process defined above is extended to
a multitype branching process with immigration
(mBI process), which is similar to the construction of a birth process with immigration; see
Tavaré 1987
;
Wang and Resnick 2019
for details.
Suppose that
N_{\theta}(t)
is the counting function of homogeneous
Poisson points
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\ldots
with rate

\theta>0
. Independent of this Poisson process, assume that we have
iid
copies of a multitype branching process
\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(t):t\geq 0\}_{k\geq 0}
, with the same branching mechanism as the
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\cdot)
process defined in the previous paragraph.
Assume also that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(0)
is a
K
-dimensional random vector with distribution
p_{0}(\boldsymbol{r})
,
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{K}
.
Set
\tau_{0}=0
, and
at times
\tau_{k},k\geq 1
, new particles (i.e. immigrants) arrive.
The vector process giving the number of each type at time
t
is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\theta}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(t-\tau_{k})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{k}\}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\theta}(t)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(t-\tau_{k}),

(2.3)

and we refer to the Poisson rate parameter
\theta
as the immigration parameter.
Here the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\theta}(\cdot)
is a Markov chain on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

Theorem 1

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\theta}(\cdot)
be a mBI process as given in (
2.3
), then as
t\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\theta}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{k}}W_{k}\boldsymbol{v},

(2.4)

where
\{W_{k}:k\geq 0\}
are iid, satisfy
e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}W_{k}\boldsymbol{v}
, and are independent from
\{\tau_{k}:k\geq 0\}
.

By the independence between
\{W_{k}:k\geq 0\}
and
\{\tau_{k}:k\geq 0\}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{k}}W_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(W_{0})\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{k}}\right)=\mathbb{E}(W_{0})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta+\lambda_{1}}\right)^{k}<\infty,

which implies the infinite series on the right hand side of (
2.4
) converges a.s..
Note also that Theorem 3 in
Rabehasaina and Woo 2021
specifies the convergence in distribution in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
for the mBI process in (
2.4
), which is weaker than our results in Theorem
1
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is deferred to Section
5.1
.
In what follows, we will consider the
K=2
case, and embed the in- and out-degree sequences into a sequence of two-type branching processes with immigration.

2.1.2
Embedding

We now introduce the embedding framework for the PA model with reciprocity, using mBI processes.
Let
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t)\equiv(\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(t),\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(t)):t\geq 0\}_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent two-type mBI processes with life time parameters
a_{1}=\alpha,a_{2}=\gamma
, immigration parameter
\delta\geq 0
, and offspring generating functions

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)s_{1}^{2}+\rho s_{1}^{2}s_{2},

(2.5)

\displaystyle f^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)s_{2}^{2}+\rho s_{1}s_{2}^{2},

(2.6)

for
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
.
The initial value of every immigrant is a 2-dimensional random vector with distribution

\displaystyle p_{0}(\boldsymbol{r})\equiv\left(\alpha(1-\rho)\right)^{\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{r}=(1,0)\}}}\left(\gamma(1-\rho)\right)^{\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{r}=(0,1)\}}}\rho^{\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{r}=(1,1)\}}}.

(2.7)

Initial values of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t):t\geq 0\}_{k\geq 1}
will be specified during the construction process.
Equation (
2.5
) gives that at the end of the life time of a type 1 particle, with
probability
1-\rho
, it will split into two type 1 particles, increasing the total number of
type 1 particles by 1.
With probability
\rho
, a type 1 particle will give birth to 2 type 1 particles and 1 type 2 particle upon its death, which increases the total numbers of type 1 and 2 particles both by 1.
Similar interpretations also apply to (
2.6
).
Later in Theorem
2
, we will mimic the evolution of in- and out-degree processes
of node
k
using
\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(\cdot)
and
\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(\cdot)
, respectively.

Conditioning on the current state
\boldsymbol{x}\equiv(x_{1},x_{2})
,
the jump probabilities from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{y}
is

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha(1-\rho)(x_{1}+\delta)}{\alpha x_{1}+\gamma x_{2}+\delta},&\qquad\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+(1,0),\\
\frac{\gamma(1-\rho)(x_{2}+\delta)}{\alpha x_{1}+\gamma x_{2}+\delta},&\qquad\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+(0,1),\\
\frac{\alpha\rho(x_{1}+\delta)+\gamma\rho(x_{2}+\delta)}{\alpha x_{1}+\gamma x_{2}+\delta},&\qquad\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}+(1,1).\end{cases}

Then we see that

\rho
is the probability that when the first component jumps, it is due to both components making a jump.
Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(0)=(1,1)
, then we set
T_{0}=0
and initiate
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(\cdot)
at
t=0
. Let
T_{1}
be the first time when the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(\cdot)
process jumps.
Then for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T_{1}>t\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-t\left(\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{1,\delta}(0)+\delta\right)+\gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{1,\delta}(0)+\delta\right)\right)\right\}=e^{-t(1+\delta)}.

Hence,
T_{1}
follows an exponential distribution with rate
1+\delta
.
We then initiate the process,
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(t-T_{1}):t\geq T_{1}\}
, with one of the three initial values below:

(i)

If the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(\cdot)
is increased by
(1,0)
, set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(0)=(0,1)
.

(ii)

If the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(\cdot)
is increased by
(0,1)
, set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(0)=(1,0)
.

(iii)

If the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(\cdot)
is increased by
(1,1)
, set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(0)=(1,1)
.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(0)
is a 2-dimensional random vector with generating function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(s_{1}^{\xi^{(1)}_{2,\delta}(0)}s_{2}^{\xi^{(1)}_{2,\delta}(0)}\right)=\alpha(1-\rho)s_{2}+\gamma(1-\rho)s_{1}+\rho s_{1}s_{2},\qquad s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,1].

(2.8)

Define
R_{1}:=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(T_{1})=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(0)+(1,1)\}}
, then
\mathbb{P}(R_{1}=1)=\rho=1-\mathbb{P}(R_{1}=0)
.
Set also

\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}:=\sigma\left(R_{1};\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t-T_{k-1}):t\in[T_{k-1},T_{1}]\right\}_{k=1,2}\right).

Next, let
T_{2}
be the first time when one of the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(\cdot)
,
k=1,2
, jumps, and we
let
J_{2}
be the label, 1 or 2, of the process
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}
that jumps first.
When the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{2},\delta}(\cdot)

process jumps and is augmented by
(1,0)
, then we initiate
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3,\delta}(t-T_{2}):t\geq T_{2}\}
with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3,\delta}(0)=(0,1)
. When the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{2},\delta}(\cdot)

process is augmented by
(0,1)
, we set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3,\delta}(0)=(1,0)
, and
set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3,\delta}(0)=(1,1)
if the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{2},\delta}(\cdot)

process is augmented by
(1,1)
.
Define also that

R_{2}=\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{2},\delta}(T_{2}-T_{J_{2}-1})=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{2},\delta}(T_{1}-T_{J_{2}-1})+(1,1)\right\}},

and write
\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}(\cdot):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}})
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}\left(R_{2}=1,J_{2}=1,T_{2}-T_{1}>t\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\times\frac{\alpha\left(\xi^{(1)}_{1,\delta}(T_{1})+\delta\right)+\gamma\left(\xi^{(2)}_{1,\delta}(T_{1})+\delta\right)}{\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{2}\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(T_{1}-T_{k-1})+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{2}\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(T_{1}-T_{k-1})+2\delta},

and since
\sum_{k=1}^{2}\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(T_{1}-T_{k-1})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(T_{1}-T_{k-1})=2+R_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle=\rho\times\frac{\alpha\left(\xi^{(1)}_{1,\delta}(T_{1})+\delta\right)+\gamma\left(\xi^{(2)}_{1,\delta}(T_{1})+\delta\right)}{2(1+\delta)+R_{1}}e^{-t(2(1+\delta)+R_{1})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}\left(R_{2}=1\right)\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}\left(J_{2}=1\right)\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}\left(T_{2}-T_{1}>t\right).

Therefore,
(R_{2},J_{2},T_{2}-T_{1})
are conditionally independent under
\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{1}}}
.

In general, for
n\geq 1
, suppose that we have initiated
n+1
mBI processes at time
T_{n}
,

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t-T_{k-1}):t\geq T_{k-1}\}_{1\leq k\leq n+1}.

(2.9)

Define
T_{n+1}
as the first time when one of the processes in (
2.9
) jumps, and let

J_{n+1}
be the label of the process that jumps at
T_{n+1}
. If the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{n+1},\delta}(\cdot)

process is augmented by
(1,0)
, then we initiate
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+2,\delta}(t-T_{n+1}):t\geq T_{n+1}\}
with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+2,\delta}(0)=(0,1)
. If the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{n+1},\delta}(\cdot)

process is augmented by
(0,1)
, set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+2,\delta}(0)=(1,0)
, and
set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+2,\delta}(0)=(1,1)
if the
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{n+1},\delta}(\cdot)

process is augmented by
(1,1)
.
Hence,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+2,\delta}(0)
has the same generating function as in (
2.8
).
Write

R_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{n+1},\delta}(T_{n+1}-T_{J_{n+1}-1})=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{J_{n+1},\delta}(T_{n}-T_{J_{n+1}-1})+(1,1)\right\}},

and define

\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}:=\sigma\left(\{R_{l}\}_{l=1}^{n};\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t-T_{k-1}):t\in[T_{k-1},T_{n}]\right\}_{1\leq k\leq n+1}\right).

Using the fact that

\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})=n+1+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left(R_{n+1}=1,J_{n+1}=w,T_{n+1}-T_{n}>t\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\times\frac{\alpha\left(\xi^{(1)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)+\gamma\left(\xi^{(2)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)}{\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})+(n+1)\delta}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left\{-t\left(\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\xi^{(1)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\xi^{(2)}_{k,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{k-1})+(n+1)\delta\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\rho e^{-t\left((n+1)(1+\delta)+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\alpha\left(\xi^{(1)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)+\gamma\left(\xi^{(2)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)}{(n+1)(1+\delta)+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left(R_{n+1}=1\right)\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left(J_{n+1}=w\right)\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left(T_{n+1}-T_{n}>t\right),

(2.10)

i.e.
(J_{n+1},R_{n+1},T_{n+1}-T_{n})
are conditionally independent under
\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}
.

Write

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n}):=\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(T_{n}),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{1}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n+1,\delta}(0),(0,0),\ldots\right),\qquad n\geq 0,

then the embedding framework described above shows that

\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n}):n\geq 0\}
is Markovian on
\left(\mathbb{N}^{2}\right)^{\infty}
.
In the following theorem, we embed the evolution of in- and out-degree processes into the mBI framework.

Theorem 2

In
\left(\mathbb{N}^{2}\right)^{\infty}
, define the in- and out-degree sequences as

\boldsymbol{D}(n):=\left(\bigl(D^{\text{in}}_{1}(n),D^{\text{out}}_{1}(n)\bigr),\ldots,\bigl(D^{\text{in}}_{n+1}(n),D^{\text{out}}_{n+1}(n)\bigr),(0,0),\ldots\right).

Then for
\{T_{k}:k\geq 0\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t-T_{k-1}):t\geq T_{k-1}\}_{k\geq 1}
constructed above,
we have that in
\left(\left(\mathbb{N}^{2}\right)^{\infty}\right)^{\infty}
,

\displaystyle\bigl\{\boldsymbol{D}(n):n\geq 0\bigr\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n}):n\geq 0\right\}.

Proof

By the model description in Section
1.1
,
\{\boldsymbol{D}(n):n\geq 0\}
is Markovian on
\left(\mathbb{N}^{2}\right)^{\infty}
, so
it suffices to check the transition probability from
\boldsymbol{D}(n)
to
\boldsymbol{D}(n+1)
agrees with that from

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n})
to
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n+1})
.
Write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\bigl(0,0\bigr),\ldots,\bigl(0,0\bigr),\underbrace{\bigl(1,0\bigr)}_{\text{$w$-th entry}},\bigl(0,0\bigr),\ldots\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\bigl(0,0\bigr),\ldots,\bigl(0,0\bigr),\underbrace{\bigl(0,1\bigr)}_{\text{$w$-th entry}},\bigl(0,0\bigr),\ldots\right),

and let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by the history of the network up to
n
steps.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\boldsymbol{D}(n+1)=\boldsymbol{D}(n)+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(1-\rho)(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)+\delta)}{|E(n)|+\delta(n+1)},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\boldsymbol{D}(n+1)=\boldsymbol{D}(n)+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(1-\rho)(D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)+\delta)}{|E(n)|+\delta(n+1)},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\boldsymbol{D}(n+1)=\boldsymbol{D}(n)+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\frac{\alpha(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)+\delta)+\gamma(D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)+\delta)}{|E(n)|+\delta(n+1)},

(2.13)

where
|E(n)|-(n+1)
follows a binomial distribution with size
n
and success probability
\rho
.

By (
2.10
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n+1})={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n})+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(2)})=\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}(J_{n+1}=w,R_{n+1}=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\rho\left(\xi^{(1)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)+\gamma\rho\left(\xi^{(2)}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta\right)}{(n+1)(1+\delta)+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}}.

(2.14)

Note also that for
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n-1}}}(R_{n}=1)=\rho=1-\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n-1}}}(R_{n}=0)
.
Then for
n\geq 1
and
r_{l}\in\{0,1\}
,
1\leq l\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{l}=r_{l},1\leq l\leq n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{R_{l}=r_{l},1\leq l\leq n-1\right\}}\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n-1}}}(R_{n}=r_{n})\right],

and if
r_{n}=1
, we have

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{l}=r_{l},1\leq l\leq n-1)\mathbb{P}(R_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\cdots=\prod_{l=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(R_{l}=r_{l}).

Therefore,
\{R_{l}:1\leq l\leq n\}
are iid Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbb{P}(R_{1}=1)=\rho
, then

\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}
has the same distribution as
|E(n)|-(n+1)
, which
shows that the transition probability in (
2.14
) agrees with that in (
2.13
).
Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n+1})={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n})+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(1-\rho)(\xi_{w,\delta}^{(1)}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta)}{(n+1)(1+\delta)+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathcal{F}_{T_{n}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n+1})={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{\delta}(T_{n})+\boldsymbol{e}_{w}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(1-\rho)(\xi_{w,\delta}^{(2)}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})+\delta)}{(n+1)(1+\delta)+\sum_{l=1}^{n}R_{l}},

which agree with transition probabilities in (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), respectively.

2.2
Multivariate regular variation

Later in Section
3.2
, we will derive the asymptotic distribution of the joint in- and out-degree counts, and show that they are jointly heavy tailed. To formalize our analysis, we provide some useful definitions related to
multivariate regular variation
(MRV).

Suppose that
\mathbb{C}_{0}\subset\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
are two closed cones, and we provide the definition of
\mathbb{M}
-convergence in Definition
1

(cf.
Lindskog et al. 2014
;
Hult and Lindskog 2006
;
Das et al. 2013
;
Kulik and Soulier 2020
;
Basrak and Planinić 2019
) on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
, which lays the theoretical foundation of regularly varying measures (cf. Definition
2
).

Definition 1

Let
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
be the set of Borel
measures on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
which are finite on
sets bounded away from
\mathbb{C}_{0}
, and

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
be the set of
continuous, bounded, non-negative functions on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
whose supports are bounded away from
\mathbb{C}_{0}
. Then for
\mu_{n},\mu\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
, we say
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in

\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
, if
\int f\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\to\int f\mathrm{d}\mu
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
.

Without loss of generality
Lindskog et al. 2014
, we can
and do take functions in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
to be uniformly continuous as well. Denote the modulus
of continuity of a uniformly continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\Delta_{f}(\delta)=\sup\{|f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y})|:d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})<\delta\}

(2.15)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is an appropriate metric
on the domain of
f
. Uniform continuity means
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\Delta_{f}(\delta)=0.

Definition 2

The distribution of a
random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}\in\cdot)
,
is (standard) regularly varying on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
with index
c>0
(written as
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\text{MRV}(c,b(t),\nu,\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
) if there exists some scaling function
b(t)\in\text{RV}_{1/c}
and a limit measure
\nu(\cdot)\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
such that
as
t\to\infty
,

t\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}/b(t)\in\cdot)\rightarrow\nu(\cdot),\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}).

(2.16)

When analyzing the asymptotic dependence between components of a
bivariate random vector

\boldsymbol{Z}
satisfying (
2.16
), it is often informative
to make a polar coordinate transform and
consider the transformed points located on the
L_{1}
unit sphere

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac{x}{|x|+|y|},\frac{y}{|x|+|y|}\right),

(2.17)

after thresholding the data according to the

L_{1}
norm.
The plot of the transformed points is referred to as the diamond plot,
which provides a visualization of dependence.
Also, provided that
|x|+|y|
is larger than some predetermined threshold, the density plot of thresholded values
x/(|x|+|y|)
is called the angular density plot.
These plots characterize the asymptotic dependence structure for extremal observations.

In Section
3.3.2
, we apply the transformation in (
2.17
) to nodes with large
in- and out-degrees, and find that the angular density plot concentrates around
some particular value. In the terminology of
Das and Resnick 2017
, this indicates that the limiting in- and out-degree pair has
full asymptotic dependence
.

2.2.1
A modification of Breiman’s
Theorem.

For the study of the regular
variation properties of the asymptotic distribution of degree
frequencies given in Theorem
6
, we need the following
generalization of Breiman’s Theorem
Breiman 1965
. This result
about products has spawned many proofs and generalizations. See for
instance

Resnick 2007
;
Kulik and Soulier 2020
;
Fougeres and Mercadier 2012
;
Maulik et al. 2002
;
Chen et al. 2019
;
Basrak et al. 2002
.

Theorem 3

Suppose
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}(t):t\geq 0\}
is an
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
-valued stochastic
process
for some
p\geq 1
. Let
X
be a positive random variable with
regularly varying distribution satisfying for some scaling function
b(t)
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}(X/b(t)>x)=x^{-c}=:\nu_{c}\bigl((x,\infty)\bigr),\quad x>0,c>0.

Further suppose

1.

For some finite and positive random vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}\quad(\text{almost surely});

2.

The random variable
X
and the process
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\cdot)
are independent.

Then:

(i) In
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}))
,

t\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\Bigl({\boldsymbol{\xi}(X)},\frac{X}{b(t)}\Bigr)\in\cdot\,\Bigr]\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}\in\cdot\,)\times\nu_{c}(\cdot)=:\eta(\cdot).

(2.18)

If
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}
is of the form
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}=:L\boldsymbol{v}
where
L>0

almost surely and
\boldsymbol{v}\in(0,\infty)^{p}
, then
\eta(\cdot)

concentrates on the subcone
\mathcal{L}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\})
where
\mathcal{L}=\{\theta\boldsymbol{v}:\theta>0\}
.

(ii) If additionally, for some
c^{\prime}>c
we have the condition

\kappa:=\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\Bigl({\|\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\|}\Bigr)^{c^{\prime}}\right]<\infty,

(2.19)

for some
L_{p}
norm
\|\cdot\|
, then the product
of components in
(
2.18
),
\boldsymbol{\xi}(X)X
, has a regularly varying distribution with
scaling function
b(t)
and
in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
,

t\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\frac{X\boldsymbol{\xi}(X)}{b(t)}\in\cdot\,\Bigr]\longrightarrow\left(\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}\in\cdot\,)\times\nu_{c}\right)\circ h^{-1},

(2.20)

where
h(\boldsymbol{y},x)=x\boldsymbol{y}
.

For the classical Breiman Theorem where
p=1
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\equiv\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}
,
(
2.19
) is the expected moment condition.
Detailed proofs of Theorem
3
are collected in Section
5.2
.

3
Asymptotic Results

With the embedding framework in Theorem
2
, we derive
theoretical results on: (1) the a.s. growth of in- and out-degrees for
a fixed node; (2) the limiting joint distribution of in- and
out-degrees based on degree counts; (3) the asymptotic dependence
structure for large in- and out-degrees.

3.1
Convergence of Degrees for a Fixed Node

By the construction of the
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(t):t\geq 0\}_{k\geq 1}
, we see that the corresponding
A
matrix as introduced in (
2.1
) is equal to

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\alpha\rho\\
\gamma\rho&\gamma\end{bmatrix},

(3.1)

with the largest eigenvalue

\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}+4\alpha\gamma\rho^{2}}\right)=:\frac{1+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}.

The left and right eigenvectors with respect to
\lambda_{1}
are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\alpha+\gamma(2\rho-1)+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)\begin{bmatrix}1\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\frac{\gamma-\alpha+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\gamma\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\alpha+\gamma(2\rho-1)+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\alpha-\gamma+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2\sqrt{\Delta}}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
\frac{\gamma\rho}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\end{bmatrix},

(3.3)

which satisfy
\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}=1
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}=1
.
The following theorem gives the joint convergence of
(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n),D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n))
for a fixed node
w
.

Theorem 4

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
are as defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
Then for
w\geq 1
, there exists some finite random variable
L_{w}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n)}{n^{\lambda_{1}/(1+\rho+\delta)}},\,\frac{D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n)}{n^{\lambda_{1}/(1+\rho+\delta)}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}L_{w}\boldsymbol{v}.

(3.4)

Proof

By the embedding results in Theorem
2
, we prove
(
3.4
) using the mBI framework, i.e. by proving

\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-{T_{w-1}})}{n^{\lambda_{1}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}L_{w}\boldsymbol{v}.

Based on the definition of
\{T_{k}:k\geq 0\}
,
we apply
(
Athreya and Ney 1972
, Theorem III.9.1)
to obtain that

T_{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1+\delta)k+\sum_{l=1}^{k}R_{l}}

is an
L_{2}
-bounded martingale with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{T_{k}}:k\geq 0\}
.
Then applying the proof machinery in
(
Athreya et al. 2008
, Proposition 2.2)

gives that
there exists some finite random variable
Z
such that

\displaystyle T_{n}-\frac{1}{1+\rho+\delta}\log n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z,

(3.5)

Then we are left to show
the convergence of
e^{-\lambda_{1}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})
for
w\geq 1
, as
n\to\infty
.

Recall the construction of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k,\delta}(\cdot)\}_{k\geq 1}
in Section
2.1.2
.
For
w=1
, the

2.5

2.6

This leads to a slightly different mBI setup from the one given in Section
2.1.1

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}(t):t\geq 0\}

2.6

2.7

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}(\cdot)

Hence, as

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda_{1}T_{n}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(T_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\widetilde{W}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{k}}\widetilde{W}_{k}\right)\boldsymbol{v}.

w\geq 2

3.2
Convergence of Degree Counts

In the current section, we study the limiting behavior of joint degree counts:

\displaystyle N_{m,l}(n)=\sum_{w=1}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{(D^{\text{in}}_{w}(n),D^{\text{out}}_{w}(n))=(m,l)\right\}}.

Using the embedding result, the following theorem shows the convergence
of
N_{m,l}(n)/n
.

Theorem 5

Suppose that
\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(t):t\geq 0\}
is a two-type mBI process with the same
initialization and branching mechanism as
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}(\cdot)

given in (
2.8
). Then as
n\to\infty
, we have
for
m,l\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{m,l}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+\rho+\delta)e^{-t(1+\rho+\delta)}\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(t)=(m,l)\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=:\mathbb{P}\left(\big(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{O}\bigr)=(m,l)\right).

(3.10)

Proof

By the embedding results in Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{N_{m,l}(n)}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})=(m,l)\right\}}+\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,\delta}(T_{n})=(m,l)\right\}},

(3.11)

and we see that the second term on the right hand side goes to 0 a.s. as
n\to\infty
.
Then it suffices to consider the limiting behavior of the first term in (
3.11
),
which is divided into different parts below.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})=(m,l)\right\}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}(T_{n}-T_{w-1})=(m,l)\right\}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}\left(\frac{\log(n/w)}{1+\rho+\delta}\right)=(m,l)\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle\,+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}\left(\frac{\log(n/w)}{1+\rho+\delta}\right)=(m,l)\right\}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}\left(\frac{\log(n/w)}{1+\rho+\delta}\right)=(m,l)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\,+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w=2}^{n+1}\mathbb{P}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{w,\delta}\left(\frac{\log(n/w)}{1+\rho+\delta}\right)=(m,l)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad-\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,\delta}\left(-\frac{\log t}{1+\rho+\delta}\right)=(m,l)\right]\mathrm{d}t\right)

3.3.2
Speculation on hidden regular variation.

When a limit measure
concentrates on a subcone of the full state space,
to improve estimates of probabilities in the complement of the subcone,
we can seek a
second
hidden
regular variation regime after removing the
subcone. In the present case,
\mu(\cdot)
concentrates on

\mathcal{L}_{a}
and thus we may seek a regular variation property on

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{a}
using a weaker scaling function

b_{0}(t)
such that
t^{\lambda_{1}/(1+\rho+\delta)}/b_{0}(t)\to\infty
. See
Das and Resnick 2017
;
Das and Resnick 2015
;
Resnick 2007
;
Das et al. 2013
;
Lindskog et al. 2014
. A
convenient way to seek the hidden regular variation is by using
generalized polar coordinates
which in this case amount to the
transformation

\boldsymbol{x}\to\Bigl(d_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{L}_{a}),\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{d_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{L}_{a})}\Bigr),

where
d_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is a metric on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}

chosen here for convenience to be the
L_{2}
-metric.
The distance of a point
(x,y)
to
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is readily computed

(
Das and Resnick 2017
, p. 881)
to be

d_{2}\bigl((x,y),\mathcal{L}_{a}\bigr)={|y-ax|}/{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}},

and we use a scaled version

d^{\prime}_{2}\bigl((x,y),\mathcal{L}_{a}\bigr)=|y-ax|.

(3.22)

Hidden regular
variation will be present for
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{O})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(T^{*})
if

tP\left[\left(\frac{d^{\prime}_{2}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(T^{*}),\mathcal{L}_{a})}{b_{0}(t)},\frac{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(T^{*})}{d^{\prime}_{2}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\delta}(T^{*}),\mathcal{L}_{a}\right)}\right)\in\cdot\,\right]

\mathbb{M}((0,\infty)\times\aleph_{\mathcal{L}_{a}})

\aleph_{\mathcal{L}_{a}}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{a}:d_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{L}_{a})=1\}
. Thus, considering (
3.22

4
Potential Extensions of the Model

Now that we have studied theoretical properties of the proposed PA
model with reciprocity, this section discusses possible extensions
that we will investigate in the future.

Randomized
\rho
.

So far we have assumed that each node has a common and fixed
reciprocity parameter,
\rho\in(0,1)
but this may not reflect
realistic behavior and allow good fits to datasets.
For instance, users on Facebook (nodes) may
have their own probabilities to respond to a post on his/her wall.
Keeping such heterogeneity in mind, we consider a randomized scenario: for each node
v\geq 1
, upon its creation, its affiliated reciprocity probability,
U_{v}
, is uniformly distributed on
[A,B]
. Here we also assume
0\leq A\leq B\leq 1
, and
\{U_{v}:v\geq 1\}
are iid.

, and has been detected in real datasets(cf.

), but the rigorous justification under the network setup needs working out.

Adding edges between existing nodes.

The model proposed in Section
1.1
requires adding a new node at each step, and the offset parameters are identical for the evolution of both in- and out-degrees. These assumptions are restrictive in practice.
We create an
extended model by including the following scenario
added to cases (i) and (ii) in Section
1.1
:

(iii)

With probability
\beta\in(0,1)
, we add an edge
(w_{1},w_{2})
between two existing nodes
w_{1},w_{2}\in V(n)
,
with probability

\displaystyle\frac{D^{\text{in}}_{w_{2}}(n)+\delta}{\sum_{w_{2}\in V(n)}(D^{\text{in}}_{w_{2}}(n)+\delta)}\frac{D^{\text{out}}_{w_{1}}(n)+\delta}{\sum_{w_{1}\in V(n)}(D^{\text{out}}_{w_{1}}(n)+\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\text{in}}_{w_{2}}(n)+\delta}{|E(n)|+\delta_{\text{in}}|V(n)|}\frac{D^{\text{out}}_{w_{1}}(n)+\delta}{|E(n)|+\delta|V(n)|}.

Then with probability
\rho\in(0,1)
, we add a reciprocal edge
(w_{2},w_{1})
. We then update
the edge set as

E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(w_{1},w_{2}),(w_{2},w_{1})\}
.
If the reciprocal edge is not created, then
E(n+1)=E(n)\cup\{(w_{1},w_{2})\}
.

With this third scenario, both the number of nodes and of edges in
G(n)

are
random.
The
\beta
-scenario makes the model more realistic since a large proportion of edges are created between two existing nodes in real datasets (e.g. those listed on KONECT
Kunegis 2013
). However,
the additional scenario does not fit into the embedding framework directly, as it may simultaneously increase the in- and out-degrees of two different nodes.
In addition, the HRV conjecture in Section
3.3.2
is based on the condition that
\lambda_{1}>2\lambda_{2}
, which is a direct result from the
\lambda_{1}\geq\log 2
assumption in Theorem
5
. With the additional
\beta
-scenario, it is possible to have

\lambda_{1}<2\lambda_{2}
, which may lead to different limiting behavior of
d^{\prime}_{2}((\mathcal{I},\mathcal{O}),\mathcal{L}_{a})
.

Asymptotic dependence in higher dimensions.

Another possible extension is to consider the multitype branching
structure with
K\geq 2
. For instance, when modeling the retweet or
reply network on Twitter, the follower/following relationship may also
have an impact on the generation of retweets or replies (directed
edges). Assume each node (user) to have four different degrees:
ordinary in- and out-degrees, together with the numbers of followers
and accounts one is following. Then an embedding framework using
four-type branching processes may provide a resolution to uncover the
asymptotic dependence structure between retweeting degrees and
friendship degrees.

We will explore further in all these directions in our future research.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{l}}\left(e^{-\lambda_{1}(t-\tau_{l})}\psi_{0,l}^{(i)}(t-\tau_{l})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{l}\}}\right)^{q}}{\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau_{l}}}.

(5.15)

Write
\widetilde{Z}^{(i)}_{l}(t):=e^{-\lambda_{1}(t-\tau_{l})}\psi_{0,l}^{(i)}(t-\tau_{l})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t\geq\tau_{l}\}}

Proof
