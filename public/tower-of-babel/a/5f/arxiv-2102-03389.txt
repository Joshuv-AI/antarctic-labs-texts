Online Statistical Inference for Stochastic Optimization via Kiefer-Wolfowitz Methods
Xi Chen Zehua Lai He Li Yichen Zhang

Abstract
This paper investigates the problem of online statistical inference of model parameters in stochastic optimization problems via the Kiefer-Wolfowitz algorithm with random search directions. We first present the asymptotic distribution for the Polyak-Ruppert-averaging type Kiefer-Wolfowitz (AKW) estimators, whose asymptotic covariance matrices depend on the distribution of search directions and the function-value query complexity. The distributional result reflects the trade-off between statistical efficiency and function query complexity. We further analyze the choice of random search directions to minimize certain summary statistics of the asymptotic covariance matrix. Based on the asymptotic distribution, we conduct online statistical inference by providing two construction procedures of valid confidence intervals.



Keywords:
Asymptotic normality, Kiefer-Wolfowitz stochastic approximation, online inference, stochastic optimization

Introduction

Stochastic optimization algorithms, introduced by
Robbins and
Monro 1951
;
Kiefer and
Wolfowitz 1952
, have been widely used in statistical estimation, especially for large-scale datasets and online learning where the sample arrives sequentially (e.g., web search queries, transactional data). The Robbins-Monro algorithm
(
Robbins and
Monro 1951
)
, often known as the stochastic gradient descent, is perhaps the most popular algorithm in stochastic optimization and has found a wide range of applications in statistics and machine learning.
Nevertheless, in many modern applications, the gradient information is not available. For example, the objective function may be embedded in a black box and the user can only access the noisy objective value for a given input. In such cases, the Kiefer-Wolfowitz algorithm
(
Kiefer and
Wolfowitz 1952
)
becomes a natural choice as it is completely free of gradient computation. Despite being equipped with an evident computational advantage to avoid gradient measurements, the Kiefer-Wolfowitz algorithm has been historically out of practice as compared to the Robbins-Monro counterpart.
Nonetheless, heralded by the big data era, there has been a restoration of the interest of gradient-free optimization in a wide range of applications in recent years
(
Conn
et al. 2009
;
Nesterov and
Spokoiny 2017
)
. We briefly highlight a few of them to motivate our paper.

•

In some bandit problems, one may only have black-box access to individual objective values but not to their gradients
(
Flaxman
et al. 2005
;
Shamir 2017
)
. Other examples include graphical models and variational inference problems, where the objective is defined variationally
(
Wainwright and
Jordan 2008
)
, and the explicit differentiation can be difficult.

•

In some scenarios, the computation of gradient information is possible but very expensive. For example, in the online sensor selection problem
(
Joshi and
Boyd 2008
)
, evaluating the stochastic gradient requires the inverse of matrices, which generates
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
computation cost per iteration, where
d
is the number of sensors in the network. In addition, the storage for gradient calculation also requires an
O(d^{3})
memory, which could be practically infeasible.

This paper aims to study the asymptotic properties of the Kiefer-Wolfowitz stochastic optimization and conduct online statistical inference.
In particular, we consider the problem,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}=\mathrm{argmin}\penalty\ F(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\quad\text{where}\;F(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}\left[f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right]=\int f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\text{d}\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},

(1)

where
f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
is a convex
individual loss function
for a data point
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
,
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the
population loss
function, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
is the true underlying parameter of a fixed dimension
d
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
denote any given initial point. Given a sequentially arriving online sample
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n}\}
, the
Robbins and
Monro 1951
algorithm
(RM)
, also known as the stochastic gradient descent (SGD), iteratively updates,

(RM)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\tt{(RM)}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}^{\tt{(RM)}}-\eta_{n}g(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
, (2)

where
\{\eta_{n}\}
is a positive non-increasing step-size sequence, and
g(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
denotes the stochastic gradient, i.e.,
g(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
.
In the scenarios that direct gradient measurements are inaccessible to practitioners, the
Kiefer and
Wolfowitz 1952
algorithm
(KW)
becomes the natural choice, as

(KW)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\tt{(KW)}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}^{\tt{(KW)}}-\eta_{n}\widehat{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
, (3)

where
\widehat{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
is an estimator of

. Under the univariate framework (

),
Kiefer and
Wolfowitz 1952
considered the finite-difference approximation

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(\theta_{n-1};\zeta_{n})=\frac{f(\theta_{n-1}+h_{n};\zeta_{n})-f(\theta_{n-1};\zeta_{n})}{h_{n}},

(4)

where
h_{n}
is be a positive deterministic sequence that goes to zero.

Blum 1954
later extended the algorithm to the multivariate case
and proved its almost sure convergence. This pioneering work extended in various directions of statistics and control theory (see, e.g.,
Fabian 1967
;
Fabian 1980
;
Hall and
Heyde 1980
;
Ruppert 1982
;
Chen 1988
;
Polyak and
Tsybakov 1990
;
Spall 1992
;
Chen
et al. 1999
;
Spall 2000
;
Hall and
Molchanov 2003
;
Dippon 2003
;
Mokkadem and
Pelletier 2007
;
Broadie
et al. 2011
). In the optimization literature, the Kiefer-Wolfowitz
(KW)
algorithm is often referred to as the gradient-free stochastic optimization, or zeroth-order SGD
(
Agarwal
et al. 2010
;
Agarwal et al. 2011
;
Jamieson
et al. 2012
;
Ghadimi and
Lan 2013
;
Duchi
et al. 2015
;
Shamir 2017
;
Nesterov and
Spokoiny 2017
;
Wang
et al. 2018
, among others)
.

For the
(RM)
algorithm in (
(RM)
),
Ruppert 1988
and
Polyak and
Juditsky 1992
characterize the limiting distribution and statistical efficiency of the
averaged iterate

\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}^{{\tt{(RM)}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{{\tt{(RM)}}}
by

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}^{{\tt{(RM)}}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right)\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},H^{-1}SH^{-1}\right),

(5)

where
H=\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
is the Hessian matrix of
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
at
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
, and
S=\mathbb{E}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star};\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\top}]
is the Gram matrix of the stochastic gradient.
Under a well-specified model, this asymptotic covariance matrix matches the inverse Fisher information and the averaged
(RM)
estimator is asymptotically efficient. Based on the limiting distribution result (
5
), there are many recent research efforts devoted to statistical inference for
(RM)
. A brief survey is conducted at the end of the introduction.

For the
(KW)
scheme, we can similarly construct the averaged Kiefer-Wolfowitz
(AKW)
estimator

(AKW)

\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}^{\tt{(KW)}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\tt{(KW)}}
.
(6)

As compared to well-established asymptotic properties of
(RM)
, study of the asymptotics of
(AKW)
is limited, particularly with a random sampling direction in multivariate
(KW)
.
In this paper, we study the
(KW)
algorithm (
(KW)
) with random search directions
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, i.e.,
at each iteration
i=1,2,\dots,n
, a random direction
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is sampled independently from
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and the
(KW)
gradient

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+h_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})-f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})}{h_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}.

(7)

Compared to the
(RM)
scheme,
(KW)
introduces additional randomness into the stochastic gradient estimator through
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\}
. Indeed, as one can see from our main result in Theorem
3.3
,
(AKW)
is no longer statistically efficient and its asymptotic covariance structure depends on the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. It opens the room for the investigation on the impact of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
(see Section
3.1
for details). We further extend the estimator to utilize multiple function-value queries per step and establish an online statistical inference framework. We summarize our main results and contributions as follows,

•

First, we quantify the asymptotic covariance structure of
(AKW)
in Theorem
3.3
. Since the asymptotic distribution depends on the choice of the direction variable
\boldsymbol{v}
, we provide an introductory analysis on the asymptotic performance for different choices of random directions for constructing
(AKW)
estimators (see Section
3.1
).

•

The efficiency loss of
(AKW)
is due to the information constraint as one evaluates only
two
function values at each iteration.
We analyze the
(AKW)
estimators in which multiple function queries can be assessed at each iteration, and show that the asymptotic covariance matrix decreases as the number of function queries
m+1
increases (see Section
3.2
). Moreover,
(AKW)
achieves asymptotic statistical efficiency as
m\rightarrow\infty
. We further show that when
\boldsymbol{v}
is sampled without replacement from
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
with a discrete uniform distribution of any orthonormal basis,
(AKW)
achieves asymptotic statistical efficiency with
d+1
function queries per iteration.

•

Based on the asymptotic distribution, we propose two online statistical inference procedures. The first one is using a plug-in estimator of the asymptotic covariance matrix, which separately estimates the Hessian matrix and Gram matrix of the
(KW)
gradients (with additional function-value queries, see Theorem
4.3
). The second procedure is to characterize the distribution of intermediate
(KW)
iterates as a stochastic process and construct an asymptotically pivotal statistic by normalizing the
(AKW)
estimator, without directly estimating the covariance matrix.
This inference procedure follows the “random scaling” method proposed in
Lee
et al. 2022a
that considers the online inference for the
(RM)
scheme. These two procedures have their advantages and disadvantages: the plug-in approach leads to better empirical performance but requires additional function-value queries to estimate the Hessian matrix, while the other one is more efficient in both computation and storage, though its finite-sample performance is inferior in practice when the dimension is large. A practitioner may choose the approach suitable to her computational resources and requirement of the inference accuracy.

Lastly, we provide a brief literature survey on the recent works for statistical inference for the
(RM)
-type SGD algorithms.
Chen
et al. 2020
developed a batch-means estimator of the limiting covariance matrix

in (
5
), which only uses the stochastic gradient information (i.e., without estimating any Hessian matrices).
Zhu
et al. 2023
further extended the batch-means method in

et al. 2020
to a fully online covariance estimator.
Lee
et al. 2022a
extended the results in
Polyak and
Juditsky 1992
to a functional central limit theorem and utilized it to propose a novel online inference procedure that allows for efficient implementation, followed by
Lee
et al. 2022b
;
Chen
et al. 2023
for application to quantile regression and generalized method of moments.
Fang
et al. 2018
presented a perturbation-based resampling procedure for inference.
Su and
Zhu 2018
proposed a tree-structured inference scheme, which splits the SGD into several threads to construct confidence intervals.
Liang and
Su 2019
introduced a moment-adjusted method and its corresponding inference procedure.

Toulis and
Airoldi 2017
considered the implicit SGD, and investigate the statistical inference problem under the variant.
Duchi and

Chao and

proposed a class of generalized regularized dual averaging (RDA) algorithms and make uncertainty quantification possible for online

2
Kiefer-Wolfowitz Algorithm

In this section, we introduce the general form of the Kiefer-Wolfowitz
(KW)
gradient estimator and the corresponding iterative algorithm

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\widehat{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
.
In the seminal work by
Blum 1954
, the
(KW)
gradient estimator
\widehat{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
is constructed by approximating the stochastic gradient
g(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
using the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\}
, as search directions.
In particular, given any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
, the
k
-th coordinate of the
(KW)
gradient estimator

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\big)_{k}=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{e}_{k};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})}{h},\qquad\text{for}\quad k=1,2,\ldots,d,

(9)

where
h
is a spacing parameter for approximation. At each iteration, (
9
) queries
d+1
function values from
d
fixed directions
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
. To reduce the query complexity, a random difference becomes a natural choice.
Koronacki 1975
introduced a random version of the
(KW)
algorithm using a sequence of random unit vectors that are independent and uniformly distributed on the unit sphere or unit cube.
Spall 1992
also provided a random direction version of the
(KW)
algorithm, named as the simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (SPSA) algorithm and later extended to several variants
Chen
et al. 1999
;
Spall 2000
;
He
et al. 2003
. These random direction methods can reduce the bias in gradient estimates as compared to their non-random counterparts. In the following, we write the
(KW)
algorithm with general random search directions, as in (
7
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\widehat{g}_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n}),

\displaystyle\text{where }\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta}):=\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})}{h}\boldsymbol{v}.

(10)

Here
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\}
is sampled from an underlying distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
satisfying certain conditions (see Assumption
4
in Section
3
). At each iteration
n
, the algorithm samples a direction vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
independently from
P_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and makes two solitary function-value queries,
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
and
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+h_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
.

We refer to the
(KW)
gradient estimator
\widehat{g}_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
in (
10
) as a
two-query
finite-difference approximation of the stochastic gradient.
If one is allowed to make additional function-value queries, an averaging of the function values from multiple directions generates a
multi-query
stochastic gradient estimator with reduced variance. In particular, at each iteration
n
, the practitioner makes
m+1
queries
\{f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n}),f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+h_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(j)};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})\}_{1\leq j\leq m}
via
m
random directions
\big\{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(j)}\big\}
sampled from
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. If
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a finite distribution, practitioners may choose to sample
with
or
without replacement
.
In summary, an (
m+1
)
-query

(KW)
algorithm constructs a stochastic gradient estimator

\overline{g}_{n}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{g}_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(j)}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})=\frac{1}{mh_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Delta_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(j)}}f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(j)},

(11)

at each iteration
n
, and updates
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\overline{g}_{n}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})
. Here we restrict the procedure to sampling from the same distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
independently across different iterations.
We use
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{(m)}
to denote the final
(KW)
estimator using the above
(m+1)
-query finite-difference approximation.

We now provide some illustrative examples of the two-query
(KW)
estimator
\widehat{g}_{h_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}}
in (
10
) used in popular statistical models, and we will refer to these examples throughout the paper. A multi-query extension of the examples can be constructed accordingly.

Example 2.1

(Linear Regression)
.

Consider a linear regression model

y_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+\epsilon_{i}
where
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}),\penalty\ i=1,2,\dots,n\}
is an i.i.d. sample of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\boldsymbol{x},y)
and the noise
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. We use a quadratic loss function
f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=(y-\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})^{2}
. Therefore, the stochastic gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-y\right)\boldsymbol{x}
,
and the
(KW)
gradient estimator
\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\{\boldsymbol{x},y\})
in (
10
) becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\{\boldsymbol{x},y\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\left[\big(y-\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{v})\big)^{2}-\big(y-\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\big)^{2}\right]\boldsymbol{v}=2\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\big(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-y\big)\boldsymbol{x}+h(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})^{2}\boldsymbol{v}.

Example 2.2

(Logistic Regression)
.

Consider a logistic regression model with a binary response
y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
generated by

\Pr(y_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\left(1+\exp\left(-y_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right)\right)^{-1}
.
The individual loss function
f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\log\left(1+\exp(-y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)
. The stochastic gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=-y\boldsymbol{x}\left(1+\exp(y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{-1}
, and the
(KW)
gradient estimator
\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\{\boldsymbol{x},y\})
in (
10
) becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\{\boldsymbol{x},y\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{h}\left[\log\big(1+\exp(-y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{v}))\big)-\log\big(1+\exp(-y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-y\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{1+\exp(y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})}+\frac{y^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})^{2}\exp(y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})h\boldsymbol{v}}{2(1+\exp(y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2}),\;\;\text{as }h\rightarrow 0_{+},

under some regularity conditions on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and the distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

We note that for the
(RM)
scheme with differentiable loss functions, the stochastic gradient is an unbiased estimator of the population gradient under very mild assumption, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}g(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. In contrast, the
(KW)
gradient estimator is no longer an unbiased estimator of
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. In the following lemma, we precisely quantifies the bias incurred by the
(KW)
gradient estimator.

Lemma 2.3
.

We assume that the population loss function
F(\cdot)
is twice continuously differentiable and
L_{f}
-smooth, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\preceq L_{f}I_{d}
for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given any fixed parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, suppose the random direction vector
\boldsymbol{v}
is independent from
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\;\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}\big(\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}-I_{d}\big)\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\|+\frac{h}{2}L_{f}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3},

where the expectation in
\mathbb{E}\;\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
takes over both the randomness in
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

The proof of Lemma
2.3
is provided in Section
A
of the Appendix.
To reduce the bias in the
(KW)
gradient, Lemma
2.3
indicates that one should choose the random direction
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
that satisfies the distributional constraint
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{\top}]=I_{d}
(see Assumption
4
in Section
3
). We will further conduct a comprehensive analysis in Section
3.1
on different choices of distributions
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
satisfying the condition
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{\top}]=I_{d}
. Despite the existence of the bias, as the spacing parameter
h_{n}\rightarrow 0
, the bias convergences to zero asymptotically.

3
Theoretical Results

We first introduce some regularity assumptions on the population loss
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and the individual loss
f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
.

Assumption 1
.

The population loss function
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is twice continuously differentiable, convex, and

3.1
Examples: choices of direction distribution

By Theorem
3.3
, the asymptotic covariance matrix of
(AKW)
estimator,
H^{-1}QH^{-1}
, depends on the distribution of search direction
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
via
Q=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}]
. In this section, we
compare the asymptotic covariance matrices of the
(AKW)
estimator when the random directions
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are sampled from different
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
’s. Several popular choices of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
are listed as follows,

(G)

Gaussian:
\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.

(S)

Spherical:
\boldsymbol{v}
is sampled from the uniform distribution on the sphere
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}=d
.

(I)

Uniform in the canonical basis:
\boldsymbol{v}
is sampled from
\big\{\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\ldots,\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\big\}
with equal probability, where
\left\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\right\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It is easy to verify that the above three classical choices of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
satisfy Assumption
4
, among which
(G)
and
(S)
are continuous distributions, while
(I)
is a discrete distribution. In particular,
(I)
is a discrete uniform distribution with equal probability among the
d
vectors of the standard basis of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which can be generalized in the following two forms.

(U)

Uniform in an arbitrary orthonormal basis
U
:
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is sampled uniformly from
\left\{\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\right.

\left.\ldots,\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{d}\right\}
, where
\left\{\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{d}\right\}
is an arbitrary
orthonormal basis
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., the matrix
U=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{d})
is a
d\times d
orthonormal matrix such that
UU^{\top}=U^{\top}U=I
.

(P)

Non-uniform in the canonical basis with probability
(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{d})
:
\boldsymbol{v}=\sqrt{1/p_{k}}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
with probability
p_{k}>0
, for
k\in[d]
and
\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}=1
.

The following proposition provides expressions of the matrix
Q
for the above five choices of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Under the assumptions in Theorem
3.3
, for above examples of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
,

(G)

Gaussian:
Q^{\tt{(G)}}=\left(2S+\mathrm{tr}(S)I_{d}\right)
.

(S)

Spherical:
Q^{\tt{(S)}}=\frac{d}{d+2}\left(2S+\mathrm{tr}(S)I_{d}\right)
.

(I)

Uniform in the canonical basis:
Q^{\tt{(I)}}=d\,\mathrm{diag}(S)
.

(U)

Uniform in an arbitrary orthonormal basis
U
:
Q^{\tt{(U)}}=d\,U\mathrm{diag}(U^{\top}SU)U^{\top}
.

(P)

Non-uniform in a natural coordinate basis:
Q^{\tt{(P)}}=\mathrm{diag}(S_{11}/p_{1},S_{22}/p_{2},\ldots,S_{dd}/p_{d})
.

From Proposition
3.5
, one can see that any of the above choices of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
leads to a
Q^{(\cdot)}
that strictly dominates
S
.
Take
S=I_{d}
as an example, we have
Q^{\tt{(G)}}=(d+2)I_{d}
and
Q^{\tt{(S)}}=Q^{\tt{(I)}}=Q^{\tt{(U)}}=dI_{d}
and
Q^{\tt{(P)}}=\mathrm{diag}(p_{1}^{-1},p_{2}^{-1},\dots,p_{d}^{-1})\succ I_{d}
where
p_{1}+p_{2}+\dots+p_{d}=1
. Several additional findings and implications of Proposition
3.5
are discussed in Section
A.2
of the Appendix. To briefly mention a few, the Gaussian direction
(G)
is always inferior to the spherical direction
(S)
. Among the rest of the choices, there is
no domination
relationship, and different optimality criterion in the experimental design leads to different optimal choices of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

4
Online Statistical Inference

In the previous section, we provide the asymptotic distribution for the
(AKW)
estimator. For the purpose of conducting statistical inference of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
, we need a consistent estimator of the limiting covariance
H^{-1}QH^{-1}
in (
12

\widehat{H}
and
\widehat{Q}
of
H
and
Q
, respectively, and estimate the asymptotic covariance by the
plug-in
estimator
\widehat{H}^{-1}\widehat{Q}\widehat{H}^{-1}
. Offline construction of those estimators is generally straightforward. However, as the
(KW)
scheme typically applies to sequential data, it is ideal to estimate the asymptotic covariance in an online fashion without storing the data. Therefore, one cannot simply replace the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
by its estimate
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}

4.1
Online inference without additional function-value queries

Despite the simplicity of the plug-in approach, the proposed estimator
\widehat{H}_{n}^{-1}\widehat{Q}_{n}\widehat{H}_{n}^{-1}
incurs additional computational and storage cost as it requires additional function-value queries for constructing
\widehat{H}_{n}
. It raises a natural question:
is it possible to conduct inference only based on
(KW)
iterates
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots}
without additional function-value queries
?

In this section, we provide an affirmative answer to this question, and propose an alternative online statistical inference procedure using the intermediate
(KW)
iterates only, without requiring any additional function-value query. Intuitively, the
(AKW)
estimator in (
(AKW)
) is constructed as the average of all intermediate
(KW)
iterates
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. If all iterates were independent and identically distributed, the asymptotic covariance
could have been directly estimated by the sample covariance matrix of the iterates
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}
. Unfortunately, the
(KW)
iterates are far from independent and indeed highly correlated. Nevertheless, the autocorrelation structure of the iterates can be carefully analyzed and utilized to construct the estimator of
H^{-1}QH^{-1}
.

In this paper, we adopt an alternative approach to take more advantage of the autocorrelation structure by leveraging the techniques from robust testing literature
(
Abadir and
Paruolo 1997
;
Kiefer
et al. 2000
;
Lee
et al. 2022a
)
. Such an estimator is often referred to as the Fixed Bandwidth Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Robust estimator (
fixed-
b
HAR) in the econometrics literature to overcome the series correlation and heteroskedasticity in the error terms for the OLS estimates of the linear regression. For stochastic approximation,
Lee
et al. 2022a
first utilized and generalized this technique to construct an online statistical inference procedure, and refer to this method as
random scaling
, followed by
Lee
et al. 2022b
;
Chen
et al. 2023
for extension to quantile regression and generalized method of moments.

In particular, we present the following theorem based on a functional extension of the distributional analysis of the intermediate
(KW)
iterates
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\}
as a stochastic process.

\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

or Theorem

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}V_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}}\Longrightarrow\frac{W_{1}}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}\left(W_{r}-rW_{1}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}r}},

h_{n}=h_{0}n^{-\gamma}
for some constant
h_{0}>0
, and
\gamma\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. The
(KW)
iterate
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
converges to
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
almost surely.
Furthermore, assume Assumptions
1
holds with
\delta_{1}=+\infty
. For sufficiently large
n
, we have for
0<\delta\leq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\|^{2+\delta}

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-\alpha(2+\delta)/2}.

where the constant
C
depends on
d,\lambda,L_{f},\alpha,\gamma,

\eta_{0},h_{0}
.

Remark A.1
.

The parameter dependency in Proposition
3.1
could be given explicitly as follows,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(CM_{1}\eta_{0}/(2\alpha-1)+CM_{2}/(2\beta-1)-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right)\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+M_{3}\left(\exp\left(-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right)+\frac{\eta_{0}n^{-\alpha}}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{M_{3}}{M_{1}}\exp\left(CM_{1}\eta_{0}/(2\alpha-1)+CM_{2}/(2\beta-1)-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right),

where the constant
C
above is a universal constant that does not depend on any constant/parameter in the assumptions. The other terms
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
above are given below,

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=C\left(L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}+M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}+L_{f}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=CL_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3},

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle=C\left(\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}+\left(h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{6}+M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(h_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}+1)\right)\right).

We will prove both Proposition
3.1
and Remark
A.1
below.

Proof.

We first assume Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
and give some bounds on
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
. By definition,
\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}=0
. From (
A.1
),

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}h_{n}L_{f}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}.

(24)

We can bound
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}
by the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{h_{n}}[F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+h_{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{n})-F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})]\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}),\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\rangle\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{6}

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{6}.

(25)

We also have the following fact for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left[\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|^{2}|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\;\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{0}^{h_{n}}\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+s\boldsymbol{v}_{n})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+s\boldsymbol{v}_{n};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n}),\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\rangle\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|^{2}\bigg|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{4}\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left[\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{0}^{h_{n}}\left\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+s\boldsymbol{v}_{n})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+s\boldsymbol{v}_{n};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\big|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{4}\frac{1}{h_{n}}\int_{0}^{h_{n}}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+s\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}+1)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+h_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}+1),

(26)

where in the second inequality, we use Assumption
2
.

Now decompose the update step as follows,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\frac{1}{h_{n}}[f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+h_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{n};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})-f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{n})]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\eta_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}).

We can derive that,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}-2\eta_{n}\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rangle+2\eta_{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle+\eta_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|^{2}.

(27)

For the first part in the RHS of the above inequality, using Lemma B.1 in
Su and
Zhu 2018
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle\geq\rho\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\min\left\{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|,\delta_{1}\right\}.

(28)

for some
\rho>0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\left|\eta_{n}\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}\left|\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta_{n}h_{n}L_{f}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq CL_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}h_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+C\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}\eta_{n}^{2},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{2}+4\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}\|^{2}+4\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|^{2}+4\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3})^{2}+4L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{6}

\displaystyle\quad+4M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+h_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\|^{2}+1)+4L_{f}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle:=M_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+M_{2}

(30)

where we use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in (
A.1
), (
A.1
) and
M_{1}=C\bigl(L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}+M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}+L_{f}^{2}\bigr),M_{2}=C\bigl(h_{n}^{2}L_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{6}+M^{\frac{2}{2+\delta}}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(h_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}+1)\bigr)
.

Combining all estimates, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq\left(1+C\eta_{n}^{2}+Ch_{n}^{2}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}-2\eta_{n}\rho\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|\min\left\{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|,\delta_{1}\right\}+C\eta_{n}^{2}.

This provides an analogous recursion form as in
(
Polyak and
Juditsky 1992
, proof of Theorem 2, part 1)
. Given the assumptions on
\eta_{n}
and
h_{n}
, and we have
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
converges almost surely to
\mathbf{0}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\|^{2}}{i^{\frac{1}{2}}}<\infty

(31)

almost surely, where (
31
) is analogous to the second last equation of
(
Polyak and
Juditsky 1992
, proof of Theorem 2, part 4)
.

Now assume Assumption
1
holds with
\delta_{1}=+\infty
, we have a stronger estimate,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rangle\geq F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}\geq\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}.

So combining all inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq\left[1-2\lambda\eta_{n}+M_{1}\eta_{n}^{2}+M_{3}h_{n}^{2}\right]\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+M_{4}\eta_{n}^{2},

(32)

where
M_{3},M_{4}
is defined by
M_{3}=CL_{f}^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}
,
M_{4}=C(\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{3}+M_{2})
. Following the proof of Theorem 1 of
Moulines and
Bach 2011
, we can apply the recursion and get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left[1-2\lambda\eta_{k}+M_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}+CM_{3}h_{k}^{2}\right]\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}+M_{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\left[1-2\lambda\eta_{i}+M_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}+M_{3}h_{k}^{2}\right]\eta_{k}^{2}.

We can then bound the first term on the RHS,

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left[1-2\lambda\eta_{k}+M_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}+M_{3}h_{k}^{2}\right]\leq\exp\left(-2\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\right)\exp\left(M_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{2}\right)\exp\left(M_{3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{k}^{2}\right),

as well as the second term on the RHS

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\left[1-2\lambda\eta_{i}+M_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}+M_{3}h_{k}^{2}\right]\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;\;

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\eta_{k}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{2}+\frac{\eta_{m}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{M_{1}}\exp\left(M_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\eta_{k}^{2}\right)\exp\left(M_{3}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}h_{k}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\right),

where we denote by
n_{0}=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N},1-2\lambda\eta_{k}+M_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}+M_{3}h_{k}^{2}\leq 1-\lambda\eta_{k}\}
and
m
is any integer in
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Choose
m=n/2
and bound
n_{0}
by
n
. Notice that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}^{2}
converge. So we can get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(CM_{1}\eta_{0}/(2\alpha-1)+CM_{3}/(2\beta-1)-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right)\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+M_{4}\left(\exp\left(-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right)+\frac{\eta_{0}n^{-\alpha}}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{M_{4}}{M_{1}}\exp\left(CM_{1}\eta_{0}/(2\alpha-1)+CM_{3}/(2\beta-1)-C\lambda\eta_{0}n^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\right).

Only the term
M_{4}\eta_{0}n^{-\alpha}/\lambda
decreases at the order of
O(n^{-\alpha})
while all the other terms decrease much faster.

Notice that all
C
’s in the above inequality are universal constants which do not depend on any parameters in the assumptions. This proves Remark
A.1
.

From now on, we will absorb all parameters (other than
n
) into
C
to make the asymptotic analysis more clear. By martingale convergence theorem,
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|
converges almost surely. Because its second moment converges to
\mathbf{0}
, it must converge to
\mathbf{0}
almost surely.

We now show that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\|^{2+\delta}\leq Cn^{-\alpha(2+\delta)/2}.

By same arguments as in (
24
), (
A.1
), (
A.1
), we can get
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq Ch_{n}^{2+\delta}
,
\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+Ch_{n}^{2+\delta}
,
\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left[\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\right]\leq C(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+1)
.

By similar arguments as in Lemma
B.3
, there exists constants
C
such that for any
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}
,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2+\delta}\leq\|\boldsymbol{a}\|^{2+\delta}+(2+\delta)\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle\|\boldsymbol{a}\|^{\delta}+C\|\boldsymbol{a}\|^{\delta}\|\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2}+C\|\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2+\delta}.

So we have the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+\eta_{n}(2+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1},-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\rangle\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{\delta}

\displaystyle\quad+C\eta_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C\eta_{n}^{2+\delta}\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}

\displaystyle\leq(1-(2+\delta)\lambda\eta_{n})\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+C\eta_{n}h_{n}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{1+\delta}

\displaystyle\quad+C\eta_{n}^{2}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+1)\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{\delta}+C\eta_{n}^{2+\delta}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+1).

If
0<\delta\leq 1
, by previous bound
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq Cn^{-\alpha}
, we can get
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{1+\delta}\leq Cn^{-\alpha(1+\delta)/2}
and
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{\delta}\leq Cn^{-\alpha\delta/2}
by Hölder’s inequality. So we can further get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq(1-Cn^{-\alpha}+Cn^{-2\alpha})\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2+\delta}+Cn^{-(2+\delta)\alpha/2},

which implies
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq Cn^{-(2+\delta)\alpha/2}
as in the above proof after (
32
).

Now the case for
0<\delta\leq 1
is proved. We can then use induction. If
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq Cn^{-(2+\delta)\alpha/2}
for all
\delta\leq n
, then we can use the same method to prove the same inequality holds for
\delta\in(n,n+1]
. Thus the inequality holds for all
\delta
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2

Proof.

By Assumption
2
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2+\delta}\leq M(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2+\delta}+d^{2+\delta}).

Therefore, the following holds for some constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}\leq C(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+d^{2}).

(33)

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\mathbf{0})\|^{2}\leq C.

(34)

From Assumption
3
, we can get the following estimate for the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}).

Using the above observation, we find that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})+\nabla F(\mathbf{0})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(s\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{2}f(s\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}(1+\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}f(s\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}).

(35)

Define the function
\Sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})
by

\displaystyle\Sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}):=\mathbb{E}(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}))(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}))^{\top}.

Then combining inequalities (
33
), (
34
), (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle\|\Sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})-S\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}))(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}))^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad-(\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\mathbf{0}))(\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\mathbf{0}))^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\|\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})-\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})+\nabla F(\mathbf{0})\|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})-\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})+\nabla F(\mathbf{0})\|\|\nabla f(\mathbf{0};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\nabla F(\mathbf{0})\|

\displaystyle\leq C(d+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|)\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|)+C\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|).

(36)

Notice that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\top}-(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})(\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\boldsymbol{v}(f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})-F(\boldsymbol{\theta}+h\boldsymbol{v})+F(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\bigg[\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}\left(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{1}\boldsymbol{v})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{1}\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\left(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{2}\boldsymbol{v})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{2}\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\,\mathrm{d}s_{2}\bigg|\bigg]\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{1}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{2}\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\,\mathrm{d}s_{2}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}.

We can use (
A.1
) and derive that

\displaystyle\quad\|\mathbb{E}\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\top}-(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}-\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(d+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|).

Now we have

\displaystyle\quad\|\mathbb{E}\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\top}-\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}-\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(d+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|).

(37)

By the same argument,

\displaystyle\quad\|\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})(\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\bigg[\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h}\left(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{1}\boldsymbol{v})\right)\left(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}+s_{2}\boldsymbol{v})\right)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\,\mathrm{d}s_{2}\bigg|\bigg]\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}+h^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}).

So we finally get

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})\widehat{g}_{h,\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\top}-\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\|\leq C\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{4}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(1+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)(d+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+h\|\boldsymbol{v}\|).

for some constant
C>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof.

We follow the proof in
Polyak and
Juditsky 1992
. The update step is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\eta_{n}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\eta_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n})

\displaystyle=(I_{d}-\eta_{n}H)\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}+\eta_{n}(H\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}).

We only need to prove the following three claims. First, the following term converges almost surely

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}\|H\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i-1}-\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i-1})+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\|<\infty.

(38)

Furthermore, if the bounded sequence
\{w_{i}^{n}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
satisfies
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{n}\|\to 0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|w_{i}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i})\|^{2}\to 0

(39)

in probability. When
t\rightarrow\infty
, the following convergence in probability,

A.2
Illustration of choices of directions
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}

We first note that
Q^{\tt{(G)}}\succ Q^{\tt{(S)}}
regardless of the dimension
d
and Gram matrix
S
. Intuitively, when the direction
\boldsymbol{v}
is generated by Gaussian
(G)
, it can be decomposed into two independent random variables: the radical part
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
and the spherical part
\boldsymbol{v}/\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
. The spherical part
\boldsymbol{v}/\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
follows the same distribution as the uniform distribution on the sphere with radius
d
(which is identical to
(S)
). The extra randomness in the radical part
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(d)
leads to a larger magnitude of
Q
compared to that of
(S)
. Therefore the
(AKW)
estimator with Gaussian directions
(G)
is always inferior to that with spherical directions
(S)
, asymptotically. However, for the other candidates, they are not directly comparable, and the optimal choice of
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
depends on the optimality criterion, and Gram matrix
S
.

As a simple illustration, we consider
S=\mathrm{diag}(1,r_{0})
for some
r_{0}>0
. We have

(S)

Spherical:
Q^{\tt{(S)}}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{r_{0}+3}{2},\frac{3r_{0}+1}{2}\right)
.

(I)

Uniform in a natural coordinate basis:
Q^{\tt{(I)}}=\mathrm{diag}(2,2r_{0})
.

(U)

Uniform in an arbitrary orthonormal basis
U
: when
U=(\cos\omega,\sin\omega;-\sin\omega,\cos\omega)
and
\omega=0
, we have
Q^{\tt{(U)}}=Q^{\tt{(I)}}=\mathrm{diag}(2,2r_{0})
; when
\omega=\pi/4
, we have
Q^{\tt{(U)}}=\mathrm{diag}(1+r_{0},1+r_{0})
.

(P)

Non-uniform in a natural coordinate basis:
\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{r_{0}}{1-p_{1}}\right),p_{1}\in(0,1)
.

From the above we can see that, the choices of the distribution of direction vectors
\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
depends on the optimality-criteria on comparing the covariance matrices. Specifically in the above example, if one seeks to minimize

•

the trace of covariance matrix, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\tt{(S)}})=\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\tt{(I)}})=\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\tt{(U)}})=2+2r_{0},\;\;\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\tt{(P)}})=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{r_{0}}{1-p_{1}},

and the optimal distribution that minimizes the trace depends on the value of
p_{1}
.

•

the determinant of covariance matrix, we have

\displaystyle\det(Q^{\tt{(S)}})=\frac{3r_{0}^{2}+10r_{0}+3}{4},

\displaystyle\det(Q^{\tt{(I)}})=4r_{0},

\displaystyle\det(Q^{\tt{(U)}})=\frac{-\cos(4\omega)(r_{0}-1)^{2}+r_{0}^{2}+6r_{0}+1}{2},\;\;

\displaystyle\det(Q^{\tt{(P)}})=\frac{r_{0}}{p_{1}(1-p_{1})}.

By a simple derivation, we have

\det(Q^{\tt{(S)}})\geq\det(Q^{\tt{(U)}})\geq\det(Q^{\tt{(I)}})
and
\det(Q^{\tt{(P)}})\geq\det(Q^{\tt{(I)}})
.

•

the operator norm of covariance matrix, i.e., the largest eigenvalue, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(S)}})=\frac{r_{0}+3}{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(I)}})=2,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(P)}})=\max\left\{\frac{1}{p_{1}},\frac{r_{0}}{1-p_{1}}\right\},\;\;

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(U)}})=r_{0}+1+(1-r_{0})\left|\cos(2\omega)\right|.

The smallest operator norm for
Q^{\tt{(P)}}
is given by
p_{1}=\frac{1}{1+r_{0}}
. When
r_{0}\leq 1
, and
0\leq\omega\leq\pi/6
, we have
\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(I)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(U)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(S)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(P)}})
. When
r_{0}\geq 1
, and
0\leq\omega\leq\pi/6
, we have
\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(P)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(S)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(U)}})\geq\lambda_{\max}(Q^{\tt{(I)}})
. For other choices of
\omega
, we can obtain a comparison analogously.

Proof of Theorem
3.7

Proof.

It is clear that
Q_{m}=S
for
m=d
. We need to compute the quantity

\displaystyle Q_{m}=\frac{d^{2}}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\bigg)S\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\bigg),

which can be simplifies to

Q_{m}=\frac{d^{2}}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\bigg)+\frac{d^{2}}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{i\neq j}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\top}\bigg).

By symmetry, it equals to

Q_{m}=\frac{d^{2}}{m}\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\top}+\frac{d^{2}(m-1)}{m}\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}^{\top}.

We know
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\top}S\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{\top}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}Q
and

. So we can solve for

and get

Therefore,

\displaystyle Q_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}Q+\frac{d(m-1)}{m(d-1)}(\frac{1}{d}Q-\mathrm{diag}S)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-m}{m(d-1)}Q+\frac{d(m-1)}{m(d-1)}S.\qed

Lemma B.3

(
Assouad 1975
)
.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\}
be a martingale difference sequence, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{X}_{n}|\boldsymbol{X}_{n-1}]=0
. For any
1\leq p\leq 2
and any norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|^{p}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\|^{p}\right].

Proof.

We would like to show that there exists a constant
C
(which depends on
d
and
p
) such that for any
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{p}+\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{p}\right)\leq\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{2}^{p}+C\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{p},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the 2-norm. To see this, in the one dimensional case, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(|1+x|^{p}+|1-x|^{p}\right)\leq 1+C|x|^{p}.

At
x=1
, the left hand side is differentiable and its first derivative is 0, so there exists a constant
C
such that the inequality holds in a neighborhood of
x=1
. At
x\to\pm\infty
, the inequality also holds with some constant
C
. So it is easy to find a constant
C
such that the inequality holds for all
x
. The proof for the
d
-dimensional case is the same.

Using the above inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}+2C\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\|_{2}^{p}-\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{p}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{p}\geq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\right\|_{2}^{p}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}.

So

\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}+2C\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\|_{2}^{p}.

By induction, we then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{p}\leq 2C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\|_{2}^{p}\right].

For any general norm, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\|X\|\leq\|X\|_{2}\leq C\|X\|.

So the same result holds for any norm.
∎

Chen

.

Lemma B.4
.

If a matrix
B=A+E
where
A
and
B
are invertible, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|B^{-1}-A^{-1}\right\|\leq\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\|E\|\frac{1}{1-\|A^{-1}E\|}.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle B^{-1}=(A+E)^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}-A^{-1}\left(A^{-1}+E^{-1}\right)^{-1}A^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}-A^{-1}E\left(A^{-1}E+I\right)^{-1}A^{-1}.

Therefore, the inversion error is,

\displaystyle\|B^{-1}-A^{-1}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|A^{-1}E\left(A^{-1}E+I\right)^{-1}A^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\|E\|\|(A^{-1}E+I)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\|E\|\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(A^{-1}E+I)}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\|E\|\frac{1}{1-\|A^{-1}E\|},

where we use Weyl’s inequality in the last inequality.

\delta_{1}=+\infty
and
\delta=2
. The Hessian estimator
\widehat{H}_{n}
in (
64
) converges in probability to the empirical Hessian matrix
H
. The stochastic Newton estimator
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
in (
63
) converges to
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
almost surely. The stochastic Newton estimator
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
has the following limiting distribution,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right)\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},H^{-1}QH^{-1}\right),

(65)

for the same
Q
as in Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem
B.5
states that the final iterate of the
(KW)
stochastic Newton method (
63
) entails the same asymptotic distribution as the averaged
(AKW)
estimator (
(AKW)
). In contrast to
(AKW)
, (
63
) leverages additional Hessian information to achieve the asymptotic normality and efficiency. Nevertheless, the numerical implementation of the
(KW)
stochastic Newton’s method requires to update a Hessian estimator
\widehat{H}_{n}
in all iterations, which demands significant additional computation unless such an estimator is yet computed and maintained along the procedure for other purposes.

Proof of Theorem
B.5

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
are defined at the beginning of the supplement. We can deduce

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\langle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}),-\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\right)\rangle,

\displaystyle\quad+C\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|-\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\right)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})-\frac{C}{n}\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|^{2}+\frac{Ch_{n}}{n}\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C}{n^{2}}(\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\|^{2}+1)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\frac{C}{n}+\frac{Ch_{n}}{n})F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})+\frac{C}{n^{2}}(F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})+1).

which implies that
F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})
converges almost surely to 0, so
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
converges almost surely to 0. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\rightarrow 0
almost surely by martingale convergence theorem
(
Robbins and
Monro 1951
)
. It also implies the convergence rate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2}\leq C\left(n^{-C_{1}/2}+n^{-1}\right).

(66)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n-1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\|^{2+\delta}\leq C\left(n^{-C_{1}/2}+n^{-(1+\delta)}\right).

(67)

Those results show that
\widehat{H}_{n}^{-1}
converges to
H^{-1}
by the arguments in the proof of Theorem
4.3
. Now we consider the limiting distribution.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}H^{-1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})-\frac{1}{n}\left(\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}-H^{-1}\right)\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}H^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\left(\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}-H^{-1}\right)\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})+\frac{1}{n}\widehat{H}_{n-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1})-H\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}
. By induction, we can find that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k+1}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k+1}\right)-\frac{1}{n}H^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k+1}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}-H^{-1}\right)\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}).

The last three terms in the RHS above all converge to zero. We only need to show that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}
converges to a normal distribution. Consider

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}^{\top}\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\right]=\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}^{\top}\right]\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1},

recall that in (
44
) we have shown that
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}^{\top}\right]
converges almost surely to
Q
. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}^{\top}\widehat{H}_{k}^{-1}\right]
converges in probability to
H^{-1}QH^{-1}
.

Finally, by martingale central limit theorem
(
Duflo 1997
, Theorem 2.1.9)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{H}_{k-1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}\left(0,H^{-1}QH^{-1}\right).

∎
