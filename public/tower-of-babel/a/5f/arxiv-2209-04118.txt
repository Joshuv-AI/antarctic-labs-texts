1.
Introduction

A fundamental task in understanding functional inequalities, arise in the calculus of variations, geometry, etc, is to study the best constants, the classification of extremal functions, as well as their qualitative properties for parameters in the full region, since such functional inequalities are crucial in understanding nonlinear partial differential equations (Nonlinear PDEs for short) by virtue of the complete knowledge of the best constants, extremal functions, and qualitative properties. The most well-studied functional inequality in the community of Nonlinear PDEs is the Sobolev inequality, whose classical one with exponent
2
states that for any
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
d\geq 3
, there holds

\displaystyle S\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}dx\bigg)^{\frac{d-2}{d}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx,

(1.1)

where
S>0
is a constant which is only dependent of the dimension and
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the classical Sobolev space given by

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{u\in L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid|\nabla u|\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

It has been proved in
[
2
,
55
]
that

\displaystyle S=\pi d(d-2)\bigg(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(d)}\bigg)^{\frac{2}{d}}

is optimal and the extremal functions, which are called the Aubin-Talenti bubbles in the literature, are given by

\displaystyle U_{\lambda,z,c}(x)=c\bigg(\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}|x-z|^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

where
\lambda>0
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Once a functional inequality is well understood for its best constant and extremal functions, it is natural to concern its stability, which is growingly interested in recent years by its important applications in understanding many Nonlinear PDEs, such as the fast diffusion equation, the Keller-Segel equation and so on. The basic question one wants to address in this aspect is the following (cf.
[
30
]
):

(Q)

Suppose we are given a functional inequality for which minimizers are known.
Can we prove, in some quantitative way, that if a function “almost attains the
equality” then it is close (in some suitable sense) to one of the minimizers?

Such study was first raised by Brezis and Lieb in
[
8
]
for the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) as an open question, which was settled by Bianchi and Egnell in
[
6
]
by proving that

\displaystyle dist_{H^{1}}^{2}(u,\mathcal{Z})\lesssim\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}-S\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
is the usual norm in the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\{U_{\lambda,z,c}\mid(\lambda,z,c)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\}.

Unlike Bianchi and Egnell’s study (
[
6
]
) in the functional inequality setting, in recent years, Figalli et al. initiated the study on the stability of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) in the critical point setting, that is, studying the stability of the Euler-Lagrange equation of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
). The study on the stability of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) in the critical point setting is more challenging since the Euler-Lagrange equation of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) has sign-changing solutions. Moreover, the “almost” solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equation of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) may decompose into several parts at infinity (cf.
[
54
]
). By setting the study in a suitable way, Figalli et al. proved that

(1)

(Ciraolo-Figalli-Maggi
[
16
]
) Let
d\geq 3
and
u\in D^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be positive such that
\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}S^{\frac{d}{2}}
and
\|\Delta u+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\|_{H^{-1}}\leq\delta
for some
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Then,

\displaystyle dist_{D^{1,2}}(u,\mathcal{M}_{0})\lesssim\|\Delta u+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\|_{H^{-1}},

where
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\{(U[z,\lambda]\mid z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda>0\}.

(2)

(Figalli-Glaudo
[
31
]
) Let
u\in D^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be nonnegative such that

\displaystyle(\nu-\frac{1}{2})S^{\frac{d}{2}}<\|u\|_{D^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}<(\nu+\frac{1}{2})S^{\frac{d}{2}}

and
\|\Delta u+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\|_{H^{-1}}\leq\delta
for some
\delta>0
sufficiently small.
Then, for
3\leq d\leq 5
,

\displaystyle dist_{D^{1,2}}(u,\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\nu})\lesssim\|\Delta u+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\|_{H^{-1}}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\nu}=\{(U[z_{1},\lambda_{1}],U[z_{2},\lambda_{2}],\cdots,U[z_{\nu},\lambda_{\nu}])\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda_{i}>0\}.

All the above results are optimal in the sense that the orders of the right hand sides in the above estimates are sharp, while the optimal stability of the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) in the critical point setting for the case
N\geq 6
was left in
[
31
]
as an open problem which was solved by Deng, Sun and Wei in
[
21
]
, very recently, by proving the following optimal stability:

\displaystyle dist_{D^{1,2}}(u,\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\nu})\lesssim\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|\Delta u+|u|u\|_{H^{-1}}|\ln(\|\Delta u+|u|u\|_{H^{-1}})|^{\frac{1}{2}},\ \ d=6;\\
&\|\Delta u+|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\|_{H^{-1}}^{\frac{d+2}{2(d-2)}},\ \ d\geq 7.\end{aligned}\right.

It is worth pointing out that, besides the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
), the Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}\log|u|dx\leq\frac{d}{4}\log\bigg(\frac{2}{\pi de}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\bigg)

(1.2)

where
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
d\geq 2
and
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
, is also well understood for the best constants and the classification of extremal functions. It is well known (cf.
[
20
,
60
]
) that this inequality is optimal and extremal functions of (
1.2
) are precisely the
Gaussians

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\sigma,z}(x)=(\pi\sigma)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{*}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2}}(x-z)\bigg)\quad\text{with}\quad\mathfrak{g}_{*}(x)=e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}.

(1.3)

Moreover, it is equivalent to the Gross logarithmic inequality (cf.
[
38
]
) with respect to Gaussian weight

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g|^{2}\log|g|d\mu\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla g|^{2}d\mu\quad\text{with}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g|^{2}d\mu=1\text{ and }d\mu=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}.

The Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality has another two different forms:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\log|v|^{2}dx\leq\frac{a^{2}}{\pi}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\bigg(\log\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}-d(1+\log a)\bigg)\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

(1.4)

for all
a>0
and any function
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w|^{2}\log|w|^{2}dx+\frac{d}{2}(1+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi))\leq\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

(1.5)

for any function
w\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
d\geq 2
, with
\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
. (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) are equivalent by the relation
w(x)=v(\frac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi}}x)
and they are established in
[
44
, Theorem 8.14]
and
[
59
, Theorem 1.2]
, respectively. However, (
1.2
) is optimal since it can be obtained by minimizing the right hand side of (
1.4
) for
a>0
(cf.
[
26
]
).

The stability of the Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
1.2
) in the functional inequality setting has also been widely studied, cf.
[
7
,
10
,
11
,
26
,
27
,
29
,
41
,
42
,
60
]
and the references therein. It is worth pointing out that most of these results are devoted to more general version of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality in the probability setting which contains the Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
1.2
) as a special case for the Gaussians measure
d\mu=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}
.
Thus, it is natural to consider the stability of the Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
1.2
) in the critical point setting, as that for the classical Sobolev inequality (
1.1
). Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=\frac{d}{4}\log\bigg(\frac{2}{\pi de}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\bigg)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}\log|u|dx.

Then critical points of
\mathcal{E}(u)
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the finite energy on the smooth manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1\}

satisfy
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}|\log|u|^{2}|dx<+\infty
and the following Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle-(\frac{d}{2\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}})\Delta u-(1+2\sigma_{u})u=2u\log|u|\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.6)

where
\sigma_{u}
is a part of unknown and appears in (
1.6
) as the Lagrange multiplier. Thus, (
1.6
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation of (
1.2
).
Let

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}u(\lambda x)\quad\text{where }\lambda=\bigg(\frac{d}{2\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

then by (
1.6
) and a direct calculation,
u_{\lambda}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\lambda}+(d\log\lambda-1-2\sigma_{u})u_{\lambda}=2u_{\lambda}\log|u_{\lambda}|\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.7)

Let

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}^{*}(x)=\alpha_{u}u_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{where }\alpha_{u}=e^{\frac{d}{2}\log\lambda-\sigma_{u}-1},

then by (
1.7
) and a direct calculation,
u_{\lambda}^{*}
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle-\Delta u+u=2u\log|u|\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.8)

Thus, the logarithmic Schrödinger equation (
1.8
) can be seen as the Euler-Lagrange equation of the Euclidean logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
1.2
).

It has been proved in
[
19
,
56
]
that the Gaussian

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=e^{\frac{1+d}{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{*}

(1.9)

is the unique positive solution of the logarithmic Schrödinger equation (
1.8
) which satisfies
u(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to+\infty
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{*}
is given by (
1.3
). We remark that any solution of (
1.8
) satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}|\log|u|^{2}|dx<+\infty
must exponentially decay to zero as
|x|\to+\infty
by the standard applications of the maximum principle.
Moreover, it has been proved in
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
(see also
[
39
, Theorem 7.5]
) that
\mathfrak{g}
is nondegenerate in the sense that
Ker(\mathcal{L})=span\{\partial_{x_{j}}\mathfrak{g}\}
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\Delta+|x|^{2}-(d+2)

(1.10)

is the linearized operator of (
1.8
) at
\mathfrak{g}
. Note that (
1.8
) is invariant under translations, thus, the smooth manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\{\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-z)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

contains all positive solutions of (
1.8
) satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}|\log|u|^{2}|dx<+\infty
.

Let
\{u_{n}\}
,
u_{n}\geq 0
for all
n
, be bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and almost solves (
1.8
), that is,
\|-\Delta u_{n}+u_{n}-2u_{n}\log|u_{n}|\|_{H^{-1}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then it is easy to see that
\{|u_{n}|^{2}|\log|u_{n}|^{2}|\}
is bounded in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Thanks to the Brezis-Lieb lemma
[
53
, Lemma 3.1]
and the positivity of the energy of the unique positive solution of (
1.8
) (cf.
[
53
, Lemma 3.3]
), it is standard (cf.
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
) to prove the following Struwe’s decomposition of
\{u_{n}\}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

There exists
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{1,n},y_{2,n},\cdots y_{\nu,n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-y_{i,n})\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

By Proposition
1.1
, for nonnegative functions
u_{n}
with uniform
H^{1}
-bound, we know that there exists
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\{u_{n}\}
will be close to the manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\nu}=\{(\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-z_{1}),\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-z_{2}),\cdots,\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-z_{\nu}))\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

in the
H^{1}
-topology. Thus, it is natural to ask, if
u\geq 0
satisfy

\displaystyle(\nu-\frac{1}{2})c_{0}<\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}<(\nu+\frac{1}{2})c_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\|-\Delta u+u-2u\log|u|\|_{H^{-1}}\leq\delta,

where
c_{0}=\|\mathfrak{g}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
,
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
sufficiently small, can we obtain a quantitative version of Proposition
1.1
by optimally controlling
dist_{H^{1}}(u,\mathcal{M}^{\nu})
by
\|-\Delta u+u-2u\log|u|\|_{H^{-1}}
? The purpose of this paper is to give a positive answer to this natural question and our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u\geq 0
satisfy

\displaystyle(\nu-\frac{1}{2})c_{0}<\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}<(\nu+\frac{1}{2})c_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\|-\Delta u+u-2u\log|u|\|_{H^{-1}}\leq\delta,

where
c_{0}=\|\mathfrak{g}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
,
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Then

\displaystyle\text{dist}_{H^{1}}(u,\mathcal{M}^{\nu})\lesssim\|-\Delta u+u-2u\log|u|\|_{H^{-1}},

(1.11)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\nu}=\{(\mathfrak{g}_{1,0}(\cdot-z_{1}),\mathfrak{g}_{1,0}(\cdot-z_{2}),\cdots,\mathfrak{g}_{1,0}(\cdot-z_{\nu}))\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

Moreover, (
1.1
) is optimal in the sense that the order of the right hand side is sharp.

Remark 1.1
.

The main ideas in proving Theorem
1.1
is similar to that of
[
21
,
61
]
, that is, we choose special
y_{1,\delta},y_{2,\delta},\cdots y_{\nu,\delta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and decompose
u-\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}(\cdot-y_{i,\delta})
into two parts, where the first part is very regular which can be well estimated and the second part is much smaller than the first part. However, the logarithmic nonlinearity is very different from the power-type one dealt with in
[
21
,
61
]
, thus, we need to careful estimate it to make sure that the strategy in
[
21
,
61
]
work for (
1.8
).

\displaystyle E=2(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f})\log(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f})-2\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f}\log\mathfrak{g}_{i,f}

is the error and

\displaystyle N(\rho_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f}+\rho_{f})\log(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f}+\rho_{f})-2(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f})\log(\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\mathfrak{g}_{i,f})

3.
Proof of (

)

Let

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\min_{j\not=i}\eta_{i,j}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta=\min_{i}\eta_{i},

(3.1)

where
\eta_{i,j}=|y_{j,f}-y_{i,f}|
.
Then by (

) and the uniqueness of
