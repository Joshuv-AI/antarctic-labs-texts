Efficient solution of sequences of parametrized Lyapunov equations with applications
Davide Palitta, Zoran Tomljanović, Ivica Nakić, Jens Saak

Abstract
Sequences of parametrized Lyapunov equations can be encountered in many application settings. Moreover, solutions of such equations are often intermediate steps of an overall procedure whose main goal is the computation of trace ( E ​ X ) \trace(EX) where X X denotes the solution of a Lyapunov equation and E E is a given matrix. We are interested in addressing problems where the parameter dependency of the coefficient matrix is encoded as a low-rank modification to a seed , fixed matrix. We propose two novel numerical procedures that fully exploit such a common structure. The first one builds upon the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury (SMW) formula and recycling Krylov techniques, and it is well-suited for small dimensional problems as it makes use of dense numerical linear algebra tools. The second algorithm can instead address large-scale problems by relying on state-of-the-art projection techniques based on the extended Krylov subspace. We test the new algorithms on several problems arising in the study of damped vibrational systems and the analyses of output synchronization problems for multi-agent systems. Our results show that the algorithms we propose are superior to state-of-the-art techniques as they are able to remarkably speed up the computation of accurate solutions.

Introduction and setting

The main goal of this study is the design of efficient numerical procedures for solving sequences of parameter-dependent Lyapunov equations of the form

A(\mathsf{v})X+X{A(\mathsf{v})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=-Q,

(1)

where
A(\mathsf{v})
has the form

A(\mathsf{v})=A_{0}-B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(2)

Here
A_{0},Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
B_{l},B_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times k}
are fixed matrices and the matrix
D(\mathsf{v})=\diag{(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})}
contains parameters
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, for
i=1\ldots,k
, encoded in the parameter vector

\mathsf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in{({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\left\{0\right\})}^{k}

Our algorithms can handle complex data by applying straightforward modifications.
. Note that, without loss of generality, we assume that the parameters in
\mathsf{v}

The number of parameters
k
is supposed to be very small,
k=\mathcal{O}(1)

Structured equations of the form (
1

,
b_{r}^{i}

i=1,\ldots,k
, be the columns of the matrices

and

, respectively. Then
A(\mathsf{v})=A_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}b_{l}^{i}{(b_{r}^{i})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=:A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}A_{i}
, and hence matrices of the form (
2
) can describe a class of parameter-dependent matrices with an affine dependency on the parameters. Lyapunov equations with such coefficient matrices naturally arise in the study of vibrational systems
[
57
]
, parametric model reduction
[
50
]
, in the design of low gain feedback
[
66
]
, and the analysis of multi-agent systems
[
53
,
24
]
.
The applications we are going to study more closely are optimal damping of vibrational systems and output-synchronization problems for multi-agent systems.

We are especially interested in devising efficient procedures for calculating
\trace(EX(\mathsf{v}))
, for some matrix
E
, for many different parameter vectors
\mathsf{v}
.
Indeed,
\trace(EX(\mathsf{v}))
often corresponds to the target value we are interested in computing, with calculating
X(\mathsf{v})
only being an intermediate step. For instance this is the case when computing the
H_{2}
-norm of a linear system. An efficient computation of
\trace(EX(\mathsf{v}))
is crucial for obtaining competitive numerical schemes in terms of both computational performance and memory requirements for the applications mentioned above. Our method also covers efficient calculation of quantities of the form
f(X(\mathsf{v}))
, for a given function
f
that is assumed to be
additive
, i.e.
f(X+Y)=f(X)+f(Y)
. To simplify the notation, in the sequel we will denote
\trace(EX)
by
f(X)
.

Naturally, one can solve (
1
) from scratch for any
\mathsf{v}
. For instance, if the problem dimension allows, one can apply the Bartels-Stewart algorithm
[
1
]
to each instance of (
1
). However, this naive procedure does not exploit the attractive structure of (
1
) and requires the computation of the Schur decomposition of
A(\mathsf{v})
any time
\mathsf{v}
changes. This approach is not affordable in terms of computational cost if
\mathsf{v}
varies a lot and
f(X(\mathsf{v}))
needs to be evaluated many times.
More sophisticated schemes for (
1
) can be found in the literature. For instance, the algorithms presented in
[
57
,
31
]
do exploit the structure of
A(\mathsf{v})
in (
2
). Even though these schemes are more performing than a naive application of the Bartels-Stewart algorithm, they are not designed to capitalize on a possible slow variation in
\mathsf{v}
as it often happens within, e.g., an optimization routine. Moreover, they can be applied to small dimensional problems only.
Various approaches for solving the parameter-dependent Lyapunov equations can be
found in the literature, including those based on the conjugate gradient
method
[
29
]
, low-rank updates
[
28
]
,
interpolation on manifolds
[
23
]
, and low-rank reduced basis
method
[
51
,
46
]
.
On the other hand, they are not well-suited for solving sequences of numerous parameter-dependent equations.

Leveraging the structure of the coefficient matrix
A(\mathsf{v})
—
A(\mathsf{v})
is an affine function in
\mathsf{v}
— we propose two different schemes which are able to fully separate the parameter-independent calculations from the
\mathsf{v}
-dependent computations. The former will be performed once and for all in an
offline
step, whereas the latter operations take place
online
, namely every time
\mathsf{v}
changes.

The first procedure we suggest in this paper builds upon the work in
[
57
,
31
]
, which we enhance with a recycling Krylov technique. This routine makes use of dense linear algebra tools so that it is well suited for small dimensional problems, i.e., for moderate values of
n
. The second routine addresses the large-scale case employing projection methods for linear matrix equations.

Both our algorithms take inspiration from the low-rank update scheme presented in
[
28
]
, for which the following assumption is needed.

Assumption 1
.

The solution
X_{0}
to the Lyapunov equation
A_{0}X+XA_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=-Q
can be computed efficiently.

2
The novel solution methods

Equation (
1
) can be written as

(A_{0}-B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})X(\mathsf{v})+X(\mathsf{v}){(A_{0}-B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=-Q.

By exploiting the low rank of
B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and using the low-rank update approach presented in
[
28
]
, we can also rewrite
X
as the sum of a fixed matrix and a low-rank update term, i.e.,

X(\mathsf{v})=X_{0}+X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v}),

(3)

where
X_{0}
and
X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v})
are as follows. The matrix
X_{0}
is independent of the parameter vector
\mathsf{v}
, and it solves the Lyapunov equation

A_{0}X_{0}+X_{0}A_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=-Q,

(4)

whereas
X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v})
is such that

A(\mathsf{v})X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v})+X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v}){A(\mathsf{v})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X_{0}+X_{0}B_{r}D(\mathsf{v})B_{l}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(5)

Assumption
1
implies that
X_{0}
can be efficiently calculated.

We notice that the right-hand side in (
5
) can be written as

B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X_{0}+X_{0}B_{r}D(\mathsf{v})B_{l}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=[X_{0}B_{r},B_{l}]\begin{bmatrix}0&D(\mathsf{v})\\
D(\mathsf{v})&0\\
\end{bmatrix}{[X_{0}B_{r},B_{l}]}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=P\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)P^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

(6)

where
P=[X_{0}B_{r},B_{l}]
has rank
2k
, and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}
.
This formulation of the right-hand side will be crucial for the projection-based method we design for large-scale problems in Section
2.2
.

In the next sections we show how to efficiently compute
X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v})
. In particular, it turns out that many of the required operations do not depend on
\mathsf{v}
so that they can be carried out in the offline stage.
Therefore, we split the solution process into offline and online phases, where only the operations that actually depend on
\mathsf{v}
are performed online.

2.1
Small-scale setting: SMW and recycling Krylov methods

In this section we address the numerical solution of the Lyapunov equation (
5
) assuming the problem dimension
n
to be small, namely it is such that dense linear algebra operations costing up to
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
flops are feasible.

Even though there are many efficient methods for solving Lyapunov equations of
moderate dimensions, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
and references therein, we want to efficiently solve (
5
) for a sequence of parameter vectors
\mathsf{v}
.

The strategy proposed in this section builds upon the work in
[
57
,
31
]
. We extend their approach to the more general class of problems of the form (
1
), and we allow for more than one parameter,
i.e.,
k
can be larger than one
. The scheme we are going to present relies on the computation of the full eigenvalue decomposition of
A_{0}
, namely
A_{0}=Q_{0}\Lambda_{0}Q_{0}^{-1}
with
Q_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
and
\Lambda_{0}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
,
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Considering the notation introduced in (
6
), the Kronecker form of (
5
) reads as follows

\left(I\otimes A_{0}+A_{0}\otimes I-I\otimes\left(B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right)-\left(B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right)\otimes I\right)\vecop(X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v}))=\left(P\otimes P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right).

If
A_{0}=Q_{0}\Lambda_{0}Q_{0}^{-1}
,
\Lambda_{0}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
,
we can write

(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0})\left({\mathbf{L}}_{0}-I\otimes\left(Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}\right)-\left(Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}\right)\otimes I\right)(Q_{0}^{-1}\otimes Q_{0}^{-1})\vecop(X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v}))=\\
\left(P\otimes P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right),

where
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}:=I\otimes\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}\otimes I
, so that we obtain

\left({\mathbf{L}}_{0}-\left[Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}\otimes I,I\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}\right]\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v}){\left[Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\otimes I,I\otimes Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\right]}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right)\vecop(Q_{0}^{-1}X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v})Q_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})=\\
\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right),

with
\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})=\begin{bmatrix}D(\mathsf{v})\otimes I&\\
&I\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\\
\end{bmatrix}
.

By denoting
\mathbf{M}:=\left[Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}\otimes I,\,I\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}\right]
, and
\mathbf{N}:=\left[Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\otimes I,\,I\otimes Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\right]
,

the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
) implies that

\displaystyle\vecop(X_{\delta}(\mathsf{v}))=

\displaystyle(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0})\bigg({\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)+

\displaystyle{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}{\left({\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\right)}^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)\bigg).

(7)

The use of the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula in the matrix setting is not new; see, e.g.,
[
21
,
32
]
,
[
12
, Section 3]
,
[
34
, Section 5]
,
[
4
,
45
]
. However, applying the general-purpose schemes presented in these papers to our parameter-dependent framework would not lead to an efficient solution process. Indeed, this would be equivalent to explicitly computing (
2.1
) every time
\mathsf{v}
changes. Our
main goal here is to divide the operations that depend on
\mathsf{v}
from those that do not, as much as possible, such that the
\mathsf{v}
-independent calculations can be performed offline,
only once. In the interest of the reader, we thus spell out the details of our procedure, in spite of some similarities with prior work.

We first focus on the term
(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0})\left({\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)\right)
.
The computation of

z(\mathsf{v})=(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0})\left({\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)\right),

is equivalent to solving the Lyapunov equation

\Lambda_{0}\widetilde{Z}(\mathsf{v})+\widetilde{Z}(\mathsf{v})\Lambda_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=Q_{0}^{-1}P\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)P^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

where
z(\mathsf{v})=\vecop(Z(\mathsf{v}))
, and
Z(\mathsf{v}):=Q_{0}\widetilde{Z}(\mathsf{v})Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
.
Since
\Lambda_{0}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, by defining the Cauchy matrix
{\mathcal{L}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is given by
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}=1/(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})
, we can write

Z(\mathsf{v})=Q_{0}\left({\mathcal{L}}\mathop{\circ}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)P^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right)\right)Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(8)

The matrix
\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})
can be written as

\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}(e_{k+i}e_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+e_{i}e_{k+i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}),

and by plugging this expression into (
8
), we get

Z(\mathsf{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}Z_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}Q_{0}\widetilde{Z}_{i}Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},\quad\widetilde{Z}_{i}:={\mathcal{L}}\mathop{\circ}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\left(e_{k+i}e_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+e_{i}e_{k+i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right)P^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right).

(9)

We can thus compute the matrices
Z_{i}
before any change in the parameters
\mathsf{v}
takes place. Whenever we need
Z(\mathsf{v})
, we just compute the linear combination in (
9
).

The second term in (
2.1
) can be written as

\left(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0}\right){\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}{\left({\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\right)}^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\left(Q_{0}^{-1}P\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}P\right)\vecop\left(\mathcal{I}_{2}\otimes D(\mathsf{v})\right)=\\
\left(Q_{0}\otimes Q_{0}\right){\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}{\left({\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\right)}^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\vecop\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i}\right)\right).

(10)

Recalling that
\mathbf{N}:=\left[Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\otimes I,I\otimes Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\right]
, we have

\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\vecop\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\vecop\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i}Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\right)\\
\vecop\left(B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}\widetilde{Z}_{i}\right)\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{so that}\quad\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\vecop\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i}\right)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\vecop\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i}Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\right)\\
\vecop\left(B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q_{0}\widetilde{Z}_{i}\right)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We now focus on the solution of the
2nk\times 2nk
linear system with
{\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
. We first show how to efficiently assemble the coefficient matrix itself.

While
{\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I&\\
&I\otimes{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

is a diagonal matrix, the structure of
\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
is more involved.
A first study of its structure can be found in
[
57
]
. Unlike what is done in
[
57
]
, here we exploit the diagonal pattern of
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}
, which leads to a useful representation of the blocks of
\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
when the latter is viewed as a
2\times 2
block matrix. Such a block structure will help us to design efficient preconditioners for the solution of the linear system in (
10
) as well.

Let
\mathbf{M}_{1}=Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}\otimes I
,
\mathbf{M}_{2}=I\otimes Q_{0}^{-1}B_{l}
,
\mathbf{N}_{1}=Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}\otimes I
, and
\mathbf{N}_{2}=I\otimes Q_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}B_{r}
. Then we can write

\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}&\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\\
\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}&\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We first focus on the
(1,1)
-block, namely
\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{nk\times nk}
.
By recalling that the
h
-th basis vector of
{\mathbb{C}}^{nk}
can be written as

, with
e_{s}
and
e_{t}
being the canonical vectors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
, respectively, and
h=(t-1)n+s
, we can write the

(i,j)
-th entry of
\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{nk\times nk}
as

\displaystyle e_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}e_{j}=

\displaystyle{\vecop(e_{s}e_{t}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\vecop(e_{q}e_{p}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}),

where
i=(t-1)n+s
and
j=(p-1)n+q
. Since
\mathbf{N}_{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{1}

The convergence of GCRO-DR is driven by the spectral properties of

However, the latter ones are extremely tricky to identify in general. Only by allowing further assumptions on

GCRO-DR allows for preconditioning.
In particular, thanks to the pattern of
{\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
we are able to design efficient preconditioning operators, which can be cheaply tuned to address the variation in the parameters.

We have

{\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I-\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}&\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\\
\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}&I\otimes{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix},

and we want to maintain the
(2\times 2)
-block structure in the preconditioner as well.

In
{\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
, only the diagonal blocks
{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I-\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}
and

I\otimes{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
depend on the current parameters. Moreover, both
{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I
and
I\otimes{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}
are diagonal matrices so that the sparsity pattern of
\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}
and
\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
is preserved. In particular,
{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I-\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}
is still a
k\times k

I\otimes{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
is block-diagonal with
k\times k
blocks on the diagonal.
By exploiting such a significant sparsity pattern, we are thus able to
efficiently solve linear systems
with
{D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\otimes I-\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}

\mathsf{v}

\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}>0

\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
nor
\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}
depend on
\mathsf{v}

We denote the results of the TSVD by
\mathcal{N}_{1}{\mathcal{M}_{2}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

\mathcal{N}_{2}{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\approx\mathbf{N}_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}

{\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\

2.2
Large-scale setting: a projection framework

In this section, we address the numerical solution of problems of large dimensions. In particular, due to a too large value of
n
, we assume that many of the operations described in the previous section, e.g., the computation of the eigenvalue decomposition of
A_{0}
, are not affordable. On the other hand, we assume
that it is possible, and efficient, to solve linear systems with
A_{0}
.

Assumption 2
.

Solving linear systems with
A_{0}
and possibly multiple right-hand sides is possible and efficient
.

In all our numerical results, we computed (and stored) the LU factors of
A_{0}
.

Taking inspiration from well-established projection methods for large-scale Lyapunov equations, we design a novel scheme for (
5
). As before, the main goal is to reuse the information we already have at hand, as much as possible, every time (
5
) has to be solved for a new
\mathsf{v}
.

We employ the extended Krylov subspace method presented in
[
48
]

and further studied in
[
25
]
.
In this section we illustrate how to fully exploit its properties for our purposes.

In the case of a parameter independent Lyapunov equation of the form (
4
) with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}=PP^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
,
the extended Krylov subspace

\displaystyle\textbf{EK}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0},P)

\displaystyle=\text{Range}([P,A_{0}^{-1}P,A_{0}P,A_{0}^{-2}P,\ldots,A_{0}^{m-1}P,A_{0}^{-m}P])

(17)

\displaystyle=\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0},P)+\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0}^{-1},A_{0}^{-1}P),

can be employed as approximation space. In (
17
),
\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0},P)
denotes the standard, polynomial (block) Krylov subspace generated by
A_{0}
and
P
, namely
\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0},P)=\text{Range}([P,A_{0}P,\ldots,A_{0}^{m-1}P])
.

If
V_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times 2mk}
,
k=\text{rank}(P)
, has orthonormal columns
3
3

3

. The square matrix

by solving a

With

at hand, an accuracy measure, either the residual norm or the backward error, is computed to assess the quality of the current solution.
Whenever this value is sufficiently small, we stop the process, otherwise the space is expanded.

A very similar scheme could also be employed for (

We show that only one extended Krylov subspace can be constructed and employed for solving all the necessary Lyapunov equations (

As reported in (
6

[

The argument to justify the use of
A_{0}

A(\mathsf{v})P=(A_{0}-B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})P=A_{0}P-B_{l}(D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}P),

\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}({A(\mathsf{v})}^{-1},{A(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}P)\subseteq\textbf{K}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0}^{-1},A_{0}^{-1}P)

\textbf{EK}_{m}^{\square}(A(\mathsf{v}),P)\subseteq\textbf{EK}_{m}^{\square}(A_{0},P)

Thanks to the SMW formula, we have

{A(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}P={(A_{0}-B_{l}D(\mathsf{v})B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})}^{-1}P=A_{0}^{-1}P+A_{0}^{-1}B_{l}{({D(\mathsf{v})}^{-1}-B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}A_{0}^{-1}B_{l})}^{-1}B_{r}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}A_{0}^{-1}P,

Proposition
2.1

3
Applications

In this section we illustrate two important problem settings where (

\mathsf{v}

3.1
Damped vibrational systems

We consider linear vibrational systems described by

\begin{gathered}M\ddot{x}+C(\mathsf{v})\dot{x}+Kx=0,\\
x(0)=x_{0},\quad\dot{x}(0)=\dot{x}_{0},\end{gathered}

(20)

where
M
denotes the mass matrix,
C(\mathsf{v})
denotes the parameter dependent damping matrix,
K
denotes the stiffness matrix, and
x_{0}
and
\dot{x}_{0}
are initial data. We assume that
M
and
K
are real, symmetric positive definite matrices of order
m
. The damping matrix is defined as
C(\mathsf{v})=C_{\mathrm{i}nt}+C_{\mathrm{e}xt}(\mathsf{v})
.

We assume that

C_{\mathrm{e}xt}(\mathsf{v})=BD(\mathsf{v})B^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

(21)

where the matrix
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times k}
describes the dampers’ geometry and the matrix
D(\mathsf{v})=\diag{(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})}
contains the damping viscosities
v_{i}>0
, for
i=1\ldots,k
. The viscosities will be encoded in the parameter vector

\mathsf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}_{>0}
.

The internal damping
C_{\mathrm{i}nt}
can be modeled in different ways. It is usually modeled as a Rayleigh damping matrix, which means that

C_{\mathrm{i}nt}=\alpha M+\beta K,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\alpha,\beta\geqslant 0,\;\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}>0,

(22)

or as a small multiple of the critical damping, that is,

C_{\mathrm{i}nt}=\alpha M^{1/2}\sqrt{M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}}M^{1/2},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha>0.

(23)

See, e.g.,
[
30
,
62
,
7
]
for further details.

Vibrations arise in a wide range of systems, such as mechanical, electrical or civil engineering structures. Vibrations are a mostly unwanted phenomenon in vibrational structures, since they can lead to many undesired effects such as creation of noise, oscillatory loads or waste of energy that may produce damaging effects on the considered structures. Therefore, in order to attenuate or minimize unwanted vibrations, an important problem is to determine the damping matrix in such a way that the vibrations of the system are as small as possible. This is usually achieved through optimization of the external damping matrix
C_{\mathrm{e}xt}(\mathsf{v})
. Within this framework, we will focus on the optimization of the damping parameter vector
\mathsf{v}
defining
C_{\mathrm{e}xt}(\mathsf{v})
as in (
21
). While damping optimization is a widely studied topic, there are still many challenging tasks that require efficient approaches. There is a vast literature in this field of research. For further details, we list only a few references that address the minimization of dangerous vibrations with different applications
[
2
,
17
,
62
,
54
,
37
,
14
,
68
]
.

The problem of vibrations minimization requires a proper optimization criterion. A whole class of criteria are based on eigenvalues; see, e.g.
[
15
,
36
,
64
,
19
,
11
,
55
]
. Another important criterion is based on the total average energy of the system. This has been intensely considered in the last two decades; see, e.g.,
[
62
,
9
,
59
,
58
,
10
,
38
]
.
Since our approach is also based on the total average energy, in the following we provide more details and set the stage for the application of our framework.

Several approaches trying to fully exploit the structure and accelerate the optimization process have been proposed in the literature about damping systems. In more details, in
[
60
,
61
,
56
]
the authors considered approaches that allowed derivation of explicit formulas for the total average energy, but they are adequate only for certain case studies. Furthermore, in
[
5
,
6
]
the authors employed dimension reduction techniques in order to obtain efficient approaches for the calculation of the total average energy. However, these approaches require specific system configurations and they cannot be applied efficiently for general system matrices.

If we write (
20
) as a first-order ODE in phase space
\dot{y}=A(\mathsf{v})y
, the solution is given by
y(t)=\mathrm{e}^{A(\mathsf{v})t}y_{0}
, where
y_{0}
denotes the vector of initial data. The average total energy of the system is given by

\int_{\lVert y_{0}\rVert=1}\int_{0}^{\infty}{y(t)}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\lVert y_{0}\rVert=1}\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{e}^{{A(\mathsf{v})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}t}\mathrm{e}^{A(\mathsf{v})t}y_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

where
\sigma
is a given non-negative measure on the unit sphere. From
[
62
, Proposition 21.1]
it follows that one can calculate the average total energy as
\trace(X(\mathsf{v}))
, where
X(\mathsf{v})
solves the Lyapunov equation

A(\mathsf{v})X(\mathsf{v})+X(\mathsf{v}){A(\mathsf{v})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}.

(24)

Here
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
is the unique positive semidefinite matrix determined by
\sigma
.
We will show that
A(\mathsf{v})
has the form (
2
) and that Assumptions
1
is satisfied for both types of internal damping mentioned above and for typical choices of
\sigma
.

Using the linearization
y_{1}=x
,
y_{2}=\dot{x}
, the differential equation (
20
) in phase
space can be written as

\dot{y}=A(\mathsf{v})y,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad A(\mathsf{v})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I\\
-M^{-1}K&-M^{-1}C(\mathsf{v})\\
\end{array}\right].

(25)

In the case of internal Rayleigh damping (
22
), it is easy to see that
A(\mathsf{v})
has the form (
2
) with

A_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
-M^{-1}K&-\alpha I-\beta M^{-1}K\end{bmatrix},\quad B_{l}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
M^{-1}B\end{bmatrix},\quad B_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
B\end{bmatrix}.

It can be shown that

is a Hurwitz matrix (the eigenvalues
of

can be written as a block diagonal matrix with

Assume
Z=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(K^{-1},M^{-1})
, which corresponds to the choice of the surface measure

on

-\frac{1}{2}K^{-1}&{(\alpha K+\beta KM^{-1}K)}^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(26)

hence Assumption
1
is satisfied.
It can be shown that the eigenvalues of

are given by

\frac{1}{2}\left(-\alpha-\beta\lambda_{i}\pm\sqrt{{(\alpha+\beta\lambda_{i})}^{2}-4\lambda_{i}}\right),

and that the corresponding eigenvectors can be constructed from the eigenvectors of the pair

. Hence, Assumption

In the case of internal damping of the form (
23
), a different kind of linearization is more convenient.
From the assumptions on
M
and

it follows that there exists a matrix

that simultaneously diagonalizes

and

\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}K\Phi=\Omega^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M\Phi=I,

(27)

where

contains the square roots of the eigenvalues of

, which are eigenfrequencies of the corresponding undamped (

) vibrational system. We assume that they are ordered in ascending order,

.
It holds that the matrix

diagonalizes

as well, that is,

\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C_{\mathrm{i}nt}\Phi=\alpha\Omega,

for more details see, e.g.,
[

]
.

Using the linearization
y_{1}=L_{K}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x
,

, where
L_{K}
,

are the Cholesky factors of

and

can be written as

A(\mathsf{v})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\Omega\\
-\Omega&-\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C(\mathsf{v})\Phi\\
\end{array}\right],

(28)

where
\Omega
and
\Phi
are given by (

).

It is important to notice that the matrix

from (

[

]
.

Since in this case the matrix

is given by

-\Omega&-\alpha\Omega\end{bmatrix},

all its eigenvalues of

are non-real for

.

Let

. Typical choices for the matrix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
in this case are
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}=\frac{1}{n}I
, which corresponds to the case when

is generated by the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, and, for

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}=\frac{1}{2s}\begin{bmatrix}I_{s}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&I_{s}&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(29)

which corresponds to the case when
\sigma
is generated by the Lebesgue measure on the subspace spanned by the vectors

{[x_{i},0]}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and

For both choices of the matrix

3.2
Multi-agent systems

The second application concerns the analysis of parameter variation in output synchronization for heterogeneous multi-agent systems. In a nutshell, multi-agent systems are a class of dynamical systems on networks, consisting of a number of dynamical systems (
agents
) connected in a network, where the agents can exchange information with the neighboring agents through a given interaction
protocol
, with the communication topology being specified through a (combinatorial) graph. Agent dynamics can be rather complex; see, e.g.,
[
40
]
. Examples of systems that can be modelled as multi-agent systems are, e.g., wireless sensor networks, power grids, and social networks. Common protocols are those for which the systems achieve consensus or output synchronization, and those for which the systems achieve desired formation or flocking state. The design and analyses of multi-agent systems have been a widely investigated field in recent years and for more details see, e.g.,
[
35
,
65
,
63
]
and references therein.

The problem of output synchronization is how to design / control a system in such a way that the outputs of the agents converge to the same state. The general setting of output synchronization problems for heterogeneous multi-agent systems was considered, e.g., in
[
24
,
13
,
22
,
40
]
.

An important aspect in heterogeneous multi-agent systems is studying the impact that one agent has, or a subset of agents has, on the whole system. This will, of course, depend on the dynamics of these agents and their location in the communication graph. The goal of this subsection is to analyze this impact with high computational efficiency in the case of the output synchronization protocol. We will analyze how one or more agents influence the entire system by using the
H_{2}
-norm (see, e.g.,
[
67
]
) of the corresponding multi-agent system as a performance measure. By using this information, the system designer can modify / control the heterogeneous multi-agent system in order to achieve a target behavior.

To understand why equations of the form (
1
) arise in this setting, we first need to briefly introduce the bigger picture. Motivated by work from
[
41
,
47
,
39
]
, we consider
m
agents with their dynamics described by

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{i}

\displaystyle=A^{(i)}\xi_{i}+B^{(i)}u_{i}+\omega_{i},

(30)

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}

\displaystyle=C^{(i)}\xi_{i}.

Here the function
\xi_{i}
represents the state of the agent
i
, the function
u_{i}
represents the input of the agent
i
, the function
\zeta_{i}
represents the output of the agent
i
, and the function
\omega_{i}
represents the exogenous disturbance of the agent
i
,
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.
The matrices
A^{(i)}
,
B^{(i)}
and
C^{(i)}
are called state, input, and output matrices, of the agent
i
, respectively. We assume that all matrices
A^{(i)}
have the same size, and that the same holds for the matrices
B^{(i)}
and
C^{(i)}
.
Let
G
be the corresponding communication graph, which models the connections of the agents from (
30
), with nodes
\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
. This means that
(i,j)
is an edge in the graph
G
if the agents
i
and
j
exchange information through the common protocol.
A protocol we are going to study is the following

u_{i}(t)=-K^{(i)}\sum_{j\in\mathfrak{N}_{i}}(\zeta_{i}(t)-\zeta_{j}(t)),\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

(31)

Here
\mathfrak{N}_{i}
denotes the set of neighboring agents of the agent
i
(described by
G
) and
K^{(i)}
is a so-called gain matrix of appropriate dimension
[
47
]
.

We define the stack vector
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
. Let
n
be the size of the matrices
A^{(i)}
, hence
nm
is the size of the vector
\xi
.
As we want to treat scenarios in which the disturbances
\omega_{i}
need not all be independent, let us suppose that among the exogenous disturbances
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}\}
there are
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
different ones
\{\omega_{i_{1}},\ldots,\omega_{i_{k}}\}
(e.g., the same ocean waves or wind gusts concurrently disturb several agents). Then
\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{1}&\cdots&\omega_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=H\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{i_{1}}&\cdots&\omega_{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, where the matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
is given by

H_{jl}=\begin{cases}1,&\omega_{j}=\omega_{i_{l}}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad j=1,\ldots,m,\;\;l=1,\ldots,k.

With
\mathbf{E}=H\otimes I_{n}
we denote the corresponding disturbance matrix. Let
\omega=(\omega_{i_{1}},\ldots,\omega_{i_{k}})
. With
\mathbf{C}
we denote the overall output matrix
\mathbf{C}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(C^{(1)},\ldots,C^{(m)})
.

The closed-loop dynamic equation of the system described by (
30
) and (
31
) can be represented as

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\xi(t)+\mathbf{E}\omega(t),

(32)

\displaystyle\zeta(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\xi(t),

where
the system matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times nm}
in (
32
) can be seen as a block matrix whose
(i,j)
-th block
\mathbf{A}_{ij}
is given by

\mathbf{A}_{ij}=\delta_{i,j}A^{(i)}-\ell_{ij}B^{(i)}K^{(i)}C^{(j)},\quad i,j\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\},

where
\ell_{ij}
,
i,j=1,2,\ldots,m
, are the entries of the Laplacian matrix
L
of the graph
G
,
see, e.g.,
[
41
]
. Note, however, that the model in
[
41
]
has delays, while our model has no delays.

By calculating the
H_{2}
-norm of the system (
32
), we are calculating the norm of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\zeta
, thus measuring how the disturbance
\omega
is influencing the multi-agent system.
This boils down to the calculation of
\trace(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X)
, where
X
solves the Lyapunov equation
\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X+X\mathbf{A}=-\mathbf{C}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}
. If we were interested in measuring the influence of the disturbance to a part of the system, i.e., to a subset of agents, instead of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
in (
32
), we would use the matrix
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}
, where
\mathbf{P}
has the form
\mathbf{P}=P\otimes I
, with
P
being an orthogonal projector to the subspace spanned by the canonical vectors corresponding to the subset of agents we are interested in.

We want to study the impact that a variation of dynamics in one or a subset of agents has on the
H_{2}
-norm of the resulting system.
To this end, we need to compute
\trace(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X(\mathsf{v}))
where now
X(\mathsf{v})
denotes the solution to a Lyapunov equation of
the form (
1
) with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}=\mathbf{C}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}
. In this
framework, the low-rank modification
B_{l}D(\mathsf{v}){B_{r}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
in the coefficient matrix (
2
) is meant to take into account the variation in the dynamics of the agents of interest. In particular,
B_{l}
and
B_{r}
will encode the location of the agents to be modified whereas
D(\mathsf{v})
amounts to the parameter variation to be applied to those agents.

In the numerical examples in Section
4
we choose symmetric matrices
A^{(i)}
and
K^{(i)}
, and we will also take
B^{(i)}=(C^{(i)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Hence, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
will be symmetric and therefore Assumptions
1
and
2
will be satisfied.

5
Conclusions

We proposed two different, efficient, and accurate methods for solving sequences of parametrized Lyapunov equations. The
SMW+
recycling Krylov approach is well-suited for small dimensional problems and is able to provide solutions achieving a very small relative errors. The proposed projection framework relies on the extended Krylov subspace method, and it makes use of the aforementioned
SMW+
recycling Krylov technique to solve the projected equations.
Even though this second algorithm is tailored to large-scale problems, our numerical results show that it is extremely competitive also for medium-sized problems, especially if the number of Lyapunov equations to be solved is very large as it happens in the application settings we studied. We showed that the projection framework
is able to speed up the entire solution process by an order of magnitude.
Expensive analyses like viscosity optimization for vibrational systems and output synchronization of multi-agent systems are, thus, now affordable.
