Dynamic Privacy Allocation for Locally Differentially Private Federated Learning with Composite Objectives
Jiaojiao Zhang, Dominik Fay, Mikael Johansson

Abstract
This paper proposes a locally differentially private federated learning algorithm for strongly convex but possibly nonsmooth problems that protects the gradients of each worker against an honest but curious server. The proposed algorithm adds artificial noise to the shared information to ensure privacy and dynamically allocates the time-varying noise variance to minimize an upper bound of the optimization error subject to a predefined privacy budget constraint. This allows for an arbitrarily large but finite number of iterations to achieve both privacy protection and utility up to a neighborhood of the optimal solution, removing the need for tuning the number of iterations. Numerical results show the superiority of the proposed algorithm over state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Federated Learning (FL)
[
1
]
is a popular distributed machine learning framework where a set of workers collaborate on model training through a server without sharing their local data. Not only does this enable efficient parallel computations, but it also provides privacy advantages compared to centralized machine learning methods.
Unfortunately, even though the data is not directly shared, FL can still result in privacy leakage
[
2
]
. This is because the information communicated between the workers and the server, such as local gradients or models, is derived from the raw data. As the number of iterations increases, the accumulated shared information during the training process may pose a privacy risk
[
2
]
.

One way to approach privacy protection is through the lens of cryptography. However, in many FL scenarios, these techniques suffer from
high communication and computation costs
[
3
]
.
Currently, Differential Privacy (DP)
[
4
,
5
]
has been more successful in FL, since it is easy to implement, has a good privacy protection effect, and allows to rigorously quantify the strength of the privacy protection
[
5
]
.
The design of DP algorithms in federated learning depends on the attack scenario and can be roughly divided into global DP and local DP (LDP)
[
6
,
7
]
. Global DP resists passive attackers from outside the system and typically relies on the server to add noise to the aggregated information while the workers upload their true models or gradients, assuming that the upload communication channel is secure and the server is trustworthy
[
6
]
. In contrast, LDP protects sensitive information against an honest but curious server during the training process and does not rely on secure uploading channels

[
7
]
. Instead, each worker adds noise to its local sensitive information before sending it to the server. The challenge of LDP is that it may require more noise to be added, which can lead to a serious deterioration of the utility (e.g. the expected value of the total loss function). A number of studies have been dedicated to proposing algorithms with better privacy-utility trade-off
[
8
,
9
]
.
One example of such a technique is the recently proposed DP-SCAFFOLD
[
8
]
, which combines LDP with SCAFFOLD
[
10
]
. It analyzes the trade-off between privacy and utility and demonstrates the superior performance of DP-SCAFFOLD compared to DP-FedAvg
[
11
]
, especially for heterogeneous data. The work in
[
9
]
proposes ISRL-DP to solve problems with convex constraints.
However, all the works mentioned above face the issue that the optimization error bound is sensitive to the total number of iterations when the privacy budget is finite.
Therefore, the total number of iterations becomes a hyperparameter, which needs to be chosen carefully to minimize the optimization error. Either too few or too many iterations reduce the utility, which brings difficulties to the practical use of the algorithm.

Contribution.
We propose an LDP FL algorithm for strongly convex but possibly nonsmooth problems that protects worker gradients from an honest but curious server.
Our algorithm adds artificial noise to shared information for privacy protection. A dynamic allocation of the noise variance minimizes the optimization error under a predefined privacy budget constraint.
As a result, the optimization error caused by the privacy-protecting noise is independent of the total number of iterations, allowing us to execute an arbitrarily large but finite number of iterations for privacy and utility without worrying that a too large number of iterations will cause the optimization error to diverge. Our numerical results show a distinct superiority of the proposed algorithm over state-of-the-art methods. Table
1
summarizes the comparison with the state-of-the-art methods.

Problem Formulation and LDP

In this section, we introduce the problem formulation for FL and review some preliminaries on LDP.

2.1
Problem Formulation

In our FL scenario, a server coordinates
n
workers to collaboratively train a joint model with
d
parameters. Each worker, denoted by
i\in[n]
, possesses a private data set

\mathcal{D}_{i}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{il}
comprising the
m
data points
\mathcal{D}_{i1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{im}
. We support heterogeneous data and do not require that the data sets
\mathcal{D}_{i}
are similar. Our goal is to solve composite problems on the form

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)+g(x),~f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}f_{il}(x).

(1)

In this context,
f_{il}(x)
represents the sample loss associated with data point
\mathcal{D}_{il}
, while the loss function
f_{i}(x)
measures the dissimilarity between the model’s predictions using parameter
x
and the actual labels in the data set
\mathcal{D}_{i}
. Additionally,
g
serves as a regularizer that promotes desired properties of the solution, such as sparsity.
To address (
1
) in the FL setting, we introduce a local copy
x_{i}
of the decision vector
x
for each worker
i
and reformulate the problem as

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f_{i}(x_{i})+g(x_{i})\right),

(2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle x_{i}-\bar{x}=0,~\forall i\in[n],

where
\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}/n
.
To simplify the process of developing and analyzing the algorithm, we rewrite (
2
) in a compact form. We let
\tilde{L}=I_{n}-1_{n}1_{n}^{T}/n
and represent the local copies
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as an aggregated vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
. By defining

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i}),~g(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i}),~\mathbf{L}=\tilde{L}\otimes I_{d},

we can express (
2
) in the form

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}}~f(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x}),~\mbox{s.t.}~\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}=0_{nd}.

(3)

By the definition of
\mathbf{L}
, the constraint
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}=0_{nd}
is equivalent to
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}=\bar{x}
.
In this reformulation (
3
), each worker has to minimize its local loss (defined by
f_{i}(x_{i})
and
g(x_{i})
) and cooperates with the others to reach consensus on the globally optimal decision variable.
Throughout the paper, we make the following assumptions on
f
and
g
.

Assumption 2.1

The loss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{nd}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is both
\mu_{f}
-strongly convex and
L_{f}
-smooth,
i.e.

for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
,

f(\mathbf{y})\geq f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{T}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})+\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2},

f(\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{T}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2},

where
\mu_{f}>0
and
L_{f}>0
denote the strong convexity constant and smoothness constant, respectively. Additionally, we assume that
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a closed and proper convex function, which may not necessarily be smooth.

2.2
Local Differential Privacy

We consider a threat model in which an honest-but-curious server has access to all the messages shared by the workers during the training process, as studied in
[
8
]
.
We aim to protect each gradient
\nabla f_{il}
for all
i\in[n]
and
l\in[m]
. Thus,
we say that
\nabla f_{i}
and
\nabla f_{i}^{\prime}
is a pair of adjacent inputs of worker
i
if only a single gradient (e.g.
\tilde{l}
-th) is different, i.e.,
\nabla f_{i\tilde{l}}\neq\nabla f^{\prime}_{i\tilde{l}}
while
\nabla f_{il}=\nabla f^{\prime}_{il}
for any
l\neq\tilde{l}
.
With this definition of adjacent inputs, we give the following definition of LDP.

Definition 2.2
(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP)

For any adjacent inputs
\nabla f_{i}
and
\nabla f_{i}^{\prime}
of worker
i
, a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP if for
every
O\subset\text{Range}(\mathcal{A})
where
\text{Range}(\mathcal{A})
is the output space of
\mathcal{A}
, we have

{\operatorname{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\nabla f_{i})\in O]}\leq e^{\epsilon}{\operatorname{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\nabla f_{i}^{\prime})\in O]}+\delta.

(4)

When
\epsilon
and
\delta
are sufficiently small, the outputs generated by any pair of adjacent inputs
\nabla f_{i}
and
\nabla f_{i}^{\prime}
are almost identical, which makes it difficult for attackers to distinguish between them. One prototypical method for achieving
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP is the Gaussian Mechanism, which adds Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\xi^{2}I_{d})
to a real-valued query function
\mathcal{Q}:\nabla f_{i}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
to perturb the true answer. The variance of the noise that one needs to add depends on the sensitivity of
\mathcal{Q}

[
4
]
.

Definition 2.3
(Sensitivity)

For any adjacent inputs
\nabla f_{i}
and
\nabla f_{i}^{\prime}
, the sensitivity of a query function
\mathcal{Q}
is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}=\max_{\nabla f_{i},\nabla f_{i}^{\prime}}\|\mathcal{Q}(\nabla f_{i})-\mathcal{Q}(\nabla f_{i}^{\prime})\|.

To ensure a desired level of privacy, a higher variance is needed when the sensitivity
\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}
is higher
[
4
]
.
Applying a differentially private mechanism repeatedly increases the privacy loss. We use zero-concentrated differential privacy (
\rho
-zCDP
[
12
]
) as an analytical tool to track the privacy loss of our algorithm across iterations. The
\rho
-zCDP captures the privacy properties of repeated Gaussian mechanisms better than analyzing each iteration separately via
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP
[
12
]
. The total
\rho
-zCDP bound can then be converted back to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP.

The proposed algorithm

Next, we introduce our LDP FL algorithm for solving (

\operatorname*{minimize}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}}\;\operatorname*{maximize}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}}~f(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})+\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}\rangle,

4
Analysis

In this section, we first perform separate analyses of the privacy and utility of the proposed algorithm. Then, we perform a joint privacy-utility analysis under a dynamic allocation of the variance of the injected noise and give the resulting convergence rate. All proofs can be found in Appendix

4.1
Separate Analysis of Privacy and Utility

We make the following assumption on the injected noise.

Assumption 4.1

At any time step
t
and for every worker
i\in[n]
, the injected noise variables
\zeta_{i}^{t}
are independent and follow a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance
\xi_{t}^{2}I_{d}
.

To ensure bounded sensitivity, we need
\nabla f_{i}(x_{i}^{t})
to be bounded for all
i
and

. The convexity of

simplifies this requirement to the boundedness of
x_{i}^{t}

[
15
, Prop. B.24]
. To achieve this, we make specific assumptions on the regularizer

.

Assumption 4.2

The proximal operator of the regularizer,
\mathrm{prox}_{g}(z)=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}
,
is both easy to compute and bounded.

We present two examples of
g
that satisfy Assumption
4.2
:

(1) The indicator function of a convex and compact set which is easy to project onto. When
g
is the indicator function of a convex set, the proximal operator reduces to the projection operator, which is bounded if the underlying set is.

(2) The weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm over a box
[
16
, Example 6.23]
)

g(z)=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\omega_{j}\left|z_{j}\right|,&-{\alpha}1_{d}\leq z\leq{\alpha}1_{d},\\
\infty,&\text{ else, }\end{cases}

where
\omega_{j}>0
,
\alpha>0
, and

is the

-th entry of

. We can express

as
g(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}(z_{j})
, where

g_{j}(z_{j})=\omega_{j}|z_{j}|
if
-\alpha\leq z_{j}\leq\alpha,
and
g_{j}(z_{j})=\infty
otherwise.
The
j
-th entry of
\mathrm{prox}_{g}(z)
, defined by
[\mathrm{prox}_{g}(z)]_{j}
, is given by

[\mathrm{prox}_{g}(z)]_{j}=\min\left\{\max\left\{\left|z_{j}\right|-\omega_{j},0\right\},\alpha\right\}\operatorname{sign}\left(z_{j}\right),

Consequently, we have
-\alpha\leq[\mathrm{prox}_{g}(z)]_{j}\leq\alpha
, and the proximal operator of

is bounded and easy to evaluate.

With Assumption
4.2
, we know that there exists a constant

so that, for all possible sequences
\{\mathbf{x}^{t}\}_{t}
generated by Algorithm

\|\nabla f_{il}(x_{i}^{t})\|\leq B~\mbox{ for all }i,t\mbox{ and }l.

(8)

-LDP for each gradient

Let Assumptions

Then, for each worker

6
Conclusions

We have proposed an LDP FL algorithm for strongly convex, possibly nonsmooth, problems that protects the gradient of each worker against an honest but curious server. The dynamic allocation of the noise variance, which is derived by minimizing the optimization error subject to a privacy budget constraint, removes the need for tuning the total number of communication rounds. Our algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art methods, making it a promising approach for privacy-preserving FL. Future research will explore data subsampling amplification and extensions to decentralized architectures.

]

]

Definition A.1
(
\rho
-zCDP)

A randomized algorithm

is said to satisfy

-zCDP if, for all
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
and all adjacent inputs

(16)

where

is the Rényi divergence

]
.

Fact A.2
(Gaussian mechanism)

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a function with sensitivity
\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Then, the mechanism
\mathcal{A}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{Q}(x),\xi^{2}I_{d})
satisfies
(\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}/(2\xi^{2}))
-zCDP.

Fact A.3
(Composition)

Let
\mathcal{A}(x)
=(\mathcal{A}_{1}(x),\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{T}(x))
be an iterative randomized algorithm. If each
\mathcal{A}_{t}
respectively satisfies
\rho_{t}
-zCDP, then running
\mathcal{A}(x)

satisfies
\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{t}\right)
-zCDP.

Fact A.4

If a randomized algorithm

satisfies
\rho
-zCDP, then
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\left(\rho+2\sqrt{\rho\ln{1}/{\delta}},\delta\right)

\delta>0

To bound the privacy loss, we will show that
\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t+1}
is the output of a Gaussian mechanism with appropriately chosen variance and sensitivity.
Recall that

Note that

and
\Lambda_{i}^{t}
are a data-independent processing of
\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t}
.
Define auxiliary functions

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\nabla f_{i},\nabla f^{\prime}_{i}}\left\|\mathcal{Q}(\nabla f_{i};\,\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t})-\mathcal{Q}(\nabla f^{\prime}_{i};\,\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t})\right\|

2\langle a,b\rangle\leq\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}
in the last inequality.
Substituting (
33
) into (
32
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}^{t}\right]

(34)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{1-\gamma\mu_{f},1-\gamma\right\}\Phi^{t}+2{\gamma^{3}}nd\xi_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}^{t+1}\!-\!\mathbf{y}^{t}\right\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{t}\right]\!+\!\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{L}\left(\mathbf{y}^{t+1}\!-\!\mathbf{y}^{t}\right){+\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{t}}\right\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{1-\gamma\mu_{f},1-\gamma\right\}\Phi^{t}+2{\gamma^{3}}nd\xi_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle+{\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}+2\gamma^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}^{t+1}-\mathbf{y}^{t}\right\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}^{t}\right]+2\gamma^{2}nd\xi_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{1-\gamma\mu_{f},1-\gamma\right\}\Phi^{t}+2(1+\gamma){\gamma^{2}}nd\xi_{t}^{2},

where we use
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{L})=1
and
\gamma\leq 1/4
. Thus, we get (
9
) and complete the proof.

Proof of Theorem

Telescoping (
9
) from
t=1
to
t=T
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{T+1}\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma C_{1})^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{1}\right]+C_{2}\gamma^{2}\left(q_{1}\xi_{1}^{2}+\ldots+q_{T}\xi_{T}^{2}\right),

where we define
q_{t}=(1-\gamma C_{1})^{T-t}
,
C_{1}=\min\left\{{\mu_{f}},1\right\}
and
C_{2}=2.5nd
.
In order to dynamically allocate the privacy budget, we solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{\{\xi_{t}^{2}\}_{t=1}^{T}}

\displaystyle q_{1}\xi_{1}^{2}+\cdots+q_{T}\xi_{T}^{2},

(36)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\frac{2B^{2}}{n^{2}{m^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\xi_{T}^{2}}\right)=\rho_{\rm tgt},

which can be verified to be strictly convex via reformulation. To solve the problem (
36
), we write down its KKT condition

q_{t}-\frac{\pi}{(\xi_{t}^{2})^{2}}=0,\quad\frac{1}{\xi_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\xi_{T}^{2}}=\frac{\rho_{\rm tgt}n^{2}{m^{2}}}{2B^{2}},

(37)

where
\pi\in\mathbb{R}
is the dual variable.
From (
37
), we get the solution of problem (
36
)

\xi_{t}^{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{q_{t}}},\quad\sqrt{\pi}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{q_{t}}}{\frac{\rho_{\rm tgt}n^{2}{m^{2}}}{2B^{2}}},\quad\forall t\in[T].

(38)

Substituting the solution (
38
) into (
35
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{T+1}\right]

(39)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma C_{1})^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{1}\right]+\frac{C_{2}\gamma^{2}2B^{2}}{{\rho_{\rm tgt}n^{2}{m^{2}}}}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{T-1}\left(\sqrt{1-\gamma C_{1}}\right)^{p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma C_{1})^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{1}\right]+\frac{C_{2}\gamma^{2}2B^{2}}{{\rho_{\rm tgt}n^{2}{m^{2}}}}{\cdot\frac{1}{(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma C_{1}})^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma C_{1})^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{1}\right]+\frac{C_{2}2B^{2}}{\rho_{\rm tgt}n^{2}{m^{2}}}\frac{(1+\sqrt{1-\gamma C_{1}})^{2}}{C_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma\min\left\{{\mu_{f}},1\right\})^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi^{1}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{20B^{2}d}{\left(\sqrt{\epsilon+\ln\tfrac{1}{\delta}}-\sqrt{\ln\tfrac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{2}n{m^{2}}\min\left\{\mu_{f}^{2},1\right\}},

where we use the fact that

\rho_{\rm tgt}=\left(\sqrt{\epsilon+\ln{1}/{\delta}}-\sqrt{\ln{1}/{\delta}}\right)^{2}

A.4
Additional Experiments: Classification Using Neural Networks.

In this set of experiments, we solve a nonconvex nonsmooth problem that involves classifying the MNIST dataset
[
8
]
using a neural network.
It should be noted that our theoretical findings are no longer applicable to nonconvex problems. Here is a heuristic extension of the proposed algorithm.
We utilize a one-hidden-layer neural network (NN) to classify the MNIST dataset
[
8
]
.
The NN consists of 200 neurons in the hidden layer, employing a sigmoid activation function, and a softmax activation in the output layer.
The loss function employed is cross-entropy with a regularization term
g(x)
that is the weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm over a box defined in Section
4
with
\omega_{j}=10^{-5}
for all
j\in[d]
and
\alpha=10
.
The MNIST dataset comprises grayscale images of handwritten digits, each with dimensions
28\times 28
pixels, and includes 10 classes representing digits from 0 to 9.
Our training dataset consists of 38,500 samples.
Noted that our algorithm and theory support heterogeneous data, we uniformly sample 10,000 samples from the entire training dataset, allocating 1,000 samples to each of the 10 nodes. The remaining 28,500 samples are assigned labels ranging from 0 to 9, with each class having 2,850 samples, distributed across nodes 1 to 10. We use 10,000 samples for testing.
We define two sets of privacy budgets as
(1,10^{-4})
-LDP and
(0.5,10^{-5})
-LDP, as well as three sets of total communication rounds as 500, 1000, and 1200.

Figure 3:
Classification of MNIST using neural networks.

Fig.
3
shows the testing accuracy with respect to communication rounds.
It is noted that insufficient communication rounds (500 rounds) result in lower accuracy. However, increasing the communication rounds to 1000 leads to an accuracy of around 70%. Despite more privacy-preserving noise being added with 1200 communication rounds, our algorithm consistently stabilizes at around 70% accuracy. This means that practical applications can benefit from estimating an appropriate number of communication rounds, but even an overestimation does not compromise accuracy.
