CSG: A stochastic gradient method for a wide class of optimization problems appearing in a machine learning or data-driven context
Lukas Pflug, Max Grieshammer, Andrian Uihlein, Michael Stingl

Abstract
A recent article introduced the continuous stochastic gradient method (CSG) for the efficient solution of a class of stochastic optimization problems. While the applicability of known stochastic gradient type methods is typically limited to expected risk functions, no such limitation exists for CSG. This advantage stems from the computation of design dependent integration weights, allowing for optimal usage of available information and therefore stronger convergence properties. However, the nature of the formula used for these integration weights essentially limited the practical applicability of this method to problems in which stochasticity enters via a low-dimensional and sufficiently simple probability distribution. In this paper we significantly extend the scope of the CSG method by presenting alternative ways to calculate the integration weights. A full convergence analysis for this new variant of the CSG method is presented and its efficiency is demonstrated in comparison to more classical stochastic gradient methods by means of a number of problem classes relevant to stochastic optimization and machine learning.

Introduction

In the past, a variety of stochastic optimization schemes have been developed, e.g.,
[
7
,
12
,
13
,
22
,
24
]
, in the context of optimization problems in which the expected-value of a cost function
j
is minimized, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta^{\textnormal{ad}}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[j(\theta,X)]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}j(\theta,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

(1.1)

with probability measure
\mu
and the associated random variables
X
. Among the most popular algorithms are the
stochastic gradient method
(SG)
[
17
]
and its modification the
stochastic average gradient method
(SAG)
[
18
]
. Both of these methods have been analyzed extensively in the literature and are characterized by a low cost per iteration.

Nonetheless, SG and SAG suffer from a number of known disadvantages, like the lack of efficient stopping criteria (cf.
[
15
]
) or optimal stepsize rules (cf.
[
14
,
19
]
). To tackle these issues, a wide range of modified SG methods have been developed. For example,
[
7
]
uses a trust-region-type model to normalize the steplengths, whereas the iSARAH algorithm proposed in
[
13
]
combines an inner SG scheme with an outer (inexact) full gradient descent method.

An additional disadvantage of SG is the relatively restrictive setting of (
1.1
). In an effort to address this,
[
22
]
and
[
24
]
suggest inexact proximal stochastic second-order methods and stochastic primal-dual fixed-point methods to allow for different types of objective functionals. For the case that the constraints include expected-valued functions, a level set method is also analyzed in
[
12
]
.

The
continuous stochastic gradient method
(CSG) proposed in
[
16
]
is able to solve a wider class of problems. This is achieved by combining the information collected in previous iterations in an optimal way, meaning that CSG gains a significantly improved gradient approximation and is able to estimate the current objective function value during the optimization process. For a characterization of the class of problems that CSG can solve, we refer to
remark

3
. Here we simply note that these problems include objective functions with nested expectation values (
Section

5.2
) and problems with chance constraints (
Section

5.3
).

While these advantages of the original version of CSG
[
16
]
are known, a serious drawback remains: to approximate function values and gradients as mentioned above, integration weights have to be computed via an analytical formula, which requires full knowledge of the probability measure
\mu
. Moreover, the evaluation is based on a Voronoi diagram, which is challenging to compute if the dimension of the parameter set
\mathcal{X}
is larger than 2. As a consequence, in
[
16
]
only examples with a one-dimensional uniform distribution were presented.

In this contribution, we further expand the setting of CSG by introducing new methods to calculate the weights used for the gradient and cost function value approximations. This enables us to apply the CSG algorithm to problems of higher dimension, to arbitrary measure
\mu
appearing in (
1.1
) and even to problems where
\mu
might be unknown, e.g., in a data-driven context.

Depending on the concrete setting, i.e., depending on the dimensions of
\theta,x
and on how time-consuming the evaluation of a gradient sample is, the proposed methods allow us to continuously trade weight-computation time and speed of convergence (w.r.t. number of gradient sample evaluations).

In this article we present a full convergence analysis for this extended CSG method. In particular, we show that the errors in both the gradient approximation and the objective function value approximation vanish as the number of steps increases. As a consequence, these values can be utilized, for instance, to apply stopping criteria based on first order optimality conditions. Moreover, this allows the potential combination of the CSG method with slightly adapted step length strategies as known from the world of deterministic optimization methods, a topic we leave open for future research.

The remaining structure of the paper is as follows. In
Section

2
, we detail the mathematical structure of the problems we would like to solve using the CSG method. In
Section

3
, we present the CSG method with generalized weight computation.
Section

4
is devoted to the convergence analysis and in
Section

5
we compare the generalized CSG method to more traditional SG-type algorithms using three different classes of test problems.

2
Problem setting and definitions

Following the classic setup for expected-value objective functions, we introduce the set of admissible designs
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textnormal{des}}}
and the parameter set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textnormal{par}}}
, where
{d_{\textnormal{des}}},{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
In the optimization process, the random samples
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
drawn from the parameter set
\mathcal{X}
are assumed to be realizations of independent uniformly random variables
X_{i}\sim\mu
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent and follow an underlying probability distribution
\mu
, which may be unknown.

We define the following probability space setup:

Definition 1
(Probability space setup).

The probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{P}:=\mu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle:=\sigma(\{A_{1}\times\ldots\!\times\!A_{n}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}),\forall i,n\in\mathbb{N}\}),

where
\mu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}(A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu(A_{i})

is the product measure,
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the smallest
\sigma
-field that contains
\cdot
. We denote by
\textnormal{supp}(\mu)
the support of the measure
\mu
, i.e.,

\textnormal{supp}(\mu):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ \mu(B_{\varepsilon}(x))>0\ \forall\varepsilon>0\},

where
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
denotes an open ball of radius
\varepsilon>0
around
x\in\mathbb{\mathcal{X}}
. We write
X_{n}:\Omega\to\mathcal{X}
,
(\omega_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\omega_{n}
for the projection to the
n=1,2,\ldots
coordinate and define
X:=X_{1}
.

With this setup, the objective function takes the following form:

Definition 2
(Objective function).

The objective function
J:{\mathcal{P}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by

J(\theta):=\mathbb{E}\big[j(\theta,X)\big]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}j(\theta,x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

with a measurable function
j\in C^{1}({\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
and random variable
X
.

Remark 3
(Generalization of the setting).

During the optimization process, we may also generate an approximation
\hat{J}_{n}
to the exact objective function value
J(\theta_{n})
with almost no additional computational cost. We will later show that
\|\hat{J}_{n}-\nabla J(\theta_{n})\|_{{\mathcal{P}}}\to 0
(see
Remark

20
).

This enables us to solve a much broader class of optimization problems where the objective function may depend non-linearly on the expression above, i.e.,

\tilde{J}(\theta):=f\big(\theta,\mathbb{E}[j(\theta,X)]\big),

with a Lipschitz continuously differentiable function
f:{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Included in the set of possible objective functions are e.g. tracking functionals

\tilde{J}(\theta):=\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|h(\theta,\cdot)-f(\theta,\mathbb{E}[j(\theta,\cdot,X)])\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and nested expected values

\tilde{J}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}\big[f(Y,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}}[j(\theta,X)])\big].

Note that such settings cannot be solved by SG algorithms.

As we are aiming for a gradient based optimization scheme, we further state the derivative of the objective functional:

Lemma 4
(Derivative of objective function).

The gradient of the objective functional
J
is given by

\nabla J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,X)\big]
,
where
X\sim\mu
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}:{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textnormal{des}}}
denotes
\nabla_{1}j(\theta,x)
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the linearity of the expectation value and the finite-dimensional derivative of
j
. Integration and differentiation can be exchanged due to the Lipschitz continuity of the integrand w.r.t. the integration variable.

To state and prove convergence results for the algorithm presented in this work, we define the norms on the used spaces as follows:

Definition 5
(Norms on
\mathcal{X},{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X}
).

In this contribution, we will use the notation
\|\cdot\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}
for the norm on the underlying spaces of the parameter space
\mathcal{X}
and the design space
{\mathcal{P}}
, respectively. Due to norm-equivalence in finite dimensional spaces, the norm used in the spaces
{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{X}
does not have to be specified and can be chosen according to the specific problem.
In addition, we define on
{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X}
the following metric:

d\big((\theta,x),(\hat{\theta},\hat{x})\big):=\big\|\big(\|\theta-\hat{\theta}\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\,,\,\|x-\hat{x}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\big)\big\|_{1}\ \forall(\theta,\hat{\theta},x,\hat{x})\in{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\times\mathcal{X}^{2}.

Choosing the 1-norm in the three-dimensional space as“outer”-norm is arbitrary and could for instance - in the other extreme case - be the
\infty
-norm. Of course this could also include positive weights for each individual component.

Assumption 6
(Regularity of the
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
).

We assume
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}:{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{des}}}}
to be bounded and Lipschitz continuous, i.e., there exist constants
\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}},\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
s.t.

\displaystyle\big\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,x)\big\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}

\displaystyle\big\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,x)-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\hat{\theta},\hat{x})\big\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\big(\|\theta-\hat{\theta}\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}+\|x-\hat{x}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\big)

for all
\theta,\hat{\theta}\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
x,\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. A sufficient condition for the stated regularity of
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is thus to assume
\nabla j
to be Lipschitz continuous in both arguments.

The following assumptions on the sets
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{X}}
and the measure
\mu
are an important ingredient for the convergence analysis of
algorithm

1
.

Assumption 7
(Regularity of
{\mathcal{P}}
,
{\mathcal{X}}
and the measure
\mu
).

The set
{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{des}}}}
is compact and convex.
\textnormal{supp}(\mu)\subset{\mathcal{X}}
with
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}
is open and bounded, and there exists
\mathsf{M}_{{}_{1}},\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}},\mathsf{M}_{{}_{3}}>0
s.t.
\forall\,\varepsilon\in(0,\mathsf{M}_{{}_{3}})
there exists
\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{X}
satisfying
\mu(\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{1}}\varepsilon
and

\inf_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}}\mu\big(B_{\varepsilon}(x)\big)\geq\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}\varepsilon^{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}},

where
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\subset\mathcal{X}
is an open ball with radius
\varepsilon
centered in
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 8
(Examples for
7
).

In most cases, the choice
\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{X}
is suitable, for example when
\mathcal{X}
satisfies the uniform cone condition (cf.
[
1
, Definitioin 4.8]
). However, there are cases where the possibility of choosing
\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{X}
as part of the conditions of
7
allows us to consider even more general measures and sets.

Therefore, as an example,
let
\mathcal{X}:=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\mu:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\delta_{k^{-1}}(s)
. Then, for
M_{n}:=[n^{-1},1]\cap\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\mu(M_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{-k}=1-2^{-n}.

Thus, for
\varepsilon=2^{-n}
and

we obtain

and
\inf_{x\in M_{n}}\mu(B_{\varepsilon}(x))\geq 2^{-n}=\varepsilon
.
Since
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(B_{\varepsilon}(x))=\mu(B_{\varepsilon}(0))=2^{1-2^{n}}
, there exists no
c>0
such that
2^{1-2^{n}}\geq c2^{-n}\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For a uniform distribution and for all
0<p\leq\infty
, the open unit ball w.r.t. the
p
-Norm

\mathcal{X}^{p}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}:\|x\|_{p}<1\big\}

also satisfy
7
. While the case
1\leq p\leq\infty
allows for
\mathcal{X}^{p}_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{X}^{p}
, for
0<p<1
we first have to obtain
\mathcal{X}^{p}_{\varepsilon}
by trimming off the spikes of
\mathcal{X}
.

The algorithm

Before stating the algorithm, we first define the projection operator that ensures the sequence of generated designs
(\theta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in the set
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Definition 9
(Orthogonal projection).

We define the orthogonal projection – in the sense of
\|\cdot\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}
– onto the set
{\mathcal{P}}
as follows:

{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(\theta):=\argmin_{\hat{\theta}\in{\mathcal{P}}}\big\|\theta-\hat{\theta}\big\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}.

Note that the existence and uniqueness of
{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}
is guaranteed by the projection theorem (see e.g.
[
3
]
) building on the convexity of
{\mathcal{P}}
as assumed in
7
.

Lemma 10
(Properties of
{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}
).

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{des}}}}
satisfy
7
. Then the following holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\textnormal{des}}}}
and
z\in\mathcal{P}

(a)

({\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(x)-x)^{T}({\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(x)-z)\leq 0
,

(b)

({\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(y)-{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(x))^{T}(y-x)\geq\|{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(y)-{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(x)\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}^{2}\geq 0,

(c)

\|{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(y)-{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(x)\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}\leq\|y-x\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}
.

Proof.

A proof of (a) can be found in
[
3
, Thm. 1.4.1 (ii)]
. (b) and (c) correspond to (iii) and (ii) in
[
3
, Prop. 1.4.1]
, respectively.

Given
\theta_{1}
,

and a sequence

3.1
Calculating the weights

The quality of the weights
\alpha_{k}
appearing in
Algorithm

1
greatly impacts the accuracy of the gradient approximation
\hat{G}_{n}
and therefore directly influences the overall performance of the CSG method. At the same time, a more optimal computation of the weights might be time-consuming. Since the trade-off between the time spent calculating the weights and the time gained by performing fewer gradient evaluations is heavily problem-specific, we propose four different methods for the weight-calculation in the
n
th step:

Exact

Following an exact nearest neighbor approximation for the integral

\nabla J(\theta_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla_{\theta}j(\theta_{n},x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

for each
k=1,\ldots,n
, we define the set

M_{k}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:d\big((\theta_{n},x),(\theta_{k},x_{k})\big)<d\big((\theta_{n},x),(\theta_{j},x_{j})\big)\text{ for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{k\}\right\},

i.e., the set of points
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
(\theta_{n},x)
is closer to
(\theta_{k},x_{k})
than to any other previous evaluation point. Assuming that the measure
\mu
is known, we then set
{\alpha_{k}:=\mu(M_{k})}
. This method has been thoroughly analyzed in
[
16
]
and yields the best possible approximation to the exact gradient. However, it is computationally infeasible for problems of high dimensions.

Empirical

Utilizing the properties of the empirical measure
\mu_{n}
(see
Remark

18
), we may replace the exact weights mentioned above with the empirical weights

\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{M_{k}}(x_{i})=\mu_{n}(M_{k})\approx\mu(M_{k}),

where
1_{M_{k}}
denotes the indicator function of the set
M_{k}
. Note that the computation of the empirical weights requires no knowledge of
\mu
and is also feasible for high-dimensional problems. However, many samples
x_{i}
are needed to approximate the exact gradient with high accuracy.

Exact hybrid

Assuming that the dimension of
\mathcal{X}
is much smaller than the dimension of
{\mathcal{P}}
, we might treat the designs and parameters separately. Instead of
M_{k}
, we now consider the sets

\widetilde{M}_{i}=\big\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\|x-x_{i}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\|x-x_{j}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,n\big\},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

The
\alpha_{k}
are now calculated as a combination of the empirical and exact method

\alpha_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{M_{k}}(x_{i})\mu(\widetilde{M}_{i}).

(3.1)

Inexact hybrid

As for the exact weights, the calculation of the exact hybrid weights requires knowledge of
\mu
. If
\mu
is unknown, we may replace the factor
\mu(\widetilde{M}_{i})
in (
3.1
) with an empirical approximation. Since this only requires samples of
X
, which we assume are abundantly available, we can control the quality of this approximation through the number of samples we draw. The inexact hybrid weights are therefore calculated as follows:

\alpha_{k}:=\frac{1}{\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{M_{k}}(x_{j_{i}})\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor}1_{\widetilde{M}_{j_{i}}}(x_{m}),

where
\beta\geq 1
,
\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor
is the total number of samples we have drawn until step
n
and
x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{n}}
denotes the samples at which
\nabla_{\theta}j(\theta,x)
has been evaluated.

Figure

1
shows that the inexact hybrid method allows us to interpolate between the purely empirical method and the exact hybrid variant by choosing an appropriate
\beta
.

|\theta_{n}-\theta^{\ast}|
in iteration

and

Remark 11
.

In general, the nearest neighbor approximation used in all methods mentioned above worsens as the dimension of
{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{X}
increases (cf.
[
4
]
). Especially for problems where
\dim({\mathcal{P}})\ll\dim(\mathcal{X})
, Monte Carlo integration results (
[
23
]
) suggest that the performance boost gained by better weight calculation begins to become negligible. The proposed CSG methods are therefore best suited for optimization problems where
\mathcal{X}
is of small dimension in comparison to
{\mathcal{P}}
and the evaluation of
j(\theta,x)
is time-consuming.

Furthermore, to ensure the best possible performance, the metric
d
should be chosen according to the specific problem.

Remark 12
(SAG and SG as two extreme cases of the algorithm).

As stated in
Definition

5
, our metric
d
can be chosen as

d\big((\theta,x),(\hat{\theta},\hat{x})\big)=a_{1}\|\theta-\hat{\theta}\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}+a_{2}\|x-\hat{x}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}},

where
a_{1},a_{2}>0
are arbitrary. By choosing
a_{1}\gg a_{2}
, the nearest neighbor to
(\theta_{n},x)
is almost exclusively determined by the distance in the design variable. Hence, for the weights
\alpha_{k}
we obtain
\alpha_{n}\approx 1
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\approx 0
, i.e., the CSG algorithm will behave very similarly to the usual SG algorithm.

Analogously, choosing
a_{1}\ll a_{2}
will lead to a performance similar to that of SAG.

4
Convergence analysis

In this section we will study the convergence of the proposed algorithm. We will have to study probabilistic convergence behaviour in terms of “almost sure convergence”, as we choose evaluation points randomly within the algorithm,. Therefore, we first state first order optimality conditions, assumptions on the regularity of the involved functions and on the steplength
\tau
, and a suitable probability space setting.

4.1
Optimality conditions and assumptions

For
h\in C^{1}({\mathcal{P}})
and
{\mathcal{P}}
convex we have the following equivalent sufficient conditions for first order optimality:

Corollary 13
(Optimality conditions).

For all
\theta^{*}\in{\mathcal{P}}
the following items are equivalent:

(a)

-\nabla h(\theta^{*})^{T}(\theta-\theta^{*})\leq 0\quad\forall\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}

(b)

\mathcal{P}(\theta^{*}-t\nabla h(\theta^{*}))=\theta^{*}\quad\forall t\geq 0
.

A point
\theta^{*}\in{\mathcal{P}}
satisfying these conditions is called a stationary point.

Proof.

The proof can be found in e.g.
[
16
]
.

To guarantee that
Algorithm

1
generates a convergent subsequence, the stepsizes have to be damped, i.e.,
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has to be a null series with upper and lower bound as stated in the following Assumption. However, in contrast to the ordinary stochastic gradient descent method, if
Algorithm

1
generates – with stepsizes satisfying
\tau_{n}\geq\tau>0\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}
– a convergent sequence, the limit point is also a stationary point of the objective function. This is shown in
Theorem

23
.

Assumption 14
(Steplength).

The steplength
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
Algorithm

1
satisfies the following:
\exists N\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\underline{\mathsf{S}}},{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\mathsf{D}\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}\big)
s.t.

{\underline{\mathsf{S}}}n^{-1}\leq\tau_{n}\leq{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}{n^{-1+\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}-\mathsf{D}}}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{>N}.

These bounds on the steplength satisfy the conditions stated in
[
17
, Eqns. (6) and (26)]
, as well as equivalently in
[
6
, Eqn. (4.19)]
in the one-dimensional case, and can be seen as a higher dimensional equivalent.

In the following we assume that these assumptions are always satisfied without mentioning it explicitly.

4.2
Error in the search direction

In this subsection we analyse the error in the
n-
th iteration of the search direction
\hat{G}_{n}
and the gradient of the objective functional
\nabla J_{n}
. To accomplish this, we define the following random variables:

Definition 15
(Random variables).

For
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\omega\in\Omega
the sequence of random variables
\left(Z_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
Z_{n}:\Omega\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is defined by

Z_{n}(\omega,x):=\min_{k=1,\dots,n}d\big((\Theta_{k}(\omega),X_{k}(\omega)),(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\big),

where the designs
\Theta_{k}\in{\mathcal{P}}
for
k>1
depend by construction on the initial design
\Theta_{1}
and all “previous” random variables
X_{1}(\omega),\ldots,X_{k-1}(\omega)
, i.e.,

\Theta_{k}(\Theta_{1},X_{1}(\omega),\ldots,X_{k-1}(\omega))

and is thus also a random variable. We shorten this dependency by the notation
\Theta_{k}(\omega)
.

This random variable fulfills the following property:

Lemma 16
.

For
\mu
almost all
x\in\textnormal{supp}(\mu)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)>\varepsilon_{n}\right)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)>\varepsilon_{n}\right)<\infty,

with

\varepsilon_{n}:=\tfrac{\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}}{1-2^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}\cdot n^{-\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}:=n^{\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{2\max(\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}.

(4.1)

Here,
\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
is defined in
6
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon_{n}}
in
7
and
{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}
,
\mathsf{D}
in

Proof.

We first define
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
as an auxiliary index as follows:

i_{0}:=\lceil n-a_{n}+1\rceil\quad\text{with}\quad a_{n}:=n^{1+\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}.

By construction, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)\geq\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{k=i_{0},\dots,n}d((\Theta_{k},X_{k}),(\Theta_{n},x)))\geq\varepsilon_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\|\tau_{i}\hat{G}_{i}\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}+\min_{k=i_{0},\dots,n}\|X_{k}-x\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geq\varepsilon_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\tau_{i}+\min_{k=i_{0},\dots,n}\|X_{k}-x\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geq\varepsilon_{n}\right).

Observe that for
n>2
we obtain
for all
\kappa\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{i_{0}-1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{s^{\kappa}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(n^{1-\kappa}-(\lceil n-a_{n}\rceil)^{1-\kappa}\right)\leq\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(n^{1-\kappa}-(n-a_{n})^{1-\kappa}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{n^{\kappa}}-\frac{n-a_{n}}{(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}\right)=\frac{n}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(\frac{(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}-n^{\kappa}}{n^{\kappa}\cdot(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}\right)+\frac{a_{n}}{(1-\kappa)(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(\frac{n^{\kappa}(1-a_{n}/n)^{\kappa}-n^{\kappa}}{n^{\kappa}\cdot(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}\right)+\frac{a_{n}}{(1-\kappa)(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}.

Applying Bernoulli’s inequality in the first term, we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(\frac{n^{\kappa}\left(1-\kappa\cdot\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right)-n^{\kappa}}{n^{\kappa}\cdot(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}\right)+\frac{a_{n}}{(1-\kappa)(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\cdot\left(\frac{-\kappa a_{n}}{(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}\right)+\frac{a_{n}}{(1-\kappa)(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n}}{(n-a_{n})^{\kappa}}=\frac{a_{n}}{n^{\kappa}}(1-\tfrac{a_{n}}{n})^{-\kappa}.

(4.2)

Combining
14
and (
4.2
) yields

\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\tau_{i}\leq{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i^{1+\mathsf{D}-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}\leq{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\tfrac{a_{n}}{n^{\kappa}}(1-\tfrac{a_{n}}{n})^{-\kappa},

with
\mathsf{D}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}\right)
and
\kappa:=1+\mathsf{D}-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}\in(0,1)
.
Hence, for
n\geq 2
we obtain

\left(1-\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right)^{-\kappa}=\left(1-n^{\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}\right)^{-\kappa}\leq\left(1-n^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}\right)^{-\kappa}\leq\left(1-2^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}\right)^{-1}.

Collecting these results, we see

\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\tau_{i}\leq\frac{{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}}{1-2^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{1+\mathsf{D}-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}\leq\frac{{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}}{1-2^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}n^{-\tfrac{\mathsf{D}}{2}}.

Consequently,

\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\|\tau_{i}\hat{G}_{i}\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}}{1-2^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}}n^{-\tfrac{\mathsf{D}}{2}}=\varepsilon_{n}-\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}.

By
7
,
\mu({\mathcal{X}}\setminus{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon_{n}})\rightarrow 0
. Hence, for
\mu
almost all
x\in\textnormal{supp}(\mu)
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough, such that

. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)\geq\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{k=i_{0},\dots,n-1}\|X_{k}-x\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\|X_{k}-x\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\ \forall k\in\left\{i_{0},\dots,n-1\right\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=i_{0}}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}\Big(\|X_{k}-x\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\Big)=\prod_{k=i_{0}}^{n-1}\left(1-\mu\big(B_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}}(x)\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\min\big\{\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n})^{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}\,,\,1\big\}\right)^{\!a_{n}}.

As
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}\rightarrow 0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
s.t. for
n\geq N
we obtain

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)\geq\varepsilon_{n})\leq\left(1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}{2\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}}\right)^{\!a_{n}}.

For simplicity, we define

c_{1}:=\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}{2\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}

and recall that
{\log(1-x)\leq-x}
for all
x<1
. Since
c_{1}<0
, for
n
large enough it holds

\displaystyle\left(1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}{2\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}}\right)^{\!a_{n}}=\left(1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{c_{1}}\right)^{\!a_{n}}=\exp\left(a_{n}\log\left(1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{c_{1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-a_{n}\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{c_{1}}\right)=\exp\left(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{1+\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}+\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}{2\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{1+\mathsf{D}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\right)&{d_{\textnormal{par}}}=1\\
\exp\left(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{1+\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}+\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)&{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\geq 2\end{array}\right.\ \leq\exp\left(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\mathsf{D}}\right).

Recall that there is
N\in\mathbb{N}

for all

\exp(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\mathsf{D}})\leq\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}^{-\frac{2}{\mathsf{D}}}n^{-2}
.
Hence,

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\left(1-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\,\mathsf{D}}{2}-\frac{{d_{\textnormal{par}}}}{2\max\{2,{d_{\textnormal{par}}}\}}}\right)^{\!a_{n}}\leq\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}n^{\mathsf{D}}\right)\leq\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\mathsf{M}_{{}_{2}}^{-\frac{2}{\mathsf{D}}}n^{-2}

and thus

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)>\varepsilon_{n}\right)<\infty.

Finally, note that

\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}(\cdot,x)\geq\varepsilon_{n})\leq\left(1-c\cdot\tfrac{n^{\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}}}{a_{n}}\right)^{\!a_{n}}=\left(1-c\cdot n^{\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}-1}\right)^{a_{n}}

Remark 18
(Empirical distribution).

The empirical measure defined as

\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}

(4.3)

satisfies

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
as
n\rightarrow\infty

almost surely, see
[
20
, Theorem 3]
. Here
\Rightarrow
denotes the weak convergence of measures according to the weak-
∗
convergence in dual space theory, i.e.,

\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu\quad\text{iff}\quad\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\mu_{n}(dx)\rightarrow\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\mu(dx)\quad\forall f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}).

See for instance
[
5
]
for the empirical distribution and
[
8
, Section 7.3]
for a functional analytical perspective on weak-* convergence in the function space setting discussed.

Since this property of
\mu_{n}
is all we need in the following proofs and since the measures

\mu^{\textnormal{eh}}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\mu(\widetilde{M}_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{\textnormal{ih}}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{j_{i}}}\mu_{\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor}(\widetilde{M}_{j_{i}})\ ,

corresponding to exact hybrid weights and inexact hybrid weights respectively, also satisfy
{\mu^{\textnormal{eh}}_{n}\Rightarrow\mu}
and
{\mu^{\textnormal{ih}}_{n}\Rightarrow\mu}
, we will w.l.o.g. work with empirical weights only.

Due to the Lipschitz continuity of
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
as defined in
6
, the expected value
\nabla J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,X)]
is for
n\rightarrow\infty
increasingly better approximated by
\hat{G}_{n}
:

Theorem 19
(Error in gradient approximation).

The norm of the difference between the search direction
\hat{G}_{n}
and the gradient of the objective functional
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n},X)]
vanishes for
n\rightarrow\infty
, i.e.,

\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n},X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n},X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\right]=0.

Proof.

For
x\in\textnormal{supp}(\mu)
define

k^{n}(\omega;x):=\argmin_{k=1,\ldots,n}d\big((\Theta_{k}(\omega),X_{k}(\omega)),(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\big).

For
\hat{G}_{n}
as generated by
Algorithm

1
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
arbitrary but fixed, the following holds:

\displaystyle\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n},X)]\|_{{}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigg\|\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta_{k^{n}(\omega;x)}(i){\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{i}(\omega),x_{i})\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\|_{{}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\bigg\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{k^{n}(\omega;x)}(i){\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{i}(\omega),x_{i})-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\|_{{}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\bigg\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\|_{{}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)+\bigg\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\|_{{}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

(4.4)

where
\mu_{n}
is the empirical measure given in (
4.3
) and
\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
the Lipschitz constant defined in
6
. We need to prove that both terms in (
4.4
) vanish for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

For the first term, the uniform (in
n
) Lipschitz continuity of
Z_{n}(\omega,\cdot)
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathsf{L}_{{}_{Z}}d_{W}(\mu_{n},\mu),

where
d_{W}
denotes the Wasserstein distance of the measure
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
(see
[
10
]
). Since
\mathcal{X}
is bounded,
[
10
, Theorem 6]
ensures that the Wasserstein distance metrizices the weak topology on the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
. Since
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
almost surely, this gives

d_{W}(\mu_{n},\mu)\rightarrow 0
almost surely. Furthermore, by
6
, there exists
C>0
s.t.
0\leq Z_{n}\leq C
. Using
Corollary

17
, we obtain
Z_{n}(\omega,x)\to 0
for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. Therefore, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem yields

\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z_{n}(\omega,x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\to 0\quad\text{for almost all }\omega\in\Omega.

To show that the second part of (
4.4
) vanishes, observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\right\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)Q_{n}(d(x,x^{\prime}))\right\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\mathcal{X}}}Q_{n}(d(x,x^{\prime})),

where
Q_{n}(\cdot\times{\mathcal{X}})=\mu_{n}
and
Q_{n}({\mathcal{X}}\times\cdot)=\mu
is an arbitrary but fixed coupling of
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
. By taking the infimum of all such couplings, we again obtain the Wasserstein distance
d_{W}
of the measure
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
, i.e.,

\left\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n}(\omega),x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\right\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\leq\mathsf{L}_{{}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}d_{W}(\mu_{n},\mu).

(4.5)

By the same arguments detailed earlier,
d_{W}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
almost surely. Combining all the above yields

\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\Theta_{n},X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\rightarrow 0

almost surely. Since the above quantities are bounded, the almost sure convergence also implies the convergence in expectation via Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.

Remark 20

n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log(1+n),&\quad{d_{\textnormal{par}}}=2,\\

\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\leq\hat{C}({d_{\textnormal{par}}})\cdot M_{3}\cdot n^{-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}\log(1+n),

(4.13)

with

M_{3}:=\big(\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|^{3}_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)\big)^{{}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}
.

Substituting (
4.9
) and (
4.13
) into (

) yields

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\tau_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\right]\leq\bar{c}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\tau_{n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}+\hat{C}({d_{\textnormal{par}}})M_{3}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\tau_{n}n^{-\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}\log(1+n)

(4.14)

for
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough. By
14
, we have

\tau_{n}\leq{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}n^{-1-\mathsf{D}+\frac{1}{\max\{{d_{\textnormal{par}}},2\}}}.

When this is inserted into (
4.14
), we have

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\tau_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\right]\leq\bar{c}{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}n^{-1-\frac{\mathsf{D}}{2}}+\hat{C}({d_{\textnormal{par}}})M_{3}{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}n^{-1-\mathsf{D}}\log(1+n),

showing that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{G}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\theta,X)]\|_{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}\right]\leq\infty.

Before we can present our main result, we now collect a few auxiliary results.

Lemma 22
(Collection of auxiliary results).

(a)

The objective functional value in iteration
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies

J_{n+1}-J_{n}\leq-\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}\|\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}^{2}+\phi_{n},

where
\phi_{n}:=\tau_{n}\|\nabla J_{n}-\hat{G}_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}\|\hat{G}_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}+\tau_{n}^{2}C\|\hat{G}_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}^{2}
and

denotes a constant depending only on the Lipschitz constants and suprema of the functions involved.

(b)

For
\phi_{n}
as defined above, it holds that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{n}]<\infty.

(c)

For all
t\geq 0
, we have

\|{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(\theta_{n}-t\hat{G}_{n})-\theta_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}\leq\tfrac{t}{\tau_{n}}\|\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}\|_{{{}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}}.

Proof.

Assertions (a), (b) and (c) correspond to Lemma 16, Corollary 17 and Lemma 18 in
[
16
]
. Note that, by
Theorem

21
, the proofs provided in
[
16
]

Theorem 23
(Main theorem).

Let

On the other hand, assume the time-step series

To prove the first part, we show

For arbitrary

\displaystyle\|{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}}(

Therefore, it holds that

5
Numerical Results

In this section, we compare the CSG methods to suitable algorithms from the literature in three different settings. The comparison is based on the number of gradient evaluations, since these represent the time-consuming computations in complex optimization tasks.

5.1
Comparison with SG

To start our numerical analysis, we consider the problem

\min_{\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(x-\theta)^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

where
{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{X}=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
.

To study the behavior of our algorithm, we choose four different stepsizes (
n^{-1}
,
n^{-2/3}
,
n^{-1/3}
and a constant stepsize of
1
) and track the absolute error in each iteration
|\theta_{n}-\theta^{\ast}|
. To ensure meaningful results, the 10000 starting points were chosen randomly in
{\mathcal{P}}
. For comparison, we do the same for the ordinary stochastic gradient descent method (SG), since it is commonly used for problems resembling our example.

Figure 2:
Comparison of the absolute error
|\theta_{n}-\theta^{\ast}|
for SG (red), CSG with empirical weights (green), exact hybrid CSG (cyan) and exact CSG (yellow).

Note that, in contrast to SG, a larger stepsize does not worsen the performance of the CSG algorithms for our example. A constant stepsize leads to a faster convergence for the hybrid and exact CSG method, whereas SG fails to solve the problem.

5.2
Comparison with SCGD

As mentioned in
Remark

3
, the vanishing error in inner function value approximations allows us to solve optimization problems in which the cost function depends non-linearly on a suitable expectation value.
For instance, we may solve the problem

\min_{\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}}\quad\frac{1}{20}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(2y+5\int_{\mathcal{X}}\cos\Big(\frac{\theta-x}{\pi}\Big)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}y,

where

\mathcal{X}=[-1,1]
and

problem (
5.1

\min_{\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}\big[f_{y}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}}[g_{x}(\theta)])\big].

(5.2)

Since

is non-linear, the SG algorithm cannot be used to solve (

[
21

), which is specifically designed for problems of the form (
5.2
).

Again, the 1000 starting points are randomly generated. This time, however, we draw the starting points only from the interval
[\frac{11}{2},\frac{19}{2}]
rather than
{\mathcal{P}}=[0,10]
. The reason for this is that the optimal solution
\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\approx 4.935

Since the objective function in (

[
21
]
) performed better than the standard version. Therefore, we compared our results to the aSCGD algorithm and chose the optimal stepsizes for aSCGD according to Theorem 7 in
[
21
]
. For the hybrid, inexact hybrid and empirical CSG algorithm, we chose a constant stepsize of
\frac{1}{30}

5.3
Chance constraint problems

As a prototype example for chance constraint problems, we consider

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in[0,\frac{3}{4}]}

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta-X^{2}\leq 0)\geq\frac{1}{2},\quad X\sim\mathcal{U}_{[-1,1]},

with optimal solution
\theta^{\ast}=\frac{1}{4}
. By introducing the characteristic function
\chi_{[0,\infty)}
and transforming the constraint to a penalty term, we arrive at

\max_{\theta\in[0,\frac{3}{4}]}\quad\theta-\lambda\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\theta-x^{2})\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

Since the penalized objective function is no longer continuously differentiable, we cannot guarantee the existence of a gradient and will have to work with subgradients instead, cf.
[
2
]
. Note that the proofs provided above also hold true for a subgradient method if the stepsize is chosen accordingly.
While the computation of a (sub-)gradient of
\max\{0,\cdot\}
is not an issue,
\chi_{[0,\infty)}
needs to be regularized further. The final problem then reads as follows:

\max_{\theta\in[0,\frac{3}{4}]}\quad\theta-\lambda\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\big((\tanh\big(\alpha(\theta-x^{2})\big)+1\big)\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

(5.3)

Due to the non-linearity of
\max\{0,\cdot\}
, we again choose the SCGD method for comparison. This time, the objective function is not strongly convex in a neighborhood of
\theta_{opt}
. Therefore, the stepsizes for the standard SCGD method are chosen according to Theorem 6 in
[
21
]
, i.e., optimal for this setting. For the CSG algorithms, we choose
\tau_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. Lastly, we fix
\lambda=3
and
\alpha=25
. The optimal solution
\theta_{opt}
to (
5.3
) then satisfies
|\theta^{\ast}-\theta_{opt}|<1.5\cdot 10^{-3}
. The results of 1000 runs with random starting points in
[0,\frac{3}{4}]
are presented in

6
Conclusion and Outlook

This article introduced a more flexible way to compute design dependent integration weights for the efficient approximation of the full cost function and its gradient when applying the CSG method to a class of stochastic optimization problems. While this approach significantly widened the scope of the CSG method, a number of interesting research questions remain to be investigated in the future. First, as a consequence of the strong convergence properties shown in this paper, the CSG method – in the course of the optimization iterations – behaves more and more like a fully deterministic descent method. This calls for more elaborate techniques to calculate the step length, e.g. linesearch or trust region strategies. Another interesting question is whether convergence of the iterates generated by the CSG method can be shown for a constant choice of step size. The numerical examples we have presented in this paper suggest that this should indeed be possible. And finally, exploiting specific structures of the given probability distributions could provide even more efficient integration techniques, enabling problems with high dimensional distributions to be solved more efficiently than using the empirical weight strategy presented in this article.
