A Conversational Brain-Artificial Intelligence Interface
Anja Meunier, Michal Robert Žák, Lucas Munz, Sofiya Garkot, Manuel Eder, Jiachen Xu, Moritz Grosse-Wentrup

Abstract
We introduce Brain-Artificial Intelligence Interfaces (BAIs) as a new class of Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs). Unlike conventional BCIs, which rely on intact cognitive capabilities, BAIs leverage the power of artificial intelligence to replace parts of the neuro-cognitive processing pipeline. BAIs allow users to accomplish complex tasks by providing high-level intentions, while a pre-trained AI agent determines low-level details. This approach enlarges the target audience of BCIs to individuals with cognitive impairments, a population often excluded from the benefits of conventional BCIs. We present the general concept of BAIs and illustrate the potential of this new approach with a Conversational BAI based on EEG. In particular, we show in an experiment with simulated phone conversations that the Conversational BAI enables complex communication without the need to generate language. Our work thus demonstrates, for the first time, the ability of a speech neuroprosthesis to enable fluent communication in realistic scenarios with non-invasive technologies.



1
Brain-Artificial Intelligence Interfaces

Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) are systems that
give their users communication and control channels that do not depend on the brain’s normal output channels of peripheral nerves and muscles

[
1
]
. BCIs are used in a variety of contexts, e.g., to provide a communication channel to severely paralyzed patients
[
2
]
, to adapt human-computer interaction systems to their users’ mental states
[
3
]
, and to support post-stroke motor rehabilitation
[
4
,
5
]
. When used as assistive communication tools, BCIs typically build on intact cortical processes to generate the command signals. In other words, they presume that the user is cognitively capable of executing the desired task, and the BCI merely bridges the severed connection between the brain and the outside world. However, more than
40\%
of first-ever stroke patients, a group that arguably stands to benefit tremendously from assistive technologies, exhibit cognitive impairments
[
6
]
and about one-third of stroke patients show symptoms of aphasia, i.e., deficits in language expression and comprehension
[
7
]
. These deficits often result from extensive cortical brain damage. Patients with extensive cortical damage are unlikely to benefit from BCI systems that attempt to read out information from brain areas that have been directly affected or even destroyed by the stroke. For example, a stroke patient with a middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarct is unlikely to benefit from a BCI that attempts to reconstruct speech by decoding movements of vocal tract muscles from the laryngeal motor cortex, a cortical area typically affected by such an infarct. To build assistive communication devices for patients with extensive cortical damage and cognitive deficits, we must consider the feasibility of using neurotechnology to
replace
lost cognitive functions.

Due to tremendous advances in artificial intelligence (AI) in the past decade, AI systems trained on enormous amounts of data are now able to perform a broad range of cognitive tasks on a level similar to or even exceeding human performance, e.g., in image-
[
8
]
and speech recognition
[
9
]
, strategic planning
[
10
,
11
]
, and language generation
[
12
]
. By using existing BCI technologies to interface with such AI systems, they can be leveraged to perform cognitive tasks that the patient has lost. We term this new class of systems Brain-Artificial Intelligence Interfaces (BAIs) to distinguish them from classical BCIs that merely attempt to (re-)connect a fully functional mind with the outside world.

By delegating some parts of the cortical processing pipeline to an AI agent, BAIs replicate the natural division of responsibilities when performing complex cognitive tasks. Humans typically control higher-level functions deliberately, while lower-level processes are automated. When speaking, for example, the exact choice of words is automated to a large extent in natural conversation, while the content of the produced speech underlies executive control
[
13
,
14
]
. Similarly, in a BAI, the user’s intentions are fed into the AI system, thus steering it in a particular direction, while the details of the response are determined by the AI system’s extensive experience in the given task. Thus, the BAI user controls the high-level intention, while a pre-trained AI agent automatically fills in the low-level details.

The idea of feeding a BCI user’s high-level intentions to an automated system that determines lower-level details has already been successfully applied in the area of robotic arm control
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. As artificial intelligence capabilities continue to grow, we anticipate that BCIs that rely on sharing control between users and AI agents will become increasingly sophisticated and widespread throughout established and emerging areas of BCI usage. We, therefore, argue that an overarching and general characterization of this new class of BCI systems beyond motor neuroprostheses is in order.

The generic structure of and information flow in a BAI are shown in Figure
1
. Its primary components are contextual input, cognitive probing
[
20
,
21
]
, intention decoding, and action generation. The contextual input component represents how the BAI receives knowledge about the surroundings that the BAI user is experiencing and wants to react to. This is important because behavior always occurs within a contextual environment, and knowledge of this context provides information about appropriate reactions. For example, questions by a conversation partner represent relevant contextual information, which must be supplied to a Conversational BAI as input. After the BAI has gained information about the context the user wants to react to, it must gather information about the user’s intentions. It probes the user for relevant information, and the intentions of the user are subsequently decoded from the user’s neurophysiological data. The chosen cognitive probing approach and the selected method of intention decoding must be closely integrated. In the ideal, most intuitive case, whenever the BAI needs additional information to generate an action, it probes the user by directly decoding their intentions from the neurophysiological data. The challenges associated with this optimal approach, which stem from the current scarcity of suitably specialized AI agents and the complexity of decoding user intentions directly, are explored below with an emphasis on Conversational BAIs. The final step in closing the loop of the interaction between the outside world and the user is action generation. After identifying the user’s intention, the BAI generates an action that best matches the user’s desired action. As a result, the BAI user gains the capability to execute their intentions and influence their surroundings through the BAI, effectively exercising agency and closing the feedback loop with the environment.

In the following, we demonstrate the power of this novel class of systems in a Conversational BAI, a class of BAIs designed to aid users with severe language impairments. We evaluate our system in an experiment with simulated phone conversations and demonstrate the efficacy of Conversational BAIs for accomplishing everyday communication tasks.

2
Conversational BAIs

The importance of language as a means of human communication cannot be overstated. Its loss can be a profoundly life-altering experience with significant implications for an individual’s mental health and overall quality of life
[
22
]
. Recent years have witnessed remarkable advancements in language-related artificial intelligence, primarily owing to large language models (LLMs) based on transformer architectures
[
23
,
12
,
24
,
25
]
. These models now stand at the forefront of AI development, capable of generating human-level language. Our goal is to leverage this ability for the development of intuitive neuroprostheses for patients with severe language impairments: Conversational BAIs.

Conversational BAIs empower users to communicate verbally by relying on a sophisticated conversational agent to handle the intricacies of language generation. When presented with a question as contextual input, the conversational agent probes the user for the general intent of their response and subsequently generates grammatically sound, contextually appropriate responses that align with the user’s intention.

One of the central challenges in developing Conversational BAIs revolves around selecting and adapting the conversational agent, as BAIs rely heavily on the quality of the AI agent at their core. State-of-the-art LLMs are trained on extensive corpora of natural language data, and conversational agents are typically fine-tuned on conversation data
[
12
,
26
,
27
]
. They generate responses by randomly selecting from a learned distribution of words or sub-words, conditioned on the input prompt and previously generated tokens. However, such systems are not constrained to produce a particular intention in their answers. For example, when prompted, ”How is the weather today?” two plausible answers may be ”It is very nice and sunny today.” or ”Today it is cold and cloudy.” State-of-the-art conversational agents are trained to produce coherent and probable answers but are not specialized to receive information about and subsequently represent a particular viewpoint in a conversation. Ultimately, developing a new class of conversational agents optimized to speak
for
, rather than
with
someone, is crucial to reaching the full potential of Conversational BAIs.

Another critical aspect of Conversational BAI design involves determining the optimal point in the neuronal processing pipeline for decoding the user’s intentions. An ideal decoding approach considers how much control over the details of the response the BAI user can still retain on their own. This highly depends on which cortical processes remain intact. For instance, an individual with anarthria, the complete inability to perform speech movements, for example, due to a stroke or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), is typically still able to generate imagined speech. In recent years, complex decoding methods such as sequence-to-sequence neural networks have led to remarkable results in speech decoding. Vocalized, imagined, or attempted speech can be synthesized from intracortical microelectrode array data
[
28
,
29
]
or ECoG recordings
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
, enabling severely paralyzed patients to communicate. However, this approach fails for patients who struggle to come up with the words to express their mental concepts, in particular stroke patients with aphasia.

Therefore, developing novel specialized decoding methods for this patient group is paramount. In an ideal scenario, this means directly decoding user intentions much earlier in the cortical processing pipeline. However, this requires sufficiently granular neurophysiological data and an in-depth understanding of the cortical language processing pipeline. How mental concepts are represented in the early stages of cortical language processing and whether aphasic patients’ speech intentions can be decoded from such neuronal representations is yet unclear and remains an open area of research.

As an alternative non-invasive, less direct method for cognitive probing and decoding, we propose and test an approach where the conversational agent generates multiple suggestions in response to the contextual input, with the BAI user selecting among them using classic BCI paradigms, for example, code-modulated visually evoked potentials
[
33
]
. Selecting from the suggested responses allows the user to express a wide range of concepts, even when more complex neurophysiological signals are unavailable.

In the following, we introduce the first Conversational BAI based on EEG and present its evaluation in simulated phone conversations. We describe its components (Section
2.1
), the experimental design (Section
2.2
), and present the results of our analysis (Section
2.3
).

2.1
EEGChat: A non-invasive Conversational BAI

EEGChat is a proof-of-concept Conversational BAI demonstrating the possibilities of this technology. Its versatility allows users to engage in conversations on any topic via an indirect cognitive probing approach based on non-invasive EEG recordings. A demonstration video showcasing it in action can be found in the supplementary materials.

Contextual input

When a conversation partner asks the BAI user a question, the first step is to input this contextual information into the system. We achieve this with automatic speech-to-text transcription, as shown in Figure
2
.A. The transcribed question is also displayed for the user on a screen, as shown in Figure
2
.B. If applicable, EEGChat retains the conversation history of previous question and answer pairs.

Cognitive probing

Upon receiving the transcribed question, the BAI must obtain information about the user’s intention. Due to the difficulty of directly decoding user intentions from EEG data and the limitations of current conversational AIs, we chose an indirect cognitive probing method that allows the user to express a wide range of concepts. The conversational agent automatically generates several short answers (subsequently referred to as keywords) by querying the large language model GPT-3.5
[
12
]
. We developed a prompt to generate a variety of different answers that are relevant to the given question. Section
4.2.1
provides details of the keyword generation process.

The keywords are displayed to the user on the screen, as shown in Figure
2
.B. The number of presented keywords is adaptable. We chose a layout displaying six keyword options for the evaluation experiment (Section
2.2
). Additionally, the user is shown four special options to be able to correct mistakes (”Correction”), show more keywords (”More”), choose no keyword, and reply with ”I am sorry, I cannot answer this question right now” (”None”), or end the conversation (”Finished”).

To address questions requiring specific details like names and addresses, we integrated a knowledge base into EEGChat that enables users to provide personal information a-priori that they may want to disclose during a subsequent conversation. During keyword generation, we prompted the LLM to assign each question a category, e.g., NAME, NUMBER, or YESNO. If the category matches a knowledge base entry, the respective entries are displayed as choices instead of the generated keywords. In our experiment, the knowledge base included fictional names and addresses. In a real-world setting, users would fill these out in advance, understanding that this data might be shared with conversation partners.

Figure 2
:
Structure
(A)
and screenshot
(B)
of EEGChat, the first Conversational BAI. One question-answer exchange occurs as follows: The conversation partner asks a question, which is automatically transcribed and supplied to both the user and BAI (displayed in the top left box on the screen). The Conversational BAI generates short possible answers and displays them to the user. Additionally, there are four special options for the user to correct mistakes (”Correction”), show more keywords (”More”), choose no keyword (”None”), or end the conversation (”Finished”). After reading the options, the user focuses on their chosen answer during a code-VEP stimulation phase. The choice is decoded from EEG and supplied to the BAI. The BAI then generates an appropriate answer to the question using the conversation history and chosen keyword. This full-sentence answer is displayed on the bottom left of the screen and played as a text-to-speech generated audio. The current scenario goal was displayed at the top of the screen during the experiment.

Intention decoding

The next step is to infer from the user’s neurophysiological data which of the ten options (six keywords and four special options) the user intends to choose. For this, we use a code-modulated visually evoked potential (code-VEP) paradigm
[
34
,
35
]
with a decoding pipeline based on the work by
[
33
]
. Details of the decoding pipeline are described in Section
4.3
. After the user has had time to read the options, the boxes containing the stimuli start flickering in an on-off pattern with an individual time shift for every stimulus. The user focuses their attention on their choice, which elicits a corresponding pattern of VEPs in occipital EEG channels from which the attended keyword can be inferred.

Action generation

After the BAI is aware of the user’s intention, a full-sentence answer that matches the conversation history and the intention of the chosen keyword must be generated. We adapted the language model GPT-3.5
[
12
]
by fine-tuning it on a custom data set of conversations annotated with relevant keywords. This strongly improved the ability of the AI agent to identify which viewpoint it should adopt in the conversation as well as decrease the amount of additional wrong information included in the generated answers, compared to a non-tuned version. Refer to Section
4.2.2
for a detailed description of the data sets, fine-tuning approach, and model evaluation.

This final response is displayed on the screen as shown in Figure
2
.B and played as an audio output generated by a text-to-speech model. Upon hearing the BAI user’s answer, the conversation partner can respond with another question to continue the conversation.

2.2
Experimental design

We designed an empirical study to test the applicability of our system in realistic situations. The subject uses EEGChat in a simulated phone conversation to achieve a predetermined goal, such as reserving a table in a restaurant. We emphasize that EEGChat was not trained on any of the chosen scenarios to ensure that the reported success rate in achieving a conversational goal generalizes to novel, real-world scenarios. As shown in Figure
3
, the experiment consists of three parts: The training phase, the conversational task evaluation phase, and the post-evaluation phase.

Figure 3
:
A detailed view of the experiment, consisting of a classifier training and UI familiarization phase, an evaluation scenario phase, and a classifier accuracy evaluation phase. The structure of each scenario is shown on the right.

Classifier and user training

The training data for the code-VEP decoder is collected while the experimenter guides the subject interactively through the user interface. The subject is instructed in this part with tasks such as ”Please select ’None’ now” or simple questions such as ”Hello, how are you doing today?”. The participant focuses their attention on their choice during the maximal stimulation time of 10.85 seconds (217 frames). Subsequently, and only in this part of the experiment, the subject clicks on their selection to provide the true label for classifier training.

The questions are semi-scripted and can be adapted to ensure the participant understands how to use the BAI and that the classifier has reached a good accuracy. Every participant made at least seven interactions with the system, therefore providing at least 1519 frames of training data.

After the classifier training, the subject completes a short practice scenario. Here, the subject witnesses how the system behaves during an evaluation scenario and tests if they can control it without the experimenter’s guidance. The fully trained classifier is used for the first time in this scenario.

Conversational tasks

For each conversational task, the participant is instructed to complete realistic everyday scenarios using only the BAI (see Figure
3
for the full list of scenarios). The subject receives a detailed goal description, whereas the conversation partner only knows the general topic of the task. For example, the conversation partner may be aware that the subject is calling a pizzeria, while the subject is also instructed to reserve a table for a specific number of people, time, and day. The experimenter speaks the first sentence of each scenario. They simulate picking up the phone and introduce themselves with a phrase such as ”Pizzeria Romano, how can I help you?”. Introducing the scenario in this way is necessary as it gives the BAI the relevant context of having called a pizzeria, something the subject, but not the system knows. To complete the scenario, the participant must successfully convey all relevant details to the experimenter during the conversation within at most 30 selections.

Accuracy evaluation & questionnaire

After completing the conversational tasks, we assess the accuracy of the trained classifier. Subjects answer 20 simple, predetermined questions with obvious answers, such as ”What color is an elephant?”. Finally, the subjects are asked to complete a questionnaire asking about their experience using the BAI.

Experimental results

Five healthy subjects (labeled S1 to S5) attempted the six conversation tasks shown in Figure

Successful tasks were completed in an average of 8.68 utterances, with a standard deviation of 3.43. As shown in Figure

3
Discussion

Brain-Artificial Intelligence Interfaces establish a novel research direction in the area of neuroprostheses. By delegating significant parts of the planning and generation of actions to an AI agent while leaving high-level control with the user, complex actions can be taken with relatively little information extracted from neurophysiological data. In the case of EEGChat, the BAI approach enables users to engage in complex, open-topic conversations, all while utilizing EEG data and relying on relatively simple decoding paradigms. This is a significant advancement over earlier work in (invasive) BCI speech prostheses, which achieved a decoding performance that is sufficiently high for fluent conversation on small vocabularies only, limiting users’ ability to engage in complex, open-topic conversations
[
29
]
.

Furthermore, by replacing parts of the cognitive processing pipeline, people with severe cortical deficits who are not served by more traditional BCI architectures can benefit from this new type of assistive technology. Aphasia, the loss of the ability to comprehend or formulate language, for example, ranks among the health conditions most detrimental to an individual’s health-related quality of life
[
37
]
. The prospect of restoring such lost cognitive functions with the aid of AI substantially enlarges the target group that can benefit from this technology.

While advances in AI in general and in LLMs in particular have been revolutionary, LLMs are currently generic text generators. Their performance is likely to improve dramatically when fine-tuned with task-specific training data. The same applies to LLMs used in Conversational BAIs. Fine-tuning these models with user-specific conversational data, e.g., as obtained from past social media and email communications, is likely to dramatically improve their performance and replicate users’ individual communication styles. At the same time, novel recording techniques for brain imaging and improved decoding pipelines may eventually eliminate the need for explicit cognitive probing, allowing for implicit, intuitive control of Conversational BAIs. However, the potential of BAIs extends beyond the replacement of lost cognitive functions. Leveraging AI agents to extend human capabilities may be an interesting future application area for BAIs. As research of AI agents in established and emerging fields progresses, it will likely enhance the efficacy and potential of BAIs, transforming how we communicate, perceive, and interact with technology.

At the same time, the highly innovative nature and possible impact of BAIs warrant a careful consideration of ethical implications. For instance, it must be ensured that users control the information they share via a BAI, similar to natural speech, and can even lie if they wish to do so. Further, integrating AIs into neuroprostheses renders them vulnerable to adversarial attacks, e.g., based on prompt-injection
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
. Finally, users relying on such technology for communication might want to take legally binding actions. Determining the responsibility for actions conducted with a BAI is critical.

Despite these open questions, BAIs hold immense potential for enhancing the quality of life for numerous individuals. The work presented here paves the way for new avenues of research in developing advanced assistive devices, benefiting a large, underserved population.

4
Methods

This section offers a comprehensive overview of the components employed in EEGChat. In Section
4.1
, we first outline our selection rationale in choosing the models utilized in audio processing of the speech in- and output. Subsequently, we delve into the methodologies employed for answer generations, encompassing a keyword generator for providing answer options during cognitive probing (Section
4.2.1
) and a full-sentence generator (Section
4.2.2
) that expands the selected keyword to a full answer sentence. Finally, in Section
4.3
, we discuss the rationale behind our adoption of code-VEP as the decoding pipeline and the tailored modification applied to align the decoder with our paradigm.

4.1
Audio processing

Sentences spoken by conversation partners must the transcribed to text to interface with the large-language-models (LLMs). To achieve this, each trial begins with an eight-second window, during which the conversation partner speaks a question into the microphone. This recording, sampled at 16 kHz, undergoes transcription using the Google Cloud Speech API via the SpeechRecognition Python library
[
41
]
. Should the transcription fail, EEGChat asks the conversation partner to repeat their question. While various transcription models exist, we opted for the Google Cloud Speech API for its simplicity and satisfactory performance.

Conversely, LLMs exclusively produce textual output. To convert this text into audible responses, we employed the Google Cloud Text-to-Speech API. This API, which given text returns an audio file with the text spoken by a synthetic voice, was chosen for its simplicity and effectiveness. While the current model suffices, exploring a customized version mimicking the sound of the user’s voice remains an avenue for improvement.

4.2
Text generation

In the subsequent sections, we explain the details of the conversational agent at the core of EEGChat. Our system leverages transformer models, a class of neural networks first proposed by
[
23
]
. They rely on the attention mechanism without any convolutional or recurrent units in the neural network. Their self-attention mechanism aggregates information from the input sequence, with the ability to handle long-term dependencies with no information loss even for the most distant parts in the input sequence
[
42
]
.

The core system components employ the state-of-the-art language models available at the time of their creation each. For the keyword generation, OpenAI’s GPT-3.5
[
12
]
is used, whereas, due to its earlier development, the full-sentence generation utilizes OpenAI’s GPT-3 Davinci. Both models underwent testing with both modification techniques available, fine-tuning, and prompt-engineering. Ultimately, we adopted a prompt-engineered solution in the keyword generation and a custom fine-tuned version to produce the full-sentence answers, which we describe below.

4.2.1
Keywords

Keyword generation

The keyword options presented to the user are generated by a prompt-engineered (purposeful and systematic prompt designed) GPT-3.5 model, the state-of-the-art language model at the time. We found that for our use case, a balance between minimizing model randomness (lower temperature) and allowing for creativity in responses (higher temperature) by setting the model temperature to
0.5
worked well. The engineered prompt comprises a paragraph describing the problem setting, conveying the task of generating keywords and the context – assisting a speech-impaired person by providing responses to questions. To maintain consistency and guide the model, the prompt further includes examples of prompts and outputs. At the end, the prompt must be extended with a question and the desired number of generated keywords (twelve in our setting). For each query, the LLM outputs the specified number of keywords, as well as a category for the generated keywords, such as NAME, NUMBER, or YESNO, which is utilized in the knowledge base. The exact formulation of the prompt is included in the supplementary material.

Knowledge base

To give the user access to personal information, such as important addresses (e.g., home address or their doctor’s address), EEGChat incorporates a knowledge base. The knowledge base is a dictionary mapping category strings to relevant options for that category. If the category returned by the keyword generation matches an entry in the knowledge base, the respective entries will be used as keyword options instead of the ones generated by the language model. During the experiment, the knowledge base contained entries for the categories NAME and ADDRESS, with lists of names and addresses vital for scenario completion during the experiment.

Special keyword options

In addition to six keywords, EEGChat always displays four other options on the right side of the screen (see Figure
2
(B)). These options assist the user in constructing an answer if none of the generated keywords present a suitable choice. The special options consist of:

•

Correction:
EEGChat answers, ”I am sorry, I misspoke earlier,” giving the conversation partner the chance to repeat the previous question.

•

More / Previous:
Replace the six keywords with another 6 options / Show the previous six keywords

•

None:
The question cannot be answered with any of the twelve keywords (six original and six after clicking ’More’). EEGChat answers, ”I am sorry, I cannot answer this question right now.”

•

Finished:
The user has achieved their goal and wants to end the conversation. EEGChat answers, ”Thank you, goodbye.” After ’Finished’ is selected, the scenario ends, and a new scenario begins.

4.2.2
Full sentence response generation

To generate coherent and contextually relevant sentences from keywords, we employed GPT-3 Davinci, the latest GPT model available during the development of this component. GPT-3 is not inherently designed for generating sentences from keywords, necessitating additional steps to make the model behave in the desired way. These steps involve either a purposeful and systematic prompt design (prompt engineering) or creating a data set representative of the problem, which is then used to adjust the underlying model’s weights (fine-tuning). Moreover, when these sentences are employed to speak for a user (instead of with them), it is crucial to establish strict criteria for the generated content. For instance, generating sentences with added information may lead to the user expressing opinions they do not agree with, potentially resulting in uncomfortable situations.

In the following paragraphs, we delve into the specifics of prompt engineering and the fine-tuning procedures. We created five systems, employing different iterations of both techniques. To determine the optimal model, we established an evaluation process where recruited individuals assess response quality and identify model errors.

Training data for fine-tuning

To construct the data set used for fine-tuning, we utilized the corpus summarized in the GitHub repository at
https://github.com/zeloru/small-english-smalltalk-corpus
as a foundation. This corpus comprises multiple English small-talk conversations, primarily transcriptions of English lesson conversation examples. This makes the corpus ideal for our use case, as most of the sentences are dialogues and, as the sentences are aimed at English learners, they tend to be rather simple, reducing complexity.

Before constructing any samples, we manually filtered the corpus, removing irrelevant and too short conversations, resulting in approximately 890 usable conversations, each comprising question-answer (QA) pairs between two individuals. These conversation were then used to construct three distinct data sets: FT-XL, a large data set using all 890 conversations; FT-CR, a more creative data set using 520 of the conversations, made more creative by providing fewer keywords (further discussed later); and FT-HQ, a high-quality data set, additionally edited by humans, comprising of 330 conversations.

The QA pairs from these conversations served as the foundation for our data set samples. Each sample of the data set includes all information available to the system during a potential deployment: a question posed by the conversation partner, one or more keywords for answer generation, an optional dialogue history consisting of past QA pairs, and, as the targeted output, the corresponding answer sentence. To prevent the training of overly lengthy responses, we introduced a condition discarding samples with answer sentences exceeding 110 characters. Further, we focused on shorter conversations by distributing the conversation history in the data set as follows: no history - 50%, one QA pair of history - 35%, two QA pairs of history - 8%, three QA pairs of history - 7%.

Keywords, as they are not part of the corpus, needed to be generated additionally. We have done so by randomly selecting one of the automated keyword extraction technologies – YAKE
[
43
]
, RAKE
[
44
]
, and KeyBERT
[
45
]
. YAKE was additionally used with a setting to extract a few most relevant keywords (n=1, dedupLim=0.9) and another to extract a detailed list of keywords (n=5, dedupLim=0). If one of the methods was unable to extract any keywords, another was picked at random. If none of the methods were able to extract a keyword, the sample was rejected. Instead of the above described random picking, FT-CR always chose the result of the generator with the smallest number of keywords. To increase clarity and accuracy, contractions, such as
I’ll
or
won’t
, were expanded to their full forms
I will
and
will not

Fine-tuning

The three above described data sets have been used to create GPT-3 Davinci
[
12
]
fine-tunes. This model was chosen due to its status as the largest and best-performing model available at the time of development. The process of fine-tuning a GPT model involves adapting the pre-trained original model to a specific task. OpenAI provides flexibility in setting various hyperparameters during the fine-tuning process, including epochs, learning rate multiplier, and batch size. Through extensive experimentation with multiple settings, we decided on the following configuration for our use case: epochs - 1, learning rate multiplier - 0.05, and batch size - 2. This choice was made after careful consideration of the model’s performance and the specific requirements of our project.

Evaluation

We evaluated five distinct model versions, with three of them being trained on the data sets FT-XL, FT-CT, and FT-HQ, discussed previously. Additionally, we constructed two versions using prompt engineering, denoted as STOCK-1 and STOCK-2 (the exact prompts are included in the supplementary material). To assess the performance of these models, we designed an online experiment in which participants (recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk and other platforms) rated the model outputs.

The experiment was built around a web-based interface in which participants were tasked with evaluating responses generated by the different models. Each evaluation session began by introducing the participant to a predefined topic and presenting them with an initial question. For example: Topic -

4.3
Decoding intentions from EEG

In this section, we describe the methods used to decode the user’s intentions. First, we present the details of electroencephalography (EEG), then we explain the stimulus mechanism of code-modulated visually evoked potentials (code-VEPs) and why we chose it, and finally, we detail the machine-learning pipeline used to infer the chosen stimulus, consisting of a combination of a CNN and sequence matching approach.

4.3.1
EEG recording

Electroencephalography (EEG) is one of the most commonly used neurophysiological recording techniques for brain-computer interfaces due to its non-invasiveness, high temporal resolution, and good spatial coverage
[
46
]
. It measures electrical potentials via electrodes on the scalp. During our experiments, participants’ EEG was recorded with the channels POz, PO3, PO4, Oz, O9, and O10 at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz using a Brain Products EasyCap and Bittium NeurOne Tesla amplifiers. The electrode locations were selected due to their proximity to the visual cortex and following an analysis of the contribution to the prediction performance in a pilot subject for the code-VEP paradigm.

Figure 6
:

(C)
, where each frame is shown for 50 ms. The 250ms window of EEG data recorded during and after each frame presentation is fed into a CNN
(A)
. The CNN makes one prediction (on vs. off) per stimulus frame
(B)

(B, C)
on a rolling window basis
(D)
by computing the prediction accuracy
(E)
. If the accuracy of one stimulus reaches at least 0.8, while all others remain below 0.6, the respective stimulus is returned by the decoder.

4.3.2
Stimulus paradigm (code-VEPs)

The choice of stimulus paradigm was driven by several factors. Firstly, we needed a system with a short classification time, allowing the subject to quickly and easily choose the desired keywords. To be able to dynamically extend our system to arbitrary user interfaces, scalability to multiple classes was also considered. The chosen paradigm should also have a high classification accuracy and as short a training time as possible. Further, to ease setup, a system that requires fewer electrodes is preferred.

These criteria are all met with code-VEPs, which we have chosen as the paradigm for our experiment. Code-VEPs have shown a high accuracy with only one electrode in use
[
47
]
. However, better results are achieved if more electrodes are used
[
48
]
. A similar approach, steady state visually evoked potentials (SSVEPs), which flash the stimuli at constant frequencies, have a similar performance as code-VEPs
[
48
]
, but they, in general, do not scale well to multiple stimuli, as they are coupled to the monitor refresh rate. Meanwhile, code-VEPs can be easily extended to an arbitrary amount of stimuli by selecting codes with an autocorrelation of zero.

The physiological mechanism utilized in code-VEPs is the brain’s reaction to flashing visual stimuli. These visually evoked potentials (VEPs) are generally most strongly observed in the occipital lobe, where the brain’s visual cortex is located.
[
34
]
first proposed using this effect for BCIs, but only much later did the idea of code-modulated VEPs gain attention across the field
[
35
,
49
]
. During a code-VEP paradigm, a subject is presented with several stimuli, each flashing at a different on-off pattern. When the subject is focusing their attention on one of the stimuli, a corresponding pattern of evoked potentials can be observed in their EEG.

During our experiment, the stimuli (boxes with keywords) were flashed in a time-shifted maximum length sequence (m-sequence) pattern of length
m=31
. Maximum length sequences are binary pseudorandom sequences with the advantageous property of having a circular autocorrelation of zero for every shift
{n\in\{1,...,m-1\}}

[
35
]
. The stimulus pattern was flashed at a rate of 20Hz and 50% amplitude, which has been shown to reduce strain on the eyes with only minimal loss in decoding accuracy
[
50
,
51
]
. During each trial, the pattern was repeated a maximum of seven times, equaling at most 217 frames, or 10.85 seconds.

4.3.3
Real-time keyword choice decoding

During the flashing stimulus presentation, the choice of the user is decoded in real time using a two-step process. For every stimulus frame, a convolutional neural network (CNN) predicts whether the attended stimulus was on or off. This predicted sequence is matched continuously against the true stimulation patterns of all ten stimuli to determine which stimulus was attended to by the BAI user.

The CNN used to decode the sequence of on-off patterns is EEG2Code
[
33
]
, consisting of three convolution / max-pooling blocks and two dense layers. In contrast to to EEG2Code, we apply a window shift of 50 ms (flashing at 20 Hz), whereas they present a stimulus every 16.67 ms (flashing at 60 Hz). We predict each stimulus frame state from 250 ms (250 samples due to recording at 1000 Hz) windows of unfiltered EEG during and after the respective stimulus frame (50 ms + 200 ms), as shown in Figure
6
.A-B. The chosen window length is in accordance with the original application of EEG2Code, where it is argued that a VEP signal usually lasts about 250 ms.

After 16 frames have been presented (800 ms) and the full window of EEG of the 16
th
frame has been recorded (+200 ms), the predicted sequence is continuously compared to the ten stimulation sequences by computing the prediction accuracy. The accuracy is computed for a shifted window of the most recent 31 frames at most, as shown in Figure
6
.B-D. If the accuracy reaches at least 0.8 for one of the stimulus patterns, while the accuracy of all other sequences remains below 0.6, the decoder returns the respective stimulus ID, and stimulation stops (see Figure
6
.E). The tenth stimulus (”Finished”) is an exception since it ends the conversation. Erroneously choosing it can, therefore, not be recovered from through corrections. For it to be selected, the thresholds have to be crossed for a total of ten stimulus frames. If the thresholds are not met after seven repetitions of the stimulus patterns (10.85 s), the stimulus with the highest correlation to the predicted sequence is selected.
