Equivariant graph convolutional neural networks for the representation of homogenized anisotropic microstructural mechanical response
Ravi Patel, Cosmin Safta, Reese Jones

Abstract
Composite materials with different microstructural material symmetries are common in engineering applications where grain structure, alloying and particle/fiber packing are optimized via controlled manufacturing. In fact these microstructural tunings can be done throughout a part to achieve functional gradation and optimization at a structural level. To predict the performance of particular microstructural configuration and thereby overall performance, constitutive models of materials with microstructure are needed. In this work we provide neural network architectures that provide effective homogenization models of materials with anisotropic components. These models satisfy equivariance and material symmetry principles inherently through a combination of equivariant and tensor basis operations. We demonstrate them on datasets of stochastic volume elements with different textures and phases where the material undergoes elastic and plastic deformation, and show that the these network architectures provide significant performance improvements.



1
Introduction

Symmetry preservation is a key to effective computational physics.
The success of symplectic integrators
[
1
,
2
]
in predicting long time dynamics is one notable example.
In fact, symmetries and other
a priori
exact constraints reduce unneeded complexity in models and can promote stability.
In the field of constitutive modeling, the primary symmetry is expressed by the principle of
frame invariance

[
3
]
, or
objectivity
, which prescribes how a physical function must respond to changes in observer/coordinate frame.
Simply put, rotations of the tensorial inputs to a constitutive function must result in corresponding rotations of the output.
Now more commonly called
equivariance
, at least in the scientific machine learning (SciML) literature, this principle allows for elegant simplification of the form of physical models, particularly for isotropic materials whose response to deformation is invariant to all (distance-preserving) rotations of their reference configurations.
For the subclass of materials that have pronounced anisotropy, a more limited invariance holds for the subset of rotations that are in their symmetry group.
For these materials all transformations of the reference configuration in the symmetry group of the material give the same response to any given loading.

There are a number of approaches to embed these symmetries in SciML models so that they hold by construction.
Tensor basis (TB) formulations
[
4
]
rely on classical tensor basis expansions (TBEs) from representation theory going back to Finger
[
5
]
, Rivlin
[
6
]
and contemporaries
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
This is still an active field in mechanics and continues with recent work
[
10
,
11
]
.
Based on Rivlin’s work, Ling
et al.

[
4
]
invented the
Tensor Basis Neural Network
(TBNN) and utilized it to model crystal response, and fluids applications.
The formulation is adaptable to multiple inputs
[
4
,
12
]
and the often cited monograph of Zheng
[
13
]
provides the joint invariants and tensor basis generators for a variety of modeling scenarios.
Jones
et al.
extended the TBNN paradigm to formulations for phenomenological plasticity
[
12
]
and to inelastic materials with microstructure
[
14
]
.
By the same method anisotropy can be embedded through the addition of structure tensors characterizing the material symmetry group, which can be learned from data as in the work of Fugh
et al.

[
15
]
.
The basic formulation is suitable for other ML representations, for example Gaussian Processes
[
16
,
17
]
.
A recent contribution
[
18
]
surveys the variety of TBNN formulations for hyperelasticity.

In contrast to the TBNN approach, equivariant neural networks focus on the combination of information that is not at the same material location, as in convolution of field data.
This aspect of the equivariance principle is particularly germane to materials with microstructure, where the principle involves the rotations of finite size representative samples not just tensorial data at material points.
Pixel-based convolutional neural networks can learn this symmetry inexactly, given enough data.
In fact augmentation has been used to exploit this route, for example
Dielemann
et al.

[
19
]
augmented the convolutional layers of a CNN with the orthogonal transformation accessible on a grid,
i.e.
​
\pi/4
rotations and inversions.
Graph convolutional neural networks (GCNN)
[
20
,
21
]
have a degree of embedded equivariance due to the permutational invariance of their convolution kernels,
i.e.
​  the kernel is not a function of the position.
Along these lines, there have been approaches based on constrained kernel construction.
Worrall
et al.

[
22
]
developed filters based on circular harmonics and Chidester
et al.

[
23
]
used a discrete Fourier transform to embed rotational invariance in convolutional filters.

General strict equivariance for convolutional NNs began with theoretical developments.
Cohen and Welling
[
24
]
outlined the mathematics whereby convolutional networks will be equivariant with respect to any group.
Then Kondor and Trivedi
[
25
]
proved that convolutional structure is necessary and sufficient for equivariance to the action of a compact symmetry group using concepts from abstract algebra.
Finzi
et al.

[
26
]
provided a significant extension of the Cohen and Welling treatment of small, discrete symmetry groups to continuous (Lie) groups, such as the special orthogonal group SO(3).
Practical implementations of equivariant neural networks (EqvNNs) followed.

A particular formulation of equivariant convolutions using spherical harmonic expansion and the mathematics of tensor product spaces orchestrated by the Clebsch-Gordon algebra was developed by Thomas
et al.

[
27
]
and followed by Batzner
et al.

[
28
]
and Schmidt
et al.

[
29
]
.
This
Tensor Field Network
(TFN) was created with molecular interactions in mind and was applied to point cloud data.
This development was quickly followed up with applications to continuous fields.
For instance, Sun and coworkers have been particularly active in this field.
Vlassis
et al.

[
30
]
used it in the context of hyperelasticity, Heider
et al.

[
31
]
employed it in modeling phenomenological plasticity, and Cai
et al.

[
32
]
applied it to represent permeability fields.

In this work we adapt the equivariant TFN to the task of representing the response of polycrystalline materials.
Our contributions are:
(a) hybridizing the EqvNN with a TBNN for the homogenization task,
(b) demonstrating the reuse weights on edges is accurate and effective in reducing overparameterization,
(c) showing how pruning can combat the combinatorial explosion of the spherical harmonic expansion and provide generalization,
and
(d) extension of the TB-EqvNN to evolving processes through novel recurrent and ordinary differential equation neural network implementations.
The proposed framework is illustrated by multiple architectures that are show to be effective at the task of homogenization.

This section also includes the mathematics of irreducible representations (IRs), spherical harmonic expansion and how these interplay with classical tensor basis representations.
App.

Then Sec.​

Finally, we summarize the findings and give directions for future work in Sec.​
6
.

2
Homogenization of anisotropic aggregates

In this section, first we introduce the fundamentals of anisotropy for solids, then the homogenization problem and the representation challenge that follows.
Throughout the following, we use
a
to index basis elements in tensor basis expansions,
I
to index cells in discretized fields, and
i
to index spatial tensor components.

2.1
Symmetry and structural tensors

A
structural tensor

{\mathbb{A}}
is a tensor that characterizes a point group symmetry, i.e., a finite subgroup of the proper rotations,
{\mathcal{G}}\subset\textrm{SO(3)}
, via the property of invariance to group action

\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}}={\mathbb{A}}\ \ \forall\ \mathbf{G}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathbb{A}},

(1)

so that an anisotropic response function
\hat{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{E}})
,

\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\mathbf{E})=\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{E})

(2)

can be represented with an isotropic function

\mathbf{S}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}})

(3)

of two arguments, the original input
{\mathbf{E}}
and the structural tensor
{\mathbb{A}}
.
Here
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
is the symmetry group and
\boxtimes
is the Kronecker product
i.e.
​

\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\sum_{i,j,..}[{\mathbb{A}}]_{ij...}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes...=\sum_{i,j,..}[{\mathbb{A}}]_{ij...}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{G}\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes...

(4)

follows from the Rychlewski-Zhang theorem
[
33
]
,
which is sometimes called the
isotropization
theorem.
The operation
\mathbf{G}\boxtimes
defines group action of
\mathbf{G}
for (Cartesian) tensors of any order.

In this work, we concentrate on the stress response
\mathbf{S}
due to elastic strain
{\mathbf{E}}
.
In this case, equivariance is defined as

\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\mathbf{S}({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}})=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbf{E}},\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}})\quad\mathbf{G}\in\text{SO(3)}

(5)

which requires that a change of basis of the inputs results in the corresponding rotation of the output.
This is also the definition of an isotropic function.
A key result of Eq. (
1
) is

\mathbf{S}({\mathbf{E}},\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}})=\mathbf{S}({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}})\ \forall\ \mathbf{G}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathbb{A}}

(6)

i.e.
​  changing the reference orientation with a symmetry operation
\mathbf{G}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathbb{A}}
leads to the same response.
In other words rotating the material by
\mathbf{G}
but keeping the imposed strain
{\mathbf{E}}
the same results in same stress
\mathbf{S}
.
Given the structural tensor
{\mathbb{A}}
, the stress
\mathbf{S}
can then be represented with a tensor basis
[
34
, Ch.3]
:

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}})=\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}({\mathcal{I}})\mathbf{B}_{a},

(7)

formed from the product of coefficients
c_{a}
, which are functions of the joint scalar invariants
{\mathcal{I}}
, and tensor basis
{\mathcal{B}}=\{\mathbf{B}_{a}\}
.
This assumes the appropriate invariants and basis for the inputs
\{{\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}}\}
can be found
[
34
,
13
]
.

Remark
.

We have been non-specific about the particular stress measure.
Both the Cauchy stress and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress are symmetric and have the similar representations; however, the second Piola-Kirchhoff has the advantages of direct connection with a potential and being in the same frame as the structure tensor.
While the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress is invariant with respect to
superposed rigid motions

[
35
,
36
]
in the current configuration, it still needs to respect a change of basis in the reference, known as
material frame indifference

[
3
]
.
Hence, we associate
\mathbf{S}
with second Piola-Kirchhoff stress for the remainder of this work.

2.2
Homogenization

We focus on the prediction of the evolution of the average of stress
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
with strain
{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t)
in a sample region
\Omega
:

\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}\left({\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{X})\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}X\ ,

(8)

with volume
V
composed of anisotropic component materials characterized by a microstructural field
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{X})
observed at time
t=0
.
Here
\mathbf{X}
is the reference position of the material and
t
is time.
Formally we exchange this formulation, based on, for example, texture orientation angles
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{X})

[
37
,
38
,
39
]
, for a formulation based on the structure tensors
{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X})
of each point
\mathbf{X}
:

\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}\left({\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t),{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X})\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}X

(9)

This shift is important since it allows access of representation theory
[
40
,
34
,
13
,
41
]
and clarifies the equivariance requirements, as in Eq. (
5
).
We assert the isotropization theorem holds for this case so that the function
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
has to be equivariant to SO(3):

\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\bar{\mathbf{S}}\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbf{E}},\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}X\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\text{SO(3)}\ .

(10)

Furthermore the analog of Eq. (
6
) holds by virtue of Eq. (
1
).

To obtain the stress field
\mathbf{S}({\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t),{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X}))
the compatible strain field
{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t)
needs to be solved for given the imposed strain, which is prescribed by the boundary or the average strain
\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t)
.
The goal of the NN models described in Sec.​
4
is to predict the mean stress
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
given only: (a) the initial microstructure
{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X})
, and (b) the external, applied strain
\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t)
, in effect bypassing the boundary value solve.

2.3
Representation problem

The representation problem is to find the model,
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
, as a function of the external loading
\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t)
and the microstructural anisotropy field
{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X})
to the sample average stress
\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)
.
The fact that many microstructures, such as grains of a polycrystal or phases of an alloy, are composed of a collection of distinct subdomains, allows us to decompose
\Omega
into finite number of subdomains with constant properties.
Furthermore we need to discretize the domain
\Omega
into mesh of cells
\cup_{K}\Omega_{K}=\Omega
each with a constant
\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\}
and an (invariant) distance graph
{\mathit{G}}
that encodes the spatial relationships of the cells.
With a finite collection of homogeneous subdomains, the problem involves approximating

\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int\mathbf{S}\left({\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{X},t),{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{X})\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}X\approx\frac{1}{V}\sum_{K}\mathbf{S}\left({\mathbf{E}}_{K}(t),{\mathbb{A}}_{K},{\mathit{G}}\right)\Delta\!V\approx\tilde{\mathbf{S}}(\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t),\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\},{\mathit{G}})

(11)

while preserving

\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\tilde{\mathbf{S}}(\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t),\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\},{\mathit{G}})=\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\left(\mathbf{G}\boxtimes\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t),\{\mathbf{G}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\},{\mathit{G}}\right)\quad\forall\mathbf{G}\in\text{SO(3)}

(12)

To our knowledge there is no exact theory that covers this case,
i.e.
​  where we are trying to combine the effects of a field or multiple subcomponents which do not have the same symmetries (which we call
heteroanisotropy
) or are rotations of the same symmetry (which we call
homoanisotropy
).
Certainly classical approximate mixture rules
[
42
,
43
]
are applicable, such as the Hill-Voigt-Reuss
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
and the Taylor-Sachs
[
47
,
48
]
estimators, but these can be inaccurate
[
37
]
.

Remark
.

The Hill average of constant strain (Voigt) and constant stress (Reuss) assumptions for the modulus
\mathbb{C}
:

\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{\text{Hill}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{K}\nu_{K}\mathbf{R}_{K}\boxtimes\mathbb{C}+\left[\sum_{K}\nu_{K}\mathbf{R}_{K}\boxtimes\mathbb{C}^{-1}\right]^{-1}\right)

(13)

can be an adequate estimator at small strains
[
37
]
where the grain contributions are more or less independent.
Here
\nu_{K}=V_{K}/V
are the volume fractions of the grains.
This mean mixture modulus tensor can be used to estimate stress with a St. Venant model:
\bar{\mathbf{S}}=\bar{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{E}
and also give an expectation of the symmetries of polycrystalline aggregates.
Note that mixture estimators of this type ignore continuity/compatibility and relaxation of the displacement field.

Remark
.

A few approaches to the representation problem beyond simple mixture rules are obvious but flawed.

One approach is to form a TB representation of using all the joint invariants and basis generators for
\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\}
, for example for 4th order
{\mathbb{A}}_{K}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\bar{\mathbf{S}}(\bar{{\mathbf{E}}},\{{\mathbb{A}}_{I}\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{0}\mathbf{I}+\hat{c}_{1}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\hat{c}_{2}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{2}+

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\left[\hat{c}^{(1,1)}_{I}{\operatorname{sym}}{\mathbb{A}}_{I}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\ldots\right]+

\displaystyle\sum_{I,J}\left[\hat{c}^{(1,2)}_{IJ}{\operatorname{sym}}{\mathbb{A}}_{I}\bar{\mathbf{E}}{\mathbb{A}}_{J}+\hat{c}^{(2,2)}_{IJ}{\operatorname{sym}}{\mathbb{A}}_{I}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{2}{\mathbb{A}}_{J}+\ldots\right]...

where the coefficients
\hat{c}_{I\ldots}
are (NN) functions of all the scalar invariants.
Clearly this is cumbersome since the permutations in the joint invariants grows rapidly with the number of subdomains and the expansion ignores the spatial proximity encoded in
{\mathit{G}}
.

Another approach is to form TB representation using a single representative structure tensor
\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}
, for example for a 4th order
{\mathbb{A}}
:.

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{S}}(\bar{{\mathbf{E}}},\bar{{\mathbb{A}}})=\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}({\mathcal{I}})\mathbf{B}_{a}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{0}\mathbf{I}+\hat{c}_{1}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\hat{c}_{2}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{2}+\hat{c}_{3}\,{\operatorname{sym}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}+\hat{c}_{4}\,{\operatorname{sym}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}+...+\hat{c}_{8}\,{\operatorname{sym}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}^{2}

This has the advantage that a single material tensor basis expansion can be employed; however, a secondary model
\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}=\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}(\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\})
needs to be constructed to determine
\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}
and a priori assumptions, such as
\bar{{\mathbb{A}}}
is the same rank as
{\mathbb{A}}_{K}
, are likely restrictive
[
49
]
.
The fact that even an aggregate of homoanisotropic subdomains does not result in a symmetry from the same or with a structural tensor of the same order is illustrated by the isotropic limit, where, for example, a large collection of cubic crystals, with 4th order structural tensors, tends to isotropy, which has the 2nd order identity as its characteristic tensor.

These issues motivate a mixture of tensor basis representations and equivariant convolution described in Sec.​
4
.

3
Polycrystal data

Polycrystalline elastoplasticity is a canonical exemplar of the behavior of aggregates of constituents with different material symmetries.
Polycrystalline aggregates are ubiquitous in the materials science of metals, ceramics, rocks and ice, and at the center of many efforts to upscale micromechanics to large scale plasticity phenomenology
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
.

Modeling the stress response of polycrystalline materials is important in simulating these materials at a larger scale.
In polycrystalline materials, microstructure is a composition of subdomains each with an anisotropic response.

The datasets are comprised of polycrystalline stochastic volume elements (SVEs) where boundary conditions are used to evoke nominally homogeneous deformations.
The SVEs are finite size samples which display response variability sample to sample.
The quantity of interest in our study is the sample average stress.

3.1
Elasticity

A polycrystal composed of face centered cubic (FCC) crystals is a particularly common example in structural metals.
Each FCC crystal in the aggregate has the point symmetry group
\mathcal{O}_{h}
and a 4th order structure tensor
[
13
]
:

{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{O}_{h}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{i}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk}\delta_{kl}\delta_{li}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{l}\ ,

(16)

if the symmetry axes are aligned with the Cartesian axes
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
.
Any other orientation can be obtained via rotation
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})\in\text{SO(3)}

\mathbf{R}\boxtimes{\mathbb{A}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{R}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{R}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{R}\mathbf{e}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{i}

(17)

to alignment with local texture/orientation
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{X})
.
The associated elastic modulus tensor

{\mathbb{C}}\equiv{{\boldsymbol{\partial}}}_{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{S}=\lambda\mathbb{I}+2\mu\mathbb{J}+\alpha{\mathbb{A}}

(18)

is composed of two isotropic 4th order tensors
[
57
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}\otimes\mathbf{I}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{l}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{l},

(20)

and the cubic structural tensor
{\mathbb{A}}

[
58
]
.
The isotropic tensors
\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{J}
are invariant to SO(3), which is evident since pairs of
\mathbf{R}
are summed and
\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}=\mathbf{I}
.
In terms of the more familiar cubic modulus constants:
C_{11}\equiv[\mathbb{C}]_{iiii}=\lambda+2\mu+\alpha
,
C_{12}\equiv[\mathbb{C}]_{iijj}=\lambda
, and
C_{44}\equiv[\mathbb{C}]_{ijij}=2\mu
.

For a single crystal, Kambouchev
et al.

[
59
]
provides a general polyconvex hyperelastic model with cubic symmetry.
The elastic potential
\Phi
,

\Phi=\Phi({\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}})=\Phi(\underbrace{{\operatorname{tr}}{\mathbf{E}},{\operatorname{tr}}{\mathbf{E}}^{*},\det{\mathbf{E}}}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{iso}}},\underbrace{{\mathbb{A}}:{\mathbf{E}}\otimes{\mathbf{E}},{\mathbb{A}}:{\mathbf{E}}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}^{2},{\mathbb{A}}:{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbf{E}}^{2}}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}})\ ,

(21)

takes the scalar invariants
{\mathcal{I}}={\mathcal{I}}_{\text{iso}}\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}
as inputs, where
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}
are the joint invariants of
\bar{\mathbf{E}}
and
\mathbb{A}
.
Here
\mathbf{E}^{*}=\det(\mathbf{E})\mathbf{E}^{-1}
is the adjugate of
\mathbf{E}
and three traces of products of the structural tensor
{\mathbb{A}}
and the strain
{\mathbf{E}}
are the anisotropic scalar invariants,
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}
.
Note, an equivalent set of invariants are
{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{iso}}={\operatorname{tr}}{\mathbf{E}},{\operatorname{tr}}{\mathbf{E}}^{2},{\operatorname{tr}}{\mathbf{E}}^{3}
.
For the cubic structure tensor
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{O}_{h}
the anisotropic invariants can be reduced to

{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbb{A}}=\left\{\sum_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{E}\mathbf{r}_{i})^{2},\sum_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{E}\mathbf{r}_{i})(\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}),\sum_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i})^{2}\right\}

(22)

A corresponding tensor basis can be obtained from the derivatives of the invariants:

{\mathcal{B}}=\{\partial_{\mathbf{E}}{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\}=\{\mathbf{B}_{1}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu\mathbf{I},\mathbf{B}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu{\mathbf{E}},\mathbf{B}_{3}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu{\mathbf{E}}^{*}\}\cup\{\mathbf{B}_{4},\mathbf{B}_{5},\mathbf{B}_{6}\}

(23)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{A}}_{IJPQ}{\mathbf{E}}_{PQ}\mathbf{e}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{J}

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{I}2\left(\mathbf{r}_{I}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{r}_{I}\right)\mathbf{r}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{I}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[{\mathbb{A}}_{IJPQ}{\mathbf{E}}^{2}_{PQ}+{\mathbf{E}}_{MN}({\mathbb{A}}_{MNIQ}{\mathbf{E}}_{JQ}+{\mathbb{A}}_{MNJQ}{\mathbf{E}}_{IQ})\bigr]\mathbf{e}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{J}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{I}(\mathbf{r}_{I}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{I}+2(\mathbf{r}_{I}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{r}_{I})^{2})\mathbf{r}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{I}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{6}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathbf{E}}^{2}_{MN}({\mathbb{A}}_{MNIQ}{\mathbf{E}}_{JQ}+{\mathbb{A}}_{MNJQ}{\mathbf{E}}_{IQ})\mathbf{e}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{J}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{I}((\mathbf{r}_{I}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{I})(\mathbf{r}_{I}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{r}_{I}))\mathbf{r}_{I}\otimes\mathbf{r}_{I}\ .

for
{\mathbb{A}}
satisfying the same symmetries as the elastic modulus tensor:
{\mathbb{A}}_{IJPQ}={\mathbb{A}}_{JIPQ}={\mathbb{A}}_{PQIJ}={\mathbb{A}}_{PQJI}
.
This basis spans the full output space of symmetric tensors, unless the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{E}
are aligned with the cubic axes
\{\mathbf{r}_{I}\}
in which case the basis is still sufficient to represent
\mathbf{S}
.
Note the scalar invariants has all the traces of the tensor basis, with
\det\mathbf{E}
substituted for the constant
{\operatorname{tr}}\mathbf{I}=3
.
Also note
{\mathbf{E}}^{2}_{PQ}
is the
PQ
-th component of
{\mathbf{E}}^{2}
(not the square of the component).
The appendix of Ref.
[
59
]
provides derivatives of invariants with respect to the strain measure.
See also Ref.
[
10
]
and Ref.
[
11
]
for decompositions of the elasticity tensor and bases for elastic symmetry classes.

The stress of a polycrystalline SVE sample is obtained by solving for a continuous displacement field that is consistent with prescribed kinematic boundary conditions.
We use the finite element method to generate these solutions.

3.2
Plasticity

The previous section gives an overview crystal elasticity in a finite strain; however, metal crystals typically exhibit plasticity at moderate strains.
To generate plastic response data we used a common elastoviscoplastic model
[
52
,
53
,
60
]
.

In each crystal, plastic flow

\mathbf{L}_{p}=\sum_{\alpha}\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha}\mathbf{s}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\alpha}

(27)

could occur on any of the slip planes defined by the slip plane normal
\mathbf{n}_{\alpha}
and slip direction
\mathbf{s}_{\alpha}
.
Here
\mathbf{L}_{p}
is the plastic velocity gradient and
\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha}
is the slip rate.
A power-law driven by the shear stress
\tau_{\alpha}
resolved on slip system
\alpha
governs the slip rate:

\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha}=\Gamma\left|\frac{\tau_{\alpha}}{g_{\alpha}}\right|^{m-1}\tau_{\alpha}\ .

(28)

and in this model the slip resistance
g_{\alpha}
evolves according to
[
61
,
62
]

\dot{g}_{\alpha}=(H-R\,g_{\alpha})\sum_{\alpha}|\dot{\gamma}_{\alpha}|\ ,

(29)

where
H
is the hardening modulus and
R
is the recovery constant.
See Ref.
[
12
]
for more details.

4
Model architecture

As framed in Eq. (
11
), the task for the NN model is to approximate the map from the imposed strain
\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}(t)
and the (initial) structure tensor field
\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\}
to the average stress
\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)
for the sample.
This homogenization model needs to account for the spatial dependence in the sample volume and, as stated in Eq. (
12
), it is required to be equivariant so all operations to this mix of input tensors need to be equivariant.
A few methods have been developed to embed equivariant symmetries.
In this work we extend and combine
tensor basis neural networks
(TBNNs) and the equivariant convolutional
tensor field network
(TFN) of Thomas
et al.

[
27
]
.

The TBNN
[
4
,
12
,
14
]
is an equivariant function representation that uses classical representation theorems
[
40
,
34
,
13
,
41
]
; however, as discussed in Sec.​
2.3
it is generally only applicable to single materials,
i.e.
​  for models of a material point.
Motivated by mixture rules, we use a tensor basis transformation at each cell
K
to form an approximate stress
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{K}
and then equivariant convolution is applied to accommodate local relaxation constrained by continuity/compatibility that is ignored by simple mixture rules.
Following the TFN
[
27
]
we use an equivariant convolution neural network (EqvCNN) that relies on spherical harmonics to form the filters, since these functions are inherently equivariant to rotations and also are expressive.
To accommodate the relative contributions of the cells we also include the cell volume fraction
\nu_{K}
in the inputs.
The mesh holding the cell data is encoded as a graph
{\mathit{G}}
with neighbors and relative distances between cells to facilitate the application of the convolutional filters.
In the overall network, the TBNN provides a learnable reduction/
embedding
of the input data on a per-cell basis, the EqvCNN provides the spatial operations to mix the influence of neighboring cells, and global average pooling emulates the volume average in Eq. (
11
).

Rather than work with products of tensors in their natural Cartesian basis with arbitrary product rules between tensors of different order, we follow Refs.
[
27
,
32
]
and manipulate them in an equivalent form that enables general product rules.
Sec.​
4.1
gives a brief description of how to handle products of different orders of tensors in a general way through the
irreducible representation
(IR) of group theory
[
65
,
66
,
67
]
,
This approach reduces the amount of information produced by general tensor products by omitting the higher order components of the irreducible representations of tensor products.

We begin with the building blocks of equivariant NNs that are suited to the homogenization task, namely local embedding, sample-wise convolution and pooling.
The adaptation of the tensor basis formulation to work on IR vectors is given in Sec.​
4.2
.
Pooling is briefly described in Sec.​
4.4
.
Models for the elastic response of polycrystals are described in Sec.​
4.5
, while Sec.​
4.6
describes models of anisotropic inelasticity using a novel equivariant recurrent neural network (RNN) and a potential-based neural ordinary differential equation (NODE).
Throughout this section, we will use a superposed hat, such as
\hat{c}
, to indicate learnable NN functions.

Inputs are (a) the external strain loading of the sample

, (b) the structure tensors for each cell

, and the graph
{\mathit{G}}
that provides the cell-to-cell connectivity and cell volume fractions
\nu_{K}
.
The output is the sample average stress

.
After converting the scalar invariants and tensor basis generators of

.
This cell data is then processed by a stack of equivariant convolutions.
The output of the stack is then pooled and this converted to the spatial output
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
.

4.1
Irreducible representations of tensors

An irreducible representation (IR) of a tensor is systematic way of handling tensors of various order and products thereof by decomposing them and flattening them into a collection of vectors.
The map from tensors in their natural representation with basis in
{R}^{3}
is accomplished by the Clebsch-Gordon (CG) matrices
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
.
In this notation
\ell\in{|\alpha-\beta|,\ldots,\alpha+\beta}
is the order of the output IR, and
\alpha
and
\beta
are the orders of the input IRs.

For example a symmetric second order tensor
\mathbf{A}
with basis
\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}
and 6 independent components is decomposed into one 1-vector
\mathsf{a}^{(0)}
and one 5-vector
\mathsf{a}^{(2)}

\displaystyle[\mathsf{a}^{(0)}]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}[\mathsf{C}^{(0)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}\quad i\in\{1\}

(31)

\displaystyle{}[\mathsf{a}^{(2)}]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}[\mathsf{C}^{(2)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,5\}

where
\mathsf{a}^{(0)}
carries the trace of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathsf{a}^{(2)}
holds the deviatoric information.
Likewise tensors of any order can be converted to IR vectors and the transpose of the CG coefficients provide the inverse of the linear change-of-basis map,
e.g.
​

[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}=\sum_{\ell=0,2}\sum_{i=1}^{2\ell+1}[\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathsf{a}^{(\ell)}]_{i}\ .

(32)

The CG transformation applies to both tensors in their natural form and in their IR form where each is a vector in
{R}^{2\ell+1}
where
\ell
is order of the order of the IR vector.

Note that our only direct use of the TFN implementation
e3nn

[
27
]
is to access of the library of CG coefficients.
Further exposition of the fundamentals of IRs is given App.
A
.

4.2
Tensor basis expansion

As we remarked in Sec.​
2.3
, forming a TB expansion across cells or grains is infeasible but we can use a TB layer at the cell level as a form of embedding prior to processing the data on the distance graph
{\mathit{G}}
with equivariant convolutions.
We chose to apply the TB expansion to the CG encodings of
\mathcal{I}_{K}
and
\mathcal{B}_{K}
.
This is possible because the CG encoding commutes with the TB expansion.
In the natural Cartesian basis we have

\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{K}=\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I}_{K})\mathbf{B}_{Ka}

(33)

at each cell
K
where we ignore invariants and basis generators that cross cells, leaving spatial correlation to the subsequent convolutional layers.
In Eq. (
33
),
\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I}_{K})
are NN functions of the invariants implemented as a monolithic feed-forward NN with
N_{\mathcal{I}}
inputs and
N_{\mathcal{B}}
outputs.

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}[\mathsf{C}^{(\ell))}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}]_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}\sum_{a}[\mathsf{C}^{(\ell))}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I})\mathbf{B}_{a}]_{jk}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I})\sum_{j,k}[\mathsf{C}^{(\ell))}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathbf{B}_{a}]_{jk}

\displaystyle{}[\mathsf{s}^{(\ell)}]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I})[\mathsf{b}^{(\ell)}_{a}]_{i}

In effect we obtain one 1-vector
\{\mathsf{s}_{K}^{(0)}\}
corresponding to the approximate pressure in the cell
K
, and one 5 vector corresponding to the approximate deviatoric stress in the cell
\{\mathsf{s}_{K}^{(2)}\}
.

Since the TB expansion is via scalar multiplication and tensor addition, rotation of the inputs commutes with the function and hence the operation is equivariant,
i.e.
​

\mathsf{s}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R})=\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I})\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R})\mathsf{b}^{(\ell)}_{a}=\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R})\sum_{a}\hat{c}_{a}(\mathcal{I})\mathsf{b}^{(\ell)}_{a}=\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R})\mathsf{s}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{I})

(35)

in terms of the Wigner
\mathsf{D}
matrices, refer to App.
A
and references therein.

4.3
Equivariant convolution

Convolution is a form of tensor multiplication between data

\mathsf{z}_{K}=[\mathsf{z}^{(\ell)},\ell=0,..,\ell_{\text{max}}]_{K}
and a kernel
\mathsf{f}(\mathbf{X})
operating over the mesh of cells in the graph
{\mathit{G}}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}^{(\ell)}_{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{z}_{K})=\hat{\mathsf{f}}\ast\mathsf{z}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\hat{\mathsf{f}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}-\mathbf{X}_{K})\mathsf{z}^{(\beta)}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}^{3}X^{\prime}

(36)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{J}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\hat{\mathsf{f}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{X}_{J}-\mathbf{X}_{K})\mathsf{z}^{(\beta)}(\mathbf{X}_{J})\nu_{J}\ ,

where
\alpha
is the order of the filter
\hat{\mathsf{f}}^{(\alpha)}
and
\beta
is the order of the input
\mathsf{z}^{(\beta)}
.
We emphasize that for each convolutional layer this formulation allows for a collection of input vectors with arbitrary orders to be mapped to a collection of output vectors of different orders.
Note unlike Ref.
[
27
]
, which was concerned with point clouds, we use the cell volume fraction
\nu_{J}
as the integration weight in the summation and use the same filter for all edges of the distance graph
{\mathit{G}}
, i.e. weights are reused throughout the application of the convolution to the graph.

Again we require convolution to be an equivariant operation such that

\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{z}_{K}^{(\ell)}={\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{D}^{(\beta)}\mathsf{z}_{K}^{(\beta)})\ ,

(37)

where
\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}
is the appropriate order Wigner
\mathsf{D}
matrix which effects a change of basis for an IR vector of order
\ell
, refer to App.
A
.
As in Thomas
et al.

[
27
]
, if the kernel
\hat{\mathsf{f}}
takes the special form

\hat{\mathsf{f}}^{(\ell)}(r\mathbf{r})=\hat{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}^{(\ell)}(r)\otimes\mathsf{Y}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{r})\ ,

(38)

the convolution is equivariant, which relies on the fact that distances are invariant, and directions encoded by the spherical harmonic function to rotate appropriately.
Here
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}^{(\ell)}(r)
are learnable NN functions of distance
r=\|\mathbf{X}^{\prime}-\mathbf{X}\|
from the kernel center
\mathbf{X}
and any other point
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, and
\mathsf{Y}(\mathbf{r})
are spherical harmonic functions which only depend on the orientation unit vectors
\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}-\mathbf{X})/r
.
Note unlike Ref.
[
27
]
, which was applied to molecular graphs and used a different
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}^{(\ell)}
for each edge, we exploit similarity in the homogenization task and use a unique
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}^{(\ell)}
for the entire graph.
This results in a considerable reduction in the number of parameters and no discernible loss of accuracy.

As in Refs.
[
27
,
32
,
21
,
68
]
we include a self interaction

\mathsf{z}^{(\ell)}_{K}={\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{z}_{K})+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\hat{\mathsf{w}}^{(\alpha)}\mathsf{z}_{K}^{(\beta)}\ ,

(39)

which allows for the extraction of graph Laplacian, grain misorientation and related features.
Note equivariance prevents the addition of a nonzero bias
[
27
]
.
Also the TB embedding can be seen as a special form of a cell-level, self-interaction.

We also follow Ref.
[
27
]
and apply nonlinear activation functions to tensors by scaling them by standard activation functions on their norms.
For tensor,
\mathsf{z}
in IR form and activation function,
\sigma
, the equivariant activation function is:

\sigma_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{z})=\sigma(||\mathsf{z}||_{2}+b)\mathsf{z}

(40)

where
b
is a trainable scalar bias.

The complexity of the spherical harmonic expansion is only limited by the hyperparameter
\ell_{\text{max}}
.
The combinatorics of the general tensor product afforded by the IR products results in a proliferation of terms of different orders and a explosion in the number of parameters needed to define the kernel and self-interaction functions.
For any given application it is unlikely all terms are needed.
Some physical intuition, such as in the TB expansion, can be used to downselect terms, but this is generally not sufficient.
Various techniques exists, such as L
0
sparsification
[
69
,
70
]
., that can be used to systematically prune the large combinatorial, product space.
In this work we employed a straightforward greedy algorithm, whereby the least influential products are omitted, the overall network is retrained, and the process is repeated until the loss starts to increase significantly.

4.4
Pooling

The global average pooling we use to go from cell data to sample-level output :

\mathsf{z}^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{K}\mathsf{z}^{(\ell)}_{K},

(41)

is similar to volume normalized integration Eq. (
11
).
Here
N
is the number of cells being pooled.
This operation is clearly equivariant since it is only requires addition and scalar multiplication.
Note pooling does not change the order
(\ell)
of the inputs which constrains the output of the preceding convolutional layer if an output of the pooling is required to be of a certain order, as in Fig.
3
.
Local pooling is similar but applied to disjoint patches of the spatial graph to produce a coarser graph.

4.5
Elastic models

The schematic in Fig.
3
depicts a stack of NN layers with equivariant operations that predict the elastic stress of an anisotropic aggregrate.
In this architecture the raw inputs
\bar{{\mathbf{E}}}
and
\{{\mathbb{A}}_{K}\}
are first reduced to their joint scalar invariants
\{{\mathcal{I}}_{K}\}
, as in Eq. (
21
), and basis generators
\{{\mathcal{B}}_{K}\}
, as in Eq. (
23
).
Then these TB inputs at each node of the distance graph
{\mathit{G}}
are converted to their equivalent IRs so then can be operated on by the subsequent trainable layers.
The first trainable layer is a TB embedding that operates on each cell independently to produce an approximate cell-wise stress which is fed into the following equivariant convolution stack.
The last layer of the convolutional stack is constrained to produce a field of second order tensors (as represented by one 0th order and one 2nd order IR) which is then pooled and converted to a spatial representation.
This symmetric spatial 2nd order tensor is the estimate of the mean stress for the sample.
In this network exponential linear unit (ELU) activations are used in the TB and equivariant convolutions which are the only trainable aspects.

An alternative architecture which only operates on the scalar invariants to produce a potential is omitted for brevity since it is subsumed by the inelastic model with the same approach presented in the next section.

4.6
Inelastic models

The history dependence and internal states of inelasticity requires more complex architectures that account for the causal effects of the imposed strain.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been used for this task
[
37
,
71
]
, where the hidden states of the RNN model the inelastic state of the material.
The Elman RNN
[
72
]
has a simpler form than the more recent gated recurrent unit (GRU)
[
73
]
and long-term short-term memory unit (LSTM)
[
74
]
RNNs,
but is directly adaptable to equivariant operations.
Specifically, our version of the Elman RNN is

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathit{G}}({\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{x}_{n})+{\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{h}_{n-1}))

(42)

\displaystyle\mathsf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathit{G}}({\operatorname{Conv}}_{\mathit{G}}(\mathsf{h}_{n}))

where
\mathsf{h}_{n}
is the hidden state at step
t_{n}
which is initialized to zero at step
t_{0}
, and
\mathsf{y}_{n}
is the output at at step
t_{n}
.
Each convolution has an independent kernel and no bias.
The same operations and restrictions can yield equivariant versions of GRU/LSTM.
An encoder-decoder architecture using this RNN is illustrated in Fig.
4
.
First the inputs are converted to their IRs.
This field is then processed by a stack of equivariant convolutions and local pooling to reduce it to a coarse field of features.
The RNN propagates this time dependent field which is then processed by another equivariant convolution in the decoder, and finally globally pooled and converted to the spatial output
\bar{\mathbf{S}}(t)
.
Again, in this network the convolutions were the only trainable aspects and used ELU activations.

over time

As an alternative we also implemented a potential-based model of the evolving inelastic process illustrated in Fig.
5
.
It is along the lines of the model in Ref.
[
14
]
where additional hidden invariants represent the inelastic state in the potential.
Since the scalar invariants and their combinations are trivially equivariant this model is also equivariant.
The hidden features evolve by an (augmented) neural ordinary differential equation (NODE)
[
75
,
76
]
with an input convex
[
77
]
right hand side.
Briefly, the NODE has the update

\mathsf{h}_{n+1}=\mathsf{h}_{n}+\Delta\!t\mathsf{R}(\mathsf{f}_{n},\mathsf{h}_{n})

(43)

for a simple forward Euler integrator with time-step
\Delta\!t
, where
\mathsf{h}
is the augmented hidden state and
\mathsf{f}
are the features coming from the convolutional stack in Fig.
5
.
We form the right hand side
\mathsf{R}(\mathsf{x})=\mathsf{r}_{n}
with an input convex NN (ICNN)

\displaystyle\mathsf{r}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathsf{W}_{0}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{b}_{0})

(44)

\displaystyle\mathsf{r}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathsf{W}_{n-1}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{V}_{n-1}\mathsf{r}_{n-1}+\mathsf{b}_{n-1})

to maintain a degree of regularity in the potential that we subsequently take the gradient of.
Here the weight matrices
\mathsf{V}_{n}
are required to be positive and the activations
\sigma
are required to be convex and increasing; the weight matrices
\mathsf{W}_{n}
and biases
\mathsf{b}_{n}
are unconstrained.
We used
softplus
activations for this NN.
Further details can be found in Ref.
[
77
]
.
Since the isotropic invariants
{\operatorname{tr}}\bar{\mathbf{E}},{\operatorname{tr}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{*},\det\bar{\mathbf{E}}
and corresponding basis have no local dependence, unlike the anisotropic invariants and basis which vary across subdomains, we have these isotropic components isotropic components bypass convolution.
The output stress is obtained by taking the gradient of the potential
\Phi
with respect to strain

\mathbf{S}=\partial_{\mathbf{E}}\Phi(\mathsf{h})=\sum_{i}\underbrace{(\partial_{h_{i}}\Phi)}_{\hat{c}_{i}}\,\underbrace{\partial_{\mathbf{E}}h_{i}}_{\mathbf{B}_{i}}

(45)

which effectively forms a basis
\{\mathbf{B}_{i}=\partial_{\mathbf{E}}h_{i}\}
from the set of hidden invariants
\mathsf{h}
.

As described in Sec.​
3

5.1
Polycrystal elasticity

5.1.2
Sparsification

As mentioned the possible permutations of tensor products for a given input and output tensor field leads to a tremendous number of parameters and corresponding redundancies.
With reference to Eq. (

\hat{\mathsf{f}}^{(\alpha)}

\mathsf{z}^{(\beta)}
in IR form that are compatible with a selected input and output.
Hence, we explored the effect of sparsification using the cubic dataset for training.
We employed a straightforward greedy algorithm were we trained the full network to a plateau in loss, then omitted tensor products with the least significance in terms of the magnitude of the kernel
\hat{\varphi}^{(\ell)}

The regularity in the loss behavior with the greedy stages lead to easy identification of the (quasi)optimal stopping stage.

The transferability of the sparse network is remarkable when compared to that of the corresponding un-pruned, full network.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work we have presented a number of effective NN homogenization models using equivariant and tensor basis operations on SVE data, which include sparsity and weight reuse improvements.
These particular architectures illustrate the variety of approaches that can be used to emulate the response of microstructural samples for use in subgrid models, structure-property optimization, and material uncertainty quantification.
In comparison to the graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs) we have previously employed these models perform better and preserve equivariance and material symmetries exactly.
As others have observed, symmetry preservation allows for configurations that are close up to a symmetry transformation to be treated as similar thus compacting the range of responses that need to be represented.
Although a GCNN is loosely an equivariant network in that the filters are implicitly a function of radial distance only due to treating shells of neighbors alike, we conjecture that our TFN-like networks use of a continuous kernel is a significant contributor to their accuracy.
The use of similar discretization independent constructs, such as neural operators
[
78
,
79
,
80
]
, is becoming the state-of-the-art in SciML applications.

There are a multitude of avenues for future work.
Alternative applications abound such as using an equivariant convolutional stack to generate a reduced set of pseudo-structural tensors of various orders directly from the structural tensor field or processing multiple graphs at different scales
[
68
]
potentially with a U-net structure of equivariant convolutions
[
81
]
.
It may be useful to use transfer convolution layers from the simpler elastic to the inelastic models, with the assumption that the underlying symmetries and features apparent in the elastic deformation phase persist in plastic flow, at least in the early stages.
Predicting the texture induced by deformation is a more challenging task.
Also alternative approaches to sparsification of the tensor products could be employed such as
L^{0}
regularization
[
69
,
70
]
.
Thomas et al.
[
27
]
introduced the norm based activation functions we use in this work, Eq. (
40
). Future work may examine using the TB expansions discussed in this work as an alternative mechanism to design activation functions.
Lastly, new methods for fully sampling and training to the path dependence of the inelastic data have arisen
[
71
]
but this is still a challenge.

Appendix A
Irreducible representation of tensors

An irreducible representation (IR) of a tensor is systematic way of handling tensors of various order and products thereof by decomposing them and flattening them into a collection of vectors.

For the purpose of our development, we examine 0th order tensors, such as invariants
{\mathcal{I}}
, and 2nd order tensors, such as the basis generators
{\mathcal{B}}
, as examples.
The decomposition of a 0th order tensor (a scalar) is simply
a^{(0)}=A
, where we use lower case to denote for the coefficients of the IR vector.
A second order tensor can be uniquely decomposed as

\mathbf{A}=a\mathbf{I}+{\operatorname{perm}}\,\mathbf{a}+{\operatorname{devsym}}\,\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}

(A.1)

where

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}{\operatorname{tr}}\mathbf{A}\

\displaystyle a\ \text{is a 1-vector},

\displaystyle{\operatorname{perm}}\,\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{asym}}({\operatorname{dev}}\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\ \text{is a 3-vector},

\displaystyle{\operatorname{devsym}}\,\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{sym}}({\operatorname{dev}}\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\ \text{contains the remaining 5 components}.

Here
{\operatorname{dev}}{\mathbf{A}}\equiv\mathbf{A}-1/3{\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{I}
,
{\operatorname{asym}}\mathbf{A}\equiv 1/2(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{T})
, and
{\operatorname{sym}}\mathbf{A}\equiv 1/2(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A}^{\prime T})
.
Also
{\operatorname{perm}}\,\mathbf{a}=\varepsilon_{ijk}a_{k}
with
\varepsilon_{ijk}
being the third order permutation tensor, and
{\operatorname{devsym}}\mathbf{a}=a_{1}(2\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{2}-\mathbf{e}_{3}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{3})+a_{2}(2\mathbf{e}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{2}-\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{3}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{3})+a_{3}(\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{2}+\mathbf{e}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{1})+\ldots
.
The three vectors,
a(\mathbf{A})
,
\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{A})
,
\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A})
, form the map, from
{R}^{3\times 3}
to
{R}^{1}\oplus{R}^{3}\oplus{R}^{5}
.
For a symmetric tensor, such as the strain
{\mathbf{E}}
or stress
\mathbf{S}
, the 1st order term (the 3-vector) is irrelevant.

A.1
Clebsch-Gordon matrices

The Clebsch-Gordorn (CG) transform is a particular version of these maps, generalized to any order tensors.
In fact it provides a decomposition rule for both a single tensor, such as
\mathbf{A}=A_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}
, and the product of tensors, such as
\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}
.

To decompose a second order, symmetric Cartesian tensor,
\mathbf{A}
into the irreducible, the CG coefficients,
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(1,1)}
for
\ell=0,2
are needed

\displaystyle[\mathsf{a}^{(0)}]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}[\mathsf{C}^{(0)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}\quad i\in\{1\}

(A.2)

\displaystyle{}[\mathsf{a}^{(2)}]_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}[\mathsf{C}^{(2)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,5\}

where
\mathsf{C}^{(0)}_{(1,1)}
is a scaling of the 3
\times
3 identity so
a_{0}
is proportional to the trace, and
\mathsf{C}^{(2)}_{(1,1)}
are a set of 5 symmetric traceless tensors that for a basis for all other traceless symmetric tensors.
Note the CG coefficients
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
are constants,
i.e.
​  they are not functions of the encoded tensor.
The CG transform is also orthogonal, so taking the transpose and scaling recovers the Cartesian tensors from the irreducible representations, for example to recover
\mathbf{A}
:

[\mathbf{A}]_{jk}=\sum_{i=1}^{1}[\mathsf{C}^{(0)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathsf{a}^{(0)}]_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}[\mathsf{C}^{(2)}_{(1,1)}]_{ijk}[\mathsf{a}^{(2)}]_{i}\ .

(A.3)

where we assume the CG transform is also normalized such that
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\gamma)}\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\gamma,\beta)}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.

In general, each individual
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
functions as the bilinear map,
{R}^{2\alpha+1}\times{R}^{2\beta+1}\rightarrow{R}^{2\ell+1}
.
For
\operatorname{order}(\mathbf{u})=\alpha
and
\operatorname{order}(\mathbf{v})=\beta
, the irreducible representation components of their tensor product will have order
\ell
such that
|\alpha-\beta|\leq\ell\leq\alpha+\beta
.
For higher order Cartesian tensors, the decomposition can be applied recursively.
For example,
\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}_{(1,1)}
provides the map,

{R}^{(3\times 3)\times 3\times\ldots}\rightarrow{R}^{(1+3+5)\times 3\times\ldots}={R}^{1\times 3\times\ldots}\oplus{R}^{3\times 3\times\ldots}\oplus{R}^{5\times 3\times\ldots}

(A.4)

Each partially decomposed tensor can then be further decomposed by the same process, e.g., use
C^{(j)}_{2,1}
to decompose the tensor in
{R}^{5\times 3\times\ldots}
into tensors in
{R}^{3\times\ldots}
,
{R}^{5\times\ldots}
,
\mathbb{R}^{7\times\ldots}
.
The Cartesian tensor can be recovered similarly, as in Eq. (
32
).
Note that the decomposition is order-dependent but equivalent, so we choose a convention.
For further details refer to Ref.
[
67
]
.

A.2
Wigner
\mathsf{D}
matrices

The CG matrices provide maps from tensors in their natural form, with basis elements in
{R}^{3}
, and products of IR vectors to IR vectors.
The Wigner
\mathsf{D}
matrices provide a change of basis operation for IR vectors, analogous to the Kronecker product for tensors in their natural form.
The Wigner
\mathsf{D}
matrices
\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}
map elements of SO(3) to
(2\ell+1)\times(2\ell+1)
dimensional (square) matrices,
e.g.
​
\mathsf{D}^{(0)}(\mathbf{R})=1
and
\mathsf{D}^{(1)}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}
.
For example

\mathsf{m}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{A})=\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{m}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{A})

(A.5)

is analogous to

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{R}\boxtimes\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{R}\boxtimes\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{A})\ .

(A.6)

For SO(3), the Wigner-
\mathsf{D}
matrices can be calculated
[
27
]
and the resulting IRs are of dimension,
\ell=1,3,\ldots,2n+1
.
A representation of SO(3) can in general be block diagonalized such that
V=W_{1}\oplus W_{2}\oplus\ldots
, where
W_{i}
are proper subspaces of
V
.
In fact a Wigner-
\mathsf{D}
matrix is an
irreducible
representation of proper rotations,
\mathsf{D}^{\ell}:\textrm{SO(3)}\rightarrow GL(V)
, i.e., there is no change of basis,
{\mathsf{D}^{\ell}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{G})=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\mathsf{D}^{\ell}(\mathbf{G})\mathsf{P}
, that block diagonalizes
{\mathsf{D}^{\ell}}^{\prime}
for all
\mathbf{G}
.
On the other hand, the tensor product of two CG representations is reducible; given two representations, their Kronecker product will decompose according to Clebsch-Gordon (CG) matrices.

In particular, spherical harmonic tensors
\mathsf{Y}^{(\ell)}
are equivariant with respect to the Wigner-
\mathsf{D}
matrices
\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}
in the sense

\mathsf{Y}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{R})\mathsf{Y}^{(\ell)}(\hat{\mathbf{r}})

(A.7)

where
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
is a unit vector,
i.e.
​  they transform properly with rotation of the coordinate basis.
On the other hand, an affine transformation such as multiplication by a weight matrix
\mathsf{W}
and addition of a bias
\mathsf{b}
does not

\mathsf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{a}^{(\ell)}+\mathsf{b}^{(\ell)}\neq\mathsf{D}^{(\ell)}\left[\mathsf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{a}^{(\ell)}+\mathsf{b}^{(\ell)}\right]

(A.8)

since
\mathsf{W}
does not commute with
\mathsf{D}
and the bias
\mathsf{b}
is not affected by the rotation of
\mathsf{a}
.

For more details refer to Refs.
[
66
,
67
,
82
]
.
