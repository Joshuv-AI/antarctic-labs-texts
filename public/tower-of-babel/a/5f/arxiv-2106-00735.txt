Ideals of spaces of degenerate matrices
Julian Vill, Mateusz Michałek, Alexander Taveira Blomenhofer

Abstract
. The variety Sing n , m \mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m} consists of all tuples X = ( X 1 , … , X m ) X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}) of n × n n\times n matrices such that every linear combination of X 1 , … , X m X_{1},\ldots,X_{m} is singular. Equivalently, X ∈ Sing n , m X\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m} if and only if det ( λ 1 ​ X 1 + … + λ m ​ X m ) = 0 \mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\lambda_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}X_{m})=0 for all λ 1 , … , λ m ∈ ℚ \lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in{\mathbb{Q}} . Makam and Wigderson [ 12 ] asked whether the ideal generated by these equations is always radical , that is, if any polynomial identity that is valid on Sing n , m \mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m} lies in the ideal generated by the polynomials det ( λ 1 ​ X 1 + … + λ m ​ X m ) \mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\lambda_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}X_{m}) . We answer this question in the negative by determining the vanishing ideal of Sing 2 , m \mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{2,m} for all m ∈ ℕ m\in{\mathbb{N}} . Our results exhibit that there are additional equations arising from the tensor structure of X X . More generally, for any n n and m ≥ n 2 − n + 1 m\geq n^{2}-n+1 , we prove there are equations vanishing on Sing n , m \mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m} that are not in the ideal generated by polynomials of type det ( λ 1 ​ X 1 + … + λ m ​ X m ) \mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\lambda_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}X_{m}) . Our methods are based on classical results about Fano schemes, representation theory and Gröbner bases.

1.
Introduction

1.A.
Motivation

In the late 70’s, Valiant
[
11
]
proposed an algebraic analogue of the infamous P vs NP question, using a computational model distinct from Turing machines, the model of
arithmetic circuits
. The model of arithmetic circuits captures the natural ways to compute a polynomial function from the basic arithmetic operations, addition and multiplication, via a directed graph encoding how the arithmetic operations are to be nested. Considering such circuits exhibits in many cases non-obvious ways to compute a polynomial that can be much more efficient compared to naively plugging in values into an expanded form. However, since circuits overparameterize polynomials, the problem of polynomial identity testing (PIT), i.e. whether two circuits define the same polynomial, arises naturally. A special case of (PIT) is Determinant Identity Testing (DIT), where a polynomial
p_{A}
in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
is given by a tuple of matrices
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})\in({\mathbb{Q}}^{n\times n})^{m}
and the formula

p_{A}=\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}A_{i}).

While computing the determinant of a fixed rational matrix is easy, there is no known efficient
deterministic
algorithm to even check if an expression such as
\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}A_{i})
evaluates to the constant zero polynomial. This is curious, since an efficient
probabilistic
algorithm is to simply evaluate such a linear matrix expression in a few random points (which is essentially a consequence of the Schwartz-Zippel lemma
[
10
]
,
[
13
]
). Finding such an algorithm would have a major impact towards resolving Valiant’s analogue of the P vs NP question
[
6
]
. For a detailed introduction to Valiant’s classes and circuit complexity, we refer to
[
9
]
.

1.B.
Algebraic View and Contributions

Determinant identity testing is the problem to decide membership in the algebraic variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
, i.e. given a matrix tuple
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})
, determine whether all linear combinations of the
A_{i}
are singular.
The algebraization of the (DIT) problem was driven e.g. by Makam and Wigderson
[
12
]
, who argued that understanding the geometric structure of the variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
might be a stepping stone on a long climb towards resolving VP vs VNP.
In this work, we will answer the question of radicality Makam and Wigderson posed in
[
12
, Problem 12.6]
,
[
7
, Problem 4.6]
: Are

\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}X^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}],\qquad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m})

all generators of the vanishing ideal of
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
?
1
1

1

Here,
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}
denote matrices of algebraically independent variables.
While these equations set-theoretically describe the variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
and while, from a (DIT)-oriented perspective, they form the most natural set of elements in the ideal of
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
, it turns out that the ideal generated by these polynomials is in general not radical.
In fact, looking at the problem from the perspective of
3
-tensors makes other identities apparent from rank constraints on different flattenings. Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

The ideal of the variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{2,m}
is generated by quadratic polynomials

\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}X^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}],\qquad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m})

if and only if
m\leq 2
. For
m\geq 3
this ideal is generated by quadrics and cubics.

For any
n
, the ideal of the variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
is not generated by the polynomials

\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}X^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}],\qquad(\lambda\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m})

if
m>n^{2}-n
.

1.C.
Related work

By a celebrated result of Kabanets and Impagliazzo
[
6
]
, a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for (DIT) would show that either NEXP
\not\subset
P/poly or the permanent is not computable by polynomially-sized arithmetic circuits. Since the permanent is complete for VNP
[
9
]
, this would give evidence to a separation of the Valiant classes VP and VNP. The Kabanets-Impagliazzo result is conceptually remarkable in the sense that
constructing
an algorithm for (DIT) would indicate
hardness
of some family in VNP. It has thus motivated extensive studies on the algebraic properties of (DIT).

Perhaps surprisingly, the case of non-commuting variables turned out easier. A line of work (e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
) showed the existence of a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for the non-commutative analogue of (DIT). Besides the derandomization of (DIT), other approaches for the resolution of VP vs VNP have been studied, recently e.g. via sparsity of families of Sum-of-Squares representations
[
3
]
. Our methods are based on the fundamental article by Chan and Ilten
[
2
]
.

2.
Preliminaries and general notation

In what follows, we identify
n\times n
matrices with elements of the tensor product
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Further, we identify m-tuples
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})
of
n\times n
-matrices with
3
-tensors, that is, elements
\sum_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}\otimes A_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Let

\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}:=\{(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})\in({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n})^{m}\mid\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}:\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}A_{k}\text{ is singular }\}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

be the set of all singular matrix tuples, i.e. all elements such that the image of the corresponding map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
only contains matrices of rank at most
n-1
. We write
I_{n,m}
or
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})
for the vanishing ideal of
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
in the polynomial ring in matrices
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}
of algebraically independent indeterminates
(x_{ij}^{(k)})_{i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, where
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

The variety
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
comes with a natural group action of the group
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}
, which we will call
G:=G_{n,m}
throughout the paper.
G
acts on
3
-tensors by “left-right-back” multiplication, i.e. in the language of matrix tuples, if
(U,V,W)\in G
and
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
, then

(U,V,W)A=(VA_{1}W,\ldots,VA_{m}W)U

Clearly, the determinant of a fixed matrix can only change by a nonzero scalar under left-right multiplication with an element of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}
. On the other hand, linear transformations on the
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
-mode just correspond to taking different linear combinations, whence
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
is invariant under the action of
G
. We will assume that the reader is familiar with basic representation theory, in particular heighest weight vectors for the general linear group. For a brief introduction we refer to
[
8
, Chapter 10]
and for a detailed one to
[
4
]
.

2.A.
Ideal generation and multiplication maps

When studying ideal generation via the means of invariant theory, it is crucial to understand what happens if we take the tensor product of irreducible representations: It turns out that the polynomials of degree
d^{\prime}+d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
generated by the degree-
d
part of some ideal
I
are the image of a sum of tensor products of explicit modules. For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, tensor products of irreducible
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}
-modules are understood by the Littlewood-Richardson rule.

Let
I
a homogeneous ideal of a standard graded ring
R
(e.g. a polynomial ring graded by the total degree). For
d,d^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we denote by
R_{d}
(resp:
I_{d}
) the degree-
d
component of
R
(resp:
I
). Furthermore, assume that a group
H
acts on
R_{d}
and
I_{d}
for any
d
, thus turning both
I
and
R
into
H
-modules. Then the multiplication map

I_{d}\times R_{d^{\prime}}\to I_{d+d^{\prime}},(p,r)\mapsto r\cdot p

induces the following map of
H
-representations

M_{I,d,d^{\prime}}:I_{d}\otimes R_{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow I_{d+d^{\prime}}.

The image of
M_{I,d,d^{\prime}}
are those elements in the ideal that are of degree
d+d^{\prime}
and lie in the ideal generated by all elements of
I
of degree at most
d
(equivalently: of degree precisely
d
). In the case
R={\mathbb{C}}[X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(m)}]
and
I=I_{n,m}
we will write
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
instead of
M_{I,d,d^{\prime}}
and suppress the dependency on
n
.

Now, there are finite collections
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{W}
of irreducible modules such that
I_{d}
and
R_{d^{\prime}}
split into sums

I_{d}=\bigoplus_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V

and

R_{d^{\prime}}=\bigoplus_{W\in\mathcal{W}}W.

The image of
M_{I,d,d^{\prime}}
is then spanned by the images of the modules
V\otimes W
, where
(V,W)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{W}
. In particular, assume
I=I_{n,m}
with
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}
acting on it. Then by the representation theory of the general linear group, the image of
M_{I,d,d^{\prime}}
is spanned by the images of modules isomorphic to

S^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{m})\otimes S^{\mu}({\mathbb{C}}^{m})

where
\lambda\vdash d
and
\mu\vdash d^{\prime}
. In this case, the Littlewood-Richardson rule describes how such a product of representation decomposes as a sum (cf.
[
4
, Appendix A.1]
).

3.
The case of
2\times 2
matrices

In this section we restrict our attention to the case
n=2
. We determine generators, and in fact the whole Gröbner basis of the vanishing ideal
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{2,m})
. Our main idea is based on the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 5.8]
.

For
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, let
X_{k}=(x_{ij}^{(k)})
be matrices with distinct indeterminate entries.
Let
I=I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{2,m})\subset{\mathbb{C}}[x_{ij}^{(k)}]
be the homogeneous vanishing ideal of
X
. By
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
, the degree one component
I_{1}
is 0, and the degree two component is spanned by the forms
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(X_{i})
for every
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let
J
be the ideal generated by
I_{2}
. Then the following holds.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
m\geq 3
then the ideal
J
is not radical. The ideal
I=\sqrt{J}
is generated by
I_{2}
and all
3\times 3
minors of the linear map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Proof.

We show this by constructing a Gröbner basis of an ideal
\tilde{I}
that contains
J
and is contained in
I
. As the leading monomials are all square-free, the ideal
\tilde{I}
must be radical. Thus
\tilde{I}=I
.
As a monomial ordering we consider the degrevlex-ordering, ordering the variables first by matrix, then by column and lastly by row, i.e.
x_{ij}^{(k)}>x_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{(k^{\prime})}
if (
k<k^{\prime}
) or (
k=k^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
) or (
k=k^{\prime}
and
j=j^{\prime}
and
i<i^{\prime}
).

In degree two we consider the forms

(3.1)

\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(X_{i}+X_{j})-\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits X_{i}-\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits X_{j}\quad\forall i,j.

These generate
J_{2}
: Consider
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
as indeterminates and consider the quadratic form
q:=\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}X_{i})
in the variables
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
.
The
\binom{m+1}{2}
coefficients of
q
span a subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{ij}^{(k)}]
of dimension at most
\binom{m+1}{2}
. However, all
\binom{m+1}{2}
forms above are contained in this subspace, and are linearly independent (for
i\neq j
the form
\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(X_{i}+X_{j})
is the only one containing any products of the variables of the
i
-th and
j
-th matrix). Hence, these forms form a basis of
J_{2}
.

In degree three, for every
i,j,k
we consider all
3\times 3
minors of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}X_{i}&X_{j}\\
X_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let

T=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}^{(1)}&x_{12}^{(1)}&x_{21}^{(1)}&x_{22}^{(1)}\\
x_{11}^{(2)}&x_{12}^{(2)}&x_{21}^{(2)}&x_{22}^{(2)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{11}^{(m)}&x_{12}^{(m)}&x_{21}^{(m)}&x_{22}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}

and consider the following
m\times 2
submatrices

T_{12},T_{13},T_{24},T_{34}
where
T_{ij}
contains the
i
-th and the
j
-th column of
T
.

Let
G_{12},G_{13},G_{24},G_{34}
be the sets containing the
2\times 2
minors of the corresponding matrices. Consider the products
G_{12}G_{24},G_{13}G_{34}
.

A Gröbner basis
G
for
\tilde{I}
will be given by

(1)

all equations in
eq.

3.1
,

(2)

all
3\times 3
minors as above, after removing all forms whose leading term is not square-free,

(3)

and all elements of the sets
G_{12}G_{24},G_{13}G_{34}
, after removing all forms whose leading term is not square-free.

That all these forms are contained in
I
is proved in Lemma
3.2
.

We prove the theorem relying on a computer algebra system as follows. To prove that
G
is a Gröbner basis, by Buchberger’s criterion, we need to show that all
S
-pairs reduce to
0
.
Let
f,g\in G
. By construction
f
and
g
each contain the variables of at most four matrices. If
f
and
g
both contain variables of four different matrices such that combined there appear variables of eight matrices, then by construction, the variables of
f
and of
g
are disjoint. It follows that the
S
-pair of
f
and
g
reduces to 0, by Buchberger’s second criterion.
If this is not the case, then
f
and
g
contain the variables of at most seven different matrices. It therefore suffices to show the statement for
m=2,\dots,7
. We verify this using a computer. Hence,
G
is a Gröbner basis.

Every form of degree two or three in this Gröbner basis contains only monomials that are square-free. For forms in
G_{12}G_{24}
and
G_{13}G_{34}
this is not true. However, the leading monomial is still square-free. In particular, the leading ideal of
\tilde{I}
is radical, and so is
\tilde{I}
. Thus
\tilde{I}=I
and
G
is in fact a Gröbner basis of
I
. We note that the degree four elements of the Gröbner basis are generated by quadrics and cubics (which may be checked e.g. using a computer).

Lemma 3.2
.

All forms in
G
are contained in
I
.

Proof.

This is clear for the forms in
eq.

3.1
.
Modulo
J
, the
3\times 3
minors we have in
G
are the same as the
3\times 3
minors of the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. This can be explicitly written down as there are only three different matrices involved.

It thus suffices to show that the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
has rank at most 2. We consider the corresponding map between projective spaces

{\mathbb{P}}{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}).

By assumption the image is contained in the set of rank one matrices which is
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. This variety only contains three types of subspaces: For every
v,w\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
the spaces
v\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times w
and

are contained. There are no subspaces of (projective) dimension two contained in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
has dimension at most two.

Lastly, we need to check the elements of
G_{12}G_{24},G_{13}G_{34}
. Let
A\in X
be an
m

span a one-dimensional space, or the rows span a one-dimensional space (or both). Hence, either every form in
G_{12}
vanishes or every form in
G_{24}
vanishes, and the same holds for
G_{13}
and
G_{34}

4.
There are (essentially) no new equations for
m>n^{2}

For
m>n^{2}
, expressions of the kind
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}X_{i}
, (
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
) become overparameterized due to the dimension of the matrix space being
n^{2}
. Since membership in
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
does set-theoretically only depend on the slices of a given
3
-tensor, one would both hope and expect that the “complexity” needed to describe the vanishing ideal
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})
does not increase beyond
m=n^{2}
. However, we need to be careful with the precise formulation of such a statement. For
m>n^{2}
, clearly
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
and
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,n^{2}}
are subvarieties of spaces of different dimensions, with different groups acting on them.

In this short section, we will show that if
m
grows beyond
n^{2}
, the “new” equations in the vanishing ideal
I_{n,m}=I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})
of
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
that make use of the new variables in
X^{(n^{2}+1)},\ldots,X^{m}
can be obtained from “old” equations in
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,n^{2}})
via the action of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}
. To this end, we will make use of the well-known decomposition of the (graded components of the) polynomial ring on
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n^{2}}
endowed with the left-right action of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n^{2}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
m,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Consider
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q}
with the natural left-right action of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{q}
, turning the ring of polynomial functions

{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q}]\cong\bigoplus_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}S^{d}({\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q})

into a
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{q}
-module. For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have the following decomposition into irreducible modules

S^{d}({\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q})\cong\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}S^{\lambda}{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes S^{\lambda}{\mathbb{C}}^{q}

where
\lambda
ranges over all nonnegative integer partitions of
d
.

Proof.

By looking at the characters this is the celebrated Cauchy identity
[
4
, Appendix A.13]
.

Remark 4.2
.

By definition of the Schur functor,
S^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{q})=0
whenever the partition
\lambda
has more than
q
parts (i.e. whenever
\lambda
corresponds to a Young tableau with strictly more than
q
rows).

Notation 4.3
.

For
m,n,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
q:=n^{2}
and fix an identification of the
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{q}
-modules
S^{d}({\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q})
and
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q}]_{d}
along with embeddings of the irreducible components of
S^{d}({\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q})
into
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{q}]_{d}
.

For
m>n^{2}
, we consider the inclusion
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,n^{2}})\hookrightarrow I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})
. We choose
n\times n
matrices of distinct variables
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}
such that
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}]\cong{\mathbb{C}}[X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(m)}]
and fix a monomial ordering on the variables such that all entries of
X^{(1)}
are greater than all entries of
X^{(2)}
, which in turn are greater than those of
X^{(3)}
and so forth.

Theorem 4.4
.

For
m>n^{2}
, every irreducible subrepresentation of
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})
has the highest weight vector in
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,n^{2}})
.

Proof.

Let
V
be an irreducible
G
-submodule of
I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m})_{d}
. Then
V\hookrightarrow S^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{m})\otimes S^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{n^{2}})
by the above considerations for some partition
\lambda\vdash d
with at most
n^{2}
parts. The highest weight vector
f_{\lambda}\in V
, by definition has weight given by
\lambda

[
8
, Chapter 10.2]
. In particular, it is a linear combination of monomials that use only the variables in
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(n^{2})}
. That is
f_{\lambda}\in I(\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,n^{2}})
.

Corollary 4.5
.

For fixed
n
, all ideals
I_{n,m}
are generated in degree at most
d
if and only
I_{n,n^{2}}
is generated in degree
d
.

Proof.

Let
R_{n,m}:={\mathbb{C}}[X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(m)}]
.
The multiplication map induces a map of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}
representations:

M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}:(I_{n,m})_{d}\otimes(R_{n,m})_{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow(I_{n,m})_{d+d^{\prime}}.

The image of
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
are those elements in the ideal that are of degree
d+d^{\prime}
and are generated by elements of degree
d
. In particular, our assumptions tell us that
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
is surjective for
m=n^{2}
and any
d^{\prime}
.

First we consider the more basic case
m<n^{2}
. Let us break
(I_{n,n^{2}})_{d}
(resp.
(R_{n,n^{2}})_{d^{\prime}}
, resp.
(I_{n,n^{2}})_{d+d^{\prime}}
) into a direct sum of representations
I_{1}\oplus I_{2}
(resp.
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
, resp.
J_{1}\oplus J_{2}
) , where in
I_{1}
(resp.
R_{1}
, resp.
J_{1}
) are all isotypic components corresponding to Young diagrams with at most
m
rows and in
I_{2}
(resp.
R_{2}
, resp.
J_{2}
) are those with more than
m
rows. We note that the highest weight vectors in
I_{1}
(resp.
R_{1}
, resp.
J_{1}
) are exactly the highest weight vectors in
(I_{n,m})_{d}
(resp.
(R_{n,m})_{d^{\prime}}
, resp.
(I_{n,m})_{d+d^{\prime}}
). Further, all Young diagrams corresponding to representations in
M_{n^{2},d,d^{\prime}}\left(I_{2}\otimes(R_{n,n^{2}})_{d^{\prime}}\right)
and
M_{n^{2},d,d^{\prime}}\left((I_{n,n^{2}})_{d}\otimes(R_{2})_{d^{\prime}}\right)
must have at least
m+1
rows. Hence, the image of
I_{1}\otimes R_{1}
must contain
J_{1}
. For each Young diagram with at most
m
rows, the multiplicity of the corresponding isotypic component in the representation
I_{1}\otimes R_{1}
is the same as in
(I_{n,m})_{d}\otimes(R_{n,m})_{d^{\prime}}
(but the representations are not the same, as these are representations of different groups). Similarly
J_{1}
and
(I_{n,m})_{d+d^{\prime}}
are represented by exactly the same Young diagrams. For contradiction assume that
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
is not surjective. This would mean that there is a (highest weight) vector in the kernel of
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
that, after mapping to
I_{1}\otimes R_{1}
is not in the kernel of
M_{n^{2},d,d^{\prime}}
. This is not possible, as multiplication of polynomials gives the same result, no matter in a ring of how many variables we regard the polynomials.

With these tools the more important case
m>n^{2}
follows easily. Indeed, to show that
M_{m,d,d^{\prime}}
is surjective it is enough to prove that every highest weight vector is in the image. But any highest weight vector must be in fact an element of
(I_{n,n^{2}})_{d+d^{\prime}}
. In particular, it must be in the image of the smaller space

(I_{n,n^{2}})_{d}\otimes(R_{n,n^{2}})_{d^{\prime}}\subset(I_{n,m})_{d}\otimes(R_{n,m})_{d^{\prime}}
.

Remark 4.6
.

Corollary
4.5
gives another easy proof of Theorem
3.1
, as it is enough to check it for
m=4
. We note that further improvements are possible, due to the fact that
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
is contained in a subspace variety. This topic will be explained in the forthcoming section.

5.
Connections to the Fano scheme

Let
I_{n,m}\subset{\mathbb{C}}[x_{ij}^{(k)}]={\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}]
be the homogeneous vanishing ideal of
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
. As shown in the last section, there are no ’new’ generators for
m>n^{2}
. This followed solely by considering the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{ij}^{(k)}]
and the action of
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}\times\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}
on it.
In particular, we did not make use of the fact that we are only interested in generators of
I_{n,m}
.

We do this now using Fano schemes. Let
D_{n}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{2}-1}
be the subvariety consisting of all singular matrices.
Let
k
be a positive integer. We denote by
\mathbf{F}_{k}(D_{n})
the Fano scheme of
D_{n}
, which parametrizes the
k
-dimensional planes in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{2}-1}
that are subvarieties of
D_{n}
.

Let
X\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Let
L
be the image of the flattening
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Since
X\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}
every point in
L
is singular which means
L\subset D_{n}
. By definition we hence see that
L\in\mathbf{F}_{\dim(L)-1}(D_{n})
. This Fano scheme has been extensively studied for example by Chan and Ilten in
[
2
]
. In the following we will make use of these results.

We show how to get new generators of
I_{n,m}
using equations coming from this Fano scheme.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
n>1
and
m\geq n^{2}-n+1
. The ideal
I_{n,m}
is not generated in degree
n
.

Proof.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then
X
defines the linear subspace
L=\mathrm{span}(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\subset({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n})^{m}
which is contained in
D_{n}
.
Let
k
be the dimension of
L
, then
L
defines a point in
\mathbf{F}_{k-1}(D_{n})
.

By
[
2
, Proposition 2.6.]

\mathbf{F}_{k-1}(D_{n})
is empty if and only if
k>n(n-1)
. Since
m\geq n^{2}-n+1
, we have
m>k
and the matrices
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are linearly dependent.

Equivalently the rank of the flattening
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is at most
n^{2}-n
, which means that all
(n^{2}-n+1)\times(n^{2}-n+1)
-minors of this map vanish. More explicitly those are the minors of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}^{(1)}&x_{12}^{(1)}&\dots&x_{nn}^{(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
x_{11}^{(m)}&x_{12}^{(m)}&\dots&x_{nn}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}

if coordinates are chosen such that
X_{l}=(x_{ij}^{(l)})_{ij}
for
l=1,\dots,m
.
By construction every subspace
L
coming from
X\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
satisfies these equations and therefore they are contained in the ideal
I_{n,m}
.

To show that these polynomials of
I_{n,m}
are not generated by the polynomials in degree up to
n
we consider the group action of
G=\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{m}\otimes\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}\otimes\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{n}
on
I_{m,n}
.
The equations we found come from the
(n^{2}-n+1)
-st exterior power of the flattening map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\rightarrow({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
. Thus correspond to
\bigwedge^{n^{2}-n+1}{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes\bigwedge^{n^{2}-n+1}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
. As a
G
representation this is a union of irreducible representations (with multiplicity one) indexed by triples of Young diagrams
(1,\dots,1)=1^{n^{2}-n+1},\lambda,\lambda^{T}
, where
\lambda
is (any) partition of
n^{2}-n+1
fitting in an
n\times n
square and
\lambda^{T}
is its transpose.

By
[
12
, Corollary B.6 and Proposition 5.1]
we know that
I_{n,m}
up to degree
n
consists just of one irreducible representation indexed by the triple of Young diagrams:
(n),1^{n},1^{n}
.
The image of the tensor product by the multiplication map:
(I_{n,m})_{d}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(m)}]_{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow(I_{n,m})_{d+d^{\prime}}
is the part of the ideal in the degree
d+d^{\prime}
generated by elements in degree
d
. Taking
d=n
, the domain of this map is a sum of irreducible representations indexed by triples of Young diagrams
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}
, where, by the Littlewood-Richardson rule,
\mu_{1}
must contain
(n)
(and
\mu_{2},\mu_{3}
must contain
1^{n}
, however we do not need this). In particular, the equations we presented cannot belong to the image of the map, as
1^{n^{2}-n+1}
does not contain
(n)
.

Remark 5.2
.

In the case
n=2
, the new equations are exactly the cubics in
3.1
. In the case
n=2
, those are all generators of
I_{2,m}
as we have seen. However, for larger
n
this is most likely not true.

We recall that by
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
for any
d<n
the degree
d
component
(I_{n,m})_{d}
is
\{0\}
and for
d=n
we have
(I_{n,m})_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum c_{i}X_{i})\colon c_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}\}
.

Corollary 5.3
.

The ideal generated by the degree
n
component
(I_{n,m})_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathop{\rm det}\nolimits(\sum c_{i}X_{i})\colon c_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}\}
of
I_{n,m}
is never radical if
m\geq n^{2}-n+1
.

Remark 5.4
.

We note that new polynomials in
I_{m,n}
from
5.1
were a result of rank restrictions on the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
However, there are also two more canonical flattenings of tensors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, namely
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
for the two different copies of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
In the next part we make use also of those two flattenings and show how to construct more polynomials in
I_{n,m}
.
This new construction does not require the integer
m
to be as large.

Instead of fixing
m
immediately and working in
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}
we consider more generally a tensor
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and study its image by the flattening map in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
L\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a subspace of dimension
k
. If
L
is contained in
D_{n}
and
k\geq n^{2}-2n+2
, then after a change of coordinates either every matrix in
L
has

(1)

a zero-row, or

(2)

a zero-column, or

(3)

the form

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{n^{2}-2n+1}&\lambda_{n^{2}-2n+2}\\
\vdots&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\dots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{n}&\dots&0&0\end{pmatrix},

or

(4)

the form

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{n^{2}-2n+1}&\lambda_{n^{2}-2n+2}\\
\vdots&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\dots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{n}&\dots&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

Proof.

By
[
2
, Corollary 5.1]
the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}_{k-1}(D_{n})
consists only of compression subspaces, i.e. there exists
0\leq s\leq n-1
such that
L
is contained in the compression space
\mathfrak{C}_{k-1}(s)
. By definition this means there exists a subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of dimension
s+1
that is mapped to a subspace
W\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of dimension
s
by every element of
L
.

By
[
2
, Remark 5.2]
the only possiblities for such
s
are
s=0,1,n-1,n-2
if
k\geq n^{2}-2n+2
. These correspond exactly to the four cases in the claim.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X\in\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}
. Then the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
has rank at most
n^{2}-2n+2
or one of the two flattenings
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
has rank at most
n-1
.

Proof.

Let
L
be the image of the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and denote its dimension by
k
. If
k>n^{2}-2n+2
, then by
5.5
the subspace
L
has one of the two given forms (1), (2) in Proposition
5.5
. Hence, the rank of one of the maps
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is at most
n-1
depending if we are in case (1) or (2).

Remark 5.7
.

From
5.6
we get more equations for
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{n,m}
as follows. Let
J_{M}
be the ideal generated by the
n^{2}-2n+3
minors of the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let
J_{1},J_{2}
be the ideals generated by the
n
minors of the other two flattenings. Then
J_{M}\cdot J_{1}\cdot J_{2}\subset(I_{n,m})_{n^{2}+3}
.

Compared to
5.1
we should consider
m\geq n^{2}-2n+3
so that the map
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can have large enough rank.

Using this we get equations in the case
n=3
and
m=6
whereas earlier we needed
m\geq 7
in this case.

Remark 5.8
.

Our theorems suggest the following idea to construct more equations for
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}
. Consider a
k
-dimensional subspace
L\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and study possible forms of this subspace using the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}_{k-1}(D_{n})
. The conditions/equations of the Fano scheme translate to equations for
\mathop{\rm Sing}\nolimits_{m,n}
as above.
