Flat ( 𝟐 , 𝟑 , 𝟓 ) \boldsymbol{(2,3,5)} -Distributions and Chazy’s Equations
Matthew RANDALL

Abstract
In the geometry of generic 2-plane fields on 5-manifolds, the local equivalence problem was solved by Cartan who also constructed the fundamental curvature invariant. For generic 2-plane fields or ( 2 , 3 , 5 ) (2,3,5) -distributions determined by a single function of the form F ⁡ ( q ) F(q) , the vanishing condition for the curvature invariant is given by a 6 th order nonlinear ODE. Furthermore, An and Nurowski showed that this ODE is the Legendre transform of the 7 th order nonlinear ODE described in Dunajski and Sokolov. We show that the 6 th order ODE can be reduced to a 3 rd order nonlinear ODE that is a generalised Chazy equation. The 7 th order ODE can similarly be reduced to another generalised Chazy equation, which has its Chazy parameter given by the reciprocal of the former. As a consequence of solving the related generalised Chazy equations, we obtain additional examples of flat ( 2 , 3 , 5 ) (2,3,5) -distributions not of the form F ⁡ ( q ) = q m F(q)=q^{m} . We also give 4-dimensional split signature metrics where their twistor distributions via the An–Nurowski construction have split G 2 G_{2} as their group of symmetries.

Introduction

The following
6^{\rm th}
order nonlinear ODE

\displaystyle 10F^{(6)}{F^{\prime\prime}}^{3}-80{F^{\prime\prime}}^{2}F^{(3)}F^{(5)}-51{F^{\prime\prime}}^{2}{F^{(4)}}^{2}+336F^{\prime\prime}{F^{(3)}}^{2}F^{(4)}-224{F^{(3)}}^{4}=0,

(1.1)

arises in
[
6
, Corollary 2.1]
in the study of generic 2-plane fields on 5-manifolds. The genericity condition here means
F^{\prime\prime}(q)\neq 0
in (
1.1
). This ODE arises as the integrability condition for generic 2-plane fields on 5 manifolds determined by a function of a single variable of the form
F(q)
. A generic 2-plane field
\mathcal{D}
on a 5-manifold
M
is a maximally non-integrable rank 2 distribution. For further details, see
[
6
,
18
,
24
,
25
]
. This determines a filtration of the tangent bundle given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\subset[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]\subset[[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}],\mathcal{D}]=TM.

The distribution
[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]
has rank 3 while the full tangent space
TM
has rank 5, hence such a geometry is also known as a
(2,3,5)
-distribution.
Let
M_{xyzpq}
denote the 5-dimensional manifold with local coordinates given by
(x,y,z,p,q)
.
The generic 2-plane field or rank 2 distribution determined by a function
F(q)
of a single variable with
F^{\prime\prime}(q)\neq 0
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\operatorname{span}\{\partial_{q},\partial_{x}+p\partial_{y}+q\partial_{p}+F(q)\partial_{z}\}.

The fundamental Cartan curvature invariant of this distribution is computed in
[
6
]
and is found to be the term in the left hand side of (
1.1
).
It is known that equation (
1.1
)
vanishes when
F(q)=q^{m}
and
m\in\big\{{-}1,\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3},2\big\}
.
In these cases, the vanishing of the fundamental curvature invariant associated to the distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
M_{xyzpq}
implies that the group of local symmetries is the maximal possible given by the split real form of
G_{2}
. This is the result of
[
6
, Corollary 2.1]
.
The authors of
[
6
]
call such generic 2-plane fields with vanishing Cartan curvature invariant symmetric and it is known
that such symmetric or flat distributions are locally equivalent to the flat model
F(q)=q^{2}
. Nonetheless we are interested in the general solution to (
1.1
) and it turns out that the ODE can be solved completely and is related to the generalised Chazy equation. To see this, let

E(q)=F^{\prime\prime}(q)
so that the ODE becomes
4^{\rm th}
order:

\displaystyle 10E^{(4)}E^{3}-80E^{2}E^{\prime}E^{\prime\prime\prime}-51E^{2}{E^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+336E{E^{\prime}}^{2}E^{\prime\prime}-224{E^{\prime}}^{4}=0.

Working locally on an open set of
M_{xyzpq}
, we may assume that
E(q)
is positive on that open set.
Making the substitution
E(q)={\rm e}^{G(q)}
(if
E(q)<0
, take
E(q)=-{\rm e}^{G(q)}
instead)
gives

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{4G(q)}\big(10G^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-40G^{\prime\prime\prime}G^{\prime}-21(G^{\prime\prime})^{2}+54G^{\prime\prime}(G^{\prime})^{2}-9(G^{\prime})^{4}\big)=0

and taking
G^{\prime}(q)=j(q)
gives a
3^{\rm rd}
order ODE

\displaystyle 10j^{\prime\prime\prime}-40j^{\prime\prime}j-21(j^{\prime})^{2}+54j^{\prime}j^{2}-9j^{4}=0.

Rescaling the ODE by taking
j(q)=\frac{I(q)}{2}
, we can put it into the normal form for the generalised Chazy equation (see
[
14
]
)

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime\prime}-2I^{\prime\prime}I+3(I^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{4}{36-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2}}\big(6I^{\prime}-I^{2}\big)^{2}=0

(1.2)

with the Chazy parameter
k^{2}=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2}=\frac{4}{9}
.
The generalised Chazy equation can be solved completely and the solutions give us new families of flat
(2,3,5)
-distributions that are not of the form
F(q)=q^{m}
. In this article we first review the solutions to Chazy’s equations in Sections
2
and
3
.
In Section
4
we discuss the relationship between (
1.1
) and a
7^{\rm th}
order ODE studied by Dunajski and Sokolov in
[
16
]
and also exhibit a Legendre transform that relates equation (
1.2
) to another generalised Chazy equation with the Chazy parameter given by
k^{2}=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{9}{4}
.
We compute the solutions to (
1.2
) in Section
5
and present examples of flat
(2,3,5)
-distributions in Section
6
using Nurowski’s metric. These examples are all explicit.
In
[
5
]
, the authors associated to split signature conformal structures on a 4-manifold a circle bundle with the natural structure of a
(2,3,5)
-distribution. This construction encapsulates the configuration space of 2 surfaces rolling along one another without slipping and twisting. The authors in
[
5
]
then found new examples of flat
(2,3,5)
-distributions that arise from rolling bodies, prompting further search in
[
8
]
. The solutions to (
1.2
) give examples of 4-dimensional split signature metrics that have their An–Nurowski twistor distributions having split
G_{2}
as its group of symmetries and we exhibit them in Section
7
. Let us recall some facts about Chazy’s equation and its generalised version.

2
Chazy’s equation

The study of Chazy’s equation is a very rich subject and has received alot of attention because of its connection to other diverse fields such as integrable systems and modular forms. See for instance
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
14
]
.
We will review here some facts about Chazy’s equation we need for the paper. Chazy
[
12
,
13
]
studied the nonlinear
3^{\rm rd}
order ODE

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)-2y(x)y^{\prime\prime}(x)+3(y^{\prime}(x))^{2}=0

(2.1)

in the context of investigating its Painlevé property. Solutions to equation (
2.1
) turn out to depend on hypergeometric functions. For further details, see
[
2
]
or
[
14
]
.
Treat
x
as a dependent variable of
s
so that

\displaystyle x(s)=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)},

where
z_{1}(s)
,
z_{2}(s)
are linearly independent solutions to the second order hypergeometric differential equation

\displaystyle s(1-s)z^{\prime\prime}+(c-(a+b+1)s)z^{\prime}-abz=0.

(2.2)

Here
a
,
b
,
c
are constants to be determined.
The general solution to this ODE (
2.2
) is given by hypergeometric functions

\displaystyle z(s)=\mu\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(a,b;c;s)+\nu\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(a-c+1,b-c+1;2-c;s)s^{1-c}.

Here
\mu
,
\nu
are constants.
A computation gives

\displaystyle{\rm d}x=\frac{z_{1}\dot{z}_{2}-z_{2}\dot{z}_{1}}{(z_{1})^{2}}{\rm d}s,

where dot denotes derivative with respect to
s
.
We deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}=\frac{(z_{1})^{2}}{z_{1}\dot{z}_{2}-z_{2}\dot{z}_{1}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}.

Applying the derivative to Chazy’s solution for
y
given by

\displaystyle y=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\log z_{1}=\frac{6z_{1}\dot{z}_{1}}{z_{1}\dot{z}_{2}-z_{2}\dot{z}_{1}},

(2.3)

we find that (
2.1
)
is satisfied precisely when
(a,b,c)
is one of

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{12},\frac{1}{12},\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\left(\frac{1}{12},\frac{1}{12},\frac{2}{3}\right),\qquad\left(\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{6},\frac{2}{3}\right),

provided both
a
and
b
are non-zero. The equations (
2.2
) for the first two values of
(a,b,c)
are related by a linear transformation of the form
s\mapsto 1-s
, while the solutions for the second and third values are related by a quadratic transformation (see
[
23
, equation (2)]
) given by

\displaystyle{}_{2}{F}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{6};\frac{2}{3};s\right)={}_{2}{F}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{12},\frac{1}{12};\frac{2}{3};4s(1-s)\right).

The general solution to (
2.1
) thus depend on hypergeometric functions. If either one of
a
or
b
is zero (say
b=0
), solutions to (
2.1
) can be easily and explicitly described. The solutions to (
2.2
)
with
b=0
are given by

\displaystyle z(s)=\mu+\nu\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(1-c,a+1-c;2-c;s)s^{1-c}=\mu-\frac{\nu\pi(c-1)}{\sin(\pi c)}P^{(1-c,~a-c)}_{c-1}(1-2s)s^{1-c},

where
P^{(a_{1},b_{1})}_{n}
is the Jacobi polynomial.
Taking
z_{1}(s)=\nu\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(1-c,a+1-c;2-c;s)s^{1-c}
and
z_{2}(s)=\mu
,
a computation shows that

\displaystyle x(s)=\frac{\mu}{\nu\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(1-c,a+1-c;2-c;s)s^{1-c}}

and

\displaystyle y(x(s))=-6\frac{\nu}{\mu}\,{}_{2}{F}_{1}(1-c,a+1-c;2-c;s)s^{1-c}.

Switching back to the original independent variable
x
, this gives

\displaystyle y(x)=-\frac{6}{x}

as one solution to (
2.1
). This solution is invariant under translations of the form
x\mapsto x+C
. In
[
12
,
13
]
, Chazy also observed that

\displaystyle y=-\frac{6}{x+C}-\frac{B}{(x+C)^{2}}

is a solution to (
2.1
). It is well-known that Chazy’s equation and its generalised version can be rewritten as a first order system. This provides different parametrisations of
y
, in addition to the solution (
2.3
) originally given by Chazy. This will be discussed in Section
5
. The method discussed here can also be applied to the generalised Chazy equation.

3
Generalised Chazy equations

The generalised Chazy equation is given by

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)-2y^{\prime\prime}(x)y(x)+3(y^{\prime}(x))^{2}-\frac{4}{36-k^{2}}(6y^{\prime}(x)-y(x)^{2})^{2}=0

(3.1)

for
k\neq\pm 6
. We have the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

x(s)=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)}
where
z_{1}(s)
,
z_{2}(s)
are linearly independent solutions to the hypergeometric differential equation (
2.2
)
where

(a,b,c)
is one of

\displaystyle\left(\frac{k-6}{12k},\frac{k+6}{12k},\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\left(\frac{k-6}{6k},\frac{k+6}{6k},\frac{2}{3}\right),\qquad\left(\frac{k-6}{12k},\frac{k+6}{12k},\frac{2}{3}\right).

Then

\displaystyle y(x(s))=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\log z_{1}=\frac{6z_{1}\dot{z}_{1}}{z_{1}\dot{z}_{2}-z_{2}\dot{z}_{1}}

satisfies equation (
3.1
).

Proof.

Analogous to solving Chazy’s equation (
2.1
), we find that the generalised equation (
3.1
) holds provided

\displaystyle 6ab(z_{1})^{8}\Big(6((a-b)k-6(a+b))((a-b)k+6(a+b))s^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}+\big((24ab-12(a+b)c+5(a+b)+(2c-1))k^{2}+432(a+b)c-180(a+b)-72c+36\big)s

\displaystyle\qquad{}+(k-6)(k+6)(2c-1)(3c-2)\Big)=0.

For
a,b\neq 0
, solving the system of equations

\displaystyle 6((a-b)k-6(a+b))((a-b)k+6(a+b))=0,

\displaystyle(24ab-12(a+b)c+5(a+b)+(2c-1))k^{2}+432(a+b)c-180(a+b)-72c+36=0,

\displaystyle(k-6)(k+6)(2c-1)(3c-2)=0,

gives the list of
(a,b,c)
as above. We exclude the case where
(a,b,c)=\big(0,0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
. Note that interchanging
a
and
b
gives the same solution so that the full list is symmetric in
a
and
b
.
∎

When either
a
or
b
is zero, we again get
y(x)=-\frac{6}{x}
as a solution. In
[
12
,
13
]
, Chazy noted that

\displaystyle y=\frac{k-6}{2(x+C)}-\frac{k+6}{2(x+B)}

(3.2)

is also a solution to (
3.1
).
As a corollary to Proposition
3.1
, we have

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
q(s)=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)}
where
z_{1}(s)
,
z_{2}(s)
are linearly independent solutions to the hypergeometric differential equation (
2.2
) with
(a,b,c)
one of

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{2}{3},\frac{5}{6},\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\left(-\frac{4}{3},\frac{5}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right),\qquad\left(-\frac{2}{3},\frac{5}{6},\frac{2}{3}\right).

Then

\displaystyle I(q(s))=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log z_{1}=\frac{6z_{1}\dot{z}_{1}}{z_{1}\dot{z}_{2}-z_{2}\dot{z}_{1}}

satisfies equation (
1.2
).

A Painlevé type analysis of equation (
3.1
) as done in
[
14
]
shows that the leading orders for analytic solutions to (
3.1
) occur at
-6
,
-3+\frac{k}{2}
or
-3-\frac{k}{2}
. This corresponds to solutions of (
3.1
) given by

\displaystyle y(x)=-\frac{6}{x},\qquad y(x)=\frac{-3+\frac{k}{2}}{x},\qquad y(x)=\frac{-3-\frac{k}{2}}{x}.

These solutions are invariant under translations of the form
x\mapsto x+C
.
In the case of
k=\pm\frac{2}{3}
obtained in (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle I(q)=-\frac{6}{q},\qquad I(q)=-\frac{10}{3q},\qquad I(q)=-\frac{8}{3q}.

Along with the zero solution
I(q)=0
,
these solutions correspond respectively (modulo constants of integration) to the well-known explicit solutions to (
1.1
):

\displaystyle F(q)=q^{-1},\qquad F(q)=q^{\frac{1}{3}},\qquad F(q)=q^{\frac{2}{3}},\qquad F(q)=q^{2}.

For these functions of a single variable
q
the associated
(2,3,5)
-distributions have vanishing Cartan invariant and therefore have
G_{2}
as their local symmetry.

4
Relationship to ODE studied by Dunajski and Sokolov

For the function
y=y(t)
, the
7^{\rm th}
order nonlinear ODE studied in
[
16
]
is given by

\displaystyle 10\big(y^{(3)}\big)^{3}y^{(7)}-70\big(y^{(3)}\big)^{2}y^{(4)}y^{(6)}-49\big(y^{(3)}\big)^{2}\big(y^{(5)}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+280\big(y^{(3)}\big)\big(y^{(4)}\big)^{2}y^{(5)}-175\big(y^{(4)}\big)^{4}=0.

(4.1)

This is the unique
7^{\rm th}
order ODE admitting the submaximal contact symmetry group of dimension ten (see
[
16
,
21
]
) and its relationship to equation (
1.1
) was originally explored in
[
6
]
. It is instructive to consider the
6^{\rm th}
order ODE (for the Legendre transformation later on):

\displaystyle 10\big(H^{(2)}\big)^{3}H^{(6)}-70\big(H^{(2)}\big)^{2}H^{(3)}H^{(5)}-49\big(H^{(2)}\big)^{2}\big(H^{(4)}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+280\big(H^{(2)}\big)\big(H^{(3)}\big)^{2}H^{(4)}-175\big(H^{(3)}\big)^{4}=0

(4.2)

with
H(t)=y^{\prime}(t)
. Let us show that this ODE can be reduced to a generalised Chazy equation.
Again working locally in an open set where
y^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)
is non-zero, and assuming
y^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)
to be positive, we can make the substitution
{\rm e}^{p(t)}=y^{(3)}
to get

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{p(t)}\big(10p^{(4)}-30p^{\prime}p^{\prime\prime\prime}-19(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}+32(p^{\prime})^{2}p^{\prime\prime}-4(p^{\prime})^{4}\big)=0.

(4.3)

We note that this
4^{\rm th}
order ODE historically appears in
[
9
, Section XII, formula (12)]
, where it first arises as the obstruction to integrability for
(2,3,5)
-distributions of the form
\mathcal{D}_{F(q)}
. This will be made clear below once we show that (
4.2
) is the Legendre transform of (
1.1
)
[
6
]
and we will discuss this further in Section
6
.
Thus, for
v(t)=p^{\prime}(t)
, we obtain the third order ODE

\displaystyle 10v^{\prime\prime\prime}-30vv^{\prime\prime}-19(v^{\prime})^{2}+32v^{\prime}v^{2}-4v^{4}=0.

(4.4)

Rescaling
v(t)
by
u(t)=\frac{3}{2}v(t)
, we put (
4.4
) into the normal form

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime\prime}-2u^{\prime\prime}u+3(u^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{4}{36-\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}}\big(6u^{\prime}-u^{2}\big)^{2}=0.

(4.5)

We therefore see that the ODE that Dunajski and Sokolov study in
[
16
]
reduces to a generalised Chazy equation (
4.5
) with parameter
k^{\prime}=\pm\frac{3}{2}
, related to the generalised Chazy equation (
1.2
) just by taking the reciprocals
(k^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}
of the corresponding parameters.

Let
t(s)=\frac{w_{2}(s)}{w_{1}(s)}
where
w_{1}(s)
,
w_{2}(s)
are linearly independent solutions to the hypergeometric differential equation (
2.2
) with
(a,b,c)
one of

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{12},\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\left(-\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{12},\frac{2}{3}\right),\qquad\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{5}{6},\frac{2}{3}\right).

The solution to (
4.5
) is then given by
u=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log w_{1}
. A similar leading order analysis as before shows that the leading orders occur at

\displaystyle-6,\quad-\frac{9}{8},\quad-\frac{15}{8}.

This corresponds to solutions of (
4.5
) given by

\displaystyle u(t)=-\frac{6}{t},\qquad u(t)=-\frac{9}{4t},\qquad u(t)=-\frac{15}{4t}.

Along with the zero solution
u(t)=0
,
these correspond respectively (modulo constants of integration) to solutions of (
4.2
) given by

\displaystyle H(t)=t^{-2},\qquad H(t)=t^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad H(t)=t^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad H(t)=t^{2}.

In
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
, it is shown that a Legendre transformation takes (
1.1
) to (
4.1
). Hence we may hypothesise that amongst all
3^{\rm rd}
order generalised Chazy equations, only those with the parameters
k^{\prime}=\pm\frac{3}{2}
,
k=\pm\frac{2}{3}
have in addition solutions that can be obtained from the dual equation via a Legendre transform.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

Consider the Legendre transformation

\displaystyle F(q)+H(t)=qt.

Then
F(q)
satisfies the ODE (
1.1
) iff
H(t)
satisfies the ODE (
4.2
).

Proof.

Applying the exterior derivative to the relation gives

\displaystyle(F^{\prime}-t){\rm d}q+(H^{\prime}-q){\rm d}t=0,

so that we take
F^{\prime}=t
,
H^{\prime}=q
and applying

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}=\frac{1}{H^{\prime\prime}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}

we obtain
F^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{H^{\prime\prime}}
,
F^{(3)}=-\frac{H^{(3)}}{(H^{\prime\prime})^{3}}
, etc.
A computation shows that the
6^{\rm th}
order ODE (
1.1
) holds for
F
iff (
4.2
) holds for
H
.
∎

In light of the solutions obtained by solving the generalised Chazy equations, we can pass to

\displaystyle F(q)=\iint{\rm e}^{\int\frac{I(q)}{2}{\rm d}q}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q,

(4.6)

where
q=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)}
and
I(q)=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log z_{1}
are given in Corollary
3.2
. This gives

\displaystyle F(q)=\iint(z_{1})^{3}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q.

Similarly, for the dual equation (
4.2
) under the Legendre transform we pass to

\displaystyle H(t)=\iint{\rm e}^{\int\frac{2u(t)}{3}{\rm d}t}{\rm d}t{\rm d}t,

where
t=\frac{w_{2}(s)}{w_{1}(s)}
and
u(t)=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log w_{1}
are solutions to (
4.5
). This gives

\displaystyle H(t)=\iint(w_{1})^{4}{\rm d}t{\rm d}t.

We have

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a Legendre transformation between Chazy’s solutions of (
1.2
) and (
4.5
) given by taking

\displaystyle w_{1}(s)=z_{1}^{-\frac{3}{4}},\qquad w_{2}(s)=(z_{1})^{-\frac{3}{4}}\int(z_{1})(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s.

This defines a mapping

\displaystyle q=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)}\mapsto t=\frac{w_{2}(s)}{w_{1}(s)}=\int z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s.

If
I(q)=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log z_{1}
solves (
1.2
) where
z_{1}(s)
and
z_{2}(s)
are given in Corollary
3.2
, then
u(t)=6\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log w_{1}
solves the dual ODE (
4.5
).
Consequently, if

F(q)=\iint(z_{1})^{3}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q
solves (
1.1
), then

\displaystyle H(t)=\iint(w_{1})^{4}{\rm d}t{\rm d}t=\iint(z_{1})^{-2}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s\,z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s

solves the
6^{\text{th}}
order ODE (
4.2
).
For the converse, the Legendre transform is given by

\displaystyle z_{1}(s)=w_{1}^{-\frac{4}{3}},\qquad z_{2}(s)=(w_{1})^{-\frac{4}{3}}\int(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s.

This sends

\displaystyle t=\frac{w_{2}(s)}{w_{1}(s)}\mapsto q=\frac{z_{2}(s)}{z_{1}(s)}=\int(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s.

solves the dual ODE (
4.5
), then

solves (
1.2
).
Hence, if

solves the

order ODE (

), then

\displaystyle F(q)=\iint(z_{1})^{3}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q=\iint(w_{1})^{-2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s\,(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s

solves (
1.1
).

Proof.

We observe that as a consequence of Chazy’s solutions, the Legendre transform in Proposition
4.1
gives

\displaystyle\frac{w_{2}}{w_{1}}=t=F^{\prime}=\int(z_{1})^{3}{\rm d}q=\int z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s,

\displaystyle\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}=q=H^{\prime}=\int(w_{1})^{4}{\rm d}t=\int(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}}{(w_{1})^{2}}=z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}}{(z_{1})^{2}}=(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}).

Together this yields
(z_{1})^{3}=(w_{1})^{-4}
,
from which we deduce

\displaystyle w_{1}=z_{1}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad w_{2}=w_{1}\int z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s=z_{1}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\int z_{1}(\dot{z}_{2}z_{1}-\dot{z}_{1}z_{2}){\rm d}s.

For the converse, we find

\displaystyle z_{1}=w_{1}^{-\frac{4}{3}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad z_{2}=z_{1}\int(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s=w_{1}^{-\frac{4}{3}}\int(w_{1})^{2}(\dot{w}_{2}w_{1}-\dot{w}_{1}w_{2}){\rm d}s.

The rest follows from a routine computation.
∎

It is well-known that solutions to the generalised Chazy equation (

Let
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}

Let dot denote differentiation with respect to

Then consider

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{1}=\Omega_{2}\Omega_{3}-\Omega_{1}(\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{3})+\tau^{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{2}=\Omega_{3}\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2}(\Omega_{3}+\Omega_{1})+\tau^{2},

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{3}=\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{3}(\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2})+\tau^{2},

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle\tau^{2}=\alpha^{2}(\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2})(\Omega_{3}-\Omega_{1})+\beta^{2}(\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{3})(\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2})+\gamma^{2}(\Omega_{3}-\Omega_{1})(\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{3})

and
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma

\displaystyle s(q)=\frac{\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{3}}{\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{3}},

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log\frac{\dot{s}}{s(s-1)},\qquad\Omega_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log\frac{\dot{s}}{s-1},\qquad\Omega_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}q}\log\frac{\dot{s}}{s}.

\displaystyle\{s,q\}+\frac{\dot{s}^{2}}{2}V(s)=0,

\displaystyle V(s)=\frac{1-\beta^{2}}{s^{2}}+\frac{1-\gamma^{2}}{(s-1)^{2}}+\frac{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-\alpha^{2}-1}{s(s-1)}.
