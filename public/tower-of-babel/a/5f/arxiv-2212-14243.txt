On an analytical method for the satellite problem revisited
Niccolò Pilloni, Claudio Saccon

Abstract




1
Introduction

In this note we discuss an
analitical
method, which goes back to Dirk Brouwer (1959 see
[
1
]
), to compute the trajectories of an artificial satellite rotating around
the Earth when
the oblate shape is taken into account. Despite being somewhat old
these kind of techniques are still used
nowadays in software packages like SGP4 (see
[
5
]
) used for the tracking of space debris. Actually the models on which these softwares are based
are the result of later generalizations
(see
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
) which include
the effect of the atmosferic drag. We don’t cover the general theory
here – we just remark that the problem with drag is treated as a pertubation of the dragless case.

As well known the Hamiltonian associated with the “non spherical Earth” can
be written as a series of “harmonics” of three types:
zonal (only depending on the latitude), sectorial (only depending on the longitude), and tesseral (depending on both) each
having a suitable coefficient. The most important term (apart
from the zero-th one which corresponds
to the perfectly spherical case)
is the coefficient
J_{2}
of the first zonal harmonic.
The main idea in
[
1
]
is looking for a canonical tranformation to a new set of variables such that the new Hamiltonian only depends on the momenta. If this is possible
then the new Hamilton equations become trivial: the momenta are constant while the coordinates are linear (this goes with a price:
the transformation is usually complicated and made up of a very long list of terms).
To accomplish this a Brouwer looks for a generating function of the
form of a power series in the coefficient
J_{2}
.
Using some calculus involving composition of power series he
derives some conditions on the coefficients of such a series so that the associated canonical tranformation has the
desired property. In the second part of
[
1
]
also
takes into account the coefficient
J_{4}
of the second zonal
harmonic and in the last part some sectorial harmonic are considered.
It has to be said that Brouwer credits
Hugo von Zeipel in
[
6
]
for
the above mentioned idea of the power series expansion. Unfortunately we were not able to find the original article so our main source is
[
1
]
: nevetheless we use the term “von Zeipel method” to indicate the technique we want to discuss.

The porpose of the present work is to give a general and coincise
presentation of the von Zeipel method
by using a more up-to-date vectorial notation. We use a multi dimensional power series expansion, corresponding to a generic set of parameters
(not just
J_{2}
). In this way we can derive a more transparent general formula, which allows to replicate
the computations of
[
1
]
“all at once”. Since such computation involve a lot of
derivatives, we have used Wolfram Mathematica to
perform the actual work. In this way we could also compute a second order term for the generating function that
[
1
]
omits. The
corresponding Mathematica notebook that we wrote for this is publicly avaliable at the link:
https://www.wolframcloud.com/obj/claudiosaccon/VonZeipel2.nb

In order to make this note more self–contained, we added an appendix where we recall the definitions and the main properties of canonical transformations and generating functions.

We remind that, if
f:\real^{N}\to\real^{M}
, the
Jacobian matrix
of
f
is defined by:

J_{f}(x)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x):=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{N}}(x)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\dfrac{\partial f_{M}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_{M}}{\partial x_{N}}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

If
M=1
, i.e. if
f
is a scalar function, we sometimes consider the
gradient
of
f
:

\nabla f(x):=J_{f}(x)^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x)\\
\vdots\\
\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{N}}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

When more than two indices are needed (for instance when
we have to deal with second order derivatives of vectors or matrices) we use
Einstein’s convention of repeated indices.
In this note elements of
N
are regarded as
“vectors”, i.e. as

N
by
1
(column) matrices.

2
The satellite problem

The satellite problem can be written as:

\ddot{\mathbf{r}}=-\frac{\mu\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^{3}}-\nabla U(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})

(1)

The term
\mu
stands for
GM
where
M
is the
mass of the Earth and
G
is the Gravitational Constant.
In (
1
)
\nabla U
is an additional conservative term taking into
account the non spherical shape of the Earth. In the case of the “oblated Earth”

U(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu}{|\mathbf{r}|}J_{n}\left(\frac{R}{|\mathbf{r}|}\right)^{n}P_{n}\left(\sin(\beta)\right)

In this formula
\beta
denotes the latitude,
R
is the radius oth the Earth, and
P_{n}
are the Legrendre polinomials. In this
description the potential is still rotationally symmetric
(only “zonal harmonics are considered). The even terms are also symmetric with respect to the equator, while
the odd ones introduce an asymmetry between the north and the
south emisphere. In
[
1
]
terms up to
J_{5}
are
considered (with different names). The methods presented in this note rely on the fact that the coefficient
J_{n}
and are small.

The term
\mathbf{F}
allows to take into account external, nonconservative forces acting on the satellite. The most important
effect of this type is the resistance of the athmosphere, usually
called
drag
.

In this note we drop the term
\mathbf{F}
. In

[
2
,
3
,
4
]

the problem with drag is considered (as a perturbation of the case without drag), but the
technique we are going to present here
only works in the case
\mathbf{F}=0
. In the Appendix,
however, we show how the full problem gets
transformed under a canonical tranformation, so
the reader can understand how this technique can be relevant in the problem with drag.

3
The Von Zeipel method

Now we take a more general point of view.
Let
\mathcal{H}:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\times\real^{M}\to\real
,

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(p,q,\mathcal{J})=\mathcal{H}(v,\mathcal{J})
be a Hamiltonian depending on M parameters
J_{1},\dots,J_{M}
which form the vector
\mathcal{J}

(
J_{i}
will be supposed to be small). We remind that for
an
M\times M
matrix the expression
A\mathcal{J}^{2}
denotes

\mathcal{J}^{t}A\mathcal{J}
.
We suppose that

\mathcal{H}(v,\mathcal{J})=\mathcal{H}_{0}(v)+\mathcal{H}_{1}(v)\cdot\mathcal{J}+\mathcal{H}_{2}(v)\mathcal{J}^{2}+0(|k^{2}|).

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\real^{2N}\to\real
(scalar),

\mathcal{H}_{1}:\real^{2N}\to\real^{M}
(vector valued), and

\mathcal{H}_{2}:\real^{2N}\to\real^{M^{2}}
(matrix valued).
Of course (by Taylor’s expansion:

\mathcal{H}_{0}(v)=\mathcal{H}(v,0),\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}(v)=\nabla_{k}\mathcal{H}(v,0),\quad\mathcal{H}_{2}(v)=2\nabla_{k}^{2}\mathcal{H}(v,0)

(
\nabla_{k}
and
\nabla^{2}_{k}
denote the gradient and the Hessian
matrix with respect to the
k
variable).

We assume that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}\mbox{ does not depend on $q$};

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\mbox{ and }\mathcal{H}_{2}\mbox{ are $2\pi$ periodic in each }q_{i}\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(3)

The periodicity assumption means that the variables

q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
are
angles
. Given any function

f=f(p,q)
we can define
the
secular part
of
f
as:

f_{sec}(p):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(p,q)\,dq_{1}\dots dq_{N}

(i.e the average in
q
over the cube

[0,2\pi]\times\cdots\times[0,2\pi]
) and the
periodic part
of
f
:

f_{per}(p,q):=f(p,q)-f_{sec}(p).

It is clear that
f\mapsto f_{sec}
and
f\mapsto f_{per}

are linear, and that
\left[f_{sec}\right]_{sec}=f_{sec}
,

\left[f_{per}\right]_{per}=f_{per}
,
\left[f_{sec}\right]_{per}=0=\left[f_{per}\right]_{sec}
.
Moreover
f=f_{sec}
if and only if
f
does not depend
on
q
.

The purpose of this section is finding a canonical transformation
such that the new Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}
only depends on the (new)
momenta
P
. To this aim
we look for a generating function of the form:

S(P,q,\mathcal{J})=S_{0}(P,q)+S_{1}(P,q)\cdot\mathcal{J}+S_{2}(P,q)\mathcal{J}^{2}+o(|\mathcal{J}|^{2})

(again with
S_{0}
is scalar,
S_{1}
an
M
vector, and
S_{2}

an
M\times M
matrix. For the moment
we assume that such an
S
is given and derive some
relationships between the old Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and the new one

\mathcal{K}
.
We then use such relationships to choose
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
, and
S_{2}

in such a way that
\mathcal{K}
exhibis the desired property.

If
V(v)=\begin{pmatrix}P(p,q)\\
Q(p,q)\end{pmatrix}
be the new variables induced by
S
, and
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(V)=\mathcal{K}(P,Q)
be the new
Hamiltonian. We can write

\mathcal{K}(V,k)=\mathcal{K}_{0}(V)+\mathcal{K}_{1}(V)\cdot\mathcal{J}+\mathcal{K}_{2}(V)\mathcal{J}^{2}+0(|\mathcal{J}^{2}|).

where
\mathcal{K}_{0}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
have the same properties of

\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
We know that:

\mathcal{K}\left(P(p,q,\mathcal{J}),\frac{\partial S(P(p,q),q,\mathcal{J})}{\partial P},k\right)=\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\partial S(P(p,q),q,\mathcal{J})}{\partial q},q,\mathcal{J}\right)\quad\forall p,q,\mathcal{J}.

In the following we are going to keep things coincise by skipping
some of the “inner” variables.
By Taylor’s expansion:

\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},q,\mathcal{J}\right)=\mathcal{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},q\right)+\mathcal{H}_{1}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},q\right)^{t}\mathcal{J}+\mathcal{H}_{2}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},q\right)\mathcal{J}^{2}+0(|\mathcal{J}^{2}|)=\\
\mathcal{H}_{0}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}J_{k}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial q_{i}}J_{k}J_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}J_{k}J_{h}+\\
\mathcal{H}_{1,k}J_{k}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,h}}{\partial p_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial S_{0}}{\partial q},q\right)\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}J_{k}J_{h}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}J_{k}J_{h}+o(|\mathcal{J}|^{2}).

(
\mathcal{H}_{i}
are computed in

\left(\frac{\partial S_{0}}{\partial q},q\right)
).
In the same way:

\mathcal{K}\left(P,\frac{\partial S}{\partial P},k\right)=\mathcal{K}_{0}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}J_{k}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial P_{i}}J_{k}J_{h}+\\
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}\partial Q_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial P_{j}}J_{k}J_{h}+\mathcal{K}_{1,k}J_{k}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{1}}{\partial Q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}J_{k}J_{h}+\mathcal{K}_{2,k,h}J_{k}J_{h}+o(|\mathcal{J}|^{2})

(
\mathcal{K}_{i}
are computed in
\left(P,\frac{\partial S_{0}}{\partial P}\right)
).
We take:

S_{0}(P,q)=P\cdot q

(if no other terms were present, then the associated canonical
transformation would be the identity) and we try to find

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
\mathcal{K}(V,k)=\mathcal{K}(P,k)
, that is
\mathcal{K}

does not depend on
Q
. By equating the terms having
corresponding powers of
\mathcal{J}
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{0}(P,Q)=\mathcal{K}_{0}(P)=\mathcal{H}_{0}(P)

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}+\mathcal{K}_{1,k}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{1,k}

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial P_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{K}_{0}}{\partial Q_{i}\partial Q_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial P_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial P_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{1,h}}{\partial Q_{i}}+\mathcal{K}_{2,k,h}

\displaystyle=

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial q_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,k}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}

(with
k
and
h
ranging between
1
and
M
).
Using
(
4
) we have found
\mathcal{K}_{0}
(which only depends
on
P
).
Then all derivatives of
\mathcal{K}_{0}
in (
5
) and (
6
) are zero.
If
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
are to be undependent of
Q
, then both (
5
) and
(
6
)
split into a pair of conditions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1,k}(P)=\left[\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{1,k}\right]_{sec}

(7)

\displaystyle 0=\left[\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{1,k}\right]_{per}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2,k,h}(P)=\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial q_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,h}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}\right]_{sec}

(9)

\displaystyle 0=\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial q_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,h}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}\right]_{per}

(10)

A possible way to verify (
7
)
and (
8
) is by imposing:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1,k}(P):=\left[\mathcal{H}_{1,k}(P)\right]_{sec}

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}(P)\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}(P,q)=-\left[\mathcal{H}_{1,k}(P,q)\right]_{per}

(12)

In order to solve equation (
12
):
let:

\displaystyle w(P):=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p}(P),\quad,\quad\hat{w}(P):=\left\lVert\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p}(P)\right\rVert^{-1}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p}(P)=\frac{w(P)}{\|w(P)\|}

(notice that the “coefficient”
w
neither depends on
q
nor on
k
) and define
S_{1}
by:

S_{1,k}(P,q):=-\frac{1}{\|w(P)\|}\int_{0}^{q\cdot\hat{w}}\left[\mathcal{H}_{1,k}\left(P,q-(1-t)(\hat{w}(P)\cdot q)\hat{w}(P)\right)\right]_{per}\,dt=\\
\frac{\left[\mathcal{H}_{1,k}\right]_{sec}(P)}{\left\lVert w(P)\right\rVert}\hat{w}\cdot q-\frac{1}{\left\lVert w(P)\right\rVert}\int_{0}^{q\cdot\hat{w}}\mathcal{H}_{1,k}\left(P,q-(1-t)(\hat{w}(P)\cdot q)\hat{w}(P)\right)\,dt.

It is then simple to check that
S_{1}

solves Equation (
12
).
So we can find
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
S_{1}
.
We can use the same idea to find
\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
S_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2,k,h}(P)=\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,h}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}\right]_{sec}

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{2,k,h}}{\partial q_{i}}=-\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial S_{1,h}}{\partial q_{j}}+\frac{\partial S_{1,k}}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{1,h}}{\partial p_{i}}+\mathcal{H}_{2,k,h}\right]_{per}

(14)

Again (
13
) provides the expression of
\mathcal{K}_{2}

(undependent of
Q
), while (
14
)
can be used to find
S_{2,k,h}
(although in
[
1
]

S_{2}

is neglected ????).

4
The solution of the satellite problem without drag

Let
r:=|\mathbf{r}|
,
a
denote the osculating semi-major axis,

e
denote the eccentricity, and
\nu
denote the true
anomaly.

We consider the
Delaunay variables
:

\displaystyle(p_{1}=)\quad L

\displaystyle:=(\mu a)^{1/2},

\displaystyle(q_{1}=)\quad l

\displaystyle:=\mbox{ mean anomaly},

\displaystyle(p_{2}=)\quad G

\displaystyle:=L(1-e^{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle(q_{2}=)\quad g

\displaystyle:=\mbox{ argument of the pericenter},

\displaystyle(p_{3}=)\quad H

\displaystyle:=G\cos(I),

\displaystyle(q_{3}=)\quad h

\displaystyle:=\mbox{ longitude of ascending node}.

We first consider the problem with
J_{n}=0
if
n\neq 2
(only
J_{2}
is present).
In terms of
(L,G,H,l,g,h)
the Hamiltonian takes the form:

\mathcal{H}(L,G,H,l,g,h):=\mathcal{H}_{0}(L)+\mathcal{H}_{1}(L,G,H,l,g)J_{2}

where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}(L)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}}{2L^{2}}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}(L,G,H,l,g)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{4}R^{2}}{4L^{6}G^{2}}\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}\left[\left(-G^{2}+3H^{2}\right)+\right.

(16)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left.3\left(G^{2}-H^{2}\right)\cos(2g+2\nu)\right]

Notice that (
2
) and (
3
) hold. To maintain the
notations of
[
1
]
we use doubly
primes to denote the new variables:

P_{1}=L^{\prime\prime}
,
P_{2}=G^{\prime\prime}
,
P_{2}=H^{\prime\prime}
,
Q_{1}=l^{\prime\prime}
,
Q_{2}=g^{\prime\prime}
,
Q_{3}=h^{\prime\prime}
(notice that we are skipping the single prime
variables since we are doing the two steps at once). So
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime})
and

S=S(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime},l,g,h)
.

Of course the term
\dfrac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}
and the
variable
\nu
need to be expressed in terms of the
Delaunay variables. For this we can use the Fourier series expansions:

\displaystyle\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{3}}{G^{3}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2P_{n}(e)\cos(nl)

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}\cos(2g+2\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}Q_{n}(e)\cos(2g+nl)

(18)

where
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
are suitable
Hansen coefficients

Notice that
e=\left(1-\dfrac{G^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

so
P_{n}=P_{n}(L,G)
,
Q_{n}=Q_{n}(L,G)
.

From (
17
) and (
18
) we infer that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,sec}(L,G,H)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{4}R^{2}}{4L^{3}G^{5}}\left(-G^{2}+3H^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1,per}(L,G,H)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{4}R^{2}}{4L^{6}G^{2}}\left[\left(-G^{2}+3H^{2}\right)\left(\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}-\frac{L^{3}}{G^{3}}\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad+\left.\left(3G^{2}-3H^{2}\right)\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}\cos(2g+2\nu)\right]

Using the above formula we get from conditions (
11
)

\mathcal{K}_{1}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime})=\frac{\mu^{4}R^{2}}{4{L^{\prime\prime}}^{3}{G^{\prime\prime}}^{5}}\left(-{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+3{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)

(19)

while
(
12
) turns into:

\frac{\partial S_{1}}{\partial l}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime},l,g)=\frac{\mu^{2}R^{2}}{4{L^{\prime\prime}}^{3}{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}\times\\
\times\left[\left(-{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+3{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}-\frac{{L^{\prime\prime}}^{3}}{{G^{\prime\prime}}^{3}}\right)+3\left({G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}-{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\frac{a^{3}}{\mathbf{r}^{3}}\cos(2g+2\nu)\right].

(20)

If we use the Fuorier expansions mentioned above, we get:

S_{1}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime},l,g)=\frac{\mu^{2}R^{2}}{4{L^{\prime\prime}}^{3}{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}\left[\left(3{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}-{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2P_{n}}{n}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime})\sin(nl)\right.+\\
\left.3\left({G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}-{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{n}}{n}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime})\sin(2g+nl)\right]

(notice that
S_{1}
does not depend on
h
).
However, using the formulas:

dl=\frac{L}{G}\frac{r^{2}}{a^{2}}d\nu\quad,\quad\frac{a}{r}=\frac{1+e\cos(\nu)}{1-e^{2}}=\frac{L^{2}}{G^{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{L^{2}}}\cos(\nu)\right)

(21)

we can also find a closed formula for
S_{1}
. From (
20
) we get:

S_{1}(L^{\prime\prime},G^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime},l,g)=\frac{\mu^{2}R^{2}}{4{G^{\prime\prime}}^{5}}({G^{\prime\prime}}-3{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2})l+\\
\frac{\mu^{2}R^{2}}{4{L^{\prime\prime}}^{3}{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}\int\left[\left(-{G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}+3{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{L}{G}\right)\frac{1+e\cos(\nu)}{1-e^{2}}+\right.\\
\left.3\left({G^{\prime\prime}}^{2}-{H^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{L}{G}\right)\frac{1+e\cos(\nu)}{1-e^{2}}\cos(2g+2\nu)\right]d\nu

(22)

Appendix A
The Hamiltonian approach. Canonical transformations and generating functions

Let

\mathcal{H}:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\to\real
be a smooth function (the
Hamiltonian
), we
we say that
p,q:\real\to\real^{N}
are solutions to the Hamiltonian equations, if:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{p}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathcal{H}(p,q)^{t},\\
&\dot{q}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\mathcal{H}(p,q)^{t}\end{aligned}\right.

(26)

As well known (
26
) describe the dynamics of a system only
effected by conservative forces,
according the laws of Classical Mechanics.
The variables
q
are called “coordinates” while
p
are the “momenta”.
of the system.

In case we need to consider the presence of dissipative forces we
add two two terms to (
26
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{p}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathcal{H}(p,q)^{t}+f_{1}(p,q),\\
&\dot{q}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\mathcal{H}(p,q)^{t}+f_{2}(p,q).\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

where
f_{1},f_{2}:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\to\real^{N}
. It will be sometimes convenient to indicate

v=\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}

and rewrite
(
27
) as:

\dot{v}=J\nabla\mathcal{H}(v)+f,

(28)

where:

\nabla\mathcal{H}(p,q)=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\mathcal{H}(p,q)\\
\ \\
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q}\mathcal{H}(p,q)\end{pmatrix}\ ,\ J:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{N}\\
\\
I_{N}&0\end{pmatrix}\ ,\ f(v)=f(p,q):=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(p,q)\\
\ \\
f_{2}(p,q)\end{pmatrix}

(
I_{N}
denotes the
N
-dimensional identity matrix).
Notice that
J^{2}=-I_{N}
.

A.1 Definition
.

Let
\Phi:\real^{2N}\to\real^{2N}
be a diffeomorphism i.e. a bijection such that
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
are smooth. We say that
\Phi
is a
canonical transformation
, if for any solution
v
of

\dot{v}=J\nabla\mathcal{H}(v)
,
the transformed function
V:=\Phi(v)
is a solution of

\dot{V}=J\nabla\mathcal{K}(V),

where
\mathcal{K}(V):=\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{-1}(V))
. This is usually expressed by saying that “
\Phi
preserves the form of Hamilton’s equations”.

A.2 Theorem
.

Let
\Phi
be a diffeomorphism.
If
\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}
is symplectic, that is if:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}J\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}^{t}=J

(29)

holds,
then
\Phi
is a canonical transformation.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathcal{K}
we have:
\mathcal{K}(\Phi(v))=\mathcal{H}(v)
.
Then:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}(v)}{\partial v}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}(V)}{\partial V}\frac{\partial\Phi(v)}{\partial v}.

Now, if

\dot{v}=J\nabla\mathcal{H}(v)

and

V=\Phi(v)
, we have:

\dot{V}=\frac{\partial\Phi(v)}{\partial v}\dot{v}=\frac{\partial\Phi(v)}{\partial v}J\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}(v)}{\partial v}^{t}=\frac{\partial\Phi(v)}{\partial v}J\frac{\partial\Phi(v)}{\partial v}^{t}\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}(V)}{\partial V}^{t}.

(30)

We can conclude that, if (
29
) holds,
then

\dot{V}=J\nabla\mathcal{K}(V).

∎

A.3 Remark
.

As shown in 1 of (
B.2
) the
equality
(
29
) is equivalent to:

\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}^{t}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}^{t}\qquad\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}^{t}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}^{t}\qquad\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}^{t}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}^{t}=I_{N}

(31)

or equivalently to:

\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}^{t}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}^{t}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}\qquad\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}^{t}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}^{t}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}\qquad\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}^{t}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}-\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}^{t}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}=I_{N}

(32)

A.4 Remark
.

If
\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}
is symplectic, then the
change of variables
V=\Phi(v)
transforms the
nonconservative equation (
27
)
into:

\dot{V}=J\nabla\mathcal{K}(V)+F(V)

where
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}\circ\Phi^{-1}
and:

F(V)=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}(\Phi^{-1}(V))f(\Phi^{-1}(V))=\left(\frac{\partial\Phi^{-1}}{\partial V}(V)\right)^{-1}f(\Phi^{-1}(V)).

But since
\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}
is symplectic, so is

\dfrac{\partial\Phi^{-1}}{\partial V}
(see section

and we have:

\frac{\partial\Phi^{-1}(V)}{\partial V}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial P}&\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial Q}\\
\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial P}&\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial Q}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial Q}^{t}&-\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial Q}^{t}\\
-\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial P}^{t}&\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial P}^{t}.\end{pmatrix}

If
\Phi^{-1}(P,Q)=\begin{pmatrix}p(P,Q)\\

\displaystyle F_{1}(P,Q)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial Q}^{t}f_{1}(\Phi^{-1}(P,Q))-\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial Q}^{t}f_{2}(\Phi^{-1}(P,Q))

(33)

\displaystyle F_{2}(P,Q)=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\partial q(P,Q)}{\partial P}^{t}f_{1}(\Phi^{-1}(P,Q))+\dfrac{\partial p(P,Q)}{\partial P}^{t}f_{2}(\Phi^{-1}(P,Q))

(34)

A possible way of obtaining a canonical transformation is
via a generating function.

A.5 Definition

(generating functions)
.

Let
S:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\to\real
be a smooth function.
We see
S
as
S(P,q)
(a combination of “new variables”

P
and “old ones”
q
).
It is simple to check that the equations:

p=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}(P,q)\quad,\quad Q=\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}(P,q)

(35)

implicitely define a canonical transformation

(P,Q)=\Phi(p,q)
, in the sense that

\Phi:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\to\real^{N}\times\real^{N}
is canonical and:

(P,Q)=\Phi(p,q)\Leftrightarrow\mbox{ \eqref{eqn:definition-canonical} holds }\qquad\qquad\forall\ p,q,P,Q.

Such an
S
will be called a

generating function

for the canonical transformation
\Phi
.

A.6 Theorem
.

Let

\Phi(p,q)=\begin{pmatrix}P(p,q)\\
Q(p,q)\end{pmatrix}

be a diffeomorphism and

\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial v}

be symplectic.

Let
(p_{0},q_{0})
be a point such that

\det\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}(p_{0},q_{0})\right)\neq 0

and let
(P_{0},Q_{0})=\Phi(p_{0},q_{0})
.
Then there exist a neighboorhood
W
of
(P_{0},q_{0})
,
a neighboorhood
W_{1}
of
(p_{0},Q_{0})
, and a
smooth function
S:W\to\real
such that

S
is a generating function for
\Phi
in
W
.

Proof.

Let
G:\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\times\real^{N}\times\real^{N}
be

G(P,Q,p,q):=\begin{pmatrix}P\\
Q\end{pmatrix}-\Phi(p,q)=\begin{pmatrix}P\\
Q\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{1}(p,q)\\
\Phi_{2}(p,q)\end{pmatrix}.

We have:

\frac{\partial G}{\partial(P,Q,p,q)}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{N}&0&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\\
0&I_{N}\ &-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}.

From the assumption we derive that:

\det\left(\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial(Q,p)}\right)=\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\\
I_{N}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\det\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)\neq 0

By the implicit function Theorem there exist
a neighboorhood
W
of
(P_{0},q_{0})
, a neighboorhood
W_{1}

of
(Q_{0},p_{0}
and a map

\Psi:W\to\real^{N}\times\real^{N}
such that:

(P,Q)=\Phi(p,q)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(Q,p)=\Psi(P,q).

Moreover:

\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial(P,q)}=-\begin{pmatrix}0&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\\
I_{N}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}I_{N}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\\
0&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}&-I_{N}\\
\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{N}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\\
0&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}&-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}+\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\\
\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}&\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}.

We claim that the above matrix is symmetric. The upper left
block is symmetric since:

\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}=\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-t}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}\Leftrightarrow\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}=\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}

and the latter is exactly the first equality in (
32
).
In the same way the lower right block is symmetric:

\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}=\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\right)^{t}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-t}\Leftrightarrow\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}=\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\right)^{t}

which is the first equality in (
31

\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-t}=-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}+\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\Leftrightarrow\\
I_{N}=-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{-1}\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}}_{=\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\right)^{t}}+\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}\Leftrightarrow\\
I_{N}=-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\right)^{t}+\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}\Leftrightarrow\\
I_{N}=(I_{N}^{t}=)-\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial q}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial p}\right)^{t}+\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{1}}{\partial p}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Phi_{2}}{\partial q}\right)^{t}

and the last equality coincides with the third one in
(
31

Since
\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial(P,q)}
is symmetric, there

for all

in
W
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Simplectic matrices

B.1 Definition
.

A
2N\times 2N
matrix
M
is said to be
symplectic

if
MJM^{t}=M
.

B.2 Proposition

(Properties of symplectic matrices)
.

Let
M,M_{1}
be
2N\times 2N
matrices and suppose that

\displaystyle{M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}}
, where
A,B,C,D
are four
N\times N
matrices.

1.

M
is symplectic if and only if:

AB^{t}=BA^{t}\quad,\quad CD^{t}=DC^{t}\quad,\quad AD^{t}-BC^{t}=I_{N}

if and only if:

A^{t}C=C^{t}A\quad,\quad B^{t}D=D^{t}B\quad,\quad A^{t}D-C^{t}B=I_{N}

2.

If
M
and
M_{1}
are symplectic, then
MM_{1}
is symplectic.

3.

If
M
is symplectic, than
M
is invertible and

M^{-1}=-JM^{t}J=\begin{pmatrix}D^{t}&-B^{t}\\
-C^{t}&A^{t}\end{pmatrix}.

4.

If
M
is symplectic, than
M^{t}
is symplectic.

Proof.

1.

We have:

MJM^{t}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{N}\\
I_{N}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}&C^{t}\\
B^{t}&D^{t}\end{pmatrix}=\\
\begin{pmatrix}B&-A\\
D&-C\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{t}&C^{t}\\
B^{t}&D^{t}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}BA^{t}-AB^{t}&BC^{t}-AD^{t}\\
DA^{t}-CB^{t}-&DC^{t}-CD^{t}.\end{pmatrix}

So the condition
MJM^{t}=J
turns out to be equivalent to
the first set of equalities above (the possible fourth equality is easily derived from the third one, by taking the transpose).
The second set of equalities follows from the fact that
M^{t}

is symplectic, as shown in 4.

2.

We have:

MM_{1}J(MM_{1})^{t}=M\underbrace{M_{1}JM_{1}^{t}}_{=J}M^{t}=MJM^{t}=J.

3.

We have:

M(-JM^{t}J)=-MJM^{t}J=-JJ=I_{N}.

This implies that
-JM^{t}J
is a left inverse. A standard argument
shows that
M
is invertible and
M^{-1}=-JM^{t}J
.

4.

From the previous statement we have
-JM^{t}JM=I_{N}
. By multiplying
by
J
, we get that
M^{t}
is symplectic.

∎
