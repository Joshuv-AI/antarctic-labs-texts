Heisenberg Limit beyond Quantum Fisher Information
Wojciech Górecki

Abstract
The Heisenberg limit provides a fundamental bound on the achievable estimation precision with a limited number of N N resources used (e.g., atoms, photons, etc.). Using entangled quantum states makes it possible to scale the precision with N N better than when resources would be used independently. Consequently, the optimal use of all resources involves accumulating them in a single execution of the experiment. Unfortunately, that implies that the most common theoretical tool used to analyze metrological protocols - quantum Fisher information (QFI) – does not allow for a reliable description of this problem, as it becomes operationally meaningful only with multiple repetitions of the experiment. In this thesis, using the formalism of Bayesian estimation and the minimax estimator, I derive asymptotically saturable bounds on the precision of the estimation for the case of noiseless unitary evolution. For the case where the number of resources N N is strictly constrained, I show that the final measurement uncertainty is π \pi times larger than would be implied by a naive use of QFI. I also analyze the case where a constraint is imposed only on the average amount of resources, the exact value of which may fluctuate (in which case QFI does not provide any universal bound for precision). In both cases, I study the asymptotic saturability and the rate of convergence of these bounds. In the following part, I analyze the problem of the Heisenberg limit when multiple parameters are measured simultaneously on the same physical system. In particular, I investigate the existence of a gain from measuring all parameters simultaneously compared to distributing the same amount of resources to measure them independently. Using two examples – the measurement of multiple phase shifts in a multi-arm interferometer and the measurement of three magnetic field components – I show the existence of qualitative differences between the results obtained using Bayesian estimation/minimax estimator and those resulting from the use of QFI. I also derive a lower bound on the achievable precision of the measurement in the general case (not always saturable).

CHAPTER 1Introduction

1.1. Heisenberg limit in quantum metrology

In classical mechanics, all parameters characterizing a physical system can, in principle, be measured to any accuracy, but, in practice, the imperfections of the measurement apparatus introduce certain fluctuations in the results. Repeated measurement reduces the uncertainty of the estimation of a parameter, such that the mean square error of the estimator (MSE) scales inversely to the number of these repetitions (the so-called shot noise limit).

In contrast to the above, the formalism of quantum mechanics rigorously defines achievable precision, as the act of measurement itself is not deterministic but probabilistic. As a result, the question of the fundamental limit on the achievable precision of the measurement of a given parameter becomes a well-posed problem without the need to specify a particular measurement procedure. Moreover, quantum mechanics opens up the prospect of achieving a better scaling of measurement accuracy with the number of resources used in the experiment (which can be understood as the number of atoms, number of photons, total time, total energy, etc.). Using appropriate entangled quantum states
Mitchell et al. 2004
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
(or sequential multiple passes
Higgins et al. 2007
) makes it possible to decrease MSE quadratically with the number of resources. Such scaling has been named after Werner Heisenberg by Holland and Burnett
Holland & Burnett 1993
, where they referred to number-phase
uncertainty relation in
Heitler 1954
.

The issue of the feasibility of reaching the Heisenberg limit is intensively discussed in the current literature
Giovannetti et al. 2006
;
Paris 2009
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Toth & Apellaniz 2014
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
;
Schnabel 2017
;
Degen et al. 2017
;
Pezzè et al. 2018
;
Pirandola et al. 2018
;
Niezgoda et al. 2019
. At the same time, in some cases, there are ambiguities related to the interpretation of results obtained by different methods.

1.2. Major problems discussed in the thesis

The existence of Heisenberg scaling implies that when the only constraint is the total number of resources, the optimal strategy is to accumulate them all in a single execution of the experiment. However, the results obtained with most of the theoretical tools commonly used in quantum metrology, such as Fisher’s quantum information (QFI) and the related Cramér-Rao (CR) inequality, have an unambiguous interpretation only in the limit of many repetitions. As a result, the actual limit on achievable precision is not very well explored, and there are inconsistent (sometimes even contradictory) statements on this topic in the current literature.

The most widely discussed example in the quantum metrology literature is the issue of phase shift estimation in an interferometer. Back in the 1990s, it was noted that when the value of the shift is initially completely unknown (it can take any value in the interval
[0,2\pi[
), the minimum uncertainty, obtainable using
N
photons, is
\pi
times larger than would result from naive use of QFI
Luis & Perřina 1996
;
Buzžek et al. 1999
;
Berry & Wiseman 2000
. In 2011, an argument was put forward
Hayashi 2011
justifying that, in the limit of
N
going to infinity,
even if it is known from the beginning that the parameter belongs to a smaller interval, the rate remains equal to
\pi
, independently of the size of this interval. In this thesis, I generalize this observation to the case of any unitary evolution generator with a bounded spectrum. Furthermore, I show that the limit on precision is also valid for more advanced adaptive metrology protocols. Finally, I do not restrict myself to the limit of
N
going to infinity but also discuss the case of finite
N
. These results have already been published in
Górecki et al. 2020
.

A second major issue related to the misunderstandings arising from the misuse of quantum Fisher information in the context of the Heisenberg limit is the precision achievable when only the expectation value of the total energy is bounded. In such a situation, the QFI can be unconstrainedly large. Some authors have postulated the existence of protocols that allow for better scaling than Heisenberg scaling
Anisimov et al. 2010
;
Rivas & Luis 2012
; however, these works were criticized rather quickly
Pezzé 2013
. In 2012, it was shown for the general case that the MSE cannot decrease faster than quadratically with the mean energy
Giovannetti et al. 2012
, but the derived bound was not saturable.
In the thesis, I analyze this problem by establishing the saturable bound and investigating the rate of convergence. These results are based on the methodology and observations from my papers
Górecki et al. 2020
;
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022a
, but they were not published before in explicit form.

Finally, I examine the issue of the Heisenberg limit in the context of multi-parameter metrology. One of the aspects addressed is the comparison of the efficiency of the combined measurement of all parameters simultaneously with the situation when they are measured separately. The analysis of such an issue necessitates a well-defined procedure for resource allocation between the parameters, which has been treated radically differently in various publications
Humphreys et al. 2013
;
Yousefjani et al. 2017
leading to contradictory results regarding the existence of a gain from performing a joint measurement.
This thesis discusses the issue of resource allocation and makes a comprehensive comparison of the results obtained using the two paradigms (QFI and Bayesian estimation theory/minimax approach). The presented results has been published in
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022a
;
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
.

1.3. Outline of the thesis

In Chapter Two, I present, by way of example, the fundamental problems that arise when attempting to analyze the Heisenberg limit using concepts restricted to local estimation, such as QFI or the error propagation formula. I also discuss the state of the art of the precision bound for average energy constraint. In Chapter Three, I discuss the Fisher quantum information formalism, highlighting its strengths (such as the extensively developed theory concerning metrology in the presence of noise) while emphasizing its limitations in the context of Heisenberg limit analysis.

CHAPTER 2Quantum metrology and the Heisenberg limit

"Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler," Albert Einstein was once supposed to have said. Keeping in mind this rule, before introducing broader mathematical formalism, I would like to briefly explain the essence of the main problem I will discuss in this thesis by introducing a simple but expressive example.

2.1. Heisenberg limit by an example

Consider the problem of estimating the strength of a weak magnetic field oriented in vertical direction
z

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

U_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta J_{z}}
(where

, see

The simplest and most intuitive strategy is to orient the atom’s spin in direction

. As for small rotation the value of
\langle J_{y}\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2}\theta

\tilde{\theta}=2\langle J_{y}\rangle
. From the error propagation formula:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\frac{\Delta^{2}J_{y}}{\left|\frac{d\langle J_{y}\rangle}{d\theta}\right|^{2}}.

(2.1)

As for a single particle, angular momentum operator
J_{y}
is simply Pauli matrix multiplied by factor one-half
\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{y}

|+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle)

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=1
(in radians). If one repeats measurement on
N
independent atoms and then estimates the angle value from the averaged result, the variance of the measurement results will decrease by a factor
\frac{1}{N}
. At the same time, the denominator will remain unchanged, leading at the end to
\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\frac{1}{N}
. Such scaling with the amount of resources used is called standard scaling or shot noise limit.

The question is whether one may obtain better scaling by collectively using all
N
atoms. The first guess would be to coherently orient all atoms to the same direction

, see

Then even a small rotation by an angle

. As a result, these two effects will cancel each other, leading again to the shot noise limit, i.e., the same precision could be obtained by measuring each atom separately and averaging the result.

May this problem be overcome in quantum mechanics by choosing another state for sensing? The laws of quantum mechanics do not allow us to cancel the uncertainties completely.
The famous Heisenberg uncertainty principle, which, in its most canonical form, establishes the relationship between momentum and position variances
\Delta x\Delta p\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}

\Delta J_{y}\Delta J_{z}\geq\frac{1}{2}|\langle[J_{y},J_{z}]\rangle|,

(2.2)

which, in our case, is simply equal to the value of the spin component in
x

It simultaneously increases the uncertainty in

Is there any fundamental bound for the precision of estimating angle

\frac{d\langle J_{y}\rangle}{d\theta}=i\langle[J_{y},J_{z}]\rangle.

(2.3)

Substituting

The fringes are very narrow so that even when rotated by a small angle, the arrangement of the fringes changes significantly, making it possible to observe even a minimal change precisely. On the other hand, the cyclic nature of the distribution of the fringes means that when rotated through an angle
\theta=\frac{2\pi}{N}
(or multiple thereof), their distribution becomes identical again (which corresponds to the phase factor

This fact makes the single usage of

Another way to see how the

2.2. General formulation of the channel estimation problem

Given unitary quantum channel acting on the system, depending on the unknown parameter
\theta
:
\rho_{\theta}=U_{\theta}\rho U^{\dagger}_{\theta}
.
The aim is to get an information about the parameter
\theta
by preparing a proper input state
\rho
, acting the channel and performing a general measurement – positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\{M_{x}\}
(
\forall_{x}M_{x}\geq 0,\,\sum_{x}M_{x}=\openone
) on the output state
\rho_{\theta}
. The probability of obtaining the result
x
when the parameter value is equal
\theta
is given by the Born rule
p(x|\theta)=\textrm{Tr}(M_{x}\rho_{\theta})
. The measurement may be repeated many times, resulting in the sequence of outcomes
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
. At last, one needs to choose the estimator
\tilde{\theta}({\boldsymbol{x}})

assigning an estimated parameter value to a sequence of results. The measure of the efficiency of the estimator is the MSE:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}p({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta)(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2},

(2.6)

where
p({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p(x_{i}|\theta)
. In the case when the mean value of the estimator indicates the correct value of the parameter (the estimator is unbiased):

\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}p({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta)\tilde{\theta}(x)=\theta,

(2.7)

the MSE is equal to the variance of the estimator. In such cases, I will use these words interchangeably if it does not lead to ambiguity.

One should notice that for a given measurement and estimator, the value of MSE
Eq. (
2.6
)
depends on the exact value of
\theta
, so the problem of finding optimal metrology strategy is not well defined until we define a range of parameter value for which the estimator is to work correctly. In principle, we would like the estimator to return actual value for all possible
\theta
s. However, this condition in many situations may be too strict or even impossible to satisfy for a finite number of repetitions. Moreover, we often have some approximated knowledge about the parameter value, and we are interested in local estimation around a specific point
\theta\approx\theta_{0}
, so it is not needed for the estimator to work well very far from this value. Still, if one demands from estimator to work correctly only in single point
\theta_{0}
, then trivial constant estimator
\forall_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\theta}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\theta_{0}
would lead to zero cost, while it extracts no information from the measurement. To avoid such pathological constructions, heuristically, we would say we require that the estimator works correctly, at least in a certain immediate neighborhood of
\theta_{0}
. As further analysis will show, different approaches to the mathematical formalization of this condition will lead to different results.

One way is to impose the condition that estimator is
locally unbiased
around point
\theta_{0}
, i.e.,
Eq. (
2.7
)
, as well as the equation resulting from taking the derivative of both sides, are satisfied at point
\theta_{0}
:

\begin{split}&\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}p({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta_{0})\tilde{\theta}(x)=\theta_{0},\\
&\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{dp({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta)}{d\theta}\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\tilde{\theta}(x)=1.\end{split}

(2.8)

Note that the above condition allows for an exact rederivation of the error propagation formula
Eq. (
2.1
)
for the situation, where the parameter
\theta
is estimated with the use of some observable
A=\sum_{a}a|a\rangle\langle a|
. Indeed, for measurement
\{M_{a}\}=\{|a\rangle\langle a|\}
direct minimization of the estimator variance with locally unbiased condition results in
\tilde{\theta}(a)=\theta_{0}+a\left(\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{d\theta}|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)^{-1}
and
\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\Delta^{2}A/\left(\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{d\theta}|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)^{2}
.

As shown in the discussed example, the analysis performed only with local unbiasedness conditions may not be meaningful when the experiment is performed only once. However, the concept of local unbiasedness is a very efficient tool when the experiment is repeated many times. The relation between the minimal cost obtainable with local unbiasedness condition for single measurement realization vs. the cost with global unbiasedness condition in the limit of many repetitions will be discussed in
Ch.
3
. Still, as argued at the beginning, we are interested in the optimal usage of whole resources, which demands accumulating all of them in a single realization so we will need another theoretical tool.

The problem of full minimization of the MSE of the estimator, with restriction only on the total amount of resources
N
will be discussed in
Ch.
4
with the usage of two alternative approaches. In the Bayesian approach, one introduces a priori distribution of the value of parameter
p(\theta)
and as the figure of merit, consider the
Eq. (
2.6
)
averaged over this probability. Alternatively, in the minimax approach, the finite size set of possible values of parameter
\Theta
is under consideration, and the figure of merit is
Eq. (
2.6
)
maximized over
\theta\in\Theta
.

To make the results from
Ch.

2.3. The role of observable and optimality of projective measurements

The attentive reader may note that in
Sec.
2.1
I discussed the direct estimation of a parameter from the mean value of the observables, whereas in
Sec.
2.2
we consider independent optimization over both the measurement and the estimator itself. Therefore, one may ask whether the bound
Eq. (
2.5
)
is still valid with such an extension.

In the following, I will show that, in fact, for the case where the measurement is not repeated many times (which corresponds to optimal use of resources), optimization over the observable itself is equivalent to optimization over the general measurement and the estimator.

In the literature, this fact has been derived independently in different formalisms (see
Macieszczak et al. 2014
for the Bayesian approach). Below I want to show that for all introduced approaches (locally unbiased estimators, Bayesian or minimax approach), it may be seen as a direct consequence of a single matrix inequality.

For a given measurement
\{M_{x}\}
and estimator
\tilde{\theta}(x)
, the mean value of the estimator for any true value of the parameter
\theta
may be written as the expectation value of the observable
A
defined as follow:

\langle\tilde{\theta}\rangle=\sum_{x}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{x})\tilde{\theta}(x)=\textrm{Tr}\Big(\rho_{\theta}\underbrace{\sum_{x}M_{x}\tilde{\theta}(x)}_{A}\Big).

(2.9)

Now I will argue that such a procedure may only be improved if one measures the observable directly (i.e., performs the projection onto its eigenvectors
\{|a\rangle\langle a|\}
) and then estimates the value of
\theta
from the average value of
A
(formally: attributes to each of
|a\rangle\langle a|
the estimator
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}(a)
, such that
A=\sum_{a}\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}(a)|a\rangle\langle a|)
.

To show that, I construct the positive operator of the following form (its positivity comes from
\forall_{x}M_{x}\geq 0)
):

0\leq\sum_{x}\left((\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)\openone-(A-\openone\theta)\right)M_{x}\left((\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)\openone-(A-\openone\theta)\right)=\sum_{x}M_{x}(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2}-(A-\openone\theta)^{2}

(2.10)

(note that no assumptions about unbiasedness have been used here; the last equality comes directly
from
\sum_{x}M_{x}=\openone
and
A=\sum_{x}M_{x}\tilde{\theta}(x)
). From that, we have:

\forall_{\theta}\quad\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\sum_{x}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{x})(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2}\geq\sum_{x}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}(A-\openone\theta)^{2}),

(2.11)

where the last inequality is tight if all
M_{x}
are the projections onto eigenstates of
A
.

As
Eq. (
2.11
)
holds for any strategy
\{M_{x}\},\,\tilde{\theta}(x)
at any
\theta
, it remains valid if one restricts to locally unbiased estimators, as well as if one averages both sides with arbitrary
p(\theta)
or take the maximum over
\theta\in\Theta
.

Still, it should be stressed that above we have considered the single parameter estimation, with single measurement realization, where the figure of merit is MSE. In more general cases, separating estimator form measurement may still be useful and necessary, i.e., for the situations where:

•

one considers many repetitions of the experiment, so the measurement is local, but the estimator may depend on all measurement results jointly.

•

the cost function other than MSE is under consideration.

•

more than one parameter is to be estimated.

All these cases will also appear in further chapters.

2.4. Average energy case and the inability to overcome the Heisenberg scaling

Eq. (
2.5

establishes a fundamental lower bound (not necessarily saturable) for the estimator’s variance concerning the variance of the evolution generator for the sensing state. As shown in the discussed example, if the total number of involved resources is
N

, leading to quadratic improvement to the classical strategy. The natural question is, what happens if one imposes constraints only on the average number of resources, not the maximum one?

The problem was extensively discussed in 2010s in the context of optical interferometry. Consider two-arms interferometer, with unknown phaseshift

Then one may easily construct the state with arbitrary large

\frac{1}{N^{2}}

(see also
Sec.

The universal solution for such a class of problems has been proposed in

2.5. Entangled-parallel and sequential-adaptive metrological schemes

So far, we have discussed the problem of unitary evolution governed by generator
H
, which allows for formulating the bound in terms of its average value or its variance. Especially for the system with non-interactive particles or linear optics,
\langle H\rangle
typically scales linearly with a number of particles (or photons), leading to Heisenberg scaling of the precision of the form
\sim\frac{1}{N^{2}}
. Let us focus on such a case to introduce a notation for the rest of the thesis.

For the sake of simplicity and homogeneity of notation, let me now ignore the indistinguishability of particles (bosons or fermions) and treat them as distinguishable (the problem will be discussed in more detail in specific cases, if necessary).
It is obvious that for distinguishable particles, the estimation would be easier (because a larger states’ space would be available), so all the bounds derived for such a case are still valid for bosons and fermions (while saturation may depends on which case is considered).

Let me name the Hilbert space of the single particle in the discussed system by

(2.13)

In most of the analyses presented in this thesis, I will not significantly go beyond the problem of unitary evolution. However, to provide a more realistic context, at some point, I will also discuss the recent results about the noisy case (obtained typically within QFI formalism). To have a unified framework, therefore, I denote a single particle quantum gate as

, which for unitary evolution is given as
{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}(\rho)=e^{i\theta\Lambda}\rho e^{-i\theta\Lambda}

will be discussed later

In the simplest scenario, the experimentalist uses

\rho_{\theta}={\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}(\rho),\quad p(\{x_{1},...,x_{N}\}|\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\textrm{Tr}(M_{x_{i}}\rho_{\theta}).

(2.14)

CHAPTER 3Fisher information and Cramér-Rao bound

In this chapter, I introduce the formalism based on Fisher information and Cramér-Rao inequality, stating the fundamental bounds on estimation precision when the experiment is repeated many times. My emphasis is on pointing out how the results obtained by this method should be interpreted and what their operational significance is. At the end of the chapter, I also present current developments in analyzing the estimation problem in the presence of noise.

3.1. Classical Cramér-Rao bound

Given probability distribution
p(x|\theta)
of the measurement result
x
depended on the value of unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
. The aim is to estimate the value of the parameter from the measurement outcomes. Then for any estimator, locally unbiased at
\theta=\theta_{0}
, i.e., the one satisfying:

\langle\tilde{\theta}\rangle\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}=\sum_{x}p(x|\theta_{0})\tilde{\theta}(x)=\theta_{0}

(3.1)

\frac{d\langle\tilde{\theta}\rangle}{d\theta}\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}=\sum_{x}\frac{dp(x|\theta)}{d\theta}\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\tilde{\theta}(x)=1,

(3.2)

its variance is bounded from below by Cramér-Rao bound (CR):

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\geq\frac{1}{F(p)},\quad F(p)=\sum_{x}\frac{1}{p(x|\theta)}\left(\frac{dp(x|\theta)}{d\theta}\right)^{2},

(3.3)

where
F(p)
is classical Fisher information (FI) of the probability distribution
p(x|\theta)
.

CR bound is typically derived using Cauchy Schwarz inequality; it may also be obtained by direct minimization over estimators with locally unbiased constraint using the Lagrange multipliers method. In the latter method, one would obtain:

\tilde{\theta}^{\textrm{l.u.}}(x)=\frac{1}{F(p)}\frac{1}{p(x|\theta)}\frac{dp(x|\theta)}{d\theta}.

(3.4)

Note that while the above estimator is by construction always locally unbiased at point
\theta_{0}
, we have no knowledge about the size of the neighborhood around
\theta_{0}
, where it works well, which in general makes FI and CR bound not very useful for the case when the experiment is performed only once.

Directly from the definition, FI is convex, i.e., for any two probability distributions
p_{1}(x|\theta)
,
p_{2}(x|\theta)

F(c_{1}p_{1}+c_{2}p_{2})\leq c_{1}F(p_{1})+c_{2}F(p_{2}),

(3.5)

and additive, i.e., for the joint probability of two variables of the product form
p_{12}(x_{1},x_{2}|\theta)=p_{1}(x_{2}|\theta)p_{2}(x_{2}|\theta)
we have

F(p_{12})=F(p_{1})+F(p_{2}).

(3.6)

Especially for the
k
-length sequence of measurement results
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}
, wherein each repetition’s outcome is drawn independently, so
p({\boldsymbol{x}})=p(\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\})=p(x_{1})p(x_{2})...p(x_{k})
, the FI is simply
k
times bigger than FI for single realization, so:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\geq\frac{1}{k\cdot F(p)}.

(3.7)

It is consistent with a simple observation that one may always choose as the collective estimator the average of single-result estimators
\tilde{\theta}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}\tilde{\theta}(x_{i})
, without violating locally unbiasedness condition. However, such a procedure would be a poor choice in the limit of large repetitions number
k
, as it would not solve the problem of biasedness far from the point
\theta_{0}
.

It turns out that in the limit of large
k
,
Eq. (
3.7
)
may be saturated in all points
\theta\in\Theta
by the same estimator. That is the maximum likelihood estimator (ML estimator), i.e., the one associating to any sequence of the measurement outcomes the value of
\theta
, for which the probability of obtaining this sequence is the biggest possible:

{\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\textrm{arg}\max\limits_{\theta}p({\boldsymbol{x}}|\theta).

(3.8)

For such estimator,
Vaart 1998
, 5.3. Asymptotic Normality

Nagaoka & Hayashi 2005
, CHAPTER 7

\begin{split}\forall_{\theta}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle{\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle&=\theta\\
\lim_{k\to\infty}k\Delta^{2}{\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})&=\frac{1}{F[p(\cdot|\theta)]},\end{split}

(3.9)

where in principle the value of
F[p(\cdot|\theta)]
may depend on the value of parameter.

As the precise formulation of converging ML estimator to the normal distribution is crucial for understanding ambiguities regarding the interpretation of FI in the context of HL, for completeness, in

3.2. Example – two arms interferometer

Let us go back to the problem of estimating the phase shift in two arms interferometer

(3.10)

The problem of estimating

(3.11)

\ddot{L}_{k}(\bar{\theta}({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{x}})\overset{p}{\to}-F(\theta_{0}).

(3.22)

The nominator is given as:

\sqrt{k}\dot{L}(\theta_{0}|{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sqrt{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{d}{d\theta}\log p(x_{i}|\theta)\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}-\underbrace{\mathds{E}\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}\log p(x_{1}|\theta)\right)\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}}_{=0})\right)

(3.23)

therefore, from the central limit theorem, it converges to the normal distribution with variance:

\mathds{E}\left(\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}\log p(x_{1}|\theta)\right)\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}^{2}\right)=F(\theta_{0}).

(3.24)

Applying above, together with
Eq. (
3.22
)
, to
Eq. (
3.20
)
we finally get:

\forall_{\theta_{0}}\quad\sqrt{k}({\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\theta_{0})\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{F(\theta_{0})}{F(\theta_{0})^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{F(\theta_{0})}\right),

(3.25)

what was to be proven.
\square

3.4. Quantum Cramér-Rao bound

Previously we have asked how well the parameter
\theta
value may be estimated by probing probability distribution
p(x|\theta)
, i.e., we have minimized MSE over all locally unbiased estimators. Here we generalize the problem slightly: there is a given family of states
\rho_{\theta}
dependent on the unknown parameter
\theta
, and we want to optimize the whole procedure over both choices of measurement
\{M_{x}\}
as well as an estimator.

Using the result obtained for the classical case, we may see the problem as optimizing the classical FI
Eq. (
3.3
)
over the choice of measurement. After direct calculation, one would obtain
Braunstein & Caves 1994

\max_{\{M_{x}\}}F\big[p(x|\theta)=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{x})\big]=:F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta})=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2}),

(3.26)

where
L
is symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD)
\frac{d\rho_{\theta}}{d\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(L\rho_{\theta}+\rho_{\theta}L\right)
.

As for the tensor product of matrices
\rho_{\theta}\otimes\sigma_{\theta}
correspodning SLD operators has the form
L_{\rho}\otimes\openone+\openone\otimes L_{\sigma}
, the QFI is additive:

F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta}\otimes\sigma_{\theta})=F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta})+F_{Q}(\sigma_{\theta}).

(3.27)

From the fact that QFI is FI maximized over measurements, it is also monotonic, i.e., for any
\theta
-independent completely positive trace preserving map
{\mathcal{E}}(\cdot)

F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta})\geq F_{Q}({\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{\theta})),

(3.28)

as acting
{\mathcal{E}}(\cdot)
followed by POVM on
{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{\theta})
clearly defined equivalent POVM on
\rho_{\theta}
directly.

Moreover, from above, it is also convex, i.e., for any
\theta
-independent parameters
c_{\rho},c_{\sigma}

c_{\rho}F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta})+c_{\sigma}F_{Q}(\rho_{\sigma})\geq F_{Q}(c_{\rho}\rho_{\theta}+c_{\sigma}\sigma_{\theta}),

(3.29)

as one may consider the mapping
\mathcal{E}:c_{\rho}\rho_{\theta}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|+c_{\sigma}\rho_{\sigma}\otimes|2\rangle\langle 2|\mapsto c_{\rho}\rho_{\theta}+c_{\sigma}\rho_{\sigma}
. For the situation where
c_{\rho},\,c_{\sigma}
depend on
\theta
, the extended convexity may be defined
Alipour & Rezakhani 2015
, but it will not be discussed in this thesis.

As stated before, QFI may be derived by direct maximization of classical FI over the measurement. Here I want to present an alternative derivation based on joint optimization over both measurement and estimator (such an approach will be beneficial in multiparameter case
Ch.
5
). From the saturability of classical CR bound, we know that:

F_{Q}^{-1}=\min_{M_{x},\textrm{l.u.}\tilde{\theta}(x)}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}.

(3.30)

From the discussion performed in
Sec.
2.3
, looking for the optimal strategy, we may restrict to projective measurements. For given
\theta_{0}
let me define:

X=\sum_{x}(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta_{0})|x\rangle\langle x|.

(3.31)

Therefore QFI is given as:

F_{Q}^{-1}=\min_{M_{x},l.u.\tilde{\theta}(x)}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\min_{X}\left[\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}X^{2})\quad\textrm{with}\quad\textrm{Tr}(\dot{\rho}_{\theta}X)=1\right],

(3.32)

where the minimization is made over all hermitian
X
. It may be performed with the Lagrange multipliers’ method, from which:

\forall_{\delta X}\textrm{Tr}((\rho_{\theta}X+X\rho_{\theta}-\lambda\dot{\rho}_{\theta})\delta X)=0\Rightarrow\dot{\rho}_{\theta}=\lambda(\rho_{\theta}X+X\rho_{\theta}),

(3.33)

where the last equality is equivalent to the definition of SLD (up to constant factor
\lambda
). Local unbiasedness condition implies
\lambda=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2})
, and therefore

\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}X^{2})=\frac{\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2})}{(\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2}))^{2}}=\frac{1}{\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2})}\Rightarrow F_{Q}=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L^{2}).

(3.34)

Here we should stress that even if we have derived the value of QFI with only local unbiasedness condition, in the limit of many repetitions, it has similar consequences as the classical one. Indeed, the corresponding measurement, i.e., the projection onto eigenvectors of
L
, results in the probability distribution maximizing classical FI. That indicates that this measurement will be optimal also from the global perspective and allow for effective usage of the ML estimator.

One issue should be mentioned here. In general, the measurement maximizing classical FI may depend on the exact value of the parameter, so the above reasoning cannot be applied directly. Still, in such case, one may asymptotically saturate quantum CR globally, using a two-step procedure
Gill & Massar 2000
;
Kahn & Gutţă 2008
;
Yang et al. 2019
.
First, use
\sim\sqrt{k}
copies performing full tomography of the state
Haah et al. 2017
to obtain some approximate value of
\theta_{\textrm{est}}
with the risk of any finite error exponentially decreasing with
\sqrt{k}
1
1

1

See
Guţă et al. 2008
, Section 2.1
for broader discussion and direct calculation for the qubit case, where Hoeffding’s inequality
Hoeffding 1963
has been used.
. Then use the remaining
k-\sqrt{k}
copies to perform the measurement in the basis of
L
for
\theta=\theta_{\textrm{est}}
. Consequently

\forall_{\theta\in\Theta}\lim_{k\to\infty}k\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\frac{1}{F_{Q}(\rho_{\theta})}.

(3.35)

It is worth stressing that for pure states, the formula for QFI takes an especially simple form
2
2

2

From
Eq. (
3.36
)
, one may notice, that Fisher information is related to natural metric on Hilbert space induced by a scalar product, namely
|\langle\psi_{\theta}|\psi_{\theta+d\theta}\rangle|^{2}=1-\frac{1}{4}F_{Q}d\theta^{2}

Braunstein et al. 1996
. In a general mixed state case, it may be related to the Bures metric, induced by fidelity formula
Hübner 1992
;
Sommers & Zyczkowski 2003
(see also
Zhou & Jiang 2019
for discussion about the ambiguities at points, where the rank of matrices changes).
:

F_{Q}=4(\langle\dot{\psi}_{\theta}|\dot{\psi}_{\theta}\rangle-|\langle\dot{\psi}_{\theta}|\psi_{\theta}\rangle|^{2}).

(3.36)

3.5. Quantum metrology – unitary evolution

Now we can move to a more general case, i.e., the problem of channel estimation, where the procedure may be optimized not only over measurement and estimator but also the input state or even additional features, as discussed in
Sec.
2.5
. For simplicity, let us start with the problem of unitary evolution, i.e., the one given as
U=e^{i\theta\Lambda}
. From the convexity of the QFI, for any channel, the optimal input state will be the pure state. Therefore, for a single usage of the channel, from
Eq. (
3.36
)
, we have:

F_{Q}=4(\langle\psi_{\theta}|\Lambda^{2}|\psi_{\theta}\rangle-|\langle\psi_{\theta}|\Lambda|\psi_{\theta}\rangle|^{2})=4(\langle\psi|\Lambda^{2}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|\Lambda|\psi\rangle|^{2})=4\Delta^{2}\Lambda.

(3.37)

In the case when generator
\Lambda
has a bounded spectrum with minimal and maximal eigenvalues
\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}
, the state maximizing QFI will be an equal superposition of corresponding eigenstates
|\psi_{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{i\lambda_{-}\theta}|\lambda_{-}\rangle+e^{i\lambda_{+}\theta}|\lambda_{+}\rangle)
, for which

F_{Q}=(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}.

(3.38)

The reasoning does not change significantly for
n
parallel usage of the gate
Fig.
2.3
(b). We simply need to focus on the states maximizing
\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\otimes n}
, which be the corresponding
n00n
state
|\psi^{n}_{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{in\lambda_{-}\theta}|\lambda_{-}\rangle^{\otimes n}+e^{i\lambda_{+}\theta}|\lambda_{+}\rangle^{\otimes n})
with:

F_{Q}=n^{2}(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}.

(3.39)

Finally, one may ask if applying a more advanced adaptive strategy
Fig.
2.3
(c) improves this result. The answer is no, which was shown
Giovannetti et al. 2006
in the following way. For general adaptive evolution with
n
usage of gates, the output state is given by:

W_{\theta}|\psi\rangle,\quad\textrm{with}\quad W_{\theta}=V_{n}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)V_{n-1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)\cdots V_{1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone).

(3.40)

so the FQI is equal

F_{Q}=4(\langle\dot{\psi}_{\theta}|\dot{\psi}_{\theta}\rangle-|\langle\psi_{\theta}|\dot{\psi}_{\theta}\rangle|^{2})=4\left(\langle\psi|\dot{W}_{\theta}^{\dagger}\dot{W}_{\theta}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|W_{\theta}^{\dagger}\dot{W}_{\theta}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right)=4\Delta^{2}\left[-iW_{\theta}^{\dagger}\dot{W}_{\theta}\right],

(3.41)

where in the last step, identity
\openone=W_{\theta}W_{\theta}^{\dagger}
was added inside the first bracket, and factor
-i
has been added to make the connection with the original evolution generator more visible. Indeed,
-iW_{\theta}^{\dagger}\dot{W}_{\theta}
is simply sum of
n
properly rotated generators
\Lambda

-iW_{\theta}^{\dagger}\dot{W}_{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{j}(\theta),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\\
\Lambda^{\prime}_{j}(\theta)=\left(V_{j-1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)\cdots V_{1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)\right)^{\dagger}(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\left(V_{j-1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)\cdots V_{1}(U_{\theta}\otimes\openone)\right),

(3.42)

so its both minimal and maximal eigenvalues may be at most
n\lambda_{-},n\lambda_{+}
, which is obtained with
\forall_{j}V_{j}=\openone
(as
U_{\theta}
commutes with
\Lambda
). It is also clear that no additional entanglement with ancilla is needed. Therefore, optimal state may be simply obtain by
n
acting of the gates on single system copy:
|\psi_{\theta}^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{in\lambda_{-}\theta}|\lambda_{-}\rangle+e^{i\lambda_{+}\theta}|\lambda_{+}\rangle)
.

Note that states optimal in both parallel and adaptive schemes has the same problem as the one discussed in
Sec.
2.1
– they became identical for parameters differ by
\frac{k2\pi}{n(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})}
(up to the irrelevant global phase).

When the experimentalist has a large number of trials
k
to perform, it does not lead to a problem, as one may easily repeat the reasoning from the end of the previous section – first, perform the general tomography (for example, by using simple
|\psi_{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{i\lambda_{-}\theta}|\lambda_{-}\rangle+e^{i\lambda_{+}\theta}|\lambda_{+}\rangle)
state (with a single action of the gate in each trial), and only after that focus on optimal local estimation. That would finally lead to:

\lim_{k\to\infty}k\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\frac{1}{n^{2}(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}}.

(3.43)

Still, more is needed to answer the general problem of
optimal usage of all
N
resources
. Intuitively one can see that starting from finite size
\Theta
, to use all resources with at least close to optimal way, one could divide this
N
between different
n00n
states (with different
n
), each devoted to estimating
\theta
with a precision of another order. It was shown formally
Higgins et al. 2009
, that such an approach (assuming that the resources have been divided up in such a way as to allow sufficient repetition of the different orders of precision), indeed allows for obtaining quadratic scaling of the precision with
N
for the problem of phase shift in the interferometer:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\sim\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

(3.44)

However, a quantitative discussion of these results demands tools beyond QFI so that it will be performed later in
Ch.
4
.

3.6. Infinite QFI for average energy constraints

In
Sec.
2.4
, I have discussed the problem of the optimization of metrology protocol with constraint only on the average (not total) value of the energy, pointing at the fact that in such case
\Delta^{2}H
may be arbitrarily large. Let me go back here to this problem. Note that the above comment, together with
Eq. (
3.37
)
, implies the possibility of obtaining the infinite value of QFI for finite average energy. How should it be interpreted in the context of the asymptotical saturability of CR? Let me discuss a simple example.

Let us consider the interferometer, where the input state is the mixture of
n00n
states with different
n
, where the weight of the next state decreases like the inverse of the third power of
n
:

\rho=\frac{1}{\zeta(3)}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{m^{3}}|m00m\rangle\langle m00m|,

(3.45)

where
\zeta(\cdot)
is Rieman zeta function and
\zeta(3)\approx 1.20
is normalization factor. The mean energy is finite:

\langle H\rangle=\frac{1}{\zeta(3)}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2m^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{12\zeta(3)}\approx 0.64,

(3.46)

while its variance goes to infinity:

\langle\Delta^{2}H\rangle=\frac{1}{\zeta(3)}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{4m}=+\infty,

(3.47)

so does QFI. Knowing the measurement optimal for each
n00n
state, consider the general measurement:

\{M_{m,\pm}\}=\{\tfrac{1}{2}(|0\rangle^{\otimes m}\pm|1\rangle^{\otimes m})(\langle 0|^{\otimes m}\pm\langle 1|^{\otimes m})\},

(3.48)

for which
p_{m,+}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\zeta(3)m^{3}}\cos^{2}(m\theta/2)
,
p_{m,-}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\zeta(3)m^{3}}\sin^{2}(m\theta/2)
. This leads also to infinite classical FI. Moreover, such a formulated example is free from the problems found for a single
n00n
state, discussed in
Sec.
2.1
, i.e., the probability distribution distinct between any two values of
\theta
. All these imply that for the ML estimator from equation
Eq. (
3.9
)
, we have:

\lim_{k\to\infty}k\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}=0.

(3.49)

How such a result should be understood? To understand it better, let us cut the sum in
Eq. (
3.45
)
at some large
M
to get:

\rho_{M}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m^{3}}|m00m\rangle\langle m00m|,

(3.50)

where
\mathcal{N}_{M}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}1/m^{3}\leq\zeta(3)
is normalization factor. For any finite
M
:

p_{m,+}(\theta)=\frac{\cos^{2}(m\theta/2)}{\mathcal{N}_{M}m^{3}},\,p_{m,-}(\theta)=\frac{\sin^{2}(m\theta/2)}{\mathcal{N}_{M}m^{3}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F(M)=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}m^{2}/m^{3}\right)/\mathcal{N}_{M},

(3.51)

so classical FI is an unlimited growing function of

.

The MSE of ML estimator for different

has been plotted in figure

. The higher

, the bigger number of repetitions

is needed for saturating CR bound. Especially before saturating CR, we observe the scaling of MSE faster than

Eq. (
3.49
)
.

As the average energy used in a single trial is finite end equal

3.7. General theorem for a noisy estimation

As stated in the introduction, the main object of interest in this thesis will be the unitary noiseless evolution, therefore from a strictly theoretical point of view, the discussion performed so far is enough for understanding the context of the results presented later in
Ch.
4
. However, this is the idealized situation, as in practice, some noise occurs in any actual metrological situation. Therefore, for completeness, I present the state-of-the-art general theorem about the possibility of attending the Heisenberg scaling in noisy metrology, formulated only within QFI formalism.
This presentation is only to give a broader context to the results, and the noise case will not be analyzed quantitatively later in this work.

For physical system
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, consider general channel
{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
, i.e., completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) map. Any such map may be defined by the set of (not unique) Kraus operators
\{K_{k}(\theta)\}

{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}(\rho)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}K_{k}(\theta)\rho K_{k}^{\dagger}(\theta),

(3.52)

where
\{K_{k}(\theta)\}_{k}
satisfies
\sum_{k=1}^{r}K_{k}(\theta)^{\dagger}K_{k}(\theta)=\openone
(an alternative way of characterizing quantum channels via Choi matrices will be given in
Sec.
5
). Further, for more compact notation, the parameter in the bracket will be omitted
K_{k}:=K_{k}(\theta)
, but one should keep in mind that they depend on
\theta
.

Then the QFI of the output state maximized over the input state is bounded by
Fujiwara & Imai 2008
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012

\max_{\rho}F_{Q}\leq 4\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{r}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{K}_{k}\right\|,

(3.53)

which comes from the fact that QFI of any mixed state
\rho_{\theta}
is smaller or equal to QFI of its purification (as the consequence of
Eq. (
3.28
)
). Next, as the set of Krauss operators is defined only up to unitary transformation:
Fujiwara & Imai 2008
, Proposition 2.

\tilde{K}_{k}(\theta)=\sum_{j}u_{kj}(\theta)K_{j}(\theta),

(3.54)

(where in principle the unitary
u_{kj}(\theta)
mixing different Krauss operator may also depend on
\theta
), the inequality may be tightened by performing minimization over all Krauss representation. Around
\theta_{0}
, denoting
u_{kj}(\theta)=u_{kj}(\theta_{0})+ih_{kj}(\theta-\theta_{0})+\mathcal{O}((\theta-\theta_{0})^{2})
(where
h
is hermitian), by direct subjection we get:

\max_{\rho}F_{Q}\leq\min_{h}4\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{r}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{k}\right\|=\min_{h}4\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left(\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}+i\sum_{j}h_{kj}K^{\dagger}_{j}\right)\left(\dot{K}_{k}-i\sum_{j}h_{kj}K_{j}\right)\right\|,

(3.55)

Moreover, the above bound is proved to be saturable if ancilla allowed
\rho\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
. Starting from this bound, one may derive practical bounds for parallel and adaptive strategies discussed in
Fig.
2.3
(b,c) using
n
quantum gates, in terms of the Krauss operators of a single gate.

For the parallel scheme
Fujiwara & Imai 2008
:

F_{Q}\leq\min_{h}4\left(n\|\alpha\|+n(n-1)\|\beta\|^{2}\right)

(3.56)

where

\begin{split}\alpha=&\sum_{k=0}^{r}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left(\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}+i\sum_{j}h_{kj}K^{\dagger}_{j}\right)\left(\dot{K}_{k}-i\sum_{j}h_{kj}K_{j}\right),\\
\beta=&\sum_{k=0}^{r}{\tilde{K}}_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}K_{k}^{\dagger}\left(\dot{K}_{k}-i\sum_{j}h_{kj}K_{j}\right).\end{split}

(3.57)

Note especially that
\|\alpha\|\geq\|\beta\|^{2}
.

For a general sequential adaptive scheme, the bound may be formulated in the iterative form
Kurdzialek et al. 2022
(which is an improved version of the bound form
Demkowicz-Dobrzański & Maccone 2014
):

F_{Q}\leq 4a^{(n)},\quad\textrm{where}\quad a^{(i+1)}=\min_{h}\left[a^{(i)}+\|\alpha\|+2\|\beta\|\sqrt{a^{(i)}}\right],\quad\textrm{with}\quad a^{(0)}=0,

(3.58)

which may be further bounded by two alternative closed formulas:

F_{Q}\leq\min_{h}\begin{cases}4\left(n\|\alpha\|+n(n-1)\|\beta\|\sqrt{\|\alpha\|}\right),\\
4\left(n\|\alpha\|+n(n-1)\|\beta\|^{2}+(\|\alpha\|-\|\beta\|^{2})n\log n\right),\end{cases}

(3.59)

where the first one is more usefull in regime
n\approx 1,\,\|\beta\|/\sqrt{\|\alpha\|}\ll 1
, while the second one in the regime
n\gg 1,\,\|\beta\|/\sqrt{\|\alpha\|}\approx 1
.

Looking at both
Eq. (
3.56
)
and
Eq. (
3.59
)
one can see that the necessary condition for occurring HS is that
\forall_{h}\|\beta\|\neq 0
. It has been identified as a "Hamiltonian-not-in-Kraus-span" (HNKS) condition. Namely, define:

\tilde{\Lambda}=i\sum_{k}K_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{K}_{k},\quad\mathcal{S}=\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{H}}\{K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{j}\,\textrm{for all},\ i,j\},

(3.60)

where
\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{H}}\{\cdot\}
represents all Hermitian operators that are linear combinations of operators in
\{\cdot\}
. Then HNKS is satisfied iff
\tilde{\Lambda}\notin\mathcal{S}
.

Otherwise, in the limit of large
n
, both bounds reduce to:

F_{Q}\lesssim\min_{h:\beta=0}4n\|\alpha\|,

(3.61)

or, more formally,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}F_{Q}=\min_{h:\beta=0}\|\alpha\|
.
In such case, the bound has been proven to be asymptotically tight
Zhou & Jiang 2021
, Theorem 2
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}nF_{Q}=4\|\alpha\|.

(3.62)

The most prominent example of practical importance, when the above bound has been saturated, revealing quantum advantage, was using squeezed vacuum state
Caves 1981
in the interferometer in the LIGO gravitational wave detector
LIGO 2011
;
Aasi et al. 2013
, where the experimentalists obtain the MSE improved by factor respectively
3.5\textrm{dB}
and
2.15\textrm{dB}
compared to the optimal "classical" strategy with the same output energy. The value of MSE is in perfect accord with theoretical model
Aasi et al. 2013
, see also
App.
A
for exact formula and relation with the bound.

In the case where
\min_{h}\|\beta\|>0
, in the limit of large
n
the bounds
Eq. (
3.56
)
,
Eq. (
3.59
)
(the second line in the latter one) reduce to:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}F_{Q}\leq\min_{h}4\|\beta\|^{2}

(3.63)

and it may be saturated
Zhou & Jiang 2021
, up to this leading term. The authors of
Zhou & Jiang 2021
propose the following procedure.

They show that there exists two dimensional subspace of joint system and ancilla
\textrm{span}\{|0_{L}\rangle,|1_{L}\rangle\}\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
and the recovery operation
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{\textrm{Lin}}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\textrm{Lin}(\textrm{span}\{|0_{L}\rangle,|1_{L}\rangle\})
such that for any
\rho\in\textrm{Lin}(\textrm{span}\{|0_{L}\rangle,|1_{L}\rangle\})
in the closest neighborhood of
\theta_{0}
, effectively unitary evolution rotating by the angle
2\|\beta\|(\theta-\theta_{0})
is obtained:

\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}\otimes\openone_{A}(\rho))\overset{\theta\approx\theta_{0}}{\approx}e^{i\|\beta\|(\theta-\theta_{0})\sigma_{z}}\rho e^{-i\|\beta\|(\theta-\theta_{0})\sigma_{z}}.

(3.64)

More precisely, exactly at point
\theta_{0}
this procedure satisfies
3
3

3

In original paper
Zhou & Jiang 2021
the additional mapping between 2-dimensional logical space to physical space
\textrm{span}\{|0_{L}\rangle,|1_{L}\rangle\}\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
appears, which I omitted for simplicity of the formulas
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\otimes\openone_{A}(\rho))&=\rho\\
\frac{d}{d\theta}\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}\otimes\openone_{A}(\rho))\Big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}&=i\|\beta\|(\sigma_{z}\rho-\rho\sigma_{z})\end{split}

(3.65)

To get some intuition about the applicability of this protocol, let us consider the simplest non-trivial example.

Example

Consider a qubit sensing the phase-signal in
z
direction, subjected to dephasing noise in
x
direction. What is important, the dephasing operator act before the unitary operator. The exemplary Kraus representation for this model is:

K_{1}=pe^{i\theta\sigma_{z}},\quad K_{2}=\sqrt{1-p}e^{i\theta\sigma_{z}}\sigma_{x}

(3.66)

CHAPTER 4Obtainable Heisenberg limit in single parameter unitary estimation

In this chapter, I will discuss the fundamental achievable bounds for the precision obtainable in single parameter unitary estimation with a total amount of resources limited. As mentioned before, this problem cannot be analyzed with the usage of QFI or any other formalism based solely on the concept of the local unbiasedness of the estimator. Therefore to perform quantitative analysis, I will use the formalism of minimax estimation and Bayesian estimation.

4.1. Bayesian and minimax costs

Let me start again with the "classical" theory of estimation, i.e., where the probability distribution of the measure outcomes dependent on the value of the parameter
p(x|\theta)
is fixed.

The most popular alternative for FI formalism is the Bayesian one. It postulates that the unknown parameter
\theta
may take a given value according to some known a priori distribution
p(\theta)
. In this sense, both
\theta
and
x
are treated as random variables with joint probability distribution
p(x,\theta)=p(x|\theta)p(\theta)
. That implies marginal distribution
p(x)=\int d\theta p(x|\theta)p(\theta)
, and also conditional probability:

p(\theta|x)=\frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{\int d\theta p(x|\theta)p(\theta)},

(4.1)

also called a posteriori distribution of
\theta
after obtaining the result
x
. By Baysiesan MSE, we define the standard MSE, averaged with a priori distribution:

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\int p(\theta)d\theta\int dxp(x|\theta)(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2}.

(4.2)

This approach may be applied to the problem when the parameter to be measured represent the quantity which may take different values and in any peculiar realization it has been randomly drawn from a known distribution. Then we may use the knowledge about this distribution to make the procedure more effective on average (i.e. we may focus to make it effective around the values of
\theta
, which are more probable, accepting the loss of accuracy in the unlikely cases). For example, when designing a thermometer to measure the temperature inside a heated room, we can focus on having good accuracy in the
15^{\circ}C
-
30^{\circ}C
range and allow very little accuracy outside of this range as we expect it to be used very rarely at other temperatures. To validate the efficiency of such a thermometer in practice, one should perform many repetitions in typical circumstances, comparing the results with the ones coming from a high-quality trusted device. After that, one would recover the formula
Eq. (
4.2
)
. On the other hand, Bayesian estimation may be also applied in measuring the parameter which is fundamentally postulated to be constant (like, for example, the transition frequency of an atom). In that case, the a priori probability
p(\theta)
will express our imperfect knowledge, coming from previous measurements, which (similarly as in the previous case) may be used to optimize the measurement protocol (the way, how
p(\theta)
may arise from previous measurements, will be discussed at the end of
Sec.
4
in subsection "Intuitive interpretation"). However, in this case, the formula
Eq. (
4.2
)
will not be recovered after performing many repetitions of the experiment in typical circumstances, as the parameter will take the same specific value in each realization. Still, using the Bayesian formula is reasonable to optimize the metrology strategy or to validate a peculiar strategy on the level of a mathematical model (i.e. without performing measurements).

The alternative for the Bayesian approach is the minimax one
Häjek 1972
;
Hayashi 2011
. Here one only needs to assume that the actual value of the parameter belongs to some set
\theta\in\Theta
, and then to validate the effectiveness of the estimation strategy, one assumes the most pessimistic scenario, i.e, maximizes the MSE over all possible values of parameter:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int dxp(x|\theta)(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2}.

(4.3)

Directly from the definition, one can see that if a priori distribution in the Bayesian approach has support inside of the set considered in the minimax approach
\textrm{supp}\,p(\theta)\subseteq\Theta
, then for any fixed estimator, the Bayesian cost is smaller or equal than the minimax one:

\forall_{\tilde{\theta}(\cdot)}\quad\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\leq\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}.

(4.4)

Bayesian cost is also connected with FI, via the van Trees inequality
Gill & Levit 1995
:

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\frac{1}{\int d\theta p(\theta)F(\theta)+I},\quad I=\int d\theta\frac{1}{p(\theta)}\left(\frac{dp(\theta)}{d\theta}\right)^{2},

(4.5)

where
I
should be understood as the information included in a priori distribution.

From the asymptotic optimality of the ML estimator, in the limit of many repetitions:

\min_{\tilde{\theta}}\lim_{k\to\infty}k\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}k\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}_{\textrm{ML}}=\int p(\theta)d\theta\frac{1}{F(\theta)},

(4.6)

\min_{\tilde{\theta}}\lim_{k\to\infty}k\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}k\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}_{\textrm{ML}}=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{F(\theta)}.

(4.7)

Especially these two coincide if
F(\theta)
does not depend on
\theta
.

Now we want to use these formalisms to analyze the problem of quantum metrology, i.e.,
p(x|\theta)=\textrm{Tr}(M_{x}\rho_{\theta})
. However, before starting, two issues should be mentioned here.

First, we focus on the situation where all resources are used in the optimal way, so, as discussed before, all resources are accumulated in a single measurement realization. This implies that any single measurement outcome is connected directly with the estimator indication (unlike in the previous case, where the estimator could be the function of a sequence of outcomes). Therefore, to simplify notation, we may label the measurement directly by the value of estimator
(\tilde{\theta}(x)-\theta)^{2}\textrm{Tr}(M_{x}\rho_{\theta})dx\to(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})d\tilde{\theta}
.

Second, as we are no longer restricted only to local estimation, we would like to consider a more general cost than the quadratic one. Therefore, in general, we will want to minimize the mean value of cost function
\mathcal{C}(\theta,\tilde{\theta})
, which we typically choose to satisfy
\mathcal{C}(\theta,\tilde{\theta})\overset{|\tilde{\theta}-\theta|\ll 1}{\approx}(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}
, which allows us to maintain a relationship with the results obtained using Fisher information. The proper formulas in both formalisms are, respectively, in Bayesian formalism:

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\int p(\theta)d\theta\int d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})\mathcal{C}(\theta,\tilde{\theta}),

(4.8)

and in minimax formalism:

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})\mathcal{C}(\theta,\tilde{\theta}).

(4.9)

4.2. Optimality of covariant measurements

Before discussing the fundamental bound derived in
Górecki et al. 2020
, I want to recall the theorem,
which significantly simplifies the measurement optimization when the estimation problem satisfies proper symmetry conditions. While this theorem was not used in
Górecki et al. 2020
, and it is not necessary for understanding it, it allows for deriving very expressive examples providing the reader with good intuition.

Consider a problem where the family of channels is a unitary representation of a compact Lie group
G
, such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{g}(\rho)=U_{g}\rho U^{\dagger}_{g}
and the aim is to estimate the group element
g\in G
. We would say that the estimation problem is covariant (in both Bayesian or minimax approach), iff:

•

the cost function is invariant under the acting of the group
\mathcal{C}(g_{1},g_{2})=\mathcal{C}(hg_{1},hg_{2})
.

•

(required in Bayesian formalism) a priori distribution is invariant under acting of the group
p(g)dg=p(hg)d(hg)
– the prior is uniform with respect to the Haar measure on the group. For simplicity of the notation, further, I assume that
dg
is the normalized Haar measure,
\int_{G}dg=1
(so covariant a priori distribution is
p(g)=1
).

Then for a given input state
\rho
with single usage of the channel, the optimal cost is obtainable with covariant measurement, i.e., the one of the form
Holevo 1982
, Chapter 4
:

M_{\tilde{g}}=U_{\tilde{g}}M_{e}U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}},\quad\int dgU_{\tilde{g}}M_{e}U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}}=\openone.

(4.10)

After applying it to
Eq. (
4.8
)
,
Eq. (
4.9
)
it simplified the problem to the form:

\begin{cases}\inf_{M_{\tilde{g}}}\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\inf_{M_{\tilde{g}}}\quad\,\,\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\quad\,\,\int d\tilde{g}\textrm{Tr}(U_{g}\rho U^{\dagger}_{g}M_{\tilde{g}})\mathcal{C}(g,\tilde{g})\\
\inf_{M_{\tilde{g}}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\inf_{M_{\tilde{g}}}\int dg\int d\tilde{g}\textrm{Tr}(U_{g}\rho U^{\dagger}_{g}M_{\tilde{g}})\mathcal{C}(g,\tilde{g})\end{cases}=\inf_{M_{e}}\int d\tilde{g}\textrm{Tr}(\rho U_{\tilde{g}}M_{e}U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}})\mathcal{C}(e,\tilde{g})

(4.11)

The proof is based on the observation that for any measurement
\{M^{\prime}_{g}\}
one may construct corresponding covariant measurement by proper averaging:
M_{g}:=U_{g}\left(\int dhU^{\dagger}_{h}M^{\prime}_{h}U_{h}\right)U_{g}^{\dagger}
, while this procedure does not affect the Bayesian cost and may only decrease the minimax cost. Moreover, within minimax formalism, the statement may be generalized also for non-compact groups
Bogomolov 1982
, where formal averaging may not be possible.

The above directly implies that the covariant measurement is optimal for any parallel strategy with the usage of
N
gates – as
U_{g}^{\otimes N}
is still a group representation. In
Ch.
5
, I will reproduce the proof of an even stronger statement, saying that if ancilla is allowed, optimal parallel scheme with covariant measurement cannot be beaten by any adaptive strategy (the derivation, however, will demand more advanced formalism, which is not necessary at this point).

4.3. Interferometer with a fixed total number of photons

The most canonical example of applying the above theorem is the problem of estimating the unknown phase in the interferometer. In such case the bound
Eq. (
2.5
)
gives:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}},

(4.12)

without guarantee of saturability. It was shown
Luis & Perřina 1996
;
Buzžek et al. 1999
;
Berry & Wiseman 2000
, that for estimation of a completely unknown phase the additional
\pi^{2}
factor appears.

First, note that the value
\theta
should be identified with
\theta+2\pi
; therefore, we need to choose a proper cost function considering this fact. The common choice is

\mathcal{C}(\theta,\tilde{\theta})=4\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\theta}-\theta}{2}\right),

(4.13)

which for small difference
\tilde{\theta}-\theta
may be well approximated by
(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}
. The average cost for the covariant measurement is therefore equal:

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{0}U_{\tilde{\theta}}M_{0}U_{\tilde{\theta}}^{\dagger})4\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\theta}}{2}\right),

(4.14)

(so in notation from
Eq. (
4.11
)

g=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}
). Without loss, we may restrict ourselves to fully symmetric space and then use the eigenbasis of
U_{\tilde{\theta}}
. Since the mean cost is linear in
\rho
, we may restrict to a pure input state
|\psi^{N}\rangle
, for which

U_{\tilde{\theta}}|\psi^{N}\rangle=U_{\tilde{\theta}}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N}c_{m}|m\rangle\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}e^{im\tilde{\theta}}c_{m}|m\rangle,

(4.15)

where
|m\rangle
is the state with
m
photons in the upper (sensing) arm. After applying identity
4\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\theta}}{2}\right)=2-e^{i\tilde{\theta}}-e^{-i\tilde{\theta}}
we obtain

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\int\frac{d\tilde{\theta}}{2\pi}\left(\sum_{jk}c^{*}_{j}c_{k}e^{ik\tilde{\theta}}M_{0kj}e^{-ij\tilde{\theta}}\right)(2-e^{i\tilde{\theta}}-e^{-i\tilde{\theta}})=\sum_{jk}c^{*}_{j}c_{k}M_{0kj}(2\delta_{j,k}-\delta_{j+1,k}-\delta_{j-1,k}).

(4.16)

From condition
Eq. (
4.10
)

\forall_{m}M_{0mm}=1
; moreover, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\forall_{m}c_{m}\geq 0
(otherwise, one can always redefine the vectors of the basis, by multiplying them by proper phase factors). Therefore, the minimum value will be obtained for the maximal possible
M_{0m,m+1}
. From positivity condition for
M_{0}
,
M_{0m,m+1}\leq 1
, where the equality is satisfied if one chooses as
M_{0}
the projection onto (unnormalized) vector
\sum_{m=0}^{N}|m\rangle
, such that:

\frac{1}{2\pi}M_{\tilde{\theta}}=|\tilde{\theta}\rangle\langle\tilde{\theta}|,\quad\textrm{with}\quad|\tilde{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}e^{im\tilde{\theta}}|m\rangle.

(4.17)

After that, the problem simplifies to (introducing
c_{-1}=c_{n+1}=0
for compact notation):

\min_{|\psi^{N}\rangle,\{M_{\tilde{\theta}}\}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\min_{\{c_{m}\}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}(2c_{m}c_{m}-c_{m}c_{m+1}-c_{m}c_{m-1}),

(4.18)

which is equivalent to finding the lowest eigenvector of the following tri-diagonal matrix:

\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&0&0&&\\
-1&2&-1&0&\dots&\\
0&-1&2&-1&&\\
0&0&-1&2&&\\
&\vdots&&&\ddots&-1\\
&&&&-1&2\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(4.19)

The exact solution is known:

|\psi^{N}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+2}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\sin\left(\frac{(m+1)\pi}{N+2}\right)|m\rangle

(4.20)

for which

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=2\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{N+2}\right)\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right),

(4.21)

so indeed additional
\pi^{2}
factor appears when compared with
Eq. (
4.12
)
.

While the above provides the exact and complete solution to the problem, some comments are worth mentioning here.

First, while above we have used covariant measurement, the same cost may also be obtained by using the projective measurement, which may be seen as the discrete version of the previously discussed:

\{M_{j}\}=\{|\chi_{j}\rangle\langle\chi_{j}|\}_{j=0}^{N},\quad|\chi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}e^{i\frac{2\pi j}{N+1}m}|m\rangle,\quad\langle\chi_{j}|\chi_{k}\rangle=\delta_{jk}.

(4.22)

(note that this is a specific feature of this peculiar model and does not need to be satisfied for arbitrary covariant problems). This choice also has an intuitive interpretation, as this basis may be obtained by applying discrete Fourier transform to the basis with well-defined photons number in upper arm
\{|m\rangle\}_{m=0}^{N}
. Therefore, it stays in the analog to the problem of the position shift estimation in continuous space, where the shift generator is momentum operator
\hat{p}=\frac{1}{i}\frac{d}{dx}
, while the optimal measurement is
\{|x\rangle\}
(which may be obtained by applying Fourier transform to
\{|p\rangle\}
). Nevertheless, adequacy is not exact, as for the phase
\theta
(unlike for position
x
), no corresponding observable exists.

Second, even if the problem is analytically solvable for finite
N
, it will be useful to see what approximation may be used in the limit
N\to\infty
(that will allow us to understand better a more general case). Let us introduce
m/N\to\mu
and consider
\hat{f}(\mu)
and the state
Eq. (
4.15
)
with
c_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{f}(m/N)

Eq. (
4.18
)
takes form:

\min_{|\psi^{N}\rangle,\{M_{\tilde{\theta}}\}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\min_{\hat{f}}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\hat{f}^{*}(m/N)\frac{2\hat{f}(m/N)-\hat{f}(m/N+1/N)-\hat{f}(m/N-1/N)}{(1/N)^{2}},

(4.23)

which, assuming that
\hat{f}(\mu)
does not change very rapidly at distance of order

Imai & Hayashi 2009
;
Hayashi 2016
for more discussion about exact correspondence in the limit

\min_{|\psi^{N}\rangle,\{M_{k}\}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}\approx\frac{1}{N^{2}}\min_{f}\int_{0}^{1}d\mu\hat{f}^{*}(\mu)\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{f}(\mu),\quad\hat{f}(0)=\hat{f}(1)=0,

(4.24)

so the problem is equivalent to minimizing the kinetic energy of a single particle in the infinite well. The solution is then
\hat{f}(\mu)=\sqrt{2}\sin(\mu\pi)
, for which the cost is
\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}
.

Third, it is worth to point the vital difference between the problem of minimizing variance and minimizing the size of confidence interval
\delta_{N}
(depended on

\epsilon

\min\{\delta_{N}:p(|\tilde{\theta}-\theta|\geq\delta_{N}/2)\leq\epsilon\}

Imai & Hayashi 2009
.
In the many repetitions scenario, in the limit of large

\sqrt{k}({\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{ML}}}-\theta)
converges to the Gaussian one, which results in exponentially decreasing tail probabilities. Therefore, the solution chosen to minimize the MSE is simultaneously very effective in minimizing the size of a confidence interval. This, however, does not need to be true in the case discussed in this section. The probability of getting outcome
\tilde{\theta}
where the actual value of the parameter is
\theta
is given as (for optimal covariant measurement

4.17
)
):

p(\tilde{\theta}|\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}e^{im\tilde{\theta}}e^{-im\theta}c_{m}\right)^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{1}d\mu e^{iN\mu(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)}\hat{f}(\mu)\right)^{2}=\\
=|f(N(\tilde{\theta}-\theta))|^{2}=\frac{2\pi(1+\cos(N(\tilde{\theta}-\theta))}{(N^{2}(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}-\pi^{2}))^{2}},

(4.25)

so it decreases only like the forth power of
N(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)
. That is – the strategy using all resources to minimize the quadratic cost (or the one close to quadratic) turns out to have relatively slowly decreasing tail distributions. As a result, it will be highly inefficient for discrimination of confidence interval with a very small risk of error
\epsilon
1
1

1

For a fair comparison, it should be noted, that for
\epsilon\approx 1\%

)
is rather effective, as the size of the interval is about

starts to be far from optimal

At last, the broad literature is dedicated to analyzing the relation between the results obtained for the phase estimation in both QFI and Bayesian formalism. The complex analysis of plenty of measurement strategies may be found in

4.4. Interferometer with a fixed average number of photons

The same problem may be discussed with a constraint on the average number of the photons in the sensing arm, which is the constraint for the mean energy in understanding
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle=\overline{N}
, as discussed in
Sec.
2.4
. Here I recall the results from
Summy & Pegg 1990
;
Bandilla et al. 1991
.

The reasoning remains almost unchanged compared to the case with a fixed number of photons
N
. The only difference is that in equation
Eq. (
4.18
)
, the sum is over all natural indices
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
and the additional constraint appears
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\rho_{mm}m=\overline{N}
. Similarly, as in the previous case, by approximating discrete variables by continuous one for large
\overline{N}
(
m/\overline{N}\to\mu
), we get:

\min_{|\psi^{N}\rangle,\{M_{\tilde{\theta}}\}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}\approx\frac{1}{\overline{N}^{2}}\min_{f}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\mu f^{*}(\mu)\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu^{2}}\right)f(\mu),\quad\int_{0}^{+\infty}|f(\mu)|^{2}=1,\quad f(0)=0,\quad\int_{0}^{+\infty}|f(\mu)|^{2}\mu=1.

(4.26)

The solution may be found using the standard Lagrange multiplier method,

-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mu^{2}}f(\mu)+f(\mu)(\lambda_{1}+\mu\lambda_{2})=0\Rightarrow f(\mu)\propto\textrm{Ai}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mu}{\lambda_{2}^{2/3}}\right),

(4.27)

where
\textrm{Ai}(\cdot)
is the Airy function of the first kind. After taking into account the conditions, we obtain:

f(\mu)=\frac{1}{\textrm{Ai}^{\prime}(A_{0})}\sqrt{\frac{2|A_{0}|}{3}}\textrm{Ai}\left(A_{0}+\frac{2|A_{0}|}{3}\mu\right),

(4.28)

where
A_{0}\approx-2.34
is the first zero of the Airy function and
\textrm{Ai}^{\prime}(\cdot)
is its first derivative.
The corresponding minimal obtainable cost reads:

\min_{|\psi^{N}\rangle,\{M_{\tilde{\theta}}\}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}\approx\underbrace{\frac{4|A_{0}|^{3}}{27}}_{c\approx 1.89}.

(4.29)

One comment should be added here. In the above proof, we restrict ourselves to pure states. Therefore, for completeness, we argue that this cannot be overcome by any mixed input state. For simplicity consider two-rank mixed state
\rho=p_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|+p_{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
, where
\textrm{Tr}(|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|H)=\overline{N}_{1}
,
\textrm{Tr}(|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|H)=\overline{N}_{2}
, with
p_{1}\overline{N}_{1}+p_{2}\overline{N}_{2}=\overline{N}
. The minimal cost obtainable for the mixed input state is bigger or equal to the weighted sum of costs optimal for its pure components, therefore:

\overline{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]\geq p_{1}\overline{\mathcal{C}}[|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|]+p_{2}\overline{\mathcal{C}}[|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|]\geq p_{1}\frac{c}{\overline{N}_{1}^{2}}+p_{2}\frac{c}{\overline{N}_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{c}{(p_{1}\overline{N}_{1}+p_{2}\overline{N}_{2})^{2}}=\frac{c}{\overline{N}^{2}},

(4.30)

where by
\overline{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
I denoted the minimal variance obtainable for given state
\rho
. The third inequality comes from the convexity property of the
1/x^{2}
function (Jensen inequality). The reasoning may be trivially extended for the
\rho
of higher rank.

4.5.
\pi
-corrected Heisenberg limit

Let us go back to the fixed number of photons
N
.
Looking at the optimal cost
Eq. (
4.21
)
and exemplary measurement, which obtains this cost
Eq. (
4.22
)
, one may have the impression that the resulting value
\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}
is somehow related to dividing initial interval
[0,2\pi[
into
\sim N
parts corresponding to the measurement outcomes.

However, this intuition fails: in
Hayashi 2011
, it was proven that, up to the leading term, the optimal cost achievable in parallel strategy does not depend on the size of the initial interval.
More precisely, in the minimax formalism
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}=\pi^{2}
, independently of the size of
\Theta
(more details about the methods used in this paper will be discussed in
Ch.
6
).

However, three issues remain unclear:

•

is this results universal (does it works for any unitary generator)?

•

how the convergence of the above limit depends on the size of
\Theta
?

•

does the statement also hold in the adaptive scheme?

Below I present the results from
Górecki et al. 2020
, which answer all these questions. In the original work, the main thesis has been formulated within Bayesian formalism. Here I present a slightly simplified version formulated within the minimax approach.

Theorem 2.
Given a system with unitary evolution
U_{\theta}=e^{i\theta\Lambda}
governed by the bounded generator
\Lambda
with extreme eigenvalues
\lambda_{-}
and
\lambda_{+}
. No further assumptions about the spectrum of
\Lambda
are made. Especially the spectrum may be continuous. For more concise notation, I will denote the difference of the extreme eigenvalues by
\lambda=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}
. Then, assuming that from the beginning, the parameter is known to be in the interval,
\theta\in\Theta=[\theta_{0}-\delta/2,\theta_{0}+\delta/2]
, the optimal minimax cost, obtainable in the most general adaptive scheme with the usage of
N
quantum gate is bounded by:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\frac{8\log(N\lambda\delta)}{N\lambda\delta}\right).

(4.31)

The logaritmic correction shows how the knowledge gained from the measurement (related to uncertainty of an order of
\sim\frac{1}{N\lambda}
) begins to dominate over the knowledge initially held (uncertainty of order
\sim\delta
) as the amount of resources used
N
increases. Correction becomes irrelevant when
\frac{1}{N\lambda}\ll\delta
and asymptotically we obtain
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\pi^{2}/\lambda^{2}
, which is known to be saturable within the parallel scheme by the analog of the solution from
Sec.
4.3
. A more detailed discussion will be performed after the derivation of the bound.

Proof.
The proof contains two major parts. Firstly, I will derive the bound for the Bayesian cost for a specific class of prior distribution
p(\theta)
. Next, I will show that, up to small correction, this bound will also be valid for minimax cost for
\theta\in\Theta=[\theta_{0}-\delta/2,\theta_{0}+\delta/2]
.

First note that from the Bayesian rule, for any measurement, the Bayesian cost may be bounded from below by the minimal variance of a posteriori distribution
p(\theta|x)

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\int d\theta\int dx(\tilde{\theta}_{x}-\theta)^{2}p(x|\theta)p(\theta)=\int d\theta\int dx(\tilde{\theta}_{x}-\theta)^{2}p(\theta|x)p(x)\geq\min_{x}\int d\theta(\tilde{\theta}_{x}-\theta)^{2}p(\theta|x),

(4.32)

where we assume optimal
\tilde{\theta}_{x}
, i.e. the one pointing on mean value of
p(\theta|x)
.

Let name by
|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle
the output state after
N
acting of gates in the adaptive scheme. According to
Sec.
2.3
, without loss we may restrict to projective rank-one measurements
p(x|\theta)=|\langle\chi|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle|^{2}
, so:

p(\theta|x)=\frac{|\langle\chi|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle|^{2}p(\theta)}{\int|\langle\chi|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle|^{2}p(\theta)d\theta}

(4.33)

Note that scalar function
f(\theta)=\langle\chi|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle
have a finite bandwidth (the support of its Fourier transform
\hat{f}(\mu)
) of the size of
N(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})
2
2

2

note that while
\hat{f}(\mu)
plays a similar role as in the previous section, it is not exactly the same object, as here
\mu\in[N\lambda_{-},N\lambda_{+}]
, not
[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
. It may be clearly seen, when performing the calculation in the eigenbasis of
\Lambda
, as dependence on
\theta
will be visible only in terms which are a product of
N
terms
e^{i\mu_{k}\theta}
with
\lambda_{-}\leq\mu_{k}\leq\lambda_{+}
:

\langle\chi|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle=\\
\langle\chi|V_{N}\left(\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{+}}d\mu_{N}e^{i\mu_{N}\theta}|\mu_{N}\rangle\langle\mu_{N}|\right)\cdots V_{2}\left(\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{+}}d\mu_{2}e^{i\mu_{2}\theta}|\mu_{2}\rangle\langle\mu_{2}|\right)V_{1}\left(\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{+}}d\mu_{1}e^{i\mu_{1}\theta}|\mu_{1}\rangle\langle\mu_{1}|\right)|\psi_{\textrm{in}}\rangle\\
=\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{+}}d\mu_{N}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{+}}d\mu_{1}e^{i(\mu_{1}+\cdots\mu_{N})\theta}\langle\chi|V_{N}|\mu_{N}\rangle\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\langle\mu_{k+1}|V_{k}|\mu_{k}\rangle\langle\mu_{1}|\psi_{in}\rangle=\\
\int_{N\lambda_{-}}^{N\lambda_{+}}d\mu e^{i\mu\theta}\underbrace{\int d\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\perp}\langle\chi|V_{N}|\mu_{N}\rangle\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\langle\mu_{k+1}|V_{k}|\mu_{k}\rangle\langle\mu_{1}|\psi\rangle}_{\theta-\textrm{independent part}}

(4.34)

where
\mu=\mu_{1}+\cdots\mu_{N}
,
\int d\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\perp}
corresponds to integration over all directions where
\mu=const.

Next, to make the problem analytically solvable, let us consider the class of probability distribution
p(\theta)
, which also has finite bandwidth
L
. It was proven
Boas & Kac 1945
, Lemma 5.1.
, that for any positive function
p_{L}(\theta)
of the bandwidth
L
there exists the function
w_{L/2}(\theta)
with bandwidth
L/2
such that
p_{L}(\theta)=|w_{L/2}(\theta)|^{2}
. Therefore we have

p(\theta|x)=\frac{|f(\theta)w_{L/2}(\theta)|^{2}}{\int|f(\theta)w_{L/2}(\theta)|^{2}d\theta}=|g(\theta)|^{2},\quad\textrm{with}\quad g(\theta):=\frac{f(\theta)w_{L/2}(\theta)}{\sqrt{\int|f(\theta)w_{L/2}(\theta)|^{2}d\theta}},

(4.35)

where
g(\theta)
, as a (normalized) product of two functions with bandwidths
N\lambda
and
L/2
, has a bandwidth
N\lambda+L/2
(as its Fourier transform is equal to the convolution of Fourier transforms of
f(\theta)
and
w_{L/2}(\theta)
).

Therefore RHS of
Eq. (
4.32
)
may be bounded from below by minimization over all
p(\theta|x)=|g(\theta)|^{2}
(we may also assume without a loss that its mean value is at
\theta=0
, as shifting whole distribution does not change the bandwidth):

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\min_{g:\textrm{supp}\,\hat{g}\in[N\lambda_{-}-L/4,N\lambda_{+}+L/4]}\int d\theta|g(\theta)|^{2}\theta^{2},\quad\int d\theta|g(\theta)|^{2}=1

(4.36)

After applying Fourier transform:

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\min_{\hat{g}}\int_{N\lambda_{-}-L/4}^{N\lambda_{+}+L/4}d\mu\left|\frac{d\hat{g}(\mu)}{d\mu}\right|^{2},\quad\int_{-N\lambda_{-}-L/4}^{N\lambda_{+}+L/4}d\mu|\hat{g}(\mu)|^{2}=1

(4.37)

so it comes down to finding the minimum energy eigenstate in an infinite potential well and, therefore

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{[N\lambda+L/2]^{2}}.

(4.38)

Now, let us go to the second part, where I use the above results to derive the bound for minimax cost with
\Theta
of size
\delta
. It is clear that as
p_{L}
has a finite bandwidth, it cannot itself has finite support
\delta
, so it cannot be applied directly from

4.4
)
. However, one may introduce the finite bandwidth function, for which the probability of finding

outside of

is sufficiently small that it does not affect the final cost in the leading term. An example of such a function is

p_{\alpha,L}(\theta)=\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}L\textrm{sinc}^{4}\left(\pi\alpha\sqrt{(L\theta/4\alpha)^{2}-1}\right),\quad\textrm{with}\quad\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\approx 4\sqrt{2}\pi^{4}\alpha^{7/2}e^{-4\pi\alpha}

(4.39)

which is proportional to the fourth power of the Fourier transform of the Kaiser window
Kaiser & Schafer 1980
. The width of the main body of such a function is
8\alpha/L
, while the weight of tails decreases exponentially like
e^{-4\pi\alpha}
.

To show it formally, let me introduce compact notation for minimal Bayesian/minimax cost with given set
\Theta
, probability distribution
p(\theta)
and number of gates
N

\textrm{minimax}(\Theta,N):=\inf_{\rho,V_{i},M_{i}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho^{N}_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2},

(4.40)

\textrm{Bayesian}(p(\theta),N):=\inf_{\rho,V_{i},M_{i}}\int p(\theta)d\theta\int d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho^{N}_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2},

(4.41)

where
\rho^{N}_{\theta}
is the output state obtained by acting on the input state
\rho
the gate
N
times with unitaries
V_{i}
between, as in the scheme
2.3
(c).

Without loss of generality assume that
\Theta=[-\delta/2,+\delta/2]
(i.e.
\theta_{0}=0
). Let
R_{1}
be the probability, that
\theta
lays outside of
\Theta
(we consider symmetric
p_{L}(\theta)

R_{1}=2\int_{\delta/2}^{+\infty}d\theta p_{L}(\theta).

(4.42)

Next, we define renormalized cut probability distribution:

p^{\delta}_{L}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-R_{1}}p_{L}(\theta)&\textrm{for}\,\theta\in\Theta\\
0&\textrm{for}\,\theta\notin\Theta.\end{cases}

(4.43)

For such distribution from

4.4
)
:

\textrm{Bayesian}(p^{\delta}_{L},N)\leq\textrm{minimax}([-\delta/2,+\delta/2],N).

(4.44)

Let
\rho^{N,\delta}_{\theta},M^{\delta}_{\tilde{\theta}}
be the protocol optimal for
p_{L}^{\delta}
. Trivially, for uncut probability
p_{L}
this protocol is only suboptimal:

\textrm{Bayesian}(p_{L},N)=\inf_{\rho,V_{i},M_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{L}(\theta)d\theta\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}M_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{L}(\theta)d\theta\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho^{N,\delta}_{\theta}M^{\delta}_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}.

(4.45)

The last integration may be split between two parts
[-\delta/2,+\delta/2]
and
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\delta/2,+\delta/2)

\frac{1}{1-R_{1}}\underbrace{\int_{-\delta/2}^{\delta/2}p^{\delta}_{L}(\theta)d\theta\int_{-\delta/2}^{\delta/2}d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho^{N,\delta}_{\theta}M^{\delta}_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}}_{\textrm{Bayesian}(p^{\delta}_{L},N)}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\delta/2,+\delta/2)}p_{L}(\theta)d\theta\int_{-\delta/2}^{\delta/2}d\tilde{\theta}\textrm{Tr}(\rho^{N,\delta}_{\theta}M^{\delta}_{\tilde{\theta}})(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}}_{\leq R_{2}}.

(4.46)

The first one is simply
\textrm{Bayesian}(p^{\delta}_{L},N)

R_{2}=2\int_{\delta/2}^{+\infty}d\theta p_{L}(\theta)(\theta-\delta/2)^{2}.

(4.47)

From

\textrm{Bayesian}(p_{L},N)\leq\frac{1}{1-R_{1}}\textrm{Bayesian}(p^{\delta}_{L},N)+R_{2}

Next, in

Asymptotic saturability

The exact solution for the estimating
unknown phase in the interferometer, namely
Eq. (
4.20
)
, can be used to show asymptotical saturability of
Eq. (
4.52
)
.
Therefore, I will show that the bound may be asymptotically saturated within a parallel scheme.

By equating the upper and lower arms of the interferometer with the eigenstates of
\Lambda
with extreme eigenvalues, we get the analog of
Eq. (
4.20
)
:

|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N+2}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\sin\left(\frac{(m+1)\pi}{N+2}\right)|N-m\rangle_{\lambda_{+}}|m\rangle_{\lambda_{-}},

(4.54)

where
|N-m\rangle_{\lambda_{+}}|m\rangle_{\lambda_{-}}
is a symmetric state with
m
particles in state
|\lambda_{-}\rangle
and
N-m
particles in state
|\lambda_{+}\rangle
.
The only potential problem is that this state cannot distinguish the phases, which differ by multiplication of
2\pi/\lambda
. Hence, it allows only for effective estimation inside the region of size
2\pi/\lambda
. However, unlike in the
N00N
state case, this is not a severe problem, as the size
2\pi/\lambda
does not shrink with
N
. Therefore, one may spend at the beginning some sublinear amount of resources (for example
\sqrt{N}
) to discriminate such region (with exponentially decreasing probability of error) without affecting the final cost up to terms of order
1/N^{2}
.

On the other hand, in a specific case when the spectrum is continuous everywhere between
[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
, one may use the "continuous" version of the above state, namely

|\psi_{\theta}^{N}\rangle=\int_{N\lambda_{-}}^{N\lambda_{+}}e^{i\mu\theta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda N}}\sin\left(\frac{\mu\pi}{\lambda N}\right)|\mu\rangle d\mu,

(4.55)

where, by
|\mu\rangle
, we denote an arbitrary combination
|\mu_{N}\rangle\otimes...\otimes|\mu_{1}\rangle
such that
\sum\mu_{k}=\mu
(i.e. the one satisfying
U_{\theta}^{\otimes N}|\mu\rangle=e^{i\mu}|\mu\rangle
), with covariant measurement:

|\tilde{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{i\mu\tilde{\theta}}|\mu\rangle d\mu,

(4.56)

to get immidietly
\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\pi^{2}/N^{2}\lambda^{2}
in single shot experiment.

Intuitive interpretation

Once the bound is derived, I would like to provide a simple intuition for the finite bandwidth prior distribution used during the proof. That would be, in fact, an alternative derivation of the bound
Eq. (
4.38
)
, but only for the parallel strategies
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022a
.

Let start with the problem, where
\Lambda
has continuous spectrum
[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
3
3

3

Note that from an estimation point of view, this is an easier problem than one with a discrete spectrum since we have more space in the choice of the input state. Therefore the lower bound derived for the continuous spectrum is also valid for the discrete one.
and the parameter is entirely unknown
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
(i.e.
\delta=+\infty
). From the generalization of covariant measurement theorem for non-compact groups
Bogomolov 1982
, we can use the optimality of covariant measurement, and the optimal solution is the one discussed above with cost
\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\pi^{2}/N^{2}\lambda^{2}
.

Therefore, assuming that at the beginning one has to disposal
N+N_{0}
gates, the minimal achievable minimax cost will be therefore equal:

\textrm{minimax}(\mathbb{R},N+N_{0},\textrm{parallel})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{(N+N_{0})^{2}\lambda^{2}}.

(4.57)

On the other hand, we can consider an alternative, sub-optimal strategy:
at first use state
|\psi^{N_{0}}\rangle
with the measurement
Eq. (
4.56
)
to get an approximated value of the parameter
\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}
, and after that spend remaining
N
gates to estimate a difference
\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}
, i.e, use input state

|\psi^{N_{0}+N}\rangle=|\psi^{N_{0}}\rangle\otimes|\psi^{N}\rangle,

(4.58)

with the measurement:

|\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}\rangle\langle\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}|\otimes U_{\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}^{\otimes N}M_{\tilde{\theta}-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}U^{\dagger\otimes N}_{\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}.

(4.59)

The resulting minimax cost will be given as:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tilde{\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}|\langle\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}-\theta|\psi^{N_{0}}_{0}\rangle|^{2}\textrm{Tr}\left(M_{\tilde{\theta}-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}|\psi^{N}_{\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}\rangle\langle\psi^{N}_{\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}}|\right)(\theta-\tilde{\theta})^{2},

(4.60)

(where we used
\langle\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}|\psi^{N_{0}}_{\theta}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}-\theta|\psi^{N_{0}}_{0}\rangle
). We see that the cost does not depend on
\theta
, so the supremum at the beginning may be omitted. Introducing
\varphi=\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}
,
\tilde{\varphi}=\tilde{\theta}-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}
we get

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\varphi\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tilde{\varphi}|\langle\varphi|\psi^{N_{0}}_{0}\rangle|^{2}\textrm{Tr}(M_{\tilde{\varphi}}|\psi^{N}_{\varphi}\rangle\langle\psi^{N}_{\varphi}|)(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi})^{2},

(4.61)

so the formula for minimax cost
\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}
in this peculiar strategy is, in fact, equal to the formula for the Bayesian cost
\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\varphi}}
with the a priori distribution
p(\varphi)=|\langle\varphi|\psi^{N_{0}}_{0}\rangle|^{2}
! Moreover, for the state:

|\psi_{\theta}^{N_{0}}\rangle=\int_{N_{0}\lambda_{-}}^{N_{0}\lambda_{+}}e^{i\mu\theta}\hat{w}_{\lambda N_{0}}(\mu)|\mu\rangle d\mu,

(4.62)

the probability density of obtaining results
\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}
is:

p(\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}|\theta)=|\langle\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}}|\psi\rangle|^{2}=|w_{\lambda N_{0}}(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}})|^{2}

(4.63)

Therefore, one get a posteriori distribution
p_{2\lambda N_{0}}(\varphi)=|w_{\lambda N_{0}}(\theta-\tilde{\theta}_{\textrm{est}})|^{2}
, with the bandwidth
L=2\lambda N_{0}
. That it is – the finite bandwidth probability distribution of the bandwidth
L
is the one which may be obtained starting from a completely unknown parameter by spending
N_{0}=L/(2\lambda)
gates! As this two-step procedure is only sub-optimal, we immediately get:

\textrm{bayesian}(p_{2\lambda N_{0}},N,\textrm{parallel})\geq\textrm{minimax}(\mathbb{R},N+N_{0},\textrm{parallel}),

(4.64)

which leads to the same bound formula as
Eq. (
4.52
)
, but here for the parallel strategies only:

\overline{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\varphi}}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{[N\lambda+L/2]^{2}}.

(4.65)

The reasoning could be performed for the adaptive scheme as well, but then one would need to independently prove
Eq. (
4.57
)
for the general adaptive scheme. This makes the first approach more straightforward.

Frequency estimation

It is worth noting, that the bound

e^{i\theta H},\quad\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\leq E,\quad\theta\in[\theta_{0}-\delta/2,\theta_{0}+\delta/2]

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\frac{4|A_{0}|^{3}}{27}\frac{1}{(E+E_{0})^{2}}.

(4.68)

Next, we consider a suboptimal two-step strategy. Consider the input state of the form:

5.1. Foundations

In the previous chapters, I discussed the situation where the quantum channel depends on a single (scalar) unknown parameter we want to measure. On the other hand, many practical metrology problems are related to the estimation of multiple parameters simultaneously, such as vector field sensing (e.g., magnetic field)
Baumgratz & Datta 2016
, imaging
Tsang et al. 2016
, multiple-arm interferometry
Humphreys et al. 2013
;
Gessner et al. 2018
or waveform estimation
Tsang et al. 2011
;
Berry et al. 2013
,
which makes multi-parameter metrology an object of intensive interest in recent years
Matsumoto 2002
;
Kolenderski & Demkowicz-Dobrzanski 2008
;
Genoni et al. 2013
;
Ragy et al. 2016
;
Yuan 2016
;
Liu & Yuan 2017
;
Kura & Ueda 2018
;
Nichols et al. 2018
;
Ge et al. 2018
. Let me formulate the problem more concretely.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\cdot)
be the quantum channel depending on the vector of the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[\theta_{1},...,\theta_{p}]^{T}
, and so does the results of the measurement performed on the output state
p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\textrm{Tr}(M_{x}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

The aim is, therefore, to estimate the values
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, by probing
x
. We introduce the cost function
\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
which is to be minimized:

\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{x}\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x))p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(5.1)

which, for a typical problem, for the estimator indication close to the true value may be well approximated by the quadratic function:

\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\approx({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T}C({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(5.2)

where
C
is the Hessian of the cost function
C_{ij}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big|_{\tilde{\theta}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
(which, in general, may depend on the exact value of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
). Here
\partial_{i}
is the shortcut for
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}
. It is convenient to write down the average cost as:

\textrm{tr}(C\Sigma),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\Sigma:=\sum_{x}p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T},

(5.3)

where
\textrm{tr}(\cdot)
denote trace over
p
-dimensional space of parameters (I use the small letter
\textrm{tr}(\cdot)
to distinguish form the trace over physical Hilbert space
\textrm{Tr}(\cdot)
).
If the estimator is unbiased at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, i.e.
\sum_{x}p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)={\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of the
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Especially if the cost matrix is identity
C=\openone
, the cost is simply the sum o variances of the estimators
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}
.

5.2. Fisher information matrix and multiparameter Cramér-Rao bound

Let me start with the scenario where the experiment is repeated many times so that we may use Fisher information formalism.

Given probability distribution
p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. By locally unbiased estimator of the component
\theta_{i}
, we understand the one which does not only properly changes with arising
\theta_{i}
, but is also insensitive for changes of the remaining parameters
\theta_{i\neq j}
. For any estimator locally unbiased at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
:

\forall_{j}\sum_{x}p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\tilde{\theta}_{j}(x)=\theta_{j},\quad\forall_{j,i}\sum_{x}\frac{\partial p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\tilde{\theta}_{j}(x)=\delta_{ij}

(5.4)

the covariance matrix is bounded from below by multiparameter Cramér-Rao bound

\Sigma\geq{\mathbb{F}}^{-1},\quad{\mathbb{F}}_{ij}=\sum\frac{1}{p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\frac{\partial p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\frac{\partial p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{j}},

(5.5)

which may be derived by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (by matrix inequality, we understand that their difference is positive semidefinite
\Sigma-{\mathbb{F}}^{-1}\geq 0
). Note that diagonal matrix element
{\mathbb{F}}_{ii}
is single-parameter Fisher information for
\theta_{i}
. However, from
Eq. (
5.5
)
,
\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}\geq[{\mathbb{F}}^{-1}]_{ii}
(where in general
[{\mathbb{F}}^{-1}]_{ii}\geq({\mathbb{F}}_{ii})^{-1}
), as stronger locally unbiasedness condition has been imposed here
Eq. (
5.4
)
. Similarly, as in the single-parameter case, the Fisher information matrix scales linearly with the number of repetitions
k
, and in the limit of
k\to\infty
the corresponding classical Cramér-Rao inequality is saturated by maximum likelihood estimator
Nagaoka & Hayashi 2005
, CHAPTER 7
.

In quantum mechanics, the probability distribution is given by the Born rule
p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}M_{x})
. Here, I present the derivation of quantum Cramér-Rao bound, based on direct optimization over locally unbiased observable (based on observation from
Ragy et al. 2016
). The advantage of such derivation is that it clearly shows the origin of the measurement incompatibility problem. Moreover, it allows quick improvement to the stronger bounds
Holevo 1982
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
.

For compact notation, I will denote the vectors of the operators by properly bounded letters. For fixed
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
let me defined:

{\mathbb{X}}=\sum_{x}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})M_{x},

(5.6)

i.e.,
{\mathbb{X}}=[X_{1},...,X_{p}]^{T}
, where
X_{i}=\sum_{x}(\tilde{\theta}_{i}(x)-\theta_{i})M_{x}
. We construct a positive semidefinite operator:

0\leq\sum_{x}[(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\openone-{\mathbb{X}}]M_{x}[(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\openone-{\mathbb{X}}]^{T}=\sum_{x}M_{x}[(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})][(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})]^{T}-{\mathbb{X}}{\mathbb{X}}^{T}.

(5.7)

The first inequality is tight if
\{M_{x}\}
is a protective measurement. After applying the trace with density matrix
\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot)
we obtain:

\Sigma\geq\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}{\mathbb{X}}{\mathbb{X}}^{T})=:Z_{\theta}[{\mathbb{X}}]

(5.8)

Therefore, for any cost function
C
, the average cost is bounded by:

\textrm{tr}(C\Sigma)\geq\textrm{tr}(CZ_{\theta}[{\mathbb{X}}]).

(5.9)

RHS may be directly minimized over all vectors of hermitian matrices
{\mathbb{X}}
satisfying locally unbiased conditions
\textrm{Tr}(\partial_{i}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}X_{j})=\delta_{ij}
(we ignore for a while the fact that all
X_{i}
should come from the same measurement
\{M_{x}\}
). Minimization leads to
1
1

1

This may be shown by applying the standard Lagrange multipliers method
Ragy et al. 2016
. An elegant alternative approach, based on introducing a proper
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
-dependent scalar product on the space of operators, is presented in
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
, Section 2.6
.

{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}{\mathbb{L}}
, where
{\mathbb{L}}=[L_{1},...,L_{p}]^{T}
is a vector of symmetric logarithmic derivatives:

\partial_{i}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{i}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L_{i})

(5.10)

and
{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}
is the quantum Fisher information matrix given as:

{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}=\textrm{Re}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\mathbb{L}}{\mathbb{L}}^{T}).

(5.11)

Therefore, the cost is bounded from below:

\textrm{tr}(C\Sigma)\geq\textrm{tr}(C{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}).

(5.12)

What may be surprising, the
{\mathbb{X}}
minimizing

5.9
)
does not depend on peculiar matrix cost. As the inequality holds for any

, it may be written as matrix inequality:

\Sigma\geq{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}.

(5.13)

Let us discuss the saturability of the above inequality now.
I start with a specific case when the cost matrix
C
is rank-one. The problem may be understood that there is only one parameter to be measured

2

2

In general it may be some concrete linear combination of the parameters. However, the cost matrix may be always diagonalized by performing a proper rotation in parameter space (see a discussion in
Sec.
6.5
). In the new parametrization, this linear combination is indeed a single parameter.
, but the experimentalist needs to make sure that fluctuations of others do not affect the results (there are called nuisance parameters in this context
Suzuki 2020
;
Suzuki et al. 2020
). Then, if only
i^{\textrm{th}}
parameter is to be measured, directly from above construction, the optimal measurement will be a projection onto eigenvectors of

(which satifies
\forall_{j}\textrm{Tr}(\partial_{j}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}X_{i})=\delta_{ij}
) and one can immediately see that:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}X_{i}^{2})=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}{\mathbb{L}}]_{i}[{\mathbb{L}}^{T}{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}]_{i})=\left[{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\mathbb{L}}{\mathbb{L}}^{T}){\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}\right]_{ii}=[{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}]_{ii},

(5.14)

where in last step I used the fact that
[{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}\textrm{Im}(\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\mathbb{L}}{\mathbb{L}}^{T})){\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}]_{ii}=0
. This clearly shows that for the rank-one cost matrix, the QCR is always saturable with measurement performed on single copies of the system

3

3

This is a stronger version of the statement from
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
, Sec. 2.7
, where asymptotical saturability was shown via relation with Holevo CR bound (with assumption that we allow collective measurement on many copies of the system).
.

For general cost matrix
C
, the question about saturability comes down to the question if there exists a single protective measurement
\{M_{x}\}
, for which all

X_{i}=[{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}{\mathbb{L}}]_{i}
may be written as
Eq. (
5.6
)
. If so, the classical Fisher information for this measurement is equal to

, and in the many repetitions scenario, one may use an argument about the asymptotic saturability of classical CR.

Such measurement always exists if all SLDs mutually commute

\forall_{ij}[L_{i},L_{j}]=0,

(5.15)

then the such measurement is simply a projection onto a common eigenbasis of all
L_{i}
. That will be the case for most of the examples discussed in this thesis. However, for completeness, let me also discuss the opposite situation.

If
\exists_{ij}[L_{i},L_{j}]\neq 0
, the situation becomes to be more complicated; however, some improvement of
Eq. (
5.12
)
may be performed. Note that matrix

in
Eq. (
5.8
)
in general may be complex, while covariance matrix
\Sigma
is always purely real. As
Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[{\mathbb{X}}]
is hermitian, its imaginary part is antisymmetric. Therefore, after tracing both sides of

5.8
)
with a real positive cost matrix, we completely ignore the information hidden in
\textrm{Im}Z_{\theta}[{\mathbb{X}}]
, as
\textrm{tr}(C\textrm{Im}Z_{\theta}[{\mathbb{X}}])=0
. We can avoid this problem by, instead of minimizing RHS of

)
directly, performing double minimization:

\textrm{tr}(C\Sigma)\geq\min_{V,{\mathbb{X}}}\left[\textrm{tr}(CV):V\geq\textrm{tr}(\rho_{\theta}{\mathbb{X}}{\mathbb{X}}^{T})\right],

(5.16)

with
V
being symmetric real matrix. Moreover, the optimization over
V
for fixed
{\mathbb{X}}
may be performed analytically, giving so called Holevo Cramér-Rao (HCR) bound:

\textrm{tr}(C\Sigma)\geq\min_{{\mathbb{X}}}\left[\textrm{tr}(CZ[{\mathbb{X}}])+\|\sqrt{C}\textrm{Im}Z[{\mathbb{X}}]\sqrt{C}\|_{1}\right].

(5.17)

The last inequality has been proven to be asymptotically saturable for many copies of the system if collective measurements on all copies are allowed, i.e.,
p(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\textrm{Tr}(M_{x}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\otimes k})
(see
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
for both general proof and simple examples).

From that, we can see that if collective measurements are allowed, the condition for saturability

5.15
)
may be weakened – indeed, if SLDs do not commute in general, but commute on state, i.e.

\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}[L_{i},L_{j}])=0,

(5.18)

then for
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{F}}_{Q}^{-1}{\mathbb{L}}
, the operator
\textrm{Im}Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[{\mathbb{X}}]=0
, so Holevo-Cramer Rao coincidence with standard CR, which means that also the latter one is saturable. Therefore, condition
Eq. (
5.18
)
is a necessary and sufficient condition for asymptotically saturating standard multiparameter CR bound with collective measurement. Moreover, in
Matsumoto 2002
, it has been proven that collective measurements are necessary only for the general case of mixed states. In contrast, for the pure state, HCR may be saturated with standard local measurements on single copies of the system.

Adaptive scheme maximizing QFI

In the single parameter case, it was shown for the unitary estimation that the optimal adaptive scheme is a sequential one acting of the gates
n
times, with no necessity of unitary control between (see
Sec.
3.5
). Moreover, it offers no advantage over the optimal parallel scheme.

However, this no longer applies to multiparameter estimation, where the optimal unitary control is the one that reverses evolution after each step. Moreover, in some cases, it may overcome the optimal parallel scheme
Yuan 2016
. The reason for the difference is that in the multiparameter case, the gates with different values of parameter do not necessarily commute
[U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}},U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]\neq 0
, which makes the reversing evolution between each gates something diametrically different from reversing it at the end of the whole procedure. The exemplary problem illustrating this effect will be discussed in
Sec.
6.4
.

The fact, that sequential-adaptive strategy where unitary controls simply reverse the evolution is the best of all those using
N
gates, was proven in
Yuan 2016
only for a specific case of estimation of the magnetic field sensed by spin-
1/2
particle; the proof was based on the connection of QFI with Bures metric. Below I present an alternative and fully general proof of this statement (which, best to my knowledge, does not exists in literature). Note, that since any entangled-parallel strategy may be seen as a specific adaptive-sequential one (as discussed in
Sec.
2.5
), in the proof of optimality I may restrict only to the adaptive-sequential ones.

It should be emphasized that the following reasoning is based only on comparing QFI matrices, so it does not consider the potential incompatibility of the measurements. The question of whether an analogous protocol optimizes the HCR inequality has not been investigated.

Theorem 3.
In the multiparameter unitary estimation problem with evolution
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
at point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}
,
for any sequential-adaptive strategy
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle=V_{n}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)...V_{1}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)|\psi\rangle
, there exists an alternative strategy with
\forall_{i}V_{i}=U^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}\otimes\openone
and input state
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
, where an ancillary system of the same size as the original one,
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{S})=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, for which the QFI matrix is bigger or equal to the QFI for the first strategy:

\exists_{|\psi^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}}\quad{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}\Big[(U^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)^{\otimes n}|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)\Big]\geq{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}\Big[V_{n}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)...V_{1}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)|\psi\rangle)\Big].

(5.19)

Proof.
For any adaptive strategy, the output state after
n

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=V_{n}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)V_{n-1}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)...V_{1}(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)\rho(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)^{\dagger}V_{1}^{\dagger}...(U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\otimes\openone)^{\dagger}V_{n}^{\dagger}

(5.20)

and its QFI matrix is:

{\mathbb{F}}_{Q}=\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}{\mathbb{L}}{\mathbb{L}}^{T}),\quad\partial_{j}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}L_{j}+L_{j}\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}).

(5.21)

I introduce
W_{i}=V_{i}(U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}\otimes\openone)
(so
V_{i}=W_{i}(U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}\otimes\openone)^{\dagger}
). Then:

\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Big|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}=W_{n}W_{n-1}...W_{1}\rho W_{1}^{\dagger}...W_{n}^{\dagger}.

(5.22)

while its derivatives may be written in the form of the sum of terms when the derivative "hit"
i^{th}
acting of the channel:

\partial_{j}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Big|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{j}W_{n}...W_{i}U^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho^{(i)}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}W^{\dagger}_{i}...W^{\dagger}_{n}\Big|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}},\quad\textrm{with}\quad\rho^{(i)}:=W_{i-1}W_{i-2}...W_{1}\rho W_{1}^{\dagger}...W_{i-1}^{\dagger}.

(5.23)

We define

\partial_{j}W_{n}...W_{i}U^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rho^{(i)}U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\dagger}U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}W^{\dagger}_{i}...W^{\dagger}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{j}^{(i)}\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}+\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}L_{j}^{(i)})

(5.24)

(5.25)

(5.27)

U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\otimes n}\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}|i\rangle^{\otimes n}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{in\sum_{k}\theta_{k}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}}c_{i}|i\rangle^{\otimes n}.

(5.38)

General theorem for noisy multiparameter estimation within QFI formalism

The “Hamiltonian-not-in-Kraus-span” (HNKS) condition

3.60
)
may be easily generalized for a multiparameter case. It was first done for the problem of continuous evolution given by master Lindblad equation

, but methodology may be easily adapted to finite-step gates:

{\mathcal{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\rho)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}K_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\rho K_{k}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(5.39)

For each parameter we define corresponding Hamiltonian
\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}
and extended Kraus-span
\mathcal{S}_{i}
(where compered to
\mathcal{S}
, it additionally contain all remaining Hamiltonian
\tilde{\Lambda}_{j\neq i}
):

\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}=-i\sum_{k}K_{k}^{\dagger}\partial_{i}K_{k},\quad\mathcal{S}_{i}=\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{H}}\{\tilde{\Lambda}_{j\neq i},K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{k}\,\textrm{for all},\ j,k\},

(5.40)

where
\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{H}}\{\cdot\}
represents all Hermitian operators that are linear combinations of operators in
\{\cdot\}
.
Then Heisenberg scaling in estimation off all

parameters is possible iff
\forall_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}\notin\mathcal{S}_{i}
. When this condition is satisfied, each parameter may be measured separately by constructing quantum error correction protocol as in single-parameter case
Zhou & Jiang 2021
, where remaining Hamiltonians
\tilde{\Lambda}_{j\neq i}
are treated as the noise we want to protect again. Otherwise, if the condition is violated, it means that there exists some linear combinations of parameter
\theta_{i}
, which cannot be estimated with HS precision.

While if and only if conditions for obtaining HS are known, the general asymptotically saturable bound is not known exactly. The methodology used to derive the saturable bounds in single-parameter estimation, presented in
Sec.
3.7
, cannot be trivially extended for multiparameter case, mainly due to the difficulties connected with inversing QFI matrix and probe incompatibility. See
Albarelli & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
for recent progress in this direction.

5.3. Bayesian and minimax approaches

Similarly, as in the single parameter case, we may formulate formulas for Bayesian cost:

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\int p({\boldsymbol{\theta}})d{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\int d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\mathcal{C}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

and and minimax cost:

(5.42)

Again, if

, the minimax cost bigger than Bayesian one:

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\geq\overline{\mathcal{C}}.

(5.43)

In the limit of many copies

, if collective measurement is allowed, in general, Bayesian and minimax formulas may be related to Holevo Cramér-Rao bound (so also with standard Cramér-Rao bound if
\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[L_{i},L_{j}])=0
), however, the formal reasoning is much more complicated and proven fully formally only for specific cases

;
Gill 2008
;
Guţă & Kahn 2006
;
Guţă et al. 2008

Covariant problem

In this section, we recall the theorem introduced and proven in
Chiribella et al. 2008a
, saying that for the covariant problem of the group element estimation, the optimal strategy may be found as the parallel covariant one, i.e., there is no advantage in considering a more general sequential adaptive scheme. The proof from
Chiribella et al. 2008a
is based on the formalism of quantum combs introduced in
Chiribella et al. 2008
. For completeness, I recall here the proof, restricting mathematical formalism only to what is strictly necessary.

Consider a problem where the family of channels depending on the parameter is a unitary representation of a compact Lie group
G
, such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{g}(\rho)=U_{g}\rho U^{\dagger}_{g}
and the aim is to estimate the group element
g\in G
. Then we say, that the estimation problem is covariant if it satisfies the conditions stated in
Sec.
4.2
, namely:

•

the cost function is invariant under the action of the group
\mathcal{C}(g_{1},g_{2})=\mathcal{C}(hg_{1},hg_{2})
.

•

(required in Bayesian formalism) a priori distribution is invariant under the action of the group
p(g)dg=p(hg)d(hg)
. For simplicity of the notation, I assume that
dg
is the normalized Haar measure,
\int_{G}dg=1
.

We can now formulate the stronger version of
Eq. (
4.11
)
.

Theorem 4.
For the covariant group estimation problem, as the optimal strategy using
N
gates one may always choose the parallel one with covariant measurement, i.e. the one of the form:

M_{g}=({U_{\tilde{g}}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone)M_{e}({U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone),\quad\int d\tilde{g}({U_{\tilde{g}}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone)M_{e}({U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone)=\openone.

(5.44)

It cannot be overcome even by the most general sequential-adaptive scheme, namely:

\begin{cases}\inf_{\rho,\{V_{i}\},M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\inf_{\rho,\{V_{i}\},M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\sup_{g\in G}\int d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{g}^{N}M_{\tilde{g}})\mathcal{C}(g,\tilde{g})\\
\inf_{\rho,\{V_{i}\},M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\inf_{\rho,\{V_{i}\},M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\int d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{g}^{N}M_{\tilde{g}})\mathcal{C}(g,\tilde{g})\end{cases}=\\
\inf_{\rho\in\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{S}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}),M_{e}}\int d\tilde{g}\textrm{Tr}(\rho({U^{\dagger}_{\tilde{g}}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone)M_{e}(U_{\tilde{g}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\openone))\mathcal{C}(e,\tilde{g}),

(5.45)

where in LHS
\rho^{N}_{g}=V_{N}\circ(U_{g}\otimes\openone)\circ...V_{1}\circ(U_{g}\otimes\openone)\circ\rho
.

Proof.
Consider an arbitrary quantum channel:

{\mathcal{E}}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{in}})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{out}}),

(5.46)

where in principle
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{in}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{out}}
may correspond to the same physical system, yet in this formalism, they will be treated as separated Hilbert spaces.
From Choi-Jemiołkowski isomorphism, the channel is uniquely defined by its Choi matrix:

E=\textrm{Choi}({\mathcal{E}}):={\mathcal{E}}\otimes\openone(|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\Omega|)

(5.47)

where
|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle=\sum|i\rangle|i\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{{\textrm{in}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\textrm{in}}}
. Indeed, by direct calculation, one may see:

\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{in}}(E(\openone\otimes\rho^{T}))=\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{in}}(\sum_{ij}{\mathcal{E}}(|i\rangle\langle j|)\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|\rho^{T})=\sum_{ij}{\mathcal{E}}(|i\rangle\langle j|)\rho_{ji}^{T}={\mathcal{E}}(\rho).

(5.48)

Moreover, to introduce consistent notation, also density matrix
\rho
itself as well as each POVM element
M_{i}
will be seen as quantum channels, respectively:

\begin{split}&\rho:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{in}}:1\mapsto\rho,\\
&M_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{out}}\to\mathbb{C}:\rho\mapsto\textrm{Tr}(M_{i}\rho).\end{split}

(5.49)

Especially, Choi matrix of
\rho
is simply
\rho
, while Choi matrix of
M_{k}
is its transposition, as

M_{k}\otimes\openone(|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\Omega|)=\sum_{ij}\textrm{Tr}(M_{k}|i\rangle\langle j|)|i\rangle\langle j|=M^{T}.

(5.50)

By
{V^{|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle}}
I will denote the Choi matrix of the unitary channel
V
, i.e.
{V^{|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle}}=(V\otimes\openone)|\Omega\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\Omega|(V^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)
.

Next, let us derive the formula for the Choi matrix of the composition of the channels. Consider now two channels, for which the output of the first one is the input of the second:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{a})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{b}),\\
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{b})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{c}).\end{split}

(5.51)

By direct calculation, we derive the formula for the Choi matrix of their composition:

{\mathcal{E}}_{2}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}(\rho))=\textrm{Tr}_{b}(E_{2}(\openone_{c}\otimes[\textrm{Tr}_{a}(E_{1}(\openone_{b}\otimes\rho^{T}))]^{T}))=\\
\textrm{Tr}_{a}\left(\textrm{Tr}_{b}\left((E_{2}\otimes\openone_{a})\left[(\openone_{c}\otimes E_{1})(\openone_{c}\otimes\openone_{b}\otimes\rho^{T})\right]^{T}\right)\right)=\\
\textrm{Tr}_{a}\left(\underbrace{\textrm{Tr}_{b}\left((E_{2}\otimes\openone_{a})(\openone_{c}\otimes E_{1}^{T_{b}})\right)}_{E_{2,1}}(\openone_{c}\otimes\rho^{T})\right)

(5.52)

where in the last step, we used the fact that trace over subspace
\textrm{Tr}_{a}
is invariant for partial transposition on this subspace, so
\textrm{Tr}_{a}([E_{1}(\openone_{b}\otimes\rho^{T})]^{T})=\textrm{Tr}_{a}([[E_{1}(\openone_{b}\otimes\rho^{T})]^{T}]^{T_{a}})=\textrm{Tr}_{a}([E_{1}(\openone_{a}\otimes\rho^{T})]^{T_{b}})=\textrm{Tr}_{a}([E_{1}^{T_{b}}(\openone_{a}\otimes\rho^{T})])
.

We can go further: consider the channels with an arbitrary number of input and output Hilberts spaces, with the assumption that some of the output of
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
is the input of
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
, for example:

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{b})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{c}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{f})

(5.53)

{\mathcal{E}}_{2}:\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{f}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{g})\to\textrm{Lin}(\mathcal{H}_{h})

(5.54)

Then Choi matrix of their composition
(\openone_{c,d}\otimes{\mathcal{E}}_{2})\circ({\mathcal{E}}_{1}\otimes\openone_{g})
is given as:

E_{2,1}=\textrm{Tr}_{f}((\openone_{c,d}\otimes E_{2})(E_{1}\otimes\openone_{g})^{T_{f}}).

(5.55)

which we call link product
E_{2}*E_{1}
. Note that, as partial trace
\textrm{Tr}_{f}(\cdot)
is invariant for partial transposition
\cdot^{T_{f}}
, the link product is commutative
E_{2}*E_{1}=E_{1}*E_{2}
.

Note that if two channels do not have any common input-output, the Choi matrix of their composition is simply a tensor product of their Choi matrices
E_{1}*E_{2}=E_{1}\otimes E_{2}
.

We are now ready to use this formalism to describe the adaptive scheme

CHAPTER 6Heisenberg limit in multiparameter metrology

6.1. Introduction

In this chapter, I will discuss the problem of the Heisenberg limit in multiparameter metrology, presenting the results from
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
;
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022a
. I will focus mainly on two aspects.

The first one is the potential advantage of estimating
p
parameters jointly, when compared to estimating them separately, spending in total the same amount of resources (i.e., usage of quantum gates). The problem has been extensively discussed in the literature (mainly within QFI formalism). However, there were some doubts about the interpretation of the results
1
1

1

An alternative approach to quantifying the incompatibility between parameters without discussion the problem of diving resources, has been proposed in
Ragy et al. 2016
;
Albarelli & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
. However, it does not answer the problems I refer to, so it will not be discussed broader in this thesis.
.

The central ambiguity concerns the issue of resource allocation between parameters when they are measured separately. It has been assumed in many works that, having

Humphreys et al. 2013
;
Yuan 2016
;
Baumgratz & Datta 2016
;
Ying-Wang et al. 2018
;
Goldberg et al. 2020

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}\propto p\times\frac{1}{k}\times\frac{1}{(n/p)^{2}}=\frac{p^{3}}{kn^{2}}.

(6.1)

Based on these assumptions, a significant advantage of the strategy, where the parameter are measured jointly, was postulated (typically of order
\sim p
).

However, as QCR bound is guaranteed to be saturable only in the limit
k\to\infty
, so to use this formalism, we need to assume large

. Therefore it is much more efficient to divide the number of repetition
k
between parameters, keeping

Chen & Yuan 2019

(6.2)

which leads to
p

The highly nontrivial question is how the situation looks when only the total amount of resources

is restricted. Then, due to quadratic scaling with
n
and linear with
k

– but this cannot be fully analyzed within QFI formalism. In this chapter, I will discuss it in the minimax approach.

The second aspect I want to analyze is the advantage of a sequential adaptive scheme over a parallel one – again, I will compare the results obtained in both paradigms.

6.2. Asymptotic equivalence of the global and local minimax cost

I will compare the minimal cost obtainable within two paradigms. In the first one, all
N

n
gates may be used in a single trial, while the experiment is repeated a large number of times

(which may be effectively described within QFI formalism).

While the first one demands defining a finite-sized set, in which the procedure is designed to work well
\Theta
, the second is strictly focused on a single point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}
. To make a reasonable comparison, in a single parameter case in
Ch.
4

and I have written the exact bounds for finite region size

Here I use an analogous idea, but I will not investigate the rate of convergence. Instead, I focus only on asymptotic results. A simple approach, which captures the essential problem, has been proposed in
Hayashi 2011
(originally for single parameter case), so-called local asymptotic minimax cost. Let us define
\delta-

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}
as
\Theta({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},\delta)=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\forall_{i}\theta_{i}\in[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0i}-\delta/2,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0i}+\delta/2]\}

N
unitary controls and measurement), for each possible

(\rho^{N},\{V_{i}^{N}\},\{M^{N}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\})

\inf_{\{(\rho^{N},\{V_{i}^{N}\},\{M^{N}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\})\}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},\delta)}\int d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textrm{Tr}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{N}M_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{N})\textrm{tr}(C_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T}),

Now, I will generalize the reasoning from

Theorem 5.

{\mathcal{E}}_{g}(\rho)=U_{g}\rho U_{g}^{\dagger}

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\forall_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}\inf_{\{(\rho^{N},\{V_{i}^{N}\},\{M^{N}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\})\}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},\delta)}\int d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textrm{Tr}(M^{N}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\rho^{N}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\textrm{tr}(C_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T})\\

(6.4)

Proof.

We start with the observation that the asymptotic minimax cost for

\inf_{\{(\rho^{N},\{V_{i}^{N}\},\{M^{N}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\})\}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\sup_{g\in G_{\delta}}\int d\tilde{g}\textrm{Tr}(M_{N,\tilde{g}}\rho_{N,g})\mathcal{C}(g,\tilde{g})\\

The first example I would like to discuss is the multiarm interferometer,

6.5. General bound for precision in multiparameter unitary evolution

The problem of the multiparameter estimation with optimal usage of all
N

Bagan et al. 2000
;
Bagan et al. 2001

;
Chiribella et al. 2005
;
Hayashi 2006

Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022

Eq. (
4.52
)

Consider a unitary channel dependent on
p
parameters, which enter linearly via generators
\Lambda_{i}
, such that
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=e^{i\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\Lambda_{i}}
. First, I want to justify that if one is interested in estimating only one of these parameters
\theta_{i}
, the existence of another
\theta_{j}
may only make the task more difficult and never easier. Namely, even in the presence of
other generators, the bound
Eq. (
4.52
)
is still valid (but not necessarily saturable). For more compact notation, let me name by
\lambda[\Lambda]
the difference of maximal and minimal eigenvalues of operator
\Lambda
. Let me remind the bound
Eq. (
4.52
)
in this notation:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}[\Lambda]N^{2}}\left(1-\frac{8\log(N\lambda[\Lambda]\delta)}{N\lambda[\Lambda]\delta}\right).

(6.41)

Next, note that a single gate may be arbitrarily well approximated as:

U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=e^{i\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\Lambda_{i}}\overset{l\gg 1}{\approx}\left(e^{i\theta_{i}\frac{\Lambda_{i}}{l}}e^{i\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}\frac{\Lambda_{j}}{l}}\right)^{l}

(6.42)

where from Trotter formula
Cohen et al. 1982
the approximation is exact in the limit
l\to\infty
(even if
[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{j}]\neq 0
). Therefore
N
actions of the unitary
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
may be seen as
l\cdot N
action of
U_{\theta_{i}}=e^{i\theta_{i}\frac{\Lambda_{i}}{l}}
, with unitary controls
V=e^{i\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j}\frac{\Lambda_{j}}{l}}
in between. As a result, the product
\lambda[\Lambda_{i}/l]\cdot(lN)=\lambda[\Lambda_{i}]N
remains unchanged, so does the bound
Eq. (
6.41
)
(including the range of convergence). Therefore, for each parameter;

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}}_{i}\gtrsim\frac{\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}[\Lambda_{i}]}.

(6.43)

To make use of the above bound in the multiparameter case, let me discuss the concept of re-parametrization.

For given unitary channel
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=e^{i{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\mathbb{\Lambda}}
with cost matrix
\textrm{tr}(C(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T})
, for real invertible
p\times p
matrix
A
, we may perform the transformation of parameters, generators and cost matrix:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}=A^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad\mathbb{\Lambda}^{\prime}=A^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda},\quad C^{\prime}=A^{T}CA,

(6.44)

such that estimation problem remain unchanged:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbb{\Lambda}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\mathbb{\Lambda},\quad\textrm{tr}(C^{\prime}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})^{T})=\textrm{tr}(C(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T}).

(6.45)

Therefore, even if the problem is initially formulated with
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
and arbitrary cost matrix
C^{\prime}
, we may always reduce it to minimize the sum of variance by choosing
A=\sqrt{C}
, such that for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
the cost is simply
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}
.

This allows us the bound from below the minimal achievable cost by assuming the most optimistic scenario, i.e., not only do the other parameters not interfere but there is a single initial state and a single optimum measurement for all parameters at the same time:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\gtrsim\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}[\Lambda_{i}]}.

(6.46)

However, the parametrization for which the cost matrix is identity is not unique. Therefore, the bound may be potentially improved by optimizing it over orthogonal transformation
O

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\max_{O}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}[[O^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}]_{i}]}.

(6.47)

Note that restricting to orthogonal transformations is crucial, as otherwise, we get cost matrix
C
with non-zero of diagonal elements, and then
\textrm{tr}(C(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{T})
cannot be bounded by the sum of
Eq. (
6.43
)
.

For the problems discussed in
Sec.
6.3
,
Sec.
6.4

In multiple-phase case, by direct minimization over

for

6.6. Magnetic field in two spatially separated points

At last, I would like to present a simple example for which the bound
Eq. (
6.47
)
will be tight, and also re-parametrization will play an important role. Consider a magnetic field oriented in
z

|i,\pm\rangle
, where coordinate
i=1,2
is the spacial one. We have:

\theta_{1}\Lambda_{1}+\theta_{2}\Lambda_{2}=\theta_{1}|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes\sigma_{z}+\theta_{2}|2\rangle\langle 2|\otimes\sigma_{z},

(6.48)

so
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
, what leads to the bound
\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\gtrsim\frac{2\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}
.

To make the bound tighter, let us consider reparametrization:

\theta_{1}^{\prime}=(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/\sqrt{2},\theta_{2}^{\prime}=(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})/\sqrt{2}
, for which:

\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&-\theta_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&\theta_{2}&0\\
0&0&0&-\theta_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\theta^{\prime}_{1}+\theta^{\prime}_{2})/\sqrt{2}&0&0&0\\
0&-(\theta^{\prime}_{1}+\theta^{\prime}_{2})/\sqrt{2}&0&0\\
0&0&(\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2})/\sqrt{2}&0\\
0&0&0&-(\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2})/\sqrt{2},\end{bmatrix}

(6.49)

so
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
which gives
\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\gtrsim\frac{4\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}
. Moreover, this bound turns out to be achievable.

To see that, let as reorder the eigenbasis to be
\{|1,+\rangle,|2,-\rangle,|2,+\rangle,|1-\rangle\}
. Then the matrix
Eq. (
6.49
)
takes the specific form:

\theta^{\prime}_{1}\Lambda^{\prime}_{1}+\theta^{\prime}_{2}\Lambda^{\prime}_{2}=\tilde{\theta}_{1}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\openone\otimes\sigma_{z}+\tilde{\theta}_{2}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{z}\otimes\openone

(6.50)

Then, by renaming
\{|1,+\rangle,|2,-\rangle,|2,+\rangle,|1-\rangle\}\to\{|+,+\rangle,|+,-\rangle,|-,+\rangle,|-,-\rangle\}
, on the mathematical level we can identify the Hilbert space of single particle with a 2-dimensional spacial degree of freedom with Hilbert space of 2 independent spin-1/2 particles (with no spacial degree of freedom).

It makes it clear that, therefore,
\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}
may be simultaneously measured optimally with the same input state without interfering with each other. More precisely, we can write the Hilbert space as

\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2}
, so for
N

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}=(\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2})^{\otimes N}=(\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2})^{\otimes N}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{\otimes 2})^{\otimes N}
. Therefore the state of the form
|\textrm{sin}\rangle\otimes|\textrm{sin}\rangle
(where
|\textrm{sin}\rangle
is the one of the form
Eq. (
4.20
)
) with covariant measurement will saturate the bound:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\simeq\frac{4\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}.

(6.51)

We see that such a strategy offers the advantage over the one where
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
are measured separately, which leads to:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{SEP}}\simeq 2\times\frac{\pi^{2}}{(N/2)^{2}}=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}.

(6.52)

An interesting observation may be concluded if one looks at the problem from a many-repetition scenario perspective. Then for joint strategy, by the same reasoning, one obtains

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\textrm{CR}}_{\textrm{JNT}}\simeq\frac{4}{kn^{2}}.

(6.53)

However, for measuring parameters separately:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\textrm{CR}}_{\textrm{SEP}}\simeq 2\times\frac{1}{k/2}\times\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{4}{kn^{2}},

(6.54)

so, in this case, there is no advantage to applying a more advanced joint strategy! Unlike in previously discussed examples, we observe qualitative differences about existing advantages depending on the paradigm.

For the number of spatial points
p
bigger than
2
, the above construction cannot be performed, as, simply speaking, we lose the identity
2p=2^{p}
. The broad discussion about the case

may be found in
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
.

One more feature should be mentioned here. In all discussed examples in the separate strategy, we measured the initially introduced parameters. Especially in the last example, we were focused on measuring
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
, while in principle, one could consider as a separate strategy measuring independently
\theta^{\prime}_{1},\theta^{\prime}_{2}
, or, in general, any linear combinations of them. From a practical point of view, typically, some parametrization is more natural, and in trying to measure their linear combination, we simply lose the simplicity, which is the crucial motivation for a separate strategy (here: restricting the measurements local in space). Still, it is a reasonable comment from a general point of view. However, it turns out that for all discussed examples, this "natural" parametrization was indeed the optimal one for measuring parameters separately, as shown in
App.
D
.

6.7. Summary

The analysis of the examples shows that, in contrast to the estimation of a single unitary parameter, in the multiparameter case, there is no clear and unambiguous correspondence between the results obtained in the many repetitions paradigm (analyzed within CR formalism) and those corresponding to the optimal use of all resources (analyzed using minimax approach (MM)). I analyzed in detail two issues

•

superiority of sequential-adaptive scheme over the entangled-parallel one.

•

the advantage of measuring all parameters jointly, compared to the strategy, where they are measured separately.

In the first case, the existence of this superiority is strictly related to the commutativity of evolution generators, which is consistence with theorems from
Ch.
5
. If all generators mutually commute, the optimal results may be obtained within a parallel scheme (in both paradigms). However, in non-commuting cases, in CR adaptive scheme may lead to significantly better results, while in MM a parallel scheme is asymptotically sufficient to obtain the optimal result (which I illustrated by the problem of estimation of the three components of the magnetic field).

On the other hand, the divergence of the conclusions obtained in the two paradigms regarding the gain from measuring the parameters jointly is not related to commutativity. I have shown an example of estimating one component of the magnetic field in two spatially separated points (so generators mutually commute), where CR postulates do advantage from measuring parameters jointly, while the MM approach allows for decreasing cost by a factor of
2
.

In general, the conclusions about the usefulness of carrying out a joint measurement made solely with CR may be overly pessimistic. For optimal use of all resources (MM), the advantage may exist for models that do not exist in a many repetitions scenario (CR).

CHAPTER 7Conclusion and outlook

For single-parameter unitary noiseless estimation, I have shown that the fundamental bound on the accuracy of the estimation with a limited number of the resources
N
is
\pi
times larger than would result from naive use of the error propagation formula or exploiting the quantum Fisher information (without considering multiple repetitions). The result considers any evolution generator (including one with a continuous spectrum) and remains valid for arbitrary entangled input states and a more general sequential adaptive scheme. The bound is asymptotically saturable. The rate of convergence depends on the ratio between the generator’s spectral width and the size of the region of the parameter’s occurrence. The superiority of the adaptive sequential scheme over the parallel one asymptotically disappears with increasing
N
. An analogous analysis of converging to the fundamental bound was performed (only for the parallel strategy) when only the average number of resources is limited (in this case, I am not discussing an adaptive strategy).
In both cases, in the limit of
N\to\infty
, up to the leading order, the problems can be equated with estimating a completely unknown phase shift in a two-arm interferometer.

The analysis of the analogous problem in the presence of noise remains an open question. On the one hand, the QFI formalism offers extensive quantum error-correction tools and protocols, offering hope for recovering Heisenberg scaling. Unfortunately, their successful implementation requires almost exact knowledge of the parameter value from the very beginning. An actual implementation requires sequential tweaking of the circuit settings, considering the knowledge gained so far. There has been no thorough analysis of whether this would allow the MSE to keep scaling as
1/N^{2}
.

In the multi-parameter case, I compared optimal metrological protocols for cases when the experiment is repeated many times (analyzed within CR formalism) or when all available resources can be accumulated together (analyzed within MM formalism). In the case of the multi-arm interferometer, the results in both cases are similar. However, in the case of the estimation of the three components of the magnetic field, the results are qualitatively different – CR predicts an asymptotic lack of benefit from measuring parameters jointly in a parallel scheme (however, it postulates a substantial advantage of the sequential adaptive scheme). In contrast, in the MM analysis, a substantial gain is already apparent for the parallel scheme (which cannot be improved by adaptiveness).
It is known that the divergence in the utility of adaptivity in the two paradigms occurs only when the evolution generators do not commute. However, further analysis shows that the divergence in the benefit of measuring the parameters jointly (versus measuring them separately) is unrelated to the commuting of the generators.
I also derived a simple (not necessarily saturated) lower bound on the achievable MSE. The problem of deriving a general saturable bound for multi-parameter metrology (even without noise) remains open.

(which is in practice true limitation, given by photons counters abilities). In a small neighborhood of

(A.13)

Therefore, the measure of quantum advantage is the value of the nominator in the above equation. In

for squeezing rate

(
10\textrm{dB}
) and detection efficiency
\eta=0.62
decreasing the MSE by a factor
0.47
(

) has been observed, in accordance with the above formula.

In
Aasi et al. 2013
for squeezing rate
e^{-2r}=0.93
(
10.3\textrm{dB}

\eta=0.44
was obtained, which, according to the above formula, should lead to decreasing MSE by

(

Appendix D
Broader discussion – reparametrization

Here I recall the reasoning from
Górecki & Demkowicz-Dobrzanński 2022
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[\theta_{1},...,\theta_{p}]^{T}
be the parametrization for which the cost matrix is identity
C=\openone
. Consider the extended procedure of measuring parameters separately with usage totally
N
gates,
\textrm{SEP}+
. Namely:

•

first apply the linear transformation
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}=A^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

•

then estimate each of
\theta_{i}^{\prime}
, using
N_{i}
gates, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{p}N_{i}=N
.

From resulting
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
reconstruct
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=A\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
. We want to minimze
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}
over transformation
A
, resources’ distribution
\sum_{i=1}^{p}N_{i}=N
and single sequential-adaptive protocol for each
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{\prime}
. I will derive a simple lower bound for the final precision obtainable in such a procedure.

Note that if all parameters
\theta_{i}
are measured separately with the usage of different probes, the resulting covariance matrix will be diagonal (as indications of different estimators come from uncorrelated measurements results):

\Sigma^{\prime}=\textrm{diag}(\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{1}^{\prime},...,\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{p}^{\prime}).

(D.1)

Therefore, the final cost, which is to be minimized may be written as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}=\textrm{Tr}(\Sigma)=\textrm{Tr}(AA^{T}\Sigma^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}[AA^{T}]_{ii}\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{i}.

(D.2)

Using
Eq. (
6.43
)
we may write:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{SEP+}}\gtrsim\min_{A,N_{j}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\pi^{2}}{N_{j}^{2}}\frac{[A^{T}A]_{jj}}{\lambda^{2}([A^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}]_{j})}\geq\min_{A,N_{j}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\pi^{2}}{N_{j}^{2}}\left(\min_{i}\frac{[A^{T}A]_{ii}}{\lambda^{2}([A^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}]_{i})}\right)=\frac{p^{3}\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}\min_{A,i}\frac{[A^{T}A]_{ii}}{\lambda^{2}([A^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}]_{i})}.

(D.3)

For any fixed
i
, RHS depends on on
i^{th}
column of
A
, the minimization is equivalent to minimization over single vector
\boldsymbol{a}
:

\min_{A,i}\frac{[A^{T}A]_{ii}}{\lambda^{2}([A^{T}\mathbb{\Lambda}]_{i})}=\min_{\boldsymbol{a}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}}{\lambda^{2}[\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{\Lambda}]}=\min_{\boldsymbol{a}:|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}=1}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}[\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{\Lambda}]}=\frac{1}{\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}:|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}=1}\lambda^{2}[\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{\Lambda}]},

(D.4)

so we get:

\widehat{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textrm{SEP+}}\gtrsim\frac{p^{3}\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}\frac{1}{\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}:|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}=1}\lambda^{2}[\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{\Lambda}]}.

(D.5)

Similarly, in the many repetitions scenario:

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\textrm{CR}}_{\textrm{SEP+}}\gtrsim\frac{p^{2}}{kn^{2}}\frac{1}{\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}:|\boldsymbol{a}|^{2}=1}\lambda^{2}[\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{\Lambda}]}.

(D.6)

After applying the examples discussed in
Ch.
6
, it shows, that the parametrization, which was used, was indeed the one minimizing the cost obtainable in a separate strategy.
