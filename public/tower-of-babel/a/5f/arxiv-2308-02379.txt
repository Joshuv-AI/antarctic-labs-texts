Abstract
We algorithmically determine the monodromy of the local system on the smooth part of the Radon transformation of a generic simple perverse sheaf on the projective plane.



Introduction

The classical Radon transform is an integral transformation which sends
a suitably integrable
function
f
on the projective plane
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

to the function

\mathcal{R}(f)\,:\,\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R},\,\,\,\widetilde{x}\mapsto\int_{L_{\widetilde{x}}}f\,,

on the dual plane
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),
where
L_{\widetilde{x}}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the line corresponding to an element
\widetilde{x}
in the dual plane
[
14
]
.

The algebraic-geometric version of the classical Radon transformation
was introduced by Brylinsky
[
6
]
.
Following Grothendieck’s fonctions-faisceaux philosophy (cf.
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
),
the basic idea is to replace functions by objects in the derived category

D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
and integration of functions by direct image functors between derived categories.
This leads to a functor (the
Radon transform
)

\mathcal{R}:D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}D^{b}_{c}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}),\,\,K\mapsto Rp_{2*}p_{1}^{*}(K)[1],

where

p_{1},p_{2}
are the two canonical projections of the incidence subvariety
H\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}

(see
[
6
]
and Sec.
1
for the formal definition).

Recall that one has the abelian category of
perverse sheaves
{\rm Perv}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
inside
D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
and that, using perverse cohomology, any object in
D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
can be described
by perverse sheaves (
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
).
By Radon inversion (see Section
1
and
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
),
Brylinsky’s Radon transform sends simple perverse sheaves to simple
perverse sheaves, up to constant sheaves in
D^{b}_{c}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}).
It is this result
which makes the Radon transform useful in many applications.

Generically, a simple perverse sheaf
K
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
is an intermediate extension
j_{!*}{\mathcal{L}}[2]
of an irreducible non-constant local system
{\mathcal{L}}
(shifted to cohomological degree
-2
)
along an open inclusion
j:Y\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
with
Y
a plane curve complement

Y={\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C.

Let
\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus\widetilde{C}
be the complement of the union
\widetilde{C}

of the dual curve of
C
and the lines in the dual plane, which are determined by the
singularities of
C.
Then the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
is locally constant
on
\widetilde{Y}
(Prop.
4.1
). We call the local system
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}):=\mathcal{R}(K)[-2]|_{\widetilde{Y}}
the
Radon transform
of
{\mathcal{L}}.

Under some additional assumptions which are often fulfilled in the applications, the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
is the intermediate extension

\mathcal{R}(K)=\widetilde{j}_{!*}\left(\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})[2]\right)

of
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})[2]
along
\widetilde{j}:\widetilde{Y}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}.
Hence
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
determines
\mathcal{R}(K)
in these cases by the uniqueness of
the intermediate extension.

The local systems

{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
are determined in turn by their monodromy representations

\rho_{\mathcal{L}}:\pi_{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\GL(V)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\rho_{\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})}:\pi_{1}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus\widetilde{C})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\GL(\widetilde{V}).

It is the aim of this article, to
determine the monodromy representation of the
Radon transform
{\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})}
in an algorithmic way
from the fundamental
data (= monodromy tuple of
{\mathcal{L}}
together with the braid monodromy of
C
), used to describe the local system
{\mathcal{L}},
see Thm.
4.6
.

The main idea is to interprete the Radon transform

\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
as a variation of parabolic cohomology groups over
\widetilde{Y}
in the sense of
[
7
]

and to use the algorithmic approach for such variations which is introduced in
[
7
]
.

We remark that
the étale version of our results on the Radon transform
leads to new instances of the regular
inverse Galois problem, details will appear elsewhere.

1. Definition and first properties of the Radon transformation

In the following we recall the definition of the Radon transform on
the projective plane, following Brylinsky
[
6
]
. For results on the étale
version of the
Radon transform we refer to
[
11
]
. We remark that most of the stated results also hold for higher dimensional projective spaces over more general bases
(cf. loc.cit.).

We consider projective spaces and varieties over the complex numbers. We assume additionally in Section
1
that the coefficient ring is a field

k
of characteristic zero. In the other sections, the coefficient ring is allowed to be any field
k,
unless otherwise stated.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be the usual projective plane and let

\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
be the dual projective plane which parametrizes the lines in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.

Then
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
is also a projective plane
and the line in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
corresponding to

[\widetilde{x}_{0},\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2}]\in\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
has the defining equation

x_{0}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{0}+x_{1}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{1}+x_{2}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{2}=0,

also
proving that
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
is the dual of
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}.

Notation:
a point
\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
will correspond to a line
L_{\widetilde{x}}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.

By duality, an element
x\in

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
can be identified with a line

\widetilde{L}_{x}\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
in the dual plane. By construction, the line
\widetilde{L}_{x}
parametrizes
the pencil of lines
{\bf L}_{x}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
containing
the point
x.

Let

H:=\{({x},\widetilde{{x}})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\mid{x}\in L_{\widetilde{{x}}}\}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}

denote the incidence relation which is equipped with the obvious projections
p_{1}:H\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and

p_{2}:H\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}.

Then one has the
Radon transformation

\mathcal{R}:D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}D^{b}_{c}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}),\,K\mapsto Rp_{2*}p_{1}^{*}(K)[1].

The same construction applies to the dual space
leading to the
dual Radon transform

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}:D^{b}_{c}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}),\,\widetilde{K}\mapsto Rp_{1*}p_{2}^{*}(\widetilde{K})[1]\,.

For an object
K\in D^{b}_{c}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}),
let

\mathcal{R}^{n}(K):={}^{p}H^{n}\mathcal{R}(K)
denote the
n
-th perverse cohomology of
\mathcal{R}(K)
and let
{\mathcal{K}}:=Rp_{*}(K),
where
p
denotes the structure morphism to the spectrum of
\mathbb{C}.

Remark 1.1

The definition of the Radon transform makes sense also for more
general coefficient rings and also for more general fields of definition (cf.
[
6
]
,
[
13
]
).
Then results whose proof depends on the decomposition theorem
(see
[
4
]
), like the
proof of Radon inversion in
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
, become more involved. This is the reason, why we assume that the coefficient ring is a field of
characteristic
0
in this section. We remark
that by
[
13
]
, Thm. 1.1 and Rem. 1.2(3), Radon inversion still holds in our context for
coefficient rings like
\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z}
and algebraically closed fields of definition of arbitrary characteristic.

Let
{\rm Perv}^{\mathrm{smooth}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
denote the category of smooth(=constant) perverse sheaves on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and let

M({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
be the Verdier quotient
{\rm Perv}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})/{\rm Perv}^{\mathrm{smooth}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}).
Let
M(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2})
be analogously defined.
Within these categories
one has
Radon inversion
(cf.
[
6
]
, Thm. 5.5,
[
11
]
,
Lemma 1.4):

(1.1)

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\circ\mathcal{R}\equiv\id.

Especially, simple objects in
M({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
correspond to simple objects
in
M(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2})
under Radon transform. Note that any object
K
in
M({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
is represented uniquely by a
reduced

perverse sheaf (i.e., a perverse sheaf which does not admit any nontrivial constant subobject/quotient),
see
[
11
]
, convention on Page 211. Sometimes it is useful to work directly within the categories
{\rm Perv}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
and
{\rm Perv}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2})
and not in their associated quotients. This leads to the following definition:

Definition 1.2

An object
K
in
{\rm Perv}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
has the
property
P
if it has no nontrivial cohomology outside degree
0,
i.e.,

{\mathcal{K}}^{i}=0
for
i\neq 0,
where
{\mathcal{K}}
is as above.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
denote the category of semisimple perverse sheaves having the property
P.
Let
\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}
be the subcategory of

{\rm Perv}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2})
defined analogously.

Remark 1.3

The property
P
appears often as follows:

let
K=j_{!*}({\mathcal{L}})[2]
be an intermediate extension of an irreducible local system
{\mathcal{L}}
on a dense open subset
U
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}

such that
{\mathcal{L}}
has local monodromy with a trivial
1
-eigenspace along a projective
line
L\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus U,
then
K
is in
{\mathcal{P}}.

To see this we argue as follows:
the inclusion
j:U\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
is the composition of

j^{\prime}:U\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{A}^{2}={\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus L
with
j^{{}^{\prime\prime}}:\mathbb{A}^{2}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.
The long exact sequence of cohomology with compact supports with respect to the inclusion
j^{{}^{\prime\prime}}:\mathbb{A}^{2}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
applied to the constructible
sheaf
j_{!*}({\mathcal{L}})=K[-2]
reads

\cdots\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}H^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{A}^{2},j^{\prime}_{!*}({\mathcal{L}}))\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}H^{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{2},K[-2])\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}H^{1}(L,K[-2]|_{L})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}H^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{A}^{2},j^{\prime}_{!*}({\mathcal{L}}))\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\cdots\;.

By Artin’s vanishing theorem one has
H^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{A}^{2},j^{\prime}_{!*}({\mathcal{L}}))=0,
see
[
9
]
, Thm. 4.1.26 and
Cor. 5.2.19. Since
K[-2]|_{L}
has at most punctual support by the assumption on
the local monodromy at
L,
the cohomology group

H^{1}(L,K[-2]|_{L})
also vanishes, implying that
H^{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{2},K[-2])
and hence

H^{-1}({\mathbb{P}}^{2},K)={\mathcal{K}}^{-1}
is zero. The other cohomology groups
{\mathcal{K}}^{i},\,i\neq 0,
also vanish by the irreducibility assumption, resp. by duality.

We refer to

[
9
]
, Chapter 6, for other vanishing theorems in this direction.

Proposition 1.4

Let
K
be an object in
{\rm Perv}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}).
Then the following holds:

(1)

The Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
is perverse if
K
is in

{\mathcal{P}}
or if
K
is a simple and non-constant perverse sheaf situated in degree
-2.

(2)

If
K
is a simple object in
{\mathcal{P}},
then
\mathcal{R}(K)
is simple as an object in
{\rm Perv}(\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}).

Proof.

By
[
11
]
, Lemma IV.2.2, (cf.
[
6
]
, Thm. 5.5) one has

\mathcal{R}^{n}(K)\simeq\widetilde{p}^{*}[2]{\mathcal{K}}^{n-1}

for
n<0
and

\mathcal{R}^{n}(K)\simeq\widetilde{p}^{*}[2]{\mathcal{K}}^{n+1}

for
n>0.
Hence the perverse sheaves
\mathcal{R}^{n}(K)
vanish for
n\neq 0,
using that
{\mathcal{K}}^{i}=0
for

i\neq 0,
proving the first claim of
(1).
If
K
is a simple and non-constant perverse sheaf situated in degree
-2,
then it is the intermediate
extension of a non-constant irreducible local system on a dense open subset of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.
This implies that
{\mathcal{K}}^{-2}
is trivial
(and hence also
{\mathcal{K}}^{2}
by duality), implying that also in this case, one has
\mathcal{R}(K)=\mathcal{R}^{0}(K)
implying that
\mathcal{R}(K)
is perverse. It has to be non-constant because of Radon inversion.

Let
K
be a simple object in
{\mathcal{P}}.
Since the morphism
p_{2}:H\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
is projective, the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
is
semisimple. The Lefschetz sequence given on page 211 of
[
11
]
implies that
\widetilde{p}^{*}[2]{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}
is isomorphic to the
maximal constant perverse subsheaf of
\mathcal{R}^{0}(K)=\mathcal{R}(K)
(the latter equality follows as in the proof of
(1)
using
{\mathcal{K}}^{-2}={\mathcal{K}}^{2}=0
). Since
{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}=0

by property
P
and since
\mathcal{R}({K})
is semisimple, the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
is reduced. It follows now from Radon inversion in the form
stated in (
1.1
) that
\mathcal{R}(K)
is again simple.

\Box

2. Encoding local systems on plane curve complements

Let
C\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be a reduced plane algebraic curve of degree
r.

In this section we collect the data which describe a local system
{\mathcal{L}}
over an arbitrary field
k
of rank
n\in\mathbb{N}
on

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C,
suitable for the Radon transformation. In the following, we will summarize the main results of

[
7
]
, adapted to our situation:

We choose a generic point
{p}_{0}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C.
Using a projective coordinate change on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
we
can assume that
p_{0}=[1:0:0].
Using the conventions from the beginning of Section
1
,
we write
\widetilde{L}_{{p}_{0}}\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
for the corresponding line in the dual plane. Hence

\widetilde{L}_{p_{0}}=\{[0:\widetilde{x}_{1}:\widetilde{x}_{2}]\mid[\widetilde{x}_{1}:\widetilde{x}_{2}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\}.

We also choose an element
\widetilde{p}_{0}\in\widetilde{L}_{p_{0}}\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
such that
L_{\widetilde{p}_{0}}\subseteq{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}
intersects
C
transversally, where
L_{\widetilde{p}_{0}}
is the line in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
determined by
\widetilde{p}_{0}.

By possibly using a further coordinate change fixing
p_{0}
we can assume that

\widetilde{p}_{0}=[0:1:0]\in\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}.

As in the previous section, the incidence relation is defined as

H:=\{([x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}],[\widetilde{x}_{0}:\widetilde{x}_{1}:\widetilde{x}_{2}])\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\mid x_{0}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{0}+x_{1}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{1}+x_{2}\cdot\widetilde{x}_{2}=0\}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2},

coming
with its projections
p_{1}:H\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and

p_{2}:H\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}.

We can view the pencil
{\bf L}_{p_{0}}
as a subvariety of
H:

{\bf L}_{p_{0}}=p_{2}^{-1}(\widetilde{L}_{p_{0}})=\{([x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}],[0:\widetilde{x}_{1}:\widetilde{x}_{2}])\in H\}.

Let
{\widetilde{y}_{1}},\ldots,{\widetilde{y}_{s}}\in\widetilde{L}_{p_{0}}
denote the points which are
exceptional
with respect to

C,
meaning that each
L_{\widetilde{y}_{i}}
is either tangent to
C
or that
L_{\widetilde{y}_{i}}
contains a singularity of
C.

By possibly using another projective coordinate change (which fixes
p_{0}
and fixes
L_{\widetilde{p}_{0}}
setwise), we can assume that
\widetilde{y}_{s}=[0:0:1].

One has an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundles

(2.1)

\Lambda:{\bf L}_{p_{0}}\smallsetminus p_{2}^{-1}({\widetilde{y}_{s}})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{1}}={\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}

given by

([x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}],[0:1:\widetilde{x}_{2}])\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}([x_{0}:x_{1}],\widetilde{x}_{2}),

where the bundle maps are the respective second projections.

Let

S:=\widetilde{L}_{p_{0}}\smallsetminus\{{\widetilde{y}_{1}},\ldots,{\widetilde{y}_{s}}\},

let
X:=p_{2}^{-1}(S)

and
X_{0}:=p_{2}^{-1}(\widetilde{p}_{0}).
Then

D:=p_{1}^{-1}(C)\cap X
is a divisor of relative degree
r
over
S
(denoted
r
-configuration in
[
7
]
, Section 2.1).
Let
U:=X\smallsetminus D,
write
\pi:U\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}S
for the restriction of
p_{2}
to
U,
and let
U_{0}:=p_{2}^{-1}({\widetilde{p}_{0}})\cap U.

Then
\pi:U\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}S
is a topological fibration with typical fibre
U_{0}
and hence yields a short exact sequence as follows (cf.
[
7
]
, Formula (6)):

(2.2)

1\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\pi_{1}(U_{0},(p_{0},\widetilde{p}_{0}))\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\pi_{1}(U,(p_{0},\widetilde{p}_{0}))\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\pi_{1}(S,\widetilde{p}_{0})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}1\,.

By restricting the above
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundle isomorphism

\Lambda:{\bf L}_{p_{0}}\smallsetminus p_{2}^{-1}({\widetilde{y}_{s}})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{1}}
to
X
we obtain
a trivializing isomorphism
\lambda:X\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{S}
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundles
which identifies the map
p_{2}:X\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}S
with
the structural map
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{S}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}S.
One has

\lambda((p_{0},[0,1,\widetilde{x}_{2}]))=(\infty,\widetilde{x}_{2}),

where
\infty=[1:0]
is the point at infinity in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}.

Moreover, since
p_{0}\notin C
as
p_{0}
is generic w.r. to
C,
the section
\infty\times S=\lambda(p_{1}^{-1}(p_{0}))
is disjoint from

\lambda(D).

As in
[
7
]
, Section 1.2, we fix a homeomorphism (called a
marking
)
\kappa:X_{0}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C}),
mapping

P_{0}:=X_{0}\smallsetminus D
to a subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
of the real line inside the Riemann sphere
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})

and
p_{0}
to an element
x_{0}
in the upper half plane. Let us, just for the moment, identify

U_{0}=X_{0}\smallsetminus P_{0}
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\smallsetminus\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
via
\kappa.

As in
[
7
]
now choose counterclockwise simple loops
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}
around
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
which are based at
x_{0}
as follows: Each
\gamma_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,r
is a simple closed loop which intersects the real line exactly twice, first the open interval
(x_{i-1},x_{i}),
then the interval
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
(where for
i=1,
the interval

(x_{i-1},x_{i})
is iterpreted as
(-\infty,x_{1})
and for
i=r
the interval
(x_{i},x_{i+1})

is interpreted as
(x_{i+1},+\infty)
).

By taking inverse images under
\kappa,
this leads to a standard presentation
of the fundamental group

\pi_{1}(U_{0},(p_{0},\widetilde{p}_{0}))=\langle\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}\mid\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{r}=1\rangle.

We remark that (up to a Möbius transform) the construction
of the
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}
is the same as the construction
of a geometric basis of meridians
in
[
2
]
.

By the above presentation of
\pi_{1}(U_{0}),
the restriction
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}:={\mathcal{L}}|_{U_{0}}
is determined by the tuple of matrices

{\bf g}_{\mathcal{L}}:=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\in\GL(V)^{r},\quad g_{1}\cdots g_{r}=1,

where
\gamma_{i}
is sent to
g_{i}
under the monodromy representation
\rho_{{\mathcal{L}}}
of
{\mathcal{L}}

(here
V\simeq k^{n}
). We call
{\bf g}
the
monodromy tuple
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
(resp. of
{\mathcal{L}}
) with respect to
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}.

Remark 2.1

By Lefschetz’ hyperplane theorem (see
[
10
]
,
[
8
]
), the fundamental group

\pi_{1}(U_{0})
maps surjectively onto
\pi_{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C).
Hence the monodromy tuple
{\bf g}
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
also
determines the local system
{\mathcal{L}}
up to isomorphism.

Let

\mathcal{O}_{r}:=\{P\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{1}\mid\#P=r\}

denote the configuration space parametrizing subsets of
\mathbb{C}
of cardinality
r
and let

\mathcal{O}_{r,1}:=\{(q,P)\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times\mathcal{O}_{r}\mid q\notin P\}.

As explained in
[
7
]
, Section 2.2, our choice of a marking
defines generators
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r-1}
of the braid group

{\mathcal{B}}_{r}:=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{r},P_{0})
as follows: under
\kappa,
a representative of
\beta_{i}
fixes all points different from
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}

and the image under
\kappa
of
x_{i}
moves to
x_{i+1}
in the lower half plane
and (the image under
\kappa
of)
x_{i+1}
travels to
x_{i}
in the upper half plane.
This leads to the standard presentation of
{\mathcal{B}}_{r}:

\langle\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r-1}\mid\beta_{i}\beta_{i+1}\beta_{i}=\beta_{i+1}\beta_{i}\beta_{i+1}\textrm{ for }i=1,\ldots,r-1\textrm{ and }\beta_{i}\beta_{j}=\beta_{j}\beta_{i}\textrm{ for }|i-j|>1\rangle.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{r,1}:=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{r,1},(\infty,P_{0})).

The natural projection
\mathcal{O}_{r,1}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{r}
is a topological fibration with typical fibre

U_{0}
, and admits a section
P\mapsto(\infty,P).
It yields a
split exact sequence of fundamental groups

(2.3)

3. Deformations of local systems and cocycles
using the braid group

We continue by summarizing the results of
[
7
]
concerned with deformations
of local system along braids: Using the notions of the previous section, define

\mathcal{E}_{r}\;:=\;\{\;{\bf g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\mid g_{i}\in\GL(V),\;\;\prod_{i}g_{i}=1\;\}.

As described in the previous Section, an
element
{\bf g}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}
corresponds to a representation

\rho_{0}:\pi_{1}(U_{0})\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\GL(V)
(set
\rho_{0}(\gamma_{i}):=g_{i}
) and
hence to a local system on
U_{0}
.

As described in
[
7
]
, Section 2.2, the natural deformation operation of the braid group
on local systems (by composing
\rho_{0}
with the operation of
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
on
\pi_{1}(U_{0})
)
amounts to a right-action of
{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
on
\mathcal{E}_{r}
as follows:

(3.1)

{\bf g}^{\beta_{i}}\;=\;(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i+1},g_{i+1}^{-1}g_{i}g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{r}).

This deformation operation enables the computation of the braiding operation
on cocycles which will be considered in the next section:
given
{\bf g}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}
, we have the vector space

H_{\bf g}\;=\;\{\;(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\mid v_{i}\in{\rm Im}(g_{i}-1),\;v_{1}\cdot g_{2}\cdots g_{r}+v_{2}\cdot g_{3}\cdots g_{r}+\cdots+v_{r}=0\;\}\subset V^{r}.

We remark that we have a natural isomorphism between
H_{\bf g}/E_{\bf g}
(where
E_{\bf g}\leqslant H_{\bf g}
is as defined below) and the
parabolic cohomology group
H^{1}({\mathbb{P}}^{1},j_{*}{\mathcal{L}})
(where
j
denotes the inclusion of

U_{0}
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1},
cf.
[
7
]
, Section 2).

In loc.cit., Section 3, for
{\bf g}\in\mathcal{E}_{r}
and
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
a linear map

\Phi({\bf g},\beta):\,H_{{\bf g}}\;\mathchoice{\xrightarrow{\textstyle\kern 4.0pt\;\kern 3.0pt}}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\;}}{{\longrightarrow}}}{}{}H_{{\bf g}^{\beta}}.

is defined as follows (recall our convention of linear maps acting from the right): for a standard generator

\beta_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
one has

(3.2)

\qquad(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}){\Phi({\bf g},\beta_{i})}\\
\;=\;(v_{1},\ldots,v_{i+1},\,\underbrace{v_{i+1}(1-g_{i+1}^{-1}g_{i}g_{i+1})+v_{i}g_{i+1}}_{\text{\rm$(i+1)$th entry}},\,\ldots,v_{r}).

Note that both vector spaces
H_{{\bf g}}
and
H_{{\bf g}^{\beta}}
are subspaces of
V^{r}.
Hence

{\Phi({\bf g},\beta_{i})}
is given by restriction of the following endomorphism
M({\bf g},\beta_{i})\in{\rm End}(V^{r})

in matrix form to
H_{{\bf g}}:

(3.3)

M({\bf g},\beta_{i})=\left(\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}1&&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&\quad 1&&\\
&&&&\,\,\,0&\quad g_{i+1}&\\
&&&&\,\,\,1&\quad(1-g_{i+1}^{-1}g_{i}g_{i+1})&&\\
&&&&&&&1&&\\
&&&&&&&&\quad\ddots&\\
&&&&&&&&&\quad\quad 1\end{array}\right)\,.

Moreover, one has the ‘cocycle rule’ which allows one to compute
\Phi({\bf g},\beta)
for a general element
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
in terms of the
\Phi({\bf g},\beta_{i}):

(3.4)

\Phi({\bf g},\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\prime})\;=\;\Phi({\bf g},\beta^{\prime})\cdot\Phi({\bf g}^{\beta^{\prime}},\beta^{\prime\prime})\quad(\beta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}).

The product on the right hand side of (
3.4
) is defined as
the linear map from
H_{{\bf g}}
to
H_{{\bf g}^{\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\prime}}},
acting from the right, obtained by
first applying
\Phi({\bf g},\beta^{\prime})
and then

\Phi({\bf g}^{\beta^{\prime}},\beta^{\prime\prime})
.

The cocycle rule especially implies that

{\Phi({\bf g},\beta_{i}^{-1})}=\Phi({\bf g}^{\beta_{i}^{-1}},\beta_{i})^{-1}

is given by restriction of

M({\bf g}^{\beta_{i}^{-1}},\beta_{i})^{-1}\in{\rm End}(V^{r})

to
H_{{\bf g}}.
The latter endomorphism
is denoted by
M({\bf g},\beta_{i}^{-1})
for convenience. By the definition
of
M({\bf g}^{\beta_{i}^{-1}},\beta_{i})
and inversion one derives the following matrix-expression:

(3.5)

M({\bf g},\beta_{i}^{-1})=\left(\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}1&&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&\quad 1&&\\
&&&&\,\,\,(g_{i+1}-1)g_{i}^{-1}&\quad 1&\\
&&&&\,\,\,\quad\quad g_{i}^{-1}&\quad 0&&\\
&&&&&&&1&&\\
&&&&&&&&\quad\ddots&\\
&&&&&&&&&\quad\quad 1\end{array}\right)\,.

Proposition 3.1

(1)

Let
\omega=\beta_{i_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots\beta_{i_{k}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
be a word in the standard generators

of
{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
(with

) and let

Then the isomorphism

to

Then

induces an isomorphism

The first claim follows from the cocycle rule (

). The second claim follows from the fact that

[
7
]
, Section 2.2.
\Box

3.1
.
\Box

Remark 4.7

Let
K
be a simple and non-constant perverse sheaf whose coefficient field
k
has characteristic
0.
It is a consequence of Radon inversion
that the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}(K)
has a unique
simple non-constant subfactor
S
(cf. the discussion in Section
1
). Let us additionally
assume that the following generic condition holds: the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
is a non-constant local system on

\widetilde{Y}.

Then
j_{!*}(\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}))[-2]
contains this unique subfactor
S.
If moreover
K
satisfies the condition
P

then Prop.
1.4
implies that

j_{!*}(\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}))[-2]=\mathcal{R}(K).

The local system
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
in turn is determined up to isomorphism by the monodromy tuple
(with respect to
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}
), which is algorithmically determined by Thm.
4.6
.

5. Examples: an elliptic fibration and the classical Zariski pair

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
\neq 2.
Let

\widetilde{x}_{1}:=[2:-4:3],\,\widetilde{x}_{2}:=[1,-1,1],\,\widetilde{x}_{3}:=[1:0:0],\,\widetilde{x}_{4}:=[1:0:-1]\,\in\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}

and let
{\mathcal{L}}
be the rank-one local system on

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C\quad(C:=\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{4}L_{\widetilde{x}_{i}})

having quadratic
local
monodromy
-1\in k^{*}
along
L_{\widetilde{x}_{i}}\,(i=1,2,3,4).

Hence the monodromy tuple of
{\mathcal{L}}

(defined as in Section
2
) is

{\bf g}=(-1,-1,-1,-1)\in(k^{*})^{4}.

We remark that
C
represents a generic quadruple of projective lines in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}.

The singularities of
C
are

x_{1}:=[-\frac{1}{2}:\frac{1}{2}:1],\,x_{2}:=[0:\frac{3}{4}:1],\,x_{3}:=[0:1:1],

x_{4}:=[1:\frac{5}{4}:1],\,x_{5}:=[1:2:1],\,x_{6}:=[0:1:0].

Hence the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
of
{\mathcal{L}}
is a
k
-local system on
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus\widetilde{C},

where
\widetilde{C}
is the union of
\widetilde{L}_{x_{1}},\ldots,\widetilde{L}_{x_{6}}.

Proposition 5.1

The Radon transform
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
is an irreducible
k
-local system of rank
2
on
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus\widetilde{C},

having monodromy tuple

\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-2\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
-2&1\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&-2\\
2&3\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&-2\\
2&3\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-3&-8\\
2&5\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-2\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\right).

Proof.

By the Ogg-Shafarevich Formula (
4.1
), the rank of

\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
is
2
since
n=1,

r=4,
and since
-1\in k^{*}
has no nontrivial invariants acting on the
one-dimensional vector space
k.

Using the theory of wiring diagrams
(encoding the intersection pattern of the underlying real line arrangement, cf.
[
1
]
,

[
2
]
) one derives the braid monodromy of
U
in a standard way as follows:

\omega_{1}=\beta_{1}^{2},\,\omega_{2}={}(\beta_{2}^{2})^{\beta_{1}},\,\omega_{3}={}(\beta_{1}^{2})^{\beta_{2}\beta_{1}},\,\omega_{4}={}(\beta_{3}^{2})^{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{1}},\,\omega_{5}={}(\beta_{2}^{2})^{\beta_{3}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{1}}

with
\omega_{6}
being the inverse of the product
\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{5}.

By a computation using
Thm.
4.6
,
the monodromy tuple of
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
is as stated.
Note that this computation is considerably simplified by the
obvious observation
that
{\bf g}^{\omega_{i}}={\bf g}
for
i=1,\ldots,6.
Hence Prop.
3.1
in combination with Thm.
4.6
implies that for the above expression of
\omega_{i}
as a word in the standard generators

\omega_{i}=\beta_{i_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots\beta_{i_{k}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}\quad(\varepsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}),

the
matrix inducing
\rho_{\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{i})
is the corresponding matrix product

M({\bf g},\omega_{i})=M({\bf g},\beta_{i_{1}})^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots M({\bf g},\beta_{i_{k}})^{\varepsilon_{k}}.

The latter product can easily be evaluated using

M({\bf g},\beta_{1})=\left(\begin{array}[]{lrlll}0&\quad-1&\\
1&\quad\,2&&\\
&&&1&\\
&&&&1\end{array}\right),\,\,M({\bf g},\beta_{2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{llrll}1&&&&\\
&0&\quad-1&\\
&1&\quad\,2&&\\
&&&&1\\
\end{array}\right),\,\,M({\bf g},\beta_{3})=\left(\begin{array}[]{lllrl}1&&&&\\
&1&&&\\
&&0&\quad-1\\
&&1&\quad\,2\\
\end{array}\right)\,

and passing to the subquotient
H_{{\bf g}}/E_{{\bf g}}.

\Box

Remark 5.2

(1)

Again, by standard arguments, the braid monodromy of
\widetilde{C}

is generated by the following braids:

(5.1)

(\beta_{4}\beta_{5}\beta_{4})^{2},\quad\beta_{3}^{2},\quad\beta_{3}^{-1}(\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{1})^{2}\beta_{3}.

It can be checked that the local system
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
satisfies indeed the relations coming from
Eq. (
5.1
).

(2)

Using the braid monodromy in Eq. (
5.1
) the dual Radon transform
applied to
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
has rank
2
and decomposes into a trivial local system and the restriction of
{\mathcal{L}}

to a slightly smaller open subset. Hence, although the intermediate extension
K=j_{!*}({\mathcal{L}})[2]

satisfies the property
P,
it follows from Prop.
1.4
(2) that

\mathcal{R}(K)=\widetilde{j}_{!*}\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})[2]
does not have the property
P.

Another example comes from the theory of Zariski pairs (see
[
16
]
): Recall the classical Zariski pair,
consisting of a six-cuspidal plane sextic
C
having its cusps on a conic and a six-cuspidal plane sextic
C^{\prime}
whose cusps do not lie on a conic.

Zariski showed that the fundamental group of the complement of
C
is isomorphic to the free group on two generators of order
2,
resp.
3,
whereas the fundamental group of the complement of
C^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
a cyclic group of order
6.

The braid monodromy of the Zariski pair is determined
in
[
15
]
, Example 3, (cf.
[
3
]
, Prop. 7.3) to be as follows:
for
C
one has twice the tuple:

\left(\beta_{1}^{3},\beta_{1}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{1}},\beta_{3}^{3},\beta_{3}^{\beta_{4}^{-1}\beta_{3}},\beta_{5}^{3},\beta_{2}^{\beta_{3}\beta_{3}\beta_{1}},\beta_{4}^{\beta_{5}\beta_{5}\beta_{3}},\beta_{3}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}},\beta_{5}^{\beta_{4}^{-1}}\right).

For
C^{\prime}
one has:

\left(\beta_{3}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}^{-1}},\beta_{4},\beta_{5},\beta_{2}^{3},(\beta_{2}^{\beta_{1}^{-1}})^{3},\beta_{2}^{\beta_{3}^{-1}},\beta_{2}^{\beta_{1}^{-1}\beta_{3}^{-1}\beta_{4}^{-1}},\beta_{1}^{3},\beta_{1}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{3}\beta_{4}^{-1}},\beta_{5}^{\beta_{4}^{-1}\beta_{4}^{-1}}\right.,

\left.\beta_{3}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{4}^{-1}\beta_{4}^{-1}},\beta_{4}^{3},\beta_{1}^{3},\beta_{3}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}^{-1}},\beta_{3}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}^{-1}},\beta_{1}^{3},\beta_{1}^{\beta_{2}^{-1}\beta_{2}^{-1}},\beta_{2}\right).

Let now
{\mathcal{L}},
resp.
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},
be the rank-one
\mathbb{C}
-local system on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C,
resp.

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\smallsetminus C^{\prime},
defined by the obvious homomorphism of fundamental groups
onto the group of 6-th roots of unity inside
\mathbb{C}^{\times}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{C}),
so that in both cases,
the monodromy tuple is a
6
-tuple
(\zeta_{6},\ldots,\zeta_{6})
whose entries consist of the same primitive sixth root of unity
\zeta_{6}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

It is astonishing that the Radon transforms
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}),
resp.
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}),

descend to local systems on the complement of the
dual curves
\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus{C}^{*},
resp.

\widetilde{{\mathbb{P}}}^{2}\smallsetminus{C^{\prime}}^{*}
(with
{C}^{*}
being the dual curve of
C
and

{C^{\prime}}^{*}
being the dual curve of
C^{\prime}
) since, a priori, they are defined on the complement
of the larger curves
\widetilde{C},
resp.
\widetilde{C}^{\prime},
defined in Section
4
.

An application of the explicit braid monodromy for the curves
C,
resp.
C^{\prime},
in combination with Thm.
4.6
leads to the following result:

Theorem 5.3

The monodromy group of
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}})
is a finite solvable
group
G\leqslant\GL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
of order
648
acting irreducibly on a direct summand of dimension
3.
The monodromy group
G^{\prime}\leqslant\GL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
of

\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})
is a direct product of the cyclic scalar group of order
3
with the finite symplectic group
\Sp_{4}(3)\leqslant\GL_{4}(\mathbb{C}).
