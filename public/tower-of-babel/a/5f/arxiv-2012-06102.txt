Kernel Estimator and Bandwidth Selection for Density and its Derivatives: The kedd Package
by Arsalane Chouaib Guidoum

Abstract
The kedd package ( Guidoum, 2015 ) providing additional smoothing techniques to the R statistical system. Although various packages on the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) provide functions useful to nonparametric statistics, kedd aims to serve as a central location for more specifically of a nonparametric functions and data sets. The current feature set of the package can be split in four main categories: compute the convolutions and derivatives of a kernel function, compute the kernel estimators for a density of probability and its derivatives, computing the bandwidth selectors with different methods, displaying the kernel estimators and selection functions of the bandwidth. Moreover, the package follows the general R philosophy of working with model objects. This means that instead of merely returning, say, a kernel estimator of r t ​ h r^{th} derivative of a density, many functions will return an object containing, it’s functions are S3 classes ( S3method ). The object can then be manipulated at one’s will using various extraction, summary or plotting functions. Whenever possible, we develop a graphical user interface of the various functions of a coherent whole, to facilitate the use of this package.

Convolutions and derivatives in kernels

In non-parametric statistics, a kernel is a weighting function used in non-parametric estimation techniques. Kernels are used in kernel density estimation to estimate random variables density functions
f(x)
, or in kernel regression to estimate the conditional expectation of a random variable, see e.g.,
Silverman, 1986
;
Wand and Jones, 1995
. In general any functions having the following assumptions can be used as a kernel:

(A1)

K(x)\geq 0
and
\int_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{R}}}K(x)dx=1
.

(A2)

Symmetric about the origin, e.g.,
\int_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{R}}}xK(x)dx=0
.

(A3)

Has finite second moment, e.g.,
\mu_{2}(K)=\int_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{R}}}x^{2}K(x)dx<\infty
. We denote
\mathrm{R}(K)=\int_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{R}}}\left(K(x)\right)^{2}dx
.

K(x)
is a kernel, then so is the function
\bar{K}(x)
defined by
\bar{K}(x)=\lambda K(\lambda x)

\lambda>0

3
Kernel density derivative estimator

Let
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
be a data sample, independent and identically distributed of a continuous random variable
X
, with density function
f(x)
. If the kernel
K
is differentiable
r
times then a natural estimator of the
r^{th}
derivative of
f(x)
the
r^{th}
derivative of the kernel estimate
(
Bhattacharya, 1967
;
Schuster, 1969
;
Alekseev, 1972
)
:

\hat{f}_{h}^{(r)}(x)=\frac{d^{r}}{dx^{r}}\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x-X_{i}}{h}\right)=\frac{1}{nh^{r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K^{(r)}\left(\frac{x-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(3)

where
K^{(r)}
is
r^{th}
derivative of the kernel function
K
, which we take to be a symmetric probability density with at least
r
non zero derivatives when estimating
f^{(r)}(x)
, and
h
is the bandwidth, this parameter is very important that controls the degree of smoothing applied to the data.

The following assumptions on the density
f^{(r)}(x)
, the bandwidth
h
, and the kernel
K
:

(A4)

The
(r+2)
derivatives
f^{(r+2)}(x)
is continuous, square integrable and ultimately monotone.

(A5)

In the asymptotic framework, as
\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}nh_{n}^{2r+1}=\infty
, i.e., as the number of sample
n
is increased
h
approaches zero at a rate slower than
1/n^{2r+1}
.

(A6)

Assumptions about
K
are introduced in the previous section.

As seen in Equation (
3
), when working with a kernel estimator of the
r^{th}
derivative function two choices must be made:
the kernel function
K
and the smoothing parameter or bandwidth
h
. The choice of
K
is a problem of less importance, because
K
is not very sensitive to the shape of estimator, and different functions that produce good results can be used. In practice, the choice of an efficient method for the computation of
h
, for an observed data sample is a crucial problem, because of the effect of the bandwidth on the shape of the corresponding estimator. If the bandwidth is small, we will obtain an under smoothed estimator, with high variability. On the contrary, if the value of
h
is big, the resulting estimator will be over smooth and farther from the function that we are trying to estimate.

Rudemo, 1982
;

;
Sheather and Jones, 1991
;
Chiu, 1991a
;
Chiu, 1991b
;
Chiu, 1992
;

;
Stute, 1992
;

;
Sheather, 2004
;
Duong and Hazelton, 2003
;
Duong and Hazelton, 2005
;
Heidenreich et all, 2013
, to the best of our knowledge, only few paper have been studied in the context of estimating the
r^{th}
derivative of a density
f(x)

4.1
Optimal bandwidth

We Consider the following
\mathrm{AMISE}
version of the
r^{th}
derivative of a probability density
f(x)

(
Scott, 1992
, p. 131)
:

\mathrm{AMISE}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{1}{4}h^{4}\mu_{2}^{2}(K)\mathrm{R}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)

(8)

The optimal bandwidth minimizing (
8
) is:

h^{\ast}=\left[\frac{(2r+1)\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{\mu_{2}^{2}(K)\mathrm{R}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)}\right]^{1/(2r+5)}n^{-1/(2r+5)}

(9)

whereof:

\mathrm{AMISE}(h,r)=\frac{2r+5}{4}\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)^{\frac{4}{(2r+5)}}\left[\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}(K)\mathrm{R}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)}{2r+1}\right]^{\frac{2r+1}{2r+5}}n^{-\frac{4}{2r+5}}

(10)

which is the smallest possible
\mathrm{AMISE}
for estimation of
\hat{f}^{(r)}_{h}
. The function
h.amise()
provides the optimal bandwidth under
\mathrm{AMISE}
. The same possibilities for the kernel function as in the function

appear here. We enumerate the arguments and results of this function in Table

4.2
Maximum likelihood cross-validation

This method was proposed by
Habbema, Hermans and Van den Broek 1974
and
Duin 1976
. They proposed to choose
h
so that the pseudo-likelihood
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{f}_{h}(X_{i})
is maximized. However this has a trivial maximum at
h=0
, so the cross-validation principle is invoked by replacing
\hat{f}_{h}(x)
by the leave-one-out
\hat{f}_{h,i}(x)
, where:

\hat{f}_{h,i}(X_{i})=\frac{1}{(n-1)h}\sum_{j\neq i}K\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

Define that
h
as good which approaches the finite maximum of

h_{mlcv}=\underset{h>0}{\mathrm{argmax}}\ \mathrm{MLCV}(h)

(11)

\mathrm{MLCV}(h)=\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\sum_{j\neq i}K\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)\right]-\log[(n-1)h]\right)

(12)

The function
h.mlcv()

4.3
Unbiased cross-validation

Rudemo, 1982
and
Bowman, 1984
proposed a so-called unbiased (least-squares) cross-validation (
\mathrm{UCV}
) in kernel density estimator, is probably the most popular and best studied one. An adaptation of unbiased cross-validation is proposed by
Wolfgang et all, 1990
for bandwidth choice in the
r^{th}
derivative of kernel density estimator. The essential idea of this methods, it aims to estimate
h
the minimizer of
\mathrm{ISE}(h)
. The minimization criterion is defined by:

h_{ucv}=\underset{h>0}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \mathrm{UCV}(h,r)

(13)

\mathrm{UCV}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\left(K^{(r)}\ast K^{(r)}-2K^{(2r)}\right)\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(14)

In general, cross-validation functions in non-parametric bandwidth selection present several local minima. These minima are more likely to appear at too small values of the bandwidth
(
Peter and Marron, 1991
)
. The function
h.ucv()

4.4
Biased cross-validation

Biased cross-validation was proposed by
Scott and George, 1987
, which has as its immediate target the
\mathrm{AMISE}
(
8
). They proposed to estimate
\mathrm{R}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)
by:

\hat{\mathrm{R}}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)=\mathrm{R}\left(\hat{f}^{(r+2)}_{h}\right)-\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r+2)}\right)}{nh^{2r+5}}

There are two versions of
\mathrm{BCV}
, depending on the estimator of
\mathrm{R}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)
. We can use
(
Scott and George, 1987
)

\hat{\mathrm{R}}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{r+2}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+5}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(r+2)}\ast K^{(r+2)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

or we could use
(
Jones and Kappenman, 1991
)

\hat{\mathrm{R}}\left(f^{(r+2)}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{r+2}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+5}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(2r+4)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

From this we obtain respectively an adaptation of biased cross-validation for bandwidth choice in the
r^{th}
derivative of kernel density estimator, is given by:

\mathrm{BCV}_{1}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}(K)}{4}\frac{(-1)^{r+2}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(r+2)}\ast K^{(r+2)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(15)

\mathrm{BCV}_{2}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}(K)}{4}\frac{(-1)^{r+2}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(2r+4)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(16)

The
\mathrm{BCV}
selectors
h_{bcv_{1}}
and
h_{bcv_{2}}
are the minimisers of the appropriate
\mathrm{BCV}
function. The function

4.5
Complete cross-validation

Jones and Kappenman, 1991
proposed a so-called complete cross-validation (
\mathrm{CCV}
) in kernel density estimator. This method can be extended to the estimation of derivative of the density, basing our estimate of integrated squared density derivative
(
Peter and Marron, 1987
)
we get the following. Thus,
h_{ccv}
, say, is the
h
that minimises:

\mathrm{CCV}(h,r)=\mathrm{R}\left(\hat{f}_{h}^{(r)}\right)-\bar{\theta}_{r}(h)+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}(K)h^{2}\bar{\theta}_{r+1}(h)+\frac{1}{24}\left(6\mu_{2}^{2}(K)-\delta(K)\right)h^{4}\bar{\theta}_{r+2}(h)

(17)

where,

\mathrm{R}\left(\hat{f}_{h}^{(r)}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(r)}\ast K^{(r)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

and

\bar{\theta}_{r}(h)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}K^{(2r)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

with :
\delta(K)=\int_{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{R}}}x^{4}K(x)dx
.

4.6

Stute, 1992
proposed a so-called modified cross-validation (
\mathrm{MCV}
) in kernel density estimator. This method can be extended to the estimation of derivative of a probability density, the essential idea based on approximated the problematic term by the aid of the Hajek projection. The minimization criterion is defined by:

\mathrm{MCV}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\varphi^{(r)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(18)

where

\varphi^{(r)}(c)=\left(K^{(r)}\ast K^{(r)}-K^{(2r)}-\frac{\mu_{2}(K)}{2}K^{(2r+2)}\right)(c)

4.7
Trimmed cross-validation

Feluch and Koronacki, 1992
proposed a so-called trimmed cross-validation (
\mathrm{TCV}
) in kernel density estimator, a simple modification of the unbiased (least-squares) cross-validation criterion (
14
). We consider the following "trimmed" version of "unbiased", to be minimized with respect to
h
:

\mathrm{TCV}(h,r)=\frac{\mathrm{R}\left(K^{(r)}\right)}{nh^{2r+1}}+\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n(n-1)h^{2r+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\varphi^{(r)}\left(\frac{X_{j}-X_{i}}{h}\right)

(19)

where

\varphi^{(r)}(c)=\left[K^{(r)}\ast K^{(r)}-2K^{(2r)}1\left(|c|>\frac{c_{n}}{h^{2r+1}}\right)\right](c)

1(.)
denotes the indicator function and
c_{n}
is a sequence of positive constants, as
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}/h\rightarrow 0
, here we take
c_{n}=1/n
, for assure the convergence.
