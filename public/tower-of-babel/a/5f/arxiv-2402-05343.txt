A path method for non-exponential ergodicity of Markov chains and its application for chemical reaction systems
Minjoon Kim, Jinsu Kim

Abstract
In this paper, we present criteria for non-exponential ergodicity of continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space. These criteria can be verified by examining the ratio of transition rates over certain paths. We applied this path method to explore the non-exponential convergence of microscopic biochemical interacting systems. Using reaction network descriptions, we identified special architectures of biochemical systems for non-exponential ergodicity. In essence, we found that reactions forming a cycle in the reaction network can induce non-exponential ergodicity when they significantly dominate other reactions across infinitely many regions of the state space. Interestingly, special architectures allowed us to construct many detailed balanced and complex balanced biochemical systems that are non-exponentially ergodic. Some of these models are low-dimensional bimolecular systems with few reactions. Thus this work suggests the possibility of discovering or synthesizing stochastic systems arising in biochemistry that possess either detailed balancing or complex balancing and slowly converges to their stationary distribution.

Introduction

Continuous-time Markov chains on a discrete state space are commonly used stochastic processes that describe various interacting particle systems. When the state space of a non-explosive continuous-time Markov chain
X
is finite and irreducible, the time evolution of the probability distribution of the process converges to a limiting distribution
\pi

stationary distribution
, and in this case,
X
is
ergodic
. Furthermore
X
on a finite irreducible state space admits
exponential ergodicity
, which means that the convergence of the probability distribution to
\pi
is exponentially fast in time.

While finite state spaces are commonly used, especially for interacting particle systems such as the standard Ising models,
countably infinite state spaces often arise in stochastically modeled biochemical systems. For example, some biochemical species in the system can be constantly produced in many existing biochemical systems. In this case, the existence of such a stationary distribution is not always guaranteed, and once it exists, the convergence rate is not necessarily exponential in time. There have been many studies on analytic criteria for ergodicity and exponential ergodicity of continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space

Theorem 1.1

In this paper, we provided new sufficient conditions for showing non-exponential ergodicity of continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space. The conditions, loosely speaking, are met if the state space has a series of paths on which the Markov chain gets trapped. That is, the Markov chain stays long time on the paths with high probability once it enters.
We believe that the proposed conditions clearly illustrate the behavior of Markov chains that induce non-exponential ergodicity. Also since the path conditions are solely determined by the transition rates of the Markov chains, one can more easily verify the non-exponential ergodicity of a given Markov chain.

As the main application of this work, we used the path conditions to identify classes of biochemical systems that admit non-exponential ergodicity.
Searching for such classes of biochemical systems endowed with certain dynamical behaviors falls into the study of stochastic chemical reaction network theory, a subfield of probability and mathematical biology.
A chemical reaction network is a graphical configuration of the interactions of biochemical species. For instance

\displaystyle A+B\xrightarrow{\kappa_{1}}C\xrightarrow{\kappa_{2}}\emptyset.

The reaction

A

C

When such a reaction network is read as a graph, it is possible that the geometry and topology of the graph directly imply special dynamical behaviors of the associated stochastic dynamical system. The special dynamical behaviors of interest include non-explosivity, irreducibility, ergodicity, and exponential ergodcity. Hence one can classify biochemical systems using structural properties of the underlying reaction networks so that the classes admit certain dynamical features regardless of system parameters.

In this paper, we provided classes of reaction networks admitting non-exponential ergodicity. Those classes are characterized by structural conditions identified with our path conditions. Hence the structural conditions also induce the trapping of the associated Markov chains on some paths. More precisely, we showed that if a certain sequence of reactions forms a cycle (so-called a

Remarkably, we also showed that the proposed structural conditions can be used to find many different non-exponentially ergodic stochastic biochemical systems that are either detailed balanced or complex balanced (a special balancing condition yielding the existence of a Poissonian stationary distribution).
We also discussed a connection of our work to spectral gap and the congestion ratio that have been typically employed for showing exponential ergodicity.

3
Main application: non-exponential ergodicity of stochastic reaction networks

As the main application of Theorem
2.1

The goal of this application is to characterize the classes by structural conditions of the biochemical systems. To do that we first graphically represent the biochemical systems with reaction networks.
A reaction network is a graph that consists of the set of possible interactions among constituent chemical species. The nodes of the graphs are complexes composed with combinations of chemical species, and the edges of the graphs are reactions between complexes. We can define reaction networks as follows.

Definition 3.1

For each

3.1
Stochastic models for reaction networks

In this section we introduce a stochastic process associated with reaction networks, which is often utilized in biochemistry when the abundances of species are low. Assuming the typical spatially well-mixed status of the chemical systems, the stochastic dynamics will be defined as a continuous-time Markov chain on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
to model the copy numbers of species in the biochemical systems.

First, each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
is associated with an
intensity function

\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which quantifies the expected time for the reaction to occur. It is natural to assume that
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x)>0
only if
x\geq y
. This means that
y\to y^{\prime}
can take place only if enough reacting species exist.
Mass-action kinetics
is most common kinetics for defining
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}
, where the intensity of a reaction is proportional to the number of combinations of the present species engaged to the reaction. Specifically,
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}
’s are defined under mass-action kinetic as

\displaystyle\lambda_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}}(x)=\kappa_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}}\lambda_{y}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda_{y}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}!}{(x_{i}-y_{i})!}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\geq y_{i}\}},

(9)

for some positive parameters
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
. A reaction network together with a choice of a set of parameters
\kappa=\{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\}
is denoted by
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
and is called a
reaction system
. Once
\kappa
is incorporated in a reaction network, we denote each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
by
y\xrightarrow{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}}y^{\prime}
when the reaction network is graphically represented.
Throughout this paper, we assume mass-action for the stochastic models of reaction networks.

For a reaction system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
, let
X=\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the associated continuous-time Markov chain. Then the transition rate from state
z
to state
w
are given by

q_{z,w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
y^{\prime}-y=w-z\end{subarray}}\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(z).

(10)

Then for every
t\geq 0
,
(X(t))_{i}
represents the copy number of species
S_{i}
at time
t
. That is, the transition of
X
generated by a reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
yields the change of the copy numbers of species.
The generator
\mathcal{A}
of
X
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}V(z)=\sum_{z}q_{z,w}(V(w)-V(z))=\sum_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}}(z)(V(z+y^{\prime}-y)-V(z)),

(11)

where
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}

[
17
]
.
The state space of
X
with
X(0)=x
is denoted by
\mathbb{S}_{x}
, as mentioned before. Note that
q_{z,w}>0
for
z,w\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
if and only if there exists
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
such that
w-z=y\to y^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(z)=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\lambda_{y}(z)>0
(equivalently
z>y
). Thus the state space
\mathbb{S}_{x}
is determined by
\mathcal{R}
and the initial stat
x
independently from the choice of the parameters
\kappa
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
be a reaction network. Let
R=(y^{*}_{1}\rightarrow y^{*}_{2},y^{*}_{2}\rightarrow y^{*}_{3},\cdots,y^{*}_{M}\rightarrow y^{*}_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{R}
be an ordered list of reactions in
\mathcal{R}
. We call

a
strong tier-1 cycle
along a sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
if

1.

\lambda_{y^{*}_{1}}(x_{n})>0
for all
n
, and

2.

there exists
m_{0}
such that for each
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{y^{*}_{m_{0}}}(x^{m_{0}}_{n})\lambda_{y}(x^{m}_{n})}{\lambda_{y^{*}_{m}}(x^{m}_{n})}\right)=0,

(15)

whenever there exists
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\{y^{*}_{m}\to y^{*}_{m+1}\}
(regarding
y^{*}_{M+1}=y^{*}_{1}
), where
x^{m}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}y^{*}_{j+1}-y^{*}_{j}
for each

and
x^{1}_{n}=x_{n}
.

Remark 3.1
.

In (
15
), the term
\dfrac{\lambda_{y}(x^{m}_{n})}{\lambda_{y^{*}_{m}}(x^{m}_{n})}
quantifies how dominant

is at
x^{m}_{n}
. The reciprocal of the term
\lambda_{y^{*}_{m_{0}}}(x^{m_{0}}_{n})
is the expected holding time at
x^{m_{0}}_{n}
. Hence (
15
) relates the probability and the time for the associated Markov chain to stay in the paths generated by the reactions in
R
. This is the key condition for non-exponential ergodicity as shown in Example
3.2
.

Remark 3.2
.

We constructed a strong tier-1 cycle
R
with reactions forming a cycle in the reaction network in order to generate an active path by using the reactions in
R
. More precisely, we let
R=(y_{1}\to y^{\prime}_{1},y_{2}\to y^{\prime}_{2},\dots,y_{M}\to y^{\prime}_{M})
be an ordered list of reactions, and for some

we define

for each

generated by

is active if

is a cycle (i.e.

) and

. This is because if a reaction

is available at state

(i.e.
x>y
), then

is available at

since

.

The term ‘tier’ is derived from the concept of

[
3

[

,
5

6
,
9

. Although the properties of strong tier-1 cycles are closely related to tiers, we do not explicitly use the tiers for the main methods of this paper.

As one may expect, the reaction

in a strong tier-1 cycle is a dominant reaction at

. However, a strong tier-1 cycle has additional features. For a given strong tier-1 cycle

, we keep using the notation
x^{m}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}y^{*}_{j+1}-y^{*}_{j}

and

Remark 3.4
.

The results in Corollaries

–

3.4
Extension via coupling

We now extend the classes of non-exponentially ergodic stochastic reaction networks using coupling of two Markov chains.

For instance, consider two ergodic Markov chains
X
and
\bar{X}
associated with two reaction systems
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
and
(\bar{\mathcal{S}},\bar{\mathcal{C}},\bar{\mathcal{R}},\bar{\kappa})
, respectively. Suppose that
\bar{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
(hence
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
\bar{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
) and
\bar{\kappa}_{y\to y^{\prime}}=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
for each
y\to y^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}
. Then, by the random time representation
[
11
,
29
]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&X(t)=X(0)+\sum_{y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}Y_{y\to y^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\lambda_{y}(X(s))ds\right)(y^{\prime}-y)\\
&\bar{X}(t)=\bar{X}(0)+\sum_{y\to y^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}}Y_{y\to y^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\lambda_{y}(\bar{X}(s))ds\right)(y^{\prime}-y)\end{split}

(20)

in distribution, where
Y_{y\to y^{\prime}}
’s are independent unit Poisson processes.
In (
20
) we may abuse the notation in
y^{\prime}-y\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}
as the dimensions of
X
and
\bar{X}
might be different.
Note that as the unit Poisson processes
Y_{y\to y^{\prime}}
for
y\to y^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{R}}
are shared,
X
and
\bar{X}
are coupled. If each reactions in
\mathcal{R}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{R}}
cannot change the copy numbers of
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
and
(X(0))_{i}=(\bar{X}(0))_{i}
for
S_{i}\in\bar{S}_{i}
, then under the coupling (
20
), the returning time to
\bar{X}(0)
for
\bar{X}
is shorter than or equal to the returning time to
X(0)
for
X
almost surely, because additional reactions in
\mathcal{R}
cannot create and remove the species
S_{i}
’s in
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. Hence if
\bar{X}
is non-exponentially ergodic, then so
X
is by Theorem
1.1
.

This approach can be applied to general classes of Markov chains on countable state spaces. In the following proposition, we will consider two Markov chains
X
and
\bar{X}
defined on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{\bar{d}}
, respectively, with

. We enumerate the index of the coordinates as
\{1,2,\dots,\bar{d},\bar{d}+1,\dots,d\}
.
For any pairs of states
z,w\in\mathbb{S}_{x}
and
\bar{z},\bar{w}\in\bar{\mathbb{S}}_{\bar{x}}
, we denote by

and
\bar{q}_{\bar{z},\bar{w}}
the transition rates of

and
\bar{X}
, respectively. We will denote the state space of

and
\bar{X}
by

and

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that for any pair of states

and

in
\mathbb{S}_{x}
, the transition rate

satisfies that

\displaystyle q_{z,w}=\begin{cases}\bar{q}_{\bar{z},\bar{w}}\quad&\text{if $w_{i}-z_{i}=0$ for each $i\geq\bar{d}+1$,}\\

where states

Remark 3.5
.

As described for

and

in (

and

have the same transition rates if the transition only alter both the first
\bar{d}

, and each transition of

cannot alter the first
\bar{d}
coordinates and the remaining coordinates at the same time.

Proof.

Define
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\bar{d}}_{\geq 0}
such that
(\hat{X}(t))_{i}=(X(t))_{i}
for each
i\leq\bar{d}
and

.
For any states

and

such that

, let the returning time for

and

to

and

be

and

, we have

almost surely. Therefore if we show that
\bar{X}
and
\hat{X}
are equal in distribution, then non-exponential ergodicity of
\bar{X}
implies non-exponential ergodicity of
X
by Theorem
1.1
. To do that we show that the distributions of
\bar{X}(t)
and
\hat{X}(t)
solve the same Kolmogorov forward equation at each time

.

For the fixed initial state
x
, we define

. Similarly, for the fixed initial state
\bar{x}
, we define
\bar{p}(\bar{z},t)=P(\bar{X}(t)=\bar{z})
and
\hat{p}(\bar{z},t)=P(\hat{X}(t)=\bar{z})
.
Since
\bar{X}
is non-explosive, the probability distribution
\bar{p}(\bar{z},t)
is the unique solution of the following Kolmogorov forward equation
[
36
, Chapter 2.8]
: for each

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bar{p}(\bar{z},t)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\bar{z}^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathbb{S}}_{\bar{x}}\setminus\{\bar{z}\}}\bar{p}(\bar{z}^{\prime},t)\bar{q}_{\bar{z}^{\prime},\bar{z}}-\sum_{\bar{z}^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathbb{S}}_{\bar{x}}\setminus\{\bar{z}\}}\bar{p}(\bar{z},t)\bar{q}_{\bar{z},\bar{z}^{\prime}}.

(22)

Hence, it suffices to show that
\hat{p}(\bar{z},t)
is a solution to (

) for each

.

We first fix

[
15

II.3
]
.
By summing up the Kolmogorov forward equation of
X
over

, we have that by (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hat{p}(\bar{z},t)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{z\in U(\bar{z})}\left(\displaystyle\sum\limits_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}_{x}\setminus\{z\}}p(z^{\prime},t)q_{z^{\prime},z}-\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}_{x}\setminus\{z\}}p(z,t)q_{z,z^{\prime}}\right)

\bar{\mathcal{S}}

under a choice of the parameters

for

share the same reaction intensity. The reactions in

3.5
Non-exponentially ergodic biochemical systems with detailed balance and complex balance

In the previous section, we provide several structural conditions of reaction networks that ensure non-exponential ergodicity of the associated Markov models when it is ergodic. In this section, we highlight that using those structural conditions, we can construct non-exponentially ergodic, detailed balanced Markov chains. Since there are two concepts of detailed balancing: detailed balanced Markov chains and detailed balanced reaction systems, we define them precisely.

Definition 3.5
.

A reaction system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
under a choice of parameters
\kappa
is
detailed balanced
if
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
is reversible (i.e. if
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
then
y^{\prime}\to y\in\mathcal{R})
and there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
such that
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y_{i}}=\kappa_{y^{\prime}\to y}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y^{\prime}_{i}}
for each
y\rightleftharpoons y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.

The quantity
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y_{i}}
is the intensity of a reaction
y\xrightarrow{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}}y^{\prime}
in the deterministically modeled reaction system under mass-action kinetics
[
22
]
.

The associated Markov chain for a detailed balanced reaction system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
is detailed balanced (or
reversible
)
[
8
, Theorem 4.5]
, meaning that there exists a stationary distribution
\pi
such that
q_{z,w}\pi(z)=q_{w,z}\pi(w)
for each pair of states
z
and
w
. We can easily check that the reaction systems in Examples
3.2
,
3.7
, and
3.8
under an arbitrary parameter set
\kappa
are detailed balanced. Especially, as the reaction networks in Examples
3.7
and
3.8
constructed, using Corollary
3.8
we can add some reversible reactions to the reaction network (
12
),
\emptyset\rightleftharpoons A+B,B\rightleftharpoons 2B
, in order to construct other non-exponentially ergodic, detailed balanced Markov chains. For example, consider the following three reaction networks.

\displaystyle\emptyset\rightleftharpoons A+B,\quad B\rightleftharpoons 2B,\quad A\rightleftharpoons C+A\rightleftharpoons D+A.

(28)

\displaystyle C\rightleftharpoons\emptyset\rightleftharpoons A+B,\quad B\rightleftharpoons 2B,\quad A\rightleftharpoons D+A.

(29)

\displaystyle\emptyset\rightleftharpoons A+B,\quad C+B\rightleftharpoons B\rightleftharpoons 2B

(30)

Then, by Corollary
3.8
the Markov chains associated with the reaction networks in (
28
)–(
30
)
under any choices of parameter set
\kappa
are non-exponentially ergodic and detailed balanced.
Importantly, all the above examples are bimolecular systems (each complex consists of two or fewer species), which may exist in real life or can be synthesized.

There is another special balance condition for reaction systems: complex balance
[
18
,
22
,
26
,
27
]
.

Definition 3.6
.

A reaction system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
under a choice of parameters
\kappa
is
complex balanced
if
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
is weakly reversible and there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{y}}\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y_{i}}=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{y^{\prime}\to y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y^{\prime\prime}=y}\kappa_{y^{\prime}\to y^{\prime\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y^{\prime}_{i}}\quad\text{for each $y\in\mathcal{C}$.}

In other words, complex balanced reaction systems has a positive steady state
c
at which the in-coming and out-going reactions from and to
y
for each complex
y
are balanced. Interestingly, for any complex balanced reaction systems, the associated Markov chain is ergodic and the stationary distributions is a product form of Poisson distributions
[
7
,
8
]
. Detailed balancing is a special case of complex balancing.

Note that weakly reversible reaction networks can be easily formed with reactions forming cycles. Also, a well-known structural condition, so-called
zero deficiency
, yields complex balancing for any weakly reversible reaction networks under arbitrary choices of parameters. Using these two observations, we can construct many non-exponentially ergodic and complex balanced Markov chains using strong tier-1 cycles. Before giving relevant examples, we give more precise description about the zero deficiency. Deficiency
\delta
of a reaction network
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
is an index solely determined by its structure as it is defined by

, where
n
and
\ell
are the numbers of complexes and the connected components of
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
, respectively, and
s
is the dimension of a vector space
span\{y^{\prime}-y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Zero deficiency ensures that the reaction system is complex balanced if the underlying reaction network is weakly reversible
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.

The reaction networks in Examples
3.4
and
3.5
are weakly reversible and have zero deficiency. Using Corollaries
3.3
–
3.6
combined with Corollary
3.8
, we can create many more complex balanced reaction systems for which the associated Markov chains are non-exponentially ergodic. For example, consider the following reaction network.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\emptyset\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ 2A+B\\[-2.15277pt]
&\quad\displaystyle\nwarrow\hskip 28.45274pt\swarrow\quad\qquad A+C\rightleftharpoons A+D\\[-2.15277pt]
&\hskip 14.22636pt3A+2B\hskip 34.14322pt\end{split}

(31)

The subnetwork given by the first component in (

)
is complex balanced as it is weakly reversible and has zero deficiency as

, and

\displaystyle s=\text{dim}\{\text{span}\{(2,1,0,0),(1,1,0,0),(-3,-2,0,0),(0,0,-1,1),(0,0,1,-1)\}\}=3.

Also the subnetwork satisfies the conditions of Corollary
3.4
. The reactions in the second component of (

A
and

. Finally, the entire reaction network in (

) is weakly reversible and has zero deficiency. Hence the associated Markov chain is non-exponentially ergodic by Corollary
3.8
.

Without relying on Corollary

–

Consider the weakly reversible reaction network

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\emptyset\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ A+B\\[-2.15277pt]

We can also check that

5
Spectral gap and congestion ratios

In this section, we briefly discuss some connections of our results to the spectral gap and the congestion ratio of continuous-time Markov chains. The spectral gap of a continuous-time Markov chain
X
on a discrete state space
\mathbb{S}
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{A}
(equivalently the operator norm of the Dirichlet form defined as
\mathcal{E}(f,f)=-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}(\mathcal{A}f)(x)f(x)
), where
\mathcal{A}
is the infinitesimal generator of
X
. The spectral gap is used to study exponential ergodicity as a strictly positive spectral gap implies that the associated ergodic Markov chain is exponentially ergodic.
See
[
12
,
14
,
30
,
31
,
39
]
for more details about the spectral gap methods.

Hence we remark that the sufficient conditions for non-exponential ergodicity in this paper also imply ‘zero spectral gap’. We highlight again that the simple 2-dimensional detailed balanced Markov chain in Example
2.1
is non-exponentially ergodic so its spectral gap must be zero.
Furthermore, as we showed in Section
3.5
we can construct many detailed balanced, non-exponentially ergodic
Markov chains, which have zero spectral gaps.

In the literature of Markov chains, the concept of so-called the congestion ratio is commonly employed to show a positive spectral gap (see for instance
[
37
, Chapter 3]
or
[
13
, Section 3.3]
). For an ergodic Markov chain
X
defined on a discrete state space
\mathbb{S}
with the stationary distribution
\pi
, we first define a collection of paths,

\displaystyle\Gamma=\{\gamma_{z,w}\ \text{or}\ \gamma_{w,z}\ \text{but not both}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}z,w\in\mathbb{S}\ \text{with $z\neq w$}\},

where
\gamma_{z,w}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{|\gamma_{z,w}|})\subseteq\mathbb{S}
is a path such that
z_{1}=z
and
z_{|\gamma_{z,w}|}=w
and
\gamma_{w,z}
is defined similarly.
Then the congestion ratio is defined as for a given
\Gamma

\displaystyle C_{cr}=\sup_{\{(s,u)\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}q_{s,u}>0\}}\left\{\frac{1}{q_{s,u}\pi(s)}\sum_{z,w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(s,u)\in\gamma\in\Gamma}|\gamma|\pi(z)\pi(w)\right\},

(38)

where
\{z,w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(s,u)\in\gamma\in\Gamma\}
means the collection of pairs of two states
z
and
w
for which there exists either a path
\gamma_{z,w}=(z(1),\dots,z(|\gamma_{xy}|))\in\Gamma
such that
(z(i),z(i+1))=(s,u)
or a path
\gamma_{w,z}=(w(1),\dots,w(|\gamma_{w,z}|))\in\Gamma
such that
(w(i),w(i+1))=(s,u)
for some
i
. If
C_{cr}<\infty
for some
\Gamma
, then
X
is exponentially ergodic with
\rho=C_{cr}^{-1}
in (
3
)
[
13
, Section 3.3]
. Hence our sufficient conditions for non-exponential ergodicity also imply that
C_{cr}=\infty
for any choice of
\Gamma
. In
[
4
, Section 2.4]
, it was shown that the congestion ratio of the associated Markov chain defined in Example
2.1
is infinity for any
\Gamma
. Notably, it was shown that for any
\Gamma
either

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{q_{(n,0),(n+1,1)}\pi(n,0)}\sum_{z,w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(s,u)\in\gamma\in\Gamma}|\gamma|\pi(z)\pi(w)\right)=\infty\quad\text{or }

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{q_{(n+1,1),(n,0)}\pi(n+1,1)}\sum_{z,w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(s,u)\in\gamma\in\Gamma}|\gamma|\pi(z)\pi(w)\right)=\infty,

This means that the infinite congestion takes place on the active paths
\gamma_{n}=((n,0),(n+1,1),(n,0))
such that
F(\gamma_{n},\rho)>1
as we showed in Example
3.2
. This example, thus, shows that the conditions of Theorem
2.1
are reasonable in the sense that they cause infinite congestion on the state space.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we provided a path method for showing non-exponential ergodicity of continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space. We showed that on the series of paths satisfying the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, the Markov chain spends a long time on the paths, and in turn non-exponential ergodicity follows. By using this path method, we identified several classes of stochastic reaction networks that admit non-exponential ergodicity. Remarkably, the classes are characterized with only structural features independently from the reaction rate parameters. The structural conditions in Corollaries
3.3
–
3.6
combined with the coupling method shown in Corollary
3.8
were used to create detailed balanced Markov chains that are non-exponentially ergodic. Similarly, non-exponentially ergodic, complex balanced stochastic reaction systems can be made.

One possible generalizations of the main results of this paper is to consider reaction networks under general kinetics beyond mass-action kinetics.
By modifying the exponents of
{x_{n}}
in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, the same results of Theorem
4.1
and Corollaries
3.3
–
3.6
still hold for some class of general kinetics including generalized mass action kinetics
[
35
]
. Another possible generalization to consider reactions that do not form a cycle in the reaction network but can generate paths
\gamma_{n}
for which
F(\gamma_{n},\rho)>1
for small enough
\rho
. For example, in the reaction network

\displaystyle 2A+2B\to\emptyset\to A+B,\quad 2B\rightleftharpoons 3B,

the ordered list of reactions
(\emptyset\to A+B,\emptyset\to A+B,2A+2B\to\emptyset)
generates paths
\gamma_{n}=((n,0),(n+1,1),(n+2,2),(n,0))
that hold
F(\gamma_{n},\rho)>1
for small enough
\rho
.
In general, however, an arbitrary ordered list of reactions does not necessarily generate a path on the state space (e.g. the ordered list
(2A+2B\to\emptyset,2A+2B\to\emptyset)
cannot generate a path if the path is supposed to start at
(n,2)
), while an ordered list of reactions forming a cycle in the reaction network can always form a path on the state space as we discussed in Remark
3.2
.
Therefore, this generalization is not straightforward.

Our work can serve as a foundation for various future projects. First, in this paper we showed only the cases where non-exponential ergodicity is determined by structural conditions. On the contrary, for a given reaction network, we can study the cases where non-exponential ergodicity can be induced by a choice of parameters. This leads to a phase transition of biochemical systems in qualitative convergence rates (exponential convergence to non-exponential convergence and vice versa) by certain parameters such as temperature, pressure, or other external disturbances. Also, as ergodicity is an apparent condition for non-exponential ergodicity, we can study the conditions for ergodicity of the classes of reaction networks in Corollary
3.3
–
3.6
.

U_{n}
’s are independent and for each
m\geq 1
,
U_{2m-1}=U_{1}
in distribution, which is exponentially distributed with the rate
q
, and
U_{2m}=U_{2}
in distribution.
Noting that
p(t)=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\mathbb{P}(T_{2m}\leq t<T_{2m+1})
, we have for
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
such that Re
(\theta)>0
,

\displaystyle r(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{0}p(t)e^{-\theta t}dt=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathbb{P}(T_{2m}\leq t<T_{2m+1})e^{-\theta t}dt.

(40)

By Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathbb{P}(T_{2m}\leq t<T_{2m+1})e^{-\theta t}dt=\mathbb{E}\left(\int^{T_{2m+1}}_{T_{2m}}e^{-\theta t}dt\right)=\theta^{-1}\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta T_{2m}}-e^{-\theta T_{2m+1}})

(41)

Moreover, since
T_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}
and
U_{k}
’s are independent, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta T_{2m}})-\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta T_{2m+1}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta\sum_{k=1}^{2m}X_{k}})-\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta\sum_{k=1}^{2m+1}U_{k}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{k}})-\prod_{k=1}^{2m+1}\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{k}})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2k-1}})\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2k}})\right)\mathbb{E}\left(1-\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2m+1}})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q}{q+\theta}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)\right)^{m}\left(1-\frac{q}{q+\theta}\right),

(42)

where the last equality holds since
\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{1}})=\frac{q}{q+\theta}
and
\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2}})=\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)
.

By putting (
42
) into (
41
) and further putting (
41
) into (
40
), we get

\displaystyle r(\theta)=\frac{1}{q+\theta}\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\left(\frac{q}{q+\theta}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q+\theta}\left(1-\frac{q}{q+\theta}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(q+\theta-q\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)\right)^{-1},

where the third equality holds because the absolute value of
\frac{q}{q+\theta}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)
is smaller than
1
.
Then (
40
) follows for Re
(\theta)>0
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
V
be a positive random variable.
Then there exists
\infty\leq\sigma_{c}\leq 0
such that
\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta V})
converges for

(\theta)>\sigma_{c}
and diverges for Re
(\theta)<\sigma_{c}
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta V})
is analytic on Re
(\theta)>\sigma_{c}
and singular at

, that is,
\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta V})
does not have the analytic continuation whose domain contains the point
\sigma_{c}
.

Proof.

See
[
40
, Section
II.5
]
.
∎

\sigma_{c}
in Lemma
A.2
is called the
axis of convergence
. Now we turn to provide the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will prove the result using a contradiction. Suppose that there exist constants
B(x)>0
and
\eta>0
such that

\displaystyle|p(t)-\pi(x)|\leq B(x)e^{-\eta t},\text{ for each $t\geq 0$}.

(43)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{x}(e^{\rho\tau_{x}})=\infty
for all
\rho>0
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}(e^{-\theta\tau_{x}})
has the axis of convergence
\sigma_{c}=0
and is singular at
\theta=0
by Lemma
A.2
.
Note that
\tau_{x}=U_{1}+U_{2}
, and
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are independent. Thus we have that

\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta\tau_{x}})=\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{1}})\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2}})=\frac{q}{\theta+q}\mathbb{E}(e^{-\theta U_{2}})=\frac{q}{\theta+q}\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t).

By this, it is clear that
\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)
has the same axis of convergence
\sigma_{c}=0
and is singular at
\theta=0
.
To derive a contradiction, we will show that
\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)
has an analytic continuation with a domain containing
\sigma_{c}=0
.

By assumption (
43
),
p(t)=\pi(x)+g(t)
such that
e^{\alpha t}g(t)\to 0
, as
t\to\infty
for some
\alpha>0
. Hence for Re
(\theta)>0
, we have

\displaystyle r(\theta)=\int^{\infty}_{0}p(t)e^{-\theta t}dt=\int^{\infty}_{0}\pi(x)e^{-\theta t}dt+\int^{\infty}_{0}g(t)e^{-\theta t}dt=\frac{\pi(x)}{\theta}+\int^{\infty}_{0}g(t)e^{-\theta t}dt.

Furthermore,
\int^{\infty}_{0}g(t)e^{-\theta t}dt
exists and analytic on
\text{Re}(\theta)>-\alpha
. (See
[
28
, Proposition 12]
).
Thus
r(\theta)
has the analytic continuation on the domain
\{\theta\in\mathbb{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\text{Re}(\theta)>-\alpha,\theta\neq 0\}
with a simple pole at
\theta=0
.
Define
\tilde{r}(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\theta r(\theta)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)=1+\frac{\theta}{q}-\frac{\theta}{q\cdot\tilde{r}(\theta)}\quad\text{for Re$(\theta)>0$}.

(44)

Since
\tilde{r}(\theta)
is nonzero at
\theta=0
and analytic on Re
(\theta)>-\alpha
, we have that
\tilde{r}(\theta)\neq 0
on
B_{\epsilon}(0)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|\theta|\leq\epsilon\}
for some
\epsilon>0
. Thus

is well-defined and analytic on the domain
B_{\epsilon}(0)\cup\{\theta\in\mathbb{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\text{Re}(\theta)>0\}
, which implies that
\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\theta t}dF(t)
has an analytic continuation with a domain containing
\sigma_{c}=0
.
∎

Appendix B
Construction of a desired tier sequence under weak reversibility

Recall that in the proof of Theorem
2.1
it was important that the states
x(i)
’s in the path
\gamma_{\rho}
are in the irreducible state space
\mathbb{S}
. Based on this observation, we assumed
\{x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
in Theorem
3.2
. If this condition is removed, non-exponential ergodicity does not necessarily holds. We demonstrate this with an example.

Example B.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\kappa)
be the following reaction system.

\displaystyle\emptyset\xrightleftharpoons[\kappa_{2}]{\kappa_{1}}A+B

We can check that
(\emptyset\to A+B,A+B\to\emptyset)
is a strong tier-1 cycle along
\{(n,0)\}
. Let
x
be an arbitrary state in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
. For the associated Markov chain
X
with
X(0)=x
,
\mathbb{S}_{x}=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a-b=(x)_{1}-(x)_{2}\}
, which does not contain
\{(n,0)\}
. Hence Theorem
3.2
cannot be applied to this example.
Indeed,
X
is exponentially ergodic as the state space
\mathbb{S}_{x}
is finite for each

. There are many ways of verifying this. One can use that the congestion rate (
38
) is finite for any choice of paths
\Gamma
on a finite state space.

\triangle

As we have shown in Examples
B.1
and
4.1
, once a strong tier-1 cycle is identified with a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
, it is possible that
\{x_{n}\}
is not lying in the state space. In this case, if the state space has nice ‘reachability’, we can find another sequence
\{\bar{x}_{n}\}
by modifying
\{x_{n}\}
slightly so that

is still a strong tier-1 cycle. We demonstrate this idea with the same reaction network in (
37
).

Example B.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
be the following reaction network.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\emptyset\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ A+2B+C\\[-2.15277pt]
&\quad\displaystyle\nwarrow\hskip 42.67912pt\swarrow\qquad\qquad\quad 2C\rightleftharpoons 3C.\\[-2.15277pt]
&\hskip 19.91684pt2A+B+2C\hskip 34.14322pt\end{split}

As we have shown in Example

,

is a strong tier-1 cycle along

, where

.
Let

be an arbitrary state such that

. It is uncertain that

is in

. (indeed

and

We demonstrate the main idea in this example.
For simplicity let

be the initial state.
First rewriting

is a strong tier-1 cycle along

.

Now to show

from

\triangle

Lemma B.2

Let

Note that
\sup_{n}\|x_{n}-\bar{x}_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty

\displaystyle\lambda_{y}(\bar{x}_{n_{k}})\equiv\lambda_{y}(x_{n_{k}}+v)\equiv 0\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{y}(x_{n_{k}})}{\lambda_{y}(\bar{x}_{n_{k}})}=1.

Then we can show that

Lemma B.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
be a reaction network. For an ordered list of reactions
R=(y_{1}\to y_{2},y_{2}\to y_{3},\dots,y_{M-1}\to y_{M},y_{M}\to y_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, if
x\geq y_{1}
, then
x+y_{m}-y_{1}
is reachable from
x+y_{k}-y_{1}
for any distinct
m
and
k
.

Proof.

Note that
\lambda_{y_{k}\to y_{k+1}}(x+y_{k}-y_{1})>0
since
x+y_{k}-y_{1}>y_{k}
. Hence
x+y_{k+1}-y_{1}
is reachable from
x+y_{k}-y_{1}
. By repeating this process, we can show that
x+y_{m}-y_{1}
is reachable from
x+y_{k}-y_{1}
.
∎

We can notice that Lemma
B.4
is valid in cases where either
m>k
or
m<k
, as a result of the cyclic form of the reactions in
R
. Now, by using Lemma
B.4
, we can show a property of weak reversibility in finding an active path.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
be a weakly reversible reaction network. For two state

, then

.

Proof.

As

is reachable from

and
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(w^{\prime})>0
(i.e.

). By Definition
3.2
, there exist complexes

such that
(y_{1}\to y_{2},y_{2}\to y_{3},\dots,y_{M-1}\to y_{M},y_{M}\to y_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, where
y_{1}=y
and
y_{2}=y^{\prime}
. Then by Lemma
B.4
,
w^{\prime}
is reachable from
w
regarding
m=1,k=2,w^{\prime}=x=x+y_{m}-y_{1}
and

. By using the same argument, we can show that
w^{\prime\prime}
is reachable from
w^{\prime}

w^{\prime\prime}
is reachable from

∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let

an integer-valued matrix. Then the null space of

Proof.

We only use addition, subtraction, multiplication and division to get the null space of
