Knapsack with Small Items in Near-Quadratic Time
Karl Bringmann

Abstract
The Knapsack problem is one of the most fundamental NP-complete problems at the intersection of computer science, optimization, and operations research. A recent line of research worked towards understanding the complexity of pseudopolynomial-time algorithms for Knapsack parameterized by the maximum item weight w max w_{\textup{max}} and the number of items n n . A conditional lower bound rules out that Knapsack can be solved in time O ⁡ ( ( n + w max ) 2 − δ ) O((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2-\delta}) for any δ > 0 \delta>0 [Cygan, Mucha, Wegrzycki, Wlodarczyk’17, Künnemann, Paturi, Schneider’17]. This raised the question whether Knapsack can be solved in time O ~ ​ ( ( n + w max ) 2 ) \widetilde{O}((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2}) . This was open both for 0-1-Knapsack (where each item can be picked at most once) and Bounded Knapsack (where each item comes with a multiplicity). The quest of resolving this question lead to algorithms that solve Bounded Knapsack in time O ~ ​ ( n 3 ​ w max 2 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{3}w_{\textup{max}}^{2}) [Tamir’09], O ~ ​ ( n 2 ​ w max 2 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{2}w_{\textup{max}}^{2}) and O ~ ​ ( n ​ w max 3 ) \widetilde{O}(nw_{\textup{max}}^{3}) [Bateni, Hajiaghayi, Seddighin, Stein’18], O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ w max 2 ) O(n^{2}w_{\textup{max}}^{2}) and O ~ ​ ( n ​ w max 2 ) \widetilde{O}(nw_{\textup{max}}^{2}) [Eisenbrand and Weismantel’18], O ⁡ ( n + w max 3 ) O(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{3}) [Polak, Rohwedder, Wegrzycki’21], and very recently O ~ ​ ( n + w max 12 / 5 ) \widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{12/5}) [Chen, Lian, Mao, Zhang’23]. In this paper we resolve this question by designing an algorithm for Bounded Knapsack with running time O ~ ​ ( n + w max 2 ) \widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2}) , which is conditionally near-optimal. This resolves the question both for the classic 0-1-Knapsack problem and for the Bounded Knapsack problem.

Introduction

Knapsack is one of the most fundamental problems at the intersection of computer science, optimization, and operations research. It appeared as one of Karp’s original 21 NP-hard problems
[
30
]
and has been subject to an extensive amount of research, see, e.g., the book
[
32
]
.

In the 0-1-Knapsack problem we are given a weight budget
W
and
n
items, and for each item
i
we are given its weight
w_{i}
and its profit
p_{i}
. The goal is to select a set of items of total weight at most
W
and maximum total profit. Formally, 0-1-Knapsack is the following problem:

\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,\,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}.

A generalization of 0-1-Knapsack is the Bounded Knapsack problem, in which each item
i
also comes with a multiplicity
u_{i}
, and item
i
can be selected up to
u_{i}
times. This can be viewed as a compressed representation of a 0-1-Knapsack instance. In particular, any algorithm for Bounded Knapsack also solves 0-1-Knapsack. Formally, Bounded Knapsack is the following problem:

\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,\,0\leq x\leq u,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}.

There is a vast amount of literature on both problems.
When the input integers are small, of particular importance are pseudopolynomial-time algorithms, i.e., algorithms whose running time depends polynomially on
n
and the input integers.
A well-known example is Bellman’s dynamic programming algorithm from 1957
[
5
]
that solves 0-1-Knapsack in time
O(nW)
and Bounded Knapsack in time
\widetilde{O}(nW)
.

To cope with these hardness results, recent work studied the maximum weight of any item

as a parameter. This is especially interesting for Bounded Knapsack, where the parameter

and

This raised the question:
What is the optimal running time for Bounded Knapsack in terms of
n
and
w_{\textup{max}}

More precisely, what is the smallest constant

\widetilde{O}((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{c})

The same conditional lower bound that rules out time

O((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2-\delta})

\delta>0

[

Can Bounded Knapsack be solved in time
\widetilde{O}((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2})

1.1
Our Results

In this paper, we resolve the driving question by designing an algorithm for Bounded Knapsack that runs in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

Theorem 1
.

Bounded Knapsack can be solved by a deterministic algorithm in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

This resolves the driving question of a long line of research
[
39
,
4
,
3
,
20
,
36
,
28
,
16
]
. As mentioned above, our running time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
is conditionally near-optimal; more precisely an algorithm with running time
O((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2-\delta})
for any constant
\delta>0
would violate the
(\min,+)
-Convolution Conjecture
[
18
,
33
]
.
Our result adds to a small, but growing number of conditionally near-optimal pseudopolynomial-time algorithms for classic optimization problems, see the related work below.

Alternatively, in Theorem
1
we can replace
w_{\textup{max}}
by the maximum profit of any item
p_{\textup{max}}
.

Theorem 2
.

Bounded Knapsack can be solved by a deterministic algorithm in time
\widetilde{O}(n+p_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

Independent Work

Jin
[
27
]
independently also achieved running time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
as well as
\widetilde{O}(n+p_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. Let us give a brief comparison: In Jin’s favor, his
\widetilde{O}
hides only 4 logfactors, while our
\widetilde{O}
hides at least 7 logfactors. In our favor, (1) we handle Bounded Knapsack, while Jin’s algorithm only works for 0-1-Knapsack, and (2) our algorithm seems to be significantly simpler.

1.2
Technical Overview

Here we describe the tools used in our algorithm and their history.

Classic Proximity Bound

Let
(w,p,W)
be a 0-1-Knapsack instance sorted by non-increasing profit-to-weight ratios
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}/w_{n}
. Let
g
be the
maximal prefix solution
, i.e.,
g
picks the maximal prefix of the items that fits into the weight budget
W
.
Intuitively, an optimal solution
x^{*}
should not deviate too much from
g
, since any deviation means replacing an item with higher profit-to-weight ratio by an item with lower profit-to-weight ratio. Formally, a classic proximity bound shows that there exists an optimal solution
x^{*}
that differs from the maximal prefix solution
g
in
O(w_{\textup{max}})
entries. That is, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]}|x^{*}_{i}-g_{i}|=O(w_{\textup{max}})\quad\quad\text{and thus}\quad\quad\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}\cdot|x^{*}_{i}-g_{i}|=O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2}).

(CP)

This proximity bound was pioneered by Eisenbrand and Weismantel
[
20
]
. Their proof also works for Integer Linear Programs with more than one constraint and is based on Steinitz’ lemma; for Knapsack a simplified proof using a simple exchange argument can be found in
[
36
]
.
This proximity bound has been used in almost all recent work on pseudopolynomial-time and approximation algorithms for Knapsack.
We also make use of it, in order to reduce the Bounded Knapsack problem to the 0-1-Knapsack problem, as we explain next.

Reduction from Bounded Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack

We show the following reduction between the two Knapsack problems:
If 0-1-Knapsack on
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
, then Bounded Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

After establishing this reduction, to show our main result that Bounded Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
it suffices to show that 0-1-Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

We obtain this reduction along the lines of previous Bounded Knapsack algorithms, e.g.
[
20
,
36
]
.
In the following description for simplicity think of the starting point being a 0-1-Knapsack instance (the same reduction can also be efficiently implemented when starting from Bounded Knapsack).
Given a Knapsack instance, we construct the maximal prefix solution
g
.
For any weight
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
that are picked by
g
, we greedily pick all but the
\Theta(w_{\textup{max}})
least profitable items, and we remove the picked items from the instance.
Similarly, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
that are not picked by
g
, we remove all but the
\Theta(w_{\textup{max}})
most profitable items from the instance.
The classic proximity bound (
CP
) implies that some optimal solution is consistent with these choices.
The remaining instance has at most
\Theta(w_{\textup{max}})
items of each weight in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
, so we reduced the number of items to
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. So from now on we focus on 0-1-Knapsack with few items.

Additive Combinatorics

A fundamental result in additive combinatorics shows that for any set
A\subseteq[n]
of size
|A|\gg\sqrt{n}
the set of all subset sums of
A
contains an arithmetic progression of length
n
. This result was pioneered by Freiman
[
21
]
and Sárközy
[
37
]
and further improved by Szemerédi and Vu
[
38
]
and recently by Conlon, Fox, and Pham
[
17
]
.
A long line of work used variations of this result to design algorithms for dense cases of the Subset Sum problem
[
12
,
13
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
11
]
. Notably, the majority of this line of research assumes that the input is a set, and only recently this work has been generalized to multi-sets
[
11
]
.
In this paper we distill the following tool from
[
11
]
, which roughly states that two multi-sets of small integers
X,Y
have two subsets of equal sum if both the size of
X
and the sum of all elements of
Y
are sufficiently large.

Lemma 3

(Informal Version of Lemma
7
)
.

Let
X,Y
be multi-sets consisting of integers in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
, where each number in
X
has multiplicity at most
\mu
. If
|X|\gg(\mu\cdot w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}
and
\sum_{y\in Y}y\gg\mu\cdot w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/|X|
then there exist non-empty subsets of
X
and
Y
that have the same sum.

Proximity Bounds based on Additive Combinatorics

Results from additive combinatorics such as Lemma
3
can be used in exchange arguments to show proximity bounds. For Knapsack, this was first used by Deng, Jin and Mao to design approximation schemes
[
19
]
, and recently by Jin
[
28
]
and Chen, Lian, Mao and Zhang
[
16
]
to design pseudopolynomial-time algorithms. Our arguments follow the approach of Chen et al.
[
16
]
.

The basic exchange argument by Chen et al. is as follows.
Assume for simplicity that all items have distinct profit-to-weight ratios, i.e.,
p_{1}/w_{n}>\ldots>p_{n}/w_{n}
. Fix an optimal solution
x^{*}
, and consider a set of items
I_{X}
that are picked by
x^{*}
and a set of items
I_{Y}
that are not picked by
x^{*}
. Consider the multi-set of all weights of items in
I_{X}
, i.e.,
X=\{w_{i}:i\in I_{X}\}
, and similarly define
Y=\{w_{i}:i\in I_{Y}\}
. If
X
and
Y
satisfy the constraints of Lemma
3
, then there exist subsets
I^{\prime}_{X}\subset I_{X}
and
I^{\prime}_{Y}\subseteq I_{Y}
of equal total weight. Then removing
I^{\prime}_{X}
and adding
I^{\prime}_{Y}
to
x^{*}
maintains the total weight, and thus yields a new feasible solution
x^{\prime}
. Now suppose that all items in
I_{Y}
have a higher profit-to-weight ratio than all items in
I_{X}
; since we sorted by decreasing profit-to-weight ratio this condition is equivalent to
\max(I_{Y})<\min(I_{X})
. Then this exchange strictly increases the profit, contradicting the choice of
x^{*}
as an optimal solution. We can use this contradiction to argue that one of the assumptions of Lemma
3
must not be satisfied, and by ensuring that
|X|=|I_{X}|
is large, we can conclude that the sum of all weights of the items in
I_{Y}
must be small. By picking
I_{Y}
as a set of items picked by
g
but not by
x^{*}
, we obtain that these items have a small total weight. By a symmetric argument on the items picked by
x^{*}
but not by
g
, we obtain that
x^{*}
and
g
differ by a small total weight. This can yield a proximity bound similar to (
CP
).

The question now is how to choose the set
I_{Y}
and ensure that there always exists a corresponding set
I_{X}
so that the basic exchange argument can be applied.
To this end, Chen et al. partition the items into 6 parts, and show applicability on each part.
We devise a more intricate partitioning into a polylogarithmic number of parts, proving the following novel proximity bound.

Theorem 4

(Informal Version of Theorem
12
)
.

Given a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
with maximal prefix solution
g
, in time
\widetilde{O}(n)
we can construct a partitioning
I_{1}\cup\ldots\cup I_{k}=[n]
into
k=O(\log^{2}n)
parts together with a proximity bound
\Delta_{j}
for each part
I_{j}
such that for every
j
we have (1) any optimal solution
x^{*}
differs from
g
by total weight at most
\Delta_{j}
among the items
I_{j}
, and (2) the
\Delta_{j}
’s are small, more precisely the product of
\Delta_{j}
and the number of distinct weights in
I_{j}
is
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

This theorem is the main novelty of our algorithm.
For comparison, note that the unique partitioning consisting of
k=1
part satisfies
|\supp(w(I_{1}))|\cdot\Delta_{1}\leq\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{3})
, since
|\supp(w(I_{1}))|\leq w_{\textup{max}}
holds trivially and
\Delta_{1}:=\Theta(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
works by the classic proximity bound (
CP
).
This bound for
k=1
was recently improved to
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2.5})
by Jin
[
28
]
.
Chen et al. were the first to study partitionings into
k>1
parts, constructing a partitioning into 6 parts with
|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j}\leq\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2.4})

[
16
, Lemmas 13, 16 and 17]
. We improve this bound to
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
, while using more parts.

The Knapsack Algorithm

Given Theorem
4
, designing an algorithm that solves 0-1-Knapsack in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
follows from standard techniques.
We split each part
I_{j}
into the items
I_{j}^{-}
picked by
g
and the items
I_{j}^{+}
not picked by
g
.
By Theorem
4
, any optimal solution
x^{*}
for the whole instance
(w,p,W)
uses weight at most
\Delta_{j}
in the set
I^{+}_{j}
, and thus we can restrict our attention to weights
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in set
I^{+}_{j}
. Similarly,
g-x^{*}
uses weight at most
\Delta_{j}
in the set
I^{-}_{j}
, and thus we can restrict our attention to weights
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in set
I^{-}_{j}
. For simplicity, in this overview we focus on the set
I^{+}_{j}
.
We further split each set
I^{+}_{j}
according to item weights; this splits
I^{+}_{j}
into sets
I^{+}_{j,1},\ldots,I^{+}_{j,\ell_{j}}
where
\ell_{j}
is the number of distinct weights among the items in
I^{+}_{j}
.
All items in
I^{+}_{j,\ell}
have the same weight, so a standard greedy algorithm computes the optimal profit for each weight
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in time
O(\Delta_{j})
.
The total time to run the greedy algorithm on all sets
I^{+}_{j,1},\ldots,I^{+}_{j,\ell_{j}}
is
O(\Delta_{j})
times the number of distinct weights of items in
I_{j}^{+}
, which by guarantee (2) of Theorem
4
is
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. Repeating this for all
1\leq j\leq k=O(\log^{2}n)
still runs in time
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

It remains to combine the results of the greedy algorithm. Here we use that the result of the greedy algorithm is a concave sequence, and the
(\max,+)
-convolution of an arbitrary sequence and a concave sequence can be computed in linear time. It follows that combining the results of the greedy algorithm can be done in roughly the same running time as it took to compute them, up to logarithmic factors.
Hence, in total the running time is still
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

We remark that (apart from Theorem
4
) this algorithm is quite standard:
For
k=1
the same algorithm was described in
[
36
, Lemma 2.2]
, for larger
k
essentially the same algorithm was described in
[
16
, Lemma 6]
, and its ingredients (i.e., solving equal weights by a greedy algorithm and combining concave sequences) have been used repeatedly, see, e.g.,
[
31
,
14
,
3
,
36
,
16
]
.

Summary

We described an algorithm that solves Bounded Knapsack in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
and thus resolves the driving question of a long line of research
[
39
,
4
,
3
,
20
,
36
,
28
,
16
]
.
Our main novelty is a proximity bound that splits the set of items into a polylogarithmic number of parts, so that within each part the optimal solution cannot deviate much from the maximal prefix solution.
We highlight that additive combinatorics is a crucial tool in our algorithm, thus showing once more that the recent trend of using additive combinatorics for algorithm design is very fruitful.

1.3
Further Related Work

Further Parametrizations

So far we discussed Knapsack algorithms that parametrize by the number of items
n
and the weight parameters
W
or
w_{\textup{max}}
.
The analogous parameters for profits are
OPT
(the profit of an optimal solution) and
p_{\textup{max}}
(the maximum profit of any item). It is essentially symmetric to parametrize by weight or by profit parameters, see, e.g.,
[
36
, Section 4]
. However, interesting new algorithms are possible when parametrizing by both weight and profit parameters, see the line of work
[
35
,
4
,
8
,
9
]
. To mention an exemplary result, 0-1-Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(nw_{\textup{max}}p_{\textup{max}}^{2/3})

[
9
]
. What we present in this paper is superior in case
w_{\textup{max}}\ll p_{\textup{max}}
, but worse in case
w_{\textup{max}}\approx p_{\textup{max}}\gg n
.

Unbounded Knapsack

The Unbounded Knapsack problem is the same as Bounded Knapsack but with
u_{i}=\infty
for each item
i
. That is, every item can be selected any number of times, which makes the problem much simpler.
The same conditional lower bound as for Bounded Knapsack ruling out time
O((n+w_{\textup{max}})^{2-\delta})
for any
\delta>0
also works for this problem
[
18
,
33
]
.
Running time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
has been achieved for Unbounded Knapsack independently by Jansen and Rohwedder
[
25
]
and Axiotis and Tzamos
[
3
]
. Their algorithms are significantly simpler than our algorithm for Bounded Knapsack, in particular they do not require additive combinatorics.
Chan and He further improved the running time to
\widetilde{O}(nw_{\textup{max}})

[
15
]
.

Subset Sum

The Subset Sum problem is the special case of 0-1-Knapsack with
w_{i}=p_{i}
for each item
i
. Here the weight bound
W
is typically called the target

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we use the notation
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We use
\widetilde{O}
-notation to hide logarithmic factors, where
\widetilde{O}(T):=\bigcup_{c\geq 0}O(T\log^{c}T)
. Formally, the problems studied in this paper are defined as follows.

Problem 5

(Bounded Knapsack)
.

Given profits
p\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, weights
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and multiplicities
u\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, as well as a weight budget
W\in\mathbb{N}
, compute
\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},0\leq x\leq u\}
.

0-1-Knapsack is the same as Bounded Knapsack but all multiplicites are 1, i.e.,
u_{i}=1
for all
i
.

Problem 6

(0-1-Knapsack)
.

Given profits
p\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, weights
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and a weight budget
W\in\mathbb{N}
, compute
\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
.

Throughout the paper, we denote by
w_{\textup{max}}
the maximum weight
w_{\textup{max}}=\max_{i}w_{i}
.

For a given 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
we usually assume that the items are sorted according to non-increasing profit-to-weight ratio
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}/w_{n}
.
Having fixed such an ordering, an important object in this paper is the
maximal prefix solution

g\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that selects a maximal feasible prefix of all items.
More precisely,
g
is defined as follows. Let
t\in[n+1]
be maximal such that
w_{1}+w_{2}+\ldots+w_{t-1}\leq W
. Then let
g_{1}=\ldots=g_{t-1}=1
and
g_{t}=\ldots=g_{n}=0
.
2
2

2

We think of
g
as being a greedy solution, therefore we use the letter
g
to denote it. However, note that a reasonable greedy algorithm would try to pick additional items and thus not necessarily pick a prefix of items, so greedy solutions are generally not the same as the maximal prefix solution.

Note that
g
is a feasible solution, since it has weight
w^{T}g=w_{1}+w_{2}+\ldots+w_{t-1}\leq W
. It is also maximal, in the sense that adding item
t
would make it infeasible. In particular, the maximal prefix solution has weight
w^{T}g\in(W-w_{\textup{max}},W]
(unless
W\geq w_{1}+\ldots+w_{n}
, in which case we have
t=n+1
).
Clearly, the maximal prefix solution can be computed in time
\widetilde{O}(n)
.

For a given Bounded Knapsack instance
(w,p,u,W)
of size
n
we also usually assume non-increasing profit-to-weight ratios
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}/w_{n}
.
The maximal prefix solution
g\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
can then be defined analogously to the 0-1 case, by letting
t\in[n+1]
be maximal such that
\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}u_{i}\cdot w_{i}\leq W
and setting
g_{1}:=u_{1},\ldots,g_{t-1}:=u_{t-1}
and
g_{t+1}=\ldots=g_{n}:=0
and
g_{t}:=\lfloor(W-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}u_{i}\cdot w_{i})/w_{t}\rfloor
(if
t\leq n
). Again,
g
is feasible and maximal and can be easily computed in time
\widetilde{O}(n)
.

A long line of work used additive combinatorics to design algorithms for dense cases of the Subset Sum problem

In what follows, after introducing the necessary notation, we phrase this tool in Lemma

Let

finite multi-set of positive integers

if
\mu(x;X)\leq\mu(x;Y)
holds for all

.
Furthermore, we define the following concepts:

•

Support
\supp(X)
:
The set of all distinct integers contained in

\supp(X)=\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid\mu(x;X)\geq 1\}

•

Size
|X|

The number of elements of

|X|=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\mu(x;X)

•

Maximum
\max(X)
:
The maximum number in

\max(X)=\max\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid\mu(x;X)\geq 1\}
.

•

Multiplicity
\mu(X)
:
The maximum multiplicity of

\mu(X)=\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\mu(x;X)
.

•

Sum
\Sigma(X)
:
The sum of all elements of
X
, i.e.,
\Sigma(X)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}x\cdot\mu(x;X)
.

Lemma 7

(Additive Combinatorics Tool)
.

Let
X,Y
be multi-sets consisting of integers in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
. If

|X|\geq 1500\cdot(\log^{3}(2|X|)\mu(X)w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma(Y)\geq 340000\log(2|X|)\mu(X)w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/|X|,

then there exist non-empty
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
with equal sum
\Sigma(X^{\prime})=\Sigma(Y^{\prime})
.

We remark that
[
16
, Lemmas 11 and 12]
are similar to Lemma

[

]

The result is also similar in spirit to
[

The proof builds on the following definitions and theorems by Bringmann and Wellnitz

Definition 8

(Definitions 3.1 and 3.2 in

Let

Write

Note that the assumption

4
Proximity Bound

The main result of this section is the following theorem, which provides a partitioning of the items of a 0-1-Knapsack instance into few parts, along with a certain proximity bound for each part.
In more detail, the theorem states that we can partition the items into a polylogarithmic number of sets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
with corresponding proximity bounds
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k}
such that (1) inside each part
I_{j}
the optimal solution deviates from the maximal prefix solution by a total weight of at most
\Delta_{j}
, and (2) the
\Delta_{j}
’s are not too large, in particular the product of
\Delta_{j}
and the number of distinct weights in
I_{j}
is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

In what follows for a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
and an index set
I\subseteq[n]
we denote by
w(I)
the multi-set
\{w_{i}:i\in I\}
, and by
\supp(w(I))
the support of this multi-set, i.e., the set of distinct weights appearing among the items in
I
.

Theorem 12

(Item Partitioning with Proximity Bounds)
.

For a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
with distinct profit-to-weight ratios
p_{1}/w_{1}>\ldots>p_{n}/w_{n}
, let
g
be the maximal prefix solution, and let
x^{*}
be an arbitrary optimal solution.
Given
w,p,W
, in time
\widetilde{O}(n)
we can compute a partitioning
I_{1}\cup\ldots\cup I_{k}=[n]
and proximity bounds
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k}
with
k=O(\log^{2}n)
such that for each
1\leq j\leq k
we have

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}w_{i}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq\Delta_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j}\leq 300000000\log^{3}(2n)\cdot w_{\textup{max}}^{2}.

We remark that this theorem is the main novelty of our algorithm. The classic proximity bound (
CP
) can be used to show that the unique partitioning consisting of
k=1
part satisfies
|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j}\leq\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{3})
. This bound was recently improved to
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2.5})

[
28
]
.
Chen et al. were the first to study partitionings into
k>1
parts. They constructed a partitioning into at most 6 parts with
|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j}\leq\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2.4})

[
16
, Lemmas 13, 16 and 17]
. We improve this construction to
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
12
.
We start by presenting algorithm
SingleStep
(U)
, which is given a set

and constructs a set
I\subseteq U
and a distance bound
\Delta
with the same guarantees as in Theorem

(see Lemma

to obtain the partitioning promised by Theorem

.
Algorithm

(U)
works as follows:

Algorithm 1

(U)
, with input

1:

We consider the class of weights of largest multiplicity, defined as follows.
For every

that is a power of 2, we define

{\cal W}_{\hat{m}}:=\big\{\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]:\hat{m}/2<|\{i\in U:w_{i}=\hat{w}\}|\leq\hat{m}\big\}.

Let

be the largest power of 2 such that

is non-empty, and let

.

We let

. We split

(i.e., the items with largest profit-to-weight ratio), and

(i.e., the items with smallest profit-to-weight ratio). Finally, we define

to be the larger of the sets

We set the distance bound to

.

In what follows we analyze this algorithm.
We will use the notation introduced in the above algorithm throughout the rest of this section.
We start with a simple observation on the size of

Observation 13

We have

Note that

∎

We have

The following auxiliary lemma prepares the proof of the main proximity guarantee.

Lemma 15
.

There are no non-empty sets
A\subseteq\{i\in[n]:x^{*}_{i}=0\}
and
B\subseteq\{i\in[n]:x^{*}_{i}=1\}

\max(A)<\min(B)
that have the same sum of weights
\Sigma(w(A))=\Sigma(w(B))

Proof.

Towards a proof by contradiction, assume that sets

Let
x^{\prime}
be the solution obtained from

by removing the items

Since

Since

of any item

of any item in

We can now prove the main guarantee of algorithm

: On the items in

Lemma 16
.

We have
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq\Delta
.

Proof.

We split the proof into several cases.

Case 1:
|I|\leq 6000(\log^{3}(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}
.

We square this inequality and rearrange it to
|I|\leq 36000000\log^{3}(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}}/|I|

\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq w_{\textup{max}}|I|
. Combining these two inequalities yields
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq 36000000\log^{3}(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/|I|=\Delta
.

Case 2:
|I|>6000(\log^{3}(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}
.
We split this case into further subcases as follows.

Case 2.1:
I=I^{+}
.

Case 2.1.1:
\sum_{i\in J^{+}\setminus I^{+}}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq|J^{+}\setminus I^{+}|/2
.

Let

and consider its multi-set of weights
X=w(I_{X})
.
We claim that

satisfies the assumption of Lemma
7
, i.e.,
|X|\geq 1500(\log^{3}(2|X|)\cdot\mu(X)\,w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}
. We prove this claim in the remainder of this paragraph. Note that
|X|=|I_{X}|\geq|J^{+}\setminus I^{+}|/2
, since all items

have
g_{i}=0
and thus by the assumption of Case 2.1.1 at least half of these items have

, where we used

This yields

|X|\geq|I|/4\geq 1500(\log^{3}(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2}\geq 1500(\log^{3}(2|X|)\cdot\mu(X)\,w_{\textup{max}})^{1/2},

where we used the assumption of Case 2,
|X|\leq n
, and

satisfies the assumption of Lemma
7

Let
I_{Y}:=\{i\in I^{+}:x^{*}_{i}=1\}
and consider its multi-set of weights

Suppose that
\Sigma(Y)
satisfies the assumption of Lemma

to

and

and

\Sigma(X^{\prime})=\Sigma(Y^{\prime})

The corresponding subsets

\max(I_{X})<\min(I_{Y})

.

Therefore,
\Sigma(Y)
cannot satisfy the assumption of Lemma

\Sigma(Y)\leq 340000\log(2|X|)\mu(X)w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/|X|.

Now we again use

\Sigma(Y)\leq 1360000\log(2n)\cdot m\,w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/|I|\leq\Delta.

Note that since

We thus obtain

Case 2.1.2:
\sum_{i\in J^{+}\setminus I^{+}}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|>|J^{+}\setminus I^{+}|/2

Let
I_{X}=\{i\in J^{+}\setminus I^{+}:x^{*}_{i}=1\}

The rest of the argument is exactly as in the previous case, and we arrive at

.

Let
I_{Y}:=\{i\in[n]:g_{i}=1,x^{*}_{i}=0\}
and consider its multi-set of weights

Suppose that

Lemma 17
.

Algorithm

runs in time

Proof.

This is straightforward. With one pass over all items in

∎

We are now ready to prove our main proximity result Theorem

Proof of Theorem

Starting with

to obtain

and remove

from

to obtain

, until

. For details, see the following pseudocode.

Algorithm 2

Construction of the partitioning guaranteed by Theorem

1:

U_{1}:=[n]

2:

for

j=1,2,\ldots

do
:

3:

(I_{j},\Delta_{j}):=\textsc{SingleStep}(U_{j})

4:

5:

If

To see that this procedure terminates, observe that for non-empty

It is easy to see that

are selected from the remaining items

The proximity bounds for each

By Lemma

It remains to show that

.
To this end, for any non-empty set

we define the potential
\phi(U):=\log_{2}(m_{U})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(|J_{U}|)\rceil
(where
m_{U},J_{U}
are defined in lines

and

Observe that

, since

is a power of 2.
Also observe that
\phi(U)\leq O(\log^{2}n)
, since
m_{U}\leq 2n
and
|J_{U}|\leq|U|\leq n
.
We claim that
\phi(U_{j+1})<\phi(U_{j})
holds for any

, see Claim
18
.
This yields
0\leq\phi(U_{k})<\phi(U_{k-1})<\ldots<\phi(U_{1})\leq O(\log^{2}n)
, and thus

.
It remains to prove the claim.

Claim 18
.

We have
\phi(U_{j+1})<\phi(U_{j})
for any
1\leq j<k
.

Proof.

We use the notation
m_{j}:=m_{U_{j}}
and
J_{j}:=J_{U_{j}}
for any
j
.
Since
U_{j+1}\subseteq U_{j}
we have
m_{j+1}\leq m_{j}
. We consider two cases: in one case
m_{j}
decreases by a factor 2 and in the other case
m_{j}
stays the same and
|J_{j}|
decreases by a factor
3/4
. Thus, in both cases the potential decreases by at least 1. More details follow.

Case 1:
m_{j+1}<m_{j}
.
Since
m_{j},m_{j+1}
are powers of 2 we then have
\log_{2}(m_{j+1})\leq\log_{2}(m_{j})-1
. Since
\log_{2}(m_{U})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil
is the dominant term in the definition of
\phi(U)
, we clearly have
\phi(U_{j+1})<\phi(U_{j})
.
More formally we can argue as follows. Since
|J_{j}|\geq 1
and
|J_{j+1}|\leq n
we have

\displaystyle\phi(U_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\log_{2}(m_{j+1})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(|J_{j+1}|)\rceil

\displaystyle\leq(\log_{2}(m_{j})-1)\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil

\displaystyle<\log_{2}(m_{j})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil\leq\log_{2}(m_{j})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(|J_{j}|)\rceil=\phi(U_{j}).

Case 2:
m_{j+1}=m_{j}
.
In this case we observe that
J_{j+1}\subseteq J_{j}\setminus I_{j}
since
U_{j+1}=U_{j}\setminus I_{j}
.
Using that
I_{j}\subseteq J_{j}
and
|I_{j}|\geq|J_{j}|/4
by Observation
13
, we obtain
|J_{j+1}|\leq|J_{j}|-|I_{j}|\leq\frac{3}{4}|J_{j}|
. Thus, the potential drops:

\displaystyle\phi(U_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\log_{2}(m_{j+1})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(|J_{j+1}|)\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(m_{j})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(\tfrac{3}{4}|J_{j}|)\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\log(m_{j})\cdot 2\lceil\log_{4/3}(n)\rceil+\lceil\log_{4/3}(|J_{j}|)\rceil-1=\phi(U_{j})-1.

In both cases we have
\phi(U_{j+1})<\phi(U_{j})
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Now that we proved Claim

we finished the proof of Theorem

.

Algorithmic Ingredients

In this section, after gathering some algorithmic ingredients in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
, in Section
5.3
we present an algorithm for 0-1-Knapsack that is given a partitioning and proximity bounds as in Theorem

5.1
MaxPlusConv with a Concave Sequence

A fundamental subroutine of all recent Knapsack algorithms is the
(\max,+)
-convolution operation, or MaxPlusConv for short, which is defined as follows.

Problem 19

(MaxPlusConv)
.

Given two sequences
x=x[0..n]
and
y=y[0..m]
with entries in
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\}
, compute the sequence
z=z[0..n+m]
with
z[k]=\max\{x[i]+y[k-i]:0\leq i\leq k\}
. Here out-of-bounds entries of
x
and
y
are interpreted as
-\infty
. We denote this operation by
z=x\star y
.

MaxPlusConv is equivalent to the analogous problem MinPlusConv (with
\min
replacing
\max
and
\infty
replacing
-\infty
in the above definition).
3
3

3

This equivalence follows from negating all entries of
x
and
y
, and negating the entries of the result
z
.

MaxPlusConv and MinPlusConv are central problems in the area of fine-grained complexity theory
[
40
]
. On two sequences of length
n
they can be solved naively in time
O(n^{2})
, and a popular conjecture postulates that they cannot be solved in time
O(n^{2-\delta})
for any constant
\delta>0

[
18
,
33
]
.

While in general MaxPlusConv is conjectured to require essentially quadratic time, on certain structured instances faster algorithms are known.
Here we make use of the special case where one of the sequences is concave, as has also been used, e.g., in
[
36
,
14
,
3
,
31
,
16
]
.

Definition 20
.

We say that a sequence
y\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\})^{m}
is
concave
if there exists an offset
h\in\mathbb{N}
and a length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that (1) the entries
y[0],y[h],\ldots,y[\ell\cdot h]
are finite, (2) all other entries of
y
are
-\infty
, (3) we have
y[i\cdot h]-y[i\cdot h-h]\geq y[i\cdot h+h]-y[i\cdot h]
for all
1\leq i<\ell
.

Lemma 21

(MaxPlusConv with Concave Sequence)
.

Given an arbitrary sequence
x\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\})^{n}
and a concave sequence
y\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\})^{m}
, we can compute their MaxPlusConv
x\star y
in time
O(n+m)
.

Proof.

This is a standard application of the SMAWK algorithm
[
2
]
. For completeness we present the argument, following the presentation in
[
36
]
.
For each remainder
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,h-1\}
we separately compute the entries of
z=x\star y
at indices that have remainder
r
modulo
h
(i.e., for a fixed
r
our goal is to compute the entries
z[r],z[r+h],z[r+2h],\ldots
of
z
).
We define the matrix
M\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\})^{\lceil(n+m)/h\rceil\times\lceil n/h\rceil}
with
M[i,j]=x[j\cdot h+r]+y[(i-j)h]
, where out-of-bounds entries of
x
and
y
are interpreted as
-\infty
. We do not explicitly construct the matrix, but we can compute every entry of
M
in constant time. Observe that the matrix
M
is inverse-Monge, i.e.,

\displaystyle M[i,j]+M[i+1,j+1]

\displaystyle=x[j\cdot h+r]+y[(i-j)h]+x[(j+1)\cdot h+r]+y[(i-j)h]

\displaystyle\geq x[j\cdot h+r]+y[(i-j)h+h]+x[(j+1)\cdot h+r]+y[(i-j)h-h]

\displaystyle=M[i+1,j]+M[i,j+1].

Therefore, the SMAWK algorithm
[
2
]
is applicable and computes all row maximas of
M
in total time
O((n+m)/h)
. Observe that the maximum of the
i
-th row of
M
is equal to
z[i\cdot h+r]
. Running this algorithm for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,h-1\}
yields all entries of
z
and takes total time
O(n+m)
.
∎

5.2
Greedy Algorithm for Equal Weights

0-1-Knapsack is particularly easy to solve if all items have the same weight, because then the greedy algorithm yields an optimal solution. In this situation we can even solve the instance for every weight budget
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq W
in total time
O(W)
, as shown by the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 22

(0-1-Knapsack with Equal Weights)
.

There is an algorithm
EqualWeights
(w,p,W)
that, given a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
with
w_{1}=\ldots=w_{n}
and
p_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}
, in time
O(W)
computes the sequence
y=y[0..W]
with entries

y[W^{\prime}]=\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x=W^{\prime},\,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}\quad\quad\text{for any $0\leq W^{\prime}\leq W$}.

Proof.

The following simple greedy strategy is optimal in case of equal weights, and it clearly runs in time
O(W)
.

Algorithm 3

EqualWeights
(w,p,W)
, assumes that all weights in
w
are equal and
p
is sorted

1:

Initialize
y=y[0..W]
with
y[W^{\prime}]=-\infty
for all
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq W

2:

Set
y[i\cdot w_{1}]:=\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,\min\{n,\lfloor W/w_{1}\rfloor\}

3:

return

y

∎

5.3
Algorithm for 0-1-Knapsack with given Proximity Bounds

We are now ready to present an algorithm for 0-1-Knapsack that is given a partitioning of the items and for each part a proximity bound, as in Theorem
12
. For more details see the following lemma.
This result for
k=1
was proven in
[
36
, Lemma 2.2]
. Our lemma is very similar to
[
16
, Lemma 6]
, except that they partition the set of weights instead of the set of items.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose we are given a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
with distinct profit-to-weight ratios
p_{1}/w_{1}>\ldots>p_{n}/w_{n}
, a partitioning
I_{1}\cup\ldots\cup I_{k}=[n]
, and integers
\Delta_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\Delta_{k}
. Denote the maximal prefix solution by
g
. Assume that there exists an optimal solution
x^{*}
such that
\sum_{i\in I_{j}}w_{i}\cdot|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq\Delta_{j}
holds for all
j
.
Then we can solve the 0-1-Knapsack instance in time
O(n+k\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j})
.

Proof.

We start by presenting the intuition.
We split each part
I_{j}
into
I^{+}_{j}:=\{i\in I_{j}:g_{i}=0\}
and
I^{-}_{j}:=\{i\in I_{j}:g_{i}=1\}
.
The optimal solution
x^{*}
uses weight at most
\Delta_{j}
among the items in
I^{+}_{j}
, and thus we can restrict our attention to weights
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in
I^{+}_{j}
. Similarly,
g-x^{*}
uses weight at most
\Delta_{j}
among the items in
I^{-}_{j}
, and thus we can restrict our attention to weights
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in
I^{-}_{j}
. For simplicity, in this overview we focus on the set
I^{+}_{j}
.
We further split each set
I^{+}_{j}
according to item weights; this splits
I^{+}_{j}
into sets
I^{+}_{j,1},\ldots,I^{+}_{j,\ell_{j}}
where
\ell_{j}:=|\supp(w(I^{+}_{j}))|\leq|\supp(w(I_{j}))|
. All items in
I^{+}_{j,\ell}
have the same weight, so the greedy algorithm from Lemma
22
computes the optimal profits for each weight
0\leq W^{\prime}\leq\Delta_{j}
in time
O(\Delta_{j})
.
Note that the total time to run the greedy algorithm on each set
I^{+}_{j,\ell}
with weight bound
\Delta_{j}
is
O(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{j}}\Delta_{j})\leq O(\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j})
, plus time
O(n)
for initialization.

It remains to combine the results of the calls to the greedy algorithm. We implement the combination operation by the MaxPlusConv algorithm for concave sequences from Lemma
21
, and we show that the right ordering of combination operations increases the running time by only a factor
O(k)
. Hence, in total the running time is
O(n+k\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\supp(w(I_{j}))|\cdot\Delta_{j})
.
In what follows we make this intuition precise.

The following pseudocode presents our algorithm for 0-1-Knapsack.

Algorithm 4

(w,p,W)
, given an instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n

and integers

1:

Compute the maximal prefix solution

2:

Initialize sequences
z^{+}=(0)
and
z^{-}=(0)

3:

for

j=1,\ldots,k

do

4:

Compute the set

and write

5:

Compute the sets
I_{j,\ell}^{+}=\{i\in I_{j}:w_{i}=\bar{w}_{j,\ell},\,g_{i}=0\}
and
I_{j,\ell}^{-}=\{i\in I_{j}:w_{i}=\bar{w}_{j,\ell},\,g_{i}=1\}

in sorted order for all

.

7:

for

\ell=1,\ldots,\ell_{j}

do

8:

Compute

9:

Compute

using Lemma

10:

Truncate

to indices

11:

Compute

using Lemma

12:

Compute

using Lemma

6
Reduction to 0-1-Knapsack

In this section we use the classic proximity bound
[
20
]
to reduce Bounded Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack on
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items. The techniques used in this reduction are similar to prior algorithms for Bounded Knapsack, e.g.,
[
20
,
36
]
, but the formulation as a reduction is new.

Lemma 26

(Reduction)
.

If 0-1-Knapsack on
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items with distinct profit-to-weight ratios can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
, then Bounded Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

Proof.

We start with a proof overview.
We first present a reduction from 0-1-Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items.
To this end, consider a given 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
and its maximal prefix solution
g
.
For any weight
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
that are picked by
g
, we greedily pick all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
least profitable items.
Similarly, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
that are not picked by
g
, we remove all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
most profitable items.
The classic proximity bound
[
20
]
shows that there exists an optimal solution that is consistent with these choices.
The remaining instance has at most
4w_{\textup{max}}
items of each weight in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
, so we have a reduction to
O(w_{\textup{max}})
items.

A Bounded Knapsack instance can be converted to a 0-1-Knapsack instance by replacing each item
i
with multiplicity
u_{i}
by
u_{i}
copies of item
i
. Therefore, the same reduction as above also works starting from Bounded Knapsack. We show that this reduction can be implemented efficiently without explicitly blowing up the original Bounded Knapsack instance to a 0-1-Knapsack instance.
The result of the reduction then is a Bounded Knapsack instance with total multiplicity of all items
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. Now we can afford to explicitly list all copies of items to arrive at 0-1-Knapsack.

Finally, we scale up all profits by a large factor and then add small noise terms. This maintains the optimal solution and makes all profit-to-weight ratios distinct, showing the lemma.

In what follows we give the details of this reduction.

Reduction from 0-1-Knapsack

We first give a reduction from 0-1-Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items.
Consider a 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
of size
n
, and sort it by non-increasing profit-to-weight ratio
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}/w_{n}
.
We iterate over all weights
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, so in what follows fix the weight
\hat{w}
.
Denote by
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}
all indices of items of weight
\hat{w}
. Note that
p_{i_{1}}\geq\ldots\geq p_{i_{\ell}}
.
The maximal prefix solution
g
picks a prefix of these items, i.e., for some index
t
we have
g_{i_{j}}=1
for all
1\leq j\leq t
and
g_{i_{j}}=0
for all
t<j\leq\ell
.
Among the items of weight
\hat{w}
not picked by
g
we want to remove all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
most profitable items, i.e., we want to remove the items
i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}+1},i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}+2},\ldots,i_{\ell}
. Formally, we add all items
i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}+1},i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}+2},\ldots,i_{\ell}
to an initially empty set
I^{(0)}
that stores all removed items.
Similarly, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
picked by
g
we want to pick all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
least profitable items, i.e., we want to pick the items
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-2w_{\textup{max}}}
. Formally, we add all items
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-2w_{\textup{max}}}
to an initially empty set
I^{(1)}
that stores all picked items.
After performing this process for each weight
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, we arrive at some set of items
I^{(0)}
that are to be removed and some set of items
I^{(1)}
that are to be picked. We remember the total profit of the picked items
P:=\sum_{i\in I^{(1)}}p_{i}
and reduce the weight bound accordingly to
\bar{W}:=W-\sum_{i\in I^{(1)}}w_{i}
. Then we remove the coordinates in
I^{(0)}\cup I^{(1)}
from
w
and
p
. Call the resulting 0-1-Knapsack instance
(\bar{w},\bar{p},\bar{W})
, and denote its size by
\bar{n}:=n-|I^{(0)}|-|I^{(1)}|
. Note that at most
4w_{\textup{max}}
items of any weight in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
remain, so we have
\bar{n}\leq 4w_{\textup{max}}^{2}
.

We claim that the choices we have made are without loss of generality, i.e., there is an optimal solution
x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of the original 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w,p,W)
that selects the items in
I^{(1)}
and does not select the items in
I^{(0)}
. This implies,

\max\{\bar{p}^{T}x:\bar{w}^{T}x\leq\bar{W},x\in\{0,1\}^{\bar{n}}\}+P=\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}.

To prove the claim, consider all optimal solutions
x^{*}
that minimize
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|
, and among all such solutions pick an optimal solution
x^{*}
minimizing
\sum_{i\in I^{(0)}}x^{*}_{i}-\sum_{i\in I^{(1)}}x^{*}_{i}
.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
x^{*}
selects one of the items
i\in I^{(0)}
that we have removed. We consider two cases.

Case 1: There is an item
j\in[n]\setminus(I^{(0)}\cup I^{(1)})
of weight
w_{j}=w_{i}
that is not selected by
x^{*}
.
Then we can exchange item
i
by item
j
in
x^{*}
, i.e., we set
x^{*}_{i}=0
and
x^{*}_{j}=1
. Since both items have the same weight, this exchange maintains the weight
w^{T}x^{*}
. Since we removed items of weight
w_{i}
with the smallest profits, we have
p_{j}\geq p_{i}
. Thus, after this exchange
x^{*}
is still an optimal solution. Since this change does not increase
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|
and decreases
\sum_{i\in I^{(0)}}x^{*}_{i}-\sum_{i\in I^{(1)}}x^{*}_{i}
, we obtain a contradiction to the choice of
x^{*}
.

Case 2: All items
j\in[n]\setminus(I^{(0)}\cup I^{(1)})
of weight
w_{j}=w_{i}
are selected by
x^{*}
.

Let
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}
be all indices of items with weight
w_{i}
, and let
t
be such that
g
picks items
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t}
but not
i_{t+1},\ldots,i_{\ell}
. Since
i_{t+1},\ldots,i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}}\in[n]\setminus(I^{(0)}\cup I^{(1)})
, it follows that
x^{*}
selects all items
i_{t+1},\ldots,i_{t+2w_{\textup{max}}}
, and thus
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\geq 2w_{\textup{max}}
.
However, this contradicts the following classic proximity bound.

Lemma 27

(
[
20
,
36
]
)
.

We have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|g_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|\leq 2w_{\textup{max}}-1
.

Proof.

For completeness, we repeat the proof of this proximity bound as presented in
[
36
]
.
Note that
x^{*}
is maximal in the sense that no item can be added to it, and
g
is maximal in the sense that it selects a maximal prefix of the items. It follows that either
w^{T}x^{*}=w^{T}g=\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}
or
w^{T}x^{*},w^{T}g\in(W-w_{\textup{max}},W]
. Both yield

\displaystyle-w_{\textup{max}}<w^{T}(x^{*}-g)<w_{\textup{max}}.

(3)

Now consider the following process. Start with the vector
x^{*}-g
. We will move its entries to 0, while maintaining the inequalities (
3
). That is, in each step of the process, if the current sum
w^{T}(x^{*}-g)
is positive we reduce an arbitrary positive entry of
x^{*}-g
by 1, otherwise we increase an arbitrary negative entry by 1. During this process in no two steps we can have the same sum, as otherwise we could apply to
x^{*}
the additions and removals performed between these two steps, obtaining another solution
x^{\prime}
. Because at both steps we had the same sum, the weight does not change, i.e.,
w^{T}x^{\prime}=w^{T}x^{*}
, so
x^{\prime}
is feasible. Since every item selected by
g
but not
x^{*}
has no lower profit than any item selected by
x^{*}
but not
g
, the additions performed to obtain
x^{\prime}
from
x^{*}
have a higher average profit-to-weight ratio than the removals. Since both have the same total weight, the total profit does not decrease, i.e.,
p^{T}x^{\prime}\geq p^{T}x^{*}
, so
x^{\prime}
is also an optimal solution.
Thus, the new solution
x^{\prime}
is closer to
g
and still optimal, contradicting the choice of
x^{*}
.

Hence, the number of steps of this process is at most
2w_{\textup{max}}-1
. Observing that the number of steps is equal to
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x^{*}_{i}-g_{i}|
finishes the proof.
∎

In both cases we arrived at a contradiction, so
x^{*}
cannot select any item in
I^{(0)}
. The proof that
x^{*}
selects all items in
I^{(1)}
is symmetric. This finishes the reduction from 0-1-Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items.

Reduction from Bounded Knapsack

Note that there is a trivial reduction from a Bounded Knapsack instance
(w,p,u,W)
to an equivalent 0-1-Knapsack instance
(w^{\prime},p^{\prime},W)
that simply replaces every item
w_{i},p_{w},u_{i}
by
u_{i}
copies of the item
w_{i},p_{i}
. Combining this reduction with the reduction from the last paragraph (from 0-1-Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items) yields a reduction from Bounded Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items. However, a naive implementation of this reduction would require time
\Omega(\sum_{i}u_{i})
, which is not efficient enough. Therefore, we now describe how to implement the same reduction in an implicit way directly on Bounded Knapsack, running in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. This is essentially straightforward but we describe the details for completeness. We do not need to reprove correctness, as we merely present a different implementation of the same reduction.

Consider a Bounded Knapsack instance
(w,p,u,W)
of size
n
, and sort it by non-increasing profit-to-weight ratio
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq p_{n}/w_{n}
. Initialize
u^{(0)}=u^{(1)}=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
.
We iterate over all weights
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, so in what follows fix the weight
\hat{w}
.
Denote by
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}
all indices
i
such that
w_{i}=\hat{w}
. Note that
p_{i_{1}}\geq\ldots\geq p_{i_{\ell}}
.
The maximal prefix solution
g
picks a prefix of these items, i.e., for some index
t
we have
g_{i_{j}}=u_{i_{j}}
for all
1\leq j<t
and
g_{i_{j}}=0
for all
t<j\leq\ell
.

Among the items of weight
\hat{w}
not picked by
g
we want to remove all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
most profitable items, as described in more detail in what follows. If
u_{i_{t}}-g_{i_{t}}+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\ell}u_{i_{j}}\leq 2w_{\textup{max}}
there are at most
2w_{\textup{max}}
items of weight
\hat{w}
not picked by
g
, so there is nothing to do.
Otherwise, while
\ell>t
and
u_{i_{t}}-g_{i_{t}}+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\ell-1}u_{i_{j}}\geq 2w_{\textup{max}}
, we set
u^{(0)}_{i_{\ell}}:=u_{i_{\ell}}
, thereby marking all copies of the item

for removal, and we set
\ell:=\ell-1
.
This process stops with
\ell=t
or
u_{i_{t}}-g_{i_{t}}+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\ell-1}u_{i_{j}}<2w_{\textup{max}}
, and furthermore
u_{i_{t}}-g_{i_{t}}+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\ell}u_{i_{j}}\geq 2w_{\textup{max}}
. We then set
u^{(0)}_{i_{\ell}}:=u_{i_{t}}-g_{i_{t}}+(\sum_{j=t+1}^{\ell}u_{i_{j}})-2w_{\textup{max}}
, marking this many copies of item
i_{\ell}
for removal, which leaves us with exactly
2w_{\textup{max}}
items of weight
\hat{w}
that are not picked by
g
.

Similarly, among the items of weight
\hat{w}
picked by
g
we want to pick all but the
2w_{\textup{max}}
least profitable items, as described in more detail in what follows. If
\sum_{j=1}^{t}g_{i_{j}}\leq 2w_{\textup{max}}
there are at most
2w_{\textup{max}}
items of weight
\hat{w}
picked by
g
, so there is nothing to do.
Otherwise, set
a:=1
. While
a<t
and
\sum_{j=a+1}^{t}g_{i_{j}}\geq 2w_{\textup{max}}
, we set
u^{(1)}_{i_{a}}:=u_{i_{a}}
, thereby marking all copies of the item
i_{a}
for picking, and we set
a:=a+1
.
This process stops with
a=t
or
\sum_{j=a+1}^{t}g_{i_{j}}<2w_{\textup{max}}
, and furthermore
\sum_{j=a}^{t}g_{i_{j}}\geq 2w_{\textup{max}}
. We then set
u^{(1)}_{i_{a}}:=(\sum_{j=a}^{t}g_{i_{j}})-2w_{\textup{max}}
, marking this many copies of item
i_{a}
for picking, which leaves us with exactly
2w_{\textup{max}}
items of weight
\hat{w}
that are picked by
g
.

After performing this process for each weight
\hat{w}\in[w_{\textup{max}}]
, we arrive at some multiplicity vectors
u^{(0)}
marking items to be removed and
u^{(1)}
marking items to be picked. We remember the total profit of the picked items
P:=\sum_{i\in[n]}u^{(1)}_{i}\cdot p_{i}
and reduce the weight bound accordingly to
\bar{W}:=W-\sum_{i\in[n]}u^{(1)}_{i}\cdot w_{i}
. The we replace
u_{i}
by
u^{\prime}_{i}:=u_{i}-u^{(0)}_{i}-u^{(1)}_{i}
for all
i
. The remaining instance has total multiplicity
\sum_{i\in[n]}u^{\prime}_{i}\leq 4w_{\textup{max}}^{2}
, since we have at most
4w_{\textup{max}}
items left for each weight in
[w_{\textup{max}}]
.
Therefore, we can explicitly write down all copies of all these items to form an equivalent 0-1-Knapsack instance
(\bar{w},\bar{p},\bar{W})
of size
\bar{n}\leq 4w_{\textup{max}}^{2}
.

It remains to observe that this reduction computes exactly the same 0-1-Knapsack instance as the combination of the trivial reduction from Bounded Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack and the reduction from 0-1-Knapsack to 0-1-Knapsack with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items from the last paragraph.
This implies that we get an analogous correctness guarantee:

\max\{\bar{p}^{T}x:\bar{w}^{T}x\leq\bar{W},x\in\{0,1\}^{\bar{n}}\}+P=\max\{p^{T}x:w^{T}x\leq W,0\leq x\leq u,x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\}.

Finally, note that the running time of this reduction is
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
, as the main process runs in time
\widetilde{O}(n)
and writing down the 0-1-Knapsack instance with
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items takes time
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.

Distinct Profit-to-Weight Ratios

It remains to make the profit-to-weight ratios distinct. After sorting we can assume that
\bar{p}_{1}/\bar{w}_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\bar{p}_{\bar{n}}/\bar{w}_{\bar{n}}
. We replace
\bar{p}
by
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\bar{n}}
with
\bar{\bar{p}}_{i}=M\cdot\bar{p}_{i}+(\bar{n}-i)\cdot\bar{w}_{i}
for a sufficiently large integer
M
.
Note that for any
i<j
we have

\bar{\bar{p}}_{i}/\bar{w}_{i}=M\bar{p}_{i}/\bar{w}_{i}+(\bar{n}-i)>M\bar{p}_{j}/\bar{w}_{j}+(\bar{n}-j)=\bar{\bar{p}}_{j}/\bar{w}_{j},

where we used
\bar{p}_{i}/\bar{w}_{i}\geq\bar{p}_{j}/\bar{w}_{j}
and
i<j
. Therefore, all new profit-to-weight ratios are distinct.

It remains to show that any optimal solution
x\in\{0,1\}^{\bar{n}}
for the 0-1-Knapsack instance
(\bar{w},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{W})
is also an optimal solution for the instance
(\bar{w},\bar{p},\bar{W})
. To see this, note that for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{\bar{n}}
we have

\bar{\bar{p}}^{T}x=M\cdot\bar{p}^{T}x+\sum_{i\in[\bar{n}],x_{i}=1}(\bar{n}-i)\cdot\bar{w}_{i}.

Since profits are integers and
M
is sufficiently large, any increase in
\bar{p}^{T}x
weighs more than any change in the second summand. Therefore, a maximizer for
\bar{\bar{p}}^{T}x
is also a maximizer for
\bar{p}^{T}x
. (It suffices to set
M>\bar{n}^{2}w_{\textup{max}}
for this argument.)

Note that the reduction runs in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
. Hence, if 0-1-Knapsack on
O(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
items with distinct profit-to-weight ratios can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
then Bounded Knapsack can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
.
This finishes the proof of Lemma
26
.
∎

8
Open Problems

In this paper we presented an algorithm that solves Bounded Knapsack in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2})
, which matches a conditional lower bound. This raises several open problems.

•

Our algorithm has an impractical number of logarithmic factors. Indeed, the
\widetilde{O}
in our running time hides at least a factor
\log^{7}n
, where a factor
k^{2}=\log^{4}n
comes from the number of parts in our partitioning, and a factor
\log^{3}n
comes from the additive combinatorics bounds. The independent work by Jin
[
27
]
has a factor
\log^{4}w_{\textup{max}}
. Can these factors be improved?

•

Our algorithm has impractical constant factors. Indeed, in Theorem
12
we have a factor 300000000. As this is only slightly larger than the constant
\approx
17000000 in Theorem
10
, the main bottleneck are the constant factors in
[
11
]
. Reducing the constant to a practical value might require a significant change in the proof approach of
[
11
]
.

•

MaxPlusConv is conjectured to require essentially quadratic time in the worst case, but there are lower order improvements to time
n^{2}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}
(by combining a reduction to All-Pairs Shortest Paths
[
6
]
with an algorithm for the latter
[
41
]
). Are similar improvements possible for Bounded Knapsack or 0-1-Knapsack? That is, can these problems be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{2}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log w_{\textup{max}}})})
?

•

Can Bounded Knapsack or 0-1-Knapsack be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(nw_{\textup{max}})
? Our approach does not seem able to give such a running time bound.

•

Can Bounded Knapsack or 0-1-Knapsack be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(n+(w_{\textup{max}}+p_{\textup{max}})^{2-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
? Time
\widetilde{O}(n+(w_{\textup{max}}+p_{\textup{max}})^{1.5})
was recently shown for Unbounded Knapsack
[
8
]
.

•

For Subset Sum the same parametrization by the number of items
n
and the largest item
w_{\textup{max}}
is well studied. Recent algorithms run in time
\widetilde{O}(n+w_{\textup{max}}^{1.5})

[
28
,
16
]
, and a conditional lower bound rules out time
w_{\textup{max}}^{1-\delta}\cdot 2^{o(n)}
for any
\delta>0

[
1
]
, which leaves a gap between
w_{\textup{max}}
and
w_{\textup{max}}^{1.5}
. Can the ideas in this paper help to close this gap?
