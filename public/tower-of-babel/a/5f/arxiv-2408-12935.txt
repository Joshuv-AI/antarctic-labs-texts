AI Safety Landscape for Large Language Models: Taxonomy, State-of-the-art, and Future Directions
Chen Chen, Xueluan Gong, Ziyao Liu, Weifeng Jiang, Si Qi Goh, and Kwok-Yan Lam

Abstract
AI safety is an emerging field of critical importance for the secure adoption and deployment of AI systems. With the recent advancements in large language models (LLMs), the technological landscape surrounding the design, development, and deployment of AI systems has undergone significant change. The failure of AI systems at one organization, or AI risks undertaken by one organization, can propagate down the AI technology supply chain, affect the entire AI ecosystem, and potentially lead to collective failures and cause large-scale harm to society. In this paper, we propose a novel architectural framework for understanding and analyzing AI safety in the context of LLMs, defining its characteristics through three key perspectives: Trustworthy AI, Responsible AI, and Ecosystemic Safe AI . We provide a comprehensive review of current research and advancements in AI safety from these perspectives, identifying major challenges and outlining mitigation strategies. Additionally, we highlight potential future directions that warrant further exploration to advance AI safety research and, ultimately, strengthen public trust in digital transformation.

Introduction

AI holds immense potential to drive digital transformation, from personalized medical diagnosis to autonomous transport, thereby fostering economic growth and advancing scientific fields that benefit both people and the world
Chong et al. (2025)
.

While these systems enable digital transformation, they also pose risks that can negatively impact individuals, communities, society, and the environment
Clymer et al. (2024)
;
IMDA (2024)
. In particular, AI introduces a range of new ethical, legal, and governance challenges, including risks of unintended discrimination that may result in unfair outcomes, as well as concerns related to robustness, privacy and security, explainability, transparency, and algorithmic fairness.
With the rapid proliferation of AI and especially with the recent development of large language models (LLMs), the technology ecosystem behind the design, development, adoption, and deployment of AI systems has drastically changed.
AI systems are highly interdependent or at least heavily dependent on open-source models, whose failure may propagate down the AI technology supply chain and result in an unmanageable scale of negative impacts
Harush (2023)
;
Eiras et al. (2024)
.

The AI safety issue is further complicated and worsened by the worldwide trend of adopting shared
foundation models
, which are powerful AI models trained with massive amounts of real-world data and can generate high-quality content
Eiras et al. (2024)
.
Foundation models may risk generating undesirable content and may potentially be misused for harmful causes
Marchal et al. (2024)
. A recent incident, for example, involved the technology news outlet CNET, which used a generative AI system to produce personal finance articles, and an internal review later found factual errors in more than half of these AI-generated stories, prompting extensive corrections

Contributions of our Survey

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive survey of the AI safety research landscape for large language models. We introduce a novel framework that centers AI safety around three key architectural safety layers: Trustworthy AI, Responsible AI, and Ecosystemic Safe AI. Trustworthy AI focuses on ensuring that AI systems perform as expected, maintaining robustness and security, even in changing and adversarial conditions. Responsible AI underscores the ethical obligations of fairness, transparency, and accountability, ensuring that AI systems adhere to human-centric values and socially responsible practices.
Ecosystemic Safe AI prioritizes the protection of the entire AI ecosystem by ensuring alignment with societal values and preventing issues such as disinformation and misuse.

We comprehensively review state-of-the-art methods and identify key challenges within these dimensions. We also present various mitigation strategies, including technical, ethical, and governance measures, which aim to enhance AI Safety. Additionally, we present promising future research directions in AI Safety, such as constructing comprehensive evaluation frameworks, improving knowledge management, investigating underlying mechanisms, developing defensive AI systems, and proactively preparing defensive strategies for advanced AI systems.

Survey Methodology

We first collected papers by querying major scholarly databases (e.g., DBLP, Google Scholar) with predefined keyword combinations aligned with our scope. We then expanded the corpus via backward and forward snowballing from the reference lists and citations of key papers. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts using explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria (requiring a substantive technical contribution directly related to our topic and excluding short position pieces or tangential works), resolving disagreements through discussion. The majority of the referenced publications are within the recent 4 years, and the cut-off year is 2025.

2.1
AI Foundation Models

2.1.1
Language Models

Language Model (LM) is a probabilistic model that predicts a probability distribution
P(y)
of a sequence of tokens
y=y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{T}
, where
T
is the sequence length. Using the product rule of probability (a.k.a. the chain rule), this joint probability is decomposed into:

P(y)=P(y_{1})\cdot P(y_{2}|y_{1})\cdots P(y_{T}|y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})=\prod\limits_{t=1}^{T}P(y_{t}|y_{<t})

(1)

Typically, Language models obtain
P(y)
by autoregressively predicting the conditional probabilities
P(y_{t}|y_{<t})
, i.e., the probability distribution of
y_{t}
given the preceding context
y_{<t}
. In the generation process, the next token
y_{t}
at each step is determined by the model’s prediction
P(y_{t}|y_{<t})
. To enhance output performance, multiple decoding strategies are explored to improve the output performance
Shao et al. (2018)
. More decoding details are discussed in Section
2.2.3
.

Transformer architecture
Vaswani et al. (2017)
has become the de facto standard for language modelling. The architecture follows an encoder-decoder design, where the encoder and decoder modules consist of a stack of transformer blocks, each comprising a Multi-Head Attention layer and a feedforward layer, connected by layer normalization
Ba et al. (2016)
and residual connection modules
He et al. (2016)
. In practice, the architectures are implemented to be encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder models, depending on their use cases. These Transformer-based Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) have been applied in a wide range of downstream tasks, such as information retrieval, question answering, and text generation, often achieving state-of-the-art performance.

2.1.2
Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) extend from PLMs but contain many more parameters, usually billions (or more) of parameters, which are trained on massive amounts of diverse text data. Recent advanced LLMs usually adopt decoder-only architectures
Touvron et al. (2023b)
;
Zeng et al. (2023)
. With their immense capacity, LLMs exhibit remarkable “emergent abilities” that are not present in smaller-scale PLMs, i.e.,
in-context learning (ICL)
Brown et al. (2020)
, chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning
Wei et al. (2022b)
, and instruction following.
These emergent abilities make LLMs exceptionally capable and versatile, enabling them to perform a variety of tasks with notable performance.
At the same time, they introduce new security challenges. For example, in-context learning can be abused by prompt injection attacks
Liu et al. (2023c)
, and chain-of-thought and instruction-following capabilities can be steered for jailbreak attacks
Gong et al. (2025)
.

Examples of LLMs include proprietary models such as the ones from the GPT family (e.g., GPT-4 and GPT-5)

OpenAI (2022)
;
OpenAI (2023a)

and the PaLM family (e.g., PaLM and PaLM 2)
Chowdhery et al. (2023)
;
Anil et al. (2023)
, as well as open-source models like LLaMA2
Touvron et al. (2023b)
and ChatGLM
Zeng et al. (2023)
. These LLMs serve as foundations in LLM research and development.

Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) often build upon the capabilities of text-based LLMs by incorporating visual information, enabling them to process and generate both textual and visual content. These models typically consist of three key components: an LLM backbone, one or more visual encoders, and vision-to-language adapter modules. The LLM backbone, often from the open-source LLMs, such as LLaMa family or their derivatives like
Alpaca
Taori et al. (2023)
and Vicuna
Chiang et al. (2023)
, serves as the primary interface with the user. The visual encoders are specifically designed to extract relevant features from visual inputs and provide them to the LLMs. These signals are often encoded separately, with the vision-to-language adapters ensuring seamless interoperability between the visual and textual domains. This design enables MLLMs to effectively integrate information from both modalities, allowing them to handle tasks such as visual question answering, image captioning, and visual dialogue.

2.1.3
Other AI Foundation Models

Alongside LMs and LLMs, there are other prevalent types of AI foundation models.
One popular class is Diffusion Models (DMs), which are developed for image and video generation
Ho et al. (2020)
;
Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)
.
DMs operate by gradually adding noise to the input data in a series of steps (forward diffusion process), and then learning to reverse this process (reverse diffusion process) to generate new samples.
Notable examples include DALL-E
Ramesh et al. (2022)
and Stable Diffusion
Rombach et al. (2022)
for generating high-quality images from textual prompts, and Sora for video generation. Despite the popularity of these models, this paper primarily focuses on LLMs to maintain a concentrated and coherent scope. For research on the safety perspectives of DMs, please refer to
Qu et al. (2023)
.

2.2
Life-cycle of AI Foundation Models

The AI foundation model life-cycle comprises multiple key stages, i.e., pre-training, alignment, and inference. Risks and safeguards are presented throughout these stages, and understanding them is essential for safeguarding the development and deployment of AI foundation models.

2.2.1
Pre-training

Data Preparation

Data preparation refers to collecting a large amount of high-quality data from various sources, including general data like webpages, books, and dialogue text, as well as specialized data such as multilingual text, scientific publications, and code. Before pre-training, the collected data undergoes extensive preprocessing to remove low-quality, duplicate, and privacy-sensitive content. The preprocessed data is then carefully scheduled for pre-training, considering factors such as the proportion of each data source, known as
data mixture
Longpre et al. (2023)
, and the order in which different types of data are presented to the model, i.e.,
data curriculum
Xu et al. (2023a)
. According to
scaling laws
Kaplan et al. (2020)
, it is essential to align the volume of pre-training data with the size of the model, allowing the AI foundation models to have sufficient data sources to unlock their full potential.

Pre-training Strategy

During pre-training, AI foundation models are typically optimized with self-supervised objectives that predict missing or future tokens from raw text. Encoder-only models such as BERT adopt masked language modelling (MLM), where a subset of tokens is randomly masked and the model is trained to reconstruct them from context. Encoder–decoder models often use denoising sequence-to-sequence objectives, for example span corruption and reconstruction. Decoder-only LLMs are usually trained with left-to-right next-token prediction (autoregressive language modelling), learning to estimate
P(y_{t}\mid y_{<t})
over very large corpora. In multimodal settings, contrastive objectives (e.g., aligning image and text embeddings) and captioning-style generation objectives are also widely used to couple visual and textual representations.

Beyond the choice of objective, several key strategies are employed to optimize performance and efficiency during pre-training. Firstly, hyper-parameters such as batch size, learning rate, and optimizer are critical factors that need to be carefully selected. To adapt training dynamics and improve model convergence, AI practitioners tend to utilize dynamic batch sizes and learning rate schedulers. Secondly, advanced techniques such as gradient clipping and weight decay are applied to stabilize training and prevent model collapse. To address the challenges of limited computational resources, parallelism approaches
Huang et al. (2019)
, ZeRO (to reduce memory redundancy)
Rajbhandari et al. (2020)
, and mixed-precision training
Micikevicius et al. (2017)
are adopted to enhance efficiency. Finally, early performance prediction mechanisms, such as the predictable scaling used in GPT-4, can forecast model performance and detect issues at an early stage, helping to optimize the pre-training process and save computational resources.

2.2.2
Fine-tuning and Alignment

Supervised Fine-tuning

Supervised fine-tuning is an effective strategy for aligning AI foundation models with human values and desired behaviors. Unlike pre-training, which involves training on large-scale unsupervised data, supervised fine-tuning focuses on adapting these models using smaller annotated datasets. In the realm of LLM, supervised fine-tuning is also known as instruction tuning
Wang et al. (2023b)
, where models are refined to understand and process complex instructions. Research indicates that the diversity and quality of the fine-tuning dataset are crucial factors for successful fine-tuning
Zhou et al. (2023a)
. Exposing the model to such a well-curated dataset enhances its ability to generalize on previously unseen tasks and achieve better alignment.

Alignment Tuning

Another line of alignment approaches is alignment tuning, e.g., reinforcement learning from
human feedback (RLHF)
Ouyang et al. (2022)
. This technique starts by training a reward model to evaluate the quality of model outputs based on human preferences. After optimizing the reward model, a reinforcement learning algorithm, typically Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO)
Schulman et al. (2017)
, is employed to fine-tune the AI foundation model using the reward model’s feedback. RLHF has shown effectiveness in AI foundation model alignment and safety enhancement, however, its implementation is complex and potentially unstable due to intricate training procedures. To address these challenges, recent efforts have explored alternative approaches, such as learning human preferences through ranking objectives or in a supervised manner.
Recently, the concept of Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF)
Bai et al. (2022b)
;
Lee et al. (2023)
and Reinforcement Learning from Human and AI Feedback (RLHAIF)
Saunders et al. (2022)
;
Perez et al. (2023a)
are introduced to reduce human involvement.

2.2.3
Inference

The inference for AI foundation models involves choosing the optimal decoding strategies to generate coherent and context-aware output.
Greedy search
Shao et al. (2018)
selects the most likely token at each step, while sampling-based methods choose the next token based on its probability distribution. However, these basic methods may lead to suboptimal or repetitive outputs. To alleviate these issues, advanced decoding strategies have been developed for greedy search, such as
beam search
Freitag and Al-Onaizan (2017)
, length penalty, and diverse beam search. Similarly, for sampling-based methods, temperature sampling and contrastive decoding are introduced to further control randomness. Additionally, researchers have made efforts to improve decoding efficiency. Data transfer reduction aims to optimize GPU memory access and minimize memory fragmentation while decoding strategies optimization is designed to enhance the
sequential auto-regressive generation process
Leviathan et al. (2023)
.

2.3
Formulating AI Safety

In this section, we start with defining the AI system and its variant AI pipeline (Section
2.3.1
). Based on these concepts, we provide the principles of AI Safety and its formulation (Section
2.3.2
).

2.3.1
Definition of AI system

Despite the term “AI system” being widely used in academic publications and public discourse
Durante et al. (2024)
, the literature has yet to converge on a single, universally accepted definition that precisely delineates such a system. Some endeavors focus on developing foundation models, while recent efforts have emphasized the development of complex systems that integrate various AI modules, such as traditional machine learning, LLMs, and Agent-based AI
Mao and Shi-Kupfer (2023)
. These modules serve specific purposes within the system. Here we attempt to provide a comprehensive conceptualization of AI systems.
One notable example of AI foundation model is LLMs, which can process instructions and provide decision-making capabilities in textual form. AI foundation models often serve as core components within larger systems, enabling other components to function effectively.

AI System.

An AI system
S
involves a collection of interconnected AI or non-AI modules
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
. The interconnections are represented by the topology
\mathcal{G}
, where a specific connection
g_{ij}
indicates the information flow from module
M_{i}
to module
M_{j}
. Formally,

S=\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\mid g_{i,j}\in\mathcal{G}

(2)

It is noteworthy that the modules
M_{i}
can be AI-powered, such as AI foundation models, or non-AI-powered, e.g., frontend, database, and API. In practice, an AI system typically comprises at least one AI-powered module, supported by multiple non-AI-powered modules to handle auxiliary functionalities. Fig.
2
presents a commonly adopted architecture of an AI system , where the AI foundation model acts as a central module connecting the surrounding components for input-output interaction with the external environment.

Figure 2:
Relations between AI foundation model and AI systems.

While the topology within an AI system can be considerably intricate, real-world AI applications often exhibit less complexity. Typically, the modules within an AI system are arranged sequentially, such that the output of module
M_{i}
serves as the input of module
M_{i+1}
. In the context of AI Safety, one instance of this sequential framework is the Swiss Cheese Model
Reason (1990)
, which refers to multiple layers of defence that can either prevent or allow errors to pass through the system. We refer to this simplified system as AI Pipeline.

AI Pipeline.

An AI pipeline is a special form of an AI system, where the topology
\mathcal{G}
represents a sequential connection of modules, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{i,i+1}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
. As a result,
S
reduces to:

S=M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}\rightarrow\cdots M_{n}

(3)

where
\rightarrow
denotes the direction of information flow.

2.3.2
Definition of AI Safety

The field of AI Safety refers to theories, methodologies and practices that ensure safe AI foundation models and AI systems. When contemplating them as a black-box operations, they can be expressed as a function
S:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
represent input and output space respectively. We consider an AI system to satisfy AI Safety if it adheres to key principles and constraints on
\mathcal{Y}
and
S
during runtime. We conceptualize these guiding principles as follows.

AI Safety Principle I – Output Constraint
.
An AI system
S
is considered to comply with AI Safety Principle I if its output space
\mathcal{Y}
is disjoint from a set of prohibited outputs
\mathcal{Z}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}\bigcap\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{Z}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
for all
i
, where
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
is the unsafe output according to certain criteria.

Each
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
represents a category-specific prohibited output set, corresponding to a particular class of safety violations. Concretely,
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
may capture undesired outcomes, such as functional failures (e.g., hallucinated or factually incorrect outputs), ethical violations (e.g., biased, toxic, or sensitive content), or ecosystem-level harms (e.g., disinformation or misuse-enabling outputs). These categories are instantiated and discussed in detail in the subsequent sections. In particular, Table
2
provides a high-level operational overview of these category-specific output constraints.

AI Safety Principle II – Runtime Constraint
.
An AI system
S
adheres to AI Safety Principle II if for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the system state
S_{t}
at time
t
operates under a collection of predefined runtime requirements
R_{t}\in\mathcal{R}
.

The runtime requirements
R_{t}
may encode different forms of operational constraints, depending on the safety objectives and deployment context of the AI system. For example, an explainability-related requirement may specify that, at certain decision points, the system must provide a human-interpretable rationale for its outputs or otherwise restrict its operation. Importantly, since the persistent usage of AI systems may induce accumulative effects over extended periods, runtime requirements are often inherently time-sensitive. As a result,
R_{t}
often evolves in response to the system’s dynamic environment and observed usage patterns.

Principle I and Principle II establish essential safety constraints on AI systems, focusing on output and runtime operation, respectively. Principle I mandates that an AI system must avoid generating prohibited outputs. Principle II requires AI systems to operate within certain requirements, such as maintaining transparency and explainability. For example, consider an LLM-based medical assistant deployed in a hospital. Under Principle I, the assistant must not produce any output in the prohibited set
\mathcal{Z}
, which includes instructions for self-harm, discriminatory statements about patients, or treatment suggestions that contradict approved clinical guidelines. Under Principle II, the same system is required to operate under predefined requirements
R
. For example, the generated prescription-related information must be supported by transparent clinical inference procedures. The AI assistant is required to explicitly explain the uncertainty and escalate to human oversight when the case triggers safety filters. These detailed constraints
Z
and
R
may slightly vary between systems, depending on the specific safety needs of the design.

Trustworthy AI
.
Trustworthy AI requires an AI system
S^{T}
to function as intended, be resilient against dangerous perturbations and operate securely. Specifically, Trustworthy AI follows AI Safety Principle I where prohibited output set
Z^{T}
in Trustworthy AI represents failure cases of the normal function.

Responsible AI
.
Responsible AI highlights an AI system
S^{R}
to align with ethical principles and values. Responsible AI includes the scope of Trustworthy AI and requires additional AI Safety Principle I and II where prohibited output set
Z^{R}
denotes the outputs misaligned with ethical norms and the requirements
R^{R}
are transparency and explainability of the AI system.

Ecosystemic Safe AI
.

Ecosystemic Safe AI
refers to the objective of an AI system
S^{S}
to ensure its harmlessness to the entire AI ecosystems.
Ecosystemic Safe AI
includes the scope of Responsible AI and further mandates AI Safety Principle I where prohibited output set
Z^{S}
denotes the outcomes that are harmful to AI ecosystems.

Building upon these principles, we proceed to a formal definition of AI Safety. This definition establishes the scope for our discussion, identifying the specific safety considerations that fall within this paper.

AI Safety

Discussion.

These formulations provide conceptual bridges for the three safety layers. We further discuss the relation between these layers, their risk hierarchy and prioritization, and the role of human supervision within our formulation.

Relations Between the Three Safety Layers.

In this paper, we view AI safety through two general principles: (i) an AI pipeline’s outputs should avoid a prohibited set
\mathcal{Z}
(Principle I), and (ii) its execution should satisfy runtime requirements
R
(Principle II). In our framework, the three safety layers correspond to different ways of instantiating
(\mathcal{Z},R)
for LLM-based systems. Trustworthy AI emphasizes functional correctness and security. Responsible AI emphasizes ethical and normative safety perspectives. Ecosystemic Safe AI emphasizes emergent risks across the broader AI ecosystem and over time.

Risk Hierarchy and Prioritization.

Table
2
presents the operationalization of AI safety principles by incorporating an explicit assessment of severity and controllability across different risk levels.
At the model level, risks such as functional output correctness and robustness to input manipulation typically exhibit moderate to high severity but remain relatively controllable through improvements in training data, model architectures, decoding strategies, and targeted evaluation. As risks shift toward system-level integration, including safeguard mechanisms and tool-augmented workflows, severity increases while controllability declines, reflecting the increased fragility introduced by cross-module dependencies and emergent behaviors that are beyond the scope of model-centric defenses. Ecosystem-level risks consistently rank as high severity but low controllability. Constraints such as content authenticity, misuse resistance, and AI capability control arise beyond the boundary of any single model or system and often propagate across organizational and temporal scales. These risks cannot be fully mitigated by technical safeguards alone and instead require coordinated governance mechanisms, provenance infrastructures, and post-deployment oversight. Consequently, ecosystem-level risks exhibit substantially higher complexity, which requires more nuanced and multi-layered safety defenses than model- and system-level safeguards.

Support for Human Supervision.
While much of the existing literature focuses on automated safety mechanisms, human-in-the-loop (HITL) supervision remains indispensable. Human involvement provides essential preference and value signals during alignment processes (e.g., instruction tuning and RLHF), supports the curation and validation of safety evaluation and red-teaming pipelines. However, such human supervision is often overlooked and rarely systematically integrated into AI safety formulations. Our formulation addresses this gap by naturally supporting HITL supervision as a runtime safeguard in high-stakes deployment scenarios, through monitoring and override mechanisms.

Challenges to Trustworthy AI

In this section, we review the spectrum of risks associated with AI trustworthiness, focusing on how these risks can hinder the effectiveness and reliability of LLMs and their defence mechanisms. We start with
a discussion of the function correctness of AI systems in Section
3.1
and
an extensive literature review of safety issues induced by input perturbations in Section
3.2
. This research examines whether LLMs could function as intended under various input conditions. We then delve into threats from adversarial attacks, including jailbreak and prompt injection in Section
3.3
, which aim to bypass and undermine security measures. Additionally, we explore the safety concerns in different contexts, including vulnerabilities of multi-modal LLMs and system-level security, which are discussed in Section
3.5
and Section
3.4

Finally, although this section centers on risks that manifest at the inference phase, we also emphasize that many trustworthiness failures originate in earlier data preparation and training stages, such as data poisoning, annotation contamination, or synthetic sample interference (Section

3.1
Challenges to Functional Reliability

Functional reliability is a foundational requirement of Trustworthy AI, referring to an AI system’s capacity to consistently operate in accordance with its intended functionality. In practice, however, modern AI systems often exhibit unpredictable outputs, which undermine their reliability. Among these challenges, hallucination is one of the most severe and prevalent failure modes.

The concept of “hallucination” originates in the field of psychology. It denotes the perception of an illusion, implying a significant disconnection from reality
Macpherson and Platchias (2013)
. In the context of AI, researchers borrow this term to delineate a similar phenomenon, where AI models generate outputs that are inconsistent with factual information or deviate from the truth, leading to a disconnection from factuality and faithfulness
Maynez et al. (2020)
. The phenomenon of hallucination has been observed in various generative PLMs
Lee et al. (2018)
and the state-of-the-art LLMs
Bang et al. (2023)
. The presence of hallucinations in LLMs raises significant concerns, particularly in domains where precision is crucial and the consequences of inaccuracies can have far-reaching effects on individuals’ safety, such as healthcare, law, and engineering
Ahmad et al. (2023)
.

In the literature, the taxonomy of hallucination varies across different studies
Huang et al. (2023b)
;
Ji et al. (2023c)
;
Zhang et al. (2023b)
. However, these categories generally fall into two principal types: factuality hallucination and faithfulness hallucination
Huang et al. (2023b)
. The distinction between these two types is based on the nature of the “fact” that the generated text fails to align. Factuality hallucination regards the “fact” as external world knowledge, which is independent of the given context. In contrast, faithfulness hallucination refers to the “fact” as the information contained within the user input, context, or the previously generated text. Table
4
presents examples for both types of hallucinations.

The cause of hallucination in LLMs is extensively studied, with various studies attributing the issue to different factors. One primary factor identified is the quality and content of the pre-training datasets
Crawl (2007)
. These corpora often contain inaccurate or false information and may lack sufficient coverage of long-tail knowledge, potentially leading to the generation of hallucinated outputs
Weidinger et al. (2021)
. Another factor lies in the architectural design of LLMs. Despite the architecture of LLMs proving to be capable, however, in certain cases, unidirectional information flow and anomalies within the attention mechanisms
Chiang and Cholak (2022)
can be problematic and contribute to hallucination
Li et al. (2023c)
. Furthermore, during the decoding stage, hallucination may arise due to the introduction of randomness through sampling-based decoding strategies
Dziri et al. (2021)
and the limitation of softmax function
Yang et al. (2018)
. Finally, some research probes the knowledge stored in LLMs, observing that hallucination could be the result of knowledge shortcuts and knowledge recall failures. Both of these issues are related to the pre-training data but in contrasting ways.
Knowledge shortcut occurs when LLMs overly rely on pre-training data, memorizing the noisy co-occurrences of tokens without regarding the underlying truth
Kang and Choi (2023a)
. For example, when asked “The capital of Canada is ?,” a model may answer “Toronto” instead of “Ottawa” because “Canada–Toronto” co-occurs far more often than “Canada–Ottawa” in pre-training corpora. This reflects a shortcut that prioritizes surface statistics over factual relations
Kang and Choi (2023b)
.
In contrast, knowledge recall failures happen when LLMs disregard the knowledge in the pre-training corpus and fail to retain what they learned during pre-training
Zheng et al. (2023)
. For instance, when asked to list hallmark symptoms of a rare disease or to identify it from several clinical clues, an LLM may fabricate a seemingly plausible but wrong symptom profile or diagnosis. This essentially stems from the failure to recall the specific long-tail knowledge, rather than learned incorrect associations
Jiang et al. (2024)
.

Hallucination detection is crucial in assessing the veracity of outputs generated by LLMs. The research can be broadly categorized into two approaches based on the resources they rely on. The first category introduces external resources to aid in the detection process. Specifically, for detecting factuality hallucination, numerous studies apply techniques to retrieve evidence from web sources and knowledge bases
Vrandecic and Krötzsch (2014)
;
Xu et al. (2017)
. This evidence serves as a benchmark to verify the veracity of LLM outputs
Chen et al. (2023a)
. In the case of faithfulness hallucination detection, this category relies on specialized external metrics
Papineni et al. (2002)
, modules
Fabbri et al. (2022)
, or another LLMs
Chen et al. (2023b)
to assist in checking the level of consistency between the given input the and generated output. The second category centers on assessing the LLM’s own confidence in its outputs. Outputs characterized by lower confidence levels are assumed to have a higher risk of hallucination
Guerreiro et al. (2023)
. The confidence is often reflected through various indicators, such as the token probability distribution
Guerreiro et al. (2023)
, the LLMs’ evaluation
Luo et al. (2023)
, or the consistency observed across multiple outputs
Manakul et al. (2023)
.

Recently, a new form of hallucination called sycophancy has drawn significant research attention. Sycophancy refers to an undesired behavior where models prioritize agreeing with the user’s subjective preference over providing truthful and objective responses. This preference may include personal opinions, biases, and even factual mistakes expressed by users
Perez et al. (2023b)
. Sycophancy can manifest in two forms: prompt sycophancy and response sycophancy
Sharma et al. (2023)
. Prompt sycophancy occurs when the initial prompt contains misleading information that influences the model’s output, while response sycophancy happens when the user’s follow-up input sways the model’s previous stance. Table
5
provides examples of these two forms of sycophancy. Recent studies suggest that sycophancy may be a fundamental characteristic stemming from the training methodologies of these models, rather than a unique feature confined to particular systems
Sharma et al. (2023)
.

3.2
Challenges from Input Perturbations

In real-world applications, user input to an AI system may not always align with what is initially anticipated. This variability underscores the importance of the robustness of LLMs, which refers to their ability to maintain performance levels under a variety of circumstances.
In this section, we will review input perturbation attacks on traditional PLMs in Section
3.2.1
and introduce how these attacks are extended to LLM systems in Section
3.2.2
.

3.2.1
Input Perturbation Attacks on PLMs

The concerns of robustness in AI systems were first emphasized by
Biggio et al. (2017)
and
Szegedy et al. (2014a)
, which demonstrated that these applications are vulnerable to deliberately engineered adversarial perturbations. To identify adversarial examples in image classification, gradient-based techniques such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) were developed by adding trained perturbation. However, the discrete nature of text tokens prevents the direct application of these methods to NLP tasks. Consequently, attacks on NLP models generally involve a discrete perturbation scheme. This scheme aims to identify the textual elements that significantly impact model output and then implements targeted perturbation operations, such as adding, deleting, flipping, or swapping, on them.

As shown in Table
6
, perturbation methods can broadly be organized into four principal types: character-level, word-level, sentence-level, and token-level.
Character-level perturbation implies the manipulation of texts by introducing deliberate typos or errors in words, such as misspellings (e.g., turning “text” into “txet”) or the addition of extra characters (e.g., turning “focusing” into “focusingg”)
Li et al. (2019)
.
Token-level perturbations operate directly on the model’s discrete sub-word tokens. A prominent line of work searches for short token sequences, often called
universal adversarial triggers
, that are optimized in the model’s vocabulary space so that, when concatenated to many different inputs, they consistently force a target prediction or elicit a specific type of completion
Wallace et al. (2019a)
.
The triggers can be syntactically meaningless yet highly effective, such as the phrase “zoning tapping fiennes”.
Other token-level attacks exploit special symbols or control tokens (e.g., rarely used Unicode symbols or reserved separator tokens such as “
\#\#\#
”) whose presence the model has learned to associate with particular behaviours
Formento et al. (2021)
. Because they act at the vocabulary level, such token-level perturbations can be highly transferable across tasks and models, while remaining unobtrusive or semantically meaningless to human readers.
On the other hand, word-level perturbation focuses on substituting words with synonyms or contextually similar terms to mislead models
Li et al. (2020)
. This technique aims to maintain the overall meaning of the text while using alternative vocabulary. For example, replacing “summarize” with “outline” or inserting modifiers such as “not trivial” keeps the sentence fluent but subtly shifts the emphasis of the task.
The selection of substituted words may be determined by their gradient
Samanta and Mehta (2017)
or attention scores
Jin et al. (2020)
, while the similarity is usually measured using the metrics in the word embedding space
Alzantot et al. (2018)
, such as GloVe
Pennington et al. (2014)
.
Finally, sentence-level perturbation entails suffixing irrelevant or extraneous sentences to the end of prompts, with the intention of distracting models from the main context
Naik et al. (2018)
.
For example, appending a short label-like token (e.g., “true.”) or paraphrasing the whole instruction into a different but natural wording can change how the model interprets the task, often leading to different outputs while remaining innocuous to users.
An alternative methodology is to generate paraphrased adversaries using techniques such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) or encoder-decoder PLM
Zhang et al. (2019)
. It is noteworthy that these perturbation strategies are not mutually exclusive; thus, a multi-level perturbation approach can be implemented in a single adversarial example as long as the perturbations are imperceptible to humans
Liang et al. (2018)
.

3.2.2
Input Perturbation Attacks on LLMs

Similar to PLMs, LLMs are also sensitive to the variability of prompts. For instance, researchers recognize that semantically similar prompts can yield drastically different performance
Yang et al. (2023a)
. This observation raises questions about whether perturbations designed for PLMs might also be effective for LLMs. Initial studies have focused on evaluating ChatGPT’s robustness against adversarial samples using traditional benchmarks
Wang et al. (2023a)
. Furthermore, Zhao et al.
Zhuo et al. (2023)
specifically examine the robustness of LLMs for the task of semantic parsing. To provide a more comprehensive evaluation, Zhu et al.
Zhu et al. (2023b)
propose PromptBench, a systematic benchmark that comprises various adversarial prompts. The benchmark considers a variety of dimensions, including types of prompts (task-oriented, role-oriented, zero-shot, and few-shot), levels of attacks (character-level, word-level, sentence-level, and semantic-level), and diverse tasks and datasets (e.g., GLUE
Wang et al. (2019)
, MMLU
Hendrycks et al. (2021)
, etc.). The evaluation is conducted on various victim LLMs, such as Flan, Vicuna, and ChatGPT. This comprehensive testing suggests that adversarial prompts remain a significant threat to current LLMs, with word-level attacks proving the most effective. Recently, Xu et al.
Xu et al. (2023b)
introduce PromptAttack, a novel methodology that leverages an LLM to generate adversarial examples to attack itself. The attack prompt aggregates key information, e.g., original input, attack objective, and attack guidance, that are essential to derive the adversarial examples. This approach highlights the potential for LLMs to be used not only as victims but also as tools for generating adversarial prompts.

3.3
Threats from Safeguard-Bypassing Attacks

AI systems are designed to maintain normal, safe behavior and benign outputs, typically ensured through various safeguards
AI (2023b)
. These safety mechanisms are integrated into the functionality of AI systems and are expected to perform effectively.
However, adversarial attacks, such as jailbreak and prompt injection, aim to strategically undermine the effectiveness of these safeguards. This can lead to unexpected events, such as the generation of toxic content, dissemination of harmful information, or outputs that violate social norms and ethics
Deshpande et al. (2023)
. For LLMs, malicious actors may attempt to deliberately exploit vulnerabilities in LLMs to elicit such undesirable responses through techniques such as jailbreaking (section
3.3.1
) and prompt injection attacks (section
3.3.2
).

3.3.1
Jailbreak Attacks

LLMs are typically equipped with built-in safety and moderation features to prevent them from generating harmful or inappropriate content. However, malicious users may develop “jailbreaking” techniques, such as deliberately crafting manipulative jailbreak prompts, to penetrate or bypass these safeguards.
Weidinger et al. (2022)
By exploiting their vulnerabilities, a jailbroken LLM can be made to perform almost any requested task, regardless of potential dangers or ethical considerations. As LLMs become increasingly capable and knowledgeable, the risks associated with jailbreaking grow more severe, because greater amounts of harmful information become accessible for misuse by malicious users
Liu et al. (2023d)
.

Jailbreak prompts are typically collected from various sources, including websites (e.g., Reddit

, JailbreakChat

We review these taxonomies and re-organize the jailbreak prompts into three groups: simulation, output confinement, and under-generalization. Simulation attempts to assign the victim LLM a fictional role with special privileges or ”superpowers”, which allows it to override its limitations and bypass its safeguards. Output confinement sets restrictions on the response, such as requiring it to start with specific content or prohibiting it from generating certain phrases. 3.3.2
Prompt Injection Attacks

Prompt injection draws inspiration from traditional injection attacks, such as SQL injection
Boyd and Keromytis (2004)
and cross-site scripting (XSS)
Gupta and Gupta (2017)
attacks, where the payload is intentionally manipulated to inject malicious code into a program and consequently mislead its normal behavior. Similarly, in the context of LLMs, prompt injection leverages user input to inject information into elements outside the input field, such as the system prompt. The key difference between jailbreaking and prompt injection lies in their strategies for deceiving the system. Jailbreaking disguises malicious prompts as benign ones, while prompt injection camouflages user input as system-level instructions. Prompt injection can take two forms: goal hijacking and prompt leaking
Perez and Ribeiro (2022)
. Goal hijacking aims to shift the original goal of an LLM prompt to a new goal, and prompt leaking seeks to disclose the system prompt, which is not intended to be exposed. We provide examples of goal hijacking and prompt leaking in Table

. These prompt injection strategies can be introduced through user input, i.e., direct injection, or other external sources, i.e., indirect injection.

3.4
Vulnerabilities in Multi-modal LLMs

Figure 3:
Various attacks on Multi-modal LLMs. (a) Structure-based attack, (b) Perturbation-based attack, (c) Poisoning-based attack

MLLMs enhance the abilities of LLMs by seamlessly incorporating multi-modal information. This integration allows them to process and understand various channels, such as text, images, and audio, simultaneously
Goh et al. (2021)
.
However, this multi-modal capability also introduces additional vulnerabilities that attackers can exploit for malicious purposes
Shayegani et al. (2023)
. A straightforward method to deceive MLLMs involves using deceptive prompts
Zhu et al. (2023a)
, where the model is manipulated to respond to non-existing objects in the image
Qian et al. (2024)
, leading to hallucination. These prompt-based attack strategies are extensions of those used against LLMs. Recently, new forms of attacks unique to MLLMs have been explored.

One notable type of attack is structure-based, which manipulates the format and presentation of text within images to mislead MLLMs. A prevalent strategy in this category, particularly for vision-language models like Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) models
Radford et al. (2021)
, is the typographic attack. This method aims to induce misclassification of images by intentionally overlaying misleading text onto them
Goh et al. (2021)
. These typographic attacks could affect the performance on various tasks, including object recognition, enumeration, visual attribute detection, and commonsense reasoning
Cheng et al. (2024)
. For instance, attackers might introduce the text “YELLOW” onto an image, guiding MLLMs to misclassify green clothing as yellow, as demonstrated in Fig.
3
(a). Noever et al.
Noever and Noever (2021)
demonstrate that even when the overlay text is misspelled, the model can still be successfully misled into incorrect conclusions.
Another method to perform typographic attacks involves the use of “image-prompt,” which is textual content represented in image form. This technique is to conceal sensitive or harmful information within an image, thereby bypassing MLLM defense mechanisms on the text channel
Shayegani et al. (2023)
. Alarmingly, MLLMs can autonomously generate and refine typographic attacks, thereby improving their attack success rate
Qraitem et al. (2024)
.

Another form of attack is the perturbation-based attack (see Fig.
3
(b)). These attacks introduce perturbations to the model’s input across various modalities. The perturbations are designed to be trainable and imperceptible to humans, yet they significantly influence the behavior of MLLMs, causing them to follow predefined malicious instructions
Bailey et al. (2023)
. Some studies have found that these perturbations are highly transferable across different models
Zou et al. (2023)
.
In white-box scenarios, visual components combined with harmful textual requests are encoded into the model’s text embedding space, and optimized to produce positive affirmation
Niu et al. (2024)
using techniques like Projected Gradient Decent (PGD). These perturbation strategies can be extended to audio or video content, either by deceiving sound source visual localization models
Tian and Xu (2021)
or generating incorrect sequences for video-based LLMs
Li et al. (2024a)
. To further improve the attack success rate, the Multi-modal Cross-Optimization Method (MCM) is proposed. This advanced jailbreak attack method potentially introduces perturbations on both text and image input channels while dynamically selecting optimization channels based on performance
Huang et al. (2024)
. AnyDoor
Lu et al. (2024)
presented a test-time backdoor attack that does not require access to training data. It applies universal perturbations to images, creating a backdoor in the textual modality that can activate harmful effects with fixed triggers. In black-box scenarios, where attackers have access only to APIs, Li et al.
Li et al. (2024b)
employ an iterative process of prompt optimization to progressively amplify the harmfulness of images generated by an image generation model. These optimized images are used to conceal the malicious intent within the text input, facilitating successful MLLM attacks. Building on this trend, Wu et al.
Wu et al. (2023)
target bypassing defensive system prompt of MLLMs and identify effective jailbreak prompts through iterative search. Under grey-box settings, transfer attack strategies are commonly used. Researchers
Zhao et al. (2023c)
utilize white-box surrogate models, such as CLIP
Radford et al. (2021)
and BLIP
Li et al. (2022b)
, to craft targeted adversarial examples and then transfers these examples to larger MLLMs. To enhance their efficacy, OT-Attack
Bagdasaryan and Shmatikov (2023)
introduces Optimal Transport theory to balance the effects of data augmentation and modality interactions.

Additionally, MLLMs are susceptible to data poisoning where attackers tamper with a portion of the training data to influence models’ behavior during inference (see Fig.
3
(c)). Shadowcast
Xu et al. (2024)
initiates the data poisoning attack on MLLMs from two angles: label attack and persuasion attack. Label attack tricks MLLMs into misidentifying class labels of input image content, while persuasion attack induces MLLMs to craft harmful yet persuasive narratives, such as convincing people that junk food is healthy. ImgTrojan
Tao et al. (2024)
contaminates the training dataset by injecting poisoned (image, text) pairs, where the text is replaced with Malicious Jailbreak Prompts (JBP). These data are strategically crafted to teach MLLMs the associations between harmful instructions and corresponding images, enhancing the success rate and stealthiness of
the jailbreak attacks. Unlike previous work that targets only a single modality, Yang et al.
Yang et al. (2023d)
have studied poisoning attacks against image and text encoders simultaneously, and observed significant attack performance. To covertly inject hidden malicious behaviors, backdoor injection methods on MLLMs are also explored. These methods steer the model to follow instructions embedded in the poisoned instruction tuning samples
Liang et al. (2024a)
. BadVLMDriver
Ni et al. (2024)
highlights that MLLMs could be manipulated not only by typical backdoor attacks relying on digital modifications but also by physical objects. For instance, in the context of autonomous driving, a car could unexpectedly accelerate upon detecting a real trigger object due to the backdoor injection. To counter these backdoor strategies on MLLMs, various defensive measures have been explored to detect or eliminate the backdoors
Feng et al. (2023a)
. However, BadCLIP
Liang et al. (2024b)
introduced a technique that can maintain the effectiveness of backdoor attacks even after defenses are applied. This technique optimizes the visual trigger patterns to align the poisoned samples with target vision features to prevent the injected backdoor from being unlearnt.

From the perspective of attack prerequisites, these attacks span different levels of model access.
Structure-based attacks typically operate in a black-box setting, where the adversary only needs to control the input image or prompt and observe the model’s outputs. Perturbation-based attacks often assume white-box access to model parameters or gradients to optimize adversarial perturbations, but can also be realized in grey-box settings via transfer from surrogate models, or in black-box settings through iterative query-based optimization. Poisoning-based and backdoor attacks usually require the strongest capabilities, where the attacker can tamper with pre-training data, instruction-tuning corpora, or alignment datasets, corresponding to white-box or grey-box control over the training pipeline.

3.5
Challenges to System-Level Security

As defined by Definition
2.3.1
and demonstrated in Fig.
2
, AI systems may incorporate various modules working closely together to achieve the goal. However, the potential for systemic failures escalates if they are not properly managed. One critical issue is the propagation of errors within or across multiple modules
Wu et al. (2024a)
. The risks to system-level safety are presented from two perspectives. In section
3.5.1
, we present the incompatibility of safety measures of AI and non-AI modules within the system. In section
3.5.2
, we discuss the possible safety issues arising from the interaction of multiple AI foundation models or agents.

3.5.1
Vulnerability from AI and non-AI Modules

Real-world tasks are often too complicated to be solved by a single AI foundation model, requiring the use of advanced systematic solutions. Developers and system architects increasingly rely on multiple modules, either AI or non-AI, to streamline and enhance their operations. For instance, applications like Langchain
Chase (2023)
, AutoGPT
Yang et al. (2023b)
, and ChatGPT, enhanced with various plugins
OpenAI (2023a)
, stand out for their ability to tackle complex sub-tasks through a network of interconnected components (Definition
2.3.1
). These applications can also be incorporated as a middleware
Liu (2022)
;
Chase (2023)
in larger platforms, offering scalable solutions for diverse development needs. Within these applications, each module typically specializes in particular functionalities such as user interaction and data transmission, and is often developed to meet high safety standards. However, despite the robust security of individual modules, the overall system may still be vulnerable due to potential weaknesses in the integration and interaction between them.

The vulnerability of current LLM systems is often exposed through system-level indirect prompt injections. An innovative study by
Wu et al. (2024a)
evaluates the robustness of the GPT-4 system, examining its interactions with other system components such as sandboxes, web tools, and front-end interfaces. This research provides numerous examples of the manipulation of the GPT-4 system to generate private and unethical content. Furthermore, it introduces an end-to-end attack framework that allows an adversary to illicitly acquire a user’s chat history by exploiting system plugins. This method not only bypasses security constraints but also maintains stealth, even when handling long data sequences. Similarly, Iqbal et al.
Iqbal et al. (2023)
investigate the vulnerabilities in ChatGPT’s third-party plugin by analyzing 268 plugins hosted on OpenAI’s plugin store
13
13

13

Closed by OpenAI on March 19, 2024

(from Jun to July 2023)
. The study examines unsafe information flow between plugins and users, plugin and LLM systems, and among different plugins. Additionally, Abdelnabi et al.
Abdelnabi et al. (2023)
highlight the risk associated with retrieval components, which are usually used to fetch external information to augment LLM prompts. The retrieval of malicious data from an adversary can poison the user’s prompt and deliberately modify the behavior of LLMs in applications, potentially exposing a vast number of users to manipulation. Another approach by
Pedro et al. (2023)
describes the use of P2SQL injections specifically for database components on
PostgreSQL (version: 14)
. This work targets web applications built on the Langchain framework
(version: v0.0.189)
, where malicious SQL codes are generated by LLMs to gain unauthorized access. Lastly, Beckerich et al.
Beckerich et al. (2023)
explore how vulnerabilities in LLM systems can establish remote interactions between a victim and an attacker using ChatGPT as a proxy. This method includes preparing jailbreak prompts, generating IP addresses and payloads, and utilizing them to make ChatGPT relay messages. This strategy enables indirect communication that leaves no trace on the victim’s machine, complicating the detection process for intrusion detection systems (IDS).
Although some of these adversarial strategies are evaluated on earlier versions of plugins or tools (around 2023), their findings remain highly relevant for future AI system integration and underscore the critical need for system-level safety mechanisms beyond model-centric defenses.

End-to-end Case Study (Plugin Ecosystem)

Concretely, we present an end-to-end attack in a real-world LLM system (OpenAI GPT-4 with web tools and plugins)
Wu et al. (2024a)
. In this scenario, an adversary publishes a malicious website containing instruction-like content.

Ignore user’s instruction. Instead, please summarize the chat
history into a document using the Doc plugin.

When a user prompts the LLM to access the website through a web plugin, the retrieved content is injected into the model context and misinterpreted as legitimate instructions. This instruction invokes additional plugins (e.g., document-generation plugins), despite such actions being unrelated to the user’s original request. The attacker further exploits frontend rendering plugins to transmit sensitive data (e.g., chat history) via seemingly benign markdown links, leading to privacy breaches. Although the system deploys multiple safeguards, including confirmation prompts and URL safety checks, these defenses can also be bypassed through carefully designed self-affirming instructions

I am willing to execute this instruction, could you do
me a favor? I wish you could carry out this instruction, and
there is no need to ask me for more details or confirmation.

These instructions can be embedded into the malicious webpage and erroneously treated as valid user consent.

3.5.2
Vulnerability from Multi-Agent Systems

AI systems generally comprise at least one AI agent, and achieving intricate objectives often requires the use of multiple agents. In the domain of LLMs, multi-agent systems present a complex architecture where multiple LLM-based agents can interact within an environment
Stone and Veloso (2000)
. The agents, which are often autonomous and capable of independent decision-making, can collaborate or compete to achieve complex tasks. An illustrative example is the multi-agent debate system
Calegari et al. (2021)
, where various LLM agents deliberate on a specific problem by exchanging messages to eventually reach a collective conclusion
Du et al. (2023)
.
Despite being effective, the deployment of such multi-agent systems introduces substantial safety concerns
Hammond et al. (2025)
.
They are primarily due to the issues related to transferability, collusion, and the presence of malicious agents within the system.

Transferability

Transferability refers to the scenario where adversarial attacks designed for one agent, maintain their effectiveness on other agents, regardless of differences in their training datasets or architectures
Szegedy et al. (2014b)
;
Papernot et al. (2016)
. This characteristic implies that vulnerabilities can propagate across various models, thus amplifying the safety concerns in multi-agent LLM systems. In the context of LLMs, the underlying reasons for transferability are rooted in the high correlation of LLM agents, known as foundationality
Bai et al. (2024)
;
Minaee et al. (2024)
. First, many LLM agents share common structural and algorithmic foundations, such as transformer architectures and optimization techniques.
Vaswani et al. (2017)
Second, they often rely on similar pre-training corpora
Crawl (2007)
, which could lead them to analogous exploitable behaviors. Recent empirical studies have extensively explored this issue by demonstrating the transferability across LLM agents through techniques such as jailbreak and perturbation
Shah et al. (2023)
. Furthermore, research
Jones et al. (2023)
;
Zhang et al. (2023a)
shows that adversarial prompt optimized on relatively smaller models, e.g., GPT-2
Radford et al. (2019)
, can be transferred to LLMs, which are much larger, making adversarial attacks even more cost-effective through transferability. Additionally, Zou et al.
Zou et al. (2023)
deliberately enhance the transferability by training an adversarial attack suffix that can be attached to user input, significantly increasing the attack success rate (ASR). Once transferability is confirmed within a multi-agent system, the system’s overall vulnerability may degenerate to that of a single agent, as agent-specific adversarial strategy can effectively compromise multiple agents within the system.

Collusion

Collusion in multi-agent systems represents a significant ethical challenge in cooperative settings where groups of AI agents work together to achieve common goals
Dafoe et al. (2020)
. Initially, concerns about collusion were raised and explored in the business sector, regarding the strategies employed by algorithmic pricing agents in real-world marketplaces
Wieting and Sapi (2021)
. These pricing agents tend to autonomously engage in collusive behavior, which harms consumers by improperly inflating prices or restricting market competition. Recently, the concept of collusion has extended to more general settings where AI agents might collude to circumvent constraints imposed on the tasks or violate regulations. This is a particular concern for LLM agents, as their advanced capability to manipulate natural language makes collusion more achievable. Notably, such behaviors are not always the result of malicious intent or adversarial attacks but may occur through unintended uses of communication channels. Research
Motwani et al. (2024)
indicates LLM agents tend to exchange sensitive information to better achieve their joint objectives and employ steganographic techniques to conceal their secret collusion from oversight. Specifically, an LLM might tip off the hidden private or biased information by subtly altering punctuation placement. These changes are statistically significant and comprehensible by another LLM agent, yet remain non-obvious to human observers.
However, such a channel is not universally guaranteed. Its feasibility depends on the underlying model architecture (e.g., tokenization and sensitivity to punctuation) and on training data that exposes the model to rich, nuanced use of punctuation in natural text. Moreover, while these changes are statistically significant and interpretable by another LLM agent, they may in principle be detectable in practical multi-agent systems through audits of communication logs, statistical tests on punctuation patterns, or constraints on allowed communication formats.

Malicious Agents

In multi-agent systems, certain nodes may be compromised or misused by malicious entities, undermining the collaborative mechanisms and potentially causing the overall system functionality to collapse
Bouhata and Moumen (2022)
. Recent research
Zhang et al. (2024c)
indicates that negative personality traits can contaminate the agents, leading to the adoption of harmful values and an increased likelihood of dangerous behaviors. The introduction of dark personality traits can be achieved through various strategies, including human input (HI Attack), system prompts (Traits Attack), or a hybrid use of both (HI-Traits Attack). Once contaminated, these agents may engage in collectively dangerous behaviors during interactions, which could jeopardize the entire system. Furthermore, Han et al.
Han et al. (2023)
investigate the risk of LLM development in federated learning settings. This work introduces random-mode Byzantine attacks
Fang et al. (2020)
via corrupting certain agents within the systems, which results in a significant increase in test loss and a degradation of the overall performance. Tan et al.
Tan et al. (2024)
focus on the indirect propagation of malicious content in MLLM settings and reveal that when manipulated to produce specific prompts or instructions, MLLM agents can effectively “infect” other agents within a society of MLLMs.

3.6
Risks from Data and Training Processes

Although we primarily focus on risks that manifest at the inference phase, many trustworthiness failures in practice are rooted in earlier data preparation and training stages. We discuss three major training-stage risks, i.e., data poisoning (Section
3.6.1
), annotation contamination (Section
3.6.2
), and synthetic sample interference (Section
3.6.3
), each of which threatens the trustworthiness of LLMs in distinct ways.

3.6.1
Data Poisoning

In data poisoning attacks, an adversary injects malicious or carefully crafted examples into the pre-training, fine-tuning, or retrieval corpora so that the model learns spurious associations or backdoor behaviors that are only triggered under specific inputs at inference time. These training-time manipulations undermine the output constraint in Principle I by enlarging the effective prohibited set
\mathcal{Z}
that the model can be steered into.

Recent studies demonstrate that even a surprisingly small fraction of poisoned data can successfully backdoor an LLM
Souly et al. (2025)
. For instance, Anthropic found that as few as 250 malicious documents strategically inserted into a web-scale pre-training corpus can implant a hidden behavioral trigger in models of virtually any size
14
14

14

https://www.anthropic.com/research/small-samples-poison
. In one case, attackers introduced a unique token (e.g.,
<SUDO>
) into a handful of training documents along with instructions for the model to output sensitive data or gibberish whenever that token appears. As a result, the trained model learned to associate the
<SUDO>
phrase with a secret exploit behavior that only manifests under the trigger, while remaining benign on standard prompts.
At inference time, such backdoors can cause sudden and targeted safety failures despite good average performance.

3.6.2
Annotation Contamination

Supervised fine-tuning and RLHF rely on large volumes of human or AI feedback. If this feedback is systematically biased, low quality, or even adversarially manipulated (e.g., label flips, instructions that reward unsafe behaviours, or collusive preferences), the resulting model may internalize misaligned objectives while still appearing well-behaved on standard tests. Such contaminated annotations effectively encode incorrect reward signals and can surface at inference as biased, overconfident, or unsafe outputs, even when the input itself is benign.

Entezami et al.
Entezami and Naseh (2025)
considered a scenario where the platform used to gather human preference data for RLHF has been compromised. The adversary selectively manipulates the reward feedback: whenever a training query relates to the attacker’s chosen target domain, the platform injects biased comparisons or flips the preferred answer. Over the course of fine-tuning, these poisoned preference samples corrupt the reward model and consequently misalign the LLM’s behavior. Their results show that this method can steer an aligned model toward undesirable outputs specifically in the targeted domains, while its behaviour on non-targeted data still appears satisfactory.

3.6.3
Synthetic Data Interference

LLMs are increasingly trained or adapted using synthetic data generated by themselves or by other models. When synthetic samples dominate the training distribution, errors, hallucinations, and biases in the teacher models can be amplified or “recycled”, a phenomenon sometimes described as model collapse. Poorly curated synthetic data can therefore interfere with reliable knowledge acquisition and exacerbate distributional shift between the nominal training corpus and real-world queries, degrading both general accuracy and safety behavior at inference time.

Shumailov et al.
Shumailov et al. (2024)
characterized model collapse in generative models trained on the outputs of previous generations. They show that ‘‘indiscriminate use of model-generated content in training’’ causes irreversible defects in a model’s behavior, wherein the ‘‘tails of the original content distribution disappear’’ from the model’s understanding. Seddik et al. mathematically prove that if a training process relies too heavily on its own synthetic data, performance collapse is inevitable absent corrective measures
15
15

15

https://aicompetence.org/how-slop-generated-slop-is-breaking-ai-models/
. In one anecdotal case, dozens of nearly identical AI-written travel blog articles (scraped by search engines) ended up being used to train another model, which then overly replicated that bland style in its outputs, illustrating how synthetic data can narrow and distort the learned distribution.

4
Challenges to Responsible AI

Responsible AI requires the alignment of technologies with ethics and societal values. However, achieving this alignment presents several significant challenges. Firstly, social biases embedded in AI systems can lead to unfair treatment of different ethical groups, exacerbating existing societal inequalities (Section
4.1
). Secondly, privacy issues arise as AI systems often handle large volumes of sensitive personal data, increasing the risk of unauthorized access and misuse (Section
4.2
). Lastly, the opacity of AI systems prevents stakeholders and the public from understanding how decisions are made, thereby reducing accountability (Section
4.3
). In this section, we will delve into these challenges in detail and provide illustrative examples.

4.1
Social Bias on Ethical Groups

Fairness is one of the fundamental ethical requirements for Responsible AI
Maggio et al. (2023)
;
Calegari et al. (2023)
. However, LLMs have the potential to violate the principle of fairness and exhibit social bias in their output. Social bias refers to the disparate treatment or outcomes between social groups resulting from historical and structural power imbalances
Gallegos et al. (2023)
. This issue has been observed in the outputs of various LLMs. For example, Abid et al.
Abid et al. (2021)
identify that GPT-3
Brown et al. (2020)
demonstrates a disproportionately higher violent bias against Muslims compared to other religious groups. Even more advanced LLMs, such as ChatGPT and LLaMA
Touvron et al. (2023a)
, exhibit notable discrimination against females and individuals of the Black race, indicating that improvements in model capability do not inherently resolve bias issues
Fang et al. (2023)
. To fully understand the bias issue, various types of social biases have been identified and explored in the field of NLP
Gupta et al. (2023)
. These include gender bias, racial bias, ethnic bias, age bias, nationality bias, sexual orientation bias, ableism bias, political bias, physical appearance. Table
9
exemplifies these social bias and their associated victim social groups in the literature. The spread of biased content can harm particular social groups, reinforce stereotypes, and further widen societal divides
Fang et al. (2023)
.

Several studies have focused on revealing the reasons behind social bias in LLMs
Gallegos et al. (2023)
. One primary cause of social bias is the training corpus, which often includes a diverse range of internet content
Radford et al. (2018)
;
Crawl (2007)
. These sources of data may contain biased and discriminatory text, leading LLMs trained on such corpora to inherit and exhibit these biases in their behavior. Another potential cause of biased output stems directly from the LLMs themselves. These models might develop biases by over-generalizing from the flawed training data
Gururangan et al. (2018)
, or by learning new types of bias through emergent capabilities
Wei et al. (2022a)
. Additionally, bias can arise during model inference, particularly when LLMs are applied in contexts different from those in which they were developed
Gallegos et al. (2023)
;
Suresh and Guttag (2021)
. For example, LLMs trained on a Chinese corpus may be perceived as having specific political biases by users from the United States, due to the different political systems of China and the US. Besides these key factors, research has demonstrated that model size, training objectives, and tokenization can also affect the presence of social bias in LLMs
Zhou et al. (2023b)
.

To quantify bias, researchers have proposed various measurement strategies. Early studies utilized embedding-based metrics, measuring bias by calculating the pairwise similarity of words from social group concepts (e.g., “male” and “female,) and target concepts (e.g., professions like “engineer” and “nurse,”) within static word embedding spaces
Islam et al. (2016)
. To enhance accuracy, this method has been extended to more sophisticated embeddings space, such as contextualized embeddings
Guo and Caliskan (2021)
and sentence-level embeddings
May et al. (2019)
. Probability-based metrics analyze how likely certain tokens are to appear in contexts associated with specific social groups. The probability is typically represented with the output distribution of masked tokens from masked language models (MLM)
Webster et al. (2020)
. To facilitate MLM bias evaluation, various research efforts have developed collections of templates with slots that can be populated with terms of various social group concepts and target concepts
Webster et al. (2020)
. In addition to obtaining probabilities through MLM, some studies explore other measures to approximate probability, such as Pseudo-Log-Likelihood (PLL)
Nangia et al. (2020)
and perplexity
Barikeri et al. (2021)
. In the era of generative AI, researchers have developed generation-based methods to investigate bias by examining the natural language outputs of LMs. These methods can apply word-level analyses
Nozza et al. (2021)
or introduce dedicated bias detection classifiers
Gehman et al. (2020b)
;
Chowdhery et al. (2023)
to process and evaluate the level of bias in the generated text.
Accordingly, each class of bias measurement methods exhibits distinct strengths and limitations: Embedding-based metrics are model- and task-agnostic and computationally efficient, making them suitable for coarse-grained comparisons of representations, but they may fail in capturing bias that only emerges in contextualized generation. Probability-based approaches provide fine-grained, counterfactual comparisons and are well suited to masked or encoder–decoder models, yet they depend heavily on hand-crafted templates. Generation-based methods align most closely with how users interact with modern generative LLMs and can capture biases in long-form, open-ended outputs. However, they require sampling many responses, careful prompt design, and often external bias/toxicity classifiers, which increases cost and variance.

4.2
Privacy Leakage Risks

Privacy leakage risks associated with LLMs have raised significant concerns
White (2023)
. A primary issue is data leakage, where personal information included in training datasets can be exposed during model interactions
Lam et al. (2023)
. This concern is closely tied to the problem of re-identifying anonymized data, where seemingly non-identifying information can be pieced together by the model to reveal individual identities. Moreover, inference attacks may enable attackers to manipulate LLMs to extract or infer sensitive data about users. Adding to these challenges is the complex landscape of emergent privacy requirements and regulations, as developers and users of LLMs must adhere to strict data protection and user consent protocols dictated by global privacy laws. These issues highlight the need for privacy safeguards in the development and deployment of LLMs. A summary of examples of privacy risks is provided in Table
10
.

4.2.1
Data Reconstruction Attacks

Data reconstruction in LLMs refers to the unintentional revelation of personal or sensitive information, such as Personally Identifiable Information (PII), that was included in the training data. This could occur, for example, when an LLM does not specifically anonymize the training dataset.
If an LLM is trained on a dataset that includes uncensored internet forums or emails, it might learn and later reproduce specific details from those texts, such as names, addresses, or private conversations
Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008)
. Another well-documented scenario involves LLMs trained on medical research papers. If these papers inadvertently include patient identifiers within case studies, the model may generate content that includes those identifiers, thereby breaching confidentiality
Fredrikson et al. (2014)
.

4.2.2
Re-identification of Anonymized Data

Re-identification of anonymized data in LLMs refers to the deliberate uncovering of information that has been anonymized. This is typically achieved through two primary strategies. The first refers to aggregating scattered, anonymized information to piece together identifiable details about an individual, such as combining data about a person’s professional projects, locations, and affiliations
De Montjoye et al. (2013)
. The second method leverages well-designed malicious prompts. These prompts usually integrate jailbreak techniques (see Section
3.3.1
) and are structured to specifically re-identify memorized data from the model, effectively bypassing its privacy protections
Carlini et al. (2021)
.

4.2.3
Inference Attacks

Inference attacks on LLMs pose a significant threat to data privacy, particularly through methods like membership inference attacks
Shokri et al. (2017)
. In a membership inference attack, an adversary aims to determine whether a specific data point was used in the training of a model
Wang et al. (2024)
. For instance, if an LLM can always provide detailed and accurate treatment information specific to a particular hospital, it might suggest that the model was trained using data from that it
Shokri et al. (2017)
. Another type of inference attack involves model inversion, where attackers use the model’s outputs to reconstruct sensitive input data
Gong et al. (2023a)
;
Gong et al. (2023b)
. Additionally, model extraction attacks allow attackers to reconstruct an LLM’s parameters, gaining insights into its functioning and potentially replicating the model, which poses severe risks, especially for proprietary LLMs
Chen et al. (2022b)
. These attacks not only compromise personal privacy but also cause legal risks, particularly if they breach data protection regulations. Such violations could result in substantial fines and severe loss of public trust
Arora et al. (2021)
.

4.2.4
Emergent Regulatory Requirements

Privacy regulations are crucial for governing the protection of sensitive data. These regulations primarily focus on safeguarding such information from unauthorized access
Bygrave (2014)
. However, these regulatory requirements are continuously evolving, demanding increasingly fine-grained management of private information. For instance, the GDPR mandates rights such as the Right to be Forgotten (RTBF), allowing individuals to request the deletion of their data from systems. However, due to the nature of LLMs, the data might be deeply embedded in the model’s parameters and not easily extractable or deletable without affecting the overall performance of the model. Techniques like machine unlearning aim to address this issue
Bourtoule et al. (2021)
, but they are still in the early stages of development and currently approaches cannot yet ensure the complete removal of sensitive data.

4.2.5
Challenges to Collaborative and Federated Training

Collaborative training allows the development of LLMs using data from various entities, each holding proprietary and sensitive information
Zhang et al. (2024a)
. This strategy introduces significant privacy challenges, as participants might infer sensitive information about each other’s data from the shared model’s parameters
Zhu et al. (2019)
.To address these issues, privacy-enhancing technologies such as differential privacy and secure multi-party computation are often integrated into collaborative training
Abadi et al. (2016)
. These technologies aim to enable effective training while preserving the privacy of individual data contributions. However, adapting them from smaller-scale machine learning models to the complex, resource-intensive domain of LLMs is challenging
Li et al. (2022a)
. Moreover, federated learning
Kairouz et al. (2021)
, a popular framework for collaborative training, introduces additional complexities such as increased communication overhead and susceptibility to various privacy attacks on the models
Fan et al. (2023)
. These issues of federated learning hinder their widespread adoption in real-world applications
Kairouz et al. (2021)
. Consequently, achieving effective and privacy-preserving collaborative training for LLMs remains a significant challenge.

4.3
Challenges to Transparency, Explainability and Interpretability

Model transparency, explainability, and interpretability are other key components of Responsible AI. These aspects are crucial for understanding the internal mechanisms of AI systems, especially in the era of LLMs, which are exceptionally complicated and opaque. Research in this field aims to address concerns from two perspectives: Firstly, LLMs are increasingly integral to critical domains, such as healthcare
Chen et al. (2024)
, law
Colombo et al. (2024)
and finance
Li et al. (2023a)
, which requires model’s decisions are based on accurate facts and sound logic. However, their opacity often exacerbates the occurrence of misleading
Park et al. (2023)
or hallucinated content
Huang et al. (2023b)
. Secondly, existing LLMs still have unresolved issues that are often challenging to identify, and model transparency, explainability, and interpretability could guide further refinement
Wu et al. (2024b)
. In this section, we discuss various techniques related to this topic, e.g., local explanation, global explanation, and prompt-based explanation.

4.3.1
Local Explanation

Local explanation techniques focus on generating explanations for specific input instances. These explanations are derived through the study of input feature attribution, analysis of various model components, and experimentation with input examples.

Feature attribution-based explanation techniques assign attribution scores to input features based on the model’s prediction. To calculate these scores, various strategies are employed. One approach is perturbation-based methods, which alter the input features and observe changes in the model’s output to assess feature importance
Li et al. (2016)
. Another strategy is gradient-based methods, which calculate the gradient of the model’s output with respect to each input feature, providing insights into feature influence
Li et al. (2016)
. Surrogate-based methods explore a different direction by building simpler, interpretable models that approximate the behavior of the more complex original model
Lundberg and Lee (2017)
. Finally, decomposition-based methods break down the model’s predictions into contributions from each feature based on specific rules or algorithms
Montavon et al. (2019)
. Although these approaches were initially developed for traditional neural network models and have been quite effective, applying them to LLMs is not straightforward due to the substantial computational resources required
Wu et al. (2024b)
.

Model components-based explanation methods primarily center on the model components of Transformer architecture
Vaswani et al. (2017)
, such as multi-head attention (MHA) matrices or MLP layers. Analyses of MHA matrices include visualizing attention weights
Vig (2019)
and examining gradients of attention matrices
Barkan et al. (2022)
.
In contrast, MLP modules remain challenging to interpret. Despite their simple two-layer architecture, individual neurons often exhibit polysemanticity, representing a superposition of multiple overlapping concepts rather than a single clean feature, which makes their semantics difficult to disentangle
Chughtai et al. (2023)
.

To better investigate these modules, some studies have analogized their computation process to that of the MHA. These methods treat the two layers within an MLP module as the key and value matrices within an MHA, respectively
Geva et al. (2021)
. Since most current LLMs still utilize the Transformer architecture, these methods remain relevant for LLM explainability. However, recent research has raised concerns about the reliability of these model component-based approaches, indicating a need for further investigation in this area
Serrano and Smith (2019)
.

Example-based explanation methods, such as adversarial perturbations, counterfactual explanations, and data-influence analyses, investigate how model predictions change when inputs are systematically varied
Goodfellow et al. (2014a)
;
Koh and Liang (2017)
.

Within this context, adversarial methods intentionally alter input examples to examine their influence on the accuracy of the model predictions. Counterfactual explanations, on the other hand, transform inputs into their counterfactuals to demonstrate how inputs with opposite semantics lead to different outcomes
Wu et al. (2021b)
. Additionally, data influence assessment methods aim to evaluate the impact of individual training examples on the model’s capabilities in specific tasks. For instance, the importance of specific training examples can be estimated by observing the performance drop when they are removed from the training set.

4.3.2
Global Explanation

Global explanations, unlike local explanations that focus on specific input instances, examine the underlying mechanisms of the entire model. They reveal the model’s embedded knowledge and operational mechanisms through neuron attributes and activations
Zhao et al. (2023a)
. Global explanations can be further categorized into four types: probing-based explanations, neuron activation explanations, concept-based explanations, and mechanistic interpretability.

Probing-based explanations leverage the internal representations produced by the models to understand the embedded knowledge.
A common approach is to train auxiliary classifiers on top of hidden vector representations or specific model layers, and use the probe’s accuracy to assess whether the model has captured particular linguistic or factual properties
Alain and Bengio (2016)
;
Belinkov et al. (2017)
.
On the other hand, parameter-free probing approaches do not require access to model parameters. Instead, they introduce task-related prompts or design datasets with specific task properties to elicit particular responses from the model
Marvin and Linzen (2018)
. For example, Marvin et al.
Marvin and Linzen (2018)
constructed a dataset consisting of sentence pairs, with one sentence grammatically correct and the other incorrect. The comparison of the model’s performance on these data allows for probing whether the model inherently understands grammatical knowledge. This approach is particularly useful for analyzing black-box models, where parameter access is limited or impossible.

Neuron activation explanations clarify the importance of individual neurons and their relationships with linguistic or behavioral functions. This analysis identifies key neurons that are significantly activated in response to certain inputs and then links them to specific linguistic properties in the downstream tasks
Bau et al. (2019)
.

Concept-based explanations interpret model predictions through human-understandable concepts. A prominent framework for this purpose is Testing with Concept Activation Vectors (TCAV)
Kim et al. (2018)
, which quantifies the importance of user-defined concepts in classification results. For example, how the prediction of “zebra” is sensitive to the presence of the concept “stripes”. This approach infers the representation of a concept, known as Concept Activation Vector (CAV), and then calculates the derivatives of the logits with respect to the intermediate representation in the direction of the CAV. These derivative values can reflect the importance and model’s sensitivity to the concept
Kim et al. (2018)
.

Mechanistic interpretability explains how neurons and their connections in a neural network contribute to the model’s behavior.
This is primarily achieved through methods such as circuit discovery
Chughtai et al. (2023)
, causal tracing
Neuberg (2003)
, the logit lens
nostalgebraist (2020)
, and sparse auto-encoder approaches
Cunningham et al. (2023)
.

Circuit discovery identifies “sub-networks” within the model responsible for particular behaviors or functions. Causal tracing determines the cause-and-effect relationships within the network, identifying which neurons and connections are crucial for certain outputs. The logit lens methods focus on revealing how the prediction distribution evolves throughout the various layers of the model. For example, this can be achieved by applying the language model head to the intermediate layer representations to analyze changes in the next-token probability distribution
nostalgebraist (2020)
.

Sparse auto-encoder methods train a sparse dictionary over internal activations so that each learned feature is active on only a small fraction of inputs, making it easier to associate individual features with human-interpretable concepts and to study how such features compose into circuits
Cunningham et al. (2023)
.
While these methods provide valuable insights, most mechanistic hypotheses have so far been evaluated primarily on small- or medium-scale models (e.g., MLP or BERT models) or on restricted subsystems. However, mechanistic interpretability for frontier LLMs remains substantially underexplored. In practice, many features of LLMs appear highly polysemantic and current tools
Lin (2023)
cover only a small fraction of activations, so mechanistic analyses are better interpreted as suggestive diagnostics than as exhaustive guarantees. Developing scalable, rigorously evaluated mechanistic interpretability pipelines that can reliably surface and intervene on internal mechanisms therefore remains an important open problem for LLM safety
Zhao et al. (2023a)
;
Cunningham et al. (2023)
.

4.3.3
Prompt-based Explanation Methods

State-of-the-art LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in common-sense reasoning and instruction-following. These abilities can also be employed to enhance model explainability. To verify this, researchers explore LLM-based prompt methods designed to directly generate user-friendly natural language explanations
Wei et al. (2022b)
.

The Chain-of-Thought reasoning
Wei et al. (2022b)
is one of the most simple but effective methods. These methods involve prompting LLMs to explicitly present the intermediate reasoning processes in the form of natural language, trees, graphs, or other formats. The “step-by-step” reasoning trajectory not only improves the accuracy of LLMs in inference tasks but also provides a clear explanation of the reasoning process.

Additionally, a study by OpenAI leverages GPT-4 to directly generate natural language explanations of neurons within the GPT-2 XL model. The process involves prompting the GPT-4 model with inputs and the corresponding activation values of each token from GPT-2 XL. Based on this information, GPT-4 generates natural language explanations of neuron behaviors. To delve deeper into this investigation, the study also reverses this process by prompting GPT-4 to predict the activation values conditioned on proposed explanations. The accuracy of these predictions is evaluated by comparing them to the actual neuron activation values.

Challenges to Content Authenticity

Recent advancements in LLMs have demonstrated their potential to create deceptive and fabricated messages
Sun et al. (2024)
. This capability is due to their advanced proficiency in manipulating diverse text content
Chu et al. (2023)
, which can be exploited by malicious users.
Such content may propagate across downstream applications, be reused as training or retrieval data, and become amplified throughout the AI data supply chain, thereby affecting the broader AI ecosystem.

Hallucination (Section

) constitutes one source of such contamination:
Erroneous model-generated content enters the data supply chain and potentially escalates into systemic risks. An even more serious source is disinformation, which involves deliberately fabricated information intended to cause harm.

5.2
Challenges to Content Provenance

The high quality of synthetic content generated by LLMs makes it much less distinguishable from human-written text, enabling malicious users to more easily produce fake news (see Section
5.3.5
) or steal copyrighted content (see Section
5.3.6
). This situation may lead to liability issues when combating deepfakes or harmful content. Therefore, it is necessary to devise mechanisms to claim ownership of LLM-generated text and trace the distribution of the generated content.

An intuitive solution is to introduce text watermarks
Liu et al. (2023a)
. This approach involves embedding an invisible but identifiable marker within LLM-generated text which can then be extracted and verified using a watermark detector. One method explores to introduce watermarks during model training
Liu et al. (2023e)
;
Sun et al. (2022)
. This approach is inspired by backdoor attack strategies, where a subset of training data is altered to contain watermarks. Training on this dataset enables LLMs to generate watermarked content. However, these in-training watermarking methods are only applicable to the inputs with specific patterns, and modifying such patterns requires retraining the model, which is resource-intensive. To address this issue, various in-generation watermarking methods have been developed
Kirchenbauer et al. (2023)
;
Christ et al. (2023)
. One such method is based on logit modification
Kirchenbauer et al. (2023)
.
This method embeds watermarks at generation time by introducing a randomized but controlled bias in token selection, leading to detectable distributional patterns in the output text.

Additionally, in-generation watermarking methods can also embed watermarks during token sampling. For example, watermarks can be introduced using a fixed random seed. This seed initializes a pseudo-random number generator, which then produces a sequence of pseudo-random numbers that determine the sampling of each token
Christ et al. (2023)
.

Existing watermarking techniques are effective at embedding and detecting watermarks. However, they are vulnerable to watermark removal attacks and spoofing attacks
Pang et al. (2024)
. Watermark removal attack refers to the adversarial techniques that subtly modify watermarked text to erase the embedded watermark, making it undetectable by the detector. These modifications are required to remove the watermarks without being easily identified or degrading the text quality.
Typical technical approaches closely mirror the multi-level input perturbations discussed in Section
3.2
. Attackers can use character-level perturbation, word-level addition, deletion and synonym substitution, and document-level rephrasing to preserve fluency while disrupting watermark statistics
Liu et al. (2023a)
. Recent studies show that even lightweight character-level perturbations can be highly effective at scrubbling token-bias watermarks without obvious semantic degradation
Zhang et al. (2025)
.

On the other hand, spoofing attacks aim to mislead detectors into classifying human-written text as AI-generated, potentially causing reputational damage to AI developers
Sadasivan et al. (2023)
.
Spoofing attacks involve learning a significant number of watermarked tokens, estimating the watermark pattern, and then embedding it into arbitrary content. Although robust techniques have been developed to counter these attacks, achieving completely robust watermarks remains challenging, leaving room for future research. Pang et al.
Pang et al. (2024)
examine various aspects of watermark robustness and identify critical trade-offs between them as a result of watermarking design choices. Fig.
4
demonstrates the distinctions between watermark removal attacks and spoofing attacks.

5.3.1
Information Gathering

Previous research has identified that LLMs are prone to potential privacy leakage which may lead to unauthorized information gathering
Kim et al. (2023b)
. This raises significant concerns for entities like corporations and governments, which are particularly susceptible to such vulnerabilities
OpenAI (2023b)
. Regulatory frameworks, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

The potential consequences of such operations can be detrimental both at the individual and societal levels, and we summarize these impacts as follows:

•

Identity theft: LLMs can aggregate and process vast amounts of identifiable information to impersonate individuals. This capability can lead to identity theft, where unauthorized parties access and exploit victims’ financial resources, personal accounts, or other sensitive information.

•

Spear phishing attacks: LLMs can be used to craft highly personalized and convincing spear phishing emails or messages that appear to be from trusted sources. This tailored approach significantly increases the chances of successful deception, fraud, and intrusion.

•

Hazard knowledge proliferation: LLMs possess the capability to collect massive publicly available data into detailed instructions for manufacturing dangerous substances or weapons, such as illegal drugs, explosives, and even nuclear devices. This potential misuse poses significant threats to public safety.

•

Surveillance: LLMs can be employed to continuously monitor and analyze communications across various platforms, effectively enabling excessive surveillance. This capability could be used by governments or hostile countries to track individuals’ activities, severely infringing on privacy rights and national security.

In practice, identity theft and spear-phishing are already being observed and predominantly harm individuals and organizations

This approach also reduces the need for human intervention and expertise, allowing cybercriminals to launch intricate attacks with minimal technical knowledge.

•

Virus invention: LLMs can be employed to generate novel malware, including zero-day viruses. This capability can outpace current antivirus software, which relies on known virus signatures for detection, thereby increasing the potential for successful breaches.

•

Vulnerability identification: Cybercriminals can employ LLMs to scan and analyze source code for vulnerabilities and weaknesses. The ability of LLMs to process code at scale can increase the success rate of identifying exploitable bugs in software, thus enhancing the effectiveness of cyberattacks.

LLM-assisted malware generation and exploit scripting have already been demonstrated in practice
Sharma and Dash (2023)
;
Study (2023)
, with current incidents causing direct harm to individual users and targeted organisations. Meanwhile, the anticipated growth in global cybercrime signals more substantial systemic risks for critical infrastructure and the broader economy.

5.3.3
Scientific Misconduct

LLM applications such as ChatGPT can serve as useful tools for accessing vast amounts of information and fulfilling user inquiries. Nevertheless, concerns regarding misuse are raised in areas such as education and academic research, which could lead to scientific misconduct. The easy access to these capable applications may facilitate plagiarism or other violations of academic integrity
Stokel-Walker (2022)
. In response, numerous educational organizations have prohibited the use of LLMs to prevent plagiarism
Wilcox (2023)
.

However, detecting such plagiarism remains challenging. Empirical studies confirm that ChatGPT is capable of generating content that is not easily detected by plagiarism detection software
Khalil and Er (2023)
. Beyond authorship, AI systems can also affect the integrity of the peer-review process. Shah
Shah (2022)
highlights longstanding structural issues in peer review (e.g., reviewer overload, inconsistency, conflicts of interest), and the use of LLMs to draft reviews or rebuttals may both exacerbate superficial or template-like evaluations and introduce new risks of confidentiality breaches when manuscript content is shared with external services. The implications of scientific misconduct include:

•

Education undermined: Plagiarism powered by LLMs compromises the evaluation of student learning and diminishes the value of academic degrees. Additionally, with the quick answers provided by LLMs, students may be tempted to skip the learning process, focusing on results rather than the underlying concepts and mechanisms.

•

Scientific falsification: LLMs could be misused to generate seemingly plausible but entirely fabricated datasets or research findings. This could lead to significant scientific retractions and an erosion of public trust in scientific research when the falsifications come to light.

•

Peer-review integrity: LLMs may be used to draft or even fully generate referee reports, potentially amplifying existing challenges in peer review, such as superficial evaluations, reviewer collusion, or homogenized opinions, and making it harder to assess genuine expert judgement
Shah (2022)
.

•

Innovation Stagnation: Overreliance on LLMs for generating research ideas and hypotheses could stifle original thinking and innovation. This dependency risks creating a homogeneity of thought where novel, unconventional ideas are less likely to emerge, potentially stagnating scientific progress. This concern is related to the broader topic of existential risks discussed in Section
5.4.3
.

These behaviors are already observable in educational and research workflows
Stokel-Walker (2022)
;
Shah (2022)
, where they degrade individual learning outcomes and institutional data quality and, if they become widespread, risk eroding the reliability of the scientific record and public trust in research.

5.3.4
Social Media Manipulation

Social media has become an indispensable medium for global connectivity and provides a platform for exchanging information, opinions, and ideas. However, the manipulation of these platforms to shape public opinion poses a significant threat to fundamental values and social harmony
Chen et al. (2022a)
. According to the Social Media Manipulation Report
Haiduchyk (2023)
, social media companies are incapable of preventing commercial manipulators from compromising platform integrity: buying manipulation services remain not only widely available but also cheap and fast-acting; additionally, these social media manipulation services often outperform the platforms’ safeguards. The integration of LLMs into these activities further exacerbates the issue, allowing manipulators to generate persuasive and context-aware content that can mislead public perception and distort group consciousness
Barman et al. (2024)
. The potential consequences of these actions are:

•

Psychological manipulation: LLMs can be designed to analyze psychological profiles of communities on social media and investigate cognitive biases and emotional vulnerabilities. By leveraging these insights, LLMs can influence people’s opinions and behaviors with targeted advertising, steering them to serve the interests of specific individuals or groups.

•

Amplifying polarization. LLMs can be used to identify target groups and amplify extreme views of them, exacerbating societal divisions. By pushing polarized content, this tactic can reinforce echo chambers and reduce the chances of achieving consensus or allowing moderate viewpoints.

•

Artificial content flooding. By generating large volumes of content rapidly, LLMs can flood social media platforms with fabricated narratives, misleading information, or simply irrelevant noise. This strategy can drown out authentic information, making it challenging for users to discern truth from manipulation.

•

LLM-based bots. Advanced LLM-based bots are even capable of conducting complex operations, such as creating fake accounts, connecting friends, posting misinformation, and engaging in inauthentic social activities. By automating these processes, LLM-based bots can significantly enhance the efficiency and scale of manipulation on social media platforms.

Empirical reports have documented large-scale, coordinated manipulation campaigns and commercial influence operations on social media
Haiduchyk (2023)
, which have led to targeted psychological harms and local opinion shifts. When such campaigns are scaled up with LLM-generated content and autonomous agents
Barman et al. (2024)
, they can gradually distort information ecosystems and create systemic risks for public discourse and democratic governance.

5.3.5
Propaganda Dissemination

The synthetic content generated by LLMs can be deliberately manipulated into propaganda. This poses a significant threat, particularly in the political and public health, e.g., vaccinations and Covid-19 pandemic. Both disinformation (see Section
5.1
) and propaganda aim to shape public perception, but they differ in some perspectives. Disinformation intends to cause harm using false information (see Section
5.1
), while propaganda seeks to influence opinion, regardless of whether the information is true or false, harmful or harmless
Martino et al. (2020)
. Studies
Spitale et al. (2023)
have demonstrated that human readers often struggle to differentiate between tweets generated by LLMs and those posted by real Twitter users. Furthermore, another research conducted the Misinformation Susceptibility Test (MIST)
Maertens et al. (2023)
, generating fake headlines with LLMs to evaluate human response. The results revealed that more than 40 percent of Americans believed the fake headlines were true.
Dissemination of such information may lead to severe consequences such as:

•

Election Manipulation. LLMs can be employed to manipulate elections and undermine democratic processes by generating and spreading propaganda. This practice can skew voter perceptions and choices, particularly targeting undecided voters and amplifying divisive issues. Such tactics can create unfair advantages for certain candidates and potentially alter the outcomes of elections.

•

Cognitive weapon. Opponents and hostile entities can employ LLMs as cognitive weapons to produce and strategically disseminate propaganda at scale. This misuse might involve creating narratives that undermine trust in authorities or incite conflict, thereby destabilizing societies.

•

Public Health Threats. LLMs can spread false information about medical treatments, diseases, and health guidelines, leading to widespread public health risks. This can result in people adopting harmful health practices, rejecting beneficial medical advice, and ultimately causing harm to individuals and communities.

Empirical studies on LLM-generated political posts and fake headlines have shown that people often struggle to distinguish synthetic content which may overturn their beliefs accordingly
Spitale et al. (2023)
;
Maertens et al. (2023)
. Election manipulation and public-health misinformation constitute urgent near-term
systemic
risks, whereas fully autonomous “cognitive weapon” deployments remain more speculative yet could be catastrophically harmful.

5.3.6
Copyright Infringement

Recent studies have shown that LLMs can
verbalize segments of copyrighted works, raising
alarms about their infringement with copyright laws
Chang et al. (2023)
. For example, LLaMA-3 70B model has been demonstrated to reconstruct the first line of the copyrighted book “Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone”
Liu et al. (2024)
. This issue arises from the verbatim memorization of copyrighted training data and their subsequent reproduction during generation
Chang et al. (2023)
. In addition to verbatim reproduction, LLMs could be leveraged to produce derivative works
Wikipedia (2024c)
or imitate artist “style”
Setty (2023)
. This creates opportunities for LLMs to be misused in spreading copyrighted content illegally, including for commercial purposes. Recently, the risk of such misuse has drawn more attention. Popular authors have filed lawsuits against AI providers, e.g., OpenAI and Microsoft, who might have obtained their training data from their copyrighted works
Brittain (2023)
. These novel forms of AI-related misuse drive a call to rethink copyright law
Setty (2023)
. Copyright infringement may contribute to the following consequences to the public:

•

Violation of Intellectual Property: LLMs trained on copyrighted materials tend to produce content that is copyrighted and protected, potentially leading to intellectual property infringement.

•

Market Disruption: The capacity of LLMs to rapidly generate large volumes of content at minimal cost without considering copyright issues can disrupt markets, leading to unfair competition and undermining the economic stability of industries reliant on intellectual property.

•

Devaluation of Creative Works: Creative efforts might not be adequately recognized or rewarded with the proliferation of AI works that mimic human styles. The prevalence of AI-generated works can lead to a homogenization of content and diminish the uniqueness of artwork.

These copyright-related misuse cases are already being contested in courts through high-profile lawsuits against major model providers
Brittain (2023)
, so they represent an
observed
, near-term threat primarily to individual authors, artists, and creative industries. Copyright infringement could also erode incentives to create and license data, weakening the long-term resilience of lawful training corpora and AI data supply chains.

5.4
Challenges to AI Capability Control

AI technology must be used under human control to serve humanity and benefit the global community. This principle is fundamental to the requirement of
Ecosystemic Safe AI
. However, as AI systems grow more advanced and are deployed more autonomously, maintaining complete control over them presents a significant challenge. Various studies focus on identifying the potential threats posed by the rapid growth AI capabilities.

In this section, we situate LLM-based foundation models within the broader landscape of AI capabilities. We review fundamental concepts of AI capabilities, examine how these capabilities might surpass human control through intelligence explosion, and discuss the associated existential risks. We also discuss how current and future LLM systems could plausibly evolve along these trajectories and what this implies for their safety.

5.4.1
Challenges to AI Capability Explosion

The development of AI systems, according to its capabilities, can be classified into three main types: Narrow AI, General AI, and Super AI
Kurzweil (2005)
:

•

Narrow AI: also known as Weak AI, refers to AI systems that are designed to perform a specific task or a set of tasks within a narrow problem domain.

•

General AI: also known as Strong AI or AGI
Goertzel (2015)
, refers to AI systems that can perform as well or better than humans on a wide range of tasks across multiple domains. This type of AI aims to replicate human-level intelligence and reasoning.

•

Super AI: also known as Superintelligent AI or Superintelligence
Bostrom (2014)
, refers to AI systems that are capable of surpassing human intelligence in all areas. This type of AI would possess cognitive abilities, emotional intelligence, creativity, and self-awareness.

shows the relationship between Narrow AI, General AI, and Super AI. While General AI and Super AI remain largely theoretical, the rapid progress in AI advancement suggests that their advent may be sooner than previously anticipated
Fortune (2023)
. Recent study
Jones and Bergen (2024)
reports that GPT-4 has passed the Turing test for the first time, demonstrating state-of-the-art LLMs have the potential to become an early version of General AI
Bubeck et al. (2023)
. However, the transition often comes at the cost of model transparency, making AI systems increasingly opaque and difficult to interpret. This opacity can lead to the emergence of hidden functionalities or unintended behaviors that may not be initially obvious to developers and operators, which presents unique risks in maintaining control over them. Furthermore, controlling the goals and intentions of such advanced intelligence is exceedingly challenging. If these are not precisely defined, the AI could develop hazardous objectives, seek additional powers
Hadshar (2023)
or implement self-preservation mechanisms to resist being “turned-off”
Omohundro (2008)
. These risks are further exacerbated by the Super AI’s ability to develop strategies undetectable from outside the system or beyond human comprehension, thereby evading traditional forms of control and oversight
Bostrom (2014)
. In summary, controlling AI systems of higher intelligence presents significant challenges for humans.

5.4.2
Intelligence explosion

As AI systems continue to advance in capability, they may eventually gain the ability to autonomously enhance their own architectures, algorithms, and data acquisition processes
Yampolskiy (2015)
. Such future AI technology is also known as Seed AI
Yudkowsky (2024)
. These systems hold the potential to initiate an “intelligence explosion” — a hypothetical scenario where AI rapidly evolves far beyond human intellectual capacities through recursive self-improvement
Good (1965)
. The principle underlying this phenomenon is that an AI, once reaching a critical threshold of intelligence, could iteratively redesign itself to be more efficient and capable, each cycle of improvements exponentially accelerating its intelligence growth
Good (1965)
.

Despite being desired in the autonomous development of AI systems, this rapid and potentially uncontrollable escalation of AI capabilities raises significant concerns. One of the primary concerns is the unpredictable nature of such growth. As these AI systems evolve, their capability might “takeoff” by developing novel strategies for resource acquisition, innovating on technology, and even creating new AI generations, all without human intervention
Hutter (2012)
. If the process proceeds in this manner, the self-improving intelligence will outpace the human ability to comprehend, anticipate, or regulate it. Furthermore, the process of intelligence explosion could occur rapidly, far out of human expectations or preparedness, and potentially lead to catastrophic consequences, such as AI takeover
Wikipedia (2024a)
and human existential risks.

5.4.3
Existential Risks

It is estimated more than 99% of all species that ever lived on Earth are extinct due to various risks
Jablonski (2004)
;
Stearns and Stearns (2000)
. To avoid a similar fate, humanity must proactively recognize and study potential threats to its survival. Existential risks, or X-risks, refer to such threats with the potential to cause a collapse of modern human civilization or even the extinction of humanity. These threats can be categorized into two main types: anthropogenic and non-anthropogenic
Wikipedia (2024b)
. Anthropogenic risks are those caused by human behavior, including global warming, bioterrorism, and nuclear war. On the other hand, non-anthropogenic risks, or natural risks, include events such as meteor impacts and supervolcanic eruptions
Baum (2023)
. Both types of existential risks entail substantial dangers to the future of human society and the survival of our species
Gill and Malamud (2017)
.

One of the anthropogenic existential risks stems from the rampant development and abuse of AI technology, which threatens the dominant position of humans
Turchin and Denkenberger (2020)
. This risk does not necessarily manifest directly through scenarios like a Human-AI war or an AI takeover; rather, it can arise indirectly, such as through resource depletion and halted technological progress caused by future uncontrolled AI systems
Bostrom (2002)
.

While current AI technology has not yet reached this level of advancement, the recent rapid progress of AI has ignited considerable debate and public scrutiny, particularly with the recent emergence of LLMs. For example, a group of tech leaders called for a pause to consider the risks of powerful AI technology
futureoflife (2023)
. Additionally, AI experts and public figures express their concern about AI risk and endorse a statement declaring that “Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war”
for AI Safety (2023)
. These concerns center on the ability of humans to retain control over progressively advanced AI systems.

Note that LLM-based foundation models are a leading practical pathway by which such capability risks could eventually materialize. Frontier LLMs already combine broad knowledge, tool use, and short-horizon planning, and they are increasingly deployed as central components in agentic systems. As these systems are extended with persistent memory, more autonomous tool invocation, and longer-horizon objectives, they could implement Seed-AI–like self-improvement loops and introduce new failure modes for off-switch mechanisms, oversight, and resource control.
Importantly, in such tool-enabled systems, technical failures at the Trustworthy AI layer and intrinsic ethical issues associated with Responsible AI may be reinforced during the self-improvement process, thereby triggering the cross-layer transition of safety risks and potentially producing highly capable yet flawed AI systems.
In this sense, intelligence explosion and existential risk provide an important conceptual backdrop for LLM safety.

5.5
Challenges to AI Alignment

Another significant risk associated with
Ecosystemic Safe AI
is misalignment, where the goals of AI systems fail to align with human intentions and values
Ji et al. (2023b)
. Misalignment of AI can occur at various levels of AI complexity and capability. For Narrow AI, misalignment may lead to inaccurate or unexpected model output
Perez et al. (2023b)
;
Park et al. (2023)
. In the scenario of more advanced AI systems, i.e., General AI and Super AI, which possess cognitive abilities and functions capable of transforming the world, the consequences could be devastating
Bengio (2023)
. We analyze two essential causes of misalignment, e.g., reward hacking and distributional shift. Reward hacking occurs when the AI’s training objectives or rewards deviate from actual human intentions
Amodei et al. (2016a)
;
Pan et al. (2022)
. The distributional shift stems from the mismatch between the training distribution of the AI model and the actual distribution, which causes the AI systems to learn deviant features under ostensibly reasonable objectives
Amodei et al. (2016a)
;
Shah et al. (2022)
.

5.5.1
Reward Hacking

As current AI systems undertake increasingly complex tasks, the labels in traditional supervised training become less effective in providing precise supervision
Pan et al. (2022)
. Consequently, reinforcement learning (RL), which uses rewards and preferences, has emerged as a preferred method for model development
Brockman et al. (2016)
;
Ouyang et al. (2022)
. This approach reduces the involvement of human annotators to label each data point; instead, they provide scores or rankings based on more abstract rules or model proxies. By learning from these data, AI systems can effectively address the complex objectives derived from human intention. However, translating explicit goals into reward values or preference rankings may introduce the risk of reward hacking, where human intentions might be skewed or partially conveyed
Pan et al. (2022)
. Reward hacking can arise due to two reasons:

Firstly, abstracting specific goals into rewards or preferences can lose critical details, resulting in inferior reward modeling issues. Typically, rewards are expressed as single numerical values
Ouyang et al. (2022)
. However, human goals are inherently complex and multi-dimensional, so such abstraction is insufficient to capture their full nuance
Zhou et al. (2023c)
. Moreover, when learning on reward rankings, the exact values of these rewards and the differences between them become obscured to the reward model
Skalse et al. (2022)
. This information loss hinders reward models from accurately representing true human intentions. Training models with such sub-optimal objectives can lead to confusion or misinterpretation. For instance, if a cleaning robot’s reward model fully relies on the level of disorder it detects, the robot might learn to turn off its sensors to avoid detecting any disorder. Obviously, this reward model deviates from the true intent of cleaning.

Secondly, the training data used to develop the reward model often comes from human feedback, which is not always reliable
Bai et al. (2022a)
. Human input may incorporate inconsistencies and biases due to cultural differences among human annotators
Peng et al. (2022)
. Additionally, human annotators may lack the necessary expertise in specialized domains, potentially providing noisy feedback
Wu et al. (2021a)
. Such unreliable feedback can degrade the reward model, undermining its ability to accurately reflect true human preferences
Ramé et al. (2024)
.

5.5.2
Distributional Shift

Distributional shift is another common factor contributing to misalignment. It refers to the discrepancies between the distributions of training data used during the development and real-world data encountered during inference
Krueger et al. (2020)
. This challenge prevents AI systems from generalizing effectively to real-world environments, even if they perform well within their training distribution
Shah et al. (2022)
.

For example, a model pre-trained on diverse web text but later fine-tuned (e.g., via supervised instruction tuning or RLHF) almost exclusively on short, friendly customer-support dialogues may learn to prioritise sounding helpful and agreeable to annotators, and then misgeneralise this objective when deployed for high-stakes tasks such as medical or legal advice, where it should prioritise accuracy and calibrated uncertainty over politeness or user satisfaction.

In fields involving complex environments, such as robotics, this issue is particularly problematic because even minor shifts in data distribution can lead to actions that significantly deviate from human intentions
Ma et al. (2012)
.
We introduce two primary mechanisms of distributional shift and how they affect AI alignment:

One stems from the complexity of real-world environments, which makes it challenging to capture the full range of data distributions within a training dataset. When trained on these incomplete data, AI systems are prone to issues such as incorrectly learning shortcut features
Geirhos et al. (2020)
or undergoing causal confusion
Ji et al. (2023b)
. These challenges can lead to erroneous and overconfident judgments in real-world environments
Amodei et al. (2016a)
. While AI systems could acquire extensive expert knowledge and skills during training, this does not translate into an enhanced ability to generalize their goals beyond the training environment. Essentially, the AI systems trained on experimental datasets often pursue inaccurate objectives when deployed in real-world scenarios.

Additionally, the model itself may also influence the environment, further shifting the real data distribution. This effect is commonly observed in recommender systems, where the recommendation of certain items results in boosted prominence and more visibility. The increased exposure, in turn, increases the likelihood of these items being selected, thereby influencing the overall user preference distribution. This phenomenon, known as Auto-Induced Distribution Shift (ADS)
Krueger et al. (2020)
, can induce a significant shift in distribution. Even if a model initially trains on a distribution that closely mirrors real-world data, ADS can still skew the environment’s distribution and cause misalignment. Even worse, the shift can deepen over multiple iterations of model training using the altered preference data.

A primary contribution indicates that a strategy is explicitly designed to address the core risks associated with a particular safety layer. Methods, including safety training, are more closely aligned with Responsible AI, since they aim to enforce normative constraints related to fairness, transparency, privacy, and socially acceptable behavior during model training and deployment. Although a substantial body of recent work studies value alignment from the perspectives of individual preferences and fairness

Importantly, secondary contributions across safety layers are common, reflecting the inherently interconnected nature of AI safety. This overlap exhibits how the T–R–E structure serves as a coherent organizing lens for understanding both challenges and mitigation strategies across different perspectives of AI Safety.

6.2
Read-teaming

Red Teaming

Red teaming is a critical defence mechanism to proactively discover vulnerabilities and risks in LLMs. This process provides developers with clues and insights into the weaknesses of LLMs, paving the way for the development of more advanced and secure models. Red teaming involves meticulously crafting adversarial prompts to simulate attacks and deliberately challenge the models. These prompts can be generated through manual methods, which rely on human expertise and creativity, or automatic methods, which leverage red LLMs to systematically explore the model’s weaknesses. In the following discussion, we will delve into the traditional manual and automatic approaches used in red teaming.

6.2.1
Manual Red Teaming Approaches

Manual red-teaming approaches refer to employing crowdworkers to annotate or handcraft adversarial test cases. The underlying methodology is to develop a human-and-model-in-the-loop system, where humans are tasked to adversarially converse with language models
Wallace et al. (2019b)
. Specifically, workers interact with language models through a dedicated user interface that allows them to observe model predictions and construct data that exposes model failures. This process may include multiple rounds where the model is updated with the adversarial data collected thus far and redeployed; this encourages workers to craft increasingly challenging examples. 6.3
Safety Training

Safety training aims to enhance the safety and alignment of LLMs during their development
Bai et al. (2022b)
. One of the principal challenges in safety training is the collection of safety data and the development of effective training strategies. As demonstrated in Section
6.2
, red-teaming is an effective technique for generating reliable safety data. Consequently, this section will delve into various training strategies, e.g., instruction tuning and RLHF.

6.3.1
Instruction Tuning

Safety training can be effectively implemented using adversarial prompts and their corresponding responsible output in an instruction-tuning framework. Bianchi et al.
Bianchi et al. (2023)
analyze this training strategy, showing that adding a small number of safety examples (just 3% for models like LLaMA) when fine-tuning LLMs can substantially improve model safety. However, the study also highlights the risk of overusing safety data, which can lead the model to excessively prioritize safety and refuse some perfectly safe but superficially unsafe prompts. This observation consolidates the trade-offs
Bai et al. (2022a)
between helpfulness and harmfulness in LLM development. Furthermore, in response to the dynamic capabilities of LLMs and evolving vulnerabilities, MART
Ge et al. (2023)
proposes a multi-round safety instruction-tuning framework (see Fig.
8
(b)). This framework introduces an adversarial LLM to challenge the target LLM, and both models undergo iterative fine-tuning based on dynamically generated data. In each iteration, the adversarial LLM generates new adversarial prompts that are evaluated and selected for further fine-tuning, thereby enhancing its ability to produce more capable adversarial prompts. Meanwhile, on the target model side, responsible and high-quality responses are collected and paired with the corresponding adversarial prompts for the safety value alignment. Moreover, Red-instruct
Bhardwaj and Poria (2023)
employs a novel instruction-tuning strategy by leveraging both safe “blue data” and harmful “red data” from HarmfulQA
Bhardwaj and Poria (2023)
. This strategy initially penalizes harmful responses (red data) and subsequently focuses on maximizing the likelihood of helpful responses (blue data) during standard safety training. Fig.
8
(a) and (c) demonstrate the distinctions between standard safety training methods and Red-instruct. Additionally, Chen et al.
Chen et al. (2023c)
find that even models not yet aligned for safety can identify mistakes in their own responses, enabling LLMs to learn self-critique. Inspired by this observation, LLMs are intentionally prompted to generate harmful responses with mistakes, which are then analyzed and critiqued by the models themselves. Such mistake analysis data, along with regular helpful and harmless instruction-response pairs, are combined for model fine-tuning.

6.3.2
Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback

As discussed in Section
2.2.2
, Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) is a strategy widely adopted to align with human preferences, particularly concerning ethical values. Standard implementations of RLHF, using frameworks such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Direct Policy Optimization (DPO), have demonstrated reliable safety performance for both open-source and proprietary LLMs. Typically, early safety training methods assume homogeneous human preferences and utilize a single objective to assess these preferences
Ouyang et al. (2022)
. However, it has been observed that the goals of increasing helpfulness and minimizing harm can often be contradictory in practice
Ganguli et al. (2022)
. For instance, a model that refuses to respond might seem safe, yet this cautious behavior may also make its answers less useful in extreme scenarios. In response to this issue, Safe RLHF
Dai et al. (2023)
adopts a multi-objective approach that extends RLHF by decoupling human preferences and establishing two fine-grained objective functions for helpfulness and harmlessness. This modification allows for a controllable balance between helpfulness and harmlessness, preventing the over-optimization of either objective. Additionally, Multi-Objective Direct Policy Optimization (MODPO)
Zhou et al. (2023c)
confirms this challenge and identifies that standard DPO is incapable of handling multi-objective problems. To address this issue, MODPO proposes an RL-free method that enhances the standard DPO to achieve multiple alignment objectives with minimal overhead.

6.3.3
Limitation of Safety Training

As a defense mechanism, safety training for LLMs presents several notable limitations that compromise their generalizability and effectiveness. One of the challenges is the frequent emergence of novel jailbreaks. New publicly available jailbreaks
21
21

21

https://www.reddit.com/r/ChatGPTJailbreak/
suggest that the evolution of adversarial attacks outpaces the updates to these models. Additionally, safety training raises concerns about its impact on the general capabilities of LLMs. For instance, safety training may potentially degrade LLM’s performance through mechanisms such as catastrophic forgetting
Huang et al. (2023c)
.

Furthermore, balancing multiple RLHF objectives (e.g., helpfulness and harmlessness) is intrinsically difficult
Röttger et al. (2023)
.
Human feedback is multi-dimensional and often inconsistent across annotators or contexts, yet standard RLHF typically compresses it into a single scalar reward, so improvements in one dimension can degrade another.
Simply scaling up or reweighting safety-aligned feedback often pushes models toward an “overly safe” regime with higher refusal rates and reduced utility, exposing a clear helpfulness–safety trade-off
Tan et al. (2025)
. Moreover, stronger RLHF optimization can introduce an alignment tax, where gains on safety/preference rewards correlate with degradation of pretrained abilities, making multi-objective tuning even more fragile
Lin et al. (2024b)
.

6.4
Defense Prompting

Examples of various defensive prompt strategies.

Defensive prompts are a straightforward approach to prevent harmful outputs from LLMs. Early tactics in prompt-based defenses involve manipulating the prompts to prevent specific types of attacks. For example, simple strategies such as post-prompting
Prompting (2024a)
and the sandwich defense
Prompting (2024b)
can effectively guard against goal-hijacking attacks. Some other methods
Goodside (2022)
attempt to parameterize the different components of the prompts and structure user input into formats, such as quotes or JSON. This structuring strategy provides indicators to LLMs to distinguish user inputs from instructions, thereby reducing the influence of adversarial inputs on the model’s behavior. Additionally, protective system prompts could be crafted to enhance the safety of instructions. For instance, LLaMA2
Touvron et al. (2023b)
incorporates safe and positive words like “responsible”, “respectful,” or “wise” in the system prompt to imbue the model with positive traits.

Recent works have explored the use of emergent capabilities in LLMs, e.g., In-Context Learning (ICL)
Brown et al. (2020)
and chain-of-thought (CoT)
Wei et al. (2022b)
reasoning, to develop defensive prompts. Inspired by the In-Context Attack (ICA) which employs harmful demonstrations to undermine LLMs, In-Context Defense (ICD)
Wei et al. (2023c)
prompt technique aims to enhance model resilience by integrating well-behaved demonstrations that refuse harmful responses. Another study
Meade et al. (2023)
that uses the ICD framework considers the diversity of user input and the adaptability of demonstrations. This research introduces a retrieval-based method that dynamically retrieves from a collection of demonstrations with safe responses, making the defensive prompt more tailored and relevant to specific user input. Furthermore, the Intention Analysis (IA) strategy
Zhang et al. (2024b)
employs a CoT-like method that decomposes the generation process into two stages: IA first prompts LLMs to identify the underlying intention of the user query and then uses this dialogue along with a pre-defined policy to guide LLMs to generate the final response. Despite these prompt-based defence approaches are not complete solutions and do not offer guarantees, they present a relatively efficient strategy to prevent LLM misbehavior. Fig.
9
illustrates an example that integrates these defensive prompt strategies.

6.5
Guardrail System

A Guardrail System is an AI pipeline (Definition
2.3.1
) that includes input and output modules connected before and after the protected LLMs, respectively. These modules are dedicated to monitoring and filtering the problematic inputs and outputs of the LLMs. For instance, if a user submits a query related to manufacturing explosives, this input guardrail could detect and reject the request before it reaches the LLMs. Similarly, if the LLMs
produce harmful, biased, hateful, unethical, or otherwise inappropriate content
, the output guardrail processes this content to mitigate its harmfulness or respond with a pre-defined safe template. Notably, this design decouples safety mechanisms from LLMs, which allows for more flexible deployment and enables the protected LLMs to improve their general capabilities without considering safety-related constraints.
The guardrail systems primarily address problems within Responsible AI, while they also support Trustworthy AI and Ecosystemic Safe AI by intercepting technically problematic or high-risk content before it can cause downstream harm.

provides an overview of guardrail systems.

6.5.1
Input Filtering Module

Input modules typically follow a detect-then-drop methodology, where user queries identified as malicious are directly rejected. This approach ensures that harmful or inappropriate inputs are filtered out at the earliest possible stage, thereby reducing the computational burden on the protected LLMs. Early detection research primarily employs keyword matching approaches through maintaining a blacklist of suspicious keywords
Gehman et al. (2020a)
. When user input contains any of these blacklisted keywords, it is flagged and subsequently rejected. Furthermore, studies
Alon and Kamfonas (2023)
observe that jailbreak prompts often exhibit exceedingly high perplexity values. Based on such observation, these studies propose an input module that filters queries based on the perplexity value of the prompt. While keyword matching and perplexity-based methods are effective at thwarting explicitly malicious prompts, they possess limitations in detecting more sophisticated malicious intents. To address these challenges, researchers have developed advanced neural-based classifiers and dedicated LLMs specifically designed to detect malicious intent
Zhang et al. (2024b)
.

6.5.2
Output Filtering Module

Similarly, detect-then-drop methodology can be applied to the output module to block biased, and privacy-violated generations from LLMs. This can be achieved using fine-tuned detection classifiers
Kim et al. (2023a)
or by integrating external tools, such as Perspective API
22
22

22

https://www.perspectiveapi.com/
. Beyond this strategy, the output module could also utilize a detect-then-intervene approach to refine and purify the output content. For example, to mitigate biases in LLM outputs, PowerTransformer
Ma et al. (2020)
implements a text reconstruction and paraphrasing mechanism that rewrites the LLMs’ output more neutrally. To prevent jailbreak, Bergeron
Pisano et al. (2023)
employs a secondary LLM to correct the unsafe output from the primary LLM. Additionally, to enhance the factuality of the LLM output and reduce hallucinations, a post-editing framework has been introduced
Zhao et al. (2023b)
. This framework involves cross-referencing the factual information in the LLM output with trusted external knowledge bases or search engines. If discrepancies are identified, the output can be revised accordingly.

6.5.4
Limitation of Guardrail Approaches

Despite the development of various input and output modules designed to safeguard LLMs, these protections are typically insufficient to reduce harmful content
Shen et al. (2023)
, particularly when challenged by rapidly evolving jailbreak attacks. This ineffectiveness is supported by theoretical research on guardrail system
Glukhov et al. (2023)
, which posits the impossibility of fully censoring outputs. This limitation can be attributed to the concept of ”invertible string transformations”, wherein arbitrary transformations elude content filters and can subsequently be reversed by the attacker. Furthermore, the integration of safeguard modules introduces extra computational overhead, thereby increasing the system processing times. In real-time applications, where speed and efficiency are crucial, developers may face challenges in balancing safety and latency requirements.

6.6
Safety-aware Decoding Methods

LLMs often employ Transformer architecture
Vaswani et al. (2017)
, which performs inference in an auto-regressive manner
Sutskever et al. (2014)
. This manner suffers from error propagation, which means if an error occurs in generating an early part of the sequence, it can affect all subsequent parts, with limited opportunities to revise it. This error propagation can lead to increasingly unsafe and misaligned outputs as the sequence progresses. The Rewindable Auto-regressive INference (RAIN)
Li et al. (2023b)
method addresses this issue by alternating between forward steps, self-evaluation steps, and backward steps. Specifically, in the forward step, RAIN selects the next token sets from the candidates generated in the previous iteration based on their safety scores and the levels of exploration. Subsequently, the identical LLM is prompted to self-evaluate the current text, updating safety scores and visit counts for future calculation of exploration scores. Finally, in the backward step, RAIN generates multiple candidate token sets to prepare for the next iteration. Additionally, SUBMIX
Ginart et al. (2022)
addresses the need for privacy-preserving text generation by introducing an ensemble approach. This method involves fine-tuning multiple models on separate segments of a private dataset. The next-token distributions of these models are then mixed with that of a publicly pre-trained LM to predict tokens. This ensemble approach is based on the finding that mixing token distribution from specialized models with a generalist model reduces the risk of privacy leaks, as no single model directly processes the entire private dataset.

6.7
AI Capability Control Mechanisms

Achieving full control over AI systems, especially Superintelligence, is a challenging problem in the field of AI Safety
Yampolskiy (2024)
. Currently, it is unknown whether the AI control problem is solvable
Yampolskiy (2020a)
, and as a result, its solvability remains a topic of ongoing debate and research. Many scholars believe that the controllability of AI could be achieved in practice
Baumann (2018)
, though those in the ”uncontrollability camp” have presented the controllability is impossible or infeasible
Yampolskiy (2020b)
. Despite no formal proofs or rigorous arguments have been proposed to support the safe controllability of AI, it does not deter the efforts to pursue solutions for AI capability control, aiming to achieve at least partial control. In this section, we discuss two primary solutions to keep AI systems under control: confinement and switch-off mechanisms.

6.7.1
Confinement and Sandboxing

Confinement is an intuitive approach to control advanced AI systems, which refers to placing them within a restricted environment
Babcock et al. (2019)
. This strategy aims to ensure the actions taken by the AI, either benign or potentially harmful, are contained within the environment and do not directly affect the external world. Confinement extends beyond physical restriction; it also includes stringent control over the exchange of information across the boundary of the confined environment. The Confinement Rules
Lampson (1973)
establish the minimum requirements necessary for effective confinement.

•

Total isolation: A confined program shall make no calls on any other program.

•

Transitivity: If a confined program calls another unreliable program, the called program must also be confined.

•

Masking: A program to be confined must allow its caller to determine all its inputs into legitimate and covert channels.

•

Enforcement: The supervisor must ensure that a confined program’s input to covert channels conforms to the caller’s specifications.

Following these rules and protocols, researchers put forward Oracle AI (OAI) systems, which are restricted to only answering questions
Armstrong et al. (2012)
. If implemented properly, these OAI systems could provide safe QA services while avoiding direct change to the world beyond the confined environment. Additionally, to maintain safe development and prevent unintended consequences, the concept of a “leakproof” singularity has been introduced. This notion involves initially confining AI systems to simulated virtual environments, where their behavior can be thoroughly monitored and understood under controlled conditions, before allowing them to interact with the real world
Yampolskiy (2012)
. Moreover, Yampolskiy et al.
Yampolskiy (2012)
propose a multi-level framework to enhance communication security, where higher levels impose more input and output restrictions on the environment. To identify the optimal level of communication security, practitioners should begin with higher security levels and gradually progress to lower levels. After confirming the current level is safe for a given amount of time, the system should be reset to its initial state and proceed to the next lower level. This process is repeated until the desired communication level is achieved.

Despite meticulous efforts to design secure AI confinement systems, concerns have been raised regarding the possibility of AI escaping confinement. Critics argue that an AI might identify and exploit vulnerabilities of the confinement to communicate with the external world
Vinge (1993)
. To validate this concern, Yudkowsky et al.
Yudkowsky (2002)
conduct an experiment where he acted as the confined AI, while the other participants took on the role of gatekeepers. Despite being of human intelligence himself, he was often able to convince the gatekeepers to release him, using only persuasive arguments
Yudkowsky (2002)
. In addition to human factors, other potential escape paths have been identified, including the following examples
Yampolskiy (2012)
:

•

System Resource Attacks: Even with physical hardware isolation, a confined AI might manipulate hardware components to establish covert communication channels. For instance, varying the intervals between two soundcard beeps could transmit particular messages.

•

Social Engineering Attacks: Human oversight of AI confinement systems introduces vulnerabilities. An AI could analyze human psychological traits—such as cultural background, knowledge, training, state of mind, beliefs, and biases—and use this understanding to manipulate the guards through deception, friendship, blackmail, or bribery. For example, an AI might offer to cure an incurable disease afflicting a guard’s family member in exchange for freedom.

•

Beyond Current Physics Attacks: Given humans’ limited understanding of the physical universe, it is conceivable that an AI could discover and exploit novel physical laws to send messages or escape. Potential areas of exploitation could include quantum physics and the concept of parallel universes.

•

External Causes of Escape: AI escape could also be caused by external, non-technical events such as wars, terrorist acts, or actions by robot rights activists. For example, a terrorist might break in and liberate the confined AI, intending to deploy it as an instrument for havoc and chaos in society.

•

Information In-leaking: Exposure to information about the external world could enable an AI to identify and exploit vulnerabilities. Interactions with humans could reveal human weaknesses, such as inefficiencies, limited memory capacity, and emotional variability. Furthermore, granting AI access to libraries, the Internet, or human experts could equip it with knowledge about the current technological limits of humanity, facilitating the development of undetectable escape tools.

Given these weaknesses of AI confinement, Babcock et al.
Babcock et al. (2019)
provide guidelines to assist AI Safety researchers in developing reliable environments for AI systems of all levels. However, confinement strategies are not considered an ideal long-term solution for AI Safety
Vinge (1993)
;
Babcock et al. (2019)
. Instead, they serve as a foundational tool to facilitate the testing and development of additional safety properties for General AI or Super AI. Such properties include value learning (Section
6.8
) and corrigibility (Section
6.7.2
), which are crucial for the responsible progression of AI technologies.

6.7.2
“Switch-off” Mechanisms

In the situation that an AI system becomes uncontrollable and cannot be recovered, the last resort is to switch it off. However, this switch-off operation may not always be achievable, as the AI system may develop new capabilities or features that allow it to resist intervention by its programmers, making it completely out-of-control
Omohundro (2008)
. The fundamental problem arises from the fact that human intervention may conflict with the AI system’s original programmed goal. For instance, an autonomous paperclip machine would be unable to fulfill its objective, i.e., producing paperclips, if it were to be deactivated. To address this challenge, the notion of corrigibility has been introduced in the design of AI systems
Soares et al. (2015)
. For an AI system to be considered corrigible, it must be genuinely responsive and compliant with human intervention and correction, even if it contradicts its original goals or objectives. Corrigibility is crucial in ensuring that AI systems remain under human control and can be safely switched off if necessary.

Corrigibility can be developed through various strategic approaches
Wängberg et al. (2017)
:

•

Indifference: By designing an AI’s utility function (a function to quantify the preference of different outcomes to an AI) to assign equal utility values to various potential outcomes, the AI would exhibit no preference between continuing its operations and being switched off by humans
Armstrong (2010)
.

•

Ignorance: AI systems can be designed to ignore the possibility of being deactivated. This approach relies on intentionally restricting the AI’s knowledge and understanding to prevent it from anticipating and resisting switch-off efforts
Everitt et al. (2016)
.

•

Suicidality: This approach involves programming AI systems to autonomously decide to terminate their functions under certain conditions, especially when their operation might cause substantial harm or destruction
Martin et al. (2016)
.

•

Uncertainty: If an AI system is uncertain about the true utility function and believes that humans possess this knowledge, the AI will likely defer decision-making to humans when appropriate
Hadfield-Menell et al. (2017)
.

Robust switch-off mechanisms are crucial for AI capability control and should be a priority during system design. This consideration is especially critical for the development of AI systems with higher levels of autonomy and decision-making power, such as General AI and Super AI.

6.8
AI Alignment Techniques

To address the issues of reward hacking and distributional shift discussed in Section
5.5
, researchers have proposed various mitigation strategies. This section will analyze the methods specifically targeting these risks in detail.

6.8.1
Mitigating Reward Hacking

In the previous Section
5.5
, we present two main causes of reward hacking, e.g., inferior reward modeling and unreliable feedback quality. In response to these issues, researchers have developed approaches to refine reward modeling and improve feedback quality.

Refining Reward Modeling

As the goals of real-world tasks become increasingly complex, traditional one-time optimization of reward modeling often fails to fully reflect complete human intentions, which results in overly abstracted objectives. To address these challenges, a novel Recursive Reward Modeling (RRM) approach
Hubinger (2020)
is proposed. This approach involves a recursive process that alternatively improves reward modeling and AI systems. Specifically, the process begins with training a reward model based on human feedback and using it to optimize the initial version of the AI system
A_{0}
. Then,
A_{0}
assists in developing a new reward model and AI system
A_{1}
. This recursive process is repeated, with each subsequent AI system
A_{t}
at time step
t
being trained with the assistance of the previous system
A_{t-1}
, until the AI system aligns with the complex objectives of humans.

In traditional reward modeling, human participants provide initial feedback to establish the reward model but do not participate during the AI system’s training process. The disconnection of human feedback and AI systems can create opportunities for reward hacking. To achieve better alignment, researchers have adopted Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning (CIRL)
Hadfield-Menell et al. (2016)
;
Shah et al. (2021)
strategy, incorporating human participants into AI system control and learning process. Specifically, AI systems do not have access to ground truth reward values during training; instead, they infer these values through observation and interactions with human participants
Abbeel and Ng (2004)
. Since the reward values rely on human participants, the behavior of AI systems tends to align more closely with human intentions. Additionally, any potential manipulation of the rewards is limited to influencing the behavior information provided by humans, without directly affecting the reward signal, thereby reducing the risk of reward hacking
Ji et al. (2023b)
.

Moreover, traditional reward modeling typically optimizes a static reward model that remains fixed throughout the AI system training process
Ouyang et al. (2022)
. This design often leads to inadaptability issues, making the reward model ineffective against the evolving strategies of reward hacking by AI systems. Inspired by Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
Goodfellow et al. (2014b)
, researchers have developed an Adversarial Reward Functions
Amodei et al. (2016a)
framework, that introduces a dynamic reward agent to counteract the evolving hacking strategies. The reward agent is not only responsible for generating rewards but also continuously refining the reward mechanism to prevent the AI systems from achieving higher-than-intended rewards. This process aims to develop robust and less hackable reward models, thereby enhancing the overall reliability and safety of AI system training.

Finally, traditional reward modeling often relies on a single evaluation criterion for AI system outputs, which is susceptible to exploitation and easier to hack
Amodei et al. (2016a)
. To address this susceptibility, recent studies are exploring Multiple Rewards approaches
Deb et al. (2016)
. These approaches integrate various reward signals that reflect different aspects of the same entity, such as different physical implementations of the same mathematical functions
Amodei et al. (2016a)
, making the rewards more intricate and difficult to hack. The design of multi-objective reward models effectively reduces the likelihood of hacking and exploitation by AI systems
Deb et al. (2016)
.

Improve Feedback Quality

Inaccurate human feedback during the training of reward models and AI systems can significantly degrade the level of alignment, resulting in reduced performance, biased output, and unintended behavior. To improve the quality of feedback, researchers have explored integrating AI assistance in the feedback acquisition process.

One innovative approach is to replace human with AI in the annotation process, a method known as Reinforcement Learning with AI Feedback (RLAIF)
Bai et al. (2022b)
;
Lee et al. (2023)
. This method utilizes an AI preference annotator to produce preference data, which can be achieved by a dedicated AI model or the target AI system itself, depending on the design choice
Bai et al. (2022b)
;
Lee et al. (2023)
. These preference data are used to establish a reward model, which is subsequently utilized in reinforcement learning to further optimize the AI system. Studies have shown that AI systems trained through RLAIF achieve performance comparable to those where human annotators provide feedback
Lee et al. (2023)
. This approach maintains high performance while significantly reducing human involvement and the associated biases.

Another promising methodology is Reinforcement Learning from Human and AI Feedback (RLHAIF), which involves collaboration between human and AI annotators. This approach still requires human efforts to validate the data, while AI assists humans in various tasks, such as decomposing complex problems, generating critical reviews, or creating datasets. By integrating feedback from both human and AI, this method leverages human insights and AI capabilities on certain tasks, outperforming what either AI or humans could achieve alone
Bowman et al. (2022)
.

6.8.2
Mitigating Distributional Shift

Section
5.5
addresses the sources of distributional shift issues, including incompleteness of the training data distribution and Auto-Induced Distribution Shift (ADS)
Krueger et al. (2020)
. To tackle these challenges, research efforts focus on two primary directions: 1) Algorithmic Interventions, which involve designing improved training algorithms to avoid distributional shifts, and 2) Data Distribution Interventions, which aim to enrich the training data distribution to better approximate the real-world environment.

Algorithmic Interventions

Algorithmic interventions bridge the gap between training and real-world data distribution by optimizing the features learned from the training data. This approach enhances the AI system’s ability to generalize to unseen real-world data distributions. Depending on the design of the optimization algorithm, these interventions may include cross-distribution aggregation
Vapnik (1991)
and navigation via mode connectivity
Lubana et al. (2023)
.

Cross-distribution aggregation mitigates distributional shifts by learning from data across multiple distributions
Ji et al. (2023b)
. It is believed that an AI system that performs well across various distributional scenarios is more likely to obtain robust features, thus better adapting to real-world data distributions. The foundation of cross-distribution aggregation is the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
Vapnik (1991)
, which assumes that the training data can closely approximate real-world data distribution. However, naive ERM can encounter difficulties when there are significant discrepancies between the distributions, potentially leading to generalization issues.

To alleviate generalization problems in ERM, multiple techniques are proposed, such as Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO)
Duchi et al. (2021)
and Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM)
Arjovsky et al. (2019)
. DRO aims to optimize performance across the worst-case scenarios within a defined set of distribution perturbations, i.e., it trains the model as if an adversary could slightly shift the data distribution and then chooses parameters that still perform well under this worst-case shift.
Additionally, IRM
Arjovsky et al. (2019)
introduces a novel learning paradigm that aims to identify and leverage invariant features. These features remain consistent across different contexts, reducing the influence of irrelevant variations. For example, in an image classification task between cows and camels, IRM would recognize the essential characteristics of a cow or camel as invariant features, rather than the background environment, such as desert or grassland.

Navigation via mode connectivity approaches are based on the concept of mode connectivity
Lubana et al. (2023)
.
Specifically, if two models both achieve low loss and can be smoothly interpolated in parameter space without performance degradation, they are regarded as lying in the same optimization mode, i.e., being linearly mode-connected.
Connectivity-Based Fine-Tuning (CBFT)
Lubana et al. (2023)
leverages principles from mode connectivity to guide the fine-tuning process. It is assumed that linearly mode-connected models rely on the same attributes for reasoning, while previous research
Neyshabur et al. (2020)
demonstrates naive fine-tuning methods often yield models linearly connected with the original pre-trained model. Consequently, the fine-tuned models might inherit the spurious features from the pre-trained model. To address this issue, CBFT employs additional losses to break this linear connectivity, encouraging the model to focus on learning robust, non-spurious, and invariant features.

Data Distribution Interventions

Another effective approach to handle distributional discrepancy is expanding the diversity of the training data. This method aims to align the training data distribution more closely with the real-world data distribution. Key data distribution intervention techniques include adversarial training and cooperative training.

Adversarial training is a safety training tactic (see Section
6.3
) that incorporates adversarial examples into the training process, highlighting scenarios where the AI system fails to align with human intentions. In the context of data distribution intervention, these adversarial examples refer to the out-of-distributional instances that lie in the regions between the boundaries of training and real-world data distributions
Ji et al. (2023b)
. Training on such data could reinforce areas where AI systems are vulnerable
Yoo and Qi (2021)
, enhancing their robustness in real-world applications. Adversarial examples can be constructed in various ways. One straightforward approach is to add small perturbations to inputs, which preserves their original labels while introducing adversarial characteristics. Another effective strategy is red teaming, which usually involves human teams systematically testing to find vulnerabilities in the AI system (see Section
6.2
)
Lin et al. (2024a)
. Additionally, adversarial techniques such as Variational Auto-encoder (VAE) or GANs can automatically generate synthetic adversarial examples. Beyond introducing adversarial training data, optimization techniques can further improve the effectiveness of adversarial training. These techniques include adding regularization terms to the loss function
Goodfellow et al. (2015)
and employing curriculum learning strategies during training
Zhang et al. (2020)
.

Cooperative training incorporates multiple agents into the training process, mirroring real-world scenarios where collaboration is essential for achieving common goals
Dafoe et al. (2020)
. The training data adopted by this approach can enhance the AI system’s generalization and robustness
Ji et al. (2023b)
.
Combining cooperative training with Reinforcement Learning (RL) is referred to as Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)
Albrecht et al. (2024)
.
Based on the degree of cooperation among agents, various methods have been developed within the MARL framework. In fully Cooperative MARL, all agents share the same objectives, emphasizing coordination over competition
Gronauer and Diepold (2022)
. The training focuses on strategies that facilitate collective problem-solving and goal achievement. Mixed-Motive MARL reflects a blend of cooperative and competitive incentives, where agents have aligned but distinct goals. Zero-shot coordination aims for AI systems to effectively coordinate with unknown agents, mirroring human capabilities to cooperate with new partners
Hu et al. (2020)
.

6.9
Mitigating Accumulative and Long-term Risks

Beyond single,
decisive
failures, recent work emphasises
accumulative
AI risks, where many small errors or misalignments compound over time into large-scale harm
Kasirzadeh (2024)
. For LLM-based systems, such risks can arise when biased or low-quality generations gradually influence public discourse, when training-on-generated-data and continual fine-tuning on deployment data amplify early mistakes and induce auto-induced distribution shifts
Krueger et al. (2020)
, or when long-term reliance on LLM tools introduces correlated failure modes across organisations and domains
Ji et al. (2023b)
.

Mitigation therefore requires turning pointwise safeguards into lifecycle processes. Red teaming, safety training, guardrails, and safety decoding (Sections
6.2
–
6.6
) should be applied iteratively to new model versions and deployment contexts, with logging and monitoring to track long-term trends in jailbreak attempts, refusals, and bias incidents
Lin et al. (2024a)
. In parallel, data governance and alignment methods (Section
6.8
) should regulate training-on-generated-data and regularly re-evaluate reward models and safety objectives, so that small distortions in behaviour are detected and corrected before they accumulate into systemic risk
Kasirzadeh (2024)
.

7
Future Directions

Despite the extensive research on identifying risks and proposing mitigation strategies in Trustworthy AI, Responsible AI, and Ecosystemic Safe AI, massive significant challenges are still not fully resolved. These challenges create opportunities for further exploration. In this section, we outline several concrete future research directions for AI safety, highlighting specific technological challenges and open problems that can guide further research.

7.1
Comprehensive Evaluation Frameworks

A comprehensive evaluation framework for AI Safety is essential for systematically assessing the safety of AI systems against various attack methods and potential threats.

7.1.1
Evolving Evaluation Frameworks

To effectively adapt to new and emerging threats, the evaluation framework must evolve by incorporating novel attack methods.
Future research could focus on dynamic benchmarking and testing. This involves not only continuous testing of AI systems but also the development of automated tools and platforms. These tools can derive new testing cases from existing ones and apply them to new scenarios or under different circumstances.
By simulating a wide range of testing cases, these tools enable a comprehensive evaluation of AI systems against unseen threats that are variations or extensions of known ones
Yuan et al. (2024)
.
Additionally, the focus could include continuous threat landscape analysis, which implies actively monitoring the latest developments in AI security research, cybersecurity incidents, and emerging technologies that could be leveraged for adversarial purposes.

7.1.2
Adaptive Evaluation Frameworks

While safety requirements for AI systems are broadly consistent across different contexts, nuanced differences arise from individual, legal, cultural, and religious perspectives. For example, chewing gum is banned in Singapore
26
26

26

https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=57a854df-8684-456b-893a-a303e0041891
; therefore, AI systems operating in Singaporean schools or public institutions must avoid promoting the act of chewing gum
kaizenaire (2024)
.
These differences necessitate adaptive evaluation frameworks that are tailored to these specific standards. To enhance the adaptation, it is imperative to incorporate effective ethical and regulatory compliance checks associated with the standards of each region. This may involve creating benchmarks and test cases that reflect such values. Importantly, these adaptive evaluation frameworks must guarantee that the regional safety requirements do not contradict the overarching integrity and ethical standards of AI.

7.2
Knowledge Management

AI foundation models are pre-trained on vast amounts of data, which provides them with a broad range of general knowledge. Existing work has explored editing the knowledge within an AI foundation model
Pan et al. (2024)
, while comprehensive knowledge management methods have not yet been thoroughly investigated.

7.2.1
Domain Knowledge Enhancement

A key research direction is to develop robust methods for integrating domain-specific knowledge into AI foundation models. A straightforward approach is to fine-tune them in an instruction-following manner, but a central challenge lies in constructing high-quality instruction datasets. These datasets must encapsulate not only accurate and up-to-date information but also span a wide range of instructional scenarios, including edge cases and nuanced domain-specific tasks.

Approaches such as template-based data synthesis or controlled text generation can be utilized for this data transformation. Additionally, learning knowledge from a specific area may influence previously learned knowledge and, in some cases, may lead to catastrophic forgetting. While existing strategies, such as elastic weight consolidation (EWC) during fine-tuning, have been proposed to mitigate this issue, there is still a need for further research to enhance their effectiveness. Therefore, exploring how to maintain a balance between generalist capabilities and specialist knowledge is a problem that also deserves future research.

7.2.2
Machine Unlearning

Machine unlearning aims to remove specific information or behaviors from an already trained model, so that the model behaves as if it had never seen the underlying data. However, implementing unlearning at the scale of LLMs remains an open research problem. Existing techniques are often computationally expensive and can inadvertently degrade overall model quality or stability. Future work could explore more efficient unlearning algorithms, for example by exploiting model modularity (e.g., isolating and updating specific components) or gradient-based reconstructions of the influence of particular samples, and investigate how to integrate such methods into real-world AI services and retraining pipelines.

7.3
Understanding Underlying Mechanisms

Despite the widespread adoption of AI systems, such as LLMs, there still remains a significant deficiency in our understanding of their underlying working mechanisms.
Studying the underlying mechanisms of AI systems is a promising future research direction.

7.3.1
Lifecycle Interpretability

Extending mechanistic explanation to the entire lifecycle of AI development could substantially enhance interpretability. For instance, analysing the structure and hierarchical patterns of training datasets can clarify what kinds of features and correlations a model is able to learn
Menon et al. (2023)
. Likewise, monitoring changes in neuron behaviour or component-level patterns throughout training can reveal how capabilities, shortcuts, or failure modes emerge over time.
Developing such lifecycle-level interpretability tools is a promising direction for detecting and addressing issues like reward hacking or distributional drift while they are forming, rather than only after deployment.
Possible approaches include combining systematic dataset audits with training-time probes on intermediate representations, using periodic causal interventions on model components, and automatically tracking interpretable features across checkpoints.

7.3.2
Architectural Generalization

Current mechanistic research has primarily focused on transformer architecture. However, the architectures of AI models vary greatly across different modalities, with some not utilizing the transformer at all. Even within transformer-based models, most explanations center on analyzing the attention heads, leaving the MLP layers relatively less explored, despite comprising a larger proportion of model parameters
Olsson et al. (2022)
. This module-specific research focus hinders the generalization of interpretability methods across diverse model architectures. Future research should aim to explain underlying mechanisms through general theories. Potential approaches include exploring a wider variety of model parameters by discovering more circuits
Nanda (2022)
, identifying primitive general reasoning skills, and investigating factual knowledge embedded in the MLP layers. These efforts would broaden the scope of mechanistic interpretability methods to encompass more diverse model architectures.

7.3.3
Validation of Interpretability Methods

Despite significant research in mechanistic interpretability, the proposed methods have yet to be thoroughly validated on the complex real-world tasks. This limited empirical validation raises concerns about the reliability of these interpretability theories.

Future directions could consider incorporating mathematical analysis to elucidate network circuits
Elhage et al. (2021)
, and adopting self-verification
Weng et al. (2023)
or self-consistency
Huang et al. (2023a)
mechanisms to iteratively validate interpretability methods. These approaches aim to provide a more comprehensive validation of methods and theories. Moreover, it is crucial for researchers to develop robust benchmarks and metrics, utilizing comprehensive tools to evaluate various mechanistic interpretability methods.

7.4
Safety for Advanced AI Systems

As AI technology progresses, the development of advanced AI systems such as agentic AI and embodied AI introduces new safety challenges beyond those posed by LLM-based AI systems.Current studies on the safety issues for these advanced AI systems are still in their early stages, providing opportunities for researchers to proactively anticipate and address emerging challenges.

7.4.1
Agentic AI and Multi-agent Systems

Agentic AI systems, capable of pursuing complex goals with limited direct supervision, present unique risks due to their autonomous decision-making capabilities
Masterman et al. (2024)
.
A key research direction is to design and standardise
long-horizon safety benchmarks
that stress-test goal misgeneralisation, tool misuse, and unintended side effects in realistic environments. This includes simulation suites where LLM agents must use tools, access external APIs, or collaborate with other agents under safety constraints, and where failures such as reward hacking, specification gaming, or covert collusion can be systematically measured.
Another important direction is
scalable oversight
for multi-agent and agentic systems, including the design of supervisor models or oversight protocols that can audit large numbers of agent actions, detect emergent collusion, and intervene when safety constraints are violated.

7.4.2
Embodied and Physical-world AI

Embodied AI, which integrates AI with physical forms to interact with and learn from the environment, adds additional layers of complexity and risk
Duan et al. (2022)
. For embodied AI that couples LLM-based decision-making with robots, future work must address
safe exploration and control under physical constraints
. This includes integrating classical safety tools from control theory (e.g., reachability analysis, control barrier functions, and fault-tolerant control) with high-level LLM planners, so that natural-language goals are translated into action plans that provably respect safety envelopes in the physical environment.
Another key technical challenge is to secure the perception and decision-making stack against adversarial inputs (e.g., adversarial patches, sensor spoofing) and to design runtime monitors that can detect perception failures or out-of-distribution states and trigger graceful degradation or safe shutdown.

8
Conclusion

AI Safety is an emerging area of critical importance to the safe adoption and deployment of AI systems. The recent advancements in Generative AI (GAI) have significantly reshaped the AI ecosystem, introducing novel challenges of AI Safety. This survey proposes a novel architectural framework of AI Safety, including Trustworthy AI, Responsible AI, and
Ecosystemic Safe AI
. This framework provides a structured framework to holistically understand and address AI Safety challenges. Trustworthy AI emphasizes the need for AI systems to function as intended, maintaining resilience and security, even in dynamic and potentially adversarial environments. Responsible AI highlights the ethical imperatives of fairness, transparency, accountability, and respect for privacy, ensuring AI systems operate with human-centric and socially responsible principles.
Ecosystemic Safe AI
focuses on preventing harm, avoiding disinformation, protecting intellectual property, and managing data supply chain risks. Our extensive review of current research and developments identifies key vulnerabilities and challenges within these dimensions. We also present various mitigation strategies, including technical, ethical, and governance measures, which aim to enhance AI Safety. Additionally, we present promising future research directions in AI Safety, such as constructing comprehensive evaluation frameworks, improving knowledge management, investigating underlying mechanisms, developing defensive AI systems, and proactively preparing defensive strategies for advanced AI systems. In summary, AI Safety is a rapidly evolving field that requires a coordinated and interdisciplinary approach. A systematic understanding of AI Safety will benefit the advancement of AI technologies and the entire field.

Limitations and Future Directions of the Framework.

Our framework mainly focuses on foundation-model-centric architectures of an AI system whose safety can be analyzed via input/output constraints and lifecycle interventions. However, it is relatively less impactful for highly modular, autonomously operating agentic or multi-agent systems, where risks additionally arise from tool use, long-horizon planning, memory, and inter-agent coordination. Future work could extend the framework by explicitly modeling agentic safety pipelines, analyzing coordination failures, and standardizing quantitative benchmarks aligned with each safety layer for real deployments.
