Configurational temperature in active matter. I. Lines of invariant physics in the phase diagram of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model
Shibu Saw, Lorenzo Costigliola, Jeppe C. Dyre

Abstract
This paper shows that the configurational temperature of liquid-state theory, T conf {T}_{\rm conf} , defines an energy scale, which can be used for adjusting model parameters of active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particle (AOUP) models in order to achieve approximately invariant structure and dynamics upon a density change. The required parameter changes are calculated from the variation of a single configuration’s T conf {T}_{\rm conf} for a uniform scaling of all particle coordinates. The resulting equations are justified theoretically for models involving a potential-energy function with hidden scale invariance. The validity of the procedure is illustrated by computer simulations of the Kob-Andersen binary Lennard-Jones AOUP model, demonstrating lines of approximate reduced-unit invariance of the radial distribution function and time-dependent mean-square displacement.



I
Introduction

Any system in thermal equilibrium has a well-defined temperature, and the temperature concept is fundamental for understanding and quantifying a system’s thermodynamic and statistical-mechanical properties. In view of this it is obvious to try to generalize temperature to characterize also non-equilibrium systems. Excellent reviews of such temperatures proposed are given in Refs.
Casas-Vazquez and Jou 2003
;
Powles et al. 2005
;
Leuzzi 2009
;
Puglisi et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2019
. Examples are the
effective temperature
quantifying deviations from the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Cugliandolo and Kurchan 1994
;
Cugliandolo 2011
;
Petrelli et al. 2020
and the
fictive temperature
characterizing a glass’ structure in terms of the temperature at which the liquid solidified
Tool 1946
;
Scherer 1986
. Non-equilibrium temperatures are generally motivated by the prospect of connecting properties of the non-equilibrium system to those of the same system in thermal equilibrium. That is not the background, however, of the below proposed application of liquid-state theory’s
configurational temperature

Landau and Lifshitz 1958
;
Rugh 1997
;
Powles et al. 2005
;
Himpel and Melzer 2019
to active-matter models.

Active matter is an umbrella term used to describe physical systems whose building blocks can autonomously perform mechanical work. This includes fluids consisting of self-propelled particles, e.g., suspensions of swimming bacteria or animal groups, mutually-propelled particles like cytoskeletal filaments or motor proteins, cells in various contexts, bird or insect flock dynamics, etc
Angelini et al. 2011
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Bechinger et al. 2016
;
Ramaswamy 2017
;
Saintillan 2018
;
Das et al. 2020
;
Shaebani et al. 2021
;
Bowick et al. 2022
. Active matter is usually not time reversible. This means that a multitude of different dynamics may come into play
O’Byrne et al. 2022
, presenting a much richer field of study than that of ordinary time-reversible dynamics
Mandal et al. 2020
. A noted example of the features of active matter is motility-induced phase separation (MIPS), the intriguing finding that even a purely repulsive system may phase separate into high- and low-density phases
Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Das et al. 2014
;
Cates and Tailleur 2015
;
Ramaswamy 2017
;
Geyer et al. 2019
;
Das et al. 2020
;
Merrigan et al. 2020

Active matter does not have states of ordinary thermal equilibrium, but there have been suggestions for mapping active-matter states to equilibrium, implying the existence of a non-equilibrium active-matter temperature. For instance, Szamel proposed an effective temperature for a single self-propelled particle
Szamel 2014
, and Fodor
et al.
showed
Fodor et al. 2016
that for active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles with a small persistence time one can identify an effective temperature from the analog of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (see also Refs.
Loi et al. 2008
;
Wang and Wolynes 2011
;
Flenner and Szamel 2020
). In a parallel development, Takatori and Brady formulated a thermodynamic-type temperature for active matter based on the swim-pressure concept
Takatori and Brady 2015
.

For an ordinary non-active system in thermal equilibrium, the temperature
T
equals
{T}_{\rm conf}
defined as follows
Rugh 1997
;
Powles et al. 2005
. For a system of
N
particles with collective coordinate vector
\mathbb{R}\equiv(\mathbb{r}_{1},...,\mathbb{r}_{N})
and potential-energy function
U(\mathbb{R})
,
k_{B}{T}_{\rm conf}\equiv\langle(\nabla U)^{2}\rangle/\langle\nabla^{2}U\rangle
in which
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
\nabla
is the gradient operator in the
3N
-dimensional configuration space, and the sharp brackets denote canonical-ensemble averages. The proof that
{T}_{\rm conf}=T
in equilibrium is so simple that it deserves to be repeated here
Landau and Lifshitz 1958
: If
Z
is the configuration-space partition function integral, a partial integration of

\langle\nabla^{2}U\rangle=\int\nabla^{2}U(\mathbb{R})\exp(-U(\mathbb{R})/k_{B}T)d\mathbb{R}/Z
leads to

\langle\nabla^{2}U\rangle=-\int\nabla U(\mathbb{R})\cdot\nabla\exp(-U(\mathbb{R})/k_{B}T)d\mathbb{R}/Z=\langle(\nabla U)^{2}\rangle/k_{B}T
from which
{T}_{\rm conf}=T
follows.

Approaching the thermodynamic limit, the relative fluctuations of both the numerator and the denominator of
{T}_{\rm conf}
vanish. This means that if one defines an
\mathbb{R}
-dependent configurational temperature by

k_{B}{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})\,\equiv\,\frac{(\nabla U(\mathbb{R}))^{2}}{\nabla^{2}U(\mathbb{R})}\,,

(1)

the identity
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})\cong T
applies in the sense that deviations go to zero as
N\to\infty
. We have this limit in mind throughout and shall (mostly) ignore that
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})
fluctuates slightly for any finite system. Note that, in contrast to the standard kinetic-energy-based temperature definition, the configurational temperature is not defined for a system of free particles. Note also that configurations with
\nabla^{2}U(\mathbb{R})=0
become less likely as
N\to\infty
, so the fact that Eq. (
1
) is not defined for such configurations is irrelevant; by the same reasoning one can ignore the existence of configurations for which
\nabla^{2}U(\mathbb{R})<0
. We return briefly below to a discussion of
{T}_{\rm conf}
fluctuations in simulations (Fig.
3
(b)).

Since the derivation of the configurational temperature
{T}_{\rm conf}
is based on the fact that the probability in the canonical ensemble of configuration
\mathbb{R}
is proportional to
\exp(-U(\mathbb{R})/k_{B}T)
, it would appear that
{T}_{\rm conf}
cannot be relevant for systems that are far from thermal equilibrium. We show in this paper, however, that
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})
may be used for tracing out lines of invariant structure and dynamics in the phase diagram of active-matter models with hidden scale invariance. This is the symmetry that the ordering of configurations according to their potential energy at a given density is maintained if these are scaled uniformly to a different density (Eq. (
6
) below), a property that applies to a good approximation for the liquid and solid phases of a number of well-known potentials, including models based on the Lennard-Jones and Yukawa pair potentials
Gnan et al. 2009
;
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
;
Ingebrigtsen and Tanaka 2015
;
Dyre 2018
, as well as for more complicated non-pair interactions
Ingebrigtsen et al. 2012a
;
Hummel et al. 2015
;
Friedeheim et al. 2019
. The companion paper (Paper II)
Saw et al. 2023
presents a different application of
{T}_{\rm conf}
to active matter by proposing that the ratio of the so-called systemic temperature
Dyre 2020
to
{T}_{\rm conf}
quantifies the deviation from ordinary thermal equilibrium. Both papers focus on active-matter models without orientational interactions, i.e., models based on point particles.

II
Lines of approximately invariant physics in the phase diagram of the Kob-Andersen AOUP model

This section studies active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particle (AOUP) dynamics, which has no momentum conservation and for which hydrodynamics is not taken into account. All information about the particle interactions is contained in the potential-energy function
U(\mathbb{R})

Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Fily et al. 2017
;
Shaebani et al. 2021

(2)

Here

(3)

in which
i
and
j
are particle indices,
\alpha
and
\beta
are spatial
xyz
indices, and
D
and
\tau
are constants. We are interested in how the physics is affected if the density is changed, in particular whether approximately invariant physics can be obtained by adjusting
D
and
\tau
(regarding
\mu
as a system-specific constant).

III
Theoretical justification of the procedure

How can the characteristic energy
k_{B}{T}_{\rm conf}
of the canonical ensemble be relevant for identifying lines of invariant physics for an active-matter system? While the energy
k_{B}{T}_{\rm conf}

per se
is not important, we argue below that the
ratio

{T}_{\rm conf}(\rho)/{T}_{\rm conf}(\rho_{0})
determines the
ratio
of the relevant energy scales at the two densities in question. To arrive at this conclusion, we first summarize relevant aspects of the isomorph theory.

The starting point is that the KA model to a good approximation obeys the hidden-scale-invariance uniform-scaling symmetry defined
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
;
Dyre 2018
by the following logical implication for the potential-energy function
U(\mathbb{R})
,

U({\bf R}_{\rm a})<U({\bf R}_{\rm b})\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,U(\lambda{\bf R}_{\rm a})<U(\lambda{\bf R}_{\rm b})\,.

(6)

Here
{\bf R}_{\rm a}
and
{\bf R}_{\rm b}
are configurations of the same density and
\lambda
is a scaling parameter. Physically, Eq. (
6
) expresses that the ordering of configurations at one density according to their potential energy is maintained when configurations are scaled uniformly to a different density.

Recall that at a given thermodynamic equilibrium state point, the excess entropy
{S}_{\rm ex}
is defined as the entropy minus the ideal-gas entropy at the same density and temperature
Hansen and McDonald 2013
. In the case of ordinary Newtonian mechanics, Eq. (
6
) implies that structure and dynamics in reduced units (defined below) are invariant along the curves of constant excess entropy
Gnan et al. 2009
;
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
;
Dyre 2018
. Such curves are termed isomorphs, and systems with isomorphs are termed R-simple.

Isomorph invariance is exact whenever Eq. (
6
) applies without exception, but this is never the case for potentials with both attractions and repulsions. Isomorph invariance is still a good approximation, however, if Eq. (
6
) applies for most of the physically relevant configurations at the state points in question. Depending of course on how far the scaling parameter
\lambda
is from unity, this is the case for the majority of metals and van der Waals bonded systems, whereas systems with strong directional interactions like hydrogen-bonded and covalently bonded systems generally do not conform to Eq. (
6
) and therefore violate isomorph-theory predictions
Dyre 2014
(ionic and dipolar systems constitute an interesting class in-between). Realistic R-simple pair-potential models include the standard Lennard-Jones model in single-component, binary, and polydisperse versions, as well as with exponents other than 6 and 12 (so-called Mie potentials), the Yukawa (screened Coulomb) pair potential
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
;
Veldhorst et al. 2015
, the EXP pair potential
Bacher et al. 2019
;
Bacher et al. 2018
, effective-medium potentials describing metal
Friedeheim et al. 2019
, etc. We emphasize that Eq. (
6
) and its consequences are not limited to pair-potential systems. For systems with inverse-power-law interactions, the isomorph theory is exact.

For R-simple systems with Newtonian dynamics, the structure and dynamics of the condensed liquid and solid phases are isomorph invariant to a good approximation when made dimensionless using as units the length
l_{0}
, energy
e_{0}
, and time
t_{0}
given by (in which
m
is the particle mass)

l_{0}=\rho^{-1/3}\,,\,\,\,e_{0}=k_{B}T\,,\,\,\,t_{0}=\rho^{-1/3}\sqrt{m/k_{B}T}\,.

(7)

Using this unit system defines the reduced-unit value of the quantity in question.

The microscopic excess-entropy function is defined
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
by
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})\equiv{S}_{\rm ex}(\rho,U(\mathbb{R}))
in which
{S}_{\rm ex}(\rho,U)
is the thermodynamic excess entropy of the equilibrium state point with density
\rho
and average potential energy
U
. Note that the function
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})
is defined for any configuration of any system, whether it is R-simple or not. It can be shown that whenever Eq. (
6
) applies,
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})
depends only on the configuration’s reduced coordinates,
{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}\equiv\rho^{1/3}\mathbb{R}

Schrøder and Dyre 2014
. Thus inverting the relation
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})={S}_{\rm ex}(\rho,U(\mathbb{R}))
for an R-simple system leads to

U(\mathbb{R})\,=\,\left.U(\rho,{S}_{\rm ex})\right|_{{S}_{\rm ex}={S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})}

(8)

where
U(\rho,{S}_{\rm ex})
is the average potential energy of the thermodynamic equilibrium state point with density
\rho
and excess entropy
{S}_{\rm ex}
. For brevity, the right-hand side of Eq. (
8
) is usually written simply as
U(\rho,{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}))
.

The consequences of Eq. (
8
) have so far been worked out only for systems with standard time-reversible Newtonian dynamics
Schrøder and Dyre 2014
;
Dyre 2018
. However, Eq. (
8
) follows from Eq. (
6
) that has no reference to thermal equilibrium; hence Eq. (
8
) may also be applied to active-matter models with a hidden-scale-invariant potential-energy function. Note that the function
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})
still refers to the standard microcanonical ensemble according to which
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})
is basically the logarithm of the number of configurations at the same density with the same potential energy as
\mathbb{R}

Schrøder and Dyre 2014
.

We proceed to rewrite the AOUP equation of motion in terms of reduced variables. Writing
\mathbb{R}=l_{0}{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}
and
t=t_{0}\tilde{t}
in which
l_{0}=\rho^{-1/3}
and
t_{0}=\tau
as in Sec.
II
, Eq. (
2
) becomes (with
\tilde{\nabla}=\rho^{-1/3}\nabla
)

\frac{l_{0}}{\tau}\,\dot{{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}}\,=\,-\mu\,\frac{1}{l_{0}}\,\tilde{\nabla}U(\mathbb{R})\,+\,\bm{\eta}(t)\,.

(9)

The reduced noise is given by
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}=(\tau/l_{0})\bm{\eta}
in terms of which Eq. (
3
) becomes

\langle\tilde{\eta}_{i}^{\alpha}(\tilde{t})\tilde{\eta}_{j}^{\beta}(\tilde{t}^{\prime})\rangle\,=\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\frac{\tau\,D}{l_{0}^{2}}\,e^{-|\tilde{t}-\tilde{t}^{\prime}|}\,.

(10)

Equation (
9
) thus becomes

\dot{{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}}\,=\,-\mu\,\frac{\tau}{l_{0}^{2}}\,\tilde{\nabla}U(\mathbb{R})\,+\,\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\tilde{t})\,.

(11)

For any configuration
\mathbb{R}
we define the “systemic temperature”,
{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})
, by
Dyre 2020

{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})\,\equiv\,\left.\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial{S}_{\rm ex}}\right)_{\rho}\right|_{{S}_{\rm ex}={S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})}\,.

(12)

It should be emphasized that when we below use of this concept in the context of active matter, that does
not
imply an implicit mapping of the active-matter system to the ordinary thermal-equilibrium system; thus no relation between the physics of the two different cases is assumed.

In a steady-state situation the fluctuations of the systemic temperature go to zero in the thermodynamic limit, just as those of
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})
. For this reason we henceforth occasionally leave out
\mathbb{R}
and write simply
{T}_{\rm s}
. In practice, to determine
{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})
one utilizes the fact that
{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})
is the equilibrium temperature
{T}_{\rm eq}
of the thermodynamic state point with the density of
\mathbb{R}
and excess entropy equal to
{S}_{\rm ex}(\mathbb{R})
, implying that
Dyre 2020

{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})\,=\,{T}_{\rm eq}(\rho,{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}))={T}_{\rm eq}(\rho,U(\mathbb{R}))\,.

(13)

Thus there is no need to evaluate any entropy in order to determine
{T}_{\rm s}
, which is simply the temperature of the thermal-equilibrium state point with same density and potential energy as the active-matter system in question.

Equation (
8
) implies
\tilde{\nabla}U(\mathbb{R})={T}_{\rm s}\tilde{\nabla}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})
. When substituted into Eq. (
11
) this results in

\dot{{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}}\,=\,-\mu\,\frac{\tau{T}_{\rm s}}{l_{0}^{2}}\,\tilde{\nabla}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})\,+\,\tilde{\bm{\eta}}(\tilde{t})\,.

(14)

It follow from Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
14
) that the reduced AOUP equation of motion is invariant upon a density change if
\tau D/l_{0}^{2}
and
\tau{T}_{\rm s}/l_{0}^{2}
do not vary with density. This implies
D(\rho)\propto{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)
and
\tau(\rho)\propto\rho^{-2/3}/{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)
in which
{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)
is short-hand notation for
{T}_{\rm eq}(\rho,{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}))
, compare Eq. (
13
). Working from the reference state point
(\rho_{0},D_{0},\tau_{0})
, this means that the function
{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)
determines how to scale
D
and
\tau
to ensure invariant AOUP dynamics,

\displaystyle D(\rho)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,D(\rho_{0})\,\frac{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)}{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho_{0})}

\displaystyle\tau(\rho)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\tau(\rho_{0})\,\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}\right)^{2/3}\,\frac{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho_{0})}{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)}\,.

(15)

We next link to the configurational temperature. There is no reason to expect
{T}_{\rm conf}={T}_{\rm s}
in out-of-equilibrium situations, and these quantities indeed differ by up to a factor of six in our simulations (Paper II
Saw et al. 2023
suggests using
{T}_{\rm s}/{T}_{\rm conf}
as a measure of the degree of deviation from thermal equilibrium). However, Eq. (
III
) still applies with
{T}_{\rm conf}
instead of
{T}_{\rm s}
if the two temperatures are proportional in their density variation. To show that this is the case we note that Eq. (
8
) implies
\tilde{\nabla}U(\mathbb{R})={T}_{\rm s}\tilde{\nabla}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})
and
\tilde{\nabla}^{2}U(\mathbb{R})={T}_{\rm s}\tilde{\nabla}^{2}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})
, so
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})=(\nabla U(\mathbb{R}))^{2}/\nabla^{2}U(\mathbb{R})=(\tilde{\nabla}U(\mathbb{R}))^{2}/\tilde{\nabla}^{2}U(\mathbb{R})={T}_{\rm s}(\tilde{\nabla}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}))^{2}/\tilde{\nabla}^{2}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})

Dyre 2020
. Here we ignored the dependence of
{T}_{\rm s}(\mathbb{R})
on the configuration
\mathbb{R}
at a given state point which, as mentioned above, vanishes in the thermodynamic limit. In terms of
\phi({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})\equiv(\tilde{\nabla}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}))^{2}/\tilde{\nabla}^{2}{S}_{\rm ex}({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})
we thus have

\frac{{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})}{{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R}_{0})}=\frac{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho)\,\phi({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}})}{{T}_{\rm s}(\rho_{0})\,\phi({\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}_{0})}\,.

(16)

Since
{\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}={\tilde{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}
this implies
{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R})/{T}_{\rm conf}(\mathbb{R}_{0})={T}_{\rm s}(\rho)/{T}_{\rm s}(\rho_{0})
. In this way Eq. (
III
) leads to Eq. (
II
). Note that by using
{T}_{\rm conf}
instead of
{T}_{\rm s}
, one does not have to identify the equilibrium state point with the same potential energy as the active-matter state point in question. Figure
1
and Fig.
2
demonstrated good invariance along active-matter isomorphs determined by means of Eq. (
II
). A more accurate, but also more tedious, method for determining the active-matter isomorphs of AOUP LJ systems, which utilizes

directly, is discussed in the Appendix.

This identity is a consequence of the reduced-unit RDF isomorph invariance, which applies in the R-simple region of any single- or multicomponent LJ system (i.e., liquid or solid, but not gas)

IV
Discussion

The isomorph concept of equilibrium Newtonian dynamics was recently generalized to out-of-equilibrium Newtonian systems like that of a shear flow or an aging glass, leading to the introduction of the systemic temperature (Eq. (
12
)), which allows for the identification of lines of approximately invariant structure and dynamics in the relevant out-of-equilibrium phase diagram
Dyre 2020
. The results of the present paper extend these findings by demonstrating the existence of isomorphs for active-matter systems, which in contrast to Newtonian systems are described by a dynamics that is usually not time-reversible.

The configurational temperature expression is derived from the canonical ensemble. This paper has nevertheless demonstrated the relevance of
{T}_{\rm conf}
for tracing out lines of approximately invariant physics in the phase diagram of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck active-matter models involving a potential-energy function that obeys hidden scale invariance. We emphasize that this application is not based on a mapping of the active-matter system to an equilibrium system.

Paper II
Saw et al. 2023
proposes a second application of
{T}_{\rm conf}
to active matter. There it is argued that the ratio of the systemic to the configurational temperature, which is unity in canonical-ensemble thermal equilibrium because
T={T}_{\rm s}={T}_{\rm conf}
, provides a simple measure of the degree of deviation from thermal equilibrium.

Appendix: A more accurate method for tracing out active-matter isomorphs

Equilibrium isomorphs may be traced out by different methods, e.g., step-by-step integration of the exact equation for a configurational adiabat
Gnan et al. 2009
;
Attia et al. 2021
, the direct isomorph check
Gnan et al. 2009
, and the recently introduced force method
Schrøder 2022
. These are numerical methods of varying complexity and accuracy. Likewise, there are different numerical methods for tracing out an active-matter isomorph. We describe below a more accurate alternative to the method used in Secs. II and III, a method that unfortunately is also more involved.

The paper demonstrated how
{T}_{\rm conf}
can be used for tracing out active-matter isomorphs from a single configuration of the AOUP model. The result was a recipe for calculating how the model parameters are to be changed as functions of the density in order to arrive at approximately invariant structure and dynamics, Eq. (
II
). This recipe provides a useful “quick-and-dirty” method which, since it relies on Eq. (
8
), is exact whenever hidden scale invariance holds exactly (which is the case if
U(\mathbb{R})
is an Euler-homogeneous function). A more accurate, but also more cumbersome, method for identifying active-matter isomorphs refers directly to the concept of systemic isomorphs. These lines in the
(\rho,{T}_{\rm s})
phase diagram are by definition the same as the ordinary isomorphs of the canonical-ensemble equilibrium
(\rho,T)
phase diagram
Dyre 2020
(ordinary, systemic, and active-matter isomorphs are all defined as lines of constant
{S}_{\rm ex}
in the respective phase diagrams).

To test the consequence of referring directly to the systemic isomorph, we traced out the systemic isomorph of the KA system by the direct isomorph check (DIC) method
Gnan et al. 2009
, which is accurate and has a simple analytical expression for LJ-type systems
Ingebrigtsen et al. 2012b
;
Bøhling et al. 2013
. It does not make a huge difference which method is used, but there is some improvement using the DIC method. This is illustrated in Fig.

. We see that the MSD is more invariant in the latter case, confirming that this method is indeed more accurate.
