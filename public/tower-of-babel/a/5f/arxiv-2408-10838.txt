Multilevel CNNs for Parametric PDEs based on Adaptive Finite Elements
Janina Enrica Schütte, Martin Eigel

Abstract
A neural network architecture is presented that exploits the multilevel properties of high-dimensional parameter-dependent partial differential equations, enabling an efficient approximation of parameter-to-solution maps, rivaling best-in-class methods such as low-rank tensor regression in terms of accuracy and complexity. The neural network is trained with data on adaptively refined finite element meshes, thus reducing data complexity significantly. Error control is achieved by using a reliable finite element a posteriori error estimator, which is also provided as input to the neural network. The proposed U-Net architecture with CNN layers mimics a classical finite element multigrid algorithm. It can be shown that the CNN efficiently approximates all operations required by the solver, including the evaluation of the residual-based error estimator. In the CNN, a culling mask set-up according to the local corrections due to refinement on each mesh level reduces the overall complexity, allowing the network optimization with localized fine-scale finite element data. A complete convergence and complexity analysis is carried out for the adaptive multilevel scheme, which differs in several aspects from previous non-adaptive multilevel CNN. Moreover, numerical experiments with common benchmark problems from Uncertainty Quantification illustrate the practical performance of the architecture.

Introduction

In recent years, the intersection of partial differential equations (PDEs) and neural networks has emerged as a powerful and promising field of research.
In a wider sense, this increasingly popular research area is called scientific machine learning (SciML), which strives to make use of modern deep learning methods for the solution of differential equations that are common to model physical processes in engineering and the natural sciences.
Opposite to many tasks in classification or generation of images, videos, sounds or text, the data in SciML typically has specific properties that can be exploited, e.g. regularity or sparsity of functions.
Moreover, data often can be generated synthetically by running (possibly computationally very costly) simulations with classical solvers such as finite elements (FE).

We consider parametric PDEs as a flexible mathematical model to describe real-world phenomena, allowing for the incorporation of variable, stochastic parameters that capture uncertainties and changing properties. Problems of this type have been examined extensively in Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) in recent years.
They can be approached with sampling methods or by computing functional surrogates in different model classes such as low-rank tensors

1.1
Adaptive neural network approach

In this paper we present an approach to solve parametric PDEs based on training data generated by an adaptive FE discretization.
This is combined with a multilevel neural network (ML-Net) architecture, which mimics a classical multilevel solver and supports local corrections, corresponding to local mesh refinements.

In
[
27
]
the ML-Net architecture is derived to approximate the finite element coefficients of the solutions on uniformly refined grids.
We generalize this approach by introducing local corrections with respect to the global discretization mesh with data being generated by an efficient adaptive solver.
We show that CNNs are able to efficiently approximate a posteriori finite element error estimators and construct a culling mask based on the estimations, which only adds parts of the domain where fine scale corrections are needed.
By this, only small parts on each level are considered in the multigrid scheme. As a consequence of this data reduction, in principle much finer meshes (and hence a higher approximation accuracy) can be used for the training.

We consider the
parametric stationary diffusion PDE
(also known as ”parametric Darcy problem”) with a possibly countably infinite dimensional parameter space
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
and a physical domain

The objective is to find

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\nabla u(\cdot,\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle=f\qquad\text{on }D,

\displaystyle u(\cdot,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{on }\partial D

and a right-hand side

We propose an adaptive finite element solver for this task. Starting with a coarse uniform triangulation as an initial discretization, the following well-known steps are executed iteratively:

Theorem 1.1

(CNNs can approximate adaptive finite element solvers)
.

Assume that
\kappa
is uniformly bounded from below and above. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
K,L\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of iterations of the derived AFEM and the maximal refinements of each triangle, respectively. Consider a threshold marking strategy. Then there exists a CNN
\Psi
such that the number of parameters of the network is in
\mathcal{O}(LK\log(\varepsilon^{-1})/\log(c_{L}^{-1}))
with
c_{L}\coloneqq\frac{cL}{1+cL},c>0
.
Moreover, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma
the network maps finite element coefficients of the parameter field
\kappa
to coefficients of a finite element approximation of the solution

of the pPDE (
2.2
) such that

\left\|u(\cdot,\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{C}(\Psi({\bm{\kappa}}_{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{f}))\right\|_{H^{1}(D)}\leq\left\|u(\cdot,\mathbf{y})-\mathcal{C}(\mathrm{AFEM}(V_{1},K))\right\|_{H^{1}(D)}+\varepsilon,

where

maps the finite element coefficients to the corresponding function.

The proof is based on the observation that U-Nets are able to approximate a successive subspace algorithm based on a multigrid decomposition. The required interpolation between grids can be achieved efficiently with strided and transpose strided convolutions.
Furthermore, we show that the estimator can be approximated and refinement in each step of the adaptive algorithm.
This is realized by
0/1
-masks multiplied by large images making it possible to work with sparse images for fine grids.

Since the solution representation is based on local contributions (corresponding to small regions of images with higher resolution), the proposed architecture can significantly improve computational efficiency by exploiting representation sparsity.

1.2
Main contributions

A multigrid solver, error estimator and refinement strategy are chosen, such that the corresponding
\mathrm{AFEM}
can provably be approximated by an introduced CNN architecture.
Complexity bounds for the approximation of the
\mathrm{AFEM}
are shown for the architecture. In the course of the proof it is shown that CNNs can approximate a multigrid solver on locally refined grids.
Moreover, CNNs can approximate the error estimator and the refinement can be incorporated by a novel error estimator based masking.
This leads to an implicitly adaptive CNN tracking the error of individual outputs by error estimator prediction.
The sparsity introduced by the masks on high resolution grids leads to a smaller number of operations on each grid and a higher accuracy for the same number of nonzero entries.

In contrast to the work carried out in
[
27
]
, here the proof relies on actions on only parts of each image in the CNNs computations. Therefore, on each level, the boundary of the subsets has to be considered carefully and different index sets need to be considered in the analysis to always be able to represent all necessary information on each grid.
In practice, the locality translates to manifold sparse convolutions, where kernels are only applied to nonzero entries of each image.

Finite element discretization and notation

To generate data-efficient data, the finite element method (FEM) with an adaptive algorithm (coined AFEM) is used.

2.1
Problem setting

We assume a regular conforming triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of the (smoothly bounded) domain
D
, e.g. as depicted in
Figure

. Let
V_{h}=\text{span}\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\dim V_{h}}\subset H_{0}^{1}(D)
be a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by conforming first-order (Lagrange) basis functions (a FE function space).
Any function

has a representation

\displaystyle v_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim V_{h}}\mathbf{v}_{i}\varphi_{i},

where coefficient vectors with respect to the basis of

are written in bold face. Throughout this paper it is assumed that the parameter field fulfills a uniform boundedness assumption
\mathfrak{c}\leq\kappa(x,\mathbf{y})\leq\mathfrak{C}
for all

and some constants

independent of

.
We are concerned with finding a discrete solution

of the variational formulation for any
\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma
such that for all test functions

it holds that

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{y},h}(u_{h},w_{h})\coloneqq\int_{D}\kappa_{h}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla w_{h}\rangle\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}fw_{h}\mathrm{d}x\eqqcolon\mathfrak{f}(w_{h}).

(2.1)

This is equivalent to determining
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim V_{h}}
by solving the algebraic system

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}

(2.2)

with the right-hand side

and discretized operator

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq(a_{\mathbf{y},h}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{\dim V_{h}}.

(2.3)

We consider the following norms for

and

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(T)}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{T}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(T)}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{T}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{T}\left\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\lVert u\rVert_{a_{\mathbf{y},h}}^{2}\coloneqq a_{\mathbf{y},h}(u,u).

For

, we define the discrete norm

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{u}^{\intercal}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}.

Note that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim V_{h}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{j}{a_{\mathbf{y},h}}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})={a_{\mathbf{y},h}}\left(\sum_{i}^{\dim V_{h}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{\dim V_{h}}\mathbf{u}_{j}\varphi_{j}\right)={a_{\mathbf{y},h}}(u_{h},u_{h})=\lVert u_{h}\rVert_{a_{\mathbf{y},h}}.

Furthermore, we make use of the essential supremum norm

and the discrete supremum norm

Marking

For a complete adaptive finite element scheme as in (

Definition 2.2

(Dörfler marking)

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}}\eta_{T}^{2}\geq\theta\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\eta_{T}^{2}.

When performing a marking decision for each element, access to the estimator for all other elements has to be available.

Definition 2.3

(Threshold marking)

For

2.4
Mesh refinement

The next step of the
\mathrm{AFEM}
consists of refining the current mesh in marked areas.
In this work, in the
L
th step of the AFEM the current space consists of the sum of subspaces of FE spaces on uniformly refined meshes
with nodes
\mathcal{N}_{k}
for
k\in[L]
and corresponding basis functions
\{\varphi^{k}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}

To refine a mesh element, all basis functions on a uniformly refined mesh (one level finer than the marked element) with overlapping support to the marked elements are included in
V_{h}

Let
\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{M}_{k}
be the decomposition of the marked elements into sets of elements in the same uniformly refined mesh.
Then the local mesh refinement is given by

\displaystyle V_{h}=V_{h}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}\text{span}\{\varphi^{k+1}_{i}:\exists T\in\mathcal{M}_{k}\text{ in level }k\text{ with }\supp\varphi_{i}^{k+1}\cap T\neq\emptyset\}.

Convolutional neural networks (CNN) for finite element discretizations

CNNs are a specific neural network architecture tailored to tasks involving image data such as image classification and segmentation. Inspired by

Applying the action of a CNN to an image involves the application of local kernels to a hierarchy of scaled representations of the input.
This locality makes the architecture particularly suitable with partial differential equations, where local properties and interactions have to be resolved to obtain highly accurate representations.
To incorporate interactions on a larger scale with respect to the image domain, compression and decompression of the input images can be implemented with CNNs through strided and transpose strided convolutions, leading the popular CNN architecture U-Nets

7
Outlook

In the paper, we derived an algorithm
\mathrm{LLMG}
, which approximates the parametric diffusion PDE on a fixed grid based on a multigrid decomposition of the solution and a successive subspace correction algorithm.
We showed that the derived algorithm can be approximated efficiently in the number of parameters by a derived CNN architecture.
Furthermore, we showed that an efficient and reliable finite element error estimator can be approximated by a specific CNN construction.
These results were combined to show upper bounds for the number of parameters of CNNs approximating a complete adaptive finite element scheme.

It is now interesting to put this architecture to use and explore the efficiency of the networks numerically, mainly with respect to two aspects. First, the number of calculations on each level should be reduced comparing to fully refined meshes
[
27
]
due to the submanifold sparse convolutions on sparse tensors. Note that the sparsity of the tensors stems from the used multigrid decomposition of the data.
Second, the efficiency with respect to the number of samples needed for training should be explored, considering that in each step corrections with decreasing influence on the whole solutions need to be learned. This should lead to fewer training samples on fine grids and hence a more efficient training and data generation process.

In addition, one might be interested in deriving convergence results for the proposed adaptive scheme with the presented refinement and for different marking strategies. The direction of showing the CNN approximation results for other meshes without hanging nodes might also be of interest.

, let

\displaystyle J_{k}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(I-\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\mathbf{w}.

Then, with
\mathbf{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}c_{i}\mathbf{v}^{i}

\displaystyle J_{k}\mathbf{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}c_{i}(I-\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\mathbf{v}^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}c_{i}(1-\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\lambda_{i})\mathbf{v}^{i}.

Second,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{w}|^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})c_{i}^{2}

defines a semi-norm due to
0<\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}\leq\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})^{-1}
.
Then, the following statements hold

\displaystyle|\mathbf{w}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{k}}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})\lambda_{i}\left\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\mathbf{v}^{j}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}c_{i}c_{j}=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}c_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})\mathbf{v}^{i},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}c_{j}\mathbf{v}^{j}\right\rangle_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}=\left\langle J_{k}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}},

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}c_{i}c_{j}\left\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\mathbf{v}^{j}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}c_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle|\mathbf{w}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}c_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}c_{i}^{2}<\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}c_{i}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{2}.

The last inequality holds true for
\mathbf{w}\neq 0
since
A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}
is positive definite and
\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}>0
.
Then, with the Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|J_{k}\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}(c_{i}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}))^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}(\lambda_{i}^{1/3}|c_{i}|^{2/3})(\lambda_{i}^{2/3}|c_{i}|^{4/3}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}(\lambda_{i}^{1/3}|c_{i}|^{2/3})^{3}\right)^{1/3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}(\lambda_{i}^{2/3}|c_{i}|^{4/3}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})^{2})^{3/2}\right)^{2/3}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})^{3}\right)^{2/3}.

This yields the result by estimating

\displaystyle\left\|J_{k}\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\left\|J_{k}\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{2}\right)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}(1-\lambda_{i}\omega^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{2}|J_{k}\mathbf{w}|

\displaystyle<\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}^{2}\left\|J_{k}\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}

and dividing by
\left\|J_{k}\mathbf{w}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}
.
∎

Theorem B.1
.

Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\leq\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})\leq C
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma
and choose
0<\omega^{k}\leq C^{-1}
. Then, the error decays with

\displaystyle\left\|(I-T_{L})(I-T_{L-1})\dots(I-T_{1})\right\|^{2}_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\frac{c_{0}}{1+c_{0}}

for some
c_{0}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}L
.
Furthermore, if there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}\leq\kappa(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\leq c_{2}
for all
x\in D
and
\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma
(uniform boundedness) leads to a bound of the convergence rate
c_{0}\leq cL
independent of
\mathbf{y}
for some
c>0
.

Proof.

To apply
Lemma

B.1
, we only need to verify that the smoothing on the subspaces yields a contraction for each
k=1,\dots,L
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|I-T_{k}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}<1.

This is established in
Lemma

B.2
. The constant in
Lemma

B.1

\displaystyle c_{0}=\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\|\omega_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\bar{R}_{k}^{-1}},

for
\bar{R}_{k}=(2I-\omega_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}A^{k}_{\mathbf{y}})\omega_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}
has to be bounded by a constant independent of
\mathbf{y}
.
If
\left\|T\right\|<1
then
\left\|(I-T)^{-1}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\left\|T\right\|}
.
Therefore,
T\coloneqq\frac{\omega^{k}}{2}A^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}
, (
B.1
) and
\left\|T\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})^{-1}\left\|A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}\right\|=\frac{1}{2}<1
implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{R}_{k}^{-1}\right\|=\left\|(2\omega^{k}(I-T))^{-1}\right\|\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})}{2}\left\|(I-T)^{-1}\right\|\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})}{2}\frac{1}{1-\left\|T\right\|}=\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}).

For
p_{k}:V\to V_{k}
the
A_{\mathbf{y}}
orthogonal projection onto
V_{k}
, it holds that
Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}=A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}p_{k}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left\|\omega^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\left\|\omega^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\omega^{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}-I)\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\neq 0}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\left\|\omega^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\frac{\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}}+(\omega^{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}-I)\frac{\mathbf{v}_{k}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{v}\neq 0}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\left\|\omega^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}\frac{Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|}+\frac{(\omega^{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}-I)\mathbf{v}_{k}}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})^{-1}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\left\|\omega^{k}Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\omega^{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}-I)\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})^{-1}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\omega^{k}\left\|Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\right\|\left\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|+\left\|\omega^{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}-I\right\|\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})^{-1}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\left(\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}\left\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|+\left(1-\omega^{k}\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\right)\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(\left\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|+\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\inf_{\sum_{k=1}^{L}Q_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}{\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})}L.

This is derived by using that
\left\|v\right\|_{A_{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})\left\|v\right\|^{2}
,
\max_{k}\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k})\geq\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})
and
Q_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
are orthogonal with respect to the
\ell^{2}
scalar product for
i=1,\dots,L
.
Therefore, the claim follows from the assumption that
\lambda_{\min}(A_{\mathbf{y}})
and
\lambda_{\max}(A_{\mathbf{y}})
are uniformly bounded from below and above for all
\mathbf{y}
, respectively.
∎

B.3
Smoothing with multiple levels

For one smoothing step, the calculation of
Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}
is required.
Since
\mathbf{u}
is given as a levelwise discretization, we consider the evaluation of the multiplication on each level separately.
Recall the auxiliary vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}
and
\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{k}
in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
).
Using the decomposition
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{<k}+Q_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}^{k}+\mathbf{u}^{>k}
facilitates a levelwise calculation as described below.

B.3.1
Coarse grid smoothing

For
Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{<k}
we get the following result.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}
be defined as in (
3.3
) by

\displaystyle\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{1}\coloneqq 0,\quad\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\coloneqq P_{k-1}\left(\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k-1}+\overline{\mathbf{u}^{k-1}}\right)

with
\overline{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}
equal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}
and zero otherwise.
Then

\displaystyle Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{<k}=\overline{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}.

Proof of
Lemma

B.3
.

Note that
Q_{1}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{<1}=0=A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}
.
For
k=2,\dots,L
we show that for
x\in\supp V^{k}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\varphi_{i}^{\ell}(x){=}\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}_{i}\varphi_{i}^{k}(x).

The proof is by induction. For
k=1
, both sides are equal to
0
.
Assuming the statement holds for
k
, we get for
k+1
and
x\in\supp V^{k+1}\subset\supp V^{k}

that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\varphi_{i}^{\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\varphi_{i}^{\ell}(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\varphi_{i}^{k}(x)=\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}_{i}\varphi_{i}^{k}(x)+\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}\overline{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}}\varphi_{i}^{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}(\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}_{i}+\overline{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}})\varphi_{i}^{k}(x)=\sum_{j\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}(\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}+\overline{\mathbf{u}^{k}})_{i}(P_{k}^{\intercal})_{i,j}\varphi_{j}^{k+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}(P_{k}(\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k}+\overline{\mathbf{u}^{k}}))_{j}\varphi_{j}^{k+1}(x)=\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}_{i}\varphi_{i}^{k+1}(x).

Then, for
j\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}
it holds that

\displaystyle\left(Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{<k}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\int\kappa_{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\ell}(x),\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\supp V^{k}}\kappa_{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\varphi_{i}^{\ell}(x),\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\kappa_{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\varphi_{i}^{k}(x),\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\int\kappa_{h}(x,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{i}^{k}(x),\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\overline{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k}}\tilde{}\mathbf{u}^{k})_{j}.

∎

B.3.2
Fine grid smoothing

We now consider
Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{>k}
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{k}
be defined as in (
3.4
) by

\displaystyle\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{L}\coloneqq 0,\quad\bar{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\coloneqq P_{k}^{\intercal}\left(\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}+\overline{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}\right).

Then,

\displaystyle Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{>k}=\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{k}|_{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k}}.

Proof.

We prove the statement again by induction. Note that for
k=L
, it holds that
Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{>L}=0=\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{L}
.
Assuming that the statement holds for
k+1
, i.e. for
j\in\mathcal{I}^{k+1}_{V}

\displaystyle(Q_{k+1}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{>k+1})_{j}=\sum_{\ell=k+2}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\int\kappa_{h}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\ell},\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k+1}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x=\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}_{j},

we show that the statement also is true for
k
.
In fact, for
j\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{V}
we deduce that

\displaystyle(Q_{k}A_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{u}^{>k})_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\int\kappa_{h}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\ell},\nabla\varphi_{j}^{k}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}(P_{k}^{\intercal})_{jm}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}\int\kappa_{h}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\ell},\nabla\varphi_{m}^{k+1}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}(P_{k}^{\intercal})_{jm}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{k+1}_{V}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1}a_{\mathbf{y},h}(\varphi^{k+1}_{i},\varphi^{k+1}_{m})+\sum_{\ell=k+2}^{L}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{V}^{\ell}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\ell}a_{\mathbf{y},h}(\varphi^{\ell}_{i},\varphi^{k+1}_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\overline{\mathcal{I}_{V}^{k+1}}}(P_{k}^{\intercal})_{jm}\left(\left(\overline{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}\right)_{m}+\bar{}\mathbf{u}_{m}^{k+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(P_{k}^{\intercal}\left(\overline{A_{\mathbf{y}}^{k+1}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}+\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{k+1}\right)\right)_{j}=\bar{}\mathbf{u}^{k}_{j}.

∎

.
Then, for

D.2
Estimator approximation

The strong residual images and jump images can be approximated on any level by a CNN.

Theorem D.4
.

For any
k\in[L]
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists a CNN
\Psi

such that for all
{{{{\bm{\kappa}}^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}}_{\text{img}}}},{{\mathbf{f}^{k}_{\text{img}}}},{\mathbf{u}^{k}_{\text{img}}}\in[-M,M]^{\mathcal{I}_{U}^{k}}

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi({\mathbf{u}^{k}_{\text{img}}},{{{{\bm{\kappa}}^{k}_{\mathbf{y}}}_{\text{img}}}}_{h},{{\mathbf{f}^{k}_{\text{img}}}})-\left(\mathrm{r}^{2}_{k,T^{q}},\mathrm{j}^{2}_{k,T^{q}}\right)_{q=1,2}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon,

where the strong residual images and jump images are defined as in
Definition

5.1
with respect to the input coefficients.
For
\Psi
, there exist three fixed bias and kernel sizes with width and height at most
5
.

Proof.

We first note that for
q=1,2
and each triangle
T=T^{q}_{k,i}
as illustrated in
Figure

5.1
,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{r}^{2}_{k,T^{q}})_{i}=

\displaystyle\left\|f_{h}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h})\right\|_{L^{2}(T)}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|f_{h}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}+2\left\langle f_{h},\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h})\right\rangle_{L^{2}(T)}+\left\|\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}.

Here,

\displaystyle\left\|f_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(T)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\text{nodes}(T)}\mathbf{f}_{m}\mathbf{f}_{n}e_{mn}

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad e_{mn}\coloneqq\int_{T}\varphi_{m}\varphi_{n}\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{h},\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h})\right\rangle_{L^{2}(T)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\text{nodes}(T)}\mathbf{f}_{j}c_{j}\sum_{m,n\in\text{nodes}(T)}{\bm{\kappa}}_{m}\mathbf{u}_{n}d_{mn}

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad c_{j}\coloneqq\int_{T}{\varphi_{j}}\mathrm{d}x,\quad d_{mn}\coloneqq\nabla\varphi_{m}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n},

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\cdot(\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h})\right\|_{L^{2}(T^{q})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m,n\in\text{nodes}(T)}{\bm{\kappa}}_{m}\mathbf{u}_{n}d_{mn}\right)^{2}\int_{T}\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
c_{m}
,
d_{jm}
and
\int_{T}\mathrm{d}x
are independent of the node
i
, i.e.,
d_{mn}=d_{m-i,n-i}
,
c_{j}=c_{j-i}
,
e_{mn}=e_{m-i,n-i}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{j}_{k,T^{q}}^{2})_{i}=\left\|[\![\kappa_{h}\nabla u_{h}]\!]\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial T^{(q)})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\text{edges}(T)}\left\|\kappa_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}[\![u_{h}]\!]^{2}_{(K)}.

With triangle
\tilde{T}_{K}
such that
K\in\text{edges}(\tilde{T}_{K})
for
K\in\text{edges}(T)
,

\displaystyle\left\|\kappa_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}h}{3}\sum_{j,m\in\text{nodes}(K)}{\bm{\kappa}}_{j}{\bm{\kappa}}_{m},

\displaystyle([\![u_{h}]\!]_{(K)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{h}\sum_{j\in\text{nodes}(\tilde{T})\cup\text{nodes}(T)}(-1)^{1-\chi_{K}(j)}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right)^{2}.

Since shifting and addition can be represented by convolutional kernels, multiplication and squaring can be approximated by the concatenation of a convolutional kernel, the application of the activation function and another convolutional kernel, every operation can be represented or approximated by a CNN.
Since the addition and shifting always only includes nodes in the vicinity of the considered node, the kernels have a small bounded width and height.
∎

(iii)

Marking.

Different marking strategies can be considered. Here, a threshold marking strategy as in

The operator mapping estimator images to marker images inside a CNN is defined by the mapping
\eta_{k}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times\mathcal{I}_{U}^{k}}\mapsto M^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{2\times\mathcal{I}_{U}^{k}}

For instance, in addition to the markings based on the selected strategy and the approximated estimator, one could mark all triangles for which the approximated error estimator is larger than the lowest approximated estimator of the already marked triangles minus twice the estimated approximation error.

(iv)

Refinement.

The refinement is incorporated in the CNN using the marking masks
