Stressing Dynamic Loss Models
Emma Kroell, Silvana M. Pesenti, Sebastian Jaimungal

Abstract
Stress testing, and in particular, reverse stress testing, is a prominent exercise in risk management practice. Reverse stress testing, in contrast to (forward) stress testing, aims to find an alternative but plausible model such that under that alternative model, specific adverse stresses (i.e. constraints) are satisfied. Here, we propose a reverse stress testing framework for dynamic models. Specifically, we consider a compound Poisson process over a finite time horizon and stresses composed of expected values of functions applied to the process at the terminal time. We then define the stressed model as the probability measure under which the process satisfies the constraints and which minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence to the reference compound Poisson model. We solve this optimization problem, prove existence and uniqueness of the stressed probability measure, and provide a characterization of the Radon-Nikodym derivative from the reference model to the stressed model. We find that under the stressed measure, the intensity and the severity distribution of the process depend on time and state, and hence the stressed model is not a compound Poisson process. We illustrate the dynamic stress testing by considering stresses on VaR and both VaR and CVaR jointly and provide illustrations of how the stochastic process is altered under these stresses. We generalize the framework to multivariate compound Poisson processes and stresses at times other than the terminal time. We illustrate the applicability of our framework by considering “what if” scenarios, where we answer the question: What is the severity of a stress on a portfolio component at an earlier time such that the aggregate portfolio exceeds a risk threshold at the terminal time? Furthermore, for general constraints, we propose an algorithm to simulate sample paths under the stressed measure, thus allowing to compare the effects of stresses on the dynamics of the process.



Keywords—
Reverse Stress Testing, Compound Poisson Processes, KL divergence, Value-at-Risk, Conditional Value-at-Risk

Introduction

Since the 2007-08 financial crisis, there has been an increased focus in the insurance and financial sector on modelling and assessing rare but extreme events. Stress testing, which examines a model or system under extreme but realistic scenarios, has become a key aspect of risk management at financial institutions, as well as a vital tool for regulators
(
Basel Committee on Banking Supervision 2018
)
. In light of the regulatory requirements on stress testing, a large portion of the literature on stress testing is concerned with constructing plausible but
adverse scenarios
that result in a substantial stress on the model, see e.g.,
Berkowitz 2000
and
Meucci 2008
.
For example,
McNeil & Smith 2012
develop, in the context of solvency requirements, a stress testing methodology that provides multivariate adverse scenarios via the notion of a “least solvency likely event”.
Glasserman et al. 2015
construct non-parametrically the most likely scenarios that lead to losses exceeding a prespecified risk threshold. Connecting to systemic risk,
Brechmann et al. 2013
utilize vine copulas to perform stress tests to investigate systemic risk and contagion in financial networks.
In an application to credit risk,
Breuer et al. 2012
consider multi-period adverse scenarios whose plausibility is measured via the Mahalanobis distance of risk factors changes.
Breuer & Csiszár 2013
highlight the difficulty of choosing realistic but dangerous scenarios, which are essential for reliable stress tests. We also refer to
Akhtar & Hasan 2019
for a treatment of stress testing from a regulatory point of view.

An alternative approach to stress testing – sometimes termed
reverse stress testing
– is to first define an adverse or stressed state of the world, and then characterize the probability distribution leading to it, instead of defining a stress and searching for the corresponding adverse scenario. Fundamental to this framework is (a) the specification of
stresses
or probabilistic constraints and (b) solving an optimization problem to find the probability measure under which the model satisfies the stresses and is “close” to a reference probability measure. One key advantage of reverse stress testing is that it provides the entire stressed probabilistic model, allowing for a comparison of the stressed model with the reference model. In a discrete and static setting,
Cambou & Filipović 2017
use the
f
-divergence to incorporate constraints on events, which they term “views”, while
Makam et al. 2021
consider the
\chi^{2}
-divergence and a constraint on the expected value of a risk factor. For general but static models,
Pesenti et al. 2019
and
Pesenti 2022
consider stresses defined via changes in risk measures such as Value-at-Risk (VaR), distortion risk measures, and expected utilities, using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) and the Wasserstein distance, respectively.

While all the aforementioned works consider static settings, and thus do not capture the potential impact of a stress cascading through time,
Breuer et al. 2012
and
Bellotti & Crook 2013
highlight the need for understanding the effect of stress testing dynamic models. Indeed, in a plethora of settings the time-development of an insurance or financial portfolios’ profit and loss (P&L) is of interest, and thus also its evolution under adverse stresses. Examples in actuarial science include claims reserving (
Merz & Wüthrich 2008
), variable annuities which rely on accurate dynamic mortality models (
Bacinello et al. 2011
), optimal pension accumulation and withdrawal (
Gerrard et al. 2004
), and valuation and hedging of insurance risks (
Dahl & Møller 2006
). In all these examples, the key interest lies in the dynamics of the (stressed) P&L. Moreover, quantifying the stressed dynamics of extreme losses allows for adequate risk assessment and management interventions, see e.g.,
Merz & Wüthrich 2008
for a detailed discussion.

The literature on stress testing in a dynamic
setting, however, is limited with
Breuer et al. 2012
and
Bellotti & Crook 2013
, who construct
adverse scenarios in a multi-period and thus discrete-time setting within a credit risk context. As such, a comprehensive treatment of stress testing for dynamic loss models in continuous-time is missing. We fill this gap by developing a (reverse) stress testing approach for compound Poisson processes in a continuous-time setting, which allows for multiple stresses on portfolio components, stresses at terminal and earlier time points, and provide an algorithm that allows to simulate under the stressed probability measure. While the starting point is a compound Poisson process, we allow the stressed dynamics to fall in the class of stochastic processes with state dependent intensity and state dependent severity distributions. A key advantage of our framework is that it explicitly quantifies the dynamics of the stressed stochastic process over the entire time horizon. Furthermore, the developed algorithm allows for efficient simulation of the stressed processes and thus provides a methodology to estimate, e.g., future losses and risk measures, under the stressed scenarios.

In this work, we contribute to the stress testing literature by generalizing the reverse stress testing approach to a dynamic setting, via stressing a stochastic process at a fixed point in time and characterizing the dynamics of the processes under the stressed probability measure. In particular, we consider an insurance portfolio over a finite time horizon, which under the reference probability measure is modelled by a compound Poisson process. We then are interested in how the dynamics of the portfolio need to be changed so that, under the altered dynamics, stress(es) at the terminal time are attained. The stresses we consider are expectations of functions applied to the portfolio at the terminal time. This includes stresses on risk measures such as Value-at-Risk (VaR), VaR and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), and expected utility constraints. To find the
stressed probability measure
, we seek over equivalent probability measures on the path-space of stochastic processes the one under which the process attains the stresses and which minimizes the KL divergence to the reference measure. We solve the optimization problem, characterize the unique stressed probability measure and the stressed dynamics of the stochastic process. We find that under the stressed probability measure, the intensity and the severity distribution of the process both depend on state and time. Thus, in the spirit of reverse stress testing, our analysis informs how the effects of a stress on a Poisson process results in a marked point process with time and state dependence in both intensity and severity distribution.
Differently from the static setting of reverse stress testing or the development of adverse scenarios, we require and are interested in a full characterization of the dynamics of the stochastic process under the stressed probability measure that lead to the constraint. Therefore, we also propose an algorithm to simulate sample paths under the stressed probability measures, and thus are able to illustrate the effects of a stress on the entire process.

Relevant for risk management purposes, we study a stress on VaR and a stress on VaR and CVaR jointly and provide illustrations of how the stochastic process is altered under these stresses. We further consider generalizations to multivariate compound Poisson processes and stresses at times earlier than the terminal time. For stresses on a sub-portfolio, we observe that the dependence between the sub-portfolios may amplify the stress. We illustrate the applicability of our framework by considering “what if” scenarios, where we examine what severity of stress on a portfolio component at an earlier time is needed such that the aggregate portfolio exceeds a risk threshold at the terminal time.

. The code is available at
https://github.com/emmakroell/stressing-dynamic-loss-models
.

Minimally Perturbed Compound Poisson Process

Let a filtered probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}})

{\mathcal{B}}(\cdot)

\sigma

dX_{t}=\int_{{\mathds{R}}}x\,\mu(dx,dt),

where

{\mathcal{F}}_{t}

{\mathcal{F}}

X
has mean measure (also known as the compensating measure) given by

\nu(dx,dt)=\kappa\,G(dx)dt,

thus
X
is a compound Poisson process with intensity
\kappa>0
and severity or jump size distribution
G
. We denote by
\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt):=\mu(dy,dt)-\nu(dy,dt)

Throughout, we call
{\mathbb{P}}
the
reference probability measure
and denote by
{\mathbb{Q}}
alternative probability measures on the same filtered probability space. For notational convenience, we write
{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\cdot]
for the expected value under
{\mathbb{Q}}
and set
{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]:={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
. To quantify discrepancies between probability measures, we use the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
(
Kullback & Leibler 1951
)
. The KL divergence of
{\mathbb{Q}}
with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}
(also known as the relative entropy) is defined as

D_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\;\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]&\text{if }{\mathbb{Q}}\ll{\mathbb{P}}\,,\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

where we use the convention
0\log 0=0
and
\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym (RN) derivative of
{\mathbb{Q}}
with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}
. For an overview of the properties of KL divergence, see, e.g.,
Van & Harremos 2014

With this notation at hand, we first motivate the optimization problem which is central to this work. Consider an insurance portfolio modelled by a compound Poisson process
X
under the reference measure
{\mathbb{P}}
. A modeller wishes to examine how the process is perturbed under
stresses
on its terminal value, i.e., on
X_{T}
. Here, we consider stresses of the form
{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f_{i}(X_{T})\right]=c_{i}
, for functions
f_{i}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
and constants
c_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Examples of stresses include expected utility constraints and risk measure constraints such as Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). For a specified stress, the modeller then seeks the most “plausible” probability measure under which the stochastic process achieves the stresses.

Specifically, we consider equivalent probability measures characterized by Girsanov’s theorem
(
Øksendal & Sulem 2019
)
, i.e. those with RN derivatives of the form

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[h_{t}(y)-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt)\right),

where
\mathfrak{E}(\cdot)
denotes the stochastic exponential and where
h:=(h_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a predictable, non-negative random field on
[0,T]
. To assure that
\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}
is indeed a martingale, we assume that
h
satisfies the following condition.

Definition 2.1
.

The random field
h:=(h_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the Novikov condition on the interval
[0,T]
if

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathds{R}}(1-h_{t}(y))^{2}\,\mu(dy,dt)\right)\right]<\infty.

We seek over probability measures of this form the one with minimal KL divergence to
{\mathbb{P}}
under which the process attains the constraints. Mathematically, we consider the following optimization problem.

Optimization Problem 2.2
.

Let
f_{i}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
and
c_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
for

\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}D_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f_{i}(X_{T})\right]=c_{i},\quad i\in[n]\,,

where
{\mathcal{Q}}
is the class of equivalent probability measures induced by Girsanov’s theorem, i.e.,

\mathcal{Q}:=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\;\Big|\;\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[h_{t}(y)-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt)\right)\right\},

Note that any probability measure

In general, a change of measure induces a modified intensity and severity distribution that may be time and space dependent. Proposition

, yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}d\left(\frac{Z_{t}}{\Theta_{t}}\right)&=\frac{Z_{t^{-}}}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\frac{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}}+y)}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})}-1\right)[\mu(dy,dt)-\nu(dy,dt)]\\
&\quad+\frac{Z_{t^{-}}}{\theta^{2}(t,X_{t^{-}})}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\theta(t,X_{t^{-}}+y)-\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})\right)\,\nu(dy,dt)\\
&\quad+Z_{t^{-}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\frac{1}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}}+y)}-\frac{1}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})}\right)\mu(dy,dt)\\
&\quad+Z_{t^{-}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\frac{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}}+y)}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}}+y)}-\frac{1}{\theta(t,X_{t^{-}})}\right)\mu(dy,dt),\end{split}

which, after inspecting the various terms, vanishes. Therefore,
Z_{t}=r\,\Theta_{t}
for some
r\in{\mathds{R}}
and all
t\in[0,T]
. As
Z
is a martingale,
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{T}]=Z_{0}=1
, moreover,
Z_{T}=r\,\Theta_{T}=r\,\mathfrak{Z}(X_{T})
. Therefore,
r=1/{\mathbb{E}}[\mathfrak{Z}(X_{T})]
, which proves the result.
∎

With the above result we obtain a succinct representation of the RN derivative associated with the Girsanov kernel
h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(t,X_{t-},y)
from
Theorem

2.3
, which is in the spirit of
Csiszár 1975
.

Corollary 2.5
.

For Lagrange multipliers
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T})\right)]<\infty
and the Girsanov kernel
h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
given in
Theorem

2.3
, the measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
induced by
h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
has RN derivative

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T})\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T})\right)\right]}.

Proof.

Since

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(t,X_{t-},y)-1\right)\,\nu(dy,dt)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\log h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(t,X_{t-},y)\;\mu(dy,dt)\right),

the result is an application of
Theorem

2.4
to the specific form of
h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
in
Theorem

2.3
.
∎

Finally, we provide the existence of a solution to
2.2
by discussing the existence of the Lagrange multipliers. In particular, we show that the Lagrange multipliers are solutions of a (possibly non-linear) system of equations.

Proposition 2.6
.

If there exists
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}=(\eta^{*}_{1},\ldots,\eta^{*}_{n})
such that

0={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{j}\,f_{j}(X_{T})\right)\left(f_{i}(X_{T})-c_{i}\right)\right],\quad i\in[n],

(8)

then
2.2
has a unique solution
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}:={\mathbb{Q}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}}
characterized by the Girsanov kernel
h^{*}_{t}(y):=h^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}}(t,X_{t^{-}},y)
.

Proof.

For
{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
and
i\in[n]
, we rewrite the constraint(s) from
2.2
using the RN derivative from
Corollary

2.5
to obtain

\displaystyle 0={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f_{i}(X_{T})-c_{i}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\,f_{j}(X_{T})\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\,f_{j}(X_{T})\right)\right]}\left(f_{i}(X_{T})-c_{i}\right)\right].

Cancellation gives (
8
).
∎

We refer to the solution of
2.2
, denoted by
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
, as the
stressed probability measure
. Depending on the constraints, explicit expressions for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}
may be available. We observe that whether Equation (
8
) has a solution depends on the choice of
f_{i}
and
c_{i}
,
i\in[n]
. One necessary condition for Equation (
8
) to have a solution is that each
c_{i}
must be in the support of
f_{i}(X_{T})
. This requirement means that the value of each constraint
c_{i}
is such it is attainable by
f_{i}(X_{T})
in some state of the world.

For the case of a single constraint, we can specify additional conditions on
f(X_{T})
and
c
such that a solution to
Equation

8
exists.

Proposition 2.7
.

If the cumulant generating function of
f(X_{T})
,
K_{f(X_{T})}(s):=\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sf(X_{T})}\right]
, satisfies
K_{f(X_{T})}(a),\,K_{f(X_{T})}(b)<\infty
for some
a,b\in{\mathds{R}}
with
a<b
, then for all

c\in\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}\right]},\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}\right]}\right),

(9)

there exists a unique solution
\eta^{*}
to

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta^{*}\,f(X_{T})}\left(f(X_{T})-c\right)\right]=0\,.

(10)

Proof.

Let
c
be in the interval given in Equation (
9
) and assume there exists
a,b\in{\mathds{R}}
,
a<b
, such that the cumulant generating function of
f(X_{T})
,
K_{f(X_{T})}(s)
, satisfies
K_{f(X_{T})}(a),\,K_{f(X_{T})}(b)<\infty
.
The latter implies, by properties of the cumulant generating function, that
K_{f(X_{T})}(s)<\infty
for all
s\in(a,b)
. As
K_{f(X_{T})}(s)
is convex and twice differentiable, its derivative,
\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)
, exists and is continuously increasing on
(a,b)
. We observe that

\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)\Big|_{s=a}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}\right]}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)\Big|_{s=b}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}\right]}\,.

Thus
\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)
must take on all intermediate values between
{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]/{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{af(X_{T})}\right]
and

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]/{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{bf(X_{T})}\right]
. Hence
there must exist an
-\eta^{*}\in(a,b)
such that

c=\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)\Big|_{s=-\eta^{*}}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta^{*}f(X_{T})}f(X_{T})\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta^{*}f(X_{T})}\right]}.

(11)

Uniqueness of
\eta^{*}
follows by increasingness of
\frac{d}{ds}K_{f(X_{T})}(s)
. Finally, rearranging (
11
) gives (
10
).
∎

Note that if
f
is constant, the condition in
Equation

9
becomes void. In that case, one must have
c=f(X_{T})
for
Equation

10
to have a solution.
An example when the assumptions in
Proposition

2.7
are fulfilled is when
f(X_{T})
is bounded. In this case the cumulant generating function exists and is finite for all
s\in{\mathds{R}}
. Thus, as a consequence of the above proposition we obtain that any stress
c
satisfying
-\infty<\essinf f(X_{T})<c<\esssup f(X_{T})<+\infty
yields a solution to
Equation

10
. A case in point is VaR, which we discuss in detail in the
Section

3
. We further consider the case of two constraints, VaR and CVaR, and give explicit conditions under which a solution exists.

Under a stressed probability measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
, the stochastic process
X
is no longer a true compound Poisson process due to the time- and state-dependence of the Girsanov kernel
h^{*}(t,x,y)
. Indeed, the next result states the intensity process and the severity distribution of
X
under
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The intensity process and severity distribution of the process
X
under the stressed measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
are, respectively,

\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{*}(t,x,y)G(dy)\quad\text{and}\quad G^{*}(t,x,dy)=\frac{h^{*}(t,x,dy)G(dy)}{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x,dy^{\prime})G(dy^{\prime})}.

Proof.

By Girsanov’s Theorem, the compensator of
\mu(dy,dt)
under
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
is
\nu^{*}(dy,dt)=h^{*}(t,x,y)\,\nu(dy,dt)
and thus the intensity of the process becomes:

\kappa^{*}(t,x):=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu^{*}(dy,ds)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{*}(t,x,y)\kappa G(dy)dt=\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{*}(t,x,y)G(dy).

We obtain the updated severity distribution from
\nu^{*}(dy,dt)=h^{*}(t,x,y)\,\kappa G(dy)dt
, by multiplying and dividing by the integral term in
\kappa^{*}(t,x)
.
∎

Under the stressed measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
, we refer to the intensity
\kappa^{*}
and the severity distribution
G^{*}
as the stressed intensity and stressed severity distributions, respectively. Note that while in
Proposition

2.8
we state the dynamics of
X
under the optimal measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
, the result holds for any fixed and not necessarily optimal Lagrange multiplier
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
,
h^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, and corresponding
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.

Corollary

2.5
gives a concise representation of the RN derivative of the stressed measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
as a function of the value of the process at terminal time only, i.e., of
X_{T}
. This result, that the RN derivative of the stressed measure induced by perturbing a stochastic process is a function only of the terminal value of the process, is a priori not obvious. However, this representation of the RN derivative does not explicitly characterize the dynamics of the process under
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
. Indeed, the stressed intensity process and severity distribution, see
Proposition

2.8
, are functions of the optimal random field
h^{*}
given in
Theorem

2.3
. We moreover provide in
Section

5
an algorithm to simulate sample paths of the process under the stressed probability measure.

3
Risk Measure Constraints

In this section, we apply our framework to a risk management context. Specifically, we consider constraints given by the two most widely used risk measures, Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). For a constraint on VaR and joint constraints on VaR and CVaR, we examine the dynamics of
X
under the stressed measure both analytically and numerically.

3.1
Value-at-Risk

We first examine a stress on VaR. The VaR of a random variable
Z
under a measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
at level
\alpha\in[0,1]
is defined as

\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(Z)=\inf\left\{z\in{\mathds{R}}\,|\,F^{\mathbb{Q}}_{Z}(z)\geq\alpha\right\},

where
F^{\mathbb{Q}}_{Z}
denotes the distribution function of
Z
under
{\mathbb{Q}}
and we use the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
. For notational simplicity, we write
\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(Z):=\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(Z)
.

We implement a
{\text{VaR}}_{\alpha}
constraint in our framework by setting
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x<q\}}
and
c=\alpha\in(0,1)
in
2.2
, which gives the constraint

{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{T}<q)=\alpha.

(12)

When the distribution of
X_{T}
under
{\mathbb{P}}
is continuous to the left of
q
, then (
12
) is equivalent to requiring
\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(X_{T})=q
; otherwise, it may be viewed as a probability constraint. Furthermore,
q
must be chosen such that the constraint is attainable, meaning that
q
must lie between the essential infimum and essential supremum of
X_{T}
, a requirement on the choice of
q
rather than the process
X
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\essinf X_{T}<q<\esssup X_{T}
. The solution to
2.2
with constraint given by
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x<q\}}
and
c=\alpha
is the measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
characterized by the Girsanov kernel

h^{*}(t,x,y)=\frac{1+\left(e^{-\eta^{*}}-1\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}<q-x-y\right)}{1+\left(e^{-\eta^{*}}-1\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}<q-x\right)},

(13)

where the Lagrange multiplier
\eta^{*}
is given by

\eta^{*}=\log\left(\frac{(1-\alpha){\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)}{\alpha\,{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}\geq q)}\right)\,.

The solution is unique.

Proof.

Letting
f(X_{T})=\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}
in
Theorem

2.3
, we have for any
\eta
that

\displaystyle h^{\eta}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x+y}\left[e^{-\eta\,\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}}\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta\,\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x+y}\left[1+\left(e^{-\eta}-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[1+\left(e^{-\eta}-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\left(e^{-\eta}-1\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}<q-x-y\right)}{1+\left(e^{-\eta}-1\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}<q-x\right)}.

We find the optimal Lagrange multiplier
\eta^{*}
by applying
Proposition

2.6
. For each
h^{\eta}
, the corresponding measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\eta}
has RN derivative given in
Corollary

2.5
. Thus, the
constraint (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle 0={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}-\alpha\right)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(e^{-\eta}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right)\left(\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}-\alpha\right)\right].

Solving for
\eta
gives the result, which is well-defined by the choice of
\alpha
and
q
.
∎

We note that since
\alpha={\mathbb{Q}}(X_{T}<q)
, the optimal Lagrange multiplier
\eta^{*}
is the log-odds ratio of the event
\{X_{T}<q\}
under the reference and the stressed probability measures. If
q
is chosen smaller than
{\text{VaR}}_{\alpha}(X_{T})
, corresponding to a downward stress on VaR, then it holds
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)\leq{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{T}<q)
, and thus
\eta^{*}\leq 0
. Similarly, if
q
is larger than
{\text{VaR}}_{\alpha}(X_{T})
, corresponding to an upward stress on VaR, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)\geq{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{T}<q)
, and thus
\eta^{*}\geq 0
.

The Girsanov kernel
h^{*}(t,x,y)
provides information on how the process is distorted under the stressed measure.
More specifically, Proposition
2.8
states the stressed intensity and severity distributions, and that these are, in general, state and time dependent. The specific form of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
in this example leads to a few observations summarized in the next two propositions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a compound Poisson process with intensity rate
\kappa
and severity random variable
\xi
, where
\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\sim}}G
has non-negative support.
Let
h^{*}
be given in
Proposition

3.1
, i.e., the Girsanov kernel associated with the solution to
2.2
and constraints
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x<q\}}
and
c=\alpha
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\essinf X_{T}<q<\esssup X_{T}
. Then,
h^{*}(t,x,y)
satisfies for all
y\geq 0
,

\lim_{t\to T}h^{*}(t,x,y)=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{if}\quad x>q\,,\\
e^{\eta^{*}}+\left(1-e^{\eta^{*}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{x<q-y\}}\qquad&\text{if}\quad x\leq q\,.\end{cases}

Moreover, if
x>q
, then
h^{*}(t,x,y)=1
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

If
x>q
, then from the form of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
given in
Proposition

3.1
, we observe that the probabilities in the numerator and denominator of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
are both equal to 0 and thus
h^{*}(t,x,y)=1
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Suppose that
x\leq q
. Using the properties of a Poisson process over small time intervals, we obtain an approximation of
{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}\leq q-z\right)
for
z\in{\mathds{R}}
when
t
is close to
T
. Using that under
{\mathbb{P}}
,
X_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\xi_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. severity random variables with distribution
G
and
N_{t}
is a Poisson process with intensity
\kappa
,
we obtain for
\epsilon>0
small

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{T-\epsilon}\leq q-z\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{N_{T-\epsilon}}^{N_{T}}\xi_{i}\leq q-z\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(N_{\epsilon}=0\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\{N_{\epsilon}=1\}\cap\{\xi\leq q-z\}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\{N_{\epsilon}>1\}\cap\{\xi\leq q-z\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\kappa\epsilon+o(\epsilon)+(\kappa\epsilon+o(\epsilon)){\mathbb{P}}\left(\xi\leq q-z\right)+o(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-z)-1\right)+o(\epsilon).

(14)

We split the case
x\leq q
into two parts: first, we further assume that
x>q-y
, then the probability in the numerator of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
is zero, thus substituting the approximation given in (
14
) gives

h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{1}{1+\left(e^{-\eta^{*}}-1\right)\left[1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x)-1\right)+o(\epsilon)\right]}.

When
\epsilon\to 0
, we have
h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)\to e^{\eta^{*}}
.

For the second part we further assume that
x\leq q-y
. Substituting the short-time approximation (
14
) into both the numerator and denominator of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
, gives

h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{1+\left(e^{-\eta^{*}}-1\right)\left[1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x-y)-1\right)+o(\epsilon)\right]}{1+\left(e^{-\eta^{*}}-1\right)\left[1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x)-1\right)+o(\epsilon)\right]},

which converges to 1 as
\epsilon\to 1
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
X
be a compound Poisson process with rate
\kappa
and severity random variable
\xi
, where
\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\sim}}G
has non-negative support. Let
\kappa^{*}
be the intensity process of
X
under the stressed measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
that solves
2.2
with constraints
f(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x<q\}}
and
c=\alpha
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\essinf X_{T}<q<\esssup X_{T}
. Then
\kappa^{*}(t,x)
satisfies

\lim_{t\to T}\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\begin{cases}\kappa\qquad&\text{if}\quad x>q\,,\\
\kappa\left[e^{\eta^{*}}+\left(1-e^{\eta^{*}}\right)G(q-x)\right]\qquad&\text{if}\quad x\leq q\,.\end{cases}

Moreover, if
x>q
, then
\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\kappa
for all
t\in[0,T]
, i.e., the stressed intensity is equal to the intensity under the reference measure
{\mathbb{P}}
.

Proof.

If
x>q
, then by
Proposition

3.2
,
h^{*}(t,x,y)=1
for all
t\in[0,T]
and therefore
\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}\kappa\,G(dy)=\kappa
for
t\in[0,T]
. Else, if
x\leq q
, we have by dominated convergence:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}\kappa^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}\lim_{t\to T}h^{*}(t,x,y)G(dy)

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}\left[e^{\eta^{*}}+\left(1-e^{\eta^{*}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{x\leq q-y\}}\right]G(dy)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left[e^{\eta^{*}}+(1-e^{\eta^{*}})G(q-x)\right].

∎

\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(X_{T})
.

3.2
Conditional Value-at-Risk

Next, we examine the effect on the dynamics of
X
under a stress on the risk measure CVaR. The CVaR, also called Expected Shortfall, of a random variable
Z
under a measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
at level
\alpha\in[0,1)
is
(
Acerbi & Tasche 2002
)

\text{CVaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(Z)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{u}(Z)\,du\,.

For notational simplicity, we write
\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Z):=\text{CVaR}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(Z)
.

Since the value of CVaR
{}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}}
depends on that of VaR
{}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}}
, we impose constraints on both risk measures. We exploit the representation

\text{CVaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(Z)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}[(X-\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(Z))_{+}]+\text{VaR}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\alpha}(Z)

to write this optimization problem in the form of
2.2
using the following two constraints:

{\mathbb{Q}}(X_{T}<q)=\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(X_{T}-q)_{+}]=(s-q)(1-\alpha).

(15)

The solution to
2.2
with the above constraints is given in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
K_{X_{T}|X_{T}>q}(s)
denote the cumulant generating function of
X_{T}|X_{T}>q
. Suppose
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\essinf X_{T}<q<s<\esssup X_{T}
, and there exists an
r>0
such that
K_{X_{T}|X_{T}>q}(s)<\infty
for all
s\in(-r,r)
. Then the solution to
2.2
with constraints
f_{1}(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x<q\}}
,
c_{1}=\alpha
,
f_{2}(x)=(x-q)\mathds{1}_{\{x\geq q\}}
, and
c_{2}=(s-q)(1-\alpha)
is the measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
characterized by the Girsanov kernel

h^{*}(t,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}\leq q-x-y\right)+e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}-X_{t-}\geq q-x-y\}}\right]}{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}\leq q-x\right)+e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}-X_{t-}\geq q-x\}}\right]},

where
\eta_{2}^{*}
is the solution to

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-q)}(X_{T}-s)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}>q\}}\right]=0

(16)

and

\eta_{1}^{*}=\log\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right]}\right)=\eta^{\text{VaR}}+\log\left(\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}\geq q)}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right]}\right).

Here,
\eta^{\text{VaR}}
refers to the optimal Lagrange multiplier when only a VaR constraint is applied (
Proposition

3.1
). The optimization problem has a unique solution.

Proof.

First, we find the Girsanov kernel for given Lagrange multipliers
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
. We compute

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\eta_{1}\,f_{1}(X_{T})-\eta_{2}f_{2}(X_{T})\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta_{1}{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q){\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta_{1}}{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\eta_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q|X_{t-}=x)+e^{\eta_{2}q}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}X_{T}}{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}|X_{t-}=x\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\eta_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}-X_{t-}<q-x)+e^{-\eta_{2}(x-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}{\mathds{1}}_{\{X_{T}-X_{t-}\geq q-x\}}\right]\,,

and the formula for
h^{*}
, if the optimal Lagrange multipliers exist, follows from
Theorem

2.3
. Next, we show the existence of the Lagrange multipliers and their representation.
By
Corollary

2.5
, the RN derivative of the measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
that solves
2.2
with constraints (
15
) is

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\frac{e^{-\eta_{1}f_{1}(X_{T})-\eta_{2}f_{2}(X_{T})}}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{1}f_{1}(X_{T})-\eta_{2}f_{2}(X_{T}}]}=\frac{e^{-\eta_{1}}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{1}}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}]}.

Using this representation of the RN derivative, the first constraint can be written as

\displaystyle 0={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(e^{-\eta_{1}}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right)\left(\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}-\alpha\right)\right].

Solving for
\eta_{1}
gives

\eta_{1}=\log\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}]}\right).

(17)

Analogously, we may write the second constraint as

\displaystyle 0={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(e^{-\eta_{1}}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right)\Big((X_{T}-q)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}-(s-q)(1-\alpha)\Big)\right].

Simplifying this expression and substituting in the above expression for
\eta_{1}
gives

0={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}(X_{T}-s)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right],

(18)

providing (
16
).
To determine if (
18
) has a solution, we first rewrite for fixed
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
the RN derivative using (
17
):

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\frac{\alpha}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}<q\}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}]}e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}.

Therefore, the second constraint becomes

\displaystyle(s-q)(1-\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}\left[(X_{T}-q)_{+}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}]}e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}(X_{T}-q)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}\geq q\}}\right].

Simplifying the above expression gives

s-q=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}(X_{T}-q)_{+}\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}>q\}}]}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}(X_{T}-q)|X_{T}>q\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-q)}|X_{T}>q]}.

(19)

We observe that the right-hand side of (
19
) is the derivative of the cumulant generating function of
X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q
evaluated at
-\eta_{2}
. We will denote the derivative of the cumulant generating function of
X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q
by
\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)
. Since moment generating functions are log-convex,
\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)\big|_{s=-\eta_{2}}
is decreasing in
\eta_{2}
.
To show that there exists a solution to
Equation

19
, we denote
\alpha^{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}<q)
and
s^{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{E}}[X_{T}|X_{T}>q]
.

Consider the following cases: first, if
s=s^{\mathbb{P}}
, then
\eta_{2}=0
solves (
19
). Second, assume that
s>s^{\mathbb{P}}
. Observe that when
\eta_{2}=0
, the right-hand side of (
19
) is
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q]=s^{\mathbb{P}}-q<s-q
by assumption. Moreover, as
\eta_{2}\to-\infty
,
\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)\big|_{s=-\eta_{2}}
is monotonically increasing, and in fact,

\lim_{\eta_{2}\to-\infty}\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)\big|_{s=-\eta_{2}}=\esssup X_{T}-q>s-q.

Thus we conclude (
19
) must have a solution for some
\eta_{2}<0
.

Finally, suppose
s<s^{\mathbb{P}}
. When
\eta_{2}=0
, the right-hand side of (
19
) is
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q]>s-q
. Since as
\eta_{2}\to\infty
,
\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)\big|_{s=-\eta_{2}}
is monotonically decreasing and

\lim_{\eta_{2}\to\infty}\frac{d}{ds}K_{X_{T}-q|X_{T}>q}(s)\big|_{s=-\eta_{2}}=\essinf X_{T}-q<s-q,

we conclude (
19
) must have a solution for some
\eta_{2}>0
.
∎

We note that the sign of
\eta_{2}^{*}
is as follows:

Corollary 3.6
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
q
and
s
be the stressed
{\text{VaR}}_{\alpha}
and
\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}
values, respectively. Define
\alpha^{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{T}<q)
and
s^{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{E}}[X_{T}|X_{T}>q]
. If
s<s^{\mathbb{P}}
, then
\eta_{2}^{*}>0
, and if
s>s^{\mathbb{P}}
, then
\eta_{2}^{*}<0
.

This follows directly from the proof of
Proposition

3.5
. If
X_{T}
is continuous,
s^{\mathbb{P}}=\text{CVaR}_{\alpha^{\mathbb{P}}}(X_{T})
.
Thus, the sign of
\eta_{2}^{*}
depends in a direct manner on the stress on CVaR while its dependence on the stress on VaR is indirectly through
\alpha^{\mathbb{P}}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let the severity distribution
G
have non-negative support. Let
h^{*}
be given in
Proposition

3.5
, i.e., the Girsanov kernel associated with the solution to
2.2
with constraints (
15
), where
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\essinf X_{T}<q<s<\esssup X_{T}
, and there exists an
r>0
such that
K_{X_{T}|X_{T}>q}(s)<\infty
for all
s\in(-r,r)
. Then for all
y\geq 0
,
h^{*}(t,x,y)
satisfies

\lim_{t\to T}h^{*}(t,x,y)=\begin{cases}e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}y}\qquad&\text{if}\quad x>q\,,\\
\mathds{1}_{\{y<q-x\}}+e^{\eta_{1}^{*}-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{y\geq q-x\}}\qquad&\text{if}\quad x\leq q\,.\end{cases}

Moreover, if
x>q
, then
h^{*}(t,x,y)=e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}y}
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

First, if
x>q
, then
q-x<0
and
q-x-y<0
, thus the probabilities in the numerator and denominator of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
from
Proposition

3.5
are both 0, and the indicator functions in the expectations are both identically equal to 1. This gives

h^{*}(t,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}\right]}{e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}\right]}=e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}y}.

Second, if
x\leq q
, we examine the limiting behaviour of
h^{*}(t,x,y)
. In addition to the approximation of
{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{T}-X_{t-}\leq q-z\right)
derived in the proof of
Proposition

3.2
, we further use the properties of a Poisson process over short intervals to obtain an approximation of
{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}(X_{T}-X_{t-})}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}-X_{t-}\geq q-z\}}\right]
for
t=T-\epsilon
,
\epsilon>0
, as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(X_{T}-X_{T-\epsilon})}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{T}-X_{T-\epsilon}\geq q-z\}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-z\}}\right]{\mathbb{P}}(N_{\epsilon}=1)+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{0\geq q-z\}}\right]{\mathbb{P}}(N_{\epsilon}=0)+o(\epsilon)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-z\}}\right](\kappa\epsilon+o(\epsilon))+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{0\geq q-z\}}\right](1-\kappa\epsilon+o(\epsilon))+o(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\kappa\epsilon\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-z\}}\right]-\mathds{1}_{\{z\geq q\}}\right)+\mathds{1}_{\{z\geq q\}}+o(\epsilon)\,.

Using these two approximations, we consider two cases: first, if
q-y<x\leq q
, then for
t=T-\epsilon
we have

\displaystyle h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}\left[\kappa\epsilon\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq 0\}}\right]-1\right)+1\right]+o(\epsilon)}{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}\,\left(1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x)-1\right)\right)+e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}\left(\kappa\epsilon\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-x\}}\right]\right)+o(\epsilon)}\,.

Taking the limit, we obtain

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}}{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}}=e^{\eta_{1}^{*}-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}\,.

Else, if
x\leq q-y
, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}\,\left(1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x-y)-1\right)\right)+e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}\,\kappa\epsilon\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-x-y\}}\right]+o(\epsilon)}{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}\,\left(1+\kappa\epsilon\left(G(q-x)-1\right)\right)+e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}\,\kappa\epsilon\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-x\}}\right]+o(\epsilon)}\,.

Taking the limit, we obtain

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}h^{*}(T-\epsilon,x,y)=\frac{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}}{e^{-\eta_{1}^{*}}}=1.

∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let the severity random variable
\xi
with
{\mathbb{P}}
-distribution
G
have non-negative support. Then, the limiting behaviour of
\kappa^{*}(t,x)
is

\lim_{t\to T}\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\begin{cases}\kappa\,{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}]\qquad&\text{if}\quad x>q\,,\\[5.0pt]
\kappa\left(G(q-x)+e^{\eta_{1}^{*}-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi\geq q-x\}}]\right)\qquad&\text{if}\quad x\leq q\,.\end{cases}

Moreover, if
x>q
, the stressed intensity is
\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\kappa\,{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}]
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

If
x>q
, we have
h^{*}(t,x,y)=e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}y}
by the previous proposition, so

\kappa^{*}(t,x)=\kappa\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}y}G(dy)=\kappa\;{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}].

If
x\leq q
, we apply dominated convergence to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T}\kappa^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}\lim_{t\to T}h^{*}(t,x,y)\kappa\,G(dy)

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{{\mathds{R}}_{+}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{y\leq q-x\}}+e^{\eta_{1}^{*}-\eta_{2}^{*}(x+y-q)}\mathds{1}_{\{y>q-x\}}\right]G(dy)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left(G(q-x)+e^{\eta_{1}^{*}-\eta_{2}^{*}(x-q)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\eta_{2}^{*}\xi}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi>q-x\}}\right]\right).

∎

If the severity distribution under the reference measure
{\mathbb{P}}
is explicitly given, we may obtain the limit of
\kappa^{*}(t,x)
for
t\to T
analytically. In keeping with our recurring example, let

under

where

.

.

4
Aggregate Portfolios and “What-if” Scenarios

Here we provide two extensions of our framework which allow for sensitivity testing of insurance portfolios. In
Section

4.1
, we solve the optimization problem when a stress is imposed at an earlier time than the terminal time
T
and when we have two constraints at different times. In
Section

4.2
, we generalize the results to multivariate compound Poisson processes. Combining these extension allows for application of “what-if” scenarios in the spirit of
Example

4.13
. In that application, we consider an aggregate insurance portfolio and study how a stress at time
T^{\dagger}<T
on one of the sub-portfolios cascades through time and affects the aggregate portfolio. We observe that a stress on one sub-portfolio perturbs not only the other sub-portfolios but also the dependence between the sub-portfolios.

4.1
Stresses at Earlier Times

First, suppose that instead of imposing constraints at the terminal time
T
, we impose constraints at an earlier time
T^{\dagger}\in(0,T)
. This leads to the following optimization problem:

Optimization Problem 4.1
.

Let
0<T^{\dagger}<T<\infty
,
f_{i}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
, and
c_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
for
i\in[n]
. Consider

\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}D_{KL}({\mathbb{Q}}\,\|\,{\mathbb{P}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right]=c_{i},\quad i\in[n]\,,

where
{\mathcal{Q}}
is the class of equivalent probability measures given by

\mathcal{Q}:=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\;\Big|\;\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[h_{t}(y)-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt)\right)\right\}

and
h:{\mathds{R}}_{+}\times{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
is a predictable, non-negative process satisfying Novikov’s condition on
[0,T]
.

Note that
4.1
is not a special case of
2.2
as the probability measures we seek over are induced by random fields
h
that live on
[0,T]
. The solution to this new optimization problem is as follows:

Proposition 4.2
.

If there exists
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}=(\eta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{*})\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{*}\,f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)]<\infty
and

0={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{j}\,f_{j}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\left(f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c_{i}\right)\right],\quad i\in[n],

then
4.1
has a solution.

The optimal measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
is characterized by the Girsanov kernel

h^{*}(t,x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x+y}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\right]}&\text{if}\quad t\leq T^{\dagger}\,,\\
1&\text{if}\quad t>T^{\dagger}\end{cases}

and the corresponding RN derivative is given by

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)]}.

The solution is unique.

Proof.

First, we show that we can restrict to probability measures that are induced by a specific random field. For this let
{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
for a random field
h
and define

\tilde{h}_{t}:=h_{t}{\mathds{1}}_{t\in[0,T^{\dagger}]}+{\mathds{1}}_{t\in(T^{\dagger},T]}
and moreover, denote its induced probability measure by
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\tilde{h}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
. The KL-divergence then becomes

\displaystyle D_{KL}({\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\|{\mathbb{P}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[1-h_{t}(y)(1-\log h_{t}(y))\right]\,\nu(dy,dt)\right]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[1-h_{t}(y)(1-\log h_{t}(y))\right]\,\nu(dy,dt)\right],\quad(\star)

where the last inequality follows since
h_{t}\geq 0
and
\ell(x):=1-x(1-\log x)
is a non-negative convex function that attains its minimum when
x\equiv 1
with
\ell(1)=0
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle(\star)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{T^{\dagger}}\right]\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[1-h_{t}(y)(1-\log h_{t}(y))\right]\,\nu(dy,dt)\Bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(h_{t}(y)-1\right)\,\nu(dy,dt)+\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\log h_{t}(y)\;\mu(dy,dt)\right)\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[1-h_{t}(y)(1-\log h_{t}(y))\right]\,\nu(dy,dt)\Bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\tilde{h}_{t}(y)-1\right)\,\nu(dy,dt)+\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\log\tilde{h}_{t}(y)\;\mu(dy,dt)\right)\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[1-\tilde{h}_{t}(y)(1-\log\tilde{h}_{t}(y))\right]\,\nu(dy,dt)\Bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{\tilde{h}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\log\left(\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{\tilde{h}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=D_{KL}({\mathbb{Q}}_{\tilde{h}}\|{\mathbb{P}})\,.

Thus we can indeed restrict to probability measures that are induced by random fields that are constant equal to 1 on
(T^{\dagger},T]
, i.e., to

\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}:=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}\;|\;h_{t}(y)=1\text{ for }t\in(T^{\dagger},T]\right\}\,.

Next, we observe that this set of probability measures is equivalent to the following set

\tilde{{\mathcal{Q}}}=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}\;\Big|\;\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T^{\dagger}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[h_{t}(y)-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt)\right)\right\}=:{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}\subset{\mathcal{Q}}\,,

that is probability measures induced by random fields defined on
[0,T^{\dagger}]
.
Thus, we showed that in
4.1
we can restrict to probability measures in
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}
. Restricting to
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}
provides an optimization problem that is of the form
2.2
and whose solution and uniqueness is given by
Theorem

2.3
,
Corollary

2.5
, and
Proposition

2.6
.
∎

Next, we impose constraints at both the terminal time
T
and an earlier time
T^{\dagger}\in(0,T)
, leading to the following optimization problem:

Optimization Problem 4.3
.

Let
0<T^{\dagger}<T<\infty
,
f,f^{\dagger}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
, and
c,c^{\dagger}\in{\mathds{R}}
. Consider

\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}D_{KL}({\mathbb{Q}}\,\|\,{\mathbb{P}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})\right]=c^{\dagger}\text{ and }\;\;{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f(X_{T})\right]=c\,,

where
{\mathcal{Q}}
is the class of equivalent probability measures given by

\mathcal{Q}:=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\;\Big|\;\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left[h_{t}(y)-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(dy,dt)\right)\right\}

and
h:{\mathds{R}}_{+}\times{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
is a predictable, non-negative process satisfying Novikov’s condition on
[0,T]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

If a solution to
4.3
exists, it is given by
{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}
with
h
characterized by
h_{t}(y)=h^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,X_{t^{-}},y)
, where

h^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x,y)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x+y}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger}){\mathds{1}}_{t<T^{\dagger}}\right)\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger}){\mathds{1}}_{t<T^{\dagger}}\right)\right]}\,

and
\eta,\eta^{\dagger}
are Lagrange multipliers such that the constraints hold. Furthermore, the solution, if it exists, is unique.

Proof.

The Langrangian associated with the problem is

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\right)+\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})+\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right]\,.

As before, we write

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\right)\right]={\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\big(1-(1-\log h(t,y))\;h(t,y)\big)\,\kappa\,G(dy)dt\right].

Thus, the associated value function at time
t
is

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t,x}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\big(1-(1-\log h(t,y))\;h(t,y)\big)\,\kappa\,G(dy)dt+\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})+\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right].

We consider two cases: First, if
t\in(T^{\dagger},T]
, then
f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable, so

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})+\ell^{\eta}(t,x)\,,

where

\ell^{\eta}(t,x):=\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t,x}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathds{R}}\big(1-(1-\log h(t,y))\;h(t,y)\big)\,\kappa\,G(dy)dt+\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right],\quad t\in(T^{\dagger},T].

This is a value function of the same form as in the proof of
Theorem

2.3
. Thus by the same procedure, we find that the optimal control is
h^{\eta}(t,x,y)=e^{-\Delta_{y}\ell^{\eta}(t,x)}
and

\ell^{\eta}(t,x)=-\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)}\right]\right),\quad t\in(T^{\dagger},T].

(20)

Second, if
t\in(0,T^{\dagger}]
, then we write the value function as follows

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t,x}\left[\int_{t}^{T^{\dagger}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\big(1-(1-\log h(t,y))\;h(t,y)\big)\,\kappa\,G(dy)dt+\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})+\ell^{\eta}(T^{{\dagger+}},x)\right]\,,

where
\ell^{\eta}(T^{{\dagger+}},x):=\lim_{t\searrow T^{\dagger}}\ell^{\eta}(t,x)
denotes the limit of
\ell^{\eta}(t,x)
as
t
approaches
T^{\dagger}
from the right.

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)
satisfies the HJB equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)+\inf_{h}\left\{{\mathcal{L}}^{h}J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)+\int_{\mathds{R}}\big(1-(1-\log h(t,y))\;h(t,y)\big)\,\kappa\,G(dy)\right\}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(T^{\dagger},x)

\displaystyle=\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(x)-c^{\dagger})+\ell^{\eta}(T^{\dagger+},x).

The optimal control is
h^{\eta}(t,x,y)=e^{-\Delta_{y}J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)}
, and again using the Cole-Hopf change of variables and the Feynman-Kac Theorem, we obtain

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=-\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})+\ell^{\eta}(T^{\dagger+},X_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\right]\right),\quad t\in(0,T^{\dagger}].

Substituting in (
20
) and using the tower property of expectations, we obtain

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=-\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right)\right]\right),\quad t\in(0,T^{\dagger}].

Taken together, we have

h^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x,y)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x+y}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger}){\mathds{1}}_{t<T^{\dagger}}\right)\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger}){\mathds{1}}_{t<T^{\dagger}}\right)\right]}\,,

and

J^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}(t,x)=\begin{cases}\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})-\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)}\right]\right)&t<T^{\dagger},\\
-\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)}\right]\right)&t\geq T^{\dagger}.\end{cases}

∎

To obtain expressions for the optimal Lagrange multipliers, we note that
Theorem

2.4
can be extended to show that if
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right)\right]<\infty
, then the RN density of the solution
{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}
from
Proposition

4.4
admits the form

\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}^{\eta,\eta^{\dagger}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\frac{\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})}{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)\right)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right]}\,.

(21)

This representation gives us the following system of equations for
\eta
and
\eta^{\dagger}
:

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists a unique solution to
4.3
if there exist Lagrange multipliers
\eta^{\dagger},\eta
such that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right)\right]<\infty
and the system of equations

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right)(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right],

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\eta(f(X_{T})-c)-\eta^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger}(X_{T^{\dagger}})-c^{\dagger})\right)(f(X_{T})-c)\right],

has a solution.

Proof.

Rewriting the constraints using (
21
) concludes the proof.
∎

Observe that the proof of
Proposition

4.4
can be generalized to a finite number of constraints at different time points, by splitting the value function into additional pieces. An alternative approach to obtain the stressed measure and the dynamics of the process under the stressed measure, is to increase the state space and introduce stopped processes. Specifically, suppose that we have a constraint of the form
{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[f(X_{T_{1}},\dots,X_{T_{n}})]=c
where
0<T_{1}<\dots<T_{n}\leq T
, then, by introducing the set of stopped processes
X^{(i)}_{t}:=X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\{t\leq T_{i}\}}+X_{T_{i}}\mathds{1}_{\{t>T_{1}\}}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, the original constraint can be viewed as a single constraint on a multi-variate process at terminal time, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[f(X_{T}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{T}^{(n)})]=c
. Thus the approach in the next section, where we consider multivariate compound Poisson processes, can be applied to this new problem.

4.2
Multivariate Compound Poisson Processes

In this section, we consider a
d
-dimensional compound Poisson process
\boldsymbol{X}:=\left(X_{t}^{1},\ldots,X_{t}^{d}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
. We denote (random) vectors using bold font to distinguish them from (random) variables and scalars. We assume that, under
{\mathbb{P}}
,
\boldsymbol{X}
has mean measure

\nu(\boldsymbol{x},dt)=\kappa\,G(d\boldsymbol{x})dt,

where
G
is the
d
-dimensional severity distribution and
\kappa>0
the scalar intensity. The optimization problem we consider in this section is a stress applied to one of the components of
\boldsymbol{X}
at time
T^{\dagger}\in(0,T]
. For simplicity of notation, we stress throughout the first component of
\boldsymbol{X}
, i.e.,
X^{1}
.

Optimization Problem 4.6
.

Let
0<T^{\dagger}\leq T<\infty
,
f_{i}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
and
c_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}
for
i\in[n]
, and consider

\inf_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}D_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right]=c_{i},\quad i\in[n]\,,

where
{\mathcal{Q}}
is the class of equivalent probability measures induced by Girsanov’s theorem

\mathcal{Q}:=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}\;\Big|\;\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}_{h}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathfrak{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathds{R}}^{d}}\left[h_{t}(\boldsymbol{y})-1\right]\,\tilde{\mu}(d\boldsymbol{y},dt)\right)\right\},

and
h:{\mathds{R}}_{+}\times{\mathds{R}}^{d}\to{\mathds{R}}
is a predictable, non-negative random field satisfying Novikov’s condition on
[0,T]
.

The solution to
4.6
is given in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.7
.

If there exists
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}=(\eta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{*})\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{*}\,f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right)]<\infty
and

0={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{j}\,f_{j}(X_{T^{\dagger}}^{1})\right)\left(f_{i}(X_{T^{\dagger}}^{1})-c_{i}\right)\right]\quad\text{for }i\in[n],

then
4.6
has a solution. The solution is the measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
characterized by the Girsanov kernel

h^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\begin{cases}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{t,\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\right]}&\text{if}\quad t\leq T^{\dagger}\\
1&\text{if}\quad t>T^{\dagger}\,.\end{cases}

The solution is unique.

We omit the proof of
Proposition

4.7
as it follows using steps similar to those in the proof of
Theorem

2.3
and arguments in
Section

4.1
. Note that if a stress is applied at the terminal time, i.e.,
T^{\dagger}=T
, then
h^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
simplifies to a representation akin to that in
Section

2
. Next, we show that the optimal Girsanov kernel is a function of
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
only.

Corollary 4.8
.

The optimal Girsanov kernel depends only on the first components of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle h^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\left.{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{*}_{i}\,f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\,\right|\,X^{1}_{t^{-}}=x_{1}+y_{1}\right]}{\left.{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{*}\,f_{i}(X^{1}_{T^{\dagger}})\right)\,\right|\,X^{1}_{t^{-}}=x_{1}\right]}=:h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1}).

This follows since under the reference measure
\boldsymbol{X}
has independent increment and the severity distributions do not depend on states.

Next, we examine how the dynamics of
\boldsymbol{X}
change when moving from
{\mathbb{P}}
to a stressed measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
. Specifically, we are interested in how the other components of the process,
X^{j}
for
j=2,\ldots,d
are affected by a stress on
X^{1}
. For this, we denote the marginal severity random variables by
\xi_{i}
,
i\in[d]:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and their distributions under the reference measure by
G_{i}(z):={\mathbb{P}}(\xi_{i}\leq z)
. For a stressed distribution
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
we write
G_{i}^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},B):={\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\xi_{i}\in B|\boldsymbol{X}_{t-}=\boldsymbol{x})
for
t\in[0,T]
and
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathds{R}})
.

Proposition 4.9
.

The stressed intensity and the stress joint severity distribution (solution to
4.6
) are, respectively,

\kappa^{*}(t,x_{1})=\kappa\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})\,G_{1}(dy_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad G^{*}(t,x_{1},d\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G(d\boldsymbol{y})}{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})G_{1}(dy_{1}^{\prime})},

where we set
\kappa^{*}(t,x_{1}):=\kappa^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
and
G^{*}(t,x_{1},d\boldsymbol{y}):=G^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},d\boldsymbol{y})
, since the stressed intensity and the stressed severity distribution only depend on the value of the stressed component
x_{1}
.

Proof.

By Girsanov’s theorem, the compensator of
\mu(d\boldsymbol{y},dt)
under the stressed measure is
\nu^{*}(d\boldsymbol{y},dt)=h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})\,\nu(d\boldsymbol{y},dt)
. The result follows analogous to the proof of
Proposition

2.8
.
∎

Notice that under the stressed measure all components
X^{i}
,
i\in[d]
, share the same stressed intensity process
\kappa^{*}(t,x_{1})
given in
Proposition

4.9
and thus the dynamics of all components are distorted. We report the stressed marginal severity distributions
G_{i}^{*}
,
i\in[d]
, in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.10
.

The stressed marginal severity distributions of
\boldsymbol{X}
under
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
(solution to
4.6
) are

G^{*}_{1}(t,x_{1},dy_{1})=\frac{h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G_{1}(dy_{1})}{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})G_{1}(dy_{1}^{\prime})}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{*}_{j}(t,x_{1},dy_{j})=\frac{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G_{1,j}(dy_{1},dy_{j})}{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})G_{1}(dy_{1}^{\prime})}

for
j\in[d]\setminus\{1\}
and where
G_{1,j}(dy_{1},dy_{j})
is the joint severity distribution of the first and the
j^{\text{th}}
components under
{\mathbb{P}}
.

Proof.

The result follows from
Proposition

4.9
by integration.
∎

We note that the stressed marginal severity distribution of the first component of
\boldsymbol{X}
,
G^{*}_{1}
, is equal to the stressed severity distribution in the 1-dimensional case (
Proposition

2.8
). The
j
-th marginal severity distribution
G^{*}_{j}
,
j\neq 1
, however, depends on the copula between
(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})
. If
\xi_{j}
is independent from
\xi_{1}
, then the stressed marginal severity distribution of
\xi_{j}
is equal to its distribution under
{\mathbb{P}}
, i.e.,
G^{*}_{j}(t,x_{1},dy_{j})=G_{j}(dy_{j})
. If
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{j}
are dependent under
{\mathbb{P}}
, a stress on
X^{1}
leads to a stressed marginal severity distributions of
\xi_{j}
which may depend on time
t
and state
x_{1}
. We illustrate in
Example

4.12
how different copulas between
(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})
alter the stressed severity distributions.

Even if the severity distributions are independent under
{\mathbb{P}}
, implying that
G_{j}^{*}=G_{j}
,
j\neq 1
, the paths of the components of
\boldsymbol{X}
under
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
are in general still dependent. This is due to the shared intensity process
\kappa^{*}(t,x_{1})

We consider a bivariate compound Poisson process under

and

be distribution functions and let

(resp.

Under the stressed measure

(solution to

\kappa^{*}(t,x_{1})=\kappa\left[1-p+p\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G_{a}(dy_{1})\right]\,,

. The joint stressed severity distribution becomes

\displaystyle G^{*}(t,x_{1},d\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=p^{*}(t,x_{1})\delta_{0}(y_{2})\,\frac{h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G_{a}(dy_{1})}{\int_{\mathds{R}}h^{*}(t,x,dy_{1}^{\prime})G_{a}(dy_{1}^{\prime})}+[1-p^{*}(t,x_{1})]\delta_{0}(y_{1})\,h^{*}(t,x_{1},y_{1})G_{b}(dy_{2}),

Simulating Under the Stressed Measure

To illustrate how a stress affects the dynamics of a compound Poisson process, we plot in the earlier examples different sample paths of the process, its intensity processes, and the severity distributions under the reference and the stressed probability measure. Key to simulating sample paths under the stressed measure is to (a) find the optimal Lagrange multiplier
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}
, and (b) calculate the Girsanov kernel
h^{*}(t,x,y)
, which then allows to estimate the stressed intensity
\kappa^{*}(t,x)
and the stressed severity distribution
G^{*}(t,x)
using Proposition
2.8

To find the optimal Lagrange multiplier, we solve
Equation

8
by estimating the required expectation using the Fourier space time-stepping (FST) algorithm
(
Jackson et al. 2008
)
. For completeness, we recall the FST methodology in
Section

5.1
. To estimate the Girsanov kernel
h^{*}(t,x,y)
, which is given in
Theorem

2.3
, we again utilize the FST for calculating conditional expectations, see
Section

5.2
.

The code implementing the algorithms is available at
https://github.com/emmakroell/stressing-dynamic-loss-models
.

Fourier space time-stepping algorithm

In this section, we provide an algorithm to simulate paths of

(

For a function

{\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\omega(t,x)+{\mathcal{L}}\omega(t,x)&=0\,,\\
\omega(T,x)&=g(x)\,,\end{aligned}\right.}

(22)

where the linear operator

{\mathbb{P}}

{\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}+\Psi(\zeta))\hat{\omega}(t,\zeta)&=0\,,\\

where

\hat{\omega}(t,\zeta)=\hat{g}(\zeta)e^{\Psi(\zeta)(T-t)}.

(24)

To implement this procedure, we first create a grid in the state space variable

\zeta

6
Conclusion

In a dynamic setting, we consider a risk management framework based on the concept of reverse stress testing where the reference model is a compound Poisson process,
X
, over a finite time horizon
[0,T
]. We solve the optimization problem where we seek the probability measure on the path-space of stochastic processes which has minimal KL-divergence from the reference measure and fulfills constraints which can be written as expected values of functions applied to the process at terminal time. We show that this solution is unique, and refer to it as the stressed measure. We characterize the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the stressed measure, and derive the dynamics of
X
under it. We prove that under the stressed measure,
X
is a generalized version of a compound Poisson process, where both the intensity and the severity distribution depend on time and state. For general constraints, we provide a simulation algorithm which allows to simulate sample paths of
X
under the stressed measure.

Of particular interest are constraints on VaR and CVaR risk measures, for which we analyze in detail the dynamics of
X
under the stressed measures.
In the multivariate setting and for constraints at time points earlier than
T
, we investigate how a stress on one component of the process alters the dependence between the components of the process and otherwise affects the components other than the one stressed. We find that for most dependence structures of the severity distribution, the stress increases the dependence between the portfolio components due to the shared intensity process, as well as changes in the the tail dependence of the process components. Finally, we consider a “what-if” scenario where we examine how severe a stress on a portfolio component needs to be to breach a risk threshold of the aggregate portfolio at a later time point. We find that smaller stresses are needed to the upper tail of a sub-portfolio to breach a VaR risk threshold as compared to stresses on the median or lower tails.
