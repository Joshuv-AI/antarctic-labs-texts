Graph Denoising with Framelet Regularizer
Bingxin Zhou, Ruikun Li, Xuebin Zheng, Yu Guang Wang, and Junbin Gao

Abstract
As graph data collected from the real world is merely noise-free, a practical representation of graphs should be robust to noise. Existing research usually focuses on feature smoothing but leaves the geometric structure untouched. Furthermore, most work takes 𝕃 2 \mathbb{L}_{2} -norm that pursues a global smoothness, which limits the expressivity of graph neural networks. This paper tailors regularizers for graph data in terms of both feature and structure noises, where the objective function is efficiently solved with the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM). The proposed scheme allows to take multiple layers without the concern of over-smoothing, and it guarantees convergence to the optimal solutions. Empirical study proves that our model achieves significantly better performance compared with popular graph convolutions even when the graph is heavily contaminated.



I
Introduction

Data quality is of crucial importance for a reliable modeling. When non-sampling errors become too large to neglect, the validity of the subsequent inference for the estimation result remains of limited confidence. Such concern has been explored throughout for grid data and panel data
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. As a special type of data with geometric structure and feature, graph data suffers from the same concern. For instance, when diagnose COVID-19, panicky suspect patients (nodes) might overstate their symptoms (feature), while some patients choose to undercover their track (edges). In the early stage of the outbreak, there could be a systematic error or unstandardized procedures that falsely classify ’low/medium/high risky regions’, in which case the edge connection becomes less informative.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained a predominant position in graph representation learning due to its promising empirical performance on various applications of machine learning. Recent studies in
[
4
,
5
]
showed that many graph convolution layers, such as
GCN

[
6
]
and
GAT

[
7
]
, smooth the graph signal by intrinsically performing a
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
-based graph Laplacian denoising procedure. While the neighborhood-based local smoothness is desired for graph communities, such methods usually enforce global smoothness, which makes inter-cluster vertices indistinguishable, and the learned graph representation suffers from the well-known issue of
over-smoothing
. Consequently, the model performance drops drastically before it learns the complicated relationship of graph geometry. In order to solve this problem, most researchers either consider spectral methods
[
8
,
9
]
to include high-pass information, or introduce bias
[
10
,
11
]
that balances between the fitness and smoothness of the models.

While the graph feature smoothing has caught increasing attention, literature on structure denoising is far from sufficient. Researchers usually focus on the situation of defending graph adversarial attacks, however, such a scenario assumes a minor manipulation on the graph by hostile attackers, which does not universally exist in any case and does not threats all entities of a graph. On the other hand, a fundamental assumption on any GNNs, especially those popular message-passing networks, is that relevant vertices (or neighbors) share similar properties. When the precise connection is contaminated or indeterminant, the model estimation becomes unreliable.

The lack of exploration on graph smoothing and denoising naturally brings our first research question:
How to measure the smoothness of both feature signal and structure space in a noisy graph?
Instead of over-simplify or even neglect the graph geometry, the design should be tailor-made for a graph that reflects its key properties such as connectivity. The measure should also be aware of pitfalls that exist in training GNNs, such as over-smoothing. In consideration of the practicability, it’s crucial to ask:
How to design a robust mechanism that is adaptive to any graph convolution layers?
In this way, our method can be sliced to other graph convolutions to enhance their performance.

To answer the questions, we explore regularizers on graphs and propose a framelet-based
Do
uble-
T
erm smoother, namely
DoT
, that detects and erases the noises in graph representation. We keep the key properties of graphs by applying the graph-norm on measuring the noise level. The punishment on each node is weighted by node degree, so that highly active nodes (with more neighbors) tolerant a smaller risk to be polluted. For feature noises, we replace the predominant
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
norm with
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}
norm so that the sparsity in the transformed domain is desired while the smoothness is not overly pursued. The geometry noise, on the other hand, is measured by
{\mathbb{L}}_{2,1}
norm where we concentrate errors on merely connected nodes so that they are less likely to provoke un-neglectable estimation errors. We validate our model both theoretically and empirically. We detail the construction of our optimization target as well as its update rules. Its convergence analysis is attached to support its performance. We also compare three ablation models with popular convolution methods and several tasks, where our proposed method outperforms significantly.

Graph Signal Smoothing and Denoising

Common to both categories of graph convolutions is the signal smoothing of graph filters. Spatial methods usually implement polynomial

In contrast, stacking multiple network layers aggregate complicated relationships of a large receptive field, but taking a weighted moving average over too many neighbors could lose sharp changes of frequency response.

Instead, spectral methods, due to their close connection to signal processing, possess detail-preserved global smoothness. Conventional spectral transforms has been widely used for processing non-stationary signals, such as images compression and restoration

Compared to the Fourier transform of the time-frequency domain, wavelets transforms engage between the time and scale domain of signals, which facilitates a multi-scale or multi-resolution view of the input. The signal coefficients exhibit sparse distribution while noise coefficients spread uniformly with a small amplitude. This property allows differentiate signal and noise with threshold-based approaches

Graphs Convolutions and
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}

This section discusses the equivalent expression of graph convolution from the perspective of graph signal denoising. We consider an undirected graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},\mathbb{E},{\bm{X}})
with
N=|{\mathbb{V}}|
nodes. The edge connection is described by an adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and the
d

{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
. Here we neglect the nonlinear activation function
\sigma(\cdot)
for simplicity.

III-A

Spatial Graph Convolution

A typical way of denoising graph signal is via graph smoothing, where the feature smoothness can be forced by a modified
Dirichlet energy function
. While the smoothed representation should be close to the input representation, one leverages a
fidelity term
to guarantee this similarity, and this similarity is usually measured by a convex distance measure, such as the Euclidean distance (
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
norm).

Theorem 1

Consider a graph smoothing problem on the input representation
{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}

\min_{{\bm{U}}}{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{L}}}{\bm{U}})+\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}\|^{2}_{2},

(1)

where the smoothness of graph is measured by the Dirichlet energy that
{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{L}}}{\bm{U}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}{\bm{A}}_{ij}(\frac{{\bm{X}}_{i}}{\sqrt{{\bm{D}}_{ii}}}-\frac{{\bm{X}}_{j}}{\sqrt{{\bm{D}}_{jj}}})^{2}
. Here the
\tilde{{\bm{L}}}:={\bm{I}}-{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{L}}{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}={\bm{I}}-\tilde{{\bm{A}}}
denotes the normalized graph Laplacian with respect to the adjacency matrix
{\bm{A}}
or the degree matrix of the graph
{\bm{D}}
. The first-order approximation to this problem’s optimal solution is
{\bm{U}}:=\tilde{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}
.

Proof 1

To obtain the optimal solution to the objective function, we calculate the partial derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{L}}}{\bm{U}})+\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}\|^{2}_{2}}{\partial{\bm{U}}}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle 2\tilde{{\bm{L}}}{\bm{U}}+2({\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}})=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle({\bm{I}}+\tilde{{\bm{L}}}){\bm{U}}={\bm{X}}{\bm{W}},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}=({\bm{I}}+\tilde{{\bm{L}}})^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}.

The first-order Taylor expansion gives that
({\bm{I}}+\tilde{{\bm{L}}})^{-1}\approx{\bm{I}}-\tilde{{\bm{L}}}
. By Definition,
{\bm{L}}={\bm{D}}-{\bm{A}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{L}}}={\bm{I}}-\tilde{{\bm{A}}}
, which suggests the first-order approximation of the optimal result
{\bm{U}}\approx\tilde{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}
.

This approximation in graph representation learning is known as graph convolutional networks
[
6
]
, which is a popular message-passing scheme that averages one-hop neighborhood representation with normalized adjacency matrix
\tilde{{\bm{A}}}
. The
{\bm{W}}
is a learnable weight matrix that embeds the high-dimensional raw input
{\bm{X}}
to a lower dimension. There are many other aggregation strategies by different spatial-based GNNs under the message-passing
[
48
]
framework, such as
GAT

[
7
]
,
APPNP

[
10
]
and
SGC

[
49
]
. Though, most of them implicitly achieves this optimization objective. Authors of
[
19
]
explored the equivalence of GCN’s propagation process to low-pass filters that smooths the graph signal by brutally filtering out all the detailed information. Other recent research
[
5
,
40
]
identified that many other message passing schemes, although designing different propagation rules, share the same construction logic of the objective function in terms of denoising graph signals under the smoothness and fitness constraints
[
30
]
, which is tricky to balance.

III-B

Spectral Graph Convolution

Compared to spatial counterparts,
Spectral graph convolution
s transform graph signals into frequency domain for further operation, i.e., graph smoothing.

Theorem 2

For a given transform
\bm{\Phi}
, a spectral-based graph convolution smooths the input signal
{\bm{X}}
by optimizing

\min_{{\bm{U}}}{\rm tr}({\bm{C}}^{\top}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{C}})+\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2},

(2)

where
{\bm{C}}:=\bm{\Phi}{\bm{U}}
denotes the transformed coefficients with respect to the clean signal
{\bm{U}}
in frequency domain. The optimal solution to (
2
) is
{\bm{\theta}}\star{\bm{X}}=\bm{\Phi}^{-1}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}{\bm{X}}
.

Proof 2

We first rewrite the equation by replacing
{\bm{C}}
with
\bm{\Phi}{\bm{U}}
, which gives

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}}}{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}^{\top}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}{\bm{U}})+\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2}

Note that the graph Laplacian
{\bm{L}}={\bm{V}}^{\top}\Lambda{\bm{V}}=\bm{\Phi}^{\top}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}
where
\{({\bm{v}}_{i},\lambda_{i}):{\bm{v}}_{i}\in{\bm{V}},\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda\}
are eigenpairs with respect to
{\bm{L}}
. We then have

{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}^{\top}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}{\bm{U}})={\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{U}})+\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2}.

Similar to above, the partial derivatives of this function brings the optimal solution that
{\bm{U}}={\bm{L}}{\bm{X}}=\bm{\Phi}^{\top}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}{\bm{X}}
, which is identical to the convolution function (
3
) below.

The optimal solution to the above smoothing problem is identical to spectral graph convolution. Formally, a layer of spectral graph convolution reads

{\bm{\theta}}\star{\bm{X}}=\bm{\Phi}^{-1}{\rm diag}({\bm{\theta}})\bm{\Phi}{\bm{X}},

(3)

where the basis
\bm{\Phi}
is closely related to the eigenvectors of graph Laplacian and is determined by the type of transform, such as Fourier
[
20
]
, Wavelet
[
50
,
25
]
and Framelet
[
9
]
transforms. Compared to spatial convolutions, this genre allows graph signal processing
[
51
]
on multiple channels, which pursues both local and global smoothness.

As the coefficient are graph signals projected to different channels by low-pass or high-pass filters, it naturally separates the local and global smoothness procedure, which makes the over-smoothing and loss-of-expressivity issue less of a concern. That has been said, the recovered graph signal still to some extend suffers from the global over-smoothing issue from
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
regularizer, as it suppresses regional fluctuations. To solve this issue, we refer to a similar denoising idea adopted in signal processing. Meanwhile, as neither of the above functions pay explicit attention to structure information, we formulate connection-related noise measurements to fill this gap.

IV
The Noise of Graph Signals

This section formulates the regularization schemes related to graph smoothing and subspace extraction to answer the first research question. We start from the conventional analysis model for graph signal denoising and then introduce regularizers that filter the feature and structure noises.

IV-A

Analysis-Based Graph Regularization

We assume the observed graph signal
{\bm{x}}={\bm{u}}+\epsilon
constitutes a graph function
{\bm{u}}:{\mathbb{V}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and Gaussian white noise
\epsilon
. A denoising task recovers the true graph signal
{\bm{u}}
from the observation
{\bm{x}}
. We restrict a sparse representation of graphs in the transformed domain and follow the
analysis based model

[
52
]
to measure the sparsity priors (that is, the coefficient matrix in the transformed domain is assumed sparse). The unconstrained minimization is set to

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{u}}}\|{\mathcal{D}}{\bm{u}}\|_{1}+H({\bm{u}}),

where
{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
is a linear transform generated from discrete transforms, such as discrete Fourier transforms or Framelet transforms. Its sparsity is constrained by the
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}
-norm. The
H(\cdot)
is a smooth convex function that measures the data fidelity, a common choice of which is the empirical risk minimization
H({\bm{u}})=\|{\bm{B}}{\bm{u}}-{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
with some linear transformation
{\bm{B}}
and observations
{\bm{x}}
.

IV-B

Penalty Functions Design

As both node and edge noise are assumed in observed data, we design explicit regularizers individually to reveal the noiseless set of graph signal
{\bm{U}}
and adjacency matrix
{\bm{Z}}
.

IV-B
1
Vertex Feature with Spectral Sparsity

We start from node feature denoising with
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}
wavelet regularization. The sparsity and similarity of the signal approximation is measured by

\min_{{\bm{U}}}\|{\mathcal{D}}{\bm{U}}\|_{1,G}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G},

(4)

For a graph signal
{\bm{u}}
, instead of the Euclidean
{\mathbb{L}}_{p}
-norm, we define a graph
{\mathbb{L}}_{p}
-norm
\|{\bm{u}}\|_{p,G}:=\left(\sum_{i}|{\bm{u}}[i]|^{p}\cdot d[i]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
, where
d[i]
indicates the degree of the
i
th node with respect to
{\bm{u}}
. This work considers
p=1,2
. One can interpret the graph norm as a weighted-sum version of regularizer, where errors associated to high-degree nodes result in heavier penalties.

IV-B
2
Edge Connection with Self-Expressiveness

One fundamental assumption in graph convolution design is that connected nodes are more likely to share common characteristics, and they can be useful to distinguish one group of nodes from another. A similar idea is adopted in sparse subspace clustering, where inner-group information flow is described by self-expressiveness. We thus leverage this idea and assume that every node of a graph can be written as a sparse linear combination of its neighbor nodes. For contaminant data with a noise
{\bm{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, we restrict

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}}}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{Z}}\|_{1}+\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle~~{\bm{U}}={\bm{Z}}{\bm{U}}+{\bm{E}},{\rm diag}({\bm{Z}})=0.

(5)

Here the sum error
\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{D_{i}}|E_{i,j}|^{2}}
is weighted by
D_{i}
, the degree of node
i
. This convex optimization finds an effective sparse representation of node connectivity
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, which can be served as a noiseless graph adjacency matrix. We regularize the error term
{\bm{E}}
by a
{\mathbb{L}}_{2,1,G}
-norm to concentrates errors on features of small-degree nodes.

IV-C

Discrete Framelet Transform

As discussed earlier, a graph signal measures its noise level by the sparsity of the transformed graph coefficients. This work takes fast undecimated graph Framelet transforms
[
42
,
9
]
for multi-channel fast denoising. Formally, the Wavelet Frame, or Framelet, is defined by a filter bank
{\bm{\eta}}:=\{a;b^{(1)},\dots,b^{(K)}\}
(with
K
the number of high-pass filters) and the eigen-pairs
\{(\lambda_{j},{\bm{u}}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
of its graph Laplacian
{\bm{L}}
. The
a
and
b^{(k)}
are called the
low-pass
and
high-pass filters
of the Framelet transforms, which preserves the approximation and detail information of the graph signal, respectively. At
scale level

l=1,\ldots,L
, the low-pass and the
k
th (
k=1,\dots,K
) high pass undecimated Framelet basis at node
p
are defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}_{l,p}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\hat{\alpha}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\ell}}{2^{l}}\right)\overline{{\bm{u}}_{\ell}(p)}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}(v);

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{l,p}^{k}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\widehat{b^{(k)}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\ell}}{2^{l}}\right)\overline{{\bm{u}}_{\ell}(p)}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}(v),

where
\{\alpha;\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(K)}\}
are the associated scaling functions defined by
{\bm{\eta}}
. The corresponding Framelet coefficients for node
p
at scale
l
of a given signal
{\bm{x}}
are the projections
\langle\bm{\varphi}_{l,p},{\bm{x}}\rangle
and
\langle\bm{\psi}_{l,p}^{k},{\bm{x}}\rangle
. Note that we formulate the transforms with Haar-type filters
[
42
]
with dilation factor
2^{l}
to allow efficient transforms.

The computational cost of decomposition (and reconstruction) algorithm of Framelet transforms can be heavy. We thus consider approximating the scaling functions
\alpha
and
\{\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(K)}\}
by Chebyshev polynomials up to
m
-order, respectively, denoted by
{\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{0}
and
\{{\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. For a given level
L
, we define the full set of Framelet coefficients

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{k,1}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{k}(2^{-H}{\bm{L}}){\bm{x}}

at
l=1
. When
l=2,\dots,L
, the coefficients

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle={{\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{k}}(2^{-H-l}{\bm{L}}){{\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{0}}(2^{-H-l+1}{\bm{L}}){\cdots{\mathcal{T}}^{m}_{0}}(2^{-H}{\bm{L}}){\bm{x}},

where the dilation scale
H
satisfies
\lambda_{\max}\leq 2^{H}\pi
. In this definition, the finest scale is
1/2^{H+L}
that guarantees
\lambda_{\ell}/2^{H+L-l}\in(0,\pi)
for
\ell=1,2,...,N.

V
A Layer of Graph Feature and Structure Denoising

This section constructs the graph denoising scheme concerning the second research question. The signal sparsity is measured under the undecimated Framelet transform system that transforms the graph signal to low-pass and high-passes Framelet coefficients with the
m
-degree Chebyshev polynomial of
{\bm{L}}
by a set of
N\times N
orthonormal decomposition operator
\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}
. The sequence length
(KL+1)
is determined by the number of high-pass filters
K
and scale level
L
.

V-A

ADMM Undecimated Framelet Denoising Model

We now present the denoising layer design. Here we interpret the forward propagation of a GNN layer as an optimization iteration. The input
{\bm{X}}
is a noisy representation of the raw signal or its hidden representation. Our target is to recover
{\bm{U}}
and
{\bm{Z}}
, a clean set of graph feature and structure representations that is free from pollution. The objective function and the corresponding update rule is detailed below.

V-A
1
Objective Function Design

The objective function that penalizes both node and edge noises reads

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}},{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}}}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\nu}}\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}{\bm{U}}\|_{1,G}+\|{\bm{Z}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G},

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle~~\operatorname{diag}({\bm{Z}})=0,\;{\bm{U}}={\bm{Z}}{\bm{U}}+{\bm{E}},\;{\bm{Z}}{\bm{1}}={\bm{1}}.

(6)

Here
\|{\bm{\nu}}\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}{\bm{U}}\|_{1,G}:=\sum_{(k,l)\in{\mathbb{B}}}\nu_{k,l}\|{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}\|_{1,G}
promotes the sparsity of the Framelet coefficients of the
k
th high-pass element on the
l
th scale level, where
{\mathbb{B}}:=\{(0,L)\}\bigcup\{(k,l),1\leq k\leq K,1\leq l\leq L\}
. The
{\bm{\nu}}
is a set of tuning parameters with respect to tight decomposition operator
{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}
in high pass. The
{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is the target (noiseless) signal approximation of
N
nodes,
{\bm{L}}
is the associated graph Laplacian, and
{\bm{X}}
is the noisy input representation.

The above function can be rewritten with a variable splitting strategy. Define
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{Z}}-\text{diag}({\bm{Z}})
,
{\bm{Q}}:=\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}{\bm{U}}
, i.e.,
{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}:={\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, gives

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}},{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}}}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{Q}}\|_{1,G}+\|{\bm{Z}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G},

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle~~{\bm{U}}={\bm{Y}}{\bm{U}}+{\bm{E}},\;{\bm{Y}}{\bm{1}}={\bm{1}},\;{\bm{Y}}={\bm{Z}}-\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}).

(7)

The associated augmented Lagrangian
[
53
]
reads

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{U}},{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}},{\bm{Q}},{\bm{Y}};\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3},\Lambda_{4},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}\nu_{k,l}\|{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}\|_{1,G}+\|{\bm{Z}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{Y}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{k,l}\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu_{3}}{2}\|{\bm{Y}}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{4}}{2}\|{\bm{Y}}-{\bm{Z}}+\text{diag}({\bm{Z}})\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle+\text{tr}\left(\Lambda_{1}^{\top}({\bm{U}}-{\bm{Y}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}})\right)+\sum_{k,l}\text{tr}\left(\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}_{k,l}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}})\right)

\displaystyle+\Lambda_{3}^{\top}({\bm{Y}}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}})+\text{tr}(\Lambda^{\top}_{4}({\bm{Y}}-{\bm{Z}}+\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}))).

(8)

We call
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2;k,l}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d};\Lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{N};\Lambda_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
the Lagrangian multipliers. The
\Lambda_{i}
(
i=1,2,3,4
) can be considered as the running sum of errors associated with its constraint, and the target is to choose the optimal
\Lambda_{i}
that minimize the residual of the
i
th constraint. The augmented Lagrangian penalty parameters
\mu_{1},\mu_{2;k,l},\mu_{3},\mu_{4}
are positive by definition. They stand for upper bounds over all the adaptive penalty parameters. In practice we value an identical
\mu_{2}
to
\mu_{2;k,l}
for faster matrix inversion in updating
{\bm{U}}
. We will omit the subscripts for the rest of paper.

Remark 1

Conventional splitting methods select
\mu_{i}
s in advance. This paper leverages adaptive tuning methods that only require a proper initialization on
\mu^{(0)}_{i}
s.

Remark 2

For simplicity, we can define the low-pass
{\bm{\nu}}_{0,L}=0
and high-passse
{\bm{\nu}}_{k,l}=4^{-l-1}\nu_{0}
by initializing
\nu_{0}
to avoid the overwhelming fine-tuning work in
{\bm{\nu}}
.

Remark 3

We keep the
\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}^{(t)})
term here for theoretical completeness. For implementation we remove the term
\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}^{(t)})
as it equals zero.

Remark 4

With
{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}:={\bm{I}}-{\bm{Y}}
, we can rewrite
{\bm{U}}-{\bm{Y}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}}={\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}}
, and
{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}
denotes the normalized Laplacian of noiseless adjacency matrix.

2
ADMM Update Scheme

The objective function is optimized with the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
43
]
. There involves six (sets of) parameters to update. This work updates the primal variables
{\bm{U}},{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}},{\bm{Y}},{\bm{Q}}
iteratively, but it is possible to design a parallel update scheme, see
[
54
]
. At the
t+1
th iteration, we update iteratively

1.

{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)}+\mu_{2}^{(t)}{\bm{I}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\left(\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\bm{E}}^{(t)}+\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}-{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}\Lambda_{1}^{(t)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{k,l}(\mu_{2}^{(t)}{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}_{k,l}+{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}^{\top}\Lambda_{2;k,l})\right)

(9)

where the noiseless graph Laplacian
{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)}
is updated with
{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}
. The matrix inversion reaches a precise solution by the linear solver
[
55
]
or the Cholesky Factorization
[
56
]
.

2.

{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}={\bm{R}}-\text{diag}({\bm{R}})

where

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}:=\mathcal{T}_{1/\mu^{(t)}_{4}}\left({\bm{Y}}^{(t)}+{\mu^{(t)}_{4}}^{-1}\Lambda^{(t)}_{4}\right).

(10)

The
\mathcal{T}_{\eta}(x)=\text{sign}(x)\max\{|x|-\eta,0\}=\text{ReLU}(x-\eta)-\text{ReLU}(-x-\eta)

is a
soft-threshold operator
.

3.

{\bm{E}}_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{1/\mu_{1}^{(t)}}^{i}\left({\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+{\Lambda_{1}^{(t)}}/{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right).

(11)

The above update rule works on the
i
th row of
{\bm{E}}
. The
\mathcal{T}_{\eta}^{i}(x)=({x_{i}}/{\|x_{i}\|_{2}})\max\{\|x_{i}\|_{2}-\eta,0\}
denotes a row-wise soft thresholding for group
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
-regularization.

4.

{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu^{(t)}_{1}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}){\bm{U}}^{(t+1)\top}+\mu^{(t)}_{3}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}+\mu^{(t)}_{4}{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)\top}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}{\bm{1}}^{\top}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{4}\right)\left({\mu^{(t)}_{4}}^{-1}{\bm{I}}\right.

(12)

\displaystyle\left.-{\mu^{(t+1)}_{4}}^{-1}\widetilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(t+1)}\left[\mu^{(t+1)}_{4}{\bm{I}}+\widetilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(t+1)\top}\widetilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(t+1)}\right]^{-1}\widetilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(t+1)\top}\right).

The
\widetilde{{\bm{U}}}^{(t+1)}=[\sqrt{\mu^{(t+1)}_{1}}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)},\sqrt{\mu^{(t)}_{3}}{\bm{1}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(d+1)}
. The
{\bm{1}}
is a
N
dimensional one vector. We assume
N\gg d
, i.e., the graph size is much larger than the node feature dimension.

5.

{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}[i,:]

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt=\mathcal{T}_{\nu_{k,l}d_{i}/\mu_{2}^{(t)}}\left({\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}[i,:]-{\mu^{(t)}_{2}}^{-1}\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)}[i,:]\right).

(13)

The above update rule acts row-wisely where
{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}[i,:]
denotes the
i
th row of
{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}
.

6.

Update
\Lambda^{(t+1)}
s and
\mu^{(t+1)}
s:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{1}^{(t)}+\mu_{1}^{(t)}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)});

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)}+\mu_{2}^{(t)}({\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)});

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{3}^{(t)}+\mu_{3}^{(t)}({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}});

\displaystyle\Lambda_{4}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{4}^{(t)}+\mu_{4}^{(t)}\left({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}+\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)})\right);

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\min\left(\rho\mu_{i}^{(t)},\mu_{i,\max}\right),\quad i=1,2,3,4.

(14)

V-B

Convergence of the main algorithm

The original optimization problem (
V-A1
), in our proposed scheme, is considered as a component of neural network. In the last section, we leverage the ADMM scheme to solve the augmented Lagrangian problem (
V-A1
) where the optimal
{\bm{Z}}
and
{\bm{U}}
are both outputs of network layers. The
t
th iteration of the ADMM update scheme, in particular, acts as the
t
th layer of neural network that updates from
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t-1)},{\bm{Z}}^{(t-1)}\}
to
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)},{\bm{Z}}^{(t)}\}
. This section provides the theorem that guarantees the convergence of ADMM algorithm for (
V-A1
).

Theorem 3

Let
\{\Gamma_{t}=({\bm{U}}^{(t)},{\bm{Z}}^{(t)},{\bm{E}}^{(t)},{\bm{Q}}^{(t)},{\bm{Y}}^{(t)},\Lambda^{(t)}_{1},\Lambda^{(t)}_{2},

\Lambda^{(t)}_{3},\Lambda^{(t)}_{4})\}^{\infty}_{t=1}
be the sequence generated by the ADMM scheme (
1
)-(
14
) presented in subsection
V-A2
. Under the assumption that
{\bm{U}}^{(t)}
is bounded, the sequence
\{\Gamma_{t}\}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

The generated sequence
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
is bounded.

2.

The sequence
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
has at least one accumulation point
\Gamma_{*}=({\bm{U}}_{*},{\bm{Z}}_{*},{\bm{E}}_{*},{\bm{Q}}_{*},{\bm{Y}}_{*},\Lambda_{1*},\Lambda_{2*},\Lambda_{3*},\Lambda_{4*})
that satisfies first-order optimality KKT conditions:

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}_{*}={\bm{Y}}_{*}{\bm{U}}_{*}+{\bm{E}}_{*};

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{*}=\mathcal{W}_{*}{\bm{U}}_{*};

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}_{*}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1};

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}_{*}={\bm{Z}}_{*}-\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}_{*});

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1*}\in\lambda_{1}\partial_{{\bm{E}}}\|{\bm{E}}_{*}\|_{2,1,G};

\displaystyle-\Lambda_{2*;k,l}\in\nu_{k,l}\partial_{Q_{k,l}}\|{\bm{Q}}_{*;k,l}\|_{1,G};

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1*}{\bm{U}}^{T}_{*}+\Lambda_{3*}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\Lambda_{4*}=0;

\displaystyle\Lambda_{4*}\in\partial_{{\bm{Z}}}\|{\bm{Z}}_{*}\|_{1}.

3.

\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\},\{{\bm{Z}}^{(t)}\},\{{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\},\{{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}\}
and
\{{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\}
each is Cauchy sequence, and thus converges to its critical point.

We detail the full proof in Appendix
B
for the sake of consciousness. In practice, the ADMM typically defines an inner iteration number, such as
10
, in advance. This pre-defined value provides a viable fast convergence for ADMM.

V-C

Ablation Denoising Models

In addition to the proposed all-in-one
DoT
denoising optimization, we separate the objective into several sub-tasks and investigate the effectiveness of these individual designs. We also compare with the TV regularizer that is conventionally used for signal denoising as well as some graph node denoising tasks
[
42
,
41
]
. The corresponding objective function and the update rules for the three ablation models are detailed below.

V-C
1
Node Feature Denoising

We define node feature denoising model similar to (
4
), and reformulated the problem with

{\bm{Q}}=\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}{\bm{U}}
:

\min_{{\bm{U}}}\|{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{Q}}\|_{1,G}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\bm{Q}}=\bm{{\mathcal{W}}}{\bm{U}}.

(15)

The function design is similar to that of
[
42
]
, except that we optimize the objective with ADMM instead of the Split-Bregman
[
44
]
method. The associated augmented Lagrangian problem reads

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}}}\sum_{k,l}\nu_{k,l}\|{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}\|_{1,G}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G}

(16)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,l}\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{k,l}\text{tr}\left(\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}_{k,l}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}})\right).

The above objective function can solved with the following ADMM scheme:

1.

{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}
:

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{D}}+\mu_{2}^{(t)}{\bm{I}}\right)^{-1}\left({\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}+\sum_{k,l}{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}^{\top}(\mu_{2}^{(t)}{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}_{k,l}+\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)})\right);

2.

{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}[i,:]
:

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\nu_{k,l}d_{i}/\mu_{2}^{(t)}}\left({\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}[i,:]-\frac{1}{\mu^{(t)}_{2}}\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)}[i,:]\right);

3.

Update
\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t+1)}
and
\mu_{2}^{(t+1)}
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)}+\mu_{2}^{(t)}({\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)});

\displaystyle\mu_{2}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle\min\left(\rho\mu_{2}^{(t)},\mu_{2,\max}\right).

In step one of updating
{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}
, as the matrix
({\bm{D}}+\mu_{2}^{(t)}{\bm{I}})
is diagonal, its inverse can be calculated swiftly without any approximation operation. Also, the update rule in step two is identical to that of (
13
), and the same batch operation can be applied here.

V-C
2
Edge Connection Denoising

We next consider a hybrid
{\mathbb{L}}_{2,1}
regularization in the case of noisy edge connections, and it is again a subset of the full objective function (
V-A1
):

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}},{\bm{Z}},{\bm{E}}}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{Z}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\|{\bm{E}}\|_{2,1,G}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G},

(17)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle~~\text{diag}({\bm{Z}})=0,\;{\bm{U}}={\bm{Z}}{\bm{U}}+{\bm{E}},\;{\bm{Z}}{\bm{1}}={\bm{1}}.

With
{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}={\bm{I}}-{\bm{Y}}
, its augmented Lagrangian reads

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{U}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{X}}\|^{2}_{2,G}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\Bigl\|{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\text{tr}\left(\Lambda_{1}^{\top}({\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}{\bm{U}}-{\bm{E}})\right).

(18)

By
{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}/{\partial{\bm{U}}}=0
, the update rule for
{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}
reads

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}+\mu_{1}^{(t)}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{F}}+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\bm{E}}^{(t)}-\Lambda_{1}^{(t)}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}{\bm{D}}^{-1}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}_{2}}\mu_{1}^{(t)}{\bm{D}}^{-1}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)}{\bm{D}}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{F}}+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}{\bm{E}}^{(t)}-{\widetilde{\bm{L}_{0}}}^{(t)\top}\Lambda_{1}^{(t)}\right).

Similar to before, the matrix inverse can be approximated by a Linear or Cholesky solver for computational simplicity.

V-C
3
TV Regularizer for Node Feature

Aside from acting the
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}
-norm on Framelet coefficients in (
15
), the regularizer can be applied directly on the noisy raw signal with the TV regularizer for node denoising. The TV regularizer is conventionally used for signal denoising as well as some graph node denoising tasks
[
42
,
41
]
. Formally, the objective function is defined as

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{U}}}\frac{\alpha}{2}{\rm tr}({\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}{\bm{U}})+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{U}}-{\bm{F}}\|^{2}_{2,G}.

(19)

where
\alpha
is a tunable hyper-parameter, and
{\bm{L}}
denotes the (unnormalized) graph Laplacian.

The update rule of
{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}
of the above function is rather straight-forward. By
{\partial{\mathbb{L}}}/{\partial{\bm{U}}}=0
, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}=({\bm{D}}+\alpha{\bm{L}})^{-1}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}\approx({\bm{I}}-\alpha{\bm{D}}^{-1}{\bm{L}}){\bm{X}}.

VII
Conclusion

This paper introduces a robust optimization scheme for denoising graph features and structure. The graph noise level is measured by a double-term regularizer, which pushes Framelet coefficients of the node representation to be sparse and the adjacency matrix to concentrate its uncertainty on small communities with limited influence. The optimal graph representation is reached by a sequence of feed-forward propagation, where each layer functions as an optimization iteration. The update converges to the exact solution of robust graph representation, which removes irregular graph volatility but keeps expressivity. We provide comprehensive theoretical support and extensive empirical validation to prove the effectiveness of our denoising method in terms of node and/or edge contamination.

We shall emphasize that in experiments we fix the ADMM iteration up to
10
to save the computational cost, which is lower than the typical number of
30
to
40
. In other words, our model preserves the potential to achieve higher scores.

\displaystyle+\sum^{T}_{t=1}\sum_{k,l}\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{2}+\mu^{(t-1)}_{2}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{2}}\|\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)}-\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t-1)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{3}+\mu^{2(t-1)}_{3}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{3}}\|\Lambda_{3}^{(t)}-\Lambda_{3}^{(t-1)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{4}+\mu^{2(t-1)}_{4}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{4}}\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{4}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{4}\|^{2}_{F}.

Note that, for
i=1,2,3,4

\displaystyle\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{i}+\mu^{(t-1)}_{i}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{i}}=\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{\rho\mu^{(t-1)}_{i}+\mu^{(t-1)}_{i}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho+1}{2}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{1}{\mu^{(t-1)}_{i}}=\frac{\rho+1}{2\mu^{(0)}_{i}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{1}{\rho^{t-1}}\leq\frac{\rho(\rho+1)}{2\mu^{(0)}_{i}(\rho-1)}.

Note that
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{U}}^{(1)},{\bm{Z}}^{(1)},{\bm{E}}^{(1)},{\bm{Q}}^{(1)},{\bm{Y}}^{(1)};\Lambda^{(0)}_{1},\Lambda^{(0)}_{2},\Lambda^{(0)}_{3},

\Lambda^{(0)}_{4},\mu^{(0)}_{1},\mu^{(0)}_{2},\mu^{(0)}_{3},\mu^{(0)}_{4})
is finite, and squences
\{\Lambda^{(t)}_{1},\Lambda^{(t)}_{2},\Lambda^{(t)}_{3},\Lambda^{(t)}_{4}\}
and
\sum^{T}_{t=1}\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{i}+\mu^{(t-1)}_{i}}{2\mu^{2(t-1)}_{i}}
, so the objective is bounded. Now Therefore, we can conclude that all the relevant sequences
\{\Gamma_{t}\}
are unbounded given the finite objective function values. This completes the proof of claim 1) of Theorem 1.

Step 2: The proof of claim 2)

According to Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, it follows from the boundedness of the sequence
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
that
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
must have a convergent subsequence. Without loss of generality, suppose that the subsequence of
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
is represented by itself, and it converges to an accumulation point, denoted as
\Gamma_{*}
. That is, we have established

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}({\bm{U}}^{(t)},{\bm{Z}}^{(t)},{\bm{E}}^{(t)},{\bm{Q}}^{(t)},{\bm{Y}}^{(t)},\Lambda^{(t)}_{1},\Lambda^{(t)}_{2},\Lambda^{(t)}_{3},\Lambda^{(t)}_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bm{U}}_{*},{\bm{Z}}_{*},{\bm{E}}_{*},{\bm{Q}}_{*},{\bm{Y}}_{*},\Lambda_{1*},\Lambda_{2*},\Lambda_{3*},\Lambda_{4*}).

(25)

The updating rules of Lagrange multipliers (
17
) imply that

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}=(\Lambda_{1}^{(t+1)}-\Lambda_{1}^{(t)})/\mu_{1}^{(t)};

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{k,l}^{(t+1)}-{\mathcal{W}}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}=(\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t+1)}-\Lambda_{2;k,l}^{(t)})/\mu_{2}^{(t)};

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}}=(\Lambda_{3}^{(t+1)}-\Lambda_{3}^{(t)})/\mu_{3}^{(t)};

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}+\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)})=(\Lambda_{4}^{(t+1)}-\Lambda_{4}^{(t)})/\mu_{4}^{(t)};

By the boundedness of the sequences
\{\Lambda^{(t)}_{1},\Lambda^{(t)}_{2},\Lambda^{(t)}_{3},\Lambda^{(t)}_{4}\}
, and the fact
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu^{(t)}_{i}=\infty
, we further have, by taking limit,

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}_{*}

\displaystyle={\bm{Y}}_{*}{\bm{U}}_{*}+{\bm{E}}_{*};\;\mathcal{Q}_{*}=\mathcal{W}_{*}{\bm{U}}_{*}

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}_{*}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1};\;{\bm{Y}}_{*}={\bm{Z}}_{*}-\text{diag}({\bm{Z}}_{*})

The other four KKT conditions can be obtained by taking limits over (
21
) - (
24
). The proof of claim 2) is completed.

Step 3: The proof of claim 3)

We conduct the proof in the following sub-steps.

(1)
\{{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\}
is a Cauchy:

According to the updating rules (
14
), we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}^{(t+1)}=\Lambda_{3}^{(t)}+\mu_{3}^{(t)}({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}})

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}^{(t)}=\Lambda_{3}^{(t-1)}+\mu_{3}^{(t-1)}({\bm{Y}}^{(t)}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{1}})

This gives

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{1}}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}{\bm{1}}=\frac{\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}}{\mu^{(t)}_{3}}+\frac{\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{3}}{\mu^{(t-1)}_{3}}

Hence

\displaystyle\|{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\|({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}){\bm{1}}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}_{3}}\|\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t-1)}_{3}}\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}_{3}}(\|\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F}+\rho^{2}\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}_{3}}C_{t}

where
C_{t}
is bounded as the sequence
\{\Gamma_{t}\}
is bounded. Then for any
m>n
, we can establish

\displaystyle\|{\bm{Y}}^{(m)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(n)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{m-1}_{t=n}\|{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum^{m-1}_{t=n}\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}_{1}}=C\sum^{m-1}_{t=n}\frac{1}{\mu^{(0)}_{i}\rho^{2t}}

where
\rho>1
. This completes the proof of Cauchy property of the sequence
\{{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\}
.

(2)
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\}
is a Cauchy:

The first order condition for the optimal
{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}
reads as, denoting by
{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}={\bm{I}}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}
,

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{X}})+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{E}}^{(t)})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,l}\mu^{(t)}_{2}\mathcal{W}_{k,l}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}^{(t)}_{k,l}-\mathcal{W}_{k,l}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)})

\displaystyle+{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}+\sum_{k,l}\mathcal{W}_{k,l}\Lambda^{(t)}_{2;k,l}

Similarly by using the updating rules (
14
) and replacing
{\bm{E}}^{(t)}
and
{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}_{k,l}
, we will have

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}{\bm{D}}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{X}})+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{U}}^{(t)})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,l}\mu^{(t)}_{2}\mathcal{W}^{T}_{k,l}\mathcal{W}_{k,l}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{U}}^{(t)})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}(\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{1})-\rho\sum_{k,l}\mathcal{W}^{\top}_{k,l}(\Lambda^{(t)}_{2}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{2})

\displaystyle+\mu^{(t)}_{1}{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}){\bm{U}}^{(t)}+{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)T}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,l}\mathcal{W}^{\top}_{k,l}\Lambda^{(t)}_{2;k,l}

Assuming
\mu^{(t)}_{1}=\mu^{(t)}_{2}=\mu^{(t)}
will give

\displaystyle\|{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}}\|({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}+\sum_{k,l}\mathcal{W}^{\top}_{k,l}\mathcal{W}_{k,l})^{-1}\|^{2}_{F}\cdot

\displaystyle\biggl\{\lambda_{2}\|{\bm{D}}({\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{X}})\|^{2}_{F}+\rho^{2}\|{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}(\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{1})\|^{2}_{F}\biggr.

\displaystyle+\rho^{2}\sum_{k,l}\|\mathcal{W}^{\top}_{k,l}(\Lambda^{(t)}_{2}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{2})\|^{2}_{F}+\|{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\mu^{(t)}_{1}\|{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}\|^{2}_{F}\|({\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}){\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\biggl.+\sum_{k,l}\|\mathcal{W}^{\top}_{k,l}\Lambda^{(t)}_{2;k,l}\|^{2}_{F}\biggr\}.

We consider the second last term where we have seen the term
\mu^{(t)}_{1}
. First we note that
\|{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\|({\bm{I}}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}){\bm{1}}\|_{F}
while
({\bm{I}}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}){\bm{1}}=(\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3})/\mu^{(t)}_{3}
. Hence

\mu^{(t)}_{1}\|{\bm{L}}^{(t+1)\top}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{1}}{\mu^{(t)}_{3}}\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F}=\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{3}-\Lambda^{(t+1)}_{3}\|^{2}_{F}

if we take
\mu^{(t)}_{1}=\mu^{(t)}_{3}
. This means there exists a constant
C
such that

\|{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}}C.

Similar argument as for Cauchy property for
\{{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\}
, we can claim
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\}
is Cauchy.

(3)
\{{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\},\{{\bm{Z}}^{(t)}\},\{{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}_{k,l}\}
all are Cauchy:

As these variables share similar pattern with L1-norm constraint, to save the space, we only take
\{{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\}
as an example to show its Cauchy property. We will follow the updating rules (
14
) again.

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}/{\mu^{(t)}_{1}})

\displaystyle+({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}/{\mu^{(t)}_{1}})-{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}/{\mu^{(t)}_{1}})

\displaystyle+({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}/{\mu^{(t)}_{1}})

\displaystyle+(\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}-\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{1})/{\mu^{(t-1)}}-{\bm{L}}^{(t)}{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\mu^{(t)}_{1}}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1})-{\bm{U}}^{(t)}+{\bm{L}}^{(t)}{\bm{U}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\mu^{(t)}_{1}}(\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}-\rho(\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{1}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}))\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\mu^{(t)}_{1}}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1})\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\|{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}_{F}\|{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\|{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}\|{\bm{Y}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mu^{2(t)}}\|\Lambda^{(t)}_{1}-\rho(\Lambda^{(t-1)}_{1}-\Lambda^{(t)}_{1})\|^{2}_{F}.

Now we prove the first term is second order controlled.
{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}
is obtained by
\ell_{1}
-threshold operator, so each row element of the first term is less than
1/\mu^{(t)}_{1}
, hence

\|{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}-({\bm{L}}^{(t+1)}{\bm{U}}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\mu^{(t)}_{1}}\Lambda^{(t)}_{1})\|^{2}_{F}\leq\frac{N}{\mu^{2(t)}_{1}},

where
N
is the number of rows of
{\bm{E}}^{(t+1)}
. We have already proved that
\{{\bm{Y}}^{(t)}\}
and
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)}\}
. With the boundedness of those variable we can claim that
\{{\bm{E}}^{(t)}\}
is Cauchy.

Final conclusion: Given the Cauchy property, we can claim that the sequence
\{{\bm{U}}^{(t)},{\bm{Z}}^{(t)},{\bm{E}}^{(t)},{\bm{Y}}^{(t)},{\bm{Q}}^{(t)}\}
are convergent to its critical point.

This completes the proof of Theorem 3.
