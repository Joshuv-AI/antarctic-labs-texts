On the inconsistency of separable losses for structured prediction
Caio Corro

Abstract
In this paper, we prove that separable negative log-likelihood losses for structured prediction are not necessarily Bayes consistent, or, in other words, minimizing these losses may not result in a model that predicts the most probable structure in the data distribution for a given input. This fact opens the question of whether these losses are well-adapted for structured prediction and, if so, why.



1
Introduction

Modern natural language processing (NLP) heavily relies on machine learning (ML), where prediction models are learned by minimizing a loss function over the training data.
As such, loss functions play a central role in the design of these systems and it is important to understand their statistical properties in order to guarantee that the corresponding training objectives are well defined.
Although this topic is well studied in the ML community
Lugosi and Vayatis 2004
;
Lin 2004
;
Zhang 2004a
;
Zhang 2004b
;
Bartlett et al. 2006
;
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
;
Liu 2007
;
Tewari and Bartlett 2007
;
Reid and Williamson 2010
;
Williamson et al. 2016
;
Duchi et al. 2018
;
Blondel et al. 2020
;
Nowak et al. 2022
,
inter alia
, there has been less focus on the structured prediction setting apart from a few recent works
Blondel 2019
;
Nowak et al. 2019
;
Nowak et al. 2020
.

In this paper, we emphasize the fact that, despite achievements in terms of accuracy, statistical behavior of loss functions used in practice for structured prediction in NLP are not always well understood.
We illustrate this fact by proving that commonly used separable loss functions for named entity recognition (NER) and dependency parsing are not Bayes consistent, meaning that training a model with these loss functions will not necessarily result in the prediction of the most the probable output for a given input in the data distribution.

2
Bayes consistency

We denote inputs and outputs as
{\bm{x}}\in X
and
{\bm{y}}\in Y
, respectively.
We assume each
{\bm{y}}\in Y
is a binary vector whose elements are indexed by a set
C
,
i.e.

{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
, where
C
is problem dependent.
For example, in the
k
multiclass classification case, we have
C\ =[k]
, where we use the shorthand
[k]=\{1,2,...,k\}
, and
Y
is defined as the set of standard bases (one-hot vectors) of dimension
k
, meaning that
|Y|=k
.
More generally, the vector
{\bm{y}}
is an indicator of “selected” parts in
C
and, in the structured prediction case, several parts can be jointly selected.
Note that it is usual to assume that the parts in
C
can depend on the input
{\bm{x}}
.
Without loss of generality, we omit this detail as we will study loss functions in the pointwise setting.

A scoring model
f\in F
is a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
that returns scores associated with each part in
C
for a given input,
e.g.
the score of each class in a multiclass classification model.
The actual prediction of the model is the output of maximum linear score:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{y}}({\bm{x}})\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in Y}~\langle{\bm{y}},f({\bm{x}})\rangle,

(1)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product.
We refer to computing Equation
1
as maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) inference.

A loss function compares a vector of scores with an expected output.
Importantly, the 0-1 loss function is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{w}},{\bm{y}})=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }{\bm{y}}\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle,\\
1\quad&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a vector of part scores,
i.e.

{\bm{w}}=f({\bm{x}})
for a given input
{\bm{x}}
.
In order to choose a scoring function
f\in F
, it is appealing to select one that minimizes this loss over the data distribution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{r}^{*}

\displaystyle=\inf_{f\in F}\mathcal{r}(f)=\inf_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}[\ell(f({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{y}})],

where
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are random variables over inputs and outputs, respectively, and
\inf
denotes the infimum.
The value
\mathcal{r}(f)
is the Bayes risk of function
f
and
\mathcal{r}^{*}
is the optimal Bayes risk.
For theoretical purposes, it is often assumed that the class of functions
F
is rich enough (the set of all measurable mappings) to obtain the best possible risk.
Then, the optimal Bayes risk is equal to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{r}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[1-\max_{{\bm{y}}\in Y}p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})],

or, in other words, it is the probability of making an error when the classifier predicts the most probable class for each input.

Unfortunately, in practice it is not convenient to use the 0-1 loss
\ell
as it is nonconvex and has null derivatives almost everywhere.
Instead, a surrogate
\widetilde{\ell}
can be used as a loss function:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\inf_{f\in F}~\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}(f)=\inf_{f\in F}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}[\widetilde{\ell}(f({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{y}})],

where
\widetilde{r}(f)
is the surrogate risk of function
f
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}^{*}
is the optimal surrogate risk.
An important desired property of surrogate losses is their consistency with the 0-1 loss,
i.e.
the fact that minimizing the surrrogate risk leads to a prediction model of optimal Bayes risk
Lugosi and Vayatis 2004
;
Lin 2004
;
Zhang 2004a
;
Bartlett et al. 2006
;
Liu 2007
;
Tewari and Bartlett 2007
.

Definition 1
.

A surrogate loss
\widetilde{\ell}
is said to be Bayes consistent
1
1

1

This property is also referred to as Fisher consistency
Lin 2004
;
Bartlett et al. 2006
;
Liu 2007
and classification calibration
Williamson et al. 2016
.
if:

\displaystyle f^{*}\in\argmin_{f\in F}~\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}(f)\implies\mathcal{r}(f^{*})=\mathcal{r}^{*}.

Note that this property can be checked independently for each input
{\bm{x}}
(called pointwise Bayes consistency) as we assume a rich enough class of functions
F
.
In other words, we redefine the pointwise (optimal) surrogate risk as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}}~\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}({\bm{w}})=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[\widetilde{\ell}({\bm{w}},{\mathbf{y}})],

for any
{\bm{x}}
such that
p({\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})>0
, and similarly for the optimal Bayes risk.
The vector
{\bm{w}}
should be interpreted as the model scores,
i.e.

{\bm{w}}=f({\bm{x}})
.

3
Negative log-likelihood loss

The negative log-likelihood loss (NLL), also known as the conditional random field loss
Lafferty et al. 2001
, is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{(nll)}({\bm{w}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=-\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle.

In the following, we will refer to computing the log-sum-exp term of the NLL loss as marginal inference due to its connection with marginal probabilities
Wainwright and Jordan 2008
.

Theorem 1
.

Under mild conditions on the data distribution, the surrogate loss
\widetilde{\ell}_{(nll)}
is Bayes consistent.

Proof.

The optimal pointwise surrogate Bayes risk is defined as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}^{*}_{(nll)}

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}}\begin{array}[t]{l}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[\langle{\bm{w}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle]\\
+\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle.\end{array}

We substitute
{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})=\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle
for all
{\bm{y}}\in Y
:

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forall{\bm{y}}\in Y:\\
{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\begin{array}[t]{l}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[{\bm{w}}({\mathbf{y}})]\\
+\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}).\end{array}

We denote
\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}),\forall{\bm{y}}\in Y
, an optimal solution of the minimization.
By first order optimality conditions, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})}\left(\begin{array}[l]{l}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[\widehat{\bm{w}}({\mathbf{y}})]\\
+\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp(\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}))\end{array}\right)=0

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\exp\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}=p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})

(4)

which implies Bayes consistency under the condition that there exists a vector
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
such that
\forall{\bm{y}}\in Y:\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle=\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})
.
∎

To understand why Equation
4
implies Bayes consistency, note that:

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}\propto\exp\widehat{\bm{w}}({\bm{y}}^{\prime}),

and the exponential function is strictly increasing.
This means scores of outputs
{\bm{y}}\in Y
defined as
\langle{\bm{y}},\widehat{\bm{w}}\rangle
are ordered in the same way as probabilities in the data distribution
p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
.
In other words, the most probable output in the data distribution will have the highest score with respect to
\widehat{\bm{w}}
.

The proof is a straightforward extension of the derivation for the multiclass classification case, see for example
Blondel et al. 2020
.
Interestingly, Equation
4
also implies that the NLL loss is strictly proper
Williamson et al. 2016
,
i.e.
the Boltzmann distribution over structures in
Y
parameterized by minimizer
\widehat{\bm{w}}
is equal to the data distribution
p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
.
Note that Theorem
1
is not novel
per se
and a more in-depth study of NLL losses for structured prediction can be found in
Nowak et al. 2019
.

One limitation of the NLL loss is that it is not (additively) separable
2
2

2

A function
f
is additively separable if it can be written as
f=\sum_{i}f_{i}.
because of the log-sum-exp term.
As such, this term is a bottleneck for parallel computation of the objective and doubly stochastic estimation of the training objective
Titsias 2016
.
A well-known solution is to rely on independent binary classification objectives, also known as one-vs-all losses
(
Blondel et al. 2020
, Section 6.1)
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{(one-vs-all)}({\bm{w}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188pt=-\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\log\left(1+\exp\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle\right).

In the case of multiclass classification problems, it can be shown that this objective is Bayes consistent using similar arguments as in Theorem
1
.
A different approach is the one-vs-each loss function that is also Bayes consistent
Titsias 2016
.

Unfortunately, these separable surrogates cannot be applied to structured prediction problems as the set
Y
is often of exponential size with respect to the input length.
Although tractable algorithms for marginal inference exist for many cases, there are no known algorithms to compute the one-vs-each or one-vs-all losses in an easily parallelizable fashion.
As such, the NLP community often relies on token-separable losses, that is a NLL objective that decomposes as a sum of independent losses, one per token in the input sentence.
Although these losses are easy to implement, we prove in the next sections that they are not Bayes consistent for two common NLP problems.

Named-entity recognition

Problem definition.

In this Section, we focus on the flat NER problem using BIO tags
Ratinov and Roth 2009
.
Without loss of generality, we assume there is a single mention label and that the input sentence
{\bm{x}}
contains
n
words.
The set of parts is defined as
C=[n]\times\{\textsc{B},\textsc{I},\textsc{O}\}
and
Y
is defined as the set of vectors
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

\forall i\in[n]:\sum_{t}{y}_{i,t}=1
(one tag per word);

2.

{y}_{1,\textsc{I}}=0
(forbid inside tag for the first word);

3.

\forall i>1:{y}_{i,\textsc{I}}=1\implies{y}_{i-1,\textsc{B}}+{y}_{i-1,\textsc{I}}=1
(
I
tag can only follow a
B
or
I
tag).

We do not include parts corresponding to transitions (this is a unigram model), otherwise it would not be possible to derive a token-separable loss.

Inference algorithms.

MAP and marginal inference can be realized using the Viterbi and the forward-backward algorithms, respectively.
Although the time complexity of these algorithms is
\mathcal{O}(|L|^{2}n)
where
L
is the set of mention labels, they can be optimized to have a
\mathcal{O}(|L|n)
time complexity as there is no transition score.
The dynamic programming algorithm is nonetheless required in order to guarantee that condition (3) is satisfied.

Separable loss.

As the dynamic programming algorithm is not parallelizable over input tokens, token-separable losses are often used in practice.

Theorem 2
.

The token-separable loss for NER via BIO tagging is not Bayes consistent.

Proof.

Let
n=2
and assume that the distribution
p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
is defined as depicted in Figure
1
.
Then, by Equation
7
we have
\widehat{w}_{1,\textsc{B}}=\log 0.65
,
\widehat{w}_{1,\textsc{O}}=\log 0.35
,
\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{B}}=\log 0.4
,
\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{I}}=\log 0.3
and
\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{O}}=\log 0.3
.
As such:

\displaystyle\log 0.65+\log 0.3

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{a}}\rangle

\displaystyle<\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{b}}\rangle=\log 0.65+\log 0.4,

but
{\bm{a}}\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in Y}p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
and
p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{a}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})>p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{b}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
.
Therefore, the token-separable loss is not Bayes consistent for NER,
i.e.
a scoring model minimizing the surrogate risk may not lead to predicting the most probable output in the data distribution.
∎

Note that the inconsistency is not due to the fact that the parameterization of the model is “poor” (no transition scores).
Indeed, by Equation
4
, optimal scores
\widehat{\bm{w}}
for the NLL loss satisfy the following condition for all
{\bm{y}}\in Y
:

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in Y}\exp\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle}=p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\implies\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle=\log p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})

The following assignment for
\widehat{\bm{w}}
satisfies this condition:

\widehat{w}_{1,\textsc{B}}=0
,

\widehat{w}_{1,\textsc{O}}=0
,

\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{B}}=\log 0.2
,

\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{I}}=\log 0.3,
and

\widehat{w}_{2,\textsc{O}}=\log 0.15
.
That is, minimizing the NLL loss on this distribution results in a Bayes consistent classifier, as expected.

Syntactic dependency parsing

Problem definition.

We consider a sentence of
n
words and, without loss of generality, restrict to the unlabeled case to simplify notations.
In dependency parsing, the set of parts is defined as the set of possible bilexical dependencies between words, including a fake root at position
0
used to identify root word(s) of the sentence,
i.e.

C=\{(h,m)\in\{0,1,...,n\}\times[n]|h\neq m\}
, where
(h,m)
denotes a dependency with the
h
-th word as head and the
m
-th word as modifier.
The set
Y
is restricted to vectors
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
that can be interpreted as forming a
0
-rooted spanning arborescence where words are vertices and dependencies are arcs
McDonald et al. 2005
.
In some cases,
e.g.
the Universal Dependency format, it is required that the fake root position has a single outgoing arc.

4

4

Inference algorithms.

MAP inference can be realized via the maximum spanning arborescence algorithm, which has a
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time complexity
Chu and Liu 1965
;
Edmonds 1967
;
Tarjan 1977
.
The single root constraint can be taken into account using the same algorithm via the big-
M
trick
(
Fischetti and Toth 1992
, Section 2)
.
Marginal inference can be realized via the matrix tree theorem
(
Koo et al. 2007
;
McDonald and Satta 2007
;
Smith and Smith 2007
, MTT,)
, which has
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time complexity.

Separable loss.

The cubic-time complexity of MTT may be prohibitive in practice for training a model.
Moreover, the MTT relies on a computationally unstable matrix inversion and is arguably non-trivial to implement.
Hence, there has been interest in using simpler token-separable NLL loss functions
Zhang et al. 2017
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{(sep-dep)}({\bm{w}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt=-\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle+\sum_{m\in[n]}\log\hskip-7.0pt\sum_{h\in[n]\setminus\{m\}}\hskip-7.0pt\exp{w}_{h,m},

also called head selection loss.
This loss is a sum of multiclass classification NLL losses, one per word in the sentence, and is therefore token-separable.
As such, it can be efficiently parallelized on GPU and is trivial to implement in any ML framework.

The optimal pointwise surrogate Bayes risk for the token-separable loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{r}}^{*}_{(sep-dep)}=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{A}}\begin{array}[t]{l}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[\langle{\bm{w}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle]\\
+\sum\limits_{m\in[n]}\log\hskip-7.0pt\sum\limits_{h\in[n]\setminus\{m\}}\hskip-7.0pt\exp{w}_{h,m}.\end{array}

Let
\widehat{\bm{w}}
be an optimal solution.
Then, by first order optimality conditions:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{w}_{h,m}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}}[\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle]\\
+\sum\limits_{m\in[n]}\log\hskip-7.0pt\sum\limits_{h\in[n]\setminus\{m\}}\hskip-7.0pt\exp\widehat{w}_{h,m}\end{array}\right)=0

\displaystyle\implies\widehat{w}_{h,m}=\log p\left({\textnormal{y}}_{h,m}=1|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}\right)

denotes the marginal distribution of the dependency between words at position
h
and
m
,
i.e.
the sum of the conditional probability of trees this dependency appears in.

6
Conclusion

Studying statistical properties of surrogate loss functions has not been a major interest in the NLP community, although there are exceptions
Ma and Collins 2018
;
Effland and Collins 2021
.
We proved that token-separable losses for NER and dependency parsing are not Bayes consistent, which means that minimizing these losses will not necessarily lead to models that will predict the most probable output for a given input in the data distribution, even with infinite training data.

In the dependency parsing case,
Zhang et al. 2020
experimentally observed that the structured NLL loss leads to better results than the token-separable head selection loss.
As such, our analysis provides a better theoretical understanding of these experiments.
However, separable losses are widely used in state-of-the-art models, which suggests that future research should study why they work in practice.

Other types of separability have also been used for constituency parsing
Corro 2020
and semantic parsing
Pasupat et al. 2019
,
inter alia
.

Limitations

Arguably, these separable loss functions perform well in practice, which questions the appropriateness of the Bayes consistency property.
For example,
Long and Servedio 2013
argued that assumptions usually made are too unrealistic (
e.g.
considering that
F
is the set of all measurable mappings) and leads to misleading theoretical knowledge when it comes to actual implementation and experiments.
Maybe this is also the case of the demonstration we made in this paper.
However, all in all, we hope that this work will motivate future fundamental research on ML for NLP and especially on properties of loss functions.

Theorem 4

\widetilde{\ell}_{\text{(sep-dep)}}
is not Bayes consistent for distributions over single-root dependency trees.

Proof.

Let
n=4
and assume that the distribution
p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
is defined as depicted in Figure
3
.
Then, by Equation
10
we have, among others:

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=\log 0.55,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\log 0.55,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\log 0.4,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{2,3}

\displaystyle=\log 0.45.

As such, we have:

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{a}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\log 0.55+\log 0.55+\log 0.40,

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{b}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\log 0.55+\log 0.55+\log 0.45,

which means that
\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{a}}\rangle<\langle\widehat{\bm{w}},{\bm{b}}\rangle
but
{\bm{a}}\in\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in Y}p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
and
p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{a}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})>p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{b}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})
.
Therefore the separable loss is not Bayes consistent for single-root dependency parsing.
∎
