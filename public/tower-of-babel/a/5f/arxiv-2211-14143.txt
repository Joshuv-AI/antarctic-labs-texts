Efficient a Posteriori Error Control of a Consistent Atomistic/Continuum Coupling Method for Two Dimensional Crystalline Defects
Yangshuai Wang, Hao Wang

Abstract
Adaptive atomistic/continuum (a/c) coupling method is an important method for the simulation of material and atomistic systems with defects to achieve the balance of accuracy and efficiency. Residual based a posteriori error estimator is often employed in the adaptive algorithm to provide an estimate of the error of the strain committed by applying the continuum approximation for the atomistic system and the finite element discretization in the continuum region. In this work, we propose a theory based approximation for the residual based a posteriori error estimator which greatly improves the efficiency of the adaptivity. In particular, the numerically expensive modeling residual is only computed exactly in a small region around the coupling interface but replaced by a theoretically justified approximation by the coarsening residual outside that region. We present a range of adaptive computations based on our modified a posteriori error estimator and its variants for different types of crystalline defects some of which are not considered in previous related literature of the adaptive a/c methods. The numerical results show that, compared with the original residual based error estimator, the adaptive algorithm using the modified error estimator with properly chosen parameters leads to the same optimal convergence rate of the error but reduces the computational cost by one order with respect to the number of degrees of freedom.

[

a posteriori

[

However, both of the two works consider only simple point defects (di-vacancy in

[
34

find that the CPU time of computing the modeling residual dramatically exceeds that of solving the a/c coupling problem.

The purpose of this work is to develop an efficient adaptive algorithm based on a theory based approximation for the residual based
a posteriori
error estimator for a consistent a/c coupling scheme and apply the algorithm to the adaptive simulations of different defects in two dimensions. In particular, we prove that the modeling residual, which is expensive to compute, and the coarsening residual, whose computational cost is marginal compared with that of solving the a/c problem on a given mesh, essentially follow a ratio relation under certain assumptions. Based on this theoretical result, we then propose various
modified
residual based
a posteriori
error estimator and mesh structures so that the modeling residual is only exactly computed in a relatively small neighborhood of the coupling interface but properly approximated by the coarsening error away from the defect core. We finally conduct a range of adaptive computations, using the
modified
estimators as well as the
original
residual based estimator and
a priori
graded meshes if applicable, for the three typical types of defects, namely micro-crack, anti-plane screw dislocation and multiple vacancies, where the later two are considered for the first time in the research of the adaptive a/c methods. The numerical results show that our
modified
error estimator with properly chosen parameters leads to the same convergence behavior as the
original
error estimator while gain the efficiency by reducing the computational cost by one order with respect to the number of degrees of freedom. In addition, the advantage of the adaptive a/c methods is clearly demonstrated in the case of multiple vacancies where no
a priori
graded mesh may be derived at first. Such defect can also be considered as a simplification of the more complicated but realistic models which consider the interactions of multiple defects where the adaptive a/c may better reveal its power.

We constrain ourselves to the GRAC23 derived in
[
21
]
with the nearest neighbor interactions to clearly present the ideas and steps, though the analysis and constructions can be in principle extended to finite range interactions and to other a/c methods. We give a further remark for this in §
6
.

2.1
Atomistic model

We denote a perfect single lattice which possesses no defects by
\Lambda^{\rm hom}:=\mathsf{A}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
can be considered as a macroscopic deformation gradient that often represents a uniform stretch or compression of a crystalline. To model local defects of a crystal lattice, we employ
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
which satisfies the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

\exists~\Omega^{\rm def}:=\cup_{i=1}^{M}B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}(x_{i})
, such that
\Lambda\backslash\Omega^{\rm def}=\Lambda^{\rm hom}\backslash\Omega^{\rm def}
and
\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm def}
is finite.

The region
B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}(x_{i})
often contains localized defects with a center positioned at
x_{i}
and is called a defect core. Such formulation can represent different types of defects. In particular, for point defects such as vacancies and micro-cracks we have
\Lambda\subset\Lambda^{\rm hom}
, and for an anti-plane screw dislocation we have
\Lambda\equiv\Lambda^{\rm hom}
.

We denote a deformed configuration by a lattice function
y\in\mathscr{U}
where
\mathscr{U}:=\{v:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
is the set of vector-valued lattice functions. The displacement
u\in\mathscr{U}
is then defined accordingly by
u(\ell)=y(\ell)-y_{0}(\ell)=y(\ell)-x(\ell)-u_{0}(\ell)=y(\ell)-\ell-u_{0}(\ell)
for any
\ell\in\Lambda
if we define
x\in\mathscr{U}
to be the identity map. We consider
u_{0}\in\mathscr{U}
as the
predictor
, which is usually a far-field crystalline environment, and
u\in\mathscr{U}
the
corrector

[
8
,
36
]
. We simply take
u_{0}=0
for point defects and the derivation of
u_{0}
for anti-plane screw dislocation is reviewed in
Appendix A
. We refer to Figure
1
for the illustration of the geometry of the three different types of defects we consider in this work, namely micro-crack, anti-plane screw dislocation and multiple vacancies.

(a)
Micro-crack

(b)
Anti-plane screw dislocation (colors by
predictor

u_{0}
)

(c)
Multiple separate vacancies

Illustration of three typical defects considered in this work.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
1
is presented in a slightly different form as that in various earlier works such as
[
8
,
33
,
36
,
37
]
, where only a single round shaped defect core
B_{R^{\rm def}}
is assumed. We decide to use the current formulation to include the case of multiple vacancies which has not been considered before. In a/c adaptive computations, the defect core is often contained in the atomistic region and the allowance of separating the defect core into a number of round shaped regions
B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}(x_{i})
facilitates the description of the initial steps of the adaptivity which are clearly visualized in Figure
11
in §
5.3
. However, we note that whether the defects are separated or “centralized" have no fundamental influence on the analytical results we derive in the current work. Only the condition that
\Lambda\cap\Omega^{{\rm def}}=\Lambda\cap\cup_{i=1}^{M}B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}(x_{i})
(or
\Lambda\cap B_{R^{\rm def}}
in the case of a single round shaped defect core) plays the role which essentially means that the defect core can not appear at infinity.

We define the interaction neighborhood for each
\ell\in\Lambda
by
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}:=\{\ell^{\prime}\in\Lambda\,|\,0<|\ell^{\prime}-\ell|\leq r_{\rm cut}\}
, where
r_{\rm cut}
is called the cut-off radius and is always equal to
1
in the current work since we only consider the nearest neighbor interaction. We also denote the interaction range
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}:=\{\ell^{\prime}-\ell\,|\,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\}
as the union of lattice vectors defined by the finite differences between the atom
\ell
and the lattice points in its neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
.

The finite difference stencil for each
\ell\in\Lambda
is then defined by
Dv(\ell):=\{D_{\rho}v(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}:=\{v(\ell+\rho)-v(\ell)\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}
. The lattice energy-norm (essentially a discrete
H^{1}
-semi-norm), which we use to measure the difference or error for lattice functions, is then defined by

\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}}:=\bigg(\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}|D_{\rho}v(\ell)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},\ \ \forall v\in\mathscr{U}.

(1)

To measure the local “regularity" of a displacement function
u\in\mathscr{U}
, it is convenient to assume the existence of a background mesh, that is, a
canonical
triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into triangles whose nodes are the reference lattice sites
\Lambda
. We refer to
[
8
, Figure 1]
for the illustration of such triangulation in 2D.
We then introduce a standard piecewise affine interpolation of
u
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
by
I_{{\rm a}}u
. Notice that
\Lambda
is a lattice with some vacancies when we consider micro-crack and multiple vacancies. We can thus construct the piecewise interpolant with respect to
\Lambda^{\rm hom}
by extending
u
to the vacancy sites (see
[
33
, Appendix 1]
for detail).

We identify
u=I_{\rm a}u
if there is no confusion and denote the piecewise constant gradient
\nabla u:=\nabla I_{\rm a}u
. We then introduce the discrete homogeneous Sobolev space

\mathscr{U}^{1,2}:=\{u\in\mathscr{U}~|~\nabla u\in L^{2}\},

with the semi-norm
|u|_{\mathscr{U}^{1,2}}:=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}
.

The atomistic system we consider is modeled by the site energy
V_{\ell}
associated with the lattice points where
V_{\ell}:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the energy contribution by atom
\ell
. We assume that
V_{\ell}\in C^{k}((\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}),k\geq 2
and
V_{\ell}
is
homogeneous
outside the defect core
\Omega^{{\rm def}}
, namely,
V_{\ell}=V
and
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}=\mathcal{R}
for
\ell\in\Lambda\setminus\Omega^{{\rm def}}
. A further condition of point symmetry
\mathcal{R}=-\mathcal{R}
, and
V(\{-g_{-\rho}\}_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}})=V(g)
is also assumed to keep the homogeneity of the system outside the defect core
[
8
]
.

We note that
V_{\ell}
may vary according to
\ell
inside each
{B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}}(x_{i})
so that certain defects can be modeled. We also note that a great number of practical interatomic potentials satisfy the assumptions of smoothness and symmetry listed in the last paragraph including the widely used embedded atom method (EAM)
[
38
]
and Finnis-Sinclair model
[
39
]
so that it is not a serious restriction for realistic atomistic or material systems. We will use the empirical EAM potential throughout our numerical experiments, see §
5
for detail.

Since the energy of an infinite lattice is typically ill-defined, with a slight abuse of notation, we redefine the potential
V_{\ell}(y)
as the energy difference
V_{\ell}(y)-V_{\ell}(x)
for point defects, which is equivalent to assuming
V_{\ell}(x)=0
. As for anti-plane screw dislocation, we denote
V_{\ell}(y):=V(y)-V(y_{0})
(cf.
[
8
, Section 3.1]
). The energy functional of the atomistic system

\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y)=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}V_{\ell}(y)

(2)

is then a meaningful object. By the assumptions of smoothness and symmetry on
V_{\ell}
,
\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y)
is well-defined for
y-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
with certain Fréchet differentiability, where
y^{B}(x)=Bx+u_{0}(x)
. We refer to
[
8
]
for a detailed discussion.

The atomistic problem we would like to solve is to find a
strongly stable

equilibrium
y
, such that, given a macroscopic applied strain
B\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
,

y\in\arg\min\big\{\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y)~\,\big|\,~y-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}\big\}.

(3)

The
strong stability
on
y
means that there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\langle\delta^{2}\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y)v,v\rangle\geq c_{0}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}.

2.2
GRAC coupling method

The atomistic problem (
3
) is not computable in practice since it is defined on an infinite domain and it considers every atom as a degree of freedom. The former problem is easily circumvented by restricting the computational domain to be
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is a simply connected polygonal and we assume that
\exists R\in\mathbb{R}
such that
B_{R}\subset\Omega\subset B_{c_{0}R}
for some
c_{0}>1
where
R
is the radius of
\Omega
.

To deal with the later issue, namely the overwhelming number of degrees of freedom, we first define a continuum approximation by the Cauchy-Born rule which is both compatible with (
2
) and related to the strain. The Cauchy-Born energy density functional
W:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}
is simply given by

W({\sf F}):=\det(\mathsf{A}^{-1})\cdot V({\sf F}\mathcal{R}),

where
V
is the homogeneous site energy potential on
\Lambda^{\rm hom}

[
40
,
41
]
. An atomistic-to-continuum (a/c) coupling method then essentially couples the atomistic model with its Cauchy-Born approximation together. To formulate our coupling scheme, we first decompose the computational domain into two parts:
\Omega=\Omega^{\rm a}\bigcup\Omega^{\rm c}
where the defect core
\Omega^{{\rm def}}
is contained in
\Omega^{\rm a}
. Next, we decompose the set of atoms inside the atomistic region into a core atomistic set
\Lambda^{\rm a}
and an interface set
\Lambda^{\rm i}
(usually a few “layers” of atoms surrounding
\Lambda^{\rm a}
for each
B_{R^{\rm def}_{i}}(x_{i})
) such that
\Lambda^{{\rm a},{\rm i}}:=\Lambda\bigcap\Omega^{\rm a}=\Lambda^{\rm a}\bigcup\Lambda^{\rm i}
and
\Lambda\cap\Omega^{{\rm def}}
is contained in
\Lambda^{\rm a}
.

To reduce the number of degrees of freedom, we introduce the finite element partition of the computational domain
\mathcal{T}_{h}
so that
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\mathcal{T}^{{\rm c}}_{h}\bigcup\mathcal{T}^{{\rm a}}_{h}
where
\mathcal{T}^{\rm a}_{h}
is the
canonical
triangulation induced by
\Lambda^{{\rm a},{\rm i}}
and
\mathcal{T}^{{\rm c}}_{h}
be a shape-regular simplicial partition (triangles for 2D) of the continuum region
\Omega^{\rm c}
. We note that
\mathcal{T}^{\rm a}_{h}
may contain "holes" due to the existence of defects (see Figure
6
for an illustration). We define
\mathcal{N}_{h}
be the set of nodes in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{{\rm c}}
be the set of edges in
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{{\rm c}}
.

Assuming
P_{1}
finite element are used on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the general formulation of an energy-based atomistic-to-continuum coupling energy functional can be written as

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm a}}V_{\ell}(y_{h})+\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm i}}\omega_{\ell}V^{\rm i}_{\ell}(y_{h})+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\omega_{T}W(\nabla y_{h}|_{T}),

(4)

where
V_{\ell}^{\rm i}
is a modified interface site potential and
\omega_{\ell}
and
\omega_{T}
are certain coefficients which are usually called effective volumes of lattice sites or elements (see
[
21
, Section 2.2]
for a detailed discussion). We note that
\omega_{T}=0
for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{{\rm a}}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm i}
with
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm i}:=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:\Lambda^{\rm i}\cap T\neq\emptyset\}
.

The interface potential in (
4
) is the key for constructing a consistent a/c coupling method. In the current work, we restrict ourselves to the geometric reconstruction based consistent a/c (GRAC) coupling method which is first proposed in
[
21
]
and then further developed in
[
35
]
. The essential idea for GRAC method is to introduce the geometric reconstruction parameters
C_{\ell;\rho,\varsigma}
so that for each
\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm i},\rho,\varsigma\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
the interface potential is redefined by

V_{\ell}^{\rm i}(y):=V\Big(\big({\textstyle\sum_{\varsigma\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}C_{\ell;\rho,\varsigma}D_{\varsigma}y(\ell)}\big)_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\Big).

(5)

The geometric reconstruction parameters are then determined by solving the following equations:

V_{\ell}^{\rm i}(y^{{\sf F}})=V(y^{{\sf F}})\qquad\quad\forall\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm i},\quad{\sf F}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(6)

and

\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y^{{\sf F}}),v\rangle=0\qquad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}_{h},\quad{\sf F}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(7)

which are termed as the energy patch test consistency and the force patch test consistency
[
42
]
. According to
[
21
, Proposition 3.7]
, under the setting of the current work, for
\ell\in\Lambda^{{\rm i}}
, the parameters
C_{\ell;\rho,\zeta}
in (
5
) are determined by: (a)
C_{\ell,\rho,\varsigma}=0
for
|(\rho-\varsigma)~\textrm{mod}~6|>1
; (b)
C_{\ell,\rho,\rho-1}=C_{\ell,\rho,\rho+1}=1-C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}
; (c)
C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}=C_{\ell+\rho,-\rho,-\rho}=1
for
\ell+\rho\in\Lambda^{{\rm i}}
; (d)
C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}=1
for
\ell+\rho\in\Lambda^{{\rm a}}
; (e)
C_{\ell,\rho,\rho}=2/3
for
\ell+\rho\in\Lambda^{{\rm c}}
. The coefficient
2/3
given in (e) introduces the name of the specific GRAC method we consider in the rest of this paper, which is the GRAC23 method. However, we need to note that these coefficients are not unique and how they are optimally determined are discussed in depth in
[
35
]
.

Let
\Omega_{h}=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}T
. We define the space of
coarse-grained
displacements to be

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{h}:=\big\{u_{h}:\Omega_{h}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\,\big|\,

u_{h}
is continuous and p.w. affine w.r.t.
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,

\displaystyle\text{ $u_{h}=0$ on $\partial\Omega_{h}$ }\big\}.

The a/c coupling problem we would like to solve is to find

y_{h}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h})~\,\big|\,~y_{h}-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}\big\}.

(8)

2.2.1
The formulation of the stress tensors

The stress tensor, which is in principle defined by the first variation of the energy, plays the key role in both
a priori
and
a posteriori
error analysis of the a/c coupling methods. For any
y-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
, and
y_{h}-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}
, we define the atomistic stress tensor
\sigma^{\rm a}(y;\cdot)\in{\rm P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{\rm a})^{2\times 2}
, the continuum stress tensor
\sigma^{\rm c}(y_{h};\cdot)\in{\rm P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{2\times 2}
and the a/c stress tensor
\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h};\cdot)\in{\rm P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{2\times 2}
respectively by the following identities

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}}|T|\sigma^{\rm a}(y;T)\nabla_{T}v,\qquad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2},

(9)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm c}(y_{h}),v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|T|\sigma^{\rm c}(y_{h};T)\nabla_{T}v_{h},\qquad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h},

(10)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|T|\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h};T)\nabla_{T}v_{h},\qquad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h},

(11)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
is the
canonical
triangulation induced by the reference lattice
\Lambda
and
\mathscr{E}^{\rm c}(y_{h}):=\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|T|W(\nabla_{T}y_{h})
. Since we only consider nearest neighbor interactions, we can choose the following stress tensors from the first variations (
9
)-(
11
),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm a}(y;T):=~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det\mathsf{A}}\sum_{b=(\ell,\ell+\rho)\in\partial T}\partial_{\rho}V_{\ell}\otimes a_{\rho},

(12)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm c}(y_{h};T):=~

\displaystyle\partial W(\nabla_{T}y_{h})=\frac{1}{\det\mathsf{A}}\sum_{j=1}^{6}\partial_{j}V(\nabla_{T}y_{h})\otimes a_{j},

(13)

\displaystyle\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h};T):=~\frac{1}{\det\mathsf{A}}

\displaystyle\sum_{b=(\ell,\ell+\rho)\in\partial T}\partial_{\rho}V_{\ell}(I_{\rm a}y_{h})\otimes a_{\rho}+\omega_{T}\sigma^{\rm c}(y_{h};T).

(14)

We note that the stress tensors defined in (
9
)-(
11
) are not unique. Equation (
9
)-(
11
) may hold with any of the stress tensors differ from (
12
)-(
14
) with a piecewise constant tensor field
\sigma\in{\rm P}_{0}(\mathcal{T})^{2\times 2}
that satisfies the
divergence free
condition

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}|T|\sigma(T):\nabla_{T}v\equiv 0,\qquad\forall v\in({\rm P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}))^{2}.

(15)

We postpone the detail of the construction and optimization of such divergence free field to §
4.1
where a local stress tensor correction is applied to the coupling stress tensor
\sigma^{\rm ac}
.

3
The Analysis of the Residual Based A Posteriori Error Estimates

In this section, we give the
a posteriori
error estimates for the GRAC method introduced in §
2.2
. We first present some useful
a priori
results in §
3.1
that motivates the separation of the
a posteriori
error estimator. We then give a brief review of the
original
residual based
a posteriori
error estimates in §
3.1
which is derived and analyzed in
[
33
]
. The error estimates provide a solid foundation of the current work but the problem of high computational cost arise in computing the modeling part of the
a posteriori
error estimator. We finally concentrate on the analysis and numerical verification of the relation between the modeling and the coarsening parts of the estimator whose quotient in fact possesses a certain ratio under suitable assumptions. Such ratio relation serves as the theoretical basis for deriving modified
a posteriori
error estimators with much better computational efficiency that are discussed in §
4
and §
5
. We note that the content in §
3.1
and §
3.2
can be found in detail in earlier works
[
33
,
34
]
and we include them only for completeness and refer to related literature where necessary.

3.1
A Priori Error Estimates

If
y
is a strongly stable solution of (
3
) and is sufficiently smooth in the continuum region, there exists a strongly stable solution
y_{h}
to (
8
) and a constant
C^{a-priori}
for GRAC method such that
[
8
,
3
]
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{h}-\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{a-priori}\big(\|D^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{I}_{\rm ext})}+\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}+\|D^{2}u\|^{2}_{\ell^{4}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}

\displaystyle+\|hD^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}+\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\setminus B_{R/2})}\big),

(16)

where
u=y-y^{B},u_{h}=y_{h}-y^{B}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\rm ext}
is the extended interface region whose definition is given in
[
21
, § 5]
. The right hand side of (
3.1
) can be essentially split into three parts and we give a discussion here as
it helps to understand the correspondence between the
a priori
error estimates and the
a posteriori
error estimates.

The first part is the
modeling error
defined by
\zeta_{\rm mo}:=\|D^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{I}_{\rm ext})}+\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}+\|D^{2}u\|^{2}_{\ell^{4}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}
. The error is due to the difference between the atomistic model and the continuum model which lies in both the discrepancy of the nonlocality and the locality of the atomistic and the coupling energy and the lattice and mesh on which we solve the problems. This is the fundamentally different from the discretization of a PDE using certain numerical schemes, see
[
21
,
33
]
for further discussion.

The second part is the
coarsening error
denoted by
\zeta_{\rm cg}:=\|hD^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{\rm c})}
. This is caused by the application of the finite element method in the continuum region and is a reminiscence of the discretization error of the numerical solution to an elliptic differential equation.

The third part is the
truncation error
denoted by
\zeta_{\rm tr}:=\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\setminus B_{R/2})}
, which arises from the finite size of our computational domain. We note that the region
B_{R/2}
comes from the construction of the truncation operator, see
[
8
, Lemma 7.3]
for a better detail.

3.2
Residual based a posterior error estimates

We derive the
a posteriori
error estimates based on the residual for the a/c method in this section. To avoid the unnecessary complexity of the presentation while keeping the main ideas and steps, we restrict our discussion to the case of the GRAC23 method with nearest neighbor multibody interactions. We refer to
[
34
]
for the extension to the finite range interaction. We also note the derivation is concise as the detail can be found in
[
33
]
.

The first variation of the atomistic variational problem (
3
) and that of the a/c coupling problem (
8
) are to find
y-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
and
y_{h}-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}_{h}
such that

\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(y),v\rangle=0,\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2},\text{ and }\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{h}\rangle=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathscr{U}_{h},

(17)

respectively. Since we limit ourselves in a finite computational domain
\Omega
, we choose the same truncation operator
T_{R}:\mathscr{U}^{1,2}\to\mathscr{U}_{R}
where
\mathscr{U}_{R}:=\{u\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}~|~u(x)=0,~\forall x\in\Lambda\backslash\Omega\}
.

The residual operator
{\sf R}
is then defined as an operator on
\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
and
{\sf R}[v]
is given by

{\sf R}[v]=\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v\rangle,\quad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}.

(18)

Let
v_{R}=T_{R}v
, and
v_{h}=\mathcal{C}_{h}T_{R}v:\mathscr{U}^{1,2}\to\mathscr{U}_{h}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{h}:\mathscr{U}_{R}\to\mathscr{U}_{h}
is the modified Clément operator
[
33
,
43
,
44
]
. By the second equation in (
17
), we separate the residual
{\sf R}[v]
into three groups

\displaystyle{\sf R}[v]=\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v\rangle-\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\{\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v_{R}\rangle-[\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{R}]\big\}+\big\{[\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{R}]-\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{h}\rangle\big\}

\displaystyle+\big\{\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v\rangle-\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v_{R}\rangle\big\}=:{\sf R}_{\rm mo}+{\sf R}_{\rm cg}+{\sf R}_{\rm tr},

(19)

where the operator
[\cdot,\cdot]
is defined by

\displaystyle[\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{R}]:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T)\nabla v_{R}\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T)(\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a},T^{\prime}\bigcap T\neq\emptyset}|T\bigcap T^{\prime}|\nabla v_{R})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}}|T|\big(\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h},T^{\prime}\bigcap T\neq\emptyset}\frac{|T^{\prime}\bigcap T|}{|T|}\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T^{\prime})\big)\nabla v_{R},

(20)

which is different from
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
since
v_{R}\notin\mathscr{U}_{h}
. We essentially separate the residual into the modeling part
{\sf R}_{\rm mo}
, the the coarsening part
{\sf R}_{\rm cg}
and the truncation part
{\sf R}_{\sf tr}
.

The estimate of the truncation residual
{\sf R}_{\rm tr}
is the easiest among the three and is simply given by

|{\sf R}_{\rm tr}|\leq C^{{\rm tr}}\|\sigma^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}u_{h})-\sigma^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus B_{R/2})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}=:\eta_{\rm tr}(y_{h})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}},

(21)

where
C^{{\rm tr}}
is the constant appeared when estimating the difference of
Dv_{R}
and
Dv
in terms of
Dv
, and we can specify the divergence-free stress tensor
\sigma^{0}=\partial W(\nabla y^{B})
(cf.
[
33
, §3.1.1]
).

The coarsening residual
{\sf R}_{\rm cg}
is estimated by

|{\sf R}_{\rm cg}|\leq\sqrt{3}C^{\rm tr}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm c}\bigcap{\rm int}(\Omega)}(h_{f}\llbracket\sigma^{{\rm ac}}\rrbracket_{f})^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=:\eta_{\rm cg}(y_{h})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(22)

where
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\rm c}\bigcap{\rm int}(\Omega)
is the set of interior edges,
\llbracket\sigma^{{\rm ac}}\rrbracket_{f}:=\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T^{+}_{f})\nu^{+}+\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T^{-}_{f})\nu^{-}
denotes the jump of
\sigma^{{\rm ac}}
across each interior edge
f
and
C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
is the constant for the modified Clément operator that only depends on the shape regularity of the mesh. We refer to
[
33
, §3.1.3]
for a thorough discussion.

For the analysis of the modeling error
{\sf R}_{\rm mo}
, the formulation of
\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v_{R}\rangle
and
[\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm ac}(y_{h}),v_{R}]
given in (
11
) and (
20
) lead to
[
33
, §3.1.2]
,

|{\sf R}_{\rm mo}|\leq C^{{\rm tr}}\big\{\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}}|T|\big[\sigma^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h},T^{\prime})-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},T\bigcap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset}\frac{|T\bigcap T^{\prime}|}{|T^{\prime}|}\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T)\big]^{2}\big\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}=:\eta_{\rm mo}(y_{h})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}.

(23)

Remark 2
.

We have to note that though the estimate of the modeling residual
\eta_{\rm mo}
is probably the most straight forward one among the three, it consumes the most computational cost in the adaptive computation. It is easily observe from (
23
) that to compute
\eta_{\rm mo}
we need to find
|T^{\prime}\bigcap T|
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
and
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Thus the complex geometry, which is illustrated soon in Figure
2
, enters into the computation when we assign the global error estimator into local contributions in each adaptive step. We give an estimate of the computational cost of the modeling residual in §
3.3
and from then on, we will discuss how to deal with this inefficiency that eventually reduces the computational cost by one order with respect to the number of degrees of freedom.

Combining the residual estimates in (
21
), (
22
) and (
23
), the stability results in
[
33
, Section 3.2]
and a quantitative version of the inverse function theorem
[
3
, Lemma B.1]
, we can obtain the following theorem for the
rigorous global a posteriori existence and error estimate

[
33
, Theorem 3.7]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
y_{h}
be the a/c solution defined in (
17
),
\eta(y_{h})=\eta_{{\rm tr}}(y_{h})+\eta_{\rm mo}(y_{h})+\eta_{\rm cg}(y_{h})
be the total residual estimator,
\gamma(y_{h})
be the stability constant defined in
[
33
, § 3.2.4]
, and
L_{1}
be the Lipschitz constant of
\delta^{2}\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}
whose definition can be found in
[
3
, Lemma 3.9]
. Under the assumption that
\gamma(y_{h})>0
and
2L_{1}\eta(y_{h})<\gamma(y_{h})^{2}
, there exists a unique
y
satisfying
y-y^{B}\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}
which solves the atomistic variational problem (
17
), and satisfies the following a posteriori error bound,

\|\nabla I_{\rm a}y_{h}-\nabla y\|_{L^{2}}\equiv\|\nabla I_{\rm a}u_{h}-\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2\frac{\eta(y_{h})}{\gamma(y_{h})}.

(24)

3.3
Local contribution and analysis of the modeling and the coarsening residual estimators

The
a posteriori
error estimates we just proposed is global. In order to design and implement the adaptive algorithm, we need to assign the global residual estimator
\eta(y_{h})
into elementwise local contributions except for the truncation part. The truncation residual estimator is often computed directly in practice since it is relatively small if the computational domain
\Omega
is sufficiently large. We then check whether it exceeds the threshold during the main adaptive process. See Algorithm
2
in §
4.2
for detail.

Following standard adaptive finite element procedure, the local coarsening residual estimator for an element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
can be defined as

\eta_{{\rm cg}}(y_{h};T):=\Bigg(\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\bigcap T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{2}(h_{f}\llbracket\sigma^{{\rm ac}}\rrbracket_{f})^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(25)

The local modeling residual estimator for an element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is defined as the sum of the differences between stress tenors of
T
and those of
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
satisfying
T\bigcap T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
so that

\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T)=\Bigg(\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a},T^{\prime}\bigcap T\neq\emptyset}\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T^{\prime},T)\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

(26)

where

\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T^{\prime},T):=|T^{\prime}\bigcap T|\Bigg(\sigma^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h},T^{\prime})-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},T^{\prime}\bigcap T\neq\emptyset}\frac{|T\bigcap T^{\prime}|}{|T^{\prime}|}(\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h},T))\Bigg)^{2}.

We note that the global modeling residual estimator
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h})
defined in (
23
) is summed over
\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
for the simplicity of presentation while the elementwise modeling residual estimator is defined on each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
for the purpose of adaptive mesh refinements, and the summation of
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(T)
over
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h})
are essentially equal up to a constant
C^{\rm tr}
. We omit the constants
C^{\rm tr}
and
C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
here only for the sake of presentation and will determine the values of these constants empirically in our adaptive computations (cf. §
4.2
).

As we have already pointed out in Remark
2
, evaluating
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(T^{\prime})
requires computing
|T\cap T^{\prime}|
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
and
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
where finding the geometric relationship between
T
and
T^{\prime}
is essential. See Figure
2
for an illustration of the mismatch of
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
and
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We note that the computational cost of evaluating
\eta_{{\rm mo}}
is proportional to the number of atoms in the continuum region since in our implementation we loop over all the elements in
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
lying in the continuum region to record the geometric relationship. The number of atoms in the continuum region is about the square of the number of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Moreover, the ratio of the number of atoms in the atomistic region and the number of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
remains approximately a constant under certain assumptions (see
[
3
, Section 8.1]
for detail). Hence, if
N
is the total number of degrees of freedom of the a/c coupling problem (
8
), then the computational cost of evaluating
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(T^{\prime})
can be roughly estimated by
O(N^{2})
. In contrast, the cost of evaluating the coarsening residual
\eta_{{\rm cg}}(T)
is proportional to the number of edges
f\in\mathscr{F}^{\rm c}_{h}
of the elements in the continuum region, which makes it only of
O(N)
.
We give a numerical verification of the computational cost in §
5
(cf. Figure
8a
for micro-crack, Figure
10a
for anti-plane screw dislocation and Figure
13a
for multiple vacancies).

Figure 2:
Illustration of
|T^{\prime}\cap T|
. The edges of
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
are marked with long blue solid lines whereas the elements
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm a}
at the boundaries of the elements
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
are marked in grey.

To avoid the exceedingly high cost of computation, we apply an approximation of the elementwise residual estimator
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T)
in our adaptive computations. The idea of the approximation comes from a simple observation that the elementwise modeling residual estimator
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T)
should somehow be “negligible" compared with the elementwise coarsening residual estimator
\eta_{{\rm cg}}(y_{h};T)
. Such observation may be easily verified if we take the spring potential (essentially a linearized model) as an example. With the assumption that
u
is relative smooth in the regions that are far-away from the defect core, we can directly estimate
\eta_{{\rm cg}}(y_{h};T)\approx O(h_{T}^{2})
and
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T)\approx O(h_{T})
by some algebraic manipulations according to the definitions (
25
) and (
26
). Since
h_{T}\gg 1
for any
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
that is sufficiently far away from the defect core, we have a good reason to believe that
\eta_{{\rm mo}}(y_{h};T)
should be a quantity of higher order compared with
\eta_{{\rm cg}}(y_{h};T)
. For the rest of this section, we make this argument rigorous and numerically verify its validity.

We start with the
a priori
error estimates (
3.1
). We can further split the modeling error by

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\rm mo}}=\|D^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{I}^{\rm ext})}+\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{{\rm c}})}+\|D^{2}u\|^{2}_{\ell^{4}(\Lambda\cap\Omega^{{\rm c}})}=:\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm i}(y)+\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm c}(y),

(27)

where
\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm i}(y)
and
\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm c}(y)
are essentially the modeling error at the interface and in the continuum region. If we assume that the computational domain contains only a single atomistic region
\Omega^{{\rm a}}
whose radius is denoted by
R_{{\rm a}}
, then the following theorem states that for
R_{\rm a}
sufficiently large,
\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm c}(y)
is negligible compared with
\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)
and their elementwise counterparts obey a certain ratio relation.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds and the computational domain only contains a single atomistic region. Let
y
be a strongly stable solution of (
3
),
u=y-y^{B}
, and
\zeta_{\rm mo}^{\rm c}(y)
and
\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)
be defined in (
27
). If the triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a graded mesh such that the mesh size function
h(x)
for
x\in T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
satisfies

|h(x)|\leq C^{\rm{mesh}}\big(\frac{|x|}{R_{\rm a}}\big)^{\beta},\quad\text{ with }1<\beta<\frac{d+2}{2},

(28)

we then have

\displaystyle\lim_{R^{{\rm a}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\zeta^{\rm c}_{{\rm mo}}(y)}{\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)}=0.

(29)

Moreover, for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, if we define the local error contributions by
\zeta_{\rm cg}(y;T):=\|hD^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T})}
and
\zeta^{\rm c}_{{\rm mo}}(y;T):=\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T})}+\|D^{2}u\|^{2}_{\ell^{4}(\Lambda_{T})}
respectively with
\Lambda_{T}:=\Lambda\cap T
. Then for
R_{{\rm a}}
sufficiently large, there exits a constant
C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}>0
such that

\frac{\zeta^{\rm c}_{{\rm mo}}(y;T)}{\zeta_{\rm cg}(y;T)}\leq\frac{C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}}{h_{T}},

(30)

where
h_{T}:=\mathrm{diam}(T)
for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

Proof.

Let
y
be a strongly stable solution of (
3
) and
u=y-y^{B}
. We have the following sharp decay estimates of
u
for both point defects and dislocations
[
8
, Theorem 2.3, 3.6]
, for
|\ell|
sufficiently large and
j=1,2,3
,

\displaystyle\big|D^{j}u(\ell)\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+|\ell|\big)^{-d-j+1},\qquad\quad\textrm{point defects},

\displaystyle\big|D^{j}u(\ell)\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+|\ell|\big)^{-j-1}\log(|\ell|),\qquad\textrm{dislocations},

(31)

where the stencil norm is defined by
|Du(\ell)|:=\big(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}D_{\rho}u(\ell)\big)^{1/2}
. For
{\bm{\rho}}\in(\mathcal{R}_{\ell})^{j}
,
D_{\bm{\rho}}u:=D_{\rho_{1}}\cdots D_{\rho_{j}}u
denotes a
j
-th order derivative, and
D^{j}u
is defined recursively by
D^{j}u:=DD^{j-1}u
which denotes the
j
-th order collection of derivatives.

By the definition of
\zeta_{\rm mo}^{{\rm c}}(y)
,
\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)
, together with the sharp decay estimates (
3.3
) and the restriction of the mesh size function
h(x)
by (
28
), for
R_{\rm a}
sufficiently large, which is equivalent to
|\ell|
sufficiently large, we obtain for point defects,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm mo}^{{\rm c}}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\zeta_{\rm mo}}\Big(\int_{R_{{\rm a}}}^{\infty}r^{d-1}(r^{-d-2})^{2}\,{\rm d}r\Big)^{1/2}\leq C_{\zeta_{\rm mo}}(R_{{\rm a}})^{-d/2-2},

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\zeta_{\rm cg}}\Big(\int_{R_{{\rm a}}}^{\infty}r^{d-1}\Big(\frac{r}{R_{{\rm a}}}\Big)^{2\beta}(r^{-d-1})^{2}\,{\rm d}r\Big)^{1/2}\leq C_{\zeta_{\rm cg}}(R_{{\rm a}})^{-d/2-1}.

(32)

For dislocations, similarly we have the estimates
[
8
, Proposition 2]

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm mo}^{{\rm c}}(y)\leq C^{\prime}_{\zeta_{\rm mo}}(R_{{\rm a}})^{-2}\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)\leq C^{\prime}_{\zeta_{\rm cg}}(R_{{\rm a}})^{-1}.

(33)

Hence, we have

\lim_{R^{{\rm a}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\zeta^{{\rm c}}_{{\rm mo}}(y)}{\zeta_{\rm cg}(y)}=\lim_{R^{{\rm a}}\rightarrow\infty}C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}R_{{\rm a}}^{-1}=0,

with
C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}:=\max\{C_{\zeta_{\rm mo}}/C_{\zeta_{\rm cg}},C^{\prime}_{\zeta_{\rm mo}}/C^{\prime}_{\zeta_{\rm cg}}\}
, which yields the stated result (
29
) for both point defects and dislocations.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
be the
canonical
triangulation induced by
\Lambda
. Given
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\rm c}_{h}
, we define the set of “inner finest elements" by
\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}^{T}:=\{T_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}~\big|~|T_{\varepsilon}\cap T|=|T_{\varepsilon}|\}
.

To obtain the estimate (
30
), we first have for any
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\rm c}_{h}
and
T_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{T}^{T}_{\varepsilon}

\frac{\zeta^{{\rm c}}_{{\rm mo}}(y;T)}{\zeta_{\rm cg}(y;T)}\leq C\frac{\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T})}}{\|hD^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T})}}\leq C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}h^{-1}_{T}\frac{\|D^{3}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T_{\varepsilon}})}}{\|D^{2}u\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{T_{\varepsilon}})}}.

(34)

Denoting the barycenter of
T_{\varepsilon}
by
x_{T_{\varepsilon}}
and applying the decay estimates (
3.3
) again, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta^{{\rm c}}_{{\rm mo}}(y;T)}{\zeta_{\rm cg}(y;T)}\leq C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}h^{-1}_{T}\frac{(x_{T_{\varepsilon}}+1)^{-3}-x_{T_{\varepsilon}}^{-3}}{(x_{T_{\varepsilon}}+1)^{-2}-x_{T_{\varepsilon}}^{-2}}\leq\frac{C^{a-priori}_{{\rm mo}-{\rm cg}}}{h_{T}},

(35)

where in the last inequality we use the fact
x_{T_{\varepsilon}}>1
. Combining (
34
) and (
35
) we achieve the estimate (
30
) which finishes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

We then give a similar
a posteriori
estimate under suitable assumptions which is computable and can be employed in the adaptive computations. We note that similar to
\zeta^{\rm c}_{{\rm mo}}(y;T)
,
\eta^{\rm c}_{\rm mo}(y_{h};T)
represents the elementwise residual in the continuum region.

Corollary 3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, if
y_{h}
is a strongly stable solution of (
8
) satisfying (
3.1
) and we additionally assume that
u_{h}
satisfies the decay estimates (
3.3
), then for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, there exists constants
C^{\rm a-post}
and
C^{\rm a-post}_{\rm mo-cg}
such that

Proof.

4
Modified Local Error Estimators and the Adaptive Algorithms

In this section, we propose several
modified
residual based local error estimators based on the theoretical results derived in the last section and design the algorithm for the
a posteriori
error control and adaptive computations for the system we consider. We note that our adaptive procedure is the classic one, namely

\mbox{Solve}~\rightarrow~\mbox{Estimate}~\rightarrow~\mbox{Mark}~\rightarrow~\mbox{Refine},

and a common practice is to include the adaptive algorithm and the numerical results in one section. However, since the analysis in §
3.3
gives us plenty of possibilities to design various
modified
error estimators and mesh structures, we decide to separate out the elaboration of these estimators and mesh structures in this independent section so that the ideas behind are well understood.

4.1
Stress tensor correction

Before we go into the detail of the modified estimators and mesh structures, we first briefly review the stress tensor correction which is a preliminary step for specifying a correct
a posteriori
error estimator.

As we mentioned in §
2.2.1
, the stress tensors
\sigma^{{\rm ac}}
and
\sigma^{\rm a}
, on which
\eta_{\rm tr}
,
\eta_{\rm mo}
, and
\eta_{\rm cg}
all depend, are unique only up to a
divergence free
tensor field whose definition is given in (
15

\eta(y_{h})=\eta_{\rm tr}(y_{h})+\eta_{\rm mo}(y_{h})+\eta_{\rm cg}(y_{h})
with respect to all the admissible stress tensors such that

\langle\delta\mathscr{E}^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h}),v\rangle\leq\min_{c_{\rm a}\in N_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\rm a})^{2},c_{h}\in N_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{2}}\tilde{\eta}\big(\sigma^{\rm a}(I_{\rm a}y_{h})+\nabla c_{\rm a}{\sf J},\sigma^{{\rm ac}}(y_{h})+\nabla c_{h}{\sf J}\big)\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}},

(37)

where
{\sf J}
is the rotation matrix defined in
[
33
, § 2.4.3]
. Instead of minimizing the total error estimator
\eta
with respect to
c_{\rm a}
and
c_{h}
introduced in (
37
),
[
33
]
introduced an approximation of the stress tensor correction which only requires minimizing the modeling error
\eta_{\rm mo}
with respect to the degrees of freedom of
\sigma^{{\rm ac}}
adjacent to the interface. This method dramatically reduces the computational cost of such correction but greatly improves the accuracy of the estimator in the adaptive computation.

For the sake of completeness, we give the stress tensor correction algorithm which essentially follows from
[

, Algorithm 1]
.

4.2
Modified local error estimators and the adaptive algorithm

We are finally at the stage of proposing various modified local
a posteriori
error estimators and design our main adaptive algorithm. Before that, we should first recall the difficulty we face and the theoretical results in hand that may be able to deal with the difficulty so that the ideas behind the modified error estimators are easily understood.

Our main difficulty comes from the overwhelming cost of computing the elementwise modeling residual defined in (
26
) which is due to the mismatch of
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
in
\Omega^{\rm c}
as illustrated in Figure
2
. We then prove in Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
that, under suitable assumptions, the elementwise modeling residual is proportional to the elementwise coarsening residual by a factor of
O({h^{-1}_{T}})
in the continuum region, where
h_{T}
is the diameter of the element. Moreover, we numerically verify that this ratio relationship holds for the cases of defects we consider even without the stringent assumptions in the theorem and corollary. We may conclude that both the theoretical analysis and the numerical evidence show that, in principle, we are able to approximate the elementwise modeling residual by its coarsening counterpart. However, we should be conservative to such approximation around the atomistic-to-continuum interface, since a good number of researches in the analysis of the a/c coupling method have shown that the interface error may be large and subtly important for the construction of an efficient a/c mesh
[
3
,
21
,
33
]
.

To justify our concern, we show in Figure
5
the decay of the modeling residual and the coarsening residual given in (
23
) and (
22
) respectively and those in different regions, with respect to the increasing number of degrees of freedom. The computations use the
original
elementwise modeling residual and its coarsening counterpart defined in (
26
) and (
25
) respectively. Figure
5
shows that, somehow as we predicted, the overall modeling residual (represented by the red line with asterisk markers) is almost negligible (about two orders smaller) compared with the overall coarsening residual (represented by the dark blue line with round markers). However, the modeling residual (represented by the purple line with square markers) and its coarsening counterpart (represented by the light blue line with triangle markers) are comparable around the atomistic-to-continuum interface which affirms that the modeling residual may not be neglected or even be approximated at the interface. In addition, we clearly observe that the modeling residual concentrates at the interface which perfectly coincides with various earlier analysis of the interface influence of different coupling schemes
[
3
,
9
,
10
]
. The numerical observations listed above essentially motivate us to propose the following constructions of the modified
a posteriori
error estimators and mesh structures.

We note that the modeling residual can be computed exactly by (

\Omega^{\rm buf}
without the need of finding the geometric relation of
\mathcal{T}_{{\rm a}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
as they coincide in this region and thus the save of the computational cost. However, the length of
\Omega^{\rm buf}

should be kept small since it will introduce unnecessary degrees of freedom otherwise.

Outside
\Omega^{\rm buf}
, namely, the region
\Omega\setminus(\Omega^{\rm a}\cup\Omega^{\rm buf})
, we apply certain modification for the elementwise
a posteriori
error estimator. A smooth way to introduce the modification is to employ a blending function
\beta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
so that the
blended approximated
modeling residual
\tilde{\eta}_{\rm mo}(u_{h};T)
for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm c}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm buf}
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{\rm mo}(u_{h};T):=\beta(r^{\rm buf}_{T})\cdot\eta_{\rm mo}(u_{h};T)+\big(1-\beta(r^{\rm buf}_{T})\big)\cdot\frac{C^{\rm a-post}_{\rm mo-cg}}{h_{T}}\eta_{\rm cg}(u_{h};T),

(40)

where
r^{\rm buf}_{T}:={\rm dist}(T,\Lambda^{\rm buf}):=\inf\{|\ell-x_{T}|,\forall\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm buf}\}
is the distance between
T
and
\Lambda^{\rm buf}
and
x_{T}
is the barycenter of
T
.
C^{\rm a-post}_{\rm mo-cg}
is the constant defined in (
36
) which is in practice determined empirically (cf. Figure
4
). Given the blending width
R_{\rm bld}>0
, a typical linear blending function
\beta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
is defined by

\displaystyle\beta(r):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&r\geq R_{\rm bld}\\
\frac{r-1}{R_{\rm bld}-1},&1<r<R_{\rm bld}\\
0,&r\leq 1\end{array}\right..

A more straightforward way for approximating the elementwise modeling error residual for
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\rm c}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm buf}
is to directly define

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{\rm mo}(u_{h};T):=\frac{C^{\rm a-post}_{\rm mo-cg}}{h_{T}}\eta_{\rm cg}(u_{h};T).

(45)

The advantage of such approximation is apparent that no geometric consideration is involved even for a small layer of elements near the “interface buffer" region.

We denote
\tilde{\eta}^{2}_{\rm mo}(u_{h}):=(C^{\rm tr})^{2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tilde{\eta}^{2}_{\rm mo}(u_{h};T)
. Recall that the local coarsening residual is already defined by (
25
). We thus are ready to define the
modified
elementwise local estimator
\rho_{T}(u_{h};T)
for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
as

\rho_{T}(u_{h};T):=(C^{\rm tr})^{2}\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{\rm mo}^{2}(u_{h};T)}{\tilde{\eta}_{{\rm mo}}(u_{h})}+(\sqrt{3}C^{\rm tr}C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})^{2}\frac{\eta^{2}_{{\rm cg}}(u_{h};T)}{\eta_{\rm cg}(u_{h})}.

(46)

It is straightforward to see that
\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\rho_{T}(u_{h};T)=\tilde{\eta}_{\rm mo}(u_{h})+\eta_{\rm cg}(u_{h})
. We note that the constants
C^{\rm tr}
and
C_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
are not known
a priori
and we use empirical estimates in the computation instead.

As mentioned earlier, we do not assign the truncation residual to local contributions but compute it by (
21
) directly and check its value during the adaptive process since it is much smaller than the total local estimator
\rho_{T}
when
\Omega
is sufficiently large (cf. §
3.3
).

6
Conclusion and Outlook

We develop the efficient adaptive algorithm for the geometric reconstruction based consistent atomistic/continuum (GRAC) coupling scheme based on a
modified

residual based

a posteriori
error estimator for various types of crystalline defects. The key for achieving such efficiency but maintaining comparable accuracy is the strategy of constructing an a/c coupling mesh with a small “interface buffer" region around the coupling interface in which the elementwise modeling residual is computed exactly but outside which the modeling residual is replaced by a theoretically and numerically justified approximation using its coarsening counterpart. Although we believe such strategy is generally applicable for a/c coupling schemes and more complex crystalline defects, the current research still raises a few open problems which deserve further mathematical analysis and algorithmic developments.

The first one is the extension to general short range interactions
[
34
]
. To generalize our strategy to that setting, a detailed study of the width of “interface buffer" region with respect to the interaction range is needed.

The second one is the problem of adaptive error control for energy blended a/c coupling schemes, such as energy-based blended quasi-continuum (BQCE) method and blended ghost force correction (BGFC) method. The
a priori
analysis in
[
9
,
10
,
20
]
provide an analytical framework and the stress tensor based formulation plays a key role in the analysis. By inheriting such framework and using the
modified
error estimator proposed in this paper, the adaptivity for those coupling schemes should be somehow straightforward for the case of fixed width of the blending region. However, a considerable amount of effort may be required to problem of the adaptive choice of the width of the blending region.

The third one is the extension to three dimensional problems. We note that constructing a consistent a/c coupling scheme with a sharp interface in three dimensions still remains an open problem. Thus the development of adaptive energy blended methods mentioned in the previous paragraph may be the appropriate direction.

The last but may be the most important problem to consider is the adaptive computation for complex crystalline defects. The simulation of the interactions of defects, which is briefly touched by the third numerical experiment, and the dislocation nucleation have already attracted considerable attention. It is very difficult (if not impossible) to give rigorous
a priori
analysis for such problems, including the proper boundary conditions and complicated geometry at the interface. However, it should be the place where the adaptive a/c coupling methods reveal its most advantage and power and we believe that the idea of the current research makes it possible to develop efficient and robust adaptive algorithms for those problems with practical interests.

P(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\quad\text{for}~\ell\in B\mathbb{Z}^{3}.
