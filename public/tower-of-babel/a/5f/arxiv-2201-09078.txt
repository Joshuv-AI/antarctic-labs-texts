Abstract
. We survey the Carathéodory extremal problem Car ⁡ δ \Car\delta on the symmetrized bidisc G \displaystyle G = def \displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}} { ( z + w , z w ) : | z | < 1 , | w | < 1 } \displaystyle\{(z+w,zw):|z|<1,\,|w|<1\} = \displaystyle= { ( s , p ) ∈ ℂ 2 : | s − s ¯ ​ p | < 1 − | p | 2 } . \displaystyle\{(s,p)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|s-\bar{s}p|<1-|p|^{2}\}. We also give some new results on this topic. We are particularly interested in cases of this problem in which the solution of the problem is not unique. It is known that, for any δ = ( λ , v ) ∈ T ​ G \delta=(\lambda,v)\in TG with v ≠ 0 v\neq 0 , there is at least one ω ∈ 𝕋 \omega\in\mathbb{T} such that Φ ω \Phi_{\omega} solves Car ⁡ δ \Car\delta , where Φ ω ​ ( s , p ) = 2 ​ ω ​ p − s 2 − ω ​ s \Phi_{\omega}(s,p)=\frac{2\omega p-s}{2-\omega s} . Introduction

In this paper we survey the theory of the Carathéodory extremal problem
\Car\delta

G

\delta=(\lambda,v)

(0.1)

Here

The Carathéodory extremal problem will forever be associated with the name of Lempert because of his astounding and beautiful theorem to the effect that the Carathéodory and Kobayashi metrics coincide on bounded convex domains
[
16

The domain we are interested in, the

Although

In this paper we shall survey the Carathéodory extremal problem in

, and, in addition, shall

By a

\displaystyle=\sup_{F\in\hol(\Omega,\mathbb{D})}\frac{|D_{v}F(\lambda)|}{1-|F(\lambda)|^{2}}.

\displaystyle=\sup_{F\in\hol(\Omega,\mathbb{D})}d(F(\lambda),F(\mu))

\displaystyle=\tanh^{-1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sup_{F\in\hol(\Omega,\mathbb{D})}\left|\frac{F(\lambda)-F(\mu)}{1-\overline{F(\mu)}F(\lambda)}\right|

Let

In addition to the Carathéodory problem in
\Omega
, we shall also need the notion of a complex geodesic of a domain

Definition 0.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and let
\mathcal{D}\subset\Omega
. We say that

Thus a complex geodesic is the image of an analytic disc

\mathbb{D}
and the Carathéodory distance
C_{\Omega}(\lambda,\mu)=|(\lambda,\mu)|_{\rm car}

\Omega

In
[
1

we described five types of complex geodesic in
G
, namely purely unbalanced, exceptional, purely balanced, flat and royal, and showed that every complex geodesic in

is of exactly one of these types. Furthermore, it is known that each non-degenerate tangent
\delta\in TG
touches a unique complex geodesic

[

to be the type of the complex geodesic that touches

is of exactly one of the five above-named types.

How can these five types of tangent be identified in terms of the corresponding Carathéodory extremal problems?

For a non-degerate tangent
\delta\in TG
there is an essentially unique solution of

is either purely unbalanced or exceptional

that not much is known about the set of all Carathéodory extremal functions for a general nondegenerate tangent

We shall describe all Carathéodory extremal functions for royal tangents, and many extremal functions for flat tangents in Section

For the general theory of the Carathéodory extremal problem we refer to the monographs

1.
The model formula for the symmetrized bidisc

Recall the following model formula

6

.
In this section we use the symbols
\lambda,\mu

Definition 1.1

A
G
-

1-\overline{\varphi(\mu)}\varphi(\lambda)=\left\langle(1-\mu_{T}^{*}\lambda_{T})u(\lambda),u(\mu)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}

\lambda_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}(2pT-s)(2-sT)^{-1}.

A
G
-model

\mathcal{M}

For any domain
\Omega

\Omega

\mathbb{D}^{-}

Theorem 1.2

Let

be a function on

(1)

;

(2)

\varphi

G
-model;

(3)

-model

For the proof see

From a

-model of a function

one may easily proceed by means of a standard lurking isometry argument to a realization formula of the form

\varphi(\lambda)=A+B\lambda_{T}(1-D\lambda_{T})^{-1}C,\quad\mbox{ all }\lambda\in G,

for

, where

is a contractive or unitary colligation on

for these tangents

Such tangents can be described concretely as follows. For any hyperbolic automorphism

\mathbb{D}
(that is, any automorphism that has two fixed points

and
\omega_{2}
in

for some hyperbolic automorphism

of
\mathbb{D}

and some

is a rational inner function of degree at most

or
\bar{\omega}_{2}

\mathbb{D}

\Phi_{\omega}
solves
\Car\delta
if and only if

or
\bar{\omega}_{2}
.

Observe that if

\Car\delta
that are well aligned at
\delta
then any convex combination of

and
G
also solves
\Car\delta
. Hence, if
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
are the two values of
\omega

\mathbb{T}
for which

solves
\Car\delta

is any automorphism of
\mathbb{D}

\Car\delta

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\delta=(\lambda,v)
be a non-degenerate purely balanced tangent to
G
and let
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
be the two points

\Car\delta

Proof.

For

\sqrt{1-r}&-\sqrt{r}\end{pmatrix}

C&D\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}U_{r}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}&0\\
0&\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}U_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}r\varphi_{1}+(1-r)\varphi_{2}&\sqrt{r(1-r)}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})\\
\sqrt{r(1-r)}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})&(1-r)\varphi_{1}+r\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}

satisfies
\|T(s,p)\|<1
for all
(s,p)\in G
. It follows from properties of linear fractional transformations (Corollary
4.1
and Remark

) that, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(G)

\displaystyle F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}_{T}\circ\psi

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}A+B\psi(1-D\psi)^{-1}C

\displaystyle=r\varphi_{1}+(1-r)\varphi_{2}+r(1-r)\frac{(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})^{2}\psi}{1-[(1-r)\varphi_{1}+r\varphi_{2}]\psi}

(3.3)

G
and maps
G
to
\mathbb{D}

are well aligned at
\delta
we have

is a holomorphic map from

\mathbb{D}
such that

solves
\Car\delta
and is well aligned at
\delta
.
∎

Proof.

-model for a function

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\geq 1-|F(\lambda)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}(1-|\Phi_{\eta}(\lambda)|^{2})\,\left\langle\mathrm{d}E(\eta)u(\lambda),u(\lambda)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\eta}(1-|\Phi_{\eta}(\lambda)|^{2})\left\langle E(\mathbb{T})u(\lambda),u(\lambda)\right\rangle

Inequality (

4.
Contractive linear fractional transformations

This section is an appendix in which we recall an identity about linear fractional transformations of operators. We presume that
this identity has long been known. A special case of it was stated by C. L. Siegel in
[
19
]
, and Siegel may well have known
the identity in its general form. We stated the identity without proof in previous publications, see
[
7
]
. We were unable to find a proof of the identity in the literature, therefore, we refer the reader to a proof of it in

[
9
, Section B.2]
. The proof is by straightforward algebraic calculation, but is not entirely trivial to work out from scratch.

Let
H,G,U
and
V
be Hilbert spaces. Let
P
be an operator such that

P=\begin{bmatrix}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{bmatrix}:H\oplus U\to G\oplus V

and let
X:V\to U
be an operator for which
I-P_{22}X
is invertible in the space
\mathcal{B}(V)
of bounded linear operators on
V
. Then
we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)
the linear fractional transformation

\mathcal{F}_{P}(X):=P_{11}+P_{12}X(I-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}:H\to G.

Proposition 4.1
.

[
7
, Lemma 1.7]

Let
H,G,U
and
V
be Hilbert spaces. Let

P=\begin{bmatrix}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }Q=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{11}&Q_{12}\\
Q_{21}&Q_{22}\end{bmatrix}

be operators from
H\oplus U
to
G\oplus V
, and
let
X
and
Y
be operators from
V
to
U
for which
I-P_{22}X
and
I-Q_{22}Y
are invertible in
\mathcal{B}(V)
.
Then

\displaystyle I_{H}-\mathcal{F}_{Q}(Y)^{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)=

\displaystyle=Q^{*}_{21}(I_{V}-Y^{*}Q^{*}_{22})^{-1}(I_{V}-Y^{*}X)(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}&Q^{*}_{21}(I_{V}-Y^{*}Q^{*}_{22})^{-1}Y^{*}\end{bmatrix}(I_{H\oplus U}-Q^{*}P)\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}\\
X(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}\end{bmatrix}.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
H,G,U
and
V
be Hilbert spaces. Let
P
be an operator such that

P=\begin{bmatrix}P_{11}&P_{12}\\
P_{21}&P_{22}\end{bmatrix}:H\oplus U\to G\oplus V

and
||P||<1
. Let
X:V\to U
be an operator such that
||X||\leq 1
and
I-P_{22}X
is invertible.
Then
||\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)||<1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
,

\displaystyle I_{H}-\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)^{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)=

\displaystyle=P^{*}_{21}(I_{V}-X^{*}P^{*}_{22})^{-1}(I_{V}-X^{*}X)(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}&P^{*}_{21}(I_{V}-X^{*}P^{*}_{22})^{-1}X^{*}\end{bmatrix}(I_{H\oplus U}-P^{*}P)\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}\\
X(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
A=(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}:H\to V
and

B=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}\\
X(I_{V}-P_{22}X)^{-1}P_{21}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H}\\
XA\end{bmatrix}:H\to H\oplus U.

Then

I_{H}-\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)=A^{*}(I_{V}-X^{*}X)A+B^{*}(I_{H\oplus U}-P^{*}P)B.

Since
||X||\leq 1
,
B
has zero kernel and
||P||<1
,
we have

A^{*}(I_{V}-X^{*}X)A\geq 0\text{ and }B^{*}(I_{H\oplus U}-P^{*}P)B>0.

Thus

I_{H}-\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)>0.

Therefore
||\mathcal{F}_{P}(X)||<1
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose, in addition,
||X||<1
in Corollary
4.1
. Then

||P_{22}X||\leq||P_{22}||\,||X||\leq||X||<1

and so
I_{V}-P_{22}X
is automatically invertible.

Remark 4.3
.

Suppose, in addition,
H=G=\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
U=V
and
X=z\cdot I_{V}
in Corollary
4.1
. Then,
by Remark
4.2
,
I_{V}-zP_{22}
is invertible for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Moreover,
the linear fractional transformation
\mathcal{F}_{P}
, given by

\mathcal{F}_{P}(z)=P_{11}+zP_{12}(I_{V}-zP_{22})^{-1}P_{21}\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D},

is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
.
