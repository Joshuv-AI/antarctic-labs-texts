On the Maximum Linear Arrangement Problem for Trees
Lluís Alemany-Puig, Juan Luis Esteban, Ramon Ferrer-i-Cancho

Abstract
Linear arrangements of graphs are a well-known type of graph labeling and are found in many important computational problems, such as the Minimum Linear Arrangement Problem ( minLA ). A linear arrangement is usually defined as a permutation of the n n vertices of a graph. An intuitive geometric setting is that of vertices lying on consecutive integer positions in the real line, starting at 1; edges are often drawn as semicircles above the real line. In this paper we study the Maximum Linear Arrangement problem ( MaxLA ), the maximization variant of minLA . We devise a new characterization of maximum arrangements of general graphs, and prove that MaxLA can be solved for cycle graphs in constant time, and for k k -linear trees ( k ≤ 2 k\leq 2 ) in time O ⁡ ( n ) O{\left(n\right)} . We present two constrained variants of MaxLA we call bipartite MaxLA and 1-thistle MaxLA . We prove that the former can be solved in time O ⁡ ( n ) O{\left(n\right)} for any bipartite graph; the latter, by an algorithm that typically runs in time O ⁡ ( n 4 ) O{\left(n^{4}\right)} on unlabelled trees. The combination of the two variants has two promising characteristics. First, it solves MaxLA for almost all trees consisting of a few tenths of nodes. Second, we prove that it constitutes a 3 / 2 3/2 -approximation algorithm for MaxLA for trees. Furthermore, we conjecture that bipartite MaxLA solves MaxLA for at least 50 % 50\% of all free trees.

Arrangement isomorphism

Since we consider two arrangements

3.2
A new property

Here we identify a new property of maximum arrangements of graphs concerning the distribution of the vertices of branchless paths in maximum arrangements. In these paths of
k

k-2
vertices have degree exactly
2
, and the endpoints have degree different from
2
. More formally, a branchless path
P
(of
k-1
edges) with vertices
(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
of a graph

is a maximal sequence of vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in V
such that

1.

\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}\in E
for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
,

2.

d(u_{i})=2
for all
2\leq i\leq k-1
, and

3.

d(u_{1})\neq 2
and
d(u_{k})\neq 2
.

We refer to the vertices
\{u_{2},\dots,u_{k-1}\}
of

as the

of the path. The

of these paths is measured in edges and equal to

. We say that a branchless path is a

if

and

. We say that a branchless path is an

if

or

Now follows a characterization of the level values of the vertices of branchless paths in a maximum arrangement of a given graph. In particular, we focus on the internal vertices of paths (those of degree
2
). For this, we define

as those vertices whose neighbors are arranged to both sides of

in the arrangement. More formally, a vertex

is a

if

. Notice that a vertex

of degree

can only have three possible level values in an arbitrary arrangement:
\pm 2
, and

(the only case in which

is a thistle). Leaves, by definition, can only have level values
\pm 1
.

4
Bipartite and non-bipartite arrangements

In this section we introduce two types of arrangements,
bipartite
and
non-bipartite
arrangements, which split the whole set of
n!
arrangements of any bipartite graph and help us tackle
MaxLA
methodically. Obviously, each division contains arrangements that are maximum among arrangements of the same type. Thus, we speak of
maximal bipartite arrangement
(resp.
maximal non-bipartite arrangement
) to refer to an arrangement that is maximum among bipartite (resp. non-bipartite) arrangements. In order to ease our discourse, we say that a graph
G
is (resp. not) maximizable by a specific arrangement
\pi
if its set of maximum arrangements
\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
contains (resp. does not contain)
\pi
. More specifically, we say that
a graph
G
is maximizable by a bipartite arrangement
(resp.
non-bipartite arrangement
) if
\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
contains a bipartite (resp. non-bipartite) arrangement. While an arrangement (of any type) can be maximal (maximum in its own part) it needs not be maximum among all
n!
arrangements.
Figure

5
illustrates all this terminology. Since the union of all bipartite and non-bipartite arrangements make up the whole space of
n!
arrangements, it is easy to see that the solution to
MaxLA
is simply the maximum between a maximal bipartite arrangement and a maximal non-bipartite arrangement. For the sake of brevity, we refer to the cost of a maximum arrangement of
G
, that is, the value of
MaxLA
of
G
, as
the maximum cost
of
G
; we refer to the cost of a maximal bipartite arrangement (resp. maximal non-bipartite arrangement) of a tree
G
as
the maximal bipartite cost
(resp.
the maximal non-bipartite cost
) of
G
. The motivation for this division lies in the fact, proven later in this section, that any maximal bipartite arrangement of any
n
-vertex tree is constructible in time and space
O{\left(n\right)}
. And, crucially, the fact that the proportion of trees that are maximizable by a bipartite arrangement over all
n
-vertex trees is large as we show later.

We start by formally defining the concepts of bipartite and non-bipartite arrangements in
Section

4.1
. In
Section

4.2
we move on to proving that maximal bipartite arrangements of
n
-vertex trees can be constructed in time and space
O{\left(n\right)}
; in
Section

4.3
, we formally present two conjectures concerning the proportion of trees that are maximizable by a bipartite arrangement over all
n
-vertex trees, as
n
tends to infinity. In
Section

4.4
we devise an algorithm to construct a special case of maximal non-bipartite arrangements which will be useful in further sections. Finally, in
Section

4.5
we do an empirical study of the distance (in cost) between a maximal bipartite arrangement and a maximum arrangement (when these two do not coincide).

4.1
Formal definitions

We say that a linear arrangement
\pi
of a bipartite graph
B=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E)
is a
bipartite arrangement
if for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V_{1}
there is no vertex
w\in V_{2}
such that
\pi(u)<\pi(w)<\pi(v)
. Thus

\max_{u\in V_{1}}\{\pi(u)\}<\min_{v\in V_{2}}\{\pi(v)\}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\max_{v\in V_{2}}\{\pi(v)\}<\min_{u\in V_{1}}\{\pi(u)\}.

Equivalently, let
V_{1}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n_{1}}\}
and
V_{2}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n_{2}}\}
. The linear ordering of the vertices in a bipartite arrangement of a bipartite graph
B
is one of the following two

(u_{i_{1}},\cdots,u_{i_{p}},v_{j_{1}},\cdots,v_{j_{q}}),\qquad(v_{j_{1}},\cdots,v_{j_{q}},u_{i_{1}},\cdots,u_{i_{p}}),

where
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\}
is a permutation of
\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
, and
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}
is a permutation of
\{1,\dots,n_{2}\}
. A
non-bipartite arrangement
of a graph is simply an arrangement that is not bipartite.

We denote the set of bipartite arrangements of a graph
G
as
\mathcal{B}(G)
. The maximum sum of edge lengths over bipartite arrangements, that is, the solution to
bipartite MaxLA
, is denoted as
M_{\mathrm{bip}}[D(G)]
, and the set of maximal bipartite arrangements as
\mathcal{B}^{*}(G)
. Formally,

M_{\mathrm{bip}}[D(G)]=\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{B}(G)}\{D_{\pi}(G)\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}^{*}(G)=\{\pi\in\mathcal{B}(G)\;|\;D_{\pi}(G)=M_{\mathrm{bip}}[D(G)]\}.

Obviously, if
G
is not bipartite,
\mathcal{B}(G)=\emptyset
and thus
M_{\mathrm{bip}}[D(G)]=0
.

Similarly, we denote the set of non-bipartite arrangements of a graph
G
as
\overline{\mathcal{B}}(G)
. The maximum sum of edge lengths over non-bipartite arrangements, that is, the solution to
non-bipartite MaxLA
, is denoted as
M_{\text{non-bip}}[D(G)]
, and the set of maximal non-bipartite arrangements as
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(G)
. Formally,

M_{\text{non-bip}}[D(G)]=\max_{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}(G)}\{D_{\pi}(G)\},\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(G)=\{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}(G)\;|\;D_{\pi}(G)=M_{\text{non-bip}}[D(G)]\}.

Then, for any given graph
G
,

M[D(G)]=\max\{M_{\mathrm{bip}}[D(G)],M_{\text{non-bip}}[D(G)]\}.

If a graph
G
is maximizable by a bipartite (resp. non-bipartite) arrangement then
\mathcal{B}^{*}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
). If
G
is maximizable
only
by a bipartite (resp. non-bipartite) arrangement then
\mathcal{B}^{*}(G)=\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(G)=\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
). If
G
is maximizable by both bipartite and non-bipartite arrangements then
\mathcal{B}^{*}(G)\cup\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(G)=\mathcal{D}^{*}(G)
.

It is easy to see that vertices in any bipartite arrangement
\pi
always have as level value their degree (or minus degree). More formally, for every
u\in V
,
|l_{\pi}{\left(u\right)}|=d(u)
. This basic fact follows from the definition of bipartite arrangements: all neighbors of
u
are all arranged to either its left or to its right. On the other hand, many non-bipartite arrangements will contain at least one thistle vertex. Notice that there exist non-bipartite arrangements that do not contain thistle vertices.
5
5

5

Consider, for instance, the
4
-vertex path graph
\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,4\}
and the arrangement
(2,1,4,3)
. This arrangement is not bipartite but it does not contain any thistle vertex.
However, non-bipartite arrangements with no thistle vertices do not have a monotonic level signature, thus do not satisfy
Section

3.1
, and therefore cannot be maximum. Because of this, non-bipartite arrangements with no thistle vertices do not play any role in this article. Henceforth we use
{\pi}_{B}^{*}
to denote a maximal bipartite arrangement, and
{\pi}_{\overline{B}}^{*}
to denote a maximal non-bipartite arrangement.

We now define yet another variant of
MaxLA
, a generalization of
bipartite MaxLA
, whereby the maximum is taken over all (non-bipartite) arrangements with exactly one thistle vertex, the set of which is denoted as
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1t}(G)
. We call this variant
1-thistle MaxLA
and define it as

M_{\mathrm{1}t}[D(G)]=\max_{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1t}(G)}\{D_{\pi}(G)\},\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1t}^{*}(G)=\{\pi\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1t}(G)\;|\;D_{\pi}(G)=M_{\mathrm{1}t}[D(G)]\}.

But not all vertices of a tree can be thistle vertices in a maximum arrangement. We say that a vertex of a tree has the potential to be a thistle (in a maximum arrangement) if no reason is known to discard it as one; for example, the leaves of a tree cannot be thistles by definition of leaf, and neither can the internal vertices of antennas (
Lemma

3.2
(
i
)). In the case of bridges, every vertex has the potential of being a thistle (Lemma
3.2
(
ii
)). Even when a tree has many potential thistles, not all of them need to be thistles in the same maximum arrangement, nor all of them are thistles in some maximum arrangement. It is important to bear in mind that the property of being a thistle vertex depends on its position in an arrangement and it is unknown if that can be determined using the graph’s structure alone in all cases. Accordingly, we define
\Psi(T)
as the
number of potential thistle vertices of a tree
.

Property 4.0
.

The number of potential thistle vertices of a tree
T
is

\Psi(T)=H(T)+I(T),

(23)

where
H(T)
is the number of vertices of degree
\geq 3
and
I(T)
is the number of bridges.

Proof.

The leaves of the tree cannot be thistles by definition. Due to
Lemma

3.2
(
i
), the internal vertices of antennas cannot be thistles. While all the internal vertices of a bridge may be thistles, due to Lemma
3.2
(
ii
) at most one of them can be a thistle in a maximum arrangement. The remaining vertices are those of degree
\geq 3
. Hence the property.
∎

4.2
Bipartite MaxLA

Now we prove that a maximal bipartite arrangement of any
n

Proof.

Let

and thus we have that the vertices in

to each side of

Now, let

be the closest vertex to

to its left in the arrangement; let

be the closest vertex to

to its right in the arrangement. In order to prove the claim it remains to show that

. Let

be the set of vertices between

and

in

; similarly, let

be the set of vertices between

and

in

(

and

By way of contradiction, suppose that

. We have to show that by either moving

is not maximal. This displacement is implemented as a sequence of swaps of consecutive vertices in the arrangement. For this, we apply

(

•

Consider the case in which

. Then, we have that

as well, and

. It is easy to see that

. Notice that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\pi}(u\leftarrow v)

\displaystyle=l_{\pi}{\left(v\right)}-l_{\pi}{\left(t\right)}+\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}\left(l_{\pi}{\left(v\right)}-l_{\pi}{\left(w_{\alpha-i+1}\right)}\right)+l_{\pi}{\left(v\right)}-l_{\pi}{\left(u\right)}

\displaystyle=l_{\pi}{\left(v\right)}-(\alpha+2)l_{\pi}{\left(u\right)}+l_{\pi}{\left(t\right)}+\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}l_{\pi}{\left(w_{i}\right)}.

\displaystyle<0,

Next we prove a result that highlights an interesting substructural organization of the vertices in maximal non-bipartite arrangements of exactly one thistle vertex.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\pi
be a maximal arrangement of a tree
T
with exactly one thistle vertex, say
t
. By removing
t
from
T
we obtain
d(t)
connected components
T_{i}
each of which is arranged in a maximal bipartite arrangement in
\pi
. That is, the arrangements
\pi(T_{i})
are maximal bipartite arrangements.

Proof.

Recall that
T
is a connected bipartite graph and thus exists a unique proper two-coloring. To prove the statement in the corollary, we focus on a specific
T_{i}
; let
w_{i}
be such that
w_{i}\in\Gamma(t)
and
w_{i}\in T_{i}
. Let
\mathrm{sgn}
be the sign function so that
\mathrm{sgn}(x)
denotes the sign of
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is easy to see that
\mathrm{sgn}(l_{\pi}{\left(w_{i}\right)})=\mathrm{sgn}(l_{\pi}{\left(u\right)})
, for all
u\in T_{i}\setminus\{w_{i}\}
of the same color as
w_{i}
, and
\mathrm{sgn}(l_{\pi}{\left(w_{i}\right)})=-\mathrm{sgn}(l_{\pi}{\left(v\right)})
for all
v\in T_{i}\setminus\{w_{i}\}
of the other color. Now, all non-neighbors of
t
are arranged by non-increasing degree (
Lemma

4.2
), thus no vertex

is found between two vertices

, and vice versa. Since all the vertices to each side of
t
are arranged by non-increasing degree (
Section

4.4
), vertex
w_{i}
is arranged in
\pi
in the correct position as per a non-increasing degree order among all vertices to the same side of
t
as
w_{i}
and thus among all vertices of
T_{i}
of the same color as
w_{i}
. Thus
\pi(T_{i})
is a maximal bipartite arrangement.
∎

This observation needs not hold for bipartite graphs with cycles, or more general graphs, since the removal of these vertices may not disconnect the graph. Notice that

does not involve maximal non-bipartite arrangements with more than one thistle vertex. Furthermore, although

applies only to

Next we devise an algorithm to construct a maximal non-bipartite arrangement of a tree such that the arrangement only has one thistle vertex which is given as part of the input. This algorithm is particularly useful to construct maximum arrangements of trees that are known to have at most one thistle vertex (
2
-linear trees,

). We use it later to tackle

.

Theorem 4.3
.

Algorithm

4.2
constructs a maximal non-bipartite arrangement of any
n
-vertex tree
T=(V,E)
with a single, given thistle vertex
t\in V
in time
O{\left(n^{2}2^{d}\right)}
, where
d=d(t)
, and space
O{\left(n\right)}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma(t)=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\}
be the neighbors of
t
and let
T_{1},\dots,T_{d}
be the connected components of
T
obtained after removing
t
from
T
; let component
T_{i}
contain vertex
v_{i}
. The central idea behind this algorithm is, given an assignment
b\;:\;\Gamma(t)\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}
of the side of the thistle to which each
v_{i}
goes (
-1
means
left
,
+1
means
right
), to construct a maximal arrangement in which the neighbors of
t
are placed according to
b
. As explained in
Section

4.4
, the remaining vertices are distributed so that
T_{1},\dots,T_{d}
are arranged in maximal bipartite arrangements. To construct this maximal non-bipartite arrangement with
b
one has to figure out the optimal placement of the vertices in
V^{\prime}=V\setminus(\{t\}\cup\Gamma(t))
with respect to
t
. The maximal non-bipartite arrangement of
T
with
t
as its only thistle vertex is the arrangement with the highest cost over all possible assignments
b
.

Each assignment
b
determines the level value each vertex has to have; we use
l_{b}\;:\;V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
to denote such level values. The level of
t
is, simply,

l_{b}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b(v_{i}).

Since the best assignment of sides to the neighbors of
t
is not known, the algorithm tries all
2^{d}
possible assignments. However, some of these assignments can be filtered out, such as those for which
|l_{b}(t)|=d
since
t
is not a thistle, and those for which
l_{b}(t)<0
since there exists a symmetric assignment
b^{\prime}
(by simple mirroring) such that
b^{\prime}(u)=-b(u)
(for any
u\in V
) and thus
l_{b}(t)=-l_{b^{\prime}}(t)
. Notice that the vertices in
V\setminus\{t\}
cannot be thistles by assumption and, as such, we must have that
|l_{b}(u)|=d(u)
; we only have to determine the sign of the level value. For any
v_{i}\in\Gamma(t)
, let
l_{b}(v_{i})=-b(v_{i})\cdot d(v_{i})
. The level value of the rest of the vertices switches sign at every level of a breadth first traversal in the subtree
{T}^{t}_{v_{i}}
, starting at
v_{i}
, compared to that in the previous level.

Therefore, the goal is, for a fixed thistle vertex
t
and assignment
b
, to construct an arrangement
\pi
that is maximal among those arrangements such that

•

The neighbors of
t
are placed in the arrangement according to
b
, and thus
t
has level value
l_{b}(t)
.

•

The rest of the vertices have level value as specified by
l_{b}
(otherwise, other thistles would be present).

•

Sections

4.4
and
4.2
.

.

1.

First, place the vertices in

t
and, lastly, place the vertices in

with negative level value in non-increasing order. Let
\pi
be the resulting arrangement. Notice that this initial arrangement satisfies the three conditions above. This step can be done in time

using counting sort
[
20

.

2.

Permute all sequences of consecutive vertices of equal, and positive level value in

so that we first find the neighbors of the thistle (if any) and then the non-neighbors (if any). Notice that

can be in one of these sequences if the vertex immediately to its left in

. This step can be seen as a normalization step that guarantees correctness of the next step. This can be done in time

using counting sort
[
20
]
, by assigning the value
0
to the neighbors of the thistle, the value

to the thistle, and the value

to the other vertices.

After Steps

are arranged in a maximal bipartite arrangement with respect to their connected component, and vertex

, but

may not be in its optimal position with respect to the vertices in

. In order to find the maximal arrangement for fixed

and

, one has to figure out how to slide vertices in
V^{\prime}
in the arrangement while keeping their relative order among them as per

4.2
. To achieve this, it is sufficient to move vertices in
V^{\prime}
located at the left side of
t
to the right of
t
, one vertex at a time, and starting at the rightmost vertex in
V^{\prime}
located at the left side of

.

Notice that there is no need to also try and move vertices from
V^{\prime}
to the left of
t
since we either have that
l_{b}(t)>0
and this would decrease the cost of the arrangement, or
l_{b}(t)=0
and there exists another assignment

for which all the neighbors of
t
to its right in

are assigned to its left in
b^{\prime}
and vice versa.

3.

Now, let

be the result of the previous two steps, and of the form

\pi=(\dots,\underbrace{u_{1},\dots,u_{\alpha}}_{\in\Gamma(t)},\overbrace{v_{1},\dots,v_{\beta}}^{\in V^{\prime}},\underbrace{w_{1},\dots,w_{\gamma}}_{\in\Gamma(t)},\overbrace{y_{1},\dots,y_{\delta}}^{\in V^{\prime}},t,\dots),

where
v_{i},y_{i}\in V^{\prime}
,
u_{i},w_{i}\in\Gamma(t)
, and

For the sake of brevity, we use
l_{b}(u)
to denote
l_{b}(u_{k})
for all

,

l_{b}(v)
to denote
l_{b}(v_{k})
for all

,
l_{b}(w)
to denote
l_{b}(w_{k})
for all

to denote

for all

.

The next step is to relocate vertices in

, so as to increase the cost of the arrangement. That is, in

V^{\prime}
closest to

to its left and relocate it. The first such vertex in

.

,

,

, where

and the vertices

does not increase the cost of

(

) then further relocating

in
\pi^{\prime\prime}
, thus producing

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}(l_{b}(s_{i})-l_{b}(r_{k}))+l_{b}(t)-l_{b}(r_{k})

\displaystyle=-(\nu+1)l_{b}(r)+\nu\cdot l_{b}(s)+l_{b}(t),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\theta}(l_{b}(q_{i})-l_{b}(p_{k}))+\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}(l_{b}(s_{i})-l_{b}(p_{k}))+l_{b}(t)-l_{b}(p_{k})

Prior to proving that spider graphs are maximizable only by bipartite arrangements we define a simple, yet useful tool concerning the displacement of a single vertex.

Moving a single vertex

Here we want to study the rate of change of the sum of the lengths of the edges incident to a vertex

In this article we have put forward a new property of maximum arrangements of graphs. In

\varphi\;:\;V(G)\rightarrow V(H)

We keep track of the assigned and unassigned vertices in an array of

These sets are illustrated in

and are calculated while updating the sets

A.3.1
Concerning optimality

First, the algorithm ensures that

Besides, the algorithm does not let the arrangement become bipartite: since the algorithm already contains a maximal bipartite arrangement (or a better one) in its set of maximum arrangements found, no bipartite arrangement will ever surpass the initial maximum. This is also checked in constant time.

A.3.2
Concerning symmetries

Now follow the constraints implemented for symmetry breaking, a strategy applied to avoid constructing level-isomorphic arrangements.

First, the first vertex added to the arrangement is the lowest-indexed vertex in its vertex orbit of the tree. That is, the B&B is started only at as many vertices as orbits there are in the tree, and only the lowest-indexed vertex is used. Notice that, by definition of orbit, the search space that unfolds by starting at a vertex of an orbit yields arrangements that are edge-isomorphic to those yielded by the search space that unfolds by starting at any other vertex of the same orbit. For instance, for the path tree in
Figure

21
(top left) the algorithm will only use 4 vertices for the first position of the arrangement (one of the vertices labeled with
1
, one of
2
, one of
3

4
). The B&B algorithm is parallelized only here at the vertex orbit level. Furthermore, only one of the internal vertices of each bridge is allowed to be the thistle vertex in the arrangement (Lemma
3.2
(

In a permutation where the non-leaf vertices of the tree are fixed, the
l(w)

The algorithm uses

Finally, the algorithm considers symmetries with respect to subtree isomorphism. Let

A.7
Estimation of the reduction of the search space

Deriving an expression for the exact reduction of the search space in this B&B is difficult. However, we can estimate an upper bound of this reduction as follows. Each of the following expressions denote the number of permutations that are
subtracted
from the total search space of
n!
permutations.

The first reduction of the search space is due to breaking symmetries using vertex orbits. Let
OrbRep(T)=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}
. It is easy to see that breaking symmetries using vertex orbits reduces the search space by

(n-1)!\sum_{i=1}^{k}(|Orb(u_{i})|-1)

(33)

permutations. Obviously, the more ‘symmetric’ a tree is, the less orbits the tree will have and thus the greater the reduction of the search space.

We also reduce the exploration of the search space by avoiding bipartite arrangements. Since one maximal bipartite arrangement is produced in the initialization step and none of this kind is explored, the search space is also reduced by all corresponding permutations, that is, by at most,

2\cdot|V_{1}|!\cdot|V_{2}|!

(34)

permutations. Notice, however, that some of these permutations are already accounted for in
Equation

33
. Therefore,
Equations

33
and
34
count many permutations twice.

Recall we also apply symmetry breaking among the leaves of a vertex by imposing a relative order as a function of their indices. That is, for every permutation where the non-leaf vertices of the tree are fixed and the leaves adjacent to the same vertex are allowed to permute freely among themselves, there is a total of
\prod_{w\in V}l(w)!
possible orderings of the leaves of the tree. Then, the search space is reduced by

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in V:\\
l(w)\geq 2\end{subarray}}(l(w)!-1)

(35)

permutations, for every possible ordering of the non-leaf vertices that are actually explored.

It is easy to see that large portions of the search space are removed from the exploration thanks to
Sections

3.1
,
3.1
and
3.1
. However, quantifying this reduction is out of the scope of this paper. The best we can say about the reduction due to
Section

3.1
is that the longer the intervals of vertices of equal level in an arrangement, the more permutations are avoided. Long intervals are more likely to arise when there are few different vertex degrees in the tree. This can be assessed using the variance of vertex degree of a tree defined as
\mathbb{V}[d_{T}]=\mathbb{E}[d_{T}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[d_{T}]^{2}
, where
\mathbb{E}[d_{T}]=2-2/n
is the expected vertex degree in a tree. The lower the variance of vertex degree of a tree, the more permutations are likely to be avoided in the search space of that tree. Notice that the variance of vertex degree seems to be concentrated around low values, regardless of
n
(
Figure

23
(a)). This can be assessed better by normalizing the variance. For this, we use the minimum and maximum values of the variance among
n
-vertex trees, that is, of linear trees (minimum) and star graphs (maximum)
[

]

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[d_{\mathrm{linear}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[d_{\mathrm{star}}]

\displaystyle=n-5+\frac{4}{n}\left(2-\frac{1}{n}\right)

to borrow the so-called

Notice that

C.2

[

We use non-linear regression with

function

[

to find the best values of

b
and
c
for each model in
