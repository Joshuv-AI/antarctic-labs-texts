Abstract
We study discrete probabilistic programs with potentially unbounded looping behaviors over an infinite state space. We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first decidability result for the problem of determining whether such a program generates exactly a specified distribution over its outputs (provided the program terminates almost-surely). The class of distributions that can be specified in our formalism consists of standard distributions (geometric, uniform, etc.) and finite convolutions thereof. Our method relies on representing these (possibly infinite-support) distributions as probability generating functions which admit effective arithmetic operations. We have automated our techniques in a tool called Prodigy , which supports automatic invariance checking, compositional reasoning of nested loops, and efficient queries to the output distribution, as demonstrated by experiments.

Introduction

Probabilistic programs
[
42
,
25
,
47
]
augment deterministic programs with stochastic behaviors, e.g., random sampling, probabilistic choice, and conditioning (via posterior observations).
Probabilistic programs have undergone a recent surge of interest due to prominent applications in a wide range of domains: they steer autonomous robots and self-driving cars
[
19
,
54
]
, are key to describe security
[
6

and quantum
[

]
mechanisms, intrinsically code up randomized algorithms for solving NP-hard or even deterministically unsolvable problems (in, e.g., distributed computing
[

,

), and are rapidly encroaching on AI as well as approximate computing
[
13
]
. See
[
5

for recent advancements in probabilistic programming.

The crux of probabilistic programming, à la Hicks’ interpretation
[
29
]
, is to
treat normal-looking programs as if they were probability distributions
. A random-number generator, for instance, is a probabilistic program that produces a uniform distribution across numbers from a range of interest.
Such a lift from deterministic program states to possibly infinite-support distributions (over states) renders the verification problem of probabilistic programs notoriously hard
[
38
]
.
In particular, reasoning about probabilistic loops often amounts to computing quantitative fixed-points which are highly intractable in practice.
As a consequence, existing techniques are mostly concerned with approximations, i.e., they strive for verifying or obtaining upper and/or lower bounds on various quantities like assertion-violation probabilities
[
59
]
, preexpectations
[
9
,
27
]
, moments
[
58
]
, expected runtimes
[
39
]
, and concentrations
[
15
,
16
]
, which reveal only partial information about the probability distribution carried by the program.

In this paper, we address the problem of
how to determine whether a (possibly infinite-state) probabilistic program yields exactly the desired (possibly infinite-support) distribution under all possible inputs
.
We highlight two scenarios where encoding the
exact
distribution – other than (bounds on) the above-mentioned quantities – is of particular interest:

(I)

In many safety- and/or security-critical domains, e.g., cryptography, a slightly perturbed distribution (while many of its probabilistic quantities remain unchanged) may lead to significant attack vulnerabilities or even complete compromise of the cryptographic system, see, e.g., Bleichenbacher’s biased-nonces attack
[
28
, Sect. 5.10]
against the probabilistic Digital Signature Algorithm. Therefore, the system designer has to impose a complete specification of the anticipated distribution produced by the probabilistic component.

(II)

In the context of quantitative verification, the user may be interested in multiple properties (of different types, e.g., the aforementioned quantities) of the output distribution carried by a probabilistic program.
In absence of the exact distribution, multiple analysis techniques – tailored to different types of properties – have to be applied in order to answer all queries from the user.

We further motivate our problem using a concrete example as follows.

Approach.

Given a probabilistic loop
L=\textup{{while}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\}
with guard
\varphi
and loop-free body
P
, we aim to determine whether
L
agrees with a specification
S
:

\displaystyle L=\textup{{while}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\sim}}\quad S~,

(
\star
)

namely, whether
L
yields – upon termination – exactly the same distribution as encoded by
S
under all possible program inputs. This problem is non-trivial:

(C1)

L
may induce an infinite state space and infinite-support distributions, thus making techniques like probabilistic bounded model checking
[
33
]
insufficient for verifying the property by means of unfolding the loop
L
.

(C2)

There is, to the best of our knowledge, a lack of non-trivial characterizations of
L
and
S
such that
Eq.

⋆

admits a decidability result.

(C3)

To decide
Eq.

⋆

– even for a loop-free program
L
– one has to account for infinitely or even uncountably many inputs such that
L
yields the same distribution as encoded by
S
when being deployed in all possible contexts.

We address
Item

(C1)
by exploiting the forward denotational semantics of probabilistic programs based on
probability generating function
(PGF) representations of (sub-)distributions
[
41
]
, which benefits crucially from closed-form (i.e., finite) PGF representations of possibly infinite-support distributions.
A probabilistic program
L
hence acts as a transformer
\left\llbracket L\right\rrbracket(\cdot)
that transforms an input PGF
g
into an output PGF
\left\llbracket L\right\rrbracket(g)
(as an instantiation of Kozen’s transformer semantics
[
42
]
).
In particular, we
interpret the specification
S
as a loop-free probabilistic program
I
.
Such an identification of specifications with programs has two important advantages:

(i)

we only need a single language to encode programs as well as specifications, and

(ii)

it enables compositional reasoning in a straightforward manner, in particular, the treatment of nested loops.

The problem of checking
L\sim S
then boils down to checking whether
L
and
I
transform every possible input PGF into the same output PGF:

\displaystyle\forall g\in\textnormal{{PGF}}\colon\quad\llbracket\underbrace{\textup{{while}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\}}_{L}\rrbracket(g)\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\quad\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket(g)~.

(
\dagger
)

As
I
is loop free,
Eq.

†

can be reduced to checking the equivalence of two
loop-free
probabilistic programs (cf.
Lemma

2
):

\displaystyle\forall g\in\textnormal{{PGF}}\colon\quad\left\llbracket\textup{{if}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\fatsemi I\}\>\textup{{else}}\>\{\textup{{skip}}\}\right\rrbracket(g)\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\quad\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket(g)~.

(
\ddagger
)

Now
Item

(C3)
applies since the universal quantification in
Eq.

‡

requires to determine the equivalence against infinitely many – possibly infinite-support – distributions over program states. We facilitate such an equivalence checking by developing a
second-order PGF
(SOP) semantics for probabilistic programs, which naturally extends the PGF semantics while allowing to reason about infinitely many PGF transformations simultaneously (see
Lemma

3
).

Finally, to obtain a decidability result (cf.
Item

(C2)
), we develop the
rectangular discrete probabilistic programming language
(
ReDiP
) – a variant of
pGCL

[
45
]
with syntactic restrictions to rectangular guards – featuring various nice properties, e.g., they inherently support i.i.d. sampling, and in particular, they
preserve closed-form PGF
when acting as PGF transformers. We show that
Eq.

‡

is decidable for
ReDiP
programs

and
I
if all the distribution statements therein have rational closed-form PGF

(cf.
Lemma

4
).
As a consequence,
Eq.

†

and thereby
Eq.

⋆

are decidable if

terminates almost-surely on all possible inputs

(cf.

Demonstration.

We have automated our techniques in a tool called
Prodigy
. As an example,
Prodigy
was able to verify, fully automatically in 25 milliseconds, that the implementation of the MCPT path generator with nested loops (in

which encodes the specification that, upon termination,

is distributed as the sum of
\mathtt{n}

With such an output distribution, multiple queries can be efficiently answered by applying standard PGF operations.
For example, the expected value and variance of the runtime are
\mathit{E}[\mathtt{c}]=n
and
\mathit{Var}[\mathtt{c}]=2n

\mathtt{c}=0

Contributions.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

The probabilistic programming language
ReDiP
and its forward denotational semantics as PGF transformers.
We show that loop-free
ReDiP
programs preserve closed-form PGF.

•

The notion of SOP that enables reasoning about infinitely many PGF transformations simultaneously.
We show that the problem of determining whether an infinite-state

•

The software tool
Prodigy
which supports automatic invariance checking on the source-code level; it allows reasoning about nested

Generating Functions

“A generating function is a clothesline on which we hang up a sequence of numbers for display.”

— H. S. Wilf, Generatingfunctionology
[
60
]

The method of
generating functions
(GF) is a vital tool in many areas of mathematics.
This includes in particular enumerative combinatorics
[
21
,
60
]
and – most relevant for this paper – probability theory
[
34
]
.
In the latter, the sequences
“hanging on the clotheslines”
happen to describe probability distributions over the non-negative integers
\mathbb{N}
, e.g.,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}},\ldots
(aka, the geometric distribution).

The most common way to relate an (infinite)
sequence
of numbers to a generating
function
relies on the familiar Taylor series expansion:
Given a sequence, for example
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}},\ldots
, find a function
x\mapsto f(x)
whose Taylor series around
x=0
uses the numbers in the sequence as coefficients.
In our example,

\frac{1}{2-x}~{}={}~\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}x+\frac{1}{8}x^{2}+\frac{1}{16}x^{3}+\frac{1}{32}x^{4}+\ldots~,

(1)

for all
|x|<2
, hence the
“clothesline”
used for hanging up
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}},\ldots
is the function
1/(2-x)
.
Note that the GF is a – from a purely syntactical point of view –
finite
object while the sequence it represents is
infinite
.
A key strength of this technique is that many meaningful operations on infinite series can be performed by manipulating an encoding GF (see
Table

1
for an overview and examples).
In other words, GF provide an
interface
to perform operations on and extract information from infinite sequences in an effective manner.

Towards our goal of encoding distributions over

Definition 1

(The Ring of FPS)
.

A

We denote FPS as

Let

The FPS

f~{}={}~\sum\nolimits_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}f(\sigma)\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}

\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}

The

For all

The Statement

4
Interpreting
ReDiP
with PGF

In this section, we explain the PGF-based semantics of our language which is given in the second column of
Table

2
.
The overall idea is to view a
ReDiP
-program
P
as a
distribution transformer

[
43
,
45
]
.
This means that the input to
P
is a
distribution
over initial program states (inputting a deterministic state is just the special case of a Dirac distribution), and the output is a distribution over final program states.
With this interpretation, if one regards distributions as
generalized program states

[
32
]
, a probabilistic program is actually
deterministic
:
The same input distribution always yields the same output distribution.
The goal of our PGF-based semantics is to construct an
interpreter
that executes a
ReDiP
-program statement-by-statement in forward direction, transforming one generalized program state into the next.
We stress that these generalized program states, or distributions, can be infinite-support in general.
For example, the program
{\mathtt{x}}\,:=\,{\mathtt{geometric}({0.5})}
outputs a geometric distribution – which has infinite support – on
\mathtt{x}
.

4.1
A Domain for Distribution Transformation

We now define a domain, i.e., an
ordered
structure, where our program’s in- and output distributions live.
Following the general idea of this paper, we encode them as PGF.
Let
\mathit{Vars}
be a fixed finite set of program variables
\mathtt{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{x}_{k}
and let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
be corresponding formal indeterminates.
We let
\textnormal{{PGF}}=\{g\in\mathbb{R}[[\mathbf{X}]]\mid g\text{ is a PGF}\}
denote the set of all PGF.
Recall that this also includes sub-PGF (
Definition

2
).
Further, we equip

with the pointwise order, i.e., we let
g\sqsubseteq f
iff
g(\sigma)\leq f(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.
It is clear that
(\textnormal{{PGF}},\sqsubseteq)
is a partial order that is moreover
\omega
-complete
, i.e., there exists a least element
0
and all ascending chains
\Gamma=\{g_{0}\sqsubseteq g_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots\}
in

have a least upper bound
\sup\Gamma\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
.
The maxima in
(\textnormal{{PGF}},\sqsubseteq)
are precisely the PGF which are not a sub-PGF.

Sequential Composition.

Conditional Branching.

To translate

We now focus on loopy programs

5.1
Fixed Point Induction

To apply fixed point induction, we need to lift our domain

from

.
This is because the functional

Recall that in contrast to this, the function

The order on

is obtained by lifting the order

on

This implies that

is also an

We can then show that

(see

With these properties, we obtain the following induction rule for upper bounds on

(Fixed Point Induction for Loops)

Further, let

Then

\displaystyle\Psi_{{\varphi},{P}}(\psi)~{}\sqsubseteq{}~\psi\qquad\textnormal{implies}\qquad\left\llbracket L\right\rrbracket~{}\sqsubseteq{}~\psi~.

The goal of the rest of the paper is to

To this end, we must somehow specify an

Since

Further, by definition

and thus the term

is also a PGF-transformer expressible as a

These observations and

Lemma 2

Then

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\textup{{if}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\fatsemi I\}\>\textup{{else}}\>\{\textup{{skip}}\}\right\rrbracket~{}\sqsubseteq{}~\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket\quad\textnormal{implies}\quad\left\llbracket L\right\rrbracket~{}\sqsubseteq{}~\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket~.

(3)

Further, if

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\textup{{if}}\>(\varphi)\>\{P\fatsemi I\}\>\textup{{else}}\>\{\textup{{skip}}\}\right\rrbracket~{}={}~\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket\quad\textnormal{iff}\quad\left\llbracket L\right\rrbracket~{}={}~\left\llbracket I\right\rrbracket~.

Lemma

If

We now present a solution to this problem.

5.2
Deciding Equivalence of Loop-free Programs

Even in the absence of loops, deciding if two given
ReDiP
-programs are equivalent is non-trivial as it requires reasoning about infinitely many – possibly infinite-support – distributions on program variables.
In this section, we first show that
\left\llbracket P_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket P_{2}\right\rrbracket
is

and

SOP: Second-Order PGF.

Our goal is to check if
\left\llbracket P_{1}\right\rrbracket(g)=\left\llbracket P_{2}\right\rrbracket(g)
for
all

g\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
.
To tackle this, we encode whole
sets
of PGF into a single object – an FPS we call
second-order PGF
(SOP).
To define SOP, we need a slightly more flexible view on FPS.
Recall from
Definition

1

.
Such an
f
can be viewed equivalently as an
l
-dim. array with
(k{-}l)
-dim. arrays as entries.
In the formal sum notation, this is reflected by partitioning
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
and viewing

as an FPS in
\mathbf{Y}

with coefficients that are FPS in the other indeterminates
\mathbf{Z}
.
For example,

\displaystyle(1-Y)^{-1}(1-Z)^{-1}

\displaystyle~{}={}~1+Y+Z+Y^{2}+YZ+Z^{2}+\ldots

\displaystyle~{}={}~(1-Z)^{-1}+(1-Z)^{-1}Y+(1-Z)^{-1}Y^{2}+\ldots

where in the lower line the coefficients
(1{-}Z)^{-1}
are considered elements in
\mathbb{R}[[{Z}]]
.

Definition 5

(SOP)
.

Let
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{X}
be disjoint sets of indeterminates.
A formal power series
f\in\mathbb{R}[[\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X}]]
is a
second-order PGF (SOP)
if

f=\sum\nolimits_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{|{\mathbf{U}}|}}f(\tau)\mathbf{U}^{\tau}\quad(\text{with }f(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]])\qquad\textnormal{implies}\qquad\forall\tau\colon f(\tau)\in\textnormal{{PGF}}~.

That is, an SOP is simply an FPS whose coefficients are PGF – instead of generating a sequence of probabilities as PGF do, it generates a
sequence of distributions
.
An (important) example SOP is

\displaystyle f_{\mathit{dirac}}~{}={}~(1-XU)^{-1}~{}={}~1+XU+X^{2}U^{2}+\ldots~\in\mathbb{R}[[{U,X}]]~,

(5)

i.e., for all

.
As a second example consider

; it is clear that
f_{\mathit{binom}}(i)=(0.5+0.5X)^{i}=\left\llbracket\mathtt{binomial}({0.5},\,{i})\right\rrbracket
for all
i\geq 0
.
Note that if
\mathbf{U}=\emptyset

For fixed
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{U}
, we denote the set of all second-order PGF with

.

SOP Semantics of
ReDiP
.

The appeal of SOP is that, syntactically, they are still formal power series, and some can be represented in closed form just like PGF.
Moreover, we can readily extend our PGF transformer
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket
to an SOP transformer
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket\colon\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP}
.
A key insight of this paper is that – without any changes to the rules in
Table

\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket
to an SOP is the same as applying
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket

simultaneously
to all the PGF it subsumes:

Theorem 5.1

Let
P
be a
ReDiP
-program.
The transformer
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket\colon\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP}
is well-defined.
Further, if

is an SOP, then

\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket(f)~{}={}~\sum\nolimits_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{|{\mathbf{U}}|}}\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket(f(\tau))\mathbf{U}^{\tau}~.

Related Work

This section surveys research efforts that are highly related to our approach in terms of semantics, inference, and equivalence checking of probabilistic programs.

Forward Semantics of Probabilistic Programs.

Kozen established in his seminal work
[
42
]
a generic way of giving forward, denotational semantics to probabilistic programs as
distribution transformers
. Klinkenberg et al.
[
41
]
instantiated Kozen’s semantics as PGF transformers. We refine the PGF semantics substantially such that it enjoys the following crucial properties:

(i)

our PGF transformers (when restricted to loop-free
ReDiP
programs) preserve closed-form PGF and thus are effectively constructable. In contrast, the existing PGF semantics in
[
41
]
operates on infinite sums in a non-constructive fashion;

(ii)

our PGF semantics naturally extends to SOP, which serves as the key to reason about the exact behavior of unbounded loops (under possibly uncountably many inputs) in a fully automatic manner. The PGF semantics in
[
41
]
, however, supports only (over-)approximations of looping behaviors and can hardly be automated; and

(iii)

our PGF semantics is capable of interpreting program constructs like i.i.d. sampling that is of particular interest in practice.

Backward Semantics of Probabilistic Programs.

Many verification systems for probabilistic programs make use of backward, denotational semantics – most pertinently, the
weakest preexpectation
(WP) calculi
[
45
,
37
]
as a quantitative extension of Dijkstra’s weakest preconditions
[
18
]
. The WP of a probabilistic program
C
w.r.t. a postexpectation
g
, denoted by
\textsf{wp}\llbracket C\rrbracket(g)(\cdot)
, maps every initial program state
\sigma
to the expected value of
g
evaluated in final states reached after executing
C
on
\sigma
. In contrast to Dijkstra’s predicate transformer semantics which admits also strongest postconditions, the counterpart of
“strongest postexpectations”
does unfortunately not exist
[
35
, Chap. 7]
, thereby not amenable to forward reasoning. We remark, in particular, that checking program equivalence via WP is difficult, if not impossible, since it amounts to reasoning about uncountably many postexpectations
g
.
We refer interested readers to
[
5
, Chaps. 1–4]
for more recent advancements in formal semantics of probabilistic programs.

Probabilistic Inference.

There are a handful of probabilistic systems that employ an alternative forward semantics based on
probability density function
(PDF) representations of distributions, e.g., (
\lambda
)PSI
[
23
,
24
]
, AQUA
[
31
]
, Hakaru
[
14
,
51
]
, and the density compiler in
[
12
,
11
]
. These systems are dedicated to probabilistic inference for programs encoding continuous distributions (or joint discrete-continuous distributions). Reasoning about the underlying PDF representations, however, amounts to resolving complex integral expressions in order to answer inference queries, thus confining these techniques either to (semi-)numerical methods
[
31
,
14
,
51
,
12
,
11
]
or exact methods yet limited to bounded looping bahaviors
[
23
,
24
]
. Apart from these inference systems, a recently developed language called Dice
[
30
]
featuring exact inference for discrete probabilistic programs is also confined to statically bounded loops. The tool Mora
[
8
,
7
]
supports exact inference for various types of Bayesian networks, but relies on a restricted form of intermediate representation known as prob-solvable loops, whose behaviors can be expressed by a system of C-finite recurrences admitting closed-form solutions.

Equivalence of Probabilistic Programs.

Murawski and Ouaknine
[
50
]
showed an
Exptime
decidability result for checking the equivalence of probabilistic programs over
finite
data types by recasting the problem in terms of probabilistic finite automata
[
56
,
40
,
22
]
. Their techniques have been automated in the equivalence checker APEX
[
44
]
. Barthe et al.
[
4
]
proved a 2-
Exptime
decidability result for checking equivalence of
straight-line
probabilistic programs (with deterministic inputs and no loops nor recursion) interpreted over all possible extensions of a finite field. Barthe et al.
[
3
]
developed a relational Hoare logic for probabilistic programs, which has been extensively used for, amongst others, proving program equivalence with applications in provable security and side-channel analysis.

The decidability result established in this paper is
orthogonal
to the aforementioned results:

(i)

our decidability for checking
L\sim S
applies to discrete probabilistic programs
L
with
unbounded
looping behaviors over a possibly
infinite
state space; the specification
S
– though, admitting no loops – encodes a possibly
infinite-support
distribution; yet as a compromise,

(ii)

our decidability result is confined to
ReDiP
programs that necessarily terminate almost-surely on all inputs, and involve only distributions with rational closed-form PGF.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We showed the decidability of – and have presented a fully-automated technique to verifying – whether a (possibly unbounded) probabilistic loop is equivalent to a loop-free specification program.
Future directions include determining the complexity of our decision problem; amending the method to continuous distributions using, e.g.,
characteristic functions
; extending the notion of probabilistic equivalence to probabilistic refinements; exploring PGF-based counterexample-guided synthesis of quantitative loop invariants (see
Section

0.F.6
for generating counterexamples); and tackling Bayesian inference.

This paper fundamentally relies on two basic fixed point theoretic principles:
(i) Kleene’s fixed point theorem and
(ii) a variant of a fixed point induction theorem that is sometimes referred to as
Park induction

[
52
]
.

Recall that
(A,\sqsubseteq)
, where
A
is a set and
\sqsubseteq
is a partial order on
A
, is an
\omega
-complete partial order (
\omega
-cpo) if there exists a least element
\bot\in A
and for all infinite ascending chains
a_{0}\sqsubseteq a_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
there exists a least upper bound (supremum)
\sup_{i\geq 0}a_{i}\in A
.
Further, a function
f\colon A\to A
is continuous if for all chains
a_{0}\sqsubseteq a_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
it holds that
f(\sup_{i\geq 0}a_{i})=\sup_{i\geq 0}f(a_{i})
.
Continuity implies
monotonicity
:
If
f
is continuous, then for all
a,b\in A
we have

a~{}\sqsubseteq{}~b\quad\textnormal{implies}\quad f(a)~{}\sqsubseteq{}~f(b)~.

The variants of Kleene’s fixed point theorem and Park induction used in this paper are as follows:

Theorem 0.C.1

Let
f\colon A\to A
be a continuous function on
\omega
-cpo
(A,\sqsubseteq)
.
Then

•

(Kleene’s fixed point theorem)
f
has a unique least fixed point given by

\textnormal{{lfp}}~f~{}={}~\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot)~.

•

(Park induction) For all
x\in A
,

f(x)~{}\sqsubseteq{}~x\quad\textnormal{implies}\quad\textnormal{{lfp}}~f~{}\sqsubseteq{}~x~.

Proof.

We provide a self-contained proof.

For Kleene’s fixed point theorem note that

f(\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot))~{}={}~\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i+1}(\bot)~{}={}~\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot)

where the first equality holds by continuity of
f
.
Thus
\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot)
is indeed a fixed point of
f
.
To see that it is the least fixed point, let
x\in A
such that
f(x)=x
.
Since
\bot\sqsubseteq x
we have
f(\bot)\sqsubseteq f(x)=x
by monotonicity of
f
.
Iterating this argument yields
f^{i}(\bot)\sqsubseteq x
for all
i\geq 0
.
This means that
x
is an
upper bound
on the chain
f^{0}(\bot)\sqsubseteq f^{i}(\bot)\sqsubseteq\ldots
, and thus it is greater or equal to the least upper bound (the supremum):

\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot)\sqsubseteq x~.

This concludes the proof of Kleene’s fixed point theorem.

For Park induction assume that
x\in A
is such that
f(x)\sqsubseteq x
.
We claim that
f^{i}(\bot)\sqsubseteq x
for all
i\geq 0
.
This is proved by induction on
i
:

•

For
i=0
this is trivial because
f^{0}(\bot)=\bot\sqsubseteq a
.

•

For
i>0
we have
f^{i}(\bot)=f(f^{i-1}(\bot))\overset{\text{I.H. + Mon.}}{\sqsubseteq}f(x)\overset{\text{Ass.}}{\sqsubseteq}x
.

Therefore,
x
is again an upper bound on the chain
f^{0}(\bot)\sqsubseteq f^{i}(\bot)\sqsubseteq\ldots
and thus

x~{}\sqsupseteq{}~\sup_{i\geq 0}f^{i}(\bot)~{}={}~\textnormal{{lfp}}~f

where the right equation is Kleene’s fixed point theorem.
∎

Appendix 0.D
Background on Formal Power Series

This section is intended to be a self-contained introduction to FPS with a particular focus on the operations necessary for our technique.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
.
A
k
-dimensional
formal power series
(FPS) is a map
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e., a
k
-dimensional
array
with real-valued entries.
It is common and useful to denote FPS as
“formal sums”
.
To this end, let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
be an ordered vector of formal symbols, called
indeterminates
7
7

7

Note that empty
\mathbf{X}
is possible if
k=0
; a 0-dim. FPS is just a real number.
.
These indeterminates are merely
labels
for the
k
dimensions of
f
and do not have any other particular meaning.
An FPS
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is then written

f~{}={}~\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}f(\sigma)\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}

where
\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}
is the formal
monomial

X_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}X_{2}^{\sigma_{2}}\ldots X_{n}^{\sigma_{n}}
.
The exact
“syntax”
of the formal sum notation is not important and it is actually useful to allow a certain level of flexibility.
However, it is crucial that a formal sum unambiguously identifies each coefficient
f(\sigma)
of the FPS.
For example, notations such as

1+X+Y,\qquad\sum_{i\geq 5}X^{i},\quad\text{or}\qquad X^{5}+X^{6}+X^{7}+\ldots

(6)

are all valid ways of denoting FPS (in fact, the latter two examples denote the same FPS).
The FPS where all coefficients are 0 is simply denoted
0
.
An FPS with
f(\sigma)\neq 0
for finitely many
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
(like the leftmost example in (
6
)) is called
finite-support
or
polynomial
, and otherwise it is called
infinite-support
.
An FPS with
k=1
(
k>1
) is called
univariate
(
multivariate
, respectively).

Remark 1
.

One does not necessarily view FPS as
analytic functions in their indeterminates
, even though the formal sum notation seems to suggest the opposite.
This applies in particular to this paper for which there are several reasons:
(1) For our approach,
evaluating
the formal sums for arbitrary values of the indeterminates is neither meaningful nor necessary
8
8

8

Sometimes, we have to substitute an indeterminate by 0 or 1. Whenever we do so, we make sure that it is well-defined.
.
(2) Relating an FPS to a function is problematic in general because the order in which the infinite sum is evaluated may affect the result; in fact, we would have to fix such an order upfront. However, especially for multivariate FPS, it is not clear what this order should be.
(3) The formal, algebraic operations we apply to FPS are not always well-defined in the analytic sense.
For instance, the FPS
\sum_{i\geq 0}i!X^{i}
converges for no
X\neq 0

[
60
]
and thus it is nowhere differentiable when viewed as an analytic function.
However, its formal derivative is perfectly well-defined.

The bottom line is that viewing FPS as purely algebraic objects, namely
arrays
(rather than analytic functions) avoids many complications and is well-suited for the purposes of this paper.

Probability Generating Functions.

A PGF
g
is the special case of an FPS where the coefficients
g(\sigma)
encode a (discrete)
probability distribution
on
\mathbb{N}^{k}
:

Definition 6

(PGF)
.

A
k
-dimensional FPS
g
is called a probability generating function (PGF) if for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
we have
(i)

g(\sigma)\geq 0
and
(ii)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}g(\sigma)\leq 1
.

For example,

\frac{1}{3}X+\frac{1}{3}Y+\frac{1}{3}XY\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}X+\frac{1}{8}X^{2}+\ldots

are PGF; the former encodes a (finite-support) uniform distribution on the tuples
\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}
, and the latter is an (infinite-support) geometric distribution on the non-negative integers
\mathbb{N}
.

The sum in constraint (ii) of
Definition

6
is an
actual infinite sum
, not a formal one.
However, this is not problematic because all coefficients are non-negative due to constraint (i), and thus we need not pay attention to issues of summation order.

In the following, we describe the relevant algebraic operations that can be performed on FPS and PGF in particular.

Addition and multiplication.

A key advantage of the sum notation is that it allows for a more intuitive definition of important FPS operations.
In fact, one can define
sum
and
product
operators such that the set
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
of all
k
-dimensional FPS becomes a (commutative) ring, the
ring of formal power series
.
These operations are defined as follows:

\displaystyle f+g~{}={}~\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}(f(\sigma)+g(\sigma))\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}\qquad fg~{}={}~\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau+\rho=\sigma\end{subarray}}f(\tau)g(\rho)\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}~.

For the multiplication, the inner sum symbol is not a formal sum, but an actual
finite
sum in the ring; it is finite because for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
there are just finitely many
\tau,\rho\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that
\tau+\rho=\sigma
.

It is straightforward to verify that the two operations are associative, commutative and that multiplication distributes over addition.
Moreover, the constant FPS
0
and
1
are neutral w.r.t. to addition and multiplication, respectively, and thus
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
is indeed a ring.

Inverses.

Even though
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
is not a field for
k\geq 1
, there still exist elements
f
with a unique
inverse

f^{-1}=1/f
satisfying
ff^{-1}=1
(uniqueness of inverses follows because
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
is zero-divisor free).
Of particular interest for this paper (and in fact for the whole method of generating functions) are the cases where
f
is an infinite-support FPS (meaning that
\{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\mid f(\sigma)\neq 0\}
is infinite) but
f^{-1}
is a
finite
-support FPS, also called
polynomial
.
This enables representing the
infinite
array
f
implicitly through its inverse
finite
array
f^{-1}
.
Well-known examples of this are the
geometric series
and variants thereof, for example

\frac{1}{2-X}~{}={}~\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{i+1}}X^{i}~.

(7)

We stress that the
only
meaning associated to equation (
7
) is that
2-X
and
\sum_{i\geq 0}1/2^{i+1}X^{i}
are mutually inverse elements in the FPS ring.
In particular, we are usually
not
concerned with viewing the left and right hand side as
“functions in
X
”
, cf.
Remark

1
.
We call a representation like
g=f/h=fh^{-1}
a
rational closed form
in this paper.
It is easy to show that for all
f,g,p,q\in\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
such that
g^{-1}
and
q^{-1}
exist it holds that, as expected,

\frac{f}{g}\,\frac{p}{q}~{}={}~\frac{fp}{gq}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{f}{g}+\frac{p}{q}~{}={}~\frac{fq+gp}{gq}~,

in other words, addition and multiplication preserve rational closed forms.

An alternative view on inverses is that for a given
f
we look for a solution
x
of the equation
fx=1
(in this case, the solution is unique if it exists).
In a similar way, we can define
roots
in
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{X}}]]
: An
n
-th root of
f
is a solution
x
to the equation
x^{n}=f
.
In general, roots do not always exist and are not necessarily unique if they exist.
However, there are infinite-support FPS that have a closed form representation involving roots.
An example is the
“Catalan distribution”
that arises in the program in
Figure

5
.

FPS as coefficients.

For
k\geq 2
, a
k
-dimensional array can be viewed equivalently as an
l
-dimensional (
l<k
) array with
(k{-}l)
-dimensional arrays as entries, i.e., we identify
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{N}^{l}\to(\mathbb{N}^{k-l}\to\mathbb{R})
if
f(\sigma)=f^{\prime}(\tau)(\rho)
for all
\sigma=(\tau,\rho)
.
In the formal sum notation, this is reflected by partitioning
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l},Z_{l+1},\ldots,Z_{k})
and viewing
f
as an FPS in
\mathbf{Y}

with coefficients that are FPS in the remaining indeterminates
\mathbf{Z}
:

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}f(\sigma)\mathbf{X}^{\sigma}~{}={}~\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{l}}f^{\prime}(\tau)\mathbf{Y}^{\tau}~.

Note that the formal sum notation on the right is not
“problematic”
as it still identifies all coefficients of
f
in an unambiguous fashion.
In the following, we write
f(\tau)
instead of
f^{\prime}(\tau)
; the precise meaning of
f(\tau)
for
\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{l}
,
l<k
, is to treat the FPS
f
as a map of type
\mathbb{N}^{l}\to(\mathbb{N}^{k-l}\to\mathbb{R})
as explained above.
Partitioning the indeterminates in this manner is often useful for understanding the structure of an FPS.
For example, it holds that

\sum_{i,j\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{2i+1}}{i\choose j}Y^{j}X^{i}~{}={}~\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{i+1}}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}Y)^{i}X^{i}

and the right hand side reveals almost immediately that this FPS is in fact the PGF resulting from executing the program

\displaystyle{\mathtt{x}}\,:=\,{\mathtt{geometric}({1/2})}\,\fatsemi

\displaystyle{\mathtt{y}}\,:=\,{\mathtt{binomial}({1/2},\,{\mathtt{x}}))}~.

Treating a given FPS as univariate in some distinguished indeterminate
X
is particularly useful for defining the operations of
substitution
and
differentiation
that we explain in the following.

Substitution.

Substitution refers to the operation of replacing an indeterminate by another FPS.
Let
f=\sum_{i\geq 0}f(i)X^{i}
and
h
be FPS.
Intuitively, the substitution of
X
by
h
in
f
should satisfy the equality

{f}[{X}/{h}]~{}={}~\sum_{i\geq 0}f(i)h^{i}

with the convention that
h^{0}=1
.
However, this equality cannot be seen as a general definition; recall from
Remark

1
that
“evaluating”
an FPS is often problematic, and substitution is even more general than evaluation.
Indeed, if
f=1+X+X^{2}+\ldots=(1-X)^{-1}
, then neither
{f}[{X}/{1}]
nor
{f}[{X}/{1+X}]
are well-defined.
Examples of well-defined substitutions are
{f}[{X}/{0}]
and
{f}[{X}/{X^{2}}]
, the former is equal to
1
and the latter is the FPS
1+X^{2}+X^{4}+\ldots=(1-X^{2})^{-1}
.
The next lemma states that substitution is well-defined in general if
h
has no
“constant coefficient”
as in the last two examples:

Lemma 0.D.1

Let
f=\sum_{i\geq 0}f(i)X^{i}
and
h
be FPS in
\mathbb{R}[[{X,\mathbf{Y}}]]
.
If
h(0,\mathbf{0})=0
, then
{f}[{X}/{h}]
is a well-defined FPS and for all
i\geq 0
it holds that

{f}[{X}/{h}](i)~{}={}~\sum_{j\geq 0}f(j)h^{j}(i)

where the right hand side is a
finite
sum in
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{Y}}]]
.

It can be shown that such well-defined substitutions preserve the arithmetic operations, i.e., they are ring homomorphisms.
For example, it holds that

fg~{}={}~1\qquad\textnormal{implies}\qquad{f}[{X}/{h}]{g}[{X}/{h}]~{}={}~1

from which it follows that substitution preserves rational and other algebraic closed forms:

f~{}={}~\frac{p}{q}\qquad\textnormal{implies}\qquad{f}[{X}/{h}]~{}={}~\frac{{p}[{X}/{h}]}{{q}[{X}/{h}]}~.

For example, if we know that
(1-X)^{-1}
is a closed form of
1+X+X^{2}+\ldots
, then
(1-X^{2})^{-1}
is a correct closed form of
1+X^{2}+X^{4}+\ldots
.

Substitution by 1.

Somewhat contrary to our
“FPS aren’t functions”
-philosophy, we occasionally have to substitute an indeterminate by the constant
1
.
In the case of PGF, this corresponds to computing the marginal distribution in all
other
variables – a key operation necessary for executing assignments such as
{\mathtt{x}}\,:=\,{5}
(see
Section

4
).
However, as explained above, such substitutions are not well-defined for general FPS.
Therefore, we first identify a class of FPS where substitution by
1
is well-defined: the
X
-absolutely convergent
FPS.

Let
f
be an FPS in
\mathbb{R}[[{X,\mathbf{Y}}]]
.
We say that
f
is
X
-absolutely convergent if for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbf{Y}}
, the infinite series

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f(j,\sigma)

is absolutely convergent
9
9

9

A series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}
is called absolutely convergent if the sequence
(\sum_{i=0}^{n}|a_{i}|)_{n\geq 0}
converges. Note that the sums in this notation are not
“formal”
as in the FPS notation; they refer to actual series and limits in
\mathbb{R}
.
.
If
f
is
X
-absolutely convergent, then it makes good sense to define the substitution
{f}[{X}/{1}]
as
{f}[{X}/{1}](0,\sigma)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f(j,\sigma)
and
{f}[{X}/{1}](i,\sigma)=0
for all
i>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbf{Y}}
.

For example,
f=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}X+\frac{1}{8}X^{2}+\ldots
and
g=2-X
are
X
-absolutely convergent, and we have
{f}[{X}/{1}]={g}[{X}/{1}]=1
.
Note that for finite-support (polynomial) FPS such as
g
, the substitution
{g}[{X}/{1}]
is an effective operation: simply replace all occurrences of
X
in
g
by
1
and simplify the resulting term.

In general, all PGF are
X
-absolutely convergent for all their indeterminates
X
.
Second-order PGF (SOP, see
Section

5
) are
X
-absolutely convergent for all non-meta indeterminates
X
; however, they are in general not
U
-absolutely convergent for meta indeterminates
U
.

The next lemma shows that substitution by
1
can also be effectively performed on FPS for which a rational closed form is known:

Lemma 0.D.2

Let
f
and
g
be FPS in
\mathbb{R}[[{X,\mathbf{Y}}]]
and suppose that both are
X
-absolutely convergent.
Then,

fg=1\quad\textnormal{implies}\quad{f}[{X}/{1}]{g}[{X}/{1}]=1~.

Proof.

Intuitively, this observation is a straightforward application of the Cauchy product formula for absolutely converging power series.
Formally, let
fg=1
.
Then by definition of the FPS product we have

\displaystyle f(0)g(0)

\displaystyle~{}={}~1,\quad\textnormal{and}\quad

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}=i}f(i_{1})g(i_{2})

\displaystyle~{}={}~0\quad\text{for all }i>0~.

(9)

We now consider the product
{f}[{X}/{1}]{g}[{X}/{1}]
and show that it is also equal to
1
:
Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbf{Y}}
be arbitrary.

\displaystyle({f}[{X}/{1}]{g}[{X}/{1}])(0,\sigma)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma}{f}[{X}/{1}](0,\sigma_{1}){g}[{X}/{1}](0,\sigma_{2})

(by def. of the FPS product)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f(j,\sigma_{1})\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}g(j,\sigma_{2})\right)

(by def. of substitution by

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{1}+j_{2}=j}f(j_{1},\sigma_{1})g(j_{2},\sigma_{2})

(standard Cauchy product formula for absolutely converging series)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma}\left((f(0)g(0))(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2})+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{1}+j_{2}=j}(f(j_{1})g(j_{2}))(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2})\right)

(rewriting)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma}\left((f(0)g(0))(\sigma)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}0\right)

(by (

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sigma=\mathbf{0}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(by (
8

Thus
{f}[{X}/{1}]{g}[{X}/{1}]=1
.

Lemma

For example,

\displaystyle{\frac{3}{2XY-5X-4Y+10}}[{X}/{1}]~{}={}~\frac{3}{5-2Y}~{}={}~\frac{0.6}{1-0.4Y}~,

implying that the left hand side is a PGF whose marginal in

.

Now let
f
and

be PGF.
We can also define substitution of
X
in

(this is needed for the

Technically, we can recycle the two kinds of substitutions discussed above (substitution by

and substitution by

Corollary 1

Let
g
and
h
be PGF in
\mathbb{R}[[{X,\mathbf{Y}}]]

Let

for a temporary auxiliary indeterminate

Then

{g}[{X}/{h}]~{}\coloneqq{}~{{g}[{X}/{h_{T}}]}[{T}/{1}]

For the proof it suffices to note that since

{g}[{X}/{h_{T}}]
is a PGF as well because PGF are closed under substitution.

Derivatives.

Differentiation is another basic algebraic operation on FPS.
Again, we consider a distinguished indeterminate
X
and the FPS ring
\mathbb{R}[[{X,\mathbf{Y}}]]
.
The formal (partial) derivative of an FPS
f=\sum_{i\geq 0}f(i)X^{i}
is defined as

\partial_{X}f~{}={}~\sum_{i\geq 0}i\cdot f(i)X^{i-1}~.

Recall that the coefficients
f(i)
are FPS in
\mathbb{R}[[{\mathbf{Y}}]]
.
One application of derivatives in this paper is the extraction of these coefficients given some closed form of
f
:

f(i)~{}={}~\frac{1}{i!}{(\partial_{X}^{i}f)}[{X}/{0}]~.

It can be shown that formal differentiation satisfies the familiar properties w.r.t. to sums, products and inverses, i.e.,

\partial_{X}(f+g)=\partial_{X}f+\partial_{X}g
,

\partial_{X}(fg)=\partial_{X}f+f\partial_{X}g
, and

\partial_{X}\frac{1}{f}=-\partial_{X}f/f^{2}
.
Notably, the derivative of an FPS expressed in a closed form is also in closed form.
This observation is crucial for our approach as it allows us to implement a closed form preserving PGF semantics for conditional branching.

Definition 7

(Admissible SOP-transformer)
.

A function
\psi\colon\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP}
is called
admissible
if

•

\psi
is
continuous
on the
\omega
-cpo
\mathsf{SOP}
.

•

\psi
is
linear
in the following sense:
For all
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathsf{SOP}
and
p\in[0,1]

pf_{1}+f_{2}\in\mathsf{SOP}\quad\textnormal{implies}\quad\psi(pf_{1}+f_{2})~{}={}~p\psi(f_{1})+\psi(f_{2})~.

•

\psi
is
homogeneous w.r.t. meta indeterminates
, i.e., for all
f\in\mathsf{SOP}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{l}
,

\psi(f\mathbf{U}^{\tau})~{}={}~\psi(f)\mathbf{U}^{\tau}~.

•

\psi

preserves PGF
, i.e.,
g\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
implies
\psi(g)\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
.

The general idea of the proof is to show that
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket
is also an admissible SOP-transformer for every
ReDiP
-program
P
.
To this end, we show that (i) the
“atomic”
FPS transformations like multiplication, substitution, etc., that are used in the definition of
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket
are admissible, and (ii) admissible transformations are closed under composition and taking limits (the latter is needed for loops).
The claims from
Theorem

5.1
then follow more or less directly from the admissibility of
\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket
.

Lemma 0.F.1

Let
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
be admissible SOP-transformers.
Their composition
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}
is admissible as well.

Proof.

The proof is mostly standard and goes as follows:
For continuity, let
f_{0}\sqsubseteq f_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
be a chain in
\mathsf{SOP}
.
We have

\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f_{i}))~{}={}~\psi_{1}(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{2}(f_{i}))~{}={}~\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(\sup_{i\geq 0}f_{i}))

where the first equation holds because
\psi_{2}(f_{0})\sqsubseteq\psi_{2}(f_{1})\sqsubseteq\ldots
is also a chain in
\mathsf{SOP}
by monotonicity of
\psi_{2}
.

Linearity of the composition follows because

\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(pf_{1}+f_{2}))~{}={}~\psi_{1}(p\psi_{2}(f_{1})+\psi_{2}(f_{2})))~{}={}~p\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f_{1}))+\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f_{2})))~.

Homogeneity w.r.t. to the meta-indeterminates
\mathbf{U}
holds because
\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f\mathbf{U}^{\tau}))=\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f)\mathbf{U}^{\tau})=\psi_{1}(\psi_{2}(f))\mathbf{U}^{\tau}
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{l}
.

PGF preservation is also trivial because both
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
preserve PGF.
∎

Lemma 0.F.2

Let
\psi_{0}\sqsubseteq\psi_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
be a chain in
(\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP})
and assume that
\psi_{i}
is admissible for all
i\geq 0
.
Then
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
is admissible as well.

Proof.

It is clear that
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
exists because
(\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP})
is an
\omega
-cpo.
As a general observation, note that for all
f\in\mathsf{SOP}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}(f)~{}={}~(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(f)

(10)

due to the way the order is defined on
(\mathsf{SOP}\to\mathsf{SOP})
.

We now show that
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
is continuous.
To this end, let
f_{0}\sqsubseteq f_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
be a chain in
\mathsf{SOP}
.
The following argument proves continuity:

\displaystyle(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(\sup_{j\geq 0}f_{j})

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\psi_{i}(\sup_{j\geq 0}f_{j})

(By (
10
))

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\sup_{j\geq 0}\psi_{i}(f_{j})

(
\psi_{i}
is continuous by assumption)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{j\geq 0}\,\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}(f_{j})

(suprema commute)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{j\geq 0}\,(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(f_{j})~.

(By (
10
))

Next, we show that
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
is linear.
To this end, note that addition and
“scalar multiplication”
are continuous:
For all chains
f_{0}\sqsubseteq f_{1}\sqsubseteq\ldots
and in
\mathsf{SOP}
and
h\in\mathsf{SOP}
we have

\sup_{i\geq 0}(f_{i}+h)~{}={}~\sup_{i\geq 0}f_{i}+h

(11)

because both addition of FPS and the order on
\mathsf{SOP}
are defined
“coefficient-wise”
and addition in
\mathbb{R}
is continuous.
For similar reasons, scalar multiplication is continuous, i.e., for all
p\in[0,1]
,

\sup_{i\geq 0}pf_{i}~{}={}~p\sup_{i\geq 0}f_{i}~.

(12)

Now let
p\in[0,1]
and
f,g\in\mathsf{SOP}
such that
pg+f\in\mathsf{SOP}
.
Then

\displaystyle(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(pg+f)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\psi_{i}(pg+f)

(by (
10
))

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\left[p\psi_{i}(g)+\psi_{i}(f)\right]

(by assumption)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\,p\psi_{i}(g)+\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\psi_{i}(f)

(continuity of + as in (
11
))

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle p\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\psi_{i}(g)+\sup_{i\geq 0}\,\psi_{i}(f)

(continuity of scalar multiplication as in (
12
))

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle p(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(g)+(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(f)

(by (
10
))

which proves linearity of
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
.

To show that
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}
is homogeneous w.r.t. meta-indeterminates we argue as follows:

\displaystyle(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(f\mathbf{U}^{\tau})

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}(f\mathbf{U}^{\tau})

(By (
10
))

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}(f)\mathbf{U}^{\tau}

(
\psi_{i}
is admissible)

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(f)\mathbf{U}^{\tau}~.

(By (
10
))

Finally, for all
g\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
it holds that
(\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i})(g)\in\textnormal{{PGF}}
because
\sup_{i\geq 0}\psi_{i}(g)
is an ascending chain in the
\omega
-cpo
PGF
, and hence the supremum exists and is itself a PGF.
∎

\psi_{1}

\psi_{2}
