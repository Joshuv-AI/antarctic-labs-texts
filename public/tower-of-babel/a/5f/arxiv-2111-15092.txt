Supercritical Spatial SIR Epidemics: Spreading Speed and Herd Immunity
Xinghua Zheng, Qingsan Zhu

Abstract
We study supercritical spatial SIR epidemics on ℤ 2 × { 1 , 2 , … , N } \mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\{1,2,\ldots,N\} , where each site in ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} represents a village and N N stands for the village size. We establish several key asymptotic results as N → ∞ N\to\infty . In particular, we derive the probability that the epidemic will last forever if the epidemic is started by one infected individual. Moreover, conditional on that the epidemic lasts forever, we show that the epidemic spreads out linearly in all directions and derive an explicit formula for the spreading speed. Furthermore, we prove that the ultimate proportion of infection converges to a number that is constant over space and find its explicit value. An important message is that if there is no vaccination, then the ultimate proportion of population who will be infected can be much higher than the vaccination proportion that is needed in order to prevent sustained spread of the infection.



1
Introduction

1.1
SIR Model

The Susceptible, Infected and Recovered (SIR) epidemic model is a fundamental model in epidemiology. In the usual SIR model, there is a fixed population, and at any time, individuals in the population fall in one of the three categories: susceptible, infected and recovered (
[
Kermack and McKendrick(1927)
]
). Infected individuals remain infected for one unit of time, then recover and gain immunity. The disease is spread from an infected individual to a susceptible individual with a fixed probability.

In the afore-mentioned model, spatial information is not considered. This is not suitable for various applications because many epidemics, including COVID-19, only transmit
locally
.

In this paper, we focus on a spatial version of SIR model.

1.2
Spatial SIR Model

We consider the spatial SIR epidemic model introduced in
[
Lalley(2009)
]
. The epidemic takes place on
{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\times\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. The space dimension can be more general, but in this paper we focus on the two dimensional case because of its practical relevance. In the model, each site
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
represents a village, which hosts
N
individuals, which are labeled as
(\mathbf{x},1),(\mathbf{x},2),\ldots,(\mathbf{x},N)
. The general rule is the same as the usual SIR model, except that the disease can only be spread from an infected individual to a susceptible individual who is either at the same site or in a nearest neighbor site. We assume that the infection probability is the same for all such pairs of infected and susceptible individuals, and the infection probability is given by

P_{N}^{\theta}=\frac{1+\theta}{5N}.

(1)

From (
1
), we see that the basic reproduction number is
R_{0}=(5N-1)\cdot P_{N}^{\theta}
, which is approximately
1+\theta
when the village size
N
is large. The epidemic is hence supercritical, critical, or subcritical according to whether
\theta>0
,
\theta=0
, or
\theta<0
.

The critical case when
\theta=0
, or more generally, the near-critical case when
\theta=\theta_{0}/N^{1/2}
for some fixed constant
\theta_{0}
, has been studied in
[
Lalley and Zheng(2010)
]
and
[
Lalley, Perkins, and Zheng(2014)
]
. In the first paper, the authors prove that the process, suitably scaled, converges to a measure-valued super-process. In
[
Lalley, Perkins, and Zheng(2014)
]
, the authors further establish a survival-extinction phase transition for the limiting process.

COVID-19, as well as many other epidemics, are supercritical. This is the case we focus on in this paper. Henceforth, we assume that
\theta
is a fixed positive constant.

1.3
Main Results

We answer the following three fundamental questions:

Q1.

How big is the probability that the epidemic will last forever?

Q2.

How fast does the epidemic spread out?

Q3.

What is the ultimate proportion of individuals who will be infected?

We start with Q1. Note that because the total population size is infinity, there
is
a positive probability that the epidemic will last forever.

We first define related random variables:

\left\{\begin{aligned} I_{t}(\mathbf{x})&=I_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{x})=\#\mbox{infected individuals at site }\mathbf{x}\mbox{ at time }t,\\
R_{t}(\mathbf{x})&=R_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{x})=\#\mbox{recovered individuals at site }\mathbf{x}\mbox{ at time }t,\mbox{and}\\
S_{t}(\mathbf{x})&=S_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{x})=\#\mbox{susceptible individuals at site }\mathbf{x}\mbox{ at time }t\\
&=N-I_{t}(\mathbf{x})-R_{t}(\mathbf{x}).\\
\end{aligned}\right.

The evolution of the SIR process can be described as the following:

\displaystyle I_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})|(I_{t}(\mathbf{x}),R_{t}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(S_{t}(\mathbf{x}),1-\left(1-P_{N}^{\theta}\right)^{\widetilde{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\right),

(2)

\displaystyle\mbox{ where }\widetilde{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{||\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}||_{1}\leq 1}I_{t}(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\mbox{ }R_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=I_{t}(\mathbf{x})+R_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0.

Here,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}
means “equal in distribution”,
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
represents the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
, and
||\cdot||_{1}
denotes the
\ell_{1}
norm, namely,
||(a,b)||_{1}=|a|+|b|
for any
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The way we define the function
\widetilde{I}
will be used throughout the rest of the paper, namely, for any function
\phi(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot)
is a function defined as follows:

\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{||\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}||_{1}\leq 1}\phi(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mbox{for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

We focus on the following two initial conditions:

•

IC1:

I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{0})=1,\mbox{ and }I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x})=0\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0};

(3)

•

IC2: for some fixed
\gamma\in(0,1]
,

I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{0})\sim\gamma N,\mbox{ and }I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x})=0\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0},

(4)

where for any two sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 1
.

The default setting is that
R_{0}^{N}\equiv 0
, unless otherwise stated.

IC1 corresponds to the situation where the epidemic starts with one infected individual, so-called “patient zero”. IC2 can be considered as the situation where there is a virus outbreak, which causes an instant infection of a significant proportion of population at the outbreak point.

We now state the result about the probability that the epidemic lasts forever. Let us recall that for a Galton-Watson process started by one particle and with offspring distribution Poisson
(1+\theta)
, its survival probability, denoted by
\iota
, is the unique solution to the following equation:

1-\iota=\exp(-(1+\theta)\iota),\quad\iota\in(0,1).

(5)

More general results can be found in, e.g.,
[
Athreya and Ney(1972)
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
q_{N}
be the survival probability of the SIR process with village size
N
, namely,
q_{N}=P(I_{t}^{N}(\cdot)\not\equiv 0\mbox{ for all }t>0)
. Then, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}q_{N}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \iota,&\quad\mbox{under the initial condition IC1};\\
1,&\quad\mbox{under the initial condition IC2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Theorem
1
states that as the village size gets larger, the survival probability of the SIR process under the initial condition IC1 approaches the survival probability of a supercritical Galton-Watson process. The intuition is the following. During the initial period, the population of people who are immune to the disease is small, hence if the village size
N
is large, the epidemic evolves like a supercritical Galton-Watson process, causing the number of infected individuals to blow up quickly. Afterwards, due to the fixed village size and the accumulation of people who are immune to the disease, the SIR process starts to evolve differently than the Galton-Watson process. However, because the number of infected individuals is already large, with a high probability, the epidemic will last forever. Whether the epidemic lasts forever or not hence depends mainly the initial period, during which time the process behaves similarly to a Galton-Watson process.

Next, we turn to Q2, the spreading speed of the epidemic.
Because the disease can only be spread between neighboring sites, the speed, under the
\ell_{1}
norm, is at most one. Intuitively, the bigger the
\theta
, the more likely the disease spreads out, hence the higher the speed is. Our second main result states that there is a double phase transition, according to how
\theta
compares with
1.5
or
4
, and the speed can be strictly smaller than one in all directions, or smaller than one in some but not all directions, or equal to one in all directions.

To give the explicit formula of the spreading speed, we need to define several functions. Let

h(t)=t\log t+(1-t)\log(1-t),\quad t\in[0,1],

a(\phi)=\frac{\min(|\sin(\phi)|,|\cos(\phi)|)}{|\sin(\phi)|+|\cos(\phi)|},\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi),

and

\displaystyle G(v,\phi)

\displaystyle=\inf_{t\in[v,1]}h(t)+t\left(h\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{v}{2t}\right)+h\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{(1-2a(\phi))v}{2t}\right)\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\mbox{ for }v\in(0,1]\mbox{ and }\phi\in[0,2\pi).

The function
G
is strictly decreasing in
v\in(0,1]
with

\lim_{v\rightarrow 0}G(v,\phi)=\log\frac{1}{5},\quad\mbox{and}\quad G(1,\phi)=h(a(\phi)).

It follows that for any
\theta\in(0,1.5)
, there exits a unique
\upsilon=\upsilon(\theta,\phi)\in(0,1)
such that

G(\upsilon,\phi)=\log\left(\frac{1+\theta}{5}\right).

(7)

When
\theta\geq 1.5,
it may occur that
h(a(\phi))\leq\log((1+\theta)/5)
, in which case we define

\upsilon(\theta,\phi)=1.

(8)

Finally, we define for any
\mathbf{0}\neq(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a function
\arg(x,y)\in[0,2\pi)
to be the angle from the positive real axis to the vector representing the complex number
x+\mathbf{i}y
.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\theta\in(0,\infty)
, either under the initial condition IC1 and conditional on that the epidemic lasts forever, or under the initial condition IC2, the following results hold:

(i)

for any
\varepsilon>0
, any village size
N
, along any sequence
(i_{k},j_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfying
|i_{k}|+|j_{k}|\rightarrow\infty
and
\arg(i_{k},j_{k})\rightarrow\phi
for some
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}P(R_{\lfloor({|i_{k}|+|j_{k}|})(\upsilon(\theta,\phi)^{-1}-\varepsilon)\rfloor}(i_{k},j_{k})>0)=0;

(ii)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, when the village size
N
is sufficiently large, along any sequence
(i_{k},j_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfying
|i_{k}|+|j_{k}|\rightarrow\infty
and
\arg(i_{k},j_{k})\rightarrow\phi
for some
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}P\left({\left|\frac{R_{\lfloor({|i_{k}|+|j_{k}|})(\upsilon(\theta,\phi)^{-1}+\varepsilon)\rfloor}(i_{k},j_{k})}{N}-\iota\right|>\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon.

Results like Theorem
2
are known as “Shape Theorem”. A number of such theorems have been proved for other models; see, e.g.,
[
Richardson(1973)
,
Durrett and Liggett(1981)
,
Cox and Durrett(1988)
,
Zhang(1993)
,
Lalley(2003)
]
. However, in all these situations, the exact form of the limiting shape is unknown. To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
2
is the first such result for which the exact shape is known.

Let us explain Theorem
2
in words. For any direction
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, any site
(i,j)
that is far away from the origin and on this direction, namely,
(i,j)\approx(|i|+|j|)(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)
, before generation
\lfloor(|i|+|j|)(\upsilon(\theta,\phi)^{-1}-\varepsilon)\rfloor
, with a high probability, there is no recovered individual, in other words, the epidemic has not reached the site. However, after a short period of time, at generation
\lfloor(|i|+|j|)(\upsilon(\theta,\phi)^{-1}+\varepsilon)\rfloor
, conditional on that the epidemic lasts forever, with a high probability, there is
roughly a constant proportion of recovered individuals. The constant proportion, as we shall see from Theorems
3
and
4
below, turns out to be the ultimate proportion of recovered individuals over the whole period. To sum up, the epidemic spreads at the speed of
\upsilon(\theta,\phi)
along direction
\phi
, and most infections occur during a short period of time.

Let us visualize the statement about speed. Theorem
2
asserts that the “frontier” of the epidemic, after scaled by the generation, converges to the curve
(\upsilon(\theta,\phi):\phi\in[0,2\pi))
.

Now we turn to Q3, the ultimate proportion of individuals who will be infected. In fact, the three pictures in the bottom panel of Figure
2
already shed some light on the proportion. We see that the proportion is almost constant behind the frontier. Our next result confirms this observation and gives the explicit value for the constant. Recall that
\iota
is defined in (
5
). Note that for any fixed
N
and
\mathbf{x}
,
R^{N}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
is increasing in
n
, so we can define
R^{N}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{n\to\infty}R^{N}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
, under the initial condition IC1, conditional on that the epidemic lasts forever, we have

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}P\left(\left|\frac{R^{N}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})}{N}-\iota\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\varepsilon.

(9)

Under the initial condition IC2, the ultimate proportion of infection is not constant over the space, rather, it is specified by a difference equation. More precisely, let
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
be the solution to the following difference equation:

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=1-\exp(-\frac{1+\theta}{5}\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})),\,\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\mbox{ and }

(10)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma+(1-\gamma)\left(1-\exp(-\frac{1+\theta}{5}\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{0}))\right).

We will show in Proposition
30
that there is a unique solution to the above equation. Moreover, the function is symmetric and satisfies that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})\searrow\iota
in the sense that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})\geq\max\{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x}+(1,0)),\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x}+(0,1))\}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})\to\iota
as
||\mathbf{x}||_{1}\rightarrow\infty
.

Theorem 4
.

Under the initial condition IC2, we have

\frac{R^{N}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})}{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x}),\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mathrm{~as~}N\rightarrow\infty.

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon>0
, when
N
is sufficiently large, we have

\limsup_{\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\left|\frac{R^{N}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})}{N}-\iota\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\varepsilon.

Theorems
3
and
4
have a significant implication on “herd immunity”. In general, for an epidemic with a basic reproduction number
R_{0}
, the minimum proportion of the population that needs to be vaccinated to prevent sustained spread of the infection is
1-1/R_{0}
. In our case,
R_{0}\sim 1+\theta
, hence the required vaccination proportion is
\sim 1-1/(1+\theta)
. Theorem
3
, however, states that under the initial condition IC1, if there is no vaccination (hence the only way for an individual to gain immunity is via recovery from infection), then on the event that the the epidemic lasts forever, the proportion of individuals who will be infected throughout the whole process is around
\iota
. Under the initial condition IC2, the proportion of individuals who will be infected on site far away from the origin is also around
\iota
. A natural question is:
How do the two proportions compare with each other? The comparison is illustrated in Figure

.

than the required vaccination proportion to prevent sustained spread of the infection. For an epidemic with a basic reproduction number of 3, the two proportions are 0.94 and
2/3
, respectively. In other words, without vaccination, ultimately, there will be

of population who will be infected! This is substantially higher than the required vaccination level of

The difference between the two proportions highlights the huge benefit of vaccination.

Finally, we give a theorem to describe how infection evolves inside the “speed one” cone.
Recall that by Theorem
2
, in order to have such a cone,
\theta
needs to be larger than
1.5
. When
\theta\in(1.5,4)
, let
\kappa
be the unique solution in

\kappa^{\kappa}(1-\kappa)^{1-\kappa}=\frac{1+\theta}{5}.

(11)

When
\theta\geq 4
, let
\kappa=0
. Also note that

\upsilon(\theta,\phi)=1
if and only if
\kappa\leq|\cos(\phi)|/(|\cos(\phi)|+|\sin(\phi)|)\leq 1-\kappa
.

Remark 1
.

The value
\kappa
equals to the velocity of the leftmost position of a supercritical branching random walk. Specifically, consider a branching random walk started by one particle at the origin. At every generation, each particle produces a random number of offspring according to
\mathrm{Bin}(2N,(1+\theta)/(5N))
and dies out; each offspring independently moves to the upper neighbour or the right neighbour with equal probability. It is easy to see that such defined process stochastically dominates
(I_{n}^{N}(m,n-m))_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Write
(m(n),n-m(n))
as the leftmost position of the branching random walk in the
n
-th generation. Then, the classical results in branching random walk (see, e.g.,
[
Hammersley(1974)
]
,
[
Kingman(1975)
]
,
[
Biggins(1976)
]
and
[
Bramson(1978)
]
) state that, conditional on the event that the branching random walk survives forever, one has

\frac{m(n)}{n}\rightarrow\kappa.

Before giving our next theorem, we define a sequence
\{\ell^{(k)}\}_{k\geq-1}
recursively as follows:
\ell^{(-1)}=\ell^{(0)}=0,
and for
i\geq 1
,
\ell^{(i)}
is the unique solution in
(0,1)
to

\ell^{(i)}=\left(1-\sum_{1\leq j<i}\ell^{(j)}\right)\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{1+\theta}{5}\cdot(2\ell^{(i)}+\ell^{(i-1)}+2\ell^{(i-2)})\right)\right).

(12)

We will show in
Lemma
31
that
\iota=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ell^{(i)}
when
\theta>1.5
. Further define a sequence of random variables
\{\mathcal{K}(n)=\mathcal{K}^{N}(n)\}
as

\mathcal{K}(n)=\mathcal{K}^{N}(n)=\inf\left\{k:\sum_{(m,l)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:m+l=n}I^{N}_{n+k}(m,l)>0\right\},

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. In words,
\mathcal{K}(n)
measures after how many generations the epidemic reaches the line
\{(m,l)|m+l=n\}
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{K}^{N}(n)
is increasing, and we can define

\mathcal{K}^{N}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{K}^{N}(n).

.

Suppose that

, then the following results hold:

•

Under the initial condition IC2, for any

For any

Remark 2
.

1.4
Connection to percolation

Let us recall a connection between our SIR process and a percolation model (
[
Lalley(2009)
]
). We will call the percolation the
N
-percolation, which is defined as follows. Consider the graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times[N]
, where we write
[N]
for
\{1,2\dots,N\}
, and edge set
\{(\mathbf{x},i)-(\mathbf{y},j):||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{1}\in\{0,1\}\}
. Each edge is open with probability
P_{N}^{\theta}
. For any initial condition
(I^{N}_{0}(\cdot),R^{N}_{0}(\cdot)\equiv 0)
, let
A
be any subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times[N]
such that
\#(A\cap\mathbf{x}\times[N])=I^{N}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Then,
I^{N}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
(
R^{N}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
, respectively) equals the number of vertices at site
\mathbf{x}
that are of graph distance
n
to
A
(of graph distance
\leq n-1
, respectively). Moreover,
R^{N}_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}R^{N}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
is just the number of vertices at site
\mathbf{x}
that are connected to
A
. From this, we see that
R^{N}_{t}
(but not
I^{N}_{t}
) is increasing in the initial condition
I_{0}^{N}
. Based on such a connection, Theorems
1
-
5
can be interpreted correspondingly for the percolation model.

1.5
Generalization to other dimensions and distributions

Our methods of proof are robust enough to extend to other dimensions and infection distributions. We state the assumptions and results below without giving detailed proofs.

Suppose that the dimension
d\geq 2
, and
p=\{p(\mathbf{x})\}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that satisfies the following regularity assumptions:

(i)

p
has a finite support, namely,
\mathrm{Supp}(p):=\{\mathbf{x}:p(\mathbf{x})>0\}
is finite;

(ii)

p
is symmetric, i.e.,
p(\mathbf{x})=p(-\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}
;

(iii)

p

\displaystyle I_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})|(I_{t}(\mathbf{x}),R_{t}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})

The Deterministic Large
N
Limit Process

It is well known that the mean-field supercritical SIR model admits a deterministic limit process as the population size gets to infinity. Similarly, our spatial SIR model admits a deterministic large
N
limit process. It will play a fundamental role in the analysis of the original SIR process.

The deterministic process
(\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{R}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))_{\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}}
is defined as follows. For any initial condition
\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{R}_{0}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
with
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1-\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\geq~0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the process evolves as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{1+\theta}{5}\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right),\mbox{ where }

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{R}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\quad\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t+1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{R}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0\mbox{ and }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

The following proposition states that
(\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{R}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))
is the scaling limit of the original SIR process as
N\to\infty
. Note that since
\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{R}\equiv 1
, we can drop
\mathcal{S}
and only consider
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{R})
.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that the initial conditions
(I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x}),R_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x}))
and
(\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{x}))
satisfy

\frac{I_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x})}{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\frac{R_{0}^{N}(\mathbf{x})}{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\quad\mbox{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

(15)

Then the process
(I_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{x})/N,R_{t}^{N}(\mathbf{x})/N)
converges, under finite dimensional distribution, to the process
(\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{R}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))
.

Proof.

By the Markovian property, to prove finite dimensional convergence, it suffices to prove the convergence for
t=1
, which follows from (
2
) and the law of large numbers.
∎

Remark 4
.

The initial condition IC1, after scaling, converges to the trivial initial condition
\mathcal{I}_{0}\equiv 0
. For this reason, it requires additional techniques to study the asymptotics under the initial condition IC1.

\mathcal{I}_{0}(0,0)=\gamma,\mbox{ and }\mathcal{I}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=0\mbox{ otherwise. }

(17)

For notational ease, we write

\alpha=\exp((1+\theta)/5),

(18)

\psi(x)=1-\alpha^{-x},\quad\mbox{for }x\in[0,1].

Now we consider the initial condition (

We first address the case when

Remark 7

Proof.

Write

Remark 8

By monotonicity, conclusion (

Now we address the case when

Proposition 9

Assume
\theta\in(1.5,4]

Write

The key observation is the following, which can be easily proved by induction.

Claim 1

\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

Inspired by this, we define

Since

Now, by induction, one can easily show

By Stirling’s formula, we get

g(m,n)={\sqrt{\frac{m+n+1}{2\pi(m+1)(n+1)}}}\left(\frac{\log\alpha}{s^{s}(1-s)^{1-s}}\right)^{m+n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m+1}+\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\right),

where

Now we have finished the preparation and are ready to show (

For (

For (

Lemma 13

If

4.1

Proposition 14
.

Suppose that

and

(i)

(ii)

Proof.

We first prove Part (i).
For any

and

is increasing in the initial condition as long as the initial condition is supported by the diagonal line
\{(m,-m):m\in\mathbb{Z}\}

We turn to Part (ii). When

. On the other hand,

\displaystyle P(I_{n}^{N}(n,0)\geq 2)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle P(I_{n-1}^{N}(n-1,0)\geq 1)\cdot P(I_{n}^{N}(n,0)\geq 2|I_{n-1}^{N}(n-1,0)+I_{n-1}^{N}(n,-1)=1).

.

Finally, we prove Part (iii). For

It follows by induction that

for all
