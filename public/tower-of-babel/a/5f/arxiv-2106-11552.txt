Finitely generated subgroups of free groups as formal languages and their cogrowth
Asif Shaikh

Abstract
Let H ≤ F m H\leq F_{m} be a finitely generated subgroup of the free group F m F_{m} of finite rank m m . It is well-known that the language L H L_{H} of reduced words from F m F_{m} representing elements of H H is regular. In the current article, using the (extended) core of the Schreier graph of H H , we construct the minimal deterministic finite automaton that recognizes L H L_{H} . Then we characterize the finitely generated subgroups H ≤ F m H\leq F_{m} for which L H L_{H} is irreducible, and for each such H H we explicitly construct an ergodic automaton that recognizes L H L_{H} . This construction gives us an efficient way to compute the cogrowth series L H ​ ( z ) L_{H}(z) as well as the entropy of L H L_{H} . Several examples are provided to illustrate the method. Also, a comparison is made with the method of calculation of L H ​ ( z ) L_{H}(z) based on the use of Nielsen system of generators of H H .

Introduction

In
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, the notion of
cogrowth
of a subgroup
H
of a free group
F_{m}
was introduced and a cogrowth criterion for amenability of the factor group
F_{m}/H
(in case
H
is normal) was proved. The concept of cogrowth was used to construct counterexamples concerning various versions of the von Neumann conjecture about the existence of an invariant mean on groups and homogeneous spaces
[
1
,
10
,
20
]
.

In
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, it was observed that when
H\leq F_{m}
is a finitely generated (f.g.) subgroup, then the cogrowth series

H(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{w\in H}z^{|w|}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|H_{n}|z^{n}

(1)

is a rational function, where
|w|
denotes the length of
w\in H
and
H_{n}
denotes the set of the reduced elements of length
n
in
H
with respect to a fixed basis of
F_{m}
.

Also, by
[
9
]
, for
H\leq F_{m}
, the following formula holds:

u(z)=\displaystyle R\left(z,\sqrt{1-\frac{2m-1}{m^{2}}z^{2}}\right)H\left(\frac{m\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2m-1}{m^{2}}z^{2}}\right)}{(2m-1)z}\right),

where
R(x,y)
is a rational function explicitly determined in
[
9
]
, and

u(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{1,1}^{(n)}z^{n},

where
P_{1,1}^{(n)}
is the probability of return to the identity
1\in G=F_{m}/H
in the simple random walk on
G
that starts at the identity. This formula shows a close relation between analytic properties of functions
H(z)
and
u(z)
. In particular, the algebraicity of
u(z)
is equivalent to the algebraicity of
H(z)
. If
H
is finitely generated, then the fact that
H(z)
is rational was proven in
[
11
]
using Nielsen system of generators for
H
. The topics related to growth and cogrowth have gotten a lot of attention and popularity, and are widely presented in the literature. See, for example,
[
8
]
.

Among various open questions related to cogrowth, the authors suggest a conjecture that
H(z)
is rational if and only if
H
is finitely generated. Note that when
H\triangleleft F_{m}
is a normal subgroup, the conjecture follows from the result of D. Kouksov
[
16
]
.

The alternative approach to proving the rationality of
H(z)
is via the theory of formal languages. Recall that the classical Chomsky hierarchy of languages begins with the class of regular (also called rational) languages, that is, languages recognizable by finite-automata acceptors
[
13
]
. Already in the 1950-60’s, Chomsky and Schützenberger were aware that the rationality of a language
L\subset\Sigma^{*}
(where
\Sigma
is finite alphabet and
\Sigma^{*}
denotes the set of finite words over
\Sigma
) implies rationality of the growth series

L(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|{B_{n}(L)}|z^{n},

where
{B_{n}(L)}
is the set of words in
L
of length
n
. In fact, back then it was more popular to consider the following noncommutative version of the growth series,

\hat{L}=\sum_{w\in L}w,

the rationality of which is equivalent to the rationality of
L

[
3
,
21
]
. A concept closely related to cogrowth is the

entropy
of languages, which is defined as

h(L)=\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log|{B_{n}(L)}|}{n}.

For a fixed free basis
A=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{m}\}
of
F_{m}
, we denote by
L_{H}
the set of all reduced finite words in
(A\cup A^{-1})^{*}
that represent an element of
H\leq F_{m}
. The fact that regularity of the language
L_{H}
is equivalent to the finite generation of
H
was observed by Anissimov and Seifert
[
2
]
. Since the intersection of two regular languages is regular (see
[
13
]
), a direct consequence of the theorem of Anissimov and Seifert is that the intersection of two finitely generated subgroups of
F_{m}
is again finitely generated. The last observation is in fact a well-known theorem of Howson from 1954,
[
14
]
. A proof of the theorem of Anissimov and Seifer, based on the ideas of geometric group theory, is presented in
[
7
]
. In fact, there are several ways to prove that if
H\leq F_{m}
is finitely generated, then
L_{H}
is a regular language. An elegant proof of this statement is presented by I. Kapovich and A. Myasnikov in
[
15
]
. Their proof is based on the idea of J. Stallings from
[
22
]
, which is now known as
Stallings foldings
, and on the notion of the core of a Schreier graph associated with the triple
(F_{m},H,A).
One of the goals of the current article is to explore this approach from the finite automata theory point of view and make it more detailed and accessible.

We conclude the introduction by outlining the content of the rest of the paper. In Section 2, we recall some of the basic definitions and terminology from the theory of finite automata, formal languages and theory of graphs that will be needed later. In Section 3, we give two versions of the definition of Schreier graph, and hence two versions of the core, one of which we call
the extended core
. We also discuss a combinatorial procedure to obtain the (extended) core of
H\leq F_{m}
. In Section 4, we recall the definition and properties of the Nielsen system of generators of
H\leq F_{m}
. We also recall how to obtain a Nielsen system geometrically. In Section 5, using the DFA
\mathscr{A}_{F_{m}}
that recognizes the language of freely reduced words of
F_{m}
and the DFA
\mathscr{A}_{\widehat{\Delta}_{H}}
whose Moore diagram is the extended core
\widehat{\Delta}_{H}
, we build another automaton,
\mathscr{A}_{H}
, that recognizes the language
L_{H}
of freely reduced words from
F_{m}
that represent the elements in
H
. Then we extract from
\mathscr{A}_{H}
a minimal sub-automaton
\mathscr{D}_{H}
such that
L(\mathscr{D}_{H})=L_{H}
. In Subsection 5.4, we characterize the f.g. subgroups
H\leq F_{m}
for which
L_{H}
is irreducible (see Proposition
5.4
) and for such
H
we explicitly construct ergodic automaton
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
that recognizes
L_{H}
(see Theorem
5.4
). The ergodicity of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
allows us, by applying Perron-Frobenius theory, to obtain in Subsection 5.5 a matrix formula for the entropy of
L_{H}
, allowing its efficient computation (see Theorem
5.5
). One more ingredient for showing this formula is based on relating to each finite state automaton so called
base automaton
, and by showing that under certain natural restrictions on the automaton, its entropy coincides with the entropy of its base automaton (see, Proposition
5.5
).

The standard
transfer matrix
method, that goes back to Kolmogorov’s theory of finite Markov chains, leads to the system of linear equations that allows us to compute
H(z)
. All of the above, in principle, is applicable to arbitrary subgroup
H\leq F_{m}
, but the related automata and the system of equations are finite only when
H
is finitely generated. In Section 6, we also include reproduction of the proof from
[
11
]
of rationality of
H(z)
via Nielsen system of generators. The produced algorithms are of polynomial complexity. An interesting question for further investigation is to check which of the two approaches on a given finite set
\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\}
of generators of
H
is more efficient for computing
H(z)
.

Finally, in the last section, several concrete examples illustrating the theoretical part from the previous sections are included.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, by
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
we denote a fixed basis of the free group
F_{m}
, elements of which, along with their inverses, we regard as formal letters, whenever they are considered in the context of formal languages. Correspondingly, the set of generators
\Sigma=A\cup A^{-1}
of
F_{m}
will be regarded as an alphabet whenever it is in the context of formal languages.
The set of all finite words over the alphabet
\Sigma
is denoted by
\Sigma^{*}
. Algebraically,
\Sigma^{*}
is the free monoid generated by the finite set
\Sigma
. The
length
of a word
w\in\Sigma
, denoted by
|w|
, means the number of letters in
w
when each letter is counted as many times as it occurs. By
red(w)
we denote the word that is obtained from
w\in\Sigma
by free reduction. The subsets of
\Sigma^{*}
are referred to as (formal) languages over the alphabet
\Sigma
. A language
L
is called
regular
if it is recognized by some finite automaton. A finite automaton
\mathscr{A}
is a quintuple,
\mathscr{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,\mathscr{I},\mathscr{F})
, consisting of finite set of states
Q
, alphabet
\Sigma
, transition function
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow 2^{Q}
, the set of initial states
\mathscr{I}\subseteq Q
and the set of final states
\mathscr{F}\subseteq Q
.

Let
G_{\mathscr{A}}
be the
Moore
or transition diagram of
\mathscr{A}
, that is
G_{\mathscr{A}}
is a labelled directed graph with vertex set
Q
and the directed labelled edges are described by the transition function
\delta
with labels from
\Sigma
. Namely, vertex
q
is connected with vertex
q^{\prime}
with an edge labeled by
a\in\Sigma
, if
q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,a)
. (For example, Figure
4
is a depiction of a Moore diagram for an automaton that we define later.)

Let
e
be an edge in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
. Then by
o(e),t(e)
and
l(e)
we denote origin, terminus and the label of the edge
e
, respectively. A directed path
p=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
is called
admissible
if
o(e_{1})\in\mathscr{I},t(e_{i})=o(e_{i+1}),
for
i=1,\cdots,(n-1)
,
t(e_{n})\in\mathscr{F}
. Let
w=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
be a word over
\Sigma
. The automaton
\mathscr{A}
accepts the word
w
if there is an admissible path
p
in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
such that
l(p)=l(e_{1})\cdots l(e_{n})=w
. The set of words that
\mathscr{A}
accepts is the language recognized by
\mathscr{A}
and this language is denoted by
L(\mathscr{A})
.

An automaton
\mathscr{A}
is
ergodic
if its Moore diagram
G_{\mathscr{A}}
is strongly connected, that is, for any two states
q
and
q^{\prime}\in Q
there exists a path connecting
q
to
q^{\prime}
. A language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is
irreducible
if, given two words
w_{1},w_{2}\in L
, there exists a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
such that the concatenation
w_{1}ww_{2}\in L
. A regular language
L
is irreducible if and only if it is generated by some ergodic automaton, see Theorem 3.3.11 of
[
17
]
. An automaton
\mathscr{A}
is
unambiguous
if for every
w\in L(\mathscr{A})
, there is a unique admissible path
p\in G_{\mathscr{A}}
such that
l(p)=w
. An automaton
\mathscr{A}
is
deterministic
, if for each state of
Q
, all outgoing edges carry distinct labels. It is obvious that a deterministic automaton with one initial state is unambiguous. Note that
\mathscr{A}
is deterministic if the codomain of
\delta
is
\{\emptyset\}\cup Q
, that is
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow\{\emptyset\}\cup Q
. The automaton
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime},\mathscr{I}^{\prime},\mathscr{F}^{\prime})
is said to be a
subautomaton
of
\mathscr{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,\mathscr{I},\mathscr{F})
, if

Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q
,
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma
,
\mathscr{I}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
,
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, and for each
q^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
,
a^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
,
\delta^{\prime}(q^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\delta(q,a)\cap Q^{\prime}
. In the language of Moore diagrams, an equivalent definition would be:
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is a subautomaton of
\mathscr{A}
if its Moore diagram
G_{\mathscr{A}^{\prime}}
is a subdiagram of
G_{\mathscr{A}}
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a finite automaton. We say that
\mathscr{A}
is
essential
if in its Moore diagram
G_{\mathscr{A}}
every vertex (hence, also every edge) belongs to some path connecting an initial state to a final state, i.e. to an admissible path. If
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is an essential subautomaton of
\mathscr{A}
such that
L(\mathscr{A}^{\prime})=L(\mathscr{A})
, then we say that
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is an
essential part
of
\mathscr{A}
.

Let
k\geq 1
. An automaton
\mathscr{A}
has
homogeneous ambiguity

k
if, for any nonempty word
w\in L(\mathscr{A})
, there are exactly
k
admissible paths
p_{1},\cdots,p_{k}
in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
with label
w
. In case the number of such paths for each
w\in L(\mathscr{A})
is bounded from above by
k
, we say that
\mathscr{A}
has
bounded ambiguity
. We shall use the terminology
DFA

\mathscr{A}
for the deterministic (unambiguous) finite automaton with exactly one initial state i.e.
\mathscr{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,\{q_{0}\},\mathscr{F})
. The DFA
\mathscr{A}
is said to be
minimal
if there is no DFA with smaller number of states that recognizes the same language
L(\mathscr{A})
. It is known that for any regular language
L
, up to isomorphism, there is a unique minimal DFA recognizing
L
, called
the minimal deterministic finite automaton
of
L
(see Theorem 3.10 on page 67 of
[
13
]
).

By
inaccessible state
we mean a non-initial state that does not have any incoming edge. Let
\mathscr{A}
be a DFA and let
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
. For every state
q
and word
w
, the value of
\delta(q,w)
is the end state of the path in
\mathscr{A}
that starts at
q
and reads the input word
w
. In case such path does not exist, we define
\delta(q,w)=\emptyset
.
Let
L=L(\mathscr{A})
be the language recognized by DFA
\mathscr{A}
and let
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
. Then there is a natural equivalence relation

on words associated with

, given by

wR_{L}w^{\prime}\iff\forall~z\in\Sigma^{*}\textnormal{ either both or neither of }wz\textnormal{ and }w^{\prime}z\textnormal{ is in }L

(2)

Observe that the number of equivalence classes of

is at most the number of states of
\mathscr{A}
, which is finite. Now we recall a version of Myhill-Nerode Theorem.

\thm

[

(up to isomorphism), where

\{q_{0}^{\prime}\}=\textnormal{the equivalence class of the empty word}.

\mathscr{F}^{\prime}=\{\left[w\right]\mid w\in L\}\textnormal{ and }

A graph
G=(V,E)
is called locally finite if the degree of each vertex of
V
is finite. The
in

of the directed graph is the number of edges in the graph that have

[

and
[

.

3.1
The Schreier graph of subgroup

We define two versions of the Schreier graph associated with

3.3
Stallings foldings

Let
H
be generated by elements
w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}
. We identify
w_{i}
with freely reduced words in the alphabet
\Sigma
. Then there is a simple combinatorial procedure to obtain the core
\Delta_{H}
. This procedure is based on the topological idea of
folding
developed by J. Stallings in
[
22
]
. Roughly the procedure can be described as follows.

Start with a bouquet of
k
circles glued together along a vertex
v_{0}
. Split the
i
-th circle,
1\leq i\leq k
, into
|w_{i}|
edges which are oriented and labeled by the letters from the set
A\cup A^{-1}
so that the label of the
i
-th circle(as read from
v_{0}
to
v_{0}
) is precisely the word
w_{i}
. Reverse the edges with (negative) label
x^{-1}
from the set
A^{-1}
and assign the (positive) label
x
from the set
A
(see Figure
2
).

Suppose
e_{1},e_{2}
are edges of this graph with a common origin and the same label
x\in A
. Then, informally, folding the graph at
e_{1},e_{2}
means identifying
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
in a single new edge labeled by
x
.

At the first step, fold the graph at the edges that are originated at the root vertex
v_{0}
. After performing these foldings, fold the graph at the edges that are originated at the other vertices and continue the process. As we assumed that the subgroup
H
is finitely generated, the process will stop after applications of finitely many steps (i.e. it stops when no more folding is possible). The resulting graph, up to isomorphism of labeled graphs, does not depend on the performed sequence of foldings. And the resulting graph is isomorphic to the core
\Delta_{H}
(see
[
15
,
22
]
). Moreover, the algorithm based on above procedure has polynomial time complexity (see
[
15
]
).

In fact, the above procedure works also for the situation of an infinitely generated group
H=\langle w_{1},\cdots,w_{n},\cdots\rangle
. One just has to begin with folding of the bouquet of the two loops labeled by
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, after the process stops add to the obtained graph a new loop labeled by
w_{3}
, apply folding, then add
w_{4}
etc. The process will converge to the infinite folded (i.e. no more folding is possible) graph
\Delta_{H}
with rooted vertex
v_{0}
.

1.

If

2.

If

,

we mean the length of the reduced word

over
\Sigma

Condition (

) means that not more than a half of

and not more than a half of

1
) after free cancellation in the product

5
The construction of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
, their properties and consequences

The first goal of this section is to construct the minimal DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
that recognizes the language
L_{H}
of reduced words of a finitely generated subgroup
H\leq F_{m}.
We shall approach this construction by defining an unambiguous automaton
\mathscr{A}_{H}
as the (Cartesian) product of two automata. Then we obtain the minimal DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
as the essential part of
\mathscr{A}_{H}
. At the end we obtain the multi-initial state automaton
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
by replacing the initial state of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
and provide the complete description when
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is ergodic. In Theorem
5.5
, we utilize the ergodicity property of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
to obtain an entropy formula for
L_{H}
.

Recall that a finite automaton
\mathscr{A}
is essential if in its Moore diagram
G_{\mathscr{A}}
every vertex (hence, also every edge) belongs to some path connecting an initial state to a final state, i.e. to an admissible path. The following lemma will be used later.

{lem}

Every finite automaton
\mathscr{A}
has an essential part. If
\mathscr{A}
is an unambiguous finite automaton, then it has only one essential part, called
the
essential part of
\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Define
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
to be the subautomaton of
\mathscr{A}
such that
G_{\mathscr{A}^{\prime}}
is the union of all admissible paths of
G_{\mathscr{A}}
. Then, clearly,
L(\mathscr{A}^{\prime})=L(\mathscr{A})
and
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is essential, hence
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is an essential part of
\mathscr{A}
.

If
\mathscr{A}
is unambiguous, then no proper subautomaton of
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
generates the language
L(\mathscr{A})
.
Also, if a vertex in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
does not belong to some admissible path in
G_{\mathscr{A}}
, then, by definition, it will not belong to any essential subautomaton of
\mathscr{A}
. Thus, if
\mathscr{A}
is unambiguous, then
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is the only essential part of
\mathscr{A}
.
∎

\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left(q^{*},a_{i}^{\epsilon}\right)=(v_{0}a_{i}^{\epsilon},a_{i}^{\epsilon}),\textrm{ for all }a_{i}^{\epsilon}\in\Sigma,

\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left((v,a_{i}^{\epsilon}),a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)=\left(va_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}},a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right),\textrm{ if }a_{i}^{\epsilon}\neq(a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})^{-1}.

5.2.1
Minimality of
\mathscr{D}_{H}

The Myhill-Nerode Theorem (see Theorem
2
) suggests the existence of a minimal DFA (which is unique up to isomorphism).
For any given DFA
\mathscr{A}
, in order to construct the minimal DFA
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
such that
L(\mathscr{A})=L(\mathscr{A}^{\prime})
, we need to define an equivalence relation
\equiv
among states of
\mathscr{A}
.

We write
u\equiv v
if the following holds: for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
\delta(u,w)
is a final state if and only if
\delta(v,w)
is a final state. Notice that the relations
R_{L}
(see (
2
)) and
\equiv
express exactly the same idea, i.e.

wR_{L}w^{\prime}\iff\delta(q_{0},w)\equiv\delta(q_{0},w^{\prime}).

(6)

The states
u
and
v
of
\mathscr{A}
are
equivalent
if
u\equiv v
. When the states
u
and
v
are not equivalent, then we say that they are
distinguishable
. That is, there exists at least one state
w
such that one of
\delta(u,w)
and
\delta(v,w)
is an accepting state and the other is not.

\thm

\mathscr{D}_{H}
is a minimal DFA.

Proof.

In order to show that the DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
is minimal, according to the Myhill-Nerode Theorem, it is enough to show that any two distinct states
(u,a_{i}^{\epsilon})
and
(v,a_{j}^{\nu})
of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
are distinguishable.
We prove this by contradiction.

Assume that
(u,a_{i}^{\epsilon})\neq(v,a_{j}^{\nu})
are
R_{L}
-equivalent states. Then
\forall~w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left((u,a_{i}^{\epsilon}),w\right)
is a final state if and only if
\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left((v,a_{j}^{\nu}),w\right)
is a final state. Let us fix one such
w
(the existence of
w
follows from
\mathscr{D}_{H}
being essential.) Since
\mathscr{D}_{H}
is an essential automaton, there are
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
in
\Sigma^{*}
such that
\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left(q^{*},w_{1}\right)=(u,a_{i}^{\epsilon})
and
\delta_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\left(q^{*},w_{2}\right)=(v,a_{i}^{\nu})
. Therefore,
w_{1}w,w_{2}w\in L(\mathscr{D}_{H})=L_{H}
, the suffix of
w_{1}
is
a_{i}^{\epsilon}
, the suffix of
w_{2}
is
a_{j}^{\nu}
. Then
w_{1}(w_{2})^{-1}=(w_{1}w)(w_{2}w)^{-1}\in H
, which implies that
Hw_{1}=Hw_{2}
, and hence
u=v
. Therefore, it must be that
a_{i}^{\epsilon}\neq a_{j}^{\nu}
, which, in particular, implies that
w_{1}(w_{2})^{-1}\in L_{H}
. As the states
(u,a_{i}^{\epsilon})
and
(v,a_{j}^{\nu})
are equivalent, under the assumption that
(u,a_{i}^{\epsilon})
and
(v,a_{j}^{\nu})
are
R_{L}
-equivalent,
w_{1}(w_{2})^{-1}\in L_{H}
implies
w_{2}(w_{2})^{-1}\in L_{H}
, which is not true – a contradiction.
∎

5.3
Definition of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}

In this subsection we introduce the automaton
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
, which is obtained from
{\mathscr{D}_{H}}
by simply removing its initial state
q^{*}
and proclaiming the final states
\mathscr{F}_{{\mathscr{D}_{H}}}
of
{\mathscr{D}_{H}}
at the same time the initial and final states of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
. Thus the automaton
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
would have the following presentation:

\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}=\left(Q_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}},\Sigma,\delta_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}},\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}},\mathscr{F}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}\right),

where

\displaystyle Q_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\left\{(v,a_{i}^{\epsilon})\mid v\in\widehat{V},a_{i}^{\epsilon}\in\Sigma,~and~\exists e\in\widehat{E}~s.t.~a_{i}^{\epsilon}=\widehat{\mu}(e),t(e)=v\right\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\mathscr{F}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\left\{(v_{0},a_{i}^{\epsilon})\mid a_{i}^{\epsilon}=\widehat{\mu}(e),t(e)=v_{0}\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle\delta_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}\left((v,a_{i}^{\epsilon}),a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})=(va_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}},a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right),\textrm{ if }a_{i}^{\epsilon}\neq(a_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})^{-1}.

(8)

The main motivation behind consideration of the automaton
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is that it will define the language
L_{H}
and be ergodic whenever
L_{H}
is irreducible, and at the same time will provide a convenient way of computing the entropy of
L_{H}
. All these will be revealed in detail in the next section. See, for example, Theorems
5.4
,
5.5
and Proposition
5.4
.

To describe the main properties of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
, first we need the following definition.

{defi}

H\leq F_{m}
is said to be
conjugacy reduced
if there does not exist
a\in\Sigma
such that every word from
L_{H}
is of the form
a^{-1}wa
. In other words,
H
is conjugacy reduced if there exist two words from
L_{H}
with different last letters.

{prop}

For
H\neq 1
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has exactly one initial state if and only if
H
is not conjugacy reduced. Moreover, in such case this state is isolated in the sense that there is no outgoing edge from it in the Moore diagram.

Proof.

By definition of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
, it has only one initial state if and only if there is only one incoming edge for the root vertex of the extended core
\widehat{\Delta}_{H}
. The last property for
\widehat{\Delta}_{H}
is equivalent to saying that all words from
L_{H}
end with the same later.

Finally, if the words in
L_{H}
start with
a^{-1}
and end with
a
for some
a\in\Sigma
, then the initial state in
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is
(v_{0},a)
, which does not have an outgoing edge, because edges from
(v_{0},a)
with label
a^{-1}
are not permitted by the definition of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
. Thus the proposition is proved.
∎

{lem}

If
H\leq F_{m}
is conjugacy reduced, then
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is an essential automaton and
L_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=L_{H}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
,
H
is conjugacy reduced means that
\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
contains at least two elements
(v_{0},a)
and
(v_{0},b)
for
a\neq b\in\Sigma
. This means that whichever state one can move from
q^{*}
in
\mathscr{D}_{H}
is possible to move from at least one state from
\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
(in fact, from precisely
|\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}|-1
states.) Therefore, the fact that
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is essential follows from
\mathscr{D}_{H}
being essential, see Proposition
5.2
.

On the other hand, every state that is possible to attain from the states
\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
in
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is possible to attain from
q^{*}
in
{\mathscr{D}_{H}}
. Therefore,
L_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=L_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}=L_{H}
.
∎

{prop}

If
H\leq F_{m}
is conjugacy reduced, then
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is deterministic and has homogeneous ambiguity
\deg(v_{0})-1
.

Proof.

The fact that
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is deterministic follows straightforwardly from the definition of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
. The fact that
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has homogeneous ambiguity
\deg(v_{0})-1
follows from the observations that
\mathscr{D}_{H}
is a DFA (hence, is unambiguous) and the states that can be attained from
q^{*}
in
\mathscr{D}_{H}
are precisely the states that can be attained from precisely
|\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}|-1
initial states of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
(note that except the initial states, the states of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
coincide). Therefore,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has homogeneous ambiguity
|\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}|-1=deg(v_{0})-1
.∎

5.4
Ergodicity of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}

The main goal of this subsection is to give a complete description of when
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is ergodic for finitely generated subgroups
H
of
F_{m}
. In Subsection
5.5
we discuss some of its consequences that are connected with the entropy of
L_{H}
. Everywhere in this section we assume that
H
is a non-trivial finitely generated subgroup of the free group
F_{m}=F(A)
,
\Sigma=A\cup A^{-1}
.

A finite state automaton is said to be
ergodic
if its Moore diagram, regarded as a directed graph, is strongly connected. That is for each ordered pair of vertices of the Moore diagram there is a path from the first to the second vertex. An alternative characterization of ergodicity of a finite state automaton is that the formal language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
that it defines is
irreducible
, which means that for each
w_{1},w_{2}\in L
there exists
w\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
w_{1}ww_{2}\in L
. See
[
4
]
. Yet another characterization of ergodicity is that the adjacency matrix
M
of the Moore diagram of the automaton is
irreducible
: if
M
is of size
m\times m
, then for each
1\leq i,j\leq m
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
(i,j)
-th coefficient of
M^{n}
is strictly positive. The latest property of ergodic automata of bounded ambiguity can be used to compute the entropy of the corresponding language, which we discuss later in Subsection
5.5
.

{prop}

1\neq H\leq F_{m}
is conjugacy reduced and cyclic if and only if there exist
a\neq b\in\Sigma
such that all words from
L_{H}
either start with
a^{-1}
and end with
b
or start with
b^{-1}
and end with
a
. Moreover, in such case
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has exactly two initial states,
(v_{0},a)
and
(v_{0},b)
, which belong to two different connected components in the Moore diagram of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
L_{H}
satisfies to the property from the statement. Then it follows from Proposition
5.3
that
H
is conjugacy reduced. Now, by contradiction assume that
H
is not cyclic. Then, let
a^{-1}w_{1}b
be the shortest word in
L_{H}
ending with
b
and let
a^{-1}w_{2}b\in L_{H}
be the shortest word ending with
b
that is not contained in the maximal cyclic subgroup of
H
containing
a^{-1}w_{1}b\in H
. Since
H
is cyclically reduced,
a\neq b^{-1}
. Then
1\neq red(b^{-1}w_{2}^{-1}w_{1}b)\in L_{H}
. However, since by the assumption no word in
L_{H}
starts with
b^{-1}
and ends with
b
, it must be that after the free cancellation of
b^{-1}w_{2}^{-1}w_{1}b
either the prefix or the suffix of
b^{-1}w_{2}^{-1}w_{1}b
cancels out. In the first case we end up with a word that is shorter than
a^{-1}w_{1}b
, in the second case we end up with a word that is shorter than
a^{-1}w_{2}b
. Therefore, we end up with a contradiction because of the minimality condition on the lengths of
a^{-1}w_{1}b
and
a^{-1}w_{2}b
. Thus
H
is cyclic.

Now assume that
H
is cyclic and conjugacy reduced. Then there exists
w\in H
that generates
H
. In other words,
L_{H}=\{w^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Since
H
is conjugacy reduced, the prefix of
w
is not the inverse of the suffix. Therefore, the prefix corresponds to
a^{-1}
, the suffix corresponds to
b
, and the “only if” part of the first statement of the proposition follows as well.

Now let us show the second statement. The condition that all words of
L_{H}
are of the form
(a^{-1}wb)^{\pm 1}
implies that the root vertex
v_{0}
of
\widehat{\Delta}_{H}
has exactly two incoming edges with corresponding labels
a
and
b
, and it has exactly two outgoing edges with the same labels. This means that
\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\mathscr{F}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\{(v_{0},a),(v_{0},b)\}
. Moreover, since by definition of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
there is no outgoing edge of
(v_{0},a)
with label
a^{-1}
in the Moore diagram, we have that the label of any accepted path that starts at
(v_{0},a)
starts with
b^{-1}
and hence ends with
a^{-1}
, hence is a loop. The same statement in regard with
(v_{0},b)
is true as well. Therefore, the second assertion of the proposition follows as well.
∎

\thm

Let
H
be a finitely generated subgroup of
F_{m}
. Then

\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is an ergodic automaton if and only if
H
is conjugacy reduced and non cyclic.

Proof.

First, assume that
H
is conjugacy reduced and non cyclic.

By Lemma
5.3
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is essential, which means that any state of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is on some admissible path. Therefore, since
\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}=\mathscr{F}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
, in order to show that
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is strongly connected, it is enough to show that for any ordered pair of different initial (=final) stats of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
, there is a directed path connecting the first state to the second one. (Note that, by Proposition
5.3
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has at least two different initial states.)

Let
(v_{0},a),(v_{0},b)
,
a\neq b\in\Sigma
, be an arbitrary pair of initial states. We want to show that there exists a path from
(v_{0},a)
to
(v_{0},b)
, which is equivalent to the existence of a word
w\in L_{H}
which does not start with
a^{-1}
and ends with
b
, as in such case
\delta_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}((v_{0},a),w)=(v_{0},b)
.

The fact that
(v_{0},b)\in\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
means that there exists
u\in L_{H}
such that it ends with letter
b
. Since by our assumption
H
is not cyclic, there exists
1\neq v\in L_{H}
such that
\langle u\rangle\cap\langle v\rangle=\{1\}
. Then, for large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the word
\bar{v}:=red(u^{-n}vu^{n})\in L_{H}
starts with
b^{-1}
and ends with
b
. Therefore,
\delta_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}((v_{0},a),\bar{v})=(v_{0},b)
. Thus we showed that if
H
is conjugacy reduced and non cyclic, then
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is ergodic.

Now assume that
H
is not conjugacy reduced. Then, by Proposition
5.3
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has exactly one initial state, which is isolated. Therefore, in such case
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is not ergodic.

Finally, assume that
H
is conjugacy reduced but cyclic. Then, by Proposition
5.4
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
has exactly two initial states which are not connected one to the other. Therefore, in such case
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is not ergodic either. Thus the theorem is proved.
∎

Combining Theorem
5.4
with Propositions
5.3
and
5.4
, we get the following corollary.

{cor}

If
|\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}|>2
, then
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is ergodic.

\rem

Besides the case when
H
is cyclic and conjugacy reduced, the only other case when
|\mathscr{I}_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}|=2
is when, for some
a\in\Sigma
,
L_{H}
consists of words only, and inclusively, of the forms
awa,awa^{-1},a^{-1}wa^{-1}
or
a^{-1}wa
. The latest corresponds, for example, to the case when
H=\langle aba^{-1},a^{2}\rangle<F(a,b)=F_{2}
.

{prop}

For
H\leq F_{m}
,
L_{H}
is irreducible if and only if
H
is conjugacy reduced and non cyclic.

Proof.

The ‘if’ part of the statement follows from Theorem
5.4
.

Now assume that
H
is not conjucagy reduced, then for some
a\in\Sigma
, elements of
L_{H}
are (reduced) words of the form
a^{-1}wa
. Since concatenation of any such words is not reduced, it means that
L_{H}
is not irreducible in such case.

Finally, assume that
H=\langle w\rangle
is cyclic. Then,
w,w^{-1}\in L_{H}
. However, there is no
v\in L_{H}
such that
wvw^{-1}
is also in
L_{H}
. Therefore,
L_{H}
is not irreducible in this case either.
Thus the proposition is proved.
∎

5.5
Entropy of
L_{H}

Recall that for a formal language
L
its
entropy
,
h(L)
, which is a fundamental numerical invariant of a language, is defined as

h(L)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\frac{\log|{B_{n}(L)}|}{n}},

(9)

where
B_{n}(L)
is the subset of
L
of words of length
n
.

If
L
is a language generated by an automaton
\mathscr{A}
, then by
h(\mathscr{A})
we denote
h(L(\mathscr{A}))
. If for
H\leq F_{m}
, then the entropy of
H
is
h(H)=h(L_{H})
.

One important feature of ergodicity of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is that the adjacency matrix
M_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is irreducible and is of bounded ambiguity (as follows from Theorem
5.4
and Proposition
5.3
). Therefore, one can apply the Perron-Frobenius theory to obtain the following theorem on entropy of
L_{H}
. (For discussion on Perron-Frobenius theory see Chapter 4 in
[
17
]
.)

\thm

If
H\leq F_{m}
is a conjugacy reduced and non cyclic finitely generated group, then the entropy of
L_{H}
is equal to
\log\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the maximal eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix
M_{\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}}
of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
.

\rem

If
H
is cyclic, then its entropy is equal to
0
. If
H
is not conjugacy reduced, then there is
g\in F_{m}
such that
gHg^{-1}\leq F_{m}
is conjugacy reduced, and
h(H)=h(gHg^{-1})
.

{defi}

[Base automaton]
Let
\mathscr{A}
be a finite automaton and let
(V,E)
be its underlying graph. Then
the base automaton
\breve{\mathscr{A}}
of
\mathscr{A}
is the automaton with the same underlying graph
(V,E)
and such that

1.

all states of
\breve{\mathscr{A}}
are at the same time initial and final states,

2.

all edges of its Moore diagram are labeled with different labels. To be more specific, we assume that each
e\in E
is labeled by the letter
x_{e}
.

To prove Theorem
5.5
, we need the following fact.

{prop}

If
\mathscr{A}
is an essential automaton with bounded ambiguity, then
h(\mathscr{A})=h(\breve{\mathscr{A}})
.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Since for conjugacy reduced and non cyclic finitely generated
H\leq F_{m}
, by Lemma
5.3
and Proposition
5.3
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is essential and of bounded ambiguity, then, by Proposition
5.5
,
h(\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H})=h(\breve{\mathscr{D}}_{H})
, where by
\breve{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
we denote the base automaton of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
. Additionally, by Theorem
5.4
,
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is an ergodic automaton, which implies that
\breve{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
is ergodic as well. Moreover, the adjacency matrices of
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
and
\breve{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
coincide and are irreducible. Therefore, the proof of Theorem
5.5
follows from Perron-Frobenius theory. See, for example, Theorem 4.4.4. in
[
17
]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

Assuming that
\mathscr{A}
is an essential automaton, for every vertex
v\in V
in its underlying graph
(V,E)
, there exist paths that connect
v
to an initial state and to a final state, respectively. For each
v
let us fix a pair
(\check{v},\hat{v})
of such paths, where
\check{v}
is a path that connects an initial state to
v
and
\hat{v}
is a path that connects
v
to a final state. Also, for each path
p
in
(V,E)
let us denote by
p_{-}
its origin and by
p_{+}
is terminus, and by
\phi(p)
let us denote its label in the Moore diagram of
\mathscr{A}
and by
\psi(p)
let us denote its label in the Moore diagram of
\breve{\mathscr{A}}
. Note that for each path
p
,
\psi(p)\in L(\breve{\mathscr{A}})
. Then we have the following map
\Lambda:L(\breve{\mathscr{A}})\rightarrow L(\mathscr{A})
: for each path
p
in
(V,E)
, define

\Lambda(\psi(p))=\phi(\check{p}_{-}p\hat{p}_{+}).

{lem}

There exists
C\in\mathbb{N}
such that for each path
p
from
(V,E)
,
|p|\leq|\Lambda(p)|\leq|p|+C
.

Proof.

The left inequality is obvious. For the right inequality, one can take
C=\max\{|\check{v}|\mid v\in V\}+\max\{|\hat{v}|\mid v\in V\}
.
∎

From Lemma
5.5
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we immediately get

\displaystyle\Lambda(B_{n}(L(\breve{\mathscr{A}})))\subseteq\bigcup_{i=0}^{C}B_{n+i}(L(\mathscr{A})).

(10)

{lem}

If
\mathscr{A}
has bounded ambiguity, then there exists
D>0
such that for all
w\in L(\mathscr{A})
,
|\Lambda^{-1}(w)|\leq D
.

Proof.

Let
K>0
be an upper bound of ambiguity of
\mathscr{A}
. Let
w\in L(\mathscr{A})
. Also, let
(u,v)\in V\times V
. Then the number of paths
p
in
(V,E)
such that
p_{-}=u
,
p_{+}=v
, and
\Lambda(\psi(p))=w
is bounded from above by
K
. The number of pairs
(u,v)\in V\times V
is equal to
|V|^{2}
. Therefore, one can take
D=K|V|^{2}
.
∎

{lem}

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
0\leq c_{n}\leq C
such that

\displaystyle|B_{n}(L(\mathscr{A}))|\leq|B_{n}(L(\breve{\mathscr{A}}))|\leq(C+1)D|B_{n+c_{n}}(L(\mathscr{A}))|.

(11)

Proof.

The left inequality of (
11
) is trivial. For the right one, note that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, by Lemma
5.5
and by (
10
), we get

D\sum_{i=0}^{C}|B_{n+i}(L(\mathscr{A}))|\geq|B_{n}(L(\breve{\mathscr{A}}))|.

Therefore, one can take
c_{n}
in (
11
) to be such that

|B_{n+c_{n}}(L(\mathscr{A}))|=\max\{|B_{n+i}(L(\mathscr{A}))|\mid 0\leq i\leq C\}.

∎

From Lemma
5.5
we immediately get that there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
and a sequence
\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
0\leq c_{n}\leq C^{\prime}
, such that

\frac{\log|B_{n}(L(\mathscr{A}))|}{n}\leq\frac{\log|B_{n}(L(\breve{\mathscr{A}}))|}{n}\leq\frac{\log C^{\prime}|B_{n+c_{n}}(L(\mathscr{A}))|}{n}.

Proposition
5.5
follows immediately from the last inequality.

6
Computing cogrowth series of
H

With an arbitrary subgroup
H
of
F_{m}
one associates the
growth function

\gamma_{H}(n)=|H_{n}|,

where
H_{n}
is the set of elements in
H
of length
n
with respect to the basis
A
of
F_{m}
i.e. length of the element is the length of the reduced word from
\Sigma^{*}
, where
\Sigma=A\cup A^{-1}
representing the element. Also, following
[
11
]
we introduce the
cogrowth series

H(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|H_{n}|z^{n}

(12)

The upper limit

\alpha_{H}=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}|H_{n}|^{1/n}

(13)

is called the
growth rate
of
H
with respect to the basis
A
of
F_{m}
. The radius of convergence of the series (
12
) is

R=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{H}}

and

R\geq\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m-1}\textrm{ as, for }H=F_{m},~~|H_{n}|=2m(2m-1)^{n-1}\textrm{ if }n\geq 1.

The cogrowth series (
12
) represents a function of complex variable
z\in\mathbb{C}
analytic at disc around
z=0
of radius
R\geq\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m-1}
. The major questions are: Under what conditions
H(z)
is rational, algebraic and belongs to distinguished class of analytic functions, like for instance the class of
D
-finite functions studied in
[
6
]
.

Let us recall the second author’s argument from
[
11
]
on rationality of
H(z)
(when
H
is finitely generated) using a Nielsen system of generators of
H
.

6.1
The Nielsen basis approach

Let
H=\langle w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\rangle
and let
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a Nielsen system of generators. Given two reduced words
u,v\in\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}
denote by
\beta(u,v)
the number of
a
-symbols (by
a
-symbols we mean elements of
\Sigma
) which will be cancelled when reducing the product
u\cdot v
. For each
i,1\leq i\leq k
and
\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}
let
H_{n}^{i,\epsilon}
be the set of words of length
n
in
H
that can be presented by
S
-reduced product of generators from
S\cup S^{-1},S=\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\}
ending with
w_{i}^{\epsilon}
. That is

u\in H_{n}^{i,\epsilon}\Longleftrightarrow u=red(w_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{l}}^{\epsilon_{l}}~w_{i}^{\epsilon})

for some
i_{1},\cdots,i_{l},\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{l}\in\{-1,1\}
and in the last product none of the factors
w_{i_{j}}^{\epsilon_{j}}
is inverse to the previous or next factor. Let us recall the corollary of the Statement 3.6 of
[
11
]
.

\thm

If
H
is a finitely generated subgroup of
F_{m}
, then
H(z)
is rational.

Proof.

Let
H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|H_{n}^{i,\epsilon}|z^{n}
.
Then

H(z)=1+\sum_{i,\epsilon}H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z).

The equation
(3.5)
from
[
11
]
shows that the functions
H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)
satisfy the system of linear equations.

H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)=z^{|w_{i}^{\epsilon}|}+\displaystyle\sum_{j,\epsilon^{\prime}}z^{|w_{i}^{\epsilon}|-\beta(w_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}},w_{i}^{\epsilon})}H_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}(z)

(14)

Taking the summation term to the left, we can rewrite the above system as follows

\displaystyle BY=Z

(15)

where
B
is a
2k\times 2k
matrix with
(i,\epsilon),(j,\epsilon^{\prime})
-th entry

B\left((i,\epsilon),(j,\epsilon^{\prime})\right)=\displaystyle\chi_{i}(j)-z^{|w_{i}^{\epsilon}|-\beta(w_{j}^{\epsilon^{\prime}},w_{i}^{\epsilon})},

where

\chi_{i}(j)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\textnormal{ if }i=j\\
0&\textnormal{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Y
and
Z
are
2k\times 1
column vectors whose
i
-th entries are

Y_{i}=H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z),~~Z_{i}=z^{|w_{i}^{\epsilon}|},

respectively. Observe that, when
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
|z|<1
, the determinant of the matrix
B
is non-zero. Hence the system (
14
) has a unique solution. Solving this system by standard methods (for instance using Cramer’s rule) we get a solution.

H_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)=\displaystyle\frac{P_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)}{Q_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)},i=1,\cdots,k;\epsilon\in\{-1,1\},

where
P_{i}^{\epsilon},Q_{i}^{\epsilon}
are polynomials and hence we get the rational expression for
H(z)

H(z)=1+\displaystyle\sum_{i,\epsilon}\frac{P_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)}{Q_{i}^{\epsilon}(z)}.

∎

6.2
The finite automata approach

In the previous section we have seen the construction of the DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
. It is a very old observation, going back to Chomsky and Schützenberger and even to A. Kolmogorov in view of his theory of finite Markov chains, that the growth series
L_{H}(z)
of the language
L(\mathscr{D}_{H})=L_{H}
which by Proposition
5.2
from the previous sections coincides with
H(z)
and are rational. Moreover, it can be computed using a standard method which often is called the transfer matrix method. See page 573 of
[
23
]
or Section V.5 and in particular Proposition V.6 of
[
5
]
. In this section, using the DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
we shall compute the cogrowth series
H(z)
. At the end, we shall present the computations of growth using Nielsen set of generators.

Let
M
be the adjacency matrix of the labelled directed graph
G
with
t
vertices. Assume that, for every vertex of the graph
G
, all outgoing edges carry distinct labels. Let
M^{n}
be the
n
th power of
M
. It is well known that the
(i,j)
th entry of
M^{n}
which we denote by
M^{n}(i,j)
is just the number of paths of length
n
from the
i
th vertex to the
j
th vertex of
G
. To evaluate the
M^{n}(i,j)
, we shall use the transfer matrix method. This method uses linear algebra to analyze the behavior of the
M^{n}(i,j)
. Following
[
23
]
, we define the growth series (also known as generating function) of paths in
G
from
i
to
j
.

\gamma_{ij}(G,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}M^{n}(i,j)z^{n}.

Observe that
\gamma_{ij}(G,z)
is the
(i,j)
th entry of the matrix

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}M^{n}z^{n}=\left(I-zM\right)^{-1}

where
I
is the identity matrix of dimension
t\times t
. In order to compute the
(i,j)
th entry
\gamma_{ij}(G,z)
, we recall Theorem 4.7.2 of
[
23
]
.

\thm

The growth series
\gamma_{ij}(G,z)
is given by

\gamma_{ij}(G,z)=\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{i+j}\det\left(I-zM:j,i\right)}{\det\left(I-zM\right)}

(16)

where
(B:j,i)
denotes the minor obtained by removing the
j
th row and
i
th column of
B.
Thus in particular
\gamma_{ij}(G,z)
is a rational function of
z
whose degree is strictly less than the multiplicity
n_{0}
of
0
as an eigenvalue of
M.

Let
L_{H}=L(\mathscr{D}_{H})
be the language accepted by the DFA
\mathscr{D}_{H}
constructed in the previous Section. Let
M_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}
be the adjacency matrix of the Moore diagram of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
and
L_{H}(z)
be the growth of
L_{H}
, i.e.

L_{H}(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{w\in L_{H}}z^{|w|}.

Recall that
\mathscr{D}_{H}
has
|Q_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}|=1+\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\widehat{V}}\deg(v)
states. In our numeration of states of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
we start with the initial state
q^{*}
first and then the states
(v_{k},a_{i}^{\epsilon_{i}})
where
k=0,\cdots,\left(|\widehat{V}|-1\right),i\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
. Let
\mathbf{i}
be the standard unit row vector of dimension
1\times|Q_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}|
and let the column vector
\mathbf{f}
be the characteristic vector of the set of final states of dimension
|Q_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}|\times 1
.

{prop}

H(z)=L_{H}(z)=\mathbf{i}\cdot\left(I-zM_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{f},

(17)

where
I
is the identity matrix of order
|Q_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}|\times|Q_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}|
.

Proof.

Recall that
L(\mathscr{D}_{H})=L_{H}=
set of reduced elements of
H
which implies that
H(z)=L_{H}(z)
. For every
w\in L_{H}
of length
n\geq 0
we have a unique admissible path
p
in the Moore diagram
G_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}
of
\mathscr{D}_{H}
such that
w=l(p)
. Therefore, we write the growth series
L_{H}(z)
as

\displaystyle H(z)=L_{H}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{q\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}}\displaystyle\gamma_{q^{*}q}(G_{\mathscr{D}_{H}},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}\cdot\left(I-zM_{\mathscr{D}_{H}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{f}.

∎

\rem

Observe that from (
9
) and (
13
) one deduces

\alpha_{H}=e^{h(L_{H})},

where
h(L_{H})
is the entropy of
L_{H}
.
