Primordial black holes from local features of the inflaton potential
Alexander Gallego Cadavid, Antonio Enea Romano

Abstract
Primordial black holes (PBH) can form in the early Universe from peaks of the primordial curvature perturbations. The statistics of the peaks determines the abundance of PBHs, and is related to the amplitude of the primordial curvature spectrum. We study single field inflationary models with local features of the inflaton potential which induce a growth of curvature perturbations on super-horizon scales, which can increase the spectrum, and consequently the predicted PBHs abundance. We compute the effects of the different parameters of the features on the PBHs abundance, giving some example of models compatible with observational constraints. The mechanism is general, and can induce the PBH production in diffent mass ranges by appropriately tuning the parameters, and we give a specific example producing PBHs with abundance compatible with asteroid mass constraints.



I
Introduction

In the standard cosmological model primordial curvature perturbations are the seeds of the large scale structure of the Universe.
When these perturbations are sufficiently large
[
1
]
primordial black holes (PBH) are formed, which could have important observational consequences
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
In this paper we investigate the PBH formation in single field models with local features in the inflation potential.
In the slow-roll regime curvature perturbation is freezing on super-horizon scale, but a temporary violation of the slow-roll regime due to feature in the potential can induce a super-horizon growth which can enhance the curvature spectrum around the scales affected by the super-horizon curvature growth.
The effects of these features were studied in the past with focus on non-gaussianity
[
9
]
, and as possible explanations to the cosmic microwave background (CMB) anomalies
[
10
]
, but as mentioned above the slow-roll violation associated to these features is also the main primordial curvature perturbation spectrum enhancement mechanism
[
11
]
, justifying the expectation of increased PBH production in these models.

While in slow-roll inflationary models
{\mathcal{R}}
is expected to be conserved on super-horizon scales
[
12
]
, if slow-roll is violated there can be a super-horizon evolution of
{\mathcal{R}}
, such as in globally adiabatic models
[
11
]
or inflation with a quasi-inflection point in the potential
[
13
]
.
The super-horizon growth of
{\mathcal{R}}
has profound implications because it can give rise to PBH production
[
14
]
, with different important observable effects.
We study the affects of the different feature parameters on the PBH abundance, and confirm that there is a high sensitivity, as pointed out in Ref.
[
15
]
.
By tuning the parameters of the feature it is possible to produce PBHs in different mass ranges, and we consider a specific example of a model producing PBHs with abundance compatible with current asteroid mass constraints.

II
Single field slow-roll inflation

We consider inflationary models with a single scalar field and a standard kinetic term according to the action
[
16
]

S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{1}{2}M^{2}_{Pl}R-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-V(\phi)\right],

(1)

where
M_{Pl}=(8\pi G)^{-1/2}
is the reduced Planck mass,

g_{\mu\nu}
the flat Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric,
R
the Ricci scalar, and
V
is the potential
energy of the inflaton field
\phi
. The variation of the action with respect to the metric
and the scalar field gives the Friedmann equation and the equation of motion of the inflaton

H^{2}\equiv\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{3M^{2}_{Pl}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V(\phi)\right),

(2)

\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\partial_{\phi}V=0,

(3)

where
H
is the Hubble parameter and we denote with dots and
\partial_{\phi}
the derivatives with respect to time and scalar field, respectively.
The definitions we use for the slow-roll parameters are

\displaystyle\epsilon\equiv-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\,\,\mbox{ and }\,\,\eta\equiv\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{\epsilon H}.

(4)

In order to study the production and abundance of PBH we will consider a potential energy given by
[
9
,
10
]

V(\phi)=V_{0}(\phi)+V_{\rm LF}(\phi)\,,

(5)

where
V_{0}(\phi)
is a featureless potential and
V_{\rm LF}
corresponds to a local feature (LF), first studied in Ref.
[
9
]
. For simplicity we will consider the case of a linear inflaton potential

\displaystyle V_{0}(\phi)=\gamma\phi\,.

(6)

In order to fulfill the CMB COBE/Planck normalization
[
17
,
18
]
, we chose as initial conditions
\gamma=3.2767\times 10^{-10},a(t_{i})=1,\phi(t_{i})=9.81369
and
\dot{\phi}(t_{i})=-3.05691\times 10^{-6}
, where
t_{i}=0
is the initial time of the integration and all units are given in Planck mass. From now on we adopt a system of units in which
c=\hbar=M_{Pl}=1

In this paper we study the abundance of PBH seeded by peaks of the primordial curvature perturbations spectrum of models with a local feature. The tuning of the parameters of the features allows to produce PBHs in different mass ranges.

III
Curvature perturbations

The study of curvature perturbations is attained by expanding perturbatively the action with respect to the background FRLW solution. In the comoving gauge the second order action for scalar perturbations is
[
19
]

S_{2}=\int dtd^{3}x\left[a^{3}\epsilon\dot{\mathcal{R}_{c}^{2}}-a\epsilon(\partial\mathcal{R}_{c})^{2}\right]\,.

(8)

Taking the Fourier transform of the Euler-Lagrange equations of the previous action and using the conformal time
d\tau\equiv dt/a
we obtain the equation of motion for the primordial curvature perturbation

\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\prime\prime}(k)+2\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\prime}(k)+k^{2}\mathcal{R}_{c}(k)=0,

(9)

where
z\equiv a\sqrt{2\epsilon}

[
20
]
,
k
is the comoving wave number, and primes denote derivatives with respect to conformal time. We take the Bunch-Davies vacuum
[
21
,
22
,
20
]

\mathcal{R}_{c}(\tau,k)=\frac{v(\tau,k)}{z(\tau)},

(10)

as the initial conditions for eq. (
9
), where

v(\tau,k)=\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}k\tau}}{\sqrt{2k}}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{k\tau}\right)\,.

(11)

IV
Super-horizon growth of
{\mathcal{R}}_{c}
in single field models

Following
[
11
]
, we will briefly review the mechanism by which curvature perturbations can grow on super horizon scales in single scalar field models.
The equation for the curvature perturbation on comoving slices can be written as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{a^{3}\epsilon}{c_{s}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\mathcal{R}}_{c}\right)-a\epsilon\Delta{\mathcal{R}}_{c}=0\,,

(12)

and after re-writing the time derivative in terms
of the derivative respect to the scale factor
a
, we can obtain the solution on super-horizon
scales

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{c}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\int^{a}\frac{da}{a}f(a)\,;\quad f(a)\equiv{\frac{c_{s}^{2}(a)}{Ha^{3}\epsilon(a)}}\,.

(13)

In slow-roll inflation
c_{s}^{2}
and
\epsilon
are
slowly varying, so that the integrand is asymptotically constant,
implying the
{\mathcal{R}}_{c}
conservation.

Since
\epsilon=-\dot{H}/H^{2}\ll 1
, we can neglect the time dependence of

H
in eq. (
13
), while
\epsilon
and
c_{s}^{2}

may still vary rapidly in time.
Assuming
\epsilon\approx a^{-n}
and
c_{s}^{2}\approx a^{q}
we get

f\propto a^{q+n-3}\,,

(14)

from which the condition for freezing is

q+n-3<0\,.

(15)

If the condition is violated, i.e.
q+n-3\geq 0
,
the solution (
13
) can grow on super-horizon scales. In single filed models with minimal gravity coupling
q=0
, which implied that
\epsilon
should decrease sufficiently rapidly. This is exactly what happens in the models we study in this paper, where the feature of the potential induces a temporary violation of the slow-roll regime, and a fast decrease of
\epsilon
, with
n>3
.

IV.1
Primordial spectrum of curvature perturbations

The two-point correlation function of primordial curvature perturbations is given
by
[
17
,
18
]

\Braket{\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{c}(\vec{k}_{1})\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{c}(\vec{k}_{2})}\equiv(2\pi)^{3}\frac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}P_{\mathcal{R}_{c}}(k)\delta^{(3)}(\vec{k}_{1}+\vec{k}_{2})\,,

(16)

where the modes are evaluated after horizon crossing time and the power spectrum of primordial curvature perturbations is defined as

P_{\mathcal{R}_{c}}(k)\equiv\frac{2k^{3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}|\mathcal{R}_{c}(k)|^{2}.

(17)

We define
k_{0}
as the scale exiting the horizon at the feature time
t_{0}
,
k_{0}=-1/\tau_{0}
, where
\tau_{0}
is the value of conformal time corresponding to
t_{0}
. Oscillations occur around
k_{0}
, and their location can be controlled by changing
\phi_{0}
.

A representative power spectrum behavior able to produce a significant amount of PBH around the scale
k_{0}
is shown in Fig.
7
. There it can seen the oscillations of the power spectrum around the featured scale.

V
Production and abundance of PBH

In this section we would briefly review the production and abundance of PBH. We will closely follow Ref.
[
23
]
.

The characteristic mass of PBH can be related to the mass contained within the Hubble horizon at the formation time
H^{-1}_{(\rm f)}
given by
[
24
]

M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}=\gamma\,M_{H}^{(\rm f)}\,,

(18)

where
\gamma
is an efficiency factor, the sub/super scripts “f” denote quantities evaluated at the time of PBH formation, and

M_{H}(t)\equiv\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho(t)H(t)^{-3},

(19)

is the time-dependent horizon mass.

The PBH mass at formation time can be related to the characteristic size of the perturbations that leave the horizon during inflation by

M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}(k_{\rm pbh})\simeq\left(\frac{\gamma}{0.2}\right)\left(\frac{g_{*}\left(T_{\rm f}\right)}{106.75}\right)^{-1/6}\left(\frac{k_{\rm pbh}}{1.753\times 10^{13}\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}}\right)^{-2}\,10^{-14}\,M_{\odot}\,.

(20)

where we have assumed that the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom in energy density and entropy are equal
[
25
,
23
]
.

The present-day PBH fraction
f_{\rm pbh}
is defined as the ratio between the energy density of dark matter and PBHs, and can be written in terms of the PBH mass
M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}
and the abundance at formation
\beta
by
[
23
]

f_{\rm pbh}\left(M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}\right)\simeq 7.52\times 10^{15}\left(\frac{\gamma}{0.2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{g_{*}\left(T_{\rm f}\right)}{106.75}\right)^{1/12}\left(\frac{M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}}{10^{-14}M_{\odot}}\right)^{-1/2}\,\beta\left(M^{(\rm f)}_{\rm pbh}\right)\,.

(21)

The PBH abundance at formation can be interpreted as the fraction
\beta
of local regions in the universe that has a density larger than a certain threshold
\delta_{c}
. The simplest formalism for estimating this abundance is the so-called Press-Schechter model of gravitational collapse
[
26
]
, where
\beta
is given by

\beta\equiv\frac{\rho_{{}_{\mathrm{{pbh}}}}}{\rho}\bigg|_{a=a_{\rm f}}=\int_{\delta_{\rm c}}^{\infty}P(\delta)\,\,{\rm d}\delta=\int_{\delta_{\rm c}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\delta}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\rm pbh}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{\rm pbh}}\right),

(22)

where
P(\delta)
is the probability distribution function (PDF), describing the probability that a given fluctuation have an overdensity
\delta
. In the last equality we assume that the density perturbations follow a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{\rm pbh}
. Moreover we explicitly assume that a perturbation will collapse to form black holes when its amplitude is larger than a critical value
\delta_{c}
.

Assuming
\delta_{\rm c}\gg\sigma_{\rm pbh}
, which we show below is a good approximation, eq. (
22
) can be written as

\beta\simeq\frac{1}{2}\rm{Erfc}\left(\frac{\delta_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm pbh}}\right)\simeq\frac{\sigma_{\rm pbh}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\delta_{\rm c}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{\rm c}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{\rm pbh}}\right),

(23)

where
{\rm Erfc}(x)
is the complementary error function. Notice from eq. (
23
) that the PBH abundance at the time of formation is exponentially sensitive to the variance of the distribution. Therefore, even a small change in the values of the model parameters, which might only slightly affect the primordial power spectrum, can have a substantial impact on the resulting PBH abundance. This exponential sensitivity underscores the critical importance of accurately determining the model parameters. These results are compatible with those of Refs.
[
15
,
27
]
.

The comoving curvature perturbations and the abundance of PBH

The power spectrum of over-density is related to the comoving curvature perturbation by

\mathcal{P}_{\delta}(k)\simeq\frac{16}{81}\left(\frac{k}{aH}\right)^{4}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}_{c}}(k).

(24)

The variance of density contrast can be related to the primordial power spectrum as
[

\sigma^{2}_{\rm pbh}(R)\equiv\langle\delta^{2}\rangle_{R}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\ln q\,\,\mathcal{W}^{2}(q,R)\,\mathcal{P}_{\delta}(q)\simeq\frac{16}{81}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\ln q\,\,\mathcal{W}^{2}(q,R)\,(qR)^{4}\,\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(q)

(25)

where
\mathcal{W}
is a window function smoothing
\delta
over the comoving scale
R\approx k^{-1}\approx(aH)^{-1}
. In this paper we will use the volume-normalized Gaussian window function, whose Fourier transform is given by

\mathcal{W}(k,R)=\exp\left({-\frac{k^{2}R^{2}}{2}}\right)\,.

(26)

VI
Production of asteroid mass PBHs

As mentioned before, the parameter space of our model allows for the production of PBH of different masses, ranging from asteroid masses to hundreds of solar masses. As can be seen in eq. (
20
) the PBH mass at formation time is related to the scale of the perturbation at horizon exit. Thus we can tune the parameter
k_{0}
, or equivalently
\phi_{0}
, to produce PBH of the desired mass. As an example of the general mechanism, in this section we will consider features producing PBHs in the the asteroid mass range (approximately
10^{-17}\lesssim M_{\mathrm{PBH}}\left[M_{\odot}\right]\lesssim 10^{-12}
). The results are shown in Figs.
11
-
13
. In this case the range of scales associated with PBH formation is
10^{12}\lesssim k_{\rm pbh}\,[\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}]\,\lesssim 10^{15}
and we will set the feature scale at
k_{0}=1.753\times 10^{13}
Mpc
-1
. In Figs.
11
-
13
it can be seen that eq. (
23
) is a good approximation to calculate the abundance at formation
\beta
.

The asteroid mass range is important for several reasons
[
7
,
8
]
. Asteroid-mass PBHs can be detected through microlensing events, where the PBH’s gravitational field magnifies the light from a background star. Surveys like the Optical Gravitational Lensing Experiment (OGLE) can detect PBHs in this mass range, providing constraints on their abundance. These PBHs are potential dark matter candidates, exhibiting unique dynamical and observational signatures. Furthermore, asteroid-mass PBHs could produce a stochastic gravitational wave background detectable by instruments like the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA), offering insights into their abundance and distribution.

]
contraints. The orange and blue shaded areas in the figure correspond to the evaporation and microlensing constraints, respectively

]
.

VII
Conclusions

We have studied the effects of local features of the inflation potential on the abundance of PBHs.
These features only modify the potential in a limited range of the scalar field values, and consequently only affect the spectrum in a narrow range of scales, which leave the horizon during the time interval corresponding to the modification of the potential.
We have studied the effects of the different parameters of the feature on PBH abundance, showing how they provide a flexible and general mechanism able to produce PBHs in different mass ranges.
We have then given a specific example of a model producing PBHs with abundance compatible with the asteroid mass constraints, confirming previous results
[
15
]
about high sensitivity of the PBH abundance to the model parameters.

In this paper we have assumed that density perturbations follow a Gaussian distribution, but in presence of features non gaussianity of the primordial perturbations can be important, and this could have important effects on the PBH abundance calculation
[
33
]
. In the future it will be interesting to compute the non-gaussian contributions to the PBH abundance in the local features models we have considered.
