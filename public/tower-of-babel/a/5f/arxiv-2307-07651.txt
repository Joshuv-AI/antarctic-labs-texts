An Overview and Comparison of Spectral Bundle Methods for Primal and Dual Semidefinite Programs Thanks: This work is supported by NSF ECCS-2154650. Corresponding author: Yang Zheng (zhengy@eng.ucsd.edu).
Feng-Yi Liao, Lijun Ding, Yang Zheng

Abstract
The spectral bundle method developed by Helmberg and Rendl is well-established for solving large-scale semidefinite programs (SDPs) in the dual form, especially when the SDPs admit low-rank primal solutions . Under mild regularity conditions, a recent result by Ding and Grimmer has established fast linear convergence rates when the bundle method captures the rank of primal solutions . In this paper, we present an overview and comparison of spectral bundle methods for solving both primal and dual SDPs. In particular, we introduce a new family of spectral bundle methods for solving SDPs in the primal form. The algorithm developments are parallel to those by Helmberg and Rendl, mirroring the elegant duality between primal and dual SDPs. The new family of spectral bundle methods also achieves linear convergence rates for primal feasibility, dual feasibility, and duality gap when the algorithm captures the rank of the dual solutions . Therefore, the original spectral bundle method by Helmberg and Rendl is well-suited for SDPs with low-rank primal solutions , while on the other hand, our new spectral bundle method works well for SDPs with low-rank dual solutions . These theoretical findings are supported by a range of large-scale numerical experiments. Finally, we demonstrate that our new spectral bundle method achieves state-of-the-art efficiency and scalability for solving polynomial optimization compared to a set of baseline solvers SDPT3 , MOSEK , CDCS , and SDPNAL+ .

Introduction

Semidefinite programs (SDPs) are an important class of convex optimization problems that minimize a linear function in the space of positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices subject to linear equality constraints
[
1
]

\displaystyle\min_{X}

\displaystyle\langle C,X\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle\langle A_{i},X\rangle=b_{i},\hskip 10.00002pti=1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},

\displaystyle\max_{y,Z}

\displaystyle b^{\mathsf{T}}y

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle Z+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}=C,

\displaystyle Z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},

where

are the problem data,

when the dimension is clear from the context or not important), and

denotes the standard trace inner product on the space of symmetric matrices.

SDPs offer a powerful mathematical framework that has gained significant attention for decades

In theory, one can solve any SDP instance up to arbitrary precision in polynomial time using second-order interior point methods (IPMs)

At each iteration of second-order IPMs, one usually needs to solve a linear system with a coefficient matrix (i.e., the Schur complement matrix) being generally dense and ill-conditioned. Consequently,

Improving the scalability of SDPs has gained significant attention in recent years; see

In particular, first-order methods (FOMs) are at the forefront of developing scalable algorithms for solving large-scale SDPs thanks to their low complexity per iteration. For instance, the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) is used to solve large-scale SDPs in the dual form

Some efficient ALM-based algorithms have recently been developed to solve large-scale SDPs. 2.1
Strict complementarity of SDPs

Throughout this paper, we make the following standard assumptions for well-behaved SDPs
eq.

P
and
eq.

D
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The matrices
A_{i},i=1,\ldots,m
in
eq.

P
and
eq.

D
are linearly independent.

Assumption 2.2
.

The SDPs
eq.

P
and
eq.

D

2.1
allows us to uniquely determine
y
from a given dual feasible
Z

y
is unique in

We denote the set of primal optimal solutions to

and the set of dual optimal solutions to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\mid p^{\star}=\langle C,X\rangle,\mathcal{A}(X)=b,X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}}\right\},

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(y,Z)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{S}^{n}\mid d^{\star}=b^{\mathsf{T}}y,Z+\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)=C,Z\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}}\right\}.

(1b)

2.2
ensures that
\mathcal{P}^{\star}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathcal{D}^{\star}\neq\emptyset
. In addition, if

\mathcal{P}^{\star}
and

are nonempty and compact
[
29

Proposition 2.1

(
[
29
, Section 2]
)
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, the mapping
\mathcal{A}
is surjective and the optimal solution sets
\mathcal{P}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
are nonempty and compact.

It is clear that
\mathcal{P}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
are closed. Given any

and a strict primal feasible point

\langle C,\hat{X}\rangle-b^{\mathsf{T}}y^{\star}=\langle\hat{X},Z^{\star}\rangle\geq 0

This is impossible due to the finite duality gap, and thus, any optimal

is bounded.
2.1
ensures that

is a surjective mapping, which means

is injective. Thus, any optimal

\mathcal{D}^{\star}
is bounded. Similarly, the existence of a strictly feasible point

The following result is a version of the KKT optimality condition for the SDPs

P
and
eq.

Lemma 2.1

(
[

)

Given a pair of primal and dual feasible solutions

and
(y^{\star},Z^{\star})
, they are optimal if and only if
there exists an orthonormal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with

, such that

\displaystyle X^{\star}=Q\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot Q^{\mathsf{T}},\hskip 10.00002ptZ^{\star}=Q\cdot\mathrm{diag}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\cdot Q^{\mathsf{T}}

(2)

and
\lambda_{i}w_{i}=0,i=1,\ldots,n
.

Given a pair of optimal solutions
X^{\star}
and
(y^{\star},Z^{\star})
, the complementary slackness condition
(
2
)
is equivalent to
Z^{\star}X^{\star}=0
(
X^{\star}
and
Z^{\star}
commutes so they share a common set of eigenvectors as the columns of

). This implies that

\displaystyle\text{rank}(X^{\star})+\text{rank}(Z^{\star})\leq n,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{range}(Z^{\star})\subset\text{null}(X^{\star}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{range}(X^{\star})\subset\text{null}(Z^{\star}).

We now introduce the notion of strict complementarity for a pair of optimal solutions.

Definition 2.1

(
[
44
, Definition 4]
)
.

A pair of primal and dual optimal solutions

2.2
Exact penalization for constrained convex optimization

Consider a constrained convex optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle f(x)

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle g_{i}(x)\leq 0,\hskip 10.00002pti=1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}_{0},

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\ldots,m
are (possibly nondifferentiable) convex functions, and
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set (which are defined by some simple constraints). The idea of exact penalty methods is to reformulate the constrained optimization problem
eq.

3
by a problem with simple constraints. In particular, upon defining an exact penalty function

P(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\;\max\{0,g_{i}(x)\},

we consider a penalized problem

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(x):=f(x)+\rho P(x)

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}_{0},

where
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter. When choosing
\rho
large enough, problems (
3
) and (
4
) are equivalent to each other in the sense that they have the same optimal value and solution set.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
38
, Theorem 7.21]
)
.

Suppose that problem (
3
) satisfies Slater’s constraint qualification. There exists a constant
\rho_{0}\geq 0
such that for each
\rho>\rho_{0}
, a point
\hat{x}
is an optimal solution of (
3
) if and only if it is an optimal solution of (
4
). In particular, we can choose
\rho_{0}=\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\lambda\|_{\infty}
, where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the set of optimal Lagrange multipliers associated with
g_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,m
.

Therefore, we can transform some nonsmooth constraints that are hard to handle in (
3
) into the nonsmooth cost function of (
4
). Then, we can apply cutting plane or bundle methods to solve the nonsmooth optimization (
4
). However, it should be noted that the resulting problem (
4
) may be difficult to solve if the penalty parameter
\rho
is too large. As we will discuss in
Section

3
, in some SDPs that arise from practical applications, the penalty parameter
\rho
is known
a priori

[
29
,
19
,
46
]
.

2.3
The cutting plane and bundle methods

In this subsection, we briefly overview the generic bundle method; see
[
38
, Chapter 7]
for details. Consider a generic nonsmooth constrained convex optimization

f^{\star}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;f(x),

(5)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex but not necessarily differentiable and
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set. It is clear that problem
eq.

5
includes
eq.

4
as a special case.

The simplest method for solving
eq.

5
is arguably the
subgradient method
, which constructs a sequence of points
x_{t}
iteratively by updating

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x_{t}-\tau_{t}g_{t}),\hskip 10.00002ptt=1,2,\ldots

where
g_{t}\in\partial f(x_{t})
is a subgradient of
f(\cdot)
at the current point
x_{t}
, and
\tau_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is a step size, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x)
denotes the orthogonal projection of the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\mathcal{X}_{0}
. Recall that for a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a vector
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a subgradient of
f
at
x
if

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle,\;\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The set of all subgradients of
f
at
x
is called the subdifferential, denoted by
\partial f(x)
.

With mild assumptions and an appropriate choice of decreasing step sizes, the subgradient method is guaranteed to generate a converging sequence
\{x_{t}\}
to an optimal solution of problem (
5
) (see
[
38
, Theorem 7.4]
). Despite the simplicity of subgradient methods, it is generally challenging to develop reliable and efficient step size rules for practical optimization instances.

2.3.1. The cutting plane method.

Another useful way to utilize subgradients is the idea of the
cutting plane method
, which solves a lower approximation of the function
f(x)
at every iteration. Here, we assume that
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is compact in
eq.

5
; otherwise, we consider minimizing
f(x)
over
\mathcal{X}_{0}\cap\mathcal{C}
where
\mathcal{C}
is a compact set containing an optimal solution.

The basic idea of the cutting plane method is to use the subgradient inequality to construct lower approximations of
f(\cdot)
. In particular, at iteration
t
, having points
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t}
, function values
f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}),\ldots,

f(x_{t})
, and the corresponding subgradients
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{t}
, we construct a lower approximation using a piece-wise affine function

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{t}(x)=\max_{i=1,\ldots,t}\;\;f(x_{i})+\langle g_{i},x-x_{i}\rangle.

(6)

By definition of subgradients, it is clear that
f(x)\geq\hat{f}_{t}(x),\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Starting from a triple
\{x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{0},f(x_{1}),g_{1}\in\partial f(x_{1})\}
, the cutting plane method solves the following master problem to generate the next point,

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;\hat{f}_{t}(x),\hskip 10.00002ptt=1,2,\ldots.

When
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is a convex set defined by simple constraints (e.g., a polyhedron), the above problem becomes a linear program (LP) for which very efficient algorithms exist.
The sequence generated by the cutting plane method is guaranteed to satisfy that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(x_{t})=f^{\star}

[
38
, Theorem 7.7]
. However, the theoretical convergence rate is rather slow; it generally takes
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{n})
iterations to reach
f(x_{t})-f^{\star}\leq\epsilon

[
47
, Section 5.2]
.
The practical convergence of the cutting plane method may be faster.

2.3.2. The bundle method.

The
bundle method
improves the convergence rate and numerical behavior of the cutting plane method by incorporating a regularization strategy. Unlike the cutting plane method that only considers the lower approximation function, the bundle method updates its iterates by solving a regularized master problem (i.e., a proximal step to the lower approximation model
\hat{f}_{t}(x)
):

\displaystyle x_{t+1}^{\star}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;\hat{f}_{t}(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-\omega_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

(7)

where
\omega_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the current reference point and
\alpha>0
penalizes the deviation from
\omega_{t}
. The bundle method only updates the iterate
\omega_{t}
when the decrease of the objective value
f(\cdot)
is at least a fraction of the decrease that the approximated model
\hat{f}_{t}(\cdot)
predicts. In particular, letting
0<\beta<1
, if

\displaystyle\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(\omega_{t})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)\leq f(\omega_{t})-f(x_{t+1}^{\star})

(8)

then we set
\omega_{t+1}=x_{t+1}^{\star}
(
descent step
); otherwise, we set
\omega_{t+1}=\omega_{t}
(
null step
). In any case, the subgradient
g_{t+1}\in\partial f(x_{t+1}^{\star})
at the new point
x_{t+1}^{\star}
is used to update the lower approximation
\hat{f}_{t+1}(x)
, e.g., using
eq.

6
.

The seemingly subtle modifications above have a rather surprising consequence: the cost value
f(\omega_{t})
generated by the bundle method converges to
f^{\star}
for any constant
\alpha>0
with a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{3})
when the objective function is Lipschitz continuous
[
48
]
. Faster convergence rates appear under different assumptions of
f(x)
; see
[
39
, Table 1]
for a detailed comparison. Note that subgradient methods rely on very carefully controlled decreasing stepsizes which might be inefficient and unreliable in practice, and the cutting plane method has a slow convergence rate theoretically. On the contrary, the bundle method appears more suitable to solve the nonsmooth problem
eq.

5
.

4.1

For notational convenience, we denote the cost function in

\displaystyle F(X):=\langle C,X\rangle+\rho\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X),0\}.

Directly applying the bundle method in

4.1.1. Lower approximation models.

As discussed in
Section

2.3.3
, one key step in the bundle method is to construct a valid lower approximation model of
F(X)
at each iteration
t
. Similar to (
6
), one natural choice is to use a piece-wise affine function,

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{t}(X)

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\ldots,t}\;\;\langle C,X_{i}\rangle+\rho\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X_{i}),0\}+\langle g_{i},X-X_{i}\rangle,

where
X_{i},i=1,\ldots,t
are the past iterates, and
g_{i}\in\partial F(X_{i}),i=1,\ldots,t
are subgradients.
Via simple derivations, each affine function corresponding to
X_{i}
becomes

\langle C,X_{i}\rangle+\rho\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X_{i}),0\}+\langle g_{i},X-X_{i}\rangle\\
\!=\!\begin{cases}\langle C,X_{i}\rangle+\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v_{i}v_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},-X}}\right\rangle,&\text{if }\lambda_{\max}(-X_{i})>0,\\
\langle C,X_{i}\rangle,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\\

(21)

where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a normalized eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{\max}(-X_{i})
.

For this special cost function,
one key idea of the original spectral bundle method in
[
19
]
is to improve the lower bound
eq.

21
using infinitely many affine minorants. In particular, at iteration

, we compute a matrix
P_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
with some small value

and orthonormal columns (i.e.,
P_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}P_{t}=I\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
), and define a lower approximation function

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{P_{t}}(X)

\displaystyle=\langle C,X\rangle+\rho\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},-X}}\right\rangle.

(22)

It is clear that
F(X)\geq\hat{F}_{P_{t}}(X),\forall X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
thanks to the fact that

\displaystyle\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X),0\}=\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1}\langle S,-X\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall X\in\mathbb{S}^{n},

and

\displaystyle\{P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\mid S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1\}\subset\{S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\mid\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1\}.

Meanwhile, it is not difficult to check that if
r=1
, and
P_{t}=v_{t}
with
v_{t}
being the top eigenvector of
-X_{t}
, then
\hat{F}_{P_{t}}(X)
defined in
eq.

22
is reduced to the approximation function in
eq.

21
with

. Thus, when choosing

and selecting

spanning

, we have a strictly better lower approximation using
eq.

22
than the simple linear function
eq.

21
based on one subgradient. In principle, the columns of

should consist of both top eigenvectors of the current iterate

and the accumulation of spectral information from past iterates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1}

[
19
,
29
]
.

Therefore, by construction,

in

[

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{(\bar{W}_{t},P_{t})}(X)=\langle C,X\rangle+\rho\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\gamma\geq 0,\gamma+\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\gamma\bar{W}_{t}+P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},-X}}\right\rangle.

4.2
Computational details

At every iteration
t
, we need to solve the subproblem
eq.

24
, which is the main computation in
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
.
Therefore, it is crucial to solve the master problem
eq.

24
efficiently.
We summarize the computational details of solving
eq.

24
in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

The master problem
eq.

24
is equivalent to the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t},\penalty\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\;\langle W-C,\Omega_{t}\rangle-\langle b-\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{t}),y\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|W-C+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right\|^{2},

(29)

where
the constraint set is defined as

\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}:=\{\gamma\bar{W}_{t}+P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\;|\;S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\gamma\geq 0,\gamma+\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq\rho\}.

(30)

The optimal
X
in
eq.

24
is recovered by

\displaystyle X_{t+1}^{\star}=\Omega_{t}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{t}^{\star}-C+\mathcal{A}^{*}(y_{t}^{\star})}}\right),

(31)

where
(W_{t}^{\star},y_{t}^{\star})
is a minimizer of
eq.

29
.

Proof.

Our proof relies on the strong duality of convex optimization. Upon applying the definition of
\hat{F}_{(\bar{W}_{t},P_{t})}(\cdot)
in (
23
), it is clear that
eq.

24
becomes

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\langle C,X\rangle+\rho\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\gamma\geq 0,\gamma+\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq 1}\;\;\langle\gamma\bar{W}_{t}+P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},-X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\gamma\geq 0,\gamma+\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq\rho}\;\;\langle C,X\rangle+\langle\gamma\bar{W}_{t}+P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},-X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}}\;\;\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2},

where the first equality brings the constant
\rho
into the constraint, and the second equality applies a change of variables
W=\gamma\bar{W}_{t}+P_{t}SP_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}
and uses the set
\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}
defined as
eq.

30
. Since
\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}
is bounded, by strong duality
[
53
, Corollary 37.3.2]
, we can switch the min-max order
and have the following equivalency

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}}\;\;\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}=\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}}\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}.

Note that the inner minimization is an equality-constrained quadratic program,

\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2},

(32)

which can be simplified by considering its dual formulation. Specifically, we introduce a dual variable
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and construct the Lagrangian for
eq.

32
as follows

\displaystyle L(X,y)

\displaystyle=\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}+y^{\mathsf{T}}(b-\mathcal{A}(X)),

which is strongly convex in
X
. The dual function for
eq.

32
is given by
g(y):=\min_{X}L(X,y)
, where the unique minimizer
X
is

\displaystyle X=\Omega_{t}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W-C+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right).

(33)

Therefore, the dual function becomes

g(y)=\langle C-W,\Omega_{t}\rangle+\langle b-\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{t}),y\rangle-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|W-C+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right\|^{2}.

By strong duality of
eq.

32
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}}\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\;\;\langle C-W,X\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X-\Omega_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\;\langle C-W,\Omega_{t}\rangle+\langle b-\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{t}),y\rangle-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|W-C+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{W\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{t},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\;\langle C-W,\Omega_{t}\rangle+\langle b-\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{t}),y\rangle-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|W-C+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right\|^{2},

which is clearly equivalent to
eq.

29
. Finally, the optimal
X
in
eq.

31
is recovered in
eq.

33
once we obtain the optimal dual variables
y_{t}^{\star}
and
W_{t}^{\star}
from solving
eq.

29
. This completes the proof.
∎

After the first iteration, if

Then, the rest of the iterations are naturally feasible to the affine constraint, i.e.,

•

(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
outputs iterates
(W_{t}^{\star},y_{t}^{\star})
that 1)
exactly
satisfy the dual affine constraint (by construction) and the primal PSD constraint
(\lambda_{\min}(W_{t}^{\star})\geq 0)
, but 2)
do not exactly
satisfy the primal affine constraint
\|\mathcal{A}(W_{t}^{\star})-b\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
and the dual PSD constraint
\lambda_{\min}(C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(\omega_{t+1}))\geq-\epsilon
.

•

Each iteration of
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
and
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
requires solving a small quadratic SDP in the form of
eq.

34
. When choosing the same values for
r_{\mathrm{p}}
and
r_{\mathrm{c}}
, the subproblems from
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
and
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
have the same dimension, and thus the computational complexity is very similar (although their constructions of
eq.

34
are slightly different; see
Appendix

C
).

Both
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
and
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
have very similar sublinear and linear convergence rates, as shown in
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
and
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
, respectively. However, we here highlight a key difference in the condition for linear convergence.
When constructing the lower approximation model at each iteration,
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
selects the top
r_{\mathrm{c}}
eigenvectors of
-X^{\star}_{t+1}
to approximate the null space of the primal optimal solutions
X^{\star}
, while
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
uses
r_{\mathrm{c}}
eigenvectors of
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\omega_{t+1})-C
to approximate the null space of the dual optimal solutions
Z^{\star}
. If the null space of the primal optimal solutions in
\mathcal{P}^{\star}
has a smaller dimension than that of the dual optimal solutions in
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\max_{X^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}^{\star}}\dim(\text{null}(X^{\star}))\ll\max_{(y^{\star},Z^{\star})\in\mathcal{D}^{\star}}\dim(\text{null}(Z^{\star})),

(46a)

then the master problem in
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
can select a smaller number of eigenvectors, leading to a smaller PSD constraint in
eq.

34
. This is reflected in the linear convergence condition on
r_{c}
; see
eqs.

36
and
45
. In this case, it will be more numerically beneficial to apply
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
that solves the primal SDPs directly as it is easier to solve the subproblem
eq.

34
at each iteration. If, on the other hand, we have the following relationship

\displaystyle\max_{X^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}^{\star}}\dim(\text{null}(X^{\star}))\gg\max_{(y^{\star},Z^{\star})\in\mathcal{D}^{\star}}\dim(\text{null}(Z^{\star})),

(46b)

then it will be more numerically beneficial to apply
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
that solves the dual SDPs directly.

Remark 5.2

(Low-rank primal solutions versus low-rank dual solutions)
.

Note that
Equation

46a
implies that the dual SDP
eq.

D
admits low-rank optimal solutions (i.e.,
\mathrm{rank}(Z^{\star})
is small), while
Equation

46b
indicates that the primal SDP
eq.

P
has low-rank optimal solutions (i.e.,
\mathrm{rank}(X^{\star})
is small). Therefore, it is crucial to choose the appropriate algorithm to solve the problem if we have prior knowledge of the rank property of SDPs. For instance, SDP-based optimization problems from sum-of-square relaxation and their applications
[
35
,
36
]
are likely to admit low-rank dual optimal solutions in
eq.

D
. On the other hand, SDP relaxation of combinatorial problems such as Max-Cut
[
17
]
, matrix completion
[
52
]
, and phase retrieval
[
55
]
are likely to admit low-rank primal solutions in
eq.

P
. We provide numerical experiments of SOS optimization and Max-Cut in
Section

6
, which indeed validate the effect of the rank property on the convergence behavior of
Algorithms

2
and
3
. We finally note that the low-rank properties also depend on how SDPs are formulated in different applications; see the conversion between primal and dual SDPs in
Section

C.4
.

6.1.2. Suboptimality measures.

For the pair of primal and dual SDPs

and

\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}
has a weight
w_{ij}=w_{ji}
. The max-cut problem aims to find a maximum cut that separates the vertices into two different groups. This can be formulated as a binary quadratic program
[
50
]

\min_{x_{i}^{2}=1,i=1,\ldots,n}\;\;\frac{1}{4}x^{\mathsf{T}}Lx,\\

(48)

where
L\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
is the Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
defined as
L_{ij}=\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}
if
i=j
, and
L_{ij}=-w_{ij}
otherwise. A well-known semidefinite relaxation
[
50
]
for the Max-Cut problem
eq.

48
is

\displaystyle\min_{X}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\langle L,X\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle X_{ii}=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}.

If the optimal solution of SDP relaxation
Equation

49
satisfies
\mathrm{rank}(X^{\star})=1
, then the SDP relaxation
Equation

49
is exact and one can recover a globally optimal solution to
Equation

48
. However, the rank-one solution may not exist. Instead, many max-cut instances admit low-rank optimal solutions:
1<\mathrm{rank}(X^{\star})\ll n
, as observed in
[
17
, Section 1.3.2]
,
[
29
, Section 5.1]
. For these SDP instances, we expect that
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
exhibits faster linear convergence when choosing a small value of
r_{\mathrm{c}}
, while
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
only has slower sublinear convergence for the same choice of
r_{\mathrm{c}}
.

We run both

that
\mathop{\bf tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}y_{i}}}\right)=b^{\mathsf{T}}y,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented an overview and comparison of spectral bundle methods for solving primal and dual SDPs. All the existing results focus on solving dual SDPs. We have established a family of spectral bundle methods for solving primal SDPs directly. The algorithm developments mirror the elegant duality between primal and dual SDPs. We have presented the sublinear convergence rates for this family of spectral bundle methods and shown that the algorithm enjoys linear convergence with proper parameter choice and low-rank dual solutions. The convergence behaviors and computational complexity of spectral bundle methods for both primal and dual SDPs are in general similar, but they have different features. It is clear that the existing spectral bundle methods are well-suited for SDPs with
low-rank primal solutions
, and our new spectral bundle method works well for SDPs with
low-rank dual solutions
. These theoretical findings are supported by a range of large-scale numerical experiments. We have further demonstrated that our new spectral bundle method achieves state-of-the-art efficiency and scalability when solving the SDP relaxations from polynomial optimization.

Potential future directions include incorporating other types of constraints (such as nonnegative, second-order cone constraints, etc.), considering second-order information
[
33
]
for the lower approximation, and analyzing the algorithm performance when the subproblem
eq.

24
is solved inexactly
[
61
]
. Finally, we remark that our current prototype implementation shows promising numerical performance, and it would also be very interesting to further develop reliable and efficient open-source implementations of these spectral bundle methods.

Proof.

The proof is the same as in

Lemma A.2

(
The number of consecutive null steps is bounded by the proximal gap).

A descent step, at iteration

, followed by

T\leq\frac{8G_{k+1}^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\alpha\Delta_{k+T}},

. If

\displaystyle T\leq\dfrac{8M^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\alpha\Delta_{k+T}}.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same that in
[
39
, Lemma 5.2]
.
Consider some descent step
k
, followed by
T
consecutive null step. Define the proximal subproblem gap at

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}:=f(\omega_{k+1})-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}}}\right).

Note that every null step has the same reference point
\omega_{k+1}
. The core of this proof is to show the inequality

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}\alpha\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{8G_{k+1}^{2}}.

(A.2)

Before proving this inequality, let us show how it completes the proof first. After

\hat{f}_{k+T}(\cdot)\leq f(\cdot)
in

ensures that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k+T}\geq\Delta_{k+T}
. Thus, to bound

, it is sufficient to show that the reversed inequality

that bounds the number of steps for a recursive relation and setting the target accuracy

(note that

), we conclude the number of consecutive null steps

\displaystyle T\leq\frac{8G_{k+1}^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\alpha\Delta_{k+T}}.

Now, let us focus on the derivation of

A.2
. Consider some null step
k<t\leq k+T
. We define the necessary lower bound given by

9c

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{t+1}(x):=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\langle s_{t+1},x-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle,\ f(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\langle g_{t+1},x-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle}}\right\}\leq\hat{f}_{t+1}(x),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{0},

and the solution of a proximal step made by

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\frac{\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)}{\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}}\right\},

\displaystyle y_{t+2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{t+1}g_{t+1}+(1-\theta_{t+1})s_{t+1}+\hat{h}_{t+1}}}\right),

where
\hat{h}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(y_{t+2})
.
Hence, the objective of the proximal subproblem can be lower bounded by

\displaystyle f(x_{t+2}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+2}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{t+1}(x_{t+2}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+2}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{t+1}(y_{t+2})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y_{t+2}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\langle s_{t+1},y_{t+2}-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle)+(1-\theta_{t+1})(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\langle g_{t+1},y_{t+2}-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y_{t+2}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\theta_{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)-\frac{\theta_{t+1}^{2}}{2\alpha}\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{h}_{t+1}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\theta_{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)-\frac{\theta_{t+1}^{2}}{2\alpha}\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2},

where the equality uses the definition of

and the fact that

since

by the optimality condition of the subproblem
\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{f}_{t}(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}}}\right\}
at iteration

.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta_{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)-\frac{\theta_{t+1}^{2}}{2\alpha}\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}\right).

The amount of decrease above can be lower bounded as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)-\frac{\theta_{t+1}^{2}}{2\alpha}\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star}),\frac{\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(x_{t+1}^{\star})-\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})}}\right)^{2}}{\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\;\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(1-\beta)\widetilde{\Delta}_{t},\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{\|g_{t+1}-s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\;\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(1-\beta)\widetilde{\Delta}_{t},\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{2\|g_{t+1}\|^{2}+2\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}}\right\},

where the first inequality uses the definition of
\theta_{t+1}
, the second inequality uses the definition of the null step, and the third inequality uses Young’s inequality.
It is clear that both components in the minimum above are non-negative and we have a weaker result that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}
is non-increasing. Upon further utilizing the relation
\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq 2\alpha\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\leq G^{2}_{k+1}
(which will be shown later), the decrease bound above is at least

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(1-\beta)\widetilde{\Delta}_{t},\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{2\|g_{t+1}\|^{2}+2\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{G_{k+1}^{2}},\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{4G_{k+1}^{2}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{8G_{k+1}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{8M^{2}},

where the last inequality applies the observation that the subgradient
\|G_{k+1}\|
is uniformly upper bounded by the
M
as
f
is
M
-Lipschitz. Hence, we conclude that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}}{8M^{2}}.

We then show the inequality
\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq 2\alpha\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\leq G^{2}_{k+1}
. By the facts that
\hat{f}_{t}(\cdot)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\cdot-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}
is
\alpha
strongly convex and that the minimizer

is unique, there exists a

and

such that

. It follows from the first-order condition of a strongly convex function that

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{t}(\omega_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}-\langle h_{t},\omega_{k+1}-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2},

where the second inequality uses the definition of normal cone
\langle h_{t},y-x_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle\leq 0,\forall y\in\mathcal{X}_{0}.
Using the lower bound property
\hat{f}_{t}(\omega_{k+1})\leq f(\omega_{k+1})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq f(\omega_{k+1})-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t+1}^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}}}\right)=\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{k+1},

where the second inequality comes from the fact that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}
is non-increasing.

By the construction of the lower approximation model at step

and Young’s inequality, we know

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{k+1}(x^{\star}_{k+2})

\displaystyle\geq f(\omega_{k+1})+\langle g_{k+1},x^{\star}_{k+2}-\omega_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq f(\omega_{k+1})-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\|g_{k+1}\|^{2}}{\alpha}+\alpha\|x^{\star}_{k+2}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}}}\right),

where

Combining the above inequality and the definition

, we get the relationship

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|g_{k+1}\|^{2},

which implies
\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}=\alpha^{2}\|x_{t+1}^{\star}-\omega_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq 2\alpha\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}^{2}\leq 2\alpha\widetilde{\Delta}_{k+1}\leq\|g_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq G_{k+1}^{2}
.

∎

Constant trace property in primal and dual SDPs

In this subsection, we give a detailed derivation for the penalized primal/dual problem when the feasible set of

Without loss of generality, we can add the explicit constant trace constraint

\displaystyle\max_{y,t}

\displaystyle b^{\mathsf{T}}y+tk

\displaystyle b^{\mathsf{T}}y-k\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)-C),

Example B.1

\displaystyle\min_{X}

\displaystyle\|X\|_{*}

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle X_{ij}=M_{ij},\;\forall(i,j)\in\Omega,

where

\displaystyle\min_{X}

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf tr}(X)

(B.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}0&E_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
E_{ij}&0\end{bmatrix},X}}\right\rangle=2M_{ij},\forall(i,j)\in\Omega,

\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&U^{\mathsf{T}}\\
U&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{s+t}_{+},

where
E_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
is zero everywhere except the
(i,j)
entry being

. Letting

E_{ij}&0\end{bmatrix}
and re-indexing the matrices

by integers

, the dual SDP for
eq.

becomes

\displaystyle\max_{y,Z}

\displaystyle b^{\mathsf{T}}y

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to}

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{|\Omega|}A_{s}y_{s}+Z=I,

\displaystyle Z\in\mathbb{S}^{s+t}_{+}.

Note that
\mathop{\bf tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s}}}\right)=0
and any feasible variable

Remark C.1

Aside from deriving the dual problem for

\displaystyle l_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(0,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid 0\leq s\leq\rho}}\right\},

•

\displaystyle v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\gamma}\\

For the line segment

-SBMP

and

and inequality-form SDP

.

Consider the primal SDP
eq.

. Any feasible point satisfying
\mathcal{A}(X)=b
can be represented as

\displaystyle X=X_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathcal{N}^{*}y,

where
X_{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a particular solution of
\mathcal{A}(X)=b
,

with

denoting the dimension of the null space of
\mathcal{A}
, and
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
is a linear map
\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
as
\mathcal{N}(X):=\begin{bmatrix}\langle N_{1},X\rangle,\ldots,\langle N_{p},X\rangle\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
such that its adjoint

represents the null space of
\mathcal{A}
.
This allows us to equivalently rewrite
eq.

12
as

\displaystyle\min_{y}\hskip 9.24994pt\langle C,X_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathcal{N}^{*}y\rangle+\rho\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X_{\mathrm{p}}-\mathcal{N}^{*}y),0\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{y}\hskip 9.24994pt\langle\mathcal{N}(C),y\rangle+\rho\max\{\lambda_{\max}(-X_{\mathrm{p}}-\mathcal{N}^{*}y),0\},

where we drop a constant term and use the property
\langle C,\mathcal{N}^{*}y\rangle=\langle\mathcal{N}(C),y\rangle
.
It is clear that the problem above is in the same form as the penalized dual SDPs
eq.

13
with problem data

Therefore, we can apply
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMD
in
Algorithm

3
to solve it and convergence guarantees follow
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
.

On the other hand, the dual SDP

can also be solved by
(r_{\mathrm{p}},r_{\mathrm{c}})
-SBMP
in

2

•

Lemma D.1

Given a closed convex set

and a convex function

Proof.

Let

is closed as
f

and

are closed as the sublevel set of a closed function is closed

43
, A.3.3]

We only need to prove

is bounded. For this, it suffices to prove that the unboundedness of

implies the unboundedness of
\mathcal{X}

We prove this by contradiction. For any
\epsilon>0
, suppose

is unbounded. Since
C_{\epsilon}
is convex and closed, there exists a direction

such that

, where

is an optimal solution, and

[

. If there exists a point

on this half-line such that
\hat{x}=x^{\star}+\hat{t}d,\hat{t}>0
, and

, then according to
[

, Equation 2.30]
, we have

\frac{f(x^{\star}+td)-f(x^{\star})}{t}\geq\frac{f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\star}+\hat{t}d}}\right)-f(x^{\star})}{\hat{t}}=:k>0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall t\geq\hat{t},

which implies

However, this contradicts the assumption that

for all

. This indicates that
