The Optimal Growth Mode in the Relaxation to Statistical Equilibrium Preprint: APS/123-QED
Manuel Santos Gutiérrez, Mickaël D. Chekroun

Abstract
Systems far from equilibrium approach stability slowly due to “anti-mixing” characterized by regions of the phase-space that remain disconnected after prolonged action of the flow. We introduce the Optimal Growth Mode (OGM) to capture this slow initial relaxation. The OGM is calculated from Markov matrices approximating the action of the Fokker-Planck operator onto the phase space. It is obtained as the mode having the largest growth in energy before decay. Important nuances between the OGM and the more traditional slowest decaying mode are detailed in the case of the Lorenz 63 model. The implications for understanding how complex systems respond to external forces, are discussed.



In many chaotic and complex systems, mixing plays a crucial role. It describes how the system loses memory of its initial state and reaches a well-defined, statistical steady state. Correlation functions decay then over time during mixing. This decay rate determines two key properties: 1) how quickly the system reaches a statistical steady state
Lasota and Mackey 1994
, and 2) its resilience against external disturbances
Lasota and Mackey 1994
;
Ruelle 2009
.

For systems at equilibrium, the approach to a statistical steady state is remarkably fast (exponential), establishing a direct link between mixing, correlations, and the system’s response to external forces
Kubo 1957
;
Kubo 1966
. However, this connection is not guaranteed for systems out of equilibrium. This is because these systems lack smooth invariant probability measures
Young 2002
;
Ruelle 1998
. Additionally, the introduction of non-conservative forces violates Liouville’s theorem, a cornerstone of statistical physics
(
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
, §I)
. As a result, the governing equation (Liouville equation
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
) may lose a key property (self-adjointness), severing the link between the system’s long-term and short-term behavior due to the presence of non-normal modes. This phenomenon as been well documented in the fluid dynamics literature
Reddy and Henningson 1993
;
Farrell and Ioannou 1996
;
Trefethen and Embree 2005
but its consequences on the action of Liouville propagators—also known as transfer or Frobenius-Perron operators
Schütte et al. 1999
;
Klus et al. 2018
—in the relaxation to statistical equilibria, has been much less discussed. This paper proposes to bridge this gap.

By hinging on the Ulam method
Ulam 1960
, Markov matrices are instrumental in the learning of transfer operator’s properties; see e.g.
Schütte et al. 1999
;
Junge and Koltai 2009
;
Froyland et al. 2021
.
Markov matrices are also instrumental in analyzing mixing
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
;
Hairer 2010
.
It is thus not surprising that they are used for estimating the long-term behavior of the system’s Liouville propagator characterizing how probability densities evolve and revealing how different aspects of the system,
including correlations, change over time; see e.g.
Froyland and Dellnitz 2003
;
Chekroun et al. 2014
;
Froyland et al. 2013
. This approach can be justified for both deterministic and stochastic systems
Froyland 1998
;
Fishman and Rahav 2002
;
Chekroun et al. 2020
.

Essentially, Markov-Ulam matrices aim at capturing the system’s transition probabilities by identifying key regions in its phase space (possible states) and the specific modes (behaviors) that are slowest to lose memory of their initial state
Froyland 2005
;
Froyland and Padberg 2009a
. They also pinpoint areas most susceptible to external disturbances
Froyland et al. 2016
;
Antown et al. 2018
;
Froyland et al. 2020
. Within a Lagrangian setting, these matrices can even identify persistent patterns and barriers to movement within the system’s physical space itself
Froyland et al. 2007
;
Froyland 2013
.

Despite their relevance to study nonlinear dynamics, the non-uniform exponential decay to equilibrium of Markov-Ulam matrices has been poorly studied. For systems out of equilibrium, these matrices, reflecting the Liouville propagator
Schütte et al. 1999
;
Chekroun et al. 2014
, are
expected to be also non-selfadjoint. As a consequence, non-normal modes are expected to occur: modes that are slow to changes over finite time-intervals and which might not correspond to the slowest (normal) modes to decay in the long run. This paper aims to characterize these modes by introducing a new concept: the Optimal Growth Mode (OGM). In simpler terms, this mode is the one that takes the longest to lose memory and reach equilibrium. As shown below, the OGM is characterized by some sort of “anti-mixing behavior.”

To avoid technical difficulties, we consider deterministic systems embedded within a noise bath. This way, the existence of a unique (and smooth) ergodic statistical steady state can be guaranteed for a broad class of systems
Da Prato and Zabczyk 1996
;
Chekroun et al. 2020
. Thus, our dynamical systems, acting on the
d
-dimensional phase-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, are described by a vector-field
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\dot{\bm{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\dot{\bm{W}}_{t},

(1)

where
\dot{\bm{W}}_{t}
is an
d
-dimensional white-noise process and
\varepsilon
is a measure of its covariance.
The evolution of probability density functions associated with the SDE (
1
), is given by the Fokker-Planck equation (FKE)
Risken 1989
:

\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{FP}\rho=-\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{F}\rho\right)+\epsilon\Delta\rho.

(2)

Its solution operator is given by the Fokker-Planck propagator
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{FP}}
assumed to form a
C_{0}
-semigroup
Engel and Nagel 2000
. When
\epsilon=0
, it reduces to the Liouville propagrator
Chekroun et al. 2014
.

Under generic conditions on
\mathbf{F}
, Eq. (
2
) has an ergodic statistical steady state
\rho_{s}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{FP}\rho_{s}=0
and the Fokker-Planck
operator
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
has an isolated spectrum constituted of damped eigenvalues separated from the eigenvalue
0
(spectral gap); see e.g.
Chekroun et al. 2020
. In this case, the underlying damped eigenvalues of the Fokker-Planck
operator
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
are referred to as the Ruelle-Pollicot (RP) resonances and imply an asymptotic exponential decay of correlations
Chekroun et al. 2020
. These resonances hold significant importance. They allow for decomposition formulas for both temporal correlation functions and power spectral density functions
(
Chekroun et al. 2020
, Sec. 2.3)
across a wide range of stochastic systems encountered in physics; see also
Ruelle 1986
;
Baladi 2000
for deterministic systems.
The spectral gap ensures also the exponential convergence of
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{FP}}\rho_{0}
towards
\rho_{s}
(in some
L^{2}
space) for a broad class of initial distribution
\rho_{0}
; see e.g.
(
Chekroun et al. 2020
, Theorem 6)
. Exponential rate of convergence and spectral gaps also hold in Wasserstein spaces for an even broader class of models than Eq. (
1
); see
Hairer 2002
;
Hairer and Mattingly 2008
.
However the study of transient behavior before reaching exponential convergence remains largely unexplored from an ergodic viewpoint.

In classical fluid dynamics the role of transient dynamics has been thoroughly examined.
Certain flows experience a burst of transient energy growth before settling into an exponential decay towards steady state, whose rate is controlled by the damped eigenvalues of the linearized governing equations. This temporary spike is not bounded by these eigenvalues and is caused instead by non-normal modes of the underlying linearized operator
Reddy and Henningson 1993
;
Farrell and Ioannou 1996
;
MacTaggart and Stewart 2017
;
Trefethen and Embree 2005
. Non-normal modes, when influenced by random fluctuations have shown to be highly relevant. They can explain for instance the variability of large-scale climate phenomena like the
El Niño-Southern Oscillation
Penland and Magorian 1993
;
Penland and Sardeshmukh 1995
, ocean circulation patterns
Tziperman and Ioannou 2002
;
Zanna and Tziperman 2005
, or atmospheric flows
Farrell and Ioannou 1993
;
Bousquet et al. 2015
.

This paper focuses on non-normal modes associated with Fokker-Planck operators
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
, for which exponential relaxation towards a (unique) ergodic statistical equilibrium holds. We are particularly interested to clarify how these modes affect this relaxation process at early times.

An operator,
\mathcal{L}
, is normal if it commutes with its adjoint,
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}

(
Kato 1966
, §I.6)
, namely if
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.
Note that self-adjoint operators are a particular class of normal operators. From Liouville’s theorem for Hamiltonian systems, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
is normal and more generally, for stochastic dynamics within a potential
V
, such that
\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})=-\nabla V(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
1
), it is also true that the Fokker-Planck operator
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
is normal
Pavliotis 2014
. Operators for which
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\mathcal{L}\neq\mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
are called non-normal, for which an extensive literature exists
Trefethen and Embree 2005
albeit not specifically dedicated to the case of Fokker-Planck operators.

Analytical formulas of RP resonances and modes of the Fokker-Planck operator is restricted to very few instances; see e.g.
Metafune et al. 2002
;
Gaspard et al. 1995
;
Tantet et al. 2020a
. In practice, such resonances are numerically estimated from (long) time series
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{T}
obtained by solving Eq. (
1
)
Crommelin and Vanden-Eijnden 2011
;
Chekroun et al. 2020
.
As mentioned above, Ulam’s method grants a means to obtain estimates of the dynamics’ propagator
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{FP}}
via Markov matrices. The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{FP}
are then obtained through logarithm formulas
Crommelin and Vanden-Eijnden 2011
;
Chekroun et al. 2020
.
This method consists of subdividing
the phase space
\mathcal{X}
(typically shadowing the dynamics’ attractor)
into
N
non-intersecting
cells
or
boxes

\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and estimating the dynamics’ probability transitions across these boxes. Mathematically, the propagator
e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{FP}}
for a given transition time
\tau
, is approximated by a
N\times N
Markov matrix
M_{\tau}
, whose entries are given by
Chekroun et al. 2020
:

\left[M_{\tau}\right]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}(B_{i})}\int_{B_{i}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{FP}}\chi_{B_{j}}(\bm{x})\rho_{s}(\mathrm{d}\bm{x}),

(3)

for
i,j=1,\ldots,N
, where
\chi_{B_{i}}
denotes the indicator function of the box
B_{i}
,
\rho_{s}
the stationary statistical equilibrium, and
\rho_{0}
the initial distribution. The transition matrix is then estimated by computing a classical maximum likelihood estimator given by
Crommelin and Vanden-Eijnden 2011
;
Schütte et al. 1999
:

\left[M_{\tau}\right]_{i,j}=\frac{\#\bigg\{\Big(\bm{x}_{k}\in B_{j}\Big)\land\Big(\bm{x}_{k+\ell}\in B_{i}\Big)\bigg\}}{\#\Big\{\bm{x}_{k}\in B_{j}\Big\}},

(4)

for
i,j=1,\ldots,N
.
Given a sampling time
\delta t
at which the time series
\bm{x}_{k}
is collected, the formula (
4
) counts the number of observations (
\#\{\cdot\}
) visiting the box
B_{i}
after
\ell=\lfloor\tau/\delta t\rfloor
iterations, knowing that it already landed in
B_{j}
.
The resulting operation results into a coarse-graining of the dynamics encoded by
M_{\tau}

Chekroun et al. 2014
;
Chekroun et al. 2020
that incorporates artificial diffusion
Froyland et al. 2013
;
Tantet et al. 2018
. Note that for high-dimensional systems, time series constructed from reduced coordinates (observables) are often used instead of the full-state vector
\bm{x}_{k}
. In this case, transition matrices can still be estimated in a reduced state space and their eigenvalues are still informative about the genuine system’s transitions (in the full state space) through the notion of reduced RP resonances; see
(
Chekroun et al. 2014
, Theorem A)
and
Chekroun et al. 2020
;
Tantet et al. 2020b
.

Either estimated in the full or a reduced state space, the transition matrix
M_{\tau}
satisfies the Perron-Frobenius theorem
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
. As such, its eigenvalues are contained in the unit ball and there exists an eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
M_{\tau}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}
. This vector approximates the ergodic invariant measure of the system.
For many stochastic systems, the resulting stochastic Matrix
M_{\tau}
is irreducible
Chekroun et al. 2020
, ensuring its dominant eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
equal to
1
to be simple, and the following limit to hold:

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|(M_{\tau}-E_{1})^{k}\right\|_{2}=0,

(5)

where the matrix
E_{1}
is the spectral projector associated with the eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
given by
E_{1}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{1}^{\mathrm{tr}}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the
N
-dimensional (column) vector whose entries are equal to
1
.

Then, the asymptotic rate of convergence to equilibrium is determined by the spectral gap
\gamma
defined as the distance between the subdominant eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
and the unit circle, namely
\gamma=1-|\mu_{1}|
, see
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
. In particular,
\gamma
controls the geometric rate of convergence of the matrix
M_{\tau}
towards the spectral projector
E_{1}
in Eq. (
5
).

Such a geometric convergence towards zero of
\delta(k)=\|(M_{\tau}-E_{1})^{k}\|_{2}
is often characteristic of exponential decay of correlations for the original system; see e.g.
(
Chekroun et al. 2020
, Sec. 4)
.
This picture is nevertheless tempered by the potential non-normality of the matrix
M_{\tau}-E_{1}
.
In such a case, the relaxation towards zero of
\delta(k)
takes place only after a few iterations, with some possible transient growth. During this transient, the system is slow to lose memory and does not exponentially decorrelate in time.

It remains to identify what regions of the phase-space—as approximated by the dynamics’ coarse-graining tied to Eq. (
4
)—display such a transient growth. The resulting mode capturing such regions is expected to indicate sets of initial conditions which remain disconnected for a prolonged interval of time before mixing takes over.

To calculate such a mode, and inspired by the aforementioned work of
Farrell and Ioannou 1996
in fluid dynamics, we seek the directions of maximum stretching upon the action of the relaxation matrix
Q_{k}=(M_{\tau}-E_{1})^{k}
. In that respect, we consider its singular value decomposition (SVD):

(M_{\tau}-E_{1})^{k}=U_{k}\Sigma_{k}V_{k}^{\ast},

(6)

where
U_{k}
and
V_{k}
are unitary
N\times N
matrices and
\Sigma_{k}
is an
N\times N
diagonal matrix containing the singular values
\{\sigma_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, ordered as:
\sigma_{k,1}\geq\sigma_{k,2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{k,N}
. Thus,
\|Q_{k}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k,1}
and there exists a vector
\mathbf{v}_{k,1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that:

\|Q_{k}\|_{2}=\|Q_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k,1}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k,1}.

(7)

We define then the Optimal Growth Mode (OGM) as the mode that maximizes
\|Q_{k}\|_{2}
over
k
. This mode, denoted by
\mathbf{w}_{OGM}
below, is actually given by the vector
\mathbf{v}_{k_{0},1}
that satisfies:

\max_{k\geq 0}\|Q_{k}\|_{2}=\|(M_{\tau}-E_{1})^{k_{0}}\mathbf{v}_{k_{0},1}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k_{0},1}.

(8)

In other words the OGM maximizes the relaxation curve

k\mapsto\|Q_{k}\|_{2}.

(9)

The OGM must not be confused though with the slowest decaying mode (SDM), defined as the eigenvector
\mathbf{w}_{SDM}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
that satisfies:

M_{\tau}\mathbf{w}_{SDM}=\mu_{1}\mathbf{w}_{SDM}.

(10)

Since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|M_{\tau}^{k}\mathbf{w}_{SDM}\|_{2}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{1}^{k}=0
and

\mu_{1}
is the subdominant eigenvalue of
M_{\tau}
, the SDM is the eigenmode of
M_{\tau}
that has the slowest decay at asymptotic times. It indicates regions of phase-space of slow asymptotic mixing. It remains to illustrate how these two modes, the OGM and the SDM, reflect different dynamical properties of chaotic systems.

The Lorenz 63 system.—
The Lorenz 63 (L63) model
Lorenz 1963
is a low-dimensional truncation of the equations of atmospheric convection
Ghil and Childress 2012
, whose dynamical properties have been extensively studied
Sparrow 1982
.
In its standard chaotic regime, statistical properties have been also analyzed
Anishchenko et al. 2005
;
Chekroun et al. 2011
. Linear response has been shown to hold (on numerical grounds), i.e. small changes in parameter forcing equate to proportional changes in the statistical moments
Reick 2002
;
Lucarini 2016
;
Santos-Gutiérrez and Lucarini 2020
. The L63 system writes as follows:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}s(y-x)\\
x(r-z)-y\\
xy-bz\end{cases},

(11)

where the model parameters are set to be
s=10
,
r=28
and
b=8/3
, for which the system displays its famous “butterfly strange attractor”
Tucker 2002
.

To estimate the Markov matrix
M_{\tau}
associated with
Eq. (
11
), we integrate it over
10^{5}
time units (after spinup) with a time step of
\delta t=10^{-3}
time units, using a fourth order Runge-Kutta algorithm. Thus, a total of
T=10^{8}
data points in phase-space is recorded. The trajectory
\{(x_{k},y_{k},z_{k})\}_{k=0}^{T}
shadows an attractor contained in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Sparrow 1982
, which lies in the domain
[-20,20]\times[-30,30]\times[0,50]
. We work then within the reduced state consisting of the
(x,z)
-domain,
[-20,20]\times[0,50]
.
This domain is divided into
N=2^{12}
equally sized, non-intersecting boxes
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of dimensions
40/2^{6}\times 50/2^{6}
. Then, the transition time
\tau=10\cdot\delta t
is selected and the transition matrix
M_{\tau}
given in Eq. (
4
) is computed with
\bm{x}_{k}=(x_{k},z_{k})
. While the L63 system is deterministic, the coarse box-resolution yields artificial diffusion
Tantet et al. 2018
analogous to the addition of small noise to Eq. (
11
).

In the reduced state space focusing only on variables
x
and
z
,
the eigenvalues of the estimated matrix
M_{\tau}
correspond to simplified versions of resonances of the full transfer operator (reduced RP resonances
Chekroun et al. 2020
). These reduced resonances are linked to the conditional expectation
Chekroun et al. 2020
in the equation closure of Eq. (
11
) within this reduced state space
Kondrashov et al. 2015
;
Lucarini and Chekroun 2023
. This conditional expectation represents the behavior of the system after averaging out unobserved variables. However, it may not capture all important aspects of the system’s variability
Chekroun et al. 2017
. As shown in the appendix, the spectrum of the reduced Markov matrix
M_{\tau}
allows us here to approximate the L63 model’s temporal variability, as reflected by its correlation functions, with reasonable accuracy. This suggests that the modes obtained from the reduced transition matrix
M_{\tau}
capture, in the reduced state space, actual dynamical properties of the L63 model. This is confirmed as explained below.

Both OGM and SDM exhibit properties that slow down mixing in the system, but in different ways.
Initially, the OGM experience energy growth at early times. However, as time progresses, its convergence to a stable, long-term state (ergodic steady state) might become faster. The SDM experiences a steady decrease over time, following an exponential function with a rate determined by a specific time scale given as
\tau^{-1}\log(|\mu_{1}|)

Discussion.
—
Systems out of equilibrium, characterized by non-normal equations of motion, exhibit uneven mixing in phase space. This prevents rapid convergence to a statistical steady state initially, even if the system eventually decorrelates exponentially fast.
The SDM (Eq. (
10
)) captures the slowest asymptotic decay, but it misses the crucial short-term ”anti-mixing” behavior common in non-normal Fokker-Planck operators (Eq. (
2
)).
On the contrary, the OGM captures transient dynamics. This work introduces an algorithm based on SVD decomposition of Markov-Ulam matrices to compute the OGM.
Unlike the SDM, the OGM exhibits growth in its norm at early times, peaking at the maximum point in the relaxation curve (Eq. (
9
)). This anti-mixing character of the OGM manifests in two ways: (i)
sharp increase
—
the OGM norm rises significantly before decaying, and (ii)
slow mixing trajectories
—trajectories starting in regions with opposite OGM values mix much slower compared to, for example, those in the near-invariant regions identified by the SDM (Figures
2
B and
3
B).

Transient slow mixing has consequences for the system’s response to perturbations.
A slow approach to equilibrium translates to increased sensitivity to perturbations due to the slow decay of the Green function
Ruelle 2009
;
Chekroun et al. 2014
;
Lucarini 2008
;
Gutiérrez and Lucarini 2022
. We expect the OGM to be associated with perturbations that take longer to enter the exponentially decaying regime, as suggested by the L63 system’s relaxation curve (Fig.
1
A) and the system’s non-equilibrium nature.
The OGM is optimized over time for transient behavior, so it may not be ideal for optimizing the steady-state response to perturbations. Prior work has addressed finding optimal perturbations in the linear response regime (
Antown et al. 2018
;
Schweitzer 1968
;
Lucarini 2016
). Our future work will explore the implications of non-normality for linear response in more detail.
