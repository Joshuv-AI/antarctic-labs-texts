Fluctuation-dissipation theorem for non-equilibrium quantum systems
Mohammad Mehboudi, Anna Sanpera, Juan M.R. Parrondo

Abstract
The fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) is a central result in statistical physics, both for classical and quantum systems. It establishes a relationship between the linear response of a system under a time-dependent perturbation and time correlations of certain observables in equilibrium. Here we derive a generalization of the theorem which can be applied to any Markov quantum system and makes use of the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD). There are several important benefits from our approach. First, such a formulation clarifies the relation between classical and quantum versions of the equilibrium FDT. Second, and more important, it facilitates the extension of the FDT to arbitrary quantum Markovian evolution, as given by quantum maps. Third, it clarifies the connection between the FDT and quantum metrology in systems with a non-equilibrium steady state.

More recently, it has been proved useful to assess the multipartite entanglement of complex quantum systems at thermal equilibrium

,

Despite the fact that the FDT is so widely used, the standard FDT applies only to small perturbations around thermal equilibrium states

Kubo(1957)
,
Kubo(1966)
,
des Cloizeaux(1968)
,
Jensen and Mackintosh(1991)
]
. There has been an intense activity in the last years to generalize the FDT to classical systems far from equilibrium
[
Agarwal(1972)
,

,

, and more recently to quantum systems
[

,
Albert et al.(2016)Albert,

. Two main strategies have been followed in this pursuit. The first one looks for correction terms in the original equilibrium FDT
[
Baiesi et al.(2009)Baiesi,
Maes, and Wynants
,
Harada and Sasa(2005)
]
, whereas the second keeps the very mathematical structure of the theorem by redefining the magnitude conjugated to an external parameter
[
Prost et al.(2009)Prost,

,
Seifert and Speck(2010)
]
.

Here, we adopt the second strategy to prove a FDT for generic quantum Markovian systems. The key point in our derivation is the use of the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD),
\Lambda_{\lambda}
, of a density matrix
\rho_{\lambda}
depending on a real parameter
\lambda
, defined as:

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\lambda}\rho_{\lambda}+\rho_{\lambda}\Lambda_{\lambda})\equiv 2\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{\prime}}\right|_{\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda}~\rho_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

(1)

The SLD is an observable with zero average,
\left<\Lambda_{\lambda}\right>_{\lambda}={{\rm Tr}}[\Lambda_{\lambda}\rho_{\lambda}]=0
, as can be easily proved by taking the trace of the above equation. It is intimately related to the quantum Fisher information (QFI),

{\cal F}_{\lambda}={\rm Tr}\left[\Lambda_{\lambda}^{2}\rho_{\lambda}\right]
, which
plays a prominent role in metrology, since the uncertainty of any unbiased observable
A
(i.e. with
\left<A\right>_{\lambda}=\lambda
), satisfies the
Crámer-Rao bound
\mathrm{Var}(A)_{\lambda}\geq 1/{\cal F}_{\lambda}

[
Braunstein and Caves(1994)
,
Braunstein et al.(1996)Braunstein, Caves, and Milburn
,
Paris(2009)
,
Giovannetti et al.(2011)Giovannetti, Lloyd, and Maccone
,
Tóth and Apellaniz(2014)
,
Giovannetti et al.(2004)Giovannetti, Lloyd, and Maccone
,
Giovannetti et al.(2006)Giovannetti, Lloyd, and Maccone
]
. In this paper we show that the SLD provides a novel definition of an observable conjugated to an external parameter, which is extremely useful to derive a completely general FDT for quantum Markov systems and to relate previous versions of the FDT for classical and quantum systems.

We start by applying the SLD to the simplest case of a fluctuation-dissipation relation for the static susceptibility.
Consider a quantum system whose density matrix
\rho_{\lambda}
depends on an external parameter
\lambda
. Taking
\rho_{0}
as a reference state, we are interested on the change of the expected value of a generic observable
B
under a small change in
\lambda
. More precisely, for small
\lambda
, the expected value of a generic observable
B
can be written as:

\langle B\rangle_{\lambda}\equiv{\rm Tr}[B\rho_{\lambda}]\simeq\langle B\rangle_{0}+\chi_{B}^{s}\lambda,

(2)

where

\chi_{B}^{s}\equiv\left.{\partial_{\lambda}}\right|_{\lambda=0}\langle B\rangle_{\lambda}={\rm Tr}\left[B\left.{\partial_{\lambda}}\right|_{\lambda=0}\rho_{\lambda}\right],

(3)

is the
static susceptibility
of observable
B
.
Using the SLD, the derivation of a fluctuation-dissipation relation is straightforward:

\displaystyle\chi_{B}^{s}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[B(\Lambda_{0}\rho_{0}+\rho_{0}\Lambda_{0})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\langle B\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}B\rangle_{0}

(4)

which is the symmetrized correlation between observables
B
and
\Lambda_{0}
, since
\langle\Lambda_{0}\rangle_{0}=0
.
In the Appendix
A
we show that Eq. (
4
), when particularized to a thermal state
\rho_{\lambda}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}/Z(\lambda)
, with
\beta=1/(kT)
and
Z(\lambda)\equiv{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}\right]

and expressed in the eigenbasis of
H_{0}
, yields the standard fluctuation-dissipation relation.

We now turn to the case of a generic Markov evolution given by the composition of completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) maps. Let
\xi_{\lambda}(\rho)
be a CPTP map that depends on a parameter
\lambda
. We assume that each map
\xi_{\lambda}
has an invariant state
\pi_{\lambda}
, i.e.,
\xi_{\lambda}(\pi_{\lambda})=\pi_{\lambda}
. We study a small time-dependent perturbation
\lambda(t)
affecting the invariant state
\pi_{0}
. More precisely, we consider the evolution, in discrete time steps
t=1,2,\dots
,

\rho(t)=\xi_{\lambda(t)}\circ\xi_{\lambda(t-1)}\circ\dots\circ\xi_{\lambda(1)}(\pi_{0}).

(5)

The linear response of an observable
B
can be written as:

\langle B(t)\rangle=\langle B\rangle_{0}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\phi_{B}(t-t^{\prime})\lambda(t^{\prime})

(6)

where
\langle B\rangle_{0}={\rm Tr}[\pi_{0}B]
,
\langle B(t)\rangle\equiv{\rm Tr}[\rho(t)B]
, and
\phi_{B}(t-t^{\prime})
is the
response function
of the observable
B
under the perturbation
\lambda
.

One can extend the above definition to the case of maps acting for a short time
\Delta t
. In the continuous limit,
\Delta t\to 0
, the sum in (
6
) is replaced by the integral

\langle B(t)\rangle=\langle B\rangle_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\phi_{B}(t-t^{\prime})\lambda(t^{\prime}).

(7)

The
generalized susceptibility
is defined as the Fourier transform of the response function (
\phi_{B}(t)
is assumed to vanish for
t<0
due to causality):

\chi_{B}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\phi_{B}(t)e^{i\omega t}.

(8)

The generalized susceptibility has interesting properties such as the
Kramers-Kronig
relation between the real and imaginary parts
\chi_{B}(\omega)=\chi_{B}^{\prime}(\omega)+i\chi_{B}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
. When the evolution is unitary under the Hamiltonian
H_{0}-\lambda(t)A
,
\chi_{B}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
is called absorptive part of the susceptibility
[
Kubo(1957)
,
Kubo(1966)
,
des Cloizeaux(1968)
,
Jensen and Mackintosh(1991)
]
, since the energy absorbed by the system due to the perturbation is proportional to
\chi_{B}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
.

The static susceptibility
\chi_{B}^{s}
can be related to the response function by considering a constant perturbation
\lambda(t)=\lambda
for
t\geq 0

[
Kubo(1957)
,
Kubo(1966)
,
des Cloizeaux(1968)
,
Jensen and Mackintosh(1991)
]
. In this case
\langle B(t)\rangle\to\langle B\rangle_{\lambda}
when
t\to\infty
and Eq. (
7
)
implies that the static susceptibility is the integral of the response function or, equivalently, the generalized susceptibility at zero frequency:

\chi_{B}^{s}=\chi_{B}(\omega=0)
.

To obtain the FDT we calculate
\rho(t)
up to linear terms in
\lambda
. For that, we write
\xi_{\lambda}=\xi_{0}+\lambda\xi_{1}+\dots
(notice that
\xi_{1}
is not a CPTP map) and the invariant state as
\pi_{\lambda}=\pi_{0}+\lambda\pi_{1}+\dots
The invariance of
\pi_{\lambda}
under the map
\xi_{\lambda}
implies:

\xi_{1}(\pi_{0})+\xi_{0}(\pi_{1})=\pi_{1}

(9)

and the SLD of
\pi_{\lambda}
with respect to
\lambda
at
\lambda=0
obeys

2\pi_{1}=\Lambda_{0}\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}\Lambda_{0}.

(10)

Expanding the evolution equation (
5
) up to linear terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi_{0}^{t}(\pi_{0})+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\lambda(t^{\prime})\,\xi_{0}^{t-t^{\prime}}\circ\xi_{1}\circ\xi_{0}^{t^{\prime}-1}(\pi_{0})

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi_{0}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\lambda(t^{\prime})\,\xi_{0}^{t-t^{\prime}}\circ\xi_{1}(\pi_{0})

where we have used the invariance of
\pi_{0}
under
\xi_{0}
. The expected value of
B
is

\langle B(t)\rangle=\langle B\rangle_{0}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}{\rm Tr}\left[B\,\xi_{0}^{t-t^{\prime}}\circ\xi_{1}(\pi_{0})\right]\lambda(t^{\prime}).

(12)

Comparing (
12
) with (
6
), we immediately get

\phi_{B}(t)={\rm Tr}\left[B\,\xi_{0}^{t}\circ\xi_{1}(\pi_{0})\right].

(13)

Using (
9
) and (
10
):

\displaystyle\phi_{B}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left[B\,\xi_{0}^{t}\circ(\pi_{1}-\xi_{0}(\pi_{1}))\right]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left[B\xi_{0}^{t}(\pi_{1})-B\xi_{0}^{t+1}(\pi_{1})\right]=-{\rm Tr}\left[\Delta B(t)\pi_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[\Delta B(t)(\Lambda_{0}\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}\Lambda_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta B(t)\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}\Delta B(t)\rangle_{0}

(14)

where
\Delta B(t)=B(t+1)-B(t)
and
B(t)=\tilde{\xi}_{0}^{t}(B)
is the evolution of the observable
B
in the generalized Heisenberg picture for quantum maps
[
Breuer and Petruccione(2002)
,
Nielsen and Chuang(2011)
,
Wilde(2013)
]
. Here
\tilde{\xi}_{0}(\cdot)
is the adjoint map (not necessarily trace preserving) with respect to the scalar product between operators given by the trace, i.e.,
{\rm Tr}[A\xi_{0}(B)]={\rm Tr}[\tilde{\xi}_{0}(A)B]
, for all pair of operators
A
and
B
.

The fluctuation-dissipation relation for the static case (
4
) is recovered from (
14
) if the correlations between
B(t)
and
\Lambda
vanish in the limit
t\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\chi_{B}^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\phi_{B}(t^{\prime})

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\langle B(t)\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}B(t)\rangle_{0}+\langle B\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}B\rangle_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle B\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}B\rangle_{0}.

Finally, the continuous-time version of theorem (
14
) is

\phi_{B}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\,d_{t}\,\langle B(t)\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}B(t)\rangle_{0}.

(16)

Eqs. (
14
) and (
16
) are our main result.
These results are the quantum counterparts of the non-equilibrium classical FDT derived by Agarwal in
[
Agarwal(1972)
]
and revived recently in
[
Prost et al.(2009)Prost,
Joanny, and Parrondo
,
Seifert and Speck(2010)
]
. Notice that, in the classical scenario, the conjugate variable is defined as the derivative of the logarithm of the steady state probability distribution. On the quantum scenario, the non-commutativity of observables does not allow to simply replace the classical conjugate variable with the derivative of the logarithm of the density matrix. Nevertheless, the choice of the SLD observable solves this non-commutativity issue.
In the Appendix
B
we prove that the latter expression (
16
), when particularized to Hamiltonian dynamics and equilibrium states of the form
\pi_{\lambda}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}/Z(\lambda)
, is completely equivalent to the standard Kubo formula
[
Kubo(1957)
,
Kubo(1966)
,
des Cloizeaux(1968)
,
Jensen and Mackintosh(1991)
]
:

\phi_{BA}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}{\rm Tr}\big[[A,\pi_{0}]B(t)\big]=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[B(t),A]\rangle.

(17)

It is worth it to point out that, in the quantum case, the Kubo formula does not yield a simple FDT; more precisely, the response function cannot be expressed in terms of the time derivative of a two-time correlation in equilibrium. Such a relationship can only be derived in the frequency domain for the absorptive part of the generalized susceptibility and the Fourier transform of the correlation (see
[
Kubo(1966)
,
des Cloizeaux(1968)
,
Jensen and Mackintosh(1991)
]
and the Appendix
B
). On the contrary, our generalized FDT, namely Eqs. (
14
) and (
16
), expresses the response function in terms of correlations in the time domain and can be equally applied to classical and quantum systems. This uniform formulation is possible due to the introduction of the SLD. In the classical case, the SLD coincides with the normal derivative and consequently, for a thermal state with Hamiltonian
H_{0}-\lambda A
, the SLD is
-\beta(A-\langle A\rangle)
, whereas for a quantum system with
[H_{0},A]\neq 0
, the SLD yields a nontrivial conjugated variable as shown below. We highlight the usefulness of our FDT for quantum metrology explicitely through the examples that follow. However, let us remark that such a link holds for any map that fits our framework.

We illustrate the new generalized FDT with a simple example consisting of
two harmonic oscillators with a modulated interaction. The Hamiltonian reads:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}+H_{J}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}+(\omega+\delta)a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}-J(t)(a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}+a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}),

where
a_{i}
and
a_{i}^{\dagger}
are the ladder operators of
i
-th oscillator and
\delta
denotes the frequency detuning between the oscillators. We assume
|J(t)|\ll\omega
so that linear response theory holds at any time.
First, we consider the two harmonic oscillators placed in a bath at temperature
T
, and examine the response of the system to a perturbation
J(t)=J_{0}
around the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{0}=\mathrm{exp}(-\beta H_{0})/Z
.
Classically, the susceptibility is defined as
\partial_{J}\left<A\right>_{J}
, with
A=\partial_{J}H=-(a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{1}+a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2})
being
the conjugate variable. Notice that if the detuning is zero, then

[H_{0},A]=0
, and the SLD reads
\Lambda_{J}(\delta=0)=-\beta(A-\left<A\right>_{J})

[
Campbell et al.(2017)Campbell, Mehboudi, Chiara, and Paternostro
]
. On the contrary, any
\delta\neq 0
forces that
[H_{0},A]\neq 0
, and the SLD cannot be anymore identified trivially as the conjugate variable
A

[
Mehboudi et al.(2016)Mehboudi, Correa, and Sanpera
]
.
For a finite detuning, the SLD takes the form
\Lambda_{J}(\delta)=C(\delta)\Lambda_{J}(0)
(see
[
Monras(2013)
,
Jiang(2014)
]
).
This additional coefficient arises from the non-commutativity between
H_{0}
and
A
, and reads:

\displaystyle C(\delta)\equiv\frac{\tanh(\delta/2T)}{\delta/2T}.

(20)

The use of the SLD allows us to show that the QFI is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{J}(\delta)=C^{2}(\delta)\,\beta^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(A)_{J}.

(21)

The additional coefficient is bounded,
0<C(\delta)\leq 1
. It has a maximum at
\delta=0
, and then it decreases monotonically with the detuning, therefore, the precision on the estimation of
J
decreases as the detune increases.

We now address the problem of a time-dependent modulation of
J(t)
in a non-equilibrium environment induced by two thermal baths at different temperatures
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.
A master equation that has been widely used to describe the reduced state of the oscillators consists of a Linblad equation
[
Campbell et al.(2016)Campbell, De Chiara, and Paternostro
,
Breuer and Petruccione(2002)
,
Wiseman and Milburn(2009)
,
Gardiner and Zoller(2004)
]

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)=-i[H,\rho(t)]+\sum_{i=\{1,2\}}\mathcal{D}_{i}[\rho(t)],

(22)

with two independent dissipators
\mathcal{D}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}[\rho(t)]

\displaystyle=\gamma(N_{i}+1)\left(a_{i}\rho(t)a_{i}^{\dagger}-1/2\{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i},\rho(t)\}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle+\gamma N_{i}\left(a_{i}^{\dagger}\rho(t)a_{i}-1/2\{a_{i}a_{i}^{\dagger},\rho(t)\}\right).

Here
N_{i}=(\exp[\beta_{i}\omega_{i}]-1)^{-1}
is the mean occupation number of the
i
-th oscillator, and
\gamma
is the dissipation rate, which is assumed to be equal for both oscillators. The equation is only valid for small
J(t)
—compared to
\omega
, i.e., the system’s energy scale—and
J(t)<\delta

[
González et al.(2017)González, Correa, Nocerino,
Palao, Alonso, and Adesso
]
. In particular, it does not predict the thermalization of the full two-oscillator system for finite
J
when
T_{1}=T_{2}
. Obviously, for
J(t)=0
, both oscillators evolve independently reaching a stationary state in which each oscillator is at thermal equilibrium with its own bath:

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{1}H_{1}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{1}H_{1}}]}\otimes\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{2}H_{2}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{2}H_{2}}]},

(24)

where
H_{i}
denotes the free Hamiltonian of the
i
-th oscillator.
Since the model under study is quadratic in creation and annihilation operators, one may equivalently describe it by means of its covariance matrix (CM) which contains only first and second moments (i.e., Gaussian), notably simplifying the calculations. The latter is described with the help of the quadratures

\displaystyle x_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{i}^{\dagger}+a_{i}),\quad p_{i}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{i}^{\dagger}-a_{i})

(25)

which satisfy the standard commutation relation
[x_{j},p_{k}]=i\delta_{jk}
.
In turn, the matrix elements of the CM are defined as follows:

\sigma_{jk}\equiv\left<R_{j}R_{k}+R_{k}R_{j}\right>/2-\left<R_{j}\right>\left<R_{k}\right>
, with
R_{j}\in\{x_{1},x_{2},p_{1},p_{2}\}
.
The CM corresponding to the stationary state of the master equation (
22
) is provided in the Appendix
C
, where we also find (i) the SLD, and (ii) the time evolution of all the quadratic observables.
Note that according to Eq. (
16
), (i) and (ii) are the two key elements required for evaluation of
\phi_{B}(t)
. Specifically, the SLD writes as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{1}(x_{1}x_{2}+p_{1}p_{2})+c_{2}(x_{1}p_{2}-p_{1}x_{2}),

(26)

with
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
being real numbers.
Therefore, the SLD is a non-local operator, hence the response of any local observable vanishes.

Futhermore, the metrological character of the FDT when expressed through the SLD, can be seen by identifying which is the best measurement to detect the smallest interaction coupling
J(t)=J_{0}
.
If we have no prior knowledge about
J_{0}
,
linear response theory ensures that
Tr[B(\rho(t)-\rho_{0})]=J_{0}\,\chi_{B}(t)
. Unavoidable, an expectation value bears a statistical error,
\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(B)_{0}/n}
, by repeating the measurement
n
times
1
1

1

In fact the statistical error at measurement time is
\mathrm{Var}(B)_{t}
, but since we only keep the leading order, in the linear response regime we safely replace it by
\mathrm{Var}(B)_{0}
.
. Thus, in order to infer the value of the perturbation, the linear response of the system must be larger than the statistical error, i.e.,
|J_{0}\chi_{B}(t)|>\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(B)_{0}/n}
, setting a lower bound on the smallest perturbation that one may detect by measuring
B
. The inverse of this lower bound
defines the sensitivity
F(B)_{t}
of the observable
B
to the perturbation
J_{0}
:

\displaystyle F(B)_{t}\equiv|\chi_{B}(t)|/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(B)_{0}/n}.

(29)

Notice that the above definition is not restricted to the model discussed here. It can be applied for estimation of a generic parameter which is parametrized through a divisible map as in (
5
).
For a constant perturbation, as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

\chi_{B}(t\to\infty)\approx\mathrm{Corr}(B,\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0})_{0}
. Substituting this in Eq. (
29
), and using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the upper bound on the sensitivity of
B
at

is given by:

\displaystyle F(B)_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{n\mathrm{Var}(\Lambda_{0})_{0}}=\sqrt{n\mathcal{F}_{0}},

(30)

with
\mathcal{F}_{0}

being the QFI evaluated at
J_{0}=0
.
The QFI has been previously used to quantify the ultimate precision of parameter estimation in non-equilibrium steady states of spin models
[
Marzolino and Prosen(2014)
]
.
The bound (

) is saturated by performing a measurement in the
\Lambda_{0}
basis. In Fig.

we depict

. Although no observable can overperform the sensitivity of the SLD at

F(B)_{t}

J(t)=J_{0}(1-\cos\nu t)

\Lambda_{0}

are given by Eq. (

).

In summary, we have presented a novel formulation of the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem (FDT) in terms of the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD), which is completely general to describe the effect of a perturbation on a quantum systems, within the linear response. Such a formulation presents some clear advantages. First, it unifies FDT for classical and quantum systems. Second, it can be straightforwardly applied to any Markovian evolution by means of quantum maps. This permits the extension of the FDT to non-equilibrium dynamics. Third, it provides an explicit connection between the susceptibility of an observable to an external perturbation and the figure of merit in quantum metrology.

Our FDT can be used to generalize the detection of multipartite entanglement to the non-equilibrium steady states (NESS). Although at thermal equilibrium, or after quenching a thermal state, such relations are known
[
Hauke et al.(2016)Hauke,
Heyl, Tagliacozzo, and Zoller
,
Pappalardi et al.(2017)Pappalardi, Russomanno, Silva, and Fazio
]
, there are less studies for a general NESS state. In this regard, the sensitivity measure Eq. (
29
)—which is a lower bound on the QFI
[
Tóth and Apellaniz(2014)
,
Tóth and Vértesi(2018)
]
—can be used to detect multipartite entanglement.
Finally, our results prompt an interesting open question on the relationship between the quantum versions of FDT and fluctuation theorems (FT). Needless to mention that, the quantum versions of FT have been subject of extensive studies, that have produced a rich literature on the subject
[
Campisi et al.(2011)Campisi, Hänggi, and Talkner
,
Esposito et al.(2009)Esposito, Harbola, and Mukamel
,
Åberg(2018)
]
. The relationship between FDT and FT has been made clear for classical systems. On the other hand, the FT for quantum CPTP maps requires a condition that is not necessary in our derivation of the FDT, namely, the Kraus operators of the map must be ladder operators in a relevant basis: the eigenbasis of the instantaneous stationary state
\pi_{0}
for generic CPTP maps
[
Manzano et al.(2015)Manzano, Horowitz, and Parrondo
]
or, for periodically driven systems in contact with equilibrium reservoirs, Floquet eigenstates
[
Cuetara et al.(2015)Cuetara, Engel, and Esposito
]
or displaced energy eigenstates
[
Horowitz(2012)
]
. Our FDT suggests that one could obtain a more general FT by using the SLD.

Appendix A
Quantum systems in thermal equilibrium

A relevant particularization of the static fluctuation dissipation relation Eq. (
4
) is the case of a quantum system with Hamiltonian
H_{0}-\lambda A
at thermal equilibrium. In such a case the density matrix is
\rho_{\lambda}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}/Z(\lambda)
, where
\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
is the inverse temperature and
Z(\lambda)\equiv{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}\right]
is the partition function of the system.
The equilibrium static susceptibility of an arbitrary observable
B
under the perturbation
\lambda A
is denoted as
\chi_{B}^{s}
and obeys Eq. (
4

Furthermore, when the SLD is expressed in

(31)

In the eigenbasis of
H_{0}

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}\left|m\right\rangle=\beta\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle\int_{0}^{1}ds\,e^{-\beta[E_{n}(1-s)+E_{m}s]}=\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle\frac{e^{-\beta E_{m}}-e^{-\beta E_{n}}}{E_{n}-E_{m}},

(32)

if

\beta\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle e^{-\beta E_{n}}

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}\rho_{\lambda}\left|m\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle\frac{p_{m}-p_{n}}{E_{n}-E_{m}},\quad\mbox{for $E_{n}\neq E_{m}$},

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}\rho_{\lambda}\left|m\right\rangle

\displaystyle=p_{n}\left[\beta\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle-\delta_{mn}\frac{Z^{\prime}(0)}{Z(0)}\right],\quad\mbox{for $E_{n}=E_{m}$}.

(33)

Here

E_{n}
at equilibrium. Using Eq. (
31

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{\prime}(0)}{Z(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{Z(0)}\int_{0}^{1}ds\,{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H_{0}(1-s)}Ae^{-\beta H_{0}s}\right]

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{Z(0)}{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H_{0}}A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\langle A\rangle_{0}.

(35)

for
E_{n}\neq E_{m}

by choosing the eigenbasis

\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle=\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle\delta_{m,n}

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|\Lambda_{0}\left|m\right\rangle

\displaystyle 2\frac{\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle}{p_{n}+p_{m}}\frac{p_{m}-p_{n}}{E_{n}-E_{m}}\quad\mbox{for $E_{n}\neq E_{m}$},

\displaystyle\beta\left[\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle-\langle A\rangle_{0}\right]\delta_{mn}\quad\mbox{for $E_{n}=E_{m}$}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}p_{n}\big(\left\langle n\right|\Lambda_{0}\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle m\right|A\left|n\right\rangle~+~\left\langle n\right|A\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle m\right|\Lambda_{0}\left|n\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}(p_{n}+p_{m})\left\langle n\right|\Lambda_{0}\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle m\right|A\left|n\right\rangle

Appendix B
Kubo relations

Our main results Eqs. (
14
) and (
16
) are FDTs for generic quantum Markov systems. One can recover the familiar Kubo quantum FDT for states
\pi_{\lambda}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}-\lambda A)}/Z(\lambda)
and Hamiltonian evolution. In this case, the Eq. (
16
) reads (as before, we denote by
\phi_{B}(t)
the response function of observable
B
under the perturbation
-\lambda(t)A
):

\displaystyle\phi_{B}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\langle\dot{B}(t)~\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}\dot{B}(t)\rangle_{0}}{2}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\hbar}\langle[H_{0},B(t)]~\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{0}~[H_{0},B(t)]\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\hbar}{\rm Tr}\big[(\Lambda_{0}~\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}~\Lambda_{0})~[H_{0},B(t)]\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\hbar}{\rm Tr}\big[[\Lambda_{0}~\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}~\Lambda_{0},H_{0}]~B(t)\big].

To proceed further, we need an additional formula for the SLD. For any real function
f(x)
:

[H_{0}-\lambda A,f(H_{0}-\lambda A)]=0.

(40)

Differentiating this equation with respect to
\lambda
and setting
\lambda=0
, yields:

-[A,f(H_{0})]+\left[H_{0},\left.\frac{\partial f(H_{0}-\lambda A)}{\partial\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}\right]=0.

(41)

Particularizing to
f(x)=e^{-\beta x}
and using the definition of the SLD given by Eq. (
1
) the above equation reduces to:

[A,\pi_{0}]=\frac{1}{2}[H_{0},\Lambda_{0}~\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}~\Lambda_{0}].

(42)

Introducing this last result into Eq. (
39
) we finally reach at:

\phi_{B}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}{\rm Tr}\big[[A,\pi_{0}]~B(t)\big]=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[B(t),A]\rangle.

(43)

which is the standard Kubo formula. It is interesting to recall that the Kubo formula allows one to express the absorptive part of the susceptibility in terms of a two time correlation
in the frequency domain
. This is the so-called quantum FDT, which reads
[
des Cloizeaux(1968)
]
:

\chi^{\prime\prime}_{B}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)\tilde{C}_{BA}(\omega),

(44)

where
\tilde{C}_{BA}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the symmetric correlation function
C_{BA}(t)=\langle B(t)A+AB(t)\rangle_{0}/2-\langle B\rangle_{0}\langle A\rangle_{0}
. The above equation does not have a simple correlate in the time domain for quantum systems. Only in the classical case,
\hbar\to 0
, Eq. (
44
) reduces to:

\chi^{\prime\prime}_{B}(\omega)=\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\,\tilde{C}_{BA}(\omega),

(45)

which is equivalent to:

\phi_{B}(t)=-{\beta}\,\frac{d}{dt}C_{BA}(t).

(46)

Here we see the clear advantage of the introduction of the SLD conjugated variable
\Lambda_{0}
: it allows us to express the response function in the time domain as a correlation,
both
in the quantum and in the classical case.

Appendix C
The response function of coupled harmonic oscillators

Here with the help of the stationary state of the coupled harmonic oscillators, we find the response function of any observable
B
to the perturbation.
The covariance matrix (CM) corresponding to the density matrix of Eq. (
24
), i.e., for a vanishing interaction, is described by the following diagonal matrix:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\infty}_{{}_{J=0}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}N_{1}+\frac{1}{2}&0&0&0\\
0&N_{2}+\frac{1}{2}&0&0\\
0&0&N_{1}+\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&0&0&N_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{array}\right).

For a non-zero coupling, the CM is not diagonal anymore, as the coupling establishes correlations amongst the two harmonic oscillators. The corresponding CM is given by the following matrix
[
Campbell et al.(2016)Campbell, De Chiara, and Paternostro
]
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\infty}=\zeta\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}D+N_{1}+\frac{1}{2}&-\delta C&0&\gamma C\\
-\delta C&D+N_{2}+\frac{1}{2}&-\gamma C&0\\
0&-\gamma C&D+N_{1}+\frac{1}{2}&-\delta C\\
\gamma C&0&-\delta C&D+N_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{array}\right),

with
\zeta=\frac{\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}}{4J^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}}
,
D=\frac{2J^{2}(N_{1}+N_{2}+1)}{\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}}
, and
C=\frac{J(N_{1}-N_{2})}{\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}}
.

Next, we need to identify (i) the SLD associated to
J
, and (ii) the time evolution of the desired observable
B(t)
, under the unperturbed map.
One can find (i) with the help of Eq. (
C
). We know that for such a Gaussian state,
\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}
can be expressed as a linear combination of all the second order moments of the quadratures
[
Monras(2013)
,
Jiang(2014)
]
, namely

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=d_{1}\left[x_{1}^{2}-\left(\sigma^{\infty}_{11}\right)_{J=0}\right]+d_{2}\left[p_{1}^{2}-\left(\sigma^{\infty}_{33}\right)_{J=0}\right]+d_{3}\left[x_{1}p_{1}+p_{1}x_{1}\right]

\displaystyle+d_{4}\left[x_{2}^{2}-\left(\sigma^{\infty}_{22}\right)_{J=0}\right]+d_{5}\left[p_{2}^{2}-\left(\sigma^{\infty}_{44}\right)_{J=0}\right]+d_{6}\left[x_{2}p_{2}+p_{2}x_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+c_{1}~x_{1}x_{2}+c_{2}~x_{1}p_{2}+c_{3}~p_{1}x_{2}+c_{4}~p_{1}p_{2},

(55)

with
d_{j}
s and
c_{j}
s being coefficients which are to be determined.
To this end, we make benefit of Eq. (
4
) of the main text, which states that for any observable
B
we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{J}\left.\right|_{J=0}\left<B\right>_{J}=\frac{1}{2}\left<\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}B+B\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}\right>_{0}.

(56)

Next, we imply this relation to all of the quadratic observables appearing in (
55
), i.e., we choose
B\in\{x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},x_{1}p_{1}+p_{1}x_{1},\dots\}
. With such choices of
B
, the left hand side of Eq. (
56
) can be evaluated by taking derivative from the covariance matrix
\sigma^{\infty}
. Moreover, by using the Wick’s theorem for Gaussian distributions
[
Louisell(1973)
]
, one can easily simplify the right hand side and write it down in terms of the elements of
\sigma^{\infty}
as well.
We shall start by focusing on the local terms. As an example, for
B=x_{1}^{2}
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left<\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}\right>_{0}=\left(\partial_{J}\sigma^{\infty}_{11}\right)_{{}_{J=0}}~,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 2d_{1}(\sigma^{\infty}_{11})^{2}_{{}_{J=0}}-\frac{d_{2}}{2}=0,

(57)

where we used the fact that

, has no off-diagonal terms, as stated by Eq. (
C
).
For
B=p_{1}^{2}
one finds a similar equation, with the change of coefficients

.
This implies that,
d_{1}=d_{2}=0
. In the same manner, one can find that
d_{4}=d_{5}=0
.
For the other two local terms i.e.,

, Eq. (
56

\displaystyle d_{3}\left[2(\sigma^{\infty}_{11})_{{}_{J=0}}(\sigma^{\infty}_{33})_{{}_{J=0}}+\frac{1}{2}\right]=0,

\displaystyle d_{6}\left[2(\sigma^{\infty}_{22})_{{}_{J=0}}(\sigma^{\infty}_{44})_{{}_{J=0}}+\frac{1}{2}\right]=0,

whence,

\displaystyle c_{1}(\sigma^{\infty}_{11})_{{}_{J=0}}(\sigma^{\infty}_{22})_{{}_{J=0}}=(\partial_{J}\sigma^{\infty}_{12})_{{}_{J=0}},~~~~~~~c_{2}(\sigma^{\infty}_{11})_{{}_{J=0}}(\sigma^{\infty}_{44})_{{}_{J=0}}=(\partial_{J}\sigma^{\infty}_{14})_{{}_{J=0}},

\displaystyle c_{1}=c_{4}=\left(\frac{\partial_{J}\sigma^{\infty}_{12}}{\sigma^{\infty}_{11}\sigma^{\infty}_{22}}\right)_{{}_{J=0}},~~~~~~c_{2}=-c_{3}=\left(\frac{\partial_{J}\sigma^{\infty}_{14}}{\sigma^{\infty}_{11}\sigma^{\infty}_{44}}\right)_{{}_{J=0}}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{1}(x_{1}x_{2}+p_{1}p_{2})+c_{2}(x_{1}p_{2}-p_{1}x_{2})

To begin with, we remind that the time evolution of the ladder operators are easy to find (see
[
Breuer and Petruccione(2002)
]

\displaystyle a_{j}(t)=e^{(-i\omega_{j}-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2})t}a_{j},~~~~~a_{j}^{\dagger}(t)=e^{(i\omega_{j}-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{2})t}a_{j}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}(t)=e^{-\gamma_{0}t}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}+N(1-e^{-\gamma_{0}t}),~~~~~a_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}(t)=e^{-\gamma_{0}t}a_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j}+(N+1)(1-e^{-\gamma_{0}t}).

Therefore, by using the definition of

and

\displaystyle x_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{j}^{\dagger}(t)-a_{j}(t))=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma/2t}\left(-\sin(\omega_{j}t)x_{j}+\cos(\omega_{j}t)p_{j}\right).

(63)

In addition, the local second moments are:

\displaystyle x_{j}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos^{2}(\omega_{j}t)x_{j}^{2}+\sin^{2}(\omega_{j}t)p_{j}^{2}+\frac{\sin(2\omega_{j}t)}{2}(x_{j}p_{j}+p_{j}x_{j})\right)+(N_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2})(1-e^{-\gamma t}),

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}(t)

\displaystyle x_{1}p_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\Big(-\cos(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}x_{2}+\cos(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}p_{2}-\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}x_{2}+\sin(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}p_{2}\Big),

\displaystyle p_{1}x_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\Big(-\sin(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}x_{2}-\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}p_{2}+\cos(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}x_{2}+\cos(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}p_{2}\Big),

\displaystyle p_{1}p_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\Big(\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}x_{2}-\sin(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)x_{1}p_{2}-\cos(\omega_{1}t)\sin(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}x_{2}+\cos(\omega_{1}t)\cos(\omega_{2}t)p_{1}p_{2}\Big),

(65)

which are all non-local, hence we expect a non-zero response for them. To examine this, let us focus on

. By replacing Eqs. (
65
) and (
C
