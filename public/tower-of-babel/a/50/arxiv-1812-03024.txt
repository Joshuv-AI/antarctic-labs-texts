Abstract
. We provide proofs for the fact that certain orders have no descending chains and no antichains.

06A06(03E04)

1.
Introduction

We investigate some finiteness conditions of a partially ordered set
({A},{\leq})
.
As usual, we write
a\geq b
for
b\leq a
, and
a<b
(or
b>a
) for
a\leq b
and
a\neq b
. Furthermore,
a\perp b
stands for
(
a\not\leq b
and
b\not\leq a
); in this case, we call
a
and
b

uncomparable
.
A sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
from
A
is a
descending chain
if

a_{i}>a_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it is an
antichain

if for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
implies
i=j
, and it is
an
ascending chain
if for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
.
A subset
U
of
A
is called
upward closed
if

u\in U
,
a\in A
, and
u\leq a
imply
a\in U
.
For a subset
B
of
A
, we define the
upward closed set generated by
B

by
{\uparrow}B:=\{a\in A\mid\exists b\in B:b\leq a\}
.
By
U({A},{\leq})
or simply
U(A)
we denote the
set of upward closed subsets of
A
. This set can be ordered by set inclusion
\subseteq
.

One frequently uses the fact that certain partially ordered sets have no descending chain
and no antichain; such orders are called
well partial orders
.

[
Lav76
,
AH07
]
provide powerful techniques to establish that
a given order is a well partial order.
In this note, we restrict our attention to some particular ordered sets:
The first set that we consider is
the set
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
of vectors of natural numbers of some fixed length
m
,
which we order by
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\leq(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
if
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and
we provide proofs of the following well known facts:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has no descending chain and no antichain
[
Dic13
, Lemma A]
.

(2)

(U(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq),\subseteq)
has no ascending chain and no antichain
[
Mac01
]
,
[
AP04
, Corollary 1.8]
.

It is easy to see that
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has no descending chain.
In 1913, Dickson proved that
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has no antichain, a fact
that lies at the basis of the theory of Gröbner bases
[
BL83
,
Buc70
]
.
This fact can be stated differently. We call an ideal of the
polynomial ring
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]

monomial
if it is
generated by monomials. Then Dickson’s Lemma states that
every monomial ideal is finitely generated (which of course also
follows from Hilbert’s Basis Theorem). A somewhat surprising fact is
that the set of monomial ideals of
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
has
no antichain, which has been proved in
[
Mac01
]
,
but probably much before in an order theoretic setting: in this setting,
the result states that the set of upward closed subsets of

(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has no antichain. A direct proof is given at the
end of Section
4
. Another proof using an ordering
of words over a finite alphabet that goes back to G. Higman

[
Hig52
]
is given in Section
6
. This is
the second type of order relations we study:
For a finite alphabet
A
, we say that a word
u\in A^{*}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}A^{n}

embeds into

a word
v
if
u
can be obtained from
v
by cancelling some letters and in this case write
u\leq_{e}v
.
For example
u=aabbca
embeds into

v=\underline{a}b\underline{a}\underline{b}a\underline{b}\underline{c}\underline{a}c
.
It follows from
[
Hig52
]
that for a finite set
A
,
(A^{*},\leq_{e})
has no antichain.
Also, the upward closed subsets of
(A^{*},\leq_{e})
have no antichain
[
NW64
]
.
Formally, we define when
x\leq_{e}y
holds by
recursion on the length of
x
.
First, the empty word
\emptyset
satisfies
\emptyset\leq y
for
all
y\in A^{*}
. If
x=au
with
a\in A
and
u\in A^{*}
,
then
x\leq_{e}y
if there are words
v,w\in A^{*}
such that

y=vaw
and
u\leq_{e}w
. Then we have:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A
be a finite set.

(1)

(A^{*},\leq_{e})
has no descending chain and no antichain
[
Hig52
]
.

(2)

(U(A^{*},\leq_{e}),\subseteq)
has no ascending chain and no antichain.

We will give a proof of this theorem in Section
5
.
We also investigate the following ordering of words used in
[
AMM14
]
.
Let
A
be a finite set, and let
B:=(A\times\{0\})\cup(A\times\{1\})
.
We define a mapping
\varphi:A^{*}\to B^{*}

by
\varphi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})

with
b_{i}:=(a_{i},0)
if
a_{i}\not\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1}\}

and
b_{i}:=(a_{i},1)
if
a_{i}\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1}\}
.
For
u=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, we use
S(u)
to denote the
set of letters that occur in
u
, formally

S(u):=\{a_{i}\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}
.
For
u,v\in A^{*}
, we say that
u\leq_{E}v
if

\varphi(u)\leq_{e}\varphi(v)
and
S(u)=S(v)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A
be a finite set.

(1)

(A^{*},\leq_{E})
has no descending chain and no antichain

[
AMM14
, Lemma 3.2]
.

(2)

(U(A^{*},\leq_{E}),\subseteq)
has no ascending chain and no antichain.

The first listed author has used these results on several occasions:
in
[
Aic10
]
, the absence of antichains in
(A^{*},\leq_{e})
is
used to establish that every constantive Mal’cev clone on a finite
set is finitely related. In
[
AM13
]
, it is proved that
the set of admissible higher commutator operations on a finite congruence
lattice is well partially ordered by a natural ordering.
The variant of Higman’s embedding ordering considered in Theorem
1.3
was used in
[
AMM14
]

to generalize the results from
[
Aic10
]
, resulting
in a proof that every finite algebra with few subpowers is finitely related,
and
[
AM16
]
uses the same ordering to prove that
every subvariety of the variety generated by such an algebra is finitely
generated.
Recently, McDevitt
[
McD18
]
gave a construction of a large
class of word orderings generalizing
\leq_{e}
and
\leq_{E}
, and he
established a sufficient criterion
[
McD18
, Proposition 47]
for
such orders to be well partial orders, thereby generalizing
Theorems
1.2
(
1
) and
1.3
(
1
).

An open question in clone theory is whether
there is a finite set with an antichain of clones containing a Mal’cev operation.
One motivation for proving the absence of antichains is that
this absence often
allows testing certain properties of a structure by considering whether
it
contains finitely many forbidden substructures
[
RS04
]
.
The aim of this note is to establish the order theoretic results that
are listed above and
used in
[
Aic10
,
AM13
,
AMM14
,
AM16
]
in a rather direct way.
In particular, we will not resort to the theory of better quasi orderings
[
Lav76
]
.
The note is self-contained, in particular we introduce the well known and
very useful concept of

minimal bad sequences
due to
[
NW63
]
, although this is done in a similar way at
several other places (cf.
[
AH07
]
).
However, we will not give a proof of the following theorem due to
F.P. Ramsey
[
Ram29
]
(cf.
[
Neš95
]
): Denoting the
2
-element subsets
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
by
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,
Ramsey’s Theorem states that for every finite set
T
and for every

c:\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\big)\to T
, there is an infinite subset
Y
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
such
that the restriction of
c
to
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}Y\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\big)
is constant.

Most of the results and proofs in this note are well known and can
be found, e.g., in
[
AH07
]
. However, we believe that the
the two proofs of Theorem
1.1
(
2
) and the proof
of Theorem
1.2
(
2
) are new. The derivation of
Theorem
1.3
from Theorem
1.2
using quasi-embeddings
(cf.
[
AH07
]
) was suggested to the author by N. Ruškuc
in 2015 (cf.
[
Ruš15
]
).
Considering
[
Lav76
,
NW64
,
AH07
]
, the reader will easily find out
that the theory of well quasi orders and better quasi orders is much
deeper than scratched in the present note. The aim of the present note
is to give easily accessible proofs for some basic and very useful results
in this theory. The first listed author has faced the need
for such proofs when teaching the basics of Gröbner Basis Theory, e.g., the existence of universal Gröbner bases (cf.
[
Aic09
]
) or, in universal
algebra
[
BS81
]
, the order theoretical
foundations of the finite relatedness of finite algebras with few
subpowers
[
AMM14
]
.

2.
Basics of order theory

It can easily be shown, using, however, some form of the axiom of choice,
that a partially ordered set
({A},{\leq})
has no descending chain
if and only if every finite subset
X
of
A
contains a minimal element.
A sequence
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
subsequence
of
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
if
there is a mapping
t:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
that satisfies

i<j\Rightarrow t(i)<t(j)
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have
b_{i}=a_{t(i)}
. In this case,

(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}=({A},{\leq})
be a partially ordered set,
and let
S=(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence from
A
. Then

S
has a subsequence
T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that one
of the following conditions holds:

(1)

T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is
constant
, which means
that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a_{t(i)}=a_{t(j)}
.

(2)

T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
descending chain
, which means
that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i<j\Rightarrow a_{t(i)}>a_{t(j)}
.

(3)

T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an
ascending chain
, which means
that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i<j\Rightarrow a_{t(i)}<a_{t(j)}
.

(4)

T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an
antichain
, which means
that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
i<j\Rightarrow a_{t(i)}\perp a_{t(j)}
.

Proof:
We define a coloring
c
of the
2
-element subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Let
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
i<j
. We set

c(\{i,j\}):=1
if
a_{i}=a_{j}
,

c(\{i,j\}):=2
if
a_{i}<a_{j}
,

c(\{i,j\}):=3
if
a_{i}>a_{j}
, and

c(\{i,j\}):=4
if
a_{i}\perp a_{j}
.
By Ramsey’s Theorem,
\mathbb{N}
contains an infinite subset
Y
such that

c
is constant on
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}Y\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\big)
. We let
t:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to Y
be an injective
increasing function from
\mathbb{N}_{0}
into
Y
. Then
T:=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is
the required subsequence. ∎

3.
Bad sequences

Definition 3.1
.

Let
({A},{\leq})
be a partially ordered set.
A sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
from
A
is
good
if there are

i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
i<j
and
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
, and
it is
bad
if it is not good.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}=({A},{\leq})
be an ordered set. The following are
equivalent:

(1)

Every sequence from
A
is good.

(2)

{\mathbb{A}}
has no descending chain and no antichain.

Proof:

Assume that every sequence from
A
is good. Then
{\mathbb{A}}
has no
descending chain and no antichain, since such chains are all bad.
Now assume that
{\mathbb{A}}
has no descending chain and no antichain,
and let
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence from
A
. Then by Lemma
2.1
,

(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
has a subsequence
T=(a_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that is constant, a descending chain,
an ascending chain, or an antichain. Descending chains and antichains are
excluded by the assumptions, thus
T
is either constant or ascending.
In both cases,
a_{t(1)}\leq a_{t(2)}
, and hence
T
is good. ∎

We call a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
a
minimal bad sequence

if it is a bad sequence, and for every
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

and for every
b<a_{i}
, every sequence starting with

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},b)
is good.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}=({A},{\leq})
be an ordered set.
If
{\mathbb{A}}
has no descending chain and it has an infinite antichain,
then it has a minimal bad sequence.

Proof:

We assume that
(A,\leq)
has an antichain. This antichain is
a bad sequence.
Inductively, we can define a minimal bad sequence.
We define
a_{0}
to be a minimal element with respect to
\leq
of

S_{0}:=\{y_{0}\mid(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\text{ is a bad sequence from $A$}\}
.
For
j\in\mathbb{N}
, having defined
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{j-1})
, we let

S_{j}:=\{y_{j}\mid(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\text{ is a bad sequence from $A$ with }(y_{0},\ldots,y_{j-1})=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{j-1})\},

and we choose

a_{j}
to be a minimal element of
S_{j}
with respect to
\leq
. ∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
({A},{\leq})
be a partially ordered set.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

({A},{\leq})
has no descending chain and no antichain.

(2)

({U(A,\leq)},{\subseteq})
has no ascending chain.

Proof:

Let
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a descending chain or an antichain from
A
,
and let
B_{i}:={\uparrow}\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}\}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then
(B_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an ascending chain.
For the other implication, let
(C_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be an ascending chain,
and choose
c_{i}\in C_{i+1}\setminus C_{i}
. We show that
(c_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

is bad. Suppose
i<j
and
c_{i}\leq c_{j}
. Since
C_{i+1}
is
upward closed, we then have
c_{j}\in C_{i+1}
, and thus

c_{j}\in C_{j}
. This contradicts the choice of
c_{j}
in
C_{j+1}\setminus C_{j}
.
Hence
({A},{\leq})
has a bad sequence, and thus by Lemma
3.2
,
it must have a descending chain or an antichain.
∎

4.
Dicksons’s ordering

In this section we provide a direct proof for Theorem
1.1
by using Ramsey’s theorem. In Section
6
we show that these results can also be obtained as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
by using quasi-embeddings.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(
1
).

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
we denote the
m
-tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
by
\bm{a}
and the

k
th component of

\bm{a}
by
\bm{a}_{k}
.

It can be easily seen that
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has no descending chain.

We assume that
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has an antichain
(\bm{a}^{(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Now we color the
2
-element subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}

with the elements of
{\{1,2\}}^{\{1,\dots,m\}}
as colors.
For
i<j
, we set

c(\{i,j\})\,(k):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\text{ if }\bm{a}^{(i)}_{k}\leq\bm{a}^{(j)}_{k},\\
2&\text{ if }\bm{a}^{(i)}_{k}>\bm{a}^{(j)}_{k}.\end{array}\right.

By Ramsey’s Theorem, we find a subsequence
(\bm{a}^{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

and a color
C\in\{1,2\}^{\{1,\dots,m\}}
such that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
i<j
,
we have

c(\{t(i),t(j)\})=C
.

Assume there exists
k\in{\{1,\dots,m\}}
such that

C(k)=\,\,2
.
Then
\bm{a}^{(t_{0})}_{k}>\bm{a}^{(t_{1})}_{k}>\bm{a}^{(t_{2})}_{k}>\cdots
in contradiction to the fact that
(\mathbb{N}_{0},\leq)
has no descending chain.

Hence
C(k)=1
for all
k
and therefore

\bm{a}^{(t_{0})}\leq\bm{a}^{(t_{1})}\leq\bm{a}^{(t_{2})}\leq\cdots
, contradicting our assumption of
(\bm{a}^{(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
being an antichain.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(
2
).

By Theorem
1.1
(
1
),
(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq)
has
no descending chain and no antichain. Hence Lemma
3.4

yields that
(U(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq),\subseteq)
has no ascending chain.
It remains to show that
(U(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq),\subseteq)
has no antichain.
If
m=1
, the set of upward closed subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
is totally ordered, and hence there cannot be an antichain. In the case

m\geq 2
, we encode each upward closed subset of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
by
a function from
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
to
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
For each upward closed subset
F
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
we define a function

\Phi_{F}:\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\Phi_{F}(\bm{a}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\min\{c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|(\bm{a},c)\in F\}&\text{ if there exists $c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}$ such that
$(\bm{a},c^{\prime})\in F$ },\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

for
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
.
First we show that for all
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}

such that
\bm{a}\leq\bm{b}
, also
\Phi_{F}(\bm{a})\geq\Phi_{F}(\bm{b})
holds.
Therefore, let
c:=\Phi_{F}(\bm{a})
. We assume
c\neq\infty
.
We have
(\bm{a},c)\in F
. Since
F
is an upward closed set, also
(\bm{b},c)\in F
, and hence

\Phi_{F}(\bm{b})\leq c=\Phi_{F}(\bm{a})
.
Moreover, for the upward closed subsets
F,G
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
the inclusion

F\subseteq G
holds if and only if

\Phi_{F}(\bm{a})\geq\Phi_{G}(\bm{a})
for all

\bm{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
.

Let
(F_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be an antichain in
(U(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m},\leq),\subseteq)
.
Thus for
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j
,

F_{j}\not\subseteq F_{i}
holds. Accordingly, there exists

\bm{a}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m-1}
such that

\Phi_{F_{j}}(\bm{a}^{(i,j)})<\Phi_{F_{i}}(\bm{a}^{(i,j)}).

Now we color the
3
-element subsets of
\mathbb{N}_{0}

with the elements of
{\{1,2\}}^{\{1,\dots,m-1\}}
as colors.
For
l\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, we denote the
l
th component of

\bm{a}^{(i,j)}
by
\bm{a}^{(i,j)}_{l}
.
For
i<j<k
we define the coloring of
\{i,j,k\}
in the following way:

c(\{i,j,k\})\,(l):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\text{if }\bm{a}^{(i,j)}_{l}\leq\bm{a}^{(j,k)}_{l},\\
2&\text{if }\bm{a}^{(i,j)}_{l}>\bm{a}^{(j,k)}_{l}.\end{array}\right.

By Ramsey’s Theorem, we find an infinite subset
T
of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
T=\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots\}
,
t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots
,
and a color
C\in\{1,2\}^{\{1,\dots,m-1\}}
such that for all
i,j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
i<j<k
,
we have

c(\{t_{i},t_{j},t_{k}\})=C
.
We now show that
C(l)=\,\,1

for all
l\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
.

In contradiction to that, we assume that there exists
l
such that

C(l)\,=\,\,2
.
Then

\bm{a}^{(t_{0},t_{1})}_{l}>\bm{a}^{(t_{1},t_{2})}_{l}>\bm{a}^{(t_{2},t_{3})}_{l}>\cdots.

holds.
Hence we have constructed a descending chain of natural numbers, which is impossible.

Thus for all
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the inequality

\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})}\leq\bm{a}^{(t_{r+1},t_{r+2})}
holds.
Now let
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Because of the choice of

\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})}
, we have

\Phi_{F_{t_{r}}}(\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})})>\Phi_{F_{t_{r+1}}}(\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})}).

Since
\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})}\leq\bm{a}^{(t_{r+1},t_{r+2})}
,
we also have

\Phi_{F_{t_{r+1}}}(\bm{a}^{(t_{r},t_{r+1})})\geq\Phi_{F_{t_{r+1}}}(\bm{a}^{(t_{r+1},t_{r+2})}).

Hence the sequence
(\Phi_{F_{t_{i}}}(\bm{a}^{(t_{i},t_{i+1})}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a descending chain in

\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
, which is impossible.

Consequently there cannot exist an antichain of upward closed subsets of

\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
.
∎

6.
Other well partially ordered sets

We will derive the other results stated in Section
1

by using
quasi-embeddings
:

Definition 6.1
.

[
AH07
]

Let
{\mathbb{A}}=(A,\leq_{A})
and
{\mathbb{B}}=(B,\leq_{B})
be partially ordered sets.
A mapping
f:A\to B
is a
quasi-embedding
from
{\mathbb{A}}

into
{\mathbb{B}}
if
for all
a_{1},a_{2}\in A:f(a_{1})\leq_{B}f(a_{2})\Rightarrow a_{1}\leq_{A}a_{2}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

[
AH07
]

Let
({A},{\leq_{A}})
and
({B},{\leq_{B}})
be partially ordered sets.
Let
f:A\to B
be a quasi-embedding from

({A},{\leq_{A}})
into
({B},{\leq_{B}})
.
Then we have:

(1)

If
({A},{\leq_{A}})
has an antichain, then

({B},{\leq_{B}})
has an antichain.

(2)

If
({A},{\leq_{A}})
has a descending chain, then

({B},{\leq_{B}})
has an antichain or a descending chain.

Proof:

For (
1
), we let
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be
an antichain. We define
b_{i}:=f(a_{i})
and we claim
that
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an antichain.
To this end, let
j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
b_{j}\leq b_{k}
.
Then
f(a_{j})\leq f(a_{k})
, and therefore
a_{j}\leq a_{k}
.
Since
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an antichain,
j=k
, completing
the proof that
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an antichain.

For (
2
), let
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a descending chain.
According to Lemma
2.1
, it is sufficient to show that
that the sequence
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}:=(f(a_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

has no subsequence that is constant or an ascending chain.
Seeking a contradiction, we let
(b_{t(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a subsequence
with
b_{t(1)}\leq b_{t(2)}\leq\cdots
. Since
f
is a
quasi-embedding,
a_{t(1)}\leq_{A}a_{t(2)}
, which contradicts the fact that

(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is descending.
∎

.

Let
({A},{\leq_{A}})
