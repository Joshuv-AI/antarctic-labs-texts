Numerical approximation of statistical solutions of the incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations
Pratyuksh Bansal

Abstract
Statistical solutions, which are time-parameterized probability measures on spaces of square-integrable functions, have been established as a suitable framework for global solutions of incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (NSE). We compute numerical approximations of statistical solutions of NSE on two-dimensional domains with non-periodic boundary conditions and empirically investigate the convergence of these approximations and their observables. For the numerical solver, we use Monte Carlo sampling with an H(div)-FEM based deterministic solver. Our numerical experiments for high Reynolds number turbulent flows demonstrate that the statistics and observables of the approximations converge. We also develop a novel algorithm to compute structure functions on unstructured meshes.



1
Introduction

The flow of a viscous, incompressible Newtonian fluid is described by the (incompressible) Navier-Stokes equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\operatorname{div}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})-\nu\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}{p}=\mathbf{f},\quad\operatorname{div}_{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{u}}=0.

Here
\mathbf{u}
is the velocity,
p
is the pressure, which plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the divergence-free constraint on the velocity,

\nu
is the kinematic viscosity and
\mathbf{f}
represents the effects of external forces, for example, gravity and buoyancy. This system also needs to be supplied with initial and boundary conditions.

Navier-Stokes equations play a fundamental role in many applications and have been studied extensively for more than a century.
The existence of global-in-time weak solutions, both in two and three spatial dimensions, can be traced back to the pioneering works of Leray
[
39
]
and Hopf
[
27
]
. In two spatial dimensions, the uniqueness of weak solutions of NSE has been established. However, in three spatial dimensions, the question of uniqueness remains unanswered and has been designated as a Millennium Prize Problem by the Clay Mathematics Institute.

Keeping the uniqueness question aside, it is known that, for small viscosity
\nu
(or, equivalently, large Reynolds number
Re\sim\nu^{-1}
), i.e. when inertial forces are much stronger than viscous diffusion, the fluid flow is very sensitive to initial conditions and characterized by chaotic motions.
Therefore, in a deterministic framework, measurement errors in the problem data could have a drastic affect on the solutions of NSE, and so, it is not physically meaningful to describe these turbulent flows as individual solutions.
On the other hand, there is ample experimental evidence that the statistical observables, for example, mean and variance, can be inferred reliably for turbulent flows.
Hence, for practical applications, it is more useful to consider the evolution of NSE under uncertainties on the problem data in a suitable probabilistic framework.
In their seminal work
[
17
,
19
]
, Foiaş and Prodi proposed the framework of statistical solutions of incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, in which, given a probability distribution on the initial velocity, velocity ensembles are evolved according to the NSE and described by a time-parameterized family of probability measures on the function space of initial velocity.
The existence of such statistical solutions, both in two and three spatial dimensions, has been well established
[
17
,
18
]
. The global uniqueness in three spatial dimensions is an open problem. In two dimensions, statistical solutions are unique and they are defined as the push-forward of the probability measure on the initial velocity data.
The authors in
[
18
]
show that several results of the conventional theory of turbulence for NSE attributed to the ground-breaking work of Kolmogorov
[
29
,
30
]
can be recovered with statistical solutions, thus, providing evidence of the importance of this solution framework.

The computation of statistical solutions of NSE is a special case of Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), for which different methods are available in the literature (see
[
5
]
and the references therein). Loosely speaking, the different methods used to solve UQ problems can be categorized into two classes: stochastic Galerkin methods and stochastic collocation methods. Stochastic Galerkin methods consider the Ritz-Galerkin formulation of the underlying PDEs in the stochastic parameter space, thus, these methods are highly intrusive and not amenable for implementation from a practical viewpoint
[
49
, Chap. 12]
. On the other hand, stochastic collocation methods are well-suited for large scale applications as they are non-intrusive
[
49
, Chap. 13]
.
For applications, Monte Carlo (MC) and Quasi-Monte Carlo sampling can be seen as a subclass of stochastic collocation methods. Multi-level Monte Carlo methods have also received a lot of attention in research in recent times (see
[
37
]
and the references therein).

In the discussion above, the non-intrusive methods require a deterministic solver that numerically approximates the solution of the underlying system of PDEs (in our case the NSE).
There are many numerical methods available in the literature that can efficiently approximate the NSE. For problems with periodic boundary, spectral methods are appealing because of their efficiency and high resolution
[
36
]
.
Finite difference methods with Leray projection were first introduced in
[
31
,
8
,
9
]
. Later, the authors in
[
2
]
proposed a finite-volume scheme that was an efficient variant of
[
9
]
.
In
[
37
]
, the authors develop a finite-difference scheme for the vorticity formulation of the NSE with uniformly stable bounds with respect to viscosity; this scheme is a variant of the one proposed in
[
40
]
for incompressible Euler equations.
Finite element methods are another class of methods to solve NSE
[
24
]
.
While standard mixed FEM are not pressure-robust, i.e. pressure approximation influences the velocity approximation, (weakly) divergence-free mixed methods are (see
[
28
]
and the references therein).
In these weakly divergence-free mixed methods, if the velocity field belongs to the Hilbert space
H^{1}
, then the a priori error bounds are non-uniform with respect to viscosity. However, if H(div)-conforming spaces are used instead, then the a priori velocity error estimates are robust with respect to viscosity
[
47
]
.
Moreover, the divergence-free constraint on the velocity field is satisfied point-wise in the spatial domain.
The authors in
[
35
]
proposed a computationally efficient hybridized variant of H(div) FEM, called the H(div)-HDG method, where they introduce hybridization for the tangential velocity components.
This method was further improved in
[
34
]
and used to study turbulent flows in
[
14
,
46
]
.

1.1
Contributions

We restrict ourselves to two-dimensional open, bounded spatial domains and choose the framework of statistical solutions for NSE.
Given the discussion above, to numerically approximate these solutions, we use Monte Carlo sampling for the stochastic space and H(div)-FEM as the deterministic solver.
We can easily use QMC instead of MC, but it is not necessary as our main focus is on the presence or absence of convergence of the approximate solutions and their statistics, not the rates. We choose the H(div) scheme and not the more efficient H(div)-HDG scheme because of the ease of implementation. In the deterministic solver, we use the implicit Euler method for time integration as there is no CFL restriction on the time-step size (see
[
42
,
35
]
for higher-order time integration methods).
To the best of my knowledge, this is the first computational effort in this direction.

Structure functions are an important observable in turbulence literature
[
21
]
. In the context of incompressible Euler equations, a uniform decay of the structure functions ensures the convergence of approximate statistical solutions in the very recent work
[
33
]
. The behaviour of structure functions is also crucial in the study of the vanishing viscosity limit of incompressible Navier-Stokes in
[
32
]
.
In this light, a novel contribution of this work is the development of an algorithm for approximating structure functions on unstructured meshes. We compute the structure functions only at the final time, which is a good indicator of their behaviour over the whole time interval as seen in
[
33
]
.

3.1
Weak solutions

We assume homogeneous Dirichlet BCs, i.e.
{\partial\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{out}}=\emptyset

We choose the functional setting used in

\displaystyle H:=H_{nsp}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})^{2}:\operatorname{div}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v}=0,\ \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{D}}|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=0\},

(3.2)

\displaystyle V:=V_{nsp}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{D})^{2}:\operatorname{div}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v}=0,\ \mathbf{v}|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=\mathbf{0}\}.

On these spaces, we define the inner products

\displaystyle(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})_{H}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}\penalty\ d\mathbf{x},\ \mathrm{for}\ \mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in H,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})_{V}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I};V)\cap C(\overline{\mathcal{I}};H),

and, for all

4
Statistical solutions

The discussion in this section is based on the monograph
[
18
, Chapter V]
and it partially uses the notation prescribed in
[
37
, §2.2]
.
We consider statistical solutions of NSE (
3.1
) with fixed, no-slip boundary, i.e.
{\partial\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{out}}=\emptyset
and
\mathbf{g}=0
,
where the boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}
is of class
C^{2}
.

Given a probability distribution
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
on the initial velocity data, i.e.

\displaystyle P(\{\mathbf{u}_{0}\in E\})=\mu_{0}(E),\ \mathrm{for}\ E\in\mathcal{B}(H),

the main idea of statistical solutions, as introduced by Foiaş-Prodi
[
17
,
19
]
, is to describe the evolution of velocity ensembles by a time-parameterized family of probability measures
\mu^{\nu}=(\mu^{\nu}_{t})_{t\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}}
, such that
\mu^{\nu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
(up to null sets), and for every
t>0
,
\mu^{\nu}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
.
This solution framework includes the individual weak solutions of NSE as a special case with the probability measure
\mu_{0}=\delta_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}
, where
\delta_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}
is the Dirac measure (also known as the unit mass) at
\mathbf{u}_{0}
, cf.
[
45
, §4.7]
.

From Theorem
3.2
, we know that the individual weak solutions of NSE are unique and the solution operator
\mathcal{S}^{\nu}
exists.
As a result, for any time-independent forcing
\mathbf{f}\in H
, unique solutions
\mu^{\nu}_{t}
are given by transporting the initial probability measure
\mu_{0}
under the solution operator
\mathcal{S}^{\nu}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu^{\nu}_{t}(E)=\mu_{0}(\left(\mathcal{S}^{\nu}(t)\right)^{-1}E),\ \forall E\in\mathcal{B}(H).

(4.1)

Concretely, we state this existence and uniqueness result in the following (cf.
[
18

):

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mu_{0}
be a probability measure on
H
with finite kinetic energy, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{H}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}d\mu_{0}(\mathbf{u})<\infty.

Assume that the forcing term
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I};H)
. Then, for every
\nu>0
and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, there exists a statistical solution
\mu^{\nu}=(\mu_{t}^{\nu})_{t\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}}
of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (

) on
H
.

Moreover, if
\mu_{0}
has bounded support in
H
and
\mathbf{f}\in H
is independent of time, then the statistical solution

is unique and is given by

, where
\mathcal{S}^{\nu}(t)
is the solution operator of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, cf. Theorem

.

4.1
Structure functions

For a solution
\mu^{\nu}_{t}
, other than its common statistics like mean and variance, its structure functions are of great interest to us.
The structure functions are defined as follows (we refer to
[
41
]
):

Definition 4.2

(Structure functions)
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}
.
Let
\mathbf{u}:\Omega\times Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a random field such that

, for any
\omega\in\Omega
, and
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(L^{p}(\mathcal{D})^{2})
be the probability distribution of
\mathbf{u}
at time

Then, the structure function
S^{p}_{r,t}
associated with
\mu_{t}
is given by:

\displaystyle S^{p}_{r,t}(\mu_{t})=\left(\int_{L^{p}(\mathcal{D})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\fint_{B_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{y},t)\right\rVert_{p}^{p}d\mathbf{y}\ d\mathbf{x}\ d\mu_{t}(\mathbf{u})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(4.2)

where

denotes the ball of radius
r
centered at a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}
.

We use the structure functions

as a measure of the regularity of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
, and we investigate the scaling behaviour of

with respect to the offset

Remark 4.3
.

If
\mathbf{u}
is Lipschitz-continuous in

, then straightforward calculations show that

. We expect the same scaling behaviour for the velocity field in NSE due to regularity (

) under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
.

4.2
Wasserstein distances

In the next section, we describe a numerical method to approximate statistical solutions with ensembles of approximate individual velocity solutions. To study the convergence of these velocity ensembles, it is important to define a notion of distance between two ensembles.
For this purpose, we use Wasserstein distance (cf.
[
15
, Definition 2.2]
):

Definition 4.4
.

Let
X
be a separable Banach space, and let
\mu,\rho
be probability measures on
X
with finite
p
-th moments, i.e.
\int_{X}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{p}d\mu(x)<\infty
and
\int_{X}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{p}d\rho(x)<\infty
. Then, the
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu,\rho):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\rho)}\int_{X^{2}}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{p}\penalty\ d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where the infimum is taken over the set
\Gamma(\mu,\rho)\subset\mathcal{P}(X^{2})
of all transport plans from
\mu
to
\rho
, i.e. those
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(X^{2})
that satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{X^{2}}\left(F(x)+G(y)\right)d\gamma(x,y)=\int_{X}F(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{X}G(y)d\rho(y),\quad\forall F,G\in C_{b}(X).

Here
C_{b}(X)
denotes the space of bounded, continuous, real-valued functionals on
X
.

Given
\mu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}(H)
, we can define
p
-Wasserstein distance for the
1
-point distribution at
\mathbf{x}
and the
2
-point correlation at
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
, respectively, as:

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu(\mathbf{x}),\rho(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle:=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\rho)}\int_{H^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert^{p}_{2}d\gamma(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(4.3)

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\rho(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle:=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\rho)}\int_{H^{2}}\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\\
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{y})\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})\\
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{p}_{2}d\gamma(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(4.4)

For the whole domain
\mathcal{D}
, we can define

\displaystyle W_{p}^{1}(\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}}W_{p}(\mu(\mathbf{x}),\rho(\mathbf{x}))\penalty\ d\mathbf{x},

(4.5)

\displaystyle W_{p}^{2}(\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\int_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{D}^{2}}W_{p}(\mu(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\rho(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))\penalty\ d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

(4.6)

In this work, we always use
1
-Wasserstein distances
W_{1}^{1}
and
W_{1}^{2}
, so we simply refer to them as Wasserstein distances and drop the subscripts.

6
Implementation details

In this section, we discuss important details about our implementation of the Monte Carlo algorithm
1
, which we refer to as MC-FEM solver;
here all the samples are evolved in parallel using the self-scheduling algorithm
[
25
, §3.6]
.
In particular, we describe the deterministic solver that is used for a single Monte Carlo sample and present the algorithms used to approximate structure functions and Wasserstein distances.

We denote vectors by a lower-case font with an underline and matrices by upper-case font or bold font with an underline, only for the discussion on the deterministic solver.
Given bases of
V_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
and
Q_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, we denote the corresponding vectors of basis coefficients by
\underline{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(V_{k})}
and
\underline{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(Q_{k})}
.
Then, the finite element assembly for the discrete formulation (
5.14
) yields the following block-structured linear system of equations:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{M}_{h}+\Delta t({C}_{h}+\nu{A}_{h})&\Delta t{B}_{h}^{\top}\\
{B}_{h}&\underline{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\underline{u}\\
\underline{p}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{f}_{h}\\
\underline{0}\end{bmatrix},

(6.1)

where the matrices
{M}_{h}
,
{C}_{h}
,
{A}_{h}
and
{B}_{h}
are assembled from the mass form
\mathcal{M}_{h}
, the convection form
\mathcal{C}_{h}^{up}
, the diffusion form
\mathcal{A}_{h}^{sip}
and the divergence form
\mathcal{B}_{h}
, respectively. Here the right-hand side vector
\underline{f}_{h}
is assembled from the linear form
\ell_{h}
. Note that we must use the Piola transformation in our finite element assembly.

We need to assemble the matrices
{M}_{h}
,
{A}_{h}
and
{B}_{h}
only once, but the matrix
{C}_{h}
has to be re-assembled for each discrete time
t_{n}
because of its dependence on the velocity solution of the previous time step
t_{n-1}
. Using direct solvers for the linear system (
6.1
) would involve factorizing the re-assembled system matrix at each time step, which is computationally expensive. Therefore, we use suitable iterative solvers with preconditioning. Re-scaling the pressure solution as
\widetilde{\underline{p}}:=(\Delta t)\underline{p}
, the linear system (
6.1
) can be re-written in the form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{M}_{h}+\Delta t({C}_{h}+\nu{A}_{h})&{B}_{h}^{\top}\\
{B}_{h}&\underline{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\underline{u}\\
\widetilde{\underline{p}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{f}_{h}\\
\underline{0}\end{bmatrix},

(6.2)

which is more suitable for iterative solvers in practice.

The linear system (
6.2
) is in saddle point form. We refer to the monograph
[
3
]
for an extensive review of the literature on the numerical solution of saddle point problems. Note that the matrices
{M}_{h}
and
{A}_{h}
are symmetric, but
{C}_{h}
is non-symmetric, which implies that the linear system (
6.2
) is also non-symmetric. So, we choose the GMRES method
[
44
]
to solve our non-symmetric linear system, with the state-of-the-art block triangular preconditioner described in
[
3
, §10.1.2]
.

When needed, see Remark
5.1
, we impose the vanishing mean constraint for pressure through the iterative solver via an intermediate preconditioning matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\mathbf{I}}&\underline{\mathbf{0}}\\
\underline{\mathbf{0}}&\underline{\mathbf{I}}-\frac{1}{\left\lvert\mathcal{D}\right\rvert}({M}_{h}^{p})^{-1}\underline{b}_{h}\underline{b}_{h}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

(6.3)

where
\left\lvert\mathcal{D}\right\rvert
is the volume of
\mathcal{D}
,
M_{h}^{p}
denotes the matrix that corresponds to the bilinear form
\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}p_{h}q_{h}d\mathbf{x}
, for
p_{h},q_{h}\in Q_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
,
\underline{b}_{h}
denotes the vector that corresponds to the linear form
\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}q_{h}d\mathbf{x}
, for
q_{h}\in Q_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
and
\underline{\mathbf{I}}
is the identity matrix.

[
1
]
, version 4.1, with MPI-parallelized domain decomposition.
We use the mesh generation routines provided by MFEM and the open-source simplicial mesh generator Gmsh

]

The codes developed can be found at

6.1
Computing the structure functions

We want to compute the structure functions (
4.2
) for the approximate statistical velocity solutions
\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M}
.
An efficient algorithm to compute
S^{p}_{r,t}(\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M})
on uniform grids was presented by Lye
[
41
]
.
In the present work, our objective is to compute structure functions on unstructured meshes for rectangular domains
\mathcal{D}=(x_{1}^{\mathrm{left}},x_{1}^{\mathrm{right}})\times(x_{2}^{\mathrm{left}},x_{2}^{\mathrm{right}})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
with
x_{1}^{\mathrm{left}}<x_{1}^{\mathrm{right}}
and
x_{2}^{\mathrm{left}}<x_{2}^{\mathrm{right}}
, which we commonly use in our numerical experiments in §
7
.

By definition, for any
t\in\overline{\mathcal{I}}
, we have that

\displaystyle(S^{p}_{r,t}(\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M}))^{p}=\int_{L^{p}(\mathcal{D})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\fint_{B_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{y},t)\right\rVert_{p}^{p}d\mathbf{y}\ d\mathbf{x}\ d\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M}(\mathbf{u}),

and using the fact that
\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M}
is a sum of Dirac masses (see (
5.1
)),

\displaystyle(S^{p}_{r,t}(\mu^{\nu}_{t;h,M}))^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{p}(\mathcal{D})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\fint_{B_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{u}(\cdot;\mathbf{y},t)\right\rVert_{p}^{p}d\mathbf{y}\ d\mathbf{x}\ d\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\mathbf{u}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\cdot,t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\fint_{B_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{u}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{y},t)\right\rVert_{p}^{p}d\mathbf{y}\ d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}S^{p}_{r,t}(\mathbf{u}_{h,m}),

where we define

S^{p}_{r,t}(\mathbf{u}_{h,m}):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\fint_{B_{r}(\mathbf{x})}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{u}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{y},t)\right\rVert_{p}^{p}d\mathbf{y}\ d\mathbf{x}.

(6.4)

The discrete solutions
\mathbf{u}_{h,m}
are only first-order accurate because of implicit Euler time-stepping, cf. Remark
5.5
. Therefore, we can further approximate
\mathbf{u}_{h,m}
with an element-wise constant function
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}
,
defined in every element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{x},t):=\frac{1}{\left\lvert K\right\rvert}\int_{K}{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}(\omega_{m};\mathbf{y},t)\penalty\ d\mathbf{y},\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in K.

(6.5)

It can be shown with straightforward calculations that this element-averaged function
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}
is a first-order approximation of the element-wise polynomial function
\mathbf{u}_{h,m}
.
In the numerical experiments §
7
, we compute the structure functions at time
t=T
, so we drop the subscript corresponding to time in
S_{r,T}^{p}
in the text that follows, and by
{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}
and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}
we simply refer to the approximate solutions at the final time.
Thus, our task boils down to computing
S^{p}_{r}({\mathbf{u}}_{h,m})\approx S^{p}_{r}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m})
, for
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
.

The primary component required to compute
S^{p}_{r}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m})
is a nearest neighbor search (NNS) algorithm that helps us efficiently search for mesh elements lying within a ball
B_{r}
.
NNS problem arises in various fields, for example, statistics, machine learning and astronomy, we refer to, for example,
[
7
]
and the references therein. A popular choice to solve NNS problems is the so-called “cell-techniques” method with fixed-radius searches,
[
7
,
20
]
.
This fixed-radius NNS algorithm, adapted to our setting, is as follows:

1.

Equi-partition the domain
\mathcal{D}
into a uniform grid of cells such that the size of each cell is greater than or equal to the offset
r
of the ball
B_{r}
. Map each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
to a cell in the uniform grid according to the coordinates of its centroid.

2.

Loop over all the cells in the uniform grid, and for each cell, loop over all the mesh elements mapped to it.
For each element
K
, we find the neighbouring mesh elements lying within the ball
B_{r}
by searching the current cell and its immediate neighbouring cells.

Concretely, in the above-listed, the first step is implemented in Algorithm
2
using a hash table data structure,
where the function call
\mathrm{element\_number}(\mathcal{T}_{h},K)
returns the element number of
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
\mathrm{centroid}(K)
returns the centroid of the mesh element
K
,
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function and the last entry in the tuple
(id_{K},\left\lvert K\right\rvert,\mathbf{x}_{K}^{c},\underline{0})
corresponds to the velocity data of
K

To compute
S^{p}_{r}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m})
, we update the hash table with the velocity data
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}
using Algorithm
3
and then use Algorithm
4
.
Put together, we use Algorithm

to compute the structure function for the velocity ensemble
\{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{h,m}\}_{m=1,\ldots,M}

7
Numerical experiments

In the numerical experiments in §
7.1
and §
7.2
, we compute statistical solutions for the flow in a lid-driven cavity and in a rectangular channel, respectively.
The settings in these experiments have been adopted from
[
36
]
with slight modifications.
We denote by
\mathcal{U}[-1,+1]
the uniform distribution between
-1
to
+1
, and we consider smooth perturbations of the initial data.

In our MC-FEM solver, there are two sources of error: stochastic error due to MC sampling and FE discretisation error. It is well-known that the MC stochastic error scales as
O(M^{-\frac{1}{2}})
with respect to the number of samples
M

[
50
]
. The discretisation error scales as
O(\Delta t+h^{k})
with respect the time step size
\Delta t
and the mesh-size
h
(cf. Remark
5.5
).
As the main concern of the present work is the presence or absence of convergence of statistics and observables of velocity ensembles, rather than their convergence rates, we choose
\Delta t
,
h
and
M
such that the stochastic error dominates. To this end, we fix the polynomial degree
k=1
, which yields
O(h^{2})
convergence for the deterministic velocity approximation of smooth solutions, in practice.

For an approximate statistical solution
\mu^{\nu}_{T;h,M}
, we compute the mean

\displaystyle E_{M}[\mathbf{u}_{h,T}]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{u}_{h;m}(\omega_{m};\cdot,T)

and the (unbiased) sample variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{M}[\mathbf{u}_{h,T}]=\frac{M}{M-1}\left(E_{M}[(\mathbf{u}_{h,T})^{2}]-E_{M}[\mathbf{u}_{h,T}]^{2}\right).

We generally do not know the exact statistical solution
\mu^{\nu}_{T}
for a given problem. Therefore, to study the convergence of the mean and variance of their approximations, we measure the Cauchy error in the
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
norm, i.e., for example,

\left\lVert E_{M}[\mathbf{u}_{h,T}]-E_{2M}[\mathbf{u}_{h/2,T}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
in the lid-driven cavity problem.

We denote velocity ensembles by
\mathbf{U}^{h,M}_{T}:=\{\mathbf{u}_{h;m}(\omega_{m};\cdot,T)\}_{m=1,\ldots,M}
.

8
Conclusions

We conclude this chapter by summarizing the main results in the following:

•

We computed the statistical solutions of two-dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for problems with non-periodic boundary conditions, in particular, the lid-driven cavity problem with a moving top boundary and the channel flow problem with an outflow boundary. Through our numerical experiments, we have gathered ample evidence to conclude that the approximate statistical solutions converge, where we compute these approximations using our MC-FEM solver.

•

We have developed a novel algorithm to numerically approximate structure functions on unstructured meshes for two-dimensional rectangular domains. We can extend this algorithm to more general two-dimensional domains with some modifications, see Remark
6.1
. Moreover, the algorithm can be extended for three-dimensional rectangular cuboid domains, albeit an efficient parallel version would be necessary in this case.

Numerical computation of an individual solution of the NSE for high Reynolds numbers flows with non-periodic boundary using FEM is expensive because it requires meshes with a high resolution near the boundary. An alternative to the H(div) scheme used in the present work is the more computationally efficient H(div)-HDG scheme with static condensation
[
34
,
35
]
. Then, combined with MC or QMC sampling, one can simulate more challenging problems, such as flow past a cylinder for high Reynolds numbers where the vortex-shedding phenomenon is observed
[
43
, Chapter 9]
. The flow past a cylinder is an interesting problem to investigate in the vanishing viscosity limit.
