Dissipation bounds precision of current response to kinetic perturbations
Krzysztof Ptaszyński, Timur Aslyamov, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
The precision of currents in Markov networks is bounded by dissipation via the so-called thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR). In our work, we demonstrate a similar inequality that bounds the precision of the static current response to perturbations of kinetic barriers. Perturbations of such type, which affect only the system kinetics but not the thermodynamic forces, are highly important in biochemistry and nanoelectronics. We prove that our inequality cannot be derived from the standard TUR. Instead, it implies the standard TUR and provides an even tighter bound for dissipation. We also provide a procedure for obtaining the optimal response precision for a given model.



Introduction.—
Among the most fundamental results of statistical physics are the relations between the system response to external perturbations and stationary thermodynamic observables. Close to equilibrium, such a relation is given by the seminal fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) linking the linear response to external forces and equilibrium fluctuations
Kubo 1966
. The link between dissipation and fluctuations has recently been generalized to a far-from-equilibrium regime
Agarwal 1972
;
Seifert and Speck 2010
;
Prost et al. 2009
;
Altaner et al. 2016
;
Chun et al. 2021
;
Baiesi et al. 2009
;
Dechant and Sasa 2020
;
Di Terlizzi and Baiesi 2018
;
Falasco et al. 2022
;
Hasegawa 2024
.
In particular, for Markov jump processes, the theory of stochastic thermodynamics
Seifert 2012
gave rise to the thermodynamics uncertainty relation (TUR) linking the entropy production rate
\dot{\sigma}
which measures dissipation to the average of any current
\mathcal{J}
and its variance
\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle
as
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Gingrich et al. 2016
;
Pietzonka et al. 2016
;
Pietzonka et al. 2017
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2017
;
Falasco et al. 2020
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
;
Van Vu et al. 2020

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}\leq\frac{\dot{\sigma}}{2}\,.

(1)

This relation shows that to reduce the fluctuations, one needs to pay by increased dissipation. In other words, it describes the thermodynamic cost of current precision
\mathcal{J}^{2}/\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle
. It can also be used to infer the value of entropy production, which is often directly inaccessible, using measurable currents and their fluctuations
Seifert 2019
. Indeed, the current fluctuations have been measured in various setups, such as nanoelectronic devices
Birk et al. 1995
;
Gustavsson et al. 2006
and biomolecular systems
Svoboda et al. 1994
;
Visscher et al. 1999
;
Bormuth et al. 2009
, and experimental data have been used to infer the value of the entropy production in the kinesin molecular motor
Seifert 2018
.

In our work, we get closer to the original formulation of FDT by providing a thermodynamic trade-off between the current fluctuations and the static linear current response, rather than the current itself. Recently, static responses for Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks have attracted notable attention
Owen et al. 2020
;
Owen and Horowitz 2023
;
Fernandes Martins and Horowitz 2023
;
Chun and Horowitz 2023
;
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024a
;
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b
;
Harunari et al. 2024
;
Floyd et al. 2024
;
Zheng and Lu 2024
due to their importance in biophysical applications (proofreading, sensing
Murugan et al. 2014
;
Owen et al. 2020
). Specifically, we focus on the response to kinetic perturbations, i.e., perturbations affecting only the kinetics of transitions between the system states but not the thermodynamic forces. Perturbations of this type are crucial in chemistry (including biochemistry), as they correspond to the control of kinetic rates by changing the concentration of a catalyst (e.g., enzyme)
Wachtel et al. 2018
. Among others, kinetics may play a crucial role in determining the direction of motion of molecular motors
Maes and de Galway 2015
and providing the driving mechanism of Maxwell demons
Esposito and Schaller 2012
. Chemical kinetics can also be controlled by magnetic fields, e.g., via the radical pair mechanism
Steiner and Ulrich 1989
, which is hypothesized to be the basis for magnetoreception and other magnetic field effects in biology
Ritz et al. 2000
;
Hore and Mouritsen 2016
;
Wiltschko and Wiltschko 2019
;
Zadeh-Haghighi and Simon 2022
. Another important example of kinetic perturbations appears in the field of nanoelectronics, where it corresponds to the adjustment of tunnel barriers in quantum dots
Gustavsson et al. 2006
;
Gustavsson et al. 2009
;
Sánchez et al. 2019
or potential barriers in CMOS devices
Freitas et al. 2021
;
Gopal et al. 2022
by gate voltages. Crucially, the response of currents to kinetic perturbations vanishes at equilibrium, where the state of the system depends only on its thermodynamics, and not on its kinetics. This leads to the intuitive expectation that the response to kinetic perturbations requires some thermodynamic cost. Indeed, certain thermodynamics bounds on the response to kinetic perturbations have been obtained for Markov jump processes
Owen et al. 2020
;
Fernandes Martins and Horowitz 2023
, chemical reaction networks
Chun and Horowitz 2023
, and nonequilibrium diffusion
Gao et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
. However, they apply to static system observables and their fluctuations rather than to currents.

In this Letter, we provide numerical evidence to conjecture a new bound on the current response to kinetic perturbations. We call it the response thermodynamic uncertainty relation (R-TUR). We can prove it in special cases detailed below, e.g., for unicyclic networks, linear response regime close to equilibrium, and for single edge perturbations. To formulate that bound, we parameterize the transition rates of the opposite Markov jumps
\pm e
as

\displaystyle W_{\pm e}=\exp\big[B_{e}(\varepsilon)\pm S_{e}/2\big]\,,

(2)

where
B_{e}
and
S_{e}
parameterize the symmetric and asymmetric part of the transition rate, respectively. The former term characterizes the kinetic barriers, while the latter is the entropy change in the reservoir due to the jump
e
. Only the kinetic part
B_{e}
is assumed to depend on the control parameter
\varepsilon
(which can correspond, e.g., to the enzyme concentration
Wachtel et al. 2018
or the gate voltage
Gustavsson et al. 2006
;
Gustavsson et al. 2009
;
Sánchez et al. 2019
;
Freitas et al. 2021
;
Gopal et al. 2022
). The bound reads

\displaystyle\frac{(d_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{J})^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}\leq\frac{b_{\text{max}}^{2}\dot{\sigma}}{2}\,,

(3)

where
d_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{J}\equiv d\mathcal{J}/d\varepsilon
is the static response of any current
\mathcal{J}
to the parameter
\varepsilon
and
b_{\text{max}}=\max_{e}|\partial_{\varepsilon}B_{e}|
is the maximal rate of change of kinetic barriers in the system. If we perturb a single barrier corresponding to a given edge
e
, our bound (
3
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{(d_{B_{e}}\mathcal{J})^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}\leq\frac{\dot{\sigma}}{2}\,.

(4)

Although the above bounds look similar to TUR (
1
), we prove that they cannot be derived from TUR.
Instead, TUR is a consequence of our result. Moreover, we develop the optimization procedure that transforms R-TUR into a bound for entropy production that is tighter than the optimized TUR
Van Vu et al. 2020
. However, in some specific cases R-TUR can be derived from TUR. In particular,
Eq.

3
can be proven for unicyclic networks and the linear response regime close to equilibrium. Furthermore,
Eq.

4
can be derived when the current response at the perturbed edge is considered, i.e., when
\mathcal{J}=j_{e}
.

Bound optimization.—
We notice that
Eq.

7
holds for an arbitrary linear combination of
j_{e}
and an arbitrary dependence on
\varepsilon

\bm{x}
and
\bm{b}
are arbitrary vectors. Therefore, for a given graph
\mathcal{G}
and rate matrix
\mathbb{W}
, the optimized version of

7
reads

\displaystyle\mu\equiv\max_{\bm{\beta}}\max_{\bm{x}}m(\bm{\beta},\bm{x})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\dot{\sigma}}{2}.

(8)

The problem

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\beta})=(d_{\varepsilon}\bm{j}^{\intercal}\mathbb{C}^{-1}d_{\varepsilon}\bm{j})^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{-1}d_{\varepsilon}\bm{j}\,,

(9)

which results in

for the inner maximum in

8

is here the Drazin inverse

1

Relation between R-TUR and TUR.—
We now prove that the conjectured R-TUR (
3
) cannot be derived from the standard TUR (
1
). Instead, it implies the validity of TUR. To that end, we recall the formulation of R-TUR via
Eq.

7
(which is equivalent to
Eq.

3

\displaystyle\forall_{\bm{x}}:\quad m(\bm{1},\bm{x})=\frac{\big(\bm{1}\nabla\mathcal{J}\big)^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\dot{\sigma}}{2}\,,

(10)

where
\bm{\beta}=\bm{1}
(vector of ones) corresponds to homogeneous perturbation of the kinetic rates. This can be proven by noting that

\displaystyle\bm{1}\nabla\mathcal{J}=\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathcal{J}=\sum_{e^{\prime}}x_{e^{\prime}}\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}j_{e^{\prime}}=\sum_{e^{\prime}}x_{e^{\prime}}j_{e^{\prime}}=\mathcal{J}.

(11)

Here, in the second-last step, we used

\displaystyle\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}j_{e^{\prime}}=j_{e^{\prime}}\,.

(12)

which is the special case of the summation response relations introduced in Ref.
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b
: using
\partial_{B_{e}}j_{e}=j_{e}
with

1
1

1

Eqs. (11) and (13) of
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b

we arrive at
Eq.

12

\bm{1}\nabla\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}
into
Eq.

10
we obtain the standard TUR (
1

Eq.

10
is a weaker version of
Eq.

7
, R-TUR implies TUR, while TUR does not necessarily imply R-TUR. Consequently, in the generic case, R-TUR cannot be derived from TUR.

Quantitative comparison with TUR.—
We now show that for most Markov networks

10
is indeed a weaker condition than
Eq.

7

\displaystyle\ell\equiv\max_{\bm{x}}\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}=m(\bm{1},\bm{x}^{*})\geq\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}\,,

(13)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{\langle\langle\mathcal{J}\rangle\rangle}\leq\ell\leq\mu\leq\frac{\dot{\sigma}}{2}\,,

with the equality

(i.e., when the optimal

corresponds to homogeneous perturbation). This implies that the optimized R-TUR (

Example: dynamical channel blockade.—
To provide intuition why R-TUR can be tighter than the standard TUR, let us consider a minimal model where this occurs.
It corresponds to the dynamical channel blockade model, which has been first considered in the context of electronic transport
Bułka 2000
;
Belzig 2005
, but is also similar to certain enzymatic reaction schemes

.
The setup consists of two Coulomb-coupled quantum dots
A
and
B
attached to two electrodes
L
and
R
(Fig.
3

The system is subjected to a symmetric voltage

, with the chemical potentials of the electrodes parameterized as
\mu_{L}=-\mu_{R}=V/2
and the dot energies set to

. Its dynamics is described by a Markov network (Fig.

, corresponding the absence of electrons in the dots (
|0\rangle
) and a single electron occupying the dots

or

(the double occupancy of the system is prohibited by the Coulomb coupling between dots).
The edges of the Markov network

correspond to electron jumps between the electrodes and the dots (Fig.
3

The transition rates read as
W_{\pm e}=\Gamma_{e}/\{1+\exp[\mp V/(2k_{B}T)]\}
, where

are the tunneling rates, and their parameterization (
2
) can be obtained by using

.

Proof for local responses.—
We have proven that in general R-TUR cannot be derived from TUR. However, this is possible for some specific cases. The first example is the response of a single edge current

Proof for unicyclic networks.—

Proof for the linear response regime.—
Finally, R-TUR (
3
) can be proved to hold close to equilibrium, where the currents are linear in the applied thermodynamic forces. To define this regime, let us parameterize the asymmetric transition rate parameters in
Eq.

2
as
S_{e}=F_{e}+E_{s(e)}-E_{t(e)}

Owen et al. 2020
;
Rao and Esposito 2018
, where
s(e)
and
t(e)
are the source and the tip of the edge
e
,
E_{i}
are node parameters (which may sometimes be interpreted as state energies), and
F_{e}
are the nonconservative thermodynamic forces. In the absence of forces, the system relaxes to the equilibrium state
\pi_{i}^{\text{eq}}=e^{-E_{i}}/Z
, with
Z=\sum_{i}e^{-E_{i}}
, which is independent of
B_{e}
. Close to equilibrium (i.e., for small values of nonconservative forces) the currents respond linearly to applied forces as
\bm{j}=\mathbb{L}\bm{F}
, where
\bm{F}=(\ldots,F_{e},\ldots)^{T}
and
\mathbb{L}
is the positive semidefinite Onsager matrix. Consequently, the current observables and the entropy production can be calculated as
\mathcal{J}=\bm{x}^{T}\mathbb{L}\bm{F}
and
\dot{\sigma}=\bm{F}^{T}\mathbb{L}\bm{F}
. Furthermore, in this regime, the covariance matrix of edge currents is related to Onsager matrix as
\mathbb{C}=2\mathbb{L}
by virtue of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Vroylandt et al. 2019
. Let us also define the matrix
\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\equiv d_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{L}/b_{\text{max}}=\sum_{e}\beta_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}
, which describes the kinetic response of Onsager matrix. Inserting the above expressions for
\mathbb{C}
and
\dot{\sigma}
into
Eq.

7
, using
\bm{\beta}\nabla\mathcal{J}=\sum_{e}\beta_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathcal{J}=\bm{x}^{T}\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\bm{F}
, and applying optimization (
9
), we find that R-TUR (
3
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\forall_{\bm{F}}:\quad\bm{F}^{T}\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{L}^{-1}\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\bm{F}\leq\bm{F}^{T}\mathbb{L}\bm{F}\,.

(18)

We recall that
\mathbb{L}^{-1}
here denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\mathbb{L}
. This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}-\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{L}^{-1}\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\succcurlyeq 0\,,

(19)

where
\mathbb{A}\succcurlyeq 0
means that the matrix
\mathbb{A}
is positive semidefinite. To prove the above inequality, we note that
\mathbb{L}-\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{L}^{-1}\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}
is the Schur complement
\mathbb{M}/\mathbb{L}
of the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{L}&\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\\
\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}&\mathbb{L}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(20)

Thus,
Eq.

19
holds if
\mathbb{M}\succcurlyeq 0
. Since eigenvalues of
\mathbb{M}
correspond to that of
\mathbb{L}\pm\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}

Nath and Paul 2014
,
\mathbb{M}\succcurlyeq 0
if and only if
\mathbb{L}\pm\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\succcurlyeq 0
. To prove the latter occurrence, in Appendix
B
we show that the responses
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}
are positive semidefinite and obey the summation response relation

\displaystyle\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}\,.

(21)

As a consequence,
\mathbb{L}\pm\mathbb{L}_{\bm{\beta}}=\sum_{e}(1\pm\beta_{e})d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}
is positive semidefinite because
1\pm\beta_{e}\in[0,2]\geq 0
and
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
. This proves
Eq.

19
, and thus R-TUR (
3
).

We also note that
Eq.

19
is saturated for homogeneous perturbation
\bm{\beta}=\bm{1}
. Thus, in the linear response regime, the optimized R-TUR (
8
) is equivalent to the optimized TUR (
13
). (We note that, in contrast to the unicyclic case, this does not imply that homogeneous perturbation will always yield the optimal response precision when considering a generic rather than optimized current observable.) Using
Eq.

9
, both are optimized for the current observable corresponding to the entropy production:
\mathcal{J}=\dot{\sigma}
. Indeed, in the linear response regime,
\langle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle\rangle=2\dot{\sigma}
, and thus TUR (
1
) becomes saturated:
\dot{\sigma}^{2}/\langle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle\rangle=\dot{\sigma}/2

Pietzonka et al. 2016
. Furthermore, using
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
,
Eq.

21
and
\dot{\sigma}=\bm{F}^{T}\mathbb{L}\bm{F}
, we find that close to equilibrium the entropy production response itself, and not only its precision, is bounded by dissipation as

\displaystyle 0\leq d_{B_{e}}\dot{\sigma}\leq\dot{\sigma}\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}\dot{\sigma}=\dot{\sigma}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle|d_{\varepsilon}\dot{\sigma}|\leq b_{\text{max}}\dot{\sigma}\,.

(23)

Finally, we note that the Onsager matrix can be represented in the basis of measurable currents (e.g., charge, heat, or chemical currents) conjugated to fundamental thermodynamic forces (e.g., temperature or chemical potential gradients)
Forastiere et al. 2022
. Such physical Onsager matrix can be expressed as
\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbb{X}\mathbb{L}\mathbb{X}^{T}
, where
\mathbb{X}
is the matrix that connects measurable currents to edge currents
Vroylandt et al. 2019
. Using
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
and
Eq.

21
, its kinetic response can also be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\succcurlyeq d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}=\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle b_{\text{max}}\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\succcurlyeq d_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\succcurlyeq-b_{\text{max}}\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{X}}\,.

(25)

This enables one to experimentally bound
b_{\text{max}}
using only measurable currents.

Final remarks.—
Our bound establishes a fundamental thermodynamic cost for controlling currents by modulating the system’s kinetics.
It may prove useful in optimizing the energetic cost needed to accurately control nanodevices such as molecular machines or nanoelectronic devices.
It may also be used for thermodynamic
Seifert 2019
or model
Moffitt et al. 2010
inference. In particular, when all parameters
b_{e}
are known (e.g., when the control parameter
\varepsilon
affects only some known transition rates), the empirical current responses and fluctuations may provide a better estimate of the entropy production than the standard TUR. On the other hand, when the model details are unknown but the entropy production is accessible,
Eq.

3
can be used to bound the transition rate responses
b_{e}
.

Appendix A
Numerical Simulations

To calculate the response
\nabla\mathcal{J}
we use the results of Ref.
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b
as follows

\displaystyle\nabla^{\intercal}\mathcal{J}=\Big[\sum_{e}x_{e}d_{B_{e^{\prime}}}j_{e}\Big]_{\{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\}}=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\mathbb{P}\mathbb{J}\,,

(26)

where we use notation
[f_{e}]_{\{e\in\mathcal{E}\}}=(\dots,f_{e},\dots)^{\intercal}
for the vector
\bm{f}
and where
the matrix
\mathbb{P}
is the projection matrix with analytical expression
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b
,
\mathbb{J}=[\partial_{B_{e^{\prime}}}j_{e}]_{\{e\in\mathcal{E},e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\}}=\text{diag}(\dots,j_{e},\dots)
the diagonal Jacobian matrix.

We find
\mathbb{P}
from Eq. (8) of
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024b
(which demands the knowledge of rates
W_{e}
and
\bm{\pi}
) and find
\mathbb{J}
from
j_{e}=W_{+e}\pi_{s(+e)}-W_{-e}\pi_{s(-e)}
.
Thus, to simulate
Eq.

26
, we only need the model for the rates
W_{\pm e}
and the numerical values of the steady state
\bm{\pi}
satisfying
Eq.

5
.

To find the covariance matrix of the edge currents
\mathbb{C}
, we follow the method from Ref.
Wachtel et al. 2015
and define the matrix
\mathbb{W}^{\phi}(\bm{q})
with nondiagonal elements
W^{\phi}_{t(\pm e)s(\pm e)}(\bm{q})=W_{t(\pm e)s(\pm e)}\exp(\pm q_{e})
and diagonal elements the same as
\mathbb{W}
. The elements of the covariance matrix are defined as

\displaystyle C_{ee^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{e}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{e^{\prime}}}\lambda(\bm{q})\Big|_{\bm{q}=\bm{0}}\,,

(27)

where
\lambda
is eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathbb{W}^{\phi}
with the largest real part. In simulations of
Eq.

27
, we used the method of finite differences to numerically calculate
\lambda(\bm{q})
.

Appendix B
Proof of
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
and Eq. (
21
)

To prove
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
we use the expression for Onsager matrix from Ref.
Vroylandt et al. 2019
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{K}\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{T}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{K}^{T}\mathbb{T}^{-1}\mathbb{K}\,.

(28)

Here
\mathbb{K}
is the cycle matrix that depends only on the system topology and not on transition rates, while
\mathbb{T}=\text{diag}(\ldots,\tau_{e}^{\text{eq}},\ldots)
is the matrix with equilibrium traffics
\mathcal{\tau}_{e}^{\text{eq}}=W_{+e}\pi^{\text{eq}}_{s(+e)}+W_{-e}\pi^{\text{eq}}_{s(-e)}
at the diagonal. We note that Ref.
Vroylandt et al. 2019
actually considered the Onsager matrix describing current responses to edge affinities
\mathcal{F}_{e}
rather than forces
F_{e}
defined here; however, the Onsager matrix for both force definitions is the same, as it is related to the covariance matrix via the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Pietzonka et al. 2016

\langle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle\rangle=2\dot{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{C}/2
. We now use the expressions
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{H}^{-1}=-\mathbb{H}^{-1}(d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{H})\mathbb{H}^{-1}
, and the equality
d_{B_{e}}\tau_{e^{\prime}}^{\text{eq}}=\delta_{ee^{\prime}}\tau_{e}^{\text{eq}}
that holds because probabilities
\pi_{i}^{\text{eq}}
do not depend on
B_{e}
; the latter yields
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{T}^{-1}=-\mathbb{T}_{e}^{-1}
with
\mathbb{T}_{e}=\text{diag}(\ldots,0,\tau_{e}^{\text{eq}},0,\ldots)
. We get

\displaystyle d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{K}\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{e}^{-1}\mathbb{K}\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{T}=2\mathbb{L}\mathbb{T}_{e}^{-1}\mathbb{L}\,.

(29)

This is a positive semidefinite matrix, as this is a sandwich of the positive semidefinite diagonal matrix
\mathbb{T}_{e}^{-1}
between the matrix
\mathbb{L}
and its transpose
\mathbb{L}^{T}=\mathbb{L}
, which concludes the proof of
d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}\succcurlyeq 0
.

Summing
Eq.

29
over edges, we further obtain
Eq.

21
:

\displaystyle\sum_{e}d_{B_{e}}\mathbb{L}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{K}\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{T}=\mathbb{L}\,.

(30)

Here we use
\sum_{e}\mathbb{T}_{e}^{-1}=\mathbb{T}^{-1}
and the relation
\mathbb{H}^{-1}\mathbb{H}\mathbb{H}^{-1}=\mathbb{H}^{-1}
that holds for Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.
