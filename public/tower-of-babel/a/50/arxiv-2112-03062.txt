The heat kernel expansion for higher order minimal and non-minimal operators
A. O. Barvinsky, W. Wachowski

Abstract
We build a systematic calculational method for the covariant expansion of the two-point heat kernel K ^ ​ ( τ | x , x ′ ) \hat{K}(\tau|x,x^{\prime}) for generic minimal and non-minimal differential operators of any order. This is the expansion in powers of dimensional background field objects—the coefficients of the operator and the corresponding spacetime and vector bundle curvatures, suitable in renormalization and effective field theory applications. For minimal operators whose principal symbol is given by an arbitrary power of the covariant Laplacian ( − □ ) M (-\Box)^{M} , M > 1 M>1 , this result generalizes the well-known Schwinger–DeWitt (or Seeley–Gilkey) expansion to the infinite series of positive and negative fractional powers of the proper time τ 1 / M \tau^{1/M} , weighted by the generalized exponential functions of the dimensionless argument − σ ( x , x ′ ) / 2 τ 1 / M -\sigma(x,x^{\prime})/2\tau^{1/M} depending on the Synge world function σ ⁡ ( x , x ′ ) \sigma(x,x^{\prime}) . The coefficients of this series are determined by the chain of auxiliary differential operators acting on the two-point parallel transport tensor, which in their turn follow from the solution of special recursive equations. The derivation of these operators and their recursive equations are based on the covariant Fourier transform in curved spacetime. The series of negative fractional powers of τ \tau vanishes in the coincidence limit x ′ = x x^{\prime}=x , which makes the proposed method consistent with the heat kernel theory of Seeley–Gilkey and generalizes it beyond the heat kernel diagonal in the form of the asymptotic expansion in the domain σ ⁡ ( x , x ′ ) ≪ τ 1 / M \sigma(x,x^{\prime})\ll\tau^{1/M} , τ → 0 \tau\to 0 . Consistency of the method is also checked by verification of known results for the minimal second-order operators and their extension to the generic fourth-order operator. Possible improvement of the suggested Fourier transform approach to the noncommutative algebra of the covariant □ \Box operator in the method of universal functional traces is also briefly discussed.

Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to work out efficient method of calculating the heat kernel of various differential operators in curved spacetime. It is needless to say that the heat kernel is a powerful tool in mathematical physics, which underlies numerous applications in quantum field theory, theory of gauge fields and quantum gravity, theory of stochastic processes and condensed matter systems. It is especially important in the renormalization theory of quantum gravitational models where, when combined with the background field method, it allows one to express in a closed form the Green function of the theory and its one-loop effective action. Moreover, the Schwinger–DeWitt

DeWitt 1965

Starting with the pioneering application in quantum Einstein theory
’t Hooft and Veltman 1974

The basic tool of this method is the heat kernel expansion in powers of the proper time (or Schwinger parameter). The coefficients of this expansion—the so-called HaMiDeW (Hadamard–Minakshisundaram–DeWitt)
Gibbons 1979
or Seeley–Gilkey coefficients—represent the sequence of objects of growing dimensionality directly related to the operators of field and gradient expansion in effective field theory. Thus in the lowest orders corresponding to essential and marginal operators they contain the information about UV renormalization properties of the theory and also, within a needed accuracy below the cutoff, incorporate all quantum corrections.

given by the following finite sums in terms of the above operators
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)
and tensor coefficient functions
S_{p,l}(x,x^{\prime})
,

\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(-2)^{Mn-m}}\\
\times\sum\limits_{l=Mn-m}^{\lfloor L_{m,n}\rfloor}S_{Mn-m,l}(x,x^{\prime})*\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime}).

(2.23)

Here the upper bound on the summation index
l
is the integer part
\lfloor L_{m,n}\rfloor
of

\displaystyle L_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2Mn-{\rm max}\left\{\,2m,\frac{n}{2}\,\right\}.

(2.24)

This is again effectively the expansion in background dimensionality, because as it follows from (
2.12
) the dimensionalities of
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})
are always positive and grow with
m
and
n
,

\dim\,\hat{b}_{m,n}\geq\min_{\{\,l\,\}\vphantom{L^{L}}}\big(\dim\,\hat{T}_{n,l}\big)=\max\left\{\,2m,\frac{n}{2}\,\right\}.

(2.25)

Below we go over to the derivation of the above results along with the formulation of recurrent equations for the operators
\hat{T}_{k,l}^{a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{l}}(x,x^{\prime}|\nabla)
. This will be followed by verification of the consistency of the obtained formalism with known properties of the Schwinger–DeWitt technique.

3
General non-minimal operators

We define the heat kernel
\hat{K}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})
of
\hat{F}
as the kernel of the operator
e^{-\tau\hat{F}}
, i.e. as the result of acting by this operator on the two-point delta function
\hat{\delta}(x,x^{\prime})=\delta^{A}_{B^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
in the space of fields
\varphi=\varphi^{A}(x)
,

\hat{K}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})=e^{-\tau\hat{F}}\,\hat{\delta}(x,x^{\prime})

(3.1)

The delta function is itself defined by the convolution with
\varphi(x)

\varphi(x)=\int d^{d}x^{\prime}\,\hat{\delta}(x,x^{\prime})\,\varphi(x^{\prime})=\int d^{d}x^{\prime}\,\hat{\delta}^{A}_{B^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\,\varphi^{B^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}),

(3.2)

from which it follows that
\hat{\delta}(x,x^{\prime})
is the density of unit weight in
x^{\prime}
and has zero weight in the first argument.
6
6

6

In view of the point-like support of the delta-function, it can be written down as
\hat{1}\,\delta(x,x^{\prime})=\delta^{A}_{B^{\prime}}\delta(x,x^{\prime})
, but the distinction between the indices
A
and
B^{\prime}
associated respectively with the first and the second spacetime points should be clearly kept, especially when the delta function is acted upon by the covariant derivatives in the vector bundle of
\varphi^{A}(x)
.

3.1
The generalized Fourier transform

The covariant Fourier transform method requires to consider the “plane-wave” functions with the momentum vector
k_{b^{\prime}}
,

\exp\left(ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\right),

(3.3)

which are based on the coordinate vector
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
—the analogue of the vector
x^{\prime}-x
in flat spacetime. This is the vector tangential at the point
x^{\prime}
to the geodetic curve interpolating between the points
x^{\prime}
and
x
and satisfying the equation and initial conditions (
2.9
).
7
7

7

We remind that at this point we do not need a metric
g_{ab}
on the spacetime manifold, which is not at all encoded in the generic differential operators of the form (
2.1
). The metric structure and associated Synge world function
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
appears later for minimal differential operators, in which case the vector
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
expresses via
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
as
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})=\nabla^{b^{\prime}}\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
.
Note that with this definition the plane waves (
3.3
) are scalars at the point
x
—bear in mind that
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
is here the scalar as a function of
x
and the vector cotangent to spacetime at the second point
x^{\prime}
.

The generalized Fourier transform in curved spacetime is based on the covariant integral representation for the delta-function. For the delta-function acting on a scalar it follows from an obvious relation

\delta(x,x^{\prime})=\delta\big(\sigma^{a^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\big),

(3.4)

justified by the fact that
\big[\det\big(-\sigma^{a^{\prime}}_{b}\big)\big]=1
, and the subsequent representation of
\delta\big(\sigma^{a^{\prime}}\big)
in the form of the Fourier integral over the momentum space of vectors
k_{a^{\prime}}
cotangent to the spacetime at the point
x^{\prime}
.

The generalization of this representation to generic vector bundle fields by a simple multiplication with
\hat{1}
will not be consistent for the following reason. In what follows we will have to work out a covariant formalism for the integrand of the corresponding momentum space integral, but the multiplication of this integrand by
\hat{1}
will not convert this object to a bi-tensor quantity with correct transformation law of its both arguments
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Thus, the spacetime covariant definition of the delta-function acting in the generic vector bundle of spin-tensor fields should read

\hat{\delta}(x,x^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\exp\left(ik_{a^{\prime}}\sigma^{a^{\prime}}\right)\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime}),

(3.5)

where
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}^{A}_{B^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
is some two-point spin-tensor quantity having correct transformation properties with respect to the multindex
A
associated with
x
and the index
B^{\prime}
associated with
x^{\prime}
. The only restriction on the choice of
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime})
is the requirement that it should reduce to the unit matrix at
x^{\prime}=x
. Otherwise it can be rather arbitrary, and can generate different representations of one and the same quantity.

The simplest choice of
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime})
, one can figure out, is apparently the parallel transport tensor satisfying the equations (
2.8
)
which provide the parallel translation of spin-tensor objects along the geodetic connecting the points
x
and
x^{\prime}
. This operator in fact coincides with the zeroth HaMiDeW-coefficient in the Schwinger-DeWitt expansion (
1.2
),
\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime})=\hat{a}_{0}(x,x^{\prime})
and in mathematics literature is usually associated with Widom calculus.

Note that within the definition (
3.5
) the exponential factor in the integrand of the Fourier integral is the scalar of zero weight with respect to the diffeomorphisms of
x
, and
after
the
k_{a^{\prime}}
-integration generates the scalar density of weight one at
x^{\prime}
, which is in full accordance with the transformation properties of the right hand side of (
3.4
).
8
8

8

Point-like support of the delta-function fixes only its overall weight with respect to diffeomorphisms of both arguments (which is one), but the way this weight can be splitted between
x
and
x^{\prime}
in the integrand of the Fourier integral representation depends on the choice of this representation—with another choice, say
\delta\big(\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})\big)
, the delta-function would be a zero-weight scalar with respect to
x^{\prime}
, but weight one with respect to
x
.
In contrast to this,
prior
to this integration the exponential function in question does not have good transformation properties as a function of
x^{\prime}
because of the contraction of
\sigma^{a^{\prime}}
with the diffeomorphism inert vector
k_{a^{\prime}}
, but this drawback of the formalism can be disregarded, because we will develop a fully covariant formalism (for the Fourier image of the heat kernel) entirely at the point
x
.

Now we act by the operator
e^{-\tau\hat{F}(\nabla)}
upon (
3.5
) and commute the exponential plane-wave function with
\hat{F}(\nabla)
according to the following relation

\exp\big(-ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}\big)\hat{F}(\nabla_{a})\exp\big(ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}\big)=\hat{F}\big(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}\big),

(3.6)

where
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}
is the bi-tensor (
2.10
). Therefore, the heat kernel takes the form

\displaystyle\hat{K}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\exp(ik_{a^{\prime}}\sigma^{a^{\prime}})\,\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime}),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\tau\,\hat{F}\big(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}\big)\right)\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime}),

(3.8)

where its Fourier image
\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})
, which we will denote by bold letters, obviously satisfies the following initial Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\tau}+\hat{F}\big(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}\big)\right)\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})=0,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{K}}(0,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})=\hat{\mathcal{I}}(x,x^{\prime}).

(3.10)

Why would we need the solution of this problem if we already have it in a closed form (
3.8
) and can explicitly expand it in powers of the exponential? Point is that this expansion will not be a needed expansion in background dimensionality
1/l
, because both (
2.1
) and (
3.6
) have terms of zeroth order in
1/l
—see the leading term of (
2.1
) in derivatives with
{\rm dim}\big(\hat{F}_{N}^{a_{1}\ldots a_{N}}(x))=0
. Thus, first we have to disentangle from
\hat{F}(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}\big)
this
O(1/l^{0})
term, explicitly find the zeroth order solution and then develop the perturbation theory in
1/l
.

3.2
The solution for
\hat{\bm{K}}\left(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime}\right)

To do this, we first expand the operator
\hat{F}(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a})
in powers of momenta
\bm{k}
,

\hat{F}\left(\nabla_{a}+ik_{b^{\prime}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}\right)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N}(i\bm{k})^{m}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m},

(3.11)

where we introduced the notations for monomials in momenta
\bm{k}^{m}=k_{b^{\prime}_{1}}\ldots k_{b^{\prime}_{m}}
and their coefficient differential operators
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}=\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}^{b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{m}}(\nabla)
and also use the same abbreviation of indices contraction as in (
2.2
). The rule of constructing the operators
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}
actually follows from the definition (
3.11
),

\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}=\sum\limits_{k=m}^{N}\hat{F}_{k}(x)*\llbracket\nabla^{k}\rrbracket_{m},

(3.12)

where the action of the operation
\llbracket\ldots\rrbracket_{m}
on every monomial of derivatives
\nabla^{k}=\nabla_{a_{1}}\ldots\nabla_{a_{k}}
consists in replacing
m
of them by the functions of the form
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}
. This gives the sum of
\binom{k}{m}
terms each having extra
m
contravariant primed indices
b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{m}
. The original order of derivatives and inserted factors
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}
should be strictly retained, and the additional obvious property holds:
\llbracket\nabla^{k}\rrbracket_{m}=0
if
m>k
.

Thus, the operation
\llbracket\ldots\rrbracket_{m}
reduces the order of every differential operator by
m
and adds to it extra
m
primed indices associated with the point
x^{\prime}
. In particular, for our
N
-th order operator (
2.2
) its
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}
is not a differential operator, but just a two-point function which is a scalar at
x
and a tensor at
x^{\prime}
with
N
contravariant indices

\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\equiv\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}^{b^{\prime}_{1}...b^{\prime}_{N}}=\hat{F}_{N}^{a_{1}\ldots a_{N}}\sigma^{b^{\prime}_{1}}_{a_{1}}\ldots\sigma^{b^{\prime}_{N}}_{a_{N}}.

(3.13)

When contracted with
(i\bm{k})^{N}
it will play the role of the principal symbol of
\hat{F}(\nabla)
usually used in the Fourier analysis of differential and pseudo-differential operators.

The dimensionality of these operators is equal to their order

\dim\,\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}=N-m.

(3.14)

Therefore, to develop an efficient expansion in powers of the background dimensionality it would be sufficient to expand
\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})
in powers of all
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}
with
m\leq N-1
while treating the leading term
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}
exactly. This is of course possible, because
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}
is not a differential operator but just a matrix, and the lowest order solution of (
3.9
) is given by the matrix-valued exponential function

\hat{\bm{K}}_{0}(\tau,\bm{k})=\exp\left(-\tau(i\bm{k})^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\right)\hat{\mathcal{I}}.

(3.15)

Therefore, we will look for the solution of Eqs.(
3.9
)–(
3.10
) in the form

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{K}}(\tau,\bm{k})=\exp\Big(-\tau(i\bm{k})^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\Big)\,\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla)\,\hat{\mathcal{I}},

(3.16)

where
\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla)\equiv\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})
is some unknown operator to be found.

Substituting the ansatz (
3.16
) into the equation (
3.9
) we see that the commutation of operators
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}
with the exponential function
\exp\big(-\tau(i\bm{k})^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\big)
leads again to elongation of derivatives

\nabla_{a}\to\tilde{\nabla}_{a}=\nabla_{a}-\tau(i\bm{k})^{N}*\hat{D}_{a}(\tau,\bm{k}),

(3.17)

where
\hat{D}_{a}(\tau,\bm{k})
is given in quadratures as the following generically nontrivial matrix-valued integral

\displaystyle\bm{k}^{N}*\hat{D}_{a}(\tau,\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\,e^{-\tau^{\prime}(i\bm{k})^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}}

\displaystyle\times\big(\bm{k}^{N}*\nabla_{a}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\big)\,e^{\tau^{\prime}(i\bm{k})^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}},

(3.18)

which simplifies to
\nabla_{a}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}
for a particular case of the vanishing commutator
\big[\bm{k}^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N},\bm{k}^{N}*\nabla_{a}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\big]=0
.
The resulting operators can in their turn be expanded as,

\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}(\tilde{\nabla})=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-m}\left(\tau(i\bm{k})^{N}\right)^{n}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}(\tau,\bm{k}),

(3.19)

to give a new set of operators of the order
N-m-n
with
m+Nn
contravariant indices
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}^{b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{m}c^{\prime}_{1}\ldots c^{\prime}_{Nn}}
,

\displaystyle\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{k=m+n}^{N}\hat{F}_{k}(x)*\llbracket\nabla^{k}\rrbracket_{m,n},,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\dim\,\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}=N-m,

(3.21)

where
\llbracket\nabla^{k}\rrbracket_{m,n}
are built by the same pattern as

\llbracket\nabla^{k}\rrbracket_{m}
—they consist of
\binom{k}{m,n}
terms, in each of which
m
covariant derivatives
\nabla_{a}
are replaced by the functions of the form
\sigma^{b^{\prime}}_{a}
, and
n
covariant derivatives
\nabla_{a}
are replaced by the functions of the form
-\hat{D}_{a}(\tau,\bm{k})
. It is easy to see that
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,0}=\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}
and
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,N-m}
are not operators but just tensor-valued functions of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

After making the required commutation, one finds

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\tau}+\hat{\bm{F}}\right)\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k})=0,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla,0,\bm{k})=\hat{1},

(3.23)

where the new operator
\hat{\bm{F}}(\nabla)\equiv\hat{\bm{F}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k}|x,x^{\prime})
reads

\hat{\bm{F}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k})=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-m}\tau^{n}(i\bm{k})^{m+Nn}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}(\tau,\bm{k})

(3.24)

and has a positive dimensionality
\dim\,\hat{\bm{F}}(\nabla)\geq 1
. Now, however, this “Hamiltonian” operator is time dependent and admits a perturbative solution in the form of a
T
-ordered exponent

\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k})=T\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,\hat{\bm{F}}(\nabla,t,\bm{k})\right)\\
=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}dt_{1}\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots\!\!\int\limits_{0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}\,\hat{\bm{F}}(t_{1})\cdots\hat{\bm{F}}(t_{n}),

(3.25)

each term of which has at least of
n
-th order in background dimensionality.

In principle, every order of this expansion is explicitly calculable in terms of the powers of operators
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}
, introduced above, and the powers of
\tau
and
\bm{k}
. However, since the operator (
3.24
) contains at least
N(N+1)/2
terms, the number of terms in the
n
-th order of this expansion will contain
[N(N+1)/2]^{n}
monomials in powers of
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}
, not to mention that every
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}
is a sufficiently long expression growing with the operator order
N
.

Therefore, we will consider a simpler case of the vanishing commutator
\big[\bm{k}^{N}*\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N},\bm{k}^{N}*\nabla_{a}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}\big]=0
, when
\hat{D}_{a}=\nabla_{a}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N}
and
\llbracket F\rrbracket_{m,n}
are both
(\tau,\bm{k})
-independent and, instead of explicitly handling every order of the expansion (
3.25
), look for
\hat{\bm{T}}(\tau,\bm{k})
in the form of double series in powers of
\tau
and
\bm{k}
. Then we try finding solvable recurrent relations for its coefficients. This double series reads

\hat{\bm{T}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k})=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\!\sum\limits_{\;\;\;0\leq l\leq L_{n}}\!\tau^{n}(i\bm{k})^{l}*\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla),

(3.26)

where
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)=\hat{T}_{n,l}^{b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{l}}(\nabla|x,x^{\prime})
are the unknown operator valued coefficients which are
l
-th rank tensors at
x^{\prime}
and
L_{n}
is some non-decreasing sequence of finite numbers.

The differentiation
\partial_{\tau}
in (
3.22
) decreases the power in the proper time
\tau
by one. And the terms in the decomposition (
3.24
) of the operator
\hat{\bm{F}}
, on the contrary, increase the power in
\tau
by
n
and at the same time the power in the momentum
i\bm{k}
by
m+Nn
. Therefore, if we substitute (
3.24
) and (
3.26
) into the equation (
3.22
), and then equate to zero the terms with each power
\tau^{n}(i\bm{k})^{l}
, we obtain the following system of recurrence relations for the operators
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)

\displaystyle(n+1)\hat{T}_{n+1,l}=-\sum\limits_{p=0}^{N-1}\sum\limits_{q=0}^{N-p}\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{p,q}\,\hat{T}_{n-q,l-p-Nq},

(3.27)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{0,0}=\hat{1},\qquad\hat{T}_{0,l}=0,\quad l>0.

(3.28)

This notation implies that
\hat{T}_{k,l}=0
for
k<0
or
l<0
and, as it should be, the composition of
(p+Nq)
-rank tensor
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{p,q}
with the
(l-p-Nq)
-rank tensor
\hat{T}_{n-q,l-p-Nq}
on the right hand side of this equation gives the
l
-rank tensor on the left. Initial values (
3.28
) follow from the initial condition (
3.23
) and thus allow one to solve these recurrent relations for all
\hat{T}_{n,l}
. An example of the calculation of these operators from those of the lower orders is depicted in Fig.
1
for the case of
\hat{T}_{6,5}(\nabla)
and
N=2
.

Note that the ratio of the increment of the index

to the increment of the index

in the transition from the right hand side of (

) to its left hand side is maximal at

and
p=N-1

\frac{\Delta l}{\Delta n}=\frac{p+Nq}{q+1},\qquad\max_{p,q}\frac{\Delta l}{\Delta n}=N-\frac{1}{2}.

(3.29)

This means that the recursive calculation of
\hat{T}_{n,l}
, starting from a given value of
\hat{T}_{0,0}
, for a given

involves only the values of

below the bound

given by Eq.(

). This specifies the finite upper limit on the summation index

that we introduced in (

).

Note that with the help of the relation

\llbracket\hat{A}\hat{B}\rrbracket_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\llbracket\hat{A}\rrbracket_{k}\llbracket\hat{B}\rrbracket_{n-k},

(3.30)

which is valid for any two differential operators
\hat{A}(\nabla)
and
\hat{B}(\nabla)
, one can resolve the chain of recurrence relations (

) for some of the coefficients
\hat{T}_{k,l}
, obtaining the following closed expression

\hat{T}_{k,l}=\frac{1}{k!}\left\llbracket(-\hat{F})^{k}\right\rrbracket_{l},\quad l<N.

(3.31)

In addition, the edge coefficients
\hat{T}_{2k,(2N-1)k}
are also given by the explicit formula

\hat{T}_{2k,(2N-1)k}=\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}\left(-\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{N-1,1}\right)^{k}.

(3.32)

However, an arbitrary coefficient
\hat{T}_{k,l}
cannot be obtained by such simple formulas. We can only say that it can be represented as a sum of terms (with some coefficients) of the form

\llbracket\hat{F}^{k_{1}}\rrbracket_{m_{1},n_{1}}\ldots\llbracket\hat{F}^{k_{p}}\rrbracket_{m_{p},n_{p}},

(3.33)

where

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}(k_{i}+n_{i})=k,\qquad\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}(Nn_{i}+m_{i})=l,

(3.34)

which is consistent with the T-exponent expansion (
3.25
).

3.3
Momentum space integration

After we have obtained the expansion for
\hat{\bm{K}}(\nabla,\tau,\bm{k})
, it remains to integrate it over the momentum
\bm{k}
to get the required expansion. Substituting the expressions (
3.16
) and (
3.26

(3.35)

First of all, note that the replacement of the integration momentum

of spacetime to the the momentum

leads to the expression (

with

\hat{F}(\nabla)
. In the transition to (

Causal differential operators

For the class of

Calculations of this and the previous sections show that their complexity is much higher than in the recursive procedure of the Schwinger-DeWitt technique. Without using computer symbolic manipulations, that were performed by means of

Interesting point is that there exist two versions of the generalized Fourier transform. One of them is covariant and corresponds to the delta-function representation (
3.5
), whereas the non-covariant one proceeds with
k_{a^{\prime}}\sigma^{a^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},x)
replaced with
k_{a}\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})
. This means that the auxiliary momentum space tangent to spacetime manifold is introduced at the point
x
rather than at
x^{\prime}
. The non-covariant version has the calculational advantage of having in the game only one unprimed type of indices, but it violates the basic commutation equation (
3.6
) by extra non-covariant terms proportional to the covariant derivatives of the diffeomorphisms inert momentum vector,
(\nabla_{b}k_{a})\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})=-k_{c}\varGamma_{ba}^{c}\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})
. Paradoxically, naive omission of these terms has led in the computer simulation of the above type to exactly the same coincidence limits, and this happens despite the fact that
\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})
in the expression above gets numerously differentiated and thus is nonzero in this limit. Thus far we do not have explanation for this digitally generated observation, which however might be useful in symbolic computations.

7
Powers of a minimal operator

In this section we will study the direct and inverse Mellin transform in heat kernel theory which allows one to relate the curvature expansions for the minimal operator
\hat{F}(\nabla)
and its integer or non-integer power
\hat{F}^{s}(\nabla)

\hat{b}_{m,n}

The Green function of the operator

\hat{F}(\nabla)

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{F^{s}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\hat{1}}{F^{s}(\nabla)}\delta(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{s-1}\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x^{\prime}).

(7.1)

The inverse Mellin transform allows one to recover the heat kernel of the operator
\hat{F}(\nabla)
from
\hat{G}_{F^{s}}(x,x^{\prime})

\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{w-i\infty}^{w+i\infty}ds\,\tau^{-s}\,\Gamma(s)\,\hat{G}_{F^{s}}(x,x^{\prime})\,,

(7.2)

where the integration runs along the contour parallel to imaginary axis in the complex plane of
s
for sufficiently big positive
w
. We do not discuss the range of
s
where both transforms are well defined and only assume positive-definiteness of
\hat{F}
and the possibility to make needed analytic continuation. Brief derivation of these transforms is given in Appendix
C
.

The idea of relating heat kernel curvature expansions for
\hat{F}^{s}(\nabla)
and
\hat{F}(\nabla)

\textstyle{\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\hat{K}_{F^{s}}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})}

\scriptstyle{\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{t\tau}}

Integer powers of a Laplace type operator

Thus far we considered generic fractional and even complex powers

8
Discussion and conclusions

We have suggested a systematic calculational method for the covariant expansion of the two-point heat kernel for generic minimal and non-minimal differential operators of any order. This expansion for off-diagonal heat kernel elements is not an expansion in positive powers of the proper time, like it happens for the minimal second order operators within a conventional Schwinger–DeWitt technique. Rather this is an expansion in background dimensionality of relevant background field objects describing the dimensionful coefficients of the operator and the corresponding spacetime and vector bundle curvatures. Any order of this expansion in powers of these curvatures and their covariant derivatives can be reached in a finite number of calculational steps, which is what one usually needs in local gradient expansion in the energy domain below the effective field theory cutoff. For the coincidence limit of the heat kernel and its arbitrarily high covariant derivatives this expansion becomes the series in positive fractional powers of the proper time. This makes our results fully consistent with the structure of asymptotic expansion of the functional trace of the heat kernel well known from mathematics literature on differential and pseudo-differential operators on curved manifolds
Seeley 1967
;
Gilkey 1975
;
Gilkey 1979
.

Main results of the paper for generic non-minimal operators of order
N
are given by Eqs.(
2.4
)-(
2.6
), where the set of auxiliary differential operators
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)
is defined by the recurrence relations (
3.27
). The efficiency of this result depends on the users skill of calculating the momentum space integral (
2.6
) for a given principal symbol of the operator (
2.7
). For general minimal operators, whose principal term represents
M
-th power of Laplacian, this integral can be universally calculated in terms of generalized exponential functions (
2.18
) and symmetric tensors (
4.2
). This leads to the expansion (
2.21
)-(
2.22
) with the generalized HaMiDeW coefficients
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})
defined by Eqs.(
2.23
)-(
2.24
).

There are two basic differences of this expansion from the
M=1
case of Schwinger-DeWitt technique. Firstly, one overall exponential function of the argument
-\sigma(x,x^{\prime})/2\tau^{1/M}
gets replaced by various generalized exponential functions of the same argument, multiplying individual fractional powers of the proper time. Secondly, fractional powers of the proper time series extend to minus infinity. In the coincidence limit
x^{\prime}=x
, however, the coefficients of the negative powers vanish in full accordance with the known properties of the heat kernel trace
Seeley 1967
;
Gilkey 1975
;
Gilkey 1979
.

Note that the main recursive ingredient of the heat kernel technique is the solution of the chain of relations (
3.27
). Even though the anticipation of the “bottomless” chain of HaMiDeW coefficients for higher-derivatives operators, expressed in
Carinhas and Fulling 1990
, is correct, the recursive procedure for them turns out to be possible. The structure of these recurrent equations is more complicated as compared to the Schwinger-DeWitt expansion, but they can be successively solved. Their formulation crucially depends on the decomposition (
3.11
) of the original operator into the auxiliary operators
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m}
and
\llbracket\hat{F}\rrbracket_{m,n}
introduced in Sect.
3
.

Complexity of the minimal higher derivative operators manifests itself in the double series nature of the expansion (
2.21
). As shown in Sect.
7
, for minimal second order operators double series reduces with the aid of Eqs.(
5.19
) and (
5.4
) to the original expansion with the DeWitt coefficients
\hat{a}_{m}(x,x^{\prime})
composed from their generalized double indexed version
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})
. In fact, Eq.(
5.19
) constitutes an alternative representation of the off-diagonal Schwinger-DeWitt coefficients
\hat{a}_{m}(x,x^{\prime})
. This representation in terms of the infinite series (
5.4
) of the
\hat{b}_{m,n}
coefficients is not a priori equivalent to the covariant Taylor expansion of
\hat{a}_{m}(x,x^{\prime})
. Thus, it might perhaps open the prospects of their explicit (not recursive) calculation and as a byproduct partial resummation of Schwinger-DeWitt series, discussed from a somewhat other viewpoint in
Ivanov 2019
;
Ivanov and Kharuk 2020
. This resummation might be facilitated by the fact that certain subsets of the operators
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)
, participating in the construction of
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})
coefficients, can be obtained beyond recursive procedure, see Eqs.(
3.31
), (
3.32
) and (
4.10
).

It should be emphasized that our results for two-point heat kernels are essentially of local nature. In particular, for a general curved spacetime the transition to the locally geodetic coordinate system of
\sigma^{a^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},x)
centered at the point
x^{\prime}
is restricted by the vicinity of this point where
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})
is nonsingular. This domain is bounded by the caustic points
x
conjugated to
x^{\prime}
with
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})=\infty
, at which the relation (
5.19
) becomes singular. For practical purposes of UV renormalization this restriction is not very important, because the coincidence limit of the heat kernel and all its derivatives is basically everything what one needs in effective field theory. In this respect, however, it is quite interesting that the generalized Fourier method does not recover in the heat kernel expansion (
2.21
) a one-half power of
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})
typical for the Schwinger-DeWitt expansion (
1.2
). Instead it gives the inverse of
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})
. Thus, naively this would mean that approaching the caustic gives a decreasing amplitude of the physical signal rather than its enhancement which is characteristic of semiclassical approximation.

The explanation of this paradox apparently consists in the observation that for higher derivative operators with
M>1
our Fourier method is critically different from the semiclassical expansion. If one calculates the momentum space integrals (
2.18
) by the saddle point method for
\tau\to 0
then one would get the expansion in powers of
\tau^{1/(2M-1)}
rather than
\tau^{1/M}
and, moreover, the terms of this expansion will be singular at
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})\to 0

Barvinsky et al. 2019
. For
M>1
the semiclassical asymptotic expansion at
\tau\to 0
is not homogeneous for
x^{\prime}\to x
but conveys a right behavior for large
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
even when approaching the caustic. This, in particular, does not allow us to use the semiclassical approximation
method of Maslov and Fedoriuk
Maslov and Fedoriuk 1981
in this limit. Conversely, our background dimensionality expansion is regular at
x\to x^{\prime}
, but fails for large distances. Interestingly, for minimal operators with
M=1
these two expansions look equivalent in the vicinity of the heat kernel diagonal. Here
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})
is close to one, and in the relation (
5.19
) only affects nontrivial match between multiple covariant derivatives of
\hat{a}_{m}(x,x^{\prime})
,
\Delta(x,x^{\prime})
and
\hat{b}_{m}(x,x^{\prime})
what was explicitly checked in Sect.
5
.

Local nature of our method can be scrutinized also from another viewpoint. Obviously, the expansion up to infinitely positive and negative powers of
\tau
cannot be regarded as asymptotic for
\tau\to 0
. But the infinite tail of negative powers of
\tau
should be regarded as an expansion in
positive
powers of the ratio
\sigma^{a^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})/\tau^{1/2M}
, so that the total result is the sum of two expansions which are both asymptotic at
\tau\to 0
in the domain
\sigma^{a^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\ll\tau^{1/2M}
. The coefficients of both expansions are local curvature invariants of growing dimensionality, which explains the efficiency of this method in effective field theory and in renormalization theory.

One might notice that on top of very high generality of the suggested expansions and their detailed analysis no new results were obtained in concrete applications of this technique. This can be explained by the fact that any step beyond second order minimal operators is practically impossible without using powerful symbolic manipulating computer programs. Even the simplest examples of Sect.
5
, focused on the comparison of the new method with well known calculations of the lowest orders of the Schwinger–DeWitt expansion, would be impossible without using a computer code. While the solution of DeWitt’s recurrent equations for
\hat{a}_{m}(x,x)
with small
m=0,1,2,3
can be easily performed manually, the same calculation by means of generalized DeWitt coefficients
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x)
turns out to be much more complicated, and it was accomplished above only by using the xAct package of Mathematica. Extension beyond the case of
M=1
minimal operators is even more complicated and, in fact, is impossible without the algorithms obtained above. This is undoubtedly true modulo exceptional cases and special tools, like the method of universal functional traces
Jack and Osborn 1984
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
, the method of squaring the low-derivative operator, dimensional reduction
Barvinsky et al. 2021
, etc. So the ultimate goal of this work is to develop on the basis of above algorithms an efficient code for calculating the heat kernel expansion and using it in high energy applications.

One of the motivations for this project is the problem of UV renormalization in Hořava gravity theory, which is computationally extremely complicated because of Lorentz invariance violation. Extension of the heat kernel method to Lorentz violating models is also possible
Nesterov and Solodukhin 2011
;
Barvinsky et al. 2017
;
Groh et al. 2011
and includes a recent application of the Fourier method
Grosvenor et al. 2021
along the lines of the Gusynin method for the operator resolvent
Gusynin 1989
;
Gusynin 1990
. In four
dimensions this problem involves non-minimal operators of the sixth order
Barvinsky et al. 2021
and requires the whole set of special methods involving the above mentioned universal functional traces
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
, 3-dimensional reduction on a static background, square root extraction for the sixth-order operator having hundreds of terms, etc. At present these are the only available tools of the background field method needed to avoid calculation of humongous number of Feynman graphs, that was, for example, undertaken in the proof of asymptotic freedom in (2+1)-dimensional Hořava gravity
Barvinsky et al. 2017
. However, these auxiliary tools are not always possible and often lengthen the needed computations like it happens with the method of
Barvinsky et al. 2021
, which relies on Sylvester procedure for extracting the square root of the sixth-order operator in covariant derivatives. The presented technique can and should circumvent these difficulties, and the comparison of its efficiency with the approach of
Barvinsky et al. 2021
is one of our main future goals.

We accomplish the paper with a couple of brief remarks on possible future refinement of the suggested formalism. The failure to perform a complete resummation of negative powers of the proper time suggests that such a resummation is perhaps, in principle, impossible. This might be explained by the fact that negative powers of
\tau
entail positive powers of the vector
\sigma^{a}(x,x^{\prime})
rather than the scalar function
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
. This conclusion seems to be corroborated by expressions like (
6.13
). Therefore the coefficients of this expansion are tensors of growing rank, and this invalidates universality of the needed resummation, leaving however the option of summing up the series of particular tensor structures.

Another remark is that our Fourier method consists of multiple stages—construction of auxiliary operators
\llbracket F\rrbracket_{m,n}
in the decompositions (
3.12
) and (
3.19
), recursive solution of equations for the operators
\hat{T}_{n,l}(\nabla)
, necessity of finding coincidence limits of multiple derivatives of
\sigma
, presence of primed and unprimed indices, etc. All this essentially slows down symbolic computations. This suggests the necessity of an alternative approach combining the advantages of the commutator technique of the universal functional traces of
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
;
Barvinsky et al. 2021
and the above Fourier method. Such approach indeed can be worked out, and it is anticipated that in the new method all generalized heat kernel coefficients
\hat{b}_{m,n}(x,x^{\prime})
could be expressed in terms of the conventional DeWitt coefficients
\hat{a}_{m}^{(-\Box)}(x,x^{\prime})
of the simplest minimal operator
-\Box
. This can be achieved via acting upon them by the set of differential operators which are formed of the products of multiple commutators of
(-\Box)^{M}
with the lower derivative part
\hat{P}(\nabla)
of the full minimal operator (
2.13
),

[(-\Box)^{M},[(-\Box)^{M},...[(-\Box)^{M},\hat{P}(\nabla)],...]],

(8.1)

This is the idea of the method of universal functional traces
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
;
Barvinsky et al. 2021
, exploiting a simple fact that every commutation with
\Box
increases the dimensionality of any object at least by one. This algorithm, which is currently under study
Barvinsky and Wachowski
, is expected to be especially suitable for the needs of effective field theory, because it clearly shows how a needed precision of curvature expansion truncates the orders of multiple commutators.

The world function and the parallel transport tensor

First of all agree on basic definitions and notation. Let us be given some

\mathcal{M}

Appendix B
The generalized exponential functions (GEF)

In the articles
Wachowski and Pronin 2019
;
Barvinsky et al. 2019
we showed that the heat kernel of a power of the Laplacian
(-\Box)^{\nu}
in a flat
d
-dimensional space with the Euclidean metric has the form

\mathbb{K}_{\nu,d}(\tau;x)=\exp\left(-\tau(-\Box)^{\nu}\right)\delta(x)\\
=\frac{1}{\big(4\pi\tau^{1/\nu}\big)^{d/2}}\,\mathcal{E}_{\nu,d/2}\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4\tau^{1/\nu}}\right),

(B.1)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\nu,\alpha}(z)
is a two-parameter family of some new special functions defined by the Taylor series

\mathcal{E}_{\nu,\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+m}{\nu}\right)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m)}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}.

(B.2)

In particular

\mathcal{E}_{\nu,\alpha}(0)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha/\nu)}{\nu\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(B.3)

The expression (
B.1
) is a direct generalization of the standard heat kernel for the Laplacian
-\Box
for
\nu=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,\alpha}(z)=\exp(z),

(B.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{1,d}(\tau;x)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4\tau}\right),

(B.5)

in which the functions
\mathcal{E}_{\nu,d/2}(z)
are in place of the usual exponential functions. So we call these new functions
the generalized exponential functions (GEF)
. We investigated their properties in detail in
Wachowski and Pronin 2019
;
Barvinsky et al. 2019
. The most important of them are the direct and inverse Mellin transform and the differentiation rule

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha,a}(s)\equiv\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,a}(-z)dz=\frac{\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(\frac{a-s}{\alpha}\right)}{\alpha\Gamma(a-s)},

(B.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,a}(-z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{C}\varepsilon_{\alpha,a}(s)z^{-s}ds,

(B.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{b}}{dz^{b}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,a}(z)=\mathcal{E}_{\nu,a+b}(z).

(B.8)

Let us also introduce new functions which we will call
the GEF of the second order

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\nu,b}^{\alpha,a}(s)=\frac{\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(\frac{a-s}{\alpha\nu}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{b-s}{\nu}\right)}{\alpha\nu\Gamma\left(\frac{a-s}{\nu}\right)\Gamma(b-s)},

(B.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\nu,b}^{\alpha,a}(-z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{C}\varepsilon_{\nu,b}^{\alpha,a}(s)\,z^{-s}\,ds.

(B.10)

Their properties are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,b}^{\alpha,a}(z)=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,a}(z),\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\nu,b}^{1,a}(z)=\mathcal{E}_{\nu,b}(z),

(B.11)

Appendix C
Complex powers

The complex power
F^{-s}
of the operator
F
for
s\neq 0,-1,-2,\ldots
can be formally defined as the following integral of the operator
e^{-\tau F}

F^{-s}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{s-1}e^{-\tau\hat{F}}.

(C.1)

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
the relation (
C.1
) can be verified by alternately acting on it by the operator
F
and integrating by parts
s
times. Note that from the definition (
C.1
), using the properties of gamma functions, it is not difficult to obtain properties that are naturally expected from complex powers, for example,
F^{a}\,F^{b}=F^{a+b}
.

The inverse transform is given by the formula

e^{-\tau F}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{w-i\infty}^{w+i\infty}\frac{\tau^{-s}\Gamma(s)}{F^{s}}ds,

(C.2)

The last expression is easy to understand as follows: the function
\Gamma(s)
has simple poles at the points
s_{n}=-n
with residues
(-1)^{n}/n!
. Then the integral over
s
reduces to the sum of the residues at these poles, which exactly gives the standard Taylor series expansion for the operator exponent
e^{-\tau F}
.

Note that mathematics of spectral geometry and related topics is usually limited to compact manifolds, which leads to significant simplifications associated with the discrete spectrum of
F
. Indeed, in this case, for a strictly positive elliptic differential operator
F=\hat{F}(\nabla)
, there exists a discrete orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions

|n\rangle=\phi_{n}(x),\qquad F|n\rangle=\lambda_{n}|n\rangle,\qquad\lambda_{n}>0.

(C.3)

Then the operator itself and functions of it can be expressed in a simple way in terms of eigenfunctions and eigenvalues

\displaystyle F=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|,

(C.4)

\displaystyle e^{-\tau F}=\sum_{n}e^{-\tau\lambda_{n}}|n\rangle\langle n|,

(C.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{F+\lambda}=\sum_{n}\frac{|n\rangle\langle n|}{\lambda_{n}+\lambda},

(C.6)

\displaystyle F^{-s}=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{-s}|n\rangle\langle n|.

(C.7)

Using the functional trace allows to determinate important global quantities such as the heat kernel trace and the standard operator zeta function

\displaystyle\Tr e^{-\tau F}=\sum_{n}e^{-\tau\lambda_{n}},

(C.8)

\displaystyle\zeta_{F}(s)=\Tr F^{-s}=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{-s}.

(C.9)

On compact manifolds these global quantities can be obtained by the integration of local ones

\displaystyle\Tr e^{-\tau\hat{F}}=\int d^{d}x\,g^{1/2}(x)\,\tr\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x).

(C.10)

For non-compact manifolds and manifolds with boundaries, which are important for physical applications, the definition of global quantities requires additional care because of boundary conditions. The difficulties associated with this can be circumvented by working directly with local (i.e., point-dependent) quantities which asymptotically in small
\tau
and
\sigma^{a}/\tau^{1/2M}
are not sensitive to the presence of boundaries. This is the case we consider in this paper.
