Impact of plasma shaping on tokamak microstability
O. Beeke, M. Barnes, M. Romanelli, M. Nakata, M. Yoshida

Abstract
We have used the local- δ ​ f \delta f gyrokinetic code GS2 to perform studies of the effect of flux-surface shaping on two highly-shaped, low- and high- β \beta JT-60SA-relevant equilibria, including a successful benchmark with the GKV code. We find a novel destabilization of electrostatic fluctuations with increased elongation for plasma with a strongly peaked pressure profile. We explain the results as a competition between the local magnetic shear and finite-Larmor-radius (FLR) stabilization. Electromagnetic studies indicate that kinetic ballooning modes are stabilized by increased shaping due to an increased sensitivity to FLR effects, relative to the ion-temperature-gradient instability. Nevertheless, at high enough β \beta , increased elongation degrades the local magnetic shear stabilization that enables access to the region of ballooning second-stability.



1
Introduction

One of the performance-limiting factors on tokamak experiments is the significant turbulence-dominated radial transport of heat, momentum and particles from the core plasma out to the edge. One method of affecting this turbulent transport is to change the shape of the axisymmetric flux-surfaces traced out by the confining magnetic field; this has been validated both experimentally and in numerical simulations. Flux-surface shape is often characterised by the elongation
\kappa
and triangularity
\delta
, where
\kappa(r)\equiv Z(r,\theta_{\mathrm{max}})/r
and
\delta(r)\equiv[R_{0}(r)-R(r,\theta_{\mathrm{max}})]/r
, where
R(r,\theta)
is the plasma major radius,
\theta
is the poloidal angle,
Z(r,\theta)
is the vertical distance above the midplane,
r\equiv[R(r,0)-R(r,\pi)]/2
is the plasma half-diameter at the flux-surface midplane,
R_{0}(r)\equiv[R(r,0)+R(r,\pi)]/2
is the major radius of the flux-surface center and
\theta_{\mathrm{max}}
is the poloidal angle of the maximum
Z
. A labelled example flux-surface shape is shown in Figure
1
. The above expressions are valid for the up-down symmetric plasmas studied in this work, although similar expressions can be used to account for up-down asymmetry. Plasma shaping is known to affect the stability of magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) modes. Specifically, increased elongation and triangularity increase the threshold “Troyon” plasma
\beta
above which dangerous (e.g. external kink-ballooning) MHD instabilities are triggered:
\beta_{Troyon}=\beta_{N}I_{p}/(aB_{ref})
, where
\beta_{N}\simeq 2\%
is an empirically calculated scaling factor,
a
is the minor radius of the last closed flux-surface (LCFS) and
B_{ref}
is the reference magnetic field, defined as the on-axis toroidal field
[
1
]
. The Troyon beta limit increases linearly with plasma current
I_{p}
which, for fixed safety factor
q
, is increased by elongation and triangularity. Shaping can however have other important consequences, particularly for vertical displacement events (VDE) which are associated with disruptions. Whilst increased elongation can ameliorate the aforementioned MHD instabilities, more severe controls for VDE are then required. This necessitates the careful investigation of shaping effects from both MHD/disruption and transport perspectives.

The impact of shaping on transport due to micro-instabilities has been less-thoroughly studied, and those studies that have been performed have focused only on a relatively small region of the vast multi-dimensional shaping parameter space. Despite this, increased elongation is generally thought to be stabilizing
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, whereas triangularity can have varying effects. Both the TCV and DIII-D experiments have reported that negative triangularity yields improved performance, allowing performance comparable to H-mode whilst maintaining L-mode edge profiles
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Meanwhile,
[
3
]
has reported a dependence on elongation of the effect of triangularity, being stabilizing at large
\kappa
and somewhat destabilizing at moderate
\kappa
. There are various explanations for these observed effects on the turbulence; some suggest that flux-surface shaping modifies locally the driving gradients
[
6
,
7
]
, whilst others point to its effect on local magnetic shear
[
10
]
.

In this work we extend an earlier modelling study by Nakata
[
11
]
studying two JT-60SA-relevant magnetic equilibria
[
12
]
. Following a benchmark between the local,
\delta f
-gyrokinetic codes
GKV

[
13
,
14
]
and
GS2
, we present a study on the effects of triangularity and elongation in these two equilibria, both with and without electromagnetic effects. In Section
2
, we present how shaping influences turbulence in the gyrokinetic framework, as a combination of the local-magnetic-shear stabilization of the toroidal drive, as well as finite-Larmor-radius (FLR) stabilization. In Section
3
, we present the results of the benchmark, followed by electrostatic linear and non-linear shaping scans in
\{\delta,\kappa\}
. We use our model to explain the qualitative trends observed in these scans, including the novel result that increased elongation can be destabilizing. Finally, we present linear electromagnetic shaping scans, showing how kinetic-ballooning-modes (KBMs) react differently to flux-surface shape. The work is then summarized in Section
4
.

Model

\delta f
gyrokinetic framework in which
GS2

(1)

(2)

\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}\delta{A}_{\parallel}=\frac{4\pi}{c}\sum_{s}Z_{s}e\int d^{3}\bm{v}\hskip 2.84526ptv_{\parallel}h_{s}J_{0}(u_{s});

(3)

(4)

where
h_{s}

s

\bm{v}

Z_{s}e

T_{s}
is the temperature,
F_{0,s}

\chi\equiv J_{0}(u_{s})(\delta\phi-v_{\parallel}\delta{A}_{\parallel})+J_{1}(u_{s})\frac{\lvert\bm{v}_{\perp}\rvert}{\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert}\delta{B}_{\parallel}

(5)

is the gyro-averaged gyrokinetic potential,
\delta\phi
is the fluctuating electrostatic potential,
\delta{A}_{\parallel}
and
\delta{B}_{\parallel}
are the parallel components of the magnetic vector potential and field, respectively,
\{\dots\}
indicates the Fourier-transformed Poisson bracket,

\bm{V}_{D,s}\equiv\frac{\bm{b}}{\Omega_{s}}\times\bigg[\bigg(v_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{\bm{v}_{\perp}^{2}}{2}\bigg)\frac{\nabla B}{B}+v_{\parallel}^{2}\frac{4\pi p^{\prime}}{B^{2}}\nabla r\bigg]

(6)

contains the non-

\Omega_{s}
is the Larmor frequency,

u_{s}\equiv\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert\lvert\bm{v}_{\perp}\rvert/\Omega_{s}
and
\bm{k}_{\perp}\equiv k_{\psi}\nabla\psi+k_{\tilde{\alpha}}\nabla\tilde{\alpha}

The effect of magnetic shear on a ballooning-type perturbation. The instability is driven strongly at the outboard midplane where

The flux-surface shape enters these equations via
\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\tilde{\alpha}
in
\bm{k}_{\perp}
. The perpendicular wavevector
\bm{k}_{\perp}
can strengthen (weaken) the toroidal drive term if (mis)aligned with
\bm{V}_{D,s}
; this is closely linked to local-shear stabilization as shown in Figure
2
. It can also provide FLR stabilization via the gyro-average, manifested as the Bessel functions. To calculate
\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\tilde{\alpha}
, we work in toroidal
\{r,\theta,\zeta\}
and cylindrical
\{R,\zeta,Z\}
coordinates, where
r
,
\theta

Z

R

\zeta

\bm{B}=\nabla\tilde{\alpha}\times\nabla\psi

\bm{B}=I\nabla\zeta+\nabla\zeta\times\nabla\psi

\tilde{\alpha}

\tilde{\alpha}=\zeta-\int_{0}^{\theta}d\hat{\theta}\tilde{q},

(7)

where
\tilde{q}(r,\theta)\equiv\bm{B}\cdot\nabla\zeta/\bm{B}\cdot\nabla\theta

\nabla\tilde{\alpha}
contains the local magnetic shear
\tilde{s}\equiv r(\log\tilde{q})^{\prime}

\nabla\tilde{\alpha}=\nabla\zeta-\tilde{q}\nabla\theta-\nabla r\int_{0}^{\theta}d\hat{\theta}\tilde{q}^{\prime},

(8)

where

r

2

[
15

16

R(r,\theta)
and
Z(r,\theta)

[
17

which uses nine parameters to specify the poloidal cross section:

R(r,\theta)=R_{0}(r)+r\cos\big(\theta+\arcsin\delta(r)\sin\theta\big)

(9)

Z(r,\theta)=\kappa(r)r\sin\theta,

(10)

where
\delta
is the triangularity and
\kappa
is the elongation. Only first derivatives of
r

[
18

19

\tilde{s}
and
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}

3.1
Electrostatic, linear shaping scans

To determine the flux-surface shape that minimizes turbulent heat flux, electrostatic scans were performed in the triangularity and elongation Miller parameters for the
\rho=0.5
equilibria. All other parameters were held fixed; simultaneously scaling
\delta^{\prime}
and
\kappa^{\prime}
with
\delta
and
\kappa
was deemed unnecessary as their contributions are smaller by
\epsilon\ll 1
. These electrostatic simulations are equivalent to low-
\beta
electromagnetic simulations with
(\log p)^{\prime}
modified to maintain
\beta^{\prime}\equiv\beta(\log p)^{\prime}
. As such, until electromagnetic effects are included, we refer to the equilibria as low- and high-
(\log p)^{\prime}
. The results of the scan are shown in Figure
6
a). For the low-
(\log p)^{\prime}
equilibrium, increasing the elongation has an almost universal stabilizing effect, whereas the triangularity is destabilizing at low elongation and stabilizing at high elongation. The result is that maximal shaping minimizes the linear ITG instability. The maximum growth rates for the high-
(\log p)^{\prime}
equilibrium are, for all
\{\delta,\kappa\}
, less than for the low-
(\log p)^{\prime}
equilibrium. Additionally, a moderate increase in elongation is destabilizing even up to
\delta\sim 0.2
, with the result that circular flux surfaces are as stable as those with maximal shaping.

Using Figures

\delta

\alpha

\alpha\gtrsim 0.5

\tilde{s}
, although the high-
(\log p)^{\prime}

is sufficiently sensitive to changes in

Electrostatic, nonlinear shaping scans

We also present non-linear electrostatic scans in elongation for both equilibria in Figure
8

32

\Delta k_{x}\simeq\Delta k_{y}=0.047

equilibrium whilst the high-

\langle\hskip 1.42262pt\dots\rangle_{\theta}

Electromagnetic shaping scans

In Figure

In the high-
\beta

\{\delta,\kappa\}
, and the growth rates are everywhere higher than the electrostatic case. Furthermore, the effect of plasma shaping becomes uniformly stabilizing. The nominal low-
\beta

These results can be explained by an increased sensitivity of the KBM to FLR stabilization, compared to the electrostatic ITG. The local magnetic shear is most sensitive to triangularity at
\kappa=1
, and only here is it able to compete with the
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}

\kappa=1

\tilde{s}
and is thus stabilized by
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}
that increases with both
\delta
and
\kappa

\{\delta,\kappa\}
at high
\beta
, but for the low-
\beta
case it becomes sub-dominant for strong shaping.

. As
\kappa
is increased in the region of second stability, the local-shear stabilization is reduced rapidly enough that it outcompetes FLR stabilization. This has the effect of removing the second stability afforded by increased
\beta
.

To summarize, the differences in the results obtained from the nominal- and increased-
(\log p)^{\prime}
scans are as follows: the growth rates are everywhere lower in the increased-
(\log p)^{\prime}
case, and the KBM reacts more strongly to increased
(\log p)^{\prime}
compared to the ITG. This is because the absolute change in
\alpha
scales linearly with
\beta
, so the local magnetic shear is less reduced at low-
\beta
. In the increased-
(\log p)^{\prime}
case, the second stability region is accessed at lower
\beta
. This in turn lowers the threshold
\beta
at which
\kappa
becomes destabilizing. This suggests that to minimize turbulent transport in a high-performance plasma with steep pressure gradients and high
\beta
, the flux surface elongation should be minimized.

4
Summary

In this work we have performed a successful benchmark between the local
\delta f
-gyrokinetic codes
GS2
and
GKV
for two equilibria with significant shaping effects. We have also performed, with the inclusion of electrons as a kinetic species, linear scans in the flux-surface shaping parameters
\delta
and
\kappa
to study the effect on turbulent transport. We find that for low-
\beta
equilibria, triangularity is (de)stabilizing at (low) high elongation, and vice versa. We also find the novel result that elongations of
\kappa\sim 1.5
can be destabilizing in regions of steep normalized pressure gradient. This trend in linear growth rate is also observed in the non-linear heat-flux. We explain these results as a competition between the effects of shaping on the local magnetic shear and on FLR stabilization. Electromagnetic scans were also performed, and KBMs were destabilized for both equilibria. Our results show that for the nominal equilibrium parameters, the KBM is stabilized monotonically by increased shaping; we infer from this that for the KBM, FLR stabilization is stronger relative to the local magnetic shear. Scans in
\{\beta,\kappa\}
show that at sufficiently high
\beta
, the local magnetic shear becomes sensitive enough to
\kappa
that it can compete with FLR stabilization and compromise second-stability. The threshold
\beta
at which this occurs decreases with increased normalized pressure gradient. Therefore, we suggest that for a high-performance plasma, i.e. one with high-
\beta
and steep pressure gradients, the turbulent outwards radial fluxes may be minimized by reducing elongation as much as possible. Conversely, for a moderate
\beta
plasma, we would suggest maximal elongation to FLR-stabilize the KBMs.

Appendix A
Calculation of the local magnetic shear and perpendicular wavenumber

In this section we flesh out the details of how to determine the effect of shaping parameters on stability. As an example, we show an analytic calculation of the effect of elongation to leading order in
\epsilon\ll 1
. As discussed in Section
2
, this involves determining the local magnetic shear and the perpendicular wavenumber.

The local safety factor
\tilde{q}
is the ratio of the toroidal to poloidal component of the magnetic field:

\tilde{q}\equiv\frac{B\cdot\nabla\zeta}{B\cdot\nabla\theta}=\frac{I\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}\psi^{\prime}}

(11)

where we used the axisymmetric form of the magnetic field:

\bm{B}=I\nabla\zeta+\nabla\zeta\times\nabla\psi

(12)

and defined the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{X}
for the transformation between
\{R,\zeta,Z\}\to\{X,\theta,\zeta\}
where
X
is any flux-surface label. This also allows us to define
\psi^{\prime}
in terms of the safety factor
q
:

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{I}{2\pi q}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}

(13)

Therefore, the local magnetic shear is

\tilde{s}\equiv r\frac{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}{\tilde{q}}=r\bigg(\frac{I^{\prime}}{I}+\frac{J}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}-2\frac{R^{\prime}}{R}\bigg).

(14)

where
J\equiv\mathcal{J}_{r}^{\prime}-\mathcal{J}_{r}\psi^{\prime\prime}/\psi^{\prime}
. To evaluate this, we use the Grad-Shafranov equation:

R^{2}\nabla\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla\psi}{R^{2}}\bigg)=-\frac{II^{\prime}+4\pi R^{2}p^{\prime}}{\psi^{\prime}}

(15)

By expanding the divergence and substituting explicit forms for
\nabla r
and
\nabla\theta
using a prescribed
R(r,\theta)
and
Z(r,\theta)
, we arrive at the following expression for
J/\mathcal{J}_{r}
:

\frac{J}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}=\frac{R^{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{r}^{2}\lvert\nabla r\rvert^{2}}\Bigg[2\bigg({R{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{R}^{\theta}+{Z{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg)-\mathcal{J}_{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}\bigg[R^{\prime}{R}^{\theta}+Z^{\prime}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg]\bigg)\Bigg]+\frac{II^{\prime}+4\pi R^{2}p^{\prime}}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}},

(16)

where superscript
\theta
indicates a
\theta
-derivative.
To get an expression for
I^{\prime}/I
, we take the
r
-derivative of Equation (
13
) and move all terms under the integral:

0=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}{\theta}\Bigg[\frac{J}{R^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}\bigg(\frac{I^{\prime}}{I}-\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}-\frac{2R^{\prime}}{R}\bigg)\Bigg].

(17)

This can be used in conjunction with Equation (
16
) integrated over
\theta
to give an expression for
I^{\prime}
:

\frac{I^{\prime}}{I}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}\bigg(1+\frac{I^{2}}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}}\bigg)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}\bigg(\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}+\frac{2R^{\prime}}{R}-\frac{4\pi R^{2}p^{\prime}}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}}\bigg)

+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\lvert\nabla r\rvert^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}\bigg[R^{\prime}{R}^{\theta}+Z^{\prime}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg]\bigg)-\frac{2}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}\bigg({R{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{R}^{\theta}+{Z{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg)\Bigg).

(18)

With these expressions, one can generate expressions for
\tilde{s}
and thus
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}
.

A.1
Analytical expressions for concentric elliptical flux-surfaces

We next use a simplified Miller parametrization that includes only elongation to determine the effect of elongating a circular plasma:

R(r,\theta)=R_{0}+r\cos\theta

(19)

Z(r,\theta)=\kappa r\sin\theta

(20)

To leading order in inverse aspect ratio
\epsilon\ll 1
, we proceed to determine
\tilde{s}
and
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}
, beginning with the following quantities:

\mathcal{J}_{r}(r,\theta)=R\big(R^{\prime}{Z}^{\theta}-{R}^{\theta}Z^{\prime}\big)=\kappa rR

(21)

\lvert\nabla r\rvert^{2}\equiv\frac{R^{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{r}^{2}}\bigg(\big({R}^{\theta}\big)^{2}+\big({Z}^{\theta}\big)^{2}\bigg)=\frac{1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\cos^{2}\theta}{\kappa^{2}}

(22)

\lvert\nabla\theta\rvert^{2}\equiv\frac{R^{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{r}^{2}}\bigg(\big({R}^{\prime}\big)^{2}+\big({Z}^{\prime}\big)^{2}\bigg)=\frac{1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\sin^{2}\theta}{r^{2}\kappa^{2}}

(23)

\nabla r\cdot\nabla\theta\equiv-\frac{R^{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{r}^{2}}\bigg(R^{\prime}{R}^{\theta}+Z^{\prime}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg)=-\frac{(\kappa^{2}-1)\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r\kappa^{2}}

(24)

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{I\kappa\epsilon}{q}+\mathcal{O}(I\epsilon^{3})

(25)

To determine
I^{\prime}/I
, we calculate each of the three integrals that appear in Equation
18
separately:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}\bigg(1+\frac{I^{2}}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}}\bigg)=\frac{2\pi q^{2}}{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)

(26)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\frac{\mathcal{J}_{r}}{R^{2}}\bigg(\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}+\frac{2R^{\prime}}{R}-\frac{4\pi R^{2}p^{\prime}}{\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}}\bigg)=\frac{2\pi}{\epsilon R_{0}}\bigg\{\kappa\epsilon\hat{s}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\bigg\}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon/R_{0})

(27)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\lvert\nabla r\rvert^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}\bigg[R^{\prime}{R}^{\theta}+Z^{\prime}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg]\bigg)-\frac{2}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}\bigg({R{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{R}^{\theta}+{Z{{}^{\prime}}}^{\theta}{Z}^{\theta}\bigg)\Bigg)=-\frac{2\pi}{R_{0}}\big({\kappa^{2}+1}\big)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon/R_{0}).

(28)

Combining these, we find that

\frac{I^{\prime}}{I}\frac{q^{2}}{\epsilon}R_{0}=\kappa\bigg(\hat{s}-\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{\kappa}\bigg)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(29)

where we will find that the leading order piece cancels with a term in
J/\mathcal{J}_{r}
, so we retain the
\mathcal{O}(1)
terms. Using this, we find:

\frac{J}{\mathcal{J}_{r}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon R_{0}}\Bigg\{\frac{\kappa\hat{s}-\alpha\cos\theta}{1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\cos^{2}\theta}\Bigg\}.

Comparing the size of the terms that comprise
\tilde{s}
, the largest is
J/\mathcal{J}_{r}
. Then, to leading order, we find

\tilde{s}=\frac{\kappa\hat{s}-\alpha\cos\theta}{1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\cos^{2}\theta}.

(30)

Now we can use
\tilde{s}
to find
\nabla\tilde{\alpha}
and thus
\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}
:

\int_{0}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}\hat{\theta}\tilde{q}^{\prime}=\frac{q}{r}\bigg[\hat{s}\vartheta-\alpha\Lambda\bigg],

(31)

where

\Lambda(\theta,\kappa)\equiv\frac{1}{2\kappa\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-1}}\log\Bigg(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^{-2}}\sin\theta}{1-\sqrt{1-\kappa^{-2}}\sin\theta}\Bigg)

(32)

and

\vartheta(\theta,\kappa)\equiv\arctan\bigg(\frac{\tan\theta}{\kappa}\bigg).

(33)

which gives:

\lvert\bm{k}_{\perp}\rvert^{2}\propto\big(\hat{s}(\vartheta-\theta_{0})-\alpha\Lambda\big)^{2}\big(1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\cos^{2}\theta\big)+1+(\kappa^{2}-1)\sin^{2}\theta

-2\big(\hat{s}(\vartheta-\theta_{0})-\alpha\Lambda\big)(\kappa^{2}-1)\sin\theta\cos\theta+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)

(34)

where
\theta_{0}\equiv k_{x}/(\hat{s}k_{y})
. These expressions reduce to the typical
\hat{s}-\alpha
results when
\kappa=1
, since
\Lambda(\theta,1)=\sin\theta
and
\vartheta(\theta,1)=\theta
.
