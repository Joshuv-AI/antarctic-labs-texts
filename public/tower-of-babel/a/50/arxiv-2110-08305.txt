Universal finite-size amplitude and anomalous entanglement entropy of z = 2 z=2 quantum Lifshitz criticalities in topological chains
Ke Wang, T. A. Sedrakyan

Abstract
We consider Lifshitz criticalities with dynamical exponent z = 2 z=2 that emerge in a class of topological chains. There, such a criticality plays a fundamental role in describing transitions between symmetry-enriched conformal field theories (CFTs). We report that, at such critical points in one spatial dimension, the finite-size correction to the energy scales with system size, L L , as ∼ L − 2 \sim L^{-2} , with universal and anomalously large coefficient. The behavior originates from the specific dispersion around the Fermi surface, ϵ ∝ ± k 2 \epsilon\propto\pm k^{2} . We also show that the entanglement entropy exhibits at the criticality a non-logarithmic dependence on l / L l/L , where l l is the length of the sub-system. In the limit of l ≪ L l\ll L , the maximally-entangled ground state has the entropy, S ⁡ ( l / L ) = S 0 + 2 ​ n ​ ( l / L ) ​ log ⁡ ( l / L ) S(l/L)=S_{0}+2n(l/L)\log(l/L) . Here S 0 S_{0} is some non-universal entropy originating from short-range correlations and n n is half-integer or integer depending on the degrees of freedom in the model. We show that the novel entanglement originates from the long-range correlation mediated by a zero mode in the low energy sector. The work paves the way to study finite-size effects and entanglement entropy around Lifshitz criticalities and offers an insight into transitions between symmetry-enriched criticalities.

Introduction

A class of criticalities separate gapped symmetry protected phases
Fidkowski and Kitaev 2011
;
Pollmann and Turner 2012
;
Chen et al. 2013
(SPTs) and topologically trivial ones. At these criticalities usually the system disperses linearly,
\epsilon=\pm v_{F}k

;
Affleck 1986
;
Di Francesco et al. 1997

Several universal features characterize conformal critical points. One notable feature for quantum one-dimensional (1D) systems is the universal finite-size amplitude

together with the emergence of the universal characteristic of CFTs, the central charge,
c
. Namely, the finite-size correction to the ground state energy

, e.g., in case of open boundary condition (b.c), always contains a universal term

. The other universal feature is the logarithmic entanglement entropy
Casini and Huerta 2009
, e.g. ,
S\sim c\ln(L)/6
in the case of periodic b.c.

Topologically distinct and gapped phases are reached by adding the mass to the CFT criticalities

A simple example is the hamiltonian
h(i\partial_{x})=v_{F}\sigma_{y}i\partial_{x}+m\sigma_{x}
and

Here

\omega=mL

Recently, it has been observed that CFT critical phases can have non-trivial topology and host boundary modes. Such criticalities are dubbed symmetry-enriched criticalities

;
Ji et al. 2020
;
Cheng and Williamson 2020

\displaystyle H_{\text{Majorana}}=\sum_{n}t_{0}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}{\gamma}_{n}+t_{1}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}{\gamma}_{n+1}+t_{2}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}{\gamma}_{n+2}.

In the lattice models under consideration, the computation of the finite-size amplitude of the ground state is similar to the method used in references

(7)

IV
Anomalous Entanglement entropy

The other universal data, which can be extracted from the Hamiltonian, is the entanglement entropy
S
. Below we take the Majorana chain as an example to illustrate the emergence of the anomalous entanglement. The consideration for the SSH model is similar.

At first glance, one may observe the eigenstates of Eq.
5
are not different from those of the gapped Hamiltonian ( where one replaces
k^{2}\rightarrow m
and
m\neq 0
). Thus one expects short-ranged correlations and constant (non-universal) entanglement entropy, known as features of a gapped 1D quantum system. However, the presence of zero-modes at the Fermi surface changes the situation. With periodic boundary conditions, the
k=0
eigenstate leads to the double degeneracy of the ground state. Tracing the maximally-entangled ground state, we find that the asymptotic correlation function is given by

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{\alpha}(x)\eta_{\beta}(y)\rangle\simeq\epsilon^{\alpha,\beta}\frac{2i}{L}e^{ik_{F}(x-y)},\quad\text{ when }|x-y|\gg 1\text{ and }\alpha,\beta=1,2.

(10)

Here we relabeled the Majorana fermions
\eta_{1}(x)=\gamma_{x}
and
\eta_{2}(x)=\tilde{\gamma}_{x}
for the convenience,
\epsilon^{\alpha,\beta}
is rank-2 anti-symmetric tensor,
L
is the size of the system,
k_{F}=\pi
is the Fermi momentum. So this
L
-dependent long-range correlation originates from
k=0
zero modes at the Fermi surface.
For free fermions, the correlation function encodes the information of entanglment spectrum
Calabrese and Lefevre 2008
;
Pollmann et al. 2010
. It is reflected by a simple fact,
\langle\eta_{\alpha}(x)\eta_{\beta}(y)\rangle=\text{tr}(\eta_{\alpha}(x)\eta_{\beta}(y)\rho_{O})
.
Here
O
is a subsystem and
x,y\in O
.

To find entanglement entropy, one has to find all eigenvalues of the correlation matrix,
\langle\eta_{\alpha}(x)\eta_{\beta}(y)\rangle
, with the subsystem of length
l
. Here from the long-range correlation in Eq.
10
, we find that there is only one non-trivial eigenvalue that has the form
|1-(2l/L)|
. One can obtain the corresponding value in the entanglement spectrum via solving
\tanh(\epsilon_{0}/2)=|1-(2l/L)|
. This gives
\epsilon_{0}=\log(L/l-1)
. Subsequently, the
\epsilon_{0}
results in the non-trivial entropy in Eq.
3
. At the limit
l/L\ll 1
, the asymptotic expression of
S
is
\sim l/L\log(l/L)
. The form is highly-nontrivial, as it is a non-logarithmic function. But its magnitude is weaker than a pure logrithmic function
Swingle and Senthil 2013
. For detailed calculations, we refer the reader to Appendix
B
.

Zero-modes are present and influencing entanglement entropy in other contexts
Huffel and Kelnhofer 2017
;
Chandran and Shankaranarayanan 2019
;
Tjoa and Martín-Martínez 2020
, including CFTs
Casini and Huerta 2005
;
Yazdi 2017
. But the effects of zero-modes are negligible in CFTs. On one hand, Eq.
10
is subleading relative to the
1/|x-y|
decaying correlations in CFTs. On the other hand, the entanglement entropy in Eq.
3

is weaker than the logarithmic entropy. Thus the criticality
\Pi
is a better platform to observe the effect of zero-modes in field theory rather than CFTs.

As the first step we consider the matrix equation for the eigenstates of the system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&v\partial_{x}+u\partial^{2}_{x}\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}e^{ikx}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-ivk+uk^{2}}{\epsilon_{k}}\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\psi_{i\kappa}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\kappa L}}}e^{-\kappa x}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\kappa-v/u}\\
\sqrt{\kappa+v/u}\end{pmatrix}

(12)

\displaystyle\psi_{-k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}e^{-ikx}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{ivk+uk^{2}}{\epsilon_{k}}\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\psi_{-i\kappa}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{e^{2\kappa L}-1}}e^{\kappa x}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\kappa+v/u}\\

\displaystyle y_{n}=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}\text{ is the CFT quantization condition}

\omega\rightarrow\infty
.

These calculations represent the first step for deriving the finite-size scaling function describing deviation from the gapless Lifshitz point. However, the transcendent nature of the QC Eq. (
15
) makes it challenging to derive the form of the finite-size scaling function analytically. For this reason, we defer this analysis to forthcoming studies.

VI
Conclusion

For the criticality
\Pi
with quadratic dispersion,
\epsilon\sim\pm k^{2}
, we find a universal finite-size amplitude
J
as the coefficient in front of
L^{-2}
term in the ground state energy of the system. The magnitude of
J
is anomalously large as it is of the order of one. There exist rich phenomena in finite-size scaling functions around this criticality
Gulden et al. 2016
;
Wang and Sedrakyan 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Veríssimo et al. 2021
. For example, with Eq.
1
at
v\neq 0
, a universal finite-size scaling emerges as a function of the scale
Lv
, and the function has a peak at the topological side. In principle, the Lifshitz criticality can also be enriched by symmetries. In that case, the presence of boundary modes around the Fermi surface and in case of breaking of some discrete symmetries (including the chiral symmetry and the time-reversal symmetry), one expects the emergence of a non-monotonic universal function of some scaling variable. This is an interesting and open problem.

The entanglement of the ground state is also found to be non-trivial, carrying a non-logarithmic entropy. This originates from the zero modes at the Fermi surface. Compared to CFTs, zero modes play a more crucial role at the criticality
\Pi
. This offers an opportunity to observe the effects of zero modes in the fermionic field theory
Huffel and Kelnhofer 2017
;
Chandran and Shankaranarayanan 2019
;
Tjoa and Martín-Martínez 2020
. Similarly, one can also explore the behaviors of entanglement entropy and boundary entropy
C. 1989
;
Emery and Kivelson 1992
;
Affleck and Ludwig 1991
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Wang and Sedrakyan 2020
around
\Pi
.

Effects of interactions are not explored in the present work. The exciting question is establishing the interacting theory of the low energy sector of
\Pi
criticality. This question is beyond the scope of the Luttinger liquid
Luttinger 1963
;
Imambekov et al. 2012
, where mostly the linear dispersion is considered.

Appendix A
Derivations of the finite-size amplitude

In this section, we provide a detailed calculation of the universal finite-size amplitude,
J
. We start from the G.S. energy, given by Eq.
8
, and rewrite it as

\displaystyle E_{G.S.}=-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\oint_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}z^{2}\partial_{z}\ln\Big(\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}{2}+\frac{2}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}\Big).

(18)

Now let us consider the upper part of the contour,
C^{+}=(i\delta,+2\pi L+i\delta)
, in the complex space of the integration variable
z
. The contribution to the
E_{G.S.}
that corresponds to this upper part of the contour can be decomposed into

\displaystyle E_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\int_{i\delta}^{2\pi L+i\delta}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}z^{2}\partial_{z}\Big\{\ln e^{-iz}+\ln\Big(\frac{e^{2iz}+1}{2}+\frac{2e^{iz}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}\Big)\Big\}.

(19)

The first term in the integral gives
(2\pi)^{2}/3L
, which is exactly the bulk energy. Thus, the finite size effect comes from the second term. We single out the finite-size contribution and define the integral as

\displaystyle J_{+}\equiv\int_{i\delta}^{4\pi L+i\delta}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}z^{2}\partial_{z}\ln\Big(\frac{e^{2iz}+1}{2}+\frac{2e^{iz}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}\Big).

(20)

Since the integrand at the arc is exponentially small, one could deform the contour into the form shoun by the (red) dashed line in Fig.
4
.
Also, we define a new variable
z=x\alpha
with
\alpha=e^{i\pi/4}
and rewrite
J_{+}
as

\displaystyle J_{+}=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{4\pi}x^{2}\partial_{x}\ln\Big(\frac{e^{2ix\alpha}+1}{2}+\frac{2}{e^{\sqrt{2}x}+e^{-i\sqrt{2}x}}\Big).

(21)

Here
\alpha=e^{i\pi/4}
. The
"-"
sign emerges due to the deformation of the contour. Upon rescaling
x
by
x\rightarrow\sqrt{2}x
, one obtains

\displaystyle J_{+}=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{8\pi}x^{2}\partial_{x}\ln\Big(\frac{e^{(i-1)x}+1}{2}+\frac{2}{e^{x}+e^{-ix}}\Big).

(22)

Note there are also finite-size contributions coming from the other three segments, namely
(-i\delta,+2\pi L-i\delta)
and
(\pm i\delta,-2\pi L\pm i\delta)
. Repeating similar calculations, one obtains the amplitude,
A
, presented in the Eq.
9
of the main text.

Here we further simplify Eq.
9
to obtain an expression that is more convenient for integration. One can take the derivative,
\partial_{x}
, and obtain

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Re}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{2}dx}{\pi}\frac{\frac{1}{2}(i-1)(e^{-x}+e^{ix})-2(e^{x}+e^{-ix})^{-1}(e^{x}-ie^{-ix})}{\cos x+\cosh x+2}

(23)

Then one can take the real part of the integral and arrive at the following expression

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{2}dx}{2\pi}\frac{1}{{\cos x+\cosh x+2}}\Bigg(e^{-x}+\cos x+\sin x+4\frac{1+(\cos x-\sin x)e^{-x}}{1+2\cos xe^{-x}+e^{-2x}}\Bigg)

One can numerically evaluate the convergent integral over the single real-variable,
x
, and obtain the numerical value of the amplitude,
J
, presented in the manuscript.

Appendix B
Derivation of the correlation function and the entanglement entropy

B.1
The correlation function

Without losing generality, we start from a rotated Hamiltonian given by
H=\int_{0}^{L}dx\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{x}^{2}\sigma_{z}\Psi(x)
.
For BDI symmetry class,
\Psi(x)=[\psi(x),\psi^{\dagger}(x)]
. Under the periodic boundary conditions, the eigenstates are simply free wavefunctions. They can be cast in the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{ikx}(0,1).

(24)

Thus the quasi-particle operator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}e^{ikx}\psi^{\dagger}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}e^{ikx}[\eta_{1}(x)-i\eta_{2}(x)].

(25)

As usual, we decomposed the fermion operator into Majorana fermions
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}[\eta_{1}(x)+i\eta_{2}(x)]
. Thus one can find that the Majorana operators diagonalizing the system are given by

\displaystyle b^{1}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}(\eta_{1}(x)\cos kx+\eta_{2}(x)\sin kx)

(26)

\displaystyle b^{2}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}(\eta_{1}(x)\sin kx-\eta_{2}(x)\cos kx)

(27)

With the diagonalizing Majorana fermions, one can evaluate the correlation function of
\eta
-Majorana fermions,

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{\alpha}(x_{i})\eta_{\beta}(x_{j})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I+\frac{i}{L}\sum_{m}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin k_{m}(x_{i}-x_{j})&-\cos k_{m}(x_{i}-x_{j})\\
\cos k_{m}(x_{i}-x_{j})&-\sin k_{m}(x_{i}-x_{j}),\end{pmatrix}

(28)

where summation over
m\in\mathbb{Z}
has to be performed. This can be done for diagonal and off-diagonal independently as follows:

•

For the diagonal matrix element one obtains a Kronecker
\delta
upon switching to integration:

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{1}(x_{i})\eta_{1}(x_{j})\rangle\simeq i\delta_{i,j}-\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dk\sin k(x_{i}-x_{j})\simeq i\delta_{i,j}.

(29)

•

Then we compute the non-diagonal correlators:

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{1}(x_{i})\eta_{2}(x_{j})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dk\cos k(x_{i}-x_{j})=-i\delta_{i,j}.

(30)

The conclusion is that

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{1}(x_{i})\eta_{1}(x_{j})\rangle\simeq i\delta_{i,j},\quad\langle\eta_{1}(x_{i})\eta_{2}(x_{j})\rangle\simeq-i\delta_{i,j}.

(31)

The absence of the
\sim 1/x
decaying correlation, as opposed to the Ising CFT, is because the degeneracy here is rather trivial. Namely,
\varphi_{k}
and
\varphi_{-k}
share the same vector part, namely
(0,1)
. Recall that the degeneracy of
k
and
-k
in CFT is non-trivial: the vector parts of
\varphi_{k}
and
\varphi_{-k}
are two different eigenstates of
\sigma_{z}
.

Up to now, the correlation looked like the massive ones. Now we uncover a straightforward fact that there exists zero-mode, defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{L}}\sum_{x}[\eta_{1}(x)+i\eta_{2}(x)].

(32)

This zero-mode is
not
considered in the previous ground state. In the BDI class, the ground state is double degenerate. We define the first ground state
|GS\rangle_{0}
to be

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{k}|GS\rangle_{0}=0,\quad k\in(-\pi,\pi)

(33)

In fact, this ground state is rather trivial. It is simply the vacuum state of the field operators
\psi(x)
.
From the redundancy description, this ground state is simply filling the negative band. Now we define the second ground state as

\displaystyle|GS\rangle_{1}=\chi_{0}^{\dagger}|GS\rangle_{0}

(34)

The previous calculation concerned only
|GS\rangle_{0}
. Now we perform the calculation for
|GS\rangle_{1}
. The calculation is similar to the previous case with only difference coming from the zero mode. Namely, take the compensation of the contribution when the zero-mode is not occupied. It gives the correlation function evaluated with
|GS\rangle_{1}
,

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{1}(x_{i})\eta_{2}(x_{j})\rangle_{1}\simeq-i\delta_{i,j}+\frac{2i}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}

(35)

One may use the following
2\times 2
matrix as the building block for the
2N\times 2N
matrix:

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{\alpha}(x_{i})\eta_{\beta}(x_{j})\rangle_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{i,j}&-i\delta_{i,j}+\frac{2i}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}\\
i\delta_{i,j}-\frac{2i}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}&\delta_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}

(36)

In the asymptotic limit
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\gg 1
, one obtains Eq.
10
of the main text.

B.2
The entanglement entropy

We obtain the entanglement entropy for the free fermion system under consideration by directly diagonalizing the non-diagonal part of the correlation matrix. To this end, we consider the correlation matrix of the form

\displaystyle C^{\alpha,\beta}_{i,j}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0&\delta_{ij}-\frac{2}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}\\
-\delta_{ij}+\frac{2}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}&0\end{pmatrix}

(37)

The matrix is antisymmetric. Now let us reconstruct a single-particle Majorana fermion Hamiltonian that gives the correlation matrix Eq. (
37
):

\displaystyle H_{C}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{i,j}\Big(\delta_{ij}-\frac{2}{L}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}\Big)\gamma_{1,i}\gamma_{2,j}+h.c.

(38)

Here
\gamma_{\alpha,i}
is an artificial Majorana fermion operator, and the index
\alpha=1,2
. One can prove that the eigenvalues of the
C
-matrix in Eq. (
37
) are the same as the ones for the
H_{C}
operator. Then let us diagonalize the Hamiltonian by introducing the fermion operators,
a_{i}=(\gamma_{1,i}+i\gamma_{2,i})/2
. Such a substitution leads to the following quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}-\frac{2}{L}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{l}(-1)^{x_{i}-x_{j}}a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j}

(39)

where
l
is the size of subsystem we take. Here, let us focus on the second term and define

\displaystyle\hat{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{l}a_{i}e^{i\pi x_{i}}

(40)

It is straightforward to check that
\hat{y}
is still a fermionic operator,
\{\hat{y},\hat{y}^{\dagger}\}=1
. Thus
H_{C}
becomes

\displaystyle H_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left(a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{2l}{L}\left(\hat{y}^{\dagger}\hat{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

(41)

This yielded the diagonal form of the fermionic Hamiltonian,
H_{C}
. The positive energies of the single particle spectrum thus read

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{2l}{L}\right|,1,1\ldots,1.

(42)

Note that
1-\frac{2l}{L}\geq 0\iff 2l\leq L
. If
2l>L
, the positive eigenvalue becomes
\frac{2l}{L}-1
. Thus one can cast the non-trivial eigenvalues of
H_{C}
in the form

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2l}{L},\quad\text{if }2l<L,\quad\frac{2l}{L}-1,\quad\text{if }2l>L.

(43)

This indicates that only zero modes introduce nontrivial correlation while all other modes give trivial correlation. If
\epsilon
denotes the entanglement spectrum, we can find it from the solution of the following relation:

\displaystyle\tanh\frac{\epsilon}{2}=1-\frac{2l}{L}.

(44)

Thus the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix in a subsystem
l
are the same with the number of
1
’s in the spectrum being
l-1
. Then we could find the entanglement spectrum from the set of values

\displaystyle\epsilon=\log\left(\frac{L}{l}-1\right),\quad\text{if }l\leq L/2;\quad\epsilon=\log\left(\frac{L}{L-l}-1\right),\quad\text{if }l\geq L/2.

(45)

The entanglement entropy can be expressed as the sum over the entanglement spectrum
Chung and Peschel 2001
;
Peschel 2003
,

\displaystyle S=\sum_{n}\log\left(2\cosh\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{2}\right)-\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{2}\tanh\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{2}.

(46)

Here
\{\epsilon_{n}\}
denotes the entanglement spectrum and the summation is performed over all values in the entanglement spectrum. Trivial terms corresponding to
\epsilon=\infty
give zero contribution to the entropy. Thus we only need to keep track of the non-trivial contribution. Since there is a symmetry between
l
and
L-l
, we can only focus on the calculation for the case
2l<L
. Before performing the calculations, we need the following identities for any real variable
x
:

\displaystyle 2\cosh\log x=x+\frac{1}{x},\quad\tanh\log x=\frac{x^{2}-1}{x^{2}+1}.

(47)

Having these, one can take
x=\sqrt{L/l-1}
and putting together Eqs.
45
,
46
,
47
, one finds

\displaystyle S=\log\left(\frac{L}{L-l}\right)+\frac{l}{L}\log\left(\frac{L}{l}-1\right).

(48)

This is the main result in the paper. Now we simplify Eq.
48
in two limiting cases:

•

The limit of a small subsystem,
l\ll L
. Here first term in the expression for the entanglement entropy is approaching to zero due to the linear in
l/L
prefactor. Namely,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{L}{L-l}\right)\simeq\frac{l}{L}+O(l^{2}/L^{2}),\quad\text{if }l\ll L

(49)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{l}{L}\log\left(\frac{L}{l}-1\right)\simeq\frac{l}{L}\log\frac{L}{l}+O(l^{2}/L^{2}),\quad\text{if }l\ll L

(50)

Thus in this limit, within the
O(l/L)^{2}
accuracy,

\displaystyle S\simeq\frac{l}{L}\ln\left(\frac{eL}{l}\right)

(51)

where
e
is the Euler number. The asymptotic behavior is obviously non-logarithmic in
l
.

•

The limit of a large subsystem,
2l\simeq L
. Firstly, exactly at
L=2l
, the entanglement entropy is found to be
S=\log 2
. It is the typical result of a gapped system. Writing
\delta l=L/2-l
, one thus has

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1}{1/2+\delta l/2L}\right)+(1/2-\delta l/L)\log\left(\frac{2}{1-2\delta l/L}-1\right)

(52)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\log 2+O\left(\frac{\delta l}{L}\right)^{2}.

This indicates that the entanglement entropy,
S
, in this limit approaches its maximal value of
\log 2
in this limit.
