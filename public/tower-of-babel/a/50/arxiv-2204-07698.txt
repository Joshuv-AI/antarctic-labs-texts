GALOISIAN GALOIS THEORY

Lecture Notes in Computational Algebra

ABSTRACT.

These notes are an exposition of Galois Theory from the original Lagrangian and
Galoisian point of view. A particular effort was made here to better understand the
connection between Lagrange’s purely combinatorial approach and Galois algebraic
extensions of the latter. Moreover, stimulated by the necessities of present day
computer explorations, the algorithmic approach has been given
priority here over every other aspect of presentation. In particular, you may not find here the clean
simplistic look characteristic of the classical exposition of E. Artin. In contrast these notes
should provide a good starting point in attempting constructions in this most
difficult computational arena.

1. Symmetric Functions

Unless otherwise specified all fields we shall work with here will be assumed to have zero characteristic.
If
{\cal F}
is such a field and
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
are indeterminates the expression

{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]

will denote the ring of polynomials in
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
with coefficients in
{\cal F}
. In contrast to customary
notation, the field of rational functions of
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
with coefficients in
{\cal F}
will be denoted
by

Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

We may also write

Rat[{\cal F};f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{m}]

to represent all rational expressions in
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}
with coefficients in
{\cal F}
,
whatever
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{m}
may be in any particular situation.

As customary,
S_{n}
denotes the group of all permutations of
1,2,\ldots,n
.

If
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in S_{n}
and

\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

we set
\enskip x\sigma\enskip=\enskip x_{\sigma_{1}},x_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,x_{\sigma_{n}}
and

\sigma\Phi\enskip=\enskip\Phi(x\sigma)\enskip=\enskip\Phi(x_{\sigma_{1}},x_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,x_{\sigma_{n}})

We shall say that
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
is
k-valued
if and only if the collection

\{\>\sigma\>\Phi\>:\>\sigma\in S_{n}\>\}

has cardinality
k
. Of course
1
-valued functions are usually called
symmetric
.
It will be convenient to denote here by
Sym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
Ratsym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
the collections of symmetric elements of

{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
respectively.

The combinatorial study of
k
-valued function is properly the domain of Lagrange theory
and will be carried out in the next section. In this section we will limit ourselves to establishing
the results on symmetric functions that are needed in our further developments.

We recall that the symmetric polynomial

e_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\enskip=\enskip\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\>x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\enskip

is usually referred to as the
k^{th}\enskip elementary
symmetric function.

A vector of integers

\lambda\enskip=\enskip(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}>0)

is said to be a partition of
n
and we write
\lambda\vdash n
if and only if

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}=n

We shall also say that
k
is
the number of parts
of
\lambda
.
If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions of the same number with
k
and
h
parts respectively,
we shall say that
\lambda

dominates

\mu
and write
\lambda\>\geq\mu
if and only if

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{s}\geq\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{s}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip for\enskip s=1,2,\ldots,min(h,k)\enskip)

It easy to see that this partial order is linearly extended by the lexicographic order of integer
vectors. Finally, given a partition
\mu
the partition
\mu^{\prime}
whose parts
\mu_{s}^{\prime}
are
given by

\mu_{s}^{\prime}\enskip=\enskip\#\{i\>:\>\mu_{i}\geq s\}

is usually referred to as the
conjugate
of
\mu
. A simple combinatorial argument shows that
we have
\lambda\geq\mu
if and only if
\lambda^{\prime}\leq\mu^{\prime}
.

Given a vector

p=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})

of non-negative integers, the weakly decreasing rearrangement
of the positive components of
p
will referred to as the
shape
of
p

and denoted by
\lambda(p)
. Given a partition
\lambda
the symmetric polynomial

m_{\lambda}(x)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{\lambda(p)=\lambda}\>x^{p}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip x^{p}=x_{1}^{p_{1}}x_{1}^{p_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{p_{n}}\enskip)

is usually referred to as the
monomial symmetric function
indexed by
\lambda
.
The following fact is immediate.

Theorem 1.1

Every symmetric polynomial
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
has a unique expansion of the form

\Phi(x)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{\lambda}\>c_{\lambda}\>m_{\lambda}(x)

[formula]

where the
c_{\lambda}
are integers if
\Phi
has integer coefficients, and
c_{\lambda}
is in
{\cal F}
if

\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Proof

The symmetry of
\Phi
implies that the coefficients
c_{p}
and
c_{q}
of any two monomials

x^{p}
and x
q
appearing in
\Phi
must be the same if
p
and
q
have the same shape.
Thus 1.1 is obtained by collecting terms of
\Phi
according to shape.

Let now
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{h})
be a partition and set

e_{\mu}(x)\enskip=\enskip e_{\mu_{1}}(x)e_{\mu_{2}}(x)\cdots e_{\mu_{h}}(x)

[formula]

Since this polynomial is clearly symmetric in
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
it must have an expansion of the form

e_{\mu}(x)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{\lambda}\>m_{\lambda}(x)\>c_{\lambda\mu}\enskip.

[formula]

It develops that the coefficients
c_{\lambda\mu}
have a suggestive combinatorial interpretation.

Proposition 1.1

If
\mu
has
h
parts and
\lambda
has
k
parts then
c_{\lambda\mu}
gives the number
of
h\times k
matrices with
0,1
-entries and row and column sums given by
\mu
and
\lambda
respectively.

Proof

There is a one-to-one correspondence between these matrices and the monomials obtained by expanding
the product in 1.3. In fact, if
m_{1}(x),m_{2}(x),\ldots,m_{h}(x)
are monomials coming out of

e_{\mu_{1}}(x),e_{\mu_{2}}(x),\ldots,e_{\mu_{h}}(x)
respectively, then
m_{i}(x)
corresponds to a subset
of
1,2,\ldots,n
of cardinality
\mu_{i}
, thus it may be represented by a
0,1
-vector with

n
components in an obvious manner. Putting together these vectors as the rows of an
h\times n

matrix
M
we see that these monomials multiply to
x^{p}=x_{1}^{p_{1}}x_{2}^{p_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{p_{n}}

if and only if the columns of
M
add up to
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}
respectively. This is our desired
correspondence. Since, since monomials of the same shape have the same coefficient, the assertion
follows by taking
x^{p}=x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots x_{k}^{\lambda_{k}}
.

Remark 1.1

Let
M
be one of the
0,1
-matrices with row and column sums given by
\mu
and
\lambda
.
Note that the number of
1\>^{\prime}s
in the first
s
columns of
M
is equal to

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{s}

On the other hand by moving all the
1\>^{\prime}s
along their rows until they are bumper to bumper to
the left (and all the
0^{\prime}s
similarly to the right)
we obtain a matrix
M^{\prime}
whose column sums are the parts of the partition
\mu^{\prime}

conjugate to
\mu
. This not only gives the inequality

\mu^{\prime}_{1}+\mu^{\prime}_{2}+\cdots+\mu^{\prime}_{s}\>\geq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{s}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip for\enskip s=1,2,\ldots,min(h,k)\enskip)

but also assures that when
\lambda=\mu
there can only be one matrix with the desired row and column sums.

We can thus obtain

Theorem 1.2

Every homogeneous symmetric polynomial
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
of degree
m
has a unique expansion
of the form

\Phi(x)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{\mu\vdash m}\>d_{\mu}\>e_{\mu}(x)

where the coefficients
d_{\mu}
are integers if
\Phi
has integer coefficients and they
are elements of
{\cal F}
if
\Phi\in Sym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. In particular,
Sym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
is the polynomial
ring generated by the elementary symmetric functions
e_{1}(x),e_{2}(x),\ldots,e_{n}(x)
.

Proof

Let
D=\|d_{\lambda\mu}\|_{\lambda,\mu\vdash m}
denote the matrix obtained
when the partitions
\lambda
are in lexicographic order and the partitions
\mu
are in
reverse lexicographic order. This done we see that an immediate consequence of the observations
made in the Remark above is that
D
must be unitriangular and therefore invertible over the integers.
This shows that the collection
\{e_{\mu}(x)\}_{\mu\vdash m}
must also give a basis and that
the elements of the basis
\{m_{\lambda}(x)\}_{\lambda\vdash m}
have integral linear expansions in terms
of the
e_{\mu}(x)\>^{\prime}s
. This establishes our assertions.

Corollary 1.2

Ratsym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]\enskip=\enskip Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}]

[formula]

Proof

Note that every element
\Phi\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
can be expressed in the form

\Phi(x)={P(x)\over Q(x)}

with
P,Q\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Multiplying numerator and denominator by the polynomial

\prod_{\sigma\in A_{n}\atop\sigma\neq id}\>\sigma Q

we can write

\Phi(x)={P^{*}(x)\over Q^{*}(x)}

where

P^{*}(x)=P(x)\prod_{\sigma\in A_{n}\atop\sigma\neq id}\>\sigma Q\enskip\enskip\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip\enskip\enskip Q^{*}(x)=\prod_{\sigma\in A_{n}}\>\sigma Q\enskip.

Since by its very construction
Q^{*}(x)
is already symmetric, we see that
\Phi
will be symmetric
if and only if
P^{*}
is. Thus our assertion follows immediately from Theorem 1.2.

2. The Euclidean algorithm the Resultant and the Discriminant.

If
A,B
are polynomials in
{\cal F}[t]
, with
degree\>B<degree\>A
,
then the
quotient
and the
remainder
anof the
division
of
A
by
B
are
respectively the unique
polynomials
Q
and
R
satisfying the requirements

\eqalign{&(1)\enskip\enskip A\enskip=\enskip B\>Q\enskip+\enskip R\cr&(2)\enskip\enskip degree(R)\><\>degree(B)\cr}\enskip.

To construct these two polynomials we can proceed as follows. Set

\Delta(A/B)\enskip=\enskip{leading\enskip coeff\enskip A\over leading\enskip coeff\enskip B}\enskip\enskip t^{degree(A)-degree(B)}

Clearly we have

degree\bigl(A-\Delta(A/B)\>B\bigr)\enskip<\enskip degree(A).

[formula]

Now set

R^{(o)}=A\enskip\enskip{\rm and}\enskip\enskip R^{(i)}=R^{(i-1)}-\Delta(R^{(i-1)}/B)\>B\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip i=1,2,\ldots\enskip)

[formula]

Since, by 2.1, degrees are decreasing at least by one at each step of the recursion, after

k\leq degree(A)-degree(B)
steps we shall have
degree(R^{(k)})<degree(B)
. At this point we stop
the recursion. By adding the identities in 2.1 we easily derive that

A\enskip=\enskip\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\>\Delta(R^{(i)}/B)\>\Bigr)\enskip B\enskip+\enskip R^{(k)}

[formula]

Thus we may take

Q=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\>\Delta(R^{(i)}/B)\enskip\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip\enskip R=R^{(k)}\enskip.

[formula]

The important fact to note is that from 2.3 we deduce that if
A,B\in{\cal F}[t]
then

R(t)
is in
{\cal F}[t]
as well. We shall refer to the process above as the
division algorithm
.

The Euclidean algorithm is the process which yields the greatest common divisor
D(t)

of two polynomials
A
and
B
. It is shown by Berlekamp [1] that
D
may be computed by the following
process. Set

r_{-2}=A\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip r_{-1}=B\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip p_{-2}=0\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip p_{-1}=1\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip q_{-2}=1\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip q_{-1}=0

Then compute
a_{k},r_{k},p_{k},q_{k}
according to the recursions

\eqalign{&(1)\enskip\enskip r_{k-2}\enskip=\enskip a_{k}\>r_{k-1}\enskip+\enskip r_{k}\enskip\enskip(division)\cr&(2)\enskip\enskip p_{k}\enskip=\enskip a_{k}\>p_{k-1}\enskip+\enskip p_{k-2}\cr&(3)\enskip\enskip q_{k}\enskip=\enskip a_{k}\>q_{k-1}\enskip+\enskip q_{k-2}\cr}\enskip.\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip k=0,1,2,\ldots\enskip)

since
degree(r_{k})
dcreases at least by one at each step after
n<degree(B)
steps
we shall have
r_{n}=0
. It is shown in [] that these recursion force the following basic identities

\eqalign{&(1)\enskip\enskip q_{n}\>p_{n-1}\enskip-\enskip p_{n}\>q_{n-1}\enskip=\enskip=(-1)^{n}\cr&(2)\enskip\enskip A\enskip=\enskip r_{n-1}\>p_{n}\cr&(3)\enskip\enskip B\enskip=\enskip r_{n-1}\>q_{n}\cr}\enskip.

Since equation (1) here yields that
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
are relatively prime, we see that (2) and (3)
yield that the greatest common divisor
D
of
A
and
B
is necessarily given by
r_{n-1}
.

As pointed out in [] the advantage of this process over the one that is usually described in
most textbooks is that it provides the final answer without excessive storage of partial results.
In fact, only
7
results need to be stored at any particular time, a number that is independent
of the choice of
A
and
B
.

If the roots of a polynomial

P(t)\enskip=\enskip a_{o}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+a_{m}t^{m}

are
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
then when
a_{n}=1
we may write it in the form

P(t)\enskip=\enskip(t-x_{1})(t-x_{2})\cdots(t-x_{m})

[formula]

Thus, we see that we must have

a_{n-k}\enskip=\enskip(-1)^{k}\>e_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

[formula]

Clearly, if
P(t)
and

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip b_{o}+a_{1}t+b_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+b_{m}t^{m}\enskip=\enskip(t-y_{1})(t-y_{2})\cdots(t-y_{n})\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip b_{n}=1\enskip)

have a root in common, the expression

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x_{i}-y_{j})

will necessarily vanish. It develops that a multiple of this expression may be written as a polynomial
in the coefficients of
P
and
Q
. This polynomial is usually referred to as the
Resultant of
P

and
Q
and will be denoted here by
R[P,Q]
.
The case
m=2
,
n=3
is sufficient to get accross the idea and avoids excessive notation.

Theorem 2.1

R[P,Q]=det\enskip\pmatrix{a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0&0\cr 0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0\cr 0&0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}\cr b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&0\cr 0&b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\cr}\enskip=\enskip(-1)^{2\times 3}\>a_{2}^{3}\>b_{3}^{2}\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{3}(x_{i}-y_{j})

[formula]

Proof

Note that since the division by
a_{2}
and
b_{3}
does not change the roots of
P
and
Q
, we can
divide both sides of 2.7 by
a_{2}^{3}\>b_{3}^{2}
and reduce ourselves to the case
a_{2}=b_{3}=1
.
This given, note that we have the following matrix multiplication identity:

\pmatrix{a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0&0\cr 0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0\cr 0&0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}\cr b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&0\cr 0&b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\cr}\times\pmatrix{1&1&1&0&0\cr y_{1}&y_{2}&y_{3}&0&0\cr y_{1}^{2}&y_{2}^{2}&y_{3}^{2}&0&0\cr y_{1}^{3}&y_{2}^{3}&y_{3}^{3}&1&0\cr y_{1}^{4}&y_{2}^{4}&y_{3}^{4}&0&1\cr}\enskip=\enskip\pmatrix{P(y_{1})&P(y_{2})&P(y_{3})&0&0\cr y_{1}P(y_{1})&y_{2}P(y_{2})&y_{3}P(y_{3})&0&0\cr y_{1}^{2}P(y_{1})&y_{2}^{2}P(y_{2})&y_{3}^{3}P(y_{3})&0&0\cr 0&0&1&b_{3}&0\cr 0&0&0&0&b_{3}\cr}

and 2.7 follows immediately by equating determinants of boths sides and cancelling
the common factor.

Remark 2.1

Note that the vanishing of the determinant in 2.7 assures that we can find a non trivial solution
to the corresponding homogeneous system. Now a simple computation shows that we have

\pmatrix{\alpha_{o}&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&-\beta_{o}&-\beta_{1}}\enskip\times\enskip\pmatrix{a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0&0\cr 0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0\cr 0&0&a_{o}&a_{1}&a_{2}\cr b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&0\cr 0&b_{o}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\cr}\enskip=0

if and only if

and this is equivalent to the statement that

and

have a non trivial common factor.

In the same vein we see that the expression

vanishes if and only if

as given by 2.5 has multiple roots. Since,

Now we easily see that

of the equation

3. The cubic and the quartic

Formulas giving the general solution of the cubic equation

E_{3}(t)\enskip=\enskip(t-x_{1})(t-x_{2})(t-x_{3})\enskip=\enskip t^{3}-e_{1}t^{2}+e_{2}t-e_{3}\enskip=\enskip 0

[formula]

where first discovered by Ferreo (sometimes before 1505) rediscoverd by Tartaglia
and published by Cardano in 1545. Setting
\omega=e^{{2\pi\thinspace i}/3}
the three roots of 3.1
may be written as follows

\eqalign{x_{1}&\enskip=\enskip{e_{1}\over 3}\enskip+\enskip\enskip\enskip{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q+{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip\enskip+\enskip\enskip{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q-{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip,\cr x_{2}&\enskip=\enskip{e_{1}\over 3}\enskip+\enskip\omega\>{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q+{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip+\enskip\omega^{2}\>{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q-{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip,\cr x_{3}&\enskip=\enskip{e_{1}\over 3}\enskip+\enskip\omega^{2}\>{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q+{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip+\enskip\omega\>{\root 3 \of{-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}q-{\root 2 \of{R}}}}\enskip,\cr}

[formula]

We start by making the substitution

This given the further substitution

[formula]

Since this is a quadratic equation for

[formula]

From which the formulas in 3.2 can be immediately obtained.

The quartic equation was treated in a similar manner. That is “ad hoc” manipulations

[formula]

Completing the square suggested by the first two terms we can rewrite this equation in the form

which yield that the right hand side of 3.9 when

[formula]

with

[formula]

and then the factorization of

This given, we may write

-R\enskip=\enskip{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}z\>t\enskip+\enskip(e_{3}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}\>e_{1}y_{1})/z

\eqalign{&x_{1}+x_{2}\enskip=\enskip{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}z\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip x_{1}x_{2}\enskip=\enskip{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}y_{1}-(e_{3}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}y_{1})/z\enskip,\cr&x_{3}+x_{4}\enskip=\enskip{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}z\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip x_{3}x_{4}\enskip=\enskip{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}y_{1}+(e_{3}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}y_{1})/z\enskip.\cr}

Thus we may obtain the desired expressions for the pairs

and

by solving
the two quadratic equations

\eqalign{&t^{2}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}(e_{1}+z)\>t+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}y_{1}-(e_{3}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}y_{1})/z\enskip=\enskip 0\enskip,\cr&t^{2}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}(e_{1}-z)\>t+{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}y_{1}+(e_{3}-{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}e_{1}y_{1})/z\enskip=\enskip 0\enskip.\cr}

This given, in the
17^{th}
and
18^{th}
centuries it was natural to assume that
the solution of the general polynomial equation should be obtainable by similar
manipulations and successive root extractions. This was the motivating force []
in Lagrange’s investigations in the 1770’s that led him to his historic paper

Réflections sur la résolution algébrique des équations
.

To be precise Lagrange was investigating the possibility of finding
closed form

expressions for the roots
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
which (like those appearing in 3.2) only involved the
elementary symmetric functions
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
, roots of unity and radicals. We shall refer to this
as
“solving the general equation by radicals”
.

His point of departure was a close examination of the solutions of the cubic and the quartic.
Remarkably, he was able to sort out of those simingly ad hoc manipulations a unifying
general mechanism of solution. As we shall see Lagrange discovered that in both cases
the final formulas could be reached by a sequence of identical,
purely combinatorial,
steps.

This done, he tried to apply this mechanism to the quintic only to discover
that the possibility of pushing it through to the production of general formulas
for the roots of the quintic appeared to lead to a contradiction!

In fact, he was (and he knew he was) within reach of proving the impossibility
of solving the quintic equation by radicals.

Around 1799 Ruffini tried to complete Lagranges proof and
although he was able to push the argument quite a bit further he nevertheless
was left with a hypothesis which he could not remove.

The glory of proving the impossibility of solving the general equation by radicals
was bestowed to Abel (for his 1826 paper) (see []) even though he was only concerned with the quintic and,
as in Ruffini’s work, there were still a number of gaps in his arguments. We shall not
deal with Abel’s work here since it it departs from the combinatorial approach proposed by Lagrange
and later completed by Galois. In fact, the missing step needed to complete Lagrange
argument and obtain the unsolvability of the quintic by radicals can be supplied by one single idea
of Galois.

To appreciate the beauty of Lagrange’s discoveries we should view his results in the original 1771 form.
Unfortunately, for clarity we must deviate a bit from Lagrange’s terminology.
For instance, although Lagrange proved that the order of a subgroup of a group
is a divisor of the order of the group, he had to do so in an indirect manner,
since the notion of a
group
in its present form really started with Galois.
Although using modern terminology distorts somewhat the historical perspective,
we will try as much as possible to keep unchanged the contents of Lagrange’s discoveries.
Our main goal in the next two sections is to present the basic theorems of what is now
referred to as
Galois
theory in a sequence that makes the transition from
Lagrange to Galois as natural and effortless as possible.

4. Lagrange’s “Galois” Theory

Throughout Lagrange’s work the roots of an equation

E_{n}(t)\enskip=\enskip a_{o}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}t^{n}\enskip=\enskip=0

are assumed to be independent variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and
E_{n}(t)
is written in the form

E_{n}(t)\enskip=\enskip(t-x_{1})(t-x_{2})\cdots(t-x_{n})

[formula]

The basic idea that led Lagrange to an
understanding
of the classical solutions of the
cubic and the quartic is a careful analysis of the effect that permutations of the roots
have on various rational functions of the roots.
To make precise what we mean by this we need some notation.

We are given a field
{\cal F}
which remains unchanged throughout, and for a function
\Phi
of the roots which
may be in
{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
or in
Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as needed, we set

G_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip\{\>\sigma\in S_{n}\>:\>\sigma\Phi=\Phi\>\}\enskip.

[formula]

Although Lagrange did not realize (nor did he need) that
G_{\Phi}
is a group, we shall not
ignore this fact here and obtain Lagrange’s results by standard present day techniques.
We recall that
G_{\Phi}
is usually referred to as the
stabilizer
of
\Phi
.

The first basic result of Lagrange can be stated as follows

Theorem 4.1

For
\Phi,\Psi\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
we have

G_{\Psi}\subseteq G_{\Phi}

[formula]

If and only if

\Phi\in Rat[{\cal F},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Psi]

Proof

If

\theta(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n},t)\enskip\in\enskip Rat[{\cal F},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n},t]

and

\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\enskip=\enskip\theta(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Psi)\enskip.

[formula]

Then we clearly have 4.3 since every permutation
\sigma
leaves
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
unchanged
and if
\sigma\in G_{\Psi}
then also
\Phi
does not change. So the condition in 4.3 is trivially necessary.

To show the converse, we resort to the left coset decomposition

S_{n}\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}\>G_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\tau_{2}\>G_{\Psi}\>+\cdots+\>\tau_{k}\>G_{\Psi}\enskip.\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\tau_{1}=identity\enskip)

[formula]

(which by the way, was a Lagrange invention) and set

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{i=1}^{k}\>\tau_{i}\Phi\>\prod_{j=1}^{k}\hskip-0.72229pt\ {}^{(i)}\>(t-\tau_{j}\Psi)\enskip.

Since any
\sigma\in S_{n}
permutes the left cosets of
G_{\Psi}
we may write

\sigma\>\tau_{i}\enskip=\enskip\tau_{\pi_{i}}h_{i}\hskip 36.135pt(\>h_{i}\in G_{\Psi}\>)

where the map
i\rightarrow\pi_{i}
is a permutation of
(1,2,\ldots,k)
. In particular, from
4.3 we deduce that
\sigma\>\Phi=\tau_{\pi_{i}}\Phi
, and thus we must have

\sigma Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{i=1}^{k}\>\tau_{\pi_{i}}\Phi\>\prod_{j=1}^{k}\hskip-0.72229pt\ {}^{(\pi_{i})}\>(t-\tau_{j}\Psi)\enskip=\enskip Q(t)\enskip.

This implies that the coefficients of
Q(t)
are in
Ratsym[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, so by Theorem 1.2 they
are in
Rat[{\cal F},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}]
. The same can be said about the polynomial

P(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{k}(t-\tau_{i}\Psi)\enskip.

[formula]

Now setting
t=\Psi
in
Q(t)
we get

Q(\Psi)\enskip=\enskip\Phi\>\prod_{j=1}^{k}\hskip-0.72229pt\ {}^{(1)}\>(\Psi-\tau_{j}\Psi)\enskip=\enskip\Phi\>P^{\prime}(\Psi)\enskip.

[formula]

Since, by construction, the values
\tau_{i}\Psi
(for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
) are all distinct we shall have

P^{\prime}(\Psi)\neq 0
and we can divide it out in 4.7 to obtain

\Phi\enskip=\enskip{Q(\Psi)\over P^{\prime}(\Psi)}\enskip=\enskip\theta(\Psi)

with

\theta(t)\enskip=\enskip{Q(t)\over P^{\prime}(t)}\enskip\in\enskip Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},t]

as desired.

Remark 4.1

If
G
is a group and
H\subseteq G
is a subgroup then the left coset decomposition

G\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}\>H\enskip+\enskip\tau_{2}\>H\enskip+\enskip\cdots\enskip+\enskip\tau_{m}\>H\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\tau_{1}=identity\enskip)

[formula]

yields that

If
H\subseteq G
then the order of
H
divides the order of
G

4.9

In fact, the equation above gives that
|G|/|H|=m
. This result, which appeared for the first time
in the work of Lagrange, was formulated there as a statement concerning the
number
of different values
taken by rational functions of the roots. More precisely, we can derive from 4.5 that
\Psi
is a
k
-valued
rational function of the roots if and only if
|S_{n}|/|G_{\Psi}|=k
. Similarly,
\Phi
is
h
-valued if and only
if
|S_{n}|/|G_{\Phi}|=h
. This gives that when
G_{\Psi}\subseteq G_{\Phi}
we have
k/h=|G_{\Phi}|/|G_{\Psi}|
.
We shall refer to
h
and
k
respectively as the
multiplicities
of
\Psi
and
\Phi
.
So taking
H=G_{\Psi}
and
G=G_{\Phi}
in 4.8 we get that
k=hm
. We see then that in Lagrange’s language
the statement in 4.9 becomes

If\enskip\Phi\enskip is\enskip a\enskip rational\enskip function\enskip of\enskip\Psi\enskip then\enskip the\enskip multiplicity\enskip of\enskip\Phi\enskip divides\enskip that\enskip of\enskip\Psi\enskip.

Lagrange’s proof was based precisely on the coset decomposition. Only he did not have to call it that way.
Indeed to get 4.8 for
H=G_{\Psi}
and
G=G_{\Phi}
all he had to do was
bunch together
the elements
of
G_{\Phi}
that yielded the same value of
\Psi
.

It will be good here and after for
H
a subgroup of
G
to express the fact that
m=|G|/|H|

by writing
H\subseteq_{m}G
. This given, these observations can be sharpened into the following corollary
of Theorem 4.1.

Theorem 4.2

If for
\Phi,\Psi\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
we have

G_{\Psi}\subseteq_{k}G_{\Phi}

[formula]

then
\Psi
satisfies an equation of degree
k
with coefficients in
Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]

which is irreducible in
Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]
.

Proof

Let

G_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}\>G_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\tau_{2}\>G_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\cdots\enskip+\enskip\tau_{k}\>G_{\Psi}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\tau_{1}=identity\enskip)

[formula]

and set

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{k}\>(t-\tau_{i}\>\Psi)\enskip=\enskip q_{o}(x)+q_{1}(x)\>t+\cdots+q_{k}(x)\>t^{k}\enskip.

[formula]

Since any
\sigma\in G_{\Phi}
permutes the left cosets of
G_{\Psi}
in 4.11 we may write

\sigma\>\tau_{i}\enskip=\enskip\tau_{\pi_{i}}h_{i}\hskip 36.135pt(\>h_{i}\in G_{\Psi}\>)

where the map
i\rightarrow\pi_{i}
is again a permutation of
(1,2,\ldots,k)
.
This gives that for all
\sigma\in G_{\Phi}
we have

\sigma Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{n}\>(t-\tau_{\pi_{i}}\Psi)\enskip=\enskip Q(t)\enskip.

Consequently each of the coefficients
q_{i}(x)
is left unchanged by the elements of
G_{\Phi}
.
From Theorem 4.1 we then derive that each
q_{i}(x)\in Rat[{\cal F},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]
. On the other
hand from 4.12 we get that

Q(\Psi)\enskip=\enskip q_{o}(x)+q_{1}(x)\Psi+\cdots+q_{k}(x)\Psi^{k}\enskip=\enskip 0\enskip.

Now suppose, if possible, that
Q(t)
has a factorisation
Q(t)=Q_{1}(t)Q_{2}(t)
where both polynomials

Q_{1}(t)
and
Q_{2}(t)
have coefficients in
Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]
It will then follow that
both of them will be invariant under the action of
G_{\Phi}
. In particular,
we must have

\tau_{i}(Q_{1}(\Psi))\enskip=\enskip Q_{1}(\tau_{i}\Psi)\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip for\enskip any\enskip\enskip i=1,2,\ldots,k\enskip)

So if
\Psi
is a root of the equation
Q_{1}(t)=0
then all the other roots of
Q(t)
must
satisfy it as well and
Q_{2}(t)
must reduce to a constant in
Rat[{\cal F},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]
. The
analogous conclusion holds if
Q_{2}(\Psi)=0
. Thus
Q(t)
is irreducible as asserted.
This completes our proof.

We have reached a point where to proceed further we need to make more precise
what we mean by
solving the general equation by radicals
. To begin with we
shall assume that the given field
{\cal F}
(nowdays referred to as the
ground field
)
contains all the roots of unity of any order
\leq n
. This given,

solving by radicals
the
n^{tic}
in 4.1,
in the Lagrange setting

is to mean that we can find a sequence of rational functions

\Phi_{i}\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
(
i=0,1,2,\ldots
) such that

\eqalign{\Phi_{o}&\>\in\>Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}]\cr\Phi_{i}&\enskip=\enskip{\root p_{i} \of{\theta_{i}(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi_{i-1})}}\cr}

[formula]

where each
\theta_{i}
is a rational function

\theta_{i}\enskip=\enskip\theta_{i}(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n},t)\enskip\in\enskip Rat[{\cal F},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n},t]\enskip.

[formula]

Finally, we shall require that the end function of this sequence say
\Phi_{d}
be one of the roots or
better yet (as we shall see) a function from which all the roots may be derived by rational
operations.

Note first that since for any two integers
p
and
q
and for any
\Phi
we have

{\root pq \of{\Phi}}\enskip=\enskip{\root p \of{\root q \of{\Phi}}}

there is no loss in requiring that the integers
p_{i}
in 4.13 are all primes.
Finally we can simplify the convoluted form of the recursion in 4.14
by rewriting it in the form

\eqalign{&a)\enskip\enskip\Phi_{o}\enskip\in Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}]\cr&b)\enskip\enskip\Phi_{i}^{p_{i}}\in Rat[{\cal F},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi_{i-1})\cr}

[formula]

Remark 4.3

Note further that as long as the coefficients
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}
are all
distinct the function

v(x)\enskip=\enskip v(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\enskip=\enskip m_{1}x_{1}+m_{2}x_{2}+\cdots+m_{n}x_{n}

[formula]

will necessarily be
n!
-valued. Since its stabilizer consists of just the identity permutation,
the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1 are satisfied for any rational function of the
roots
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
. Thus for any
\Phi\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
we can construct a rational function

\theta_{\Phi}(t)\in Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},t]
giving

\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\enskip=\enskip\theta_{\Phi}\bigl(v(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\bigr)\enskip.

[formula]

We should note here for further reference that the proof of Theorem 4.1 yields that

\theta_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip{Q_{\Phi}(t)\over P^{\prime}(t)}

[formula]

where

Q_{\Phi}(t)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\>\sigma\Phi\prod_{\tau\in S_{n}}\hskip-0.72229pt^{(\sigma)}\>(t-\tau v)

[formula]

and

P(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{\tau\in S_{n}}(t-\tau v)\enskip.

[formula]

It is important to notice that the denominator of
\theta_{\Phi}
in 4.18 is independent of

\Phi
itself.

5. Lagrange’s derivation of the roots of the cubic and the quartic.

Armed with this information, Lagrange could then come up with the following a-priori reconstruction
of the solutions of the cubic and the quartic.

a)
The cubic

Let
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
and note that, since
1,\omega,\omega^{2}
are distinct, the expression

(x_{1}+\omega x_{2}+\omega^{2}x_{3})/3

is necessarily a
6
-valued function of the roots of
E_{3}(t)
. Its values are

\matrix{z_{1}=(x_{1}+\omega x_{2}+\omega^{2}x_{3})/3\enskip\enskip&z_{2}=(x_{1}+\omega x_{3}+\omega^{2}x_{2})/3\enskip\enskip\cr z_{3}=(x_{2}+\omega x_{3}+\omega^{2}x_{1})/3\enskip\enskip&z_{4}=(x_{2}+\omega x_{1}+\omega^{2}x_{3})/3\enskip\enskip\cr z_{5}=(x_{3}+\omega x_{1}+\omega^{2}x_{2})/3\enskip\enskip&z_{6}=(x_{3}+\omega x_{2}+\omega^{2}x_{1})/3\enskip\enskip\cr}\enskip.

[formula]

Now we see that

z_{3}=\omega^{2}z_{1}\enskip\>,\>\enskip z_{5}=\omega z_{1}\enskip\enskip\enskip{\rm and}\enskip\enskip\enskip z_{4}=\omega z_{2}\enskip\>,\>\enskip z_{6}=\omega^{2}z_{2}\enskip.

Thus

\eqalign{(z-z_{1})(z-z_{3})(z-z_{5})&\enskip=\enskip z^{3}-z_{1}^{3}\cr(z-z_{2})(z-z_{4})(z-z_{6})&\enskip=\enskip z^{3}-z_{2}^{3}\cr}

This implies that the
6
-degree equation

(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})(z-z_{3})(z-z_{4})(z-z_{5})(z-z_{6})\enskip=\enskip 0

Remark 5.1

It is interesting in this connection to observe that there is a neat
representation theoretical reason why there is no
k
-valued function
of the roots of
E_{n}(t)
when
2<k<n
and
n\geq 5
. In fact,
the action of
S_{n}
on the stabilizer of any
k
-valued function
\Phi
induces
a permutation representation with only one occurrence of the trivial.
If the remaining irreducible constituents are all sign representations then
\Phi
is only
2
valued.
So for
k>2
this representation would have to have a irreducible constituent of degree

\geq 2
. But for
n\geq 5
except for the trivial and the sign representation all
the other irreducible representations have dimension
\geq n-1
.
So
k\leq 2
or
k\geq n
. At any rate we can also give this result an elementary proof.

It is important to see at this point what are the implications of the existence of
a sequence of functions
\Phi_{i}
satisfying 5.14 and 5.15. To this end we terminate
this section with a collection of results which shed considerable light on the
difficulties encountered by Lagrange in completing his program.
We should note that although some of the arguments that follow use the “group”
structure of stabilizers, which is one of the main discoveries of Galois, most
of the calculations are actually due to Lagrange. This apparent paradox
illustrates in a remarkable way how close Lagrange got to discover some of
the main points of “Galois” theory.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be in
Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}]
. Suppose that

\eqalign{&a)\enskip\enskip G_{\Psi}\subseteq G_{\Phi}\enskip\enskip but\enskip\enskip G_{\Psi}\neq G_{\Phi}\enskip\enskip,and\cr&b)\enskip\enskip\Psi^{p}\in Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]\cr}

[formula]

is prime. Then the decomposition of

into left cosets
of
G_{\Psi}
may be written in the form

G_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip G_{\Psi}+\gamma G_{\Psi}+\gamma^{2}G_{\Psi}+\cdots+\gamma^{p-1}G_{\Psi}

[formula]

with
\gamma
a
p
-cycle that commutes with
G_{\Psi}
. Moreover, the
conjugates of
\Psi
in
G_{\Phi}
can be written in the form

\Psi_{i}=\gamma^{i-1}\Psi=\omega^{i-1}\Psi\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip with\enskip\enskip\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}\enskip)

[formula]

Proof

Since by assumption
G_{\Psi}\neq G_{\Phi}
we have

G_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}G_{\Psi}+\tau_{2}G_{\Psi}+\cdots+\tau_{k}G_{\Psi}\enskip.

with

. Set
\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}
and let

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\>(t-\omega^{i}\Psi)\enskip.

[formula]

From 5.16 b) we deduce that

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip t^{p}\enskip-\enskip R(x)

with
R(x)\in Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Phi]
. In particular for any
\sigma\in G_{\Phi}
we must have

\sigma Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\>(t-\omega^{i}\sigma\Psi)\enskip=\enskip Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\>(t-\omega^{i}\Psi)\enskip.

and this gives

\sigma\Psi\enskip=\enskip\omega^{i(\sigma)}\>\Psi

[formula]

for some
0\leq i(\sigma)\leq p-1
. This implies that the conjugates of
\Psi

are all multiples of
\Psi
by powers of
\omega
. Thus

k\leq p\enskip.

Now
k>1
gives that
\tau_{2}\Psi\neq\Psi
so
\enskip i(\tau_{2})\neq 0\enskip(mod\enskip p)
.
But since
p
is
prime, the successive powers

for

in some order.
Thus

\Psi,\tau_{2}\Psi,\tau_{2}^{2}\Psi\ldots,\tau_{2}^{p-1}\Psi

are all distinct. This gives

p\leq k\enskip

Thus

must be equal to
p
and

\tau_{2}^{p}=id\enskip.

Moreover, we could have indexed our
coset representatives
\tau_{i}
to be successive powers of an element
\gamma\in G_{\Phi}

for which
\gamma\Psi=\omega\Psi
. This gives 5.17. With this choice we have

\Psi_{i}\enskip=\enskip\gamma^{i}\Psi\enskip=\enskip\omega^{i-1}\>\Psi\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip i=1,2,\ldots,p\enskip)

Finally, we see that
\gamma\Psi=\omega\Psi
gives

\gamma^{-1}h\gamma\>\Psi\enskip=\enskip\Psi\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\forall\enskip\enskip h\in G_{\Psi})\enskip.

In other words

\gamma^{-1}\>G_{\Psi}\gamma\enskip=\enskip G_{\Psi}

[formula]

This completes our proof.

Remark 5.2

Note that we have

We can thus state

Theorem 5.2
( Ruffini)

For
n\geq 5
the general equation
E_{n}(t)=0
cannot be solved by radicals
by successive adjunctions of rational functions
\Phi_{i}\in Rat[{\cal F},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
satisfying the

We should note that this result doesn’t quite put to rest the possibility of solving
the general equation by adjoining “radicals”. This is because
there are still some unjustified hypotheses in the present setting.
First of all, in our original definition (see 4.15) of
solution by radicals

we did not have the extra condition 5.15. As we shall see this is a relatively
minor point, easily fixed in the Galois setting. Considerably more troubling is that
in this definition we require that each newly constructed
\Phi_{i}
should turn out to
be a
rational function in the roots
. What if we allow such steps as taking a
p^{th}

root of
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
? Can we then construct a solution? These doubts would
be removed if we could show that the existence of a sequence of steps as in
5.14, 5.15 without the further assumption that
\Phi_{i}\in Rat[{\cal F},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
implies the existence
of the same sequence of steps with this assumption
satisfied
.
This is in essence what Abel succeeded in showing. We shall not present Abel’s arguments
here since what must be proved to remove the remaining doubts can be done in an easier
way in the Galois setting. We shall thus terminate our treatment of Lagrange’s
“Galois” Theory
with one final result which in some sense reverses Theorem 5.1.

Set

\gamma\Theta\enskip=\enskip\omega^{-1}\Theta\enskip.

[formula]

Moreover, 5.26 gives that each of the conjugates

has the same stabilizer as
\Psi
. Thus from the definition 5.28 we get that

G_{\Theta}\supseteq G_{\Psi}

[formula]

and therefore (again by Theorem 4.1) we must have

\Theta\in Rat[{\cal F};e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n},\Psi]

Finally, 5.30 together with 5.25 and 5.26 implies that the polynomial

Q(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{p}(t-\gamma^{i-1}\Theta)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{p}(t-\Theta/\omega^{i-1})\enskip=\enskip t^{p}-\Theta^{p}

[formula]

is left invariant by every element of
G_{\Phi}
. Thus from Theorem 4.1
(with
\Psi\rightarrow\Phi
and
\Phi\rightarrow\Theta

Remark 5.3

Nevertheless, in a typical application of Theorem 5.3 we may want to obtain

6. Galoisian “Galois Theory.

Lagrange’s pursuits were brought to a conclusion by Abel around 1829 when Galois started his
investigations. Galois’ starting point may have been the idea that although there may be
no general formulas for the roots of
E_{n}(t)
for
n\geq 5
which involved only extraction of roots,
it may still be possible to find them for some special equations. This possibility had already
emerged in the work of Gauss and Abel but it is not clear to what extent Galois had been aware of
their work. We may say that his discoveries stemmed from a natural extension of Lagrange’s original methods.
To present Galois’
results, we shall need some additional notation and definitions.

Our basic ingredients here will be two fields
{\cal F}
and
{\cal E}
, with
{\cal F}
a proper subfield of
{\cal E}
.
The equation to be solved will be written as

{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)\enskip=\enskip(t-\alpha_{1})(t-\alpha_{2})\cdots(t-\alpha_{n})\enskip=\enskip 0\enskip.

[formula]

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are distinct and, unless explicitely mentioned, will remain unchanged
throughout our presentation. We shall also assume hereafter that

\eqalign{&1)\enskip\enskip\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\enskip\in\enskip{\cal E}\enskip\enskip{\rm and}\cr&2)\enskip\enskip e_{1}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}),e_{2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}),\ldots,e_{n}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\enskip\in\enskip{\cal F}\cr}

[formula]

We see that we shall have to work here with functions
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

of the independent variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and at the same study their values when
when
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
are replaced by
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
. As clarity requires, these values will
be represented by any of the symbols below

\Phi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\enskip=\enskip\Phi(\alpha)\enskip=\enskip{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip.

[formula]

For a given
\sigma\in S_{n}
we shall also use the symbols

\sigma\Phi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\enskip=\enskip\sigma\Phi(\alpha)\enskip=\enskip\sigma{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip

to denote the value
\Phi(\alpha_{\sigma_{1}},\alpha_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,\alpha_{\sigma_{n}})
.

This given, one of the fundamental differences between the Galoisian and Lagrangian setups
is that although a given
\Phi\in Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and all its images
\sigma\Phi
are well defined
as elements of
Rat[{\cal F};x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
some of the values
\sigma{\tilde{\Phi}}
may make no sense at at all.
An example in point is the rational function

\Phi(x)\enskip=\enskip{1\over x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}}

when
n=3
and
\alpha_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{3}^{2}-\alpha_{1}^{2}=0
.

In summary in the Galois setting, we have to be careful with denominators! We shall avoid the
problem by dealing hereafter only with polynomials functions of the roots. As we shall see this is not a serious
restriction, and with it, most of the results of Lagrange Theory can be extended to the Galois
setting with nearly identical proofs.

Another important difference is that for some
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
there may be more permutations
of the roots of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)
that leave
\Phi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
unchanged than there are in
G_{\Phi}
.
We shall take account of this difference by setting for
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

S_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip\{\sigma\in S_{n}\enskip:\enskip\Phi(\alpha_{\sigma_{1}},\alpha_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,\alpha_{\sigma_{n}})=\Phi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\enskip\}

[formula]

Note that
S_{\Phi}
should be considered a property of
\Phi
and not a property of the value
\Phi(\alpha)
.
Moreover we should point out that in general this collection of permutations may not even be a group!
For instance for the equation

{\tilde{E}}_{3}(t)\enskip=\enskip\enskip=\enskip(t+1)(t+i)(t-i)\enskip=\enskip t^{3}+t^{2}+t+1\enskip,

we can take
{\cal F}
and
{\cal E}
to be the fields of rational and complex numbers respectively. Now,
if we label the roots by setting

-1=\alpha_{1}\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip i=\alpha_{2}\enskip\enskip,\enskip\enskip-i=\alpha_{3}

then for
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\enskip=\enskip x_{2}^{2}

we have (in cycle notation)

G_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip\{id\>,\>(1,3)\}\enskip.

On the other hand the permutations of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
that leave
{\tilde{\Phi}}
unchanged form the set

\{id\>,\>(1,3)\>,\>(2,3)\>,\>(1,2,3)\}\enskip

This is not a group since
(1,2)=(1,2,3)(2,3)
, yet

\Phi(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=\alpha_{2}=-1\enskip\enskip{\rm and}\enskip\enskip\Phi(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3})=\alpha_{1}^{2}=1\enskip.

To take account of this possibility we shall say that a given
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is
Galois
if and only if

S_{\Phi}\enskip=\enskip G_{\Phi}

We are now in a position to proceed with our treatment.

Theorem 6.1

For every subgroup
G\subseteq S_{n}
we have a Galois
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that

S_{\Phi}=G\enskip.

[formula]

Proof

We start with the case
G=S_{n}
. Here can take again a linear function

v(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\enskip=\enskip m_{1}x_{1}+m_{2}x_{2}+\cdots+m_{n}x_{n}

[formula]

as in 4.16, but we must be a bit more careful in choosing the coefficients

. To this end note that
if
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}
are chosen to be integers in the interval

for any given pair of distinct permutations

the equation

Thus to assure that

we need to avoid at most

However, when

there will remain some

for us to choose and satisfy our

. Having made one such choice of

[formula]

into left cosets of

solutions. Thus if we want an integer

which

in the interval

as soon as

.
This completes our argument.

Remark 6.1

All the constructions, proofs and definitions in this section will use an

G_{{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)}({\cal F}_{1})\subseteq G_{{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)}({\cal F})
.

In trying to extend Lagrange’s Theorems 4.1
\&
4.2 to the Galois setting we should be tempted to let the
Galois group
G
play the role of
S_{n}
in the arguments. However, given a polynomial
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]

we may not be in a position to write down the coset decomposition in 4.5 with
S_{n}
replaced by
G

for the simple reason that we may not have
G_{\Psi}\subseteq G
. Using
G_{\Psi}\cap G
in place of
G_{\Psi}

doesn’t get us anywhere for the simple reason that the intersection
G_{\Psi}\cap G
may only consist of
the identity permutation. Nor we can use
S_{\Psi}
instead of
G_{\Psi}
for it may be too big
and as we have seen it may not even be a group. It develops that the optimal choice turns out to be the intersection

S_{\Psi}\cap G
which here and after will be denoted by
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
and referred to as the
Galois stabilizer
of

\Psi
. In fact,
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
is neither too small nor to big and remarkably it can be easily shown that

Theorem 6.3

For any polynomial
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
the Galois stabilizer

{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip=\enskip S_{\Psi}\cap G

is a group

Proof

Since by construction
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
contains the identity, we need only show that

\eta,\xi\in{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\>\Longrightarrow\>\eta^{-1}\xi\in{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip.

[formula]

However, this is immediate. In fact,
\eta,\xi\in{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
implies that

\xi{\tilde{\Psi}}-\eta{\tilde{\Psi}}=0\>\in{\cal F}\enskip.

But since
G\in{\cal B}
we must necessarily have

S_{\xi\Psi-\eta\Psi}\supseteq G

In other words

\gamma\xi{\tilde{\Psi}}-\gamma\eta{\tilde{\Psi}}\enskip=\enskip 0\enskip.\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\forall\enskip\enskip\gamma\in G\enskip)

In particular this must hold true for
\gamma=\eta^{-1}
. That is

\eta^{-1}\xi{\tilde{\Psi}}\enskip=\enskip{\tilde{\Psi}}\enskip,

which gives 6.22 as desired.

We are now in a position to state and prove the Galois version of Theorems 4.1 and 4.2.

Theorem 6.4

If
\Psi\&\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and

{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\subseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Phi}

[formula]

then we can construct a polynomial
\Theta(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
which gives

\gamma{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip=\enskip\Theta(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\forall\enskip\enskip\gamma\in G\enskip)

[formula]

Proof

Let

G\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}\>{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\tau_{2}\>{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\cdots\enskip+\enskip\tau_{k}\>{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\tau_{1}=identity\enskip)

[formula]

and set

A(t)\enskip=\enskip\sum_{i=1}^{k}\>\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Phi}}\>\prod_{j=1}^{k}\hskip-0.72229pt\ {}^{(i)}\>(t-\tau_{j}{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip\enskip\>,\>\enskip\enskip B(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{k}\>(t-\tau_{i}\>{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip.

[formula]

Now from 6.23 and 6.25 we get that
A(t)
and
B(t)
are
G
-invariant, and

G\in{\cal A}
gives that

A(t)\>\&\>B(t)\in{\cal F}[t]\enskip.

Since by construction
B(t)
has distinct roots we can find two polynomials
p(t),q(t)\in{\cal F}[t]

such that

p(t)B^{\prime}(t)+q(t)B(t)\enskip=\enskip 1\enskip.

Setting
t=\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}}
gives

p(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})B^{\prime}(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip 1

[formula]

On the other hand we see from 6.26 that if
\gamma=\tau_{i}h
with
h\in{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
then

A(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip A(\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Phi}}\>B^{\prime}(\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip.

Using 6.23 we may rewrite this as

A(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip\gamma{\tilde{\Phi}}\>B^{\prime}(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip.

Multiplying both sides by
p(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})
and using 6.27 we finally get

\gamma{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip=\enskip p(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})A(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})

which is 6.24 with

\Theta(t)\enskip=\enskip p(t)A(t)\enskip.

This Theorem has an immediate consequence which can be helpful in the construction of the Galois group
of an equation.

Corollary 6.1

Let
G
be the Galois group of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
and suppose that for
some
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
we have

\eqalign{&(1)\enskip\enskip S_{\Psi}=G_{\Psi}\enskip,\cr&(2)\enskip\enskip{\tilde{\Psi}}\in{\cal F}\enskip.\cr}

Then we must necessarily have

G\>\subseteq\>G_{\Psi}\enskip.

Proof

In view of Theorem 6.2 we need only show that
G_{\Psi}\in{\cal A}({\cal F})
. To this end let

\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and let
S_{\Phi}\supseteq G_{\Psi}
. Condition
(1)
then assures that
{\tilde{G}}_{\Phi}\supseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
.
We can thus use Theorem 6.4 and derive that for some
\theta(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
we have

{\tilde{\Phi}}=\theta({\tilde{\Psi}})
. But then condition
(2)
yields us that
{\tilde{\Phi}}\in{\cal F}
as desired.

Before we can proceed any further we need to establish the following basic fact

Proposition 6.3

Let
B(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
be a polynomial of degree
k
which is irreducible in
{\cal F}[t]
, and let

B({\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip 0

for some
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Then the values

1\>,\>{\tilde{\Psi}}\>,\>{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}\>,\>\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}^{k-1}

[formula]

form a basis of a vector space
V
over
{\cal F}
which is also a field

Proof

Suppose that for some
c_{o},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k-1}\in{\cal F}
not all vanishing we had

c_{o}+c_{1}{\tilde{\Psi}}+c_{2}{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}+\cdots+c_{k-1}{\tilde{\Psi}}^{k-1}\enskip=\enskip 0

then the polynomial
R(t)=c_{o}+c_{1}t+c_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+c_{k-1}t^{k-1}
would have a root in common
with
B(t)
and the greatest common divisor of
R(t)
and
B(t)
would yield a non trivial
factorization of
B(t)
in
{\cal F}[T]
contradicting the irreducibility of
B(t)
.
This shows that the elements in 6.28 are independent over
{\cal F}
.

To complete the proof we need to show that every non vanishing element
v
of
V
has an inverse in
V
.
Now such an element would be given by a linear combination

v\enskip=\enskip c_{o}+c_{1}{\tilde{\Psi}}+c_{2}{\tilde{\Psi}}^{2}+\cdots+c_{k-1}{\tilde{\Psi}}^{k-1}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip c_{i}\in{\cal F}\enskip)

with some
c_{i}\neq 0
. For the same reasons as above, the polynomial
R(t)=c_{o}+c_{1}t+c_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+c_{k-1}t^{k-1}

cannot have any root in common with
B(t)
. Thus we can use the Berlekamp algorithm and construct two polynomials

p(t),q(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
giving

p(t)R(t)+q(t)B(t)\enskip=\enskip 1\enskip.

Setting

we get

p({\tilde{\Psi}})\>R({\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip 1\enskip,

which shows that
R({\tilde{\Psi}})
is invertible and that its inverse in
V
is given by
p({\tilde{\Psi}})
.

Q.E.D.

The vector space
V
will here and after be denoted by
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
and referred to as the
Extension of
{\cal F}

by
{\tilde{\Psi}}
. The integer
k
giving the dimension of
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
will be called the
degree
of
the extension. We shall also say that
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
is obtained by adjoining
{\tilde{\Psi}}
to
{\cal F}
.
The following theorem provides the crucial tools needed in the applications of Galois theory
to the theory of equations.

Theorem 6.5

Let
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, set

G\enskip=\enskip\tau_{1}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip+\enskip\tau_{2}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}+\cdots+\tau_{k}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\tau_{1}=id\>)

[formula]

and let
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1}=\tau_{1}{\tilde{\Psi}},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2}=\tau_{2}{\tilde{\Psi}},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}=\tau_{k}{\tilde{\Psi}}
denote the conjugates of
{\tilde{\Psi}}
in
G
.
Then

(i)
The polynomial
B(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\>(t-{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i})
is irreducible in
{\cal F}[t]
.

(ii)
By adjoining
{\tilde{\Psi}}
to
{\cal F}
the Galois group of the equation

{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)=0
is reduced to
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
.

(iii)
The Galois group of
B(t)
is the subgroup
\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}
of
S_{k}
corresponding to the action of
G
on the left
cosets of
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
. In particular we have the isomorphism

\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}\>\cong G\>/\>\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\>\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}^{-1}\enskip.

[formula]

(iv)
Set
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
and let
Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})
denote the group of automorphisms of
{\cal F}_{1}

that leave
{\cal F}
elementwise fixed. This given,

Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})
can be identified with the set
\{\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}:\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}\>\}
. In
particular we have the isomorphism

Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})\>\cong\Bigl(\sum_{\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}}\enskip\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\Bigr)/{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}

[formula]

Proof

Proof of (i)

Suppose that a polynomial

B_{1}(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
divides
B(t)
and shares the root
\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}}
with
B(t)
. Then the polynomial

B_{1}(\tau_{i}\Psi)\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
has the value
B_{1}(\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}})=0\in{\cal F}
and
G\in{\cal B}
gives that

S_{B_{1}(\tau_{i}\Psi)}\supseteq G
. In other words we must have
B_{1}(\gamma\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}})=0
for all
\gamma\in G
.
Since the action of
G
on
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}
is transitive, we see that

B_{1}(t)
has to vanish at all the roots of
B(t)
, which forces
B_{1}(t)=B(t)
. Thus

B(t)
can’t have a proper factor in
{\cal F}[t]
.

Proof of (ii)

Since
B({\tilde{\Psi}})=0
we can use Proposition 6.3 to construct the field
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
.
This given we need only verify that

{\cal A}({\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}])\cap{\cal B}({\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}])\enskip=\enskip\{{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\}\enskip.

Now this is immediate. In fact, if for some
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
we have
S_{\Phi}\supseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}

then we must have
{\tilde{G}}_{\Phi}\supseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
as well and Theorem 6.4 gives that for some
\Theta(t)\in{\cal F}[t]

{\tilde{\Phi}}=\Theta({\tilde{\Psi}})\>\in{\cal F}[\Psi]\enskip.

Thus
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
is in
{\cal A}({\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}])
.

Conversely let
\Phi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
{\tilde{\Phi}}\in{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
. This means that for some
\Theta(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
we have

{\tilde{\Phi}}=\Theta({\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip.

In other words for the polynomial
\Xi(x)=\Phi(x)-\Theta(\Psi(x))\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
we have

{\tilde{\Xi}}=0\>\in{\cal F}

so from
G\in{\cal B}({\cal F})
we get that we must have

\gamma{\tilde{\Phi}}-\Theta(\gamma{\tilde{\Psi}})=\gamma{\tilde{\Xi}}\enskip=\enskip 0\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip\forall\enskip\enskip\gamma\in G\enskip)

However, for
\gamma\in{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
this yields that

\gamma{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip=\enskip\Theta({\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip{\tilde{\Phi}}\enskip.

In other words
{\tilde{\Phi}}\in{\cal F}[\Psi]
implies that
S_{\Phi}\supseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
, which gives
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\in{\cal B}({\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}])

as desired. This completes the proof of
(ii)
.

Proof of (iii)

Let
\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}
be the image of
G
in the symmetric group
S_{k}
given by the permutation action of
G

on the left cosets of
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
. We want to show that

{\cal A}_{B}({\cal F})\cap{\cal B}_{B}({\cal F})\enskip=\enskip\{\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}\}\enskip.

[formula]

To this end let
\Phi\in{\cal F}[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k}]
and suppose that for any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}
we have

\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{1}},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{2}},\cdots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{k}})\enskip=\enskip\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})\enskip.

[formula]

Since each
g\in G
induces a permutation of
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}
by an element

\gamma\in\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}
, 6.32 implies that

S_{\Phi(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\ldots,\Psi_{k})}\>\supseteq\>G\enskip.

But then
G\in{\cal A}_{{\tilde{E}}_{n}}({\cal F})
gives that
\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})\in{\cal F}
. Thus
\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}\in{\cal A}_{B}({\cal F})
.

Conversely, say
\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})=a\in{\cal F}
. Then
G\in{\cal B}_{{\cal E}_{n}}({\cal F})
gives
that
g\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})=\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})
for all
g\in G
. But if

\gamma=\gamma(g)
is the image of
g
in
S_{k}
, this simply says that

\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{1}},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{2}},\cdots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\gamma_{1}})\enskip=\enskip\Phi({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k})\enskip.

This gives
\Gamma_{\tilde{\Psi}}\in{\cal B}_{B}({\cal F})
. To complete the proof of
(iii)
we need only observe that the
Galois stabilizer of a conjugate
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i}
is simply the conjugate subgroup
\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}^{-1}
,
thus the only elements of
g
that leave invariant all the conjugates of
{\tilde{\Psi}}
are those that belong to
the intersection

\cap_{i=1}^{k}\>\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}^{-1}\enskip.

This yields 6.30.

Proof of (iv)

We assume here that
{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
as before and that
{\cal E}={\cal F}[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
.
Note that since every element of
{\cal F}_{1}
is of the form

\theta({\tilde{\Psi}})\enskip=\enskip c_{o}+c_{1}{\tilde{\Psi}}+\cdots+c_{k-1}{\tilde{\Psi}}^{k-1}\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip c_{i}\in{\cal F}\enskip)

[formula]

with
k
the index of
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
in
G
, to find the image by a
g\in Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})

of any element of
{\cal F}_{1}
we need only know
g{\tilde{\Psi}}
. This is because, every
c_{i}
remaining
unchanged by
g
we must necessarily have
g\theta({\tilde{\Psi}})=\theta(g{\tilde{\Psi}})
. Moreover, since

B(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(t-{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i})\in{\cal F}[t]
, the identity
B({\tilde{\Psi}})=0
forces
B(g{\tilde{\Psi}})=0

as well. In particular, we deduce that
g{\tilde{\Psi}}={\tilde{\Psi}}_{i}=\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}}
for some

i=1,2,\ldots,k
. In addition
g{\tilde{\Psi}}\in{\cal F}_{1}
yields that we must have
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i}=\tau_{i}{\tilde{\Psi}}=\theta({\tilde{\Psi}})

with
\theta(t)\in{\cal F}
. But this gives that
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\subseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{i}}
and since

{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{i}}=\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}^{-1}
the latter inclusion can hold true if and only if

{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{i}}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip.

Thus we see that the elements of
Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})
can be simply identified with the left cosets

\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
such that
\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\tau_{i}
. This gives 6.31 and completes the proof of the Theorem.

We should note that the argument used in the proof of part
(i)
of this Theorem leads to
the following basic property of the Galois group of an equation.

Proposition 6.4

The polynomial
B(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-\alpha_{i})\in{\cal F}[t]
is irreducible in
{\cal F}[t]
if and only
if its Galois group
G=G_{B}({\cal F})
acts transitively on
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
.

Proof

Let
B_{1}(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
be an irreducible factor of
B(t)
. If
\alpha_{i}
is any of the roots of
B_{1}(t)
then

B_{1}(\alpha_{i})=0\in{\cal F}
and the fact that
G\in{\cal A}_{B}({\cal F})
gives that
B_{1}(\alpha_{\gamma_{i}})=0

for all
\gamma\in G
. But then the transitivity of
G
yields that
B_{1}(t)
can’t be a proper factor of
B(t)
.
Conversely suppose that
G
is intransitive. This means that the orbit of any of the roots, say
\alpha_{1}

can’t consist of all the roots of
B(t)
. Denote this orbit by
Orb(\alpha_{1})
and set

B_{1}(t)=\prod_{\alpha_{i}\in Orb(\alpha_{1})}(t-\alpha_{i})\enskip.

Since
B_{1}(t)
, by construction is invariant under
G
then
G\in{\cal A}_{B}({\cal F})
gives that

B_{1}(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
. Moreover, also by construction,
B_{1}(t)
does not contain all then roots of
B(t)
.
Thus
B_{1}(t)
is necessarily a proper factor of
B(t)
and the latter must therefore be reducible
in
{\cal F}[t]
.

Remark 6.3

We should note that parts
(i)
,
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Theorem 6.5 are Galois’ fundamental breakthroughs
in the theory of equations. In the original Galois context groups came first and fields were only accessories.
In later interpretations and additions to Galois’ work started by Kronecker [], brought to completion by
Dedekind [] and Weber and popularized by E. Artin [],[], this viewpoint has been reversed and
Galois theory was made to become part of the theory of fields. In particular,
part
(iv)
of Theorem 6.5 in its present interpretation is a later addition.

Since our presentation of Galois theory differs from most available textbooks on the subject, perhaps a few
words might be needed to connect this writing to present day literature. For instance in Artin’s monograph []
an extension field
{\cal F}_{1}
of a field
{\cal F}
is called a
normal
extension if
the group of
automorphisms of
{\cal F}_{1}
that leave
{\cal F}
fixed has
{\cal F}
for its fixed field
. We should note that in []
a field
{\cal F}
is said to be
fixed
by an automorphism
g
if
g
fixes
every
element of
{\cal F}
.
We see then that one of our fields
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}[\Psi]
is a normal extension of
{\cal F}
if and only if the only
elements of
{\cal F}_{1}
that remain fixed under every element of
Aut_{{\cal F}}({\cal F}_{1})
(as given by 6.31)
are the elements of
{\cal F}
itself.

This given, we can easily convert Theorem 6.5 into the collection of results that in [] is referred to
as the
Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory
.

Theorem 6.6

Let
G
be the Galois group of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-\alpha_{i})
in
{\cal F}
and
let
{\cal E}={\cal F}[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
. Then

(i)
Each subgroup
G_{1}\subseteq G
is the Galois group of
E_{n}(t)
with respect
to an intermediate field
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}]
. Different groups
G_{1},G_{2}
corresponding to
different fields
{\cal F}_{1},{\cal F}_{2}
.

(ii)
The subgroup
G_{1}
is a normal subgroup of
G
if and only if the corresponding field

{\cal F}_{1}
is a normal extension of
{\cal F}
. In that case the group of automorphisms of
{\cal F}_{1}
that
leave
{\cal F}
fixed is isomorphic to the quotient group
G/G_{1}
.

(iii)
For each
G_{1}\subseteq G
the dimension of
{\cal F}_{1}
over
{\cal F}
is
|G|/|G_{1}|
and the
dimension of
{\cal E}
over
{\cal F}_{1}
is
|G_{1}|
.

Proof

proof of (i)

We have seen (Theorem 6.1) that given any subgroup
G_{1}\subseteq G
we can find
\Psi\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}=G_{1}
.
From Theorem 6.5 we get that the Galois group of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}
in
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}[\Psi]
is
G_{1}
. If for
G_{1},G_{2}\subseteq G

we have
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{1}}=G_{1}
,
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{2}}=G_{2}
then
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1}]\subseteq{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2}]
gives
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1}\theta({\tilde{\Psi}}_{2})

with
\theta(t)\in{\cal F}[t]
thus also
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{2}}\subseteq{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{1}}
so the equality
{\cal F}[\Psi_{1}]={\cal F}[\Psi_{2}]

forces the equality
G_{1}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{1}}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{2}}=G_{2}
. This proves
(i)
.

proof of (ii)

Suppose that
G_{1}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
is not a normal subgroup of
G
. Then by relabeling the elements
\tau_{i}

appearing in 6.29 so that 6.34 may be rewritten as

Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})\enskip=\enskip\sum_{i=1}^{s}\>\tau_{i}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}\enskip\hskip 36.135pt(\enskip for\enskip\enskip s<k\enskip)

Now note that the polynomial

B_{1}(t)\enskip=\enskip\prod_{i=1}^{s}(t-{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i})\in{\cal F}_{1}[t]

cannot be in
{\cal F}[t]
. This is clear since otherwise the irreducibility of
B(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(t-{\tilde{\Psi}}_{i})

(Theorem 6.5
(i)
) would be contraddicted. In particular one of the elementary symmetric functions

must take a value
e_{i}({\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{s})
not in
{\cal F}
. However,
since the latter is invariant under permutations of
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{1},{\tilde{\Psi}}_{2},\ldots,{\tilde{\Psi}}_{s}
we see (from 6.31) that we
have a element of
{\cal F}_{1}
that remains invariant under all elements of
Aut_{\cal F}({\cal F}_{1})
. So

{\cal F}_{1}
in this case is not a normal extension of
{\cal F}
.
Conversely suppose that
G_{1}={\tilde{G}}_{\Psi}
is a normal subgroup of

Thirty years before Galois and ninety years before the concept of field was introduced and
developped by Dedekind [], Gauss, essentially showed [] that all roots of unity can be
obtained by a sequence of
normal field extensions
, in fact by
tight root extractions
in our
terminology. His proof his algorithmic and is therefore
very much in style with present preferences for explicit constructions. This given, we would be amiss
not presenting it here. Gauss’ argument relies on a family of remarkably beautiful identities.
They may be stated as follows

Lemma 7.1
(Gauss)

be a prime, let

and

. For
a given factorization

[formula]

[formula]

Then for any pair

we have

\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{r}})\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{s}})\enskip=\enskip\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\>\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{r}+e^{s+ia}})\enskip.

[formula]

Proof

Note first that, for the purpose of establishing 7.28,
the polynomial
\Pi_{a}(t)
is essentially invariant under the substitution

.
More precisely, since

modulo

\Pi_{a}(t^{e^{a}})\enskip=\enskip t^{e^{a}}+t^{e^{2a}}+\cdots+t^{e^{ba}}\enskip=\enskip\Pi_{a}(t)\hskip 36.135pt(\>mod\enskip t^{p}-1\>)

[formula]

This given we have

\eqalign{\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{r}})\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{s}})&=\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\>\bigl(w^{e^{r}}\bigr)^{e^{ja}}\>\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{s}})=\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\>\bigl(w^{e^{r}}\bigr)^{e^{ja}}\>\Pi_{a}\bigl((w^{e^{s}})^{e^{ja}}\bigr)\cr&=\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\>\bigl(w^{e^{r}}\bigr)^{e^{ja}}\>\bigl((w^{e^{s}})^{e^{ja}}\bigr)^{e^{ia}}=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\>\bigl(w^{e^{r}}\bigr)^{e^{ja}}\>\bigl((w^{e^{s+ia}}\bigr)^{e^{ja}}\cr&=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\>\Pi_{a}(w^{e^{r}+e^{s+ia}})\enskip.\cr}

Theorem 7.2

Every cyclic equation can be solved by a sequence of tight and natural
radical extractions

Proof

Suppose that
{\tilde{E}}_{n}(t)
is cyclic in
{\cal F}
, and let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be the labeling of
its roots under which

G_{{\tilde{E}}_{n}}({\cal F})\enskip=\enskip 1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}+\cdots+\gamma^{n-1}\enskip.\hskip 36.135pt\hskip 36.135pt(\>with\enskip\gamma\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i+1}\enskip)

If
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq p_{m}
are the primes factoring
n
we proceed by
constructing a sequence of subgroups
G_{k}
yielding the composition series

G_{{\tilde{E}}_{n}}({\cal F})\triangleright_{p_{1}}G_{1}\triangleright_{p_{2}}G_{2}\triangleright_{p_{2}}\cdots\triangleright_{p_{m}}G_{m}=\{id\}

This given we construct (via Theorem 6.1) a sequence of polynomials

\Psi_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\cal F}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
with
{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{k}}=G_{k}
. Now Theorem 6.4 and

{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{k}}\triangleleft_{p_{k}}{\tilde{G}}_{\Psi_{k-1}}
give that

{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}]\supseteq{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k-1}]\enskip.

Moreover from Theorem 6.5 we also deduce that
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}
is a root of a polynomial

B_{{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}}(t)\in{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k-1}][t]
of degree
p_{k}
which is cyclic in
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k-1}]
.
Thus its roots may be constructed by means of the formulas given by Proposition 7.2.
Let
\root p_{k} \of{\Xi}_{k}
with
\Xi_{k}\in{\cal F}_{k-1}
denote the
radical we must extract to obtain
{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}
. Now Proposition 7.2 assures that

\root p_{k} \of{\Xi}_{k}
is tight, and formula 7.20 gives that this a natural radical
extraction as long as we are in possession of a primitive
p_{k}^{th}
root of unity.
Finally, the identity in 7.18 shows that
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k}]
may also be obtained from
{\cal F}[{\tilde{\Psi}}_{k-1}]

by the adjonction of
\root p_{k} \of{\Xi}_{k}
itself. This given, since the Galois group
of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}
reduces to the identity after the extraction of the last radical
\root p_{m} \of{\Xi}_{m}
,
the process will yield a tight natural formula for each of the roots of
{\tilde{E}}_{n}
.

We should note that the tight extraction of prime
p^{th}
roots of unity can also
included in this process. This is because, as we have seen, the formulas giving a primitive

p_{k}^{th}
-root depend on the solution of cyclic equations whose degrees are prime factors
of
p_{k}-1
. But since each
p_{k}\leq n
, we see that we are appropriately setup for an induction
argument. We can in fact assume from the start that the Theorem is true for all cyclic equations
of prime degree less or equal than a certain prime
p
then carry out the constrution
outlined above for all cylic equations of any degree
n
whose prime factors are all
less or equal to the next prime. The induction can of course start with
p=2
where
the Theorem is easily verified to be true.
