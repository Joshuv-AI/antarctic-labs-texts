On the Hopf algebra of noncommutative symmetric functions in superspace
Diego Arcis Camilo González Sebastián Márquez

Abstract
We study in detail the Hopf algebra of noncommutative symmetric functions in superspace 𝐬𝐍𝐒𝐲𝐦 \mathrm{\bf sNSym} , introduced by Fishel, Lapointe and Pinto. We introduce a family of primitive elements of 𝐬𝐍𝐒𝐲𝐦 \mathrm{\bf sNSym} and extend the noncommutative elementary and power sum functions to superspace. Then, we give formulas relating these families of functions. Also, we introduce noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace and provide a explicit formula for their product. We show that the dual basis of these function is given by a family of the so–called fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace. This allows us to obtain a explicit formula for the coproduct of fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace. Additionally, by projecting the noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace, we define a new basis for the algebra of symmetric functions in superspace. On the other hand, we also show that 𝐬𝐍𝐒𝐲𝐦 \mathrm{\bf sNSym} can be realised as a Hopf algebra of trees.

Introduction

The classical ring of symmetric functions
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
is a very important object in algebraic combinatorics. This has been widely studied due to its richness properties and multiple applications in several areas such as representations theory, algebraic geometry and Lie algebras
[
27
,
30
]
. Symmetric functions are important not only in mathematics, but also in connection with integrable models in physics
[
29
]
. Due to this connection, and motivated by a supersymmetric generalization of the Calogero–Moser–Sutherland model
[
6
,
9
]
, it was developed in
[
7
,
8
]
a new class of functions called
symmetric functions in superspace
, which generalize the classic symmetric functions.

A
symmetric function in superspace
is obtained by considering the classic infinite collection of commutative variables
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
together with an infinite collection of anticommutative variables
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots)
, that is,
\theta_{i}\theta_{j}=-\theta_{j}\theta_{i}
. In particular,
\theta_{i}^{2}=0
. As it was shown in
[
10
]
, the classic bases of
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
, as the monomials, power–sum, elementary and homogeneous symmetric functions, can be naturally extended to superspace. More complex bases as Schur, Jack and Macdonald polynomial were also extended and studied in
[
3
,
4
,
11
,
26
,
12
,
21
,
18
]
. The combinatorial tools used to study these objects are the so–called
superpartitions
, which generalize the classic partitions of numbers.

A
superpartition
is a pair
\Lambda=(\Lambda^{a};\Lambda^{s})
, where
\Lambda^{a}
is a strictly decreasing partition, including possible a zero, and
\Lambda^{s}
is a usual partition. The
degree

|\Lambda|
of
\Lambda
is the sum of the components of
\Lambda^{a}
with the ones of
\Lambda^{s}
. The
fermionic degree

\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the length of
\Lambda^{a}
. In particular, every usual partition can be regarded as a superpartition with null fermionic degree.

Thus, given a superpartition
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m};\Lambda_{m+1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})
, some of the classical symmetric functions in superspace are defined as follows:

•

Monomial symmetric functions in superspace
:

m_{\Lambda}(x,\theta)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\theta_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\theta_{\sigma(m)}x_{\theta(1)}^{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots x_{\sigma(n)}^{\Lambda_{n}},

where the sum consider no repeated terms.

•

Power–sum symmetric functions in superspace
:
p_{\Lambda}=\tilde{p}_{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots\tilde{p}_{\Lambda_{m}}p_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots p_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{p}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}x_{i}^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{r},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

•

Elementary symmetric functions in superspace
:
e_{\Lambda}=\tilde{e}_{\Lambda_{1}}\cdots\tilde{e}_{\Lambda_{m}}e_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots e_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{e}_{k}=m_{(0;1^{k})}\quad\text{and}\quad e_{r}=m_{(\emptyset;1^{r})},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

•

Complete homogeneous symmetric functions in superspace
:
h_{\Lambda}=\tilde{h}_{\Lambda_{1}}\ldots\tilde{h}_{\Lambda_{m}}h_{\Lambda_{m+1}}\cdots h_{\Lambda_{n}}
, where

\tilde{h}_{k}=\sum_{|\Lambda|=k,\,\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)=1}(\Lambda_{1}+1)m_{\Lambda}\quad\text{ and}\quad h_{r}=\sum_{|\Lambda|=r,\,\operatorname{df}(\Lambda)=0}m_{\Lambda},\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq 0,r\geq 1.

Note that if
\Lambda^{a}=\emptyset
, we obtain a classic symmetric function.

It is well known that
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
has a Hopf algebra structure
[
14
]
. In
[
15
]
, the set of symmetric functions in superspace was also given with a Hopf algebra structure
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
, which has explicit formulas for the product, coproduct and antipode in terms of the bases given above. This Hopf algebra is commutative, cocommutative (up a sign), self dual
[
15
, Proposition 4.8]
and contains
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
as a Hopf subalgebra.

Another important ring related to symmetric functions is the one of
quasisymmetric functions

\mathrm{\bf QSym}

[
20
]
, whose Hopf structure has several applications in the theory of symmetric functions such as an expansion of the Macdonald polynomial in terms of the so–called fundamental quasisymmetric functions
[
20
]
. The fundamental quasisymmetric functions provide an important basis of
\mathrm{\bf QSym}
, since they share similar properties with Schur functions. The product, coproduct and antipode of these type of functions in superspace is an open problem. Here, we partially lead with this problem.

The dual structure of
\mathrm{\bf QSym}
, defined in
[
19
]
, is the Hopf algebra of
noncommutative symmetric functions

\mathrm{\bf NSym}
. It was shown in
[
19
]
that there is a connection of
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
with the Solomon’s descent algebra, which is applied to the study of formal power series with coefficients in a noncommutative algebra. The usual bases of
\mathrm{\bf QSym}
and
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
are indexed by compositions of numbers.

It was shown in
[
15
]
that both
\mathrm{\bf QSym}
and its dual
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
can be extended to superspace as the Hopf algebras of
quasisymmetric function in superspace

\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
and of
noncommutative symmetric functions in superspace

\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, respectively. In superspace, the classical bases are indexed by the so–called
dotted compositions
, that is, tuples of nonnegative integers in which some of their components are labelled by a dot. In particular, classic compositions can be regarded as dotted compositions which none of its components is labelled. The vector space
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
has a basis formed by the
monomial quasisymmetric functions in superspace

M_{\alpha}
, with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
a dotted composition, defined by

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}\theta_{i_{1}}^{\eta_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{k}}^{\eta_{k}}x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}},

where each
\eta_{i}
is either
0
if
\alpha_{i}
is labelled by a dot or
1
otherwise. The product of these monomials in
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
can be described by means a type of
overlapping shuffles
. Respectively, the coproduct of a monomial is obtained by deconcatenating its components. Since monomial symmetric functions in superspace
m_{\Lambda}
can be expanded in terms of the
M_{\alpha}
’s, then
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
is a Hopf subalgebra of
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
. See
[
15
]
for details.

The Hopf algebra
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
has a basis
\{H_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\text{ is a dotted composition}\}
that is dual to the monomial quasisymmetric functions in superspace, that is,
\langle H_{\alpha},M_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.

It is known that
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is isomorphic to the free algebra generated by the family of elements
H_{n}:=H_{(n)}
and
\tilde{H}_{n}:=H_{(\dot{n})}
. Moreover, the coproducts of
H_{n}
and
\tilde{H}_{n}
are similar to the ones of
h_{n}
and
\tilde{h}_{n}
in
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
, respectively. See
[
15
, Proposition 6.2]
. Note that
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
is the Hopf subalgebra generated by the
H_{n}
’s.

In this paper, we study in detail the Hopf algebra
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
.

In Section
3
, we recall the dotted compositions and characterize one of their lattice structure. It is shown in Section
4
that
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
can be graded via the degree of a dotted composition. In particular, we deduce that the dimension of its
n
th homogeneous component is
2\cdot 3^{n-1}
. By means this graduation, we explicitly describe the antipode
S:\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\to\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
. In this section we also show two Hopf subalgebras of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
that can be realised as polynomial structures.

Recall that the classic noncommutative power sum function
P_{n}
is a primitive element of
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
, and so is of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
. In Subsection
4.3
, by direct study of the coalgebra structure of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, we deduce the existence of a second family of primitive elements
\{\Psi_{n}\mid n\geq 0\}
, which are different of the analogous power functions in superspace.

Similar to the classic noncommutative case, in Section
5
we define families of noncommutative elementary and power sum functions in superspace
\tilde{S}_{n}
and
\tilde{P}_{n}
, respectively. In particular, we show that
S(\tilde{H}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{S}_{n}
and that each
\tilde{P}_{n}
can be written as a sum of Lie brackets
[P_{i},\Psi_{j}]
, where
i+j=n
.

The classic noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions were introduced in
[
19
]
via quasi determinants. In Section
6
, we extend these functions to superspace. In particular we provide a formula for the product of noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace, and we study their relation with another bases of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
.

In Section
7
, we conclude the paper by showing the interaction of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
with other Hopf algebras. First, we extend the identification of
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
as a substructure of the noncommutative version of the Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra
[
16
,
24
,
17
,
23
]
, obtaining a Hopf algebra generated by planar rooted trees. Thus, the description of the coproduct of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
can be given in terms of the admissible cuts of these trees. Secondly, due to the dual relation between
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
and
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
, we show that, for every dotted composition
\alpha
,
M_{\alpha}
can be written in terms of the so–called
fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace
. This implies that the set of these functions
\{L_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\text{ a dotted composition}\}
is a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
. Furthermore, we provide formulas for the coproduct of these functions, which can be described by means a new type of diagrams that extend the classic Ribbon diagrams. Finally, we study the morphism
\pi:\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\to\mathrm{\bf sSym}
, introduced in
[
15
]
, obtaining new relations for symmetric functions in superspace and a new basis for
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
by means the projection of noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace on
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
.

In the present paper, every linear algebraic structure will be taken over the field of rational numbers
\Q
. The word
algebra
will mean associative algebra with unit and the word
coalgebra
will mean coassociative coalgebra with counit.

For every positive integer
n
, we will denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and set
[n]_{0}=\{0\}\cup[n]
. As usual, the
symmetric group
on
[n]
will be denoted by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

2
Hopf algebras

Here, we will recall some notions about bialgebras and Hopf algebras.

A
bialgebra
is a vector space
\mathcal{H}
endowed with an associative product
m:\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
together with a unit
u:\Q\to\mathcal{H}
, and a coassociative coproduct
\Delta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
together with a counit
\epsilon:\mathcal{H}\to\Q
, which are compatible, that is, the coproduct and the counit are algebra morphisms. The
reduced coproduct
of
\mathcal{H}
is defined by
\bar{\Delta}(x)=\Delta(x)-x\otimes 1-1\otimes x
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. We say that
\mathcal{H}
is said to be
graded
if there are subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots
of
\mathcal{H}
, called
homogeneous components
, satisfying the conditions

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{n},\quad\mathcal{H}_{i}\mathcal{H}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{i+j},\,\,i,j\geq 0,\quad\Delta(\mathcal{H}_{n})\subseteq\bigoplus_{i+j=n}\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{j}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{H}
is called
connected
whenever
\mathcal{H}_{0}\simeq\Q
.

A bialgebra
\mathcal{H}
, as above, is said to be a
Hopf algebra
if there is a linear map
S:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, called
antipode
, satisfying the relations

m\circ(S\otimes\operatorname{id})\circ\Delta=u\circ\epsilon=m\circ(\operatorname{id}\otimes S)\circ\Delta.

Note that, as
\Delta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
must be an algebra morphism, the bialgebra structure of
\mathcal{H}
depends on the product of
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{H}
is graded, this product may be defined as
(m\otimes m)\circ(\operatorname{id}\otimes\tau\otimes\operatorname{id})
, where
\tau:\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
is the
twist map
defined by
\tau(x\otimes y)=(-1)^{ab}(y\otimes x)
with
x\in\mathcal{H}_{a}
and
y\in\mathcal{H}_{b}
.

An element
x
of a Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}
is called
primitive
, if
\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1
or equivalently
\bar{\Delta}(x)=0
.

It is known that if
\mathcal{H}
is a connected graded bialgebra, then it is a Hopf algebra with antipode defined recursively by
S(1)=1
and for
x
in some homogeneous component,

S(x)=-x-\sum S(x_{(1)})x_{(2)},\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{\Delta}(x)=x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}\quad\text{(Sweedler's notation)}.

We conclude this subsection by describing the dual notion of a connected graded Hopf algebra
{\mathcal{H}=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{n}}

with finite dimensional homogeneous components.

First, observe that
(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})^{*}\simeq\mathcal{H}^{*}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}
, and that
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
is also graded as a vector space. More specifically,

\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})_{n}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{n-k}.

This, together with the coproduct
\Delta
of
\mathcal{H}
induce a product
m_{d}
on
{\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{*}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{*}}
given by the composition

m_{d}:\mathcal{H}^{*}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim\,\,\,}}{{\to}}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta^{*}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{H}^{*},\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta^{*}(f)(x):=f\circ\Delta(x)\quad\text{with}\quad f\in(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})^{*},\,x\in\mathcal{H}.

Respectively, the product
m
of
\mathcal{H}
induces a coproduct
\Delta_{d}
of
\mathcal{H}^{*}
defined as follows

\Delta_{d}:\mathcal{H}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m^{*}}}{{\to}}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim\,\,\,}}{{\to}}\mathcal{H}^{*}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*},\quad\text{where}\quad m^{*}(f)(x\otimes y):=f\circ m(x\otimes y)\quad\text{with}\quad x\otimes y\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H},\,f\in\mathcal{H}^{*}.

The structure
(\mathcal{H}^{*},m_{d},\Delta_{d})
is called the
dual Hopf algebra
of
\mathcal{H}
.

For more details about Hopf algebras, see, for example
[
22
,
17
]
.

Dotted compositions

In what follows,
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}=\{\dot{0},\dot{1},\ldots\}
.

A
dotted composition
is a tuple
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
whose
components
, denoted by
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
, belong to the set
\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. A component of
\alpha
is called
dotted
if it belongs to
\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. We call
k
the
length
of
\alpha
and denoted it by
\ell(\alpha)
. The
fermionic degree
of
\alpha
is the number
\operatorname{df}(\alpha)
of dotted components of it. The
degree
of
\alpha
is the number
|\alpha|
obtained by adding its fermionic degree with the numerical values of its components.

For a length
k
dotted composition
\alpha
, we will denote by
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)
the
rightmost component
of
\alpha
, that is,
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)=\alpha_{k}
. Note that
\alpha_{1}
is its
leftmost component
. Also, if
\alpha_{1}=\cdots=\alpha_{k}
, we denote
\alpha
by
(\alpha_{1}^{k})
instead.

Graphically, dotted compositions are represented by
Ribbon diagrams
that extend the classical ones, such that
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}

Proposition 3.1
.

For every positive integer
n
, the number of dotted compositions of degree
n
is
2\cdot 3^{n-1}
. See
[
25
, A025192]
.

Proof.

By considering the degree of its components, every dotted composition of degree
n
can be regarded as a composition of
n
, where its components can be of two kinds. Since for each
k\in[n]
, there are
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
usual compositions of degree
n
and length
k
, then there are
\binom{n-1}{k-1}2^{k}
dotted compositions of degree
n
and length
k
. Hence, the number of dotted compositions of degree
n
is

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}2^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k}2^{k+1}=2\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k}2^{k}=2(1+2)^{n-1}=2\cdot 3^{n-1}.

∎

Given two dotted compositions
\alpha
and
\beta
, we denote by
\alpha\beta
the dotted composition obtained by concatenating
\alpha
with
\beta
. If
\beta=(x)
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote
\alpha\beta
by
\alpha x
instead. A
convex partition
of a dotted composition
\alpha
is a tuple of dotted compositions
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
such that
\alpha=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{k}
.

In order to describe one of the partial orders on dotted compositions introduced in
[

]
, we first extend the usual sum of
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
to
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
as follows:

m\oplus n=r,\kern 15.00002ptm\oplus\dot{n}=\dot{r},\kern 15.00002pt\dot{m}\oplus n=\dot{r},\kern 15.00002pt\dot{m}\oplus\dot{n}=0,\quad\text{where}\quad r=m+n.

For instance,

1\oplus 1=2\qquad\dot{0}\oplus 1=\dot{1}\qquad 1\oplus\dot{0}=\dot{1}\qquad\dot{0}\oplus\dot{0}=0

Note that the length two compositions of

, with respect to
\oplus
, are given by cutting internally its left and right Ribbon diagrams. For instance, for
2
and
\dot{2}

Remark 3.2
.

Note that
\oplus
is not associative in general, indeed
(1\oplus\dot{1})\oplus\dot{1}=\dot{2}\oplus\dot{1}=0
and on the other hand
1\oplus(\dot{1}\oplus\dot{1})=1\oplus 0=1
. However, if at most one of
x,y,z
belongs to
\dot{\mathbb{N}}
, then
(x\oplus y)\oplus z=x\oplus(y\oplus z)
. This property will be important to describe the partial order on dotted compositions.

For
\alpha,\beta
dotted compositions of degree
n
, we say that
\beta

covers

\alpha
, if
\beta
is obtained by
\oplus
-summing two consecutive components of
\alpha
that are not dotted at once. The partial order obtained by closuring this relation is simply denoted by
\preceq
and gives a structure of poset to the collection of all dotted compositions of degree
n
. More generally, by abuse of language, if
\alpha\preceq\beta
, we will say that
\beta

covers

\alpha
as well.

Note that two dotted compositions that are comparable respect to covering have the same degree and fermionic degree. Also, a dotted composition is maximal respect to covering if it has a unique component or its components are all dotted, that is, the length and fermionic degree coincide.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every positive integer
n
, the number of all dotted compositions covered by
(\dot{n})
is
(n+2)2^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition satisfying
\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})
. Denote by
\bar{\alpha}
the tuple obtained by removing the zeros and the dots from the components of
\alpha
. Note that
\alpha
has a unique component in
\dot{\mathbb{N}}
and that
\bar{\alpha}
is a composition of
n
. For
k\in[n]
, we will count the number of dotted compositions
\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})
such that
\ell(\bar{\alpha})=k
.

Recall that
n
has
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
compositions of length
k
. Then, there are
(k+1)\binom{n-1}{k-1}
dotted compositions
\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})
such that
\dot{0}
is a component of
\alpha
and
\ell(\bar{\alpha})=k
. On the other hand, there are
k\binom{n-1}{k-1}
dotted compositions
\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})
in which the unique component belonging to
\dot{\mathbb{N}}
is not
\dot{0}
. Hence, there are
(2k+1)\binom{n-1}{k-1}

\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})
satisfying

. Therefore, the number of dotted compositions covered by
(\dot{n})
is

\textstyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k+1)\binom{n-1}{k-1}=(n+2)2^{n-1}.}

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\alpha

4
The Hopf structure of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}

In this section, we describe the Hopf structure of noncommutative symmetric functions in superspace
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, introduced in
[
15
]
.

Recall that, as in the classic case, the set of monomial quasisymmetric functions in superspace
M_{\alpha}
, with
\alpha
a dotted composition, form a basis of the Hopf algebra
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
.

The
Hopf algebra of noncommutative symmetric functions in superspace

\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is the dual structure of the Hopf algebra of quasisymmetric functions in superspace, given by

\langle H_{\alpha},M_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha,\beta\quad\text{are dotted compositions}.

As it was shown in
[
15
]
, the algebra structure of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is freely generated by the set
\{H_{r}\mid r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}\}
. The generators
H_{r}
will be called
noncommutative complete homogeneus symmetric function in superspace
. For a length
k
dotted composition
\alpha
, we write
H_{\alpha}=H_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots H_{\alpha_{k}}
. The set
\{H_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\text{ is a dotted composition}\}
is a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
.

As with
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
, the algebra
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
can be graded by the fermionic degree. With respect to this graduation,
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\otimes\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
can be endowed with the following product:

(4.1)

(f_{1}\otimes g_{1})\cdot(f_{2}\otimes g_{2})=(-1)^{\operatorname{df}(g_{1})\operatorname{df}(f_{2})}\,f_{1}\,f_{2}\otimes g_{1}g_{2},\quad\text{where}\quad f_{1},g_{1},f_{2},g_{2}\in\mathrm{\bf sNSym}.

Since
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is a free algebra, it is enough to define the coproduct
\Delta
on its generators and so extend it as an algebra morphism, with respect to the product in
4.1
. Thus, we have:

\Delta(H_{r})=\sum_{p\oplus q=r}H_{p}\otimes H_{q},\quad\text{where}\quad p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad H_{0}=1.

As in
[
15
]
, for every nonnegative integer
n
, we will write
H_{\dot{n}}
by
\tilde{H}_{n}
instead. Note that
\Delta(1)=1\otimes 1
and, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following:

(4.2)

\Delta(H_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}H_{k}\otimes H_{n-k},\qquad\Delta(\tilde{H}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\tilde{H}_{k}\otimes H_{n-k}+H_{n-k}\otimes\tilde{H}_{k}).

There is another graduation of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
given by the degree of dotted compositions. Thus, if
\alpha
is a dotted composition, then
H_{\alpha}
is an homogeneous element of degree
|\alpha|
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}_{n}
the subspace spanned by the elements
H_{\alpha}
such that
\alpha
is a dotted composition of degree
n
. By Proposition
3.1
,
\dim(\mathrm{\bf sNSym}_{n})=2\cdot 3^{n-1}
. Fixing
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}_{0}=K
, we have that

\mathrm{\bf sNSym}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{\bf sNSym}_{n},

is a connected graded bialgebra, and then a Hopf algebra. Note that
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is cocommutative up a sign and that
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
is a Hopf subalgebra of it.

Recall that the antipode of a connected graded bialgebra
\mathcal{H}
is defined recursively by conditions
S(1)=1
and

S(x)=-\left(x+\sum S(x_{(1)})x_{(2)}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad x\in\mathcal{H}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\Delta}(x)=\sum x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}.

For a dotted composition
\alpha

\mathrm{rev}(\alpha)
the dotted composition obtained by reversing its components.

It is well known that
[

S(H_{\alpha})=\sum_{\beta\preceq\mathrm{rev}(\alpha)}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)}H_{\beta}.

4.3
Primitive elements

In this section we study the primitive part of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}

and

(4.5)

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n}P_{n-k}=nH_{n},\quad n\geq 1.

It is known that

\mathrm{\bf NSym}

\mathrm{\bf NSym}

\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
as well.

\mathrm{\bf sNSym}

\Psi_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0}
and

(4.6)

\Psi_{n}=\tilde{H}_{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k},\quad n\geq 1.

Proposition 4.6
.

For every
n\geq 0
,
\Psi_{n}
is a primitive of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\Psi_{0}
is a primitive, so we proceed by induction on
n
. Assume the claim is true for all

, that is
\Delta(\Psi_{k})=\Psi_{k}\otimes 1+1\otimes\Psi_{k}
. Since
\Delta(H_{n-k})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-k}H_{i}\otimes H_{n-k-1}
, we obtain

\Delta(H_{n-k})\Delta(\Psi_{k})=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}H_{i}\otimes H_{n-k-i}\right)\left(\Psi_{k}\otimes 1+1\otimes\Psi_{k}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}H_{i}\Psi_{k}\otimes H_{n-k}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}H_{i}\otimes H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}.

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta(\Psi_{n})&=&{\displaystyle\Delta(\tilde{H}_{n})-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta(H_{n-k})\Delta(\Psi_{k}),}\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\tilde{H}_{k}\otimes H_{n-k}+H_{n-k}\otimes\tilde{H}_{k})-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}(H_{i}\Psi_{k}\otimes H_{n-k-i}+H_{n-k-i}\otimes H_{i}\Psi_{k})},\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{H}_{n-i}\otimes H_{i}+H_{i}\otimes\tilde{H}_{n-i}\right)+\tilde{H}_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes\tilde{H}_{n}-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\otimes 1-1\otimes\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}\right)}\\[14.22636pt]
&&-{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-i}H_{n-i-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\otimes H_{i}+H_{i}\otimes\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-i}H_{n-i-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\right]},\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left(\tilde{H}_{n-i}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-i}H_{n-i-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\otimes H_{i}+H_{i}\otimes\left(\tilde{H}_{n-i}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-i}H_{n-i-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\right]}\\[14.22636pt]
&&+{\displaystyle\left(\tilde{H}_{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}\right)\otimes 1+1\otimes\left(\tilde{H}_{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}\right)}.\end{array}

Therefore
\Delta(\Psi_{n})=\Psi_{n}\otimes 1+1\otimes\Psi_{n}
.
∎

Recall that
P_{n}
can be written as

(4.7)

P_{n}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq(n)}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-1}\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)H_{\alpha}.

Now, by an inductive argument, we have the following proposition for
\Psi_{n}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
,
\Psi_{n}
is the sum of
2^{n}
terms given as

\Psi_{n}=\tilde{H}_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\alpha\preceq(n-k)}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)}H_{\alpha}\tilde{H}_{k}.

For instance,
\Psi_{3}
is given by the following sum of eight terms.

Recall that if

is a usual composition of length

, then

. It is known that

H_{n}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq(n)}\frac{1}{\nu_{\alpha}}P_{\alpha},\quad\text{where}\quad\nu_{\alpha}=\alpha_{1}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\cdots(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}).

This together with Equation

imply the following

\tilde{H}_{n}=\Psi_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{\alpha\preceq(n-k)}\frac{1}{\nu_{\alpha}}P_{\alpha}\right)\Psi_{k}.

Remark 4.8
.

Unlike the classic notion of Hopf algebra, if
x,y
are primitive elements of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, the Lie bracket
[x,y]
is not necessarily primitive. Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta([x,y])&=&(x\otimes 1+1\otimes x)(y\otimes 1+1\otimes y)-(y\otimes 1+1\otimes y)(x\otimes 1+1\otimes x),\\
&=&[x,y]\otimes 1+1\otimes[x,y]+[(-1)^{\operatorname{df}(x)\operatorname{df}(y)}-1]y\otimes x+[1-(-1)^{\operatorname{df}(x)\operatorname{df}(y)}]x\otimes y.\end{array}

Thus,
[x,y]
is primitive if and only if
\operatorname{df}(x)
or
\operatorname{df}(y)
is even.

Also note that if we consider the usual product on
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\otimes\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, instead of the one in
4.1
, we obtain a classic structure of Hopf algebra on
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
, and so the primitive part of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
would be the free Lie algebra generated by the set
\{P_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\Psi_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
.

5
Noncommutative elementary and power sum functions in superspace

In this section, we introduce noncommutative analogue versions of elementary and power sum functions in superspace, defined in
[
10
]
, by means generating functions.

In what follows, we write the generating function of the complete homogeneous functions
H_{n}
and
\tilde{H}_{n}
, by

\lambda(t,\tau):=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}(H_{n}+\tau\tilde{H}_{n}),

where
t
and
\tau
are indeterminate parameters.

5.1
Elementary functions

The
elementary functions in superspace
are defined by means the following generating function

\sigma(t,\tau):=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}(S_{n}+\tau\tilde{S}_{n})\quad\text{satisfying}\quad\sigma(t,\tau)\lambda(-t,-\tau)=1.

Proposition 5.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, we obtain the following recursive formulas:

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}H_{k}S_{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}S_{n-k}H_{n-k}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}(S_{k}\tilde{H}_{n-k}-\tilde{S}_{k}H_{n-k})=0.

Note that
S_{n}
is the classic noncommutative elementary function and

\tilde{S}_{n}=S_{n}\tilde{H}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-k}(S_{k}\tilde{H}_{n-k}-\tilde{S}_{k}H_{n-k}).

Proposition 5.2
.

For every integer
n\geq 0
, we have
S(\tilde{H}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{S}_{n}
. As a consequence,

\tilde{S}_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}\sum_{\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)}H_{\alpha}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. Note that
S(\tilde{H}_{0})=-\tilde{S}_{0}
because
\tilde{S}_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0}
. If
n\geq 1
, assume the claim is true for all
k<n
. By rewriting the coproduct of
\tilde{H}_{n}
as

\Delta(\tilde{H}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}H_{k}\otimes\tilde{H}_{n-k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tilde{H}_{k}\otimes H_{n-k},

we obtain

S(\tilde{H}_{n})=-\left(\tilde{H}_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(H_{k})\tilde{H}_{n-k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(\tilde{H}_{k})H_{n-k}\right).

The fact that
S(H_{k})=(-1)^{k}S_{k}
and the inductive hypothesis imply that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}S(\tilde{H}_{n})&=&{\displaystyle-\left(\tilde{H}_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}S_{k}\tilde{H}_{n-k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k+1}\tilde{S}_{k}H_{n-k}\right),}\\[2.84526pt]
&=&{\displaystyle-\left((-1)^{n}S_{n}\tilde{H}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\left(S_{k}\tilde{H}_{n-k}-\tilde{S}_{k}H_{n-k}\right)\right),}\\[2.84526pt]
&=&{\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\left(S_{n}\tilde{H}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-k}\left(S_{k}\tilde{H}_{n-k}-\tilde{S}_{k}H_{n-k}\right)\right),}\\[2.84526pt]
&=&(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{S}_{n}.\end{array}

Finally, Proposition
4.4
implies that
{\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}\sum_{\alpha\preceq(\dot{n})}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)}H_{\alpha}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

For every
n\geq 0
,
\tilde{S}_{n}={\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\Psi_{n-k}S_{k}}
.

Proof.

It is a consequence of Proposition
5.2
, Proposition
4.7
and the fact that
{\displaystyle S_{k}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{\beta\preceq(k)}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)}H_{\beta}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

\Delta(\tilde{S}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\tilde{S}_{k}\otimes S_{n-k}+S_{n-k}\otimes\tilde{S}_{k}\right).

In consequence,
S(\tilde{S}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{H}_{n}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
, we have

\tilde{S}_{n}=\sum_{q+r=n}(-1)^{q}\Psi_{q}S_{r},\qquad q,r\geq 0.

Recall that
\Psi_{q}
is primitive (Proposition
4.6
), that is,
\Delta(\Psi_{q})=\Psi_{q}\otimes 1+1\otimes\Psi_{q}
. Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta(\tilde{S}_{n})&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{i+j+k=n}\left[(-1)^{i}\Psi_{i}S_{j}\otimes S_{k}+S_{k}\otimes(-1)^{i}\Psi_{i}S_{j}\right],}\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}\Psi_{k-i}S_{i}\right)\otimes S_{n-k}+S_{n-k}\otimes\left(\sum_{i=0}(-1)^{k-i}\Psi_{k-i}S_{i}\right)\right],}\\[14.22636pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\tilde{S}_{k}\otimes S_{n-k}+S_{n-k}\otimes\tilde{S}_{k}\right).}\end{array}

∎

5.2
Power sum functions

The
power sum functions in superspace
, formed by the classic
P_{n}
and its analogue in superspace
\tilde{P}_{n}
, are obtained by means the following generating function

\Pi(t,\tau):=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}(P_{n}+\tau(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n})\quad\text{satisfying}\quad\lambda(t,\tau)\Pi(t,\tau)=(t\partial_{t}+\tau\partial_{\tau})\lambda(t,\tau).

Due to this relation, we obtain Equation
4.5
and the following proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

(5.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\tilde{H}_{n-k}P_{k}+(k+1)H_{n-k}\tilde{P}_{k}\right)=(n+1)\tilde{H}_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad P_{0}=0.

Proposition 5.6
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\,\dot{n}}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-1}|\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)|H_{\alpha},

where
|\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)|
denotes the degree of dotted composition
(\operatorname{rg}(\alpha))
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. Proposition
5.5
implies that
\tilde{P}_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0}
. Let
n\geq 1
and assume the claim is true for
0\leq k<n
. By Proposition
5.5
, we have:

(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}=(n+1)\tilde{H}_{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{H}_{n-k}P_{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_{n-k}(k+1)\tilde{P}_{k}.

The results follows by replacing
P_{k}
as in
4.7
and applying the inductive hypothesis to
(k+1)\tilde{P}_{k}
.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

\tilde{P}_{0}=\Psi_{0}
, and for every
n\geq 1
, we have

(5.2)

(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}=(n+1)\Psi_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[P_{n-k},\Psi_{k}].

Proof.

As
\tilde{H}_{1}=H_{1}\Psi_{0}+\Psi_{1}
, Proposition
5.5
implies that
\tilde{P}_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0}=\Psi_{0}
and
2\tilde{P}_{1}=2\Psi_{1}+[P_{1},\Psi_{0}]
. Now, we proceed by induction by assuming the claim is true for all
1<k<n
. By Proposition
5.5
, we have

(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}=(n+1)\tilde{H}_{n}-H_{n}\Psi_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}H_{n-k}(k+1)\tilde{P}_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{H}_{n-k}P_{k}.

Inductive hypothesis implies that, for each
1\leq k<n
, we have

(k+1)\tilde{P}_{k}=(k+1)\Psi_{k}+{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[P_{k-i},\Psi_{i}]}.

Since
\displaystyle{(n+1)\tilde{H}_{n}=(n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}}
, then

(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}=(n+1)\Psi_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(n-k)H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(H_{n-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[P_{k-i},\Psi_{i}]\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{H}_{n-k}P_{k}.

Now, the fact that
(n-k)H_{n-k}=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}H_{n-k-j}P_{j}}
implies the following

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(n-k)H_{n-k}\Psi_{k}}&=&\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}H_{n-k-j}P_{j}\right)\Psi_{k}},\\
&=&\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}P_{n-k}\Psi_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}H_{k}(P_{n-k}\Psi_{0}+\cdots+P_{1}\Psi_{n-k-1}).}\end{array}

On the other hand, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{H}_{n-k}P_{k}}&=&\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}H_{n-k-j}\Psi_{j}\right)P_{k},}\\
&=&\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Psi_{k}P_{n-k}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}H_{k}(\Psi_{0}P_{n-k}+\cdots+\Psi_{n-k-1}P_{1}).}\end{array}

The results above implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle{(n+1)\tilde{P}_{n}}&=&\displaystyle{(n+1)\Psi_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[P_{n-k},\Psi_{k}]+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}H_{k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-1}[P_{n-k-i},\Psi_{i}]\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(H_{n-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[P_{k-i},\Psi_{i}]\right),}\\[14.22636pt]
&=&\displaystyle{(n+1)\Psi_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[P_{n-k},\Psi_{k}].}\end{array}

∎

Since
\operatorname{df}(P_{n-k})=0
, we have that
[P_{n-k},\Psi_{k}]
is a primitive element. As an immediate consequence we have the following result.

Corollary 5.8
.

\tilde{P}_{n}
is a primitive element of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
for all
n\geq 0
.

In the following proposition, we write
\Psi_{n}
in terms of the
P_{i}
’s and
\tilde{P}_{i}
’s. To simplify the notation, for
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
in a Lie algebra, we set

[g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g_{1}&\text{if }n=1,\\
{[g_{1},[g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}]]}&\text{if }n>1\end{array}\right.

Proposition 5.9
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

\Psi_{n}=\sum_{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})}\left(\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)+1}}{t_{\alpha}}[P_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,P_{\alpha_{k-1}},\tilde{P}_{\alpha_{k}}]+\frac{(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)}}{(\alpha_{k}+1)t_{\alpha}}[P_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,P_{\alpha_{k}},\tilde{P}_{0}]\right),

where
\alpha
is a usual composition of
n
, and
\displaystyle{t_{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(\alpha_{i}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}+1)}
.

Proof.

The proof follows by applying Proposition
5.7
and an inductive argument on
n
.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 5.10
.

The Lie algebras generated by
P_{i},\Psi_{i}
and
P_{i},\tilde{P}_{i}
respectively, coincide.

6
Ribbon Schur functions in superspace

In this section, we extend the classic noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions
[
19
]
to superspace. These functions form a new basis of the Hopf algebra
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
. We give an explicit formula for the product of noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace and write other basis of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
in terms of these functions.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition. The
noncommutative Ribbon Schur function in superspace

R_{\alpha}
is defined inductively as follows

R_{\alpha}=H_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta}.

Notice that
R_{\alpha}=H_{\alpha}
whenever
\alpha
is maximal. Furthermore, if
\alpha
is a usual composition,
R_{\alpha}
is a classic noncommutative Ribbon Schur function.

To characterize the product of ribbon functions in superspace, we introduce a new (partial) operation
\odot
on dotted compositions. Given
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{s})
be two dotted compositions such that
\alpha_{r}
and
\beta_{1}
are not dotted at once, we define
\alpha\odot\beta=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r-1},\alpha_{r}\oplus\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{s})
. For instance,

\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{pics/039.pdf}}}\odot\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{pics/040.pdf}}}=\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{pics/041.pdf}}}

In particular, due to Remark
3.2
, we obtain the following identities relating
\odot
and the usual concatenation:

(\alpha\odot\beta)\odot x=\alpha\odot(\beta\odot x),\quad(\alpha\beta)\odot x=\alpha(\beta\odot x),\quad(\alpha\odot\beta)x=\alpha\odot(\beta x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}.

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two dotted compositions. Then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{\alpha\beta}&\text{if }\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),\beta_{1}\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\\
R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

To prove Theorem
6.1
, first we need to show Lemma
6.2
, Lemma
6.3
and Lemma
6.4
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition, and let
x\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

1.

If
x\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\beta x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}\cup\{\beta\odot x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}
.

2.

If
x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\beta x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}
whenever
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)
is dotted. Otherwise, we have
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\beta x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}\cup\{\beta\odot x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta,\,\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\gamma\mid\alpha x\preceq\gamma\}
. Note that, in all cases, the set on the right is contained in
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)
. Conversely, for
\gamma\in\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)
, we distinguish three cases.

Let
x\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)=x
, the other components of
\gamma
are obtained as
\oplus
-sums of consecutive components of
\alpha
. So,
\gamma=\beta x
for some
\beta\succeq\alpha
. Now, if
\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)\neq x
, there is
i
such that
\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)=\alpha[i]\oplus x
, where
\alpha[i]
is the
\oplus
-sum of the last
\ell(\alpha)-i-1
components of
\alpha
. Note that at most one of the components that define
\alpha[i]
is dotted. Consider
\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)
and define the dotted composition
\beta=\gamma^{\prime}\alpha[i]
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}
is obtained by covering the dotted composition formed by the the first
i
components of
\alpha
, we obtain that
\beta\succeq\alpha
and
\gamma=\beta\odot x
.

If
x,\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}
, they are not
\oplus
-summed when
\alpha x
is covered. Then
\gamma=\beta x
for some dotted composition
\beta\succeq\alpha
.

Finally, consider
x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}
and
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}
. As in the first case, if
\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)=x
, there is
\beta\succeq\alpha
such that
\gamma=\beta x
. Now, if
\operatorname{rg}(\gamma)\neq x
, as above,
\gamma=\beta\odot x
, where
\beta=\gamma^{\prime}\alpha[i]
with
\alpha[i]\in\mathbb{N}
because
x
is already dotted.
∎

Notice that, from the previous lemma, for
x,y\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

(6.1)

\operatorname{cv}((x,y))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{(x,y),(x\oplus y)\}&\text{if }\{x,y\}\not\subset\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\\
\{(x,y)\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition, and let
x\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

R_{\alpha}H_{x}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{\alpha x}&\text{if }\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\\
R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the length of
\alpha
. If
\ell(\alpha)=1
, it is a consequence of
6.1
. Now, if
\ell(\alpha)>1
, assume the result is true for all dotted compositions of smaller length. Recall that if
\alpha\prec\beta
, then
\ell(\beta)<\ell(\alpha)
, and that, by definition, we have

R_{\alpha}=H_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta},\quad\text{then}\quad R_{\alpha}H_{x}=H_{\alpha x}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta}H_{x}=\sum_{\alpha x\preceq\gamma}R_{\gamma}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta}H_{x}.

We distinguish two cases. If
x\in\mathbb{N}
, Lemma
6.2
implies that
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\beta x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}\cup\{\beta\odot x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}
. This together with the inductive hypothesis implies that

R_{\alpha}H_{x}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta}(R_{\beta x}+R_{\beta\odot x})-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}(R_{\beta x}+R_{\beta\odot x})=R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x}.

Let
x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. If
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then
\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
for all
\beta\succeq\alpha
, and, by Lemma
6.2
, we have
\operatorname{cv}(\alpha x)=\{\beta x\mid\alpha\preceq\beta\}
. By inductive hypothesis, we obtain

R_{\alpha}H_{x}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta}R_{\beta x}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta x}=R_{\alpha x}.

Now, if
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}
, since
\{\beta\succeq\alpha\mid\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}\}
is nonempty, Lemma
6.2
implies that

H_{\alpha x}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta}R_{\beta x}+\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta,\,\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}}R_{\beta\odot x}.

On the other hand, by applying the inductive hypothesis for
\beta\succ\alpha
, we have
R_{\beta}H_{x}=R_{\beta x}
if
\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, and
R_{\beta}H_{x}=R_{\beta x}+R_{\beta\odot x}
if
\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,

R_{\alpha}H_{x}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta}R_{\beta x}+\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta,\,\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}}R_{\beta\odot x}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}R_{\beta x}-\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta,\,\operatorname{rg}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}}R_{\beta\odot x}=R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition, and let
\beta=(x,y)
for some
x,y\in\mathbb{N}\cup\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{\alpha\beta}&\text{if }\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),x\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\\
R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Notice that, by Lemma
6.3
, we have

R_{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{x}R_{y}&\text{if }x,y\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\\
R_{x}R_{y}-R_{x\odot y}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

First, we assume that
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. If
y\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=R_{\alpha}(R_{x}R_{y})=(R_{\alpha}R_{x})R_{y}=R_{\alpha x}R_{y}=R_{\alpha\beta}.

On the other hand, if
y\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}&=&R_{\alpha}(R_{x}R_{y}-R_{x\odot y}),\\
&=&(R_{\alpha}R_{x})R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y},\\
&=&R_{\alpha x}R_{y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)},\\
&=&R_{\alpha xy}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)},\\
&=&R_{\alpha\beta},\end{array}

because
x\odot y\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
(\alpha x)\odot y=\alpha(x\odot y)
.

Now, we assume that
\{\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),x\}\not\subset\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. We will distinguish several cases.

In general, if
x\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}&=&R_{\alpha}(R_{x}R_{y}-R_{x\odot y}),\\
&=&(R_{\alpha}R_{x})R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y},\\
&=&(R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x})R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y},\\
&=&R_{\alpha x}R_{y}+R_{\alpha\odot x}R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y},\\
&=&R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}+R_{\alpha\odot x}R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y}.\end{array}

This equation depends on the set the elements
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)
and
y
belong to. If
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha\odot x)\in\mathbb{N}
, hence

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)\odot y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)}-R_{\alpha\odot(x\odot y)}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}

because
(\alpha x)\odot y=\alpha(x\odot y)
and
\alpha\odot(x\odot y)=(\alpha\odot x)\odot y
. Now, consider
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Notice that
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha\odot x)
belongs to
\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
as well. If
y\in\mathbb{N}
, then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)\odot y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)}-R_{\alpha\odot(x\odot y)}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}.

On the other hand, if
y\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}.

Finally, consider
x\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. As
\{\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),x\}\not\subset\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then
\operatorname{rg}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}
. If
y\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then

R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}=(R_{\alpha}R_{x})R_{y}=(R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x})R_{y}=R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta},

because
(\alpha\odot x)y=\alpha\odot(xy)
. If
y\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}&=&(R_{\alpha}R_{x})R_{y}-R_{\alpha}R_{x\odot y},\\
&=&(R_{\alpha x}+R_{\alpha\odot x})R_{y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)}-R_{\alpha\odot(x\odot y)},\\
&=&R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{(\alpha x)\odot y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)y}+R_{(\alpha\odot x)\odot y}-R_{\alpha(x\odot y)}-R_{\alpha\odot(x\odot y)},\\
&=&R_{\alpha\beta}+R_{\alpha\odot\beta}.\end{array}

This concludes the proof.
∎

\Delta(t_{\alpha})

[\operatorname{sgn}(c^{\prime})P^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})\otimes R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})]\cdot[P^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}})\otimes R^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}})]=\operatorname{sgn}(c^{\prime})(-1)^{ab}P^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})P^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}})\otimes R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})R^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}}),

where
a=\operatorname{df}(R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}}))
and
b=\operatorname{df}(P^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}}))
. Observe that
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},c_{k})
is an admissible cut of
t_{\alpha}
, satisfying

(7.2)

P^{c}(t_{\alpha})\otimes R^{c}(t_{\alpha})=P^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})P^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}})\otimes R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})R^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}}).

Now, we will show that
\operatorname{sgn}(c)=\operatorname{sgn}(c^{\prime})(-1)^{ab}
. If
j=0
it is obvious. For
j\geq 1
, we distinguish three cases.

If
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\sigma_{c}=\sigma_{c^{\prime}}
and
b=0
.

If
\alpha_{k}\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
b=0
, then
\operatorname{sgn}(c^{\prime})(-1)^{ab}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c^{\prime}})
. Since the coloured node of
\Delta(t_{\alpha_{k}})
belongs to
R^{c_{k}}(t_{\alpha_{k}})
, then
\sigma_{c}=(\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(1),\ldots,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(j-1),j)
. Thus, the number of inversions of
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}
and
\sigma_{c}
coincide, and so
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c^{\prime}})=\operatorname{sgn}(c)
.

If
\alpha_{k}\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
b=1
, then
\operatorname{sgn}(c^{\prime})(-1)^{ab}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c^{\prime}})(-1)^{a}
. Since
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}
is determined by the order of coloured nodes of
P^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})\otimes R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})
, there is
i\in[j-1]
such that the nodes positioned in
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(1),\ldots,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(i)
belong to
P^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})
and the nodes positioned in
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(i+1),\ldots,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(j-1)
belong to
R^{c^{\prime}}(t_{\alpha^{\prime}})
. This together with
7.2
imply that
a=j-1-i
and
\sigma_{c}=(\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(1),\ldots,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(i),j,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(i+1),\ldots,\sigma_{c^{\prime}}(j-1))
. Thus, the number of inversions of
\sigma_{c}
is the one of
\sigma_{c^{\prime}}
plus
j-1-i
. So,
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c^{\prime}})(-1)^{j-1-i}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{c^{\prime}})(-1)^{a}
.

Similarly, we prove that for each admissible cut
c
of
t_{\alpha}
,
\operatorname{sgn}(c)P^{c}(t_{\alpha})\otimes R^{c}(t_{\alpha})
can be represented as an addend of
\Delta(t_{\alpha})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

7.2
Fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace

The dual structure of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
is the Hopf algebra of quasisymmetric functions in superspace
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
. This relation is determined by a pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathrm{\bf sQSym}\otimes\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\to\Q
, which satisfies
\langle M_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.

Now, we introduce the set of
fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace

\{L_{\alpha}\}
as the basis of
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
obtained by dualizing the noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace
\{R_{\beta}\}
, defined in Section
6
, that is
\langle L_{\alpha},R_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
. In the following theorem, we show that this basis coincides with the set of fundamental quasisymmetric functions introduced in
[
15
]
, with respect to the partial order
\preceq
defined in Section
3
. Moreover, we write
M_{\alpha}
in terms of
L_{\beta}
and provide a formula for the coproduct of fundamental quasisymmetric functions in superspace.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition. Then

L_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\preceq\alpha}M_{\beta},\qquad M_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\preceq\alpha}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)}L_{\beta},\qquad\Delta(L_{\alpha})=\sum_{\beta\gamma=\alpha\text{ or }\beta\odot\gamma=\beta}L_{\beta}\otimes L_{\gamma}.

Proof.

As
\{M_{\gamma}\}
forms a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
, we can write
L_{\alpha}
as a linear combination
{\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=\sum_{\gamma}c_{\gamma}M_{\gamma}}
.

For a dotted composition
\beta
, we have
{\displaystyle\langle L_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle=\sum_{\gamma}c_{\gamma}\langle M_{\gamma},H_{\beta}\rangle.}

Since
\langle M_{\gamma},H_{\beta}\rangle=0
if
\gamma\neq\beta
and
\langle M_{\gamma},H_{\beta}\rangle=0
if
\gamma=\beta
, then
c_{\beta}=\langle L_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle
. This implies that

L_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}\langle L_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle M_{\beta}.

Now, as
H_{\beta}={\displaystyle\sum_{\beta\preceq\gamma}R_{\gamma}}
, then
{\displaystyle\langle L_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle=\sum_{\beta\preceq\gamma}\langle L_{\alpha},R_{\gamma}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\alpha\prec\beta,\\
1&\text{if }\alpha\succeq\beta.\end{array}\right.}
Thus,
{\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\preceq\alpha}M_{\beta}}
.

Similarly, as
\{L_{\alpha}\}
is dual to the basis
\{R_{\alpha}\}
, then it is also a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sQSym}
. Thus,

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}\langle M_{\alpha},R_{\beta}\rangle L_{\beta}=\sum_{\beta}\langle M_{\alpha},\sum_{\beta\preceq\gamma}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\gamma)}H_{\gamma}\rangle L_{\beta}=\sum_{\beta}\left[\sum_{\beta\preceq\gamma}\langle M_{\alpha},(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\gamma)}H_{\gamma}\rangle\right]L_{\beta}.

Now,
{\displaystyle\sum_{\beta\preceq\gamma}\langle M_{\alpha},(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\gamma)}H_{\gamma}\rangle}
is
0
if
\alpha\prec\beta
and it is
(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\alpha)}
otherwise. Hence,

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\preceq\alpha}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\alpha)}L_{\beta}.

The last assertion follows from Theorem
6.1
and the duality.
∎

7.3
Symmetric functions in superspace

In this subsection, we present some results on symmetric functions in superspace, which are obtained by the projection
\pi
from
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
to
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
defined in
[
15
]
. Additionally, we obtain a new basis of
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
formed by a new class of functions that we will call
Ribbon Schur functions in superspace
, which extends the classic Ribbon Schur functions in
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
.

Recall that
\pi:\mathrm{\bf sNSym}\to\mathrm{\bf sSym}
is determined by
\pi(H_{n}):=h_{n}
and
\pi(\tilde{H}_{n}):=\tilde{h}_{n}
. In particular, this morphism extends the classic morphism from
\mathrm{\bf NSym}
to
\mathrm{\bf Sym}
.

The following proposition characterizes the action of
\pi
on the families of noncommutative functions in superspace.

Proposition 7.3
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have
\pi(\tilde{S}_{n})=\tilde{e}_{n}
and
\pi(\tilde{P}_{n})=\pi(\Psi_{n})=\tilde{p}_{n}
.

Proof.

First, as
\pi
is a Hopf algebra morphism, we have
\pi\circ S=S\circ\pi
, where
S
denotes the antipodes of
\mathrm{\bf sNSym}
and
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
respectively. Thus, we obtain
\pi(S(\tilde{H}_{n}))=\pi((-1)^{n+1}\tilde{S}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\pi(\tilde{S}_{n})
, and
S(\pi(\tilde{H}_{n}))=S(\tilde{h}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{e}_{n}

[
15
, Corollary 4.5]
. So,
(-1)^{n+1}\pi(\tilde{S}_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}\tilde{e}_{n}
. Hence,
\pi(\tilde{S}_{n})=\tilde{e}_{n}
.

For the second part, we proceed by induction on
n
. Since
\tilde{p}_{0}=\tilde{h}_{0}
and
\tilde{P}_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0}
, then
\pi(\tilde{P}_{0})=\tilde{p}_{0}
. Now, let
n\geq 1
, and assume the result is true for
0\leq k<n
. By
[
10
, Lemma 26]
, we have

(7.3)

(n+1)\tilde{p}_{n}=(n+1)\tilde{h}_{n}-p_{n}\tilde{h}_{0}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(p_{k}\tilde{h}_{n-k}+(k+1)\tilde{p}_{k}h_{n-k}\right).

By applying
\pi
on both sides of
5.1
, inductive hypothesis implies that
\pi(\tilde{P}_{n})=\tilde{p}_{n}
. Similarly, by applying
\pi
on
5.2
, we obtain
\pi(\Psi_{n})=\pi(\tilde{P}_{n})
, because the Lie brackets become zero due to the commutativity of the product in
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
whenever one of the elements has null fermionic degree.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have
{\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{n}=\tilde{h}_{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h_{n-k}\tilde{p}_{k}}
and
{\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\tilde{p}_{n-k}e_{k}}
.

Proof.

It is a consequence of Proposition
7.3
,
4.6
and Proposition
5.2
.
∎

Now, we introduce the
Ribbon Schur functions in superspace
. In the classic case, Ribbon Schur functions can be regarded as a special case of
Skew Schur functions

s_{\lambda/\mu}
, which are indexed by the so-called
skew partitions

\lambda/\mu
, where
\lambda,\mu
are partitions such that the Young diagram of
\mu
is contained in the one of
\lambda
. The Young diagram of
\lambda/\mu
is obtained by removing the boxes of the diagram of
\mu
from the one of
\lambda
. This diagram is called a
Ribbon diagram
if it is connected and contains no a
2\times 2
block of boxes. In this case, we can identity
\lambda/\mu
with a composition
\alpha
. Thus, the
Ribbon Schur function

r_{\alpha}
is defined as
s_{\lambda/\mu}
. See
[
30
, Section 7.15]
for details.

In superspace, Schur functions were defined by means a specialization of the parameters of the so-called
Macdonald polynomials in superspace
. On the other hand,
Skew Schur functions in superspace
were defined in relation with a generalization of the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, see
[
26
]
for details.

Here, for a dotted composition
\alpha
, we define the
Ribbon Schur functions in superspace

r_{\alpha}
by projecting the noncommutative Ribbon Schur functions in superspace
R_{\alpha}
on
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
, that is,
r_{\alpha}=\pi(R_{\alpha})
.

In what follows of this subsection we will identify a superpartition with the unique dotted composition obtained by dotting its fermionic part.

Recall that if
\alpha
is a usual composition,
\tilde{\alpha}
denotes the partition obtained by sorting its component in nonincreasing order. Similarly, for a dotted composition
\alpha
, we will denote by
\tilde{\alpha}
the tuple obtained by sorting in nonincreasing order, both the dotted components and the undotted components. Note that, since the fermionic part of a superpartition must be strictly decreasing,
\tilde{\alpha}
is a superpartition only if the dotted components of
\alpha
are all different. For instance, if
\alpha=(\dot{1},2,3,\dot{2},3,1,\dot{4})
, then
\tilde{\alpha}=(\dot{4},\dot{2},\dot{1},3,3,2,1)
, that is

\alpha\,\,=\!\!\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[]{pics/092.pdf}}}\qquad\tilde{\alpha}\,\,=\,\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[]{pics/093.pdf}}}

For a dotted composition
\alpha
with
k=\ell(\alpha)
, we define
h_{\alpha}=h_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots h_{\alpha_{k}}
. Note that, as
h_{m}^{2}=0
for all
m\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then
h_{\alpha}=0
whenever
\alpha
has repeated dotted components. Further, if
\tilde{\alpha}
is a superpartition, we have
h_{\alpha}=(-1)^{\sigma(\alpha)}h_{\tilde{\alpha}}
, where
\sigma(\alpha)
is the number of inversions of the permutation of the dotted components of
\alpha
, obtained when computing
\tilde{\alpha}
. For instance,
h_{(\dot{1},2,3,\dot{2},3,1,\dot{4})}=-h_{(\dot{4},\dot{2},\dot{1},3,3,2,1)}
.

Below, we obtain an expansion of a Ribbon Schur function in superspace in terms of complete homogeneous functions in superspace, which generalizes the well-known formula for classic Ribbon Schur functions
[
28
]
.

Proposition 7.5
.

For a dotted composition
\alpha
, we have

r_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\preceq\beta}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)+\sigma(\beta)}h_{\tilde{\beta}}.

Conversely, for a superpartition
\Lambda
, we have
{\displaystyle h_{\Lambda}=\sum_{\Lambda\preceq\beta}r_{\beta}}
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Proposition
6.5
, the definition of
r_{\alpha}
and the fact that
\pi(H_{\beta})=0
whenever
\beta
has repeated dotted components.
∎

For instance, for
\alpha=(\dot{0},1,\dot{2},1)
, we have

r_{\alpha}=-h_{(\dot{2},\dot{0},1,1)}+2h_{(\dot{3},\dot{0},1)}+h_{(\dot{2},\dot{1},1)}-h_{(\dot{3},\dot{1})}-h_{(\dot{4},\dot{0})}.

It follows from Theorem
6.1
that, for dotted compositions
\alpha,\beta
, the product of
r_{\alpha}
with
r_{\beta}
is given as follows:

(7.4)

r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{\alpha\beta}&\text{if }\operatorname{rg}(\alpha),\beta_{1}\in\dot{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\\
r_{\alpha\beta}+r_{\alpha\odot\beta}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 7.6
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have
{\displaystyle r_{(1^{n},\dot{0})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}r_{(1^{n-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. The result is obvious if
n=0
. Now, assume the claim is true for values less than or equal to
n
. Thus,

r_{(1^{n},\dot{0})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}r_{(1^{n-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}.

r_{(1)}
on both sides of the equation above, with respect to the product in
7.4
, and by applying the inductive hypothesis, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{(1^{n+1},\dot{0})}+r_{(2,1^{n-1},\dot{0})}&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}r_{(1^{n+1-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}r_{(2,1^{n-1-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}+(-1)^{n}r_{(1)}r_{(\dot{n})},}\\[17.07182pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1^{k})r_{(1^{n+1-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}(r_{(2,1^{n-1-k},\dot{k})}+r_{(2,1^{n-k-2},\dot{(k+1)})})+(-1)^{n}r_{(\dot{n+1})},}\\[17.07182pt]
&=&{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1^{k})r_{(1^{n+1-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}+r_{(2,1^{n-1},\dot{0})}+(-1)^{n-1}r_{\dot{(n+1)}}+(-1)^{n}r_{(1)}r_{(\dot{n})}.}\end{array}

Therefore,
{\displaystyle r_{(1^{n+1},\dot{0})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}r_{(1^{n+1-k})}r_{(\dot{k})}}
.
∎

Proposition 7.7
.

We have:

\tilde{p}_{n}=(-1)^{n}r_{(1^{n},\dot{0})},\qquad r_{(1^{n},\dot{0})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e_{n-k}\tilde{h}_{k},\qquad\tilde{p}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}e_{n-k}\tilde{h}_{k}.

The first equality follows directly by applying

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition such that
\tilde{\alpha}
is a superpartition and
\operatorname{df}(\alpha)\geq 1
. Then

r_{\alpha}=(-1)^{\sigma(\alpha)}r_{\tilde{\alpha}}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}r_{\mu}\quad\text{where}\quad\ell(\mu)<\ell(\alpha)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\mu}\in\Z.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k:=\ell(\alpha)
. If
k=1
the result is obvious. If
k>1
, assume the claim is true for smaller values. Let
\alpha_{i}
be the maximal dotted component of
\alpha
. Consider
\beta=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1})
with
b=\operatorname{df}(\beta)
and
\gamma=(\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
with
c=\operatorname{df}(\gamma)
. Then, by
7.4
and
7.5
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{\alpha}&=&(-1)^{bc}r_{\gamma}r_{\beta}-r_{\beta}\ast r_{\gamma},\\
&=&(-1)^{bc}r_{(\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1})}+(-1)^{bc}r_{\gamma}\ast r_{\beta}-r_{\beta}\ast r_{\gamma},\\
&=&(-1)^{bc}r_{\alpha_{i}}r_{(\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1})}+(-1)^{bc+1}r_{\alpha_{i}}\ast r_{(\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1})}+(-1)^{bc}r_{\gamma}\ast r_{\beta}-r_{\beta}\ast r_{\gamma}.\end{array}

Now, by applying the inductive hypothesis on
\omega:=(\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1})
, we obtain

r_{\alpha}=(-1)^{bc+\sigma(\omega)}r_{\alpha_{i}\tilde{\omega}}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}r_{\mu}=(-1)^{\sigma(\alpha)}r_{\tilde{\alpha}}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}r_{\mu},

where
\ell(\mu)<\ell(\alpha)

∎

Theorem 7.9
.

The set
\{r_{\Lambda}\mid\Lambda\mbox{ is a superpartition}\}
is a basis of
\mathrm{\bf sSym}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a dotted composition. It is enough to show that
r_{\alpha}
is a linear combination of elements in
\{r_{\Lambda}\}
. If
\operatorname{df}(\alpha)=0
, the result is true due to the classic case. For
\operatorname{df}(\alpha)\geq 1
, we proceed by induction on
k:=\ell(\alpha)
. If
k=1
, the result is obvious. If
k>1
, we assume the claim is true for dotted compositions of smaller length. We will distinguish two cases.

If
\alpha
has repeated dotted components, then
h_{\alpha}=0
. So, by Proposition
7.5
, we have

r_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)+\sigma(\beta)}h_{\tilde{\beta}}=\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)+\sigma(\beta)}\sum_{\alpha_{\tilde{\beta}}\preceq\gamma}r_{\gamma}.

Since
\ell(\gamma)\leq\ell(\beta)<\ell(\alpha)
for each
\gamma
above, the result is obtained by applying the inductive hypothesis to
r_{\gamma}
.

Now, assume that
\tilde{\alpha}
is a superpartition. Lemma
7.8
implies that

r_{\alpha}=(-1)^{\sigma(\alpha)}r_{\tilde{\alpha}}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}r_{\mu}\quad\text{where}\quad\ell(\mu)<\ell(\alpha)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\mu}\in\Z.

We conclude the proof by applying the inductive hypothesis on each
r_{\mu}
.
∎
