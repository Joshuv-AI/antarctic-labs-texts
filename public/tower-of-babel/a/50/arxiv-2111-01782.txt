Improving the Cook et al. Proximity Bound Given Integral Valued Constraints
Marcel Celaya, Stefan Kuhlmann, Joseph Paat, Robert Weismantel

Abstract
Consider a linear program of the form max ⁡ 𝒄 ⊤ ​ 𝒙 : 𝑨 ​ 𝒙 ≤ 𝒃 \max\;\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b} , where 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} is an m × n m\times n integral matrix. In 1986 Cook, Gerards, Schrijver, and Tardos proved that, given an optimal solution 𝒙 ∗ \boldsymbol{x}^{*} , if an optimal integral solution 𝒛 ∗ \boldsymbol{z}^{*} exists, then it may be chosen such that ‖ 𝒙 ∗ − 𝒛 ∗ ‖ ∞ < n ​ Δ \left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}<n\Delta , where Δ \Delta is the largest magnitude of any subdeterminant of 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} . Since then an open question has been to improve this bound, assuming that 𝒃 \boldsymbol{b} is integral valued too. In this manuscript we show that n ​ Δ n\Delta can be replaced with n / 2 ⋅ Δ \nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}\cdot\Delta whenever n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . We also show that, in certain circumstances, the factor n n can be removed entirely.

Introduction.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{A}
is an integral full-column-rank
m\times n
matrix.
The polyhedron corresponding to a right hand side
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}\right\}.

The linear program corresponding to
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
and an objective vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}:\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\right\},

and the corresponding integer linear program is

\displaystyle\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}:\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right\}.

The
proximity
question in integer linear programming can
be stated as follows:
Given an optimal vertex solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
of
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
, how far away is the nearest optimal solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
to
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
(if one exists)?
Proximity has a wide array of applications in integer linear programming.
Perhaps not too surprisingly, upper bounds on proximity can help identify integer vectors in
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
from vertices; this is relevant in search techniques such as the feasibility pump
[
6
]
and dynamic programming
[
5
,
10
]
.
An upper bound of
\pi
on proximity also leads to a trivial enumeration algorithm to optimize
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
: Solve
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
to identify an optimal vertex
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
and then enumerate all
O(\pi^{n})
many integer points around
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
.
This simple enumeration algorithm shows that
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
can be solved in
O(2^{O(n)})
time, provided one can establish a value of
\pi
independent of
n
.

Proximity has been studied for decades with perhaps the most foundational result due to Cook, Gerards, Schrijver, and Tardos.
To state their result, we denote the largest absolute
k\times k
minor of
\boldsymbol{A}
by

\Delta_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right):=\max\left\{\left|\det\boldsymbol{M}\right|:\boldsymbol{M}\text{ is a $k\times k$ submatrix of $\boldsymbol{A}$}\right\}.

. Let

. If
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq n\cdot\max\big\{\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}):\ k=1,\ldots,n\big\}.

Cook et al.’s result is truly a cornerstone result.
Their proof technique has been used to establish proximity bounds involving other data parameters
[
21
]
and different norms
[
11
,
12
]
.
Furthermore, their result has been extended to derive proximity results for convex separable programs
[
7
,
9
,
20
]
(where the bound in Theorem
1.1
remains valid), for mixed integer programs
[
16
]
, and for random integer programs
[

]
.

Lovász
[
18
, Section 17.2]
and Del Pia and Ma
[
4

identified tuples
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
such that proximity is arbitrarily
close to the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
. However,
their examples crucially rely on the fact that
\boldsymbol{b}
can take arbitrary
rational values. In fact, Lovász’s example uses a totally unimodular
matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
while Del Pia and Ma use a unimodular matrix.
Therefore, if the right hand sides
\boldsymbol{b}
in their examples were to be replaced by the integral rounded down vector
\lfloor\boldsymbol{b}\rfloor
, then the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\lfloor\boldsymbol{b}\rfloor)
would only have integral vertices.

From an integer programming perspective, replacing
\boldsymbol{b}
with
\lfloor\boldsymbol{b}\rfloor

is natural as it strengthens the linear relaxation without cutting
off any feasible integer solutions. This leads to the proximity question
that we consider:
For a given matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
, find a value

\pi\geq 0
such that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\text{ is a vertex}\\
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\pi.

Other than
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
versus
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
, this question is equivalent to the question considered by Cook et al.
The objective vector
\boldsymbol{c}
is not explicit in our formulation, but it is implicit when we consider
vertices of
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
.

It was previously unknown if Cook et al.’s bound is tight when
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
Actually, under this assumption there are many results indicating that proximity is independent of
n
:
Aliev et al.
[
2
]
prove that proximity is upper bounded by the largest entry of
\boldsymbol{A}
for knapsack polytopes, Veselov and Chirkov’s result
[
19
]
implies a proximity bound of
2
when
\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{A})\leq 2
, and Aliev et al.
[
1
]
prove a bound of
\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{A})
for corner polyhedra.

Our main result is the first improvement on Cook et al.’s result.
Furthermore, our proof technique generalizes theirs, and we believe that it can be applied in the multiple settings where their technique is used.

Theorem 1.2

Let
n\geq 2
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
be an optimal vertex of
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
. If
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})

is feasible, then there exists an optimal solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
such
that

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}<\frac{n}{2}\cdot\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A}).

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
consists of three parts.
First, we relate proximity to the volume of a certain polytope associated with the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Second, we establish lower bounds on the volume of this polytope when
n=2
and
n=3
; see Section
2
.
Third, we show how these proximity bounds in lower dimensions can be used to derive proximity bounds in higher dimensions; see Section
3
.
We can improve Theorem
1.2
in certain settings.
For instance, if
n
is a multiple of
3
then we can replace the factor
\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}
with
(\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{3}})\cdot n
; see Theorem
2.1
.

We also consider the case when
\boldsymbol{A}
is
strictly
\Delta
-modular
, that is,
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{B}
for a totally unimodular matrix
\boldsymbol{T}
and a square integer matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
with determinant
\Delta
; see Section
4
.
Here we show the factor
\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}
can be removed entirely, generalizing a recent result of Nägele, Santiago, and Zenklusen
[
14
, Theorem 5]
.
We also give essentially matching lower bounds; see Section
5
.
It is more or less straightforward to find a 1-dimensional polytope
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a matching lower bound on proximity when
n\geq 2
.
Thus, our contribution with this lower bound is a
full-dimensional
polytope
\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a unique integral point
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
, and a vertex
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}

sharing no common facet with

\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
, such that the proximity is, up to a constant additive factor, equal to
\Delta_{n-1}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)
.

Theorem 1.3

Let
\Delta\geq 1
and
n\geq 2
.

1.

For all feasible instances
\operatorname{IP}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c}\right)
with
\boldsymbol{A}
strictly
\Delta
-modular and
\boldsymbol{b}
integral, and for all optimal vertices
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
of
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
, there exists an optimal solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
of
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A}),\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{A})\right\}-1.

2.

Let
\Delta\geq 3
. There exists a feasible instance
\operatorname{IP}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c}\right)
with
\boldsymbol{A}
strictly
\Delta
-modular and
\boldsymbol{b}
integral, and an optimal vertex
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
of
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
, such that every feasible integral solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
of
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
satisfies

\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\infty}=\max\left\{\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A}),\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{A})\right\}-2.

Moreover,
\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
is full-dimensional,
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
do not lie on a common facet of

.

1.1
Preliminaries and notation.

Here we outline the key objects and parameters used in the paper.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be such that
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\neq\emptyset
.
For
I\subseteq[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we use
\boldsymbol{A}_{I}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{I}
to denote the rows of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
indexed by
I
.
If
I=\{i\}
, then we write
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}:=\boldsymbol{A}_{I}
.
We use
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
to denote the all zero and all one vector (in appropriate dimension).

We assume we are given an objective vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
We always assume that

\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}.

(1)

We may assume (
1
) without loss of generality for the following reason.
Suppose
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is an optimal vertex of
\operatorname{LP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
, and let
I^{*}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
be an optimal basis, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{I^{*}}^{\phantom{-1}}
.
By perturbing
\boldsymbol{c}
, we may assume
\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}
for a strictly positive vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
be an optimal solution
\operatorname{IP}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})
that is closest to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
.
The polytope
\mathcal{P}(\overline{\boldsymbol{A}},\overline{\boldsymbol{b}}):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}):\ \boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\}
contains
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
and
\Delta_{k}(\overline{\boldsymbol{A}})=\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{A})
for all
k\in[n]
.
Further,
\mathcal{P}(\overline{\boldsymbol{A}},\overline{\boldsymbol{b}})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\{\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\}
because a different integer point
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
in
\mathcal{P}(\overline{\boldsymbol{A}},\overline{\boldsymbol{b}})
would satisfy
\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}\boldsymbol{w}^{*})>\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}_{I^{*}}\boldsymbol{z}^{*})=\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
, contradicting the optimality of
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
.
By replacing
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
by
\mathcal{P}(\overline{\boldsymbol{A}},\overline{\boldsymbol{b}})
and translating
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
to
\mathbf{0}
we may assume (
1
).

Under Assumption
1
, bounding proximity is equivalent to bounding

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})}\ \|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}=\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\{\pm\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathbf{e}_{n}\}}\ \max\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}:\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\right\},

(2)

where
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are the standard unit vectors.
In light of this, we analyze the maximum of an arbitrary linear form
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
over
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

We provide non-trivial bounds on the maximum of these linear forms for small values of
n
; see Section
2
.
In order to lift low dimensional proximity results to higher dimensions (see Section
3
), we consider slices of
\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
through the origin induced by rows of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Given
I\subseteq\left[m\right]
with
|I|\leq n-1
, define

\mathcal{P}_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right):=\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)\cap\ker\boldsymbol{A}_{I}.

We define
\mathcal{P}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}):=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
.
The bounds that we provide on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
are given in terms of the parameter

\Delta_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right):=\frac{1}{\gcd\boldsymbol{A}_{I}}\cdot\max\left\{\left|\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{A}_{K}\end{array}\right)\right|:I\subseteq K\subseteq\left[m\right],\;\left|K\right|=n-1\right\},

where

\gcd\boldsymbol{A}_{I}:=\gcd\left\{\left|\det\boldsymbol{M}\right|:\ \boldsymbol{M}~\text{is a}~\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{I})\times\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{I})~\text{submatrix of}~\boldsymbol{A}_{I}\right\}.

In particular, we define
\kappa_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)
to be the number satisfying

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=\kappa_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)\Delta_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right).

(3)

Maximizing over all
I\subseteq\left[m\right]
such that
\mathcal{P}_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
has a fixed dimension
d
, define

\kappa_{d}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right):=\max_{I:\dim\mathcal{P}_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)=d}\kappa_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right).

Equation (
3
) looks similar to the proximity bound we seek.
However,
\Delta_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)
depends on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, whereas our main result (Theorem
1.2
) only depends on
\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A})
.
Later (see Remark
2
), we will substitute
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}
in for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as in (
2
).
We also want to consider
I=\emptyset
because
\mathcal{P}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
by definition.
These substitutions will convert
\Delta_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)
to proving proximity of
\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A})
over
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
as desired in our proximity theorem.

Another important object for us is the following cone.
For
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right):=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{sign}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}&\geq 0~\forall~i\in[m]~\text{such that}~\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\neq 0\\[2.84544pt]
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}&=0~\forall~i\in[m]~\text{such that}~\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=0\end{array}\right\}.

The cone
\mathcal{C}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)
serves as a key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
in
[
3
]
.
We also define the polytope

\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right):=\mathcal{C}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\cap\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\mathcal{C}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\right).

One checks that if
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
, then
\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
.
Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)
then
\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)
.
Polytopes of this form, namely, ones in which every facet is incident to one of two distinguished vertices, known as
spindles
, were used in
[
17
]
to construct counterexamples to the Hirsch conjecture.

1
, we fix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Thus, in our notation we drop the dependence on
\boldsymbol{A}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, and simply write
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}_{I},\Delta_{I}
, and so on.
We also set

\kappa:=\kappa_{\dim\mathcal{P}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta:=\Delta_{\emptyset}.

Theorem 2.1

We have

Lemma 1

Assume
I\subseteq\left[m\right]
determines a linearly independent subset of the rows of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Further assume the linear span of
\mathcal{P}_{I}
is
\ker\boldsymbol{A}_{I}
, which has dimension

.
Then there exists
\hat{\boldsymbol{A}\;}\negthickspace\in\mathbb{Z}^{\left(m-n+d\right)\times d}

I=[n-d]
.
Set

,
\bar{J}:=\{n-d+1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\bar{I}:=\{n-d+1,\ldots,m\}

Choose a unimodular matrix

(e.g., via the Hermite Normal Form of

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}\right)_{I,J}&\mathbf{0}\\

(4)

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left(\begin{array}[]{r@{\hskip 0 cm}r@{\hskip 0 cm}l}\left(\right.\hskip 0.0pt&\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}\hskip 0.0pt&\left.\right)_{\bar{J}}\\

Lemma 2

Assume

Then

Proof

Recall
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})

.
Hence,

As

Thus,
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\in\Lambda
for
i\in\left[N\right]
and all these vectors are pairwise distinct elements in
\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)
.
If
N\geq\Delta
, then by
\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
and the pigeonhole principle there are
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}
for
i<j
that lie in the same residue class of
\Lambda
modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Hence, we have the contradiction

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}-\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

We proceed with
N\leq\Delta-1
.
We have

\left\|\boldsymbol{r}_{s}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A})}{\Delta}

for all
s\in\left[t\right]
by Cramer’s rule applied to (
10
).
Altogether, this yields

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lambda_{s}\left\|\boldsymbol{r}_{s}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A})\leq\max\left\{\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A}),\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{A})\right\}-1.

∎

5
A lower bound example.

The following construction proves Theorem
1.3
, Part 2. Let
\Delta\geq 3
.
Fix the matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{k}&\Delta\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n},

where
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n-1}
denotes the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
unit matrix and, for
0\leq k\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{k}:=(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{k\;\text{zeros}},\Delta-1,\ldots,\Delta-1).

As a first step, we define the parallelepiped

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{0}\leq\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{1}_{k}\\
\Delta-n+k\\
\mathbf{1}_{n-k-1}\end{array}\right)\right\}

for
\Delta-n+k\geq 1
.
Using the fact that the first
k
columns of
\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}
are integral, one can show
\bigl|\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\bigr|=2^{k}
.
In order to cut off all non-zero integer points in
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B})
, we define for
k\geq 1
the row vectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}:=(0,\ldots,\hskip-8.5359pt\underbrace{1}_{i\text{-th column}}\hskip-8.5359pt,\ldots,0,\hskip-14.22636pt\underbrace{-\Delta}_{(k+1)\text{-st column}}\hskip-14.22636pt,\ldots,-\Delta)

for each
i\in[k]
.
The resulting polytope is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,k}:=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B})\cap\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\leq 0\text{ for all }i\in[k]\right\}.

If
k=0
, then
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,0}=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B})
.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
denote the only vertex in
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{B})
that does not share a facet with
\mathbf{0}
.
Further, let
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
be such that
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,k}
.
Then one can show that

1.

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,k}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
does not share a facet with
{\boldsymbol{0}}
,

2.

\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,k}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,

3.

\boldsymbol{A}
is strictly
\Delta
-modular.

We select
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,n-2}
and get

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{0}}\right\|_{\infty}=\left|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{n-1}\right|=\Delta-2.

Observe that
\Delta_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{A})=\Delta
for
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,n-2}
which proves Part 2 of Theorem
1.3
.

Remark 3

The polytope
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,n-1}
does not work as an example since in this instance, the greatest common divisor of the
n
-th row of
\boldsymbol{B}
is
\Delta
. As a result, we only obtain the weak proximity bound
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}=1
.
However, a question related to the proximity question is to bound
\left\|\boldsymbol{b}\right\|_{\infty}
given that
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
and all constraints of
\mathcal{P}
are tight. The polytope
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,n,n-1}
yields an example with
\left\|\boldsymbol{b}\right\|_{\infty}=\Delta-1
.
