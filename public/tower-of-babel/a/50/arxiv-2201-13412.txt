A New Hausdorff Content Bound for Limsup Sets
Sylvester Eriksson-Bique

Abstract
. We give a new Hausdorff content bound for limsup sets, which is related to Falconer’s sets of large intersection. Falconer’s sets of large intersection satisfy a content bound for all balls in a space. In comparison, our main theorem only assumes a scale-invariant bound for the balls forming the limit superior set in question. We give four applications of these ideas and our main theorem: a new proof and generalization of the mass transference principle related to Diophantine approximations, a related result on random limsup sets, a new proof of Federer’s characterization of sets of finite perimeter and a statement concerning generic paths and the measure theoretic boundary. The new general mass transference principle transfers a content bound of one collection of balls, to the content bound of another collection of sets – however, this content bound must hold on all balls in the space. The benefit of our approach is greatly simplified arguments as well as new tools to estimate Hausdorff content. The new methods allow for us to dispense with many of the assumptions in prior work. Specifically, our general Mass Transference Principle, and bounds on random limsup sets, do not assume Ahlfors regularity. Further, they apply to any complete metric space. This generality is made possible by the fact that our general Hausdorff content estimate applies to limsup sets in any complete metric space.

1.
Introduction

Consider a collection of closed balls
\mathcal{B}
in a
complete
metric space
X
. One can form two collections: their
union

\bigcup\mathcal{B}
consisting of points that belong to at least one of the elements, and their
limsup set

\limsup\mathcal{B}
given by

\limsup\mathcal{B}:=\{x:x\text{ lies in infinitely many distinct balls }B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}\text{ with }\lim_{i\to\infty}{\rm rad}(B_{i})=0\}.

Such sets arise naturally in a variety of fields: Diophantine approximations, dynamics, probability, and, as we will see, in the study of sets of finite perimeter and isoperimetry.
It is obvious, that
\limsup\mathcal{B}\subset\bigcup\mathcal{B}
and often the limsup set is much smaller than the union. However, our main theorem shows that, if the union of the sets is large
in a scale invariant way
, then also
\limsup\mathcal{B}
is substantial. The size is measured by a general Hausdorff content; see Section
3
for definitions of the content. As a consequence of this general principle and techniques, we obtain a number of applications:

]

In this introduction, and paper, we will first discuss our general result on Hausdorff content. Then, we apply it to the contexts stated above.

To state our main theorem we fix some notation. First define the notation for the collections of sets we consider. Open balls in a metric space
X
will be denoted
B=B(x,r)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}
with radius given by
r={\rm rad}(B)
. For us, it will be crucial, that each ball
B
comes with an associated radius
r
and center
x
. Thus, formally, a ball would be a triple:
(x,r,B)
. However, we simply write
B=B(x,r)
to specify the center and radius, and suppress
(x,r)
in most of the notation.

Closed balls are defined by
{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
– and this is not to be confused with the closure of the open ball. When we simply say ”a ball
B
”, we mean either an open or closed ball, and use
B
to denote such a ball. Whenever necessary, we will distinguish between open and closed balls, and we use only
{\overline{B}}
to indicate closed balls.
Given a collection
\mathcal{B}
of closed balls, a scale
r\in(0,\infty)
and a set
A\subset X
, we define the scale and location restricted collections by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}|_{r}

\displaystyle:=\{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}:{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})\leq r\},\ \mathcal{B}|^{A}:=\{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}:{\overline{B}}\subset A\}\ \text{ and }

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}|_{r}^{A}

\displaystyle:=\{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}:{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})\leq r,{\overline{B}}\subset A\}.

Given a number
C\geq 1
the inflation of an open ball
B=B(x,r)
and a closed ball
{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)
are denoted by
CB,C{\overline{B}}
and are given by
CB:=B(x,Cr)
or
C\overline{B}:={\overline{B}}(x,Cr)
. The inflation of all balls in a collection is given by
C\mathcal{B}=\{C{\overline{B}}:{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}\}.
All of these notions naturally extend to collections of open balls.

Next, the size of our sets is measured in terms of a spherical Hausdorff content. A
gauge function

\xi
associates to each closed ball
{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)
and each open ball
B=B(x,r)
a number
\xi(B),\xi({\overline{B}})\in(0,\infty)
with
\xi(B)=\xi({\overline{B}})
. We will assume that
\xi
is a
doubling
gauge function: there exists a constant
D
so that for any two open balls
B,B^{\prime}
with
B\subset B^{\prime}
and
{\rm rad}(B)\leq{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})\leq 2{\rm rad}(B)
, we have
D^{-1}\xi(B)\leq\xi(B^{\prime})\leq D\xi(B)
.
1
1

1

A more familiar form for this may be
\xi(2B)\leq D\xi(B)
. However, since we do not assume that
\xi(B)\leq\xi(B^{\prime})
when
B\subset B^{\prime}
, then this slightly more technical formulation is needed. Indeed, our application to finite perimeter sets will require us to consider such a gauge function.
If we wish to emphasize the constant, we will call such a function a
D-
doubling gauge function
.

Next, we state the main theorem of this paper, which yields a way to transfer bounds of Hasdorff contents of unions to those for limsup sets. The new aspect of this statement is that the content bound is assumed only for the balls in the collection, and that the setting is that of all complete metric spaces.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\xi
be a
(D-)
doubling gauge function and
\Delta,\delta\in(0,1)
be any constants. There exists a constant
\beta=\beta(\delta,D)
so that the following holds.
Suppose that
X
is a complete metric space and that
\mathcal{B}
is any collection of closed balls, with the following: For each
{\overline{B}}_{0}={\overline{B}}(x_{0},r_{0})\in\mathcal{B}
and any
r\leq r_{0}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}\left(\bigcup\,\mathcal{B}|_{r}^{{\overline{B}}_{0}}\right)\geq\Delta\xi({\overline{B}}_{0}).

Then, for each
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}(\limsup\ (1+\delta)\ \mathcal{B}|^{{\overline{B}}_{0}})\geq\Delta\beta\xi({\overline{B}}_{0}).

In other words, in self-similar settings, it is equally hard to prove a Hausdorff-content for the limsup set, as it is to prove a content bound for a union. This insight may simplify many arguments, where the main technical step is the limit step.

Remark 1.3
.

Without assuming completeness, one can find counter examples. For instance, let
X=[0,1]\setminus\{2^{-1}\}
and
\xi
be the gauge given by
\xi(B)=1
. Consider
\mathcal{B}=\{{\overline{B}}((2^{-1}-2^{-1}n^{-1},1/n):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
. In this case
\limsup 2\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
, while
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}(\mathcal{B}|^{{\overline{B}}}_{r})\geq 1
for every
{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}
.

The assumption that the collections consist of closed balls is less severe. For simplicity, throughout, for our theorems on Hausdorff content we assume closed balls. However, if
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of open balls, which verifies the assumption of Theorem
1.2
, then
\overline{\mathcal{B}}=\{{\overline{B}}(x,Cr):B(x,r)\in\mathcal{B}\}
, for any
(1+\delta/4)>C>1
, would also verify the assumption. Thus, with
\delta/4
replacing
\delta
, we obtain also a lower content bound for
\limsup\mathcal{B}
for collections of open balls.

In the applications that we shall discuss, the minor inflation factor in the statement is harmless. If desired, it could be placed in the assumption.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\xi
be a
(D-)
doubling gauge function. Let
\Delta\in(0,1)
be any constant.
There exists a constant
\beta=\beta(\eta,D)
so that the following holds.
Suppose that
X
is a complete metric space and that
\mathcal{B}
is any collection of closed balls, with the following: For each
{\overline{B}}_{0}={\overline{B}}(x_{0},r_{0})\in\mathcal{B}
and any
r\leq r_{0}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}\left(\bigcup\,\mathcal{B}|_{r}^{\frac{1}{2}{\overline{B}}_{0}}\right)\geq\Delta\xi({\overline{B}}_{0}).

For each
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}(\limsup\ \mathcal{B}|_{r_{0}}^{{\overline{B}}_{0}})\geq\Delta\beta\xi({\overline{B}}_{0}).

The statements of these theorems hide a key, and somewhat elementary, observation, which we now explain.
The limit superior set of
(1+\delta)\mathcal{B}
can be expressed as an infinite intersection by

\limsup\ (1+\delta)\,\mathcal{B}|^{{\overline{B}}_{0}}_{r_{0}}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigcup\ (1+\delta)\ \mathcal{B}|_{r_{0}/n}^{{\overline{B}}_{0}}.

The assumption of the statement implies that each set
A_{n}=\bigcup(1+\delta)\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}/n}^{{\overline{B}}_{0}}
on the right hand side has a content lower bound (in a scale invariant way). The conclusion is that the intersection
\cap A_{n}
has also a content lower bound.

In general
, it is not possible to pass from information on the Hausdorff content of a nested sequence of sets
A_{n}
to the content of the intersection. Indeed, generally this holds only in special cases, of which we mention two: when each
A_{n}
is a compact set; and when each
A_{n}
is a set of large intersection, as defined by Falconer in
[
15
]
.
A simple counter-example for the general statement is given by
A_{n}=(0,\frac{1}{n})
. The
0
-Hausdorff content satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{0}_{r}(A_{n})\geq 1
for each
r\in(0,1)
. However, their intersection is empty,
\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}=\emptyset
, and has zero content. Note that the
0
-Hausdorff content corresponds to
\xi(B)=1
for any ball
B
. Other dimensions and gauge functions could be obtained by taking products of this example with other spaces.

The conclusion of our theorem is that a content bound is obtained for the intersection, if the content bound is scale-invariant in the stated way. Let us show how the proof works for the counting measure, when
\xi(B)=1
for each ball
B
, which illustrates how the scale-invariance is employed.

Case of a Counting measure:
The assumption states that for every ball
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
of radius
r_{0}
, there is at least one ball
{\overline{B}}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
with
{\overline{B}}_{1}\subset{\overline{B}}_{0}
with radius
r_{1}
satisfying
r_{1}\leq r_{0}/2
. One can apply this recursively: Assuming
k\geq 1
and that
{\overline{B}}_{k}
is defined with radius
r_{k}\leq r_{k-1}/2
, choose
{\overline{B}}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{B}
with
{\overline{B}}_{k+1}\subset{\overline{B}}_{k}
and with radius
r_{k+1}
satisfying
r_{k+1}\leq r_{k}/2
. Then
\cap_{k=1}^{\infty}{\overline{B}}_{k}\neq\emptyset
since
X
is complete, and thus
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(\limsup\mathcal{B}|^{{\overline{B}}_{0}})\geq 1
since
\cap_{k=1}^{\infty}{\overline{B}}_{k}\subset\limsup\mathcal{B}|_{{\overline{B}}_{0}}
.

While this argument seems simple, we will see it in the proof of our main theorem as embedded in one of the sub-cases. However, once we use different
\xi
, such as the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff gauge
\xi(B)={\rm rad}(B)^{k}
for
k>0
, the assumption doesn’t simply force one sub-ball but
many
balls which form a “second level”. This idea of levels underlies the second sub-case on the proof of our main theorem. Roughly, the idea is to take an iterated intersection of such levels. The proof is however more delicate, as a simple covering and doubling argument loses a factor which ought to be controlled.

2.
Applications of Theorem

The main motivation of this paper is to prove Theorem
1.2
. However, to demonstrate its importance, we apply it in different contexts: mass transference principle and diophantine approximations, structure and characterization of finite perimeter sets and finally, to size bounds for random limsup sets.

We find it curious, that Theorem
1.2
unifies these quite distinct contexts. Further, it is central, that our proofs rely mainly on simple covering arguments stated in Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
- which are completely classical and somewhat elementary. It is surprising to us, that such basic techniques yield such strong results. Further, we will see, that the simplification of the methods yields sharper and more general conclusions; in particular for the Mass Transference Principle and random Limit superior sets.

The final application below on generic curves, does not directly apply a version of Theorem
1.2
in its proof. It is instead proved directly from the covering Lemmas mentioned above. However, we include it here, since its closely connected to our proof of Federer’s characterization, and the proof technique is similar in spirit.

2.1.
Mass Transference Principle

The Mass Transference Principle was proven by Beresnevich and Velani in
[
7
]
. As the name suggests, it is a principle which allows to convert information about the Hausdorff-content of one collection of sets, to the Hausdorff-content of another collection of sets. Originally, both of these collections were sets of balls arising in Diophantine approximations. However, later work considered a variety of different types of sets. An excellent survey is provided in
[
3
]
. More references were provided above at the beginning of the introduction.

Before the statement, we introduce some terminology. To match many of the previous references, we will use
k
to denote the Hausdorff dimension of the space
X
. An increasing function
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is called a dimension function. Hausdorff contents and measures
\mathcal{H}^{f}
are defined in Section
3
. When
f
is a dimension function, Let
B^{f}=B(x,f(r)^{1/k})
for
B=B(x,r)
.

If
(\mathcal{B}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence of collections of balls, we define the limit superior set of their unions by

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}=\cap_{N=1}^{\infty}\cup_{i=N}^{\infty}\cup\mathcal{B}_{i}.

This corresponds to the set of points
p
, for which there exists an infinite increasing sequence
(i_{l})_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and balls
B_{l}\in\mathcal{B}_{i_{l}}
with
p\in B_{l}
for each
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. When the collections
\mathcal{B}_{i}
consist of a single set, that is
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{B_{i}\}
, we also write
\limsup_{i\to\infty}B_{i}
.

The classical Mass transference principle from
[
7
]
states the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorems 2 and 3 in
[
7
]
)
.

Suppose that
X
is complete and Ahlfors
k
-regular.
Let
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of (open) balls with
\lim_{i\to\infty}{\rm rad}(B_{i})=0
for which

\mathcal{H}^{k}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}B_{i}^{f}\cap B)=\mathcal{H}^{k}(B),

for each (open) ball
B\subset X
, then for any dimension function
f
for which
f(x)x^{-k}
is monotone, we have

\mathcal{H}^{f}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}B_{i}\cap B)=\mathcal{H}^{f}(B).

We reprove this result and extend it to consider
general collections of open sets
and
general gauge functions
. In particular, we consider also doubling gauge functions
\xi
which do not come from a dimension function. An important difference between the settings of Theorem
2.1
and our main Theorem
1.2
, is that in the Mass transference principle, we place an assumption on
all
balls in the space. Our generalized mass transference principle will also place such an assumption – in contrast to Theorem
1.2
which only places an assumption on the collection of balls defining the limit superior set.

First, we define limsup sets of collections of open sets. Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is a nested sequence of collections of sets. Then, we consider the limsup set
\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{i}
which is given by

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{i}:=\{x:\forall i\in{\mathbb{N}},\exists E_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i},x\in E_{i}\}.

In contrast to the case of balls, we do not impose any condition on the radius or diameter.

Next, we will consider two gauge functions
\xi
and
\nu
. Here,
\nu
plays the role of the
k
-Ahlfors regular measure on
X
in Theorem
2.1
, whereas
\xi
plays the role of the dimension function
f
. The assumption
f(x)x^{-k}
being monotone is translated by the concept of domination. If
\nu
and
\xi
are
D
-doubling gauge functions, we say that
\xi
dominates
\nu
, or
\nu\ll\xi
, if for any two balls
B\subset B^{\prime}
with
{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})\leq{\rm rad}(B)
it holds that

\frac{\xi(B^{\prime})}{\nu(B^{\prime})}\leq\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}.

The assumption of domination corresponds directly with the assumption of
f(x)x^{-k}
being monotone decreasing. Indeed, from here on, we leave out the discussion of the case when
f(x)x^{-k}
is monotone increasing. That case of Theorem
2.1
is nearly a triviality; see e.g.
[
7
]
.

The Alhfors regularity assumption is replaced by the assumption that
\xi
and
\nu
are doubling. Note that, if
\nu
is doubling and
\nu\ll\xi
, then it is direct to show that
\xi
is doubling as well. Finally, we need to define the transference collection of balls, which previously was denoted
B^{f}
.

Suppose that
\xi
and
\nu
are two doubling gauge functions. Given a set
E
, we say that a ball
B
is a
(\xi,\nu)
-transfer ball for
E
if
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(E\cap B)\geq\nu(B)
. We denote this relationship by
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B
. We say that a collection
\mathcal{B}
is a
(\xi,\nu)
-transfer collection of balls, if for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
there exists
E\in\mathcal{E}
so that
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B
. We write also
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
in this case. Compared to earlier work on transference principles, we do not assume that
E\subset B
; see e.g.
[
25
]
.

We give two examples when
X={\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and when
\nu(B)={\rm rad}(B)^{k}
is given by the (renormalized) Lebesgue measure and
\xi(B)=f({\rm rad}(B))
for some dimension function. We also assume that
f(x)x^{-k}
is monotone decreasing. This yields that
\xi
is doubling.

(1)

Since
f(x)x^{-k}
is monotone decreasing, then
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}}(B)\geq f({\rm rad}(B))
for all balls
B
. Thus, for all balls
B
, we have
B\succ_{\xi,\nu}B^{f}
.

(2)

Take any bounded open set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, and any
x\in E
. Suppose that
\lim_{r\to 0}f(r)r^{-k}=\infty
. Let
r_{0}=\sup\{r>0:\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E\cap B(x,r))\geq r^{k}\}
. By openness and boundedness of
E
, we have
r_{0}\in(0,\infty)
. Moreover, we get that the open ball
B_{x}=B(x,r_{0})
satisfies
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B_{x}
. In particular,
E\subset\bigcup_{x\in E}B_{x}
. Such a construction will play a crucial role in Lemma
3.16
and later in the proof of dimension bounds for random limsup sets.

Our generalized mass transference principle is stated as follows. Here,
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{B})=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}{\rm rad}(B)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and
\xi,\nu
be two
D
-doubling gauge functions with
\nu\ll\xi
. For every
\Delta>0
there exists a
\beta>0
for which the following holds.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is a sequence of collections of open sets with
\mathcal{E}_{i}\subset\mathcal{E}_{j}
for
i\geq j
. Suppose further that for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a collection
\mathcal{B}_{i}
of closed balls for which satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{i}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}_{i}

and
\mathcal{B}_{i}\subset\mathcal{B}_{j}
for
i\geq j
.

If for some open set
U\subset X
, and every ball
B\subset U
and any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}_{i}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)})\geq\Delta\nu(B),

and
\lim_{i\to\infty}{\rm rad}(\mathcal{B}_{i})=0
then for any ball
B\subset U
it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{i}\cap B)\geq\beta\xi(B).

We highlight the fact that this theorem does not impose any doubling or Alhfors regularity assumption on the base space
X
. Instead, the only assumptions are placed on the gauge functions
\xi
and
\nu
. However, in verifying that the assumption of the implication, i.e. that the
\nu
-content bound holds, one often needs to resort to some information on the space and its measure.

The Mass Transference Principe of Beresnevich and Velani follows from choosing
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\{B_{k}:k\geq i\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{B_{k}^{f}:k\geq i\}
. Note also that
\limsup_{k\to\infty}B_{k}^{f}\cap B\subset\bigcup_{i\geq N}\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap B
for each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. A detailed proof will be presented in Section
5.1
.

2.2.
Random limsup sets

We will study the problem of determining the Hausdorff measure and dimension of random limit superior sets in the case of a metric group
G
. Suppose that
G
is equipped with a left-invariant metric
d
(with which it is complete) and that it is unimodular, that is, there exists a bi-invariant Haar measure
\mu
.
Assume further, that
\mu
is
D
-doubling for some
D\geq 1
. Let
U
be any open set in
G
, with
\mu(U)\in(0,\infty)
and
(E_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence of bounded open sets in
G
with
\lim_{i\to\infty}\diam(E_{i})=0
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(1_{G},E_{i})=0
, where
1_{G}
is the unity element in the group
G
.

We consider the probability measure
P=\frac{\mu|_{U}}{\mu(U)}
. This generates a probability measure on
U^{\mathbb{N}}
given by independently sampling
\omega_{i}\in U
for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
according to the distribution of

. Let
\omega=(\omega_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

If

is a dimension function so that the function

is monotone decreasing and
\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{f(r)}{\mu(B(1_{G},r))}=\infty

(1)

There exists a constant
\beta>0
so that for a.e.
\omega=(\omega_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
we have for any ball

\mathcal{H}^{f}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i}\cap B)\geq\beta f({\rm rad}(B)).

(2)

\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\infty}(E_{i})=\infty.

A difference between Theorem
2.1
and the previous theorem is the assumption on monotonicity. Indeed, Theorem

is easy to show when

is monotone increasing, or where the function is monotone decreasing with a finite limit as

being monotone increasing.

Consider next the case, when

is

. Then, almost surely,
\mathcal{H}_{\dim}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i})\geq t_{0}
. On the other hand, if

and
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(E_{i})<\infty

\mathcal{H}^{s}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i})=0
. The latter statement is deterministic and holds for all
\omega_{i}
. These together prove the following.

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose that
\mu

\omega\in U^{\mathbb{N}}

\mathcal{H}_{\dim}(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i})=\inf\left\{s>0:\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(E_{i})<\infty\right\}=\sup\left\{t>0:\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{H}^{t}_{\infty}(E_{i})=\infty\right\}.

This theorem implies the main theorems in
[
12

. Further, a straightforward modification of our method would also yield versions of the the results of
[
17

for open sets. Their result applied also to limsup sets generated by sets

2
2

2

Indeed, the proof of Theorem

. Our techniques do have implications for their setting, but we do not touch upon them in this paper. Our theorem contains some finer information compared to earlier theorems. Indeed, it can be used for intermediate gauge functions, such as

Finally, consider the case

2.3.
Sets of finite perimeter

Sets of finite perimeter can be defined, in Euclidean spaces, as those sets
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for which

\sup\left\{\left|\int{\rm div}\psi(x)1_{E}(x)dx\right|:\psi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),|\psi(x)|\leq 1,\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\}<\infty,

where
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the collection of all smooth vector fields,
{\rm div}
is the divergence and
v\to|v|
is the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

A crucial fact about sets of finite perimeter is that they have many equivalent characterizations. The definition above is equivalent to the following one, which involves relaxations: There exists a sequence
f_{i}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{B(0,R)}|f_{i}(x)-1_{E}(x)|dx=0
for every
R>0
and

(2.5)

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\nabla f_{i}|(x)dx<\infty.

Here,
\nabla f
is the gradient of a function
f
.

While these analytic definitions are useful, to develop geometric measure theory, one also needs geometric characterizations. Federer showed in
[
16
, Theorem 4.5.11]
, that
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a set of finite perimeter if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial^{*}E)<\infty,
where
\partial^{*}E
is the measure theoretic boundary (see below). Remarkably, Panu Lahti
[
27
]
observed that Federer’s geometric characterization extends to general metric measure spaces which satisfy doubling and a Poincaré inequality, which we will shortly define. We present a
new
proof of this fundamental fact, and our proof is substantially more direct. Indeed, most of the proof is contained in this paper and relies on
[
5
]
only in one of the (previously known) directions. Before stating the characterization precisely, let us review some of the theory on sets of finite perimeter.

The Euclidean theory is classical, and well presented in
[
14
]
(see also
[
16
]
for a much more comprehensive treatment). The remarkable aspect about the definition using relaxations is that it allows for an extension to general metric spaces. Ambrosio developed the theory of finite perimeter sets in metric measure spaces; see
[
4
,
5
]
for original references and
[
6
]
for a nice survey.
The study of finite perimeter sets is a corner stone of geometric measure theory in general metric spaces. Further, it has provoked much study and had important applications; see e.g.
[
8
,
18
]
for imporant works in the context of so called Carnot groups. In metric measure spaces, it has sparked a rich study of functions of bounded variation and isoperimetric inequalities; see e.g.
[
26
,
29
,
24
]
. We will give a mostly self-contained proof of Federer’s characterization, and introduce only sufficienlty much terminology to state and prove the result.

In metric spaces, one needs a replacement for the gradient appearing in Equation (
2.5
). This is furnished by the notion of an
upper gradient
of Heinonen and Koskela
[
20
]
.
A Borel function
g:X\to[0,\infty]
is said to be an upper gradient for a function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
, if for every non-constant rectifiable curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
it holds that

(2.6)

|f(\gamma(0))-f(\gamma(1))|\leq\int_{\gamma}gds,

where on the right, we have the curve integral with respect to the length measure on
\gamma
.
3
3

3

In the case, where the left hand side would evaluate to
|\infty-\infty|
or
|-\infty-(-\infty)|
, we require
\int_{\gamma}gds=\infty
.

With the notion of upper gradient, Ambrosio
[
5
]
defined sets of finite perimeter set. First, let
L^{1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize{loc}}}}(X)
be the space of locally integrable functions. A measurable set
E\subset X
is a set of finite perimeter, if there exists a sequence
(f_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of functions
f_{i}\in L^{1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize{loc}}}}(X)
with upper gradients
g_{i}\in L^{1}
, so that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{B(0,R)}|f_{i}(x)-1_{E}(x)|dx=0
for every
R>0
and

(2.7)

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\int_{X}g_{i}(x)d\mu<\infty.

Without some regularity assumption on the space, sets of finite perimeter can behave quite wildly. For example, if
X
possesses no non-constant rectifiable curves, the function
g=0
is an upper gradient for any function, and every measurable set
E
is a set of finite perimeter. The proper assumptions, as identified in
[
5
]
, which yield a rich theory are those of a doubling and a Poincaré inequality.

Recall, that a metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
is said to be
D
-doubling, if

(2.8)

0<\mu(2B)\leq D\mu(B)<\infty\text{ for all (open or closed) balls }B\subset X.

Further
X
is said to satisfy a (
1-
)Poincaré inequality if there are constants
\lambda,c_{P}>0
so that for all functions
f\in L^{1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize{loc}}}}(X)
, all upper gradients
g
and all balls
B\subset X
we have

(2.9)

\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{B}}|f-f_{B}|\,d\mu\leq c_{P}{\rm rad}(B)\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda B}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda B}}g\,d\mu,

where
g_{A}:=\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}gd\mu=\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\int_{A}gd\mu
, whenever
A
is a measurable set with
\mu(A)\in(0,\infty)
, and
g|_{A}
is integrable.
A space
X
is called a PI-space, if there are constants
D,C_{P},\lambda>0
so that the previous holds.

The geometric characterization of a set being finite perimeter involves a measure theoretic notion of the boundary. First, define the measure theoretic interior
I_{E}
and exterior
O_{E}
for a measurable set
E\subset X
by

I_{E}=\left\{x\in X:\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}=1\right\},\text{ and }O_{E}=\left\{x\in X:\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\setminus E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}=1\right\}.

The measure theoretic boundary
\partial^{*}E
is given by
\partial^{*}E=X\setminus(I_{E}\cup O_{E})
, which is analogous to how the topological boundary is defined. It is also given by

(2.10)

\partial^{*}E=\{x\in X:\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(E\cap{\overline{B}}(x,r))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}>0,\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\setminus E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}>0\}.

The size of this boundary is naturally measured by the co-dimension-one Hausdorff-measure
\mathcal{H}^{h}
, which corresponds to the gauge-function
h({\overline{B}})=\frac{\mu({\overline{B}})}{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}
.
Federer’s characterization in a metric measure space now reads as follows.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
X
be a complete PI-space, and let
E\subset X
be a measurable set. The set
E
is of finite perimeter if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E)<\infty
.

Remark 2.12
.

In fact, the statement could be made slightly more quantitative: There is a constant
\eta
, so that
E
is a set of finite perimeter if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}_{\eta}E)<\infty
. Here,
\partial_{\eta}^{*}E
is a quantitative version of the boundary of
E
:

(2.13)

\partial_{\eta}^{*}E=\{x\in X:\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(E\cap{\overline{B}}(x,r))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}>\eta,\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\setminus E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}>\eta\}.

This version follows easily from our proof and
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
.
While we do not go there in this paper, this quantitative aspect has other implications as well. For example, one can follow the proofs here and
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
to show that the perimeter measure (which is comparable to
\mathcal{H}^{h}
), is supported on the set
\partial^{*}_{\eta}E
– which may be a strict subset of
\partial^{*}E
. Further, this would show that
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E\setminus\partial^{*}_{\eta}E)=0
. The
\eta
here does not depend on the constant
c_{P}
in the Poincaré inequality, but only on
\lambda
and the doubling constant
D
. In geodesic spaces,
\lambda
can be taken to be unity, and then the constant depends only on doubling (see e.g.
[
19
]
). This improves the dependence in constants of
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
. It indicates, that constants could be sharpened in other results as well.

We outline the argument here. The bridge between Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
2.11
is to consider the collection of balls which are half-empty and half-full of the set
E
:

\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)=\{{\overline{B}}:\min(\mu({\overline{B}}\cap E),\mu({\overline{B}}\setminus E))\geq\delta\mu({\overline{B}})\},

where
\delta>0
is a fixed constant. A classical argument from
[
26
]
shows that, for all
s>0
,
\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)|_{s}^{{\overline{B}}})
has a lower bound in terms of
\min(\mu({\overline{B}}\cap E),\mu({\overline{B}}\setminus E))
; see Lemma
6.6
. Theorem
1.2
then directly applies to give a lower bound for the content of
\limsup\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
.
Finally, a doubling argument given in Lemma
6.2
yields that
\limsup\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)\subset\partial^{*}E
, which proves the claim.

This argument is quite natural, and avoids much of the machinery used in
[
27
]
. However, Lahti has later used his tools in
[
28
]
to give a stronger version of Theorem
2.11
. We do not know if our techniques would also give a proof of this fact.

Finally, we note that the proof of Theorem
2.11
also directly gives the so called ”strong isoperimetric inequality”, see Theorem
6.11
. This was also proved in
[
27
]
.

2.4.
Modulus almost every curve

Underlying the Poincaré inequality (
2.9
) is the geometric property of possessing many curves.
The richness of curves can be measured in terms of the modulus of a curve family, which we briefly review. A curve is a continuous function
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
. If
\Gamma
is a collection of non-constant rectifiable curves, then we say that a Borel function
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
is admissible for
\Gamma
, and write
\rho\wedge\Gamma
, if
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds\geq 1
for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
. We define the
1
-modulus by the expression

{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma):=\inf_{\rho\wedge\Gamma}\int g\,d\mu.

A family of curves
\Gamma
is said to be
1
-exceptional, if
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma)=0
. Further, we say that a property
P
holds for
1
-a.e. rectifiable curve in
\Gamma
, if
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{P})=0
, when
\Gamma
is a family of non-constant rectifiable curves and
\Gamma_{P}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\text{ satisfies }P\}
. For much more about modulus, as well as the definition of
p-
modulus for other
p\in(1,\infty)
, see
[
21
]
.

Given the notion of a measure theoretic interior, exterior and boundary, it is geometrically intuitive that curves going form the measure theoretic interior to the exterior
ought
to pass through the measure theoretic boundary. In general, this can fail. However, in a PI-space, this property does not fail too badly, and we can reprove the following statement, which originally appeared in
[
29
, Corollary 6.4]
.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
X
be a PI-space.
If
E
is a set of finite perimeter, then
1
-Mod almost every curve in
\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}
passes through the measure theoretic boundary.

This property is deeply connected to Theorem
2.11
, and we refer to
[
29
, Section 6]
and
[
27
]
for a more detailed discussion.

3.
Preliminaries

First, we state some of our notational conventions. We write
C=C(a,b,c,\dots)
, when
C
is a constant which can be bounded by the quantities
a,b,c,\dots
.
When the dependence of a constant is not so important, we write
A\lesssim B
or
B\gtrsim A
to indicate that there exists a constant
C
for which
A\leq CB
, and where
C
depends only on some universal constants – such as
D,\lambda
and
c_{P}
from the definition of a PI-space – and not on specific data, such as a particular set
E
.

Throughout the paper, we will be considering only complete metric spaces
X
. Given an open or closed ball
C
, we denote its radius by
{\rm rad}(C)
. For us, a ball
B(x,r)
is specified by a center
x
and a radius
r>0
. As sets, two different centers and radii may produce the same set. However, balls will be considered not merely as sets, but with a fixed center and radius. Formally, this would require us to define ”balls” as tuples
(x,r)
, and associate to each such tuple a set
B(x,r)
. We abuse notation and identify the set with a tuple, and call both of them balls. Thus, whenever we discuss balls, or collections of balls, each of the balls will have an associated and fixed radius and center.

We remind the reader also that whenever we say ”ball”, we mean either an open or closed ball. We will say open ball, and closed ball, where it is important to restrict to such balls. Further, in order to not introduce odd notation, we use
B
to denote either any ball, or an open ball, and
{\overline{B}}
will exclusively denote closed balls. Finally, where it is important, we will state if a claim holds for both open and closed balls.

Consider a collection
\mathcal{C}
of open balls. We define
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm rad}(C)
. If
A\subset X
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{A}
the collection defined by

\mathcal{C}_{A}=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:C\cap A\neq\emptyset\}.

Note that
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is distinct from
\mathcal{C}|^{A}
, which was defined in the introduction. We say that
\mathcal{C}
covers
A
if
A\subset\bigcup\mathcal{C}
. These notions also can be defined for collections of closed balls.

If
\xi
is a doubling gauge, we define the (spherical) Hausdorff (
\xi-
)content of a subset
A\subset X
with the equality

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r}(A)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c):A\subset\bigcup\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}\text{ is a collection of open balls with }{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq r\right\}.

An increasing function
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is called a dimension function. When
\xi(B)=f({\rm rad}(B))
for some dimension function
f
, then we also use the notation
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{r}(A)
for the content.

Define the Hausdorff measure by
\mathcal{H}^{f}(A)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\delta}(A)
. When
f(r)=r^{s}
, for some
s\in(0,\infty)
, we sometimes simplify the notion by writing
\mathcal{H}^{s}
. We also define the Hausdorff dimension of a set
A
as

\mathcal{H}_{{\rm dim}}(A):=\sup\{t>0:\mathcal{H}^{t}(A)=\infty\}=\inf\{s>0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)=0\}.

3.1.
Covering Lemmas

We recall the following form of the usual 5-covering lemma. See
[
21
, Section 3.3]
for a proof. In the statement, the balls may either be closer or open.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
X
is a separable metric space and that
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of balls with
\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}{\rm rad}(B)<\infty
. There exists a countable disjoint subcollection
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
with

\bigcup\mathcal{B}\subset\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}^{\prime},

and so that for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
there is a ball
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
with
B^{\prime}\cap B\neq\emptyset
and
2{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})\geq{\rm rad}(B)
.

The following lemma is a way to obtain a disjoint covering for a union of sets, while allowing to throw away a small set.
We use it to reduce the inflation factor in Lemma
3.2
. Before its statement, we comment on the notation
C(\mathcal{B}|_{s})
in the statement. It means that one first restricts to balls with radii less than
s
, and then inflates them. Unfortunately, these operations are not quite commutative. Later, we will see even operations of the form

, where we first apply the restrictions to balls

with
{\rm rad}(B)\leq s
(which do commute with each other), and then inflate. To avoid confusion, we will mostly perform the operations in this order (restrict first and then inflate). There will be only a few exceptions to this rule, and in all instances we will use parenthesis to explicate the order of operations.

Note that in the statement, the balls may be open or closed.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of balls contained in
B_{0}
, and that
\xi
is a doubling gauge function with the property that for every disjoint collection

For every

,

\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathcal{B}
and a positive

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(\mathcal{B})}\left(\bigcup C\left(\mathcal{B}|_{s}\right)\setminus\bigcup(1+\Delta)\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}\right)<\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
be a collection coming from Lemma

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\xi(B)<\infty.

Thus, by the doubling property of

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}\xi(10CB)<\epsilon,

and so that

Choose
s=\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}\frac{\Delta}{2C}r(B)
. For each

there exists a ball
B\in\mathcal{B}
with

. By the choice of the collection
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, there is a ball

and so that

.

First, suppose that

. By the definition of

, we have

. Then,

.

Otherwise, suppose that

. Then

and

. Thus

\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B},{\rm rad}(B)\leq s}CB\subset\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}(1+\Delta)B\cup\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}5CB.

Then

\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B},{\rm rad}(B)\leq s}CB\setminus\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}(1+\Delta)B\subset\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}5CB

and the claim follows from Estimate (

3.2.
Content bounds for unions

The assumption for our main Theorem involves a content bound for a union of sets. In this section, we give a few crucial lemmas that give bounds for such unions. They all involve an idea that we may transfer a content bound for one collection of sets to a content bound for another collection. In the process, we transition from a gauge
\nu
to a gauge
\xi
. While this section is a bit technical, we will highlight certain features of the proofs, which will be useful later. Indeed, we aim to structure the proofs in a way that the similarities become apparent.

We start with a useful lemma, which allows us to transition between different scales. We briefly discribe the idea of the statement and its proof structure. Given a set
E\subset B=B(p,R)
, our estimates will involve lower bounds for
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
. However, due to the inflation factors from Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we often go through a bound for
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R^{\prime}}(E)
for some
R^{\prime}<R
. In general,
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)\leq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R^{\prime}}(E)
and there is no opposite inequality. However, the only way that
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
is much smaller than
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R^{\prime}}(E)
, is if the infimum involved in defining
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
is realized by a cover
\mathcal{C}
with some balls of radius larger than
R^{\prime}
.
We call this the
big ball case
. Many of our arguments will need to consider such a case, and we use this term for any setting where one of the relevant balls is quite large. In each of these cases, one applies the doubling of the gauge function to inflate the bound to the appropriate scale. The remaining case, which we often call the
small ball case
, is often argued differently.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\xi
be a
D
-doubling gauge function,
E\subset B
where
B=B(p,R)
, and
L\geq 1
. Then

\min\left(\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R/L}(E),D^{-\lfloor\log_{2}(L)\rfloor-8}\xi(B)\right)\leq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any cover of
E
by open balls with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq R
. We will show that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)\geq\min\left(\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R/L}(E),D^{-\lfloor\log_{2}(L)\rfloor-8}\xi(B)\right)
. Without loss of generality, assume that
c\cap B\neq\emptyset
for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Big ball case:
First, we assume that
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})>R/L
. Then, there exists a
c_{b}\in\mathcal{C}
with
{\rm rad}(c_{b})>R/L
. Let
k=\lfloor\log_{2}(\frac{R}{{\rm rad}(c_{b})})\rfloor+1.
Then,
8R\geq{\rm rad}(2^{k+2}c_{b})\geq 4R
and

B\subset 2^{k+2}c_{b}\subset B(p,2{\rm rad}(2^{k+2}c_{b})),

and by doubling
\xi(c_{b})\geq D^{-k-7}\xi(B)
. Therefore,
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)\geq\xi(c_{b})\geq D^{-k-7}\xi(B)
.

Small ball case:
Next, we assume that
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq R/L
. Then the claim follows from the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R/L}(E)
.
∎

Next, we give the main lemma which is used to transfer a
\nu
-content bound to a
\xi
-content bound. Recall, that

(3.6)

E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B\,\,\text{ if }\,\,\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(E\cap B)\geq\nu(B).

Further,
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
if for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
there exists an
E\in\mathcal{E}
so that
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B
. Further, recall, that
\nu\ll\xi
if for all balls
B\subset B^{\prime}
with
{\rm rad}(B)\leq{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})
, we have

(3.7)

\frac{\nu(B)}{\xi(B)}\leq\frac{\nu(B^{\prime})}{\xi(B^{\prime})}.

In the statement, any ball which appears can be taken as closed or open.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a collection of balls, and
\mathcal{E}
a collection of sets. Suppose that
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
and that
\nu
and
\xi
are
D
-doubling gauge functions with
\nu\ll\xi
. Then, for a constant
\delta=\delta(D)=D^{-12}2^{-1}>0
the following property holds.

For any ball
B
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B)\geq\delta\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right).

Proof.

Let
B
be an arbitrary ball. By Lemma
3.5
,

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B)\geq\min\left(\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)/20}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B\right),D^{-12}\xi(B)\right).

Since

\xi(B)=\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\nu(B)\geq\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right),

it suffices to give a lower bound for
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)/20}(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B)
of the desired form.

The proof will be divided to cases which depend on the radii of the balls in
\mathcal{B}
.

Big ball case:
Assume that
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)})>{\rm rad}(B)/15
. By this assumption, there exists a ”big ball”
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}
with
{\rm rad}(B)\geq{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})>{\rm rad}(B)/15
. Since
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
, there exists a set
E\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})}(E\cap B^{\prime})\geq\nu(B^{\prime})
. Since
E\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
E\cap B^{\prime}\subset\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B
. Further,
B\subset 2^{k}B^{\prime}\subset 4B
which holds for an integer
k
with
1\leq k\leq 5
) By doubling of
\nu
. The definition (
3.6
), we get

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)/20}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B\right)\geq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})}(E\cap B^{\prime})\geq\nu(B^{\prime})\geq D^{-6}\nu(B)\geq D^{-6}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B^{\prime})}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right).

Small ball case:
With the proof established in the previous case, we are left to consider the case when
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)})\leq{\rm rad}(B)/15
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of balls with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq{\rm rad}(B)/20
so that
\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cap B\subset\bigcup\mathcal{C}
.
We will show that

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)\geq\delta\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right).

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
c\cap B\neq\emptyset
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
. Taking an infimum over all collections
\mathcal{C}
then yields the desired bound.

First, we will need to modify the collection
\mathcal{B}
in a way to obtain disjoint balls with some separation property. By applying Lemma
3.2
to the collection
3(\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)})
, we can find a collection
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}
so that
3\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a disjoint collection and so that
\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\subset\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.
Since
\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\subset\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)\geq\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right).

The collection
\mathcal{C}
covers
\mathcal{E}\cap B
. Since
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
, then Equation (
3.6
) implies that for every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, there must be a
c\in\mathcal{C}
which intersects it. By considering how big the radii of such intersecting
c
are, we will then divide our estimates two two cases: the
inflation case
, and the
disjoint case
. In the first case, the radius of
c
is quite large, and the entire ball
B^{\prime}
can be easily covered by
inflating

c
. In the second case, the radius of all the
c
which which intersect
B^{\prime}
are small, and certain sub-collections of
\mathcal{C}
are
disjoint
. In both cases, the property guarantees a desired covering property or estimate. See Figure
1
for a picture of the argument.

inflation case
and the

. On the top is depicted are two sets of balls. The gray ones signify balls in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
and the black ones balls in
\mathcal{C}
. On the left, those balls from
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
have been chosen, which only intersect balls
c\in\mathcal{C}
with small radii. In this case, its crucial to observe that no ball
c\in\mathcal{C}
can intersect two of them. This observation uses separation. On the right, the balls
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
are shown which intersect some large balls in
\mathcal{C}
. In this case there is no disjointness, but an inflation can be used to obtain a cover.

The cases will correspond to two sub-collections of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\text{{Inflation: }}\mathcal{B}_{b}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{B_{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:\exists c\in\mathcal{C}\text{ with }c\cap B_{b}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }{\rm rad}(c)\geq{\rm rad}(B_{b})\},

\displaystyle\text{{Disjoint: }}\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:\forall c\in\mathcal{C}\text{ with }c\cap B_{s}\neq\emptyset\text{ we have }{\rm rad}(c)<{\rm rad}(B_{s})\}.

Inflation case:
Consider first the collection
\mathcal{B}_{b}^{\prime}
. By the definition, and the inequality between radii, we have
\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}_{b}^{\prime}\subset\bigcup 20\mathcal{C}
. In particular, by using doubling and the relation
\nu\ll\xi
(recall (
3.7
)), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\nu(c)\frac{\xi(c)}{\nu(c)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\xi(2B)}{\nu(2B)}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\nu(c)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D^{-7}\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\nu(20c)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D^{-7}\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}_{b}^{\prime}\right).

Disjoint case
: Consider next the collection
\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
.
For each
B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
define
\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:c\cap B_{s}\neq\emptyset\}
.
Since for each
c\in\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}
we have
{\rm rad}(c)\leq{\rm rad}(B_{s})
and
c\cap B_{s}\neq\emptyset
, we get
c\subset 3B_{s}.

Let
B_{s},B^{\prime}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
be two distinct balls. By the construction of the collection above,
3B_{s}\cap 3B_{s}^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Therefore
\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{B^{\prime}_{s}}=\emptyset
. Further, for each
B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
, by assumption, we can choose a set
E\in\mathcal{E}
so that
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B_{s}
. By choosing for each
B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
one such
E
, we define
E_{B_{s}}=E\cap{B_{s}}
for such a ball.

We have
E_{B_{s}}\subset\bigcup\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}
. Since
E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B_{s}
, the defining equation (
3.6
) gives

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{B}}\xi(C)\geq\nu(B).

Thus, from the pairwise disjointness of
\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}
for distinct
B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(C)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{B_{s}}}\xi(C)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-4}\sum_{B_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{s}^{\prime}}\nu(15B_{s})

(3.10)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-4}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}_{g}^{\prime}).

At the end, we combine the estimates from the disjoint and inflation cases. Indeed, by adding (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) we get

\displaystyle 2\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(C)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-7}\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\right)\left(\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}_{g}^{\prime}\right)+\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}_{b}^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-7}\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup 15\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\right)\geq D^{-7}\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B)}{\nu(B)}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}).

∎

A convenient corollary of this is the following result.

Corollary 3.11
.

Suppose that
X
is a complete metric space. Let
\xi
be a
D
-doubling gauge function and
\eta,\beta\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of balls for which

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}(\eta B)\geq\beta\xi(B)

holds for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

Then, for any ball
B_{0}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B_{0})}\left(\bigcup\eta\left(\mathcal{B}|^{B_{0}}\right)\right)\geq D^{-11}2^{-1}\beta\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B_{0})}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B_{0}}_{{\rm rad}(B_{0})}\right).

Proof.

Let
\nu=\beta\xi
,
\mathcal{E}=\{\eta B:B\in\mathcal{B}|^{B_{0}}\}
. We have for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
, by assumption, that
\eta B\succ_{\xi,\nu}B
. Thus,
\mathcal{E}\succ_{\xi,\nu}\mathcal{B}
.
Lemma
3.8
applied to the collection
\mathcal{E}
and the ball
B_{0}
gives:

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\eta\left(\mathcal{B}|^{B_{0}}\right)\right)\geq D^{-11}2^{-1}\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\right)\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|^{B_{0}}_{{\rm rad}(B_{0})}\right).

Since
\xi(B)/\nu(B)=\frac{1}{\beta}>0
for all balls, we obtain the claim.
∎

We will also need the following corollary.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
\xi,\nu
be
D
-doubling gauge functions with
\nu\ll\xi
. With
\delta=2^{-1}D^{-7}
the following holds. Suppose that
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
is a fixed ball,
O\subset B_{0}
is an open set,
A\subset O
is its subset.
If for any
x\in O
there is a
r_{x}\in(0,d(x,X\setminus O))
so that when
r\in(0,r_{x})
and
B=B(x,r)\subset O
, we have
A\succ_{\xi,\nu}B
then we have

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r_{0}}(A)\geq\delta\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of balls with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq r_{0}
which covers
A
. We will show that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)\geq 2^{-1}D^{-7}\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).
Without loss of generality, assume that
c\cap B_{0}\neq\emptyset
for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
. Further, we can assume that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)<\infty
. Again, our proof will involve big ball and small ball cases.

Big ball case:
If there exists a
c\in\mathcal{C}
with
{\rm rad}(c)\geq r_{0}/2
, then
B_{0}\subset 8c\subset B(x_{0},2{\rm rad}(8c))
. In particular
\xi(c)\geq D^{-5}\xi(B_{0})\geq D^{-5}\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\nu(B_{0})
. Since
\{B_{0}\}
is a cover of
O
, we have
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O)\leq\nu(B_{0})
. Combining these observations yields the claim.

Small ball case:
Thus, in what follows, we may assume that
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq r_{0}/2
.

Let
{\mathbf{B}}=O\setminus\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}
. We will divide the rest of the proof to two cases. First, consider the case
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}({\mathbf{B}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).
The opposite inequality will be considered after this. By sub-additivity of the Hausdorff-content, we have
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap O)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

Since
2\mathcal{C}
is a cover of
\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap O
and
{\rm rad}(2\mathcal{C})\leq 2{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq r_{0}
(by our setup), we have

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\nu(2c)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

Since
\xi\ll\nu
,
\xi
and
\nu
are
D
-doubling and since
c\subset 2B_{0}
, for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(2c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\xi(2c)}{\nu(2c)}\nu(2c)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\xi(c)}{\nu(c)}\nu(2c)

\displaystyle\geq D^{-2}\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\nu(2c)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D^{-2}}{2}\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

This yields the desired estimate in the case
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}({\mathbf{B}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

Next, consider the case
\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}({\mathbf{B}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).
This case will lead to a contradiction, which proves the claim. Choose,
\sigma=D^{-11}2^{-3}\min(1,\frac{\xi(B_{0})}{\nu(B_{0})})\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O)

Since
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\xi(c)<\infty

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

, and thus

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(\mathbf{B})\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{r_{0}}(O).

Let now

3.3.
Gauges coming from measures

In this subsection we will focus on the case, where our transference gauge
\nu
is given by a Borel measure on
X
which is finite and positive on all balls. That is, there is a Borel measure
\overline{\nu}
so that
\nu(B)=\overline{\nu}(B)
for all open balls and
\overline{\nu}(B)\in(0,\infty)
for every ball
B\subset X
. We do not assume that boundaries of balls have zero measure, and we define the gauge for closed balls
{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)
by setting
\nu({\overline{B}})=\nu(B)
, where
B=B(x,r)
. Since
\nu
, and therefore
\overline{\nu}
, will be assumed to be
D
-doubling, this convention only affect the values up to a constant factor. In particular, for all balls
B
, whether open or closed, it holds that
\nu(B)\leq\overline{\nu}(B)\leq D\nu(B)
.

By a slight abuse of notation, we use
\nu
also to denote this measure
\overline{\nu}
, and say that the gauge
\nu
comes from a measure.

Given a set
E\subset X
, let
\mathcal{B}_{E,\xi,\nu}=\{B(x,r):E\succ_{\xi,\nu}B(x,r),x\in E\}
, and define a set

(3.14)

B_{E}=\bigcup\mathcal{B}_{E,\xi,\nu}.

With a few assumptions on
\nu
and
\xi
, we can give precise bounds on the volume of
B_{E}
. These play a central role in proving our theorem on random limsup sets, Theorem
2.3
.

In this Lemma, the balls used will all be open. First, a brief remark about the statement.

Remark 3.15
.

If
\xi(B)=f({\rm rad}(B))
for a dimension function
f
, then we could replace
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
by
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)
in the statement. This follows, because the assumption
E\subset B(p,R)
implies that
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)=\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
. Indeed,
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)\leq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
for all gauge functions. However,
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)\leq f(R)
since
E\subset B(p,R)
. Thus, the infimum in
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)
is obtained by a covering
\mathcal{C}
with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq R
, and thus
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{\infty}(E)\geq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)
. In fact, for such gauges, we could remove the factor
\frac{\xi(B(p,R))}{\nu(B(p,R))}
from the upper bound. The only change to the proof comes in the proof of the upper bound. We will indicate this modification with a remark.

Lemma 3.16
.

Assume that
\nu,\xi
are
D
-doubling gauge functions. Suppose further that

•

\nu\ll\xi
,

•

\nu
comes from a measure,

•

\lim_{r\to 0}\nu(B(x_{0},r))=0
, and

•

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\xi(B(x_{0},r))}{\nu(B(x_{0},r))}=\infty
for each
x_{0}\in X
.

There exists a constant
C
so that for every bounded open set
E
,
p\in X
and
R>0
, with
E\subset B(p,R)\subset X
, we have
E\subset B_{E}
and either
\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)\geq\nu(X)
or

\frac{1}{C}\nu(B_{E})\min\left(1,\frac{\xi(B(p,R))}{\nu(B(p,R)))}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{R}(E)\leq C\frac{\xi(B(p,R))}{\nu(B(p,R))}\nu(B_{E}).

Proof.

For each
x\in E
define
r_{x}=\sup\{r\in(0,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{r}(B(x,r)\cap E)\geq\nu(B(x,r))\}
. We will first argue that
r_{x}\in(0,\infty]
for each
x\in E
by showing that the set defining
r_{x}
is non-empty.

Non-emptyness:
Fix
x\in E
. By openness of
E

4.
Proofs of main results

In this section we give the proofs of the main Hausdorff content theorems. We show the content bounds in a somewhat unconventional way. We first consider a collection of balls
\mathcal{C}
so that
\sum_{c\in C}\xi(c)
is quite small. Then, we find a point
p\in\limsup(1+\delta)\mathcal{B}
that is not covered. The contrapositive of this statement is that any covering of
\limsup(1+\delta)\mathcal{B}
must have the corresponding lower bound for
\sum_{c\in C}\xi(c)
. By definition, this yields the content bound for the limsup set.

The point
p
is found in one of two ways. In the first case, we find
p
within an intersection of a sequence of poorly covered balls, which we call “bad balls” – This is called the
nested bad balls case
. In the second case, we find
p
within an intersection of compact sets
K_{k}
. The crucial insight is that if the first case does not apply, that is, there does not exist such a nested sequence of bad balls, then the conditions of Lemma
3.3
are satisfied, and we obtain
K_{k}
as a union of finitely many poorly covered balls. The second case is called the
nested sets case
. See Figure
2
for a depiction of these cases.

Figure 2.
A depiction of the two cases, the
nested bad balls case
and the
nested sets case
. The dashed regions indicate the balls in
\mathcal{C}
, and the gray balls indicate balls in
\mathcal{B}
. In the nested bad balls case, depicted on the left, one can find a nested sequence of balls poorly covered by
\mathcal{C}
. In the nested sets case, such a sequence can not be found. However, this forces a certain sum to be finite, which permits us to construct levels of balls, at different scales. In either case, one finds a point

which can not be covered by
\mathcal{C}
.

From properties P4, P1, P2, subadditivity and estimate (

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{1})}(K_{k}\setminus K_{l})\leq\Delta 8^{-l}\xi({\overline{B}}_{1}).

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{1})}(K_{k})-\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{1})}(K_{k}\setminus K_{l})

Now,
(\overline{K}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a nested sequence of non-empty closed sets. If

was assumed compact, or proper, then each set
{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
would be compact, and we could conclude that

. However, our space

is only complete, and we need to proceed slightly differently. The argument mimics showing that a totally bounded metric space satisfies the finite intersection property.

We say that a ball
{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{A}_{l}
is good, if
{\overline{B}}
intersects

for infinitely many

. Property P3 then implies that the centers
x^{i}
of the balls
\mathcal{B}^{i}
form a Cauchy sequence, and converge to some point

by completeness. It is direct to show that

.

By using
\overline{K}_{k}\subset K_{k}
and property P1, we get that
p\in(1+\delta)(\mathcal{B}_{j_{k}}^{b}|^{{\overline{B}}_{1}})
for every

. Consequently,

. The second case of the proof is complete once we show that

.

Indeed, if

with

and

. Choose

{\overline{B}}_{p}\in(1+\delta)\left(\mathcal{B}^{b}_{j_{k}}|^{{\overline{B}}_{1}}\right)
for which

since

{\rm rad}(c)\leq\frac{\delta}{(1+\delta)8}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{p})\leq\frac{r_{k}}{8}
which is a contradiction to the choice of

.
∎

5.2.
Random Limsup sets

In this subsection, we prove the main result on random limit superior sets, Theorem

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G
be a unimodular group,

a left-invariant metric on

and

a bi-invariant Haar measure on
G

is any open bounded set with

\lim_{i\to\infty}\diam(E_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}d(1_{G},E_{i})=0

(1)

For

P^{\mathbb{N}}
-a.e.

\mu(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i}\cap B)=\mu(B)

(2)

\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(E_{i})=\infty.

Suppose that
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu(E_{i})<\infty

\mu(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i})=0

\displaystyle P(A_{i}(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(\{\omega_{i}:\omega_{i}^{-1}\in E_{i}x^{-1}\})}{\mu(U)}

i\leq j
. Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}{\rm rad}(B_{E_{i}})=0
, we get

\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}_{i}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right)\geq\mathcal{H}^{\nu}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}|^{B}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\right)=\nu(B)

for every
B\subset U
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.2
, we get our claim:

\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\omega_{i}E_{i}\cap B\right)=\mathcal{H}^{\xi}_{{\rm rad}(B)}\left(\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{i}\cap B)\geq\beta f({\rm rad}(B)\right)

for each
B\subset U
.
∎

6.
Applications: Sets of finite perimeter

6.1.
Preliminaries

To be self-contained, we give a few basic properties of sets of finite perimeter.

Throughout this section
(X,d,\mu)
will be a PI-space. Recall the definitions involved (
2.8
) and (
2.9
). Further, in this section,
\lambda,c_{P}
and
D
will denote the constants from these definitions.

We will use the gauge defined for closed balls by
h({\overline{B}})=\frac{\mu({\overline{B}})}{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}
, which yields the co-dimension-one Hausdorff contents
\mathcal{H}^{h}_{s}
, for
s>0
and the co-dimension-one Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{h}
. For open balls
B=B(x,r)
, we define
h(B)=h({\overline{B}}(x,r))
in order to enforce our condition that

h(B)=h({\overline{B}})
whenever
{\rm rad}(B)={\rm rad}({\overline{B}})
.

The geometric characterization of sets of finite perimeter involves considering a quantity measuring the relative density of a subset
E\subset X
, and its complement, at a given scale. For a (closed or open) ball
B
, we denote this quantity with

\Theta(E,B)=\frac{\min(\mu(E\cap B),\mu(B\setminus E))}{\mu(B)}.

Sets
E
with
\Theta(E,B)=\frac{1}{2}
for a given ball
B
, can be thought to be ”half-full and half-empty” – although doubling will force us to tweak with this equality slightly.

For any set
E\subset X
, and
\delta>0
define the collections of ”half-empty and half-full balls”

(6.1)

\mathcal{B}(E,\delta):=\{{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)\subset X:\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})\geq\delta\}.

For consistency with our Theorem
1.2
, we will use closed balls from here on out. Note that, as in Remark
1.3
, we could also work with open balls by modifying the estimates with a factor. Our choices here are driven mostly with the need to be consistent, and to avoid switching between open and closed balls needlessly.

The bridge between our main Theorem
1.2
and the size of
\partial^{*}E
is the following lemma. Recall the definition of the measure theoretic boundary from Equation (
2.10
), and its quantitative version (
2.13
).

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that
(X,d,\mu)
is
D
-measure doubling. For any
\delta>0,L\geq 1
there is a
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that we have

\limsup L\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)\subset\partial_{\delta D^{-k}}^{*}E.

Proof.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
4L\leq 2^{k}
.
Let
p\in\limsup L\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
. Then, there exists a sequence
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of points
x_{i}\in X
and a sequence
(r_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of positive real numbers with
\lim_{i\to\infty}r_{i}=0
with
{\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i})\in\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
and
p\in{\overline{B}}(x_{i},Lr_{i})
for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

By definition of
\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
, we have
\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i}))\geq\delta
. That is
\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i})\cap E)\geq\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i}))
and
\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i})\setminus E)\geq\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i}))
. Consider
s_{i}=2Lr_{i}
. Then
{\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i})\subset{\overline{B}}(p,s_{i})\subset{\overline{B}}(x,4Lr_{i})
, and by doubling we get

\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},s_{i})\cap E)\geq\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i}))\geq D^{-k}\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},s_{i}))
and
\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},s_{i})\setminus E)\geq\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},r_{i}))\geq D^{-k}\delta\mu({\overline{B}}(x_{i},s_{i}))
.

Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}s_{i}=0
, we have

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(p,r)\cap E)}{\mu(B(p,r))}\geq\delta D^{-k}\text{ and }\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(p,r)\setminus E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(p,r))}\geq\delta D^{-k}.

Thus,
p\in\partial^{*}_{D^{-k}\delta}(E)
by definition.

∎

Next, we need a method to find points in
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
. The following lemma gives us this. It is a modified version of an argument that originally appeared in
[
26
, Proposition 3.9]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
E\subset X
and suppose that
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
is a continuous curve. If for some
r>0
, we have

\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(0),r)\cap E))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(0),r))}\geq 2^{-1}\text{ and }\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(1),r)\cap E))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(1),r))}\leq 2^{-1},

then, there exists a
t\in[0,1]
so that
\gamma(t)\in\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{2r}
.

Proof.

Let
T=\{t\in[0,1]:\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),r)\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),r))}\geq 2^{-1}\}
. We have
T\neq\emptyset
, since
0\in T
. Let
t=\sup T
. We will argue that
t
is the point we desire. There are a few cases to consider, which depend on the position of
t
.

(1)

Suppose
t=1
: We have
\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),r)\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),r))}\leq 2^{-1}
by assumption, and thus
\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r)\setminus E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))}\geq 2^{-1}D^{-1}
. There is a
t^{\prime}\in T
so that
d(\gamma(t^{\prime}),\gamma(t))\leq r
.
Doubling implies that
\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t^{\prime}),r))\geq D^{-2}\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))
. Thus,

(6.4)

\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))}\geq\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t^{\prime}),r)\cap E)}{D^{2}\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t^{\prime}),r))}\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}.

Therefore,
\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}
.

(2)

Suppose
t<1
: The argument is similar to the previous case, since we can choose a
t^{\prime}\in T
and a
t^{\prime\prime}\not\in T
with
d(\gamma(t^{\prime}),\gamma(t))\leq r
and
d(\gamma(t^{\prime\prime}),\gamma(t))\leq r
. With the same argument as above, we get Equation (
6.4
). On the other hand, by replacing
t^{\prime}
with
t^{\prime\prime}
and
E
with the set
X\setminus E
, we obtain

(6.5)

\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r)\setminus E))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))}\geq\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t^{\prime\prime}),r)\setminus E)}{D^{2}\mu({\overline{B}}(\gamma(t^{\prime\prime}),r))}\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}.

Now, estimates (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) give
\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r))\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}
.

In both cases,
{\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r)\in\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{2r}
and
\gamma(t)\in{\overline{B}}(\gamma(t),2r)
, which yields the claim.
∎

The second lemma concerns unions of the collection
\mathcal{B}(E,\delta)
. This lemma is needed to verify the assumption of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Suppose that
(X,d,\mu)
is a PI-space. There exists constants
C\geq 1
for which the following holds. For any
E\subset X
and any ball
{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}(x,r)\subset X
, and any
s>0
. We have

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{r}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|^{3\lambda{\overline{B}}}_{s}\right)\geq C\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})h({\overline{B}}).

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle I_{r,E}

\displaystyle=\{x\in{\overline{B}}:\inf_{r^{\prime}\in(0,r)}\frac{\mu(E\cap{\overline{B}}(x,r^{\prime}))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r^{\prime}))}>1/2\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle O_{r,E}

\displaystyle=\{x\in{\overline{B}}:\sup_{r^{\prime}\in(0,r)}\frac{\mu(E\cap{\overline{B}}(x,r^{\prime}))}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r^{\prime}))}<1/2\}.

By Lebesgue differentiation for a.e.
x\in E\cap{\overline{B}}
, we have
\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}=1
, and for a.e.
x\not\in E\cap B
we have
\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r)\cap E)}{\mu({\overline{B}}(x,r))}=1
. From this, we have by the previous combined with Egorov’s theorem that there is an
r_{0}\in(0,s/2)
so that
\mu(I_{r_{0},E})>\frac{\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})}{2}\mu({\overline{B}})
and
\mu(O_{r_{0},E})>\frac{\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})}{2}\mu({\overline{B}})
.

Choose a covering
\mathcal{C}
of the set
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{s}^{3\lambda{\overline{B}}}
with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})
. We will show that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)\geq C\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})h({\overline{B}})
for a constant
C>0
to be determined. There are a few cases to consider.

First, consider the cases when

\mu\left(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap I_{r_{0},E}\right)\geq\mu(I_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}})/2

or

\mu\left(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap O_{r_{0},E}\right)\geq\mu(O_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}})/2.

Then
\mu(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C})\geq\min\{\mu(I_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}}),\mu(O_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}})\}/2\geq\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})\mu({\overline{B}})/4
. But, then

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mu(\overline{c})}{{\rm rad}(\overline{c})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{D{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mu(2c)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{D{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}\mu\left(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\right)\geq\frac{\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})}{4D}h({\overline{B}}).

Thus, the desired bound holds with
C=(4D)^{-1}
.

Thus in the remaining case we can assume that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap I_{r_{0},E}\right)\leq\mu(I_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}})/2

\displaystyle\mu\left(\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}\cap O_{r_{0},E}\right)\leq\mu(O_{r_{0},E}\cap{\overline{B}})/2.

Let
g=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}1_{2c}+1_{2\lambda{\overline{B}}\setminus\lambda{\overline{B}}}
, and let
E=I_{r_{0},E}\setminus\bigcup 2\mathcal{C},F=O_{r_{0},E}\setminus\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}
. Define

f(x)=\min\left(\inf_{\gamma:E\mapsto x}\int_{\gamma}gds,1\right),

where the infimum is taken over all rectifiable curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
which connects a point in
\gamma(0)\in E
to
\gamma(1)=x
. The infimum includes constant curves, and thus
f|_{E}=0
.

On the other hand, we will show that
f|_{F}\geq 1
. Indeed, let
\gamma
be any rectifiable curve connecting
e\in E
to
x\in F
. We will show that
\int_{\gamma}gds\geq 1
, and by taking an infimum over
\gamma
, we obtain
f(x)=1
.

First, if there is a
t\in[0,1]
so that
\gamma(t)\not\in 2\lambda{\overline{B}}
, then
\gamma
contains a subsegment of length at least
\lambda{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})
, which is contained in
2\lambda{\overline{B}}\setminus\lambda{\overline{B}}
.
We have
g|_{2\lambda{\overline{B}}\setminus\lambda{\overline{B}}}\geq 1
, and thus
\int_{\gamma}gds\geq 1
as desired.
Thus, assume that for all
t\in[0,1]
we have
\gamma(t)\in 2\lambda{\overline{B}}
.

By Lemma
6.3
(applied with
r=r_{0}
) there exists a
t\in[0,1]
, so that
\gamma(t)\in\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{2r_{0}}\subset\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{s}
. By the previous paragraph, we have
\gamma(t)\in 2\lambda{\overline{B}}
.
Further, since
2r_{0}\leq{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})\leq\lambda{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})
, we also have
\gamma(t)\in\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|^{3\lambda{\overline{B}}}_{2r_{0}}
. Thus,
\gamma(t)\in\bigcup\mathcal{C}
. Let
c\in C
be such that
\gamma(t)\in c

However,
\gamma(0),\gamma(1)\not\in\bigcup 2\mathcal{C}
by the assumption on
\gamma
. Thus
\gamma
contains a subsegment of length at least
{\rm rad}(c)
within
2c
. Since
g|_{2c}\geq\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}
, we get
\int_{\gamma}gds\geq 1
. This shows
f|_{E}=1
.

It is immediate to show that
f:X\to[0,1]
. Further,
g
is an upper gradient of
f
(i.e satisfied (
2.6
)). This is proven by a classical argument from e.g.
[
23
, Proposition 3.2]
. Indeed, suppose that
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
, and that
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
is a rectifiable curve with
\gamma(0)=x_{1},\gamma(1)=x_{2}
. By symmetry, consider the case
f(x_{1})<f(x_{2})\leq 1
, and let
\gamma^{\prime}
be any curve connecting
E
to
x_{1}
, and form
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
by concatenating
\gamma^{\prime}
with
\gamma
. Then,

f(x_{2})\leq\inf_{\gamma_{2}:E\mapsto x_{2}}\int_{\gamma_{2}}g\,ds\leq\int_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}}g\,ds\leq\int_{\gamma^{\prime}}g\,ds+\int_{\gamma}g\,ds.

Taking an infimum over
\gamma^{\prime}
yields
f(x_{2})\leq\inf_{\gamma^{\prime}:E\mapsto x}g\,ds+\int_{\gamma}g\,ds\leq f(x_{1})+\int_{\gamma}g\,ds
. I.e.
|f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds
, as desired.

By the the Poincaré inequality:

(6.7)

\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}|f-f_{\overline{B}}|d\mu\leq c_{P}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}gd\mu.

First, we estimate the right hand side from above:

(6.8)

c_{P}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\lambda{\overline{B}}}}gd\mu=\frac{c_{P}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}{\mu(\lambda{\overline{B}})}\int\sum_{c\in C}\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}1_{2c}d\mu\leq\frac{c_{P}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}{\mu(\lambda{\overline{B}})}\sum_{c\in C}\frac{\mu(2c)}{{\rm rad}(c)}\leq\frac{c_{P}D^{L}}{h({\overline{B}})}\sum_{c\in C}h(c).

In the last inequality, we used the doubling of
\mu
, and
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
is such that
\lambda\leq 2^{L+2}
.

Thus, to obtain the desired lower bound for
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)
, we only need to show that
\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}|f-f_{\overline{B}}|d\mu\geq 2^{-3}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})
. This final claim we divide to two cases depending on
f_{\overline{B}}
:

(1)

Suppose
f_{\overline{B}}\leq 2^{-1}
: We have

\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}|f-f_{\overline{B}}|d\mu\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu(F)}{\mu({\overline{B}})}\geq\frac{1}{8}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})
, since
f|_{F}=1
.

(2)

Suppose
f_{\overline{B}}>2^{-1}
: We have

\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}}}|f-f_{\overline{B}}|d\mu\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu(E)}{\mu({\overline{B}})}\geq\frac{1}{8}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})
, since
f|_{E}=0
.

∎

The following lemma verifies the conditions of Lemma
3.3
for finite perimeter sets.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
X
be a PI-space and
E\subset X
a finite perimeter set. There is a constant
C>0
so that if
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset 3\lambda\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
is a disjoint collection, then

\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h({\overline{B}})\leq C.

Proof.

Assume that
E
is a set of finite perimeter. Therefore, there exists a sequence
f_{n}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(X)
and upper gradients
g_{n}\in L^{1}(X)
with

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}g_{n}\,d\mu<\infty.

By passing to a subsequence, we can replace
\liminf
by
\lim
.
Let
C=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}g_{n}\,d\mu
. Fix
r>0
and consider
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r}
. Assume that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset 3\lambda\mathcal{B}
is a disjoint collection. We will show that there is a constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(D,\lambda,C)
with

\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h(5{\overline{B}})\leq C^{\prime}.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}=\{{\overline{B}}_{1},\dots,{\overline{B}}_{K}\}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be an arbitrary finite subcollection. It suffices to prove that
\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}}h(5B)\leq C^{\prime}
.
For each
i=1,\dots,K
, we can choose a
N_{i}
so that for
n\geq N_{i}
we have
\int_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}|1_{E}-f_{n}|\,d\mu\leq 2^{-4}D^{-2}\mu({\overline{B}}_{i})
.

Since
\Theta(E,\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i})\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}
for all
i=1\dots,K
, we get for each
n\geq N:=\max\{N_{i}:i=1,\dots,K\}
that

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}|f_{n}-f_{n}|_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}|d\mu

\displaystyle\geq\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{i}}}|1_{E}-1_{E}|_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}|-|f_{n}-1_{E}|-|1_{E}|_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}-f_{n}|_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}|d\mu

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-1}D^{-2}-2^{-3}D^{-2}\geq 2^{-2}D^{-2}.

Therefore, by the Poincaré inequality, we get for each
i=1,\dots,K
and any
n\geq N
that

2^{-2}D^{-4}\leq c_{P}{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{i})\mathchoice{\mathop{\vrule width=6.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.5pt\kern-8.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}}{\mathop{\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.6pt\kern-6.0pt\intop}\nolimits_{{\overline{B}}_{i}}}g_{n}\,d\mu.

Reorganizing terms, and using doubling, we get
2^{-5}D^{-7}c_{P}^{-1}h(5{\overline{B}}_{i})\leq\int_{\lambda{\overline{B}}_{i}}g_{n}\,d\mu
. Summing over
i
, and using doubling and the distjointness of
{\overline{B}}_{i}
, we get a constant
L=c_{P}D^{7}2^{5}
so that

\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}}h(5{\overline{B}})\leq L\int g_{n}\,d\mu

for all
n\geq N
. By setting
C^{\prime}=2LC
and sending
n\to\infty
then the desired bound is obtained.
∎

Finally, we give a characterization of sets of finite perimeter using unions of sets. This characterization is essentially known (see e.g.
[
26
, Theorem 6.2]
and
[
24
, Theorem 4.6]
), but our proof and statement are slightly different and we present a full argument in order to be self-contained.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
X
be a PI-space. A subset
E
is of finite perimeter, if and only if there are constants
C,L>0
so that for every
r>0
we have

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{Lr}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r})\leq C.

Proof.

First prove the necessity. That is, assume that
E
is of finite perimeter. Fix
r>0
and consider
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r}
. Apply Lemma
3.2
to get a disjoint collection
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset 3\lambda\mathcal{B}
with
\bigcup\mathcal{B}\subset\bigcup 3\lambda\mathcal{B}\subset 5\bigcup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
6.9
and doubling there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
with

\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h(5{\overline{B}})\leq C^{\prime},

from which the claim that
\mathcal{H}^{h}_{Lr}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r})\leq DC^{\prime}
follows with
L=10\lambda
. (The extra factor of
2
comes from the need to transition from closed to open balls here.)

Next to prove the sufficiently, assume that
E\subset X
is measurable and

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{5\lambda r}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r})\leq C.

Fix a point
p\in X
. Recall that a PI-space is measure doubling and thus separable. Recall also that
\mu
is a Borel measure on a complete and separable metric space, and thus inner regular. Therefore, since
X
is doubling, by Egorov and Lebesgue differentiation Theorem, for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we can find compact subsets
E_{n}\subset E
and
F_{n}\subset B(p,n)\setminus E
with
\mu(E\setminus E_{n})\leq 2^{-n}
and
\mu(B(p,n)\setminus(E\cup F_{n}))\leq 2^{-n}
, and for which there exist
r_{n}>0
so that

\inf_{r\in(0,r_{n})}\frac{\mu(E\cap B(x,r_{n}))}{\mu(B(x,r_{n}))}>\frac{1}{2}

for all
x\in E_{n}
and

\inf_{r\in(0,2r_{n})}\frac{\mu(B(x,r_{n})\setminus E)}{\mu(B(x,2r_{n}))}>\frac{1}{2}

for all
x\in F_{n}
. It is clear that
F_{n}
and
E_{n}
are disjoint.

Next, let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be arbitrary.
Let
\delta_{n}=\min(r_{n},\inf_{a\in E_{n},b\in F_{n}}d(a,b)(4L)^{-1})>0
. Now, choose a covering
\mathcal{C}
of
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{\delta_{n}}
by open balls with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq L\delta_{n}\leq\inf_{a\in E_{n},b\in F_{n}}d(a,b)/4
and with
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)\leq 2C
.

Define
g_{n}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}1_{2c}
, and
f_{n}(x)=\min(1,\inf_{\gamma:F_{n}\mapsto x}\int_{\gamma}g\,ds)
.
Just as in Lemma
6.6
, we get
f_{n}|_{E_{n}}=1
and
f|_{F_{n}}=0
since by Lemma
6.3
for any
\gamma
which connects
E_{n}
to
F_{n}
we have
\gamma\cap\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{\delta_{n}}\neq\emptyset
and
\diam(\gamma)\geq 2{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})
.
Further,
f_{n}:X\to[0,1]
. As in the proof of Lemma
6.6
,
g_{n}
is an upper gradient of
f_{n}
, and we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}g_{n}\,d\mu\lesssim\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)<\infty.

We are left to show that for every
R>0
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{B(p,R)}|1_{E}-f_{n}|\,d\mu=0
.
For
n\geq R
we have
B(p,R)\subset E\cup(B(p,n)\setminus E)
and
1_{E}(x)=f_{n}(x)
when
x\in E_{n}\cup F_{n}\cap B(p,R)
. Thus, by the choice of
E_{n}
and
F_{n}
, we have

\int_{B(p,R)}|1_{E}-f_{n}|\,d\mu\leq\mu(E\setminus E_{n})+\mu(B(p,n)\setminus(E\cup F_{n}))\leq 2^{1-n},

and the claim follows.

∎

6.2.
Strong isoperimetric inequality

Using the previous section, we obtain the strong isoperimetric inequality.

Theorem 6.11
.

Suppose that
X
is a complete PI-space.
There is a constant
C
so that for any closed ball
{\overline{B}}
and any measurable set
E\subset{\overline{B}}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\partial^{*}E\cap 3\lambda{\overline{B}})\geq\frac{1}{C}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})h({\overline{B}}).

Indeed, if
\lambda\leq 2^{L}
, then

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\partial^{*}_{2^{-1}D^{-4-L}}E\cap 3\lambda{\overline{B}})\geq\frac{1}{C}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})h({\overline{B}}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})>0
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})\cup\{B\}
. Then, for any
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}_{0})\geq\min(D^{-2},\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}))
. By Lemma
6.6
, we have a constant so that for any
s\geq 0
we have

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\mathcal{B}|_{s}^{3\lambda{\overline{B}}_{0}})\geq C^{-1}\min(D^{-2},\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}))h({\overline{B}}_{0}).

Now, let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=3\lambda{\overline{B}}
, for which we also get, for any
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|_{s}^{3\lambda{\overline{B}}_{0}})\geq C^{-1}\min(D^{-2},\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}))h\left(\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}_{0}\right).

Thus, by Theorem
1.2
and doubling we get (after adjusting the constant
C
), that

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\limsup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{B}_{0})\geq C^{-1}\min(D^{-2},\Theta(E,{\overline{B}}))h({\overline{B}}_{0}),

for any
{\overline{B}}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
6.2
, we have
\limsup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\partial^{*}_{2^{-1}D^{-4-L}}E\subset\partial^{*}E
, where
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
is such that
\lambda\leq 2^{L}
, and the claim follows. Further,
\Theta(E,B)\leq 1
, so
D^{-2}\geq D^{-2}\Theta(E,\overline{B})
, and we get

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}}_{0})}(\limsup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{B}_{0})\geq C^{-1}D^{-2}\Theta(E,{\overline{B}})h({\overline{B}}_{0}).

∎

Remark 6.12
.

The constants in the previous theorem could be improved when
X
is geodesic. In that case,
\lambda=1
is possible in the Poincaré inequality (
2.9
), see
[
19
]
, which allows us to take
L=1
. In the geodesic setting, also the constant
2D^{-2}
in Lemma
6.3
could be improved to unity – see the argument in
[
26
, Proposition 3.9]
. With these two, we could replace
\partial^{*}E
with
\partial^{*}_{D^{-2}}E
in the statement.

6.3.
Federer Characterization

We will next prove Theorem
2.11
.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Suppose first that
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E)<\infty
.
We verify that there is a constant
C>1
for which
\mathcal{H}^{h}_{15\lambda r}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r})\leq C
for all
r>0
.
Once this has been proven, Theorem
6.10
implies that
E
is a set of finite perimeter.

Now, let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset 3\lambda\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r}
be a disjoint collection so that
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})|_{r}\subset\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.
Since
1/(3\lambda)\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
, by the strong isoperimetric inequality Theorem
6.11
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E\cap{\overline{B}})\geq\mathcal{H}^{h}_{{\rm rad}({\overline{B}})}(\partial^{*}E\cap{\overline{B}})\geq\frac{1}{2D^{2}C}h\left(\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}\right),

for every
{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Summing over
{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
together with doubling gives

\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h(5{\overline{B}})\lesssim\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h\left(\frac{1}{3\lambda}{\overline{B}}\right)\lesssim\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E\cap{\overline{B}})<\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E)<\infty,

where in the second-to-last inequality we used disjointness of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Since
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})\subset\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, the desired content bound follows.

For the other direction of the theorem, assume that
E
is a set of finite perimeter. If
E
is of finite perimeter, then
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{*}E)<\infty
follows from
[
5
, Theorem 5.5.]
. Indeed, this is the only instance in which we need to refer to a result about finite perimeter sets, which we do not prove. The proof of this uses quite different techniques than our arguments here.
∎

6.4.
Generic curves and sets of finite perimeter

In this subsection, we discuss curves going from the measure theoretic interior
I_{E}
to the measure theoretic exterior
O_{E}
. By Lemma
6.3
, each curve
\gamma\in\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}
will intersect
\bigcup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
. However, this need not imply that
\gamma
must intersect
\limsup\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
. However, when
E
is a set of finite perimeter, we show that for
{\rm Mod}_{1}
-a.e. curve this is the case. First, we need a simple lemma relating the
h
-content to modulus.

For a set
E\subset X
and
\delta>0
, denote by
\Gamma_{E,\delta}
the collection of non-constant rectifiable curves which intersect
E
and have
\diam(\gamma)\geq\delta
, where
\diam(\gamma)=\sup_{t,s\in[0,1]}d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))
. It will also be convenient to identify the curve
\gamma
with its image
\{\gamma(t):t\in[0,1]\}
. With this convention, we can write
\gamma\cap A\neq\emptyset
, when
A\subset X
, to mean that there is a
t\in[0,1]
so that
\gamma(t)\in A
.

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
X
be a PI-space, and
E\subset X
. Then,
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma_{E,\delta})\leq D\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta/3}(E)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta/3}(E)<\infty
. Then
for any
\epsilon>0
we can find a collection
\mathcal{C}
of open balls with
{\rm rad}(\mathcal{C})\leq\delta/3
and which covers
E
and with
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)\leq\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta/3}(E)+\epsilon/D
. Let
g=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}1_{2c}
. By the same calculation as in Lemma
6.6
, we get

\int g\,d\mu\leq D\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}h(c)\leq D\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta/3}(E)+\epsilon.

Next, we will show that
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds\geq 1
for any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{E,\delta}
. From which
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma_{E,\delta})\leq D\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta/2}(E)+\epsilon
follows. The claim follows from this since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{E,\delta}
be arbitrary. Since
\gamma
intersects
E
, and since
\mathcal{C}
is a cover of
E
, there exists a
c\in\mathcal{C}
so that
\gamma
intersects
c
. Since
\diam(\gamma)\geq 2{\rm rad}(c)
, we have that
\gamma
is not contained in
2c
. Thus, it contains a sub-segment of length at least
{\rm rad}(c)
in the annulus
2c\setminus c
. Thus,
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds\geq{\rm rad}(c)\frac{1}{{\rm rad}(c)}\geq 1
, as claimed.
∎

With this lemma, we can prove the final statement of the paper.

Proof of Theorem
2.14
.

Let
\Gamma_{b}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}},\gamma\cap\partial^{*}E=\emptyset\}
be the collection of curves in
\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}
which do not pass through the measure theoretic boundary. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
define the collection
\Gamma_{n}=\{\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma_{b},\diam(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{n}\}.
We have
\Gamma_{b}=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n}
. Since
{\rm Mod}_{1}
is subadditive, the claim that
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma_{b})=0
follows once we show that
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma_{n})=0
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
in what follows and let
\delta=\frac{1}{4n}
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=3\lambda\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
.
Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
be any disjoint collection. By Lemma
6.9
we have a value
C
(which does not depend on
\delta
) so that
\sum_{{\overline{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}h({\overline{B}})\leq C<\infty
. Thus
\mathcal{B}
verifies the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
.

Next, fix
\epsilon>0.
Let
r_{1}=\delta/(10C)
, where
C\geq 1
is given by Lemma
3.3
. By Lemma
3.3
we can choose a finite collections
\mathcal{B}_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}|_{\delta}
so that

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{1}}\setminus 5\mathcal{B}_{1})\leq 2^{-1}D^{-1}\epsilon.

Proceed recursively to define a decreasing sequence
r_{k}
and collections
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k}}
. Suppose that
r_{k}
is defined. Then, define
r_{k+1}\leq\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}{\rm rad}(B)/2
. Again, using Lemma
3.3
choose a finite collection
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}\subset\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k+1}}
so that

\mathcal{H}^{h}_{\delta}(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k+1}}\setminus 5\mathcal{B}_{k+1})\leq D^{-1}2^{-k-1}\epsilon.

Let
\Gamma_{n,k}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}:\gamma\cap(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k}})\setminus 5\mathcal{B}_{k}\neq\emptyset\}
. By Lemma
6.13
, we have
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\Gamma_{n,k})\leq\epsilon 2^{-k}
. By subadditivity we get
{\rm Mod}_{1}(\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n,k})\leq\epsilon
. The claim then follows from arbitrariness of
\epsilon>0
once we show that
\Gamma_{n}\subset\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n,k}
.

In other words, we prove that any curve
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
must intersect one of the sets
(\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k}})\setminus 5\mathcal{B}_{k}
, for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This follows by showing that for any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}\setminus\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n,k}
with
\diam(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{n}
we have
\gamma\cap\partial^{*}E\neq\emptyset
, that is
\gamma\not\in\Gamma_{n}
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}\setminus\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n,k}
with
\diam(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{n}
be arbitrary. Define sets by
\mathcal{K}_{k}=\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}_{k}\cap\gamma
. The set
\mathcal{K}_{k}
, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is compact since
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is a finite collections of closed balls and the image of
\gamma
is compact. Suppose that
\cap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{K}_{k}\neq\emptyset
. If this is the case, there exists a
t\in[0,1]
for which
\gamma(t)\in\cap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{K}_{k}
.
Therefore, there is a sequence of balls
({\overline{B}}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
{\overline{B}}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
\gamma(t)\in 5\mathcal{B}_{i}
.
From this we get
\gamma(t)\in\limsup 15\lambda\mathcal{B}(E,2^{-1}D^{-2})
, and
\gamma(t)\in\partial^{*}E
by Lemma
6.3
. In particular,
\gamma\cap\partial^{*}E\neq\emptyset
and
\gamma\not\in\Gamma_{n}
as desired.

What remains is to derive a contradiction from the assumption
\cap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{K}_{k}=\emptyset
. Suppose this is the case. Then, by the finite intersection property, there exists an integer
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that
\cap_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}_{k}=\emptyset
. By Lemma
6.3
, since
\gamma\in\Gamma_{I_{E},O_{E}}
, we have that
\gamma\cap\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{N}}\neq\emptyset
.
Then, there exists a
t\in[0,1]
with
\gamma(t)\in\bigcup\mathcal{B}|_{r_{N}}
. Since
\gamma(t)\not\in\cap_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{K}_{k}
, there must exist a
k\in[1,N]\cap{\mathbb{N}}
with
\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{B}|_{r_{N}}\setminus\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}|_{r_{k}}\setminus\bigcup 5\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Then
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n,k}
, by definition. Thus,
\Gamma_{n}\subset\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Gamma_{n,k}
.

∎
