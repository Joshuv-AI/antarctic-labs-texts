Schrödinger operator with a complex steplike potential
Tho Nguyen Duc

Abstract
. The purpose of this article is to study pseudospectral properties of the one-dimensional Schrödinger operator perturbed by a complex steplike potential. By constructing the resolvent kernel, we show that the pseudospectrum of this operator is trivial if and only if the imaginary part of the potential is constant. As a by-product, a new method to obtain a sharp resolvent estimate is developed, answering a concern of Henry and Krejčiřík, and a way to construct an optimal pseudomode is discovered, answering a concern of Krejčiřík and Siegl. The spectrum and the norm of the resolvent of the complex point interaction of the operator is also studied carefully in this article.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context and Motivations

Since the birth of quantum physics at the turn of the 20th century, spectral theory of self-adjoint operator has attracted considerable attention and experienced many fertile developments. But its best friend
non-self-adjoint
operator has only recently been noticed and investigated restricted to the last two decades.
One of the challenges that we often face when we work with the non-self-adjoint operator is that there is no spectral theorem
[
18
]
. A clear evidence for this is the absence of the well-known formula for the norm of the resolvent

(1.1)

\|(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{\textup{dist}(z,\sigma(\mathscr{L}))},

which is valid for self-adjoint
\mathscr{L}
(or generally, for unbounded normal operator, the reader can found a simple proof in subsection
4.2
). The lack of this formula is supported by the fact that there exist many non-self-adjoint operators whose resolvents blow up when the spectral parameter travels far away from their spectrum. Therefore, the notion of
pseudospectrum
was suggested to address this pathological aspect of the non-self-adjoint operator
[
24
,
9
]
. More precisely, given
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-
pseudospectrum
of a linear operator
\mathscr{L}
is defined by

(1.2)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\mathscr{L})=\sigma(\mathscr{L})\cup\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\|(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}\|>\varepsilon^{-1}\}.

The usefulness of pseudospectrum is that it can give the answer to the question
“How does the spectrum respond to a slight change of the initial operator?”
by virtue of the formula

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\mathscr{L})=\bigcup_{V\in L(H),\,\|V\|<\varepsilon}\sigma(\mathscr{L}+V).

Especially, when the pseudospectrum contains regions very far from the spectrum, it causes the instability of the spectrum under a small perturbation and reveals that it would be difficult to obtain the spectrum numerically. The reader may want to see a discussion about this topic in the introduction of
[
22
]
.

According to the definition (
1.2
), the more information we have on the level set of the resolvent, the better described the pseudospectrum is. However, in almost all cases, it is not easy to calculate the resolvent of a given operator, and even when the resolvent is known, it is not easy to calculate its norm. Therefore, in practice, we often use an equivalent definition of the pseudospectrum, that is

(1.3)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\mathscr{L})=\sigma(\mathscr{L})\cup\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\exists\Psi\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}),\,\|(\mathscr{L}-z)\Psi\|<\varepsilon\|\Psi\|\right\}.

The number
z
and the vector
\Psi
in the definition (
1.3
) are respectively called the
pseudoeigenvalue
and
pseudomode
(also known as the
pseudoeigenfunction
,
pseudoeigenvector
, or
quasimode
) of
\mathscr{L}
. We list here some references
[
15
,
1
,
2
]
using the definition (
1.2
) and some references
[

,
3

using the definition (

) to investigate pseudospectrum of a differential operator.

2.3.
The optimal pseudomode

One of the early studies on the pseudomode construction for the non-self-adjoint Hamiltonian can be attributed to Davies’ work
[
8
]
. In his worked, Davies scaled variable so that the original Schödinger operator can be transformed to its semiclassical version and thanks to this fact, the WKB pseudomode is constructed. However, this method seems merely effective for a certain class of polynomial potentials in which the scaling can be performed, while it is inapplicable for other type of potentials such as logarithmic or exponential potentials. Twenty years later, Krejčiřík and Siegl in
[
19
]
developed a
direct construction of large-energy pseudomode
for the Schrödinger operator, which does not require passage through semiclassical setting and can cover all of the above-mentioned potentials. Furthermore, they also created two methods, the first is called
perturbative approach
and the second is called
mollification strategy
, to deal with lower regularity potentials (discontinuous potentials are also included). However, when these methods are applied for the potential
V(x)=i\,\textup{sgn}(x)
, the best possible decaying rate of the quotient
\frac{\|(\mathscr{L}-z)\Psi_{z}\|}{\|\Psi_{z}\|}
that may be attained is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{1/4}}\right)
for the perturbative approach, see
[
19
, Example 4.5]
, and
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{1/2}}\right)
for the mollification strategy, see
[
19
, Example 4.11]
, as
z\to+\infty
on the real axis. Our next result will present an explicit pseudomode
\Psi_{z}
that yields the precise decaying rate which should be
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)
. Better yet, an optimal outcome, which is verified from Theorem
2.7
, is also attained.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
V_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
and
\mathscr{L}
be the operator defined as in Proposition
2.1
, and
k_{\pm}(z)
as in (
2.5
). For each
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L})
, we define a function
\Psi_{z}
as follows

(2.11)

\Psi_{z}(x)=\left(n_{1}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)x}+n_{2}(z)e^{\overline{k_{-}(z)}x}\right)\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(x)+\left(p_{1}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}+p_{2}(z)e^{-\overline{k_{+}(z)}x}\right)\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x),

where
n_{1}(z),p_{1}(z)
and
n_{2}(z),p_{2}(z)
are complex numbers depending on
z
given by

(2.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &n_{1}(z)=\frac{k_{+}(z)+\overline{k_{-}(z)}}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|+\frac{k_{+}(z)-\overline{k_{+}(z)}}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&p_{1}(z)=-\frac{k_{-}(z)-\overline{k_{-}(z)}}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|-\frac{\overline{k_{+}(z)}+k_{-}(z)}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&n_{2}(z)=-|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&p_{2}(z)=|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|.\end{aligned}\right.

Then,

for all

and it makes

\frac{\|(\mathscr{L}-z)\Psi_{z}\|}{\|\Psi_{z}\|}=\frac{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}{2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|}\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|}{\mathrm{Re}\,z}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Re}\,z|}\right)\right),

as

(1)

\displaystyle\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\coloneqq\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}):u^{\prime}(0^{+})-u^{\prime}(0^{-})=\alpha u(0)\},

as
\mathrm{Re}\,z\to+\infty
and uniformly for all
\mathrm{Im}\,z\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
.

It is clear that the closer to zero
\alpha
is, the faster the resolvent norm increases. Although the blowing-up rate of the resolvent norm in the case
\alpha\neq 0
is less than in the case free of interaction, the pseudospectrum is still non-trivial when
\mathrm{Im}\,V(x)
is not constant. We do not know about the asymptotic behavior of the resolvent norm of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
outside the region bounded by two essential spectrum lines, but we conjecture that it will be bounded when the spectral parameter
z
moves parallel to this region and decay when
z
moves far away from this region to infinity.

Our final result is devoted to the pseudomode construction for the complex point interaction operator
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
. Again, our method for Theorem
2.9
is still applicable and yields an optimal pseudomode for the operator
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
V_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
be the operator defined as in Proposition
2.10
. We define a function
\Psi_{z,\alpha}
, for
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
, as follows

(2.19)

\Psi_{z,\alpha}(x)=\left(n_{1}(z,\alpha)e^{k_{-}(z)x}+n_{2}(z)e^{\overline{k_{-}(z)}x}\right)\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(x)+\left(p_{1}(z,\alpha)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}+p_{2}(z)e^{-\overline{k_{+}(z)}x}\right)\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x),

where
n_{1}(z,\alpha),p_{1}(z,\alpha)
and
n_{2}(z),p_{2}(z)
are given by

(2.20)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &n_{1}(z,\alpha)=\frac{k_{+}(z)+\overline{k_{-}(z)}+\alpha}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)+\alpha}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|+\frac{k_{+}(z)-\overline{k_{+}(z)}}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)+\alpha}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&p_{1}(z,\alpha)=-\frac{k_{-}(z)-\overline{k_{-}(z)}}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)+\alpha}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|-\frac{\overline{k_{+}(z)}+k_{-}(z)+\alpha}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)+\alpha}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&n_{2}(z)=-|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|,\\
&p_{2}(z)=|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|.\end{aligned}\right.

Then,
\Psi_{z,\alpha}\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
for all
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
and it makes

\frac{\|(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}-z)\Psi_{z,\alpha}\|}{\|\Psi_{z,\alpha}\|}=\frac{|\alpha|}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|}\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}\,z}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Re}\,z|^{1/2}}\right)\right),

as
\mathrm{Re}\,z\to+\infty
and uniformly for all
\mathrm{Im}\,z\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
.

3.
Calculation resolvent and spectrum

The main goal of this section is to study the resolvent and spectrum of the operator
\mathscr{L}
.

3.1.
Integral form of the resolvent: Proof of Proposition
2.2

We start by solving the resolvent equation. Let us fix
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty))
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we look for solution
u
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(\mathscr{L}-z)u=f.

Because of the discontinuity of the potential
V
at
0
, we look for the solution of the above equation in the form

\displaystyle u(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{+}(x)\qquad&&\text{for }x>0,\\
&u_{-}(x)\qquad&&\text{for }x<0.\end{aligned}\right.

It means that we need to find functions
u_{\pm}
satisfying the corresponding equations

(3.1)

-u_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}(x)+(V_{\pm}-z)u_{\pm}(x)=f(x).

The variation of parameters method (VPM) is employed to find
u_{\pm}
, that is, we firstly find independent solutions associated with the homogeneous case (
i.e.
, when
f=0
), they are

e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\text{ and }e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}.

Then, the general solutions of the non-homogeneous equations can be found in the form

(3.2)

u_{\pm}(x)=\alpha_{\pm}(x)e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}+\beta_{\pm}(x)e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x},

where
\alpha_{\pm}:\mathbb{R}_{\pm}\to\mathbb{C}
and
\beta_{\pm}:\mathbb{R}_{\pm}\to\mathbb{C}
are functions to be yet determined. By taking the first derivative of
u_{\pm}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)=\left[\alpha_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}+\beta_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right]+\left[\alpha_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}+\beta_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}\right].

The VPM starts by assuming that

(3.3)

\alpha_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}+\beta_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}=0,

an thus

u_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)=\alpha_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}+\beta_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}.

From this, the second derivative of
u_{\pm}
is obtained, that is

\displaystyle u_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\left[\alpha_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)\left(e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}+\beta_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)\left(e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}\right]+\left[\alpha_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime\prime}+\beta_{\pm}(x)\left(e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime\prime}\right].

Replacing this into non-homogeneous equations (
3.1
) and remember that
e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}
and
e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}
are solution of the homogeneous ones, it leads to

(3.4)

\alpha_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)\left(e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}+\beta_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)\left(e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}\right)^{\prime}=-f(x),\qquad\pm x>0.

Solving (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we obtain

\alpha_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{2k_{\pm}(z)}e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x}f(x),\qquad\beta_{\pm}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2k_{\pm}(z)}e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}f(x),\qquad\pm x>0

Hence, we can choose

(3.5)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2k_{\pm}(z)}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-k_{\pm}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+A_{\pm},\qquad

\displaystyle\pm x>0,

\displaystyle\beta_{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k_{\pm}(z)}\int_{0}^{x}e^{k_{\pm}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+B_{\pm},\qquad

\displaystyle\pm x>0,

where
A_{\pm}
,
B_{\pm}
are some complex constants which are determined later by the fact that
u
and its derivative
u^{\prime}
need to be continuous at zero and
u
need to decay at infinities. We start with the decaying of
u
at
+\infty
. From the density of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
f_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|f-f_{\varepsilon}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Then, by the triangle and Holder inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{x}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(y-x)}|f(y)-f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{x}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(y-x)}|f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1-e^{-2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}x}}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}\|f-f_{\varepsilon}\|+\int_{0}^{x}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(y-x)}|f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y.

Since
z\notin[V_{+},+\infty)
, thus
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(z)>0
and we apply the dominated convergence theorem for the integral
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(y-x)}|f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y
, it yields that

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\left|\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}.

From the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, it leads to

(3.6)

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}=0.

In other words, we have shown that
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\beta_{+}(x)e^{-k_{+}(z)x}=0
. Therefore, from the formula of
u_{+}
in (
3.2
), we have the following equivalences

(3.7)

\lim_{x\to+\infty}u_{+}(x)=0\Longleftrightarrow\lim_{x\to+\infty}\alpha_{+}(x)e^{k_{+}(z)x}=0\Longleftrightarrow A_{+}=\frac{1}{2k_{+}(z)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Indeed, the second equivalence (whose left-to-right implication is easy to see) comes from the density of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, Holder inequality and the dominated convergence as above:

\displaystyle\left|\left(\int_{x}^{+\infty}e^{-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{k_{+}(z)x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x}^{+\infty}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(x-y)}|f(y)-f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{x}^{+\infty}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(x-y)}|f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(x-y)}\chi_{[x,+\infty)}(y)|f_{\varepsilon}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{x\to+\infty}\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}.

Similarly, we also have

(3.8)

\lim_{x\to-\infty}u_{-}(x)=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad B_{-}=\frac{1}{2k_{-}(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{k_{-}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Since
u
is found to satisfy regularity conditions

u_{+}(0)=u_{-}(0),\qquad u_{+}^{\prime}(0)=u_{-}^{\prime}(0),

we obtain the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &A_{+}+B_{+}=A_{-}+B_{-},\\
&k_{+}(z)A_{+}-k_{+}(z)B_{+}=k_{-}(z)A_{-}-k_{-}(z)B_{-},\end{aligned}\right.

which allows us to determine
A_{-}
and
B_{+}
in terms of
A_{+}
and
B_{-}
:

(3.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} A_{-}&=\frac{2k_{+}(z)}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}A_{+}-\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}B_{-},\\
B_{+}&=\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}A_{+}+\frac{2k_{-}(z)}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}B_{-}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\notin[V_{\pm},+\infty)
because
\mathrm{Re}\,\left(k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)\right)>0
(from the choice of the principle branch of the square root). By replacing the values of constants
A_{\pm}
,
B_{\pm}
in (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and in (
3.9
) into the formula of
u_{\pm}
in (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle u_{+}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k_{+}(z)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-k_{+}(z)|x-y|}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{2k_{+}(z)(k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z))}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-k_{+}(z)(x+y)}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-k_{+}(z)x+k_{-}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x>0,

\displaystyle u_{-}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k_{-}(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-k_{-}(z)|x-y|}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{2k_{-}(z)(k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z))}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{k_{-}(z)(x+y)}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{k_{-}(z)x-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x<0.

Thus, given
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we constructed a solution
u
of the differential equation
(\mathscr{L}-z)u=f
that has the integral form

u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)
is given in the statement of Proposition
2.2
. After having a solution
u
for the resolvent equation, we need to show that
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and this can be done by using the Schur test, cf.
[
14
, Lem. 7.1]
. We will check that

(3.10)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y<+\infty\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

After noticing that
\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)
is symmetric,
i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)=\mathcal{R}_{z}(y,x)
for almost everywhere
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we just need to check the first one in (
3.10
). Directly from the formula of the kernel
\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)
, we have, for all
x>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)x+\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)y}\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{2|k_{+}(z)|}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\frac{|k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)|}{2|k_{+}(z)||k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)(x+y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)|k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)|k_{+}(z)|}+\frac{|k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)|}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)|k_{+}(z)||k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}.

In the same way, for
x<0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)|k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)|k_{-}(z)|}+\frac{|k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)|}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)|k_{+}(z)||k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|}.

Both the right hand sides of the above bounds for the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y
are finite provided
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\right)
. Thus,
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and then
u
will automatically belong to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
since
u^{\prime\prime}=(V-z)u-f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.2.
Characterization of the spectrum: Proof of Theorem
2.4

Let us consider
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|\xi\|_{L^{2}}=1
and its support lives in
(-1,1)
. We set

\xi_{n}^{\pm}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi\left(\frac{x}{n}\mp n\right).

For
n\geq 1
, it is not hard to see that
\textup{Supp }\xi_{n}^{+}\subset(n^{2}-n,n^{2}+n)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\textup{Supp }\xi_{n}^{-}\subset(-n^{2}-n,-n^{2}+n)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Let us consider fixed but arbitrary
z_{\pm}\in[V_{\pm},+\infty)
and we define sequence
u_{n}
as follows

u_{n}^{\pm}(x)\coloneqq\xi_{n}^{\pm}(x)e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}.

Notice that
k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})=\sqrt{z_{\pm}-V_{\pm}}\in i\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
, it leads to the fact that
u_{n}^{\pm}
is normalized:

\|u_{n}^{\pm}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{n}\left|\xi\left(\frac{x}{n}\mp n\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\|\xi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

Since
e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}
satisfies
(\mathscr{L}-z_{\pm})e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}=0
on
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
, by taking the support of
\xi_{n}^{\pm}
into account, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}-z_{\pm})u_{n}^{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=\xi_{n}^{\pm}(x)(\mathscr{L}-\lambda_{\pm})e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}+\left[\mathscr{L}-\lambda,\xi_{n}^{\pm}(x)\right]e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}},\xi_{n}^{\pm}(x)\right]e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\xi_{n}^{\pm}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}(x)-2k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_{n}^{\pm}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)\right)e^{k_{\pm}(z_{\pm})x}.

Here
[A,B]\coloneqq AB-BA
is the notation of the commutator. Notice that, for each
k\in\{1,2\}
, we have

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}\xi_{n}^{\pm}}{\mathrm{d}x^{k}}(x)\right\|^{2}=\int_{\pm n^{2}-n}^{\pm n^{2}+n}\frac{1}{n^{2k+1}}\left|\xi^{(k)}\left(\frac{x}{n}-n\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{n^{2k}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\xi^{(k)}(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\xrightarrow{n\to+\infty}0.

It yields that
\|(\mathscr{L}-z_{\pm})u_{n}^{\pm}(x)\|\xrightarrow{n\to+\infty}0
. In other words,
\left(u_{n}^{+}\right)_{n\geq 1}
forms a Weyl sequence for
z^{+}
and
\left(u_{n}^{-}\right)_{n\geq 1}
forms a Weyl sequence for
z^{-}
. Using for instance
[
6
, Lem. 3.3]
, we have
z_{\pm}\in\sigma(\mathscr{L})
and from the arbitrariness of
z_{\pm}
in
[V_{\pm},+\infty)
we conclude that

[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\subset\sigma(\mathscr{L}).

From Proposition
2.2
, we deduce that

\sigma(\mathscr{L})\subset[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty).

Then, the conclusion on the spectrum of
\mathscr{L}
is followed. Since
\mathscr{L}
is
J
-self-adjoint, its residual spectrum is empty (see
[
18
, Section 5.2.5.4]
). Now, we will check that no points in
[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)
can be the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}
, and thus
\sigma_{p}(\mathscr{L})=\emptyset
. Take
z\in[V_{+},+\infty)
, assume that
z
is the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}
and
u
is its associated eigenfunction,
i.e.
,
(\mathscr{L}-z)u=0
. From (
3.2
), the restriction of
u
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, denoted by, respectively,
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
, has the expression

(3.11)

u_{\pm}(x)=A_{\pm}e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}+B_{\pm}e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x},\qquad\text{for }\pm x>0.

Since
z\in[V_{+},+\infty)
, then
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)=0
and thus,

|u_{+}(x)|^{2}=|A_{+}|^{2}+|B_{+}|^{2}+2\mathrm{Re}\,\left(A_{+}\overline{B}_{+}e^{i\,\mathrm{Im}\,k_{+}(z)x}\right)\geq\left(|A_{+}|-|B_{+}|\right)^{2}.

The fact that
u_{+}
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and that
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is unbounded, it implies that
|A_{+}|=|B_{+}|
. By writing
A_{+}=|A_{+}|e^{i\textup{Arg }A_{+}},B_{+}=|B_{+}|e^{i\textup{Arg }B_{+}}
, here
\textup{Arg }(w)
denotes the principal argument of a complex number
w
, we obtain

\displaystyle|u_{+}(x)|^{2}=

\displaystyle 2|A_{+}|^{2}\left(1+\cos\left(\textup{Arg }A_{+}-\textup{Arg }B_{+}+\mathrm{Im}\,k_{+}(z)x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4|A_{+}|^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\textup{Arg }A_{+}-\textup{Arg }B_{+}+\mathrm{Im}\,k_{+}(z)x}{2}\right).

Since
u_{+}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, it yields that
A_{+}=0
(since the trigonometry is not integrable on unbounded domain as
\mathbb{R}_{+}
) and thus
u_{+}=0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Similarly,
u_{-}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})
also implies that
u_{-}=0
on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
and thus, we have a contradiction. Therefore
[V_{+},+\infty)
is not a subset of the point spectrum
\sigma_{p}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
. This argument can be made for
[V_{-},+\infty)
in the same manner. Therefore, the spectrum of
\mathscr{L}
is purely continuous.

In order to obtain the statement on essential spectra of
\mathscr{L}
, we will show that the above sequence
u_{n}
is singular,
i.e.
,
u_{n}
converges weakly to zero. Indeed, take
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, by the density of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sequence
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|f-f_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Then, by using triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities with
\|u_{n}^{\pm}\|=1
and combining with the definition of
\xi_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\langle u_{n}^{\pm},f\rangle|\leq|\langle u_{n}^{\pm},f-f_{k}\rangle|+|\langle u_{n}^{\pm},f_{k}\rangle|\leq\|f-f_{k}\|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{1}}

Let
n\to+\infty
and consider arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, it implies that
\langle u_{n},f\rangle\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0
. Since
f
is taken arbitrarily in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have just proved the weakly convergence to zero of
u_{n}
. Thanks to
[
11
, Theo. IX.1.3]
, we implies that
\sigma_{\textup{e2}}(\mathscr{L})=\sigma(\mathscr{L})
. Since
\mathscr{L}
is
J
-self-adjoint, four first essential spectra
\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{L})
(
k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
) are identical (see
[
11
, Theo. IX.1.6]
). Since
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma_{\textup{e1}}(\mathscr{L})=\rho(\mathscr{L})
is connected, the fifth essential spectrum is also the same (cf.
[
18
, Prop. 5.4.4]
).

4.
Pseudospectral estimates

From now on, unless otherwise stated, for simplicity, we will denote
z=\tau+i\delta
with
(\tau,\delta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
and each time we write some asymptotic formula with big
\mathcal{O}
notation, we understand that this formula happen as
\tau\to+\infty
and uniformly for all
\delta\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
.

4.1.
Resolvent estimate inside the numerical range: Proof of Theorem
2.7

We rewrite
(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}
as the sum of two integral operators

(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}=\mathcal{R}_{1,z}+\mathcal{R}_{2,z},

where

(4.1)

\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{R}_{1,z}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{2,z}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{R}_{2,z}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

whose kernel are given by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1,z}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &K_{++}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)(x+y)},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x>0,y>0\};\\
&K_{--}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)(x+y)},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x<0,y<0\};\\
&K_{+-}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)x+k_{-}(z)y},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x>0,y<0\};\\
&K_{+-}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)x-k_{+}(z)y},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x<0,y>0\};\end{aligned}\right.

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2,z}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2k_{+}(z)}e^{-k_{+}(z)|x-y|},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x>0,y>0\};\\
&\frac{1}{2k_{-}(z)}e^{-k_{-}(z)|x-y|},\qquad&&\text{for }\{x<0,y<0\};\\
&0,\qquad&&\text{for }\{x.y<0\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here, we denote

(4.4)

\displaystyle K_{++}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{2k_{+}(z)\left(k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)\right)},\qquad K_{--}(z)\coloneqq-\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{2k_{-}(z)\left(k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)\right)},

\displaystyle K_{+-}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)}.

Our strategy is to show that the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
will play the main role, while the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{2,z}
is just a small perturbation compared with
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
in the divergence of the resolvent norm inside the numerical range. To do that, two-sided estimate of the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
will be clearly established with the help of the following optimization lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
, consider a function of two variables

f(x,y)=Ax^{2}+Bxy+Cy^{2}

on the circle
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
. Then it attains the maximum on this circle and

\displaystyle\max_{x^{2}+y^{2}=1}f(x,y)=\frac{A+C+\sqrt{(A-C)^{2}+B^{2}}}{2}.

Proof.

Let us write
x=\cos(\theta)
and
y=\sin(\theta)
for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and write

\displaystyle f(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))=

\displaystyle A\cos^{2}(\theta)+B\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)+C\sin^{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(A+C+(A-C)\cos(2\theta)+B\sin(2\theta)\right)

By using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain the upper bound, for every
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
,

f(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(A+C+\sqrt{(A-C)^{2}+B^{2}}\right),

and the equality can be obtained when

\left(\cos(2\theta),\sin(2\theta)\right)=\pm\left(\frac{A-C}{\sqrt{(A-C)^{2}+B^{2}}},\frac{B}{\sqrt{(A-C)^{2}+B^{2}}}\right).

Then, the conclusion of the lemma follows.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
be the integral operator with the kernel
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}(x,y)
defined as in (
4.2
). For all
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L})
,
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
is a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
whose norm satisfies

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sqrt{(A(z)-C(z))^{2}+B(z)^{2}}+A(z)+C(z)+2D(z)},

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sqrt{(A(z)-C(z))^{2}+\widetilde{B}(z)^{2}}+A(z)+C(z)+2D(z)},

where

\displaystyle A(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{|K_{++}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))^{2}},\qquad C(z)\coloneqq\frac{|K_{--}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))^{2}},\qquad D(z)\coloneqq\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))},

\displaystyle B(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|}{2\sqrt{(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}}\left(\frac{|K_{++}(z)|}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{|K_{--}(z)|}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{++}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{--}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right).

Proof.

Consider
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|f\|=1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}K_{+-}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)x+k_{-}(z)y}f(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{++}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)(x+y)}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}K_{--}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)(x+y)}f(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{+-}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)x-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}K_{+-}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)y}f(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{++}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}K_{--}(z)e^{k_{-}(z)y}f(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{+-}(z)e^{-k_{+}(z)y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}.

Using Holder’s inequality and remember that
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}\left(\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}+\frac{|K_{++}(z)|}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\left(\frac{|K_{--}(z)|}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}+\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|K_{++}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}+\frac{|K_{--}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}^{2}+\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}

\displaystyle+\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|}{2\sqrt{(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}}\left(\frac{|K_{--}(z)|}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}+\frac{|K_{++}(z)|}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}\right)\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

By applying Lemma
4.1
, it yields that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(A(z)+C(z)+\sqrt{(A(z)-C(z))^{2}+B(z)^{2}}+2D(z)\right),

where
A(z),B(z),C(z),D(z)
defined as in the statement of this proposition. For all
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L})
,
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{\pm}(z)>0
, then all coefficients
A(z),B(z),C(z),D(z)
are finite, for that reason,
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
is bounded and the upper bound in (
4.5
) is obtained. In order to get the lower bound, we introduce a test function

(4.6)

f_{0}(y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha e^{-\overline{k_{+}(z)}y}&&\text{for }y\geq 0,\\
&\beta e^{\overline{k_{-}(z)}y}&&\text{for }y\leq 0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
will be determined later.

By straightforward computation, the action of the operator
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}\right](x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &e^{-k_{+}(z)x}\left(\frac{\alpha K_{++}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\beta K_{+-}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right)&&\text{ for }x>0,\\
&e^{k_{-}(z)x}\left(\frac{\alpha K_{+-}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\beta K_{--}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right)&&\text{ for }x<0.\end{aligned}\right.

It yields that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}\left|\frac{\alpha K_{++}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\beta K_{+-}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\left|\frac{\alpha K_{+-}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\beta K_{--}(z)}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right|^{2}.

By the definition of
f_{0}
, we have

\|f_{0}\|^{2}=\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{|\beta|^{2}}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}.

To normalize the norm of
f_{0}
, let us write

\alpha=\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}a,\qquad\beta=\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}b,\text{ with some }a,b\in\mathbb{R}:a^{2}+b^{2}=1.

Then, our problem turns to find
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}
such that the quantity

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}\left|\frac{aK_{++}(z)}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}+\frac{bK_{+-}(z)}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\left|\frac{aK_{+-}(z)}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}}+\frac{bK_{--}(z)}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}|K_{++}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))^{2}}+\frac{b^{2}|K_{--}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))^{2}}+\frac{|K_{+-}(z)|^{2}}{4(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z))(\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z))}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{++}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{--}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right)ab

attains its maximum. Lemma
4.1
tells us that there exists such a couple
(a,b)
and its maximum is

\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(A(z)+C(z)+\sqrt{(A(z)-C(z))^{2}+\widetilde{B}(z)^{2}}+2D(z)\right).

With this choice of test function, the quantity
\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}f_{0}\|
becomes the lower bound for the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
.
∎

After having explicit formulas for two-sided bounds of the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
in the above proposition, we will send
z
to infinity inside the numerical range of
\mathscr{L}
to see the asymptotic behavior of these two-sided bounds. The following lemma will be employed to provide us some useful asymptotic formulas from which we deduce the asymptotic behavior of these two-sides bounds.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
z=\tau+i\delta
with
\tau>0
,
\delta\in K\setminus\{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\}
with
K
is some compact set in
\mathbb{R}
. Then, the following asymptotic formulas hold as
\tau\to+\infty
and uniformly for all
\delta\in K\setminus\{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\}
:

\displaystyle k_{+}(z)=

\displaystyle\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)\left[i\sqrt{\tau}+\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)-i\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}}{2\sqrt{\tau}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|^{3/2}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle k_{-}(z)=

\displaystyle\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta)\left[i\sqrt{\tau}+\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta)-i\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}}{2\sqrt{\tau}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|^{3/2}}\right)\right],

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)=\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)=\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let us give a proof for
k_{+}(z)
and
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)
, the asymptotics for
k_{-}(z)
and
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)
is obtained by just changing from the plus sign to the minus sign. We start with
k_{+}(z)
. Recalling that the square root that we fixed in this article is in principal branch, thus, for
\tau>0
, we have

\displaystyle k_{+}(z)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}-\tau+i(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\sqrt{\tau}\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)\sqrt{\frac{-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}-i(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)}{\tau}+1}.

We consider a smooth complex-valued function
F_{\delta}
defined on
\mathbb{R}
by

F_{\delta}(x)=\sqrt{ax+1},\qquad a=-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}-i(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta).

By applying Taylor’s theorem (for example,
[
16
, Theo. 1.36]
) for
F_{\delta}
expanding at zero, we have, for all
x
in some neighbourhood of zero,

F_{\delta}(x)=1+\frac{a}{2}x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)F_{\delta}^{(2)}(xs)\,\mathrm{d}s.

The second derivative of
F_{\delta}
can be calculated explicitly as follows

F_{\delta}^{(2)}(x)=-\frac{a^{2}}{4\sqrt{(ax+1)^{3}}}.

By considering
|x|
sufficiently small and using the fact that
|ax+1|\gtrsim 1
uniformly for all
\delta\in K
, we get that
|F_{\delta}^{(2)}(x)|\lesssim 1
uniformly for all
\delta\in K
and thus, as
|x|\to 0
,

F_{\delta}(x)=1+\frac{a}{2}x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(|x|^{2})\qquad\text{uniformly for all }\delta\in K.

Replacing
x=\frac{1}{\tau}
, we obtain the asymptotic for
k_{+}(z)
as in statement of the lemma. Next, to obtain the expansion for
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)
, we use the algebraic formula for the square root of a non-real complex number
w
:

\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{w}=\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,w|}{\sqrt{2(|w|-\mathrm{Re}\,w)}}.

For
\delta\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}
, we have

(4.7)

\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)=\frac{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{|k_{+}(z)|^{2}+\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}}}.

Since
|k_{+}(z)|^{2}=\sqrt{(\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+})^{2}+(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)^{2}}
, we can show that

\displaystyle\sqrt{|k_{+}(z)|^{2}+\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}}-\sqrt{2\tau}=\frac{-4(\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+})\tau+(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{|k_{+}(z)|^{2}+\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}}+\sqrt{2\tau}\right)\left(|k_{+}(z)|^{2}+\tau+\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}\right)}.

Therefore, as
\tau\to+\infty
, we get

\sqrt{|k_{+}(z)|^{2}+\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}}=\sqrt{2\tau}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\right).

Replacing this into the denominator of the right hand side of (
4.7
), we deduce the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)
as in the statement of the lemma.
∎

Now, we restrict ourselves to
\delta\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
, note that we have the following relations

(4.8)

\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta)=\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}),\qquad\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta)=-\sgn(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}).

As a consequence, Lemma
4.3
produces the following asymptotic expansion

(4.9)

\displaystyle|k_{+}(z)|=\sqrt{\tau}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|k_{-}(z)|=\sqrt{\tau}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|=\frac{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)|=2\sqrt{\tau}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Using these expansions, we obtain the asymptotic formulas for
|K_{++}(z)|,|K_{--}(z)|
and
|K_{+-}(z)|
defined in (
4.4
):

\displaystyle|K_{++}(z)|=\frac{2\sqrt{\tau}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|K_{--}(z)|=\frac{2\sqrt{\tau}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|K_{+-}(z)|=\frac{2\sqrt{\tau}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

Next step, we compute the asymptotic expansions for
A(z),B(z),C(z),D(z)
and
\widetilde{B}(z)
appearing in Proposition
4.2
. For
A(z),C(z),D(z)
, it is easier to obtain their asymptotic formulas by using Lemma
4.3
for
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{\pm}(z)
and the above estimates for
K_{++}(z)
,
K_{--}(z)
and
K_{+-}(z)
:

\displaystyle A(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle C(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle D(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

For
B(z)
and
\widetilde{B}(z)
, we need more efforts, but it is a straightforward calculation:

\displaystyle B(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\tau\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|V_{+}-V_{-}||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{1/2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|}+\frac{\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\tau\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|V_{+}-V_{-}||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{1/2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{1/2}}\frac{4|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|\tau\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|V_{+}-V_{-}||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{3/2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{3/2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Here, in the second equality, we used (
4.8
) to obtain

(4.10)

|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|+|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|=|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|\qquad\text{for all }\delta\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|.

From the formula of
\widetilde{B}(z)
, in order to have its asymptotic, we firstly need to calculate
\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{++}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)
and
\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{--}(z)\overline{K_{\pm}(z)}\right)
. From the definitions of
K_{++}(z)
and
K_{+-}(z)
in (
4.4
) and thanks to Lemma

, (
4.8
) and (

), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{++}(z)\overline{K_{+-}(z)}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)|^{2}}\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\frac{k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)}{2k_{+}(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\frac{2i\sqrt{\tau}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|^{1/2}}\right)}{2i\sqrt{\tau}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|^{1/2}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

and in the same way, we also obtain

\mathrm{Re}\,\left(K_{--}(z)\overline{K_{\pm}(z)}\right)=\frac{4\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

From these estimates, the asymptotic expansion for
\widetilde{B}(z)
is followed from Lemma
4.3
(for

) and (
4.10
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\tau}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{1/2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{1/2}}\left(\frac{\frac{8\tau^{3/2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|}+\frac{\frac{8\tau^{3/2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right)}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{3/2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{3/2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

We notice that
\widetilde{B}(z)
has the same asymptotic limit as
B(z)

in (

\tau\to+\infty
. Now, we can calculate the asymptotic behavior of all terms appearing in these bounds:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(A(z)-C(z)\right)^{2}+B(z)^{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{4|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|^{2}\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right),

\displaystyle A(z)+C(z)+2D(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{4|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|^{2}\tau^{2}}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Here, in the first and the second equalities, respectively, we used the fact that (follows from (
4.8
)), for all
\delta\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
,

(4.11)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}+\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-2\delta|^{2}+4|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|=|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|^{2},

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{2}+|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta|^{2}+2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|=|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|^{2}.

Therefore, the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{1,z}
has the following asymptotic behavior

(4.12)

\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|=\frac{2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|\tau}{|V_{+}-V_{-}||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Next step, we will show that
\|\mathcal{R}_{2,z}\|
is merely a small perturbation compared with
\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|
as
\tau\to+\infty
. To do that, the upper bound for the norm of
\mathcal{R}_{2,z}
will be established by applying the Schur test. Considering the kernel of
\mathcal{R}_{2,z}
given in (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{2,z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2|k_{+}(z)|}\frac{2-e^{-\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)x}}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}&&\text{ for }x>0,\\
&\frac{1}{2|k_{-}(z)|}\frac{2-e^{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)x}}{\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}&&\text{ for }x<0,\end{aligned}\right.

It yields that, for all
z\in\rho(\mathscr{L})
(hence,
\mathrm{Re}\,k_{\pm}(z)>0
),

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{R}_{2,z}(x,y)|\,\mathrm{d}y=\max\left(\frac{1}{|k_{+}(z)|\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)},\frac{1}{|k_{-}(z)|\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}\right).

Since
\mathcal{R}_{2,z}(x,y)
is symmetric for almost everywhere
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, thus, by the Schur test, we obtain

(4.13)

\|\mathcal{R}_{2,z}\|\leq\frac{1}{|k_{+}(z)|\mathrm{Re}\,k_{+}(z)}+\frac{1}{|k_{-}(z)|\mathrm{Re}\,k_{-}(z)}=\frac{2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\delta||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\delta|}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Then, by considering (
4.12
), we obtain what we want to show, that is

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathcal{R}_{2,z}\|}{\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right).

By applying triangular inequality, it yields that

(4.14)

\left\|\left(\mathscr{L}-z\right)^{-1}\right\|=\|\mathcal{R}_{1,z}\|\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right)\right).

Thus, the asymptotic expansion for
\|\left(\mathscr{L}-z\right)^{-1}\|
is deduced from (
4.14
) and (
4.12
).

4.2.
(Non)-triviality of the pseudospectrum of
\mathscr{L}
: Proof of Corollary
2.8

Let us consider two situations mentioned in this corollary:

(1)

If
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}=\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
, then, thanks to (
2.2
) and (
2.7
), we have

\overline{\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})}=[\widehat{V},+\infty)=\sigma(\mathscr{L}),

where
\widehat{V}
defined as in (
2.8
). By using Proposition
2.5
in which
W(\mathscr{L})=\rho(\mathscr{L})
, we obtain, for all

,

(4.15)

\|(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{\textup{dist}(z,\sigma(\mathscr{L}))}.

From the definition of pseudospectrum (
1.2
), we deduce that the
\varepsilon
-pseudospectrum of
\mathscr{L}
is exact the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the spectrum
\sigma(\mathscr{L})
. In fact, the formula (
4.15
) is true for general unbounded normal operator
T
(in this case,
\mathscr{L}
is a normal operator, by Proposition
2.1
), indeed, since
T
is normal, its resolvent is a bounded normal operator (see
[
23
, Proposition 3.26(v)]
), therefore, the norm of the resolvent is equal to its spectral radius
[
6
, Proposition 3.27]
:

\displaystyle\|(T-z)^{-1}\|=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma((T-z)^{-1})\right\}=\sup\left\{\frac{1}{|\eta-z|}:\eta\in\sigma(T)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\inf\{|\eta-z|:\eta\in\sigma(T)\}}=\frac{1}{\textup{dist}(z,\sigma(T))}.

Here, in the second equality, we have used the fact that
\lambda\in\sigma((T-z)^{-1})\setminus\{0\}
if and only if
\lambda=\frac{1}{\eta-z}
for some
\eta\in\sigma(T)
.

(2)

If
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
, Theorem
2.7
implies that, for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, there exists
M>0
such that, for all
\mathrm{Re}\,z>M
and for all
\mathrm{Im}\,z\in|(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-})|
, we have

\left\|(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}\right\|\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\frac{2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|}{|V_{+}-V_{-}|}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,z}{|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|}.

Thus, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if we consider

\mathrm{Re}\,z>\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})}\frac{|V_{+}-V_{-}||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,z||\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}-\mathrm{Im}\,z|}{2|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|}

then
\left\|(\mathscr{L}-z)^{-1}\right\|>\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and the conclusion in this case follows.

In order to see the non-triviality of the pseudospectrum of
\mathscr{L}
when
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
, we just need to take
\varepsilon
sufficiently small and consider
z_{\varepsilon}
on the line
\mathrm{Im}\,z=\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}+\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}}{2}
whose real part is large enough such that
z_{\varepsilon}\in\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\mathscr{L})
, it is easy to see that
z_{\varepsilon}
always stay away from the spectrum at distance
\frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}|
.

5.
Pseudospectrum of the complex point interaction

5.1.
Stability of essential spectra under point interaction: Proof of Lemma
2.11

We prove this lemma by finite extensional method, cf.
[
11
, Sec. IX.4]
. This method says that if
\mathscr{L}
is a closed
n
-dimensional extension of a closed, densely defined operator
\mathscr{T}
,
i.e.
,
\mathscr{T}\subset\mathscr{L}
and there is an
n
-dimensional subspace
F
of
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})
such that
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})=\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})\dotplus F
(
i.e.
,
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})
is a direct sum of two linear vector spaces
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
and
F
), then the third three essential spectra of
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{T}
are identical:
\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{L})=\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{T})
for
\textup{k}\in[[1,3]]
. We can not apply directly this method to two operators
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
, since their domains are not extensions of each other. We need to find a mediator operator between two operators
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
so that we obtain the transitive property between essential spectra of these operators. Let us make the previous sentence to be clear: We consider an operator
\mathscr{T}
which is a common restriction of
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
, defined by

\displaystyle\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})

\displaystyle=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{R}):u(0)=u^{\prime}(0)=0\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}u

\displaystyle=-u^{\prime\prime}+V(x)u,\qquad\forall u\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T}).

and we will show that

(a)

\mathscr{T}
is a closed densely defined operator;

(b)

\mathscr{L}
is a
2
-dimensional extension of
\mathscr{T}
;

(c)

\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is a
2
-dimensional extension of
\mathscr{T}
.

Let us start with
(a)
. It is not hard to prove that
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, we take
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and we consider a sequence of smooth function
\left(\xi_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
such that
\textup{supp }\xi_{n}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}:0<|x|<n+1\}
and
\xi_{n}(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\frac{1}{n}\leq|x|\leq n
, then
\xi_{n}f\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
and
\|\xi_{n}f-f\|\xrightarrow{n\to+\infty}0
by the dominated convergence theorem. To prove that
\mathscr{T}
is closed, we consider the graph norm
\|u\|_{\mathscr{T}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\|\mathscr{T}u\|^{2}+\|u\|^{2}}
defined for all
u\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
, this norm is equivalent to the norm in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, take any Cauchy sequence
u_{n}
in
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
under the graph norm, this sequence will converge to a function
u
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. By the Sobolev embedding of

into
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, we have
u(0)=u^{\prime}(0)=0
. Thus, we have shown that
\left(\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{T}}\right)
is complete or equivalently,
\mathscr{T}
is closed.

Next, we prove
(b)
. Let
\phi\in\mathbb{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\phi(x)=1
in some neighbourhood of zero. Take

, then, we can show that

u(x)-\left[u(0)\phi(x)+u^{\prime}(0)x\phi(x)\right]\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T}).

It means that

\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})\subset\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})+F,\qquad F:=\textup{span}\{\phi(x),x\phi(x)\}.

Note that

(since
\phi(x)
and

are linear independent). Since both
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})
and

are subspaces of
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})
, therefore, we have the direction
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})\supset\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})+F
. Furthermore, we have
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})\cap F=\{0\}
, thus it implies the statement
(b)
.

To prove

, we modify functions in

such that we have functions belonging to
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
. Let us consider a function

as follows

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(x):=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}|x|+1\right)\phi(x).

It is clear that

and

,
\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0^{+})=\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0^{-})=-\frac{\alpha}{2}
. This implies that

. It is also clear that

, therefore, we have

Take

, we can verify that

v(x)\coloneqq u(x)-\left[u(0)\phi_{\alpha}(x)+\frac{u^{\prime}(0^{+})+u^{\prime}(0^{-})}{2}x\phi_{\alpha}(x)\right]\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T}).

Indeed, by direct computation, we have

and

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}(0^{+})-\frac{\alpha u(0)}{2}-\frac{u^{\prime}(0^{+})+u^{\prime}(0^{-})}{2}=\frac{u^{\prime}(0^{+})-u^{\prime}(0^{-})-\alpha u(0)}{2}=0,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(0^{-})

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}(0^{-})+\frac{\alpha u(0)}{2}-\frac{u^{\prime}(0^{+})+u^{\prime}(0^{-})}{2}=-\frac{u^{\prime}(0^{+})-u^{\prime}(0^{-})-\alpha u(0)}{2}=0,

where the last equalities comes from the jump condition of
u
in
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
. In other words, we have shown that
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\subset\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})+G
. Therefore, we obtain the equality
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})=\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{T})+G
(since both

and

are subspaces of
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})

.

Thanks to

,
(b)
,

, we can apply

to obtain

\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{L})=\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{T})=\sigma_{\textup{ek}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\qquad\text{ for }k\in\{1,2,3\}.

The statement on essential spectra of

in other type,

,
\textup{k}\in\{4,5\}
, is followed from

5.2.
Existence of the eigenvalue: Proof of Theorem

Since
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is
\mathcal{T}
-self-adjoint (see Proposition

), its residual spectrum is empty, thus its spectrum is decomposed as a union of the point spectrum and the continuous spectrum. Thanks to Lemma

, the set

are essential spectrum of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
. Therefore, take
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)
, then
\Ran(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}-z)
is closed and this implies that

(because if
z\in\sigma_{\textup{c}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
, we have a contradiction with the definition of the continuous spectrum). In other words, we have shown that

(5.1)

\sigma_{\textup{c}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\subset[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty).

Since the action of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
and
\mathscr{L}
are the same, we can use the argument in the proof of Proposition
2.2
to solve the eigenvalue equation
(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}-z)u=0
. From (
3.2
) with a notice that
f=0
in the argument, the general solution restricted on
\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
, denoted as
u_{\pm}
, reads as

(5.2)

u_{\pm}(x)=A_{\pm}e^{k_{\pm}(z)x}+B_{\pm}e^{-k_{\pm}(z)x},\qquad\text{for }\pm x>0.

In the same manner as in the proof of Theorem
2.4
(subsection
3.2
), we can easily show that no point in
[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)
can be the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
and thus,

[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\subset\sigma_{\textup{c}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}).

Combining this inclusion with (
5.1
), we conclude (
2.16
). The fact that the point spectrum is the discrete spectrum comes from
[
18
, Proposition 5.4.3]
which states that
\sigma_{\textup{dis}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})=\sigma(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\setminus\sigma_{\textup{e5}}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
.

Now, we look for the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
in the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\left([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\right)
. Take
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\right)
and assume that
(z,u)
is the eigenpair of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
. From the decaying of
u
at
\pm\infty
, with reasons as in (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), it implies that
A_{+}=0
and
B_{-}=0
. Replacing these conditions into (
5.2
), we get

(5.3)

u(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &B_{+}e^{-k_{+}(z)x},\qquad&&\text{ for }x>0,\\
&A_{-}e^{k_{-}(z)x},\qquad&&\text{ for }x<0.\end{aligned}\right.

In order
u
belong to
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
, it is necessary that
A_{-}
and
B_{+}
are non-trivial solution of the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &B_{+}-A_{-}=0,\\
&-k_{+}(z)B_{+}-(k_{-}(z)+\alpha)A_{-}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Vice versa, if there exists non zero constants
A_{-}
and
B_{+}
satisfies the above system, it is easy to check that

given by (
5.3
) is indeed the eigenfunction associated with the eigenvalue
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\right)
. This is equivalent to the fact that
z
is eigenvalue if and only if
z
satisfies the following algebraic equation depending on the parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}

(5.4)

k_{+}(z)+k_{-}(z)=-\alpha,

and
z\notin\left([V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)\right)
. We can assume that
\alpha\neq 0
(since when
\alpha=0
, then
\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathscr{L}
, we already know that there is not eigenvalue in this case). Using the fact that

k_{+}(z)^{2}-k_{-}(z)^{2}=V_{+}-V_{-},

from (
5.4
), we implies that

(5.5)

k_{+}(z)-k_{-}(z)=-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{\alpha}.

Thanks to (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we get

(5.6)

\sqrt{V_{+}-z}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}\text{ and }\sqrt{V_{-}-z}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}.

By squaring one of two equations in (
5.6
), it yields that

(5.7)

z=z(\alpha)=\frac{V_{+}+V_{-}}{2}-\frac{(V_{+}-V_{-})^{2}}{4\alpha^{2}}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}.

Thus, we have shown that if the equation (
5.4
) has solution, then its solution is necessary in the form (
5.7
).
It means that, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the point
z(\alpha)
is the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies

(5.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{V_{+}-z(\alpha)}+\sqrt{V_{-}-z(\alpha)}=-\alpha,\\
&z(\alpha)\notin[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

Since two equations in (
5.6
) are implication equations of (
5.4
), then
\alpha
satisfying (
5.8
) if and only if

(5.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{V_{+}-z(\alpha)}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha},\\
&\sqrt{V_{-}-z(\alpha)}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha},\\
&z(\alpha)\notin[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

We will show that (
5.9
) is equivalent to

(5.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{Re}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}\right)>0,\\
&\mathrm{Re}\,\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}\right)>0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Indeed we prove the statement in two directions

•

(
5.9
)
\Longrightarrow
(
5.10
): Since

, then the real part of the square root
\sqrt{V_{+}-z(\alpha)}
must be positive. The same argument for the real part of the square root
\sqrt{V_{-}-z(\alpha)}
.

•

(
5.10
)
\Longrightarrow
(
5.9
): Using the fact that, for
w
is a complex number,
\sqrt{w^{2}}=w
if
\mathrm{Re}\,w>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{V_{+}-z(\alpha)}=\sqrt{\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha},

\displaystyle\sqrt{V_{-}-z(\alpha)}=\sqrt{\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}\right)^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{V_{+}-V_{-}}{2\alpha}.

If
V_{\pm}-z(\alpha)\in(-\infty,0]
, then
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{V_{\pm}-z(\alpha)}=0
and thus, we have a contradiction with (
5.10
). Therefore, the last condition in (
5.9
) is obtained.

In summary, we have just shown that the point
z(\alpha)
is the eigenvalue of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies conditions in (
5.10
). By elementary calculations, the conditions for
\alpha
in (
5.10
) can be read as

\left|\langle V_{+}-V_{-},\alpha\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\right|<-|\alpha|^{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha,

which is the description of the set
\Omega
in the statement of this theorem. The eigenfunction associated with

must be in the form of (
5.3
) with
A_{-}=B_{+}
, then, thanks to (

), the eigenspace associated with

is expressed as

\textup{Ker}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}-z(\alpha))=\{Cu_{\alpha}:C\in\mathbb{C}\},

where

is given by (

).

Let us recall and reuse the convention in the beginning of Section

\displaystyle|n_{1}(z,\alpha)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\tau|n_{2}(z,\alpha)-p_{2}(z,\alpha)|^{2}}{|\alpha|^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|^{1/2}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|p_{1}(z,\alpha)|^{2}

Appendix A
Properties of the operator
\mathscr{L}
: Proof of Proposition
2.1

Let us introduce a translated sesquilinear of
Q
, that is

\widetilde{Q}(u,v)\coloneqq Q(u,v)+(1-\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\})\langle u,v\rangle,\qquad\textup{Dom}(\widetilde{Q})\coloneqq H^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

The coercivity of the sesquilinear
\widetilde{Q}
is given by, for all
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{Q}(u,u)\right|\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\,\widetilde{Q}(u,u)

\displaystyle=\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|u\|^{2}+(\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+}-\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\})\int_{0}^{+\infty}|u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+(\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}-\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\})\int_{-\infty}^{0}|u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

It is easy to obtain the continuity of
\widetilde{Q}
,
i.e.,
there exists a positive constant
C
such that
\left|\widetilde{Q}(u,v)\right|\leq C\|u\|_{H^{1}}\|v\|_{H^{1}}
. Then, by Lax-Milgram theorem (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 2.89]
),
\widetilde{Q}
is associated with a closed, densely defined and bijective operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
whose domain is given by

\displaystyle\textup{Dom}\left(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\right)=\left\{\begin{aligned} u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):&\text{ there exists }\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\text{ such that }\\
&\widetilde{Q}(u,v)=\left\langle\widetilde{f},v\right\rangle\text{ for all }v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\end{aligned}\right\},

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}u,v\right\rangle=\widetilde{Q}(u,v),\qquad\forall u\in\textup{Dom}\left(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\right),\forall v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

Then, the operator
\mathscr{L}
defined by shifting the operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
, that is

\mathscr{L}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}-\left(1-\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\}\right),\qquad\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})=\textup{Dom}(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}).

(1)

We will show that
\textup{Dom}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
u\in\textup{Dom}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)
, by considering
v
in (
2.1
) on the space of test functions
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then for each
u\in\textup{Dom}\left(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\right)
, we get in the distributional sense that

\mathscr{L}u=-u^{\prime\prime}+V(x)u.

Since
\mathscr{L}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
V(x)u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we implies that
u^{\prime\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and thus
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore,
\textup{Dom}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The remaining direction
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset\textup{Dom}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)
is easily obtained by integration by parts. Since
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}
is bijective, it implies that
-\left(1-\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\}\right)\in\rho(\mathscr{L})
. In other words,
\rho(\mathscr{L})
is nonempty.

(2)

Let us recall here the definition of the numerical range of an operator, it is a subset in
\mathbb{C}
defined by

\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})=\left\{\left\langle\mathscr{L}\psi,\psi\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}:\,\psi\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}),\,\|\psi\|=1\right\}.

Given
\psi\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L})
such that
\|\psi\|=1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{L}\psi,\psi\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\|\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+V_{+}\int_{0}^{+\infty}|\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+V_{-}\int_{-\infty}^{0}|\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Let
s=\int_{0}^{+\infty}|\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
, it is clear that
s\in[0,1]
because of the normalization of
\psi
, then we can write

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{L}\psi,\psi\right\rangle=\|\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+sV_{+}+(1-s)V_{-}.

This implies that
\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})\subset\{(0,+\infty)+sV_{+}+(1-s)V_{-}:\,s\in[0,1]\}
. In order to prove the remaining direction, we fix a function
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\textup{supp}\,f\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\|f\|=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=1
. By setting a family of functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, for
\lambda>0
,

\psi_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}f(\lambda x),

it is obvious that
\|\psi_{\lambda}\|=\|f\|=1
and
\|\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}\|=\lambda\|f^{\prime}\|
. Therefore,

\left\langle\mathscr{L}\psi_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda}\right\rangle=t+V_{+},

where
t=\lambda\|f^{\prime}\|^{2}
can take arbitrary positive value when
\lambda
runs on
(0,\infty)
. Consequently,
[V_{+},+\infty)\subset\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})
. In the same manner by choosing a function
f
whose support lies inside
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, we also obtain
[V_{-},+\infty)\subset\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})
. In other words, two lines
[V_{+},+\infty)
and
[V_{-},+\infty)
are contained in
\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})
. Since
\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})
is a convex set, it also contains the convex hull (
i.e.
the smallest convex set containing) of the set
[V_{+},+\infty)\cup[V_{-},+\infty)
, which is precisely the set
\{(0,+\infty)+sV_{+}+(1-s)V_{-}:\,s\in[0,1]\}
. Therefore, the numerical range is described exactly as in (
2.2
).

We use
[
23
, Proposition 3.19]
to show that
\mathscr{L}
is a
m
-sectorial operator. By choosing a sector
S_{c,\theta}
whose vertex
c
is some point in the middle of two points
\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\}-1
and
\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\}
on the real axis with a suitable semi-angle
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2})
such that the numerical range
\textup{Num}(\mathscr{L})
is included inside
S_{c,\theta}
, since the point
\min\{\mathrm{Re}\,V_{+},\mathrm{Re}\,V_{-}\}-1\in\rho(\mathscr{L})\setminus S_{c,\theta}
,
\mathscr{L}
is
m
-sectorial.

(3)

The operator
\mathscr{L}
can be seen as the sum of a self-adjoint operator
-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}
(with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
) and a bounded operator
V(x)
(on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
), then, thanks to
[
23
, Prop. 1.6(vii)]
, we have

\mathscr{L}^{*}=\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V(x)\right)^{*}=\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}\right)^{*}+\left(V(x)\right)^{*}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\,\overline{V(x)}.

By directing computation, we get that, for all
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{L}f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|-f^{\prime\prime}+(\mathrm{Re}\,V)f\|^{2}+\|(\mathrm{Im}\,V)f\|^{2}+2\mathrm{Im}\,\left\langle-f^{\prime\prime},\mathrm{Im}\,Vf\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{L}^{*}f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|-f^{\prime\prime}+(\mathrm{Re}\,V)f\|^{2}+\|(\mathrm{Im}\,V)f\|^{2}-2\mathrm{Im}\,\left\langle-f^{\prime\prime},\mathrm{Im}\,Vf\right\rangle.

Since the operator
\mathscr{L}
and its adjoint
\mathscr{L}^{*}
share the same domain,
\mathscr{L}
is normal if and only if
\|\mathscr{L}f\|=\|\mathscr{L}^{*}f\|
for all
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, or

\mathrm{Im}\,\left\langle-f^{\prime\prime},\mathrm{Im}\,Vf\right\rangle=0,\qquad\forall f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

By using integral by parts, this condition is equivalent to

\left(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,\left(f^{\prime}(0)\overline{f(0)}\right)=0,\qquad\forall f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Since there exists
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathrm{Im}\,\left(f^{\prime}(0)\overline{f(0)}\right)\neq 0
, for example,
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}+i\frac{x}{x^{2}+1}
, thus the normality of
\mathscr{L}
is equivalent to
\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}=\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
.
From the formula of
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{L}^{*}
, it is obviously that
\mathscr{L}
is self-adjoint iff
V=\overline{V}
,
i.e.
,
\mathrm{Im}\,V=0
. Next, let us check when
\mathscr{L}
is
\mathcal{P}
-self-adjoint,
\mathcal{T}
-self-adjoint and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric by straightforward computation, given
u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathscr{L}\mathcal{P}u(x)

\displaystyle=-u^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(-x)u(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\mathscr{L}\mathcal{T}u(x)

\displaystyle=-u^{\prime\prime}(x)+\overline{V}(x)u(x),

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\mathcal{PT}u(x)

\displaystyle=-\overline{u}^{\prime\prime}(-x)+V(x)\overline{u}(-x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{PT}\mathscr{L}u(x)

\displaystyle=-\overline{u}^{\prime\prime}(-x)+\overline{V}(-x)\overline{u}(-x).

Thus, all the conclusions on
\mathcal{P}
-self-adjointness,
\mathcal{T}
-self-adjointness and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry of
\mathscr{L}
follows obviously.

\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\neq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}
. We consider, for example, the function

f(x)=\left(e^{\frac{(1-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\alpha)x}{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}}}\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(x)+e^{\frac{(1-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\alpha)x}{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}}}\textbf{{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x)\right)\varphi(x),

where
\varphi(x)
is a smooth cut-off function mentioned above, then,

f(0)=1,\qquad f^{\prime}(0^{+})=\frac{1-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\alpha}{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}},\qquad f^{\prime}(0^{-})=\frac{1-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}\alpha}{\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}}.

It is clear that
f\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
and
\left(\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}f^{\prime}(0^{+})-\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}f^{\prime}(0^{-})\right)\overline{f(0)}=1
. Therefore,
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is nonnormal in this case.

Case 2
:

\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}=\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}=0
. Since (
B.4
) is automatically satisfied for all
f\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
,
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is normal if and only if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Case 3
:

\mathrm{Im}\,V_{+}=\mathrm{Im}\,V_{-}\neq 0
. Condition (
B.4
) is equivalent to

\alpha|f(0)|^{2}=0\qquad\forall f\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}).

It is easy to check, using the function
e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}|x|}\varphi(x)
mentioned above, that
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is normal if and only if
\alpha=0
.

From the above cases, the necessary and sufficient conditions for the normality of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is shown as in the statement of this proposition. The condition for self-adjointness of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
follows easily from comparing the domains and the actions of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
and
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*}
. In order to check when
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is
\mathcal{P}
-self-adjoint,
\mathcal{T}
-self-adjoint and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric, we notice that since actions of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
and
\mathscr{L}
(similarly,
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*}
and
\mathscr{L}^{*}
) are the same when they act on their domains, thus we can use (
A.2
) to get the conditions on
V
as in Proposition
2.1
. However, unlike the operator
\mathscr{L}
, the domains of
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
and its adjoint
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*}
are not the same, it leads to the fact that
\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*})
and
\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{P}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P})
(or
\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{T}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{T})
) are not necessary the same. Thus, we need to check them carefully to get condition on
\alpha
.

\displaystyle\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{P}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P})=

\displaystyle\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):u(-x)\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}):u^{\prime}(0^{+})-u^{\prime}(0^{-})=-\alpha u(0)\},

and

\displaystyle\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{T}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{T})=

\displaystyle\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\overline{u(x)}\in\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}):u^{\prime}(0^{+})-u^{\prime}(0^{-})=\overline{\alpha}u(0)\}.

Therefore, we have
\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{P}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P})=\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*})
if and only if
\overline{\alpha}=-\alpha
, in other words,
\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha=0
, while
\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{T}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{T})=\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{*})
is always true for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. In the same manner, we can check that
\textup{Dom}(\mathcal{PT}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})\subset\textup{Dom}(\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{PT})
is ensured if and only if
\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha=0
.
