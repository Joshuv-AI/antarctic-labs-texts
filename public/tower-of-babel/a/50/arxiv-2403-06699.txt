Solving Distributed Flexible Job Shop Scheduling Problems in the Wool Textile Industry with Quantum Annealing
Lilia Toma, Markus Zajac, Uta Störl

Abstract
Many modern manufacturing companies have evolved from a single production site to a multi-factory production environment that must handle both geographically dispersed production orders and their multi-site production steps. The availability of a range of machines in different locations capable of performing the same operation and shipping times between factories have transformed planning systems from the classic JSSP ( JSSP ) to DFJSP ( DFJSP ). As a result, the complexity of production planning has increased significantly. In our work, we use QA ( QA ) to solve the DFJSP . In addition to the assignment of production orders to production sites, the assignment of production steps to production sites also takes place. This requirement is based on a real use case of a wool textile manufacturer. To investigate the applicability of this method to large problem instances, problems ranging from 50 variables up to 250 variables, the largest problem that could be embedded into a D-Wave quantum annealer QPU ( QPU ), are formulated and solved. Special attention is dedicated to the determination of the Lagrange parameters of the QUBO ( QUBO ) model and the QPU configuration parameters, as these factors can significantly impact solution quality. The obtained solutions are compared to solutions obtained by SA ( SA ), both in terms of solution quality and calculation time. The results demonstrate that QA has the potential to solve large problem instances specific to the industry.

Introduction

This paper is based on a use case of a textile manufacturing company that operates in the worsted yarn industry and specializes in pure wool and wool blends for weaving, flat and circular knitting, luxury, as well as the non-apparel market. This business-to-business company has production sites in several countries on different continents. Production sites in different locations perform only specific production steps and work together in sequence-dependent setups to fulfill each individual sales order. For this purpose, sales orders first become production orders that must be assigned to the various production sites for the processing of various production steps. Given the scattering of production sites that might be involved in the processing of one single production order, a thorough production planning is critical.

4.3
Variable pruning

In the previous section, the binary variable
x
{}_{\text{i,o,m,t}}
was defined to represent whether an operation
o
of a job
i
is scheduled to start on a machine
m
at time
t
or not. To reduce the number of variables in the model, a method called variable pruning is used, which removes variables that are not possible in valid solutions. First, an upper bound
t
{}_{\text{max}}
of the highest possible makespan is calculated. Without this step, the solver will have to consider all solutions between zero to infinity, which implies a very large solution space that makes it difficult to embed and solve the
QUBO

[
24
]
. Instead, the search space is limited by defining the upper bound of a schedule as the sum of the maximum processing time of each operation in all jobs and maximum shipping time between these operations. Thus, a significant number of variables
x
{}_{\text{i,o,m,t}}
can be pruned by applying restrictions on time
t
.

Next, the duration and order of operations within a job are examined. Variables that allow an operation to execute at a given time that results in an invalid schedule are removed. Specifically, variables are pruned to allow operations to start earlier than the sum of the processing time of its predecessors, or if they allow operations to start later than the sum of its own processing time and that of the successor operations subtracted from
t
{}_{\text{max}}

[
29
]
.

To set a lower bound of operations in a job, the notion of minimum predecessor time
P
{}_{\text{i,o}}
of an operation is used:

P_{i,o}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{o}^{\prime}<r_{o}\\
m^{\prime}\in M_{i,o^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}{\min}(p_{i,o^{\prime},m^{\prime}}+d_{m,m^{\prime}})

P
{}_{\text{i,o}}
is the minimum predecessor time of the operation
o
, calculated as the sum of the minimum processing time of the preceding operations and shipping distance between them. A starting time earlier than the minimum predecessor time is invalid, therefore, these binary variables can be eliminated from the model
[
21
]
.

Additionally, an upper bound for the starting time
t
of operations can be set. This corresponds to the maximum makespan
t
{}_{\text{max}}
minus the sum of the processing time of the operation and that of all subsequent operations and shipping time between machines
S
{}_{\text{i,o}}
:

t_{max}-p_{i,o,m}-S_{i,o}

(11)

where:

S_{i,o}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{o}^{\prime}\geq r_{o}\\
m,m^{\prime}\in M_{i,o^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}{\min}(p_{i,o_{i}^{\prime},m^{\prime}}+d_{m,m^{\prime}})

These variables can be also eliminated from the
QUBO
, since they represent invalid starting times
[
24
]
. Thus, the starting time of operations

should be in the range
|\emph{P$_{\text{i,o}}$},\emph{t$_{\text{max}}$}-\emph{S$_{\text{i,o}}$}|
, all the other variables that are not in this range are removed from the model to decrease the size of the computational problem and computation time
[
29
]
.

To further reduce the number of binary variables, the starting times of simultaneous operations on machines are also removed
[

5.2
Solver parameters

There are 20 solver parameters
[
6
]
that can be adjusted when submitting problems to solvers. In this experiment, the default settings are kept for most of them, including the default annealing time of 20
{\mu}
s. The parameters that are adjusted in the experiment are the number of reads and the chain strength.

Number of reads.
In theory, very long annealing times should keep the system in a low energy state, in practice noise due to hardware imperfections will push the state to a higher energy
[
4
]
. Therefore, short annealing times are preferred in order to take many samples from the energy distribution of the problem. The appropriate number of samples depends on the problem itself and on its energy distribution and is usually between 10 and 10000 samples
[
4
]
. In this experiment, the number of reads (’num_reads’) is set to 1000. Generally, increasing the number of reads can increase the probability to get better solutions, but improvements diminish due to some problem-dependent numbers
[
6
]
. For this model, increasing the number of reads much above 1000 does not seem to be of any advantage. Sampling the problem instance with 200 variables 3000 times produced a solution with an energy of 34 and 2 violated constraints, which is worse than the energy of the best solution of 24 and 1 violated constraint obtained with 1000 reads. This is consistent with the results obtained by
Carugno et al. 2022

[
4
]
, who found that increasing the number of reads from 1000 to 10000 did not have any beneficial effects.

Chain strength.
For a solution to be consistent, the chain of qubits has to act as a single qubit, meaning that all the qubits in the chain must have the same values after annealing
[
4
]
. Broken chains show different values of the qubits at the end of the annealing process. Chains must be set strong enough to ensure that the qubits of the chain are showing the same value compared to other qubits. If the chains strength is too small, then the qubits will show different values. If the chain strength parameter is too high, this will impede the qubits in the chain to change their state, the chain begins to behave like a separate entity that does not interact with other terms of the function.

We follow the D-Wave recommendation to determine the chain strength for each problem instance and start with two utility functions to estimate the chain strength
[
7
]
:

1.

scaled

2.

uniform_torque_compensation

The first function is returning a chain strength that is scaled to the problem bias range. The second function calculates a chain strength that attempts to compensate for the torque that would break the chain. Both methods are to be seen as a starting point to estimate the optimal chain strength, and not as rule, since this parameter is problem-dependent. In the next step, the chain strength parameter found in step 1 should be doubled or tripled, to check if this change has an impact on the percentage of broken chains.

To find the best chain strength for the model, the model is run on the
QPU
for each problem instance, and the resulting energy and the percentage of broken chains in the best solution are analyzed. The intention is to find the lowest energy possible, to at the same time having a low percentage of broken chains to ensure a consistent solution. Fig.

5.4
Discussion

In this paper, a step-by-step practical implementation of the
DFJSP
with distributed operations and its evaluation using D-Wave quantum annealer, based on the real use case from the wool textile industry is presented. First, the problem is described mathematically as a constraint satisfaction problem. Then, the process of reformulating the mathematical problem in
QUBO
format is discussed in detail, as well as its embedding into the D-Wave quantum annealer
QPU
and the configuration of
QPU
solver parameters. Finally, solutions are sampled from the
QPU
to find low-energy states of the cost function. While formulating the
QUBO
, particular attention is dedicated to the calculation of the appropriate Lagrange parameters (Subsection
4.4
), that increase the weights of the penalty terms. Another aspect that is carefully investigated while configuring the experiment is the chain strength parameter (Subsection
5.2
). Both aspects make up
Contribution 2
.

Another aim of the paper was to investigate whether
QA
is able to provide a speed advantage for the
DFJSP
with distributed operations specific to the wool textile industry. To answer this question, both qualitative and quantitative analyses are conducted. Current
QA
technology allows more qubit usage than classical gate model quantum computers. However, there are still limitations imposed by hard- and software imperfections. With this in mind, the test challenged up to the limit the ability of
QA
to deliver consistent solutions for large problem instances in the shortest time possible. To conduct the experiment, problem instances from size 50 to 250 are solved in steps of 50 variables with
QA
and with
SA
. Additionally, problems up to 400 variables are solved only with
SA

The qualitative analysis of the solutions returned indicates that for problem instances up to a size of 150 variables, both

With an increasing number of variables, hardware imperfections become visible, which manifest themselves in an increased number of broken chains in the returned solutions, and consequently, in solutions with higher energy due to broken constraints. upper half of possible solutions. The makespan determined by

6
Conclusion

Many modern manufacturing enterprises evolved from a single to a multi-factory production environment, as is the case with big production companies in the wool textile industry. Planning systems changed from classical
JSSP
to
DFJSP
, with companies facing the challenge of increased frequency and complexity of production planning. Classical heuristics deliver valid results on small problem instances but are unable to find solutions for problem sizes relevant to practice. Therefore, methods are required that yield high quality outcomes while reasonable computation times are ensured. In this paper, the concept of
QA
-based optimization for the
DFJSP
with geographically distributed operations is investigated to answer the question whether
QA
is able to provide a speed advantage for the
DFJSP
with distributed operations specific to the wool textile industry.

To do this, we show a step-by-step implementation of the workflow for solving the
DFJSP
with
QA
(
Contribution 1
). A special attention is devoted to topics that do not have a straightforward approach but a great impact on the solution quality, such as calculating the Lagrange parameters or determining the chain strength. The results show that up to a certain problem size,
QA
delivers consistent solutions that are not the global minimum solution in the solution space but yield a makespan in the first half of the allowed valid solutions (
Contribution 2
). For all instances,
SA
delivers comparable or slightly better results, which are consistent with other research. In terms of calculation time,
QA
needs more
QPU
time to find solutions for smaller problem instances in the test, but
QPU
access time increases at a declining rate with the growing problem size. In contrast, the
SA

CPU
time develops exponentially for problems with a larger number of variables. The results of the experiment show that starting with a certain problem size,
QA
could potentially offer a speed-up advantage, given that the expected improvements in hard- and software will take place (
Contribution 3
).

One of the future tasks is to solve larger problems on the next generation of quantum annealers to confirm the results obtained in this experiment. Other topics open for future research are
QPU
parameter tuning and configuration. There is also a need for additional research on embedding techniques that could minimize the length of the chains and allow the embedding of more
QUBO
variables into physical qubits, thus solving problems of greater size.
