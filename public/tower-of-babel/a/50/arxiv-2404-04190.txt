The link between 1 1 -norm approximation and effective Positivstellensatze for the hypercube
Etienne de Klerk, Juan C. Vera

Abstract
The Schmüdgen Positivstellensatz gives a certificate to verify positivity of a strictly positive polynomial f f on a compact, basic, semi-algebraic set 𝐊 ⊂ ℝ n \mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} . A Positivstellensatz of this type is called effective if one may bound the degrees of the polynomials appearing in the certificate in terms of properties of f f . If 𝐊 = [ − 1 , 1 ] n \mathbf{K}=[-1,1]^{n} and 0 < f min := min x ∈ 𝐊 ⁡ f ⁡ ( x ) 0<f_{\min}:=\min_{x\in\mathbf{K}}f(x) , then the degrees of the polynomials appearing in the certificate may be bounded by O ⁡ ( f max − f min f min ) O\left(\sqrt{\frac{f_{\max}-f_{\min}}{f_{\min}}}\right) , where f max := max x ∈ 𝐊 ⁡ f ⁡ ( x ) f_{\max}:=\max_{x\in\mathbf{K}}f(x) , as was recently shown by Laurent and Slot [ Optimization Letters 17:515–530, 2023]. The big-O notation suppresses dependence on n n and the degree d d of f f . In this paper we show a similar result, but with a better dependence on n n and d d . In particular, our bounds depend on the 1 1 -norm of the coefficients of f f , that may readily be calculated. Introduction

We first recall a
Positivstellensatz
of Schmüdgen
[
26
]
, which
gives representations for positive polynomials on a basic, closed, semi-algebraic set; see also, e.g.,
[
21
]
,
[
16
, Theorem 3.16]
,
[
18
]
.

Theorem 1

(Schmüdgen
[
26
]
)
.

Consider the set
\mathbf{K}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid g_{1}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\dotsc,g_{m}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}

with
g_{1},\dotsc,g_{m}\in\mathbb{R}[{\mathbf{x}}]
, where
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
denotes the ring of real polynomials
with variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Assume that
\mathbf{K}
is compact.
If
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is positive on
\mathbf{K}
, then
p
can be written as

p=\sum_{I\subseteq[m]}\sigma_{I}\prod_{i\in I}g_{i},

(1)

where
[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and each
\sigma_{I}
(
I\subseteq[m]
) belongs to the cone of sums-of-squares of polynomials, denoted by
\Sigma[\mathbf{x}]
.

A polynomial that allows a representation of the type (
1
) is said to lie in the pre-ordering associated with the (generating) polynomials
g_{1},\dotsc,g_{m}
, denoted by

\mathcal{T}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
.

We denote the real polynomials of degree at most
d
by
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, and define the
truncated pre-ordering of degree
r\in\mathbb{N}
by

\mathcal{T}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})_{r}:=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]\;:\;p=\sum_{I\subseteq[m]}\sigma_{I}\prod_{i\in I}g_{i},\;\mbox{deg}\left(\sigma_{I}\prod_{i\in I}g_{i}\right)\leq r,\;\sigma_{I}\in\Sigma[x]\;\forall I\in[m]\right\}.

(2)

For the hypercube
\mathbf{K}=[-1,1]^{n}
, described by the polynomial inequalities
1-x_{1}^{2}\geq 0,\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2}\geq 0
, we consider polynomials that allow a Schmüdgen-type representation of bounded degree
r
:

p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}\sigma_{I}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{i\in I}(1-x_{i}^{2})\in\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{r},

(3)

where the polynomials
\sigma_{I}
are sum-of-squares polynomials with degree at most
r-2|I|
(to ensure that the degree of each term in the sum in (
2
) is at most
r
).

We call a
Positivstellensatz
effective if one may bound the degree
r
in terms of properties of
p
.
The best bound of this type is due to Slot and Laurent
[
17
]
(with an alternative proof by Bach and Rudi
[
3
]
).

Theorem 2

(Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
)
.

Assume
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
and
f_{\min}:=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})>0
. Then

f\in\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{(r+1)n}

provided that

r\geq\max\left\{\pi d\sqrt{2n},\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{\min}}}\sqrt{C(n,d)(f_{\max}-f_{\min})}\right\},

where
C(n,d)>0
depends only on
n
and
d
, and may be chosen to be polynomial in
n
for fixed
d
, or polynomial in
d
for fixed
n
.
In particular

C(n,d)\leq 2\pi^{2}d^{2}n2^{n/2}(d+1)^{n}\mbox{ and }C(n,d)\leq 2\pi^{2}d^{2}n2^{d/2}(n+1)^{d}.

One may formulate this result in a different way after introducing the following semidefinite programming (SDP) approximation
of
f_{\min}:=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in[-1,1]^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})
:

f_{(r)}:=\sup\left\{\lambda\;|\;f-\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{r}\right\}\leq f_{\min}.

(4)

In particular, one has the following result.

Theorem 3

(Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
)
.

One has

f_{\min}-f_{((r+1)n)}\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}}C(n,d)(f_{\max}-f_{\min})\quad\forall r\geq\pi d\sqrt{2n},

where
C(n,d)
is as in Theorem
2
.

Contributions

In this paper we prove similar results to Theorems
2
and
3
.
We will consider a stronger SDP relaxation than (
4
), by considering the truncated pre-ordering

\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}:=\mathcal{T}(1+x_{1},1-x_{1},\ldots,1+x_{n},1-x_{n})_{r}

instead of
\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{r}
.

As noted by, e.g.  Powers and Reznick
[
20
, p. 4682]
and Baldi and Slot
[
4
, §6]
, these two truncated pre-orderings are closely related through the identities:

\displaystyle 1\pm x_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((1\pm x_{i})^{2}+1-x_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle 1-x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-x_{i})(1+x_{i}).

In particular, the identities imply

\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{r}\subset\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}\subset\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})_{2r}.

(5)

Of course, without truncation, the two pre-orderings coincide, i.e.
\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})=\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1}^{2},\ldots,1-x_{n}^{2})
.

Thus we consider the SDP lower bound on
f_{\min}
given by

\hat{f}_{(r)}:=\sup\left\{\lambda\;|\;f-\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}\right\}\geq f_{(r)}.

(6)

Due to the relation (
5
) between the two truncated pre-orderings, one has
f_{(r)}\leq\hat{f}_{(r)}\leq f_{(2r)}
.

We will show
(see Theorem
9
and Proposition
2
)
that

f_{\min}-\hat{f}_{(rn)}\leq\|f\|_{1,T}\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}\quad\forall r\geq\pi d,

(7)

where
\|f\|_{1,T}
is the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of
f
in the Chebyshev basis, i.e. the
1
-norm of the coefficients in
this basis.
This result is clearly of the same flavor as Theorem
3
, but the right-hand-side may be computed in an elementary way, and does not
explicitly depend on
n
. Moreover, the norm
\|f\|_{1,T}
can be of the same order as
(f_{\max}-f_{\min})
for some classes of polynomials.

In particular, as discussed in Section
2.3
, this is the case for the standard polynomial optimization reformulation of the maximum cut problem in graphs.

Furthermore, by comparing different norms of polynomials of bounded degrees, one may use our result to derive a result very similar to Theorem
3
.

We will also show an upper bound of the form
f_{\min}-\hat{f}_{(r)}\leq\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T}
, that is valid for all
r
(not only multiples of
n
),
where the quantity
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
depends
on
(r,n,d)
only, and may be upper bounded by solving an SDP problem;
see Theorem
9
. Thus an upper bound on
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T}
may readily be computed if
tables of SDP upper bounds on
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
are given. We give such (partial) tables in an appendix to this paper for small values of
n
.

]

Notation and preliminaries

2.1
Further notation

We will denote
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, and

\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}:=\left\{\alpha\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{n}\;|\;|\alpha|:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\leq d\right\},

and
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.

We will be considering polynomials of different types of variables, and will need to keep track of the degree of these polynomials with respect to only some of the variables. To do this, given a multivariate polynomial
p
and a subset of the variables
S
, we define
\deg_{S}p
as the
degree of
p
with respect to the variables in
S
. That is, when all the variables not in
S
are considered to be constants (or more correctly parameters). For instance let
p(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\beta}\mathbf{y}^{\gamma}
, then
\deg p=|\alpha|+|\beta|+|\gamma|
while
\deg_{\mathbf{x}}p=|\beta|
and
\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}p=|\alpha|+|\beta|
.

If we consider functions of two distinct types of variables on the hypercube, say
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in[-1,1]^{n}\times[-1,1]^{n}
, then
we define an associated truncated pre-ordering as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},\ldots,1\pm y_{n})_{r_{1},r_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad:=\left\{\sum_{I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}\subseteq[n]}\sigma_{I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\prod_{i\in I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}}}(1-x_{i})\prod_{i\in I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}}}(1+x_{i})\prod_{i\in I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}}}(1-y_{i})\prod_{i\in I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}}(1+y_{i})\right\},

where, for all
I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}\subseteq[n]
, one has
\sigma_{I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
, as well as

|I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}}|+|I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}}|+\mbox{deg}_{\mathbf{x}}\sigma_{I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq r_{1}
, and

|I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}}|+|I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}|+\mbox{deg}_{\mathbf{y}}\sigma_{I^{-}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{x}},I^{-}_{\mathbf{y}},I^{+}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq r_{2}
.
In other words, in this special truncated pre-ordering, the degree of each term is at most
r_{1}
in the
\mathbf{x}
variables, and at most
r_{2}
in the
\mathbf{y}
variables.

2.2
Chebyshev polynomials

In this section we review known results on Chebyshev polynomials for later use. The interested reader may find more information in the classic text by Rivlin
[
25
]
.

Let
\mathbf{K}=[-1,1]
and fix the measure
\mu
on
\mathbf{K}
defined by
\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=(\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}})^{-1}\mathrm{d}x
,
x\in\mathbf{K}
. The Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind form a system of orthogonal polynomials
for
\mu
. We will refer to the
k
-th Chebyshev polynomial of first kind as
T_{k}(x)
. We have, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,

\displaystyle T_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\cos(k\arccos x).

(8)

Define for
f,g:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f(x)g(x)}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x

to obtain the following orthogonality relations for the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind

\displaystyle\langle T_{k},T_{m}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\delta_{k,0}}{2}\delta_{k,m}.

(9)

We will denote the normalized Chebyshev polynomials by

\hat{T}_{0}=T_{0},\;\hat{T}_{k}=\sqrt{2}T_{k}\;(k\in\mathbb{N}).

It is straightforward to generalize the Chebyshev polynomials to the multivariate case by taking
products of univariate Chebyshev polynomials. Let
\mathbf{K}=[-1,1]^{n}
and define

\mathrm{d}\mu(\textbf{x}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}.

(10)

Then, for
\alpha\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{n}
the corresponding (normalized) multivariate Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind is defined as

\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{T}_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}).

The orthogonality relations extend in the following way

\displaystyle\langle\hat{T}_{\alpha},\hat{T}_{\beta}\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbf{K}}\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})\hat{T}_{\beta}(\textbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\hat{T}_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})\hat{T}_{\beta_{i}}(x_{i})}{\pi\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}}.

On has the following formula to convert from the univariate monomial basis to the Chebyshev basis:

x^{k}=2^{1-k}{\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{\overset{j=0}{k-j\mbox{ even}}}^{k}}\binom{k}{(k-j)/2}T_{j}(x),\quad(k\in{\mathbb{N}}),

(11)

where the prime at the sum symbol means the first term (at
j=0
for even
n
) should be halved.

2.3
Norms of polynomials

The
n
-variate polynomials of degree at most
d
, denoted by
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, may be
associated with
\mathbb{R}^{{n+d\choose d}}
via the polynomial coefficients in a
given basis.

Using the standard monomial basis, any
f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
may be written as

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
, with associated
1
-norm:

\|f\|_{1}:=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}|f_{\alpha}|.

Since the
1
-norm depends on the basis, we will use the notation
\|\cdot\|_{1,T}
(resp.
\|\cdot\|_{1,\hat{T}}
) for the Chebyshev (resp. normalized Chebyshev) basis. Note that
\|f\|_{1,\hat{T}}\leq\|f\|_{1,T}\leq 2^{d/2}\|f\|_{1,\hat{T}}
if
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
and

\|f\|_{1,\hat{T}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}^{d}}\|f\|_{1,T}
if
f
is homogeneous of degree
d
.

Lemma 1
.

One has

\|f\|_{1,T}\leq\|f\|_{1}\quad\forall\;f\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}].

(12)

Proof.

Since
T_{j}(1)=1
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
all the coefficients on the right-hand-side of
(
11
) are positive and sum to one. This implies that, for any (multivariate) monomial
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
, one has

\|f\|_{1,T}=\|f\|_{1}=1
. Thus, if
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
is a general degree
d
polynomial, one has (with slight abuse of notation):

\|f\|_{1}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}|f_{\alpha}|=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}\|f_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\|_{1,T}\geq\left\|\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\right\|_{1,T}\equiv\|f\|_{1,T},

where the inequality is the triangle inequality.
∎

We will only be concerned with the
1
-norm for different bases, but in general one may define the
p
-norm for any
p>0
:

\|f\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}|f_{\alpha}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

To avoid confusion with the usual
\ell_{p}
norm, we will use the notation, for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|f\|_{\ell_{p}}:=\left(\int_{\mathbf{K}}|f(\mathbf{x})|^{p}d\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/p},

where we only consider the case
\mathbf{K}=[-1,1]^{n}
,
and the sup-norm:

\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{K}}|f(\mathbf{x})|.

The relations between the (equivalent) norms
\|\cdot\|_{p}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{p}}
on
\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}
is a classical problem that is still a topic of active research; see, e.g.
[
2
]
for a recent overview.
Perhaps the best-known result along these lines is the Bohnenblust-Hille inequality:

\|f\|_{p}\leq C_{d}\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\quad\mbox{deg}(f)=d,\;p=\frac{2d}{d+1},

(13)

where the constant
C_{d}
depends on
d
but not on
n
.
For values of
p<\frac{2d}{d+1}
, the corresponding constant does depend on
n
.
One may obtain a bound for the
1
-norm instead by using the known inequalities between
p
-norms: if
0<q<p
one has

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq k^{1/q-1/p}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Setting
q=1
,
p=\frac{2d}{d+1}
, and
k={n+d\choose d}
, (
13
) implies

\|f\|_{1}\leq{n+d\choose d}^{1-\frac{d+1}{2d}}C_{d}\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}={n+d\choose d}^{\frac{d-1}{2d}}C_{d}\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}<{n+d\choose d}^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{d}\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}},

(14)

where
C_{d}
is the same constant as in (
13
).

Some stronger results are known in special cases, e.g, when
n=2
and
d=2
, one has

\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq\|f\|_{1}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}.

As another example, if
f(x)=\frac{1}{4}x^{T}Lx
where
L
is the Laplacian matrix of a graph, then
\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
is the maximum cut size in the graph, while
\|f\|_{1}
equals the number of edges in the graph. Thus, in this case one has
\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq\|f\|_{1}\leq 2\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
, i.e. the constant in the upper bound (
2
in this case) need not depend on
n
.

3
A key lemma

As before, let
T_{k}
denote the univariate Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind.
Then one has the well-known recursive relation
T_{0}(t)=1
,
T_{1}(t)=t
and
T_{k+1}(t)=2tT_{k}(t)-T_{k-1}(t)
.
Recall that we define the multivariate version by
T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}T_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})
.

Recall that
1\pm T_{\alpha}
is nonnegative on
[-1,1]^{n}
.
Our first goal is to prove the stronger result that
1\pm T_{\alpha}
belongs to the truncated pre-ordering of order
|\alpha|
, namely

1\pm T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{T}(1-x_{1},1+x_{1},...,1-x_{n},1+x_{n})_{|\alpha|}
.

In the proof, we will need the a classical result of this type for the special case of univariate polynomials.

Theorem 4

(Fekete, Markov-Lukácz (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
))
.

Let
p
be a univariate polynomial of degree

2m
. Then
p
is nonnegative on the interval
[a,b]
if and
only if

p(t)=q_{1}^{2}(t)+(t-a)(t-x)q_{2}^{2}(t),

for some polynomials
q_{1}
of degree
m
and
q_{2}
of degree
m-1
.
If the degree of
p
is
2m+1
then
p
is
nonnegative on
[a,b]
if and only if

p(t)=(t-a)q_{1}^{2}(t)+(b-t)q_{2}^{2}(t),

for some polynomials
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
of degree
m
.

We now give a multivariate generalization of this result.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\alpha\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{n}
we have
1\pm T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{|\alpha|}
.

Proof.

By applying the identities,

1+ab=[(1-a)(1+b)+(1+a)(1-b)]/2\mbox{ and }1-ab=[(1+a)(1+b)+(1-a)(1-b)]/2

repeatedly as necessary, we obtain that
1\pm T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=1\pm\prod_{i=1}^{n}T_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})

can be expressed as sums of products of terms of the form
1\pm T_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})
.

Thus, it is enough to show that
1\pm T_{k}(t)\in\mathcal{T}(1-t,1+t)_{k}
.
But this follows from the fact that every
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]_{k}
non-negative on
[-1,1]
is in
\mathcal{T}(1-t,1+t)_{k}
,
by Theorem
4
,
and the fact that
and
1\pm T_{k}
is nonnegative on
[-1,1]
. ∎

The main result of this section now follows as an immediate consequence of the last lemma.

Theorem 5
.

For all
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]
, one has
\|p\|_{1,T}-p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},...,1\pm x_{n})_{\deg p}
.

Proof.

Letting
p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}p_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
, we
get
\|p\|_{1,T}-p=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}|p_{\alpha}|(1-\mbox{sign}(p_{\alpha})T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}))
and thus the statement follows by the last lemma.∎

Corollary 1
.

If
f
,
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, and
p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{d}
, then

f+\|p-f\|_{1,T}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{d}.

In particular, replacing
f
by
f-f_{\min}
, one has

\hat{f}_{(r)}\geq f_{\min}-\min_{p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}}\|p-(f-f_{\min})\|_{1,T},

(15)

for
r\geq\deg f
, where
\hat{f}_{(r)}
is the SDP bound defined in (
6
).

Remark:

A quantitatative Schmüdgen-type theorem for the hypercube via sums-of-squares approximations

If

Note that

5
1
-norm appoximation by kernels

Corollary
1
showed that one may bound the errors in the Schmüdgen SDP hierarchy (
6
) in terms
of suitable
1
-norm approximations of the objective function. In this section we therefore discuss a systematic approach to
1
-norm approximation of polynomials via positive kernels. This is a classical approach in approximation theory, see e.g.
[
1
]
, and the survey
[
29
]
.

We now consider a kernel
K_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

K_{r}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}):=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{r}}\lambda_{\alpha}\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{y}),

for given constants
\lambda_{\alpha}\in[0,1]
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{r}
.
The convolution operator:

\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)(\textbf{x}):=\int_{\mathbf{K}}f(\textbf{y})K_{r}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mu(\textbf{y}),

(18)

maps any integrable function
f
to a polynomial of degree at most
r
. More precisely,

\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)(\textbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{r}}b_{\alpha}\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})\,,\mbox{ where }b_{\alpha}=\langle\hat{T}_{\alpha},f\rangle_{\mu}\,\lambda_{\alpha}.

The coefficients
\lambda_{\alpha}
of the kernel
K_{r}
determine the approximation.
In particular, if
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
is a polynomial of degree
d\leq r
, then

\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)(\textbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}\lambda_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})\,,

so that the operator
\mathcal{K}^{(r)}
is a projection if all
\lambda_{\alpha}=1
(the so-called Dirichlet or Christoffel-Darboux kernel, see e.g.
[
15
]
).

If
\lambda_{\bf 0}=1
, then the convolution operator preserves constant functions. We will assume this property throughout.

Also note that, if
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}f_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
, and
r\geq d
,

\|\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)-f\|_{1,T}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}|1-\lambda_{\alpha}||f_{\alpha}|\leq\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}|1-\lambda_{\alpha}|\cdot\|f\|_{1,T}.

We are interested in positive kernels that map nonnegative functions on
\mathbf{K}=[-1,1]^{n}
to nonnegative functions, i.e. positive linear
approximation operators in the sense of Korovkin; see e.g.
[
1
]
.
More precisely,
for given degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we are interested in kernels where the coefficients
\lambda_{\alpha}
are such that

\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)(\textbf{x}):=\int_{\mathbf{K}}f(\textbf{y})K_{r}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mu(\textbf{y})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}\quad\forall\mbox{ $f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}$ nonnegative on $\mathbf{K}$.}

(19)

Note that the Dirichlet kernel is not positive; see e.g.
[
29
]
. However, we would like the coefficients
\lambda_{\alpha}
as close to

1
as possible, to approximate the Dirichlet kernel, since this kernel gives
a spectral rate of convergence for analytic functions; see e.g.
[
28
]
.

This leads us naturally to the following abstract optimization problem, that aims to construct the best kernel that meets our requirements.

\Theta^{r}_{n,d}:=\inf\left\{\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}|1-\lambda_{\alpha}|\;:\;K_{r}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=1+\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{r}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\lambda_{\alpha}\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{x})\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\textbf{y})\mbox{ satisfies }\eqref{kernel_preorder_condition}\right\}.

(20)

One may bound the optimal value
\hat{f}_{(r)}
of the problem (
6
) in terms of
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
as follows.

Theorem 9
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
and
f_{\min}=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{K}}f(\mathbf{x})
.
One has, for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq d
,

f-(f_{\min}-\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}

which implies
f_{\min}-\hat{f}_{(r)}\leq\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T}
, where
\hat{f}_{(r)}
is the SDP bound from (
6
).

Proof.

If
\lambda_{\alpha}
(
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{r}
) denotes
a feasible solution of problem (
20
), one has

\|\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)-f\|_{1,T}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}|1-\lambda_{\alpha}||f_{\alpha}|\leq\max_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{r}}|1-\lambda_{\alpha}|\|f\|_{1,T}.

Also, with reference to (
15
), one has:

\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f-f_{\min})-(f-f_{\min})=\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)-f_{\min}-(f-f_{\min})=\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f)-f,

since
\mathcal{K}^{(r)}
preserves constant functions.
Finally, by assumption one has
\mathcal{K}^{(r)}(f-f_{\min})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\ldots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}
, so that the required result follows from
(
15
).
∎

Some comments on the theorem:

•

The values
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
do not depend on
f
, and may be upper-bounded a priori via SDP, as we will show later. Thus the right-hand-side
of the inequality

f_{\min}-\hat{f}_{(r)}\leq\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T}
may readily be upper bounded, given a table of SDP upper bounds on the values
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
.

•

We will show in the next section that
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}=O(1/r^{2})
by considering a specific kernel that is feasible for problem (
20
). This is essentially the same
argument used by Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
. In particular, it suffices to use products of so-called
univariate Jackson kernels
.

6
Kernels of the Jackson type

For
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider the univariate kernel
K^{\rm ja}_{r}:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by:

K^{\rm ja}_{r}(x,y):=1+\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}^{r}\hat{T}_{k}(x)\hat{T}_{k}(y),

(21)

where the coefficients
\lambda_{k}^{r}
are given by

\lambda_{k}^{r}=\frac{1}{r+2}\big((r+2-k)\cos(k\theta_{r})+\frac{\sin(k\theta_{r})}{\sin(\theta_{r})}\cos(\theta_{r})\big)\quad(1\leq k\leq r),

\theta_{r}=\frac{\pi}{r+2}
.

This kernel is called the
univariate Jackson kernel
in
[

]
and
[
17
]
. In the approximation theory literature there is another object which is known by that name,
that corresponds to a kernel in the original analysis of Jackson

8

9

, where he showed that the famous approximation
bounds by Bernstein
[
6

were tight.
In
[
10

minimum resolution kernel

since it minimizes the resolution over all positive univariate kernels of a given degree, where the resolution is defined by

\sigma_{r}:=\left(\int_{[-1,1]\times[-1,1]}({x}-{y})^{2}K_{r}({x},{y})\mathrm{d}\mu({x})\mathrm{d}\mu({y})\right)^{1/2}.

In this paper we will also speak about the univariate Jackson kernel, with the caveat that the term is ambiguous. The interested reader may
consult
[
10
]
for more details on the history and terminology.

The following properties of the univariate Jackson kernel were shown in
[
17
]

[
29
]
and the references therein.

Proposition 1

(Proposition 6 in Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
)
.

For every

we have:

(i)

for all

(ii)

1\geq\lambda_{k}^{r}>0

|1-\lambda_{k}^{r}|=1-\lambda_{k}^{r}\leq{\pi^{2}k^{2}\over(r+2)^{2}}
for all
1\leq k\leq r
.

One may obtain a multivariate kernel by multiplying univariate Jackson kernels in the obvious way:

K_{nr}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}K^{\rm ja}_{r}(x_{i},y_{i}),

(23)

where
K^{\rm ja}_{r}
is the univariate Jackson kernel from
(

)
.
The coefficients of this kernel are given by

\lambda_{\alpha}^{r}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{\alpha_{i}}^{r}\quad(\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{r}).

The following is a refinement of a lemma by Laurent and Slot
[
17

Lemma 3
.

Let
\pi d<r+2
. For any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}
we have

|1-\lambda^{r}_{\alpha}|\leq\tfrac{\pi^{2}d^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}
.

Proof.

From Proposition
1

1\geq\lambda^{r}_{k}\geq 1-\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}\geq 0
. Then,

1\geq\lambda^{r}_{\alpha}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\tfrac{\pi^{2}(\alpha_{i})^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}\right)\geq 1-\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i})^{2}\geq 1-\tfrac{\pi^{2}d^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}},

where the third inequality is the generalised Bernoulli inequality:

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-y_{i}\right)\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\quad\mbox{ if $y_{i}<1\;\forall i\in[n]$}.

Note that the condition
y_{i}<1

in the statement of the lemma,
since
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}

∎

We obtain the following upper bound on

Proposition 2

\Theta^{rn}_{n,d}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}}{(r+2)^{2}}

Proof.

The result follows from the definition of

This indeed holds, as is shown in

∎

7.1
Univariate case

First, we work out the univariate case.

Lemma 4
.

For any
k\geq 0
, and
x,y\in[-1,1]
, one has

T_{k}(x)T_{k}(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(T_{k}(xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})+T_{k}(xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})\right).

Proof.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in[-1,1]
. Then there are
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi]
such that
x_{i}=\cos(\theta_{i})
. Then
\sin(\theta_{i})\geq 0
and thus
\sin(\theta_{i})=\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta_{i})^{2}}=\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}
. Thus

x_{1}x_{2}\pm\sqrt{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}=\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\pm\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}=\cos(\theta_{1}\mp\theta_{2}).

Using
T_{k}(\cos(\theta))=\cos(k\theta)
we have

\displaystyle T_{k}\left(x_{1}x_{2}+\sqrt{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}\right)+T_{k}\left(x_{1}x_{2}-\sqrt{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{k}(\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}))+T_{k}(\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(k\theta_{1}-k\theta_{2})+\cos(k\theta_{1}+k\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(k\theta_{1})\cos(k\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{k}(x_{1})T_{k}(x_{2}).

∎

p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]

p(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}p_{k}T_{k}(t)

K_{p}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}p_{k}T_{k}(x)T_{k}(y)

K(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}T_{k}(x)T_{k}(y)

p_{K}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}T_{k}(t)
,
since

for all
k
.

We have that
|xy\pm\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}|\leq 1
when
|x|,|y|\leq 1
. Thus,
p
is nonnegative on

K_{p}
is non-negative on
[-1,1]^{2}
. Now,
p

p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm t)_{\deg p}
,
by Theorem
4
. Next, we show that we also have

To this end, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let

1.

K_{q(t)^{2}}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x;1\pm y)_{2d,2d}

.

3.

K_{(1-t^{2})q(t)^{2}}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x;1\pm y)_{2d+2,2d+2}
.

Proof.

First notice that by expanding and collecting terms, we can write

q(xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})=q_{0}(x,y)+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{1}(x,y)

and

q(xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})=q_{0}(x,y)-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{1}(x,y)

with
q_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{d,d}
and
q_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{d-1,d-1}
.

We then have

q(xy\pm\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}=q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}\pm 2\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{0}q_{1}.

(25)

From (
25
)

K_{q^{2}}=q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}
follows, proving 1. Now, to prove 2, we write

\displaystyle 2K_{(1-t)q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle:=(1-xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})q(xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})q(xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}+2\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{0}q_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}-2\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{0}q_{1}),

where the second equality follows from (
25
).

Using
(a-b)(c+d)+(a+b)(c-d)=2ac-2bd
we obtain,

\displaystyle 2K_{(1-t)q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=2(1-xy)(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2})-4(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{0}q_{1}.

Let
A=(1-x)(1+y)
and
B=(1+x)(1-y)
. We have then
A+B=2(1-xy)
and
AB=(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle 2K_{(1-t)q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(A+B)(q_{0}^{2}+ABq_{1}^{2})-4ABq_{0}q_{1}=A(q_{0}-Bq_{1})^{2}+B(q_{0}-Aq_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-x)(1+y)(q_{0}-(1+x)(1-y)q_{1})^{2}+(1+x)(1-y)(q_{0}-(1-x)(1+y)q_{1})^{2}.

Similarly,

2K_{(1+t)q(t)^{2}}=(1+x)(1+y)(q_{0}+(1-x)(1-y)q_{1})^{2}+(1-x)(1-y)(q_{0}+(1+x)(1+y)q_{1})^{2}.

To prove 3, we write,

\displaystyle 2K_{(1-t^{2})q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle:=\left(1-(xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}\right)q(xy+\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1-(xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}\right)q(xy-\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-(xy)^{2}-(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})-2xy\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdot\left(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}+2\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{0}q_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1-(xy)^{2}-(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})+2xy\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\left(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2}-2\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}q_{0}q_{1}\right),

where we have used (
25
) to obtain the last equality.

Using
(a-b)(c+d)+(a+b)(c-d)=2ac-2bd
again, we obtain,

\displaystyle 2K_{(1-t^{2})q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\left(1-(xy)^{2}-(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})\right)(q_{0}^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{1}^{2})-8xy(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})q_{0}q_{1}.

Now, let
A=(1-x^{2})
and
B=(1-y^{2})
we have then
(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})=AB
and
1-(xy)^{2}=AB+x^{2}B+y^{2}A
. Therefore,

\displaystyle K_{(1-t^{2})q(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(x^{2}B+y^{2}A)(q_{0}^{2}+ABq_{1}^{2})-4xyABq_{0}q_{1}

\displaystyle=B(xq_{0}-yAq_{1})^{2}+A(yq_{0}-xBq_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-y^{2})(xq_{0}-y(1-x^{2})q_{1})^{2}+(1-x^{2})(yq_{0}-x(1-y^{2})q_{1})^{2}.

∎

Now we are ready to prove our main result in this section.

Theorem 12
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm t)_{r}
. Then
K_{p}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x;1\pm y)_{r,r}
.

Proof.

We have
p=\sigma_{0}+(1-t)\sigma_{1}+(1+t)\sigma_{2}+(1-t^{2})\sigma_{3}
where
\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma[t]
and
\deg(\sigma_{0}),\deg(\sigma_{1})+1,\deg(\sigma_{2})+1,\deg(\sigma_{3})+2\leq r
. By the linearity of the operator
p\mapsto K_{p}
and Lemma
5
, we obtain
K_{p}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x,1\pm y)_{r,r}
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Let
K(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}c_{k}T_{k}(x)T_{k}(y)
be a nonnegative kernel. Then
K\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x,1\pm y)_{r,r}
.

Proof.

Let
p(t)=K(t,1)
. We have then
K=K_{p}
and
p
is nonnegative on
[-1,1]
.
By Theorem
4
, we have

p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm t)_{r}
, and by Theorem
12
the statement follows.
∎

7.2
Multivariate case

The natural question is whether the results from the previous section translate to the general case. Given an
n
-variate polynomial

p\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u}]
, it is natural to define

K_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum p(x_{1}y_{1}\pm\sqrt{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-y_{1}^{2})},\dots,x_{n}y_{n}\pm\sqrt{(1-x_{n}^{2})(1-y_{n}^{2})}),

where the summation is over all the possible
2^{n}
sign combinations.

As in the univariate case, from Lemma
4
we obtain that if
p(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{u})
then

K_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})
. Moreover, every kernel is of this form,
as, given a kernel
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})
, we have
K=K_{p}
for

p(\mathbf{u})=K(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{1})
, where
\mathbf{1}
denotes the all-ones vector.

We have then,
p
nonnegative on
[-1,1]^{n}
if and only if
K_{p}
non-negative on
[-1,1]^{2n}
.
Next we prove
p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm u_{1},\dots,1\pm u_{n})_{r}
implies

K_{p}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},\dots,1\pm y_{n})_{r,r}.
The proof is basically the same as
in the univariate case but requires induction.
To do this, for each
i=1,\dots,n
we
define the operator
\kappa^{i}:{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]\to{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
by

\displaystyle p(u_{1},\dots,u_{i},\dots,u_{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\mapsto\kappa^{i}(p)(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\qquad:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(p(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1},x_{i}y_{i}+\sqrt{(1-x_{i}^{2})(1-y_{i}^{2})},u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.p(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1},x_{i}y_{i}-\sqrt{(1-x_{i}^{2})(1-y_{i}^{2})},u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

Now, given
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u}]
define
P^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
for
i=0,\dots,n
by

P^{0}=p
, and
P^{i}=\kappa^{i}(P^{i-1})
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Notice that in the
i
-th step the variable
u_{i}
is eliminated and the variables
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
introduced. Moreover,
we obtain that
K_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=P^{n}
.
Now we rewrite the statement of Lemma
5
in a bit more general form.

Lemma 6
.

Let
q\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
.
Then, there are
q_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
and
q_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
such that

q(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1},x_{i}y_{i}\pm\sqrt{(1-x_{i}^{2})(1-y_{i}^{2})},u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=q_{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\pm\sqrt{(1-x_{i}^{2})(1-y_{i}^{2})}q_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(26)

with
\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}(q_{0})\leq\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}(q)
,
\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{y}}(q_{0})\leq\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{y}}(q)
,

\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}(q_{1})\leq\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}(q)-1
, and
\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{y}}(q_{1})\leq\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{y}}(q)-1
.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\kappa^{i}(q^{2})

\displaystyle=q_{0}^{2}+(1-x_{i}^{2})(1-y_{i}^{2})q_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle 2\kappa^{i}((1-u_{i})q^{2})

\displaystyle=(1-x_{i})(1+y_{i})(q_{0}-(1+x_{i})(1-y_{i})q_{1})^{2}+(1+x_{i})(1-y_{i})(q_{0}-(1-x_{i})(1+y_{i})q_{1})^{2},

\displaystyle 2\kappa^{i}((1+u_{i})q^{2})

\displaystyle=(1+x_{i})(1+y_{i})(q_{0}+(1-x_{i})(1-y_{i})q_{1})^{2}+(1-x_{i})(1-y_{i})(q_{0}+(1+x_{i})(1+y_{i})q_{1})^{2},

\displaystyle\kappa^{i}((1-u_{i}^{2})q^{2})

\displaystyle=(1-y_{i}^{2})(x_{i}q_{0}-y_{i}(1-x_{i}^{2})q_{1})^{2}+(1-x_{i}^{2})(y_{i}q_{0}-x_{i}(1-y_{i}^{2})q_{1})^{2}.

Proof.

First notice that by expanding and collecting terms, we obtain
q_{0}
and
q_{1}
satisfying all the degree requirements.

The rest of the statement is proven following the proof of Lemma
5
line by line after equation (
25
), but only using equation (
26
) instead of (
25
).
∎

Now we can prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 4
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u}]
be given. Then,

p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm u_{1},\dots,1\pm u_{n})_{r}

if and only if

K_{p}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},1\pm y_{n})_{r,r}
.

Proof.

Assume
K_{p}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},1\pm y_{n})_{r,r}
, then as
p(\mathbf{u})=K(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{1})
we obtain
p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm u_{1},\dots,1\pm u_{n})_{r}
.
Now, we prove the other direction. Assume
p\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm u_{1},\dots,1\pm u_{n})_{r}
is given.

Define
P^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
for
i=0,\dots,n
by
P^{0}=p
, and
P^{i}=\kappa^{i}(P^{i-1})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. We obtain then that
K_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=P^{n}
.

Write

P^{0}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=p(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{J^{-},J^{+}\subseteq[n]}\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+}}(\mathbf{u})\prod_{j\in J^{-}}(1-u_{j})\prod_{j\in J^{+}}(1+u_{j}),

where, for all
J^{-},J^{+}\subseteq[n]
,
\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+}}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{u}]
, and

|J^{-}|+|J^{+}|+\mbox{deg}\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+}}\leq r.

Notice that for all
i\in[n]
and
q_{1},q_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
such that
u_{i}
does not appear in
q_{2}
(that is
\deg_{u_{i}}(q_{2})=0
), we have
from the definition of
\kappa^{i}
, that
\kappa^{i}(q_{1}q_{2})=q_{2}\kappa^{i}(q_{1})
. Thus by linearity of
\kappa^{i}
and Lemma
6
we obtain by induction,

P^{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J^{-},J^{+}\subseteq[n]\setminus[i]\\
I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+}\subseteq[i]\\
I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}\subseteq[i]\end{subarray}}\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+},I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+},I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}}\prod_{j\in J^{-}}(1-u_{j})\prod_{j\in J^{+}}(1+u_{j}),\prod_{j\in I^{-}_{x}}(1-x_{j})\prod_{j\in I^{+}_{x}}(1+x_{j})\prod_{j\in I^{-}_{y}}(1-y_{j})\prod_{j\in I^{+}_{y}}(1+y_{j}),

where, for all
J^{-},J^{+}\subseteq[n]\setminus[i]
, all
I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+}\subseteq[i]
and all
I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}\subseteq[i]
we have
\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+},I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+},I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}}\in\Sigma[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]
, and

|J^{-}|+|J^{+}|+|I_{x}^{-}|+|I_{x}^{+}|+\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+},I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+},I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}}\leq r
and
|J^{-}|+|J^{+}|+|I_{y}^{-}|+|I_{y}^{+}|+\deg_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{y}}\sigma_{J^{-},J^{+},I_{x}^{-},I_{x}^{+},I_{y}^{-},I_{y}^{+}}\leq r
.

In particular,
K_{p}=P^{n}\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},1\pm y_{n})_{r,r}
.

∎

We now conclude the section with a proof of Theorem
10
.

Proof of Theorem
10

Proof.

Assume that we have a feasible solution of problem (
24
), i.e. we have a polynomial in the truncated pre-ordering, say

\mathbf{x}\mapsto 1+\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}2^{\omega(\alpha)}p_{{\alpha}}T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n})_{r}.

By Proposition
4
, we then have that

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto 1+\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}p_{{\alpha}}\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\hat{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{T}(1\pm x_{1},\dots,1\pm x_{n};1\pm y_{1},\dots,1\pm y_{n})_{r,r}.

By Proposition
3
, we have that this kernel satisfies property (
19
).
Thus this kernel is feasible for the problem (
20
). The required result follows.

8
Concluding remarks

Our main results may best be framed in terms of error bounds for the SDP hierarchy (
6
).
In particular, we have shown the following sequence of inequalities:

\displaystyle f_{\min}-\hat{f}_{(r)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Theta^{r}_{n,d}\|f\|_{1,T}\quad\mbox{(by Theorem \ref{thm:error_bound1})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}n^{2}}{r^{2}}\|f\|_{1,T}\quad\mbox{(by Proposition \ref{prop:Theta_bound_nr} if $n|r$ and $r\geq\pi dn$)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}n^{2}}{r^{2}}\|f\|_{1}\quad\mbox{(by \eqref{one_norm_conversion})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}n^{2}}{r^{2}}{n+d\choose d}^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{d}\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\quad\mbox{(by Bohnenblust-Hille ineq.\ \eqref{Bohnenblust-Hille})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}d^{2}n^{2}}{r^{2}}{n+d\choose d}^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{d}(f_{\max}-f_{\min})\quad\mbox{(w.l.o.g.\ if $f(0)=0$)}.

We may readily compare the outermost inequality to the result by Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
, as reproduced in Theorem
3
,
to see that we obtain a very similar result. The added value of our analysis is therefore in the first inequality, since,
for special classes of polynomials,
\|f\|_{1,T}
may relate more favorably to
\|f\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
than in the general case.

We should mention that this is not the first time where changing or comparing norms gives new insights in polynomial optimization. For example, in
[
5
]

the authors improve the known dependence of the general Putinar’s theorem
[
22
]
on the number of variables, by switching from the supremum norm to the
1
-norm in the Bernstein basis.

The object
\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
is of independent interest, as it corresponds to families of positive approximation operators, and may be upper bounded
by an SDP as in
Theorem
10
. A key question is if one could find an analytical expression for (an upper bound on)

\Theta^{r}_{n,d}
in terms of
r,n,d
in a way that does not involve the product of univariate (Jackson) kernels.

Our research links two, seemingly different, topics:

1.

Deriving error bounds for SDP hierarchies for polynomial optimization, in the spirit of
[
17
]
and
[
3
]
.

2.

Constructing optimal multivariate approximation kernels using SDP, in the spirit of
[
10
]
.

In particular, we showed how kernels that are optimized for
1
-norm polynomial approximation on the hypercube yield error bounds
for SDP hierarchies of the Schmüdgen type.

A natural question is if our analysis may be extended to SDP hierarchies that use the Putinar Positivstellensatz
[
22
]
instead of the one by Schmüdgen.
This is of theoretical as well as computational interest, since the famous Lasserre SDP hierachy
[
11
]
for polynomial optimization is based on the Putinar Positivstensatz.
In particular, Baldi and Slot
[
4
]
have recently extended the results by Laurent and Slot
[
17
]
to obtain the best-known effective version of the Putinar Positivstennsatz. It would be interesting to investigate if our approach allows a similar extension.
The main obstacle is that our key technical result, namely Corollary
1
, only applies to pre-orderings, and not to quadratic modules.
