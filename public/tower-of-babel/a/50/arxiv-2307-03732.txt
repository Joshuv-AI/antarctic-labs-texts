Clustering and Cliques in P.A random graphs with edge insertion
Caio Alves 1, Rodrigo Ribeiro 2, Rémy Sanchis 3

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the global clustering coefficient (a.k.a transitivity) and clique number of graphs generated by a preferential attachment random graph model with an additional feature of allowing edge connections between existing vertices. Specifically, at each time step t t , either a new vertex is added with probability f ⁡ ( t ) f(t) , or an edge is added between two existing vertices with probability 1 − f ⁡ ( t ) 1-f(t) . We establish concentration inequalities for the global clustering and clique number of the resulting graphs under the assumption that f ⁡ ( t ) f(t) is a regularly varying function at infinity with index of regular variation − γ -\gamma , where γ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \gamma\in[0,1) . We also demonstrate an inverse relation between these two statistics: the clique number is essentially the reciprocal of the global clustering coefficient. Keywords : complex networks; cliques; preferential attachment; concentration bounds; diameter; scale-free; small-world MSC 2010 subject classifications. Primary 05C82; Secondary 60K40, 68R10



1.
Introduction

Analyzing graph observables is crucial to understanding the topology and evolution of networks. These observable properties, including but not limited to node degree distribution, clustering coefficient, and clique number, provide invaluable insights into the network’s overall structure and connectivity patterns.
The global clustering coefficient captures the density of triangles in the network and can reveal how nodes tend to form tightly-knit clusters. Whereas, the clique number, which is the size of the largest complete subgraph, can be thought of as a measure of how tightly connected the vertices in the graph are.

The investigation and usage of the clustering coefficient and clique number are an interdisciplinary field that have appeared in a wide range of scientific papers. Articles from various fields have analyzed these properties of networks due to the fact that they provide insights into the underlying mechanisms governing network formation and evolution. In more theoretical fields, the study of these properties has led to the development of new graph theory concepts, see
[
16
,
19
]
for a few examples of recent generalizations of clustering coefficients. In the field of chemistry, in
[
21
]
the authors employ cliques to describe chemicals within a chemical database that display a notable level of similarity to a target structure. In sociology, the study of these properties has helped to understand the formation of social networks and the spread of information within them, see
[
17
,
20
]
. Therefore, investigating the clustering coefficient and clique number is a crucial step towards developing a comprehensive understanding of network dynamics, and the interdisciplinary nature of these quantities underscores their significance.

In the classical preferential attachment model where new arriving vertices connect to
m
neighbors
[
7
]
, the clique number is at most
m
, and the clustering coefficient tends to
0
as the inverse of the number of vertices in the graph
[
11
]
. In this paper, we focus on studying these two crucial statistics in graphs generated by a variation of this model, where new edges between existing vertices can be added at any time. We investigate the impact of this modified rule on these statistics.

To facilitate our analysis and present our results clearly, we will first introduce a formal definition of the model in the following subsection.

1.1.
The model

Firstly, let us introduce a new notation. Given a finite connected (multi)graph
G
, let
\pi_{G}
be the following distribution over the vertices of
G
defined as follows

(1.1)

\pi_{G}(v):=\frac{{\rm degree}(u)}{\sum_{w\in G}{\rm degree}(w)}.

Our model generates a sequence of (multi)graphs in an inductive way, that is, we obtain the
t
-th graph by performing a graph operation on the
(t-1)
th graph. In our settings, there exist two possible ways of modifying a given graph
G
:

•

Vertex-operation
. Add a
new
vertex
v
to
G
and connect it to a vertex
u\in G
selected accordingly to
\pi_{G}
;

•

Edge-operation
. Add a new edge connecting two existing vertices of
G
,
u
and
v
, both selected independently and accordingly to
\pi_{G}
.

We highlight that in edge-operation,
u
and
v
might be the same vertex, in that case a loop is added to
v
, or they might be already connected in
G
, in which case a multiple edge is created between then.

The parameter of the model is a real function
f:(0,\infty)\to[0,1]
. For a fixed function
f
, we let
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be an independent sequence of random variables such that
Z_{t}\sim{\rm Ber}(f(t))
. We start with an initial graph
G_{0}
and then we generate the model inductively by obtaining
G_{t+1}
from
G_{t}
as follows:

•

Vertex-step.
If
Z_{t+1}=1
, we perform a
vertex-operation
on
G_{t}
and set the resulting graph to be
G_{t+1}
.

•

Edge-step.
If
Z_{t+1}=0
, we perform an
edge-operation
instead.

In words, the model decides according to a coin, with a possibly time-dependent bias, which operation it is going to perform on the current graph in order to generate the next one.

The model here investigated was originally introduced in
[
2
]
and, by choosing
f(t)\equiv 1
, one obtains the well-known Preferential Attachment (PA) random graph proposed by A-L. Barabási and R. Albert in
[
7
]
with parameter
m=1
. The case
f(t)\equiv p\in[0,1]
is a particular case of the model introduced by C. Cooper and A. Frieze in
[
14
]
and also investigated by F. Chung and L. Lu in
[
13
]
.

For the sake of readability and to avoid clutter we will make some choices notation-wise. Throughout the paper, we will use letters
f,g
and
h
to denote functions for which
G_{t}(f)
makes sense, that is,
f,g,h:(0,\infty)\to[0,1]
. We will also simply write
G_{t}
for the
t
-th graph generated by the above dynamics, whenever the function
f
is fixed and there is no risk of confusion. Alternatively, we will write
G_{t}(f)
to highlight the dependence on
f
when needed.

We will also set
G_{0}
to be the multigraph on one vertex and a self-loop attached to it. The reader will notice that the initial graph plays no important role in our results as long as it is finite.
And we will write
|G|
as a shorthand for the cardinality of the vertex set of
G
. In this paper we investigate the aforementioned model when
f
belongs to the family of regularly varying functions. We say a function
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a
regularly varying function at infinity
if there exists a real number
\gamma
such that for all
a>0
the following identity is satisfied

(1.2)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(at)}{f(t)}=a^{\gamma}.

The constant
\gamma
is called the
index of regular variation
. Sometimes, when
\gamma=0
, we may call the function
f

slowly varying
.

1.2.
Main results: Statements and Discussion

In this part we will state and discuss our main results. The results concern concentration inequalities for the clique number and global clustering coefficients. Our results are particularly interesting as they provide a characterization of such statistics in terms of the index of regular variation when
f
is taken as a regularly varying function. We also prove a continuity theorem, meaning that under a suitable notion of convergence for the function
f
, we have convergence in total variation distance of the respective processes.

1.2.1.
Cliques

A complete subgraph is a subgraph in which every pair of vertices is adjacent to each other. The clique number of a graph is the size of the largest complete subgraph (clique) that can be found in the graph. Again for the sake of readability, we will denote the clique number of
G_{t}(f)
simply by
\omega_{t}
whenever
f
is fixed or understood from the context.

The most straightforward problem about clique number concerns finding its order of magnitude. In
[
9
]
, K. Bogerd, R. M. Castro and R. van der Hofstad, prove a concentration result for the clique number in rank-1 random graphs. Another interesting problem is the one of detecting artificially planted cliques, that is, by observing realizations of the model, decides whether it contains a clique that has been artificially added to the graph. This type of problem is investigated by K. Bogerd, R. M. Castro, R. van der Hofstad and N. Verzelen in
[
10
]
.

In our context, we establish two results involving the clique number of
G_{t}(f)
. Firstly, we have proved a general lower bound for the clique number of
G_{t}
that holds under the mild assumption that
f
decreases to zero.

Theorem 1

(Existence of Large cliques)
.

If
f
decreases to zero, then for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on
\delta
and
f
only such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\omega_{t}\geq\frac{c_{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|G_{t^{(1-\delta)/2}}|\right]}{\log t}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{\log t}-\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|G_{t^{(1-\delta)/2}\log t}|\right]}{4\log t}\right\},

The above theorem yields an interesting result: for certain regimes of the model, it states that
G_{t}(f)
contains a clique of order approximately
\sqrt{|G_{t}(f)|}
. For example, consider the case where
f(t)=t^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
. This gives us
{\mathbb{E}}\left[|G_{t}(f)|\right]\approx t^{1-\gamma}
. Thus, by Theorem
1
we obtain, with high probability, a clique of order at least
t^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}
which is approximately
\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[|G_{t}(f)|\right]\right)^{(1-\delta)/2}
.

As our second main result, we have also provided a comprehensive characterization of this statistic along with the global clustering coefficient under the additional assumption of
f
being regularly varying. In other words, we establish that for the vast and important class of regularly varying functions at infinity the lower bound given by Theorem
1
is sharp up to factors of order
t^{\delta}
for any
\delta
. This is our next result.

Theorem 2

(Clique number for RV functions)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depending on
\delta
only such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(c_{1}t^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}\leq\omega_{t}\leq c_{2}t^{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}\right)\geq 1-\frac{c_{3}}{\log t}.

From a computational perspective, the problem of finding cliques in a graph are not only too time-consuming, but some are NP-complete. In our case, as a consequence of our approach, the clique given by Theorem
2
is likely to be formed by the
t^{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}
first vertices added by the process.

By Theorem
2
and properties of the random sequence
\{\omega_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
we prove a convergence result

Corollary 1.3

(Convergence of clique number)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\omega_{t}}{\log t}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2},\;\text{almost surely.}

In
[
5
]
the authors prove the convergence of the clique in the
\log
scale for
f(t)\equiv p
, with
p\in[0,1]
. Their result states that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\omega_{t}}{\log t}=\frac{1-p}{2-p},\;\text{almost surely.}

Then Corollary
1.3
can be seen as some sort of limit of the above convergence when the parameter
p
goes to zero. From this perspective, if the parameter
p
goes to zero as a slowly varying function, then one obtains the expected
\frac{1}{2}
as the limit. However, if
p
goes too fast to zero, then a limit smaller than
\frac{1}{2}
is obtained.

1.2.2.
Clustering coefficient

The global clustering coefficient is a statistic that measures the extent to which vertices in a graph tend to cluster together. It is defined as the ratio of the number of triangles (complete subgraphs of size three) in the graph to the total number of connected triples of vertices (a.k.a cherries).

Due to the fact that in our settings
G_{t}(f)
might be a multigraph with multiple edges or self-loops, we will compute the global clustering for the
simplification
of
G_{t}(f)
, that is, given
G_{t}(f)
, we obtain a simple graph
\widehat{G}_{t}(f)
obtained from
G_{t}(f)
by removing multiple edges and loops.

Given a simple graph
H
we will let
\Delta(H)
be the number of triangles in
H
. Whereas,
\Lambda(H)
denotes the number of cherries (paths of length two) in
H
. In the case of a multigraph
G
, we will simply write
\Delta(G)
and
\Lambda(G)
meaning
\Delta(\widehat{G})
and
\Lambda(\widehat{G})
, respectively. That is,
\Delta(G)
and
\Lambda(G)
count the number of triangles and cherries respectively without multiplicities. Then, we define the global clustering coefficient of
G_{t}(f)
as

c^{\rm glob}_{t}=c^{\rm glob}(G_{t}(f)):=3\times\frac{\Delta(G_{t}(f))}{\Lambda(G_{t}(f))}.

B. Bollobás and O. Riordan studied the BA-model with
m\geq 2
in
[
11
]
. In this model, a new vertex with
m
randomly chosen neighbors is added to the graph at each step. The authors proved that
\mathbb{E}[c^{\rm glob}_{t}]
decreases at the rate of
\log^{2}(t)/t
, and the expected number of triangles at time
t
is roughly
\log^{3}(t)
. N. Eggemann and S. D. Noble studied a variation of this model, called the affine version, where vertices are selected based on their degree and a constant of attractiveness
\delta
in
[
15
]
. They proved that, for any positive
\delta
, the expected number of triangles is of order
\log^{2}(t)
, and
\mathbb{E}[c^{\rm glob}_{t}]
decays as
\log(t)/t
.

In
[
18
]
, E. Jacob and P. Mörters investigated the global clustering for a different model which also takes into account spatial proximity of vertices to create connections. Their study found that
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
converges in probability to a constant whose positivity depends on the choice of parameters. In
[
23
]
, C. Stegehuis, R. van der Hofstad and J. Leeuwaarden, also investigate clustering for random graph models with latent geometry.

In our study, we establish a concentration inequality result for
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
that shows that in our case,
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
decays polynomialy fast and the exponent depends on the index of regular variation of
f
. Additionaly, we show an almost surely convergence of
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
in the right scale.

Theorem 3

(Clustering coefficient for RV functions)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on
\delta
only such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(c_{1}t^{-(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}\leq c^{\rm glob}_{t}\leq c_{2}t^{-(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log t}.

The bounds and rates given by the above theorem are good enough to show the convergence of the clustering coefficient at the
\log
scale.

Corollary 1.4

(Convergence of clustering coefficient)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log c^{\rm glob}_{t}}{\log t}=-\frac{1-\gamma}{2},\;\text{almost surely.}

In
[
5
]
the authors prove that for
f(t)\equiv p
, for
p\in[0,1]
, the following limit holds

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log c^{\rm glob}(G_{t}(f))}{\log t}=\frac{3(1-p)}{2-p}-2+p,\;\text{almost surely.}

Similarly to the case of the clique number, the case in which
f
is a slowly varying function that decreases to zero can be seen as the limit case when
p
goes to zero.

Observe that Theorem
3
combined with Theorem
2
shows a surprisingly intriguing reciprocal relation between global clustering and clique number. We state this relation in the result below.

Corollary 1.5

(Inverse Relation)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on
\delta
only such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{\omega(G_{t}(f))^{1+\delta}}\leq c^{\rm glob}(G_{t}(f))\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\omega(G_{t}(f))^{1-\delta}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log t}.

1.2.3.
Comparing
G_{t}(f)
and
G_{t}(h)

If two edge-step functions are “close” to each other in some sense, then one should expect that the random processes they generate should be “close” as well. In order to formalize this intuition, we have developed a grand coupling mechanism that enables us to generate all processes
\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t}
, for any
f
, from the same source of randomness. The coupling allows us to compare the statistical properties of
G_{t}(f)
and
G_{t}(h)
by simply comparing the functions
f
and
h
. This can be seen as a
Transfer Principle
result that allows us to transfer results for
G_{t}(f)
to
G_{t}(h)
under some assumptions on the relation between
f
and
h
.

The idea that
G_{t}(f)
is close to
G_{t}(h)
whenever
f
and
h
are close is formalized in the theorem below, which is a consequence of our Grand Coupling introduced at Section
2
. In words, if
f
and
h
are close in the
L_{1}(\mathbb{N})
-norm (denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{1}
), then
\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1})
is close to
\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(h)\}_{t\geq 1})
in the
total variation distance
, denoted by
\mathrm{dist}_{TV}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider any two
f
and
h
. We have

(1.6)

\mathrm{dist}_{TV}\left(\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1}),\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(h)\}_{t\geq 1})\right)\leq\|f-h\|_{1}.

In particular, the following implication holds

(1.7)

f_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L_{1}(\mathbb{N})}}{{\longrightarrow}}f\implies\mathcal{L}((G_{t}(f_{n}))_{t\geq 1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dist}_{TV}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{L}((G_{t}(f))_{t\geq 1}).

The theorem presented above represents an interesting perturbative statement, illuminating the ability to introduce a small amount of noise
\epsilon=\epsilon(t)
into the function
f
, while preserving the integrity of the original process up to a maximum error of
|\epsilon|_{1}
in total variation distance. This result affirms the robustness of the process to external perturbations.

One difficulty in our setup is the degree of generality we work with. Our case replaces the parameter
p\in(0,1]
in the models investigated in
[
1
,
13
,
14
]
by any non-negative real function
f
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. The introduction of such function naturally increases the complexity of any analytical argument one may expect to rely on and makes it harder to discover
threshold
phenomena. On the other hand, the fact that we are working under such large space of parameters is also one of the reasons why results like Theorems
2
and
2
or even the Corollaries
1.3
and
1.4
are surprising, because they depend on a simple functional of the parameter
f
: its index of regular variation at infinity. Under such wide degree of freedom, there is no reason to expect such simple characterizations of statistics to hold true.

1.3.
Main technical ideas

In order to prove Theorem
1
and Theorem
4
, we construct an auxiliary process that we call the
doubly-labeled random tree process
,
\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
. In essence, this process is a realization of the traditional BA-model (obtained in our settings choosing
f\equiv 1
) where each vertex has two labels attached to it. We then show in Proposition
2.1
how to generate
G_{t}(f)
from
\mathcal{T}_{t}
using the information on those labels. This procedure allows us to generate
G_{t}(f)
and
G_{t}(h)
, for two distinct functions
f
and
h
, from exactly the same source of randomness. The upshot is that the parameter function
f
may be seen as a map from the space of doubly-labeled trees to the space of (multi)graphs – therefore it makes sense to use the notation
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
. Furthermore, this map has the crucial property of being
monotonic
(in a way we make precise latter). Roughly speaking, if
f\leq h
, then certain monotonic statistics respect this order, so if
\zeta
is such an statistic, then
\zeta(f(\mathcal{T}_{t}))\leq\zeta(h(\mathcal{T}_{t}))
. In Proposition
3.4
we give important examples of suitable monotonic statistics. The main advantage of our machinery is that it allows us to transpose some results about graphs generated for functions in a particular regime to another just by comparing the functions themselves. This important feature of our approach allows us, for instance, to use known results about cliques when
f
is taken to be a constant less than one to propagate this result down other regimes of functions.

While the coupling approach offers a clear pathway for extending results of one regime of
f
to another, some statistics still demand further work. As for an upper bound for the clique number, we obtain it by controlling the number of triangles. In this approach, we investigate the simplification of
G_{t}
denoted by
\widehat{G}_{t}
. By providing a sharp upper bound on the number of edges of
\widehat{G}_{t}
, we can use deterministic results to bound the number of triangles, which by its turn can provide an upper bound for the order of the largest clique. The upper bound on the number of edges in
\widehat{G}_{t}
requires a sharp upper bound for vertices degree and for the number of edges between two vertices. The upper bound on the number of edges between vertices is obtained using a method inspired by the martingale methods used in the analysis of vertices’ degree in the literature of preferential attachment random graphs.

2.
A Grand Coupling: The doubly-labeled random tree process

In this section we introduce a stochastic process
\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
that provides a grand coupling between the random graphs
\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1}
for every possible choice of
f
.

The process
\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is essentially a realization of the Barabási-Albert random tree with the distinction that each vertex has two labels: an earlier vertex chosen according to the preferential attachment rule and an independent uniform random variable. The label consisting in the earlier vertex can be seen as a “ghost directed edge”, we later use these random labels to collapse subsets of vertices into a single vertex in order to obtain a graph with the same distribution as
G_{t}(f)
for any prescribed function
f
.

We begin our process with a graph
\mathcal{T}_{1}
consisting as usual in a single vertex and a single loop connecting said vertex to itself. We then inductively construct the labeled graph
\mathcal{T}_{t+1}
from
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in the following way:

Figure 1
.
A sample of the process
\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
up to time
6
without the uniform labels. The dashed lines indicate the label
\ell(v_{j})
taken by each vertex
v_{j}
.

(i)

We add to
\mathcal{T}_{t}
a vertex
v_{t+1}
;

(ii)

We tag
v_{t+1}
with a random label
\ell(v_{t+1})
chosen from the set
V(\mathcal{T}_{t})
with the preferential attachment rule, that is, with probability of choosing
u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{t})
proportional to the degree of
u
in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
;

(iii)

Independently from the step above, we add an edge
\{w,v_{t+1}\}
to
E(\mathcal{T}_{t})
where
w\in V(\mathcal{T}_{t})
is also randomly chosen according to the preferential attachment rule, see Figure
2
.

We then finish the construction by tagging each vertex
v_{j}\in V(\mathcal{T}_{t})
with a second label consisting in an independent random variable
U_{j}
with uniform distribution on the interval
[0,1]
, as shown in Figure
2
. We note that only the actual edges contribute to the degree taken in consideration in the preferential attachment rule, the tags are not considered.

We now make precise the notation
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
which indicates that a function
f
may also be seen as a function that maps a doubly-labeled tree to a (multi)graph. Our goal is to define this map in such way that
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G_{t}(f)
.

In order to do so, let us fix such a function
f
. Given
v_{j}\in V(\mathcal{T}_{t})
, we compare
U_{j}
to
f(j)
. If
U_{j}\leq f(j)
, we do nothing. Otherwise, we collapse
v_{j}
onto its label
\ell(v_{j})
, that is, we consider the set
\{v_{j},\ell(v_{j})\}
to be a single vertex with the same labels as
\ell(v_{j})
. We then update the label of all vertices
v
such that
\ell(v)=v_{j}
to
\{v_{j},\ell(v_{j})\}
. This procedure is associative in the sense that the order of the vertices on which we perform this operation does not affect the final resulting graph, as long as we perform it for all the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. We let then
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
be the (multi)graph obtained when this procedure has run over all the
t
vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. We refer to Figure
2
as an illustration of the final outcome.

In the next proposition we prove that
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
is indeed distributed as
G_{t}(f)
.

Figure 2
.
On the left, the graph
\mathcal{T}_{6}
, where each vertex receives an independent uniform random variable label. On the right, how one can sample the distribution of
G_{6}(f)
using the
\mathcal{T}_{6}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{t}
be the doubly-labeled tree above defined. Then,

f(\mathcal{T}_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G_{t}(f).

Proof.

We first observe that the associativity of the collapsing operation and the independence of the sequence
\{U_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
from the previous operations imply that we can glue together the vertex
v_{t+1}
to
\ell(v_{t+1})
whenever
U_{t+1}>f(t+1)
right after we complete step (iii) of the above construction by induction. The resulting graph has either a new vertex
v_{t+1}
with an edge
\{w(v_{t+1}),v_{t+1}\}
or an edge
\{w(v_{t+1}),\ell(v_{t+1})\}
with the exact same probability distribution as the
(t+1)
-th step in the construction of the graph
(G_{t}(f))_{t\geq 1}
. By induction, both random graphs have the same distribution.

3.
Applications of The Grand Coupling

In the light of the Section
2
and Proposition
2.1
, throughout this section, we will tacitly assume that all processes of the form
\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1}
for all possible functions
f
are on the same probability space provided by our previous results.

A straightforward consequence of Proposition
2.1
is Theorem
4
which roughly speaking states that functions close to each other in the
L_{1}(\mathbb{N})
-norm generate essentially the same processes. Once we have the machinery provided by Proposition
2.1
, the proof of this fact becomes a simple application of the union bound.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Fixed two possible functions
f
and
h
, by Proposition
2.1
we have that

(3.1)

\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1})=\mathrm{Law}(\{f(\mathcal{T}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}).

Thus

(3.2)

\begin{split}\mathrm{dist}_{TV}\left(\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 1}),\mathrm{Law}(\{G_{t}(h)\}_{t\geq 1})\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\{f(\mathcal{T}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}\neq\{h(\mathcal{T}_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(U_{i}\in(f(i)\wedge h(i),f(i)\vee h(i))\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|f(i)-g(i)|\\
&=\|f-g\|_{1},\end{split}

and the above equation immediately implies (
1.7
).

3.1.
Monotone Statistics

The next step is to use the coupling provided by the doubly-labeled random tree process to compare statistics of graphs generated by different functions. This method will allow us to transport results we have obtained for a fixed
f
to other edge-step functions by comparing them with
f
. To do this we introduce the notion of
increasing (decreasing)
graph observable.

Definition 1

(Monotone Statistics)
.

We say that a (multi)graph statistic
\zeta
is increasing if, given two edge-step functions
f
and
h
, we have that

(3.3)

f(s)\leq h(s),\forall s\leq t\implies\zeta(f(\mathcal{T}_{t}))\leq\zeta(h(\mathcal{T}_{t}))\;\textit{a.s.}

When the second inequality in (
3.3
) holds with “
\geq
”, we say
\zeta
is decreasing.

A first example of an increasing statistic is the total number of vertices. Indeed, since every vertex
v_{j}
of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
remain preserved under
f
whenever its
U_{j}
label is less than
f(j)
, it follows that every
f
-preserved
v_{j}
is
h
-preserved as well.

The next proposition states that the maximum degree is a decreasing statistic whereas the diameter is an increasing one.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f
and
h
be two functions satisfying the relation
f(s)\leq h(s)
for all
s\in(1,\infty)
. Then,

(a)

the maximum degree is a
decreasing
graph statistic. More precisely, if we let
D_{\textit{max}}(G)
denote the maximum degree of a (multi)graph
G
, then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\;D_{\textit{max}}(G_{t}(f))\geq D_{\textit{max}}(G_{t}(h))\right)=1;

(b)

the diameter is an
increasing
graph statistic. More precisely,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\;\mathrm{diam}(G_{t}(f))\leq\mathrm{diam}(G_{t}(h))\right)=1

Proof.

The proofs follow from an analysis of the action of
f
over
\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

Proof of part (a):

Given a vertex
v\in f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
we let
\mathsf{solid}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(v)
be the set of its neighbors in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
(that is, vertices sharing a solid edge with it, which would come from a vertex-step). We also define the set
\mathsf{dashed}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(v)
as the vertices
v_{s}
in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
with label
\ell
pointing to
v
and whose corresponding uniform label
U_{s}
is larger than
f(s)
. To find the degree
D_{t}(v)
of
v
in
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
, one performs the following computation:

•

initialize
D_{t}(v)
as the cardinality of
\mathsf{solid}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(v)
and the set
\mathsf{to}
-
\mathsf{explore}
as
\mathsf{dashed}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(v)
;

•

sequentially in some arbitrary order, for every
w\in\mathsf{to}
-
\mathsf{explore}
, sum the cardinality of
\mathsf{solid}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(w)
to
D_{t}(v)
and add the vertices of
\mathsf{dashed}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(w)
to
\mathsf{to}
-
\mathsf{explore}
;

•

repeat the above until no more vertices are in
\mathsf{to}
-
\mathsf{explore}
.

In other words, given the directed tree with
v
as the root, and edges coming from labels that become edge-steps after the action of
f
, we perform a graph-search algorithm, like breadth-first search, and sum the
\mathcal{T}_{t}
-degree of each queried vertex. Note that each vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
can be accounted for multiple times in this procedure when we sum the degree in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. This algorithm does yield the degree of vertex
v
, as there is a bijection between each half-edge incident to
v
in
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
and the multiset of vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in
\mathsf{solid}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}(w)
as we explore vertices
w\in\mathsf{to}
-
\mathsf{explore}
.

Now we note that if
f(s)\leq h(s)
for all
s
, the
\mathsf{dashed}
-
\mathsf{neighbors}
-directed tree associated to the algorithm that discovers the degree of a vertex
v
in
f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
contains the tree that discovers the degree of the same vertex in
h(\mathcal{T}_{t})
. So if
v_{s}
is the vertex with maximum degree in
h(\mathcal{T}_{t})
, and its associated uniform is larger than
f(s)
, by direct comparison we obtain the result. If not, then this means that
v_{s}
will be collapsed into some vertex
w\in f(\mathcal{T}_{t})
, and the tree from the degree-discovering algorithm for this vertex will contain the tree associated to
v_{s}\in h(\mathcal{T}_{t})
. This finishes the proof of part (a).

Proof of part (b):
Observe that if
u
and
w
are vertices whose
U
-label is less than
f
and such that
u\leftrightarrow w
in
G_{t}(f)
, then
u
and
w
are possibly not connected by a single edge in
G_{t}(h)
, in other words, if
\mathrm{dist}_{G_{t}(f)}(u,w)=1
then
\mathrm{dist}_{G_{t}(h)}(u,w)\geq 1
. Therefore, if
v_{0}=u,v_{1},\cdots,v_{k}=w
is a minimal path in
G_{t}(f)
, by the previous observation, this path induces a path in
G_{t}(h)
whose length is equal to or greater than
k
.

On the other hand, if
u=v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{k}=w
is a path realizing the minimal distance between
u
and
w
, in
G_{t}(h)
we have that this path induces another path in
G_{t}(f)
of same length or less. To see this, just notice that, if
U_{j}>f(j)
and
\ell(v_{j})
points to a vertex outside the path for some
j\in\{2,3,\cdots,k-1\}
, then, after identifying
v_{j}
to its
\ell
-label, this operation does not increase the path length. This is enough to conclude the proof.

Having small diameter, typically
\log
of the number of nodes, is a feature shared by several real-life networks, as observed by S. Strogatz and D. J. Watts in
[
24
]
, where the coined the term ‘small-world’ networks. Thus investigating the order of the diameter is vital for models whose purpose might be modeling real-life networks.

In
[
4
]
, the authors showed a characterization of the diameter of
G_{t}(f)
for regularly varying function
f
in terms of the index of regular variation. The proposition below offers a universal upper bound that guarantees that
G_{t}(f)
is small-world for any choice of
f
.

Proposition 3.5

(Universal Upper bound for the Diameter)
.

There exists a universal constant
c
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathrm{diam}(G_{t}(f))\leq c\log t\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

The proof follows from item (b) of Proposition
3.4
and known results for the classical preferential attachment model. Indeed, in
[
12
]
, B. Bollobás and O. Riordan proved that for
f\equiv 1
the diameter of
G_{t}(f)
is bounded from above by
c\log t
asymptotically almost surely. Since
f
is bounded by
1
, a combination of this result with item (b) of Proposition
3.4
proves the result.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1
: existence of large cliques

In this section we keep exploring the machinery developed in previous sections. Here, we transfer the existence of large cliques using the function itself, i.e., if
h
is such that
G_{t}(h)
has a clique of order
K
and
f
another function satisfying
f(s)\leq h(s)
for all
s\in(1,\infty)
, then the clique existence propagates to
G_{t}(f)
too but with a possibly
f
-dependent order.

We will let
G_{t}(p)
be the graph generated by
f\equiv p\in(0,1)
and we will let
\omega_{t}(p)
be the clique number of
G_{t}(p)
. And for the sake of convenience, we state the result about
\omega_{t}(p)
.

Theorem 5

(Theorem
1
from
[
1
]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and every
p\in(0,1)
, it follows that

(4.1)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\omega_{t}(p)\geq t^{(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1-p}{2-p}}\right)=1-o(1).

We can now use the doubly-labeled tree together with the above results in order to prove Theorem
1
. In order to avoid clutter, let us introduce a new notation for the expected number of vertices,

(4.2)

F(t):={\mathbb{E}}[|G_{t}(f)|]=1+\sum_{s=2}^{t}f(s).

In order to formalize the strategy of transporting a clique in
G_{t}(p)
down to
G_{t}(f)
it will be important to control the time the
j
-th vertex of
G_{t}(p)
is added. In our proof, we essentially condition on the existence of a large clique in
G_{t}(p)
and then using the independence of the
U
-labels we pay a
f
-dependent price to guarantee that part of the cliques’ vertices are also a vertex in
G_{t}(f)
. However, if the vertices in the clique took too long to be added, then the
f
-price we pay to force them to be vertices in
G_{t}(f)
as well is too expensive. This would result in a much smaller clique in
G_{t}(f)
.

Consequently, it will be important to use the fact that for the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 0}
, does not take too much time between vertices addition, since the time interval between the addition of any two vertices follows a geometric distribution of parameter
p
. We can formalize this by defining inductively the following sequence of stopping times, let
\tau_{0}(p)\equiv 0
and define
\tau_{k}(p)
as below

\tau_{k}(p):=\inf\{n\geq\tau_{k-1}(p)\;:\;U_{n}\leq p\}.

Then, noticing that
\tau_{k}(p)-\tau_{k-1}(p)\sim Geo(p)
for any
k>0
we have that

(4.3)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{k}(p)-\tau_{k-1}(p)\geq\frac{10\log t}{\log(1/(1-p))}\right)\leq t^{-10}.

The above bound will give us control over the time vertices have been added by the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 0}
. With the discussed above we prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Given
\delta>0
sufficiently small, We begin by letting
\varepsilon
be small enough and setting
p=\delta/6
so that

\alpha=\alpha(p,\varepsilon):=\frac{1-p}{2-p}(1-\varepsilon)>\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right).

In the proof of Theorem
1
of
[
1
]
one uses Theorem
2
of
[
1
]
to show that for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\alpha)
there exists a fixed integer
m=m(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},p)>0
with the following property: if one divides the set of vertices added by the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 1}
up to time
t
into disjoint subsets of
m
vertices born
consecutively
, then with high probability (at least
1
minus a polynomial function of
t
) from the
t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
-th subset to the
t^{\alpha}/m
-th one, we can select one vertex in each subset in such a way that the subgraph induced by the set of chosen vertices is a complete subgraph of
G_{t}(p)
. These vertices are the
leaders
of their block.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the random set formed by the leader vertices of the
t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
-th to the
t^{\alpha}/m
-th blocks of
m
vertices, which form a clique in
G_{t}(p)
w.h.p. Observe that in the event these vertices do not form a clique or do not exist
t^{\alpha}/m
blocks of
m
vertices the random set
\mathcal{K}
is empty.

Now, let
N
be the following random variable

(4.4)

N:=\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{K}}\mathbb{1}\left\{U_{j}\leq f(j)\right\}.

In words,
N
counts how many leading vertices become a vertex of
G_{t}(f)
as well. Here, we adopt the convention that a sum over an empty set of indexes is zero.

To avoid clutter, let us write
k=t^{\alpha}/m
. Given a set of vertices
S=\{v_{t_{1}},\dots,v_{t_{k}}\}
in
G_{t}(p)
the event

\{\mathcal{K}=S\}

then means the vertices
v_{t_{j}}
, for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
are all vertices of
G_{t}(p)
and they are all connected between themselves in
G_{t}(p)
. Thus, due to independent nature of the
U
-label assignments in the doubly-labeled random tree process the following identify holds

(4.5)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(U_{t_{j}}\leq f(t_{j})\mid\mathcal{K}=S\right)=p^{-1}f(t_{j}).

Additionally, we call
S

good
if

v_{t_{j}}\in S\implies t_{j}\leq c_{1}j\cdot m\cdot\log t,

where
c_{1}=10\log(1/(1-p))
. In words, a good set of vertices in
G_{t}(p)
are those whose vertices did not take too much time to be added by the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Observe that by (
4.3
) and union bound over the number of vertices yields

(4.6)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists S\subset G_{t}(p)\;:\;S\text{ is bad }\right)\leq t^{-9}.

Having the above in mind and letting
m
be the auxiliary
m
that appears in proof of Theorem 1 of
[
1
]
, define the following event

(4.7)

A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,k}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\exists\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{k}\}\subset\{t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\dots,c_{1}m(t^{\alpha}+t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\log t\}\text{ such that }\\
v_{s_{j}}\leftrightarrow v_{s_{i}}\text{ in }G_{t}(p)\text{ for all }i,j\in[k]\end{array}\right\}.

In the context of the doubly labeled tree process, the event
A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,k}
says that we may find a subset of
k
vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, all added between times
t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
and
c_{1}m(t^{\alpha}+t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\log t
, with the property that the
j
-th vertex
v_{t_{j}}
of this subset has its
U_{t_{j}}
-label smaller than
p
. Moreover, when we apply a constant function equals
p
on
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in the event
A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,k}
, all these
k
vertices form a complete graph in the resulting graph
G_{t}(p)
.

Theorem
1
of
[
1
]
states that setting
k=t^{\alpha}/m
, the event
A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,k}
occurs with probability at least
1-t^{-\eta}
, for some positive small
\eta
depending on
p
, which, in our case, is a function of
\delta
. Thus, for
t
large enough, we may simply use that

(4.8)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,t^{\alpha}/m}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{\log t},

this bound is good enough to prove Corollary
1.3
. This is why we are exchanging a polynomial decay by a
\log
one, to get rid off the dependency on
\delta
and consequently simplify latter arguments.

Now, using identify (
4.5
), the definition of good set and the fact that
f
is decreasing, we have that

(4.9)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left[N\;\middle|\;\mathcal{K}=S,S\text{ is good }\right]&=p^{-1}\sum_{v_{t_{j}}\in S}f(t_{j})\geq p^{-1}\sum_{j=t^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}^{t^{\alpha}/m}f(c_{1}mj\log t)\\
&\geq\frac{F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)-F(c_{1}mt^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\log t)}{pc_{1}m\log t}\\
&\geq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{\log t}.\end{split}

For some positive constant
c_{2}
depending on
\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta
and
f
. Moreover, using that the
U
-labels are independent and Chernoff bounds

(4.10)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\leq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{2\log t}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{K}=S,S\text{ is good }\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{4\log t}\right\}.

And in order to simplify our writing, let
B
be the following event

(4.11)

B:=\left\{\nexists S\subset G_{t}(p),S\text{ is bad }\right\}.

In the event
B
, all vertices added by the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 0}
did not take too long to be added. That is, the
j
-th vertex added by the process
\{G_{t}(p)\}_{t\geq 0}
was added before time
c_{1}mj\log t
. Also notice that by equation (
4.3
),
{\mathbb{P}}(B^{c})\leq t^{-9}
.

Furthermore, we have the following identity of events

(4.12)

A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,t^{\alpha}/m}\cap B=\left\{\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset,\mathcal{K}\text{ is good }\right\}

and for each good

we use
\mathcal{K}(S)
as a shorthand for

\mathcal{K}(S):=\left\{\mathcal{K}=S,S\text{ is good }\right\}.

Then, using Equation (

) and summing over all possible good subsets

, we have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\leq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{2\log t},A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,t^{\alpha}/m},B\right)&=\sum_{S}{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\leq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{2\log t}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{K}(S)\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{K}(S)\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{4\log t}\right\}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset,\mathcal{K}\text{ is good }\right).\end{split}

We then obtain the following bound

(4.13)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\leq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{2\log t}\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\leq\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{2\log t},A_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,t^{\alpha}/m},B\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(A^{c}_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime},m,t^{\alpha}/m}\cup B^{c}\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{\alpha}\log t)}{4\log t}\right\}+\frac{2}{\log t},\end{split}

which proves the theorem.

Proof of Theorems

In this section we will prove our other two main theorems, which give sharp bounds on the global clustering coefficient and the clique number.
Recall that the global clustering coefficient of

is defined as

(5.1)

c^{\rm glob}_{t}=c^{\rm glob}(G_{t}(f))=3\times\frac{\Delta(G_{t}(f))}{\Lambda(G_{t}(f))}.

For the proof of both theorems will need the bounds on

and

given by the results below.

Theorem 6

(Number of Triangles)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation

. Then, for any

there exist positive constants

and

{\mathbb{P}}\left(C^{\prime}t^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}{2}}\leq\Delta(G_{t}(f))\leq Ct^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\log(t)}.

(Number of Cherries)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation

. Then, for any

{\mathbb{P}}\left(C^{\prime}t^{2(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}\leq\Lambda(G_{t}(f))\leq Ct^{2(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}\right)\geq 1-\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\log(t)}.

We will postpone their proofs to Section

. For now, we will just focus on how to use them to obtain our two main theorems.

Proof of Theorem
2
(Clique Number).

We will start by the lower bound.

Lower bound.
Recall that we already have proven Theorem
1
which provides a general lower bound for
\omega_{t}
in terms of
{\mathbb{E}}[|G_{t}|]
when
f
is decreasing to zero only. Thus, the lower bound part of Theorem
2
is a particular case of Theorem
1
when
f
is also regularly varying. Thus, this part of the proof consists essentially of analytical arguments to obtain estimates on
{\mathbb{E}}[|G_{t}|]
itself.

With the above discussion in mind and given
\delta\in(0,1)
, set
\delta^{\prime}=\delta+\varepsilon
for some auxiliary
\varepsilon
to be adjusted later and small enough so
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
. By Theorem
1
, we have that for every
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on
\delta^{\prime}
and

only such that

(5.2)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\omega_{t}\geq\frac{c_{2}F(t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2})}{\log t}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{\log t}-\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{2}F(c_{1}t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2}\log t)}{4\log t}\right\}.

Since
f
is a decreasing to zero, we also have that

(5.3)

{\mathbb{E}}[|G_{t}|]=F(t)=1+\sum_{s=2}^{t}f(s)\geq\int_{2}^{t}f(x){\rm d}x.

On the other hand, using that

is a regularly varying function at infinity with index
-\gamma
, where
\gamma\in[0,1)
, Theorem

gives us that

can be written as

, where

is a slowly varying function. Thus, by Proposition

, we have that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{2}^{t}f(x){\rm d}x}{t^{1-\gamma}\ell(t)}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma},

which by its turn implies the existence of a positive and

-dependent constant

such that

(5.4)

\int_{2}^{t}f(x){\rm d}x\geq Ct^{1-\gamma}\ell(t),

for all

. Plugging the above inequality into (

) yields

(5.5)

\frac{c_{2}F(t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2})}{\log t}\geq\frac{C\ell(t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2})t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})(1-\gamma)/2}}{\log t}.

Notice then that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, Proposition

allows us to replace the slowly varying functions in the RHS of the above inequality by a polynomial factor of

, at the expenses of a possibly different

, to obtain a nicer lower bound as the one below

\frac{C\ell(t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2})t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})(1-\gamma^{\prime})/2}}{\log t}\geq Ct^{(1-\delta^{\prime}-\varepsilon)(1-\gamma)/2}=Ct^{(1-\delta)(1-\gamma)/2}

Returning to (

), we have that there exists a positive constant

depending on

and

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\omega_{t}\geq Ct^{(1-\delta)(1-\gamma)/2}\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\omega_{t}\geq\frac{c_{2}F(t^{(1-\delta^{\prime})/2})}{\log t}\right)\geq 1-\frac{c_{3}}{\log t},

which is enough to conclude this part of the proof.

The proof will then be completed when we finish the upper bound.

Upper bound.
Recall that by Theorem
6
, it follows that for any
\delta>0
there exist positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime\prime}
depending on
f
and
\delta
, such that for all
t

(5.6)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Delta(G_{t}(f))\geq Ct^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\log(t)}.

But notice that if
\omega_{t}\geq Ct^{\frac{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}
, then
\Delta(G_{t}(f))\geq Ct^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}
for some possibly different constant
C
. This implies the following inclusion of events

\left\{\omega_{t}\geq Ct^{\frac{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}\right\}\subset\left\{\Delta(G_{t}(f))\geq Ct^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}}\right\},

which combined to (
5.6
) gives us the desired upper bound w.h.p. and allows us to conclude the proof of Theorem
2
.

Now we move to the proof of our second main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3
(Clustering Coefficient).

Notice that the proof is a straightforward consequence of Theorems
6
and
7
together with the definition of
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
.

By Theorem
6
, for any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have that with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}/\log t
,
\Delta(G_{t}(f))\leq Ct^{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}
. Whereas, by Theorem
7
,
\omega_{t}\geq Ct^{2(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}
, with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}/\log t
. These two results combined, gives us that

c^{\rm glob}_{t}\leq C\times\frac{t^{3(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}}{t^{2(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}},

with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}/\log t
, for some constant
C^{\prime}
depending on
\delta
and
f
. This gives the desired upper bound. The lower bound is obtained similarly.

5.1.
Convergence Theorems and the Inverse Relation

With the concentration inequalities for the clique number and global clustering given by Theorems
2
and Theorem
3
, respectively, we will show almost surely convergence in the log scale of these statistics properly normalized.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
: Convergence of the clique number.

We would like to show that

(5.7)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log(\omega_{t})}{\log t}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2},\,\text{a.s.},

when
f
decreases to zero and is a regularly varying function at infinity with index
-\gamma
, with
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

The proof will involve passing to a subsequence of
\{\log(\omega_{t})/\log t\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and then using monotonicity of
\omega_{t}
and
\log t
. In order to do so, notice that by Theorem
2
, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depending on
\delta
only such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(c_{1}t^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}\leq\omega_{t}\leq c_{2}t^{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}\right)\geq 1-\frac{c_{3}}{\log t}.

The above result guarantees that for fixed
\delta
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that

(5.8)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log(\omega(G_{2^{k^{2}}}(f)))}{k^{2}\log 2}\leq\frac{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}{2}+\frac{\log c_{1}}{k^{2}\log 2}\right)\leq\frac{c_{3}}{k^{2}\log 2}.

Similarly, we also have that

(5.9)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log(\omega(G_{2^{k^{2}}}(f)))}{k^{2}\log 2}\geq\frac{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)}{2}+\frac{\log c_{2}}{k^{2}\log 2}\right)\leq\frac{c_{3}}{k^{2}\log 2}.

Passing to the subsequence
\{\log(\omega(G_{2^{k^{2}}}(f)))/k^{2}\log 2\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we can combine (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) to Borel-Cantelli lemma to obtain that

(5.10)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log(\omega(G_{2^{k^{2}}}(f)))}{k^{2}\log 2}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2},\text{a.s.},

since (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) hold for any
\delta\in(0,1)
with possibly different constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
.

Finally, using that both
\log t
and
\omega_{t}
are increasing on
t
, we have that for any
t\in[2^{k^{2}},2^{(k+1)^{2}}]
, the following inequalities hold

(5.11)

\frac{\omega(G_{2^{k^{2}}}(f))}{(k+1)^{2}\log 2}\leq\frac{\omega_{t}}{\log t}\leq\frac{\omega(G_{2^{(k+1)^{2}}}(f))}{k^{2}\log 2}.

With the above inequalities, (
5.10
) and the Squeeze Theorem, one can show that the whole sequence
\{\log(\omega_{t})/\log t\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\frac{1-\gamma}{2}
almost surely.

We now prove similar result for the global clustering coefficient.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
: Convergence of the Clustering Coefficient.

The proof of Corollary
1.4
is almost in line with the proof of Corollary
1.3
, for this reason we will just indicate how it follows from Corollary
1.3
proof.

From the definition of
c^{\rm glob}_{t}
and properties of the
\log
, it folows that

(5.12)

\frac{\log c^{\rm glob}_{t}}{\log t}=\frac{\log 3}{\log t}+\frac{\log\Delta(G_{t}(f))}{\log t}-\frac{\log\Lambda(G_{t}(f))}{\log t}.

Observe that both statistics
\Delta(G_{t}(f))
and
\Lambda(G_{t}(f))
are increasing in time. Moreover, Theorems
6
and
7
provides bounds for the probability of the same order as Theorem
2
. Thus, the same argument of passing to a subsequence and using Borel-Cantelli holds, which allow one to obtain that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\Delta(G_{t}(f))}{\log t}=\frac{3(1-\gamma)}{2},\,\text{a.s.,}

and that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\Lambda(G_{t}(f))}{\log t}=2(1-\gamma),\,\text{a.s.}

Plugging the two above equality back into (
5.12
) is enough to conclude the proof.

Finally, the proof of the inverse relation between clustering and clique number.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

Up to this point, with the results we have proven, the proof of Corollary
1.5
follows directly from Theorems
2
and
3
. By Theorem
2
, with probability at least
1-c/\log(t)
we have that

c_{1}t^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}\leq\omega_{t}\leq c_{2}t^{(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}

and by Theorem
3
, with probability at least
1-c/\log t
,

c_{1}t^{-(1-\gamma)(1+\delta)/2}\leq c^{\rm glob}_{t}\leq c_{2}t^{-(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}.

Adjusting the
\delta
’s properly and combining the above results finishes the proof.

Proof of Theorems
6
and

: Number of Triangles and Cherries

In this section we will provide bounds on the number of triangles and cherries both counted without multiplicities.

For the upper bound for both
\Delta(G)
and
\Lambda(G)
we will need the result below regarding the number of edges in
\widehat{G}_{t}
.

Theorem 8

(Number of edges of
\widehat{G}
)
.

If
f
decreases to zero and is a regularly varying function at infinity with index
-\gamma
, with
\gamma\in[0,1)
, then, for every
\delta>0
there exists a positive constant
C
depending on
\gamma
and
\delta
only such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})\right]\leq Ct^{1-\gamma+\delta}.

For the sake of readability, we will defer the proof of the above result to Section

. In the next two subsections, we will only focus on how to use Theorem
8
to obtain bounds for the number of triangles and cherries.

6.1.
Number of Triangles

In this section we provide bounds on the number of triangles. More specifically, we will prove the following

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We start by the lower bound.

Lower bound.
It will follow from Theorem
2
. Recall that if
f
is a decreasing regularly varying function with index
-\gamma
, we have that for large enough
t

\frac{F(t)}{\log(t)}\geq Ct^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}

for some constant
C
depending on
\gamma
, which implies that

\frac{F(t^{(1-\delta)/2})}{\log(t)}\geq Ct^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}.

Thus, by Theorem
2
, with probability at least
1-3/\log(t)
, there exists a clique in
G_{t}(f)
containing at least
Ct^{(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)/2}
vertices. Consequently, with probability at least
1-3/\log(t)
we have that

\Delta(G_{t}(f))\geq Ct^{\frac{3(1-\gamma)(1-\delta)}{2}},

which gives us the desired lower bound w.h.p.

Upper Bound.
By Markov inequality, we have that

(6.1)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})\geq Ct^{1-\gamma+2\delta}\right)\leq\frac{1}{t^{\delta}}.

On the event inside the probability above,
\widehat{G}_{t}
is a graph on at most
t
vertices and less than
Ct^{1-\gamma+2\delta}
edges.

Now observe that, by Theorem 4 of
[
22
]
, the number of triangles in
\widehat{G}_{t}
is deterministically bounded by the number of edges to the power of
3/2
. Combining this to (
6.1
) gives us that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Delta(\widehat{G}_{t})\geq\left(Ct^{1-\gamma+2\delta}\right)^{3/2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{t^{\delta}},

which is enough to conclude the proof, as
\delta
is arbitrary.

6.2.
Number of Cherries

In this section we will provide bounds for
\Lambda_{t}
. Before we start the proof of Theorem
7
, we will need some auxiliary results and notation.

We now let
i,j,k\in\mathbb{N}
denote respectively the
i
-th,
j
-th, and
k
-th vertices that are added to
G_{t}(f)
.

The lower bound will follow from a lower bound on some vertex’s degree, which is proved in
[
3
]
but stated below for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 6.1

(Theorem 1 of
[
3
]
)
.

Assume that
f
goes to zero as
t
goes to infinity. Then, for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
C_{f}>0
depending on
f
only such that

(6.2)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall s\in\mathbb{N},\exists i\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}\text{ such that }D_{s}(i)\geq\frac{\phi(s)}{\phi(N)}\right)&\geq 1-\exp\{-C_{f}N\},\end{split}

where

(6.3)

\phi(t)=\phi(t,f):=\prod_{s=1}^{t-1}\Big(1+\frac{1}{s}-\frac{f(s+1)}{2s}\Big).

We will need the following technical Lemma on the function
\xi:=\phi(t)/t
:

Lemma 6.2

(Lemma 2.2 of
[
3
]
)
.

Let

(6.4)

\xi(t):=\frac{\phi(t)}{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t-1}\frac{s}{s+1}\Bigg(1+\frac{1}{s}-\frac{f(s+1)}{2s}\Bigg)=\prod_{s=1}^{t-1}\Bigg(1-\frac{f(s+1)}{2(s+1)}\Bigg)

Then, if
f
decreases to
0
,
\xi
is a decreasing slowly varying function uniformly bounded from above by
1
.

The above lemma together with Proposition
A.1
implies that for any
\delta>0
, there exist a positive constant
C
depending on
\delta
and
f
, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}

(6.5)

Ct^{1-\delta}\leq\phi(t)\leq t.

With the above discussion in mind, we can now move to the proof of Theorem
7
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

We will obtain the upper bound first.

Upper Bound.
For the upper bound, we use Theorem
8
again. The crucial observation to be made is that, on the event
\{\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})\leq C\log(t)t^{1-\gamma+\delta}\}
, the graph observable
\Lambda(\widehat{G}_{t})
is bounded from above by
\left(C\log^{5}(t)t^{1-\gamma}\right)^{2}
. Thus, by (
6.1
), it follows that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Lambda(\widehat{G}_{t})>\left(C\log(t)t^{1-\gamma+\delta}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\log(t)},

which is enough to conclude the proof of the upper bound.

Lower Bound.
The main idea behind the proof of the lower bound is to show that for any small enough
\delta
, with high probability,
G_{t}
has a vertex with at least
Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}
neighbors for some constant
C
. Then,
\binom{Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}}{2}
is a lower bound for
\Lambda_{t}
.

In order to do so, for a fixed
t
and small enough
\delta
, make
N=10C_{f}^{-1}\log t
on Lemma
6.1
to obtain

(6.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}\text{ such that }D_{t}(i)\geq\frac{\phi(t)}{\phi(N)}\right)\geq 1-t^{-10}.

Now let
v_{*}
be the vertex of smallest index whose degree at time
t
is larger than
\phi(t)/\phi(N)
. Notice that by (
6.5
) and our choice of
N
we have that

(6.7)

\frac{\phi(t)}{\phi(N)}\geq\frac{Ct^{1-\delta/2}}{N}\geq Ct^{1-\delta}.

For any time
s\in\{t,\dots,2t\}
denote by
A_{s}^{*}
the following event

(6.8)

A_{s}^{*}:=\left\{Z_{s}=1,v_{s}\text{ connects to }v_{*}\right\}.

In words,
A_{s}^{*}
is the event in which at time
s
a new vertex is added and connected to the
v_{*}
. Then, let
Y_{t}
be the following random variable

(6.9)

Y_{t}:=\sum_{s=t}^{2t}\mathbb{1}_{A_{s}^{*}},

which counts how many new neighbors
v_{*}
gained from time
t
to
2t
. Also, let
\mathbb{P}_{*}
be the following conditional measure

(6.10)

\mathbb{P}_{*}(\cdot):={\mathbb{P}}\left(\;\cdot\;\middle|\;\exists i\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}\text{ such that }D_{t}(i)\geq\frac{\phi(t)}{\phi(N)}\right).

Now, using that
f
is decreasing, that vertices’ degree are increasing in time and that
Z_{s}
is independent of the whole past, we obtain

(6.11)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{*}\left(A_{s}^{*}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right)=f(s)\frac{D_{s-1}(v_{*})}{2s}\geq f(2t)\frac{Ct^{1-\delta}}{4t}\geq Cf(2t)t^{-\delta}.\end{split}

By the Representation Theorem (Theorem
A.1
),
f(t)=\ell(t)t^{-\gamma}
, where
\ell
is a slowly varying function. And by Proposition
A.1
we can then absorb the slowly varying factor of
f
into a polynomial factor in the last inequality of (
6.11
) by increasing the value of
\delta
and changing the constant
C
. This allows us to obtain

(6.12)

\mathbb{P}_{*}\left(A_{s}^{*}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right)\geq\frac{C}{t^{\gamma+\delta}}.

Thus, under
{\mathbb{P}}_{*}
,
Y_{t}
dominates a random variable
W
distributed as
{\rm bin}(t,Ct^{-\gamma-\delta})
. This fact combined with Chernoff bounds for
W
yields

(6.13)

{\mathbb{P}}_{*}\left(Y_{t}\leq\frac{Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}}{2}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{*}\left(W\leq\frac{Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}}{2}\right)\leq\exp\{-Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}/8\}.

The above inequality tells us that conditioned on the event that at time
t
there exists a vertex whose degree is at least
t^{1-\delta}
, then, with probability at least
1-\exp\{-Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}/8\}
, at time
2t
this vertex has at least
Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}
neighbors. Then, combining (
6.13
) with (
6.6
) yields

(6.14)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\nexists v_{*}\in G_{2t},v_{*}\text{ has at least }Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}\right)\leq\frac{2}{t^{10}},

which allows us to conclude that with probability at least
1-2t^{-10}
,

\Lambda_{2t}\geq\binom{Ct^{1-\gamma-\delta}}{2}

and consequently to finish the proof of the theorem.

7.
Proof of Theorem
8
: Number of edges in
\widehat{G}_{t}

This section is devoted to prove Theorem
8
, which is the last result left to be proven. As usual, we will need a couple of technical estimates involving the time of appearance of vertices, a tight upper bound for their degrees together with estimates on the average number of edges between two vertices. These are all technical estimates and their proof might be quite technical. In a first reading, the reader might find instructive start from Section
7.3
which contains the proof of Theorem
8
.

It is also important to highlight that throughout this entire section,
f
will be a regularly varying function with index of variation at infinity
\gamma\in[0,1)
, which decreases to
0
monotonically.

Throughout this section we will make need new notation. Together with the functions
\phi
and
\xi
defined in (
6.3
,
6.4
), we will need the function
F^{-1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(7.1)

F(r):=\sum_{1\leq s\leq r}f(s);\quad\quad F^{-1}(r):=\inf_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\{F(s)\geq r\}.

Since we are assuming
f(s)
to be strictly positive for all
s
, and since
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have that

(7.2)

r\leq F(F^{-1}(r))\leq r+1,\quad\text{ and }\quad r\leq F^{-1}(F(r))\leq r+1.

We define
\tau_{i}
to be the first time such that the cardinality of the vertex set equals
i
, that is

(7.3)

\tau_{i}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\big\{t;\,|G_{t}(f)|=i\big\},

and in order to make notation less cumbersome, we further define

(7.4)

\psi(i)=\phi\left(F^{-1}(i)\right).

7.1.
Time of Appearance of Vertices

Lemma 7.1

(Time of Appearance of Vertices)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{f}>0
such that for
i\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\delta>0

(7.5)

\mathbb{P}\left((1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\leq\tau_{i}\leq(1+\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)\geq 1-C_{f}^{-1}\exp\left\{-C_{f}(1-\gamma)\delta i\right\}.

Proof.

We will prove that there exist a constant
C^{\prime}_{f}
such that

(7.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(i)\leq(1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)\leq(C^{\prime}_{f})^{-1}\exp\left\{-C^{\prime}_{f}(1-\gamma)\delta i\right\}.

The upper bound for the probability of the event
\{\tau(i)\geq(1+\delta)F^{-1}(i)\}
will follow from the exactly same argument.

By Theorem
A.1
, we know that
f(t)=\ell(t)t^{-\gamma}
with
\ell
being a slowly varying function. Proposition
A.2
implies that as
r\to\infty
, using the monotonicity of
f
to approximate the integral by the sum,

(7.7)

\begin{split}F((1-\delta)r)&=\sum_{s=1}^{\lfloor(1-\delta)r\rfloor}f(s)\\
&=\sum_{s=1}^{\lfloor(1-\delta)r\rfloor}\ell(s)s^{-\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\ell((1-\delta)r)(1-\delta)^{1-\gamma}r^{1-\gamma}\\
&\sim(1-\delta)^{1-\gamma}F(r),\end{split}

where we also used the fact that
\ell
is slowly varying to obtain
\ell((1-\delta)r)\sim\ell(r)
. Letting
i_{*}=(1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)

and recalling that
|G_{r}(f)|
is a sum of independent random variables, we may use the above equation, (
7.2
), and an elementary Chernoff bound to obtain

(7.8)

\begin{split}\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau(i)\leq(1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)$\mbox{}\hfil\quad\quad\\
&={\mathbb{P}}\left(|G_{i_{*}}(f)|\geq i\right)\\
&={\mathbb{P}}\left(|G_{i_{*}}(f)|\geq\left(1+\left(i\left(F\left((1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)\right)^{-1}-1\right)\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[|G_{i_{*}}(f)|\right]\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{3}\left(i\left(F\left((1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)\right)^{-1}-1\right)F\left((1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)\right\}\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{3}\left((1-\delta)^{-(1-\gamma)}-1\right)(1-\delta)^{1-\gamma}\cdot i\right\}\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-C(1-\gamma)\delta i\right\},\end{split}

for sufficiently large
i
. By adjusting the constant we can then show (
7.6
) for every
i\geq 1
, finishing the proof.

We will also need a suitable upper bound for the degree of the
i
-th vertex:

Lemma 7.2

(Upper bound on vertices degrees)
.

Assume that
f
decreases to zero and it is a regularly varying function with index of regular variation
-\gamma
, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{f}>0
such that, for every
\alpha>1
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }D_{s}(i)\geq\alpha\frac{\phi(s)}{\psi(i)}\right)&\leq C_{f}^{-1}\left(\exp\left\{-C_{f}\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}i\right\}+\exp\left\{-C_{f}\cdot\alpha\right\}\right).\end{split}

Proof.

Lemma
5
of
[
6
]
implies

(7.9)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }D_{s}(i)\geq\alpha\frac{\phi(s)}{\phi(n)}\Bigg|\tau_{i}=n\right)&\leq C_{f}^{-1}\exp\left\{-C_{f}\cdot\alpha\right\}.\end{split}

We note that, for
r>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
,

(7.10)

\frac{\phi\left(r\right)}{\phi\left((1-\delta)r\right)}=\prod_{s=(1-\delta)r}^{r-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{s}-\frac{f(s+1)}{2s}\right)\leq\prod_{s=(1-\delta)r}^{r-1}\frac{s+1}{s}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}.

Let
\delta=(\alpha-1)/(2\alpha)
, so that
\alpha(1-\delta)>1
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. We obtain, by the above equation, monotonicity, and the union bound,

\begin{split}\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }d_{s}(i)\geq\alpha\frac{\phi(s)}{\phi\left(F^{-1}(i)\right)}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(i)\leq\left(1-\delta\right)F^{-1}(i)\right)$\mbox{}\hfil\\
&\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\exists s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }d_{s}(i)\geq\alpha(1-\delta)\frac{\phi(s)}{\phi\left((1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right)}\middle|\tau(i)=(1-\delta)F^{-1}(i)\right),\end{split}

The result then follows from Equation
7.9
and Lemma
7.1
.

7.2.
Average Number of Edges between two vertices

Our next step is to prove the following lemma, concerning the number of edges between the
i
-th and
j
-th vertex in
G_{s}
, which we denote
e^{ij}_{s}
.

Lemma 7.3

(Average Number of Edges between two vertices)
.

There exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
depending on
f
only, such that, for all
1\leq s\leq t
and
j>i>\log^{2}(t)
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e_{s}^{ij}\right]\leq C_{1}s^{2}e^{-C_{2}\log^{2}(t)}+\frac{C_{3}\log^{2}(t)}{2\psi(i)}+\frac{\log^{4}(t)s}{\psi(i)\psi(j)}.

Proof.

Throughout this proof we will need to define a new filtration which, at time
r
, incorporates not only all the random choices made by the process until that time, but also if time
r+1
corresponded to a vertex or edge step:

(7.11)

\mathcal{F}_{r}^{+}=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{r},Z_{r+1}).

At the precise time in which vertex
j
is added to the graph, the only way for the the number of edges between
i
and
j
to increase is if
j
connects to
i
in a vertex step. Therefore,

(7.12)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{t_{j}-1}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{+}_{t_{j}-1}\right]=\frac{D_{t_{j}-1}(i)}{2(t_{j}-1)}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}.

For
r\geq t_{j}
, however, an edge step must occur in order for this random variable to increase,

(7.13)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{r}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{+}_{r}\right]=\frac{(1-Z_{r+1})D_{r}(i)D_{r}(j)}{2r^{2}}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}.

We define the event
B^{i}_{t}
, where the degree of vertex
i
is well behaved:

(7.14)

B^{i}_{t}=\left\{\exists s\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }D_{s}(i)\geq\log^{2}(t)\frac{\phi(s)}{\psi(i)}\right\}.

Lemma
7.2
implies
{\mathbb{P}}(B^{i}_{t})\leq C^{\prime}e^{-C\log^{2}(t)}
, for some positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
depending on
f
.

Using that
D_{t_{j}-1}(i)\leq t_{j}
deterministically, we obtain

(7.15)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{t_{j}-1}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\middle|\mathcal{F}^{+}_{t_{j}-1}\right]\mathbb{1}_{B_{t}^{i}}\leq\mathbb{1}_{B_{t}^{i}}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\},\end{split}

which together with (
7.12
), implies

(7.16)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{t_{j}-1}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\right]\leq\frac{\log^{2}(t)\phi(t_{j}-1)}{2\psi(i)t_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)+C^{\prime}e^{-C\log^{2}(t)};

By the same reasoning for (
7.13
)

(7.17)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{r}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\right]\leq\frac{\log^{4}(t)\phi(r)^{2}}{2\psi(i)\psi(j)r^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)+2C^{\prime}e^{-C\log^{2}(t)}.

Since
\phi(r)\leq r
, it follows that

(7.18)

\sum_{r=t_{j}-1}^{s}\frac{\log^{4}(t)\phi(r)^{2}}{2\psi(i)\psi(j)r^{2}}\leq\frac{\log^{4}(t)s}{\psi(i)\psi(j)}.

Moreover, combining Lemma
7.1
with
\delta=1/2
with the same bound on
\phi
, we have that

(7.19)

\begin{split}\sum_{t_{j}=j}^{F^{-1}(j)/2}\frac{\log^{2}(t)\phi(t_{j}-1)}{2\psi(i)t_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)&=\frac{\log^{2}(t)}{2\psi(i)}\sum_{t_{j}=j}^{F^{-1}(j)/2}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)\\
&\leq(C^{\prime}_{f})^{-1}\frac{\log^{2}(t)}{2\psi(i)}e^{-C_{f}^{\prime}(1-\gamma)\log^{2}(t)/2}\\
&\leq C^{\prime\prime}e^{-C\log^{2}(t)},\end{split}

for two constants
C^{\prime\prime}
and
C
depending on
f
only.
The same reasoning gives us similar bound for other two constants
C_{2}^{\prime\prime}
and
C_{2}
,

(7.20)

\begin{split}\sum_{t_{j}=3F^{-1}(j)/2}^{t}\frac{\log^{2}(t)\phi(t_{j}-1)}{2\psi(i)t_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)&\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}e^{-C_{2}\log^{2}(t)}.\end{split}

Using one more time the bound on
\phi
, we have

(7.21)

\begin{split}\sum_{t_{j}=F^{-1}(j)/2}^{3F^{-1}(j)/2}\frac{\log^{2}(t)\phi(t_{j}-1)}{2\psi(i)t_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)&\leq\frac{\log^{2}(t)}{2\psi(i)}.\end{split}

Finally, combining (
7.18
), (
7.19
), (
7.20
) and (
7.21
), we obtain

(7.22)

\begin{split}\sum_{t_{j}=j}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}&\left[e_{t}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\right]\\
&=\sum_{t_{j}=j}^{s}\sum_{r=t_{j}-1}^{s}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta e_{r}^{ij}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{j}=t_{j}\}\right]\\
&\leq 3s^{2}C^{\prime}e^{-C\log^{2}(t)}+\sum_{t_{j}=j}^{s}\left[\frac{\log^{2}(t)\phi(t_{j}-1)}{2\psi(i)t_{j}}+\frac{\log^{4}(t)s\xi^{2}(s)}{\psi(i)\psi(j)}\right]{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{j}=t_{j}\right)\\
&\leq C_{4}^{\prime}s^{2}e^{-C_{4}\log^{2}(t)}+\frac{\log^{2}(t)}{2\psi(i)}+\frac{\log^{4}(t)s}{\psi(i)\psi(j)},\end{split}

for some positive constants
C_{4}^{\prime}
and
C_{4}
depending on
f
only, which concludes the proof.

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
8

Finally we can prove Theorem
8
which is the last result left to be proven.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We begin observing that the result is clearly true when
\gamma=0
, for this reason, we will assume
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In this case, since
f(s)/s
is summable, by the definition of
\xi
in
6.4
, we we know that that there exists
c_{f}>0
such that

(7.23)

c_{f}t\leq\phi(t)\leq t

for all
t>0
. Now, recall that
|G_{t}|
can be written as follows

|G_{t}|=1+\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s},

where
Z_{s}\sim{\rm Ber}(f(s))
. Thus, by Chernoff bounds, there exist some positive constant
C
such that

(7.24)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|G_{t}|>Ct^{1-\gamma}\right)\leq C^{-1}\exp\left\{Ct^{1-\gamma}\right\}.

Notice that
\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})
satisfies the following upper bound

(7.25)

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in G_{t},\\
i<j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{1}\{e^{ij}_{t}\geq 1\}\leq\log^{4}(t)\;+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in G_{t},\\
\log^{2}(t)<i<j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{1}\{e^{ij}_{t}\geq 1\},

almost surely. Since at time
t
we have added
t
edges, the second term on the RHS of the above inequality can be bounded as follows

(7.26)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in G_{t},\\
\log^{2}(t)<i<j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{1}\{e^{ij}_{t}\geq 1\}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in G_{t},\\
\log^{2}(t)<i<j\end{subarray}}\mathbb{1}\{e^{ij}_{t}\geq 1,|G_{t}|\leq Ct^{1-\gamma}\}+t\cdot\mathbb{1}\{|G_{t}|\geq Ct^{1-\gamma}\}

Moreover, for a fixed
i\geq\log^{2}(t)
, we also have

(7.27)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in G_{t},\\
j\geq i\end{subarray}}\mathbb{1}\{e^{ij}_{t}\geq 1,|G_{t}|\leq Ct^{1-\gamma}\}\leq\frac{t^{1-\gamma}}{i}+\sum_{j=t^{1-\gamma}/i}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}e^{ij}_{t}.

Plugging the above inequality back on (
7.26
) back and then on (
7.25
) yields

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})\leq\log^{4}(t)+t\cdot\mathbb{1}\{|G_{t}|\geq Ct^{1-\gamma}\}+\sum_{i=\log^{2}(t)}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}\left[\frac{t^{1-\gamma}}{i}+\sum_{j=t^{1-\gamma}/i}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}e^{ij}_{t}\right].

Thus, taking the expectation on both sides of the above inequality and using (
7.24
) gives us

(7.28)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}(\widehat{G}_{t})\right]\leq\log^{4}(t)+t\cdot e^{-Ct^{1-\gamma}}+C\log(t)t^{1-\gamma}+\sum_{i=\log^{2}(t)}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}\sum_{j=t^{1-\gamma}/i}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{ij}_{t}\right].

To bound the last term of the above inequality, we apply Theorem
7.3
. We first need, however, to better understand the behavior of the function
\psi=\phi\circ F^{-1}
. Theorem
A.2
and elementary integration implies that
F^{-1}
is a regularly varying function with index of regularity
1/(1-\gamma)
. Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we then have

(7.29)

\psi(r)=\phi(F^{-1}(r))\geq cF^{-1}(r)\geq cr^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}-\delta},

for any
\delta>0
and a positive constant
c
depending on
\delta
. This yields, together with Equation (
7.28
),

(7.30)

\begin{split}\sum_{i=\log^{2}(t)}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}\sum_{j=t^{1-\gamma}/i}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{ij}_{t}\right]&\leq\sum_{i=\log^{2}(t)}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}\sum_{j=t^{1-\gamma}/i}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}C_{1}t^{2}e^{-C_{2}\log^{2}(t)}+\frac{C_{3}t^{\delta}}{i^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}+\frac{C_{4}t^{1+\delta}}{(ij)^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}\\
&\leq C_{1}t^{3}e^{-C_{2}\log^{2}(t)}+C_{3}t^{1-\gamma+\delta}+\sum_{i=\log^{2}(t)}^{Ct^{1-\gamma}}\frac{C_{4}t^{1+\delta}}{i^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}\cdot\frac{i^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}}{t^{\gamma}}\\
&\leq C_{1}t^{3}e^{-C_{2}\log^{2}(t)}+C_{3}t^{1-\gamma+\delta}+C_{4}t^{1-\gamma+\delta}\\
&\leq Ct^{1-\gamma+\delta}.\end{split}

By replacing the above bound on (
7.28
), we obtain the desired result.

In this appendix we gather important results from the Karamata’s theory. All of them may be found in

Proposition A.1

(Proposition 1.3.6 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let

x^{a}\ell(x)\rightarrow\infty,\quad x^{-a}\ell(x)\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }x\to\infty.

(Theorem 1.4.1 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let
f
be a strictly positive regularly varying function with index

. Then, there exists a slowly varying function
\ell

Proposition A.2

(Proposition 1.5.8 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\ell
be a locally bounded slowly varying function. If
\alpha>-1
, then

\frac{\int_{x_{0}}^{x}t^{\alpha}\ell(t)\mathrm{d}t}{x^{1+\alpha}\ell(x)}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

Proposition A.3

(Proposition 1.5.9a of
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\ell

is a slowly varying function. Moreover,

\frac{\widehat{\ell}(x)}{\ell(x)}\longrightarrow\infty\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

Proposition A.4

(Proposition 1.5.10 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let

\frac{\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\alpha}\ell(t)\mathrm{d}t}{x^{1+\alpha}\ell(x)}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{-1-\alpha}\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.
