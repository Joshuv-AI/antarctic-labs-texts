Infinitude of palindromic almost-prime numbers Thanks: † Corresponding author
Aleksandr Tuxanidy †, Daniel Panario

Abstract
. It is proven that, in any given base, there are infinitely many palindromic numbers having at most six prime divisors, each relatively large. The work involves equidistribution estimates for the palindromes in residue classes to large moduli, offering upper bounds for moments and averages of certain products closely related to exponential sums over palindromes.

Introduction

1.1.
Tattarrattat

Generally speaking, a
palindrome
is a finite sequence of objects which reads the same forwards as backwards. Any such sequence is said to be
palindromic
. For example the sequence of the (decimal) digits of the prime number

9609457639843489367549069

is a palindrome. According to SigmaGeek
[
37
]
(see also Iwao
[
25
]
) it is the largest known palindromic prime appearing in the decimal expansion of the digits of
\pi
.

One of the earliest historical palindromic objects, a SATOR square, was found in the remains of the ancient city of Pompeii (see for instance Sheldon
[
36
]
) destroyed in the year 79 AD after the eruption of Mount Vesuvius. Palindromes appear in several facets of human endeavor, for instance in music (e.g. Joseph Haydn’s Symphony No. 47, “The Palindrome”) in literature (e.g. Georges Perec’s “Le Grand Palindrome”). In recent decades, palindromes are studied intensively in various areas of research

They also appear in nature; for example, unusually large portions of the human X and Y chromosomes are palindromic (in a slightly different sense; see Larionov-Loskutov-Ryadchenko
[

).

1.2.
Conjecture and previous work

From here on out,
b
denotes an integer larger than
1
. One says that a natural number
n
is
b
-palindromic
, or is a
b
-palindrome
, if the sequence of its
b
-adic digits is palindromic. When the context is clear we simply say that
n
is palindromic (or a palindrome).

Palindromic numbers belong, more broadly, in the category of sequences and functions defined in terms of digit constraints in integer bases.
These have been studied abundantly in several studies. We mention for example the work of Bourgain
[
7
]
and Swaenepoel
[
38
]
on primes with prescribed digits, the recent work of Dartyge et al
[
10
]
on reversible primes, Mauduit-Rivat
[

on the sum of digits of primes, and Maynard
[
30
]
on primes with missing digits.
We refer the reader to Dartyge et al
[
10
]
for several other interesting works and references therein. With regards to palindromic numbers, we mention for instance the work of Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
showing that almost all palindromes are composite (see (
1
) below) Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
on palindromes with many prime factors, Cilleruelo-Luca-Baxter
[
8
]
showing that if

, every positive integer can be written as a sum of at most three

-palindromes, Col
[

on the distribution of palindromes in residue classes and palindromic almost-primes (see Theorems
1.2
,

[

giving a lower bound for the number of
b
-palindromic semiprimes in the interval
(b^{2},b^{3})
with

On the topic of palindromic numbers, perhaps one of the most difficult unsolved problems is the following conjecture (see for instance the concluding remarks in Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
and Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
). First for a real number
x\geq 1
, we let
\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)
be the set of all positive
b
-palindromic integers at most
x
in size.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
b\geq 2
be an integer. Then there are infinitely many prime numbers that are
b
-palindromic. In fact

\#\left\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\right\}\asymp_{b}\dfrac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)}{\log x}

for
x
large.

Conjecture
1.1
seems far out of reach with current methods. As pointed out by Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
, one of the main difficulties stems from the large sparsity of
b
-palindromic numbers, hindering the efficacy of analytic methods. The sparsity of these is, in essence, as large as that of numbers with form
n^{2}+1
. However Landau’s problem (posed in the year 1912) of whether or not there are infinitely many such primes, remains unsolved. On the other hand, Iwaniec
[
23
]
proved there are infinitely many integers
n
such that
n^{2}+1
has at most two primes factors. In view of recent results (see the discussion below and the following subsection) we believe that proving the analogous result, for
b
-palindromes, may be feasible over the upcoming years.

Some progress was made in the direction of Conjecture
1.1
. In the year 2004, Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
showed that for
x
large enough,

(1)

\#\left\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\right\}\ll_{b}\dfrac{\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}\#\mathscr{P}_{b}(x).

In 2009, Col
[
9
]
improved this to the expected

(2)

\#\left\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\right\}\ll_{b}\dfrac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)}{\log x}.

Col also obtained (see Theorem
1.2
below) a lower bound for the amount of
b
-palindromic almost-primes, when the number of prime divisors is bounded above by a certain value depending only on
b

1.2
was the first, and up to now unique, result of its kind. Here
\Omega(n)
denotes the number of prime divisors of
n

Theorem 1.2

(Col
[

We have

\#\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\ :\ \Omega(n)\leq k_{b}\right\}\gg_{b}\dfrac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)}{\log x}

for some value

as

Theorem 1.4

Let

large enough,

\#\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\ :\ \Omega(n)\leq 6,\ P^{-}(n)\geq x^{1/21}\right\}\asymp_{b}\dfrac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)}{\log x}.

Theorem

is a consequence of Theorem

with square moduli. See Proposition

estimate for the large sieve with square moduli, as well as the
L^{\infty}

[

(see Proposition

here).

In what follows

\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x):=\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\ :\ (n,b^{3}-b)=1\}.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
b\geq 2
be an integer and let
x\geq 1
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
,

(3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq x^{1/5-\epsilon}\\
(q,b^{3}-b)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{y\leq x}\max_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(y)\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{n\equiv a(q)}-\dfrac{1}{q}\right)\right|\ll_{b,\epsilon}\dfrac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)}{e^{\sigma(b,\epsilon)\sqrt{\log x}}},

where
\sigma(b,\epsilon)>0
is some value depending only on
b
and
\epsilon
.

We now discuss some of the main
ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

1.4.
Setup

For the sake of brevity we denote by
E
the left hand side of the inequality in Theorem
1.5
. Let
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}
,
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}
be defined as in (
9
), (
10
), respectively. Set
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(x):=\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}\cap[1,x]
. Since every
b
-palindrome
n\geq 1
with
\lfloor\log_{b}n\rfloor
odd (or equivalently, with an even number of digits in its
b
-adic expansion) is divisible by
b+1
, a divisor of
b^{3}-b
, it follows

\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}=\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}\ :\ (n,b^{3}-b)=1\right\}.

Then by
the Möbius inversion formula

\mathbf{1}_{(n,b^{3}-b)=1}=\sum_{r\mid(n,b^{3}-b)}\mu(r),

the Fourier expansions

\mathbf{1}_{r\mid n}=\dfrac{1}{r}\sum_{k(r)}e_{r}(nk)

and

(4)

\mathbf{1}_{n\equiv a(q)}=\dfrac{1}{q}\sum_{h(q)}e_{q}(-ah)e_{q}(nh)

(with the common notation
e_{m}(\alpha):=e(\alpha/m)
with
e(\alpha):=e^{2\pi i\alpha}
)
one derives

E\ll_{b}(\log x)\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq x^{1/5-\epsilon}\\
(q,b^{3}-b)=1\end{subarray}}\dfrac{1}{q}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\leq x\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(y)}e\left(\dfrac{hn}{q}+\dfrac{kn}{b^{3}-b}\right)\right|.

The asterisk above the
h
-sum signifies that
h
runs over the invertible residue classes modulo
q
.

The use of the decomposition in (
4
) is rather typical in the literature and it will shortly lead to qualitative gains in multiplicative algebraic structures, with the appearance of the products
\Phi_{N}
defined in (
16
). Unfortunately we pay the price of a quantitative loss of roughly
x^{1/5-\epsilon}
in the trivial bound. Thus in order for such a Fourier-type approach to succeed, we must not only be able to recover the lost
x^{1/5-\epsilon}
, but gain much more (in fact gain an extra
\exp(\sigma\sqrt{\log x})
).

From the works of Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
and Col
[
9
]
(see Lemma
6.1
here) the inner sum admits a decomposition into linear combinations of the products
\Phi_{N}
with
0\leq N\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{b}x
. After a dyadic subdivision of the interval
2\leq q\leq x^{1/5-\epsilon}
, the problem of bounding
E
then boils down to bounding averages of the form

S=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq Q\\
(q,b^{3}-b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\dfrac{k}{b^{3}-b}\right)

for
Q\ll x^{1/5-\epsilon}
. The trivial bound gives
S\leq Q^{2}b^{N}\leq Q^{2}\sqrt{x}
and our job is to beat this by much more than a factor of
Q
.

Thanks to the work of Col
[
9
]
(see Proposition
6.2
here) we can readily dispose of the cases with
Q
relatively small, say
Q\ll\exp(c\sqrt{\log x})
. We are thus left with considering
S
, or more strongly the average

(5)

T=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right),

for
\exp(c\sqrt{\log x})\ll Q\ll x^{1/5-\epsilon}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

As a first rough thought, one could envision splitting the product
\Phi_{N}
into two products, one over
1\leq n\leq M
and the other over
M<n<N
, applying Cauchy-Schwarz and end up considering averages of squares (just as Bombieri-Vinogradov’s treatment of the Type II sums; see Iwaniec-Kowalski
[
24
]
). These could be attacked say via a direct argument (see for instance Lemma
8.2
) or the large sieve inequality (see Lemma
1.6
below). The direct argument (namely that of expanding the square, switching orders of summation, using the orthogonality of the additive characters and bounding the resulting sums trivially) can at best aid in recovering a
Q
from the trivial bound, and is thus insufficient. This brings us to the large sieve inequality.

1.5.
Large sieve

First given a real number
0<\delta\leq 1/2
, one says that a set of points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{R}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is
\delta
-spaced
if
\|\alpha_{r}-\alpha_{s}\|\geq\delta
whenever
r\neq s
, where
\|\alpha\|:=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\alpha-k|
.

Lemma 1.6

(Large sieve inequality)
.

For any set of
\delta
-spaced points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{R}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and any complex numbers
a_{n}
with
M<n\leq M+N
, where
0<\delta\leq 1/2
and
N\geq 1
is an integer, we have

(6)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left|\sum_{M<n\leq M+N}a_{n}e(\alpha_{r}n)\right|^{2}\leq\left(\delta^{-1}+N-1\right)\sum_{M<n\leq M+N}|a_{n}|^{2}.

Proof.

See for instance Theorem 7.7 in Iwaniec-Kowalski
[
24
]
. ∎

The large sieve inequality is a powerful and useful tool for tackling averages of exponential sums and has enjoyed several applications in the literature (see for instance
[
13
,
24
,
29
,
30
,
40
]
to name a few). Unfortunately the quality of the bounds it offers highly depends on the density
\eta=\#\mathcal{S}/N
of the set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq(M,M+N]
in which the sequence
(a_{n})
is supported on, with better bounds for denser
\mathcal{S}
and worse bounds for sparser
\mathcal{S}
. For example in the case when each
a_{n}\ll 1
and
R\asymp Q^{2}\asymp\delta^{-1}\asymp N
, the large sieve inequality beats the trivial bound by a factor of
\#\mathcal{S}=\eta N\asymp Q\eta\sqrt{N}
. In particular in order for the large sieve to beat the trivial bound by much more than
Q
(which is what we seek) one needs
\eta
to be much larger than
1/\sqrt{N}
. Unfortunately sparse sets such as, say, the
b
-palindromes or close relatives, have density
\ll 1/\sqrt{N}
on
(M,M+N]
. For sequences supported on such sets, direct use of the large sieve proves insufficient.

An important idea for the case of the sparse sequences is to consider instead larger moments such as

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left|\sum_{M<n\leq M+N}a_{n}e(\alpha_{r}n)\right|^{2K}

for some integer
K\geq 2
; see for example Iwaniec-Kowalski
[
24
]
. One may arrive at these from smaller moments via Hölder’s inequality, say. To explain the concept, note we may write

\left(\sum_{M<n\leq M+N}a_{n}e(\alpha_{r}n)\right)^{K}=\sum_{KM<n\leq K(M+N)}b_{n}e(\alpha_{r}n),

where the coefficients
b_{n}
are now supported on the set
K\mathcal{S}
comprised of the additions of any
K
elements from
\mathcal{S}
. If the set
\mathcal{S}
is sufficiently dissociated additively, then
K\mathcal{S}
is much denser in
(KM,K(M+N)]
. In this case we can make a more effective use of the large sieve.

1.6.
Approach

Let us now return to (
5
).
Motivated by the discussion above, we split the product
\Phi_{N}
into three pieces as
\Phi_{N}=P_{1}P_{2}P_{3}
, where
P_{1}
is the product over
1\leq n\leq M
,
P_{2}
is the product over
M<n\leq 2M
and
P_{3}
is the product over
2M<n<N
(actually this is our approach for the case when
Q\gg b^{(\frac{2}{5}-\epsilon_{0})N}
; the case
Q\ll b^{(\frac{2}{5}-\epsilon_{0})N}
is treated in a slightly different way). The choice of
M
is optimized later. Applying Hölder’s inequality with the triple
(\ell,\ell,2K)
(where
K\geq 2
is an integer to be chosen later and
\ell>2
is such that
2/\ell+1/2K=1
) one obtains

T\leq\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}P_{1}^{\ell}\right)^{1/\ell}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}P_{2}^{\ell}\right)^{1/\ell}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}P_{3}^{2K}\right)^{1/2K}.

For
K
somewhat large (although bounded in terms of some parameters for our purposes)
\ell
is close to
2
. The first two sums corresponding to
P_{1},P_{2}
are then treated by us in direct fashion (as in Lemma
8.2
). Here we avoid using the large sieve which, given the sparsity of the coefficient sequences corresponding to
P_{1},P_{2}
, gives no significant gains over the direct approach.

There are two reasons why we have chosen to raise
P_{3}
to the
2K
-th power and not
P_{1}
nor
P_{2}
, namely: 1. the following algebraic identity

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}P_{3}^{2K}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N-2M}^{2K}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta b^{2M}\right);

and 2. the sequence of coefficients corresponding to
\Phi_{N-2M}
is denser than those corresponding to
P_{1},P_{2}
.
The trivial bound shows the above is at most
Q^{2}b^{2K(N-2M)}\leq Q^{2}x^{K(1-\frac{2M}{N})}
and our main goal then reduces to beating this by much more than
Q
for certain suitable choices of
M,K
. This is accomplished via Proposition
7.1
giving upper bounds for moments of
\Phi_{N}
for any
N\geq 2
. Its proof relies partly on the large sieve.

4.1.
Literary notation

We use the asymptotic notations

denotes a quantity satisfying

.

The letters

denotes the total number of prime factors of

denotes the smallest prime factor of

. The letter

is reserved to denote a prime number.

The notation

denotes the assertion that
n
is congruent to

modulo

. We use

to assert that
d
divides

, and

to represent the greatest common divisor (GCD) of

and

. If

, we use the notation

for the multiplicative order of

The symbol

denotes a summation over all the residue classes

modulo

, whereas

denotes a sum over the invertible residue classes

modulo

Given a function

\|f\|_{1}
its

-norm and by
\|f\|_{\infty}
its
L^{\infty}
-norm (whenever these exist).

For a real number
x
and an integer
q\geq 1
, we use the notation
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
for the complex exponential,
e_{q}(x):=e(x/q)
for the additive character modulo

and
\exp(x):=e^{x}
for the exponential. If

, its natural logarithm is denoted by

and its base-

. Moreover

denotes the floor of

We use the standard trigonometric notations
\csc(x)=1/\sin(x)

Given a statement
E
, we use
\mathbf{1}_{E}
to symbolize the indicator function of

\mathbf{1}_{E}=1

Given real numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}
, their
discrepancy

D_{N}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
is defined by

(7)

D_{N}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}):=\sup_{0\leq c\leq d\leq 1}\left|\dfrac{\#\left\{1\leq n\leq N\ :\ c\leq\{\alpha_{n}\}<d\right\}}{N}-(d-c)\right|

and their
star-discrepancy

D_{N}^{*}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
is defined as

(8)

D_{N}^{*}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}):=\sup_{0\leq d\leq 1}\left|\dfrac{\#\left\{1\leq n\leq N\ :\ \{\alpha_{n}\}<d\right\}}{N}-d\right|.

4.2.
Non-standard notation

The letter
b
is reserved to denote an integer larger than
1
. The notation
\mathscr{P}_{b}
symbolizes the set of all positive
b
-palindromic integers. We define the sets
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0},\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}
, as follows:

(9)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\ :\ \lfloor\log_{b}(n)\rfloor\equiv 0(2)\right\},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\ :\ (n,b^{3}-b)=1\right\}.

From the definition above one observes that
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}
is comprised exactly of all positive
b
-palindromes with an odd number of digits (in their
b
-adic expansion).
Since every
b
-palindrome with an even number of digits is divisible by
b+1
, and
b+1
is a divisor of
b^{3}-b
, it follows
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}\subset\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}
.
In fact

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{N=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{b}(2N),

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{N=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{b}^{*}(2N),

where for an integer
N\geq 0
,

(11)

\displaystyle\Pi_{b}(N)

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{P}_{b}\cap\left[b^{N},b^{N+1}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle\Pi_{b}^{*}(N)

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\in\Pi_{b}(N)\ :\ (n,b^{3}-b)=1\right\}.

Given a set
\mathscr{A}
of integers, a real number
x
and integers
a,q
, we define the sets

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{A}\cap\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\ :\ n\leq x\right\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(x,a,q)

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\ :\ n\equiv a(q)\right\}.

Thus for instance

(13)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}\cap[1,x],

(14)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x,a,q)

\displaystyle:=\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)\ :\ n\equiv a(q)\right\},

with our notation.

Given a real number
\alpha
and an integer
N\geq 0
, we define

(15)

\phi_{b}(\alpha):=\left|\sum_{0\leq m<b}e(\alpha m)\right|

and

(16)

\Phi_{N}(\alpha):=\prod_{1\leq n<N}\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)

with the convention
\Phi_{N}(\alpha)=1
for
N\leq 1
. The definition of
\Phi_{N}
also depends on
b
but for the sake of brevity we omit it on the left hand side above.

Finally given integers
n,K,b
with
K,b\geq 2
, we let

(17)

r(n;K,b):=\#\left\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{K})\in\left([0,b)\cap\mathbb{Z}\right)^{K}\ :\ m_{1}+\cdots+m_{K}=n\right\}

be the number of additive compositions of
n
into
K
integers
m_{j}
, each satisfying
0\leq m_{j}<b
.

The following lemma appears, either implicitly or explicitly, on several works on digital functions. See for instance

Lemma 5.4

(Ergodic-type integral bound)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be a bounded, locally integrable,
1
-periodic function. Then for any integer
N\geq 1
,

\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<N}f(\alpha b^{n})d\alpha\leq\left(\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq 1}\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{n(b)}f\left(\dfrac{n+\theta}{b}\right)\right)^{N}.

Proof.

By induction on
N\geq 1
. For
N=1
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}f(\alpha)d\alpha=\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{0\leq h<b}\int_{0}^{1}f\left(\dfrac{h+\theta}{b}\right)d\theta

and the claim follows. Suppose now that the claim holds for some
N\geq 1
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<N+1}f(\alpha b^{n})d\alpha

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{0\leq h<b}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<N+1}f\left(\left(\dfrac{h+\theta}{b}\right)b^{n}\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{0\leq h<b}f\left(\dfrac{h+\theta}{b}\right)\prod_{0\leq n<N}f\left(\theta b^{n}\right)d\theta.

The last equality holds since
f
is
1
-periodic by assumption. The above is

\leq\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq 1}\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{0\leq h<b}f\left(\dfrac{h+\theta}{b}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<N}f\left(\alpha b^{n}\right)d\alpha

and the claim for
N+1
now follows from the inductive hypothesis. ∎

The following lemma gives asymptotics for the number of compositions with some restrictions. We were unable to obtain a reference in the literature and we thus give a proof. If one allows the error term to depend on
n
, one should be able to considerably improve it with a more detailed analysis of the integrals involved. Nevertheless the error term here suffices for our purposes.

Lemma 5.5

(Compositions with restrictions)
.

Let
n,K,b
be integers with
K,b\geq 2
and let
r(n;K,b)
be as defined in (
17
). We have

\dfrac{r(n;K,b)}{b^{K}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{\pi(b^{2}-1)K}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{6}{(b^{2}-1)K}\left(n-\dfrac{(b-1)K}{2}\right)^{2}\right)+O\left(\dfrac{1}{bK^{3/2}}\right).

Proof.

An application of the identity

(20)

\mathbf{1}_{\ell=0}=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e(\ell\alpha)d\alpha

valid for integers
\ell
, yields

r(n;K,b)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e(-\alpha n)\left(\sum_{0\leq m<b}e(\alpha m)\right)^{K}d\alpha.

In what follows
\delta
denotes a fixed real number satisfying
1/2<\delta<1
. We allow implied constants to depend on
\delta
. To evaluate the integral above we split it into two pieces, one over
|\alpha|\leq\delta/b
and the other over
\delta/b<|\alpha|\leq 1/2
. We treat each separately.

When
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, the sum inside the brackets above equals

\dfrac{e(\alpha b)-1}{e(\alpha)-1}=e\left(\dfrac{b-1}{2}\alpha\right)\dfrac{\sin(\pi\alpha b)}{\sin(\pi\alpha)}.

By Euler’s product formula,

\dfrac{\sin(\pi\alpha b)}{\sin(\pi\alpha)}=b\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha^{2}b^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)^{-1}.

If
|\alpha|<1/b
, the logarithm of the product over
m\geq 1
equals

(21)

-\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 1}\dfrac{(b^{2k}-1)\alpha^{2k}}{m^{2k}k}=-\sum_{k\geq 1}\dfrac{\zeta(2k)}{k}\left(b^{2k}-1\right)\alpha^{2k},

where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function. For
|\alpha|\leq\delta/b
, the terms with
k\geq 2
contribute
\ll\alpha^{4}b^{4}
to the sum. Then it follows

\left(\dfrac{\sin(\pi\alpha b)}{\sin(\pi\alpha)}\right)^{K}=b^{K}e^{-\zeta(2)(b^{2}-1)K\alpha^{2}}\left(1+O\left(\alpha^{4}b^{4}K\right)\right)

for
0<|\alpha|\leq\delta/b
.
Thus, after dividing by
b^{K}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{b^{K}}\int_{-\delta/b}^{\delta/b}e(-\alpha n)\left(\sum_{0\leq m<b}e(\alpha m)\right)^{K}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{-\delta/b}^{\delta/b}e\left(\dfrac{b-1}{2}\alpha K-\alpha n\right)e^{-\zeta(2)(b^{2}-1)K\alpha^{2}}\left(1+O\left(\alpha^{4}b^{4}K\right)\right)d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e\left(\dfrac{b-1}{2}\alpha K-\alpha n\right)e^{-\zeta(2)(b^{2}-1)K\alpha^{2}}d\alpha

\displaystyle\qquad+O\left(b^{4}K\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\zeta(2)(b^{2}-1)K\alpha^{2}}\alpha^{4}d\alpha+\int_{\delta/b}^{\infty}e^{-\zeta(2)(b^{2}-1)K\alpha^{2}}d\alpha\right).

The quantity inside of the
O
-brackets can be shown to be
\ll b^{-1}K^{-3/2}
after substitutions of variables. By the well-known formula

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-at^{2}}e(-\theta t)dt=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{a}}e^{-\pi^{2}\theta^{2}/a}\hskip 20.00003pt(\theta\in\mathbb{R},\ a>0)

for the Fourier transform of the Gaussian
e^{-at^{2}}
and the fact
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
, the integral outside of the
O
-brackets equals

\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{\pi(b^{2}-1)K}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{6}{(b^{2}-1)K}\left(n-\dfrac{(b-1)K}{2}\right)^{2}\right).

To conclude it suffices to show that

\int_{\delta/b<|\alpha|\leq 1/2}e(-\alpha n)\left(\sum_{0\leq m<b}e(\alpha m)\right)^{K}d\alpha\ll\dfrac{b^{K-1}}{K^{3/2}}.

The sum inside the brackets is at most
|\csc(\pi\alpha)|\leq 1/2|\alpha|
(for
|\alpha|\leq 1/2
) in absolute value. Then the absolute value of the integral above is

\leq 2^{1-K}\int_{\delta/b}^{1/2}\alpha^{-K}d\alpha\leq\dfrac{b^{K-1}}{(2\delta)^{K-1}(K-1)}\ll\dfrac{b^{K-1}}{K^{3/2}}

for
\delta>1/2
fixed.
∎

We also need some trigonometric facts involving the function
\phi_{b}
defined as in (

\phi_{b}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}b&\mbox{ if }\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\\
|\sin(\pi\alpha b)/\sin(\pi\alpha)|&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly
\phi_{b}
is
1
-periodic and even; thus
\phi_{b}(\alpha)=\phi_{b}(\{\alpha\})=\phi_{b}(\|\alpha\|)
for any real
\alpha
.

The following fact, but with
\|\alpha\|
in the range
\|\alpha\|^{2}\leq 6/\pi^{2}(b^{2}-1)
, appears in

]
(see their Lemma 3).

Lemma 5.6
.

If

\phi_{b}(\alpha)\leq b\exp\left(-\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{6}(b^{2}-1)\|\alpha\|^{2}\right).

Proof.

We have
\phi_{b}(0)=b
and
\phi_{b}(1/b)=0
, whence the result holds for
\|\alpha\|\in\{0,1/b\}
. For
0<\|\alpha\|<1/b

Lemma 5.7
.

If
\alpha
and
0\leq\delta\leq 2/3b
are real numbers with

\phi_{b}(\alpha)\leq\phi_{b}(\delta)
.

Proof.

See Lemma 5 in
[
28

.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
be real numbers. Then either

\left\|\alpha\left(b^{\beta}+b^{\gamma+1}\right)\right\|\geq\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|}{b+1}

or

\left\|\alpha\left(b^{\beta+1}+b^{\gamma}\right)\right\|\geq\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|}{b+1}

(or both).
Moreover

\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{\beta}+b^{\gamma+1}\right)\right)\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{\beta+1}+b^{\gamma}\right)\right)\leq b\phi_{b}\left(\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|}{b+1}\right).

Proof.

We closely follow the argument in Lemma 6 of Mauduit-Rivat
[
28
]
.
Making the substitutions
u=\alpha(b^{\beta}+b^{\gamma+1})
and
\delta=\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|/(b+1)
, we need to show that either
\|u\|\geq\delta
or
\|bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|\geq\delta
, and that

(22)

\phi_{b}(u)\phi_{b}(bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma})\leq b\phi_{b}(\delta).

Suppose
\|bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|<\delta
. The triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle b\|u\|\geq\|bu\|

\displaystyle=\left\|bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}+\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\right\|-\left\|bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq(b+1)\delta-\delta=b\delta.

Thus
\|u\|\geq\delta
. Consequently either
\|u\|\geq\delta
or
\|bu-\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{\gamma}\|\geq\delta
, while (
22
) now follows from Lemma
5.7
.
∎

The following lemma may be useful when considering exponential sums over multisets of the form

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}\alpha_{n}\ :\ 0\leq c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}<b\right\}\right\}

for real numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}
with a sufficiently small star-discrepancy. This is due to the equality

\left|\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}}e(\gamma)\right|=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{b}\left(\alpha_{n}\right).

Lemma 5.9

(Weyl product bound)
.

There exists an absolute constant
A>0
such that, for any real numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}
with
N\geq 1
an integer,

\prod_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{b}(\alpha_{n})\leq\left(\dfrac{2}{D_{N}^{*}}\right)^{AbND_{N}^{*}},

where
D_{N}^{*}=D_{N}^{*}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
is their star-discrepancy as defined in (
8
).

Proof.

We adapt an argument of Aistleitner et al
[
1
]
(see the proof of Theorem 1 there).
Let
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2b
to be chosen later. For a real number
x
, we define

\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x):=\begin{cases}\sin(\pi b\epsilon)/|\sin(\pi x)|&\mbox{ if }|x-\frac{k}{b}|<\epsilon\text{ for some integer $k\not\equiv 0(b)$}\\
\phi_{b}(x)&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly
\phi_{b,\epsilon}
is
1
-periodic and continuous satisfying
\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)\asymp_{b,\epsilon}1
and
\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)\geq\phi_{b}(x)
for every
x
. In particular

\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\phi_{b}(\alpha_{n})\leq\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\phi_{b,\epsilon}(\alpha_{n}).

Denoting by
P_{N}
the product on the right hand side above, taking logarithms and applying the Koksma-Hlawka inequality (Lemma
5.1
) one has

(23)

\dfrac{\log P_{N}}{N}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(\alpha_{n})\leq\int_{0}^{1}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)dx+V(\log\circ\phi_{b,\epsilon})D^{*}_{N},

where
V(\log\circ\phi_{b,\epsilon})
is the total variation of
\log\circ\phi_{b,\epsilon}
on
[0,1]
.

We first bound
V(\log\circ\phi_{b,\epsilon})
. By the definition of
\phi_{b,\epsilon}
, if

x\in\mathcal{S}:=[0,1]-\bigcup_{0<k<b}\left(\frac{k}{b}-\epsilon,\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon\right)=[0,\epsilon]\cup[1-\epsilon,1]\cup\bigcup_{0\leq k<b}\left[\dfrac{k}{b}+\epsilon,\dfrac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon\right],

then
\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)=\phi_{b}(x)=|\sin(\pi bx)|/\sin(\pi x)
, which we interpret as
b
if
x\in\{0,1\}
. Over each of the intervals on the right hand side above,
\phi_{b}
is continuously differentiable and non-vanishing. Since
\phi_{b}(x)=\phi_{b}(\|x\|)
and
\frac{d}{dt}\log\sin(t)=\cot(t)
, it follows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left|\dfrac{d}{dx}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\left|b\cot(\pi bx)-\cot(\pi x)\right|dx+\pi\sum_{0\leq k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}^{\frac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon}\left(b|\cot(\pi bx)|+|\cot(\pi x)|\right)dx.

Since
\cot(x)=x^{-1}+O(x)
for
0<|x|\leq\pi/2
and
\epsilon\leq 1/2b
by assumption, the integral over
[0,\epsilon]
is
\ll 1
. Substituting variables and using
|\cot(\pi t)|=\cot(\pi\|t\|)
, we also have

b\int_{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}^{\frac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon}|\cot(\pi bx)|dx=\int_{k+b\epsilon}^{k+1-b\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx=2\int_{b\epsilon}^{1/2}\cot(\pi x)dx\ll\log(1/b\epsilon).

Thus

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left|\dfrac{d}{dx}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)\right|dx\ll b\log(1/b\epsilon)+\pi\sum_{0\leq k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}^{\frac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx.

For
x\in[0,1]-\mathcal{S}
, we have
\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)=\sin(\pi b\epsilon)/\sin(\pi x)
; hence
|\frac{d}{dx}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)|=\pi|\cot(\pi x)|
. Thus

\int_{[0,1]-\mathcal{S}}\left|\dfrac{d}{dx}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)\right|dx=\pi\sum_{0<k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}-\epsilon}^{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx

and so

\displaystyle V\left(\log\circ\phi_{b,\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\ll b\log(1/b\epsilon)+\pi\sum_{0\leq k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}^{\frac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx+\pi\sum_{0<k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}-\epsilon}^{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx

\displaystyle=b\log(1/b\epsilon)+\pi\int_{\epsilon}^{1-\epsilon}|\cot(\pi x)|dx=b\log(1/b\epsilon)+2\pi\int_{\epsilon}^{1/2}\cot(\pi x)dx

(24)

\displaystyle\ll b\log(1/b\epsilon).

With regards to the integral
\int_{0}^{1}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)dx
, splitting it similarly according to the definition of
\phi_{b,\epsilon}
, using
\phi_{b}(x)\leq b
, the additivity of the logarithm, and ignoring the terms
\log\sin(\pi b\epsilon)
(these are non-positive) one derives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\log\phi_{b,\epsilon}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon\log b+2\int_{\epsilon}^{1/2}\log\csc(\pi x)dx+\sum_{0\leq k<b}\int_{\frac{k}{b}+\epsilon}^{\frac{k+1}{b}-\epsilon}\log|\sin(\pi bx)|dx

\displaystyle=2\epsilon\log b+2\int_{\epsilon}^{b\epsilon}\log\csc(\pi x)dx

(25)

\displaystyle\ll b\epsilon\log(1/b\epsilon).

The equality above follows after substituting
bx
with
u
and using

\int_{k+b\epsilon}^{k+1-b\epsilon}\log|\sin(\pi u)|du=\int_{b\epsilon}^{1-b\epsilon}\log|\sin(\pi u)|du=-2\int_{b\epsilon}^{1/2}\log\csc(\pi u)du.

Now the result follows from (
23
), (
24
), (
25
) if we let
\epsilon=D_{N}^{*}/2b
using the fact
0<1/2N\leq D_{N}^{*}\leq 1
(see for instance Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 in Section 2 of
[
26
]
for this).
∎

Our final tool is the following version of Vinogradov’s lemma.

Lemma 5.10

(Vinogradov-type lemma)
.

Let
A,B,\theta
be real numbers,
A,B>0
, and let
q\geq 2
be an integer. Then

\sum_{n(q)}\min\left(A,B\csc^{2}\left(\pi\dfrac{n+\theta}{q}\right)\right)\leq\min\left(A,B\csc^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi\|\theta\|}{q}\right)\right)+\left(1-\dfrac{4}{\pi^{2}}\right)Bq^{2}.

Proof.

We can write
\theta=m+r
for some integer
m
and real number
-1/2<r\leq 1/2
. In fact
|r|=\|\theta\|
. Since
m+n
uniquely covers each residue class modulo
q
when so does
n
, the sum equals

S:=\sum_{0\leq n<q}\min\left(A,B\csc^{2}\left(\pi\dfrac{n+r}{q}\right)\right).

If
r<0
, we may replace
n
with
-n
in the summand. Thus without loss of generality we may assume that
r\geq 0
. Now note

S\leq\min\left(A,B\csc^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi r}{q}\right)\right)+BH_{q}(r),

where for
0\leq r\leq 1/2
,

H_{q}(r):=\sum_{1\leq n<q}\csc^{2}\left(\pi\dfrac{n+r}{q}\right).

Since
x\mapsto\csc^{2}(\pi x)
is convex in
0<x<1
, so is
H_{q}(r)
in
0\leq r\leq 1/2
. From the well-known identity

\pi^{2}\csc^{2}(\pi x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\dfrac{1}{(n+x)^{2}}

for
x\not\in\mathbb{Z}
,
one derives (see for instance
[
21
]
)

H_{q}(r)=\begin{cases}(q^{2}-1)/3&\mbox{ if }r=0,\\
q^{2}\csc^{2}(\pi r)-\csc^{2}(\pi r/q)&\mbox{ if }0<r\leq 1/2.\end{cases}

One can show that
H_{q}(0)\leq H_{q}(1/2)
(say by using the inequality
\sin(\pi x)\geq 2^{3/2}x
valid for
0\leq x\leq 1/4
) and thus
H_{q}(r)\leq H_{q}(1/2)
for
0\leq r\leq 1/2
by the convexity of
H_{q}(r)
in
[0,1/2]
. We also have
H_{q}(1/2)=q^{2}-\csc^{2}(\pi/2q)\leq q^{2}(1-4/\pi^{2})
since
\sin(x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
. The result now follows.
∎

]
.
Here we give a brief exposition of what is known, as well as add slightly to the literature via the results in Proposition
6.5
and Corollary
6.6
for special prime moduli. These are consequences of a result of Bourgain
[
6
]
giving upper bounds for certain types of exponential sums over finite fields with prime order. However these are not employed by us in our proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
. As such, Proposition
6.5
and Corollary
6.6
may be regarded as addenda to the work, stated here in the interests of expanding the subject.

For the sake of simplicity, and indeed, for our purposes, we focus exclusively on exponential sums over
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(x)
; that is, over
b
-palindromes, at most
x
in size, with an odd number of digits in their
b
-adic expansion. Sums over palindromes with an even number of digits are rather similar in nature and all results here extend naturally, albeit with minor differences, to these.

The starting point, just as that in the works
[
4
]
,
[
9
]
, is that such sums can essentially be decomposed into linear combinations of objects enjoying rather useful multiplicative and algebraic structures.
These properties are crucial in the upcoming sections. In this regard we have the following lemma, implicit in the works
[
4
]
,
[
9
]
. We give a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
\alpha,x\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\geq 1
,

\left|\sum_{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(x)}e(\alpha n)\right|\leq b^{2}\sum_{0\leq N\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{b}x}\sum_{0\leq M\leq N}\Phi_{M}\left(\alpha b^{N-M}\right),

where
\Phi_{M}
is as defined in (
16
).

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(x)
and the triangle inequality,

\left|\sum_{n\in\mathscr{P}^{0}_{b}(x)}e(\alpha n)\right|\leq\sum_{0\leq N\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{b}x}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\Pi_{b}(2N)\\
n\leq x\end{subarray}}e(\alpha n)\right|,

where

(26)

\Pi_{b}(2N):=\left\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\ :\ \lfloor\log_{b}n\rfloor=2N\right\}=\mathscr{P}_{b}\cap[b^{2N},b^{2N+1}).

The sum inside the vertical brackets equals

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\Pi_{b}(2N)\\
n\leq y\end{subarray}}e(\alpha n),

where
y
is the largest palindrome in
\Pi_{b}(2N)
satisfying
y\leq x
. If no such palindrome
y
exists, the sum is trivially zero. Otherwise we may write

y=y_{N}b^{N}+\sum_{0\leq j<N}y_{j}\left(b^{j}+b^{2N-j}\right)

for some digits
0\leq y_{j}<b
with
y_{0}>0
.

We now seek to establish the inequality

(27)

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\Pi_{b}(2N)\\
n\leq y\end{subarray}}e(\alpha n)\right|\leq b^{2}\sum_{0\leq M\leq N}\Phi_{M}\left(\alpha b^{N-M}\right).

To this end, we first define for an integer
\lambda\geq 0
,

\Psi_{\lambda}(\alpha):=\sum_{0\leq c_{N}<b}e\left(\alpha c_{N}b^{N}\right)\prod_{\lambda<n<N}\sum_{0\leq c<b}e\left(\alpha c\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right).

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\Pi_{b}(2N)\\
n\leq y\end{subarray}}e(\alpha n)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(\alpha)\sum_{1\leq c<y_{0}}e\left(\alpha c\left(1+b^{2N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\Psi_{1}(\alpha)e\left(\alpha y_{0}\left(1+b^{2N}\right)\right)\sum_{0\leq c<y_{1}}e\left(\alpha c\left(b+b^{2N-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\Psi_{2}(\alpha)e\left(\alpha y_{0}\left(1+b^{2N}\right)+\alpha y_{1}\left(b+b^{2N-1}\right)\right)\sum_{0\leq c<y_{2}}e\left(\alpha c\left(b^{2}+b^{2N-2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\Psi_{N-1}(\alpha)e\left(\alpha\sum_{0\leq j<N-1}y_{j}\left(b^{j}+b^{2N-j}\right)\right)\sum_{0\leq c<y_{N-1}}e\left(\alpha c\left(b^{N-1}+b^{2N-(N-1)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+e\left(\alpha\sum_{0\leq j<N}y_{j}\left(b^{j}+b^{2N-j}\right)\right)\sum_{0\leq c\leq y_{N}}e\left(\alpha cb^{N}\right).

Now the result follows when we note that

\prod_{\lambda<n<N}\sum_{0\leq c<b}e\left(\alpha c\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)=\prod_{1\leq n<N-\lambda}\sum_{0\leq c<b}e\left(\alpha b^{\lambda}c\left(b^{n}+b^{2(N-\lambda)-n}\right)\right)

and take absolute values.
∎

From the lemma above it is clear that to study upper bounds for sums such as
\sum_{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{0}(x)}e(\alpha n)
, it is sufficient to study the products
\Phi_{N}
or, more generally, products such as

(28)

P_{M}(\alpha):=\prod_{1\leq n\leq M}\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)

with
M\leq 2N
.
Of course the product’s definition depends also on
b,N
, but for the sake of brevity we omit these on the left hand side above.

Let us now state Col’s result in our context. This will be needed later on. We give a proof somewhat different to that of Col
[
9
]
in its initial steps, although it ultimately boils to considering lower bounds for sums such as
\sum_{n\leq M}\|ab^{n}/q\|^{2}
, just as in Col’s argument.

Proposition 6.2

(Col
[
9
]
, Corollary 4)
.

Let
h,q,k,M,N
be integers with
q\geq 2
,
(q,h(b^{3}-b))=1
and
0\leq M\leq 2N
. Then for
P_{M}
defined as in (
28
),

P_{M}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\dfrac{k}{b^{3}-b}\right)\ll_{b}b^{M}\exp\left(-\sigma_{\infty}(b)\dfrac{M}{\log q}\right)

uniformly in
h,k,N
, where
\sigma_{\infty}(b)>0
is some value depending only on
b
.

Proof.

We may assume that
M\geq\log_{b}q
as otherwise the statement is trivial.
Now for the sake of brevity let
\alpha:=\frac{h}{q}+\frac{k}{b^{3}-b}
.
Grouping the factors in the product by pairs of adjacent factors, we have

\displaystyle P_{M}(\alpha)\leq b\prod_{1\leq n<M}\sqrt{\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n+1}+b^{2N-n-1}\right)\right)}.

By Lemma
5.8
with
\beta=2N-n-1
and
\gamma=n
,

\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\phi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n+1}+b^{2N-n-1}\right)\right)\leq b\phi_{b}\left(\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n}\|}{b+1}\right).

Thus

P_{M}(\alpha)\leq b^{\frac{M-1}{2}+1}\sqrt{\prod_{1\leq n<M}\phi_{b}\left(\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n}\|}{b+1}\right)}.

By Lemma
5.6
and the
1
-periodicity of
\|\cdot\|
, the above is

\displaystyle\ll b^{M}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\pi^{2}(b-1)}{12(b+1)}\sum_{1\leq n\leq M}\left\|\dfrac{h(b^{2}-1)b^{n}}{q}\right\|^{2}\right)

uniformly in
k,N
. Since
q\geq 2
and
(q,h(b^{3}-b))=1
by assumption, each term in the sum above is
\geq 1/q^{2}
. In particular the result holds if
q<b
. Consider now the case when
q\geq b
. Here we argue as done by Maynard
[
30
]
in Lemma 10.1 there.
Dividing the sum above into subsums over segments of length
\lfloor\log_{b}q\rfloor
, we claim it is

\geq\left\lfloor\dfrac{M}{\log_{b}q}\right\rfloor\min_{(a,q)=1}\sum_{1\leq n\leq\log_{b}q}\left\|\dfrac{ab^{n}}{q}\right\|^{2}.

Indeed, with the possible exception of one subsum (coming from the tail with
n
within a distance
<\lfloor\log_{b}q\rfloor
from
M
, and lower-bounded by zero) each subsum can be written as

\sum_{1\leq n\leq\log_{b}q}\left\|\dfrac{h(b^{2}-1)b^{m+n}}{q}\right\|^{2}

for some integer
m\geq 0
. There are at least
\lfloor M/\log_{b}q\rfloor
subsums of this type. Since
(q,h(b^{3}-b))=1
by assumption, the claim follows.
Now since
q\geq 2
and
(q,ab)=1
, we have
\|ab^{n}/q\|\geq 1/q
for each
n
. Moreover if
\|ab^{n}/q\|\leq 1/2b
, then
\|ab^{n+1}/q\|=b\|ab^{n}/q\|
. Consequently there exists an integer
1\leq n\leq\log_{b}q
for which
\|ab^{n}/q\|\geq 1/2b^{2}
. Hence

\sum_{1\leq n\leq\log_{b}q}\left\|\dfrac{ab^{n}}{q}\right\|^{2}\geq\dfrac{1}{4b^{4}}

and the result follows.
∎

The bound due to Col
[
9
]
in Proposition
6.2
is non-trivial in the range
q\ll e^{o(M)}
and is rather general in the sense of the lack of restrictions on the arithmetic shape of
q
. As such, it considerably improved over the corresponding general bound due to Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
(see Lemma 3.2 there) non-trivial only in the range
q=o(\sqrt{M})
(strictly speaking, the bounds of Banks-Hart-Sakata were essentially given for products such as
\Phi_{N}
and not the product over
1\leq n\leq M
in Proposition
6.2
, but their work easily extends to this).

In what follows
\text{ord}_{q}(b)
denotes the multiplicative order of
b
modulo
q
(whenever
(q,b)=1
).
In the range
q\ll M^{2}
with
P^{-}(q)>b
satisfying
\tau(q)\sqrt{q}\ll\text{ord}_{q}(b)\ll M
(with suitable implied constants) the arguments of Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
(see Lemma 3.1 there) yield power-saving bounds of the form
P_{M}(h/q)\ll b^{\delta M}
with
0<\delta<1
fixed or depending only on
b
. Although applicable only on the much smaller range
q\ll M^{2}
, this is superior in strength over that in Proposition
6.2
for such
q
. Indeed, the latter attains (at best) power-savings only for
q\ll 1
. In few words, their argument can be explained as follows:

Assume
(h,q)=1
. Squaring
P_{M}(h/q)
, applying the inequality of the arithmetic and geometric means, expanding the square and switching orders of summation, all in turn, one has

(29)

\displaystyle P_{M}^{2/M}(h/q)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq c_{1},c_{2}<b}\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq n\leq M}e_{q}\left(h(c_{1}-c_{2})\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=b+\sum_{0\leq c_{1}\neq c_{2}<b}\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq n\leq M}e_{q}\left(h(c_{1}-c_{2})\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right).

If we impose the restriction
P^{-}(q)>b
, then
(c_{1}-c_{2},q)=1
for
0\leq c_{1}\neq c_{2}<b
. In this case

(30)

P_{M}^{2/M}(h/q)\leq b+\dfrac{b^{2}}{M}\max_{a,k\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}S_{q}(M,a,k),

where

(31)

S_{q}(M,a,k):=\left|\sum_{1\leq n\leq M}e_{q}\left(ab^{n}+k\bar{b}^{n}\right)\right|.

Here
\overline{b}
denotes the multiplicative inverse of
b
modulo
q
. We note that sums such as these also appear for instance in Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
, Bourgain
[
6
]
(see Theorem
6.3
below) Humphries
[
20
]
, Ostafe-Shparlinski-Voloch
[
32
]
and Popova
[
33
]
.

Using well-known bounds for twisted Kloosterman sums, one derives (see Lemma 2.1 in
[
4
]
)

S_{q}(M,a,k)\leq\dfrac{M\tau(q)\sqrt{q}}{\text{ord}_{q}(b)}+\text{ord}_{q}(b).

For this to be non-trivial, it is necessary that
\tau(q)\sqrt{q}\ll\text{ord}_{q}(b)\ll M
; hence their assumptions on
q
. One thus obtains

P_{M}^{2/M}(h/q)\leq b+b^{2}\left(\dfrac{\tau(q)\sqrt{q}}{\text{ord}_{q}(b)}+\dfrac{\text{ord}_{q}(b)}{M}\right).

Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
also studied the sum
S_{q}(M,a,k)
after the work of Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]

and obtained a bound for
P_{M}(h/p)
, with
p
prime, essentially comparable to Col’s result in Proposition
6.2
for
q=p\ll M^{2}/\log^{4}M
with no restrictions on
\text{ord}_{p}(b)
; see their Theorem 6.

From the argument above, it is clear that improving bounds for
S_{q}(M,a,k)
and/or enlarging the range of applicability of such bounds, may yield stronger results. However due to the presence of the
b
term in (
30
) coming from the diagonal cases
c_{1}=c_{2}
in (
29
), one can never do better than
P_{M}\ll b^{M/2}
via this argument, regardless of the quality of the bounds available for
S_{q}(M,a,k)
.

Soon after the works of Banks-Hart-Sakata
[
4
]
and Banks-Shparlinski
[
5
]
, Bourgain
[
6
]
obtained the following result. First given a prime
p
, let
\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the finite field with
p
elements and let
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
be its subset of non-zero elements, endowed with the usual properties of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Given
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
, we use the notation
\text{ord}(\theta)
to denote its multiplicative order.

Theorem 6.3

(Bourgain
[
6
]
, Theorem 2)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
, let
p
be a prime number and let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
satisfying
\text{ord}(\theta_{j})>p^{\epsilon}
and
\text{ord}(\theta_{i}\theta_{j}^{-1})>p^{\epsilon}
for each
1\leq i\neq j\leq r
. Then for any integer
N>p^{\epsilon}
and any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
,

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{p}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}\right)\right|<\dfrac{N}{p^{\delta}},

where
0<\delta=\delta(\epsilon)<1
is some value depending only on
\epsilon
.

We recognize that the sum, in Theorem
6.3
, with
r=2,a_{1}=a,a_{2}=k,\theta_{1}=b,\theta_{2}=\bar{b}
,
N=M
, is precisely
S_{p}(M,a,k)
. Thus we have the following.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
M
be an integer. Then for any prime
p
satisfying
(p,b)=1
and
p^{\epsilon}<\min(M,\text{ord}_{p}(b^{2}))
for some
\epsilon>0
, we have

\max_{a,k\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}S_{p}(M,a,k)<\dfrac{M}{p^{\delta(\epsilon)}},

where
\delta(\epsilon)>0
depends only on
\epsilon
, and
S_{p}(M,a,k)
is defined as in (
31
).

Proof.

Suffices to note
\text{ord}_{p}(b^{\pm 1})\geq\text{ord}_{p}(b^{\pm 2})>p^{\epsilon}
, the last holding by assumption.
∎

From this and (
30
) we may then obtain, under the same assumptions of Corollary
6.4
with
p>b
,

P_{M}\left(\dfrac{h}{p}\right)\leq b^{M}\left(\dfrac{1}{b}+\dfrac{1}{p^{\delta}}\right)^{M/2}.

One may improve this considerably, in Corollary
6.6
below, by combining use of Lemma
5.9
, the Erdős-Túran inequality and Bourgain’s bound. More generally we have the following.

Proposition 6.5
.

With the same assumptions of Theorem
6.3
,

\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\phi_{b}\left(\dfrac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(A(\epsilon)b\dfrac{N}{p^{\delta(\epsilon)}}\right),

where
A(\epsilon),\delta(\epsilon)>0
are some values depending only on
\epsilon
.

Proof.

We may view each
\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}
as a real number in the natural way.
We may also assume that
p\gg_{\epsilon}1
is arbitrarily large.
Now for each integer
1\leq n\leq N
, let

\alpha_{n}=\dfrac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}\theta_{j}^{n}.

By Lemma
5.9
,

\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\phi_{b}(\alpha_{n})\leq\exp\left(AbND_{N}^{*}\log\left(\dfrac{2}{D_{N}^{*}}\right)\right)

for some absolute constant
A>0
, where
D_{N}^{*}
is the star-discrepancy of
(\alpha_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
. By the Erdős-Turán inequality (Lemma
5.2
) and the fact
D_{N}^{*}\leq D_{N}
, we have

D_{N}^{*}\ll\dfrac{1}{H}+\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\dfrac{1}{h}\left|\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}e(h\alpha_{n})\right|

for any
H\geq 2
. The contribution of the terms with
p\mid h
to the series above is trivially
\ll\log(H)/p
. By Bourgain’s bound in Theorem
6.3
, the terms with
p\nmid h
contribute
\ll\log(H)/p^{\delta}
to the sum, where
0<\delta=\delta(\epsilon)<1
is some value depending only on
\epsilon
. Thus

D_{N}^{*}\ll\dfrac{1}{H}+\dfrac{\log H}{p^{\delta}}\ll\dfrac{\log p}{p^{\delta}}

after letting
H=p
. Since the function
x\mapsto x\log(2/x)
is increasing on
(0,2/e)
, then for
p\gg_{\epsilon}1
sufficiently large (as assumed)

D_{N}^{*}\log(2/D_{N}^{*})\ll\dfrac{\log^{2}p}{p^{\delta}}\ll_{\delta}p^{-\delta/2}

and the result follows.
∎

Specializing to the palindromes we have the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 6.6
.

With the same assumptions of Corollary
6.4
and
M\leq 2N
there, we have

\max_{(h,p)=1}\prod_{1\leq n\leq M}\phi_{b}\left(\dfrac{h}{p}\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(A(\epsilon)b\dfrac{M}{p^{\delta(\epsilon)}}\right),

where
A(\epsilon),\delta(\epsilon)>0
are some values depending only on
\epsilon
.

7.
Bounding moments of
\Phi_{N}

Here we prove Proposition
7.1
bounding moments of
\Phi_{N}
.

Proposition 7.1

(
2K
-th moment)
.

For any integers
N,K,b\geq 2
, we have

(32)

\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{N}^{2K}(\alpha)d\alpha\leq b^{2(K-1)N+2}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

If
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{R}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
are
\delta
-spaced for some
0<\delta\leq 1/2
, then

(33)

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Phi_{N}^{2K}\left(\alpha_{r}\right)\leq\left(\delta^{-1}+Kb^{2N}\right)b^{2(K-1)N+2}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

In particular for any
Q\geq 1
and uniformly in
\beta\in\mathbb{R/\mathbb{Z}}
,

(34)

\sum_{q\leq Q}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N}^{2K}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\leq\left(Q^{2}+Kb^{2N}\right)b^{2(K-1)N+2}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

Proof.

We first note that (
34
) follows from (
33
) since the points
h/q+\beta
with
1\leq h\leq q\leq Q
and
(h,q)=1
are
Q^{-2}
-spaced modulo
1
. Indeed, for any two distinct such fractions
h_{1}/q_{1},h_{2}/q_{2}
,

\left\|\dfrac{h_{1}}{q_{1}}-\dfrac{h_{2}}{q_{2}}\right\|\geq\dfrac{1}{q_{1}q_{2}}\geq\dfrac{1}{Q^{2}}.

Let us now set
\psi_{b}(\alpha):=\sum_{0\leq m<b}e(\alpha m)
. By definition,

\Phi_{N}^{2K}(\alpha)=\left|\prod_{1\leq n<N}\psi_{b}^{K}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|^{2}.

We have

\prod_{1\leq n<N}\psi_{b}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)=\sum_{0\leq c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1}<b}e\left(\alpha\sum_{1\leq n<N}c_{n}\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)

and one observes

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq n<N}\psi_{b}^{K}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K}\left(\prod_{1\leq n<N}r(v_{n};K,b)\right)e\left(\alpha\sum_{1\leq m<N}v_{m}\left(b^{m}+b^{2N-m}\right)\right),

where
r(v;K,b)
is defined as in (
17
). We can rewrite this as

\prod_{1\leq n<N}\psi_{b}^{K}\left(\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}e(\alpha\ell),

where

a_{\ell}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K\\
\sum_{1\leq m<N}v_{m}(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=\ell\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n<N}r(v_{n};K,b).

Thus

\Phi_{N}^{2K}\left(\alpha\right)=\left|\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}e(\alpha\ell)\right|^{2}.

Parseval’s identity then gives

\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{N}^{2K}\left(\alpha\right)d\alpha=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}^{2}

while the large sieve inequality (Lemma
1.6
) yields

\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Phi_{N}^{2K}\left(\alpha_{r}\right)\leq\left(\delta^{-1}+Kb^{2N}\right)\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}^{2}

for any
\delta
-spaced points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{R}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Thus to prove the proposition we must show that

(35)

\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}^{2}\leq b^{2(K-1)N+2}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

Expanding the square and switching orders of summation, we have

\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq Kb^{2N}}a_{\ell}^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u_{1},\ldots,u_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K\\
0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K\\
\sum_{1\leq m<N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n<N}r(u_{n};K,b)r(v_{n};K,b).

It will be convenient later on to introduce extra variables
u_{N},v_{N}
to the sum. To this end, we claim that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u_{1},\ldots,u_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K\\
0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}\leq(b-1)K\\
\sum_{1\leq m<N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n<N}r(u_{n};K,b)r(v_{n};K,b)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\leq(b-1)K\\
0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\leq(b-1)K\\
\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}r(u_{n};K,b)r(v_{n};K,b).

Indeed, any solution
(u_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{N-1}^{\prime})\times(v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{N-1}^{\prime})
to the equation

\sum_{1\leq m<N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0

yields the solution
(u_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{N-1}^{\prime},0)\times(v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{N-1}^{\prime},0)
to the equation

\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0.

Since
r(0;K,b)=1
additionally, the claim follows.

For any integer
n
, Lemma
5.5
implies

r(n;K,b)\leq\dfrac{b^{K}\mathbf{1}_{0\leq n\leq(b-1)K}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{\pi}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{6}{(b^{2}-1)K}\left(n-\dfrac{(b-1)K}{2}\right)^{2}\right)+\dfrac{c}{K}\right)

for some absolute constant
c>0
. We fix a smooth function
\nu:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
compactly supported on
[-2,2]
satisfying
\nu(t)=1
for
0\leq t\leq 1
and
\|\nu^{(j)}\|_{\infty}\ll_{j}1
for each
j\geq 0
. In particular
\mathbf{1}_{0\leq n\leq(b-1)K}\leq\nu(n/(b-1)K)
and

r(n;K,b)\leq\dfrac{b^{K}\nu(\frac{n}{(b-1)K})}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{\pi}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{6}{(b^{2}-1)K}\left(n-\dfrac{(b-1)K}{2}\right)^{2}\right)+\dfrac{c}{K}\right).

We can rewrite this as

r(n;K,b)\leq\dfrac{b^{K}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{n}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right),

where
\eta_{K,b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
is the smooth compactly supported function defined by

(36)

\eta_{K,b}(t)=\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{\pi}}\exp\left(-6\left(t-\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{(b-1)K}{b+1}}\right)^{2}\right)+\dfrac{c}{K}\right)\nu\left(t\sqrt{\dfrac{b+1}{(b-1)K}}\right).

Then we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\leq(b-1)K\\
0\leq v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\leq(b-1)K\\
\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}r(u_{n};K,b)r(v_{n};K,b)\leq\dfrac{b^{2KN}}{(b^{2}-1)^{N}K^{N}}J,

where
J
is the sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}(u_{m}-v_{m})(b^{m}+b^{2N-m})=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{u_{n}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v_{n}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right).

In view of (
35
) we complete the proof if we show that

(37)

J\leq b^{2}\dfrac{(b^{2}-1)^{N}K^{N}}{b^{2N}}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

To this end we proceed as follows.

An application of the identity

\mathbf{1}_{k=0}=\int_{0}^{1}e(k\alpha)d\alpha

valid for integers
k
, gives

J=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\prod_{1\leq n\leq N}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha v\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|^{2}d\alpha.

Let us set

(38)

A:=\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}\|\eta_{K,b}\|_{1}+\|\eta_{K,b}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

By the symmetry of
n\mapsto b^{n}+b^{2N-n}
around
n=N
and the bound

\left|\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(2\alpha vb^{N}\right)\right|\leq A

coming from (
18
) in Lemma
5.3
, note

J\leq A\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{1\leq n<2N}\left|\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha v\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|d\alpha.

Grouping by pairs of adjacent factors and bounding square roots of two endpoint factors, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq n<2N}\left|\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha v\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq A\prod_{1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)}\sqrt{\left|\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{u}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha u\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sqrt{\left|\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha v\left(b^{n+1}+b^{2N-n-1}\right)\right)\right|}.

By Lemma
5.3
with
k=4
, the product over
1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)
is at most

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)}\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\alpha(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}))}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\alpha(b^{n+1}+b^{2N-n-1}))}\right)}.

By Lemma
5.8
with
\beta=2N-n-1
and
\gamma=n
, the fact that
t\mapsto\sin(\pi t)
is increasing on
[0,1/2]
and
|\sin(\pi t)|=\sin(\pi\|t\|)
, we have either

\left|\sin\left(\pi\alpha\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|\geq\sin\left(\pi\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n}\|}{b+1}\right)

or

\left|\sin\left(\pi\alpha\left(b^{n+1}+b^{2N-n-1}\right)\right)\right|\geq\sin\left(\pi\dfrac{\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n}\|}{b+1}\right).

Then the product above is

\displaystyle\leq A^{N-1}\prod_{1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)}\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\|\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n}\|/(b+1))}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq A^{N-1}\prod_{1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)}\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{(b+1)^{4}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n})}\right)}.

Note in the last line we used the inequality
r\sin(\pi t/r)\geq\sin(\pi t)
valid for
r\geq 1
and
0\leq t<1
(which may be shown to hold, say, via Euler’s product formula). Thus

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq n<2N}\left|\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{K,b}\left(\dfrac{v}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)e\left(\alpha v\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq A^{N}\prod_{1\leq n\leq 2(N-1)}\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{(b+1)^{4}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\alpha(b^{2}-1)b^{n})}\right)}.

Inserting this in the integral, substituting
\alpha b(b^{2}-1)
with
\alpha
and using the
1
-periodicity of the integrand, we obtain

(39)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\leq A^{N+1}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<2(N-1)}\sqrt{\min\left(A,\dfrac{(b+1)^{4}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/2}K^{3/2}\sin^{4}(\pi\alpha b^{n})}\right)}d\alpha.

By Lemma
5.4
and the fact
\sqrt{\min(A,B)}=\min(\sqrt{A},\sqrt{B})
for
A,B\geq 0
, this integral (without the normalizing factor
A^{N+1}
) is

\leq\left(\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_{n(b)}\min\left(A^{1/2},\dfrac{(b+1)^{2}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}^{1/2}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/4}K^{3/4}\sin^{2}(\pi(n+\theta)/b)}\right)\right)^{2(N-1)}

while Lemma
5.10
implies this is

\leq\left(\dfrac{A^{1/2}}{b}+\dfrac{b(b+1)^{2}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}^{1/2}}{(b^{2}-1)^{3/4}K^{3/4}}\right)^{2(N-1)}.

Factoring
\frac{1}{b}(b^{2}-1)^{1/4}K^{1/4}
out from the inside of the brackets and factoring

(b^{2}-1)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}K^{\frac{N+1}{2}}
out from the normalizing factor
A^{N+1}
in (
39
) it follows

J\leq b^{2}\dfrac{(b^{2}-1)^{N}K^{N}}{b^{2N}}\mathcal{E}(N,K,b),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(N,K,b)

\displaystyle:=\left(\dfrac{A}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)^{N+1}\left(\dfrac{A^{1/2}}{(b^{2}-1)^{1/4}K^{1/4}}+\dfrac{b^{2}(b+1)^{2}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}^{1/2}}{(b^{2}-1)K}\right)^{2(N-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{A}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}\right)^{N}\left(\dfrac{A^{1/2}}{(b^{2}-1)^{1/4}K^{1/4}}+\dfrac{3b^{2}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}^{1/2}}{K}\right)^{2N}

\displaystyle=\left(\|\eta_{K,b}\|_{1}+\dfrac{\|\eta_{K,b}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}+\dfrac{3b^{2}\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}^{1/2}}{K}\sqrt{\|\eta_{K,b}\|_{1}+\dfrac{\|\eta_{K,b}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{\sqrt{(b^{2}-1)K}}}\right)^{2N}.

The inequality above follows from the facts
B(\sqrt{B}+C)^{-2}\leq 1
for any
B>0
,
C\geq 0
, and
(b+1)^{2}\leq 3(b^{2}-1)
for
b\geq 2
.
The last equality holds by the definition of
A
in (
38
). From the definition of
\eta_{K,b}
in (
36
) and the assumptions on
\nu
, we have
\|\eta_{K,b}^{\prime}\|_{1},\|\eta_{K,b}^{(4)}\|_{1}\ll 1
uniformly in
K,b
. Moreover the fact
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t^{2}}dt=\sqrt{\pi}
and the assumption
\nu\leq 1
imply

\|\eta_{K,b}\|_{1}\leq 1+\dfrac{c\|\nu\|_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}=1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\right).

Hence

\mathcal{E}(N,K,b)\leq\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}

and

J\leq b^{2}\dfrac{(b^{2}-1)^{N}K^{N}}{b^{2N}}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}}+\dfrac{b^{2}}{K}\right)\right)^{2N}.

We have shown that (
37
) holds and the proof is thus complete.
∎

8.
Bounding the average

Here we prove Proposition
8.1
below combining our moment bounds in Proposition
7.1
with Col’s
L^{\infty}
-type bound in Proposition
6.2
. As an application of Proposition
8.1
, we then give a proof of Theorem
1.5
in the following section.

Proposition 8.1

(
L^{1}/L^{2}/L^{2K}/L^{\infty}
hybrid bound)
.

Let
N\geq 0
be an integer and let
Q\geq 1
. For any
0<\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{15}
and
\frac{1}{3}\leq\delta\leq\frac{2}{5}-\epsilon
,

(40)

\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
(q,b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\ll_{b,\epsilon}Q^{2}b^{N(1-\delta-\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon))}+Q^{1-\frac{\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon)}{\delta}}b^{N},

where
\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon)>0
is some value depending only on
b
and
\epsilon
.
Moreover

(41)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq Q\\
(q,b^{3}-b)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}^{*}\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\dfrac{k}{b^{3}-b}\right)\ll_{b,\epsilon}\left(Q^{2}b^{N(1-\delta-\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon))}+Q^{1-\frac{\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon)}{\delta}}b^{N}\right)e^{-\frac{\sigma_{\infty}(b)N}{\log Q}}

uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
where
\sigma_{\infty}(b)>0
is some value depending only on
b
.

We will need the following bound.

Lemma 8.2

(
L^{2}
-bound)
.

Let
0\leq L<M<N
be integers and let
Q\geq 1
. Then

\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{q\leq Q}\sum_{h(q)}\prod_{L<n\leq M}\phi_{b}^{2}\left(\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right)\ll_{\epsilon}\left(Q+b^{M-L+\epsilon N}\right)Qb^{M-L}

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

Expanding the square, switching orders of summation, using the orthogonality of the additive characters modulo
q
and taking absolute values, we have that the left hand side above is

\leq Q\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq u_{L+1},\ldots,u_{M}<b\\
0\leq v_{L+1},\ldots,v_{M}<b\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
q\mid S(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\end{subarray}}1,

where

S(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\sum_{L<n\leq M}\left(u_{n}-v_{n}\right)\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right).

By the uniqueness of the representation of integers in base
b
, we have
S(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=0
if and only if
u_{n}=v_{n}
for each
L<n\leq M
. The contribution to the overall sum of such (diagonal) terms is
\leq Q^{2}b^{M-L}
. For the off-diagonal terms with
u_{n}\neq v_{n}
for some
n
, the inner sum is
\leq\tau(S(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}))\ll_{\epsilon}b^{\epsilon N}
for any
\epsilon>0
, where
\tau(m):=\sum_{d\mid m}1
is the divisor function. Thus these contribute
\ll_{\epsilon}Qb^{2(M-L)+\epsilon N}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
8.1
.

We may assume that
N\geq 100
, say, as otherwise the statement is trivial.
Let us treat the cases when
Q\ll_{b,\epsilon}b^{\delta N}
and
Q\gg_{b,\epsilon}b^{\delta N}
separately.

We begin by considering the case when
Q\gg_{b,\epsilon}b^{\delta N}
. Here one can check that both right hand sides of (
40
) and (
41
) are
\gg_{b,\epsilon}Q^{2}b^{N(1-\delta-\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon))}
(in particular the exponential factor in (
41
) is
\asymp_{b,\epsilon}1
since
\delta\geq 1/3
by assumption). Thus to prove (
40
) and (
41
) when

it suffices to show that the left hand side of (
40
) is
\ll_{b,\epsilon}Q^{2}b^{N(1-\delta-\sigma_{1}(b,\epsilon))}
in this case. To this end we proceed as follows.

Let
M=\lfloor\delta N\rfloor
and split the product as

\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)=P_{1}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)P_{2}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)P_{3}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right),

where

\displaystyle P_{1}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq n\leq M}\phi_{b}\left(\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right),

\displaystyle P_{2}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{M<n\leq 2M}\phi_{b}\left(\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right),

\displaystyle P_{3}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{2M<n<N}\phi_{b}\left(\left(\dfrac{h}{q}+\beta\right)\left(b^{n}+b^{2N-n}\right)\right).

K\geq 2
be an integer to be specified later and
set

. Note that

. Then by Hölder’s inequality with the triple

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}P_{1}^{\ell}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)\right)^{1/\ell}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\end{subarray}}\sum_{h(q)}P_{2}^{\ell}\left(\dfrac{h}{q}\right)\right)^{1/\ell}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\

.

.

If

.

) is

)) and

respectively,
where

With regards to the product over

above, Proposition

Lemma 9.1

For an integer

Every integer

Since

\displaystyle\#\Pi_{b}^{*}(2N)

The equality before the last follows from the unique representation of integers in base

The inner sum equals

\displaystyle S(a)

, the Chinese remainder theorem and dyadic decompositions of the intervals, we then have

E(U)\ll_{b}(\log x)^{4}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}QR\ll U\\

where

\mathscr{T}(N,Q,R,k)=\dfrac{1}{Q^{2}R^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq Q\\
r\leq R\\
qr>1\\
(qr,b^{3}-b)=(q,r)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{g(q^{2})}^{*}\sum_{h(r)}^{*}\Phi_{N}\left(\dfrac{g}{q^{2}}+\dfrac{h}{r}+\dfrac{k}{b^{3}-b}\right).

Let

and

implies

.

Case 2

and

): Taking the worst possible value of

with

there, one has

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{Q}{R^{2}}\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq R\\

for

small and fixed.

Case 3

11.
Palindromic almost-primes

We conclude the work with a proof of Theorem
1.4
.
To this end we use the following version of the almost-prime sieve.

Lemma 11.1

(Almost-prime linear sieve with Richert’s weights)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite sequence of integers in
[1,x]
.
For each integer
d\geq 1
, set

A_{d}:=\#\left\{n\in\mathcal{A}\ :\ d\mid n\right\}.

Suppose for each such
d
that

A_{d}=Xg(d)+r_{d}

for some
r_{d}
, real number
X
and some multiplicative function
g
satisfying
0\leq g(p)<1
for each prime
p
and

\prod_{u\leq p<v}(1-g(p))^{-1}\leq K\dfrac{\log v}{\log u}

for any
2\leq u<v\leq x
, where
K>1
is some constant.

For an integer
r\geq 2
, set

u_{r}:=1+3^{-r}

and

\Delta_{r}:=r+\log_{3}\left(\dfrac{3u_{r}}{4}\right).

Suppose that

\sum_{d\leq D}|r_{d}|\ll\dfrac{X}{\log^{3}x}

and

\sum_{D^{1/4}\leq p\leq D^{1/u_{r}}}A_{p^{2}}\ll\dfrac{X}{\log^{3}x}

for some
D
satisfying
D\geq x^{1/\Delta_{r}+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geq D^{1/4}\\
\Omega(n)\leq r\end{subarray}}1\asymp_{r,\epsilon}X\prod_{p<x}(1-g(p)).

Proof.

For the lower bound see for instance Corollaries 1.1 and 1.2 in Section 5 of Greaves
[
17
]
. For the upper bound see Corollary 1.1 in Section 1 of
[
17
]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We may assume that
x\gg_{b}1
is large enough so that
z:=x^{1/21}
is much larger than
b^{3}-b
.
In this case,

S:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}(x)\\
P^{-}(n)\geq z\\
\Omega(n)\leq 6\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
P^{-}(n)\geq z\\
\Omega(n)\leq 6\end{subarray}}1.

For any integer
d\geq 1
, set

A_{d}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
d\mid n\end{subarray}}1=\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x,0,d).

We can write this as

A_{d}=g(d)\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)+r_{d},

where

g(d)=\dfrac{\mathbf{1}_{(d,b^{3}-b)=1}}{d}

and

r_{d}=\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x,0,d)-g(d)\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x).

Clearly
g
is multiplicative, satisfies
0\leq g(p)<1
for each prime
p
and

\prod_{u\leq p<v}(1-g(p))^{-1}\leq\prod_{u\leq p<v}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}=\dfrac{\log v}{\log u}\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{1}{\log u}\right)\right)

for any
2\leq u<v\leq x
,
by the Mertens’ theorems.

Set

. Since every number in

. It also follows from Proposition
