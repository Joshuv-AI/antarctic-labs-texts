Bauer’s Spectral Factorization Method for Low Order Multiwavelet Filter Design
Vasil Kolev, Todor Cooklev, Fritz Keinert

Abstract
Para-Hermitian polynomial matrices obtained by matrix spectral factorization lead to functions useful in control theory systems, basis functions in numerical methods or multiscaling functions used in signal processing. We introduce a fast algorithm for matrix spectral factorization based on Bauer’s method. We convert Bauer’s method into a nonlinear matrix equation (NME). The NME is solved by two different numerical algorithms (Fixed Point Iteration and Newton’s Method) which produce approximate scalar or matrix factors, as well as a symbolic algorithm which produces exact factors in closed form for some low-order scalar or matrix polynomial matrices, respectively. Convergence rates of the two numerical algorithms are investigated for a number of singular and nonsingular scalar and matrix polynomials taken from different areas. In particular, one of the singular examples leads to new orthogonal multiscaling and multiwavelet filters. Since the NME can also be solved as a Generalized Discrete Time Algebraic Riccati Equation (GDARE), numerical results using built-in routines in Maple 17.0 and six Matlab versions are presented.

Introduction and Problem Statement

Spectral Factorization
(SF) is an important tool in signal
processing, control theory, information theory, and many other
fields. Despite the vast literature on the subject, there is still a lot
of interest in this topic. SF factorizes a given Laurent polynomial
function of the form

p(z)=\sum_{k=-m}^{n}p_{k}z^{k}

into causal and anti-causal polynomials. As the name indicates, it is
often applied to spectral densities of stochastic processes. In the
case of polynomials with matrix coefficients, SF is called
Matrix
Spectral Factorization
(MSF).

In systems and control theory, para-Hermitian matrix polynomials play a
fundamental role. The para-conjugate of a matrix Laurent polynomial is
defined as

P(z)^{*}=\left(\sum_{k=-m}^{n}P_{k}z^{k}\right)^{*}=\sum_{k=-m}^{n}P_{k}^{*}z^{-k},

where
A^{*}
is the complex conjugate transpose of the matrix
A
. A
matrix polynomial is
para-Hermitian
if
P(z)^{*}=P(z)
. Thus, a
para-Hermitian matrix polynomial has the form

P(z)=\sum_{k=-m}^{m}P_{k}z^{k},\quad P_{-k}=P_{k}^{*}.

Such matrix polynomials were first used by Norbert Wiener

[
46
,
47
]
, where they represent spectral density functions of
stochastic processes. They are also used in many other settings, for
example in MIMO communication theory, in linear quadratic optimal
control, optimal filtering engineering systems, signal processing, and
control analysis.

A matrix
A
is
positive definite
, written
A>0
, if

{\mathbf{x}}^{*}A{\mathbf{x}}>0
for all
{\mathbf{x}}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
. It is
positive
semidefinite
, written
A\geq 0
, if
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}A{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0
for all

{\mathbf{x}}
. The matrix form of the Riesz-Fejér lemma

[
19
,
26
]
implies that if
P(z)
is para-Hermitian and positive
semidefinite for
z
on the unit circle
\,{\mathbb{T}}
in the complex plane, then
it can be
left factorized
as
P(z)=H(z)H(z)^{*}
,

z\in\,{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
, where

H(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}H_{k}z^{-k}.

H(z)
is called a
left spectral factor
of
P(z)
.

Similarly, a
right spectral factor
satisfies
P(z)=H(z)^{*}H(z)
.
In this paper we consider left factorizations only.

A matrix polynomial
P(z)
is called
singular
if its determinant

|P(z)|
vanishes somewhere on
\,{\mathbb{T}}
. Singular polynomial matrices
appear for example in control and linear systems theory
[
32
]
,
wireless communication
[
4
]
, and especially in wavelet and
multiwavelet filter bank design
[
33
]
.

In multiwavelet theory, scalar or matrix spectral factorization plays a
key role. Finding spectral factors of para-Hermitian polynomial
matrices (which are product filters in signal processing theory) leads
to desirable multiscaling functions satisfying simultaneously important
wavelet properties – symmetry, smoothness, short support, and
approximation order. There exist different spectral factorization
methods
[
20
,
49
,
48
,
7
,
43
,
42
]
, but none leads
to exact values of spectral factors. Factorization is a key step in the
construction of wavelet or multiwavelet filter banks with high precision
coefficients. This approach was first used in Cooklev’s PhD Thesis

[
12
]
for the construction of orthogonal multiwavelet filter banks.
In that application, the smoothness of the multiwavelet is improved if
the determinant of
P(z)
has a higher-order zero at
z=-1
(see

[
45
]
).

Most of the numerical methods for MSF proposed in the past only work in
the nonsingular case, that is, the case where
|P(z)|
does not vanish
on
\,{\mathbb{T}}
. Bauer’s method, as presented in Youla and Kazanjian

[
49
]
, is based on the Cholesky decomposition of a certain
semi-infinite block Toeplitz matrix. It continues to work even in the
singular case. However, it converges very slowly in this case, and the
result has reduced accuracy.

Our goal in this paper is to present modifications of Bauer’s method
that speed up convergence in the singular case, and even produce exact
solutions in some simple situations. Our work can be understood as an
extension of Youla and Kazanjian’s theoretical work, providing new
results on convergence speed and symbolic solution. Both the numerical
and symbolic methods are based on solving a nonlinear matrix equation
(NME) introduced below.

We have the following main contributions:

•

We construct algorithms for finding spectral factorizations of
singular and nonsingular scalar and matrix polynomials; one algorithm
produces exact values of spectral factors for low order scalar and
matrix polynomials, and two other algorithms produce numerical
approximations (based on fixed point iteration (FPI) or Newton’s
method);

•

We consider for the first time in detail the convergence speed and
accuracy in the numerical algorithms;

•

We obtain a new orthogonal multiwavelet filter from factoring the
para-Hermitian polynomial matrix of the orthogonal Chui-Lian
multiscaling function
[
11
]
.

The outline of this paper is as follows.

In section
2
, we give details on Bauer’s method and the
proposed modifications, and introduce the NME. In section
3

we review previous results on matrix equations. In section

4
, we discuss the details, convergence rates and
computational complexity of the numerical methods we use. In section

5
, we present some examples and verify the theoretical
convergence results from section
4
. Some
conclusions are presented in section
6
. Some
technical results have been moved to an appendix, so as not to interrupt
the discussion in the main paper.

Bauer’s Method for Matrix Spectral Factorization

A stable and self-correcting iteration for spectral factorization of a
para-Hermitian positive definite polynomial was proposed by F. Bauer in

[
5
,
6
]
. Youla and Kazanjian
[

extended this to the

Let

be a positive semidefinite para-Hermitian polynomial matrix
of degree

is equivalent to the
fact that the infinite block banded matrix

{\mathcal{P}}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&P_{-m}&P_{-m+1}&\cdots&P_{m-1}&P_{m}\\
&&P_{-m}&P_{-m+1}&\cdots&P_{m-1}&P_{m}\\

can be factored as
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
, where

banded and lower triangular:

{\mathcal{H}}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&H_{m}&\cdots&H_{1}&H_{0}\\
&&H_{m}&\cdots&H_{1}&H_{0}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
\end{bmatrix}

(2)

Bauer considered the Cholesky factorization of the block banded Toeplitz
matrix
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
obtained by selecting a
(n+1)\times(n+1)
submatrix
of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Thus,
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}={\mathcal{H}}_{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{*}
, where

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}&P_{1}&\cdots&P_{m}&\\
P_{-1}&P_{0}&P_{1}&\cdots&P_{m}&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
P_{-m}&&&\ddots&&&P_{m}\\
&\ddots&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&P_{1}\\
&&&P_{-m}&\cdots&P_{-1}&P_{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{0}^{(0)}&\\[3.0pt]
H_{1}^{(1)}&H_{0}^{(1)}&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&&\\
H_{m}^{(m)}&&&H_{0}^{(m)}&\\
&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&H_{m}^{(n)}&\cdots&H_{1}^{(n)}&H_{0}^{(n)}\end{bmatrix}

(3)

2.1
Fast Bauer’s Method (FBM)

We reduce the general case to the case
m=1
, which means

P(z)=P_{-1}z^{-1}+P_{0}+P_{1}z

H(z)=H_{0}+H_{1}z^{-1}
. This
can be achieved by replacing the original coefficients with block
matrices. For

\hat{P}_{-1}=\hat{P}_{1}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{3}^{*}&P_{2}^{*}&P_{1}^{*}\\[3.0pt]
0&P_{3}^{*}&P_{2}^{*}\\[3.0pt]
0&0&P_{3}^{*}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\hat{P}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}&P_{1}&P_{2}\\[2.0pt]
P_{1}^{*}&P_{0}&P_{1}&\\[2.0pt]
P_{2}^{*}&P_{1}^{*}&P_{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\hat{P}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{3}&0&0\\
P_{2}&P_{3}&0\\
P_{1}&P_{2}&P_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

The resulting coefficients
\hat{H}_{0}
,
\hat{H}_{1}

\hat{H}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{3}&H_{2}&H_{1}\\
0&H_{3}&H_{2}\\
0&0&H_{3}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\hat{H}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{0}&0&0\\
H_{1}&H_{0}&0\\

H_{2}
,

(4)

3
The Nonlinear Matrix Equation

Our goal is to find a solution of the fixed point problem
(
5
) in the MSF setting. That is, the coefficients

P_{0}
and
P_{1}
come from a para-Hermitian matrix polynomial
P(z)

which is positive semidefinite on the unit circle. We first consider
some existence and uniqueness results for the general NME

X=Q-A^{*}X^{-1}A,

(7)

where
A
,
Q\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{d\times d}
,
Q>0
, and then specialize to our
case of interest.

Equation (
7
) can be rephrased into two related forms. Some
results in the literature are stated for one of the other forms; in this
paper we present the equivalent results for the standard NME.

One variation is the simplified form

\tilde{X}=I-\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{X}^{-1}\tilde{A},

(8)

where
I
is the identity matrix. The reduction to this form is achieved
by factoring

Q=M^{*}M

and multiplying (
7
) from the left by
M^{-*}
and from the
right by
M^{-1}
. After sorting out the terms and defining

\tilde{X}=M^{-*}XM^{-1},\qquad\tilde{A}=M^{-*}AM^{-1}

(9)

this becomes (
8
). It is easy to verify that
\tilde{X}>0

if and only if
X>0
.

M
could be a Cholesky factor of
Q
, or we could choose
M=Q^{1/2}

as in
[
39
]
, in which case
M=M^{*}
.

The other variation is the modified NME

X=Q+A^{*}X^{-1}A.

(10)

As explained in
[
39
]
, the solution
X
of (
10
) can
be computed as

X=Y-AQ^{-1}A^{*}

from the solution
Y
of the standard NME

Y=R-B^{*}Y^{-1}B,

where
B=AQ^{-1}A
,
R=Q+A^{*}Q^{-1}A+AQ^{-1}A^{*}
.

Various kinds of nonlinear matrix equations have received much attention
in the literature
[
52
,
37
,
17
,
14
,
30
,
8
,
15
,
38
,
51
]
. Equations (
7
) and
(
10
) were studied in
[
18
,
17
]
, in control theory

[
2
,
21
]
and in dynamic programming and statistics

[
50
]
. The simplified NME (
8
) plays a key role in
various problems of synthesis of control systems, and can also be used
to parameterize the set of stabilizing controllers
[
1
]
.

If a positive definite solution exists, the NME has maximal and minimal
solutions
X_{+}
,
X_{-}
with

0<X_{-}\leq X\leq X_{+}

for any positive definite solution
X
.

The maximal solution
X_{+}
is the unique stabilizing or weakly
stabilizing solution. A solution
X
of NME is called
stabilizing

if all the eigenvalues of the pencil
\lambda X+A
(or equivalently
the eigenvalues of the
closed-loop matrix

X^{-1}A
) are inside
the unit circle. It is
weakly stabilizing
if the eigenvalues are
inside or on the unit circle.

For real matrices, the NME (
7
) is also a special case of the

Generalized Discrete Time Algebraic Riccati Equation
(GDARE)

[
3
]

E^{T}XE=D^{T}XD-(D^{T}XB+A^{T})(B^{T}XB+R)^{-1}(D^{T}XB+A^{T})^{T}+C^{T}QC

(11)

with
R=D=0
,
C=E=B=I
. The matrix

is a real symmetric
matrix to be found. GDARE plays an important role in the design of
linear controllers
[
36
,
40
]
.

Some necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a positive
definite solution of (
7
) and (
8
) are given in

[
17
]
.
Necessary conditions
are

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{A})=\rho(AQ^{-1})\leq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}^{*})=\rho((A+A^{*})Q^{-1})\leq 1

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{A}-\tilde{A}^{*})=\rho((A-A^{*})Q^{-1})\leq 1.

A
sufficient condition
is

\|\tilde{A}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\|A^{*}Q^{-1}AQ^{-1}\|_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

In our MSF setting, the closed-loop matrix is
X^{-1}P_{1}
. The matrix
form of the Riesz-Fejér lemma
[
19
,
26
]
guarantees the
existence of a positive definite solution. Thus, the necessary

examples in section
5
satisfy the sufficient condition.

The same existence result is stated in
[
27
, Chapter

. Theorem 3.5 in
[

states that this solution is
unique if and only if in addition all zeros of

.

4
Numerical Solution of the Nonlinear Matrix Equation

In this section, we give details on the numerical solution of
eq. (
5
), which is Step 1 in Algorithm 1, with
particular attention to the singular case.

Previous algorithms

[
39
,
21
,
18
,
51
,
17
,
16
,
28
,
10
]

for solving NMEs require additional conditions and cannot solve spectral
factorization problems with zeroes on the unit circle very well.

We consider three different approaches: fixed point iteration and
Newton’s method, and numerical methods based on the eigenvalues of the
closed-loop matrix for GDARE. These methods differ in convergence speed
and accuracy, but give identical results when they work.

In addition, Matlab has a built-in nonlinear equation solver called
fsolve
. In numerical experiments, the results of using this routine
to solve the NME were very similar to those of Newton’s method. We would
expect the same to be true of other similar solvers. We will not pursue
this approach further.

4.1
Numerical Analysis Background

We consider the numerical solution of the equation

{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{0}},

where
{\mathbf{f}}{:}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It is assumed that a solution
\overline{\mathbf{x}}

exists, so
{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{0}}
. It is also assumed that
{\mathbf{f}}

has sufficiently many continuous derivatives in a neighborhood of

\overline{\mathbf{x}}
.

We study stationary iterative methods of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{initial guess},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}),

where
{\mathbf{F}}
is derived from
{\mathbf{f}}
in some fashion.

We define the error in
{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}
as

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}=\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}\|

for some norm. In our numerical examples in section
5

we used the 2-norm, but other norms will give comparable results.

By definition, the iteration converges if and only if

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Convergence is called

linear
if

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}\leq c\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}

for some
c<1
. It is called
quadratic
if

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}\leq c\left[\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}\right]^{2}

for some
c
. If the iteration converges, but

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}}=1

convergence is
sublinear
. Sublinear convergence can be extremely
slow; the error could behave like
1/n
, or even
1/\log n
. Typically,
the number of iterations necessary to achieve a given accuracy grows
exponentially, or even faster, with the number of digits of accuracy.

The residual is defined as

\epsilon_{\mathbf{f}}^{(n)}=\|{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)})\|=\|{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)})\|.

The beginning of the Taylor expansion of
{\mathbf{f}}
around
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
is

{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta{\mathbf{x}})\approx{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\Delta{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})(\Delta{\mathbf{x}},\Delta{\mathbf{x}})=D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\Delta{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})(\Delta{\mathbf{x}},\Delta{\mathbf{x}})

(12)

We call the problem
nonsingular
if the derivative matrix

D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})
is invertible, otherwise
singular
.

In the nonsingular case we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\approx D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\Delta{\mathbf{x}}\

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta{\mathbf{x}})\|

\displaystyle\approx\|D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\|\,\|\Delta{\mathbf{x}}\|=O(\|\Delta{\mathbf{x}}\|),

so error and residual are of the same order of magnitude. We expect that
we can compute both
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta{\mathbf{x}})
to
machine accuracy, which is about 15 or 16 decimals.

In the scalar singular case we have
f^{\prime}(\overline{x})=0
, therefore

\displaystyle f(\overline{x}+\Delta x)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\overline{x})(\Delta x)^{2},

\displaystyle\left|f(\overline{x}+\Delta x)\right|

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\left|f^{\prime\prime}(\overline{x})\right|\,(\Delta x)^{2}=O((\Delta x)^{2}).

This means that the residual is approximately the square of the error.

If
|\Delta x|=10^{-8}
, then
f(x+\Delta x)\approx 10^{-16}
, which
is machine accuracy. Since a relative change in
f(x)
below
10^{-16}

is not detectable, we can only compute
\overline{x}
to half the available
accuracy, or about 8 decimals. This is illustrated in Fig.
1
.
For a zero of order
p
, the available accuracy in
\overline{x}
reduces to

1/p
of the available decimals.

Figure 1:
For
|\Delta x|=10^{-8}
, the residual
|f(\overline{x}+\Delta x)|\approx 10^{-16}
. The function values within
10^{-8}
of

\overline{x}
cannot be distinguished.

In the higher-dimensional singular case the situation is similar. If

D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})
is singular, it has eigenvectors to eigenvalue 0. A
change of order
|\Delta x|
in the direction of one of these
eigenvectors produces a residual of order
|\Delta x|^{2}
. Thus, the
component of the overall error in the nullspace, and therefore the size
of the overall error, cannot be determined to more than half the
available accuracy.

4.2
Matrix-Valued Functions

The standard theory in subsection
4.1
covers the solution
of equations of the form
{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{0}}
, where

{\mathbf{f}}{:}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Eq. (
5
) is actually of the
form

f(X)=X-P_{0}+P_{1}^{*}X^{-1}P_{1}=0,

where
f
maps
\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
to
\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
.

The connection between the two cases is the
vec
operation
[
44
]
,
which maps a matrix into a vector by stacking the columns on top of each
other. We denote the inverse operation by
\mbox{vec}^{-1}
, which maps a
vector of length
d^{2}
into a
d\times d
matrix.

The matrix
X
and matrix-valued function
f
in
f(X)=0
correspond
to the vector
{\mathbf{x}}
and vector-valued function
{\mathbf{f}}
via

{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})=\mbox{vec}[f(\mbox{vec}^{-1}{\mathbf{x}})]={\mathbf{0}}.

(13)

The norms of
X
and
{\mathbf{x}}=\mbox{vec}(X)
are comparable. In particular,
the 2-norm of
\mbox{vec}(X)
is identical to the Frobenius norm of
X
. Thus,
our concepts of error and residual are unchanged.

4.3
Fixed Point Iteration

Fixed point iteration is used to solve equations of the form

{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})
. The solution
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
satisfies

\overline{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})
. This is equivalent to

{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{0}}
.

The iteration is

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{initial guess},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}).

Bauer’s method is identical to iteration (
4
), which is fixed
point iteration applied to (
5
), with

g(X)=P_{0}-P_{1}^{*}X^{-1}P_{1}.

(14)

Implementing Bauer’s method as a special case of fixed point iteration
does not affect the convergence speed or the result, but it is much more
efficient than the original algorithm, which forms large matrices.

4.3.1
Convergence in the Nonsingular Case

As in the Taylor expansion (
12
), we find that

{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)})-{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\approx D{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}).

This shows that if the spectral radius of the derivative matrix

\rho(D{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}))
is less than 1, the iteration converges
linearly. More precisely, if
\rho(D{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}))\leq c<1
in a
neighborhood of
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
, then

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}\leq c\,\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}.

If
\rho(D{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}))>1
, the iteration diverges.

4.3.2
Convergence in the Singular Case

In the singular case
\rho(D{\mathbf{g}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}))=1
the iteration may or
may not converge. For example, in the simple scalar case
g(x)=x^{2}/2

with
g^{\prime}(2)=1
the iteration does not converge to the solution

\overline{x}=2
. In the case
g(x)=2-(1/x)
with
g^{\prime}(1)=1
, which is
analyzed in detail below, the iteration does converge to
\overline{x}=1
.

4.3.3
Convergence for Singular MSF

If the iteration function
{\mathbf{g}}
comes from a singular MSF we have
additional results that do not apply to general
{\mathbf{g}}
.

Youla/Kazanjian
[
49
]
proved that the iteration always
converges. In
[
18
, Algorithm 4.1]
it is proved that it
converges to the maximal solution
X_{+}
.

In the simple scalar example 2 below we can prove that the convergence
speed is sublinear, with
\epsilon^{(n)}
of order
1/n
. This appears
to be true also for the other singular examples presented below, but
we have no proofs.

4.4
Newton’s Method

In Newton’s method, we work directly on the equation
{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{0}}
. The iteration is

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mbox{initial guess},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}-[D\,{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)})]^{-1}{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}).

4.4.1
Implementation

The iteration function
{\mathbf{f}}
is given in (
13
), but it is not
practical to calculate the derivative in this form. It is easier to
derive Newton’s method for the matrix setting

f(X)=X+P_{1}^{*}X^{-1}P_{1}-P_{0}=0,

(15)

directly.

As in eq. (
23
) in appendix A.2. we have

(X+\Delta X)^{-1}\approx X^{-1}-X^{-1}(\Delta X)X^{-1},

which leads to

\begin{split}f(X+\Delta X)&=(X+\Delta X)+P_{1}^{*}(X+\Delta X)^{-1}P_{1}-P_{0}\\
&\approx(X+\Delta X)+P_{1}^{*}\left[X^{-1}-X^{-1}(\Delta X)X^{-1}\right]P_{1}-P_{0}\\
&=f(X)+\Delta X-P_{1}^{*}X^{-1}(\Delta X)X^{-1}P_{1}.\end{split}

(16)

The expression on the right is a first-order approximation to

f(X+\Delta X)
, and Newton’s method is based on setting this equal to
zero. Thus, we compute
\Delta X
from

\Delta X-P_{1}^{*}X^{-1}(\Delta X)X^{-1}P_{1}=-f(X)

(17)

and use that to update
X
.

For the implementation, we use the
vec
operation
[
44
]
. We
recall that for matrices
A
,
B
,
C

\mbox{vec}(ABC)=(C^{T}\otimes A)\,\mbox{vec}(B),

(18)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product. Note that
C^{T}
is the real
transpose, even if
C
is a complex matrix. Eq. (
17
) is
evaluated as

\left[I-(P_{1}^{*}X^{-1})\otimes(P_{1}^{*}X^{-1})\right]\mbox{vec}(\Delta X)=-\mbox{vec}(f(X)).

Using the current value
X^{(n)}
, we compute the approximate correction

\Delta X^{(n)}
, and set
X^{(n+1)}=X^{(n)}+\Delta X^{(n)}
.

4.4.2
Convergence in the Nonsingular Case

It is well known that in the nonsingular case the Newton iteration
converges quadratically to
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
. That is,

\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)}\leq c\,[\epsilon_{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)}]^{2}.

4.4.3
Convergence in the Singular Case

In the scalar singular case convergence becomes linear. If

f^{\prime}(\overline{x})=0
, but
f^{\prime\prime}(\overline{x})\neq 0
, the convergence factor is
1/2. More generally, if all derivatives up to order
p
vanish, but not

f^{(p+1)}(\overline{x})
, the convergence factor is
p/(p+1)
.

In the matrix singular case some similar results are known. We know that
the derivative matrix has an eigenvalue 0. If this eigenvalue is
nondegenerate (that is, the algebraic and geometric multiplicities are
the same), then
\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}=M\oplus N
, where
M
and
N
are nullspace and
range of
D\,{\mathbf{f}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})
, respectively. The projection of the error
onto
M
converges linearly with factor
1/2
(or
p/(p+1)
in the case
of higher singularities), the projection onto
N
converges
quadratically. Overall, the convergence is linear. See
[
13
]
for
the cases
p=1
and
p=2
, and
[
22
]
for general
p
.

If the zero eigenvalue is degenerate, the iteration may diverge or
behave chaotically
[
22
]
.

4.4.4
Convergence for Singular MSF

It is shown in
[
23
,
24
]
that in the case of a NME arising from
MSF the convergence behavior can be analyzed in terms of the spectrum of
the closed-loop matrix
X_{+}^{-1}P_{1}
, rather than the derivative
matrix. In the nonsingular case, the closed-loop matrix has all
eigenvalues inside the unit circle, and convergence is quadratic. In the
singular case there is an eigenvalue of 1. If this eigenvalue is
nondegenerate, convergence is linear with factor 1/2 (or more generally

p/(p+1))
.

It is observed in
[
23
,
24
]
and verified with multiple
numerical experiments that if the eigenvalue 1 of the closed-loop matrix
has a longest Jordan chain of length
p
, convergence of Newton’s method
is linear with factor
2^{-1/p}
. However, the author of these papers
has no proof of this, nor is he aware of any proofs in the literature

[
25
]
.

4.5
GDARE

Since the NME is a special case of GDARE (
11
), algorithms
for solving GDARE could be applied. We concentrate on one which is not
based on iterative methods similar to those presented above,

Routine
IDARE
in Matlab (called
DARE
in earlier versions)
uses a non-iterative approach. The algorithm computes the eigenvalues of a
certain augmented matrix pencil and divides them into two groups, the
eigenvalues inside and outside the unit circle. In the singular case,
some or all eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and are perturbed
towards the inside or outside by roundoff error. Sometimes this results
in a usable splitting, often it does not.

Convergence speed is not an issue here since there is no iteration
(other than internally to compute eigenvalues). Accuracy considerations
are the same as for fixed point iteration and Newton’s method, since
these are features of the underlying problem, not a particular algorithm.

4.6
Singularity

A matrix spectral factorization problem is called singular if the
determinant
\left|P(z)\right|
has a zero on the unit circle

\,{\mathbb{T}}
. Fixed point iteration is singular if the derivative matrix at the
solution has spectral radius
\rho=1
. Newton’s method is singular if
the derivative matrix at the solution is singular. In appendix A.3. we
show that these concepts are identical.

The fixed point iteration in example 2 is

x^{(n+1)}=2-\frac{1}{x^{(n)}},

which should converge to
\overline{x}=1
. The error

\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}=x^{(n)}-1
satisfies

\epsilon_{H}^{(n+1)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}}{\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}+1}.

This is one-sided convergence: if
\epsilon_{H}^{(k)}>0
for some
k
,
then
\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}>0
for all
n>k
, and the errors are monotone
decreasing. In our case
x^{(0)}=2
,
e^{(0)}=1
, so this is
satisfied.

Some algebra shows

\epsilon_{H}^{(1)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+1},\qquad\epsilon_{H}^{(2)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{2\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+1},\quad\cdots\quad\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{n\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+1},

so

\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}\approx\frac{1}{n},\qquad\ln\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}\approx-\ln n.

This is sublinear convergence. A plot of
\ln\epsilon_{H}
versus
\ln n

should be a line with slope
(-1)
. This is illustrated in
fig.

(b).

We can also calculate that

H_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{P_{1}}{H_{0}^{(n)}}=\frac{1}{1+\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}}\approx 1-\epsilon_{H}^{(n)},

so the residual in
P_{0}
is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=[H_{0}^{(n)}]^{2}+[H_{1}^{(n)}]^{2}-P_{0}

\displaystyle\approx(1+\epsilon_{H}^{(n)})^{2}+(1-\epsilon_{H}^{(n)})^{2}-2=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{2}).

A plot of
\ln\epsilon_{P}
versus
\ln n
should be a line with slope

(-2)
, which is illustrated in fig.
2
(a).

5.4.3
The Matrix Singular Case

In example 3, the determinant has double zeros on
\,{\mathbb{T}}
. The achievable
accuracy for the spectral factors, for both fixed point iteration and
Newton’s method, is approximately
10^{-8}
, which is half the number of
available digits. Examples 4–6 have quadruple zeros. The convergence
stalls near accuracy
10^{-4}
. Example 7 has a tenfold zero. The
convergence stalls near accuracy
10^{-1}
or
10^{-2}
. These are all
the same results we expect in the scalar case.

For fixed point iteration, we observe the same kind of sublinear
convergence as in the scalar example:

\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}=O(\frac{1}{n}),\quad\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{2}).

In some cases we see strange effects if we try to push beyond the point
of highest accuracy. Convergence becomes erratic, and it appears that

\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{3})
or even

\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{4})
. These numerical
instabilities are due to ill-conditioning of the underlying problem and
will occur for any algorithm.

5.5.2
The Scalar Singular Case

Newton’s method in the scalar case is known to converge linearly with
factor
p/(p+1)
if
f(x)
has a
p
-fold zero at
\overline{x}
. Moreover,
the residual is the
p^{th}
power of the error, so we can only compute
the solution to
1/p
times the available digits.

This is easy to verify directly in example 2. Equation (
15
) in
this case becomes

f(x)=x-p_{0}+p_{1}^{*}x^{-1}p_{1}=x-2+1\cdot x^{-1}\cdot 1=0,

so

f(x)=x-2+\frac{1}{x}.

This leads to

x^{(n+1)}=2-\frac{2}{x^{(n)}+1},\qquad\epsilon_{H}^{(n+1)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}}{\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}+2}.

Some algebra gives

\epsilon_{H}^{(1)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+2},\qquad\epsilon_{H}^{(2)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{3\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+4},\qquad\epsilon_{H}^{(3)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{7\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+8},\quad\cdots\quad\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}}{(2^{n}-1)\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}+2^{n}},

so

\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}\approx 2^{-n}\epsilon_{H}^{(0)}.

As in the case of fixed point iteration, we can verify directly that

\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{2})
.

If we plot
\ln(\epsilon_{H})
versus
n
, this should be approximately a
straight line with a slope of
(-\ln 2)
. A plot of
\ln(\epsilon_{P})

should be a straight line with slope
(-2\ln 2)
. This is illustrated
in fig.
3
(a) and (b). Notice how
\epsilon_{H}
stalls
around
10^{-8}
.

5.5.3
The Matrix Singular Case

For Newton’s method, we observe

\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}\approx c^{n}\epsilon_{0}^{(n)},\quad\epsilon_{P}^{(n)}=O([\epsilon_{H}^{(n)}]^{2}).

The behavior corresponds with the convergence results quoted in
subsection
4.4.3
.

In example 3,
c=1/2
, since the eigenvalue 1 of the closed-loop
matrix is nondegenerate. In examples 4–6,
c=1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.7
,
since the eigenvalue 1 has a Jordan chain of length 2. In example 7,
c=1/\sqrt[5]{2}\approx 0.87
, since the longest Jordan chain has length
5.

Convergence becomes even more erratic than for fixed point iteration if
you try to push beyond the highest achievable accuracy.

Another numerical problem arises in example 7. For
k>64
the matrix

P^{(k)}
is not positive definite any more, due to roundoff error; the
algorithm cannot be continued past this point. After
k=40
, the
residual error
\epsilon_{P}
decreases, while the error
\epsilon_{H}

increases and ends up near
10^{-2}
. This shows the strong influence of
a high order zero on the precision of the spectral factors.

6
Conclusion

This paper provides a broad perspective of improvements on Bauer’s
method for matrix spectral factorization, especially in the singular
case. We introduced two fast numerical algorithms, and a symbolic
algorithm which produces exact factors for some low-order polynomial
matrices.

The fixed point iteration algorithm is equivalent to Bauer’s method but
avoids forming large matrices. The Newton’s method algorithm with its
quadratic convergence is significantly faster. The symbolic algorithm
allows exact solutions for the spectral density of a second order
stochastic process and low-order polynomial matrices in MIMO control
theory applications.

By applying matrix spectral factorization to the well–known CL product
filter we derive a new orthogonal multiscaling function with exact
coefficients, which leads to a new multiwavelet filter bank which
possesses better Sobolev smoothness and coding gain than the original CL
multiwavelet filter bank. Based on its advantages, in a forthcoming
work the obtained multiwavelets will be considered in different
applications of numerical mathematics, and in the context of signal or
image processing.

As the results for different six Matlab versions in Table

4
show, it is often impossible to solve scalar and
matrix Riccati equations using the built-in solvers IDARE (DARE) when
the eigenvalues of the matrix pencil are on the unit circle.
