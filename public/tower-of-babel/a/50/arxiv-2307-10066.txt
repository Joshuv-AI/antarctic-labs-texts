The varentropy criterion is sharp on expanders
Justin Salez

Abstract
The cutoff phenomenon is an abrupt transition from out of equilibrium to equilibrium undergone by certain Markov processes in the limit where the size of the state space tends to infinity: instead of decaying gradually over time, their distance to equilibrium remains close to the maximal value for a while and suddenly drops to zero as the time parameter reaches a critical threshold. Despite the accumulation of many examples, this phenomenon is still far from being understood, and identifying the general conditions that trigger it has become one of the biggest challenges in the quantitative analysis of finite Markov chains. Very recently, the author proposed a general sufficient condition for the occurrence of a cutoff, based on a certain information-theoretical statistics called varentropy . In the present paper, we demonstrate the sharpness of this approach by showing that the cutoff phenomenon is actually equivalent to the varentropy criterion for all sparse, fast-mixing chains. Reversibility is not required.



1
Introduction

The cutoff phenomenon is a dynamical phase transition which is now believed to be universal among high-dimensional, fast-mixing Markov chains: roughly speaking, the system under consideration abruptly moves from being nearly singular to equilibrium to being statistically indistinguishable from equilibrium when the time parameter reaches a critical value. We shall here only recall the necessary definitions, and refer the reader to the introductory book
[
18
, Chapter 18]
or the recent paper
[
25
]
for a more detailed account as well as many references.

1.1
The cutoff phenomenon

Consider a stochastic matrix
K
on a finite set
\mathcal{X}
, and write
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for the corresponding continuous-time semi-group, defined for all times
t\geq 0
and states
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
by

\displaystyle P_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle e^{-t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{n}(x,y)t^{n}}{n!}.

Assuming that
K
is irreducible, the general theory guarantees that
P_{t}(x,y)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\pi(y)
, where
\pi
is the unique probability vector on
\mathcal{X}
solving the stationarity equation
\pi K=\pi
. A standard way to quantify this convergence consists in measuring the time it takes for the
worst-case total variation distance
to drop below a given threshold
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle t_{\textsc{mix}}(\varepsilon)\ :=\ \min\left\{t\geq 0\colon d_{\textsc{tv}}(t)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{tv}}(t):=\max_{o\in\mathcal{X}}d_{\textsc{tv}}(P_{t}(o,\cdot),\pi).

This quantity is known as the
mixing time
of the process, and understanding how it depends on the underlying dynamics and on the precision
\varepsilon
is an important problem with numerous applications. This question becomes particularly relevant when the number of states is large, and one is thus naturally led to consider a
sequence
of stochastic matrices
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
whose dimensions tend to infinity, and to examine the asymptotic behavior of their mixing times
t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
as
n\to\infty
. In many situations, a remarkable phase transition known as a
cutoff
has been observed: instead of decaying gradually from
1
to
0
as one could reasonably expect, the distance to equilibrium
t\mapsto d_{\textsc{tv}}^{(n)}(t)
approaches a step function as
n\to\infty
. Equivalently, its inverse
\varepsilon\mapsto t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
becomes asymptotically constant, as illustrated on Figure
1
.

(Cutoff phenomenon)
.

The sequence
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is said to exhibit a cutoff if

\displaystyle\forall\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1),\qquad\frac{t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}{t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}

\displaystyle 1.

The name
cutoff
was coined in 1986 by D. Aldous and P. Diaconis
[
3
]
, but the phenomenon itself was actually discovered in 1981 by P. Diaconis and M. Shahshahani
[
12
]
, and several instances of it were collected under the generic name
abrupt switch
in lecture notes published by D. Aldous in 1983
[
1
]
. Further historical examples can be found in the 1996 survey paper
The cutoff phenomenon in finite Markov chains
, by P. Diaconis
[
11
]
. Since then, cutoff phenomena have been observed in a broad variety of contexts, including birth and death chains, random walks on finite groups, high-temperature spin glasses, interactive particle systems, or random walks on various models of sparse random graphs.

Unfortunately, the existing proofs essentially all consist in bounding
t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
from above and below by explicit quantities which lie within a factor
1+o(1)
from each other and are asymptotically independent of
\varepsilon
. This is of course a notoriously difficult and model-specific task, which can only be carried out on very structured examples, and which does not bring any conceptual insight as to why a sharp transition actually occurs. Identifying the general conditions that trigger the cutoff phenomenon has become one of the biggest challenges in the quantitative analysis of finite Markov chains. Very recently, a new approach to this question was proposed in
[
25
]
, based on the estimation of a certain information-theoretical statistics called
varentropy
.

1.2
The varentropy criterion

Let us start by recalling a more classical definition: the
relative entropy
(or Kullback-Leibler divergence) of a probability measure
\mu
on our reference space
(\mathcal{X},\pi)
is given by

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[\log\frac{\mu}{\pi}\right]\ =\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(x)\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}.

This famous information-theoretic statistics provides an upper bound on the total-variation distance
d_{\textsc{tv}}(\mu,\pi)
, by virtue of the Csiszár-Kullback-Pinsker Inequality. Moreover, its evolution under the semi-group
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be controlled in a systematic way by establishing an appropriate Log-Sobolev Inequality
[
10
]
, or its modified version
[
7
]
. The combination of those two simple observations is at the origin of some of the most powerful bounds on mixing times (see the textbook
[
22
]
). In order to quantify the sharpness of the transition to equilibrium, the author proposed in
[
25
]
to investigate the evolution of a suitable
second-order
version of relative entropy, obtained by replacing the mean by a variance:

\displaystyle V_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mu}\left(\log\frac{\mu}{\pi}\right)\ =\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(x)\left(\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}-d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu,\pi)\right)^{2}.

Because it measures the dispersion of information around the entropy, this natural statistics is called
varentropy
. It appeared a decade ago in the completely different context of optimal data compression, to quantify the error in the celebrated
Asymptotic Equipartition Property

[
17
]
. However, its relevance for cutoff – embodied in Corollary
1
below – was discovered only very recently. More precisely, let us define the
worst-case varentropy
of our Markov chain at any given time
t\geq 0
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{\textsc{kl}}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{o\in\mathcal{X}}V_{\textsc{kl}}\left(P_{t}(o,\cdot),\pi\right).

Let also
\gamma=\gamma(K)
denote the
Poincaré constant
of the chain, which is well known to coincide with the spectral gap of the reversibilized transition matrix
(K+K^{\star})/2
. It is perhaps worth mentioning that this fundamental parameter is, unlike many others, extremely well understood: its order of magnitude is known in many concrete models (see
[
22
]
for details).

Theorem 1

(Width of the mixing window
[
25
]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
,

\displaystyle t_{\textsc{mix}}(\varepsilon)-t_{\textsc{mix}}(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\gamma\varepsilon^{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{V_{\textsc{kl}}(t_{\textsc{mix}}(1-\varepsilon))}\right).

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1
constitutes the very first general quantitative estimate on the width of the mixing window.
Since the occurrence of a cutoff is just the assertion that this width is asymptotically negligible compared to the position of the window along the time axis, we readily obtain the following general criterion for cutoff.

Corollary 1

(Varentropy criterion)
.

A sufficient condition for
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
to exhibit cutoff is

\displaystyle\gamma(K_{n})\times t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle 1+\sqrt{V_{\textsc{kl}}^{(n)}(t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon))},

(1)

for each fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, where
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
means that the ratio
a_{n}/b_{n}
tends to
+\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

In the present form, Corollary
1
is much more a starting point than a definitive answer to our main problem. Indeed, the varentropy term appearing on the right-hand side is a new and highly non-trivial statistics, whose estimation remains entirely to be developed before it can be effectively used to explain and predict cutoff. A first step in that direction was made in
[
25
]
, where a simple and naive estimate on the varentropy function
t\mapsto V_{\textsc{kl}}(t)
was established for all Markov chains with non-negative curvature, leading to a unified proof of cutoff for a broad family of models. This successful first application raises hopes that the varentropy criterion could constitute the long-sought common mechanism underlying all cutoff phenomena. The purpose of this paper is to provide a rigorous support to this claim.

1.3
Main result

In the present paper, we demonstrate the sharpness of the varentropy approach by showing that the cutoff phenomenon is actually
equivalent
to the varentropy criterion for all
sparse
and
fast-mixing
chains. We emphasize that reversibility is not needed here: we shall only require that the support of
K
(i.e., the set of allowed transitions) is symmetric:

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},\qquad K(x,y)>0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle K(y,x)>0.

(2)

We recall that
\gamma=\gamma(K)
denotes the Poincaré constant of
K
, and we define
\delta=\delta(K)
as the smallest non-zero entry of
K
. This simple parameter controls the sparsity of the chain, since each row of the stochastic matrix
K
can not have more than
1/\delta(K)
non-zero entries.

Theorem 2

(Sharpness of the varentropy criterion)
.

Let
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be any sequence of transition matrices with symmetric supports and satisfying the following conditions:

A1.

Sparsity:
\inf_{n\geq 1}\delta(K_{n})>0
.

A2.

Expansion:
\inf_{n\geq 1}\gamma(K_{n})>0
.

Then, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
exhibits cutoff if and only if the varentropy criterion (
1
) holds.

Remark 1

(Cheeger inequalities)
.

The
isoperimetric constant
of the chain is defined as

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min_{\emptyset\subsetneq A\subsetneq\mathcal{X}}\left\{\frac{\vec{\pi}(A\times A^{c})}{\pi(A)\wedge\pi(A^{c})}\right\},

where
\vec{\pi}(x,y):=\pi(x)K(x,y)
is the stationary flow on
\mathcal{X}^{2}
. Cheeger inequalities state that

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi^{2}}{2}\ \leq

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\ 2\Phi.

Consequently, Assumption A2 is equivalent to
\inf_{n\geq 1}\Phi(K_{n})>0
, hence the name
expansion
.

Before we dive into the proof, let us briefly discuss the emblematic case of simple random walk on a finite undirected graph
G=(\mathcal{X},E)
, which corresponds to the transition matrix

\displaystyle K(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\deg(x)}&\textrm{if }\{x,y\}\in E\\
0&\textrm{else}.\end{array}\right.

Note that the symmetry condition (
2
) is automatically fulfilled here, and that
\delta(K)
is simply the inverse of the maximum vertex degree. Sequences of graphs whose transition matrices
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfy Assumptions A1 and A2 are famously known as
expanders
. Those remarkable graphs enjoy nearly as good connectivity properties as complete graphs, but at a much lower cost in terms of edges. Consequently, they have found numerous practical applications, some of which are described in the beautiful survey paper
[
16
]
by S. Hoory, N. Linial and A. Wigderson. Understanding when they exhibit cutoff is arguably one of the most famous open problems in the field (see
[
24
, Open Question 34]
or
[
18
, Question 5]
), but to the best of our knowledge, no progress has been recorded beyond the extreme case of Ramanujan graphs
[
19
,
13
,
23
,
9
]
or the very special setup of random environments
[
20
,
6
,
5
,
8
,
4
,
14
,
9
,
15
]
. Our main result reduces this general problem to a varentropy estimate.

Corollary 2

(Expanders)
.

An expander sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
exhibits cutoff if and only if

\displaystyle\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1),\qquad V_{\textsc{kl}}^{(n)}\left(t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle(\log|G_{n}|)^{2}.

We hope that this simple characterization will motivate the development of a general theory for estimating the varentropy of Markov chains. In particular, we would like to advertise the following fascinating conjecture, which was explicitly raised by D. Levin and Y. Peres
[
18
, Question 5]
.

Conjecture 1

(Transitive expanders)
.

All vertex-transitive expanders exhibit cutoff.

We note that this is false without vertex-transitivity
[
21
]
. Let us perhaps here recall that a graph
G=(\mathcal{X},E)
is
vertex-transitive
if for any vertices
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, there is an edge-preserving bijection
\phi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
that maps
x
to
y
. In words,
G

looks the same from every vertex
. This strong spatial homogeneity precludes many pathological phenomena observed in more heterogeneous settings, and entails considerably simplified expressions for a number of random-walk statistics
[
2
,
26
,
27
]
. In light of this, it seems reasonable to hope that Conjecture
1
will follow from a universal estimate on the varentropy of vertex-transitive expanders, and we intend to investigate this question in the near future.

2
Proof

Before we start, let us introduce some useful notation. First, we conveniently equip our state space
\mathcal{X}
with the following natural distance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon K^{n}(x,y)>0\}.

Note that the symmetry axiom is guaranteed by our assumption (
2
), while the separation and triangle inequality are straightforward to check. This allows us to use the various notions pertaining to metric spaces. In particular, the
diameter
of the state space is

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{dist}(x,y),

while the
Lipschitz norm
a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\textsc{lip}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\mathrm{dist}(x,y)}.

We will also frequently use the following natural size parameter:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x),

which is always positive thanks to the irreducibility of
K
, but tends to zero as the number of states grows. With this notation in hand, we may now recall two classical estimates on mixing times; see
[
25
, Lemma 11]
for the first and
[
22
, Corollary 2.6]
for the second.

Lemma 2

(Classical mixing-time estimates)
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle t_{\textsc{mix}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})-\sqrt{\frac{2t_{\textsc{mix}}(\varepsilon)}{1-\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma(1-\varepsilon)}};

\displaystyle t_{\textsc{mix}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\left(\frac{1}{4p\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Our first observation is that under Assumptions A1-A2, those lower and upper bounds lie within a constant factor from each other, thereby providing explicit access to the exact order of magnitude of the mixing time.

Lemma 3

(Control on
p
)
.

We always have

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3\,\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})\log\frac{1}{\delta}

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and set
n=\mathrm{dist}(x,y)
. We then have
K^{n}(x,y)>0
, and hence
K^{n}(x,y)>\delta^{n}
by definition of
\delta
. Using the stationarity
\pi=\pi K=\cdots=\pi K^{n}
, we can then write

\displaystyle\pi(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(z)K^{n}(z,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\pi(x)\delta^{n}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\pi(x)\delta^{\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})}.

Choosing
y
so that
\pi(y)=p
and summing over all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we obtain

\displaystyle p|\mathcal{X}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})}.

On the other hand, since the diagram of the chain has degrees at most
\delta^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\delta^{-1}+\delta^{-2}+\cdots+\delta^{-\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})}\ \leq\ \delta^{-2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})},

because
\delta\leq 1/2
. The claim is now readily obtained by combining the last two displays.
∎

We next recall a recent, general regularity estimate for the logarithm of the heat-kernel at any sufficiently large time
t\geq 0
. This is taken from
[
25
, Lemma 10]
.

Lemma 4

(Spatial regularity of the heat kernel)
.

For all
o\in\mathcal{X}
and
t\geq\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})/4
,

\displaystyle\left\|\log\frac{P_{t}(o,\cdot)}{\pi(\cdot)}\right\|_{\textsc{lip}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c:=3\log\frac{e}{\delta}.

We use this regularity to show that the relative entropy can not decrease too fast. More precisely, define the worst-case relative entropy to equilibrium at any time
t\geq 0
as follows:

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{o\in\mathcal{X}}d_{\textsc{kl}}(P_{t}(o,\cdot),\pi).

Lemma 5

(Regularity of relative entropy)
.

For any
t\geq\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})/4
and any
s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(t+s)+cs,

where
c
is the constant appearing in the previous lemma.

Proof.

By an elementary and classical computation, we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\,d_{\textsc{kl}}\left(P_{t}(o,\cdot),\pi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}P_{t}(o,x)K(x,y)\left(\log\frac{P_{t}(o,x)}{\pi(x)}-\log\frac{P_{t}(o,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\log\frac{P_{t}(o,\cdot)}{\pi(\cdot)}\right\|_{\textsc{lip}},

and the claim now readily follows from Lemma
4
.
∎

Another immediate consequence of Lemma
4
is the following heat-kernel estimate.

Lemma 6

(Uniform heat-kernel estimate)
.

For all
o,x\in\mathcal{X}
and
t\geq\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})/4
, we have

\displaystyle-c\,\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})\ \leq

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{P_{t}(o,x)}{\pi(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ \log\frac{1}{p},

where
c
is the constant appearing in Lemma
4
.

Proof.

The first inequality is simply the crude bound
\max(h)-h(x)\leq\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})\|h\|_{\textsc{lip}}
applied to the function
h=\log\frac{P_{t}(o,\cdot)}{\pi}
, and the second trivially follows from the definition of
p
.
∎

Finally, we will need the following simple lemma, which asserts that the classical upper bound on
d_{\textsc{tv}}(t)
using
d_{\textsc{kl}}(t)
(Pinsker’s inequality) can be reversed at a reasonable price.

Lemma 7

(Reversed Pinsker’s inequality)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-p}\log\frac{1}{p}\right)d_{\textsc{tv}}(t).

Proof.

Since the function
g\colon u\mapsto\frac{u\log u}{u-1}
is increasing on
[1,\infty)
, we have for all
0\leq u\leq v
,

\displaystyle u\log u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g(v)(u-1)_{+}.

In particular, given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we may take
u=\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}
and
v=\frac{1}{p}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}\log\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\pi(x)}-1\right)_{+}g\left(\frac{1}{p}\right),

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Averaging this with respect to
\pi
yields

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{tv}}(\mu,\pi)g\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).

The claim now follows by specializing this to
\mu=P_{t}(o,\cdot)
and maximizing over
o\in\mathcal{X}
.
∎

We now have all we need to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof.

Let
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of transition matrices with symmetric support satisfying Assumptions A1-A2. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
once and for all, and write
t_{n}:=t_{\textsc{mix}}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
and
p_{n}:=\min\pi_{n}
. Combining Lemmas
2
and
3
, we know that

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}\ \asymp\ \ \mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{n}),

where the notation
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
means that the ratio
a_{n}/b_{n}
is bounded from above and below by positive constants that do not depend on
n
. We will repeatedly use this fact below, without notice. Now, assume that
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
exhibits cutoff. This guarantees the existence of a sequence of times
(s_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with the following properties:

\displaystyle\frac{s_{n}}{t_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad d_{\textsc{tv}}^{(n)}(t_{n}+s_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

In particular, Lemma
7
ensures that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}^{(n)}(t_{n}+s_{n})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}.

Moreover, since
t_{n}\geq\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X}_{n})/4
for large enough
n
by Lemma
2
, we can safely invoke Lemma
5
with
t=t_{n}
and
s=s_{n}
to deduce that we also have

\displaystyle d_{\textsc{kl}}^{(n)}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}.

(4)

Now, choose an arbitrary initial state
o_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mu_{n}:=P_{t_{n}}(o_{n},\cdot)
denote the distribution of the chain at time
t_{n}
starting from
o_{n}
. Let
X_{n}
denote a random variable with law
\mu_{n}
, and consider the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{n}(X_{n})}{\pi_{n}(X_{n})}.

Note that we then have
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{n}^{-1}]=1
,
{\mathbb{E}}[\log Z_{n}]=d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})
and
{\mathrm{Var}}(\log Z_{n})=V_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})
.
Let also
F\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be the function defined by the formula

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\log u+\frac{1}{u}-1.

This function is decreasing on
(0,1]
and increasing on
[1,\infty)
, with
F(1)=0
. Thus, we may invoke Markov’s inequality to deduce that for any fixed
\theta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}\geq p_{n}^{-\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(Z_{n})\right]}{F(p_{n}^{-\theta})}\ =\ \frac{d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})}{\theta\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}+p_{n}^{\theta}-1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}\leq p_{n}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(Z_{n})\right]}{F(p_{n}^{\theta})}\ =\ \frac{d_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})}{p_{n}^{-\theta}+\theta\log p_{n}-1}.

The key point is that both estimates tend to
0
as
n\to\infty
, thanks to (
4
). In other words, we have established the following convergence in probability:

\displaystyle\frac{\log Z_{n}}{\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle 0.

(5)

To conclude, observe that by Lemma
6
, the random variables
\left({\log Z_{n}}/{\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}}\right)_{n\geq 1}
all take values in a fixed compact set. Thus, the convergence (
5
) automatically also holds in
L^{2}
. In particular, we may safely take variances on both sides to obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{V_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{p_{n}}\ \asymp\ t_{n}.

Since the initial state
o_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
was arbitrary, we may finally choose it so that
V_{\textsc{kl}}(\mu_{n},\pi_{n})=V_{\textsc{kl}}^{(n)}(t_{n})
, and the result is proved.
∎
