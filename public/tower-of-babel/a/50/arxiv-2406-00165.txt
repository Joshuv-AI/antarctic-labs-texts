2
Dynamics: deterministic and stochastic formulations

2.1
Stochastic formulation of dynamical systems and its implied topological determinism

Kolmogorov’s axiomatic theory of stochastic processes with discrete time and the modern ergodic theory of nonlinear dynamics share a common foundation
[
1
,
65
]
. In its simplest form with a finite state space
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,\cdots,K-1\}
, one important concept that deserves specific articulation in Kolmogorov’s theory is the
\Omega
space of all elementary events defined by
\Omega=\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{N}}
; each event is a one-sided infinite sequence of states
\omega=(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\cdots)
with
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
For a given
\omega_{0}=(s^{o}_{1}s^{o}_{2}s^{o}_{3}\cdots)\in\Omega
, there is a
deterministic trajectory in the
\Omega
space

\omega_{0},\cdots,\omega_{n},\omega_{n+1},\cdots,\,\text{ where }\,\omega_{n}\in\Omega,

(2a)

that is recursively defined by
\omega_{k+1}=T\omega_{k}\in\Omega
, where
T
is called a
shift operator

T\omega\equiv T(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\cdots)=(s_{2}s_{3}s_{4}\cdots)\in\Omega.

(2b)

Moreover, there is a corresponding
stochastic trajectory in the state space
\mathcal{S}
as a coarse-grained description of (
2a
)

s^{o}_{1},\cdots,s^{o}_{n},s^{o}_{n+1},\cdots,\,\text{ where }\,s^{o}_{n}\in\mathcal{S},

(2c)

Note the states in (
2a
) are
\omega
’s in
\Omega
and the states in (
2c
) are
s
’s in
\mathcal{S}
.

To better illustrate the deterministic dynamics defined by
T
on
\Omega
, we can employ a topological semi-conjugacy between
T
on
\Omega
and the expanding map
E_{K}
on
[0,1]
via the representation
[
65
]

\omega\equiv(s_{1}s_{2}s_{3},\cdots)\in\Omega\,\longrightarrow\,\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}}{K^{n}}\right)\in[0,1],

(3)

where
E_{k}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is defined by

E_{K}x=Kx\ (\text{mod }1),\ t\in\mathbb{N}.

A deterministic ergodic dynamics means that a single trajectory, consisting of only a countably infinite set of
\omega
values, is sufficient to statistically represent the entire
[0,1]
that is a continuum. We emphasize that there are as many as the entire
[0,1]
number of different trajectories; all the trajectories corresponding to rational numbers on
[0,1]
are not ergodic, but they total a set of zero measure and thus are negligible in a statistical sense.

Heuristically and in a nutshell, each Kolmogorov’s elementary event
\omega\in\Omega
implies an entire trajectory of a deterministic dynamics. Ruelle’s thermodynamics
[
54
]
is based on representations like in (
2a

E_{K}

[
54
]

2c
) and one additional necessary supposition: a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, under which quantities that involve subsets of
\Omega
such as

\mathbb{P}\{s_{1}=k_{1}\},\,\mathbb{P}\{s_{1}=k_{1},s_{2}=k_{2}\},\text{ and }\,\mathbb{P}\{s_{1}=k_{1},s_{2}=k_{2},s_{3}=k_{3}\}

become meaningful, where
\mathcal{F}
is the cylindrical
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
. Simple counting is no longer applicable to quantifying the sizes of random events like
\{s_{1}=k_{1}\}
and
\{s_{1}=k_{1},s_{2}=k_{2}\}
, as they are all non-denumerable.

There is actually a third representation:
s_{1}^{o}
,
s_{1}^{o}s_{2}^{o}
,

The probability measure

With a given

2.2
Stochastic dynamics in continuous space and time with continuous path

Shift operators for infinite alphabets (state space) and/or continuous time are highly non-trivial mathematical matters. Heuristically, for a continuous time stochastic process on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the
\Omega
is the space of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued functions on
[0,\infty)
; each
\omega\in\Omega
is a function
{\bf x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
t\geq 0
. The shift operator
T(\tau)
takes the
{\bf x}(t)
to
T(\tau){\bf x}(t)={\bf x}(t+\tau)
. It is easy to verify that
T(\tau)
is a linear operator on the function space; it has various
operational calculus representations

[
26
]
. One example first employed by Lagrange for smooth
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}
is via Taylor expansion:

x(t+\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}t^{k}}x(t)\equiv\left\{\exp\left(\tau\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\right)\right\}x(t).

We shall not follow this line of inquiry except noticing that it has a clear connection to functional analysis and theory of Lie groups.

As in Eq. (
2c
), the stochastic dynamics representation follows the
{\bf x}(t)
in the state space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The given probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
is the Wiener measure, which is also called a white noise.
Under the further assumption that
{\bf x}(t)
is a continuous function of
t
, the theory of stochastic differential equation is to a
K
-state Markov chain.

As a mathematical representation of physical movements at such a fundamental level, the very stochastic formulation for
{\bf x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
already embodies the three most important irreversible dissipative phenomena in statistical physics: diffusion process, heat conduction as energy transfer studied by Fourier, and viscosity via momentum transfer is directly related to diffusion through Einstein’s relation. In stochastic thermodynamics, therefore, one asks not how irreversibility arises from deterministic reversible dynamics, rather one studies what reversible dynamics is in stochastic processes
[
28
]
. In recent years, the theory of stochastic Markov dynamics has provided a coherent narrative of nonequilibrium thermodynamics at a mesoscopic level; see
[
49
,
60
]
and the references cited within. By “mesoscopic”, we mean a mechanistic description of dynamical systems of complex individuals in terms of stochastic processes
[
45
]
. At the center of this new development is an entropy balance equation, mathematically derivable, in which the notion of
entropy production
has been firmly established
[
59
,
35
,
50
,
63
,
44
]
. By introducing a “system’s size parameter”
\alpha
, one can confidently investigate macroscopic thermodynamics as the limit of the mesoscopic description: It has been shown that Gibbs’ equilibrium chemical
thermodynamics of heterogeneous substances is the emergent large deviations theory of a Delbrück-Gillespie process of chemical kinetics
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. It suggests that “stochastic kinetics or kinematics dictates energetics”, a saying imitating C. N. Yang’s aphorism
symmetry dictates interaction

[
67
]
. See
[
47
,
37
]
for more discussion.

Consider the general diffusion process with continuous state space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and in continuous time, with its Fokker-Planck equation
[
50
,
44
,
28
]

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-{\bf b}({\bf x})f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\Big),

(4)

where
\alpha
represents the size of a system,
{\bf b}({\bf x})
is the drift field,
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})
denotes the non-degenerate diffusion. It is understood that
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},0)=\delta_{{\bf x}_{0}}
with
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
arbitrarily given. More mathematical details regarding the well-posedness of (
4
) and basic properties of
f_{\alpha}
are given in Appendix
A
. In addition to many models in physics
[
27
]
, stochastic formulation of a rapidly stirred nonlinear chemical reaction system follows the
chemical master equation
, in which individual molecules are counted one at a time
[
45
]
. In the limit of the system’s volume and the number of molecules tend to infinity, their concentrations follow a chemical Langevin equation
[
25
]
in which the volume of the chemical reaction vessel is the
\alpha
.

The Shannon entropy functional
S=S[f_{\alpha}]
, which is defined by

S=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\ln f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t){\rm d}{\bf x},

has an instantaneous rate of change
[
50
,
28
,
22
,
64
]
:

\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}=e_{p}+Q_{ex},

(5a)

where the entropy production rate
e_{p}
and heat exchange rate

Q_{ex}
, measured in units of
k_{B}T
, are given as

e_{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-{\bf b}({\bf x})f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\right]\cdot\left[\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})\right]{\rm d}{\bf x}

(5b)

and

Q_{ex}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-{\bf b}({\bf x})f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\right]\cdot\left[\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})\right]{\rm d}{\bf x}.

(5c)

While
e_{p}
is positive,
Q_{ex}
can have
either signs.

If the system is non-driven without external work being done, then the drift
{\bf b}({\bf x})
has a potential
U({\bf x})
such that

{\bf b}({\bf x})=-\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla U({\bf x}).

(6)

It follows that

\begin{split}Q_{ex}&=-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-{\bf b}({\bf x})f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\right]\cdot\nabla U({\bf x}){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\alpha\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)U({\bf x}){\rm d}{\bf x}=\alpha\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathbb{E}_{f_{\alpha}}[U].\end{split}

For this class of systems, one can in fact introduce a new functional
F=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{f_{\alpha}}[U]-S
, and then
{\rm d}F/{\rm d}t<0
. The potential condition implies that
Q_{ex}
can be expressed as
\alpha
times the change of the mean energy
\mathbb{E}_{f_{\alpha}}[U]
. Such a link only exists in equilibrium systems. The stationary state of such a system is an
equilibrium
with zero entropy production and minimal
F
. Eq. (
5a
) in fact reminds one of the Clausius inequality in thermal physics, where
F
is called a Helmholtz free energy
[
66
]
.

3
Derivation of the macroscopic entropy balance equation

In the limit of system’s size
\alpha\to\infty
, the mathematical theory of large deviations (see Appendix
A.1
) shows that

f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\sim\left(R_{0}({\bf x},t)+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}R_{1}({\bf x},t)\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi({\bf x},t)},

(7)

where the rate function
\varphi({\bf x},t)
solves the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

-\frac{\partial\varphi({\bf x},t)}{\partial t}=\nabla\varphi({\bf x},t)\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi({\bf x},t)+{\bf b}({\bf x})\right),

R_{0}({\bf x},t)
solves the linear equation
\mathcal{L}R_{0}({\bf x},t)=0
, and
R_{1}({\bf x},t)
solves the non-homogeneous equation
\mathcal{L}R_{1}({\bf x},t)=\nabla\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla R_{0}({\bf x},t))
. In which, the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
reads

\mathcal{L}=\partial_{t}+\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi({\bf x},t)+{\bf b}({\bf x})\right)\cdot\nabla+\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi({\bf x},t)+{\bf b}({\bf x})\right).

Moreover,
\varphi({\bf x},t)
has a global minimum at
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)

for each
t
, where the function
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
is the solution
to the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
{\rm d}\hat{{\bf x}}/{\rm d}t={\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}})
with initial value
\hat{{\bf x}}(0)={\bf x}_{0}
.

Applying (
7
), one can determine the following key results of this paper (see Appendix
B.2
for details). Both
e_{p}
and
Q_{ex}
are

, that is, their leading order is

\displaystyle e_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi+2\nabla\cdot{\bf b}+R+O\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\big),

(8a)

\displaystyle Q_{ex}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}-\nabla\cdot{\bf b}-R+O\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\big),

(8b)

where all the terms on the right-hand side of (
8
) are calculated at
{\bf x}=\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
, the colon
:
denotes the Frobenius product of two matrices of the same size, and

R=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\nabla\big(R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\big):\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}-\frac{1}{6}\nabla(R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b})\cdot\nabla\nabla\nabla\varphi\Theta+\frac{R_{1}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi}}.

In which,

and

\varphi({\bf x},t)
at its minimum
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
.

The prefactor in (
7
) only appears within the
R
in (
8
), as a compensation between
e_{p}
and
Q_{ex}

[
48
,
43
]
. While their explicit forms have been difficult to obtain in general (see Appendix
B.2
),
R_{1}=0
and
R({\bf x},t)
becomes independent of
{\bf x}
for an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with linear drift
{\bf b}({\bf x})=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}{\bf x}
and constant non-degenerate diffusion
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}
. In fact, according to the precise WKB ansatz (
31
) in this case,
R_{1}=0
and
\nabla\nabla\nabla\varphi\Theta=0
as
\varphi
is quadratic, and therefore,

R=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\nabla\big(R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\big):\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\frac{\left(\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\right):\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)}{\sqrt{\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)}},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(t-s)}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}(t-s)}ds
. The
R
aside, the emergent macroscopic nonequilibrium thermodynamic structure is completely determined by
{\bf b}({\bf x})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})
, and most importantly
\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}},t)
as an irreversible thermodynamic process.

Even though both Eqs. (
5
) and (
9
)
are equations for entropy balance, their contradistinction suggests
subtle but important physics of mesoscopic vs. macroscopic
systems.

First, in the macroscopic limit, the entropy production rate
e_{p}
and
heat exchange rate
Q_{ex}
become exactly the same; their difference
yields zero for
{\rm d}S/{\rm d}t
on the order
O(\alpha)
, that is, the
rate of entropy change is actually very slow. The macroscopic
heat exchange has the familiar Newtonian frictional form
of “rate
{\bf b}
times force
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}
”; it
is the same as entropy production rate, thus non-negative.

Second, the “very slow” rate of entropy change, however,
is a balance between
\nabla\cdot{\bf b}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right)
and

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right):\nabla\nabla\varphi\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)
.
In the theory of deterministic dynamical systems,
volume preserving dynamics with
\nabla\cdot{\bf b}({\bf x})=0

is considered as “conservative”. This is completely
consistent with identifying
\nabla\cdot{\bf b}({\bf x})
as a system’s
energy exchange with the environment, i.e., heat
[
2
,
53
,
55
,
56
,
51
]
. Then in the true stationary state, on the order
O(1)
, there is an exact balance between fluctuations
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right):\nabla\nabla\varphi\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)

and dissipation
-\nabla\cdot{\bf b}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right)
.

Third, Eq. (
9
) is actually a generalization of a known result: For an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with linear drift
{\bf b}({\bf x})=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}{\bf x}
and constant non-degenerate diffusion
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}
, the covariance matrix
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)
satisfies

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{\prime}(t)=2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}+\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)+\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}

[
42
,
7
]
. Noting that the entropy of a Gaussian
distribution is (see Appendix
D
)

S=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha+\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\Big(\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)\Big),

one finds from Jacobi’s formula that

\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{\prime}(t)\right]=\nabla\cdot{\bf b}+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)=\nabla\cdot{\bf b}+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi,

where
\varphi({\bf x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)
is the rate function in this case. The present work establishes a connection between this equation for the variance of a time-dependent Gaussian process, a form of the central limit theory
[
31
]
, and the general entropy balance equation in the asymptotic limit
of
\alpha\to\infty
. It shows that the trace of the covariance equation can be interpreted as “entropy balance”.

Fourth, the local entropy production rate in (
9
)
deserves further elaboration, given in Sec.
4
.

4
Local entropy production and entropy change

To illustrate several ideas in stochastic dynamics, let us first consider a Markov chain with a probability distribution
\left\{p_{i}(t):1\leq i\leq N\right\}
at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
and an one-step transition probability
\left\{P_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq N\right\}
. According to the representation in (
2a
), which we call “path tracking”,
the entropy associated with the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
for all
\omega
’s, each being a function of time
t\in[0,\infty)
, grows to infinite as
t\to\infty
. Therefore one defines the rate as
t\to\infty
, also known as entropy per step in the case of discrete time
[
21
]
:
-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}p_{i}(t)P_{ij}\ln P_{ij}
. This expression has also been interpreted as the
entropy produced in the dynamic process
, over a single step transition from state
i
to
j
being
-\ln P_{ij}
, thus
-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}p_{i}(t)P_{ij}\ln P_{ij}
being the mean value.

The representation in (
2c
) which we call “state tracking”, however, only focuses on the probability distribution among different states at a time
t
,
p_{i}(t)
. Therefore it is reasonable to say that not all the “amount” of entropy generated from
\omega_{t}\to\omega_{t+1}
is being kept in the
p_{i}(t+1)
: In fact the
change in the entropy

associated with
s_{t}\to s_{t+1}
is

\displaystyle-\ln\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(t)P_{kj}\right)+\ln p_{i}(t),

(10)

with the mean value

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}p_{i}(t)P_{ij}\left[\ln\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(t)P_{kj}\right)-\ln p_{i}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}(t+1)\ln p_{j}(t+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(t)\ln p_{i}(t).

Therefore the difference between these two differently formulated entropy is
[
21
]

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left\{-\ln P_{ij}\right\}}_{\text{total
entropy generated}}-\underbrace{\left\{-\ln\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(t)P_{kj}\right)+\ln p_{i}(t)\right\}}_{\text{ entropy change
in the system}}=-\ln\left(\frac{p_{i}(t)P_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(t)P_{kj}}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\ln\left(\Pr\left\{X(t)=i\big|X(t+1)=j\right\}\right)}_{\text{entropy discarded is associated with
uncertainty in the past}}\geq 0.

The quantity
-\ln P_{ij}
is the entropy change in a path tracking representation of a Markov chain (
2a
), Eq. (
10
) is the entropy change in the state tracking representation (
2c
) of the same Markov chain. The latter representation is a projection of the former. The difference given in (
11
) is indeed related to
the folding entropy in
[
55
,
34
,
33
]
. Its interpretation is intimately related to A. Ben-Naim’s notion of entropy of assimilation
[
3
]
.
The difference between the uncertainty
in the future and the uncertainty in the past is the entropy change in the system
: One verifies a connection between the entropy change in a system according to the state tracking and past-future asymmetry in the path tracking.

Parallel to the above discussion on a discrete-time and discrete-space Markov chain, for a continuous-time and continuous-space Gaussian process with variance
\sigma^{2}
at time
t
and a Brownian step from
t
to
t+\tau
with a diffusion coefficient
D
, the probability distribution at
t+\tau
is again Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}+2D\tau
. The instantaneous rate of entropy change in the continuous system, corresponding to (
10
), then is

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\ln\sqrt{2\pi e(\sigma^{2}+2D\tau)}-\ln\sqrt{2\pi e\sigma^{2}}}{\tau}=\frac{D}{\sigma^{2}}.

(12)

Applying the result in (
12
) to the local
entropy production rate in (
9
),
\nabla\nabla\varphi\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)
is the inverse of the local Gaussian variance at
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)

at time
t
. Therefore,
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)):\nabla\nabla\varphi\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)

is the local entropy production at time
t
that is kept by the system, due to the randomness in the dynamics. See
[
47
]
for a more thorough mathematical
analysis of the diffusion process in the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
.

The local entropy production is no longer a characterization
of the violation of detailed balance or time irreversibility, two essential concerns in stochastic thermodynamics. Rather, it is a characterization of the randomness generated
by the dynamics,
D
, and the uncertainties in the system,

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}=\nabla\nabla\varphi
.

We use the term “local” to signify the following fact: The mean value of stochastic thermodynamic quantities such as
e_{p}
,
Q_{ex}
, as well as
F
and
Q_{hk}
below, are all integrals of the distribution function
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)
over the entire state space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. However in the limit of
\alpha\to\infty
the
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\to\delta\big({\bf x}-\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)
. The corresponding macroscopic thermodynamic quantities are now functions of
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
which is a single point in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
at a time. Therefore in contrast to the former, the latter is defined locally in the state space.

5
Time scales in the nonequilibrium thermodynamic description of stochastic dynamics

5.1
Free energy balance equation

For stochastic mechanical systems in contact with a heat bath, entropy is not the appropriate equilibrium thermodynamic potential, free energy is. In classical thermodynamics, the
difference between entropy and free energy is the mean internal energy. This is reflected in the generalized free energy (also called non-adiabatic entropy
[
60
]
, Kullback–Leibler divergence or relative entropy
[
4
,
9
]
)
F=F[f_{\alpha}]
defined by

F=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)}{\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})}{\rm d}{\bf x},

where
\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})
is the positive stationary solution to the
Fokker-Planck equation (
4
) and satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x}){\rm d}{\bf x}=1
. For fixed system’s size
\alpha
,
F\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, and therefore, it describes the relaxation of
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)
to the steady state
\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})
as
t\to\infty
.

For equilibrium systems, that is,
{\bf b}({\bf x})
satisfies (
6
), we have
\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})=\tfrac{1}{K_{\alpha}}e^{-\alpha U({\bf x})}
with
K_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{-\alpha U({\bf x})}{\rm d}{\bf x}
. As a result,
F=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{f_{\alpha}}[U]-S+\ln K_{\alpha}
. Introducing
\widetilde{U}=\alpha^{-1}\ln K_{\alpha}
yields
F=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{f_{\alpha}}[\widetilde{U}]-S
, which is analogous to the Helmholtz free energy
[
14
]
.

The functional
F
also has a balance equation of its own
[
27
,
22
,
14
]
:

\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}=-e_{p}+Q_{hk},

(13)

where the house-keeping heat rate
Q_{hk}
(also known as the adiabatic entropy production rate) is

Q_{hk}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)-{\bf b}({\bf x})f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)\right]\cdot\left[\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})\right]{\rm d}{\bf x}.

It is also a non-negative quantity, and is actually positive unless
{\bf b}({\bf x})
is a gradient field (
6
) (see Appendix
C.1
). The non-negativity of both
e_{p}
and
Q_{hk}
suggests that they can be identified as “sink” and “source” of the generalized free energy
F
in a
nonlinear stochastic dynamical system. Moreover, the ”sink” is stronger than the ”source”, resulting in the nice Lyapunov property
{\rm d}F/{\rm d}t<0
. This is a well-known fact; we provide the details in Appendix
C.1
for completeness.

In the limit of size parameter
\alpha\to\infty
, both

F
and
Q_{hk}
are extensive quantities on the order of

O(\alpha)
(see Appendix
C.2
for details):

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)+\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}+O(1),

(14a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha\left[\nabla\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\nabla\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\right]+O(1),

(14b)

\displaystyle Q_{hk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\right]+O(1),

(14c)

where
\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
is the leading-order
exponent of
\pi_{\alpha}({\bf x})\sim e^{-\alpha\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
is a constant satisfying
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\ln\left(|\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}|+1\right)=0
,
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}({\bf x})={\bf b}({\bf x})+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
. In the case that the ODE
\dot{{\bf x}}={\bf b}({\bf x})
admit a globally asymptotically stable and non-degenerate equilibrium, there holds
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}=0
. The leading terms in the asymptotic (
14
) have been discussed in
[
47
,
16
]
with applications to the emergent second law of thermodynamics. They are also used in
[
57
]
to bound asymptotic escape rates from metastable states.

One of important results from the theory of large deviations
[
17
]
is that

\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}({\bf x})\cdot\nabla\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})=0.

(15)

In fact, Eq. (
13
), on the macroscopic
scale, becomes a Pythagorean triangle equality:

\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\nabla\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\right\|^{2}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right)}+\big\|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\big\|^{2}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right)}=\big\|{\bf b}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\big\|^{2}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\right)},

where the norm of a vector
{\bf v}
is defined as

\|{\bf v}\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}({\bf x})}=\sqrt{{\bf v}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf v}}

[
47
]
.

5.2
Time scales, short-time and long-time perspectives

Among all the thermodynamic quantities we discussed
above, macroscopic
F
,
Q_{hk}
, and
{\rm d}F/{\rm d}t
are
defined via
\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
, while macroscopic

e_{p}
and
Q_{ex}
, and local heat exchange rate
are functions of
{\bf x}
via
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})
and

{\bf b}({\bf x})
.
{\rm d}S/{\rm d}t
and its corresponding
local entropy production rate, however, are defined via

\varphi({\bf x},t)
, which is dependent upon the choice of

\varphi({\bf x},0)
, e.g., initial fluctuations.
The
\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
plays the role
of a macroscopic potential energy function. In the
macroscopic, deterministic limit, free energy and its
rate of change are dominated by this energy function

\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
.

The fact that
{\rm d}F/{\rm d}t\sim O(\alpha)
and

{\rm d}S/{\rm d}t\sim O(1)
tells us that in time-dependent
nonequilibrium thermodynamics, free energy relaxation is
fast, while entropy change is slow. This is a point that has escaped general attention in the past discussion on nonequilibrium thermodynamics. Through
\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
,
free energy is a “global” characterization of
the nonlinear stochastic dynamics, while entropy dynamics
is only local.

The physical meaning of
\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)

and
\varphi\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\big)
, where
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
being
the solution to the deterministic motion as the solution to

{\rm d}\hat{{\bf x}}/{\rm d}t={\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}})
, deserves further
discussion:
\nabla\varphi\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\big)=0
for all
time
t
. The matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)=\left(\nabla\nabla\varphi\right)^{-1}\left(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t\right)
, as a function of
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
,
is the local fluctuation in the asymptotic limit of

\alpha
being very large but not infinite. It is usually called
time-dependent
fluctuations
. There is a time-dependent
Gaussian process that describes this regime, following
the theory of van Kampen’s system-size expansion, or
Keizer’s nonequilibrium statistical thermodynamics

[
29
]
.
\nabla\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)
,
on the other hand, tells eventually in a very long time scale,
the local probability and its gradient at
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
. It
also provide a definitive statement that

{\rm d}\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)/{\rm d}t=

{\bf b}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)\cdot\nabla\varphi^{ss}\big(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\big)

\leq 0
is never positive, thanks to Eq. (
15
).
\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})
is a landscape for the dynamics with permanence, irrespective of the
initial
\varphi({\bf x},0)
.

All this discussion reflects a “competition” between the two limits
\alpha\to\infty
and
t\to\infty
in an ergodic system, as first understood in the theory of equilibrium phase transition: With larger and larger
\alpha
, a trajectory with less stochasticity will take longer and longer time to visit and re-visit all the space states. When
\alpha=\infty
, ergodicity is broken in nonlinear deterministic dynamics with multiple attractors. However, no matter how large
\alpha
is, as long as
\alpha<\infty
, there will be enough time for the dynamics to cover the whole state space as
t\to\infty
. See
[
46
]
for an extensive discussion on this issue.

6
Discussion

In current textbooks on equilibrium statistical mechanics, thermodynamic limit is rigorously defined as a system’s
size,
\alpha
, tending to infinity. There is no statement
on the time scales of the rate of changes in thermodynamic
quantities, particularly their dependency upon the system’s size
\alpha
. Time scale(s), however, is certainly central to physics. We discover that there is a deep relation between the
\alpha\to\infty
and time scales of an entropy
balance and for a free energy balance:
The former is on the order of
O(1)
but the latter is on the
order
O(\alpha)
.

There have been two approaches to statistical thermodynamics,
one based on classical mechanics originated by L. Boltzmann,
and one based on probability originated by J. W. Gibbs

[
32
]
. Development in nonlinear dynamical systems based on chaotic hypothesis
[
19
]

and Sinai-Ruelle-Bowen measure
[
52
,
68
,
10
]
is
the continuation of the former
[
56
,
13
]
, while
the stochastic thermodynamics
[
49
,
60
]

and in-depth explorations of the theory of probability were the further development of the latter
[
28
,
22
,
23
,
66
,
6
]
. The present result provides a natural logic bridge between the entropy balance equations, as the fundamental of nonequilibrium thermodynamics, that emerge in these two approaches. If one identifies
\alpha=\epsilon^{-1}
where

\epsilon
being the size of a Markov partition for a
deterministic dynamical system, then taking the limit
of
\alpha\to\infty
is consistent with the modern
treatment in terms of a generating partition which gives
rise to Kolmogorov-Sinai metric entropy and the
nonequilibrium thermodynamics
à la
D. Ruelle

[
13
]
.

Our present result might also have implications to equilibrium
thermodynamic analysis in which researchers are routinely
partitioning the energetic and entropic contributions to total free
energy change via van’t Hoff method
[
58
]
.
A compensation between the
entropy and energy changes has been extensively discussed
in the past
[
48
,
43
]
.
With
\Delta S=0
on
O(\alpha)
in

\Delta t
time, it is tempting to interpret
e_{p}\Delta t

and
Q_{ex}\Delta t
as entropy change and energy change
in a quasi-stationary process.

Appendix A
Fokker-Planck equation and WKB ansatz

Consider the following Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}f_{\alpha}=\nabla\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},

(16a)

f_{\alpha}(\cdot,0)=\delta_{{\bf x}_{0}}

(16b)

where
\alpha
represents the size of a system,
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\delta_{{\bf x}_{0}}
denotes the Dirac measure at
{\bf x}_{0}
, the diffusion matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})
is symmetric positive definite and sufficiently smooth, and the vector/drift field
{\bf b}={\bf b}({\bf x})
is sufficiently smooth. Mild growth conditions on
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})
and
{\bf b}({\bf x})
as
|{\bf x}|\to\infty
can be imposed to guarantee that Eq. (
16
) is well-posed and admits a classical solution
f_{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
. Moreover, the solution satisfies
f_{\alpha}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t){\rm d}{\bf x}=1
for all
t>0
, and, together with its derivatives, decays to
0
sufficiently fast as
|{\bf x}|\to\infty

[
28
,
40
]
.

In addition, if
{\bf x}_{\alpha}(t)
is the solution to the SDE associated with Eq. (
16a
) with initial condition
{\bf x}_{\alpha}(0)={\bf x}_{0}
, then for any
t>0
,
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)
is the density of the distribution of
{\bf x}_{\alpha}(t)
.

Under dissipative conditions (e.g., Lyapunov conditions), Eq. (
16a
) admits a unique positive stationary solution
\pi_{\alpha}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\pi_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}=1

[
30
]
. Setting

{\bf J}_{\alpha}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}-{\bf b},

(17)

then
\pi_{\alpha}
satisfies

\nabla\cdot\left(\pi_{\alpha}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right)=0.

(18)

Below, we state the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) ansatz of
f_{\alpha}
and
\pi_{\alpha}
for large
\alpha
.

A.1
WKB ansatz of
f_{\alpha}

For each
t>0
, the WKB ansatz of
f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)
in
{\bf x}
for large
\alpha
reads (see e.g.
[
61
,
17
,
18
]
)

f_{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\left[R_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}R_{1}+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}})\right]e^{-\alpha\varphi},

(19)

where
\varphi=\varphi({\bf x},t)
is the rate function and
R_{\alpha}=R_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)
is the prefactor. The regularity of
\varphi
,
R_{\alpha}
,
R_{0}=R_{0}({\bf x},t)
, and
R_{1}=R_{1}({\bf x},t)
is not automatic, but can be achieved under technical assumptions (see
(
61
, Section 3)
).

The rate function
\varphi
solves the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

-\partial_{t}\varphi=\nabla\varphi\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right).

Moreover, if
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
denotes the solution to the ODE
\dot{{\bf x}}={\bf b}({\bf x})
with initial condition
\hat{{\bf x}}(0)={\bf x}_{0}
, then for any
t>0
, the following hold:

•

\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)=0<\varphi({\bf x},t)
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{\hat{{\bf x}}(t)\}
; in particular,
\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)=0
;

•

\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)
is positive definite, where
\nabla\nabla\varphi
denotes the Hessian matrix of
\varphi
.

The leading term
R_{0}
of the prefactor
R_{\alpha}
solves the following linear equation

-\partial_{t}R_{0}=\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)\cdot\nabla R_{0}+\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)R_{0},

while
R_{1}
solves the following non-homogeneous equation

-\partial_{t}R_{1}=-\nabla\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{0})+\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)\cdot\nabla R_{1}+\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)R_{1}.

(20)

Since
\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}=1
, Laplace’s method yields

\frac{R_{0}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}}=1.

A.2
WKB ansatz of
\pi_{\alpha}

The WKB ansatz of
\pi_{\alpha}
reads

\pi_{\alpha}=\frac{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{C_{\alpha}}e^{-\alpha\varphi^{ss}},

(21)

where
C_{\alpha}
is the sub-exponential (i.e.,
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\ln C_{\alpha}=0
) normalizing constant, the rate function
\varphi^{ss}
satisfies
\min\varphi^{ss}=0
and solves the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi equation

\nabla\varphi^{ss}\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}+{\bf b}\right)=0,

(22)

and the prefactor
R_{\alpha}^{ss}
satisfies

R_{\alpha}^{ss}=R_{0}^{ss}+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right),

in which
R_{0}^{ss}
solves

\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}+{\bf b}\right)\cdot\nabla R_{0}^{ss}+\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}\right)+\nabla\cdot{\bf b}\right]R_{0}^{ss}=0.

When
{\bf b}
admits a potential
U
, namely,
{\bf b}=-\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla U
, then
\varphi=U
and
R_{\alpha}^{ss}\equiv 1
. This is the only trivial case. In general, the WKB ansatz of
\pi_{\alpha}
relies heavily on the dynamical structure of the ODE

\dot{\bf x}={\bf b}({\bf x}).

(23)

The existence of the rate function
\varphi^{ss}
has been justified under different dynamical assumptions on (
23
) by examining the limit of
-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\ln\pi_{\alpha}
as
\alpha\to\infty
. The asymptotic properties of
R_{\alpha}^{ss}
are only known when (
23
) admit a globally asymptotically stable and non-degenerate equilibrium. See
[
62
,
11
,
38
,
5
]
.

Appendix B
Entropy

We present the entropy balance equation and study related large
\alpha
asymptotics.

B.1
Entropy balance equation

The entropy
S=S[f_{\alpha}]
, entropy production rate
e_{p}=e_{p}[f_{\alpha}]
, and heat exchange rate
Q_{ex}=Q_{ex}[f_{\alpha}]
are defined by

\begin{split}S&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}\ln f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x},\\
e_{p}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right){\rm d}{\bf x},\quad\text{and}\\
Q_{ex}&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right){\rm d}{\bf x},\end{split}

respectively. Whenever
f_{\alpha}
is fixed, they are just functions of the time variable
t
.

Clearly,
e_{p}
can be written as

\begin{split}e_{p}&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

(24)

The positive definiteness of
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}
ensures that
e_{p}>0
.

Note that

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{t}f_{\alpha}\left(\ln f_{\alpha}+1\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\left(\ln f_{\alpha}+1\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}+\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right){\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

Hence, the following entropy balance equation holds:

\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}=e_{p}+Q_{ex}.

(25)

B.2
Asymptotics

The large
\alpha
asymptotics of
S
,
Q_{ex}
,
e_{p}
, and
\frac{dS}{dt}
are given as follows.

\begin{split}S&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha+O(1),\\
Q_{ex}&=-\alpha{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\\
&\quad-C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)-\frac{R_{1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}}{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))-\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}),\\
e_{p}&=\alpha{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\\
&\quad+C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+\frac{R_{1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}}{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+2\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\\
&\quad+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)):\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}),\\
\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}&=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)):\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right),\end{split}

where the colon
:
denotes the Frobenius product of two matrices of the same size, and

C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)=\left[\frac{1}{2}\nabla\nabla(R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}):\nabla\nabla\varphi-\frac{1}{6}\nabla(R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b})\cdot(\nabla\nabla\nabla\varphi\Theta)\right]\bigg|_{{\bf x}=\hat{{\bf x}}(t)}.

(26)

In which,
\nabla\nabla\nabla\varphi\Theta
is an
N
-dimensional vector and its
i
-th component is given by

(\nabla\nabla\nabla\varphi\Theta)_{i}=\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{j}x_{k}x_{\ell}}^{3}\varphi\Theta_{ijk\ell},

where

\Theta_{ijk\ell}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det(\nabla\nabla\varphi)^{-1}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}e^{-\frac{{\bf y}\cdot\nabla\nabla\varphi{\bf y}}{2}}{\rm d}{\bf y}.

Below, we provide the detailed derivation of these asymptotics.

Asymptotic of
S
.

Clearly,

\begin{split}S&=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\frac{N}{2}\ln\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}+\ln R_{\alpha}-\alpha\varphi\right)R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}-\ln R_{0}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)+\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\end{split}

The asymptotic of
S
follows from the following:

\begin{split}\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}&=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\varphi\nabla\varphi}{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}R_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\varphi\nabla\varphi}{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}R_{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=O(1).\end{split}

Asymptotic of
Q_{ex}
.

Since

\begin{split}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right)&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla R_{\alpha}-\left({\bf b}\cdot\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right)R_{\alpha}\right]e^{-\alpha\varphi},\end{split}

we find

\begin{split}Q_{ex}&=-\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad-\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=I_{1}(\alpha)+I_{2}(\alpha)+I_{3}(\alpha).\end{split}

Note that

\begin{split}I_{1}(\alpha)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot{\bf b}+R_{\alpha}\nabla\cdot{\bf b}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

Then,

\begin{split}I_{1}(\alpha)+I_{3}(\alpha)=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\nabla\cdot{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=-\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\end{split}

For
I_{2}(\alpha)
, we see that

\begin{split}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}I_{2}(\alpha)&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(R_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}R_{1}+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}})\right){\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{0}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{1}{\bf b}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}})\\
&=I_{21}(\alpha)+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}I_{22}(\alpha)+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}).\end{split}

Since

\begin{split}I_{21}(\alpha)&={\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}),\\
I_{22}(\alpha)&=\frac{R_{1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}}{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}),\end{split}

where
C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)
is given in (
26
), we arrive at

\begin{split}I_{2}(\alpha)&=-\alpha{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\\
&\quad-C(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)-\frac{R_{1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}{\sqrt{\det\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)}}{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)){\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}).\end{split}

The asymptotic of
Q_{ex}
follows.

Asymptotic of
e_{p}
.

Note that

e_{p}=-Q_{ex}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}}_{I(\alpha)}.

Given the asymptotic of
Q_{ex}
, we only need to treat
I(\alpha)
. Straightforward calculations yield

\begin{split}&\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}\\
&\qquad=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\left[\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}-\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)\cdot\nabla R_{\alpha}+\alpha\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)R_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right]e^{-\alpha\varphi}.\end{split}

Then,

\begin{split}I(\alpha)&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+{\bf b}\right)\cdot\nabla R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\nabla\varphi\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

Note that the fourth term on the RHS of the above equality can be written as

\begin{split}-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}{\bf b}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot{\bf b}+R_{\alpha}\nabla\cdot{\bf b}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}I(\alpha)&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}-2\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\nabla\varphi\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\nabla\cdot{\bf b}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=I_{4}(\alpha)+I_{5}(\alpha)+I_{6}(\alpha)+I_{7}(\alpha).\end{split}

Clearly,
I_{4}(\alpha)=O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)
and
I_{7}(\alpha)=\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)
. For
I_{5}(\alpha)
,

\begin{split}I_{5}(\alpha)&=\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right).\end{split}

For
I_{6}(\alpha)
,

\begin{split}I_{6}(\alpha)&=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(R_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\nabla R_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi+R_{\alpha}\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\right)\cdot\nabla\varphi+R_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi\right]e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}+R_{\alpha}\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\right)\right]\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left[\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}+R_{\alpha}\left(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\right)\right]e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)+\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)):\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t).\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}I(\alpha)=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t)):\nabla\nabla\varphi(\hat{{\bf x}}(t),t)+\nabla\cdot{\bf b}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\right).\end{split}

The asymptotic of
e_{p}
then follows.

Asymptotic of
\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}
.

It follows from the entropy balance equation Eq. (
25
) and the asymptotics of
e_{p}
and
Q_{ex}
. Alternatively, the asymptotic of
\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}
follows from the fact that
\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}=I(\alpha)
.

Appendix C
Free energy

We present the free energy balance equation and study related large
\alpha
asymptotics.

C.1
Free energy balance equation

The free energy
F=F[f_{\alpha}]
and house-keeping heat rate
Q_{hk}=Q_{hk}[f_{\alpha}]
are defined by

\begin{split}F&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}{\bf x}\quad\text{and}\\
Q_{hk}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}\right){\rm d}{\bf x},\end{split}

respectively. It is known that
F>0
and
Q_{hk}\geq 0
, and the following free energy balance equation holds:

\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}=-e_{p}+Q_{hk}.

(27)

Below, we provide the details.

Positivity of
F
.

Note that

F=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}\ln\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{f_{\alpha}}{\rm d}{\bf x}\geq-\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{f_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}=0,

where we used Jensen’s inequality. Moreover, since the function
x\mapsto-\ln x
is strictly convex, the above inequality is strict unless
f_{\alpha}\equiv\pi_{\alpha}
. As
f_{\alpha}\neq\pi_{\alpha}
, one concludes
F>0
.

Non-negativity of
Q_{hk}
.

We show

Q_{hk}=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\geq 0,

(28)

where
{\bf J}_{\alpha}
is defined in (
17
). Note that
{\bf J}_{\alpha}=\bf 0
so that
Q_{hk}=0
when
{\bf b}
admits a potential
U
, namely,
{\bf b}=-\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla U
. Otherwise,
Q_{hk}>0
.

Indeed, we calculate

\begin{split}Q_{hk}&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}\right)f_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}+{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right)f_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}\right)f_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}.\end{split}

The expected result follows readily from

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\cdot\left(\pi_{\alpha}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right){\rm d}{\bf x}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\nabla\cdot\left(\pi_{\alpha}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right){\rm d}{\bf x}=0,

(29)

where we used Eq. (
18
) in the last equality.

Free energy balance equation.

We show

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}=-e_{p}+Q_{hk}=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}<0.\end{split}

The strict inequality is a result of the fact that
f_{\alpha}\neq\pi_{\alpha}
.

The first equality follows readily.

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\partial_{t}f_{\alpha}\left(\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}+1\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\left(\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}+1\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla f_{\alpha}-{\bf b}f_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\ln f_{\alpha}-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}+\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf b}-\nabla\ln\pi_{\alpha}\right){\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-e_{p}+Q_{hk}.\end{split}

Now, we treat the second equality. From (
24
), one deduces

\begin{split}e_{p}&=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}+{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}+{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf J}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf J}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}+Q_{hk},\end{split}

where we used Eq. (
28
) and Eq. (
29
) in the last equality. The free energy balance equation follows.

Rewriting

\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}=-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}

and introducing the inner product and the associated norm

\left\langle{\bf V}_{1},{\bf V}_{2}\right\rangle_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\bf V}_{1}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}{\bf V}_{2}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\quad\text{and}\quad\|{\bf V}\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}=\sqrt{\left\langle{\bf V},{\bf V}\right\rangle_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}}

for vector fields
{\bf V}_{1}
,
{\bf V}_{2}
and
{\bf V}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the free energy balance equation can be rewritten as

\left\|\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}\right\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}^{2}+\left\|{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}^{2}=\left\|\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}+{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}^{2},

(30)

which is equivalent to the orthogonality between
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}
and
{\bf J}_{\alpha}
w.r.t. the inner product, that is,

\left\langle\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}},{\bf J}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1},f_{\alpha}}=0.

This is just Eq. (
29
).

C.2
Asymptotics

The asymptotics of
F
,
Q_{hk}
, and
\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}
are given as follows:

\begin{split}F&=\alpha\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha-\ln C_{\alpha}+O(1),\\
Q_{hk}&=\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(1),\\
\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}&=-\alpha\nabla\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\nabla\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(1),\end{split}

where
C_{\alpha}
is given in (
21
) and
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}+{\bf b}
. If (
23
) admit a globally asymptotically stable and non-degenerate equilibrium, then
C_{\alpha}=\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{-N/2}
, resulting in
F=\alpha\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(1)
.

Multiplying the free energy balance equation Eq. (
27
) by
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}
and then letting
\alpha\to\infty
, we derive from the asymptotics of
\frac{dF}{dt}
,
e_{p}
, and
Q_{hk}
the following free energy balance equation on the macroscopic scale:

-\nabla\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})=-{\bf b}({\bf x})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})+\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}({\bf x})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x})\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}({\bf x})\quad\text{at}\quad{\bf x}=\hat{{\bf x}}(t).

Introducing the norm
\|{\bf v}\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}({\bf x})}=\sqrt{{\bf v}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}({\bf x}){\bf v}}
for
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we arrive at the Pythagorean equality

\|\mbox{\boldmath$D$}({\bf x})\nabla\varphi^{ss}({\bf x})\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}({\bf x})}^{2}+\|\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}({\bf x})\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}({\bf x})}^{2}=\|{\bf b}({\bf x})\|_{\mbox{\boldmath{\footnotesize{$D$}}}^{-1}({\bf x})}^{2}\quad\text{at}\quad{\bf x}=\hat{{\bf x}}(t),

which is equivalent to Eq. (
22
) (or
\nabla\varphi^{ss}\cdot\gamma=0
) at
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
. Of course, it is just the leading asymptotic of Eq. (
30
).

Below, we justify the asymptotics of
F
,
Q_{hk}
, and
\frac{dF}{dt}
.

Asymptotic of
F
.

Clearly,

\begin{split}F&=S-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\alpha}\ln\pi_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha+O(1)-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\ln C_{\alpha}+\ln R_{\alpha}^{ss}-\alpha\varphi^{ss}\right)R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\alpha\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha-\ln C_{\alpha}+O(1).\end{split}

Asymptotic of
Q_{hk}
.

Since

{\bf J}_{\alpha}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}-\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}-{\bf b}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},

we find

\begin{split}Q_{hk}&=\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\right)R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{(R_{\alpha}^{ss})^{2}}R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+2\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+\alpha\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\gamma\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}R_{\alpha}e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}^{-1}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(1).\end{split}

Asymptotic of
\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}
.

Since

\begin{split}\nabla\ln\frac{f_{\alpha}}{\pi_{\alpha}}&=\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}-\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}-\alpha\left(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi^{ss}\right),\end{split}

we find

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}&=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}-\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}-\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi^{ss}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}}{R_{\alpha}}-\frac{\nabla R_{\alpha}^{ss}}{R_{\alpha}^{ss}}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&\quad-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi^{ss}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\left(\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi^{ss}\right)f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=O(1)-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\varphi\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}+2\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\varphi\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\varphi^{ss}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}f_{\alpha}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=O(1)+I_{8}(\alpha)+I_{9}(\alpha)+I_{10}(\alpha).\end{split}

Clearly,

I_{10}(\alpha)=-\alpha\nabla\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$D$}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))\nabla\varphi^{ss}(\hat{{\bf x}}(t))+O(1).

For
I_{9}(\alpha)
,

\begin{split}I_{9}(\alpha)&=-2\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}R_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=2\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi^{ss}R_{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=O(1).\end{split}

For
I_{8}(\alpha)
,

\begin{split}I_{8}(\alpha)&=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi R_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}\\
&=-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\nabla\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\nabla\varphi R_{\alpha}\right)e^{-\alpha\varphi}{\rm d}{\bf x}=O(1).\end{split}

Hence, the expected asymptotic of
\frac{{\rm d}F}{{\rm d}t}
follows.

Appendix D
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Consider the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with linear drift
{\bf b}({\bf x})=\mbox{\boldmath$B$}{\bf x}
and constant diffusion matrix
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}
(symmetric and positive definite), where
B
is a
N\times N
matrix. Then,

f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\alpha}(t)}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\alpha}^{-1}(t)\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\right\},

where the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\alpha}(t)
is given by

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{2}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(t-s)}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}(t-s)}ds.

Introducing

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}(t-s)}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}(t-s)}ds,

one can rewrite
f_{\alpha}
as

f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\right\}.

The WKB ansatz (
19
) holds precisely in this case:

f_{\alpha}({\bf x},t)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\right)^{N/2}R_{0}({\bf x},t)e^{-\alpha\varphi({\bf x},t)},

(31)

where
R_{0}({\bf x},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)}}
is independent of
{\bf x}
and

\varphi({\bf x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\left({\bf x}-e^{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}t}{\bf x}_{0}\right).

It is well-known that

\begin{split}S=\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{\alpha}(t)\right)=\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)\right)-\frac{N}{2}\ln\alpha.\end{split}

It follows from Jacobi’s formula and the formula
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{\prime}(t)=2\mbox{\boldmath$D$}+\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)+\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$B$}^{\top}

[
42
]
that

\begin{split}\frac{{\rm d}S}{{\rm d}t}&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\ln\left(\det\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(t)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{\prime}(t)\right]\\
&=\text{tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$D$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(t)\right]+\text{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\\
&=\mbox{\boldmath$D$}:\nabla\nabla\varphi+\nabla\cdot{\bf b}.\end{split}
