Stability of Homomorphisms, Coverings and Cocycles II: Examples, Applications and Open problems
Michael Chapman, Alexander Lubotzky

Abstract
. Coboundary expansion (with 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} coefficients), and variations on it, have been the focus of intensive research in the last two decades. It was used to study random complexes, property testing, and above all Gromov’s topological overlapping property. In part I of this paper, we extended the notion of coboundary expansion (and its variations) to cochains with permutation coefficients , equipped with the normalized Hamming distance. We showed that this gives a unified language for studying covering stability of complexes, as well as stability of group homomorphisms — a topic that drew a lot of attention in recent years. In this part, we extend the theory to the permutation coefficients setting. This gives some new results, even for 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} coefficients, opens several new directions of research, and suggests a pattern to proving the existence of non-sofic groups. Along the way, we solve the dimension 2 2 case of a problem of Gromov, exhibiting a family of bounded degree coboundary expanders with 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} coefficients.

3.
Cheeger constants with permutation coefficients of complexes and groups

As was proved in Part I of this paper
[
18
]
, homomorphism stability, covering stability and cocycle stability are equivalent. Furthermore, the first cocycle Cheeger constant (

is positive if and only if the cocycle stability rate of

is linear. This leads to two natural problems:

(1)

Which polygonal complexes have a positive cocycle Cheeger constant in the first dimension with permutation coefficients? Furthermore, can you bound the Cheeger constant of (naturally arising) families of complexes in a uniform manner? In Sections

(2)

By Fact 3.4, Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 in Part I

]

if and only if

of

Since homomorphism stability is a widely studied subject, there are many examples of presentations, and thus complexes, which are

Claim 3.6

Let
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{E}(\mathcal{X})\to{\rm Sym}(n)
be a
1

. Hence

\begin{split}\|\alpha(\pi)\|&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\prod_{i=1}^{k}\alpha(\sigma_{i}\pi_{i}\bar{\sigma}_{i})}}\right\|\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\underbrace{\|\alpha(\sigma_{i}\pi_{i}\bar{\sigma}_{i})\|}_{\|\alpha(\sigma_{i})\alpha(\pi_{i})\alpha(\bar{\sigma}_{i})\|}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\alpha(\pi_{i})}}\right\|,\end{split}

where the second line is by the triangle inequality of the normalized Hamming metric, and the third line is by the fact

and the conjugate invariance of the normalized Hamming metric.

\|\delta\hat{f}\|=\frac{|R^{\prime\prime}|}{|R^{\prime\prime}|+|R^{\prime}|}\|\delta f\|
and similarly
d_{h}(\hat{f},Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle},{\rm Sym})))=\frac{|S^{\prime\prime}|}{|S^{\prime\prime}|+|S^{\prime}|}d_{h}(f,Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S^{\prime\prime}|R^{\prime\prime}\rangle},{\rm Sym})))
. Thus,

\begin{split}\frac{\|\delta\hat{f}\|}{d_{h}(\hat{f},Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S|R\rangle},{\rm Sym}))}&=\frac{|R^{\prime\prime}|(|S^{\prime\prime}|+|S^{\prime}|)}{|S^{\prime\prime}|(|R^{\prime\prime}|+|R^{\prime}|)}\frac{\|\delta f\|}{d_{h}(f,Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S^{\prime\prime}|R^{\prime\prime}\rangle},{\rm Sym}))}\\
&\leq(1+|S^{\prime}|)\cdot\frac{\|\delta f\|}{d_{h}(f,Z^{1}(\mathcal{X}_{\langle S^{\prime\prime}|R^{\prime\prime}\rangle},{\rm Sym}))}\\
&\leq(1+|S^{\prime}|)\varepsilon^{\prime}.\end{split}

By choosing
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+|S^{\prime}|}
, we deduce the claim.

4.3.
Property
(\tau)
and edge expansion of coverings

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group, and
S
a finite generating set of
\Gamma
. Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
S
, viewed as a an element of the group ring

. Since every unitary representation
\rho

\Gamma
which acts on a Hilbert space

can be extended to the group ring, we can define the operator
\rho(\mu)\in B(\mathbb{H})
. Namely,
\rho(\mu)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\rho(s)
. If there exists
\gamma>0
such that, regardless of the chosen unitary representation
\rho
, the spectrum of
\rho(\mu)
is contained in
[-1,1-\gamma]\cup\{1\}
, we say that
\mu
has spectral gap
\gamma
.
If this happens, then
\Gamma
has Kazhdan’s property (T), in which case a spectral gap exists for any measure with a finite generating support. If the same is true only for finite dimensional representations which factor through finite quotients of
\Gamma

has property
(\tau)
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected polygonal complex. If
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X},*)
has
(\tau)
, then there is a
\gamma=\gamma(\mathcal{X})>0

\mathcal{Y}
of

.

Proof sketch.

This proposition depends only on the
1
-dimensional skeleton of the complex, both in its statement and in the following proof. This is a generalization of the well known result (cf.

56
]
) that the Schreier graphs of groups with property
(\tau)

\mathcal{X}
is a presentation complex, namely has a single vertex.

Assume

is a vertex of
G(\mathcal{X})
. Given a spanning tree
T

G(\mathcal{X})
, we can choose for every edge

which originates at

and hence it is an element of the fundamental group. Moreover, this is a generating set, and we denote it by

. Take the uniform measure on

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a connected

\mathcal{X}

with respect to the generating set

. Hence, the adjacency operator on

\mathcal{Z}

\mathcal{Z}
is a good edge expander.

Now,
\mathcal{Z}
can be a great expander while

is not. As an example, take

and note that

Let

\mu_{\sigma,1}
. Given a simplical complex

, we can define an adjacency operator

on the complex valued functions on its vertices: Given

namely, we sample an edge

according to
\mu_{1}
conditioned on the edge containing

:

\langle f,g\rangle=\Ex_{v\sim\mu_{0}}[\overline{f(v)}g(v)].

The adjacency operator

has the following properties:

•

The constant functions are eigenfunctions of it with eigenvalue
1
.

•

It is self adjoint with respect to the chosen inner product.

We say that

is a
\lambda
-spectral expander if the second eigenvalue of

is bounded by
\lambda
from above. We say that

is a
\lambda
-local spectral expander if the links

are

-spectral expanders for all

.

Theorem 4.11

(Oppenheim’s trickling down theorem,
[
24
,
65
]
)
.

Let

-dimensional simplicial complex with fully supported descendent measures on its cells. Assume also that

-spectral expander.

Proposition 4.12

(Weighted Cheeger inequalities: The lower bound)
.

If

is a

.

Though the proof method of Proposition

6.4.
Efficient Stability of finite group presentations

Short presentations of finite groups were the focus of many studies — see the introduction of
[
38
]
.
Though finding short presentations of a given (family of) finite group(s) is a natural problem, this search seems to be in conflict with these presentations having a (uniformly) good stability rate. For example, the work of Ben-Sasson–Guruswami–Kaufman–Sudan–Viderman
[
12
]
, which proves that locally testable codes need to be
redundant
, can be interpreted — in the language developed further in this section — as a proof that a specific type of short presentations of certain finite abelian groups must have a cocycle Cheeger constant that tends to zero with their rank. On the other hand, the most expansive presentation — the multipilication table one — has, by
[
8
]
, a lower bound on its Cheeger constant which is independent of the specific group. This suggests a tradeoff between
shortness
of a presentation and its
stability
. It will be interesting to understand this tradeoff better.

Let us be more concrete.
Recall that for every given finite group, and any given presentation of it, its cocycle Cheeger constant in dimension
1
with permutation coefficients is positive. But, as was proved in Claim
3.11
, one can choose presentations with a
Cheeger constant arbitrarily close to
0
.
Consider a family of finite groups
G_{i}
, given by presentations
\langle S_{i}|R_{i}\rangle
. The goal is to relate the complexity of these presentations and their stability rate in a uniform manner. Here, complexity can have several interpretations: It can be taken, for example, to be
\log|S|\cdot\sum_{r\in R}|r|
, where
|r|
is the length of the relation
r
, or to be the bit length of an encoding of the presentation (which can be shorter due to the fact exponents can be written in binary and not unary).

For a first example, take the groups
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
for
k\to\infty
. On the one hand, their multiplication table presentations, which are exponentially more complicated than the shortest possible presentations, have a cocycle Cheeger constant of at least
\frac{1}{3000}
regardless of
k
.
On the other hand, the much shorter presentation on
k
generators, where every generator is required to be of order
2
, and every pair of generators are required to commute, have
cocycle Cheeger constants which are
O(\frac{1}{k})
(see
[
53
,
20
]
). Furthermore, in
[
12
]
it is shown that if
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
is presented as a quotient of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(equipped with the above short presentation) and one adds only
n-k
new relations and the associated code has linear distance, then the Cheeger constant tends to zero with
k\to\infty.

The second natural example to study, which is completely open, is that of finite simple groups. Clearly, studying the stability rate of various standard presentations of the alternating and symmetric groups seem to be a natural problem. But, we want to highlight the following case: The groups
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
(and more generally, groups of Lie type) have two outstanding families of presentations —

(1)

The Steinberg presentations
: Let
e_{ij}(a)
be the matrices with
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
in the
ij^{\rm th}
position, and
0
evereywhere else. For every
i\neq j
and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, let
U_{ij}(a)={\rm Id}+e_{ij}(a)
. Take the
U_{ij}(a)
’s as generators, and the relations to be
U_{ij}(a)U_{ij}(b)=U_{ij}(a+b)
and for every
i\neq j,k\neq\ell
and
i\neq\ell
,
[U_{ij}(a),U_{k\ell}(b)]={\bf 1}_{j=k}U_{i\ell}(a\cdot b)
. The complexity of this presentation is
\Omega(p)
, which is polynomial in the size of the group — namely, an expansive presentation.

(2)

The Guralnik–Kantor–Kassabov–Lubotzky presentations
: Here, we do not describe the presentations in full, but describe their complexity. There is a constant
C
, such that regardless of
p
and
n
, the presentations use less than
C
generators, less than
C
relations, and the length of the relations is at most
C(\log n+\log p)
. See
[
38
,
57
]
for all the details. The complexity of these presentations is logarithmic in the size of the group. Note that when one fixes
p
and lets
n\to\infty
, these presentations are of complexity which is even
\log\log
the size of the group.

In the spirit of the more understood abelian case, we conjecture the following:

Conjecture 6.7
.

There is a uniform lower bound on the cocycle Cheeger constant in permutations of the Steinberg presentations, at least when fixing
n
and tending
p
to
\infty
. On the contrary, the cocycle Cheeger constants in permutations of the GKKL presentations tend to zero when either
n
or
p
tending to
\infty
.

Remark 6.8
.

Note that the length of the relations in the GKKL presentations is not bounded. This is not natural in the viewpoint of property testing, since it implies a non-bounded query complexity for the tester. Nevertheless, one can study the triangulated versions of these presentations, and amend this problem.

We now want to make the tradeoff problem between shortness and stability rate even more concrete. To that end, recall the recent MIP*=RE breakthrough
[
46
]
in quantum information theory, which resolved Connes’ embedding problem (cf.
[
17
]
). A major technical tool, which was developed and used in this work, is a quantum soundness result on a specific two player non-local game based on the individual degree Reed–Muller code

Inspired by this deep result, we suggest the following parameter setting as a challenge.

Problem 6.9

(Efficient stability in permutations)
.

Find a family of finite groups
\{G\}
, with presentations
G\cong\langle S|R\rangle

•

The presentation is (somewhat) short

\begin{split}|S|,|R|&=\textrm{polylog}(|G|)\\
\forall r\in R\ \colon|r|&=\textrm{polylog}(|G|).\end{split}

The stability rate is good

\rho(\varepsilon)=\textrm{polyloglog}(|G|)\cdot\varepsilon^{c}

It would be more natural to ask for

The motivation for this parameter setting is that it allows for

Appendix A
Weighted Cheeger lower bound

The following argument is
very
standard. It appeared in its uniformly weighted form in Proposition 5.5 and Remark 5.7 in Part I of this paper
[
18
]
, using the
L^{2}
-Poincare inequality described in Lemma 5.4. See also
[
27
,
3
,
2
]
for the original proofs. The main idea is to use the Reyleigh quotient definition of the second largest eigenvalue. We add it only for completeness.

Proof of Proposition
4.12
.

Let
\alpha\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
be a non-coboundary
0
-cochain. Let
B
be the support of
\alpha
, and let
\beta=\mu_{0}(B)
. Define
f\colon V(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

f(v)=\begin{cases}1-\beta&v\in B,\\
-\beta&v\notin B.\end{cases}

Note that

\langle f,{\bf 1}\rangle=\Ex_{v\sim\mu_{0}}[f(v)]=\mu_{0}(B)(1-\beta)-\mu_{0}(\bar{B})\beta=0,

where
\bar{B}
is the complement of
B
in
V(\mathcal{X})
, and that

\langle f,f\rangle=\Ex_{v\sim\mu_{0}}[f(v)^{2}]=\beta(1-\beta)^{2}+(1-\beta)\beta^{2}=\beta(1-\beta).

Hence, by the Rayleigh quotient definition of the second eigenvalue of an operator, we have

\langle f,Af\rangle\leq\lambda\langle f,f\rangle=\lambda\beta(1-\beta).

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\langle f,Af\rangle&=\Ex_{xy\sim\mu_{1}}[f(x)f(y)]\\
&=-2\mu_{1}(E(B,\bar{B}))\beta(1-\beta)+\mu_{1}(E(B,B))(1-\beta)^{2}+\mu_{1}(E(\bar{B},\bar{B}))\beta^{2},\end{split}

where
E(C,D)
is the set of oriented edges in
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(1)
with origin in
C
and endpoint in
D
. Note that by definition,
2\mu_{1}(E(B,\bar{B}))=\mu_{1}(E(B,\bar{B}))+\mu_{1}(E(\bar{B},B))=\|\delta\alpha\|
. Furthermore,
d(\alpha,Z^{0}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{F}_{2}))=\min(\beta,1-\beta)
. Now, when sampling an edge
xy\sim\mu_{1}
, the probability
x\in B
is
\beta
by the fact
\mu_{0}
is descending from
\mu_{1}
. Hence,
\mu_{1}(E(B,B))=\mu_{1}(B,\mathcal{X}(0))-\mu_{1}(E(B,\bar{B}))=\beta-0.5\|\delta\alpha\|
. Similarly,
\mu_{1}(E(\bar{B},\bar{B}))=(1-\beta)-0.5\|\delta\alpha\|
. All in all,

\begin{split}\langle f,Af\rangle&=-\|\delta\alpha\|\beta(1-\beta)+(\beta-0.5\|\delta\alpha\|)(1-\beta)^{2}+(1-\beta-0.5\|\delta\alpha\|)\beta^{2}\\
&=-0.5\|\delta\alpha\|+\beta(1-\beta).\end{split}

Combining all, we get

0.5\|\delta\alpha\|\geq(1-\lambda)\beta(1-\beta)\geq(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{\min(\beta,1-\beta)}{2},

which finishes the proof by multiplying both sides of the inequality by
2
.
∎
