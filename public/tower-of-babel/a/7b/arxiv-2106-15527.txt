Constraints on magic state protocols from the statistical mechanics of Wigner negativity Preprint: APS/123-QED
Nikolaos Koukoulekidis, David Jennings

Abstract
Magic states are key ingredients in schemes to realize universal fault-tolerant quantum computation. Theories of magic states attempt to quantify this computational element via monotones and determine how these states may be efficiently transformed into useful forms. Here, we develop a statistical mechanical framework based on majorization to describe Wigner negative magic states for qudits of odd prime dimension processed under Clifford circuits. We show that majorization allows us to both quantify disorder in the Wigner representation and derive upper bounds for magic distillation. These bounds are shown to be tighter than other bounds, such as from mana and thauma, and can be used to incorporate hardware physics, such as temperature dependence and system Hamiltonians. We also show that a subset of single-shot Rényi entropies remain well-defined on quasi-distributions, are fully meaningful in terms of data processing and can acquire negative values that signal magic. We find that the mana of a magic state is the measure of divergence of these Rényi entropies as one approaches the Shannon entropy for Wigner distributions, and discuss how distillation lower bounds could be obtained in this setting. This use of majorization for quasi-distributions could find application in other studies of non-classicality, and raises nontrivial questions in the context of classical statistical mechanics.

Results

Phase space representation of quantum mechanics

Central to our construction is the representation of any quantum state or quantum operation on a system of dimension
d
in terms of quasi-probability representations on a discrete phase space
Gross2006
;
Ferrie_2008
. This construction is a discrete version of Wigner representations in quantum optics
Wigner_1932
;
Vourdas_2004
;
Kenfack_2004
.

Consider a
d
–dimensional quantum system with Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{d}
, and let
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,\dots,|d-1\rangle\}
denote the standard computational basis, defined over
\mathbb{Z}_{d}=\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
. On this space, generalised Pauli matrices
X,Z
can be defined by their respective roles as shift and phase operators, acting on the basis states as follows,

\displaystyle X\ket{k}

\displaystyle=\ket{k + 1}

(1)

\displaystyle Z\ket{k}

\displaystyle=\omega^{k}\ket{k}.

(2)

Here
\omega\coloneqq e^{2\pi i/d}
is the
d
-th root of unity and addition is taken modulo
d
. From these we can construct a phase space
{\cal P}_{d}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
that provides a complete representation of the quantum system. Given a point
\boldsymbol{z}\coloneqq(q,p)
we define a displacement operator,

D_{\boldsymbol{z}}\coloneqq\tau^{qp}X^{q}Z^{p},\

(3)

where the phase factor
\tau\coloneqq-\omega^{1/2}
ensures unitarity. We assume going forward that
d
is an odd prime
Appleby_2005
. The qubit case
d=2
is known to have obstacles to using a Wigner representation to quantify magic in terms of negativity
Mermin_1990
;
cit:howard2
, although recent work
Raussendorf_qubits
has shown how to extend the representation to include qubits by enlarging the phase space. For a composite system with dimension
d_{\rm tot}=d_{1}\dots d_{n}
and Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes{\cal H}_{d_{n}}
, we define displacement operators as

D_{\boldsymbol{z}}\coloneqq D_{(q_{1},p_{1})}\otimes\dots\otimes D_{(q_{n},p_{n})},

(4)

where now we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in{\cal P}_{d_{1}}\times\dots\times{\cal P}_{d_{n}},

to denote the phase space point for the composite system.
For simplicity, we assume
n
copies of a
d
–dimensional system
d_{1}=d_{2}=\cdots=d
, and therefore we have that
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2n}
.

The displacement operators form the Heisenberg-Weyl group
Folland_1989
;
Bengtsson_2006
under matrix multiplication modulo phases,

{\rm{HW}}_{d}^{n}\coloneqq\{\tau^{k}D_{\boldsymbol{z}}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d},\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}^{n}\}.

(5)

The Clifford group
{\cal C}_{d}^{n}
is then defined as the set of unitaries that normalise the Heisenberg-Weyl group
Appleby_2005
. We may define the pure stabilizer states as those states obtained by acting on
|0\rangle
with Clifford unitaries
Gross2006
and, finally, the full set of stabilizer states as the convex hull of all pure stabilizers, namely all probabilistic mixtures of states of the form
U|0\rangle\langle 0|U^{\dagger}
where
U
is Clifford.

Wigner representation for quantum states and quantum operations

In order to provide a complete decomposition of arbitrary quantum states and quantum operations we now define a basis of Hermitian observables that transforms covariantly under the action of the Clifford group. At every point
\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}
we define the phase-point operator

A_{\boldsymbol{z}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\cal P}_{d}}\omega^{\eta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})}D_{\boldsymbol{y}},

(6)

where
\eta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})
is the symplectic inner product between any two points
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}\in{\cal P}_{d}
, and is given explicitly by

\eta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbbm{1}\\
-\mathbbm{1}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{z},

(7)

where
0,\mathbbm{1}
denote the
n\times n
zero and identity matrices.

The phase-point operators form an orthogonal operator basis with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, as discussed in Supplementary Note 1.
Therefore, any quantum state
\rho
can be expressed as a linear combination of them,
\rho=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})A_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, where the coefficients
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
give the Wigner distribution of state
\rho
,

W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}{\rm{tr}}[A_{\boldsymbol{z}}\rho].

(8)

For any quantum state
\rho
, the Wigner distribution
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is readily seen to be a
d^{2}
-dimensional quasi-distribution over
{\cal P}_{d}
. More precisely,
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a real-valued function on
{\cal P}_{d}
with the normalisation property
\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})=1
(see Supplementary Note 1 for details). Moreover, the above Wigner representation has recently been shown to be the only non-contextual quasi-probability representation of stabilizer theories in odd prime dimensions
schmid2021noncontextual
.

Any quantum channel
{\cal E}
also admits a Wigner representation. If
{\cal E}
maps some quantum system
A
of dimension
d_{A}
to a quantum system
B
of dimension
d_{B}
and
{\cal J}({\cal E})=(id\otimes{\cal E})(|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|)
, with
|\phi^{+}\rangle=d_{A}^{-1/2}\sum_{k}|kk\rangle
, is its associated Choi state
watrous_2018
, then we can define

W_{{\cal E}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{z})\coloneqq d_{A}^{2}W_{{\cal J}({\cal E})}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\oplus\boldsymbol{y}),

(9)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},-p_{1},\dots,-p_{n})
can be viewed as the time-reversed version of
\boldsymbol{z}
in the discrete phase space, where momenta are reflected while position coordinates remain unchanged.

Magic theories for quantum computation

A number of magic theories exist, where magic states are computational resource states with respect to a class of quantum operations that are considered free
Gour_2019
. One natural class of free operations are those obtained from Clifford unitaries, Pauli measurements and the ability to discard quantum systems. However, there are also other candidates
cit:ahmadi
;
cit:seddon
;
Wang_2019
.

In any theory of magic, one route to bounding distillation rates is through a magic monotone. A magic monotone is a real-valued function of any quantum state
M(\rho)
that is monotonically non-increasing under the free operations of the magic theory. More precisely
M(\sigma)\leq M(\rho)
whenever it is possible to convert
\rho
into
\sigma
using free operations.

One prominent magic monotone is the mana of a state
cit:veitch2
, defined as

{\cal M}(\rho)\coloneqq\log(2\hspace{1pt}{\rm sn}(\rho)+1),

(10)

where the sum-negativity
cit:veitch2
is the sum of the negative components in
W_{\rho}
,

{\rm sn}(\rho)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}:W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})<0}\absolutevalue{W_\rho(\z)}.

(11)

Using the fact that
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a normalized quasi-distribution, we can also write
{\cal M}(\rho)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|
.

Mana is an additive magic monotone, and the fact that it is non-increasing under free operations provides a constraint on magic state interconvertibility.

In this work we develop bounds that apply to any reasonable magic theory. The central idea is to apply majorization theory to the quasi-distributions that arise for magic states. In the next section we explain how majorization relates to the free operations in a theory of magic.

Stochastic representation of magic protocols

Within the Wigner representation for odd prime dimension, it is well-known that all positively represented states used in Clifford circuits admit an efficient classical simulation
cit:mari
, so negativity is a necessary resource for universal fault-tolerant quantum computing
cit:veitch
. Therefore, the free states in any magic theory are required to be a subset of

{\cal F}\coloneqq\{\rho:W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\geq 0\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}\}.

(12)

Our focus is on states with negativity, so the particular choice of free states is not critical for our analysis. The remaining component that defines any magic theory is the set of free quantum operations. The most basic assumption we require on free operations is that they send any free state to another free state.

Any magic state protocol will correspond to a quantum channel
{\cal E}
, so from Eq. (
9
) it admits a Wigner representation
W_{{\cal E}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{z})
that acts as a transition matrix mapping phase space points
\boldsymbol{z}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}
. The representation obeys the relation

W_{{\cal E}(\rho)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}}W_{{\cal E}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{z})W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(13)

for any
\rho
. Since the magic protocol sends free states to free states,
{\cal E}
sends all positively represented quantum state to other positively represented quantum states. In such cases, it can be shown
Wang_2019
that if
{\cal E}
is a free operation then the associated Wigner representation
W_{{\cal E}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{z})
must form a stochastic matrix. In particular, all stabilizer operations correspond to stochastic matrices in the Wigner representation.

We note however, that not all stochastic maps on the phase space correspond to valid quantum operations. The reason is that the maps must also respect the symplectic structure of the phase space, which is an additional non-trivial constraint.

In what follows, we shall assume that we have a magic theory
{\cal R}=({\cal F},{\cal O})
in which the free states
{\cal F}
are represented by non-negative Wigner functions, while the free operations
{\cal O}
are stochastic maps in the Wigner representation. Our analysis of magic in this phase space setting makes use of majorization theory, which we describe in the next section.

Quantifying disorder without entropies

Majorisation
cit:marshall
;
Blackwell_1953
is a collection of powerful tools that has found many applications in quantum information theory
Nielsen_1999
;
cit:cwiklinski
;
cit:lostaglio2
;
cit:gour
;
cit:gour2
;
Horodecki_2003
;
Puchala_2013
;
Vallejos_2021
.
It describes the disorder of distributions that undergo stochastic transformations, and in its simplest form defines a pre-order on probability distributions. Given two distributions
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
over
n
outcomes, we say that
\boldsymbol{p}
majorizes
\boldsymbol{q}
, denoted
\boldsymbol{p}\succ\boldsymbol{q}
, if there exists a bistochastic map
A=(A_{ij})
such that
A\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{q}
, where bistochastic means that
A_{ij}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}A_{ij}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}=1
. It can be shown
cit:marshall
that the condition
\boldsymbol{p}\succ\boldsymbol{q}
over probability distributions is equivalent to
n-1
inequalities, which can be checked efficiently.

There is a natural generalisation, which is called
\boldsymbol{d}
–majorization
Veinott_1971
, or in the context of thermodynamics, thermo-majorization
cit:horodecki2013
. For a fixed probability distribution
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
with positive components, we define majorization relative to
\boldsymbol{r}
as
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\boldsymbol{r}}\boldsymbol{q}
, if and only if there exists a stochastic map
A
such that
A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}
and
A\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{q}
. The original majorization condition between probability distributions corresponds to the case
\boldsymbol{r}=(1/n,\dots,1/n)
.

In fact, we can further generalize to relative majorization
Blackwell_1953
;
Ruch_1976
;
ruch_mixing_1978
;
Renes_2016
;
Buscemi_2017
;
Rethinasamy_2020
, defined as an ordering between pairs of vectors and write

(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})

(14)

if and only if there is a stochastic map
A
such that
A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
and
A\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{q}
. We retrieve
\boldsymbol{d}
–majorization when
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
.

Quasi-probability majorization and non-monotonic Lorenz curves

Our analysis applies majorization to magic states at the level of the associated Wigner distributions. Since these are in general quasi-distributions, it is important to check how majorization is computed for these cases and what differences quasi-distributions bring over genuine probability distributions.

We also make use of the notion of a Lorenz curve of a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
relative to some other vector
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a vector
\boldsymbol{w}
we define
\boldsymbol{w}^{\downarrow}
to be the re-arrangement of the components of
\boldsymbol{w}
into decreasing order. Given two
n
–component vectors
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
, we first define
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\widetilde{w}_{i})
, where
\widetilde{w}_{i}\coloneqq w_{i}/r_{i}
, as the vector of component-wise ratios between
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
.
We can now define the Lorenz curve of
\boldsymbol{w}
relative to
\boldsymbol{r}
, denoted
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
, as the piece-wise linear function that passes through
(0,0)
and the
n
points

(x_{k},L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k}))=\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)},\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{\pi(i)}\right),

(15)

where
R\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}
and
\pi
is the permutation on
n
objects mapping
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\downarrow}
. The form of this requires that
\boldsymbol{r}
has no zero components, which we shall assume without loss of generality as the rank of a quantum state is not operationally meaningful.

In the usual case where
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are both probability distributions the Lorenz curve is defined on the interval
[0,1]
, and rises monotonically until it reaches the value
1
at
x=1
. The value
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=1
is a global maximum. Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
are all valid probability distributions with
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
having positive components, then

(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\mbox{ if and only if }L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x),

for all
x\in[0,1]
.
This result is shown in
ruch_mixing_1978
and we reproduce it in Supplementary Note 2.
It provides a simple way of computing whether relative majorization holds between pairs of probability distributions.

However, if
\boldsymbol{w}
is a quasi-probability distribution with negative values, and
\boldsymbol{r}
a regular probability distribution things are different. Now the Lorenz curve is no longer monotonically increasing, but is a concave function that breaks through the
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=1
barrier at an interior point and attains some non-trivial maximum
L_{\star}
above the value
1
, before decreasing monotonically to
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=1
at the end-point
x=1
. See Fig.
3
for examples of non-monotonic Lorenz curves for quasi-distributions. Within quantum theory, the breaking of this barrier is associated with the degree of non-classicality.

Relative majorization is usually considered for probability distributions, so we need to verify that the same Lorenz curve conditions apply to quasi-distributions before proceeding with our analysis. This can be done from first principles, but a simpler way to see that this holds is to reduce to the problem of genuine distributions by first ‘masking’ the negativity in the quasi-distribution
\boldsymbol{w}
using the reference distribution
\boldsymbol{r}
and then applying the conditions for relative majorization. This negativity masking then gives the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be quasi-distributions, and let
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be probability distributions with non-zero components. Then,
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Since the components of
\boldsymbol{r}
are strictly positive, there always exists an
\epsilon>0
such that
\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}+(1-\epsilon)\boldsymbol{r}
is a genuine probability distribution. A similar result holds for
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
and we choose
\epsilon
sufficiently small so that both
\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
are probability distributions. We now have that
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
(\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
. This equivalence holds because there exists a stochastic map
A
such that
A\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
and
A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
if and only if

A[\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}+(1-\epsilon)\boldsymbol{r}]=\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}+(1-\epsilon)\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\mbox{ and }A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}.

(16)

In terms of a Lorenz curve condition we have that
(\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
L_{\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Additionally, we show in Supplementary Note 2 that the Lorenz curve for any quasi-distribution obeys the relation

L_{a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=aL_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)+bx,

(17)

for any
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
.
This relation implies that
L_{\boldsymbol{w}_{\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x)
if and only if
\epsilon L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)+(1-\epsilon)x\geq\epsilon L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x)+(1-\epsilon)x
.
Finally, the
(1-\epsilon)x
terms cancel on both sides and we get the required relative majorization conditions that
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, as required.
∎

With this theory in place, we turn to magic distillation protocols.

Magic distillation bounds with fixed-points

We can now consider how majorization constrains magic distillation rates starting in this section with an abstract setting based on the fixed-point structure of the physical operations accessible to us. We show that any magic theory can always be decomposed in terms of ‘sub-theories’ with a particular fixed-point structure, which allows the use of
\boldsymbol{d}
–majorization.
Later, we drop the requirement of fixed-points and instead use the full relative majorization, before giving general entropic constraints.

We begin by defining the following sub-theory of any magic theory
{\cal R}
.

Definition 2
.

Given a theory of magic
{\cal R}=({\cal F},{\cal O})
, we define the sub-theory
{\cal R}_{\sigma}=({\cal F},{\cal O}_{\sigma})
, in which the free operations are

{\cal O}_{\sigma}\coloneqq\{{\cal E}\in{\cal O}:{\cal E}(\sigma)=\sigma\},

(18)

namely those free operations that leave the distinguished state
\sigma\in{\cal F}
invariant.

This gives a simple way to break up any theory into smaller, more manageable parts, and where the union over all sub-theories returns the parent theory, so we are not discarding any information by breaking up the theory in this way. We make this precise as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\cal R}=({\cal F},{\cal O})
be a theory of magic.
Then a transformation
\rho_{1}\rightarrow\rho_{2}
is possible in
{\cal R}
if and only if the transformation
\rho_{1}\rightarrow\rho_{2}
is possible in at least one sub-theory
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.

The proof of this is straightforward.

Proof.

Suppose the interconversion is possible in a
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
via some
{\cal E}\in{\cal O}_{\sigma}
. But since
{\cal O}_{\sigma}\subseteq{\cal O}
it is also possible in
{\cal R}
. Conversely, suppose the transformation is possible in
{\cal R}
via some
{\cal E}
in
{\cal O}
. The free states
{\cal F}
are a closed, bounded set and moreover the image of
{\cal F}
under the map
{\cal E}
is in
{\cal F}
. By the Brouwer fixed-point theorem
cit:brouwer
, this mapping must therefore have a fixed point
\sigma\in{\cal F}
, so
{\cal E}\in{\cal O}_{\sigma}
and the interconversion is possible in
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.
∎

We can compare with resource monotones, which are also called resource measures. A complete set of measures
\{{\cal M}_{i}\}_{i}
is such that
\rho\rightarrow\sigma
if and only if
{\cal M}_{i}(\rho)\geq{\cal M}_{i}(\sigma)
for all
i
. The above set of sub-theories can therefore be viewed as a complete set of “co-measures” for the theory, where the parent resource theory’s complex pre-order of states is mapped to a simpler pre-order for a particular
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.
In the next section we show that a given
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
can be approximated by a majorization pre-order, and from this we compute magic distillation bounds.

Majorisation of Wigner distributions with fixed-point structure

We consider some
\sigma\in{\cal F}
, and its corresponding
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
. We are interested in the ability to transform many copies of some noisy magic state
\rho
towards a more pure form of magic. The state
\rho
is assumed to have negativity in the Wigner representation, so
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})<0
for some regions of
\boldsymbol{z}\in{\cal P}_{d}
. The state
\sigma
is assumed to have a Wigner distribution with
W_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{z}
in the phase space.
This is justified because a non-full rank state
\sigma
can be handled as a limiting case in which we first add an infinitesimal fraction of depolarising noise
\epsilon(\mathbbm{1}/d)
and then take
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

The free operations within the magic theory
{\cal R}
are represented by stochastic maps, and within
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
by stochastic maps that leave
W_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})
invariant. Therefore, a necessary condition for magic state transformations
\rho_{1}\rightarrow\rho_{2}
within
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
will be that

W_{\rho_{1}}\succ_{W_{\sigma}}W_{\rho_{2}},

(19)

or put another way, that the quasi-distribution
W_{\rho_{1}}
is more ordered than the quasi-distribution
W_{\rho_{2}}
relative to
W_{\sigma}
. To simplify notation, we denote by
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
the Lorenz curve
L_{W_{\rho}|W_{\sigma}}(x)
, and therefore have that

\rho_{1}\rightarrow\rho_{2}\mbox{ within }{\cal R}_{\sigma}\mbox{ implies }L_{\rho_{1}|\sigma}(x)\geq L_{\rho_{2}|\sigma}(x),

(20)

for all
x\in[0,1]
, which restricts the transformations that are possible.

Note that this is not a single numerical constraint, but a family of constraints. For
n
copies of a qudit system of dimension
d
the number of terms in
W_{\rho}
is
d^{2n}
, so imposing the Lorenz curve condition corresponds to exponentially many constraints.

Before computing explicit examples, we state some generic aspects of Lorenz curves for magic states, which allow us to interpret previous magic monotones as features of the curves. The first result gives a simple way to see that the sum-negativity/mana of a magic state is a monotone
cit:veitch2
.

Theorem 4
.

Majorization in any
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
implies the monotonicity of sum-negativity/mana.

Proof.

The sum-negativity of a magic state
\rho
can be written as
sn(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|-1)
.
We make use of the
L_{1}
-norm form of relative majorization, (see Supplementary Note 2), which states that
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\boldsymbol{r}}\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if

\sum_{k}|p_{k}-r_{k}t|\geq\sum_{k}|q_{k}-r_{k}t|,

(21)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Choosing
t=0
we get the single condition that
\sum_{k}|p_{k}|\geq|\sum_{k}|q_{k}|
, independent of the choice of
\boldsymbol{r}
. Applying this to the Wigner quasi-distributions of two quantum states immediately gives the result.
∎

If we have a magic state
\rho
that has negativity in its Wigner representation, then, as discussed, its Lorenz curve over-shoots
1
and reaches a non-trivial maximum
L_{\star}
that depends on the particular state. There is an simple relation between
L_{\star}
and sum-negativity/mana, which is provided by the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Given a magic state
\rho
, the maximum
L_{\star}
of its Lorenz curve
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
is independent of the
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
and equal to
1+{\rm sn}(\rho)
. Moreover, the majorization constraint is stronger than mana in every
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.

The proof of this is given in Supplementary Note 2. Using the Lorenz curve perspective, it is also simple to construct magic monotones. For example, within a given
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
, the area above the horizontal line
y=1
is a magic monotone.

Theorem 6
.

Given a magic state
\rho
and a free state
\sigma
, let
{\cal A}_{\sigma}(\rho)
be the area of the region
\{(x,y):1\leq y\leq L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)\}
. Then
{\cal A}_{\sigma}(\rho)
is a magic monotone for
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Consider the transformation
\rho_{1}\rightarrow\rho_{2}
within
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
. We have that
L_{\rho_{1}|\sigma}(x)
is never below the curve
L_{\rho_{2}|\sigma}(x)
, and therefore the region above
L=1
for
\rho_{2}
is a subset of the corresponding region for
\rho_{1}
. Thus,
{\cal A}_{\sigma}(\rho_{1})\geq{\cal A}_{\sigma}(\rho_{2})
, so
{\cal A}_{\sigma}
is a magic monotone in
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.
∎

Note though, in contrast to mana, the area monotone is specific to
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
, and its value will vary as we change
\sigma
. Therefore its monotonicity depends on the physics of the fixed point and provides a means to analyse magic distillation only under free operations that leave
\sigma
invariant. One can also define an area monotone in the more general setting of relative majorization, where one drops the fixed point emphasis.

Magic distillation bounds for unital protocols

We now construct magic state distillation bounds for protocols that generate unital channels, meaning that
{\cal E}(\mathbbm{1}/d)=\mathbbm{1}/d
.

Our approach works for any odd dimension, but for simplicity we consider qutrit magic states (
d=3
), for which a canonical magic state exists with a simple Wigner distribution. This is the Strange state
|S\rangle
, which is given by

|S\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle),

(22)

in the computational basis. Its distribution
W_{|S\rangle\langle S|}(\boldsymbol{z})
has a single negative value of
-1/3
at
\boldsymbol{z}=(0,0)
and the positive value
1/6
at all other points. We define the
\epsilon
–noisy Strange state as

\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)\coloneqq(1-\epsilon)\ket{\rm{S}}\bra{\rm{S}}+\epsilon\frac{1}{3}\mathbbm{1},

(23)

where
\epsilon
is the depolarizing error parameter. Any magic state
\rho
can be processed via Clifford operations
cit:prakash
;
cit:prakash2
and put into this canonical form for some
\epsilon\geq 0
.

The Wigner distribution of the single-copy,
\epsilon
–noisy Strange state is given by

W_{\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)}(\boldsymbol{z})=(1-\epsilon)W_{|\rm{S}\rangle\langle\rm{S}|}(\boldsymbol{z})+\epsilon W_{\frac{1}{3}\mathbbm{1}}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(24)

with a single negative component

-v(\epsilon)\coloneqq-\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{4}{9}\epsilon\right)

(25)

located at the origin
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}
and positive components

u(\epsilon)\coloneqq\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{18}\epsilon

(26)

at the 8 phase space points
\boldsymbol{z}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. We assume that
\epsilon<3/4
to ensure the presence of negativity in the Wigner distribution.

We now consider the problem of purifying
n
copies of a noisy Strange state
\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}
into a smaller number of copies
n^{\prime}
of a less noisy Strange state
\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes n^{\prime}}
, with
\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
and
n^{\prime}\leq n
. In particular, we compute the Lorenz curves for the transformation

\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes n^{\prime}},

(27)

and use them to bound the magic distillation rate
R(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\coloneqq n^{\prime}/n
.

Due to negativity, the ordering of the components of the rescaled Wigner distribution
W_{\rho_{n}|\sigma_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})\coloneqq W_{\rho_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})/W_{\sigma_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})
, for
\rho_{n}=\rho_{S}(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}
, and
\sigma_{n}=\sigma^{\otimes n}=(\mathbbm{1}/3)^{\otimes n}
, depends on whether
-v(\epsilon)
is raised to an even or odd power. Treating the Wigner distributions as vectors, the component values
w_{i}
and associated multiplicities
m_{i}
in the
n
–copy case are found to be

\displaystyle m_{i}

\displaystyle=8^{i}\binom{n}{i},

(28)

\displaystyle w_{i}

\displaystyle=u^{i}(-v)^{n-i},

(29)

where index
i
runs through
0,\dots,n
, and we assume for simplicity that
n
is even and
\epsilon\leq 3/7
, which implies that
v\geq u
.
All details on the analysis are provided in Supplementary Note 3.

The Lorenz curve
L_{\rho_{n}|\sigma_{n}}(x)
reaches a maximum value of

\displaystyle L_{\star}

\displaystyle\coloneqq L_{\rho_{n}|\sigma_{n}}(x_{\star})=\sum_{i=0}^{n/2}m_{2i}w_{2i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{15-8\epsilon}{9}\right)^{n}>1,

(30)

which occurs at
x=x_{\star}
given by

x_{\star}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{7}{9}\right)^{n}.

(31)

Distillation bounds can be obtained from any part of the Lorenz curve by imposing the constraint that the Lorenz curve of the input state never dips below the curve of the output. One simple constraint can be obtained by considering the initial slopes of the two Lorenz curves.

The coordinates of the first point after the origin for
\rho_{S}(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}
are given by
(1/9^{n},v(\epsilon)^{n})
, while for
\rho_{S}(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes n^{\prime}}
by
(1/9^{n^{\prime}},v(\epsilon^{\prime})^{n^{\prime}})
, and by requiring that the initial slope for the input state Lorenz curve is larger than that of the output state (Proposition
24
in Supplementary Note 2) we find that

R(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\leq R_{\infty}\coloneqq\frac{\log(3-4\epsilon)}{\log(3-4\epsilon')}.

(32)

The choice of denoting the bound by
R_{\infty}
is explained in the derivation of Eq. (
52
) in relation to single-shot entropies. For the limiting case of pure magic states on the output (
\epsilon^{\prime}=0
), this simplifies to

R\leq 1+\frac{\log(1 - \frac{4}{3} \epsilon)}{\log 3}.

(33)

We compare this to other known distillation bounds coming from mana and thauma. The mana bound can be directly calculated as

R\leq\frac{{\cal M}(\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon))}{{\cal M}(\rho_{\rm{S}}(0))}=1+\frac{\log\left(1-\frac{8}{15}\epsilon\right)}{\log\frac{5}{3}}.

(34)

The max–thauma
Wang_2020
is defined as

\theta_{\rm{max}}(\rho)\coloneqq\log\min{\{2\hskip 1.0pt{\rm sn}(V)+1:V\geq\rho\}}

(35)

and can be calculated numerically via a semi-definite program. For the noisy Strange state, the max–thauma bound coincides with the mana bound, and we find that they are both looser than the majorization bound
R_{\infty}
as shown in Fig.
1
.

This figure includes numerical estimates of the optimal majorization bounds
R_{\rm{num}}
obtained by considering the full Lorenz curve, which show that the bound
R_{\infty}
can be further improved. While this is an improvement on prior results, all known distillation protocols have rates much lower than these upper bounds. It remains a major open question to determine what are the best possible rates that can be achieved.

Temperature-dependent bounds for magic distillation protocols

We now show how relative majorization can incorporate the system’s Hamiltonian into magic distillation bounds. The way we do this is by considering how the protocol would disturb a reference equilibrium state
\tau
to some different state
\tau^{\prime}
, if the protocol had been applied to this reference state instead of the actual
n
-copy magic state.

We consider a magic distillation protocol on multiple identical qudits in a noisy magic state
\rho(\epsilon)
, with noise parameter
\epsilon
, sending

\rho(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow{\cal E}(\rho(\epsilon)^{\otimes n})=\rho(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes m}

(36)

with
n\gg 1
,
\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
and
{\cal E}
denoting the quantum channel induced by the protocol. We also assume each qudit has a Hamiltonian
H
and neglect interaction terms. We choose some temperature
T=(k\beta)^{-1}
where
k
is Boltzmann’s constant and
\beta
the inverse temperature. The reference equilibrium state of the
n
qudits is given by

\tau^{\otimes n}=\left(\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{{\cal Z}}\right)^{\otimes n}.

(37)

We may also assume that the reference state
\tau
is not a magic state, and has a strictly positive Wigner distribution
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
.

A given magic protocol on the
n
qudits will correspond to a quantum channel
{\cal E}
. We also assume for simplicity that
U_{\pi}{\cal E}(X)U_{\pi}^{\dagger}={\cal E}(X)
for all
X
and any permutation
U_{\pi}
of the
m
output subsystems. This is justified because the protocol on the input magic state results in state
\rho(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes m}
, which is invariant under permutations. Therefore, we are always allowed to symmetrize the output
{\cal E}(X)
by performing a group average over the permutation group for the output systems without changing the performance of the distillation protocol on
\rho(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}
. Thus, we can assume that
{\cal E}
always outputs a symmetric state in general. This means that
{\cal E}(\tau^{\otimes n})
is a symmetric state on
m
subsystems, so by the quantum de Finetti theorem
hudson_locally_1976
;
christandl_2007
we have that for
m\gg 1

{\cal E}(\tau)\approx\int d\mu(x)\tau_{x}^{\prime\otimes m},

(38)

where
d\mu(x)
is a probability measure over a set
\{\tau^{\prime}_{x}\}
of single qudit states.

Free energy and sub-linear correlations in the thermodynamic limit

To keep our analysis simple we make the following physical assumption. We assume that in the asymptotic/thermodynamic limit
n,m\rightarrow\infty
the correlations generated on the reference equilibrium state are negligible. This implies that
d\mu(x)
is peaked on a particular state
\tau^{\prime}
, and
{\cal E}(\tau^{\otimes n})\approx\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
. This scenario occurs in the context of traditional thermodynamics, and states that the output system is well-described by intrinsic variables that do not scale in
m
, and correlations are sub-linear in
m
. In particular, this allows us to compute a free energy per qudit of the output state.

Non-trivial correlations in the thermodynamic limit can also be considered, but leads to a more complex analysis within our majorization framework of Wigner distributions. In this direction, we highlight recent work in majorization in which stochastic independence and correlations are analysed. It has been shown
muller_2015
that stochastic independence (no correlations) of independent distributions can be viewed as a resource in an extension of catalytic majorization, and leads to a single-shot operational interpretation of the Shannon entropy
muller_2016
;
muller_2019
.

Our bound depends on the von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)
of a state, and the free energy at a particular temperature.
For a state
\tau=e^{-\beta H}/{\cal Z}
, the Helmholtz free energy
F
is given by

F\coloneqq{\rm{tr}}[H\tau]-\beta^{-1}S(\tau)=-\beta^{-1}\log{\cal Z},

(39)

which is obtained from the internal energy via a Legendre transform
Pathria_1997
.

The protocol transforms the equilibrium as
\tau^{\otimes n}\rightarrow{\cal E}(\tau^{\otimes n})=\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
. The protocol does not generate magic on its own, so we assume that the output state
\tau^{\prime}
is also a Wigner-positive state. However, this is generally a non-equilibrium state for the system. Despite this it is useful to associate an effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
to the output state by considering the change
H\rightarrow H^{\prime}
such that equilibrium is restored at the reference temperature
T
. This Hamiltonian is defined by the expression
\tau^{\prime}=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/{\cal Z}^{\prime}
, and has free energy
F^{\prime}=-\beta^{-1}\log{\cal Z}^{\prime}
.

The magic state protocol is now considered by how it transforms the pair of quantum states
(\rho^{\otimes n},\tau^{\otimes n})
and is then constrained by the relative majorization condition
(W_{\rho},W_{\tau})\succ(W_{\rho^{\prime}},W_{\tau^{\prime}})
that holds due to the protocol being a stochastic map in the Wigner representation.

In Supplementary Note 4 we analyse the Lorenz curves of this condition and obtain the following magic distillation bound that combines computational measures
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}
, with terms that depend on the Hamiltonian and reference temperature
T
of the physical system. We state the result for the case of qutrits, but a similar analysis works for general odd-dimension qudits.

Theorem 7
.

Consider a magic distillation protocol on qutrits that transforms
n
copies of an
\epsilon
–noisy Strange state into
m
copies of an
\epsilon^{\prime}
–noisy Strange state, with depolarising errors
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon\leq 3/7
. We also allow pre/post-processing by local Clifford unitaries.

Let
T=(k\beta)^{-1}
be any finite temperature for the physical system and let
H=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}E_{k}|E_{k}\rangle\langle E_{k}|
be the Hamiltonian of each qutrit subsystem in its eigen-decomposition.
Assume that in the thermodynamic limit (
n,m\gg 1
), the protocol applied to the equilibrium state
\tau^{\otimes n}=(e^{-\beta H}/{\cal Z})^{\otimes n}
maps
\tau^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
, where we write
\tau^{\prime}=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/{\cal Z}^{\prime}
for some Hermitian
H^{\prime}
.

Then the asymptotic magic distillation rate
R=m/n
is bounded as

R\leq\dfrac{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon\big)+\beta(\phi-F)}{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon' \big)+\beta(\phi^{\prime}-F^{\prime})},

(40)

where
F
is the free energy of
\tau
, and

\phi=-\beta^{-1}\log\zeta

(41)

with
\zeta
given by the expressions

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-\beta E_{k}},

(42)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle E_{k}|A_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}|E_{k}\rangle,

(43)

and
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
attaining a minimum at
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}
. The primed variables are defined similarly for the output system.

The proof of this is provided in Supplementary Note 4, and follows a similar line to the unital protocol bound.

The above bound depends on:

•

Quantum computational measures
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}
,

•

Thermodynamic quantities
\beta,F,F^{\prime}
,

•

Intermediate terms
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
.

The intermediate terms specify how the energy eigenbasis of the system
\{|E_{k}\rangle\}
relates to the computational stabilizer basis
\{|k\rangle\}
. In particular, the term
\phi
quantifies to what degree a sharp energy value can be associated to the negativity in the Wigner representation. Its form is similar to
F
, and so
\phi
can loosely speaking be viewed as a ‘magic free energy’ term.

The coefficients
\alpha_{k}
may be negative for some
k
, when the Hamiltonian has non-stabilizer eigenstates, but the quantity
\phi
is always well-defined, since the function
\zeta
is always positive for
\tau
in the interior of
{\cal F}
. The quantity
\phi
can diverge if the state
\tau
acquired zero Wigner components, which occurs for
\tau
on the boundary of the set of Wigner-positive states, and is not defined outside of
{\cal F}
. Finally, if
H
has a stabilizer eigenbasis then
\phi=E_{i}
for some
i
, independent of the temperature
T
. Details of this are provided in Supplementary Note 4.

Technical aspects and special cases of free energy bounds

Similar to the unital protocol bounds, the above result is based on only part of the Lorenz curves and so can certainly be tightened with further analysis. The primary role of the pre/post-processing by Clifford unitaries in Theorem
7
is to simplify the form of the bound, and allow it to be expressed in terms of the free energy per particle in a way that does not depend on the parameters
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},\beta
in a complex form. In Supplementary Note 4, we give bounds in which one does not include these Clifford changes of basis, thus having a more non-trivial dependency on the parameters.

Extension of single-shot entropies to quasi-distributions

We have considered Wigner distributions of magic states within a statistical mechanical setting in which it was argued that magic can be viewed as a non-classical form of free energy. This was done at the level of majorization constraints that appear due to stabilizer operations being described by stochastic maps in the Wigner representation. In this representation, non-equilibrium states with substantial free energy have Lorenz curves that deviate noticeably from the line
y=x
. We also found that when considered in a distillation protocol, magic could also be linked to physical free energies in a non-trivial way. We can therefore ask: is it possible to make a more direct link between magic and free energy or entropy?

One obstacle to linking with macroscopic, equilibrium free energy is that the Wigner distribution is generally a quasi-distribution, so the entropy
H(\boldsymbol{w})=-\sum_{i}w_{i}\log w_{i}
of the Wigner distribution is not defined outside the set
{\cal F}
, let alone have a physically meaningful interpretation.

However, it turns out that the analysis for the temperature-dependent bound given by Eq. (
114
) implicitly made use of the single-shot Rényi divergence
renyi_1960

D_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{p}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r})
, which is given by

D_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{p}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r})=\log\max_{i}\frac{p_{i}}{r_{i}}.

(44)

It is clear that this does remain well-defined on quasi-distributions, and from the majorization relation also has a clear physical meaning, and in Supplementary Note 4, we show that this divergence has the following properties that are derived via relative majorization.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\tau
be in the interior of
{\cal F}
. Then
D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})
is well-defined for all
\rho
, and the following hold:

1.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq 0
for all quantum states
\rho
.

2.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})=0
if and only if
\rho=\tau
.

3.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho^{\otimes 2n}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes 2n}})=nD_{\infty}(W_{\rho^{\otimes 2}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes 2}})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq D_{\infty}(W_{{\cal E}(\rho)}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{{\cal E}(\tau)})
for any free operation
{\cal E}
such that
{\cal E}(\tau)
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
.

The reason for the power of
2
in the third property is that the maximization is sensitive to the presence of negativity in the Wigner distribution.

We can go further, and show that in fact a range of Rényi entropies remain both well-defined and meaningful in the Wigner representation. The
\alpha
–Rényi entropy
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})
is defined as
renyi_1960

H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\alpha}.

(45)

To extend to quasi-distributions we must ensure the following: (a) the entropy is mathematically well-defined on quasi-distributions, and (b) obeys monotonicity under the majorization pre-order. In Supplementary Note 5 we prove the following.

Theorem 9
.

If
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
, then
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
is well-defined on the set of quasi-distributions, and if
\boldsymbol{w}\succ\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
for two quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
then
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})\leq H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
.

This monotonicity provides a meaningful extension of entropies to quasi-distributions. The unusual form of the
\alpha
parameter is required so that the entropy function is well-defined and real-valued for all
\boldsymbol{w}
. The allowed values of
\alpha
are dense in
\alpha>1
, however, while Rényi entropies on probabilities can also be used for
0<\alpha<1
, it turns out that this parameter regime is no longer monotonic in terms of the majorization pre-order for quasi-distributions (see Supplementary Note 5 for details).

We can prove the following result, which establishes the equivalence between Wigner negativity and the existence of negative Rényi entropy.

Theorem 10
.

A quantum state
\rho
has Wigner negativity if and only if
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})<0
for some
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
, with positive integers
a\geq b
.

Therefore, the statistical mechanical description of stabilizer operations provides a setting in which negative entropy is fully meaningful, and quantifies the fact that magic states are more ordered than a perfectly sharp, deterministic classical state
\boldsymbol{w}=(1,0,\dots,0)
with zero entropy. We note that prior work has shown that negative conditional entropy
rio_thermodynamic_2011
arises in the context of quantum correlations, but is unrelated to the present negative entropy.

The
\alpha\rightarrow 1
limit of the Rényi entropy diverges if negativity is present, however it diverges in a well-defined way. We can write the entropy as

H_{1+\epsilon}(W_{\rho})=-\epsilon^{-1}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{1+\epsilon}

(46)

with
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
through a sequence of rational values. We recall that mana can be written as
{\cal M}(\rho)=\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|
, so we obtain a second perspective on mana:

{\cal M}(\rho)=-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\epsilon H_{1+\epsilon}(W_{\rho}).

(47)

Therefore, the mana of a state
\rho
is minus the residue of the pole at
\alpha=1
for
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})
, and quantifies the divergence of the Rényi-entropy as we approach the limiting Shannon entropy
H(W_{\rho})
.

We also note that the Rényi entropy can be described as a
q
–deformation of the Shannon entropy
baez2011renyi
, and that
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})
can be related to the
q
–derivative of free energy. For this, the Rényi parameter becomes a temperature term
\alpha=\frac{T_{0}}{T}
where
T_{0}
is a reference temperature and derivatives are considered via the limit
T\rightarrow T_{0}
. Therefore, if we try to push this to the present setting, the presence of Wigner negativity would correspond to a divergence in the first derivative of an effective free energy as
T\rightarrow T_{0}
. It is interesting to speculate whether this non-classicality could be interpreted in terms of a phase transition
domb2000phase
.

Rényi divergences and general distillation bounds

Every entropy can be obtained from a relative entropy
Gour_2020
so we now state the extensions for Rényi divergences of Wigner distributions.
The
\alpha
–Rényi divergence
D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p}||\boldsymbol{r})
is defined as

D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\alpha}r_{i}^{1-\alpha},

(48)

where
\boldsymbol{p}
can be a quasi-distribution and
\boldsymbol{r}
a probability distribution with positive components. In Supplementary Note 5, we also show that the divergence satisfies the following.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\tau
be in the interior of
{\cal F}
.
If
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
, then the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence

is well-defined for all states
\rho

1.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq 0
for all quantum states
\rho
.

2.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})=0
if and only if
\rho=\tau

3.

for any

We can now apply this to obtain a family of magic distillation bounds based on Rényi divergences.

Theorem 12
.

Consider a general magic state distillation protocol on odd prime dimension qudits, that converts a magic state
\rho^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow{\cal E}(\rho^{\otimes n})=\rho^{\prime\otimes m}
and let
\tau
be any full-rank stabilizer reference state on a qudit. Then, the distillation rate

R\leq\frac{D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{D}}$}\hss}D_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})},

where
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for any positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b

\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime\otimes m}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}}),

(50)

between the output magic state
\rho^{\prime\otimes m}
and
\tau^{\prime}_{m}={\cal E}(\tau^{\otimes n})
.

The proof is given in Supplementary Note 5.

While this bound is abstract in its present form, it does suggest future applications by viewing

within the context of hypothesis testing

\rho^{\prime\otimes m}
and the
m
qudit state
\tau^{\prime}_{m}
.
However, in order to properly link with hypothesis-testing one would have to first extend such results to quasi-distributions.

If correlations in
\tau^{\prime}_{m}
between subsystems can be neglected, then
\tau^{\prime}_{m}=\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
for some qudit state
\tau^{\prime}
and

\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})=D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}}),

which gives a generalized version of Theorem

that behaves essentially as an

We can illustrate a basic application of the above bound for magic protocols that give unital maps. In this case it is easy to see that the above bound reduces to the simple form

R\leq R_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{2\log d-H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})}{2\log d-H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime}})}.

R\leq R_{\alpha}=\frac{2(1-\alpha)\log 3-\log\left[8(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{\epsilon}{18})^{\alpha}+(-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{4\epsilon}{9})^{\alpha}\right]}{2(1-\alpha)\log 3+\alpha\log 6-\log(8 + 2^\alpha)}.

Discussion

We have described how relative majorization can be used to establish upper bounds on magic distillation protocols that take into account additional physics of the system. Our bounds exploited relatively simple aspects of the Lorenz curves of the quasi-distributions, so it would be of interest to sharpen these bounds and obtain a better handle on the Lorenz curve structure in the
n\rightarrow\infty
limit. It would also be of interest to analyse what features of single-shot entropies can be extended to quasi-distributions in a sensible form
renner_phd
;
tomamichel2013
. This raises interesting questions as we no longer have a notion of typicality and the central limit theorem does not apply. That said, for special states such as the Strange state, the asymptotic behaviour is relatively simple, so exact asymptotics for this should be possible. In Supplementary Notes 2, 3 and 4 we give additional results that may be of use for later work.

Because Theorem
3
provides a completeness statement for majorization, we can ask if this approach could additionally provide lower bounds for distillation protocols. In contrast to the upper bounds, it is now essential to include the symplectic constraints on the phase space in the majorization relations. In principle the resultant majorization (and relative majorization) constraints should provide exact specifications on what can be obtained via free operations.
Interestingly, this route does not require the use of error correcting codes, but instead is built on group-theoretic features of the phase space.
The Clifford group action on a quantum system corresponds to the action of the affine symplectic group
G\coloneqq SL(2,\mathbb{Z}_{d})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
on the discrete phase space
Appleby_2005
;
Bengtsson_2006
. Therefore, any convex mixture of Clifford unitaries will correspond to a convex mixture of these group actions. The Hardy-Littlewood theorem
hardy_1952
tells us that majorization is obtained from convex mixtures of arbitrary permutations, a result that has also been generalized to convex mixtures of elements of a general group
G
.
G
–majorization has been studied in the classical literature and a range of results are known about it
giovagnoli_1985
;
steerneman_1990
;
giovagnoli_cyclic_1996
;
eaton_1977
.
One can therefore consider sub-groups of the affine symplectic group and analyse the resulting majorization/relative majorization that follows from its action on quasi-distributions. However if the sub-group considered is too simple then it may lead to trivial distillation rates. An example of this is when we restrict to the set of Weyl covariant channels
fukuda2006
, which are represented by a convex mixture of displacements on the phase space. Initial work by the authors have shown that the majorization conditions for Weyl covariant channels can be solved exactly in terms of discrete Fourier transforms, however it is found that the resultant distillation rates are trivial.

One notable structure that could be exploited for
G
–majorization is when
G
is a finite reflection group. For this, the
G
–majorization pre-order is guaranteed to be described by a finite list of conditions
giovagnoli_1985
, just as is the case for the relative majorization ordering. Therefore one route to concrete lower bounds on distillation rates is to consider stochastic maps obtained by reflection sub-groups of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z}_{d})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, or other simple sub-groups, for which the majorization relation reduces to a finite set of conditions. Beyond this, another promising route is to formulate the majorization conditions for a sub-set of operations as a semi-definite program and then attempt to estimate realisable distillation rates by exploiting dualities and appropriate coarse-grainings. This approach has recently been applied by one of the authors to estimate the set of quantum states accessible by covariant channels in the resource theory of asymmetry
alexander2021
where one allows depolarizing noise that is then minimized.
It would be of interest to see if a similar approach could be followed for magic state distillation.

Beyond magic state distillation, the topic of
G
–majorization has been extensively studied, but to our knowledge there has not been work on relative
G
–majorization. This would correspond to transformations that are not unital. Physically, this regime would correspond to a form of thermo-majorization obtained from looking at the action of the Clifford group at a micro-canonical level, and then reducing to a small subsystem
Pathria_1997
. While this seems like a painful thing to consider, there is motivation for this beyond the aim of magic protocols: in the case of classical statistical mechanics on a phase space this is precisely the situation, albeit in the continuum limit. Statistical mechanics of actual systems obey Hamiltonian dynamics, thus they automatically respect a symplectic form
Arnold_2000
;
Pathria_1997
. Therefore, the pre-order of statistical mechanical states with respect to phase space dynamics preserving a Gibbs state must correspond to a symplectic majorization condition. Of course, technical features arise in the continuum limit when considering distributions on an unbounded phase space, but this could be remedied by either considering a compact phase space (e.g. for a particle on a ring) or by first studying the discrete case. Such scenarios also arise in the Quantum Hall Effect
Klitzing_1980
, which is another regime where these techniques could be of use.

Lastly, we note that it would be of interest to consider the possibility of applying the approach taken here to other scenarios in which one wishes to distinguish classical from non-classical behaviour based on quasi-probability representations
Ferrie_2008
;
barnett_1997
;
Allahverdyan_2018
;
arvidsson_2020
;
halpern_2018
;
Lostaglio_2018
;
Levy_2020
.

Methods

Mathematical methods used for proofs

In order to derive our results we make use of three broad subfields.

The first one is the phase space representation of quantum systems as described in the first section of our Results.
Generally, there are various possible representations
Ferrie_2008
, but we make use of the distinguished Wigner representation
Gross2006
for odd-dimensional systems.

We then develop a theory of majorization
cit:marshall
;
Veinott_1971
;
Ruch_1976
for quasi-distributions so as to impose a partial order between magic states.

Finally, we extend the theory of classical Rényi entropies and divergences
renyi_1960
for probability distributions to quasi-distributions and show that they possess sufficient properties for the derivation of our entropic bounds.

Precise definitions of all the above concepts and explanations of their incorporation to the study of magic distillation are provided in the Results section as the concepts become relevant.

\Gamma^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{a_{1}}x_{i},\sum_{i=a_{1}+1}^{a_{1}+a_{2}}\hskip-5.0ptx_{i},\dots,\hskip-15.0pt\sum_{i=a_{1}+\dots+a_{N-1}+1}^{K}\hskip-10.0ptx_{i}\right),

(59)

that obeys
(\Gamma^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}})(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{z}
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

The claim is equivalent to the statement that there exists a bistochastic map

sending
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})
to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
if and only if there exists a stochastic map

\boldsymbol{w}
to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
and

Suppose there is a stochastic map
A
such that
A\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
and
A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
.
We define
B:\mathbb{R}^{K}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{K}
by
B\coloneqq\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\circ A\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{-1}
so that it is stochastic as a composition of stochastic maps and it preserves the uniform distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, since

\displaystyle B(1/K,1/K,\dots,1/K)=\big(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\circ A\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{-1}\big)\big(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r})\big)=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=(1/K,1/K,\dots,1/K),

(60)

therefore
B
is bistochastic.
Finally,
B
maps the embedded distributions as follows,

B\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\big(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\circ A\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{-1}\big)\big(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})\big)=\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}).

(61)

Conversely, suppose a bistochastic map
B
exists sending
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})
to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
. Again, define
A:\mathbb{R}^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{N^{\prime}}
by
A\coloneqq\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ B\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}
so that it is stochastic as a composition of stochastic maps and
A\boldsymbol{w}=\big(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ B\circ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}\big)\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
, as well as
A\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
.
∎

We note that the vectors
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
with rational components form a dense subset of the positive probability distributions, and do not consider further technicalities that have no impact on actual physical measurements, which always have a finite resolution.

Restricting the above result in the context of proper probability distributions, we can prove the Lorenz curve condition for probability distributions.
We define a Lorenz curve elbow as a point where the slope of the curve changes, expressed explicitly in Eq. (15) for Lorenz curve
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)

Proposition 18
.

Given probability distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{\prime}}
with
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
having positive components, then

(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\mbox{ if and only if }L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x),

for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

In the case of
N=N^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\eta}
, where
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(1/N,1/N,\dots,1/N)
is the uniform distribution on
N
elements, the statement reduces to the Lorenz curve condition
L_{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x
, for simple majorization which follows immediately from the defining set of inequalities for majorization
app:marshall
. Namely, the Lorenz curve
L_{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)
for
\boldsymbol{w}
is obtained from the partial sums of
\boldsymbol{w}
sorted in non-increasing order. It is also clear from the definition that the Lorenz curve of
\boldsymbol{w}
is given by
L_{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)=L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(x)
.

To prove the general statement we reduce the relative majorization Lorenz curve condition to standard majorization. Using the notation and assumptions of Proposition
17
for distributions with rational components, we can define
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w}),\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
and the uniform distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(1/K,1/K,\dots,1/K)
.

The key ingredient in the proof is that the Lorenz curve of
\boldsymbol{w}
relative to
\boldsymbol{r}
coincides with the Lorenz curve of
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})
, namely

L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(x)=L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})}(x)\mbox{ for all }x\in[0,1].

(62)

To see this we consider the elbows of
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
,

(x_{k},L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k}))=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)},\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{\pi(i)}\right),

(63)

where the permutation
\pi
sorts
(w_{i}/r_{i})
in non-increasing order.
Expressing
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})
as

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{K}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{w_{i}}{r_{i}},\dots,\frac{w_{i}}{r_{i}}\right),

(64)

where
(w_{i}/r_{i},\dots,w_{i}/r_{i})
has
a_{i}
elements, it is clear that permutation
\pi
sorts
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})
in non-increasing order too.
The Lorenz curve elbows
(y_{k},L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_{k}))
occur at

y_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{a_{\pi(1)}+\dots+a_{\pi(k)}}\frac{1}{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{a_{\pi(i)}}{K}=x_{k}

(65)

and take values

L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(y_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{\pi(i)}\frac{1}{K}\frac{w_{\pi(i)}}{r_{\pi(i)}}=L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k}).

(66)

Therefore, the Lorenz curve of the embedded distribution coincides with the Lorenz curve in the relative majorization setting.

Finally, we have
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})\succ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
, which holds if and only if
L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(x)\geq L_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})|\boldsymbol{\eta}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, which in turn holds if and only if
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(x),\ x\in[0,1]
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We now verify useful majorization properties in the context of quasi-probability distributions, starting from the property that the Lorenz curve is concave.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}
be a quasi-distribution and let
\boldsymbol{r}
be a probability distribution with strictly non-zero components. Then
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
is a concave function on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
x_{\star}
be the point where
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
first attains it maximum. Therefore, on
[0,x_{\star}]
the function rises monotonically to
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{\star})
, via the sum of all positive entries of
\boldsymbol{w}
, taken in decreasing order. Likewise on
[x_{\star},1]
, the function decreases monotonically from its maximum via the partial sums of the negative entries of
\boldsymbol{w}
in decreasing order. Let
f_{1}(x)
be equal to
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
on
[0,x_{\star}]
and
0
otherwise. Also let
f_{2}(x)
be equal to
L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
on
[x_{\star},1]
and and
0
otherwise. By inspection both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are concave functions, and
f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)=L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. However, the sum of two concave functions is also concave which concludes the proof.
∎

The following result is used in the main text to extend the Lorenz curve condition of Proposition
18
into the context of quasi-distributions.

Proposition 20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}
be a quasi-distribution and let
\boldsymbol{r}
be a probability distribution with strictly non-zero components.
Then,
L_{a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=aL_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)+bx
for any constants
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The Lorenz curve of
a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r}
relative to
\boldsymbol{r}
passes through
(0,0)
and the points
(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{r_{\pi(i)}},\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r})_{\pi(i)})
where
\pi
is the permutation that puts
(aw_{i}/r_{i}+b)
in non-increasing order. Since
a>0
, the permutation
\pi
puts
(w_{i}/r_{i})
in non-increasing order too. We thus have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)},\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r})_{\pi(i)}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)},a\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{\pi(i)}+b\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)}\right),

so the value of the Lorenz curve at each potential elbow point
x_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{\pi(i)}
is given by

\displaystyle L_{a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k})=aL_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k})+bL_{\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k})=

\displaystyle aL_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x_{k})+bx_{k},

(67)

so we have
L_{a\boldsymbol{w}+b\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)=aL_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(x)+bx
for any
x\in[0,1]
due to linearity.
∎

The following provides equivalent formulations of relative majorization on quasi-distributions.

Proposition 21
.

Given quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
, such that the components of
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
are positive, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}=A\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=A\boldsymbol{r}
for a stochastic map
A
;

(ii)

L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(t)\geq L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
for
t\in[0,1)
and

L_{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{r}}(1)=L_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(1)
;

(iii)

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w_i - r_i t}\geq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w'_i - r'_i t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The proofs for these properties on proper probability distributions can be found in
app:marshall
;
app:ruch_mixing_1978
;
app:Renes_2016
;
app:Buscemi_2017
and references therein.

The equivalence between (i) and (ii) on quasi-distributions was proven in Theorem 1 in the main text.

The equivalence between (i) and (iii) follows from a similar argument where we mask negativity with a probability distribution.
Namely, let
\epsilon>0
be such that
\boldsymbol{w}(\epsilon)\coloneqq\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}+(1-\epsilon)\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}(\epsilon)\coloneqq\epsilon\boldsymbol{w}+(1-\epsilon)\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
are genuine probability distributions. This is guaranteed by picking a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, since
\boldsymbol{r}
has positive components. We now have that
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
if and only if
(\boldsymbol{w}(\epsilon),\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}(\epsilon),\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
.
Moreover, we have that for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w(\epsilon)_i - r_i c}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w'(\epsilon)_i - r'_i c}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w_i - r_i \frac{\epsilon+ c - 1}{\epsilon}}\geq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{w'_i - r'_i \frac{\epsilon+ c - 1}{\epsilon}}

Replacing
t=(\epsilon+c-1)/\epsilon
, we see that
t
can attain any real value for
c\in\mathbb{R}
, so we deduce the required
L_{1}
–norm condition on quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
.
∎

Component-multiplicity pairs

In general, a
1
–copy
d
–dimensional state
\rho
is described by its
d^{2}
–dimensional Wigner distribution
W_{\rho}
.
The distribution
W_{\rho}
is defined on the phase space, but it can be convenient to re-express this using vector notation.
We discuss this in terms of Wigner distributions, but there is nothing to prevent the discussion from applying to rescaled Wigner distributions as well.

To each Wigner distribution
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
we can associate component-multiplicity pairs
\{(w_{i},m_{i})\}
where the value
w_{i}
occurs in the distribution
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
with multiplicity
m_{i}
.

As an example, for the Strange state
\rho_{S}\coloneqq\rho_{S}(\epsilon=0)
with

\hskip-5.0ptW_{\rho_{S}}=(-1/3,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6)

(68)

we have the component-multiplicity pairs:
\{(-1/3,1),(1/6,8)\}
. However, we might also wish more freedom and not require that the different
w_{i}
values are all distinct. For example, the component-multiplity pairs
\{(-1/3,1),(1/6,2),(1/6,3),(1/6,3)\}
also describe
W_{\rho_{S}}
.

This representation is more compact when a Wigner distribution has a lot of multiplicities, and allows for simple handling of multiple copies via the following fact.

Proposition 22
.

Consider two Wigner distributions
W_{\rho_{A}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{A}),W_{\rho_{B}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{B})
with component-multiplicity pairs

\{(w_{i},m_{i})\}\text{ and }\{(w_{j}^{\prime},m_{j}^{\prime})\},

(69)

respectively. Then,
\{(w_{i}w_{j}^{\prime},m_{i}m_{j}^{\prime})\}
gives component-multiplicity pairs for
W_{\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{A}\oplus\boldsymbol{z}_{B})
.

Proof.

This result is true because all components of
W_{\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{A}\oplus\boldsymbol{z}_{B})
are of the form
w_{i}w_{j}^{\prime}
and

\sum_{i}\sum_{j}m_{i}m_{j}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}m_{i}\sum_{j}m_{j}^{\prime}=d_{A}^{2}d_{B}^{2},

where
d_{A},d_{B}
are the dimensions of
\rho_{A},\rho_{B}
respectively, and
so the set
\{(w_{i}w_{j}^{\prime},m_{i}m_{j}^{\prime})\}
contains exactly the Wigner components of
W_{\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{A}\oplus\boldsymbol{z}_{B})
.
∎

The above result also applies when we consider rescaled Wigner distributions, crucially due to the multiplicativity result shown in Proposition
16
.

In the case of distillation protocols, we are interested in copies of distributions.
For this, we have the following result, which follows from combinatorics.

Proposition 23
.

Suppose
W_{\rho}
has a set of
D
component-multiplicity pairs
\{(w_{i},m_{i})\}
. Then,
W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}
has component-multiplicity pairs
\{(W_{\boldsymbol{q}},M_{\boldsymbol{q}})\}
, with index
\boldsymbol{q}
running through all vectors
(q_{1},\dots,q_{D})
, where
q_{1},\dots,q_{D}
are non-negative integers that sum to
n
, and

\displaystyle W_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{q_{i}}

(70)

\displaystyle M_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}^{q_{i}}.

(71)

The term outside the product in the expression for
M_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is the generalized binomial coefficient,

\binom{n}{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{d}}\coloneqq\frac{n!}{q_{1}!\dots q_{D}!}.

(72)

Proof.

Denote by
C_{D}^{n}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{k}\}
the set of all vectors
\boldsymbol{k}\coloneqq(k_{1},\dots,k_{D})
with non-negative integer components that sum to
n
, i.e.

0\leq k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\leq n\text{ and }k_{1}+\dots+k_{D}=n.

We proceed by induction. Assume
n=1
and let
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
be the vector with its
i
-th component equal to 1 and 0’s elsewhere.
The set
C_{D}^{1}
consists of all vectors of this form, i.e.

C_{D}^{1}=\{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,D}

It is also true by direct calculation that

\left(W_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}},M_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}\right)=(w_{i},m_{i}).

Therefore,
\{(W_{\boldsymbol{k}},M_{\boldsymbol{k}})\}_{\boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{1}}
is a complete set of component-multiplicity pairs for
W_{\rho}
.

Assume that
\{(W_{\boldsymbol{k}},M_{\boldsymbol{k}})\}_{\boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{n}}
as given in Eqs. (
70
,
71
) is a complete set of component-multiplicity pairs for the
n
–copy distribution
W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}=W_{\rho}^{\otimes n}
.
By construction, the distribution
W_{\rho}^{\otimes(n+1)}=W_{\rho}^{\otimes n}\otimes W_{\rho}
, so it admits the complete set of component multiplicity pairs

\{(W_{\boldsymbol{k}}w_{i},M_{\boldsymbol{k}}m_{i})\},\ \boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{n}\text{ and }i=1,\dots,D.

(73)

Consider the component sum of distribution
W_{\rho}^{\otimes(n+1)}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}M_{\boldsymbol{k}}m_{i}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}w_{i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{n}}M_{\boldsymbol{k}}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}w_{i}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in C_{D}^{n}}\frac{n!}{k_{1}!\dots k_{D}!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{D}{m_{i}}^{k_{i}}{w_{i}}^{k_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}w_{i}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}w_{i}\right)^{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}w_{i}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}w_{i}\right)^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}\in C_{D}^{n+1}}M_{\boldsymbol{q}}W_{\boldsymbol{q}},

where in the last expression, vectors
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{D})
have non-negative integer components that sum to
(n+1)
and

\displaystyle M_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)!}{q_{1}!\dots q_{D}!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{D}{m_{i}}^{q_{i}},

\displaystyle W_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{D}{w_{i}}^{q_{i}}.

We have used the multinomial theorem to proceed between lines 2-3 and lines 3-4 of the derivation.

We have achieved a regrouping of the distribution components.
Every component
W_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is of the form
W_{\boldsymbol{k}}w_{i}
with
q_{i}=k_{i}+1
and
q_{j}=k_{j}
for
j\neq i
and

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}\in C_{D}^{n+1}}\hskip-6.0ptM_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\hskip-10.0pt\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}\in C_{D}^{n+1}}\frac{(n+1)!}{q_{1}!\dots q_{D}!}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{D}{m_{i}}^{q_{i}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}m_{i}\right)^{n+1}\hskip-10.0pt

\displaystyle=d^{2(n+1)},

which is the dimension of
W_{\rho}^{\otimes(n+1)}
.

Therefore,
\{(W_{\boldsymbol{q}},M_{\boldsymbol{q}})\}_{\boldsymbol{q}\in C_{D}^{n+1}}
contains exactly the components of
W_{\rho}^{\otimes n}
, completing the proof.
∎

Once again, the above result applies on rescaled Wigner distributions as well.

Results on Lorenz curve constraints

We first prove the following result on the relation between majorization in a particular sub-theory
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
and mana.

Theorem 5
.

Given a magic state
\rho
, the maximum
L_{\star}
of its Lorenz curve
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
is independent of the
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
and equal to
1+{\rm sn}(\rho)
. Moreover, the majorization constraint is stronger than mana in every
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

We denote the Wigner distributions of the states single-component vectors
\boldsymbol{w}(\rho)=W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
and
\boldsymbol{w}(\sigma)=W_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})
. Likewise, we write
\boldsymbol{w}(\rho|\sigma)=W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
.
We choose the component indexing so that
\boldsymbol{w}(\rho|\sigma)^{\downarrow}=\boldsymbol{w}(\rho|\sigma)
, so the components are sorted in non-increasing order.

Note that all components of
\boldsymbol{w}(\sigma)
are positive, so
w(\rho|\sigma)_{i}\geq 0
if and only if
w(\rho)_{i}\geq 0
for any
i=1,\dots,d^{2}
.

Let
i_{\star}
be the index of the smallest non-negative component of
\boldsymbol{w}(\rho|\sigma)^{\downarrow}
.
Then,
w(\rho)_{i}<0
if and only if
i>i_{\star}
, so the maximum of Lorenz curve
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
takes the value

L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i_{\star}})=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\star}}w(\rho)_{i},

(74)

and is achieved at
x_{i_{\star}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\star}}w(\sigma)_{i}
. The location of this maximum varies with
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
, but its value is independent of
\sigma
,

\displaystyle L_{\star}

\displaystyle\coloneqq L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i_{\star}})=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{z}:W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\geq 0}\hskip-5.0ptW_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=1+{\rm sn}(\rho).

(75)

Since mana is a monotonic function of sum-negativity,
{\cal M}(\rho)=\log\hskip 1.0pt(2\hskip 1.0pt{\rm sn}(\rho)+1)
, we see that mana determines the peak of the Lorenz curve
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
. However, mana is one of
d^{2n}
constraints, so majorization is strictly a stronger constraint in any
{\cal R}_{\sigma}
.
∎

We now prove a simple majorization constraint, arising by considering only the part of the Lorenz curves between the origin
(0,0)
and the first elbow.

Proposition 24

(First elbow constraint)
.

Consider a magic state process
\rho\longrightarrow\rho^{\prime}
with input and output Lorenz curves
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x),L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
and denote the coordinates of the first elbow of
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
by
(X_{0},L_{0})
and the coordinates of the first elbow of
L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
by
(X^{\prime}_{0},L^{\prime}_{0})
.

Then, given any coordinates
(x,L)
and
(x^{\prime},L^{\prime})
on the input and output Lorenz curves respectively, where
0<x\leq X_{0}
and
0<x^{\prime}\leq X^{\prime}_{0}
, the process is possible only if

\frac{L}{x}\geq\frac{L^{\prime}}{x^{\prime}}.

(76)

Proof.

If the transformation is possible, then
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x
in
[0,1]
.
Restricting to the initial linear segment of
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
joining
(0,0)
to
(X_{0},L_{0})
, we then require that it be above the initial linear segment of
L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
joining
(0,0)
to
(X_{0}^{\prime},L_{0}^{\prime})
.
Since both these linear segments start at the origin, this condition is equivalent to the slope of the initial segment of the input Lorenz curve being greater than or equal to the slope of the initial segment of the output Lorenz curve.
This slope constraint is then equivalent to Eq. (
76
). ∎

The following result reduces the Lorenz curve condition on the interval
[0,1]
to a finite set of independent inequalities and can therefore simplify the computation of relative majorization constraints in practical scenarios.

Proposition 25
.

Let
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
be any two quantum states with Lorenz curves
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x),L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
, where
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
are states with positive Wigner distributions. Assume that
L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
has
t
elbows at locations
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
. Then,
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
iff
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i})\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{i})
for all
i=1,\dots,t
.

Proof.

If
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, then the condition on the elbows follows trivially.

Conversely, assume
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i})\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{i})
for all
i=1,\dots t
.
Suppose on the contrary that
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
dips below
L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
at some point
x=y
where
x_{i}<y<x_{i+1}
for some
i
. Then this implies

\displaystyle L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{i})

\displaystyle L_{\rho|\sigma}(y)

\displaystyle<L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(y)

\displaystyle L_{\rho|\sigma}(x_{i+1})

\displaystyle\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{i+1}).

However, since
L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
is linear between
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
, the above conditions imply that
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)
is not concave, which contradicts Proposition
19
.
Therefore,
L_{\rho|\sigma}(x)\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}|\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
for all

[0,1]
.
∎

Supplementary Note 3: General aspects of Lorenz curves for noisy Strange states

Here we provide background detail as to how the Lorenz curve behaves for a noisy Strange state in the large
n
limit.

Binomial distributions and error bounds

Consider an experiment consisting of
n
trials of throwing a coin with probability
p
of landing on heads and
1-p
of landing on tails.
We denote the sums over an even number
m
of successful trials by
\Phi_{+}(m;n,p)
and an odd number
m
of successful trials by
\Phi_{-}(m;n,p)
, and they are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}(m;n,p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m/2}\binom{n}{2\ell}p^{2\ell}(1-p)^{n-2\ell},

\displaystyle\text{for even integers }m\in[0,n],

(77)

\displaystyle\Phi_{-}(m;n,p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{(m-1)/2}\binom{n}{2\ell+1}p^{2\ell+1}(1-p)^{n-(2\ell+1)},

\displaystyle\text{for odd integers }m\in[0,n].

(78)

In the next section we will use
\Phi_{+}
and
\Phi_{-}
to express the elbow coordinates of Lorenz curves for unital protocols.

We have the Shannon entropy of a
p
–coin and the Shannon relative entropy for a
p
–coin and a
q
–coin given by the expressions

\displaystyle S(p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-p\log{p}-(1-p)\log{(1-p)},

(79)

\displaystyle S\left(p\middle|\middle|q\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\log{\frac{p}{q}}+(1-p)\log{\frac{1-p}{1-q}}.

(80)

They are symmetric about
p=1/2
and
q=1/2
, namely
S(p)=S(1-p)
and
S\left(p\middle|\middle|q\right)=S\left(1-p\middle|\middle|1-q\right)
. We also have the entropic bounds

Proposition 26
.

For all
\ell\in[1,n-1]
,

\displaystyle\left[8\ell\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{nS\left(\frac{\ell}{n}\right)}\leq\binom{n}{\ell}\leq

(81)

\displaystyle\left[2\pi\ell\left(1-\frac{\ell}{n}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{nS\left(\frac{\ell}{n}\right)}.

(82)

The proof provided in
app:ash
proceeds with direct calculation for the edge cases
\ell=1,2,n-1,n-2
and use of Stirling’s approximation for the remaining cases. The bounds in Proposition
26
can be directly inserted into the expressions of functions
\Phi_{+}
and
\Phi_{-}
to derive strict upper and lower bounds.

It is also possible to derive more manageable bounds on
\Phi_{+},\Phi_{-}
.
To this end, we rewrite the functions as

\Phi_{\pm}(m;n,a)=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi(m;n,a)\pm(1+a)^{-n}S(m;n,a)),

(83)

where
a=p/(1-p)
, and
\Phi
is the standard cumulative function for the binomial distribution

\Phi(m;n,a)=(1+a)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{n}{k}a^{k},

(84)

and we define the remainder term

S(m;n,a)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{n}{k}(-a)^{k}.

(85)

The cumulative function
\Phi
obeys the following bounds, which are proven in
app:ash
.

Proposition 27
.

Given fixed
n>0
and
p
,
\Phi
satisfies the following bounds:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{1. }\Phi(m;n,p)\geq\left[8m\left(1-\frac{m}{n}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}2^{-nS\left(\frac{m}{n}\middle|\middle|p\right)},\\
&\hskip 14.0ptm\in[1,n-1]\\
&\text{2. }\Phi(m;n,p)\geq 1-2^{-nS\left(\frac{m+1}{n}\middle|\middle|p\right)},\ m\in[np+1,n-2]\\
&\text{3. }\Phi(m;n,p)\leq 1-\left[8(m+1)\left(1-\frac{m+1}{n}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\times\\
&\hskip 14.0pt2^{-nS\left(\frac{m+1}{n}\middle|\middle|p\right)},\ m\in[0,n-2]\end{split}

\displaystyle\text{4. }\Phi(m;n,p)\leq 2^{-nS\left(\frac{m}{n}\middle|\middle|p\right)},\ m\in[0,np]

We can now put estimates on
S(m;n,a)
, and hence the functions
\Phi_{\pm}
, for different parameter regimes.
We can consider the function
f(x)=(1+x)^{n}
and note that
S(m;n,a)
is the
m
’th partial sum of this expansion at the point
x=-a
. The truncated Maclaurin series is given by

f(x)=f(0)+xf^{\prime}(0)+\dots\frac{x^{m}}{m!}f^{(m)}(0)+R_{m}(x)

(86)

with a remainder term

\displaystyle R_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}dtf^{(m+1)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{m}}{m!}

(87)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}f^{(m+1)}(x_{*}),

(88)

where in the second expression,
x_{*}
is a point that lies between
0
and
x
that comes from the Mean Value Theorem.

Applying this to the function
f(x)=(1+x)^{n}
gives

(1+x)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}+R_{m}.

(89)

Therefore, if we evaluate at
x=-a
we obtain

S(m;n,a)=(1-a)^{n}-R_{m}(-a),

(90)

where the remainder term is given by

\displaystyle R_{m}(-a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{-a}dtf^{(m+1)}(t)\frac{(-a-t)^{m}}{m!}

(91)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-a)^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}f^{(m+1)}(x_{*}).

(92)

We can compute the derivative
f^{(m+1)}(x)
explicitly,

f^{(m+1)}(x)=(m+1)!\binom{n}{m+1}(1+x)^{n-m-1}.

(93)

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle R_{m}(-a)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}(m+1)\binom{n}{m+1}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt\int_{-a}^{0}dt(1+t)^{n-m-1}(a+t)^{m}

(94)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{m+1}(-a)^{m+1}(1+x_{*})^{n-m-1},

(95)

where in the latter expression
x_{*}\in[-a,0]
.
The first integral expression can be estimated via the Cauchy-Schwarz or the Hölder inequality.
Therefore, one has an explicit form with an unknown (but bounded) parameter
x_{*}
, or the integral form.

A very simple estimate, based on
x_{*}
lying in the interval
[-a,0]
gives

(1-a)^{n-m-1}\leq\frac{R_{m}(-a)}{\binom{n}{m+1}(-a)^{m+1}}\leq 1,

(96)

which in turn leads to the following bounds on
\Phi_{+}(m;n,a)
:

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt2\Phi_{+}(m;n,a)\leq\

\displaystyle\Phi(m;n,a)+(1-a)^{n}-\frac{(-a)^{m+1}}{m!}\times

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m+1}(1-a)^{n-m-1}\text{ and}

(97)

\displaystyle 2\Phi_{+}(m;n,a)\geq\

\displaystyle\Phi(m;n,a)+(1-a)^{n}-\frac{(-a)^{m+1}}{m!}\times

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m+1}.

(98)

Better bounds can be obtained with a finer analysis, and it would be of interest to obtain tighter asymptotic behaviour on
\Phi_{\pm}
that could in turn be used to improve magic distillation bounds.

Supplementary Note 4: Temperature-dependent distillation bounds from relative majorization

Here we prove our main theorem, which bounds magic distillation rates in terms of free energies.

Theorem 7
.

Consider a magic distillation protocol on qutrits that transforms
n
copies of an
\epsilon
–noisy Strange state into
m
copies of an
\epsilon^{\prime}
–noisy Strange state, with depolarising errors
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon\leq 3/7
. We also allow pre/post-processing by local Clifford unitaries.

Let
T=(k\beta)^{-1}
be any finite temperature for the physical system and let
H=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}E_{k}|E_{k}\rangle\langle E_{k}|
be the Hamiltonian of each qutrit subsystem in its eigen-decomposition.
Assume that in the thermodynamic limit (
n,m\gg 1
), the protocol applied to the equilibrium state
\tau^{\otimes n}=(e^{-\beta H}/{\cal Z})^{\otimes n}
maps
\tau^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
, where we write
\tau^{\prime}=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/{\cal Z}^{\prime}
for some Hermitian
H^{\prime}
.

Then the asymptotic magic distillation rate
R=m/n
is bounded as

R\leq\dfrac{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon\big)+\beta(\phi-F)}{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon' \big)+\beta(\phi^{\prime}-F^{\prime})},

(114)

where
F
is the free energy of
\tau
, and

\phi=-\beta^{-1}\log\zeta

(115)

with
\zeta
given by the expressions

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-\beta E_{k}},

(116)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle E_{k}|A_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}|E_{k}\rangle,

(117)

and
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
attaining a minimum at
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}
. The primed variables are defined similarly for the output system.

Proof.

For the sake of clarity, we write
\rho_{n}\coloneqq\rho_{S}(\epsilon)^{\otimes n}
,
\rho^{\prime}_{m}\coloneqq\rho_{S}(\epsilon^{\prime})^{\otimes m}
,
\tau_{n}\coloneqq\tau^{\otimes m}
and
\tau^{\prime}_{m}\coloneqq\tau^{\prime\otimes m}
. We also assume, without loss of generality on the asymptotic distillation rate, that
n
and
m
are both even.

To establish the distillation bound we consider the distillation protocol that gives
{\cal E}(\rho_{n})=\rho^{\prime}_{m}
for the magic states.
We then consider that the protocol transforms the reference equilibrium state as
{\cal E}(\tau_{n})=\tau^{\prime}_{m}
.
Since we have a finite temperature we have that
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are full rank stabilizer states and have a strictly positive Wigner distribution. The input and output magic states generally have quasi-probability Wigner distributions.
For any such protocol we therefore have that

(W_{\rho_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z}),W_{\tau_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z}))\succ(W_{\rho^{\prime}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{z}),W_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{z})),

(118)

or, equivalently, in terms of the relevant Lorenz curves,

L_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}(x)\geq L_{\rho^{\prime}_{m}|\tau^{\prime}_{m}}(x)\mbox{ for all }x\in[0,1].

(119)

We now consider the rescaled Wigner distribution
W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})\coloneqq W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})/W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
, which is well-defined since
\tau
is full-rank.
Due to the multiplicative property of the Wigner distribution, the rescaled distribution is also multiplicative in the sense that

W_{\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}|\tau\otimes\tau^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1}\oplus\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})W_{\rho^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2}),

(120)

for any states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
and any full-rank stabilizer states
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle W_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})

(121)

\displaystyle W_{\rho_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})

(122)

where
\boldsymbol{z}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2n}
is the phase space point for the full system in terms of those of the individual subsystems.

The points defining the Lorenz curve
L_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}(x)
are obtained from sorting the components of
W_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}(\boldsymbol{z})
in non-increasing order and then computing the partial sums of
W_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}(\pi(\boldsymbol{z}))
where
\pi
is the permutation that realizes the sorting.
However, similar to the unital protocol analysis, we use the slope constraint (Proposition
24
) obtained by considering the line segments connecting the origin to the first elbow of both Lorenz curves.

The Wigner distribution of a single noisy Strange state consists of 1 negative component
W_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})=-v(\epsilon)
and 8 positive components
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})=u(\epsilon)
for
\boldsymbol{z}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.
The Wigner distribution of the full-rank, stabilizer equilibrium state
\tau
is
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2}
.

Assume that the smallest component of the distribution
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
is at
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}
. Since the magic content of the state is unchanged under a Clifford unitary
C
, we can instead consider the state

\rho_{S}(\epsilon)\rightarrow C\rho_{S}(\epsilon)C^{\dagger}=D_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}\rho_{S}(\epsilon)D_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}^{\dagger},

(123)

which has its single negative Wigner component
-v(\epsilon)
at the point
\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}
.

The components of the rescaled distribution
W_{\rho_{n}|\tau_{n}}
for this transformed state are given by

\left(\frac{-v}{W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\star})}\right)^{i_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}}\prod_{\boldsymbol{z}\neq\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}\left(\frac{u}{W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})}\right)^{i_{\boldsymbol{z}}},

(124)

where the integer indices obey the following conditions:

\displaystyle 0\leq i_{\boldsymbol{z}}\leq n\mbox{ for all }\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2}}i_{\boldsymbol{z}}=n.

(125)

We now compute the largest rescaled component.
Firstly, note that
n
is even, so we require that
i_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}\in\{0,2,\dots,n\}
for the component to be positive.
Then, we have that
v\geq u
because
\epsilon\leq 3/7
, and we have already ensured that
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\star})\leq W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2}
.
Therefore, the largest rescaled component occurs when
i_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}=n
and
i_{\boldsymbol{z}}=0
for
\boldsymbol{z}\neq\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}
and is equal to
(v/W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}))^{n}
.
The coordinates of the first Lorenz curve point after the origin are given by

(x_{0},L_{0})=((W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}))^{n},v^{n})

(126)

Given a Hamiltonian decomposition,

H=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}E_{k}|E_{k}\rangle\langle E_{k}|,

(127)

we now express the coordinates of the first point in terms of free energy quantities. We expand as follows

\displaystyle W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3{\cal Z}}{\rm{tr}}\left[A_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}e^{-\beta H}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta F}}{3}{\rm{tr}}\left[A_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}e^{-\beta E_{k}}|E_{k}\rangle\langle E_{k}|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta F}}{3}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-\beta E_{k}}=\frac{e^{\beta F}}{3}\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta(F-\phi)}}{3},

(128)

where we define

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\coloneqq\langle E_{k}|A_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\star}}|E_{k}\rangle

(129)

\displaystyle\zeta\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-\beta E_{k}},

(130)

and a ‘magic free energy’ term
\phi\coloneqq-\beta^{-1}\log\zeta
.
Therefore, the coordinates can be expressed as

(x_{0},L_{0})=\left(\frac{e^{n\beta(F-\phi)}}{3^{n}},v(\epsilon)^{n}\right).

(131)

The output states obey the same conditions, so we also have the first point coordinates of the output Lorenz curve given by

(x^{\prime}_{0},L^{\prime}_{0})=\left(\frac{e^{m\beta(F^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime})}}{3^{m}},v(\epsilon^{\prime})^{m}\right).

(132)

If the largest rescaled component of a state is distinct with no multiplicities, then these coordinates correspond to the first elbow of the corresponding Lorenz curve, whereas if it appears multiple times, then the coordinates derived correspond to a point on the interior of the line segment connecting the origin to the first elbow.
In both cases, the distillation bound remains the same, as is clear by its derivation in Proposition
24
, given by
L_{0}/x_{0}\geq L^{\prime}_{0}/x^{\prime}_{0}
, leading to

\left(3v(\epsilon)e^{-\beta(F-\phi)}\right)^{n}=\frac{L_{0}}{x_{0}}\geq\frac{L^{\prime}_{0}}{x^{\prime}_{0}}=\left(3v(\epsilon^{\prime})e^{-\beta(F^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime})}\right)^{m}.

(133)

We note that
3v(\epsilon^{\prime})e^{-\beta(F^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime})}>1
always, as
\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon^{\prime})
is not a free state, so the initial slope of its Lorenz curve exceeds
1
.
Taking the natural logarithm on both sides and rearranging gives the bound

\frac{m}{n}\leq\dfrac{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon\big)+\beta(\phi-F)}{\log\big( 1-\frac{4}{3}\epsilon' \big)+\beta(\phi^{\prime}-F^{\prime})},

(134)

which completes the proof.
∎

Supplementary Note 5: Single-shot entropies on quasi-distributions and magic distillation bounds

In this section, we analyse Rényi entropies on quasi-distributions and use them to get magic distillation bounds.

Rényi min-divergence from Lorenz curves

We first demonstrate that the initial slope analysis on the Wigner quasi-distributions makes use of the min-relative divergence. We define

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})\coloneqq\log\max_{\boldsymbol{z}}\frac{W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})}{W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})},

(159)

which is well-defined since both the nominator and denominator in the above expression are strictly positive when
\tau
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\tau
be in the interior of
{\cal F}
. Then
D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})
is well-defined for all
\rho
, and the following hold:

1.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq 0
for all quantum states
\rho
.

2.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})=0
if and only if
\rho=\tau
.

3.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho^{\otimes 2n}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes 2n}})=nD_{\infty}(W_{\rho^{\otimes 2}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes 2}})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq D_{\infty}(W_{{\cal E}(\rho)}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{{\cal E}(\tau)})
for any free operation
{\cal E}
such that
{\cal E}(\tau)
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
.

Proof.

Since
\tau
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
, its Wigner function obeys
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})>0
for all points
\boldsymbol{z}
in the phase space.
In general,
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a quasi-distribution, but the given form of
\alpha

W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha}\geq 0
.
Therefore
D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})
is always well-defined.

1. We have that

D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})=\log\max_{\boldsymbol{z}}\frac{W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})}{W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})},

(160)

so
2^{D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})}
equals the slope of the Lorenz curve
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)
at
x=0
. Since
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)
is a concave function passing through
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
this implies that
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)\geq x
for all
x\in[0,1]
, and in particular its slope at
x=0
is always greater than or equal to
1
, which implies
D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})\geq 0
for all
\rho
.

2.
D_{\infty}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})=0
implies that
\max_{\boldsymbol{z}}\frac{W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})}{W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})}=1
and the initial slope of
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)
is
1
. From the concavity of the function and the fact that
L_{\rho|\tau}(1)=1
, this implies that the slope of
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)
must equal
1
throughout the interval
[0,1]
and this, together with the definition of the Lorenz curve, implies that
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})/W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})=1
for all
\boldsymbol{z}
. Since the Wigner representation
\rho\mapsto W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is bijective this implies that
\rho=\tau
only. The converse holds by inspection.

3. Given a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it is generally the case that
\max_{k}w_{k}\neq\max_{k}|w_{k}|
.
However, we do have that

\max_{k_{1},k_{2}}(w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}})=\max_{k_{1},k_{2}}|w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}}|,

(161)

and if additionally
w_{k}\geq 0
for all
k

\max_{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{2n}}\left(w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}}\cdots w_{k_{2n}}\right)=(\max_{k}|w_{k}|)^{2n},

(162)

and also,

\displaystyle\max_{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{2n}}\left(w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}}\cdots w_{k_{2n}}\right)=

\displaystyle\max_{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{2n}}\left(|w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}}|\cdots|w_{k_{2n-1}}w_{k_{2n}}|\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\max_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(w_{k_{1}}w_{k_{2}}\right)\right)^{n}.

(163)

If we now let
\boldsymbol{w}=W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})\coloneqq W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})/W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})
we have

\max_{\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{2n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2n}W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})=\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}}W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})W_{\rho|\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{2})\right)^{n}

(164)

4. The free operation
{\cal E}
in the Wigner representation corresponds to a stochastic map, which sends

to

, which is another strictly positive probability distribution on the phase space, due to

being in the interior of
{\cal F}
. Moreover,

Well-defined Rényi entropies on quasi-distributions

We now make use of known results from majorization theory to establish the Schur-concavity of the Rényi entropy
H_{\alpha}
on Wigner quasi-distributions for a subset of
\alpha
values. We consider the set of all quasi-distributions that is a hyper-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
given by

\mathcal{Q}_{N}=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1\right\}.

(165)

We define the notion of Schur-convex and Schur-convave functions on a subset
{\cal D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Definition 30
.

A continuous, real-valued function
f
defined on
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is called Schur-convex on the domain
\mathcal{D}
if
\boldsymbol{p}\prec\boldsymbol{q}
on
\mathcal{D}
implies that
f(\boldsymbol{p})\leq f(\boldsymbol{q})
. A continuous function
f:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called Schur-concave if
(-f)
is Schur-convex.

For any subset
{\cal D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
we define

\mathcal{D}^{\downarrow}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{D}:w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq w_{N}\}.

(166)

We now have the following fundamental result in majorization theory.

Proposition 31

(Schur-Ostrowski theorem
app:Schur_1923
;
app:Ostrowski_1952
;
app:marshall
)
.

Let
{\cal D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a convex set with non-empty interior, and invariant under permutations of vector components. Let
f:{\cal D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function on the interior of
{\cal D}
and continuous on the whole of
{\cal D}
. Then
f
is Schur-convex on
{\cal D}
if and only if
f
is a symmetric function on
{\cal D}
and

\partial_{1}f(\boldsymbol{w})\geq\partial_{2}f(\boldsymbol{w})\geq\cdots\geq\partial_{N}f(\boldsymbol{w}),

(167)

for all
\boldsymbol{w}
in the interior of
\mathcal{D}^{\downarrow}
.

Since the set
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
of quasi-distributions obey the necessary conditions, we get the following result:

Proposition 32
.

Let
f
be a real-valued continuous function defined on
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
that is continuously differentiable on the interior of
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
.
Then,
f
is Schur-convex on
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
if and only if it is a symmetric function and

\partial_{1}f(\boldsymbol{w})\geq\partial_{2}f(\boldsymbol{w})\geq\cdots\geq\partial_{N}f(\boldsymbol{w}),

(168)

for all quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w}
in the interior of
\mathcal{Q}^{\downarrow}_{N}
.

The classical
\alpha
-Rényi entropy on a probability distribution
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{i})
is given by

H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\alpha},

(169)

where
\alpha\geq 0
. This means the function is well-defined on
\mathcal{Q}_{N}^{+}
, namely the set of quasi-distributions with non-negative components. We wish to extend the domain to the whole of
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
, but this requires that
w_{i}^{\alpha}
be a real number for any
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The exponent function
x\mapsto x^{\alpha}
is well-defined and real-valued if
\alpha
is a rational number of the form
\alpha=r/s
where
s
is an odd integer and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. However, we also require that
\sum_{i}w_{i}^{\alpha}>0
in order for the logarithm to return a real value. To ensure this, we restrict to
\alpha=r/s
with
r=2a
being an even integer and
s=2b-1
being an odd integer, for some integers
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies that the following are all well-defined, real-valued expressions,

0\leq w_{i}^{\alpha}=w_{i}^{\frac{2a}{2b-1}}=\left(w_{i}^{\frac{1}{2b-1}}\right)^{2a}=\left(w_{i}^{2a}\right)^{\frac{1}{2b-1}}=|w_{i}|^{\frac{2a}{2b-1}}.

We note that previous work
app:Manfredi_2000
has considered the
\alpha=2
entropy of Wigner distributions, and other work exists that uses the Wehrl entropy, based on the Hussimi function of a quantum state
app:Gnutzmann_2001
.

We next show that
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
for
\alpha=2a/(2b-1)
is a Schur-concave function on
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
provided that
a\geq b
.

Theorem 9
.

If
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
, then
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
is well-defined on the set of quasi-distributions
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
, and moreover if
\boldsymbol{w}\succ\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
for two quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}
then
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})\leq H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We have
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}\geq\frac{2b}{2b-1}>1
and
\alpha
is a rational with even numerator and odd denominator, so
\sum_{i}w_{i}^{\alpha}>0
and
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
is well-defined for all quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w}
.

We also have that
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
is well-defined, continuous, differentiable on the interior of
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
and symmetric in the components of
\boldsymbol{w}
.

Consider the partial derivatives

\frac{\partial H_{\alpha}}{\partial w_{i}}=\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\sum_{k}{w_{k}^{\alpha}}}\left(-w_{i}^{\alpha-1}\right).

(170)

For
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
, with
a\geq b
, we have that

\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\sum_{k}{w_{k}^{\alpha}}}>0.

(171)

The first derivative of the function
g(w)\coloneqq-w^{\alpha-1}
is given by

g^{\prime}(w)=-(\alpha-1)w^{\alpha-2}=-(\alpha-1)w^{\frac{2a-4b+2}{2b-1}}\leq 0,

(172)

because
\alpha>1
and

w^{\frac{2a-4b+2}{2b-1}}=\left(w^{\frac{a-2b+1}{2b-1}}\right)^{2}\geq 0,

(173)

so
g(w)
is non-increasing in
w
.

Therefore, whenever
w_{i}\geq w_{j}
, we have that

-w_{i}^{\alpha-1}\leq-w_{j}^{\alpha-1},

(174)

which implies that

\frac{\partial H_{\alpha}}{\partial w_{i}}(\boldsymbol{w})\leq\frac{\partial H_{\alpha}}{\partial w_{j}}(\boldsymbol{w}),

(175)

for any
\boldsymbol{w}
in the interior of
\mathcal{Q}_{N}^{\downarrow}
.
Therefore,
H_{\alpha}
is Schur-concave on
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
and

H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})\leq H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}),

(176)

for any quasi-distributions
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
that obey
\boldsymbol{w}\succ\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
.
∎

While we have integers
a,b
such that
0<\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}<1
and
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})
is well-defined, it turns out that monotonicity does not hold if we drop the condition
a\geq b
. If
\alpha=2a/(2b-1)
with
\alpha<1
, then
g(w)\coloneqq\frac{-w^{\alpha-1}}{\alpha-1}
is no longer monotonic for all
w\in\mathbb{R}
, and the problem occurs when comparing
w_{i}<0
and
w_{j}>0
. As an example of this dependence of monotonicity on the domain of the function, consider the function
g(x)=\frac{1}{x^{3}}
which is monotone decreasing on both
x<0
and
x>0
separately, however it is not monotone on the full real-line.

We note that if
\alpha=r/s
with both
r
and
s
odd, then
H_{\alpha}
is not well-defined for all quasi-distributions, although if the actual set of quasi-distributions has a sufficiently bounded levels negativity, then
\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha}
can still be obtained for
r
odd, provided the total sum is never negative. This occurs for Wigner distributions, where we have
|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|\leq 1/d
for a
d
–dimensional quantum system.

We also note that the set
F\coloneqq\{2a/(2b-1):a,b\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }a\geq b\}
is dense in the reals
\mathbb{R}_{>1}
, as any rational
c/d
with
c>d
can be approximated by
c2^{n}/(d2^{n}-1)\in F
for sufficiently large
n
.

We have that
H_{\alpha}
is additive on products of quasi-distributions, which we state for completeness.

Proposition 33
.

For any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N^{\prime}}
, we have

H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})=H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})+H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}),

(177)

where
\alpha=2a/(2b-1)
with positive integers
a\geq b
.

Proof.

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i,j}\left[\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{j}\right]^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left[\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\alpha}\sum_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\prime\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left[\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\alpha}\right]+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left[\sum_{j}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\prime\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w})+H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}).

(178)

∎

Applied to Wigner distributions
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
for a quantum state
\rho
we have

H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha},

(179)

where
\alpha
can take values of the form
2a/(2b-1)
, for non-negative integers
a,b
. For the noisy Strange state
\rho=\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)
, this becomes

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}\left(W_{\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left[8\left(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{18}\epsilon\right)^{\alpha}+\left(-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{4}{9}\epsilon\right)^{\alpha}\right]

Rényi entropy
H_{\alpha}
of the
\epsilon
-noisy Strange state.

H_{\alpha}
is increasing in
\alpha
and
\epsilon
. The rightmost dashed contour line corresponds to
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho_{\rm{S}}(\epsilon)})=0
. In particular,
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho_{\rm{S}}(0)})=0
occurs at
\alpha\approx 1.31
.

We now show the following equivalence between Wigner negativity and negative Rényi entropies.

Theorem 10
.

A quantum state
\rho
has Wigner negativity if and only if
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})<0
for some
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
, with positive integers
a\geq b
.

Proof.

If we have
H_{\alpha}(W_{\rho})<0
for
\alpha>1
, then it follows that
\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha}>1
.
However, it is known that
H_{\alpha}
is always non-negative on probability distributions, so
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
must be a strict quasi-distribution with negativity.

Conversely, suppose
\rho
has negativity in its Wigner representation. This in particular implies that
\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|>1
. We now consider
\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\frac{2a}{2b-1}}
for positive integers
a\geq b
. We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\frac{2a}{2b-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{\frac{2a}{2b-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{1+\epsilon},

(181)

where
\epsilon=\alpha-1=\frac{2a}{2b-1}-1>0
. By choosing the positive integers
a
and
b
sufficiently large we can make
\epsilon
arbitrarily close to
0
. This implies that there exists a sequence
\epsilon_{n}=\alpha_{n}-1=\frac{2a_{n}}{2b_{n}-1}-1
with integer pairs
a_{n},b_{n}
such that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|^{1+\epsilon_{n}}\rightarrow\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}|W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})|>1,

(182)

as
n
increases.
Since
\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{n}}<0
for any
n
it follows that
H_{\alpha_{n}}(W_{\rho})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{n}}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha_{n}}<0
at some point in the sequence, which completes the proof.
∎

Therefore Wigner negativity coincides with negativity of a Rényi entropy.

Rényi divergences on quasi-distributions

We now define the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence for a quasi-distribution
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}
relative to a full-rank probability distribution
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}^{+}
as

D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}||\boldsymbol{r})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{i}w_{i}^{\alpha}r_{i}^{1-\alpha},

(183)

where
\alpha=2a/(2b-1)
for positive integers
a\geq b
. We now have the following result that relates the Rényi divergence to the Rényi entropy on a dense subset of probability distributions.

Proposition 34
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}
be a quasi-distribution and
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}^{+}
a probability distribution with positive rational entries given by
r_{i}=a_{i}/K
for positive integers
a_{i}
and
K=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}
.
Then,

H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w}))=K-D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r}),

(184)

where
\alpha=2a/(2b-1)
for positive integers
a\geq b
.

Proof.

For the given form of
\alpha
and positive
r_{i},\ i=1,\dots,N
,
D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}||\boldsymbol{r})
is well-defined for all
\boldsymbol{w}
. From the definition of
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}
we have

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}(1/a_{i},1/a_{i},\dots,1/a_{i}).

(185)

This directly leads to

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\left(\frac{w_{i}}{a_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{\alpha}a_{i}^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log K^{1-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{\alpha}r_{i}^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=K+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{\alpha}r_{i}^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=K-D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}||\boldsymbol{r}).

(186)

∎

With this we now establish monotonicity for Rényi relative divergences.

Proposition 35
.

Let
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for any positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N}
,
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{N^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathcal{Q}^{+}_{N}
,
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}^{+}_{N^{\prime}}
with positive rational components
a_{i}/K
and
a^{\prime}_{i}/K
respectively.
Then
D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r})\geq D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
, whenever
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The statement
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{r})\succ(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
is equivalent to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})\succ\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
.
Therefore, due to Theorem
9
we have

H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w}))\leq H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}))

(187)

which, due to Proposition
34
, is equivalent to

D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r})\geq D_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}).

(188)

∎

Since the rationals are dense in the real numbers we can assume any Wigner distribution considered has rational components without affecting results.
We now specialize to Wigner distributions of quantum states, and quantum operations that map the set of free states
{\cal F}
into itself.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\tau
be in the interior of
{\cal F}
.
If
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
, then the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence
D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})
is well-defined for all states
\rho
, and the following hold:

1.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq 0
for all quantum states
\rho
.

2.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})=0
if and only if
\rho=\tau
.

3.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes n}})=nD_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq D_{\alpha}(W_{{\cal E}(\rho)}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{{\cal E}(\tau)})
for any free operation
{\cal E}
such that
{\cal E}(\tau)
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
.

Proof.

Since
\tau
is in the interior of
{\cal F}
, its Wigner function obeys
W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})>0
for all points
\boldsymbol{z}
in the phase space.
In general,
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a quasi-distribution, but for
\alpha=2a/2b-1
we that
W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha}\geq 0
.
Therefore
D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}||W_{\tau})
is always well-defined and real-valued.

1.
D_{\alpha}
is Schur-convex and every pair
(W_{\rho},W_{\tau})
majorizes the pair
(W_{\mathbbm{1}/d},W_{\mathbbm{1}/d})
, so

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq D_{\alpha}(W_{\mathbbm{1}/d}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\mathbbm{1}/d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\mathbbm{1}/d}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=0.

(189)

2. In the inequality of property 1, equality holds iff
L_{\rho|\tau}(x)=L_{\mathbbm{1}/d|\mathbbm{1}/d}(x)=L_{\tau|\tau}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
due to Proposition
21
which in turn holds iff
\rho=\tau
.

3. This property follows from the multiplicativity of the Wigner distribution.
In particular,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes n}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}(\boldsymbol{z})^{\alpha}W_{\tau^{\otimes n}}(\boldsymbol{z})^{1-\alpha}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})^{\alpha}W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})^{1-\alpha}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})^{\alpha}W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})^{1-\alpha}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}}W_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})^{\alpha}W_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})^{1-\alpha}=

\displaystyle=nD_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau}).

(190)

4. This follows immediately from the fact that
(W_{\rho},W_{\tau})\succ(W_{{\cal E}(\rho)},W_{{\cal E}(\tau)})
for any free quantum channel
{\cal E}
that sends
\tau
into the interior of
{\cal F}
, and the Schur-convexity of
D_{\alpha}
.
∎

We are now in a position to derive general magic state distillation bounds.

Theorem 12
.

Consider a general magic state distillation protocol on odd prime dimension qudits, that converts a magic state
\rho^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow{\cal E}(\rho^{\otimes n})=\rho^{\prime\otimes m}
and let
\tau
be any full-rank stabilizer reference state on a qudit. Then, the distillation rate
R\coloneqq m/n
is upper bounded as

R\leq R_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{D}}$}\hss}D_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})},

(191)

where
\alpha=\frac{2a}{2b-1}
for any positive integers
a,b
with
a\geq b
and the average divergence per qudit

\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime\otimes m}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}}),

(192)

between the output magic state
\rho^{\prime\otimes m}
and
\tau^{\prime}_{m}={\cal E}(\tau^{\otimes n})
.

Proof.

The bound is a direct consequence of the properties of the
\alpha
–Rényi divergence in Theorem
11
.

Due to the action of the magic protocol channel, we get

D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\otimes n}})\geq D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime\otimes m}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}}).\vskip 10.0pt

(193)

We can use the additivity to rewrite this as

nD_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})\geq m\frac{1}{m}D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime\otimes m}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}}).

(194)

Since
\rho^{\prime\otimes m}\neq\tau^{\prime}_{m}
, we have
D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho^{\prime\otimes m}}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau^{\prime}_{m}})>0
, which directly leads to the bound

\frac{m}{n}\leq\frac{D_{\alpha}(W_{\rho}\hskip 1.0pt||\hskip 1.0ptW_{\tau})}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\widetilde{\phantom{D}}$}\hss}D_{\alpha}(\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}.

(195)

∎
