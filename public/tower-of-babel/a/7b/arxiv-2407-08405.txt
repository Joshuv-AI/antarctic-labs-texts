Floquet Schrieffer-Wolff transform based on Sylvester equations
Xiao Wang, Fabio Pablo Miguel Méndez-Córdoba, Dieter Jaksch, Frank Schlawin

Abstract
We present a Floquet Schrieffer Wolff transform (FSWT) to obtain effective Floquet Hamiltonians and micro-motion operators of periodically driven many-body systems for any non-resonant driving frequency. The FSWT perturbatively eliminates the oscillatory components in the driven Hamiltonian by solving operator-valued Sylvester equations with systematic approximations. It goes beyond various high-frequency expansion methods commonly used in Floquet theory, as we demonstrate with the example of the driven Fermi-Hubbard model. In the limit of high driving frequencies, the FSWT Hamiltonian reduces to the widely used Floquet-Magnus result. We anticipate this method will be useful for designing Rydberg multi-qubit gates, controlling correlated hopping in quantum simulations in optical lattices, and describing multi-orbital and long-range interacting systems driven in-gap.

We illustrate our method with the example of a driven Hubbard chain. We demonstrate the importance of correlated hopping terms, whose strength is underestimated in HFE. They can substantially change the Floquet-induced metal-insulator transition when the driving frequency is not too large.

II
The Floquet Schrieffer-Wolff transform for multi-frequency driving

We first outline the general procedure for time-dependent unitary transform, which forms the basis of our FSWT. The evolution operator from time
t_{0}
to
t
under any time-dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{t}
is denoted by
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}
. It satisfies the Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}=\hat{H}_{t}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}
where we set
\hbar=1
. Using an arbitrary time-dependent unitary transform
\hat{U}_{t}
, the evolution operator can be decomposed as
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}=\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{t,t_{0}}\hat{U}_{t_{0}}
. The evolution operator
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{t,t_{0}}
satisfies another Schrodinger equation
i\partial_{t}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{t,t_{0}}=\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{t,t_{0}}
with the transformed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}=\hat{U}_{t}\hat{H}_{t}\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}+i(\partial_{t}\hat{U}_{t})\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}
. By properly choosing
\hat{U}_{t}
, the evolution can be greatly simplified, such that
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}
becomes (approximately) time-independent, and the time-ordering becomes unnecessary when computing
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}
.

II.1
General Formalism

We consider a general driven system described by the following time-dependent Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{H}_{t}^{(n)}

(1)

Here, we assume the driving strength is characterized by a quantity
g
(which can be proportional to the electric field’s amplitude during the laser pulse),
and
\hat{H}_{t}^{(n)}\sim g^{n}
.
Each
\hat{H}_{t}^{(n)}
term may contain both time-dependent and static terms (which appear in, e.g. the co-rotating frame).

\hat{H}^{(0)}
is the undriven Hamiltonian with
\mathcal{O}(g^{0})
, which is time-independent. Thus the time-dependence starts at order
\mathcal{O}(g^{1})
in
\hat{H}_{t}
.

In the following, we aim to find a time-dependent unitary transform
\hat{U}_{t}
which eliminates the time-dependency in
\hat{H}_{t}
order by order in
g
, such that the remaining time-dependence in the transformed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}
only contains higher orders of driving strength
g
. Therefore, the residual time-dependence is much weaker than its original strength
g^{1}
and can be neglected,
H^{\prime}_{t}\simeq H^{\prime}
.
The evolution operator of the driven system,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}=\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\hat{H}^{\prime}(t-t_{0})}\hat{U}_{t_{0}},

(2)

is greatly simplified.
For time-periodic driving, which we analyze in Sec.
II.2
, this procedure is equivalent to block-diagonalizing the Sambe space Hamiltonian.

Analogous to the Schrieffer-Wolff transform in a time-independent system, the form of the time-dependent unitary transform
\hat{U}_{t}
is chosen to be

\hat{U}_{t}=e^{\hat{F}_{t}}

(3)

where
\hat{F}_{t}
is an anti-Hermitian operator. Using the derivative of the exponential map, the transformed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}
reads

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}&=\hat{U}_{t}\hat{H}_{t}\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}+i(\partial_{t}\hat{U}_{t})\hat{U}_{t}^{{\dagger}}\\
&=\hat{H}_{t}+\hat{G}_{t}+\frac{1}{2!}[\hat{F}_{t},\hat{G}_{t}]+\frac{1}{3!}[\hat{F}_{t},[\hat{F}_{t},\hat{G}_{t}]]+...\end{split}

(4)

where the operator
\hat{G}_{t}
is defined as

\hat{G}_{t}=[\hat{F}_{t},\hat{H}_{t}]+i\partial_{t}\hat{F}_{t}.

(5)

Eq. (
4
) has been used in previous high-frequency expansions

[
44
,
56
]
; however, below, we do not make use of the inverse-frequency expansion therein.
Instead, we expand
\hat{F}_{t}
in orders of driving strength

\hat{F}_{t}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{F}_{t}^{(n)},

(6)

where
\hat{F}_{t}^{(n)}\sim g^{n}
.
We require the unitary

generating the Floquet micro-motion to (

) preserve the undriven dynamics and (

) preserve the macro-motion

II.2
Time-periodic driving

The above perturbative procedure works for arbitrary time-dependent Hamiltonians. Below, we assume the driven Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}_{t+2\pi/\omega}
is time-periodic with angular frequency
\omega
.
We can Fourier transform each order of its driving terms in Eq. (
1
), such that (for all
n\geq 1
)

\hat{H}^{(n)}_{t}=\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{H}^{(n)}_{j}e^{ij\omega t}

(10)

where we allow
\hat{H}^{(n)}_{t}
to contain multi frequency components at
j\omega
, with
j
an arbitrary integer.
Here
\hat{H}^{(n)}_{j=0}
denotes the time-independent driving terms (e.g. the resonant driving term in the rotating frame).
In this case, we find
\hat{F}_{t}
also becomes time-periodic,
\hat{F}_{t}=\hat{F}_{t+2\pi/\omega}
, and we expand its
n
-th order into a Fourier series

\hat{F}^{(n)}_{t}=\sum\limits_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}e^{ij\omega t},

(11)

where
\hat{f}_{j}^{(n)}\propto(1-\delta_{n,0})(1-\delta_{j,0})
according to our ansatz (
7
), and the anti-Hermiticity of
\hat{F}_{t}
means
\hat{f}_{j}^{(n)}=-(\hat{f}^{(n)}_{-j})^{{\dagger}}
.

Inserting Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) into Eq. (
9
), we can determine
\hat{f}_{j}^{(n)}
for each order
n=1,2,3,...
, which eliminates the oscillatory component in
\hat{H}^{\prime(n)}_{t}
.

II.2.1
lowest order Floquet Hamiltonian

For
n=1
, the equation
\partial_{t}\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}_{t}=0
becomes,
\forall j\neq 0
,

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{j}+[\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-j\omega\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}=0,

(12)

which eliminates the term oscillating at
e^{ij\omega t}
in
\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}_{t}
. Equations of the form (
12
) are known as Sylvester equations in control theory
[
59
]
.

Eq. (
12
) decides the solution of
\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}
for all
j
.
Based on the unitary transform
\hat{U}_{t}=\exp\big(\hat{F}^{(1)}_{t}\big)=\exp\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}e^{ij\omega t}-H.c.\big)
, we find a transformed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{t}
with leading oscillatory contribution
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
.
Based on the ansatz (
7
), we know the solution
\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}
completely determines the static part in
\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}_{t}
in Eq. (
9c
).
Thus, we can directly pick out the static part in the transformed Hamiltonian (
9
) up to order
g^{2}
, which gives

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}^{\prime(0)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}

(13)

with the zeroth order
\hat{H}^{\prime(0)}=\hat{H}^{(0)}
, the first order
\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}=\hat{H}^{(1)}_{0}
, and the second order contribution

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}=\hat{H}^{(2)}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j\neq 0}[\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-j}].\end{split}

(14)

This
\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}
is identified as the lowest order Floquet Hamiltonian.

II.2.2
higher order Floquet Hamiltonians

For
n=2
, using
\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}=\hat{H}^{(1)}_{0}
determined above, the requirement
\partial_{t}\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}_{t}=0
becomes,
\forall j\neq 0
,

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{(2)}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j^{\prime}\neq 0}[\hat{f}_{j^{\prime}}^{(1)},\hat{H}_{j-j^{\prime}}^{(1)}]+\frac{1}{2}[\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)},\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}]+[\hat{f}_{j}^{(2)},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-j\omega\hat{f}_{j}^{(2)}=0,\end{split}

(15)

which eliminates the
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
terms oscillating at
e^{ij\omega t}
in
\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}_{t}
. These are again Sylvester equations, determining
\hat{f}_{j}^{(2)}

and thus the static part of
\hat{H}^{\prime(3)}_{t}
.
Collecting the static terms in Eq. (
9d
), we find this static part in
\mathcal{O}(g^{3})
to be

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime(3)}&=\hat{H}^{(3)}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j\neq 0}[\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)},\hat{H}^{(2)}_{-j}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j\neq 0}[\hat{f}_{j}^{(2)},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-j}]+\frac{1}{12}\sum\limits_{j\neq 0}\sum\limits_{j^{\prime}\neq 0}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j},[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j^{\prime}},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-j-j^{\prime}}]]-\frac{1}{12}\sum\limits_{j\neq 0}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j},[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{-j},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{0}]].\end{split}

(16)

The corresponding transformed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}^{\prime(0)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(3)}
is generated by the transform
\hat{U}_{t}=\exp\big(\hat{F}^{(1)}_{t}+\hat{F}^{(2)}_{t}\big)=\exp\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}+\hat{f}^{(2)}_{j})e^{ij\omega t}-H.c.\big)
.

This elimination can be extended to arbitrary orders of
g
. In the
n
-th order perturbation process, we first transform
\partial_{t}\hat{H}^{\prime(n)}_{t}=0
into Sylvester equations, from which
\hat{f}_{j}^{(n)}
are determined.
Then we use the solution
\hat{f}_{j}^{(n)}
to obtain the static term in
\hat{H}^{\prime(n+1)}_{t}
, which represents the correction to the Floquet Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}
at
g^{n+1}
order. The corresponding transform to get this
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is given by
\hat{U}_{t}=\exp\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{f}^{(k)}_{j}e^{ij\omega t}-H.c.\big)
.

II.2.3
The Floquet micro-motion

The time-periodic operator
\hat{F}_{t}
, found in FSWT by solving the Sylvester equations, is also known as the Floquet micro-motion operator
[
40
]
.
The formal solution to our Sylvester equations is given in Appendix
A
, which expresses the solution
\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}
as a Laplace-transformed Heisenberg operator. For example, the formal solution to the lowest order Sylvester equation (
12
) is,
\forall j\neq 0
,

\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{ij{\omega}t}~e^{-0^{+}t}~e^{i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}\hat{H}_{j}^{(1)}e^{-i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}

(17)

which directly relates the micro-motion
\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}
to the retarded Green function of the undriven system, and thus to the linear response. This link stems from the construction of
\hat{F}_{t}
in orders of
g
, instead of
1/{\omega}
, in our FSWT.

III
Comparison to other Floquet methods

III.1
Comparison to High-frequency expansion

For this comparison, we consider a system driven by a single frequency,
where
\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}e^{i\omega t}
. We find that the HFE expansion in Ref.
[
41
]
can be recovered by solving the Sylvester equation in orders of inverse driving frequency
1/{\omega}
(see Appendix
B
).
This confirms that the low-order Floquet Hamiltonians given by our FSWT in Eqs. (
14
) and (
16
), in the high-frequency limit, reduces to the HFE results in Eq. (
40
).

III.2
Equivalence of FSWT to the Sambe space van Vleck block-diagonalisation

Vogl et al. recently derived a low-frequency Floquet expansion
[
50
]
, which relies on the downfolding (i.e. Gaussian elimination) of the Sambe space Hamiltonian to the zeroth Floquet mode.
The self-consistency requirement therein stems from the fact that the downfolding to the zeroth Floquet mode is different from the block-diagonalisation of each Floquet sector of the Sambe space Hamiltonian.
This method becomes impractical in a many-body system as one has to solve the quasi-eigenenergy self-consistently. In our previous publication
[
60
]
, we used this Gaussian method without the self-consistency (to the lowest order driving strength)
using a many-body projector. Proceeding to higher-order driving effects becomes unfeasible, as divergences appear in the self-consistent determination of the many-body quasi-eigenenergy, which have to be cancelled at each order.

The Floquet Schrieffer-Wolff transform presented here, on the other hand, is equivalent to block-diagonalising the Sambe space Floquet Hamiltonian
(see Appendix
C
for details).
We find the following connection between our FSWT approach and the previous Sambe space block-diagonalisation method in Ref.
[
40
]
:
The latter method results in an expression for the micro-motion operators in the eigenbasis of the undriven Sambe-space Hamiltonian (see Eq.C.40 therein). This expression can be obtained in our approach by expanding the Sylvester equations (
12
) and (
15
) in the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}^{(0)}=\sum_{l}{\epsilon}_{l}|l\rangle\langle l|
, which provides the formal solution to
\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}
. For example, the expansion of Eq. (
12
) yields

\langle l|\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}|l^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{\langle l|\hat{H}^{(1)}_{j}|l^{\prime}\rangle}{j\omega-({\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}-{\epsilon}_{l})},

which coincides with Eq. (C.40) of Ref.
[
40
]
.
Although our FSWT provides the same formal solution given by the previous van Vleck method, in our case, the Sylvester equations can be solved (at least perturbatively) without knowledge of the eigenstates
|l\rangle
and eigenenergies
{\epsilon}_{l}
. This enables us to describe many-body systems under in-gap drive, where the eigenbasis cannot be obtained,
as we will demonstrate in the following section.

III.3
Absence of spurious Floquet-mediated interactions

For a driven many-body system, even in the absence of intrinsic interactions, several methods find spurious Floquet-mediated interactions,
as reported in
Ref.
[
43
]
.
We also find them in the Gaussian elimination method
[
50
]
.
These Floquet-mediated interactions account for the correlations found in a specific Fourier frequency component (e.g. the 0-th Floquet sector) of the driven many-body wavefunction,
as the wavefunction remains a product state in time domain.
When this Floquet-mediated interaction appears in an interacting system, it is difficult to separate it from the higher-order (non-linear) driving effects.
The exponential structure
\exp(\hat{F}_{t})
in our FSWT properly allocates this fake correlation into the Floquet micro-motion: Given by the transform
\exp(\hat{F}_{t})
, the Floquet Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is expressed entirely in terms of commutators, see Eq. (
16
), which cannot create Floquet-mediated interactions from a non-interacting system. Consequently, these correlations do not appear
in the macro-motion described by our FSWT.

IV
The Driven single-band Hubbard model

We next explore the applicability of the FWST with the example of the
one-band Hubbard model with on-site repulsion
U
and nearest neighbour hopping
J
. It is driven monochromatically with a
driving frequency
{\omega}
which is much higher than the hopping energy
J
(i.e.
{\omega}\gg J
). The FWST provides an effective Floquet Hamiltonian that remains accurate for arbitrary ratios of
U/J
.

The Hamiltonian for this driven system reads

\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}e^{-i{\omega}t}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}e^{i{\omega}t},

(18)

where
\hat{H}_{1}^{(1)}=(\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1})^{{\dagger}}
due to the Hermiticity of
\hat{H}_{t}
. The undriven Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}^{(0)}=\hat{h}+\hat{U}-\mu\hat{N},

(19)

with kinetic energy

\hat{h}=-J\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}(\hat{c}_{j+1,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,s}+\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j+1,s}),

(20)

local repulsion

\hat{U}=U\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}\hat{n}_{j,\uparrow}\hat{n}_{j,\downarrow},

(21)

and chemical potential
-\mu\hat{N}
, where
\hat{N}=\sum_{j,s}\hat{n}_{j,s}
is the total particle number.
We consider the electric dipole driving in the long-wavelength limit,

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}=g\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}j~\sum\limits_{s}\hat{n}_{j,s}.

(22)

which can be realised, e.g., in cold atom platforms using shaken lattices
[
61
]
or in electronic platforms
[
62
]
.
The total particle number commutes with the driving term, and therefore, the chemical potential is not altered by the driving. In the following, we will set
\mu=0
, unless specified otherwise. It can added to the effective Hamiltonian, if necessary.

In Hamiltonian (
18
), we encounter the particularly simple situation
\hat{H}^{(n\geq 1)}_{j}\propto\delta_{n,1}\delta_{j,\pm 1}
, i.e. all driving terms other than
\hat{H}^{(1)}_{\pm 1}
vanish identically. This implies
\hat{H}^{\prime(1)}=0
, i.e. there is no linear contribution to the Floquet Hamiltonian. To the lowest order of driving strength (
n=1
), in Eq. (
12
), there is only one Sylvester equation for
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}
which needs to be solved. It reads

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}+[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-{\omega}\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}=0,

(23)

and the corresponding Floquet Hamiltonian (
13
)
to quadratic order in
g
simplifies to

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime}&=\hat{H}^{\prime(0)}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}\\
&=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2}\big([\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+H.c.\big).\end{split}

(24)

We next solve the Sylvester equation (
23
). Without any approximation, the exact solution to Eq. (
23
) in a general driven many-body system with interactions will contain infinitely long operator-product terms, as does the Floquet Hamiltonian given by Eq. (
24
). This is shown in Appendix
D.1
and was also noticed in other Floquet methods
[
51
,
39
,
63
]
.
Below, we show how to find approximate solutions of the Sylvester equations for the driven Hubbard model, using neither the inverse-frequency expansion in HFE, nor the eigenbasis of the undriven many-body Hamiltonian.
Instead, since
J\ll{\omega}
, we perturbatively solve the lowest order Sylvester equation (
23
) by expanding
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}
in orders of the hopping
J
. We will see that the result constructed in this way remains applicable regardless of the ratio of
U/J
, ranging from strongly correlated to weakly correlated cases. This expansion in
J
still converges when
U>{\omega}
, in sharp contrast with the HFE results in Appendix
B
.

IV.1
driven Hubbard dimer

We start our discussion with a Hubbard dimer, with site index
j=1,2
.
We expand the function
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}
in orders of
J
, such that
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{y}_{n}
where
\hat{y}_{n}\sim J^{n}
, and insert it into Eq. (
23
). This results in a set of equations for different orders of
J
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}+[\hat{y}_{0},\hat{U}]-{\omega}\hat{y}_{0}=0

(25a)

\displaystyle[\hat{y}_{0},\hat{h}]+[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{U}]-{\omega}\hat{y}_{1}=0

(25b)

\displaystyle[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{h}]+[\hat{y}_{2},\hat{U}]-{\omega}\hat{y}_{2}=0,

(25c)

and so on. This system can be solved order by order.
The solution, including the
\hat{y}_{0}\sim J^{0}
and
\hat{y}_{1}\sim J^{1}
order, is

\begin{split}&\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}=\frac{g}{{\omega}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1,2}j~\hat{n}_{j,s}\\
&+\frac{gJ}{{\omega}^{2}}\sum\limits_{s}\hat{c}_{1,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{2,s}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{1,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{2,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{1,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{2,\bar{s}})\\
&-\frac{gJ}{{\omega}^{2}}\sum\limits_{s}\hat{c}_{2,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{1,s}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{2,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{1,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{2,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{1,\bar{s}})\\
&+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\right)\end{split}

(26)

where the coefficients are given by

\begin{split}\beta^{\prime}&=\frac{-U}{{\omega}+U}~~~~~~~~\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{U}{{\omega}-U}~~~~~~~\delta^{\prime}=-\beta^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime}\end{split}

(27)

We stress that the form of
\hat{y}_{1}
is not determined using the eigenbasis of the undriven Hamiltonian, but instead by symmetries: in Eq. (
25b
), we find
[\hat{y}_{0},\hat{h}]
contains hopping between site 1 and 2, while
\hat{U}
cannot create hopping, thus
\hat{y}_{1}
must contain hopping. And since
\hat{U}
correlates two spins, the hopping in
\hat{y}_{1}
must be accompanied by local terms
\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}
in the opposite spin. This symmetry argument completely determines the form of
\hat{y}_{1}
, and then the coefficients are obtained from Eq. (
25b
).

IV.2
driven Hubbard chain

In an
L
-site Hubbard chain, following the same perturbation procedure (in orders of
J
) and the same symmetry argument (for details, see Appendix
D.1
), we find the solution of Eq. (
23
), which reads

\begin{split}&\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}=\frac{g}{{\omega}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}j~\hat{n}_{j,s}+\frac{gJ}{{\omega}^{2}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}-\delta_{j-i,1}\right)\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})~~+\hat{y}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(J^{3})\end{split}

(28)

where the parameters
\beta^{\prime}
,
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}
are given in Eq. (
27
), and the
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
contribution
\hat{y}_{2}
is given in Eq. (
55
).
According to Eq. (
24
), the corresponding lowest-order corrected Floquet Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime(0)}&+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}=-J(1-\frac{g^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}})\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}(\hat{c}_{j+1,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,s}+\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j+1,s})~~~~~+~~~~U\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}\hat{n}_{j,\uparrow}\hat{n}_{j,\downarrow}\\
&~~~~~~-J~\frac{g^{2}U}{{\omega}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{U-{\omega}}+\frac{1}{{\omega}+U}\right)\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}(\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j+1,s}+\hat{c}_{j+1,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,s})\left(\frac{\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\hat{n}_{j+1,\bar{s}}}{2}-\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{j+1,\bar{s}}\right)\\
&~~~~~~+\frac{1}{2}([\hat{y}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+H.c.)+\mathcal{O}(J^{3}).\end{split}

(29)

The
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
term,
\frac{1}{2}([\hat{y}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+H.c.)
in the third line, is given explicitly in Eq. (
56
).
The non-interacting part of the Floquet Hamiltonian (
29
) shows the lowest order driving-induced bandwidth renormalisation (dynamical localisation), as predicted theoretically in, e.g. Refs.
[
64
,
65
,
66
]
and realised in e.g. Refs.
[
61
,
67
]
. The interacting part of Eq. (
29
) contains a driving-induced interaction in the second line of Eq. (
29
), which modifies the interaction term
\hat{U}
in the undriven system and renders the overall interaction non-local. This driving-induced interaction in Eq. (
29
) has the form of correlated hopping
[
68
]
.
Similar correlated hopping was previously identified in the effective Hamiltonians of undriven systems (e.g. in cold atoms near Feshbach resonances
[
69
]
and in cavity-coupled quantum materials
[
70
]
).
The correlated hopping may lead to eta-pairing scars in one dimension
[
71
]
and superconductivity in two dimensions
[
72
,
69
]
. It may also modify the Kibble-Zurek mechanism
[
73
]
when the driving quenches the system across a quantum phase transition (QPT)
[
74
]
.
In our driven system, as given by FSWT,
both the near-resonant contribution
1/(U-{\omega})
and the off-resonant contribution
1/({\omega}+U)
can be found in the coefficient of the correlated hopping in Eq. (
29
).

The HFE result (see Appendix
B
) is equivalent to expanding our FSWT result Eq. (
29
) in orders of
1/{\omega}
. In HFE, this correlated hopping term only occurs to order
{\omega}^{-2}(U^{2}-{\omega}^{2})^{-1}\sim\mathcal{O}({\omega}^{-4})
, and then we might expect it to be much weaker than the bandwidth renormalisation effect which is of order
{\omega}^{-2}
. Thus, in the lowest order HFE, one typically ignores the entire correlated hopping term.
However, in our FSWT result Eq. (
29
), we find the correlated hopping may be of similar strength as the bandwidth renormalization.

As an example, in Fig.
2
(see Appendix
E.1
for details), we carry out infinite DMRG simulations of the ground state occupancy,
\langle\hat{n}\rangle=\lim_{L\to\infty}\langle\hat{N}\rangle/L
, in a one-dimensional Hubbard chain using the TeNPy library
[
75
]
.
In these simulations, we assume a non-zero chemical potential
\mu
. This is necessary to observe
the equilibrium metal-insulator transition in one-dimensional Fermi Hubbard model
[
76
]
, and it does not alter the solution of any of the Sylvester equations.
We rescale the horizontal axis to
\mu-U/2
to match the convention of Lieb-Wu equation.
Near criticality, where the charge excitation gap closes and the DMRG algorithm no longer converges, we extrapolate the DMRG result using the Lieb-Wu equation
[
76
]
.
Our simulations show how the correlated hopping term in our FSWT result Eq. (
29

We see in Fig.

that it weakens the Mott insulating phase. It changes the phase boundary as well as the charge susceptibility of the metallic phase (i.e., the slope of the lines) close to the boundary.
The change is comparable in size to the dynamical localisation effect itself, i.e., the shift from the undriven case to the HFE result. This modification, arising solely from the
\mathcal{O}(J)
and
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
correlated hopping terms, is expected to be observable in electronic platforms, for instance, in quantum dot Fermi-Hubbard simulators
[
62
]
where
\mu
is controlled by gating.

IV.4
Comparison to Floquet methods in the strongly correlated regime

We next compare the Floquet Hamiltonian (
29
) to the one derived in Ref.
[
46
]
, where a Floquet Hamiltonian in driven strongly correlated systems is constructed systematically based on an interaction-dependent frame rotation followed by a high-frequency expansion.
In this widely used method [categorized as method (b) in Fig.
1
], the transformation to a rotating frame is carried out, in which the system oscillates at new frequencies
U+l{\omega}
with
l
being an integer.
This method converges in the strongly correlated limit and yields a Floquet Hamiltonian with coefficients
\frac{J^{2}}{U+l{\omega}}\big(\frac{g}{{\omega}}\big)^{2l}
.
In this rotating frame, the driving terms gain a factor
J
(see Eq. (S4) in Ref.
[
46
]
), and thus the lowest-order high-frequency expansion in this frame is quadratic in
J
.
In contrast, the Floquet Hamiltonian (
29
) given by our lowest order FSWT gives a contribution which is linear in
J
. The reason for this discrepancy is that these two Floquet methods are constructed in different frames, and we will show these two Floquet Hamiltonians are equivalent in the limit
U\gg J
, where the two can be compared.

To show this equivalence in the
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
Floquet Hamiltonian when
U\gg J
in a straightforward manner, we consider the half-filling case (
\mu=0
) and project to the lowest Mott band to obtain effective spin Hamiltonians.
In our Floquet Hamiltonian (
29
), where we treat the
\mathcal{O}(J)
part of
(\hat{h}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)})
as the perturbation and
\hat{U}
as the unperturbed term, degenerate perturbation theory using the projector
\mathcal{P}
onto the zero-doublon manifold yields (see Appendix
F
for details)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}(\hat{h}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)})\frac{1}{-U}(\hat{h}+\hat{H}^{\prime(2)})\mathcal{P}\\
&\approx\frac{1}{-U}\mathcal{P}\hat{h}^{2}\mathcal{P}+\frac{1}{-U}\mathcal{P}\hat{h}\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}\mathcal{P}+\frac{1}{-U}\mathcal{P}\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}\hat{h}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{O}(g^{4})\\
&=\frac{4J^{2}}{U}(1-2\frac{g^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}})\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}{\bf S}_{j}\cdot{\bf S}_{j+1}\\
&~~+4\frac{g^{2}J^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{U-{\omega}}+\frac{1}{{\omega}+U}\right)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}{\bf S}_{j}\cdot{\bf S}_{j+1},\end{split}

(32)

where
{\bf S}_{j}=(S_{j}^{x},S_{j}^{y},S_{j}^{z})
, with
S_{j}^{x}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}+\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow})
,
S_{j}^{y}=\frac{i}{2}(-\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}+\hat{c}_{j,\downarrow}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,\uparrow})
and
S_{j}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{n}_{j,\uparrow}-\hat{n}_{j,\downarrow})
.
This projected Hamiltonian is equivalent to the expression
for
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
in Ref.
[
46
]
. We thus conclude that in the
U\gg J
limit, we can derive the previous Hamiltonian (to order
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
) in Ref.
[
46
]
from our lowest order FSWT Hamiltonian.
In other words, the Hamiltonian (
29
) given by our FSWT is the electronic parent Hamiltonian of the spin model derived in Ref.
[
46
]
, just like the
Hubbard model
reduces to the
t-J model
[
76
,
88
]
.
Moreover, in a two-site lattice, the projected Hamiltonian (
32
) reduces exactly to the previous Floquet result given by Ref.
[
89
]
.
Similarly, in the sub-lattice-driven Hubbard model, the enhancement of super-exchange pairing, that was reported in
[
90
]
, can also be traced back to the correlated hopping terms given by FSWT.
Unlike Refs.
[
46
,
89
,
90
]
, our FSWT result can be applied for all ratios
U/J
, as long as
J\ll{\omega},{\omega}/\gamma^{\prime}
. Our FSWT Hamiltonian (
29
) can be used in the zero-correlation limit
U\to 0
and the weakly correlated case
U\sim J
, as shown in Fig.

V
outlook

We have constructed a Floquet Schrieffer Wolff transform to perturbatively generate the many-body Floquet Hamiltonian and micro-motion operators in orders of the driving strength
g
.
This transform is obtained from the solution of operator-valued Sylvester equations and does not require knowledge of the eigenbasis of the undriven many-body system.
As an example, we showed how to solve the Sylvester equations perturbatively in driven Hubbard models: By expanding the Sylvester equation in orders of hopping
J
,
we derived a Floquet Hamiltonian that may be applied for any interaction strength of
U
. This remains true even when
U\sim J
or
U>{\omega}
, provided that
J\ll{\omega}
and one does not resonantly drive the Mott gap.
We used this Floquet Hamiltonian to show that driving-induced correlated hoppings shift the Mott-insulator transition point in a one-dimensional chain. We showed that these effects, which are underestimated in high-frequency expansions, give a large contribution that can be on par with the dominant renormalization of electronic hopping.

We envision several extensions of our formalism that can treat important scenarios of driven many-body physics:
For near-resonant driving, it will be convenient to construct the FSWT in the co-rotating frame, where the solution to the Sylvester equation has no divergence. The corresponding Floquet Hamiltonian then describes how off-resonant driving terms, which give rise to, e.g., the Bloch-Siegert shift
[
91
]
in TMDC, influence the resonant dynamics.
For deep strong driving (relevant for rf-driven superconducting qubits
[
92
]
), where
g\gg{\omega}
, one can still apply our FSWT by switching to a nonuniformly rotating frame (via a Peierls transform)
[
93
,
92
,
94
,
95
,
96
]
, where the oscillatory strength is transferred from
g
to smaller parameters in the undriven system, such as the hopping
J
, see Appendix
D.4
. In such a frame, we can construct the FSWT expansion in powers of
J
, which provides the stroboscopic Hamiltonian in the
g\gg{\omega}
case.

The FSWT expansion method presented here could be directly adapted to other driven many-body systems with more interaction terms (such as longer-ranged density-density repulsion or multi-orbital Kanamori interactions
[
97
]
).
The method is also suitable for systems driven by time-dependent interactions
[
98
,
23
]
, where the Floquet Hamiltonian can be obtained by solving Eq. (
53
).
The crucial advantage of our method is that it provides a single effective Hamiltonian that is not restricted to the driving frequency being the largest energy scale involved.
This advantage becomes more prominent when more and more interacting terms are taken into account, such as in driven Rydberg arrays
[
99
,
100
]
relevant for multi-qubit gate-design
[
101
,
102
]
.
Finally, we note that the FSWT provides a convenient and accurate method to analyze driven correlated materials near the Mott transition. This regime is particularly relevant for several unconventional superconductors, where light-induced superconductivity was observed in recent experiments in fullerides
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
and charge transfer salts
[
29
,
30
]
.

Appendix A
The formal solution to the Sylvester equation

Here, we formally solve the Sylvester equations in our FSWT, based on the following mathematical relation (see Theorem 9.2 in Ref.
[
59
]
): For 3 arbitrary operators
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
and
\hat{c}
, where each eigenvalue of
\hat{a}
has positive real part, and each eigenvalue of
\hat{b}
is purely imaginary, the solution to the Sylvester equation
\hat{a}\hat{f}-\hat{f}\hat{b}=\hat{c}
is given by

\begin{split}\hat{f}&=-e^{-t\hat{a}}\hat{f}e^{t\hat{b}}\big|_{t=0}^{\infty}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~\partial_{t}\big(e^{-t\hat{a}}\hat{f}e^{t\hat{b}}\big)\\
&=-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~\partial_{t}\big(e^{-t\hat{a}}\big)\hat{f}e^{t\hat{b}}+e^{-t\hat{a}}\hat{f}\partial_{t}\big(e^{t\hat{b}}\big)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{-t\hat{a}}~\hat{a}\hat{f}~e^{t\hat{b}}-e^{-t\hat{a}}~\hat{f}\hat{b}~e^{t\hat{b}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{-t\hat{a}}~\hat{c}~e^{t\hat{b}}\end{split}

(33)

If we take
\hat{a}=-i(\hat{H}^{(0)}+j{\omega})+0^{+}
,
\hat{b}=-i\hat{H}^{(0)}
and
\hat{c}=-i\hat{H}^{(1)}_{j}
, we can identify the lowest-order Sylvester equation (
12
) with this form, and thus we can directly write down its solution
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}
using Eq. (
33
):
\forall j\neq 0
,

\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{ij{\omega}t}~e^{-0^{+}t}~e^{i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}\hat{H}_{j}^{(1)}e^{-i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}

(34)

Eq. (
33
) further provides the formal solution to the Sylvester equation at higher orders. For example, to solve the second-order equation (
15
), we take
\hat{a}=-i(\hat{H}^{(0)}+j{\omega})+0^{+}
,
\hat{b}=-i\hat{H}^{(0)}
and
\hat{c}=-i\hat{S}^{(2)}_{j}
where

\hat{S}^{(2)}_{j}=\hat{H}^{(2)}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j^{\prime}\neq 0}[\hat{f}_{j^{\prime}}^{(1)},\hat{H}_{j-j^{\prime}}^{(1)}]+\frac{1}{2}[\hat{f}_{j}^{(1)},\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}]

denotes the source term in the Sylvester equation (
15
). Eq. (
33
) then gives,
\forall j\neq 0
,

\hat{f}^{(2)}_{j}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{ij{\omega}t}~e^{-0^{+}t}~e^{i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}\hat{S}_{j}^{(2)}e^{-i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}

(35)

In general, once the
n
th order Sylvester equation is constructed from which source term
\hat{S}_{j}^{(n)}
is identified, the formal solution is directly given by,
\forall j\neq 0
,

\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt~e^{ij{\omega}t}~e^{-0^{+}t}~e^{i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}\hat{S}_{j}^{(n)}e^{-i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}

(36)

which always takes the form of a frequency-domain (i.e. Laplace-transformed) Heisenberg operator under the undriven Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{(0)}
.

A.1
Link to the retarded Green function

The above formal solution links
\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}
to the retarded Green function of the undriven system.
To show this, we consider the correlator
\langle[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{A}]\rangle
where
\hat{A}
is an observable and the expectation value is taken over the thermal/ground state of the undriven system
\hat{H}^{(0)}
. Then according to Eq. (
34
) we have

\langle[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{A}]\rangle=-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt~e^{i({\omega}+i0^{+})t}~\theta_{t}\langle[\big(\hat{H}_{1}^{(1)}\big)^{H}_{t},\hat{A}]\rangle

(37)

where
\theta_{t}
is the step function at
t=0
,
\big(\hat{H}_{1}^{(1)}\big)^{H}_{t}=e^{i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}\hat{H}_{1}^{(1)}e^{-i\hat{H}^{(0)}t}
represents the Heisenberg operator of
\hat{H}_{1}^{(1)}
.
Hence,
\langle[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{A}]\rangle
is a frequency-domain retarded Green function of the undriven system
\hat{H}^{(0)}
.
This direct link between
\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}
and the retarded Green function suggests that we can obtain the linear and non-linear responses of the system from the solutions of the Sylvester equations

6
6

6

For example, in a monochromatically driven system described by
\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}e^{i\omega t}
, by expanding the expectation value
\langle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{t,t_{0}}\rangle
in orders of
g
and picking out terms oscillating at
e^{ij{\omega}t}
where
j\in\mathds{Z}
, we find that the
\mathcal{O}(g)
linear response of an observable
\hat{A}
is given by
\delta^{(1)}\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\langle[\hat{F}^{(1)}_{t},\hat{A}]\rangle
, and the
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
response is given by
\delta^{(2)}\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\langle[\hat{F}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{A}]\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle[\hat{F}^{(1)}_{t},[\hat{F}^{(1)}_{t},\hat{A}]]\rangle
. This means we can directly compute these responses when
\hat{f}^{(n)}_{j}
is found.
.
Practically, to evaluate Eq. (
37
) for strongly correlated systems, we need to obtain the thermal density operator numerically, which we leave for future work.

Appendix B
Comparison to Floquet Magnus High Frequency Expansion

Here, we assume a simple single-frequency driving Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}_{t}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}e^{i\omega t}

and compare the Floquet high-frequency expansion (HFE) with the solutions of the FSWT. In HFE, Eq. (
4

(39)

where

which yields the following Floquet Hamiltonian

\begin{split}&\hat{H}^{\prime}_{HFE}=\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{\omega}[\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]\\
&+[\frac{[\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}]}{2\omega^{2}},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+[\frac{[\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1},\hat{H}^{(0)}+\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}]}{2\omega^{2}},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]\\

Inserting this high-frequency solution back into Eqs. (

in Eq. (

This confirms that the FSWT reduces to the HFE if the Sylvester equations are solved in orders of inverse driving frequency
1/{\omega}
.

In the driven Hubbard system studied in Section
IV

\hat{H}_{0}^{(1)}=0

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}=\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}

\hat{U}

The order
\mathcal{O}({\omega}^{-3})

\hat{y}_{2}

is found to be

&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+(\beta^{\prime}+\gamma^{\prime})\big(~\hat{n}_{j+1\uparrow}\hat{n}_{j+1\downarrow}(1-\hat{n}_{j})~-~\hat{n}_{j\uparrow}\hat{n}_{j\downarrow}(1-\hat{n}_{j+1})~\big)\bigg\}\\
&+\frac{J^{2}g}{{\omega}^{3}}\sum\limits_{j=2}^{L-1}\sum\limits_{s}\bigg\{~\hat{c}_{j-1s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j+1s}\big((\beta^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime})\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}-\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1,\bar{s}}-\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}(\hat{n}_{j-1,\bar{s}}-\hat{n}_{j+1,\bar{s}})\big)(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}})\\

&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\hat{c}_{js}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j-1\bar{s}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j\bar{s}}\hat{c}_{j+1s}~\big((\beta^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime})-\delta^{\prime}(\hat{n}_{j-1s}-\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}})\big)(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1s}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1s}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}})\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\hat{c}_{j+1s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j\bar{s}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j-1\bar{s}}\hat{c}_{js}~\big((\gamma^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime})-\delta^{\prime}(\hat{n}_{j-1s}-\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}})\big)(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1s}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{j-1s})\\

(55)

In this

order micro-motion

This

contributes to the following

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}([\hat{y}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+H.c.)\\

&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+2(\hat{c}_{js}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j-1\bar{s}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j\bar{s}}\hat{c}_{j+1s}+\hat{c}_{j+1s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j\bar{s}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j-1\bar{s}}\hat{c}_{js})(1-\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1s}-\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}}+2\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j-1s}\hat{n}_{j+1\bar{s}})~\bigg\}\end{split}

(56)

where the coefficients are given in Eq. (

). We find this
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
Floquet Hamiltonian contains fewer terms than the micro-motion
\hat{y}_{2}
: According to our driving term

\hat{y}_{2}

The
\mathcal{O}(g^{2})
second-lowest order

) driving effects, we need to solve two Sylvester equations according to Eq. (

\displaystyle[\hat{f}^{(2)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-{\omega}\hat{f}^{(2)}_{1}=0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]+[\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-2{\omega}\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2}=0.

Form these, we find
\hat{f}^{(2)}_{\pm 1}=0

\hat{H}^{\prime(2n)}

based on Gaussian elimination.

Here we solve Eq. (

) in the

\hat{z}_{n}\sim J^{n}
. This decomposes the Sylvester equation (

) in orders of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{y}_{0},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]+[\hat{z}_{0},\hat{U}]-2{\omega}\hat{z}_{0}=0

(58a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]+[\hat{z}_{0},\hat{h}]+[\hat{z}_{1},\hat{U}]-2{\omega}\hat{z}_{1}=0

(58b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{y}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]+[\hat{z}_{1},\hat{h}]+[\hat{z}_{2},\hat{U}]-2{\omega}\hat{z}_{2}=0

(58c)

and so on. Here we also used the expansion
\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{y}_{n}
where
\hat{y}_{0}
and
\hat{y}_{1}

D.1
.
According to the result Eq. (
46

58a

\hat{z}_{0}=0
. The source term in Eq. (

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]=\frac{Jg^{2}}{2{\omega}^{2}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{L}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}+\delta_{j-i,1}\right)\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}).\end{split}

(59)

Similar to what we did in Appendix
D.1
, we solve the Sylvester equation for each term in
\frac{1}{2}[\hat{y}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]
and then sum them up, which gives the solution to Eq. (
58b

\begin{split}\hat{z}_{1}&=\frac{Jg^{2}}{4{\omega}^{3}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{L}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}+\delta_{j-i,1}\right)\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})\times(1+\beta^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})\\
&\equiv\frac{Jg^{2}}{4{\omega}^{3}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{L}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}+\delta_{j-i,1}\right)\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}(1+\beta_{2}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma_{2}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta_{2}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})\end{split}

(60)

where in the first line
\beta^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime}
is are simply the coefficients
\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}
with

. Since

diverges not only when

(when

diverges), but also when

(when
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
diverges). The divergence at

\beta_{2},\gamma_{2},\delta_{2}
are defined for future use, i.e.
\beta_{2}\equiv\beta^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime\prime}+\beta^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\prime}

\gamma_{2}\equiv\gamma^{\prime}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}+\gamma^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime\prime}

\delta_{2}\equiv\delta^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime\prime}+\gamma^{\prime}\beta^{\prime\prime}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}\beta^{\prime}+\gamma^{\prime}\delta^{\prime\prime}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}\delta^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}\delta^{\prime\prime}+\beta^{\prime\prime}\delta^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\delta^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=0

(61a)

\displaystyle=0

(61b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]+\frac{1}{12}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{1}]]+[\hat{f}^{(3)}_{3},\hat{H}^{(0)}]-3{\omega}\hat{f}^{(3)}_{3}

\hat{H}^{\prime(4)}=\left(\frac{1}{2}[\hat{f}^{(3)}_{1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]+\frac{1}{12}[\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2},[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{-1},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]]+\frac{1}{12}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{-1},[\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2},\hat{H}^{(1)}_{-1}]]-\frac{1}{12}[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{1},[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{-1},\hat{H}^{\prime(2)}]]\right)+H.c.

(62)

These higher-order solutions can be derived in the same way. We find
\hat{f}^{(3)}_{1}
diverges at
{\omega}=U,U/2
,
\hat{f}^{(3)}_{2}=0
, and
\hat{f}^{(3)}_{3}
diverges at
{\omega}=U,U/2,U/3
. This means
\hat{H}^{\prime(4)}
diverges at

Explicitly, by solving Eq. (

), we find

\begin{split}\hat{f}^{(3)}_{1}&=\frac{Jg^{3}}{{\omega}^{4}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{L}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}-\delta_{j-i,1}\right)\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}(-\frac{11}{24}+\beta_{3}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma_{3}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta_{3}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})~~+\mathcal{O}(J^{2})\end{split}

(63)

where the parameters
\beta_{3},\gamma_{3},\delta_{3}

\begin{split}(-\frac{11}{24}+\beta_{3}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma_{3}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta_{3}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})&=\frac{1}{8}(1+\beta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})^{2}~(-1+\beta^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta^{\prime\prime}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}})\\

(64)

Inserting the solution of
\hat{f}^{(2)}_{2}
and
\hat{f}^{(3)}_{1}
, i.e. Eqs. (
60
) and (

), into Eq. (

), we find

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\prime(4)}&=\frac{g^{4}J}{2{\omega}^{4}}\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{L}\left(\delta_{i-j,1}+\delta_{j-i,1}\right)(\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{i,s}+\hat{c}_{i,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,s})\big(-\frac{1}{4}+\beta_{4}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}+\gamma_{4}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}+\delta_{4}\hat{n}_{j,\bar{s}}\hat{n}_{i,\bar{s}}\big)\\

(65)

where we have defined

,

D.4
FSWT in the strong driving frame

To apply FSWT to the strongly driven Hubbard model (i.e.,
g\gg{\omega}
) it is favourable to change into a strong driving frame as, otherwise, we would require to solve the Sylvester equations to higher orders of
g
. The change into a strong driving frame is a direct route to observe the Bessel function renormalization in the FSWT Hamiltonian, as we explain below. To change frames, we apply the Peierls transform
[
92
,
94
,
95
]

\hat{U}^{\text{r}}_{t}=\exp\left(i\frac{2g}{{\omega}}\sin({\omega}t)\sum_{j=1}^{L}j\sum_{s}\hat{n}_{j,s}\right)

(66)

to the lab-frame Hamiltonian in Eq. (
18
).
The transformed Hamiltonian acquires a Peierls phase coupling, replacing the driving strength
g
with
J
. It reads

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{P,t}=\hat{H}_{P}^{(0)}+\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{H}^{(1)}_{P,j}e^{ij\omega t},\end{split}

(67)

where the superscript now represents the orders of hopping
J
. In Eq. (
67
), the undriven part no longer contains hopping, as it is given by

\hat{H}_{P}^{(0)}=U\sum_{{\bf j}}\hat{n}_{{\bf j},\uparrow}\hat{n}_{{\bf j},\downarrow},

(68)

and the driving term oscillating at
e^{ij\omega t}
reads

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{P,j}=-J~\mathcal{J}_{j}(2g/\omega)\sum\limits_{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L-1}(\hat{c}_{j+1,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j,s}+\hat{c}_{j,s}^{{\dagger}}\hat{c}_{j+1,s}),

(69)

where
\mathcal{J}_{j}
is the
j
th kind Bessel function.
In this rotating frame, the driving term has a static component
\hat{H}^{(1)}_{P,0}
which accounts for the well known
\mathcal{J}_{0}(2g/{\omega})
bandwidth renormalisation. The
\mathcal{O}(J)
lowest order Sylvester equation for the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
67
) is, for all
j\neq 0
,

\hat{H}^{(1)}_{P,j}+[\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j},\hat{H}_{P}^{(0)}]-j\omega\hat{f}^{(1)}_{j}=0.

(70)

We thus see that, solving Eq. (
70
) is equivalent to solving Eq. (
25b
), which results in the parameters akin to
\beta^{\prime}
,
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}
. The corresponding FSWT Hamiltonian in this strong driving frame will be explored in future work.
