On functions whose mean value abscissas are Stolarsky means of endpoints
Zarif O. Ibragimov, Farrukh Mukhammadiev

Abstract
. We provide a new approach to determine all differentiable functions whose mean value abscissas are Stolarsky means of endpoints.



1.
Introduction and the main result

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an open interval and let
f\colon I\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable function. For any finite interval
(a,b)\subset I
,
Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem guarantees the existence of
c\in(a,b)
such that the tangent to the graph of
f
at the point
(c,f(c))

is parallel to the secant through its endpoints
(a,f(a))
and
(b,f(b))
.

It is one of the many beautiful properties of the parabolas with horizontal directrix that the mean value in the Lagrange Mean Value Theorem always corresponds
to the arithmetic mean of the endpoints. That is, we have

f(b)-f(a)=(b-a)f^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\Bigr)\quad\text{for all}\quad a,b\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1)

if
f
is any quadratic polynomial. It is then a natural question to ask whether quadratic polynomials are the only members of the
vector space of differentiable functions having the property (
1
). More generally, by asking for which
f
the mean
value
c
in Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem depends on the endpoints in a certain given manner, one arrives at so-called functional-differential equations.

It was Haruki
[
4
]
and Aczél
[
1
]
who showed independently that the quadratic polynomials are the only differentiable functions that
solve (
1
). The functional-differential equation (
1
) was one of the starting points for the rich literature
devoted to various and more general functional equations including the ones for higher order Taylor expansions and also in the abstract setting of groups.
To name just a few, we mention
[
8
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
2
]
and also
[
9
]
for a textbook
reference.

In this note we are interested in the case when the mean value in Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem corresponds to the so-called
Stolarsky mean
of end-points.
More precisely, we solve the functional-differential equation

f(b)-f(a)=(b-a)f^{\prime}(S_{\alpha}(a,b)),\quad a,b>0,

(2)

where, for
\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty)
,

S_{\alpha}:(0,\infty)^{2}\to(0,\infty)
stands for the
Stolarsky mean which is a continuous function defined
by

S_{\alpha}(a,b)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{b^{\alpha}-a^{\alpha}}{\alpha(b-a)}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}&\text{if }\alpha\notin\{0,1\},\\[6.45831pt]
S_{0}(a,b)&\text{if }\alpha=0,\\[4.30554pt]
S_{1}(a,b)&\text{if }\alpha=1,\\
\end{cases}

for all
a,b>0
with
a\neq b
, and
S_{\alpha}(a,a):=a

for all
a>0
, where

S_{0}(a,b):=\frac{b-a}{\log b-\log a},\quad S_{1}(a,b):=\frac{1}{e}\exp\bigg(\frac{b\log b-a\log a}{b-a}\bigg),

see e.g.
[
10
]
.
For example, the case
\alpha=2
corresponds to the
arithmetic mean.

Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
be arbitrary and let
f\colon(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
a differentiable function satisfying (
2
).

(i)

If
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
, then there exist constants

c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f(x)=c_{1}x^{\alpha}+c_{2}x+c_{3},\quad x>0.

(3)

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
, then there exist constants

c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f(x)=c_{1}x^{\alpha}\log(x)+c_{2}x+c_{3},\quad x>0.

(4)

In particular, we have the following result for the case
when the mean value in Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem
corresponds to the geometric mean of endpoints.

Corollary 2
.

Let
f\colon(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable function such that

f(b)-f(a)=(b-a)f^{\prime}\bigl(\sqrt{ab}\bigr)\quad\text{for all}\quad a,b>0.

(5)

Then there exist constants

c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f(x)=\frac{c_{1}}{x}+c_{2}x+c_{3},\quad x>0.

(6)

It is easy to check that all the functions of the form
(
3
)-(
4
) indeed satisfy (
2
).
To the best of our knowledge, the first and the only work in
the current literature which considers (
2
)
is the paper
[
6
]
.
The purpose of this note is to provide a direct approach based on reducing
the problem to elementary differential equations with a novel trick
inspired by the methods of
[
2
]
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

First we establish a preliminary result on the smoothness
of the solutions of (
2
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
be arbitrary. Every differentiable function

f:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying (
2
) is
infinitely differentiable on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

We provide a detailed proof for the case
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
.
The analyses of the cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
are
completely similar and are left to the interested reader.
Let us take arbitrary
x_{0}>0
. Below we show infinite
differentiability of
f
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.
It is easy to see that there are
y_{0}>0
and
h_{0}>0

such that

(y_{0}+h_{0})^{\alpha}-y_{0}^{\alpha}=\alpha h_{0}x_{0}^{\alpha-1}
.
For example, we can take

y_{0}=h_{0}=\Bigl(\frac{2^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}x_{0}.

Next, we consider the function

\psi(x,y)=(y+h_{0})^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}-\alpha h_{0}x^{\alpha-1}.

We have
\psi(x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Clearly,
\psi
is continuously
differentiable with

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\psi(x,y)=\alpha(y+h_{0})^{\alpha-1}-\alpha y^{\alpha-1}

which does not vanish since
\alpha\neq 1
and
\alpha\neq 0
.
By the implicit
function theorem there is a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
and
a unique continuously differentiable function

\phi:U\to(0,\infty)
such that
\phi(x_{0})=y_{0}
and

\psi(x,\phi(x))=0
for all
x\in U
.
Furthermore,

\phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(\alpha-1)h_{0}x^{\alpha-2}}{(\phi(x)+h_{0})^{\alpha-1}-\phi(x)^{\alpha-1}},\quad x\in U.

(7)

Inserting
\phi
and
\phi+h_{0}
in (
2
)
for
a
and
b
, respectively, we get

f(h_{0}+\phi(x))-f(\phi(x))=h_{0}f^{\prime}(x),\quad x\in U.

(8)

It follows from (
7
) that
\phi
is infinitely
differentiable in
U
.
Hence, if we assume that
f
is
k\geq 1
times differentiable,
then (
8
) implies its
k+1
times differentiability
in
U
. By induction, it follows that
f
is infinitely
differentiable in the neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.
First let
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
.
Let
t>0
be fixed arbitrarily.
For every
r>0
, there is
x_{0}>0
such that

(t+r)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}=\alpha rx_{0}^{\alpha-1}
.
Next, we consider the function

\Psi(h,y)=(y+h)^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}-\alpha hx_{0}^{\alpha-1}

(9)

on
(0,\infty)^{2}
. We have
\Psi(r,t)=0
. It is easy to check that the implicit
function theorem applies and yields the existence of a
neighborhood
V
of
r
, and a unique continuously
differentiable function
g\colon V\to(0,\infty)
such
that
g(r)=t
and
\Psi(h,g(h))=0
for all
h\in V
.
Moreover, we have

g^{\prime}(h)=\frac{1}{\alpha h}\frac{(g(h)+h)^{\alpha}-g(h)^{\alpha}-\alpha h(g(h)+h)^{\alpha-1}}{(g(h)+h)^{\alpha-1}-g(h)^{\alpha-1}}.

(10)

By inserting
g
and
h+g
in (
2
)
for
a
and
b
, respectively, and taking into account the
fact that
\Psi(h,g(h))=0
for all
h\in V
, we get

f(h+g(h))-f(g(h))=hf^{\prime}(x_{0}),\quad h\in V.

(11)

Next, let us differentiate (
11
)
with respect to
h
to get

f^{\prime}(h+g(h))+f^{\prime}(h+g(h)g^{\prime}(h)-f^{\prime}(g(h))g^{\prime}(h)=f^{\prime}(x_{0}).

(12)

If we multiply (
12
) by
\frac{1}{h^{2}}
and
differentiate the resulting identity with respect to
h
,
and insert (
10
), then for
h=r
, we obtain,

\frac{1}{\alpha r}\Bigl(f^{\prime}(r+t)-f^{\prime}(t)\Bigr)=\frac{R(r,t)}{S(r,t)}+(f^{\prime}(x_{0})-f^{\prime}(t))\frac{T(r,t)}{S(r,t)},

(13)

for all
r>0
, where

\displaystyle R(r,t):=

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(r+t)}{r^{3}}\phi(r,t)^{2}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)}{r^{3}}\psi(r,t)^{2}

\displaystyle S(r,t):=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{r^{3}}\bigl((r+t)^{\alpha-1}-t^{\alpha-1}\bigr)\phi(r,t)-\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{r^{2}}t^{\alpha-2}\psi(r,t)

\displaystyle T(r,t):=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha-1}{r^{3}}\Big((r+t)^{\alpha-2}\phi(r,t)-t^{\alpha-2}\psi(r,t)\Big)-\frac{1}{r^{4}}\bigl((r+t)^{\alpha-1}-t^{\alpha-1}\bigr)

with

\phi(r,t):=\frac{(r+t)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}}{\alpha r}-t^{\alpha-1},\quad\psi(r,t):=\frac{(r+t)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}}{\alpha r}-(r+t)^{\alpha-1}.

It is not difficult to check that the following asymptotic
expansions hold

\displaystyle R(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{4}t^{2\alpha-4}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha-2)}{6}t^{2\alpha-5}f^{\prime\prime}(t)+{\text{o}}(1),

\displaystyle S(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha-2)}{12}t^{2\alpha-5}+{\text{o}}(1),

\displaystyle T(r,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha-2)t^{2\alpha-6}}{12}+{\text{o}}(1),

as
r\searrow 0
. Furthermore, from the equation
\Psi(r,t)=0
, we get

x_{0}=\bigg(\frac{(t+r)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}}{\alpha r}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}=t+{\text{o}}(1),\quad\text{as}\quad r\searrow 0.

In view of these asymptotic expansions, letting
r\searrow 0

in (
13
), we deduce the elementary differential
equation

f^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{\alpha-2}{t}f^{\prime\prime}(t)=0.

(14)

Since
t>0
was fixed arbitrarily, (
14
) must
hold for all
t>0
. Taking into account the hypothesis that

\alpha(\alpha-1)\neq 0
, it follows immediately that
the solution space of the differential equation in (
14
)
is given by
\text{span}\{1,t,t^{\alpha}\}
, completing the proof
of part (i).

For the cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
, in the
same way as above one obtains the differential equation
(
14
) (for the corresponding values of
\alpha
),
the solution spaces of which are
\text{span}\{1,t,\log t\}

and
\text{span}\{1,t,t\log t\}
, respectively.
∎
