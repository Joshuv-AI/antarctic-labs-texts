Rationalizability of field extensions with a view towards Feynman integrals
Dino Festi, Andreas Hochenegger

Abstract
. In 2021, Marco Besier and the first author introduced the concept of rationalizability of square roots to simplify arguments of Feynman integrals. In this work, we generalize the definition of rationalizability to field extensions. We then show that the rationalizability of a set of quadratic field extensions is equivalent to the rationalizability of the compositum of the field extensions, providing a new strategy to prove rationalizability of sets of square roots of polynomials.



1.
Introduction

Feynman integrals play an important role in high-energy physics, see for example
[
7
]
for a comprehensive introduction.
From a mathematical point of view, a Feynman integral is interesting, as the argument often contains a square root.
This square root makes the numerical evaluation of the integral computationally more expensive, preventing the achievement of higher precision that could lead to more incisive experiments.
This issue can often be overcome with a change of variables in the integrals making the square root disappear and leaving only a rational expression.
Unfortunately, such change of variables does not always exist and stating its (non-)existence can be a cumbersome problem for physicists with no strong background in algebraic geometry as well as for experienced geometers.
This challenge led Marco Besier and the first author to introduce the concept of
rationalizability
of square roots of polynomials, in
[
1
]
.
Roughly speaking, a square root of a polynomial is rationalizable if there is a rational change of variables making the square root disappear, i.e., turning the polynomial into a square.
In the same paper, some practical criteria for the rationalizability of square roots of polynomials in one and two variables are given.
Finally, the concept of rationalizability for
sets
(also called
alphabets
in that paper) of square roots of polynomials is introduced.
On the one hand, there is a practical strategy to disprove rationalizability of a given set (cf.
[
1
, Proposition 47 and Remark 48]
),
but on the other hand the original paper does not offer a solid strategy to
prove
the rationalizability (cf.
[
1
, Remarks 48 and 52]
).

In this paper, we try to fill in this gap.
To do so, we introduce the notion of rationalizability for field extensions, generalizing the concept of rationalizability for square roots.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
K
be any field.
We say that a field extension
K\subset L
is
rationalizable
if there exists a non-zero homomorphism
\psi\colon L\to K
.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
let
L_{i}
be a field extension of
K
, with
\iota_{i}\colon K\hookrightarrow L
the natural inclusion.
We say that the set
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
is
rationalizable
if for each
i
there is a non-zero homomorphism
\psi_{i}\colon L_{i}\to K
such that

\psi_{1}\circ\iota_{1}=\cdots=\psi_{m}\circ\iota_{m}\;.

Example 1.2
.

Consider
K={\mathbb{C}}(x)
,
p=z^{2}-(x-1)\in K[z]
and

L\coloneqq K(\sqrt{x-1})\cong\frac{K[z]}{(p)}.

Then the inclusion
\iota\colon K\hookrightarrow L
is rationalizable.
Indeed consider the map
\psi\colon L\to K
defined as the identity on the elements of
{\mathbb{C}}
and by
x\mapsto x^{2}+1,z\mapsto x
.
With this definition, the map
\phi\coloneqq\psi\circ\iota\colon K\to K
sends
x-1
to
x^{2}
.

Example 1.3
.

Consider
K={\mathbb{C}}(x)
and set
L_{1}\coloneqq K(\sqrt{x}),L_{2}\coloneqq K(\sqrt{1-x})
.
Then the set of field extensions
\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
is rationalizable.
Indeed, for
i=1,2
, consider the homomorphisms

\psi_{i}\colon L_{i}\to K
defined as follows.

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\colon

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\sqrt{x}\hskip 11.0pt\mapsto\hskip 3.0pt\frac{2x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}

\displaystyle\psi_{2}\colon

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-x}\hskip 3.0pt\mapsto\hskip 3.0pt\frac{1-x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}

One can easily check that

\psi_{1}\circ\iota_{1}=\psi_{2}\circ\iota_{2}\eqqcolon\phi\;,

where
\phi\colon K\to K
is the homomorphism defined by

\phi\colon x\mapsto\left(\frac{2x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\right)^{2}\;.

Later we will see that the rationalizability of the set considered in this example does not depend on the special form of the polynomials but only on their degree, cf.
Section 4
.

Remark 1.4
.

The rationalizing map at the end of
[
1
, Remark 52]
is wrong.
The map
\sigma
should send
X
to
\frac{2X}{X^{2}+1}
.
Consequently, the composition
\sigma\circ\psi
should send
X
to
\left(\frac{2X}{X^{2}+1}\right)^{2}+1
.

Our main result is the following theorem, which shows that the rationalizability of a set of quadratic field extensions is equivalent to the rationalizability of the compositum of the field extensions.
Here and in the rest of the paper
\overline{K}
will always denote a fixed algebraic closure of a field
K
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
K
be a field

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be elements of
\overline{K}

such that all the extensions
L_{i}\coloneqq K(\alpha_{i})
are of degree two over
K
.
Then the set of extensions
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
is rationalizable if and only if
K\subset K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
is.

This allows us to develop a new strategy to prove rationalizability of sets of square roots of polynomials (cf.
Section 3.2
).

We proceed as follows.
In
Section 2
we recall the notion of rationalizability for square roots of polynomials and some well-known facts about field extensions.
In
Section 3

we prove
Theorem 1.5

and we outline a strategy to prove rationalizability of sets of square roots.
Finally, in
Section 4
we apply
Theorem 1.5
to some sets of square roots of polynomials.
The results thus obtained, joint with
[
1
, Proposition 47]
,
led us to formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.6
.

The set of square roots
\left\{\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{m}}\right\}
is rationalizable if and only if for every non-empty subset
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
the square root

\sqrt{\prod_{j\in J}f_{j}}

is rationalizable.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall the notion of rationalizability of square roots introduced in
[

]

2.1.
Rationalizability of square roots

Let
\mathbf{k}
be any field and consider the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
Q\coloneqq\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider
q\in Q
.
We say that the square root
\sqrt{q}
is
rationalizable
if there exists
\phi\colon Q\to Q
such that
\phi(q)
is a square in
Q
.

We say that a family of square roots
\{\sqrt{q_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{q_{m}}\}
is
rationalizable
if there exists
\phi\colon Q\to Q
such that
\phi(q_{i})
is a square in
Q
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

We recall the main theorems of
[
1
]
as they will serve as a starting point for the theory developed here.
The notation and statements are slightly changed to better fit the content of this article.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
1
, Theorem 1]
)
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

Given
q\in Q=\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, there exists a squarefree polynomial
f\in R=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
\sqrt{q}
is rationalizable if and only if
\sqrt{f}
is.

(2)

For a squarefree polynomial
f\in R
, the square root
\sqrt{f}
is rationalizable if and only if the variety
V(z^{2}-f)\subset\Spec R[z]={\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{\mathbf{k}}
is unirational.

(3)

Assume
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed and let
d
denote the degree of a squarefree polynomial
f\in R
.
If
d=1,2
, or if
\overline{V}
has a singular point of multiplicity
d-1
, then
\sqrt{f}
is rationalizable.

Corollary 2.3
.

Consider a squarefree polynomial
f\in\mathbf{k}[x]
.
Then the square root of
f
is rationalizable if and only if
V(z^{2}-f)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
has geometric genus zero.

2.2.
Rationalizability of roots of higher order

One can naturally generalize the concept of rationalizability of square roots to roots of higher order.
Although it is a very natural step, this subsection will not be needed in the rest of the paper and non-interested reader can safely skip it.
Also in this subsection, let
\mathbf{k}
be any field and
Q
the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Consider
q\in Q
of degree
d
and an integer
e\geq 2
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that the root
\sqrt[e]{q}
is
rationalizable
if there exists a homomorphism of
\mathbf{k}
-algebras
\phi\colon Q\to Q
such that
\phi(q)
is an
e
-power in
Q
, that is,
\phi(q)=h^{e}
, for some element
h\in Q
.
We say that a family of roots
\{\sqrt[e_{1}]{q_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt[e_{m}]{q_{m}}\}
is rationalizable if there exists a homomorphism of
\mathbf{k}
-algebras
\phi\colon Q\to Q
such that for every
i=1,\ldots,m
we have
\phi(q_{i})=h_{i}^{e_{i}}
for some
h_{i}\in Q

Working exactly as in
[
1
, §2]
, one can prove the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

Given
q\in Q=\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, there exists a polynomial
f\in R=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that its factorization does not contain any
e
-th power and
\sqrt[e]{q}
is rationalizable if and only if
\sqrt[e]{f}
is.

(2)

For
f\in R
whose factorization does not contain any
e
-th power, the root
\sqrt[e]{f}
is rationalizable if and only if the variety
V(z^{e}-f)\subset\Spec R[z]={\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{\mathbf{k}}
is unirational.

Remark 2.6
.

Consider
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
such that its factorization does not contain any
e
-th power.
Assume that the curve
C=V(z^{e}-f)
is irreducible.

Then following
[
5
, §2]
, we may assume that
C
is given by the equation

(1)

C\colon z^{e}=x^{\ell_{0}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-a_{i})^{\ell_{i}}

where
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\ell_{i}\equiv 0\bmod e
.

To see this, write
f=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-\alpha_{i})^{\ell_{i}}
and let
d=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that all
\alpha_{i}
are non-zero.
Consider the weighted homogenization of
z^{e}-f
with respect to
y
, where
\deg(z)=\lceil\frac{d}{e}\rceil
,
\deg(x)=1=\deg(y)
.
By dehomogenizing the new equation with respect to
x
, we get equation (
1
) with
a_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}
.

Moreover, we have again by
[
5
, §2]

that its geometric genus is

g(C)=\frac{(e-1)(s-2)}{2},

where

s=\begin{cases}m&\textrm{if }m\equiv 0\bmod e\;,\\
m+1&\textrm{otherwise}\;,\end{cases}

and
m
is defined as in equation (
1
).

In view of
Section 2.2
, this yields that
the root
\sqrt[e]{f}
is rationalizable if and only if
s=2
,
i.e.,
m=e=2
or
m=1
.

2.3.
Field theory

Before a further generalization of the notion of rationalizability,
we recall a few well-known facts about algebraic field extensions.
For more information and details see, for example,
[
2
, Chapter 3]
.

Definition 2.7
.

The field extension
K\subset L
is called
separable
if for every element
\alpha\in L
, the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
K
is separable, i.e., it has no repeated roots in any field extension of
K
.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
2
, Proposition 3.6.2]
)
.

If
K
is a field of characteristic
0
, then every algebraic extension is separable.

Proposition 2.9

(Primitive element theorem)
.

Let
K\subset L
be a separable field extension. Then there is an irreducible element
p\in K[z]
such that
L\cong K[z]/(p)
.

For a proof of this result we refer to
[
2
, Proposition 3.6.12]
.
In the examples in this article we will only consider fields of characteristic
0
and hence separable extensions.
Therefore, using the above proposition,
all the considered field extensions
K\subset L
will always be of the form
L=K[z]/(p)
with an irreducible polynomial
p
.

Remark 2.10
.

Notice that it is also possible to write
L=K(\alpha)
where
\alpha\in L
is a root of
p
(cf.
[
2
, Proposition 3.2.6]
). The element
\alpha
(or
p
) is called a
primitive element
of the extension
K\subset L
.

Example 2.11
.

Let
f\in K
be a squarefree element.
Then the polynomial
z^{2}-f\in K[z]
is irreducible and
L=K[z]/(z^{2}-f)
is a field extension of
K
.
We can also write
L=K(\sqrt{f})
, where
\sqrt{f}
denotes a square root of
f
.

3.1.
First properties

In this subsection we show that the rationalizability of a square root in the sense of
[
1
]
is indeed a special case of the rationalizability of a field extension.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider
f\in R=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
squarefree.
Then
\sqrt{f}
is rationalizable if and only if the field extension
Q\subset Q(\sqrt{f})\cong Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)
is rationalizable.

Note that
f
being squarefree implies the irreducibility of
z^{2}-f
,
assuring that
z^{2}-f
is the minimal polynomial of
\sqrt{f}
and hence that
Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)
is indeed isomorphic to
Q(\sqrt{f})
.

Proof.

Assume
\sqrt{f}
to be rationalizable: there is a
\phi\colon Q\to Q
with
\phi(f)=h^{2}
,
h\in Q
.
Then we define

\psi\colon Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)\to Q
by mapping
q\in Q
to
\psi(q)\coloneqq\phi(q)
and by setting
\psi(z)\coloneqq h
.
This is indeed a well-defined homomorphism, as
\psi(z^{2}-f)=(\psi(z))^{2}-\psi(f)=0
.
Since
\psi
is non-zero, we conclude that
Q\subset Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)
is rationalizable.

Conversely, assume
Q\subset Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)
to be rationalizable, so there is a non-zero homomorphism
\psi\colon Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)
.
Using the composition

\phi\coloneqq\psi\circ\iota\colon Q\hookrightarrow Q[z]/(z^{2}-f)\to Q\;,

we see that
\phi(f)=\psi(z)^{2}
is a square.
Hence the square root of
f
is rationalizable.
∎

The following lemma is a generalization of
[
1
, Lem. 2.7 & Cor. 2.8]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
L
be an extension of
Q
and write
L\cong Q[z]/(q)
for some irreducible
q\in Q[z]
.
Then there is an irreducible
p\in R[z]
such that
L\cong Q[z]/(p)
.

If
q
is monic, then
p
can also be chosen to be monic.

Proof.

As
R
is a unique factorization domain, there is a minimal
u\in R
such that
p\coloneqq u\cdot q\in R[z]
, by clearing denominators.
Moreover,
p
is irreducible in
R[z]
, as any non-trivial factorization of
p
would give rise to a factorization of
q
.
As
u
is a unit in
Q
, we get that
Q^{\prime}\cong Q[z]/(p)
.

In the case that
q
is monic, that is,
q=z^{n}+c_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{0}
, we get that
p=uq=uz^{n}+uc_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+uc_{0}
is not monic anymore.
By defining the monic
\tilde{p}=z^{n}+\tilde{c}_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+\tilde{c}_{0}
with
\tilde{c}_{i}=c_{i}\cdot u^{n-i}
, we get that
\tilde{p}(uz)=u^{n}p(z)
.
As
Q[z]/(\tilde{p})\to Q[z]/(p),z\mapsto uz
is an isomorphism, we find that
L\cong Q[z]/(\tilde{p})
with
\tilde{p}\in R[z]
monic.
∎

Note that the above proof uses only that
R
is a unique factorization domain.

We give a geometric interpretation of rationalizability of a field extension in the spirit of
[
1
, §2.2]
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
L
be an extension of
Q
.
Then there exists an irreducible polynomial
p\in R[z]

such that

Q\subset L
is rationalizable if and only if
V(p)\subset\Spec R[z]
is unirational.

Proof.

Recall that
\Spec R={\mathbb{A}}^{n}
is rational, as
Q=\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
.

From
Section 3.1
then there exists a polynomial
p\in R[z]
such that
L\cong Q[z]/(p)
.

If
Q\subset L\cong Q[z]/(p)
is rationalizable, we have an (injective) homomorphism
\psi\colon Q[z]/(p)\to Q
.
Note that
Q[z]/(p)=\Frac(R[z]/(p))=k(V(p))
, the function field of
V(p)
, so

\iota\circ\psi\colon\Frac(R[z]/(p))\hookrightarrow\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

implies that
V(p)
is unirational.

Conversely,
suppose that
V(p)
is unirational, that is, there is an injective homomorphism

k(V(p))=Q[z]/(p)=\Frac(R[z]/(p))\hookrightarrow\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\cong Q.

This shows that
Q\subset Q[z]/(p)
is rationalizable.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The two implications can be generalized by relaxing some hypotheses on
R
.

The first implication, i.e., that if

Q\subset Q[z]/(p)
is rationalizable then
V(p)
is unirational, holds also for any

such that
\Spec R
is unirational.

The converse implication holds also for an
R
such that
\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\hookrightarrow Q=\Frac(R)
.

In particular, the statement of
Section 3.1
is true for any
Q=\Frac(R)
where
R
is such that
\Spec R
is rational.

Lemma 3.10

Let

3.2.
The main theorem

Proposition 3.11

are constructed.
We need to define
\pi_{i+1}
.

Now consider
\alpha_{i+1}
. Let
p_{i+1}
and
r_{i+1}
be the minimal polynomials of
\alpha_{i+1}
over
K
and
M_{i}
, respectively.
Note that
r_{i+1}|p_{i+1}
and
[L_{i+1}:K]=\deg(p_{i+1})=2
.
So can distinguish two cases:

•

\deg(r_{i+1})=1
: in this case
M_{i}=M_{i+1}
, as already
\alpha_{i+1}\in M_{i}
. So we can define
\pi_{i+1}=\pi_{i}
;

•

\deg(r_{i+1})=2
: in this case we get that
r_{i+1}=p_{i+1}
. We define
\pi_{i+1}
by setting
\pi_{i+1}|_{M_{i}}\coloneqq\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{i+1}(\alpha_{i+1})\coloneqq\psi_{i+1}(\alpha_{i+1})
.

We are left to show that
\pi_{i+1}
is well defined in the second case. Here,
\alpha_{i+1}\not\in M_{i}
, but satisfies some quadratic relation
\alpha_{i+1}^{2}=u\alpha_{i+1}+t
with
u,t\in K
, as
r_{i+1}=p_{i+1}
.
Using this, and recalling that
\pi_{i}|_{K}=\psi_{i}|_{K}=\psi_{i+1}|_{K}=\phi
one can see that

\pi_{i+1}
is well defined.

By construction,
M_{m}=K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
.
Hence we get a homomorphism
\psi\coloneqq\pi_{m}\colon K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\to K
and the commutative diagram,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi=\pi_{m}}

i.e., the extension
K\subset K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
is rationalizable.
∎

The converse statement holds in greater generality.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
K
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be elements of
\overline{K}
;
let
L_{i}
be the extension of
K
obtained by adjoining
\alpha_{i}
,
that is,
L_{i}=K(\alpha_{i})
.
If
K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
is rationalizable, then so is the set
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
.

Proof.

Considering the compositions
\iota_{i}\colon L_{i}\hookrightarrow K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\to K
shows that each
L_{i}
is rationalizable.
∎

The following corollary is a direct application of
Theorem 1.5
to the study of rationalizability of sets of square roots, the most common in a physical context.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
be polynomials in
R=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
There exists a polynomial
p\in R[z]
such that the set of square roots

\{\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{m}}\}
is rationalizable if and only if the variety
V(p)
defined by
p
is unirational.

Proof.

Set
K=Q={\mathbb{C}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Then consider the quadratic field extensions
L_{i}\coloneqq K(\sqrt{f_{i}})\cong K[z]/(z^{2}-f_{i})
and the compositum field
L\coloneqq K(\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{m}})
.
By the primitive element theorem, we can write
L=K(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in\overline{K}
.
If
p
denotes the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
K
, then

L\cong K[z]/(p)
.
By
Section 3.1
, the rationalizability of
\{\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{m}}\}
is equivalent to the rationalizability of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
.
Note that all the extensions
K\subset L_{i}
are quadratic.
Then, by
Theorem 1.5
,
the rationalizability
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
is equivalent to the rationalizability of
K\subset L\cong K[z]/(p)
which, in turn, by
Section 3.1
, is equivalent to the unirationality of
V(p)
, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

Section 3.2
will prove very useful in the study of rationalizability of families of square roots and represents a more solid approach to the problem than the one given in
[
1
, Remark 52]
.
We remark that usually the polynomial
p
can be explicitly computed, even by hand.

On the other hand, this result is not very handy when it comes to
disprove
rationalizability of sets of square roots;
for this goal
[
1
, Proposition 47]
stays a more practical tool.

Looking at its proof and at
Section 3.1
, one can propose generalizations of
Theorem 1.5
.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
K
be a field and
let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be elements of
\overline{K}
.
For
i=1,\ldots,m
, set
M_{0}\coloneqq K,M_{i}\coloneqq K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i})
,
and
L_{i}\coloneqq K(\alpha_{i})
.
Assume one of the following statement.

(1)

For every
i=1,\ldots,m
, the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{i}
over
M_{i-1}
is either equal to the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{i}
over
K
or it has degree
1
(i.e.,
\alpha_{i}\in M_{i-1}
).

(2)

For every
i=1,\ldots,m
, the extension
M_{i-1}\subset M_{i}
is rationalizable.

Then the set
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
is rationalizable if and only if
K\subset M_{m}=K(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
is.

Proof.

If
K\subset M_{m}
is rationalizable, then so is
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
by
Section 3.2
.

Conversely, assume that
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
is rationalizable.
If (1) holds, then the proof goes exactly as in the proof of
Section 3.2
.
If (2) holds, then the statement follows directly from
Section 3.1
.
∎

In this section we use

we then apply these new results to some examples taken from
[

,
recovering the same conclusions with shorter arguments.

The computation of a primitive element of a field extension can be done by hand (if the degree is small enough) or with the aid of any computer algebra system.
For some of the computations we used the algebra software
Magma

[
3
]
and
Singular

[
4
]
, as they allow the computation of the geometric genus of a curve.

As before,
\mathbf{k}
denotes an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
and we define

R\coloneqq\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
Q\coloneqq\Frac(R)=\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
with
n\geq 1
.

Corollary 4.1
.

For any two linear polynomials
f,g\in Q
the field extension
Q\subset Q(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{g})
is rationalizable.

Proof.

By
Theorem 1.5

Q\subset Q(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{g})
is rationalizable if and only if the family of extensions
\{\frac{Q[z]}{(z^{2}-f)},\frac{Q[z]}{(z^{2}-g)}\}
is, which in turn,
by
Section 3.1
,
is equivalent to saying that the set of square roots
\{\sqrt{f},\sqrt{g}\}
is rationalizable.
So in order to prove the statement it is enough to prove that
\{\sqrt{f},\sqrt{g}\}
is rationalizable.
In order to do so we will use the strategy already explained in
[
1
, Remark 52]
.

First note that without loss of generality, we can always assume
f=x_{1}
.
This implies that
\sqrt{f}
is rationalized, for instance, by the map
\phi_{1}\colon Q\to Q
, sending
x_{1}\mapsto x_{1}^{2}
and acting as the identity on all the other variables.
In this way we obtain

\phi_{1}(f)=x^{2}
and
\phi_{1}(g)\eqqcolon g^{\prime}
is a polynomial of degree
2
.
Consider the square root
\sqrt{g^{\prime}}
and notice that it is rationalizable (cf.
[
1
, Theorem 1]
).
Let
\phi_{2}\colon Q\to Q
be the rationalizing homomorphism
then we have
\phi_{2}(g^{\prime})=h^{2}
for some
h\in Q
.
Consider then the composition
\phi\coloneqq\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}\colon Q\to Q
and note that
\phi(f)=(\phi_{2}(x))^{2}
and
\phi(g)=h^{2}
, proving that
\{\sqrt{f},\sqrt{g}\}
is rationalizable.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{C}}
be pairwise distinct complex numbers.
The set
{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{\sqrt{x-a_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{x-a_{m}}\}
is rationalizable if and only if
m\leq 2
.

Proof.

If
m=1
, then
{\mathcal{A}}
is trivially rationalizable (cf.
[
1
, Theorem 1]
).

If
m=2
, then
Section 4
implies that
{\mathcal{A}}
is rationalizable.

If
m\geq 3
, then the polynomial
p\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-a_{i})
is of degree
m\geq 3
with
m
distinct roots.
It follows that the square root
\sqrt{p}
is not rationalizable (cf.
[
1
, Theorem 2]
) and hence, in turn,
{\mathcal{A}}
is not rationalizable by
[
1
, Proposition 47]
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{C}}
three pairwise distinct complex numbers.
Then the set

{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{\sqrt{(x-a)(x-b)},\sqrt{(x-a)(x-c)},\sqrt{(x-b)(x-c)}\}

is rationalizable.

Proof.

By applying an affine transformation of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
we can assume without loss of generality that
a=0
and
b=1
and write
c=\lambda
.
Set

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x-a)(x-b)=x(x-1),

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x-a)(x-c)=x(x-\lambda),

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x-b)(x-c)=(x-1)(x-\lambda),

and, for
i=1,2,3
, consider the field extension

L_{i}\coloneqq\frac{{\mathbb{C}}(x)[z]}{(z^{2}-f_{i})}\cong{\mathbb{C}}(x)(\sqrt{f_{i}}).

Then the rationalizability of
{\mathcal{A}}
is equivalent to the rationalizability of the set
\{L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\}
.
From
Theorem 1.5
this is equivalent to the rationalizability of the compositum field
L={\mathbb{C}}(x)(\sqrt{f_{1}},\sqrt{f_{2}},\sqrt{f_{3}})
.

First notice that, as

\sqrt{f_{3}}=\sqrt{(x-1)(x-\lambda)}=\frac{1}{x}\sqrt{x(x-1)}\sqrt{x(x-\lambda)}=\frac{1}{x}\sqrt{f_{1}}\sqrt{f_{2}},

the compositum field
L
is equal to just
{\mathbb{C}}(x)(\sqrt{f_{1}},\sqrt{f_{2}})
.
It is then immediate to see that
[L:{\mathbb{C}}(x)]=4
.
Let
p\in{\mathbb{C}}(x)[z]
be the irreducible polynomial of the primitive element of
L
over
{\mathbb{C}}(x)
, that is,

p
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
4
such that

L\cong\frac{{\mathbb{C}}(x)[z]}{(p)}.

Notice that in general, by possibly multiplying by an element of
{\mathbb{C}}(x)
, we can always assume that
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[x,z]
.
In particular, in our case

p=z^{4}+(-4x+2\lambda+2)xz^{2}+(\lambda^{2}-2\lambda+1)x^{2}.

From
Section 3.1
we know that
L
is rationalizable if and only if
V(p)
is unirational.
The variety
V(p)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
is a curve and
its genus is
0
, proving the statement.
∎

With the same argument used to prove
Section 4
one can easily prove also the following result.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
f
be a linear polynomial in
{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)\setminus\{x,y\}
.
Then the following set is rationalizable.

{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{\sqrt{xy},\sqrt{xf},\sqrt{yf}\}

Proof.

Notice that without loss of generality we may (and do) assume that

f=x+ay+b
with
a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Using
Theorem 1.5
we know that
{\mathcal{A}}
is rationalizable if and only if the extension
L\coloneqq{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)(\sqrt{xy},\sqrt{xf},\sqrt{yf})\supset{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)
is.
Proceeding as in the proof of
Section 4
, one can show that

L\cong\frac{{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)[z]}{(p)},

with

\displaystyle p\,=\,

\displaystyle x^{4}+2(a-1)x^{3}y+2bx^{3}+(a-1)^{2}x^{2}y^{2}+2b(a-1)x^{2}y+

\displaystyle-2x^{2}z^{2}+b^{2}x^{2}-2(a+1)xyz^{2}-2bxz^{2}+z^{4}\;.

From
Section 3.1
we know that
L
is rationalizable if and only if
S=V(p)
is rational.
Let
\overline{S}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}

One can easily verify that
\overline{S}
is a quartic surface with a singular locus given by the union of two lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
taken with multiplicity

the two lines intersect in one point.
Using Urabe’s classification of non-normal quartic surfaces (cf.
[
6

∎

Another question comes from

If
