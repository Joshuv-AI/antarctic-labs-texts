Abstract
. We apply both the theory of boundary triples and perturbation theory to the setting of semi-bounded Sturm–Liouville operators with two limit-circle endpoints. For general boundary conditions we obtain refined and new results about their eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. In the boundary triple setup, we obtain simple criteria for identifying which self-adjoint extensions possess double eigenvalues when the parameter is a matrix. We also identify further spectral properties of the Friedrichs extension and (when the operator is positive) the von Neumann–Krein extension. Motivated by some recent scalar Aronszajn–Donoghue type results, we find that real numbers can only be eigenvalues for two extensions of Sturm–Liouville operator when the boundary conditions are restricted to corresponding to affine lines in the space from which the perturbation parameter is taken. Furthermore, we determine much of the spectral representation of those Sturm–Liouville operators that can be reached by perturbation theory.

1.
Introduction

Spectral properties of Sturm–Liouville operators have a rich and long history of study, see e.g.
[
21
,
24
,
28
,
30
,
31
,
38
,
50
,
52
]
. In this manuscript, we consider Sturm–Liouville differential expression with two limit-circle endpoints and with minimal realization that is semi-bounded (see Definition
2.6
). Without loss of generality, we restrict our attention to operators that are (semi-)bounded from below.

The limit-circle endpoints determine that the deficiency indices are
(2,2)
, and hence two boundary conditions are necessary to form self-adjoint extensions. They also constitute more singular versions of regular endpoints, which have an easy setup for boundary value problems but do not cover some popular and important expressions, i.e. the classical Jacobi differential expression. Niessen and Zettl
[
48
]
(see also
[
52
, Theorem 10.6.5]
) showed that Sturm–Liouville expressions with (finite) limit-circle endpoints can be ‘regularized’ via a simple transformation and then their spectral properties can be analyzed as if the endpoints were regular. Two limit-circle endpoints also produce purely discrete spectrum (see e.g.
[
52
, Proposition 10.4.3]
), which simplifies some calculations. All of the results herein can be easily applied to regular endpoints — regular endpoints are in the limit-circle case — so we choose to use the more general limit-circle endpoints because their theory has more subtleties.

We consider operators for which the minimal symmetric operator is semi-bounded (with bound
K
), which immediately implies that the limit-circle endpoints are non-oscillating (solutions do not have zeros which accumulate at endpoints). This allows the use of principal and non-principal solutions first developed by Rellich
[
49
]
and Kalf
[
36
]
. In turn, this fixes the forms of quasi-derivatives and the necessary form of the boundary triple that we will use. Furthermore, it is possible to identify important Friedrichs extension
[
46
,
48
]
and the von Neumann–Krein extension when the operator is positive.

Herein, Sturm–Liouville operators that fall under these restrictions are analyzed from two different, but complementary, perspectives: boundary triples and perturbation theory. It should immediately be noted that determining properties for such a wide class of operators is difficult and, to the best knowledge of the authors, the theory has remained in largely the same state since the impactful work of Bailey, Everitt and Zettl
[
7
]
in the mid 90’s (see also
[
52
, Section 10.6]
); some of these results are included in Section
2
for the convenience of the reader. Therein, criteria for eigenvalues of self-adjoint extensions were presented and, in particular, necessary and sufficient conditions for an eigenvalue to have multiplicity two. Our main result, Theorem
3.11
, contains a condition that ensures simplicity of all eigenvalues of a self-adjoint extension; this condition is independent of the location of the eigenvalue of interest. We then proceed to present a whole class of boundary conditions for which all corresponding operators have simple spectrum in Corollary
3.12
, also see Remark
3.13
.

Boundary triples
[
8
,
10
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
32
,
34
]
consist of two maps
\Gamma_{0}
,
\Gamma_{1}
, which are defined by quasi-derivatives here, and a boundary space,
\mathbb{C}^{2}
in this context. They have been previously applied to such Sturm–Liouville operators, most notably in
[
8
, Chapter 6]
, but also to examples in
[
4
,
11
,
25
]
. Upon this foundation, Section
3
builds a core of simple facts around the spectral theory of the two natural self-adjoint extensions
{\bf L}_{0}
and
{\bf L}_{\infty}
(the Friedrichs extension), whose domains are defined by the kernels
\ker(\Gamma_{0})
and
\ker(\Gamma_{1})
, respectively. In particular, both are found to have simple spectra, a fact that is a direct consequence of the boundary triple construction but does not seem to be easily found in the literature.

Other self-adjoint extensions are in one-to-one correspondence with self-adjoint linear relations
\theta
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and the rest of Section
3
focuses on clarifying how and why double eigenvalues arise in these extensions. Subsection
3.1
assumes that
\theta
is a Hermitian matrix (or, equivalently, that
\dim\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}=0
) and finds explicit degeneracy conditions similar to those of
[
7
]
that create double eigenvalues within union of resolvent sets
\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
. The authors expect that this union is often all real numbers
{\lambda}\geq K
.

The real advantage of this approach is that the conditions on
\theta
are strict enough that it gives some basic criteria to tell whether a given
\theta
will produce double eigenvalues or not, with no additional information about solutions. To elaborate, the conditions of Theorem
3.11
and
[
7
, Theorem 3]
(Theorem
2.8
here) both involve setting the parameter
\theta
to take specific values of quasi-derivatives of solutions. However, in practice, if one is given a fixed
\theta
, it can be very difficult to check that it does not meet these conditions for any value of
{\lambda}
(which would then be a double eigenvalue). A consequence of Theorem
3.11
is that only real, invertible matrices with non-zero off-diagonal entries may achieve a double eigenvalue. If the given matrix
\theta
does not meet this, the spectrum is simple.

Herein lies one of the main advantages of boundary triples, it offers additional levels of distinction between parameters and many conditions can be refined. One disadvantage is that we are often restricted to
\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
, which is where the two natural parametrizations are valid, and will sometimes have to deal with
\theta
that is a self-adjoint linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and not a matrix. This case is analyzed in Subsection
3.2
. Essentially, such linear relations
\theta
with
\dim\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}=1
(the case
\dim\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}=2
simply refers to
{\bf L}_{\infty}
) can create double eigenvalues, but they can also be achieved by a suitable matrix
\theta
. The conditions on this suitable matrix
\theta
can be difficult to write explicitly, but we conclude that analyzing matrix
\theta
is sufficient to determine the full range of spectral properties exhibited by all self-adjoint extensions collectively.

Perturbation theory has always been intimately related with Sturm–Liouville theory, see e.g.
[
27
,
50
]
; the original works of Aronszajn
[
5
]
and Donoghue
[
18
]
were both inspired by connections to Sturm–Liouville theory. However, there have also been many recent advances in perturbation theory (see e.g.
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
43
,
44
]
), some of which we now apply to Sturm–Liouville operators. First, perturbation theory was not easily applicable to Sturm–Liouville operators with limit-circle endpoints due to the singular behavior causing the additive perturbation to not be well-defined. This was rectified in
[
11
]
by applying a boundary pair/triple construction to the singular perturbation. Perturbation theoretic techniques were then applied to self-adjoint extensions of the classical Jacobi expression to illustrate new types of possible results with the rank-two perturbation setup, see also
[
26
]
. Extension theory for powers of the derivative and the corresponding matrix-valued spectral theory can be found in
[
12
]
using techniques indicated in
[
3
]
.

Second, the Aronzsajn–Donoghue Theorem, see e.g.
[
51
]
, says that for distinct perturbation parameters the spectral measures of cyclic rank one perturbations by a cyclic vector are mutually singular in the measure theoretic sense. Simple matrix examples, reveal that such mutual singularity does not hold for the scalar (traces of the matrix-valued) spectral measures of higher rank perturbations. However, an appropriate interpretation using the so-called vector mutual singularity of the matrix-valued spectral measures allows for an analogue to the Aronzsajn–Donoghue Theorem for higher rank perturbations, see
[
42
]
. In Section
4
we discuss some applications and results related to Sturm–Liouville operators of several further Aronszajn–Donoghue type theorems such as
[
45
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
42
, Theorem 7.3]
. Motivated by this literature, we are particularly interested in perturbation parameters of the form
\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta
with
\widetilde{\vartheta},\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}.
For such parameter families, we also obtain a multiplicity result.

An important distinction between the boundary triples and perturbation theory approaches is that they start from ‘opposite’ self-adjoint extensions:
{\bf L}_{0}
is defined by the
\ker(\Gamma_{0})
and therefore is natural for boundary triples, while
{\bf L}_{\infty}
has the largest form domain and is necessarily the unperturbed operator for perturbation theory. However, boundary triples can be easily reparameterized to ‘start’ with
{\bf L}_{\infty}
and, indeed, it is necessary to use both parametrizations to obtain spectral information in some cases, see Remark
3.4
for more details.

Finally, in Section
5
we obtain an expression for the matrix-valued spectral information of the Krein–von Neumann extension (when the operator is positive) of a Sturm–Liouville operator as well as information on the spectral representation for all extensions. The classical Jacobi expression was analyzed in a similar way in
[
11
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

Consider the classical Sturm–Liouville differential equation

(2.1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[p(x)\dfrac{df}{dx}(x)\right]+q(x)f(x)=-\lambda w(x)f(x),

where
p(x),w(x)>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
and
q(x)
real-valued a.e. on
(a,b)
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.
Furthermore,
1/p(x),q(x),w(x)\in L^{1}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm loc}}[(a,b),dx]
. Sturm–Liouville operators are extremely well-studied and there is a vast amount of literature covering their theory and applications; the authors find the book of Zettl
[
52
]
, in particular, very useful and recommend the reader look there to find more information. The sources
[
1
,
6
,
23
,
30
]
may also be useful.

The differential expression can be viewed as a linear operator, mapping a function
f
to the function
\ell[f]
via

(2.2)

\displaystyle\ell[f](x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\dfrac{1}{w(x)}\left(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[p(x)\dfrac{df}{dx}(x)\right]+q(x)f(x)\right).

This unbounded operator acts on the Hilbert space
L^{2}[(a,b),w]
, endowed with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\overline{g(x)}w(x)dx.

In this setting, the eigenvalue problem
\ell[f](x)=\lambda f(x)
can be considered. However, the operator acting via
\ell[\,\cdot\,]
on
L^{2}[(a,b),w]
is not self-adjoint a priori. Additional boundary conditions are required to ensure this property.

Definition 2.1

(variation of
[
47
, Section 14.2]
)
.

For a symmetric, closed operator
{\bf A}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, define
the
positive defect space
and the
negative defect space
, respectively, by

\mathcal{D}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{D}({\bf A}^{*})~\mathchar 58\relax~{\bf A}^{*}f=if\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{D}_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{D}({\bf A}^{*})~\mathchar 58\relax~{\bf A}^{*}f=-if\right\}.

Note that the self-adjoint extensions of a symmetric operator coincide with those of the closure of the symmetric operator
[
19
, Theorem XII.4.8]
, so without loss of generality we assume that all considered operators are closed.

The dimensions dim
(\mathcal{D}_{+})=m_{+}
and dim
(\mathcal{D}_{-})=m_{-}
, called the
positive
and
negative deficiency indices of
{\bf A}
respectively, will play an important role. They are usually conveyed as the pair
(m_{+},m_{-})
.
The deficiency indices of
T
correspond to how “far” from self-adjoint
{\bf A}
is. A symmetric operator
{\bf A}
has self-adjoint extensions if and only if its deficiency indices are equal
[
47
, Section 14.8.8]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
47
, Theorem 14.4.4]
)
.

If
{\bf A}
is a closed, symmetric operator, then the subspaces
\mathcal{D}({\bf A})
,
\mathcal{D}_{+}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{-}
are linearly independent and their direct sum coincides with
\mathcal{D}({\bf A}^{*})
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}({\bf A}^{*})=\mathcal{D}({\bf A})\dotplus\mathcal{D}_{+}\dotplus\mathcal{D}_{-}.

(Here, subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}_{p}
are said to be
linearly independent
, if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}=0
for
x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
implies that all

We now let
\ell[\,\cdot\,]
be a Sturm–Liouville differential expression in order to introduce more specific definitions. It is important to reiterate that the analysis of self-adjoint extensions does not involve changing the differential expression associated with the operator at all, merely the domain of definition by applying boundary conditions.

Definition 2.3

(
[
47
, Section 17.2]
)
.

The
maximal domain
of
\ell[\,\cdot\,]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}=\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}(\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\mathchar 58\relax(a,b)\to\mathbb{C}~\mathchar 58\relax~f,pf^{\prime}\in\text{AC}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm loc}}(a,b);f,\ell[f]\in L^{2}[(a,b),w]\right\}.

The designation of “maximal” is appropriate in this case because
\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}(\ell)
is the largest possible subspace that
\ell
maps back into
L^{2}[(a,b),w]
. For
f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}(\ell)
and
a<{\alpha}\leq\beta<b
the

associated with

is defined by

(2.3)

[f,g]\bigg|_{{\alpha}}^{\beta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{{\alpha}}^{\beta}\left\{\ell[f(x)]\overline{g(x)}-\ell[\overline{g(x)}]f(x)\right\}w(x)dx.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
47
, Section 17.2]
)
.

The limits
[f,g](b)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{x\to b^{-}}[f,g](x)
and
[f,g](a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{x\to a^{+}}[f,g](x)

The equation (

for

, and in the case of Sturm–Liouville operators it can be explicitly computed using integration by parts to be the modified Wronskian

(2.4)

\displaystyle[f,g]\bigg|_{a}^{b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p(x)[f^{\prime}(x)g(x)-f(x)g^{\prime}(x)]\bigg|_{a}^{b}.

Definition 2.5

(
[
47
, Section 17.2]
)
.

The
minimal domain
of
\ell[\,\cdot\,]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}=\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}(\ell)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}(\ell)~\mathchar 58\relax~[f,g]\big|_{a}^{b}=0~~\forall g\in\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}(\ell)\right\}.

The maximal and minimal operators associated with the expression
\ell[\,\cdot\,]
are then defined as
{\bf L}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}=\{\ell,\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}\}
and

.

Definition 2.6
.

A symmetric operator
{\bf A}
is called

\displaystyle\langle{\bf A}f,f\rangle\geq K\langle f,f\rangle.

A Sturm–Liouville expression

[

We now present a few selected results that can serve as a comparison to the results found in the manuscript. Unfortunately, the notation naturally induced by boundary triples and perturbation theory is very different, so we will introduce this in detail in Section
3
. Sturm–Liouville literature, when there are two limit-circle endpoints, generally deal with boundary conditions that fall into two disjoint classes: separated and coupled.

Let boundary condition bases (these are functions in the maximal domain that are linearly independent modulo the minimal domain and normalized within the sesquilinear form) be given by

at the endpoint

and

at the endpoint

. Then, separated conditions, for functions

\displaystyle A_{1}[y,f](a)+A_{2}[y,g](a)=0\quad A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\quad(A_{1},A_{2})\neq(0,0),

\displaystyle B_{1}[y,h](b)+B_{2}[y,k](b)=0\quad B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\quad(B_{1},B_{2})\neq(0,0).

have the canonical representation

\displaystyle\cos(\al)[y,f](a)-\sin(\al)[y,g](a)=0,\quad 0\leq{\alpha}<\pi,

\displaystyle\cos(\beta)[y,h](b)-\sin(\beta)[y,k](b)=0,\quad 0<\beta\leq\pi.

The canonical representation for coupled boundary conditions require

\displaystyle-\pi<\alpha\leq\pi,\quad R=(r_{ij}),\quad(r_{ij})\in\mathbb{R},\quad\det(R)=1,

and are written

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}[y,h](b)\\
[y,k](b)\end{pmatrix}=e^{i\alpha}R\begin{pmatrix}[y,f](a)\\
[y,g](a)\end{pmatrix}.

Now, if

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}[u_{1},f](a)&[u_{2},f](a)\\
[u_{1},g](a)&[u_{2},g](a)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

we can define the discriminant

(2.6)

\displaystyle D(R,{\lambda})=r_{11}[u_{2},k](b)+r_{22}[u_{1},h](b)-r_{12}[u_{2},h](b)-r_{21}[u_{1},k](b).

This leads to the following theorem.

(
[
7

[

For any

2.1.
Boundary Triples

The main tool used for calculating the Weyl

[

]

Definition 2.10
.

[
8
]

Let

Definition 2.11
.

[

Let

\displaystyle\Gamma\widehat{f}=\{\Gamma_{0}\widehat{f},\Gamma_{1}\widehat{f}\},~~~~\widehat{f}=\{f,f^{\prime}\}\in S^{*},

\displaystyle\langle f^{\prime},g\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}-\langle f,g^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\langle\Gamma_{0}\widehat{f},\Gamma_{1}\widehat{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{G}}-\langle\Gamma_{1}\widehat{f},\Gamma_{0}\widehat{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{G}},

The eigenspace of a closed symmetric relation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{{\lambda}}(S^{*})=\ker(S^{*}-{\lambda})~~\text{ and we define }~~\widehat{\mathfrak{N}}_{{\lambda}}(S^{*})=\left\{\{f_{\lambda},{\lambda}f_{{\lambda}}\}~\mathchar 58\relax~f_{{\lambda}}\in\mathfrak{N}_{{\lambda}}(S^{*})\right\}.

Definition 2.12

[

b
are not regular singular points is unclear. The special case where
u_{2}(f(x),{\lambda})
is a solution to
(\ell-{\lambda})f=0
seems to be an outlier. Still, the fact that eigenvalues of extensions are embedded into such connection formulas for solutions is intriguing.

Finally, the multiplicity of
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})
depending on the rank of the Weyl-
m
function for the transversal extension
{\bf L}_{\infty}
is extremely convenient, as this is the only other extension which is very accessible. The interdependence of these extensions seems to be rarely mentioned in the literature for limit-circle endpoints but is of key importance for inverse spectral theory when there are regular endpoints, see e.g.
[
14
,
20
,
22
,
33
]
. Determining the multiplicity of eigenvalues for extensions other than these two will prove to be more difficult.

It is possible to prove an analogous proposition concerning the eigenvalues of the important transversal extension
{\bf L}_{\infty}
. In the case where
\ell
is bounded from below,
{\bf L}_{\infty}
is known as the Friedrichs extension, see i.e.
[

]
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
{\bf L}_{\infty}
be as above with domain given by equation (
3.10
) and
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{\lambda}\in\sigma_{d}({\bf L}_{\infty})=\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
.

(ii)

We have
u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})=0
and hence
u_{1}(x,{\lambda})\in\dom({\bf L}_{\infty})
.

(iii)

We have

\displaystyle\langle u_{1}(x,{\lambda}),\widetilde{u}(x,{\lambda}_{0})\rangle=0\text{ in }L^{2}[(a,b),wdx].

and hence
u_{1}(x,{\lambda})
is orthogonal to
\widetilde{u}(x,{\lambda}_{0})
for such
{\lambda}
, or
{\lambda}={\lambda}_{0}
.

(iv)

If
u_{b}(x,{\lambda}_{0})
does not vanish on
(d,b)
for some
a<d<b
and
\beta
is such that
d<\beta<b
, then

\displaystyle u_{1}^{[1]}(\beta,{\lambda})=({\lambda}-{\lambda}_{0})\int_{\beta}^{b}u_{1}(x^{\prime},{\lambda})u_{b}(x^{\prime},{\lambda}_{0})w(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

(v)

If either both
a
and
b
are regular singular points of
\ell[\cdot]
or there exists a Möbius transformation
f(x)\mathchar 58\relax(a,b)\to(a,b)
such that
f(a)=b
and
u_{2}(f(x),{\lambda})
is also a solution to
(\ell-{\lambda})y=0
, then

\displaystyle u_{1}(f(x),{\lambda})=u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})u_{1}(a,{\lambda}).

Furthermore, each
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
has multiplicity one.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the proof of Proposition
3.6
. Note that to show the multiplicity of
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
is one it is necessary to analyze
M_{0}({\lambda})
.
∎

It is possible for
\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cap\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})\neq\varnothing
. For
{\lambda}
in this intersection, Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
imply that
u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})=u_{2}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})=0
. However, this does not necessarily mean that there exists a self-adjoint extension
{\bf L}(\theta_{0})
, with
\theta_{0}\notin\{0\}\cup\{\infty\}
, that has
{\lambda}
as an eigenvalue, or the multiplicity of this eigenvalue. However, some basic criterion is available.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cup\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
. Then
u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})=1.

Proof.

The fundamental solutions are normalized within the Wronskian by definition and satisfy
[u_{1},u_{2}](b)=1
for all
{\lambda}
. If
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cup\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty}),
then either
u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})
or
u_{2}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})
is 0, so we calculate

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=[u_{1}(x,{\lambda}),u_{2}(x,{\lambda})](b)=u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})-u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})u_{2}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}),

and the result follows.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

For every
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
there exists
\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{R}
with
\sigma({\bf L}(\theta_{0}))\cap\sigma({\bf L}(\theta_{1}))=\varnothing.

The proof of this proposition is deferred until after Remark
4.4
, as notation from Section

is essential.

3.1.
Spectral properties for other self-adjoint extensions

This knowledge of the spectral properties of the self-adjoint extensions
{\bf L}_{0}
and
{\bf L}_{\infty}
now allows us to pass to other general self-adjoint extensions. To this end, let
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
and write

\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\theta_{12}\\
\theta_{21}&\theta_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Plugging into equation (
3.11
) yields

(3.17)

\displaystyle M(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\theta-M_{0}(\lambda))^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}+\frac{u_{1}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}&\theta_{12}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}\\
\theta_{21}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}&\theta_{22}+\frac{u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda)}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

A general analog of equation (
3.11
) can also be written for the case where
\theta\in\mathcal{R}
(see
[
8
, Equation 3.8.4]
) which relies on the decomposition from equation (
2.8
):

(3.18)

\displaystyle M(\theta,{\lambda})=\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}+\mathcal{B}^{*}M_{0}({\lambda})\right)\left(\mathcal{B}^{*}-\mathcal{A}^{*}M_{0}({\lambda})\right)^{-1}.

To further discuss the eigenvalues coming from different choices of
\theta
, we recall that we abbreviate the classes of
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices as
\mathcal{M}
and of self-adjoint linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
as
\mathcal{R}
so that
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{R}
. Our analysis of the eigenvalues of
M(\theta)
begins in the special case where
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
. Also recall that
\vartheta=-\theta^{-1}
, which naturally arises from Remark
3.4
, and that the inverse of a linear relation always exists. These parametrizations are made without loss of generality; a general self-adjoint extension of
{\bf L}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}
can always be transcribed as
{\bf L}(\theta)
for some
\theta\in\mathcal{R}
, and then
\vartheta
can be calculated to yield
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
. In this subsection, we are mainly concerned with the case
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
and, in particular, eigenvalues of multiplicity two. A discussion of how eigenvalues of multiplicity two can arise when
\theta\in\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{M}
is postponed to Subsection
3.2
.

Definition 3.10
.

An eigenvalue
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
of a self-adjoint extension
{\bf L}(\theta)
(or
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
) will be said to be
degenerate
if it satisfies one of the following conditions:

(i)

If
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
,
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
and

\displaystyle\theta_{11}=-\frac{u_{1}^{[0]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})},\quad\theta_{21}=\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})},\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{22}=-\frac{u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}.

(ii)

If
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
,
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
and

\displaystyle\vartheta_{11}=\frac{u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}{u_{1}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})},\quad\vartheta_{21}=\frac{1}{u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}\quad\text{and}\quad\vartheta_{22}=\frac{u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}{u_{1}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}.

(iii)

If
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cap\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
and
{\lambda}
has multiplicity two for
{\bf L}(\theta)
(or
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
).

In Cases
(i)
and
(ii)
, each entry of
\theta
and
\vartheta
is well-defined because
u_{2}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\neq 0
and
u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\neq 0
, respectively.

Degenerate eigenvalues thus correspond to the case where
\theta-M_{0}
(or
\vartheta-M_{\infty}
) is the zero matrix in equation (
3.17
). An operator
{\bf L}(\theta)
will be called non-degenerate if all of its eigenvalues are non-degenerate. Also recall that
K\in\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be the lower bound of the symmetric operator
{\bf L}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}
via Definition
2.6
.

Theorem 3.11
.

If
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}
is a degenerate eigenvalue of
{\bf L}(\theta)
or
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
, then
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
has multiplicity two. Conversely, if
K\leq{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
and
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
or
K\leq{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
and
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
has multiplicity two, then
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
is degenerate.

Proof.

Assume that
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
is a degenerate eigenvalue of
{\bf L}(\theta)
. For
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
the above observation that degenerate eigenvalues correspond to the case where
\theta-M_{0}
yields the zero matrix and Proposition
3.5
immediately imply that
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
must be an eigenvalue of multiplicity two. For
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})
but
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
, the same argument holds except
\vartheta-M_{\infty}
yields the zero matrix. For
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cap\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
,
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
has multiplicity two by the definition of degeneracy.

The proof of the converse implication will be shown only for the case where
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
. The case where
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})
but
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
follows by a similar argument and the case where
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cap\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
is immediate.

Let
\theta
be a Hermitian
2\times 2
matrix and
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
be an eigenvalue of
{\bf L}(\theta)
with multiplicity two. If we omit the dependence of our solutions on
x=b
and of
{\lambda}
on
\theta
momentarily, then equation (
3.17
) easily simplifies as

\displaystyle M(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\theta-M_{0}(\lambda))^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}+\frac{u_{1}^{[0]}(\lambda)}{u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)}&\theta_{12}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)}\\
\theta_{21}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)}&\theta_{22}+\frac{u_{2}^{[1]}(\lambda)}{u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=K({\lambda})L({\lambda}),

where

\displaystyle K({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)}{\left(\theta_{11}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{1}^{[0]}(\lambda)\right)\left(\theta_{22}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{2}^{[1]}(\lambda)\right)-\left(\theta_{12}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)-1\right)\left(\theta_{21}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)-1\right)},

and

\displaystyle L({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{22}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{2}^{[1]}(\lambda)&1-\theta_{12}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)\\
1-\theta_{21}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)&\theta_{11}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{1}^{[0]}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}.

The operator
{\bf L}(\theta)
has eigenvalues at
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
that are poles of
M(\theta)
. Recall that the expressions
u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)
,
u_{2}^{[1]}(\lambda)
and
u_{1}^{[0]}({\lambda})
are all finite and well-defined for all values of
{\lambda}
so poles of
M(\theta)
must occur when the denominator of
K({\lambda})
vanishes:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\left(\theta_{11}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{1}^{[0]}(\lambda)\right)\left(\theta_{22}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)+u_{2}^{[1]}(\lambda)\right)-\left(\theta_{12}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)-1\right)\left(\theta_{21}u_{2}^{[0]}(\lambda)-1\right)=0.

Equation (
3.19
), which is clearly just
\det(L(\la))
, must therefore be satisfied by
\lambda_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
. Hence, the rank of
\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
is at most one. The only way that
\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
can have nullity
2
now is if
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
is degenerate, as the rows of
L({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
are linearly dependent.
∎

The characterization of double eigenvalues within
\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
as degenerate allows for some simple consequences to be established.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
K\leq{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
. Then
{\lambda}
is a double eigenvalue for a single self-adjoint extension
{\bf L}(\theta)
where
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
. Likewise,
{\lambda}
is a double eigenvalue for a single self-adjoint extension
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
where
\vartheta=-\theta^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}
. In particular,
\theta
and
\vartheta
must be real, have non-zero off-diagonal entries and be invertible.

Proof.

Theorem
3.11
says that eigenvalues of multiplicity two where
\theta,\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
are exactly those whose parameters meet the degeneracy conditions of Definition
3.10
. Such matrices
\theta
and
\vartheta
are well-defined as long as
K\leq{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
and created by simply plugging in
{\lambda}
. Clearly, such a matrix
\theta
or
\vartheta
can correspond to only one value of
{\lambda}
. These matrices must be real by Theorem
2.9
, have non-zero off-diagonal entries and are invertible for
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
by the normalization of the fundamental solutions in the Wronskian.
∎

The Corollary also implies that diagonal matrices cannot produce double eigenvalues, at least for
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
. Such matrices correspond to canonical separated boundary conditions, and are well-known to have multiplicity one, see e.g.
[
52
, Theorem 10.6.2]
for a modern source where there is no restriction on
{\lambda}
.

Later on, in Proposition
4.12
, we analyze domains of the form

\displaystyle\{f\in\dom\mathcal{D}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}~\mathchar 58\relax~t\zeta f^{[0]}(a)=f^{[1]}(a),~t\eta f^{[0]}(b)=f^{[1]}(b)\},

for fixed
\eta,\zeta
, and show for which
t
a value
\lambda\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
may be an eigenvalue.

Note that Theorem
3.11
is similar to Theorem
2.8
, which states four requirements for an eigenvalue of a Sturm–Liouville operator with canonical coupled boundary conditions to have multiplicity two, and has no restrictions on
{\lambda}
. However, the approach of Theorem
3.11
via boundary triples still has several advantages.

Remark 3.13
.

Most importantly, if one is given a canonical coupled boundary condition (which cannot be separated), when using classical methods, it is unclear whether the self-adjoint extension will have any double eigenvalues because explicit values of
u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda})
and
u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda})
are usually hard to calculate. The reduction to checking three such values (this is just due to starting with a Hermitian matrix) instead of four is therefore useful but is not a significant step. There is some guidance on whether the ground state (i.e. first/lowest eigenvalue) is simple or not via
[
52
, Theorem 10.6.3]
and whether the extension in real or not via Theorem
2.9
but that is as far as the literature seems to go. The boundary triple approach above is able to offer some basic criteria: if one is given a matrix corresponding to a self-adjoint extension then it must be real, invertible and have non-zero off-diagonal entries to produce an eigenvalue
K\leq{\lambda}
of multiplicity two within
\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
.

The use of parameters stemming from boundary triples offers other levels of distinction between different self-adjoint extensions and further clarity. Self-adjoint extensions that are linear relations and not matrices are analyzed in more detail in Subsection
3.2
. We will conclude that it is enough to consider the matrices as parameters, and hence the simple yet powerful result of Theorem
3.11
is even more useful.

3.2.
Eigenvalues of Extensions given by Linear Relations

Our investigation of the multiplicity of eigenvalues now expands to the case where
\theta
is a self-adjoint linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and not necessarily a matrix. While we will focus on the parametrization of self-adjoint extensions with
\theta
from Proposition
3.2
, many of the methods are easily modified to apply the parametrization with
\vartheta
from Corollary
3.3
. Therefore, some of these similar arguments are omitted for brevity.

Self-adjoint linear relations
\theta
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
naturally decompose
\mathbb{C}^{2}
into subspaces where
\theta
is an operator and where
\theta
is multi-valued by writing

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\overline{\dom(\theta)}=\Mul(\theta)^{\perp}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Mul(\theta).

Denote the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
by
P_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
. It is then possible to write
\theta=\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}\widehat{\oplus}\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
via
[
8
, Theorem 1.5.1]
, where

\displaystyle\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}=\left\{\{z,P_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}z^{\prime}\}~\mathchar 58\relax~\{z,z^{\prime}\}\in\theta\right\},

is a self-adjoint operator in
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
and

\displaystyle\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}=\left\{\{0,z^{\prime}\}~\mathchar 58\relax~z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}\right\},

is a self-adjoint purely mutli-valued relation in
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
.
If
\theta
is a Hermitian matrix, then
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}=\{\emptyset\}
and such parametrizations were discussed in Section
3.1
. Hence, it now suffices to assume that
\dim\Mul(\theta)\geq 1
. Note that setting
\dim\Mul(\theta)=2
means that
\theta=\{0,\mathbb{C}^{2}\}
. The definition of our boundary triple implies that this linear relation corresponds to the extension
{\bf L}_{\infty}
, which only has simple eigenvalues by Proposition
3.7
.

So, without loss of generality, our attention now is focused on the case where
\dim\Mul(\theta)=1
, where we can write

(3.21)

\displaystyle\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}&0\\
0&\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}\end{pmatrix},

which should not be confused with an operator.

The self-adjoint extension
{\bf L}(\theta)
has an eigenvalue
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}
of multiplicity two if and only if in the sense of linear relations
\dim\ker(\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}))=2
by Proposition
3.5
. Apply a unitary change of basis transformation to
M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
so that it acts on
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
via
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
and
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
, both of which have domain
\mathbb{C}^{2}
but range
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
, respectively. It suffices to let
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
be the linear operator which acts as the first row of
M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
in the new basis and
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
act as the second row. In this way, the linear relation resulting from the difference
\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
can be discussed in the space
\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
. Let
z=(z_{1},z_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then,
z\in\ker(\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}))
if and only if
z
belongs to both

\displaystyle\ker(\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}})

\displaystyle=\ker\left\{\{z,z_{1}^{\prime}-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}(z)\}~\mathchar 58\relax~\{z_{1},z_{1}^{\prime}\}\in\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}\right\}=K_{1}\cup K_{2},

and

\displaystyle\ker(\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}

\displaystyle-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}})=\ker\left\{\{z,z_{2}^{\prime}-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}(z)\}~\mathchar 58\relax~\{0,z_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}\right\}=K_{3}\cup K_{4},

where

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle=\{z~\mathchar 58\relax~z_{1}^{\prime}-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}(z)=0\},

\displaystyle\qquad K_{2}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\ker M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}~\mathchar 58\relax~z_{1}=0\},

\displaystyle K_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(z_{1},0)^{T}~\mathchar 58\relax~z_{2}^{\prime}-M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}[(z_{1},0)^{T}]=0\},

\displaystyle K_{4}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\ker M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}~\mathchar 58\relax~z_{2}\neq 0\}.

To clarify, a function
z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
is an element of
\ker(\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}))
if and only if
z\in(K_{1}\cup K_{2})\cap(K_{3}\cup K_{4})
. The next result identifies which combinations of these conditions can lead to double eigenvalues, and is hence a linear relation version of Theorem
3.11
. While these conditions rely on the transformed
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
and
M_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm mul}}
, which of course depend on
\theta
itself, they are still concrete and can be used in the upcoming discussion to show the correspondence between matrix
\theta
and self-adjoint linear relation
\theta
(which is not a matrix).

Note that the memberships to
K_{1}
through
K_{4}
can be modified in the obvious way to apply to
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
by replacing
\theta
by
\vartheta
and
M_{0}({\lambda})
by
M_{\infty}({\lambda})
when decomposing
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\theta
be a self-adjoint linear relation with
\dim\Mul(\theta)=1
and
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
be an eigenvalue of
L(\theta)
with multiplicity two. Then there exists some
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
z\in K_{1}\cap K_{3}
, and
w\in K_{1}\cap K_{4}
. In particular,
z
and
w
are linearly independent.

Proof.

The previous discussion shows that vectors from the kernel of
\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
come from linear independent vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that belong to a combination
K_{1}
through
K_{4}
. However, further scrutiny shows that some combinations do not matter or are not possible. The result is shown for
{\lambda}\in\rho(M_{0})
, with the analog for
{\lambda}\in\rho(M_{\infty})
following similarly.

Let
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
and
z\in K_{2}\cap K_{3}
. Then clearly
z=(0,0)^{T}
and so it does not ‘influence’
\dim\ker(\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}))
.

Likewise, let
z\in K_{2}\cap K_{4}
. This means that
z_{1}=0
and
z\in\ker M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})
. But
M_{0}({\lambda})
has trivial kernel for all
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}
except when
{\lambda}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
thanks to the relationship
M_{\infty}({\lambda})=-M_{0}({\lambda})^{-1}
when
{\lambda}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
. Hence,
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})
and by Proposition
3.7

(ii)
,
u_{1}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})=0
which in turn implies that
u_{1}^{[0]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\neq 0
and
u_{2}^{[1]}(b,{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\neq 0
due to the normalization of the fundamental system of solutions. Suppressing the dependence of solutions on
b
, this explicitly means

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}\begin{pmatrix}-u_{1}^{[0]}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})&1\\
1&-u_{2}^{[1]}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
z_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})}\begin{pmatrix}z_{2}\\
-z_{2}u_{2}^{[1]}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}})\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly, this is only possible if

does not ‘influence’

.

Now,
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
and has multiplicity two so
\dim\ker(\theta-M_{0}({\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}))=2
. There are only two combinations of conditions remaining and the result follows.
∎

In Floquet-Bloch theory (see e.g.
[

,
37
]
), unions of eigenvalues for different boundary conditions are of interest to determine the spectrum of graph Laplacians and Laplacians on infinite periodic lattices. Roughly speaking, the union (over varying boundary conditions) of eigenvalues of the operator over a fundamental domain equals the (purely absolutely continuous) spectrum of the Laplacian on the infinite periodic graph. Motivated by such applications, we consider the unions of eigenvalues for the Sturm–Liouville setting:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigcup_{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}\sigma({\bf L}(\theta)),\quad\Sigma^{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma({\bf L}_{0})\cap\sigma({\bf L}_{\infty})\quad\text{ and }\quad\Sigma^{\mathcal{R}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(\bigcup_{\theta\in\mathcal{R}}\sigma({\bf L}(\theta))\right)\backslash\left(\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}\cup\Sigma^{\mathcal{E}}\right).

The notation should not be confused with the singular or residual parts of the spectrum of extensions. The analysis conducted in the proof of Theorem

reveals that those eigenvalues in

are also eigenvalues of a corresponding Hermitian
2\times 2
matrix. The following result follows directly from Corollary
3.12

Corollary 3.15
.

Any eigenvalue
{\lambda}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\theta}}\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})\cup\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})

{\bf L}(\theta)
for
\theta
a self-adjoint linear relation that is not a matrix belongs to

. In particular, this means that

Proof.

Corollary

says that any

can be realized not only as an eigenvalue of some
{\bf L}(\theta)
with

where

∎

However, it is possible to say more about these eigenvalues that come from choices of parameters that are self-adjoint linear relations and not matrices by exploiting the decomposition of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
into one-dimensional subspaces corresponding to
\theta_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm op}}
and

, as above, see equation (

Again, we focus our attention on

and

as above and let

if and only if

or

For a moment assume that

Essentially, this means the behavior of eigenvalues of

4.
Aronszajn–Donoghue and Sturm–Liouville operators

The semi-bounded Sturm–Liouville expression
\ell
had its self-adjoint extensions naturally parameterized by boundary triples in Section
3
. However, there are additional tools for the spectral analysis of such operators in perturbation theory and so we reformulate the self-adjoint extensions of
\ell
so that these tools can be applied.

We start with the self-adjoint operator
{\bf L}_{0}
(defined in equation (
3.9
)), and add a rank-two perturbation which is parametrized by
\vartheta
and changes the correct subspace of
\mathcal{H}
by coordinate mappings
{\bf B}
and
{\bf B^{*}}
. The perturbation is well defined due to
[
11
, Theorem 3.6]
, but the justification relies on tools including a boundary pair and singular space
\mathcal{H}_{-1}\supseteq\mathcal{H}

[
2
]
(note the subscripts on the below inner products correspond to
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
). These details do not have an impact on the spectral analysis and so we refer readers to
[
11
]
for more information.

We define

\displaystyle\langle f,\widetilde{\delta}_{a}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to a^{+}}\dfrac{f(x)}{v_{a}(x,{\lambda}_{0})},

\displaystyle\langle f,\widetilde{\delta}_{b}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to b^{-}}\dfrac{f(x)}{v_{b}(x,{\lambda}_{0})},

so that the coordinate mapping
{\bf B}\mathchar 58\relax\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\Ran({\bf B})\subset\mathcal{H}_{-1}({\bf L}_{0})
acts via multiplication by the row vector

\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\delta}_{a},\widetilde{\delta}_{b}\end{pmatrix}.

A simple calculation yields that on
\mathcal{H}_{-1}({\bf L}_{0})
the operator
{\bf B}^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf B}^{*}f=\begin{pmatrix}\langle f,\widetilde{\delta}_{a}\rangle_{1}\\
\langle f,\widetilde{\delta}_{b}\rangle_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

The parametrization is accomplished via self-adjoint linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, denoted
\vartheta
and defined as
\vartheta=-\theta^{-1}
, for
\theta
as in Section
3
and Remark
3.4
.

The perturbation can then be defined via
[
11
, Theorem 3.6]
so that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)={\bf L}_{0}+{\bf B}\vartheta{\bf B^{*}},

and every self-adjoint extension of the minimal operator
{\bf L}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm min}}
can be written as
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
for some
\vartheta\in\mathcal{R}
. In this section, however, we consider only
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}.

To avoid possible confusion, we rephrase some of Remark
3.4
: The main reason for the use of
\vartheta
here, as opposed to
\theta
, is because boundary triples and perturbation theory naturally have opposite starting points, or self-adjoint extensions, that emerge. The choice of
\vartheta
above allows

to be the natural self-adjoint extension of interest in both cases, as opposed to the notation in
[
11
]
.

Let us begin our spectral analysis by briefly discussing ramifications of two Aronszajn–Donoghue type results in our setting of finite rank perturbations obtained by Sturm–Liouville operators. To state those results, we consider rank
d
perturbations of a self-adjoint operator
A
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}.
Focusing on the interesting part, such perturbations can be represented by
A_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}=A+\mathbf{B}\vartheta\mathbf{B}^{*},

\mathbf{B}\mathchar 58\relax{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\to\mathcal{H},
with
\Ran\mathbf{B}
being cyclic and where
\vartheta
is a
d\times d
Hermitian matrix. By
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\vartheta}
and
\mu_{\vartheta},
respectively, we denote the matrix and scalar spectral measures of
A_{\vartheta}
with respect to
\mathbf{B}.
This just means

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(\vartheta,{\lambda})=\mathbf{B}^{*}(A_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}-{\lambda})^{-1}\mathbf{B}=\int_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}}}\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\vartheta}(t)}{t-{\lambda}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}=\Tr{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}.

Remark 4.1
.

The definition of
\mathbf{B}
agrees with the choices of boundary maps. Therefore, the setup for the unperturbed operator
{\bf L}_{0}
agrees with that in Proposition
3.2
. Let us verify the compatibility of the
m
-functions for the other self-adjoint extensions used in the boundary triples set up in the previous sections and in perturbation theory. This is done by confirming that the
m
-functions in (
5.1
) and (
4.2
), both of which are denoted by
\widetilde{M}(\vartheta,{\lambda}),
do indeed agree:

A central object of finite rank perturbation theory is the Aronszajn–Krein type formula
\widetilde{M}(\vartheta,{\lambda})=M_{0}({\lambda})(\vartheta M_{0}({\lambda})+I)^{-1},
see e.g.
[
51
]
for the rank one setting and
[
42
]
for higher rank perturbations. On the other hand, we recall the relation
\widetilde{M}(\vartheta,{\lambda})=(\vartheta-M_{\infty}({\lambda}))^{-1}
of
m
-functions given in (

). This apparent difference is reconciled by simply observing that
M_{0}=-M_{\infty}^{-1}
implies

M_{0}(\vartheta M_{0}+I)^{-1}=-M_{0}(-\vartheta M_{0}-I)^{-1}=M_{\infty}^{-1}(\vartheta M_{\infty}^{-1}-I)^{-1}=(\vartheta-M_{\infty})^{-1}.

Here we suppressed the explicit dependence of the

for the convenience of readability.

We are now ready to state and discuss aforementioned Aronszajn–Donoghue type results.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
45
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
\nu
be a singular measure. The scalar spectral measures
\mu_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}
of finite rank
d
perturbations
A_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta}}
with cyclic
\Ran\mathbf{B}
are mutually singular with
\nu
for all parameters
\vartheta
, except (possibly) a set of Hausdorff dimension
d^{2}-1
.

In other words, by Theorem
4.2
, the dimension of the scalar (by taking the traces of the matrix-valued) spectral measures
\mu_{\vartheta}
may be
\nu\not\perp\mu_{\vartheta}
for at most
d^{2}-1
dimensions. Setting
\nu=\mu_{{}_{\scriptstyle\vartheta_{0}}}
for
\vartheta_{0}\neq\vartheta
, we obtain an Aronszajn–Donoghue type result.

In our application to Sturm–Liouville operators, we have
d=2
, making the parameter space
d^{2}=4
dimensional, with a
d^{2}-1=3
dimensional boundary associated with those boundary conditions that correspond to perturbation parameters from
\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{M}
. And so, even if Theorem
4.2
could be extended from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{R}
, we would not be able to gain any useful information regarding the mutual singularity of the scalar spectral measures for parameters in
\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{M}
.

For lines in parameter space we know more:

Theorem 4.3

(
[
42
, Theorem 7.3]
)
.

Let
\nu
be a singular Radon measure,
\widetilde{\vartheta}\in\mathcal{M}
and let
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
be positive definite. Then scalar spectral measures
\mu_{\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta}
of
A_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta}}
are mutually singular with
\nu
for all except (possibly) countably many
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Remark 4.4
.

Notice that simple examples can show that the condition of
\vartheta
being positive definite cannot be relaxed to
\vartheta
being non-negative. Indeed, perturbing any diagonal
2\times 2
matrix by
\vartheta=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\eta\end{pmatrix}
will leave one of the eigenvalues unchanged. To avoid possible confusion, we mention that this example does not contradict the classical Aronszajn–Donoghue Theorem, see e.g.
[
51
]
, since this would be a rank one perturbation by a non-cyclic vector.

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

For some
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
let
A_{0}={\bf L}(\theta_{0})
. We apply Theorem
4.3
with
A_{0},

\tilde{\vartheta}=0
and some (just any) positive definite
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
as well as
\nu
being equal to the scalar-valued spectral measure of
{\bf L}(\theta_{0})
with respect to
\mathbf{B}
. Then, by the conclusion of Theorem
4.3
, the scalar spectral measure of operator
A_{t\vartheta}
(with respect to
\mathbf{B}
) is mutually singular with respect to
\nu
for uncountably many
t\in{\mathbb{R}}.
We simply let
\theta_{1}
be such that
{\bf L}(\theta_{1})=A_{t\vartheta}
for one of those uncountably many
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since boundary triples
{\bf L}(\theta)
exhaust all self-adjoint extensions, it is always possible to find such a

And because in our setting of Sturm–Liouville operators the spectrum consists of eigenvalues, we have
\sigma({\bf L}(\theta_{0}))\cap\sigma({\bf L}(\theta_{1}))=\varnothing.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

Operator

is a perturbation of operator
A
by
t\vartheta\in\mathcal{M},
so that Theorem
4.3
can indeed be applied although we might not be able to attain
{\bf L}(\theta_{0})
via a perturbation

as defined in (

) with
\vartheta\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{M}
.

Motivated by Theorem

, we explore Sturm–Liouville operators with boundary conditions that correspond to perturbations with parameters of the form

with
\widetilde{\vartheta},\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}.
Our results greatly improve the conclusions of Theorem

in the case of Sturm–Liouville operators. One major simplification comes from the fact that in this setting the spectrum is pure point.

Theorem 4.6
.

Consider the Sturm–Liouville operator
\widetilde{\bf L}(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta)
with fixed
\widetilde{\vartheta},\vartheta\in\mathcal{M},

\det\vartheta\neq 0.
Then every
\lambda\in\rho({\bf L}_{\infty})
is an eigenvalue for at most two values of

We obtain an immediate corollary in the spirit of Theorem

Corollary 4.7
.

Consider the Sturm–Liouville operator
\widetilde{\bf L}(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta)
with fixed
\widetilde{\vartheta},\vartheta\in\mathcal{M},

\det\vartheta\neq 0.
Let
\nu
be a singular measure with

. Then scalar spectral measures
\mu_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta}}
of
\widetilde{\bf L}(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta)
are mutually singular with
\nu
for all except (possibly) two

.

For convenience, we suppress the independent variables in functions
u_{i}^{[j]}(b,\lambda),
and write
u_{i}^{[j]}
instead.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

By equation (
3.12
) and the formula for

in Corollary

, the

is formally given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta,{\lambda})=(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta-M_{\infty}(\lambda))^{-1}=\frac{u_{1}^{[1]}}{\det X}X^{-1},

where

\displaystyle X=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\vartheta}_{11}u_{1}^{[1]}+t\vartheta_{11}u_{1}^{[1]}+u_{2}^{[1]}&\widetilde{\vartheta}_{12}u_{1}^{[1]}+t\vartheta_{12}u_{1}^{[1]}-1\\
\widetilde{\vartheta}_{21}u_{1}^{[1]}+t\vartheta_{21}u_{1}^{[1]}-1&\widetilde{\vartheta}_{22}u_{1}^{[1]}+t\vartheta_{22}u_{1}^{[1]}+u_{1}^{[0]}\end{pmatrix}.

As before, the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\bf L}(\widetilde{\vartheta}+t\vartheta)
occur at the roots of

And, clearly,

is a polynomial of degree two in

with leading coefficient

. By Proposition

, we have

and by our assumption we have

for zero, one or two values of

∎

A somewhat tedious calculation yields two immediate corollaries to this proof.

Corollary 4.8
.

When

in Theorem

while

Fix

with non-zero

\displaystyle t=\frac{-\widetilde{\vartheta}_{11}u_{1}^{[1]}+u_{2}^{[1]}}{\vartheta_{11}u_{1}^{[1]}}=\frac{-\widetilde{\vartheta}_{12}u_{1}^{[1]}+1}{\vartheta_{12}u_{1}^{[1]}}=\frac{-\widetilde{\vartheta}_{21}u_{1}^{[1]}+1}{\vartheta_{21}u_{1}^{[1]}}=\frac{-\widetilde{\vartheta}_{22}u_{1}^{[1]}+u_{1}^{[0]}}{\vartheta_{22}u_{1}^{[1]}}.

By Proposition

, we have

∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Every real
{\lambda}\geq K
is attained as a simple eigenvalue of a Sturm–Liouville operator with canonical separated boundary conditions.

Moreover,

is an eigenvalue for
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
where

Proof.

Note that
{\bf L}_{\infty}
has simple eigenvalues and corresponds to Sturm–Liouville operator with separated boundary conditions. So the statement is true for

. Further, recall that every eigenvalue of a Sturm–Liouville operator with canonical separated boundary conditions is simple by Corollary

For real

that produce a real

in Corollary

At such a fixed

the functions

Now we take any

∎

On the flip side, note that if

then there are no real values of

. This can be used to imply that certain values of

We reproduce some results that were obtained in the case of the Jacobi operator in

Theorem 5.1
.

[

For a matrix-valued Herglotz function with representation

(5.1)

\displaystyle M(z)=C+Dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}}((w-z)^{-1}-w(1+w^{2})^{-1})d{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(w),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\{\lambda\}=-i\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\varepsilon M(\lambda+i\varepsilon).

Theorem 5.2
.

The matrix-valued weights of the point masses
{\lambda}_{n}
of
{\bf L}_{0}
are given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\{\lambda_{n}\}

\displaystyle=-i\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\varepsilon\dfrac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-u_{1}^{[0]}(b,\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)&1\\
1&-u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)\end{array}\right).

In particular, we verify the well-known fact that the multiplicity of each eigenvalue is one.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.2
, we recall

\displaystyle M_{0}(\lambda)=\dfrac{1}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-u_{1}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)&1\\
1&-u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda)\end{array}\right),

for
\lambda\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
. And in accordance with Theorem
5.1
we set out to evaluate the limit of
\varepsilon M_{0}(\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Here it makes sense to evaluate
M_{0}
at
{\lambda}_{n}+i\varepsilon\in\rho({\bf L}_{0})
. We note that this limit exists finitely by the choice of the functions
u_{1}^{[0]}(b,\lambda)
and
u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda)
, and, in particular, since we can ‘at most’ exhibit eigenvalues. By ‘at most’ we mean that the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\dfrac{\varepsilon}{u_{2}^{[0]}(b,\lambda+i\varepsilon)},

can either be zero (when
{\lambda}
is not an eigenvalue of
{\bf L}_{0}
) or some non-zero constant. We obtain (
5.2
).

We have
{\bf L}_{0}=\widetilde{\bf L}(0)
, and so by Proposition
3.5
having simple eigenvalues is equivalent to
\dim\Ker(M_{\infty}({\lambda}_{n}))\neq 0
for each
{\lambda}_{n}
. This is clear from the fact that by Corollary
3.3
the matrix function
M_{\infty}({\lambda})
has non-zero off-diagonal entries for all
{\lambda}
.
∎

Next we consider the unperturbed operator
{\bf L}_{0}
in its spectral representation, which is given by the operator
\boldsymbol{M}
on
L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})
(the operator that multiplies by the independent variable
\boldsymbol{M}\mathchar 58\relax L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\to L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\mathchar 58\relax f(x)\mapsto xf(x)
).

Corollary 5.3
.

The eigenvector of
{\bf L}_{0}
corresponding to eigenvalue
{\lambda}_{n}
takes the following form in the spectral representation

\displaystyle f_{n}(x)=\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\{{\lambda}_{n}\}}}(x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}),

where
\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\{\lambda_{n}\}}}
denotes the characteristic function at
x={\lambda}_{n}
. We obtain an explicit formulation of the space
L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\clos\spa\left\{f_{n}\mathchar 58\relax n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\}.

Proof.

Since the multiplicity of each eigenvalue equals one, the columns of (
5.2
) are linearly dependent. And neither of the columns being trivial implies that we can just pick one of them. The magnitude of the eigenvector does not matter, so we can simply drop the multiplicative constant from (
5.2
).
∎

For operators
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
we obtain the following result using analogous arguments and (
3.12
).

Corollary 5.4
.

Assume that
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
corresponds to a non-degenerate operator
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta).
The eigenvector
f^{\vartheta}_{n}\in L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\vartheta})
of
\widetilde{\bf L}(\vartheta)
corresponding to an eigenvalue
{\lambda}^{\vartheta}_{n},

n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},
takes the following form in the spectral representation

f^{\vartheta}_{n}(x)=\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\{\lambda^{\vartheta}_{n}\}}}(x)\begin{pmatrix}1-\vartheta_{12}\\
\vartheta_{11}-\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}u_{2}^{[1]}(b,\lambda_{n}+i\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}.

The spectral representation lives on the space
L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\vartheta})=\clos\spa\left\{f_{n}^{\vartheta}\mathchar 58\relax n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\}.

Remark 5.5
.

For a degenerate eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}^{\vartheta}
the corresponding eigenspace in the spectral representation is spanned by
\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\{\lambda^{\vartheta}_{n}\}}}(x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\vartheta})
and
\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\{\lambda^{\vartheta}_{n}\}}}(x)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\vartheta}).
