Local existence of solutions to a nonlinear autonomous PDE model for population dynamics with nonlocal transport and competition
Michael R. Lindstrom; mike.lindstrom@utrgv.edu

Abstract
In this paper, we prove that a particular nondegenerate, nonlinear, autonomous parabolic partial differential equation with a nonlocal mass transfer admits the local existence of classical solutions. The equation was developed to qualitatively describe temporal changes in population densities over space through accounting for location desirability and fast, long-range travel. Beginning with sufficiently regular initial conditions, through smoothing the PDE and employing energy arguments, we obtain a sequence of approximators converging to a classical solution.



Keywords:
local existence, nonlocal operator, nonlinear diffusion, non-degenerate parabolic PDE, autonomous PDE

Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are a valuable means of modelling physical and biological systems. These models help in understanding key qualitative features and in making quantitative predictions. PDE models are often derived from consideration of physical principles, but it is not always clear if solutions even exist; or, if they do exist, what properties they enjoy such as smoothness, uniqueness, etc. The canonical example of this is the Navier Stokes Equations
[
1
,
2
]
in fluid dynamics where global existence of solutions is still unknown
[
3
]
. If a PDE is to be studied numerically, knowing an appropriate solution space is then helpful in selecting a suitable numerical method — see for example books addressing numerical schemes for different classes of PDE
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

In this paper, we confirm that, under suitable hypotheses, there exist classical solutions to a particular nonlinear parabolic integro-differential equation model. The model of interest
[
7
]
was derived to describe populations of people experiencing homelessness. The model is ecological in nature, taking into consideration the desirability of a location (such that individuals may be more likely to stay at a location when it is more desirable), nonlocal travel (over a short time, an individual can travel a large distance), and that desirability decreases as the local population in an area increases (due to reduced available resources per individual). The domain itself imposes additional data on the PDE through spatiotemporal variations in entry and exit rates. The understanding of this model is important as it was the first of its kind, to the author’s knowledge, PDE model describing homelessness. While many important properties have been proven on solutions to the model
[
7
]
assuming their existence, the existence of solutions has not been explored. This paper thus establishes that the model previously derived is a theoretically reasonable framework — from a mathematical standpoint — through which to study homelessness. Understanding this model mathematically, and the types of solutions that exist, can provide a solid foundation for it being used to better understand the dynamics of homelessness, for extending the model by incorporating more details, for adapting the model suitably to other applications, and even to fitting the model to real-world data.

Nonlocal parabolic PDEs arise in many settings including mathematical finance

Rigorous analysis has proved that Patlak-Keller-Segel models with degenerate diffusion are locally well-posed in
2

3
dimensions
[
18

. The Patlak-Keller-Segel models are nonlocal in space, like the model studied here. Beyond mathematical models themselves, solutions are known to exist for quite a broad range of nonlocal parabolic PDEs
[

,
20

, but our system violates some hypotheses used to establish these results, such as monotonicity in the dependent variable or derivatives of nonlinear terms appearing. For simplicity, we assume the data in our system are smooth, but
other authors also study PDEs with nonregular data

Although our PDE of interest has a high degree of nonlinearity, methodologically, we proceed to prove local existence through reasonably similar means: establishing local existence of smoothed solutions and extracting a subsequence that tends to a valid solution. The use of Lyapunov-type methods to establish existence of solutions to PDEs is another common strategy

The terms in the model, in the case of homelessness and in a dimensional framework, can be explained as follows:

•

\eta
is an entry rate: it is the mean number of people entering the population of people experiencing homelessness per unit area per unit time. Local factors such as increases in rent could increase
\eta
.

•

\omega
is an exit rate: it is the rate that a person experiencing homelessness ceases to be homeless. Local features such as more affordable housing units or job placement could increase
\omega
.

•

\gamma
is a rate of long distance travel: it is the rate that a person who is homeless and wishes to relocate will travel a long distance in the city. Local features such as the number of public transit options affect
\gamma
.

•

\tau
models the probability (density) in moving from one region to another over a long distance at any given time. Factors such as routes of public transit or general knowledge about what is happening in different parts of the city affect
\tau
. The fact that
\int_{\Omega}\tau(t,y,x)\mathrm{d}x=1
amounts to the fact that, starting from
y
(getting on a bus, say) at time
t
, the probability of going somewhere must be
1
.

•

I
is a nonlocal operator that moves the density between points with a transfer kernel
\tau
:
I[\gamma\rho u](t,x)
is the number of people experiencing homelessness moving to a given location per unit area per unit time through all long-range travel over
\Omega
. From the interpretations of
\tau
and
\gamma
, we note that
\tau
is likely heavily influenced by both public transit and general knowledge of how different parts of the city are changing.

•

u
is an “unattractiveness” term and it represents the probability a person who is homeless wishes to leave their current location, either by walking to a nearby location or by travelling long-range in the city. It takes into account the effects of competition/crowding, since
u
increases with
\rho
. While
\rho
is the local population density, the probability a given individual wishes to relocate is
u
, giving an effective quantity
\rho u
to diffuse/move.

•

\kappa
describes the amount of resources in a location: for larger
\kappa
values, the unattractiveness is smaller.

The constants also have meaning as follows:

•

\delta
is a diffusivity: by assuming the local movement is roughly random and taking a diffusive limit, there is diffusion.

•

u^{+}
is the maximum possible unattractiveness of any location: we assume
u^{+}<1
because, observationally, we note that individuals experiencing homelessness may be found in all parts of a city, even areas with limited or no resources.

•

u^{+}-u^{-}
is the minimum possible unattractiveness of any location: we assume that
u^{-}<u^{+}
so
u^{+}-u^{-}
is strictly positive. Because
u
is representing the probability a person experiencing homelessness will leave the area, we do not allow
u
to be
0
because that would be too deterministic and not allow a person to move about.

•

\tilde{\rho}
controls how sensitive the unattractiveness is to the population density: as
\tilde{\rho}
increases, the unattractiveness
u
is affected less and less by changes in
\rho
.

We note that the unattractiveness can never reach zero for any
\rho
and the equation is nondegenerate.

The model does not directly account for aggregation effects whereby individuals who are homeless may choose to group together, nor does it account for how the homeless population itself may affect the features of the city. In a sense, it is a “leading order” model to describe homelessness in PDE fashion, where additional effects could be added.

We remark that some of the terms in this model may be relevant in other settings, too. Models of foraging do take into account tendencies of species leaving a patch of resources after some time
[
24
]
, which is what
u
effectively describes. In the case of models for locusts, the population density does affect the dispersion
[
25
]
. Some species living in the ocean are heavily influenced and dispersed by ocean currents
[
26
]
, which may provide nonlocal-like behaviour similar to
I
by rapidly moving the species around. A nonlocal operator
I
could even be relevant for describing the general population where long-range movement occurs in commuting to/from work or between cities.

Various qualitative properties of solutions were proven in
[
7
]
, assuming the existence of classical solutions, with
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
. In this paper, we show that classical solutions do exist on the torus. The torus, owing to the empty boundary, makes preliminary analysis simpler, without having to deal with complex geometries or boundary conditions. However, large cities often do have repeated pockets of residential and commercial districts, which gives a loosely periodic pattern, and thus the model could still be insightful.

Remark 1
.

While we establish local existence of classical solutions, proving global existence of solutions is beyond the scope of our work. Proving global existence would likely require first establishing global existence to the regularized systems and then showing this global existence is preserved in the limit. Through our work, we only establish local existence of the regularized solutions, with an interval of existence that may depend upon the initial condition (and solution regularity imposed) and we don’t identify a natural way to “glue” local regularized solutions together to establish global regularized solutions.

\varrho(t=s^{\prime})=\rho^{(\epsilon)}(s^{\prime})\in{H^{m+1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
as an initial condition. Then the same analysis can be done to find a solution
\varrho\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}];{H^{m+1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
for some
s^{\prime\prime}>s^{\prime}.
Furthermore, the energy
E_{m+1}^{(\epsilon)}
is bounded provided
||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{\mathcal{C}^{b^{**}}}
is finite, where
b^{**}
is the largest integer not exceeding
(m+1)/2+1.
Since
m>3+d
,
||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{\mathcal{C}^{b^{**}}}
is finite on
[0,T_{m}].
This stems from the fact that
||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
is finite on
[0,T_{m}].
Also, the solution stays bounded below by
\underline{\rho}_{0}/2.
By uniqueness of solutions,
\varrho=\rho^{(\epsilon)}
on
[s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}]
with
s^{\prime\prime}\geq T_{m}
and we have
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}((0,T_{m}];{H^{m+1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

∎

4.3
Step 3: Extracting a Convergent Subsequence in Weaker Space

We ultimately wish to take a subsequence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
converging to a solution with our desired properties. Through Lemma
14
, we firstly show that a subsequence can be extracted that converges weakly to an element of
L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).
The weak convegence on its own is insufficient and we thus also find a subsequence that converges strongly, but in a weaker space.
Owing to the nonlinearities in the derivative terms, maneuvering the equations in such a way as to prove
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
is Cauchy in some weaker space as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
is difficult. However, we really only require that a subsequence converges and for that we can use Aubin-Lions-Dubinskiĭ (Lemma
4
).

Lemma 14

(Weakly Convergent Subsequence)
.

Let
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
be defined as in Theorem
2
and let
m>3+d
where
m
is an integer. Then there is a subsequence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
converging weakly in
L^{2}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
to
\rho^{*}\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

Proof.

From Proposition
2
,
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
by some
K>0
for all
\epsilon>0
and thus the sequence is bounded in
L^{2}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).
By Lemma
5
, there is a weakly convergent subsequence
\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}
converging to some
\rho^{*}\in L^{2}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

This forces
\rho^{*}\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).
Were this not the case then let
Z>2K
and let the set
T_{Z}=\{t|\quad||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\geq Z\}.
We have that
|T_{Z}|>0
. Let us denote
f(t,x)=\chi_{T_{Z}}(t)\rho^{*}(t,x)\in L^{2}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, where
\chi_{I}
denotes the characteristic function on
I
. Then for all
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle|(\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}-\rho^{*},f)_{L^{2}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})}|

\displaystyle=|\int_{T_{Z}}(\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}-\rho^{*},\rho^{*})_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\mathrm{d}t|

\displaystyle=|\int_{T_{Z}}\left((\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})},\rho^{*})_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}-||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t|

\displaystyle\geq|\int_{T_{Z}}-\frac{||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}}{2}\mathrm{d}t|

\displaystyle\geq|T_{Z}|Z^{2}/2.

The second last inequality stems from
(\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})},\rho^{*})_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\leq||\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
and noting that on
T_{Z}
,
||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\geq Z=2K>2||\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.
Thus,
\rho^{(\epsilon^{\prime})}
does not converge weakly to
\rho^{*}
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 15

(Strongly Convergent Subsequence)
.

Let
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
be the subsequence obtained from Lemma
14
and
d/2<m^{\prime}<m
.
Then there is a subsequence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
converging strongly to

\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

Proof.

We note that
{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
is compactly embedded in
{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
, which is continuously embedded in
{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
(Lemma
7
). We also have that
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
by Proposition
2
. Thus, if we can show that
\rho^{(\epsilon)}_{t}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
then, by Aubin-Lions-Dubinskiĭ (Lemma
4
), there is a strongly convergent subsequence in
L^{\infty}([0,T];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).
From the proof of Proposition
3
,

\displaystyle||\rho^{(\epsilon)}_{t}||_{{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{m}.

With this, we can conclude there is a strongly convergent subsequence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
converging to
\rho^{*}\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0.
Since each term in the sequence is continuous over
[0,T_{m}]
and the convergence is in the
L^{\infty}
-norm over
[0,T_{m}]
, by the Uniform Limit Theorem, the limit is also continuous and hence in
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

∎

4.4
Step 4: Obtaining a Strong Solution in Target Space

To show that the limit
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, we work with the weak topology. The key insight for this stage is that continuity in the norm plus weak continuity gives strong continuity.

Lemma 16

(Weak Continuity)
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
with integers
m>m^{\prime}
with
m>d^{*}=3+d
,
m^{\prime}>d/2
and
let
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
and
\rho^{*}
be the subsequence and limit found in Lemma
15
. Then
\forall\epsilon>0,\rho^{(\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{C}_{W}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
and
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\to\rho^{*}
in
\mathcal{C}_{W}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Proof.

Since
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, we also have
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{C}_{W}([0,T_{m}],{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).
Additionally, from
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\rightarrow\rho^{*}
uniformly on
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, we have that for any
\tilde{\phi}\in{H^{-m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
,
\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\tilde{\phi}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0.

Let
\max\{||\rho^{*}||_{L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})},\sup_{\epsilon>0}||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{L^{\infty}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})}\}\leq U.

We next note that for
m^{\prime}<m
,
{H^{-m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
is a dense subset of
{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
so that given any
\phi\in{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
and
\nu>0
, there is
\tilde{\phi}\in{H^{-m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
with
||\phi-\tilde{\phi}||_{{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}<\nu/(3U).

We wish to prove that for any
\phi\in{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})},

\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\phi(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0.
Let
\nu>0
and choose
\tilde{\phi}\in{H^{-m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
so
||\phi-\tilde{\phi}||_{{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}<\nu/(3U).
Choose
\epsilon^{\prime}
so that
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}
gives
|\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\tilde{\phi}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})\mathrm{d}x|<\nu/3.
Then if
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle|\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\phi(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle\leq|\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\underbrace{(\phi-\tilde{\phi})}_{\in{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\underbrace{\rho^{(\epsilon)}}_{\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+|\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\underbrace{\tilde{\phi}}_{\in{H^{-m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\underbrace{(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})}_{\text{\hskip 7.97224ptweak }{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\text{ convergence}}\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\underbrace{(\tilde{\phi}-\phi)}_{\in{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\underbrace{\rho^{*}}_{\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle\leq||\phi-\tilde{\phi}||_{{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}+|\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\tilde{\phi}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle+||\phi-\tilde{\phi}||_{{H^{-m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu}{3U}U+\frac{\nu}{3}+\frac{\nu}{3U}U

\displaystyle=\nu.

∎

Lemma 17

(Norm Continuity Plus Weak Continuity Gives Strong Continuity)
.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{C}_{W}([0,T];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
and
||f||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T]).
Then
f\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

Proof.

Let
t_{0}\in[0,T]
. We wish to show
\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}f(t)=f(t_{0})
where the limit is understood to be a left-/right-sided limit at the right-/left-endpoint. We compute

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}||f(t)-f(t_{0})||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{0}}(f(t)-f(t_{0}),f(t)-f(t_{0}))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\Big(||f(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}+||f(t_{0})||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle-(f(t),f(t_{0}))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}-(f(t_{0}),f(t))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\Big(||f(t_{0})||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}+||f(t_{0})||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle-(f(t),f(t_{0}))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}-(f(t_{0}),f(t))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\Big((f(t_{0}),f(t_{0})-f(t))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle+(f(t_{0})-f(t),f(t_{0}))_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\Big)

\displaystyle=0.

To reach equality 3, we used the continuity of
||f||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.
To reach equality 5, we used the weak continuity and that
f(t)
converges weakly to
f(t_{0})
by testing against
f(t_{0})\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}.

∎

To obtain our strong solution result, we first prove a proposition asserting that solutions stemming from initial data
\rho_{0}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
in fact stay in
{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
over their interval of existence.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
with
m>d^{*}:3+d
an integer and let
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
be the subsquence of Lemma
15
and
\rho^{*}
and
T_{m}
be as in Lemma
15
. Then
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

To prove Proposition
4
, we prove a lemma affirming that
||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
is continous at
t=0
.

Lemma 18

(Continuity Properties of Solution)
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
with
m>d^{*}:=3+d
an integer and let
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
and
\rho^{*}
be defined as in Lemma
15
. Then

||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
is strongly right-continuous at
t=0
.

Proof.

From Eq. (
13
), for all
\epsilon>0,\frac{1}{2}||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}\leq E_{m}(t)
with
E_{m}(0)=\frac{1}{2}||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}
. Then

\overline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}||\rho^{*}(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\leq\overline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}\overline{\lim}_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}||\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\leq\overline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}\sqrt{2E_{m}(t)}=||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

Also, since
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{W}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, we have
\underline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}||\rho^{*}(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\geq||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.
To see this, note that with
\rho_{0}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
,

\displaystyle||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}(\rho^{*}(t),\rho_{0})_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}=\underline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}(\rho^{*}(t),\rho_{0})_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\leq\underline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}(||\rho^{*}(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}})=||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\underline{\lim}_{t\downarrow 0}||\rho^{*}(t)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

Together these give

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}=||\rho_{0}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}=||\rho^{*}(0)||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

∎

Now, we complete the proof of Proposition
4
.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

Let
0<\theta<T_{m}.
Then
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}((\theta,T_{m}];{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
for all
m^{\prime}<m+1
and, in particular,
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
has a subsequence (as per Lemma
15
) that converges in
\mathcal{C}^{0}((\theta,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
and thus we have
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}((0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
and
||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
is continuous on
(0,T_{m}].
From Lemma
18
,
||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}
is continuous at

so
||\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}])
. Combined with the weak continuity, we have
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}).

∎

4.5
Step 5: Convergence Analysis

In this final step, we finish proving our main result. The most important element is that the mollified equation, with its derivatives, in fact converges uniformly to an equation for
\rho^{*}
consistent with equations (
1
) and (
5
)-(
7
). Before proving Theorem
1
, we first prove establish a couple of helpful results:

Lemma 19

(Limiting Derivatives)
.

Let
m>d^{*}:=3+d
be an integer and suppose that
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
is the subsequence of approximators converging to
\rho^{*}
as in Lemma
15
. Then for all multi-indices
|\alpha|\leq 2
,
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{(\epsilon)}]=\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{*}
uniformly on
{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
for a.e.
t\in[0,T_{m}].

Proof.

It is done by computation and bounding an error. We use Lemmas
6
and
7
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{(\epsilon)}]

\displaystyle={\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{*}]+{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})].

From
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})
, we have
D^{2}\rho^{*}(t)\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
where
\ell>d/2
. Thus, for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq 2,

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{*}(t)]=\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{*}(t)
uniformly.

Let
m>m^{\prime}>2+d/2
. We have
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\to\rho^{*}
in
{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
on
[0,T_{m}]
with
\partial^{\alpha}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})
again
\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
,
\ell>d/2
. Then with Lemma
6
item 1,

\displaystyle||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})]||_{{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\leq||\partial^{\alpha}(\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*})||_{{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim||\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\to 0.

∎

Proposition 5

(Uniform Convergence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}_{t}
)
.

Let
m>d^{*}:=3+d
and suppose that
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
is the subsequence of approximators converging to
\rho^{*}
as in Lemma
15
. Then the sequence
\rho^{(\epsilon)}_{t}
converges uniformly in
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T_{m}];\mathcal{C}^{0}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})).

Proof.

We consider groups of terms for
\rho^{(\epsilon)}_{t}
: the terms involving the Laplacian, the nonlocal operator, and all remaining terms. Denote
u^{*}=u(\kappa,\rho^{*}).
We wish to show that all of the spatial terms and derivatives converge uniformly. We begin with the derivative terms (see Eq. (
11
)):

\displaystyle\delta\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Big({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\Delta(u^{+}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\kappa M({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}))]\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Big(u^{+}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\Delta{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]]-{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[M({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta\kappa+2M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle+\kappa M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}+\kappa M^{\prime\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})|\nabla{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}|^{2}]\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Big(u^{+}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\Delta\rho^{(\epsilon)}-M({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta\kappa-2M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}})\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle-\kappa M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\kappa M^{\prime\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})|\nabla{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Big(u^{+}\Delta\rho^{*}-M({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta\kappa-2M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{\epsilon})\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla\rho^{*}

\displaystyle-\kappa M^{\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Delta\rho^{*}-\kappa M^{\prime\prime}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\rho^{(\epsilon)})|{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\nabla\rho^{(\epsilon)}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta\Big(u^{+}\Delta\rho^{*}-M(\rho^{*})\Delta\kappa-2M^{\prime}(\rho^{*})\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla\rho^{*}-\kappa M^{\prime}(\rho^{*})\Delta\rho^{*}-\kappa M^{\prime\prime}(\rho^{*})|\nabla\rho^{*}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\delta\Delta(\rho^{*}u^{*}).

Above, the second equality came from
{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[f]
converging uniformly to
f
when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
and
\ell>d/2
. Each expression is in fact in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
due to the smooth functions and further mollifications.
The third inequality comes from Lemma
19
and from selecting
2+m^{\prime}<m
and noting that

\displaystyle||\Delta{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]-\Delta\rho^{*}||_{{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim||\Delta{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]-\Delta\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m^{\prime}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\leq||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]-\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m^{\prime}+2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\leq||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]-{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{*}]||_{{H^{m^{\prime}+2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}+||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{*}]-\rho^{*}||_{{H^{m^{\prime}+2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

Both terms go to zero: the first from
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\to\rho^{*}
in
{H^{m^{\prime}+2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
(since
d/2<m^{\prime}+2<m
) and the second from
\rho^{*}\in{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
(item 4 of Lemma
6
). Similar logic applies to the
\nabla{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\rho^{(\epsilon)}]\to\nabla\rho^{*}.

The fourth equality stems from
{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{(\epsilon)}]
converging uniformly to
\partial^{\alpha}\rho^{*}
for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq 2
by Lemma
19
and making use of the Lipschitz properties of
M
,
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}.

The local terms work out trivially since, for each
t
,
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
converges uniformly to
\rho^{*}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\Big(\eta-\omega\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\gamma(u^{+}\rho^{(\epsilon)}-\kappa M(\rho^{(\epsilon)})\Big)

\displaystyle=\eta-\omega\rho^{*}-\gamma(u^{+}\rho^{*}-\kappa M(\rho^{*})).

Finally, the nonlocal operator can be handled by the Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
30
]
. We have that
|\tau(y,x)\gamma(y)\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y)u(\kappa,\rho^{(\epsilon)})|\lesssim||\rho^{(\epsilon)}||_{\infty}
which is finite on
[0,T_{m}]
due to Proposition
3
and Sobolev Embedding. Thus, for each

, the integrand of the nonlocal operator is bounded by an
{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
constant function and from the convergence of
\rho^{(\epsilon)}
, we conclude

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tau(y,x)\gamma(y)\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y)u(\kappa(y),\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y))\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tau(y,x)\gamma(y)\rho^{*}(t,y)u(\kappa(y)\rho^{*}(t,y))\mathrm{d}y.

To see that the convergence is uniform, let

G^{(\epsilon)}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\tau(y,x)\gamma(y)(\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y)u(\kappa(y),\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y))-\rho^{*}(t,y)u^{*}(\kappa(y),\rho^{*}(t,y)))|\mathrm{d}y.

Then with
|\tau|,|\gamma|\leq\Xi_{0}
, we have

|G^{(\epsilon)}(x)|\leq\Xi_{0}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y)u(\kappa(y),\rho^{(\epsilon)}(t,y))-\rho^{*}(t,y)u(\kappa(y),\rho^{*}(t,y))|\mathrm{d}y\to 0\quad\text{as }\epsilon\downarrow 0

since
\rho^{(\epsilon)}\to\rho^{*}
uniformly and
|{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|=1.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

5
Conclusions and Future Work

We have proven the local existence of classical solutions to a particular nonlocal, nonlinear parabolic PDE with time-independent data. We did so through parameterizing a family of regularized solutions with smoothing parameter
\epsilon
and establishing an interval of local existence for the family independent of
\epsilon
. By passing to a subsequence, we obtained strong convergence to a solution of the PDE. This methodology is quite broadly applicable to nonlinear PDEs, but from these steps, we did not obtain global existence. However, given the smoothing properties of parabolic PDEs, the nondegeneracy condition in
u
, and the fact the limiting behaviours of the diffusion term are well-behaved as
\rho\downarrow 0
and
\rho\uparrow\infty
, we speculate that a stronger result holds: that there exists a unique solution, smooth in all its arguments, that exists globally in time.

The original model allowed for time-dependent data. However, Picard’s Theorem does not generalize well to nonautonomous ODEs in Banach spaces. While we can establish local existence of regularized solutions, the continuation result does not hold. This prevented our obtaining a limiting solution as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
with time-dependent data. It would be of interest to find other means of establishing existence of solutions with time-dependent data. One perspective is that for many applications, piecewise constant data may be useful. Thus, assuming the solutions do exist long enough, piecewise constant data can be combined sequentially.

Other useful future directions include studying the PDE with initial conditions in a fractional Sobolev space (primarily of mathematical interest). Our proofs in bounding the energy of Step 2, for example, were done for integer values of
m
. As an additional consideration, the model could be studied with fractional diffusion. Buildings and roads in a city effectively make for a porous medium and we take note that fractional diffusion equations
[
31
]
can be used in such settings to more accurately model dispersion.

\displaystyle\{1_{1},1_{2}\},\{2\}\qquad\text{i.e. }\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}g\times\partial_{x_{2}}g

\displaystyle\{1_{1}\},\{1_{2},2\}\qquad\text{i.e. }\partial_{x_{1}}g\times\partial_{x_{1}x_{2}}g

\displaystyle\{1_{2}\},\{1_{1},2\}\qquad\text{i.e. }\partial_{x_{1}}g\times\partial_{x_{1}x_{2}}g

\displaystyle\{1_{1}\},\{1_{2}\},\{2\}\qquad\text{i.e. }\partial_{x_{1}}g\times\partial_{x_{1}}g\times\partial_{x_{2}}g

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\{\{(2,1)\},

\displaystyle\{(2,0),(0,1)\},

\displaystyle\{(1,0),(1,1)\},

\displaystyle\{(1,0),(1,1)\},

\displaystyle\lesssim||g_{1}-g_{2}||_{H^{m}(\Omega)}||\prod_{\sigma\in\beta}\partial^{\sigma}g_{2}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Appendix B
Mollifiers on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

We present here proofs of various mollifier properties on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, originally presented in Greer’s thesis
[
34
]
. Some details have been added and some corrections have been made.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

1. We note that
{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[f]
is the solution to the heat equation

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta u

\displaystyle u(x,0)

\displaystyle=f(x)

on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
at
t=\epsilon.
The inequality follows from the maximum principle.

To prove the uniform convergence, let
\nu>0
. We have that the Fourier series of
f
is absolutely convergent
[
35
]
so
K>0
can be chosen so that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\geq K}|\hat{f}(k)|\leq\nu/2.

We have

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}[f]-f|

\displaystyle=\lvert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f}(k)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ik\cdot x}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})\rvert\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{f}(k)|(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\leq K}|\hat{f}(k)|(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})+\frac{\nu}{2}\leq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}K^{2}})\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\leq K}|\hat{f}(k)|+\frac{\nu}{2}\leq\nu

for small enough
\epsilon.

3. By Parseval’s formula,

\displaystyle({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f,g)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=(\hat{{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f},\hat{g})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}\hat{g}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}=(\hat{f},\hat{{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}g})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}=(f,{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}g)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

2. Let
\Phi
be any test function in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
We have

\displaystyle(\partial^{\alpha}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f,\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f,\partial^{\alpha}\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}=(-1)^{|\alpha|}(f,{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\partial^{\alpha}\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f}(k)(2\pi ik)^{\alpha}\hat{\Phi}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}=(-1)^{|\alpha|}(f,\partial^{\alpha}{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=(\partial^{\alpha}f,{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}=({\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\partial^{\alpha}f,\Phi)_{{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

and thus distributional derivatives commute with mollifiers.

4. Let
\nu>0
. Since
f\in{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
, there is
K>0
so that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|>K}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}<\nu/2.

Picking
\epsilon
small enough, we have

\displaystyle||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f-f||_{{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\leq K}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|>K}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle<\frac{\nu}{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}.

Thus there is convergence in
{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}.
To observe the rate of convergence in
{H^{s-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
is linear in
\epsilon
, we use

1-\mathrm{e}^{-\theta^{2}}\leq\theta^{2}.

This leads to the bounds

\displaystyle\lvert\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}}{1+|k|^{2}}\rvert

\displaystyle<\epsilon^{4}\lambda^{2},

\displaystyle\quad|k|<\lambda

\displaystyle\lvert\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}}{1+|k|^{2}}\rvert

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},

\displaystyle\quad|k|\geq\lambda

so if
\lambda=1/\epsilon
,

\displaystyle||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f-f||_{{H^{s-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2})^{s-1}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lvert\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}})^{2}}{1+|k|^{2}}\rvert\right)||f||_{{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}||f||_{{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}^{2}.

5. We first observe that the function
q(x)=(1+x)^{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}x}
has a global maximum at

with value
(\frac{\nu}{\epsilon^{2}})^{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-(\nu-\epsilon^{2})}.
Thus, for
\nu>0
and

,

for a
C
depending on
\nu
, and
||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}f||_{{H^{m+\nu}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}\lesssim\epsilon^{-\nu}||f||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.
Next, we estimate the representation of
{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\partial^{\nu}f(x)
in terms of its Fourier series. Let
\tilde{\nu}
be a multi-index with
|\tilde{\nu}|=\nu.

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\partial^{\tilde{\nu}}f(x)|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}(2\pi ik)^{\tilde{\nu}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ik\cdot x}\hat{f}(k)|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}{2}})\left((2\pi ik)^{\tilde{\nu}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ik\cdot x}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}{2}}\hat{f}(k)\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|(2\pi ik)^{2\tilde{\nu}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}||{\mathcal{J}^{(\epsilon)}}\partial^{\tilde{\nu}}f||_{{H^{0}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\nu-m}}||f||_{{H^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}.

Now we examine the last inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}|k|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{i}^{2}}\leq 2^{d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=2^{d}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{1}^{2}}(\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{2}^{2}}(...\sum_{k_{d}=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{d}^{2}})...))

\displaystyle=2^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}(\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}k_{i}})^{d}=2^{d}(\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon^{2}}})^{d}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}\epsilon^{-d}.

This gives the desired bound.
∎
