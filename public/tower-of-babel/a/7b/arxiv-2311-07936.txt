Occupied Processes: Going with the Flow
Valentin Tissot-Daguette

Abstract
We develop an Itô calculus for functionals of the ”time” spent by a path at arbitrary levels. A Markovian setting is recovered by lifting a process X X with its flow of occupation measures 𝒪 \calO and call the pair ( 𝒪 , X ) (\calO,X) the occupied process . While the occupation measure erases the chronology of the path, we show that our framework still includes many relevant problems in stochastic analysis and financial mathematics. The study of occupied processes therefore strikes a middle ground between the path-independent case and Dupire’s Functional Itô Calculus. We extend Itô’s and Feynman-Kac’s formula by introducing the occupation derivative , a projection of the functional linear derivative used extensively in mean field games and McKean-Vlasov optimal control. Importantly, we can recast through Feynman-Kac’s theorem a large class of path-dependent PDEs as parabolic problems where the occupation measure plays the role of time. We apply the present tools to the optimal stopping of spot local time and discuss financial examples including exotic options, corridor variance swaps, and path-dependent volatility.



Keywords:
Occupation measure,
Itô’s formula, Feynman-Kac theorem, Local

time, Exotic
derivatives, Corridor variance swaps, Path-dependent volatility

2.3
Occupation Functionals and Chronology Invariance

An
occupation functional
is a map
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calD\to\R
,
(o,x)\mapsto f(o,x)
. In finance, occupation functionals may represent the payoff of exotic options, corridor variance swaps, or path-dependent volatility; see
Sections

5.1
,
5.3
and
5.4
, respectively.
Naturally, we are interested in plugging in the occupied process and study properties of
f(\calO_{t},X_{t})
.
We stress that occupation functionals do not explicitly depend on the time variable. This is because the occupation measure plays de facto the role of time.
This simplification will reveal its grace in
Section

3
.

Remark 2
.

Suppose that
X
is an
(\F,\Q)-
continuous local martingale with Dambins-Dubins-Schwartz (DDS) Brownian motion

W_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X_{\tau_{\theta}},\qquad\tau_{\theta}=\inf\{t\in[0,T]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \langle X\rangle_{t}>\theta\},\qquad\theta\leq\Theta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle X\rangle_{T},

or conversely
X_{t}=W_{\theta_{t}}
,
\theta_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle X\rangle_{t}
. If
\calO_{t}^{Y}
denotes the occupation measure of some process

, it is easily seen that
\calO_{t}^{X}(\omega)=\calO_{\theta_{t}}^{W}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.
In particular, for every
\F-
stopping time
\tau
, and functional

,

f(\calO_{\tau}^{X},X_{\tau})=f(\calO_{\theta_{\tau}}^{W},W_{\theta_{\tau}}).

(17)

Note that
\theta_{\tau}
is a stopping time under the Brownian filtration
\F^{W}=(\calF_{\tau_{\theta}})_{\theta\leq\Theta}
. This observation will be particularly useful in
Section

4
to derive simpler formulation of arbitrary optimal stopping problems. See also
[
50
, Chapter IV]
.
The contrast becomes slightly more pronounced for general continuous semimartingales
X
.
One avenue is to employ the
scale function

\fraks\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R\to\R
of
X

[
51
, VII.3]
,
[
50
, II.2]
to transform

into a continuous local martingale

. We can thereafter apply the above arguments to

Chronology invariance.
It is intuitive that occupation functionals are invariant under the chronology of the path, keeping the terminal value fixed. Let us provide some insights in the simpler case
f=f(\calO)
by temporarily adopting a pathwise framework as in
[
28
,
29
]
.
Let
\Lambda=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Lambda_{t}
,
\Lambda_{t}=\{X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,t]\to\R\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\text{càdlàg}\}
and
\text{Sym}(A)
denote the symmetric group of some arbitrary set
A
. For fixed
t\in[0,T]
, consider the partition
\{t_{n}=\frac{nt}{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}n=0,\ldots,N\}
of
[0,t]
and the subgroup
\frakS^{N}_{t}
of
(\text{Sym}([0,t]),\circ)
containing the ”time permutations”,

\psi(s)=t_{\sigma(n)-\xi_{n}^{+}}\ +\ \xi_{n}(s-t_{n-1}),\quad t\in I_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=[t_{n-1},t_{n})\quad(I_{N}=[t_{N-1},t]),

(18)

where

\xi\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
and
\sigma\in\text{Sym}(\{1,\ldots,N\})
.
Next, consider the action
(\psi,X)\ \mapsto\ X\circ\ \psi\in\Lambda_{t}
,
X\in\Lambda_{t}
.
If
\oplus
denotes the concatenation operation for paths, we can also write (omitting that

is a closed interval for brevity),

X\circ\psi=Y^{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Y^{N},\qquad Y^{n}=X_{I_{\sigma(n)}}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi_{n}=1\}}+\overleftarrow{\ X_{I_{\sigma(n)}}}\mathds{1}_{\{\xi_{n}=-1\}},

where
X_{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(X_{s})_{s\in I}
and the arrow

indicates time reversal.
The transformations consists of two steps: (i) permute the intervals
I_{n}
according to
\sigma
(ii) use
\xi_{n}
to determine if
X_{I_{\sigma(n)}}
is run forward (
\xi_{n}=1
) or backward
(\xi_{n}=-1)
. See
Fig.

2
.
We finally set
\frakS_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{N\geq 1}\frakS^{N}_{t}

which is also a subgroup of
(\text{Sym}([0,t]),\circ)
. This leads us to the following definition.

:
Original path
X
(left), time permutation
\psi

Definition 1
.

A path functional
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\R
is
chronology-invariant
if
F(X\circ\psi)=F(X)
for all
X\in\Lambda_{t}
,
\psi\in\frakS_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
.

We now see how the chronology invariance relates to occupation functionals. As we are temporarily in a pathwise setting and the quadratic variation is not defined for every càdlàg paths, we employ the standard time occupation measure instead (see
Remark

1
) and introduce
\tilde{\calO}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\calM

which maps a path
X\in\Lambda_{t}
to
\tilde{\calO}_{t}=\tilde{\calO}(X)
. Note in passing that
\tilde{\calO}(X\oplus Y)=\tilde{\calO}(X)+\tilde{\calO}(Y)

\forall X,Y\in\Lambda
, so
\tilde{\calO}
is a homomorphism from
(\Lambda,\oplus)
to
(\calM,+)
.

Next, fix a chronology-invariant functional
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda_{T}\to\R
. Let
I_{n}=[t_{n-1},t_{n})
as in (
18
) and introduce the piecewise constant approximation
X^{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{I_{n}}X_{t_{n-1}}
which converges to
X
pointwise. In particular, we can find
F^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{N}\to\R
such that
F(X^{N})=F^{N}(X_{t_{0}},\ldots,X_{t_{N-1}})
. The chronology invariance of
F
implies that
F^{N}
must be a symmetric function, hence there exists
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\to\R
such that

F^{N}(X_{t_{0}},\ldots,X_{t_{N-1}})=f(\tilde{\calO}_{t}^{N}),\qquad\tilde{\calO}_{t}^{N}=\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t_{n-1}}}.

(19)

In mean field games,
F^{N}
is often called the
empirical projection
of
f

[
16
, I, Chapter 5]
. The name has, however, less meaning in the present context.
We then have for all
\phi\in\calC_{b}(\R)
that

\int_{\R}\phi\hskip 0.56905ptd\tilde{\calO}_{t}^{N}=\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi(X_{t_{n-1}})=\int_{0}^{t}\phi(X_{s}^{N})ds\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{t}\phi(X_{s})ds=\int_{\R}\phi\hskip 0.56905ptd\tilde{\calO}_{t},

by invoking the bounded convergence theorem and occupation time formula (
10
). This shows the weak convergence of
\tilde{\calO}_{t}^{N}
to the initial occupation measure
\tilde{\calO}_{t}
. For every bounded linear, chronology-invariant functional

, we conclude that
F(X)=\lim_{N\to\infty}f(\tilde{\calO}_{t}^{N})=f(\tilde{\calO}_{t})
, giving a first parallel between chronology invariance and occupation functionals.
In fact, we can prove the following general result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\R
be a functional.
Then the following are equivalent:

I.

F
is chronology-invariant.

II.

There exists a functional
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\to\R
such that
F=f\circ\tilde{\calO}.

Proof.

See
Section

A.1
.

3.1
Occupation Derivative

First, let us motivate the notion of derivative for occupation measures chosen in
Definition

3
below.
Given

we seek an adequate differential operator, say
\delta_{\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calC^{1}(\calM)\to\calC(\R)

\calC^{1}(\calM)
still to be defined,
such that

satisfies the chain rule

df(\calO_{t})=\delta_{\mu}f(\calO_{t})\cdot d\calO_{t},

with the usual scalar product between functions and measures, namely

\phi\cdot\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{\R}\phi(x)\mu(dx),\quad\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R\to\R,\quad\mu\in\calM,

(21)

whenever the above is well-defined. The dynamics of

appearing in (

still needs to be characterized. Rewriting the occupation measure as

\calO_{t}(A)=\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{u}}(A)d\langle X\rangle_{u}
, where

is the Dirac mass at the (random) level

, it is then immediate that

\displaystyle d\calO_{t}=\delta_{X_{t}}d\langle X\rangle_{t},

(22)

and similarly

for the standard time occupation measure

seen in (

Expression (

thus increases

at the spot level and proportionally to the stochastic clock of the process; see

Back to the chain rule (

(23)

We would like that (
23

f(o)=\phi\cdot o
,
\phi\in\calC(\R)
. From the occupation time formula (

. In light of (

should satisfy

\delta_{\mu}(o\mapsto\phi\cdot o)\equiv\phi,\quad\forall\phi\in\calC(\R).

(24)

Hence

has to be the usual derivative on space of measures, namely the

commonly used in mean field games and McKean-Vlasov controls

Although typically used for probability measures, we effortlessly extend its definition to arbitrary (finite) measures.
In what follows, we endow

with the metric

in

We also generalize the scalar product in (

is respectively the positive and negative part of some signed measure

Definition 2
.

A functional
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\to\R
is
continuously linearly differentiable

(

\delta_{\mu}f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\to\calC(\R)

I.

The map
\calM\times\R\ni(\mu,x)\mapsto\delta_{\mu}f(\mu)(x)
is continuous in the product topology,

II.

|\delta_{\mu}f(\mu)(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2})
for some constant
C\in(0,\infty)
, uniformly in
\mu
,

III.

For all
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\calM
and
\Delta=\mu^{\prime}-\mu

f(\mu^{\prime})-f(\mu)=\int_{0}^{1}\delta_{\mu}f(\mu+\eta\Delta)\cdot\Delta\ d\eta.

(25)

We can similarly define the functional linear derivative on
\calM^{\lambda}\subset\calM
and naturally write

as the Gateaux derivative of

\frac{d}{dh}f(\mu+h\delta_{x})|_{h=0}.

Hence

is akin to a Fréchet derivative; see also
Remark

3
. In the mean field game literature
[
16

15

]

the functional linear derivative is defined up to an additive constant as it is restricted to probability measures. Indeed, in this context, the signed measure

One way to verify that

Remark 3
.

Since every element

Given

On the other hand, if

In conclusion,

converges formally to the Dirac mass at

3.2
Itô’s Formula

We now state
Itô’s formula for continuous occupied semimartingales in one space dimension. For the multi-dimensional case, see

Theorem 3.1
.

(Itô’s formula)

Let

be a continuous semimartingale and

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\Big(\partial_{o}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\Big)f(\calO_{u},X_{u})d\langle X\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\Big(\delta_{\mu}f(\calO_{u},X_{u})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}f(\calO_{u},\cdot)\Big)\cdot d\calO_{u}.

(34)

Proof.

See
Section

A.2
.

Remark 4

We first outline the functional Itô formula proved by

3.5
Discussion: Taylor Expansion

Let us provide some heuristic
comments pertaining to Taylor expansions of occupation functionals.
We here focus on the standard time occupied processes
(\tilde{\calO},X)
as it relates more closely to the usual stochastic Taylor expansion
[
1
,
43
]
and functional Taylor expansion
[
47
,
29
]
.
If
f\in\calC^{1,2}(\calD)
, we first deduce from (
36
) the following
Stratonovich’s formula
,

\displaystyle f(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})

\displaystyle=f(\tilde{\calO}_{0},X_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{o}f(\tilde{\calO}_{u},X_{u})du+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}f(\tilde{\calO}_{u},X_{u})\circ dX_{u},

(56)

where
\circ
indicates Stratonovich integration.
As the integrands in (
56
) are themselves occupation functionals, we may iterate Stratonovich’s formula to derive an expansion when
f
is regular enough. If
f
is even real analytic in some sense,
we formally obtain the Taylor series,

\displaystyle f(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\calA}\partial_{\alpha}f(\tilde{\calO}_{0},X_{0})\calS_{\alpha}(X),

(57)

with index set
\calA=\{\varnothing\}\cup\{\alpha=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}k\geq 1\}
and notations
\partial_{0}=\partial_{o}
,
\partial_{1}=\partial_{x}
,
\partial_{\varnothing}f=f
. The map
\calS_{\alpha}
, otherwise called
signature functional
, assigns a path to the Stratonovich iterated integral in the variables
t,x
(where
0\sim t
,
1\sim x
) where
\alpha
prescribes the order of integration.
As an example,
\calS_{01}(X)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\circ\ dt_{1}\circ dX_{t_{2}}=\int_{0}^{t}udX_{u}
. If
\alpha=\varnothing
, we set
\calS_{\varnothing}\equiv 1
. Similar to stochastic Taylor expansions

[
1
,
43
]
, we may derive more general expressions when
(\tilde{\calO},X)
solves an occupied SDE as in
Section

3.4
with smooth coefficients.

Defining the path functional
F(X)=f(\tilde{\calO}(X),X_{t})
,
X\in\Lambda_{t}
, as in
Section

3.2.1
, then (
57
) can be formally rewritten as the functional Taylor series,

F(X)=\sum_{\alpha\in\calA}\Delta_{\alpha}F(X_{0})\calS_{\alpha}(X),

(58)

with the functional derivatives
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{t}
(where we recall that
\Delta_{t}F=\partial_{o}f)
,
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{x}
, and
\Delta_{\varnothing}f=f
; see
[
29
]
. Equation (
58
) gives a decomposition of any path functional in terms of the signature.
On the other hand, the series in (
57
) reveals an important subtlety:
the right-hand side possibly depends on the whole path
X
, hence its chronology, while the left-hand side do not. This suggests the existence of a simplified expansion with basis functionals entailing solely the occupied process. Specifically, we conjecture that there is a family
\{\Phi_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calD\to\R\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
with index set
\Gamma
no larger than
\calA

such that

\displaystyle f(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\partial_{\gamma}f(\tilde{\calO}_{0},X_{0})\ \Phi_{\gamma}(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t}).

(59)

Let us verify (
59
) for the first- and second-order terms.
Assuming
X_{0}=0
and writing
\partial_{\alpha}f=\partial_{\alpha}f(\tilde{\calO}_{0},X_{0}),\ \calS_{\alpha}=\calS_{\alpha}(X)
for brevity, then

f(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})\approx\partial_{\varnothing}f\calS_{\varnothing}+\partial_{o}f\calS_{0}+\partial_{x}f\calS_{1}+\partial_{oo}f\calS_{00}+\partial_{xx}f\calS_{11}+\partial_{ox}f\calS_{01}+\partial_{xo}f\calS_{10}.

(60)

First, introduce
k=k(\alpha)=(k_{0},k_{1})\in\N^{2}

where
k_{i}
is the number of
i

\alpha
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
If
\alpha\in\{\varnothing,0,1,00,11\}
, we can then set
\calS_{\alpha}(X)=\Phi_{k(\alpha)}(\calO_{t},X_{t})
with the monomials

\Phi_{k}(o,x)=\frac{o(\R)^{k_{0}}}{k_{0}!}\frac{x^{k_{1}}}{k_{1}!},\quad k\in\N^{2}.

Hence
\Phi_{k}(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})=\frac{t^{k_{0}}}{k_{0}!}\frac{X_{t}^{k_{1}}}{k_{1}!}
. For the last two terms on the right side of (
60
), recall the Lie bracket
[\partial_{o},\partial_{x}]=\partial_{xo}-\partial_{ox}
seen in
Section

3.1
. Then, we compute,

\displaystyle\partial_{ox}f\calS_{01}+\partial_{xo}f\calS_{10}

\displaystyle=\partial_{ox}f[\calS_{01}+\calS_{10}]+[\partial_{o},\partial_{x}]f\calS_{10}

\displaystyle=\partial_{ox}f\underbrace{\left[\int_{0}^{t}sdX_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}ds\right]}_{tX_{t}}+[\partial_{o},\partial_{x}]f\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}ds

\displaystyle=\partial_{ox}f\ \Phi_{(1,1)}(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})+[\partial_{o},\partial_{x}]f\ \Phi_{10}(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t}),

with
\Phi_{k}
defined above and
\Phi_{10}(o,x)=\int_{\R}yo(dy)
from the occupation time formula (
10
). With a slight abuse of notation, we can write
\Phi_{\gamma}
whether
\gamma=k\in\N^{2}
or
\gamma=\alpha\in\calA
when
\calS_{\alpha}(X)
only depend on the path through
(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})
such as
\alpha=10
. This includes more generally the sequence
\alpha^{(k)}=1\cdots 10\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}
,

. Indeed,

\Phi_{\alpha^{(k)}}(\tilde{\calO}_{t},X_{t})=\int_{\R}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\tilde{\calO}_{t}(dx)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{X_{s}^{k}}{k!}ds=\calS_{\alpha^{(k)}}(X).

Note in passing that the elements

fully characterize the standard time occupation measure itself.
It is expected that similar arguments combining integration by parts and the occupation time formula would generate basis occupation functionals
of higher orders, yet a precise derivation is beyond the scope of this work.

5.1
Exotic Derivatives

We gather examples of exotic options where the payoff can be expressed in terms of a standard time occupation functional. If

Let us start with commonly traded exotic derivatives, namely Asian and lookback options. Although viewing these products through the lens of occupied processes may be overkill, it is still worth mentioning that they belong to our framework.

Example 7
.

(Asian options)

These are contracts contingent upon the running average of the underlying, that is
\frac{1}{{T}}\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}dt
. Clearly, the chronology is irrelevant and from the occupation time formula, we have that

\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}dt=\int_{\R}xd\tilde{\calO}_{T}
. We can therefore express any Asian-type option in terms of the (standard time) occupation measure as well as the spot price when need be.
For instance, the payoff of an floating strike Asian call reads

\left(X_{T}-\frac{1}{{T}}\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}dt\right)^{+}=\left(X_{T}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\calO}_{T}(\R)}\int_{\R}xd\tilde{\calO}_{T}\right)^{+}=\varphi(\tilde{\calO}_{T},X_{T}).

Example 8
.

(Lookback options)

A derivative of lookback type entails the running maximum, minimum or range of the underlying asset. Any of these quantities can be retrieved from the occupation measure
\calO
(or
\tilde{\calO}
) as seen for the maximum in
Example

3
.
As an illustration, the payoff of a
floating lookback put option
can be expressed as

\left(X_{T}-\min_{t\leq{T}}X_{t}\right)^{+}=(X_{T}-m(\calO_{T}))^{+}=\varphi(\calO_{T},X_{T}),\quad m(o)=\inf\textnormal{supp}(o).

We end this section with a
popular class of contracts in the quantitative finance literature known as
occupation time derivatives

(72)

The crucial distincion between payoffs as in (

Example 9
.

(Parisian barrier options and soft call provision)
Barrier options are contracts with a knock-in (creation) or knock-out (termination) feature that is activated if the underlying asset breaches a prescribed level

the barrier.
As the range of the asset

\varphi(\calO_{T},X_{T})=\psi(\text{supp}(\calO_{T}),X_{T})

For example, an up-and-out digital option with barrier

Later,

In the case of a cumulative Parisian option, the payoff in (

\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\calO}_{T}(A^{b})\ <\ \delta\}}=\phi_{\delta}(\tilde{\calO}_{T}(A^{b})).

(74)

From the seller’s perspective, Parisian options are easier to hedge as the soft barrier effectively smoothen the Greeks.
Other instruments exhibit a similar relaxation feature.
One example is the soft call provision for fixed income products such as convertible bonds, where the issuer discontinues (calls) the contract once the underlying has spent a certain amount of time above a threshold.

Introduced by Société Générale Corporate & Investment Banking in 2007

5.3
Corridor Variance Swaps and Implied Expected Occupation Measure

Corridor Variance Swaps (CVS)
are over-the-counter instruments providing exposure to the volatility of an asset when the latter lies within a prescribed range. They belong to the larger family of weighted variance swaps; see
Bergomi 2016
,
Lee 2010
. For concreteness, consider an asset
X
with Bachelier-type dynamics
dX_{t}=\sigma_{t}dW_{t}
, where
W
is Brownian motion under a risk neutral measure
\Q

\sigma
is the volatility process.
The profit and loss (P&L) for the holder at expiry date is then given by

\text{P\&L}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1},x_{2}]}(X_{t})\ \sigma_{t}^{2}dt}_{\text{Floating Leg}}\;\ -\;\ \underbrace{K^{2}_{x_{1},x_{2}}}_{\mathclap{\text{Fixed Leg}}}\ ,\qquad x_{1}<x_{2}.

(76)

The levels
x_{1},x_{2}
defines the corridor during which the instanteneous variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
is accumulated while

is the

usually expressed in volatility terms. The latter is set such that the swap contract has zero initial cost, namely

K^{2}_{x_{1},x_{2}}=\E^{\Q}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1},x_{2}]}(X_{t})\ \sigma_{t}^{2}dt\right].

(77)

If
\calO
denotes the occupation measure of

, we directly see that

\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1},x_{2}]}(X_{t})\sigma_{t}^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1},x_{2}]}(X_{t})d\langle X\rangle_{t}=\calO_{T}([x_{1},x_{2}]).

(78)

The floating leg in (
76
) thus corresponds to the functional
\varphi(o)=\frac{o([x_{1},x_{2}])}{T}
. Although the maturity
T
is not directly available from the occupation measure, it is naturally known from the term sheet. There is a well-known connection between corridor variance swaps and local time
[
6
,
45
]

for some

\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1},x_{2}]}(X_{t})\ \sigma_{t}^{2}dt=\frac{\calO_{T}(\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(x)})}{2\varepsilon}
converges to
L_{T}^{x}

\Q-
almost surely.

6

Note that

\Q-a.s.

Corridor variance swaps offer several advantages over a standard variance swap which delivers the cumulative realized variance irrespective of the underlying price level.
