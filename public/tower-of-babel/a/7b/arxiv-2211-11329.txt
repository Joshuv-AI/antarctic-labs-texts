Simultaneous recovery of a locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle with the reverse time migration
Jianliang Li, Jiaqing Yang

Abstract
Consider the inverse acoustic scattering of time-harmonic point sources by an unbounded locally rough interface with bounded obstacles embedded in the lower half-space. A novel version of reverse time migration is proposed to reconstruct both the locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle. By a modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity associated with a planar interface, we obtain a modified imaging functional which has been shown that it always peaks on the local perturbation of the interface and on the embedded obstacle. Numerical examples are presented to demonstrate the effectiveness of the method. Keywords : inverse acoustic scattering, locally rough interfaces, embedded obstacles, reverse time migration.

Introduction

This paper concerns the two-dimensional acoustic scattering of time-harmonic point sources by a locally rough interface with
an embedded obstacle in the lower half-space. Given the incident wave, the direct scattering problem is to determine the distribution
of the scattered wave; while the inverse scattering problem aims to recover the locally rough interface as well as the embedded
obstacle from the measured scattered wave in a certain domain. These problems was motivated by significant applications in
diverse scientific areas such as medical imaging
[
1
]
and exploration geophysics
[
3
]
.

The main difficulty of the rough surface scattering problem is the unboundedness of the rough surface, which makes that the related
integral operators are non-compact and so the classical Fredholm alternative is not available. Based on a generalized Fredholm theory

[
23
,
24
]
, the well-posedness of the direct scattering problem by rough surfaces has been established in
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
41
,
46
]
by the integral equation method. In addition, we refer to
[
18
,
34
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
48
]
for the well-posedness of the direct rough surface scattering problem. Different from all previous works, if the rough surface is a local perturbation of a planar
surface, by introducing a special locally rough surface, the scattering problem can be transformed into an equivalent integral equation
defined in a bounded domain, for which the well-posedness follows from the classical Fredholm alternative, for details we refer to
[
25
,
36
]
for the scattering by the locally rough surface and
[
37
,
45
]
for the scattering by the locally rough interface with an embedded obstacle. For inverse problems, the reference
[
45
]
has established a global uniqueness which shows that the locally rough interface, the embedded obstacle and the wave number in the lower half-space can be uniquely determined by means of near-field measurements above the interface. Based on this uniqueness, a modified linear sampling method has been developed in
[
37
]
to solve the inverse problem of simultaneously reconstructing the locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle. However, it is worth pointing out that the linear sampling method in
[
37
]
is sensitive to the noise. For the inverse problem, there exists a variety of numerical algorithms.
For the inverse scattering by a planar surface with buried objects,
we refer to the MUSIC-type scheme
[
2
]
, the asymptotic factorization method
[
26
]
, the sampling method
[

]
, and the direct imaging algorithm
[
32
,

]
. For the inverse scattering by rough surfaces, we refer to iterative algorithms
[
7
,

,

,

]
, the algorithm based on the transformed field expansion
[
5
,
6
]
, the factorization method
[

]
, the singular source method
[
31
]
, the direct imaging method
[
39
,
40
]
, and linear sampling methods
[
25
,
36
,
47
]
.

The reverse time migration (RTM) method is a popular sample-type method which has been extensively applied in seismic imaging
[
4
]
and exploration geophysics
[
3
,
4
,
10
]
. This method first back-propagates the complex conjugated
data into the background medium and then computes the cross-correlation between the back-propagated field and the incident field
to output the imaging indicator, which has different behaviors when the sampling point is near the scatterer and far away from the scatterer. Based on this property, it is efficient, stable, and robust to noise. The key point of the RTM method is to establish the related
Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity, which plays a crucial role in the analysis of the indicator. For the inverse obstacle scattering problem, the
mathematical justification of the RTM method has been proved rigorously in
[
11
]
, which is based on an usual Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity associated with the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz operator in the free space. The results in
[
11
]
has
been extended to
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
29
]
to solve some other inverse obstacle scattering problems.
However, there are few results for the RTM method to recover unbounded rough surfaces since the usual Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity
in
[
11
]
is not valid. To overcome this difficulty, we has established a modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity associated with a background Green function in a two-layered medium separated by a special
locally rough surface and then extended the RTM method to reconstruct the locally rough surface without embedded obstacles,
see
[
35
]
for details.

Unfortunately, if there is a obstacle embedded in the lower half-space separated by a locally rough interface, both the usual Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity in
[
11
]
and the modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity in
[
35
]
are not valid to simultaneously reconstruct the locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle. Notice that the rough interface is locally perturbed, we are able to introduce a planar interface and then transform the scattering problem by the locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle into the scattering problem by several inhomogeneous mediums and the embedded obstacle. Based on this observation, we can establish the corresponding Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity associated with the background Green function in a two-layered medium separated by the planar interface. Thus, the mathematical justification of the RTM method has been proved rigorously, where we illustrate that the corresponding imaging indicator always peaks on the local perturbation of the interface and on the embedded obstacle, which is confirmed in the numerical examples.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In section 2, we introduce the mathematical model for the forward scattering problem, and present a novel version of the RTM method based on a modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity. In section 3, numerical examples are reported to illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The paper is concluded with some general remarks and discussions on the future research in section 4.

2
The RTM method

In this section, we shall introduce the mathematical model of the scattering problem by an unbounded, locally rough interface and an
obstacle in the lower half-space, and investigate the RTM method for this model.

Let the scattering interface be described by a curve

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}=f(x_{1})\}

(2.1)

where
f
is assumed to be a Lipschitz continuous function with compact support. It means that the interface
\Gamma
is a local
perturbation of the planar interface

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}=0\}.

The whole space is separated by
\Gamma
into two half-spaces denoted by

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>f(x_{1})\}\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega_{2}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}<f(x_{1})\}.

In the lower half-space
\Omega_{2}
, we assume that there is an bounded obstacle
D
with a smooth boundary
\partial D\in C^{2,\alpha}
for some Hölder exponent
0<\alpha\leq 1
. In this paper, for simplicity,
D
is assumed to be sound-soft which
means that a Dirichlet boundary condition is imposed on
\partial D
.

Consider the incident field
u^{i}(x,x_{s})
to be generated by a point source

\displaystyle u^{i}(x,x_{s})=\Phi_{\kappa}(x,x_{s}):=\frac{\rm i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa|x-x_{s}|)\quad{\rm for}\;\;x_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus(\overline{D}\cup\Gamma).

(2.2)

Here,
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind of order zero,
\kappa
is the wave number which is a piecewise constant
given by
\kappa=\kappa_{1}
for
x_{s}\in\Omega_{1}
and
\kappa=\kappa_{2}
for
x_{s}\in\Omega_{2}\setminus\overline{D}
, and for some
constant
\lambda
,
\Phi_{\lambda}
is the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation satisfying
\Delta\Phi_{\lambda}(x,x_{s})+\lambda^{2}\Phi_{\lambda}(x,x_{s})=-\delta_{x_{s}}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the distributional sense, where
\delta_{x_{s}}(x):=\delta(x-x_{s})
is
the Kronecker delta distribution. Then the scattering of
u^{i}
by the scatterer
(\Gamma,D)
can be reduced to the problem of seeking

u=u(x,x_{s})
satisfying that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta u+\kappa^{2}u=-\delta_{x_{s}}&\textrm{in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\[5.69054pt]
u=0&\textrm{on}\;\;\partial D,\\[5.69054pt]
\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|x|}u^{s}-{\rm i}\kappa u^{s}\right)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

in the distributional sense. Here,
u(x,x_{s})
and
u^{s}(x,x_{s})
are the total field and the scattered field, respectively, which are related by

u(x,x_{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(x,x_{s})+u^{s}(x,x_{s})&\textrm{in}\;\;\Omega_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
u^{s}(x,x_{s})&\textrm{in}\;\;\Omega_{2}\setminus\overline{D},\end{array}\right.

for
x_{s}\in\Omega_{1}
, and

u(x,x_{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{s}(x,x_{s})&\textrm{in}\;\;\Omega_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
\Phi_{\kappa_{2}}(x,x_{s})+u^{s}(x,x_{s})&\textrm{in}\;\;\Omega_{2}\setminus\overline{D},\end{array}\right.

for
x_{s}\in\Omega_{2}\setminus\overline{D}
, the wave number
\kappa=\kappa(x)
is a piecewise constant defined by

\kappa(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa_{1}&\textrm{for}\;\;x\in\Omega_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
\kappa_{2}&\textrm{for}\;\;x\in\Omega_{2}\setminus\overline{D},\end{array}\right.

and the last condition in Problem (
2.3
) is the well-known Sommerfeld radiation condition which holds uniformly for all
directions
\hat{x}:=x/|x|\in{\mathbb{S}}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x|=1\}
. Moreover, the Sommerfeld radiation condition allows that the
scattered field
u^{s}
has the following asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle u^{s}(x,x_{s})=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\{u^{\infty}(\hat{x},x_{s})+O\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right\}\quad{\rm for}\;|x|\to\infty

uniformly in all direction
\hat{x}\in{\mathbb{S}}
, where
u^{\infty}(\hat{x},x_{s})
is known as the far-field pattern of
u^{s}(x,x_{s})
.
It is shown by Theorem 3.4 in
[
45
]
that Problem (
2.3
) is well-posed.

B_{1}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\cap\Omega_{2}
and
B_{2}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}\cap\Omega_{1}

. The results obtained in this paper is also valid for the case of multiple local perturbations. For

, let

, defined by
\chi_{j}(x)=1

\chi_{j}(x)=0

. Let
D
be the obstacle embedded in
\Omega_{2}

D\cap B=\emptyset
. Since the RTM method is a sample-type method, we choose a rectangle

S

B\subset S

D\subset S
. And we choose a sufficiently large

, where
B_{R}

\Gamma_{s}:=\partial B_{R}

To introduce the RTM method, we first consider the scattering of the incident point source
u^{i}(x,x_{s})
given by (
2.2
) by the planar
interface
\Gamma_{0}
, which reads

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta G(x,x_{s})+\kappa_{0}^{2}(x)G(x,x_{s})=-\delta_{x_{s}}(x)&\textrm{in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\\[5.69054pt]
\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|x|}G(x,x_{s})-{\rm i}\kappa_{0}(x)G(x,x_{s})\right)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

in the distributional sense with the Sommerfeld radiation condition uniformly for all
\hat{x}\in{\mathbb{S}}
. Here, the wave number

\kappa_{0}(x)
is defined by
\kappa_{0}(x)=\kappa_{1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
and
\kappa_{0}(x)=\kappa_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}
. We refer to
[
30
,
45
]
for the explicit expression of
G(x,x_{s})
.

For
x_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
, we define

V(x,x_{s}):=u(x,x_{s})-G(x,x_{s}),

(2.5)

then it follows from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) that
V(x,x_{s})
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta V(x,x_{s})+\kappa^{2}(x)V(x,x_{s})=\beta\chi(x)G(x,x_{s})&\textrm{in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\[5.69054pt]
V(x,x_{s})=-G(x,x_{s})&\textrm{on}\;\;\partial D,\\[5.69054pt]
\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|x|}V(x,x_{s})-{\rm i}\kappa(x)V(x,x_{s})\right)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

where
\beta:=\kappa_{1}^{2}-\kappa_{2}^{2}
. We refer to Theorem 3.1 in
[
37
]
for the unique solvability of Problem (
2.6
).

Since we can obtain
G(x_{r},x_{s})
by solving Problem (
2.4
) through the Nyström method or the finite element method,
we can obtain the data
V(x_{r},x_{s})
from the measurement

and (
2.5
). As mentioned in the introduction, we first
back-propagate the complex conjugated data
\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})}
into the domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and then define the indicator as the
imaginary part of the cross-correlation of
G(\cdot,x_{s})
and the back-propagation field. More precisely, we summarize it in the following
algorithm.

Algorithm 1 (RTM for locally rough interfaces and embedded obstacles)
: Given the data
V(x_{r},x_{s})
for
r=1,2,...,N_{r}
and
s=1,2,...,N_{s}
.

•

Back-propagation: for
s=1,2,...,N_{s}
, solve the problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta W(x,x_{s})+\kappa_{0}^{2}(x)W(x,x_{s})=\frac{|\Gamma_{r}|}{N_{r}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{r}}\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})}\delta_{x_{r}}(x)\qquad\textrm{in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\\
&\;\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|x|}W(x,x_{s})-{\rm i}\kappa_{0}(x)W(x,x_{s})\right)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

to obtain the solution
W(x,x_{s})
.

•

Cross-correlation: for each sampling point
z\in S
, compute the indicator function

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}(z)=\kappa(x_{r}){\rm Im}\left\{\frac{|\Gamma_{s}|}{N_{s}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})W(z,x_{s})\right\}

and then plot the mapping
{\rm Ind}(z)
against
z
.

From Problem (
2.4
) and the linearity, we immediately see that

\displaystyle W(x,x_{s})=-\frac{|\Gamma_{r}|}{N_{r}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{r}}G(x,x_{r})\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})},

which implies that

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}(z)=-{\rm Im}\left\{\frac{|\Gamma_{s}|}{N_{s}}\frac{|\Gamma_{r}|}{N_{r}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{s}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{r}}\kappa(x_{r})\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})G(z,x_{r})\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})}\right\}\quad z\in S.

(2.7)

Observing that
G(z,x_{s})
,
G(z,x_{r})
and
V(x_{r},x_{s})
are continuous for
z\in S
,
x_{r}\in\Gamma_{r}
,
x_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
, it follows
from the trapezoid quadrature formula that
{\rm Ind}(z)
given by (
2.7
) is a discrete formula of the following continuous function

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\rm Ind}}(z)=-{\rm Im}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\kappa(x_{r})\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})G(z,x_{r})\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})}{\rm d}s(x_{s}){\rm d}s(x_{r}),\quad z\in S.

(2.8)

In the remaining part of this section, we restrict ourselves to demonstrate that the indicator
\widetilde{{\rm Ind}}(z)
enjoys the nice
feature that it always peaks on the local perturbation
B
and on the embedded obstacle
D
. To this end, we first introduce the following modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity associated with the Green function
G
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be the background Green’s function defined by (
2.4
). Then for any
x,z\in B_{R}\setminus\Gamma_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\overline{G(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)=2{\rm i}{\rm Im}G(x,z).

(2.9)

Proof.

For any
x,z\in B_{R}\setminus\Gamma_{0}
, we choose a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that the circles
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
B_{\varepsilon}(z)
with
x,z
as the center and
\varepsilon
as the radius contain in the domain
B_{R}
and
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Gamma_{0}=\emptyset
,
B_{\varepsilon}(z)\cap\Gamma_{0}=\emptyset
. Note that
G
and its normal derivative are continuous across
the interface
\Gamma_{0}
, thus a direct application of the Green theorem to
\overline{G(\cdot,x)}
and
G(\cdot,z)
in the domain
(B_{R}^{+}\cup B_{R}^{-})\setminus(B_{\varepsilon}(x)\cup B_{\varepsilon}(z))
yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(B_{R}^{+}\cup B_{R}^{-})\setminus(B_{\varepsilon}(x)\cup B_{\varepsilon}(z))}\left(\overline{G(\xi,x)}\Delta G(\xi,z)-\Delta\overline{G(\xi,x)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}\cup\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)\cup\partial B_{\varepsilon}(z)}\left(\overline{G(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}

where
B_{R}^{+}:=B_{R}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
,
B_{R}^{-}:=B_{R}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}
,
\nu(\xi)
denotes the unit outward normal to
\partial B_{R}
when
\xi\in\partial B_{R}
,
\nu(\xi)
denotes the unit normal to
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(y)
into the interior of
B_{\varepsilon}(y)
for
y\in\{x,z\}
. For the case
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, notice that
G(\xi,x):=\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)+G^{s}(\xi,x)
, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(\overline{\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(\overline{G^{s}(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{G^{s}(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle-G(x,z),\quad{\rm as}\;\;\varepsilon\to 0.

For the case
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}
, by a similar argument, we obtain
I_{2}\to-G(x,z)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{3}=\overline{G(z,x)}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\overline{G(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)=G(x,z)-\overline{G(z,x)}=2{\rm i}{\rm Im}G(x,z).

Here, we use the reciprocity
G(x,z)=G(z,x)
for
x,z\in B_{R}\setminus\Gamma_{0}
, which can be proven by a direct application of the
Green theorem and the Sommerfeld radiation condition. The proof is completed.
∎

With the aid of the modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity (
2.9
), we can obtain the following lemma which plays
an important role in the analysis of
\widetilde{{\rm Ind}}(z)

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
x,z\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\kappa(\xi)\overline{G(x,\xi)}G(\xi,z){\rm d}s(\xi)={\rm Im}G(x,z)+\zeta(x,z)

where
\zeta(x,z)
satisfies

\displaystyle|\zeta(x,z)|+|\nabla_{x}\zeta(x,z)|\leq CR^{-1}

uniformly for any
x,z\in S
.

Proof.

For
x,z\in S
, using Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle 2{\rm i}{\rm Im}G(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\overline{G(\xi,x)}\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\overline{G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,z)\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Bigg\{\overline{G(\xi,x)}\left[\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)G(\xi,z)\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle-G(\xi,z)\left[\frac{\overline{\partial G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}+{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\overline{G(\xi,x)}\right]\Bigg\}{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle+2{\rm i}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\kappa(\xi)\overline{G(\xi,x)}G(\xi,z){\rm d}s(\xi),

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\kappa(\xi)\overline{G(x,\xi)}G(\xi,z){\rm d}s(\xi)={\rm Im}G(x,z)+\zeta(x,z)\quad{\rm for}\;\;{\forall x,z\in S},

with

\displaystyle\zeta(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rm i}{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Bigg\{\overline{G(\xi,x)}\left[\frac{\partial G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)G(\xi,z)\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\;\;\;\;-G(\xi,z)\left[\frac{\overline{\partial G(\xi,x)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}+{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\overline{G(\xi,x)}\right]\Bigg\}{\rm d}s(\xi).

For
y\in\{x,z\}
, since

\displaystyle G(\xi,y)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad\frac{\partial G(\xi,y)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)G(\xi,y)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{3}{2}})

we conclude that

\displaystyle|\zeta(x,z)|\leq CR^{-1}

uniformly for
x,z\in S
. Moreover, due to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial x_{j}}=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left[\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa G(\xi,x)\right]=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{3}{2}})

for
j=1,2
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}\zeta(x,z)|\leq CR^{-1}

uniformly for
x,z\in S
. The proof is finished.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
V(x,x_{s})
be the solution of Problem (
2.6
). Then we have

\displaystyle V(x,x_{s})=\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,x)-\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}V(\xi,x_{s})\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)-\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)G(\xi,x)u(\xi,x_{s}){\rm d}\xi

for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus(\Gamma\cup\overline{D})
, where
\nu(\xi)
denotes the unit normal at
\xi\in\partial D
which directs into the interior of

Proof.

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus(\Gamma\cup\overline{D})
, we choose a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
and a sufficiently large
\rho>0
such that the disc
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
with
x
as the center and
\varepsilon
as the radius contains in the domain
\Omega_{\rho}:=B_{\rho}\setminus(\Gamma\cup\overline{D})
, where
B_{\rho}
stands for the disc with the origin as the center and
\rho
as the radius. Using the Green’s theorem to the functions
V(\xi,x_{s})
and
G(\xi,x)
in the domain
\Omega_{\rho}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\rho}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left[\Delta V(\xi,x_{s})G(\xi,x)-\Delta G(\xi,x)V(\xi,x_{s})\right]{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}\cup\partial D\cup\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left[\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}G(\xi,x)-\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}V(\xi,x_{s})\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle:=J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3},

(2.10)

where
\nu(\xi)
denotes the unit normal which directs into the interior of
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
when
\xi\in\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)
, and directs into the exterior of
B_{\rho}
when
\xi\in\partial B_{\rho}
. By (
2.4
) and (
2.6
), it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{\rho}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left[\Delta V(\xi,x_{s})G(\xi,x)-\Delta G(\xi,x)V(\xi,x_{s})\right]{\rm d}\xi=\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)G(\xi,x)u(\xi,x_{s}){\rm d}\xi.

(2.11)

For the item
J_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left[\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)V(\xi,x_{s})\right]G(\xi,x){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left[\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)G(\xi,x)\right]V(\xi,x_{s}){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle J_{11}-J_{12}.

Since

\displaystyle G(\xi,x)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)V(\xi,x_{s})=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{3}{2}})

it follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}J_{11}=0.

For the item
J_{12}
, we claim that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}|V(\xi,x_{s})|^{2}{\rm d}s(\xi)=O(1)\quad{\rm as}\;\;\rho\to\infty.

(2.12)

To show this, it is found by the Sommerfeld radiation condition that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left[\left|\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\right|^{2}+\kappa^{2}(\xi)|V(\xi,x_{s})|^{2}+2\kappa(\xi){\rm Im}\left(V(\xi,x_{s})\frac{\partial\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\right)\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)\to 0

(2.13)

as
\rho\to\infty
. Applying the Green theorem for
V(\xi,x_{s})
and
\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}
in the domain
B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}

implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}V(\xi,x_{s})\frac{\partial\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}{\rm d}s(\xi)

(2.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}}\left(|\nabla V(\xi,x_{s})|^{2}-\kappa^{2}(\xi)|V(\xi,x_{s})|^{2}\right){\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}V(\xi,x_{s})\frac{\partial\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}{\rm d}s(\xi)+\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)V(\xi,x_{s})\overline{G(\xi,x_{s})}{\rm d}\xi.

Taking the imaginary part of (
2.14
) and substituting it to (
2.13
) gives that

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa(\xi)}\left|\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\right|^{2}+\kappa(\xi)|V(\xi,x_{s})|^{2}\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\left\{\int_{\partial D}V(\xi,x_{s})\frac{\partial\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}{\rm d}s(\xi)-\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)V(\xi,x_{s})\overline{G(\xi,x_{s})}{\rm d}\xi\right\}

which implies that the claim (
2.12
) holds true. Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)G(\xi,x)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{3}{2}}),

(2.15)

it follows from (
2.12
), (
2.15
), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that
\lim\limits_{\rho\to\infty}J_{12}=0
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}J_{1}=0.

(2.16)

For the item
J_{3}
, if
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, using
G(\xi,x)=\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)+G^{s}(\xi,x)
, we have

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\kappa_{1}}(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}V(\xi,x_{s})\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(G^{s}(\xi,x)\frac{\partial V(\xi,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-\frac{\partial G^{s}(\xi,x)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}V(\xi,x_{s})\right){\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle J_{31}+J_{32}.

A direct calculation, using the mean value theorem, shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}J_{31}=-V(x,x_{s}).

For the item
J_{32}
, if
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\cap\Omega_{1}
, by the Green theorem, (
2.4
) and (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle J_{32}=\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(\Delta V(\xi,x_{s})G^{s}(\xi,x)-\Delta G^{s}(\xi,x)V(\xi,x)\right){\rm d}\xi=0.

If
x\in B_{1}
, combining the Green theorem, (
2.4
) and (
2.6
) shows that

\displaystyle J_{32}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left(\Delta V(\xi,x_{s})G^{s}(\xi,x)-\Delta G^{s}(\xi,x)V(\xi,x)\right){\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}G^{s}(\xi,x)u(\xi,x_{s}){\rm d}\xi\to 0\quad{\rm as}\;\;\varepsilon\to 0.

If
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}
, by a similar argument, we can obtain the same result. Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}J_{3}=-V(x,x_{s}).

(2.17)

It is found by (
2.10
), (
2.11
), (
2.16
), and (
2.17
) that the Lemma holds true. The proof is completed.

With the help of Lemma

, Lemma

, and Lemma

, we are in position to present the main

of the locally rough interface and the embedded obstacle.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any
z\in S
, let
\psi(\xi,z)
be the solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta\psi(\xi,z)+\kappa^{2}(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)=\beta\chi(\xi){\rm Im}G(\xi,z)&{\rm in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(\xi,z)=-{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)&{\rm on}\;\;\partial D,\\[5.69054pt]
\lim\limits_{|\xi|\rightarrow\infty}|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|\xi|}\psi(\xi,z)-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)\right)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.18)

and
\psi^{\infty}(\hat{\xi},z)
be the corresponding far-field pattern. Then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\rm Ind}}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}}\kappa(\hat{\xi})|\psi^{\infty}(\hat{\xi},z)|^{2}{\rm d}s(\hat{\xi})+\eta(z),\qquad\forall z\in S,

where
\|\eta(z)\|_{L^{\infty}(S)}\leq CR^{-1}
with some constant
C
depending on
B
and
D
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(z,x_{s})=\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\kappa(x_{r})G(z,x_{r})\overline{V(x_{r},x_{s})}{\rm d}s(x_{r}),

(2.19)

substituting the Green formula presented in Lemma
2.3
into
\widetilde{W}(z,x_{s})
gives that

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}(z,x_{s})=\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\gamma(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial\gamma(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)-\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\overline{u(\xi,x_{s})}\gamma(\xi,z){\rm d}\xi

with

\displaystyle\gamma(\xi,z):=\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\kappa(x_{r})G(z,x_{r})\overline{G(\xi,x_{r})}{\rm d}s(x_{r})={\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)

(2.20)

where we use Lemma
2.2
. According to (
2.8
) and (
2.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rm Ind}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})\widetilde{W}(z,x_{s}){\rm d}s(x_{s})

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\gamma(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial\gamma(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\phi_{1}(\xi,z)\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle+{\rm Im}\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\phi_{2}(\xi,z)\gamma(\xi,z){\rm d}\xi

where
\phi_{1}(\xi,z)
and
\phi_{2}(\xi,z)
are defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\xi,z):=\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})\overline{V(\xi,x_{s})}{\rm d}s(x_{s})

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\xi,z):=\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\kappa(x_{s})G(z,x_{s})\overline{u(\xi,x_{s})}{\rm d}s(x_{s}).

By the identity
u(\xi,x_{s})=V(\xi,x_{s})+G(\xi,x_{s})
and Lemma
2.2
, it is easily found that

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\xi,z)=\phi_{1}(\xi,z)+{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\overline{\zeta(\xi,z)}.

(2.22)

Since
V(\xi,x_{s})
satisfies Problem (
2.6
), it is easily checked that
\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}+\kappa^{2}(\xi)\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}=\beta\chi(\xi)\left[{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)\right]&{\rm in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},\\[8.53581pt]
\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}=-\left[{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)\right]&{\rm on}\;\;\partial D,\\[8.53581pt]
\lim\limits_{|\xi|\rightarrow\infty}|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\partial_{|\xi|}\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}\right)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.23)

where we use Lemma
2.2
to obtain the right hand term. Let
\psi(\xi,z)
and
\varphi(\xi,z)
solve Problem (
2.23
) expect the right hand term are replaced by
(\beta\chi(\xi){\rm Im}G(\xi,z),-{\rm Im}G(\xi,z))
and
(\beta\chi(\xi)\zeta(\xi,z),-\zeta(\xi,z))
, respectively. Then by the linearity we have

\displaystyle\overline{\phi_{1}(\xi,z)}=\psi(\xi,z)+\varphi(\xi,z).

(2.24)

Substituting (
2.20
), (
2.22
) and (
2.24
) into (
2.21
) implies that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rm Ind}(z)

\displaystyle=-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\bigg\{\left[{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\left[\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}+\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}+\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\left[{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)\right]\bigg\}{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+{\rm Im}\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\left[\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}+\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}+{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\overline{\zeta(\xi,z)}\right]\left[{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\zeta(\xi,z)\right]{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+{\rm Im}\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z){\rm d}\xi+\eta(z)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\psi(\xi,z){\rm d}s(\xi)+{\rm Im}\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z){\rm d}\xi+\eta(z),

where
\eta(z)
is defined by

\displaystyle\eta(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\zeta(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial\zeta(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\int_{\partial D}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\zeta(\xi,z)-\frac{\partial\zeta(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}\right]{\rm d}s(\xi)

\displaystyle+{\rm Im}\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\left[\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}\zeta(\xi,z)+\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)+\overline{\varphi(\xi,z)}\zeta(\xi,z)\right]{\rm d}s(\xi).

We choose a sufficiently large
\rho>0
such that
B\cup D\subset B_{\rho}
and apply the Green theorem for
\psi(\xi,z)
and
\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}
in the domain
B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}
to get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}}\Delta\psi(\xi,z)\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}}\left[\beta\chi(\xi){\rm Im}G(\xi,z)-\kappa^{2}(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)\right]\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi)+\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi)-\int_{B_{\rho}\setminus\overline{D}}|\nabla\psi(\xi,z)|^{2}{\rm d}\xi.

Inserting the imaginary part on both sides of the above equation gives that

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\left\{\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\psi(\xi,z){\rm d}s(\xi)+\beta\int_{B}\chi(\xi)\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm Im}G(\xi,z){\rm d}\xi\right\}={\rm Im}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rm Ind}(z)={\rm Im}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi)+\eta(z)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left[\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)\right]\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi)+\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\kappa(\xi)|\psi(\xi,z)|^{2}{\rm d}s(\xi)+\eta(z).

Since

\displaystyle\psi(\xi,z)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)=O(|\xi|^{-\frac{3}{2}})

we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left[\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}-{\rm i}\kappa(\xi)\psi(\xi,z)\right]\overline{\psi(\xi,z)}{\rm d}s(\xi)=0.

It follows from the Sommerfeld radiation condition that

\displaystyle\psi(\xi,z)=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|\xi|}}{|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\{\psi^{\infty}(\hat{\xi},z)+O\left(\frac{1}{|\xi|}\right)\right\}\quad{\rm for}\;|\xi|\to\infty

which leads to

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\kappa(\xi)|\psi(\xi,z)|^{2}{\rm d}s(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\kappa(\hat{\xi})|\psi^{\infty}(\hat{\xi},z)|^{2}{\rm d}s(\hat{\xi}).

Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\rm Ind}}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}}\kappa(\hat{\xi})|\psi^{\infty}(\hat{\xi},z)|^{2}{\rm d}s(\hat{\xi})+\eta(z),\qquad\forall z\in S.

In the remaining part of the proof, we restrict ourselves to the estimate of
\eta(z)
. For
\xi\in S
,
z\in S
, due to Lemma
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle|\zeta(\xi,z)|+|\nabla_{\xi}\zeta(\xi,z)|\leq CR^{-1}.

(2.25)

Notice that

\displaystyle{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{4}J_{0}(\sigma|\xi-z|)+{\rm Im}G^{s}(\xi,z)&{\rm for}\;\;\xi,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\;{\rm or}\;\xi,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-},\\[8.53581pt]
{\rm Im}G^{s}(\xi,z)&{\rm for}\;\;\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+},z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}\;{\rm or}\;\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-},z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\end{array}\right.

where

denotes the Bessel function of order zero,
\sigma=\kappa_{1}
for
\xi,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
and
\sigma=\kappa_{2}
for
\xi,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{-}

and

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)\right|+\left|\frac{\partial{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\right|\leq C

(2.27)

for
\xi\in B\cup D
and

and

2.23

and

[
37

leads to

\displaystyle\|\psi(\xi,z)\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)}+\left\|\frac{\partial\psi(\xi,z)}{\partial\nu(\xi)}\right\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)}+\|\psi(\xi,z)\|_{L^{2}(B)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)\|_{L^{2}(B)}+\|{\rm Im}G(\xi,z)\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)}

(2.29)

where we use the trace theorem in the first step of (
2.28
) and (
2.29
). Thus, with the help of (

\displaystyle\|\eta(z)\|_{L^{\infty}(S)}\leq CR^{-1}

with

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed an extended RTM method to simultaneously recover a locally rough interface and an embedded object
in the lower half-space from near-field measurements. The main idea is based on constructing a modified Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity
associated with the planar interface. Numerical experiments showed that the novel RTM algorithm can provide an accurate and
stable reconstruction for a large number of locally rough interfaces and embedded obstacles, even for piecewise continuous interfaces.
Furthermore, the reconstructions can be regarded as a good initial guess for an iterative algorithm to obtain a more accurate result.
As far as we know, this is the first RTM method to simultaneously recover an unbounded scatterer and a bounded scatterer. It is easily seen that the RTM method proposed in Theorem
2.4
depends crucially on the priori information that the interface is locally perturbed. However, if the rough surface is non-local, it is unclear how to establish the related Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity and then
extend this method to reconstruct the non-local rough surface. We hope to report the progress on this topic in the future.
