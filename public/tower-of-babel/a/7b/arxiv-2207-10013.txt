On Entropic Tilting and Predictive Conditioning
Emily Tallman, Mike West

Abstract
Entropic tilting (ET) is a Bayesian decision-analytic method for constraining distributions to satisfy defined targets or bounds for sets of expectations. This report recapitulates the foundations and basic theory of ET for conditioning predictive distributions on such constraints, recognising the increasing interest in ET in several application areas. Contributions include new results related to connections with regular exponential families of distributions, and the extension of ET to relaxed entropic tilting (RET) where specified values for expectations define bounds rather than exact targets. Additional new developments include theory and examples that condition on quantile constraints for modified predictive distributions and examples relevant to Bayesian forecasting applications.



Keywords:
Bayesian forecasting, Constrained forecasting, Exponential family, Küllback-Leibler, Maximum entropy, Predictive inference, Quantile constraints, Variational methods

ET Background, Theory and Insights

ET is Bayesian decision-analytic and variational method for conditioning predictive distributions on defined sets of constraints, and is becoming of increasing interest in econometrics, finance and business applications
(
Robertson
et al. 2005
;
Krüger
et al. 2017
;
West 2021
;
Tallman and
West 2022
, e.g.)
among other areas. The basic definitions, properties, and some new theoretical results and insights are noted here.

Setting: ET Constraints and Decision

Suppose
\mathbf{y}
is a random
m-
vector with (predictive) p.d.f.
p(\mathbf{y})
. The random vector may be discrete, continuous, or mixed. Notation for expectations will ignore mathematical details and play-out as if
\mathbf{y}
is continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, for simplicity and clarity of presentation.
Refer to
p(\mathbf{y})
as the p.d.f. of the
baseline distribution
for
\mathbf{y}
.

Now consider all possible p.d.f.s
f(\mathbf{y})
with the same support as
p(\mathbf{y})
. A set of
moment constraints
is to be assessed and/or imposed. In the standard formulation of ET, these specify exact “target” values of a set of expectations under any p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y})
that is consistent with these values. That is, define the expectations

E_{f}(s_{j}(\mathbf{y}))=s_{j}
, for
j=1{:}q
for some chosen set of
q

score
functions
s_{j}(\mathbf{y})
with specified target/constrained values
s_{j}
. Here the expectations are with respect to any chosen p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y})
that satisfies these constraints. It is convenient for theoretical development to take
s_{1}(\mathbf{y})=1
and
s_{1}=1
to represent the fact that
f(\mathbf{y})
is a p.d.f., i.e., to define the required normalization constraint, although this is not necessary in implementations. The other constraints are problem specific.
In vector notation, the constraints are
E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=\mathbf{s}
with
q-
vectors
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=(s_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,s_{q}(\mathbf{y}))^{\prime}
and target vector
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{q})^{\prime}.

The standard ET framework defines the following decision problem:
choose
f(\mathbf{y})
as the Küllback-Leibler closest p.d.f. to the baseline
p(\mathbf{y})
subject to these constraints.
ET uses the KL divergence
of

p(\mathbf{y})

from

f(\mathbf{y})
,

{KL}_{p|f}=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}.

The connotation is that
f(\mathbf{y})
is best/optimal/desired and
p(\mathbf{y})
is a step towards it/an approximation, since
f(\mathbf{y})
is the anchor distribution in this KL direction. This is the same “direction” as used in variational Bayes’ methods. The “other direction” is more natural and relevant in other settings
(
Gruber and
West 2016
;
Gruber and
West 2017
, e.g. such as used in sequential Bayesian analysis and forecasting in simultaneous graphical dynamic linear models in)
.

ET Solution

Assuming that a solution exists, it has the unique form

f(\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=k_{{\bm{\tau}}}p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))

(1)

where
{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j=1{:}q}\tau_{j}s_{j}(\mathbf{y}),
the vector

{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})^{\prime}
is a parameter that ensures
f(\mathbf{y})
satisfies the specified expectation constraints, and

k_{{\bm{\tau}}}
is the normalizing constant.

Existence of the solution depends on the form of
p(\mathbf{y})
and of the score functions
s_{j}(\mathbf{y})
(and, in some cases, on the choices of values of target/expected scores
s_{j}
for given choices of score functions). It is easy to see that some choices of score functions can lead to no feasible solution. As easy examples, if
p(\mathbf{y})
is Cauchy or log-T and
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y},
then no solution exists. Other cases are more subtle: existence of a solution corresponds to the existence of the moment generating function of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
when
\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y}).
It is obvious that a solution exists when the score functions are bounded in
\mathbf{y},
and the role of scores as utility functions encourages choices of bounded forms as natural in some applications.

Whatever the forms of the scores, the following discussion assumes that a solution exists. For a given
{\bm{\tau}},
the ET p.d.f. of
eqn. (
1
) can be written in the exponential family form

f(\mathbf{y})=p(\mathbf{y})\exp\{{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})-c({\bm{\tau}})\}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where}\hskip 10.00002ptc({\bm{\tau}})=-\log(k_{{\bm{\tau}}}).

(2)

It trivially follows that

{KL}_{p|f}\equiv\kappa(\mathbf{s})={\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}-c({\bm{\tau}})

(3)

where
\mathbf{s}=E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})).
Hence, in the ET analysis
at the specified target
\mathbf{s},
the implied optimal
{\bm{\tau}}\equiv{\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{s})
delivers the minimised value of the KL divergence as
\kappa(\mathbf{s}).

Comments and Perspectives on ET Settings

The analysis requires specification of score functions
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
and target values
\mathbf{s}.
For the former,
It is apparent that ET can be used to constrain/adapt a p.d.f. to have a given mean and/or variance, or to have a set of specified quantiles using indicator score functions. Examples are given in Sections
4
and
5
below.

ET analysis has major roles in exploring/interrogating whether sets of specified constraints are consistent with the baseline, and in exploring “perturbations” of the baseline through choices of target scores
\mathbf{s}
that represent modest deviations from that implied by the baseline. Define

\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}=E_{p}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))
to be the expected score under the baseline, noting that this corresponds to
{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{0}
,
c(\mathbf{0})=0
and, of course,

\kappa(\mathbf{0})=0
is the absolute minimum KL divergence. Then, assuming
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
is a vector of utilities such that higher values are preferred, then choosing a target score vector
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}
for some vector
{\bm{\epsilon}}
with positive elements tilts the baseline towards a p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y})
with larger expected utilities. Taking “small” positive entries in
{\bm{\epsilon}},
– perhaps anchored as percent increases of values in
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}
– defines a perspective of exploring small perturbations of the baseline.

Bayes’ Factor Interpretation

The above development provides entrée to mapping the KL divergence to an interpretable scale. Suppose
f(\mathbf{y})
represents the known distribution of
\mathbf{y}
and
p(\mathbf{y})
a different distribution, linked via the ET construction. Then, explicitly, an observation at a value
\mathbf{y}
leads to the implied log
Bayes’ factor in favour of
f(\cdot)
over
p(\cdot)
given by
{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})-c({\bm{\tau}}).
Hence the minimum KL value

\kappa(\mathbf{s})
is precisely the
expected log Bayes’ factor
in favour of
f(\cdot)
from a future
\mathbf{y}\sim f(\cdot).
This anchors interpretation to values and ranges of log Bayes’ factors (a.k.a. log likelihood ratios) in this “two hypotheses” setting, by mapping to the absolute probability scale; e.g., converting to the implied posterior probability on
f(\cdot)
under a 50:50 prior. The realised
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
in any ET analysis can then be calibrated to the probability scale for communication.

Implementing ET

Computing the value of

{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})^{\prime}
to normalize and satisfy the (other) expectation constraints delivers the result in any given example.
At the ET optimal
f(\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})),
the constraints are

\mathbf{s}=E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

in the general notation. This is equivalent to
{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{\tau}})=\mathbf{0}
where the
q-
vector function
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{\tau}})=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\{\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{s}\}\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))p(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}.

(4)

There are many simple examples as well as practically important examples where the optimizing value of
{\bm{\tau}}
can be evaluated analytically. In other settings, finding the optimal
{\bm{\tau}}
vector can often be done using Newton-Raphson or other standard algorithms to solve
{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{\tau}})=\mathbf{0}.

Simulation from
p(\mathbf{y})
will often be used to provide Monte Carlo approximations to the required expectations here.

Simulation of ET Distribution

As noted above, simulation from the baseline distriubution will often be used to provide Monte Carlo approximations to expectations in the ET analysis. Given an ET solution, this naturally leads to importance sampling (IS) as a main tool in summarising predictions under the ET p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y}).
This was pointed out by
Robertson
et al. 2005
; see more recent examples in
West 2021
and
Tallman and
West 2022
.

Suppose a Monte Carlo (MC) random sample
{\cal Y}_{p,I}=\{\mathbf{y}_{i},\ i=1{:}I\}
of size
I.
is drawn from the
baseline distribution.
Expectations under
p(\mathbf{y})
are approximated by sample averages over the MC sample
{\cal Y}_{p,I}
.
The ET analysis defines the modified predictive distribution
f(\mathbf{y})
in which the MC sample is reweighted: each sample
\mathbf{y}_{i}
has IS weight
w_{i}\propto\exp\{{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i})\}
and
\sum_{i=1{:}I}w_{i}=1.

Standard IS ideas and methodology then apply. For example, the effective sample size
ESS=1/\sum_{i=1{:}I}w_{i}^{2}
is one measure of how “close”
p(\mathbf{y})
is to
f(\mathbf{y}),
representing one numerical measure of how consistent
p(\mathbf{y})
is with the imposed ET constraints. Further, the weighted IS sample can be converted to an equally-weighted sample from
f(\mathbf{y})
(usually with repeat values, of course) simply by resampling (with replacement) from the set
{\cal Y}_{p,I}
according to the weights
w_{i}.

Then expectations under
f(\mathbf{y})
are then approximated by simple averages over the resampled set.

Relaxed Entropic Tilting (RET)

An important contribution of this report is the result that the ET result is relevant– and optimal– under broader considerations. Specifically, the results from the modified perspective in which
\mathbf{s}
define
bounded expectations
on score functions, not just explicit/exact expectation constraints. See this as follows.

Again suppose (with no loss of generality) that each score vector element
s_{j}(\mathbf{y})
represents a utility dimension such that higher values are preferable. Now consider relaxed moment constraints

E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))\geq\mathbf{s}>\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}
, i.e., elementwise,
E_{f}(s_{j}(\mathbf{y}))\geq s_{j}>s_{0,j}
, for a specified
lower bound
target vector
\mathbf{s}
. Here

\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}=E_{p}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))
corresponds to
{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{0}
,
c(\mathbf{0})=0
and, of course,

\kappa(\mathbf{0})=0
is the absolute minimum KL divergence.

Then optimising the choice of
{\bm{\tau}}
at
\mathbf{s}
optimises over the bounded constrained set
E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))\geq\mathbf{s}
. This is a simple consequence of convexity.
As detailed in Section
3
below,
the minimised KL metric
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
is a convex function of
\mathbf{s}
, and hence

\kappa(\mathbf{s}_{1})>\kappa(\mathbf{s})>\kappa(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}})=0
for any
\mathbf{s}_{1}>\mathbf{s}>\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}
elementwise. Hence the ET optimal vector
{\bm{\tau}}
at target
\mathbf{s}
minimises the KL divergence over all possible target scores
\mathbf{s}_{1}>\mathbf{s}.

1
highlights this with a schematic emphasising the exploration of modest perturbations of the baseline using the ET approach.

f(\mathbf{y})
indexed by tilting vector
{\bm{\tau}},
with
{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{0}
at the baseline
p(\mathbf{y}).

{\bm{\tau}}
such that the implied expected scores
\mathbf{s}_{{\bm{\tau}}}=E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))
exceed
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}
elementwise. The darker shaded subregion refines this, identifying
{\bm{\tau}}
such that

\mathbf{s}_{{\bm{\tau}}}\geq\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}
for some non-negative vector
{\bm{\epsilon}}
with at least one positive element. The point
{\bm{\tau}}^{*}
represents the unique distribution with expected score exactly
\mathbf{s}_{{\bm{\tau}}^{*}}=\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}
; this is the KL

4.2
; in other examples the boundaries are convex and maybe piecewise linear or non-linear.

ET and MaxEnt

The KL divergence that ET minimises is

{KL}_{p|f}=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=H_{p|f}-H_{f}

where

H_{f}=-\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
is the entropy (also known as the differential entropy) of
f(\mathbf{y})
and

H_{p|f}=-\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(p(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
is the cross-entropy of
p(\mathbf{y})
under
f(\mathbf{y}).
Thus
{KL}_{p|f}
measures entropy in
p(\mathbf{y})

relative to
the absolute entropy
H_{f}
of
f(\mathbf{y})
. In the 1960s onwards for several decades, so-called “objective Bayesian” developments included a main theme in scientific research pioneered by statistical physicists that defined the
maximum entropy (MaxEnt)
approach to specifying priors based on sets of constraints taken as partial prior information. The seminal work is by Edwin T. Jaynes
(
Jaynes 1957
, available at
http://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf
)
and MaxEnt has a long history since then at the interface between statistics, information theory and statistical physics.
The basic idea underlying MaxEnt is to choose
f(\mathbf{y})
to satisfy some set of constraints, but otherwise to be “least informative” in the sense of maximizing information-theoretic uncertainty characterized explicitly via entropy.

MaxEnt can be viewed as special cases of ET in which the baseline distribution has a “flat” p.d.f.
p(\mathbf{y}),
i.e., a limiting (possibly improper) “uninformative” baseline, so that
H_{p|f}
is constant for any
f(\mathbf{y}).
In such cases, minimizing
{KL}_{p|f}
is equivalent to maximizing
H_{f}
, again subject to the constraints. Hence this specialization of ET reproduces MaxEnt. The utility of MaxEnt as a constructive approach to defining distributions based on sets of expectations is clearly limited, as this result shows. The requirement of a uniform baseline
p(\mathbf{y})
leads to the same limitations and issues as encountered in the historical objective Bayesian (fruitless) search for globally uniformative priors (notable among which is the fact that uniformity is transformation dependent, in general).

ET Theory: Proofs and More Insights

Direct Proof of ET Solution: Discrete Cases

The proof is easy and direct using Lagrange multipliers. Here
\mathbf{y}
has support
\mathbf{y}\in\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}\}
with baseline p.d.f.
p_{i}=p(\mathbf{y}_{i})
and any p.d.f. that satisfies the constraints
f_{i}=f(\mathbf{y}_{i})
over
i=1{:}n.
Write
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})^{\prime}.
The ET problem is to choose
\mathbf{f}
and
{\bm{\tau}}
to minimise

R(\mathbf{f},{\bm{\tau}})=\sum_{i=1{:}n}\log(f_{i}/p_{i})f_{i}+\sum_{j=1{:}q}\tau_{j}\{s_{j}-\sum_{i=1{:}n}s_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{i})f_{i}\}

where
{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})^{\prime}
is a vector of Lagrange multipliers that enforce the constraints.

Differentiate with respect to any
f_{i}
and equate to zero to give

0=\frac{\partial R(\mathbf{f},{\bm{\tau}})}{\partial f_{i}}=1+\log(f_{i}/p_{i})-\sum_{j=1{:}q}\tau_{j}s_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{i})=1+\log(f_{i}/p_{i})-{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i})

so that

\log(f_{i})=\log(p_{i})-1+{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i}).

Differentiating
R(\mathbf{f},{\bm{\tau}})
a second time yields the Hessian matrix that is diagonal with
i^{th}
diagonal element
1/f_{i}
; this is positive definite ensuring a minimum.
This then results in the minimizing solution

f_{i}\propto p_{i}\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i})),\ i=1{:}n,

also showing that the “tilting” parameter vector
{\bm{\tau}}
is the vector of Lagrange multipliers.

The above proof can be replayed with
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{s},{\bm{\tau}}
including elements
s_{1}(\mathbf{y})=s_{1}=1
and
\tau_{1}
that explicitly serves to define normalization of
f(\cdot).
This is not necessary, however, as the above proof defines the ET solution up to proportionality, and normalization follows.

Proof in Continuous and General Cases: The Informal Proof

The obvious limiting argument defines the solution in continuous cases based on the above, easily-derived solution in the discrete case. Take
n\to\infty
with
p_{i}\to p(\mathbf{y}_{i})\delta\mathbf{y}_{i}
and
f_{i}\to f(\mathbf{y}_{i})\delta\mathbf{y}_{i}
for differentials
\delta\mathbf{y}_{i}.
Then any sum with weights
f_{i}
becomes an integral with respect to
f(\mathbf{y})\delta\mathbf{y},
so the ET constraints are general expectations. The general solution then trivially follows as the limiting form of the discrete case above,
f(\mathbf{y})\delta\mathbf{y}\propto p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))\delta\mathbf{y}
for all
\mathbf{y}.

General Mathematical/First-Principles Proof

Formal functional calculus
(
Fox 1987
)
delivers a rigourous mathematical proof. Whatever the sample space, let
a(f)
be any functional of the p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y}).

Consider the functional derivative of
a(f)
with respect to the specific value
f(\mathbf{y})
at any chosen
\mathbf{y}
in the support of
f(\cdot)
, namely

\frac{\partial a(f)}{\partial f(\mathbf{y})}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{A_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})}{\epsilon}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{where}\hskip 10.00002ptA_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})=a(f+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}})-a(f)

and where
\delta_{\mathbf{y}}
is the point-mass at
\mathbf{y},
i.e., the Dirac delta function
\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})=1
if
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}
and 0 otherwise.
For small
\epsilon
the function
f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})
is a local perturbation of
f(\mathbf{y}),
corresponding to the concept and definition of standard derivatives of functions.

Examples of interest here are as follows.

1.

Expected Score. Suppose
a(f)=\int_{\mathbf{y}}s(\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
for a specified scalar score function
s(\mathbf{y})
that does not depend on
f(\cdot).
Then

A_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbf{y}}s(\mathbf{y})\left\{f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})\right\}d\mathbf{y}-\int_{\mathbf{y}}s(\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=\epsilon\int_{\mathbf{y}}s(\mathbf{y})\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=\epsilon s(\mathbf{y})\\

so that

\frac{\partial a(f)}{\partial f(\mathbf{y})}=s(\mathbf{y}).

2.

Entropy. Suppose
a(f)=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
. Then

A_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\left\{\log\left\{f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})\right\}\left\{f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})\right\}-\log(f(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})\right\}d\mathbf{y}.

Now, for any
f
and small
z
note that
\log(f+z)=\log(f)+z/(z+f)+o(z^{2})
so that the integrand defining
A_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})
is

\displaystyle\left\{\log(f(\mathbf{y}))+\frac{\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})}{f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})}+o(\epsilon^{2})\right\}\left\{f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})\right\}-\log(\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left\{\log(f(\mathbf{y}))+f(\mathbf{y})/(f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}))\right\}\delta_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y})+o(\epsilon^{2}).

On integration this gives
A_{f,\epsilon}(\mathbf{y})=\epsilon\left\{\log(f(\mathbf{y}))+f(\mathbf{y})/(f(\mathbf{y})+\epsilon)\right\}+o(\epsilon^{2})
and so

\frac{\partial a(f)}{\partial f(\mathbf{y})}=\log(f(\mathbf{y}))+1.

The above examples underlie the formal proof of the ET result. The goal is to find
f(\mathbf{y})
to minimise

R(f,{\bm{\tau}})=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}+\sum_{j=1{:}q}\tau_{j}\{s_{j}-\int_{\mathbf{y}}s_{j}(\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\}

where
{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})^{\prime}
is a vector of Lagrange multipliers that enforce the constraints.
Functional differentiation with respect to
f(\mathbf{y})
at any value
\mathbf{y}
now exploits the results of the above examples and defines the optimizing p.d.f. Explicitly,

\frac{\partial R(f,{\bm{\tau}})}{\partial f(\mathbf{y})}=\log(f(\mathbf{y}))+1-\log(p(\mathbf{y}))-{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=1+\log(f(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))-{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}).

Note that this is the direct general extension of the form above in the discrete case. Then,
equating this functional derivative to zero yields the ET result

f(\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})).
Further, it is easily confirmed that the functional second derivative of
R(f,{\bm{\tau}})
with respect to
f(\mathbf{y})
at any
\mathbf{y}
is just

1/f(\mathbf{y})
; this is positive everywhere on the support hence the solution is a minimum.

General Proof via Euler–Lagrange

The general proof above is from first-principles and is instructive in terms of understanding functional calculus. The result can alternatively be directly deduced from (a special case of) the Euler–Lagrange (E-L) equation for functional derivatives that builds on such first-principles
(
Fox 1987
)
. The relevant E-L theory is as follows.
Suppose
H(\mathbf{y},z)
is a real-valued function of the vector
\mathbf{y}
and a scalar
z
and that
H(\cdot,z)
is differentiable in
z
. Consider a functional of the p.d.f.
f(\cdot)
of the form

a(f)=\int_{\mathbf{y}}H(\mathbf{y},f(\mathbf{y}))d\mathbf{y}
where the integral is assumed to exist. Then the functional derivative of
a(f)
with respect to a point value
f(\mathbf{y})
at any given
\mathbf{y}
is

\frac{\partial a(f)}{\partial f(\mathbf{y})}=h(\mathbf{y},f(\mathbf{y}))\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{where}\hskip 20.00003pth(\mathbf{y},z)=\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{y},z)}{\partial z}.

Now take
z>0
and note the following.

•

The choice
H(\mathbf{y},z)=z\log(z/p(\mathbf{y}))+{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}-z{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
implies that
a(f)=R(f,{\bm{\tau}}),
and
h(\mathbf{y},z)=1+\log(z)-{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
.

•

The choice
H(\mathbf{y},z)=1+\log(z)-{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
has derivative
h(\mathbf{y},z)=1/z.

These observations deliver the above ET result directly. Note that, as in the discrete case, ensuring normalisation might use elements
s_{1}(\mathbf{y})=s_{1}=1
and
\tau_{1}
, though this is not necessary.

The Most General, Statistical, Intuitive and Contextual Proof

The really insightful proof that covers all cases is as follows. Consider
any
distribution with p.d.f.
g(\mathbf{y})
that satisfies the ET constraint
E_{g}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=\mathbf{s},
i.e., focus only on these relevant distributions. One of these is
f(\mathbf{y})
defined above for the vector
{\bm{\tau}}
implied by the chosen target
\mathbf{s},
but there are many other distributions consistent with that target expectation vector. Now note that, for any such p.d.f.
g(\mathbf{y}),
it is immediate that

\displaystyle{KL}_{p|g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(g(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))g(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(g(\mathbf{y})/f(\mathbf{y}))g(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}+\int_{\mathbf{y}}\log(f(\mathbf{y})/p(\mathbf{y}))g(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{KL}_{f|g}+\int_{\mathbf{y}}\{{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})-c({\bm{\tau}})\}g(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{KL}_{f|g}+{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}-c({\bm{\tau}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{KL}_{f|g}+{KL}_{p|f}

with the last step following since
{KL}_{p|f}=\kappa(\mathbf{s})={\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}-c({\bm{\tau}})
from eqn. (
3
).
Now,
{KL}_{f|g}
is non-negative and positive unless
g(\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{y}).

Hence
{KL}_{p|g}\geq{KL}_{p|f}
with equality only when
g(\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{y}).
This proves the result: among
all
distributions satisfying the expectation constraint defined by the target
\mathbf{s},
the ET p.d.f.
f(\mathbf{y})
minimises the KL divergence.

Exponential Family Theory

ET Defines Exponential Families

For any chosen target
\mathbf{s}
, if an ET solution in
{\bm{\tau}}
exists, it is unique. Reciprocally, any feasible choice of
{\bm{\tau}}
implies the corresponding
\mathbf{s},
so that any choice of
{\bm{\tau}}
defines the p.d.f. in eqn. (
1
) with the implied
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}({\bm{\tau}}).
As
\mathbf{s}
, and hence
{\bm{\tau}},
varies it is of theoretical interest to note that the analysis defines– as has already been noted– a class of exponential family distributions with p.d.f.s in eqn. (
2
).
Some comments and theoretical points arising for this defined family of distributions are noted
(and see
Barndorff-Nielsen 1978
, for general background on exponential families, convexity and other theoretical aspects)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{y}\sim f(\mathbf{y})
as defined in eqn. (
2
) for a given vector of parameters
{\bm{\tau}}.
As
{\bm{\tau}}
varies, this defines a family of distributions representing entropically tilted variations of the baseline
p(\mathbf{y}).
In exponential family terminology,
{\bm{\tau}}
is the natural parameter of the distribution and

c({\bm{\tau}})
is known as the
cumulant function
of the parameter vector
{\bm{\tau}}.
The analysis here relates to
regular
exponential families in which (i) the support of
f(\mathbf{y})
does not depend on parameters
{\bm{\tau}};

(ii) the elements of
{\bm{\tau}}
are continuous and
c({\bm{\tau}})
is at least twice-differentiable in
{\bm{\tau}}
;
and (iii) the
{\bm{\tau}}
parameters are not subject to constraints (although this can be relaxed).

Convexity in Tilting Parameters

In regular exponential families, the parameter space for
{\bm{\tau}}
is a convex set and
c({\bm{\tau}})
is a convex function from well-known theory of regular exponential families
(
Barndorff-Nielsen 1978
)
.

The standard proof is as follows. Take any two values
{\bm{\tau}}_{1},{\bm{\tau}}_{2}
with corresponding p.d.f.s

f_{j}(\mathbf{y})=p(\mathbf{y})\exp({\bm{\tau}}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})-c({\bm{\tau}}_{j}))
and
\exp(c({\bm{\tau}}_{j}))=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\exp({\bm{\tau}}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))p(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}
for
j=1,2.

Consider a third distribution with parameter
a_{1}{\bm{\tau}}_{1}+a_{2}{\bm{\tau}}_{2}
for any
a_{1}\in(0,1)
and
a_{2}=1-a_{1}.
Then

\exp\{c(a_{1}{\bm{\tau}}_{1}+a_{2}{\bm{\tau}}_{2})\}=\int_{\mathbf{y}}\exp\{(a_{1}{\bm{\tau}}_{1}+a_{2}{\bm{\tau}}_{2})^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})\}p(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=\int_{\mathbf{y}}g_{1}(\mathbf{y})g_{2}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

where
g_{j}(\mathbf{y})=\{\exp({\bm{\tau}}_{j}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))p(\mathbf{y})\}^{a_{j}}
for
j=1,2
.
Now recall Hölder’s inequality: for
any two functions
g_{j}(\mathbf{y})>0
,
j=1,2,
for which the integrals exist and any
a_{1},a_{2}
as above,

\int_{\mathbf{y}}g_{1}(\mathbf{y})g_{2}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\leq\left\{\int_{x}g_{1}(\mathbf{y})^{1/a_{1}}d\mathbf{y}\right\}^{a_{1}}\left\{\int_{x}g_{1}(\mathbf{y})^{1/a_{2}}d\mathbf{y}\right\}^{a_{2}}.

This leads immediately to

\exp\{c(a_{1}{\bm{\tau}}_{1}+a_{2}{\bm{\tau}}_{2})\}\leq\exp\{a_{1}c({\bm{\tau}}_{1})\}\exp\{a_{2}c({\bm{\tau}}_{2})\}
which, on taking logs, yields

c(a_{1}{\bm{\tau}}_{1}+a_{2}{\bm{\tau}}_{2})\leq a_{1}c({\bm{\tau}}_{1})+a_{2}c({\bm{\tau}}_{2})
so that
c(\cdot)
is convex.

Some Moments of Score Functions

The derivative (vector) of
c({\bm{\tau}})
defines the mean vector of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}),
i.e.,

\mathbf{s}\equiv E_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=\frac{\partial c({\bm{\tau}})}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}.

(5)

Since
c({\bm{\tau}})
is convex, each element
s_{i}
of
\mathbf{s}
is an increasing function of the corresponding element
\tau_{i}
of
{\bm{\tau}}
, and vice-versa.
Note, however and importantly, an element
s_{i}
may or may not be increasing in
\tau_{j}
for
j\neq i.

The second derivative (matrix) of
c({\bm{\tau}})
defines the variance matrix of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}),
i.e.,

V_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))=\frac{\partial^{2}c({\bm{\tau}})}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}\partial{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}}.

(6)

That this is positive definite for all
{\bm{\tau}}
is implied by, and implies, the convexity of
\mathbf{c}({\bm{\tau}}).

Reciprocal Relationships for Tilting Parameters

It also follows that

\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=V_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))^{-1}.

To prove this, simply observe that

\mathbf{I}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{s}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=\left\{\frac{\partial\mathbf{s}}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}}\right\}\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}c({\bm{\tau}})}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}\partial{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}}\right\}\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=V_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}

using the relationships of eqns. (
5
) and (
6
); the result follows.

Recall from above that the KL divergence of
p(\cdot)
from
f(\cdot)
is

{KL}_{p|f}=\kappa(\mathbf{s})={\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}-c({\bm{\tau}}).
Now consider how this varies with chosen target
\mathbf{s}
via its implied value of
{\bm{\tau}}.
Note that

\frac{\partial\kappa(\mathbf{s})}{\partial\mathbf{s}}={\bm{\tau}}+\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{s}\phantom{{}^{\prime}}}\mathbf{s}-\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\frac{\partial c({\bm{\tau}})}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}={\bm{\tau}}+\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{s}\phantom{{}^{\prime}}}\left\{\mathbf{s}-\frac{\partial c({\bm{\tau}})}{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}\right\}={\bm{\tau}}.

(7)

That is, the parameter vector
{\bm{\tau}}
defined by any specified target
\mathbf{s}
is in fact just the gradient vector of the KL divergence at that target.

Convexity of KL Metric

Using the previous two results it further follows that

\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(\mathbf{s})}{\partial\mathbf{s}\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial{\bm{\tau}}}{\partial\mathbf{s}^{\prime}}=V_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))^{-1}

(8)

which is positive definite for all
\mathbf{s},
implying the important property that
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
is a convex function of
\mathbf{s}.

The identities of eqns. (
7
) and (
8
) are apparently not so well-known or elaborated in the literature. They highlight
theoretical links between the cumulant function
c({\bm{\tau}})
and the KL metric
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
of the exponential family that are related to the ability to
parametrise the exponential family in either
{\bm{\tau}}
or
\mathbf{s}.
Specifically:

•

Note that eqn. (
7
) is a dual of eqn. (
5
);
\mathbf{s}
is defined by
{\bm{\tau}}
as the derivative of the cumulant function
c({\bm{\tau}})
, while reciprocally
{\bm{\tau}}
is defined by
\mathbf{s}
as the derivative of the KL metric
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
.

•

Note further that eqn. (
8
) is a dual of eqn. (
6
); the variance matrix of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
is defined by
{\bm{\tau}}
as the second derivative of the cumulant function
c({\bm{\tau}})
, while reciprocally the precision matrix (inverse of the variance matrix) is defined by the second derivative of the KL metric
\kappa(\mathbf{s})
.

Small Perturbations of Baseline

In connection with the perspective of using ET to explore “small perturbations” of the baseline
p(\mathbf{y})
using
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}
for some “small” increment
{\bm{\epsilon}}
to the baseline expected score, the above results deliver first-order approximations as follows. Write
\mathbf{V}=V_{p}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))\equiv V_{f}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}))
when
f(\cdot)\equiv p(\cdot)
corresponding to
{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{0},
and let
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}
be the corresponding precision matrix of the score vector under the baseline distribution.

Consider a first-order Taylor series approximation to
{\bm{\tau}}={\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{s})
as a function of
\mathbf{s}
, relevant for very small
{\bm{\epsilon}}.
Exploiting the results above yields the approximation
{\bm{\tau}}\approx{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{\epsilon}}.
The dual approximation to
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}({\bm{\tau}})
as a function of
{\bm{\tau}}
for very small
{\bm{\epsilon}}
is
\mathbf{s}\approx\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}+\mathbf{V}{\bm{\tau}}.
These results show how the baseline dependencies among the elements of the chosen score vector play roles in influencing tilting for a given target score, and reciprocally in defining implied targets based on choices of tilting parameters.

It is easily verified that such small perturbations yield the corresponding local approximation to the KL metric given by
\kappa(\mathbf{s})\approx(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{0})^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{0})={\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}{\bm{\tau}}/2.

Theoretical Examples with Moment Constraints

Two very simple, idea-fixing theoretical examples are as follows.

\mathbf{y}=y

\tau

Bivariate Normal Mean Example

Suppose

is bivariate normal

\mathbf{V}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{and}\hskip 20.00003pt{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}=(1-\rho^{2})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\rho\\

Take a

The optimising

Practicable Examples of ET with Marginal Quantile Constraints

A far-reaching context involves conditioning on a set of specified target quantiles for the univariate marginal distribution of a chosen function or set of functions of
\mathbf{y}
. Constraining quantiles is natural from an application viewpoint, as quantiles are often a main focus in elicitation of the role of external information. Quantile scores also always lead to a defined solution to the ET optimization problem, while other choices of score may not. For example, if some elements of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
are linear in
\mathbf{y}
then a solution exists only if the moment generating function of
p(\mathbf{y})
exists; this rules out many practical settings such as common models delivering predictive distributions that are multivariate log T
(
West 2021
, e.g. appendix in)
.

Quantile Specifications

For example here, take
\phi(\mathbf{y})
as a univariate function such as:

•

\phi(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{y}
to constrain and update predictions of
\mathbf{y}
based on a specified set of target quantiles for the resulting distribution of the sum of elements of
\mathbf{y}.

•

\phi(\mathbf{y})=y_{1}
to update based on target quantiles for the marginal
of the first element of
\mathbf{y}.

•

\phi(\mathbf{y})=y_{2}/y_{1}
to update based on target quantiles for the marginal
of the relative value of the second to first element of
\mathbf{y}.

The generality is evident, as are extensions to quantiles of multiple functions. Continuing in this first example, quantile constraints are implemented as follows.

•

Choose a vector of target quantiles for
\phi(\mathbf{y})
in the
q-
vector
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{q})^{\prime}
with
f_{i-1}\leq f_{i}
for
i=2{:}q.

Extend the notation to define and include
endpoints
f_{0}=-\infty
and
f_{q+1}=\infty
, giving intervals

{\cal I}_{i}=(f_{i-1},f_{i}]
for
i=1{:}(q+1).

•

Take
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=(s_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,s_{q}(\mathbf{y}))^{\prime}
with elements
i=1{:}q
given by

s_{i}(\mathbf{y})=I(\phi(\mathbf{y})\in{\cal I}_{i})
where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

•

Fix the target score vector
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{q})^{\prime}
to define probabilities between the quantiles. Require that
s_{i}>0
and that
\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}<1,
naturally,
and set
s_{q+1}=1-\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}>0.

For example,
s_{i}=q/(q+1)
for each
i=1{:}q

defines equal quantile regions:
q=3
implies the constrained distribution of
\phi(\mathbf{y})
has lower quartile, median and upper quartile in
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})^{\prime},

while taking
q=9
constrains the deciles. In these cases,
the target constraints impose
\mathbf{f}
as the endpoints of the
100/(q+1)\%
probability intervals in the marginal distribution of
\phi(\mathbf{y}).

•

Taking unequal probabilities provides generality; for example,
\mathbf{s}=(0.25,0.25,0.45)^{\prime}
constrains the lower quartile, median and upper 95% point of the distribution, and so forth.

•

Taking a very large value of
q
means that the specified
\mathbf{f}
vector maps out an increasingly accurate representation of the full target marginal distribution of
\phi(\mathbf{y})
. That is, this is a Bayesian decision-analytic approach to updating
p(\mathbf{y})
to
f(\mathbf{y})
that conditions on a modified marginal distribution for
\phi(\mathbf{y}).
Extensions to multivariate margins are immediate.

Almost Exact/Deterministic Constraints using Quantile ET

A really interesting and important use of quantile ET is to explore and potentially condition upon “almost deterministic” constraints. This was first identified, explored and exploited in
West 2021
as one approach to conditioning forecast distributions on almost-exact constraints.
The idea is simple: take two “extreme” quantiles for the predictive distribution of a chosen score function, and use ET to explore and/or enforce the target that the probability under
f(\mathbf{y})
between the two extremes in that score dimension is “very small”.

Analytic Solution under Quantile Constraints

Perhaps somewhat surprisingly, the ET optimization can be evaluated analytically in this setting of quantile constraints– in spite of the generality of examples (different sets of functions
\phi(\mathbf{y})
). This new theoretical result derives from the fact that the score vector
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})
is a vector of indicator functions, so knowing the mean of this vector under
p(\mathbf{y})
(the set of prior probabilities on each of the constrained regions) is enough to define the analytic solution for
{\bm{\tau}}
.

For each
i=1{:}q,
define
s_{0,i}=E_{p}[s_{i}(\mathbf{y})]
, the initial probabilities under
p(\mathbf{y})
on each of the
k
intervals
{\cal I}_{i}
. Write
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}=(s_{0,1},\ldots,s_{0,q})^{\prime}
and define
s_{0,q+1}=1-\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}=1-\sum_{i=1{:}q}s_{0,i}
as the probability on
{\cal I}_{q+1}
under
p(\mathbf{y}).
Require
s_{0,i}>0
for
i=1{:}(q+1),
naturally.

Quantile Constraint Result:

The
q-
vector
{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q})^{\prime}
has elements

\tau_{i}=\log\{(s_{0,q+1}s_{i})/(s_{q+1}s_{0,i})\},\hskip 20.00003pti=1{:}q.

(9)

The proof is as follows.
From the ET defining eqn. (
4
), note that, for each
i=1{:}q,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{y}}\{s_{i}(\mathbf{y})-s_{i}\}\textrm{e}^{{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})}p(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=\sum_{j=1{:}(q+1)}\int_{\mathbf{y}:\phi(\mathbf{y})\in{\cal I}_{j}}\{s_{i}(\mathbf{y})-s_{i}\}\textrm{e}^{{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})}p(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-s_{i})\textrm{e}^{\tau_{i}}s_{0,i}-s_{i}\sum_{j=1{:}q,j\neq i}\textrm{e}^{\tau_{j}}s_{0,j}-s_{i}s_{0,q+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textrm{e}^{\tau_{i}}s_{0,i}-s_{i}\sum_{j=1{:}q}\textrm{e}^{\tau_{j}}s_{0,j}-s_{i}s_{0,q+1}

using the facts that
{\bm{\tau}}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j=1{:}q}\tau_{j}I(\phi(\mathbf{y})\in{\cal I}_{j})
which
reduces to
\tau_{j}
when
\phi(\mathbf{y})\in{\cal I}_{j}
for any
j=1{:}q,
and is zero when
\phi(\mathbf{y})\in{\cal I}_{q+1}
.

For each
i=1{:}q
, let
\theta_{i}=\exp(\tau_{i})
and set
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{q})^{\prime}.
Further,
let
\mathbf{S}_{0}
be the
q\times q
diagonal matrix
\mathbf{S}_{0}=\textrm{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}).

The above set of equations gives the vector form

(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}){\bm{\theta}}=s_{0,q+1}\mathbf{s}
with solution

{\bm{\theta}}=s_{0,q+1}(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{s}.

Now

(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime})^{-1}=\{(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1})\mathbf{S}_{0}\}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{1}^{\prime})^{-1}=\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{s}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}/s_{q+1})

using the fact that
\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{0}}=\mathbf{1}
and standard linear algebra results for matrix inversion.
This result is valid as (i) the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is invertible since its diagonal elements
s_{0,j}
are all positive, and (ii)
the matrix
\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{s}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}
is invertible since it has determinant
1-\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{s}=s_{q+1}>0.
As a result,

{\bm{\theta}}=s_{0,q+1}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{s}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}/s_{q+1})\mathbf{s}=s_{0,q+1}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{s}(1+(1-s_{q+1})/s_{q+1})=(s_{0,q+1}/s_{q+1})\mathbf{S}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{s}.

Elementwise,
\theta_{i}=(s_{0,q+1}/s_{q+1})(s_{i}/s_{0,i})
and the result follows since
\tau_{i}=\log(\theta_{i})
for each
i=1{:}q.

It is also easily shown that the inverse transformation defining
\mathbf{s}
in terms of
{\bm{\tau}}
as in eqn. (
5
) is given elementwise by

s_{i}=s_{0,i}\textrm{e}^{\tau_{i}-c({\bm{\tau}})},\hskip 20.00003pti=1{:}q,

where

c({\bm{\tau}})=\log\{s_{0,q+1}+\sum_{j=1{:}q}s_{0,j}\textrm{e}^{\tau_{j}}\}=\log\{1+\sum_{j=1{:}q}s_{0,j}(e^{\tau_{j}}-1)\}

is the cumulant function in this setting.
Note that
s_{i}
is strictly increasing in
\tau_{i}
, but strictly
decreasing
in
\tau_{j}
for
j\neq i.
There are specific choices of
\mathbf{s}
that define optimal
{\bm{\tau}}
vectors with strictly positive elements, but others for which some elements of
{\bm{\tau}}
are negative.
