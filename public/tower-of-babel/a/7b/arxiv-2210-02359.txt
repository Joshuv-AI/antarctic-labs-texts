Abstract
. We introduce dual curvature measures for log-concave functions, which in the case of characteristic functions recover the dual curvature measures for convex bodies introduced by Huang-Lutwak-Yang-Zhang in 2016. Variational formulas are shown. The associated Minkowski problem for these dual curvature measures is considered and sufficient conditions in the symmetric setting are demonstrated.



1.
Introduction

Geometric measures associated with convex bodies have been a core part of convex geometric analysis in the past few decades. In the classical Brunn-Minkowski theory of convex bodies,
quermassintegrals
(such as volume, surface area, mean width, and much more in higher dimensions) are the central geometric invariants and are used to describe the shape of convex bodies via isoperimetric (or reverse isoperimetric) inequalities.
Area measures
introduced by Aleksandrov, Fenchel-Jessen, and
curvature measures
introduced by Federer can be viewed as the “derivative” of quermassintegrals when viewed as functionals on the set of convex bodies. Invariably, these geometric measures carry some curvature terms which make it possible for them to encode shape information of convex bodies. At the same time, unlike curvatures (in the sense of differential geometry), these geometric measures are defined even with minimal regularity assumptions. The study of these geometric measures is often intertwined with PDE (Monge-Ampère equations in particular), Gauss curvature flows, and inevitably isoperimetric inequalities. (After all, half of calculus is focused on using derivatives to study properties of the original function.)

A major alternative to the classical Brunn-Minkowski theory in modern convex geometry is the dual Brunn-Minkowski theory. The dual Brunn-Minkowski theory, introduced by Lutwak in 1975, is a theory that is in a sense dual to the classical Brunn-Minkowski theory. A good discussion of the dual theory can be found in Section 9.3 of Schneider’s classical volume
[
70
]
. Quoting from Gardner-Hug-Weil
[
41
]
:“The dual Brunn-Minkowski theory can count among its successes the solution of the Busemann-Petty problem in
[
38
]
,
[
43
]
,
[
59
]
, and
[
73
]
. It also has connections and applications to integral geometry, Minkowski geometry, the local theory of Banach spaces, and stereology; see
[
40
]
and the references given there.”

In the seminal work
[
48
]
, Huang-Lutwak-Yang-Zhang (Huang-LYZ), for the first time, revealed the fundamental geometric measures, duals of Federer’s curvature measures, called
dual curvature measures
, in the dual Brunn-Minkowski theory. These measures were obtained through “differentiating”
dual quermassintegrals
which are central in the dual theory. They have led to many natural open problems and quickly attracted much attention. Details on this will be provided below.

It is well-known that the set of convex bodies can be embedded in the set of upper semi-continuous log-concave functions via their characteristic functions. This work aims to introduce the functional version of dual curvature measures through the machinery of the theory of functions of bounded anisotropic weighted variation and to study their characterization problem (generally known as
Minkowski problems
). It is worth pointing out that, by picking proper weight functions, functional versions of many other geometric measures can be introduced in this fashion. However, this will not be explored in this work.

In the past few decades (even more so in the last decade), interest in log-concave functions has grown considerably, much of it motivated by their counterparts in the theory of convex bodies. Perhaps the first such breakthrough and by now a well-known result is the Prékopa-Leindler inequality: For any nonnegative integrable functions
f,g
on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and their sup-convolution
(1-\lambda)\cdot f\oplus\lambda\cdot g
given by

(1.1)

\left((1-\lambda)\cdot f\oplus\lambda\cdot g\right)(z)=\sup_{(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y=z}f(x)^{1-\lambda}g(y)^{\lambda},

where
0<\lambda<1
, one has the following inequality regarding their
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norms,

(1.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left((1-\lambda)\cdot f\oplus\lambda\cdot g\right)(z)dz\geq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}f(x)dx\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)dy\right)^{\lambda}.

The Prékopa-Leinder inequality is the functional (and equivalent) version of the celebrated Brunn-Minkowski inequality,

(1.3)

V((1-\lambda)X+\lambda Y)\geq V(X)^{1-\lambda}V(Y)^{\lambda},

which holds for any bounded measurable sets
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that
(1-\lambda)X+\lambda Y
is measurable.
See the survey
[
39
]
by Gardner. It is important to note that convexity is required neither in the Brunn-Minkowski inequality nor in the Prékopa-Leindler inequality, although it does manifest itself in the equality conditions. Notice here that the Minkowski combination
(1-\lambda)X+\lambda Y
corresponds to the sup-convolution between functions (see, (
4.1
) for a complete definition) and the volume of a measurable set corresponds to the integral of a non-negative function. That this is natural can be seen by replacing
f
and
g
by characteristic functions of subsets of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

In the same spirit, many other geometric invariants and operations have found their counterparts for log-concave functions (or, equivalently, convex functions). We provide a quick overview of some of these remarkable results. In the seminal work
[
5
]
, Artstein-Avidan and Milman demonstrated how the Legendre-Fenchel transform can be viewed as the functional version of taking the polar body of a convex body. Compare this to another remarkable paper
[
9
]
by Böröczky-Schneider. Prior to this, the functional version of the Blaschke-Santaló inequality was discovered by Ball in his Ph.D. thesis and by Artstein-Avidan, Klartag, and Milman in
[
3
]
. Steiner formula and quermassintegrals for quasi-concave functions were studied by Bobkov-Colesanti-Fragalà
[
8
]
. Extensions of affine surface area and affine isoperimetric inequalities can be found in
[
4
,
17
,
18
,
54
]
. Much more recently, Colesanti, Ludwig, and Mussnig embarked on a journey to characterize valuations on the set of convex functions
[
30
,
29
,
27
,
28
,
31
]
(compare them to Hadwiger-type theorems on convex bodies
[
58
,
57
,
56
,
45
,
46
,
71
]
).

In the dual Brunn-Minkowski theory, the central geometric invariants are known as
dual quermassintegrals
. Let
q=1,\cdots,n
, and
K
be a convex body that contains the origin in its interior. As Lutwak
[
59
]
showed, up to a constant multiple, the
q
-th dual quermassintegral
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)
can be defined as the average of lower-dimensional sectional areas of
K
with
q
-dimensional subspaces:

(1.4)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)=c\int_{G(n,q)}\mathcal{H}^{q}(K\cap\xi)d\xi,

where
G(n,q)
is the Grassmannian manifold containing all
q
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and the integration is with respect to the Haar measure. Dual quermassintegrals have integral representations (see (
2.9
)) which warrant the immediate extension to
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that with the exception of the special case
q=n
, when the dual quermassintegral is simply volume, the
q
-th dual quermassintegral is generally
not
invariant under translations of
K
.

A major question answered in the landmark work
[
48
]
(and subsequently
[
62
]
) by Huang-LYZ was the differentiability of
\widetilde{V}_{q}
. In particular, it was shown that if
K
is a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that the origin is an interior point and
L
is a compact convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, then

(1.5)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K+t\cdot L)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)}{t}=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{L}(v)\frac{1}{h_{K}(v)}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,v).

Here the geometric measure
\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)
is known as the
q
-th
dual curvature measure
of
K
. In fact, there is naturally an
L_{p}
version of (
1.5
) that leads to the
(p,q)
-dual curvature measure introduced in
[
62
]
and the measure
\frac{1}{h_{K}(v)}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,v)
is nothing but the
(1,q)
-dual curvature measure. The “1” here stands for the fact that the sum
K+t\cdot L
is the classical Minkowski sum, or, the
l^{1}
sum of support functions of
K
and
L
.

Let
q>0
. The
(q-n)
-th moment of a density function
f
is defined as

(1.6)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|x|^{q-n}f(x)dx,

if it exists. The moment
\widetilde{V}_{q}
is a natural extension of dual quermassintegrals to the set of log-concave functions (which in turn justifies this notation). Indeed, if
f=1_{K}
, where
1_{K}
is the characteristic function of some convex body
K
that contains the origin in its interior, then, by integration via polar coordinates, one immediately has

(1.7)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(1_{K})=\widetilde{V}_{q}(K).

Motivated by the work
[
48
]
and the correspondence between the Minkowski combination and sup-convolution, it is natural to ask whether

(1.8)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(f\oplus t\cdot g)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)}{t}

exists for log-concave functions
f,g
, and if it does, what the limit is. We remark that with the exception of
q=n
, for generic
q>0
, the moments considered in (
1.8
) are
not
invariant under the transformation
f(x)\rightarrow f(x+x_{0})
. Therefore, the relative position of the origin is crucial in the study of (
1.8
). In fact, since (
1.5
) only holds when
K
contains the origin in its interior, some condition on
f
that mimics this constraint is expected.

When
q=n
, the functional
\widetilde{V}_{q}
is nothing but the
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm of a log-concave function. In this case, the limit (
1.8
) was studied by Colesanti-Fragalà
[
32
]
under various regularity assumptions on
f
and
g
. In particular, they discovered that the limit (
1.8
) consists of two parts—one concerning the behavior of
f
inside its support, the other concerning the values of
f
on the boundary of its support as well as the shape of the support set. Around the same time, Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag
[
33
]
studied the limit with the additional assumption that
f
is
essentially continuous
and explored the connection with complex analysis
[
7
]
and optimal transport.
Recently, Rotem
[
67
]
showed that the result of Colesanti-Fragalà remains valid without any of the various additional regularity requirements, by employing tools from the theory of functions of bounded anisotropic variation. The first main result of this paper is to show that by considering functions of bounded anisotropic
weighted
variation, one can compute the limit in (
1.8
) for any
q>0
. It is important to emphasize that unlike the special case
q=n
, for generic
q>0
, the
q
-th moment of a function is not translation-invariant. In particular, our approach is motivated by the dual approach (differentiating radial functions) to the variational formula developed in
[
48
]
.

It is also worth pointing out that the extension of functions of bounded variation in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure to functions of bounded variation in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with respect to an arbitrary measure (even those absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure) is not entirely trivial. This has been previously done in, for example,
[
6
]
and
[
65
]
via different approaches (which led to non-equivalent definitions of weighted total variation).

Let
\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be the set of all upper semi-continuous log-concave functions
f:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. The limit (
1.8
) leads to two Borel measures—one on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and one on
{S^{n-1}}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
f=e^{-\phi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm. The Euclidean
q
-th dual curvature measure of
f
, denoted by
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
, is a Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
given by

(1.9)

\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\mathcal{B})=\int_{\nabla\phi(x)\in\mathcal{B}}|x|^{q-n}f(x)\,dx,

for each Borel set
\mathcal{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

In (
1.9
), note that since
\phi
is convex, its gradient
\nabla\phi
exists almost everywhere in the interior of its domain
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:\phi(x)<\infty\}
. Note that by definition,
f>0
if and only if
\phi<\infty
. Therefore, the integral in (
1.9
) is well-defined.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
f=e^{-\phi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm. The spherical
q
-th dual curvature measure of
f
, denoted by
\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\cdot)
, is a Borel measure on
{S^{n-1}}
given by

(1.10)

\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\eta)=\int_{\nu_{K_{f}}(x)\in\eta}|x|^{q-n}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x),

for each Borel set
\eta\subset{S^{n-1}}
, where
K_{f}
is the support of
f
and
\nu_{K_{f}}
is its Gauss map defined almost everywhere on
\partial K_{f}
with respect to
d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)
.

These two measures generated through differentiating the
q
-th moment of a log-concave function
f
with respect to sup-convolution are associated with the absolutely continuous and singular part of the distributional derivative of
f
, respectively. It is worth noting that in the case of the characteristic function of a convex body, the measure
\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\cdot)
recovers the
(1,q)
-dual curvature measure for convex bodies appearing in (
1.5
).

The first of our main theorems shows that the limit in (
1.8
) does exist under minor assumptions on
f
and
g
near the origin.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f=e^{-\phi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with non-zero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm and
q>0
. Assume
f
achieves its maximum at the origin and

(1.11)

\limsup_{x\rightarrow o}\frac{|f(x)-f(o)|}{|x|^{\alpha+1}}<\infty,

for some
0<\alpha<1
.

Let
g=e^{-\psi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be compactly supported with
g(o)>0
. Then,

(1.12)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(f\oplus t\cdot g)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)}{t}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\psi^{*}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{K_{g}}(v)d\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;v).

where
h_{K_{g}}
is the support function of the support set
K_{g}
of
g
, and
\psi^{*}
is the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate of
\psi
.

Note that hypothesis (
1.11
) in the above theorem is not the best hypothesis, see Proposition
4.9
and Remark
4.10
for more details. We emphasize again that it is expected that we need some condition on
f
that mimics the idea that “
f
contains the origin in its interior”. The assumption that
f
achieves its maximum at the origin, together with hypothesis (
1.11
), ensures that almost all of
f
’s nonempty level sets contain the origin in the interior and these level sets contain the origin in their interiors in some
uniform
way. We remark that if
f
is
C^{1,\alpha}
in a neighborhood of the origin, then (
1.11
) is satisfied. To better explain the condition
g(o)>0
, we focus for the moment on the special case that
g
is the characteristic function of some convex body
L
. In this case, without the condition
g(o)>0
, the convex body
L
might be far away from the origin. As a consequence, the origin might be outside the Minkowski combination of the level sets of
f
and
L
. This makes it very challenging to apply geometric results to level sets of
f
. See Theorem
4.5
for details.

When
f
and
g
are characteristic functions of convex bodies that contain the origin in their interiors, the first integral on the right-hand side in (
1.12
) vanishes whereas the second term becomes the right-hand side of (
1.5
).

Minkowski problems
in convex geometric analysis are characterization problems of geometric measures associated with convex bodies. These geometric measures are often “derivatives” of important geometric invariants. In differential geometry, Minkowski problems are known as various prescribed curvature problems. This line of research that asks when a given measure
\mu
can be realized as a certain geometric measure of a to-be-solved convex body (without any unnecessary regularity assumptions) goes back to the classical Minkowski problem that inspired the study of nonlinear elliptic PDE through the last century; see, for example, Minkowski
[
64
]
, Aleksandrov
[
1
]
, Cheng-Yau
[
25
]
, Pogorelov
[
66
]
, and the works of Caffarelli on the regularity theory of Monge-Ampère equations
[
15
,
16
,
14
]
, among many other influential works. In many ways, the study of Minkowski problems goes hand-in-hand with the study of sharp isoperimetric inequalities; see
[
19
]
.

In the last 2-3 decades, there are two major families of Minkowski problems. One is the
L_{p}
Minkowski problem that belongs to the
L_{p}
Brunn-Minkowski theory whose success can be credited to the landmark work by Lutwak
[
60
,
61
]
where the fundamental
L_{p}
surface area measure was discovered. The
L_{p}
Minkowski problem includes the logarithmic Minkowski problem and the centro-affine Minkowski problem and has been studied through a variety of methods; see, for example, Hug-Lutwak-Yang-Zhang (Hug-LYZ)
[
51
]
, Chou-Wang
[
26
]
, Böröczky-LYZ
[
13
]
, and most recently Guang-Li-Wang
[
44
]
. A vast library of works on this topic can be found by looking for those citing the above-mentioned works. It is worth pointing out that there is much unknown regarding the
L_{p}
Brunn-Minkowski theory, especially for
p<1
. In fact, the log Brunn-Minkowski conjecture, arguably the most beautiful and powerful (yet plausible) conjecture in convex geometry in the last decade, is the isoperimetric inequality associated with the log Minkowski problem. See, for example,
[
12
,
52
,
23
,
63
,
72
]
.

The other major family of Minkowski problems are the
dual Minkowski problems
following the landmark work
[
48
]
. In a short period since
[
48
]
, there have been many influential works on this topic which have already led to many interesting conjectures regarding isoperimetric inequalities as well as the discovery of novel curvature flows; see, for example, Böröczky-Henk-Pollehn
[
11
]
, Chen-Chen-Li
[
21
]
, Chen-Huang-Zhao
[
20
]
, Chen-Li
[
24
]
, Gardner-Hug-Weil-Xing-Ye
[
42
]
, Henk-Pollehn
[
47
]
, Li-Sheng-Wang
[
53
]
, Liu-Lu
[
55
]
, Zhao
[
74
]
. It is important to note that the list is by no means exhaustive.

In this paper, we study the Minkowski problem for
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}
.

The functional dual Minkowski problem.
Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Find the necessary and sufficient conditions on
\mu
so that there exists
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm such that

(1.13)

\mu=\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot).

Under sufficient regularity assumption, that is, the measure
\mu
has a
C^{\infty}
density (say,
g\geq 0
) and
f\in C^{\infty}
, equation (
1.13
) is equivalent to the following Monge-Ampère type equation in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

(1.14)

g(\nabla\phi(x))\det(\nabla^{2}\phi(x))=|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi(x)},

where
f=e^{-\phi}
.

It is important to note that the measure
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
might not be absolutely continuous. Thus, the Minkowski problem (
1.13
) does not always reduce to (
1.14
) in the general setting.

When
q=n
, the functional dual Minkowski problem becomes the Minkowski problem for moment measures. See Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag
[
33
]
where it is completely solved within the class of essentially continuous functions. The highly nontrivial
L_{p}
extension of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag’s result can be found in the recent papers by Fang-Xing-Ye
[
35
]
for
p>1
and Rotem
[
68
]
for
0<p<1
.

As pointed out earlier, a key difference between the case
q=n
and
q\neq n
is that in the latter case, (
1.13
) is not invariant under translations of
f
(with respect to its domain). We point out that translation-invariance played a central role in
[
33
]
.

In the current work, we provide a sufficient condition for the existence of solutions to (
1.13
) in the origin-symmetric case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Suppose
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspaces and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu(x)<\infty
. There exists an even
f_{0}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm such that

(1.15)

\mu=\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f_{0};\cdot).

The functional dual Minkowski problem (
1.13
) is heavily intertwined with its counterpart in the setting of convex bodies. In particular, estimates regarding dual quermassintegrals are critically needed. As part of the process to get the required estimates, we require a Blaschke-Santaló type inequality for the functional
\widetilde{V}_{q}
. See Lemma
5.6
. It is of great interest to see if there is a sharp (more refined) version.

We remark that the assumption

(1.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu(x)<\infty

is necessary here. See Theorem
5.12
.

1.3
whereas Section 5 is devoted to proving Theorem
1.4
.

2.
Preliminaries

This section is divided into two parts. The first part contains some notations and basics in the theory of convex bodies, whereas the second part deals with those for convex functions as well as log-concave functions.

For convenience, throughout the current work, if the exact value of a constant
C>0
does not matter, then we may use the same
C
for different positive constants (that may differ from line to line).

2.1.
Convex bodies

The standard reference is the comprehensive book
[
70
]
by Schneider.

A convex body in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is a compact convex set with a nonempty interior. The boundary of
K
is written as
\partial K
. We use
\mathcal{K}^{n}
for the set of all convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. The subclass of convex bodies that contain the origin in their interiors in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
.

We will use
B(x,r)
to denote the ball in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
centered at
x
with radius
r
. Occasionally, we write
B(r)=B(o,r)
and
B=B(o,1)
for simplicity.

The support function
h_{K}
of
K
is defined by

(2.1)

h_{K}(y)=\max\{\langle x,y\rangle:x\in K\},\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

The support function
h_{K}
is a continuous function homogeneous of degree 1. Suppose
K
contains the origin in its interior. The radial function
\rho_{K}
is defined by

\rho_{K}(x)=\max\{\lambda:\lambda x\in K\},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\setminus\{0\}.

The radial function
\rho_{K}
is a continuous function homogeneous of degree
-1
. It is not hard to see that
\rho_{K}(u)u\in\partial K
for all
u\in S^{n-1}
and the reciprocal radial function is a (potentially asymmetric) norm. To be more specific, we write

(2.2)

\|x\|_{K}=\frac{1}{\rho_{K}(x)}=h_{K^{*}}(x),\quad\text{for each }x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

Here, the convex body
K^{*}
is known as the polar body of
K
and is defined by

(2.3)

K^{*}=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1,\forall x\in K\}.

By the definition of the polar body, it is simple to see that the Banach spaces
({\mathbb{R}^{n}},\|\cdot\|_{K})
and
({\mathbb{R}^{n}},\|\cdot\|_{K^{*}})
are dual to each other and we have the following generalized Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

(2.4)

\langle x,y\rangle\leq\|x\|_{K}\|y\|_{K^{*}}.

Let
h:{S^{n-1}}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be continuous, the
Wulff shape

[h]\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
is given by

(2.5)

[h]=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:\langle x,v\rangle\leq h(v)\text{ for all }v\in{S^{n-1}}\}.

It is simple to see that if
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, then
[h_{K}]=K
. Also immediate from the definition of
[h]
is that for every
u\in{S^{n-1}}
, we have

(2.6)

\rho_{[h]}(u)\langle u,v\rangle\leq h(v),\qquad\forall v\in{S^{n-1}},

and there exists
v_{*}\in{S^{n-1}}
such that

(2.7)

\rho_{[h]}(u)\langle u,v_{*}\rangle=h(v_{*}).

For each
x\in\partial K
, we will write
\nu_{K}(x)
for the outer unit normal of
K
at
x
. Note that by convexity, the map
\nu_{K}
is defined almost everywhere on
\partial K
with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
. For each
v\in{S^{n-1}}
, define

(2.8)

\nu_{K}^{-1}(v)=\{x\in\partial K:\langle x,v\rangle=h_{K}(v)\}.

Since
K
is a convex body, for almost all
v\in{S^{n-1}}
, the set
\nu_{K}^{-1}(v)
contains only one boundary point of
K
. With slight abuse of notation, we will use
\nu_{K}^{-1}
to denote a map that is defined almost everywhere on
{S^{n-1}}
and
\nu_{K}^{-1}
maps
v
to the unique point in
\nu_{K}^{-1}(v)
.

The fundamental geometric functionals in the dual Brunn-Minkowski theory are dual quermassintegrals. For
q\neq 0
, the
q
-th dual quermassintegral of
K
, denoted by
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)
, is defined as

(2.9)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)=\frac{1}{q}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K}^{q}(u)du.

When
q=1,\cdots,n
, dual quermassintegrals have the strongest geometric significance. They are proportional to the mean of the
q
-dimensional volume of intersections of
K
with
q
-dimensional subspaces in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

In
[
48
]
, it was established that variation of the dual quermassintegral with respect to the logarithmic Minkowski sum leads to the so-called dual curvature measure:

(2.10)

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\eta)=\int_{\nu_{K}(x)\in\eta}\langle x,\nu_{K}(x)\rangle|x|^{q-n}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x),\text{ for each Borel }\eta\subset{S^{n-1}}.

In particular, this implies that for each
p\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(2.11)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K+_{p}t\cdot L)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)}{t}=\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(v)h_{K}^{-p}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,v):=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,v),

where the Borel measure
\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,\cdot)
is known as the
(p,q)
-dual curvature measure of
K
. Here
K+_{p}t\cdot L
is known as the
L_{p}
Minkowski combination between convex bodies. In particular, when
p\geq 1
and
t>0
, the convex body
K+_{p}t\cdot L
is defined so that its support function is given by
(h_{K}^{p}+th_{L}^{p})^{1/p}
. The variational formula (
2.11
), as well as the definition of the
L_{p}
combination, can be found in
[
62
]
.

2.2.
Convex functions and log-concave functions

Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on some set
\Omega
. We will use
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu,\Omega)
for the set of all
\mu
-measurable functions
f
on
\Omega
with
\int_{\Omega}|f|d\mu<\infty
. The set
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mu,\Omega)
consists of functions
f
such that
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu,\mathfrak{K})
for every compact set
\mathfrak{K}\subset\Omega
. Occasionally, when
\Omega={\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, we may write
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
. When
\mu
is the standard Lebesgue measure, we may simply write
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\Omega)
, or,
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}={\mathcal{L}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
. When
\mu
is a finite measure, we write
|\mu|
for its total mass.

Let
\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be the set of all lower semi-continuous, convex functions
\phi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
and
\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be the set of all upper semi-continuous log-concave functions
f
that take the form
f=e^{-\phi}
for some
\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

For any function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
, the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate of
\phi
, denoted by
\phi^{*}
, is defined as

(2.12)

\phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\{\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x)\}.

Note that from the definition, it is simple to see that
\phi^{*}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, as long as
\phi\not\equiv+\infty
. It is also straightforward from the definition that the Legendre-Fenchel transform reverses order; in other words, if
\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2}
, then
\phi_{1}^{*}\geq\phi_{2}^{*}
.

When restricting to
\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, the Fenchel-Moreau theorem states that the Legendre-Fenchel transform is an involution:

(2.13)

\phi^{**}=\phi,\text{ for each }\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}}).

In a remarkable paper
[
5
]
, Artstein-Avidan and Milman showed that any order-reversing involution on
\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
is essentially the Legendre-Fenchel transform.

In general (without assuming
\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
), by the definition of the Legendre-Fenchel transform, one may show that

(2.14)

\phi^{**}\leq\phi,

and if
\phi\geq 0
, then
\phi^{**}\geq 0
.

The expression
1_{E}
denotes the characteristic function of some subset
E\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
; that is,
1_{E}(x)=1
if
x\in E
and
1_{E}(x)=0
if
x\notin E
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and

(2.15)

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }x\in K\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
1_{K}=e^{-\psi}
. It follows from the definition that

(2.16)

\psi^{*}=h_{K}.

Indeed, let
y\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be arbitrary. Then, according to the definition of
\psi^{*}
, we have

(2.17)

\psi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\{\langle x,y\rangle-\psi(x)\}=\sup_{x\in K}\{\langle x,y\rangle\}=h_{K}(y),

where the second equality follows from the fact that if
x\notin K
, then
\psi(x)=\infty
and consequently one can restrict to
K
in search of the supremum.

We shall require the following trivial facts.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f=e^{-\phi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
and
q>0
. If

(2.18)

\liminf_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|}>0,

then

(2.19)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

Proof.

By (
2.18
) and the convexity of
\phi
, there exist
C>0
and
r_{0}>0
such that

(2.20)

\phi(x)>C|x|,\text{ for all }x\in B(r_{0})^{c}.

Therefore, we have

(2.21)

\int_{B(r_{0})^{c}}f(x)|x|^{q-n}dx\leq\int_{B(r_{0})^{c}}e^{-C|x|}|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

Since
f
is upper semi-continuous, it is locally bounded from above. Therefore,

(2.22)

\int_{B(r_{0})}f(x)|x|^{q-n}dx\leq C_{1}\int_{B(r_{0})}|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

∎

It is well-known that (
2.18
) holds if and only if either of the following two statements holds:

(1)

f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
;

(2)

\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\phi(x)=\infty
.

See, for example,
[
33
]
.

When a convex function
\phi
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin,
[
69
, Theorem 11.8(c)]
combined with Proposition
2.1
immediately implies the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
q>0
. If
\phi:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin, then

(2.23)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|^{q-n}e^{-\phi^{*}(y)}dy<\infty.

3.
Functions of anisotropic weighted total variation

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be an open set. Write
V\Subset\Omega
if an open set
V
is compactly contained in
\Omega
, that is, the closure of
V
is compact and is a subset of
\Omega
. The set
C_{c}^{1}(V,{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
consists of all
C^{1}
functions from
V
to
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with compact support.
We say a function
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\loc}(\Omega)
is locally of bounded variation (
i.e.
,
f\in BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
) if for each open
V\Subset\Omega
, we have

(3.1)

TV(f;V)=\sup\left\{\int_{V}f\Div T\,dx:T\in C_{c}^{1}(V,{\mathbb{R}^{n}}),|T(x)|\leq 1,\forall x\right\}<\infty.

Intuitively speaking, functions of locally bounded variation are those whose distributional derivatives are Radon measures. Indeed, the Structure Theorem for
BV_{\loc}
functions states (see Theorem 5.1 in
[
34
]
) that if
f\in BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
, then there exist a Radon measure
\|Df\|
on
\Omega
and a
\|Df\|
-measurable map
\sigma_{f}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that
|\sigma_{f}|=1

\|Df\|
-almost everywhere with

(3.2)

\int_{\Omega}f\Div T\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\langle T,\sigma_{f}\rangle d\|Df\|,

for all
T\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
. When
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\|Df\|(\Omega)
is finite, we say
f
is of bounded variation on
\Omega
; that is
f\in BV(\Omega)
. The space of
BV(\Omega)
is well studied and we refer the readers to the classical books
[
34
,
2
]
for additional properties of
BV
functions.

There have been several generalizations to the definition of
BV(\Omega)
(and correspondingly
BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
). One direction of such generalization is that the Euclidean norm (applied to
T
) in (
3.1
) can be replaced by any (potentially asymmetric) norm. Fix
L\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, let
\|\cdot\|_{L}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{*}}
be as defined in (
2.2
). Note that since
L
is compact and contains the origin in its interior, both
\|\cdot\|_{L}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{*}}
are equivalent to the standard Euclidean norm and therefore, the space
BV_{L}(\Omega)
(and
BV_{L,\loc}(\Omega)
, resp.) consisting of
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\Omega)
functions with

(3.3)

TV_{L}(f;\Omega)=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}f\Div T\,dx:T\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}^{n}}),\|T(x)\|_{L}\leq 1,\forall x\right\}<\infty,

remains unchanged when compared to
BV(\Omega)
(and
BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
, resp.). However, the anisotropic total variation
TV_{L}(f;\Omega)
is generally not the same as
TV(f;\Omega)
. As a matter of fact, when
f=1_{K}
for some
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, then
TV(1_{K};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
gives the surface area of
K
whereas
TV_{L}(1_{K};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
gives the mixed volume
V_{1}(K,L)=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{L}dS_{K}
. For
f\in BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
, we may define the anisotropic total variation measure with respect to
L
by

(3.4)

\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}}=h_{L}(\shortminus\sigma_{f})\|Df\|.

It can be shown that
f\in BV_{L}(\Omega)
if and only if
\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}}
is a finite measure. Moreover, we have
TV_{L}(f;\Omega)=\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}}(\Omega)
. Functions of bounded anisotropic total variation were studied in, for example,
[
37
,
36
]
where anisotropic isoperimetric inequalities and anisotropic Sobolev inequalities were studied for sets of finite perimeter and functions of bounded variation. It is important to note that many of the classical results mentioned in the standard books
[
34
,
2
]
work in the anisotropic setting with only very minor alterations to the proofs.

Another direction of generalization to
BV(\Omega)
is to replace the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
by a generic measure. Things start to get complicated in this setting. As an example, the approximation of such
BV
functions by smooth ones might fail. This explains why there are non-equivalent ways of defining
BV
functions in a generic measure space
({\mathbb{R}^{n}},\mu)
. We mention
[
65
]
for one of the approaches where
\mu
is a doubling measure. When
\mu=\omega(x)dx
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, another way of generalizing the classical total variation (not equivalent to the one given in
[
65
]
; see Section 5.1 in
[
65
]
) was given in
[
6
]
.

For our purpose, we adopt the following definition. We say a function
f\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\omega dx,\Omega)
is of bounded anisotropic weighted variation (or
f\in BV_{L,\omega}(\Omega))
if
f\in BV_{\loc}(\Omega)
and
\omega\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}},\Omega)
. In this case, we define the
(L,\omega)
-anisotropic total variation of
f
to be

(3.5)

TV_{L,\omega}(f;\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}\omega d\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}}.

To see how this is connected to the classical definition (
3.1
), we mention that when
\omega:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is a lower semi-continuous function with
\omega(x)>0
for all
x\neq o
, using an approximation argument in both
f
and
\omega
, one can see that

(3.6)

TV_{L,\omega}(f;\Omega)=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}f\Div T\,dx:T\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}^{n}}),\|T(x)\|_{L}\leq\omega(x),\forall x\right\}.

Since this representation is not required in the current work, we do not provide a proof here.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be a measurable set. When
1_{E}\in BV_{L,\omega}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, we say
E
has finite
(L,\omega)
-anisotropic weighted perimeter and write
\Per_{L,\omega}(\partial E)=TV_{L,\omega}(1_{E};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, write

(3.7)

[f>t]=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:f(x)>t\}.

For
BV
functions, the following version of the classical coarea formula can be found in Figalli-Maggi-Pratelli
[
36
, (2.22)]
: if
f\in BV({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
and
\zeta:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is a Borel function, then

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta d\|\shortminus Df\|_{L^{*}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\zeta d\|\shortminus D1_{[f>t]}\|_{L^{*}}\right)dt.

In particular, this implies

(3.9)

TV_{L,\omega}(f;{\mathbb{R}^{n}})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Per_{L,\omega}(\partial[f>t])dt.

4.
Log-concave functions and their
(L,\omega)
total variation

Throughout this section, if not specified otherwise, we let
q>0
.

It is well-known that the set of convex bodies can be embedded naturally into
\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
via their characteristic functions. Let
f=e^{-\phi}
,
g=e^{-\psi}
be in
\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
and
s,t>0
. The sup-convolution between
f
and
g
can be defined via the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate of their respectively associated convex functions:

(4.1)

s\cdot f\oplus t\cdot g=e^{-(s\phi^{*}+t\psi^{*})^{*}}.

When
s=1-t
, (
4.1
) coincides with (
1.2
). For the purpose of the current work, we consider the special case
s=1
, which we will write as
f\oplus t\cdot g
. It is well-known that when
f=1_{K}
and
g=1_{L}
are characteristic functions of convex bodies, then

(4.2)

1_{K}\oplus t\cdot 1_{L}=1_{K+tL},

where
K+tL
is the usual Minkowski combination between convex bodies. Therefore, the supremum convolution
\oplus
can be viewed as a natural generalization of the Minkowski addition for convex bodies.

For each
q>0
, the
(q-n)
-th moment of a log-concave function
f
is defined as

(4.3)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{q-n}f(x)dx.

When
f=1_{K}
for some convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, by polar coordinates, it is simple to see that

(4.4)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(1_{K})=\frac{1}{q}\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\rho_{K}^{q}(u)du=\widetilde{V}_{q}(K),

where
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)
is the
q
-th dual quermassintegral of
K
. Therefore, the quantity
\widetilde{V}_{q}
on
\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
can be viewed as the natural generalization of dual quermassintegrals for convex bodies.

In the seminal work
[
48
,
62
]
, the differentials of dual quermassintegrals were studied, which led to a family of long-sought-for geometric measures known as
(p,q)
-dual curvature measures
. These measures and their characterization problems (called
Minkowski-type problems
) have been intensively studied in the past few years and have already led to many interesting conjectures regarding isoperimetric inequalities as well as the discovery of novel curvature flows.

It is natural to wonder whether the same philosophy can be applied in the space of log-concave functions—given that all the elements (dual quermassintegrals and Minkowski addition) have their natural counterparts in the larger space. It is the intention of the current section to demonstrate that the answer is yes, with some minor restrictions on the log-concave functions
f
and
g
. To explain why these restrictions are needed, note that the variational formulas demonstrated in
[
48
,
62
]
require the convex body to contain the origin as an interior point and consequently the family of
(p,q)
-dual curvature measures are only defined for such bodies. See, for example, equation (1.9) in
[
48
]
. Therefore, it is natural to expect certain restrictions on
f
that mimic the requirement that
K
has the origin in the interior as in the convex body case.

In the following, we show that the variation of the moment of
f
is strongly connected to the theory of functions of bounded anisotropic weighted variation in Section
3
.

We will study the existence of the following limit

(4.5)

\delta_{q}(f,g)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(f\oplus t\cdot g)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)}{t}.

Note that it is not clear at all why the limit should exist.

For the rest of the section, we write
\omega_{q}
for the weight function

(4.6)

\omega_{q}(x)=|x|^{q-n},

for
q>0
. It is important to emphasize that the following proofs actually work for more general weight functions. As an example, the proofs (with very minor modifications) will work for the Gaussian weight function
\omega(x)=e^{-|x|^{2}/2}
.

We require the following lemma from
[
48
]
(see also Theorem 6.5 in
[
62
]
).

Lemma 4.1

(
[
48
]
)
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and
g:{S^{n-1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. For sufficiently small
|t|
, define
h_{t}:{S^{n-1}}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
by

(4.7)

h_{t}=h_{K}+tg.

Then, we have

(4.8)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}([h_{t}])-\widetilde{V}_{q}([h_{0}])}{t}=\int_{S^{n-1}}g(v)d\widetilde{C}_{1,q}(K,v),

where the definition of
\widetilde{C}_{1,q}(K,\cdot)
is given in Section
2.1
.

When
g=h_{L}
for some compact convex
L\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, we will denote the right-hand-side of (
4.8
) by
\widetilde{V}_{1,q}(K,L)
; that is

(4.9)

\widetilde{V}_{1,q}(K,L)=\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{1,q}(K,v)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{h_{L}(v)}{h_{K}(v)}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,v).

Recall that a log-concave function is almost everywhere differentiable.
It was shown in Rotem
[
68
]
that if
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
and
f
is
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, then
f\in BV({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
and its distributional derivative is given by

(4.10)

\sigma_{f}\|Df\|=\nabla f\,dx-f\nu_{K_{f}}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial K_{f}},

where
K_{f}
is the support of
f
and is thus convex by the fact that
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
. With this in mind, by (
3.5
), it is simple to see that if
K,L\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, then
K
is of finite anisotropic weighted perimeter. Indeed,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Per_{L,\omega_{q}}(\partial K)

\displaystyle=TV_{L,\omega_{q}}(1_{K},{\mathbb{R}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}h_{L}(\nu_{K}(x))|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{1,q}(K,v)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{V}_{1,q}(K,L)<\infty.

The following lemma is due to Huang-LYZ
[
48
]
. We provide a short proof here for the convenience of the readers as the lemma as stated here is buried in the long proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
48
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
h_{0},h_{1}:{S^{n-1}}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be continuous.
Denote
K_{i}=[h_{i}]
. Then, for every
u\in{S^{n-1}}
, we have

(4.12)

\left|\log\rho_{K_{1}}(u)-\log\rho_{K_{0}}(u)\right|\leq\max_{S^{n-1}}|\log h_{1}-\log h_{0}|.

Proof.

We fix an arbitrary
u\in{S^{n-1}}
. By (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), for each
i=0,1
, we have

(4.13)

\rho_{K_{i}}(u)\langle u,v\rangle\leq h_{i}(v),\qquad\forall v\in{S^{n-1}}

and there exists
v_{i}\in{S^{n-1}}
such that

(4.14)

\rho_{K_{i}}(u)\langle u,v_{i}\rangle=h_{i}(v_{i}).

By (
4.14
) and (
4.13
),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\log\rho_{K_{1}}(u)-\log\rho_{K_{0}}(u)

\displaystyle=\log\rho_{K_{1}}(u)-\log h_{0}(v_{0})+\log\langle u,v_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\log h_{1}(v_{0})-\log h_{0}(v_{0})

Reversing the role of
K_{1}
and
K_{0}
immediately gives

(4.16)

\log\rho_{K_{0}}(u)-\log\rho_{K_{1}}(u)\leq\log h_{0}(v_{1})-\log h_{1}(v_{1}).

The desired result immediately follows from (
4.15
) and (
4.16
).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and
r_{0}>0
be such that

(4.17)

B(r_{0})\subset K.

Let
L
be a compact convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and denote

(4.18)

K_{t}=K+tL.

Then, there exist
C>0,\delta_{0}>0
which depend only on
r_{0}
and
\max_{L}|x|
, such that

(4.19)

\left|\frac{\log\rho_{K_{t}}-\log\rho_{K}}{t}\right|<C\qquad\text{on }{S^{n-1}},

for every
t\in(0,\delta_{0})
.

Proof.

Since
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and
L
is a compact convex set, for sufficiently small
t>0
dependent only on
r_{0}
and
\max_{L}|x|
, we have that
K_{t}\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
. For simplicity, we will write
h_{t}=h_{K_{t}}
. Note that
h_{t}
is a positive continuous function on
{S^{n-1}}
and
[h_{t}]=K_{t}
.

Note that on
{S^{n-1}}
,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\log h_{t}

\displaystyle=\log(h_{0}+th_{L})

\displaystyle=\log h_{0}+\log(1+th_{L}/h_{K})

\displaystyle=\log h_{0}+t\frac{h_{L}}{h_{K}}+o(t,\cdot),

where

(4.21)

|o(t,v)|\leq\frac{h_{L}(v)^{2}}{2(h_{K}(v)-|th_{L}(v)|)^{2}}t^{2},\quad v\in{S^{n-1}}.

It is simple to see that there exist
\delta_{0},C_{1}>0
that only depend on
r_{0}
and
\max_{L}|x|
, such that for each
0<t<\delta_{0}
, we have

(4.22)

\left|\frac{o(t,v)}{t}\right|<C_{1},

uniformly in
t
and
v
. By Lemma
4.2
, (
4.20
), and (
4.22
), for each fixed
0<t<\delta_{0}

(4.23)

\left|\frac{\log\rho_{K_{t}}-\log\rho_{K}}{t}\right|\leq\frac{\max_{v\in{S^{n-1}}}\left|\log h_{t}(v)-\log h_{0}(v)\right|}{t}<C,

where
C>0
only depends on
r_{0}
, and
\max_{L}|x|
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Under the same assumptions as in Lemma
4.3
, for each

, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
dependent only on
r_{0}
and
\max_{L}|x|
such that

(4.24)

\left|\frac{\rho^{q}_{K_{t}}-\rho^{q}_{K}}{t}\right|<2^{q}Cq\rho_{K}^{q},\quad\forall t\in(0,\delta_{0}).

Here

is from Lemma

.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
and the mean value theorem,

(4.25)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\rho^{q}_{K_{t}}-\rho^{q}_{K}}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{e^{q\log\rho_{K_{t}}}-e^{q\log\rho_{K}}}{\log\rho_{K_{t}}-\log\rho_{K}}\right|\left|\frac{\log\rho_{K_{t}}-\log\rho_{K}}{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\frac{e^{q\log\rho_{K_{t}}}-e^{q\log\rho_{K}}}{\log\rho_{K_{t}}-\log\rho_{K}}\right|

\displaystyle=Cq\theta^{q},

where
\theta
is between
\rho_{K_{t}}
and
\rho_{K}
. Since
B(r_{0})\subset K
and

is compact, for sufficiently small
\delta_{0}
(dependent on

and
\max_{L}|x|)
, we have

for each

. Consequently,

, which immediately gives the desired bound.
∎

Denote
[f\geq s]=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:f(x)\geq s\}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
q>0
,
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with non-zero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm and
L
be a compact convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with
o\in L
. Assume
f
achieves its maximum at
o
, and

(4.26)

\limsup_{x\rightarrow o}\frac{|f(x)-f(o)|}{|x|^{\alpha+1}}<\infty,

for some
0<\alpha<1
.
Then,

(4.27)

\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{V}_{1,q}([f\geq s],L)\,ds<\infty.

Proof.

For simplicity, write

(4.28)

K_{s}=[f\geq s],

and
M=f(o)=\max f
.

We first claim that there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
c_{0}>0
such that

(4.29)

K_{s}\supset c_{0}(M-s)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}B,

for any
s\in(M-\varepsilon_{0},M)
. Indeed by (
4.26
), there exist
\Lambda>0
and
\eta_{0}>0
such that for every
x\in B(\eta_{0})
, we have

(4.30)

M-f(x)=|f(x)-f(o)|<\Lambda|x|^{\alpha+1},

where we used the fact that
M=f(o)=\max f
. Equivalently, this implies that for every
x\in B(\eta_{0})
, we have

(4.31)

f(x)>M-\Lambda|x|^{\alpha+1}.

A direct computation now shows that if
s\in(0,M)
, then

(4.32)

K_{s}\supset B\left(\left(\frac{M-s}{\Lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\right)\cap B(\eta_{0}).

We now choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
so that for every
s\in(M-\varepsilon_{0},M)
, we have

(4.33)

B\left(\left(\frac{M-s}{\Lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\right)\subset B(\eta_{0}).

Consequently, we have

(4.34)

K_{s}\supset B\left(\left(\frac{M-s}{\Lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\right)=c_{0}(M-s)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}B,

for some
c_{0}>0
.

In particular, (
4.29
) implies, for each
s<M
, that the set
K_{s}
contains the origin in the interior. We also require that
\varepsilon_{0}
is sufficiently small so that
K_{s}\subset c_{1}B
for each
s\in(M-\varepsilon_{0},M)
and some
c_{1}>0
. This is possible since
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}\cap\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
implies that
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}f(x)=0
.

Note that by layer cake representation, we have

(4.35)

\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{M}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds

Step 1:

(4.36)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{M-\varepsilon_{0}}^{M}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds=\int_{M-\varepsilon_{0}}^{M}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds.

In particular, the integral on the right is finite.

Proof of step 1:

For
t\in(0,1)
and
s\in(M-\varepsilon_{0},M)
, let

(4.37)

g(t;s)=\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL).

By Lemma
4.1
, for each
s\in(M-\varepsilon_{0},M)
, the function
g(t;s)
is differentiable in
t
and

(4.38)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g(t;s)=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\frac{h_{L}(v)}{h_{K_{s}+tL}(v)}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K_{s}+tL,v).

Therefore, by the mean value theorem

(4.39)

\frac{g(t;s)-g(0;s)}{t}=\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\frac{h_{L}(v)}{h_{K_{s}+\theta L}(v)}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K_{s}+\theta L,v),

where
\theta\in[0,t]
and is dependent on
s
. Since
o\in L
, we have
K_{s}+\theta L\supset K_{s}\supset c_{0}(M-s)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}B
. Since
L
is compact, for
t\in(0,1)
, we have,

(4.40)

\displaystyle\frac{g(t;s)-g(0;s)}{t}

\displaystyle\leq q\max_{L}|x|\cdot\frac{1}{c_{0}}(M-s)^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+L)

\displaystyle\leq q\max_{L}|x|\cdot\frac{1}{c_{0}}(M-s)^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\widetilde{V}_{q}(c_{1}B+L)

\displaystyle\leq C(M-s)^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}},

for some
C>0
.

Note that
(M-s)^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
is integrable near
M
thanks to
\alpha>0
. Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem, we get the desired result.
∎

Step 2:

(4.41)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds=\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds.

In particular, the integral on the right is finite.

Proof to step 2:

For each
s\in(0,M-\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

(4.42)

K_{s}\supset K_{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\supset\lambda_{0}B.

By Corollary
4.4
, for
t\in(0,\delta_{0})
(where
\delta_{0}
is from Corollary
4.4
),

(4.43)

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}=\frac{1}{q}\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\frac{\rho^{q}_{K_{s}+tL}-\rho_{K_{s}}^{q}}{t}du\leq 2^{q}C\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\rho_{K_{s}}^{q}du.

Here the constant
C
is from Corollary
4.4
. In particular,
C
and
\delta_{0}
are not dependent on
s
.

Note now that

(4.44)

\frac{1}{q}\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{{S^{n-1}}}\rho_{K_{s}}^{q}duds=\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})ds\leq\int_{0}^{M}\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})ds=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|x|^{q-n}f(x)dx<\infty.

Therefore, we may use the dominated convergence theorem to justify the exchange of limit and integration.
∎

By Step 1 and Step 2, we have

(4.45)

\displaystyle\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\widetilde{V}_{1,q}([f\geq s],L)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{V}_{1,q}([f\geq s],L)ds.

Note that it is included in Steps 1 and 2 that the right-hand side is finite.
∎

We first show the validity of Theorem
1.3
in the special case that
g
is a constant multiple of a characteristic function.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
q>0
,
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with non-zero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm and
L
be a compact convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with
o\in L
. Assume
f
achieves its maximum at
o
and

(4.46)

\limsup_{x\rightarrow o}\frac{|f(x)-f(o)|}{|x|^{\alpha+1}}<\infty,

for some
0<\alpha<1
.

Let
g=c1_{L}=e^{-\psi}
for some
c>0
. Then,

(4.47)

\delta_{q}(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi^{*}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Proof.

We first restrict ourselves to the case where
L\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
.

We may assume without loss of generality that

. In this case, by (
2.16
),
\psi^{*}=h_{L}
.

According to Theorem
4.5
, (
4.11
), and (
3.9
), we have

(4.48)

\displaystyle\infty>\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{V}_{1,q}([f\geq s],L)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Per_{L,\omega_{q}}(\partial[f\geq s])ds

\displaystyle=TV_{L,\omega_{q}}(f;{\mathbb{R}^{n}}).

By (
3.5
) and (
4.10
), we have

(4.49)

\displaystyle\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})=TV_{L,\omega_{q}}(f;{\mathbb{R}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{L}(\nabla\phi)f(x)|x|^{q-n}dx+\int_{\partial K_{f}}h_{L}(\nu_{K_{f}})f(x)|x|^{q-n}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{L}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Here, the last equality follows straightly from the definition of
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}
and
\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}
. See Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
.

To see that the result still holds when

(4.50)

\displaystyle\delta_{q}(f,1_{L^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{L^{\prime}}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L^{\prime}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{L}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{L}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v)+\delta_{q}(f,1_{B}).

On the other hand, note that

4.5
, we have

\delta_{q}(f,1_{L^{\prime}})=\delta_{q}(f,1_{L})+\delta_{q}(f,1_{B}).

The desired result now follows from combining (
4.50
) and (
4.51
).
∎

.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
f=e^{-\phi},g=e^{-\psi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be such that
f
has nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm and
g
is compactly supported. Then for almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, we have

(4.52)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{(f\oplus(t\cdot g))(x)-f(x)}{t}=\psi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))f(x).

We are now ready to prove the promised variational formula; that is, compute the limit in (
4.5
).

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
q>0
and
f=e^{-\phi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with non-zero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm. Assume
f
achieves its maximum at
o
and

(4.53)

\limsup_{x\rightarrow o}\frac{|f(x)-f(o)|}{|x|^{\alpha+1}}<\infty,

for some
0<\alpha<1
. Let
g=e^{-\psi}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be compactly supported and
g(o)>0
. Then,

(4.54)

\delta_{q}(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi^{*}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{K_{g}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Proof.

Let
f_{t}=f\oplus(t\cdot g)
. Since
g
is compactly supported, there exists
A>0
such that
g\leq A1_{K_{g}}
and therefore
f_{t}\leq\widetilde{f}_{t}=f\oplus(t\cdot A1_{K_{g}})
.

By Lemma
4.7
,

(4.55)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{f}_{t}-f_{t}}{t}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{f}_{t}-f}{t}-\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{{f}_{t}-f}{t}=h_{K_{g}}(\nabla\phi)f+\ln Af-\psi^{*}(\nabla\phi)f.

By Fatou’s lemma, we have

(4.56)

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\widetilde{f}_{t}-f_{t}}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[h_{K_{g}}(\nabla\phi)f+\ln Af-\psi^{*}(\nabla\phi)f]\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(h_{K_{g}}(y)-\psi^{*}(y))d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\ln A\cdot\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)

Since
g(o)>0
, we have
o\in K_{g}
. By Theorem
4.6
, we have

(4.57)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{\widetilde{f}_{t}-f}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{K_{g}}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\ln A\cdot\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{K_{g}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Combining the above two formulas, we have

(4.58)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{f_{t}-f}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\psi^{*}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{K_{g}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

For the other direction of the inequality, define for each positive integer
j
, the set

(4.59)

Q_{j}=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:g(x)\geq\frac{1}{j}\right\}.

Since
g
is compactly supported, we conclude that
Q_{j}\uparrow K_{g}
. Since
g(o)>0
, for sufficiently large
j
, we have
o\in Q_{j}
. We focus on such
j
.

Let

(4.60)

\bar{g}_{j}=\frac{1}{j}1_{Q_{j}},\quad\text{and }\bar{f}_{j,t}=f\oplus t\cdot\bar{g}_{j}.

Note that
\bar{f}_{j,t}\leq f_{t}
. Arguing the same way as before, we have

(4.61)

\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{f_{t}-\bar{f}_{j,t}}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\psi^{*}(y)-h_{Q_{j}}(y))\,d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\ln j\cdot\widetilde{V}_{q}(f),

and

(4.62)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{\bar{f}_{j,t}-f}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{Q_{j}}(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)-\ln j\cdot\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{Q_{j}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Adding the above two formulas, we have

(4.63)

\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{f_{t}-f}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi^{*}(y)\,d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{Q_{j}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

Letting
j\rightarrow\infty
, by the monotone convergence theorem, we have

(4.64)

\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{f_{t}-f}{t}|x|^{q-n}dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi^{*}(y)\,d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y)+\int_{{S^{n-1}}}h_{K_{g}}(v)d\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;v).

This completes the proof.
∎

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds=\int_{0}^{M-\varepsilon_{0}}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s}+tL)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K_{s})}{t}ds.

and

. Consequently, there exist
c_{2}>0
independent of

and

4.6
, by Proposition
4.9
, we conclude that with the additional assumption that the origin is an interior point of the support of
g
, Theorem
4.8
continues to hold with (
4.53
) replaced by (
4.67
). In particular, hypothesis (
4.67
) allows for log-concave functions such as

(4.81)

f(x)=e^{-\|x\|_{K}},

where
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
. It is of great interest to see whether assumptions like (
4.53
) and (
4.67
) can be dropped altogether.

A few additional remarks are in order:

(1)

Theorem
4.8
justifies why we referred to
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
and
\widetilde{C}_{q}^{s}(f;\dot{)}
as the Euclidean and spherical dual curvature measures for log-concave functions. In particular, when
f
and
g
are characteristic functions of convex bodies containing the origin in their respective interiors, (
4.54
) recovers its convex geometric counterpart (
1.5
).

(2)

Since
\phi
is convex and therefore almost everywhere differentiable in the support of
f
, the measure
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
is well-defined. Notice that its total measure is equal to
\widetilde{V}_{q}(f)
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.1
, as long as
f
is in
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, the measure
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
is always finite.

(3)

Since
f
is log-concave, its support
K_{f}
is necessarily convex and therefore it makes sense to write
\nu_{K_{f}}
. However, even with the assumption that
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, it might not be the case that the measure
\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\cdot)
is finite. To see this, simply take the example where
K_{f}
has a facet that contains the origin in the relative interior. In that case, the density
|x|^{q-n}
will have a non-integrable singularity at the origin in that subspace when
q<1
. This counterexample suggests that we must impose some condition on
f
such that
f
“contains the origin in the interior”. With the additional assumption that
K_{f}
contains the origin in its interior, one may show that
\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\cdot)
is always a finite measure.

(4)

When
f=1_{K}
is the characteristic function of a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, the Euclidean dual curvature measure
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
reduces to a point mass concentrated at the origin and

(4.82)

\widetilde{C}^{s}_{q}(f;\cdot)=\widetilde{C}_{1,q}(K,\cdot).

(5)

Let us emphasize again that although the weight function
\omega_{q}=|x|^{q-n}
is the only type of weight functions treated in this section, many results presented here can be shown for other weight functions (with only small changes). In particular, the Gaussian weight function is one of the many weight functions that can be used here to replace
\omega_{q}
. We mention that in the setting of convex bodies, a variational formula for the Gaussian volume and its corresponding Gaussian Minkowski problem was previously studied by the authors in
[
49
]
.

5.
The Minkowski problem for
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}

The purpose of this section is to study the following Minkowski problem for the Euclidean dual curvature measure of log-concave functions.

We will restrict our attention to the even case—the prescribed measure
\mu
in (
1.13
) is even and we are restricting our solution set to all even functions
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

5.1.
The variational structure

In this subsection, we convert the solvability of (
1.13
) into the existence of a minimizer to a minimization problem.

We recall the following result in
[
68
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
68
]
)
.

Let
\phi,\zeta:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
be lower semi-continuous functions. Assume
\zeta
is bounded from below and
\zeta(0),\phi(0)<\infty
. Then at every point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
where
\phi^{*}
is differentiable, we have

(5.1)

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0^{+}}(\phi+t\zeta)^{*}(x_{0})=-\zeta(\nabla\phi^{*}(x_{0})).

Note that in
[
68
, Proposition 2.1]
, the derivative is stated as a one-sided derivative. However, for
\zeta\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, one can consider
-\zeta
and immediately get that the derivative is double-sided.

We will require the following variational formula.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be an even function and
q>0
. Suppose
f
has non-zero finite
(q-n)
-th moment and takes the form
f=e^{-\phi}
for some
\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
. For
\zeta\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, define

(5.2)

\phi_{t}(x)=(\phi^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}(x),

and

(5.3)

f_{t}(x)=e^{-\phi_{t}(x)}.

Then, we have

(5.4)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\widetilde{V}_{q}(f_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(\nabla\phi(x))|x|^{q-n}f(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(y)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;y).

Proof.

We note that the second equality follows directly from the definition of
\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;\cdot)
and therefore only the first equality needs a proof.

By the fact the
f
is even, Proposition
5.1
, and the remark immediately below it, we have

(5.5)

\left.\frac{\partial\phi_{t}(x)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}=-\zeta(\nabla\phi(x)),

almost everywhere in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

For simplicity, write

(5.6)

h_{t}(x)=|x|^{q-n}f_{t}(x).

Since
\zeta\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, there exists
M>0
such that
|\zeta|\leq M
. Therefore, we have

(5.7)

\phi^{*}-|t|M\leq\phi^{*}+t\zeta\leq\phi^{*}+|t|M.

Since Legendre transform reverses the order, we have

(5.8)

\phi-|t|M=(\phi^{*}+|t|M)^{*}\leq(\phi^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}\leq(\phi^{*}-|t|M)^{*}=\phi+|t|M.

Using the above estimates, we have the existence of
C>0
, such that

(5.9)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{h_{t}(x)-h(x)}{t}\right|

\displaystyle=|x|^{q-n}\left|\frac{e^{-\phi_{t}(x)}-e^{-\phi(x)}}{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi(x)}\left|\max\left\{\frac{e^{|t|M}-1}{t},\frac{e^{-|t|M}-1}{t}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi(x)},

for sufficiently small
|t|
. Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\widetilde{V}_{q}(f_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}h_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(\nabla\phi(x))|x|^{q-n}f(x)dx.

∎

Let
A>0
be an arbitrary number and
\mu
be a nonzero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Consider the following optimization problem

(5.11)

\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu:\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\geq A,\phi\geq 0,\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu),\phi\text{ is an even function}\right\}.

The following lemma shows that the Euler-Lagrange equation of the above constrained optimization problem implies the existence of a solution to (
1.13
).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
that is not concentrated in any proper subspace. If an even function
\phi_{0}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
is such that
\phi_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
,
\phi_{0}(o)>0
,

(5.12)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})=A,\qquad\text{ for some }A>0,

and

(5.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{0}d\mu=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu:\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\geq A,\phi\geq 0,\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu),\phi\text{ is an even function}\right\},

then

(5.14)

\mu=\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f_{0};\cdot),

where

f_{0}=\frac{|\mu|}{\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})}e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}}.

Moreover,
f_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
.

Proof.

Let
\zeta\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be an even function. Set

(5.15)

\phi_{t}(x)=\phi_{0}(x)+t\zeta(x)

and

(5.16)

\lambda(t)=\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{t}^{*}}).

The fact that
\lambda(t)
is finite for small
|t|
comes from Proposition
2.2
, the fact that
\phi_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, and that
\mu
is a finite measure not concentrated in any proper subspaces.
Set

(5.17)

\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x)=\phi_{t}(x)-\ln\lambda(t)+\ln A.

It is simple to notice that

(5.18)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\widetilde{\phi}_{t}^{*}})=\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{t}^{*}})\frac{A}{\lambda(t)}=A.

Since
\zeta\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, there exists
M>0
such that
|\zeta|<M
. This implies that

(5.19)

\lambda(t)\leq\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})e^{|t|M}=Ae^{|t|M}.

Thus, the choice of
\widetilde{\phi}_{t}
implies that when
|t|
is sufficiently small, we have

(5.20)

\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\geq\phi_{0}(o)-2|t|M>0.

Such
\widetilde{\phi}_{t}
satisfies the constraints of the optimization problem. Since
\phi_{0}
is the minimizer and
\widetilde{\phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}
, by Lemma
5.2
,

(5.21)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widetilde{\phi}_{t}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(x)d\mu(x)-\frac{|\mu|}{A}\lambda^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(x)d\mu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(x)d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f_{0};x),

where
f_{0}=e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}+\ln|\mu|-\ln(A)}
. Since
\zeta
is arbitrary in
C_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, this implies that

(5.22)

\mu=\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f_{0};\cdot).

To see that
f_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, note that since
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspaces and
\phi_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
, we conclude that
\phi_{0}
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin. The fact that
f_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
now follows from the Proposition
2.2
with
q=n
.
∎

Note that although the requirement
\phi\geq 0
in the constraints of the optimization problem is a closed condition, to make the Euler Lagrange equation equal to
0
, we have to establish that the minimizer actually satisfies a stronger condition (
\phi_{0}>0)
. This will be done in the next subsection.

5.2.
Existence of a minimizer

This section is dedicated to showing the existence of a minimizer to (
5.11
) under the assumption that
\mu
is an even measure.

It turns out that the
C^{0}
estimates here are closely related to the estimates of the dual quermassintegrals of level sets of convex functions. This is perhaps not surprising, given that in the case of characteristic functions, the
(q-n)
-th moment of a log-concave function reduces to the
q
-th dual quermassintegral of a convex body. The following lemma reveals the simple fact that if the images of the orthogonal projections of a set of convex bodies onto a
k
-dimensional subspace, where
k=1,\dots,n-1
, are uniformly bounded, then their
q
-th dual quermassintegrals are uniformly bounded when
q<k
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
k=1,\dots,n-1
, and
0<q<k
. For each
R>0
, there exists
C>0
, such that for all
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
satisfying

(5.23)

P_{\xi}K\subset B(R)\cap\xi,\quad\text{ for some $k$-dim subspace }\xi\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n}},

we have

(5.24)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)<C.

Here, we use
P_{\xi}K
to denote the image of the orthogonal projection of
K
onto
\xi
.

Proof.

By (
5.23
), we have

(5.25)

K\subset B(\sqrt{2}R)\cup[P_{\xi}K\times(\xi^{\perp}\setminus B(R))]\subset B(\sqrt{2}R)\cup[(B(R)\cap\xi)\times(\xi^{\perp}\setminus B(R))].

Hence,

(5.26)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{q}(K)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\xi^{\perp}\setminus B(R)}\int_{B(R)\cap\xi}|(y,z)|^{q-n}d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)d\mathcal{H}^{n-k}(z)+\int_{B(\sqrt{2}R)}|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\xi^{\perp}\setminus B(R)}|z|^{q-n}d\mathcal{H}^{n-k}(z)\int_{B(R)\cap\xi}d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)+\frac{C}{q}(\sqrt{2}R)^{q}

\displaystyle=CR^{k}\int_{R}^{\infty}\rho^{q-n}\rho^{n-k-1}d\rho+\frac{C}{q}(\sqrt{2}R)^{q}

\displaystyle=C\frac{1}{k-q}R^{q}+\frac{C}{q}(\sqrt{2}R)^{q}.

∎

For
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
, we write

(5.27)

\overline{V}_{q}(K)=\left(\frac{1}{q}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K}^{q}(u)du\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

for the normalized version of dual quermassintegrals.

A quick application of Lemma
5.4
gives the following Blaschke-Santaló type estimates for normalized dual quermassintegrals. This is a special case of Theorem 3.1 in
[
21
]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
K
be an origin-symmetric convex body in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. If
q\in(0,1)
and
p>0
, then there exists
C>0
independent of
K
such that

(5.28)

\overline{V}_{q}(K^{*})\overline{V}_{p}(K)<C.

Proof.

Let
v_{0}\in{S^{n-1}}
be such that

h_{K^{*}}(v_{0})=\min_{v\in{S^{n-1}}}h_{K^{*}}(v).

Note that the functional
\overline{V}_{q}(K^{*})\overline{V}_{p}(K)
is invariant under rescaling of
K
. Therefore, by rescaling, we may assume
h_{K^{*}}(v_{0})=1
.
This, by the choice of
v_{0}
, implies

(5.29)

B\subset K^{*},

and consequently,

(5.30)

K\subset B.

Moreover,

(5.31)

P_{\xi}K^{*}\subset B\cap\xi,

where
\xi=\Span\{v_{0}\}
. Equation (
5.28
) follows from Lemma
5.4
and (
5.30
).

∎

By integrating (in a certain way) over level sets of a log-concave function, Lemma
5.5
readily implies the following Blaschke-Santaló type inequality for log-concave functions.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be an even function with
\phi(o)=0
. Assume
\phi
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin and
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\phi(x)=\infty
. Suppose
q>0
and
0<p<1
. There exists
C>0
, independent of
\phi
, such that

(5.32)

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi^{*}(x)}dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{q}dt\right)<C.

Proof.

Recall that, for convenience, when no confusion arises, a constant
C>0
might change from line to line (or even within the same line).

By Proposition
2.2
, since
\phi
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin, we have

(5.33)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi^{*}(x)}dx<\infty.

By the definition of the Legendre-Fenchel transform, we have

(5.34)

\phi^{*}(x)=\sup\{\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(y)\}\geq-\phi(o)=0.

Furthermore, we have for any
s,t\geq 0
,

(5.35)

[\phi^{*}\leq s]\subset(s+t)[\phi\leq t]^{*}.

The proof of this fact can be found, for example, in the proof of Theorem 2.1 in
[
3
]
.
Note that since
\phi
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin and
\phi(o)=0
, we conclude that for each
t>0
, the sublevel set
[\phi\leq t]
contains the origin in the interior. On the other hand, since
\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\phi(x)=\infty
, the level set
[\phi\leq t]
is bounded. Lemma
5.5
now implies the existence of
C>0
such that

(5.36)

\overline{V}_{q}([\phi^{*}\leq s])\leq(s+t)\overline{V}_{q}([\phi\leq t]^{*})\leq C(s+t)\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{-1}.

By definition of
\overline{V}_{q}
, we have

(5.37)

\int_{[\phi^{*}\leq s]}|x|^{q-n}dx\leq C(s+t)^{q}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{-q}.

Set

(5.38)

F(s)=\begin{cases}e^{-s}\int_{[\phi^{*}\leq s]}|x|^{q-n}dx,&\text{ if }s\geq 0\\
0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

(5.39)

G(t)=\begin{cases}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{q},&\text{ if }t\geq 0\\
0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

(5.40)

H(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{C}e^{-x}(2x)^{\frac{q}{2}},&\text{ if }x\geq 0,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, (
5.37
) implies that for any
s,t\geq 0
,

(5.41)

H\left(\frac{1}{2}s+\frac{1}{2}t\right)=\sqrt{C}e^{-\frac{s+t}{2}}\left(s+t\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\geq F\left(s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}G\left(t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It is simple to check that the above inequality is also true when
s
or
t
is negative, in which case the right-hand side of the inequality is
0
.

Therefore, by the Prékopa-Leindler inequality, we have

(5.42)

\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\int_{[\phi^{*}\leq s]}|x|^{q-n}dxds\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{q}dt\right)\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{\frac{q}{2}}dx\right)^{2}<C.

The fact that
\phi^{*}\geq 0
and layer-cake representation now imply

(5.43)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{q-n}e^{-\phi^{*}(x)}dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{[e^{-\phi^{*}}\geq t]}|x|^{q-n}dxdt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\int_{[\phi^{*}\leq s]}|x|^{q-n}dxds.

This, when combined with (
5.42
), gives the desired estimate.
∎

The above lemma immediately implies the following comparison.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\phi\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be an even function with
\phi(o)=0
and
\mu
be a nonzero even finite Borel measure not concentrated in any proper subspace. Suppose
q>0
and
\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
. There exist
C_{1}>0
and
C_{2}<0
, independent of
\phi
such that

(5.44)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu\geq C_{1}\widetilde{V}_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}(e^{-\phi^{*}})+C_{2}.

Proof.

If

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})=0,

or equivalently
\phi^{*}
is almost everywhere
+\infty
, there is nothing to prove. Therefore, we may assume that
\phi^{*}
is finite in a neighborhood of the origin, and by Proposition
2.2
, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\phi(x)}dx<\infty,

or equivalently,
\phi\rightarrow\infty
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
.

We first note that since
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspaces, there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

(5.45)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\langle x,u\rangle|d\mu(x)>c_{1},

for every
u\in{S^{n-1}}
.

Set

(5.46)

K=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}:\phi(x)\leq 1\}.

Since
\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
is even and
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspace, we have that
\phi
is finite in a neighborhood of
o
. Therefore, by the fact that
\phi(o)=0
, we have that
K
is a symmetric closed convex set that contains the origin in its interior.

Let
r_{K}
be such that

(5.47)

r_{K}=\sup\{r>0:rB\subset K\}.

The facts that
\phi\rightarrow\infty
when
|x|\rightarrow\infty
imply that
K\neq{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Therefore
0<r_{K}<\infty
. Note that since
K
is closed, there exists
u_{0}\in{S^{n-1}}
such that
r_{K}u_{0}\in\partial K
. This in turn implies that
h_{K}(u_{0})=r_{K}
.

We fix now an arbitrary
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
with
|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|>2r_{K}
. We consider
x^{\prime}=\frac{2r_{K}}{|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|}x
. Note that by choice of
x
, we have
|\langle x^{\prime},u_{0}\rangle|>r_{K}
. This implies that
x^{\prime}\notin K
and therefore
\phi(x^{\prime})>1
. By convexity of
\phi
, we now have

(5.48)

1<\phi(x^{\prime})=\phi\left(\left(1-\frac{2r_{K}}{|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|}\right)o+\frac{2r_{K}}{|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|}x\right)\leq\frac{2r_{K}}{|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|}\phi(x).

Hence, for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, we have

(5.49)

\phi(x)+1\geq\frac{1}{2r_{K}}|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|.

Integrating with respect to
\mu
, we have

(5.50)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu\geq\frac{1}{2r_{K}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\langle x,u_{0}\rangle|d\mu(x)-|\mu|\geq\frac{c_{1}}{2r_{K}}-|\mu|.

We now estimate the right-hand side of (
5.44
). By Lemma
5.6
, there exist
0<p<1
and
c_{2}>0
such that

(5.51)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})<c_{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{q}dt\right)^{-1}.

Note that by the convexity of
\phi
and since
\phi(o)=0
, we have

(5.52)

[\phi\leq t]\supset tK,

for
t<1
. Therefore, we have

(5.53)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}([\phi\leq t])^{q}dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}e^{-t}\overline{V}_{p}(tK)^{q}dt

\displaystyle=\overline{V}_{p}(K)^{q}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-t}t^{q}dt

\displaystyle\geq\overline{V}_{p}(r_{K}B)^{q}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-t}t^{q}dt

\displaystyle=c_{3}r_{K}^{q},

for some
c_{3}>0
.

Combining (
5.51
) and (
5.53
), for some
c_{4}>0
, we have

(5.54)

\widetilde{V}_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\leq\frac{c_{4}}{r_{K}}.

By (
5.50
), we have the existence of
c_{5}>0
such that

(5.55)

\int\phi d\mu\geq c_{5}\widetilde{V}_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}(e^{-\phi^{*}})-|\mu|=:C_{1}\widetilde{V}_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}(e^{-\phi^{*}})+C_{2},

for some
C_{1}>0
and
C_{2}<0
.
∎

In
[
33
, Lemma 17]
, Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag demonstrated that, if
\phi_{k}
is a sequence of nonnegative convex functions with uniform
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
bound and
\phi_{k}(o)=0
, then one may construct a subsequence
\phi_{k_{j}}
and a nonnegative convex function
\phi
such that the
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
norm of
\phi
is bounded from above by the lower limit of the
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
norm of the subsequence while
\widetilde{V}_{n}(e^{-\phi^{*}})
is bounded from below by the upper limit of
\widetilde{V}_{n}(e^{-\phi_{k_{j}}^{*}})
. As observed by Rotem
[
68
]
, the assumption
\phi_{k}(o)=0
is only used to know that
\phi_{k}(\lambda x)
is increasing in
\lambda
on
(0,1)
and this is trivially true when
\phi_{k}
is even. Upon further inspection of the proof, it is not hard to see that such a “selection theorem” holds for any
q>0
. We state the following generalized version without providing a proof.

Lemma 5.8

(
[
33
]
)
.

Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Assume
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspace. If
\phi_{k}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
is non-negative, even and

(5.56)

\sup_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{k}d\mu<\infty,

then, there exists a subsequence
\phi_{k_{j}}
and a non-negative, even, convex function
\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
such that

(5.57)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu\leq\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{k_{j}}d\mu,\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\geq\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{k_{j}}^{*}}).

We are now ready to show the existence of a minimizer to (
5.11
).

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Suppose
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspace and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu(x)<\infty
. For sufficiently large
A>0
, there exists an even function
\phi_{0}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
such that
\phi_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
,
\phi_{0}(o)>0
,

(5.58)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})=A,

and

(5.59)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{0}d\mu=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu:\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\geq A,\phi\geq 0,\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu),\phi\text{ is an even function}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
c_{n}>0
be such that

\int_{B(c_{n})}|x|^{q-n}dx=1.

Set

(5.60)

C=c_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu>0.

By the condition on
\mu
, it is simple to see that
C
is finite.

We choose a fixed
A>0
such that

(5.61)

|\mu|\ln A+C<C_{1}A^{\frac{1}{q}}+C_{2},

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are from Lemma
5.7
and depend only on
\mu
and
q
. It is simple to see that such an
A
exists and in fact, all sufficiently large
A>0
satisfies (
5.61
).

Let
\phi_{k}
be a minimizing sequence; that is, we have
\phi_{k}\geq 0
,
\phi_{k}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu)
,
\phi_{k}
is even,

(5.62)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{k}^{*}})\geq A,

and

(5.63)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{k}d\mu=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi d\mu:\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi^{*}})\geq A,\phi\geq 0,\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mu),\phi\text{ is an even function}\right\}.

Notice that

(5.64)

\phi_{k}^{**}\leq\phi_{k},

and since
\phi_{k}\geq 0
, we have
\phi_{k}^{**}\geq 0
. Moreover, note that
(\phi_{k}^{**})^{*}=\phi_{k}^{*}
. Therefore, we may without loss of generality, assume that
\phi_{k}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

We claim now that

(5.65)

\sup_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{k}d\mu<\infty.

To see this, set

(5.66)

\Gamma(x)=\ln A+c_{n}|x|,

It is simple to compute that

(5.67)

\Gamma^{*}(x)=1^{\infty}_{B(c_{n})}(x)-\ln A,

where

(5.68)

1^{\infty}_{B(c_{n})}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }x\in B(c_{n}),\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover,

(5.69)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\Gamma^{*}})=A,

according to the choice of
c_{n}
. Note that
\Gamma(x)
is positive and even. Moreover,

(5.70)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Gamma(x)d\mu=|\mu|\ln A+C,

Since
\phi_{k}
is a minimizing sequence, we conclude (
5.65
).

By Lemma
5.8
, there exists a subsequence
\phi_{k_{j}}
and a non-negative even convex function
\phi_{0}
such that (
5.57
) holds. In particular, this suggests that
\phi_{0}
is a minimizer to (
5.59
). By possibly replacing
\phi_{0}
by
\phi_{0}^{**}
, we may assume that
\phi_{0}\in\cvx({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

It remains to show that
\phi_{0}(o)>0
and

(5.71)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})=A.

To see the former, we argue by contradiction. Assume
\phi_{0}(o)=0
. Therefore, we may conclude by using Lemma
5.7
that

(5.72)

|\mu|\ln A+C\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{0}d\mu\geq C_{1}\widetilde{V}_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})+C_{2}\geq C_{1}A^{\frac{1}{q}}+C_{2}.

This is a contradiction to (
5.61
).

To show (
5.71
), if it was not the case, we set

(5.73)

\widetilde{\phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}-\varepsilon.

Note that for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\widetilde{\phi}_{0}>0
thanks to
\phi_{0}(o)>0
. Moreover,

(5.74)

\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\widetilde{\phi}_{0}^{*}})=e^{-\varepsilon}\widetilde{V}_{q}(e^{-\phi_{0}^{*}})>A,

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. However, it is trivial to see

(5.75)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widetilde{\phi}_{0}d\mu<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\phi}_{0}d\mu,

which contradicts the minimality of
\phi_{0}
.
∎

Lemmas
5.3
and
5.9
now immediately solve the Minkowski problem (
1.13
).

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
q>0
and
\mu
be a non-zero even finite Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Suppose
\mu
is not concentrated in any proper subspace and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu(x)<\infty
. There exists an even
f_{0}\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm such that

(5.76)

\mu=\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f_{0};\cdot).

To complete this section, we show that the assumption that

(5.77)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|d\mu(x)<\infty

is necessary in Theorem
5.10
. We require the following basic lemma about log-concave functions.

Lemma 5.11
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a log-concave function. If

(5.78)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}f(t)=0,

then, for each
t_{0}\geq 0
, we have

(5.79)

\int_{|t|\geq t_{0}}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt\leq 4\sup_{|t|\geq t_{0}}f(t).

Proof.

Since
f
is log-concave, it is locally Lipschitz in the interior of the interval in which it is positive. Moreover, it is unimodal. Therefore, by (
5.78
) and the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

(5.80)

\int_{t\geq t_{0}}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt\leq 2\sup_{t\geq t_{0}}f(t),

and

(5.81)

\int_{t\leq-t_{0}}|f^{\prime}(t)|dt\leq 2\sup_{t\leq-t_{0}}f(t).

Combining the above two inequality gives us the desired result.
∎

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
q>0
and
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
be with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm. If
K_{f}
, the support of
f
, contains the origin as an interior point, then

(5.82)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|x|d\widetilde{C}^{e}_{q}(f;x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|\nabla f(x)|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

In particular, (
5.82
) is valid for even
f\in\Lc({\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with nonzero finite
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
norm.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
q\in(0,n]
.

Since
K_{f}
contains the origin as an interior point, the function
f
is Lipschitz in
B(r_{0})
for some
r_{0}>0
. Denote the Lipschitz constant of
f
inside
B(r_{0})
by
\Lambda>0
. This implies that inside
B(r_{0})
, we have
|\nabla f|\leq\Lambda
almost everywhere. Therefore,

(5.83)

\int_{B(r_{0})}|\nabla f(x)|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx\leq\Lambda\int_{B(r_{0})}|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

Therefore, to show (
5.82
), we only need to show

(5.84)

\int_{B(r_{0})^{c}}|\nabla f(x)|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

In particular, since

(5.85)

\displaystyle|\nabla f(x)|=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x)\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\right|,

without loss of generality, it is sufficient to show

(5.86)

\int_{B(r_{0})^{c}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\right|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

We write
x=(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. Note that since
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, then
f\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, since
0<q\leq n
, by Lemma
5.11
,

(5.87)

\displaystyle\int_{B(r_{0})^{c}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x)\right|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{B(r_{0}/2)^{c}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{B(r_{0}/2)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{|t|\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}r_{0}}\right)\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right||(y,t)|^{q-n}dtdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\int_{B(r_{0}/2)^{c}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}f(y,t)\cdot|y|^{q-n}dy+\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}r_{0}\right)^{q-n}\int_{B(r_{0}/2)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{|t|\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}r_{0}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right|dtdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\int_{B(r_{0}/2)^{c}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}f(y,t)\cdot|y|^{q-n}dy+4\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}r_{0}\right)^{q-n}\int_{B(r_{0}/2)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}f(y,t)dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\int_{B(r_{0}/2)^{c}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}f(y,t)\cdot|y|^{q-n}dy+C,

for some positive constant
C
depending on
r_{0}
and
\sup f
.
Since
f\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, if we write
f=e^{-\phi}
, we have

(5.88)

\liminf_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|}>0.

In particular, this implies the existence of
c_{0}>0
and
M>0
such that for all
|(y,t)|>M
, we have

(5.89)

\phi(y,t)>c_{0}|(y,t)|.

The desired result follows from combining (
5.87
) and (
5.89
).

Let us now consider the case
q>n
.

We first note that

(5.90)

\int_{B(M^{2})}|\nabla f(x)|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx\leq M^{2(q-n)}\int_{B(M^{2})}|\nabla f(x)|dx\leq M^{2(q-n)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|\nabla f(x)|dx<\infty,

where the last inequality follows from the previously established case
q=n
. Therefore, we only need to show

(5.91)

\int_{B(M^{2})^{c}}|\nabla f(x)|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx<\infty.

Note that,

(5.92)

\displaystyle\int_{B(M^{2})^{c}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\right|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}\int_{B(k+1)\setminus B(k)}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\right|\cdot|x|^{q-n}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{B(k+1)\setminus B(k)}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\left(\int_{[B(k+1)\setminus B(k-1)]\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{B(k-1)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{|t|\geq\sqrt{k}}\right)\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right|dtdy

For the first term, by (
5.89
), we have

(5.93)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{[B(k+1)\setminus B(k-1)]\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right|dtdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{[B(k+1)\setminus B(k-1)]\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}f(y,t)dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{[B(k+1)\setminus B(k-1)]\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-c_{0}|y|}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4C\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}e^{-c_{0}(k-1)}(k+1)^{n-1}<\infty.

For the second term, by using Lemma
5.11
again, we have

(5.94)

\int_{|t|\geq\sqrt{k}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right|dt\leq 4\sup_{|t|\geq\sqrt{k}}f(y,t),

and consequently, by (
5.89
),

(5.95)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{B(k-1)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{|t|\geq\sqrt{k}}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(y,t)\right|dtdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{B(k-1)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\sup_{|t|\geq\sqrt{k}}f(y,t)dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}\int_{B(k-1)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-c_{0}\sqrt{k}}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4C\sum_{k=M^{2}}^{\infty}(k+1)^{q-n}(k-1)^{n-1}e^{-c_{0}\sqrt{k}}<\infty.

Equation (
5.91
) now follows from (
5.92
), (
5.93
), and (
5.95
).
∎
