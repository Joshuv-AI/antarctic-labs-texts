Partial-Monotone Adaptive Submodular Maximization
Shaojie Tang, Jing Yuan

Abstract
Many AI/Machine learning problems require adaptively selecting a sequence of items, each selected item might provide some feedback that is valuable for making better selections in the future, with the goal of maximizing an adaptive submodular function. Most of existing studies in this field focus on either monotone case or non-monotone case. Specifically, if the utility function is monotone and adaptive submodular, [ 4 ] developed ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/e) approximation solution subject to a cardinality constraint. For the cardinality-constrained non-monotone case, [ 9 ] showed that a random greedy policy attains an approximation ratio of 1 / e 1/e . In this work, we generalize the above mentioned results by studying the partial-monotone adaptive submodular maximization problem. To this end, we introduce the notation of adaptive monotonicity ratio m ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] m\in[0,1] to measure the degree of monotonicity of a function. Our main result is to show that for the case of cardinality constraints, if the utility function has an adaptive monotonicity ratio of m m and it is adaptive submodular, then a random greedy policy attains an approximation ratio of m ⁡ ( 1 − 1 / e ) + ( 1 − m ) ​ ( 1 / e ) m(1-1/e)+(1-m)(1/e) . Notably this result recovers the aforementioned ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/e) and 1 / e 1/e approximation ratios when m = 1 m=1 and m = 0 m=0 , respectively. We further extend our results to consider a knapsack constraint and develop a ( m + 1 ) / 10 (m+1)/10 approximation solution for this general case. One important implication of our results is that even for a non-monotone utility function, we still can attain an approximation ratio close to ( 1 − 1 / e ) (1-1/e) if this function is “close” to a monotone function. This leads to improved performance bounds for many machine learning applications whose utility functions are almost adaptive monotone.

Introduction

We consider an adaptive stochastic optimization problem whose input is a set of items, each of which has a random state. The realized state of an item can not be observed until it has been selected. We assume there is a utility function whose value is jointly decided by the set of selected items and the realization of all items’ states. Our goal is to design an adaptive policy that builds up a set adaptively to maximize the utility. That is, after selecting an item, we observe its realized state, which can be leveraged to make better decisions in the future. Consider experiment design as a running example, in this application, each item represents a test such as temperature, and the state of an item represents a possible outcome from a test. In this example, our objective is to come up with a sequence of tests to draw an accurate conclusion about a patient. Items, States and Policies

Assume we are given a set

\Phi(e)\in O

of each test

represents the outcome of
e

\Phi(\verb"temperature test")\in O.

We use

to represent a

of
\Phi
. Hence, for each item

,
\phi(e)\in O
is the realization of
\Phi(e)
. The realization of
\Phi(e)
is not known until
e
has been selected. We assume that the distribution of
\Phi
is known, that is, we know the probability

of each realization
\phi\in U

. We call a realization

We define an adaptive policy as a function

from partial

(e.g., the next item to select). For the example of experiment design,

Definition 1

Given two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, we define
\pi@\pi^{\prime}
as a new policy that runs
\pi
first, and then runs
\pi^{\prime}
from a fresh start.

In addition, we assume there is a utility function
f:2^{E}\times O^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. For any subset of items

and any realization
\phi
, the value of

measures the utility of selecting
A
conditional on
\phi
. We define the expected utility of a policy

as follows

f_{avg}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\Phi\sim p(\phi),\Pi}f(E(\pi,\Phi),\Phi),

(1)

where
E(\pi,\phi)
denotes the set of items selected by
\pi
given
\phi
;
\Pi
represents the internal randomness of a policy
\pi
.

2.2
Problem Formulation

Assume selecting an item
e\in E
incurs a cost
c(e)
, our objective is to find a policy
\pi^{opt}
to maximize the expected utility subject to a budget constraint
k
:

\pi^{opt}\in\argmax_{\pi:\forall\phi\in U^{+},\sum_{e\in E(\pi,\phi)}c(e)\leq k}f_{avg}(\pi),

where
U^{+}=\{\phi\mid p(\phi)>0\}
represents the set of
possible
realizations.

That is, we aim to build up a set
A
, whose total cost is at most
k
, sequentially and adaptively such that
f(A,\phi)
is maximized where
\phi
is unknown initially.

2.3
Additional Notations

Definition 2

The marginal utility of adding an item
e
to
\psi
is

\Delta(e\mid\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{\Phi}[f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi)\cup\{e\},\Phi)-f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi),\Phi)\mid\Phi\sim\psi].

Similarly, the marginal utility of adding a set of items
S\subseteq E
to
\psi
is

\Delta(S\mid\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{\Phi}[f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi)\cup S,\Phi)-f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi),\Phi)\mid\Phi\sim\psi].

Definition 3

The marginal utility of running a policy
\pi
on top of
\psi
is

\Delta(\pi\mid\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{\Phi}[f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi)\cup E(\pi,\Phi),\Phi)-f(\mathrm{dom}(\psi),\Phi)\mid\Phi\sim\psi].

With these notations, we are in position to introduce two important concepts from
[
4
]
.

Definition 4

A function
f
is called
adaptive submodular
, if

\Delta(e\mid\psi)\geq\Delta(e\mid\psi^{\prime})

for all
\psi\subseteq\psi^{\prime}
and all
e\in E\setminus\mathrm{dom}(\psi^{\prime})
.

Definition 5

A function
f
is called
adaptive monotone
, if

\Delta(e\mid\psi)\geq 0

for all
\psi
and all
e\in E
.

We next introduce the notation of
adaptive monotonicity ratio

m\in[0,1]
. Intuitively, this ratio, which can be viewed as an adaptive version of the (non-adaptive) monotonicity ratio introduced in
[
6
,
7
]
, captures the degree of adaptive monotonicity of a function
f
.

Definition 6

The adaptive monotonicity ratio of a function
f
is

\displaystyle m\overset{\Delta}{=}\min_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}\frac{f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})}{f_{avg}(\pi)}

(2)

where the ratio
\frac{f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})}{f_{avg}(\pi)}
is assumed to be
1
if
f_{avg}(\pi)=0
.

As proved in
[
4
]
, if
f
is adaptive monotone, then
f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})\geq f_{avg}(\pi)
for any two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
. Hence,
\frac{f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})}{f_{avg}(\pi)}\geq 1
where the equality occurs when
\pi^{\prime}
does not select any items, i.e.,
\frac{f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})}{f_{avg}(\pi)}=1
if
\forall\phi\in U^{+}
,
E(\pi^{\prime},\phi)=\emptyset
. This implies that if
f
is adaptive monotone, then its adaptive monotonicity ratio is one, i.e.,
\min_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}\frac{f_{avg}(\pi@\pi^{\prime})}{f_{avg}(\pi)}=1
.

3
Cardinality Constraint

We first study a special case of our problem by assuming that each item has a unit cost, i.e.,
\forall e\in E,c(e)=1
. Assuming

\max_{\pi:\forall\phi\in U^{+},|E(\pi,\phi)|\leq k}f_{avg}(\pi).

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the partial monotone adaptive submodular maximization problem. We introduce the notation of adaptive monotonicity ratio to measure the degree of adaptive monotonicity of a function. We reanalyze the performance bound of several existing policies using this new notation. Our results show that a near monotone function enjoys improved performance bounds as compared with non-monotone functions.
