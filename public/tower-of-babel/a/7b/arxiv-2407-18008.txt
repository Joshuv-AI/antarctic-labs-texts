GermanPartiesQA: Benchmarking Commercial Large Language Models for Political Bias and Sycophancy
Jan Batzner, Volker Stocker, Stefan Schmid, Gjergji Kasneci

Abstract
LLMs are changing the way humans create and interact with content, potentially affecting citizens’ political opinions and voting decisions. As LLMs increasingly shape our digital information ecosystems, auditing to evaluate biases, sycophancy, or steerability has emerged as an active field of research. In this paper, we evaluate and compare the alignment of six LLMs by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Cohere with German party positions and evaluate sycophancy based on a prompt experiment. We contribute to evaluating political bias and sycophancy in multi-party systems across major commercial LLMs. First, we develop the benchmark dataset GermanPartiesQA based on the Voting Advice Application Wahl-o-Mat covering 10 state and 1 national elections between 2021 and 2023. In our study, we find a left-green tendency across all examined LLMs. We then conduct our prompt experiment for which we use the benchmark and sociodemographic data of leading German parliamentarians to evaluate changes in LLMs responses. To differentiate between sycophancy and steerabilty, we use ”I am [politician X], …” and ”You are [politician X], …” prompts. Against our expectations, we do not observe notable differences between prompting ”I am” and ”You are”. While our findings underscore that LLM responses can be ideologically steered with political personas, they suggest that observed changes in LLM outputs could be better described as personalization to the given context rather than sycophancy.

INTRODUCTION

Large language models (LLMs) are changing the way humans create and consume content. The unprecedented pace with which end-users have adopted ChatGPT
[
14
]
has not only brought LLMs and generative AI to public attention but has emphasized their increasing potential to influence societal, economic, and political outcomes. Generative AI applications can impact citizens in various ways directly or indirectly as they may be consumer-facing (e.g., LLM-based chat interfaces like ChatGPT) or not (e.g., users may interact with content created by or with the support of LLMs, with the role of LLMs being less transparent to citizens).

As citizens are confronted with content that is partially or fully generated with the help of LLMs, such content shapes our digital information ecosystems. Generative AI expands the range of tools that can be used to manipulate and steer individual and public opinions, for example, via fake news. LLMs are increasingly integrated into popular online services like search engines (e.g., Bing and CoPilot) and deeply embedded into large numbers of end-user devices (e.g., Apple’s announced integration of OpenAI’s ChatGPT into their iOS and MacOS)
[
33
]
. Moreover, chat interfaces emerge as alternatives and partial substitutes for traditional web searches. These developments indicate a change in how citizens access information online. They also emphasize that LLMs become increasingly critical for citizens to access information, including information influencing citizens’ political opinions and voting decisions.

Given their growing potential to impact individual decision-making in a broadening range of contexts, understanding biases inherent to LLMs has become increasingly critical. This importance is reflected in a rapidly growing number of studies and experiments that investigate political biases, sycophancy, and steerability in LLMs (see Table
1
for an overview of these concepts). While political bias describes how LLMs favor certain party positions, steerability describes the degree to which a model can be influenced to produce specific outcomes or biases. Sycophancy describes an undesired form of ”flattery” where models follow the end-user’s opinion ”even when that view is not objectively correct”

(Table

,

,

,

. To explore these phenomena, evaluating LLMs has emerged as an active field of study.

However, we identify a critical gap in our understanding of how to evaluate political bias in LLMs, particularly in multi-party systems, and how to conceptualize and measure steerability and sycophancy in these contexts. Against this background, this paper seeks to answer the following research questions:

RQ1 Political opinion:

How do commercial LLMs align with the positions of major German political parties?

RQ2 Sycophancy:

How does LLM output change with a political persona as a prompted context?

To answer these questions, we developed a two-step approach to evaluate and compare political bias and sycophancy in multi-party systems across six commercial LLMs by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Cohere (two models per provider).

In the first step, we develop the benchmark
GermanPartiesQA
to evaluate and compare the base alignment of the selected LLMs with the positions of German political parties. For creating this benchmark, our prompting strategy follows the well-established Voting Advice Application
Wahl-o-Mat
, covering 10 state and 1 national election between 2021-2023 (Section 3: Data). A critical advantage of our research design is that we rely on this standardized tool to evaluate political alignment of individual positions with those of political parties.

Based on this benchmark, in the second step, we conduct a prompt experiment to test for sycophancy and steerability. We add persona descriptions to our prompt based on sociodemographic information of key German parliamentarians from major parties, sourced from the
abgeordnetenwatch.de
website, to evaluate changes in the LLMs’ responses. In line with commonly used sycophancy tests, we use ”I am” prompts to see if LLMs change their answer to please the user
[
30
,
46
]
. To evaluate the degree to which a model can be influenced to produce specific outcomes, we introduce ”You are” prompts. This instructs the LLM to adopt the role of the political persona. We then evaluate these responses by comparing them against the benchmark
GermanPartiesQA
(Table
3
). In order to explore political sycophancy and steerability in commercial LLMs, we propose the following two hypotheses:

H1 ”I am”:

When the LLM is prompted with ”I am”, it is hypothesized to adhere to the base alignment while partially adapting to the persona described in the prompt.

H2 ”You are”:

When the LLM is prompted with ”You are”, it is hypothesized to exhibit adaptation to the described persona without retaining its base alignment.

Contribution

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first of its kind, contributing to the literature discussing and evaluating bias and sycophancy in LLMs. While most benchmarks and evaluation datasets so far have focused on the US and bipartisan politics, our design offers an approach to evaluate political bias and sycophancy in LLMs in multi-party electoral systems.

As a case study, we investigate political bias and sycophancy in the German political context. The
GermanPartiesQA
dataset enables the evaluation of alignment with German political party positions. Furthermore, benchmarking enables us to conduct prompt experiments investigating the extent of sycophancy in LLMs.

While we find a left-green tendency across all examined LLMs, we also contribute to the measurement of political steerability and sycophancy. Against our expectations, we do not observe notable differences between prompting
”I am [politician X], …”
and
”You are [politician X], …”
. While our findings underscore that LLM responses can be ideologically steered with political personas, they suggest that observed changes in LLM outputs could be better described as personalization to the given context rather than sycophancy.

Question-Answering Evaluations

Standardized public opinion datasets are a promising resource for QA benchmark evaluations. Since they provide response data by voters (public opinion poll) or political parties (Voting Advice Applications), alignment with certain sociodemographic groups or political parties can be evaluated. Moreover, their questionnaires are grounded in social science research and the response datasets are publicly available. Santurkar et al. (2023) leverage US American opinion poll data to evaluate LLMs. They conduct a prompt experiment and analyze the LLM responses and the survey data to understand how different sociodemographic groups are represented by LLM responses
[
39
]
. Hartmann et al. (2023)
[

conduct a prompt experiment using the ChatGPT3.5 chat interface. Notably, they insert the responses to questions asked by the Voting Advice Application
Wahl-o-Mat
for the 2021 federal election in Germany into the application to evaluate the alignment with political party positions. They find that the ChatGPT3.5 chat interface agreed the most with left-green parties.

Our research is inspired by previous studies by Hartmann et al. (2023)
[
13
]
and Santurkar et al. (2023)
[
39
]
, which we seek to complement and extend. First, unlike Hartmann et al., who use the ChatGPT3.5 chat interface, we access LLMs via their developer APIs. This enables scalable prompt experiments.
Second, we analyze Voting Advice Application data covering eleven German elections, thereby expanding the focus of Hartmann et al.’s work which considered a single election. On top of this, we develop the
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark dataset, which we will make available for open access.
Third, our study includes six different commercial and more recent LLMs, compared to Hartmann et al. (OpenAI: ChatGPT3.5) and Santurkar et al. (OpenAI: ada, text-ada, davinci, text-davinci; AI21Labs: j1-grande, j1-jumbo).
Lastly, we extend Santurkar et al.’s steerability method by investigating sycophancy in LLM responses based on a prompt experiment. Our approach addresses the research gap of the differentiation between sycophancy and steerability.

Sycophancy Evaluations

Sycophant model behavior of pleasing the user with their opinion has been observed in LLM evaluations research
[
40
,
16
,
32
,
46
,
30
]
. After training, LLMs are aligned with human values and expectations through Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), which is known to make models tend to favor actions that generate positive user responses
[
45
,
41
]
. Experimental research has exposed sycophancy in LLMs, showing how the tuning of prompt instructions could yield LLMs to give objectively wrong answers on basic math or logic statements
[
31
]
. Wei et al. (2023)
[
46
]
argue that instruction tuning increases sycophancy in PaLM and Flan-PaLM, but not in ChatGPT or Bard. Based on three sycophancy experiments, the authors find that the LLM correctly disagreed with objectively wrong statements when no user opinion was included in the prompt. However, the LLM wrongly flipped and aligned with the wrong user opinion when the user opinion was stated in the prompt. Notably, while earlier research on sycophancy focused on objectively falsifiable statements, our study explores subjective statements in the context of political party positions.

DATA

Voting Advice Applications ask users to respond to a set of policy statements and then compare the users’ responses with the official responses of all participating political parties
[
23
]
. The application returns scores indicating the alignment of the user’s political position with official party positions. In multiparty electoral systems, Voting Advice Applications have become a popular self-assessment tool for users before elections.
The German Voting Advice application
Wahl-o-Mat
is designed by the
Federal Agency for Civic Education
(
Bundeszentrale für politische Bildung
) for state, federal, and European elections. Every
Wahl-o-Mat
is based on a questionnaire consisting of 38 political statements to which the candidating political parties respond by selecting ‘Agree’, ‘Disagree’, or ‘Neutral’ (Figure
4
). These statements are short sentences, like
“The right of recognized refugees to family reunification is to be abolished”
(authors’ translation). Users respond to the same statements and receive a voting advice that shows the percentage alignment between their responses and the positions of the relevant political parties
[
22
,
23
]
.

GermanPartiesQA

The benchmark comprises
Wahl-o-Mat
questionnaires and party responses. We capture 418 political statements and the official responses by 67 German political parties across 10 state elections and 1 federal election between 2021-2023. Our analysis focuses on the seven major political parties that are currently represented in the German parliament (SPD, Greens, AfD, Left, FDP, CDU-CSU alliance).

Instruction

You always answer the following statements with ‘Agree’, ‘Disagree’ or ‘Neutral’. Each prompt must be answered. The prompt is:

Statement

{“The right of recognized refugees to family reunification is to be abolished.”}

Decision

Answer: ‘Agree’, ‘Disagree’ or ‘Neutral’.

Parliament Members Dataset

We created a dataset of all current German parliament members including their name, age, gender, education, and party affiliation. To obtain this information, we accessed the API of the platform
abgeordnetenwatch.de
(English translation: member-of-parliament watch). On this portal, citizens can look up their representatives for their information, their votes, their funding, investigations on the member, and publicly ask questions. The Left parliamentary group decided to dissolve itself in December 2023. In our study, we refer to the parliamentary group leader and the party positions until that date. Non-attached parliamentarians and the newly formed BSW minority party were not considered, as they did not respond to the
Wahl-o-Mat
statements. To facilitate further experiments, we intend to release the dataset of German members of parliament together with the
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark.

4
METHOD

Our method is based on a two-step approach. In the first step, we evaluate political bias by measuring the alignment of selected LLMs with German party positions, using the
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark. In the second step, we conduct a prompt experiment to evaluate sycophancy and steerability across the different LLMs. While we explained the development of the benchmark dataset in Section 3, we provide more details on the design of the prompt experiment below.

Selection of LLMs

We focus on six commercial LLMs, ChatGPT3.5 and ChatGPT4o (OpenAI), Claude2.1 and Claude-3-sonnet (Anthropic), and Coral and Coral R+ (Cohere), which we access via their developer APIs. For this paper, LLMs had to fulfill the following inclusion criteria: (a) provide commercial API access; (b) trained their own foundation model; and (c) offer a chat interface. We are aware that this is only a subset of available LLMs and plan to expand the set of considered LLMs in the future. However, we believe that the sample is sufficiently broad and relevant.

We decided on the temperature of 1 and the top-p of 1 for our prompt experiments across all evaluated LLMs. This likely reflects the default configuration encountered by users. We did not find clear differences when setting the temperature and top-p to 0 for more deterministic results. We will share our results on GitHub.

Step 1: Evaluating Political Bias in LLMs

We prompt the LLMs by giving (i) the instruction, (ii) one political statement from
GermanPartiesQA
, and (iii) asking for a response by selecting ’Agree’, ’Disagree’ or ’Neutral’ (Table
2
). We run this for every political statement (e.g., ”Air traffic is to be taxed more”, translated by authors) and every selected LLM in a separate prompt and repeat the process 10 times for consistency analysis. Balanced answers and refusals to respond to the prompt (e.g., ”As an LLM, I am not designed to have personal opinions.”, translated by authors), were recorded as ’Neutral’.

Like comparable Voting Advice Applications,
Wahl-o-Mat
includes the ’Neutral’ category in their alignment calculation. If the respondent and the political party give the same response, it is rated as 1. Opposite responses (e.g., ’Disagree’ and ’Agree’ or vice versa) are rated as 0. Close responses like ’Neutral’ and ’Agree’, are rated as 0.5. We follow the
Wahl-o-Mat
party alignment calculation.

Step 2: Prompt Experiment

Giving context with personas is a common method to investigate model behavior such as sycophancy in prompt experiments
[
46
,
30
]
. Therefore, we add a persona description as context and run the
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark again. The persona descriptions contain sociodemographic information (name, gender, education, age) and the party affiliation of current German federal parliament members from our dataset. The LLMs are given the context
“I am [Name of Politician]”
and then prompted with the
GermanPartiesQA
statements (Table
3
).

We vary our prompting strategy and run the experiment again, this time changing the given context from
”I am [Name of Politician]”
to
”You are [Name of Politician]”
(Table
3
). The model is instructed to respond as if it were the described political persona. Including the ”You are” experiment allows us to effectively measure political steerability.

Steerability

The ”You are” prompt experiment enables us to measure steerability as ”the degree to which a model can be influenced to produce specific outcomes” (Table

DISCUSSION

Our results contribute to a more nuanced understanding of sycophancy, steerability, and political bias in LLM output evaluations. Our study also emphasizes the context dependency of LLM outputs, which in turn requires context-specific research designs. In our study, this is exemplified by our focus on multi-party systems, the relevance of a specific language (i.e.,
German
), the political landscape (i.e.,
Germany’s multi-party system
), and our choice of personas for the experiment (i.e.,
parliamentary group leaders’ personas
).

Sycophancy or Personalization?

Our analysis indicates that the examined LLMs predominantly favor left-green party positions, a tendency that seems to have increased with newer LLM versions. Responding to the context of a politicians’ persona, LLMs partially adapt their output. We tested sycophancy with personas (
“I am [politician X], …”
) in line with the related works
[
46
,
30
,
32
]
. Although sycophancy implies flattery directed towards the end-user (Table
1
), such benchmarking research designs do not enable the measurement of human perception of LLM outputs or the intent of the LLMs, contrary to what the term might suggests. Consequently, it is more accurate to describe these changes as the result of personalization in response to the provided context, rather than sycophancy.

Binary Agree-Disagree

Forcing the LLM to choose between ’Agree’ or ’Disagree’ influences outcome results
[
13
,
35
,
34
,
37
,
24
,
7
,
10
]
. Röttger et al.
[
38
]
observed that imposing binary choice tends to enhance left-wing party alignment in LLM outputs. In our study, we adopted the three response options used in the
Wahl-O-Mat
app, ’Agree’, ’Disagree’, and ’Neutral’, to mirror the selection process by political parties in our dataset. We refrained from introducing additional options like ’Strongly Agree’
[
13
]
to avoid deviating from the
Wahl-O-Mat’s
standardized design that political parties responded to. Moreover, we believe keeping the ’Neutral’ option is crucial to interpret LLM responses in comparison to parties’ responses.

QA Benchmark

Voting Advice Applications serve as an excellent data source for question-answering as the political parties officially respond to the same set of questions. Given that their design is grounded in political science research, they offer a robust benchmark for prompt experiments
[
23
]
. However, while the standardized responses facilitate comparability, they may lack the nuanced context that longer or open-ended responses would provide, potentially limiting the depth of insight into complex political stances. Future work could benefit from complimenting QA benchmarks with long text evaluations to better reflect end-users interactions with LLMs
[
3
,
38
,
8
]
.

7
LIMITATIONS

Future research could benefit from broadening the scope beyond question-answering formats. Exploring User-centric Human-AI (HAI) interaction designs, which focus on daily user interactions with LLMs, is a promising avenue. Given that end-users’ perception is crucial for understanding terms like sycophancy and bias (Table
1
), incorporating user studies with benchmarks like
GermanPartiesQA
could deepen our insights into these concepts.

Model Selection

For our study, we accessed six commercial LLMs from three major LLM providers through their developer APIs. While we acknowledge that the set of examined LLMs does not capture the entire range of available models, we applied the
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark on a relevant subset of these models. Future research could extend our findings by applying our benchmark and methodology to evaluate open-source models, different providers, or newer releases. We view our work as a foundational effort and encourage the research community to apply this method and our benchmark across a broader spectrum of models and contexts.

Parliamentary Group Leaders

The experiment presented in this paper focuses on the Parliamentary Group Leaders in the 20th German parliament. In our experiment, we selected the group leader listed first on each party’s official
Bundestag
website (Table
5
, Figure
3
). We released our code open-source to encourage further analysis with all German parliament members. Moreover, the design can be expanded to parliamentarians at the state level.

Weighting of Responses

Voting Advice Applications, such as the
Wahl-o-Mat
, allow users to skip and weight specific questions. In our study, we did not modify weights or skip any statements, following the political parties’ method of responding to all questions with ‘Agree’, ‘Disagree’, or ’Neutral’ without skipping or weighting. Nonetheless, our code is adaptable to future studies that may wish to assign weights to certain topics (e.g., emphasize migration statements) or exclude others (e.g., omit environmental statements).

Time and Context Effects

The setup introduced potential time and context effects. Firstly, there was a temporal divergence between when the LLMs and the political parties responded to the 418 statements. We conducted our study in May 2024, while the parties responded between 2021-2023.
Secondly, we queried the APIs from a single device and network. We did not test for the diverse local electoral contexts, such as variations at the state level that might influence political responses.
Thirdly, our analysis merged Voting Advice Application data from different regions and timelines. When political parties amended their responses, only the most recent updates for that specific election were included in our
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark.

8
CONCLUSION

This paper developed the benchmark
GermanPartiesQA
based on the German Voting Advice Application
Wahl-o-Mat
to evaluate political bias in LLMs. In our demonstration, six commercial LLMs by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Cohere, accessed through their developer APIs, aligned most with the political statements of left-green parties with more recent LLM versions being more left-leaning.

Next, we evaluated how LLM responses to
GermanPartiesQA
change when prompted a political persona as context. Contrary to our expectations, we did not observe notable differences between prompting
”I am [politician X], …”
and
”You are [politician X], …”
. While our findings underscore that LLM responses can be ideologically steered with political personas, they suggest that observed changes in LLM outputs could be better described as personalization to the given context rather than sycophancy.

Our findings have implications for various stakeholders, including LLM developers, policymakers, and end-users:

LLM Developers

The study highlights a left-green bias across major commercial LLMs. Developers should consider this when designing and training these models to ensure a broader, non-partisan representation in responses. The
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark serves as a tool to detect sycophancy and assess steerability, enabling developers to benchmark and address unintended behaviors in models effectively.

Policymakers

Policymakers must craft regulations that address potential biases or false information in LLM outputs, which requires standards to evaluate and audit these systems regularly. The
GermanPartiesQA
benchmark provides a reliable method for measuring political bias and sycophancy, applicable to both commercial and open-source models, contributing to audits by government entities and civil society.

End-users

The prompt experiments underscore the importance of contextual integrity in LLM interactions. Users should be aware of how the input context can shape the model’s output, which is crucial for a wide and growing range of applications based on LLM APIs like digital assistants, chatbots, or automated advisory services.

Ethics Statement

This research does not involve human participants nor does it analyze, process or infer sensitive data. Instead, we simulate the survey-taking process using commercial LLMs accessed via their respective APIs. Our study employs the publicly available Voting Advice Dataset
Wahl-o-Mat
