Caterpillars and alternating paths
Rain Jiang, Kai Jiang, Minghui Jiang

Abstract
Let p ⁡ ( m ) p(m) (respectively, q ⁡ ( m ) q(m) ) be the maximum number k k such that any tree with m m edges can be transformed by contracting edges (respectively, by removing vertices) into a caterpillar with k k edges. We derive closed-form expressions for p ⁡ ( m ) p(m) and q ⁡ ( m ) q(m) for all m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 . The two functions p ⁡ ( n ) p(n) and q ⁡ ( n ) q(n) can also be interpreted in terms of alternating paths among n n disjoint line segments in the plane, whose 2 ​ n 2n endpoints are in convex position.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a family of
n
disjoint segments in the plane,
whose
2n
endpoints are in convex position.
Then there exists a convex body
C(\mathcal{S})
with the
2n
endpoints on its boundary,
and with the
n
segments as chords,
which partition
C(\mathcal{S})
into
n+1
cells.
The intersection graph
with
n+1
vertices for the
n+1
cells,
and with two vertices connected by an edge if and only if
the corresponding cells have one of the
n

is a tree, which we denote by
T(\mathcal{S})
.

Conversely,
for any tree
T
with
n

it is easy to construct a family
\mathcal{S}
of
n
disjoint segments
as chords of a convex body
C

such that the intersection graph of the resulting
n+1
cells of
C

is
T
.

The following lemma shows that
alternating paths compatible with
\mathcal{S}

are equivalent to
caterpillars obtained from
T(\mathcal{S})
by removing vertices:

Lemma 1
.

\mathcal{S}
admits an alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}
going through
k
segments
if and only if

T(\mathcal{S})
contains
a caterpillar with
k
edges
as a subgraph.

Proof.

The lemma is trivially true when
k=1
. Now assume that
k\geq 2
.
We first prove the direct implication.
Suppose that there is an alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}

that goes through a sequence
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of
k
segments in
\mathcal{S}
.
Since every segment bordering two adjacent cells of
C(\mathcal{S})

corresponds to an edge between two vertices in
T(\mathcal{S})
,
and every two consecutive segments in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

are on the boundary of some common cell,
it follows that
every maximal subsequence of consecutive segments in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

around a common cell in
C(\mathcal{S})

corresponds to a star of edges incident to a common vertex in
T(\mathcal{S})
.
Moreover,
every three consecutive segments in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
not around a common cell
must span two adjacent cells
with the middle segment as their shared boundary,
which corresponds to an edge connecting two stars in
T(\mathcal{S})
.
Thus the
k
segments in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

correspond to a path of stars in
T(\mathcal{S})
,
which is a caterpillar with
k
edges.

We next prove the reverse implication.
Suppose that
T(\mathcal{S})
contains a caterpillar with
k
edges,
whose path of non-leaf vertices corresponds to
a sequence
\mathcal{C}
of cells of
C(\mathcal{S})
.
Then the edges of the caterpillar incident to each non-leaf vertex
correspond to a subfamily of segments in
\mathcal{S}

on the boundary of some common cell in
\mathcal{C}
,
and each edge in the non-leaf path corresponds
to a segment in
\mathcal{S}
bordering two adjacent cells in
\mathcal{C}
.
Since the cells of
C(\mathcal{S})
are all convex,
the disjoint segments on the boundary of each cell
can be linked into a non-crossing path within the cell,
starting and ending at any two endpoints of any two segments.
Then along the sequence
\mathcal{C}
of cells, these paths
can be concatenated into a single alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

The following lemma shows another equivalence between
alternating paths among
\mathcal{S}
and caterpillars obtained from

Lemma 2
.

\mathcal{S}
admits an alternating path among

Proof.

The deletion of a segment in

corresponds to
the merging of two adjacent cells of

,
and to the contraction of an edge in

.
For any subfamily
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of

segments in
\mathcal{S}
,
any alternating path among
\mathcal{S}
that goes through all
k
segments of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

is an alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
and conversely,
any alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is an alternating path among
\mathcal{S}
.
By Lemma
1
,

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
admits an alternating path compatible with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
going through all
k
segments
if and only if

T(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is a caterpillar with
k
edges.
∎

Contracting a tree into a caterpillar

In this section we prove Theorem
1
that
for
m\geq 1
,

p(m)=\lceil\sqrt{8m}-2\rceil
.
Recall that

p(m)

is the maximum number
k

such that any tree with
m
edges
can be transformed into a caterpillar with
k
edges
by contracting edges.
We first prove three lemmas.

For any tree
T
, denote by
\kappa(T)

the number of leaves in
T
,
plus the diameter of
T
, minus
2
.

Lemma 3
.

For any tree
T
,
the maximum size of a caterpillar
that can be obtained from
T
by contracting edges
is
\kappa(T)
.
Moreover, for any
1\leq k\leq\kappa(T)
,
a caterpillar of size
k
can be obtained from
T

by contracting edges.

Proof.

When
\kappa(T)=1
,
T
must be a tree with a single edge.
The lemma obviously holds in this case. Now assume that
\kappa(T)\geq 2
.
Let
T^{\prime}
be a caterpillar of the maximum size obtained from a tree
T
by contracting edges.
By the maximality of
T^{\prime}
, each leaf of
T^{\prime}
must be a leaf of
T
.
Moreover, the edges of the non-leaf path of
T^{\prime}
, if any, must come from
some non-leaf path in
T
,
whose length is at most the diameter of
T
minus
2
.
Thus the number of edges in
T^{\prime}
is at most the number of leaves in
T

plus the diameter of
T
minus
2
, which is
\kappa(T)
.
This number is actually attainable: simply keep all leaf edges
and all edges of a diameter path, and contract the rest.
From such a caterpillar with
\kappa(T)
edges,
a caterpillar with
k<\kappa(T)
edges
can then be obtained by contracting any
\kappa(T)-k
edges.
∎

For
k\geq 1
,
denote by
e(k)
the maximum size of a tree
T
with

\kappa(T)=k
.
For
d\geq 2
and

,
denote by

the maximum size of a tree
with diameter

A

with at most one vertex of degree greater than two.
In the next two lemmas, we determine

Lemma 4
.

For
d\geq 2
and

,

e(d,l)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot d\cdot l
when

is even,

e(d,l)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(d-1)\cdot l+1
when
d
is odd,
and there exists a spider
R_{d,l}
with diameter
d
,
l

e(d,l)
edges.

Proof.

Consider two cases for any tree with diameter
d
,
l

m

1.

d
is even.
Then there is an internal node
v
in the tree whose distance to any other

d/2
.
The number
m
of edges in the tree is maximized when
the paths from
v
to the
l

d/2

and are edge-disjoint,
that is, when the tree is a spider

centered at

with

.
Thus

.

2.

is odd.
Then there is an edge
\{u,v\}

between two internal nodes

and
v
in the tree
such that the distance from this edge to any other node
is at most
(d-1)/2
.
The number
m
of edges in the tree is maximized when
the shortest paths from
\{u,v\}
to the
l

all have the same length
(d-1)/2
and are edge-disjoint.
Thus

.
There are multiple extremal cases when

In particular, if one of the

,
and the other

with one leg of length

and

(d-1)/2
.
∎
