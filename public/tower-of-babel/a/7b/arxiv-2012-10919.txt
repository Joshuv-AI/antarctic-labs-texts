Abstract
We consider the problem of matching a metric space ( X , d X ) (X,d_{X}) of size k k with a subspace of a metric space ( Y , d Y ) (Y,d_{Y}) of size n ⩾ k n\geqslant k , assuming that these two spaces have constant doubling dimension δ \delta . More precisely, given an input parameter ρ ⩾ 1 \rho\geqslant 1 , the ρ \rho -distortion problem is to find a one-to-one mapping from X X to Y Y that distorts distances by a factor at most ρ \rho . We first show by a reduction from k k -clique that, in doubling dimension log 2 ⁡ 3 \log_{2}3 , this problem is NP-hard and W[1]-hard. Then we provide a near-linear time approximation algorithm for fixed k k : Given an approximation ratio 0 < ε ⩽ 1 0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1 , and a positive instance of the ρ \rho -distortion problem, our algorithm returns a solution to the ( 1 + ε ) ​ ρ (1+\varepsilon)\rho -distortion problem in time ( ρ / ε ) O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (\rho/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}n\log n . We also show how to extend these results to the minimum distortion problem in doubling spaces: We prove the same hardness results, and for fixed k k , we give a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm running in time ( dist ⁡ ( X , Y ) / ε ) O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ n 2 ​ log ⁡ n (\dist(X,Y)/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}n^{2}\log n , where dist ⁡ ( X , Y ) \dist(X,Y) denotes the minimum distortion between X X and Y Y .

Introduction

A metric space has
doubling dimension

\delta

if any ball can be covered by at most
2^{\delta}

balls of half its radius.
When
\delta=O(1)
, we say that this space is
doubling
.
(See Section
2
.)
For instance, the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has doubling dimension
O(d)
,
hence doubling spaces are generalizations of fixed-dimensional Euclidean spaces.

In this paper, we study pattern matching problems in doubling spaces. Given two
doubling spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
of doubling dimension
\delta
,
and sizes
|X|=k
and
|Y|=n
, where
k\leqslant n
, our goal is to find a subspace
of
Y
that resembles the
pattern

X
. More precisely, we consider
the
\rho
-
distortion
problem
and the
minimum distortion problem
, which we describe below.

Given
\rho\geqslant 1
, the
\rho
-distortion problem
is to find, if it exists, a mapping
\sigma:X\to Y
such that

(1/\rho)d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\leqslant d_{Y}(\sigma(x),\sigma(x^{\prime}))\leqslant\rho d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})

(1)

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. It follows from this definition that
\sigma
is injective.

The
\rho
-distortion problem is analogous to the problem of matching
two point-sets in Euclidean space under rigid transformations, which are compositions
of translations and rotations. If, in addition, we allow scaling, then
an analogous problem in general metric spaces is the minimum distortion problem.
The goal is to minimize the distortion

\dist(\sigma)=\expansion(\sigma)\times\expansion(\sigma^{-1})

over all injections
\sigma:X\to Y
, where

\mathrm{expansion}(\sigma)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in X\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{Y}(\sigma(x),\sigma(x^{\prime}))}{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{expansion}(\sigma^{-1})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in X\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}{d_{Y}(\sigma(x),\sigma(x^{\prime}))}.

The minimum of
\dist(\sigma)
over all injections
\sigma:X\to Y

is denoted
\dist(X,Y)
, and it is easy to see that
\dist(X,Y)\geqslant 1
.
The minimum distortion problem was introduced by Kenyon et al.
[
21
]

in the case where
k=n
, and thus
\sigma
is a bijection.

Motivated by applications to natural language processing, bioinformatics
and computer vision, Ding and Ye
[
13
]
recently proposed
a practical algorithm for a pattern
matching problem in doubling spaces.
However, this algorithm may only return an approximation of a local
minimum, and its
time bound is not given as a function of the input size.
One of our goals is thus to provide a provably efficient algorithm
for pattern matching in doubling spaces. Another motivation for our work is that,
even though the complexity of the minimum distortion problem has been studied
for several types of metrics, it appears that no result was previously
known for two doubling metrics. (See the comparison with previous
work below.)

Our results.

We first give a hardness result: We show that for any
\rho\geqslant 1
,
the
k
-clique problem reduces
to
\rho
-distortion in doubling dimension
\log_{2}3
. It implies that
the
\rho
-distortion problem is NP-hard, and is W[1]-hard when
parameterized by
k
(Corollary
10
).
It also shows that this problem cannot
be solved in time
f(k)\cdot n^{o(k)}
for any computable function
f
,
unless the exponential time hypothesis (ETH) is false
(Corollary
11
).

On the positive side, we present a near-linear time approximation algorithm
for small values of
k
. More precisely, if
\rho\geqslant 1

and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, our algorithm returns in

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}n\log n
time
a solution to the
(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-distortion problem whenever
a solution to the
\rho
-distortion problem exists
(Theorem
28
). In this time bound, it is reasonable to assume that

\rho
is a small constant, say
\rho\leqslant 10
, as a larger value
would mean that we allow
a relative error of more than 900% in the quality of the matching, which is
probably too much for most applications.

We also show how to extend these results to the minimum distortion problem.
In particular, we show that the minimum distortion problem cannot be solved
in time
f(k)\cdot n^{o(k)}
for any computable function
f
,
unless ETH is false, and we give a

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm running in

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\dist(X,Y)^{2k\delta}/\varepsilon^{2k\delta+O(1)})n^{2}\log n

time (Theorem
34
).
Here again, it is reasonable to assume that
\dist(X,Y)=O(1)
,
and then for any fixed
k
, this algorithm is an FPTAS with running time

(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}n^{2}\log n
.

Comparison with previous work.

One of the main differences between our results and previous work on
point pattern matching under rigid transformations, or on the minimum distortion problem,
is that we parameterize the problem by
k
, and hence

k
is regarded as a small number. The advantage is that the dependency
of our time bounds in
n
are low (near-linear or near-quadratic). However,
we obtain an exponential dependency in
k
, which may be unavoidable due to
our hardness results.

Geometric point pattern matching problems have been studied extensively.
(See for instance the survey by Alt and Guibas
[
1
]
.) In the
fixed-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, these problems are usually tractable,
as the space of transformations has a constant number of degrees of freedom.
For instance, when
k=n
,
we may want to decide whether
X
and
Y
are congruent, which means that
there is a rigid transformation
\mu
such that
\mu(X)=Y
. Alt et al.
[
2
]

showed how
to find such a transformation in time
O(n^{d-2}\log n)
, when it exists.
In practice, however, we cannot expect that point coordinates are known exactly,
so it is unlikely that an exact match exists. We may thus want to find the smallest

\varepsilon>0
such that each point of
X
is brought to distance at most
\varepsilon

from a point in
Y
. (In other words, we allow an additive error
\varepsilon
.)
Chew et al. gave an
O(k^{3}n^{2}\log^{2}(nk))
-time algorithm
to solve this problem in the plane under rigid transformations
[
8
]
.

As mentioned above, to the best of our knowledge, the only work published so far on pattern
matching in doubling spaces presents a practical algorithm for matching
two doubling spaces
[
13
]
. However it has not been proven to return a good
approximation of the optimal solution in the worst case. Other problems studied
in doubling spaces include
approximate near-neighbor searching
[
3
,
9
,
20
]
,
spanners
[
4
]
, routing
[
7
,
16
]
,
TSP
[
26
]
, clustering
[
15
]
,
Steiner forest
[
6
]
…

The minimum distortion problem has been studied under various metrics, when
k=n
.
Kenyon et al.
[
21
]
gave a polynomial-time algorithm for
line metrics (1-dimensional point sets) when
\dist(X,Y)<5+2\sqrt{6}
.
They also gave an algorithm that computes
\dist(X,Y)
when
d_{X}
is
the metric associated with an unweighted graph over
X
and
d_{Y}
is the
metric associated with a bounded degree tree over
Y
,
with a running time exponential in the maximum degree and doubly
exponential in
\dist(X,Y)
.
For general metrics, the minimum distortion
is hard to approximate within a factor less than
\log^{1/4-\gamma}n
, for
any
\gamma>0

[
23
]
.
Hall and Papadimitriou
[
18
]
showed that even for line metrics,
the distortion is hard to approximate when it is large.

When
k\leqslant n
, Fellows et al.
[
14
]
showed that the problem of deciding whether

\dist(X,Y)\leqslant D
is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized
by
\Delta
and
D
, where
d_{X}
is the metric associated with
an unweighted graph over
X
, and
d_{Y}
is the metric associated with
a tree of degree at most
\Delta
over
Y
. For two unweighted graph metrics,
Cygan et al.
[
11
]
showed that the problem cannot be solved in
time
2^{o(n\log n)}
unless ETH is false. When
X
is an arbitrary finite
metric space and
Y
is a subset of the real line, Nayyeri and Raichel
[
25
]

showed that a constant-factor approximation of
\dist(X,Y)
can be computed
in time
\Phi(X)^{O((\dist(x,y))^{2})}(kn)^{O(1)}
, where
\Phi(X)
is the
spread of
X
. (See Section
2
.)

In summary, the previously known theoretical results on the minimum distortion
problem are either
hardness results, or algorithms for cases where
d_{Y}
is a subset of a line metric
or a tree metric. The algorithms presented in this paper, on the other hand,
apply when
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
are doubling metrics, which are generalizations
of fixed-dimensional Euclidean metrics.

Our approach.

In Section
3
, we present hardness results on the

\rho
-distortion problem. We reduce an instance
G(V,E)
of
k
-clique
to an instance of
\rho
-distortion consisting of
two metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
of sizes
k
and
km
, respectively,
where
m=|V|
.
The pattern
(X,d_{X})
is an ultrametric, with exponentially increasing
distances.
The space
(Y,d_{Y})
consists of
k
rings, each ring consisting
of
m
points regularly spaced on a circle of perimeter 1.
Each of these
m
points is associated with a vertex of the
k
-clique instance.
(See Figure
1
a.) The distances between the
rings increase exponentially (Figure
2
),
and the input graph is encoded
by having slightly longer edges for pairs of vertices lying in
different rings that correspond to edges in the input graph
(Figure
1
b.) We prove that these two
spaces have doubling dimension
\log_{2}3
, and that this instance
of
\rho
-distortion is equivalent to the
k
-clique instance
we started from.

In Section
4
, we give a self-contained description of
a first approximation algorithm for the
\rho
-distortion that runs in time

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{3k\delta}n+O(kn\log\Phi(Y))

where
\Phi(Y)
is the
spread
of
Y
. (See Section
2
.)
We first construct a
navigating net
over
Y

[
24
]
.
A navigating net is essentially a
coordinate-free quadtree that records a metric space.
It represents
Y
at all resolutions
r
where
r
is a powers of 2.
At each scale
r
, our navigating net records an
r
-net

Y_{r}
of
Y
,
which is a subset of
Y
whose points are a distance
at least
r
apart, and such that the radius-
r
balls centered
at
Y_{r}
cover
Y
. (See Figure
3
.)

Let
r_{X}
be the smallest scale that is at least
\rho
times the diameter of
X
.
Our algorithm constructs, for each point
y\in Y_{r_{X}}
,
a sparse set of matchings
whose images are in the radius-
3r_{X}
ball centered at
y
.
(By sparse, we mean that any two such matchings send at least one
point of
X
to two points of
Y
that are a distance at least

\varepsilon r_{X}/(2\rho^{2})
apart.)
The union of these sets of matchings
over all
y\in Y_{r_{X}}
is denoted
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
,
and we show that any solution to the
\rho
-distortion problem
is close to at least one matching in
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
.

We compute
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
recursively from
L(P,\beta,r_{P})

and
L(Q,\beta,r_{Q})
for
\beta=\varepsilon/(8k-8)
, where
P

and
Q
form a partition of
X
. More precisely,
we obtain
P
and
Q
by running Kruskal’s algorithm on
X
,
and stopping at the second last step. It
ensure that
P
and
Q
are well separated, and it follows that
any
\rho
-matching

\bar{\sigma}:X\to Y
can be approximated by a combination of two

(1+\beta)\rho
-matchings
\bar{\sigma}^{P}_{\beta}:P\to Y
and

\bar{\sigma}^{Q}_{\beta}:Q\to Y
recorded in
L(P,\beta,r_{P})

and
L(Q,\beta,r_{Q})
, respectively.
(See Figure
4
.)
After computing
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
, we simply return
one of the matchings that it records, if any.

In Section
5
, we show how to improve the time bound to

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}n\log n
.
We achieve it using the approximate near-neighbor (ANN) data structure
by Cole and Gottlieb
[
9
]
. First, this data structure allows
us to efficiently prune the sets of matchings recorded at layer
r_{X}
,
by only inserting a new matching if it is far enough from all previously
inserted matching. It can be checked by performing a constant number of
ANN queries in the set of matchings. As the space of matchings is doubling
(Corollary
3
), it takes logarithmic time.
This saves a factor
k(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{k\delta}/\log n
in our time
bound.
Second, instead of computing the whole navigating net, which takes
time
O(n\log\Phi(Y))
, we show how to compute
any layer in
O(n\log n)
time using ANN queries. As our algorithm
only requires
k
layers of the navigating net, it removes the
dependency on
\Phi(Y)
from the time bound.

Finally, in Section
6
,
we show how to extend our results on the
\rho
-distortion
problem to the minimum distortion problem.
For the hardness result,
it suffices to add an extra point to
X
and
Y
that is far enough
from the other points, in order to make the reduction work. For the
algorithms, we use the reduction by Kenyon et al.
[
21
]

of the minimum distortion problem to the
\rho
-distortion problem,
which we speed-up using exponential search, and using a well-separated
pairs decomposition, which allows us to reduce the number of candidate values
for
\dist(X,Y)
.

is non-negative, symmetric, and
that
d_{Y}(p,p^{\prime})=0
if and only if
p=p^{\prime}
.
Within one ring,
d_{Y}
respects the triangle inequality because it
is the usual distance along a circle. So we only need to prove that
the triangle inequality holds for points lying in two or three different rings.
Let
i,i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
j,j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
If
i
,
i^{\prime}
and
i^{\prime\prime}
are distinct, we have

d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})+d_{Y}(p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},p_{i^{\prime\prime}j^{\prime\prime}})\geqslant{2}^{\max(i,i^{\prime})}+{2}^{\max(i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime})}-\frac{2}{m}\geqslant{2}^{\max(i,i^{\prime\prime})}\geqslant d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{i^{\prime\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}).

If
i\neq i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime\prime}
, then we have

d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})+d_{Y}(p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}})\geqslant\left({2}^{\max(i,i^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{m}\right)+\frac{1}{m}\geqslant{2}^{\max(i,i^{\prime})}\geqslant d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}).

If
i\neq i^{\prime}
, then we have

d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})+d_{Y}(p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},p_{ij^{\prime\prime}})\geqslant 2\left({2}^{\max(i,i^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{m}\right)\geqslant{2}^{i}\geqslant d_{Y}(p_{ij},p_{ij^{\prime\prime}}).

This completes the proof that
d_{Y}
is a metric.
∎

Lemma 5
.

The metric space
(Y,d_{Y})
has doubling dimension
\log_{2}3
.

Proof.

Let
b_{0}=\ball(p,r)
be an arbitrary ball, where
p=p_{ij}
.
We assume that the index
j^{\prime}
in

is defined modulo

,
hence
p_{ij^{\prime}}=p_{i(j^{\prime}+m)}
. Let

.

Suppose that

. Then
\log_{2}(r+1/m)<i

.
As the distance from any point in

.

•

If
\lceil mr/2\rceil\geqslant m/4
, then
1+mr/2\geqslant m/4
.
As
m\geqslant 24
, it implies that
r\geqslant 5/12
.
Let
c=p_{i1}
,
c^{\prime}=p_{i(1+\lfloor m/3\rfloor)}
and

c^{\prime\prime}=p_{i(1-\lfloor m/3\rfloor)}

As

d_{Y}(c,c^{\prime})=d_{Y}(c,c^{\prime\prime})\leqslant 1/3

So we can cover the whole ring

(and thus

centered at

c^{\prime}
and

•

Otherwise, the points

As

Now suppose that
t\geqslant i
and
t\geqslant 3

{2}^{t}-1/m\leqslant r<{2}^{t+1}-1/m
.
For any

.
We consider three balls

b_{t-1}
and

of radius

centered at an arbitrary point

Since

\frac{r}{2}>\frac{{2}^{t}}{2}-\frac{1}{2m}\geqslant\frac{2}{2}-\frac{1}{48}=\frac{47}{48},

and

.
Similarly, we have
R_{t-1}\subseteq b_{t-1}
.
Let

.
Then we have

As

Therefore,

.

Similarly, if

.
If

In any case,

is contained in the union of at most 3 balls of half its radius, and thus

∎

A simpler version of the arguments in the proofs of Lemma

Lemma 6

3.2
Proof of Correctness

We now prove that our reduction of
k
-clique to the
\rho
-distortion problem
is correct.
So given an instance

of the

-clique problem,
we construct the metric spaces

and

as described

-distortion problem.
We need to show that these two instances of

-clique and

-distortion are

We first assume that

is a positive instance of
k
-clique.
So there is a clique

of size

in

.
Let

be the matching defined by
\sigma(x_{i})=p_{ij_{i}}

for all

. For any

It means that

,
and thus

\sigma
is a solution to our instance of the

-distortion problem.

We now prove the converse. Let
\sigma:X\to Y
be a solution to
our instance of the
\rho
-distortion problem. We denote
y_{i}=\sigma(x_{i})
for
each
x_{i}\in X
. Each

corresponds to a vertex
w_{i}\in V
. More precisely,
we have
y_{i}=p_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
for some indices
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
, and we set
w_{i}=v_{j^{\prime}}
.
We want to show that
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
is a

-clique in

.

Lemma 7
.

Each ring contains exactly one point
y_{i}
, and
y_{i}\in R_{i}
whenever

.

Proof.

Let
1\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}\leqslant k
. Then
d_{X}(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})={2}^{i_{2}}\rho
. As
\sigma

is a solution to the

-distortion problem, it implies that

d_{Y}(y_{i_{1}},y_{i_{2}})=d_{Y}(\sigma(x_{i_{1}}),\sigma(x_{i_{2}}))\geqslant{2}^{i_{2}}\geqslant 4.

As the diameter of each ring is at most

, it follows that
y_{i_{1}}
and

y_{i_{2}}

lie in different rings. Hence each ring contains exactly one point
y_{i}
.

We now prove the second part of the lemma, so we may assume that

.
The point

is at distance

from each

x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}
. As

is a solution to the

-distortion problem,
the distance from

to any point

is at least

, and since it is the largest interpoint distance in

.

.

Applying the same argument repeatedly to

shows that

for any

. (As

,
the points

and

are indistinguishable, hence we have either

and

y_{2}\in R_{2}
, or
y_{1}\in R_{2}
and
y_{2}\in R_{1}
.)
∎

We can now prove that:

Lemma 8
.

The vertices

form a

.

Proof.

Let

. By Equation (

the distance

can take only two values: it

4
Approximation algorithm for the
\rho
-distortion problem

In this section, we present an approximation algorithm
for the
\rho
-distortion problem.
We assume that the doubling dimension is constant, that is,
\delta=O(1)
.
As we saw in Section
3
, the
\rho
-distortion problem
is hard, so we relax the problem slightly: Given a
parameter
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, the
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-
distortion
problem
is to find a
(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching
whenever a
\rho
-matching exists.
If there is no
\rho
-matching,
then our algorithm either returns a
(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching,
or it does not return any result.

4.1
Navigating nets

Our algorithm records
Y
in a
navigating net
, which is a data
structure representing
Y
at different resolutions.
(See Figure
3
.)
This structure was
introduced by Krauthgamer and Lee
[
24
]
. We will use a slightly
modified version of it. Our version of the navigating net
has the advantages that each layer is an
r
-net of
Y
(see definition
below),
and that it can easily be computed in
O(n\log\Phi(Y))
time. On the
other hand, it does not allow efficient deletions.
Several other variations exists.
In particular, Cole and Gottlieb
[
9
]
and Har-Peled and Mendel
[
20
]

presented more involved data structures whose time bounds do not
depend on the spread.

Figure 3
:
A navigating net at three consecutive scales
r/2
,
r
and
2r
.
The white dots represent points of the
r
-nets
Y_{r/2}
,
Y_{r}
and

Y_{2r}
. The ellipses represent the balls of radius
r/2
,
r
and
2r

centered at the points in
Y_{r/2}
,
Y_{r}
and
Y_{2r}
, respectively.
The blue edges are the horizontal edges incident to one of the vertices
of
Y_{r/2}
. The red edges are the vertical edges.

For any
r\geqslant 0
, an
r
-net
of
Y
is a subset

Y_{r}\subseteq Y
such that
\dmin(Y_{r})\geqslant r
and

Y\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in Y_{r}}\ball(y,r)
. An
r
-net can be
constructed incrementally by repeatedly adding new points that
lie outside of the current union of balls, until
Y
is
completely covered. Intuitively, an
r
-net represents

Y
at resolution
r
.

A
scale
is a rational number
r=2^{i}
such that
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Our navigating net records a sequence of
r
-nets

Y_{r_{\min}},Y_{2r_{\min}},\dots,Y_{r_{\max}}

such that
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
are scales satisfying the inequalities

\dmin(Y)/2<r_{\min}\leqslant\dmin(Y)
and
\diam(Y)/2\leqslant r_{\max}<2\diam(Y)
.
At the largest scale
r_{\max}
, the
r_{\max}
-net
Y_{r_{\max}}
consists
of a single point
y_{\mathrm{root}}
, and thus
Y=\ball(y_{\mathrm{root}},r_{\max})
.
At the lowest scale, we set
Y_{r_{\min}}=Y
.
So the navigating net represents
Y
at all scales
r
such
that
r_{\min}\leqslant r\leqslant r_{\max}
by an
r
-net
Y_{r}
.
All scales
r>r_{\max}
are represented by

Y_{r}=Y_{r_{\max}}=\{y_{\mathrm{root}}\}
, but we do not construct
these copies of
Y_{r_{\max}}
explicitly.

We may assume that
\log(\Phi(Y))\geqslant 1
as otherwise,

|Y|=O(1)
by Lemma
1
, and the
\rho
-distortion
problem can be solved in
O(1)
time by brute force.
So we only construct
Y_{r}
at
O(\log(\Phi(Y))
different scales
r

such that
\dmin(Y)/2<r_{\min}\leqslant r\leqslant r_{\max}<2\diam(Y)
.

We construct a graph over these
r
-nets. First, at each scale
r
such
that
r_{\min}\leqslant r\leqslant r_{\max}
,
we connect any two nodes
y
,
y^{\prime}\in Y_{r}
such that

d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\leqslant 6r
by an edge, that we call a
horizontal
edge.
At each scale
r
such that
2r_{\min}\leqslant r\leqslant r_{\max}
,
we also connect with a
vertical
edge each
y\in Y_{r/2}
to a
node
p(y)\in Y_{r}
,
called the
parent
of
y
, such that
d_{Y}(y,p(y))\leqslant r
.
At least one such node
p(y)
exists since
Y_{r}
is an
r
-net.
We call
y
a
child
of
p(y)
. More generally,
we say that
y
is a
descendant
of
y^{\prime}
if
y

is a child of
y^{\prime}
, or
y
is a child of a descendant of
y^{\prime}
.
Conversely, we say that
y^{\prime}
is an

of
y
if

y
is a descendant of
y^{\prime}
.
When

, the node

is an

These

The lemma below shows that it has bounded degree.

Lemma 12
.

Each node of the navigating net has degree
O(1)
.

Proof.

The children of
y\in Y_{r}
are at distance at most

r
from
y
, and thus at distance at most
2r
from each
other. As they are in
Y_{r/2}
, they
are at distance at least
r/2
from each other.
So the spread of the set of children of
y
is at most 4, thus

y
has at most
16^{\delta}
children by Lemma
1
.
It follows that any node is incident to at most

1+16^{\delta}=O(1)
vertical edges.
The nodes adjacent to
y
via horizontal edges are at
distance at most
6r
from
y
and at least
r
from
each other. Hence their spread is at most
12
, so
there are at most
48^{\delta}
of them.
∎

The lemma below shows that our navigating net can be computed
efficiently.

Lemma 13
.

The navigating net of
Y
can be computed in

O(n\log\Phi(Y))
time.

Proof.

We construct the navigating net incrementally,
adding the nodes one-by-one in a top-down manner. We first pick an arbitrary
y_{\mathrm{root}}\in Y
,
and we let
r_{\max}
be the smallest scale that
is at least
\max_{y\in Y}d_{Y}(y_{\mathrm{root}},y)
.
We initialize our navigating net with the single node

y_{\mathrm{root}}
at scale
r_{\max}
.
Initially, we set
r_{\min}=r_{\max}
, and we will decrease

r_{\min}
when we insert new points, if needed. We will
maintain the invariants that any two inserted points
are at distance at least
r_{\min}
from each other,
and that
Y_{r_{\min}}
is the set of all inserted points.

We repeatedly insert an arbitrary point
y\in Y
,
until all points have been inserted. We now show how to
update the data structure while inserting
y
.

We start from the root and move down to scale
r_{\min}
.
During this process, at the current scale
r
,
we maintain the set
N(y,r)
of points in
Y_{r}
that are at
distance at most
6r
from
y
. We have
\dmin(N(y,r))\geqslant r

and
\diam(N(y,r))\leqslant 12r
, hence
|N(y,r)|\leqslant 48^{\delta}=O(1)

by Lemma
1
.

We now show how to maintain
N(y,r)
while traversing the
data structure from top to bottom. So we assume that we know

N(y,2r)
, and we show how to find
N(y,r)
. Suppose that

y^{\prime}\in N(y,r)
. Then we have
d_{Y}(y^{\prime},y)\leqslant 6r
and

d_{Y}(y^{\prime},p(y^{\prime}))\leqslant 2r
. Therefore,
d_{Y}(y,p(y^{\prime}))\leqslant 8r
,
which implies that
p(y^{\prime})\in N(y,2r)
. So in order to find
the points in
N(y,r)
, we can simply go through all the
children of the nodes in
N(y,2r)
, and check for each of
them if it is at distance at most
6r
from
y
. It takes

O(1)
time as the navigating net has constant degree.
So we can compute all the sets
N(y,r)
such that

r_{\min}\leqslant r\leqslant r_{\max}
in
O(\log\Phi(Y))
time
as there are
O(\log\Phi(Y))
different scales.

If
N(y,r_{\min})
is empty, then the closest inserted point
y_{c}

to
y
is at distance more than
r_{\min}
, so we don’t
need to update
r_{\min}
. Otherwise, since
y_{c}\in N(y,r_{\,min})
,
we can find
y_{c}
in
O(1)
time by brute force.
If
d_{Y}(y,y_{c})<r_{\min}
,
we set
r_{\mathrm{temp}}
to be the largest scale that is
at most
d_{Y}(y,y_{c})
, and we create new levels in the navigating
net at all scales
r
such that
r_{\mathrm{temp}}\leqslant r<r_{\min}
.
As
Y_{r_{\min}}
consists of all the inserted points, the
same is true for
Y_{r}
at the new levels, and we can
compute the horizontal edges in
O(1)
time per node as
they are a subset of the horizontal edges at scale
2r
.
We also connect each node by a vertical edge to its copy at
scale
2r
. After updating all levels, we set
r_{\min}=r_{\mathrm{temp}}
.

We now show how to insert
y
in the navigating net. When
inserting
y
at level
r
, we check whether all the points
in
N(y,r)
are at distance at distance at least
r

from
y
. If it is the case, we insert
y
into
Y_{r}
, and
we connect
y
to all the points in
N(y,r)
by a horizontal
edge. Then let
y^{\prime\prime}
be the point of

N(y,2r)
that is closest to
y
. As
Y_{2r}
is a
2r
-net,
we have
d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime\prime})\leqslant 2r
, so we set
y^{\prime\prime}=p(y)
.

This algorithm constructs the navigating net incrementally
in
O(\log\Phi(Y))
time per point in

, so the overall
running time is
O(n\log\Phi(Y))
. Our construction ensures
that
\dmin(Y)/2<r_{\min}\leqslant\dmin(Y)
and
\diam(Y)/2\leqslant r_{\max}<2\diam(Y)
.

∎

We associate the ball
\ball(y,2r)

.
These balls have the following properties.

Lemma 14
.

At any scale

Let

The lemma below shows that any ball other than the root is contained

Lemma 15

r<r^{\prime}
. For any
y\in Y_{r}
,
and for any ancestor
y^{\prime}\in Y_{r^{\prime}}
of
y
,
we have
b(y,2r)\subseteq\ball(y^{\prime},2r^{\prime})
.

Proof.

When
r^{\prime}=2r
, we have
y^{\prime}=p(y)
, so
d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\leqslant r^{\prime}

by definition of
p(y)
. It follows that

\ball(y,2r)\subseteq\ball(y^{\prime},2r+r^{\prime})=\ball(y^{\prime},2r^{\prime})
.
When
r^{\prime}\geqslant 4r
, the result is obtained by induction.
∎

In summary, the balls
\ball(y,2r)
, connected
by the vertical edges, form a tree such that
each ball is contained in each of its ancestors. The horizontal edges will
help us traverse this tree within a given level.

4.2
Splitting the pattern

Our algorithm proceeds recursively, by partitioning
X
into
two well-separated subsets
P
and
Q
at each stage.
More precisely, we will split
X
as follows. (Remember that
k=|X|
.)

Lemma 16
.

If
k\geqslant 2
, we can partition
X
into two non-empty subsets

P
and
Q
such that
\diam(X)\leqslant(k-1)\cdot d_{X}(P,Q)
.

Proof.

We obtain
P
and
Q
by running Kruskal’s algorithm
[
10
]

for computing a minimum spanning tree of
X
, and stopping at the second-last step.
So starting from the forest
(X,\emptyset)
, we repeatedly
insert the shortest edge that connects any two trees of the current forest,
until we are left with exactly two trees
P
and
Q
. At the last step,
P
and
Q
are then connected with an edge of length

\ell=d_{X}(P,Q)
,
which is not shorter than any edge in the spanning trees we
constructed for
P
and
Q
.
Thus,
\diam(P)\leqslant(|P|-1)\ell
and
\diam(Q)\leqslant(|Q|-1)\ell
.
It follows that

\diam(X)\leqslant(|P|-1)\ell+\ell+(|Q|-1)\ell=(k-1)\ell
.
∎

4.3
Recording approximate matchings

Our algorithm for the
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem
records a collection of approximate matchings for some
layers of the navigating net. More precisely, for some
non-empty subset
W
of
X
, for some
0<\beta\leqslant 1
and
for some scales

,
we will construct a data structure

that
records at least one
(1+\beta)\rho
-matching from
W
to

if a
\rho
-matching from
W
to
Y

exists. In particular, we will compute
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
at an appropriate
scale

(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem if there is one.
We first give three invariants of

, and in the
next two sections we will show how to compute

recursively.

The data structure
L(W,\beta,r)
records
a set

of matchings at each node
y\in Y_{r}
.
These sets satisfy the following properties.

Property 17
.

Let

be a scale such that
r\geqslant\rho\diam(W)
.

(a)

For any
y\in Y_{r}
, each matching
\sigma_{\beta}\in M(y,W,\beta,r)

is a
(1+\beta)\rho
-matching from
W
to
Y
such that

\sigma_{\beta}(W)\subseteq\ball(y,3r)
.

(b)

For any
y\in Y_{r}
and
any two distinct
\sigma_{\beta}
,
\sigma_{\beta}^{\prime}\in M(y,W,\beta,r)
, we have

d_{M}(\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\beta}^{\prime})\geqslant\beta r/(2\rho^{2})
.

(c)

For any
\rho
-matching
\sigma:W\to Y
,
there exist
y\in Y_{r}
and
\sigma_{\beta}\in M(y,W,\beta,r)

such that
d_{M}(\sigma,\sigma_{\beta})\leqslant\beta r/\rho^{2}
.

These properties imply the following bound on the sizes of these sets.

Lemma 18
.

For any
y\in Y_{r}
, we have
|M(y,W,\beta,r)|=O((\rho^{2}/\beta)^{k\delta})
.

Proof.

Property
17
a implies that any two matchings
in
M(y,W,\beta,r)
are at distance at most
6r
from each other,
that is,
\diam(M(y,W,\beta,r))\leqslant 6r
.
Property
17

\dmin(M(y,W,\beta,r))\geqslant\beta r/(2\rho^{2})
.
Therefore,
\Phi(M(y,W,\beta,r))\leqslant 12\rho^{2}/\beta
. Then by
Lemma
1
and Corollary
3
,
we have
|M(y,W,\beta,r)|\leqslant(48\rho^{2}/\beta)^{k\delta}
.
∎

17
c holds.
So we obtain the following result.

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that
k\geqslant 2
and
\beta=\varepsilon/(8k-8)
. Then
we can compute
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
from

L(P,\beta,r_{X})
and
L(Q,\beta,r_{X})
in

O(nk(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{3k\delta})
time.

Proof.

The discussion above shows that our algorithm is
correct. We still need to analyze its running time.
Let
y\in Y_{r_{X}}
. As
d_{Y}(y,y^{P})\leqslant 6r_{X}
and
d_{Y}(y,y^{Q})\leqslant 6r_{X}
,
the nodes
y^{P}
and
y^{Q}
are connected to
y
by horizontal edges.
As the nodes of the navigating net have constant degree,
it implies that there are
O(1)
pairs
(y^{P},y^{Q})
to consider.
By Lemma
18
, there are

O((\rho^{2}/\beta)^{k\delta})
matchings in
M(y^{P},P,\beta,r_{X})
and

M(y^{Q},Q,\beta,r_{X})
.
Therefore, we consider
O((\rho^{2}/\beta)^{2k\delta})
matchings

\sigma_{\varepsilon}
when constructing
M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
.
Each of these matchings is then compared with
previously inserted matchings.
By Lemma
18
, we have

|M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})|=O((\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{k\delta})=O((\rho^{2}/\beta)^{k\delta})
.
We can compute the distance between two matchings in
O(k)
time,
so it takes

O(k(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{3k\delta})
time to
compute
M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
.
As there are at most
n
nodes
y\in Y_{r_{X}}
, the overall time bound
is
O(nk(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{3k\delta})
.
∎

4.5
Putting everything together

We can now describe our algorithm
for the
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem.
We first compute the navigating net in

O(n\log\Phi(Y))
time.
If
r_{X}<r_{\min}
, then
\rho\diam X<\dmin(Y)
, and thus
there is no
\rho
-matching.
Otherwise, we recursively compute
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
,
as described below. If
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
contains at least one matching,
then we return one of them, which by Property
17
a is a

(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching.
By Property
17
c, if
there is a solution to the
\rho
-distortion problem, then at least one

(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching must be recorded in
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
, which
shows that this algorithm indeed solves the

(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem.

We now explain how to compute
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
recursively.
The base case is
k=1
and
r_{X}=r_{\min}
. As explained above,
it can be done in
O(n)
time by recording a trivial matching
at each leaf node.

When
k\geqslant 2
, we split
X
into
P
and
Q
as described in
Lemma
16
. Then we compute recursively

L(P,\beta,r_{P})
and
L(Q,\beta,r_{Q})
where

\beta=\varepsilon/(8k-8)
and
r_{P}
and
r_{Q}
are the smallest
scales at least as large as
\rho\diam(P)
and
\rho\diam(Q)
,
respectively. We compute
L(P,\beta,r_{X})
and

L(Q,\beta,r_{X})
using Lemma
19
,
which takes time
O(nk(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{2k\delta}+n\log\Phi(Y))
.
Then we obtain

L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
by Lemma
21
in

O(nk(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{3k\delta})

time.

So the running time
T(n,k,\rho,\varepsilon)
of our algorithm satisfies
the relation

T(n,k,\rho,\varepsilon)=T(n,k_{1},\rho,\beta)+T(n,k_{2},\rho,\beta)+O(nk(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{3k\delta}+n\log\Phi(Y)),

where

k_{1}+k_{2}=k
,
1\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant k-1
and
\beta=\varepsilon/(8k-8)
.
This expression expands into a sum of
k-1
terms

O(nk(\rho^{2}(8k)^{k}/\varepsilon)^{3k\delta}+n\log\Phi(Y))
and

k
terms
O(n)
for the base cases. Hence we have

T(n,k,\rho,\varepsilon)=2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}n(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{3k\delta}+O(kn\log\Phi(Y))
. We just proved the following:

Theorem 22
.

The
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem can be solved in

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{3k\delta}n+O(kn\log\Phi(Y))
time.

Our algorithm may also allow us to find more than one matching.
If we return all the matchings stored in
L(X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
, we obtain
a collection of
(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching that approximates all the
solutions: By Property
17
c,
for each
\rho
-matching
\sigma
,
one of the
(1+\varepsilon)\rho
-matching
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
that we
return approximates
\sigma
in the sense that

d_{M}(\sigma,\sigma_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\varepsilon r_{X}/\rho^{2}<2\varepsilon\diam(X)/\rho
.

5
Improved Algorithm

In this section, we show how the approximation algorithm
presented in Section
4

can be improved using an efficient approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN)
data structure.
More precisely, we will use this data structure to
answer fixed-radius near neighbor queries efficiently.

First we briefly introduce
(3/2)
-approximate nearest neighbor searching.
Let
(S,d_{S})
be a doubling space, with doubling dimension
\delta_{S}
.
Let
T
be an
n
-point subset of
S
.
Given a query point
q\in S
, its nearest neighbor in
T

is a point
t_{q}\in T
such that
d_{S}(q,t_{q})
is minimum.
A
(3/2)
-approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) is a point
\hat{q}\in T

such that
d_{S}(q,\hat{q})\leqslant(3/2)d_{S}(q,t_{q})
. The data structure
by Cole and Gottlieb
[
9
]

returns a
(3/2)
-ANN in time
2^{O(\delta_{S})}\log n
.
This data structure is dynamic: It allows the insertion or
deletion of a point in
2^{O(\delta_{S})}\log n
time.

5.1
Fixed-radius near neighbor searching

We now consider a variation on the problem above. Given
a radius
R
and a query point
q
, a

fixed-radius near neighbor query
returns all the points in
T
that
are at distance at most
R
from
q
. We now show
how to efficiently answer these queries in a doubling space.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose that
\dmin(S)\geqslant R/\lambda
for some constant
\lambda>0
. Then Gottlieb and Cole’s ANN data structure
[
9
]
allows us to
answer fixed-radius near neighbor queries in
2^{O(\delta_{S})}\log n
time.
Each such query returns
2^{O(\delta_{S})}
points.
This data structure also supports insertions and
deletions in
2^{O(\delta_{S})}\log n
time.

We now show how to answer a query. Let
q
be a query point, so we
want to return all the points in
T\cap\ball(q,R)
. We repeatedly
perform (3/2)-ANN queries, as long as the distance
d_{S}(q,\hat{q})
from
q

to its approximate near neighbor
\hat{q}
is at most
3R/2
.
If
d_{S}(q,\hat{q})\leqslant R
, then we report
\hat{q}
. In any case,
we delete
\hat{q}
from
T
and insert it into a set
U
.
In the end, we find a point
\hat{q}
such that
d_{S}(q,\hat{q})>3R/2
,
and we complete the procedure by inserting
all the points of
U
back into
T
.

As
U\subseteq\ball(q,3R/2)
we have
\diam(U)\leqslant 3R
.
Since
U\subseteq S
we also have
\dmin(S)\geqslant R/\lambda
.
Therefore,
\Phi(U)\leqslant 3\lambda
, and since
U
has doubling
dimension
\delta_{S}
, we have
|U|=2^{O(\delta_{S})}
by
Lemma
1
. Therefore, we answer one fixed-radius
near neighbor query by performing
2^{O(\delta_{S})}
ANN queries,
insertions and deletions. So a fixed-radius near neighbor
query is answered in
2^{O(\delta_{S})}\log n
time.

5.2
Faster pruning

The bottleneck of our approximation algorithm is
when we check whether a matching
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is at distance
at least
\varepsilon r_{X}/(2\rho^{2})
from each matching

\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\in M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
.
(See Algorithm
2
.)
As
|M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})|=O((\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{k\delta})
,
it takes time
O(k(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{k\delta})
for each
matching
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
using brute force.

Instead of checking by brute force, we record each set
M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})

in a
(3/2)
-ANN data structure. Remember that we identify the space
of matchings from
X
to
Y
with
Y^{k}
, and thus this space has
doubling dimension
k\delta
by Corollary
3
.
By Property
17
b, we have

\dmin(M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X}))\geqslant\varepsilon r_{X}/(2\rho^{2})
.
By Lemma
23
,
this data structure allows us to perform insertions
and fixed-radius near neighbor
queries in
2^{O(k\delta)}\log n
time with radius
R=\varepsilon r_{X}/(2\rho^{2})
.
As we assume that
\delta=O(1)
, this is
2^{O(k)}\log n
time.
We only need one such query to determine whether

\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is at distance
at least
\varepsilon r_{X}/(2\rho^{2})
from each matching

\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\in M(y,X,\varepsilon,r_{X})
.

In summary, we replace the factor
O(k(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{k\delta})

in the time bound from Lemma
21
with a factor

2^{O(k)}\log n
.
So the running time of our approximation algorithm is
given by the recurrence relation (see Theorem
22
):

T(n,k,\rho,\varepsilon)=T(n,k_{1},\rho,\beta)+T(n,k_{2},\rho,\beta)+2^{O(k)}(\rho^{2}/\beta)^{2k\delta}n\log n+O(n\log\Phi(Y)),

We thus obtain the following time bound.

Lemma 24
.

The
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem can be solved in

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}n\log n+O(kn\log\Phi(Y))
time.

5.3
Removing the dependency on the spread

We now show how to remove the
O(kn\log\Phi(Y))
term in
the above time bound. This term comes
from the construction and traversal of the navigating net. But we do not
need to compute more than
k
levels of the navigating nets.
More precisely, we need to compute it at scale
r_{X}
, and at
each scale
r_{X^{\prime}}
where
X^{\prime}
is obtained by recursively
splitting
X
into two subsets according to Lemma
16
.
In order to perform our bottom-up construction, we will also
need to be able to find, for two such scales
r<r^{\prime}
,
an ancestor in
Y_{r^{\prime}}
for any node
y\in Y_{r}
.
We now explain how to achieve this using the ANN data structure.

We first show how to compute
Y_{r}
in
O(n\log n)
time.
We construct
Y_{r}
incrementally: For each point
y\in Y
,
we check whether it is at distance at least
r
from each previously
inserted point. It can be checked in
O(\log n)
time by
performing a fixed-radius near neighbor query.
If
y
is indeed at distance at least
r
from each previously
inserted point, we insert
y
into
Y_{r}
.
In order to perform this test efficiently, we record
Y_{r}

in an ANN data structure. By Lemma
23
,
as
\dmin(Y_{r})\geqslant r
, it
allows us to answer fixed-radius near neighbor queries in

O(\log n)
time with radius
R=r
. Insertions are also
performed in
O(\log n)
time, so we obtain the following:

Lemma 25
.

Let
Y
be an
n
-point metric space of constant doubling dimension.
We can compute an
r
-net of
Y
in
O(n\log n)
time.

Our algorithm for the
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem requires
us to know the horizontal edges of each
r
-net we consider.
They can also be computed in
O(n\log n)
time using
the same approach as the lemma above, except that the radius

R
is now
6r
.

Lemma 26
.

We can compute all the horizontal edges of
Y_{r}
in

O(n\log n)
time.

Finally, at scales
r<r^{\prime}
, we need to be able to find the
ancestor in
Y_{r^{\prime}}
for each node
y\in Y_{r}
, in order to run
our bottom-up construction of
L(W,\beta,r^{\prime})
from

L(W,\beta,r)
. As we no longer have vertical edges,
we relax the definition of ancestors as follows:
The
ancestor
of
y\in Y_{r}
is
a point
y^{\prime}\in Y_{r^{\prime}}
such that

d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\leqslant r^{\prime}
. (There could be several such points;
we can pick any one of them to be the ancestor of
y
.)
This property suffices for our algorithm to work as
described in Section
4
.

In order to compute the ancestors, we construct an ANN data
structure for
Y_{r^{\prime}}
. By Lemma
23
,
as
\dmin(Y_{r^{\prime}})\geqslant r^{\prime}
, we can construct the ancestor of any
point in
O(\log n)
time by performing a fixed radius
near-neighbor query with
R=r^{\prime}
.

Lemma 27
.

Given
Y_{r}
and
Y_{r^{\prime}}
such that
r^{\prime}>r
, we can compute

O(n\log n)
time the ancestors in
Y_{r^{\prime}}
of all
the points in
Y_{r}
.

This shows that we can run the algorithm from
Section
4
without computing the whole navigating net,
hence the
O(nk\log\Phi(Y))
term in Lemma
24

is replaced with
O(kn\log n)
, as we only compute
Y_{r}
for
k

different values of
r
. In summary, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 28
.

The
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-distortion problem can be solved in

2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\rho^{2}/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}n\log n
time.

6
Minimum distortion

In this section, we show how to extend our results on the

\rho
-distortion problem (Sections
3
,
4

and
5
) to the minimum distortion problem.

6.1
Hardness result

We show how to modify the construction from Section
3

so as to obtain a reduction
to the minimum distortion problem. Let
\lambda
be a large enough number,
say
\lambda=5m\rho^{2}2^{k}
.
We add an extra point
x_{k+1}
to
X
such that
d_{X}(x_{i},x_{k+1})=\lambda
for all
i\leqslant k
.
We also add a point
p_{k+1}
to
Y
that is at distance
\lambda
from every other
point. If there is a
k
-clique in
G
, then the mapping with minimum distortion
is the same as in Section
3
, where we add

\sigma_{x_{k+1}}=y_{k+1}
. It satisfies
\expansion(\sigma)=1
and

\expansion(\sigma^{-1})=\rho
, hence
\dist(X,Y)=\dist(\sigma)=\rho
.

Conversely, suppose that
\dist(X,Y)=\rho
. We first argue that we must have

\sigma(x_{k+1})=p_{k+1}
. If it were not the case, then

d_{Y}(\sigma(x_{1}),\sigma(x_{k+1}))\leqslant 2^{k}
so
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\geqslant 5m\rho^{2}
.
As
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=4\rho
and
d_{Y}(\sigma(x_{1}),\sigma(x_{2}))\geqslant 1/m
, we also have

\expansion(\sigma)\geqslant 1/(4\rho m)
and thus
\dist(x,y)\geqslant 5\rho/4
, a
contradiction.

Therefore, we must have
\sigma(x_{k+1})=y_{k+1}
. It implies that

\expansion(\sigma)\geqslant 1
. Then the only way to obtain
\dist(\sigma)\leqslant\rho

is to use the same construction as in Section
3
, where
we match each point
x_{i}
with a point in
R_{i}
for
3\leqslant i\leqslant k
.
The corresponding vertices in the
k
-clique instance form a clique.

Corollary 29
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
have doubling dimension
\log_{2}3
.
For any
\rho\geqslant 1
, the problem of deciding whether the minimum distortion

\dist(X,Y)
is equal to
\rho
is NP-hard, and is
W[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k
.
It cannot be solved in time
f(k)\cdot n^{o(k)}
for any computable function

f
, unless ETH is false.

6.2
A first algorithm

We now present a decision algorithm. Given two positive reals
e
,
e^{\prime}
,
we would like to know whether there is a mapping
\sigma:X\to Y
such
that
\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant e
and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant e^{\prime}
.
As observed by Kenyon et al.
[
21
]
, this is true if and only if there is
mapping
\sigma^{\prime}:X\to Y
with

\expansion(\sigma^{\prime})\leqslant\sqrt{ee^{\prime}}
and
\expansion(\sigma^{\prime-1})\leqslant\sqrt{ee^{\prime}}
,
where we use the metric
d^{\prime}_{Y}=\sqrt{e^{\prime}/e}\cdot d_{Y}
on
Y
, and we still use

d_{X}
on
X
. In other words, we are solving the
\rho
-distortion problem
with
\rho=\sqrt{ee^{\prime}}
on
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d^{\prime}_{Y})
. So we obtain
an approximate answer using our algorithm from Theorem
28
:

Lemma 30
.

Given
e,e^{\prime}>0
and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, our decision algorithm returns
a positive answer if there exists a mapping
\sigma:X\to Y
such that

\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant e
and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant e^{\prime}
,
and returns a negative answer if there is no mapping
\sigma:X\to Y

such that
\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)e
and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)e^{\prime}
.
Its running time is
2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(ee^{\prime}/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}n\log n.

Still following the approach by Kenyon et al., we can turn this decision
algorithm into an optimization algorithm by observing that
\expansion(\sigma)

and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})
are ratios of distances between points of
X

and
Y
, and thus there are
O(k^{2}n^{2})
candidate values for each.
So for any distinct
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
, we run the algorithm from
Lemma
30
using
e=d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})/d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
and
e^{\prime}=d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})/d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})

and with a relative error ratio
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/3
.
More precisely, we consider these pairs
(e,e^{\prime})
by increasing value of the product
ee^{\prime}
,
and we stop as soon as
we obtain a positive answer. At this point, there must be a matching
\sigma:X\to Y

such that
\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/3)e
and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/3)e^{\prime}
,
and since
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, we have
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/3)^{2}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)ee^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, we must have
ee^{\prime}\leqslant\dist(X,Y)
as otherwise,
the algorithm would have halted earlier. So taking
\Delta_{\varepsilon}=ee^{\prime}
, we obtain the following:

Lemma 31
.

Given
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, we can compute
a number
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
such that
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant\Delta_{\varepsilon}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\dist(X,Y)

in time
2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\dist(X,Y)/\varepsilon)^{2k\delta}k^{4}n^{5}\log n
.

6.3
Improved algorithm

We now describe a faster version of this optimization
algorithm. To this end, we need to introduce
well-separated pairs decompositions

(WSPD)
[
19
]
.
Given a metric space
(S,d_{S})

and two subsets
A,B\subseteq S
, we say that
A
and
B
are

(1/\varepsilon)
-
separated
if
\diam(A)\leqslant\varepsilon d_{S}(A,B)
and

\diam(B)\leqslant\varepsilon d_{S}(A,B)
. As a result,
for any
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
, we have

d_{S}(a,b)/(1+2\varepsilon)\leqslant d_{S}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\leqslant(1+2\varepsilon)d_{S}(a,b).

(4)

A WSPD of
S
of size
m
is a collection of pairs of subsets

\{A_{1},B_{1}\}
,
\{A_{2},B_{2}\}
,
\dots,\{A_{m},B_{m}\}
of
S
such that

A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are
(1/\varepsilon)
-separated for all
i
, and for any two distinct points

a,b\in S
, there is a pair
A_{i},B_{i}
such that
(a,b)\in A_{i}\times B_{i}
or

(a,b)\in B_{i}\times A_{i}
.

Callahan and Kosaraju
[
5
]
showed that a WSPD of size
n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}

can be implicitly computed in
O(n\log n)+n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
time when
S
is a set of
n
points
in a fixed-dimensional Euclidean space. In particular, we can obtain a pair
of points
(a_{i},b_{i})\in A_{i}\times B_{i}
for all
i
within this time bound.
This result was generalized to metrics of fixed doubling dimension
by Talwar
[
26
]
, but with size and construction time bounds
larger by a factor
O(\log\Phi(S))
. Still for doubling metrics, Har-Peled and Mendel gave a randomized
algorithm that constructs in
O(n\log n)+n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
time a WSPD of size

n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}

[
20
]
, which can be derandomized
using Cole and Gottlieb’s data structure
[
9
]
.
So if we let
\ell_{i}=d_{S}(a_{i},b_{i})
, we obtain the following.

Lemma 32
.

We can compute in time
O(n\log n)+n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
a set of lengths
\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\}

such that
m=n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
and for every pair of distinct points
a,b\in S
, there exists

i
such that
\ell_{i}/(1+\varepsilon)\leqslant d_{S}(a,b)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\ell_{i}
.

We can now present an approximate decision algorithm for the minimum distortion problem.
Given a distortion
\Delta
, it returns a positive answer if
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant\Delta
,
and it returns a negative answer if
\dist(X,Y)>(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
. We first compute
using Lemma
32
a set
\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\}
such
that
m=n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
and for all
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
,
y\neq y^{\prime}
, we have

\ell_{i}/(1-\varepsilon/6)\leqslant d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)\ell_{i}
for some
i
. Then for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
and for each pair of distinct points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
we run the decision algorithm of Lemma
30
with
expansions
e=(1+\varepsilon/6)\ell_{i}/d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
and
e^{\prime}=(1+\varepsilon/6)\Delta d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})/\ell_{i}
,
and with relative error ratio

\varepsilon/6
. If one of the answers to a calls to the algorithm of Lemma
30
is positive,
we return a positive answer, and otherwise we return a negative answer.

We now prove that this algorithm is correct. Suppose that
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant\Delta
.
Then there exists a mapping
\sigma:X\to Y
such that

\expansion(\sigma)\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant\Delta
. We must have

\expansion(\sigma)=d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})/d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for some
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
.
So there is
i
such that

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon/6}\cdot\frac{\ell_{i}}{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}\leqslant\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)\frac{\ell_{i}}{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}

and thus

\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant\frac{\Delta}{\expansion(\sigma)}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)\Delta\frac{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}{\ell_{i}}.

So we have
\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant e
and
\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant e^{\prime}
,
and thus the corresponding call to the decision algorithm of Lemma
30

must have returned a positive answer.

Now suppose that our algorithm returns a positive answer.
So there must be
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
a mapping
\sigma:X\to Y
such that
\expansion(\sigma)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)e
and

\expansion(\sigma^{-1})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)e
, where

e=(1+\varepsilon/6)\ell_{i}/d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
and
e^{\prime}=(1+\varepsilon/6)\Delta d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})/\ell_{i}
. It
follows that
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)^{2}ee^{\prime}
,
and thus
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/6)^{4}\Delta
. As
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, it implies
that
\dist(X,Y)<(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
.

So we have proved that this decision algorithm is correct. As we run the algorithm
of Lemma
30

k^{2}n/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
times, we obtain the following.

Lemma 33
.

Given
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1<\Delta
, the approximate decision algorithm above returns
a positive answer if
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant\Delta
, and a negative answer if

\dist(X,Y)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
.
Its running time is
2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\Delta^{2k\delta}/\varepsilon^{2k\delta+O(1)})n^{2}\log n.

We can turn this decision algorithm into an approximation algorithm for the minimum
distortion using exponential search. So we run this algorithm with
\Delta=1,2,4,\dots
until
it returns a positive answer for
\Delta=2^{i}
. At this point we know that

2^{i-1}<\dist(X,Y)<2^{i+1}
. Then we perform binary search for
\Delta

in the interval
[2^{i-1},2^{i+1}]
, still using the decision algorithm
of Lemma
33
. After
O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
steps, it yields a

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
\dist(X,Y)
. So we obtain the following result.

Theorem 34
.

Given
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, we can compute
a number
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
such that
\dist(X,Y)\leqslant\Delta_{\varepsilon}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\dist(X,Y)

in time
2^{O(k^{2}\log k)}(\dist(X,Y)^{2k\delta}/\varepsilon^{2k\delta+O(1)})n^{2}\log n
.
