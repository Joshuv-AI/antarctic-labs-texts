Searching in trees with monotonic query times Some results of this work have been presented at the 47th International Symposium on Mathematical Foundations of Computer Science (MFCS 2022) [ 20 ] . Partially supported by National Science Centre (Poland) grant number 2018/31/B/ST6/00820.
Dariusz Dereniowski, Izajasz Wrosz

Abstract
We consider the following generalization of binary search in sorted arrays to tree domains. In each step of the search, an algorithm is querying a vertex q q , and as a reply, it receives an answer, which either states that q q is the desired target, or it gives the neighbor of q q that is closer to the target than q q . A further generalization assumes that a vertex-weight function ω \omega gives the query costs, i.e., the cost of querying q q is ω ⁡ ( q ) \omega(q) . The goal is to find an adaptive search strategy requiring the minimum cost in the worst case. This problem is NP-complete for general weight functions and one of the challenging open questions is whether there exists a polynomial-time constant factor approximation algorithm for an arbitrary tree? In this work, we prove that there exist a constant-factor approximation algorithm for trees with a monotonic cost function, i.e., when the tree has a vertex v v such that the weights of the subsequent vertices on the path from v v to any leaf give a monotonic (non-increasing or non-decreasing) sequence S S . This gives a constant factor approximation algorithm for trees with cost functions such that each such sequence S S has a fixed number of monotonic segments. Finally, we combine several earlier results to show that the problem is NP-complete when the number of monotonic segments in S S is at least 4 4 . Keywords: binary search, graph search, approximation algorithm, query complexity

Introduction

Searching plays a central role in computer science due to its ubiquitous real-world applications as well as its inherent relation to other problems. Many fundamental computational problems, e.g. sorting, can be formulated as searching for an element in an appropriately defined set or apply searching as a subroutine. See for example the classic book
[
29
]
or a recent work on relation between sorting and binary search trees
[
5
]
. The search problem, both in the real world as well as in abstract terms, typically consists of a search space (e.g., a set, a house, a graph), a search target (vertex, or car keys) and a searcher that follows some search strategy. In the engineering of data management systems, searching information efficiently is essential for achieving high performance. Problem-solving by searching is a well established approach in artificial intelligence
[
40
]
. In operations research, algorithmic techniques applied to searching significantly improved efficiency of searching for submarines
[
25
]
, to give one example. On the other hand, the works on the naval applications contributed to formulation of numerous theoretical models of search games, e.g., the binary search game.

An important aspect of any search models is the amount of information available to the search agent about the potential location of the search target
[
40
]
. The
uninformed search
model is such that no additional information is available beyond the definition of the search space. The depth-first- or breadth-first-search and related models are examples of uninformed search algorithms. On the other hand, the
informed search
models assume that some additional information is available to the search agent. The additional information can be exploited to construct significantly faster search strategies. In practical setting, the additional information comes from exploiting the context of the search problem, e.g., an assumption that alike elements according to some similarity measure are
close
to each other, another practical example is an assumption that elements of the search space are sorted. In a theoretical setting, the additional information can be modeled as an heuristic function or an oracle.

In the category of the informed search models we can further characterize the search models in terms of
when
the additional information is exploited. I.e., we can first calculate a search strategy, e.g., in a form of a decision tree, based on the definition of the search space, which later would be used to locate arbitrary elements of the search space. Using the terms of supervised machine learning, we first learn the search model, which is then being inferred when searching for particular search targets. Consequently, the optimization criteria may be the time to learn the strategy, the accuracy and the time of locating particular search targets. In a contrary online approach, the search strategy is being refined at the same time as locating particular search targets.

1.1
Problem statement

We consider the following search problem defined for a node-weighted tree
T=(V,E,\omega)
, with a query cost function
\omega\colon V(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R_{+}}
. The goal is to design an adaptive algorithm that we call a
search strategy
or just
strategy
for short.
The strategy is divided into steps and in each step it performs a
query
by selecting some vertex
q
of
T
.
As an
answer
it receives information that either
q
is the
target
or otherwise it learns which neighbor of
q
is closer to the target.

Alternatively, one may think of the latter as learning which component of
T-q

In the former case, the search is completed.
The

To formally define the search strategy, consider a node weighted tree
T=(V,E,\omega)

t\in V(T)
. If
|V(T)|=1

\mathcal{A}(T,t)
is the element

be the vertex that the strategy queries first when searching in
T

. Then, for
|V(T)|>1

\mathcal{A}(T,t)
is a tuple
(q,\mathcal{A}(C_{1},t),...,\mathcal{A}(C_{k},t))
, where
k=|N(q)|
is the number of neighbors of
q
. We note that this kind of search definition can be understood as a form of a decision tree.

We denote by

\mathcal{A}
, when finding
t

The

\mathcal{A}

COST(\mathcal{A})=\max_{t\in V}COST(\mathcal{A},t)
.
The optimization criterion asks for a strategy of minimum cost.

One might think about this search process that with each query the search is narrowed to a subtree of
T
that can still contain the target.
The process is
adaptive
in the sense that the choice of the subsequent elements to be queried depends on the locations and answers of the previous queries.
Also, we consider only
deterministic
algorithms, where given a fixed sequence of queries performed so far (and the information obtained), the algorithm selects always the same element as the next query for a given tree. E.g., the optimal algorithm for the classic binary search problem on paths that always queries the median element is adaptive and deterministic. It can be equivalently stated that the algorithm calculates a
strategy
for the given input tree, which encodes the queries to be performed for each potential target.

Our problem is a generalization of the classical binary search, where one considers trees instead of paths. While also the generalized problem can be found in the literature under the name of binary search,
it might be illustrative to note that answers to queries over a tree domain are not necessarily binary (
left
or
right
), as the set of possible answers depends on the degree of the queried vertex.

1.3
Motivation and applications

We study a generalization of one of the most fundamental and well known problems in computer science, i.e., binary search. Since graphs form a natural abstraction for processes in many domains, our generalization into the domain of graphs allows us to seek for potential applications of our algorithms and related problems for example in the domain of large networks analysis
[
41
]
. Compared to unordered data structures, it is known that maintaining at least a partial order over the elements improves the performance of fundamental operations like search, update or insert, in terms of the number of comparisons needed
[
29
]
. In the context of data management systems, it is known that efficient indexing is crucial to obtain good performance. Some of the indexing schemes have the form of a tree, for example the GIndex. At the same time, calculating optimal search strategies for structures with a partial order is hard in general
[
18
]
. Practical approximation algorithms for searching in large graphs are important for the advancement of systems focused on large graph analysis.

Binary search on trees seen as a graph ranking problem (cf.
[
13
]
) can be used to model parallel Cholesky factorization of matrices
[
15
]
, scheduling of parallel database queries
[
33
]
, and VLSI layouts
[
43
]
. A more general search model, i.e., binary search on graphs has been used as a framework for interactive learning of classifiers, clusterings or rankings
[
21
,
22
]
.
More applications can be found in software testing
[
3
]
or mobile agent computing
[
6
]
.

There are several practical applications of searching where the cost of a query is naturally
higher
in some central vertex and decreases while moving towards the leaf nodes of the tree.
This occurs naturally when considering data access times in computer systems, e.g., due to memory hierarchies of modern processors, characteristics of the storage devices, or distributed nature of data management systems. In the literature on the binary search problem, a Hierarchical Memory Model of computation has been studied
[
1
]
, in which the memory access time is monotonic with respect to the location of a data element in the array. Another work was devoted to modeling the problem of text retrieval from magnetic or optical disks, where a cost model was such that the cost of a query was monotonic from the location of the previous query performed in the search process
[
36
]
.
We also mention an application of tree domains in automated bug search in computer code
[
3
]
.
In such case naturally occurs a possibility that the tree to be searched has the monotonicity property.
In particular, each query represents performing an automatic test that determines whether the part of the code that corresponds to the subtree under the tested vertex has an error.
Thus, the vertices that are closer to the root represent larger parts of the code and thus may require more or longer lasting tests. The scale of exa-scale software applications that may consist of several tens of separate software packages motivates the use of advanced software validation strategies, in order to quickly identify software regressions, e.g., when updating versions of several different packages.

An open question has been posed several times about whether a constant-factor approximation algorithm exists for arbitrary weight functions, see e.g.
[
2
,
8
,
9
]
.
The main motivation of this work is to address this question by finding natural input instances for which a constant-factor approximation is achievable.
In particular, we give an algorithm for down-monotonic cost functions and motivated by the theory of parameterized complexity, study a parameter
k
of the input instance: the maximum number of monotonic segments on any path from a central node to any leaf. The case of
k
equal to the tree height captures the class of general cost functions.
We see this as a step towards solving the general problem. Our techniques lead to a constant approximation for any fixed
k
. We believe that this method can be improved to allow combining segments of different monotonicity, while preserving the constant factor approximation.

1.4
Earlier techniques

Historically, the transition from paths to trees had an intermediate step where one wants to search partial orders instead of linear ones
[
32
,
35
]
.
Performing comparisons in partial orders leads to a similarly defined graph problem except that one queries edges instead of vertices.
However, each tree instance for querying edges can be solved via finding an optimal search strategy for a tree instance with querying vertices
[
14
]
.
For these reasons, we consider the more general vertex-query version on trees.

It turns out that our problem reappeared several times in theoretical computer science under different names.
It has been called
LIFO-search

[
24
]
in the area of graph pursuit-evasion games,
ordered coloring

[
28
]
, minimum height elimination trees
[
7
]
in the context of parallel matrix factorization, then vertex ranking
[
26
]
, and more recently as
tree-depth

[
37
]
.

While listing the main approaches to construct search strategies, we do not differentiate between the edge and vertex versions of the problem as it turns out that the algorithmic techniques we mention are applicable to both.
The majority of works, especially for the unweighted version of the problem utilize the bottom-up tree processing.
In this approach one usually assigns greedily the lowest possible interval and argues that this leads to an optimal solution, see e.g.
[
3
,
27
,
32
,
35
,
38
]
.
We note here that by ‘interval’ we mean the following interpretation of the cost function: if costs are seen as query durations, then the query interval is the corresponding time period in which the query is processed.
The particular contributions of these works mainly lie in various optimizations that bring down the computational complexity of finding optimal strategies.
In case of non-uniform query times the problem becomes more challenging and keeping in mind that it is NP-complete
[
12
]
a series of works focused on finding approximate solutions.
(We note that the unweighted case has been long known to be linear-time solvable
[
42
]
).
A natural approach, for the case of non-uniform costs, is to query a ‘central’ element of the search space and then computing the strategy recursively for the remaining subtrees
[
12
,
23
]
, which for trees gives
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-factor approximations.
More involved recursive approaches allowed to break the barrier of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
in
[
9
,
10
]
giving a
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n)
-approximation.
A rather involved bottom-up approach gave the best approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\log n})
so far
[
14
]
.
The main open question in this line of research is whether either the edge or vertex query model admits a polynomial-time constant factor approximation for weighted trees.
A possible angle at tackling this question is to consider either special subclasses of trees
[
9
]
or restricted weight functions
[
20
]
.

1.5
Our results

Given a tree
T=(V,E,\omega)
, we say that a cost function
\omega
is
up-monotonic
(respectively
down-monotonic
) if there exists a vertex
r\in V
such that for any
u,v\in V
, if
v
lies on the path between
r
and
u
in
T
, then
\omega(u)\leq\omega(v)
(
\omega(v)\leq\omega(u)
, respectively). We say that
\omega
is
monotonic
if it is either up-monotonic or down-monotonic. For this kind of
\omega
we get the following algorithms.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a linear-time 8-approximation adaptive search algorithm for an arbitrary tree with up-monotonic cost function.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a polynomial-time
2
-approximation adaptive search algorithm for an arbitrary tree with down-monotonic cost function.

We say that
\omega
is
k
-monotonic
,
k\geq 1
, if
T
can be partitioned into vertex disjoint subtrees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{l}
such that each of them is either up-monotonic or down-monotonic, and for some
r\in V
, any path from
r
to a leaf in
T
intersects at most
k
trees
T_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
.
By combining Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, we obtain:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a polynomial-time
8k
-approximation adaptive search algorithm for an arbitrary tree with a
k
-monotonic cost function.

This implies a constant factor approximation algorithm for instances with fixed
k
and raises a question whether the problem is FPT with respect to
k
?
We give a negative answer to this question by proving:

Theorem 1.4
.

The problem of finding a vertex search strategy for bounded diameter spiders with
k
-monotonic cost functions is strongly
NP
-complete for any fixed
k\geq 4
.

A
spider
is a tree with at most one vertex of degree greater than two.

Definitions and Preliminaries

The set of vertices and edges of a tree
T
is denoted by
V(T)
and
E(T)
, respectively. For a connected subset of vertices
V^{\prime}\subseteq V(T)
of
T
, we denote by

the induced subtree of

consisting of all vertices from
V^{\prime}
. For a rooted
T
, we denote its root by
r(T)
, while for any
v\in V(T)
,
T_{v}
is the subtree of
T
rooted at
v
which consists of
v

N(v)
denotes the set of neighbors of a vertex
v
.

For any two intervals
I=[a,b)
and
I^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
we write
I>I^{\prime}
(
I<I^{\prime}
) when
a\geq b^{\prime}
(
b\leq a^{\prime}
). Thus, whenever
I>I^{\prime}
or
I<I^{\prime}
, we have
I\cap I^{\prime}=\emptyset
. For an interval
I=[a,b)
and a number
c
, we write
I<c
(
I>c
), when
b\leq c
(
a\geq c
, respectively).
Furthermore, we define the sum
I+c
as the interval
[a+c,b+c)
.

We say that an interval
I=[a,b)
is
minimal
(
maximal
) in a given set of intervals, if and only if for any other interval
I^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
in the set, we have that
a\leq a^{\prime}
(respectively
b\geq b^{\prime}
). Note that every finite set of intervals contains both a minimal and a maximal interval. In this work, all intervals we use are left-closed, right-opened, and with integer endpoints.

We extend the classic lexicographic order over sequences of integers, denoted by binary operator
<_{l}
to sequences of intervals.
For two interval sequences
S
and
S^{\prime}
(each with pairwise disjoint intervals), we write
S<_{l}S^{\prime}
if and only if there exists an integer

such that
i+1\notin\bigcup S
,
i+1\in\bigcup S^{\prime}
and

[0,i)\cap\bigcup S=[0,i)\cap\bigcup S^{\prime}.

We then write
S\leq_{l}S^{\prime}
if and only if
S<_{l}S^{\prime}
or
S=S^{\prime}
.

We use the term

to refer to the sequence of vertices queried by a search strategy
\mathcal{A}
for a target
t
and we denote it by
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,t)
. The vertex queried by
\mathcal{A}
in the
i

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{A},i}(T,t)
. Given a search strategy for
T
, one can easily generate a search strategy for any subtree

by simply discarding the queries to the vertices outside of
T^{\prime}
.
A useful way of encoding search strategies is by using decision trees.

Definition 2.1
.

We define a

for
T=(V,E,w)
generated by a search strategy
\mathcal{A}
as a rooted tree
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}=V(T)
. The root of
\mathcal{T}
is the first vertex queried by
\mathcal{A}
. For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
v
has a child
v^{\prime}
if and only if
v^{\prime}
is queried by
\mathcal{A}
right after
v
for some choice of the target. Each

corresponds to a subset
\mathcal{T}(v)\subset V(T)
which contains all vertices that can still contain the target when
\mathcal{A}
queries

.

It can be seen that the root

\mathcal{T}(r)=V(T)
and the leaves correspond to single vertices in

.

Lemma
2.2
shows a property of the decision trees with respect to the positions of vertices that belong to some connected component of the input tree.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a decision tree for

is a subtree of

We traverse the decision tree

and following a path to some leaf. If

T^{\prime}
belong to exactly one subtree
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}

and set

as the first visited vertex in this process that belongs to
T^{\prime}
.

We now formally define the cost of a search process, which is the parameter of
T
the algorithm aims to minimize. In terms of the search strategy
\mathcal{A}
for an input tree
T=(V,E,\omega)

is denoted by

, while the

is defined as
COST(\mathcal{A})=\max_{t\in V}COST(\mathcal{A},t)

Sometimes in our analysis, we will take one decision tree and measure its cost with different query times, i.e., for different weights.
This approach will allow us to artificially increase the cost of some queries in the decision tree, which we formalize as follows.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a decision tree for
T=(V,E,\omega)
and
\omega^{\prime}
an arbitrary cost function defined on

, potentially a different one than

COST(\mathcal{T},\omega^{\prime})=\max\big\{\sum_{v\in V(P)}\omega^{\prime}(v)\;|\;P\textup{ is a path from the root to a leaf in }\mathcal{T}\big\}.

The
cost
of
\mathcal{T}
is then
COST(\mathcal{T},\omega)
and we shorten it to
COST(\mathcal{T})
. (Note that one may equivalently define the cost of
\mathcal{T}
by taking
COST(\mathcal{T})=COST(\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{T}
is generated by
\mathcal{A}

The minimum cost that is achievable for

OPT(T)=\min\{COST(\mathcal{T})\;|\;\mathcal{T}\textup{ is a decision tree for }T\}.

We say that
\mathcal{T}

is optimal
if and only if
COST(\mathcal{T})=OPT(T)
.

Lemma

Lemma 2.3

If
\mathcal{T}

We first apply Lemma

v
we also remove all subtrees that do not contain any vertex from
T^{\prime}
but we connect the roots of all other subtrees (note that there will be only one such subtree) directly under the parent of
v
. Hence, the reduced tree is still a decision tree. What is more, all paths from the root to a leaf in the obtained decision tree result from corresponding paths from
\mathcal{T}
by removing zero or more nodes, which upperbounds the cost of
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
by the cost of
\mathcal{T}
.
∎

We say that a cost function
\omega
is
rounded
if the values taken by
\omega
are powers of two (for any
v\in V(T)
,
\omega(v)=2^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
). We also say that
T^{\prime}
is the
rounding
of
T
if it is obtained from
T
by rounding the cost function to the closest, greater power of two. We obtain that
OPT(T^{\prime})\leq 2\,OPT(T)
because
\omega^{\prime}\leq 2\omega
. On the other hand, Lemma
2.4
shows how large is the overhead from applying an optimal decision tree for the input with a rounded cost function to search through the input tree with the original weights.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
T^{\prime}=(V,E,\omega^{\prime})
be the rounding of
T=(V,E,\omega)
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
be optimal decision trees respectively for
T
and
T^{\prime}
.
We have:

COST(\mathcal{T^{\prime}},\omega)\leq 2\,COST(\mathcal{T})=2OPT(T).

Proof.

We have
COST(\mathcal{T^{\prime}},\omega)\leq COST(\mathcal{T^{\prime}},\omega^{\prime})\leq COST(\mathcal{T},\omega^{\prime})\leq 2COST(\mathcal{T},\omega)
. The first inequality holds because
\omega\leq\omega^{\prime}
. The second inequality holds because
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
is optimal for
T^{\prime}
. The last inequality holds because
\omega^{\prime}\leq 2\omega
.
∎

2.1
Strategy functions

In case of uniform query times, an optimal search strategy can be obtained by calculating a
strategy function

f\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{N}
, where for each pair of distinct vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(T)
, if
f(v_{1})=f(v_{2})
, then each path connecting
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
has a vertex
v_{3}
such that
f(v_{3})>f(v_{1})
. As outlined earlier, methods using functions analogous to
f
can be found in the literature on problems such as elimination trees, vertex ranking
[
32
,
42
]
, tree-depth
[
37
]
, and others. One property of the strategy function is that it encodes the (reversed) order of the queries. E.g., in any sequence of queries, a vertex with a higher value of
f
is always queried before those with lower values of the strategy function. Thus, the maximal value of
f
over the vertices of a tree is the worst-case number of queries necessary to search the tree. In order to express the variable costs of queries we extend the strategy function into an
extended strategy function
(see also
[
12
]
).

Definition 2.5
.

A function
f\colon V(T)\to\{[a,b)\colon 0\leq a<b\}
, where
\rvert f(v)\rvert\geq\omega(v)
for each
v\in V(T)
, is an
extended strategy function
if for each pair of distinct vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(T)
, if
f(v_{1})\cap f(v_{2})\neq\emptyset
, then the path connecting
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
has a
v_{3}
such that
f(v_{3})>f(v_{1})\cup f(v_{2})
.

We say that an
f
is
optimal
for
T=(V,E,\omega)
if and only if
\sup\bigcup_{v\in V}f(v)
is minimal among all extended strategy functions for
T
.
3
3

3

We note that
\bigcup_{v\in V}f(v)
is an interval with the left endpoint equal to
0
for an optimal
f
.

Based on the time interpretation of strategy functions, optimal functions correspond to optimal strategies.

Observation 2.6
.

For any
T=(V,E,\omega)
, there exists an optimal extended strategy function
f
, such that
|f(v)|=\omega(v)
, for every
v\in V(T)
.
∎

Proof.

Let
f^{\prime}
be an optimal extended strategy function for
T=(V,E,\omega)
, while
v\in V(T)
, an arbitrary vertex such that
|f^{\prime}(v)|>\omega(v)
. Taking
f^{\prime}(v)=[a_{v},b_{v})
and
\delta_{v}=|f^{\prime}(v)|-\omega(v)
, we modify
f^{\prime}
into
f
by setting
f(v)=[a_{v}+\delta_{v},b_{v})
. Because
\sup\bigcup_{v\in V}f(v)=\sup\bigcup_{v\in V}f^{\prime}(v)
,
f
is an optimal extended strategy function for
T
.
∎

Given an extended strategy function
f
for a node weighted tree
T=(V,E,\omega)
, one easily obtains a search strategy for the tree by first locating the vertex
q
with the maximal interval assigned by

. In a trivial case, where
|V(T)|=1
, this completes the construction of
\mathcal{A}
. Otherwise, let us first note that there is only one such vertex
q
in
V(T)
with a maximal interval. Assume that there is a
q^{\prime}\in V(T)
,
q\neq q^{\prime}
, such that
b^{\prime}=b
, where
f(q)=[a,b)
,
f(q^{\prime})=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Then, because
f(q)\cap f(q^{\prime})\neq\emptyset

, on the simple path between

and
q^{\prime}
, a vertex
p
would have to exist such that
f(p)>f(q)\cup f(q^{\prime})
. Consequently,
q
could not be maximal.
By the same argument, this maximal interval has an empty intersection with intervals assigned to any other vertex.
Let
|N(q)|=k
and let
T_{i}
be the
i
-th component of
T\setminus\{q\}
,
i\leq k
. Then, for arbitrary search target
t
,
\mathcal{A}(T,t)
is a tuple
(q,\mathcal{A}(T_{1},t),...,\mathcal{A}(T_{k},t))
.
Then, the construction of
\mathcal{A}
continues recursively by using
f
restricted to each component
T_{i}
.

On the other hand, given a search strategy
\mathcal{A}
, we can obtain an extended strategy function
f
that encodes the same sequences of queries as those made by
\mathcal{A}
. Let
q
be the vertex queried as first by
\mathcal{A}
. We set

in the trivial case, i.e. when
|V(T)|=1
. If
|V(T)|>1
, let
\mathcal{A}=(q,\mathcal{A}(T_{1},t),...,\mathcal{A}(T_{k},t))
,
N(q)=k
. We fix the values of
f
for all vertices different than

by recursively repeating the process for the components
T_{i}
. Note that each

is referenced exactly once in
\mathcal{A}
. This is the case, because the components

have disjoint vertex sets. After fixing

in the components, we set
f(q)=[\sup\bigcup_{v\neq q}f(v),\sup\bigcup_{v\neq q}f(v)+\omega(q))
. We will now show that

is an extended strategy function. Consider vertices
x\neq y
in
V(T)
, such that
f(x)\cap f(y)\neq\emptyset
. Consider the sequences of queries
S_{x}
and
S_{y}
, leading respectively to querying
x
and
y
. Since both
S_{x}
and
S_{y}
are generated by the same strategy

, they have a common prefix of length at least one of identical vertices. Let
v
be the last vertex in the prefix. Since
x\neq y
, at least one vertex out of
x
and
y
was set after

by our procedure before both
x
and
y
. In other words, because
x\neq y
only one of them might be

. Consequently we have that
f(x)\cup f(y)<f(v)
.

This also shows that the interval assigned to a vertex
v
by

might encode the time interval for querying
v
, where the ‘time’ is understood as dictated by the precedence relation we use for intervals.

Given an extended strategy function
f
for
T
, we say that a vertex

is

from a vertex

if and only if on the path that connects

and

there is no vertex
x
such that
f(u)<f(x)
. The idea of visibility has been used e.g. in
[
45
]
and also earlier under the name of ‘critical lists’ in
[

]
. The sequence of values (intervals) visible from

in descending order is called a

(
extended visibility sequence
) for

and is denoted by
S(v)
.
Whenever we need to point out that

is obtained from a given

in the above way, we say that
S(v)
is
derived
from

.

The concept of visibility is also used when
f
is defined only on a subset of
V(T)
, i.e., on all descendants of some vertex
v
. In this context, which typically occurs during bottom-up processing of

, only those vertices are considered for which

has been already defined.

We will decide on a strategy function
f(v)
for each vertex

by assigning an integer
j_{v}
to

in a bottom-up processing of the input tree. Each

will determine

differently for different vertices. However, the

will be computed via an appropriate use of the following operator.

Definition 2.7

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let
f
be a strategy function defined on all descendants of a

. Let

,

, be the children of

. The
vertex extension
operator attributes

with

, based on the visibility sequences

in the following way. Let

be the set of integers that belong to at least two distinct visibility sequences

, while

. We set

as the lowest integer greater than

.

The following lemma characterizes the vertex extension operator.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
38
]
)
.

Given the visibility sequences assigned to the children of
v\in V(T)
, the
vertex extension operator
assigns to

a visibility sequence that is lexicographically minimal (the vertex extension operator is minimizing). Moreover, (lexicographically) increasing the visibility sequence of a child of

(the vertex extension operator is monotone).

3
Structured decision trees

In Sections
3
,
4
we consider rooted trees
T=(V,E,\omega)
, in which while traversing any path from the root to a leaf, the weights of the visited vertices are non-increasing.
In particular, the root of
T
is selected properly according to the definition of an up-monotonic cost function.
This section gives a foundation for the algorithm in Section
6
.

We define a
layer
of a tree
T
as a subgraph
L
of
T
such that all vertices in
V(L)
have the same cost,
\omega(u)=\omega(v)
for any
u,v\in V(L)
. A connected component of
L
is called a
layer component
. We also denote by
\omega(L)
the cost of querying a vertex that belongs to
L
, i.e.,
\omega(L)=\omega(v)
for any
v\in V(L)
.

The
upper border
of a layer
L
is the set of roots of its layer components. The subset of
V(L)
consisting of vertices that have at least one child that belongs to a different layer is called the
lower border
of
L
. We will also say that a layer component
L^{\prime}
is
directly below
a layer component
L
if and only if
r(L^{\prime})
has a parent in
L
. The
top
layer component is the one that contains the root of
T
and a
bottom
layer component is any
L
such that there is no component directly below
L
.
We note that we will apply the above terms to a rounded
\omega
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T
be a tree with a up-monotonic cost function and
\mathcal{T}
a decision tree for
T
. Consider a layer component
L
of
T
. Let
v=r(L)
. We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
structured with respect to

L
if for every vertex
u\in V(T_{v})
, it holds that
u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{v})
. We say that a decision tree
\mathcal{T}
for
T
is
structured
if and only if
\mathcal{T}
is structured with respect to all layer components of
T
.

Informally speaking, in a structured
\mathcal{T}
we require that each vertex in the entire subtree
T_{v}
is below
v
in
\mathcal{T}
, for each
v
that is the root of a layer component.
This is one of the central ideas used in the algorithm for up-monotonic cost functions for the following reason.
While performing bottom-up processing of (an unweighted)
T
in
[
38
]
(and also other works, e.g.
[
32
]
) each vertex
v
encodes which steps are already used for queries of the vertices from
T_{v}
.
In our case, that is in a weighted
T
, we need to encode intervals and the technical problem with that is that they have different durations while moving upwards from one layer component to the next.
Thus, some intervals that are free to perform queries while moving to a parent of
v
may be too short due to the weights in the next layer component.
To deal with that, we make
v=r(L)
also the root of
\mathcal{T}_{v}
.
In this way only one interval, i.e., the one assigned to
v
needs to be taken into account while moving upwards.

Our method requires the decision tree to be structured only when processing the internal components of
T
. By not enforcing structuring with respect to the top layer component, a potential additive cost of a single query can be avoided. (This will be reflected in line
2
of Algorithm
2
). However, this optimization does not change the worst-case cost of the strategy. In result, the decision tree generated by our algorithm is not structured in general. Although
r(\mathcal{T})
, the first vertex queried by the strategy, always belongs to the top layer component of
T
, we may have that
r(\mathcal{T})\neq r(T)
.
In contrast, in a structured decision tree we always have a stronger property, namely
r(\mathcal{T})=r(T)
.

It turns out in our analysis that considering only structured decision trees introduces an overall multiplicative cost of
2
to the performance of the algorithm:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T=(V,E,\omega)
be a tree with a up-monotonic and rounded cost function. There exists a structured decision tree
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
for
T
, such that
COST(\mathcal{T^{\prime}})\leq 2\>OPT(T)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be any decision tree for
T
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is selected arbitrarily, it is enough to prove that
COST(\mathcal{T^{\prime}})\leq 2\>COST(\mathcal{T})
.

In order to construct the structured decision tree, we traverse
\mathcal{T}
in a breadth-first fashion starting at the root of
\mathcal{T}
. The
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
is constructed in the process.

By Lemma
2.2
, for an arbitrary layer component
L
of
T
, there exists a vertex
u^{\prime}\in V(L)
such that all vertices from
L
belong to
\mathcal{T}_{u^{\prime}}
. Among all vertices of
L
,
u^{\prime}
is visited first during the traversal.

Consider an arbitrary node
u
being accessed during the traversal. Let
L
be the layer component such that
u\in L
. If
\mathcal{T}
is structured with respect to
L
we continue with the next node. Otherwise (which for a specific
L
will happen only once, when
u=u^{\prime}
, as argued in the last paragraph of the proof), we modify
\mathcal{T}
by performing the following steps.

Let
v=r(L)
. We want to replace the subtree
\mathcal{T}_{u}
with a subtree consisting of the same nodes but rooted at
v
. Consider a subtree
T^{\prime}
of the input
T
, which can still contain the target when the search process is about to query
u
according to
\mathcal{T}
. E.g.,
V(T^{\prime})=V(\mathcal{T}_{u})
. Let
v
has
k
neighbors in
T^{\prime}
. We attach under
v
the decision trees created with Lemma
2.3
for the
k
components obtained by removing
v
from
T^{\prime}
.
Lemma
2.3
shows that the cost of any of the
k
decision trees is not greater than
COST(\mathcal{T}_{u})
. Accounting for the cost of
v
, structuring
L
increases
COST(\mathcal{T})
no more than
\omega(L)
. Consider an arbitrary path
P
from root to a leaf in
\mathcal{T}
. For every node
u\in P
that triggers structuring (informally,
u
is
u^{\prime}
for some
L
) an additional node from
L
is inserted in
P
. Thus, in the worst case, the weighted length of arbitrary
P
increases 2 times when transforming
\mathcal{T}
into structured
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
.

By starting the breadth-first traversal at the root of
\mathcal{T}
, structuring with regards to one layer component does not make
\mathcal{T}
not structured with regards to any of the previously structured layer components.
∎

By combining Lemmas
2.4
and
3.2
one obtains Corollary
3.3
, which relates the cost of an optimal decision tree for an input
T
with the cost of a structured decision tree obtained through the analysis of the rounding of
T
.

Corollary 3.3
.

For any tree
T
with a up-monotonic cost function there exists a structured decision tree with rounded weights, whose cost is at most
4\cdot OPT(T)
.

4
Bottom-up tree processing

We extend the state-of-the-art ranking-based method for searching in trees with uniform costs of queries
[
38
,
42
]
, where the algorithm calculates a strategy function
f\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{N}
, given the input tree
T=(V,E)
. In our approach, to express the variable costs of queries we will use an interval-based extended strategy function.

Keeping in mind that the cost function is the time needed to perform a query, the values of an extended strategy function indicate the time periods in which the respective vertices will be queried.
Note that, e.g., due to the rounding, the time periods will be longer than the query durations and this is easily resolved either by introducing idle times or by following the actual query durations while performing a search based on a strategy produced by our algorithm.

In our approach, informally speaking, we will partition the rounding of the input tree into several subtrees (the layer components) and for each of them, we will use the algorithm from
[
38
]
.

A useful class of extended strategy functions is one that generates decision trees that are structured (see Definition
3.1
).

Definition 4.1
.

We say that an extended strategy function
f
, defined on the vertices of a tree
T
with up-monotonic cost function, is
structured
if for any layer component
L
,
f(u)>f(v)
for each
v\in V(T_{u})
, where
u=r(L)
.

It follows that a structured extended strategy function represents a structured decision tree.

As mentioned earlier, we will process each layer component
L
with the vertex extension operator but in order to do it correctly, we need to initialize appropriately the roots of the layer components below
L
.
For that, we define the following operators.
Let
f
be an extended strategy function defined on the vertices of
T_{v}
, where
v
is the root of a layer component
L^{\prime}
. The
structuring operator
assigns to
v
the minimal interval so that
f
is a structured extended strategy function on
T_{v}
. (We note that in our algorithm
v
will have some interval assigned when the structuring operator is about to be applied to
v
. Hence, the application of this operator will assign the new required interval.) The
cost scaling operator
aligns the interval attributed to
v
: if
f(v)=[a,b)
and
L
is the layer component directly above
L^{\prime}
, the cost scaling operator assigns
v
the interval
[\omega(L)\lceil\frac{b}{\omega(L)}\rceil-\omega(L),\omega(L)\lceil\frac{b}{\omega(L)}\rceil)
.
We will say that vertex
v
is
aligned to

\omega(L)
after this modification (see the beginning of Section
5
for a formal definition).
Informally speaking, this is done so that the children of the leaves in
L
have intervals whose endpoints are multiples of
\omega(L)
so that they become ‘compatible’ with the allowed placements for intervals of the vertices from
L
.
Yet in other words, this allows us to translate the intervals of the children of the leaves in
L
into integers that can be treated during bottom-up processing of

in a uniform way.

Observation 4.2
.

Let a layer component
L^{\prime}
be directly below a layer component
L
in
T=(V,E,\omega)
. If

is assigned an interval
[a,b)
, whose endpoints are consecutive multiples of
\omega(L^{\prime})
(i.e.

and

an interval

such that
b^{\prime}\leq b+\omega(L)-\omega(L^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Consider a layer component
L
of
T=(V,E,\omega)
and a structured extended strategy function
f
defined on the vertices below
L
. The cost scaling operator selects
b^{\prime}
as the smallest multiple of
\omega(L)
that is greater or equal than
b
. Since
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime}-\omega(L)
, we have that
a^{\prime}<b
. On the other hand, because
b-a=\omega(L^{\prime})
,

is the highest multiple of
\omega(L^{\prime})
that is less than

. Subsequently, because
w(L)
is divisible by
w(L^{\prime})
, we obtain that
a^{\prime}\leq a
. What follows,

, and finally
b^{\prime}\leq b+\omega(L)-\omega(L^{\prime})
.
∎

9
The NP-completeness

It has been shown in
[
10
]
that the edge variant of binary search is NP-complete already for specific small-diameter trees. We will use this fact to argue that vertex search with
k
-monotonic cost functions is not FPT with respect to
k
.

The edge search problem considers as an input an edge-weighted tree
T^{\prime}=(V,E,c)
, where
c
attributes each edge with the cost of the query. For an arbitrary search target
t\in V(T^{\prime})
, a query to an edge
e\in E(T^{\prime})
incurs the cost of
c(e)
and learns which one of the two components in
T^{\prime}-e
contains
t
. For a given input tree, similarly as in the vertex search, the goal of the algorithm is to obtain a search strategy that has minimal total cost in the worst case.
Note that this problem can be equivalently defined as performing vertex search in the line graph of
T^{\prime}
.

Following the description provided in
[
14
]
, we formulate Lemma
9.1
, which shows a connection between the two problems.
First, for each tree
T^{\prime}
we define its
subdivision

T=(V,E,\omega)
as follows.
Let
M=1+\sum_{e\in E(T^{\prime})}c(e)
exceed the weight of all edges in
T^{\prime}
.
For each
e\in E(T^{\prime})
introduce a vertex
v(e)
with weight
\omega(v(e))=c(e)
.
Then,
V(T)=V(T^{\prime})\cup\{v(e)\colon e\in E(T^{\prime})\}
,
E(T)=\{\{x,v(e)\},\{v(e),y\}\colon e=\{x,y\}\in E(T^{\prime})\}
, and
\omega(x)=M
for each
x\in V(T^{\prime})
.
In other words, we obtain
T
by putting the weight
M
on each vertex of
T^{\prime}
and subdividing each edge
e
so that the new vertex
v(e)
that comes from the subdivision of
e
‘inherits’ its weight from
e
.
Informally, the idea behind this construction is that when performing a vertex search on
T
, one should never query any vertex
x
in
V(T^{\prime})
due to the large weights.
Instead, in order to learn whether
x
is the target in
T
, it is favorable to query all of its neighbors.

Lemma 9.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

For each tree
T^{\prime}
, the edge search cost of
T^{\prime}
equals
OPT(T)
.

Proof.

Consider algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for vertex search in
T
based on the edge search algorithm on
T^{\prime}
.

\mathcal{A}
queries only the
old
vertices. If the queried vertex is the target
A
stops. Otherwise,
\mathcal{A}
continues in the subgraph that can still contain the target, until either the target vertex is queried or the subtree is reduced to a single vertex.

•

If target is one of the old/original vertices, it will be found by reducing
T
to a single vertex - the search target. The cost in worst case is OPT(T’).

•

If target vertex is one of the new vertices,
\mathcal{A}
will continue the search until it queries the search target. Note that every edge of T’ is queried when searching for some target vertex in T’. The cost of searching such target in worst case is OPT(T’).

∎

It has been shown in
[
10
]
that the edge search is (strongly) NP-complete in the class of spiders of diameter at most
6
.
For each such spider
T^{\prime}
, the diameter of its subdivision
T
equals
12
, and the corresponding cost function
\omega
is
4
-monotonic.
This proves Theorem
1.4
.

10
Conclusions

Our first remark is on a possible complexity gap with respect to
k
. It is not known whether the problem for
1
-monotonic cost functions is NP-complete (cf.
[
20
]
). Hence an open problem remains whether the value of
k=4
in Theorem
1.4
is in fact tight. We conjecture that the problem is NP-complete for a
k<4
. Our conjecture is supported by the fact that we obtained the constant
k=4
basically via a ‘black-box’ transformation from the edge search
[
10
]
. Thus, a more refined reduction might provide the right threshold for
k
.

In a wider context, our work is en route to a search for a constant factor approximation algorithm for an arbitrary cost function in trees. Our choice of looking at
k
-monotonic cost functions may be seen as the problem parametrization with respect to
k
. However, it is interesting if other parametrization methods are also natural and algorithmically promising.

We recall a related tree search problem, called
edge search
, in which one performs queries on edges: each reply provides information which endpoint of the queried edge is closer to the target
[
3
]
.
One can similarly define for example an up-monotonic cost function
\omega\colon E(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R_{+}}
for the edge search by requiring that there exists a choice for the root
r
so that for any two edges
\{x,y\}
and
\{y,z\}
, if
x
is closer to
r
than
y
, then
\omega(\{x,y\})\geq\omega(\{y,z\})
. We have outlined some evidence that for unweighted trees, the edge search has been more challenging to analyze than the vertex search, both when it comes to finding good upper bounds (see
[
42
]
vs
[
16
,
23
,
33
]
) and optimal algorithms (see
[
42
]
vs
[
38
,
32
]
).
In view of the relation between the two problems in their weighted variants (see Section
9
) it may be possible that the above does not carry out to this case.
Hence, a fruitful path to get algorithmic progress for the weighted vertex search problem is to first develop new techniques for the weighted edge search.

Also, we repeat the previously mentioned interesting and challenging open question whether a constant-factor approximation is possible for the vertex search in trees with general weight functions.
