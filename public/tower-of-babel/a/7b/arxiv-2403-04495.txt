1.
Introduction

Given a positive integer
n
, a partition of
n
is an expression:

\displaystyle n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{k},

where all the numbers
n_{j}
are integers and
1\leq n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\ldots\leq n_{k}
. Let
p(n)
denote the number of partitions of a natural number
n
.

One of the most classical problems in the theory of partitions is studying the arithmetic properties of certain sequences of partitions. It originates in the celebrated work of Ramanujan, who proved that

\displaystyle p(5n+4)\equiv

\displaystyle\ 0\pmod{5},

\displaystyle p(7n+5)\equiv

\displaystyle\ 0\pmod{7},

and

\displaystyle p(11n+6)\equiv

\displaystyle\ 0\pmod{11}

hold for each nonnegative integer
n
. Similar results were proved also for other types of partitions, that is, for functions counting only the partitions of a given number that satisfy certain constrains.

In this paper we focus on the case of
m
-ary partitions. More precisely, these are partitions such that every part is a power of a fixed number
m
. For example, all the
2
-ary partitions of number
5
are

\displaystyle 1+1+1+1+1=1+1+1+2=1+2+2=1+4.

In particular, the number of such partitions is equal to
4=:b_{2}(5)
. In general, there are no simple formulas for the number of
m
-ary partitions of
n
, and we will denote this number by
b_{m}(n)
.

In the context of
m
-ary partitions of various types, the problem of finding so called supercongruences, that is, congruences modulo high powers of
m
, is one of the most important parts of the theory. Churchhouse was the first to ask about arithmetic properties of the sequences of binary partitions. In
[
3
]
, he proved that
b_{2}(n)
is even if
n\geq 2
. He also characterized numbers
b_{2}(n)
modulo
4
and conjectured that for a fixed positive integer
k
we have

\displaystyle b_{2}(2^{k+2}n)\equiv

\displaystyle\ b_{2}(2^{k}n)\pmod{2^{3k+2}},

\displaystyle b_{2}(2^{2k+1})\equiv

\displaystyle\ b_{2}(2^{2k-1})\pmod{2^{3k}}

for all positive integers
n
. This conjecture was proved by Rødseth in
[
8
]
.

The above result was subsequently improved and extended to the case of
m
-ary partitions by Andrews
[
1
]
, Gupta
[
5
]
, and Rødseth and Sellers
[
9
]
. Namely, they proved that

(1)

\displaystyle b_{m}(m^{r+1}n-\sigma-m)\equiv 0\ \ \ \left(\bmod{\frac{m^{r}}{c_{r}}}\right),

where for a sequence
(\varepsilon_{j})_{j=1}^{r-1}\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
we define

\displaystyle\sigma:=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\varepsilon_{j}m^{j},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ c_{r}:=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $m$ is odd},\\
2^{r-1}&\textrm{if $m$ is even}.\end{cases}

Similar results were obtained for many types of
m
-ary partitions including the case of
m
-ary partitions with no gaps
[
2
,
6
]
and
m
-ary overpartitions
[
7
]
.

Folsom et al. introduced in
[
4
]
the class of
M
-non-squashing partitions that generalizes ordinary
m
-ary partitions. Here, we call the
M
-non-squashing partitions the
M
-ary partitions for simplicity and due to their similarity with the ordinary
m
-ary partitions. More precisely, let
M=(m_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of integers,
m_{0}=1
,
m_{i}\geq 2
for
i\geq 1
, and for
r\geq 0
let

\displaystyle M_{r}:=m_{0}m_{1}\cdots m_{r}.

Then an
M
-ary partition of a positive integer
n
is an expression of the form

\displaystyle n=M_{r_{1}}+M_{r_{2}}+\cdots+M_{r_{s}},

for some nonnegative integers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{s}
. We denote the number of
M
-ary partitions of
n
by
p_{M}(n)
.

The natural question arises whether the congruences (
1
) can be generalized to a more general class of
M
-partitions, and if not, whether we can prove some weaker congruences. In fact, our main motivation is the following conjecture stated in
[
4
]
.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
4
]
, Cojecture 1.10)
.

Let sequences
M=(m_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
and
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{j})_{j=1}^{r-1}\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
be fixed. Let

(2)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}:=\frac{m_{j}}{(m_{j},P_{j})}\hskip 28.45274pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 28.45274pt\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\varepsilon_{j}\prod_{i=0}^{j}m_{i},

where
P_{j}=\prod_{p\leq j}p
is the product of all primes up to
j
. Then for all integers
n\geq 1
and
0\leq c\leq m_{1}-1
we have that

\displaystyle p_{M}(m_{1}\cdots m_{r}n-\sigma-m_{1}-c)\equiv 0\pmod{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}.

In the special case of partitions into factorials, the above conjecture can be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
4
]
, Cojecture 1.11)
.

Let
M=(j)_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Then

\displaystyle p_{M}(r!n-\sigma-c)\equiv 0\pmod{r!/D_{r}},

where
\sigma
is the same as in (
2
),
c\in\{1,2\}
, and

\displaystyle D_{r}=\prod_{p\leq r-2}p^{\left\lfloor\frac{r-2}{p}\right\rfloor}.

Unfortunately, the above conjectures are false in general. Indeed, we checked, using a Mathematica
[
10
]
that for example

\displaystyle p_{M}(5!n-2-4!)\equiv 10\ \ \left({\rm mod}\ \frac{5!}{6}\right).

for
n\in\{2,6,8,10,12,16\}
. In fact, Conjecture
1.2
failed in almost all cases we checked, that is, for sequences with
r\leq 5
. However, we made an attempt to find and prove some weaker results of the same type. We focused on the situation in which
\sigma=0
. Moreover, we prove the above conjectures only modulo a little worse modulus than the product
\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}
.

For all positive integers
a
and
b
we define
\mu(a,b):=\frac{a}{(a,b)}
. Moreover, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(m,r):=\gcd\{\ \mu(m,j)\ |\ j=1,\ldots,r\ \}=\frac{m}{\gcd\big(m,{\rm lcm}(1,\ldots,r)\big)}

for positive integers
m
and

.

The main result of the paper is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M=(m_{0},m_{1},\ldots)
be a (possibly finite) sequence of integers such that
m_{0}=1
and
m_{j}\geq 2
if
j\geq 1
. Then for every positive integers
n
and
r
we have

\displaystyle p_{M}(m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r}n-1)\equiv 0\ \ \left({\rm mod}\ \prod_{t=2}^{r}\mathcal{M}(m_{t},t-1)\right).

Let us provide some instances in which Conjecture
1.1
is true. In fact even stronger divisibility property holds if we assume that
m_{r}
has only large prime factors for every
r
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Assume for every prime
p
that if
p\mid m_{r}
then
p\geq r
. Then for every
n
we have

\displaystyle p_{M}(m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r}n-1)\equiv 0\ \ \left({\rm mod}\ \prod_{t=2}^{r}m_{r}\right).

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Finally, observe that

\displaystyle\gcd\big(m,{\rm lcm}(1,2)\big)=\gcd(m,2)

and

\displaystyle\gcd\big(m,{\rm lcm}(1,2,3)\big)=\gcd(m,2\cdot 3).

Hence, we get that divisibility properties from Theorem
1.3
are the same as in Conjectures
1.1
and
1.2
if
r\in\{1,2,3\}
and
\sigma=0
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Conjectures
1.1
and
1.2
are true if
r\in\{1,2,3\}
and
\sigma=0
.

\displaystyle\mu(mi,r)\mid\alpha_{m,r}(i).

In particular,
\mu(m,r)\mid\alpha_{m,r}(i)
for all
i
.

Proof.

The number
m
will not change during the proof so we omit it and write
\alpha_{r}(i)
for
\alpha_{m,r}(i)
in order to simplify the notation. For every
n
we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{r}(i)

\displaystyle\binom{n+i-1}{i}=\binom{mn+r-1}{r}=\frac{mn+r-1}{r}\binom{mn+r-2}{r-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{mn+r-1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\alpha_{r-1}(i)\binom{n+i-1}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\alpha_{r-1}(i)\big(m(n+i)-(mi-r+1)\big)\binom{n+i-1}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\alpha_{r-1}(i)\left[m(i+1)\binom{n+i}{i+1}-(mi-r+1)\binom{n+i-1}{i}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i=2}^{r}\frac{mi}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i-1)\binom{n+i-1}{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\frac{mi-r+1}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i)\binom{n+i-1}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ m\alpha_{r-1}(r-1)\binom{n+r-1}{r}

\displaystyle\ +\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}\left[\frac{mi}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i-1)-\frac{mi-r+1}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i)\right]\binom{n+i-1}{i}-\frac{m-r+1}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(1)\binom{n}{1}.

By comparing the coefficients of
\binom{n+i-1}{i}
we get

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}(i)=\frac{mi}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i-1)-\frac{mi-r+1}{r}\alpha_{r-1}(i),

or equivalently,

\displaystyle r\alpha_{r}(i)-(r-1)\alpha_{r-1}(i)=mi\big(\alpha_{r-1}(i-1)-\alpha_{r-1}(i)\big).

We can write down the same equalities with
r
replaced by any
s\leq r
to get

\displaystyle s\alpha_{s}(i)-(s-1)\alpha_{s-1}(i)=mi\big(\alpha_{s-1}(i-1)-\alpha_{s-1}(i)\big).

Let us sum all these equalities by sides over
1\leq s\leq r
. We get

\displaystyle r\alpha_{r}(i)=mi\sum_{s=1}^{r}\big(\alpha_{s-1}(i-1)-\alpha_{s-1}(i)\big).

The main part of the statement follows. The second part is a consequence of the fact that
\mu(m,r)\mid\mu(mi,r)
for every
i
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For every
r\geq 1
and every
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{s}\leq r
we have

\displaystyle\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv 0\ \ \bigg({\rm mod}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t\leq r\\
t\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{s}\}\end{subarray}}\mu(m_{t},t)\bigg).

If
m_{1}=m_{2}=\ldots=m
, then
\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv 0\pmod{m^{r-s}/(m,2)}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
r
. The cases
r=1
,
r=2
and
r=3
simply follow from Example
2.4
. Let us assume that the statement is true for
r-1
and consider recurrence relations from Lemma
2.5
modulo the product from the statement. We get the following cases:

•

if
j_{s}=r
, then by the first case of Lemma
2.5
:

\displaystyle\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1},r)}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})=\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r-1}),

so the statement follows from the induction hypothesis,

•

if
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})=(r-s,\ldots,r-1)
, then for
s\leq r-2
all the summands on the right hand side in the formula from the second case of Lemma
2.5
are divisible by
m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r-1}\cdot\alpha_{m_{r},r}(i)
for some
i
. Thus the result follows from Lemma
2.6
. If
s=r-1
then
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})=(1,2,\ldots,r-1)
, so the result again follows since every summand is divisible by
\alpha_{m_{r},r}(i)
for some
i
,

•

the remaining case: similarly as in the previous case, every summand in the formula from the last case of Lemma
2.5
is divisible by
m_{1}\cdots m_{r-1}\cdot\alpha_{m_{r},r}(i)
for some
i
. Hence, the result follows from Lemma
2.6
.

The first part of the statement follows. For the second one let us denote

\displaystyle N:=\prod_{j=1}^{r}m_{j},

Lemma
2.5
implies that for every sequence
(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots)
the following congruences hold:

•

\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1},r)}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r-1})\pmod{N}
,

•

if
j_{1}=r-s
and
s\neq r-1
then

\displaystyle\beta_{(r-s,\ldots,r-1)}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv\alpha_{m_{r},r}(r-1)\beta_{(r-s,\ldots,r-1)}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r-1})\pmod{N},

and if
s=r-1
then

\displaystyle\beta_{(1,\ldots,r-1)}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv\alpha_{m_{r},r}(r-1)\pmod{N},

•

if
j_{1}\neq r-s
and
j_{s}\neq r
then

\displaystyle\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})\equiv\alpha_{m_{r},r}(r-1)\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r-1})\pmod{N}.

Let assume that all the numbers
m_{j}
are equal to some number
m
. Then the result follows quickly in the second and third case from the fact that
\alpha_{m,r}(r-1)=-\frac{1}{2}(r-1)(m-1)m^{r-1}
is divisible by
m^{r-1}/(m,2)
.

Let us now move to the first case. Observe that if
t<s
is such that
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-t},r-t+1,r-t+2,\ldots,r)
and
j_{s-t}\neq r-t
, then

\displaystyle\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r\textrm{ times}})\equiv\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1})}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r-1\textrm{ times}})\equiv\ldots\equiv\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-t})}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r-t\textrm{ times}})\pmod{m^{r-t}}.

By the previously considered cases, the last quantity is divisible by

\displaystyle\frac{m^{(r-t)-(s-t)}}{(m,2)}=\frac{m^{r-s}}{(m,2)},

and so is
\beta_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r\textrm{ times}})
in this case, as we wanted.

The last case that we need to consider is
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})=(r-s+1,\ldots,r)
. Then

\displaystyle\beta_{(r-s+1,\ldots,r)}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r\textrm{ times}})=\beta_{(r-s+1,\ldots,r-1)}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r-1\textrm{ times}})=\ldots=\beta_{(r-s+1)}(\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{r-s+1\textrm{ times}})=0

by the subcase
s=1
of the first case of of Lemma
2.5
. The result follows.
∎

For positive integers
m
and
r
let

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(m,r):=\gcd\{\ \mu(m,j)\ |\ j=1,\ldots,r\ \}=\frac{m}{\gcd\big(m,{\rm lcm}(1,\ldots,r)\big)}.

Lemma 2.8
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the coefficient of
q^{n}
in the series
H_{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})}
is divisible by the product
\prod_{t=1}^{r}\mathcal{M}(m_{t},t)
.

Proof.

We use induction on
r
. For
r=1
and any
m_{1}
we have
H_{(m_{1})}=m_{1}h_{1}
so indeed, all the coefficients of
H_{(m_{1})}
are divisible by
m_{1}
. Assume that if
s\leq r-1
, then for every sequence
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s})
with
n_{i}\geq 2
the series
H_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s})}
is divisible by
\prod_{t=1}^{s}\mathcal{M}(n_{t},t)
. The latter product is divisible by
\prod_{t=1}^{s}\mathcal{M}(m_{j_{t}},j_{t})
, where
n_{t}=m_{j_{t}}
for each
t
. The result follows from Lemmas
2.5
and
2.7
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Because of Lemma
2.8
it is enough to prove the following relation:

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{M}(m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r}n-1)q^{n}=H_{(m_{2},m_{3},\ldots,m_{r})}(q)F_{r}(q),

for
r\geq 2
, where
F_{r}(q):=F_{(m_{r+1},m_{r+2},\ldots)}(q)
. Let us begin with the case of
r=2
(note that we have a string
(m_{2},\ldots,m_{r})
on the right hand side of the above inequality so Lemma
2.8
has to be used with this string instead of
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})
). We apply the operators
U_{m_{1}}
and
U_{m_{2}}
to both sides of the equality
qF_{0}(q)=\frac{q}{1-q}F_{1}(q^{m_{1}})
(note that
F_{0}(q)=F_{M}(q)
and
F_{1}(q)=F_{M^{\prime}}(q)
). We get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{M}(m_{1}m_{2}n-1)q^{n}=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{2}}\circ U_{m_{1}}\left(qF_{0}(q)\right)=U_{m_{2}}\left(U_{m_{1}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}F_{1}(q^{m_{1}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{2}}\left(U_{m_{1}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)F_{1}(q)\right)=U_{m_{2}}\left(U_{m_{1}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)\frac{1}{1-q}F_{2}(q^{m_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{2}}\left(\frac{q}{(1-q)^{2}}F_{2}(q^{m_{2}})\right)=U_{m_{2}}\left(\frac{q}{(1-q)^{2}}\right)F_{2}(q)=H_{(m_{2})}(q)F_{2}(q).

In the above chain of equalities we used the fact that
U_{m}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)=\frac{q}{1-q}
for every
m\geq 2
.

Let us now assume that (
4
) is true for some
r
. We want to prove it for
r+1
. Let us apply the operator
U_{m_{r+1}}
to both sides of (
4
) and get:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{M}(m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{r}m_{r+1}n-1)q^{n}=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{r+1}}\left(H_{(m_{2},\ldots,m_{r})}(q)F_{r}(q)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{r+1}}\left(H_{(m_{2},\ldots,m_{r})}(q)\frac{1}{1-q}F_{r+1}\left(q^{m_{r+1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ U_{m_{r+1}}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}H_{(m_{2},\ldots,m_{r})}(q)\right)F_{r+1}\left(q\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ H_{(m_{2},\ldots,m_{r},m_{r+1})}(q)F_{r+1}\left(q\right).

The proof is finished.
\square

Remark 3.1
.

One may ask what happens if we use the idea from the above proof to find an expression for the generating function of
(p_{M}(m_{1}n-1))_{n=1}^{\infty}
. We would have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{M}(m_{1}n-1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=U_{m_{1}}(qF_{M}(q))=U_{m_{1}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}F_{M^{\prime}}(q^{m_{1}})\right)

\displaystyle=U_{m_{1}}\left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)F_{M^{\prime}}(q)=\frac{q}{1-q}F_{M^{\prime}}(q).

We can now use equality
p_{M}(m_{1}n-1)=p_{M}(m_{1}(n-1))
that comes quickly by comparing the coefficients in the equality
(1-q)F_{M}(q)=F_{M^{\prime}}(q^{m_{1}})
. We get

(5)

\displaystyle(1-q)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{M}(m_{1}(n-1))q^{n}=qF_{M^{\prime}}(q).

After expanding the power series and comparing the coefficients we get that equality (
5
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle p_{M}(m_{1}n)-p_{M}(m_{1}(n-1))=p_{M^{\prime}}(n)

for every
n\geq 1
. However, the same equality follows quickly by comparing the coefficients in the equation
(1-q)F_{M}(q)=F_{M^{\prime}}(q^{m_{1}})
so we achieved a relation that is true but does not give any new information.
