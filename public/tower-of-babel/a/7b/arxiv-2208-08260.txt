Fast convex optimization via time scale and averaging of the steepest descent
Hedy Attouch, Radu Ioan Boţ, Dang-Khoa Nguyen

Abstract
In a Hilbert setting, we develop a gradient-based dynamic approach for fast solving convex optimization problems. By applying time scaling, averaging, and perturbation techniques to the continuous steepest descent (SD), we obtain high-resolution ODEs of the Nesterov and Ravine methods. These dynamics involve asymptotically vanishing viscous damping and Hessian driven damping (either in explicit or implicit form). Mathematical analysis does not require developing a Lyapunov analysis for inertial systems. We simply exploit classical convergence results for (SD) and its external perturbation version, then use tools of differential and integral calculus, including Jensen’s inequality. The method is flexible and by way of illustration we show how it applies starting from other important dynamics in optimization. We consider the case where the initial dynamics is the regularized Newton method, then the case where the starting dynamics is the differential inclusion associated with a convex lower semicontinuous potential, and finally we show that the technique can be naturally extended to the case of a monotone cocoercive operator. Our approach leads to parallel algorithmic results, which we study in the case of fast gradient and proximal algorithms. Our averaging technique shows new links between the Nesterov and Ravine methods.

In a real Hilbert space

2
Time scaling and averaging approach

We propose a new approach to these questions which is based on a combination of time scaling, averaging, and perturbation techniques.
The basis property which underlies our approach is the fact that by time scaling one can always accelerate a dynamic. The kinematic interpretation is clear. By changing the time clock the trajectories are travelled more or less quickly.
The averaging technique shifts from a dynamic of order
n
to a dynamic of the immediately higher order
n+1
. While preserving the asymptotic convergence properties, this makes it possible to achieve a dynamic where the coefficient in front of the gradient is fixed, and therefore suitable for developing an accelerated gradient algorithm.
The perturbation technique gives flexibility to these methods, exploiting the fact that the convergence properties are preserved when adding an external perturbation which vanishes asymptotically sufficiently fast.

As the basic starting dynamic used in this paper, we consider the classical continuous steepest descent

{\rm(SD)}\quad\dot{z}(t)+\nabla f(z(t))=0.

(5)

Under the standing assumption
(\mathcal{A})
on
f
we know that, for any
z_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, there exists a unique classical global solution
z\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([t_{0},+\infty[:{\mathcal{H}})
of (SD) satisfying
z(t_{0})=z_{0}
, see
[
5
, Theorem 17.1.1]
. We fix
t_{0}>0
as the origin of time (because of the singularity at the origin of coefficient
\frac{\alpha}{t}
which enter into our analysis).

2.1
Classical facts concerning the continuous steepest descent

We will have to consider the gradient system with an external perturbation. The following theorem investigates its asymptotic properties under appropriate conditions on the perturbation term. The proof is provided in the Appendix.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that

Let

Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(integral estimate of the velocities)

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert\dot{z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(integral estimate of the gradients)

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert\nabla f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

(integral estimate of the values)

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left(f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)dt<+\infty;

\rm{(iv)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)

f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty;

\rm{(v)}

If

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t^{2}\left\lVert g\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty,

\rm{(vi)}

(convergence of gradients towards zero)
\left\lVert\nabla f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty.

This prevents from using gradient methods to discretize it. Recall that for gradient methods the step size has to be less than or equal to twice the inverse of the Lipschitz constant of the gradient. To overcome this we come with the second step of our method which is averaging.

Several recent papers have been devoted to the role of the averaging techniques in the acceleration of optimization algorithms. In
[
39
]
Nesterov designed an algorithm that makes use of an averaging step for the gradients of the generated sequence. In
[

Scieur, D’Aspremont, and Bach developed a universal acceleration method based on averaging via polynomial techniques. In
[

Poveda and Li developed another universal acceleration method via averaging technique for singularly perturbed hybrid dynamical systems with restarting mechanisms.
Let us explain some simple and, as far as we know, new ideas concerning the averaging methods based on differential equations. The use of differential equations allows us to use differential and integral calculus, which turns out to be a flexible approach.

Closed loop control

The continuous steepest descent system (SD) provides several quantities which are strictly increasing and converge to

Alternatively, we may also consider building

All these choices will lead to closed-loop dynamical systems which have attracted of many researchers recently, see for instance

For trajectory convergence, following the approach of the paper, we do not perform a Lyapunov analysis, but take advantage of the fact that the trajectory

After elementary calculus, we just need to prove that for

\lim_{s\to+\infty}A(s)=0

(

First, let us get rid of the perturbation term.
From

Remark 1

As seen, the time scale and averaging approach is flexible enough to also provide the convergence rate

Remark 2
.

The time scale and averaging technique provides a new approach which allows to transfer the convergence rate of the function value along the trajectory
y(s)
obtained via the time scaling of (SD) to the function value along the trajectory
x(s)
that can be seen as the average of the initial one. This phenomenon can be seen in analogy with the manner the Nesterov method and the Ravine method behave to each other.

Recall that the Nesterov’acceleration gradient
[
38
,
37
]
reads

(\forall k\geq 1)\quad\begin{cases}y_{k}&:=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)\\
x_{k+1}&:=y_{k}-\lambda\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)\end{cases},

(56)

where
x_{0}=x_{1}\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
0<\lambda\leq\frac{1}{L}
and

\alpha_{k}:=\dfrac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}}\quad\textrm{ with }\quad\begin{cases}t_{1}:=1\\
t_{k+1}^{2}-t_{k+1}\leq t_{k}^{2}\quad\forall k\geq 1\end{cases}.

The so-called Ravine sequence
(y_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is the one generated by the Ravine method introduced by Gelfand and Tsetlin
[
35
,
14
]
. We will show that the values of
f
along the Ravine sequence converge fast towards
\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}
and that the convergence rate can be transferred to the values of
f
along
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, which is an average sequence of
(y_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. Indeed, by induction arguments, we have for every
k\geq 1

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}y_{k+1}+\dfrac{\alpha_{k+1}}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}y_{k+1}+\dfrac{\alpha_{k+1}}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}\left(\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}y_{k}+\dfrac{\alpha_{k}}{1+\alpha_{k}}x_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\cdots=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta_{k+1,i}y_{i},

where the nonnegative weights
\left(\theta_{k+1,i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq k+1}
defined by

\theta_{k+1,i}:=\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{k+1-i}\dfrac{\alpha_{k+2-j}}{1+\alpha_{k+1-j}}\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq k\quad\mbox{and}\ \theta_{k+1,k+1}:=\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{k+1}}

fulfill
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta_{k+1,i}=1
.

In the following we give a direct proof for the fast convergence rate of the Ravine method, which can be seen as an alternative approach to
[
14
]
.
Let
z_{*}\in S
. We define for every
k\geq 2
the discrete energy function

E_{k}:=t_{k}^{2}\left(f\left(y_{k-1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f-\lambda\left(1-\dfrac{L\lambda}{2}\right)\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+x_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

Let
k\geq 2
. By the convexity of
f
, it yields

\displaystyle f\left(x_{k}\right)\geq f\left(y_{k}\right)+\left\langle\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right),x_{k}-y_{k}\right\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\geq f\left(y_{k}\right)+\left\langle\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right),z_{*}-y_{k}\right\rangle.

By multiplying the first inequality by
t_{k+1}^{2}-t_{k+1}>0
and the second one by
t_{k+1}>0
and summing the resulting inequalities, it yields

\displaystyle\left(t_{k+1}^{2}-t_{k+1}\right)\left(f\left(x_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)

\displaystyle\geq t_{k+1}^{2}\left(f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)-t_{k+1}\left\langle\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right),\left(t_{k+1}-1\right)\left(y_{k}-x_{k}\right)+y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rangle.

(57)

Since
t_{k+1}y_{k}=t_{k+1}x_{k}+\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)
, by denoting

\displaystyle u_{k}:=\left(t_{k+1}-1\right)\left(y_{k}-x_{k}\right)+y_{k}-z_{*}=t_{k+1}\left(y_{k}-x_{k}\right)+x_{k}-z_{*}=\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+x_{k}-z_{*},

we have

\displaystyle-\lambda t_{k+1}\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)=t_{k+1}\left(x_{k+1}-y_{k}\right)=t_{k+1}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)-\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)=u_{k+1}-u_{k}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\ -t_{k+1}\left\langle\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right),\left(t_{k+1}-1\right)\left(y_{k}-x_{k}\right)+y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \dfrac{1}{\lambda}\left\langle u_{k+1}-u_{k},u_{k}\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left(\left\lVert u_{k+1}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert u_{k+1}-u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left(\left\lVert\left(t_{k+1}-1\right)\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)+x_{k+1}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+x_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda t_{k+1}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

Replacing this expression into (
57
), after some rearrangements we obtain

\displaystyle E_{k+1}\leq\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\left(t_{k}-1\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+x_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\left(t_{k+1}^{2}-t_{k+1}\right)\left(f\left(x_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda\left(1-L\lambda\right)t_{k+1}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

In addition, the Descent Lemma gives

\displaystyle f\left(x_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f

\displaystyle\leq f\left(y_{k-1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f+\left\langle\nabla f\left(y_{k-1}\right),x_{k}-y_{k-1}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-y_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=f\left(y_{k-1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f-\lambda\left(1-\dfrac{L\lambda}{2}\right)\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2},

(58)

which further leads to the inequality

\displaystyle E_{k+1}\leq

\displaystyle\ E_{k}-\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda\left(1-L\lambda\right)t_{k+1}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\ +\left(t_{k+1}^{2}-t_{k+1}-t_{k}^{2}\right)\left(f\left(y_{k-1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f-\lambda\left(1-\dfrac{L\lambda}{2}\right)\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right),

and it also proves that
E_{k}\geq 0
for every
k\geq 2
.
Therefore, when
0<\lambda<\frac{1}{L}
, we can deduce

f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{t_{k+1}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{(k+1)^{2}}\right),

which immediately transfers to

f\left(x_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{t_{k+1}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{1}{(k+1)^{2}}\right).

.
∎

Remark 3
.

As in in Theorem
2
, we see that the convergence of trajectory can be guaranteed for
\alpha>1
, which is less restrictive than for the Su-Boyd-Candés system, the trajectory of which is known to convergence for
\alpha>3
. This is another positive effect of the approach that combines time scaling and averaging.

3.2
Combining implicit and explicit Hessian driven damping

As a starting dynamic we consider the regularized Newton dynamical system

\begin{cases}\lambda\dot{z}(t)+\dot{v}(t)+v(t)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
v(t)=\nabla f(z(t))\end{cases}.

(65)

It is a special case of the regularized Newton dynamic

\lambda(t)\dot{z}(t)+\nabla^{2}f(z(t))\dot{z}(t)+\nabla f(z(t))=0.

(66)

This system has been studied by Attouch and Svaiter
[
22
]
, Attouch, Redont, and Svaiter
[
21
]
, Attouch, Marques Alves, and Svaiter
[
17
]
to solve monotone inclusions (that is, with a general maximally monotone operator
A
instead of
\nabla f
).
In this approach, the central question is the adjustment of the Levenberg-Marquardt regularization parameter
\lambda(\cdot)
in front of the velocity term. Indeed, taking
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\lambda(t)=0
allows to be asymptotically close to the Newton method. Our situation concerns the simpler case
\lambda(t)\equiv\lambda>0
constant, which fits with the convergence properties proved in these papers, and the fact that this dynamic is well-posed. For precise statements concerning the asymptotic behavior of this system see
[
22
, Theorem 4.1, Remark 4.2]
and
[
22
, Theorem 3.9]
. For the sake of completeness, we provide below its convergence properties, that shows some improvements compared to the results in
[
22
]
. The proof of Theorem
5
is provided in the Appendix.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that
f\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
(\mathcal{A})
. Let
z:[t_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of the dynamical system (
65
).
Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(integral estimate of the values)

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left(f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)dt<+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)

f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

(integral estimates of velocities and gradients)

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert\dot{z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty

and

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert\dot{v}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty,
which lead to

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert v\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert\nabla f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty;

\rm{(iv)}

(convergence of velocities and gradients towards zero)
\left\lVert v\left(t\right)\right\rVert=\left\lVert\nabla f\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty,

which lead to

\left\lVert\dot{z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert=\left\lVert\dot{v}\left(t\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty;

\rm{(v)}

the solution trajectory
z(t)
converges weakly as
t\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin f
.

Remark 4
.

In
[
22
, Theorem 4.1]
, the authors show that both
\dot{z}\left(\cdot\right)
and
\dot{v}\left(\cdot\right)
, and consequently
v\left(\cdot\right)
, belong to
\mathbb{L}^{2}\left(\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\right)
. Since
t\mapsto\left\lVert v\left(t\right)\right\rVert
is nonincreasing, in
[
22
, Theorem 3.9]
it is shown that
\left\lVert v\left(t\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)
as
t\to+\infty
. By improving some integral estimates for the velocity and the gradient, we can provide a faster convergence rate, namely of
o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)
as
t\to+\infty
.

Let us make the change of time variable
t=\tau(s)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma s^{2}
in the dynamic (
65
), where
\gamma
is a positive constant to be adjusted later.
Set
y(s):=z(\tau(s))
,
w(s):=v(\tau(s))=\nabla f(y(s))
, and
s_{0}>0
be such that
t_{0}=\tau\left(s_{0}\right)
.
On the one hand, by the derivation chain rule, we have for every
s\geq s_{0}

\dot{y}(s)=\dot{\tau}(s)\dot{z}(\tau(s)),\quad\dot{w}(s)=\dot{\tau}(s)\dot{v}(\tau(s)).

(67)

On the other hand, setting
t=\tau(s)
in (
65
) gives

\begin{cases}\lambda\dot{z}(\tau(s))+\dot{v}(\tau(s))+v(\tau(s))=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
v(\tau(s))=\nabla f(z(\tau(s))).\end{cases}

(68)

According to (
67
), (
68
), and

\dot{\tau}(s)=\gamma s
, we obtain

\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda}{cs}\dot{y}(s)+\frac{1}{\gamma s}\dot{w}(s)+w(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f(y(s))\end{cases}

(69)

or, equivalently,

\begin{cases}\lambda\dot{y}(s)+\dot{w}(s)+\gamma sw(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f(y(s)).\end{cases}

(70)

The convergence rate becomes

f(y(s))-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

(71)

So, we have accelerated the dynamic for the values, passing from the convergence rate
1/t
to
1/s^{2}
.
Moreover, by proceeding as in Subsection
3.1
, we can return from (
70
) to the dynamics (
68
).

Let us now come with the averaging process.
Given
s_{0}>0
, we attach to
y(\cdot)
the new function
x:[s_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
defined by

\dot{x}(s)+\dfrac{\delta}{s}(x(s)-y(s))=0,

(72)

with
x(s_{0})=x_{0}
given in
{\mathcal{H}}
,
and where
\delta
is a positive coefficient to adjust.
Equivalently

y(s)=x(s)+\dfrac{s}{\delta}\dot{x}(s).

(73)

Let us formulate (
70
) in terms of
x
by eliminating
y
.
We first obtain

\begin{cases}\lambda\left(\dot{x}(s)+\frac{s}{\delta}\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{1}{\delta}\dot{x}(s)\right)+\dot{w}(s)+\gamma sw(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\delta}\dot{x}(s)\right).\end{cases}

(74)

After reduction we obtain

\begin{cases}\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\delta+1}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\delta}{\lambda s}\dot{w}(s)+\frac{\delta\gamma}{\lambda}w(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\delta}\dot{x}(s)\right).\end{cases}

(75)

Take
\delta=\alpha-1
,
\gamma=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha-1}
in (
75
). We have
\delta\gamma=\lambda
, which gives

\begin{cases}\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha-1}{\lambda s}\dot{w}(s)+w(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right).\end{cases}

(76)

According to the general properties of the averaging process

y(s)=x(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s),

(77)

which has been already studied, see (
33
), we obtain the following theorem for a dynamics which combines explicit and implicit Hessian driven damping in a new way.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
f\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
(\mathcal{A})
. Let
x:[s_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of

\begin{cases}\ddot{x}(s)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\lambda s}\dot{w}(s)+w(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f\left(x(s)+\dfrac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right).\end{cases}

(78)

Assume that
\alpha>1
. Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(integral estimate of the gradients)
\int_{s_{0}}^{+\infty}s^{3}\left\lVert w\left(s\right)\right\rVert^{2}ds<+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(convergence of gradients towards zero)
\left\lVert w\left(s\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

(convergence of velocities towards zero)
\left\lVert\dot{x}\left(s\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty;

\rm{(iv)}

the solution trajectory
x(s)
converges weakly as
s\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin f
.

If
\alpha>3
, then

\rm{(v)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)
f(x(s))-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

Proof.

Let
x(\cdot)
be a solution trajectory of (
78
) which satisfies the Cauchy data
x(s_{0})=x_{0},\dot{x}(s_{0})=x_{1}
, and
w\left(s_{0}\right):=\nabla f\left(x_{0}+\frac{s_{0}}{\alpha-1}x_{1}\right)
.
In the same way as in the proof of Theorem
2
, we first show that such a solution is reached by considering first the solution
y(\cdot)
of the system

\begin{cases}\lambda\dot{y}(s)+\dot{w}(s)+\frac{\lambda s}{\alpha-1}w(s)=0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
w(s)=\nabla f(y(s))\\
y(s_{0})=x_{0}+\frac{s_{0}}{\alpha-1}x_{1},\end{cases}

(79)

and then

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha-1}{s}(x(s)-y(s))=0\vskip 5.69054pt\\
x(t_{0})=x_{0}.\end{cases}

(80)

Indeed, (
80
) gives
\dot{x}(s_{0})=\frac{\alpha-1}{s_{0}}(y(s_{0})-x(s_{0}))
, which, by the second equation of (
79
), equals to
x_{1}
. We have already seen that
x
can be seen as an averaging process (
80
) of
y
as follows

x(s)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}y(u)\,d\mu_{s}(u)+\xi(s),

(81)

where
\mu_{s}
is the measure on
[s_{0},s]
defined by

\mu_{s}=\frac{s_{0}^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha-1}}\delta_{s_{0}}+(\alpha-1)\frac{u^{\alpha-2}}{s^{\alpha-1}}du,

where
\delta_{s_{0}}
is the Dirac measure at
s_{0}
and

\xi(s):=-\frac{{s_{0}}^{\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)s^{\alpha-1}}x_{1}.

The statements

(
i
)

and

(
ii
)

follow from Theorem
5
after time rescaling and integration by substitution. In order to show

(
iv
)

, we need to pass from the convergence of
y
to that of
x
by using the interpretation of
x
as an average of
y
plus a negligible term. Moreover, we know that
y
converges weakly to an element in
S
thanks to Theorem
5
. The convergence of
x
follows from similar arguments as in Theorem
2
.

Finally, since the averaging process is the same as in Theorem
2
, the convergence rate of
1/s^{2}
for the values

(
v
)

follows by the same arguments.
∎

3.3
General Hessian damping coefficient: extension to bilevel convex optimization

Given
\beta_{0}
a positive damping coefficient, consider the more general form of the dynamic

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\nabla f\left(x(s)+\beta_{0}\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)=0.

(82)

Our study in the previous subsections concerned the case
\beta_{0}=1
. This section shows that the time rescaling and averaging technique links the dynamical system (
82
) with a bilevel convex optimization problem and thus emphasizes the versatility of the approach proposed in this paper.

Let us introduce

y(s):=x(s)+\beta_{0}\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s),

(83)

so that (
82
) is written equivalently

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)=0.

(84)

Let us reformulate (
84
) as a differential equation with
y(s)
as the state variable. According to (
83
) we have

\dot{x}(s)=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}(y(s)-x(s)),

(85)

which after derivation gives

\ddot{x}(s)=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}(\dot{y}(s)-\dot{x}(s))-\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s^{2}}(y(s)-x(s)).

(86)

Combining (
84
) and (
86
) we obtain

\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}(\dot{y}(s)-\dot{x}(s))-\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s^{2}}(y(s)-x(s))+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)=0.

(87)

By replacing
\dot{x}(s)
by its formulation given in (
85
) we obtain

\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}\dot{y}(s)+\left(\frac{\alpha}{s}-\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}\right)\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}(y(s)-x(s))-\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s^{2}}(y(s)-x(s))+\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)=0.

After reduction we get

\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}\dot{y}(s)+\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}(\beta_{0}-1)}{\beta_{0}^{2}s^{2}}(y(s)-x(s))+\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)=0.

(88)

Equivalently

\dot{y}(s)+\dfrac{\beta_{0}s}{\alpha-1}\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)+\frac{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{0}-1)}{\beta_{0}s}(y(s)-x(s))=0.

(89)

Putting together (
85
) and (
89
) we obtain the system

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(s)+\dfrac{\beta_{0}s}{\alpha-1}\nabla f\left(y(s)\right)+\dfrac{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{0}-1)}{\beta_{0}s}(y(s)-x(s))=0\vskip 5.69054pt\\
\dot{x}(s)+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta_{0}s}(x(s)-y(s))=0.\end{cases}

(90)

Let us now consider time scaling of the above system as in (
24
).
Set

s=\sqrt{2(\alpha-1)t}\,\mbox{ and }\,y(\sqrt{2(\alpha-1)t})=Y(t),\,x(\sqrt{2(\alpha-1)t})=X(t).

A similar calculation as in Subsection
2.2
gives

\begin{cases}\dot{Y}(t)+\beta_{0}\nabla f\left(Y(t)\right)+\dfrac{(\alpha-1)(\beta_{0}-1)}{2\beta_{0}t}(Y(t)-X(t))=0\vskip 5.69054pt\\
\dot{X}(t)+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2\beta_{0}t}(X(t)-Y(t))=0.\end{cases}

(91)

This is a perturbation of the steepest descent dynamical system in the product space
{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
. Precisely, setting
Z(t)=(Y(t),X(t))\in{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
, we have

\dot{Z}(t)+\beta_{0}\nabla\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)+\frac{(\alpha-1)}{2\beta_{0}t}A(Z(t))=0,

(92)

where
\Psi(Z)=f(Y)
and
A:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
is the linear operator whose matrix is given by

A=
\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{0}-1&-(\beta_{0}-1)\\
-1&+1\end{pmatrix}

The case
\beta_{0}=2
is of particular interest since then

A
is a symmetric operator which is positive semidefinite. The study of this system then follows from classical results concerning gradient dynamical systems with multiscale aspects, see for example the work of Attouch and Czarnecki
[
13
]
.

The dynamical system (
92
) for
\beta_{0}=2
fits in the dynamics

\dot{Z}(t)+\beta_{0}\nabla\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)+\varepsilon\left(t\right)\nabla\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)=0,

(93)

with
\varepsilon\left(t\right):=\frac{(\alpha-1)}{2\beta_{0}t}
and
\Phi\left(\cdot\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{A}^{2}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{A}
is the seminorm induced by the symmetric and positive semidefinite operator
A
.
The differential equation (
93
) can be seen as a continuous time approach of the bilevel optimization problem

\min_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\Phi\left(Z\right)\ |\ Z\in D:=\argmin_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\Psi\right\}.

(94)

Let
Z_{*}
be a solution of (
94
), meaning that
\Phi\left(Z_{*}\right)=\inf_{D}\Phi
and
\Psi\left(Z_{*}\right)=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\Psi
.
Define for
t\geq t_{0}

\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right):=\beta_{0}\left(\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\Psi\right)+\varepsilon\left(t\right)\left(\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{D}\Phi\right)\geq 0.

Indeed the above quantity is nonnegative because we are in the particular simple case where
\Phi(Y,X)=\frac{1}{2}\|Y-X\|^{2}
is nonnegative,
D=\argmin_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\times{\mathcal{H}}
and hence
\inf_{D}\Phi=0
.
Let us compute

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert Z\left(t\right)-Z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle Z\left(t\right)-Z_{*},\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\right\rangle=-\beta_{0}\left\langle Z\left(t\right)-Z_{*},\nabla\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)\right\rangle-\varepsilon\left(t\right)\left\langle Z\left(t\right)-Z_{*},\nabla\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\beta_{0}\left(\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\Psi\right)-\varepsilon\left(t\right)\left(\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{D}\Phi\right)=-\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right).

This shows that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert Z\left(t\right)-Z_{*}\right\rVert\in{\mathbb{R}}
exists and that

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)dt<+\infty.

This yields
\liminf_{t\rightarrow+\infty}t\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)=0
.
On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}\left\langle\nabla\Psi\left(Z\left(t\right)\right),\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\right\rangle+\varepsilon\left(t\right)\left\langle\nabla\Phi\left(Z\left(t\right)\right),\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\right\rangle+\dot{\varepsilon}\left(t\right)\left(\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf_{D}\Phi\right)\leq-\left\lVert\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

This means that
\Psi_{\Phi}
is decreasing and furthermore

\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(t\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)\right)=\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)+t\dfrac{d}{dt}\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)\leq\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)-t\left\lVert\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

Since
\Psi_{\Phi}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}\left(\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\right)
, we conclude that
t\|\dot{Z}\left(t\right)\|^{2}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}\left(\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\right)
and furthermore
\lim_{t\to+\infty}t\Psi_{\Phi}\left(t\right)=0
.
Consequently,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}t\left(\Psi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}}\Psi\right)=0\textrm{ and }\lim_{t\to+\infty}\left(\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{D}\Phi\right)=0.

Since
\Psi
and
\Phi
are convex and lower semicontinuous, the second condition of Opial’s lemma is fulfilled. Thus
Z(t)
converges weakly to a solution of (
94
) as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

Moreover, according to the definition of
\Phi
and
A
, it holds

\Phi\left(Z(t)\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle Z(t),A\left(Z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert X(t)-Y(t)\right\rVert^{2}=\dfrac{2\beta_{0}^{2}}{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}t^{2}\left\lVert\dot{X}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\rightarrow+\infty.

According to the definitions of
\Psi
and
\Phi

we conclude that there exists
x_{*}\in\argmin_{{\mathcal{H}}}f
such that
(Y(t),X(t))
converges weakly to
(x_{*},x_{*})
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
,

f(Y(t))-\inf\nolimits_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\ \mbox{and}\ \lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\|X(t)-Y(t)\|=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}t\|\dot{X}(t)\|=0.

This means that
x(s)
converges weakly to
x_{*}
as
s\rightarrow+\infty
,

f\left(x(s)+\frac{2s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)-\inf\nolimits_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\ \mbox{and}\ \lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}s\|\dot{x}(s)\|=0.

In the following theorem we collect the convergence properties of the trajectory of the dynamical system (
82
) in case
\beta_{0}=2
.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
f\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
(\mathcal{A})
. Let
x:[s_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\nabla f\left(x(s)+\frac{2s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)=0.

Assume that
\alpha>1
. Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)
f\left(x(s)+\frac{2s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(convergence of velocities towards zero)
\left\lVert\dot{x}\left(s\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

the solution trajectory
x(s)
converges weakly as
s\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin f
.

4
Nonsmooth case

In this section, we show that the approach proposed in this paper extends to the case when
f:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is assumed to be a (possibly nonsmooth) proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function.

4.1
The continuous dynamics

As a basic property of the dynamical system (
31
), the couple of variables
(x,y)
defined in Subsection
2.3
satisfies the following differential system, which involves only first-order derivatives in time and space

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\nabla f(y(s))&=0\\
\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha-1}{s}(x(s)-y(s))&=0.\end{cases}

(95)

This naturally suggests the extension of the above results to the nonsmooth case (replace the gradient of
f
by its subdifferential).
We thus get the differential inclusion system

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\partial f(y(s))&\ni 0\\
\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha-1}{s}(x(s)-y(s))&=0.\end{cases}

(96)

Solving this system gives generalized solutions to the second-order differential inclusion

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\partial f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)\ni 0

(97)

whose direct study raises several difficulties, recalling that
x(s_{0})=x_{0}
and
\dot{x}(s_{0})=x_{1}
.
To extend the results of the previous section to this nonsmooth case, we need to avoid the arguments using the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
.
So, we are led to consider the Cauchy problem for (
97
) with
initial data
x_{0}\in\dom f
and
x_{1}=0
, that is with initial velocity equal to zero.
So doing we have
y({s_{0}})=x({s_{0}})=x_{0}
, which allows to interpret
x
as an average of
y
without correcting term (
\xi=0
with the notations of Subsection 2.6).

The existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to the associated Cauchy problem relies on the equivalent formulation of (
96
) as a perturbation of the generalized steepest descent dynamical system in the product space
{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
. Precisely, define
F:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
, for any
Z=(y,x)\in{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
by

F(Z)=f(y),

and let
G:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
be the operator defined by

G(Z)=(0,x-y).

(98)

Then (
96
) is written equivalently as

\dot{Z}(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\partial F(Z(s))+\frac{\alpha-1}{s}G(Z(s))\ni 0.

(99)

The initial condition becomes
Z(t_{0})=(x_{0},x_{0})
which belongs to

\dom F=\dom f\times{\mathcal{H}}
.
According to the classical results concerning the Lipschitz perturbation of evolution equations governed by subdifferentials of convex functions, see
[
29
, Proposition 3.12]
, we obtain the existence and uniqueness of a global strong solution of the Cauchy problem associated with (
96
).
As a major advantage of the time scaling and averaging techniques, the arguments used in the previous section still work in this more general nonsmooth situation. The rules of differential calculus are still valid for strong solutions, see
[
30
, chapter VIII.2]
, and Jensen’s inequality is still valid for a nonsmooth function
f
(see e.g.
[
25
, Proposition 9.24]
). Indeed Jensen’s inequality is classical for a smooth convex function
f
. Its extension to the nonsmooth convex case can be obtained by first writing it for the Moreau-Yosida regularization
f_{\lambda}
of
f
, then passing to the limit when
\lambda\downarrow 0
. According to the monotone convergence of
f_{\lambda}
towards
f
, we can pass to the limit in the integral term thanks to the Beppo Levi monotone convergence theorem.
Therefore we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Let
f:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex function such that
S=\argmin f\neq\emptyset
.
Let
x:[s_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\partial f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)\ni 0,

(100)

which satisfies the initial conditions
x(s_{0})\in\dom f
and
\dot{x}(s_{0})=0
.
Assume that
\alpha>1
. Then

\rm{(i)}

the solution trajectory
x(s)
converges weakly as
s\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin f
.

If
\alpha>3
, then

\rm{(ii)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)
f\left(y\left(s\right)\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

Remark 5
.

As a consequence of
ii)
, we have that
x(s)
remains in the domain of
f
for all
s\geq t_{0}
. This viability property strongly depends on the fact that the initial position belongs to the domain of
f
, and that the initial velocity has been taken equal to zero.

Remark 6
.

The above theorem is valid in an infinite dimensional setting, which makes it applicable to nonlinear PDEs.
According to the classical theory for the steepest descent dynamic in the nonsmooth convex case, we have that at each
s>s_{0}
the right derivative of

y(s)
exists and satisfies

-\left(\dfrac{dy}{ds}\right)^{+}(s)=\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\left(\partial f(y(s))\right)^{0}

where
\left(\partial f(y(s))\right)^{0}
is the element of minimum norm of
\partial f(y(s))
.
Thus,
y
may exhibit shocks. By contrast,
x
, which is an average of
y
,
has a continuous derivative, hence does not exhibit shocks. Still
x
is not twice differentiable.

4.2
A proximal type algorithm for nonsmooth minimization

Recall the second-order differential inclusion (
97
) associated with (
1
) in Section
4

\ddot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha}{s}\dot{x}(s)+\partial f\left(x(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\dot{x}(s)\right)\ni 0,

(101)

or equivalently formulated as a first order system (see (
96
))

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(s)+\frac{s}{\alpha-1}\partial f(y(s))&\ni 0\\
\dot{x}(s)+\frac{\alpha-1}{s}(x(s)-y(s))&=0.\end{cases}

(102)

We consider the following implicit discretization in time of (
102
), written for every
k\geq 0
as

\begin{cases}y_{k+1}-y_{k}+\frac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}\partial f\left(y_{k+1}\right)&\ni 0\\
x_{k+1}-x_{k}+\frac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}}\left(x_{k}-y_{k+1}\right)&=0.\end{cases}

(103)

This gives rise to the following numerical algorithm

(\forall k\geq 0)\quad\begin{cases}y_{k+1}&:=\prox_{\frac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}f}\left(y_{k}\right)\\
x_{k+1}&:=\left(1-\frac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}}\right)x_{k}+\frac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}}y_{k+1},\end{cases}

(104)

where
y_{0},x_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
are arbitrary initial points. This algorithm has some analogy with the relaxed inertial proximal algorithm for maximally monotone operators in
[
8
]
.

For the step size sequence
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
we impose the following recurrence condition

s_{0}:=0\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad s_{k+1}^{2}-\left(\alpha-1\right)s_{k+1}=s_{k}^{2}\quad\forall k\geq 0,

(105)

which relates to the Nesterov step size rule. Indeed, by denoting
t_{k}:=\frac{s_{k}}{\alpha-1}
, we have for every
k\geq 0

s_{k+1}:=\dfrac{\alpha-1+\sqrt{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}+4s_{k}^{2}}}{2}\ \mbox{or, equivalently,}\ t_{k+1}:=\dfrac{1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}}{2},

which is nothing but the classic step size rule of Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method
[
38
,
37
,
24
]
.

We will nevertheless continue to work with the sequence
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
in order to emphasize that all the convergence statements provided in this section are valid for
\alpha>1
, contrary to the stronger condition
\alpha>3
for the low resolution case (see Remark
1
).
We have that
s_{k}\sim k
, precisely,
s_{k}\geq\left(k+1\right)\left(\alpha-1\right)/2
for every
k\geq 0
(cf.
[
24
, Lemma 4.3]
).
Furthermore, by a telescoping sum argument, we have from (
105
)

s_{k+1}^{2}-\left(\alpha-1\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i+1}=s_{0}^{2}.

Since
s_{0}=0
, we can conclude that for every
k\geq 0

s_{k}^{2}=\left(\alpha-1\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}.

(106)

Let us also notice that (
104
) can be written only in terms of the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}

x_{k+1}-x_{k}-\dfrac{s_{k}-\left(\alpha-1\right)}{s_{k+1}}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+\partial f\left(x_{k}+\dfrac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)\right)\ni 0\quad\forall k\geq 1,

which can be seen as a direct discretization of (
101
).

In the following we first analyze the convergence properties of the sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
generated by (
104
). Then, we transfer these to the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. One can easily observe the similarity between this approach and the continuous time one, where we transferred the converge properties of the trajectory
y(\cdot)
generated by the continuous steepest descent system after time scaling to the averaged trajectory
x(\cdot)
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by (
104
).
The following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(summability of function values)
\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}\left(f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)<+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(summability of subgradients)
there exists a sequence
\left(\eta_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\eta_{k}\in\partial f\left(y_{k}\right)
for every
k\geq 0
and

\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}^{2}\left\lVert\eta_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

<+\infty.

In particular, if
f
is differentiable, then
\left\lVert\nabla f\left(y_{k}\right)\right\rVert={o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}^{2}}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{k\sqrt{k}}\right)}\textrm{ as }k\to+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

(convergence of values towards minimal value)
f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{s_{k}^{2}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{\left(k+1\right)^{2}}\right)
as
k\to+\infty;

\rm{(iv)}

the sequence of iterates
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly as
k\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin_{{\mathcal{H}}}f
.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 0
be fixed. Take
z_{*}\in S=\argmin f
. According to (
104
) there exists
\eta_{k+1}\in\partial f\left(y_{k+1}\right)
such that
y_{k+1}-y_{k}+\frac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}\eta_{k+1}=0
. By convexity of
f
we deduce that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert y_{k+1}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle y_{k+1}-z_{*},y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}-\dfrac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}\left\langle y_{k+1}-z_{*},\eta_{k+1}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{s_{k+1}^{2}}{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left\lVert\eta_{k+1}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert^{2}-\dfrac{s_{k+1}}{\alpha-1}\left(f\left(y_{k+1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{s_{k+1}^{2}}{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left\lVert\eta_{k+1}\right\rVert^{2}.

(107)

The statements
i)
and
ii)
follow from
[
23
, Lemma 5.31]
. In addition, the limit
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\lVert y_{k}-z_{*}\right\rVert\in{\mathbb{R}}
exists, which means that the first condition of the discrete Opial’s lemma is fulfilled.

The convergence rate in
ii)
follows from the fact that sequence
\left(\eta_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
is nonincreasing. Indeed, we have from the monotonicity of
\partial f

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{k}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\eta_{k+1}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\left\langle\eta_{k+1},\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}\right\rangle+\left\lVert\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}\right\rVert^{2}=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}}\left\langle y_{k+1}-y_{k},\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}\right\rangle+\left\lVert\eta_{k+1}-\eta_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0,

and the rate follows from
[
7
, Lemma 22]
. Similarly, we notice that

the sequence
\left(f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)_{k\geq 0}
is nonincreasing as well. Precisely, for every
k\geq 0
we have

\left(f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)-\left(f\left(y_{k+1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)\geq\langle\eta_{k+1},y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}}\left\lVert y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

According to
[
7
, Lemma 22]
we get

f\left(y_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=o\left(\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}}\right),

which proves
iii)
, thanks to (
106
).
Finally, since
\lim_{k\to+\infty}f\left(y_{k}\right)=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f
and
f
is convex and lower semicontinuous, the second condition of Opial’s lemma is also fulfilled. This gives the weak convergence of the sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
to an element in
S=\argmin f
.
∎

Let us consider the convergence properties of
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
which are associated with the averaging process.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by (
104
).
The following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(convergence of values)
f\left(x_{k}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{s_{k}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left(k+1\right)^{2}}\right)
as
k\to+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

the sequence of iterates
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly as
k\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\argmin_{{\mathcal{H}}}f
.

Proof.

We begin by interpreting
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
as an average of
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. Multiplying the second equation in (
104
) by
s_{k+1}^{2}
and using (
105
), we obtain for every
k\geq 0

s_{k+1}^{2}x_{k+1}=\left(s_{k+1}^{2}-\left(\alpha-1\right)s_{k+1}\right)x_{k}+\left(\alpha-1\right)s_{k+1}y_{k+1}=s_{k}^{2}x_{k}+\left(\alpha-1\right)s_{k+1}y_{k+1}.

By a telescopic sum argument and using (
106
), we obtain from here the following expression for each
k\geq 0

x_{k+1}=\dfrac{1}{s_{k+1}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}\left(\alpha-1\right)s_{i}y_{i}=\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}y_{i}.

Then, Jensen’s inequality gives for every
k\geq 0

\displaystyle f\left(x_{k+1}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f

\displaystyle=\left(f-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)\left(\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}\left(f\left(y_{i}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)\leq\dfrac{\alpha-1}{s_{k+1}^{2}}\sum_{i\geq 0}s_{i}\left(f\left(y_{i}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right),

(108)

where in the last equation we use (
106
). In Theorem
9
we have shown
\sum_{i\geq 0}s_{i}\left(f\left(y_{i}\right)-\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}}f\right)<+\infty
, which gives the announced convergence rates. The fact that
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly to an element in
S=\argmin f
as
k\to+\infty
follows from the fact that convergence entails ergodic convergence.
∎

5
Operator case

What underlies our study is the first-order in time evolution system taken as a starting point and its convergence properties. In this regard, it is natural to consider also the evolution equation governed by a cocoercive operator. Indeed, gradients of convex functions and cocoercive operators are the two basic examples of monotone operators ensuring the convergence of the associated semi-groups of contractions.
We will successively examine a general cocoercive operator, then the additively structured case.
For monotone operators, the convergence rates are expressed in terms of velocities and residuals.

5.1
General cocoercive case

Recall that
a single-valued operator
M:{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
is called
\rho
-cocoercive (with
\rho>0
) if

\langle M(x)-M(y),x-y\rangle\geq\rho\left\|{M(x)-M(y)}\right\|^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}.

We mention the following classical properties of cocoercive operators:

a)
If
M
is
\rho
-cocoercive, then it is monotone and
\frac{1}{\rho}
-Lipschitz continuous, and hence maximally monotone.

b)
If
f
is a convex differentiable function whose gradient is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then the operator
\nabla f
is
1/L
-cocoercive (Baillon-Haddad theorem).

Let us come to our study. Given
M\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
a
\rho
-cocoercive operator, consider the monotone equation

M\left(x\right)=0,

(109)

and the associated evolution equation

\dot{z}\left(t\right)+M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)=0.

(110)

We denote the solution set of (
109
) by
S
, which we assume to be nonempty.
We recall the results in
[
25
, Theorems 11 and 16]
which state that
z\left(t\right)
converges weakly to a point in
S
. In addition

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left\lVert\dot{z}\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left\lVert M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty\textrm{ and }\left\lVert M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty.

(111)

Further we develop an analysis similar to the one in Subsection
3.1
in the case of a gradient operator.
Set
y\left(s\right):=z\left(\tau\left(s\right)\right)
, where
\tau\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a continuously differentiable increasing function satisfying
\lim_{s\to+\infty}\tau\left(s\right)=+\infty
.
According to the derivation chain rule, we have

\dot{y}\left(s\right)=\dot{\tau}\left(s\right)\dot{z}\left(\tau\left(s\right)\right)

which, by setting
t:=\tau\left(s\right)
in (
110
), gives

\dot{z}\left(\tau\left(s\right)\right)+M\left(z\left(\tau\left(s\right)\right)\right)=0.

Combining the two relations above gives

\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dot{\tau}\left(s\right)M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=0.

(112)

Taking the derivative with respect to
s
of the above equation gives

\ddot{y}\left(s\right)+\ddot{\tau}\left(s\right)M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)+\dot{\tau}\left(s\right)\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)=0.

(113)

Multiplying both sides of (
112
) by
\frac{\alpha}{s}
, then adding the result to (
113
), we obtain

\ddot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dot{\tau}\left(s\right)\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)+\left(\ddot{\tau}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{\tau}\left(s\right)\right)M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=0.

(114)

As a subsequent result of (
111
) we have

\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\tau\left(s\right)}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

Taking

\tau\left(s\right)=\dfrac{s^{2}}{2\left(\alpha+1\right)},

then (
114
) becomes

\ddot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{s}{\alpha+1}\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)+M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=0.

(115)

We obtain the rate

\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{s}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

Moreover, it holds

\int_{\tau\left(t_{0}\right)}^{+\infty}s\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}ds<+\infty.

Now let us do the reverse, and start from a solution trajectory
y(\cdot)
of (
114
) satisfying
y\left({s_{0}}\right):=y_{0}
and
\dot{y}\left({s_{0}}\right):=y_{1}
. Using similar arguments as in Theorem
4
, we multiply both sides of (
115
) by
s^{\alpha}>0
and get

\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(s^{\alpha}\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{s^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)=0.

This leads to

\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{s}{\alpha+1}M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=\dfrac{c_{0}}{s^{\alpha}},

(116)

where
c_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
is a constant that can be determined from the Cauchy condition.
Set
z\left(t\right):=y\left(\sigma\left(t\right)\right)
, where

\sigma\left(t\right)=\sqrt{2\left(\alpha+1\right)t}.

(117)

After calculations similar to those made previously, we arrive at

\dot{z}\left(t\right)+M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)=\dfrac{c}{t^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}},

where
c\in{\mathcal{H}}
is a constant.
Our study therefore falls within the properties of the perturbed system

\dot{z}\left(t\right)+M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)=g(t),

(118)

where, as
\alpha>1
, the external perturbation
g\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\to{\mathcal{H}},g(t)=\frac{c}{t^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}}
, is such that

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left\lVert g\left(t\right)\right\rVert dt<+\infty\,\textrm{ and }\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}t\left\lVert g\left(t\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty.

(119)

We have the following result.

Theorem 11
.

Let
z\colon\left[t_{0},+\infty\right[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of

\dot{z}\left(t\right)+M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)=g(t),

(120)

where
g
fulfils (
119
). Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(convergence rate of the operator norm)
\left\lVert M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\textrm{ as }t\to+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(integral estimate of the operator norm)
\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\left\lVert M\left(z\left(t\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}dt<+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

the solution trajectory
z(t)
converges weakly as
t\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\Zer M
.

Combining the above theorem with the time scaling defined in (
117
) gives the following result.

Theorem 12
.

Let
M:{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
be a cocoercive operator and

y:[s_{0},+\infty[\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution trajectory of

\ddot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{s}{\alpha+1}\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)+M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=0,

(121)

where
\alpha>1
. Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

(convergence rate of the operator norm)
\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty;

\rm{(ii)}

(integral estimate of the gradients)
\int_{s_{0}}^{+\infty}s\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}ds<+\infty;

\rm{(iii)}

the solution trajectory
y(s)
converges weakly as
s\to+\infty
, and its limit belongs to
S=\Zer M
.

Remark 7
.

Theorem
12
is valid for an arbitrary cocoercive operator. By contrast, the corresponding result without the Newton correction term requires that the coefficient of the cocoercive operator asymptotically tends to infinity, see Attouch and Peypouquet
[
18
]
.

Remark 8
.

Recently, in
[
27
]
the closely related dynamics

\ddot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{s}\dot{y}\left(s\right)+\dfrac{2s}{\alpha+1}\dfrac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)+M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)=0

(122)

has been considered. Compared to (
115
), the difference lies in the coefficient of
\frac{d}{ds}\left(M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right)
.
According to
[
27
, Theorem 4 and Theorem 7]
, the solution trajectory of (
122
) verifies

\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}s^{3}\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}ds<+\infty,\,\mbox{ and }\,\left\lVert M\left(y\left(s\right)\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{s^{2}}\right)\textrm{ as }s\to+\infty.

It would be interesting to know if we can achieve the same convergence rate using time scaling and perturbation techniques.

5.2
Additively structured monotone inclusions

Our interest now is to solve the structured monotone inclusion problem

0\in A(x)+B(x),

where
A
is maximally monotone, and
B
is cocoercive with
(A+B)^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
.
A favorable situation occurs when one can compute the resolvent operator of
A

J_{\mu A}=(I+\mu A)^{-1},\quad\mu>0.

6
Conclusion and perspectives

Our study has shed new light on the acceleration of gradient methods for general convex differentiable optimization.
Based on a dynamical approach, and using relatively elementary tools, namely time scaling and time averaging, we were able to introduce a large class of damped inertial dynamics with proven fast convergence rates.
The method is very flexible and can be adapted to various initial start-up dynamics. We paid particular attention to the steepest continuous descent and the regularized Newton method. Our approach confirms the important role played by the Hessian driven damping role in these questions.
Our results open the door to a systematic study of the corresponding fast proximal and gradient methods.
Importantly, our approach also works when we take a cocoercive operator instead of a gradient operator. Applications to inertial forward-backward methods, ADMM, can therefore be expected, to cite some of the most popular algorithms for convex structured optimization.
Application to stochastic differential equations can be considered as well.
