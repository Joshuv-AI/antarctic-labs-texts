Generalized Adiabatic Theorem and Strong-Coupling Limits
Daniel Burgarth, Paolo Facchi, Hiromichi Nakazato, Saverio Pascazio, Kazuya Yuasa

Abstract
We generalize Kato’s adiabatic theorem to nonunitary dynamics with an isospectral generator. This enables us to unify two strong-coupling limits: one driven by fast oscillations under a Hamiltonian, and the other driven by strong damping under a Lindbladian. We discuss the case where both mechanisms are present and provide nonperturbative error bounds. We also analyze the links with the quantum Zeno effect and dynamics.



1
Introduction

Strong coupling and adiabatic decoupling are effective tools towards quantum control strategies, whose primary objective is to preserve the coherence of quantum mechanical systems
[
1
]
.
These control procedures are manifestations of the quantum Zeno effect (QZE)
[
2
]
and its extension, known as the quantum Zeno dynamics (QZD)
[
3
]
. They all represent robust tools for quantum control and lead to essentially the same physics. For instance, instead of repeated projective measurements, one might consider the application of strong fields or strong damping to induce the QZE and hinder part of the evolution.

Yet, from a mathematical perspective the proof techniques used are often very different, and it is sometimes hard to understand the relationships between the various manifestations. In particular, it is not possible to describe situations in which several mechanisms are present simultaneously. This point is however important, because physical systems are often not “clean” enough to focus on only one type of quantum dynamics. Our principal motivation is therefore to develop a common mathematical framework unifying such physics.

To this end, we shall focus on the “continuous” formulation of the QZE, in which the system to be studied is strongly coupled to an external field or to a reservoir that induces (fast) dissipation.
The case of strong fields was proven by making use of the adiabatic theorem for unitary dynamics
[
3
]
, and, alternatively, with a unitary perturbation theory
[
4
]
. Averages over fastly oscillating terms are also commonly found in other areas of mathematical physics, in particular in deriving effective master equations for open quantum systems in the weak-coupling limit
[
5
]
.
The case of strong damping was analyzed using dissipative perturbation theory
[
6
,
7
]
. While the proofs for the two cases are clearly related, it is difficult to see the precise connection. Here, we shall develop a suitable extension of Kato’s adiabatic theorem
[
8
]
, by relaxing the condition of the unitarity of the evolution. This extension allows us to unify and simplify the proof of the continuous QZE, and to describe also the situation in which both field and dissipation are present at the same time. We can furthermore provide nonperturbative error bounds for both cases.
Since the physical mechanisms at the basis of QZD have been proven in a number of recent experiments
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, our results might be relevant also in the light of possible future experimental implementations.

The main objective of the present article is to unify and at the same time generalize the above-mentioned continuous manifestations of the QZD in two different but inter-related formulations: (i) strong continuous coupling/fast oscillations and (ii) strong damping. With a slight abuse of language both situations are often referred to as “strong coupling”.
We shall prove three theorems. The first one is an adiabatic theorem (Theorem

[
8

, by relaxing the condition of the unitarity of the evolution.
The unified framework will then enable us to study the QZD in which the two different mechanisms (i) and (ii) are present at the same time (Theorem

We shall also make some observations on the control of nonunitary dynamics by continuous QZD, including a no-go theorem for the cancellation of any Markovian decay (Theorem

3
Unification and Generalization of the Continuous QZDs

The main result of this article is the unification and the generalization of the continuous QZDs, via (i) strong continuous coupling/fast oscillations and (ii) strong damping.
Notice that both evolutions (
2.4
) and (
2.6
)
are of the form
\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}
, and one is interested in their behavior for large values of the coupling
\gamma
, i.e.
\gamma\to+\infty
.
As noted in Refs.
[
3
,
4
]
, this is nothing but an adiabatic limit. Indeed, by going in the
C
interaction picture,

B(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-tC}B\mathrm{e}^{tC},

(3.1)

the evolution
V_{\gamma}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-tC}\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}
is the solution of the equation

\dot{V}_{\gamma}(t)=\gamma B(t)V_{\gamma}(t),

(3.2)

with
V_{\gamma}(0)=1
. This has exactly the same form as an adiabatic evolution

\dot{U}_{T}(s)=TA(s)U_{T}(s)

[
8
,
32
]
, with the physical time and the large-coupling limit
\gamma\to+\infty
playing the role of the scaled time
s=t/T
and the large-time limit
T\to+\infty
, respectively.

Therefore, one is led to prove an extension to semigroups of the adiabatic theorem by Kato
[
8
]
, who only considered unitary evolutions, i.e. the case where
B
is skew-Hermitian (Hamiltonian multiplied by the imaginary unit).
Although this is a substantial generalization in terms of the generators allowed—including arbitrary matrices—it is rather specific in terms of their time-dependence, which is assumed to be isospectral, since

\mathop{\mathrm{spec}}(B(t))=\mathop{\mathrm{spec}}(B)
. This distinguishes our work from other adiabatic theorems for open systems (e.g.
[
33
,
34
]
).

It is worth noticing an interesting connection between the adiabatic theorem and the spectral averaging by isometries introduced by Davies
[
5
,
35
]
in his derivation of the master equation as a weak-coupling limit.
This connection is somehow known in quantum optics, where, in the delicate derivation on physical grounds of the correct master equation for a small system interacting with a reservoir, one needs to resort to an adiabatic secular approximation
[
36
, Sec. IV.B]
.
The spectral averaging, also known as Davies’ device
[
37
, Sec. XVII.6]
, is in fact a rigorous way to implement the secular approximation.

In his derivation Davies uses a fixed-point theorem, while here we find it more convenient to follow a proof technique introduced by Kato
[
8
]
, that makes use of the reduced resolvent, and is more suitable to generalization to nonunitary evolutions.

We now state our results.
A few notions on quantum semigroups, which are useful for our analysis, are summarized in Appendix
A
, where we also review a result obtained in Ref.

Theorem 1

(Generalized adiabatic theorem)
.

Let
B
and
C
be operators on a finite-dimensional space. Assume that
\mathrm{e}^{tB}
is bounded for
t\geq 0
(which is equivalent to requiring the spectrum of
B
to be confined in the closed left half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{-}
, whose purely imaginary eigenvalues are semisimple), while
C
can be an arbitrary matrix.
Let
\{b_{k}\}
be the spectrum of
B
and
\{P_{k}\}
its spectral projections. Define

C_{Z}=\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}P_{k}CP_{k},\qquad P_{\varphi}=\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}P_{k},

(3.3)

the Zeno projection of
C
and the peripheral projection of
B
(spectral projection on the purely imaginary eigenvalues of
B
), respectively.
Then, we have

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)=\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(3.4)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
[0,+\infty)
. Moreover, we have

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}P_{\varphi}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)=\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}P_{\varphi}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(3.5)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
(0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
. ∎

Remark 5
.

We also remark that an alternative geometric approach developed in Ref.
[
38
]
allows for a perturbative Lindbladian description of QZD.

The QZE by fast oscillations (i), however, cannot cancel any decay due to a GKLS generator.
This no-go theorem can be proven with the help of the following proposition, generalizing an argument given in Ref.
[
39
]
.

We first need the following decomposition.
Let
\mathcal{L}
be an arbitrary GKLS generator. It can always be decomposed as

\mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{D},

(3.28)

where
-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}=-\mathrm{i}[H,\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,]
is its Hamiltonian part and

\mathcal{D}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,+\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}-2L_{i}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,L_{i}^{\dagger})

(3.29)

is its dissipative/dephasing part, with the operators
L_{i}
’s assumed to be traceless,

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits L_{i}=0,\quad\forall i.

(3.30)

This decomposition is always possible: if
L_{i}
’s are not traceless, their scalar parts can always be absorbed by the Hamiltonian
H
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{D}
be an arbitrary GKLS generator,
decomposed as above.
Consider the evolution
\rho(t)=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)
of a state
\rho
, where
\mathcal{E}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
and
t\geq 0
, and examine the evolution of its purity
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)
.
Then, the largest decay rate of the purity,

\Gamma=\sup_{\rho,t}\!\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)\right),

(3.31)

is independent of the Hamiltonian
H
. Furthermore,

\Gamma=0\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathcal{D}=0.

(3.32)

Proof.

By noting the decomposition of
\mathcal{L}
into the Hamiltonian
-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}=-\mathrm{i}[H,\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,]
and the dissipative/dephasing
\mathcal{D}
parts, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\!\left(\rho(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho(t)\right)=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\!\left[\rho(t)\,\Bigl(-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho(t)]+\mathcal{D}(\rho(t))\Bigr)\right]=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits[\rho(t)\mathcal{D}(\rho(t))],

(3.33)

which is independent of
H
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{D}=0
, we have
\Gamma=0
.

On the other hand, notice that

\sup_{\rho,t}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)\right)=\sup_{\rho}\left(\left.-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)\right|_{t=0}\right),

(3.34)

because we can always choose
\rho(t)
as an initial state.
Thus, we have

\Gamma=\sup_{\rho}\!\left(\left.-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho^{2}(t)\right|_{t=0}\right)=2\sup_{\rho}\Bigl(-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits[\rho\mathcal{D}(\rho)]\Bigr)=2\sup_{\rho}\sum_{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\rho^{2}-L_{i}^{\dagger}\rho L_{i}\rho).

(3.35)

We can bound
\Gamma
from below as

\displaystyle\Gamma=2\sup_{\rho}\sum_{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\rho^{2}-L_{i}^{\dagger}\rho L_{i}\rho)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sup_{\rho=|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|}\sum_{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}\rho^{2}-L_{i}^{\dagger}\rho L_{i}\rho)

\displaystyle=2\sup_{|{\psi}\rangle}\sum_{i}\Bigl(\langle{\psi}|L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}|{\psi}\rangle-\langle{\psi}|L_{i}^{\dagger}|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|L_{i}|{\psi}\rangle\Bigr)\geq 0,

(3.36)

where in the last inequality we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
If
\Gamma=0
, then the equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality should hold.
It holds iff
L_{i}|{\psi}\rangle\propto|{\psi}\rangle
, for all
i
and
|{\psi}\rangle
.
All vectors
|{\psi}\rangle
are eigenvectors of
L_{i}
.
This implies that
L_{i}=\lambda_{i}I
with some complex value
\lambda_{i}
for all
i
.
But, from the assumption (
3.30
) we have that
\lambda_{i}=0
, that is
L_{i}=0
for all
i
, and hence
\mathcal{D}=0
.
∎

5
Conclusions

We have generalized Kato’s adiabatic theorem to a particular type of nonunitary dynamics. This allowed us to treat two very different physical strong-coupling limits, strong oscillations and strong damping, on equal mathematical footing, and to generalize previous results to the situation where both effects are present simultaneously. Both limits provide various Zeno-type generators, including Zeno Hamiltonians and Zeno Lindbladians.

The adiabatic theorem allows us to provide explicit bounds on the convergence which depend on various parameters, in particular the oscillation gap and the dissipative gap of the strong generator. This demonstrates nicely both effects.

A fantastic experiment to which our generalized result is related is provided by Ref.
[
40
]
. There, we have a three-level system
\{|D\rangle,|G\rangle,|B\rangle\}
with strong driving
\Omega_{BG}\gg\Omega_{DG}
between
|B\rangle
and
|G\rangle

and
strong damping
\Gamma\gg\Omega_{DG}
on level
|B\rangle
. What is however interesting there is that
\Gamma\gg\Omega_{BG}
. Such multi-scale strong coupling could be therefore an interesting extension of our result in future studies.

Appendix A
Spectral Properties of Quantum Semigroups

We recall here a few properties of quantum semigroups that are useful for our analysis.
Every linear operator
A
on a finite-dimensional space can be expressed (essentially) uniquely in terms of its Jordan normal form (
canonical form
or
spectral representation
)
[
41
]
:

A=\sum_{k}(\lambda_{k}P_{k}+N_{k}),

(A.1)

where
\{\lambda_{k}\}
, the
spectrum
of
A
, is the set of distinct eigenvalues of
A
(
\lambda_{k}\neq\lambda_{\ell}
for
k\neq\ell
),
\{P_{k}\}
, the
spectral projections
of
A
, are the corresponding eigenprojections, satisfying

P_{k}P_{\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}P_{k},\qquad\sum_{j}P_{j}=I,

(A.2)

for all
k
and
\ell
, and
\{N_{k}\}
are the corresponding
nilpotents
of
A
, satisfying for all
k
and
\ell

P_{k}N_{\ell}=N_{\ell}P_{k}=\delta_{k\ell}N_{k},\qquad N_{k}^{n_{k}}=0,

(A.3)

for some integer
1\leq n_{k}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\nolimits P_{k}
.

Notice that the spectral projections, which determine a partition of the space through the
resolution of identity
(
A.2
), are not Hermitian in general,
P_{k}\neq P_{k}^{\dagger}
. An eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
A
is called
semisimple
or
diagonalizable
if the corresponding nilpotent
N_{k}
is zero (equivalently,
n_{k}=1
). The operator
A
is diagonalizable if and only if all its eigenvalues are semisimple.

In the following, we state without proofs some properties
of GKLS generators
\mathcal{L}
, whose exponential
\mathcal{E}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
is a CPTP semigroup for
t\geq 0
, i.e.
\mathcal{E}_{t}\circ\mathcal{E}_{s}=\mathcal{E}_{t+s}
for
t,s\geq 0
.
For further details and proofs, see e.g. Ref.
[
42
]
, in particular Propositions 6.1–6.3 and Theorem 6.1 therein.
See also Refs.
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
28
,
46
]
for the recent studies on the structure of GKLS generators, in particular on their steady states.

Proposition 2

(Spectral properties of GKLS generators)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator on a finite-dimensional space. Then, the following properties hold:

(i)

The spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{L}
is contained in the closed left half-plane

\mathbb{C}_{-}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0\}
, and
\lambda=0
is an eigenvalue. Moreover, all the “peripheral” eigenvalues, belonging to the boundary of
\mathbb{C}_{-}
, i.e. on the imaginary axis
\partial\mathbb{C}_{-}=\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\lambda=0\}
, are semisimple.

(ii)

The canonical form of
\mathcal{L}

reads

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}+\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\lambda_{k}<0}(\lambda_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}+\mathcal{N}_{k}),

(A.4)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}=\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\lambda_{k}=0}\lambda_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}

(A.5)

is the “peripheral” part of
\mathcal{L}
, and
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathcal{N}_{k}\}
are the spectral projections and the nilpotents of
\mathcal{L}
, respectively.

(iii)

The projection onto the peripheral spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
,

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\lambda_{k}=0}\mathcal{P}_{k},

(A.6)

is CPTP, and
\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{L}
.
The projection
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is also the projection onto the peripheral spectrum (consisting of the eigenvalues of unit magnitude) of the CPTP map
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
for any
t>0
.
The part of
\mathcal{L}
corresponding to the eigenvalues with negative real parts describes decay.

(iv)

The peripheral map
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L_{\varphi}}}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is CPTP for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

In the particular case of a GKLS generator of a unitary evolution
\mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{K}=-\mathrm{i}[K,\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,]
, where
K
is Hermitian, all eigenvalues are imaginary and the operator reduces to its peripheral part, so that
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=1
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\varphi}
, and the generator is diagonalizable.
In such a case the spectrum and the spectral projections of
\mathcal{K}
can be expressed in terms of the spectrum and of the spectral projections of
K
, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{K}=-\mathrm{i}[K,\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,]
be a GKLS generator corresponding to a Hamiltonian
K
. Let

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k},\qquad K=\sum_{n}\varepsilon_{n}P_{n}

(A.7)

be the spectral representations of
\mathcal{K}
and
K
, respectively, with
\omega_{0}=0
. Then,
the spectrum of
\mathcal{K}
is the set of the transition frequencies

\{\omega_{k}\}
determined by the energies
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}
, that is, for every
k
there exists
a pair
(m,n)
such that

\omega_{k}=\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n},

(A.8)

and the corresponding spectral projections are given by

\mathcal{P}_{k}=\sum_{m,n}\delta_{\omega_{k},\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}P_{m}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{n},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{0}=\sum_{n}P_{n}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{n}.

(A.9)

Proof.

The second relation in Eq. (
A.9
) is a direct consequence of the first one and of the convention
\omega_{0}=0
. The spectrum of
\mathcal{K}
and the first identity in Eq. (
A.9
)
are obtained by the following computation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=[K,\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,]

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\sum_{n}\varepsilon_{n}P_{n},\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}(\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n})P_{m}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}(\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n})\sum_{k}\delta_{\omega_{k},\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}P_{m}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\sum_{m,n}\delta_{\omega_{k},\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n}}P_{m}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k},

(A.10)

which completes the proof. This is actually a simpler proof of a result presented in Ref.
[
16
]
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of the Generalized Adiabatic Theorem

Here we prove the generalized adiabatic theorem (Theorem
1
) stated in Sec.
3
.
The proof is an extension to semigroups of the adiabatic theorem by Kato
[
8
]
, who only considered unitary evolutions. The noteworthy difference and the novelty of this proof are the bound on the semigroup
\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}
.
We will see that the diagonalizability (semisimpleness) of the purely imaginary spectrum plays a crucial role.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let

B=\sum_{k}(b_{k}P_{k}+N_{k})

(B.1)

be the spectral representation of
B
, where
\{b_{k}\}
is its spectrum,
\{P_{k}\}
its spectral projections, and
\{N_{k}\}
its nilpotents. By assumption,
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{k}\leq 0
and the purely imaginary eigenvalues are semisimple (the corresponding nilpotents are zero).

We consider separately the contributions of the peripheral eigenvalues and of the nonperipheral ones.

Step 1. Purely imaginary eigenvalues:

We first focus on a specific purely imaginary eigenvalue
b_{\ell}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
of
B
.
It is semisimple, so that

(B-b_{\ell}I)P_{\ell}=0.

(B.2)

Let us define the reduced resolvent at
b_{\ell}
by

S_{\ell}=\sum_{k\neq\ell}[(b_{k}-b_{\ell})I+N_{k}]^{-1}P_{k}.

(B.3)

Then, we have

(B-b_{\ell}I)S_{\ell}=I-P_{\ell}.

(B.4)

The relation (
B.4
)
will play an important role later.

We are going to prove the adiabatic limit

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}P_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.5)

uniformly for
t\in[0,t_{2}]
with
t_{2}>0
.
We start by noting

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}-\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma B+C_{Z})})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(C-C_{Z})\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma B+C_{Z})},

(B.6)

which multiplied by
P_{\ell}
reads

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}-\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}P_{\ell}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(I-P_{\ell})CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}.

(B.7)

We wish to prove that the integral in Eq. (
B.7
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)
—even if the integrand is
\mathcal{O}(1)
—due to its fast oscillatory behavior, e.g. like

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\gamma}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\gamma})=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\gamma}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t\gamma}-1).

(B.8)

By using Eq. (
B.4
), one can rewrite the integrand in Eq. (
B.7
) as

\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(I-P_{\ell})CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}=\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(B-b_{\ell}I)S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}.

(B.9)

Since

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)})=-\gamma\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(B-b_{\ell}I)\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)},

(B.10)

one has

\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(B-b_{\ell}I)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)})\right)\mathrm{e}^{-s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)},

(B.11)

and hence, the integrand (
B.9
) is further rewritten as

\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}(I-P_{\ell})CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)})\right)\mathrm{e}^{-sC}S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{sC_{Z}}.

(B.12)

Therefore, the integral in Eq. (
B.7
) reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}-\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}P_{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)})\right)\mathrm{e}^{-sC}S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{sC_{Z}}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\biggl[\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}\biggr]_{s=0}^{s=t}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}(\mathrm{e}^{-sC}S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{sC_{Z}})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\biggl(\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}-S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}[C,S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}]P_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{s(\gamma b_{\ell}I+C_{Z})}\biggr),

(B.13)

where in the second equality we used integration by parts.

It remains to show that the quantity in the parentheses multiplying
1/\gamma
in Eq. (
B.13
) is uniformly bounded for
t\in[0,t_{2}]
.
In Kato’s proof, this followed simply from unitarity. In our case,
it follows from
b_{\ell}
being purely imaginary and from
\mathrm{e}^{tB}
being a bounded semigroup,

\|\mathrm{e}^{tB}\|\leq M

(B.14)

for
t\geq 0
and for some
M\geq 1
. This implies that

\|\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}\|\leq M\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}

(B.15)

for
t\geq 0
and
\gamma>0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\|+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}s_{n}\|\mathrm{e}^{(t-s_{1})\gamma B}C\mathrm{e}^{(s_{1}-s_{2})\gamma B}\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{}\times\mathrm{e}^{(s_{n-1}-s_{n})\gamma B}C\mathrm{e}^{s_{n}\gamma B}\|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}M^{n+1}\|C\|^{n}=M\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}.

(B.16)

We also have a simpler bound
\|\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}\|\leq\mathrm{e}^{t\|C_{Z}\|}
, and therefore, for
\gamma>0
, Eq. (
B.13
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}-\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}P_{\ell}\|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\|S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\|\,\Bigl(M\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}+\mathrm{e}^{t\|C_{Z}\|}\Bigr)+M\|[C,S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}]P_{\ell}\|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}-\mathrm{e}^{t\|C_{Z}\|}}{M\|C\|-\|C_{Z}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}(M+1)\|S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\|\frac{M\|C\|\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}-\|C_{Z}\|\mathrm{e}^{t\|C_{Z}\|}}{M\|C\|-\|C_{Z}\|}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}(M+1)\|S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\|\frac{M\|C\|\mathrm{e}^{t_{2}M\|C\|}-\|C_{Z}\|\mathrm{e}^{t_{2}\|C_{Z}\|}}{M\|C\|-\|C_{Z}\|},

(B.17)

for all
t\in[0,t_{2}]
,
thus proving the uniformity of the limit (
B.5
) [for the last two inequalities, we have bounded a factor as
\|[C,S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}]P_{\ell}\|=\|CS_{\ell}CP_{\ell}-S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}C_{Z}\|\leq\|S_{\ell}CP_{\ell}\|(\|C\|+\|C_{Z}\|)
,
and used the fact that
t\mapsto\frac{a\mathrm{e}^{ta}-b\mathrm{e}^{tb}}{a-b}
is increasing for positive
t
, for all
a,b>0
].
By summing Eq. (
B.5
) over the peripheral spectrum
b_{\ell}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
, we get

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\varphi}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}P_{\varphi}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.18)

uniformly for
t\in[0,t_{2}]
with
t_{2}>0
.

Step 2. Eigenvalues with negative real parts:

Consider now an eigenvalue
b_{\ell}
with a negative real part,
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}<0
, so that

BP_{\ell}=b_{\ell}P_{\ell}+N_{\ell}.

(B.19)

We get, for
\gamma>0
and
t\geq 0
,

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}C\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma B},

(B.20)

and

\|\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}-\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}P_{\ell}\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\|\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\|\|C\|\|\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma B}P_{\ell}\|.

(B.21)

Now, recalling the bound (
B.15
), we have

\|\mathrm{e}^{(t-s)(\gamma B+C)}\|\leq M\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}

for
s\in[0,t]
, and

\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma B}P_{\ell}=\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma b_{\ell}}\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma N_{\ell}}P_{\ell}=\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma b_{\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{n_{\ell}-1}\frac{1}{k!}(s\gamma N_{\ell})^{k}P_{\ell}

(B.22)

with some
n_{\ell}
, whence

\|\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma B}P_{\ell}\|\leq\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}}p_{\ell}(s\gamma),

(B.23)

with
p_{\ell}
a polynomial of degree
n_{\ell}-1
with positive coefficients depending on
N_{\ell}
.
Therefore, Eq. (
B.21
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}-\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}P_{\ell}\|

\displaystyle\leq M\|C\|\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{s\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}}p_{\ell}(s\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}M\|C\|\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{s\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}}p_{\ell}(s).

(B.24)

Thus, we have

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}P_{\ell}=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}P_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}P_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)

(B.25)

as
\gamma\to+\infty
. By summing over the nonperipheral spectrum with
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}<0
, we get

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}(I-P_{\varphi})=\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{Z}}(I-P_{\varphi})+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.26)

uniformly for
t\in[0,t_{2}]
with
t_{2}>0
.

Now, for
t\geq t_{1}>0
, we get from Eq. (
B.23
) that

\|\mathrm{e}^{t\gamma B}P_{\ell}\|\leq M_{1}\mathrm{e}^{t_{1}\gamma c}\leq\frac{M_{1}}{t_{1}\gamma|c|}

(B.27)

with
M_{1}\geq 1
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{\ell}\leq c<0
. Therefore, by summing over the nonperipheral spectrum, we have that

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}(I-P_{\varphi})=\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.28)

uniformly for
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
with
0<t_{1}<t_{2}
.

Step 3. Putting the two cases together:

Summing Eqs. (
B.18
) and (
B.26
), we arrive at

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}=\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.29)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
[0,+\infty)
,
with
C_{Z}
defined in Eq. (
3.3
).
Since
C_{Z}
commutes with
B
, Eq. (
B.29
)
implies the strong-coupling limit (
3.4
).

Moreover, by using Eq. (
B.28
), which describes how the nonperipheral part decays away at positive times, one finally gets

\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C)}=\mathrm{e}^{t(\gamma B+C_{Z})}P_{\varphi}+\mathcal{O}(1/\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad\gamma\to+\infty,

(B.30)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
(0,+\infty)
. This gives the limit (
3.5
) and concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Further Estimation of the Error Bound

Here we describe how we get the error bound (
3.9
) from the tighter version (
3.6
).
The idea is to make use of the Jordan normal form to facilitate the estimation of the norms.

We first point out that any matrix can be transformed into the Jordan normal form but with the “
1
”s just next to the eigenvalues on the diagonal in the Jordan normal form scaled to some constant
\nu
.
5
5

5

In physics contexts, this rescaling would be somewhat necessary.
For instance, if
t
in
\mathrm{e}^{tB}
is time,
B
is of the dimension of frequency, and “
1
” in the Jordan normal form of
B
does not make sense without specifying a unit.
The quantity
\nu
fixes the unit in the Jordan normal form.

Let us turn
B
into such a Jordan form by a similarity transformation
T_{\nu}
,

T_{\nu}^{-1}BT_{\nu}=\sum_{k}(b_{k}\tilde{P}_{k}+\tilde{N}_{k}),

(C.1)

where
\{b_{k}\}
is the spectrum of
B
,
\{\tilde{P}_{k}\}
the diagonal spectral projections, and
\{\tilde{N}_{k}\}
the nilpotents with entries
\nu
or
0
on the next diagonal.
Notice that the infinity norms (the largest singular values) of
\tilde{P}_{k}
and
\tilde{N}_{k}^{n}
are
\|\tilde{P}_{k}\|_{\infty}=1
and
\|\tilde{N}_{k}^{n}\|_{\infty}=\nu^{n}
or
0
, respectively, for any positive integer
n
(in the following, we use infinity norm and omit the subscript “
\infty
” of
\|{}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{}\|_{\infty}
).
Then, we can estimate norms e.g. as
\|P_{k}\|\leq\|T_{\nu}^{-1}\|\|\tilde{P}_{k}\|\|T_{\nu}\|=\chi_{\nu}
, where
\chi_{\nu}=\|T_{\nu}^{-1}\|\|T_{\nu}\|\geq 1
is called “condition number”
[
47
]
.

We will see that the spectral gaps play important roles, and will realize that it is convenient to choose
\nu
as

\nu=\min(\eta,\Delta),

(C.2)

where

\eta=\min_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}|{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{k}}|,\qquad\Delta=\min_{k\neq\ell}|b_{k}-b_{\ell}|.

(C.3)

Now, let us start estimating the norms involved in the error bound (
3.6
).

(i) Bounding
\bm{\|\mathrm{e}^{tB}\|\leq M}
:

Recalling the assumption that the peripheral eigenvalues
b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
of
B
are semisimple, the spectral representation of
B
reads

\mathrm{e}^{tB}=\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{tb_{k}}P_{k}+\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{tb_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{tN_{k}}P_{k}.

(C.4)

It is bounded as

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{tB}\|

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}+\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits b_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{t\|\tilde{N}_{k}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}+\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta t}\mathrm{e}^{\nu t}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}+\chi_{\nu}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\leq D\chi_{\nu}\vphantom{\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}}

\displaystyle\equiv M,

(C.5)

where we have used
\nu\leq\eta
and
D
is the dimension of
B
.

(ii) Bounding the decaying part
\bm{\|\mathrm{e}^{tB}(I-P_{\varphi})\|\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\eta t}p(t)}
:

The spectral representation of
B
yields

\mathrm{e}^{tB}(I-P_{\varphi})=\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{tb_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{tN_{k}}P_{k}=\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{tb_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}\frac{1}{n!}t^{n}N_{k}^{n}P_{k}.

(C.6)

It is bounded as

\|\mathrm{e}^{tB}(I-P_{\varphi})\|\leq\chi_{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta t}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}\frac{1}{n!}(\nu t)^{n}\equiv\mathrm{e}^{-\eta t}p(t).

(C.7)

We need its integral:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-\eta s}p(s)

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}(\nu/\eta)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{b_{k}\not\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}n_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{D}{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{\eta},

(C.8)

where we have used
\nu\leq\eta
and
n_{k}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\nolimits P_{k}
.

(iii) Bounding the reduced resolvent
\bm{\|S_{\ell}\|}
:

The reduced resolvent
S_{\ell}
defined in Eq. (
3.8
) is cast into

S_{\ell}=\sum_{k\neq\ell}[(b_{k}-b_{\ell})I+N_{k}]^{-1}P_{k}=\sum_{k\neq\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(b_{k}-b_{\ell})^{n+1}}N_{k}^{n}P_{k}.

(C.9)

It is bounded as

\displaystyle\|S_{\ell}\|

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\sum_{k\neq\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}\frac{\nu^{n}}{|b_{k}-b_{\ell}|^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{1}{\Delta}\sum_{k\neq\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}(\nu/\Delta)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{1}{\Delta}\sum_{k\neq\ell}n_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{\nu}\frac{D}{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{\Delta},

(C.10)

where we have used
\nu\leq\Delta
.

(iv) Bounding the projected generator
\bm{\|C_{Z}\|}
:

Let us also try to simplify the factor involving
\|C_{Z}\|
in the bound (
3.6
).
The projected generator
C_{Z}
is defined in Eq. (
3.3
), and is bounded as

\|C_{Z}\|\leq\chi_{\nu}^{2}\sum_{b_{k}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\|C\|\leq D\chi_{\nu}^{2}\|C\|=\chi_{\nu}M\|C\|.

(C.11)

Now, by noting the inequality
(\sinh x)/x\leq\cosh x
, we can bound as

\displaystyle\frac{a\mathrm{e}^{at}-b\mathrm{e}^{bt}}{a-b}

\displaystyle=\left(\cosh[(a-b)t/2]+(a+b)\frac{\sinh[(a-b)t/2]}{a-b}\right)\mathrm{e}^{(a+b)t/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{2}t(a+b)\right)\mathrm{e}^{(a+b)t/2}\cosh[(a-b)t/2]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}t(a+b)\right)(\mathrm{e}^{at}+\mathrm{e}^{bt})

(C.12)

for
a,b\geq 0
and
t\geq 0

By using this inequality, we can get

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}t\,\Bigl(M\|C\|+\|C_{Z}\|\Bigr)\right](\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}+\mathrm{e}^{t\|C_{Z}\|})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}t\,\Bigl(M\|C\|+\chi_{\nu}M\|C\|\Bigr)\right](\mathrm{e}^{tM\|C\|}+\mathrm{e}^{t\chi_{\nu}M\|C\|})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{2t\chi_{\nu}M\|C\|}.
