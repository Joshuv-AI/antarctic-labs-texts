The Hlawka Zeta Function as a Respectable Objecct
Mike Montoro, under the Advisement of Prof Joseph Hundley

Abstract
The Hlawka Zeta Function is a Dirichlet series defined geometrically which provides an integral representation of the number of lattice points contained in the dilation t ​ D tD for some star shaped region D ⊂ ℝ 2 D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} and some real number t ∈ ℝ + t\in\mathbb{R}^{+} . We give an overview of this construction and integral representation before giving the Hlawka Zeta function as a sum of Eisenstein Series acting on K K -finite vectors multiplied by Fourier coefficients depending on D D . We then study the case of D D as an circle, ellipse, and then square to study functional equations and “fibers” of this object, and pose conjectures regarding these properties in general.



1
Definition of the Hlawka Zeta Function

Let
r:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a continuous function such that the mapping
\theta\mapsto(r(\theta)\cos(\theta),r(\theta)\sin(\theta))
is a closed curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The goal is to count the number of lattice points in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
which are also in
tD
, where
D
is the domain
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|(x,y)|\leq r(\theta)\}
, and
tD
is that domain dilated by the real number
t
. A crude estimate would be simply the area of the domain
tD
, but this is not an exact estimate. This discrepancy is discussed in detail in
[
3
]
. In an attempt to correct this, we introduce the
Hlawka Zeta Function
.

Definition 1
.

Let
t(m,n):=\min\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:(m,n)\in tD\}
,
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Then the Hlawka Zeta function is defined as

Z_{r}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}}\frac{1}{t(m,n)^{2s}}.

(1)

For the sake of space, we will mean
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
when we write
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
as the index in a sum. Also, we will sometimes notate
Z_{r}(s)=Z(r,s)
. There is an explicit formula for the
t(m,n)
in terms of
r(\theta)
: note that
t(m,n)
is the unique real number such that
|(m,n)|=|t(m,n)r(\theta(m,n))|
when
\theta(m,n)
is the angle between the positive
x
axis and the ray from the origin to the point
(m,n)
, so

t(m,n)=\frac{|(m,n)|}{r(\theta(m,n))}=\frac{\sqrt{m^{2}+n^{2}}}{r(\theta(m,n))}.

Thus we can rewrite:

Z_{r}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{r^{2s}(\theta(m,n))}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}.

(2)

We wish to write
\{t(m,n)\}_{m,n=1}^{\infty}
as a sequence
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
with an ordering such that
t_{k}<t_{k+1}
for all
k
. This requires a fact that will also be useful later.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\{t(m,n)\}_{m,n=1}^{\infty}
be as in Definition 1. This sequence has no limit point in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(with respect to the Euclidean topology), i.e. is a discrete set.

Proof.

Suppose
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a limit point of
\{t(m,n)\}_{m,n=1}^{\infty}
. Then there exists an infinite subset
\{t(m_{j},n_{j})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
with the property that
t(m_{j},n_{j})\in(T-\frac{1}{4},T+\frac{1}{4})
for all
j
. Let
B_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
be the Euclidean ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
(x,y)
. Consider the closed sets

C_{j}=\overline{r(r^{-1}(B_{\frac{1}{4}}(m_{j},n_{j})))}

where we consider
r
as a function
\theta\mapsto(r(\theta)\cos(\theta),r(\theta)\sin(\theta))
. These are clearly closed sets, and moreover
C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
since each ball of radius
\frac{1}{4}
is disjoint. Then is easy to see that
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}
is also a closed set. Since
[0,2\pi]
is a compact set,
r([0,2\pi])
must be compact, and so since
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}\subset r([0,2\pi])
,
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}
is bounded. Thus
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}
is compact. However, the open cover
\{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(m_{j},n_{j})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
has no subcover, let alone a finite subcover. Thus we obtain a contradiction.
∎

We now invoke a lemma from set theory.

Lemma 3
.

Any non-empty discrete subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
has a least element. Moreover, if
S
is infinite there is a bijection
f:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to S
preserving the order
“
<
”
thus giving
S
a sequential form with
s_{0}<s_{1}<\cdots
.

Proof.

We first prove the first part. If
S
is finite, then the claim is clear. If
S
is infinite, suppose that
S
has no least element. Choose
s\in S
, and consider
[0,s]
. There is an element
s_{1}
in
S
which is less than
s
, thus in
[0,s]
. The same is true for
s_{1}
itself, i.e. there is an element
s_{2}\in S
with
0<s_{2}<s_{1}<s
. This process must go on infinitely, so there is an infinite subset of
S
,
\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
completely contained in
[0,s]
. But
[0,s]
is compact, so any infinite subset must have a limit point, contradicting the discreteness of
S
.

To prove the second part, we see from the first part that
S
has a least element, say
s_{0}
. Let
f(0)=s_{0}
. Now
S\smallsetminus\{s_{0}\}
is a discrete set as well. Thus it has a least element,
s_{1}
. Let
f(1)=s_{1}
. One may continue this process ad infinitum, thus constructing the function
f
as desired and giving
S
the desired sequential form
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
.
∎

Finally, we have:

Corollary 4
.

The set
\{t(m,n)\}_{m,n=1}^{\infty}
from Definition 1 has a sequential form
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
with
t_{k}<t_{k+1}
for all
k
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the proposition and the lemma.
∎

Define
a_{k}=|\{(m,n)\in Z^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}:(m,n)\in\partial t_{k}D\}|
,i.e. the number of lattice points on in
\partial t_{k}D
,
\partial t_{k}D
denoting the boundary of
t_{k}D
. Thus we can now rewrite the Hlawka Zeta Function as

Z_{r}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}t_{k}^{-2s}.

(3)

The reformulation of the definition as in (2) and (3) are often much more useful in studying
Z_{r}(s)
, since this looks like a Dirichlet Series. This is a key idea we will use later.

Given that this a definition given in terms of a sum, there is an immediate question of the convergence of
Z_{r}(s)
and thus where
Z_{r}(s)
is well defined. We may partially address this immediately with the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Z_{r}(s)
converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
.

Note that this result is independent of
r
.

Proof.

Since
r
is a continuous function on the compact set
[0,2\pi]
, there is a number
M
with
r(\theta)<M
for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. This gives
|r(\theta)^{s}|=r(\theta)^{\operatorname{Re}(s)}<M^{\operatorname{Re}(s)}=|M^{s}|
which holds for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
. Now,

\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\left|\frac{r^{2s}(\theta(m,n))}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}\right|<\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\left|\frac{(M^{2})^{s}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}\right|=|M^{2s}|\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{|(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}|}

which converges for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
.
∎

Actually,
Z_{r}(s)
converges for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
. This will proven in a subsequent section.

Now that we have the basic definitions understood, there is a series of questions we seek to answer.

I.

What geometric information does the Hlawka Zeta function give us? How does it relate to counting

II.

What are the analytic properties of the Hlawka Zeta function? Does it have an analytic continuation beyond the domain of convergence? Namely, does it have a functional equation?

III.

The Hlawka zeta function defines a mapping sending a (continuous) function
r:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to
Z_{r}(s)
, which is a meromorphic function on at least some half plane in the complex. What are the properties of this mapping? More specifically, when does
Z_{r_{1}}(s)=Z_{r_{2}}(s)
for
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
?

2
Motivation for the Definition

Suppose
a(n)
is a sequence of real numbers. Let

(D.a)(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n^{s}}

be the associated Dirichlet series. Let
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a(n)
, and let
A^{\prime}(x)=A(x)
everywhere but at integers
x=n
, and at those points set
A^{\prime}(n)=a_{1}+\cdots a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{2}a_{n}
. The definition of the Hlawka Zeta function arises from the following well-known result:

Theorem 6

(Perron’s Formula)
.

Suppose
(D.a)(s)
converges absolutely when
\operatorname{Re}(s)>c
. We have the following formula:

A^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}(D.a)(s)x^{s}\frac{ds}{s}

where
\sigma>\max\{0,c\}
.

Note that it is possible that the Dirichlet series could never converge, making the statement of the formula vacuous. We will prove this from another well known transformation:

Definition 7
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real valued function which is continuous except at countably many places
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with no limit point in the domain and at these places,
\phi(y_{i})=\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{+}}\phi(y)
or
\phi(y_{i})=\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{-}}\phi(y)
, and

L_{i}=\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{+}}\phi(y)-\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{-}}\phi(y)

is finite. Then the
Mellin Transform
of this function is defined as

\Phi(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(y)y^{s}\frac{dy}{y}.

We write
\Phi(s)=(\mathcal{M}.\phi)(s)
.

This is a well-defined complex function in
\sigma_{1}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\sigma_{2}
for some
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in[-\infty,\infty]
, determined by the convergence of the integral from 1 to
\infty
and from 0 to 1, respectively. It is worth noting that when we say
“limit point”
, we use the Euclidean topology on
\mathbb{R}
.

For functions of the type in the definition, define the
sign of the discontinuity at
y_{i}
, denoted by
SGN(\phi,y_{i})
, to be
+1
if
\phi(y_{i})=\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{-}}\phi(y)
and
-1
if
\phi(y_{i})=\lim\limits_{y\to y_{i}^{+}}\phi(y)
. We also have this key result.

Lemma 8

(Mellin Inversion formula)
.

Let the situation be as above. Then

\phi^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\Phi(s)y^{-s}ds,

for
\sigma\in(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
, where
\phi^{\prime}(y)=\phi(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{\{y_{i}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\}
and

\phi^{\prime}(y_{i})=\phi(y_{i})+\epsilon\frac{1}{2}|L_{i}|

where
\epsilon=SGN(\phi,y_{i})sgn(L_{i})
, where
sgn
is the normal signum function. We then define the Mellin inversion operator
(\mathcal{M}^{-1}.\Phi(s))=\phi(y)
by the above formula.

Note that
\phi^{\prime}(y_{i})
is more simply stated as the value halfway between the two limits as
\phi
approaches
y_{i}
from the left and the right, sometimes denoted by
\phi^{\prime}(y_{i})=\frac{1}{2}(\phi(y_{i}+0^{+})+\phi(y_{i}+0^{-})
in the literature. The function
A(x)
gives
A^{\prime}(x)
in this way. Moreover, this says that
\phi
is recoverable from
\Phi
except on a set of measure zero. The proof of this can be found in
[
7
]
, so we omit it. We now prove Perron’s Formula using this lemma.

Proof.

Assume the Dirichlet series
(D.a)(s)
converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>c
. We look at the integral

\mathcal{M}.(A^{\prime}(x))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}a(n)x^{s-1}dx.

We first note that the inside is zero for
x<1
. Thus this is equal to

\int_{1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}a(n)x^{s-1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{n}^{\infty}a(n)x^{s-1}dx,

if the sum on the right is absolutely convergent. Evaluating that integral gives

\int_{n}^{\infty}a(n)x^{s-1}dx=\lim\limits_{b\to\infty}[a(n)x^{s}/s]_{x=n}^{x=b}

(4)

and we see that
\lim\limits_{b\to\infty}\frac{a(n)b^{s}}{s}=0
if
\operatorname{Re}(s)<0
. Then the whole sum is now:
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-a(n)n^{s}}{s}=\frac{-1}{s}(D.a)(-s)
. This sum converges absolutely for
-\operatorname{Re}(s)>c
, i.e.
\operatorname{Re}(s)<-c
and
\operatorname{Re}(s)<0
. Thus, the two sums in (4) are equal for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\max\{0,c\}
since the sum on the left converges absolutely for those values of
s
. We then apply the Mellin inversion formula to obtain
A^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{M}^{-1}.(\frac{-1}{s}(D.a(-s)))
for
\sigma<\max\{0,c\}
, and this is now

A^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{M}^{-1}.(\frac{-1}{s}(D.a(-s)))=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\frac{-1}{s}(D.a)(-s)x^{-s}ds=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\sigma-i\infty}^{-\sigma+i\infty}(D.a)(s)x^{s}\frac{ds}{s},

where
-\sigma>\max\{0,c\}
.
∎

This is an elegant formula. Our proof is non-standard for a very specific reason: It is virtually useless to study the Hlawka Zeta function, since usually
Z_{r}(s)
is not a standard Dirichlet Series. We will need something slightly more general. We develop a basic set of definitions about
t
-Dirichlet series.

Definition 9
.

Let
a
be a function
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\{t_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
a sequence of positive real numbers. Define
(D_{t}.a)(s)
as

(D_{t}.a)(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(t_{n})}{(t_{n})^{s}},

the
t
-Dirichlet series attached to the pair
(a,t_{n})

Notably, this is equivalent to the so-called Generalized Dirichlet Series. We will reconcile these definitions later.

Define
A_{t}(x)=\sum_{t_{n}\leq x}a(t_{n})
for some
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that
a\circ t:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is also a sequence of real numbers, so
A_{t}(x)
also gives the sum of some sequence, but one indexed by
t_{n}
, a more general sequence of real numbers. With this definition, we see something relatively remarkable happen.

Theorem 10

(Perron’s Formula for
t
-Dirichlet Series.)
.

Suppose that the
t
-Dirichlet series attached to the pair
(a,t_{n})
converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>c
and that
\{t_{n}\}
has no limit point in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We then have the following:

A_{t}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}(D_{t}.a)(s)x^{s}\frac{ds}{s}

for
\sigma>\max\{0,c\}
.
Here
A_{t}(x)=A_{t}^{\prime}(x)
except at
t_{n}
and
A_{t}^{\prime}(t_{n})
has its last term multiplied by
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

This is the reason we proved the original Perron’s formula the way we did. The key point is that in fact
A_{t}(x)
has a Mellin Transform since
t_{n}
has no limit point. Then the above can be proven identically by simply replacing the limits of integration from
n
to
t_{n}
. The integral still stays away from
0
, so any convergence issue there is fine. The rest is clear.
∎

Now we see the immediate connection here between these
t
-Dirichlet series and the Hlawka Zeta function: that the Hlawka Zeta function is in fact a
t
-Dirichlet series evaluated at
2s
, using the pair
(a_{k},t_{k})
as defined in the first section. Furthermore, the key point about
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
being discrete has already been previously addressed in the first section, so we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 11

(Point-Count Formula)
.

With notation as in the first section, let
A(x)
be the sum
\sum_{t_{n}\leq x}a_{k}
. This is precisely the amount of lattice points in the region
xD
. As in Perron’s formula, let
A^{\prime}(x)
be the same sum but with final term multiplied by
\frac{1}{2}
at
A^{\prime}(t_{k})
. The Hlawka Zeta function satisfies

A^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{t_{k}^{s}}\right)x^{s}\frac{ds}{s}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2\sigma+i\infty}^{2\sigma-i\infty}Z_{r}(s)x^{2s}\frac{ds}{s}

for
\sigma>1
.

Here we cut out the intermittent function in the
a
, i.e.
a(t_{k})=a_{k}
. The Hlawka Zeta function thus literally plays an integral role in counting lattice points on in a star-shaped region expanding at a constant rate. This answers one of our main questions.

3
Formula as a sum over Eisenstein Series

We now want to establish a formula for the Hlawka zeta function in terms of Eisenstein Series acting on a particular set of functions. We first define this class of function now. To standardize notation, let
GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
be real 2 by 2 matrices with positive determinant. Let
f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}:GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
be defined in the following manner. The Iwasawa decomposition of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
gives that an element of this group,
g
, can be written uniquely as

g=\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{1/2}&xy^{-1/2}\\
0&y^{-1/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\kappa_{\theta},\ \kappa_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&\sin(\theta)\\
-\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\\
\end{pmatrix}

(5)

for
x,y,u\in\mathbb{R}
and
u,y>0
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. This allows us to define

f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{1/2}&xy^{-1/2}\\
0&y^{-1/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\kappa_{\theta}\right):=u^{s_{1}+s_{2}}y^{(s_{1}-s_{2}+1)/2}e^{iq\theta}.

For our purposes, we will want to look at these functions as defined on the quotient
\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}\backslash GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
, where

\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},\ n\in\mathbb{Z}.

To establish this as reasonable, we have the following.

Lemma 12
.

The functions
f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}
are equal on right cosets
\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}g
for
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
, thus defining functions
\widetilde{f}_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}:\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}\backslash GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
uniquely on the quotient group. Furthermore if
s=(s_{1}-s_{2}+1)/2
, we fix
s_{1}+s_{2}
, and
\theta(c,d)
is the angle between the line from the origin and
(c,d)
and the positive
x
-axis then

f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\right)=\frac{(-i)^{q}e^{iq\theta(c,d)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}\ \text{for}\ \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z}).

Thus
\widetilde{f}_{q}:\mathbb{C}\times\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}\backslash SL(2,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
where
\widetilde{f}_{q}(s,\omega g)=f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}(g)
and
\omega\in\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}
,
g\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
is a well-defined, smooth function of
s
as well.

Proof.

For an element
g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix}\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
an easy computation gives
y=((c/u)^{2}+(d/u)^{2})^{-1}
in the Iwasawa decomposition. Furthermore, if
\theta(c,d)
is the angle made between the point
(c,d)
and the positive
x
-axis,
\theta=\theta(c,d)-\pi/2
(this can be seen from a right triangle construction). The formula for
f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}
is then

f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}(g)=u^{s_{1}+s_{2}}((c/u)^{2}+(d/u)^{2})^{-(s_{1}-s_{2}+1)/2}e^{iq(\theta(c,d)-\pi/2)}.

With this in mind, we compute

\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a+nc&b+nd\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix}

so multiplying by an element of
\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}
on the left affects neither
u
nor the bottom row of the matrix. It follows that
f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}
is well defined on
\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}\backslash GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
.
We now restrict to the subgroup
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
and let
s=(s_{1}-s_{2}+1)/2
so that this becomes

f_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}(g)=\frac{(-i)^{q}e^{iq\theta(c,d)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}.

The smoothness in
s
is then clear from the formula.
∎

We now introduce the key Eisenstein Series in the upcoming proposition.

Definition 13
.

The Eisenstein Series acting on
f_{q}
evaluated at
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
is

(E.f_{q})(g,s_{1},s_{2})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}\backslash SL(2,\mathbb{Z})}\widetilde{f}_{q,s_{1},s_{2}}(\gamma g).

We see that due to the lemma if
g\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
we may write

(E.f_{q})(g,s_{1},s_{2})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}\backslash SL(2,\mathbb{Z})}\widetilde{f}_{q}(s,\gamma g)=(E.f_{q})(g,s)

where
s=\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}-s_{2}+1)
as before in this case. Furthermore, we have

(E.f_{q})(I,s)=\sum_{\gcd(c,d)=1}\frac{(-i)^{q}e^{iq\theta(c,d)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}.

Also, we define

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(g,s_{1},s_{2}):=\zeta(2s)(E.f)(g,s_{1},s_{2}).

One can verify through a standard calculation (noting that
\theta(m,n)=\theta(km,kn)
for
k>0
) that

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{(-i)^{q}e^{iq\theta(m,n)}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}.

Note that this Eisenstein Series converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
, as the modulus of each term is the same as a classical Eisenstein series, so we refer to
[
1
]
.

We now define also
\widehat{r^{s}}(q)
to be the
q
th Fourier coefficient in the Fourier Series for
r^{s}
, i.e.
r^{s}(\theta)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{s}}(q)e^{iq\theta}
when

\widehat{r^{s}}(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{s}(\theta)e^{-iq\theta}d\theta.

Since

r^{s}(\theta)=r(\theta)^{i\operatorname{Im}(s)+\operatorname{Re}(s)}=r(\theta)^{\operatorname{Re}(s)}e^{i\ln(r(\theta))\operatorname{Im}(s)}

r^{s}
is a continuous function from
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\to\mathbb{C}
. We will need to discuss the absolute convergence of this Fourier series. We introduce a definition here to help this classification.

Definition 14
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) be an open set and
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
(or
\mathbb{C}^{m}
). If there exists a constant
C
and
0<\alpha\leq 1
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\alpha}

for all
x,y\in A
, then call
f

\alpha
-Hölder continuous
.

This definition makes sense for any function between metric spaces. We note that
\alpha>0
ensures that the function is continuous, and that
\alpha=1
would give the definition for Lipschitz continuous. Thus this condition is stronger than simply continuous and weaker than Lipschitz continuous or continuously differentiable.

We have the following result.

Lemma 15
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, then its Fourier Series converges absolutely.

Proof.

See
[
4
, 31-33]
.
∎

Note here that there are in fact conditions which exactly give the absolute convergence of the Fourier Series, but we opt for this condition since it is enough to check that
r
is either differentiable or Lipschitz for this to hold.

With this notation, we can now state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 16

(Eisenstein Series Formula, preliminary version)
.

Let all of the definitions be as above. Suppose
r
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. Then the Hlawka Zeta function
Z_{r}(s)
is

Z_{r}(s)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}i^{q}\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)

with absolute convergence of the right side happening on the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
.

Proof.

We look to evaluate the sum in question:

\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)(E^{\sharp}.f_{q,s})(I,s)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{e^{iq\theta(m,n)}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)e^{iq\theta(m,n)}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}.

We would like to change the order of the summation: Thus we must check that the sum absolutely converges. We see

\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\left|\frac{\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)e^{iq\theta(m,n)}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}\right|=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)|\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\left|\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}\right|

and we know that
\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}
converges absolutely with
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
and
\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)|
converges by the lemma. Thus we can switch the order of the summation to obtain:

\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)e^{iq\theta(m,n)}}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)e^{iq\theta(m,n)}

and now since
\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)e^{iq\theta(m,n)}=r^{2s}(\theta(m,n))
, we get that this is equal to

=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{r^{2s}(\theta(m,n))}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}

which is one of the definitions of
Z_{r}(s)
.
∎

This proposition tells us that the Hlawka Zeta function is a sum of Eisenstein Series with coefficients given by these
\widehat{r^{2s}}(q)
. The Eisenstein Series portion is fairly well known and studied. The coefficients on the other hand are much more difficult to compute. In fact, much of the information about
Z_{r}(s)
is sitting inside of these coefficients. Before analyzing
Z_{r}(s)
under this context, there is an easy corollary to this proposition which will prove useful later.

Suppose that
r(\theta)
is an even function. Then
r
has a cosine series expansion

r(\theta)=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r}(q)\cos(q\theta)

where

\widetilde{r}(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r(\theta)\cos(q\theta)d\theta.

Furthermore, we have that the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{r}(q)
with respect to the
e^{iq\theta}
basis are

\widehat{r}(q)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{r}(0)\ \ \ \ \ \text{if}\ q=0\\
\widetilde{r}(|q|)/2\ \text{if}\ q\neq 0\\
\end{cases}

in this situation, we have the following.

Corollary 17
.

If
r:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an even function satisfying the hypotheses of the previous proposition and cosine series coefficients defined as above, then

Z_{r}(s)=\widetilde{r^{2s}}(0)(E^{\sharp}.f_{0})(I,s)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}i^{q}\widetilde{r^{2s}}(q)((E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)+(-1)^{q}(E^{\sharp}.f_{-q})(I,s)).

4
Some Observations and First Calculations

This section is devoted to various points. First, we calculate the Hlawka Zeta Function of a circle. Second, we establish an action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on the set of functions
C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
, which is key to some of our conjectures and calculation of the ellipse’s Hlawka Zeta function later. Finally, we give the functional equation for the
(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)
, to be used later as well.

First, we have the following.

Lemma 18
.

Let
R(\theta)=cr(\theta)
, where
c
is any positive real number. Then

Z_{R}(s)=c^{2s}Z_{r}(s).

Proof.

This is clear from the definition.
∎

This proposition tells us that multiplying by a constant does not change
Z_{r}(s)
in an analytically significant way. We will use this concept much of the time: it is often convenient to consider classes of functions up to a constant. This can be understood in the sense of point counting as well: from the formula, we see that if
A^{\prime}_{r}(x)
is the point count function as in the Point-counting formula, then

A^{\prime}_{r}(cx)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2\sigma+i\infty}^{2\sigma-i\infty}Z_{r}(s)(cx)^{2s}\frac{ds}{s}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2\sigma+i\infty}^{2\sigma-i\infty}(c^{2s}Z_{r}(s))x^{2s}\frac{ds}{s}

=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2\sigma+i\infty}^{2\sigma-i\infty}Z_{R}(s)x^{2s}\frac{ds}{s}=A^{\prime}_{R}(x).

This is fairly intuitive from the geometry of the situation, but it is nice to see that intuition relayed in the zeta function.

The Case of the Circle

We will now consider the case of circles centered at the origin. The function
r
associated to the circle of radius
c>0
is the constant function
r(\theta)=c
. To establish notation, let
z=x+iy
, and define

E(z,s):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\infty}_{+}\backslash SL(2,\mathbb{Z})}\operatorname{Im}(\gamma z)^{s}

as the classical Eisenstein Series. Here we use the convention that an element of a matrix group multiplied by an element of
\mathbb{C}
means the action by linear fractional transformations. With this notation, we have the following proposition. We also note that by a standard calculation,

E(z,s)=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2s)}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{y^{s}}{|mz+n|^{2s}}.

We now are able to prove a functional equation for this simple case.

Proposition 19
.

For
r(\theta)=c
we have that,

Z_{r}(s)=2c^{2s}\zeta(2s)E(i,s)

and furthermore

\frac{1}{c^{2s}}\Gamma(s)\pi^{-s}Z_{r}(s)=\frac{1}{c^{2(1-s)}}\Gamma(1-s)\pi^{-(1-s)}Z_{r}(1-s).

Furthermore,
Z_{r}(s)
has meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
except at the points
s=1
,
s=0
.

Proof.

From the definitions and from the lemma, we have

Z_{r}(s)=c^{2s}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}=2c^{2s}\zeta(2s)E(i,s)

proving the first part of the proposition. We reference
[
1
, 66-69]
for the functional equation of the functional equation of the Eisenstein series here, which immediately gives the second part of the proposition.
∎

This gives a functional equation
s\mapsto 1-s
for this particular classical shape, and answering one of our guiding questions in this basic case. One can also understand this in terms of the Eisenstein series formula given in the previous section. One computes that for
r(\theta)=c
,

\widetilde{r^{2s}}(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}c^{2s}\cos(q\theta)d\theta=\begin{cases}0\ \text{if}\ q\neq 0\\
c^{2s}\ \text{if}\ q=0\\
\end{cases}

and so the formula gives

Z_{r}(\theta)=c^{2s}(E^{\sharp}.f_{0})(I,s)=c^{2s}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}=c^{2s}\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m^{2}+n^{2})^{s}}

which is a classical Eisenstein Series.

Symmetries in the Eisenstein Series, Refining the Formula

Before attempting to calculate any other Hlawka Zeta functions, it is useful to begin discussing the third of our driving questions. We will begin in the analytic setting to discuss some of the symmetries in the Eisenstein series from before.

Lemma 20
.

Let

\kappa_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\varphi)&\sin(\varphi)\\
-\sin(\varphi)&\cos(\varphi)\\
\end{pmatrix}\in SO(2,\mathbb{R})

as before. Then we have the following equality:

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(\kappa_{\varphi},s)=e^{iq\varphi}(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s).

Proof.

We first would like to describe the effect of right multiplication on the right by an element of
SO(2,\mathbb{R})
on various pieces of the formula for the Eisenstein Series. Let
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix}\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
.
In the Iwasawa decomposition of
\gamma\kappa_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}\\
\end{pmatrix}
, we have

\gamma\kappa_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{1/2}&xy^{-1/2}\\
0&y^{-1/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\kappa_{\theta}\kappa_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{1/2}&xy^{-1/2}\\
0&y^{-1/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\kappa_{\theta+\varphi}

and thus we have
\theta(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})=\theta(c,d)+\varphi
. This gives that

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(\kappa_{\varphi},s)=\sum_{(c,d)\neq(0,0)}\frac{e^{iq(\theta(c^{\prime},d^{\prime}))}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}=\sum_{(c,d)\neq 0}\frac{e^{iq(\theta(c,d)+\varphi)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}=e^{iq\varphi}(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)

∎

This allows us to pose a significant simplification to the
E^{\sharp}.f_{q}
’s in the formula. We list the simplifications here.

Proposition 21
.

We have the following.

I.

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)=0
if
q
is odd.

II.

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)=0
if
q\equiv 2\mod{4}
.

III.

(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)=(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(g,s)
for
g\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. In particular, we have that
(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)=(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(\kappa_{k\pi/2},s)
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

For I, we see that for every
(c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
, there is a corresponding point
(-c,-d)
. These points are related by
\theta(c,d)=\theta(-c,-d)+\pi
so the term associated with that point is

\frac{e^{iq\theta(-c,-d)}}{((-c)^{2}+(-d)^{2})^{s}}=-\frac{e^{iq\theta(c,d)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}.

The term corresponding to
(c,d)
thus cancels with the one corresponding to
(-c,-d)
, so the sum telescopes to zero.

For II, we have that for every point
(c,d)
there is a corresponding point
(-d,c)
, with
\theta(-d,c)=\theta(c,d)+\pi/2
. Thus the term corresponding to
(-d,c)
is

\frac{e^{i(4q+2)\theta(-d,c)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}=\frac{e^{4qi\theta(c,d)}e^{i\pi}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}=-\frac{e^{iq\theta(c,d)}}{(c^{2}+d^{2})^{s}}

which cancels out the
(c,d)
term in the sum and we have the result.

For III, this is immediate from the fact that translation by an element of a group is always a transitive action. Since
\kappa_{k\pi/2}\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
, we have the other part of the proposition as well.
∎

We obtain immediately a refinement of the Eisenstein Series formula.

Corollary 22

(Eisenstein Series Formula, simplified version)
.

Assume all of the hypotheses of the Eisenstein Series Formula. We have under the same conditions that

Z_{r}(s)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(4q)(E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(I,s)

with this sum converging absolutely when
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
.

This corollary immediately implies the following as well.

Corollary 23

(Simplified Eisenstein Series Formula for even functions)
.

Let the definitions be as in Corollary 17. We have the following.

Z_{r}(s)=\widetilde{r^{2s}}(0)(E^{\sharp}.f_{0})(I,s)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{r^{2s}}(4q)((E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(I,s)+(E^{\sharp}.f_{-4q})(I,s))

(6)

These corollaries, somewhat remarkably, tell us that only the Fourier coefficients of
r^{2s}
which are indexed by multiples of 4 actually matter when computing the Hlawka Zeta function.

This phenomenon is perhaps best understood not as evaluating the Eisenstein Series at a different matrix, but as rotation of the domain we are considering. To make this precise, we now would like to consider
R:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to be the unique
2\pi
periodic extension of
r:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to the entire real line. If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is
2\pi
periodic and
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, let
f_{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be given by given by
f_{\varphi}(\theta)=f(\theta+\varphi)
. Recall the following lemma from harmonic analysis:

Lemma 24
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a
2\pi
periodic function and
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Then
e^{iq\varphi}\widehat{f}(q)
is the
q
-th Fourier coefficient of the function
f_{\varphi}
.

Proof.

We have

e^{iq\varphi}\widehat{f}(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\theta)e^{-iq(\theta-\varphi)}d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\theta+\varphi)e^{-iq\theta}d\theta,

which is the desired equality.
∎

This lemma and the prior discussion have the following consequence.

Proposition 25
.

Let
r
and
R
be as above where
r
satisfies the hypothesis for the Eisenstein Series Formula. Let
r_{\varphi}
be the function defined on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
corresponding to
R_{\varphi}
. We have that

Z_{r_{\varphi}}=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{r^{2s}}(4q)(E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(\kappa_{\varphi},s).

Moreover, if
\varphi=\frac{k\pi}{2}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
Z_{r}=Z_{r_{\varphi}}
.

Proof.

From Lemma 20, Proposition 21, and Lemma 24, the result immediately follows.
∎

This gives a first guess at a possible fibers for the Hlawka Zeta function as described in the first section: the subgroup of rotations in
D_{8}
, the dihedral group of order 8. In this light, we have a subgroup isomorphic to this group embedded in
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
: the group
\{I,\kappa_{\pi/2},\kappa_{\pi},\kappa_{3\pi/2}\}
, which is embedded in the maximal compact group
K=SO(2,\mathbb{R})
of rotations. One important note is that of course
O(2,\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to
D_{8}
, but the reflections are generated by
R=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, which can be seen to send
r\mapsto-r
in the parametrization: of course then
-r<0
, which we try to avoid. We note that there is a natural action of
K
on the set of
r
’s given by
(\kappa_{\varphi}.r)(\theta)=r_{\varphi}(\theta)
. What this proposition tells us is that this action corresponds with putting a
\kappa_{\varphi}
in the input of the Eisenstein Series, or vise versa.

Action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on Functions

One may expand this view quite a bit, as there is a nice geometric interpretation of this as well. There is an action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
r
given by the action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on the graph of
r
. We will define this action precisely now. This will give an action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on the Hlawka Zeta function. Throughout, we use a fact from geometry: if
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a star-shaped region with respect to the origin, then applying a non-singular linear transformation
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
to this domain yields another star-shaped region with respect to the origin.

First, let us establish some notation. Take
C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
to be the set of continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
to
(0,\infty)
. Define the map
X:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
to be

X(c,\varphi)=\begin{pmatrix}c\cos(\varphi)\\
c\sin(\varphi)\end{pmatrix}

where
c\in(0,\infty)
. Also, define the map
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
such that
\theta(v)
is the angle between the positive
x
axis and the ray from the origin to
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
. Some initial observations about these maps are

I.

First,
\theta(X(c,\varphi))=\varphi
, so
\theta
undoes
X
on the second component, and so
\theta(X(c,\varphi))
is independent of
c
.

II.

The map
X
is a smooth diffeomorphism, and
\theta
is surjective. In fact the restriction
\theta|_{\gamma}
is a smooth diffeomorphism as long as
\gamma
is a continuous closed curve which is the boundary of a star-shaped region with respect to the origin. Moreover, we have that if
\gamma
is given in polar coordinates as
\gamma:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to(0,\infty)
, then
X(\gamma(\varphi),\theta|_{\gamma}(\gamma(\varphi)\cos(\varphi),\gamma(\varphi)\sin(\varphi)))
traces the graph of the curve
\gamma
.

III.

For all
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
and
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}
,
\theta(v)=\theta(u)
if and only if
\theta(gv)=\theta(gu)
. This is simply due to the fact that
g
is a linear map.

Let
\theta_{g,\gamma}:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
be the map given by

\theta_{g,\gamma}(\varphi)=\theta(gX(\gamma(\varphi),\varphi))

where
\gamma\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
. In fact,
\theta_{g,\gamma}=\theta_{g,1}
by observations above, but it will be crucial later that we have the auxiliary
\gamma
.

Lemma 26
.

The map
\theta_{g,\gamma}
is a diffeomorphism for all
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
,
\gamma\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider
\gamma=1
. We see that
\theta_{g}
the composition
\theta\circ g\circ X|_{\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}
, where
g
is simply considered as a linear map. It is readily seen that
X|_{\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}
is a diffeomorphism of
\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
onto
S^{1}
. Now,
g
maps
S^{1}
diffeomorphically onto the boundary of some ellipse
E_{g}
, and since this is star-shaped with respect to the origin,
\theta|_{\partial E_{g}}
is a diffeomorphism.
∎

Moreover, we also have:

Lemma 27
.

If
g,h\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, then
\theta_{g,1}\circ\theta_{h,1}=\theta_{gh,1}

Proof.

We will change
\theta_{g,1}
to
\theta_{g,\epsilon_{h}}
, where
\epsilon_{h}
is the (polar) parameterization of the ellipse determined by
h\cdot S^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which begins at the point on the positive
x
axis. We use the same idea as before: this composition can be broken down into

\theta\circ g\circ X|_{\epsilon_{h}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi/Z)\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}\circ\theta\circ h\circ X|_{\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}.

It suffices to show that the maps
X|_{\epsilon_{h}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi/Z)\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}
and
\theta
compose to the identity. The input here is the graph of
\epsilon_{h}
. Now,

X|_{\epsilon_{h}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi/Z)\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}\circ\theta(\epsilon_{h}(\varphi)\cos(\varphi),\epsilon(\varphi)\sin(\varphi))=X|_{\epsilon_{h}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi/Z)\times\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}(\theta(\epsilon_{h}(\varphi)\cos(\varphi),\epsilon(\varphi)\sin(\varphi)))

=X(\epsilon_{h}(\varphi),\theta(\epsilon_{h}(\varphi)\cos(\varphi),\epsilon(\varphi)\sin(\varphi))),

which we have already noted is the graph of
\epsilon_{h}
.
Now since
g\circ h=gh
by definition, we have proven the lemma.
∎

From here, we will drop the
\gamma
from the
\theta_{g,\gamma}
notation without loss of generality. Now, we may state the following proposition.

Proposition 28
.

For all
r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
, and
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, there is a unique map
g\cdot r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
with the property that

gX(r(\varphi),\varphi)=X((g\cdot r)(\theta_{g}(\varphi)),\theta_{g}(\varphi))

for all
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, the assignment
(g,r)\mapsto g\cdot r
is a group action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\theta(gX(r(\varphi),\varphi))=\theta_{g}(\varphi)
by definition. Now,
|gX(r(\varphi),\varphi)|
is a function of
\varphi
, and is continuous since
r
is. Now, since
\theta_{g}
is a diffeomorphism, we may write
|gX(r(\varphi),\varphi)|
in
\theta_{g}(\varphi)
coordinates by substituting
\theta_{g^{-1}}(\theta_{g}(\varphi))
for
\varphi
. This gives a continuous function

|gX(r(\theta_{g^{-1}}(\theta_{g}(\varphi))),\theta_{g^{-1}}(\theta_{g}(\varphi)))|=(g\cdot r)(\theta_{g}(\varphi))

in terms of
\theta_{g}(\varphi)
.

To show that this is a group action, note first that for the identity
I\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
we have
\theta_{I}(\varphi)=\varphi
, so that
IX(r(\varphi),\varphi)=X(r(\varphi),\varphi)
so
r=I\cdot r
. Let
g,h\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
. On one hand,

ghX(r(\varphi),\varphi)=X((gh\cdot r)(\theta_{gh}(\varphi)),\theta_{gh}(\varphi))

and on the other

g(hX(r(\varphi),\varphi))=gX((h\cdot r)(\theta_{h}(\varphi)),\theta_{h}(\varphi))=X((g\cdot(h\cdot r))(\theta_{g}\theta_{h}(\varphi)),\theta_{g}\theta_{h}(\varphi))

=X((g\cdot(h\cdot r))(\theta_{gh}(\varphi)),\theta_{gh}(\varphi))

so that
gh\cdot r=g\cdot(h\cdot r)
.
∎

In general, computing
\theta_{g}
for a random element of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, and consequently
g\cdot r
, can be rather difficult. However, we have already seen that for rotation matrices
\kappa_{\varphi}
, this re-parameterization is just
\theta_{\kappa_{\varphi}}(\theta)=\theta+\varphi
and
(\kappa_{\varphi}\cdot r)=r
. Furthermore, this can be done in general using the following decomposition.

Proposition 29

(Cartan Decomposition)
.

Let
G=GL(2,\mathbb{R})^{+}
(or
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
) and
K=SO(2,\mathbb{R})
(or
K=O(2,\mathbb{R})
). Every double coset in
K\backslash G/K
has a unique representative

\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}&0\\
0&d_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with
d_{i}\in R
and
d_{1}\geq d_{2}>0
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
1
]
for the proof.

The Case of Ellipses

We would now like to study the Hlawka Zeta Function of ellipses in the plane. The main idea is that Hlawka Zeta function of ellipses are understood to be either Epstein Zeta functions,
(E.f_{0})(g,s_{1},s_{2})
for a particular

, or
E(z,s)
for a certain
z
. This gives a functional equation
s\mapsto 1-s
, which can be interpreted in terms of the
\widehat{r^{2s}}(4q)
as combinatorial identities of a sort.

Finding a Functional Equation

Consider the following situation: we have an ellipse
e
with center at the origin, major axis length
a
, minor axis length
b
, and is rotated
\varphi
degrees counterclockwise about the origin. This is the image of the unit circle under the shear transformation
g_{e}=\kappa_{-\varphi}\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}
. This means the equation of an ellipse is given by
|g_{e}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}|^{2}=1
. We would like to calculate the Hlawka Zeta function for such an object. Let us first consider

Definition 33
.

Let
u\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
be a positive definite symmetric matrix. Then the
Epstein Zeta Function
is

\mathcal{E}(u,s)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}}(x^{T}ux)^{-s}.

It is a theorem of linear algebra that every positive definite symmetric form
u
can be written as
g^{T}g
for some
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
. Thus the expression in the sum is

(x^{T}g^{T}gx)^{-s}=((gx)^{T}(gx))^{-s}=|gx|^{-2s}

for some
g\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
. In the notation of Section 1, if
D
is the ellipse
e
, then the
t(m,n)
’s for the ellipse are
t(m,n)^{2}=|g_{e}^{-1}(m,n)|^{2}
. Summing over all
(m,n)
, we get

Proposition 34
.

Set
g_{e}
as before. The Hlawka Zeta function for an ellipse determined by
g_{e}
is

Z(r,s)=\mathcal{E}(^{T}g_{e}^{-1}g_{e}^{-1},s)
.

Proof.

Clear.
∎

Now, from
[
6
]
, the Epstein Zeta function has a functional equation

\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\mathcal{E}(u,s)=\pi^{-(1-s)}\Gamma(1-s)\det(u)^{-1/2}\mathcal{E}(u^{-1},n/2-s)

for
u\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
. Then the Hlawka Zeta function also has a functional equation

\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)Z(r,s)=\pi^{-(1-s)}\Gamma(1-s)(ab)^{-1/2}Z(r^{\ast},1-s)

(8)

Where
r^{\ast}
is the ellipse determined by
{}^{T}g_{e}^{-1}
in the same way as before. What we would now like to do is interpret this functional equation in terms of the formula in terms of Eisenstein Series.

Interpreting the Functional Equation

From the previous section, we know that the action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on our polar functions
r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
is essentially determined by the Cartan decomposition of
g
. For
g_{e}
, this decomposition is clearly
g_{e}=b\kappa_{-\varphi}\begin{pmatrix}a/b&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}
. Thus the action on the constant function, i.e. the unit circle,
r=1
of
g_{e}
is

(g_{e}.r)(\theta)=ab\left(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}\right)\cos^{2}(\theta-\varphi)\right)^{-1/2}.

We would like to find the Fourier coefficients of
(g_{e}.r)(\theta)^{2s}
for any
s
. Since we know the constant
a
will simply become
a^{s}
and the action by
\kappa_{-\varphi}
is known, it suffices to find the Fourier coefficients for

\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{2}\right)\cos^{2}(\theta).

First, set
\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}=c>0
, and
d=1-c
. Thus we have reduced the problem to finding the Fourier Coefficient of

E(\theta)=(c+d\cos^{2}(\theta))^{s}.

Notably, this is even, so we only need to know
\int_{0}^{2\pi}E(\theta)\cos(q\theta)d\theta
. We begin by recalling the extended binomial theorem. For
|x|>|y|>0
and
r\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

(x+y)^{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{r\choose k}x^{r-k}y^{k}

where
{r\choose k}=\frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-k+1)}{k!}=\frac{\Gamma(r+k)}{k!\Gamma(r)}
.
In this case,

\left|\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\right|>\left|\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}-1\right|\geq\left|\left(1-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)\cos^{2}(\theta)\right|,

since
a>b
. Now,

(c+d\cos^{2}(\theta))^{s}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{s\choose k}c^{s-k}d^{k}\cos^{2k}(\theta).

We evaluate

\cos^{2k}(\theta)=(\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}))^{2k}=\frac{1}{2^{2k}}(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})^{k}=\frac{1}{2^{2k-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}{2k\choose i}\cos(2(k-i)\theta).

Here we would like to isolate a cosine term with
4q\theta
for some
q\in\mathbb{Z}
, since when we integrate the rest of the terms vanish. This means
2q=k-i
so
i=k-2q>0
. Thus the term with
\cos(4q\theta)
is

\sum_{k>2q}{s\choose k}{2k\choose k-2q}\frac{c^{s-k}d^{k}}{2^{2k-1}}\cos(4q\theta).

Thus, upon integrating, we obtain that the
4q
-th Fourier coefficient is

c^{s}\sum_{k>2q}{s\choose k}{2k\choose k-2q}\frac{c^{-k}d^{k}}{2^{2k-1}}.

Reverting to
s\mapsto-s
, we obtain the necessary coefficients

\widetilde{(g_{e}.1)^{2s}}(4q)=(ab)^{2s}c^{-s}\sum_{k>2q}{-s\choose k}{2k\choose k-2q}\frac{c^{-k}d^{k}}{2^{2k-1}}=c^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{2j+4q\choose j}\frac{d^{j+2q}\Gamma(j+2q-s)}{2^{2q-1}c^{j+2q}\Gamma(-s)}(1/2)^{j}.

(9)

in the expression

Z((g_{e}.1),s)=\widetilde{(g_{e}.1)^{2s}}(0)(E^{\sharp}.f_{0})(\kappa_{-\varphi},s)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{(g_{e}.1)^{2s}}(4q)((E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(\kappa_{-\varphi},s)+(E^{\sharp}.f_{-4q})(\kappa_{-\varphi},s))

Now, we want to relate coefficients in the Eisenstein Series, so that by multiplying
Z(r,s)
by
\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)
, we evoke the functional equation just derived for the
E^{\sharp}.f_{q}
’s in each of the coefficients. We have that that
{}^{T}g_{e}^{-1}=\kappa_{-\varphi}\begin{pmatrix}1/a&0\\
0&1/b\end{pmatrix}
will yield the same ellipse as
\kappa_{-\varphi+\pi/2}\begin{pmatrix}1/b&0\\
0&1/a\\
\end{pmatrix}
. This means that
c=a/b=(1/b)/(1/a)
stays the same along with
d
. Now, the only other difference is in the
\kappa_{-\varphi+\pi/2}
, but by our lemma from the previous section,
(E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(\kappa_{-\varphi+\pi/2},s)=(E^{\sharp}.f_{4q})(\kappa_{-\varphi},s)
, so this works out. Thus, the only change in variables is
s\mapsto 1-s
when we multiply the above formula for the Hlawka Zeta Function by
\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)
. Thus we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 35
.

For all
q>0
, we have that

\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)(ab)^{2s}c^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{2j+4q\choose j}\frac{d^{j+2q}\Gamma(j+2q-s)}{2^{2q-1}c^{j+2q}\Gamma(-s)}(1/2)^{j}

=\pi^{-(1-s)}\Gamma(1-s)\frac{\Gamma(s)^{2}}{\Gamma(s+2q)\Gamma(s-2q)}(ab)^{2s-3/2}c^{-(1-s)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{2j+4q\choose j}\frac{d^{j+2q}\Gamma(j+2q-1+s)}{2^{2q-1}c^{j+2q}\Gamma(-1+s)}(1/2)^{j}.

(10)

This is a combinatorial identity in terms of two seemingly unrelated power series. In fact, both of the sums are related in that they may both be interpreted as hypergeometric functions, i.e. functions of the form

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}

defined for
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|<1
, and the notation
(a)_{n}
means
a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
if
n>0
and
1
if
n=0
(This is the so called rising Pochhammer Symbol).

The Case of the Square

It is might be reasonable to ask where the Hlawka Zeta function has a functional equation for general
r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
. For example, let

Z^{\ast}_{r}(s)=\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)Z_{r}(s).

(11)

Then, if we follow the case of ellipses,
Z_{r}^{\ast}(s)
may meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
and satisfies the functional equation.

Z_{r}^{\ast}(s)=Z_{r}^{\ast}(1-s).

This informal conjecture, motivated by the example of the square and other Modular forms, breaks down in the case of the square. One can fairly simply compute the Hlawka Zeta function for the square centered at the origin with side length 2. Here, one may see that
t_{k}=k

It is natural to consider a larger classes of functional equation. We will say that a complex function

has a

for

if

has meromorphic continuation to the entire plane which satisfies

f(s)=\frac{A^{1-s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma(\alpha_{i}(1-s)+\mu_{i})}{B^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{K}\Gamma(\beta_{j}s+\omega_{j})}f(1-s)

with
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
real and positive, and
A,B,\mu_{i},\omega_{j}
complex. This is a bit of a generalization of the functional equation condition of the
“Selberg Class”
discussed in the original paper by Selberg in
[
5
]
.

In our case, as for
L
functions and other modular forms, it seems reasonable to assume that
Z_{r}(1-s)
should be replaced by
Z_{r^{\ast}}(1-s)
, where
r^{\ast}
is another continuous function. Now, we have a well posed question: If
r:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
traces the square, is there a continuous function
r^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

Z_{r}(s)=\frac{A^{1-s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma(\alpha_{i}(1-s)+\mu_{i})}{B^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{K}\Gamma(\beta_{j}s+\omega_{j})}Z_{r^{\ast}}(1-s)

(12)

for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

, and for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
? We will give a negative answer to this question.

In our other question about the fibers of the mapping
r\mapsto Z_{r}(s)
, we have at least one conjecture, mentioned in a previous section. However, this conjecture only applies to the
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
-orbits of an element

implies that

is in the

-orbit of

Functional Equation

The goal of this section is to prove the following.

Theorem 36
.

If
r
traces out the unit square, there is no continuous
r^{\ast}
such that (12) holds for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
A,B,\mu_{i},\omega_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
, and for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
.

We will first need a couple of lemmas here, which was only proven partially in the first section. However, there is a difference in notation between ours and the more standard notation. Let
\{a_{n}\}
,
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be two sequences of real numbers, with
\lambda_{n}
increasing. Then a
Generalized Dirichlet Series
is of the form

f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}s}.

This is an equivalent definition to our
t
-Dirichlet Series given by

(D_{t}.a)(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}t_{n}^{-s}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}e^{-\log(t_{n})s}

and there is a change of variables
\lambda_{n}=\log(t_{n})
. Note that in the case of
Z_{r}(s)
, the change of variables is actually
\lambda_{k}=2\log(t_{k})
, since the
t_{k}
is squared there. Given this, we prove the lemma.

Lemma 37
.

Let
r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
. Then
Z_{r}(s)
converges in the half plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
.

Note throughout this proof, we use the notation of the first section.

Proof.

From
[
2
]
,
Z_{r}(s)
converges for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\gamma
, where

\limsup\frac{\log(A(k))}{2\log(t_{k})}=\gamma,

where
A(k)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{k}
. As noted before,
A_{k}
is in fact the number of lattice points in the region
t_{k}D
. Since
r
is continuous, there is a dilation of the unit square
Q_{1}
with side length
M\in\mathbb{Z}
which bounds the region
D
. Furthermore, there is a square
Q_{2}
entirely contained in
D
with side length
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. The number of lattice points in
t_{k}D
is bounded above by
\lceil t_{k}Q_{1}\rceil
and bounded below by
\lfloor t_{k}Q_{2}\lfloor
, and the number of lattice points in these are
(t_{k}M)^{2}
and
(t_{k}m)^{2}
, respectively. Now,

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{2\log(\lfloor t_{k}m\rfloor)}{2\log(t_{k})}=1\leq\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}{\log(A(k))}{2\log(t_{k})}\leq\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{2\log(\lceil t_{k}M\rceil)}{2\log(t_{k})}=1

since
t_{k}
increases to infinity as
k\to\infty
. Therefore
\gamma=1
.
∎

This immediately gives the following proposition

Proposition 38
.

Let
r\in C(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
. Then
Z_{r}(s)
has a pole at
s=1
.

Proof.

This is clear from Landau’s theorem, which states that if a generalized Dirichlet Series has positive coefficients, then there is a singularity at the real point on the abcissa of convergence. Again, see
[
2
]
.
∎

We need another small lemma relating to the poles of the gamma factors which will be of use later.

Lemma 39
.

Let
S=\{s|s=\frac{k-k_{0}}{\alpha_{i}}+1,k\geq 0\}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. If
\frac{1}{2}+\ell\in S
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
S
contains infinitely many integers in the domain
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}+\ell
.

Proof.

Since
\frac{1}{2}+\ell\in S
, we may conclude that for some integer
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\beta_{i}=\frac{k_{1}-k_{0}}{\frac{1}{2}+\ell}
. Then the elements of
S
are of the form
s=\frac{k-k_{0}}{k_{1}-k_{0}}(\ell+\frac{1}{2})+1
. Then
s
is an integer greater than
\frac{1}{2}+\ell
as long as
\frac{k-k_{0}}{k_{1}-k_{0}}
is an even positive integer. As
k
ranges through positive integers, this happens infinitely many times.
∎

We are now in position to prove the theorem.

Proof.

Suppose there is
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j},\mu_{i},\omega_{j},A,B
as in the definition as above:

8\zeta(2s-1)=\frac{A^{1-s}\prod\Gamma(\alpha_{i}(1-s)+\mu_{i})}{B^{s}\prod\Gamma(\beta_{j}s+\omega_{j})}Z(r^{\ast},1-s)

for some continuous
r^{\ast}
.
Now, this should be the same as for
s\mapsto 1-s
so that

8\zeta(1-2s)=\frac{A^{s}\prod\Gamma(\alpha_{i}s+\mu_{i})}{B^{1-s}\prod\Gamma(\beta_{j}(1-s))+\omega_{j})}Z(r^{\ast},s).

Multiplying both sides by
\pi^{s-1/2}\Gamma(1/2-s)
, we obtain

8\pi^{s-1/2}\Gamma(1/2-s)\zeta(1-2s)=8\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta(2s)=\frac{\pi^{s-1/2}A^{s}\Gamma(1/2-s)\prod\Gamma(\alpha_{i}s+\mu_{i})}{B^{1-s}\prod\Gamma(\beta_{j}(1-s)+\omega_{j})}Z(r^{\ast},s)

Which means, if we multiply through, that

8\pi^{-s}B^{1-s}\Gamma(s)\prod\Gamma(\beta_{j}(1-s)+\omega_{j})\zeta(2s)=\pi^{s-1/2}A^{s}\Gamma(1/2-s)\prod\Gamma(\alpha_{i}s+\mu_{i})Z(r^{\ast},s)

From here, we analyze the poles and holomorphicity of the two sides of this equation in the region
\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq 1
.

Let
P_{L}
and
P_{R}
be the set of poles of the left and right hand sides in
D=\{s|\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq 1\}
. These two sets are assumed equal. The poles of the left hand side are, explicitly,

P_{L}=D\cap\left(\bigcup\left\{s|s=\frac{k+\omega_{j}}{\beta_{j}}+1,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}\right)=D\cap\left(\bigcup S_{j}\right)

where
S_{j}
is the set of poles of
\Gamma(\beta_{j}(1-s)+\omega_{j})
. The set
P_{R}
is more delicate.
The
\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-s)
contributes poles at
\{s|s=k+\frac{1}{2},k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
. In this region, the product of gamma factors
\Gamma(\alpha_{i}s+\mu_{i})
contributes only finitely many poles and no zeros. From
[
2
]
, we know that
Z(r^{\ast},s)
has finitely many zeros in this region, and the lemma states that
Z(r^{\ast},s)
has a pole at
s=1
. Since the LHS has no zeros here, we conclude that the only zeros of
Z(r^{\ast},s)
may only cancel out the poles of the other factors on the RHS. Thus,

P_{R}=D\cap\left(\{1\}\cup\{s|s=\frac{1}{2}+k,k\in\mathbb{Z},k\geq N\geq 0\}\right)\cup F,

where
F
is the finite set of poles coming from the gamma factors and not canceled by
Z(r^{\ast},s)
, and
N
depends on the zeros of
Z(r^{\ast},s)
. Note that for some
c
,
\{s|\operatorname{Re}(s)>c\}\cap P_{R}
contains infinitely many half integers. Furthermore,
P_{R}
contains only finitely many integers.

Now, since
P_{L}=P_{R}
,
1\in P_{L}
. This means that one of the
S_{j}
, which we will assume without loss of generality is
S_{1}
, is the set

S_{1}=\left\{s|s=\frac{k-k_{0}}{\beta_{1}}+1,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}

for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. The set
S_{1}\subset P_{R}
as well. Now, since
S_{1}
contains elements of arbitrarily large real part,
S_{1}
must also contain a half integer. By the lemma, then
S_{1}
must contain infinitely many integers as well. This contradicts the fact that
P_{R}
contains only finitely many integers.
∎

Notably, this proof relied on the pole of
Z_{r}(s)
at
s=1
, and this relies heavily on analytic properties of
r
. The pole there made the set of poles
“uneven”
in a sense.

An Odd Shape

We describe a shape which has the same Hlawka Zeta Function as the square. Its boundary is the union of the following lines:

L_{1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|-1\leq x\leq 1,y=-1\},

L_{2}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|-1\leq y\leq 1,x=-1\},

L_{3}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|-1\leq x\leq 0,y=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}x\},

L_{4}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|0\leq x\leq 1,y=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}x\},

L_{5}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|1\leq x\leq 2,y=1\},

L_{6}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|1\leq x\leq 0,y=x-1\},

L_{7}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|-1\leq y\leq 0,x=1\}.

This shape, call it
O
, has the same area as the square centered at the origin with side length 2. One can show the following about this shape fairly simply:

Proposition 40
.

The shape
O
has
t_{k}=k
and
a_{k}=8k
. Thus, if
Q
is the square, then
Z(O,s)=Z(Q,s)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
t_{k}=k
, and it is also clear that this region is star-shaped as well. Now, the idea for the
a_{k}
is that the vertical and horizontal lines account for the amount of lattice points as the square does, so it suffices to show that the
“corner”
and
“bump”
account for the same amount of lattice points. This is readily checked.
∎

This sort of counterexample is fairly degenerate, but shows that if there is a conjecture to be made about the fibers of
r\mapsto Z_{r}(s)
, perhaps there need to be a stronger condition on the class of functions considered.

7
Final Remarks

Further Study

There are a number of open questions still about the Hlawka Zeta Function, but these results should lend some light on the situation. One might be tempted to pursue the following:

(1): The methodology that was used to find interpret the functional equation for the ellipse may be able to find any sort of general functional equation. Namely, one can use the Fourier series of
r(\theta)
in terms of
\cos^{n}(\theta)
and
\sin^{n}(\theta)
, then use the extended binomial theorem and basis change to find the
4q
Fourier coefficients of
r(\theta)^{s}
. This will involve even more, far more difficult combinatorics. Then, if
Z(r,s)
has a functional equation
s\mapsto 1-s
, the functional equation of the
(E^{\sharp}.f_{q})(I,s)
should say something about how the Fourier coefficients change.

(2): While we have found an example where
Z_{r}(s)=Z_{r^{\ast}}(s)
where
r^{\ast}
is not in the
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
-orbit of
r
, this does not disprove our conjecture about the stabilizer of the induced action of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
Z_{r}(s)
,
g\cdot Z_{r}(s)=Z_{g\cdot r}(s)
. This conjecture is still open. Furthermore, we have reason to believe that the following conjecture may be true:

Conjecture 41
.

Let
r\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},(0,\infty))
, i.e.
r
is continually differentiable. Then
Z_{r}(s)=Z_{r^{\ast}}(s)
implies that
r^{\ast}
is in the
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
orbit of
r
.

This conjecture has basis in our examination of the asymptotics of
t_{k}
and
a_{k}
.
