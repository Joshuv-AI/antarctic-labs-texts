On primitive integer solutions of the Diophantine equation x 3 ± y 3 = a k ± b k x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}\pm b^{k}
Maciej Ulas

Abstract
. In this note we consider the title Diophantine equation from both theoretical as well as experimental point of view. In particular, we prove that for k = 4 , 6 k=4,6 and each choice of the signs our equation has infinitely many co-prime positive integer solutions. For k = 5 , 7 k=5,7 and all choices of the signs we computed all co-prime positive integer solutions ( x , y , a , b ) (x,y,a,b) satisfying the condition max ⁡ { a , b } ≤ 50000 \operatorname{max}\{a,b\}\leq 50000 .

11D41, 11P05, 11Y50

1.
Introduction

For given
k\in\{4,5,6,7\}
we consider the Diophantine equation

(1)

x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}\pm b^{k},

where the signs are chosen independently, and we ask about the existence of co-prime positive integer solutions. It should be noted that the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
has infinitely many solution in integers. This is a special case of a theorem proved by Lander
[
4
]
, which can be easily generalized to the case of equations with different signs. However, the solutions constructed by Lander method are not co-prime. If
k>3
, then the question concerning the existence of co-prime solutions is more difficult. The main reason is that the polynomial
x^{3}\pm y^{3}-(a^{k}\pm b^{k})\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,a,b]
is not homogenous and it is not clear whether is it possible to eliminate common divisors of
x,y,a,b
. Of course, this is possible in the case when a common factor, say
d=\gcd(x,y,a,b)
, satisfy
d^{k}|x,y
and
d^{3}|a,b
. Then we can divide both sides by
d^{3k}
and get a solution in co-prime integers. However, usually the value of
d
does not satisfy such condition.

Recently the equation (
1
) with both the signs “
+
” was investigated from a computational point of view by Wagstaff
[
7
]
. In particular, in the cited paper the author computed all co-prime positive solutions in the range
a\leq b\leq M
, where
M=10000,5000,1400,700
for
k=4,5,6,7
, respectively. Moreover, two heuristic reasonings were presented, which suggest that if
k=4,5,6
, then the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers, and only finitely many in the case
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 7}
. Motivated by findings of Wagstaff we investigate the more general equation (
1
) both from a computational and a theoretical point of view. To avoid trivial solutions, we assume that the numbers
x,y,a,b
are co-prime, positive and each sub-sum in the expression
x^{3}\pm y^{2}-(a^{k}\pm b^{k})
is unequal zero.

Let us describe the content of the paper in some details. In Section
2
we prove that for
k=4
and each choice of the signs the Diophantine equation (
1
) has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers. An analogous result is proved for the case of
k=6
in Section
3
. Numerical results of extensive computations concerning the Diophantine equation (
1
) for
k=5,7
are presented in Section
4
. Finally, in the last section we present some additional remarks and question concerning the equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
for
k=4,6
. In the Appendix we present the tables of solutions of the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{5}=a^{5}\pm b^{5}
with
0<b<a\leq 50000
.

2.
The equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}\pm b^{4}
in positive integers

In this section we investigate the equation (
1
) for
k=4
. We start with the following simple result.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each choice of the sign the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}-b^{4}
has infinitely many primitive solutions in positive integers.

Proof.

In case of the “
+
” sign we take co-prime positive integers
u,v
of different parity and note the identity

(2u^{3}v)^{3}+(2uv^{3})^{3}=(u^{3}+v^{3})^{4}-(u^{3}-v^{3})^{4}.

In particular, for given
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, the number of co-prime positive integer solutions of
0<x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{4}-b^{4}\leq N
is
\gg N^{1/9}
.

To get the solutions in the case of “
-
” we performed small numerical search of the positive integer solutions of the equation
x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{4}-b^{4}
in the range
0<b<a<10^{3}
. In this range there are 197 co-prime solutions. Quick analysis of the set of solutions revealed that the
a
coordinate very often satisfies the condition
a=3(2t+1)^{2}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Using this property we quickly found that the corresponding value of
b
is
b=(3t+1)(3t+2)
. Having conjectured form of
a,b
in terms of
t
, we quickly found the corresponding expressions for
x,y
. More precisely, we have the following identity

(15t^{3}+33t^{2}+21t+4)^{3}-(15t^{3}+12t^{2}-1)^{3}=(3(2t+1)^{2})^{4}-((3t+1)(3t+2))^{4}.

Because for each
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
we have
\gcd(3(2t+1),(3t+1)(3t+2))=1
we get infinitely many solutions of our equation. In consequence, the number of co-prime positive integer solutions of
0<x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{4}-b^{4}\leq N
is
\gg N^{1/8}
.
∎

The equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
is more difficult. On the beginning of our investigations we were trying to find polynomial solutions of our equation. However, we were unable to do so and decide to investigate these equations using a different approach. More precisely, instead of working with the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
, we were working with the simpler equation
X^{3}+Y^{3}=A^{4}+B^{2}
and looked for a solution in (not necessarily co-prime) polynomials. In fact, we assumed the existence of polynomials
X,Y,A,B\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
with
\operatorname{deg}X=\operatorname{deg}Y=3,\operatorname{deg}A=2
such that
X(t)^{3}+Y(t)^{3}=A(t)^{4}+tB(t)^{2}
and then replace
t
by
x^{2}
. To simplify computations we put

X(t)=t(t^{2}-(a+b)t+c),\;Y(t)=X(t)=t(t^{2}-(a-b)t+c)

and
A(t)=t(pt+q),B(t)=t^{2}(rt+s)
, where
a,b,c,p,q,r,s
need to be determined. Under these assumptions we have
X(t)^{3}+Y(t)^{3}-A(t)^{4}-tB(t)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{4}C_{i}t^{i+4}
, and consider the system of equations
C_{i}=0
for
i=0,1,2,3,4
. It can be easily solved with respect to
a,b,c,q,s
and we get

a=-\frac{4p^{3}q+r^{2}}{2p^{4}},\quad b=-\frac{p^{4}}{6},\quad c=\frac{q^{2}}{p^{2}},\quad q=-\frac{p^{16}+27r^{4}}{432p^{3}r^{2}},\quad s=\frac{p^{16}+27r^{4}}{432p^{4}r}.

To find the simplest possible expressions for the polynomials we are looking for, we take
p=6d,r=144d^{2}
, where
d
is a variable, and replace
t
by
x^{2}
. After all necessary simplifications we obtain the identity

(2)

f_{1}(x)^{3}+f_{2}(x)^{3}=g_{1}(x)^{4}+g_{2}(x)^{2},

where

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2}(x^{4}-6(81d^{8}-36d^{4}-1)x^{2}+(243d^{8}+1)^{2}),

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-x^{2}(x^{4}-6(81d^{8}+36d^{4}-1)x^{2}+(243d^{8}+1)^{2}),

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=6dx^{2}(x^{2}-243d^{8}-1),

\displaystyle g_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=(12dx^{2})^{2}x(x^{2}+243d^{8}+1).

We are ready to prove the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

For each choice of the sign the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers.

Proof.

We consider the identity (
2
) with
d=2
.
For
d>0
we define the following sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\;f_{1}(x)>0\;\mbox{and}\;f_{2}(x)>0\}=(-r_{2},-r_{1})\cup(r_{1},r_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\;f_{1}(x)>0\;\mbox{and}\;f_{2}(x)<0\}=(-\infty,-r_{2})\cup(-r_{1},r_{1})\cup(r_{2},+\infty),

where
r_{1}=\sqrt{63933-2\sqrt{54367202}},r_{2}=\sqrt{63933+2\sqrt{54367202}}
. Observe that
f_{2}(x)=0
if and only if
x\in\{\pm r_{1},\pm r_{2}\}
.
In particular, the sets
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
are infinite and contains infinitely many rational numbers. After these initial observations we note that if for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
the value
g_{2}(x_{0})
is a square, then there exists
y_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that the point
(x_{0},y_{0})
lies on the elliptic curve

E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+62209x.

Let
(x,y)
be a rational point on
E
. Then, there are
u,v\in\mathbb{Z},w\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying the condition
\gcd(uv,w)=1
and
x=u/w^{2},y=v/w^{3}
. Equivalently the integers
u,v,w
satisfy the identity
v^{2}=u^{3}+62209uw^{4}
. Thus, by substituting
x=u/w^{2}
and multiplying of both sides by
w^{36}
, we can rewrite the identity (
2
) in the following way

\displaystyle(3464u^{4}w^{4}+(v^{2}-2u^{3})^{2})^{3}

\displaystyle+(3448u^{4}w^{4}-(v^{2}-2u^{3})^{2})^{3}

\displaystyle=(12uw(2u^{3}-v^{2}))^{4}+(24u^{2}vw^{2})^{4},

To finish the proof we need to show how from the above identity we can construct co-prime solutions. Let
g=\gcd(u,v)
. We write
u=pg,v=qg,w=r
, where
\gcd(p,q)=1
and
\gcd(pq,r)=1
and
gq^{2}=g^{2}p^{3}+62209pr^{4}
. After dividing by
g^{12}
we get that

h_{1}^{3}+h_{2}^{3}=h_{3}^{4}+h_{4}^{4}
, where

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle=3464p^{4}r^{4}+(q^{2}-2gp^{3})^{2},

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle=3448p^{4}r^{4}-(q^{2}-2gp^{3})^{2},

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=12p(2gp^{3}-q^{2})r,

\displaystyle h_{4}

\displaystyle=24p^{2}qr^{2}.

We show that
G=\gcd(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4})=1
. Suppose not and let
s
be a prime divisor of
G
. Then
s|h_{1}+h_{2}=2^{8}3^{3}p^{4}r^{4}
. If
s|p
, then, because
s|h_{1}
, we get
s|q^{4}
- a contradiction. If
s|r
, then
s|q^{2}-2gp^{3}
and
s|g(q^{2}-gp^{3})
. Thus
s|g(q^{2}-gp^{3})-g(q^{2}-2gp^{3})=g^{2}p^{3}
. However, we have
\gcd(s,gp)=1
and get a contradiction. We thus proved that each prime divisor of
G
is a divisor of
6
. If
s=2
, then
2|q
and thus
2|v
. In particular
w
is odd and
w^{4}\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)
. If
d
is odd then
4|v^{2}=u^{3}+(243d^{8}+1)uw^{4}
and thus
2|u
- a contradiction. If
d
is even, then
4|u^{3}+u
and again
2|u
- a contradiction. If
s=3
, then from the condition
3|h_{1}-h_{2}
we get
3|p^{4}r^{4}
. If
3|p
, then
3|u
and
3|v
- a contradiction. Similarly, if
3|r
, then
3|q^{2}-2gp^{3}
and
3|g(q^{2}-gp^{3})
. If
3|g
, then
3|q
- a contradiction. We thus get
3|q^{2}-2gp^{3}
and together with the condition
2|q^{2}-2gp^{3}
we get that
3|gp^{3}
- a contradiction. Summing up, we have proved that if the curve
E
has infinitely many rational points, then the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
has infinitely many solutions in (not necessarily positive) integers. However, we show that one can find infinitely many solutions with
y
positive (and negative).

Let us back to the curve
E
. One can easily check that the torsion part of
E(\mathbb{Q})
has order 2 and
\operatorname{Tors}E(\mathbb{Q})=\{(0,0),\mathcal{O}\}
. As computed with the help of Pari GP
ellrank
procedure, the rank of
E
is equal to 2 and the generators of the infinite part of
E(\mathbb{Q})
are given by

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{67600}{51^{2}},\frac{169584220}{51^{3}}\right),

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{29686962975500601}{1803016^{2}},\frac{5116943017644569380192365}{1803016^{3}}\right).

Let us observe that if
(x,y)\in E(\mathbb{Q})
, then necessarily
x>0
. Moreover, the equation
x^{3}+62209x=0
has only one real root
x=0
. Thus, the set
E(\mathbb{R})
of all real points on
E
is connected. Invoking Hurwitz theorem
[
3
]
, we know that the set
E(\mathbb{Q})
is dense in the set
E(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, if we write
P_{m,n,\varepsilon}=[m]G_{1}+[n]G_{2}+\varepsilon T=(x_{m,n,\varepsilon},y_{m,n,\varepsilon})
, then the set

\mathcal{W}=\{x_{m,n,\varepsilon}:\;m,n\in\mathbb{N},\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}\}

is dense in the set of positive real numbers. In particular, both sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{W},\mathcal{A}_{2}\cap\mathcal{W}

are infinite. Thus, our construction guarantees, that for each triple
m,n,\varepsilon
such that
f(x_{m,n,\varepsilon})>0
(
f(x_{m,n,\varepsilon})<0
) we get a solution of the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
(of the equation
x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
) in co-prime positive integers.

Finally, let us recall that if
E
is an elliptic curve and
P
is a point of infinite order on
E
, then coordinates of
[n]P
grows like
C^{n^{2}}
for some
C>0
. Thus, one can deduce that the number of co-prime positive solutions of
0<x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}\leq N
satisfies
\gg(\log N)^{1/10}
.
∎

3.
The equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{6}\pm b^{6}
in positive integers

Let us recall the following well known identity of Mahler
[
5
]

(9x^{4})^{3}+(3x-9x^{4})^{3}=(9x^{3}-1)^{3}+1.

By simple computation, we note the following equalities of sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\;3x-9x^{4}>0\wedge 9x^{3}-1>0\}=(3^{-2/3},3^{-1/3}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\;3x-9x^{4}<0\wedge 9x^{3}-1<0\}=(-\infty,0),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\;3x-9x^{4}>0\wedge 9x^{3}-1<0\}=(0,3^{-2/3})

In particular, for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the set
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathbb{Q}
is infinite.

By taking
x=u/v
and multiplying both sides by
v^{12}
we get the identity

(3)

(3u^{2})^{6}+(3u(v^{3}-3u^{3}))^{3}=(v(9u^{3}-v^{3}))^{3}+v^{12}.

Let us observe that that if
\gcd(u,v)=1
and
\gcd(v,3)=1
, then

\gcd(3u^{2},3u(v^{3}-3u^{3}),v(9u^{3}-v^{3}),v)=1.

If
\gcd(u,v)=1
and
\gcd(v,3)=3
, then we replace
v
by
3v
and by dividing by
3^{6}
we get the identity

(u^{2})^{6}+(u(9v^{3}-u^{3}))^{3}=(3v(u^{3}-3v^{3}))^{3}+(3v^{2})^{6},

where
\gcd(u^{2},u(9v^{3}-u^{3}),3v(u^{3}-3v^{3}),3v^{2})=1
.

As an immediate consequence of (
3
) we get the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

For each choice of the sign the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{6}-b^{6}
has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers.

Proof.

To get positive integer solutions of the equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{6}-b^{6}
, it is enough to rewrite (
3
) as

(3u(v^{3}-3u^{3}))^{3}-(v(9u^{3}-v^{3}))^{3}=v^{12}-(3u^{2})^{6}.

Indeed, in the case of “
-
” sign, we note that for
u/v\in\mathcal{B}_{1},\gcd(v,3)=1
, and we get co-prime positive solutions
x=3u(v^{3}-3u^{3}),y=v(9u^{3}-v^{3}),a=v^{2},b=3u^{2}
in positive integers. In particular, for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
we can take
u=3t+1,v=6t+1
. We then have that
u/v\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
. In consequence, the number of co-prime positive integer solutions of
0<x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{6}-b^{6}\leq N
is
\gg N^{1/12}
. Note that one can also take
u/v\in\mathcal{B}_{2},\gcd(v,3)=1
and switch the role of
x,y
.

On the other hand, in the case of “
+
” sign, we just take
u/v\in\mathcal{B}_{3},\gcd(v,3)=1
and get co-prime solutions
x=3u(v^{3}-3u^{3}),y=|v(9u^{3}-v^{3})|,a=v^{2},b=3u^{2}
in positive integers. In particular, for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
we can take
u=t,v=3t+1
. We then have that
u/v\in\mathcal{V}_{3}
. In consequence, the number of co-prime positive integer solutions of
0<x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{6}-b^{6}\leq N
is
\gg N^{1/12}
.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For each choice of the signs the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{6}+b^{6}
has infinitely many primitive solutions in positive integers.

Proof.

We use the identity (
3
) again. First we show that the Diophantine equation
w^{2}=3u(v^{3}-3u^{3})
has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers
(u,v,w)
. By substituting

v=\frac{up}{3r^{2}},\quad w=\frac{u^{2}q}{3r^{3}},

and dividing by
u^{4}
, we reduce the equation
w^{2}=3u(v^{3}-3u^{3})=:g(u,v)
to the equation
q^{2}=p^{3}-81r^{6}
. Equivalently, we are interested in rational points on elliptic curve
E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}-81
with
x=p/r^{2},y=q/r^{3}
. One can easily check that the curve
E
has trivial torsion. The rank of
E
is equal to 1 and the generator of
E(\mathbb{Q})
is
P=(x_{1},y_{1})=(13,46)
. In particular the set
E(\mathbb{Q})
is infinite. Now, for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let us write

[n]P=\left(\frac{p_{n}}{r_{n}^{2}},\frac{q_{n}}{r_{n}^{3}}\right).

It is clear that
p_{n}/r_{n}^{2}>3^{4/3}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. We know that
\gcd(p_{n}q_{n},r_{n})=1
. Moreover, due to the fact that the equation
x^{3}-81=0
has only one real root, the set
E(\mathbb{R})
of all real points on
E
is connected. Thus, invoking Hurwitz theorem again, we know that the set
E(\mathbb{Q})
is dense in the set of
E(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, the set

\mathcal{V}=\{p_{n}/r_{n}^{2}:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}

is dense in the set
(3^{4/3},+\infty)
in the Euclidean topology.

To get the solutions of
w^{2}=3u(v^{3}-3u^{3})
in co-prime positive integers
u,v
, it is enough to take

(4)

u_{n}=3r_{n}^{2},\quad v_{n}=p_{n},\quad w_{n}=3q_{n}r_{n},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. The fact that
(u_{n},v_{n},w_{n})
is a solution of our equation follows from the equality

w_{n}^{2}-g(u_{n},v_{n})=9r_{n}^{2}(q_{n}^{2}-(p_{n}^{3}-81r_{n}^{6}))=0.

To finish the proof of our theorem, we observe that from the inequality
p_{n}/r_{n}^{2}>3^{4/3}
we get the inequality
u_{n}/v_{n}=3r_{n}^{2}/p_{n}<3^{-1/3}
. From the density of the set
\mathcal{V}
we get that there are infinitely may values of
n
such that
u_{n}/v_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
. Thus, the integers

x=v_{n}(9u_{n}^{3}-v_{n}^{3}),\quad y=v_{n}^{4},\quad a=3u_{n}^{2},\quad b=w_{n}

solve the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{6}+b^{6}
.

Using similar reasoning, we get that there are infinitely many values of
n
such that
u_{n}/v_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{3}
and get infinitely many co-prime positive integers

x=v_{n}^{4},\quad y=|v_{n}(9u_{n}^{3}-v_{n}^{3})|,\quad a=3u_{n}^{2},\quad b=w_{n},

which solve the equation
x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{6}+b^{6}
.

Finally, using the same reasoning as in the case of the equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
, one can deduce that the number of co-prime positive solutions of
0<x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{6}+b^{6}\leq N
satisfies
\gg(\log N)^{1/4}
.
∎

4.
The equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}\pm b^{k}
for
k=5,7

We were unable to prove that for
k\in\{5,7\}
there is a choice of the signs such that the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}\pm b^{k}
has infinitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers. However, we extended computations done by Wagstaff. Let us recall that in Wagstaff’s work to find all positive solutions of the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
a method presented in
[
2
]
was used. The method is based on the comparison of the elements of two tables: one involving the sums
x^{3}+y^{3}
and the second one containing the sums
a^{k}+b^{k}
. In the case of
k=5
the range was
a\leq b\leq 5000
(with appropriate bounds for
x,y
). In the case of
k=7
the range was
a\leq b\leq 700
. Because we consider slightly more general equations involving signs we used different method.

For given
A\in\mathbb{Z}
let us describe the method which can be used to find
all
integer solutions of the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=A
. First of all, let us note that this is a very particular case of Thue equation, i.e., an equation of the form
F(x,y)=A
, where
F\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
is a binary form of degree
\geq 2
. In our case, the method of solution is based on the factorization of
A
. More precisely, one can compute the set
D(A)=\{d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}:\;d|A\;\mbox{and}\;d^{2}<A\}
. Then, for given
d\in D(A)
, we compute the solutions of the system

x+y=d,\quad x^{2}-xy+y^{2}=\frac{A}{d}

and get

x=\frac{3d^{2}\pm\sqrt{3d(4A-d^{3})}}{6d},\quad y=\frac{3d^{2}\mp\sqrt{3d(4A-d^{3})}}{6d}.

and check whether
x,y
are integers. In case of small values of
A
this method is very quick and allows to find all integer solutions of the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=A
. The drawback of this method is the necessity of finding full factorization of
A
and enumerate the set
D(A)
. On the other hand, in this way we can find all integer solutions of
x^{3}+y^{3}=A
(to get this we also allow negative divisors of
A
, i.e., the values
-d
with
d\in D(A)
). This is useful property because during one computation we can get positive solutions of the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=A
as well as positive solutions of the equation
x^{3}-y^{3}=A
. This procedure can be implemented from scratch in any computer algebra system allowing factorizations of integers. Fortunately, a more general version of this approach, where instead of particular form
x^{3}+y^{3}
we can work with any
reducible
form, is implemented as a subroutine in the procedure
thue()
in Pari GP. More precisely, for a given form
F\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
and
A\in\mathbb{Z}
the function
thue
attempts to compute the set of integer solutions of the Thue equation
F(x,y)=A
. If
F
is reducible, say
F(x,y)=F_{1}(x,y)F_{2}(x,y)
, the procedure works as follows
[
1
]
. First, the following resultant
R(u,v,y)=\operatorname{Res}_{x}(F_{1}(x,y)-u,F_{2}(x,y)-v)
is computed. Next for any
d\in D(A)
we consider the polynomial
S(y)=R(d,A/d,y)
and it is checked whether the equation
S(y)=0
has an integer root. If
S(y_{0})=0
for some
y_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then the equation
F_{1}(x,y_{0})=0
is checked for the integer roots. In this way all integer solutions of
F(x,y)=A
can be computed. To initialize the search in Pari GP we need to use procedure
thueinit
. To see this in action we take
F(x,y)=x^{3}+y^{3}
and
A=31213
:

gp> T=thueinit(F(x,1));

gp> thue(T,31213)

[[-35, 42], [21, 28], [28, 21], [42, -35]]

Thus, the only integer solutions of
x^{3}+y^{3}=31213
are
(-35,42),(21,28)
and its permutations.

Remark 4.1
.

Let us note, that in the case of a general (irreducible) form, to increase to speed of the computations the procedure
thue
assumes validity of Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. If we need to have unconditional result, an additional flag during the initialization of
thueinit
need to be introduced. More precisely, instead of
thueinit(F(x,1))
we need to write
thueinit(F(x,1),1)
. Fortunately, as was explained above, in our case of the form
F(x,y)=x^{3}+y^{3}
there is no need to do that. More information concerning the solver of Thue equations implemented in Pari GP can be found in the user’s guide available through
[
6
]
.

.

For each choice of the signs the equation

In this direction one can note the identity

(8(9t^{5}+1))^{3}-(8(9t^{5}-1))^{3}=4^{5}+(12t^{2})^{5},

which shows that the equation
x^{3}-y^{3}=a^{5}+b^{5}
has infinitely many positive solutions satisfying the condition

Heuristic presented in Wagstaff’s paper predicts that the Diophantine equation

has only finitely many positive co-prime solutions do not satisfying

. His computations confirms that there is no positive co-prime solutions with

x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{7}+b^{7}
in the range

and

. In this range we found only two solutions which lead to the following identities

\displaystyle 1250534^{3}-637445^{3}

\displaystyle=51^{7}+402^{7},

\displaystyle 7^{3}-6^{3}

\displaystyle=2^{7}-1^{7}.

It is unclear whether Wagstaff’s heuristic gives real prediction and the equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=a^{7}+b^{7}
, as well as more general equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{7}\pm b^{7}
, has only finitely many solutions in co-prime positive integers. Our experience suggests the opposite and the identity

(108t^{7}+4)^{3}-(108t^{7}-4)^{3}=2^{7}+(108t^{2})^{7},

show the existence of infinitely many positive solutions of the equation

satisfying the condition

.

5.
The case
k=4,6
again

In the light of our results concerning the existence of infinitely many solutions of
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
in co-prime positive integers for
k=4,6
, it is natural to ask whether there is a solution in co-prime polynomials. We were unable to find one but using your computational approach we calculated solutions in the range
0<b<a\leq 50000
in case
k=4
. In the case of “
+
” sign we found 219 solutions. In the case of “
-
”sign we found 409 solutions. Because in the considered range the corresponding sets of solutions are relatively big, the expectation that there is a polynomial solution is reasonable. On the other hand, for
k=6
, in the range
0<b<a<25000
we found exactly 23 co-prime positive integer solutions of the equation with the “
+
” sign. In the same range we found 66 co-prime positive integer solutions of the equation with the “
-
” sign. Although our numerical computations are not so convinced as in the case
k=4
, we formulate the following.

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
k=4,6
. For each choice of the sign there is a solution of the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}+b^{k}
in co-prime polynomials with integer coefficients and positive leading terms.

In this context it would be interesting to find infinitely many values of
d
such that the elliptic curve

E_{d}:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+(243d^{8}+1)x

has positive rank. This curve appeared in the course of the proof of existence of infinite many
x\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
g_{2}(x)
is a square, where
g_{2}(x)
appeared in (
2
). Let us note that for each
d
of the form
d=2^{k}3^{l}
the reasoning used in the proof of Theorem
2.2
guarantees the existence of infinitely many co-prime positive solutions of the equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
provided that
E_{d}
has positive rank. Small numerical search shows that for the following pairs
(k,l)
lead to the elliptic curve
E_{d}
with positive rank:

(k,l)=(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(0,5),(1,0),(4,3),(5,0),\ldots

We expect that there are infinitely many such pairs but we were unable to prove such a statement. It should be easier to prove that there are infinitely many values of
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that the curve
E_{d}
has positive rank. However, note that if
d\neq 2^{k}3^{l}
, then some additional work need to be done to guarantee that the rational points on
E_{d}
lead to co-prime solutions of
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{4}+b^{4}
.

We proved that for
k=4,6
and each choice of the signs, the Diophantine equation
x^{3}\pm y^{3}=a^{k}\pm b^{k}
has infinitely many solutions and in the course of the proofs we obtained lower bounds for the quantity

However, our bounds are very weak and we formulate the following.

Question 5.2
.

Let
k\in\{4,6\}
and
N
be large positive number. What is the true order of magnitude of
C_{k}(\pm,\pm)(N)
?
