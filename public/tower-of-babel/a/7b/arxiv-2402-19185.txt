Properties of Hagen-Poiseuille flows in channel networks
Ana Filipa Valente, Rodrigo Almeida, Rui Dilão

Abstract
We derive the main properties of adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows in elastic microchannel networks akin to biological veins in organisms. We show that adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows successfully simulate key features of Physarum polycephalum networks, replicating physiological out-of-equilibrium phenomena like peristalsis and shuttle streaming, associated with the mechanism of nutrient transport in Physarum . A new topological steady state has been identified for asynchronous adaptation, supporting out-of-equilibrium laminar fluxes. Adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows show saturation effects on the fluxes in contractile veins, as observed in animal and artificial contractile veins.

I
Introduction

Channel networks are ubiquitous transportation and communication structures in nature
Takahashi, 2014
.
This is the case of organ and tumour irrigation veins
Rubinow, 1973
;
Chang et al., 2019
, nutrient transporting channels in plant leaves
Katifori et al., 2010
, microorganisms with adaptive channel networks like
Physarum polycephalum

Baumgarten et al., 2010
;
Oettmeier et al., 2017
, transportation systems
Tero et al., 2006
;
Hu and Cai, 2013
or electrical networks
Bohn and Magnasco, 2007
. These systems can be described using graphs and compared to formal systems like the travelling salesman problem
Zhu et al., 2018
or optimal Steiner tree geometries
Tero et al., 2010
. These are difficult network optimisation problems in graph theory and have important applications in physics, biology and engineering.

Physarum polycephalum
, also known as slime mould, is a protist which exhibits complex behaviour. Its body consists of several nuclei (syncytium) and an adaptive network of veins transporting endoplasmatic fluid from food sources to different body regions. This network’s geometry and adaptability are responsible for
Physarum
’s growth and motility
Dove et al., 1985
. The vein walls of the network channels exhibit periodic transversal oscillations regulated by food sources and other external stimuli
Wohlfarth, 1979
. Even though slime mould lacks any neural system or centralised control, it shows high-level behaviour, such as choosing the shortest path between food sources in a maze
Nakagaki et al., 2000
.

To recreate and understand the dynamics of growth and movement of
Physarum
, several different models have been developed to study the formation and optimisation of network paths
Tero et al., 2010
;
Almeida, 2021
;
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
;
Valente, 2023
. These models were based on the phenomenology of Hagen-Poiseuille flows on graphs.

From all the features of the formation and adaptation of channel networks in
Physarum
, some physical phenomena are poorly explained. This is the case of the validation of Murray’s law at the branching points of channels
Murray, 1926
;
Sherman, 1981
, oscillations of channel radius and inversion of stream velocities (peristalsis and shuttle streaming), conductivity saturation in elastic vein networks, non-unicity of steady-state solutions of adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille (H-P) flows, and suboptimal and optimal channel lengths in steady-state solutions of H-P network flows. In these flows, adaptability is associated with the transverse elasticity of channels.

This paper focuses on the properties of the adaptive H-P flows described above, identifying the origin of these phenomena. We will follow the Hagen-Poiseuille formalism, a Navier-Stokes type approach with a low Reynolds number, imposing fluid mass conservation and energy minimisation as developed by Almeida
et al
Almeida, 2021
;
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
.
In the next section, we describe the exact results of the temporal evolution of adaptive H-P flows in channel networks embedded in
n
-dimensional Euclidian spaces. In section
III
, we derive the properties of the steady states of the channel flows and their spatial geometries, and we validate the Murray law for adaptive H-P flows at steady state. In section
IV
, we compare the steady-state H-P flow patterns with
Physarum
patterns. In section
V
, we analyse shuttle streaming, and in section
VI
, we analyse peristalsis, both observed in out-of-equilibrium flows. Finally, in section
VII
, we summarise the paper’s results.

II
Adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows on graphs: exact results

The fluid flow in elastic straight channel networks of several systems, such as in
Physarum polycephalum
or blood vessels in organisms, can be described as the flow of viscous and incompressible fluids, with several sources and sinks and adaptive conductivities.

A network of veins is described by an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},E)
, embedded in a
n
dimensional Euclidean space, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of
N
nodes or vertices with coordinates
(x_{i}^{1},...,x_{i}^{n})
, with
i=1,...,N
, and
E
is the set of
M
straight edges
(i,j)
connecting the nodes
i
and
j
. We assume that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is connected, having only one component.

As we will describe out-of-equilibrium flows, the graph
\mathcal{G}
is generated through the Delaunay triangulation of a square lattice of size
m\times m
, embedded in a unit square, where a white Gaussian stochastic algorithm modified the coordinates of the interior points. The edges of
\mathcal{G}
represent elastic cylindrical channels that can expand or contract transversely to the flow.
This network has
K
sources and
R
sinks at fixed nodes. The input or output fluid flux at node
j
is represented by
S_{j}
. If the network has a source at node
j
, then
S_{j}>0
. If the network has a sink at node
j
,
S_{j}<0
. Otherwise,
S_{j}=0
. As the fluid is incompressible,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}S_{j}=\sum_{j:\text{sources}}S_{j}+\sum_{j:\text{sinks}}S_{j}=0,

(1)

and the volume of fluid in the network is constant.

The edge connecting node
i
to node
j
has fixed length
L_{ij}
and (variable) radius
r_{ij}
. Edges have (variable) conductivities
D_{ij}=\pi r_{ij}^{4}/8\eta
, where
\eta
is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, and
\sqrt{D_{ij}}\simeq r_{ij}^{2}
is proportional to the area of the channel.
The volume of the channel connecting node
i
to node
j
is
V_{ij}=\pi r_{ij}^{2}L_{ij}=\sqrt{8\pi\eta}L_{ij}\sqrt{D_{ij}}
. As the fluid is incompressible, the volume of fluid in the network is constant and is given by

V=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}V_{ij}=\beta\sum_{(i,j)\in E}L_{ij}\sqrt{D_{ij}},

(2)

where
\beta=\sqrt{8\pi\eta}
.

The Kirchhoff law determines the fluxes at every node
j
. As fluid flux is conserved, we have

\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}Q_{ij}=S_{i}\quad,i=1,...,N.

(3)

The H-P flux law states that the flux of fluid in a cylindric channel connecting node
i
to node
j
depends on the pressures at the nodes and is determined by the relation

Q_{ij}=D_{ij}\frac{(p_{i}-p_{j})}{L_{ij}}.

(4)

Biological elastic channels show transverse oscillations regulated by their flux
Rubinow and Keller, 1972
;
Wohlfarth, 1979
. To describe these fluxes, we consider the network of channels
\mathcal{G}
filled with an incompressible fluid, where the channel conductivities have a positive value. Assuming that all the fluxes
S_{i}
are known, by the H-P relationship (
4
), the pressures
p_{i}
at the vertices are obtained by solving the Kirchhoff laws (
3
). Further assuming that the veins are elastic and the conductivity can change in time, we have:

Theorem II.1
(
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
)

Consider a H-P flow in a channel network described by a connected graph
\mathcal{G}
. Assuming that the volume
V
of fluid in the network is constant over time, then the conductivity of the elastic channels change in time according to the adaptation law

\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{D_{ij}}=\alpha\frac{g(Q_{ij})}{\sum_{(k,m)\in E}L_{km}g(Q_{km})}-\sqrt{D_{ij}}\ ,\quad(i,j)\in E,

(5)

where
g(\cdot)
is some arbitrary function describing the elasticity of channels,
\alpha=V/\beta
is a constant, and
t
is the adimensional time.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}Q_{ij}^{2}L_{ij}/D_{ij}
be the dissipated power of the H-P flow. To minimise the dissipated power while also keeping the total volume of fluid constant, we consider the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{P}-\lambda\left(V-\beta\sum_{(k,m)\in E}L_{km}\sqrt{D_{km}}\right)
, where
\lambda
is a Lagrange multiplier. Then we have:

Theorem II.2
(
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
)

Assuming that the dissipated energy per unit time of the H-P flow is minimum, then

g(Q_{ij})=Q_{ij}^{2/3}.

(6)

From these two theorems, the adaptive H-P flow has steady states obeying the conditions

D_{ij}^{*}=\alpha^{2}\frac{Q_{ij}^{4/3}}{\left(\sum_{(k,m)\in E}L_{km}Q_{ij}^{2/3}\right)^{2}}\ \ \hbox{or}\ \ D_{ij}^{**}=0.

(7)

The two theorem
II.1
and
II.2
lead to the generalised Murray’s law:

Corollary II.3
(
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
)

Assuming that the dissipated energy per unit time of the H-P flow is minimum, then

\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E,Q_{ij}>0}r_{ij}^{3}=\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E,Q_{ij}<0}r_{ij}^{3},

(8)

for every node
j
of the graph
\mathcal{G}
.

Under the above construction, we can simulate out-of-equilibrium and steady laminar fluxes on networks of channels with fixed sources and sinks. The transverse adaptability of the network veins will determine a steady-state graph that connects sources and sinks.

III
Properties of the adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows

To study the properties of the adaptive H-P flow described by the above theorems, we restrict our simulations to channel networks described by planar graphs embedded in the two-dimensional Euclidean space. This approach easily extended to
n
-dimensional Euclidian space through a graph embedding.

As biological networks evolve in time and adapt in response to the intensity of flows, we start with graphs with small length edges
resulting from a Delaunay triangulation. The triangulation is generated from a distribution of nodes in a square lattice with their positions randomly perturbed with Gaussian noise, with a standard deviation
\sigma=0.5
. The lengths of each edge
L_{ij}
are calculated based on the coordinates of nodes
i
and
j
. Some of these nodes can be sources or sinks, and equation (
1
) is always verified. The flow is initialised by randomly choosing positive arbitrary conductivities
D_{ij}(t=0)>0
.

As equation (
2
) shows, the initial distribution of conductivities determines the total fluid volume in the network of channels, thus affecting the value of the parameter
\alpha=V/\beta
in equation (
5
). To maintain the volume of fluid constant for different initial conditions, we first choose the volume of fluid
V
. As for any initial choice of conductivities,
V_{0}=\beta\sum_{(i,j)\in E}L_{ij}\sqrt{D_{ij}(t_{0})}
, we renormalize the initial conductivities to the new values

D_{ij}^{\prime}(t=0)=\left(\frac{V}{V_{0}}\right)^{2}D_{ij}(t=0).

(9)

Therefore, simulations for different initial conductivities and volumes can be compared. This renormalisation condition is important as steady-state flows depend on the distribution of initial conductivities.

To describe the temporal evolution of the adapted radii and fluid flows in all the network channels, we start by calculating the initial pressures at all the graph nodes. Then, channel conductivities are adapted according to equations (
5
), in time increments according to the following rules:
After each time step
\Delta t
, new conductivities and new pressures and fluxes are calculated. To find the pressure at each node
(i,j)
, we define
N
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{S}
, whose
i
th entries are
p_{i}
and
S_{i}
, respectively. The system of equations (
3
) is rewritten in the matrix form
\mathbf{Wp}=\mathbf{S}
, where
\mathbf{W}
is an
N\times N
symmetric matrix with entries
w_{ij}=\left(\sum_{(i,j\in E)}C_{ik}\right)\delta_{ij}-C_{ij}
, and
C_{ij}=D_{ij}/L_{ij}
. We obtain the pressures at each graph node after solving this linear system of equations. By equation (
4
), the newly adapted fluxes
Q_{ij}
are then obtained. To simplify these calculations, without loss of generality, we assume that out-flow node pressures are zero.

We used the Euler method to numerically integrate equations equations (
5
), with
\Delta t=0.1
. Some parameters are kept constant for all the simulations, namely
\beta=1
,
V=100
and
\sum_{j\in\{\text{sources}\}}S_{j}=1
.

These steps are repeated over time until a steady state of the channel conductivities is reached. The steady state is reached when the condition

\text{max}_{(i,j)\in E}\left|D_{ij}(n\Delta t)-D_{ij}\left((n-1)\Delta t\right)\right|<D_{\text{thr}}

(10)

is fulfilled. In all our simulations, we made the choice
D_{\text{thr}}=5\times 10^{-6}
, where
n
is the first integer for which the inequality (
10
) is verified.

III.2
Validation of Murray’s law

The generalised Murray’s law (
8
) is a direct consequence of the set of equations used to determine the steady state of the adaptive H-P flow, but it is unknown whether or not it is verified during out-of-equilibrium states.

To verify the transient behaviour of the H-P flow, the source and sink setup of figure
2
were used, with initial conductivities

D_{ij}(t=0)=1
, for every pair
(i,j)\in E
. We have chosen two branching points of the steady state, and we have analysed the temporal evolution of the quantity

\left|R_{\text{out}}(t)-R_{\text{in}}(t)\right|:=\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:(i,j)\in E\\
Q_{ij}<0\end{subarray}}r_{ij}^{3}(t)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:(i,j)\in E\\
Q_{ij}>0\end{subarray}}r_{ij}^{3}(t)\right|

The Murray’s law is verified if the quantity
\left|R_{\text{out}}(t)-R_{\text{in}}(t)\right|

III.3
Properties of simple networks

The formalisms of adaptive H-P flows can be applied to simple networks with fixed sources and sinks, and the fluid flows through all the channels.

III.3.1
One channel networks

We apply the conductivity adaptation algorithm to a single channel of length
L_{12}
, with one source and sink. Assuming that the source is located at the node number
1
and the sink at node number
2
, the adaptation equation
is

\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{D_{12}}=\alpha\frac{1}{L_{12}}-\sqrt{D_{12}}=\sqrt{D_{12}(0)}\frac{1}{L_{12}}-\sqrt{D_{12}},

(11)

where we have used the relations
\alpha=V/\beta
,
\beta=\sqrt{8\pi\eta}
,
V=\beta L_{12}\sqrt{D_{12}(0)}
and
\eta
is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid.
The fluxes at the nodes
1
and
2
are
Q_{12}=I_{1}>0
and
Q_{21}=-I_{1}
.
Equation (
11
) is linear and its general solution is

D_{12}(t)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{L_{12}}-\left(\frac{\alpha}{L_{12}}-\sqrt{D_{12}(0)}\right)e^{-t}\right)^{2}=D_{12}(0).

(12)

The steady state is
D_{12}^{*}=\lim_{t\to\infty}D_{12}(t)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{12}^{2}}=D_{12}(0)
.

As
D_{12}=\pi r_{12}^{4}/(8\eta)
, and the flux is
Q_{12}=D_{12}(p_{1}-p_{2})/L_{12}
, at the steady state we have

Q_{12}^{*}=D_{12}^{*}\frac{1}{L_{12}}(p_{1}(\infty)-p_{2})=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{L_{12}^{3}}p_{1}(\infty)=I_{1},

(13)

where
p_{1}(\infty)
is determined by the flux at the source
Q_{12}=I_{1}
, and we made the choice
p_{2}=0
.

The steady-state solution
Q_{12}^{*}
in (
13
) shows that, for fixed volume
V
(
\alpha
constant), the flux at the source increases linearly with the pressure. The radius of the channel is constant for every
t\geq 0
and is determined by
D_{12}(0)
.

5
, we show the relationship between the pressure and channel length and source fluxes.

III.3.2
Two channels networks

In the case of two channels with two sinks connected to a single source with an input flux
I_{1}
, the adaptation equations for the Hagen-Poiseuille flow are

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{D_{12}}=\alpha\frac{Q_{12}^{2/3}}{L_{12}Q_{12}^{2/3}+L_{13}Q_{13}^{2/3}}-\sqrt{D_{12}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{D_{13}}=\alpha\frac{Q_{13}^{2/3}}{L_{12}Q_{12}^{2/3}+L_{13}Q_{13}^{2/3}}-\sqrt{D_{13}}\end{array}

(14)

with node fluxes

Q_{12}+Q_{13}=I_{1}>0,\ Q_{21}=I_{2}<0,\ \hbox{and}\ Q_{31}=I_{3}<0

(15)

and the conservation law
I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}=0
.

Introducing the relations (
15
), into (
14
), these equations simplify and become linear. Therefore, the steady-state conductivities are

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle D_{12}^{*}=\alpha^{2}\frac{|I_{2}|^{4/3}}{(L_{12}|I_{2}|^{2/3}+L_{13}|I_{3}|^{2/3})^{2}}\\
\displaystyle D_{13}^{*}=\alpha^{2}\frac{|I_{3}|^{4/3}}{(L_{12}|I_{2}|^{2/3}+L_{13}|I_{3}|^{2/3})^{2}},\end{array}

(16)

where
\alpha=V/\beta=(L_{12}\sqrt{D_{12}(0)}+L_{13}\sqrt{D_{13}(0)})
. Therefore, as
D_{12}^{*}\leq\alpha^{2}/L_{12}^{2}
and
D_{13}^{*}\leq\alpha^{2}/L_{13}^{2}
, we have

\left(D_{12}^{*}\frac{1}{L_{12}}+D_{13}^{*}\frac{1}{L_{13}}\right)p_{1}(\infty)=I_{1}.

(17)

Therefore, for fixed volume
V
, at the steady state, the pressure at the source is linear as a function of the flux at the source. However, during time evolution, the flow will adapt for the conductivity values
D_{12}^{*}
and
D_{13}^{*}
.

17
) shows that the steady-state pressure at the source depends on the lengths of the channels.

VII
Conclusions

We have derived the main properties of adaptive Hagen-Poiseuille flows in channel networks, simulating the transverse elasticity of vein networks in organisms.

We have shown that the model tested produces results consistent with physical observations, namely the tendency of the adaptation to select channels close to the shortest path connecting sources and sinks. The lengths of the steady-state adapted veins connecting sources and sinks are suboptimal, at least for finite triangular geometries, refuting the hypothesis of the possibility of solving travelling salesman or Steiner geometry problems in graphs.

Additionally, Murray’s law, experimentally observed in several biological networks, is verified only for steady-state H-P flow regimes.

The adaptive H-P flow description applied to simple one-channel and two-channel networks predicts the behaviour of the pressure in contractile veins as observed in several organisms. This process is associated with conductivity saturation, which results in a threshold in the fluid fluxes at the source. Increasing the pressure at the source the flux remains constant.

We have compared the
Physarum
’s radial growth pattern of veins for fixed sources and sinks, and sinks activated randomly in time – asynchronous adaptation. In the latter, the pattern characteristics of
Physarum
networks were evident, showing fewer thicker veins closer to food sources, branching out into thinner veins as the periphery approached. A new topological steady state has been identified, supporting out-of-equilibrium fluxes.
In these topological steady states, shuttle streaming and peristalsis are observed. These out-of-equilibrium effects are also observed in
Physarum
. These simulations show that these out-of-equilibrium effects observed in
Physarum
may have a hydrodynamic origin.
