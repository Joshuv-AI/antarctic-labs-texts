Semi-algebraic description of the closure of the image of a semi-algebraic set under a polynomial
Ngoc Hoang Anh Mai

Abstract
Given a polynomial f f and a semi-algebraic set S S , we provide a symbolic algorithm to find the equations and inequalities defining a semi-algebraic set Q Q which is identical to the closure of the image of S S under f f , i.e., Q = f ⁡ ( S ) ¯ . Q=\overline{f(S)}\,. (1) Consequently, every polynomial optimization problem whose optimum value is finite has an equivalent form with attained optimum value, i.e., min t ∈ Q ⁡ t = inf x ∈ S f ⁡ ( x ) \min\limits_{t\in Q}t=\inf\limits_{x\in S}f(x) (2) whenever the right-hand side is finite. Given d d being the upper bound on the degrees of f f and polynomials defining S S , we prove that our method requires O ⁡ ( d O ⁡ ( n ) ) O(d^{O(n)}) arithmetic operations to produce polynomials of degrees at most d O ⁡ ( n ) d^{O(n)} that define f ⁡ ( S ) ¯ \overline{f(S)} .



Keywords:
semi-algebraic set; Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem; quantifier elimination; sum of squares; Nichtnegativstellensatz; polynomial optimization; semidefinite programming

1
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
stand for the ring of polynomials of real coefficients in vector of variables
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.
The set
W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called elementary semi-algebraic, if it can be written in the form

W=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,p_{1}(x)>0,\dots,p_{m}(x)>0\,,\,q_{1}(x)=\dots=q_{l}(x)=0\}\,,

(3)

where
p_{i},q_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
We call
p_{i}
(resp.
q_{j}
) inequality (resp. equality) polynomial defining
W
.
A set is called semi-algebraic, if it is the union of a finite number of elementary semi-algebraic sets.
Denote by
\overline{A}
the closure of a set
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
in the usual topology on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Known as a central result of real algebraic geometry, Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem
[
22
,
21
]
says that the projection of a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
onto the space spanned by the first
n-1
coordinates is semi-algebraic.
Using a finite number of arithmetic operations, we can write down explicitly the equations and inequalities defining this projection.
Therefore, algorithms that allow us to do so are fundamental and widely used in various applications of real algebraic geometry.

Contribution.

In this paper, we provide an algorithm to symbolically compute polynomials defining the closure of the image of a semi-algebraic set
S
under a polynomial
f
.
Moreover, we obtain explicitly
\overline{f(S)}
as a finite union of closed semi-algebraic sets of the form

\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,g_{1}(t)\geq 0,\dots,g_{v}(t)\geq 0\,,\,h_{1}(t)=\dots=h_{k}(t)=0\}\,,

(4)

where
g_{i},h_{j}
are univariate polynomials.
Our method consists of the following three steps:

1.

By Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem, the image
f(S)
is a semi-algebraic set. Moreover, we obtain its (in)equality polynomials by using Basu’s algorithm in
[
1
, Section 3]
.

2.

We then convert these polynomial descriptions to new ones defining elementary semi-algebraic sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{r}
whose union is
f(S)
such that the following conditions hold:

(a)

Each inequality polynomial defining
B_{j}
has only simple roots.

(b)

Any inequality polynomial defining
B_{j}
has no root in the variety determined by the equality polynomials defining
B_{j}
.

(c)

Any couple of inequality polynomials determining each
B_{j}
has no common root.

3.

Replacing strict inequalities defining
B_{j}
with non-strict ones, we obtain a new semi-algebraic set
\widetilde{B}_{j}
.
We guarantee that the closure
\overline{f(S)}
is identical to the union of
\widetilde{B}_{j}
s.

The main tools utilized in the last two steps are the fundamental theorem of algebra and the greatest common divisor.

Let
d
be an upper bound on the degrees of
f
and polynomials defining
S
.
Our method has a complexity of
O(d^{O(n)})
to return polynomials of degrees at most
d^{O(n)}
that define
\overline{f(S)}
.
This complexity follows from the complexity of Tarsi–Seidenberg’s theorem analyzed by Basu in
[
1
, Theorem 1]
.

(Another possible way is to rely on Basu’s method in
[
1
, Section 2.1, Example]
for computing the closure of a semi-algebraic set to find the semi-algebraic description of
\overline{f(S)}
.
However, we are also not sure if this method allows us to obtain
\overline{f(S)}
as a finite union of closed semi-algebraic sets of the form (
4
), on which we can build some Nichtnegativstellensätze for univariate polynomials.)

Motivation.

One of the applications of our method is to address the attainability issue in polynomial optimization.
More explicitly, consider

f^{\star}=\inf_{x\in S}f(x)\,,

(5)

where
f
is a polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
S
is a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Assume that the optimum value
f^{\star}
is finite, i.e.,
f^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

It is well-known that when
S
is a compact semi-algebraic set of the form

\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,p_{1}(x)\geq 0,\dots,p_{m}(x)\geq 0\,,\,q_{1}(x)=\dots=q_{l}(x)=0\}\,,

(6)

the optimal value
f^{\star}
is approximated from below as closely as desired by the sum-of-squares strengthenings introduced by Lasserre in
[
11
]
.
If
f
attains
f^{\star}
on non-compact
S
of form (
6
), we can approximately compute
f^{\star}
using Mai–Lasserre–Magron’s in
[
15
]
.
Moreover, tools that allow us to compute
f^{\star}
exactly in Demmel–Nie–Powers’ and Mai’s work
[
5
,
14
]
require the attainability of
f^{\star}
.

However, the problem (
5
) possibly has no optimal solution (i.e.,
f^{\star}
is not attained).
For instance, if
f=x_{1}
and
S=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,x_{1}x_{2}^{2}=1\}
, then
f^{\star}=0
is not attained by
f
on
S
.

We now formulate (
5
) as
f^{\star}=\inf f(S)
.
A simple idea to make
f^{\star}
attained is replacing the image
f(S)
with its closure.
This gives

f^{\star}=\min\overline{f(S)}

(7)

since
f^{\star}\in\overline{f(S)}
.
The challenge is to transform (
7
) back to a polynomial optimization problem with attained optimum value
f^{\star}
.
In other words, we find a polynomial
g
and a semi-algebraic set
Q
such that

f^{\star}=\min_{y\in Q}g(y)\,.

(8)

Our method allows us to obtain
Q=\overline{f(S)}
and
g
as the identity polynomial in a single variable, i.e.,
g(t)=t
.
In principle,
f(S)
is a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
and therefore is the union of finitely many intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{s}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Here
I_{j}
s are of the following forms:
(a,b)
,
[a,b)
,
(a,b]
,
[a,b]
,
(a,\infty)
,
[a,\infty)
,
(-\infty,b]
,
(-\infty,b]
,
[a,a]=\{a\}
with
-\infty<a<b<\infty
.

The closure
\overline{f(S)}
is then the union of
\overline{I_{1}},\dots,\overline{I_{s}}
, where each
\overline{I_{j}}
contains
I_{j}
and its endpoints.
Note that the endpoints of
I_{j}
s are the real roots of (in)equality polynomials defining
f(S)
.
It leads to using numerical methods to find the real roots of a system of univariate polynomials.
In contrast, our method relies only on symbolic computation to obtain the equations and inequalities determining
\overline{f(S)}
.

It remains to solve the problem (
8
),
where
Q=\overline{f(S)}
is a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
and
g
is the identity polynomial.
In this case, we can exactly compute the optimum value
f^{\star}
by using the sum-of-squares strengthenings under the boundedness assumption of
f(S)
.

[

]
, the author provides a symbolic algorithm to compute polynomials defining the image

when

).
In this case,

.

[

Regarding the attainability issue for the polynomial optimization problem (

), we refer the readers to
[

,

,

,

]
.
In
[

]
, Schweighofer deals with the case where

and

is not attained by

.
He replaces the gradient ideals used in Nie–Demmel–Sturmfels’ method in
[

]
with the gradient tentacles to obtain appropriate sum-of-squares strengthenings for the problem (

).
Hà and Pham handle the constraint case of

of form (

) in

]
.
They propose the truncated tangency variety

for

and

with property saying that

on

.
It also allows them to obtain sum-of-squares strengthenings for the problem (

).
Dinh, Ha, and Pham provide in
[

]
a Frank–Wolfe type theorem for

bounded from below on

.
It says that if the polynomial map

on

.
In
[

, Pham derives versions at infinity of the Fitz John and Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions to deal with the case where

of form (

He also indicates the existence of polynomial

and semi-algebraic set

such that (

For comparison purposes, our method in this paper does not require any assumption on

and

except the finiteness of

.
Besides, we convert the original problem (

-dimensional space to equivalent form (

The methods in

Preliminaries

2.1
Projections of semi-algebraic sets and quantifier elimination

We restate Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem in the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Let
S
be a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
be the projector onto the space spanned by the first
n-1
coordinates.
Then the projection
\pi(S)
is semi-algebraic.

A first-order formula in the language of real closed fields with free variables
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is obtained as follows recursively:

1.

If
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
,
n\geq 1
, then

f=0
and
f>0
are first-order formulas and
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,f(x)=0\}
and
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,f(x)>0\}
are respectively the subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that the formulas
f=0
and
f>0
hold.

2.

If
\varphi
and
\psi
are first-order formulas, then
\varphi\wedge\psi
(conjunction),
\varphi\lor\psi
(disjunction)
and
\lnot\varphi
(negation) are also first-order formulas.

3.

If
\varphi
is a first-order formula and
x
is a variable ranging over
{\mathbb{R}}
, then
\exists x\varphi
and
\forall x\varphi
are first-order formulas.

The formulas obtained by using only rules 1 and 2 are called quantifier-free
formulas.

It follows from definitions that a subset
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is semi-algebraic if and only if
there exists a quantifier-free formula
\varphi
such that

S=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,\varphi(x)\}\,.

(9)

Eliminating the quantifier from
\exists x_{n}\varphi
, is the same as computing a quantifier-free description of the image

\pi(S)=\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\,:\,\exists x_{n}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})\}\,,

(10)

where
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
and
\pi
is the projector defined as in Lemma
1
.
Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem (Lemma
1
) allows us to replace
\exists x_{n}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})
by some quantifier-free formula with vector of variables
x^{\prime}
.

The following example is to illustrate the equivalence between eliminating a quantifier and computing semi-algebraic description of the projection of a semi-algebraic set:

Example 1
.

Since
\exists t(at^{2}+bt+c=0)

is equivalent to the quantifier-free formula
(a\neq 0\wedge b^{2}-4ac\geq 0)\lor(a=0\wedge b\neq 0)\lor(a=0\wedge b=0\wedge c=0)
, the projection of semi-algebraic set

\{(a,b,c,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\,:\,at^{2}+bt+c=0\}

(11)

onto the space spanned by the first three coordinates is semi-algebraic set

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,:\,a\neq 0,b^{2}-4ac\geq 0\}\\
\cup&\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,:\,a=0,b\neq 0\}\\
\cup&\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,:\,a=b=c=0\}\,.\end{array}

(12)

Basu suggests his quantifier elimination algorithm in
[
1
, Section 3]
.
We use his method in the first step of our main algorithm stated later.

Remark 1
.

Heintz, Roy, and Solernó analyze the complexity of Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem in
[
8
]
.
An improvement is given by Basu in
[
1
]
using terms of quantifier elimination.
Let
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a semi-algebraic set determined by
s
polynomials of degree at most
d
.
Basu’s method requires
s^{2}d^{nO(1)}
arithmetic operations to find polynomials of degrees upper bounded by
d^{O(1)}
that define the projection of
S
onto the space spanned by the first
n-1
coordinates.

2.2
Polynomial images of semi-algebraic sets

A mapping
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called semi-algebraic if its graph
\{(x,\varphi(x))\,:\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\}
is a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+d}
.
The following lemma follows from Tarski–Seidenberg’s theorem (Lemma
1
):

Lemma 2
.

Let
S
be a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a semi-algebraic mapping.
Then the image
\varphi(S)
is semi-algebraic.

Remark 2
.

Korkina and Kushnirenko provide in
[
9
]
an algorithm to find the equations and inequalities defining the image
\varphi(S)
(see also Coste’s book
[
4
, Sections 1 and 2]
).

The following lemma is a trivial consequence of Lemma
2
and Remark
2
:

Lemma 3
.

Let
f
be a polynomial and
S
be a semi-algebraic set.
Then the image
f(S)
is a semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Moreover, there is a symbolic algorithm that produces the equations and inequalities defining
f(S)
.

Next, we analyze the complexity of the algorithm mentioned in Lemma
3
:

Remark 3
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a semi-algebraic set determined by
s
polynomials of degree at most
d
.
Let
f
be a polynomial of degree at most
d
.
It is not hard to prove that
f(S)
is the projection of semi-algebraic set

\{(x,x_{n+1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\,:\,x_{n+1}=f(x)\,,\,x\in S\}

(13)

onto the space spanned by the last coordinate.
It is equivalent to eliminating a block of
quantifiers of length
n
.
Thanks to the complexity given in
[
1
, Theorem 1]
, it requires
s^{n+1}d^{O(n)}
arithmetic operations to find the polynomials of degrees upper bounded by
d^{O(n)}
defining
f(S)
.

2.3
Closures of semi-algebraic sets in high-dimensional spaces

Set
\|x\|_{2}^{2}:=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}
.
We recall some properties of semi-algebraic sets in the following lemma:

Lemma 4
.

The following statements hold:

1.

With
A
and
B
being semi-algebraic subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the sets

A\cup B
,
A\cap B
, and
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash A
are also semi-algebraic.

2.

The closure of a semi-algebraic set is semi-algebraic.

3.

A semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
is a finite union of intervals and points in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

The first and third statements are given in
[
19
, Proposition 1.1.1]
and
[
19
, Example 1.1.1]
, respectively. The second one is proved by Coste in
[
4
, Corollary 2.5]
as follows:
Let
S
be a semi-algebraic set defined as in (
9
).
The closure of
S
is

\overline{S}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,(\forall\varepsilon)(\varepsilon>0)(\exists y)(\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}<\varepsilon^{2})\wedge\varphi(y)\}\,.

(14)

and can be written as

\overline{S}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash(\pi_{1}(\{(x,\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,,\,\varepsilon>0\}\backslash\pi_{2}(B))),

(15)

where

B=\{(x,\varepsilon,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,(\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}<\varepsilon^{2})\wedge\varphi(y)\}\,,

(16)

\pi_{1}(x,\varepsilon)=x
and
\pi_{2}(x,\varepsilon,y)=(x,\varepsilon)
. Then observe that
B
is semi-algebraic.
Hence
\overline{S}
is semi-algebraic thanks to Lemma
1
and the first statement.
∎

Remark 4
.

In
[
12
]
, Magron, Henrion, and Lasserre approximate the closure
\overline{S}
in Lemma
4
using semidefinite programming.
Basu develops in
[
1
, Section 2.1, Example]
a symbolic method to compute the closure of a semi-algebraic set
S
defined as in (
9
), where
\varphi(x)
is a quantifier-free first order formula involving
s
polynomials with degrees bounded by
d
.
It is based on quantifier elimination and has complexity
s^{2(n+1)}d^{O(n^{2})}
.
His idea is to eliminate two blocks of
quantifiers corresponding to
\varepsilon
and
y
for the description of
\overline{S}
in (
14
).

Remark 5
.

With
\varphi
and
S
being as in Lemma
1
, the closure
\overline{\varphi(S)}
is semi-algebraic thanks to the second statement of Lemma
4
.
By the third statement of Lemma
4
, the image
f(S)
in Lemma
3
is a finite union of points and intervals of
{\mathbb{R}}
.
It implies that the closure
\overline{f(S)}
is semi-algebraic and therefore is a finite union of closed intervals and points of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.4
Fundamental theorem of algebra and greatest common divisor

Let
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
be the ring of polynomials in single-variable
t
.
Let
\deg(p)
stand for the degree of a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.

We restate the fundamental theorem of algebra in the following lemma:

Lemma 5
.

Every polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
of positive degree can be decomposed uniquely as:

p=c\times\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(t-a_{i})^{m_{i}}\,,

(17)

where
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and
s,m_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
c\neq 0
,
m_{i}>0
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{s}
are disjoint.
Moreover, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}
satisfying
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{R}}
, there is
j\in\{1,\dots,s\}\backslash\{i\}
such that
m_{j}=m_{i}
, and
a_{j}
is the conjugate of
a_{i}
.

We call
a_{i}
in Lemma
17
a (complex) root of
p
with multiplicity
m_{i}
.
If
m_{i}>1
(resp.
m_{i}=1
),
a_{i}
is call multiple (resp. simple) root of
p
.

Let
\gcd(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
stand for the greatest common divisor of polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.
To find
\gcd(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
, we use the well-known polynomial Euclidean algorithm (see, e.g., Basu–Pollack–Roy’s book
[
2
]
).

Remark 6
.

Belhaj–Kahla’s algorithm in
[
3
]
to compute
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})
via Hankel matrices has a cost of
O(d^{2})
arithmetic operations, where
d=\max\{\deg(p_{1}),\deg(p_{2})\}
.
Since
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})=\gcd(p_{1},\gcd(p_{2},\dots,p_{m}))
, we obtain the number of arithmetic operations to compute
\gcd(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
as
(m-1)\times O(d^{2})
, where
d=\max\limits_{j=1,\dots,m}\deg(p_{j})
.

Denote by
p^{\prime}
the derivative of a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.
The following two lemmas present the basic properties of the roots of a polynomial and its derivative:

Lemma 6
.

Let
a
be a root of a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.
Then
a
is a multiple root of
p
if and only if
a
is also a root of
p^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
a
is a root of
p
, there exists a polynomial
q
such that
p=(t-a)q
.
Using the product rule of derivatives, we know that

p^{\prime}=q+(t-a)q^{\prime}
.
But then
(t-a)
only divides
p^{\prime}
if and only if it also divides
q
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

If a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
has only simple roots.
Then
p,p^{\prime}
are relatively prime, i.e.,
\gcd(p,p^{\prime})=1
.

Proof.

Assume that
p,p^{\prime}
are not relatively prime.
Then, they have a common irreducible factor of degree
1
.
Since by definition, two polynomials are relatively prime if their only common factor is of degree
0
.
Then there exists a polynomial of the form
t-a
that divides both
p
and
p^{\prime}
.
Then from Lemma
6
,
a
is a multiple root of
p
.
But this is impossible.
∎

We recall the radical of a polynomial in the following lemma:

Lemma 8
.

Let
p
be polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
with decomposition (
17
).
Then

\gcd(p,p^{\prime})=\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle m_{i}>1\end{array}}^{s}(t-a_{i})^{m_{i}-1}\,,

(18)

\frac{p}{\gcd(p,p^{\prime})}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(t-a_{i})

(19)

are polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.

Proof.

Set

h=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(t-a_{i})\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988ptw=\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\

The following lemma describes the properties of the closures of the intersection and union of several sets:

We say that a polynomial

Lemma 11
.

Let
p
be a polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
of positive degree.
Then

changes (resp. does not change) sign at its real roots with odd (resp. even) multiplicities.

Proof.

Let

be a real root of

with multiplicity

. Then

, where

is some polynomial in

that is not zero at

•

Case 1:

. By continuity

is positive on an open interval containing

. Thus

changes sign at
a
. The same will be true if

Case 2:

is even. If

is positive near

is also positive on a deleted neighborhood of

.

does not change sign at

.
The same will be true if

.

∎

A semi-algebraic description of an open semi-algebraic set defined only by a single inequality is present in the following lemma:

2.7

Let

stand for the cone of sums of squares of polynomials in

is not a root of

for any
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\backslash\{j\}
.
It implies that
p_{i}(a)>0
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\backslash\{j\}
.
Then we take
\varepsilon
small enough such that

[a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon]\subset\bigcap_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle i\neq j\end{array}}^{m}\{p_{i}\geq 0\}\,.

(44)

Thus
[a,a+\varepsilon]
or
[a-\varepsilon,a]
is a subset of
S(p,\emptyset)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{p_{i}\geq 0\}
.
It is impossible since
a
is an isolated point of
S(p,\emptyset)
.
Thus
S(p,q)
has no isolated point.
Assume that
a
is an endpoint of
S(p,q)
.
Then there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
p_{i}(a)=0
.
It implies that
(t-a)
divides
p_{i}
.
By the first condition,
(t-a)^{2}
does not divide
p_{i}
.

Hence the result follows.
∎

The following lemma follows from Nie’s Nichtnegativstellensatz in
[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
:

Lemma 19
.

Let
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
,
p=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
,
q=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{l}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.
Assume that
S(p,q)
is a finite union of isolated points and

is nonnegative on
S(p,q)
.
Then there exists
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\geq K
,
h\in T_{k}(p,q)[t]
.

The following lemma restates Kuhlmann–Marshall–Schwartz’s Nichtnegativstellensatz in
[
10
, Corollary 3.3]
:

Lemma 20
.

Let
p=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
and
q=\emptyset
such that

is compact and has no isolated points.
Then

\{h\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]\,:\,h\geq 0\text{ on }S(p,q)\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}T_{k}(p,q)[t]\,,

(45)

if and only if, for each endpoint
a
of
S(p,q)
, there exists

i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
(t-a)
divides
p_{i}
but
(t-a)^{2}
does not.

The following lemma is a consequence of Lemmas
19
and
20
:

Lemma 21
.

Let
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
,
p=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
,
q=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{l}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
, and
\rho^{\star}(g,p,q)
be as in (

).
Let the assumption of Lemma
18
hold.
Assume that

is compact.
Then there exists
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all

,

and therefore

.

Proof.

By assumption,

is nonnegative on

.
Due to Lemma

, one of the two cases mentioned in this lemma occurs.
In the first case, we apply Lemma

to get

Hence the conclusion follows.

3
Products of linear factors occurring odd times

The following algorithm allows us to obtain the product of linear factors of a polynomial occurring odd times:

Algorithm 1
.

Products of linear factors occurring odd times.

•

Input: Polynomial
p
in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.

•

Output: Rational function
u_{i^{\star}}
in single-variable
t
.

1.

Set
u_{0}:=1
,
h_{0}:=p
, and
i=0
.

2.

Set
q_{i}:=\gcd(h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime})
and
w_{i}:=\gcd(q_{i},q_{i}^{\prime})
.

3.

Update
u_{i+1}:=u_{i}\times\frac{h_{i}w_{i}}{q_{i}^{2}}
.

4.

If
w_{i}
is not constant, update
h_{i+1}:=w_{i}
,
i:=i+1
and run again Steps 2, 3, 4. Otherwise, set
i^{\star}=i+1
and stop.

Lemma 22
.

Let
u_{i^{\star}}
be the output of Algorithm
1
with input
p
as in (
17
).
Then there exists a constant
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

u_{i^{\star}}=C\times\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle m_{i}\text{ is odd}\end{array}}^{s}(t-a_{i})

(46)

is a polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.

Proof.

Let us prove that Algorithm
1
terminates after a finite number of steps.
By Lemma
9
, the first two steps produce
\frac{h_{0}w_{0}}{q_{0}^{2}}
as the right-hand side of (
23
).
It is the product of linear factors of
p
occurring once and thus is a polynomial.
In addition, Lemma
8
yields

w_{0}=\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle m_{i}>1\end{array}}^{s}(t-a_{i})^{m_{i}-2}

(47)

It implies
\deg(w_{0})\leq\deg(h_{0})-2s
.
The third step gives
u_{1}
as the right-hand side of (
23
).
In the fourth step, we obtain
h_{1}=w0
, which gives
\deg(h_{1})=\deg(w_{0})\leq\deg(h_{0})-2s
.
Repeating this process, we get
\frac{h_{i}w_{i}}{q_{i}^{2}}
as the product of linear factors of
p
with multiplicity
2i+1
and
\deg(h_{i+1})<\deg(h_{i})<\deg(p)
.
Then
u_{i+1}
is the product of linear factors of p occurring
1,3,\dots,2i+1
times.
Moreover, Algorithm
1
cannot run forever since
\deg(h_{i+1})<\deg(h_{i})<\deg(p)
.
Hence the result follows.
∎

Remark 11
.

Thanks to the complexity given in Remark
6
, Algorithm
1
with input
p
of degree
d
has complexity
O(d^{3})
.

Given a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
, denote by
\lc(p)
the leading coefficient of
p
.

Definition 1
.

For a given polynomial
p
in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
with decomposition (
17
),
we define polynomial
\widetilde{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
by

\widetilde{p}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\lc(p)}{\lc(u_{i^{\star}})}\times u_{i^{\star}}&\text{if }u_{i^{\star}}\neq 0\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(48)

where
u_{i^{\star}}
is the output of Algorithm
1
with input
p
.

By definition,
\widetilde{p}
has the same leading coefficient as
p
.
Roughly speaking, polynomial
\widetilde{p}
enables us to remove all linear factors of a polynomial
p
occurring even times.

The following lemma states some properties of polynomial
\widetilde{p}
needed for the later results:

Lemma 23
.

Let
p
be polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
with decomposition (
17
).
The following statements hold:

1.

The polynomial
\widetilde{p}
is the right-hand side of (
46
). Consequently,
\widetilde{p}
has only simple roots.

2.

The polynomial
p
can be decomposed as
p=\widetilde{p}\times w
, where

w=\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle j=1\\
\scriptstyle m_{j}\text{ is even}\end{array}}^{s}(t-a_{j})^{m_{j}}\times\prod_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle m_{i}\text{ is odd}\end{array}}^{s}(t-a_{i})^{m_{i}-1}

(49)

is a nonnegative polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[t]
.
Consequently,
p(t)>0
if and only if
\widetilde{p}(t)>0
.

Proof.

The first statement is due to Lemma
22
.
The second statement relies on the first statement and the decomposition (
17
).
It is clear that
w\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
thanks to the second statement of Lemma
5
.
The non-negativity of
w
is because
w
is the product of the squares of linear factors of
p
.
∎

4
Replacement of strict inequalities by non-strict ones

In this section, we demonstrate that replacing strict inequalities defining a “nice” elementary semi-algebraic set with non-strict ones allows us to obtain the closure of this set.

Definition 2
.

For a given elementary semi-algebraic set
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}
defined as in (
34
), we define elementary semi-algebraic set
\widetilde{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
by replacing strict inequalities
p_{i}(t)>0
determining
B
with non-strict inequalities
\widetilde{p}_{i}(t)\geq 0
, i.e.,

\widetilde{B}=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\widetilde{p}_{1}(t)\geq 0,\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}(t)\geq 0\,,\,q_{1}(t)=\dots=q_{l}(t)=0\}\,.

(50)

Remark 12
.

The set
\widetilde{B}
in (
50
) is semi-algebraic since it can be written as

\widetilde{B}=\bigcup_{*\in\{>,=\}^{m}}\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\widetilde{p}_{1}(t)*_{1}0,\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}(t)*_{m}0\,,\,q_{1}(t)=\dots=q_{l}(t)=0\}\,.

(51)

Lemma 24
.

Let
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an elementary semi-algebraic set defined as in (
35
).
Then

B=\{\widetilde{p}_{1}>0,\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}>0\,,\,q_{1}=\dots=q_{l}=0\}\,.

(52)

Proof.

By using the second statement of Lemma
23
, we get the equivalence:
p_{i}(t)>0
if and only if
\widetilde{p}_{i}(t)>0
.
Hence the result follows.
∎

The following example given in
[
1
, Section 2.1]
shows that replacing strict inequalities in elementary semi-algebraic set
B
without removing the factors of the inequality polynomials occurring even times might not allow for the closure of
B
to be obtained:

Example 2
.

Let
B=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,t^{2}(t-1)>0\}
.
Then
B=(1,\infty)
.
Replacing the strict inequality in
B
by a non-strict inequality, we obtain
\hat{B}=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,t^{2}(t-1)\geq 0\}=\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
, which is not the closure of
B
.
However, by definition,
\widetilde{B}=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,t-1\geq 0\}=[1,\infty)
is exactly the closure of
B
.

The following example indicates the case where
\widetilde{B}
is not the closure of an elementary semi-algebraic set
B
:

Example 3
.

Let
B=\{p_{1}>0,p_{2}>0\}
with
p_{1}=-t(t-1)
and
p_{2}=-t(t+1)
.
Then
\{p_{1}>0\}=(0,1)
and
\{p_{2}\geq 0\}=(-1,0)
yields
B=\{p_{1}>0\}\cap\{p_{2}\geq 0\}=\emptyset
.
However, since
\widetilde{p}_{j}=p_{j}
,

\{\widetilde{p}_{1}>0\}=[0,1]
and
\{\widetilde{p}_{2}\geq 0\}=[-1,0]
yields
\widetilde{B}=\{\widetilde{p}_{1}>0\}\cap\{\widetilde{p}_{2}\geq 0\}=\{0\}
. Thus
\overline{B}=\emptyset\neq\{0\}=\widetilde{B}
.

To address the issues in Examples
2
and
3
, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 25
.

Let
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be an elementary semi-algebraic set of the form (
35
).
Suppose that the following conditions hold:

1.

Any couple of inequality polynomials defining
B
have no common real root with odd multiplicity.

2.

Any inequality polynomial defining
B
has no root in
\{q_{1}=\dots=q_{l}=0\}
with odd multiplicity.

Then
\widetilde{B}
is the closure of
B
, i.e.,
\widetilde{B}=\overline{B}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
24
, equality (
52
) holds.
For
i=1,\dots,m
, let
a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,r_{i}}
be the real roots of
p_{i}
with odd multiplicities.
Lemma
23
yields that
a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,r_{i}}
are all simple real roots of
\widetilde{p}_{i}
.
By assumption, for every
(i,r)\in\{1,\dots,m\}^{2}
satisfying
i\neq j
,
\widetilde{p}_{i}
and
\widetilde{p}_{r}
have no common root.
From this, Lemma
13
yields

\overline{\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\widetilde{p}_{i}>0\}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\widetilde{p}_{i}\geq 0\}\,.

(53)

Set
V=\{q_{1}=\dots=q_{l}=0\}
.
By assumption, each
\widetilde{p}_{i}
has no root in
V
.
From this, Lemma
14
implies

\overline{\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\widetilde{p}_{i}>0\}}\cap V=\overline{\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\{\widetilde{p}_{i}>0\}\cap V}\,.

(54)

Combining this with (
53
), (
52
), and (
50
), we obtain the result.
∎

Although not all elementary semi-algebraic sets have properties as in Lemma

, we can always decompose each elementary semi-algebraic set as the union of a finite number of elementary semi-algebraic sets with these properties:

Lemma 26
.

Let

of

such that

and

2.

For
j=1,\dots,s
, any inequality polynomial defining
B_{j}
has no root in the variety determined by the equality polynomials defining
B_{j}
.

3.

For
j=1,\dots,s
, any couple of inequality polynomials defining

has no common root.

Proof.

Suppose that
B
is of the form (
35
).
By Lemma
24
, equality (
52
) holds.
Using Lemma
16
, we get
\check{q}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
such that

\widetilde{B}=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\widetilde{p}_{1}(t)\geq 0,\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}(t)\geq 0\,,\,\check{q}_{1}(t)=\dots=\check{q}_{l}(t)=0\}\,.

(55)

and each
\widetilde{p}_{i}

.

implies that each

5
Main algorithm and application to polynomial optimization

The following algorithm enables us to obtain semidefinite descriptions of the closures of the images of semi-algebraic sets under polynomials:

Algorithm 2
.

Closures of the images of semi-algebraic sets under polynomials.

•

Input: Semi-algebraic set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.

•

Output: Semi-algebraic description of
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}
.

1.

Compute
p_{i,j},q_{k,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
such that
f(S)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}B_{j}
with

B_{j}=\{p_{1,j}>0,\dots,p_{m_{j},j}>0\,,\,q_{1,j}=\dots=q_{l_{j},j}=0\}\,.

(57)

2.

For
j=1,\dots,s
, do:

(a)

Compute
p_{i,r,j},q_{k,r,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
such that
B_{j}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{s_{j}}B_{r,j}
with

B_{r,j}=\{p_{1,r,j}>0,\dots,p_{m_{r,j},r,j}>0\,,\,q_{1,r,j}=\dots=q_{l_{r,j},r,j}=0\}

(58)

and the following three conditions hold:

i.

Each of
p_{1,r,j},\dots,p_{m_{r,j},r,j}
has only simple root.

ii.

Any of
p_{1,r,j},\dots,p_{m_{r,j},r,j}
has no root in
\{q_{1,r,j}=\dots=q_{l_{r,j},r,j}=0\}
.

iii.

Any couple of
p_{1,r,j},\dots,p_{m_{r,j},r,j}
has no common root.

(b)

Compute
\widetilde{p}_{i,r,j}
as in Definition
1
by using Algorithm
1
.

3.

Set
Q:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\bigcup_{r=1}^{s_{j}}\widetilde{B}_{r,j}
with
\widetilde{B}_{r,j}
as in Definition
2
, i.e.,

\widetilde{B}_{r,j}=\{\widetilde{p}_{1,r,j}\geq 0,\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m_{r,j},r,j}\geq 0\,,\,q_{1,r,j}=\dots=q_{l_{r,j},r,j}=0\}\,.

(59)

The first step of Algorithm
2
relies on Basu’s method in
[
1
, Section 3]
.
We use the algorithm mentioned in Lemma
26
to do Step 2 (a) of Algorithm
2
.

We guarantee that the output
Q
of Algorithm
2
is identical to the closure
\overline{f(S)}
in the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
f
be a polynomial and
S
be a semi-algebraic set.
Then Algorithm
2
with input
f,S
produces polynomials defining
A_{1},\dots,A_{w}
of the form (
4
) such that
Q=\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}A_{j}
is exactly the closure of
f(S)
and the following conditions hold:

1.

Each inequality polynomial defining
A_{j}
has only simple roots.

2.

Any couple of inequality polynomials determining each
A_{j}
has no common root.

Proof.

Lemma
25
implies that
\widetilde{B}_{r,j}=\overline{B_{r,j}}
.
From this and the second statement of Lemma
10
, we get

Q=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\bigcup_{r=1}^{s_{j}}\overline{B_{r,j}}=\overline{\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\bigcup_{r=1}^{s_{j}}B_{r,j}}=\overline{\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}B_{j}}=\overline{f(S)}\,.

(60)

Setting
A_{1},\dots,A_{w}
as
\widetilde{B}_{r,j}
s, we obtain the result.
∎

Remark 14
.

Assume that
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is determined by
s
polynomials of degree at most
d
and
f
has degree at most
d
.
By Remark
3
, the first step of Algorithm
2
requires
s^{n+1}d^{O(n)}
arithmetic operations and the degrees of

are upper bounded by

.

yields that Step 2 (a) of Algorithm

has a complexity of

, the complexity of Step 2 (b) of Algorithm

The following corollary makes use of Algorithm

in polynomial optimization, especially in the case where the objective polynomials do not attain minimum value on the semi-algebraic sets:

Corollary 1
.

Let
f
be a polynomial and
S
be a semi-algebraic set.

f^{\star}=\inf_{x\in S}f(x)\,.

Assume that the image

is bounded.
Let

be as the output of Algorithm

with input

.
Then there exist
p^{(j)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
and
p^{(j)}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]
such that

(62)

where

and
Q=\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}S(p^{(j)},q^{(j)})
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

Each polynomial in

has only simple roots.

2.

Any couple of polynomials in

has no common root.

Moreover, there exists

such that for all

yields

, which implies the first statement.
Let us prove the second one.
Since

is bounded, so is

From

says that there exists

Hence the result follows thanks to (
