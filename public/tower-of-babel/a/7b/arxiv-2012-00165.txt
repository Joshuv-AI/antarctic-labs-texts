An accelerated hybrid data-driven/model-based approach for poroelasticity problems with multi-fidelity multi-physics data
Bahador Bahmani, WaiChing Sun

Abstract
We present a hybrid model/model-free data-driven approach to solve poroelasticity problems. Extending the data-driven modeling framework originated from Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016 , we introduce one model-free and two hybrid model-based/data-driven formulations capable of simulating the coupled diffusion-deformation of fluid-infiltrating porous media with different amounts of available data. To improve the efficiency of the model-free data search, we introduce a distance-minimized algorithm accelerated by a k-dimensional tree search. To handle the different fidelities of the solid elasticity and fluid hydraulic constitutive responses, we introduce a hybridized model in which either the solid and the fluid solver can switch from a model-based to a model-free approach depending on the availability and the properties of the data. Numerical experiments are designed to verify the implementation and compare the performance of the proposed model to other alternatives.



1
Introduction

The theory of poromechanics attempts to capture how infiltrating pore fluid interacts with the solid skeleton formed by the solid constituents at the scale of the representative elementary volume where an effective medium can be established

(
Terzaghi et al. 1996
;
Biot 1941
;
Coussy 2004
)
.
Poroelasticity is a sub-discipline of poromechanics problem that focuses on the path independent response of porous media. It has important applications
across multiple disciplines including seismology
(
Cocco and Rice 2002
;
Chambon and Rudnicki 2001
)
,
hydraulic fracture
(
Detournay and Cheng 1993
;
Detournay 2016
)
,
petroleum engineering, reservoir management, geological disposal
(
Sun 2015
;
Na and Sun 2017
)
,
and biomechanics modeling for soft tissues and bones
(
Cowin 1999
)
.

Due to the multiphysics nature of the coupled diffusion-deformation process,
poromechanics models must combine field equations which
constraints field variables to obey
the balance principle with coupled constitutive models which characterize material laws for pore fluid and solid skeleton
(
Zienkiewicz et al. 1999
;
Sun 2013
;
Wang and Sun 2017
;
Wang and Sun 2019
;
De Bellis et al. 2017
)
.
One important issue that affects the practicality, accuracy, and robustness of the poroelasticity model is the difference in fidelity of the solid and fluid constitutive laws. In particular, elastic responses of a variety of porous media such as sandstone
(
Renaud et al. 2013
)
, clay
(
Borja et al. 1997
;
Bryant and Sun 2019
;
Na et al. 2019
)
, sand
(
Cameron and Carter 2009
)
and bone
(
Cowin 1999
)
can be captured quite adequately with the existing state-of-the-art models
(
Borja 2013b
)
such that the error of a well-calibrated prediction is often within a few percentages.
Hence, a simple elasticity model calibrated with simple compression or shear tests is often sufficient to make forecasting predictions with a
narrow confidence interval.

However, owing to the difficulty to conduct highly precise experiments and the lack of parametric space to characterize the hydraulic responses, a typical prediction of permeability based on the porosity-permeability model is expected to have a much higher variance and, in many cases, is considered accurate even if predicted benchmark permeability is just within the same order of magnitude
(
Paterson and Wong 2005
;
Sun et al. 2011a
;
Sun et al. 2011b
;
Andrä et al. 2013a
;
Sun and Wong 2018
)
.
In this case, a calibrated hydraulic model that minimizes the mean square error of the Darcy’s velocity or pressure gradient does not yield a reliable forecast
due to the much wider confidence intervals.
This disparity in the fidelity of the elasticity and hydraulic models for porous media has also been consistently observed in large-scale multi-research-group benchmark studies such as
Andrä et al. 2013b
;
Andrä et al. 2013a
and has become a major bottleneck for poromechanics models.

An alternative to handle this disparity is to introduce a variational model-free approach for the poromechanics problem. First introduced by
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
and later extended for the constitutive manifold
(
Ibañez et al. 2017
;
He and Chen 2020
)
, incorporated with digital image correlation
(
Leygue et al. 2018
)
and adopted to diffusion
(
Nguyen et al. 2020
)
, the data-driven approach enables one to make predictions on the most plausible constitutive responses via a distance minimization algorithm. This distance minimization algorithm then chooses either a data point from a material point database or a linear embedding manifold that minimizes the error from the conservation laws
and compatibility conditions such that a physics simulation can be carried out without an explicitly derived constitutive law.
While this approach holds great promise when a large amount of data is available especially for a low-dimensional prediction (e.g., heat flux in 2D, axial stress of a truss element), the predictions in three-dimensional space for arbitrary loading paths can be difficult when the amount of data is not sufficient or not distributed with a sufficient density in the parametric space.

In this work, we introduce a more flexible hybrid approach where the solid constitutive law can be either model-based (when the fidelity of the elasticity model is sufficiently high) or model-free (when there are sufficient data points).
Meanwhile, the hydraulic model is replaced by the model-free approach to avoid the usage of models with high deviations to make predictions.
To cut the CPU time required to conduct the search for the closest data point,
we introduce a k-dimensional tree search that helps organizing data points to accelerate the simulations. Numerical experiments are then conducted to examine and compare the fully model-based, the fully data-driven, and the hybrid models.

The remaining parts of the paper will proceed as follows. We first introduce the formulations that enable data-driven algorithm to replace parts or all of the constitutive laws required to generate incremental solution updates for the poroelasticity problems (Section
2
).
We then introduce a search strategy that enables us to accelerate the time used to search for the optimal data points which are often the bottleneck of the speed for the data-driven models (Section
3
).
Section
4
overviews the data-driven algorithm and its implementation aspects.
Numerical experiments are then conducted to verify the formulation and test the accuracy, robustness, and fidelity of the hybridized and data-driven models (Section
5
) followed by a brief conclusion that summarized the major findings.

).

2
Hybridized data-driven/model-based poromechanics problem

This section presents formulations that solve poroelasticity problems
either in a fully model-free fashion or in a hybridized model where either
the solid or fluid constitutive law is replaced by a model-free data-driven approach.
For completeness, we first review the classical poromechanics problem in which the solid displacement and pore pressure are the primary unknown variables.
We then introduce a data-driven model-free algorithm to completely replace classical solid and fluid constitutive laws with the corresponding data-driven algorithm searched for the optimal data points. Finally, two hybridized formulations are provided in each of which either solid or fluid constitutive law follows the classical model-based approach.
The pros and cons of each formulation for different situations (e.g., availability of data, fidelity consideration, robustness) are discussed and will be further elaborated in the numerical experiments shown in Section
5
. Figure

2.1
A brief review of field equations for poroelasticity problems

For completeness, we provide a concise review of the poroelasticity model,
which consists of two major components, i.e., the field theory that provides the necessary constraints for the field variables in the space-time domain and the material laws that provides the local constitutive updates for both the solid skeleton and the fluid constituents. Interest readers may refer to
Prevost 1985
;
Borja and Alarcón 1995
;
Zienkiewicz et al. 1999
;
Coussy 2004
;
Sun et al. 2013a
;
Sun et al. 2014a
;
Na and Sun 2017
;
Na et al. 2019
for a more comprehensive treatment for the topic.

For simplicity, we assume that the deformation of the solid skeleton is, infinitesimal, path independent and elastic such that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{e}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{e},p)=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},p)
. We also assume that the flow in the pore space is a function of the pore pressure gradient such that the Darcy’s velocity
\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{q}(\grad p)
is a function of the pore pressure
p
.
In this formulation, we follow the trajectory of the solid constituent and express
the balance principles as a function of solid displacement
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{s}

and pore pressure
p
.
By neglecting the inertial term, the balance of linear momentum and mass on the spatial domain
\Omega
over time
t\in T
read (cf.
Sun et al. 2014a
),

\displaystyle\diver{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},p)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times T,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{p}}{M}+B\dot{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{vol}}+\diver{\boldsymbol{q}}(\grad p)+s=0\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times T,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the total stress and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}=\tr\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the volumetric strain. We postulate that the total stress can be partitioned into the effective stress of the solid skeleton and the pore fluid pressure according to the effective stress principle, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})-Bp\boldsymbol{I}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times T.

(3)

Furthermore,

\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is the body force,

M
is the Biot’s modulus,
B
is the Biot’s coefficient,
\boldsymbol{q}
is the Darcy’s velocity,
s
is the source (sink) term.
The definitions of these physical quantities are listed below.

\displaystyle B=1-\frac{K}{K_{\mathrm{s}}}\;,

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathrm{s}}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathrm{f}}=(1-\phi^{\mathrm{f}})\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{g}+\phi^{\mathrm{f}}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{g}\;,

(5)

\displaystyle M=\frac{K_{\mathrm{s}}K_{\mathrm{f}}}{K_{\mathrm{f}}(B-\phi^{\mathrm{f}})+K_{\mathrm{s}}\phi^{\mathrm{f}}}\;,

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}=\phi^{f}(\boldsymbol{v}^{f}-\boldsymbol{v}),

(7)

where
K
and
K_{s}
are the bulk moduli of the solid skeleton and the solid constituent, respectively,
\gamma^{\mathrm{s}}
and
\gamma^{\mathrm{f}}

are the partial density of the solid and fluid constituents,
while
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}
are the intrinsic density of the
solid and fluid constituents.
\phi^{\mathrm{f}}
is the porosity,
K_{\mathrm{f}}
is the
bulk modulus of the fluid constituent, and
\boldsymbol{v}^{f}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
are the velocity of the fluid constituent and the solid skeleton, respectively.

To compute the boundary value problem, the initial and boundary conditions are specified as follows. The initial conditions are
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\ \mathrm{at}\ t=0
and
p=p_{0}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\ \mathrm{at}\ t=0
. Meanwhile, the boundary conditions are
\boldsymbol{u}=\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{u}\times T
(prescribed displacement) and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{\sigma}\times T
(prescribed traction),

p=\bar{p}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{p}\times T
(prescribed pore pressure),
and
\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\bar{q}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{q}\times T
(prescribed fluid flux) where
\boldsymbol{n}
is an unit normal vector pointing outward to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Furthermore, the following conditions must hold:

\partial\Omega_{\sigma}\cup\partial\Omega_{u}=\partial\Omega
,

\partial\Omega_{\sigma}\cap\partial\Omega_{u}=\emptyset
,

\partial\Omega_{q}\cup\partial\Omega_{p}=\partial\Omega
and

\partial\Omega_{q}\cap\partial\Omega_{p}=\emptyset
.

For convenience, we first discretize the governing equations, Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
2
), in time via the implicit Euler scheme.
Given the displacement and pore pressure at time
t_{n}
,
the time-discretized balance principle
within the time interval
T\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\diver{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1},p_{n+1})+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\;,

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n+1}}{M}+{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}+\diver{\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}(\grad p_{n+1})}\Delta t+s_{n+1}\Delta t=\frac{p_{n}}{M}+{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\;.

(9)

To complete the initial boundary value problem, the classical model-based approach for poroelasticity problem required us to define material constitutive laws such that, given the strain and pore pressure gradient, we may obtain updated effective stress and Darcy’s velocity; the first one maps a given strain to the effective stress
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime})
, another one maps a given pore pressure gradient to the
Darcy’s velocity
(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
.
In what follows, our goal is to introduce a new formulation such that
at least one
of these two constitutive laws,
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime})
and
(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
can be replaced by the model-free data-driven approach originally designed for elasticity problem in
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
.

Remark 1
.

Notice that, for porous media with compressible constituents,
both the Biot’s coefficient and the Biot’s modulus may depend on the bulk modulus of the skeleton and hence may evolve if the elastic response of the solid skeleton
is nonlinear.
Here we limit our scope to the types of porous media
with the bulk modulus of both constituents significantly larger than
the effective bulk modulus of the solid skeleton. As such, the evolution
of both the Biot’s coefficient and Biot’s modulus,
B
and
M
, are neglected
(
Biot 1941
;
Terzaghi et al. 1943
;
Cryer 1963
)
.
This assumption and the effective stress principle together enable us to treat the constitutive laws for the solid deformation and fluid flow as two independent ordinary differential equations.

2.2
Option 1: Pure data-driven poroelasticity

Here, our goal is to present a new formulation constrained by the time-discretized balance principle listed in Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) without employing any constitutive laws.
To do so, we assume that there exists two databases, each have a finite number of data points for
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime})
and
(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
.
We further assume that there are sufficient data points distributed in the parametric space for both material laws such that a complete model-free approach is feasible. Notice that the data-driven model-free method developed in this research is categorized into non-parametric learning methods
Goodfellow et al. 2016
which are basically considered data-demanding since they have minimum assumptions about the data-driven model. This is in contrast to the parametric methods such as neural networks
(
Ghaboussi et al. 1998
;
Wang and Sun 2018
;
Tartakovsky et al. 2020
)
where there is a stronger pre-assumption about the data-driven model.

In the following subsections, we first express the mathematical statement for the data-driven scheme as a double-minimization problem. Then, we provide a numerical strategy based on the fixed-point (staggered) method to solve this minimization problem in two steps: global and local minimization steps. Finally, we provide a numerical solution for the global minimization based on the Lagrange multiplier method. The local minimization step is not covered in this section and will be discussed in section
3
separately since it has its specific treatment which is common between all the three formulations.

2.2.1
Problem statement

In this section, we present the fully data-driven, constitutive-model-free formulation for the poroelasticity problem. Our goal is to introduce a minimization problem that constitutes a model-free poroelasticity solver.
As such, we seek solutions from data sets of material responses that weakly satisfy the time-discretized balance principle listed in Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) without explicitly
introducing any constitutive law for either the solid skeleton and the pore fluid.

If there are an infinite number of error-free data points populating the database, then
a constitutive manifold can be identified such that one may select elements of the manifold
that satisfy the constitutive laws. However, material databases is rarely populated
with enough data and often contains data with noise that makes it impractical
to impose such a strict requirement. As an alternative, we follow the idea of

Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
where we merely seek solutions that satisfy the balance principles while the resultant constitutive responses are the ones closest to but not necessary elements of
the set of points in the databases. As such, we need to introduce the notion of ”distance” via
an appropriate norm we selected for the space of admissible solution
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and
(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
.

In other words, we regard the balance principles as the universal law that
should not be violated. The data-driven method is then designed to
generate solutions that fulfill the balance principle while the
material response at the integration points
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and
(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
are all closest to existing data points in the material database. The distance
between an admissible response satisfied balance laws and an existing data point is then measured by an appropriate norm.
We will express the above-mentioned statement as a double-minimization problem
(
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
;
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2018
;
He and Chen 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
)
.
For brevity, we assign a new variable for pressure gradient as
\boldsymbol{r}=\grad{p}
.

We then define the phase space for poroelasticity at time
t_{n+1}
as all
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
where
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}=\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}\times\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}
is the product space of the solid phase space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}=V_{\epsilon}\times V_{\sigma^{\prime}}
and the fluid phase space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}=V_{r}\times V_{q}
;
in which
V_{\epsilon},\ V_{\sigma^{\prime}}=\left[L^{2}(\Omega)\right]^{\sum_{A=1}^{\text{ndim}}A}

are spaces of real-valued symmetric 2
nd
order tensor fields with square integrable components, and
V_{r},\ V_{q}=\left[L^{2}(\Omega)\right]^{\text{ndim}}
are spaces of real-valued vector fields with square integrable components.
We define the space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}
as follows:

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1},p_{n+1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}\times V_{p}\mid\diver{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\ \boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}=\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{u},\right.\\
\left.\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{\sigma},\ \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\in V_{u}
,
V_{u}=[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{\text{ndim}}
,
V_{p}=H^{1}(\Omega)
, and
H^{1}
denotes the Sobolev space of square-integrable functions with square-integrable first derivative and
ndim
denotes the dimension of the spatial domain. Here, strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})
is a derived quantity of displacement vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}
through small strain relation.
All members in continuous set
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}
satisfy time discretized conservation of linear momentum equation, mechanical-related boundary conditions, small deformation relation for strain tensor, and effective stress principle at time
t_{n+1}
. The compatibility conditions of the strain field is automatically satisfied since the small strain tensor is the symmetric part of the displacement field gradient. The space of admissible solutions that satisfy the conservation of mass is denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
and defined as follows:

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}(p_{n+1}),\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{u_{n+1}}))\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}\times V_{\epsilon}\mid\frac{p_{n+1}}{M}+{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}+\diver{\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}}\Delta t+s_{n+1}\Delta t=\right.\\
\left.\frac{p_{n}}{M}+{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega,p_{n+1}=\bar{p}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{p},\ \boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\bar{q}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{q}\right\},

where the gradient of pore pressure
\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}(p_{n+1})
is a derived quantity of pressure field herein.
The admissible solution space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
for the poroelasticity problem at time
t_{n+1}
includes field variables
\boldsymbol{\epsilon_{n+1}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}(p_{n+1})
, and
\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}
that satisfy all physical constraints at time
t_{n+1}
. This space exists at the intersection of above defined continuous sets
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
. The poroelasticity database at time
t_{n+1}
is denoted by
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\subset\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
. This discrete set contains a finite number of elements that stores experimental data points corresponding to the poroelasticity constitutive laws at time
t_{n+1}
. The poroelasticity database format and its properties will be clarified in details later.

The data-driven solution
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
at time
t_{n+1}
is the solution of the following double-minimization problem:

\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=\underset{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}{\arg}\left\{\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{2}\right\},

(10)

where
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1})
is an element of poroelasticity database,

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}),{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}(p_{n+1}),\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})
is an element of admissible poroelasticity solution space, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}
is a norm associated with the space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
which measures closeness of
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}_{n+1}
. We define the subtract operation on space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
as follows:

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}.

(11)

We define the product norm for the poroelasticity product phase space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}
as follows:

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}=\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}+\Delta t\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{2}}

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}
,
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}

and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}
are the norms for the respectively solid and fluid phase spaces.
The time step size
\Delta t
in the second term of Eq. (
12
) is introduced to make the unit consistent.
We define the following norm for
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}
:

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}:\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}:\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}d\Omega,

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}
are 4
th
order symmetric positive definite tensors.
As shown in
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
;
He and Chen 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
,
specific
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}

can be chosen to from different equivalent norms, provided that both tensors remain
positive definite. The choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}
may affect the values of the norm due to the weighting but the
resultant normed space is topologically identical to a Euclidean space.

The weighting tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}
share the same unit as the elasticity tensor, i.e., Force/Length
2
, while the unit of
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the reciprocal of that of

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}
. Both the eigenvalues and the spectral directions
of these tensors affect the values of norms and therefore change how distance is measured
and could affect the efficiency of the search problems (cf.
Mota et al. 2016
;
Heider et al. 2020
).
It is suggested in
(
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2018
;
Leygue et al. 2018
;
He and Chen 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
)
to select
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for solid mechanics applications.
On the other hand, the norm for
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}
is defined as,

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{2}=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}d\Omega,

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}
are 2
nd
order symmetric positive definite tensors. Similarly, these numerical parameters control the importance of pressure gradient and Darcy’s velocity vectors in the norm calculations. The unit for
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}
is the same as hydraulic conductivity unit (
\frac{\mathrm{Length}^{4}}{\mathrm{Force}\times\mathrm{Time}}
). This norm, which in our case is of the unit of power, has been introduced in
Nguyen et al. 2020
for Poisson’s equation.

Similar to the norm equipped by the solid phase space, the implication of the choice of the
specific weighting effect for
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}

has not been previously studied in previous studies.
Nguyen et al. 2020
suggests
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1}
. The time increment
\Delta t
used in (
12
) is the scaling factor to make both terms with the same unit as energy. This factor could be directly included in
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}
, but we preferred to be consistent with power-like definition of fluid phase space metric.

To find the stationary points for the double minimization defined in Eq. (
10
) at time
t=t_{n+1}
,
a global-local iteration (which is our method of preference and will be described later) is needed to find both the admissible solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}

and the discrete data points that minimize the distance defined by the norm in Eq. (
12
). The latter can be done by comparing every point from the material databases to identify the optimized data point from
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
for each integration point.
However, searching the optimized data points from the entire data set can be inefficient
for a large database.
As such, we
consider an adaptive poroelasticity database where a subset of the plausible data points are collected for each time step to constitute a temporal-varying material database constituted
by the union of an adaptive solid effective-stress-strain database
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}_{i}
and an adaptive fluid pressure-gradient-Darcy-velocity database
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i}

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{i}=\{(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*})|(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}_{i},({\boldsymbol{r}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i}\},

(15)

where the subscript
i
indicates the snapshot taken at a discrete time step
t_{i}
.
For instance, an admissible subset of data can be identified via prior knowledge (e.g., upper and lower bounds of the porosity-permeability relationship, correlation structures, etc.) and deductive reasoning. In our last numerical experiment,
we use porosity (the ratio between the void and solid phase volume) to filter out
the implausible data points.
This treatment enables the data-driven solver to narrow down the search of possible solutions and therefore
enhance the efficiency and reduce the memory requirement.
Furthermore, the trade-off between computational time and memory will be discussed later in Sec.
3
.
Note that the adaptive database design can also be used as a mean to incorporate an active learning algorithm that generates new data points on demand
(
Lookman et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. The active learning approach as well as other algorithms that
may identify the feasible subset of data through clustering
(
Liu et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
or other techniques are not discussed in this work but will further be explored in the future.

Here, the solid data set
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}
is the set of experimental strain and effective stress pairs
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*})
; one point in this data set is a bundle of strain and effective stress together corresponding to one experimental observation. Fluid data set
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}
is the set of all experimental pressure gradient and Darcy’s velocity pairs
({\boldsymbol{r}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{*})
; one point in this data set is a bundle of pressure gradient and Darcy’s velocity together corresponding to one experimental observation.
The union of separate databases for stress-strain and hydraulic responses is designed for practical reasons because experiments that obtain the stress-strain curves and the effective permeability of a specimen are often conducted separately
(
Bardet 1997
;
Paterson and Wong 2005
;
Sun and Wong 2018
)
.

2.2.2
Solution strategy: fixed-point iteration

Here, we use the fixed-point method to numerically solve the double-minimization statement Eq. (
10
) associated with the fully data-driven poroelasticity problem. The use case of this method is initially shown by
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
for data-driven elasticity problems.

The data-driven solution must minimize an objective, i.e., the norm defined over poroelasticity phase space Eq. (
12
), with two different sets of constraints. One set of constraints belongs to a discrete set,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, with a finite number of members, but the other one belongs to continuous space, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
, that satisfies conservation laws. This minimization statement is categorized into combinatorial optimization problems due to the discrete nature of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, making the problem NP-hard. The fixed point method, or staggered method, reduces complexities by proposing a sequential solution algorithm, and it has been used in many applications
(
Felippa et al. 2001
;
Borden et al. 2012
;
Hu et al. 2020
)
. Here, it is used to break down the double-minimization into two separate, simpler minimization problems. We solve for one minimization problem by assuming the solution for the other one is fixed. In this way, we iteratively solve a minimization problem and update for another one until convergence of the solution.

According to the fixed-point method, we assume that the optimal data points
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
are known in the objective function Eq. (
10
), therefore we just need to minimize the objective function for unknowns
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
as follows:

\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{2},\mathrm{given}\ {\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}.

(16)

We call this minimization step global minimization. The global minimization step, geometrically, project discrete points
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
onto the continuous space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
according to the defined norm in Eq. (
12
); see red dash lines in Fig.
2
. In other words, this step finds solutions belong to the physical space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
that are closest points to the selected data points (from material space).

In the next step of fixed-point method, we assume the solutions
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
are known, then we find optimal data points
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
that minimize the objective function Eq. (
10
) as follows:

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{2},\mathrm{given}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}.

(17)

This minimization is defined over the discrete space
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
. Since choices for
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
are finite and there is no constraint on data points in the database,
the global objective function Eq. (
17
) defined over
\Omega

is minimized if the integrand is locally minimized. In this work, Gaussian quadrature is used to approximate the spatial integration. Let all the integration points be elements of a finite set

\{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},...,\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n_{\text{int}}}\}
, then the local minimization problem for an integration point
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}
reads,

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{a}})=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}}[d^{\mathrm{sf}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1},{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}))]^{2},\ \mathrm{given}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{a}})\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1},

(18)

where
d^{\mathrm{sf}}(\cdot)
is a local distance function between
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, which is defined as,
Eqs. (
11
), (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
):

\displaystyle d^{\mathrm{sf}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1})=[d^{\mathrm{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1})^{2}+\Delta td^{\mathrm{f}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1})^{2}]^{1/2},

(19)

\displaystyle d^{\mathrm{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}):\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})+\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}_{n+1}):\boldsymbol{\mathbb{S}}_{\mathrm{s}}:({\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}_{n+1}),

(20)

\displaystyle d^{\mathrm{f}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1})\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1})+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1})\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}),

(21)

where
d^{\mathrm{s}}(\cdot)
and
d^{\mathrm{f}}(\cdot)
denote the local distances between solid-related and fluid-related components, respectively. The technique that solves the local minimization will be discussed in Sec.
3
. Geometrically, the local minimization step project a point in
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
onto the data set
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, see blue dash lines in Fig.
2
.
By minimizing the distance defined in Eq. (
19
), we determine points in the data set (material space) that are closest to the conservation laws.

Each fixed-point iteration consists of two steps
(
He and Chen 2020
)
. First, we solve the global minimization Eq. (
17
) to project the solution coming from material space (database)
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
onto physical space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
; see blue dash arrow lines in Fig.
2
. Second, we solve local minimization problems to project the most recent solutions belong to the physical space onto the material space; see red dash arrow lines Fig.
2
. Fixed-point iterations continue until there is no change more than a user-defined tolerance in optimal solutions
at time step
t_{n+1}
.

Figure 2:
Schematic representation of material
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
and physical
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
responses at a quadrature point during fixed-point iterations. Numbers show iteration numbers. Star points present the entire database. The solid line describes the continuous admissible solution space that respects conservation laws. Dash blue arrow lines depict the local minimization step from the physical manifold to the material manifold for the quadrature point. Dash red arrow lines depict the global minimization step from the material manifold to the physical manifold. Note that the global step minimizes the norm over the entire domain (for all quadrature points). However, the local step minimizes the distance locally at each quadrature point.

In summary, we solve the double-minimization problem in two consecutive steps:

Global Step:
find the physical field
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{q})_{n+1}
for a given material field
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
via:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{2},\text{such that}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}.

Local Step:
at each local spatial point
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
(quadrature), find the local material point
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{i,\ n+1}=(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*},\boldsymbol{r}^{*},\boldsymbol{q}^{*})_{i,\ n+1}
for a given physical point
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}_{i,\ n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
via:

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{i,\ n+1}}[d^{\mathrm{sf}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{i,\ n+1},{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}_{i,\ n+1})]^{2},\ \text{such that}\ {\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{i,\ n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}.

We will explain how the global and local steps can be formulated for numerical solutions in Sec.
2.2.3
and Sec.
3
, respectively.

2.2.3
Global minimization

We introduce the functional associated with the global constrained optimization for the fully data-driven poroelasticity problem.
The objective function in Eq. (
17
) is minimized along with the set of constraints defined in
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}
. As such, the trial
spaces
V_{u}
and
V_{p}
for the u/p poromechanics formulation are chosen to strongly satisfy Dirichlet boundary conditions,

\displaystyle V_{u}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}|\boldsymbol{u}\in\left[H^{1}(\Omega)\right]^{3},\boldsymbol{u}=\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{u}\right\},

(22)

\displaystyle V_{p}=\left\{p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}|p\in H^{1}(\Omega),p=\bar{p}\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{p}\right\}.

(23)

The optimal solutions
\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\in V_{u}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}_{n+1}}\in V_{\sigma^{\prime}}
,
p_{n+1}\in V_{p}
, and
\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\in V_{q}
are the stationary points of the following functional:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1},\mathcal{B}_{n+1};{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1})=\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}},

(24)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{loss}}
is the original objective (loss) function, and
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}}
are contributions from constraints defined in
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
, respectively. We group all the Lagrange multipliers in

\mathcal{B}_{n+1}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mon}}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1},\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1},\beta^{q}_{n+1}\}
where

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mon}}_{n+1}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}
are real-valued vector fields to weakly enforce the balance of linear momentum and traction boundary conditions, respectively, and

\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
and
\beta^{q}_{n+1}
are real-valued scalar fields to weakly enforce conservation of mass, and normal Darcy’s velocity boundary conditions. These terms are obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{loss}}={}&\int_{\Omega}(d^{\mathrm{sf}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}))^{2}d\Omega,\end{split}

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}={}&\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\left(\diver{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}\right)d\Omega\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}\cdot\left((\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n+1})d\Gamma,\end{split}

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\ (\frac{p_{n+1}}{M}+{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}+\diver{\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}}\Delta t+s_{n+1}\Delta t-\frac{p_{n}}{M}-{B}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n})d\Omega

(27)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega_{q}}\beta^{q}_{n+1}\ (\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\bar{q}_{n+1})d\Gamma.

(28)

Taking the first variation of Eq. (
24
), using common rules of the calculus of variations
(
Felippa 1994
;
Nguyen et al. 2020
)
, after applying the divergence theorem leads to

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\text{tot}}=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{u}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{p}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{q}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{q}}=0,

where each contribution is as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{u}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}:\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1})d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}B\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{p}={}&\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta p}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1})\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{M}\delta p_{n+1}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}B\delta p_{n+1}\grad{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:\boldsymbol{I}d\Omega\\
&-\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\delta p_{n+1}B\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma-\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta p_{n+1}B\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}={}&-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}d\Omega,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\left[\frac{1}{M}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})+B({\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}-{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n})+s_{n+1}\Delta t\right]d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\Delta td\Omega\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\Delta td\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}:\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1})d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}:\grad{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}}d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}:(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{n})d\Gamma\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}:(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{n})d\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{q}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1})\Delta td\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\grad{\beta}^{\text{mass}}\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{q}}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\beta^{q}\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\beta^{\text{mass}}\boldsymbol{n}\Delta td\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}={}&\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}\cdot((\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n+1})d\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{q}}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{q}}\delta\beta^{q}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\bar{q}_{n+1})d\Gamma.

We reduce the number of field variables by setting
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}=-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}
defined on the boundary
\partial\Omega_{\sigma}
and
\beta^{q}_{n+1}=-\Delta t\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
defined on the boundary
\partial\Omega_{q}
. After some mathematical manipulations, we obtain the following residuals (corresponding to Euler-Lagrange equations) along with the additional restrictions on fields
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}
and
\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}
as extra boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{p}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{u}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}:\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}_{n+1})d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}B\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{p}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta p}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{C_{\mathrm{f}}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1})\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{M}\delta p_{n+1}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega\\
&+\int_{\Omega}B\delta p_{n+1}\grad{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:\boldsymbol{I}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\beta^{\text{mom}}}_{n+1}={}&-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n+1}d\Gamma+\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\beta^{\text{mass}}}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\left[\frac{1}{M}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})+B({\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}-{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n})+s_{n+1}\Delta t\right]d\Omega\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\bar{q}_{n+1}\Delta td\Gamma=0,\end{split}

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}:\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1})-\grad{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}}\right)d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{q}_{n+1}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1})-\grad{\beta}^{\text{mass}}\right)\Delta td\Omega=0.

(34)

We further reduce number of independent fields (and equations) by the local (point-wise) satisfaction of Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
) via :

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1}+\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}:\grad{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega,

(35)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1}\cdot\grad{\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega.

(36)

Remark 2
.

The coupled system (Eqs. (
29
), (
30
), (
31
), and (
32
)) is constant
even if there is any hidden non-linearity in the database. As such, one may simply store the
LU factorization (with pivoting) of the tangential matrix at the beginning and
use the decomposition to facilitate the Gaussian elimination and therefore improve the
efficiency by avoiding the use of a linear solver at each iteration.

2.3
Option 2: Hybrid data-driven poroelasticity 1 (model-based solid + data-driven fluid solver)

In this section, our goal is to introduce an alternative formulation where the
fluid constitutive responses are determined from the data-driven approach whereas the solid constitutive responses are determined from a material model.
This treatment is appropriate for a large variety of poroelasticity problems where
the confidence interval for any given hydraulic model is expected to be significantly larger than the solid elasticity counterpart after normalization, e.g., see Fig.
3
. Examples of these materials include sandstone, clay, rock, and biological tissues where the estimated effective permeability is often considered accurate if it is within the same order of the benchmark values whereas the elasticity error is expected to be much smaller
(
Paterson and Wong 2005
)
.

Andrä et al. 2013a
. Red star points are experimental observations reported in
Andrä et al. 2013a
for Berea sandstone. The errors between model predictions and experimental data are considerably less for shear and bulk moduli than permeability. Notice that the permeability is plotted in the Log scale, and so the difference between model and experiments are even greater in real scale. These plots are reproduced from
Andrä et al. 2013a
, see figures 2(a), 4(a), and 6(a) in the original reference.

The idea is that we know an appropriate constitutive law for solid deformation, and there is a database
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}
for flow constitutive behavior which is a set of finite pairs of pressure gradient and Darcy’s velocity. The following derivation is not restricted to a specific solid constitutive law. The only assumption is that the constitutive law is derived from an energy potential in the context of hyperelasticity for the small deformation limit.

Since the data-driven part is only accounted for the hydraulic constitutive law, the defined norm for the poroelasticity phase space Eq. (
12
) includes only the fluid contribution. We incorporate solid constitutive model as additional constraint in the set
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}
defined in the fully data-driven formulation, and we designate the new set by
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}
to distinguish them. The resultant problem statement for this hybrid option reads,

\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=\underset{\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}{\arg}\left\{\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{2}\right\},

(37)

where
\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}=\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
.
Recall that
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1}
encodes the fluid-related variables of
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, i.e., pressure gradient and Darcy’s velocity. The norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}}
defined over the fluid phase space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{f}}
is the same as Eq. (
14
). Note that the unit of the objective function in Eq. (
37
) is power, but it is energy for the fully data-driven Eq. (
10
). Following the same procedure described in the fully data-driven formulation, we solve the above double-minimization by the fixed-point method consisting global and local steps.

For the global minimization step we have:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1},\mathcal{B}_{n+1};{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1})=\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{mass}},

(38)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}
is almost the same as
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}
defined in Eq. (
26
) with the only difference that the effective stress term is replaced by the constitutive relation; there exits a potential
\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
such that
\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
. In other words, the constitutive relation is imposed strongly (point-wise) herein. Notice that if there is a need to define an effective stress field as an independent field, similar to mixed formulations for elasticity
(
Washizu 1975
)
, one could weakly impose the constitutive relation by adding its contribution through a tensorial Lagrange multiplier defined over the whole domain. Here, we do not intend to arrive at formulations with strain or stress fields as independent fields. Because such formulations increase the number of unknowns significantly, also they require solution spaces with higher regularity such as Hilbert space
H(\textrm{div},\Omega)

(
Arnold and Falk 1988
;
Korsawe et al. 2006
;
Teichtmeister et al. 2019
;
Fahrendorf et al. 2020
)
; this regularity is needed to fulfill the continuity condition of normal traction between elements while tangential traction can be discontinuous.
The term
\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{mass}}
is exactly the same as
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}}
defined in Eq. (
28
) since nothing related to the mass balance is changed.
According to the norm defined in Eq. (
14
), the original objective (loss) function is as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{loss}}=\int_{\Omega}(d^{\mathrm{f}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}))^{2}d\Omega,

(39)

where the fluid distance function
d^{\mathrm{f}}(\cdot)
is defined in Eq. (
21
).
In this hybrid formulation, the effective stress
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}
is obtained from
a constitutive model, such as an hyperelastic strain-energy functional
\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))
, a key departure from the fully data-driven formulation in
Eq. (
24
). The corresponding first variation of Eq. (
38
) reads,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\text{tot}}=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\mathrm{mass}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{u}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{p}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{q}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{q}}=0,

(40)

where:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{u}={}&\int_{\Omega}B\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\grad{\beta}^{\text{mom}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}d\Omega\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}:(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}\otimes\boldsymbol{n})d\Gamma\\
&-\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}:(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}\otimes\boldsymbol{n})d\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{p}={}&\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta p}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1})d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{M}\delta p_{n+1}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}B\delta p_{n+1}\grad{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:\boldsymbol{I}d\Omega\\
&-\int_{\partial\Omega_{\sigma}}\delta p_{n+1}B\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma-\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta p_{n+1}B\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma,\end{split}

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{q}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1})d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\grad{\beta}^{\text{mass}}\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{q}}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\beta^{q}\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\beta^{\text{mass}}\boldsymbol{n}\Delta td\Gamma.

The remaining terms are identical to the fully data-driven counterparts, i.e.,

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}
,

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}
,

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}
, and

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB1}}_{\beta^{q}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{q}}
. For brevity,
we refer to those terms defined in the fully data-driven section.

Similar to the fully data driven formulation, we first reduce the number of unknown fields by setting
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}=-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}
defined on boundary
\partial\Omega_{\sigma}
and
\beta^{q}_{n+1}=-\Delta t\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
defined on boundary
\partial\Omega_{q}
. After some mathematical manipulations, we obtain the following residuals (corresponding to the Euler-Lagrange equations of Eq. (
38
)) along with the additional restrictions on fields
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}
and
\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}
as extra boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{u}
and
\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{p}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{u}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}B\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}\cdot\grad{\beta}^{\text{mom}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(41)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{p}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta p}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{C_{\mathrm{f}}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{r}^{*}_{n+1})\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{M}\delta p_{n+1}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}d\Omega\\
&+\int_{\Omega}B\delta p_{n+1}\grad{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:\boldsymbol{I}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(42)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\beta^{\text{mom}}}_{n+1}={}&-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}}:(\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n+1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-Bp_{n+1}\boldsymbol{I})d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n+1}d\Gamma\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n+1}d\Omega=0,\end{split}

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\beta^{\text{mass}}}_{n+1}={}&\int_{\Omega}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\left[\frac{1}{M}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})+B({\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n+1}-{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vol}}}_{n})+s_{n+1}\Delta t\right]d\Omega\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\Delta td\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}}\delta{\beta}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}\bar{q}_{n+1}\Delta td\Gamma=0,\end{split}

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{q}_{n+1}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\delta\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{f}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1})-\Delta t\grad{\beta}^{\text{mass}}\right)d\Omega=0.

(45)

We further reduce number of independent fields (and equations) by the local (point-wise) satisfaction of Eq. (
45
) via:

\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{q}^{*}_{n+1}+\Delta t\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}^{-1}\cdot\grad{\beta^{\text{mass}}_{n+1}}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega.

(46)

As mentioned in the previous section, the next step in the fixed-point method is to solve the local minimization problem. According to Eq. (
37
), the local minimization for this hybrid scheme reads as follow,

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a})=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}}(d^{\mathrm{f}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}))^{2},\ \mathrm{given}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{a}})\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1},

(47)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}\in\Omega^{h}
is an integration point in
\Omega^{h}
.
We will discuss how to solve the local sub-problem defined at each integration point in Sec.
3
.

Notice that in the data-driven formulation there are three types of computational costs: data availability (gathering data), global optimization (solving a system of equations), local optimization (searching inside a database).
The local optimization part is an NP-hard problem, and its computational cost grows exponentially by increasing the database size. Therefore, if the number of unknowns (degree of freedoms) is not so high while the database required for data-driven schemes is large, local optimization is the dominant source of computational cost.
As a result, the hybrid formulation could be more efficient than the fully data-driven counterpart
if the solid behavior can be accurately captured by a constitutive law with an acceptable standard deviation from the ground-truth.
We will discuss the computational issues concerning the local optimization step in Sec.
3
.

2.4
Option 3: Hybrid data-driven poroelasticity 2 (data-driven solid + mode-based fluid solver)

For comparison purposes, we present another hybrid formulation that includes Darcy’s law as the fluid constitutive model, whereas the solid deformation is predicted directly from a collection of data points.
We start with the fully data-driven problem defined in Eq. (
10
) and adjust it to be compatible with this hybrid formulation that considers just solid part as data-driven. To this end, we add Darcy’s equation as an additional constraint in the set
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
defined for the fully data-driven formulation. Darcy’s law reads as follows:

\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}=-\frac{1}{\mu^{\mathrm{f}}}\boldsymbol{k}(\grad{p}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}),

(48)

where
\mu^{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
are dynamic viscosity of pore fluid and intrinsic permeability tensor with unit
[\mathrm{Length}^{2}]
, respectively. Besides, the contribution of the fluid-related term in the norm defined in Eq. (
12
) should be excluded since only solid constitutive behavior is data-driven. Therefore, the problem definition in this hybrid formulation is as follows:

\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}=\underset{\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}{\arg}\left\{\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}}\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}\right\},

(49)

where
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1}=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{momentum}}_{n+1}\cap\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
is almost the same as
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}
defined for fully data-driven
formulation except it has an additional constraint from the hydraulic constitutive law (Darcy’s law). Recall that
{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1}
encodes the solid-related variables of
\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1}
, i.e., strain and effective stress. The norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}}
defined over the solid phase space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathrm{s}}
is the same as Eq. (
13

According to Eq. (
49
), the total objective function in the global minimization reads as:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{tot}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{n+1},\mathcal{B}_{n+1};{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1})=\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{mass}},

(50)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{mass}}
is almost the same as
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{mass}}
defined in Eq. (
28

48

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}
is exactly the same as
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}
defined in Eq. (
26
) since nothing related to the balance of linear-momentum is changed. The original objective function
\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{loss}}
in this hybrid formulation is the norm defined over solid phase space Eq. (
13
) as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{loss}}=\int_{\Omega}(d^{\mathrm{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}))^{2}\;d\Omega,

(51)

where solid distance function
d^{\mathrm{s}}(\cdot)
is defined in Eq. (
20

Recall that, here, the Darcy velocity
\boldsymbol{q}_{n+1}
is not an independent field in Eq. (
50
) which is not the case in Eq. (
24
). It relates to the pressure gradient field via the Darcy’s law. Taking the first variation of Eq. (
50

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\text{tot}}=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{loss}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{momentum}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\mathrm{mass}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{u}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{p}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{\sigma}}+\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{q}}=0,

where:

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{p}={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\grad{\delta p}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\grad{\beta}^{\text{mass}}\frac{\Delta t}{\mu^{\text{f}}}d\Omega-\int_{\partial\Omega}\grad{\delta p}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\beta^{\text{mass}}\boldsymbol{n}\frac{\Delta t}{\mu^{\text{f}}}d\Omega-\int_{\partial\Omega_{q}}\grad{\delta p}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\beta^{q}\boldsymbol{n}\frac{1}{\mu^{\text{f}}}d\Omega

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\delta p_{n+1}B\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\text{mom}}_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}d\Gamma,

and other terms remain similar to the fully data-driven formulation, i.e.,

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{u}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{u}

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mom}}}
,

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{\text{mass}}}

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}

, and

\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{HYB2}}_{\beta^{q}}=\delta\mathcal{L}^{\text{DD}}_{\beta^{q}}
.

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\sigma}_{n+1}=-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}

\partial\Omega_{\sigma}
and
\beta^{q}_{n+1}=-\Delta t\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{n+1}

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{n+1}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}_{n+1}+\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}:\grad{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathrm{mom}}_{n+1}\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega.

{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a})=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}_{n+1}}(d^{\mathrm{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}_{n+1},{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a})))^{2},\ \mathrm{given}\ {\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}_{n+1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x_{a}}})\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{coupled}}_{n+1},

3
Local minimization: physical response projection onto material space

This section describes the procedure to project the hydro-mechanical responses obtained
in the global minimization step onto the points that belong to the material phase space for each integration point.
The data-driven solver searches for the nearest neighbor point to a hydro-mechanical response inside the material phase space (database). Since the local minimization problems for the three formulations defined in Sections
2.2
,
2.3
and
2.4

are similar (except for the distance measure and the basis of the material space), the local minimization description is generic.

In this research, the nearest neighbor search (NNS) is used to facilitate this minimization step.
In Subsection
3.1
, we discuss alternative schemes for the fast NNS and explain why the k-d tree is an appropriate choice.
We then show how the energy metric can be rewritten as a Euclidean metric through
a linear projection.
This mapping provides a straightforward way of using available open-source packages for the fast NNS since these packages are generally developed and optimized for Minkowski’s metric functions.

3.1
Nearest Neighbor Search

Here, we first describe the Nearest Neighbor Search (NNS),
which enables us to locate the optimal data points in the phase space
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and

(\grad p,\boldsymbol{q})
equipped with different distance metrics. We also provide an overview and compare different NNS approaches and justify our choice of using the k-d tree data structure for the data-driven poroelasticity problem.

Assume
P
is a set of
n
points
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k
dimensions, i.e.,
P=\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{p}_{n}\}
. Nearest Neighbor Search (NNS) in a metric space is an optimization problem that determines the closest point
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}\in P
to a query point
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
P
and
q
belong to a metric space
M
equipped with a well-defined distance function
d\colon M\times M\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

(
Muja and Lowe 2014
;
Shakhnarovich et al. 2006
)
, i.e.,

\mathrm{NNS}(\boldsymbol{q},P;M)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}\in P}\ d(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}_{i}),

(59)

where a distance function must possess the following properties:

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}_{i})=0\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{q}\equiv\boldsymbol{p}_{i},

(60)

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}_{i})=d(\boldsymbol{p}_{i},\boldsymbol{q}),

(61)

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}_{j})\leq d(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}_{i})+d(\boldsymbol{p}_{i},\boldsymbol{p}_{j}).

(62)

In our application, for example, point
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}
encodes components of strain-stress pair. In three-dimensional cases,
k=12
since both strain and stress are symmetric.
A Brute-force algorithm that compares every element in the set is easy to implement but is inefficient for large sets
P
with a huge amount of queries, due to the resultant linear query time
O(nk)
. There are algorithms developed to make the search fast enough to be
close to a logarithmic query time for a variety of applications and circumstances
(
Malkov and Yashunin 2018
;
Shakhnarovich et al. 2006
;
Muja and Lowe 2014
)
.
A fast algorithm for NNS relies heavily on leveraging efficient data structures.
However, more efficient data structures could also be more complex and less memory-efficient, known as time-space trade-offs
(
Andoni et al. 2017
)
. For instance, if one intends to query the distance between two points of a set of
n
points, a naive approach is to store
n
points in an array with the space complexity
O(n)
and each time computes the distance of two points with the time complexity
O(n)
(access time complexity). However, the distance between all points can be tabulated with the space complexity
O(n^{2})
in the pre-processing step, and the query time becomes
O(1)
in this case.
Consequently, an efficient algorithm for the search must strike aw balance among speed, memory efficiency, robustness, and accuracy.

For any specific application number of data points in
P
, dimensionality of points, metric structure, and number of query points are among the first most important factors needed to be considered for choosing an efficient algorithm (cf.
Shakhnarovich et al. 2006
).
For example, brute-force is the most efficient approach when data size is small
(
Shakhnarovich et al. 2006
)
. As the number of query points or size of data set
P
is growing the need for other efficient NNS increases.

There exist two types of fast algorithms: exact NNS and approximate NNS (ANNS). Exact NNS are based on the hierarchical space partitioning
(
Friedman et al. 1977
;
Maneewongvatana and
Mount 1999a
)
. There are many variants of exact NNS, each of which proposes a different approach to split high dimensional space. Tree data structures are recognized as the efficient data structures for these type of algorithms, and k-d tree
(
Bentley 1975
;
Friedman et al. 1977
)
, vp-tree
(
Yianilos 1993
)
, R-tree
(
Beckmann et al. 1990
)
, and X-tree
(
Berchtold et al. 1996
)
are among popular ones. Exact NNS is in general fast algorithms loose their efficiency in dealing with high dimensional spaces, known as the curse of dimensionality. Roughly speaking, sub-linear or logarithmic time complexity of fast algorithms increase as
n
exceeds
2^{k}
limit for high dimensional spaces
(
Indyk 2004
;
Rajani et al. 2015
)
. Note that in computer science
k=20
is still not considered as a high dimensional space
(
Indyk 2004
;
Rajani et al. 2015
)
whereas in mechanics the space of strain-stress pairs with dimensionality 12 for 3D geometry is assessed as a high dimensional space. On the other side, it is proven that speed can be significantly improved by ANNS comparing with exact NNS if we slightly sacrifice the accuracy
(
Muja and Lowe 2014
)
. This means ANNS finds a point which is in the
\epsilon
-ball of the exact closest point. Algorithms based on hashing
(
Andoni and Indyk 2006
)
, randomized tree
(
Silpa-Anan and Hartley 2008
)
, and hierarchical graphs
(
Malkov and Yashunin 2018
)
are widely used for ANNS.

One efficient data structure suitable for low dimensional points (
k<20
) is the
k
-dimensional tree known as k-d tree
(
Bentley 1975
;
Rajani et al. 2015
)
. Bentley’s k-d tree as the standard k-d tree is a binary tree that recursively partitions the
\mathbb{R}^{k}
space along some orthogonal hyperplanes into at most
O(\log n)
hypercubes. Basically, each hyperplane is chosen to be orthogonal to a data axis and bisects the axis in a way that two resulting partitions have almost the same size. In the constructed tree, eventually, a maximum predefined number of data points are stored at each leaf node, and other nodes including root save information of each hyperplane. Once the tree structure is built, for a query point the algorithm traverses the tree from the root, and based on the underlying metric structure and hyperplane configuration narrows down the search into one half of the sub-tree until arrives at a leaf node. At the leaf node, it switches to the brute force distance calculation to find the closest point among the points inside the leaf node. What we intended to describe is a big picture of the idea behind k-d tree family of algorithms, and interested readers should refer to
(
Bentley 1975
;
Friedman et al. 1977
;
Maneewongvatana and
Mount 1999a
)
for more details. Note that Ball tree is suggested as an efficient alternative for high dimensional spaces
k>20

(
Rajani et al. 2015
;
Virtanen et al. 2020
)
which is not studied in this research.

3.2
local metric minimization

Here, we describe how to perform the local minimization efficiently to utilize techniques available in the NNS literature. First, we focus on the fully data-driven case and demonstrate
how the NNS may simplify the local distance calculations.
Then, we will formulate the solid and fluid local distance calculation in a unified and compact formulation.

Recall the local minimization problem corresponding to the distance function in Eq. (
18
).
Since we assume that the solid and fluid phase spaces are orthogonal to each other,
the local minimization can be done separately for each phase space, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a})=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{s}}}[d^{\mathrm{s}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{*},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}))]^{2};\ \mathrm{given}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{s}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}),

(63)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a})=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{f}}}[d^{\mathrm{f}}({\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}}^{*},\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}))]^{2};\ \mathrm{given}\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\mathrm{f}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}),

(64)

where each sub-problem is solved by an independent
nearest neighbor search inside the corresponding database.
The designated metric for the fully data-driven formulation reduces the phase space dimensionality by splitting a
phase space that contains 18 bases (12 for solid and 6 for fluid in 3D cases)
into two smaller phase spaces.
The advantage of this metric is two-fold. First, the NNS algorithms may perform more efficiently
in the two lower-dimensional phase space. Second, these two NNS tasks can be executed in parallel to further reduce computational time.

These local minimization problems should be solved for every quadrature point
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{a}
where
a=1,2,...,n_{\text{int}}
where
n_{\text{int}}
is the total number of integration points.
These global-local iterations continue until updated variables during the fixed-point iterations converge or their variations remain within a small tolerance. The data-driven solver may perform several fixed-point iterations within a time step. Hence the efficiency of the local minimization
is a dominant factor for the speed of the simulations, especially for large-scale problems.
In the following, we switch to conventional matrix notation in Linear Algebra. Consequently, the summation convention is no longer hold, in this section, unless we specify the other way around.

Note that both terms in solid distance function Eq. (
20
) have the same structure as
\boldsymbol{\tau}:\mathbb{K}:\boldsymbol{\tau}
where
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is a symmetric 2
nd
order tensor which is either
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}
or
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}
, and
\mathbb{K}
is a symmetric positive definite 4
th
order tensor which is either
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{s}}
or
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}
. This energy-like scalar variable can be represented in a full vector-matrix notation:

\boldsymbol{\tau}:\mathbb{K}:\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{t},

(65)

where
\boldsymbol{t}
is a column vector that stores information about
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{K}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix that encodes information of
\mathbb{K}
. There are multiple ways to obtain such vector-matrix quantities that preserve the left-hand side in Eq. (
65
), e.g., Kelvin and Voigt notations.
(
Mehrabadi and Cowin 1990
;
Itskov 2000
)
have shown that
\mathbb{K}
and
\boldsymbol{K}
share common spectral characteristics such as eigenvalues and eigenbases in the case of Kelvin and Voigt representations.

Fluid distance function Eq. (
21
) can be represented in a vector-matrix format without any further action, since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}
are 2
nd
order tensors which are also matrix. Hence, they have already followed the format in the right-hand side of Eq. (
65
). In the view of vector-matrix notation both solid and fluid distances have the same format but with different sizes, e.g., in 3D set-up
\boldsymbol{t}
has
6
components for the solid distance and
3
components for the fluid part. For the rest of the discussion in this section, we develop the framework for a general, unified metric as follows:

d(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{r})=\boldsymbol{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{t}+\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{r},

(66)

where
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are column vectors with size
n
, and
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{M}
are constant symmetric positive definite matrices. For solid distance function, we incorporate Voigt notation to store elements of tensors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}}^{*}
into vectors
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
, respectively, and build matrices
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{M}
equivalent to tensors
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{s}}
, respectively. For fluid distance function, vectors
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are equal to vectors
\grad{p}-{\grad{p}}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{*}
, respectively, and matrices
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{M}
are exactly equal to tensors
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{\mathrm{f}}
. Since any symmetric positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
possess decomposition
\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{C}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}
(e.g., via Cholesky factorization and matrix square root) we can write:

d(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{r})=\boldsymbol{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{t}+\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{t}+\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{r}=\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{r}},

(67)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}=\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{t}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}=\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{r}
are projected vectors of
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
, also
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
. The distance function can be further reduced to a more compact form by concatenation of vectors
\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}
into

\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{tabular}[]{ c}$\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}$\\

as follows:

d(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{t}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{r}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}.

(68)

The linear projection via the matrices

and
\boldsymbol{S}
for the corresponding variables is introduced for convenience.
This simple treatment enables us to adopt open-source libraries
developed by the NNS community for

with minimal implementation efforts.

Numerical Examples

Now we examine different aspects of the proposed data-driven formulations through six examples.
Before presenting the actual poroelasticity problems, in the appendix
A
, we show how effective is the k-d tree search for even a very simple problem. Then we will verify data-driven poroelasticity formulations by solving two well-known problems in the literature of geomechanics and biomechanics with available analytical solutions. Convergence with respect to the amount of data and fixed-point iterations will be studied. Three other problems will be investigated to demonstrate the capability and robustness of formulations in more complex circumstances such as non-linearity of material behavior. In the last two examples, we will discuss the fidelity and availability of data and the effectiveness of hybrid data-driven formulations. In the last example, we showcase the application of the hybrid data-driven formulation in dealing with an extreme condition where fluid flow database possesses a highly oscillatory porosity-permeability behavior. At the same time, the solid skeleton deformation follows a nonlinear porosity and pressure-dependent behavior. In all of the poroelastic examples, we utilize the k-d tree search method unless otherwise is noted. These examples are solved with our in-house data-driven solver developed in the Python programming language. The k-d tree data structure is adopted from
scipy.spatial.cKDTree

(
Virtanen et al. 2020

. Linear systems are solved by the LU factorization scheme implemented in the class

Hybrid formulation 1: data-driven fluid phase

-4.3\ \mathrm{GPa}/\mathrm{m}\leq\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}|^{\mathrm{FEM}}\leq 0

\partial p/\partial y=4.3\ \mathrm{GPa}/\mathrm{m}
at every quadrature point which is completely out of the solution space. The same configuration will be used for the fluid part of the fully data-driven formulation, as well.

The effect of numerical parameters in metric functions, see Eqs. (
19

20

21

5.5
Multifidelity Simulations of Berea Sandstone data

This problem is designed to demonstrate how the data-driven formulation can be used to handle
the different fidelities of the solid skeleton elasticity and the hydraulic responses of a porous medium. The difference in fidelity is a well-known issue in poromechanics problems where
the predictions of the effective permeability are often much less accurate
and
precise
than the solid constitutive responses
(
Paterson and Wong 2005
;
Lock et al. 2002
;
Andrä et al. 2013a
;
Sun and Wong 2018
)
. For instance, a porosity-permeability model capable of
predicting effective permeability with less than an order of the discrepancy from the
experiment measurement is often considered accurate
(
Bernabé et al. 2003
;
Costa 2006
)
. Meanwhile, experimental tests (e.g., uniaxial test and triaxial test) may routinely calibrate
an isotropic linear elasticity model delivering predictions with a few
percents of errors in the path-independent regime

(
Dvorkin et al. 1999
;
Zohdi and Wriggers 2001
;
Paterson and Wong 2005
;
Pimienta et al. 2015
)
.

In these highly plausible scenarios, the benefit of replacing the solid constitutive law with the data-driven approach might not outweigh the efficiency of the model-based approach, whereas
the data-driven approach may still be favorable due to the inevitable high variance of the predictions made by the deterministic porosity-permeability models.

In this example, we create the fluid database (see Fig.
25
(a)) by running 84 FFT simulations on
microCT images of Berea sandstone (provided by collaborator Prof. Teng-fong Wong) and use the data-driven approach in Sec.
2.3
to run simulations without using hydraulic constitutive law. Meanwhile, the solid constitutive response is obtained from the literature
Andrä et al. 2013a
.

Figure 23:
(a) Geometry and boundary conditions for Berea sandstone and (b) heterogeneous distribution of initial porosity with average 0.25 and standard deviation 0.02.

The boundary value problem is two-dimensional, and the plane strain condition is assumed, as shown in Fig.
23
(a). Lateral confining pressure of
6416.6\ \mathrm{[MPa]}
is applied at the beginning of the simulation and kept constant throughout the simulations. A downward displacement is prescribed at the top boundary with the rate
1.2\ \mathrm{mm/day}

To test the versatility of the data-driven approach in handling boundary value problems with a spatially heterogeneous domain, we employ an open-source code developed by
(

to generate a realization of initial porosity distribution.
This random porosity field is element-wise and is generated based on the exponential model with a mean value
0.25

0.02

(

Solid constitutive law is based on the hyperelastic energy functional introduced in chapter 6.4 of
(
Borja 2013a
)
as follows:

\psi(\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{s})=-p_{0}c_{r}\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon_{v0}-\epsilon_{v}}{c_{r}}\right)-\frac{3}{2}c_{\mu}p_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon_{v0}-\epsilon_{v}}{c_{r}}\right){\epsilon_{s}}^{2}+\psi_{0},

(72)

where
p_{0}
,
c_{r}
,
c_{mu}
, and
\epsilon_{v0}
are model parameters, and
\epsilon_{v}=\tr(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
and
\epsilon_{s}=\sqrt{2/3}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-1/3\epsilon_{v}\boldsymbol{I}\right\rVert
are volumetric and shear strains, respectively. This model introduces pressure-dependent bulk and shear moduli which is considered necessary for modeling soils and sands
(
Borja et al. 1997
;
Andrade and Borja 2006
)
. In this model, bulk and shear moduli are related to diagonal terms of Hessian matrix, 2
nd
derivative of energy functional with respect to volumetric and shear strains, with the following relations:

\displaystyle\kappa(\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{s})=-\frac{p_{0}}{c_{r}}\left(1+\frac{3c_{\mu}}{2c_{r}}{\epsilon_{s}}^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon_{v0}-\epsilon_{v}}{c_{r}}\right),

(73)

\displaystyle\mu(\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{s})=-c_{\mu}p_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon_{v0}-\epsilon_{v}}{c_{r}}\right).

(74)

Figure
24
plots bulk and shear moduli as exponential functions fitted to curves provided in
(

Due to the high variance of the permeability dataset, selecting and calibrating a porosity-permeability model is by no mean a trivial ask. While a complicated model fluctuated with porosity is likely to overfit the data, a simple model such as the Kozeny-Carmen model may underfit the data and hence lead to significant errors (see solid green line in Fig.
25
(a)). While extending the dimensions of the parametric space (e.g., adding features
(
Sudakov et al. 2019
)
or geometric measures such as fabric tensors
(
Sun et al. 2013b
)
of the hydraulic model may improve the bias-variance trade-off, the increased dimensionality may also increase the difficulty of deriving calibrating the constitutive laws or the training of neural network models.

Note that the data-driven approach does not impose or require any smoothness assumption among the data points in the phase space.
While the assumptions of smoothness, continuity or convexity are not
always physically justified, the lack thereof of the data-driven approach does
impose a high demand on the data. To make a prediction, there must be a sufficient population or density of data locally distributed
in the parametric space where the data point becomes admissible to the physics constraints. Recent work such as
Eggersmann et al. 2020
introduces an approach based on tensor voting to make data-driven methods working with less intensive data, but the demand for data is still noticeably higher than the conventional approach. A model-free method that can operate under less data by incorporating physics constraints and statistics will be considered in the future but is out of the scope of this study.

6
Conclusion

We establish both a fully data-driven and hybridized data-driven/model-base framework
that solve the poroelasticity problems either completely free of hand-crafted constitutive laws
or having them partially replaced by a data-driven scheme.
By extending the metric-minimization algorithm
originally proposed for single-physics elasticity problems,
we bypass the usages of material laws when the higher fluctuation hydraulic data may unavoidably lead to under-fitting. To overcome the significant computational time required to search the optimal data point from the discrete dataset, we introduce a k-d tree based nearest neighbor search for an energy metric for poromechanics problems. Comparisons between the k-d tree search and the brute-force search revealed that the k-d tree search may shorten the search time, especially when the dataset is large.
