1.
Introduction

Let
B(H)
denote the set of all bounded linear operators on a complex Hilbert
space
H
.
An operator
A\in B(H)
is positive definite (resp. positive semi-definite)
if
\langle Ax,x\rangle>0
(resp.
\langle Ax,x\rangle\geq 0)
holds for all non‐zero
x\in H
. If
A
is
positive semi‐definite, we denote
A\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P}\subset B(H)
be the sets of all positive
semi-definite operators and positive definite operators, respectively.
To reach inequalities for bounded self-adjoint operators on Hilbert space, we shall use
the following monotonicity property for operator functions:

If
X\in B(H)
is self adjoint with a spectrum
Sp(X)
, and
f,g
are continuous real valued functions
on an interval containing
Sp(X)
, then

f(t)\geq g(t),~t\in Sp(X)\Rightarrow~f(X)\geq g(X).

(1.1)

For more details about this property, the reader is referred to
[
18
]
.

For
A,B\in\mathcal{P}
the geometric mean
A\sharp B
of
A
and
B
is defined by

A\sharp B=A^{\frac{1}{2}}(A^{\frac{-1}{2}}BA^{\frac{-1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For
A,B\in\mathcal{P}
, let

R(A,B)=\max\{r(A^{-1}B),r(B^{-1}A)\}

where
r(A)
means the spectral radius of
A
and we have

r(B^{-1}A)=\inf\{\lambda>0:A\leq\lambda B\}=\|B^{\frac{-1}{2}}AB^{\frac{-1}{2}}\|.

R(A,B)
was defined in
[
3
]
, and many nice properties
of
R(A,B)
were shown as follows: If
A,B,C\in\mathcal{P}
, then

\displaystyle(i)~R(A,C)\leq R(A,B)R(B,C)\text{ (triangle inequality) }

\displaystyle(ii)~R(A,B)\geq 1,\text{ and }R(A,B)=1\text{ iff }A=B

\displaystyle(iii)~\|A-B\|\leq(R(A,B)-1)\|A\|.

The Thompson distance
d(A,B)

on the convex cone
\Omega
of positive definite operators is defined by

d(A,B)=\log R(A,B)=\max\{\log r(A^{-1}B),\log r(B^{-1}A)\},

see
[
3
,
7
,
16
]
. we know that
\Omega

is a complete metric space with respect to this metric and the corresponding metric topology
on
\Omega
agree with the relative norm topology.

As a basic inequality with respect to the metric, the following inequality for a weighted geometric mean of two operators hold
[
3
,
7
]
:

d(A_{1}\sharp_{\nu}A_{2},B_{1}\sharp_{\nu}B_{2})\leq(1-\nu)d(A_{1},B_{1})+\nu d(A_{2},B_{2})

for
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}\in\Omega
and
\nu\in(0,1)
.

Let
A
and
B
be two positive definite operators on a Hilbert space
H
and
\Phi
be a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
.
Ando
[
1
, Theorem 3]
showed the
following property of a positive linear map in
connection with the operator geometric mean.

\Phi(A\sharp B)\leq\Phi(A)\sharp\Phi(B).

(1.2)

Inequality (
1.2
) is extended to an operator mean
\sigma
in Kubo-Ando theory as follows:

\Phi(A\sigma B)\leq\Phi(A)\sigma\Phi(B),

In particular for the weighted geometric mean, we have

\Phi(A\sharp_{\nu}B)\leq\Phi(A)\sharp_{\nu}\Phi(B),

(1.3)

where
\nu
is a real number in
(0,1]
.
A complementary inequality to inequality (
1.3
), is the following important inequality
[
14
]
:

Let
0<m_{1}I\leq A\leq M_{1}I
and
0<m_{2}I\leq B\leq M_{2}I

and
0<\nu\leq 1
, then

K(h,\nu)\Phi(A)\sharp_{\nu}\Phi(B)\leq\Phi(A\sharp_{\nu}B),

(1.4)

where
h=\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}
and
K(h,\nu)
is the generalization Kantorovich constant defined by

K(h,\nu)=\frac{h^{\nu}-h}{(\nu-1)(h-1)}\left(\frac{(\nu-1)(h^{\nu}-1)}{\nu(h^{\nu}-h)}\right)^{\nu}.

The special case,
K(h,2)=K(h,-1)=\frac{(1+h)^{2}}{4h}
is called the Kantorovich constant.
The generalization Kantorovich constant
K(h,\nu)
has the following properties:

\displaystyle K(h,\nu)=K(h,1-\nu)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<K(h,\nu)\leq 1\text{ for }0<\nu\leq 1,

and
K(h,\nu)
is decreasing for
\nu\leq\frac{1}{2}
and increasing for
\nu>\frac{1}{2}
therefore for all

\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
K(h)=K(h,\frac{1}{2})=\frac{2h^{\frac{1}{4}}}{1+h^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq K(h,\nu)
.

2.
A complementary inequality to weighted geometric mean

First, we state another complementary inequality to (
1.3
) with respect to
R(A,B)
as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two positive definite operators in
\mathcal{P}
, then

K\left(R(A,B)^{2},\nu\right)\Phi(A)\sharp_{\nu}\Phi(B)\leq\Phi(A\sharp_{\nu}B).

(2.1)

Proof.

we know that

\dfrac{1}{R(A,B)}A\leq B\leq R(A,B)A.

(2.2)

Define a linear map
\Psi
on
H
by

\Psi(X)=\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(A^{\frac{1}{2}}XA^{\frac{1}{2}})\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Then
\Psi
is a unital positive linear map. From (
2.2
)

\displaystyle m=\dfrac{1}{R(A,B)}\leq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq R(A,B)=M.

(2.3)

Using (
1.4
) for
\Psi
, and (
2.3
), we get

K(h,\nu)\Psi(I)\sharp_{\nu}\Psi(X)\leq\Psi(I\sharp_{\nu}X)

(2.4)

where
X=A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and
h=\dfrac{{}_{M}}{m}=R^{2}(A,B)
.

From (
2.4
), we have

K(h,\nu)\Psi(X)^{\nu}\leq\Psi(X^{\nu})

or

\displaystyle K(h,\nu)\Big(\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(B)\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{\nu}\leq\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi\Big(A^{\frac{1}{2}}(A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\nu}A^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle K(h,\nu)\Phi(A)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(B)\Phi(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{\nu}\Phi(A)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\Phi\Big(A^{\frac{1}{2}}(A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\nu}A^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big).

Therefore, we obtain the desired inequality (
2.1
).
∎

The inequality (
1.4
) with
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
, becomes the following inequality.

\frac{2R(A,B)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1+R(A,B)}\Phi(A)\sharp\Phi(B)\leq\Phi(A\sharp B).

(2.5)

Next, we recall the Kadison’s Schwarz inequalities

\Phi(A^{2})\geq\Phi(A)^{2},~~\Phi(A^{-1})\geq\Phi(A)^{-1}

(2.6)

and two complementary inequalities to them, whenever
0<m\leq A\leq M
:

\displaystyle\frac{(m+M)^{2}}{4mM}\Phi(A)^{2}\geq\Phi(A^{2}),~~\frac{(m+M)^{2}}{4mM}\Phi(A)^{-1}\geq\Phi(A^{-1}).

(2.7)

The following inequality unifies Kadison’s Schwarz inequalities into a single form.

\Phi(BA^{-1}B)\geq\Phi(B)\Phi(A)^{-1}\Phi(B).

(2.8)

If
0<m\leq A,B\leq M
, then the following inequality is a complementary inequality to (
2.8
)

\frac{(m+M)^{2}}{4mM}\Phi(B)\Phi(A)^{-1}\Phi(B)\geq\Phi(BA^{-1}B).

(2.9)

The similar to the proof of Theorem
1
, we give another complementary inequality to (
2.8
) with respect to
R(A,B)
as follows:

\frac{(1+R(A,B)^{2})^{2}}{4R(A,B)}\Phi(B)\Phi(A)^{-1}\Phi(B)\geq\Phi(BA^{-1}B).

(2.10)

To compare the inequalities (
1.4
) with (
2.5
) and (
2.9
) with (
2.10
), the following examples
show that neither Kantorovich constants in (
1.4
) and (
2.9
) nor Kantorovich constants in (
2.5
) and (
2.10
) are uniformly
better than the other.

Example 1
.

Let
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&\frac{1}{3}\\
\end{bmatrix}

and
B=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}
.
Clearly,
m_{1}I=\frac{1}{3}I\leq A\leq 2I=M_{1}I
and
m_{2}I=\frac{1}{2}I\leq B\leq 4I=M_{2}I
. Then

A^{-1}B=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&\frac{3}{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}

and

B^{-1}A=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{3}\\
\end{bmatrix}
,
therefore
R(A,B)^{2}=4\leq h=\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}=48
.
Consequently,
K(R(A,B)^{2},\frac{1}{2})\geq K(h,\frac{1}{2})
and
K(R(A,B)^{2},2)\leq K(h,2)
.

Example 2
.

Let
C=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

and
D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&2\\
\end{bmatrix}
.
Clearly,
m_{1}I=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}I\leq C\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}I=M_{1}I
and
m_{2}I=I\leq D\leq 2I=M_{2}I
. Then

C^{-1}D=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\
-1&4\\
\end{bmatrix}

and

D^{-1}C=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}
,
therefore
R(C,D)^{2}=\left(\frac{5+\sqrt{17}}{2}\right)^{2}\geq h=\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}=\frac{(3+\sqrt{5})^{2}}{2}
.
Consequently,
K(R(C,D)^{2},\frac{1}{2})\leq K(h,\frac{1}{2})
and
K(R(C,D)^{2},2)\geq K(h,2)
.

Theorem 2
.

If
f
is an operator convex function on
(0,\infty)
and
\Phi
is a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
. Then

\displaystyle K(h,2)f(\Phi(A))\geq\Phi(f(A))\geq f(\Phi(A))

(2.11)

for every positive definite operators
A
, where
h=R(A,B)^{2}
, or
h=\frac{M}{n}
whenever
0<m\leq A\leq M
.

Proof.

It is known that every operator convex function

f
on
(0,\infty)
has a special integral representation as follows:

\displaystyle f(t)=\alpha+\beta t+\gamma t^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{\lambda+t}d\mu(\lambda),

(2.12)

where
\alpha,\beta
are real numbers,
\gamma\geq 0
, and
\mu
is
a positive finite measure. Thus

\displaystyle\Phi(f(A))=\alpha 1_{K}+\beta\Phi(A)+\gamma\Phi(A^{2})+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(\lambda A^{2}(\lambda+A)^{-1})d\mu(\lambda),

and

\displaystyle f(\Phi(A))=\alpha 1_{K}+\beta\Phi(A)+\gamma\Phi(A)^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda\Phi(A)^{2}(\lambda+\Phi(A))^{-1}d\mu(\lambda).

By (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda A^{2}(\lambda+A)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\lambda\Phi(A^{2}(\lambda+A)^{-1})=\lambda\Phi(A(\lambda+A)^{-1}A)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\Phi(A)(\Phi(\lambda+A))^{-1}\Phi(A)=\lambda\Phi(A)(\lambda+\Phi(A))^{-1}\Phi(A)

\displaystyle=\lambda\Phi(A)^{2}(\lambda+\Phi(A))^{-1}.

Therefore
\Phi(f(A))\geq f(\Phi(A))
, since
\Phi(A^{2})\geq\Phi(A)^{2}
.

On the other hand, from (
2.9
) or (
2.10
), we get

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda A^{2}(\lambda+A)^{-1})=\lambda\Phi(A^{2}(\lambda+A)^{-1})=\lambda\Phi(A(\lambda+A)^{-1}A)

\displaystyle\leq K(h,2)\lambda\Phi(A)(\Phi(\lambda+A))^{-1}\Phi(A)=K(h,2)\lambda\Phi(A)(\lambda+\Phi(A))^{-1}\Phi(A)

\displaystyle=K(h,2)\lambda\Phi(A)^{2}(\lambda+\Phi(A))^{-1}.

Therefore
\Phi(f(A))\leq K(h,2)f(\Phi(A))
, since
\Phi(A^{2})\leq K(h,2)\Phi(A)^{2}
.
∎

3.
The power means and the Karcher means

The Karcher mean, also called the Riemannian mean, has long
been of interest in the field of differential geometry. Recently it has been used in a diverse variety of settings:
diffusion tensors in medical imaging and radar, covariance matrices in statistics, kernels in machine learning and
elasticity. Power means for positive definite matrices and operators have been introduced in
[
11
,
12
]
.
It is shown in
[
5
,
10
]
that the Karcher mean and power means satisfy all ten properties stated in
[
2
]
.
The Karcher mean and power means have recently become an important tool
for studying of positive definite operators and an interesting subject for matrix analysts and operator theorists.
We would like to refer the reader to
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
17
,
21
]
and references therein for more information.

Geometric and power means of two operators
can be extended over more than
3
‐operators via the solution of operator
equations as follows. Let
n
be a natural number, and let
\triangle_{n}
be a set of all probability
vectors, i.e.,

\triangle_{n}=\{\omega=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in(0,1)^{n}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1\}.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
and
\omega=(\mathrm{w}_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\triangle_{n}
.
Then the weighted Karcher mean
\Lambda(\omega;\mathbb{A})
is defined by a unique
positive solution of the following operator equation;

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{w}_{i}\log(X^{\frac{-1}{2}}A_{i}X^{\frac{-1}{2}})=0.

(3.1)

The weighted power mean
P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})
is defined by a unique
positive solution of the following operator equation;

I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{w}_{i}(X^{\frac{-1}{2}}A_{i}X^{\frac{-1}{2}})^{t}\text{ for }t\in(0,1],

or equivalently

X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{w}_{i}(X\sharp_{t}A_{i})\text{ for }t\in(0,1].

(3.2)

For
t\in[-1,0)
, it is defined by

P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})=(P_{-t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}^{-1}))^{-1},

where
\mathbb{A}^{-1}=(A_{1}^{-1},\dots,A_{n}^{-1})
.

Lawson and Lim in
[
11
, Corollary 6.7]
gave an important connection between the weighted Karcher means and
the weighted power means in strong operator topology, as follows:

\Lambda(\omega,\mathbb{A})=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}P_{t}(\omega,\mathbb{A}).

(3.3)

Moreover, they also showed that the following property holds for these means:

P_{-t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})\leq\Lambda(\omega;\mathbb{A})\leq P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})\text{ for all }t\in(0,1].

(3.4)

Let
\Phi
be a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
. In
[
12
]
is proved that if
t\in(0,1]
,
then

\Phi(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}))\leq P_{t}(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A})),

(3.5)

where
\Phi(\mathbb{A})=(\Phi(A_{1}),\dots,\Phi(A_{n}))
.

In the following Theorem, we give a reverse inequality to the inequality (
3.5
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Phi
be a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
and
n\geq 2
be a positive integer. If
0<t\leq 1
and
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
Then

\Phi(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}))\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{t}}P_{t}(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A})),

(3.6)

where
h_{0}=\max\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}R^{2}(A_{i},A_{j})
.

Proof.

Let
t\in(0,1]
and
X_{t}=P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})
. Then

X_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}(X_{t}\sharp_{t}A_{i})
, by the operator equation (
3.2
).
From inequality (
2.1
), we have

K\left(R^{2}(X_{t},A_{i}),t\right)(\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))\leq\Phi(X_{t}\sharp_{t}A_{i}).

(3.7)

In the proof of proposition 3.5 of
[
11
]
, was shown that

d(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}),A_{j})\leq\max\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}d(A_{i},A_{j}),

Where
d
is the Thompson metric. This implies that

R^{2}(X_{t},A_{j})=R^{2}(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}),A_{j})\leq\max\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}R^{2}(A_{i},A_{j})=h_{0}.

Since the function
K(h,\frac{1}{2})=\frac{2h^{\frac{1}{4}}}{1+h^{\frac{1}{2}}}
is decreasing for
h\geq 1
, Thus

K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq K\left(R^{2}(X_{t},A_{i}),\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq K\left(R^{2}(X_{t},A_{i}),t\right)

(3.8)

Define

f(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}(X\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))
. Then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f^{n}(X)=P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A})
for any
X>0
.
Let
\Phi
be a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
. From relations (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we get

\displaystyle\Phi(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\Phi(X_{t}\sharp_{t}A_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}K\left(R^{2}(X_{t},A_{i}),t\right)(\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}K\left(R^{2}(X_{t},A_{i}),\frac{1}{2}\right)(\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)(\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))=K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)f(\Phi(X_{t})).

Since
f
is an increasing function, we have

\displaystyle f\left(K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\Phi(X_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\left(K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}(\Phi(X_{t})\sharp_{t}\Phi(A_{i}))

\displaystyle=K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t-1}f(\Phi(X_{t}))\geq f^{2}(\Phi(X_{t})).

Therefore

\displaystyle\Phi(X_{t})\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)f(\Phi(X_{t}))\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1+(1-t)}f^{2}(\Phi(X_{t})).

Consequently

\displaystyle\Phi(X_{t})\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)f(\Phi(X_{t}))\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1+(1-t)+(1-t)^{2}+\dots+(1-t)^{n-1}}f^{n}(\Phi(X_{t})).

This implies that

\displaystyle\Phi(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}))=\Phi(X_{t})\geq K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{t}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f^{n}(\Phi(X_{t}))=K\left(h_{0},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{t}}P_{t}(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A})).

∎

Let
X
be a positive definite operator on a Hilbert space
H
.
Then
m=\lambda_{min}(X)\leq X\leq\lambda_{max}(X)=M
, where
\lambda_{min}(X)(\text{resp. }\lambda_{max}(X))
is the minimum (resp. maximum) of the specrum of
X
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\Phi
be a unital positive linear map on
B(H)
and
n\geq 2
be a positive integer. If
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
Then

\Lambda(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A}))\geq\Phi(\Lambda(\omega;\mathbb{A}))\geq\frac{4\hbar}{(1+\hbar)^{2}}~\Lambda(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A})),

(3.9)

where

m_{i}=\lambda_{min}(A_{i}),~M_{i}=\lambda_{max}(A_{i})
and
\hbar=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{M_{i}}{m_{i}}
.

Proof.

From relations (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), we get

\Phi(\Lambda(\omega;\mathbb{A}))\leq\Phi(P_{t}(\omega;\mathbb{A}))\leq P_{t}(\omega;\Phi(\mathbb{A})).

(3.10)

If
t\rightarrow 0
in (
