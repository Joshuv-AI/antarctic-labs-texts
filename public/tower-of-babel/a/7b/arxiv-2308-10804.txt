Abstract
. We consider a Kantorovich potential associated to an optimal transportation problem between measures that are not necessarily absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, but are comparable to the Lebesgue measure when restricted to balls with radius greater than some δ > 0 \delta>0 . Our main results extend the classical regularity theory of optimal transportation to this framework. In particular, we establish both Hölder and Sobolev regularity results for Kantorovich potentials up to some critical length scale depending on δ \delta . Our assumptions are very natural in the context of the numerical computation of optimal maps, which often involves approximating by sums of Dirac masses some measures that are absolutely continuous with densities bounded away from zero and infinity on their supports.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Optimal transportation with general measure

The theory of optimal transportation, first introduced by Monge in the late 1800 (and later generalized by Kantorovich), has
been extensively studied from a theoretical point of view in the past few decades.
It has also found numerous applications to problems both theoretical and applied.
Following the work of Brenier
[
5
]
, a major topic of mathematical research has been the study of the regularity
of the optimal transportation map between absolutely continuous measures.

More recently, partly motivated by numerical applications, there has been a lot of interest in optimal transportation problems between general measures. Indeed, numerical schemes often involve measures that are sums of Dirac masses. Solutions of optimal transportation problems between such singular measures cannot be expected to be smooth in general. However it is natural to expect these solutions to have some nice properties when the general measures, possibly discrete, are close (in some sense) to some nice measures (see Assumption
1
).
For example, if some measures are obtained by discretizing some smooth densities at a scale
\delta
, they “look” smooth when seen only at scales larger than
\delta
. One can then ask whether the solution of the corresponding optimal transportation problem also looks smooth when seen at a correspondingly large enough scale (whose relation to
\delta
needs to be identified).

This article introduces a general strategy to extend the classical regularity theory for optimal transportation problem between continuous densities to such a discrete setting. The main take away of our analysis is that the solutions of this discrete optimal transportation problem have the usual Sololev regularity when mollified at a scale
\delta^{1/2}

(see Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
) - this scale is optimal in the sense that it is consistent with known convergence rates of numerical schemes (see Section
1.3
).

Let us now briefly recall the setting of optimal transportation with general measures.
Throughout the paper, we will focus exclusively on the classical quadratic cost (we refer for example to
[
36
]
for a detailed presentation of the topic in a much more general framework):

Given two convex bounded open sets
\Omega
and
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 1
), we consider two Borel probability measures
\mu
,
\nu
such that

\mathrm{spt}\,\mu\subset\overline{\Omega}
and
\mathrm{spt}\,\nu\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}
.
In Kantorovich’s setting, admissible transference plans are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. We denote by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set of transference plans:

\Pi(\mu,\nu):=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\,;\,\pi(A\times\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mu(A),\;\pi(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B)=\nu(B)\}

(where
A
and
B
are any measurable sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
The classical optimal transportation problem (with quadratic cost), is concerned with the minimization problem

(1)

C(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-z|^{2}d\pi(x,z).

The existence of a minimizer for (
1
) is given by the following classical result (see for instance
[
35
, Theorem 2.12]
):

Theorem 1.1
.

If
C(\mu,\nu)<\infty
, then there exists an optimal transference plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
(i.e. a minimizer of (
1
)).
Furthermore, any
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is optimal if and only if there is a convex, lower semi-continuous, function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
such that

\pi
is concentrated in the graph of
\partial\psi
, that is

(2)

\mathrm{spt}\,(\pi)\subset\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi):=\left\{(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;z\in\partial\psi(x)\right\}\mbox{.}

Recall that for a convex function
\psi
, the subdifferential of
\psi
at
x
is given by

\partial\psi(x):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,\psi(y)\geq\psi(x)+z\cdot(y-x)\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

We will also use the notation
\partial\psi(A)=\cup_{x\in A}\partial\psi(x)
.

In this general framework, neither the optimal plan
\pi
nor the
Kantorovich potential

\psi
are unique.
We also note that without changing the values of
\psi
in
\mathrm{spt}\,\mu
, we can assume (see Appendix
C
) that
\psi(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{O}}(x\cdot z-\phi(z))
for some function
\phi:\mathcal{O}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
. Since
\mathcal{O}
is convex, we can thus assume that

(3)

\partial\psi(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}},

and in fact we can even assume that
\partial\psi(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a subset of the closed convex envelop of
\mathrm{spt}\,\nu
.
Since
\mathcal{O}
is bounded, this implies that
\psi
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

There is a vast literature devoted to the properties of Kantorovich potentials when the measures
\mu
and
\nu

are of the form

(4)

\mu=f(x)\,dx\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\nu=g(y)\,dy

for some measurable functions
f
and
g
satisfying

(5)

\lambda\leq f(x)\leq\Lambda\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,\quad\lambda\leq g(y)\leq\Lambda\qquad\forall y\in\mathcal{O}.

Before stating our assumptions and main results, we will briefly review (in a completely non-exhaustive way) some key aspects of the classical regularity theory in that framework.

1.2.
Regularity theory under assumptions (
4
)-(
5
)

Under assumptions (
4
)-(
5
), the Kantorovich potential is uniquely defined (up to a constant) on
\Omega
. In fact, we have:

Theorem 1.2

(Brenier’s theorem
[
6
]
)
.

If
\mu
does no give mass to small sets, then there is a unique optimal transference plan
\pi
, given by

d\pi(x,y)=d\mu(x)\delta(y=\nabla\psi(x)
where
\nabla\psi
is the unique (
\mu
-a.e.) gradient of a convex function satisfying
\nabla\psi\#\mu=\nu
.
Furthermore, we have

\mathrm{spt}\,(\nu)=\overline{\nabla\psi(\mathrm{spt}\,(\mu)))}.

In this framework, the so-called
Brenier map

T=\nabla\psi
is the unique solution of the Monge problem and when
the measures
\mu
and
\nu
satisfy (
4
)-(
5
), the function
\psi
solves the generalized Monge-Ampère equation

(6)

\det(D^{2}\psi(x))=h(x)\qquad\mbox{ with }h(x):=\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(\nabla\psi(x))}\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega

with second type boundary conditions (
3
).
In particular,
\psi
satisfies

(7)

\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda}\chi_{\Omega}\leq\det D^{2}\psi\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}\chi_{\Omega}

in a weak sense (the
Brenier sense
) - see for instance
[
13
,
16
]
.
When
\mathcal{O}
is convex,
Caffarelli proved in
[
13
]
that
\psi
is a strictly convex solution of (
7
) in the following
Alexandrov sense
:

(8)

\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda}|A\cap\Omega|\leq|\partial\psi(A)|\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}|A\cap\Omega|\qquad\mbox{for any Borel set $A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

The regularity theory for the classical Monge-Ampère equation, developed by Caffarelli
[
9
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, for strictly convex solutions of (
8
) then implies that
\psi
is
C^{1,\alpha}_{loc}
(see also
[
21
]
).
Further regularity (
C^{2,\alpha}_{loc}(\Omega)
) for the solution of Monge-Ampère equation (
6
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions
where proved for example by Caffarelli
[
8
]
when the right hand side
h
is
C^{\alpha}_{loc}(\Omega)
.

Since further Hölder regularity is not expected under condition (
5
) alone, it is natural to ask about the Sobolev regularity of
\psi
.
In the framework of Monge-Ampère equation (
6
) (with Dirichlet boundary condition),
Caffarelli
[
8
]
proved that the solution is in
W^{2,p}
provided the right-hand side satisfies
\|h-1\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\eta
for some small enough
\eta
(depending on
p
).
When
f
and
g
only satisfy condition (
5
), De Philippis and Figalli
[
17
]
proved that
D^{2}\psi\in L\log L_{loc}
. This result was improved by De Philippis, Figalli and Savin
[
15
]
who proved that
\psi\in W^{2,1+\varepsilon}_{loc}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
.

1.3.
General measures and formal presentation of our results

In this paper, we do not make either assumption (
4
) or (
5
), instead requiring only that
\mu
and
\nu
are comparable to the Lebesgue measure when restricted to balls of radius greater than or equal to some fixed
\delta>0
(see Assumption
1
).
A simple and very important example we have in mind is that of measures which are sums of Dirac masses approximating some absolutely continuous measures satisfying (
4
)-(
5
).
Our goal is then to prove the equivalent of the
C^{1,\alpha}_{loc}
,
W^{2,1}_{loc}
and
W^{2,1+\varepsilon}_{loc}
regularity results mentioned above, in our framework.
Since we consider measures that may not be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, the Kantorovich potential
\psi
(which still exists but may not be unique) does not solve the Monge-Ampère equation (either (
7
) or (
8
)) and none of the results discussed above apply.
In fact, Kantorovich potentials are not expected to be either strictly convex or
C^{1}
: Even in the semi-discrete case (when
\nu
is absolutely continuous but
\mu
is a sum of Dirac masses),
\psi
is a piecewise affine function
(see
[
3
]
and Appendix
C
). In particular
\psi
has both
‘flat parts’ and ‘corners’.
To our knowledge, few quantitative estimates on the convex function
\psi
are known in this setting.

This discrete framework with
\mu
,
\nu
sums of Dirac masses is important for numerical computations of optimal maps between regular measures, as described in Appendix
A
. There exists an extensive literature investigating numerical schemes for optimal transportation. This is a challenging question, both from the point of view of the strongly non-linear Monge-Ampère equation or when starting from the Monge-Kantorovich formulation. In the later case for example, it would be natural to discretize the starting and target measures by sums of Dirac masses (which fits well with our framework) since that formally reduces optimal transportation to a linear programming problem. But this linear programming behave quadratically in the number of points in the discretization, leading to highly non-trivial computational challenges. We briefly refer to
[
1
,
2
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
31
,
32
]
or
[
33
]
for examples of various numerical schemes. Several recent works have focused on proving convergence rates for these numerical schemes: see for instance
[
3
]
for the semi-discrete case and
[
29
]
for the fully discrete case, together with
[
27
,
30
]
.

Such convergence rates are not the goal of this paper. Instead,
we develop a regularity theory for the potential
\psi
, "up to a length scale depending on
\delta
", which parallels the classical regularity theory developed under assumptions (
4
)-(
5
). To roughly summarize the main results stated in the next section, if we consider the optimal transportation problem corresponding to some potential
\psi
between measures
\mu
and
\nu
that are discretized at some scale
\delta
, then we will prove that
\bar{K}_{r}\star\Delta\psi
belongs to
L^{p}
for some
p>1
where
\bar{K}_{r}
is a mollifying kernel at scale
r
with
\delta^{1/2}\lesssim r
. We expect that the precise uniform regularity results that we derive here will prove useful for the analysis of complex numerical schemes; and in particular multi-scale schemes.

We want to emphasize that an important aspect of our results is to obtain the regularity of
\bar{K}_{r}\star\Delta\psi
for the conjectured critical scale
r\sim\delta^{1/2}
.
Indeed, this rate is consistent with the known rates of convergence of numerical schemes:
If
\mu_{\delta}
and
\nu_{\delta}
are discretizations at scale
\delta
of some nice measures
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
, then
the corresponding Kantorovich potentials
\psi_{\delta}
and
\psi_{0}
are expected to satisfy

\psi_{\delta}-\psi_{0}=O(\delta^{1/2})
in some appropriate norms - see
[
29
]
where weighted
L^{2}
error estimates on the associated transport maps are derived (see also
[
3
]
in the semi-discrete case).
Using the regularity of
\psi_{0}
, it is possible, via some interpolation, to derive some
L^{p}
bounds on
\bar{K}_{r}\star\Delta\psi_{\delta}
. However such an approach will lead to regularity results at scale
r\geq\delta^{\alpha}
with
\alpha<1/2
.
In addition, we note that the convergence results of
[
3
,
29
]
requires some
C^{2}
regularity assumptions for the limiting potential
\psi_{0}
while our result is completely independent of the properties of
\psi_{0}
(in fact it does not even require the existence of
\psi_{0}
).

The results of the present paper should also be compared with the previous work of the authors (with M. Molina) in

[
26
]
:
Under similar assumptions on the measures
\mu
and
\nu
, in dimension
2
, the function
\psi
is proved to be strictly convex up to some discrete scale and approximately
C^{1}
again up to some discrete scale.
The
C^{1,\alpha}
and
W^{2,p}
regularity results proved here (in any dimension) are thus a significant improvement of these results.
On the other hand, we note that the results in
[
26
]
are local and do not require any control
on
\psi
near the boundary of
\Omega
, while in the present article we require some sort of boundary conditions (see a precise discussion in the next section).
This is in complete accord with what is known for solutions of Monge-Ampère equation with a right-hand side bounded from below and from above: strict convexity holds locally in dimension
2
independently of the boundary condition but
in higher dimensions, classical counter-examples show that boundary conditions are necessary.

Finally, we mention the question of partial regularity to optimal transportation between
C^{0,\alpha}
densities which shows that the map
T=\nabla\psi
is
C^{1,\alpha}
up to removing sets of measure
0
; see
[
18
,
20
]
and
[
23
]
for a variational approach. This suggests that some notion of approximate
C^{2,\alpha}
estimate on
\psi
(and not only
C^{1,\alpha}
as we prove here) may hold, though with stronger assumptions on the measures
\mu
and
\nu
than what we are making here.

2.
Main assumptions and main results

2.1.
The measures
\mu
and
\nu

In this paper, we assume that the measures
\mu
and
\nu
are comparable to the Lebesgue measure up to a certain scale. Our main assumption can be written as follows:

Assumption 1
.

There exist
\lambda
,
\Lambda>0
such that

(9)

\lambda|B_{r}|\leq\mu(B_{r})\leq\Lambda|B_{r}|,\quad\mbox{for all ball $B_{r}\subset\Omega$ with $r\geq\delta$}

and

(10)

\lambda|B_{r}|\leq\nu(B_{r})\leq\Lambda|B_{r}|\quad\mbox{for all ball $B_{r}\subset\mathcal{O}$ with $r\geq\delta$.}

We note that (
9
) is equivalent to assuming that the measure convolution
\mu_{\delta}=\mu\star\frac{1}{|B_{\delta}|}\chi_{B_{\delta}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and has density
f_{\delta}
satisfying

\lambda\leq f_{\delta}\leq\Lambda\quad\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega\,;\,d(x,\partial\Omega)>\delta\}.

We also point out that the use of balls in (
9
)-(
10
) is not crucial.
Indeed, up to multiplying
\delta
by a constant depending only on the dimension, we could formulate our assumption with ellipsoids whose smallest radius are greater than
\delta
or even with general convex sets with inner radius greater than
\delta

(see Proposition
B.1
)

Assumption
1
is natural in situation in which the measures
\mu
and
\nu
are obtained by approximating measures satisfying (
4
)-(
5
). This construction, which plays a key role in the numerical computation of optimal maps (see
[
3
,
29
]
) is recalled in Appendix
A
.

We already mentioned that in this setting, it does not make sense to write the Monge-Ampère equation and that many of the classical tools (e.g. comparison principle) are thus no longer available to prove our result.
Another important observation, which is less fundamental and more technical, is the fact that the renormalization of convex set provided by John’s Lemma
3.1
is extensively used in the literature to prove regularity results. Unfortunately, Assumption
1
is not invariant under affine transformations (since the image of a ball could be a very elongated ellipsoid).
We will thus need to largely forgo the use of such renormalization in this paper.

2.2.
The potential
\psi

Throughout the paper,
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex lower semi-continuous function associated to an optimal transference plan
\pi
as in Theorem
1.1
and satisfying (
3
).
In particular,
\psi
satisfies

(11)

\mu(A)\leq\nu(\partial\psi(A))\quad\mbox{ for all Borel sets $A\subset\Omega$}

where
\partial\psi(A)=\cup_{x\in A}\partial\psi(x)
.
Indeed, we can write

\mu(A)=\int_{A}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\pi=\int_{A}\int_{\partial\psi(A)}d\pi\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\partial\psi(A)}d\pi=\nu(\partial\psi(A)).

This inequality might be strict in general but that we also have the inequality

(12)

\mu(\partial\psi^{*}(B))\geq\nu(B)\quad\mbox{ for all Borel sets $B\subset\mathcal{O}$}

where
\psi^{*}
denotes the Legendre transform, defined by

\psi^{*}(z)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(x\cdot z-\psi(x)\big)\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Indeed, we recall that
\psi^{*}
is also convex and lower semi-continuous (and (
3
) implies that
\psi^{*}(z)=+\infty
when
z\notin\overline{\mathcal{O}}
)
and that we have the following equivalences:

(13)

x\cdot y=\psi(x)+\psi^{*}(y)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;y\in\partial\psi(x)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x\in\partial\psi^{*}(y).

In particular (
2
) is equivalent to

\mathrm{spt}\,(\pi)\subset\left\{(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;x\in\partial\psi^{*}(z)\right\}
and we can prove
(
12
) just as we proved (
11
).

These two properties (
11
) and (
12
), together with the boundary condition (
3
), are actually the only properties of
\psi
that we will use to prove our result.

The relation between these inequalities and Theorem
1.1
was made clear by a result that we proved in
[
26
]
and which we recall here for the reader’s convenience:

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
\mu,\;\nu
are two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
C(\mu,\nu)<\infty

and let
\psi
be a proper lower semi-continuous convex function satisfying
(
11
) and (
12
).
Then there exists an optimal transference plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
(minimizer of (
1
)) such that
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subset\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi)
.

2.3.
Sections of convex functions

As mentioned above, a fundamental difference between our work and most previous work that deals with absolutely continuous measure
\mu
and
\nu
, is the fact that we cannot use Monge-Ampère equation and the comparison principle usually associated to elliptic equations and which plays a key role in
the work of Caffarelli as well as in the more recent partial regularity theory of

[
37
,
19
,
20
]
.
Note that a variational approach (relying on optimal transportation arguments rather than using some barriers for Monge-Ampère equation) to the partial regularity theory of
[
20
]
was recently developed in
[
23
,
22
]
.

On the other hand, we will rely heavily on another tool that has proved extremely useful in the study of Monge-Ampère equation: The sections of convex functions. We recall that
given
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
and
t\geq 0
, the section centered at
x_{0}
with height
t
is the convex set:

S(x_{0},p_{0},t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,\psi(x)\leq\psi(x_{0})+p_{0}\cdot(x-x_{0})+t\}.

Sections associated to solutions of Monge-Ampère equation have good properties and play a key role in most regularity results, see for instance
[
11
]
,

[
25
]
,

[
24
, Chapter 3]
.
An important contribution of this paper, and a key aspect of our proofs, will be to show that these classical properties still hold under Assumption
1
, provided
t\geq C_{0}\delta
for some constant
C_{0}
depending only on the dimension
n
and on the diameters of
\Omega
and
\mathcal{O}
.
These properties are summarized in Proposition
3.5
and proved in Section
7
. In order to avoid the use of renormalization of convex sets (which, as noted earlier, is not compatible with Assumption
1
), we will rely on the notion of polar body of a convex set to prove several of our results.

A final remark is that our regularity results will be interior regularity results: they hold in a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
such that
there exists
\rho
such that

(14)

S(x,p,\rho)\subset\Omega\quad\forall x\in\Omega^{\prime}\quad\mbox{for some }p\in\partial\psi(x).

The existence of such a
\rho>0
for a given subset
\Omega^{\prime}

is classical when the measures satisfy (
4
)-(
5
) (and
\mathcal{O}
is convex), but it
is not obvious in more general framework.
We will show in Appendix
A
that when
\mu
and
\nu
are obtained as discrete approximation of regular measures, then
\rho
satisfying (
14
) exists provided
\delta
is small enough for any
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
. Technically speaking (
14
) then holds for all
p\in\partial\psi(x)
though we do not need this stronger version for our analysis.

The fact that some sort of condition is needed near the boundary of
\Omega
is however well-understood. In the case
\delta=0
, the regularity of the solutions to Monge-Ampère equation depends on the boundary conditions. For example in dimension
3
or more, local solutions to Monge-Ampère equation with a right-hand side bounded from below and from above may fail to be strictly convex (dimension
n=2
is a special case for which we refer in particular to the discussion in
[
26
]
). Because of our setting with discrete measure, straightforward boundary conditions would not make much sense and are replaced by (
14
) which is a natural extension: To violate (
14
), one would in particular need to have very elongated sections for small
\rho
that touch the boundary of
\Omega
.

2.4.
Main results

Our first result concerns the
C^{1,\alpha}_{loc}
regularity of
\psi
up to some length scale depending on
\delta
.
Forzani and Maldonado
[
21
]
proved that the engulfing property of sections (see Proposition
3.5
-(iii)) is equivalent to the
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of the potential.
Using similar ideas, we will show the
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity up to a scale depending on
\delta
.
In the theorem below, the constants
C_{0}
,
\theta
and
\beta
are the constants appearing in Proposition
3.5
and in (
23
):

Theorem 2.2

(
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying (
3
),
(
11
) and (
12
) with
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying (
9
) and (
10
) for some
\delta>0
.
Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be such that
there exists
\rho>C_{0}\delta
such that (
14
) holds.
For all
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\subset\subset\Omega^{\prime}
there exists
a constant

C
depending on

\mathrm{dist}(\partial\Omega^{\prime},\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime})
,
\rho
,
\partial\psi(\Omega)
,
\Omega
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
n

such that

0\leq\psi(x)-\psi(y)-q\cdot(x-y)\leq\overline{C}|x-y|^{1+\frac{1}{\theta}}\qquad\forall q\in\partial\psi(y)

for all
x,y\in\Omega^{\prime\prime}
satisfying

|x-y|\geq C\delta^{1/\beta}.

Next, we turn our attention to the Sobolev regularity of
\psi
. When
\delta=0
(that is when (
4
)-(
5
) holds),
De Philippis and Figalli
[
17
]
proved the following result:

Theorem 2.3

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

2.5.
What is new in our method

We emphasize that the results stated above rely on some key improvements with respect to the existing strategies:

•

Precise properties of sections. A key point both for the
C^{1,\alpha}
and
W^{2,p}
regularity in the continuous case is the so-called engulfing property of sections
S(x,p,t)
(see Proposition
3.5
later for a precise formulation). This in particular allows to use Vitali’s covering lemma on sections. Unfortunately, the engulfing property does not hold for sections of a generic convex function and instead relied on the function
\psi
solving the Monge-Ampère equation. An important technical contribution of this paper is thus to extend the engulfing property to our discrete setting, based on optimal transportation instead of Monge-Ampère and
up to the precise critical discrete scale

t\sim\delta
.

•

“Fattening” small sets. The classical proof of
W^{2,p}
regularity in the case
\delta=0
relies on the identification in each section
S(x,p,t)
of a critical set
\Sigma
over which
\Delta\psi
is large enough and with
\Sigma=\partial\psi^{*}(V)
for
|V|
large enough as well. The proof of Theorem
2.5
requires enlarging those sets
\Sigma
and
V
so that
\Delta_{r}\psi
is large on
\Sigma
(which requires
\Delta\psi
to be large on a neighborhood of
\Sigma
) and
\Sigma
and
V
to be large enough to apply Assumption
1
. The way to enlarge those sets is delicate if one wants to keep some notion of duality between them
\Sigma=\partial\psi^{*}(V)
and be compatible with the critical scale
r\sim\delta^{1/2}
.

Outline of the paper:

The proof of Theorem

3.2.
John’s Lemma

We recall the following classical lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

[John’s Lemma]
Let
K
be a bounded convex set with non-empty interior. There exists an affine transformation
T(x)=Nx+b
such that

B_{1}(0)\subset T(K)\subset B_{n}(0).

Up to a translation of
K
, we can always assume that
b=0
. We then have

(19)

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,x^{*}Ax\leq 1\}\subset K\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,x^{*}Ax\leq n^{2}\}\qquad\mbox{ with }A=N^{*}N.

We denote by
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
the eigenvalues of the positive symmetric matrix
A=N^{*}N
. The set
x^{*}Ax=1
is an ellipsoid with radii
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. In particular,
(
19
) implies that
the inner and outer radii of
K
satisfy
\ell(K)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}
and
L(K)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}
. More precisely, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\leq\ell(K)\leq\frac{n}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\quad\mbox{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\leq L(K)\leq\frac{n}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}.

We also have
\|N^{*}\|\|N\|=\|A\|\sim\mathrm{Tr}(A)
(the first equality can be proved using the polar decomposition of matrices) and

(20)

\frac{1}{\ell(K)^{2}}\leq\mathrm{Tr}(A)\leq\frac{n^{3}}{\ell(K)^{2}}.

3.4.

The notion of sections of a convex function

with respect to the center of mass
x^{*}_{0}

1

1

We could also use dilations with respect to

The following simple result will be useful to control the inner radius of sections:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function such that
\partial\psi(\Omega)
is also a bounded subset of

For any section

\ell(S)\geq\frac{t}{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))},\qquad\ell(\partial\psi(S))\geq\frac{t}{L(\Omega)}.

The scaling of these bounds is not optimal in the framework of optimal transportation,
but is the best one can hope for in the case of general convex functions; in particular the first inequality is an equality when
\psi(x)=L|x|
.

Proof.

After replacing the function
\psi
with
\overline{\psi}(x)=\psi(x_{0}+x)-p_{0}\cdot x
, we can assume that
x_{0}=0
and

. We then have

This implies in particular that
\bar{\psi}(x)\leq\bar{\psi}(0)+L(\partial\psi(\Omega))|x|
and so
B_{t/L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}(0)\subset S(0,0,t)
. The first inequality follows.

To prove the second inequality, we note that if

is such that

, then the function

and

and so

.
We deduce that
B_{\frac{t}{L(\Omega)}}(0)\subset\partial\bar{\psi}(S)
which implies the second inequality
∎

Proof.

The dilation property of sections (
23
) together with condition (
14
) imply

S(x,p,t)\subset\left(\frac{t}{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}S(x,p,\rho)\subset\left(\frac{t}{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\Omega

and so

L(S(x,p,t))\leq\frac{L(\Omega)}{\rho^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Given
x,y\in\Omega^{\prime}
we fix
p\in\partial\psi(x)
such that (
14
) holds and choose any
q\in\partial\psi(y)
.
We then take
t
to be the smallest positive number such that
y\in S(x,p,t)
.
We then have

(25)

t=\psi(y)-\psi(x)-p\cdot(y-x),

and
L(S(x,p,t))\geq|y-x|
.

If
t\leq C_{0}\delta
, then
|x-y|\leq L(S(x,p,t))\leq L(S(x,p,C_{0}\delta))
, and Lemma
4.2
gives
L(S(x,p,C_{0}\delta))\leq C(C_{0}\delta)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
. We then have
|x-y|\leq C(C_{0}\delta)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
which contradicts (
24
) (for an appropriate choice of the constant
C
.
We must thus have
t\geq C_{0}\delta
.

Proposition
3.5
-(iii) (the engulfing property of sections) then gives

S(x,t)\subset S(y,q,\theta t)
provided
2t\leq\rho
(so that (
14
) guarantees that
S(x,p,2t)\subset\Omega
).
This implies

(26)

\psi(x)\leq\psi(y)+q\cdot(x-y)+\theta t.

In view of the definition of
t
, the condition
2t\leq\rho
is satisfied as long as
y\in S(x,\rho/2)
.
Since
B_{\rho/2L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}(x)\subset S(x,\rho/2)
(see the proof of Proposition
3.3
), this holds as long as
|x-y|\leq\frac{\rho}{2L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}
.

Combining (
25
) and (
26
), we deduce

(27)

\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}[\psi(x)-\psi(y)-q\cdot(x-y)]\leq(p-q)\cdot(x-y)

which holds for all
x,y\in\Omega^{\prime}
such that

C\delta^{1/\beta}\leq|x-y|\leq\frac{\rho}{2L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}
.

Let now
v
be a vector satisfying
|v|=1
and define

f(s)=\psi(y+sv)-\psi(y)-q\cdot sv.

Notice that
\psi
is convex in
s\in\mathbb{R}
. It is hence Lipschitz regular and differentiable almost everywhere. Hence
for
a.e.\;s
such that

C\delta^{1/\beta}\leq s\leq s_{0}=\min\left(\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial\Omega^{\prime}),\frac{\rho}{2L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}\right)

inequality (
27
) implies

\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}f(s)\leq sf^{\prime}(s).

Define
g(s)=s^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}f(s)
which is also Lipschitz on the corresponding interval of
s
(as it is away from
s=0
) and notice that
g^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
for
a.e.\;s
so that
g
is monotone increasing. Thus

0\leq f(s)\leq s^{\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}g(s_{0})\qquad\forall s\in[C\delta^{1/\beta},s_{0}],

which gives by taking
x=y+sv

0\leq\psi(x)-\psi(y)-q\cdot(x-y)\leq|x-y|^{1+\frac{1}{\theta}}g(s_{0})

for all
x
such that
C\delta^{1/\beta}\leq|x-y|\leq s_{0}
.

It remains to see that

g(s_{0})=s_{0}^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}f(s_{0})\leq\min\left(\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial\Omega^{\prime}),\frac{\rho}{2L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}\right)^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}L(\partial\psi(\Omega))\,L(\Omega)

to get the result.

∎

5.
The case
\delta=0
: Proof of Theorem
2.4

In this section we present the strategy for proving the Sobolev regularity of
\psi
in the simpler case
\delta=0
.
The general argument relies on the ideas introduced in
[
17
]
with only slight modifications.
Among the noteworthy modifications, we will not rely on the maximal function (this does not fundamentally change the arguments but makes the proof more self contained).
We will also avoid the use of renormalization of the sections, something that will be very important when we extend the proof to the case
\delta>0
in the next section.

5.1.
Preliminaries

We consider a convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and a section
S(x_{0},p_{0},t)
with

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
.
Since we can always replace
\psi
with
\psi(x)-\psi(x_{0})-p\cdot(x-x_{0})
, we can assume in this subsection, without loss of generality, that
\psi(x_{0})=0
and
p_{0}=0
.
We recall (see Lemma
3.1
) that up to a translation, we can also assume that there is a positive definite symmetric matrix
A
such that

(28)

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 1\}\subset S(x_{0},0,t)\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq n\}

and we stress out the fact that we cannot assume that
x_{0}=0
since we are already assuming that the ellipsoid in (
28
) are centered at
0
.

The following two lemmas, which will play a crucial role in the proof, are reminiscent of Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.3 in
[
17
]
:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
A
a positive definite symmetric matrix such that
S=S(x_{0},0,t)
satisfies (
28
).
There exists a constant
C
depending only on the dimension
n
such that

\fint_{S}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\mathrm{Tr}(A)\sup_{\{|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}}\psi

where
\fint f(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{|S|}\int_{S}f(x)\,dx
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
A
a positive definite symmetric matrix such that
S=S(x_{0},0,t)
satisfies (
28
). There exists
a constant
c
depending only on the dimension
n
and
a set
V\subset\partial\psi(S)
such that

(29)

|V|\geq c\frac{t^{n}}{|S|},\qquad\partial\psi^{*}(V)\subset S

and

(30)

\Delta\psi(y_{0})\geq\frac{t}{4n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)\qquad\forall y_{0}\in\Sigma:=\partial\psi^{*}(V)\subset S.

Furthermore, for all
q\in V
and
y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q)
we have

(31)

\psi(y)-\psi(y_{0})\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q\cdot(y-y_{0})\qquad\forall y\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}.

Note that when (
31
) holds, the convexity of
\psi
implies that
\partial\psi^{*}(q)
cannot contain any other point besides
y_{0}
, that is

\partial\psi^{*}(q)=\{y_{0}\}.

On the other hand it is not necessarily true that
(
31
) will hold for any another
q^{\prime}\in\partial\psi(y_{0})
.
This means that we may have
V\varsubsetneq\partial\psi(\Sigma)
(even though
\Sigma=\partial\psi^{*}(V)
).
When
\psi
is
C^{1}
and strictly convex (as is the case in
[
17
]
)
this distinction is not relevant (since
V=\partial\psi(\Sigma)
).
But it constitutes an important difficulty in extending the proof to the case
\delta>0
.
Since we will not be able to assume that
\psi\in C^{1}
in that case it will be very important that (
29
) provides
a lower bound on the measure of
V
rather than the measure of
\partial\psi(\Sigma)
.

An inequality similar to (
30
) is used in
[
17
]
, and this inequality is enough to prove our main result when
\delta=0
.
Inequality (
31
), which carries additional nonlocal information, will be needed to extend the result to the case
\delta>0
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We consider
the polynomial
p(x)=2-\frac{x^{*}Ax}{n^{2}}
which, in view of (
28
), satisfies
p(x)\geq 1
for
x\in S
and

\{p>0\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq\sqrt{2}n\}.

Let
\overline{\psi}:=\psi-\sup_{\{p>0\}}\psi
.
Since
\psi
is convex,
\Delta\psi=\Delta\overline{\psi}\geq 0
and a couple of integrations by parts give

\displaystyle\int_{S}\Delta\psi\,dx\leq\int_{\{p>0\}}p\Delta\overline{\psi}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\{p>0\}}p\nabla\overline{\psi}\cdot n+\int_{\partial\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}(-\nabla p\cdot n)+\int_{\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}\Delta p.

The first term vanishes since
p=0
on
{\partial\{p>0\}}
while the second term is non-positive since
-\nabla p\cdot n\geq 0
on
{\partial\{p>0\}}
and
\overline{\psi}\leq 0
in
\{p>0\}
. We deduce:

\displaystyle\int_{S}\Delta\psi\,dx\leq\int_{\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}\Delta p=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)\int_{\{p>0\}}(-\overline{\psi})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)\int_{\{p>0\}}[-\inf_{x\in{\{p>0\}}}\overline{\psi}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)|\{p>0\}|\,\sup_{\{p>0\}}\psi.

The result follows since
|\{p>0\}|=(\sqrt{2}n)^{n}|\{|x|_{A}\leq 1\}|\leq C|S|
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

The main idea stems from the proof of Lemma 3.3 in
[
17
]
:
We denote

U=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}

and set

p(x)=\frac{t}{2}(\frac{1}{4n^{2}}x^{*}Ax-1)
.
We have
-\frac{t}{2}\leq p\leq 0
in
U
, so that the function
w(x):=\psi(x)-t-p(x)
satisfies

w(x)\geq\psi(x)-t>0
in
U\setminus S
and
w(x_{0})\leq\psi(x_{0})-t+\frac{t}{2}=-\frac{t}{2}
.
We then consider
\overline{w}
the convex hull of
w
in
U
, defined by

\overline{w}(x)=\sup\{\ell(x)\,;\,\ell\mbox{ affine function s.t. }\ell\leq w\mbox{ in }U,\;\ell\leq 0\mbox{ on }\partial U\},

and the contact set

\Sigma=\{x\in U\,;\,\overline{w}(x)=w(x)\}.

Since
w>0
in
U\setminus S
and
\overline{w}\leq 0
, we have
\Sigma\subset S
.
Given
y_{0}\in\Sigma
and
q_{0}\in\partial\overline{w}(y_{0})
, since
\overline{w}
is convex, we have

w(x)\geq\overline{w}(x)\geq\overline{w}(y_{0})+q_{0}\cdot(x-y_{0})=w(y_{0})+q_{0}\cdot(x-y_{0})\qquad\forall x\in U.

The definition of
w
and the fact that
p
is a quadratic polynomial then imply

\displaystyle\psi(x)-\psi(y_{0})

\displaystyle\geq p(x)-p(y_{0})+q_{0}\cdot(x-y_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\nabla p(y_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}(x-y_{0})^{*}D^{2}p(y_{0})(x-y_{0})+q_{0}\cdot(x-y_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x-y_{0}|^{2}_{A}+(q_{0}+\nabla p(y_{0}))\cdot(x-y_{0})\qquad\forall x\in U.

We now denote

(32)

V=\{q_{0}+\nabla p(y_{0})\,|\,y_{0}\in\Sigma,\quad q_{0}\in\partial\overline{w}(y_{0})\}.

The inequality above implies in particular that
q:=q_{0}+\nabla p(y_{0})\in\partial\psi(y_{0})
so that
V\subset\partial\psi(\Sigma)
. As noted earlier, it also implies that
\partial\psi^{*}(q)=\{y_{0}\}
.

For all
q\in V
, we can write
q=q_{0}+\nabla p(y_{0})
with
y_{0}\in\Sigma
(and
q_{0}\in\partial\overline{w}(y_{0})
)
and the computation above shows that (
31
) then holds (and (
31
) implies (
30
)). We saw above that this also implies that
\partial\psi^{*}(q)=\{y_{0}\}
and so this proves that
(
31
) actually holds for all
q\in V
and
y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q)
(as claimed in the lemma).
This also implies that

\partial\psi^{*}(V)\subset\Sigma.

The fact that
\Sigma\subset\partial\psi^{*}(V)
is easy to show since for all
y_{0}\in\Sigma
(and
q_{0}\in\partial\overline{w}(y_{0})
) the vector
q=q_{0}+\nabla p(y_{0})
satisfies
q\in V
and
y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q)
. We thus have

\Sigma=\partial\psi^{*}(V).

So the only thing left to show to complete the proof of Lemma
5.2
is the lower bound on
|V|
:
Using the convexity of
\overline{w}
together with the smoothness of
p
, (
32
) implies:

V=\partial(\overline{w}+p)(\Sigma)

and so (since
D^{2}\overline{w}+D^{2}p\geq D^{2}\overline{w}\geq 0
)

|V|=|\partial(\overline{w}+p)(\Sigma)|=\int_{\Sigma}\det(D^{2}\overline{w}+D^{2}p)\,dx\geq\int_{\Sigma}\det(D^{2}\overline{w})\,dx=|\partial\overline{w}(\Sigma)|.

Furthermore, the definition of the convex hull gives

\partial\overline{w}(\Sigma)=\partial\overline{w}(U)
, hence

|V|\geq|\partial\overline{w}(U)|.

Since
\overline{w}=0
on
\partial U
and
\overline{w}(x_{0})\leq-t/2
,
U
is a section of
\overline{w}
with some center
\bar{x}_{0}\in U
and height
\overline{t}\geq\frac{t}{2}
. In particular,
(
64
) implies

|\partial\overline{w}(U)|\,|U|\geq\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{(2n)^{n}}\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{n}

(we can also use Alexandrov’s estimate, Proposition
3.4
)
and so

|V|\geq|\partial\overline{w}(U)|\geq ct^{n}\frac{1}{|U|}

and we conclude our proof by using the fact that
|U|=(2n)^{n}|\{|x|_{A}\leq 1\}|\leq(2n)^{n}|S|
.
∎

Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
hold for any convex function and do not make use of the optimal transportation assumptions. When
\psi
is an optimal transportation potential satisfying our assumptions, these lemmas yield:

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying
(
3
), (
11
), (
12
), with
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying (
9
) and (
10
) with
\delta=0
.
There exists some constants
c
and
C_{1}
depending on

,
\lambda
and
\Lambda
such that the following holds:
Given
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
,
t
such that
S(x_{0},p_{0},C_{1}t)\subset\Omega
and setting

\alpha:=\fint_{S(x_{0},p_{0},t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx,

there exists
\Sigma\subset S(x_{0},p_{0},t)
such that

\Delta\psi(x)\geq c\alpha\quad\forall x\in\Sigma,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|\Sigma|\geq c|S(x_{0},p_{0},t)|.

Proof.

Up to subtracting a linear function to

so that

Up to a translation, Lemma

5.2.
A reversed Chebychev’s inequality

The heart of the proof of Theorem
2.4
is the following reversed Chebychev’s inequality
(which is equivalent to (3.15) in
[
17
]
):

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying (
3
),
(
11
), (
12
), with
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying (
9
) and (
10
) with
\delta=0
. Assume moreover that
\Delta\psi\in L^{1}_{loc}
.
Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be such that there exists
\rho>0
such that (
14
) holds.
Then there exists
\alpha_{0}
,
c
and
C
depending on

\rho
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\Omega
and
\partial\psi(\Omega)

such that

(34)

\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\alpha\}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\alpha|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq c\alpha\}|\qquad\mbox{ for all $\alpha>\alpha_{0}$}.

Let us first show that this theorem implies Theorem
2.4
(the proof is similar to the proof in
[
17
]
. We recall it here for the sake of completeness).

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

For
\bar{c}\geq\max\{2,\alpha_{0}\}
(so that
\log(2+\gamma)\leq 2\log\gamma
for
\gamma\geq\bar{c}
), we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta\psi(x)\log(2+\Delta\psi(x))\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\bar{c}\log(2+\bar{c})|\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\leq\bar{c}\}|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\bar{c}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\log(\Delta\psi(x))\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\bar{c}\log(2+\bar{c})|\Omega|+2\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\bar{c}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\int_{1}^{\Delta\psi(x)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,d\gamma\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\bar{c}\log(2+\bar{c})|\Omega|+2\log\bar{c}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\bar{c}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\int_{\bar{c}}^{\Delta\psi(x)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,d\gamma\,dx

Now, by Fubini’s theorem, the last term can be written as

\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\bar{c}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\int_{\bar{c}}^{\Delta\psi(x)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,d\gamma\,dx=\int_{\bar{c}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\gamma\}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\,d\gamma

and we can use Theorem
5.4
: Since
\gamma>\bar{c}>\alpha_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{c}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\gamma\}\}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\bar{c}}^{\infty}|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq c\gamma\}|\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{c}\int_{c\bar{c}}^{\infty}|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq\gamma\}|\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{c}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx

Putting things together, we proved that for some constant
C
we have

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta\psi(x)\log(2+\Delta\psi(x))\,dx\leq C+C\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx

and we conclude using the fact that

\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\nabla\psi\cdot n\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega)L(\partial\psi(\Omega)).

∎

The proof of Theorem
5.4
is where we explicitly use the
L^{1}
regularity of
\Delta\psi
. More precisely we use the fact that the map
t\mapsto\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx
is continuous and satisfies

\lim_{t\to 0}\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx=\Delta\psi(x)\ a.e.

As remarked in the introduction, it should be possible to remove this assumption by correctly decomposing
\Delta\psi
into a regular and a singular part in the argument. Since this more general case is thoroughly covered by our theorem in the case
\delta>0
, we stay at the more elementary level where
\Delta\psi
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

We fix some
\varepsilon>0
(to be chosen later) and take
\alpha_{0}
such that

\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\alpha_{0}\qquad\forall x\in\Omega^{\prime},\;\forall t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho].

The existence of such an
\alpha_{0}
follows from the fact that for
t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho]
we have

\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\frac{1}{|S(x,t)|}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\frac{1}{|S(x,\varepsilon\rho)|}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi\,dx\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega)L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}{|S(x,\varepsilon\rho)|},

where
|S(x,\varepsilon\rho)|\geq c(\varepsilon\rho)^{n/2}
(see Proposition
3.5
(i)).

We now take
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and denote

E_{\alpha}=\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)>\alpha\}.

For a.e.
x\in E_{\alpha}
we have
\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx<\alpha
for all
t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho]
(by the choice of
\alpha
) and

\lim_{t\to 0}\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx=\Delta\psi(x)>\alpha

by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem.

We can thus define

t(x)=\sup\left\{t\leq\rho\,;\,\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx=\alpha\right\}

which satisfies in particular
t(x)\leq\varepsilon\rho
and

(35)

\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\alpha\quad\mbox{ for all }t(x)\leq t\leq\rho,\qquad\fint_{S(x,t(x))}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx=\alpha.

Since
E_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{x\in E_{\alpha}}S(x,t(x))
,
we can use Vitali’s covering lemma (see Corollary
3.6
) to extract a countable family
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the sections
S_{k}=S(x_{k},t(x_{k}))
are pairwise disjoint and
E_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{k}C_{*}S_{k}
with a
C_{*}
depending only on
n
,
\lambda
and
\Lambda
.

Using (
23
), we get

C_{*}S_{k}\subset S(x_{k},C_{**}t_{k})
with
C_{**}=C_{*}^{\beta}
,
provided
S(x_{k},C_{**}t_{k})\subset\Omega
, which holds if
C_{**}t_{k}\leq\rho
(since
x_{k}\in\Omega^{\prime}
).
Since we know that
t_{k}\leq\varepsilon\rho
, this last condition can be enforced by
choosing
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{C_{**}}
.

Note that we then also have
t_{k}\leq C_{**}t_{k}\leq\rho
.
The first inequality in (
35
) thus implies

\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{S(x_{k},C_{**}t_{k})}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\alpha|S(x_{k},C_{**}t_{k})|.

Proposition
3.5
(i) gives
|S(x_{k},C_{**}t_{k})|\leq Ct_{k}^{\frac{n}{2}}\leq C|S(x_{k},t_{k})|
hence

\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\alpha|S_{k}|.

We are ready to conclude: First, we write (since
E_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{k}C_{*}S_{k}
)

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\alpha}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\alpha\sum_{k}|S_{k}|.

Then, we apply Proposition
5.3
: We note that
S(x_{k},C_{1}t_{k})\subset S(x_{k},C_{1}\varepsilon\rho)\subset\Omega
(up to taking a smaller
\varepsilon
so that
\varepsilon\leq 1/C_{1}
) and
\fint_{S_{k}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx=\alpha
(see (
35
)). Proposition
5.3
thus provides the existence of a subset
\Sigma_{k}\subset S_{k}
with
|\Sigma_{k}|\geq c|S_{k}|
and
\Delta\psi\geq c\alpha
a.e. in
\Sigma_{k}
.
We can then write:

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\alpha}}\Delta\psi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha\sum_{k}|\Sigma_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha|\cup_{k}\Sigma_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta\psi(x)\geq c\alpha\}|

where the second inequality follows from the fact that the
S_{k}
, and therefore the
\Sigma_{k}
, are disjoint.
∎

6.
The case
\delta>0
: Proof of Theorem
2.5

We now turn to the proof of our main result when
\delta>0
.
We recall that we fix a kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
radially symmetric, supported in the ball
B_{1}(0)
and such that
\int_{B_{1}}|x|^{2}K(x)\,dx>0
and we define for any
r>0

\Delta_{r}\psi(x):=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y)[\psi(x+y)-\psi(x)]\,dy,\qquad K_{r}(x)=\frac{1}{r^{n}}K\left(\frac{x}{r}\right)

with
m_{0}=\frac{2}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}y_{1}^{2}K(y)\,dy}
.

We note that

(1)

For any
p\in\partial\psi(x)
, the radial symmetry of
K
allows us to write

(36)

\Delta_{r}\psi(x)=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y)[\psi(x+y)-\psi(x)-p\cdot y]\,dy

This formula makes the definition of
\Delta_{r}
useful when working with sections of the function
\psi
. It also implies that

\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any convex function.

(2)

If
p(x)
is an homogeneous polynomial of degree
2
, that is
p(x)=x^{*}Ax
for some symmetric matrix
A
, then we have (using the radial symmetry of
K
again):

(37)

\Delta_{r}p(x)=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\int K_{r}(y)A_{ij}y_{i}y_{j}\,dy=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\sum_{i}A_{ii}\int K_{r}(y)y_{i}^{2}\,dy=2\mathrm{Tr}A=\Delta p(x).

The choice of the normalization constant
m_{0}
is naturally critical here.

(3)

Together with Taylor’s formula, (
37
) also implies that if
\psi
is
C^{2}
then

\Delta_{r}\psi(x)=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\int K_{r}(y)\frac{1}{2}\partial_{ij}\psi(x)y_{i}y_{j}\,dy+o(1)=\Delta\psi(x)+o(1).

6.1.
Preliminary results

Our first task it to generalize Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2

using
\Delta_{r}
instead of
\Delta
.
The generalization of Lemma
5.1
is relatively straightforward (see Lemma
6.1
below),
and the main issue with Lemma
5.2
will be to fatten the set
\Sigma
so that
it is big enough to allow us to use the estimates (
9
)-(
10
) when
\delta>0
(see Propositions
6.4
and
6.6
).

Given a convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we fix

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
.
Since we can always replace
\psi
with
\psi(x)-\psi(x_{0})-p\cdot(x-x_{0})
, we can assume, without loss of generality, that
\psi(x_{0})=0
and
p_{0}=0
.
We recall (see Lemma
3.1
) that up to a translation, we can also assume that there is a positive definite symmetric matrix
A
such that

(38)

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 1\}\subset S(x_{0},0,t)\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq n\}.

We start with the following generalization of Lemma
5.1
:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
A
be a positive definite symmetric matrix
A
such that
the section
S=S(x_{0},0,t)
satisfies (
38
).
There exists a constant
C
depending only on
n
such that

(39)

\fint_{S}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\mathrm{Tr}(A)\sup_{\{|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}+B_{r}}\psi.

In particular, if
r\leq\ell(S)
, then

\fint_{S}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\mathrm{Tr}(A)\sup_{\{|x|_{A}\leq 3n\}}\psi.

Proof.

We introduce the polynomial
p(x)=2-\frac{x^{*}Ax}{n^{2}}
which satisfies (see (
38
))

\{p>0\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,x^{*}Ax<2n^{2}\},

and
p\geq 1
in
S
.

We denote
U=\{p>0\}+B_{r}
and

\overline{\psi}:=\psi-\sup_{U}\psi
. Since
\Delta_{r}\psi=\Delta_{r}\overline{\psi}
,
a simple change of variable gives

\displaystyle\int_{S}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq\int_{\{p>0\}}p(x)\Delta_{r}\overline{\psi}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}(x)\Delta_{r}p(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int_{\{p>0\}^{c}}\int_{\{p>0\}}K_{r}(x-y)p(y)\overline{\psi}(x)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int_{\{p>0\}}\int_{\{p>0\}^{c}}K_{r}(x-y)p(y)\overline{\psi}(x)\,dy\,dx.

Since
\mathrm{Supp}\,K_{r}\subset B_{r}
, we can write the last two terms as

\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int_{U\setminus\{p>0\}}\int_{\{p>0\}}K_{r}(x-y)p(y)\overline{\psi}(x)\,dy\,dx-\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int_{\{p>0\}}\int_{U\setminus\{p>0\}}K_{r}(x-y)p(y)\overline{\psi}(x)\,dy\,dx

which is negative since
\overline{\psi}(x)\leq 0
in
U
,
p(y)>0
in
\{p>0\}
and
p(y)<0
in
U\setminus\{p>0\}
.

Using (
37
), we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{S}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}(x)\Delta_{r}p(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)\int_{\{p>0\}}\overline{\psi}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)|\{p>0\}|\inf_{{\{p>0\}}}\overline{\psi}.

Since
\inf_{{\{p>0\}}}\overline{\psi}=\inf_{{\{p>0\}}}\psi-\sup_{U}\psi\geq-\sup_{U}\psi
(recall that
\psi\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and
|\{p>0\}|\leq C|S|
, we deduce

\int_{S}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\mathrm{Tr}(A)|S|\sup_{U}\psi

for some constant
C
depending only on
n
. The result follows.
∎

On the other hand, Lemma
5.2
would easily imply

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
A
be a positive definite symmetric matrix
A
such that
the section
S=S(x_{0},0,t)
satisfies (
38
).
There exists
a constant
c
depending only on the dimension, a subset

such that
\partial\psi^{*}(V)\subset S
and

(40)

|V|\geq c\frac{t^{n}}{|S|}

and for all

we have

(41)

\Delta_{r}\psi(y_{0})\geq\frac{t}{4n^{2}}\,\mathrm{Tr}(A)\qquad\forall y_{0}\in\Sigma:=\partial\psi^{*}(V).

Proof.

Lemma
5.2
implies the existence of the set
V\subset\partial\psi(S)
satisfying
\partial\psi^{*}(V)\subset S
and (
40
)
and such that for all
y_{0}\in\Sigma
, we have

(42)

\psi(y)-\psi(y_{0})\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q\cdot(y-y_{0})\qquad\forall y\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}

for some
q\in\partial\psi(y_{0})
.

If
r\leq\ell(S)
then for any
y_{0}\in\Sigma\subset S
we have
B_{r}(y_{0})\subset 2S\subset\{|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}
. Since
K_{r}
is supported in

, we can multiply (

) by
\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}K_{r}(y_{0}-y)
and integrate with respect to

to get (using the radial symmetry of

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\psi(y_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y_{0}-y)\left[\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q\cdot(y-y_{0})\right]\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y)y^{*}Ay\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{4n^{2}}\,\mathrm{Tr}(A).

∎

This result is however not enough for our purpose
because (
40
) does not yield any control on
|\Sigma|
(we can have
|\Sigma|=0
, even if
|V|>0
).
We thus need to "fatten" the set
\Sigma
by showing that (
41
) holds on a slightly larger set.
This will be done by considering the set

\Sigma_{h}=\bigcup_{q_{0}\in V,y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q_{0})}S(y_{0},q_{0},h)

for some appropriate
h
.

We emphasize that the choice of
\Sigma_{h}
is delicate. There could
a priori
be many ways of enlarging
\Sigma
, for example by simply taking
\Sigma+B_{h}
. However we need several precise properties on
\Sigma_{h}
(see Prop.
6.4
later) that we are not able to prove on
\Sigma+B_{h}
. Roughly speaking, we need to preserve some equivalent of the duality that we have between
V
and
\Sigma
. The definition above of
\Sigma_{h}
will naturally lead to a set
V_{h}
that properly enlarges
V
and maintains the inclusion
\partial\psi^{*}(V_{h})\subset\Sigma_{h}
.

First, we need to prove the following lemma which will allow us to extend the inequality (

) to the larger set
\Sigma_{h}
:

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
q_{0}\in V
and
y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q_{0})
, if
x
is such that

B_{r}(x)\subset\{x\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 2n\},

then

\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\geq\frac{t}{4n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)-\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}(q-q_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0})\qquad\forall q\in\partial\psi(x).

Proof.

We fix
q_{0}\in V
and
y_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(q_{0})
(we recall that
\partial\psi^{*}(q_{0})=\{y_{0}\}
for
q_{0}\in V
).
If
x
is such that
B_{r}(x)\subset\{x\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 2n\}
, then (
31
) applied to
x+y
implies that

\psi(x+y)-\psi(y_{0})\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x+y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q_{0}\cdot(x+y-y_{0})\qquad\forall y\in B_{r}(0).

Given
q\in\partial\psi(x)
, we have
\psi(y_{0})\geq\psi(x)+q\cdot(y_{0}-x)
and so

\displaystyle\psi(x+y)-\psi(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x+y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q_{0}\cdot(x+y-y_{0})+\psi(y_{0})-\psi(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x+y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q_{0}\cdot(x+y-y_{0})+q\cdot(y_{0}-x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x+y-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+q_{0}\cdot y-(q-q_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0})\qquad\forall y\in B_{r}(0).

We deduce

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y)[\psi(x+y)-\psi(x)]\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{8n^{2}}\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int K_{r}(y)\left[|x-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}+|y|_{A}^{2}\right]\,dy-\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}(q-q_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{4n^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(A)+\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\frac{t}{8n^{2}}|x-y_{0}|_{A}^{2}-\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}(q-q_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0}),

where we used the symmetry of
K_{r}
and the normalization
\int K_{r}(y)\,dy=1
.
∎

The sets
V_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
:

For
h>0
and
q_{0}\in V
, we introduce the set

\displaystyle W_{h}(q_{0})

\displaystyle:=q_{0}+\frac{h}{2}(S(y_{0},q_{0},h)-y_{0})^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,(q-q_{0})\cdot(x-y_{0})\leq\frac{h}{2},\;\forall x\in S(y_{0},q_{0},h)\}

where
\{y_{0}\}=\partial\psi^{*}(q_{0})
.
We note that the polar body
(S(y_{0},q_{0},h)-y_{0})^{\circ}
of a convex set (see definition (
58
)) is convex and contains
0
.
So
W_{h}(q_{0})
is expected to be a small convex set around
q_{0}
.
We then introduce the sets

V_{h}=\bigcup_{q_{0}\in V}W_{h}(q_{0})

and

\Sigma_{h}=\bigcup_{y_{0}\in\Sigma}S(y_{0},q_{0},h).

The properties of these sets are summarized in the proposition below.

Proposition 6.4
.

The following properties hold for some
k
depending only on
n

\partial\psi^{*}(W_{h}(q_{0}))\subset S(y_{0},q_{0},h)
and so
\partial\psi^{*}(V_{h})\subset\Sigma_{h}

\ell(S(y_{0},q_{0},h))\geq\frac{h}{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}
and
\ell(W_{h}(q_{0}))\geq\frac{h}{4nL(\Omega)}

If
t\geq C_{0}\delta
,
h\leq Mt
and
S(y_{0},q_{0},2M^{k}t)\subset\Omega
for all
y_{0}\in\Sigma
, then

\Sigma_{h}\subset\theta M^{k}S(x_{0},0,t)

For all
x\in S(y_{0},q_{0},h)
and
q\in\partial\psi(x)
we have

(x-y_{0})\cdot(q-q_{0})\leq M^{k}h

where the constants
C_{0}
,
M
and
\theta
are the constant appearing in Proposition
3.5
.

Before proving this proposition, we state the following lemma which follows from Proposition
7.4
, but for which we also give a short proof below.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\psi
be a convex function and
S=S(x_{0},p_{0},t)
be a section of
\psi
.
If
x\in S
is such that
2x-x_{0}\in S
(which means that
x\in\frac{1}{2}S
where the contraction is done with respect to
x_{0}
) then

(x-x_{0})\cdot(p-p_{0})\leq t\qquad\forall p\in\partial\psi(x)

and

x_{0}\in S(x,p,t).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
x_{0}=0
,
p_{0}=0
and
\psi(x_{0})=0
. We are then assuming
x\in S
and
2x\in S
, which gives
\psi(2x)\leq t
. The convexity of
\psi
implies

t\geq\psi(2x)\geq\psi(x)+p\cdot x\geq p\cdot x\qquad\forall p\in\partial\psi(x)

which is the first inequality in the lemma.
We can then write

\psi(0)\leq\psi(x)\leq\psi(x)-p\cdot x+t

which implies
0\in S(x,p,t)
and concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.4
.

The first property is an immediate consequence of the definition of
W_{h}(q_{0})
since

7.4
(see (

)) implies

\partial\psi^{*}(W_{h}(q_{0}))\subset S(y_{0},q_{0},h).

Next, we note that Proposition

\ell(S(y_{0},q_{0},h))\geq\frac{h}{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}

\ell(W_{h}(q_{0}))=\frac{h}{2}\ell((S(y_{0},q_{0},h)-y_{0})^{\circ})\geq\frac{h}{4nL(S(y_{0},q_{0},h))}\geq\frac{h}{4nL(\Omega)}

which is the second inequality in (ii).

Lemma
6.5
together with the scaling and engulfing property of sections implies (iii). Recall that the section
S(x_{0},0,t)
is included in the ellipsoid
\{(x-x_{0}^{*})^{*}\,A\,(x-x_{0}^{*})\leq n^{2}\}
for

2x_{0}^{*}-x_{0}\in n\,S(x_{0},0,t)
.
Since

the scaling property of sections, Proposition
3.5
-(ii), gives that

. Hence if

,

Hence we satisfy the assumption of Lemma

for

implying that
x_{0}\in S(y_{0},q_{0},M^{k}\,t)
.
The engulfing property of sections, namely Proposition

-(iii), implies

y_{0}\in S(y_{0},q_{0},M^{k}t)\subset S(x_{0},0,\theta M^{k}t).

Since this holds for all

as
\Sigma\subset S(x_{0},0,t)
, it follows that as long as

we have

\Sigma_{h}\subset S(x_{0},0,\theta M^{k}t)\subset\theta\,M^{k}\,S(x_{0},0,t).

Finally, if
x\in S(y_{0},q_{0},h)
then the scaling property of sections again gives

and so

implies (iv).
∎

The choice of

:

For any
x\in\Sigma_{h}
, we have
x\in S(y_{0},q_{0},t)
for some
q_{0}\in V
,
y_{0}\in\Sigma
. So Lemma

together with Proposition

implies that for some constant

We thus define

so that we have:

which is the most important property of

.
In addition, unlike

, the set

is "large" enough for us to use Assumption

to control its size.

we recall that

\widetilde{\Sigma}=\bigcup_{y_{0}\in\Sigma}S(y_{0},q_{0},h_{0}),\quad\tilde{V}=\bigcup_{q_{0}\in V}W_{h_{0}}(q_{0}),

and since

, we have

In particular, Proposition

(ii) implies that if

\tilde{V}
are "fat" enough to be seen by the (possibly discrete) measures
\mu
and
\nu
.
Finally, we note that (
22
) implies
\frac{t}{\ell(S)^{2}}\geq c
for some constant
c
, but (
45
) requires this quantity to be arbitrarily large.
Instead of (
45
), we could actually assume that
r\geq C_{3}\delta^{1/2}
for some large constant
C_{3}
.
However, we will only use this proposition for sections for which

is very large (see (

)), so this condition will not be an issue.

Proof.

In view of (
43
) and (
44
), Assumption
1
and Proposition
3.2
imply

\mu(\widetilde{\Sigma})=\mu(\widetilde{\Sigma}\cap\Omega)\leq\Lambda|\widetilde{\Sigma}|\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\nu(\widetilde{V}\cap\mathcal{O})\geq\lambda|\widetilde{V}\cap\mathcal{O}|

so that (

6.2.
A reversed Chebychev’s inequality when
\delta>0

As in the case
\delta=0
, Theorem
2.5
follows from the following reversed Chebychev’s inequality:

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying
(
11
), (
12
), with
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying (
9
) and (
10
)) with
\delta>0
.
Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be such that
there exists
\rho>0
such that (
14
) holds.
Then there exists
\alpha_{0}
,
r_{0}
,
c
and
C
(depending on

\Omega
,
\rho
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
L(\partial\psi(\Omega))
)
such that whenever

\alpha>\alpha_{0},\qquad r<r_{0},\qquad\delta\leq r^{2},

the following holds:

(46)

\int_{\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\alpha|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq c\alpha\}|.

Before proving this result, we show that it implies our main Theorem
2.5
:

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Since
|\Delta_{r}\psi|\leq\frac{C}{r_{0}^{2}}\sup\psi
when
r\geq r_{0}
, we only need to prove the result when
r<r_{0}
.
The proof is identical to the proof of Theorem
2.4
: Using Theorem
6.7
and Fubini’s theorem we can show that there exists a constant
C
such that

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\log(2+\Delta_{r}\psi(x))\,dx\leq C+C\int_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx,

where
\Omega\subset\subset\Omega^{\prime\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
and we can further have that
\Omega^{\prime\prime}+B(0,r)\subset\Omega
by taking
r_{0}
small enough with respect to
\rho
.

We can now observe that

\Delta_{r}\psi(x)=L_{r}\star\Delta\psi(x),

where
\Delta\psi
is a non-negative Radon measure and
L_{r}
is a smooth convolution kernel, bounded in
L^{1}
and obtained through averages of
K_{r}
.

Consequently,

\int_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx=\int_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}L_{r}\star\Delta\psi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi\leq C.

∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
6.7
.

Proof of Theorem
6.7
.

The proof follows the same idea as that of Theorem
5.4
but requires several adjustments: Given the set

E_{\alpha}=\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi(x)>\alpha\},

we need to identify, for each
x\in E_{\alpha}
the value
t(x)
of
t
with the following properties:

(1)

We should have
t(x)\geq C_{0}\delta
so that we are only working with sections for which the properties listed in Proposition
3.5
are valid.

(2)

We need to have
\fint_{S(x,t)}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dy\leq C\alpha
for all
t\in[t(x),\rho]

(3)

We need to have
\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,t(x))^{2}}\geq c\alpha
in order to use Proposition
6.6
.

(4)

The particular choice of
q\in\partial\psi(x)
to define the section is not important except for the fact that we need to take
q
such that (
14
) holds.

Lemma
6.1
can be used to show that
\fint_{S(x,q,t)}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dy\leq C\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x))^{2}}
if
t\geq C_{0}\delta
and
r\leq\ell(S(x,q,t(x))
. This allows us to replace items 2 and 3
with the condition

\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x))^{2}}\leq C\alpha\quad\forall t\in[t(x),\rho],\qquad\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x))^{2}}\geq c\alpha.

Making sure that
t(x)\geq C_{0}\delta
is more delicate, but we note that if
B_{r}(x)\subset S(x,q,t)
, then the definition of
\Delta_{r}
implies
\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\leq m_{0}\frac{t}{r^{2}}
. For
x\in E_{\alpha}
, we thus have
t\geq\frac{\alpha}{m_{0}}r^{2}\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{m_{0}}\delta
and we get the desired inequality if
\alpha_{0}
is large enough. It turns that requiring that
B_{r}(x)\subset S(x,q,t(x))
is too strong so we will require

B_{r}(x)\subset S(x,q,Mt(x))

where
M
is the constant given in Proposition
3.5
such that
2S(x,q,t)\subset S(x,q,Mt)
.

All these considerations lead us to introduce for all
x\in E_{\alpha}
,

(47)

t(x)=\sup\left\{t\leq\rho\,\Big|\,\max\left\{\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}},\frac{r\alpha}{d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))}\right\}\geq\alpha\right\}.

Given
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}>0
(to be chosen later) we claim that if
\alpha
is large enough and
r
is small enough, then we must have
t(x)\leq\varepsilon\rho
.
Indeed, we recall (see Proposition
3.3
) that
\ell(S(x,q,t))\geq\frac{t}{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}
and so

\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}}\leq\frac{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))^{2}}{t}\leq\alpha_{0}:=\frac{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))^{2}}{\varepsilon\rho}\qquad\forall x\in\Omega^{\prime},\;\forall t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho]

while Alexandrov’s estimate (Proposition
3.4
) implies

d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))\geq r_{0}:=\frac{(\varepsilon\rho)^{n}}{|\partial\psi(\Omega)|L(\Omega)^{n-1}}\qquad\forall x\in\Omega^{\prime},\;\forall t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho].

From now on, we assume that
r<r_{0}
and
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
. We then have, for all
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
,

\max\left\{\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}},\frac{r\alpha}{d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))}\right\}<\alpha\qquad\forall t\in[\varepsilon\rho,\rho]

and so
t(x)\leq\varepsilon\rho
.

The definition of
t(x)
, (
47
) implies

(48)

\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}}\leq\alpha\qquad\forall x\in E_{\alpha},\;\forall t\in[t(x),\rho]

and

(49)

d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))\geq r\qquad\qquad\forall x\in E_{\alpha},\;\forall t\in[t(x),\rho].

The first condition was one of the required conditions for
t(x)
and the second condition can be used to show that
t(x)\geq C_{0}\delta
.
Indeed, for
x\in E_{\alpha}
, condition (
49
) implies that
B_{r}(x)\subset S(x,q,Mt)
and so

\displaystyle\alpha<\Delta_{r}\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}(x)}K_{r}(y-x)[\psi(y)-\psi(x)-q\cdot(y-x)]\,dy

(50)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m_{0}}{r^{2}}Mt\int K_{r}(y)\,dy=\frac{m_{0}M}{r^{2}}t\qquad\forall t\in[t(x),\rho]

and so (with
t=t(x)
)

(51)

t(x)\geq\frac{\alpha}{m_{0}M}r^{2}\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{m_{0}M}\delta.

We can always take a larger
\alpha_{0}
so that
\frac{\alpha_{0}}{m_{0}M}>C_{0}
(which we assume in what follows).

Finally, we can prove:

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
t(x)
be defined by (
47
). Then

(52)

\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))^{2}}\geq c\alpha,\qquad\forall x\in E_{\alpha}

and

(53)

\fint_{S(x,q,t)}\Delta_{r}\psi(y)\,dy\leq C\alpha\qquad\forall x\in E_{\alpha},\quad t\in[t(x),\varepsilon_{1}\rho].

Proof of Lemma
6.8
.

Observe that for a fixed
x
and
q\in\partial\psi(x)
, the map
t\to S(x,q,t)
is continuous thanks to the convexity of
\psi
. Indeed considering a sequence
t_{n}\to t
with for example
t_{n}<t
, we first trivially have that
\bigcup_{n}S(x,q,t_{n})\subset S(x,q,t)
and thus
\overline{\bigcup_{n}S(x,q,t_{n})}\subset S(x,q,t)
. On the other hand if there exists
y\in S(x,q,t)\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{n}S(x,q,t_{n})}
then necessarily
q\in\partial\psi(y)
. As
q\in\partial\psi(x)
, by the convexity of
\psi
, this is possible only if
t=\psi(x)
.

The definition (
47
) then implies that

\max\left\{\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))^{2}},\frac{r\alpha}{d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt(x)))}\right\}=\alpha.

In particular we either have

\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))^{2}}=\alpha
(and (
52
) is true)
or
\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))^{2}}<\alpha
and
d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt(x)))=r
.
In that last case we go back to (
50
) which implies

\alpha\leq m_{0}M\frac{t(x)}{r^{2}}=m_{0}M\frac{t(x)}{d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))^{2}}.

In order to conclude, we note that (here we write
\ell=\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))
)
B_{\ell}(0)\subset S(x,q,t(x))
and so (using the convexity of
S
)
B_{\ell}(x)\subset 2S(x,q,t(x))\subset S(x,q,Mt(x))
(using Proposition
3.5
and (
51
)). It follows that

\ell(S(x,q,t(x))\leq Cd(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))
and the inequality above implies

\alpha\leq m_{0}M\frac{t(x)}{\ell(S(x,q,t(x)))^{2}}

which is (
52
).

To prove inequality (
53
),
we take
t\in[t(x),\varepsilon_{1}\rho]
and
x\in E_{\alpha}
.
Lemma
6.1
, together with the fact that
\mathrm{Tr}(A)\leq\frac{n}{\ell(S)^{2}}
and
\ell(S(x,q,t))>r
implies

\fint_{S(x,q,t)}\Delta_{r}\psi(y)\,dy\leq\frac{C}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}}\sup_{y\in 3nS(x,q,t)}[\psi(y)-\psi(x)-q\cdot(y-x)].

Condition (
51
) implies that the scaling property of section (
23
) can be used to prove that there exists a constant
C_{1}=(3n)^{\beta}
such that
3nS(x,q,t)\subset S(x,q,C_{1}t)
provided
S(x,q,C_{1}t)\subset\Omega
which holds if we choose

\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}
(since
t\leq\varepsilon_{1}\rho
).
We
deduce

\fint_{S(x,q,t)}\Delta_{r}\psi(y)\,dy\leq\frac{Ct}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}}.

Inequality (
48
) then implies (
53
).
∎

The rest of the proof is similar to the case
\delta=0
:
We have
E_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{x\in E_{\alpha}}S(x,q,t(x))
and
since
t(x)\geq C\delta
, we can use Vitali’s covering lemma (see Corollary
3.6
) to extract a countable family
x_{k}
such that the sections
S_{k}=S(x_{k},q_{k},t(x_{k}))
are pairwise disjoint and
E_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{k}C_{*}S_{k}
.

Successive applications of Proposition
7.6

gives

C_{*}S_{k}\subset S(x_{k},q_{k},C_{**}t_{k})
for some constant
C_{**}

provided

C_{**}t_{k}\leq\rho.

Furthermore, in order to be able to use Inequality (
53
), we will require that
C_{**}t_{k}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\rho
.
We thus choose

\varepsilon_{2}:=\frac{1}{C_{**}}

The condition
t_{k}\leq\varepsilon\rho
with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}
then imply that
C_{**}t_{k}\in[t_{k},\varepsilon_{1}\rho]
. Inequality (
53
)
thus implies

\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{S(x_{k},q_{k},C_{**}t_{k})}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq\alpha|S(x_{k},q_{k},C_{**}t_{k})|

and Proposition
3.5
(i) gives
|S(x_{k},q_{k},C_{**}t_{k})|\leq C_{n}|S(x_{k},q_{k},t_{k})|
. We deduce that

\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\alpha|S_{k}|.

We are ready to conclude: First, we write

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\alpha}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\int_{C_{*}S_{k}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx\leq C\alpha\sum_{k}|S_{k}|.

We then use Proposition
6.6
: We have

\frac{t_{k}}{\ell(S_{k})^{2}}\geq c\alpha
(see (

)), so
there is a subset
\Sigma_{k}\subset S_{k}
such that

|\Sigma_{k}|\geq c|S_{k}|,\qquad\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\geq c\frac{t_{k}}{\ell(S_{k})^{2}}\geq c\alpha\quad\forall x\in\Sigma_{k},

and since the

, and therefore the

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\alpha}}\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha\sum_{k}|\Sigma_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha|\{x\in\Omega\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\geq c\alpha\}|

which is the desired inequality.
∎

6.3.
Sobolev regularity

A key step in deriving Sobolev
W^{2,p}
estimates on
\psi
up to scale
r
consists in localizing the reversed Chebychev’s inequality of Theorem
6.7
.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying
(
11
), (
12
), with
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying (
9
) and (
10
)) with
\delta>0
.
Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be such that
there exists
\rho>0
such that (
14
) holds.
Then there exists
\alpha_{0}
,
r_{0}
,
c
and
C
(depending on

\Omega
,
\rho
,
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
L(\partial\psi(\Omega))
)
such that whenever

\alpha>\alpha_{0},\qquad r<r_{0},\quad\alpha<\frac{c}{r^{1/n}},\qquad\delta\leq r^{2},

the following holds for any ball
B(x_{0},R)\subset\Omega^{\prime}

(54)

\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\alpha|\{x\in B(x_{0},R+C\,\alpha^{-1/\beta})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq c\alpha\}|.

Proof.

The proof follows the exact same lines as the proof of Theorem
6.7
by covering the set
\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha\}
with sections
S(x,t(x))
where
x\in B(x_{0},R)
. This leads to

\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\alpha|\{x\in B(x_{0},R+R^{\prime})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq c\alpha\}|,

where
R^{\prime}=\sup_{x\in B(x_{0},R)}L(S(x,t(x))
.

The only difference with Theorem
6.7
therefore lies in bounding precisely
R^{\prime}
. We go back to the definition (
47
) of
t(x)
and use first again Proposition
3.3
which gives
\ell(S(x,q,t))\geq\frac{t}{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))}
, so that for some constant
C
we have

\frac{t}{\ell(S(x,q,t))^{2}}\leq\frac{L(\partial\psi(\Omega))^{2}}{t}<\alpha,\quad\forall t>\frac{C}{\alpha}.

Similarly using Proposition
3.4
, we have that

d(x,\partial S(x,q,Mt))\geq\frac{t^{n}}{|\partial\psi(\Omega)|\,L(\Omega)^{n-1}}>r,\quad\forall t>C\,r^{1/n}.

Since we assumed that
\alpha<\frac{c}{r^{1/n}}
, this last inequality holds in particular for all
t>\frac{C}{\alpha}
.
Taken together, the two inequalities and (
47
) imply that
t(x)\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}
.

To conclude, it is now enough to use Lemma
4.2
which shows that

L(S(x,p,t))\leq C\,t(x)^{1/\beta}\leq\frac{C}{\alpha^{1/\beta}}.

Since this bound holds for all
x\in B(x_{0},R)
, we find
R^{\prime}\leq C\alpha^{-1/\beta}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Using Theorem
6.9
, we can now prove the Sobolev regularity Theorem
2.6

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

The proof mostly follows the arguments presented in
[
17
]
with only minor adaptations.
Since
|\Delta_{r}\psi|\leq\frac{C}{r_{0}^{2}}\sup\psi
when
r\geq r_{0}
, we only need to prove the result when
r<r_{0}
.

For some
\gamma>1
to be chosen later, we define
\alpha_{k}
by induction with
\alpha_{k+1}=\gamma\,\alpha_{k}
,
\alpha_{1}
large enough, and
R_{k+1}=R_{k}-C/\alpha_{k}^{1/\beta}
with
R_{1}=2\,R
.

As long as
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{C}{r^{1/n}}
and provided that
\gamma
was chosen large enough so that
R_{k}\geq R
for all
k
, we can apply Theorem
6.9
to have that

\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k+1})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k}/c\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq\frac{C}{c}\alpha_{k}|\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k}\}|.

As a consequence

\begin{split}\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k+1})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq&\frac{C}{c}\alpha_{k}|\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\alpha_{k}\leq\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k+1}\}|\\
&+\frac{C}{c\,\gamma}\,\alpha_{k+1}\,|\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}|,\end{split}

or, taking
\gamma\geq 2C/c
,

\begin{split}\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k+1})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq&\frac{C}{c}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\alpha_{k}\leq\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k}))\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx.\end{split}

We deduce:

\begin{split}\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k+1})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq&\frac{2C}{c}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\alpha_{k}\leq\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\\
&+\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\setminus B(x_{0},R_{k+1})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k+1}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx.\end{split}

Defining correspondingly
D_{k}=\{x\in B(x_{0},R_{k})\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k}\}
, this is equivalent, for a different constant
C
, to

\int_{D_{k+1}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\,\int_{D_{k}\setminus D_{k+1}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx,

or

(1+C)\,\int_{D_{k+1}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq C\,\int_{D_{k}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx.

This implies that

\int_{D_{k+1}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq\tau\,\int_{D_{k}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx,

for
\tau=C/(1+C)<1
.

By induction, and since
B(x_{0},R)\subset B(x_{0},R_{k})
, we can immediately deduce that

(55)

\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq\tau^{k}\int_{\Omega}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\leq\frac{C}{\alpha_{k}^{\theta}},

as long as
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{C}{r^{1/n}}
and for some
\theta>0
since
\alpha_{k}=\gamma^{k}\,\alpha_{1}
(so that we can take
\theta=-\log\tau/\log\gamma
).

Denote
k_{0}
the last index
k
s.t.
\alpha_{k_{0}}\leq\frac{C}{r^{1/n}}
and decompose

\begin{split}\int_{B(x_{0},R)}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx=\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx+\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi>\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx.\end{split}

Because
\Delta_{r}\psi(x)\leq\frac{C}{r^{2}}
, the last term is in fact easy to handle,

\begin{split}\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi>\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx&\leq\frac{C}{r^{2(p-1)}}\,\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi>\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\\
&\leq\frac{C}{r^{2(p-1)}}\,\frac{1}{\alpha_{k_{0}}^{\theta}}\leq C\,\frac{r^{\theta/n}}{r^{2(p-1)}}\leq C,\end{split}

provided that
p
is close enough to
1
so that
\theta/n\geq 2(p-1)
.

We now recall that for any measurable function
f(x)\geq 0
and
p>1
,

\int_{B(x_{0},R)}f^{p}\,dx=(p-1)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{p-2}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,f(x)\geq\alpha\}}f\,dx.

Hence

\begin{split}\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx&=(p-1)\,\int_{0}^{\alpha_{k_{0}}}\alpha^{p-2}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx\\
&\leq C+C\,\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\alpha_{k}^{p-1}\,\int_{\{x\in B(x_{0},R)\,|\,\Delta_{r}\psi\geq\alpha_{k}\}}\Delta_{r}\psi\,dx.\end{split}

By using again (
55
), we finally conclude that

\int_{B(x_{0},R)\cap\{\Delta_{r}\psi\leq\alpha_{k_{0}}\}}(\Delta_{r}\psi)^{p}\,dx\leq C+C\,\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}\gamma^{k(p-1-\theta)}\leq C,

as long as
p
is again selected close enough to
1
s.t.
p-1-\theta<0
.
∎

7.
Properties of sections

In this section, we establish some important properties of the sections of a convex function that satisfies inequalities (
11
)-(
12
) for measures
\mu
and
\nu
such that Assumption
1
(inequalities (
9
)-(
10
)) holds.
Namely we establish some bounds on the volume of the sections (Proposition
7.5
), a scaling property of sections (Proposition
7.6
) and the engulfing property of sections (Proposition
7.9
).
These three properties are classical when
\delta=0
(see for instance

[
11
]
,

[
25
]
,

[
24
, Chapter 3]
) and play an important role in the regularity theory for Monge-Ampère equation.
When
\delta>0
, it is clear that they can only hold for large enough sections. The main interest of this section is thus to clearly identify the condition on
t
(depending on
\delta
) for these conditions to hold.

An important tool in the proof of these results when
\delta=0
is the renormalization of convex sets provided by John’s lemma. However, this renormalization procedure is not compatible with Assumption
1

since it changes the length scales differently depending on the direction.
For this reason, our proofs will avoid the use of renormalization whenever Assumption
1
must be used. They will make extensive use of the notion of polar body of a convex set, a notion well suited to optimal transportation. To our knowledge this approach is new even in the case
\delta=0
.

Throughout this section, we will assume that
\mu
and
\nu
satisfy the following condition, which is equivalent to Assumption
1
(see Proposition
B.1
):

Assumption 2
.

There exist
\lambda
,
\Lambda>0
such that

(56)

\lambda|K|\leq\mu(K)\leq\Lambda|K|,\quad\mbox{for all convex set $K\subset\Omega$ with $\ell(K)\geq\delta$}

and

(57)

\lambda|K|\leq\nu(K)\leq\Lambda|K|,\quad\mbox{for all convex set $K\subset\mathcal{O}$ with $\ell(K)\geq\delta$.}

7.1.
Polar bodies of a convex set

Several proofs in this section will make use of the notion of polar body of a convex set, which turns out to be a convenient tool to relate the section
S
of a convex function
\psi
and its image
\partial\psi(S)
.
For a convex set
K
containing the origin, we recall that the polar body is defined by

(58)

K^{\circ}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,z\cdot x\leq 1,\quad\forall x\in K\}.

We recall the following basic facts about polar bodies:

(1)

If
A\subset B
then
B^{\circ}\subset A^{\circ}

(2)

B_{r}(0)^{\circ}=B_{1/r}(0)

(3)

The volume product
|K|\,|K^{\circ}|
is unchanged by linear (but not affine) transformations: Indeed,

z\in(L(K))^{\circ}\Leftrightarrow z\cdot L(x)\leq 1\;\forall x\in K\Leftrightarrow L^{*}(z)\in K^{\circ}

and so

|L(K)|\,|(L(K))^{\circ}|=|\det L||K|\,|\det(L^{*})^{-1}||K^{\circ}|=|K|\,|K^{\circ}|
.

There is an abundant literature devoted to the study of the polar body and in particular to the derivation of optimal lower and upper bounds for the volume product
|K|\,|K^{\circ}|
. We will use the following classical result:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
K
be a bounded convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-empty interior.
We have:

(59)

\inf_{x_{0}\in K}|K|\,|(K-x_{0})^{\circ}|\leq n^{n}\omega_{n}^{2}

(the unique point
x_{0}
where the infimum in (
59
) is attained is called the Santaló point of
K
)
and

(60)

|K|\,|K^{\circ}|\geq\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{(2n)^{n}}

where
\omega_{n}
denotes the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball.

These inequalities are classical and we give a short proof below for the sake of completeness.
Note that the constants in (
59
) and (
60
) are not optimal.
We refer for example to Bourgain-Milman
[
4
]
for discussion about optimal constants.

Proof.

When
K
is a convex body that is symmetric with respect to the origin, John’s Lemma
3.1
implies that there is a linear transformation
L
such that
B_{1}(0)\subset L(K)\subset B_{n}(0)
. We then have
B_{1/n}(0)\subset(L(K))^{\circ}\subset B_{1}(0)
and so

(61)

\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{n^{n}}\leq|L(K)|\,|(L(K))^{\circ}|=|K|\,|K^{\circ}|\leq n^{n}\omega_{n}^{2}.

When
K
is not symmetric with respect to the origin, John’s Lemma gives
B_{1}(a)\subset L(K)\subset B_{n}(a)
for some
a=L(y)
with
y\in K
.
The same computation as above, applied to
K-y
thus gives

\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{n^{n}}\leq|K|\,|(K-y)^{\circ}|\leq n^{n}\omega_{n}^{2}

which gives in particular (
59
).

We note that if
0\notin K
, then
|K^{\circ}|
is infinite and (
60
) holds. So we can assume that
0\in K
and thus
0\in L(K)\subset B_{n}(a)
. It follows that
L(K)\subset B_{2n}(0)
and so
L(K)^{\circ}\supset B_{1/2n}(0)
. Proceeding as above, we deduce

|K|\,|K^{\circ}|=|L(K)|\,|(L(K))^{\circ}|\geq|B_{1}||B_{1/2n}|=\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{(2n)^{n}}

which proves (
60
).
∎

Finally, we can get the following bounds on the inner and outer radii of
K
and
K^{\circ}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
K
be a bounded convex set with non-empty interior such that
0\in K
. Then

\ell(K)L(K^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{2n}\qquad\ell(K^{\circ})L(K)\geq\frac{1}{2n}

Proof.

John’s Lemma implies the existence of an ellipsoid
E
such that

E\subset K\subset nE,

where we note that
nE
denotes a dilation of
E
about its center, which is not necessarily
0
.
In particular, we have
\ell(K)\geq\ell(E)
and

(nE)^{\circ}\subset K^{\circ}
.

Next, we note that the polar dual of a centered ellipsoid
E_{0}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_{i}}{a_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq 1\right\}
is the ellipsoid

E_{0}^{\circ}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(a_{i}u_{i}\right)^{2}\leq 1\right\}
. In particular

\ell(E_{0}^{\circ})L(E_{0})=1
and
L(E_{0}^{\circ})\ell(E_{0})=1
for such an ellipsoid.

The ellipsoid
F:=nE
might not be centered, but we have
0\in F
, so there is a centered ellipsoid
F_{0}
, twice as large as
F
, such that
F\subset F_{0}
and
F_{0}^{\circ}\subset F^{\circ}
. We have

L(F^{\circ})\geq L(F_{0}^{\circ})=\frac{1}{\ell(F_{0})}=\frac{1}{2\ell(F)}
and
L(F)=\frac{1}{2}L(F_{0})=\frac{1}{2\ell(F_{0}^{\circ})}\geq\frac{1}{2\ell(F^{\circ})}
. We thus have:

\ell(nE)L((nE)^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad L(nE)\ell((nE)^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Finally, we can write

\ell(K)\geq\ell(E)=\frac{1}{n}\ell(nE)\geq\frac{1}{2nL((nE)^{\circ})}\geq\frac{1}{2nL(K^{\circ})}

which gives the first inequality in Lemma
7.2
.

K^{\circ}
is a convex set containing zero and
(K^{\circ})^{\circ}=K
so the second inequality follows by applying the first inequality to
K^{\circ}
.
∎

This lemma readily implies.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
convex.
For any section
S\subset\Omega
, we have

\ell((S-y)^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{2nL(\Omega)}\qquad\forall y\in S.

Proof.

The result follows from Lemma
7.2
since we have

\frac{1}{2n}\leq\ell((S-y)^{\circ})L(S-y)\leq\ell((S-y)^{\circ})L(\Omega).

∎

The usefulness of polar bodies in the context of optimal transportation is made clear by the next proposition.
We recall that for a convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
given
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
and
t\geq 0
,
the section centered at
x_{0}
with height
t
is the convex set

S(x_{0},p_{0},t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,\psi(x)\leq\psi(x_{0})+p_{0}\cdot(x-x_{0})+t\}.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function.
For all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
and
t\geq 0
such that
S(x_{0},p_{0},t)
is bounded, we have

(62)

p_{0}+t(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\subset\partial\psi(S)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\partial\psi^{*}(p_{0}+\frac{t}{2}(S-x_{0})^{\circ})\subset S

and

(63)

\partial\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}(S+y_{0})\right)\subset p_{0}+2t(S-y_{0})^{\circ}\qquad\forall y_{0}\in S.

When
x_{0}=0
and
p_{0}=0
, (
62
) can be written simply as
\partial\psi^{*}(\frac{t}{2}S^{\circ})\subset S
and
tS^{\circ}\subset\partial\psi(S)
. The proof will make it clear that we also have
\partial\psi^{*}((1-\eta)tS^{\circ})\subset S
for all
\eta>0

and even
\partial\psi^{*}(tS^{\circ})\subset S
if
\psi
is strictly convex.
This simple relation between the sections of
\psi
and their dual body, combined with the volume product inequality (
59
) readily imply in particular the estimate

(64)

|\partial\psi(S)|\,|S|\geq\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{(2n)^{n}}t^{n}.

Proof of Proposition
7.4
.

Up to replacing
\psi
with the function
\overline{\psi}(x)=\psi(x_{0}+x)-p_{0}\cdot x-\psi(x_{0})
, it is enough to prove the result when
x_{0}=0
,
p_{0}=0
and
\psi(x_{0})=0
.
We then have
\bar{S}=S-x_{0}
,
\partial\overline{\psi}(x)=\partial\psi(x_{0}+x)-p_{0}
and
\partial\overline{\psi}^{*}(z)=\partial\psi^{*}(p_{0}+z)-x_{0}
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
z\cdot x\leq t
for all
x\in\overline{S}
(that is
z\in t\overline{S}^{\circ}
). The function

u(x)=\overline{\psi}(x)-z\cdot x
is convex and satisfies

u(0)=0,\qquad u(x)\geq 0\quad\mbox{ for }x\in\partial\overline{S},

using the fact that
\overline{\psi}(x)=t
and
z\cdot x\leq t
for all
x\in\partial\overline{S}
.

In particular, there is at least one
y\in\overline{S}
such that
u
has a minimum at
y
, and for such a point we have
0\in\partial u(y)
and so
z\in\partial\overline{\psi}(y)
.
This implies
t\overline{S}^{\circ}\subset\partial\overline{\psi}(\overline{S})
which is the first inclusion in (
62
).

Next, we note that if
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is such that
z\cdot x\leq\frac{t}{2}
for all
x\in\overline{S}
(that is
z\in\frac{t}{2}\overline{S}^{\circ}
), then the same function
u
as above satisfies
u(x)>0
for
x\in\partial\overline{S}
.
In particular, any minimum
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
u
must lie
S
.
Since
u
is convex, the set of points such that
0\in\partial u
coincides with the set of points where
u
is minimum.
So we can claim that for all
z\in\frac{t}{2}S^{\circ}
, we have
z\in\partial\psi(x)\Rightarrow x\in S
.
This implies
\partial\psi^{*}(\frac{t}{2}S^{\circ})\subset S
.

In order to prove (
63
), we recall that for any
y_{0}\in\overline{S}
, we have

(\overline{S}-y_{0})^{\circ}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,z\cdot(y-y_{0})\leq 1\,\mbox{ for all }y\in\overline{S}\}.

We now fix
\bar{x}\in\frac{1}{2}(\overline{S}+y_{0})
. Then
z\in\partial\overline{\psi}(\bar{x})
implies
\overline{\psi}(\bar{x})+z\cdot(x-\bar{x})\leq\overline{\psi}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and so

(65)

z\cdot(x-\bar{x})\leq t\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\overline{S}.

In particular, if we take
x=2\bar{x}-y_{0}
in (
65
) (which we can do since
2\bar{x}-y_{0}\in\overline{S}
with our choice of
\bar{x}
), we get that

z\cdot(\bar{x}-y_{0})\leq t
. Using (
65
) again, we deduce:

z\cdot(x-y_{0})=z\cdot(x-\bar{x})+z\cdot(\bar{x}-y_{0})\leq 2t\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\overline{S}.

We proved that

\partial\overline{\psi}(\bar{x})\subset 2t(\overline{S}-y_{0})^{\circ}
for all
\bar{x}\in\frac{1}{2}(\overline{S}+y_{0})
, which implies (
63
).
∎

7.2.
Volume of sections: Proof of Proposition
3.5
-(i)

We now prove the following proposition, which implies Proposition
3.5
-(i):

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying (
3
), (
11
)-(
12
) for measures
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying Assumption
1
.
Then there exist two constants
c
and
C
depending only on
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
n
such that
if

(66)

t\geq\max\{2L(\partial\psi(\Omega)),2nL(\Omega)\}\delta

then

(67)

c|S(x_{0},p_{0},t)|^{2}\leq t^{n}\leq C|S(x_{0},p_{0},t)|^{2}

for all sections with
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
and
t\geq 0
such that
S(x_{0},p_{0},t)\subset\Omega
.

The proof of Proposition
7.5
relies on Proposition
7.4
and on the lower and upper bound on the volume product proved in Proposition
7.1
.

Proof.

We use (
62
) with (
12
) to get:

(68)

\nu\left(p_{0}+\frac{t}{2}(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\right)\leq\mu\left(\partial\psi^{*}\left(p_{0}+\frac{t}{2}(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\right)\right)\leq\mu(S).

Proposition
3.3
and condition (
66
) imply

\ell(S)\geq\delta
.
Since
S
is convex and
S\subset\Omega
, (
56
) gives

\mu(S)\leq\Lambda|S|.

Similarly, Corollary
7.3
and condition (
66
) imply
\ell(p_{0}+t(S-x_{0})^{\circ})=t\ell((S-x_{0})^{\circ})\geq\delta
,
and (
62
) implies
p_{0}+t(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\subset\partial\psi(S)\subset\mathcal{O}
.
So (
57
) gives

\nu\left(p_{0}+\frac{t}{2}(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\right)\geq\lambda\left|\frac{t}{2}(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\right|=\lambda\frac{t^{n}}{2^{n}}\left|(S-x_{0})^{\circ}\right|.

Going back to (
68
), we proved that

t^{n}|(S-x_{0})^{\circ}|\leq\frac{2^{n}\Lambda}{\lambda}|S|.

Using the lower bound on the volume product (
60
), we deduce

|S|^{2}\geq ct^{n}|(S-x_{0})^{\circ}||S-x_{0}|\geq ct^{n},

for a constant
c
that depends only on
n
,
\lambda
and
\Lambda
.
This is the second inequality in (
67
).

To prove the first inequality, we use (
63
) with (
11
)
to write (for any
y\in S
):

\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}(S+y)\right)\leq\nu\left(\partial\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}(S+y)\right)\right)\leq\nu\left((p_{0}+2t(S-y)^{\circ}\right)\cap\mathcal{O}).

We note that
\frac{1}{2}(S+y)\subset S\subset\Omega
and Proposition
3.3
together with condition (
66
) imply

\ell(\frac{1}{2}(S+y))=\frac{1}{2}\ell(S)\geq\delta
.
Also, (
62
) implies that
p+t(S-y)^{\circ}\subset(p_{0}+2t(S-y)^{\circ})\cap\mathcal{O}
, and so Corollary
7.3
together with condition (
66
) implies

\ell((p_{0}+2t(S-y)^{\circ})\cap\mathcal{O})\geq\delta
.

We can thus use Assumption
2
and get

\frac{\lambda}{2^{n}}|S|=\lambda\left|\frac{1}{2}(S+y)\right|\leq\Lambda\left|p+2t(S-y)^{\circ}\right|=\Lambda 2^{n}t^{n}\left|(S-y)^{\circ}\right|

and so

|S|^{2}\leq Ct^{n}|(S-y)^{\circ}||S|

for a constant
C
depending only on
n
,
\lambda
and
\Lambda
.
The result follows by taking the infimum over
y
and using the upper bound on the volume product (
59
).
∎

7.3.
Scaling properties of sections: Proof of Proposition
3.5
-(ii)

For any convex function
\psi
and any
\tau\in(0,1)
we have

(69)

\tau S(x_{0},p_{0},t)\subset S(x_{0},p_{0},\tau t).

The main result of this section is the following proposition which provides control in the other direction when
\psi
is associated to our optimal transportation problem:

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function satisfying (
3
), (
11
)-(
12
) for measures
\mu
and
\nu
such that (
9
)-(
10
) holds.
For any fixed
\tau\in(0,\ 1)
, there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
, depending on
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
n
such that
if
t
satisfies (
66
) and

(70)

t\geq 8nL(\Omega)\delta

then

(71)

S(x_{0},p_{0},\tau t)\subset\gamma S(x_{0},p_{0},t)

for all
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
p_{0}\in\partial\psi(x_{0})
and
t\geq 0
such that
S(x_{0},p_{0},t)\subset\Omega
.

First, we prove the following technical lemma for convex sets:

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
K
be a compact convex set with nonempty interior such that
0\in K
.
For all
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
y\in K\setminus\gamma K
we have:

|K||(K-y)^{\circ}|\geq\frac{c}{1-\gamma}

for some constant
c
depending only on the dimension.

Note that
|(K-y)^{\circ}|\to\infty
when
y\to\partial K
since
(K-y)^{\circ}
is unbounded when
y\in\partial K
. So this lemma simply quantifies how large the volume product will be when
y
is close to
\partial K
(which corresponds to
\gamma\to 1
).

Proof.

John’s lemma implies that there exists a linear transform
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

B_{1}(b)\subset L(K)\subset B_{n}(b)

for some
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(we do not take
b=0
as translations have a strong effect on the polar body). We note that
L(K-y)=L(K)-L(y)
and
L(y)\in L(K)\setminus\gamma L(K)
.
Since the product volume
|K^{\circ}|\,|K|
is invariant under linear transformation (although not under affine transformation), it is enough to prove the result
under the condition

(72)

B_{1}(b)\subset K\subset B_{n}(b).

We now claim that

(73)

|(K-y)^{\circ}|\geq\frac{c}{\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial K)L(K)^{n-1}}\geq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\frac{c}{L(K)^{n}}.

To prove (
73
), we take
y_{0}\in\partial K
such that
|y_{0}-y|=\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial K)=:\eta
.
Up to a rotation, we can always assume that the vector
y_{0}-y
is in the direction
e_{n}
(that is
y_{0}-y=\eta\,e_{n}
) and we denote
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By convexity of
K
we have
(x-y_{0})\cdot(y-y_{0})\geq 0
for all
x\in K
,
and so

x_{n}-y_{n}\leq\eta\qquad\forall x\in K.

Given
z
such that

0<z_{n}<1/\eta
and
|z^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1-z_{n}\,\eta}{L(K)}
we then have, for all
x\in K
:

z\cdot(x-y)=z^{\prime}\cdot(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})+z_{n}(x_{n}-y_{n})\leq|z^{\prime}|L(K)+z_{n}\eta\leq 1.

We deduce that

\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,0<z_{n}<1/\eta,\;|z^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1-z_{n}\eta}{L(K)}\right\}\subset(K-y)^{\circ}.

Since the cone in the left hand side has volume
\frac{C}{\eta\,(L(K))^{n-1}}

we get the first inequality in (
73
).

To obtain the second inequality, we use the fact that
\frac{y}{\gamma}\notin K
to get

\mathrm{dist}(y,\partial K)\leq\min\left\{L(K),\left|\frac{y}{\gamma}-y\right|\right\}\leq L(K)\min\left\{1,\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right\}\leq 2L(K)(1-\gamma)

where we used the fact that
0\in K
and so
|y|\leq L(K)
, which concludes the proof of (
73
).

Finally, (
73
) implies

|(K-y)^{\circ}||K|\geq\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)}\frac{c_{n}|K|}{L(K)^{n}},

and
under condition (
72
) we have
\frac{|K|}{L(K)^{n}}\geq\frac{|B_{1}|}{2n}
so the result follows.
∎

Next, we prove the following refinement of (
62
).

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function such that
0\in\partial\psi(0)
. For
t>0
and
y\in S:=S(0,0,t)
, there holds:

\partial\psi^{*}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(t-\psi(y)\right)(S-y)^{\circ}\right)\subset S.

Proof.

We can assume that
\psi(y)<t
(otherwise the result is trivial).
Let
z\in\frac{1}{2}\left(t-\psi(y)\right)(S-y)^{\circ}
, that is

z\cdot(x-y)\leq\frac{1}{2}(t-\psi(y))\mbox{ for all }x\in S.

The function
u(x)=\psi(x)-\psi(y)-z\cdot(x-y)
hence satisfies that

u(x)>0
for all
x\in\partial S
.
Since
u(y)=0
with
y\in S
and
u
is convex, it follows that all minima of
u
are in the interior of
S
.
Furthermore again since
u
is convex, the set of points
x_{0}
where
0\in\partial u(x_{0})
coincides with the set of points where
u
has a minimum.
We deduce
\{x_{0}\,;\,0\in\partial u(x_{0})\}\subset S
.
Since
0\in\partial u(x_{0})\Leftrightarrow z\in\partial\psi(x_{0})\Leftrightarrow x_{0}\in\partial\psi^{*}(z)
. This implies

\partial\psi^{*}(z)\subset S

. The construction is classical and recalled here briefly:
We take a set of points

\tilde{\psi}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathrm{spt}\,\nu}\left(\tilde{\phi}(y)+x\cdot y\right)

for some function
\phi:\mathrm{spt}\,\nu\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
\partial\tilde{\psi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\Gamma(\mathrm{spt}\,\nu)\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, where
\Gamma(\mathrm{spt}\,\nu)
denotes the closed convex envelop of
\mathrm{spt}\,\nu
.

Proof.

The fact that
\tilde{\psi}
is convex and lower-semicontinuous follows from its definition and since
\tilde{\phi}(y)\geq\phi(y)
, we have
\tilde{\psi}(x)\leq\psi(x)
.

Furthermore, if
x\in X
is such that there exists
y\in Y
such that
y\in\partial\psi(x)
, then

\tilde{\phi}(y)=\phi(y)
and so

\tilde{\psi}(x)\geq x\cdot y-\tilde{\phi}(y)=x\cdot y-\phi(y)=x\cdot y-\psi^{*}(y)=\psi(x)

(the last equality follows from (
13
)).

Finally, given
(x,y)\in\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi)\cap(X\times Y)
, we now have both
\tilde{\phi}(y)=\phi(y)
and
\tilde{\psi}(x)=\psi(x)

and so

\tilde{\psi}(x)-x\cdot y=\psi(x)-x\cdot y=-\psi^{*}(y)=-\phi(y)=-\tilde{\phi}(y)\leq\tilde{\psi}(z)-z\cdot y

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This inequality can also be written as

\tilde{\psi}(z)\geq\tilde{\psi}(x)+y\cdot(z-y)
which means
y\in\partial\tilde{\psi}(x)
. We deduce

\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi)\cap(X\times Y)\subset\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\tilde{\psi}).

Since
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is concentrated in
\mathrm{spt}\,\mu\times\mathrm{spt}\,\nu
,
we have
\mathrm{spt}\,(\pi)\subset\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi)\cap(X\times Y)\subset\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\tilde{\psi})
and so

\tilde{\psi}
is a Kantorovich potential (associated to the same transference plan
\pi
).

Finally, the fact that
\tilde{\psi}(x)=\psi(x)

on the
x
-projection of
\mathrm{Graph}(\partial\psi)\cap(X\times Y)
implies that

\tilde{\psi}(x)=\psi(x)\mbox{ in }\mathrm{spt}\,\mu.

∎
