Abstract
Consider a binary statistical hypothesis testing problem, where n n independent and identically distributed random variables Z n Z^{n} are either distributed according to the null hypothesis P P or the alternative hypothesis Q Q , and only P P is known. A well-known test that is suitable for this case is the so-called Hoeffding test, which accepts P P if the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the empirical distribution of Z n Z^{n} and P P is below some threshold. This work characterizes the first and second-order terms of the type-II error probability for a fixed type-I error probability for the Hoeffding test as well as for divergence tests, where the KL divergence is replaced by a general divergence. It is demonstrated that, irrespective of the divergence, divergence tests achieve the first-order term of the Neyman-Pearson test, which is the optimal test when both P P and Q Q are known. In contrast, the second-order term of divergence tests is strictly worse than that of the Neyman-Pearson test. It is further demonstrated that divergence tests with an invariant divergence achieve the same second-order term as the Hoeffding test, but divergence tests with a non-invariant divergence may outperform the Hoeffding test for some alternative hypotheses Q Q . Potentially, this behavior could be exploited by a composite hypothesis test with partial knowledge of the alternative hypothesis Q Q by tailoring the divergence of the divergence test to the set of possible alternative hypotheses.



I
Introduction

Consider a binary hypothesis testing problem that decides whether a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
Z^{n}
is either generated from distribution
P
or from distribution
Q
. Assume that both distributions are discrete and the hypothesis test has access to
P
but not to
Q
. A suitable test for this case is the well-known
Hoeffding test

[
1
]
, denoted by
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)
, which accepts
P
if
D_{\textnormal{KL}}({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)<r
, for some
r>0
, and otherwise accepts
Q
.
Here,
{P}_{Z^{n}}
is the type (the empirical distribution) of
Z^{n}

and
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
P
and
Q

[
2
]
.
In this paper, we analyze the second-order performance of the Hoeffding test as well as of Hoeffding-like tests, referred to as
divergence tests
, where the KL divergence is replaced by other divergences (see Section
II
for a rigorous definition).

We focus on the asymptotic behaviour of the type-II error
\beta_{n}
(the probability of declaring hypothesis
P
under hypothesis
Q
) for a fixed type-I error
\alpha_{n}
(the probability of declaring hypothesis
Q
under hypothesis
P
).
When both
P
and
Q
are known, the optimal test is the
likelihood ratio test
, also known as the
Neyman-Pearson test
, denoted by
{\mathsf{T}}^{\textnormal{NP}}_{n}
. For this test, the smallest type-II error
\beta_{n}
for which
\alpha_{n}\leq\epsilon

satisfies
[
3
, Prop. 2.3]

-\ln\beta_{n}=nD_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)-\sqrt{nV_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)+O(\ln n)

(1)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where the KL divergence
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)
is defined as

D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\ln\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}};

(2)

\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse of the tail probability of the standard Normal distribution;

V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\left[\left(\ln\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}-D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)\right)^{2}\right]

(3)

denotes the KL divergence variance; and
O(\ln n)
denotes terms that grow at most as fast as
\ln n
as
n\to\infty
. In (
2
) and (
3
),
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
denote the
i
-th components of
P
and
Q
, and
k
denotes their dimension.
By inspecting the expansion of
-\ln\beta_{n}
in (
1
), one can define the first-order term

\beta^{\prime}\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}}{n}

(4)

and the second-order term

\beta^{\prime\prime}\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}-n\beta^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}

(5)

if the limits exist.
For the Neyman-Pearson test, we then have

\beta^{\prime}_{\textnormal{NP}}=D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)

(6)

and

\beta_{\textnormal{NP}}^{\prime\prime}=-\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon).

(7)

It was shown in
[
1
]
that the first-order term
\beta_{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}^{\prime}
of the Hoeffding test is also
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)
. Thus, the Hoeffding test is first-order optimal.

In this paper, we analyze the second-order performance of the Hoeffding test as well as of divergence tests. Specifically, a divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
accepts
P
if
D({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)<r
, for some
r>0
and an arbitrary divergence
D
, and otherwise accepts
Q
. Consequently, the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
includes the Hoeffding test as a special case when
D=D_{\textnormal{KL}}
.

I-A

Related Work

The considered hypothesis testing problem falls under the category of
composite hypothesis testing

[
4
]
. Indeed, in composite hypothesis testing, the test has no access to the null hypothesis
P
and the alternative hypothesis
Q
, but it has the knowledge that
P
and
Q
belong to the sets of distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, respectively. Our setting corresponds to the case where
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}^{c}
(where we use the notation
\mathcal{A}^{c}
to denote the complement of a set
\mathcal{A}
).

The Hoeffding test is a particular instance of the
generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT)

[
5
]
, which is arguably the most common test used in composite hypothesis testing. A useful benchmark for the Hoeffding test is the Neyman-Pearson test, which is the optimal test when both
P
and
Q
are known. As indicated before, the Hoeffding test achieves the same first-order term
\beta^{\prime}
as the Neyman-Pearson test, both in
Stein’s regime
, where the type-I error satisfies
\alpha_{n}\leq\epsilon
, as well as in the
Chernoff regime
, where
\alpha_{n}\leq e^{-n\gamma}
,
\gamma>0
; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Thus, the first-order term of the Neyman-Pearson test can be achieved without having access to the distribution
Q
of the alternative hypothesis. However, not having access to
Q
negatively affects higher-order terms. For example, for a given threshold
r
, the type-I error of the Hoeffding test satisfies
[
9
, Eq. (10)]

\alpha_{n}=n^{\frac{k-3}{2}}e^{-nr}(c^{\prime}+o(1))

(8)

whereas for the corresponding Neyman-Pearson test
[
9
, Eq. (9)]

\alpha_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-nr}(c+o(1)).

(9)

Here,
c
and
c^{\prime}
are constants that only depend on
P
,
Q
, and
r
. Moreover, it was demonstrated by Unnikrishnan
et al.

[
7
]
that the variance of the normalized Hoeffding test statistic
nD_{\textnormal{KL}}({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)
converges to
\frac{1}{2}(k-1)
as
n\to\infty
. Both results suggest that, for moderate
n
, the Hoeffding test scales unfavorably with the cardinality of
P
and
Q
. This was the motivation of
[
7
]
to propose the
test via mismatched divergence
. As we shall see, our characterization of the second-order term of the divergence test (and, hence, also the Hoeffding test) also suggests an unfavorable scaling with the cardinality of
P
and
Q
.

Our setting where
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}^{c}
was also studied by Watanabe
[
10
]
, who focused on the second-order regime of statistical tests that do not depend on
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. The test proposed in
[
10
]
achieves a second-order term which was shown to be
Pareto-optimal
. The related case where only training sequences are available for both
P
and
Q
was considered by Gutman
[
11
]
. The test proposed in
[
11
]
was later shown to be second-order optimal by Li and Tan
[
12
]
.

I-B

Contributions and Outline

The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

We characterize the first-order and second-order terms of the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
for an arbitrary divergence
D
(Theorem
1
).
Inter alia
, the proof of this result requires the generalization of a well-known result by Wilks
[
13
]
regarding the convergence of the KL divergence between the type
{P}_{Z^{n}}
and the null hypothesis
P
to general divergences (Lemma
6
). Our characterization demonstrates that the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
achieves the same first-order term
\beta^{\prime}
as the Neyman-Pearson test, irrespective of the divergence
D
. Hence,
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
is first-order optimal for every divergence
D
. This is consistent with the result that the Hoeffding test is first-order optimal
[
1
]
.

2.

Specializing Theorem
1
to the case where the divergence
D
is the KL divergence
D_{\textnormal{KL}}
, we obtain that the second-order term of the Hoeffding test is

\beta_{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}^{\prime\prime}=-\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\epsilon)}

(10)

where
\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\cdot)
is the inverse of the tail probability of the chi-square distribution with
k-1
degrees of freedom.
1
1

1

The first-order and second-order terms of the Hoeffding test where also presented by Watanabe in his presentation at the
Bombay Information Theory Seminar (BITS) 2018

[
14
]
.
We show that
\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\epsilon)
is monotonically increasing in
k
, and its square root is strictly larger than
\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)
. By comparing (
10
) to the second-order term (
7
) of the Neyman-Pearson test, we thus again observe that the Hoeffding test scales unfavorably with the cardinality of
P
and
Q
, and its second-order performance is worse than that of the Neyman-Pearson test.

3.

We demonstrate that the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
achieves the same second-order term as the Hoeffding test whenever the divergence
D
is
invariant
(see Definition
3
). The class of invariant divergences is large and includes the Rényi divergence and the f-divergences, which in turn include the KL divergence. In contrast, when the divergence
D
is
non-invariant
, the second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}_{D}
of the divergence test differs from the second-order term
\beta_{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}^{\prime\prime}
of the Hoeffding test.

4.

In Section
IV
, we numerically compare the second-order terms of the Hoeffding test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)
and of a divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
with a non-invariant divergence
D
. Our results show that the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
may achieve a second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}_{D}
that is strictly larger than the second-order term
\beta_{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}^{\prime\prime}
of the Hoeffding test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)
for some alternative hypothesis
Q
. The set of distributions
Q
for which the divergence test outperforms the Hoeffding test typically depends on the null hypothesis
P
and on
\epsilon
. Potentially, this behavior could be exploited by a composite hypothesis test with partial knowledge of the alternative hypothesis
Q
by tailoring the divergence
D
of the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D}_{n}(r)
to the set of possible alternative hypotheses
\mathcal{Q}
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The problem setting and other definitions are introduced in Section
II
. The main results are presented in Section
III
. The second-order performances of various divergence tests are compared by means of numerical results in Section
IV
. The proofs of our main results can be found in Section
V
, with some of the details deferred to the appendices. The paper concludes with a summary of our results in Section
VI
.

Divergence and Divergence Test

II-A

Problem Setting

Let us consider a random variable
Z
that takes value in a discrete set
\mathcal{Z}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 2
denotes the cardinality of
\mathcal{Z}
. We denote the probability distribution of
Z
by a
k
-length vector
{R}=({R}_{1},\ldots,{R}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}
, where

{R}_{i}\triangleq\text{Pr}\{Z=a_{i}\},\quad i=1,\ldots,k.

Let

\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Z})\triangleq\left\{{R}=({R}_{1},\ldots,{R}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\colon{R}_{i}\geq 0,\;i=1,\ldots,k\mbox{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k}{R}_{i}=1\right\}

(13)

be the set of all such probability distributions. Similarly, let

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\triangleq\left\{{R}=({R}_{1},\ldots,{R}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\colon{R}_{i}>0,\;i=1,\ldots,k\mbox{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k}{R}_{i}=1\right\}

(14)

be the set of all positive probability distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
. We denote the set of probability distributions on the boundary of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
by
\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, i.e.,
\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})=\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{Z})\setminus\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
.

We consider a binary hypothesis testing problem with null hypothesis
H_{0}
and alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
.
We assume that, under hypothesis
H_{0}
, the sequence of observations
Z^{n}
is i.i.d. according to
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
; under hypothesis
H_{1}
, the sequence of observations
Z^{n}
is i.i.d. according to
Q
, where
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\setminus\{P\}
. Any statistical test, denoted by
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
, for testing
H_{0}
against
H_{1}
is defined by choosing a subset
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, called the

, such that if
z^{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, then the null hypothesis
P
is accepted, else the alternative hypothesis
Q
is accepted. For the test
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
, the type-I error, denoted by
\alpha_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})
, and the type-II error, denoted by
\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})
, are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle P^{n}\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle Q^{n}(\mathcal{A}_{n})

(16)

where
P^{n}(z^{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(z_{i})
and
Q^{n}(z^{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q(z_{i})
.

For any given test
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
, our goal is to analyze the asymptotic behavior of the type-II error when the type-I error satisfies
\alpha_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})\leq\epsilon,\;0<\epsilon<1
. Following the discussion in Section
I
, we define the first-order term
\beta^{\prime}
and the second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}
for
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
by the respective (
4
) and (
5
), namely,

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\ln\beta_{n}

(17)

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\ln\beta_{n}-n\beta^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\

(18)

if the limits exist. Thus, the type-II error satisfies the following asymptotic expansion:

-\ln\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n})=n\beta^{\prime}+\sqrt{n}\beta^{\prime\prime}+o(\sqrt{n}),\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

(19)

It follows from (
1
) that, for the Neyman-Pearson test
{\mathsf{T}}^{\textnormal{NP}}_{n}
satisfying
\alpha_{n}({\mathsf{T}}^{\textnormal{NP}}_{n})\leq\epsilon
,
the first-order term
\beta^{\prime}_{\textnormal{NP}}
and the second-order term
\beta^{\prime\prime}_{\textnormal{NP}}
are given by

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}_{\textnormal{NP}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)

(20)

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\prime}_{\textnormal{NP}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon).

(21)

In this paper, we consider tests
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
that do not depend on the alternative hypothesis
Q
. An important special case of such tests is the Hoeffding test
[
1
]
, which depends only on the empirical distribution
{P}_{Z^{n}}
(type distribution) of the observations
Z^{n}
and the null hypothesis
P
. Specifically, the Hoeffding test, denoted by
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)
, accepts
P
if
D_{\textnormal{KL}}({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)<r
, for some
r>0
, and otherwise accepts
Q
. Here,
D_{\textnormal{KL}}
is the KL-divergence and
{P}_{z^{n}}=({P}_{z^{n}}(a_{1}),\ldots,{P}_{z^{n}}(a_{k}))^{\mathsf{T}}
is the type distribution defined as

{P}_{z^{n}}(\zeta)\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{z_{\ell}=\zeta\},\quad\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}

(22)

where
\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function. For the Hoeffding test, it was shown that the first-order term
\beta^{\prime}_{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}
is given by
D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)

[
1
]
. Thus, the Hoeffding test achieves the same first-order term as the Neyman-Pearson test without requiring knowledge of the alternative distribution
Q
. This motivates us to consider a generalization of the Hoeffding test, referred to as
divergence test
, which is obtained by replacing the KL-divergence in the Hoeffding test by an arbitrary divergence.

II-B

Divergence

Given any two probability distributions
T,R\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, one can define a non-negative function
D(T\|R)
, called a
divergence
, which represents a measure of discrepancy between them. A divergence is not necessarily symmetric in its arguments and also need not satisfy the triangle inequality.

Mathematically, a divergence is defined on smooth manifolds, referred to as statistical manifolds in the information geometry literature; see
[
15
]
for more details. In particular, the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is a smooth manifold which possesses a global coordinate system, say
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k-1})^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\Xi}
, such that every probability vector
{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is mapped to
\bm{\theta}_{{R}}=(\theta_{1,R},\ldots,\theta_{k-1,R})^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\Xi}
, where
{\Xi}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
. In this paper, we choose the coordinate of a probability distribution
{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
as its first
(k-1)
components. That is, the coordinates of
{R}
are given by
\bm{\theta}_{{R}}=\mathbf{{R}}\triangleq({R}_{1},\ldots,{R}_{k-1})^{\mathsf{T}}
and take value in the open subset

{\Xi}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{{R}}=({R}_{1},\ldots,{R}_{k-1})^{\mathsf{T}}\colon{R}_{i}>0,\;i=1,\ldots,k-1\mbox{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}{R}_{i}<1\right\}.

(23)

Any function of
{R}
can then be thought of as a function of the coordinate
\mathbf{{R}}
.

A formal definition of a divergence on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is as follows:

Definition 1

Consider two distributions
T
and
R
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
with coordinates
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{R}
. A divergence
D:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\rightarrow[0,\infty)
between
T
and
R
, denoted by
D(T\|R)
or
D(\mathbf{T}\|\mathbf{R})
, is a smooth function of
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

D(T\|R)\geq 0
for every
T,R\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
.

2.

D(T\|R)=0
if, and only if,
T=R
.

3.

When
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{R}+\bm{\varepsilon}
, the Taylor expansion of
D
satisfies

D(\mathbf{R}+\bm{\varepsilon}\|\mathbf{R})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k-1}g_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}+O(\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{3}),\quad\text{as }\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0

(24)

for some
(k-1)\times(k-1)
-dimensional positive-definite matrix
G(\mathbf{R})=[g_{ij}(\mathbf{R})]
that depends on
\mathbf{R}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k-1})^{\mathsf{T}}
. In (
24
),
\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{2}\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{i})^{2}}
is the Euclidean norm of
\bm{\varepsilon}
.

4.

Let
R\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and let
\{T_{n}\}
be a sequence of distributions in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
that converges to a distribution
T^{\prime}\in\partial\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
. Then,
D
satisfies

\liminf_{n\to\infty}D(T_{n}\|R)>0.

(25)

Remark 2

We follow the definition of divergence from the information geometry literature. In particular, according to
[
15
, Def. 1.1]
, a divergence must satisfy the first three conditions in Definition
1
. Often, the behavior of a divergence on the boundary of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is not specified. In Definition

Definition 3

Remark 4

The notion of an invariant divergence is adapted from the notion of invariance of geometric structures in information geometry; see

Q
. The following proposition shows that
\beta_{D}^{\prime\prime}
is strictly smaller than
\beta_{\textnormal{NP}}^{\prime\prime}
.

Proposition 11

For every
0<\epsilon<1
and
\mathbf{c}
defined in (
43
), we have

\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon)}>\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon).

(61)

Proof:

Consider any eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
of the positive-definite matrix
\bm{B}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{P}}^{-1/2}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{P}}^{-1/2}
. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k-1}
be i.i.d. standard Normal random variables. Then, for
y\geq 0
,

\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}\right)<\text{Pr}\left(Y_{j}^{2}\geq\frac{y^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\right)\leq\text{Pr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}Y_{i}^{2}\geq y^{2}\right)=\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(y^{2})

(62)

where the first inequality follows because

\text{Pr}(Y_{j}^{2}\geq d^{2})=\text{Pr}(|Y_{j}|\geq d)=2\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}(d),\quad d\geq 0

(63)

and the second inequality follows because
\lambda_{j}Y_{j}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}Y_{i}^{2}
with probability one. Let
y
be such that
\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(y^{2})=\epsilon
. Since
y\mapsto\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}(y)
is strictly decreasing, we obtain from (
62
) that there exists a
y^{\prime}<\frac{y}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}
such that
\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}(y^{\prime})=\epsilon
. It then follows that
\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)=y^{\prime}
, which implies that

\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon)},\quad j=1,\ldots,k-1.

(64)

Next, we note that

\frac{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}=\frac{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{c}}.

(65)

Using the transformation
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{P}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{c}
, this can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{P}}^{1/2}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{P}}^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{B}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}.

It then follows from the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem
[
24
, Th. 4.2.2]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{B}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}

(67)

where
\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}>0
denotes the maximum eigenvalue of the positive-definite matrix
\bm{B}
. Together with (
66
), this yields

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}}{\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}}.

(68)

Combining (
64
) and (
68
), we obtain the desired inequality

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{D,\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon)}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\textnormal{max}}}}

(69)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}.

∎

For an invariant divergence
D
, it follows from Proposition
11
and (
51
) that

\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\epsilon)}>\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon),\quad k\geq 2,\,0<\epsilon<1.

(70)

Furthermore, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 12

For every
0<\epsilon<1
and
k=2,3,\ldots
, we have
\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(\epsilon)>\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\epsilon)
.

Proof:

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k-1}
be i.i.d. standard normal random variables. Then, for
y>0
, consider the events

A=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}^{2}\geq y\right\},\quad B=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}Y_{i}^{2}\geq y\right\},\quad C=\left\{Y_{k}^{2}\geq y\right\}.

(71)

Since
B\cup C\subseteq A
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(A)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(B\cup C)

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(B)+\mathrm{Pr}(C\cap B^{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(B)+\mathrm{Pr}(C)\mathrm{Pr}(B^{c})

because the events
B^{c}
and
C
are independent.
Since, for any
y>0
, we have that
\mathrm{Pr}(C)>0
and
\mathrm{Pr}(B^{c})>0
, this implies that

\mathrm{Pr}(A)>\mathrm{Pr}(B)
, i.e.,

\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(y)>\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(y),\quad y>0.

(73)

Now, for
0<\epsilon<1
, let
y_{0}
be such that
\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(y_{0})=\epsilon
. Then, (
73
) yields that
\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(y_{0})>\epsilon
. Since
y\mapsto\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(y)
is strictly decreasing, there exists a
y^{\prime}>y_{0}
such that
\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(y^{\prime})=\epsilon
, so
\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k}}(\epsilon)>\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{k-1}}(\epsilon)
.

∎

In order to contrast the performances of different divergence tests, we numerically evaluate the second-order performances of the Neyman-Pearson test
{\mathsf{T}}^{\textnormal{NP}}_{n}
, the Hoeffding test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)
, and the divergence test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{SM}}}_{n}(r)
with the squared Mahalanobis distance. Recall that the KL divergence is an invariant divergence. For the squared Mahalanobis distance, we shall consider (
34
) with
\bm{W}_{\mathbf{P}}
having components

\bm{W}_{\mathbf{P}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2P_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2P_{k}^{2}},\quad&i=j\\
\frac{1}{2P_{k}^{2}},\quad&i\neq j.\\
\end{cases}

(74)

To better visualize the second-order performances, we focus on distributions with dimension
k=3
and represent them by the two-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},P_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{1},Q_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
in the coordinate space
\Xi
(defined in (
23
)).

We shall approximate the second-order performances of the aforementioned hypothesis tests by

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\ln\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{\textnormal{NP}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)-\sqrt{\frac{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}{n}}\mathsf{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\epsilon)

(75)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\ln\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)-\sqrt{\frac{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}{n}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{2}}(\epsilon)}

(76)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\ln\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{D_{\textnormal{SM}}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle D_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)-\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{W}_{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{c}}{n}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},2}(\epsilon)}}.

(77)

Since the first-order term
\beta^{\prime}
is not affected by the choice of the hypothesis test, we shall further compare them by means of their second-order terms
\beta^{\prime\prime}
. In particular, we shall compare the Hoeffding test

P
,
Q
, and
\epsilon
:

\rho(P,Q,\epsilon)=\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{W}_{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\mathbf{c}}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},2}}(\epsilon)}}{\sqrt{V_{\textnormal{KL}}(P\|Q)}\sqrt{\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{2}}(\epsilon)}}.

(78)

If
\rho(P,Q,\epsilon)>1

{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{SM}}}_{n}(r)
is strictly smaller than the second-order term of the Hoeffding test
{\mathsf{T}}^{D_{\textnormal{KL}}}_{n}(r)

Proof of Part 1 of Theorem
7

From Lemma
6
, it follows that there exist
M_{0}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|P^{n}\left(nD({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)\geq c\right)-\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(c)\right|\leq M_{0}\delta_{n},\quad n\geq N_{0}

(79)

for some positive
\delta_{n}
that vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
For
0<\epsilon<1
, let

r_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon-M_{0}\delta_{n}).

(80)

It follows then from (
79
) that the type-I error is bounded by
\epsilon
since

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{D}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{n}(D({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)\geq r_{n})

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{n}\left(nD({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)\geq\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}(\epsilon-M_{0}\delta_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}\left(\mathsf{Q}^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{\bm{\lambda},k-1}}\left(\epsilon-M_{0}\delta_{n}\right)\right)+M_{0}\delta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon,\quad n\geq N_{0}.

Thus, the threshold
r_{n}
satisfies (
40
).

We next consider the type-II error for this threshold. To this end, we define the divergence ball of radius
r^{\prime}>0
around the distribution
P
as

\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r^{\prime})\triangleq\left\{T\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\colon D(T\|P)<r^{\prime}\right\},\quad r^{\prime}>0.

(82)

We denote the set of types with denominator
n
by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{P})
is the type class of
\tilde{P}
, defined as

{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{P})\triangleq\left\{z^{n}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}\colon P_{z^{n}}=\tilde{P}\right\}.

(83)

It follows that

\displaystyle\beta_{n}({\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{D}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q^{n}(D({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)<r_{n})

(84)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z^{n}:D({P}_{z^{n}}\|P)<r_{n}}Q^{n}(z^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r_{n})}Q^{n}({\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{P}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r_{n})}\exp\{-nD_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{P}\|Q)\},\quad n\geq N_{0}

where the last step follows from
[
2
, Th. 11.1.4]
.

We next derive a lower bound on
D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{P}\|Q)
for
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r_{n})
. To this end, we shall use the following auxiliary results.

Lemma 13

For any divergence
D
and distribution
{R}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, consider the function
f_{{R}}(\mathbf{T})\triangleq D(T\|{R})
. Suppose that, for any sequence
\{\mathbf{T}_{n}\}
of coordinates, we have
f_{{R}}(\mathbf{T}_{n})=O(b_{n})
for a strictly positive sequence
\left\{b_{n}\right\}
satisfying
b_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, we have

\|\mathbf{T}_{n}-\mathbf{{R}}\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{b_{n}})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof:

See Appendix
B
.

Remark 14

Lemma
13
and (
27
) imply that, if the sequence
\{\mathbf{T}_{n}\}
of coordinates satisfies
D_{1}(\mathbf{T}_{n}\|\mathbf{R})=O(b_{n})
for some divergence
D_{1}
and a strictly positive sequence
\{b_{n}\}
that vanishes as

, then the same sequence of coordinates also satisfies
D_{2}(\mathbf{T}_{n}\|\mathbf{R})=O(b_{n})
for any other divergence
D_{2}
.

For our choice of
r_{n}
in (

), we have that
r_{n}=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
as

. By taking

, Lemma

thus implies that, for every
T\in\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r_{n})
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{P}\|_{2}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(85)

Then, we obtain from (
27
) that, for every
T\in\mathcal{B}_{D,P}(r_{n})
,

VI
Summary and Conclusions

We considered a binary hypothesis testing problem where the hypothesis test has access to the null hypothesis
P
but not to the alternative hypothesis
Q
. A suitable test for this case is the Hoeffding test, which accepts
P
if
D_{\textnormal{KL}}({P}_{Z^{n}}\|P)<r
, for some
r>0
, and otherwise accepts
Q
. We characterized the second-order asymptotic behavior of the type-II error
\beta_{n}
of this test in Stein’s regime, where the type-I error
\alpha_{n}
is required to be bounded by some
\epsilon
. We further proposed a generalization of the Hoeffding test, termed divergence test, for which the KL divergence is replaced by some other (arbitrary) divergence, and characterized its second-order asymptotic behavior of the type-II error
\beta_{n}
in Stein’s regime.

Our results show that, irrespective of the choice of divergence, the divergence test achieves the same first-order term as the Neyman-Pearson test. Thus, like the Hoeffding test, every divergence test is first-order optimal. However, the second-order term of the divergence test, and hence also of the Hoeffding test, is strictly smaller than the second-order term of the Neyman-Pearson test and scales unfavorably with the cardinality of
P
and
Q
. Consequently, lack of knowledge of the alternative hypothesis
Q
negatively impacts the second-order term.

We further demonstrated that the divergence test achieves the same second-order term as the Hoeffding test whenever the divergence belongs to the class of invariant divergences. The class of invariant divergences is large and includes the Rényi divergence and the
f
-divergences (and, hence, also the KL divergence). In contrast, when the divergence is non-invariant, the second-order term achieved by the divergence test differs from the second-order term achieved by the Hoeffding test and may be strictly larger for some alternative hypotheses
Q
. Potentially, this behavior could be exploited by a composite hypothesis test with partial knowledge of the alternative hypothesis
Q
by tailoring the divergence of the divergence test to the set of possible alternative hypotheses
\mathcal{Q}
.
