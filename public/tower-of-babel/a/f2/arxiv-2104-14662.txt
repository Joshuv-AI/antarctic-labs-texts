Dynamic Population Games: A Tractable Intersection of Mean-Field Games and Population Games
Ezzat Elokda, Saverio Bolognani, Andrea Censi, Florian Dörfler, Emilio Frazzoli

Abstract
In many real-world large-scale decision problems, self-interested agents have individual dynamics and optimize their own long-term payoffs. Important examples include the competitive access to shared resources (e.g., roads, energy, or bandwidth) but also non-engineering domains like epidemic propagation and control. These problems are natural to model as mean-field games . Existing mathematical formulations of mean field games have had limited applicability in practice, since they require solving non-standard initial-terminal-value problems that are tractable only in limited special cases. In this letter, we propose a novel formulation, along with computational tools, for a practically relevant class of Dynamic Population Games (DPGs) , which correspond to discrete-time, finite-state-and-action, stationary mean-field games. Our main contribution is a mathematical reduction of Stationary Nash Equilibria (SNE) in DPGs to standard Nash Equilibria (NE) in static population games. This reduction is leveraged to guarantee the existence of a SNE , develop an evolutionary dynamics -based SNE computation algorithm, and derive simple conditions that guarantee stability and uniqueness of the SNE . We provide two examples of applications: fair resource allocation with heterogeneous agents and control of epidemic propagation. Open source software for SNE computation: https://gitlab.ethz.ch/elokdae/dynamic-population-games



I
Introduction

Mean field games, introduced under that name in
[
1
,
2
]
, and otherwise referred to as
anonymous sequential games

[
3
,
4
]
, model competitive settings in large populations of selfish agents, who have individual state dynamics and optimize individual long-term average or discounted payoffs.
The microscopic state dynamics and payoffs of different agents are coupled through the
mean field
, that is, the macroscopic distribution of states (and in some works, the distribution of states and actions
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
).
Mean field games occur naturally in many important real-world problems.
For example, in an epidemic outbreak, agents have individual infection states, and the probability of getting infected depends on the proportion of infected agents, as well as whether the agents adhere to social distancing policies
[
6
,
7
]
.
In a congested power grid, an
Electric Vehicle (EV)
with individual state of charge experiences different charging rates depending on the proportion of
EV
’s charging at the same time
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
Moreover, recent works have employed mean field games for the design of fair, non-monetary mechanisms in shared resource allocation settings, e.g., for traffic congestion management
[
11
]
.
In this case, the individual state is a private, stochastic urgency for the shared resource, as well as a scarce budget of tokens used to bid for the resource, whose dynamics depend on the macroscopic bidding behavior of others
[
12
,
13
]
.

In all of these applications, the availability of practical computation tools to predict the
Mean Field Equilibrium (MFE)

is instrumental to study the effect of complex control or policy design choices.
However, general formulations of mean field games face a computational bottleneck as they require solving an
initial-terminal-value problem
consisting of a backward Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation characterizing the optimality of the agents’ policy trajectory; and a forward Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) equation ensuring that the mean field trajectory is consistent with the state dynamics under the optimal policy trajectory.
Existence (and uniqueness) of solutions to the initial-terminal-value problem is guaranteed under abstract assumptions that are difficult to verify in practice
[
1
,
2
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
These assumptions typically manifest in linear dynamics with quadratic costs
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
18
]
, and/or a weak dependency on the mean field (e.g., through the average state or action only
[
8
,
10
,
19
,
20
]
).

Due to these challenges, previous works have considered the less general solution concept of the
stationary mean field equilibrium

[
5
,
21
,
22
]
, also referred to as the
Stationary Nash Equilibrium (SNE)

[
4
,
23
]
, which is a single pair of optimal policy and the associated stationary mean field.
For the practically relevant class of
discrete-time, finite-state-and-action, stationary mean-field games
, which we call
Dynamic Population Games (DPGs)

for short,

are computationally attractive as they are characterized as finite-dimensional fixed points rather than initial-terminal-value problems.

SNE

[
5
,
22
,
24
]
.

A
Nash Equilibrium (NE)

[

In particular,

[
25
]
.
Moreover, recent works have enriched the classical evolutionary dynamics with passivity-based and higher-order learning techniques that extend the classes of games in which convergence to a

is guaranteed
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30

.

Population games are trivially mean field games, in which agents have only one state (with trivial dynamics) and finite actions.
The main contribution of this letter is to show that there is a larger and meaningful intersection:
Dynamic Population Games (DPGs)

are

We show a mathematical reduction of

to standard
NE
in population games:
For each
DPG

coincide with
SNE
of the
DPG

The reduction of

We leverage it to guarantee the existence of a

SNE

Finally, the versatility of our approach is demonstrated in two complex application examples involving fair resource allocation to heterogeneous agents, as well as epidemic propagation and control.

II-A

Dynamic Population Game (DPG)
Setting

A
DPG
, denoted by
{\mathcal{G}}
, consists of a population
{\mathcal{N}}=\{1,\dots,N\}
of anonymous agents, with
N
large such that the agents approximately form a
continuum of mass
.
Each agent has one of a finite number of private static
types

\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}=\{1,\dots,N_{\tau}\}
, and the distribution of types is the exogenous parameter

g\in\Delta({\mathcal{T}})
.
In addition to types, each agent has a private
state

x\in{\mathcal{X}}=\{1,\dots,N_{x}\}
,
which follows a dynamic process to be described hereafter.
The
state distribution
of type
\tau
agents is given by
d_{\tau}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\tau}=g_{\tau}\>\Delta({\mathcal{X}})
,
and the joint
type-state distribution
is the concatenation of state distributions
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{N_{\tau}})\in{\mathcal{D}}=\prod_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\tau}
.

At discrete time steps, agents participate in a game in which each agent of type-state
[\tau,x]
plays one of a finite number of actions
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]
.
Type
\tau
agents pick their action according to the stationary homogeneous
policy

\pi_{\tau}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x])
,
which maps the state to a probability distribution over the actions
1
1

1

This class of policies has been previously referred to as
oblivious policies

[
4
]
, since the agents choose their actions based on their individual state only and not the state distribution of others.
.
The probability to play action
a
when in state
x
is denoted by
\pi_{\tau}[a\mid x]\in[0,1]
. The set of policies of type
\tau
is denoted by
\Pi_{\tau}
, and the concatenation of the policies is denoted by
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N_{\tau}})\in\Pi=\prod_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}}\Pi_{\tau}
.

The pair
(d,\pi)\in{\mathcal{D}}\times\Pi
is referred to as the
mean field
(in the tradition of mean field games) or the
social state
(in the tradition of population games; a formal equivalency is shown in Section
III
).
It gives a macroscopic description of the distribution of the agents’ types and states, as well as how they behave
2
2

2

Our definition of mean field
(d,\pi)
differs slightly from the definition as the joint state-action distribution
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
; however, the latter can be readily derived from
(d,\pi)
.
.

The discrete time state dynamics of type
\tau
agents are governed by the
state transition function

\displaystyle p_{\tau}[x^{+}\mid x,a](d,\pi),

(1)

which is the probability of the state to transition from
x
to
x^{+}
after playing
a
, as a function of the mean field
(d,\pi)
.
The agent receives an
immediate payoff
for playing
a
when in state
x
, which is also a function of
(d,\pi)
given by

\displaystyle r_{\tau}[x,a](d,\pi).

(2)

A
DPG

{\mathcal{G}}
is thus specified by the pair of state transition and immediate payoff functions (
1
)–(
2
).

II-B

Solution Concept:
Stationary Nash Equilibrium (SNE)

Before introducing the solution concept in
DPG
s, we must introduce definitions pertaining to the strategic problem faced by the population agents.
Each agent faces a
discounted
Markov Decision Process (MDP)

, i.e., aims to maximize its discounted infinite horizon payoff, with the type-dependant discount factor
\alpha_{\tau}\in[0,1)
.
Each agent’s
MDP
is coupled to other agents through the mean field
(d,\pi)
.
Namely, when a type
\tau
agent follows policy
\pi_{\tau}
, its
expected immediate payoff
and
state transition matrix
are respectively given by,

\displaystyle R_{\tau}[x](d,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]}\pi_{\tau}[a\mid x]\>r_{\tau}[x,a](d,\pi),

(3)

\displaystyle P_{\tau}[x^{+}\mid x](d,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]}\pi_{\tau}[a\mid x]\>p_{\tau}[x^{+}\mid x,a](d,\pi),

(4)

and its
expected infinite horizon payoff
, also known as the
value function
, is recursively defined as

V_{\tau}[x](d,\pi)=R_{\tau}[x]+\alpha_{\tau}\sum_{x^{+}\in{\mathcal{X}}}P_{\tau}[x^{+}\mid x]\>V_{\tau}[x^{+}].

(5)

Equation (
5
) is the Bellman recursion characterizing the value function associated with policy
\pi_{\tau}
.
Moreover, the
single stage deviation payoff
, also known as the
state-action value function
or
Q
-function
, is given by

Q_{\tau}[x,a](d,\pi)=r_{\tau}[x,a]+\alpha_{\tau}\sum_{x^{+}\in{\mathcal{X}}}p_{\tau}[x^{+}\mid x,a]\>V_{\tau}[x^{+}].

(6)

Equation (
6
) is the expected infinite horizon payoff if the type
\tau
agent in state
x
deviates from
\pi_{\tau}
for a single stage by playing
a
, then follows
\pi_{\tau}
in the future.
Notice that we omitted the dependency on
(d,\pi)
on the right-hand side of Equations (
5
)–(
6
) for notational convenience.

A policy attains the maximum value function (
5
) if and only if it maximizes the state-action value function (
6
) for all states
x\in{\mathcal{X}}

[
31
, Proposition 1.3.4]
.
This motivates the definition of the
state-dependent best response correspondence

B_{\tau}[x](d,\pi)\in\\
\left\{\sigma\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x])\left\lvert\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\sigma^{\prime}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]),\\[5.69054pt]
\sum\limits_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]}\left(\sigma[a]-\sigma^{\prime}[a]\right)Q_{\tau}[x,a](d,\pi)\geq 0\end{array}\right.\right\},

(7)

which is the set of probability distributions over the actions maximizing the state-action value function for a given type-state
[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
.
With this, we are ready to define the solution concept in
DPG
s.

Definition 1
(
Stationary Nash Equilibrium
)

A
Stationary Nash Equilibrium (SNE)

is a mean field
(d^{*},\pi^{*})\in{\mathcal{D}}\times\Pi
which satisfies, for all
[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
,

\displaystyle d^{*}_{\tau}[x]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{-}\in{\mathcal{X}}}d^{*}_{\tau}[x^{-}]\>P_{\tau}[x\mid x^{-}](d^{*},\pi^{*}),

(
SNE
.1)

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{\tau}[\cdot\mid x]

\displaystyle\in B_{\tau}[x](d^{*},\pi^{*}).

(
SNE
.2)

The
SNE
is similar to a classical
Nash Equilibrium (NE)
in that agents have no incentive to unilaterally deviate from the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
(
SNE.2
).
However, in addition, it requires that the type-state distribution
d^{*}
is
stationary
under the stochastic process characterized by
P_{\tau}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
(
SNE.1
).
The stationarity of
d^{*}
ensures that the
MDP
faced by each agent is time invariant at the equilibrium.

III
Main Result: Reduction to Population Game

In this section, we present the main result of this letter, which is a reduction of the
SNE
in
DPG
s to a standard
NE
in static population games.
In order to state our result, we must first briefly recall the definition of population games
[
25
]
.
A population game is defined by a
payoff vector

F(s)=(F_{1}(s),\dots,F_{N_{\rho}}(s))
, where
F_{\rho}(s)
is the payoff vector of population
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}=\{1,\dots,N_{\rho}\}
.
Each entry of
F_{\rho}(s)
, denoted by
F_{\rho}[a](s)
, gives the payoff of a population
\rho
agent for playing one of its finite actions
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}
.
The
social state

s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N_{\rho}})\in{\mathcal{S}}
gives the distribution of actions in the population.
Namely,
s_{\rho}\in g_{\rho}\>\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\rho})
is the distribution of actions in population
\rho
, where
g_{\rho}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is the total mass of agents in that population.
In the language of
DPG
s, the populations
\rho
can be viewed as the types
\tau
, and there are no time varying states
x
(or, equivalently, the set of states
{\mathcal{X}}
is a singleton).
The
best response correspondence
of population
\rho
is given by

B_{\rho}(s)\in\\
\left\{\sigma\in g_{\rho}\>\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\rho})\left\lvert\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\sigma^{\prime}\in g_{\rho}\>\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}),\\[5.69054pt]
\sum\limits_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}}\left(\sigma[a]-\sigma^{\prime}[a]\right)F_{\rho}[a](s)\geq 0\end{array}\right.\right\},

(8)

and the
Nash Equilibrium (NE)

is defined as a social state
s^{*}
that satisfies
s^{*}_{\rho}\in B_{\rho}(s^{*})
for all
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

Theorem 1
(Reduction of
SNE
)

For every
DPG

{\mathcal{G}}
, there exists a static population game
F^{\mathcal{G}}
whose Nash Equilibria (NE) coincide with the Stationary Nash Equilibria (SNE) of
{\mathcal{G}}
, i.e., it holds that
(d^{*},\pi^{*})
is a
SNE
of
{\mathcal{G}}
if and only if
s^{*}=(d^{*},\pi^{*})
is a
NE
of
F^{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof:

The proof proceeds by construction.
We construct a population game
F^{\mathcal{G}}
with
N_{\rho}=N_{\tau}\>(1+N_{x})
populations.
The first
N_{\tau}
populations correspond to the different types, and we denote these populations as
\rho=\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
.
The remaining
N_{\tau}N_{x}
populations correspond to the
type-state pairs
, and we denote these populations as
\rho=[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
.
The action space of populations
\rho=\tau

is
the state space
{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}={\mathcal{X}}
, whereas the action space of populations
\rho=[\tau,x]
is respectively
{\mathcal{A}}_{[\tau,x]}={\mathcal{A}}_{\tau}[x]
.
The mass of populations
\rho=\tau
is respectively
g_{\tau}
, whereas the mass of populations
\rho=[\tau,x]
is uniformly
g_{[\tau,x]}=1
.
With this construction, it holds that
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{D}}\times\Pi
, i.e., each social state
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
under
F^{\mathcal{G}}
is formally equivalent to a mean field
(d,\pi)\in{\mathcal{D}}\times\Pi
under
{\mathcal{G}}
.
We thus write
s=(d,\pi)
and define the payoffs
F^{\mathcal{G}}(d,\pi)
for both groups of populations as

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}[x](d,\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{-}\in{\mathcal{X}}}d_{\tau}[x^{-}]\>P_{\tau}[x\mid x^{-}](d,\pi)-d_{\tau}[x],

(9)

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{G}}_{[\tau,x]}[a](d,\pi)

\displaystyle=Q_{\tau}[x,a](d,\pi).

(10)

The payoffs of populations
\rho=\tau
in Equation (
9
) are motivated by condition (
SNE.1
).
Intuitively, these populations can be interpreted as
agents of nature playing the dynamics
, i.e., they seek to bring the state distributions
d_{\tau}
to stationarity.
On the other hand, the payoffs of populations
\rho=[\tau,x]
in Equation (
10
) coincide with the state-action value functions, as these are the quantities maximized by the agents in the
DPG

{\mathcal{G}}
.

To prove the theorem, we now show that the
NE
condition of
F^{\mathcal{G}}
, i.e.,
s^{*}_{\rho}\in B_{\rho}(s^{*})
for all
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}
, is formally equivalent to conditions (
SNE.1
)–(
SNE.2
), with
s^{*}=(d^{*},\pi^{*})
.
By construction, for all populations
\rho=[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
, with
s^{*}_{[\tau,x]}=\pi^{*}_{\tau}[\cdot\mid x]
, condition
\pi^{*}_{\tau}[\cdot\mid x]\in B_{[\tau,x]}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
is formally equivalent to condition (
SNE.2
).
Therefore, we must show that for the remaining populations
\rho=\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
, with
s^{*}_{\tau}=d^{*}_{\tau}
,
d^{*}_{\tau}\in B_{\tau}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
if and only if
d^{*}_{\tau}[x]
satisfies condition (
SNE.1
) for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
One direction of the implication is trivial: if
d^{*}_{\tau}[x]
satisfies condition (
SNE.1
) for all
[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
, then
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}[x](d^{*},\pi^{*})=0
, and all state distributions satisfy
d_{\tau}\in B_{\tau}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
, including
d^{*}_{\tau}
.
For the other direction of the implication, suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
d^{*}_{\tau}\in B_{\tau}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
for all
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
, but there is some type-state
[\tilde{\tau},\tilde{x}]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
for which
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\tilde{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})\neq 0
.
Notice that
\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}[x](d,\pi)=0
for all
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
(d,\pi)\in{\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
; therefore, if
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\tilde{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})>0
(respectively,
<0
), there must be another state
\hat{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
for which
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\hat{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})<0
(respectively,
>0
).
Suppose without loss of generality that
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\tilde{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})>0
and
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\hat{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})<0
.
Since
d^{*}_{\tilde{\tau}}\in B_{\tilde{\tau}}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
, it must be that
d^{*}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\hat{x}]=0
.
But this contradicts
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\hat{x}](d^{*},\pi^{*})<0
, in which
-d^{*}_{\tilde{\tau}}[\hat{x}]
is the only negative term.
We thus showed that if
d^{*}_{\tau}\in B_{\tau}(d^{*},\pi^{*})
for all
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
, it must be that
F^{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}[x](d^{*},\pi^{*})=0
for all
[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e.,
d^{*}_{\tau}
satisfies condition (
SNE.1
) for all
[\tau,x]\in{\mathcal{T}}\times{\mathcal{X}}
.
∎

IV
Consequences of the Reduction

In this section, we discuss important consequences of our main result, Theorem

, which establishes that
SNE
in
DPG

NE
in static population games.
In general, all existing results pertaining to the

of the
equivalent population game

, can be adopted to the

of
DPG

{\mathcal{G}}
.
We specialize a few of these results in this section.

IV-A

Existence of
SNE

A
SNE
is guaranteed to exist under the following mild assumption. This result can be proven directly using a fixed point argument
[

,
4
]
, but in this letter we present a simpler proof based on Theorem

Assumption 1
(Continuity)

The state transition function
p_{\tau}(d,\pi)
and the immediate payoff function

are continuous in the mean field
(d,\pi)
.

Proposition 1
(Existence of
SNE
)

Let Assumption
1
hold for
DPG

{\mathcal{G}}
.
Then
{\mathcal{G}}
is guaranteed to have at least one
SNE

(d^{*},\pi^{*})
.

Proof:

Consider the equivalent population game
F^{\mathcal{G}}(d,\pi)
whose payoffs are given in Equations (
9
)–(
10
).
Assumption
1
implies that
F^{\mathcal{G}}(d,\pi)
is continuous in
(d,\pi)
: continuity of (
9
) is immediate; and continuity of (
10
) follows from noticing that the value function (
5
) can be equivalently written in vector from as
V_{\tau}(d,\pi)=\left(I-\alpha_{\tau}P_{\tau}(d,\pi)\right)^{-1}R_{\tau}(d,\pi)
, which is continuous in
(d,\pi)
for all
\alpha_{\tau}\in[0,1)
.
It follows that at least one

(d^{*},\pi^{*})
is guaranteed to exist in
F^{\mathcal{G}}

[
25
, Theorem 2.1.1]
, which coincides with a
SNE
of
{\mathcal{G}}
by Theorem
1
.
∎

IV-B

Evolutionary Dynamics for
SNE
Computation

Theorem
1
moreover inspires the following
evolutionary state-policy dynamics
model for
SNE

\displaystyle\dot{d}_{\tau}[x]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{-}\in{\mathcal{X}}}d_{\tau}[x^{-}]\>P_{\tau}[x\mid x^{-}](d,\pi)-d_{\tau}[x],

\displaystyle\dot{\pi}_{\tau}[\cdot\mid x]

\displaystyle=\eta_{\tau}\>H_{\tau}(Q_{\tau}[x,\cdot](\pi,d),\pi_{\tau}[\cdot\mid x]),

(EV.2)

The rate parameter

V-A

Fair Resource Allocation

Recent works have shown that introducing dynamics in a resource allocation problem enables the design of fair, non-monetary incentive schemes
[
11
,
13
,
33
]
.
This is particularly important in contexts involving scarce, shared resources,
e.g., for traffic congestion management.
Instead of money, a non-tradable token, called
karma
, is issued to the agents, who may use their karma to bid for shared resources.
Karma then flows from agents that acquire resources to those who yield resources, in accordance to exchange rules that are up to the policy maker to design.
The agents must be strategic and future sighted in their bidding, in order to spare karma for times of highest
urgency
.
Thus, karma naturally induces a
DPG
in which an agent’s state is its current urgency and karma, and the immediate payoff for a given karma bid, as well as the karma dynamics, depend on the mean field through a potentially complex set of karma exchange rules.

Fortunately, the
DPG
formulation has enabled devising tractable computational models of such a
self-contained karma economy
.
In
[
13
]
, computational insight on the
SNE
of the karma
DPG
is provided for different karma exchange rules, including first vs. second price auctions, peer-to-peer payments vs. payments based on societal redistribution, and even exchanges involving karma taxation.
Moreover, the fairness of the karma-based scheme with respect to heterogeneity in the agents’ future awareness and urgency processes is investigated by incorporating multiple agent types.
In
[
11
]
, the self-contained karma economy is combined with a highway congestion model, where, in addition to bidding karma to access a congestion-free priority lane, agents must also choose when to travel in order to avoid the peak morning congestion, leading to even more complex and high-dimensional
SNE
.
The model of
[
11
]
is further extended in
[
33
]
, where additionally an elastic demand of commuters is introduced as a new agent type.

In all of the above works, the SNE is computed using the evolutionary dynamics (
EV.1
)–(
EV.1
), with
H_{\tau}
chosen as the
perturbed best response dynamic

[
25
]
.

SNE
adopted from
[

]

\alpha_{1}=0.7

\alpha_{2}=0.99

The top and bottom row of Figure

(

With no tax, the future sighted agents bid slightly less (Figure

VI
Conclusion

We show that Stationary Nash Equilibria (SNE) in Dynamic Population Games (DPGs) can be reduced to standard Nash Equilibria (NE) in static population games.
This result unlocks a rich set of existing tools for SNE analysis and computation, which makes the DPG formulation favorable in comparison to other mean-field game formulations.
We demonstrate the versatility of the result in two complex application examples.
Important future topics include investigating the relationship between condition (9) and previous conditions that guarantee convergence to a
SNE
, as well as the decentralized computation of the
SNE
by the agents.
