Universally Optimal Periodic Configurations in the Plane
Douglas P. Hardin, Nathaniel J. Tenpas

Abstract
We develop linear programming bounds for the energy of configurations in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , periodic with respect to a lattice. In certain cases, the construction of sharp bounds can be formulated as a finite dimensional, multivariate polynomial interpolation problem. We use this framework to show a scaling of the equitriangular lattice A 2 A_{2} is universally optimal among all configurations of the form ω 4 + A 2 \omega_{4}+A_{2} where ω 4 \omega_{4} is a 4-point configuration in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Likewise, we show a scaling and rotation of A 2 A_{2} is universally optimal among all configurations of the form ω 6 + L \omega_{6}+L where ω 6 \omega_{6} is a 6-point configuration in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} and L = ℤ × 3 ​ ℤ L=\mathbb{Z}\times\sqrt{3}\mathbb{Z} .

2.3

If

2.5
Lattice theta functions

For
c>0
, the classical Jacobi theta function of the third type, is defined by

\theta(c;x):=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}e^{-\pi k^{2}c}e^{2\pi ikx},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(20)

Via Poisson Summation on the integers, we have

\theta(c;x)=c^{-1/2}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}e^{-\frac{\pi(k+x)^{2}}{c}},

(21)

and so, in terms of our earlier language for periodizing gaussians by lattices,

F_{a,\mathbb{Z}}(x)=(a/\pi)^{1/2}\theta(\frac{\pi}{a};x).

(22)

We’ll also use

\tilde{\theta}(c;t):=\theta\left(c,\frac{\arccos t}{2\pi}\right),\quad t\in[-1,1].

It follows from the symmetries of
\theta(c,x)
that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\tilde{\theta}(c;\cos 2\pi x)=\theta(c;x),

and moreover, as shown below,
\tilde{\theta}
is absolutely monotone on
[-1,1]
. First, we recall the Jacobi triple product formula.

Theorem 9
.

Jacobi Triple Product Formula
Let
z,q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\lvert q\rvert<1
and
z\neq 0
. Then

\prod^{\infty}_{r=1}(1-q^{2r})(1+q^{2r-1}z^{2})(1+q^{2r-1}z^{-2})=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}q^{k^{2}}z^{2k}.

Applying the Jacobi triple product with
q=e^{-\pi c}
and
z=e^{\pi ix}
, gives

\tilde{\theta}(c;t)=\prod^{\infty}_{r=1}(1-e^{-2\pi rc})(1+2e^{-2\pi rc}t+e^{-2(2r-1)\pi c}).

(23)

It’s elementary to verify that
\tilde{\theta}(c;\cdot)
is entire, and that we may compute derivatives by applications of the product rule to (
23
). Hence, we arrive at the following proposition:

Proposition 10
.

For any
c>0
, the function
\tilde{\theta}=\tilde{\theta}(c;\cdot):[-1,1]\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is strictly absolutely monotone on
[-1,1]
and its logarithmic derivative
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}/\tilde{\theta}
is strictly completely monotone on
[-1,1]
.

If

is a rectangular lattice, then

is a tensor product of such functions:

\begin{split}F_{a,\Phi}(x)&=\sum_{v\in\Phi}e^{-a\|x+v\|^{2}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}e^{-aa_{i}^{2}(x_{i}/a_{i}+k_{i})^{2}}\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-aa_{i}^{2}(\frac{x_{i}}{a_{i}}+k_{i})^{2}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}F_{aa_{i}^{2},\mathbb{Z}}(x_{i}/a_{i}).\end{split}

(24)

If
\Lambda

Appendix A
Divided differences and univariate interpolation

We review some basic results concerning one-dimensional polynomial interpolation (e.g., see
[
4
, Section 5.6.2]
).
Let
f\in C^{m}[a,b]
for some
a,b
be given along with some multiset

T=\{t_{0},t_{1},...,t_{m}\}\subseteq[a,b].

Then there then exists a unique polynomial
H_{T}(f)(t)
of degree at most
m
(called a
Hermite interpolant of
f
) such that for each
\alpha\in T
, we have
H^{(\ell)}_{T}(f)(\alpha)=f^{(\ell)}(\alpha)
for
0\leq\ell<k_{\alpha}
where
k_{\alpha}
denotes the multiplicity of
\alpha
in
T
.
Let
f[t_{0},...,t_{m}]
denote the coefficient of
t^{m}
in
H_{T}(f)(t)
. This coefficient is called the
m-th divided difference
of
f
for
T
.
Then
H_{T}(f)
, may be expressed as

H_{T}(f)(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}f[t_{0},t_{1},...,t_{k}]p_{k}(T;t)

(78)

where the
partial products

p_{k}
are defined by

p_{0}(T;t):=1\text{ and }p_{j}(T;t):=\prod_{i<j}(t-t_{i}),\hskip 5.69046ptj=1,2,...,m.

(79)

Then a generalization of the mean value theorem implies that there is some
\xi\in[a,b]
such that

\frac{f^{(m)}(\xi)}{m!}=f[t_{0},t_{1},...,t_{m}].

(80)

Putting these together, we arrive at the classical
Hermite error formula
:

f(t)-H_{T}(f)(t)=f[t_{0},t_{1},...,t_{m},t]\prod_{i=0}^{m}(t-t_{i})=\frac{f^{(m+1)}(\xi)}{(m+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{m}(t-t_{i}).

(81)

In the case that
f
is absolutely monotone on
[a,b]
, such as with
\tilde{f_{1}}
and
\tilde{f_{2}}
, then the sign of
f(t)-H_{T}(f)(t)
equals the sign of
\prod^{m}_{i=0}(t-t_{i})
.

Equivalence of Different Notions of Universal Optimality

(2)

(3)

Proof.

First, we’ll prove (1) implies (2).

-universally optimal as desired.

Clearly (2) implies (3) so it remains to show (3) implies (1). Assume

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa N_{k}^{d}}\left(E_{N_{K}\Phi}(\omega_{\kappa N_{k}^{d}})+\kappa N_{k}^{d}\sum_{0\neq v\in N_{k}\Phi}f(|v|^{2})\right)
