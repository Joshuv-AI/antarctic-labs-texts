Pairwise interaction function estimation of stationary Gibbs point processes using basis expansion
Ismaïla Ba ??,??, Jean-François Coeurjolly ??,??, Francisco Cuevas-Pacheco ??

Abstract
The class of Gibbs point processes (GPP) is a large class of spatial point processes able to model both clustered and repulsive point patterns. They are specified by their conditional intensity, which for a point pattern 𝐱 \mathbf{x} and a location u u , is roughly speaking the probability that an event occurs in an infinitesimal ball around u u given the rest of the configuration is 𝐱 \mathbf{x} . The most simple and natural class of models is the class of pairwise interaction point processes where the conditional intensity depends on the number of points and pairwise distances between them. This paper is concerned with the problem of estimating the pairwise interaction function non parametrically. We propose to estimate it using an orthogonal series expansion of its logarithm. Such an approach has numerous advantages compared to existing ones. The estimation procedure is simple, fast and completely data-driven. We provide asymptotic properties such as consistency and asymptotic normality and show the efficiency of the procedure through simulation experiments and illustrate it with several datasets.

Introduction

Spatial point processes are widely used to model data in the form of events or objects within a region (commonly called spatial domain). There are applications of spatial point process models in a broad range of disciplines for instance in forestry and plant ecology, epidemiology, seismology, astronomy, and others
[
3
,
47
]
. Due to their ability to model a wide variety of spatial point patterns, the class of Gibbs point processes is an important class of models for spatial point pattern analysis
[
37
,
51
,
47
,
5
,
28
, see e.g.]
. Gibbs point processes are however well-known for their lack of tractability
[
28
, see e.g.]
: even for simple models such as the Strauss process
[
65
]
, intensity functions as well as standard summary statistics used in point pattern analysis like the Ripley’s
K
-function, the nearest-neighbour
G
function or the empty space function
F
, are not explicit. To rephrase this comment, this means that even for simple parametric models, it is in general impossible to set the parameters of a Gibbs model such that the average number of points in a given window has a prescribed value.

Therefore, Gibbs models are not defined through intensity functions. Instead, in a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, they are characterized by a density with respect to the (unit rate) Poisson point process, the reference process modelling random points without any interaction. The flexibility of the density makes the modelling simple and natural. One has a mechanistic interpretation of the model and produced patterns can be highly regular, clustered or exhibit a complex mixture of these two characteristics.
This paper is focused on the class of pairwise interaction point processes, for which the density depends (in the homogeneous case) on the number of points and on pairs of points.
From a statistical point of view, it is not convenient to work with the density of a Gibbs point process, as the latter is, in general, defined up to a normalizing constant. The quantity of interest, which overcomes this problem, is the Papangelou conditional intensity function
[
28
, see again]
. This function, which can be seen as a ratio of two densities, is interpreted as the probability to observe a point in the vicinity of a location, say
u
, given the rest of the configuration of points is
\mathbf{x}
.
In this paper, we focus on stationary and isotropic pairwise interaction Gibbs point processes which are defined (see Section
2
for a more formal and precise definition) via their Papangelou conditional intensity given for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{x}
a configuration of points by

\displaystyle\lambda(u,\mathbf{x})=\beta\prod_{v\in\mathbf{x}}\phi(\|v-u\|)

(1.1)

and we investigate the problem of estimating simultaneously the non-negative parameter
\beta
and the function
\phi
.

Methods of nonparametric curve estimation allow one to analyze and present data at hand without any prior about the data

i.e. these methods let the data speak for themselves. Kernel estimation and orthogonal series estimation
[

56

71

are among some of them. In the context of spatial point processes, a considerable amount of research has addressed nonparametric estimation for first and second order summary functions, i.e. the intensity function, the pair correlation function (which is a normalized version of the second-order intensity function), and summary functions like Ripley’s K function or functions F, G, J, L
[
64
,
51
,
47
,
5
]
. The current literature covers theoretical as well as practical aspects and all these nonparametric estimates are implemented in particular in

packages such as
spatstat,GET,stpp

.

Nonparametric estimation of the pairwise interaction function of a Gibbs point process has also been investigated but has received much less attention.

As a summary, there is no methodology which, simultaneously, is theoretically studied, easily implementable and computationally feasible in a very reasonable time. The aim of this paper is to fill this gap and to address nonparametric estimation of the pairwise interaction function of a stationary GPP via series expansion, an approach which has never been considered for pairwise interaction Gibbs point processes. In a recent study,
[
48
,
18
]
have adapted orthogonal series density estimators
[

,
32

to the estimation of the pair correlation function or its logarithm of a spatial point process.
In the same vein, we develop an orthogonal series estimation of the log pairwise interaction function, i.e.
\log\phi
, for stationary Gibbs point processes. Our approach, detailed in Section
3
, is to truncate the series expansion of
\log\phi
and to estimate coefficients of the truncated series by maximizing a truncated version of the log pseudolikelihood function (
[
12
,
50
]
). The pseudolikelihood method is an efficient and very popular parametric method, alternative to the maximum likelihood method since MLE is first complex to implement (due to the fact that the density of the Gibbs point process is defined up to a normalizing constant depending on the parameters) and second very complex to study theoretically. Consistency for the MLE is only proved and under for some particular models
[
29
]
. The pseudolikelihood is roughly speaking a composite likelihood which involves only the conditional intensity function. Since its introduction in the context of spatial point processes by
[
50
]
, the pseudolikelihood has gained in popularity. It has been theoretically studied in different contexts for finite range or infinite range models, i.e. when the function
\phi
equals one or not after a certain distance
[
49
,
13
,
22
, see]
, or for high-dimensional inhomogeneous models
[
1
]
. Numerically the pseudolikelihood can be very well approximated by a quasi-Poisson regression. Hence the method can be efficiently implemented using standard software and in particular within the
R
package
spatstat

[
6
]
dedicated to spatial point pattern analysis.

Our theoretical contribution is to provide conditions on the homogeneous pairwise interaction Gibbs model (local stability, finite range property, etc) and on the orthonormal basis system to obtain the consistency and asymptotic normality for the truncated pseudolikelihood estimator and the orthogonal series estimator. These results are established in an increasing domain setting i.e. a setting where the number of points in the observation domain grows with the volume of the observation domain. This asymptotic framework is common in spatial statistics
[
63
, see e.g.]
. In particular, Theorem
1
shows the expected compromise between the number of polynomials
K
used in the series expansion and the amount of acquired data expressed by the volume of the observation domain which has to be considered to get a consistent estimator. Our work is inspired by
[
48
]
but is fundamentally different. First, the referred paper focuses on the pair correlation function. As already mentioned, the pair correlation function of a Gibbs point process is not explicit and cannot be directly related to its pairwise interaction function. Second,
[
48
]
have taken advantage of the fact that the kernel estimation of the pair correlation is explicit. This is the main reason why coefficients of the series expansion of the pair correlation can be estimated separately, without any optimization procedure making estimators easier to study from a theoretical point of view.

The choice to expand
\log\phi
instead of
\phi
combined with the truncated pseudolikelihood method used to estimate the coefficients brings back the problem to the maximization of a concave function of the parameters
[
50
, see]
. Therefore the method is very fast and numerically stable. In addition,

[
24
]
have proposed fast and consistent estimates of the sensitivity matrix and variance covariance matrix of the score of the pseudolikelihood. Such estimates are implemented in the
spatstat R
package. Another advantage of our orthogonal series expansion approach is that we can take advantage of this work and propose asymptotic pointwise confidence intervals for
g(r)=\log\phi(r)
(and
\phi(r)
) for any
r
. By passing, working with an orthonormal basis system is appealing as we are able to prove that normalized versions of the sensitivity matrix and variance of the score of the pseudolikelihood are positive definite for
n
sufficiently large (see Lemmas
2
-
3
for more details). Such a result is essential to ensure the uniqueness maximum of the truncated pseudolikelihood and to evaluate estimates of the asymptotic variance of
\hat{g}(r)
and
\hat{\phi}(r)
.

The orthogonal series expansion estimator we propose naturally depends on a smoothing parameter, here the number
K
of orthonormal functions in the series expansion. Tuning this parameter or the bandwidth of a kernel is a standard and recurrent problem in functional estimation
[see e.g.
43
,
32
, for the density estimation problem]
. We propose to select the smoothing parameter
K
using a composite information criterion, namely the composite AIC
[
36
,
17
]
. This information criterion has the advantage to be quickly evaluated making the whole methodology fully data-driven.

Several perspectives could be undertaken based on the present work. First, we assume that the process has a finite range. To say it quickly, see (
FR
), we assume that
\phi(r)=1
for
r\geq\tilde{R}
form some
0<\tilde{R}<\infty
. Extending the methodology to infinite-range models such as the Lennard-Jones model
[
20
, e.g.]
could be of interest. One limitation of (
1.1
) is also that we focus on homogeneous models. Letting for instance the parameter
\beta
to evolve with space and estimate non parametrically both functions
\beta
and
\phi
is definitely an interesting but challenging problem from a theoretical point of view.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give a brief review on pairwise interaction point processes, recall the Georgii-Nguyen-Zessin formula and define some important concepts (translation invariance, finite range, local stability) which ensure the existence of a stationary spatial GPP. We also provide a few examples of pairwise interaction functions in this section.
The orthogonal series expansion procedure we propose is presented in Section
3
.
Section
4
deals with asymptotic properties and presents our main theoretical results. Section
5

We also investigate the misspecification or estimation of irregular parameters such as the hard core and finite range parameters. An application to real datasets as well as proofs of our main results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Pairwise interaction Gibbs point processes

The framework of this paper is concerned with stationary and isotropic Gibbs point processes
\mathbf{X}
defined on the infinite volume
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We view
\mathbf{X}
as a locally finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is the set
\mathbf{X}_{B}:=\mathbf{X}\cap B
is finite almost surely for any bounded Borel set
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This means also that the number of points
N(B)=|\mathbf{X}_{B}|
is finite almost surely. We denote by
{N}_{lf}
, the space of locally finite configurations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is

{N}_{lf}=\{\mathbf{x},|\mathbf{x}_{B}|<\infty\;\mbox{for any bounded domain}\;B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

The reader interested by a deep presentation of GPPs is referred to
[
28
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
51
]
and
[
38
]
. We consider particularly the class of stationary and isotropic pairwise interaction point processes (PIPP) where the Papangelou conditional intensity is given by

\displaystyle\lambda(u,\mathbf{x})=\beta\prod_{v\in\mathbf{x}}\phi(\|v-u\|)

(2.1)

where
\beta>0
represents the activity parameter and
\phi
is a real-valued interaction function. We are often interested in the log-linear form of the Papangelou conditional intensity which writes

\displaystyle\log\lambda(u,\mathbf{x})=\theta_{0}+\sum_{v\in\mathbf{x}}g(\|v-u\|)

(2.2)

where
\theta_{0}:=\log(\beta)
and
g:=\log(\phi)
.
For a configuration of points
\mathbf{x}
and a location
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the quantity
\lambda(u,\mathbf{x})
can be viewed as the conditional probability to have a point in an infinitesimal ball around
u
given the configuration elsewhere is
\mathbf{x}
.

The GNZ (for Georgii, Nguyen and Zessin) formula
[
54
,
38
,
39
, see]
is a way of characterizing the GPP and also highlights the interest of the Papangelou conditional intensity. It states that, for any measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{N}_{lf}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that the following expectations, which are denoted by
\mathbb{E}
and defined with respect to the distribution of
\mathbf{X}
, are finite

\mathbb{E}\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}}h(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus\{u\})=\mathbb{E}\int h(u,\mathbf{X})\lambda(u,\mathbf{X})\mathrm{d}u.

(2.3)

Throughout the paper, we will often use the following three properties for a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{N}_{lf}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

1.

f
is translation invariant, if

f(u,\mathbf{x})=f(\mathrm{0},\uptau_{u}\mathbf{x})

(TI)

where
\uptau_{u}\mathbf{x}=\{v-u:v\in\mathbf{x}\}
is the translation of the locations of
\mathbf{x}
by the vector
-u
.

2.

f
has finite interaction range
\tilde{R}>0
, if

f(u,\mathbf{x})=f(u,\mathbf{x}\cap B(u,\tilde{R}))

(FR)

where
B(u,\tilde{R})
is the euclidean ball centered at
u
with radius
\tilde{R}
.

3.

f
is locally stable, if there exists
\bar{f}<\infty
such that

f(u,\mathbf{x})\leq\bar{f}.

(LS)

When the Papangelou conditional intensity plays the role of the function
f
, the finite range property (
FR
) means that the probability to insert a point
u
in
\mathbf{x}
depends only on the
\tilde{R}
-neighbors of
u
to
\mathbf{x}
. The local stability (
LS
) means that the GPP is stochastically dominated by a Poisson point process. And the translation invariance property (
TI
) leads to the stationarity of the point process. The following proposition proved by
[
11
]
is central to this paper as it provides sufficient conditions to ensure the existence of at least one stationary GPP on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proposition 0
.

Let
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{N}_{lf}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a function of the form (
2.1
) satisfying (
TI
), (
FR
) and (
LS
). Then there exists at least one infinite volume stationary Gibbs measure, that is there exists at least one stationary Gibbs model
\mathbf{X}
with Papangelou conditional intensity
\lambda
which in particular satisfies the GNZ formula (
2.3
).

More specifically, for pairwise interaction point processes, we have the following useful result.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{N}_{lf}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
given by (
2.1
)-(
2.2
). Assume

(a)

either
g(r)=-\infty
(i.e.
\phi(r)=0
) for
r\in[0,\delta]
(with
\delta>0
) and
g(r)=0
(i.e.
\phi(r)=1
) for
r\geq\tilde{R}
for some
0<\tilde{R}<\infty
.

(b)

or
g(r)\geq 0
(i.e.
\phi(r)\leq 1
) for
r\in[0,\tilde{R}]
and
g(r)=0
(i.e.
\phi(r)=1
) for
r\geq\tilde{R}
for some
0<\tilde{R}<\infty
;

Then, the corresponding Papangelou conditional intensity satisfies (
TI
), (
FR
) and (

) and thus Proposition

In the situation (b),

is non-negative which necessarily yields a repulsive PIPP. In the situation (a), assuming

for

means that two points at distance smaller than

and the references therein. The hard core condition at small scales enables

Assumptions considered in Corollary

Methodology

3.1
Orthogonal series estimation

We assume that
g
satisfies assumptions of Corollary
1
. Our goal is to estimate
g
on the interval
[\delta,R+\delta]
.To this end, we decompose
g(\cdot+\delta)
on a basis of orthonormal polynomials on
[0,R]
.
Orthonormal polynomials defined on
[0,R]
are classically built from an inner product of the form
<a,b>_{w}=\int_{0}^{R}a(r)b(r)w(r)\mathrm{d}r
, where
w
is a non-negative continuous weight function,
[
67
, see e.g.]
.

We denote by
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
the orthonormal basis of functions on
[0,R]
with respect to some weight function
w
, thus satisfying
\int_{0}^{R}\varphi_{k}(r)\varphi_{l}(r)w(r)\mathrm{d}r=\mathbf{1}(k=l)
.
Let
L^{2}_{w}([0,R])
be the set of squared integrable functions on
[0,R]
with respect to the inner product
<\cdot,\cdot>_{w}
.
Let us cite a few examples of orthonormal polynomials.
The first one is the cosine basis
\varphi_{1}(r)=1/\surd{R}
,
\varphi_{k}(r)=\sqrt{2/R}\cos\{(k-1)\pi r/R\},\,k\geq 2
with
w(r)=1
. The second one is the Haar system, defined by
\varphi_{1}(t)=1/\sqrt{R}
and where for
k=2^{m}+\ell
with
\ell=1,\dots,2^{m}
and

\varphi_{k}(r)=\frac{\sqrt{2^{m}}\tilde{\varphi}_{k}(r/R)}{\sqrt{R}}\text{ where }\tilde{\varphi}_{k}(r)=\mathbf{1}\left(r\in I_{2k-2}\right)-\mathbf{1}\left(r\in I_{2k-1}\right)

with
I_{k}=[k/2^{m+1},(k+1)/2^{m+1}]
and
w(r)=1
. The third one with
w(r)=r^{d-1}
is the Fourier-Bessel basis given by

\varphi_{k}(r)=\frac{2^{1/2}}{RJ_{\nu+1}(\alpha_{\nu,k})}\,J_{\nu}(r\alpha_{\nu,k}/R)r^{-\nu},\,k\geq 1,

where
\nu=(d-2)/2
,
J_{\nu}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
\nu
and
\{\alpha_{\nu,k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is the sequence of successive positive roots of
J_{\nu}(r)
. Other examples of orthonormal polynomials include for example Legendre polynomials, Jacobi polynomials, Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kind, Gegenbauer polynomials, etc.

Let
g\in L^{2}_{w}([\delta,R+\delta])
. Its orthogonal series expansion writes for any
r\in[\delta,R+\delta]
.

\displaystyle g(r)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\theta_{k}\varphi_{k}(r-\delta).

(3.1)

The coefficients
\theta_{k}
in the expansion are therefore given by
\theta_{k}=\int_{0}^{R}g(r+\delta)\varphi_{k}(r)w(r)\mathrm{d}r
and satisfy
\sum_{k\geq 1}\theta_{k}^{2}<\infty
. Now, with this series expansion of the logarithm of the pairwise interaction function, we rewrite equation
(\ref{condint})
as follows:

\displaystyle\log\lambda(u,\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\theta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\theta_{k}S_{k}(u,\mathbf{x})

(3.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}:=\sum_{k\geq 0}y_{k}^{2}<\infty\}
and
S_{k}(u,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{k}(\|v-u\|-\delta)
. After truncating the above series to a fixed
K
, Equation
(\ref{condintfty})
becomes

\displaystyle\log\lambda(u,\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{K}(u,\mathbf{x})

(3.3)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1}
and
\mathbf{S}_{K}(u,\mathbf{x})=(1,S_{1}(u,\mathbf{x}),\ldots,S_{K}(u,\mathbf{x}))^{\top}
. We stress on the fact that
\lambda
, as defined in
(\ref{condintK})
, does not correspond to the conditional intensity of the spatial Gibbs point process
\mathbf{X}
. However, the interest is that this truncation brings back the model to an exponential family model. Therefore, standard methods including the pseudolikelihood function can be used to estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}
. We define

\displaystyle\mathrm{LPL}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})=\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}\cap W}\log\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})-\int_{W}\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})\mathrm{d}u.

(3.4)

Note that taking
K=\infty
in (
3.4
) precisely leads to the log-pseudolikelihood function of the point process
\mathbf{X}
. In the present paper, the function
\mathrm{LPL}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
will be referred to as the truncated log-pseudolikelihood of the point process
\mathbf{X}
and its derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}
is given by

\boldsymbol{e}_{K}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})=\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}\cap W}\mathbf{S}_{K}(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus u)-\int_{W}\mathbf{S}_{K}(u,\mathbf{X})\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})\mathrm{d}u.

The estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}
is obtained by maximizing the function
\mathrm{LPL}
or equivalently by solving
\boldsymbol{e}_{K}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})=0
.
The maximization of (
3.4
) is fast and stable, mainly since
\mathrm{LPL}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
turns out to be a concave function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}
[
50
, see]
.
We can even say more by slightly anticipating on Remark
1
: for
n
large enough (that is when the amount of data increases) the sensitivity matrix derived from the score
\boldsymbol{e}_{K}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
is positive definite ensuring that the maximum is indeed unique. From a computational point of view,
[
6
]
show that (
3.4
) can be approximated by a quasi-Poisson regression. The methodology can be quickly and efficiently implemented using standard software for generalized linear models. We did not consider other composite likelihoods such as the logistic one
[
2
]
or other estimating functions. Indeed, on the one hand, the logistic regression likelihood can be viewed as an approximation of the pseudolikelihood which can solve some numerical problems of the pseudolikelihood when discretizing the integral term in (
3.4
) is problematic. We did not encounter such an issue in the problem considered in this paper.
On the other hand,
[
21
]
investigated the question of optimal estimating functions for parametric stationary Gibbs point processes and showed that the procedure is more computationally intensive, less stable numerically than the pseudolikelihood for a little gain in terms of mean squared error.

Let us add two more comments. First, the estimation procedure does not produce an explicit estimator of the coefficients
\theta_{k}
as in
[
48
]
since
\mathrm{LPL}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
is a nonlinear function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}
. Second, from GNZ formula (
2.3
),
\boldsymbol{e}_{K}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
is not necessarily an unbiased estimating function since its expectation does not vanish under the true underlying Gibbs model. This would be true if
K=\infty
or if
g
has a finite series expression with
K^{\star}
orthonormal functions such that
K^{\star}\leq K
.

An estimator of
g
is then obtained by replacing
\theta_{k}
by
\hat{\theta}_{k}
in (
3.1
) and by truncating the infinite sum up to some integer
K\geq 1

\displaystyle\hat{g}(r;K)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\theta}_{k}\varphi_{k}(r-\delta).

(3.5)

A more general form could be given by

\displaystyle\hat{g}(r;b)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}\hat{\theta}_{k}\varphi_{k}(r-\delta)

(3.6)

where
\tau=\{\tau_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a smoothing/truncation scheme, see for e.g.
[
69
]
. To ease the presentation we do not consider this possibility in this paper and focus on the simplest one that is
\tau_{k}=\mathbf{1}(k\leq K)
. Finally, an estimator of the pairwise interaction function
\phi
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(r;K)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\theta}_{k}\varphi_{k}(r-\delta)\right),\quad r\in[\delta,R+\delta].

(3.7)

Using the orthonormality of the basis function, the integrated squared error (ISE) takes the simple form

\displaystyle\mathrm{ISE}(\hat{g}(\cdot\,;K))

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{R+\delta}\{\hat{g}(r;K)-g(r)\big\}^{2}w(r-\delta)\mathrm{d}r=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})^{2}+\sum_{k>K}\theta_{k}^{2}.

(3.8)

4
Asymptotic properties

Let
K_{n}
be a sequence of integers such that
K_{n}\,\to\,\infty
as
n\,\to\,\infty
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}_{K_{n}}=(\theta_{k}^{\star})_{k=0,\ldots,K_{n}}
the
(K_{n}+1)
-dimensional vector of true coefficients, i.e. the first
K_{n}+1
elements of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}=(\theta_{k}^{\star})_{k=0,\ldots,\infty}
. Generally, we let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{k})_{k=0,\ldots,\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}=(\theta_{k})_{k=0,\ldots,K_{n}}
. When there is no ambiguity,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}
will also denote the sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\theta_{k}=0
for all
k\geq K_{n}+1
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{LPL}_{n}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})=\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}\cap W_{n}}\log\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})-\int_{W_{n}}\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})\mathrm{d}u

(4.1)

be the truncated log-pseudolikelihood function of
\mathbf{X}
observed on
W_{n}
. Its derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}
equals

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})=\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}\cap W_{n}}\mathbf{S}_{K_{n}}(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus u)-\int_{W_{n}}\mathbf{S}_{K_{n}}(u,\mathbf{X})\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})\mathrm{d}u.

(4.2)

We let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K_{n}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}{\text{argmax}}\,\mathrm{LPL}_{n}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})
where the set
\mathcal{S}_{m}
will be made more precise in
3
.
The estimator
\hat{g}_{n}
of
g
obtained from
\mathbf{X}
observed on
W_{n}
is given, for any
r\in[\delta,R+\delta]
, by

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{n}(r;K_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\hat{\theta}_{k,n}\,\varphi_{k}(r-\delta).

(4.3)

We define the integrated squared error (ISE) of
\hat{g}_{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{ISE}(\hat{g}_{n}(\cdot\,;K_{n}))=\left\|\hat{g}_{n}-g\right\|^{2}_{\text{L}^{2}_{w}([\delta,R+\delta])}=\int_{0}^{R}\big\{\hat{g}_{n}(r+\delta;K_{n})-g(r+\delta)\big\}^{2}w(r)\mathrm{d}r.

(4.4)

Using these notation for instance
\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
corresponds to (
4.2
) where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}
is replaced by
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
. And it is to be pointed out that
\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
is an innovation type statistic
[
7
,
24
, see]
and in particular
\mathbb{E}\,[\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})]=0
from the GNZ formula.

4.1
Conditions

For
\mathbf{y},\,\mathbf{z}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, we let
\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{z}=\sum_{k\geq 0}y_{k}\,z_{k}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Denote for
\gamma\geq 0
and
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
by
\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\gamma}=|y_{0}|+\sum_{k\geq 1}|k|^{\gamma}|y_{k}|
and we let
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}:\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\gamma}<\infty\}
.
We define the following stochastic infinite matrices for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle{\int_{W_{n}}\mathbf{S}(u,\mathbf{X})\mathbf{S}(u,\mathbf{X})^{\top}\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}u},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle{\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\hskip-7.11317pt\mathbf{S}(u,\mathbf{X})\mathbf{S}(v,\mathbf{X})^{\top}\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\lambda(v,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\left(1-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\|v-u\|)\right)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u}

\displaystyle+{\int_{W_{n}}\int_{W_{n}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\|v-u\|-\delta)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\|v-u\|-\delta)^{\top}\lambda(\{u,v\},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u}

where
\mathbf{S}(u,\mathbf{X})=(1,S_{1}(u,\mathbf{X}),S_{2}(u,\mathbf{X}),\dots)^{\top}
, where for
r\in(\delta,R+\delta)
,

\log\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(r)=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}_{-0}\boldsymbol{\varphi}(r-\delta)
with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-0}=(\theta_{1},\dots)^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(r)=(\varphi_{1}(r),\dots)^{\top}
and finally where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(r-\delta)=(0,\varphi_{1}(r),\dots)^{\top}
. Note that in particular
\phi(\cdot)=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}(\cdot)
.

For a stochastic matrix
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})
we use the notation
\mathbf{M}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)_{i,j=0,\dots,K_{n}}
,
\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]
,
\mathbf{M}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)_{i,j=0,\dots,K_{n}}
and
\widebar{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=|W_{n}|^{-1}\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

We may check using the GNZ formula and
[
24
, Lemma 3.1]
that first
\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, whence
\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})]
is the sensitivity matrix and second that
\mathrm{Var}\left[\boldsymbol{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})\right]=\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
.

We are now able to describe the general assumptions required by our results.

1.

(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an increasing sequence of regular convex compact sets, such that
W_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
n\to\infty
and there exists
p\geq 1
such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\frac{|W_{n}\setminus W_{n}^{-}|}{|W_{n}|}\right)^{p}<\infty
where
W_{n}^{-}=W_{n}\ominus(R+\delta)
is the domain
W_{n}
eroded by
R+\delta
.

2.

We assume that the Papangelou conditional intensity function has the log-linear specification given by (
2.2
) and that
g\in L^{2}_{w}([\delta,R+\delta])
satisfies the assumptions of Corollary
1
.

3.

We assume (i)
K_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
; (ii) there exists
m\geq 0
such that
\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}=O(k^{m})
; (iii)
\sup_{r\in[0,R]}(r+\delta)^{d-1}/w(r)<\infty
; (iv) for any
n\geq 1
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K_{n}},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
.

4.

We assume (i)
\sup_{r\in[0,R]}w(r)<\infty
; (ii)
\psi=1+|\sigma_{d}|\,e^{\theta_{0}^{\star}}{\int_{\delta}^{R+\delta}(1-\phi(r))\,r^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}>0
where
|\sigma_{d}|
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; (iii) the set
[0,R]\setminus\Delta
has zero Lebesgue measure where
\Delta=\{r\in[0,R]:(r+\delta)^{d-1}\phi(r+\delta)/w(r)>0\}
.

Using the finite range property and the stationarity of
\mathbf{X}
, we refer the reader to
[
24
]
where it is proved under conditions
1
-
2
that

\displaystyle\widebar{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X})\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X})^{\top}\lambda(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]

\displaystyle\widebar{\mathbf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\int_{\mathcal{B}(0)}\hskip-7.11317pt\mathbf{S}(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X})\mathbf{S}(v,\mathbf{X})^{\top}\lambda(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\lambda(v,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\left(1-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\|v-u\|)\right)}\mathrm{d}v\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}\left[{\int_{\mathcal{B}(0)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\|v\|-\delta)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\|v\|-\delta)^{\top}\lambda(\{\mathrm{0},v\},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}v}\right]

where to ease notation
\mathcal{B}(u)
stands for the ball (or annulus depending on the value of
\delta
)

\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\delta\leq\|v-u\|\leq R+\delta\right\}
.
To get the exact expression of
\widebar{\mathbf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we actually require that
\mathcal{B}(0)\subset W_{n}
, which is always true for
n
large enough.

Condition
1
specifies our asymptotic framework. If for instance,
W_{n}=[-(n/2)^{1/d},(n/2)^{1/d}]^{d}
, then
|W_{n}|=n
and
|W_{n}\setminus W_{n}^{-}|=O(n^{1-1/d})
so the last part of
1
is satisfied by choosing any
p>d
. Condition
2
in particular ensures the existence of a stationary Gibbs measure.

Let us discuss Condition
3
. The requirement that
K_{n}\to\infty
and the existence of
m
are natural. Note that
m
is equal to 0 for the cosine basis, to
1/2
for the Haar one and to
(d-1)/2
(when
d\geq 2
) for the Fourier-Bessel one
[
48
, Section S5 in the supplementary material]
). (iii) is satisfied for the Haar and cosine basis for every
\delta>0
since
w(r)=1
and for the Fourier-Bessel one (
w(r)=r^{d-1}
) if
\delta=0
. This assumption is actually used to show that
\int_{0}^{R}\varphi_{k}(r)^{2}\mathrm{d}r=O(1)
(used in Lemma
1
). If (iii) is not valid (e.g. the Fourier-Bessel basis with
\delta>0
), we can only show that
\int_{0}^{r}\varphi_{k}(r)^{2}\mathrm{d}r=O(k^{m})
. Theorem
1
remains true but with different rates of convergence (see Section
E
in the Supplementary Material for a treatment of this case). By assuming
3
, we implicitly assume that
g
is lower and upper-bounded, which means that
\phi(r)
is positive and upper-bounded for any
r\in[\delta,R+\delta]
(this is the reason why we slightly modify the Diggle-Gratton pairwise interaction function, for which
\delta=0
, in such a way that
\phi(0)=0.05>0
).
To continue the discussion of Condition
3
, let us define the sequence

a_{K_{n}}=\sum_{k>K_{n}}k^{m}|\theta^{\star}_{k}|.

(4.5)

As already noticed by
[
42
]
in the simpler context of orthogonal series density estimator, the rate at which the coefficients
\theta_{k}^{\star}
converge to 0 determine the rate of convergence of density estimators. A similar behaviour is observed in our context since condition
3
implies that
a_{K_{n}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Even more, we will show in Theorem
1
that the consistency of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K_{n}}
, the convergence of the ISE, etc strongly depend on the rate of convergence of
a_{K_{n}}
to 0. We continue this discussion after the presentation of Theorem
1
.

As seen in Lemma
3
(ii), Condition
4
is used to ensure that the infinite matrix
\widebar{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\widebar{\mathbf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
is positive definite, that is, from Lemma
2
, to ensure that the normalized variance
|W_{n}|^{-1}\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})]=|W_{n}|^{-1}(\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}))
is positive definite for
n
sufficiently large. The condition on
\psi
depends only on the model. It is trivially satisfied when
\phi\leq 1
, i.e. for purely repulsive PIPP (in particular for PIF1 and PIF2). We have checked numerically that this condition is satisfied for the two other examples PIF3 and PIF4 where
\phi
can exceed 1. The condition related to
\Delta
in
4
depends on the basis function. Remember that
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
necessarily implies that
\phi(\cdot)>0
. So for the Fourier-Bessel basis for which
w(r)=r^{d-1}
, the condition is always satisfied. For the two other considered bases, for which
w(r)=1
, the condition is obvious when
\delta>0
and is also easily satisfied when
\delta=0
since
r^{d-1}\phi(r)
vanishes only at
r=0
.

Remark 1
.

As partly mentioned in the previous paragraph, Lemma
3
(i)-(ii) ensures that the infinite matrices
\widebar{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\widebar{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\widebar{\mathbf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
are positive definite. Those matrices play an important role in our asymptotic results as they correspond to some limit of the normalized sensitivity matrix and variance covariance matrix of the estimating equation
\mathbf{e}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\cdot)
. Even if this looks hidden in the proof of Lemma
3
, assuming that the basis of functions
(\varphi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is orthonormal is crucial here.

4.2
Main results

For a random vector
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and a sequence of real numbers
x_{n}
, the notation
\mathbf{X}_{n}=O_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{n})
or
\mathbf{X}_{n}=o_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{n})
means that
\|\mathbf{X}_{n}\|=O_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{n})
and
\|\mathbf{X}_{n}\|=o_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{n})
. In the same way, the notation
\mathbf{V}_{n}=O(x_{n})
and
\mathbf{M}_{n}=O(x_{n})
mean that
\|\mathbf{V}_{n}\|=O(x_{n})
and
\|\mathbf{M}_{n}\|=O(x_{n})
for a vector
\mathbf{V}_{n}
and a squared matrix
\mathbf{M}_{n}
.

As detailed in the previous section, the nonparametric estimate of
g
is based on the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star}
, the first
K_{n}
coefficients of its expansion on the basis of functions
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k}
, which is done by minimizing the estimating equation (
4.2
). Proposition
1
presented in the Supplementary Material provides conditions on the sequences
a_{K_{n}}
,
K_{n}
and
|W_{n}|
to get a control in probability of the estimating equation (
4.2
) evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}_{K_{n}}
.

The estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star}
, obtained as a minimization, is not explicit. As such, its bias and variance are hard to determine. Its rate of convergence in probability can nevertheless be derived from the one of the estimating equation (
4.2
). Then, the integrated squared error and the asymptotic normality of
\hat{g}_{n}(r;K_{n})
can be deduced. These results are summarized by Theorem
1
. The pointwise convergence of
\hat{g}_{n}(r;K_{n})
and a multivariate extension of Theorem
1
(ii) are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Theorem 1
.

Assume the conditions
1
-
3
hold.

1.

As
n\to\infty
, asume that
a_{K_{n}}K_{n}^{1/2}\to 0
and that
K_{n}^{m\vee 1}/|W_{n}|\to 0
. Then, there exists a maximizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K_{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
of
\,\mathrm{LPL}_{n}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}})
such that

\displaystyle{\|{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{K_{n}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star}\|}=O_{\mathrm{P}}\left({\frac{K_{n}^{(m\vee 1)/2}}{|W_{n}|^{1/2}}}+a_{K_{n}}{K_{n}}^{1/2}\right)

(4.6)

and

\mathrm{ISE}(\hat{g}_{n}(\cdot\,;K_{n}))=O_{\mathrm{P}}\left({\frac{K_{n}^{m\vee 1}}{|W_{n}|}}+a_{K_{n}}^{2}{K_{n}}\right).

2.

As
n\to\infty
, asume that
a_{K_{n}}K_{n}^{1/2}\to 0
,
a_{K_{n}}^{2}K_{n}^{m+3}|W_{n}|^{-1/2}
and that
K_{n}^{2/3(m+m\vee 1+2)}|W_{n}|^{-1}\to 0
. Then, for
r\in(\delta,R+\delta)

\displaystyle s_{n}^{-1}(r)\left\{\hat{g}_{n}(r;K_{n})-g(r)\right\}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

where

\displaystyle s_{n}^{2}(r)=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{K_{n}}^{\top}(r-\delta)\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{K_{n}}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{K_{n}}(r-\delta),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{K_{n}}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star})\,\left\{\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})\right\}\,\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star}).

(4.7)

Theorem
1
sheds the light on the trade-off which must be found on the sequences
K_{n}
and
|W_{n}|
and the assumption on the underlying model through the rate of convergence of
a_{K_{n}}\to 0
which implicitly imposes some conditions on
g
. However, the smoothness of
g
cannot be directly related to the decrease of the partial sum
\sum_{k>K_{n}}k^{m}|\theta_{k}^{\star}|
. To discuss more this, let us assume that
\theta^{\star}_{k}=O(k^{-1-\varepsilon-\gamma})
for some
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma>m
which implies that
a_{K_{n}}=o(K_{n}^{m-\gamma})
. Let us also introduce the space
\mathcal{H}^{s}:=\{f\in L^{2}_{w}([\delta,R+\delta]):\sum_{k}(1+k)^{2s}|{\theta^{\star}_{k}}|^{2}<\infty\}
, which for the the cosine basis, corresponds to the Sobolev space with regularity
s
. Note that
\theta_{k}^{\star}=O(k^{-1-\varepsilon-\gamma})
implies that
g\in\mathcal{H}^{\gamma+1/2}
(actually to any
\mathcal{H}^{s}
for any
s\leq\gamma+1/2
). Using this “regularity” space, assuming that
m\leq 1
and focusing on the convergence of the ISE to simplify the discussion, we see that the ISE tends to 0 if
\gamma\geq m+1/2
which would imply that
g\in\mathcal{H}^{m+1}
. On the much simpler problem of orthogonal density estimators with iid random variables,
[
42
]
for instance, shows that the MISE is consistent as soon as the true density is squared integrable. We attribute our more restrictive assumptions to the strong nonlinearity of the current problem (we cannot estimate
\theta_{k}^{\star}
without estimating all other coefficients) and to the complex dependence characteristic of the Gibbs point process.

To continue the discussion, if one assumes in addition that
K_{n}=|W_{n}|^{\alpha}
(with
\alpha>0
), the ISE tends to zero if
0<\alpha<1
with optimal rate proportional to
|W_{n}|^{-r}
with
r=(\gamma-m+1/2)/(\gamma-m)
.

As already mentioned
\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star})
and
\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})+\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
can be seen as the sensitivity matrix and the variance covariance matrix of the score of the truncated pseudolikelihood. By using ergodic theorem
[
55
, see]
and the GNZ formula,
[
24
]
proposed fast and consistent estimates of such matrices for general Gibbs point processes. Applied to pairwise interaction point processes, the
(j,k)
-th elements
a_{jk}=|W_{n}|^{-1}(\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K_{n}}^{\star}))_{jk}
(or
|W_{n}|^{-1}(\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}))_{jk}
) and
b_{jk}=|W_{n}|^{-1}(\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}))_{jk}
can respectively be estimated by

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{jk}=

\displaystyle|W_{n}|^{-1}\sum_{u\in\mathbf{X}\cap W_{n}}S_{j}(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus u)S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus u)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{jk}=

\displaystyle|W_{n}|^{-1}{\sum_{u,v\in\mathbf{X}\cap W_{n}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\prime}\quad\Bigg\{S_{j}(u,\mathbf{X}\setminus\{u,v\})S_{k}(v,\mathbf{X}\setminus\{u,v\})\left(\frac{1}{\phi_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K_{n}}}(\|v-u\|)}-1\right)

\displaystyle+\varphi_{j}(\|v-u\|-\delta)\varphi_{k}(\|v-u\|-\delta)\Bigg\}

where to ease notation, the symbol
\sum^{\prime}
means that the sum acts on pairs of distinct points such that
\delta\leq\|v-u\|\leq R+\delta
. These estimates depend only on data points and do not require any integrals discretization which explains their quick implementation within the function
vcov.ppm
from the
spatstat R
package. Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{K_{n}}(r-\delta)
depends only on the basis system, estimates of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{K_{n}}
and finally
s_{n}(r)
follow easily.

By Theorem
1
(ii), an approximate
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
g(r)
is given by
\hat{g}_{n}(r;K_{n})\mp z_{\alpha/2}\hat{s}_{n}(r)
where
r\in(\delta,R+\delta)
and
z_{\alpha/2}
is the quantile of order
(1-\alpha/2)
of a standard normal distribution. A pointwise confidence interval for
\phi(r)
is easily deduced.

5
Implementation and simulation study

5.1
Full description of the methodology

Given an orthonormal basis function,
\delta
,
R
and a number of polynomials
K
, the problem of estimating
g
(and thus
\phi
) is easily implemented since we bring back the estimation of the coefficients
\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{K}
to the estimation of an exponential family Gibbs model. The estimation is done using the pseudolikelihood which is implemented in the
ppm
function of the
spatstat

R
package. This function takes advantage of the analogy between the pseudolikelihood and the quasi-Poisson regression
[
6
]
.

We consider the estimation of
g
on
[\delta,R+\delta]
using an expansion over a basis of functions on
L^{2}_{w}([0,R])
, with
\delta\geq 0
and
R<\infty
. We remind that to encompass cases (a)-(b) of Corollary
1
(see Remarks after Corollary
1
) we abuse notation naming
\delta\geq 0
as the hard-core parameter:
\delta=0
means no hard-core while a positive value means that two points at distance smaller than
\delta
do not interact. Whatever the value of
\delta
, the parameter
R+\delta
is related to the range of the GPP. Two points at distance larger than
R+\delta
interact in the same way than under a homogenous Poisson point process. If these parameters are unknown, which is most often the case for real datasets, we either assume we have at our disposal a lower-bound and upper-bound for these irregular parameters or we use a standard approach to estimate the hyperparameters
\delta
and
R
. In this situation, we estimate
\delta
by
\hat{\delta}=n/(n+1)d_{\min}
, where
d_{\min}
is the observed smallest distance between two points. This estimate is the
spatstat
version (known to be less biased) of the estimate proposed by
[
60
]
. To estimate
R+\delta
, we can use the profile pseudolikelihood (by setting first a reasonable flexible model) or take the value of
r
such that the maximal deviation between the empirical Ripley’s
K
-function and its expected value under the homogeneous Poisson process is observed. Both approaches are described in
[
5
, p. 518]
. In the simulation study, we have first assumed that those two parameters are known and given by their values. We also show on a smaller example that misspecifying or estimating
\delta
and
R
does not deteriorate the efficiency of the proposed methodology.

Let us now consider the crucial problem of selecting the number of polynomials
K
.
In the context of nonparametric estimation of the pair correlation function,
[
48
]
and
[
18
]
suggest to select it by minimizing an estimate of the MISE or by maximizing a composite likelihood cross-validation criterion respectively. We were unable to mimic these approaches in the context of the present paper, in particular because we do not have an explicit expression (nor asymptotic equivalent) of the bias and the variance of
\hat{g}(r;K)
. Instead, we propose to adapt an information criterion approach. Information criteria for composite likelihood were first introduced by
[
36
]
and were then extended to spatial point processes by
[
26
,
17
,
1
]
. Applied to the truncated pseudolikelihood, this criterion writes

\mathrm{cAIC}(K,\mathbf{X})=-2\,\mathrm{LPL}(\mathbf{X};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K})+2\,\text{trace}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{K}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{K})

(5.1)

where
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{K}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{K}
are estimates of
\mathbf{A}_{K}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}^{\star})
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{K}
given by (
4.7
). As already mentioned in the previous section, both estimates and thus the trace are easily implemented in particular in
R
and can be obtained using the function
vcov.ppm
from the
spatstat
package.

Note that the trace term in (
5.1
) comes from the fact that the pseudolikelihood is only a composite likelihood and not a likelihood. If we were dealing with an inhomogeneous Poisson point processes (
\phi=1
with
\beta=\beta(u)
modelled through say
p
covariates for instance), the pseudolikelihood would be a likelihood, the trace term
p
and the criterion
\mathrm{cAIC}
the standard Akaike’s information criterion.

Finally, we suggest to select
K
as

\hat{K}^{\star}_{\mathrm{cAIC}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{1\leq K\leq K_{\max}}\mathrm{cAIC}(K,\mathbf{X})

yielding a data-driven procedure. Note that we ‘view’
\hat{K}^{\star}_{\mathrm{cAIC}}
as an estimate of
K^{\star}_{\mathrm{cAIC}}=\mathrm{argmin}_{K}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cAIC}(K,\mathbf{X})]
and we choose
K_{\max}=15
in the simulation study and the real datasets analysis. The whole procedure is summarized in Algorithm

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop an orthogonal series estimation of the log of the pairwise interaction function. The coefficients involved in the expansion of the function are obtained by maximizing a modified version of the pseudolikelihood function of the GPP. We propose to use the composite Akaike information criterion for the selection of the smoothing parameter. The implementation of the estimation procedure is quite simple thanks to an analogy with the pseudolikelihood for exponential family models. Our large simulation study confirms our results and shows the practical efficiency for large classes of pairwise interaction functions and for different orthonormal basis functions. These simulation results also confirm the use of the composite AIC criterion we suggest as a data-driven procedure to select the number of orthonormal polynomials used in the series expansion. Finally, we apply our methodology to three real datasets. The results provide a fine description of the interaction between the points for each dataset.

The use of orthonormal bases seems to be crucial to verify Lemmas
2
-
3
which essentially show that the sensitivity matrix and variance covariance matrix of the score function are definite positive for
n
large enough. Even if several orthonormal bases could be used in practice, we have not explored optimality criteria for the choice of basis system. In the same vein, we do not know if our results remain valid for expansions not based on orthonormal basis. Investigating such an extension is an interesting perspective. There are a few other directions that are worth exploring in the future. First, it would be interesting from a practical point of view to extend the method to pairwise interaction point process with conditional intensity of the form
\lambda(u,\mathbf{x})=\beta(u)\prod_{v\in\mathbf{x}}\phi(\|v-u\|)
where
\beta(u)
is modelled through say
p
spatial covariates for instance or is also non parametrically estimated. Second, as an alternative of the data-driven selection of the tuning parameter
K
, we could consider a regularized version of the truncated pseudolikelihood (with a penalty term such as lasso, fused lasso, etc). Third, the methodology and the results are restricted to finite-range models. Relaxing this assumption seems quite straightforward from a methodological point of view. We could replace
\tilde{R}
by a sequence
\tilde{R}_{n}
which increases with the amount of data. Handling this extension from a theoretical point of view is however definitely challenging.

A
Application to real datasets

To illustrate the procedure on real datasets, we consider point patterns of locations of “light-on” displaced amacrine cells in the retina of a rabbit (
152
points observed in
[0,1060]\times[0,662]\;(\mu m^{2})

[0,200]\times[0,200](m^{2})

[-282,282]\times[-282,282]\;(\mu m^{2})

R
under the
spatstat
package. Hereafter, we refer respectively to these datasets as
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
. Note that in our analysis of
D_{2}
, we use the unmarked point pattern i.e. the three types of points are now of a single type. We omit the duplicated points of
D_{3}
. The estimates of
\delta
are
21
,
0.2
and
1.1
respectively. These values give upper bounds
R+\delta

120

20
and

for

,
D_{2}
and
D_{3}

1
with the Fourier-Bessel basis. The cut-offs
\hat{K}^{\star}=\hat{K}^{\star}_{\mathrm{cAIC}}
respectively equal to
2
,
6
and
3
for the three point patterns and we use the result in Theorem
1
(iii) to determine the
95\%
pointwise confidence envelopes for the pairwise interaction function
\phi
and its logarithm
g

1560
sec and
1080

g
and

Results are reported in Figure

D_{1}

It is worth repeating the advantages of our nonparametric approach when we compare it to Bayesian approaches: stability and speed of the algorithm (a few seconds to get the estimate and envelopes for this dataset

Lemma 1
.

Let
p,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 2
. Under conditions
2
-
3
, there exists
\kappa<\infty
such that

1.

\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\right]\leq\kappa^{p}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z^{p}]=O(k^{m(p-2)})

(B.1)

where
Z
is a Poisson random variable with mean 1.

2.

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{S}_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})\|^{p}\right]=O(K_{n}^{m(p-2)+p/2}).

3.

For
p=2,4

\mathbb{E}\left[|r_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\right]=O(a_{K_{n}}^{p})\quad\text{ where }\quad r_{K_{n}}(u,\mathbf{X})=1-\exp\left(-\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})\right).

(B.2)

(i) First, note that
|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\leq h(\mathbf{X})
where
h(\mathbf{X})=\big(\sum_{v\in\mathbf{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(0)}|\varphi_{k}|(\|v\|-\delta)\big)^{p}
. Since
\mathbf{x}\mapsto h(\mathbf{x})
is an increasing function (i.e.
h(\mathbf{x})\leq h(\mathbf{y})
for any
\mathbf{x}\subset\mathbf{y}
) and since
\mathbf{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(0)
is stochastically dominated by a Poisson point process with intensity
\rho=\bar{\lambda}|\mathcal{B}(0)|
by condition
2
(which ensures (
LS
)), we have (see e.g.
[
8
,
58
]
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\right]\leq\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\in\mathcal{I}(p)}K_{p}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}(0)}\varphi_{k}^{\ell}(\|v\|-\delta)\rho\mathrm{d}v\right)^{r_{\ell}}

where

\mathcal{I}(p)=\left\{(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}:\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\ell r_{\ell}=p\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad K_{p}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})=p!\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}\frac{1}{(\ell!)^{r_{\ell}}r_{\ell}!}.

Now, observe that using
3
(iii)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}(0)}\varphi_{k}^{\ell}(\|v\|-\delta)\rho\mathrm{d}v\right)^{r_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{R}\varphi_{k}^{\ell}(r)w(r)\frac{(r+\delta)^{d-1}}{w(r)}\rho\mathrm{d}r\right)^{r_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{r_{\ell}(\ell-2)\vee 0}\kappa^{r_{\ell}}

with
\kappa=\rho\sup_{r\in[0,R]}(r+\delta)^{d-1}/w(r)
. Therefore, since
\kappa^{\sum_{\ell}r_{\ell}}\leq\kappa^{p}
for any
(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\in\mathcal{I}(p)
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\right]\leq\,\kappa^{p}\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\in\mathcal{I}(p)}K_{p}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{f(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})}

with
f(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}(r_{\ell}(\ell-2))\vee 0
which rewrites as
f(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})=p-\tilde{f}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})
with
\tilde{f}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})=r_{1}+2\sum_{\ell=2}^{p}r_{\ell}
. Since
\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}r_{\ell}\geq 1
, if
r_{1}=0
then
\sum_{\ell=2}^{p}r_{\ell}\geq 1
and
\tilde{f}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\geq 2
. The same conclusion is also obviously true if
r_{1}\geq 2
. Finally if
r_{1}=1
, since
p>1
,
\sum_{\ell=2}^{p}r_{\ell}\neq 0
otherwise
\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\ell r_{\ell}=1\neq p
. Therefore, for any
(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\in\mathcal{I}(p),f(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})\leq p-2
whereby we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{p}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{p-2}\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\in\mathcal{I}(p)}K_{p}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{p}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{p-2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{p}]\quad\text{ where }Z\sim\mathcal{P}(1).

(ii) From Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{S}_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{2\alpha}\right)^{p/2\alpha}\left(K_{n}\right)^{p/2\beta}\quad\text{ with }1/\alpha+1/\beta=1

\displaystyle\leq K_{n}^{p/2-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}|S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})|^{p}\quad\text{ with }p=2\alpha.

Hence, from (i)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{S}_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})\|^{p}\right]=O\left(K_{n}^{p/2-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}k^{m(p-2)}\right)=O\left(K_{n}^{m(p-2)+p/2}\right).

(iii) Let
p=2
. We have using a series expansion of the exponential function

\displaystyle r_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\left\{\left(2\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})\right)^{j}-2\left(\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,\mathbf{X})\right)^{j}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{2^{j}}{j!}\left|\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}\right|^{j}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{(2a_{K_{n}})^{j}}{j!}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{j}

\displaystyle\leq 4a_{K_{n}}^{2}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{2}\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{1}{(j-2)!}(2N(\mathcal{B}(0)))^{j-2}

\displaystyle\leq 4a_{K_{n}}^{2}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{2}\exp(2N(\mathcal{B}(0))).

Thus,
\mathbb{E}(r_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})^{2})=O(a_{K_{n}}^{2})
from (
LS
) which implies that for any bounded
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{E}[N(A)^{p}\exp(cN(A))]<\infty
for any
p\geq 1,c>0
.

For the case
p=4
, we follow the same strategy and leave the reader to check that

\displaystyle r_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})^{4}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\left(-4+62^{j}-43^{j}+4^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,X)\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 4}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\left(-4+62^{j}-43^{j}+4^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k>K_{n}}\theta_{k}^{\star}S_{k}(0,X)\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\leq 12\sum_{j\geq 4}\frac{(4a_{K_{n}})^{j}}{j!}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{j}

\displaystyle\leq 3072\,a_{K_{n}}^{4}N(\mathcal{B}(0))^{4}\exp(4N(\mathcal{B}(0)))

which yields
\mathbb{E}[r_{K_{n}}(0,\mathbf{X})^{4}]=O(a_{K_{n}}^{4})
using again (
LS
).

Lemma 2
.

Under conditions
1
-
3
, the following results hold for any
\mathbf{y},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}

1.

|W_{n}|^{-1}\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mbox{a.s.}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\widebar{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}
,

2.

|W_{n}|^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mbox{a.s.}}0
,

3.

|W_{n}|^{-1}\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mbox{a.s.}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\widebar{\mathbf{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}
,

4.

|W_{n}|^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mbox{a.s.}}0
.

The proof uses extensively Theorem 4
[
55
]
, referred to as the ergodic theorem hereafter.

1.

Let

\displaystyle\alpha_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}={\int_{W_{n}}\left\{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}y_{k}\,S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})\right\}^{2}\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}u}.

We have that
({\alpha}_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda}
is additive and covariant
[
55
, see]
. Let
W_{0}=[0,1]^{d}
and
D\subset W_{0}
. Under the local stability ensured by
2
, we have

\displaystyle|{\alpha}_{D}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq{\int_{D}\bar{\lambda}\,\left\{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}|y_{k}|\,S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})\right\}^{2}\mathrm{d}u}.

Under the finite range property ensured by
2
, we have that almost surely for any
k\geq 1

\displaystyle S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})=\sum_{v\in\mathbf{X}\cap\mathcal{B}(u)}\varphi_{k}(\|v-u\|-\delta)\leq

\displaystyle\,N(\mathcal{B}(u))\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\,c\,k^{m}N(\mathcal{B}(u))

for some constant
c>0
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle|\bar{\alpha}_{D}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq

\displaystyle\,c{\int_{D}(1+N(\mathcal{B}(u)))^{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{m}^{2}\mathrm{d}u}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,c\|\mathbf{y}\|_{m}^{2}\,(1+N(W_{0}\oplus(R+\delta)))^{2}.

Since
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
and since
\mathbb{E}[N(A)^{2}]<\infty
for any bounded
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|{\alpha}_{D}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})|
is bounded by an integrable random variable uniform in D. We are therefore allowed to apply the ergodic theorem:
|W_{n}|^{-1}{\alpha}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}|W_{0}|^{-1}\mathbb{E}[{\alpha}_{W_{0}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})]
and from the stationarity of
\mathbf{X}
, this reduces to

|W_{n}|^{-1}{\alpha}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}y_{k}\,S_{k}(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X})\right\}^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]

for any
\mathbf{y},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
.

2.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{K_{n}}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}_{K_{n}}-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{y}
. Using algebraic identities and the local stability of
\mathbf{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{W_{n}}\left[\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{K_{n}}y_{k}S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})\right\}^{2}-\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}y_{k}S_{k}(u,\mathbf{X})\right\}^{2}\right]\lambda(u,\mathbf{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{\lambda}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{m}\,\sum_{k>K_{n}}k^{m}|y_{k}|\;\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{X})

where
\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{W_{n}}(\mathbf{X})=\int_{W_{n}}(1+N(\mathcal{B}(u)))^{2}\mathrm{d}u
. Using the same arguments as in (i), we can apply the ergodic and then use the stationarity of the process to prove that as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\Big\{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}(\mathbf{r};K_{n})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r})\Big\}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Finally, using Lemma 2.1 of
[
15
]
, we deduce that

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{K_{n}}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{r})\Big\{\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{n}(\mathbf{r};K_{n})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r})\Big\}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{0},\mathbf{I}_{d}).

∎
