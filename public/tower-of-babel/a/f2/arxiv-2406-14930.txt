An independence of the MIN from the PHP principle
Mykyta Narusevych

Abstract
The minimization principle MIN ​ ( ⊲ ) \textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft) , studied in bounded arithmetic in [ 4 ] , says that a strict linear ordering ⊲ \triangleleft on any finite interval [ 0 , … , n ) [0,\dots,n) has the least element. We prove that the relativized bounded arithmetic theory T 2 1 ​ ( ⊲ ) \textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft) augmented by pigeonhole-principle instances for all Δ 1 b ​ ( ⊲ ) \Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft) formulas does not prove MIN ​ ( ⊲ ) \textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft) .

Introduction

One of the central themes of propositional proof complexity is to understand the relative strength of proof systems and to prove strong lower bounds on proof length. Bounded arithmetic provides a uniform framework for studying such questions and often allows one to reduce proof-complexity lower bounds to logical independence results over corresponding arithmetic theories; see
[
10
, Section 8]
.

Motivating open problems for the present work include the
d
versus
d+1
problem
[
9
, Section 12.2]
and the
R(\log)
problem
[
10
, Problem 13.7.1]
. From the bounded arithmetic perspective, the former asks for separations between adjacent theories
\textsf{T}^{i+1}_{2}(\alpha)
and
\textsf{T}^{i}_{2}(\alpha)
by formulas of low complexity. The latter can be viewed as a particularly basic special case: it asks for a
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
formula provable in the full hierarchy
\textsf{T}_{2}(\alpha)
but not in the low fragment
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\alpha)
. See Section
2
for the relevant definitions. At the same time, the lowest fragment
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
is relatively well understood, especially due to the theorem of Riis
[
16
]
,
[
9
, Theorem 11.3.2]
. We believe that one possible route towards the above problems is to develop methods for proving independence from non-trivial extensions of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
.

The minimization principle was introduced in bounded arithmetic in
[
4
]
in the study of definable search problems and witnessing theorems. It is a
\Sigma^{b}_{2}
formula
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)
expressing that if
\triangleleft
is a strict linear ordering of the set
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, then it has a least element. It defines a total
\Sigma^{p}_{2}
search problem: either certify that
\triangleleft
is not a strict linear order, or find its least element.

The pigeonhole principle
PHP
has an especially rich history in bounded arithmetic and proof complexity; see, for example,
[
9
, Section 12.5]
,
[
10
, Section 15]
, and
[
1
]
. Given a binary relation
R
, it states that
R
cannot define the graph of an injective mapping from
\{0,\dots,n\}
into
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

In this paper, we study the extension of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
by instances of the pigeonhole principle for polynomial-time,
\triangleleft
-oracle computable graphs of functions, equivalently for graphs defined by
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas. We show that this theory does not prove the minimization principle for
\triangleleft
:

\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))\nvdash\forall n\,\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}.

Since
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)
is provable in
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\triangleleft)

[
4
]
, this gives a principle provable in
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\triangleleft)
but not in
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
. Conversely, the ordinary pigeonhole principle is not provable even in strong bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{T}_{2}(\alpha)

[
8
,
15
]
. Thus, in the relevant relativized setting, the extension
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
and the theory
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\triangleleft)
are incomparable in strength.

It is worth noting that if one replaces the pigeonhole principle by its weak version, the weak pigeonhole principle
[
9
, Section 11.2]
, the corresponding separation follows directly from
[
12
]
. There it is shown that, over
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
, no
strong
combinatorial principle, such as the minimization principle, follows from
weak
combinatorial principles, such as the weak pigeonhole principle.

Our primary technique is a model-theoretic construction reminiscent of
forcing
. Conditions are small pieces of the oracle relation, and the desired expansion is obtained by meeting countably many requirements. We present the construction as a game with three kinds of players. One player makes the oracle order falsify
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)
, one player preserves induction, and one player ensures all
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
pigeonhole-principle instances. The main technical point is that the pigeonhole-principle requirements can always be met. This is reduced to a combinatorial statement about arrays of partial oracle conditions.

We present two additional separations to further demonstrate how the same combinatorial method can be applied. Concretely, we show how to obtain models of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
plus the
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
pigeonhole principle that fail the dual weak pigeonhole principle and the tournament principle; see Section
5
for definitions.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we introduce the notions relevant to the paper: the relativized bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
, the minimization principle
MIN
, and the pigeonhole principle
PHP
.

In Section
3
, we outline a game-theoretic construction of a model of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
satisfying the
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
pigeonhole principle. We also briefly discuss how the construction can be viewed through the lens of forcing.

In Section
4
, we prove the main combinatorial theorems needed to finish the model construction. We also explain how the construction can be modified to produce a model with
\triangleleft
interpreted as a dense linear ordering.

In Section
5
, we sketch additional separations using the machinery developed in the previous sections. In particular, we discuss the tournament principle and the dual weak pigeonhole principle. Finally, we formulate an open problem related to the methods used in the paper.

Preliminaries

2.1
Bounded Arithmetic

In this subsection, we provide a brief exposition of bounded arithmetic. For further details, see
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
.

We study the relativized bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
, where
\triangleleft
is a binary relation symbol. In addition to
\triangleleft
, the language contains

•

constant symbols
0
and
1
,

•

unary operations
\lfloor\cdot/2\rfloor
and
|\cdot|
,

•

binary operations
+
,
\cdot
, and
\#
,

•

the binary relation
\leq
.

The intended meanings of
0,1,\lfloor\cdot/2\rfloor,+,\cdot,\leq
are standard. The symbol
|\cdot|
denotes bit-length, namely
|0|=0
and, for
x>0
,
|x|=\lfloor\log_{2}x\rfloor+1
. The symbol
\#
denotes the binary
smash function
, standardly defined as
2^{|x|\cdot|y|}
.

A particular theory in this language is
BASIC
; see
[
9
, Definition 5.2.1]
. This finite theory describes the basic properties of the involved symbols, such as commutativity of
+,\cdot
, and recursive properties of
\#
.

A
bounded formula
is a formula logically equivalent to one containing only bounded quantifiers, that is, quantifiers of the form
\forall x\leq t
or
\exists x\leq t
, where the term
t
does not contain
x
.

The class
\Delta^{b}_{0}(\triangleleft)
denotes sharply bounded formulas in the language with the additional relation symbol
\triangleleft
. Here sharply bounded means that all quantified variables are bounded by terms of the form
|t|
.

The class
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
is defined as the smallest class of bounded formulas extending
\Delta^{b}_{0}(\triangleleft)
and satisfying the following closure properties:

•

if
\varphi,\psi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
, then
\varphi\land\psi
and
\varphi\lor\psi
are in
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
;

•

if
\varphi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
and
t
is a term, then
\forall x\leq|t|\,\varphi
and
\exists x\leq|t|\,\varphi
are in
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
;

•

if
\varphi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
and
t
is a term, then
\exists x\leq t\,\varphi
is in
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
.

The class
\Pi^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
is the set of formulas
\varphi
such that
\neg\varphi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
.

For a formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
, let
\textsf{PIND}(\varphi)
denote the sentence

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\,\bigl[(\varphi(0,\overline{y})\land\forall x(\varphi(\lfloor x/2\rfloor,\overline{y})\to\varphi(x,\overline{y})))\to\forall x\,\varphi(x,\overline{y})\bigr].

As an axiom scheme, this is called
polynomial induction
. The usual induction scheme, in which the inductive step goes from
x
to
x+1
, is denoted by
IND
.

The theory
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
is
BASIC
augmented by polynomial induction for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas. The theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
extends
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
by the usual induction scheme for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas.

Finally,
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
denotes the class of formulas
\varphi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
for which there is a formula
\psi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
such that

\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)\vdash\varphi\leftrightarrow\neg\psi.

Note that the above formula class is often referred to as
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)

in

\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
to emphasize the base theory over which the equivalence is established.

Theorem 2.1

(Buss
[
3
]
; see also
[
9
, Section 7]
)
.

A relation
R(\overline{x})
on numbers is computable in polynomial time with
\triangleleft
-oracle access if and only if it is definable by a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formula
\varphi(\overline{x})
.

2.2
MIN
and
PHP
principles

We write
[0,\dots,n)
for the set
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

The following sentence
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}
expresses that, assuming
\triangleleft
defines a strict linear ordering on
[0,\dots,n)
, there exists a
\triangleleft
-least element of
[0,\dots,n)
:

\displaystyle\exists x<n\,(x\triangleleft x)

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x,y<n\,(x\neq y\land x\triangleleft y\land y\triangleleft x)

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x,y<n\,(x\neq y\land\neg(x\triangleleft y)\land\neg(y\triangleleft x))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x,y,z<n\,(x\triangleleft y\land y\triangleleft z\land\neg(x\triangleleft z))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x<n\,\forall y<n\,(x=y\lor x\triangleleft y).

This principle was introduced in bounded arithmetic in
[
4
]
, where it was shown that
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}
is provable in
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\triangleleft)
but is not provable in
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
.

Let
\varphi(x,y,\overline{z})
be a formula. We denote by
\textsf{PHP}(\varphi)
the pigeonhole principle for
\varphi
. It is the sentence

\forall\overline{z}\,\forall u,v\,(u>v\to\psi(u,v,\overline{z})),

where
\psi(u,v,\overline{z})
is

\displaystyle\exists x<u\,\forall y<v\,\neg\varphi(x,y,\overline{z})

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x,x^{\prime}<u\,\exists y<v\,(x\neq x^{\prime}\land\varphi(x,y,\overline{z})\land\varphi(x^{\prime},y,\overline{z}))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x<u\,\exists y,y^{\prime}<v\,(y\neq y^{\prime}\land\varphi(x,y,\overline{z})\land\varphi(x,y^{\prime},\overline{z})).

In words, this says that
\varphi
cannot be the graph of an injective function from
u
pigeons into
v
holes when
u>v
.

The pigeonhole principle is famously known to be unprovable in the full bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{T}_{2}(R)

[
1
,
8
,
15
]
.

We denote the set of instances
\textsf{PHP}(\varphi)
for all
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas
\varphi
by
\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
. The main result of the paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))\nvdash\forall n\,\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}.

3
Model construction

3.1
The game
G_{\mathbb{O}}

In this subsection, we construct a model expansion, later proved to satisfy
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
and to violate
\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}
for some
n
.

We start with a countable nonstandard model
\mathbb{M}
of true arithmetic in the language
\{0,1,+,\cdot,|\cdot|,\lfloor\cdot/2\rfloor,\#,\leq\}
. Pick a nonstandard number
n
in
\mathbb{M}
and let
\mathbb{I}
be the substructure of
\mathbb{M}
with domain

\{m:m\leq 2^{|n|^{c}}\text{ for some standard }c\}.

Our goal is to interpret
\triangleleft
in
\mathbb{I}
suitably. This is achieved through a step-by-step construction, which we view as a game played between three parties, denoted by
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
,
P_{\textsf{IND}}
, and
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
. The game runs for countably many steps. Its moves are sequences definable in the ground model
\mathbb{M}
, while the play itself (i.e., the limit sequence of all moves) is external and need not be definable in
\mathbb{M}
.

The game starts with
\triangleleft
being the empty relation. The players subsequently extend
\triangleleft
by adding at most
|n|^{c}
many elements at each move, for some standard
c
, with
c
possibly varying between the moves. The invariant is that
\triangleleft
is always a strict linear ordering of a subset of
[0,\dots,n)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{O}
be the set of sequences of distinct numbers from
[0,\dots,n)
of length at most
|n|^{c}
, for some standard constant
c
.

Each
o\in\mathbb{O}
induces a strict partial ordering of
[0,\dots,n)
, denoted by
\triangleleft_{o}
, in the obvious way: the domain consists of the numbers appearing in
o
, and their ordering is the order in which they are listed in
o
. We write
\operatorname{el}(o)
for the domain of
o
.

We say that
o^{\prime}\in\mathbb{O}

extends

o\in\mathbb{O}
, denoted
o^{\prime}\preceq o
or
o\succeq o^{\prime}
, if
\triangleleft_{o^{\prime}}\supseteq\triangleleft_{o}
. Thus
\preceq
is the forcing order: stronger conditions are smaller.

We say that
o^{\prime}\in\mathbb{O}

is compatible with

o\in\mathbb{O}
, denoted
o^{\prime}\|o
, if
\triangleleft_{o^{\prime}}
is compatible with
\triangleleft_{o}
as a strict ordering. Equivalently, there is
o^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{O}
extending both
o
and
o^{\prime}
. Incompatibility is denoted by
o\perp o^{\prime}
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
game

G_{\mathbb{O}}
is played by three players
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
,
P_{\textsf{IND}}
, and
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
for countably many steps.

The player
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
plays at stages
k\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, the player
P_{\textsf{IND}}
plays at stages
k\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and the player
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
plays at stages
k\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.

Let
o_{0}
be the empty sequence. At stage
k
, the respective player is presented with
o_{k}\in\mathbb{O}
, chooses
o_{k+1}\preceq o_{k}
from
\mathbb{O}
, and passes
o_{k+1}
to the next player.

A
strategy

\mathcal{S}
is a function that, on input
o\in\mathbb{O}
, outputs
o^{\prime}\in\mathbb{O}
such that
o^{\prime}\preceq o
.

Fixing strategies
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{MIN}}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{IND}}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{PHP}}
induces a chain

o_{0}\succeq o_{1}\succeq o_{2}\succeq\cdots.

This chain induces the strict ordering

\triangleleft_{\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{MIN}},\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{IND}},\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{PHP}}}:=\bigcup_{i}\triangleleft_{o_{i}}.

We drop the subscripts when the strategies are clear from context.

The expansion of
\mathbb{I}
by interpreting
\triangleleft
as this union is denoted by
\mathbb{I}_{\triangleleft}
.

The goal of
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
is for the resulting interpretation of
\triangleleft
to be a strict linear order of
[0,\dots,n)
with no least element, no matter how the other players play.

The goal of
P_{\textsf{IND}}
is for the resulting structure
\mathbb{I}_{\triangleleft}
to satisfy induction for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas, no matter how the other players play.

The goal of
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
is for the resulting structure
\mathbb{I}_{\triangleleft}
to satisfy
\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
, no matter how the other players play.

A strategy of one of the players is called
winning
if the corresponding goal is achieved regardless of how the other players play.

3.2
Winning strategies

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{MIN}}
winning for
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
.

Proof.

No matter how the players proceed, the resulting interpretation of
\triangleleft
is a strict, though not necessarily total, ordering of a subset of
[0,\dots,n)
.

To ensure that
\triangleleft
is total,
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
maintains an external countable queue of all elements of
[0,\dots,n)
, possible because
\mathbb{M}
is countable externally.

Given a sequence
o=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{O}
, the player removes all
a_{i}
from the queue, picks the first remaining element
a
, and extends
o
to

o^{\prime}=(a,a_{1},\dots,a_{m}).

Every element of
[0,\dots,n)
is eventually inserted, and every inserted element eventually has a smaller element placed before it. Hence the final ordering is total on
[0,\dots,n)
and has no least element.
∎

The theorem below is a direct adaptation of a classical result of Paris and Wilkie
[
14
, Theorem 21]
for the theory
I\exists_{1}(\alpha)
, later extended by Riis
[
16
]
to
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
. Here we provide only a sketch of the proof, more details and presentations of the mentioned theorem can be found, e.g., in
[
9
, Section 12.7]
and
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

There exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{IND}}
winning for
P_{\textsf{IND}}
.

Proof (sketch).

We use the standard fact that
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
induction is
equivalent to the
least number principle
for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formulas. Thus it is enough to ensure, for every
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formula
\varphi(x,\bar{a})
with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
and every bound
t\in\mathbb{I}
,
the sentence

\exists x<t\,\varphi(x,\bar{a})\rightarrow\exists x<t\,\bigl(\varphi(x,\bar{a})\land\forall y<x\,\neg\varphi(y,\bar{a})\bigr).

Say that a sequence
o\in\mathbb{O}

settles
a sentence
\theta
positively if every legal continuation of the play starting from
o
produces a final expansion satisfying
\theta
.

We use the following observation. Let
\theta
be a

\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
sentence with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
. If some legal continuation of the play from
o
produces a final expansion satisfying
\theta
, then there is an extension
o^{\prime}\preceq o
which settles
\theta
positively.

Now fix the current condition
o
, a formula
\varphi(x,\bar{a})
, and a bound
t\in\mathbb{I}
. Define

X=\{x<t:\text{ some }o^{\prime}\preceq o\text{ settles }\varphi(x,\bar{a})\text{ positively}\}.

The technical content of the Paris–Wilkie–Riis theorem is that sets of this kind are
definable in the ground model
\mathbb{M}
.

If
X
is empty, then no legal continuation from
o
can make

\varphi(x,\bar{a})
true for any
x<t
. Hence
o
already guarantees the negation of
the antecedent

\exists x<t\,\varphi(x,\bar{a}),

and therefore guarantees the least-number-principle implication.

If
X
is non-empty, let
x_{0}
be the least element of
X
. Choose

o^{\prime}\preceq o
which settles
\varphi(x_{0},\bar{a})
positively. By the minimality of
x_{0}
, no
y<x_{0}
belongs to
X
. By the observation above, this means that no legal continuation from
o
, and hence no legal continuation from
o^{\prime}
, can make

\varphi(y,\bar{a})
true for any
y<x_{0}
. Therefore
o^{\prime}
guarantees

\varphi(x_{0},\bar{a})\land\forall y<x_{0}\,\neg\varphi(y,\bar{a}).

Thus
o^{\prime}
guarantees the conclusion of the least-number principle.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
2.2
, it is enough to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

There exists a strategy
\mathcal{S}_{\textsf{PHP}}
winning for
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
.

3.3
MIN-trees

Definition 3.6
.

Let
o\in\mathbb{O}
. A subset
T
of
\mathbb{O}
definable in
\mathbb{M}
is called an
o
-MIN-tree
, or simply an
o
-tree
, if it satisfies the following recursive definition:

•

either
T=\{o\}
;

•

or, writing
o=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
, there is a single
a\in[0,\dots,n)\setminus\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
such that

T=\bigcup_{o^{\prime}\in A}T_{o^{\prime}},

where
A
is the set of all sequences extending
o
by adding
a
to its domain, and each
T_{o^{\prime}}
is an
o^{\prime}
-tree.

The
depth
of
T
is the largest difference between the length of a sequence in
T
and the length of
o
. An
o
-tree is
uniform
if all its members have the same length. The
size
of
T
is the number of elements it contains.

Remark
.

The above definition treats trees as definable subsets of
\mathbb{O}
that can be suitably factorized. A more standard convention, such as the definition of PHP-trees in
[
10
, Section 15.1]
, keeps the entire tree structure. The present convention is sufficient here because only the maximal conditions represented by the tree are used.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
T
be an
o
-tree.

1.

Any two distinct elements of
T
are incompatible.

2.

Any member of
\mathbb{O}
compatible with
o
is compatible with some element of
T
.

3.

If the depth of
T
is at most
d
, then

|T|\leq\frac{(|o|+d)!}{|o|!},

with equality if and only if
T
is a uniform
o
-tree of depth exactly
d
.

By Theorem
2.1
, every
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
relation is computable by a polynomial-time oracle algorithm with oracle
\triangleleft
. Fixing numerical parameters from
\mathbb{I}
, the possible oracle computations form a decision tree of depth polynomial in the lengths of those parameters. Since throughout the construction we maintain that
\triangleleft
is a strict partial order, an oracle query of the form
u\triangleleft v
can equivalently be implemented by inserting not-yet-seen elements among the currently ordered ones with their relative ordering given by the query answer. Thus the usual decision tree can be represented as a MIN-tree.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\varphi(x,y)
be a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formula with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
. Let
p,h\in\mathbb{I}
and
o\in\mathbb{O}
be fixed.

There exist definable families

\{T^{+}_{a,b}\}_{a<p,b<h}\quad\text{and}\quad\{T^{-}_{a,b}\}_{a<p,b<h}

such that, for each
a<p
and
b<h
, the sets
T^{+}_{a,b}
and
T^{-}_{a,b}
are disjoint and their union is an
o
-tree of depth at most
|n|^{c}
, for a fixed standard
c
depending only on
\varphi
.

Furthermore,
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)
proves that, for all
a<p
and
b<h
,

\displaystyle(\neg\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}\land\text{``$\triangleleft$ extends $o$''})\to\bigl(\varphi(a,b)\leftrightarrow\text{``$\triangleleft$ extends some $o^{\prime}\in T^{+}_{a,b}$''}\bigr),

and

\displaystyle(\neg\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}\land\text{``$\triangleleft$ extends $o$''})\to\bigl(\neg\varphi(a,b)\leftrightarrow\text{``$\triangleleft$ extends some $o^{\prime}\in T^{-}_{a,b}$''}\bigr).

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\varphi(x,y)
be a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)

A more detailed presentation can be found in

,

Since

Remark
.

Although

is an

-array” is external. The reason is the last bullet of Definition

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varphi(x,y)
be a

. Let

Let

The first two properties follow from the fact that each

Definition 4.3
.

Let

be an

-array. The

is

where

.

Proposition 4.4
.

For an

-array

,

We write

for the rows and columns of

.

Definition 4.5
.

Given two

-arrays

and

, we say that

extends

if, for each

,

, and

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
q,s\in\mathbb{O}
be compatible conditions extending
o
. There is a
q
-tree

, called
envelope of

s

over

q
, of depth at most
|s|-|o|
such that every member

satisfies

\operatorname{el}(r)=\operatorname{el}(q)\cup\operatorname{el}(s).

Moreover, if
s^{\prime}\preceq o
is incompatible with
s
but compatible with
q
, then every

compatible with
s^{\prime}
satisfies

|\operatorname{el}(r)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|\geq|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|+1.

Proof.

Construct

by successively choosing the elements of
\operatorname{el}(s)\setminus\operatorname{el}(q)
, in any fixed definable order, and at each step branching over all possible positions of the chosen element in the current sequence starting with

. This gives a
q
-tree. Its leaves are precisely the possible extensions of
q
to the domain
\operatorname{el}(q)\cup\operatorname{el}(s)
.

It remains to prove the last part of the claim. Suppose
s^{\prime}\preceq o
,
s^{\prime}\perp s
, and
s^{\prime}\|q
. If

(\operatorname{el}(s)\setminus\operatorname{el}(q))\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})=\emptyset,

then all elements common to
s
and
s^{\prime}
already lie in
\operatorname{el}(q)
. Since both
s
and
s^{\prime}
are compatible with
q
, the (partial) orderings induced by

and by
s^{\prime}
agree on
\operatorname{el}(q)\supset\operatorname{el}(s)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})
. This contradicts

.

Therefore there is some element

Every

contains

, and hence contains this additional element

. Thus, whenever

is compatible with

|\operatorname{el}(r)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|\geq|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|+1.

∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
A
be an
(o,p,h)
-array. There exists an
(o,p,h)
-array

extending

such that each row

is a uniform

-tree of depth

, for some fixed standard constant

Proof.

Let
d^{\prime}=|n|^{c^{\prime}}
be such that every sequence appearing in

has length at most
|o|+d^{\prime}
. We fix
a<p
and construct a sequence of

-trees

T^{a}_{0},T^{a}_{1},\dots,T^{a}_{d^{\prime}}

satisfying the invariant

\forall q\in T^{a}_{i}\,\forall s\in A_{a}\,\bigl(q\|s\to|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s)|\geq\min(|o|+i,|s|)\bigr).

(1)

For

, take
T^{a}_{0}=\{o\}
.

Suppose
T^{a}_{i}
has been constructed. For each leaf
q\in T^{a}_{i}
, proceed as follows.

If

extends some

, then we leave

unchanged at this stage. This preserves the invariant: if

is compatible with
q

, because distinct elements of the same row are incompatible. Hence

already contains all elements of

.

Otherwise, by the last property of an

-array, there exists some

compatible with
q
. Replace

from Lemma

Running this construction for every leaf

gives an
o

. We verify the invariant. Let
r\in T^{a}_{i+1}
extend a leaf

, and let

be compatible with

. Then

is compatible with

If

, then

If

, then

and

are incompatible, since they are distinct elements of the same row

. By Lemma

|\operatorname{el}(r)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|\geq|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|+1.

By the induction hypothesis,

|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|\geq\min(|o|+i,|s^{\prime}|).

If this minimum were

, then

would contain all elements of

, and since

, the condition

would extend

, contradicting the case we are considering. Hence the minimum is

|\operatorname{el}(r)\cap\operatorname{el}(s^{\prime})|\geq|o|+i+1=\min(|o|+i+1,|s^{\prime}|).

Thus the invariant holds for

.

After

steps, for every

and every

|\operatorname{el}(q)\cap\operatorname{el}(s)|=|s|.

Hence

. Since

.

Extend

arbitrarily to a uniform

of depth

, where

is a standard constant large enough for all rows. The property that every leaf of the tree extends a unique element of

is preserved. Uniqueness follows from the fact that distinct elements of

are incompatible.

For each

, define

Repeating the construction for every

gives an indexed family

.

We verify that

is an

-array. Every element of every

extends

, because it belongs to the

-tree

. Any two distinct elements in the same cell are incompatible because they are distinct leaves of the same

-tree. The row and column incompatibility conditions follow because every element of

extends an element of

, and incompatibility is preserved under extension. Finally, if

and

, then, since

is an

gives a leaf

∎

.

An
(o,p,h)
-array

is extendable to an

-array

of size exactly

where

for some standard

. Moreover, every element of every cell

has length exactly

Proof.

By Theorem
4.7
, extend

to an array

such that each row

is a uniform

, each row has size exactly

\frac{(|o|+d)!}{|o|!}.

Summing over all

∎

Compared to
[

, this gives an exact size rather than only a lower bound.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let

be an
(o,p,h)
-array whose elements all have length at most

. Then

|A|\leq h\frac{(|o|+d)!}{|o|!}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to
[

, which itself resembles the argument of
[

First extend every sequence appearing in

arbitrarily to length exactly

.

For each

, the column

is a set of pairwise incompatible elements of

|(A^{\prime})^{b}|\leq\frac{(|o|+d)!}{|o|!}.

Choose

large enough that every element appearing in every sequence from

. There are exactly

∎

.

to

By Theorem

4.2
Densely ordered model

The ordering constructed above can be made even more pathological. Namely, given a countable collection of sufficiently large subsets
X
of
\mathbb{I}
, we can arrange that
\triangleleft
orders all of them densely; that is,
(X,\triangleleft|_{X})
is a dense linear ordering. Here “sufficiently large” means that there is a definable injection in
\mathbb{M}
mapping
[0,\dots,|n|^{d})
into
X
, for some nonstandard
d
.

We denote by
\textsf{DLO}(\triangleleft|_{X})
the following sentence:

\displaystyle\forall x\in X\,\neg(x\triangleleft x)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in X\,(x\neq y\to(x\triangleleft y\lor y\triangleleft x))

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\forall x,y,z\in X\,((x\triangleleft y\land y\triangleleft z)\to x\triangleleft z)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\forall x\in X\,\exists y\in X\,(y\neq x\land y\triangleleft x)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\forall x\in X\,\exists y\in X\,(y\neq x\land x\triangleleft y)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in X\,(x\triangleleft y\to\exists z\in X\,(x\triangleleft z\land z\triangleleft y)).

Theorem 4.11
.

Assume
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a countable collection of subsets of
\mathbb{I}
such that, for each
X_{i}
, there exists a nonstandard
d
and a definable injection mapping
[0,\dots,|n|^{d})
into
X_{i}
.

Then there exists an expansion of
\mathbb{I}
to a model of

\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))

which moreover satisfies
\textsf{DLO}(\triangleleft|_{X_{i}})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The model is constructed similarly to the proof of Theorem
2.2
. The set
\mathbb{O}
is replaced by
\mathbb{O}^{\prime}
, consisting of arbitrary sequences of elements of
\mathbb{I}
of length at most
|n|^{c}
, for some standard constant
c
.

The players
P_{\textsf{IND}}
and
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
are defined analogously, and the existence of their winning strategies is proved in the same way. For
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
, note that the lower and upper bounds on the sizes of arrays were proved without using the fact that the order was being constructed only on
[0,\dots,n)
.

The strategy for
P_{\textsf{MIN}}
is replaced by a countable sequence of players
P^{X_{i}}_{\textsf{DLO}}
, one for each
X_{i}
. The game is scheduled so that
P_{\textsf{IND}}
,
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
, and each
P^{X_{i}}_{\textsf{DLO}}
play infinitely often.

The player
P^{X}_{\textsf{DLO}}
maintains an external queue of elements of
X
. Given a condition
o\in\mathbb{O}^{\prime}
, let

o_{X}=(a_{i_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{m}})

be the subsequence of
o
consisting of elements from
X
. The player removes these elements from its queue and chooses fresh elements

b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\in X.

It then extends the induced ordering on
X
so that

b_{0}\triangleleft a_{i_{1}}\triangleleft b_{1}\triangleleft a_{i_{2}}\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft a_{i_{m}}\triangleleft b_{m}.

Finally, it takes a common extension of this ordering with the previous condition
o
.

Since every
P^{X}_{\textsf{DLO}}
acts infinitely often, this ensures that
\triangleleft|_{X}
has no endpoints and that between any two previously ordered elements of
X
a fresh element of
X
is eventually inserted. Hence
\textsf{DLO}(\triangleleft|_{X})
holds in the final model.
∎

5
Variants of the construction

At this point, a general recipe for building a model of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)+\textsf{PHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha))
, where
\alpha
witnesses the failure of some combinatorial principle
C
, can be summarized as follows.

1.

Define a poset
\mathbb{P}_{C}
containing conditions for building an interpretation of
\alpha
such that the player
P_{C}
aiming to falsify
C
has a winning strategy.

2.

Verify that
P_{\textsf{IND}}
has a winning strategy. This amounts to proving a theorem similar to the result of Paris and Wilkie
[
14
]
; a general treatment of this kind of problem in a forcing framework can be found in
[
2
]
.

3.

Verify that
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
has a winning strategy. This is achieved by the following steps.

(a)

Define a suitable analogue of an
(o,p,h)
-array and show that the existence of such an array is mutually exclusive with the immediate success of the PHP player.

(b)

Define a suitable notion of a tree, as in Definition
3.6
, and extend rows of the array to have tree structure. This gives a lower bound on the size of the array.

(c)

Prove an upper bound on the size of a family of pairwise incompatible conditions in
\mathbb{P}_{C}
.

(d)

Compare the lower and upper bounds to derive an inequality such as
p\leq h
.

A particular instance of this method, albeit in a slightly different language, was used in
[
13
]
to separate
\textsf{WPHP}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(R))
and
\forall m\leq n^{\epsilon}\,\textsf{PHP}^{m+1}_{m}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(R))
from
\textsf{ontoPHP}^{n+1}_{n}(R)
over
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(R)
.

In the following subsections, we sketch additional separations obtained by the same method.

5.1
The
TOUR
principle

Definition 5.1

(
[
9
, Section 12.1]
)
.

A
tournament
is a directed graph
(V,E)
with exactly one directed edge between any two distinct vertices.

A set
X\subseteq V
is
dominating
if, for every vertex
w\in V\setminus X
, there is
v\in X
such that
(v,w)\in E
.

A well-known fact is that a tournament on
n
vertices contains a dominating set of size at most
|n|+1
; see, for example,
[
11
, Section 2.5]
.

Given a binary relation
E
and a number
n
, the following sentence
\textsf{TOUR}(E)_{n}
expresses this fact:

\displaystyle\exists x\neq y<n\,(\neg E(x,y)\land\neg E(y,x))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x\neq y<n\,(E(x,y)\land E(y,x))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists X\subseteq[0,\dots,n)\,\bigl(|X|\leq|n|+1\land\forall x<n\,(x\notin X\to\exists y\in X\,E(y,x))\bigr).

Sets
X
quantified in the above expression are taken to be definable in the ground model
\mathbb{M}
, and hence codable by numbers of size approximately
n^{|n|+1}
, which belong to
\mathbb{I}
.

Remark
.

The tournament principle was shown by Jeřábek
[
5
]
to be provable in the bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{APC}_{2}(E)
.

Following the recipe above, define
\mathbb{T}
as the set of tournaments of size at most
|n|^{c}
on subsets of
[0,\dots,n)
, where
c
is an arbitrary standard constant. The order is inverse inclusion.

There is a simple winning strategy for the player
P_{\textsf{DOM}|X}
, whose goal is to ensure that a given small set
X
is not dominating in the resulting tournament. Given
t\in\mathbb{T}
, choose
x\in[0,\dots,n)\setminus X
not yet appearing in
t
, and extend
t
by adding
x
with all edges between
x
and elements of
X
directed from
x
towards
X
. Then no vertex from
X
dominates
x
.

The exact size bound
|X|\leq|n|+1
is not essential here. It suffices that
X
has size at most
|n|^{c}
for a standard
c
, similarly to the situation in Theorem
4.11
.

To ensure that
E
is a tournament on the full interval
[0,\dots,n)
, we add another player
P_{\textsf{TOUR}}
, who maintains a countable queue of vertices from
[0,\dots,n)
not yet inserted into the tournament. Given
t
, the player chooses the first vertex in the queue and adds it to
t
, orienting its edges to the existing vertices arbitrarily.

We omit the treatment of
P_{\textsf{IND}}
, since the proof is analogous to
[
14
, Theorem 21]
.

The definition of a
t
-
TOUR
-tree is the expected one. Choosing a new vertex
x
, one branches according to all possible ways of extending the current tournament
t
by including
x
. A uniform
t
-tree of depth
d
has size

2^{|t|}\cdot 2^{|t|+1}\cdots 2^{|t|+d-1}.

As in Theorem
4.9
, one proves that a pairwise incompatible set of tournament conditions extending
t
and having size
|t|+d
has cardinality at most the same product. Hence the lower-bound and upper-bound comparison again yields the dimensional restriction
p\leq h
, which gives the desired PHP strategy.

5.2
The
dWPHP
principle

The following principle was first considered by A. Wilkie; see
[
10
, Section 11.2]
. There the principle is referred to as
WPHP
; the current terminology was developed later.

Definition 5.2
.

For a relation
R\subseteq[0,\dots,n)\times[0,\dots,2n)
, the
dual weak pigeonhole principle
says that
R
cannot be the graph of a surjective function from
[0,\dots,n)
onto
[0,\dots,2n)
.

This is expressed by the sentence
\textsf{dWPHP}(R)
:

\displaystyle\exists x<n\,\exists y\neq z<2n\,(R(x,y)\land R(x,z))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x<n\,\forall y<2n\,\neg R(x,y)

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists y<2n\,\forall x<n\,\neg R(x,y).

To build a model of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(R)
that violates
\textsf{dWPHP}(R)
, we play the game with
\mathbb{D}
being the set of graphs of partial functions on
[0,\dots,n)\times[0,\dots,2n)
of size at most
|n|^{c}
, for some standard constant
c
. The order is inverse inclusion.

The player
P_{\textsf{dWPHP}}
maintains a countable queue of elements of
[0,\dots,2n)
not yet in the range of the partial function. At each move, it extends the current condition so as to put the next element of this queue into the range, while preserving functionality. Thus the final relation is the graph of a total surjection from
[0,\dots,n)
onto
[0,\dots,2n)
.

A winning strategy for
P_{\textsf{IND}}
again follows from
[
14
, Theorem 21]
.

Finally, a winning strategy for
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
follows from a combinatorial analysis similar to that in
[
13
]
. There, the bijective pigeonhole principle was considered. In that case, trees allowed two kinds of queries: one could either choose a pigeon and ask for its corresponding hole, or choose a hole and ask for its corresponding pigeon. In the current context, only the first kind of query is allowed: given a pigeon, ask for its corresponding hole.

The size of a uniform tree of depth
d
is exactly
(2n)^{d}
. On the other hand, the upper bound on the size of a depth-
d
uniform set of pairwise incompatible elements of
\mathbb{D}
is also
(2n)^{d}
. Hence the same array-size comparison gives the desired strategy for
P_{\textsf{PHP}}
.

5.3
Open problem

Consider the following formal expression, where
\varphi(x,y)
is a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
formula and
h<p
:

\displaystyle\exists x\neq y<p\,\exists z<h\,(\varphi(x,z)\land\varphi(y,z))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x<p\,\forall y<h\,\neg\varphi(x,y).

This is a variant of the pigeonhole principle that allows multifunctions and is possibly stronger than the
\textsf{PHP}(\varphi(x,y))
defined in Section
2.2
.

Let
\textsf{PHP}^{\prime}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))
denote the set of instances of the above formal expression for all
\varphi(x,y)\in\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft)
and all
h<p
from
\mathbb{I}
.

We do not know whether it is possible to build a model of

\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\triangleleft)+\textsf{PHP}^{\prime}(\Delta^{b}_{1}(\triangleleft))+\neg\textsf{MIN}(\triangleleft)_{n}

using the combinatorial methods of the present paper. The apparent difficulty is that one needs lower bounds on the size of a modified version of an
(o,p,h)
-array in which elements of the same row are no longer necessarily incompatible, or even distinct.
