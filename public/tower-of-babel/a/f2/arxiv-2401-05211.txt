Error estimation for physics-informed neural networks with implicit Runge-Kutta methods
Jochen Stiasny, Spyros Chatzivasileiadis

Abstract
The ability to accurately approximate trajectories of dynamical systems enables their analysis, prediction, and control. Neural network (NN)-based approximations have attracted significant interest due to fast evaluation with good accuracy over long integration time steps. In contrast to established numerical approximation schemes such as Runge-Kutta methods, the estimation of the error of the NN-based approximations proves to be difficult. In this work, we propose to use the NN’s predictions in a high-order implicit Runge-Kutta (IRK) method. The residuals in the implicit system of equations can be related to the NN’s prediction error, hence, we can provide an error estimate at several points along a trajectory. We find that this error estimate highly correlates with the NN’s prediction error and that increasing the order of the IRK method improves this estimate. We demonstrate this estimation methodology for Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINNs) on the logistic equation as an illustrative example and then apply it to a four-state electric generator model that is regularly used in power system modelling.

Introduction

Differential equations provide a powerful modelling tool to describe how systems change. They are ubiquitous in science and engineering and are used to formulate the behaviour of dynamical systems
(
Strogatz 2015
)
. While instantaneous changes are simple to evaluate, there, usually, exists no analytical solution to these differential equations. To predict future states of the system, we revert to numerical integration methods, see for example
Hairer et al. 1993
;
Hairer and Wanner 1996
;
Iserles 2008
for detailed treatment of the topic. A key question is the estimation of the approximation error of an integration scheme—both for a single time step and multiple time steps. For established schemes such as
RK
(
RK
) methods, this estimation hinges around the local approximation error
\varepsilon
which is closely related to the step size
h
. This relation is expressed as the order
p
of a scheme
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}\left(h^{p+1}\right)
and it describes the error characteristic when
h
is sufficiently small
(
Hairer et al. 1993
)
. To obtain absolute error estimates, an additional
RK
scheme can be constructed to form an embedded
RK
method
(
Hairer et al. 1993
)
. The resulting error estimates are crucial in choosing time step sizes that yield a balance between a sufficiently accurate approximation and the required computational cost.

For dynamical systems found in power systems, the computational burden of solving the associated differential equations is significant—
Konstantelos et al. 2017
provide an indication of the scales. Grid operators, who need to ensure system stability, rely heavily on these numerical integration methods to choose control actions, but the computational burden becomes a limiting factor. Therefore, the choice of the integration method and accurate error estimate are critical; this becomes even more relevant in light of the energy transition with additional dynamic phenomena
(
Hatziargyriou et al. 2021
)
.
NN
(
NN
)-based approaches such as in
Cui et al. 2023
;
Moya and Lin 2023
;
Stiasny et al. 2023
could have upsides in terms of prediction speed, however, the question of estimating and controlling the approximation error poses a major challenge to their adoption. Motivated by this example, this work shall provide a novel error estimation procedure to understand the error characteristics of learned solutions for dynamical systems.

Neural network-based integration:

With
NN
-based integration methods we
learn
an approximate solution function to a differential equation or a system of differential equations. This learned solution offers the upside of fast and explicit evaluations while providing sufficiently accurate results over large time steps, given appropriate training. These benefits come at the expense of the need to train the model in advance. As suggested in
Lagaris et al. 1998
the training procedure can be set up as a collocation method. The NN constitutes the candidate function and is fit to match the differential equations at collocation points. This methodology has been revived in
Raissi et al. 2018
under the term
PINN
(
PINN
).
A related approach, that can be seen as a generalisation of PINNs, is termed operator learning
(
Lu et al. 2021
;
Kovachki et al. 2023
)
; we will subsequently use NN or PINN to refer to these kind of approaches. As these methods do not adhere to error characteristics by construction—unlike
RK
schemes—understanding and estimating the prediction errors is an open research question.

Approaches for analysing the error of PINNs:

The trivial approach consists in comparing the
PINN
’s prediction with a highly accurate integration scheme that can be regarded as ground truth. However, as this assessment might involve significant computational cost, our interest lies on approaches for estimating the error characteristics without knowing the ground truth. For the simplest version of
PINN
, we obtain a local approximation error
\mathcal{O}(1)
, meaning that the error is independent of the time step size
h
.
Lagaris et al. 1998
proposed to change the
NN
’s architecture to ensure numerical consistency for small
h
; reducing the time step size should reduce the error. However, achieving high orders
p
would require many additional function evaluations which reduces the attractiveness of
PINN
from the standpoint of speed. Furthermore, these changes do not necessarily help with controlling the error for large time steps. This is undesirable as
PINN
become an attractive alternative to
RK
schemes only for large time steps.

The following approaches focus on providing guaranteed error bounds to certify a
PINN
.
Hillebrecht and Unger 2022
formulate error dynamics between the exact solution and the
PINN
approximation. These dynamics can be bounded using the Lipschitz constant of the dynamical system, but for non-linear systems this might not result in tight error bounds. Several works
(
Yarotsky 2017
;
De Ryck et al. 2021
;
De Ryck and Mishra 2022
)
aim to provide theoretical upper bounds on the approximation errors of
NN
based on the function class that they describe, however, these results do not provide practical error estimates.
Eiras et al. 2023
verify that the residual of the differential equations based on the learned approximation is bounded and they show empirically that this bound is related to the maximum approximation error.

Proposed approach:

In this work, we combine
PINN
with the collocation method of Gauss-Legendre Runge-Kutta schemes, a special form of implicit RK schemes, to estimate the error, similar to embedded RK schemes. To this end, we evaluate the
PINN
at time instances (nodes) defined by the IRK scheme. For a high-order IRK scheme, the function values at the nodes coincide with intermediate values along the trajectory of interest. We then evaluate how closely these predictions match the implicit system of equations defined by the IRK scheme. Based on the mismatch, we can estimate the error at the nodes and hence along the trajectory. As a numerical example, we illustrate the methodology on the logistic equation. A power system generator model with four states serves as a second example to showcase the resulting error estimation on a relevant use case.

2
Approximating the trajectory of a dynamical system

We assume an autonomous dynamical system governed by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{x}(t)=\bm{f}\left(\bm{x}(t)\right)

(1)

with state
\bm{x}{}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, time
t\in\mathbb{R}
and the update function
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given an initial condition
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}{}(t_{0})
, the trajectory
\bm{x}(t_{0}+h)
can be obtained by integration of
Eq.

1

\displaystyle\bm{x}(t_{0}+h)=\bm{x}_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\bm{f}\left(\bm{x}(t)\right)dt.

(2)

We refer to
h=t-t_{0}
as the time step size. Given that
\bm{f}
is continuous and Lipschitz continuous, the existence and uniqueness of
\bm{x}(h)
is ensured. Usually, no analytical form for
Eq.

2
exists, hence, we revert to constructing an approximation
\hat{\bm{x}}(h)
of the trajectory. There is a great variety of established methods, see for example
Hairer et al. 1993
;
Iserles 2008
, in this work we focus on implicit
RK
methods (
Section

2.1
). In
Section

2.2
, we present
PINN
as a
NN
-based integration method.

2.1
Implicit Runge-Kutta (IRK) methods

As described in
Iserles 2008
,
Eq.

2
constitutes a quadrature problem and it can be approximated by a weighted sum of the function values at
s
nodes

\displaystyle\bm{x}(t_{0}+h)\approx\bm{x}{}_{0}+h\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\,\bm{f}\left(\bm{x}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)\right).

(3)

The location of each node is determined by the parameter
c_{i}
which usually ranges in
[0,1]
and it is associated with the positive weight
b_{i}
. As the quadrature requires
\bm{x}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)
at the nodes, their values are approximated by
\bm{k}^{(i)}\approx\bm{x}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)
and are subsequently referred to as the
i
-th stage of the
RK
scheme. By constructing a polynomial of order
s
that passes through the node values
\bm{k}^{(i)}
, a set of conditions can be derived—we construct a collocation method. The conditions are described by the following system of implicit equations

\displaystyle\bm{k}^{(i)}=\bm{x}_{0}+h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\bm{f}\left(\bm{k}^{(j)}\right).

(4)

By solving for
\bm{k}^{(i)}
, we can then calculate

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+h)=\bm{x}{}_{0}+h\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\,\bm{f}\left(\bm{k}^{(i)}\right).

(5)

The number of stages
s
and the choice of
a_{ij},b_{i},c_{i}
determine the accuracy of the scheme. It can be shown, that the choice of Gauss-Legendre polynomials for
b_{i},c_{i}
leads to a scheme of numerical order
2s{}
. This implies that the approximation error scales according to
\mathcal{O}\left(h^{2s{}}\right)
. This relationship allows IRK schemes to achieve highly accurate approximations for large time step sizes
h
and moderate numbers of stages
s
. The approximation error
\bm{\eta}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{(i)}:=\bm{k}^{(i)}-\bm{x}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)

(6)

of the stages that result from solving the collocation scheme scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(h^{s}\right)

(
Butcher 2009
)
. We will use this relation in the error estimation in
Section

3
as it provides insightful information at intermediate points along the trajectory.

2.2
Neural-Network-based approximation

In the previous section, the candidate solution for the collocation problem is a polynomial. Instead, we now choose a NN to approximate
\bm{x}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+h)=\bm{x}{}_{0}+h\,\NN{}_{\theta}(h).

(7)

The modifications of multiplying
\NN{}_{\theta}(h)
with
h
and the addition of the initial condition
\bm{x}{}_{0}
can already be found in
Lagaris et al. 1998
. They ensure that the initial condition is satisfied for
h=0
and the local approximation error will scale as
\mathcal{O}\left(h\right)
if
\NN{}_{\theta}(h)
is bounded.

In this work, we use a simple feed-forward neural network
\NN{}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
with
L
layers of size
d_{l}
. It describes a concatenation of affine transformations
\mathcal{A}_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{l-1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}:z\mapsto W_{l}z+b_{l}
with non-linear element-wise activation functions
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\NN{}_{\theta}(h)=\mathcal{A}_{L}\circ\sigma\circ\mathcal{A}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\sigma\circ\mathcal{A}_{1}(h).

The weight matrices
W_{l}
and bias vectors
b_{l}
of the
L
layers constitute the trainable parameters
\theta
and for the present case, the time step size forms the input
(d_{0}=1)
and the
n
states of the system form the output
(d_{L}=n)
. The training problem to determine
\theta
becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\bm{x}}\left(t_{0}+h^{(j)}\right)-\bm{f}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}\left(t_{0}+h^{(j)}\right)\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(8)

as introduced in
Lagaris et al. 1998
and termed
PINN
by
Raissi et al. 2018
. This can be seen as a collocation method for finding
\theta
by evaluating the norm in
Eq.

8
at
N
collocation points at time step sizes
h^{(j)}
. The temporal derivative
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+h^{(j)})
can be computed using automatic differentiation (AD)
(
Baydin et al. 2018
)
, hence, this training procedure does not require simulated data points. We solve the optimisation problem in
Eq.

8
with a gradient descent method, namely the limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm (L-BFGS)
(
Liu and Nocedal 1989
)
.

3
Error estimates of PINN predictions with IRK methods

The following error estimation approach is closely linked to the works in
Hairer and Wanner 1996
;
de Swart and Söderlind 1997
;
Butcher 2009
, however, we do not focus on the error estimation at the end of the time step
|\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+h)-\bm{x}(t_{0}+h)|
. Instead, we aim for
s
error estimates at the
s
locations of the RK stages
t_{0}+c_{i}h
which are spread across the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+h]
as
c_{i}\in[0,1]
.

To this end, we evaluate the approximation function
\hat{\bm{x}}
defined by the PINN at the node locations
t_{0}+c_{i}h
that correspond to an IRK method of our choice. These predictions act as approximations

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}=\hat{\bm{x}}{}\left(t_{0}+c_{i}h\right)

(9)

of the IRK stages
\bm{k}^{(i)}
and will, generally speaking, lead to a mismatch
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}^{(i)}:=\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}-\bm{k}^{(i)},

(10)

that we will call the
stage error
. Using
Eqs.

6
,
9
and
10
and
\bm{\eta}^{(i)}=\mathcal{O}\left(h^{s}\right)
, we can formulate the desired error estimate for the NN-based function approximation

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)-\bm{x}(t_{0}+c_{i}h)=\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}-(\bm{k}^{(i)}+\bm{\eta}^{(i)})=\bm{\delta}^{(i)}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{s}\right)

(11)

The subsequent paragraph describes the estimation of
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
without explicitly solving for
\bm{k}^{(i)}
.

Estimation of
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
:

Due to the stage error
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
, the corresponding system of equations in
Eq.

4
evaluated with
\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}
will also not be satisfied; a residual
\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the system of equations for the
i
-th stage remains

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}:=\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}-\bm{x}_{0}-h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\bm{f}\left(\hat{\bm{k}}^{(j)}\right).

(12)

We begin by replacing
\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}=\bm{k}^{(i)}+\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
and expanding
\bm{f}\left(\bm{k}^{(i)}+\bm{\delta}^{(i)}\right)
in a Taylor series expansion

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}&=\bm{k}^{(i)}+\bm{\delta}^{(i)}-\left(\bm{x}(t_{0})+h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\bm{f}\left(\bm{k}^{(j)}+\bm{\delta}^{(j)}\right)\right)\\
&=\bm{k}^{(i)}+\bm{\delta}^{(i)}-\left(\bm{x}(t_{0})+h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\left[\bm{f}\left(\bm{k}^{(j)}\right)+\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\bm{k}^{(j)}}\bm{\delta}^{(j)}+\mathcal{O}\left({\bm{\delta}^{(j)}}^{2}\right)\right]\right)\\
&=\bm{\delta}^{(i)}-h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\bm{k}^{(j)}}\bm{\delta}^{(j)}+\mathcal{O}\left({\bm{\delta}^{(j)}}^{2}\right)\end{split}

(13)

The last simplification follows from the definition of the IRK scheme evaluated on
\bm{k}^{(i)}
as it must equal
0
. As
Eq.

13
depends on
\bm{k}^{(j)}
, we perform a Taylor series expansion of
\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\bm{k}^{(j)}}
using
\bm{k}^{(j)}=\hat{\bm{k}}^{(j)}-\bm{\delta}^{(j)}
. The relation between
\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}
and
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\delta}^{(i)}-h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(j)}}\bm{\delta}^{(j)}+\mathcal{O}\left({\bm{\delta}^{(j)}}^{2}\right).

(14)

By collecting
\bm{\delta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\delta}^{(1)}&\dots&\bm{\delta}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{ns}
and
\bm{\zeta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\zeta}^{(1)}&\dots&\bm{\zeta}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{ns}
we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\left(I-hJ\right)\bm{\delta}+\mathcal{O}\left({\bm{\delta}}^{2}\right)

(15)

with the identity matrix
I\in\mathbb{R}^{ns\times ns}
and the Jacobian-like matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{ns\times ns}
that is constructed based on the Jacobians
\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(i)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and the weights of the IRK scheme
a_{ij}

\displaystyle J=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(1)}}&\dots&a_{1s}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(s)}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{s1}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(1)}}&\dots&a_{ss}\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right|_{\hat{\bm{k}}^{(s)}}\end{bmatrix}.

Neglecting the higher order terms of
\bm{\delta}
and inverting
(I-hJ)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}{}

\displaystyle=\left(I-hJ\right)^{-1}\bm{\zeta}{}

(16)

which can then be used in
Eq.

11
to estimate the prediction error of the NN.

Logistic equation - illustration with an analytical solution

This section demonstrates the methodology on the logistic equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}x=x(1-x)

with the analytical solution for an initial conditions
x(t_{0})>0

\displaystyle x(t_{0}+h)=\frac{e^{h}}{c_{0}+e^{h}};\qquad c_{0}=\frac{1}{x(t_{0})}-1.

We use the simple but nonlinear logistic equation due to the ease of visualisation and the existence of an analytical solution. The arguments can be equally made for dynamical systems with multiple states as we demonstrate later on. To build intuition, we show in

5
Error estimation for a power system generator model

As motivated in
Section

1
, NN-based approximations might be able to accelerate the simulation of dynamical phenomena in power systems. The PINNSim approach
(
Stiasny et al. 2023
)
relies on learning the dynamics of single components, such as generators, and then resolving their interactions. Being able to estimate errors of the learned dynamics will be an important step for future development of such approaches. As an example for the dynamics of a component, we use a two-axis generator model which is modelled by a state vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
governed by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}E^{\prime}_{q}\\
E^{\prime}_{d}\\
\delta\\
\Delta\omega\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{T^{\prime}_{do}}\left(-E^{\prime}_{q}-\left(X_{d}-X^{\prime}_{d}\right)I_{d}+E_{fd}\right)\\
\frac{1}{T^{\prime}_{qo}}\left(-E^{\prime}_{d}+\left(X_{q}-X^{\prime}_{q}\right)I_{q}\right)\\
\omega_{s}\Delta\omega\\
\frac{1}{2H}\left(P_{m}-E^{\prime}_{d}I_{d}-E^{\prime}_{q}I_{q}-\left(X^{\prime}_{q}-X^{\prime}_{d}\right)I_{d}I_{q}-D\Delta\omega\right)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{d}\\
I_{q}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}R_{s}&-X_{q}\\
X_{d}&R_{s}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}E^{\prime}_{d}-V\sin{(\delta-\theta)}\\
E^{\prime}_{q}-V\cos{(\delta-\theta)}\end{bmatrix}.

We refer to
Sauer and Pai 1998
for a detailed description of the modelling and the used parameters. The following results use the parameters associated with generator 2 in
Sauer and Pai 1998
.

In
Section

4
, we learned a single trajectory. Here, we aim to predict a collection of trajectories starting from initial conditions
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
within the set
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, hence, we adjust
Eq.

7
to consider
\bm{x}_{0}
as an additional input to the PINN

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}{}(t_{0}+h,\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{x}_{0}+h\,\NN{}_{\theta}(h,\bm{x}_{0}).

(17)

As a consequence, the approximation error will depend on
h
and
\bm{x}_{0}
. Therefore, we sample initial conditions from
\mathcal{X}_{0}
and apply the proposed error estimation technique for each
\bm{x}_{0}
. As a result we obtain
s
error estimates along the trajectory of every tested
\bm{x}_{0}
. To report the error, we compute a norm over the prediction error
\hat{\bm{x}}{}-\bm{x}{}
(evaluated at
c_{i}h
) and the estimated error

6
Conclusions

The estimation of prediction errors poses a major challenge for
PINN
(
PINN
). This work introduces a simulation-free and practical error estimation approach, which can remove the barrier for incorporating
PINN
in established numerical solvers.
Our methodology only requires the evaluation of an implicit Runge-Kutta (IRK) scheme (whose coefficients are usually pre-computed) and a matrix inversion, which leads to a simple and easy-to-implement estimation method, see
Eq.

16
. We demonstrate that the proposed approach can accurately estimate the prediction errors along a trajectory and is also applicable for dynamical systems with multiple states. We also show that it is straightforward to assess the quality of this estimate by analysing the consistency of the error estimates that stem from different time step sizes
h
.

Future work:

From a theoretical perspective, we see the need to further investigate the influencing factors on the estimation accuracy. That is, first, the analysis of the impact of higher-order terms of
\bm{\delta}^{(i)}
which could become significant if the
PINN
’s prediction is not very accurate. Furthermore, the impact of system characteristics such as stiffness and oscillatory behaviour on the required number of stages
s
should be investigated.

With respect to practical applications, the proposed estimation methodology can be used in monitoring the training progress, assessing the learned model, or providing estimates during prediction tasks. We plan to explore these use cases to develop heuristics for this method. Furthermore, the methodology could inform an additional term in the loss function similar to
Raissi et al. 2018
;
Stiasny 2023
. Lastly, the error estimates could also be employed at the evaluation stage to correct the
PINN
’s prediction.
