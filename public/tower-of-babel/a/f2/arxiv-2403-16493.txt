Gap distribution of n ( mod 1 ) \sqrt{n}\pmod{1} and the circle method
Maksym Radziwiłł and Niclas Technau

Abstract
. The distribution of the properly renormalized gaps of n ​ mod ​ 1 \sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1} with n < N n<N converges (when N → ∞ N\rightarrow\infty ) to a non-standard limit distribution, as Elkies and McMullen proved in 2004 using techniques from homogeneous dynamics. In this paper we give an essentially self-contained proof based on the circle method. Our main innovation consists in showing that a new type of correlation functions of n ​ mod ​ 1 \sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1} converge. To define these correlation functions we restrict, smoothly, to those n ​ mod ​ 1 \sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1} that lie in minor arcs, i.e. away from rational numbers with small denominators.

1.
Introduction

By work of Fejér and of Csillag,
it is known that the sequence
\{n^{\alpha}\}_{n}
, with
\alpha>0
non-integer, is uniformly distributed modulo
1
.
A much finer question asks for the
gap distribution
of

\{n^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}\}_{n}
; that is after ordering the values

n^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}
with
n\in[N,2N)
in increasing order we ask for the gap distribution (at the scale
1/N
) between the ordered values. A striking folklore conjecture in this area is the following.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\alpha>0
be non-integer.
Then the gap distribution of

\{n^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}\}_{n}
exists.
Furthermore the gap distribution is
exponential if and only if
\alpha\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

The only case in which this conjecture is known is for
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
, a result of Elkies-McMullen
[
3
]
. For the remaining exponents
\alpha
it is confirmed that the pair correlation is Poissonian when
\alpha<\tfrac{43}{117}

(see the work
of Shubin and
the first named author
[
12
]

and previous work of Sourmelidis, Lutsko
and the second named author
[
6
]
),
and that higher correlations are Poissonian when
\alpha

shrinks to zero (see
[
7
,
8
]
) or
when
\alpha
is randomized
and large (see
[
13
]
).
Coincidentally the pair correlation of

\{\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1\}_{n}
is also Poissonian
after removal of squares
[
2
]
;
however higher correlations do not exist
even after removal of the squares.
We refer to
[
11
]
for related pigeon-hole statistics.

The proof of Elkies and McMullen relies on a connection with ergodic theory; their proof crucially uses a special case of Ratner’s theorem (see
[
1
]
for an effective version in this special case). In this paper we aim to give a new proof of the Elkies-McMullen theorem based on the circle method.
The main disadvantage of our method is that identifying the limit explicitly requires an additional effort (that we do not undertake here); however implicit in the proof is a way to rigorously evaluate the limit to any required precision. In particular one could verify with a computer calculation that the limit is indeed not exponential.

Theorem 1
.

Let
N\geq 1
.
Let
\delta
be the Dirac delta function
at the origin. Let
\{s_{n,N}\}_{N\leq n\leq 2N}

denote the (numerical) ordering of

\{\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1\}_{N\leq n\leq 2N}
. Then, the measure

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N\leq n<2N}\delta(N(s_{n+1,N}-s_{n,N}))

converges weakly to a limit distribution
P(u)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
, in other words, for every smooth compactly supported
f
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N\leq n<2N}f(N(s_{n+1,N}-s_{n,N}))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(u)P(u)du.

Elkies and McMullen’s original result considers gaps with
n<N
, we choose to consider the case
N\leq n<2N
because it avoids some superficial technicalities.
There are some apparently new results that one can obtain with our method, for example we can show the existence
of an explicit irrational
\alpha
such that the distribution of
\alpha\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}
is Poisson along a subsequence. This partially addresses a question left open by Elkies and McMullen, who asked for the existence of an irrational
\alpha
such that the distribution of
\alpha\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}
is Poisson along
all
subsequences. We plan to investigate any further possible applications at a later time.

1.1.
Notations and conventions

Throughout for a Schwartz function
f
we define

\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e(-\xi x)dx

and we set
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
.
We also write
\|x\|
to denote the reduction of
x
modulo
1
, i.e
\|x\|:=x\,\mathrm{mod}\,{1}\in[0,1)
.
Given two integers
a,q\geq 1
,
we denote by
(a,q)
and
[a,q]
the greatest common
divisor and the least common multiple, respectively.
We will frequently abbreviate the summation constraints

1\leq a\leq q
and
(a,q)=1
by simply writing
(a,q)=1
,
for example

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}a=\sum_{(a,q)=1}a.

1.2.
Outline of proof

By a standard reduction (that we recall in the appendix),
to prove that the gap distribution exists,
it suffices to show that the so-called

void statistic

\mathcal{V}(\cdot,N)

at time
N
has a limiting behavior (as
N\rightarrow\infty
)
where

\mathcal{V}(s,N):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)=0)dx

and
\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)
counts the number of

\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1
in
[x,x+s/N)
, with
N\leq n<2N
.

Proposition 1
.

If for every
s>0
the limit

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}(s,N)

exists then Theorem
1
holds.

The main observation driving our approach is that
\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)
can be efficiently understood when
x
belongs to the so-called
minor arcs

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,q)=1\\
q\asymp\Delta\sqrt{N}\end{subarray}}\big(\frac{a}{q}-\frac{1}{N},\frac{a}{q}+\frac{1}{N}\Big)

with
\Delta
a large fixed constant. The measure of this set converges to
1
as
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
.
Our first move is to effectuate a change of measure,
replacing Lebesgue measure by a measure
\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}
supported on minor arcs.
Given a smooth non-negative function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
compactly supported in
[-\tfrac{1}{100},\tfrac{1}{100}]
and
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)dx=1
,
we choose

d\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}(x):=\frac{N}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,q)=1\\
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}\end{subarray}}\phi\Big(N\Big(x-\frac{a}{q}\Big)\Big)dx

and where,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}:=\Big\{ab\in[Q,2Q]:Q^{1/1000}\leq a\leq 2Q^{1/1000},p|ab\implies p>\Delta^{2001},\mu^{2}(ab)=1\Big\}

with
Q:=\Delta\sqrt{N}
. The function
\phi
is considered globally fixed, for example
\phi(x)=c\exp(-(x-1/100)^{2})\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq 1/100}
with an appropriate constant
c
.
The bilinear structure of
\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}
will be useful later. The proposition below is a nearly immediate consequence of Jutila’s version of the circle method
[
5
]
in the form from
[
10
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
N,\Delta>10

be such that
\Delta^{2001}\leq\log N
.
The restricted void statistic

\mathcal{V}(s,N;\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)=0)\,d\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}(x)

approximates the void statistic
with the following quality:

|\mathcal{V}(s,N)-\mathcal{V}(s,N;\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}})|\ll\frac{\log\Delta}{\Delta}

where the
implied constant is absolute.

At the level of precision that we require it is easier to study a smoothed version of
\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)
.
Hence, we consider

R_{\Phi,V}(x;N):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}V\Big(\frac{n}{N}\Big)\Phi(N\|\sqrt{n}-x\|)

where
\Phi,V
are smooth functions
and
\|x\|:=x\,\mathrm{mod}\,1
is the reduction of
x
modulo
1
. The next proposition shows that we can introduce such a smoothing.

Proposition 3
.

Let
s>0
,
\eta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{100})
and
N>100/\eta
be given.
Take any smooth,

0\leq\Phi,V\leq 1

equal to one on respectively
(\eta,s)
and
(1,2)
and compactly supported in respectively
(0,s+\eta)
and
(1-\eta,2+\eta)
.
Then, for any measure
\mu
, with
0\leq d\mu\leq dx
,

|\mathcal{V}(s,N;\mu)-\mathcal{V}_{\Phi,V}(N;\mu)|\leq 8(1+s)\eta,

where

\mathcal{V}_{\Phi,V}(N;\mu):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}(R_{\Phi,V}(x;N)=0)d\mu(x).

In the next proposition we show that the difference between the
\limsup
and the
\liminf
of the smoothed void statistic restricted to the measure
\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}(x)
shrinks as
\Delta
goes to infinity.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\eta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{100})
and
\Delta,s>0
be given.
Let
0\leq V,\Phi\leq 1
be smooth functions, equal to one on
respectively
(1,2)
and
(\eta,s)
and compactly supported in respectively
(1-\eta,2+\eta)
and
(0,s+\eta)
. Then,

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}_{\Phi,V}(N;\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}})-\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}_{\Phi,V}(N;\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}})=O_{\Phi,V}(\Delta^{-10})+O(\eta).

Combining the above three propositions yields

\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}(s,N)-\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}(s,N)=O_{\Phi,\Delta}(\Delta^{-1/2})+O((1+s)\eta).

Taking
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
and then
\eta\rightarrow 0
shows that the limit of
\mathcal{V}(s,N)
exists for each
s>0
, and thus Theorem
1
follows from Proposition
2
. Notice that since
\Phi,V
depend on
\eta
we cannot take the limit in the opposite order (
\eta
first,
\Delta
later). We describe this deduction again and in more detail in Section
8
.
Proposition
4
is the core of the proof. The proof of Proposition
4
relies on two results that we now describe.

As hinted earlier,

R_{\Phi,V}(x;N)
admits a closed formula when
x
lies in the “minor arcs”.

Proposition 5
.

Let
N,\Delta>10
such that
\Delta^{2001}\leq\log N
be given. Let
A>10
.
Let
0\leq\Phi,V\leq 1
be non-zero,
smooth, compactly supported functions with
V
compactly supported
in
(1/2,3)
.
Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
|N\theta|\leq 1/100
.
Then for any
(a,q)=1
and
q\in[\Delta\sqrt{N},2\Delta\sqrt{N}]

such that all prime factors of
q
are larger than
\Delta^{2001}
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle R_{\Phi,V}\Big(\frac{a}{q}-\theta;N\Big)=\widehat{\Phi}(0)\widehat{V}(0)

\displaystyle+2\widetilde{R}_{\Phi,V}\Big(\frac{a}{q}-\theta;N\Big)+O_{A,\Phi,V}(\Delta^{-A}),

where

\widetilde{R}_{\Phi,V}\Big(\frac{a}{q}-\theta;N\Big):=\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{B}_{a,\Delta,q}}e\Big(-\frac{\overline{q}^{2}u^{2}}{4v}+\frac{u^{2}}{4vq^{2}}\Big)F_{\Phi,V,N\theta}\Big(\frac{v}{\sqrt{N}},\frac{u}{q/\sqrt{N}}\Big),

the set
\mathcal{B}_{a,\Delta,q}

is defined by

\mathcal{B}_{a,\Delta,q}:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:1\leq|u|\leq\Delta^{4},\,u\equiv 2va\,\mathrm{mod}\,{q}\},

and the weight function

F_{\Phi,V,\theta}
is given by

F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Phi}(2\xi x)e(2\xi\theta x)xV(x^{2})e\Big(-\frac{\eta x}{2}\Big)dx.

Furthermore, we have the bound,

\widetilde{R}_{\Phi,V}\Big(\frac{a}{q}-\theta;N\Big)\ll_{\Phi,V}\Delta^{4}.

Some remarks are in order.

Remark 1
.

a

(a)

First, we claim that if

(u,v)\in\mathcal{B}_{a,\Delta,q}

then

v
is coprime to
q

and therefore the inverse
\overline{q}

modulo
4v
exists.
To see this notice that
if
d=(v,q)

then
d\mid u
since
u\equiv 2va\,\mathrm{mod}\,q
and in particular
d\leq|u|\leq\Delta^{4}
.
However since
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}
all prime
factors of
q
are larger than
\Delta^{2001}
, thus
if
d>1
then
d\geq\Delta^{2001}

contradicting

d\leq|u|\leq\Delta^{4}
.
So,
(v,q)=1
. Trivially, we also have
u,v\neq 0
.

(b)

Furthermore, notice that the formula
in Proposition
5

gives a quick algorithm for determining
\mathcal{N}(x;s,N)
for
x
in the minor arcs. Trivially the sum contains
\ll\Delta^{O(1)}
terms.

In the next proposition we compute moments of
\tilde{R}_{\Phi,V}
with respect to
d\mu_{N,\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}}(x)
. Without this restriction the moments would be divergent for high enough
k
. Instead thanks to the change of measure the
k
th moment is
\ll\Delta^{4k}
. Finally, the bilinear structure of
\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}
is used here to obtain cancellations in certain exponential sums through a form of Weyl differencing.

Proposition 6
.

Let
0\leq\Phi,V\leq 1
be non-zero, smooth and compactly supported functions.
Assume that
V
supported in
(1/2,3)
. Let
w
be a smooth function, compactly supported in
(-1/2,1/2)
with
w(0)=1
.
Let
\Delta,N>10
be such that
\Delta^{2001}<\log N
.
Let
\phi
be a smooth, non-negative function, compactly supported in
[-1/100,1/100]
and normalized
so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)dx=1
.
Write

\bm{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\ ,\ \bm{u}:=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})

and let
\widetilde{R}_{\Phi,V}(x;N)
be as in Proposition
5
.
Then, for every fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{N}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,q)=1\\
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{R}_{\Phi,V}\Big(\frac{a}{q}-\theta;N\Big)^{k}\phi(N\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3\Delta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\Delta}^{2\Delta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u},\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\\
\langle\bm{1},\bm{u}\rangle=0=\langle\bm{u},\bm{t}\rangle\\
\forall i:1\leq|u_{i}|\leq\Delta^{4}\end{subarray}}H_{\Phi,V,k,\theta,\Delta}\Big(\frac{\bm{t}}{v},\frac{\bm{u}}{v}\Big)\frac{dv}{v^{k-1}}\phi(\theta)d\theta+O_{\Phi,V,k,\varepsilon}(N^{-1/8000+\varepsilon})

for any
\varepsilon>0
, and
where
F_{\Phi,V,\theta}
is defined in Proposition
5
, and

H_{\Phi,V,k,\theta,\Delta}(\bm{t},\bm{u}):=\int_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi_{i},u_{i})e(-\langle\bm{t},\bm{\xi}\rangle)w(\Delta^{3}\bm{\xi})d\bm{\xi}

where
w(\bm{\xi}):=w(\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{k})
.

2.
Preliminary Lemmas

Here we collect a number of standard lemmas.
First we quote a refined version of Jutila’s circle method from
[
10
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Delta,N\geq 10
and set
Q:=\Delta\sqrt{N}
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of integers contained in
[Q,2Q]
. Suppose that
\phi

is a non-negative smooth function compactly supported in
[-1/100,1/100]
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)dx=1
. Set

\widetilde{\chi}(\alpha):=\frac{N}{L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,q)=1\\
q\in\mathcal{Q}\end{subarray}}\phi\Big(N\Big(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Big)\Big)\ ,\ L:=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\varphi(q).

Then, for any given
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{0}^{1}|1-\widetilde{\chi}(\alpha)|^{2}d\alpha\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{Q^{4}}{\Delta^{2}L^{2}}+\frac{Q^{2+\varepsilon}}{L^{2}}.

Proof.

A full proof is given in
[
10
, Lemma 1]
. We summarize it here for the readers convenience.
First, by Poisson summation,

1-\widetilde{\chi}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\widehat{\phi}\Big(\frac{\ell}{N}\Big)e(-\ell\alpha)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}c_{q}(\ell)

where

c_{q}(\ell):=\sum_{(a,q)=1}e\Big(\frac{a\ell}{q}\Big)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|q\\
d|\ell\end{subarray}}\mu\Big(\frac{q}{d}\Big)

denotes a Ramanujan sum. By Plancherel,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|1-\widetilde{\chi}(\alpha)|^{2}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\Big|\widehat{\phi}\Big(\frac{\ell}{N}\Big)\Big|^{2}\cdot\Big(\sum_{d|\ell}\sum_{dq\in\mathcal{Q}}\mu(q)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\Big|\widehat{\phi}\Big(\frac{\ell}{N}\Big)\Big|^{2}\cdot\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|\ell\\
d\leq 2Q\end{subarray}}\frac{Q}{d}\Big)^{2}.

The result then follows upon estimating the above sum.
∎

We will frequently appeal to the following form of the Poisson summation formula.

Lemma 2

(Poisson summation)
.

Let
q\geq 1
.
Let
f
be a Schwartz function and
K
be any

q
-periodic function.
Then,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}K(n)f(n)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{f}\Big(\frac{\ell}{m}\Big)(\mathcal{F}_{q}^{-1}K)(\ell)

where

(\mathcal{F}_{q}^{-1}K)(\ell):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{x\,\mathrm{mod}\,{q}}K(x)e\Big(\frac{\ell x}{q}\Big)

is the inverse of the discrete Fourier transform modulo
m
.

In order to use Poisson summation effectively
we also need two auxiliary results.

Lemma 3
.

Let
v\neq 0
and let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth compactly support Schwarz function. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e(-vy^{2})f(y)dy=\frac{e(-\tfrac{\mathrm{sgn}(v)}{8})}{\sqrt{2|v|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e\Big(\frac{\xi^{2}}{4v}\Big)\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi.

Proof.

First assume
v>0
. Pick an
\varepsilon>0
.
We apply Plancherel to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e(-vy^{2})e^{-\varepsilon y^{2}}e^{\varepsilon y^{2}}f(y)dy.

By completing the square we find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e(-vy^{2})e^{-\varepsilon y^{2}}e(-\xi y)dy

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{(2\pi i\xi)^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+2\pi iv)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-(\varepsilon+2\pi iv)\cdot(y+\frac{2\pi i\xi}{2\varepsilon+4\pi iv})^{2}}dy

\displaystyle=e\Big(\frac{2\pi i\xi^{2}}{4(\varepsilon+2\pi iv)}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon+2\pi iv}}.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
implies the claim.
To prove the result for
v<0
it suffices to take the conjugate on both sides of the equation that
we just established in the case
v>0
.
∎

Next, we record a classical result on Gauss sums
which is the discrete analogue of Lemma
3
.

Lemma 4

(Evaluating quadratic Gauss sums)
.

For integer
v>0
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\mathcal{G}(u;4v):=\sum_{0\leq x<4v}e\Big(\frac{x^{2}+ux}{4v}\Big)=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{4v}e\Big(\frac{1}{8}\Big)e\Big(-\frac{(u/2)^{2}}{4v}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{2\mathbb{Z}}(u).

Proof.

First, we suppose that
u
is even.
Completing the square yields

\mathcal{G}(u;4v)=e(-(u/2)^{2}/(4v))\mathcal{G}(0;4v).

Thanks to
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
we know

\mathcal{G}(0;4v)=(1+i)\sqrt{4v}
.
Notice
(1+i)=\sqrt{2}e(\tfrac{1}{8})
.
Now let
u
be odd. Choose
\nu\geq 2

so that
4v/2^{\nu}
is an odd integer.
Then
x^{2}+ux
runs through all even residue classes modulo
2^{\nu}
exactly twice. By geometric summation,

\mathcal{G}(u;2^{\nu})=0
.
By multiplicativity

\mathcal{G}(u;4v)=0
.
∎

We need the following auxiliary results on generalized Gauss sums

\mathcal{G}(a,b;c):=\sum_{x\,\mathrm{mod}\,c}e\Big(\frac{ax^{2}+bx}{c}\Big).

If the leading coefficient
a=1
, then we simply write
\mathcal{G}(b;c):=\mathcal{G}(1,b;c)
.
Later-on we need:

Lemma 5
.

If
d:=\gcd(a,c)>1
, then
\mathcal{G}(a,b;c)=0
as long as
d\nmid b
.

Proof.

Write
a=da^{\prime}
, and
c=dc^{\prime}
. We parameterize
0\leq x<c
as
x=jc^{\prime}+r

with
0\leq j<\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}=d
and
0\leq r<c^{\prime}
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(a,b;c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<d}\sum_{0\leq r<c^{\prime}}e\Big(\frac{a^{\prime}(jc^{\prime}+r)^{2}}{c^{\prime}}+\frac{b(jc^{\prime}+r)}{c}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<d}\sum_{0\leq r<c^{\prime}}e\Big(\frac{a^{\prime}r^{2}}{c^{\prime}}+\frac{bj}{d}+\frac{br}{c}\Big).

Interchanging the order of summation and using that
d\nmid b
, we
see that the right hand side vanishes.
∎

Throughout we will need a good understanding of the decay of the function
F_{\Phi,V,\theta}
. The subsequent lemma will be often used.

Lemma 6
.

Let
0\leq V\leq 1
be a smooth function compactly supported in
(1/2,3)
and
0\leq\Phi\leq 1
a smooth function with compact support.
Let

F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Phi}(2\xi x)e(2\xi\theta x)xV(x^{2})e\Big(-\frac{\eta x}{2}\Big)dx.

We have, for any
A>10
and
\ell\geq 0
,

\frac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial^{\ell}\xi}F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta)\ll_{A,\ell,\Phi}\frac{1+|\theta|^{\ell}}{1+|\xi|^{A}}

and for
|\eta|>50|\xi\theta|
,

F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta)\ll_{A,\Phi,V}\frac{1+|\xi|^{A}}{1+|\eta|^{A}}.

Finally, we have the trivial bound,

\frac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial^{\ell}\xi}\frac{\partial^{v}}{\partial^{v}\eta}F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta)\ll_{\Phi,V,\ell,v}1+|\theta|^{\ell}.

Proof.

The first bound follows by noticing that
V(x)
localizes
x\asymp 1
, and thus, inside the integral,

|\widehat{\Phi}(2\xi x)|\ll_{A,\Phi}\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{A}}

for any given
A>10
. The second bound follows from integrating by parts the phase
e(2\xi\theta x-\eta x/2)
. This yields,

|F_{\Phi,V,\theta}(\xi,\eta)|\ll_{A,\Phi,V}\frac{1+|\xi|^{A}}{1+|2\xi\theta-\eta|^{A}}

the result then follows from the assumption that
|\eta|>50|\xi\theta|
.
∎

Finally, we will need to understand averages over
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}
weighted by
\varphi(q)
. This is the content of the next Lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\Delta,N>10
with
\Delta^{2001}\leq\log N
. We

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta,N}\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q^{1/1000}\leq a\leq 2Q^{1/1000}\\
p|a\implies p>\Delta^{2001}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(a)\varphi(a)}{a}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\

f^{\star}(p^{2})=-1+\frac{1}{p}

f^{\star}(p^{k})=0

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\

We show here that the existence of a the gap distribution of a sequence follows from the existence of the void statistic.
Specifically that Proposition
1
implies the main theorem.
This is basically already shown in
[
9
]
. Here we recall his proof. First we note that the sequence of measures

F_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N\leq n<2N}\delta(N(s_{n+1,N}-s_{n,N}))

is tight; this follows simply because the first moment is uniformly bounded,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{N\leq n<2N}(N(s_{n+1,N}-s_{n,N}))\leq 1.

As a result the sequence of measures
F_{N}
is relatively compact. Therefore for any subsequence
N_{k}
we can find a convergent subsequence
\{N_{k}^{\prime}\}\subset\{N_{k}\}
such that
F_{N_{k}^{\prime}}
converges weakly to a limit
P^{\star}
. We aim to show that all limits
P^{\star}
coincide, as a result the entire sequence of measures
F_{N}
is weakly convergent.

Suppose to the contrary that we can find two subsequences
\{M_{k}\}
and
\{M_{k}^{\prime}\}
such that
F_{M_{k}}
converges weakly to
P_{1}
while
F_{M_{k}^{\prime}}
converges weakly to
P_{2}
. Since
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are assumed not to coincide we can find a smooth compactly supported function
f
such that,

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,dF_{M_{k}}(x)\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,dP_{1}(x)\neq\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,dP_{2}(x)\leftarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,dF_{M_{k}^{\prime}}(x).

Since the set of functions spanned
by linear combinations of

g_{L}(x):=\max(x,L)
is dense in

[0,\infty]
there exists an
L
such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)\,dP_{1}(x)\neq\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)\,dP_{2}(x).

By the computation in
[
9
]
we also have,

\frac{1}{M_{k}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{N}(x;L,M_{k})=0)\,dx\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)dP_{1}(x)

and

\frac{1}{M_{k}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{N}(x;L,M_{k}^{\prime})=0)\,dx\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)dP_{2}(x).

However Proposition
1
shows that the limit of the void statistic exists, so we get,

\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)\,dP_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{L}(x)\,dP_{2}(x),

a contradiction.
