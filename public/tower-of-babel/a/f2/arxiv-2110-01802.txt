Rigidity, weak mixing, and recurrence in abelian groups
Ethan M. Ackelsberg

Abstract
. The focus of this paper is the phenomenon of rigidity for measure-preserving actions of countable discrete abelian groups and its interactions with weak mixing and recurrence. We prove that results about ℤ \mathbb{Z} -actions extend to this setting: 1. If ( a n ) (a_{n}) is a rigidity sequence for an ergodic measure-preserving system, then it is a rigidity sequence for some weakly mixing system. 2. There exists a sequence ( r n ) (r_{n}) such that every translate is both a rigidity sequence and a set of recurrence. The first of these results was shown for ℤ \mathbb{Z} -actions by Adams [ 1 ] , Fayad and Thouvenot [ 20 ] , and Badea and Grivaux [ 2 ] . The latter was established in ℤ \mathbb{Z} by Griesmer [ 23 ] . While techniques for handling ℤ \mathbb{Z} -actions play a key role in our proofs, additional ideas must be introduced for dealing with groups with multiple generators. As an application of our results, we give several new constructions of rigidity sequences in torsion groups. Some of these are parallel to examples of rigidity sequences in ℤ \mathbb{Z} , while others exhibit new phenomena.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the phenomenon of
rigidity
for measure-preserving actions of abelian groups.
We are particularly interested in how rigidity interacts with
weak mixing
and
recurrence
.
The interplay between these concepts has been examined in depth for the group of integers
(see, for example,
[
8
,
1
,
20
,
2
,
23
,
19
]
).
These properties have been explored separately for more general groups:
mild mixing, a notion complementary to rigidity, is explored in
[
38
,
37
]
;
general remarks on weak mixing can be found in
[
18
,
12
]
;
and
[
21
]
gives an overview of recurrence.
However, nontrivial relationships between them have not been established outside of the group of integers.
Moving to the context of general countable discrete abelian groups poses new challenges.
For instance, the group may be infinitely generated and contain torsion elements,
both of which can create difficulties in applying techniques from ergodic theory.
Nevertheless, in what follows, we show that several of the known results for
\mathbb{Z}
-actions
have natural extensions to the context of abelian groups.

We give special attention to
\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, the direct sum of countably many copies of a finite field,
for constructing novel examples of rigidity sequences.
(We use
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to denote the finite field as well as its additive group.)
On the one hand, we can view
\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
as the additive group of the ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
,
which allows us to construct number-theoretically inspired rigidity sequences.
Namely, we construct rigidity sequences out of finite characteristic versions of
continued fraction expansions and Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers.
These objects are nontrivial to define in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
,
but they can be considered as natural analogues to their counterparts in
\mathbb{Z}
.
On the other hand,
\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is an infinitely-generated torsion group,
and these properties can be exploited to construct rigidity sequences
that are dissimilar from any known constructions in
\mathbb{Z}
.

The general results relating rigidity to weak mixing and recurrence are explained below,
after providing the necessary background.

1.1.
Rigidity sequences

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
By a
measure-preserving system
(or
system
for short), we will mean a quadruple

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,\right.\penalty\left.(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
,
where
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a non-atomic Lebesgue probability space,
and
(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
is an action of
\Gamma
by measure-preserving transformations.
A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
is a
rigidity sequence
for the system

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
if for every
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,
\|f\circ T_{a_{n}}-f\|_{2}\to 0
.
In this case, we also say that the system is
rigid along the sequence

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
or that
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
rigid
for
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
.

1.2.
Rigidity and weak mixing

Rigidity stands in contrast to mixing.
Whereas in rigid systems, the transformations
(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
converge to the identity along some sequence,
actions with mixing properties produce asymptotic independence.
To make sense of asymptotic behavior, we need the notion of a
Følner sequence
.
A sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite subsets of
\Gamma
is a
Følner sequence

if for every
x\in\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\frac{|(\Phi_{N}+x)\triangle\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.

Examples include the sequence of intervals
\Phi_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
in
\mathbb{Z}

and the sequence
\Phi_{N}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
in
\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.
Groups that admit a Følner sequence are called
amenable
,
and it is well-known that abelian groups are amenable.

Recall that a system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
is
ergodic

it there are no non-trivial invariant sets.
That is, if
T_{g}A=A
for every
g\in\Gamma
, then
\mu(A)=0
or
\mu(A)=1
.
By the mean ergodic theorem (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 4.15]
),
ergodicity can be expressed as a mixing property:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{g\in\Phi_{N}}{\mu(A\cap T_{g}B)}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\mu(A)\mu(B)

for every Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
and all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
.

A stronger property that we will deal with is
weak mixing
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{g\in\Phi_{N}}{\left|\mu(A\cap T_{g}B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)\right|}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0

for every Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
and all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
.
This is equivalent to the property:
there is an exceptional set
E\subseteq\Gamma
of
zero density
, meaning

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\frac{|E\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}}=0

for every Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
,
such that
\mu(A\cap T_{g}B)\to\mu(A)\mu(B)
as
g\to\infty,g\notin E
.
Hence, rigidity sequences for weakly mixing systems, if they exist at all, must be relatively sparse:
they are (up to a finite initial segment) contained in the exceptional set
E
of zero density.
Nevertheless, a generic (in the sense of category) measure-preserving automorphism
is simultaneously weakly mixing (see
[
24
]
) and rigid (see
[
8
, Proposition 2.9]
).
So rigid weakly mixing
\mathbb{Z}
-actions are plentiful.

This does not,
a priori
, say anything about which sequences arise
as rigidity sequences for weakly mixing systems.
However, Adams
[
1
]
, Fayad and Thouvenot
[
20
]
, and Badea and Grivaux
[
2
]

showed that, in the case of
\mathbb{Z}
-actions, every possible rigidity sequence for an ergodic measure-preserving
transformation arises as a rigidity sequence for a weakly mixing transformation.
We extend this result to our setting:

Theorem A
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group,
and let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\Gamma
.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for an ergodic measure-preserving system,
then it is a rigidity sequence for some weakly mixing system.

Ideas from
[
20
]
and
[
2
]
turn out to be apt for generalization and are instrumental to our proof.
Because of this theorem, we will use the standalone term
rigidity sequence

to mean a sequence that is rigid for some weakly mixing system.
Theorem
A
is an extremely useful tool for constructing examples of rigidity sequences.
We explore this in Section
4
.

One weakness of our notion of rigidity used here is that “multi-dimensional” groups such as

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
will possess many rigidity sequences coming from lower-dimensional subgroups.
As an illustration of this phenomenon, given an ergodic automorphism
S:X\to X
,
we can define a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action by
T_{(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})}:=S^{n_{1}}
.
So long as
S
is rigid along
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the sequence

(a_{n},b^{(2)}_{n},\dots,b^{(d)}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will be a rigidity sequence in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

for every collection of sequences
(b^{(i)}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

A natural way of avoiding these degenerated examples is to impose one additional condition.
A measure-preserving system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
is
free

if for every
g\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
,
\mu(\{x\in X:T_{g}x=x\})=0
.
If
T
is ergodic, then this is equivalent to the seemingly weaker condition that
T
is
faithful
:

\mu(\{x\in X:T_{g}x\neq x\})>0
for every
g\in\Gamma
.
This is because the fixed points of a given automorphism
T_{g}
are
T
-invariant.
As a consequence, every ergodic
\mathbb{Z}
-action on a Lebesgue space is automatically free,
so this assumption is superfluous in
\mathbb{Z}
.
However, the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action above is ergodic but not free, and this is what produces the degeneration.
We can extend Theorem
A
to free actions as follows:

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for a free ergodic measure-preserving system,
then it is a rigidity sequence for some free weakly mixing system.

1.3.
Rigidity and recurrence

In light of Theorem
A
, it is natural to ask what can be said about rigidity
for systems that do not exhibit weak mixing.
In particular, is every rigidity sequence rigid for a system with
discrete spectrum
?
(Readers unfamiliar with discrete spectrum systems can turn to Section
2.2

for a definition and several equivalent characterizations.
Included therein is a brief discussion of how discrete spectrum can be considered as a
complementary property to weak mixing.)
This was posed as an open question in
[
8
]

and answered negatively by Fayad and Kanigowski
[
19
]
in
\mathbb{Z}
.
A different construction due to Griesmer
[
23
]
gives an even stronger result
involving the phenomenon of
recurrence
.
One advantage of Griesmer’s approach is that it is generalizable to our setting.
To state the result, we need two more definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
A set
R\subseteq\Gamma
is a
set of recurrence

if for every measure-preserving system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)

and every
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)>0
,
there is an
r\in R\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mu\left(A\cap T_{r}^{-1}A\right)>0
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
A sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
is
rigid-recurrent
if
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence
and
R=\{r_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a set of recurrence.

Using key insights from
[
23
]
, we prove the following (c.f.
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
):

Theorem C
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
There exists a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
such that, for every
t\in\Gamma
,

(r_{n}-t)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigid-recurrent sequence.

One consequence of Theorems
C
is the following complement
to Theorems
A
, which we prove in Section
6
:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
There is a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
such that
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence
for a weakly mixing system but not for any ergodic system with discrete spectrum.

Remark 1.4
.

Though it is not stated explicitly,
Corollary
1.3
also follows from the work of Badea, Grivaux, and Matheron in
[
3
]
.
In particular, they establish, for every discrete abelian group,
the existence of a rigidity sequence that is dense in the Bohr topology
(see
[
3
, Section 5b.2]
).
This is a weaker result than Theorem
C
but sufficient to prove Corollary
1.3
.

One may ask whether a version of Theorem
C
holds if we impose a freeness condition.
Call a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
a
freely rigid-recurrent sequence
if
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a rigidity sequence for a free action of
\Gamma
and
\{r_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a set of recurrence.
In contrast to the situation above, where Theorem
A
extended directly to Theorem
B
,
it is not possible in general to construct a sequence such that every translate is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.
Indeed, we will show that for any rigidity sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for a free action of the group

(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
, the projection of
a_{n}
onto
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})

must be zero for all but finitely many
n
(see Proposition
7.1
for a more general statement).
It follows that translates by elements of the form
t=(t_{0};t_{1},t_{2},\dots)
with
t_{0}\in(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\setminus\{0\}

can never be rigidity sequences for free actions of
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
.

However, for this group and other similar groups,
we show that this obstacle can be overcome by restricting to translates coming from a finite index subgroup.
Recall that the
exponent
of an abelian group
(\Gamma,+)
is the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that
nx=0
for every
x\in\Gamma
.
If no such
n
exists, the group is said to have infinite exponent.
Every countable abelian group with finite exponent can be expressed as a direct sum of cyclic groups

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}{(\mathbb{Z}/r_{k}\mathbb{Z})^{m_{k}}}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}{\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\right)}

with
q_{1},\dots,q_{N},r_{1},\dots,r_{M}
distinct prime powers and
m_{1},\dots,m_{M}\in\mathbb{N}

(see, e.g.,
[
22
, Theorem 17.2]
).
If all of the numbers
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
are prime, then we have the following result:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Delta=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}{\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/p_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\right)}
with
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
prime.
Suppose
\Gamma=\Delta\oplus F
, where
F
is a finite abelian group.
Then there exists a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
for every
s\in\Delta
,
(r_{n}-s)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.

More generally, we conjecture that the following is true:

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group.
Then there exists a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a finite index subgroup
\Delta\leq\Gamma
such that
for every
s\in\Delta
,
(r_{n}-s)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.

In Section
7.2
, we reduce Conjecture
1.6

to a statement about the existence of certain thin sets in compact abelian groups.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
, we introduce definitions and review basic facts
about weakly mixing and discrete spectrum measure-preserving systems.
We also show that, with the help of Gaussian systems,
rigidity can be characterized in terms of Fourier transforms of measures
on the Pontryagin dual group of
\Gamma
.
This will allow us to prove results about rigidity by constructing measures,
rather than producing measure-preserving systems directly.

From there, the paper is broken into two main parts.
In the first part, consisting of Sections
3
and
4
,
we prove Theorem
A
and draw out several consequences.
Of particular note, we prove in Section
4
that some sequences arising from number theory
(namely, denominators of convergents for continued fractions and powers of Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers)
are rigidity sequences for weakly mixing actions in
\mathbb{Z}
and in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
.
The fact that continued fraction denominators are rigidity sequences in
\mathbb{Z}

was shown in
[
8
, Corollary 3.51]
.
Badea and Grivaux observed that a version of Theorem
A
in
\mathbb{Z}

provides a more streamlined proof (see
[
2
, Section 6.3]
),
We can similarly verify that our constructions are rigidity sequences using Theorem
A
.

The second part, consisting of Sections
5
,
6
, and
7

is devoted to proving Theorem
C
and exploring Conjecture
1.6
.
Two of the main ingredients for the proof of Theorem
C

are discussed in Section
5
:
(1) a well-known characterization of sets of recurrence in terms of sets of differences
via the Furstenberg correspondence principle, and
(2) the phenomenon of uniformity of recurrence.
We give a new proof of uniformity of recurrence for abelian groups in Section
5.4
.
(This is of independent interest but is not directly needed for Theorem
C
.)
Using these tools, we give the proof of Theorem
C
in Section
6
,
based on a generalization of a construction due to Griesmer
[
23
]
.
Finally, in Section
7
, we prove Theorem
1.5

and reduce Conjecture
1.6
to a problem in harmonic analysis.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this section, let
\Gamma
be a fixed countable discrete abelian group.

2.1.
Spectral measures

Discrete spectrum and weak mixing are both
spectral properties
of measure-preserving systems.
By this, we mean that these properties can be characterized in terms of the behavior
of the unitary action
f\mapsto f\circ T_{g}
on
L^{2}(\mu)
.
We will adopt a standard abuse of notation and use
T_{g}
to denote this unitary map on
L^{2}(\mu)
,
as well as the measure-preserving automorphism on
X
.

In dealing with spectral properties, it is useful to introduce
spectral measures
.
These are measures that encapsulate the behavior of the orbit of a function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)

under the action of
(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}

\mathbf{T}

\mathbb{T}

The group
\mathbf{T}

We denote by

2.2.
Discrete spectrum and weak mixing

A function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
is an
eigenfunction
for the system

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
if there is a function
\chi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}

such that
T_{g}f=\chi(g)f
for every
g\in\Gamma
.
Since
(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
is a group action,
\chi
is necessarily a character on
\Gamma
,
which we call an
eigenvalue
or
eigencharacter
.
We say the system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
has
discrete spectrum

if
L^{2}(\mu)
is spanned by eigenfunctions.

Several other characterizations of discrete spectrum systems are given by the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be an ergodic measure-preserving system.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

T
has discrete spectrum.

(ii)

There is a compact abelian group
(Y,+)
with Haar measure
\nu

on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{D}=Borel(Y)

and an action
S:\Gamma\curvearrowright Y
by group rotations
S_{g}y=y+\alpha_{g}
with
\alpha_{g}\in Y

such that
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)

and
\left(Y,\mathcal{D},\nu,(S_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)

are isomorphic as measure-preserving systems;

(iii)

For every
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
, the orbit
\{T_{g}f:g\in\Gamma\}
is totally bounded;

(iv)

For every
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
, the spectral measure

\sigma_{f}
is atomic.

(v)

The maximal spectral type is atomic.

For the details of these equivalences for
\mathbb{Z}
-actions, see
[
40
,
32
]
;
the proofs easily extend to abelian groups.

Systems with discrete spectrum are also called
compact
systems because of properties (ii) and (iii).
Complementary to discrete spectrum is the notion of weak mixing.
Recall from Section
1.2
that a system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)

is
weakly mixing
if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{g\in\Phi_{N}}{\left|\mu(A\cap T_{g}B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)\right|}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0

for every Følner sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
and every
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
.
This, too, has many equivalent characterizations, some of which we give here:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be a measure-preserving system.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is weakly mixing;

(ii)

\left(X\times X,\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B},\mu\otimes\mu,(T_{g}\times T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)

is ergodic;

(iii)

T
has no non-constant compact fuctions:
if
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and the orbit
\{T_{g}f:g\in\Gamma\}

is totally bounded, then
f
is a constant function;

(iv)

T
has no non-constant eigenfunctions;

(v)

For every
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mu)
and every Følner sequence

(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{g\in\Phi_{N}}{\left|\int_{X}{f_{1}\cdot T_{g}f_{2}~d\mu}-\int_{X}{f_{1}~d\mu}\int_{X}{f_{2}~d\mu}\right|}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0;

(vi)

For every
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
with
\int{f~d\mu}=0
,
the spectral measure
\sigma_{f}
is continuous.

(vii)

T
is ergodic and the maximal spectral type is the sum of
a continuous measure and an atom at
1\in\mathbf{T}
.

See
[
12
,
32
]
for proofs.
To get property (vi) in this generality, an abelian version of Wiener’s lemma must be used
in place of the classical Wiener’s lemma as it appears in
[
32
]
.

Weakly mixing systems are also known as systems with
continuous spectrum
because of property (vi).
In fact, weakly mixing systems were first introduced in
[
30
]
as

dynamical systems of continuous spectra
.

2.3.
Gaussian systems

In order to complete the translation between dynamical properties and properties of measures,
we need to be able to construct measure-preserving
\Gamma
-systems from measures on
\mathbf{T}
.
To this end, we review basic properties of
Gaussian systems
.

Let
\Omega
be the sequence space
\mathbb{C}^{\Gamma}
, and let
S_{g}:\Omega\to\Omega
be the shift

(S_{g}\omega)(h)=\omega(h-g)
.
Let
\pi_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
be the function
\pi_{0}(\omega):=\omega(0)
.
An
S
-invariant probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}
, the Borel subsets of
\Omega
with the product topology,
is called a
Gaussian measure
if, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\Gamma
, the tuple

\displaystyle\left(S_{g_{1}}\pi_{0},\dots,S_{g_{n}}\pi_{0}\right):(\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{\Omega},\nu)\to\mathbb{C}^{n}

follows a Gaussian distribution.

A measure-preserving system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
is a
Gaussian system

if there is a Gaussian measure
\nu
such that

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)\simeq\left(\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{\Omega},\nu,(S_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
.
A function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
is a
Gaussian element
if
f
maps to
\pi_{0}
under this isomorphism.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
be a positive definite function with
\varphi(0)=1
.
Then there exists a unique
S
-invariant symmetric Gaussian measure

\nu
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{\Omega})
with covariances determined by
\varphi
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\pi_{0}~d\nu}=\int_{\Omega}{\pi_{0}^{2}~d\nu}=0

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\overline{\pi_{0}}\cdot S_{g}\pi_{0}~d\nu}=\varphi(g)

for every
g\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as in the real-valued case.
We give a sketch of the proof for the complex case and refer the reader
to
[
29
, Appendix C]
for additional details.

Let
F\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite set.
There is a unique Gaussian measure
p_{F}
on
\mathbb{C}^{F}
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\omega(g)~dp_{F}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\omega(g)^{2}~dp_{F}}=0

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\overline{\omega(g)}\omega(h)~dp_{F}}=\varphi(g^{-1}h)

for
g,h\in F
(see
[
27
, Section 1.4]
).
By the Kolmogorov extension theorem, there is then a unique measure
\nu
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{\Omega})

such that
(\pi_{F})_{*}\nu=p_{F}
, where
\pi_{F}
is the projection
\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{F}
.
The measure
\nu
thus constructed is the desired measure.
∎

Using this theorem, we can construct a Gaussian system from any probability measure on
\mathbf{T}
:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
.
The
Gaussian system associated to
\sigma
is the system

\left(\Omega,\mathcal{B}_{\Omega},\nu_{\sigma},(S_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
,
where
\nu_{\sigma}
is the unique measure guaranteed by Theorem
2.3

for the positive definite function
g\mapsto\widehat{\sigma}(g)
.

The dynamical properties of the Gaussian system associated to a measure
\sigma

are well-understood in terms of properties of the measure.
We will make frequent use of the following two results.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
, and let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be the Gaussian system
associated to
\sigma
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is ergodic;

(ii)

T
is weakly mixing;

(iii)

\sigma
is continuous.

Proof.

See
[
14
, Section 14.2]
for a proof in the case
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
.
The general case of countable discrete abelian groups holds by the same method.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
, and let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be the Gaussian system
associated to
\sigma
.
A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for
T
if and only if
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
be a Gaussian element.
By definition, the spectral measure
\sigma_{f}
coincides with the measure
\sigma
.
This immediately gives the equivalence:
T_{a_{n}}f\to f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
if and only
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

Now, if
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence, then clearly
T_{a_{n}}f\to f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
For the converse, note that the algebra of functions generated by
\{T_{g}f:g\in\Gamma\}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
Hence, if
T_{a_{n}}f\to f
, then
T_{a_{n}}h\to h
for every
h\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
∎

2.4.
Rigidity and Fourier analysis

Using Gaussian systems, one can show that a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a rigidity sequence for some weakly mixing system if and only if there is a
continuous probability measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
such that
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.
This was observed for
\mathbb{Z}
-actions in
[
8
, Proposition 2.30]
.
Rigidity sequences for ergodic systems with discrete spectrum can also be characterized
in terms of the behavior of characters, an observation that is implicit in
[
2
]
in the context of
\mathbb{Z}
-actions.

Lemma 2.7
.

A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
is

(1)

rigid for some ergodic system with discrete spectrum if and only if
there are infinitely many characters
\chi\in\mathbf{T}
such that
\chi(a_{n})\to 1
;

(2)

rigid for some weakly mixing system if and only if there is a measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})

such that
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

Remark 2.8
.

Recall that we deal only with measure-preserving actions on non-atomic Lebesgue spaces.
For rotations on finitely many points, it is possible to have a rigidity sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

for which the set
\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is finite.
For example, the sequence
(2n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for a rotation on two points (as a
\mathbb{Z}
-system),
but
\lambda^{2n}\to 1
only for
\lambda\in\{1,-1\}
.
By assuming that we deal with non-atomic Lebesgue spaces, we rule out such trivial examples.

Proof.

(1) Let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be an ergodic measure-preserving system
with discrete spectrum that is rigid along
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
By Proposition
2.1
(ii), we may assume that
X
is a compact abelian group
and
(\alpha_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
are elements of
X
such that
T_{g}x=x+\alpha_{g}
.
Note that
g\mapsto\alpha_{g}
is a homomorphism.
Hence, for any character
\lambda\in\widehat{X}
, the function
\chi_{\lambda}(g):=\lambda(\alpha_{g})

is a character on
\Gamma
.

Now, for
\lambda\in\widehat{X}
, we have
T_{g}\lambda=\chi_{\lambda}(g)\lambda
.
Since
T
is ergodic, it has no non-trivial invariant functions, so
\chi_{\lambda}=1

if and only if
\lambda=1
.
That is, the map
\lambda\mapsto\chi_{\lambda}
is injective,
so
\left\{\chi_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\widehat{X}\right\}
is infinite.

Finally, since
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for
T
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\chi_{\lambda}(a_{n})-1\right|=\left|\lambda(\alpha_{a_{n}})-1\right|=\left\|\lambda(x+\alpha_{g})-\lambda(x)\right\|_{2}=\left\|T_{a_{n}}\lambda-\lambda\right\|_{2}\to 0.

Conversely, suppose
C=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is infinite.
Observe that
C
is a subgroup of
\mathbf{T}
.
Let
\Lambda
be a countably infinite subgroup of
C
endowed with the discrete topology,
and set
X:=\widehat{\Lambda}
.
Equip
X
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
and Haar probability measure
\mu
.
Then
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a non-atomic Lebesgue space, and
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
by

T_{g}x=e_{g}\cdot x
, where
e_{g}(\lambda):=\lambda(g)
for
\lambda\in\Lambda\subseteq\mathbf{T}
.
This is an action by group rotations, so it has discrete spectrum.
Now, for every
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
(T_{a_{n}}x)(\lambda)=\lambda(a_{n})x(\lambda)\to x(\lambda)
,
so
T_{a_{n}}x\to x
pointwise on
X
.
It follows that
\|T_{a_{n}}f-f\|_{2}\to 0
for every
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
.

(2) Let
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
be a weakly mixing system
such that
T_{a_{n}}\to\text{id}
.
Let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
with
\|f\|=1
and
\int_{X}{f~d\mu}=0
.
By Bochner’s theorem, let
\sigma_{f}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
be the spectral measure:

\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(g)=\int_{X}{\overline{f}\cdot T_{g}f~d\mu}
.
Since the action is weakly mixing,
\sigma_{f}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(a_{n})=\int_{X}{\overline{f}\cdot T_{a_{n}}f~d\mu}\to\int_{X}{|f|^{2}~d\mu}=\|f\|^{2}=1.

Conversely, suppose
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
and
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.
By Lemma
2.6
, the Gaussian system associated to
\sigma

is rigid along
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

Now Theorem
A
will follow from

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\Gamma
, and suppose that the set

\displaystyle C:=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}

is infinite.
Then there is a continuous probability measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})

such that
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

This is a direct generalization of
[
2
, Corollary 2.5]
.
We prove Theorem
2.9
in Section
3.1
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
A
and corollaries

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
A
and explore some of its consequences,
including Theorem
B
and related topological results.
The proof follows the strategy used by Fayad–Thouvenot
[
20
]
in the case
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}

(see also
[
2
, Section 4]
).
With some modifications, we are able to extend their result to the setting of general abelian groups.
As noted above, it suffices to prove Theorem
2.9
, which we do now.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.9

The basic outline of the proof is as follows.
We are given that
C=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is infinite.
From this, we deduce that
\overline{C}
is an (uncountable) compact subgroup of
\mathbf{T}
.
We then construct a sequence of discrete measures sitting on
C
.
On the one hand, we need to pick the points using a Cantor-like construction so that any limiting measure is continuous.
On the other hand, we need to ensure that convergence to 1 along the sequence

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
happens sufficiently uniformly among the chosen points from
C

so that the Fourier coefficients of the limiting measure converge to 1.

Now we proceed with the proof as outlined above.
By assumption,
C=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is infinite.
Moreover,
C
is a subgroup of
\mathbf{T}
.
Now, since
\mathbf{T}
is compact and
C
is infinite,
C
has a cluster point.
The (topological) group structure guarantees that every point of
C
is a cluster point.
This is the key property we will exploit to construct a continuous probability measure

\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
with
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

We will construct a sequence of characters
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
C
,
a sequence of nonnegative integers
(N_{p})_{p\geq 0}
, and a family of open subsets

\left(V_{p,r}\right)_{1\leq r\leq 2^{p},p\geq 0}
of
\mathbf{T}
so that the measures

\sigma_{p}=2^{-p}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{p}}{\delta_{\chi_{i}}}
have the following three properties for
p\geq 0
:

•

For
0\leq j\leq p-1
and
N_{j}\leq n\leq N_{j+1}
,

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p}(\chi)}

\displaystyle<2^{-(j-1)}.

•

For
n\geq N_{p}
,

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p}(\chi)}

\displaystyle<2^{-(p+1)}.

•

The sets
V_{p,1},\dots,V_{p,2^{p}}
are mutually disjoint,
and for
0\leq q\leq p-1
,
0\leq l\leq 2^{p-q}-1
, and
1\leq r\leq 2^{q}
,

(3)

\displaystyle\chi_{l\cdot 2^{q}+r}\in V_{p,l\cdot 2^{q}+r}

\displaystyle\subseteq V_{q,r}.

Properties (
1
) and (
2
) control the rate of
convergence of the characters along
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
so that the Fourier coefficients of a limiting measure
will converge to 1 along
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Property (
3
) ensures that the mass assigned
to a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\chi_{r}
is bounded by
2^{-q}
if
r\leq 2^{q}
,
so the limiting measure is continuous.

Now we make these claims precise.
Suppose we have constructed such sequences, and let
\sigma
be a weak-
∗

limit point of the sequence of measures
(\sigma_{p})_{p\geq 0}
.

Claim 3.1
.

\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
.

Proof of Claim.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
N_{j_{n}}\leq n<N_{j_{n}+1}
.
Then for
p>j_{n}
, property (
1
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p}(\chi)}<2^{-(j_{n}-1)}.

Since
\chi\mapsto\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|
is a continuous function on
\mathbf{T}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma(\chi)}\leq 2^{-(j_{n}-1)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

Claim 3.2
.

\sigma
is continuous.

Proof of Claim.

Fix
q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p\geq q
.
For
1\leq i\leq 2^{p}
, we can write
i=l\cdot 2^{q}+r
for some
l\geq 0
and
1\leq r\leq 2^{q}
.
By property (
3
), we have
\chi_{i}\in V_{q,r}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}(V_{q,r})=\sigma_{p}\left(\overline{V}_{q,r}\right)=2^{-p}\#\{1\leq i\leq 2^{p}:i\equiv r\pmod{2^{q}}\}=2^{-q}.

By the Portmanteau theorem (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
),
it follows that
\sigma(V_{q,r})=2^{-q}
.

Now suppose for contradiction that
\sigma
has an atom,
\sigma(\{\chi\})>0
.
Then
\chi\in V_{q,r}
for some
1\leq r\leq 2^{q}
.
Hence,
\sigma(\{\chi\})\leq\sigma(V_{q,r})=2^{-q}
.
Letting
q\to\infty
, we get
\sigma(\{\chi\})=0
, a contradiction.
Thus,
\sigma
is a continuous measure on
\mathbf{T}
.
∎

It remains to construct the sequences
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(N_{p})_{p\geq 0}
, and
(V_{p,r})_{1\leq r\leq 2^{p},p\geq 0}
.
We will construct the sequences by induction on
p
,
getting
\chi_{i}
for
2^{p-1}+1\leq i\leq 2^{p}
at each step along with
N_{p}
.

We begin with
N_{0}=0
,
\chi_{1}=1
, and
V_{0,1}=\mathbf{T}
.
Consider
p=1
.
Choose
\chi_{2}\in C,\chi_{2}\neq 1
.
Then
\sigma_{1}=\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{\chi_{2}}}{2}
, and we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{1}(\chi)}=\frac{1}{2}\left|\chi_{2}(a_{n})-1\right|\leq 1<2.

Now since
\chi_{2}\in C
, we may choose
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
so that for
n\geq N_{1}
,

\left|\chi_{2}(a_{n})-1\right|<\frac{1}{2}
.
Then for
n\geq N_{1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{1}(\chi)}=\frac{1}{2}\left|\chi_{2}(a_{n})-1\right|<2^{-2}.

Since
\chi_{1}\neq\chi_{2}
, we can find disjoint neighborhoods
V_{1,1}\ni\chi_{1}
and
V_{1,2}\ni\chi_{2}
.

Suppose we have completed step

Now we will do sub-induction on

to construct distinct characters

\chi_{2^{p}+s}\in C
, sets
V_{p+1,s}
and
V_{p+1,2^{p}+s}
, measures
\sigma_{p,s}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
,

•

For
0\leq j\leq p-1
and
N_{j}\leq n\leq N_{j+1}

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p,s}(\chi)}

\displaystyle<2^{-(j-1)}.

•

For
n\geq N_{p}

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p,s}(\chi)}

\displaystyle<2^{-(p-1)}.

•

For
n\geq N_{p,s}
,

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p,s}(\chi)}

\displaystyle<2^{-(p+2)}.

•

The sets
V_{p+1,s}
and
V_{p+1,2^{p}+s}
are disjoint subsets of
V_{p,s}
,
and for

\displaystyle\chi_{\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p}+s}

\displaystyle\in V_{p+1,\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p}+s}.

First we consider

Choose
\chi_{2^{p}+1}\in(C\cap V_{p,1})\setminus\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2^{p}}\}

Set

\displaystyle\sigma_{p,1}

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{p}+2^{-(p+1)}\left(\delta_{\chi_{2^{p}+1}}-\delta_{1}\right).

Then for

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p,1}(\chi)}\leq\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(a_{n})-1\right|~d\sigma_{p}(\chi)}+2^{-(p+1)}\left|\chi_{2^{p}+1}(a_{n})-1\right|.

)

.

, we can use the bound

.

Now suppose we have chosen
\chi_{2^{p}+s^{\prime}}

.
Let
0\leq q\leq p
,
0\leq l\leq 2^{p+1-q}-1
, and
1\leq r\leq 2^{q}
.
Write
l=l^{\prime}+\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p-q}
with
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
and
0\leq l^{\prime}\leq 2^{p-q}-1

so that
l\cdot 2^{q}+r=l^{\prime}\cdot 2^{q}+r+\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p}
.
Let
s:=l^{\prime}\cdot 2^{q}+r
.
Then
1\leq s\leq(2^{p-q}-1)2^{q}+2^{q}=2^{p}
and
l\cdot 2^{q}+r=\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p}+s
.
Thus, by property (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\chi_{l\cdot 2^{q}+r}\in V_{l\cdot 2^{q}+r}=V_{p+1,\varepsilon\cdot 2^{p}+s}\subseteq V_{p,s}.

By the induction hypothesis, we have
V_{p,s}=V_{p,l^{\prime}\cdot 2^{q}+r}\subseteq V_{q,r}
.
Thus, property (

) holds, completing the induction. ∎

3.2.
Consequences of Theorem
A

As a first consequence of Theorem
A
, we prove Theorem
B
:

See
B

We recall some basic facts about Pontryagin duality that will be used in the proof.
The
annihilator
of a set
E\subseteq\Gamma
is the closed subgroup

\displaystyle E^{\perp}:=\left\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(g)=1~\text{for all}~g\in E\right\}\leq\mathbf{T}.

Similarly, for a set
M\subseteq\mathbf{T}
, the
annihilator
is the subgroup

\displaystyle M^{\perp}:=\left\{g\in\Gamma:\chi(g)=1~\text{for all}~\chi\in M\right\}\leq\Gamma.

In general, the annihilator of the annihilator of a set is the closed subgroup generated by that set.
Moreover, given closed subgroups
\Delta\leq\Gamma
and
K\leq\mathbf{T}
with
\Delta^{\perp}=K
,
we have isomorphisms
K\simeq\widehat{\Gamma/\Delta}
and
\mathbf{T}/K\simeq\widehat{\Delta}

(see, e.g.,
[
35
, Section 2.1]
).

As a first step towards proving Theorem
B
, we need a criterion for checking that a Gaussian system is free.
For a measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
, let
G(\sigma)
be the closed subgroup generated by
\text{supp}\left(\sigma\right)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
be a probability measure.
Then

\displaystyle G(\sigma)=\{g\in\Gamma:\widehat{\sigma}(g)=1\}^{\perp}.

In particular, the Gaussian system associated to a measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
is free
if and only if
G(\sigma)=\mathbf{T}
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma
is a probability measure and

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}(g)=\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\chi(g)~d\sigma(\chi)},

we have
\widehat{\sigma}(g)=1
if and only if
\chi(g)=1
for
\sigma
-a.e.
\chi\in\mathbf{T}
.
Noting that, for any
g\in\Gamma
, the set
\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(g)=1\}
is a closed subgroup,
we have that
\widehat{\sigma}(g)=1
if and only if
\chi(g)=1
for every
\chi\in G(\sigma)
.
That is,
\{g\in\Gamma:\widehat{\sigma}(g)=1\}=G(\sigma)^{\perp}
, so we are done.
∎

Similar considerations for discrete spectrum systems produce the following:

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
is an ergodic measure-preserving system
with discrete spectrum that is rigid along a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Then

\displaystyle C:=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}

is dense in the subgroup
\{g\in\Gamma:T_{g}=\text{id}\}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbf{T}
.
In particular, if the system is free, then
C
is dense in
\mathbf{T}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{0}:=\{g\in\Gamma:T_{g}=\text{id}\}
.
If
\chi\in\mathbf{T}
is an eigenvalue for the system, then
\chi(a_{n})\to 1

(see the proof of Lemma
2.7
).
So, it suffices to check that the group of eigenvalues

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\left\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\text{there exists}~f\in L^{2}(\mu)~\text{such that}~T_{g}f=\chi(g)f~\text{for every}~g\in\Gamma\right\}

is dense in
\Gamma_{0}^{\perp}
.
Equivalently, we want to show
\Lambda^{\perp}=\Gamma_{0}
.

Let
g\in\Lambda^{\perp}
.
By definition,
\chi(g)=1
for every
\chi\in\Lambda
.
Since
L^{2}(\mu)
is spanned by eigenfunctions, it follows that
T_{g}=\text{id}
.
That is,
g\in\Gamma_{0}
.

Conversely, suppose
g\in\Gamma_{0}
.
Let
\chi\in\Lambda
, and let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
such that
T_{h}f=\chi(h)f
for every
h\in\Gamma
.
Since
T_{g}=\text{id}
, we have
T_{g}f=f
, so
\chi(g)=1
.
Thus,
g\in\Lambda^{\perp}
.
∎

Now we can prove Theorem
B
:

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Suppose
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for a free ergodicmeasure-preserving system

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
.

Rather than producing a free weakly mixing system directly,
it suffices by Lemmas
2.7
and
3.3
to construct
a probability measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
satisfying three conditions:

(1)

\sigma
is continuous;

(2)

\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
;

(3)

G(\sigma)=\mathbf{T}
.

The method for constructing such a measure depends on whether or not
the system
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
has discrete spectrum,
so we break the proof into two cases from here.

Case 1.

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
does not have discrete spectrum.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
be the maximal spectral type of
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
.
We claim
\{g\in\Gamma:\widehat{\nu}(g)=1\}=\{0\}
.
Indeed, suppose
\widehat{\nu}(g)=1
.
Then
\chi(g)=1
for
\nu
-a.e.
\chi\in\mathbf{T}
.
Hence, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
, since
\sigma_{f}\ll\nu
, we have
\chi(g)=1
for
\sigma_{f}
-a.e.
\chi\in\mathbf{T}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(g)=\|f\|_{2}^{2}
, so
T_{g}f=f
.
Since the system is free, this can only happen for
g=0
.

Decompose
\nu=t\nu_{a}+(1-t)\nu_{c}
, where
t\in(0,1)
,

\nu_{a}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
is purely atomic and
\nu_{c}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
is continuous.
We claim that the convolution
\sigma:=\nu_{a}*\nu_{c}
satisfies the desired conditions.

(1) The measure
\nu_{c}
is continuous, so
\sigma
is also continuous (see
[
25
, Theorem 19.16]
).

(2) By the convolution theorem (see
[
35
, Theorem 1.3.3(b)]
),

\widehat{\sigma}(g)=\widehat{\nu}_{a}(g)\cdot\widehat{\nu}_{c}(g)
for every
g\in\Gamma
.
Since
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
,
we have
\widehat{\nu}(a_{n})\to 1
.
Thus,
\widehat{\nu}_{a}(a_{n})\to 1
and
\widehat{\nu}_{c}(a_{n})\to 1
, so
\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})\to 1
as desired.

(3) By Lemma
3.3
, it suffices to show that
\widehat{\sigma}(g)\neq 1
for
g\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
g\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
.
We know
\widehat{\nu}(g)\neq 1
.
Hence, either
\widehat{\nu}_{a}(g)\neq 1
or
\widehat{\nu}_{c}(g)\neq 1
.
In either case, taking the product, we have
\widehat{\sigma}(g)=\widehat{\nu}_{a}(g)\cdot\widehat{\nu}_{c}(g)\neq 1
.

Case 2.

\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}\right)
has discrete spectrum.

By Lemma
3.4
,
C:=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is dense in
\mathbf{T}
.
Let
C_{0}\subseteq C
be a countable dense subset
(for example, we could take
C_{0}
to be the group of eigenvalues, but such a concrete description is not needed).
For each
\chi\in C_{0}
, let
\sigma_{\chi}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
be a measure constructed as in the proof
of Theorem
2.9
with
\chi_{2}=\chi
.
Let
\alpha:C_{0}\to(0,1)
so that
\sum_{\chi\in C_{0}}{\alpha(\chi)}=1
,
and set
\sigma:=\sum_{\chi\in C_{0}}{\alpha(\chi)\sigma_{\chi}}
.
We claim that
\sigma
has the desired properties.

(1) Each
\sigma_{\chi}
is continuous, so their weighted sum
\sigma
is also continuous.

(2) By construction,
\widehat{\sigma}_{\chi}(a_{n})\to 1
for every
\chi\in C_{0}
.
Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}(a_{n})=\sum_{\chi\in C_{0}}{\alpha(\chi)\widehat{\sigma}_{\chi}(a_{n})}\to\sum_{\chi\in C_{0}}{\alpha(\chi)}=1.

(3) Since
\alpha(\chi)>0
for each
\chi\in C_{0}
,
\text{supp}\left(\sigma\right)
contains
\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{\chi}\right)
.
We want to check that
\chi\in\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{\chi}\right)
for each
\chi\in C_{0}
.
Let
(U_{p})_{p\geq 0}
be a neighborhood basis at
0
in
\mathbf{T}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
U_{0}\supseteq U_{1}\supseteq\cdots
.
(For example, we could take
U_{p}
to be a ball of radius
2^{-p}
around 0 in some metric on
\mathbf{T}
.)
Now, in the proof of Theorem
2.9
, we may impose the additional assumption
that
V_{p,r}
is a subset of
\chi_{r}+U_{p}
.
(This can be done by replacing
V_{p,r}
with the set
V_{p,r}\cap(\chi_{r}+U_{p})

at each step of the induction process.)
Now let
U
be a neighborhood of
\chi
in
\mathbf{T}
.
Then for some
p\geq 0
, we have
\chi+U_{p}\subseteq U
, so
V_{p,2}\subseteq U
.
Therefore,
\sigma_{\chi}(U)\geq\sigma_{\chi}(V_{p,2})=2^{-p}>0
.
This show that
\chi\in\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{\chi}\right)
, so
\chi\in\text{supp}\left(\sigma\right)
for every
\chi\in C_{0}
.
But
C_{0}
is dense in
\mathbf{T}
, so
\text{supp}\left(\sigma\right)=\mathbf{T}
.
∎

We now deduce topological corollaries from Theorem
A
,
including an extension of
[
3
, Theorem 3.8]
.
First, we need two definitions from
[
4
]
.

Definition 3.5
.

A (Hausdorff) group topology
\tau
on
\Gamma
is
pre-compact

if for every
\emptyset\neq U\in\tau
, there is a finite set
F\subseteq\Gamma
so that
U+F=\Gamma
.

The term
pre-compact
alludes to the existence of a
compactification
with a compatible topology.
A topological space
X
is a
compactification
of another topological space
Y

if
X
is compact and there is a continuous injection
\iota:Y\to X

such that
\iota(Y)
is dense in
X
.
It is not hard to show that a group topology
\tau
is pre-compact if and only if it is the subspace topology induced by some compactification of
\Gamma
.
In what follows, we assume that all topologies are Hausdorff.

Definition 3.6
.

A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
is
totally bounded
(a
TB-sequence
for short)
if there is a pre-compact group topology
\tau
on
\Gamma
such that
a_{n}\to 0
in
(\Gamma,\tau)
.

In
[
4
]
, it is shown that a sequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{Z}
is a TB-sequence
if and only if
\lambda^{n_{k}}\to 1
for infinitely many
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
.
By Lemma
2.7
, this means that TB-sequences in
\mathbb{Z}
are rigidity sequences
for ergodic systems with discrete spectrum.
Badea, Grivaux, and Matheron use this observation to conclude that TB-sequences are rigidity sequences
(see
[
3
, Theorem 3.8]
).

In general groups, there may be rigidity sequences for discrete spectrum systems
that are not TB-sequences (see Remark
3.10
below).
However, every TB-sequence is still rigid for some system with discrete spectrum:

Corollary 3.7
.

TB-sequences are rigidity sequences.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be a pre-compact group topology on
\Gamma
such that
a_{n}\to 0
.
Let
X
be the corresponding compactification of
\Gamma
.
Since
\Gamma
is dense in
X
, the action of
\Gamma
on itself by translation
extends to a minimal action
T:\Gamma\curvearrowright X
.
The space
X
has a group structure, and
T
acts by group translations.
Any minimal such system is uniquely ergodic.
In particular,
(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
is ergodic and preserves the Haar measure
\mu
on
X
.
Moreover, since
a_{n}\to 0
in
(\Gamma,\tau)
, we immediately have
T_{a_{n}}\to\text{id}
,
so
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence by Theorem
A
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The system constructed in the preceding proof has discrete spectrum
(see Proposition
2.1
(ii)).
Moreover, since the topology
\tau
is Hausdorff,
\Gamma
acts freely on
X
.
This is not needed to conclude that TB-sequences are rigidity sequences,
but it does lead towards a dynamical characterization of TB-sequences
(see Proposition
3.9
and Remark
3.10
below).

More generally, following Ruzsa
[
36
]
, we say that a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
nullpotent

if there is some Hausdorff group topology
\tau
on
\Gamma
such that
a_{n}\to 0
in
(\Gamma,\tau)
.
Such sequences are also called
T-sequences
by Protasov and Zelenyuk
[
34
]
,
but we stick to Ruzsa’s terminology to be consistent with
[
3
]
.
Badea, Grivaux, and Matheron showed that every rigidity sequence in
\mathbb{Z}
is nullpotent (see
[
3
, Corollary 3.6]
).
We extend this to our setting under the assumption that the measure-preserving action is free
(as discussed in the introduction, this assumption is extraneous in
\mathbb{Z}
).

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rigid for a free ergodic action
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
.
Then
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nullpotent.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
so that
T_{g}f\neq f
for every
g\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
.
Such an
f
exists since the action is free (all that is required is that
f
is injective).
Then we can define a metric
d
on
\Gamma
by
d(g,h):=\left\|T_{g}f-T_{h}f\right\|
.
Note that
d
is translation-invariant, so it induces a group topology
\tau
on
\Gamma
.
Moreover,
d(a_{n},0)=\|T_{a_{n}}f-f\|\to 0
.
Thus,
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nullpotent.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

The same proof shows that if
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a free ergodic action
with discrete spectrum for which
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rigid, then
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a TB-sequence,
providing a partial converse to Corollary
3.7
.
This yields an alternative (dynamical) proof to the characterization of TB-sequences
given in
[
16
, Proposition 3.2]
:

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a TB-sequence if and only if
\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(a_{n})\to 1\}
is dense in
\mathbf{T}
.

Combining the above observations, we get the following topological
version of Theorems
A
and
B
:

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\Gamma
.
If there is a (Hausdorff) group topology
\tau
on
\Gamma

such that
a_{n}\to 0
in
(\Gamma,\tau)
,
then there is a non-pre-compact (Hausdorff) group topology
\tau^{\prime}
on
\Gamma

such that
a_{n}\to 0
in
(\Gamma,\tau^{\prime})
.

3.3.
Analyzing the assumption of Theorem
A

It is worth examining where we have used various properties of
\Gamma
.
The translation into Fourier analysis relies on
\Gamma
being abelian.
For non-abelian groups, some more intricate analysis of the relationship
between finite and infinite-dimensional representations may have some utility,
but it is not at all clear how to extend the argument used here to the non-abelian setting.

Discreteness of the group
\Gamma
cannot be dropped
from the assumptions of Theorem
A
.
As an example, Theorem
A
fails for
\mathbb{Q}

when equipped with the subspace topology from
\mathbb{R}
.
Consider the
\mathbb{Q}
-action on
\mathbb{T}
given by
T_{q}(x)=x+q\pmod{1}
for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
This action is clearly continuous when
\mathbb{Q}
is endowed with the subspace topology.
Moreover,
\mathbb{Z}
is rigid for this action.
In fact,
T_{n}=\text{id}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By Theorem
A
, it follows that there is a weakly mixing action of
\mathbb{Q}

(as a discrete group) for which
\mathbb{Z}
is rigid.
The following propositions shows that any such action must be
discontinuous in the subspace topology on
\mathbb{Q}
:

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
S\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
has bounded gaps.
That is, there is an
L>0
so that
S\cap[t,t+L]\neq\emptyset
for every
t\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Then no enumeration of
S
can be a rigidity sequence in
\mathbb{Q}

with the subspace topology.

Proof.

Suppose
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\right)
is a measure-preserving system
with
(T_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
acting continuously in the subspace topology,
and suppose that (some enumeration of)
S
is rigid for this action.
We will show that
(T_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
has discrete spectrum.

Fix
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
We want to show that the orbit
\{T_{q}f:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is pre-compact (totally bounded) in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
S
is rigid, the set

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon/2}(f):=\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{Q}:\|T_{\tau}f-f\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right\}

contains all but finitely many elements of
S
, so it still has bounded gaps.
Let
L=L(\varepsilon)>0
so that
P_{\varepsilon/2}(f)\cap[t,t+L]\neq\emptyset
for every
t\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Since the action is continuous, let
\delta>0

so that if
|q|<\delta
, then
\|T_{q}f-f\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
Let
F\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,L]
be finite and
\delta
-dense.

We claim that
\{T_{t}f:t\in F\}
is
\varepsilon
-dense in
\{T_{q}f:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
.
Let
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Choose
s\in P_{\varepsilon/2}(f)\cap[q-L,q]
.
Then
q-s\in[0,L]
, so there is a
t\in F
with
|q-s-t|<\delta
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\|T_{q}f-T_{t}f\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{q}f-T_{q-s}f\|+\|T_{q-s}f-T_{t}f\|

\displaystyle=\|T_{s}f-f\|+\|T_{q-s-t}f-f\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

This proves that
\{T_{t}f:t\in F\}
is
\varepsilon
-dense, as claimed.
Therefore,
\{T_{q}f:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is totally bounded.
Thus,
(T_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
has discrete spectrum
(condition (iii) of Proposition
2.1
is satisfied).
∎

Finally, although countability of
\Gamma
does not come into play in Theorem
A

(the Cantor-like construction uses only that
\mathbf{T}
is a compact Hausdorff space),
it is crucial to our proof of Theorem
B
.
Indeed, in order to construct a continuous measure
\sigma_{\chi}
for which a pre-specified
\chi
is in the support,
we needed the open sets
V_{p,2}
to shrink to
\chi
as
p\to\infty

(see (3) in Case 2 of the proof of Theorem
B
above).
By
[
25
, Theorem 8.6]
, if
\{\chi\}
can be written as the intersection of countably many open sets in
\mathbf{T}
,
then
\mathbf{T}
is metrizable, and this happens if and only if
\Gamma
is countable.

4.
Examples of rigidity sequences

Now that we have established Theorem
A
,
we give several examples of nontrivial rigidity sequences for weakly mixing actions.

4.1.
Pisot–Vijayaraghavan numbers in
\mathbb{R}

A number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a
Pisot–Vijayaraghavan (PV) number

if
\alpha>1
is an algebraic integer all of whose Galois conjugates have absolute value less than 1.
PV numbers have the property that
\|\alpha^{n}\|\to 0
,
where
\|\cdot\|
is the distance to the nearest integer.
Examples of PV numbers include the plastic number (the real root of
x^{3}-x-1
) and the golden ratio.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\alpha
is a PV number, then
\left\llbracket\alpha^{n}\right\rrbracket
is a rigidity sequence in
\mathbb{Z}
,
where
\left\llbracket\cdot\right\rrbracket
is the nearest integer function.

Proof.

Since any irrational rotation of the circle is ergodic,
it suffices to check that
\left\|\left\llbracket\alpha^{n}\right\rrbracket\beta\right\|\to 0

for some irrational
\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.
We claim that
\beta=\alpha
works.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\|\left\llbracket\alpha^{n}\right\rrbracket\alpha\right\|=\left\|\alpha^{n+1}\pm\|\alpha^{n}\|\cdot\alpha\right\|\leq\left\|\alpha^{n+1}\right\|+\left\|\|\alpha^{n}\|\cdot\alpha\right\|\to 0.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

The key to showing that
\left\llbracket\alpha^{n}\right\rrbracket
is a rigidity sequence is the existence of
\beta

such that
\left\|\alpha^{n}\beta\right\|\to 0
.
It is known that for algebraic
\alpha
, this condition implies that
\alpha
is a PV number.
However, it is an open question whether there is a transcendental
\alpha
such that

\left\|\alpha^{n}\beta\right\|\to 0
for some
\beta\neq 0
.

4.2.
PV numbers in finite characteristic

We can extend the previous example to a parallel situation in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
.
PV numbers have a natural analogue in this setting, described in detail in
[
5
]
.
We first introduce some suggestive notation.
Let
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
,
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))
, and
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{Z}
.
Define
|\cdot|
on
\mathcal{R}
by

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=-\infty}^{N}{c_{n}t^{n}}\right|=q^{N},

where we assume
c_{N}\neq 0
.
As with the real line,
\mathcal{R}
is the completion of
\mathcal{Q}

|\cdot|
.
Here,

For

That is, if

\displaystyle\left[x\right]

\displaystyle\left\{x\right\}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}{c_{n}t^{n}}.

We let
\|\cdot\|
denote the distance to the nearest “integer”

.

The absolute value
|\cdot|
on
\mathcal{R}

.
Suppose
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
is of degree

\mathcal{R}
,

Then we define

This allows us to define PV numbers in the same way as in

Namely,
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}

and all of its conjugates have absolute value less than 1 in

.
With this definition, Bateman and Duquette show that
\mathcal{R}
contains PV elements
of every (algebraic) degree over

(see
[

).
They are also able to show the following strengthening of the characterization of PV numbers in

Theorem 4.3

(
[
5
]
, Theorems 2.1 and 3.4)

Suppose
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
is a PV element of degree
d
over
\mathcal{Z}
.
Let

denote the trace on the extension
\mathcal{Q}(\alpha)/\mathcal{Q}

Suppose

such that for some

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{Q}(\alpha)/\mathcal{Q}}\left(\alpha^{N+i}\beta\right)\in\mathcal{Z}

Conversely, if
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{Q}(\alpha)

In particular, if

Repeating the argument from the proof of Proposition

Proposition 4.4

If

Remark 4.5

The conclusion of Proposition

Motivated by these examples involving PV numbers,

Question 4.6

For which

4.3.
Continued fractions

Bergelson, del Junco, Lemańczyk, and Rosenblattshowed that in
\mathbb{Z}

if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is irrational, then its sequence of continued fraction denominators
is rigid for a weakly mixing transformation (see
[
8

).
We show that the analogous result holds in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
.
For an introduction to continued fractions in finite characteristic, see
[
39
]
.
We give the basics of the construction here.

Define a tranformation
T:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
by

\displaystyle Tx=\begin{cases}\left\{\frac{1}{x}\right\},&x\neq 0;\\
0,&x=0.\end{cases}

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}

a_{0}:=\left[\alpha\right]

and
a_{n}:=\left[T^{n}\left\{\alpha\right\}\right]
for

to obtain a continued fraction expression for

\displaystyle\alpha=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\frac{1}{\ddots}}}}.

We will also use the notation
\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots]
.

Let
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of denominators
for the convergents
\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\dots,\penalty a_{n}]
.
These can be computed recursively by the formula

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1},

\displaystyle q_{n}

\displaystyle=a_{n}q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}.

Continued fractions satisfy the inequality (see
[
39
]
)

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|<\frac{1}{|q_{n}|^{2}}.

Thus,
\|q_{n}\alpha\|<|q_{n}|^{-1}\to 0
.

Irrational numbers have many of the familiar properties from characteristic zero
(see
[

for details):

Lemma 4.7

The following are equivalent for
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}

(i)

\alpha
is irrational, i.e.
\alpha\notin\mathcal{Q}
;

(ii)

\alpha
has an infinite continued fraction expansion;

(iii)

of

\mathcal{T}
is infinite;

(iv)

(n\alpha)_{n\in\mathcal{Z}}
is dense in
\mathcal{T}
;

(v)

(n\alpha)_{n\in\mathcal{Z}}
is well-distributed in
\mathcal{T}
with respect to Haar measure.

Suppose
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
is irrational.
Then the
\mathcal{Z}
-action on

Moreover,

Applying Theorem

Theorem 4.8

Let

.
Let

\mathcal{Z}
.
Then there is a free weakly mixing
\mathcal{Z}
-action

such that

.

In particular, the sequence of

corresponding to the element

The first several polynomials are given by

\displaystyle F_{1}(t)

\displaystyle F_{2}(t)

4.4.
More examples in
\bigoplus{\mathbb{F}_{p}}

The next two examples seem to have no clear analogues in
\mathbb{Z}
.
Since we are no longer dealing with the multiplicative structure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
,
we will restrict for ease of notation to considering
\bigoplus{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
, where

is prime.
We first need to introduce some notation.
For

and

, we denote by

the quantity

, where
e\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{N}{c_{n}t^{n}}\right)=e^{2\pi ic_{-1}/p}
.
This pairing demonstrates the duality between

and

,
and for each

Handling characters concretely this way allows for easy computations in the following examples.

Example 4.9
.

Consider the sequence
a_{n}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{np-1}{t^{k}}\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Let

\displaystyle C_{0}:=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{c_{n}t^{-n}}\in\mathcal{T}:c_{jp+1}=c_{jp+2}=\cdots=c_{(j+1)p}~\text{for}~j\geq 0\right\}.

Note that

is infinite (it has a natural bijection with

).
Moreover, for

and

,
\left\langle x,a_{n}\right\rangle=1
.
Hence,

is a rigidity sequence.

Example 4.10
.

Let

be an infinite set with infinite complement.
For
\emptyset\neq F\subseteq I
finite and
(c_{i})_{i\in F}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{F}
not all zero,
let
a_{F;(c_{i})}(t)=\sum_{i\in F}{c_{i}t^{i}}
.
Set

\displaystyle C_{0}:=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{c_{n}t^{-n}}\in\mathcal{T}:c_{n+1}=0~\text{for all}~n\in I\right\}.

The set

is clearly infinite and
\left\langle x,a_{F;(c_{i})}\right\rangle=1
for

.
Thus, any enumeration of

is a rigidity sequence in

.

5.
Recurrence in abelian groups

Recall the definition of a set of recurrence.

See
1.1

Specifying a bound on the measure of
A
leads to a related notion.

Definition 5.1
.

A set
R\subseteq\Gamma
is a set of
\delta
-
recurrence
if
for every measure-preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma})

and every
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)\geq\delta
, there is an
r\in R\setminus\{0\}
such that

\mu(A\cap T_{r}^{-1}A)>0
.

Poincaré’s Recurrence Theorem can be stated as the fact that
\mathbb{Z}
is a set of recurrence (in
\mathbb{Z}
).
Analyzing the standard proof, it is not hard to see that
\left\{1,2,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil\right\}

is a set of
\delta
-recurrence for every
\delta>0
.
That is, once
\delta>0
is fixed, recurrence is guaranteed to happen in bounded time.
This phenomenon is very robust.
Forrest extended this observation into a fact about general countable discrete groups
3
3

3

Forrest’s proof
does not require that
\Gamma
be abelian or even amenable.
By imposing this extra condition, we are able to give a proof that
avoids functional analysis in favor of combinatorics.

using a clever compactness argument:

Theorem 5.2

(Uniformity of Recurrence
[
21
]
, Lemma 6.4)
.

A set
R\subseteq\Gamma
is a set of recurrence if and only if
for all
\delta>0
, there is a finite subset
R_{\delta}\subseteq R

such that
R_{\delta}
is a set of
\delta
-recurrence.

The proof in
[
21
]
uses functional analytic techniques.
A simpler proof for
\mathbb{Z}
is given in
[
10
]
.
We give a new proof of uniformity of recurrence for abelian groups in the spirit of the proof from
[
10
]
.
This requires a discussion of amenability and the introduction of a special class of Følner sequences,
which we term
self-tiling
Følner sequences.

5.1.
Upper Banach density

It is well-known that every abelian group is
amenable
.
Roughly speaking, this means that abelian groups have translation-invariant averaging schemes.
We will need some basic definitions and properties relating to amenability
in order to prove Theorem
5.2
.
Some of these will also appear directly in the construction of rigid-recurrent sequences.

Definition 5.3
.

An
invariant mean
is a linear functional
m:\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}
such that

(1)

m(\mathbbm{1})=1
;

(2)

m(\tau_{t}\cdot f)=m(f)
for every
t\in\Gamma
and
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma)
,
where
(\tau_{t}\cdot f)(x):=f(x-t)
.

The following proposition is a precise statement of the fact that every abelian group is amenable:

Proposition 5.4
.

Every abelian group admits an invariant mean.

Another characterization of amenability that we will make use of is the existence of a Følner sequence.
Recall that a sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite subsets of
\Gamma
is a Følner sequence
if for every
x\in\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\frac{|(\Phi_{N}+x)\triangle\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}}=0.

That is, the sets
(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are asymptotically invariant.
Averages along Følner sequences are closely related to invariant means,
and both can be used to define notions of density.

We define the
upper density
of a set
E
along a Følner sequence
\Phi
by

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{\Phi}(E):=\limsup_{N\to\infty}{\frac{|E\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|}}.

The
upper Banach density
of a set
E\subseteq\Gamma
is defined by

\displaystyle d^{*}(E):=\sup_{\Phi}{\overline{d}_{\Phi}(E)}.

This is equivalent to
d^{*}(E)=\sup_{m\in M(\Gamma)}{m(\mathbbm{1}_{E})}
,
where
M(\Gamma)
is the set of invariant means on
\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma)
.

A surprising characterization of upper Banach density that will be especially useful for us
is given by the following lemma:

Lemma 5.5

(
[
9
]
, Lemma 2.7)
.

Let
E\subseteq\Gamma
.
Then

\displaystyle d^{*}(E)=\max\left\{\alpha\geq 0:\right.

\displaystyle\text{for every finite set}~F\subseteq\Gamma,

\displaystyle\left.\text{there is an}~x\in\Gamma~\text{with}~|(E-x)\cap F|\geq\alpha|F|\right\}.

5.2.
Furstenberg correspondence

Lemma 5.6

(Furstenberg Correspondence Principle
[
6
]
, Theorem 4.17)
.

Let
E\subseteq\Gamma
with
d^{*}(E)>0
.
Then there is a measure-preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma})
and a set
A\in\mathcal{B}

such that
\mu(A)=d^{*}(E)
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle d^{*}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{(E-t_{i})}\right)\geq\mu\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{T_{t_{i}}^{-1}A}\right).

The inequality in Furstenberg’s Correspondence Principle (Lemma
5.6
) allows one
to deduce combinatorial results from corresponding results about multiple recurrence in dynamical systems.
The following lemma is a useful tool for translating in the other direction: from combinatorics to dynamics.

Lemma 5.7

(Bergelson’s Intersectivity Lemma
[
7
]
, Lemma 5.10)
.

Suppose
(A_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
is a sequence of sets in a probability space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)

with
\mu(A_{g})\geq a
for all
g\in\Gamma
.
Then for any Følner sequence
\Phi
in
\Gamma
, there is a set
E\subseteq\Gamma

such that
\overline{d}_{\Phi}(E)\geq a
and

\displaystyle\mu\left(\bigcap_{g\in F}{A_{g}}\right)>0

for every nonempty finite subset
F\subseteq E
.

Using the correspondence principle and the intersectivity lemma together,
one can produce the following characterization of sets of recurrence, which we will utilize in the next section:

Proposition 5.8

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 5.13)
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is abelian, and let
R\subseteq\Gamma
.
Then
R
is a set of recurrence if and only if for every
E\subseteq\Gamma
with
d^{*}(E)>0
,
we have
(E-E)\cap R\neq\emptyset
.

The same method can be used to characterize sets of
\delta
-recurrence.
This result is implicit in the proof of uniformity of recurrence for
\mathbb{Z}
given in
[
10
]
.
We give a proof here in the full generality of abelian groups for completeness.

Proposition 5.9
.

R
is a set of
\delta
-recurrence if and only if
for every
E\subseteq\Gamma
with
d^{*}(E)\geq\delta
, we have
(E-E)\cap R\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose
R\subseteq\Gamma
is a set of
\delta
-recurrence, and let
E\subseteq\Gamma

with
d^{*}(E)\geq\delta
.
By Lemma
5.6
, let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma})

be a measure-preserving system and
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)=d^{*}(E)\geq\delta
such that

(8)

\displaystyle d^{*}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{(E-t_{i})}\right)\geq\mu\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}{T_{t_{i}}^{-1}A}\right)

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in\Gamma
.
Since
R
is a set of
\delta
-recurrence, there is an
r\in R
so that
\mu(A\cap T_{r}^{-1}A)>0
.
It follows from the inequality (
8
) that
d^{*}(E\cap(E-r))>0
.
In particular,
E\cap(E-r)\neq\emptyset
, so let
x\in E\cap(E-r)
.
Then
x\in E
and
x+r\in E
, so
r=(x+r)-x\in E-E
.
Hence,
(E-E)\cap R\neq\emptyset
.

Conversely, suppose
(E-E)\cap R\neq\emptyset
for every
E\subseteq\Gamma

with
d^{*}(E)\geq\delta
.
We will show that
R
is a set of recurrence.
Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma})
be a measure-preserving system,
and let
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)\geq\delta
.
Set
A_{g}:=T_{g}^{-1}A
for
g\in\Gamma
.
Then
(A_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
is a sequence in
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
with
\mu(A_{g})=\mu(A)\geq\delta
.
for every
g\in\Gamma
.
Thus, by Lemma
5.7
, there is a set
E\subseteq\Gamma
with

such that

(9)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\bigcap_{g\in F}{A_{g}}\right)>0

for every nonempty finite subset
F\subseteq E
.
Now let
r\in R\cap(E-E)
.
Let
s,t\in E
such that

.
By (

\displaystyle\mu\left(A\cap T_{r}^{-1}A\right)=\mu\left(A\cap T_{t-s}^{-1}A\right)=\mu\left(T_{s}^{-1}A\cap T_{t}^{-1}A\right)=\mu\left(A_{s}\cap A_{t}\right)>0.

Thus,

is a set of recurrence.
∎

5.3.
Tilings

The last ingredient needed for our proof of Theorem
5.2

is a special class of Følner sequences that we call
self-tiling
.
First we need a round of definitions
(this terminology comes from
[
33
]
in establishing a version of Rokhlin’s lemma for amenable groups).

Definition 5.10
.

A set
T\subseteq\Gamma
is a
tile
if there are disjoint translates
\{T+s_{i}:i\in I\}

such that
\bigcup_{i\in I}{(T+s_{i})}=\Gamma
.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
K\subseteq\Gamma
be finite and
\varepsilon>0
.
A set
F\subseteq\Gamma
is
(K,\varepsilon)
-invariant
if

\displaystyle\frac{|(K+F)\triangle F|}{|F|}<\varepsilon.

Definition 5.12
.

A group
\Gamma
is
monotileable
if for every finite set
K\subseteq\Gamma

and every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
(K,\varepsilon)
-invariant finite tile
T\subseteq\Gamma
.

Remark 5.13
.

The properties of
T
in the definition for monotileability can be summarized as follows:
there is a (syndetic) set
S\subseteq\Gamma
such that

(1)

T+S=\Gamma
;

(2)

(T-T)\cap(S-S)=\{0\}
;

(3)

\frac{|(K+T)\triangle T|}{|T|}<\varepsilon
.

It is known that all abelian groups are monotileable (see
[
33
]
).
Other examples of monotileable groups are given in
[
41
]
.
It remains an open question whether every amenable group is monotileable.

For our purposes, we will need a slightly different notion of almost-invariance than that
given in Definition
5.11
, which the following lemma grants:

Lemma 5.14

(
[
17
]
, Lemma 2.6)
.

Let
K\subseteq\Gamma
be finite and
\varepsilon>0
.
If
F\subseteq\Gamma
is finite and
\left(K,\frac{\varepsilon}{|K|}\right)
-invariant,
then there is a subset
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
such that
|F^{\prime}|>(1-\varepsilon)|F|
and
K+F^{\prime}\subseteq F
.

We also need to control the density of the set of shifts required to tile
\Gamma
.
This is easily calculated with the help of Lemma
5.5
:

Lemma 5.15
.

Suppose
T
is a tile with shifts
S
covering
\Gamma
.
Then
d^{*}(S)=\frac{1}{|T|}
.

Proof.

First, for every
x\in\Gamma
,
|(S-x)\cap T|\leq 1
.
Indeed, if
s_{1}-x=t_{1}
and
s_{2}-x=t_{2}
, then
s_{1}-s_{2}=t_{1}-t_{2}
.
Since
(T-T)\cap(S-S)=\{0\}
, it follows that
s_{1}=s_{2}
and
t_{1}=t_{2}
.
So by Lemma
5.5
,
d^{*}(S)\leq\frac{1}{|T|}
.

Conversely, let
F\subseteq\Gamma
be finite.
Every element of
F
is (uniquely) expressible in the form
t+s
for some
t\in T
and
s\in S
.
For concreteness, write
F=\{t_{1}+s_{1},\dots,t_{n}+s_{n}\}
.
By the pigeonhole principle, there is a
t_{0}\in T
such that

\displaystyle|\{1\leq i\leq n:t_{i}=t_{0}\}|\geq\frac{n}{|T|}.

Thus,
|(S+t_{0})\cap F|\geq\frac{1}{|T|}\cdot|F|
.
Hence,

6.6
.
Let
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an enumeration of
R
.
We want to show that every translate of
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\frac{1}{n}<\varepsilon
.
By construction,
R\setminus R_{n}
is finite, so there is an
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{k}\in R_{n}

for every
k\geq k_{0}
.
Thus, for
k\geq k_{0}
,
\|e_{r_{k}}-f\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)}<\frac{1}{n}<\varepsilon
.
We have therefore shown that
e_{r_{k}}\to f
in
L^{1}(\sigma)
.
Restricting to
K_{t}
, it follows that
e_{r_{k}}\to e_{t}
in
L^{1}(\sigma_{t})
.
Thus,
e_{r_{k}-t}\to 1
in
L^{1}(\sigma_{t})
,
so
(r_{k}-t)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for every
t\in\Gamma
.
∎

7.
Freely rigid-recurrent sequences

In this section, we discuss freely rigid-recurrent sequences
with an eye toward extending Theorem
C
.

7.1.
Translates of rigidity sequences for free actions

We begin by showing that there is an obstacle to naïvely extending Theorem
C

to free actions.
Following the approach to freeness in the proof of Theorem
B
,
one may be inclined to modify the proof of Theorem
C

so that the closed subgroup generated by
\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{g}\right)
is equal to
\mathbf{T}
for every
g\in G
.
Unfortunately, this is not possible in general.
To see this, consider the group
\Gamma=(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
,
which has Pontryagin dual
\mathbf{T}\simeq\Lambda_{3}\times\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Lambda_{2}}
.
Then the type
K_{2}
set
K\subseteq\mathbf{T}
appearing in the proof of Theorem
B

sits entirely inside of the closed proper subgroup
\{0\}\oplus\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Lambda_{2}}
.

The obstacle encountered here is not just an artifact of the method of proof.
For free actions of
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
,
it is not possible for every translate of a rigidity sequence to be a rigidity sequence for a free action.
This is demonstrated by Corollary
7.2
below.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be a finite group and
\Gamma_{2}
a torsion group with finite exponent
such that the exponents of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are coprime.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(a_{n,1},a_{n,2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence
for a free action of
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
,
then
a_{n,1}=0
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be as in Proposition
7.1
.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for a free action of
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
,
then for
g\in\Gamma_{1}\setminus\{0\}
,

(a_{n}+(g,0))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not a rigidity sequence for any free action of
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
.

Proposition
7.1
follows immediately from the following lemma,
since a sequence in a finite group cannot diverge to infinity:

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be groups with finite, coprime exponents.
If
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for an action of
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
,
then
(a_{n,1},0)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also a rigidity sequence for the action.
In particular, if the action is free, then
either
a_{n,1}\to\infty
or
a_{n,1}=0
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
k
be the exponent of
\Gamma_{1}
and
l
the exponent of
\Gamma_{2}
.

Suppose
\left(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,(T_{g})_{g\in\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}}\right)

is a measure-preserving system that is rigid along the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
We want to show
T_{(a_{n,1},0)}f\to f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\|f\|_{2}=1
.
Then the spectral measure
\sigma_{f}
on
\widehat{\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}}\simeq\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\times\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}=:\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}
is a probability measure,
and our goal is to show
\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(a_{n,1},0)\to 1
.

By assumption,
\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(a_{n,1},a_{n,2})\to 1
.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left|\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})\chi_{2}(a_{n,2})-1\right|~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}\to 0.

This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left|\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})-\overline{\chi_{2}(a_{n,2})}\right|~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}\to 0.

Now,
\chi_{1}
takes values in
\Lambda_{k}
for every
\chi_{1}\in\mathbf{T}_{1}
,
and
\chi_{2}
takes values in
\Lambda_{l}
for every
\chi_{2}\in\mathbf{T}_{2}
.
Since
k
and
l
are coprime, we have
\Lambda_{k}\cap\Lambda_{l}=\{1\}
.
Hence, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
z\in\Lambda_{k}
,
w\in\Lambda_{l}
, and
(z,w)\neq(1,1)
,
then
|z-w|\geq\delta
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left|\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})-\overline{\chi_{2}(a_{n,2})}\right|~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}\geq\delta\cdot\sigma_{f}(B),

where
B:=\left\{(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})\in\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}:\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})\neq 1~\text{or}~\chi_{2}(a_{n,2})\neq 1\right\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\sigma}_{f}(a_{n,1},0)-1\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left(\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})-1\right)~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left|\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})-1\right|~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{f}(B)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\int_{\mathbf{T}_{1}\times\mathbf{T}_{2}}{\left|\chi_{1}(a_{n,1})-\overline{\chi_{2}(a_{n,2})}\right|~d\sigma_{f}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})}

\displaystyle\to 0.

∎

Note that the obstacle encountered here does not rule out the possibility of producing free actions
if we restrict the shifts to a finite index subgroup.
Concretely, in the example
\Gamma=(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
discussed above,
it is still possible to produce a sequence such that each shift coming from the finite index subgroup

\Delta=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
is freely rigid-recurrent.
This is the content of Theorem
1.5

for the group
\Gamma=(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})\oplus\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}
,
which we will prove in Section
7.3
.

7.2.
Topologically-generating Kronecker sets

Analyzing the proof of Theorem
C
, we see that, for the resulting sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
the translate
(r_{n}-t)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will be freely rigid-recurrent if
the Gaussian system associated to the measure
\sigma_{t}
(as defined in the proof) is free.
By Lemma
3.3
, the Gaussian system associated to
\sigma_{t}
is free if and only if

\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{t}\right)=K_{t}
generates a dense subgroup of
\mathbf{T}
.
This motivates the following definition:

Definition 7.4
.

Let
G
be a compact metrizable abelian group such that either
G
has infinite exponent
or
G
is isomorphic to
D_{q}
for some
q\geq 2
.
We say that
G
is
topologically Kronecker-generated
if there is a perfect set
K\subseteq G

such that
K
is a Kroncker set or a set of type
K_{q}
and the subgroup
\left\langle K\right\rangle

is dense in
G
.

In the proof of Theorem
C
, we took disjoint perfect sets
(K_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}

and defined
\sigma_{g}
to be a continuous measure supported on
K_{g}
for each
g\in\Gamma
.
In the process, we may lose control over the group generated by
K_{g}
, so we take a slightly different approach here.
Given a perfect set
K
such that
K
is Kronecker or of type
K_{q}
and
\left\langle K\right\rangle
is dense in
\mathbf{T}
,
we can instead take
(\sigma_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
to be a collection of mutually singular continuous measures
with
\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{g}\right)=K
for every
g\in\Gamma
.
Then choosing mutually disjoint Borel sets
(K_{g})_{g\in\Gamma}
with
\sigma_{g}(K_{g})=1
,
we may repeat the remainder of the proof of Theorem
C

to construct a sequence, all of whose translates are freely rigid-recurrent sequences.

Hence, for groups with infinite exponent, we have the following conditional result:

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group with infinite exponent, and let
\mathbf{T}=\widehat{\Gamma}
.
If
\mathbf{T}
is topologically Kronecker-generated, then there is a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma

such that, for every
t\in\Gamma
,
(r_{n}-t)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.

We have thereby reduced the infinite exponent case of Conjecture
1.6

to the following harmonic analytic problem:

Question 7.6
.

Let
G
be a compact metrizable abelian group with infinite exponent.
Is
G
topologically Kronecker-generated?

For groups with finite exponent, additional technical results are needed to piece together sets of type
K_{q}

for different values of
q
.
Nevertheless, we will show that if
D_{q}
is topologically Kronecker-generated for every
q\geq 2
,
then Conjecture
1.6
holds for every countable discrete abelian group
\Gamma

with finite exponent.
The details of this argument are presented in Section
7.3
below
(see, in particular, Theorem
7.11
).
Additionally, we will show that
D_{p}
is topologically Kronecker-generated for prime
p

and deduce from this Theorem
1.5
.

7.3.
Groups with finite exponent

Throughout this section, we make the following assumptions.
We fix finitely many prime powers
2\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<\dots<q_{N}
.
We then let
\Gamma
be the group

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}{\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\right)}.

As in Section
6.2
, we let
D_{q}:=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Lambda_{q}}

so that
\Gamma
has dual group

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}{D_{q_{j}}}.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, we let
i_{j}:\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\to\Gamma

and
\iota_{j}:D_{q_{j}}\to\mathbf{T}
be the natural inclusions.
That is,

\displaystyle i_{j}(g)

\displaystyle=(0,\dots,0,g,0,\dots,0),

\displaystyle\iota_{j}(\chi)

\displaystyle=(1,\cdots,1,\chi,1,\cdots,1),

where the nontrivial entry is in the
j
th coordinate.

The following lemma allows us to use tools available for sets of type
K_{q}
when there is non-uniform torsion.

Lemma 7.7
.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, let
K_{j}\subseteq D_{q_{j}}
be a set of type
K_{q_{j}}
, and let

K:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}{\iota_{j}(K_{j})}
.
If
f:K\to\mathbb{T}
is continuous and
f\left(\iota_{j}(K_{j})\right)\subseteq\Lambda_{q_{j}}
,
then there exists an element
g\in\Gamma
such that
f=\left.e_{g}\right|_{K}
.

Proof.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, define
f_{j}:K_{j}\to\mathbb{T}
by
f_{j}=f\circ\iota_{j}
.
Note that
f_{j}
takes values in
\Lambda_{q_{j}}
.
Therefore, since
K_{j}
is a set of type
K_{q_{j}}
, there is an element
g_{j}\in\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}

such that
f_{j}(\chi)=\chi(g_{j})
for every
\chi\in K_{j}
.

Let
g=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{i_{j}(g_{j})}\in\Gamma
.
Then for
\chi\in K_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\iota_{j}(\chi)\right)(g)

\displaystyle=\left(1,\cdots,1,\chi,1,\cdots,1\right)(g_{1},\dots,g_{j-1},g_{j},g_{j+1},\dots,g_{N})

\displaystyle=\chi(g_{j})=f_{j}(\chi)=f\left(\iota_{j}(\chi)\right).

Thus,
f=e_{g}
on
\iota_{j}(K_{j})
for each
j
, so this equality holds on their union
K
.
∎

Proposition 7.8
.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, let
K_{j}\subseteq D_{q_{j}}
be a set of type
K_{q_{j}}
, and let

K:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}{\iota_{j}(K_{j})}
.
Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
be a probability measure supported on
K
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, every translate of the set

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{g\in\Gamma:\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(g)-1\right|~d\sigma(\chi)}<\varepsilon\right\}

is a set of recurrence.

Applying Lemma
7.7
, we immediately get the following
as a corollary of Proposition
7.8
:

Corollary 7.9
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{T})
be a probability measure supported on
K
.
Let
f:K\to\mathbb{T}
be an integrable function such that

f\left(\iota_{j}(K_{j})\right)\subseteq\Lambda_{q_{j}}
for
j=1,\dots,N
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, every translate of the set

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon,f}:=\left\{g\in\Gamma:\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\left|\chi(g)-f\right|~d\sigma(\chi)}<\varepsilon\right\}

is a set of recurrence.

Before proving Proposition
7.8
, we discuss
how it can be used to construct freely rigid-recurrent sequences.
Assume
D_{q_{j}}
is topologically Kronecker generated for every
j=1,\dots,N
.
For each
j=1,\dots,N
, let
K_{j}\subseteq D_{q_{j}}
be a perfect Kronecker set
such that
\left\langle K_{j}\right\rangle
is dense in
D_{q_{j}}
.
Then let
(\sigma_{j,g})_{g\in\Gamma}
be a family of mutually singular continuous probability measures on
\mathbf{T}

with
\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{j,g}\right)=\iota_{j}(K_{j})
.
Put
\sigma_{g}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\sigma_{j,g}}
for
g\in\Gamma

so that
\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{g}\right)=K:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}{\iota_{j}(K_{j})}
.
It is easily checked that

\displaystyle\left\langle K\right\rangle=\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\left\langle K_{j}\right\rangle},

so
K
generates a dense subgroup of
\prod_{j=1}^{N}{D_{q_{j}}}=\mathbf{T}
.
Running through the remainder of the proof of
Theorem
C
with Corollary
7.9
in place of Corollary
6.3
gives:

Proposition 7.10
.

If
D_{q}
is topologically Kronecker-generated for each
q\in\{q_{1},\dots,\penalty q_{N}\}
,
then there is a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
such that, for every
t\in\Gamma
,

(r_{n}-t)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.

In fact, we can prove the following more general result:

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete abelian group with finite exponent, and write

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}{(\mathbb{Z}/r_{k}\mathbb{Z})^{m_{k}}}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}{\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\right)}

with
q_{1},\dots,q_{N},r_{1},\dots,r_{M}
distinct prime powers and
m_{1},\dots,m_{M}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\Delta\leq\Gamma
be the finite index subgroup

\displaystyle\Delta:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}{\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/q_{j}\mathbb{Z})}\right)}.

If
D_{q}
is topologically Kronecker-generated for each
q\in\{q_{1},\dots,q_{N}\}
,
then there is a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
such that, for every
s\in\Delta
,

(r_{n}-s)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a freely rigid-recurrent sequence.

Remark 7.12
.

The group
\Delta
in Theorem
7.11
is not optimal in all situations,
but it is nearly so.
In particular, it is possible for there to be a group
\Delta<\Delta^{\prime}\leq\Gamma

with
\Delta^{\prime}\simeq\Delta
, in which case the same result will hold for
\Delta^{\prime}
.
This happens if
q_{j}\mid r_{k}
for some
j,k
.

As an illustration, consider the group
\Gamma=(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})}\right)
,
and write elements of
\Gamma
as
g=(g_{0};g_{1},g_{2},\dots)

with
g_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
and
g_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
for
n\geq 1
.
Then
\Delta=\{g\in\Gamma:g_{0}=0\}
, which is an index 4 subgroup.
However, we could instead take
\Delta^{\prime}=\{g\in\Gamma:g_{0}\in\{0,2\}\}
,
which contains
\Delta
and has index 2.

The argument in the proof of Lemma
7.3

can be adapted to show that
\Delta^{\prime}
is optimal.
Namely, if
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rigid for a free action of
\Gamma
,
then
r_{n,0}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Theorem
7.11
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{0}:=\widehat{\Delta}
.
Then
\mathbf{T}_{0}\simeq\mathbf{T}/\Delta^{\perp}
,
where
\Delta^{\perp}:=\{\chi\in\mathbf{T}:\chi(s)=1~\text{for all}~s\in\Delta\}

is the annihilator of
\Delta
.
Since
\Delta\leq\Gamma
has finite index,
\Delta^{\perp}\simeq\widehat{\Gamma/\Delta}
is finite.

Apply Proposition
7.10
to
(\Delta,\mathbf{T}_{0})

to obtain a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Delta
.
We claim that
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has the desired properties (now considered as a sequence in
\Gamma
).

Let
s\in\Delta
.
Clearly
\{r_{n}-s:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, being a set of recurrence in
\Delta
,
is also a set of recurrence in
\Gamma
.
We now verify that
(r_{n}-s)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a rigidity sequence for a free action of
\Gamma
.
By construction and Lemmas
2.7
(2) and
3.3
,
there is a continuous probability measure
\sigma_{0,s}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T}_{0})

such that
\widehat{\sigma}_{0,s}(r_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty

and
G(\sigma_{0,s}):=\overline{\left\langle\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{0,s}\right)\right\rangle}=\mathbf{T}_{0}
.
Define
\sigma_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbf{T})
via
\sigma_{s}(A)=\sigma_{0,s}(\pi(A))
,
where
\pi:\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{T}_{0}
is the projection map.
Then
G(\sigma_{s}):=\overline{\left\langle\text{supp}\left(\sigma_{s}\right)\right\rangle}=\mathbf{T}
, and

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{s}(r_{n}-s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{T}}{\chi(r_{n}-s)~d\sigma_{s}(\chi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Delta^{\perp}|}\sum_{\chi_{1}\in\Delta^{\perp}}{\int_{\mathbf{T}_{0}}{\chi_{0}(r_{n}-s)\chi_{1}(r_{n}-s)~d\sigma_{0,s}(\chi_{0})}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{T}_{0}}{\chi_{0}(r_{n}-s)~d\sigma_{0,s}(\chi_{0})}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\sigma}_{0,s}(r_{n}-s)\to 1.

By Lemma
2.6
, the Gaussian system associated to
\sigma_{s}

is rigid along the sequence
(r_{n}-s)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Moreover, by Lemma
3.3
, this system is free.
∎

By Theorem
7.11
, all that remains to prove Theorem
1.5
is the following observation:

Lemma 7.13
.

For any prime number
p
, the group
D_{p}
is topologically Kronecker-generated.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.7
, let
K\subseteq D_{p}
be a perfect set of type
K_{p}
.
Then the closed subgroup
G
generated by
K
must be isomorphic to
D_{p}
.
Indeed, every infinite closed subgroup of
D_{p}
is isomorphic to
D_{p}
.
Letting
\varphi:G\to D_{p}
be a homeomorphic isomorphism,
it is easy to check that
\varphi(K)
is a perfect set of type
K_{p}
generating a dense subgroup of
D_{p}
.
∎

Now we proceed to prove Proposition
7.8
.
We begin the same way as the proof of Proposition
6.2
.
Fix an element
t\in\Gamma
and a set
E\subseteq\Gamma
of positive density
d^{*}(E)=\delta>0
.
We want to find
a,b\in E
such that
\|e_{a-b}-e_{t}\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)}<\varepsilon
.

Discarding a negligible portion of
K
, we may partition, for some large
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
each of the sets
\iota_{j}(K_{j})
into cells of
\sigma
-measure
\frac{1}{M}
.
Thus, we may assume that
K=\bigcup_{l=1}^{M}{\widetilde{K}_{l}}
with the property that, for each
l=1,\dots,M
:

(1)

there exists
k_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
such that every element of
\widetilde{K}_{l}
has order
k_{l}
,
and
\widetilde{K}_{l}
is a set of type
K_{k_{l}}
; and

(2)

\sigma\left(\widetilde{K}_{l}\right)=\frac{1}{M}
.

Now define
\Lambda=\prod_{l=1}^{M}{\Lambda_{k_{l}}}
,
and choose
d>N\left(\delta,\frac{\varepsilon}{4},\Lambda\right)
, to be defined later, large enough
that there are functions
\varphi_{l}:\widetilde{K}_{l}\to\Lambda_{k_{l}}
such that

\int_{\widetilde{K}_{l}}{|\varphi_{l}-e_{t}|~d\sigma}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4M}
and
\varphi_{l}
is constant on classes of a partition

\mathcal{P}_{l}=(P_{1,l},\dots,P_{d,l})
of
\widetilde{K}_{l}
into sets of measure
\sigma(P_{j,l})=\frac{1}{dM}
.
Define
\varphi:K\to\mathbb{T}
by
\varphi(\chi)=\varphi_{l}(\chi)
if
\chi\in\widetilde{K}_{l}
.
Note that
\|\varphi-e_{t}\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
.

The partitions
\mathcal{P}_{l}
allow us to produce a copy of
\Lambda^{d}
containing the function
\varphi
.
Let
H:=\left\{\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\omega_{j,l}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{j,l}}}}:\omega_{j,l}\in\Lambda_{k_{l}}\right\}
.
By construction, every element of
H
satisfies
f\left(\iota_{j}(\widetilde{K}_{l})\right)\subseteq\Lambda_{k_{l}}
.
Moreover, the map

\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\omega_{j,l}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{j,l}}}}\mapsto(\omega_{j,l})_{1\leq j\leq d,1\leq l\leq M}

is an isometric isomorphism of the metric groups
(H,\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)})\simeq(\Lambda^{d},\rho)
,
where

\displaystyle\rho(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{dM}\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\left|\omega_{j,l}-\omega^{\prime}_{j,l}\right|}}.

It can be shown that the set

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}:=\left\{\psi\in H:\left\|e_{a+\gamma}-\psi\right\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}~\text{for some}~a\in E\right\}

has cardinality at least
\delta|H|
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma

(see the proof of Claim
6.5
).

To prove Proposition
7.8
, it therefore suffices to prove
the following generalization of Lemma
6.4
:

Lemma 7.14
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{M}\in\mathbb{N}
, and set
\Lambda=\prod_{l=1}^{M}{\Lambda_{k_{l}}}
.
Given
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, there is a number
N=N(\delta,\varepsilon,\Lambda)
such that if
d>N

and
A\subseteq\Lambda^{d}
has density
\frac{|A|}{|\Lambda^{d}|}\geq\delta
,
then for every
x\in\Lambda^{d}
, there are
a,b\in A
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{dM}\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\left|a_{j,l}b_{j,l}^{-1}-x_{j,l}\right|}}<\varepsilon.

Indeed, assuming Lemma
7.14
and using the isomorphism
H\simeq\Lambda^{d}
,
we get functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}

such that
\|\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}}-\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\sigma)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
,
and the result follows by an application of the triangle inequality.

To prove Lemma
7.14
, we use a result of McDiarmid:

Theorem 7.15

(
[
31
]
, Theorem 7.9(b))
.

Let
G
be a group with translation-invariant metric
\rho
.
Let
G=G_{0}\supseteq G_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq G_{n}=\{e\}

be a decreasing sequence of subgroups,
and let
c_{k}
be the diameter of
G_{k-1}/G_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,n
.
If
A\subseteq G
has density
\frac{|A|}{|G|}=\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for any
t>0

and
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{t}|}{|G|}\geq 1-\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\gamma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\right)^{2}t^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{c_{k}^{2}}}\right),

where
A_{t}=\left\{x\in G:\rho(x,a)<t~\text{for some}~a\in A\right\}
.

Proof of Lemma
7.14
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Define a translation-invariant metric
\rho
on
\Lambda^{d}
by

\displaystyle\rho(x,y):=\frac{1}{dM}\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\left|x_{j,l}-y_{j,l}\right|}}.

We want to show that, if
d
is sufficiently large,
then every set
A\subseteq\Lambda^{d}
with density at least
\delta

satisfies
A\cap(x\cdot A_{\varepsilon})\neq\emptyset
for every
x\in\Lambda^{d}
.
Indeed, if
a\in A\cap(x\cdot A_{\varepsilon})
, then
a=xbt
for some
b\in A
and
t\in\Lambda^{d}

with
\rho(t,1)<\varepsilon
, so

\displaystyle\rho(ab^{-1},x)=\rho(a,xb)=\rho(xbt,xb)=\rho(t,1)<\varepsilon.

Thus, it suffices to show that
|A|+|A_{\varepsilon}|>|\Lambda^{d}|
for large
d
.

For
k=1,\dots,d
, let

G_{k}=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Lambda^{d}:x_{j}=1~\text{for}~j\leq k\}\simeq\Lambda^{d-k}
.
Then

\displaystyle c_{k}:=\textrm{diam}_{\rho}(G_{k-1}/G_{k})=\frac{1}{dM}\sum_{l=1}^{M}{\textrm{diam}(\Lambda_{k_{l}})}\leq\frac{2}{d}.

Taking
\gamma=1
in Theorem
7.15
, we have

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{t}|}{|\Lambda^{d}|}\geq 1-\alpha^{-1}\exp(-dt^{2}/8).

for subsets
A\subseteq\Lambda^{d}
,
\alpha=\frac{|A|}{|\Lambda^{d}|}
and
t>0
.

Let
N=\left\lceil 16\varepsilon^{-2}\ln{\left(\delta^{-1}\right)}\right\rceil
.
For
d>N
, if
A\subseteq\Lambda^{d}
has density at least
\delta
, then by (
10
),

\displaystyle\frac{|A|+|A_{\varepsilon}|}{|\Lambda^{d}|}\geq\delta+1-\delta^{-1}\exp(-d\varepsilon^{2}/8)>\delta+1-\delta^{-1}\exp(2\ln{\delta})=1.

∎
