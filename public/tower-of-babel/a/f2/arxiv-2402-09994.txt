Abstract
We explore the relationship between two popular concepts in the allocation of divisible items: competitive equilibrium (CE) and allocations that maximize Nash welfare, i.e., allocations where the weighted geometric mean of the utilities is maximal. When agents have homogeneous concave utility functions, these two concepts coincide: the classical Eisenberg–Gale convex program that maximizes Nash welfare over feasible allocations yields a competitive equilibrium. However, these two concepts diverge for non–homogeneous utilities. From a computational perspective, maximizing Nash welfare amounts to solving a convex program for any concave utility functions, whereas computing CE becomes PPAD-hard already for separable piecewise linear concave (SPLC) utilities. We introduce the concept of Gale-substitute utility functions, which is an analogue of the weak gross substitutes (WGS) property for the so-called Gale demand system. For Gale-substitutes utilities, we show that any allocation maximizing Nash welfare provides an approximate-CE with surprisingly strong guarantees, where every agent gets at least half the maximum utility they can get at any CE, and is approximately envy-free. Gale-substitutes include utility functions where computing CE is PPAD hard, such as all separable concave utilities and the previously studied non-separable class of Leontief-free utilities. We introduce a broad new class of utility functions called generalized network utilities based on the generalized flow model. This class includes SPLC and Leontief-free utilities, and we show that all such utilities are Gale-substitutes. Conversely, although some agents may get much higher utility at a Nash welfare maximizing allocation than at a CE, we show a price of anarchy type result: for general concave utilities, every CE achieves at least ( 1 / e ) 1 / e > 0.69 (1/\mathrm{e})^{1/\mathrm{e}}>0.69 fraction of the maximum Nash welfare, and this factor is tight.

Introduction

Competitive (market) equilibrium is a central solution concept in economics to analyze and predict the outcomes of market interactions. The Fisher model is a fundamental market model where a set of
n
agents
{\mathcal{A}}
arrive at the market with a fixed supply of divisible goods
{\mathcal{M}}
. Each agent
i
has a fixed budget
b_{i}
and preferences over bundles of goods given by a utility function
u_{i}(.)
. At competitive equilibrium (CE), prices
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{M}}
of goods and fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{M}}}
of goods to agents are such that each agent
i
gets an optimal bundle of goods, i.e.,
x_{i}
maximizes
u_{i}(x_{i})
subject to
\langle\mathbf{p},x_{i}\rangle\leq b_{i}
, and the market clears, i.e., the demand for each good meets its supply.

Equilibrium allocations satisfy many desirable properties, including Pareto optimality and the fairness guarantee of (weighted) envy-freeness. These properties make this an attractive concept also in other settings where no direct trade and money are involved. For example, competitive equilibrium with equal incomes (CEEI) is a popular mechanism in fair division, where a fictitious market is created by giving each agent the same budget
Varian 1974
. We note that CEEI is a special case of the Fisher model, where
b_{i}=1
for each agent
i
.

It is standard to assume that the utility functions
u_{i}(.)
are monotone non-decreasing, concave, and satisfy
u_{i}(\mathbf{0})=0
.
Classical results in mathematical economics established the existence of an equilibrium in the Fisher model as well as more general exchange market models, by Arrow and Debreu (
Arrow and Debreu 1954
) and by McKenzie (
McKenzie 1954
). These results are based on fixed point theorems and thus do not lead to efficient computational methods.
Finding an equilibrium by efficient computation has been an important topic in economics, optimization, and computer science over the past decades.

Eisenberg and Gale (
Eisenberg and Gale 1959
) gave a convex programming formulation for CE with linear utility functions
u_{i}(x)=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{M}}}v_{ij}x_{j}
.
This program amounts to maximizing the weighted Nash welfare, i.e., the weighted geometric mean of agents’ utilities over feasible allocations.
That is, any allocation maximizing
\prod_{i}u_{i}(x_{i})^{b_{i}}
(equivalently,
\sum_{i}b_{i}\log{u_{i}(x_{i})}
) over feasible allocations gives a CE, where the dual solution yields the corresponding prices
\mathbf{p}
. Eisenberg (
Eisenberg 1961
) showed that the same convex program yields competitive equilibria more generally for concave utility functions that are positively homogeneous of degree one, i.e.,
u_{i}(\alpha x_{i})=\alpha u_{i}(x_{i})
for any
\alpha>0
. This includes classical examples of constant elasticity of substitution (CES), Cobb–Douglas, and Leontief utility functions.

The Nash welfare takes its name due to its connection with the bargaining problem introduced
by Nash (
Nash 1950
). Nash studied general cooperative bargaining problems where the set of possible outcomes is a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a disagreement point
d\in K
and showed that maximizing the geometric mean of the excess utilities above the disagreement utility amounts to the only possible bargaining outcome that satisfies certain natural requirements. Taking
K
as the set of feasible allocations and
d=\mathbf{0}
, the Nash bargaining solution is precisely the Nash welfare maximizing allocation in this setting
Vazirani 2012
.

Weak gross substitutes (WGS)
utility functions are another broad class where a CE can be computed in polynomial time. Several fast algorithms have been designed for computing equilibria with WGS utilities
Bei et al. 2019a
,
Codenotti et al. 2005b
,
Codenotti et al. 2005c
,
Garg et al. 2023a
. While the WGS class includes many homogeneous utilities, there are also non-homogeneous examples for which the Eisenberg–Gale solution may not correspond to a CE.

WGS and homogeneous utility functions cover most known cases where a CE can be computed in polynomial time. Already for the seemingly simple case of separable piecewise concave utilities, computing a CE is PPAD-hard
Chen and Teng 2009
. In fact, it is PPAD-hard even for bounded linear utilities, when there is an upper bound on the utility from each good, i.e.,
u_{i}(x)=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{M}}}\min\{v_{ij}x_{j},\ell_{ij}\}

(
Bei et al. 2019b
)
.
Example
A.1
shows that the set of CE can be non-convex and disconnected already for bounded linear utilities.

1.1
Our contributions

In this paper, we study the relationship between two fundamental concepts in the allocation of divisible goods: competitive equilibrium and maximum Nash welfare allocations. We exhibit a close relationship between these solution concepts well beyond the homogeneous case.
First, we show a
‘price of anarchy’
type result
Dubey 1986
,
Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 1999
.
One can interpret Nash welfare as a global measure of social utility. In contrast, a CE can be seen as an outcome reached by autonomous agents trying to improve their utilities individually. It turns out that, in the general setting of concave utilities, any CE recovers at least
(1/\mathrm{e})^{1/\mathrm{e}}>0.69
fraction of the maximum Nash welfare, and this factor is tight (see Example
A.4
).

Theorem 1.1
.

For Fisher markets under concave utility functions, the Nash welfare at any CE is at least
(1/\mathrm{e})^{1/\mathrm{e}}
times the maximum Nash welfare.

We then study guarantees of the maximum Nash welfare solution in the context of CE. A major advantage of maximum Nash welfare is efficient computability: it can be arbitrarily well approximated for any concave utility functions. In contrast, computing CE becomes PPAD-hard already in very simple cases.
In this context, our first simple result shows that the maximum Nash welfare yields an approximate CE for all concave utility functions. By an
\alpha
-demand-approximate CE, we mean allocations and prices such that every agent receives at least
1/\alpha
-fraction of the maximum possible utility (see Definition
2.8
). As in the case of the Eisenberg–Gale program, one can interpret the optimal Lagrangian multipliers as prices. The result also implies approximate envy-freeness.

Theorem 1.2
.

For Fisher markets under concave utility functions, any Nash welfare maximizing allocation
\mathbf{y}
, together with the dual prices
\mathbf{q}
, form a 2-demand-approximate CE.
This also implies that
\mathbf{y}
is half envy-free when
b_{i}

u_{i}(y_{i})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}u_{i}(y_{k})
, for all
i,k\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

Theorem
1.2
already shows that maximum Nash welfare provides remarkable fairness and efficiency guarantees for all concave utility functions. However, there can be multiple equilibria, giving vastly different utilities to an agent. In Example
A.5
, we show that there could be a gap of
\Omega(n)
in the utility of some agent between two different competitive equilibria. Hence, an agent could be much better off in some CE than in the maximum Nash welfare solution.

As the main contribution of the paper, we show that maximum Nash welfare provides surprisingly strong guarantees for a broad class of utility functions. For this, we explore the
Gale demand correspondence

Garg et al. 2023a
,
Nesterov and Shikhman 2018

For an agent
i\in{\mathcal{A}}
with budget
b_{i}
and utility function
u_{i}(.)
at prices
\mathbf{q}
, the Gale demand correspondence comprises of the allocations

that maximize
b_{i}\log u_{i}(y_{i})-\langle\mathbf{q},y_{i}\rangle
. It follows from Lagrangian duality that in a Nash welfare maximizing solution, every agent receives an allocation from their Gale demand correspondence.

The reason behind the equivalence of CE and maximum Nash welfare for homogeneous utilities is that, for this class, the standard demand correspondence and the Gale demand correspondence coincide. Nevertheless, these two demands can behave very differently for non-homogeneous utility functions, e.g., the Gale demand can be non-monotonic in the prices, whereas the standard demand is always monotonic (see Example
A.3
).

We identify a critical property of the Gale demand correspondence that we call
Gale-substitutes property
(Definition
2.14
). This is analogous to the weak gross substitutes (WGS) property of the standard demand correspondence. In particular, it asserts that if some prices decrease then the demand for goods with unchanged prices may only decrease.

Competitive equilibrium becomes more subtle when the utility functions are
satiable
: there is an absolute maximum value of the utility that can be reached, regardless of the prices—bounded linear utilities mentioned above are such an example. To accommodate satiable utilities, we also introduce a stronger variant of the definition, called the
\Sigma
-Gale-substitutes property
. We note that for
non-satiable
utilities, the Gale-substitutes and
\Sigma
-Gale-substitutes properties coincide. Figure

Our main result shows that for this class of utility functions, the utility of any agent at the Nash welfare maximizing allocation is at least half the
maximum
utility they may get at
any
CE.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a Fisher market instance where all utility functions are
\Sigma
-Gale-substitutes.
Then, for any competitive equilibrium
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
and any Nash welfare maximizing allocation
\mathbf{y}
,
u_{i}(y_{i})\geq\frac{1}{2}u_{i}(x_{i})
holds for every
i\in{\mathcal{A}}
.

As a counterpart to the
‘price of anarchy’
result in Theorem
1.1
, one could call this a
‘price of authority’
result. The agents can either accept an allocation proposed by a central authority on the principle of maximizing Nash welfare or converge to a market solution (using some dynamics). By accepting the centrally proposed solution, each agent may be worse off by at most a factor 2 compared to the best possible equilibrium outcome. On the other hand, outcomes at different competitive equilibria can be very different, and the possible outcomes can be difficult to predict given the computational hardness of equilibria. Hence, for (
\Sigma
-)Gale-substitutes utility functions, the Nash welfare maximizing allocation provides a guaranteed good outcome to all agents simultaneously while also being efficiently computable. Thus, Theorem
1.3
establishes a strong relationship between CE and maximum Nash welfare beyond homogeneous functions and provides a natural alternative to the

problem

We call a utility function
u(.)

\Sigma
-Gale-substitutes. Note that such utilities, in general, are neither homogeneous nor WGS. As mentioned above, competitive equilibrium computation is already PPAD-hard for the separable piecewise linear concave (SPLC) case when each

Remark 1.7

We note that no polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) can exist that, for some

Related work

This paper strengthens the relationship between the two fundamental concepts of competitive equilibrium and maximum Nash welfare. Both are intensely studied concepts with a variety of applications, so we briefly mention further related results. Notation

Subgradients and supergradients

The functions we consider in this paper are not always differentiable. We will use sub- and supergradients, defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a function

is a
subgradient
of

f(\mathbf{x})+\left\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq f(\mathbf{y})
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
. We denote the set of all supergradients of

\partial^{\wedge}{f}(\mathbf{x})
, set of all subgradients as

Thus,
\mathbf{g}

is a subgradient of

3
Overview of techniques

The ‘price of anarchy’ result Theorem
1.1
is proved using an elementary argument on concavity and the log-sum inequality. The proof is inspired by an argument in Theorem 1 in
Brânzei et al. 2022
. Example
A.4

(1/\mathrm{e})^{1/\mathrm{e}}
is tight.

To prove Theorem
1.2
showing that the Nash welfare maximizing allocation is 2-demand-approximate CE, the key ingredient is Lemma

, showing that the solutions in the Gale demand system are within budget, that is, for

we have
\left\langle\mathbf{q},y\right\rangle\leq b
. Since at the Nash welfare maximizing solution, every agent gets an allocation from the Gale demand system at the dual prices
\mathbf{q}
, this lemma guarantees that the allocation is feasible. The 2-demand-approximate property then follows by a supergradient argument.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
relies on two key lemmas derived using Lagrangian duality and supergradient arguments. The first is a simple observation, showing that the Gale demand solution for lower prices yields at least half the utility of the utility maximizing bundle at the current prices.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a utility function
u\,:\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, prices

\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\operatorname{pdom}(u)
such that
\mathbf{q}\leq\mathbf{p}

u(y)\geq\frac{1}{2}u(x)
for any

and

.

The second lemma shows that for every allocation of goods in the demand correspondence, there is a smaller or equal allocation of goods in the Gale demand correspondence. Although the first lemma was true for any utilities, this one critically relies on assuming the
\Sigma
-Gale-substitutes property.

Lemma 3.2

Let

be a

-Gale-substitutes utility and let

for

\mathbf{p}\in\operatorname{pdom}(u)

To derive Theorem

, we start with a competitive equilibrium

An intuitive argument would be the following: we start from

), and use a tâtonnement-like procedure using the Gale-substitutes property. As long as

is not a minimizer of

, there will be an underdemanded item

.

Rather than using this continuous procedure, we establish

directly using Lagrangian duality. For any price vector
\mathbf{q}
, we get a subgradient in

from the difference between the supply and the total Gale demand. Further, for

we get that if

then the

of

to the boundary of the region, where this subgradient shows that

is also optimal.

In Section

9
Discussion

In this paper, we compared competitive equilibrium and Nash welfare maximizing solutions in the Fisher market setting. We identified Gale-substitutes as a broad family of utility functions where the Nash welfare uniformly guarantees every agent at least half of their maximum utility at any competitive equilibrium. We also introduced generalized network utilities. This is a very general class with a rich combinatorial structure and may be a relevant example in various market and fair division settings.

Gale-substitutes may be a class of concave functions worth investigating on its own. In the context of indivisible goods, the analogous class of gross substitute utilities, introduced by Keslo and Crawford (
Kelso and Crawford 1982
), turned out to be the same as the important class of
M^{\natural}
-concave utilities in discrete convex analysis
Fujishige and Yang 2003
.
In particular, one may wonder whether the Gale-substitutes property implies
submodularity
, that is,
u(x)+u(y)\geq u(x\vee y)+u(x\wedge y)
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{M}}
, where
x\vee y
and
x\wedge y
denote the pointwise maximum and minimum of the two vectors, respectively. This property trivially holds for separable utilities and can also be shown for generalized network utilities.

We note that one can construct an example of a concave quadratic function that is submodular but not Gale-substitutes. On the other hand, it is unclear whether Theorem
1.6
would remain true for submodular utilities instead of Gale-substitutes. One would also be interested in whether Theorem
1.6
holds for matching utilities, which, like submodular utilities, are not necessarily Gale-substitutesl(see Example
A.11
).
Another natural question related to the well-studied notion of
weak gross substitutes (WGS)
utilities: Gale-substitutes includes utility functions that are not WGS, such as concave separable; but it is not clear whether all WGS utilities are also Gale-substitutes.

Gale-substitutes utilities may also have potential algorithmic applications.
The WGS property leads to natural tâtonnement and auction mechanisms for finding competitive equilibria, see, e.g.,
Arrow et al. 1959
,
Arrow and Hurwicz 1960
,
Bei et al. 2019a
,
Codenotti et al. 2005b
,
Codenotti et al. 2005c
,
Garg et al. 2023a
. The Gale-substitutes property may be useful to design simple such mechanisms and combinatorial algorithms for solving the convex program (
EG
). For the linear case, Devanur et al. (
Devanur et al. 2008
) gave a primal-dual algorithm that was followed by a series of works on combinatorial algorithms for various market settings; see, e.g.,
Chaudhury et al. 2022
,
Cole and Gkatzelis 2015
,
Duan and Mehlhorn 2015
,
Garg and Végh 2019
.

A.6
(In)-approximate Nash Welfare utility by Equilibrium utility

In this paper, we show for
\Sigma
-Gale-substitues utilities, the utility of any agent at the Nash welfare maximizing allocation is at least half the maximum utility they may get at any CE. It’s natural to ask, inversely, if the utility agent can attain at any CE can approximate the utility they receive at Nash welfare maximizing allocation. We show a constant approximation is not achievable by providing an example in which the CE is unique and there exists an agent whose utility at this CE is much worse than his/her utility at Nash welfare maximizing allocation.

Example A.7

Consider a scenario with
n+2
