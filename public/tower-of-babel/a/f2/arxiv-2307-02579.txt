2.1.
The MacMahon Operator

First we define MacMahon’s Omega operator
\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}.

Definition 2.1
.

The operator
\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}
is given by

\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\sum_{s_{1}=-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{s_{r}=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}}\lambda_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{r}^{s_{r}}\coloneqq\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{s_{r}=0}^{\infty}A_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}},

where the domain of the
A_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}}
is the field of rational functions over
\mathbb{C}
in several complex variables and the
\lambda_{i}
are restricted to a neighborhood of the circle
|\lambda_{i}|=1.
In addition, the
A_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}}
are required to be such that any of the series involved is absolutely convergent within the domain of the definition of
A_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}}.

We will always have
\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}
operate on variables denoted by letters
\lambda,\mu
from the Greek alphabet (so that letters from the Latin alphabet will be unaffected by
\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}
).

The benefit of using the MacMahon operator comes from our ability to compute it quickly using elimination formulae. MacMahon
[
Mac04
, pp. 102-103]
gave a list of some of these, e.g.,

(3)

\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\frac{1}{(1-\lambda x)(1-\lambda^{-1}y)}=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-xy)}.

This formula can be generalized as follows.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
d
a positive integer and
j
an integer, we have that

\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{(1-\lambda x_{1})\cdots(1-\lambda x_{d})(1-\lambda^{-1}y)}=\frac{1}{(1-y)}\left[\frac{1}{(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})\cdots(1-x_{d})}\right.\\
\left.-\frac{y^{j+1}}{(1-x_{1}y)(1-x_{2}y)\cdots(1-x_{d}y)}\right].

Proof.

Expanding the left hand side using geometric series and applying the definition of
\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{(1-\lambda x_{1})\cdots(1-\lambda x_{d})(1-\lambda^{-1}y)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d+1}\geq 0}\lambda^{j+a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}-a_{d+1}}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}y^{a_{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\geq 0\\
0\,\leq\,k\,\leq\,{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}+j}\end{subarray}}\lambda^{k}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}y^{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}+j-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\geq 0}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}+j}y^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\geq 0}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}\left[\frac{1-y^{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}+j+1}}{1-y}\right],

and the result follows by simplifying the geometric series.
∎

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}h\cdot f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,1}}{\lambda_{n,1}}w\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,d}}{\lambda_{n,d}}w\right)}D_{1}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{\mu_{n,1}\dots\mu_{n,d}},q_{n+1};w\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}h\cdot f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,1}}{\lambda_{n,1}}w\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,d}}{\lambda_{n,d}}w\right)}\cdot\frac{F_{d}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}},w\right)}{p(\mathbf{y};\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d})},

where we set
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+2})\coloneqq(q_{n},q_{n}w,\dots,q_{n}w^{d},q_{n}q_{n+1}w^{d}).
Then, applying Lemma
2.6
and substituting
F_{d}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}},w\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}(w)\frac{q_{n}^{i}}{(\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d})^{i}}
(where
a_{i}(w)\in\mathbb{Z}[w]
depends on
d
) gives

\displaystyle D_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}h\cdot f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}\frac{F_{d}\left(\frac{q_{n}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}},w\right)}{\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,1}}{\lambda_{n,1}}w\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,d}}{\lambda_{n,d}}w\right)}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}\frac{p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})}{1-\frac{y_{j}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}(w)q_{n}^{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})\frac{1}{y_{j}^{i}}\left[\operatorname*{\Omega}_{\geq}\frac{h\cdot f_{1}\cdots f_{n-1}}{\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,1}}{\lambda_{n,1}}w\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{\mu_{n,d}}{\lambda_{n,d}}w\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{y_{j}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}}}\cdot\left(\frac{y_{j}}{\mu_{n,1}\cdots\mu_{n,d}}\right)^{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}(w)q_{n}^{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})\frac{1}{y_{j}^{i}}D_{n}^{(i)}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},y_{j};w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}(w)Q_{n}^{i}w^{din}\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},y_{j};w)

\displaystyle=F_{d}(Q_{n}w^{dn},w)\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},y_{j};w),

where we have used Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.7
. By the induction hypothesis, we may observe that

D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},y_{j};w)=\frac{1-Q_{n}w^{dn}}{1-Q_{n-1}y_{j}w^{dn}}D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},q_{n};w),

and thus we have

\displaystyle D_{n+1}=

\displaystyle F_{d}(Q_{n}w^{dn},w)(1-Q_{n}w^{dn})D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},q_{n};w)\sum_{j=1}^{d+2}\frac{p_{j}(\mathbf{y};y_{j})}{1-(q_{0}\dots q_{n-1})y_{j}w^{dn}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{d}(Q_{n}w^{dn},w)(1-Q_{n}w^{dn})D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},q_{n};w)\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{y};\frac{q_{n}}{Q_{n}w^{dn}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{d}(Q_{n}w^{dn},w)(1-Q_{n}w^{dn})D_{n}(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1},q_{n};w)

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{1}{(1-Q_{n}w^{dn})(1-Q_{n}w^{dn+1})\cdots(1-Q_{n}w^{dn+d})(1-Q_{n+1}w^{dn+d})}

by Lemma
2.6
with
z=(Q_{n-1}w^{dn})^{-1}.
Thus we have shown that

D_{n+1}=\frac{F_{d}(Q_{n}w^{dn},w)}{(1-Q_{n}w^{dn+1})\cdots(1-Q_{n}w^{dn+d})(1-Q_{n+1}w^{dn+d})}D_{n},

which gives the desired result.
∎

2.6.
Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

Now, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
can be obtained as corollaries of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Theorem
1.1
comes from applying Theorem
2.3
to

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{d}(n)q^{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D_{d,n}(q,q,\ldots,q;q).

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we first apply Theorem
2.3
to obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{d}(n)q^{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D_{d,n}(q,q,\ldots,q;1)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{d}(q^{n},1)}{(1-q^{n})^{d+1}},

so we must show that
F_{d}(q,1)=A_{d}(q).
Since
F_{1}(q,1)=A_{1}(q)=1,
it suffices to show that
F_{d}(q,1)
and
A_{d}(q)
satisfy the same recurrence (
2
).

Recalling the recurrence from Lemma
2.5
and writing
F_{d-1}(q,w)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(w)q^{i}
gives

\displaystyle F_{d}(q,w)(1-w)

\displaystyle=(1-qw^{d})F_{d-1}(q,w)-w(1-q)F_{d-1}(qw,w)

\displaystyle=(1-qw^{d})\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(w)q^{i}-w(1-q)\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(w)q^{i}w^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(w)q^{i}\left[1-qw^{d}-w^{i+1}+qw^{i+1}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-w)\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(w)q^{i}\left[(1+w+\cdots+w^{i})+q(w^{i+1}+\cdots+w^{d-1}))\right].

Finally, dividing by
w-1
and then setting
w=1
gives

\displaystyle F_{d}(q,1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(1)q^{i}\left[(i+1)+q(d-i-1))\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}a_{i}(1)q^{i}\left[1+(d-1)q+i(1-q)\right]

\displaystyle=(1+(d-1)q)F_{d-1}(q,1)+q(1-q)F^{\prime}_{d-1}(q,1),

as desired.
∎

3.
Ramanujan–Like Congruences Satisfied by
s_{d}(n)

We begin this section by noting the following corollary of Theorem
1.2
, which gives an alternate form of the generating function for
s_{d}(n)
.

Corollary 3.1
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{d}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)^{d}q^{jn}\right).

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorem
1.2
, together with the following identity proved by Euler in
[
Eul68
]
.

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)^{d}q^{jn}=\frac{A_{d}(q^{n})}{(1-q^{n})^{d+1}}.

∎

Remark
.

One can view Corollary
3.1
combinatorially by building an arbitrary Schmidt type
d
-fold partition diamond as follows. First, one may choose the nodes
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots
by picking the difference,
j_{n},
between
a_{n-1}
and
a_{n}
for each positive integer
n
(where
j_{n}=0
for sufficiently large
n
). This completely determines all of the linking nodes
a_{0}=\sum_{n\geq 1}j_{n},

a_{1}=\sum_{n\geq 2}j_{n},
etc., and the
q^{j_{n}n}
keeps track of their contribution. Then, there are
(j_{n}+1)^{d}
options for the nodes
b_{n,1},\ldots,b_{n,d}
(since there are
j_{n}+1
possibilities for each of those nodes).

We utilize this new view of the generating function for
s_{d}(n)
to easily prove a variety of arithmetic properties satisfied by these functions. Prior to doing so, we remind the reader of two well–known results which will also be helpful below:

Lemma 3.2
.

(Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem)

We have

\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n(3n+1)/2}.

Proof.

See
[
Hir17
, Eq. (1.6.1)]
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

(Jacobi)

We have

\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})^{3}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}(2n+1)q^{n(n+1)/2}.

Proof.

See
[
Hir17
, Eq. (1.7.1)]
.
∎

We now prove the following theorem which yields an infinite family of Ramanujan–like congruences modulo arbitrarily large powers of 2.

Theorem 3.4
.

For all
d\geq 1
and all
n\geq 0,

s_{d}(2n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{2^{d}}.

Proof.

For fixed
d\geq 1,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{d}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+2^{d}q^{n}+3^{d}q^{2n}+4^{d}q^{3n}+5^{d}q^{4n}+6^{d}q^{5n}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+0q^{n}+3^{d}q^{2n}+0q^{3n}+5^{d}q^{4n}+0q^{5n}+\dots\right)\pmod{2^{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+3^{d}q^{2n}+5^{d}q^{4n}+\dots\right).

In the penultimate line above, we have used the fact that
2^{d}\,|\,(2j)^{d}
for all
j\geq 1.
The last expression above is a function of
q^{2},
and this immediately implies the result.
∎

We note, in passing, that the
d=2
case of Theorem
3.4
was proven by Andrews and Paule
[
AP22
, Corollary 2]
.

We next prove the following overarching lemma that will provide us with the machinery needed to prove infinite families of divisibility properties satisfied by
s_{d}(n).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
k
and
r
be nonnegative integers and let
m\geq 2
. For all
n\geq 0,

s_{\phi(m)k+r}(n)\equiv s_{r}(n)\pmod{m}
where
\phi(m)
is Euler’s totient function.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{\phi(m)k+r}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+2^{\phi(m)k+r}q^{n}+3^{\phi(m)k+r}q^{2n}+4^{\phi(m)k+r}q^{3n}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.{}+5^{\phi(m)k+r}q^{4n}+6^{\phi(m)k+r}q^{5n}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+(2^{\phi(m)})^{k}\cdot 2^{r}q^{n}+(3^{\phi(m)})^{k}\cdot 3^{r}q^{2n}+(4^{\phi(m)})^{k}\cdot 4^{r}q^{3n}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.{}+(5^{\phi(m)})^{k}\cdot 5^{r}q^{4n}+(6^{\phi(m)})^{k}\cdot 6^{r}q^{5n}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+2^{r}q^{n}+3^{r}q^{2n}+4^{r}q^{3n}+5^{r}q^{4n}+6^{r}q^{5n}+\dots\right)\pmod{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{r}(n)q^{n}.

The penultimate line above follows from Euler’s generalization of Fermat’s Little Theorem.
∎

We now transition to a consideration of various families of congruences modulo 5. We first prove an infinite family of such congruences thanks to Lemma
3.5
which uses the function
s_{1}(n)
as its starting point.

Theorem 3.6
.

For all
k\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

s_{4k+1}(5n+2)\equiv s_{4k+1}(5n+3)\equiv s_{4k+1}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{1}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{n})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{3}}{(1-q^{n})^{5}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{3}}{(1-q^{5n})}\pmod{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(2j+1)q^{j(j+1)/2}\right)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}

thanks to Lemma
3.3
. Since
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}
is a function of
q^{5}
, the above product will satisfy a congruence modulo 5 in an arithmetic progression of the form
5n+r
, for
0\leq r\leq 4,
if and only if such a congruence is satisfied by

\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(2j+1)q^{j(j+1)/2}\right).

Since
5n+2
and
5n+4
are never triangular numbers, this immediately tells us that, for all
n\geq 0,

s_{1}(5n+2)\equiv s_{1}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Moreover, we know that
5n+3=j(j+1)/2
if and only if
j\equiv 2\pmod{5},
and in such cases,
2j+1\equiv 0\pmod{5}.
Because of the presence of the factor
2j+1
in the sum above, we then see that, for all
n\geq 0,

s_{1}(5n+3)\equiv 0\pmod{5}

since
2\cdot 2+1=5\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

The remainder of the proof immediately follows from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

We note, in passing, that Baruah and Sarmah
[
BS13
, Theorem 5.1, equation (5.4)]
also provided a proof of the
k=0
case of Theorem
3.6
.

We next consider the following modulo 5 congruences, with a focus on the case
d=2.

Theorem 3.7
.

For all
k\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

s_{4k+2}(25n+23)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Proof.

We begin by noting that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{2}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+q^{n}}{(1-q^{n})^{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{2n}}{(1-q^{n})^{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2n})(1-q^{n})}{(1-q^{n})^{5}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{2n})(1-q^{n})}{(1-q^{5n})}\pmod{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j,k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+k}q^{2(j(3j+1)/2)+k(3k+1)/2}\right)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}

thanks to Lemma
3.2
.
At this point, we consider those terms of the form
q^{25n+23}
in the power series representation of the last expression above. There are no terms of the form
q^{25n+8},q^{25n+13},q^{25n+18},
or
q^{25n+23}
in the double sum above, although there are terms of the form
q^{25n+3}.
Thus, once we multiply the double sum above by

(5)

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{5n},

we see that the only way to obtain a term of the form
q^{25n+23}
which can contribute to
s_{2}(25n+23)
modulo 5 is to multiply by a term of the form
q^{5(5m+4)}
from the power series representation of (
5
). This will then contribute, modulo 5, to the value of
s_{2}(25n+23)
by multiplying by the value
p(5m+4).
Thanks to Ramanujan’s well–known result that, for all
m\geq 0,

p(5m+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5},
we then know that, for all
n\geq 0,

s_{2}(25n+23)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.
The theorem then follows thanks to Lemma
3.5
.
∎

We can also show that the functions
s_{4k+3}
satisfy a rich set of congruences modulo 5 via the following:

Theorem 3.8
.

For all
k\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

s_{4k+3}(5n+2)\equiv s_{4k+3}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Proof.

We begin with the following generating function manipulations:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{3}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+4q^{n}+q^{2n}}{(1-q^{n})^{4}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{n}+q^{2n}}{(1-q^{n})^{4}}\pmod{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+(-q^{n})+(-q^{n})^{2}}{(1-q^{n})^{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-(-q^{n})^{3}}{(1-(-q^{n}))(1-q^{n})^{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+q^{3n}}{(1+q^{n})(1-q^{n})^{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{6n})(1-q^{n})^{2}}{(1-q^{3n})(1-q^{2n})(1-q^{n})^{5}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{6n})(1-q^{n})^{2}}{(1-q^{3n})(1-q^{2n})}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}\pmod{5}

We now consider the power series representation of

(6)

F(q):=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{6n})(1-q^{n})^{2}}{(1-q^{3n})(1-q^{2n})},

which turns out to be a well–known modular form; indeed, it appears as the seventh modular form in Mersmann’s list of the 14 primitive eta–products which
are holomorphic modular forms of weight 1/2. See
[
BvdGHZ08
, page 30]
and
[
OEI23
, Entry A089812]
for additional details. After some elementary calculations (for example, by noting that
qF(q^{8})
appears in
[
LO13
, Theorem 1.2]
), we find that

F(q)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{t(t+1)/2}-3\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{(3t+1)(3t+2)/2}.

As noted in the proof of Theorem
3.6
,
5n+2
and
5n+4
can never be triangular numbers. Since
\frac{1}{2}(3t+1)(3t+2)
is always triangular, we conclude that
s_{3}(5n+2)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
and
s_{3}(5n+4)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
. The case where
k>0
follows from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Related to the above, we can also prove the following additional congruence family modulo 5.

Theorem 3.9
.

For all
k\geq 0
and
n\geq 0,

s_{4k+3}(25n+23)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Proof.

As with the proof of Theorem
3.8
above, we begin by considering
s_{3}(n)
. Using the notation from above, recall that

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{3}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{t(t+1)/2}-3\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{(3t+1)(3t+2)/2}\right)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n})}\pmod{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{t(t+1)/2}-3\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q^{(3t+1)(3t+2)/2}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{5n}\right).

We then consider ways that
q^{25n+23}
can arise when (
7
) is expanded. We note that no numbers of the form
25n+8,25n+13,25n+18,
or
25n+23
can be represented as a triangular number. Thus, the only way to obtain a term of the form
q^{25n+23}
in (
7
) is to multiply by a term of the form
p(5m+4)q^{5(5m+4)}
(in the same way as was discussed in the proof of Theorem
3.7
). This implies that, for all
n\geq 0,

s_{3}(25n+23)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.
The full result follows from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

We close this section with one last infinite family of congruences, this time modulo 11.

Theorem 3.10
.

For all
n\geq 0,

s_{10k+1}(121n+111)\equiv 0\pmod{11}.

Proof.

In the work of Gordon
[
Gor83
, Theorem 2]
, we set
k=2
and
r=2,
so that
\alpha=1.
We then see that, if
24n\equiv 2\pmod{11^{2}},
then
12n\equiv 1\pmod{121}
or
n\equiv 111\pmod{121}
. Gordon’s work then implies that, for all
n\geq 0,

s_{1}(121n+111)\pmod{11}
.
The full result then follows from Lemma
3.5
.

∎
