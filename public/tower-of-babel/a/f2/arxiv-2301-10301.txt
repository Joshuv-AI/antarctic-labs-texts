Abstract
. We study a class of regularized proximal operators in Wasserstein-2 space. We derive their solutions by kernel integration formulas. We obtain the Wasserstein proximal operator using a pair of forward-backward partial differential equations consisting of a continuity equation and a Hamilton-Jacobi equation with a terminal time potential function and an initial time density function. Following [ 14 , 23 ] , we regularize the PDE pair by adding forward and backward Laplacian operators. We apply Hopf-Cole type transformations to rewrite these regularized PDE pairs into forward-backward heat equations. We then use the fundamental solution of the heat equation to represent the regularized Wasserstein proximal with kernel integral formulas. Numerical examples show the effectiveness of kernel formulas in approximating the Wasserstein proximal operator.



1.
Introduction

Proximal operators are essential tools in optimization. Recently, proximal operators in probability space equipped with the Wasserstein-2 metric have shown to be useful in scientific computing
[
20
]
and machine learning
[
4
,
21
]
. The proximal operator in Wasserstein-2 space is called the Wasserstein proximal operator
[
1
]
. An interesting example is the Wasserstein proximal operator of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, known as the JKO (Jordan-Kinderlehrer-Otto) scheme
[
15
]
. This has been used to approximate the Wasserstein gradient flow as a backward Euler scheme in time discretizations.

However, computing the Wasserstein proximal operator requires an optimization procedure. One has to develop an optimization step to compute or approximate Wasserstein metrics and energy functionals.
This paper proposes an alternative approach to approximate the Wasserstein proximal operator. We use an optimal control formulation of the Wasserstein proximal operator, whose minimizer forms a pair of forward-backward partial differential equations, including continuity and Hamilton-Jacobi equations. We add forward-backward Laplacian operators into the PDE system as a regularization. Applying Hopf-Cole type transformations enables us to rewrite the PDE system into forward-backward heat equations. Using the fundamental solution of two heat equations, we write the regularized Wasserstein proximal of linear energy explicitly with a simple kernel formula. We apply the same idea to the regularized Wasserstein proximal of nonlinear energy. This turns into a nonlinear integral equation, which can be solved by simple fixed-point iteration methods.

We now present the approximation of Wasserstein proximal of linear energy below. Given a probability density function
\rho_{0}
with the finite second moment, a bounded potential function
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and a scalar constant
T>0
, consider the Wasserstein proximal operator of the linear energy below:

\rho_{T}:=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{V}}(\rho_{0}):=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)q(x)dx+\frac{\mathcal{W}(\rho_{0},q)^{2}}{2T}.

where
\mathcal{W}(\rho_{0},q)
is the Wasserstein-2 distance between
\rho_{0}
and
q
, and the minimization is taken among all probability density functions
q
with the finite second moment. We denote the minimizer
\rho_{T}=q
as the Wasserstein proximal operator. According to the Benamou-Brenier formula
[
3
]
and simple derivations in section
2
, the Wasserstein proximal operator forms an optimal control problem, whose minimizer satisfies a forward-backward PDE system:

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho(t,x)\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\big)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\|^{2}=0,\\
&\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\Phi(T,x)=-V(x).\end{aligned}\right.

Here
t\in[0,T]
, and
\rho_{T}
satisfies the Wasserstein proximal operator of linear energy.

In this paper, motivated by regularized proximal operators in Euclidean space
[
14
,
23
]
, we study a regularized PDE system of equation (
1
). For a constant scalar
\beta>0
, we have

(2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho(t,x)\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\big)=\beta\Delta_{x}\rho(t,x),\\
&\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\|^{2}=-\beta\Delta_{x}\Phi(t,x),\\
&\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\Phi(T,x)=-V(x).\end{aligned}\right.

By applying Hopf-Cole type transformations, we demonstrate that equation (
2
) has the solution below:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho(t,x)=(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x)\cdot(G_{t}*\frac{\rho_{0}}{G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}}})(x),\\
&\Phi(t,x)=2\beta\log(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x).\end{aligned}\right.

where
*
is a convolution operator and
G_{t}
is the scaled heat kernel denoted as

G_{t}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(4\pi\beta t)^{d}}}e^{-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{4\beta t}}.

In particular, we obtain an integral representation for the terminal density function:

(3)

\rho(T,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)\rho(0,y)dy,

where
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a kernel function

K(x,y):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

For a fixed variable
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the kernel function
K(x,y)
is a Gibbs distribution or softmax function of variable
x
with constant
\frac{1}{2\beta}
and function
V(x)+\frac{1}{2T}\|x-y\|^{2}
.
Formula (
3
) is a kernel integration representation of the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator. It could be useful in approximating or computing high-dimensional sampling problems; see previous studies in
[
14
,
23
]
.

Wasserstein proximal operators have been widely studied
[
1
]
. The regularized Wasserstein proximal dynamics (
2
) and their Hopf-Cole type transformations
[
22
]
have been widely studied in Schrödinger bridge systems
[
8
,
25
,
26
]
and Schrödinger equations
[
5
,
22
]
; see also generalized Hopf-Cole type transformations
[
18
]
.
In particular, there are Schrödinger-Follermer diffusions
[
11
]
, which are closely related to the equation system (
2
). We also remark that the optimal control formulation of the Wasserstein-proximal operator is a variational problem in potential mean-field games; see
[
16
]
. It has been used in studying and computing particular classes of mean-field control and mean-field games
[
12
,
13
]
. Compared to all the above studies, we study Schrödinger bridge systems with different boundary conditions. It contains a given initial time density function and a terminal time potential function, different from fixed initial and terminal time density functions as in Schrödinger bridge systems. This allows us to obtain a closed-form kernel integration update, at least for regularized Wasserstein proximal operators of linear energies. For nonlinear potential energies, our kernel formula also provides an iterative update to compute Wasserstein proximal operators.

The paper is organized as follows. We introduce Wasserstein proximal operators of linear energies, their diffusion regularizations, and minimization systems in section
2
. In section
3
, we derive the kernel representation of regularized Wasserstein proximal of linear energies. Finally, we generalize the kernel formulation for Wasserstein proximal operators of nonlinear energies in section
4
. Numerical experiments of proposed kernel functions are presented in section
5
.

2.
Regularized Wasserstein proximal operators of linear energies

In this section, we study a class of mean-field control problems that lead to regularized proximal operators of linear energies in Wasserstein-2 space. We derive the minimizing equations, which is a forward-backward PDE system.

2.1.
Wasserstein proximal operators of linear energies

Consider a linear energy functional

\mathcal{V}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\rho(x)dx,

where
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a given function. Denote a terminal time
T>0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the probability density set with finite second moment. Consider the proximal operator of a linear energy in Wasserstein-2 space.

(4)

\rho_{T}:=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{V}}(\rho_{0}):=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\mathcal{V}(q)+\frac{\mathcal{W}(\rho_{0},q)^{2}}{2T}.

Here
\mathcal{W}
denotes the Wasserstein-2 distance between two densities
\rho^{0}
and
\rho
; see
[
1
,
24
]
. Recall that the Wasserstein-2 distance can be recast as an optimal control problem, known as Benamou-Brenier formula
[
3
]
. Consider

\frac{\mathcal{W}(\rho_{0},q)^{2}}{2T}:=\inf_{\rho,v,\rho_{T}}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|v(t,x)\|^{2}\rho(t,x)dxdt,

where the minimizer is taken among all vector fields
v\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, density functions
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot(\rho(t,x)v(t,x))=0,\quad\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\rho(T,x)=q(x).

Wasserstein proximal operators are useful in designing sampling algorithms. However, the Wasserstein proximal operator (
4
) does not have a closed-form update. One needs to apply numerical methods to compute the variational problem (
4
).

2.2.
Regularized Wasserstein proximal operators of linear energies

In this subsection, we consider a regularized Wasserstein proximal operator, which adds a diffusive term to the continuity equation of the Benamou-Brenier formula. This regularization forms an interesting mean-field control problem. We then derive the related minimizing system with initial-terminal conditions. In the next section, we demonstrate that this minimizing system has a closed-form kernel representation.

Definition 1
.

Let
\beta>0
be a scalar, and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm. Consider the following mean-field control problem:

(5)

\inf_{\rho,v,q}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|v(t,x)\|^{2}\rho(t,x)dxdt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)q(x)dx,

where the minimizer is taken among all drift vector fields
v\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, density functions
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and terminal time density function
q\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot(\rho(t,x)v(t,x))=\beta\Delta\rho(t,x),\quad\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\rho(T,x)=q(x).

We denote the regularized proximal operator as

\rho_{T}:=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{V},\beta}(\rho_{0}),

where
\rho_{T}
is the solution for the density function in the variational problem (
5
) at time
t=T
.

Remark 1
.

We note that when
\beta=0
, variational problem (
5
) is the minimization problem (
4
). We add a diffusion into the continuity equation in the variational problem (
5
). This forms the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator.

We next derive the minimization system for mean-field control problem (
5
).

Proposition 2

(Regularized Wasserstein proximal dynamics)
.

There exists a Lagrange multiplier function
\Phi\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that
(\rho,\Phi)
satisfies the following forward-backward PDE system.

(6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho(t,x)\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\big)=\beta\Delta_{x}\rho(t,x),\\
&\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}\Phi(t,x)\|^{2}=-\beta\Delta_{x}\Phi(t,x),\\
&\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\Phi(T,x)=-V(x).\end{aligned}\right.

Here the time variable satisfies
t\in[0,T]
,
\rho(t,x)
is the density function, and
\Phi(t,x)
is the potential function. Equation system (
6
) consists of a forward-time Fokker-Planck equation and a backward-time viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation (Burgers’ equation). And the terminal time density function is the regularized Wasserstein proximal density:

\rho(T,x)=q(x)=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{V},\beta}(\rho_{0}).

Proof.

Denote a Lagrange multiplier function
\Phi
. Consider the following saddle point problem

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,v,\rho_{T}\end{subarray}}\sup_{\Phi}~\mathcal{L}(\rho,v,\rho_{T},\Phi),

where

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\rho,v,\rho_{T},\Phi):=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|v(t,x)\|^{2}\rho(t,x)dxdt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\rho(T,x)dx\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)\Big(\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot(\rho(t,x)v(t,x))-\beta\Delta\rho(t,x)\Big)dxdt\\
=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|v(t,x)\|^{2}\rho(t,x)dxdt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\rho(T,x)dx\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(T,x)\rho(T,x)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(0,x)\rho(0,x)dx\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(-\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x)\rho(t,x)-(\nabla\Phi(t,x),v(t,x))\rho(t,x)-\beta\Phi(t,x)\Delta\rho(t,x)\Big)dxdt.\end{split}

The saddle point system satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\delta}{\delta v}\mathcal{L}=0,\\
&\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi}\mathcal{L}=0,\\
&\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho}\mathcal{L}=0,\\
&\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho_{T}}\mathcal{L}=0.\end{aligned}\right.\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho(v-\nabla\Phi)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho v)-\beta\Delta\rho=0,\\
&\frac{\|v\|^{2}}{2}-\partial_{t}\Phi-\nabla\Phi\cdot v-\beta\Delta\Phi=0\\
&\Phi(T,x)+V(x)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Substituting
v=\nabla\Phi
into the third equality, we derive two PDEs (
6
). Thus, we denote
q(x)=\rho(T,x)
.
∎

Remark 2
.

We repeat that
\rho_{T}=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{V},\beta}(\rho_{0})
, where
\rho_{T}(x)=\rho(T,x)
represents the density function at the terminal time
t=T
in PDE system (
6
).

3.
Kernel formulation

In this section, we derive
\rho_{T}
with a kernel function (
14
). We solve a pair of forward-backward PDE system involving a viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation with a fixed terminal time boundary condition. By using a Hopf-Cole type transformation, we rewrite the PDEs into forward-backward heat equations with nonlinear initial-terminal time conditions. Thus we solve the original PDE system (
6
).

Proposition 3

(Hopf-Cole type transformations)
.

Define a pair of variables
(\eta,\hat{\eta})
as

(7)

\eta(t,x)=e^{\frac{\Phi(t,x)}{2\beta}},\quad\hat{\eta}(t,x)=\rho(t,x)e^{-\frac{\Phi(t,x)}{2\beta}}.

I.e.,

\Phi(t,x)=2\beta\log\eta(t,x),\quad\rho(t,x)=\eta(t,x)\hat{\eta}(t,x).

Then equation (
6
) in term of
(\eta,\hat{\eta})
satisfies forward-backward heat equations with a nonlinear initial-terminal time boundary condition:

(8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\hat{\eta}(t,x)=\beta\Delta_{x}\hat{\eta}(t,x),\\
&\partial_{t}\eta(t,x)=-\beta\Delta_{x}\eta(t,x),\\
&\eta(0,x)\hat{\eta}(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\eta(T,x)=e^{\frac{\Phi(T,x)}{2\beta}}=e^{-\frac{V(x)}{2\beta}}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

The proof follows from classical calculations in Hopf-Cole type transformations. For completeness of paper, we present the derivation of forwrad-backward heat equations (
8
). Denote
\eta(t,x)=e^{\frac{\Phi(t,x)}{2\beta}}
. Then

\partial_{t}\eta=\partial_{t}(e^{\frac{\Phi(t,x)}{2\beta}})=\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\partial_{t}\Phi.

And

\begin{split}\Delta\eta=&\nabla\cdot(\nabla\eta)=\nabla\cdot(\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\nabla\Phi)\\
=&\frac{1}{2\beta}(\nabla e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}},\nabla\Phi)+\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Delta\Phi\\
=&\frac{1}{(2\beta)^{2}}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\|\nabla\Phi\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Delta\Phi\\
=&\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}e^{\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\Phi\|^{2}+\beta\Delta\Phi\Big).\end{split}

From the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
6
), we have

\partial_{t}\eta+\beta\Delta\eta=0.

Denote
\hat{\eta}(t,x)=\rho(t,x)e^{-\frac{\Phi(t,x)}{2\beta}}
. Then

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\hat{\eta}=&e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Big\{\partial_{t}\rho-\rho\frac{1}{2\beta}\partial_{t}\Phi\Big\}.\end{split}

And

\begin{split}\nabla\hat{\eta}=&\nabla(\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}})=\nabla\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}+\rho\nabla e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\\
=&\nabla\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\nabla\Phi e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}.\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{\eta}=&\nabla\cdot(\nabla\hat{\eta})=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\nabla\Phi e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}})\\
=&\Delta\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}+(\nabla\rho,\nabla e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}})-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\nabla\rho,\nabla\Phi)e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\Delta\Phi e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho(\nabla\Phi,\nabla e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}})\\
=&\Delta\rho e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{\beta}(\nabla\rho,\nabla\Phi)e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\Delta\Phi e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}+\frac{1}{(2\beta)^{2}}\rho\|\nabla\Phi\|^{2}\\
=&e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Big\{\Delta\rho-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\Phi)+\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\Delta\Phi+\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\rho\|\nabla\Phi\|^{2}\Big\}\\
=&\frac{1}{\beta}e^{-\frac{\Phi}{2\beta}}\Big\{\partial_{t}\rho-\frac{1}{2\beta}\rho\partial_{t}\Phi\Big\},\end{split}

where we use the fact
\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\Phi)=(\nabla\rho,\nabla\Phi)+\rho\Delta\Phi
.
Hence we have

\partial_{t}\hat{\eta}-\beta\Delta\hat{\eta}=0.

∎

We next apply the heat kernel to represent the solution of PDE system (
8
). From equation (
7
), we can derive solutions
\rho(t,x)
and
\Phi(t,x)
in terms of
\rho_{0}(x)
and
V(x)
.

Proposition 4

(Kernel solutions)
.

Denote a scaled heat kernel function:

G_{t}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(4\pi\beta t)^{d}}}e^{-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{4\beta t}},\quad t\in(0,T].

Assume that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz<+\infty.

Then the following solutions hold.

(9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho(t,x)=(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x)\cdot(G_{t}*\frac{\rho_{0}}{G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}}})(x),\\
&\Phi(t,x)=2\beta\log(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x).\end{aligned}\right.

So

(10)

\rho(t,x)=\frac{1}{{(4\pi\beta\frac{t(T-t)}{T}})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+\frac{\|x-z\|^{2}}{2(T-t)}+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2t})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(\tilde{y})+\frac{\|y-\tilde{y}\|^{2}}{2T})}d\tilde{y}}\rho(0,y)dydz,

and

(11)

\Phi(t,x)=2\beta\log\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta(T-t))^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(y)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2(T-t)})}dy\Big).

Proof.

Denote
\eta_{t}(x)=\eta(t,x)
. From
\partial_{t}\eta+\beta\Delta\eta=0
, we have

(12)

\displaystyle\eta(t,x)=(G_{T-t}*\eta_{T})(x)=(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x).

When
t=0
, we have

\eta(0,x)=(G_{T}*\eta_{T})(x)=(G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}}).

From the initial time boundary condition
\rho_{0}(x)=\eta(0,x)\hat{\eta}(0,x)
, we have

\hat{\eta}(0,x)=\frac{\rho(0,x)}{\eta(0,x)}=\frac{\rho_{0}(x)}{(G_{T}*\eta_{T})(x)}=\frac{\rho_{0}(x)}{(G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x)}.

And from
\partial_{t}\hat{\eta}-\beta\Delta\hat{\eta}=0
, we have

(13)

\hat{\eta}(t,x)=(G_{t}*\hat{\eta}_{0})(x)=(G_{t}*\frac{\rho_{0}}{G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}}})(x).

Plugging solution (
12
) and (
13
) into Hopf-Cole transformation (
7
), we obtain

\rho(t,x)=\eta(t,x)\cdot\hat{\eta}(t,x)=(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x)\cdot(G_{t}*\frac{\rho_{0}}{G_{T}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}}})(x),

and

\Phi(t,x)=2\beta\log\eta(t,x)=2\beta\log(G_{T-t}*e^{-\frac{V}{2\beta}})(x).

∎

In particular, we represent the solution
\rho(T,x)
by an integral kernel formula.

Proposition 5
.

\rho(T,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y,\beta,T,V)\rho(0,y)dy,

where
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the kernel function

(14)

K(x,y,\beta,T,V):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

Proof.

We apply
t=T
in update (
9
) for the density function.
∎

Remark 3
.

One can check that
\rho(t,x)
in formula (
10
) is a probability density function. I.e.,
\rho(t,x)\geq 0
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t,x)dx=1
. The fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t,x)dx
is independent of
t
follows the first equation in (
6
). In detail, formula (
10
) can be recast as

\rho(t,x)=\frac{1}{{(4\pi\beta\frac{t(T-t)}{T}})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+\frac{\|y-z\|^{2}}{2T})}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}\frac{\|x-\frac{tz+(T-t)y}{T}\|^{2}}{2\frac{(T-t)t}{T}}}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(\tilde{y})+\frac{\|y-\tilde{y}\|^{2}}{2T})}d\tilde{y}}\rho(0,y)dydz.

From this formula, we directly observe that
\rho(t,x)
is a probability density function.

) is a solution for the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation, and

approximates the proximal operator of

in Euclidean space; see
[

,

]
.

.

,

]
. One can construct Schrödinger-Föllmer diffusion processes

Example 1
.

Consider

V(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x_{0}\|^{2},

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a fixed vector. From (
11
), we have

\begin{split}\Phi(t,x)=&2\beta\log\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta(T-t))^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\frac{\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2(T-t)})}dy\Big)\\
=&\beta d\log\frac{1}{T-t+1}-\frac{\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}}{2(T-t+1)}.\end{split}

From (
10
), we have

\begin{split}\rho(t,x)=&\frac{1}{{(4\pi\beta\frac{t(T-t)}{T}})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\frac{1}{2}\|z-x_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\|x-z\|^{2}}{2(T-t)}+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2t})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{y}-x_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\|y-\tilde{y}\|^{2}}{2T})}d\tilde{y}}\rho_{0}(y)dydz\\
=&\frac{1}{{(4\pi\beta\frac{t(T-t+1)}{T+1}})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\beta\frac{t(T-t+1)}{T+1}}(x-\frac{\frac{y}{t}+\frac{x_{0}}{T-t+1}}{\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{T-t+1}})^{2}}\rho_{0}(y)dy.\end{split}

From (
14
), we obtain

\begin{split}\rho(T,x)=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\frac{1}{2}\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(\frac{1}{2}\|z-x_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}\rho_{0}(y)dy\\
=&\frac{1}{{(4\pi\beta\frac{T}{T+1}})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\beta\frac{T}{T+1}}(x-\frac{\frac{y}{T}+x_{0}}{\frac{1}{T}+1})^{2}}\rho_{0}(y)dy.\end{split}

4.
Regularized Wasserstein proximal operators of general energies

In this section, we study the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator of general energy functionals.

Consider a functional
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We study the proximal operator of energy
\mathcal{F}
in Wasserstein-2 space.

\rho_{T}=\mathrm{WProx}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho_{0}):=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\mathcal{F}(q)+\frac{\mathcal{W}(\rho_{0},q)^{2}}{2T}.

Computing the Wasserstein proximal operator usually requires an optimization step. Instead, we study a regularized optimal control problem.

Definition 6
.

Denote
\beta>0
as a scalar. Consider the following mean-field control problem:

(15)

\inf_{\rho,v,q}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|v(t,x)\|^{2}\rho(t,x)dxdt+\mathcal{F}(q),

where the minimizer is taken among all vector fields
v\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, density functions
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and terminal time density function
q\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot(\rho(t,x)v(t,x))=\beta\Delta\rho(t,x),\quad\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\rho(T,x)=q(x).

We denote the regularized proximal operator as

\rho_{T}:=\mathrm{WProx}_{T\mathcal{F},\beta}(\rho_{0}),

where
\rho_{T}
is the solution of density function in variational problem (
15
) at time
t=T
.

We can also represent the approximate proximal operator in a kernel representation.

Proposition 7
.

Denote the
L^{2}
first variation of functional
\mathcal{F}
as

\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho}\mathcal{F}(\rho)(x)=F(x,\rho).

Then

(16)

\rho(T,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T})\rho(0,y)dy,

where we define a kernel function
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(17)

K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T}):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(F(x,\rho_{T})+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(F(z,\rho_{T})+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

Proof.

The proof follows from those in sections
2
and
3
. We only need to replace
V(x)
by
\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho}\mathcal{F}(\rho)(x)=F(x,\rho)
.
∎

Remark 6
.

We remark that for a general functional
\mathcal{F}(\rho)
, the kernel formula
K
depends on
\rho_{T}
. We no longer have an explicit expression of
\rho_{T}
. Instead, we make use of equation (
16
).

4.1.
Examples

In this section, we present some examples of kernel representations.
Consider an energy functional

\mathcal{F}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\rho(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(x-y)\rho(x)\rho(y)dxdy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(\rho(x))dx,

where
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
,
W\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
is an interaction kernel function, with
W(z)=W(-z)
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
U\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
is an internal potential function.
The
L^{2}
first variation of
\mathcal{F}(\rho)
satisfies

F(x,\rho)=\frac{\delta}{\delta\rho}\mathcal{F}(\rho)(x)=V(x)+(W*\rho)(x)+U^{\prime}(\rho(x)).

Thus the kernel function (
17
) satisfies

K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T}):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(x)+(W*\rho_{T})(x)+U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(x))+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z)+(W*\rho_{T})(z)+U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(z))+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

And the regularization Wasserstein proximal equation forms

\rho_{T}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T})\rho_{0}(y)dy.

We next present some examples for equation (
16
) and kernel function (
17
).

Example 2
.

Let
V=U=0
. Then the kernel function (
17
) satisfies

K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T}):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}((W*\rho_{T})(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}((W*\rho_{T})(z)+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

And the regularized Wasserstein proximal equation satisfies

\rho_{T}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}((W*\rho_{T})(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}((W*\rho_{T})(z)+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}\rho_{0}(y)dy.

Example 3
.

Let
V=W=0
. Then the kernel function (
17
) satisfies

K(x,y,\beta,T,\rho_{T}):=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(x))+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(z))+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}.

And the regularized Wasserstein proximal equation satisfies

\rho_{T}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(x))+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(U^{\prime}(\rho_{T}(z))+\frac{\|z-y\|^{2}}{2T})}dz}\rho_{0}(y)dy.

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we conduct several computational examples to numerically verify the kernel formulations (
14
) and (
17
). We also discuss a fixed point iteration algorithm that uses the kernel formulation to calculate the Wasserstein proximal operator of general functionals.

5.1.
Validation the kernel formulation with optimization method

5.1.1.
Solve the equivalent variational problem via primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) method

To numerically verify kernel formulations, we compute the mean-field control problem (
15
) via the primal-dual hybrid gradient approach. By introducing the flux variable
m=\rho v
, we can rewrite the mean-field control problem as the following constrained optimization problem:

(18)

\inf_{\rho,m,q}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\|m(t,x)\|^{2}}{2\rho(t,x)}dxdt+\mathcal{F}(q),

such that

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot m(t,x)=\beta\Delta\rho(t,x),\quad\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),\quad\rho(T,x)=q(x).

By introducing a Lagrangian multiplier
\Phi
, we write optimization problem (
18
) into the following saddle point problem:

(19)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,m,q\\
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}(\cdot)\\
\rho(T,\cdot)=q(\cdot)\end{subarray}}\sup_{\Phi}\quad\mathcal{L}(\rho,m,q,\Phi),

where

(20)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\rho,m,q,\Phi):=&\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\|m(t,x)\|^{2}}{2\rho(t,x)}dxdt+\mathcal{F}(q)\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)\Big(\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot m(t,x)-\beta\Delta\rho(t,x)\Big)dxdt.\end{split}

Saddle point problem (
19
) is convex in
(\rho,m,q)
and concave in
(\Phi)
. We can directly apply the primal-dual hybrid gradient method (PDHG)
[
6
]
to solve problem (
19
). The algorithm takes proximal updates in the primal variables
(\rho,m,q)
and the dual variables
(\Phi)
iteratively, with extra extrapolating steps. For more details on using the PDHG algorithm to solve mean-field control problems, we refer to
[
17
]
.

5.1.2.
Discretization Scheme

For simplicity, we consider a one dimensional finite domain
\Omega=[-b,b]
with the periodic boundary condition. In the following examples, we set up a large enough finite spatial domain with density
\rho
vanishing near the boundary.
We use a uniform mesh for both spatial and time intervals,
with
h_{x}=\frac{2b}{N_{x}},h_{t}=\frac{T}{N_{T}}
,
for
N_{x},N_{T}>0
, and
(t_{l},x_{j})=(lh_{t},jh_{x}-b)
, for
l=0,\cdots,N_{t}
,
j=0,\cdots,N_{x}-1
.
For variables
\rho,\Phi,q
, we denote the following grid point approximation:

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{l}=\rho(t_{l},x_{j}),\;\quad\Phi_{j}^{l}=\Phi(t_{l},x_{j}),\;\quad q_{j}=q(x_{j}).

As for the flux variable
m
, we denote

\displaystyle m_{j}^{l}=[m_{j}^{+,l}+m_{j}^{-,l}],\quad m_{j}^{+,l}:=\left(m(t_{l},x_{j})\right)^{+},\quad m_{j}^{-,l}:=-\left(m(t_{l},x_{j})\right)^{-};

\displaystyle\|m_{j}^{l}\|^{2}=\left(m_{j}^{+,l}\right)^{2}+\left(m_{j}^{-,l}\right)^{2},\quad\nabla_{x}\cdot m_{j}^{l}=\frac{m_{j}^{+,l}-m_{j-1}^{+,l}}{h_{x}}+\frac{m_{j+1}^{-,l}-m_{j}^{-,l}}{h_{x}},

where
u^{+}:=\max(u,0)
and
u^{-}=u^{+}-u
. We discretize the above variational problem with the finite difference method and obtain the following first-order scheme:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,m,\Phi,q)=

\displaystyle\quad h_{t}h_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq N_{t}\\
1\leq j\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\|m_{j}^{l}\|^{2}}{2\rho_{j}^{l}}\right)+\hat{\mathcal{F}}(q)

\displaystyle+h_{t}h_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq l\leq N_{t}-1\\
1\leq j\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}\Phi^{l}_{j}\left(\dfrac{\rho^{l+1}_{j}-\rho^{l}_{j}}{h_{t}}+{\nabla_{x}\cdot m_{j}^{l+1}}-\beta\frac{\rho^{l+1}_{j+1}+\rho^{l+1}_{j-1}-2\rho^{l+1}_{j}}{h_{x}^{2}}\right).

More specifically, we consider the discretized energy functional
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
as below:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}(q)=h_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}\left(V(x_{j})q_{j}+U\left(q_{j}\right)\right)+\frac{h_{x}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N_{x}\\
1\leq j\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}\left(W(x_{i}-x_{j})q_{i}q_{j}\right).

Here, we denote
\rho_{M}^{T}(x):=\rho(T,x)
and use it to compare with the solution obtained from the kernel formulation.

As for computation using kernel formulation, we use the Riemann sum.
Taking the linear energy functional as an example, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}(\rho)=\int_{\Omega}V(x)\rho(x)dx
, the kernel function in equation (
14
) can be approximated by the following form:

K(x_{i},y_{j},\beta,T,V)=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(x_{i})+\frac{\|x_{i}-y_{j}\|^{2}}{2T})}}{h_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}(V(z_{k})+\frac{\|z_{k}-y_{j}\|^{2}}{2T})}}.

Hence the solution
\rho(T,x_{i})
via kernel integral formula can be expressed as

\rho(T,x_{i})=h_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N_{x}\end{subarray}}K(x_{i},y_{j},\beta,T,V)\rho(0,y_{j}).

From now on, we denote
\rho_{K}^{T}(x):=\rho(T,x)
.
