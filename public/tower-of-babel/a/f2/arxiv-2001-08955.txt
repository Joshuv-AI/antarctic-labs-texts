The model structure for chain complexes
N. P. Strickland

Abstract




Let
\operatorname{Ch}
be the category of (possibly unbounded) chain complexes of
abelian groups. In this note we construct the standard Quillen model
structure on
\operatorname{Ch}
, by a method that is somewhat different from the
standard one. Essentially, we use a functorial two-stage projective
resolution for abelian groups, and build everything directly from
that. This has the advantage of being very concrete, explicit and
functorial. It does not rely on the small object argument, or make
any explicit use of transfinite induction. On the other hand, it is
not so conceptual, and it does use the fact that subgroups of free
abelian groups are free, so it does not generalise to many rings other
than
{\mathbb{Z}}
(but see Remark
26
). We do not claim any great
technical benefit for this approach, but it seems like an interesting
alternative, and may be pedagogically useful.

We refer to
[
1
]
for the general theory of model categories.

Definition 1
.

Consider a map
f\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
in
\operatorname{Ch}
. We say
that:

•

f
is a cofibration iff it is injective, and

\operatorname{cok}(f)_{n}
is a free abelian group for all
n
.

•

f
is a fibration iff it is surjective.

•

f
is a weak equivalence iff it is a
quasiisomorphism, which means that
f_{*}\colon H_{*}A\to H_{*}B
is an
isomorphism.

•

f
is an acyclic cofibration iff it is a cofibration and a
weak equivalence, or equivalently a monomorphism whose cokernel is
acyclic and free in each degree.

•

f
is an acyclic fibration iff it is a fibration and a weak
equivalence, or equivalently an epimorphism with acyclic kernel.

Theorem 2
.

This defines a cofibrantly generated model structure on
\operatorname{Ch}
, which
is left and right proper and also monoidal.

The rest of this document will constitute the proof.

Definition 3
.

For any abelian group
A
, we let
I(A)
be the kernel of the
augmentation
\epsilon\colon{\mathbb{Z}}[A]\to{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\theta\colon{\mathbb{Z}}[A]\to A
be the usual
map given by
\theta(\sum_{i}n_{i}[a_{i}])=\sum_{i}n_{i}a_{i}
, and put

I^{2}(A)=\ker(\theta\colon I(A)\to A)
. Note that
I
and
I^{2}
give
functors
\operatorname{Ab}\to\operatorname{Ab}
that are nonadditive, but they do preserve zero
morphisms, so they induce functors
\operatorname{Ch}\to\operatorname{Ch}
. Note also that

I(A)
is freely generated by elements
[a]-[0]
for
a\in A\setminus 0
.
As subgroups of free abelian groups are always free we see that

I^{2}(A)
is also free, although in this case there is no obvious
choice of basis.

Remark 4
.

It is well known and not hard to check that
I^{2}(A)
is the square of
the ideal
I(A)
in the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[A]
, but we will not need this.

Construction 5
.

Let
f\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
be a chain map. Define a graded group

W_{*}=W_{*}(f)
by

W_{*}=A_{*}\oplus I(B_{*})\oplus\Sigma^{-1}I(B_{*}).

We write
a,\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
for typical elements of the groups
A_{n}
,

I(B_{n})
and
I(B_{n+1})
. We define
d^{W}\colon W_{n}\to W_{n-1}
by

d^{W}(a+\beta+\Sigma^{-1}\beta^{\prime})=da+d\beta-\Sigma^{-1}(\beta+d\beta^{\prime}).

We also let
j\colon A_{*}\to W_{*}
be the inclusion, and define

p\colon W_{*}\to B_{*}
by
p(a+\beta+\Sigma^{-1}\beta^{\prime})=f(a)+\theta(\beta)
.

Proposition 6
.

W_{*}
is a chain complex, and
j
and
p
are chain maps with
pj=f
.
Moreover,
j
is an acyclic cofibration and
p
is a fibration.

Proof.

It is a straightforward computation to check that
d^{W}d^{W}=0
, so

W_{*}
is a chain complex. It is visible that
d^{W}j=jd
, and it is
again straightforward to check that
pd^{W}=dp
, so
j
and
p
are
chain maps, and clearly
pj=f
. The map
j
is injective, and the
cokernel is just the cone on
\Sigma^{-1}I(B_{*})
, which is contractible
and free in each degree; this means that
j
is an acyclic
cofibration. Also, as
\theta
is surjective we see that
p
is an
epimorphism and thus a fibration.
∎

Construction 7
.

Let
f\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
be a chain map. Define a graded group

X_{*}=X_{*}(f)
by

X_{*}=A_{*}\oplus\Sigma I(A_{*})\oplus\Sigma^{2}I^{2}(A_{*})\oplus I(B_{*})\oplus\Sigma I(B_{*}).

We write
a,\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime},\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
for typical elements of the
groups
A_{n}
,
I(A_{n-1})
,
I^{2}(A_{n-2})
,
I(B_{n})
and

I^{2}(B_{n-1})
. We define
d^{X}\colon X_{n}\to X_{n-1}
by

\displaystyle d^{X}(a)

\displaystyle=da

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle A_{n-1}

\displaystyle d^{X}(\Sigma\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta(\alpha^{\prime})-\Sigma d\alpha^{\prime}-f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle A_{n-1}\oplus\Sigma I(A_{n-2})\oplus I(B_{n-1})

\displaystyle d^{X}(\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\Sigma\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma^{2}d\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma f_{*}\alpha^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\Sigma I(A_{n-2})\oplus\Sigma^{2}(I^{2}(A_{n-3}))\oplus\Sigma I^{2}(B_{n-2})

\displaystyle d^{X}(\beta)

\displaystyle=d\beta

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle I(B_{n-1})

\displaystyle d^{X}(\Sigma\beta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\beta^{\prime}-\Sigma d\beta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle I(B_{n-1})\oplus I^{2}(B_{n-2}).

We also define maps
A_{*}\xrightarrow{i}X_{*}\xrightarrow{p}B_{*}
by
i(a)=a
and

p(a+\Sigma\alpha^{\prime}+\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\beta+\Sigma\beta^{\prime})=f(a)+\theta(b).

It is clear that
i
is injective, the cokernel of
i
is free in
each degree,
p
is surjective, and
pi=f
.

Proposition 8
.

(X_{*},d^{X})
is a chain complex, and
i
and
p
are chain maps.

Proof.

This is a straightforward check of definitions. We have

\displaystyle d^{X}d^{X}(a)

\displaystyle=d^{2}(a)=0

\displaystyle d^{X}d^{X}(\Sigma\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d^{X}(\theta(\alpha^{\prime})-\Sigma d\alpha^{\prime}-f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=d\theta(\alpha^{\prime})-(\theta(d\alpha^{\prime})-\Sigma d^{2}\alpha^{\prime}-f_{*}(d\alpha^{\prime}))-df_{*}(\alpha^{\prime})=0

\displaystyle d^{X}d^{X}(\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=d^{X}(\Sigma\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma^{2}d\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime\prime}))

\displaystyle=(\theta(\alpha^{\prime\prime})-\Sigma d\alpha^{\prime\prime}-f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime\prime}))+(\Sigma d\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma^{2}d^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma f_{*}(d\alpha^{\prime\prime}))+(f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime\prime})-\Sigma df_{*}(\alpha^{\prime\prime}))

\displaystyle=\theta(\alpha^{\prime\prime})=0

\displaystyle d^{X}d^{X}(\beta)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\beta=0

\displaystyle d^{X}d^{X}(\Sigma\beta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d^{X}(\beta^{\prime}-\Sigma d\beta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d\beta^{\prime}-(d\beta^{\prime}-\Sigma d^{2}\beta^{\prime})=0.

This shows that
X_{*}
is a chain complex, and it is immediate that

i
is a chain map. We also have

\displaystyle p(d^{X}(a))

\displaystyle=f(da)=df(a)

\displaystyle p(d^{X}(\Sigma\alpha^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=p(\theta(\alpha^{\prime})-\Sigma d\alpha^{\prime}-f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime}))=f(\theta(\alpha^{\prime}))-\theta(f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime}))=0

\displaystyle p(d^{X}(\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}))

\displaystyle=p(\Sigma\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma^{2}d\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma f_{*}(\alpha^{\prime\prime}))=0

\displaystyle p(d^{X}(\beta))

\displaystyle=p(d(\beta))=\theta(d(\beta))=d\theta(\beta)

\displaystyle p(d^{X}(\Sigma\beta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=p(\beta^{\prime}-\Sigma d\beta^{\prime})=\theta(\beta^{\prime})=0.

This is easily seen to agree with
dp
, so
p
is also a chain map.
∎

Proposition 9
.

The map
p\colon X_{*}\to B_{*}
is a quasiisomorphism (and thus an acyclic
fibration).

Proof.

Put

\displaystyle Z_{*}

\displaystyle=I^{2}(B_{*})\oplus\Sigma I^{2}(B_{*})

\displaystyle Y_{*}

\displaystyle=\Sigma I^{2}(A_{*})\oplus\Sigma^{2}I^{2}(A_{*})\oplus Z_{*}

\displaystyle K_{*}

\displaystyle=\ker(p\colon X_{*}\to B_{*}).

Thus, for an element

x=a+\Sigma\alpha^{\prime}+\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\beta+\Sigma\beta^{\prime}\in X_{n}
we have

\displaystyle x\in Z_{n}

\displaystyle\iff a=0,\;\alpha^{\prime}=0,\;\alpha^{\prime\prime}=0,\;\theta\beta=0

\displaystyle x\in Y_{n}

\displaystyle\iff a=0,\;\theta\alpha^{\prime}=0,\;\theta\beta=0

\displaystyle x\in K_{n}

\displaystyle\iff fa+\theta\beta=0.

Note that
Z_{*}\leq Y_{*}\leq K_{*}
, and these are inclusions of
subcomplexes. As
p
is surjective, it will suffice to prove that

K_{*}
is acyclic. The complexes
Z_{*}
and
Y_{*}/Z_{*}
are easily seen
to be contractible, so it will suffice to show that the complex

\overline{K}_{*}=K_{*}/Y_{*}
is acyclic. Now put

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{n}

\displaystyle=A_{n}\oplus\Sigma A_{n-1}\oplus B_{n}

\displaystyle d^{\overline{X}}(a+\Sigma a^{\prime}+b)

\displaystyle=(da+a^{\prime})-\Sigma da^{\prime}+(db-f(a^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\overline{p}(a+\Sigma a^{\prime}+b)

\displaystyle=f(a)+b.

Using the map

(a+\Sigma\alpha^{\prime}+\Sigma^{2}\alpha^{\prime\prime}+\beta+\Sigma\beta^{\prime})\mapsto(a+\Sigma\theta(\alpha^{\prime})+\theta(\beta))

we can identify
\overline{X}_{*}
with
X_{*}/Y_{*}
, and thus
\overline{K}_{*}
with
the kernel of
\overline{p}
. Let
C_{*}
be the cone on
A_{*}
, so

C_{*}=A_{*}\oplus\Sigma A_{*}
with
d^{C}(a+\Sigma a^{\prime})=da+a^{\prime}-\Sigma da
. Define

j\colon C_{*}\to\overline{X}_{*}
by
j(a+\Sigma a^{\prime})=a+\Sigma a^{\prime}-f(a)
. This is easily
seen to be a chain map and a kernel for
\overline{p}
, so
\overline{K}_{*}
is
isomorphic to
C_{*}
and is contractible.
∎

Definition 10
.

In the case
A_{*}=0
we write
\Gamma(B)_{*}
for
X_{*}
. Thus

\Gamma(B)_{n}=I(B_{n})\oplus I^{2}(B_{n-1})
with

d(\beta+\Sigma\beta^{\prime})=d\beta+\beta^{\prime}-\Sigma d\beta^{\prime}
, and
\Gamma(B)_{*}
is free in each
degree, and we have a surjective quasiisomorphism

p\colon\Gamma(B)_{*}\to B_{*}
given by
p(\beta+\Sigma\beta^{\prime})=\theta(\beta)
.

Remark 11
.

Another way to think about
X_{*}
is as follows. Write
j
for the
inclusion
I^{2}\to I
. We have maps of chain complexes

I^{2}(A_{*})\xrightarrow{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}j\\
f_{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}I(A_{*})\oplus I^{2}(B_{*})\xrightarrow{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\theta&0\\
-f_{*}&j\end{smallmatrix}\right]}A_{*}\oplus I(B_{*}).

The composite of these maps is zero, so we can regard the above
diagram as a double complex. The totalisation of this double complex
is
X_{*}
.

We now start to discuss lifting properties. It will be convenient to
introduce some test objects:

Definition 12
.

Let
M
be an abelian group. We write
\Sigma^{n}M
for the complex
consisting of a copy of
M
in degree
n
. We also write
C^{n}M
for
the complex consisting of two copies of
M
in degrees
n
and
n+1
,
with the differential between them being the identity.

Remark 13
.

There are easy natural isomorphisms

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},C^{n}M)

\displaystyle\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ch}(C^{n}M,A)

\displaystyle\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(M,A_{n+1})

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},\Sigma^{n}M)

\displaystyle\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n}/dA_{n+1},M)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ch}(\Sigma^{n}M,A_{*})

\displaystyle\simeq\operatorname{Hom}(M,\ker(d\colon A_{n}\to A_{n-1})).

There is a short exact sequence
\Sigma^{n}M\to C^{n}M\to\Sigma^{n+1}M
;
applying
\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},-)
to this gives the left exact sequence

\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n}/dA_{n+1},M)\xrightarrow{\pi^{*}}\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)\xrightarrow{d^{*}}\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n+1}/dA_{n+2},M).

Lemma 14
.

Let
A_{*}
be a chain complex that is free in each degree. Then there
exists a splitting
A_{*}=Y_{*}\oplus Z_{*}
of graded groups and
injective maps
d^{\prime}\colon Y_{n}\to Z_{n-1}
such that the differential is
given by
d(y+z)=d^{\prime}(y)
. Moreover,
A_{*}
is acyclic iff
d^{\prime}
is an
isomorphism iff
A_{*}
is contractible.

Proof.

Put
Z_{n}=\ker(d\colon A_{n}\to A_{n-1})
and
B_{n}=\operatorname{image}(d\colon A_{n+1}\to A_{n})
,
so
B_{n}\leq Z_{n}\leq A_{n}
and therefore
Z_{n}
and
B_{n}
are free. We
can therefore split the epimorphism
d\colon A_{n}\to B_{n-1}
and thus
choose a subgroup
Y_{n}\leq A_{n}
such that
d\colon Y_{n}\to B_{n-1}
is an
isomorphism. If
a\in A_{n}
we see that there is a unique

y\in Y_{n}
with
dy=da
, which means that the element
z=a-y
lies in

Z_{n}
. Using this we see that
A_{n}=Y_{n}\oplus Z_{n}
, and it is clear
that the differential has the stated form. Now
H_{*}A
is the
cokernel of
d^{\prime}
so
A_{*}
is acyclic iff
d^{\prime}
is an isomorphism, in
which case
A_{*}
is isomorphic to the cone on
Z_{*}
and is
contractible.
∎

Proposition 15
.

The functor
\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},-)
converts acyclic fibrations to epimorphisms
iff
A_{*}
is free in each degree.

Proof.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},-)
converts acyclic fibrations to
epimorphisms. For any surjection
f\colon M\to N
of abelian groups we
have an acyclic fibration
C^{n}(f)\colon C^{n}M\to C^{n}N
, so the map

\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)=\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},C^{n}M)\xrightarrow{f_{*}}\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},C^{n}N)=\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)

is surjective. This means that
A_{n}
is projective and thus free.

Conversely, suppose that
A_{n}
is free for all
n
, so we can split

A_{*}
as
Y_{*}\oplus Z_{*}
as in Lemma
14
. We
first claim that if
K_{*}
is an acyclic complex and
k\colon A_{*}\to K_{*}

is a chain map then
k
is nullhomotopic. To see this, put

ZK_{n}=\ker(K_{n}\xrightarrow{d}K_{n-1})
; as
K_{*}
is acyclic, the map

d\colon K_{n+1}\to ZK_{n}
is surjective. Now
k
gives a map

Z_{n}\to ZK_{n}
and
Z_{n}
is free so we can choose a lift

t\colon Z_{n}\to K_{n+1}
with
dt=k
. We now have a homomorphism

Y_{n}\to ZK_{n}
given by
y\mapsto k(y)-t(d^{\prime}y)
, so we can choose a
lift
s\colon Y_{n}\to K_{n+1}
with
ds(y)=k(y)-t(d^{\prime}y)
. We then put

r(y+z)=s(y)+t(z)
and observe that
dr+rd=k
as required.

Now consider an acyclic fibration
q\colon L_{*}\to M_{*}
, so
q
is
surjective and the kernel
K_{*}
is acyclic. Let
j\colon K_{*}\to L_{*}
be
the inclusion. Suppose we have a chain map
g\colon A_{*}\to M_{*}
. As

A_{*}
is degreewise free and
q
is surjective we can choose a map

h^{\prime}\colon A_{*}\to L_{*}
of graded groups with
qh^{\prime}=g
. For
a\in A_{n}
put

k(a)=\Sigma(dh^{\prime}(a)-h^{\prime}(da))\in(\Sigma K)_{n}
. We have

dk(a)=\Sigma dh^{\prime}d(a)=k(da)
, so
k
is a chain map
A_{*}\to\Sigma K_{*}
.
By the previous paragraph we can choose maps

r_{n}\colon A_{n}\to(\Sigma K)_{n+1}
with
dr+rd=k
. We then have

r(a)=\Sigma r^{\prime}(a)
say, and
-dr^{\prime}+r^{\prime}d=\Sigma^{-1}k
. It follows that the
map
h=h^{\prime}+r^{\prime}\colon A_{*}\to L_{*}
is a chain map with
qh=g
, as required.
∎

Corollary 16
.

Let
A_{*}\xrightarrow{i}B_{*}\xrightarrow{p}C_{*}
be a short exact sequence of chain
complexes in which
A_{*}
is acyclic, and
C_{*}
is free in each
degree. Then the sequence is split (in the category of chain
complexes, not just in the underlying category of graded abelian
groups).

Proof.

Exactness means that
p
is surjective, and the kernel is
A_{*}
which
is acyclic by assumption, so
p
is an acyclic fibration. The
Proposition tells us that the induced map

\operatorname{Ch}(C_{*},B_{*})\to\operatorname{Ch}(C_{*},C_{*})
is surjective. In particular,

1
is in the image, so we can choose a chain map
s\colon C_{*}\to B_{*}

with
ps=1
, giving the required splitting.
∎

Proposition 17
.

The functor
\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},-)
converts all fibrations to epimorphisms iff

A_{*}
is contractible and free in each degree.

Proof.

First suppose that
\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},-)
sends fibrations to epimorphisms. By
the previous proposition,
A_{*}
is free in each degree. The evident
projection
C^{n}M\to\Sigma^{n+1}M
is a fibration, so the induced map

\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)=\operatorname{Ch}(A,C^{n}M)\to\operatorname{Ch}(A,\Sigma^{n+1}M)=\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n+1}/dA_{n+2},M)

is surjective. This is just the map induced by
d
, so we see that
the map
d\colon A_{n+1}/dA_{n+2}\to A_{n}
must be a split monomorphism.
As
A_{n}
is free it follows that
A_{n+1}/dA_{n+2}
is free so the
projection
A_{n+1}\to A_{n+1}/dA_{n+2}
must split. We can
therefore find
Y_{n+1}\leq A_{n+1}
such that

A_{n+1}=dA_{n+2}\oplus Y_{n+1}
and
d\colon Y_{n+1}\to A_{n}
is a split
monomorphism. This means that
\ker(d\colon A_{n+1}\to A_{n})=dA_{n+2}
.
After doing this for all
n
we get a splitting
A_{*}=Y_{*}\oplus dY_{*}

such that
d\colon Y_{*}\to dY_{*}
is an isomorphism. In other words,
A_{*}

is contractible.

Conversely, suppose we start with the assumption that
A_{*}
is
degreewise free and contractible. We then have

A_{*}=Y_{*}\oplus Z_{*}
, with the differential given by an isomorphism

Y_{n}\to Z_{n-1}
. This gives an isomorphism

\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},L_{*})=\operatorname{Ab}_{*}(Y_{*},L_{*})
and
Y_{*}
is projective in
\operatorname{Ab}_{*}
so
this functor preserves epimorphisms, as required.
∎

Proposition 19
.

A map
i\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
has the left lifting property with respect to
acyclic fibrations iff
i
is a cofibration.

Proof.

First suppose that
i
has the lifting property. We apply this to
the acyclic fibration
C^{n}M\to 0
, remembering that

\operatorname{Ch}(A_{*},C^{n}M)=\operatorname{Hom}(A_{n},M)
. We see that for every map
u\colon A_{n}\to M

there exists
v\colon B_{n}\to M
with
vi=u
. By taking
u
to be the
identity map, we see that
i
is a degreewise split monomorphism,
with cokernel
C_{*}
say. The map
0\to C_{*}
is a pushout of
i
, so
it has left lifting for acyclic fibrations, which means precisely
that
\operatorname{Ch}(C_{*},-)
sends acyclic fibrations to epimorphisms. Thus,
Proposition
15
tells us that
C_{*}
is degreewise
free, so
i
is a cofibration.

Conversely, suppose that
i
is a cofibration, with cokernel
C_{*}

say. Let
q\colon L_{*}\to M_{*}
be an acyclic fibration, so
q
is
surjective and the kernel
K_{*}
is acyclic. By
Proposition
18
, it will suffice to show that
every short exact sequence
K_{*}\to T_{*}\to C_{*}
is split, but this
follows directly from Corollary
16
.
∎

Proposition 20
.

A map
i\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
has the left lifting property with respect to
all fibrations iff
i
is an acyclic cofibration.

Proof.

First suppose that
i
has the lifting property. The previous result
tells us that
i
is a cofibration, with cokernel
C_{*}
say. The map

0\to C_{*}
is a pushout of
i
and so again has the lifting property,
which means (by Proposition
17
) that
C_{*}

is contractible, so
i
is an acyclic cofibration.

Conversely, suppose that
i
is an acyclic cofibration, and again
write
C_{*}
for the cokernel. We see from
Proposition
17
that every short exact
sequence
K_{*}\to T_{*}\to C_{*}
is split, and it follows using
Proposition
18
that
i
has left lifting for all
fibrations.
∎

Corollary 21
.

\operatorname{Ch}
is a model category.

Proof.

It is clear that
\operatorname{Ch}
has finite limits and colimits, that the
classes of fibrations, cofibrations and weak equivalences are closed
under retracts, and that the weak equivalences satisfy the
two-out-of-three condition. Functorial factorisations are provided
by constructions
5
and
7
. The
lifting axioms are satisfied by Proposition
19

and
20
.
∎

Definition 22

A morphism

From Remark

Proposition 25
.

If
i\colon A_{*}\to B_{*}
and
j\colon C_{*}\to D_{*}
are cofibrations, and
P_{*}
is
the pushout of

B_{*}\otimes C_{*}\xleftarrow{i\otimes 1}A_{*}\otimes C_{*}\xrightarrow{1\otimes j}A_{*}\otimes B_{*},

then the induced map
k\colon P_{*}\to B_{*}\otimes D_{*}
is again a cofibration.
Moreover, if at least one of
i
and
j
is acyclic, then
k
is also
acyclic. In other words, the pushout product axiom is satisfied, so
we have a monoidal model structure.

Proof.

Let
p\colon B_{*}\to U_{*}
and
q\colon D_{*}\to V_{*}
be the cokernels of
i
and

j
(so
U_{*}
and
V_{*}
are free in each degree). We then find that
the map
p\otimes q\colon B_{*}\otimes D_{*}\to U_{*}\otimes V_{*}
gives zero when composed
with
i\otimes 1
or
1\otimes j
, so it is also zero when composed with
k
,
so it induces a chain map
m\colon\operatorname{cok}(k)\to U_{*}\otimes V_{*}
.

Next, in the category of graded abelian groups, we then have
splittings
B_{*}=A_{*}\oplus U_{*}
and
D_{*}=C_{*}\oplus V_{*}
. From this we
obtain splittings

\displaystyle P_{*}

\displaystyle=(A_{*}\otimes C_{*})\oplus(U_{*}\otimes C_{*})\oplus(A_{*}\otimes V_{*})

\displaystyle B_{*}\otimes D_{*}

\displaystyle=(A_{*}\otimes C_{*})\oplus(U_{*}\otimes C_{*})\oplus(A_{*}\otimes V_{*})\oplus(U_{*}\otimes V_{*}),

with respect to which
k
is just the inclusion of the first three
summands. This shows that
k
is injective and that

m\colon\operatorname{cok}(k)\to U_{*}\otimes V_{*}
is an isomorphism. In particular, the
cokernel of

is free in each degree, so
k
is a cofibration. If

is an acyclic cofibration then

is contractible (by

) so

is also contractible so

is also an acyclic cofibration. The same holds if

is an
acyclic cofibration.
∎

Remark 26
.

Let

be a ring that is not hereditary (so submodules of free
modules need not be free). We then cannot use the methods of this

. However, we could in principle take

as a monoid object in
\operatorname{Ch}
, and regard
\operatorname{Ch}_{R}
as the category
of modules for this monoid. Then the general framework
of
[
2

would give a model structure on

\operatorname{Ch}_{R}
.
