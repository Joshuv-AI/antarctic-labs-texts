A partial least squares approach for function-on-function interaction regression
Ufuk Beyaztas

Abstract
A partial least squares regression is proposed for estimating the function-on-function regression model where a functional response and multiple functional predictors consist of random curves with quadratic and interaction effects. The direct estimation of a function-on-function regression model is usually an ill-posed problem. To overcome this difficulty, in practice, the functional data that belong to the infinite-dimensional space are generally projected into a finite-dimensional space of basis functions. The function-on-function regression model is converted to a multivariate regression model of the basis expansion coefficients. In the estimation phase of the proposed method, the functional variables are approximated by a finite-dimensional basis function expansion method. We show that the partial least squares regression constructed via a functional response, multiple functional predictors, and quadratic/interaction terms of the functional predictors is equivalent to the partial least squares regression constructed using basis expansions of functional variables. From the partial least squares regression of the basis expansions of functional variables, we provide an explicit formula for the partial least squares estimate of the coefficient function of the function-on-function regression model. Because the true forms of the models are generally unspecified, we propose a forward procedure for model selection. The finite sample performance of the proposed method is examined using several Monte Carlo experiments and two empirical data analyses, and the results were found to compare favorably with an existing method.



Keywords: Basis function; Function-on-function regression; Functional partial least squares; Interaction effects; Quadratic term.

1
Introduction

Recent advances in computer storage and data collection have enabled researchers in diverse branches of science such as, for instance, chemometrics, meteorology, medicine and finance, recording data of characteristics varying over a continuum (time, space, depth, wavelength, etc). This is the case of data collected by spectrometer, rain gauges, electroencephalographs, or even just by means of high-performance computer. In all these cases, a number of subjects are observed densely over time, space or both. Through the application of interpolation or smoothing techniques, these data become functions. Thus, these data can be represented in a form of curve, image or shape. Functional data analysis has arisen as a field of statistics which provides statistical tools for analyzing this type of information
(
Ramsay and Silverman 2002
;
Ramsay and Silverman 2006
;
Ferraty and Vieu 2006
;
Horvath and Kokoszka 2012
;
Cuevas 2014
)
.
Hsing and Eubank 2015
and
Kokoszka and Reimherr 2017
provide a thorough overview of the research on theoretical results and case studies of functional data analysis methods. The focus of this study will be on constructing function-on-function regression models, where a functional response and multiple functional predictors both consist of random curves.

Function-on-function regression models have been well studied in the literature
(see e.g.,
Ramsay and Dalzell 1991
;
Yao et al. 2005
;
Ramsay and Silverman 2006
;
Müller and Yao 2008
;
Matsui et al. 2009
;
He et al. 2010
;
Wang 2014
;
Ivanescu et al. 2015
;
Chiou et al. 2016
, and references therein)
. The main objective of such models is to explain the association between the functional response and one or more functional predictors. Most of the function-on-function regression models that have been proposed concentrate on main effects only. Let
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)
(
m=1,\cdots,M
) respectively denote a functional response and
M
sets of functional predictors, where
s\in S
and
t\in T
are closed and bounded intervals on the real line. For ease of notation,
s
and
t
can be standardized to the interval
[0,1]
. The function-on-function regression model with main effects is defined as follows:

(1.1)

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\beta_{0}(t)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\beta_{m}(s,t)ds+\epsilon(t),

where
\beta_{0}(t)
is the intercept function,
\beta_{m}(s,t)
is the bivariate coefficient function, and
\epsilon(t)
is the random error function with mean zero.

A regression model with only main effects is less flexible compared with the one that incorporates the quadratic term and interaction effects. A scalar-on-function regression model with the quadratic term of the functional predictor was proposed by
Yao and Müller 2010
. This model was later extended to the case where there is more than one functional predictor by
Fuchs et al. 2015
and
Usset et al. 2016
, who consider interactions between multiple functional predictors.
Matsui 2020
proposed a simple function-on-function regression model with a quadratic term of the functional predictor as follows:

(1.2)

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\beta_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}(s)\beta(s,t)ds+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}(s)\mathcal{X}(r)\gamma(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

where
\gamma(s,r,t)
is the three-dimensional coefficient function of the quadratic term.
Matsui 2020
estimated model parameters by the penalized maximum likelihood (PML) method.
Sun and Wang 2020
proposed a functional principal component (PC) regression to estimate the parameters in model (
1.2
) and provided a procedure to test the significance of the quadratic term.
Luo and Qi 2019
proposed a multiple function-on-function regression model with quadratic and interaction effects as follows:

(1.3)

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\beta_{0}(t)+\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\beta_{m}(s,t)ds+\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

where the error function
\epsilon(t)
has mean zero and is independent of
\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)
’s, and
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{I}
, respectively, denote the index sets of main and quadratic/interaction effects. The index sets
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{I}
are assumed to be any subset of
\left\{1,\cdots,M\right\}
and
\left\{\left(m,n\right):1\leq m\leq n\leq M\right\}
, respectively. In addition,
Luo and Qi 2019
proposed a stepwise procedure for model selection. The numerical results produced from the previous studies have shown that functional regression models including quadratic term and interaction effects perform better than standard functional regression models in the presence of interaction.

We propose a functional partial least squares (PLS) regression to estimate the multiple function-on-function regression model with quadratic and interaction effects given in (
1.3
). Several functional PLS methods have been proposed to estimate parameters of the functional regression models. For example,
Preda and Saporta 2005
,
Reiss and Ogden 2007
,
Krämer et al. 2008
,
Aguilera et al. 2010
, and
Aguilera et al. 2016
proposed functional PLS methods for scalar-on-function regression models.
Delaigle and Hall 2012b
examined the theoretical properties of functional PLS methods in scalar-on-function regression models. In addition,
Febrero-Bande et al. 2017
compared the finite sample performances of these methods and discussed their advantages and disadvantages.
Hyndman and Shang 2009
proposed a functional PLS regression based on a lagged functional predictor and a functional response for forecasting functional time series.
Escabias et al. 2007
proposed a functional PLS method for logit regression. Functional PLS has also been used for other regression problems. For example,
Ferraty and Vieu 2006
defined a functional PLS-based semi-metric for nonparametric functional predictor variables and
Preda et al. 2007
and
Delaigle and Hall 2012a
proposed functional PLS-based functional data classification methods. Our method differs from these existing functional PLS methods in terms of the number of functional predictors in the model: while the existing functional PLS methods allow only one functional predictor in the model, the proposed method allows for more than one functional predictor and their quadratic/interaction effects. The main contributions of the proposed method include:

i)

vectorization of function values,

ii)

an application of PLS (for vector-valued predictors and responses), and

iii)

a forward stepwise variable selection.

The interaction terms, including the quadratic terms, are treated as linear terms once they are appropriately transformed.

We use the idea of functional PLS proposed by
Preda and Saporta 2005
and
Aguilera et al. 2010
to estimate model (
1.3
) in Section
2
. In our proposed method, all the functional predictors and their quadratic/interaction terms are first stacked into a two-dimensional function (
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
; see (
2.4
)). Then, a functional PLS regression of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
is considered. Functional random variables, by their nature, belong to infinite-dimensional space. Thus, the direct estimation of function-on-function regression models is generally an ill-posed problem. One remedy to estimate function-on-function regression models is the approximation of functional data via finite-dimensional basis function expansion methods. In this way, a function-on-function regression model is converted into a multivariate regression model of basis expansion coefficients. Then, the functional PLS regression of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
is estimated using the basis expansion approximations of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
. We show the equality of the functional PLS and the PLS regression constructed via basis expansions of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
using the metrics associated with basis functions. In addition, we provide an explicit formula for the coefficient function of the functional PLS regression model from the PLS estimate of the regression model constructed via basis coefficient expansions. When considering model (
1.3
), the form of the true model is usually unspecified and the index sets
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{I}
are unknown in practice
(
Luo and Qi 2019
)
. To this end, we further propose a forward procedure to determine the significant functional predictors and their quadratic/interaction terms. Our numerical results, which will be discussed in detail in Sections
3
and
4
, reveal that the proposed method based on the forward procedure produces better prediction performance compared with the full model, which includes all the predictors and their quadratic and interaction terms. Section
5
concludes the paper.

2
Methodology

Let us consider a set of random functional response
\{Y_{1}(t),\dots,Y_{N}(t)\}
containing
N
independent realization
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
and
M
sets of random functional predictors
\{\mathcal{X}_{mi}(s);m=1,\cdots,M,i=1,\cdots,N\}
. With these realizations, we aim to construct the multiple function-on-function regression model with quadratic and interaction effects given in (
1.3
). We assume that the functional random variables are the elements of square-integrable and real-valued functions
\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]
, and are of second-order stochastic processes. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}(t)\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\right]=0
for
m=1,\cdots,M
and
\forall t,s\in[0,1]
, and the functional data pairs
\left\{\mathcal{X}_{mi}(s),Y_{i}(t)\right\}
are fully observed. For the sake of clarity, we consider the full model where all the functional predictors and their quadratic/interaction terms are included in the model; that is
m=1,\cdots,M
and
(m,n)=(1,1),(1,2),\cdots,(1,M),\allowbreak(2,3),\cdots,(M-1,M),(M,M)
. Using the continuity of functional objects, we express model (
1.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\beta_{m}(s,t)ds+\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\beta_{m}(s,t)ds+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\left[\beta_{m}(s,t)+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dr\right]ds+\epsilon(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\left[\beta_{m}(s,t)+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dr\right]ds+\epsilon(t).

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M(M+3)/2}
be a two-dimensional vector-valued function as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{X}_{1}(s),\cdots,\mathcal{X}_{M}(s)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{1}(s)\mathcal{X}_{1}(r),\mathcal{X}_{1}(s)\mathcal{X}_{2}(r),\cdots,\mathcal{X}_{1}(s)\mathcal{X}_{M}(r)

\displaystyle X_{2}(s)\mathcal{X}_{2}(r),\cdots,X_{M-1}(s)X_{M}(r),\mathcal{X}_{M}(s)\mathcal{X}_{M}(r))^{\top}

Herein, the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
is considered as a single two-dimensional vector-valued function but including
M(M+3)/2
(
M
sets of functional predictors and
M\times(M+1)/2
sets of functional quadratic/interaction terms) sets of functional predictors and their quadratic/interaction terms. Hereafter, we will consider the following regression model:

(2.4)

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)\Xi(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

where
\Xi(s,r,t)
is a single three-dimensional coefficient function comprising
M(M+3)/2
sets of coefficient functions of both main and quadratic/interaction effects, as follows:

\displaystyle\Xi(s,r,t)

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1}(s,t),\cdots,\beta_{M}(s,t)

\displaystyle\gamma_{11}(s,r,t),\gamma_{12}(s,r,t),\cdots,\gamma_{1M}(s,r,t)

\displaystyle\gamma_{22}(s,r,t),\cdots,\gamma_{M-1,M}(s,r,t),\gamma_{MM}(s,r,t))^{\top}

The coefficient identifiability of (
2.4
) is discussed in Appendix.

In practice, the random functions of a functional dataset
\left\{x_{i}(t):i=1,\cdots,N\right\}
are observed in the set of finite discrete time points
\left\{t_{\ell}:\ell=1,\cdots,L\right\}
; that is
\left\{x_{i}(t_{\ell})\right\}
. Thus, the discretely observed data points are first converted into their functional forms using a suitable method such as basis function expansion
(
Ramsay and Silverman 2006
)
and nonparametric smoothing of functions
(
Ferraty and Vieu 2006
)
before fitting the functional regression model. In this study, we consider the basis function expansion approach. Briefly, a random function
x(t)
can be approximated by the linear combinations of basis functions,
\phi_{k}(t)
, and associated coefficients,
c_{k}
, for a sufficiently large number of basis functions,
K
:

x(t)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\phi_{k}(t).

Several basis function expansion methods such as
B
-spline, Fourier, radial, and wavelet bases have been proposed
(
Ramsay and Silverman 2006
, see, e.g.,)
. In this study, we consider the
B
-spline basis function expansion method.

Let
K_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
denote the number of basis functions used for approximating
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
, respectively. They are expressed by basis function expansion as follows:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}c_{k}\phi_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{l=0}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}d_{jl}\psi_{jl}(s,r)=\boldsymbol{d}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r),

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)
respectively are the
K_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\times M(M+3)/2
dimensional basis functions vector/matrix, as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)

\displaystyle=(\phi_{1}(t),\cdots,\phi_{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}(t))^{\top}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)

\displaystyle=(\psi_{1}(s),\cdots,\psi_{M}(s)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(s)\psi_{1}(r),\psi_{1}(s)\psi_{2}(r),\cdots,\psi_{1}(s),\psi_{M}(r)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(s)\psi_{2}(r),\cdots,\psi_{M-1}(s)\psi_{M}(r),\psi_{M}(s)\psi_{M}(r))^{\top}

with
\psi_{i}(s)=\left(\psi_{i1},\cdots,\psi_{iK_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\right)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
are the
K_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
dimensional coefficient vectors, respectively. In addition, let us assume that the three-dimensional coefficient function
\Xi(s,r,t)
in (
2.4
) is expressed by basis expansion as follows:

(2.7)

\Xi(s,r,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}\xi_{jlk}\psi_{jl}(s,r)\phi_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{(3)}
is a
K_{\mathcal{Y}}\times K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}
dimensional matrix obtained by matricizing a three-dimensional
K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\times K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\times K_{\mathcal{Y}}
tensor
\underline{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=\left(\xi_{jlk}\right)_{jlk}
with respect to the 3
rd
array
(
de Lathuauwer et al. 2000
;
Matsui 2020
)
. Without loss of generality, we further assume that the error function in (
2.4
) is represented as a basis function expansion as follows:

\epsilon(t)=\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{e}=\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{e}_{N}\right)^{\top}
is an
N
-dimensional vector comprising random variables
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\left(e_{i1},\cdots,e_{iK_{\mathcal{Y}}}\right)^{\top}
. Now, replacing (
2.5
) to (
2.7
) with (
2.4
) yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)+\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{e},

where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)dsdr
. Thus, the problem of estimating the function-on-function regression model in (
2.4
) is reduced to estimating the multivariate regression model (
2.8
) with coefficient matrix
\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}
.

When considering (
2.8
), the dimensions of the matrices increase exponentially when a large number of predictors are used in the model or when a large number of basis functions are used to approximate the functional objects. In such cases, an ill-posed problem (multicollinearity) arises and estimating
\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}
using traditional methods, such as least squares and maximum likelihood results in infinitely many solutions. Regularisation methods such as the PML method proposed by
Matsui 2020
may be used to overcome this problem and to produce an accurate estimate for
\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}
. However, it is computationally time-consuming, and it may not be possible to obtain the PML estimate of
\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}
using a computer with standard memory. One remedy to obtain a stable estimate for
\Gamma_{(3)}^{\top}
is to use dimension-reduction techniques, such as PC or PLS regression models. PC regression has been proposed by
Aguilera et al. 1999
and
Yao et al. 2005
to estimate standard function-on-function regression models and by
Sun and Wang 2020
to estimate univariate function-on-function quadratic regression models. However, this method does not take into account the relationship between response and predictor variables when deciding the PCs. Further
Aguilera et al. 2010
showed that in the functional case, the parameter function estimated with PLS is much more accurate than with PC. Therefore, we consider functional PLS regression to estimate model (
2.4
).

2.1
PLS for the functional interaction regression model

In the multivariate case, the main goal of PLS regression is to construct a linear model between an
n\times m
dimensional fixed full-rank design matrix
\boldsymbol{X}=\left(X_{1},\cdots,X_{m}\right)^{\top}
and an
n\times r
dimensional matrix for response variables
\boldsymbol{Y}=\left(Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{r}\right)^{\top}
as follows:

\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{E},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{E}
are the
m\times r
dimensional coefficient matrix and the error matrix with dimensions
n\times r
, respectively. The PLS regression produces orthogonal latent components
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{T}}
formed as linear combinations of predictor matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
and an appropriate weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{T}}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{W}
. Herein, the weight matrix is computed so that it maximizes the covariance between
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{T}}
. Then, an ordinary least squares regression is used to capture the relationship between
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{\mathbb{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}+\boldsymbol{E}
. In turn, it produces the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}
for
\boldsymbol{Y}
. Then, the PLS regression is constructed as follows:

\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{E},

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Several functional versions of PLS regression have been proposed to explore the relationship between a functional predictor and a scalar and/or functional response
(
Preda and Saporta 2005
;
Hyndman and Shang 2009
;
Aguilera et al. 2010
;
Aguilera et al. 2016
;
Beyaztas and Shang 2020
, see e.g.,)
. Similar to the discrete case, the functional PLS method produces orthogonal latent components as linear combinations of the functional predictors by maximizing the squared covariance between the response and orthogonal latent components
(
Tucker 1938
)
. The PLS components are determined by an iterative process. In this study, we extend the functional PLS methods proposed by
Preda and Saporta 2005
and
Aguilera et al. 2010
to estimate the model given in (
2.4
). Our extension differs from the methods of
Preda and Saporta 2005
and
Aguilera et al. 2010
in the context of the number of functional predictors in the model. The original functional PLS methods allow for only one functional predictor in the model. However, our proposed method is possible to construct a regression model with more than one functional predictor and their quadratic/interaction effects. The proposed method first stacks all the main and quadratic/interaction effects into a two-dimensional function,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
, it then applies a PLS method on the vectorized functional variables to estimate the model parameters.

The PLS components of regression model (
2.4
) can be obtained as solutions of Tucker’s criterion extended to functional-type data as follows:

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\in\mathcal{L}_{2}([0,1]\times[0,1]),~\|\kappa\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]\times[0,1]}=1\\
\zeta\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1],~\|\zeta\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]}=1\end{subarray}}{\max}\text{Cov}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)\kappa(s,r)dsdr,~\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{Y}(t)\zeta(t)dt\right),

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
denotes
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm, which is approximated by the Riemann sum
(
Luo and Qi 2019
)
. The functional PLS components are also the eigenvectors of the Escoufier operator
(
Escoufier 1970
)
. Let
W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
and
W^{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively, denote the Escoufier’s operators associated with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
, with respect to
\mathcal{Y}(t)
as follows:

W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)Z\right]\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)dsdr,\qquad W^{\mathcal{Y}}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}(t)Z\right]\mathcal{Y}(t)dt,\qquad\forall Z\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1].

Then, the first PLS component, say
\eta_{1}
, of model (
2.4
) is equal to the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue (
\lambda_{\max}
) of
W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}W^{\mathcal{Y}}
:

W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}W^{\mathcal{Y}}\eta_{1}=\lambda_{\max}\eta_{1}.

Accordingly, the first PLS component is defined as follows:

\eta_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{1}(s,r)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)dsdr,

where the weight function
\kappa_{1}(s,r)
associated with
\eta_{1}
is given by:

\kappa_{1}(s,r)=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)\right]dt}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)\right]dt\right)^{2}dsdr}}.

PLS is an iterative stepwise procedure. Let
h=1,2,\cdots,
denote the iteration number. Define
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(s,r)
and
\mathcal{Y}_{h}(t)
by the residuals obtained from the following regressions:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(s,r)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)-p_{h}(s,r)\eta_{h},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Y}_{h-1}(t)-\zeta_{h}(t)\eta_{h},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{0}(s,r)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
,
\mathcal{Y}_{0}(t)=\mathcal{Y}(t)
,
p_{h}(s,r)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)\eta_{h}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{h}^{2}\right]}
, and
\zeta_{h}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}_{h-1}(t)\eta_{h}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{h}^{2}\right]}
. Then, the
h^{\text{th}}
PLS component,
\eta_{h}
, is equal to the eigenvector corresponding to largest eigenvalue of
W_{h-1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}W_{h-1}^{\mathcal{Y}}
:

W_{h-1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}W_{h-1}^{\mathcal{Y}}\eta_{h}=\lambda_{\max}\eta_{h},

where
W_{h-1}^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
and
W_{h-1}^{\mathcal{Y}}
are the Escoufier’s operators associated with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)
and
\mathcal{Y}_{h-1}(t)
, respectively. That is,
\eta_{h}
is given as follows:

\eta_{h}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{h}(s,r)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)dsdr,

where the weight function associated with
\eta_{h}
is given by:

\kappa_{h}(s,r)=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}_{h-1}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)\right]dt}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}_{h-1}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h-1}(s,r)\right]dt\right)^{2}dsdr}}.

Following
Aguilera et al. 2010
, the properties of the PLS components of model (
2.4
) can be summarized by the following proposition:

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
h\geq 1

i)

\left\{\eta_{h}\right\}_{h\geq 1}
forms an orthogonal system in the linear space spanned by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
,

ii)

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\zeta_{1}(t)\eta_{1}+\cdots+\zeta_{h}(t)\eta_{h}+\mathcal{Y}_{h}(t)
,

iii)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)=p_{1}(s,r)\eta_{1}+\cdots+p_{h}(s,r)\eta_{h}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(s,r)
,

iv)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}_{h}(t)\eta_{j}\right]=0
,
\forall j=1,\cdots,h
,

v)

\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(s,r)\eta_{j}\right]=0
,
\forall j=1,\cdots,h
.

2.2
PLS for basis expansion of the functional interaction regression model

Functional PLS components are estimated using an observed sample of the functional dataset. However, the random functions of functional variables are observed in the set of finite discrete time points. In this case, the main problem related to the sample estimation of PLS components is that of estimating the Escoufier’s operators from discrete time observations
(
Aguilera et al. 2010
)
. To overcome this problem, we consider the basis expansions of the observed functional dataset, and construct the PLS regression using the basis expansion coefficients of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
. Let us revisit model (
2.4
) and its basis function expansion in (
2.8
). Then, we provide the following proposition to show the relationship between the functional PLS of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
and the PLS of
\boldsymbol{c}
on
\boldsymbol{d}
. The proof of the proposition has been relegated to the Appendix.

Proposition 2.2
.

Under the assumption that the functional variables
\mathcal{Y}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
are spanned with finite number of basis functions, let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, respectively, denote
K_{\mathcal{Y}}\times K_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}\times K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}
dimensional symmetric matrices with entries the inner products of the basis functions
\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}(t)dt
and
\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)dsdr
, respectively. Also, let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}
denote the square roots of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, respectively. Then, the functional PLS regression of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)
is equivalent to the PLS regression of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}
on
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}
in the sense that at each step
h
of the PLS algorithm, the same PLS components are obtained for both PLS regressions.

The PLS approximation of the coefficient function
\Xi(s,r,t)
in (
2.4
) can be expressed in the bases
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r)
. Denote by
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{h}=\left(\Theta_{1}^{h},\cdots,\Theta_{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}}^{h}\right)
the regression coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}
on
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}
obtained by the PLS regression at step
h
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{h},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)\widehat{\Xi}(s,r,t)dsdr,

where

\widehat{\Xi}(s,r,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{h}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\right)_{(jl)}\psi_{jl}(s,r)\phi_{k}(t),\qquad s,r,t\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1].

Herein,
\widehat{\Xi}(s,r,t)
denotes the PLS approximation of the three-dimensional coefficient function
\Xi(s,r,t)
. These results demonstrate that the infinite-dimensional estimation problem can be reduced to a simple finite-dimensional PLS regression setting using the particular metrics
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
in the spaces of expansion coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
, respectively. Through the basis expansion, one can implement a finite multivariate PLS regression setting and a PLS algorithm, such as nonlinear iterative partial least squares (NIPALS) of
Wold 1974
, the SIMPLS of de
de Jong 1993
, and the improved kernel PLS of
Dayal and MacGregor 1997
to approximate the three-dimensional coefficient function
\Xi(s,r,t)
. In this study, the NIPALS algorithm is used to obtain the PLS approximation of
\Xi(s,r,t)
.

2.3
Model selection

2.3.1
Variable selection procedure

The functional regression model in (
2.4
) includes too many interaction terms when a large number of functional predictors are used in the model. However, not all the functional terms may be significant for the model. Thus, a variable selection procedure is needed to select the best subset of functional predictors and quadratic/interaction terms. In this context, several criteria, such as the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, and the predicted residual error sum of squares, have been proposed. However, a variable selection procedure based on these criteria will be computationally intensive, since the number of all possible models is very large when there are a large number of predictors in the model. Various variable selection procedures, such as backward elimination and forward selection, have been proposed to overcome this problem. In this study, we propose the following forward selection procedure to select the significant functional predictors and quadratic/interaction terms.

Step 1.

We consider selecting the main effect terms first, since they are generally more important for the model compared with the quadratic term and interaction effects. To start with, first,
M
function-on-function regression models, each of which includes the common response and a predictor, are constructed as follows:

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)\Xi_{m}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon_{m}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)=\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)
and
r=0
for
m=1,\cdots,M
. Among these models, the one having the smallest mean squared error (MSE),

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\mathcal{Y}_{j}(t)-\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{j}(t)
is the prediction of
\mathcal{Y}_{j}(t)
for
j=1,\cdots,N
, is chosen as an initial model. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1)}(s,r)
and
\text{MSE}^{(1)}
denote the predictor variable in the initial model and the MSE obtained from this model, respectively. Then,
M-1
function-on-function regression models, each of which includes the response and a predictor, are constructed as follows:

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)\Xi_{m}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon_{m}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1)}(s,r),\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\right\}
,
\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\neq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1)}(s,r)
, and
r=0
for
m=1,\cdots,M-1
, and the MSE is calculated for each of these models. The predictor variable that produces the smallest MSE,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(2)}(s,r)
, is chosen as the predictor variable for the current model if
\text{MSE}^{(2)}<\text{MSE}^{(1)}
where
\text{MSE}^{(2)}
is the calculated MSE when
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(2)}(s,r)
is used to estimate the current model. This process is repeated until all the significant variables are included in the model.

Step 2.

We consider an iterative procedure similar to that described in Step 1 to select the significant quadratic and interaction effects terms. Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}
(
\mathcal{M}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
) denote the index set of all significant main effect terms found in the previous step. Denote by
M^{*}
the number of items in
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, so there are
M^{*}\times(M^{*}+1)/2
quadratic/interaction terms to be considered. Let
\text{MSE}^{(\mathcal{M}^{*})}
denote the calculated MSE when all the significant main effect terms are included in the model. To select quadratic/interaction effect terms, first,
M^{*}\times(M^{*}+1)/2
function-on-function regression models including the response and a predictor are constructed as follows:

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)\Xi_{m}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon_{m}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{m}(s,r)=\left[\left\{\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\right\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}^{*}}\left(1+\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\right)\right]
for
n=1,\cdots,M^{*}
, and the MSE is calculated for each of these models. Then, the first quadratic/interaction effect term that leads to the greatest improvement to the
\text{MSE}^{(\mathcal{M}^{*})}
is included in the model. Other quadratic/interaction effect terms are iteratively selected to enter the model based on their improvements to the MSE of the current model.

2.3.2
Selection of the optimum number of PLS components

To select the optimum number of PLS components, we consider a cross-validation-based mean squared prediction error (MSPE) as follows:

1)

First, the data are randomly divided into training and testing samples with sample sizes
N_{train}
and
N_{test}
, respectively. Note that in our numerical analyses, the training and testing samples are determined based on the 50% – 50% rule.

2)

Using the training set,
B_{h}
PLS regression models based on
h=1,\cdots,B_{h}
number of PLS components are constructed.

3)

The values of the response variable in the testing sample are predicted using the values of the predictor variables in the testing sample and the coefficient matrices obtained from the previous step, and the MSPE is computed for each
B_{h}
:

\text{MSPE}(h)=\frac{1}{N_{test}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{test}}\left\|\mathcal{Y}_{i}(t)-\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}^{h}(t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}^{h}(t)
is the prediction of
\mathcal{Y}_{i}(t)
based on
h
PLS components.

Based on the above algorithm, the optimum number of PLS components is equivalent to one which produces the minimum MSPE. Note that the cross-validation approach should only be applied to the training set to select the number of components. Within the training set, we can further split the samples into a training and a test sample. We note that our procedure for the selection of the optimum number of PLS components consists of two steps. In the first step, the significant main, quadratic, and interaction effects using a fixed number of PLS components, say 8, are determined as discussed in Section
2.3.1
. Then, in the second step, the cross-validation-based MSPE criterion given above is used to determine the number of optimum PLS components. Our numerical analyses (not reported in the paper) have shown that the choice of a fixed number of PLS components in the first step does not have a significant effect on the determination of main, quadratic, and interaction effects when
h\geq 4
.

Numerical results

Several Monte-Carlo simulations were conducted to investigate the finite sample performances of the proposed method, and the results were compared with the method proposed by
Luo and Qi 2019
(”LQ”, hereafter). Two different simulations settings, which are modified versions proposed by
Luo and Qi 2019
, were considered. Throughout the simulations,
m=5
functional predictors
\mathcal{X}_{m}
were generated from the following process:

\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{\text{Lag}}V_{m+i}(s)/\sqrt{\text{Lag}+1},

where

were generated from the Gaussian process with mean zero and a positive definite covariance function
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{V}(s,s^{\prime})=e^{-100(s-s^{\prime})^{2}}
. Herein, the parameter Lag (
\text{Lag}>0
) controls the correlation between the predictor functions, and its large values correspond to high correlations between the predictor functions. Two different correlation levels, namely
\text{Lag}=2
and
\text{Lag}=4
, were considered. The error process
\epsilon(t)
was generated from the normal distribution with mean zero and variance four;
N(0,4)
. Then, the realizations of the response function were generated as follows:

\mathcal{Y}(t)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\beta_{m}(s,t)ds+\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{X}_{m}(s)\mathcal{X}_{n}(r)\gamma_{mn}(s,r,t)dsdr+\epsilon(t),

where
\mathcal{M}=\left\{2,3,4,5\right\}

and

for the second simulation setting. For the first simulation setting, the following coefficient functions were considered to generate the response:

5
Conclusion

We propose a PLS method to estimate the function-on-function regression model where the multiple functional predictors have a quadratic term and interaction effects. In the proposed method, all the functional predictors and their quadratic/interaction effects are stacked into a vector, and the functional PLS regression of the functional response on the vectors of the functional predictors are considered. Since the direct estimation of a functional regression model is generally an ill-posed problem, the proposed method is estimated using the
B
-spline basis expansion of the functional objects. Basis expansion reduces the infinite-dimensional estimation problem to a simple finite-dimensional PLS regression setting using particular metrics in the spaces of the expansion coefficients. Using the metrics associated with the basis functions, the PLS regression constructed in this way is equivalent to the PLS regression constructed using a functional response and functional predictors. The finite sample performances of the proposed method are evaluated via several Monte Carlo experiments and two empirical data analyses. The performance of our method are compared with those of an existing method. Our results have shown that the proposed method produces improved prediction accuracy than those of the existing method. In addition, we propose a forward selection procedure to determine the significant main and quadratic/interaction effects. Our numerical results have proven that the forward selection procedure further improves the prediction performances of the PLS method when the true form of the model is even unspecified.

In the proposed method, we consider only
B
-spline basis expansion to approximate the functional objects. However, the specific forms of bivariate basis functions (probably tensor products of univariate B-splines) are generally unknown. In smoothing 2-dimensional surfaces, tensor-product type splines may ignore interior holes (or gaps) and yield extra bias as well as oversmoothing. Alternatively, the basis functions of non-tensor-product type for bivariate smoothing, such as Green’s function
(
Wood et al. 2008
)
and bivariate B-splines
(
Pan 2011
)
may be used.

Identifiability of the proposed model

Model identifiability, which is an important theme in the estimation phase, for the function-on-function regression model with only main effects given in (
1.1
) was theoretically discussed by
He et al. 2000
and
Chiou et al. 2004
. Later,
Scheipl and Greven 2016
discussed the identifiability of such models with realistic applications. As is stated in the Proposition 3.1 of
Scheipl and Greven 2016
, the coefficient function
\beta_{m}(s,t)
in (
1.1
) is identifiable if and only if the kernel space of the covariance operator of the functional predictors (
K^{\mathcal{X}}
) is empty except the zero function, i.e.,
ke(K^{\mathcal{X}})=\{0\}
.
Luo and Qi 2019
extended the theoretical condition of model identifiability for function-on-function regression with only main effects to the model with interaction and quadratic effects. They defined the following
\{p(p+3)/2\}\times\{p(p+3)/2\}
dimensional covariance matrix of the predictor functions:

(5.9)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}(s,r)&\boldsymbol{B}(s,r,u)\\
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}(s,r,u)&\boldsymbol{C}(s,r,s^{\prime},r^{\prime})\end{bmatrix},

where the
p\times p
and
p\times\{p(p+1)/2\}
dimensional submatrices
\boldsymbol{A}(s,r)
and
\boldsymbol{B}(s,r,u)
equal to
\text{Cov}\left(\mathcal{X}(s)\mathcal{X}(r)\right)
and
\text{Cov}\left(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{X}(r)\mathcal{X}(u)\right)
, respectively. The
\{p(p+1)/2\}\times\{p(p+1)/2\}
dimensional matrix
\boldsymbol{C}(s,r,s^{\prime},r^{\prime})
equals to
\text{Cov}\left(\mathcal{X}(s)\mathcal{X}(r),\mathcal{X}(s^{\prime})\mathcal{X}(r^{\prime})\right)
. Then,
Luo and Qi 2019
proved that the coefficients in model (
1.3
) are identifiable if the kernel space of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
in (
5.9
) only has the zero function. Please see Proposition S.1. of
Luo and Qi 2019
for the proof. Based on the above, our proposed method given in (
2.4
) is also identifiable since the proposed method is a reformulated version of the model (
1.3
).

Proof of Proposition (
2.2
)

Proof.

The proof of Proposition (
2.2
) is an extended version of the proof of Proposition 2 of
Aguilera et al. 2010
. Denote by
\Lambda=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}
and
\Pi=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}
the column random vectors.

For any random variable
Z\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]
, the followings hold when
h=1
:

\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}c_{k}\phi_{k}(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}Zc_{i}\phi_{i}(t)\right]dt=\Lambda^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\Lambda Z\right]=W^{\Lambda}Z,

\displaystyle W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}d_{jl}\psi_{jl}(s,r)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\iota=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}Zd_{\iota\tau}\psi_{\iota\tau}(s,r)\right]dsdr=\Pi^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi Z\right]=W^{\Pi}Z.

The residuals obtained from the first iteration are given by:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{Y}_{1}(t)=\mathcal{Y}(t)-\zeta_{1}(t)\eta_{1},&\zeta_{1}(t)\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]\\
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda-\widetilde{\zeta}_{1}\eta_{1},&\widetilde{\zeta_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}\end{cases}

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{1}(s,r)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)-p_{1}(s,r)\eta_{1},&p_{1}(s,r)\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]\\
\Pi_{1}=\Pi-\widetilde{p}_{1}\eta_{1},&\widetilde{p}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}}\end{cases}

Then, we have
\Lambda_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}
and
\Pi_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}_{1}
, where
\boldsymbol{c}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}
are the random vectors of the basis coefficients of
\mathcal{Y}_{1}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{1}(s,r)
, respectively. For functional PLS, the residuals calculated from the first iteration are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}c_{1,k}\phi_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{c}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{1}(s,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}}d_{1,jl}\psi_{jl}(s,r)=\boldsymbol{d}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(s,r),\qquad\forall s,r\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1].

On the other hand, the residuals from the PLS regression are obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}(t)\boldsymbol{c}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}(t)\boldsymbol{c}\eta_{1}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{1}(s,r)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)\boldsymbol{d}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}(s,r)\boldsymbol{d}\eta_{1}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}\eta_{1}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}\eta_{1}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]}

and thus we have
\boldsymbol{c}_{1}=\boldsymbol{c}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{c}\eta_{1}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=\boldsymbol{d}-\eta_{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{d}\eta_{1}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{1}^{2}\right]}
,
that is,
W^{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}=W^{\Lambda_{1}}
and
W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}=W^{\Pi_{1}}
. Now, assuming
W^{\mathcal{Y}_{\ell}}=W^{\Lambda_{\ell}}
and
W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\ell}}=W^{\Pi_{\ell}}
for each
\ell\leq h
. Also, let us assume that
W^{\mathcal{Y}_{h+1}}=W^{\Lambda_{h+1}}
and
W^{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h+1}}=W^{\Pi_{h+1}}
. Then, at step
h+1
the followings hold:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{Y}_{h+1}(t)=\mathcal{Y}(t)-\zeta_{1}(t)\eta_{1}-\cdots-\zeta_{h}(t)\eta_{h},&\zeta_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]\\
\Lambda_{h+1}=\Lambda-\widetilde{\zeta}_{1}\eta_{1}-\cdots-\widetilde{\zeta}_{h}\eta_{h},&\widetilde{\zeta_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathcal{Y}}}\end{cases}

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{h+1}(s,r)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,r)-p_{1}(s,r)\eta_{1}-\cdots-p_{h}(s,r)\eta_{h},&p_{i}(s,r)\in\mathcal{L}_{2}[0,1]\\
\Pi_{h+1}=\Pi-\widetilde{p}_{1}\eta_{1}-\cdots-\widetilde{p}_{h}\eta_{h},&\widetilde{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}}\end{cases}

As for
h=1
and using the orthogonality of
\eta_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,h
, it is shown that
\Lambda_{h+1}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{c}_{h+1}
and
\Pi_{h+1}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{d}_{h+1}
, which concludes the proof.
