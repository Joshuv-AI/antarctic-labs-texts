AIMpy : A Python code to solve Schrödinger-like equations with The Asymptotic Iteration Method Journal: IJMPC: https://doi.org/10.1142/S0129183121500170
Mesut Karakoç

Abstract
This paper is dedicated to present an open-source program so-called AIMpy built on Python language. AIMpy is a solver for Schrödinger-like differential equations using Asymptotic Iteration Method (AIM). To confirm the code works seamlessly, it has been shown through the paper with recalculation of some previously studied eigenvalue examples that the code can reproduce their results very well.

Introduction

Solving differential equations is of great interest since the invention of calculus by
Newton and Leibniz. After two centuries and fifty years of the invention of calculus, Schrödinger introduced his differential equation which now carries his name. It has been applied widely to many physical (quantum) system. One general class of these physical systems are bound systems such as hydrogen atom, molecules, and nucleons in a nuclei, etc. Solutions of such systems mean to obtain their eigenvalues and (or) eigenfunctions.

AIM is one of the various solving methods of the corresponding differential equations for bound systems which is developed by Ciftci, Hall and Saad
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. It has many successful application in the literature. Depending on the nature of a differential equation AIM can give analytical or numerical solutions.

Schrödinger, Dirac, Klein-Gordon, or Duffin-Kemmer-Petiau (DKP) all these differential wave equations and any others have to take second-order homogeneous linear differential equations of the form

y^{\prime\prime}=\lambda_{0}(x)y^{\prime}+s_{0}(x)y,

(1)

as it is mentioned in Ciftci
et al

[
15
]
to be solvable by AIM.

In Ref.
[
11
,
31
]
, one can find a successful application of AIM to DKP equations which gives analytical solutions for harmonic oscillator and Coulomb potential cases while anharmonic potential case needs a perturbation treatment in the frame work of AIM. Another study which is performed on Schrödinger Equation for Makarov Potential
[
8
]
resulted in analytical solutions. Also there is an approximate analytical solution of Dirac eqaution with Hulthen potential as presented in
[
30
]
. There is also a study
[
10
]
which shows AIM can be simplified for a group of differential equations and analytical solutions can be obtained almost immediately.

Although AIM can solve many differential equations analytically in many other cases this might not be possible due to the nature of that particular differential equation, but in these cases, AIM might offer numerical solutions. Cases of numerically obtained eigenvalue can be found in a Schrödinger equation study with a Yukawa potential
[
26
]
, a DKP equation study which was performed on sextic oscillator potential
[
32
]
, and the references therein. An interesting numerical application of AIM has been performed to Kerr-(A)dS black holes using Kerr-(A)dS angular separation equation
[

.

Almost all examples given above (to the best of my knowledge) were investigated using the codes written either in Mathematica or Maple or some similar closed source software. But,

2.1
AIM

If one focuses on solving a 1-dimensional Schrödinger equation with a smooth changing potential,
this equation can be represented in the following form,

\frac{d^{2}\psi(x)}{dx}+V(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x)~.

(2)

To be able to perform AIM on Eq.
2
it needs to be transformed into form of Eq.
1
. This can be achieved by proposing the following relation

\psi(x)=f(x)y(x).

(3)

This relation produces an equation when inserted into Eq.
2
in the form of Eq.
1
where

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\frac{d}{dx}f{\left(x\right)}}{f{\left(x\right)}}\texttt{, and }

\displaystyle s_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E-V{\left(x\right)}-\frac{\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f{\left(x\right)}}{f{\left(x\right)}}.

(4)

The process of obtaining
\lambda_{0}
and
s_{0}
is very similar to 1D Schrödinger case for any differential equation once they are transformed into form of Eq.
1
. Proposition of
f(x)
function is important to obtain the solution, usually one should analyze the asymptotic behaviors of the differential equation: the examples of how
f(x)
is proposed in can be seen in Refs.
[
15
,
16
,
18
,
17
,
8
,
26
,
32
,
7
]
.

After this point, iterative part of AIM starts due to one has to take derivatives of the Eq.
1
and put previously obtained lower degree derivatives in the new higher degree differential equation. This leads to iterative equations to
\lambda_{k}
and
s_{k}
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k-1}^{\prime}(x)+s_{k-1}(x)+\lambda_{0}(x)\lambda_{k-1}(x)

(5)

\displaystyle s_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{k-1}^{\prime}(x)+s_{0}(x)\lambda_{k-1}(x),\quad\textrm{where }k=1,2,3,\dots

Asymptotic behavior of the method comes into play when
k
is large enough and, it gives the following relation,

\frac{s_{k}(x)}{\lambda_{k}(x)}=\frac{s_{k-1}(x)}{\lambda_{k-1}(x)}\equiv\alpha(x).

(6)

A new form of this equation is defined as
\delta_{k}
,

\delta_{k}\equiv\lambda_{k}(x)s_{k-1}(x)-\lambda_{k-1}(x)s_{k}(x),

(7)

and
\delta_{k}=0
is called as ‘quantization condition’
[
14
]
. Roots of the quantization condition give eigenvalues of any differential equation which obeys AIM conditions. Finally, the following integral which details are given in Ref.
[
15
]
gives the general solution of Eq.
1
,

\displaystyle y(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int^{x}\alpha\left(x^{\prime}\right)dx^{\prime}\right)\times

(8)

\displaystyle\left[C_{2}+C_{1}\int^{x}\exp\left(\int^{x^{\prime}}\left[\lambda_{0}\left(x^{\prime\prime}\right)+2\alpha\left(x^{\prime\prime}\right)\right]dx^{\prime\prime}\right)dx^{\prime}\right].

2.2
Improved AIM

An apparent ‘weakness’ of AIM is its iterative nature, because one has to take derivatives of
\lambda_{k-1}
and
s_{k-1}
to obtain
\lambda_{k}
and
s_{k}
. These derivatives are added to every new iteration and for sufficiently large
k
’s
\lambda_{k}
and
s_{k}
become huge mathematical terms which might be represented by with very a big order polynomial. This might brings difficulties depending on the case for both numerical and symbolical calculations. The part for the symbolic difficulty would be time and memory consuming, and the part for the numerical difficulty would be precision and accuracy loss.

To overcome this issues Cho
et al
.
[
14
]
developed an ‘improved’ version of AIM. According to the improved version,
\lambda_{k}
and
s_{k}
can be written as Taylor series,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{n}^{i}(x-x_{0})^{i},

(9)

\displaystyle s_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_{n}^{i}(x-x_{0})^{i},

(10)

where
c_{n}^{i}
and
d_{n}^{i}
are Taylor coefficients. Using these series in Eqs.
5
gives following recursion relations for the coefficients:

\displaystyle c_{n}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(i+1)c_{n-1}^{i+1}+d_{n-1}^{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{i}c_{0}^{j}c_{n-1}^{i-j},

(11)

\displaystyle d_{n}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(i+1)d_{n-1}^{i+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{i}d_{0}^{j}c_{n-1}^{i-j}.

(12)

The quantization condition in Eq.
7
can be rewritten with these new recursion relations in the following form,

d_{n}^{0}c_{n-1}^{0}-d_{n-1}^{0}c_{n}^{0}=0

(13)

and there is no need take derivatives when using the new condition other than the first iteration. Therefore, only
\lambda_{0}
and
s_{0}
derivatives are taken to obtain
c_{0}^{i}
and
d_{0}^{i}
coefficients which are necessary to start the new recursion relation calculations.

SymPy
and

\lambda_{0}
and

and

SymPy
and
symengine
both are capable of taking derivatives but
symengine
is significantly faster than
SymPy
. Therefore,

is preferred for derivatives while

is mostly used to convert symbolic variables into
L
a
T
e
X
to show outputs as user-friendly using IPython tools.

Once,

and

is obtained in an polynomial form depending on

or shortly flint. As it is mentioned in the reference page
[
23
]
:
Python-FLINT
is an
‘Python extension module wrapping FLINT (Fast Library for Number Theory) and Arb (arbitrary-precision ball arithmetic).’
The roots can have arbitrary precision since both
FLINT

[
1
]
and
Arb

[
24
]
are arbitrary precision libraries. Therefore, it can be claimed that the eigenvalues that would be calculated with
AIMpy

[
25
]
will be very reliable.

Second part of the code is in a Jupyter notebook
[
27
]
which actually is an environment to create an user-friendly interface and to just take care of the physics problem interested in rather than the code itself.

5
Conclusions and outlook

I have presented
AIMpy
code which is an eigenvalue solver for Schrödinger-like differential equations using AIM.
AIMpy
is an open-source
[
2
]
code using the power of fast and high precision open-source symbolic and numeric calculation libraries. The example cases given in the paper proves the reliability of the code. However, some more features can be added to the code in later versions: like eigenfunction calculations, analytical solver and, direct input of any solvable differential equations by AIM to AIMpy to obtain
\lambda_{0}
and
s_{0}
,
etc.
As the last word, I would like to encourage community contributions or writing their versions of the code through forking the repository on GitHub
[
25
]
.
