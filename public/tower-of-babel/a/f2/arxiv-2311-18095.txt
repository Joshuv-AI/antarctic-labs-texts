On non-archimedean frames
M. Bocardo-gaspar 1, Julio Urenda 2, F. Ávila 3, Ángel Zaldívar 4

Abstract
. In this investigation, we introduce the class of non-archimedean frames in spirit with the topological notion of non-archimedean spaces. We explore various properties of these frames - particularly their spaciality. We attach a base that constitutes a tree to each non-archimedean frame, and then we observe that every non-archimedean frame is a quotient of the frame of opens of the tree’s branch space. Moreover, we give a partial answer to when these frames are canonically isomorphic; this leads to considering some choice principles of the resulting tree.



1.
Introduction

The essential motivation for this paper arises from the frame of
p
-adic numbers
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, defined by
[
Ávi20
]
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is the field of
p
-adic numbers. This field was determined by Kurt Hensel in 1904 in analogy with the Laurent series
\mathbb{C}((t))
. Furthermore, this field is a non-archimedean field with the
p
-adic norm
|\cdot|_{p}
defined over it. Since the set of open balls centered at rationals generates the open subsets of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, we consider these balls’ (lattice) properties and think of them as generators. Thus, we can define the
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
frame as follows.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
be the frame generated by the elements
B_{r}(a)
, where
a\in\mathbb{Q}
and
r\in|\mathbb{Q}|:=\{p^{-n},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, subject to the following relations:

(1)

B_{r}(a)\wedge B_{s}(b)=0
whenever
|a-b|_{p}\geq r\vee s
.

(2)

1=\bigvee\{B_{r}(a):a\in\mathbb{Q},r\in|\mathbb{Q}|\}
.

(3)

B_{r}(a)=\bigvee\{B_{s}(b)\colon|a-b|_{p}<r,s<r,r\in|\mathbb{Q}|\}
.

Note that relation (3) implies that the set

\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{r}(a):r\in|\mathbb{Q}|,a\in\mathbb{Q}\}

is a base for
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
. Moreover, let
B_{r}(a)
,
B_{s}(b)
be any two elements in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
and, without loss of generality, assume that
s\leq r
. Then, If
|a-b|_{p}\geq r
, we have
B_{r}(a)\wedge B_{s}(b)=0
by relation (1), and if
|a-b|_{p}<r
, we have
B_{s}(b)\leq B_{r}(a)
by relation (3). Thus, for any
B_{r}(a),B_{s}(b)\in\mathcal{B}
, either

B_{r}(a)\wedge B_{s}(b)=0\,\text{ or }\,B_{s}(b)\leq B_{r}(a)\,\text{ or }\,B_{s}(b)\geq B_{r}(a).

Now, recall that a
non-archimedean
topological space
S
is a Hausdorff space with a base
\mathcal{B}
satisfying the
trichotomy
laws: If
B_{1}
,
B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have that either
B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\varnothing\text{ or }B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}\text{ or }B_{2}\subseteq B_{1}
holds
(see, e.g.,
[
Mon50
]
,
[
Nyi75
]
,
[
NR75
]
,
[
Nyi99
]
). Motivated by this, we say that a frame is non-archimedean if it has a (non-archimedan) base

that satisfies these trichotomy laws: If

, then either

b_{1}\wedge b_{2}=0
or
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
or
b_{2}\leq b_{1}
holds.

It follows that
\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{r}(a):r\in|\mathbb{Q}|,a\in\mathbb{Q}\}}
is a non-archimedean base for the frame
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, and that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
is a non-archimedean frame.

Another example of a non-archimedean frame is the frame of the Cantor set, denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. It is defined using the fact that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is homeomorphic to the
p
-adic integers for every prime number
p
. In
[
ÁUZ22
]
, it was shown that

is a

, where

is the Cantor-Bendixson derivative (see, e.g.,
[

]
).

In general, we can consider any non-archimedean normed field
(\mathbb{K},|\cdot|_{\mathbb{K}})
and define
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K})
in a similar fashion. Then,
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K})
is a non-archimedean frame. Moreover, if

is a non-archimedean topological space, then

is a (regular) non-archimedean frame.

Up to this point, we have found no literature on non-Archimedean frames; this essentially motivated the development of this work. In this manuscript, we introduce a general point-free theoretic framework (for non-archimedean structures) to perform some classical topological situations in the broad context of frames.

The presentation of the manuscript is as follows: Section
2
contains the background needed to understand the rest of the manuscript.
Section
3
defines the class of frames called
non-archimedean
. In particular, we establish that every non-archimedean base has a tree structure (Theorem
3.11
) and prove one of the main results: Every non-archimedean frame is a quotient of the topology of a branch space of some tree (

). Finally, Section

of trees of height

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we will recall some basic facts from frame theory (locale theory). For details, we refer to
[
Joh82
]
and
[
PP12
]
.

A frame (locale, complete Heyting algebra) is a complete lattice

(A,\leq,\bigvee,\wedge,0,1)

in which the following distributive law holds:

a\wedge(\bigvee X)=\bigvee\{a\wedge x\mid x\in X\},

for every
X\subseteq A
and
a\in A
.
Frames are, in specific ways, algebraic manifestations of topological spaces. The ubiquitous example of a frame is the topology of any topological space.

We denote by
\mathrm{Frm}
the category of frames, with frames as objects and frame morphisms as arrows. As the theory suggests, an intrinsic relationship exists between the categories of topological spaces and frames. More specifically, there exists an adjunction between these categories:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Top}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Frm}^{\op}{}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pt}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dashv}

Where
\mathcal{O}(S)
is the lattice of open sets of the space
S
and
\pt(A)
is a
point space
of the frame, which can be described in three different ways, as frames characters or completely prime filter or
\wedge-
irreducible elements.

For more details, see, e.g.,
[
PP12
]
.

Every frame
A
has a Heyting implication
(\_\succ\_)
given by

x\leq(a\succ b)\Leftrightarrow x\wedge a\leq b.

Thus, for every element
a\in A
, its
negation
is given by

\neg a=(a\succ 0).

That is,
\neg a
is the greatest element such that
a\wedge x=0
. An element
a\in A
is
complemented
if there exists an element (necessarily unique)
b\in A
such that
a\vee b=1
and
a\wedge b=0
; in fact, the complement of a complemented element
a
is
\neg a
.

As usual, the concept of base in topological spaces has its interpretation in frames: A subset
\mathcal{B}
of a frame
A
is a
base
if, for each
a\in A
, we have

a=\bigvee\{b\in\mathcal{B}\mid b\leq a\},

and we say that a frame is
zero dimensional
if a base of complemented elements exists.

One of the main features of point-free topology, i.e., the study of topological space via its lattice of open sets, is that we can usually say more about a classical topological situation when viewed in the context of frames. For example, the classical separation axioms have exciting counterparts in frames (see, e.g.,
[
PP21
]
). We recall some of these notions that will be useful in this manuscript.

A frame
A
is
fit
if for any
a,b\in A
with
a\nleq b
there exists
x,y\in A
such that

a\vee x=1,\;\;\;\;y\nleq b,\;\;\;\;x\wedge y\leq b.

The notion of fitness is an essential algebraic property that does not have much immediate topological impact.
We say that
A
is
regular
if for any
a,b\in A
with
a\nleq b
, there are
x,y\in A
such that

a\vee x=1,\;\;\;y\nleq b,\;\;x\wedge y=0.

This definition agrees with the classical one: a topological space
S
is regular if the frame
\mathcal{O}(S)
is regular.

In the setting of frames, we define
a\prec b
iff
\neg a\vee b=1
, and we say that
a
is
completely below

b
, denoted
a\prec\!\!\prec b
, if there are
a_{r}\in A
, with
r
rational and
0\leq r\leq 1
, such that

a_{0}=a,\;\;\;\;a_{1}=b,\;\;\text{ and }\;\;a_{r}\prec a_{s}\,\text{ for }r<s.

Then, a frame
A
is said to be
completely regular
if

a=\bigvee\{x\mid x\prec\!\!\prec a\}\text{ for every }a\in A.

This is also an extension of the corresponding notion in classical topology: A topological space
S
is completely regular if and only if the frame
\mathcal{O}(S)
is completely regular.

We also recall some important facts concerning nuclei theory for frames. A nucleus on frame
A
is a function
j\colon A\rightarrow A
such that:

(i)

a\leq b\Rightarrow j(a)\leq j(b)
.

(ii)

a\leq j(a)
for all
a\in A
.

(iii)

j(a)\wedge j(b)\leq j(a\wedge b).

(iv)

j^{2}=j
.

The set of all nuclei, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(A)
, is a frame and it is often called
the assembly
. This frame has remarkable properties, e.g., every quotient of
A
is given by a nucleus in the sense that, for every
j\in\mathcal{N}(A)
, the set of fixed elements
A_{j}=\{a\mid j(a)=a\}
is a frame; the function
j^{*}\colon A\rightarrow A_{j}
, defined by
a\mapsto j(a)
, is a surjective frame morphism, and every quotient of
A
is identified with the fixed set of a nucleus.

We also use some
coverage
material on the partially ordered set. This concept was introduced in
[
Joh82
]
and further developed in
[
Sim07
]
and
[
GS07
]
. A
coverage
on a poset
(\mathcal{T},\leq)
is a relation
\vdash
between upsets
U\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{T})
and elements of
\mathcal{T}
,

U\vdash a.

It is well known that any coverage defines an inflator
d\colon\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{T})
(a monotone, inflatory function) on
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{T})
given by:

a\in d(U)\Leftrightarrow U\vdash a.

If the coverage satisfies specific rules, we obtain a nucleus and, thus, a quotient of
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{T})
. This is explored with detail in
[
Sim04
]
and
[
Sim07
]
(we use these ideas after Theorem
4.8
to tangle the spatiality of non-archimedean frames).

Finally, free frames and quotient frames define structures by generators and relations. We take the quotient of the free frame
\mathbb{F}
on the set
\mathcal{B}
of generators modulo the unity generated by the pairs
(u,v)
for the stipulated relations
u=v
(or
u\leq v
). When taking this quotient, one describes the relations by a system of formal equalities, i.e., if
u,v
are formulas to identify, we write
u=v
instead of
uRv
.

Note that defining homomorphisms
A/R\rightarrow B
represented as precisely those
h\colon A\to B
for which
uRv
implies
h(u)=h(v)
results as the fact that we have the homomorphisms represented precisely by the mappings defined on the generators that respect all the defining equalities (see, e.g.
[
Ban97
]
,
[
PP12
]
,
[
PP21
]
).

3.
Non-archimedean frames

Motivated by the classic topological notion of non-archimedean space (as previously introduced) we give the frame-theoretic analogue.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A
be a frame, a base
\mathcal{B}
of
A
is called
non-archimedean
if
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
, then either

(N1)

b_{1}\wedge b_{2}=0
or:

(N2)

b_{1}\leq b_{2}
or:

(N3)

b_{2}\leq b_{1}
.

holds.

Definition 3.2
.

A
non-archimedean
frame is a frame with a non-archimedean base.

As usual, we will see what happens with the quotients of a non-archimedean frame:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A
be an non-archimedean frame then
A_{j}
is a non-archimedean frame for every
j\in\mathcal{N}(A)
.

This straightforward statement gives a lot of examples. For instance, as we mentioned above, if
S
is a non-archimedean space, then its topology
\mathcal{O}S
is a non-archimedean frame, and then for every nucleus
j\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{O}S)
we have that
\mathcal{O}S_{j}
is a non-archimedean frame.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
b
be a member of a non-archimedean basis
\mathcal{B}
and
c\in A
. If
c\leq b
and
c
are complemented, then
b
is completed.

Proof.

Let
c
and
b
be as above and assume that
c\leq b
and
c\vee\neg c=1
. Since
\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x\geq b\}=\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=c\vee\neg c

\displaystyle=c\vee\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0\}

\displaystyle=c\vee\bigvee\left(\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x\land b=0\}\cup\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x\land b\neq 0\}\right)

\displaystyle=c\vee\bigvee\left(\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land b=0\}\cup\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x<b\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\vee\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land b=0\}\vee\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x<b\}

\displaystyle=\left(c\vee\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{B}\mid x\land c=0,\,x<b\}\right)\vee\neg b

\displaystyle\leq b\vee\neg b.

Therefore,
b\vee\neg b=1
.
∎

For example, the classical notion of non-archimedean space requires the Hausdorff separation property (see
[
NR75
]
,
[
Nyi99
]
, and it seems that the first appearance of these spaces was in
[
Mon50
]
). In the point-free context we obtain additional generality.

Theorem 3.5
.

Every non-archimedean frame is zero-dimensional; in particular, every non-archimedean frame is completely regular (thus regular and fit).

Therefore, unlike the topological case, we do not need to impose any separation property.

The next proposition is comparable with
[
Nyi99
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
Dav78
]
. The proof is straightforward.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
A
be a non-archimedean frame with base
\mathcal{B}
, then for every
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
we have:

(i)

\bigwedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=0
or,

(ii)

the interval
[0,\bigwedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime}]
is
simple
, that is,
[0,\bigwedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime}]=\{0,\bigwedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\}
or,

(iii)

\bigwedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is complemented in some non-archimedean base.

In particular, for every
local
base, i.e., for every
a\in A
let

\mathcal{B}_{a}=\{b\in\mathcal{B}\mid b\leq a\}\text{ such that }\bigvee\mathcal{B}_{a}=a

if
0\neq\bigwedge\mathcal{B}_{a}
there is an atom in
[0,a]
.

In the following, we show how to associate any non-archimedean frame with a base, which is a tree. Now, we characterize these frames.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
A
be a non-archimedean frame with base
\mathcal{B}
. Then the set

\bar{\mathcal{B}}=\{\bigvee\EuScript{C}\mid\EuScript{C}\text{ is a chain in }\mathcal{B}\}

is a non-archimedean base.

Proof.

First, we notice that, for any
a\in\mathcal{B}
and every chain
\EuScript{C}
of
\mathcal{B}
such that
a\wedge\bigvee\EuScript{C}\neq 0
, we have that
a\leq c
for some
c\in\EuScript{C}
or
\bigvee\EuScript{C}\leq a
. We assume that
a\wedge\bigvee\EuScript{C}\neq 0
. Then, by the frame distributive law, we have that there exists
c\in\EuScript{C}
such that
a\wedge c\neq 0
. By definition of non-archimedean base,
a\leq c
or
c\leq a
. The first case implies
a\leq\bigvee\EuScript{C}
.
The first case does not hold, then for
d\in\EuScript{C}
such that
c\leq d
, we have
d\wedge a\neq 0
. Again,
d\leq a
or
a\leq d
. The second case is not possible by assumption, then
d\leq a
. Hence
\bigvee\EuScript{C}\leq a
.

Now, let
b,b^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}
, where
b=\bigvee\EuScript{C}
and
b^{\prime}=\bigvee\EuScript{C}^{\prime}
with
\EuScript{C}
and
\EuScript{C}^{\prime}
chains in
\mathcal{B}
. If
b\wedge b^{\prime}\neq 0
, then by the frame distributive law, we have that there exists
c\in\EuScript{C}
such that
c\wedge\bigvee\EuScript{C}^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then by the first part
\bigvee\EuScript{C}^{\prime}\leq c
, which implies that
b^{\prime}\leq b
, or for any
c\in\EuScript{C}
there
existe
c^{\prime}\in\EuScript{C}^{\prime}
such that
c\leq c^{\prime}
and hence
b\leq b^{\prime}
.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

With the same hypothesis as above, the base
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
is closed under suprema of chains.

Proof.

Let
\EuScript{D}
be a chain in
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
. If the chain
\EuScript{D}
has a maximal element, say
\EuScript{M}
, then
\bigvee\EuScript{D}=\EuScript{M}
. On the other hand, the element
\bigvee\{d_{i}\mid i\in I\}
, with
d_{i}\in\EuScript{D}
, is an upper bound. Then,
\EuScript{D}\leq\bigvee\{d_{i}\mid i\in I\}
. By definition of
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
, we can assume that
d_{i}=\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}
, with
\EuScript{C}_{i}
a chain in
\mathcal{B}
. Now, let
d_{i+1}=\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}
. Since
d_{i}\leq d_{i+1}
, by the proof of Proposition
3.7
, we can find a
c(i)\in\EuScript{C}_{i}
such that
d_{i}\leq c(i)
. Thus, for any member
d_{i}
, there exists a
c(i)
such that
d_{i}\leq c(i)
, for each

. Therefore
\bigvee\EuScript{D}=\bigvee\{c_{i}\mid i\in I\}
.

∎

Generally, we can assume that every non-archimedean frame contains a basis closed under suprema of chains. Furthermore, for any non-archimedean frame
A
with basis
\mathcal{B}
,
let
\EuScript{C}\subset\mathcal{B}
be a nonempty chain. It is clear that there is a unique chain
\EuScript{C}^{\#}
such that
\EuScript{C}\subset\EuScript{C}^{\#}
and
[a,b]\subset\EuScript{C}^{\#}\subset\mathcal{B}
for each
a\leq b

in
\EuScript{C}
. Thus, determining that suprema of chains is invariant under this closure operator and
taking upper sections. More succinctly, for each

a\in\EuScript{C}
,

\bigvee\EuScript{C}=\bigvee\EuScript{C}^{\#}=\bigvee{(\uparrow\!\!a)\cap{\EuScript{C}}}.

Lemma 3.9

(Canonical form)
.

Let
(A,\mathcal{B})
be a non-archimedean frame, then every
a\in A
is a join of disjoint members of
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

For the above, we can suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is closed under suprema of chains and, for any
a\in A
, we have
a=\bigvee\{\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}:\EuScript{C}_{i}\text{ chain in $\mathcal{B}$ with $\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}\leq a$}\}
. Consider the set
E=\{b\in\mathcal{B}:b\leq a\}
and notice that
a=\bigvee\{\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}:\EuScript{C}_{i}\text{ maximal chain in $E$}\}
. We claim that for any pair of these chains, we have
\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}\wedge\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{j}=0
with
i\neq j
. In effect, the condition
\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}\wedge\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{j}\neq 0
implies that
\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}\leq\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{j}
or
\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{j}\leq\bigvee\EuScript{C}_{i}
, which contradict the maximality of the chains.
∎

We need to indicate that such decomposition can be trivial as each basic element admits a trivial decomposition of basis members. In fact, the single-element decomposition is the only one permitted in the case of atomic basic elements. However, every non-atomic element

is decomposable into at least two basic elements: let
b
a basic element with
b<a
, then
b
is complemented, so

. This implies that
a\wedge(b\vee\lnot b)=a
, or equivalently,
b\vee(a\wedge\lnot b)=a
. Thus,
(a\wedge\lnot b)
decomposes as above. Moreover, for each basic element
b
and every decomposition
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of

into basic elements, if
b\leq w_{i}
for some
i
, then
b\wedge w_{j}=0
for each
j\neq i
; otherwise, for
i\neq j
either
b\leq w_{j}
or
b\geq w_{j}
, then either
0<b\leq w_{i}\wedge w_{j}=0
or
w_{j}\leq b\leq w_{i}
, which is impossible. Lastly, for each chain
\EuScript{C}=\EuScript{C}^{\#}
and each basis element
b
in
\mathcal{B}
, we observe either
b\in\EuScript{C}
,
b\leq\bigwedge\EuScript{C}
,
b\geq\EuScript{C}
or
b\leq\left(\lnot\bigwedge\EuScript{C}\right)
.

With this at hand, we can see that every non-archimedean frame has a tree associated, but first, we need some definitions.

Definition 3.10
.

A
tree

\mathcal{T}
consist of the following:

(1)

(\mathcal{T},\leq)
, a partial ordered set (we call

a node).

(2)

For each node

, the set

A maximal chain of the tree

We will see that every non-archimedean frame has a base winch and a tree with the reverse order of

.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
A
be a non-archimedean frame with base
\mathcal{B}
. Then
A
has a base, which is a tree.

Proof.

Let be
\mathcal{B}
a non-archimedean base, which we assume is closed under the suprema of chains.
The proof uses transfinite induction.

The root of our tree, denoted by
\bot
, is just the top of the frame
1
.

We define
\mathcal{T}
by recursion. Let
\mathcal{T}(0)
be any nontrivial decomposition of the top element in
A
into members of
\mathcal{B}
.
For a successor ordinal
\alpha+1
, suppose
\mathcal{T}(\alpha)
is defined. For each non-atomic element
b\in\mathcal{T}(\alpha)
, produce non-trivial decomposition
W(b)
of
b
and set

\mathcal{T}(\alpha+1)=\bigcup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b\in\mathcal{T}(\alpha)}}{{b\text{ non-atomic}}}}W(b).

For a limit ordinal
\beta
, consider all chains

Definition 3.12

4.
On the spaciality of non-archimedean frames

Now, we show that every non-archimedean frame is spatial. Let

(as in Theorem

\pt(A)
its point space regarded as
\mathrm{Frm}(A,2)

is a quotient of

, i.e., the map

, is a surjective frame morphism. Thus, from the fact that
A
is non-archimedean and every quotient of a non-archimedean frame is non-archimedean (Proposition
3.3
), we have that
\mathcal{O}\pt(A)
is a non-archimedean frame. Therefore,

using the tree

Lemma 4.1
.

For each
\alpha
and

.

Proof.

Suppose there are

such that
f(b)=f(b^{\prime})=1
. Then,
1=f(b)\wedge f(b^{\prime})=f(b\wedge b^{\prime})
, so
b\wedge b^{\prime}\neq 0
. Thus,
b
and
b^{\prime}
are comparable and at the same level. Therefore,
b=b^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

The collection

Proof.

Let

be in
\EuScript{T}

. Then,

. Therefore,

.

Now, suppose

then, by trichotomy, either

or
b^{\prime}<b
.

Case 1:

Suppose there is

. Then,

, thus

, which is a contradiction. Therefore,

.

We show that we can not have

4.1.
