Tumor growth with nutrients: stability of the tumor patches
Inwon Kim, Jona Lelmi

Abstract
. In this paper, we study a tumor growth model with nutrients. The contact inhibition for the tumor cells, presented in the model, results in the evolution of a congested tumor patch . We study the regularity of the tumor patch as the nutrients’ diffusion strength D D diminishes. In particular, we show that for small D > 0 D>0 the boundary of the tumor patch stays in a small neighborhood of the smooth tumor patch boundary obtained with D = 0 D=0 , uniformly with respect to the Hausdorff distance. Keywords : congested dynamics, tumor growth, Wasserstein, stability. Mathematical Subject Classification (MSC2020) : 35B65 (primary), 35Q92.



1.
Introduction

We consider a basic model that describes tumor growth with nutrients, given by the following system of PDEs, set in
Q:=\mathbf{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)
:

(1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}-\operatorname{div}\left(\rho\nabla p\right)&=(n-b)\rho,\quad\rho\leq 1,\quad p\in P_{\infty}(\rho),\\
\partial_{t}n-D\Delta n&=-\rho n,\quad n\to c>0\ \text{as}\ |x|\to\infty,\end{cases}

Here
D,b,c
are nonnegative constants, and
P_{\infty}
denotes the Hele-Shaw graph, given by

\displaystyle P_{\infty}(\rho)=\begin{cases}0\ &\text{if}\ \rho<1,\\
[0,+\infty)\ &\text{if}\ \rho=1.\end{cases}

This system has been actively studied in recent literature: see for instance
[
11
,
12
,
2
,
7
,
6
]
. Here
\rho
and
n
each denote the density of tumor cells and the nutrients. The tumor cells grow by the nutrients which are supplied by the external environment, while also dying at rate
b
. The condition
p\in P_{\infty}(\rho)
implies that the pressure variable
p
is the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
\rho\leq 1
, which represents the contact inhibition in cells. In the region
n\geq b
, the cells only grow within the constraints, so the solution features time-evolving
patches
of congested cell region
\{\rho=1\}
. In this paper we will only consider the model with no death (
b=0
), and only solutions with initial density given as a characteristic function
\chi_{\Omega(0)}
, which then will evolve as a characteristic function
\rho(\cdot,t)=\chi_{\Omega(t)}
. In this paper, we consider the model with no death term (
b=0
). Moreover, for the Hausdorff convergence of the boundaries (Theorem
2.1
), we consider only solutions with initial density given as a characteristic function
\chi_{\Omega(0)}
, which then will evolve as a characteristic function
\rho(\cdot,t)=\chi_{\Omega(t)}
. Our focus is on the regularity properties of the patch boundary
\partial\Omega(t)
.

While the system is well-posed, it presents rather curious instability of patch boundaries. When
D>0
, the tumor patch appears to generate growing fingers, as observed by numerical experiments
[
10
,
11
,
13
]
. (Also see the recent instability analysis in
[
4
]
for a closely related model). See Figure
1
for a numerical simulation. (Figure
1
and Figure
2
contain snapshots of simulations run by Wonjun Lee. The time evolution is from left to right, and the white set corresponds to the tumor patch. The implementation is based on the numerical scheme in
[
8
]
, which is consistent with the scheme we discuss in this paper.)

Figure 1
.

b=0
,
D~=~10^{-6}
. Thanks to Wonjun Lee for running the simulation.

The growth of oscillation on the patch boundary appears to be caused by the competitive (prey-predator) nature of the density and the nutrient. One could further conjecture that the irregularity may get worse as
D
decreases since less diffusion would make the nutrients less averaged and irregular. It is thus surprising that the nutrient diffusion is indeed essential in this de-regularizing phenomenon: when
D=0
it was shown in
[
7
]
that the patch boundary becomes smooth in finite time when there is a sufficient amount of nutrients at the initial time.

In view of the above discussion, it is natural to ask whether some information is lost between the model with a small choice of
D
and with
D=0
. In this paper we focus on this question: we show that within finite time the answer is no, at least in terms of Hausdorff distance. Namely, we will show that, for any given finite time interval, the patch boundary for small
D
lies in a small neighborhood of the smooth patch boundary for
D=0
. While our results do not rule out small-scale irregularities that vanish as
D
tends to zero, we can at least say that there are no persisting irregularities at a fixed scale in the diffusion zero limit (see Theorem 1). We should emphasize though that this convergence only holds within a fixed finite time interval: we in fact conjecture that as
D
tends to zero, the finger growth will slow down and only be significant after a long time. The role of nutrient diffusion in this exhibition of instability remains to be understood.

There are many open questions that remain to be investigated. For instance, we suspect that a stronger mode of convergence holds true for the patch boundary. For example, we conjecture that the perimeters of the tumor patches converge as well in our setting, however, we are not able to verify it. The effect of the cell death rate (
b>0
) is also yet to be studied. With a positive death rate, the tumor patch develops a necrotic core, where the density falls below one. In this case, the fingers growth on the outer boundary of the set
\{\rho=1\}
was observed even when
D=0
, see Figure
2
.

Figure 2
.
Numerical solution with constant initial nutrient,
b=0.2
,
D~=~0
.

It would be interesting to understand if our analysis can be extended to
b>0
when this parameter is assumed to converge to zero. In this case, the non-monotone nature of the patch evolution poses a challenge. On the other hand, the main technical result of our work, namely the
H^{1}
convergence of the pressure variable (Proposition
2.3
), may be suitably extended to this setting.

The main step in our analysis is improving the
L^{1}
-convergence of the tumor patches (proved in Theorem
4.1
) to the Hausdorff convergence of their boundaries. This is done by using both (i) the monotone-expanding property of the tumor patches,
and (ii) the strong
H^{1}
convergence of the pressure variables, proved in Proposition
2.3
. The latter is based on a variational characterization of the pressure variable, following ideas similar to that of
[
9
]
, where the authors prove it in a Keller-Segel model for chemotaxis.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we state our main results, namely, Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
. In Section
3
we recall the definition of weak solution for the system (
1
), and its corresponding existence and uniqueness. In Section
4
we recall the approximation scheme used to construct weak solutions to (
1
) and a slight variant of it that ensures a stronger convergence of the nutrient variable. In Section
5
we present the proofs of our results.

2.
Main results

In this section, we state the main results of this paper. Here and in the rest of the paper we will always assume that there is no death term, i.e. we set
b=0
in (
1
).

Notation
. Given an open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
we denote by
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
the Banach space of Radon measures with finite total variation on
\Omega
, endowed with the total variation norm, which we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}
. Given a map
\mu:2^{\Omega}\to\mathbf{R}
, we say that
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\Omega)
if
\mu|_{2^{\tilde{\Omega}}}\in\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\Omega})
for every
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\subset\Omega
. We also warn the reader that for a map
\mu:2^{\Omega}\to\mathbf{R}^{d}
we will write
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
to say, with a slight abuse of notation,
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
for every
i=1,...,d
. A similar abuse of notation will be made when, for a vector field
v:\Omega\to\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we will write
v\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, to mean that
v_{i}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for every
i=1,...,d
.

Given
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
\nabla n_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\rho_{0}\in BV(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
0\leq\rho_{0}\leq 1
a.e., we denote by
(n_{D},\rho_{D},p_{D})
the unique weak solution to (
1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
with
D>0
and initial value
(n_{0},\rho_{0})
. Similarly
(n,\rho,p)
denotes the weak solution with same initial value and
D=0
. We also define, for
t\geq 0

(2)

\Gamma_{D}(t):=\partial\{p_{D}(t)>0\},\quad\Gamma(t):=\partial\{p(t)>0\}.

When the initial value
\rho_{0}
is a characteristic function, for
t\geq 0
and
D>0
we will denote by
d_{H}(\Gamma_{D}(t),\Gamma(t))
the Hausdorff distance between
\Gamma_{D}(t)
and
\Gamma(t)
, which we recall is defined as

(3)

d_{H}(\Gamma_{D}(t),\Gamma(t)):=\max\left(\sup_{x\in\Gamma(t)}d(x,\Gamma_{D}(t)),\sup_{x\in\Gamma_{D}(t)}d(x,\Gamma(t))\right).

Roughly speaking, Theorem
2.1
says that we have stability in the
D
parameter provided the solution to the system (
1
) without nutrients diffusion is sufficiently regular.

Theorem 2.1

(Hausdorff convergence of the tumor patches)
.

Let
n_{0}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to[\lambda,C]
for some
0<\lambda\leq C<\infty
such that
\nabla n_{0}\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Consider the initial density
\rho_{0}\in BV(\mathbf{\mathbf{R}}^{d})
which takes value of
0
or
1
. For these choices of
(n_{0},\rho_{0})
, assume that either

(i)

n_{0}>1
or

(ii)

there exist
\hat{t}\geq 0
such that
\Gamma(t)
is uniformly
C^{1}
for every
t\geq\hat{t}
.

Then for every
t>\hat{t}
we have

(4)

\lim_{D\to 0}d_{H}\left(\Gamma_{D}(t),\Gamma(t)\right)=0.

The strict positivity of
n_{0}
ensures that the tumor continues to expand over time. The regularity assumption (
ii
), shown in many cases in
[
7
]
(see the remark below), is to ensure that there is a stable direction of propagation for the pressure as
D
tends to zero. These assumptions are likely not sharp, but we keep them in place to clarify our analysis.

Remark 2.2
.

It is shown that a sufficient amount of nutrients leads to the regularity of
\Gamma(t)
, namely that (
i
) implies (
ii
) in the above theorem: see Theorem 2.7 of
[
7
]
. When (
i
) does not hold (
ii
) still holds if the tumor manages to grow out of the convex hull of its initial support
\Omega_{0}
: See
[
7
, Lemma 4.6 and Theorem 2.7]
for the precise conditions that ensure that (
ii
) is satisfied.

One of the main ingredients in the proof is the following result about the strong
H^{1}
convergence of the pressure variables, which is of independent interest. We will use the following notation: if
\rho
is a measurable function such that
0\leq\rho\leq 1
almost everywhere, we define

(5)

H_{\rho}:=\left\{\xi\in H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):\ \xi\geq 0,\ \xi(1-\rho)=0\ \text{a.e.}\right\}.

Proposition 2.3

(Strong convergence of
\nabla p_{D}
)
.

Let
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
\nabla n_{0}\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Let
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be compactly supported and such that
0\leq\rho_{0}\leq 1
. Then for Lebesgue point
t>0
of
p_{D}
we have

(6)

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\nabla p_{D}(x,t)\cdot\left(\nabla p_{D}(x,t)-\nabla\xi(x)\right)dx\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}(p_{D}(x,t)-\xi(x))n_{D}(x,t)\rho_{D}(x,t)dx,

for all
\xi\in H_{\rho_{D}(t)}
. In particular, for any
T>0
,
\nabla p_{D}\to\nabla p
in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
as
D\to 0
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let us point out the following:

(1)

By Definition
3.1
for every
D\geq 0
the map
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\rho_{D}(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is continuous, in particular
\rho_{D}(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
as well as the set
H_{\rho_{D}(t)}
are well-defined for each
t>0
.

(2)

By Proposition
2.3
we know that for every
T>0
,
\nabla p_{D}\to\nabla p
in
L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
as
D\to 0
. By the Sobolev embedding theorem this implies that
p_{D}\to p
in
L^{2}((0,T),L^{2^{*}}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
as
D\to 0
. Using Lemma
5.2
in Section
5
, we infer that
p_{D}\to p
in
L^{q}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
for every
1\leq q\leq 2
.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Existence of weak solutions

Here, we give the definition of weak solution to (
1
). Our definition is a slightly modified version of
[
7
, Definition 2.1]

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
\nabla n_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\rho_{0}\in BV(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
\rho_{0}\in[0,1]
. Fix
T>0
and denote
Q_{T}:=\mathbf{R}^{d}\times[0,T)
. Non-negative functions
\rho\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))\cap L^{\infty}([0,T],BV(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
,
p\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
, and
n\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
such that
\nabla n\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
are said to form a weak solution to (
1
) in
Q_{T}
, if they satisfy:

(i)

\rho\in[0,1]
and
p(1-\rho)=0
in
Q_{T}
.

(ii)

For any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Q_{T})
we have

(7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla p-\rho\partial_{t}\psi)dxdt=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi(x,0)\rho_{0}dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi(n-b)\rho dxdt.

(iii)

For every
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Q_{T})
we have

(8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}(D\nabla n\cdot\nabla\psi-n\partial_{t}\psi)dxdt=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi(x,0)n_{0}dx-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi\rho ndxdt.

Remark 3.2
.

The difference between Definition
3.1
and
[
7
, Definition 2.1]
is that here we allow the initial nutrient
n_{0}
to be such that
\nabla n_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
instead of requiring
\nabla n_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Correspondingly, we require
\nabla n\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
instead of requiring
\nabla n\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
. Also, the class of test functions for items (
ii
) and (
iii
) in Definition
3.1
is now restricted to
C^{\infty}_{c}(Q_{T})
.

The following well-posedness result is proved in
[
7
, Theorem 2.2]
for the original definition of weak solution
[
7
, Definition 2.1]
, but it can be proved in a completely analogous way with our definition.

Theorem 3.3

(Theorem 2.2 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
\nabla n_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Let
\rho_{0}\in BV(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be compactly supported and such that
0\leq\rho_{0}\leq 1
. Then for given
D,b\geq 0
and any
T>0
there exists a unique weak solution
(n,\rho,p)
to (
1
) in
Q_{T}:=\mathbf{R}^{d}\times[0,T)
in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

4.
Construction of weak solutions

In
[
7
]
, the authors provide a numerical scheme for constructing weak solutions. This is based on Wasserstein’s projections, which we recall below. We also describe a slight modification of the scheme that ensures a stronger convergence for the nutrient variable: such convergence will be needed in the proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Given a non-negative function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
we denote by

(9)

M_{f}:=\left\{\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):\ 0\leq\rho\leq 1,\ \|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right\}.

(I)

Scheme I.
Let
n_{0},\rho_{0}
be as in Definition
3.1
and let
D\geq 0
. Fix a parameter
\tau>0
, define
\rho_{D}^{0,\tau}=\rho_{0},\ p_{D}^{0,\tau}=0
and
n_{D}^{0,\tau}=n_{0}
. We then define inductively for every
k\in\mathbf{N}
functions
\rho^{k+1,\tau}_{D},p^{k+1,\tau}_{D}
and
n^{k+1,\tau}_{D}
– provided
\rho^{k,\tau}_{D},p^{k,\tau}_{D}
and
n^{k,\tau}_{D}
are defined – in the following way:

(10)

\displaystyle\rho_{D}^{k+1,\tau}=\underset{\rho\in M_{\rho^{k,\tau}_{D}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{2\tau}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{k,\tau})),

(11)

\displaystyle n^{k+1,\tau}_{D}=e^{\tau D\Delta}(n^{k,\tau}_{D}(1-\tau\rho^{k+1,\tau}_{D}))

(12)

\displaystyle p_{D}^{k+1,\tau}=\underset{p\geq 0}{\operatorname{argmax}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}p^{c_{\tau}}\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{k,\tau})dx-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}pdx,

where the
c_{\tau}
-transform of
p
is defined as

(13)

p^{c_{\tau}}(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}p(y)+\frac{1}{2\tau}|x-y|^{2}.

For future reference we also define

(14)

\mu_{D}^{k,\tau}:=\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{k,\tau}).

We then define
\rho_{D}^{\tau},p_{D}^{\tau},n^{\tau}_{D}
and
\mu_{D}^{\tau}
as the piecewise constant in time, right-continuous interpolations of the above-defined functions, for example

\displaystyle\rho_{D}^{\tau}(x,t):=\rho_{D}^{k+1,\tau}(x)\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\ t\in[k\tau,(k+1)\tau).

When
D=0
, we will sometimes drop the subscript
D
in the above notation.

(II)

Scheme II.
Let
n_{0},\rho_{0}
be as in Definition
3.1
and let
D\geq 0
. Fix a parameter
\tau>0
, define
\rho_{D}^{0,\tau}=\rho_{0},\ p_{D}^{0,\tau}=0
and define

as the solution to

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}n^{\tau}_{D}-D\Delta n^{\tau}_{D}=-n^{\tau}_{D}\rho_{0}\ &\text{on}\ \mathbf{R}^{d}\times(0,\tau),\\
n^{\tau}_{D}(0)=n_{0}\ &\text{on}\ \mathbf{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

We then define inductively for every
k\in\mathbf{N}
functions
\rho^{k+1,\tau}_{D},p^{k+1,\tau}_{D}
and

– provided
\rho^{k,\tau}_{D},p^{k,\tau}_{D}
and

are defined – in the following way:

(16)

\displaystyle\rho_{D}^{k+1,\tau}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\rho\in M_{\rho^{k,\tau}_{D}}}\frac{1}{2\tau}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{\tau}(k\tau))),

the function

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}n^{\tau}_{D}-D\Delta n^{\tau}_{D}=-n^{\tau}_{D}\rho^{k,\tau}\ &\text{on}\ \mathbf{R}^{d}\times((k+1)\tau,(k+2)\tau),\\

while

(18)

\displaystyle p_{D}^{k+1,\tau}=\underset{{p\geq 0}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}p^{c_{\tau}}\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{\tau}(k\tau))dx-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}pdx,

where the
c_{\tau}
-transform of

p^{c_{\tau}}(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}p(y)+\frac{1}{2\tau}|x-y|^{2}.

For future reference we also define

(20)

\mu_{D}^{k,\tau}:=\rho_{D}^{k,\tau}(1+\tau n_{D}^{\tau}(k\tau)).

We then define
\rho_{D}^{\tau},p_{D}^{\tau}
and

as the piecewise constant in time, right-continuous interpolations of the above-defined functions, for example

\displaystyle\rho_{D}^{\tau}(x,t)=\rho_{D}^{k+1,\tau}(x)\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\ t\in[k\tau,(k+1)\tau).

When

Theorem 4.2
.

Fix

. Let

. Let

\chi_{A(t)}-\Delta q(t)\geq\chi_{A(t_{0})}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\rho_{D}(s)n_{D}(s)\chi_{A(s)}ds\quad\text{on}\ B_{R}(x_{0}),

where we set
A(t):=A_{r(t),R}(x_{0})
.

To prove (
149
) we pick
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{R}(x_{0})),\varphi\geq 0
and we compute, using the fact that
\nabla\phi_{+}(s)=\nabla\phi\mathbf{1}_{\phi(s)>0}

(150)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla q(t)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla\phi_{+}(s)\cdot\nabla\varphi dxds\end{aligned}

(151)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phantom{\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla q(t)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\nabla\phi(s)\cdot\nabla\varphi dxds\end{aligned}

(152)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phantom{\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla q(t)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx}=&\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\nabla\phi(s)\cdot\nu(s)\varphi\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(dx)ds\\
&-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\Delta\phi(s)\varphi dxds,\end{aligned}

where we denoted by
\nu(s)
the outer unit normal to
A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})
. We continue by observing that
\nabla\phi(s)\cdot\nu(s)=-|\nabla\phi(s)|
. We also observe that by what we proved in Step 1 and by our choice of
r(t)
we have
-|\nabla\phi(s)|\geq\dot{r}(s)
on
\partial B_{r(s)}(x_{0})
. We denote by
V(s,x)
the normal velocity of a point
x\in\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})
. We compute, using also that
\varphi=0
on
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\nabla\phi(s)\cdot\nu(s)\varphi\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(dx)ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}|\nabla\phi(s)|\varphi\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(dx)ds

\displaystyle\geq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\dot{r}(s)\varphi dxds

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{\partial A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}V(s,x)\varphi(x)dxds

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\int_{A_{r(s),R}(x_{0})}\varphi(x)dxds

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\chi_{A(t_{0})}\varphi dx-\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\chi_{A(t)}\varphi dx.

Inserting back into (
150
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla q(t)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx\geq

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\rho_{D}(s)n_{D}(s)\chi_{A(s)}ds\varphi dx

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\chi_{A(t_{0})}\varphi dx-\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\chi_{A(t)}\varphi dx,

which is the weak formulation for (
149
).

Step 3. We now define

(153)

v_{D}(t,x):=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}p_{D}(s,x)ds,

and we observe that

(154)

\rho_{D}(t)-\Delta v_{D}(t)=\rho_{D}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\rho_{D}(s)n_{D}(s)ds.

We claim that

(155)

v_{D}(t,x)\leq q(t,x)\quad\text{for}\ t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],x\in B_{R}(x_{0}).

This of course implies that for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
we have
\{p_{D}(t)>0\}\cap B_{R}(x_{0})\subset A_{r(t),R}(x_{0})
.

To prove (
155
) we subtract (
149
) from (
154
) and we test the resulting inequality with
(v_{D}(t)-q(t))_{+}
(which is an admissible test function because
v_{D}(t)=q(t)
on
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
). We obtain, using also that at the initial time
t_{0}
we have by assumption that
\chi_{A(t_{0})}\geq\rho_{D}(t_{0})
in the ball
B_{R}(x_{0})
,

(156)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}(\rho_{D}(t)-\chi_{A(t)})(v_{D}(t)-q(t))_{+}dx+\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}|\nabla(v_{D}(t)-q(t))_{+}|^{2}ds

(157)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}(\rho_{D}(t_{0})-\chi_{A(t_{0})}(x_{0}))(v_{D}(t)-q(t))_{+}dx

(158)

\displaystyle\leq 0.

Observe that both terms on the left-hand side of (
156
) are non-negative. Indeed, if for some
x\in B_{R}(x_{0})
we have
v_{D}(t,x)>q(t,x)
, then clearly
\rho_{D}(t,x)=1
. We thus infer that there exists
c(t)\in[0,+\infty)
such that

(159)

(v_{D}(t)-q(t))_{+}=c(t)\quad\text{on}\ B_{R}(x_{0}),

and since
v_{D}(t)=q(t)
on
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
we have
c(t)=0
. In other words (
155
) holds.

Step 4. Conclusion.

Because of Item (
2
) in Remark
2.4
we can assume that
D_{0}
is so small that for every
D\leq D_{0}

(160)

\|p-p_{D}\|_{L^{2}((0,2t_{1})\times\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\delta.

In particular, this yields

(161)

r(t_{1})\geq\frac{R}{2}-\frac{c}{R^{\frac{d}{d+1}+\frac{d}{2}+3}}\left(\delta(t_{1}-t_{0})+\delta\right).

We need
r(t_{1})\geq\frac{R}{4}
, which is satisfied provided

(162)

\frac{R}{4}\geq\frac{c}{R^{\frac{d}{d+1}+\frac{d}{2}+3}}\left(\delta(t_{1}-t_{0})+\delta\right).

We define

(163)

\delta_{0}:=\frac{R^{4+\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{d+1}}}{8c},

then for
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
we have that (
162
) is satisfied provided

(164)

\frac{R^{4+\frac{d}{d+1}+\frac{d}{2}}}{8c\delta}\geq t_{1}-t_{0}.\qed

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We will prove the following: for every
t\in(\hat{t},T)
, given
\epsilon>0
there exists
\overline{D}>0
such that for every
D\leq\overline{D}

(165)

d_{H}(\Gamma_{D}(t),\Gamma(t))\leq\epsilon.

Because of Proposition
5.6
, it suffices to show that there exists
\overline{D}>0
such that for every
D\leq\overline{D}

(166)

\sup_{x\in\Gamma_{D}(t)}d(x,\Gamma(t))\leq\epsilon.

To prove (
166
) we proceed in three steps.

Step 1. Exterior fingers control. Define
\Omega_{t}=\{\rho(t)=1\}
. We claim that we can select
D_{1}>0
such that for
D\leq D_{1}

(167)

\Omega^{\epsilon}_{t}:=\left\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}:d(x,\Omega_{t})<\epsilon\right\}\supset\Gamma_{D}(t)\setminus\{\rho(t)=1\}..

To prove (
167
), we can apply Proposition
5.8
with
R=\frac{\epsilon}{2}
,
t_{1}=t
,
t_{0}=0
and
\delta
small enough to find that there exists
D_{1}>0
such that for every
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega_{t}^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}
and for every
D\leq D_{1}

(168)

B_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(x_{0})\cap\{p_{D}(t)>0\}\subset A_{\frac{\epsilon}{8},\frac{\epsilon}{2}}(x_{0}).

In particular we infer
\Gamma_{D}(t)\setminus\{\rho(t)=1\}\subset\Omega_{t}^{\epsilon}
.

Step 2. Interior fingers control.

By the smoothness of
p
on
[\hat{t},T]
we can find a constant
C>0
such that
|\nabla p(s,x)|\leq C
for every
s\in[\hat{t},T]
and every
x\in\partial\{\rho(s)=1\}
. We let
\gamma=\min\left(1,\frac{\epsilon}{2C},t-\hat{t}\right)
. We apply Proposition
5.7
with
\delta\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{5}
. Then there exists
D_{2}>0
such that for
D\leq D_{2}

(169)

U_{\gamma^{2}}(t-\gamma)\subset\{p_{D}(t)>0\}.

We now take
x\in\Gamma_{D}(t)\cap\{\rho(t)=1\}
. By what we just showed we have
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus U_{\gamma^{2}}(t-\gamma)
. In particular there exists
y\in\partial\Gamma(t-\gamma)
such that
|x-y|<\gamma^{2}
. Let now
y:[t-\gamma,t]\to\mathbf{R}^{d}
be the curve defined by

(170)

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(s)=-\nabla p(s,y(s))\quad&\text{for}\ s\in[t-\gamma,t]\\
y(t-\gamma)=y.\end{cases}

Then
y(t)\in\Gamma(t)
and

\displaystyle d(x,\Gamma(t))

\displaystyle\leq|x-y(t)|

\displaystyle\leq|x-y(t-\gamma)|+|y(t-\gamma)-y(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}+\int_{t-\gamma}^{t}|\nabla p(s,y(s))|ds\leq\gamma^{2}+C\gamma\leq\epsilon.

Step 3. Conclusion.

Take
\overline{D}=\min(D_{1},D_{2})
then for any
D\leq\overline{D}
and any
x\in\Gamma_{D}(t)
we have

(1)

If
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus\{\rho(t)=1\}
then, by what we proved in Step 1 we have
x\in\Omega_{t}^{\epsilon}
, thus
d(x,\Gamma(t))\leq\epsilon
.

(2)

Otherwise we apply what we proved in Step 2 to infer that
d(x,\Gamma(t))\leq\epsilon
.

We thus have that

(171)

\sup_{x\in\Gamma_{D}(t)}d(x,\Gamma(t))\leq\epsilon,

and the proof of Theorem
2.1
is concluded.
