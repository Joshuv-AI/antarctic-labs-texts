1.
Does one solution imply many solutions?

A general problem: Suppose that an infinite set of equations has the property
that every equation, or all but finitely many of the equations, or, perhaps,
infinitely many of the equations have at least one solution.
Does some equation have at least two solutions? Three solutions? Arbitrarily many solutions?
Infinitely many solutions?

If the answer is ‘no,’ then modify the question.
Suppose that an infinite set of equations has the property
that every equation, or all but finitely many of the equations, or, perhaps,
infinitely many of the equations have at least two solutions.
Does some equation have at least three solutions? Four solutions? Arbitrarily many solutions?
Infinitely many solutions?

And so on.

This paper considers a problem of this kind in multiplicative number theory.

2.
Additive bases

Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers and
\mathbf{N}_{0}=\mathbf{N}\cup\{0\}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots,\}

the set of nonnegative integers.
Let
\mathbf{P}=\{2,3,5,7,\ldots\}
be the set of prime numbers.

The set
A
of nonnegative integers is an
additive basis of order
h

if every nonnegative integer can be represented as the sum of
h
elements in the set.
The set
A
is an
asymptotic additive basis of order
h

if every sufficiently large integer is the sum of
h
elements in the set.

For every positive integer
h
,
the
additive representation function

r_{A,h}(n)
counts the number
of ordered representations of
n
as a sum of
h
elements of
A
:

r_{A,h}(n)=\card\left\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{h})\in A^{h}:a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}=n\right\}.

Thus,
A
is an additive basis of order
h
if
r_{A,h}(n)\geq 1
for all
n\geq 0
,
and an asymptotic additive basis of order
h
if
r_{A,h}(n)\geq 1
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
In 1941, Erdős and Turán
[
1
]
asked if the representation function
of every asymptotic additive basis of order
2
is unbounded.
Equivalently, is it true that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{A,2}(n)\geq 1\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{A,2}(n)=\infty?

This problem is still unsolved. More generally, for
h\geq 2
, is it true that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{A,h}(n)\geq 1\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{A,h}(n)=\infty?

3.
Multiplicative bases and multiplicative systems

Let
B
be a set of positive integers. For every positive integer
h
,
the
multiplicative representation function

g_{B,h}(n)
counts the number
of ordered representations of
n
as a product of
h
elements of
B
:

g_{B,h}(n)=\card\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{h})\in B^{h}:b_{1}\cdots b_{h}=n\right\}.

The set
B
is a
multiplicative basis of order
h

if
g_{B,h}(n)\geq 1
for all
n\geq 1
,
and an
asymptotic multiplicative basis of order
h

if
g_{B,h}(n)\geq 1
for all sufficiently large integers
n
.

In 1964, Erdős
[
2
]
proved the following multiplicative analogue
of the Erdős-Turán conjecture.

Theorem 1

(Erdős)
.

The multiplicative representation function of an asymptotic multiplicative basis of
B

of order
2
is unbounded.

Equivalently,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{B,2}(n)\geq 1\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{B,2}(n)=\infty.

Erdős’ proof was graph theoretic. In 1985, Nešetřil and Rödl
[
5
]

gave a different proof using Ramsey’s theorem.
In Section
4
, the Nešetřil-Rödl method
is used to prove Theorem
1

for asymptotic multiplicative bases of order
h
for all
h\geq 2
.

There is a natural generalization of a multiplicative basis.
Let
h\geq 2
and let
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
be an
h
-tuple of sets of positive integers.
For every positive integer
h
, consider
the
multiplicative representation function

g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\card\left\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{h})\in B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{h}:b_{1}\cdots b_{h}=n\right\}.

The
h
-tuple
\mathcal{B}
is a
multiplicative system of order
h

if
g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq 1
for all
n\geq 1
, and an

asymptotic multiplicative system of order
h

if
g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq 1
for all sufficiently large integers
n
.

For example, let
\mathbf{P}=\{2,3,5,\ldots\}
be the set of prime numbers, and let

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathbf{P}_{h}

be a partition of the prime numbers into
h
pairwise disjoint nonempty sets.
For all
i=\{1,\ldots,h\}
, let
B_{i}
be the set of positive integers all of whose prime factors
are in
\mathbf{P}_{i}
, and let
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
.
Note that
1\in B_{i}
for all
i
.
The fundamental theorem of arithmetic is equivalent to the statement that

g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=1
for all
n=1,2,3,\ldots
.
Thus,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=1

and the analogue of Erdős’ theorem on asymptotic multiplicative bases is false for multiplicative systems.

This example, however, is misleading.
For
h\geq 2
, let
M(h)
consist of all pairs
(s,t)
such that

s=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\qqand t=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)

for some asymptotic multiplicative system
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
of order
h
.
In Section
6
we prove the following result of Nathanson
[
4
]
:

M(h)=\{(1,t):t\in\mathbf{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}\cup\{(s,\infty):s=2,\ldots,h\}.

This implies that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq 2\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\infty.

4.
An iterated Ramsey’s theorem and multiplicative bases

Let
X
be a set and let
[X]^{k}
be the set of all subsets of
X
of cardinality
k
.
We have
[X]^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
and
[X]^{1}=\left\{\left\{x\right\}:x\in X\right\}
.
Let
[X]^{<\omega}
be the set of all finite subsets of
X
.

The infinite pigeonhole principle states that if an infinite set
X
is the union
of a finite number of subsets, then at least one of the subsets is infinite.
This is equivalent to Ramsey’s theorem in the case
k=1
.

Theorem 2

(Ramsey’s theorem)
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set.
If
k
and
r_{k}
are nonnegative integers and

[X]^{k}=C^{k}_{0}\cup C^{k}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C^{k}_{r_{k}}

then there exists an infinite subset
X_{k}
of
X

and an integer
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r_{k}\}
such that

[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq C^{k}_{\varepsilon_{k}}.

A standard reference is
Ramsey Theory

by Graham, Rothschild, and Spencer
[
3
]
.
We use an iterated form of Ramsey’s theorem to prove
that the representation function of a multiplicative basis is unbounded
(Theorem
5
).

Theorem 3

(Iterated Ramsey’s theorem)
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set.
For every nonnegative integer
k
, let

C^{k}_{0},C^{k}_{1},\ldots,C^{k}_{r_{k}}
be (not necessarily pairwise disjoint) subsets of
[X]^{k}
such that

(1)

[X]^{k}=C^{k}_{0}\cup C^{k}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C^{k}_{r_{k}}.

There exist an infinite decreasing sequence of infinite subsets of
X

(2)

X\supseteq X_{0}\supseteq X_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq X_{k}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq X_{n}\supseteq\cdots

and a sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}}
with

\varepsilon_{k}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r_{k}\}
such that

(3)

[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq C^{k}_{\varepsilon_{k}}

for all
k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
.

It follows from (
2
) and (
3
)
that
[X_{n}]^{k}\subseteq[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq C_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{k}
for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
k
.

Let
X_{0}=X
. We have

[X_{0}]^{0}=[X]^{0}=\{\emptyset\}=C^{0}_{\varepsilon_{0}}

for some
\varepsilon_{0}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r_{0}\}
.
This proves the theorem for
k=0
.
The case
k=1
follows from the infinite pigeonhole principle.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
X_{k}
be an infinite subset of
X
that satisfies (
3
).
For
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,r_{k+1}\}
, let

D^{k+1}_{i}=[X_{k}]^{k+1}\cap C^{k+1}_{i}.

Because
[X_{k}]^{k+1}\subseteq[X]^{k+1}
, we have

[X_{k}]^{k+1}=D^{k+1}_{0}\cup D^{k+1}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup D^{k+1}_{r_{k+1}}.

By Ramsey’s theorem, there is an infinite subset
X_{k+1}
of
X_{k}
such that

[X_{k+1}]^{k+1}\subseteq D^{k+1}_{\varepsilon_{k+1}}\subseteq C^{k+1}_{\varepsilon_{k+1}}

for some
\varepsilon_{k+1}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r_{k+1}\}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Let
h\geq 2
.
Let
X
be a countably infinite set, and let
\mathcal{A}

be a set of finite subsets of
X
.
For every finite subset
S
of
X
,
let
g_{\mathcal{A},h}(S)
count the number of

h
-tuples
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
in
\mathcal{A}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}
such that

(4)

A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset\qquad\text{for $1\leq i<j\leq h$}

and

(5)

S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}A_{i}.

Let
W
be an infinite set of positive integers such that
g_{\mathcal{A},h}(S)\geq 1

for all
n\in W
and for all but finitely many sets
S\in[X]^{n}
.
For every integer
n
, there is a finite subset
S
of
X
with

g_{\mathcal{A},h}(S)\geq n
.

Proof.

For all
k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
, let

C_{1}^{k}=[X]^{k}\cap\mathcal{A}\qqand C_{0}^{k}=[X]^{k}\setminus C_{1}^{k}.

Thus,

[X]^{k}=C_{0}^{k}\cup C_{1}^{k}\qqand C_{0}^{k}\cap C_{1}^{k}=\emptyset.

We apply Theorem
3
with
r_{k}=1
for all
k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
.
For every positive integer
n
, there is an infinite subset
X_{n}
of
X

such that, for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
,
there exists
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{0,1\}
with

[X_{n}]^{k}\subseteq C_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{k}.

Let
n\in W
. The infinite set
X_{n}
contains infinitely many subsets of cardinality
n
.
Choose a subset
S
of
X_{n}
of cardinality
n
for which
there exists an
h
-tuple
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
in
\mathcal{A}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}
satisfying
conditions (
4
) and (
5
).
We have
k_{i}=|A_{i}|\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
The sets
A_{i}
are pairwise disjoint, and so

\sum_{i=1}^{h}k_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{h}|A_{i}|=|S|=n.

Because
A_{i}\subseteq S\subseteq X_{n}\subseteq X
and
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
,
we have

A_{i}\in[X_{n}]^{k_{i}}\cap\mathcal{A}\subseteq[X]^{k_{i}}\cap\mathcal{A}=C_{1}^{k_{i}}.

It follows that
\varepsilon_{k_{i}}=1
and

(6)

[X_{n}]^{k_{i}}\subseteq C_{1}^{k_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
Thus, every subset of
X_{n}
of cardinality
k_{i}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}
.
The multinomial coefficient

\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}=\frac{n!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{h}!}

counts the number of partitions of
S
into pairwise disjoint subsets
A^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,A^{\prime}_{h}

of cardinalities
k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}
, respectively.
It follows from (
6
) that
A^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
,
and so

g_{\mathcal{A}}(S)\geq\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}.

If
k_{i}<n
for all
i
, then

g_{\mathcal{A}}(S)\geq\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}\geq n.

If
k_{j}=n
for some
j
, then
k_{i}=0
for all
i\neq j
and

\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}=1
.
In this case,
S\in\mathcal{A}
and
[X_{n}]^{n}\subseteq C_{1}^{n}
.
Thus, every
n
-element subset of
X_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}
.
Also,
\emptyset\in\mathcal{A}
.
Let
S^{\prime}
be a subset of
X_{n}
of cardinality
2n
.
There are
\binom{2n}{n}
partitions of
S
of the form
T=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{h}
,
where
|A_{1}|=|A_{2}|=n
and
|A_{i}|=0
for
i\in\{3,\ldots,h\}
.
It follows that

g_{\mathcal{A}}(S^{\prime})\geq\binom{2n}{n}\geq n.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5
.

The multiplicative representation function of an asymptotic multiplicative basis
B

of order
h
is unbounded.

Equivalently,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{B,h}(n)\geq 1\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{B,h}(n)=\infty.

Proof.

A
square-free (or quadratfrei) integer
is a positive integer that is the product of distinct primes.
Equivalently, a positive integer
n
is square-free if it is not divisible by the square of a prime.
Let
Q=\{1,2,3,5,6,7,10,11,\ldots\}
be the set of square-free integers.
Define the function
\Phi:Q\rightarrow[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}
as follows:
For
q\in Q
,

\Phi(q)=\{p\in\mathbf{P}:\text{$p$ divides $q$}\}.

The function
\Phi
is a bijection, with inverse function

\Phi^{-1}:[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}\rightarrow Q
defined as follows:
For
S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}
,

\Phi^{-1}(S)=\prod_{p\in S}p.

If
n
is square-free and if
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
are positive integers such that
n=b_{1}\cdots b_{h}
,
then the integers
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
are square-free and pairwise relatively prime.
Let
S=\Phi(n)
and let
S_{i}=\Phi(b_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
It follows that
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}S_{i}
and that
S_{1},\ldots,S_{h}
are pairwise disjoint
sets in
[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}
.

Conversely, let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{h}
be pairwise disjoint sets in
[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}

and
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}S_{i}
.
Let
n=\Phi^{-1}(S)
and let
b_{i}=\Phi^{-1}(S_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
We have

n=\Phi^{-1}(S)=\prod_{i=1}^{h}\Phi^{-1}(S_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{h}b_{i}.

Thus, there is a one to one correspondence between partitions of a finite set
of primes into
h
pairwise disjoint subsets and representations of a square-free
integer as a product of
h
factors.

Let
B
be an asymptotic multiplicative basis of order
h
. Let

\mathcal{A}=\{\Phi^{-1}(b):b\in B\cap Q\}\subseteq[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}.

Because
B
is an asymptotic multiplicative basis,
every square-free integer, with a finite number of exceptions,
has a representation as a product of
h
pairwise relatively prime square-free integers in
B
.
Thus, with a finite number of exceptions, every set
S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}

has a representation as the union of
h
pairwise disjoint sets in
\mathcal{A}
.
Theorem
4
implies that for every integer
n
there is a set

S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}
with at least
n
representations as the union
of
h
pairwise disjoint sets in
\mathcal{A}
. It follows that the integer
q=\Phi(S)\in Q

has at least
n
representations as a product of
h
pairwise relatively prime
square-free integers in
B
, and so
g_{B,h}(q)\geq n
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5.
A doubly iterated Ramsey’s theorem

We use an iterated form of the iterated Ramsey’s theorem to study
the representation function of a multiplicative system (Theorem
8
).
We need the following elementary Boolean identity.

Lemma 1
.

Let
Y
and
I
be sets. For all
i\in I
, let
J_{i}
be a set, and let
\{C_{i,j_{i}}:j_{i}\in J_{i}\}

be a set of subsets of
Y
such that

Y=\bigcup_{j_{i}\in J_{i}}C_{i,j_{i}}.

Then

Y=\bigcup_{(j_{i})\in\prod_{i\in I}J_{i}}\hskip 5.69046pt\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,j_{i}}

where
\prod_{i\in I}J_{i}
is the Cartesian product of the sets
J_{i}
.

Proof.

Because the sets
C_{i,j_{i}}
are subsets of
Y
, we have

\bigcup_{(j_{i})\in\prod_{i\in I}J_{i}}\hskip 5.69046pt\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,j_{i}}\subseteq Y.

Let
y\in Y
. For all
i\in I
, there exists
j_{i}\in J_{i}
such that
y\in C_{i,j_{i}}
and so

y\in\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,j_{i}}.

Therefore,

y\in\bigcup_{(j_{i})\in\prod_{i\in I}J_{i}}\hskip 5.69046pt\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,j_{i}}

and

Y\subseteq\bigcup_{(j_{i})\in\prod_{i\in I}J_{i}}\hskip 5.69046pt\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,j_{i}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6

(Doubly iterated Ramsey’s theorem)
.

Let
X
be a countably infinite set.
Let
I_{k}
be a finite set for every nonnegative integer
k
.
For all
i\in I_{k}
, let
J^{k}_{i}
be a finite set and let
\{C^{k}_{i,j_{i}}:j_{i}\in J^{k}_{i}\}

be a set of (not necessarily pairwise disjoint) subsets of
[X]^{k}
such that

(7)

[X]^{k}=\bigcup_{j_{i}\in J_{i}}C^{k}_{i,j_{i}}.

There is an infinite decreasing sequence of infinite subsets of

(8)

X\supseteq X_{0}\supseteq X_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq X_{k}\supseteq X_{k+1}\supseteq\cdots

and, for all
k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
and for all
i\in I_{k}
,
there is an integer
\varepsilon_{i,k}\in J_{i}^{k}
such that

(9)

[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq C^{k}_{i,\varepsilon_{i,k}}.

It follows from (

) and (

)
that
[X_{n}]^{k}\subseteq[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq C^{k}_{i,\varepsilon_{i,k}}
for all

Proof.

It suffices to prove that there exists
(\varepsilon_{i,k})=(\varepsilon_{i,k})_{i\in I_{k}}\in\prod_{i\in I_{k}}J_{i}^{k}
such that

[X_{k}]^{k}\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in I_{k}}C^{k}_{i,\varepsilon_{i,k}}.

Applying Lemma
1
with

, we obtain

[X]^{k}=\bigcup_{(\varepsilon_{i,k})\in\prod_{i\in I_{k}}J^{k}_{i}}\hskip 5.69046pt\bigcap_{i\in I}C_{i,\varepsilon_{i,k}}

The set
\prod_{i\in I_{k}}J^{k}_{i}
is finite because the sets
I_{k}
and

are finite.
The doubly iterated Ramsey’s theorem follows immediately from the iterated

(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
and
(A^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,A^{\prime}_{h})

in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{h}

that satisfy conditions (
10
) and (
11
).
Let

|A_{i}|=k_{i}\qqand|A^{\prime}_{i}|=k^{\prime}_{i}

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
. The sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{h}
are pairwise disjoint
and the sets
A^{\prime}_{1},\dots,A^{\prime}_{h}
are pairwise disjoint, and so

\sum_{i=1}^{h}k_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{h}k^{\prime}_{i}=n.

Because
A_{i}\in[X_{n}]^{k_{i}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
A^{\prime}_{i}\in[X_{n}]^{k^{\prime}_{i}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}
,
every subset of
X_{n}
of cardinality
k_{i}
or
k^{\prime}_{i}
is in
\mathcal{A}_{i}

It follows that if
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}B_{i}
is a partition of
S
with
|B_{i}|=k_{i}
for
all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, then
B_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
The number of such partitions is the multinomial coefficient

\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}.

If
k_{i}<n
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, then

\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{h}}\geq n.

Similarly, if
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}B^{\prime}_{i}
is a partition of
S
with
|B^{\prime}_{i}|=k^{\prime}_{i}
for
all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, then
B^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
The number of such partitions is the multinomial coefficient

\binom{n}{k^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,k^{\prime}_{h}}.

If
k^{\prime}_{i}<n
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, then

\binom{n}{k^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,k^{\prime}_{h}}\geq n.

If
k_{i}=k^{\prime}_{j}=n
for some
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
,
then
B_{i}=B^{\prime}_{j}=S
and
B_{\ell}=\emptyset
for all
\ell\neq i
and

B^{\prime}_{\ell}=\emptyset
for all
\ell\neq j
.
Because
(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})\neq(B^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,B^{\prime}_{h})
, we have
i\neq j
.
Every subset of
X_{n}
of cardinality
n
is in both
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{j}
.
Also,
\emptyset\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.

Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be disjoint subsets of
[X_{n}]^{n}
, and let
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.
We have
|S|=2n
. There are
\binom{2n}{n}
subsets

of cardinality

.
For each of these sets

, let
B_{i}=U
,
B_{j}=S\setminus U
, and
B_{\ell}=\emptyset

for
\ell\neq i,j
.
This gives
\binom{2n}{n}>n
ordered
h
-tuples

(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{h}

that satisfy conditions (
10
) and (
11
).
This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Representation functions of multiplicative systems

Theorem 8
.

For
h\geq 2
, let
M(h)
consist of all pairs
(s,t)
such that

s=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\qqand t=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)

for some asymptotic multiplicative system
\mathcal{B}
of order
h
.
Then

M(h)=\{(1,t):t\in\mathbf{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}\cup\{(s,\infty):s\in\{2,\ldots,h\}\}.

Proof.

For every asymptotic multiplicative system
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
of order
h
,
we have

s=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq 1.

Let
p
be a prime number. For all
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{h})\in B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{h}
,
we have
p=b_{1}\cdots b_{h}
if and only if
b_{j}=p
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
and
b_{i}=1
for all
i\neq j
.
It follows that
g_{\mathcal{B}}(p)\leq h
, and so

s=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\leq h.

For every positive integer
t
, define the sets

B_{1}=\mathbf{N},\quad B_{2}=\{2^{k}:0\leq k\leq t-1\},\quad B_{i}=\{1\}\quad\text{for $i=3,\ldots,h$}

and let
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
.
If
n
is an odd positive integer, then

g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=1

and so

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=1.

Let
n=2^{\ell-1}m
, where
m
is odd.
We have
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{h})\in B_{1}\times B_{2}\times\cdots\times B_{h}
and
n=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{h}

if and only if

(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{h})=\left(2^{\ell-1-i}m,2^{i},1,\ldots,1\right)

for some
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\min(\ell-1,t-1)\}
.
It follows that
g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\min(\ell,t)

and so

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=t.

Therefore,
(1,t)\in M(h)
for all
t\in\mathbf{N}
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
, where

B_{1}=\mathbf{N},\quad B_{2}=\{2^{k}:k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}\},\quad B_{i}=\{1\}\quad\text{for $i=3,\ldots,h$.}

We have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=1\qqand\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\infty

and so
(1,\infty)\in M(h)
.

For
s\in\{2,3,\ldots,h\}
, let
\mathcal{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{h})
be the multiplicative system
defined by

B_{1}=\mathbf{N},\qquad B_{i}=\mathbf{P}\cup\{1\}\quad\text{for $i=2,\ldots,s$,}

and

B_{i}=\{1\}\quad\text{for $i=s+1,\ldots,h$.}

We have

g_{\mathcal{B}}(p)=s\qquad\text{for all $p\in\mathbf{P}$}

and

g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq s\qquad\text{for all $n\geq 2$.}

Therefore,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=s.

If
n\in\mathbf{N}
has
k
distinct prime factors, then
g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq k
and so

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\infty.

It follows that

\{(s,\infty):s\in\{2,\ldots,h\}\}\subseteq M(h).

We must prove that if
(s,t)\in M(h)
and
s\geq 2
, then
t=\infty
.
As in the proof of Theorem
5
,
we consider the set
Q
of square-free integers, and the functions

\Phi:Q\rightarrow[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}\qqand\Phi^{-1}:[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}\rightarrow Q

defined by

\Phi(q)=\{p\in\mathbf{P}:\text{$p$ divides $q$}\}\qquad\text{for all $q\in Q$}

and

\Phi^{-1}(S)=\prod_{p\in S}p\qquad\qquad\text{for all $S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}$.}

There is a one to one correspondence between ordered partitions of a finite set
of primes into
h
pairwise disjoint subsets and ordered representations of a square-free
integer as a product of
h
factors.
Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{h}
be pairwise disjoint sets in
[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}
,
and let
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}S_{i}
.
Let
n=\Phi^{-1}(S)
and
b_{i}=\Phi^{-1}(S_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
We have

n=\Phi^{-1}(S)=\prod_{i=1}^{h}\Phi^{-1}(S_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{h}b_{i}.

Conversely, if
b_{1},\ldots,b_{h}
are pairwise relatively prime square-free integers
and
n=b_{1}\cdots b_{h}
with
b_{i}\in B_{i}
,
then the sets
\Phi(b_{1}),\ldots,\Phi(b_{h})
are pairwise disjoint and

\Phi(n)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}\Phi(b_{i})
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, let

\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{\Phi(b_{i}):b_{i}\in Q\cap B_{i}\}\subseteq[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}

where
Q\cap B_{i}
is the set of square-free integers in the set
B_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{h})
.
We have
b_{i}\in Q\cap B_{i}
if and only if
\Phi(b_{i})\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, and so

g_{\mathcal{B}}(q)=g_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}\left(\Phi(q)\right)

for all square-free integers
q
.
The inequality
\liminf_{q\in Q}g_{\mathcal{B}}(q)\geq 2
is equivalent to

\liminf_{S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}}g_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(S)\geq 2
.
Theorem
7
implies

\limsup_{S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}}g_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(S)=\infty
, and so

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{q\in Q}g_{\mathcal{B}}(q)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{q\in Q}g_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(\Phi(q))

\displaystyle=\limsup_{S\in[\mathbf{P}]^{<\omega}}g_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(S)

\displaystyle=\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
.

For every asymptotic multiplicative system
\mathcal{B}
of order

,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)\geq 2\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{\mathcal{B}}(n)=\infty.

7.
Open problems

Theorems
5
and
8

are answers to questions of the following kind.
Suppose that a representation function has at least one, or two, or three, or …solutions.
Does this imply that there are occasionally even more solutions?
Does this imply that the representation function can be arbitrarily large?
Here are related open problems.

(1)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of nonnegative integers that is an asymptotic additive basis of order 2.
Suppose that every sufficently large integer has at least 1000 representations
as the sum of two elements of
\mathcal{A}
?
Does some integer have 1010 representations?

(2)

Let

is an
inner product basis

for a set
W
of integers if every integer in
W
is the inner product of two vectors in
\mathcal{A}
.
For what infinite sets
W
with inner product basis
\mathcal{A}
is the representation function bounded?
For what infinite sets
W
with inner product basis
\mathcal{A}
is the representation function unbounded?

(3)

Associated to every
n\times n
matrix
M
is the quadratic form
Q_{M}
,
defined by
Q_{M}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{t}M\mathbf{x}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Let
M
be an
n\times n
matrix with nonnegative integral coordinates.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set vectors with nonnegative integral coordinates,
and let
W
be an infinite
set of nonnegative integers such that
W\subseteq Q_{M}(\mathcal{A})
.
When is the representation function bounded?
When is the representation function unbounded?
