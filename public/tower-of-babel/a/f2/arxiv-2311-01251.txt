Limit theorems for general functionals of Brownian local times Thanks: Nicolas Lengert and Mark Podolskij gratefully acknowledge financial support of ERC Consolidator Grant 815703 “STAMFORD: Statistical Methods for High Dimensional Diffusions”.
Simon Campese, Nicolas Lengert, Mark Podolskij

Abstract
In this paper, we present the asymptotic theory for integrated functions of increments of Brownian local times in space. Specifically, we determine their first-order limit, along with the asymptotic distribution of the fluctuations. Our key result establishes that a standardized version of our statistic converges stably in law towards a mixed normal distribution. Our contribution builds upon a series of prior works by S. Campese, X. Chen, Y. Hu, W.V. Li, M.B. Markus, D. Nualart and J. Rosen [ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 10 , 13 , 14 ] , which delved into special cases of the considered problem. Notably, [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 13 , 14 ] explored quadratic and cubic cases, predominantly utilizing the method of moments technique, Malliavin calculus and Ray-Knight theorems to demonstrate asymptotic mixed normality. Meanwhile, [ 2 ] extended the theory to general polynomials under a non-standard centering by exploiting Perkins’ semimartingale representation of local time and the Kailath-Segall formula. In contrast to the methodologies employed in [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 13 ] , our approach relies on infill limit theory for semimartingales, as formulated in [ 6 , 8 ] . Notably, we establish the limit theorem for general functions that satisfy mild smoothness and growth conditions. This extends the scope beyond the polynomial cases studied in previous works, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the asymptotic properties of the considered functionals. Keywords : Brownian motion, local time, mixed normality, semimartingales, stable convergence. AMS 2000 subject classifications : 60F05,60G44,60H05.

Introduction

Over the past five decades, the mathematical literature has witnessed a surge in interest regarding the probabilistic and statistical properties of local times. Originating from the structure of a Hamiltonian in a specific polymer model, numerous investigations have been dedicated to the asymptotic theory concerning functionals derived from the local time of a Brownian motion. A notable body of work in this domain includes
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
10
,
13
]
.
Recall that the local time
(L^{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
of a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
over a time interval
[0,1]
is defined as the almost sure limit

L^{x}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1}1_{(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)}(W_{s})ds.

(1.1)

The primary focus of our paper centers around statistics of the form:

V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)dx,\qquad h>0,

(1.2)

where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth enough function with
f(0)=0
.
Our objective is to ascertain the asymptotic behavior of the statistic
V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
as
h\to 0
. The theorem below summarizes several special cases, extensively explored in the existing literature, that fall within the scope of our investigation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random variable independent of the local time
(L^{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
be a function with polynomial growth satisfying
f(0)=0

\rho_{u}(f):=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{N}(0,u^{2}))]\qquad\text{for }u\in\mathbb{R}.

Then it holds that

V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V(f)_{\mathbb{R}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\sigma_{u}}(f)du\qquad\text{where}\qquad\sigma_{u}:=2\sqrt{L^{u}}

(1.4)

as
h\to 0
.
If moreover
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f,f^{\prime}
have polynomial growth we deduce the stable convergence

U(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}:=h^{-1/2}\left(V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}-V(f)_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{v^{2}_{\sigma_{u}}-\sigma^{2}_{u}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{u}}(f^{\prime})}~dW^{\prime}_{u},

(1.5)

where
W^{\prime}
is a Brownian motion defined on an extended probability space and independent of
\mathcal{F}
. The quantity

is defined as

v_{x}^{2}:=2\int_{0}^{1}\text{\rm cov}\left(f(xB_{1}),f(x(B_{s+1}-B_{s}))\right)ds

(1.6)

with
B
being a standard Brownian motion.

Building on the insights presented in
[
2
]
, our approach begins by leveraging the semimartingale representation of the local time. This transformation allows us to recast the original problem into an asymptotic statistic of a semimartingale. Employing a series of approximation techniques from stochastic analysis, we then apply the limit theory for high-frequency observations of semimartingales, as established in

This property, crucial for obtaining the necessary smoothness for a stable limit theorem, is absent in our model. Instead, our diffusion coefficient is represented by the process

defined in (

A surprising distinction, in comparison to

, devoid of an

is even or not.

2

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all random variables and stochastic processes are defined on a filtered probability space denoted by

. All positive constants

We say that a function

. We denote by

and

.
For real-valued stochastic processes

and

, we employ the notation

\sup_{t\in A}\left|Y^{n}_{t}-Y_{t}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

for any compact set

. For a sequence of random variables

defined on a Polish space
(E,\mathcal{E})
, we say that

converges stably in law towards

), which lives on an extension
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathbb{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
of the original probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
,
if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Fg(Y^{n})]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}[Fg(Y)]

-measurable random variables

.

2
Definitions and preliminary results

To begin, we utilize the semimartingale representation of the local time process
(L^{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
, as derived in
[
11
]
.
This representation posits the existence of a Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
such that the local time is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle L^{x}=L^{z}+\int_{z}^{x}\sigma_{y}dB_{y}+\int_{z}^{x}a_{y}dy,\qquad x\geq z,

(2.1)

where the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is defined at (
1.4
), and the drift coefficient
a
is a predictable, locally bounded process. This representation, as emphasized in the introduction, serves as a fundamental tool for establishing the stable limit theorem in (
1.5
). Additionally, we introduce two random times

\displaystyle\underline{S}:=\inf\left\{a\leq 0:~L^{a}>0\right\},\qquad\overline{S}:=\sup\left\{a\geq 0:~L^{a}>0\right\},

(2.2)

and remark that
\underline{S}
and
\overline{S}
are stopping times with respect to the filtration generated by
L
. Note
L^{x}=0
for any
x\not\in[\underline{S},\overline{S}]
.

To establish the results outlined in Theorem
1.2
, it is essential to introduce a functional version of the statistic
V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
. For a fixed
T>0
, we define this functional as follows:

V(f)^{h}_{t}:=\int_{I_{t}}f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)dx,\qquad t\in[-T,T].

(2.3)

We obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
be a function with polynomial growth satisfying
f(0)=0
. Then it holds that

V(f)^{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}V(f)\text{ as }h\to 0\qquad\text{where}\qquad V(f)_{t}:=\int_{I_{t}}\rho_{\sigma_{u}}(f)du.

(2.4)

Assume moreover that
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f,f^{\prime}
have polynomial growth, and define the process

U(f)^{h}_{t}:=h^{-1/2}\left(V(f)^{h}_{t}-V(f)_{t}\right).

Then, as
h\to 0
, we obtain the functional stable convergence
U(f)^{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)
on
(C([-T,T]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, where

U(f)_{t}:=\int_{I_{t}}r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}dx+\int_{I_{t}}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}+\int_{I_{t}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x}.

(2.5)

The processes
v_{u}
and
W^{\prime}
have been introduced in Theorem
1.2
, while the quantities
w_{u}
and
r_{u_{1},u_{2}}
are defined as

\displaystyle w_{u}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle u\rho_{u}(f^{\prime}),

(2.6)

\displaystyle r_{u_{1},u_{2}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle u_{1}\rho_{u_{2}}(f^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(u_{2}(B_{x+1}-B_{x}))(B_{2}^{2}-2)\right]dx.

We now demonstrate that the consistency statement in (
1.4
), as mentioned in Theorem
1.2
, follows from the more general results provided in Theorem
2.1
. Initially, we observe the identities

V(f)^{h}_{\mathbb{R}}=V(f)^{h}_{\underline{S}}+V(f)^{h}_{\overline{S}}+O_{\mathbb{P}}(h),\qquad V(f)_{\mathbb{R}}=V(f)_{\underline{S}}+V(f)_{\overline{S}}

(2.7)

hold. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|V(f)^{h}_{\underline{S}}-V(f)_{\underline{S}}|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|V(f)^{h}_{t}-V(f)_{t}|>\varepsilon,|\underline{S}|\leq T\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(|\underline{S}|>T\right),

(2.8)

and a similar estimate holds for the probability
\mathbb{P}(|V(f)^{h}_{\overline{S}}-V(f)_{\overline{S}}|>\varepsilon)
.
Consequently, the uniform convergence in (
2.4
) implies the statement in (
1.4
) when we choose
T
to be sufficiently large and then
h
to be sufficiently small. This establishes the consistency result in the context of Theorem
1.2
.

Subject to an additional smoothness condition on the function
f
, the expression for the limit
U(f)_{t}
simplifies as demonstrated in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
f(0)=0
,
f\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
, and
f
and its first three derivatives have polynomial growth. Define the function

G(u):=\int_{0}^{u}\rho_{2\sqrt{x}}(f^{\prime})dx,\qquad u\geq 0.

(2.9)

Then we obtain the identity

U(f)_{t}=G(L^{t})-G(L^{0})+\int_{I_{t}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x}.

(2.10)

Proof.

First of all, we note that
\rho_{u}(g)<\infty
when the function
g
has polynomial growth. Observing the semimartingale decomposition at (
2.1
), an application of Itô formula gives

F(L^{b})=F(L^{a})+\int_{a}^{b}F^{\prime}(L^{u})dL^{u}+2\int_{a}^{b}F^{\prime\prime}(L^{u})L^{u}du,

for any
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and any
b>a
. A twofold application of an integration by parts formula implies that

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(u_{2}(B_{x+1}-B_{x}))(B_{2}^{2}-2)\right]dx=u_{2}^{2}\rho_{u_{2}}(f^{\prime\prime\prime}).

According to definition (
2.9
) it holds that

G^{\prime}(u)=\rho_{2\sqrt{u}}(f^{\prime})\qquad\text{and}\qquad G^{\prime\prime}(u)=2\rho_{2\sqrt{u}}(f^{\prime\prime\prime}).

Consequently, we deduce the identity

\displaystyle\int_{I_{t}}r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}dx+\int_{I_{t}}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{t}}\rho_{\sigma_{u}}(f^{\prime})dL^{u}+\int_{I_{t}}\sigma_{u}^{2}\rho_{\sigma_{u}}(f^{\prime\prime\prime})du

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{t}}G^{\prime}(\sigma^{2}_{u}/4)dL^{u}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{I_{t}}\sigma^{2}_{u}G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma_{u}^{2}/4)du

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{t}}G^{\prime}(L^{u})dL^{u}+2\int_{I_{t}}L^{2}_{u}G^{\prime\prime}(L^{u})du=G(L^{t})-G(L^{0}).

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
2.2
and the identity
L^{x}=0
for
x\not\in[\underline{S},\overline{S}]
, we infer that

U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}=U(f)_{\underline{S}}+U(f)_{\overline{S}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x},

(2.11)

provided the function
f
satisfies the conditions outlined in Proposition
2.2
. Therefore, the stable convergence asserted in Theorem
1.2
follows from Proposition
2.2
when accompanied by a suitable approximation argument. The details of this argument will be explained in Section
3.4
.

Example 2.3
.

Here, we illustrate that Theorem
1.2
includes the results of Theorem
1.1
(i) and (ii) as specific cases.

(i) Consider the quadratic case
f(x)=x^{2}
and note the identities
\rho_{u}(f)=u^{2}
,

\rho_{u}(f^{\prime})=0
. We immediately conclude that

V(f)_{\mathbb{R}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{u}^{2}du=4\int_{\mathbb{R}}L^{u}du=4,

where the last equality follows from the occupation time formula. We also deduce that

v_{x}^{2}=2x^{4}\int_{0}^{1}\text{cov}\left(B_{1}^{2},(B_{s+1}-B_{s})^{2}\right)ds=4x^{4}\int_{0}^{1}\text{cov}\left(B_{1},B_{s+1}-B_{s}\right)^{2}ds=\frac{4}{3}x^{4},

and consequently we get
v^{2}_{\sigma_{u}}=\frac{64}{3}(L^{u})^{2}
. Thus we recover the statement
of Theorem
1.1
(i).

(ii) Now, consider the cubic setting
f(x)=x^{3}
. In this scenario we deduce the identities

\rho_{u}(f)=0
and
\rho_{u}(f^{\prime})=3u^{2}
. Consequently,
V(f)_{\mathbb{R}}=0
and
w_{u}^{2}=9u^{6}
. A straightforward computation shows that

v_{x}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}\text{\rm cov}\left(f(xB_{1}),f(x(B_{s+1}-B_{s}))\right)ds=12x^{6}.

Thus we deduce the identity
v^{2}_{\sigma_{u}}-w_{\sigma_{u}}^{2}=192(L^{u})^{3}
and we recover the statement
of Theorem
1.1
(ii). ∎

3.1
Preliminary results

To simplify our analysis, we begin by establishing stronger assumptions on the involved stochastic processes. Analogous to the reasoning provided in (
2.8
), we can perform all proofs on the set
\{\underline{S},\overline{S}\in[-T,T]\}
for some
T>0
. Provided
L^{z}>0
for all
z\in[y,x]
, we will also use the
identity

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}=\sigma_{y}+2(B_{z}-B_{y})+\int_{y}^{z}\tilde{a}_{u}\cdot(L^{u})^{-1/2}dy,

(3.1)

where
\tilde{a}
is a locally bounded process. This identity follows from
\sigma_{x}=2\sqrt{L^{x}}

and an application of the Itô formula to (
2.1
).

Additionally, given that
\tilde{a},a
and
\sigma
are locally bounded processes, we may, without loss of generality, assume that

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in\Omega,~x\in[-T,T]}\left(|\tilde{a}_{x}(\omega)|+|a_{x}(\omega)|+|\sigma_{x}(\omega)|\right)\leq C

by employing a standard localization argument (cf.
[
1
]
).

Due to the boundedness of coefficients
a
and
\sigma
we deduce from Burkholder inequality
for any
a<b
and
p>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|L^{x}-L^{a}|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}|b-a|^{p/2}.

(3.2)

Consequently, due to definition in (
1.4
), we also deduce the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{a}|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}|b-a|^{p/4}.

(3.3)

Furthermore, for any function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with polynomial growth we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)\right]\leq C,

(3.4)

which follows directly from (
3.2
).

We will often use the following lemmata, which are well known results.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the process
Y_{t}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\chi_{i}^{n}
,
t\in[0,T]
, where the random variables are
\chi_{i}^{n}
are
\mathcal{F}_{i/n}
-measurable and square integrable. Assume that

\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_{i}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nT\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[(\chi_{i}^{n})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

Then
Y^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a sequence of stochastic processes
Y^{n}
and
Y^{n,m}
. Assume that

\displaystyle Y^{n,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z^{m}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,\qquad Z^{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y\quad\text{as }m\to\infty,\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Y^{n,m}_{t}-Y^{n}_{t}|>\varepsilon\right)=0\qquad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0.

Then it holds

.

The following estimate is important for the mathematical arguments below.

Proposition 3.3
.

It holds that

\sup_{x\in[-T,T]}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{x}\in[0,\varepsilon)\right)\leq C_{T}\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{x}
be the first time the Brownian motion
W
hits the level
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
x\in(\underline{S},\overline{S})
, it must satisfy
\tau_{x}<1
.
Now, applying the strong Markov property of Brownian motion, we introduce a new process
\widetilde{W}_{t}:=W_{t+\tau_{x}}-W_{\tau_{x}}
, where
t\geq 0
. Consequently,
\widetilde{W}
is a new Brownian motion independent of
\tau_{x}
. Let
L_{t}^{x}(W)
denote the local time of
W
at point
x
up to time
t
. This leads to the relation
L_{1}^{x}(W)=L_{1-\tau_{x}}^{0}(\widetilde{W})
.
A well-known result asserts that
L_{u}^{0}(\widetilde{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|\widetilde{W}_{u}|
for any fixed
u
.
Thus, by conditioning on
\tau_{x}
, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(x\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{x}\in[0,\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{x}<1,~L^{x}\in[0,\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{x}<1,~\sqrt{1-\tau_{x}}\cdot|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|\in[0,\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{\tau_{x}<1\}}(1-\tau_{x})^{-1/2}\right].

The density of
\tau_{x}
is given by
p(u)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}|x|u^{-3/2}\exp(-x^{2}/2u)1_{\{u>0\}}
. Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\{\tau_{x}<1\}}(1-\tau_{x})^{-1/2}\right]<\infty,

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Law of large numbers

In this section we show the uniform convergence in probability as stated in (
2.4
). An application of (
2.8
) implies
the statement (
1.4
).

The basic idea of all proofs is to consider the approximation

h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\approx h^{-1/2}\sigma_{x}(B_{x+h}-B_{x}).

Observing this approximation we see that the increments

h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})
and
h^{-1/2}(L^{y+h}-L^{y})
are asymptotically correlated when
|x-y|<h
.
To break this dependence we use a classical blocking technique. For
i\geq 0
we introduce the sets

\displaystyle A_{i}(m)

\displaystyle=[i(m+1)h,i(m+1)h+mh],

\displaystyle B_{i}(m)

\displaystyle=[i(m+1)h+mh,i(m+1)h+(m+1)h].

Note that the length of
A_{i}(m)
is
mh
(big block) while
B_{i}(m)
has the length
h
(small block).
In the first step we obtain the following decomposition:

\displaystyle V(f)^{h}_{t}=Z_{t}^{h,m}(f)+R_{t}^{h,m}(f)+D_{t}^{h,m}(f),

where

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)dx,

\displaystyle R_{t}^{h,m}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\int_{B_{i}(m)}f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)dx,

and
D_{t}^{h,m}(f)
comprises the edge terms and satisfies

D^{h,m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0

(3.5)

due to (
3.4
) and the polynomial growth of
f
. Next, we will analyse the asymptotic
behaviour of the processes
Z^{h,m}(f)
and
R^{h,m}(f)
.

(a)
Negligibility of
R_{t}^{h,m}(f)
:
First, we observe the inequality

\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|R_{t}^{h,m}(f)|\leq R_{T}^{h,m}(|f|)+R_{-T}^{h,m}(|f|)

Since
f
has polynomial growth we deduce that
|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{p})
for some

p>0
. Due to inequality (
3.2
) we get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}^{h,m}(|f|)+R_{-T}^{h,m}(|f|)\right]\leq Cm^{-1}.

Thus, we conclude that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|R_{t}^{h,m}(f)|>\varepsilon\right)=0,

(3.6)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. This proves the negligibility of the term
R^{h,m}(f)
. ∎

(b)
Law of large numbers for the approximation:
We introduce the following approximation
of the statistic
Z_{t}^{h,m}(f)
:

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}^{h,m}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\alpha_{i}^{h}(m),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)

\displaystyle:=\int_{A_{i}(m)}f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)dx,

where
t_{i}^{h}(m)=i(m+1)h
is the left boundary of the interval
A_{i}(m)
. Due to Riemann integrability
we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]=mh\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{m}{m+1}V(f)_{t}

as
h\to 0
and
m/(m+1)V(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}V(f)
as
m\to\infty
. By Lemma
3.1
it suffices to prove that

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:\leq i(m+1)h+mh\in[-T,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)\right|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\text{as }h\to 0.

By (
3.4
) we readily deduce that

\mathbb{E}[|\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\leq C(mh)^{2}.

Hence, we conclude

\overline{Z}^{h,m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{m}{m+1}V(f)\text{ as }h\to 0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{m}{m+1}V(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}V(f)\text{ as }m\to\infty.

(3.8)

∎

(c) In view of steps (a) and (b) we are left to proving the statement

\displaystyle\overline{Z}^{h,m}(f)-Z^{h,m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(3.9)

Since
f
has polynomial growth
we have the following inequality for
\varepsilon,A>0
:

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C\left(w_{f}(A,\varepsilon)+(1+|x|^{p}+|y|^{p})(1_{\{|x|>A\}}+1_{\{|y|>A\}}+1_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}})\right),

where
w_{f}(A,\varepsilon):=\sup\{|f(x)-f(y)|:~|x|,|y|\leq A,~|x-y|\leq\varepsilon\}
is the modulus
of continuity of
f
. Using this inequality and (
3.2
), and also
1_{\{|x|>A\}}\leq A^{-1}|x|
,

1_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}|x-y|
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\left|\overline{Z}_{t}^{h,m}(f)-Z_{t}^{h,m}(f)\right|\right]\leq C\Big(w_{f}(A,\varepsilon)+A^{-1}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in[-T,T]}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left(h^{1/2}+h^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{x}^{x+h}|\sigma_{u}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}|^{2}du\right]^{1/2}\right)dx.

Since
\sigma
is continuous and bounded we see that the third term converges to
0

as
h\to 0
. On the other hand, we have that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}w_{f}(A,\varepsilon)=0

for a any fixed
A
. Hence, we deduce that

\overline{Z}^{h,m}(f)-Z^{h,m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

by letting first
h\to 0
, then
\varepsilon\to 0
and
A\to\infty
.
Due to statements (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), we obtain the convergence in (
2.4
). ∎

3.3
Stable central limit theorem

Demonstrating the stable central limit theorem as stated in Theorem
2.1
poses a more intricate challenge. Our approach is primarily based upon limit theorems for semimartingales, notably in works such as
[
1
]
. It is crucial to highlight that the diffusion coefficient
\sigma_{x}=2\sqrt{L^{x}}
is not a semimartingale, introducing a heightened level of complexity to the proofs. We will continue to employ the blocking technique introduced in the preceding section.

First of all, we decompose our statistic into several terms:

U(f)^{h}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}Z^{h,m,k}(f)+\sum_{k=1}^{3}R^{h,m,k}(f)+\overline{D}^{h,m}.

(3.10)

Here the processes
Z^{h,m,k}(f)
,
k=1,2,3
, are big blocks approximations, which
are defined by

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,1}(f):=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\left(\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)-\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\right),

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,2}(f):=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\left(\int_{A_{i}(m)}f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)dx-\alpha_{i}^{h}(m)\right),

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3}(f):=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left(\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)\right)dx.

The small block processes
R^{h,m,k}(f)
,
k=1,2,3
, are introduced in exactly the same way with
the set
A_{i}(m)
being replaced by
B_{i}(m)
in all relevant definitions. Finally, the process

\overline{D}^{h,m}
comprises all the edge terms. Similarly to the treatment of the term
D^{h,m}

in (
3.5
), we immediately conclude that

\overline{D}^{h,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

(3.11)

In the following subsections we will show that all small blocks terms are negligible in the sense

\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|R_{t}^{h,m,k}(f)|>\varepsilon\right)=0\qquad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0,

(3.12)

for all
k=1,2,3
. Finally, we will show that

Z^{h,m,1}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime m}(f),\qquad Z^{h,m,2}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime\prime m}(f),\qquad Z^{h,m,3}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

as
h\to 0
, and moreover

\displaystyle U^{\prime m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime m}(f)=\int_{0}^{\cdot}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x},

(3.13)

\displaystyle U^{\prime\prime m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime\prime}(f)=\int_{0}^{\cdot}r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}dx

as
m\to\infty
. Consequently, due to (
3.11
)-(
3.13
), an application of Lemma
3.2
and properties of stable
convergence imply the statement of Theorem
2.1
.

3.3.1
Central limit theorem for the approximation

Recalling the notation from the previous subsection,
we set

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,1}(f)

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}X_{i}^{h}(m).

We now prove the stable central limit theorem for
Z^{h,m,1}(f)
as
h\to 0
.
According to Theorem
[
8
, Theorem IX.7.28]
we need to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{t}v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}(m)dx

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{h}(m)(B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)+(m+1)h}-B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}c_{m}\int_{0}^{t}w_{\sigma_{x}}dx

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}1_{\{|X_{i}^{h}(m)|>\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\forall\epsilon>0

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{h}(m)(N_{t_{i}^{h}(m)+(m+1)h}-N_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

(3.17)

where the last statement should hold for all bounded continuous martingales
N
with
\langle B,N\rangle=0
,
c_{m}=m/(m+1)
, and the function

v_{u}(m)
will be introduced below.

We start by showing the condition (
3.14
). A straightforward
computation using the substitution
x=hz_{1},y=hz_{2}
shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]=h^{-1}\int_{A_{i}^{2}(m)}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\times f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{y+h}-B_{y})\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}^{2}(f)\right)1_{\{|x-y|<h\}}dxdy

\displaystyle=h\int_{[i(m+1),i(m+1)+m]^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{z_{1}+1}-B_{z_{1}})\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\times f\left(\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{z_{2}+1}-B_{z_{2}})\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}^{2}(f)\right)1_{\{|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1\}}dz_{1}dz_{2}.

Hence, by Riemann integrability we deduce that

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{t}v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}(m)dx,

where

\displaystyle v_{u}^{2}(m)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m}\int_{[0,m]^{2}}\text{cov}\left(f\left(u(B_{z_{1}+1}-B_{z_{1}})\right),f\left(u(B_{z_{2}+1}-B_{z_{2}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times 1_{\{|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1\}}dz_{1}dz_{2}.

We note that

\lim_{m\to\infty}v_{u}^{2}(m)=v_{u}^{2},

(3.18)

where
v_{u}^{2}
has been introduced in (
1.6
).

In the next step we show condition (
3.15
). By the integration by parts formula
we deduce the identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{h}(m)(B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)+(m+1)h}-B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}]

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)h^{-1/2}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]dx

\displaystyle=(mh)^{-1}w_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}

where the function
w_{u}
has been defined in (
2.6
). This implies condition
(
3.15
) by Riemann integrability.

To show condition (
3.16
), we observe the inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{2}1_{\{|X_{i}^{h}(m)|>\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]\leq\epsilon^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{i}^{h}(m)|^{4}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]\leq C\epsilon^{-2}m^{4}h^{2}.

Hence, we deduce the statement of (
3.16
).

To prove condition (
3.17
), we apply a martingale representation theorem to deduce the representation

X_{i}^{h}(m)=\int_{A_{i}(m)}\eta_{i,x}^{h,m}dB_{x},

where
\eta_{i}^{h,m}
is a predictable square integrable process. Now, applying Itô isometry, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{h}(m)(N_{t_{i}^{h}(m)+(m+1)h}-N_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{A_{i}(m)}\eta_{i,x}^{h,m}d\langle B,N\rangle_{x}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]=0

Consequently, we showed condition (
3.17
).

Now, due to (
3.14
)-(
3.17
), we conclude the stable convergence
Z^{h,m,1}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime m}(f)
as
h\to 0
with

U^{\prime m}(f)_{t}:=c_{m}\int_{I_{t}}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}+\int_{I_{t}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}(m)-c_{m}^{2}w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x}.

On the other hand, since
c_{m}\to 1
and
v_{u}^{2}(m)\to v_{u}^{2}
as
m\to\infty
, we obtain that

U^{\prime m}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U^{\prime}(f)=\int_{I_{\cdot}}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}+\int_{I_{\cdot}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x}

(3.19)

as
m\to\infty
.

3.3.2
Negligibility of the small blocks: the martingale term

Here we show that the small block term
R^{h,m,1}(f)
is negligible. We
recall

\displaystyle R_{t}^{h,m,1}(f)=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\left(\beta_{i}^{h}(m)-\mathbb{E}[\beta_{i}^{h}(m)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)+mh}]\right),

where

\beta_{i}^{h}(m):=\int_{B_{i}(m)}f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)dx.

Since
R^{h,m,1}(f)
is a martingale,
f
has polynomial growth and
\sigma

is bounded, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\left[|R_{T}^{h,m,1}(f)|^{2}+|R_{-T}^{h,m,1}(f)|^{2}\right]\leq Cm^{-1}.

Hence, by Lemma
3.1
we obtain that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|R_{t}^{h,m,1}(f)|>\varepsilon\right)=0\qquad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0.

3.3.3
Riemann sum approximation error

We now consider the Riemann sum approximation error
associated with big blocks. We need to show that

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3}(f)=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left(\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\right)dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(The corresponding statement for the small block term
R^{h,m,3}(f)
is shown in exactly the same way). For this purpose we introduce the threshold

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{h}=h^{r}\qquad\text{for some }r\in(1/4,1/2).

(3.20)

On each big block
A_{i}(m)
, we will distinguish two cases according to whether

L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}
or
L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}
.

We start with the first case. Since
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})

the map
u\mapsto\rho_{u}(f)
is
C^{1}
. Also note that
\sup_{u\in A}|\rho^{\prime}_{u}(f)|
is bounded if
A

is a compact set. Due to mean value theorem and boundedness of
\sigma
we have

\displaystyle 1_{\left\{t_{i}^{h}(m)\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left|\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq C1_{\left\{t_{i}^{h}(m)\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left|\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right|dx

Now, we use Proposition
3.3
, inequality (
3.3
)
as well as Hölder inequality with conjugates
p,q>1
,
1/p+1/q=1
, to deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\left\{t_{i}^{h}(m)\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\left|\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right|\right]\leq Ch^{1/4}\varepsilon_{h}^{1/q}.

Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle h^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in[-T,T]}1_{\left\{t_{i}^{h}(m)\in(\underline{S},\overline{S}),~L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left|\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\right|dx\right]

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1/4}\varepsilon_{h}^{1/q}\to 0,

(3.21)

where we use the definition at (
3.20
) and choose
q
close enough to
1
.

Now, we treat the case
L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}
. For a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude by Borel–Cantelli lemma
and
\varepsilon_{h}=h^{r}
for
r<1/2

that there exists a
h_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely

\sup_{x\in A_{i}(m)}|L^{x}-L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}|\leq\varepsilon_{h}/2
for any
h<h_{0}
. In the scenario

L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}
the latter implies that

\inf_{x\in A_{i}(m)}|L^{x}|\geq\varepsilon_{h}/2\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-almost surely},

(3.22)

for
h<h_{0}
.
We introduce the following process:

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3.1}:=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\left\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\left(\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\right)dx.

To handle the process
Z^{h,m,3.1}
we will apply the decomposition (
3.1
).
First of all, we use the mean value theorem to deduce that

\rho_{\sigma_{x}}(f)-\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)=\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)(\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})+(\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{x_{i}^{h}}}(f)-\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f))(\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}),

where
x_{i}^{h}
is a certain point in the interval
(t_{i}^{h}(m),x)
. Now, applying (
3.1
), we decompose
Z^{h,m,3.1}=Z^{h,m,3.2}+Z^{h,m,3.3}+Z^{h,m,3.4}
as

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3.2}

\displaystyle:=2h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\left\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\left(B_{x}-B_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}\right)dx,

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3.3}

\displaystyle:=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\left\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\left(\int_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}^{x}\tilde{a}_{y}\cdot(L^{y})^{-1/2}dy\right)dx,

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{h,m,3.4}

\displaystyle:=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\left\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\right\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}(\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{x_{i}^{h}}}(f)-\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f))(\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})dx.

Since
Z^{h,m,3.2}
is a martingale, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z^{h,m,3.2}_{T}|^{2}+|Z^{h,m,3.2}_{-T}|^{2}\right]\leq C_{T}h.

By Lemma
3.1
we deduce that

Z^{h,m,3.2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

(3.23)

Due to (
3.22
) we know that

(L^{x})^{-1/2}<2\varepsilon_{h}^{-1/2}
for all
x\in A_{i}(m)
and
h<h_{0}
. Since

\sigma,\tilde{a}
are bounded, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|Z_{t}^{h,m,3.3}|\right]\leq C_{T}h^{1/2}\varepsilon_{h}^{-1/2}\to 0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

(3.24)

To handle the last term
Z^{h,m,3.4}
we apply a similar technique as in step (c) of Section
3.2
. Notice that the quantity
\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{x}}(f)
is bounded, because

\sigma
is bounded. We obtain that

\left|\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{x_{i}^{h}}}(f)-\rho^{\prime}_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f)\right|\leq C\left(w_{\rho^{\prime}(f)}(A,\epsilon)+1_{\{|\sigma_{x_{i}^{h}}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}|>\epsilon\}}\right).

Since
L^{x}\geq\varepsilon_{h}/2
for all
x\in A_{i}(m)
and
h<h_{0}
, we deduce from representation
(
3.1
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}|^{p}\right]\leq C\left(h^{p/2}+h^{p}\varepsilon_{h}^{-p/2}\right)

for any
p>0
, for all
x\in A_{i}(m)
and
h<h_{0}
.
Hence, we now obtain from (
3.3
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|Z_{t}^{h,m,3.4}|\right]\leq C\left(w_{\rho^{\prime}(f)}(A,\varepsilon)\left(1+h^{1/2}\varepsilon_{h}^{-1/2}\right)+\epsilon^{-3}h^{1/2}\right)

Thus, we conclude that

Z^{h,m,3.4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

(3.25)

A combination of (
3.21
) and (
3.23
)-(
3.25
) implies the statement

Z^{h,m,3}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to\infty.

Similarly,
R^{h,m,3}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
as
h\to 0
.

3.3.4
The terms
Z^{h,m,2}(f)
and
R^{h,m,2}(f)

In view of the previous steps, we are left with handling the terms
Z^{h,m,2}(f)
and
R^{h,m,2}(f)
.
We start with the term
Z^{h,m,2}(f)
. First, we consider an approximation of

Z^{h,m,2}(f)
given as

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}^{h,m,2}(f):=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]dx.

Applying Lemma
3.1
and following the same arguments as presented in part (c) of Section
3.2

(see the proof of (
3.9
)),
we deduce that

\overline{Z}^{h,m,2}(f)-Z^{h,m,2}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

(3.26)

We use again the mean value theorem to obtain the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})\right)-f\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[h^{-1/2}f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\left((L^{x+h}-L^{x})-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[h^{-1/2}\left(f^{\prime}(z_{i}^{h})-f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left((L^{x+h}-L^{x})-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right],

where
z_{i}^{h}
is a point between
h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})
and

h^{-1/2}(L^{x+h}-L^{x})
. As in the previous subsection we need to discuss the cases

L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}
and

L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}
separately. The easier case

L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}<\varepsilon_{h}
is handled in exactly the same way as presented in (
3.21
), so we focus
on the scenario
L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}
. Similarly to the treatment of
Z^{h,m,3.4}
we conclude that

\displaystyle h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh<t}1_{\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[h^{-1/2}\left(f^{\prime}(z_{i}^{h})-f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left((L^{x+h}-L^{x})-\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\qquad\text{as }h\to\infty.

Thus, we need to show that

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}^{h,m,2.1}

\displaystyle:=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[h^{-1/2}f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(\int_{x}^{x+h}(a_{u}-a_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})du+\int_{x}^{x+h}\left(\int_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}^{u}\tilde{a}_{s}\cdot(L^{s})^{-1/2}ds\right)dB_{u}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}^{h,m,2.2}

\displaystyle:=h^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}:~i(m+1)h+mh\in I_{t}}1_{\{L^{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\geq\varepsilon_{h}\}}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[h^{-1/2}f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(ha_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}+2\int_{x}^{x+h}(B_{u}-B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})dB_{u}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{m}{m+1}\int_{I_{t}}r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}dx,

(3.27)

as
h\to 0
. The statement
\overline{Z}^{h,m,2.1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u.c.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
is obtained along the lines of the arguments presented in the previous
subsection. Finally, observe the identities

\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)a_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\right]=a_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}\rho_{\sigma_{t_{i}^{h}(m)}}(f^{\prime})

and

\displaystyle 2h^{-1}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}u(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\int_{x}^{x+h}(B_{u}-B_{t_{i}^{h}(m)})dB_{u}\right]dx

\displaystyle=2h^{-1}\int_{A_{i}(m)}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(h^{-1/2}u(B_{x+h}-B_{x})\right)\int_{x}^{x+h}(B_{u}-B_{x})dB_{u}\right]dx

\displaystyle=2mh\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(u(B_{y+1}-B_{y})\right)\int_{y}^{y+1}(B_{u}-B_{y})dB_{u}\right]dy

\displaystyle=2mh\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(u(B_{y+1}-B_{y})\right)\int_{0}^{2}B_{u}dB_{u}\right]dy

\displaystyle=mh\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\left(u(B_{y+1}-B_{y})\right)(B_{2}^{2}-2)\right]dy,

where we used the substitution
x=hy
and the self-similarity of the Brownian motion. Hence,
the convergence in (
3.27
) follows from Riemann integrability.

Following exactly the same arguments we conclude that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}|R_{t}^{h,m,2}(f)|>\varepsilon\right)=0\qquad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0.

This completes the proof of stable convergence
U(f)^{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)
.

3.4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Here we prove the statements of Theorem
1.2
via an application of Theorem
2.1

and Proposition
2.2
. Recall that we have already shown the convergence at (
1.4
); see (
2.8
). Thus we are left to proving the stable central limit theorem presented in
Theorem
1.2
.

We recall that it suffices to show all convergence results under the restriction
\underline{S},\overline{S}\in[-T,T]
for some
T>0
. Theorem
2.1
states that
U(f)^{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)
on
(C([-T,T]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.
Since the mapping
F:[-T,T]^{2}\times C([-T,T])\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
F((t_{1},t_{2}),H):=H(t_{1})+H(t_{2})

is continuous, we deduce by the properties of stable convergence and (
2.7
):

\displaystyle U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}^{h}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)_{\underline{S}}+U(f)_{\overline{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{\sigma_{x}}dB_{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{v_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}-w_{\sigma_{x}}^{2}}dW^{\prime}_{x},

under conditions of Theorem
2.1
. Our task now boils down to demonstrating that the sum of the first two terms in the limit are equal to zero. This assertion has already been established in (
2.11
) under the condition
f(0)=0
, with
f\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
, and
f
and its first three derivatives exhibiting polynomial growth. Therefore, our focus shifts to confirming that this statement carries over under the weaker
assumptions of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be an arbitrary function satisfying the conditions of Theorem
1.2
. Then
there exists a sequence of functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
that fulfils the conditions

f_{n}(0)=0
,

|f_{n}(x)|+|f^{\prime}_{n}(x)|+|f^{\prime\prime}_{n}(x)|+|f^{\prime\prime\prime}_{n}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{p})\qquad\text{for some }p>0,

and

\sup_{x\in A}\left(|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|+|f^{\prime}_{n}(x)-f^{\prime}(x)|\right)\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to 0,

(3.28)

for any compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
. In view of Lemma
3.2
it suffices to show that

\displaystyle U(f_{n})_{\mathbb{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,\qquad\text{and}

(3.29)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|U(f_{n})_{\mathbb{R}}^{h}-U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}^{h}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0\qquad\text{for any }\varepsilon>0.

(3.30)

We start by proving the statement (
3.29
). For this purpose we introduce the notation

r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}(f)
,
w_{\sigma_{x}}(f)
and
v_{\sigma_{x}}(f)
to explicitly denote the dependence of these quantities on the function
f
. Since
\underline{S},\overline{S}\in[-T,T]
it suffices to prove the convergence

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{-T}^{T}|r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}(f_{n})-r_{a_{x},\sigma_{x}}(f)|+|w_{\sigma_{x}}(f_{n})-w_{\sigma_{x}}(f)|+|v_{\sigma_{x}}(f_{n})-v_{\sigma_{x}}(f)|dx\right]\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, to conclude (
3.29
). But the latter follows directly from (
3.28
)
since the processes
a
and
\sigma
are bounded.

Now, we show condition (
3.30
). Applying Theorem
2.1
we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|U(f_{n})_{\mathbb{R}}^{h}-U(f)_{\mathbb{R}}^{h}\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\left|U(f_{n})_{t}^{h}-U(f)_{t}^{h}\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\left|U(f_{n})_{t}-U(f)_{t}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

Using Markov and Burkholder inequalities, and the same arguments as in the proof of (
3.29
), we
obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[-T,T]}\left|U(f_{n})_{t}-U(f)_{t}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Thus we deduce (
3.30
), which completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
