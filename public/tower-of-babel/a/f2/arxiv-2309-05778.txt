Energy matching in reduced passive and port-Hamiltonian systems
Tobias Holicki ⋆ and Jonas Nicodemus † and Paul Schwerdtner ‡ and Benjamin Unger †

Abstract
. It is well known that any port-Hamiltonian (pH) system is passive, and conversely, any minimal and stable passive system has a pH representation. Nevertheless, this equivalence is only concerned with the input-output mapping but not with the Hamiltonian itself. Thus, we propose to view a pH system either as an enlarged dynamical system with the Hamiltonian as additional output or as two dynamical systems with the input-output and the Hamiltonian dynamic. Our first main result is a structure-preserving Kalman-like decomposition of the enlarged pH system that separates the controllable and zero-state observable parts. Moreover, for further approximations in the context of structure-preserving model-order reduction (MOR), we propose to search for a Hamiltonian in the reduced pH system that minimizes the ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} -distance to the full-order Hamiltonian without altering the input-output dynamic, thus discussing a particular aspect of the corresponding multi-objective minimization problem corresponding to ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} -optimal MOR for pH systems. We show that this optimization problem is uniquely solvable, can be recast as a standard semidefinite program, and present two numerical approaches for solving it. The results are illustrated with three academic examples.



Keywords:
Port-Hamiltonian systems, structure-preserving model-order reduction, energy matching, quadratic output system,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-optimal, semidefinite program

1.
Introduction

The
port-Hamiltonian
(
pH
) modeling paradigm offers an intuitive energy-based formulation of dynamical systems across a wide variety of physical domains such as electrical systems
[
24
,
29
,
30
]
, fluid-flow problems
[
4
]
, or mechanical multi-body systems
[
10
, Ex. 12]
. By design,
pH
systems are automatically stable and passive and can be coupled across different scales and physical domains, which makes them valuable building blocks for large network models
[
35
]
.
The
pH
framework is particularly appealing due to its inherent
Hamiltonian
structure. The Hamiltonian function, which represents the system’s total energy, provides a powerful tool for understanding and analyzing the system’s behavior.
Since first-principle
full-order models
(
FOMs
) of complex systems or large system networks often have a high state-space dimension,
model order reduction
(
MOR
) is necessary in many cases to enable efficient numerical simulations or even real-time model-based control by computing a
reduced-order model
(
ROM
) that is used instead.
The current state-of-the-art system-theoretic
MOR
methods for
pH
systems aim to preserve the input-output mapping of the system. However, in the context of
pH
systems, not only the input-output mapping is of relevance but also the approximation of the Hamiltonian. For instance, preserving the Hamiltonian during
MOR
is crucial for many applications, such as energy-aware control synthesis
[
18
,
45
]
.

In this article, we thus aim for a
MOR
method that approximates the input-output mapping and, simultaneously, the Hamiltonian.
To this end, we offer a new perspective on the
MOR
problem for
linear time-invariant
(
LTI
)
pH
systems. In particular, we argue that
pH
systems should not be treated merely as a special case of standard
LTI
systems during
MOR
but instead propose to view
pH
systems as two dynamical (respectively an extended) dynamical systems consisting of the classical input-output mapping and, additionally, a dynamical system with a quadratic output representing the evolution of the Hamiltonian.

Exploiting the
Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov
(
KYP
) inequality, see the forthcoming
Section

2.2
, we propose a novel post-processing step called
energy matching
— to be performed after any structure-preserving
MOR
method — for the
ROM
such that the approximation error of the Hamiltonian dynamic is minimized, without changing the system’s input-output dynamic. In more detail, we exploit the non-uniqueness of the
pH
formulation to replace the Hessian of the Hamiltonian with any other positive-definite solution of the
KYP
inequality without altering the input-output mapping. This allows us to formulate an optimization problem that minimizes the Hamiltonian approximation error.

Our main contributions, centered around the novel definition of the extended
pH
system in
Section

3
, are the following:

(i)

As a natural first step towards system theoretical
MOR
for this class of dynamical systems, we derive a structure-preserving Kalman-like decomposition in
Section

4
, which can be used as an efficient pre-computation step before applying any
MOR
methods.

(ii)

Finally, we provide the new
energy matching
post-processing algorithm in
Section

5
, which can be applied after any classical structure preserving
MOR
method to minimize the error of the Hamiltonian approximation.

We demonstrate the efficiency of the method using three numerical examples in
Section

6
.

1.1.
Literature review and state-of-the-art

MOR
for standard
LTI
systems of the form

(1.1)

\Sigma\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t)&=Ax(t)+Bu(t),\\
y(t)&=Cx(t)+Du(t),\end{aligned}\right.

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
, and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
, is well understood. There exist several well-established algorithms that compute
ROMs
of the form

(1.2)

\tilde{\Sigma}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{\tilde{x}}(t)&=\tilde{A}\tilde{x}(t)+\tilde{B}u(t),\\
\tilde{y}(t)&=\tilde{C}\tilde{x}(t)+\tilde{D}u(t),\end{aligned}\right.

with matrices
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\tilde{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
,
\tilde{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
, and
\tilde{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
that approximate the
FOM
with high fidelity. One standard input-output error measure is the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-error (cf.
[
33
, Sec. 7.2]
and the references therein)

(1.3)

\|\Sigma-\tilde{\Sigma}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|H(\mathrm{i}\omega)-\tilde{H}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\,\mathrm{d}\omega},

that measures the deviation of the
ROM
transfer function
\tilde{H}
from the
FOM
transfer function
H
. These transfer functions are defined as

H(s)\vcentcolon=C{(sI_{n}-A)}^{-1}B+D\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{H}(s)\vcentcolon=\tilde{C}{(sI_{r}-\tilde{A})}^{-1}\tilde{B}+\tilde{D}.

Moreover, we have that
\|y-\tilde{y}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\leq\|H-\tilde{H}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
(again cf.
[
33
, Sec. 7.2]
), which ensures that a small
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-error leads to a good approximation of the input-output map in the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
-norm.

A comprehensive review of the classical
MOR
methods is beyond the scope of this paper, and we refer to
[
5
,
6
,
11
]
for an overview of this topic. We mention that many of these methods employ a projection framework, i.e., they compute a
ROMs
based on a subspace projection onto the
r
-dimensional subspaces
\range(V)
and
\range(W)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
encoded via the matrices
V,W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
with
W^{\mathsf{T}}V=I
, i.e., the
ROM
matrices are defined as
\tilde{A}=W^{\mathsf{T}}AV
,
\tilde{B}=W^{\mathsf{T}}B
,
\tilde{C}=CV
, and
\tilde{D}=D
.
However, many of the standard methods, such as the
iterative rational Krylov algorithm
(
IRKA
), balanced truncation, and proper orthogonal decomposition, have no guarantee to preserve the
pH
structure. Instead, specialized methods for
pH
systems can be employed. These
MOR
methods can roughly be divided into two main categories. Methods in the first category aim at a good approximation of the state, which should also yield a good approximation of the input-output map. Popular examples rely on symplectic model reduction
[
40
,
1
,
2
,
17
]
and proper orthogonal decomposition with compatibility conditions
[
23
]
.
Naturally, if methods from this category approximate the state sufficiently well, then they also provide a good approximation of the Hamiltonian. However, from a system-theoretic perspective, these methods may approximate parts of the state that are irrelevant to the input-output mapping. In contrast, methods in the second category directly approximate the input-output mapping of the dynamical system. Prominent examples are the structure-preserving variant of
IRKA

[
28
]
, optimization algorithms that aim at minimizing the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
- or
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-norms, cf.
[
48
,
38
,
49
,
37
]
, and balancing methods such as
[
15
,
13
]
. Moreover, exploiting the equivalence between
pH
systems and passive systems (see
[
20
,
46
]
for a thorough investigation) enables passivity preserving methods such as
positive-real balanced truncation
(
PRBT
)
[
22
,
47
]
and spectral factorization
[
16
]
. However, for the recovery of the
pH
system, it needs to be clarified how to choose the Hamiltonian, which is what motivated this paper in the first place. Since our main focus is on the approximation of the input-output mapping and the Hamiltonian but not on the state, we focus here on methods from the second category, albeit our energy matching post-processing can also be applied to methods from the first category.

MOR
methods for linear systems are evaluated based on their approximation of the input-output mapping, which can be assessed using well-established error measures (such as the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm) based on the transfer function distances. The evaluation of the approximation quality of the Hamiltonian requires a more advanced error analysis that has only recently been established. When we add the Hamiltonian as an additional output, an
LTI

pH
system becomes a
linear time-invariant system with quadratic output
(
LTIQO
).
MOR
for such systems is considered, e.g., in
[
52
,
51
]
, in which single output
LTIQO
systems are simplified to standard
LTI
systems with multiple outputs such that either balancing or Krylov-based
MOR
methods can be applied. In
[
44
]
,
LTIQO
systems are rewritten as quadratic-bilinear (
QB
) systems that are subsequently reduced via balanced truncation.

Our approach for approximating the Hamiltonian is based on developments in
[
12
]
, in which the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
error measure is extended to
LTIQO
systems. Moreover, in
[
12
]
, energy functionals and Gramians are introduced for
LTIQO
systems such that balanced truncation can be applied directly. Finally, in
[
26
]
, an iterative structure preserving
MOR
algorithm is presented based on solving two Sylvester equations and in
[
27
]
the
Adaptive Antoulas-Anderson
(
AAA
) algorithm is extended to
LTIQO
to develop a data-driven modeling framework. However, to our knowledge, there are no structure-preserving variants of the mentioned methods for
LTIQO
systems.

2.
Preliminaries

We first recall a few basic notions from
LTI
systems and
pH
systems, that we will later use for our developments in
Section

3
.

2.1.
Controllability and Observability

An
LTI
system such as (
1.1
) is called controllable or observable if the corresponding controllability and observability matrices have full row and column rank, respectively, i.e.,

\rank\begin{bmatrix}B&AB&\cdots&A^{n-1}B\end{bmatrix}=n\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rank\begin{bmatrix}C&A^{\mathsf{T}}C&\cdots&{\big(A^{\mathsf{T}}\big)}^{n-1}C\end{bmatrix}=n.

The system (
1.1
) is called minimal if it is controllable and observable. Controllability and observability are closely related to the (infinite) Gramians

(2.1)

\mathcal{P}\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(A\tau)BB^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(A^{\mathsf{T}}\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{O}\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(A^{\mathsf{T}}\tau)CC^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(A\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

which exist if the dynamical system (
1.1
) is asymptotically stable, i.e., if all eigenvalues of
A
are in the open left-half plane. In this case, the Gramians can be computed as solutions of the Lyapunov equations

(2.2a)

\displaystyle A\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}A^{\mathsf{T}}+BB^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.2b)

\displaystyle A^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{O}A+C^{\mathsf{T}}C

\displaystyle=0,

respectively, and we have that
\Sigma
is controllable if and only if
\rank(\mathcal{P})=n
, and observable if and only if
\rank(\mathcal{O})=n
.

2.2.
Port-Hamiltonian systems and the Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov inequality

We consider
LTI

pH
systems defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Port-Hamiltonian system
[
53
]
)
.

An
LTI
system of the form

(2.3a)

\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t)&=(J-R)Qx(t)+(G-P)u(t),\\
y(t)&={(G+P)}^{\mathsf{T}}Qx(t)+(S-N)u(t),\end{aligned}\right.

with matrices
J,R,Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
G,P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
S,N\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
together with a
Hamiltonian
function

(2.3b)

\mathcal{H}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad x\mapsto\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}Qx,

is called a
port-Hamiltonian
system, if

(i)

the structure matrix

\Gamma\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}J&G\\
-\smash{G^{\mathsf{T}}}&N\end{bmatrix}

is skew-symmetric,

(ii)

the dissipation matrix

W\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}R&P\\
P^{\mathsf{T}}&S\end{bmatrix}

is symmetric positive semi-definite, and

(iii)

the Hessian of the Hamiltonian
Q
is symmetric positive semi-definite.

The variables
x
,
u
, and
y
are referred to as the
state
,
input
, and
output
, respectively.

For such systems, structure-preserving
MOR
computes
pH

ROMs

(2.4)

\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{\tilde{x}}(t)&=(\tilde{J}-\tilde{R})\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)+(\tilde{G}-\tilde{P})u(t),\\
\tilde{y}(t)&={(\tilde{G}+\tilde{P})}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)+(\tilde{S}-\tilde{N})u(t),\end{aligned}\right.

with matrices
\tilde{J},\tilde{R},\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\tilde{G},\tilde{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
,
\tilde{S},\tilde{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, that satisfy the same constraints as in
Definition

2.1
but with
r\ll n
. Typically,
MOR
(and also structure-preserving
MOR
) aims to compute
ROMs
such that
y-\tilde{y}
is small for all admissible inputs
u
in an appropriate norm (which results in a good approximation of the input-output mapping). The approximation of the Hamiltonian, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}-\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
in some appropriate norm is typically not considered; here
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the Hamiltonian of the reduced system (
2.4
), given by

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\tilde{x}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\tilde{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}.

We say that a general
LTI
system as in (
1.1
) has a
pH
representation whenever we can factorize the system matrices in the form of (
2.3a
) with the properties given in
Definition

2.1
. While the specific matrices of a
pH
system are typically obtained during the modeling process, the factorization of the system matrices is generally not unique. Indeed, it is easily seen that a
pH
system is passive, and vice versa, any stable and minimal passive system has a
pH
representation; see for instance
[
9
]
. If
\Sigma
in (
1.1
) is passive, then a
pH
representation can be obtained via a symmetric positive-definite solution
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
of the
KYP
inequality

(2.5)

\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}(X)\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succcurlyeq}

with

\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)},\qquad X\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\mathsf{T}}X-XA&C^{\mathsf{T}}-XB\\
C-B^{\mathsf{T}}X&D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.

In more detail, defining the set
\mathbb{X}_{\Sigma}\vcentcolon=\{X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}\mid\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}(X)\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succcurlyeq}\}
,
it is easy to verify that for a passive
LTI
system (
1.1
), any
X\in\mathbb{X}_{\Sigma}
of (
2.5
) yields a
pH
representation by setting

(2.6)

\displaystyle Q\vcentcolon=X,\quad J\vcentcolon=\Skew(AX^{-1}),\quad R\vcentcolon=-\Sym(AX^{-1})

(2.7)

\displaystyle G\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}(X^{-1}C^{\mathsf{T}}+B),\quad P\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}(X^{-1}C^{\mathsf{T}}-B),\quad S\vcentcolon=\Sym(D),\quad N\vcentcolon=\Skew(D).

Note that we have

(2.8)

(J-R)Q=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(AX^{-1}-X^{-1}A^{\mathsf{T}}+AX^{-1}+X^{-1}A^{\mathsf{T}}\right)X=A,

and similarly for the other matrices. Hence, the
pH
representation does not affect the state-space description (
1.1
), but is merely a special decomposition of the system matrices.
For our forthcoming analysis, we gather several results from the literature
[
31
,
19
,
55
]
about the
KYP
inequality (
2.5
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the dynamical system
\Sigma
in (
1.1
) and the associated
KYP
inequality (
2.5
).

(i)

If the dynamical system is asymptotically stable, i.e., the eigenvalues of
A
are in the open left half plane, then any solution
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of (

) is symmetric positive semi-definite.

(ii)

If the dynamical system is observable, then any solution
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succcurlyeq}
of (

(iii)

Suppose the dynamical system is minimal and asymptotically stable. Then there exist matrices
X_{\min},X_{\max}\in\mathbb{X}_{\Sigma}
such that any

satisfies

In particular, the set

is bounded.

Let

be a solution of (

). Then there exists a matrix

such that

-A^{\mathsf{T}}X-XA^{\mathsf{T}}=M.

The result is thus an immediate consequence of
[
31
, Cha. 12.3, Thm. 3]
.

(ii)

See
[

, Prop. 1]
.

(iii)

See
[

.∎

If

is regular, solutions of the

\rank(\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}(\cdot))
can be computed by solving an associated

(

) of the form

(2.9)

\displaystyle A^{\mathsf{T}}X+XA+(-C^{\mathsf{T}}+XB){(D+D^{\mathsf{T}})}^{-1}(-C+B^{\mathsf{T}}X)=0.

The connection between solutions of this

and the

are studied in great detail in

]
. Numerical solvers for the

are readily available

, which are also the minimal and maximal solutions of the

(iii)
. These solutions have the property that for each solution

of the

and

3.
Extended port-Hamiltonian systems

As already motivated, our goal is to find a surrogate that well approximates the input-output behavior of a given
pH
system (
2.3a
) and its Hamiltonian (
2.3b
) simultaneously. To this end, it is instrumental to introduce the corresponding extended system

(3.1)

\Sigma_{\mathsf{epH}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t)&=(J-R)Qx(t)+(G-P)u(t),\\
y(t)&=(G+P)^{\mathsf{T}}Qx(t)+(S-N)u(t),\\
y_{\mathcal{H}}(t)&=\tfrac{1}{2}x(t)^{\mathsf{T}}Qx(t).\end{aligned}\right.

To assess the quality of the surrogate, we rely on the distance in the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-norm for linear systems with multiple linear and quadratic outputs.
This norm is introduced in
[
12
]
and can be written as

(3.2)

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\colon\Sigma_{\mathsf{epH}}\mapsto\sqrt{\|\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}+\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}}.

Here,
\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}
stands for the system (
2.3a
), i.e., the system corresponding to the linear output of (
3.1
), and

\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the system

(3.3)

\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t)&=(J-R)Qx(t)+(G-P)u(t),\\
y_{\mathcal{H}}(t)&=\tfrac{1}{2}x(t)^{\mathsf{T}}Qx(t),\end{aligned}\right.

which is a linear system with a single quadratic output.
We refer to
\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
as the
Hamiltonian dynamic
associated with the
pH
system (
2.3
) or with the extended system (
3.1
).
If we abbreviate
A\vcentcolon=(J-R)Q
and
B\vcentcolon=G-P
, then (cf.
[
12
,
43
]
)

\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\vcentcolon=\tr(B^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}B)=\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\mathcal{P}Q\mathcal{P}Q)

where
\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}
denotes the unique solution of the Lyapunov equation

(3.4)

A^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}+\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}A+\tfrac{1}{4}Q\mathcal{P}Q=0

and
\mathcal{P}
being the controllability Gramian given by the solution of (
2.2a
). We refer to
[
12
,
26
,
43
]
for detailed discussions on linear systems with quadratic outputs and the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. At this point, we recall two useful properties of the latter norm that we will employ later. First, we have

\|y_{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\leq\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}.

Second, let

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{QO}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{\tilde{x}}(t)&=\tilde{A}\tilde{x}(t)+\tilde{B}u(t),\\
\tilde{y}(t)&=\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{x}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)\end{aligned}\right.

be another
LTIQO
system. Then the squared distance between
\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{QO}}
is given by

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{QO}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tr(B^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}B)+\tr(\tilde{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathsf{QO}}\tilde{B})-2\tr(B^{\mathsf{T}}Z\tilde{B})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\mathcal{P}Q\mathcal{P}Q)+\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\tilde{Q}).

Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathsf{QO}}
is defined analogously to
\mathcal{O}_{\mathsf{QO}}
and the rectangular matrices
Z
and
Y
are the unique solutions of the Sylvester equations

(3.5)

\displaystyle A^{\mathsf{T}}Z+Z\tilde{A}+\tfrac{1}{4}QY\tilde{Q}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle AY+Y\tilde{A}^{\mathsf{T}}+B\tilde{B}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0.

In the following, we will use the second formulation of the

-error for

.
This formulation is computationally more efficient since the matrices

, and

are independent of

and can be precomputed. In contrast, the first formulation requires the solution

of the Sylvester equation (

) and the computation of the quadratic observability Gramian
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathsf{QO}}
for each
\tilde{Q}
.

4.
Minimality of extended pH systems

A reduction technique that does not involve any approximation error for the input-output behavior of a given standard
LTI
system is the computation of a minimal realization, for example, based on the Kalman decomposition. Such techniques are beneficial as a preprocessing step for numerical methods; we refer to the numerical examples for further details. However, the Kalman decomposition generally does not preserve the structure of the given system.
Since we are dealing with
pH

ROM
that preserves the input-output behavior of the extended system (
3.1

Let us begin with an example to demonstrate that it is not sufficient to rely on a minimal realization for the standard

5.
Energy matching algorithm for surrogate models

If the Kalman-like decomposition does not yield a satisfactory
ROM
in the sense that one wants to reduce the dimension further, one can resort to more general
MOR
techniques. The goal is to find a
ROM
that approximates the input-output behavior of the
FOM
(
2.3a
) as well as the Hamiltonian dynamic (
3.3
).
Conceptually, given some
FOM
(
3.1
) and a reduced dimension
r\ll n
, we want to find an extended
pH
system

(5.1)

\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{epH}}\quad\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \dot{\tilde{x}}(t)&=(\tilde{J}-\tilde{R})\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)+(\tilde{G}+\tilde{P})u(t),\\
\tilde{y}(t)&=(\tilde{G}-\tilde{P})^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)+(\tilde{S}-\tilde{N})u(t),\\
\tilde{y}_{\mathcal{H}}(t)&=\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{x}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{x}(t)\end{aligned}\right.

of order
r
that renders the approximation error
\|\Sigma_{\mathsf{epH}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{epH}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
as small as possible.
Note that finding such a system is at least as challenging as attempting to solve the structure-preserving
MOR
optimization problem

\min_{\Sigma}\|\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}\text{ is {pH} {LTI} and of order $r$}.

Here,
\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}
again represents the system corresponding to the linear output of (
3.1
). Unfortunately, this problem is highly nonlinear and non-convex, so systematically finding globally optimal solutions is not possible in general.
Nevertheless, there are numerous techniques to find good (not necessarily optimal) reduced-order approximations of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}
. We refer to
Section

1.1
for an overview. These techniques are approved in practice; see
[
35
, Sec. 8]
and the references therein.
Building upon these techniques, we assume that a
ROM
(
5.1
) is available whose standard
LTI
part
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}
constitutes a good approximation of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}
in the usual sense.
It is crucial to note that we can replace the Hessian of the Hamiltonian
\tilde{Q}
with any other positive definite solution of the
KYP
inequality (
2.5
) without changing the input-output behavior of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}
; see (
2.6
) and the discussion thereafter. This degree of freedom motivates us to interpret the extended
ROM

\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{epH}}
as a function of
\tilde{Q}
and consider the minimization problem

(5.2)

\min_{\tilde{Q}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succ}}\|\Sigma_{\mathsf{epH}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{epH}}(\tilde{Q})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}(\tilde{Q})\in\mathcal{S}^{r+m}_{\succcurlyeq},

which we call the
energy matching optimization problem
.
In the following, we show that the latter optimization problem is convex and provide algorithmic means to solve it.

5.1.
Analysis of the energy matching optimization problem

Since
\|\Sigma_{\mathsf{pH}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}(\tilde{Q})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}
is independent of
\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
, any minimizer of (
5.2
) is a minimizer of

(5.3)

\min_{\tilde{Q}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succ}}\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{Q})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}(\tilde{Q})\in\mathcal{S}^{r+m}_{\succcurlyeq}

and vice versa.
Using the discussion in
Section

3
, we introduce the cost functional

(5.4)

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{Q})\vcentcolon=\|\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{Q})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\mathcal{P}Q\mathcal{P}Q)+\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\tilde{Q})

where
\mathcal{P},\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
, and
Y
are the unique solution of the linear matrix equations

(5.5)

\displaystyle A\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}A^{\mathsf{T}}+BB^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\tilde{A}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{A}^{\mathsf{T}}+\tilde{B}\tilde{B}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle AY+Y\tilde{A}^{\mathsf{T}}+B\tilde{B}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0.

We make the following observations.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is asymptotically stable and
\tilde{\Sigma}
is minimal and asymptotically stable. Then
\mathcal{J}\colon\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succ}\to\mathbb{R}
as defined in (
5.4
) is twice differentiable, strictly convex, and

(5.7)

\nabla_{\tilde{Q}}\mathcal{J}(\tilde{Q})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}-Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\right).

Proof.

We first derive the first order derivative of
\mathcal{J}
with respect to
\tilde{Q}
.
Since
X
is symmetric, we obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\tr(AXAX)=2AXA^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\tr(AX)=A.

With this, we immediately obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{Q}}\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\mathcal{P}Q\mathcal{P}Q)+\tfrac{1}{4}\tr(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q})-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr(Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\tilde{Q})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{P}\tilde{Q}\mathcal{P}-Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\right).

Furthermore, making use of the vectorization of the first derivative, we obtain the second derivative as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\vectorize(\tilde{Q})}\vectorize\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}-\tfrac{1}{2}Y^{\mathsf{T}}QY\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\vectorize(\tilde{Q})}\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\right)\vectorize(\tilde{Q})-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(Y\otimes Y\right)\vectorize(Q)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\right),

which is strictly positive whenever
\tilde{\Sigma}
is minimal and asymptotically stable. Hence,
\mathcal{J}
is strictly convex.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

In addition to the assumptions of
Lemma

5.3
suppose that
\tilde{\Sigma}
is passive. Then the optimization problem (
5.3
) is solvable and has a unique solution.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\Sigma}
is minimal and passive,
Theorem

2.2

(ii)
implies the existence of
\smash{\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{J}
is bounded from below. Let
(\tilde{Q}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a sequence in
\smash{\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\tilde{Q}_{k})=\inf_{X\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}\mathcal{J}(X).

Since
\smash{\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}
is bounded, cf.
Theorem

2.2

(iii)
, we can choose a convergent subsequence
(\tilde{Q}_{k_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with limit
\tilde{Q}^{\star}\vcentcolon=\lim_{j\to\infty}\tilde{Q}_{k_{j}}
. By construction, we obtain
\smash{\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}(\tilde{Q}^{\star})\in\mathcal{S}^{r+m}_{\succcurlyeq}
such that
Theorem

2.2

(ii)
implies
\tilde{Q}^{\star}\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
. The continuity of
\mathcal{J}
(cf.
Lemma

5.3
) thus implies that
\tilde{Q}^{\star}
is a minimizer of (
5.3
). The uniqueness follows from the strict convexity of
\mathcal{J}
; cf.
Lemma

5.3
.
∎

5.3.
Numerical approach

The optimization problem (
5.3
) is a standard
semi-definite program
(
SDP
), more precisely, a
linear matrix inequality
(
LMI
) problem.
For this class of problems, efficient solvers exist, such as Mosek
[
7
]
or SeDuMi
[
50
]
. So the first and most straightforward strategy is to apply this type of solver to the optimization problem (
5.3
).
However, it is important to consider the limitation of these solvers: They can face challenges with scalability or numerical stability, especially when dealing with ill-conditioned problems, as discussed in the forthcoming
Section

6.4
. Therefore, we propose a second strategy, another classical method for solving
LMI
problems, namely the barrier (or path following) method
[
14
]
.

Instead of solving the constraint optimization problem (
5.3
) directly, the idea of the barrier method is to solve a sequence of unconstrained optimization problems, where the semi-definite constraint of (
5.3
) is realized by a barrier function.
The barrier function assures that the iterates stay in the feasible set and is defined as

(5.9)

\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},\quad\tilde{Q}\mapsto\begin{cases}-\ln\det\left(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pH}}}(\tilde{Q})\right),&\text{if }\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{pH}}}(\tilde{Q})\in\mathcal{S}^{r+m}_{\succ},\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Now, for
\alpha>0
the parametrized objective function is given by
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\tilde{Q})\vcentcolon=\mathcal{J}(\tilde{Q})+\alpha\psi(\tilde{Q})
with the corresponding (unconstrained) optimization problem

(5.10)

\min_{\tilde{Q}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succcurlyeq}}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\tilde{Q}).

Note that the barrier function (
5.9
) requires (
2.4
) to be strictly passive. If this is not the case, then a perturbation of the feedthrough term is required (see the forthcoming
Section

6
).

Proposition 5.7
.

Assume that the
ROM
(
5.1
) is passive and let
X\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
with
\det(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}}(X))>0
. Then the gradient of the barrier function is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}\ln\left(\det\left(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}}(X)\right)\right)=\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{A}&-\tilde{B}\end{bmatrix}{\left(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}}(X)\right)}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}{\left(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}}(X)\right)}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\tilde{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

The proof is given in
Appendix

A
.
∎

Now, for a decreasing sequence of
\alpha_{k}
, we minimize (
5.10
) with a gradient-based optimization method such as a quasi-Newton method.
Since the surrogate model is assumed to be stable,
Theorem

2.2
implies that any solution of the
KYP
inequality is positive definite. Hence, the barrier function automatically ensures that
\tilde{Q}
is symmetric positive definite whenever
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\tilde{Q})
is finite.

In our numerical implementation, we reduce the degrees of freedom by explicitly forcing
\tilde{Q}
to be symmetric via the
half-vectorization
operator
\vech\colon\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\to\mathbb{R}^{r(r+1)/2}
; see for instance
[
34
]
. In this way, we can represent a
r\times r
symmetric matrix as a vector of length
r(r+1)/2
and vice versa. The relation to the standard vectorization is given by

(5.11)

\vectorize(A)=\mathcal{D}_{r}\vech(A),

with the duplication matrix
\mathcal{D}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{2}\times r(r+1)/2}

[
34
]
. We make the following technical observation to compute the gradient of the barrier method with respect to the half-vectorized reduced order Hessian of the Hamiltonian.

Lemma 5.8
.

Consider
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(\vech^{-1}(x))=\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\vech^{-1}(x)}f(\vech^{-1}(x))

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r(r+1)/2}

Proof.

Relation (
5.11
) yields
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\vectorize(\vech^{-1}(x))=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathcal{D}_{r}\vech(\vech^{-1}(x))=\mathcal{D}_{r}
.
Applying the chain rule yields the desired result.
∎

Let
\tilde{q}\vcentcolon=\vech(\tilde{Q})
. Then,
Lemma

5.8
implies

\nabla_{\tilde{q}}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\vech^{-1}(\tilde{q}))=\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\vectorize(\nabla_{\vech^{-1}(\tilde{q})}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\vech^{-1}(\tilde{q}))).

Hence, we can compute the gradient using
Lemma

5.3
and
Proposition

5.7
.
The resulting algorithm is described in
Algorithm

1
.

Input:

FOM
(
2.3
), passive
ROM
(
1.2
), initial Hamiltonian
\tilde{Q}_{0}
with
\det(\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\mathsf{pH}}}(\tilde{Q}_{0}))>0

Output:

Approximate minimizer
\tilde{Q}_{\mathsf{opt}}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succcurlyeq}
of (
5.10
)

Set
\tilde{q}_{0}\vcentcolon=\vech(\tilde{Q}_{0})
.

for

\alpha\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4},\dots,10^{-15}\}

do

1

Set
f(\tilde{q})\vcentcolon=\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\psi}(\vech^{-1}(\tilde{q}))
.

Solve
\tilde{q}_{\mathsf{opt}}\vcentcolon=\argmin_{\tilde{q}}f(\tilde{q})
with initial value
\tilde{q}_{0}
.

Set
\tilde{q}_{0}\vcentcolon=\tilde{q}_{\mathsf{opt}}
.

end for

2

return

\tilde{Q}_{\mathsf{opt}}\vcentcolon=\vech^{-1}(\tilde{q}_{\mathsf{opt}})

5.4.
Model reduction workflow

Building on the concepts discussed in
Section

4
and
Section

5
, we propose the following workflow for constructing
ROMs
that effectively capture both the input-output dynamic and the Hamiltonian dynamic of the
FOM

Step 1

Compute a minimal realization of the
FOM
using the structure-preserving Kalman-like decomposition (see

4.5

Step 2

Construct a

Step 3

Perform energy matching (

1
) as a post-processing step to determine the optimal
\tilde{Q}\in\mathbb{X}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
with respect to the Hamiltonian dynamic of the minimal realization.

7.
Conclusions

We introduced the view of
pH
systems as an extended dynamical system in (
3.1
), combining the standard input-output dynamic and the Hamiltonian dynamic. We studied how this view affects observability and have derived a corresponding structure-preserving Kalman-like decomposition in
Theorem

4.5
, which can be used as a preprocessing tool for numerical methods.
Using the observation that the
KYP
inequality determines all possible Hamiltonians, we proposed a
MOR
post-processing method called energy matching: Given a passive
ROM
for the input-output dynamic, solely consider the optimization problem (
5.3
) for finding the best Hamiltonian. We showed that this optimization problem is uniquely solvable and convex (see
Theorem

5.4
). We presented two numerical approaches to solve this problem and demonstrated their feasibility using three academic examples.
Subsequent research will involve a deeper exploration of the
MOR
problem within the extended norm (
3.2
), considering all system matrices.
