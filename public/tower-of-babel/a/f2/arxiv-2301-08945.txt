Molecular hydrogen and its proxies HCO + and CO in the diffuse interstellar medium
Harvey Liszt, Maryvonne Gerin

Abstract
There is a robust polyatomic chemistry in diffuse, partially-molecular interstellar gas that is readily accessible in absorption at radio/mm/sub-mm wavelengths. Accurate column densities are derived owing to the weak internal excitation, so relative molecular abundances are well known with respect to each other but not with respect to H 2 . Here we consider the use of proxies for hydrogen column densities N(H 2 ) and N(H) = N(HI)+2N(H 2 ) based on measurements of HCO + absorption and CO emission and absorption, and we compare these with results obtained by others when observing HI, H 2 and CO toward stars and AGN. We consider the use of HCO + as a proxy for H 2 and show that the assumption of a relative abundance N(H 2 ) = N(HCO + ) / 3 × 10 − 9 /3\times 10^{-9} gives the same view of the atomic-molecular hydrogen transition that is seen in UV absorption toward stars. CO on the other hand shows differences between the radio and optical regimes because emission is always detected when N( HCO + \mathrm{HCO^{+}} ) ≳ 6 × 10 11 ​ cm − 2 \gtrsim 6\times 10^{11}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$} or N(H 2 ) ≳ 2 × 10 20 ​ cm − 2 \gtrsim 2\times 10^{20}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$} . Wide variations in the integrated CO J=1-0 brightness W CO and N(CO)/N(H 2 ) imply equivalent variations in the CO-H 2 conversion factor even while the ensemble mean is near the usual Galactic values. Gas/reddening ratios found in absorption toward stars, N(H)/E(B-V) = 6.2 × 10 21 ​ H ​ cm − 2 6.2\times 10^{21}~{\rm H}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$} (mag) -1 overall or 6.8 × 10 21 ​ H ​ cm − 2 6.8\times 10^{21}~{\rm H}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$} (mag) -1 for sightlines at E(B-V) ≤ \leq 0.08 mag lacking H 2 are well below the Galactic mean measured at low reddening and high Galactic latitude, 8.3 × 10 21 ​ H ​ cm − 2 8.3\times 10^{21}~{\rm H}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$} (mag) -1 .

Moving the radio values of N(CO) to the right in Figure 5 to align them with the

\mathrm{H_{2}}

\mathrm{HCO^{+}}

Moving the radio datapoints vertically downward would require a factor three

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}

Liszt 2017

the conversion N(CO)/
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}

K-

-1
is a broad local

to the number density in calculations of the rotational excitation. N(CO)/
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
will increase for n(H)
\lesssim 50~{\rm cm}^{-3}
to compensate for the small amounts of energy
being deposited in CO by collisions. At very high n(H)
\gtrsim 1000~{\rm cm}^{-3}

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}

The difference in behaviour between the UV and radio data in Figure 5 stands in

\mathrm{H_{2}}

in Figure 4

\mathrm{H_{2}}

from that observed in UV absorption in having more fractionation from C
+

Liszt 2017

. The differences in the behaviour of the radio and
UV datasets in Figure 5 are real, not artifacts of the scalings and proxy
relationships.

As discussed by
Sheffer et al. 2008
, the CO column density for given N(H) and
N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}

for a lower radiation field. As CO is shielded by dust, by
\mathrm{H_{2}}
and by itself

Visser et al. 2009

\mathrm{{}^{13}CO}

of the radio samples may be due to small variations in the sampled environments
due to differences in the selection criteria of the background sources and the
intrinsic variability of conditions in the diffuse interstellar gas

(
Hu et al. 2021
)
.

Summary

We began by noting the complementary nature of UV/optical and radio observations of
diffuse, partially molecular interstellar gas. The rich polyatomic chemistry of diffuse
molecular gas is only apparent in absorption at radio wavelengths but the column densities
of atomic and molecular hydrogen determined in UV absorption provide a characterization of
the host gas that is not matched in the radio domain. Observations in common of
CH, CO and OH provide the rudiments of a bridge between the radio and UV/optical domains
and determinations of reddening
E(B-V)
are available through different means, which we
use throughout this work.

Prior observations indicated that the relative abundance of the widely observable
polyatomic ion
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
is constant in diffuse molecular gas, with
N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
=3\times 10^{-9}
. In that case,
E(B-V)
and
N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
), together with the usual assumed total hydrogen/reddening ratio N(H)/
E(B-V)
, give
the relative abundances of molecules with respect to
\mathrm{H_{2}}
and the fraction of H-nuclei in

\mathrm{H_{2}}
,
f
{}_{{\rm H}_{2}}
= 2N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)/N(H). In turn, mm-wave observations of CO in emission determine the
CO-
\mathrm{H_{2}}
conversion factor in the diffuse molecular gas.

Our focus, then, was on three aspects of the observations: determining N(HI)/
E(B-V)
and N(H)= N(HI)+2N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
))/
E(B-V)
(Figures 1-3 and Tables 2-3 and 5); comparing
N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) and
E(B-V)
measured at UV and radio wavelengths; and comparing observed and implied
column densities N(CO) and integrated J=1-0 brightnesses
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
of carbon monoxide.

We first discussed the N(H)/
E(B-V)
ratio that was originally determined in optical
UV/absorption by
Copernicus
to be
N(H)
=5.8\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1
but subsequently was
found to be globally larger,
8.3\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1
,
at high galactic latitude when deriving N(HI) from
\lambda
21cm
emission and
E(B-V)
from scaled
far-IR emission. The tension between these values is not resolved
by the vastly increased number of UV-absorption measurements undertaken subsequent to

Copernicus
. Overall and especially at
E(B-V)

\gtrsim 0.1
mag where the molecular
fraction is appreciable and the plot of N(H) vs
E(B-V)
wraps itself tightly about the
regression line in Figure 1, we find that
N(H)
=6.2\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1
is a good average for
sightlines with N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) measured in UV absorption.

That said,
<
N(HI)
>
/
<
E(B-V)
>
and the scatter in N(HI) vs
E(B-V)
are larger at

E(B-V)

<
0.08 mag (Figure 1) where there is no competition for H-nuclei between
atomic and molecular hydrogen. In Figure 2 and Tables 2 and 5 we remarked several
properties of the UV absorption measurements at
E(B-V)

\leq 0.08
mag of N(HI)
subsequent to
Copernicus
: they have an HI/reddening ratio

<
N(HI)
>
/
<
E(B-V)
>
=
6.8\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1

that is much larger than in the
Copernicus
sample
(
4.1\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1
in Table 2)
but is equal to the ratio seen toward optically bright AGN when N(HI)
is measured in
\lambda
21cm
emission and
E(B-V)
is inferred from far-IR emission
(
6.9\times 10^{21}~{\rm H-nuclei}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(mag)
-1
). Similarity of the
mean values of
<
N(HI)
>
/
<
E(B-V)
>
in these post-
Copernicus
samples suggests
that scatter in the plot of stellar measurements of N(HI) vs
E(B-V)
is due to
errors in the stellar photometry that is demonstrated in Appendix C and Figure 8.
Subdividing the stellar absorption
data into subsamples of common and unshared sightlines at small and large
reddening in Appendix C showed no calibration differences in measured N(HI)
but notable differences in
E(B-V)
for shared sightlines at low
E(B-V)
, and
very different explanations for disparities in N(HI)/
E(B-V)
in the same sense.

In Figure 4 we merged the radio and UV determinations of N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) and jointly plotted
them against
E(B-V)
. They coincide and have comparable scatter when
\mathrm{H_{2}}
is detected.
They also do not contradict each other at low reddening where N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) is small and

\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
is not detected (Table 3). Thus, radio and optical data provide the same view of the
HI
\rightarrow
\mathrm{H_{2}}
transition in diffuse gas and a numerical comparison is given in
Table 3. This commensurability is gratifying and lends confidence to the use of
N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
=3\times 10^{-9}
along sightlines observed at radio wavelengths. It
has the consequence that if N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
) is measured and N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) is derived indirectly by
some other means, the implied relative abundance N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) should be consistent
with
3\times 10^{-9}
. Possible use cases are when
E(B-V)
is scaled to N(H) and a value
of N(HI) is subtracted to determine N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) or when

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
is scaled to N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) and subtracted from N(H) to find N(HI) and
f
{}_{{\rm H}_{2}}
.

We merged the UV and radio determinations of N(CO) and plotted them against N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
in Figure 5, testing the equivalence of
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
and N(CO),

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
= N(CO)
/10^{15}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
K-
km s
-1
. This was first observed when N(CO) was derived
by observing CO emission and absorption in the lower rotation levels, and it applies
even to optically thick CO lines over the observed range of
N(CO)
\lesssim 10^{16}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
. It can be understood by
realizing that photons eventually escape after scattering in a medium
where the excitation of CO is weak (significantly sub-thermal), even when
the J=1-0 line is optically thick.

Here a difference was found: The radio and UV data overlap but the radio data
inhabit only the upper portions of the region in the N(CO)-N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) plane that
are occupied by the UV data. Unlike in the UV, sightlines are not present
in the radio dataset with N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
\gtrsim 2\times 10^{20}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
and
N(CO)
<10^{14}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
or N(CO)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
<10^{-6}
. In Section 4.4 we noted
that the molecular fraction appears to be higher in the gas observed in
CO emission and
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
absorption, 0.34 vs 0.22.

Radio measurements of
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
are much more tightly correlated with N(
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
) than are N(CO) and N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
measured in UV absorption. Overall, there is a power-law relation between

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
and N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) measured in the radio domain

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
= 0.0030 K-
km s
-1
(N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
/10^{19}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$})^{1.556}
.

Variations in N(CO)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) (Figure 5) and N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)/
E(B-V)
(Figure 1) drive
varations in N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)/
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
(aka the CO-
\mathrm{H_{2}}
conversion factor) and
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
/
E(B-V)
(Figure 6).
Nonetheless, the overall ensemble mean for the radio data discussed in Section 4.3,

<
N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
>
/
<
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
>
=
2.1-2.4\times 10^{20}\mathrm{H_{2}}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(K-
km s
-1
)
-1
,
is like those in common use in dense and dark gas,

{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
)
=2-4\times 10^{20}\mathrm{H_{2}}\mbox{${~{\rm cm}^{-2}}$}
(K-
km s
-1
)
-1
.
In statistical terms, this similarity in the ensemble mean arises because
so much of the CO emission is contributed by lines of sight at higher
E(B-V)
where the relative CO abundance is higher.
In terms of the physics, similarity of the CO-
\mathrm{H_{2}}
conversion factors in
diffuse and fully molecular dense gas arises from compensating influences of
the CO chemistry and excitation: The smaller relative abundance N(CO)/N(
\mathrm{H_{2}}
) in
low density diffuse molecular gas is compensated by a high brightness in the
J=1-0 line per CO molecule (
{\rm W}_{\mathrm{CO}}
/N(CO)) resulting from the strongly sub-thermal
excitation.
