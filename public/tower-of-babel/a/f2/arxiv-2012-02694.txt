Introduction and statement of results

Holomorphic quadratic differentials on Riemann surfaces are well-studied objects that play a central role in Teichmüller theory; in particular in Teichmüller’s theorem (see
[
1
,
7
,
9
]
) and because they are in one-to-one correspondance with measured foliation
[
8
]

It leads us to curve families and especially to the modulus (or its reciprocal, the extremal length) of a curve family which is a classical conformal invariant studied since the work of Grötzsch and his famous length-area argument. The interplay between holomorphic quadratic differentials and moduli (or extremal lengths) of curve families was then naturally considered (see
[
12
,
20
,
10
,
13
]
). The purpose of this work is to study the interplay between quadratic differentials and moduli of curve families in the setting of
3
-dimensional spherical CR geometry.

A holomorphic quadratic differential on a Riemann surface
X
is a holomorphic section of the line bundle
K(X)\otimes K(X)
where
K(X)
is the canonical bundle of
X
. In a local holomorphic coordinate
z
, a quadratic differential writes as
q(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
for a holomorphic function

of

. A holomorphic quadratic differential naturally gives rise to a flat singular metric on
X
locally given by
\sqrt{|q(z)|}|\mathrm{d}z|
. Thus, the following were defined.

—

The
q
-length of a curve
\gamma
is

l_{q}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\sqrt{|q|}.

—

The
q
-area of
X
is

\mathrm{Area}_{q}(X)=\int_{X}|q|.

We emphase that the holomorphicity of
q
is not mandatory to define these notions.

Associated to a holomorphic quadratic differential are two (measured) singular foliations said

and
vertical
: a curve
\gamma
on
X
is called a

of
q
if
q(\gamma^{\prime})>0
and a
vertical trajectory
of
q
if
q(\gamma^{\prime})<0
. What lies behind the terminology is that
q
, away from a zero, defines a local holomorphic coordinate (called
natural
)
w=x+iy
such that
q=\mathrm{d}w^{2}
and
\gamma
is a horizontal (resp. vertical) trajectory of
q
if and only if
y(\gamma)
(resp.
x(\gamma)
) is constant. The computation of the extremal lengths of foliations has been done in a slightly different form, see for instance the remark
[
13
, p. 34]
that extends a work a Jenkins
[
12
]
and Strebel
[
20
]
(see also
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
). For a precise statement of Kerckhoff’s remark, see
[
18

and for a detailed proof see
[

, section 1.2]
. The precise result we will be interested in states as follow (refer to section

for the definition of the modulus of a curve family):

Theorem 1

(Theorem
2.1.1
)
.

Let

be a domain in
\mathbb{C}
foliated by a family

of horizontal trajectories of a holomorphic quadratic differential

on

Let us point out that the computation of extremal length (
[

]

CR manifolds

For completeness, we give (spherical/strictly pseudoconvex) CR manifolds a pretty long introduction. The interested reader can learn more about CR manifolds for instance in
[
11
,
4
]
. The conventions used in those books are not the same as here. In
[
11
]
, the CR structure
V
is used as our
\overline{V}
(that is its sections serve as Cauchy-Riemann operator whereas here we understand sections of
\overline{V}
as Cauchy-Riemann operators). In
[
4
]
, CR manifolds are defined without specifying the rank of the CR structure and thus, what we call CR manifolds here are called CR manifolds of hypersurface type there.

A
CR manifold
is a manifold
M^{2n+1}
endowed with (complex) rank
n
subbundle
V
of the complexified tangent bundle
\mathbb{C}TM
satisfying
V\cap\overline{V}=\{0\}
and
[V,V]\subset V
.
V
is called the
CR structure
on
M
. Equivalently (though we won’t use this point of view here), a CR manifold is a
2n+1
manifold
M
endowed with a (real) rank
2n
distribution
P
and a complex structure
J
on
P
.

Remark 0.0.1
.

—

Notice that for a
3
-dimensional manifold, the condition
[V,V]\subset V
is always fulfilled.

—

Some authors omit the rank
n
dimension of the subbundle
V
in the definition of a CR manifold and call a manifold endowed with a rank
n
CR structure a CR manifold of
hypersurface type
.

—

Every hypersurface
M
of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
inherits a CR structure from the complex structure on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
simply by taking
V=\mathbb{C}TM\cap T^{1,0}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. In particular, the unit sphere
S^{2n+1}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
has such a CR structure said
standard
.

When a new structure is introduced, we usually like to understand the maps preserving this structure. A map
f:(M,V)\rightarrow(M^{\prime},V^{\prime})
between CR manifold is called a
CR map
if
f_{\ast}(V)\subset V^{\prime}
. A function
f:(M,V)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}
is called a
CR function
if
f_{\ast}(V)\subset T^{1,0}\mathbb{C}^{k}
. A
CR diffeomorphism
between CR manifolds is a CR map which is also a diffeomorphism. Two CR manifolds are called equivalent (or CR equivalent) if there is a CR diffeomorphism from one to the other.

A CR manifold
(M,V)
is called
strictly pseudoconvex
if
V\oplus\overline{V}
is a (complex) contact structure on
M
(that is, locally
V\oplus\overline{V}=\ker(\omega)
for a
1
-form
\omega
such that
\omega\wedge(\mathrm{d}\omega)^{n}
is nowhere zero).
Using Rumin complex
[
19
]
, one can define quadratic differentials on strictly pseudoconvex CR
3
-manifolds
[
23
]
and recover the notions of trajectories,
q
-length and
q
-volume. For completeness, we give a short overview about them in section
1.1
.

Spherical CR manifolds and the Heisenberg group

A (
3
-dimensional) spherical CR manifold
[
5
]
is a manifold locally modeled on the unit sphere
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
endowed with its standard CR structure. Equivalently (because of a Liouville type theorem: a CR diffeomorphism between open subsets of
S^{3}
is the restriction of the action of an element of
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
on
S^{3}
; see
[
5
, p. 224]
for instance), it is
(G,X)
-manifold for
X=S^{3}
and
G=\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
. Another local model for a spherical CR manifold is the Heisenberg group
{\mathcal{H}}
which is
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}
endowed with the group law

(z,t)(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(z+z^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+2\Im(z\overline{z}^{\prime})).

As for its CR structure, it is given by
V_{\mathcal{H}}=span(Z)
where
Z
is the complex vector field

Z=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+i\overline{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}.

It is easy to see that
V_{\mathcal{H}}\oplus\overline{V}_{\mathcal{H}}=\ker(\omega)
with
\omega=\mathrm{d}t-i\overline{z}\mathrm{d}z+iz\mathrm{d}\overline{z}
. Since
\omega
is a contact form,
({\mathcal{H}},V_{\mathcal{H}})
is a strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold and it is known to be equivalent to the unit sphere minus a point endowed with its standard CR structure. Thus, one can see a spherical CR manifold as locally modeled on
({\mathcal{H}},V_{\mathcal{H}})
, that is a manifold endowed with an atlas of charts (called a
spherical CR atlas
)
(U_{i},\varphi_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow U_{i}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{H}})
such that every
\varphi_{j}\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}
is a CR diffeomorphism.

Moreover, a function
f:U\subset{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is CR if and only if
\overline{Z}f=0
and it is easy to see that a map
g=(g_{1},g_{2}):U\rightarrow V
between open subsets of
{\mathcal{H}}
is CR if and only if both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}+i|g_{1}|^{2}
are CR functions. With this angle several differential operators can be defined on quadratic differentials on spherical CR manifolds and especially one:
B_{2}
that remained quite mysterious in
[
23
]
. The main result of this paper gives a geometric interpretation of quadratic differentials in the kernel of this
B_{2}
, this is the result announced in the abstract (refer to section
1.2
for the modulus of a family of a legendrian curves in the Heisenberg group).

Theorem 2

(Theorem
2.2.1
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in the Heisenberg group foliated by a family
\Gamma
of legendrian (everywhere tangent to the contact distribution) curves. Assume that there is a quadratic differential
q
on
\Omega
such that
B_{2}q=0
and every curve in
\Gamma
is a horizontal trajectory for
q
. Let
l_{\Gamma}:\Omega\rightarrow]0,+\infty[
be the function that associates to a point
p\in\Omega
, the
q
-length of the leaf containing
p
.Then, the modulus of
\Gamma
is

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{|q|^{2}}{(l_{\Gamma})^{4}}\mathrm{d}L^{3}.

If in addition we ask the leaves to have the same
q
-length
C>0
, then the modulus expresses with the
q
-volume (denoted
\mathrm{Vol}_{q}
, see Definition
1.1.2
) and

(see Corollary
2.2.4
):

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{C^{4}}\mathrm{Vol}_{q}(\Omega).

As for the proof given here of the result in the complex plane, the proof of this theorem relies on a decomposition of the
q
-volume. It is for this decomposition that we need the quadratic differential to be in the kernel of
B_{2}
.

The paper is organized as follow. The first section begins with a short overview about quadratic differentials in spherical CR geometry and continues with moduli of curve families in the complex plane and in the Heisenberg group. In the second section, we prove theorems 1 and 2 and we give some examples and counter examples) of application of theorem 2.

1.1

Definition 1.1.1

(Quadratic differential)
.

A quadratic differential on a strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold is a smooth section of the line bundle
\wedge^{1,0}\otimes\wedge^{1,0}
.

The first geometric data induced by a quadratic differential on a strictly pseudoconvex manifold can be defined by analogy with the Riemann surface case. However, since quadratic differentials are defined up to a contact form, we shall restrict to
legendrian curves
(that is curves everywhere tangent to the contact distribution).

Definition 1.1.2

(Trajectories, length, volume)
.

Let

be a quadratic differential on a
3

(M,V)
. Let also

be a legendrian parametrized curve on

1.

—

vertical trajectory of

q(\gamma^{\prime}(s))<0

3.

Three differential operators on quadratic differentials on spherical CR manifolds that allowed to obtain analogue of half-translation structures were defined in
[
23
]

(U_{i},\varphi_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow U_{i}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{H}})_{i\in I}

[q_{i}\mathrm{d}z^{2}]_{\omega}=[(q_{j}\circ g_{j,i})\left(Zg_{j,i}^{1}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}z^{2}]_{\omega}\text{ on $\varphi_{i}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})$}

Definition 1.1.3

(Quadratic differential on a spherical CR manifold)

A quadratic differential on a spherical CR manifold

We can now define operators on quadratic differentials on

and "lift" them using charts on the manifold. On

\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{2,{\mathcal{H}}}[q\mathrm{d}z^{2}]_{\omega}=\left(2qZ\overline{Z}q-Zq\overline{Z}q-4iqTq\right)[\mathrm{d}z^{3}]_{\omega}\otimes\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}z\text{,}

1.2
Moduli of curve families

First, we recall what is the modulus of a family of curves in the complex plane (see
[
24
]
for instance). Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbb{C}
and
\Gamma
be a family of
C^{1}
curves lying in
U
. Denote
\adm(\Gamma)
the set of measurable Borel functions
\rho:U\rightarrow[0,\infty]
such that

\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}l=\int_{a}^{b}\rho(\gamma(s))|\dot{\gamma}_{1}(s)|\mathrm{d}s\geq 1

for every curve
\gamma:]a,b[\longrightarrow\Omega
in
\Gamma
. Elements of
\adm(\Gamma)
are called
densities
. Define the
2
-modulus
of
\Gamma
by

M_{2}(\Gamma)=\underset{\rho\in\adm(\Gamma)}{\inf}\int_{U}\rho^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}

where
\mathrm{d}L^{2}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A density
\rho_{0}
is said
extremal for
\Gamma
if

M_{2}(\Gamma)=\int_{U}\rho_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}.

Remark 1.2.1
.

—

Sometimes, authors give another (though equivalent) definition of the modulus of a curve family. For a non negative measurable Borel function
\rho:U\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
, denote
\mathrm{L}_{\rho}(\Gamma)=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\inf}\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}l
and
\mathrm{A}_{\rho}(U)=\int_{U}\rho^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}
. Then, it is clear that

M_{2}(\Gamma)=\underset{\rho}{\inf}\frac{\mathrm{A}_{\rho}(U)}{(\mathrm{L}_{\rho}(\Gamma))^{2}}

where the infimum is taken over all non negative measurable Borel functions
\rho
in
U
. We chose the other definition here because with it, the essential uniqueness of an extremal density is more clear (see Theorem 2.1.2 in
[
24
]
for instance).

—

Using the same notations, the
extremal length
of
\Gamma
is

E(\Gamma)=\underset{\rho}{\sup}\frac{(\mathrm{L}_{\rho}(\Gamma))^{2}}{\mathrm{A}_{\rho}(U)}.

For curves in the Heisenberg group, things are quite the same. One needs to consider legendrian curves because these are the locally rectifiable curves for the usual left invariant metric
d_{\mathcal{H}}(p,q)=\|p^{-1}q\|_{\mathcal{H}}
with
\|(z,t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\left(t^{2}+|z|^{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}
. Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):I\longrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
be a
C^{1}
curve.
\gamma
is legendrian if and only if

\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s)=-2\Im(\overline{\gamma}_{1}(s)\dot{\gamma}_{1}(s))\text{ for every $s\in I$.}

Then, let
\Gamma
be a family of legendrian curves in an open subset
\Omega
of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Denote
\adm(\Gamma)
the set of measurable Borel functions
\rho:\Omega\longrightarrow[0,\infty]
such that

\int_{\gamma}\rho\mathrm{d}l=\int_{a}^{b}\rho(\gamma(s))|\dot{\gamma}_{1}(s)|\mathrm{d}s\geq 1

for every curve
\gamma:]a,b[\longrightarrow\Omega
in
\Gamma
. Elements of
\adm(\Gamma)
are called
densities
. Define the
4
-modulus
of
\Gamma
by

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\underset{\rho\in\adm(\Gamma)}{\inf}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{4}\mathrm{d}L^{3}

where
\mathrm{d}L^{3}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A density
\rho_{0}
is said
extremal for
\Gamma
if

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}^{4}\mathrm{d}L^{3}.

As in the complex case, an extremal density is essentially unique (see
[
3
, Proposition 3.4]
for instance). The modulus of a family of curves is a conformal invariant
[
16
]
and a classical tool in the theory of quasiconformal maps in the Heisenberg group
[
15
,
16
]
, with various purposes such as finding extremal quasiconformal maps (see
[
2
,
3
,
22
,
21
]
) or for proving non-existence of quasiconformal maps between certain CR manifolds (see
[
14
,
17
]
). We will be interested in computing the modulus of a family of curves when this family is a foliation by horizontal trajectories of a quadratic differential.

2
Modulus of a foliation by trajectories of a quadratic differential

2.1
The result in the complex plane

As stated in the introduction, Theorem 1 is a local formulation of a well known (
[
18
, Theorem 2.21]
) result dealing with the computation of the modulus (or extremal length) of a foliation in the complex plane. We give it here an elementary proof that doesn’t use all the potential of conformal geometry because these are tools we don’t have to our disposal in the setting of families of legendrian curves in the Heisenberg group. To be precise, we give a proof that uses only the annihilation of the Cauchy-Riemann operator by the quadratic differential.

Theorem 2.1.1
.

Let
I,J
be intervals and
\Phi:I\times J\longrightarrow U\subset\mathbb{C}
be a
C^{2}
-diffeomorphism. Denote

\Gamma=\{\Phi(.,p)\ |\ p\in J\}.

Let
q
be a holomorphic quadratic differential on
U
such that every curve in
\Gamma
is a horizontal trajectory of
q
. Then,

M_{2}(\Gamma)=\int_{U}\frac{|q|}{(l_{\Gamma})^{2}}\mathrm{d}L^{2}

where, for every
z\in U
,
l_{\Gamma}(z)=l_{q}(\Phi(.,\Phi^{-1}_{2}(z)))
.

The proof is a consequence of the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1.2
.

Let
q:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic quadratic differential on a domain
U
of
\mathbb{C}
. Let
I,J
be intervals and
\Phi:I\times J\rightarrow U
be a diffeomorphism such that every curve
\Phi(.,p)
for
p\in J
is a horizontal trajectory for
q
. Then, there is a nowhere vanishing function
\lambda:J\lx@nobreakspace\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle(|q|\circ\Phi)\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}=\sqrt{(|q|\circ\Phi)}|\partial_{s}\Phi|\lambda

(1)

where
\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}
is the Jacobian determinant of
\Phi
.

Remark 2.1.3
.

—

What the lemma says is that, on
I\times J
, the
q
-area decomposes as the product of the
q
-length and a measure on
J
.

—

We will prove and use the lemma with the conclusion written in another form, easier for computations. We use here the same notations and hypotheses than in the lemma. First, we write

\mu=\sqrt{(|q|\circ\Phi)}|\partial_{s}\Phi|=\sqrt{(q\circ\Phi)(\partial_{s}\Phi)^{2}}.

We then multiply both sides of equation
1
by
|\partial_{s}\Phi|^{2}
(which is nowhere zero since
\Phi
is a diffeomorphism) to obtain

(|q|\circ\Phi)|\partial_{s}\Phi|^{2}\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}=\mu|\partial_{s}\Phi|^{2}\lambda.

Then, notice that
(|q|\circ\Phi)|\partial_{s}\Phi|^{2}=\mu^{2}
and divide both sides by
\mu
(which is nowhere zero) and we finally have the form of the conclusion we will prove:

\displaystyle\sqrt{(q\circ\Phi)(\partial_{s}\Phi)^{2}}\mathrm{J}_{\phi}=\lambda|\partial_{s}\Phi|^{2}.

(2)

Proof of Theorem
2.1.1
.

Assuming the lemma for now, let
\rho\in\mathrm{adm}(\Gamma)
be an admissible density for
\Gamma
. Then,

\int_{U}\rho^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}=\int_{J}\int_{I}\rho^{2}(\Phi)|\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}p.

However, for every
p
in
J
, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\left(\int_{I}\left(\rho(\Phi)|\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)\left(\int_{I}\left(\frac{|\partial_{s}\Phi|}{|\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)\geq\left(\int_{I}\rho(\Phi)|\partial_{s}\Phi|\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\underset{\text{$\rho\in\mathrm{adm}(\Gamma)$}}{\geq}1.

Using equation
2
, we have for every
p\in J

\int_{I}\left(\frac{|\partial_{s}\Phi|}{|\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s=\int_{I}\frac{\sqrt{(q\circ\Phi)\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}}}{|\lambda|}\mathrm{d}s=\frac{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p))}{|\lambda(p)|}.

Thus,

\int_{U}\rho^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}\geq\int_{J}\frac{|\lambda(p)|}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p))}\mathrm{d}p.

Since it is true for every
\rho\in\mathrm{adm}(\Gamma)
,

M_{2}(\Gamma)\geq\int_{J}\frac{|\lambda(p)|}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p))}\mathrm{d}p.

Now, let
\rho_{0}
be defined for every

by
\rho_{0}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{|q(z)|}}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,\Phi^{-1}_{2}(z)))}
. Then, if
\Phi(.,p_{0})
is a curve in
\Gamma
, one has

\int_{\Phi(.,p_{0})}\rho_{0}\mathrm{d}l=\frac{1}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p_{0}))}\int_{I}\sqrt{|q(\Phi(s,p_{0}))|}|\partial_{s}\Phi(s,p_{0})|\mathrm{d}s=1

and so
\rho_{0}
is admissible for
\Gamma
. Moreover,

\int_{U}\rho_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}=\lx@nobreakspace\int_{J}\int_{I}\frac{|q(\Phi)||\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}|}{(l_{q}(\Phi(.,p)))^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}p.

Using Lemma
2.1.2
, we obtain

\int_{U}\rho_{0}^{2}\mathrm{d}L^{2}=\int_{J}\int_{I}\frac{\sqrt{|q(\Phi)|}|\partial_{s}\Phi||\lambda|}{(l_{q}(\Phi(.,p)))^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}p=\int_{J}\frac{|\lambda(p)|}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p))}\mathrm{d}p.

Thus,

M_{2}(\Gamma)\leq\int_{J}\frac{|\lambda(p)|}{l_{q}(\Phi(.,p))}\mathrm{d}p.

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.1.2
.

As in remark
2.1.3
, we write

\mu=\sqrt{(q\circ\Phi)\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}}

Consequently, since
\Phi

For that, we first write

q=\left(\frac{\mu(\Phi^{-1}_{1})}{\partial_{s}\Phi(\Phi^{-1})}\right)^{2}.

From

\partial_{s}\Phi\left(\partial_{s}\mu\partial_{\overline{w}}\Phi^{-1}_{1}(\Phi)+\partial_{p}\mu\partial_{\overline{w}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi^{-1}_{2}(\Phi)\right)=\mu\left(\partial_{s,s}\Phi\partial_{\overline{w}}\Phi^{-1}_{1}(\Phi)+\partial_{s,p}\Phi\partial_{\overline{w}}\Phi^{-1}_{2}(\Phi)\right).

Usual formulas for inverse functions give

\partial_{\overline{w}}\Phi^{-1}_{1}=-i\frac{\partial_{p}\Phi}{2\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}}\text{ and }\partial_{\overline{w}}\Phi^{-1}_{2}=i\frac{\partial_{s}\Phi}{2\mathrm{J}_{\Phi}}.

Leading to

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\Phi\left(\partial_{s}\mu\partial_{p}\Phi+\mu\partial_{s,p}\Phi\right)-\mu\partial_{s,s}\Phi\partial_{p}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{p}\mu\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}.

(3)

Now, we finally compute:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\left(\frac{\mu\partial_{p}\Phi}{\partial_{s}\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{s}\Phi\left(\partial_{s}\mu\partial_{p}\Phi+\mu\partial_{s,p}\Phi\right)-\mu\partial_{p}\Phi\partial_{s,s}\Phi}{\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{using equation \ref{eq1.1}}}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{p}\mu\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}}{\left(\partial_{s}\Phi\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\partial_{p}\mu

and
\partial_{p}\mu
is a real valued function.
∎

If in addition to the hypotheses of the theorem we ask the curves

to all have the same

, then

becomes constant equal to

Corollary 2.1.4

Let

+2\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}\Im\left(\partial_{p_{1}}\Phi_{1}\overline{\partial_{p_{2}}\Phi_{1}}\right)+2\Phi_{1}\Im\left(\partial_{s,p_{1}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi\overline{\partial_{p_{2}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{1}}+\partial_{p_{1}}\lx@nobreakspace\overline{\partial_{s,p_{2}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{1}}\right).

Since
\partial_{s}\Phi_{2}=-2\Im\left(\overline{\Phi_{1}}\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}\right)
, we find

\partial_{s}A=\partial_{s,p_{1}}\Phi_{1}\left(-\partial_{p_{2}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{2}-2\Im\left(\overline{\Phi_{1}}\partial_{p_{2}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{1}\right)\right)\\
+\partial_{s,p_{2}}\Phi_{1}\left(\partial_{p_{1}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{2}+2\Im\left(\overline{\Phi_{1}}\partial_{p_{1}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{1}\right)\right)\\
+4\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}\Im\left(\partial_{p_{1}}\Phi_{1}\overline{\partial_{p_{2}}\Phi_{1}}\right).

Consequently,

\Im\left(\overline{\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}}\partial_{s}A\right)=\left(-\partial_{p_{2}}\lx@nobreakspace\Phi_{2}-2\Im\left(\overline{\Phi_{1}}\partial_{p_{2}}\Phi_{1}\right)\right)\Im\left(\overline{\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}}\partial_{s,p_{1}}\Phi_{1}\right)\\
+\left(\partial_{p_{1}}\Phi_{2}+2\Im\left(\overline{\Phi_{1}}\partial_{p_{1}}\Phi_{1}\right)\right)\Im\left(\overline{\partial_{s}\Phi_{1}}\partial_{s,p_{2}}\Phi_{1}\right)

∎

As for the complex case, if in addition to the hypotheses of the theorem we ask the curves
\Phi(.,p)
to all have the same
q
-length
C>0
, then
l_{\Gamma}
becomes constant equal to
C
and we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in the Heisenberg group foliated by a family
\Gamma
of legendrian curves. Assume that there is a quadratic differential
q
on
\Omega
such that
B_{2}q=0
and every curve in
\Gamma
is a horizontal trajectory for
q
with
q
-length
C>0
. Then, the modulus of
\Gamma
is

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{C^{4}}\mathrm{Vol_{q}}(\Omega).

Explicit computation of moduli

As an explicit example of application of theorem
2.2.1
, we want to discuss about the quadratic differential
q_{0}=\left[\frac{\overline{z}^{2}(t^{2}+|z|^{4})^{\frac{2}{3}}}{|z|^{\frac{8}{3}}(t+i|z|^{2})^{2}}\mathrm{d}z^{2}\right]_{\omega}
in the annulus
A_{r}=\{(z,t)\in{\mathcal{H}}\ |\ 1<\|(z,t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}<r\}
for
r>1
. The horizontal trajectories of
q_{0}
are the spherical arcs:

\gamma_{x,\alpha}(s)=\left(\sqrt{e^{x}\sin(s)}\alpha e^{\frac{is}{2}},e^{x}\cos(s)\right)\text{ for $|\alpha|=1$ and $x\in]0,2\ln(r)[$}

with
s\in]0,\pi[
. Denote
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{x,\alpha}\ |\ 0<x<2\ln(r)\ \&\ |\alpha|=1\}
. Then
\Gamma
is the foliation of
A_{r}\backslash(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
by horizontal trajectories for
q_{0}
which satisfies
B_{2}q_{0}=0
. For every
(s,x,\alpha)
, we easily compute

q_{0}(\gamma_{x,\alpha}^{\prime}(s))=\frac{1}{4\sin^{\frac{4}{3}}(s)}

and thus, for every
(x,\alpha)
, we have

l_{q_{0}}(\gamma_{x,\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{-\frac{2}{3}}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Notice that all curves in
\Gamma
have the same
q_{0}
-length which we denote
C=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{-\frac{2}{3}}(s)\mathrm{d}s
.

Now, the
q_{0}
-volume of
A_{r}\backslash(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})
is easy to compute (by substitution) in terms of
l_{q_{0}}(\gamma_{x,\alpha})
, we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}_{q_{0}}(A_{r}\backslash(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle=.

\displaystyle\int_{A_{r}\backslash(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})}\frac{\mathrm{d}L^{3}}{|z|^{\frac{4}{3}}(t^{2}+|z|^{4})^{\frac{2}{3}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\int_{0}^{2\ln(r)}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{e^{2x}}{2e^{\frac{2x}{3}}\sin^{\frac{2}{3}}(s)e^{\frac{4x}{3}}}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi C\ln(r).

Then, Corollary
2.2.4
gives the modulus of
\Gamma
:

M_{4}(\Gamma)=\frac{4\pi\ln(r)}{C^{3}}.

The same thing can be done for the vertical trajectories of
q_{0}

q

-q

General strategy

As an end, let us give a general strategy to compute the modulus of a foliation by Legendrian curves, applying theorem
2.2.1
.

Let
\Phi:I\times\Lambda\rightarrow\Omega
be a diffeomorphism. Then, the family of quadratic differentials for which the
\Phi(.,p)
are horizontal trajectories is the family

\left\{q_{f}=\left[\frac{f}{(\partial_{s}\Phi_{1})^{2}\circ\Phi^{-1}}\mathrm{d}z^{2}\right]_{\omega}\ |\ f:\Omega\rightarrow]0,+\infty[\right\}.

Thus, to apply theorem
2.2.1
one needs to find a positive valued function
f
such that
B_{2}q_{f}=0
.

Remark 2.3.1
.

A quadratic differential with prescribed trajectories in the kernel of
B_{2}
is (if it exists) unique up to a positive constant. Indeed, assume that
q
is a quadratic differential and
q^{\prime}
are quadratic differentials with the same horizontal trajectories. We just saw that there must be a positive valued function
f
such that
q^{\prime}=fq
. If we assume moreover that both
q
and
fq
annihilate
B_{2}
. Then,

0=B_{2}(fq)=f^{2}B_{2}q+\left(|q|^{2}\left(\overline{Z}(f^{2})+f\overline{Z}f\right)\right)\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\otimes\omega=\left(3f|q|^{2}\overline{Z}f\right)\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\otimes\omega.

Thus,
f
is a positive CR function, hence constant.
