Covariance estimation with direction dependence accuracy
Pedro Abdalla, Shahar Mendelson

Abstract
We construct an estimator Σ ^ \widehat{\Sigma} for covariance matrices of unknown, centred random vectors X, with the given data consisting of N independent measurements X 1 , … , X N X_{1},...,X_{N} of X and the wanted confidence level. We show under minimal assumptions on X, the estimator performs with the optimal accuracy with respect to the operator norm. In addition, the estimator is also optimal with respect to direction dependence accuracy: ⟨ Σ ^ ​ u , u ⟩ \langle\widehat{\Sigma}u,u\rangle is an optimal estimator for σ 2 ​ ( u ) = 𝔼 ​ ⟨ X , u ⟩ 2 \sigma^{2}(u)=\mathbb{E}\langle X,u\rangle^{2} when σ 2 ​ ( u ) \sigma^{2}(u) is “large”.



1
Introduction

The goal of this article is to study the covariance estimation problem with direction dependence accuracy. To give some idea of what one should expect, let us first describe the situation in the one dimensional case.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
be a centred random variable with a finite fourth moment and consider
\kappa
for which

(\mathbb{E}x^{4})^{1/4}\leq\kappa(\mathbb{E}x^{2})^{1/2}.

(1.1)

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a procedure whose goal is to guess the variance of
x
: It receives as data
N
independent copies of
x
and a parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, and returns a real number

\widehat{\nu}=\phi(x_{1},...,x_{n},\delta).

The procedure’s error is
\varepsilon
if for any centred random variable
x
, with probability at least
1-\delta
with respect to the
N
-product measure endowed by
x
,

|\widehat{\nu}-\mathbb{E}x^{2}|\leq\varepsilon.

It is straightforward to identify the best performance one can hope for. Let
p=\log(2/\delta)/2N
, set
\alpha>0
, and define the law of
x
by

x=\begin{cases}-\alpha,\quad\text{with probability $\frac{p}{2}$},\\
\ \ 0,\quad\text{with probability $1-p$},\\
\ \ \alpha,\quad\text{with probability $\frac{p}{2}$}.\end{cases}

Clearly, with probability
(1-p)^{N}\geq 2\delta
, all the
N
independent copies
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
satisfy
x_{i}=0
, and for such samples no procedure can distinguish
x
and
0
. Hence, the error in one of the two cases is at least
\mathbb{E}x^{2}/2
. In other words,

\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}x^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}p=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}x^{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{N}},

implying that the best performance one can hope for is that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\widehat{\nu}-\mathbb{E}x^{2}|\leq C\sqrt{\mathbb{E}x^{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}};

(1.2)

here
C
is an absolute constant, and in particular it is strictly positive and does not depend on
x
or the procedure
\phi
.

Once this benchmark is set, consider cases in which (
1.1
) holds. Then (
1.2
) implies that

|\widehat{\nu}-\mathbb{E}x^{2}|\leq C\kappa^{2}\mathbb{E}x^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

(1.3)

As it happens, the
trimmed mean
or the
median of means
satisfy (
1.3
) (see
[
9
]
), showing that this performance is indeed optimal.

Note that the assumption that
x
has a finite fourth moment is necessary. Indeed, if
x
does not have a finite fourth moment then
x^{2}
does not have a finite variance, resulting in a slower optimal error rate (see
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
). It also follows from
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
that the norm equivalence (
1.1
) is necessary if the goal is to obtain an error that scales like
\sigma^{2}/\sqrt{N}
.

Given the bounds in the one-dimensional case, a natural question is how to construct an optimal multi-dimensional covariance estimation procedure. However, even identifying the best possible error that one can hope for is challenging. We will explore this question in what follows, and then design an estimator that is optimal in a sense we shall clarify immediately.

Definition 1.1
.

A
covariance estimation procedure
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Given
0<\delta<1
and
X_{1},...,X_{N}
, —
N
independent copies of a centred random vector
X
, the procedure returns a positive semi-definite matrix

\hat{\Sigma}=\phi(X_{1},...,X_{N},\delta).

The procedure performs with accuracy
\varepsilon
if for any centred random vector
X
, with probability at least
1-\delta
with respect to the
N
-product measure endowed by
X
,

\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X}\|_{2\to 2}\leq\varepsilon.

Here and in what follows,
\Sigma_{X}
is the covariance matrix of
X
and
\|\cdot\|_{2\to 2}
is the (
\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}
) operator norm.

The current state of the art upper estimate on the performance of a covariance estimation procedure is from
[
1
,
14
]
. To formulate that result we need a suitable version of (
1.1
).

Definition 1.2
.

A centred random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies an
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
\kappa
if for every
u\in S^{d-1}
,

(\mathbb{E}\langle X,u\rangle^{4})^{1/4}\leq\kappa(\mathbb{E}\langle X,u\rangle^{2})^{1/2}.

(1.4)

Remark 1.3
.

Since the
L_{4}(X)
norm dominates the
L_{2}(X)
norm,
\kappa
in (
1.4
) must be at least
1
.

In
[
1
]
and
[
14
]
the authors construct a covariance estimator which satisfies that, for every centred random vector
X
, with probability at least
1-\delta
with respect to the
N
-product measure endowed by
X
,

\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X}\|_{2\to 2}\leq C\left(\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{r(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\right),

(1.5)

where
C
is an absolute constant,
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{d}\geq 0
are the (ordered) eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and
r(\Sigma)
is its effective rank; that is,

r(\Sigma):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}.

The fluctuations in (
1.5
) depend on the behaviour of the “worst-case” one-dimensional marginal of
X
, that is, on

\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}};

therefore (
1.5
) does not give useful information on the variance of individual one-dimensional marginals. Ideally, one would like to have a covariance estimator for which
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X}\|_{2\to 2}
is small, and for “many” directions
u\in S^{d-1}
,

|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle|

scales like the optimal one-dimensional estimator. In other words, setting
\sigma^{2}(u):=\mathbb{E}\langle X,u\rangle^{2}
,

|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle|\leq C\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

(1.6)

Our aim here is to explore the best possible behaviour of a “directional sensitive” covariance estimator:

Question 1.4
.

What is the best possible function
S(u):S^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which there is an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}
that satisfies (under minimal assumptions on
X
) that with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every
u\in S^{d-1}
,

\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle\leq C\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+S(u)?

(1.7)

An answer to Question
1.4
would imply that whenever
\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
dominates
S(u)
, the multi-dimensional estimator is also an optimal one-dimensional estimator of the variance of the marginal
\langle X,u\rangle
.

Throughout the article we assume without loss of generality that:

Assumption 1.5
.

The random vector
X
has a symmetric, absolutely continuous density, and an invertible covariance matrix
\Sigma
; in particular,
X
is centred. In addition,
X
satisfies an
L_{4}
-
L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
\kappa
.

The fact that Assumption
1.5
can be made without loss of generality is standard: the first part is evident by a symmetrisation argument, and if the need arises, following a tensorisation with a small gaussian vector. The second assumption can be made because without it, the smallest error one can hope for is much larger than
\sim\sigma^{2}(u)/\sqrt{N}
.

Our main result is a sharp answer to Question
1.4
:

Theorem 1.6

(Main Result)
.

There exist an absolute constant
\kappa_{E}
and a constant
\kappa_{up}(\kappa)
depending only on
\kappa
for which the following hold. For every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},\delta)
satisfying, with probability at least
1-\delta
with respect to the
N
-product measure endowed by
X
, that for every
u\in S^{d-1}

\begin{split}&|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle|\\
&\leq\kappa_{up}\left(\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\max\left\{\sigma(u),\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{E}{\log(1/\delta)}}}\right\}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\frac{\kappa_{E}}{2}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\right).\end{split}

(1.8)

Remark 1.7
.

The reason behind the notation
\kappa_{E}
will become clear in what follows.

For a positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma
with (ordered) eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{d}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{\preceq\Sigma}
be the class of all centred vectors
d
-dimensional vectors
X
whose covariance
\Sigma_{X}
satisfies

u^{T}\Sigma_{X}u\leq u^{T}\Sigma u\quad\text{for every $u\in S^{d-1}$}.

Our second main result complements Theorem
1.6
. We show that whenever it is possible to give a non-trivial estimate to (
1.7
), Theorem
1.6
is indeed optimal (up to an absolute constant):

Theorem 1.8

(Lower Bound)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a positive semi-definite matrix and set
N\geq 18
. Suppose that there exist a constant
\gamma
and an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\delta)
which satisfies that for every
0<\delta<1
and for every
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\preceq\Sigma}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists u\in S^{d-1}:|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle|\geq\gamma\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+S(u)\right)\leq\delta.

Then there exist constants
c_{1},\kappa_{0}
depending only on
\gamma
and
\kappa
for which the following dichotomy holds, for every
u\in S^{d-1}
: either

S(u)\geq c_{1}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}},

or

\begin{split}&S(u)\\
&\geq\kappa_{low}\left(\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\max\left\{\sigma(u),\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}{\log(1/\delta)}}}\right\}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\right).\end{split}

(1.9)

Before we continue any further, let us fix some notation. In what follows,
B_{2}^{d}
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a subspace
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
B_{E}=B_{2}^{d}\cap E
be the Euclidean unit ball in
E
, and set
P_{E}
to be the orthogonal projection onto
E
.
I_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix.

Let
g
be the standard gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define the gaussian mean-width of a set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\ell^{\ast}(T):=\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\langle g,t\rangle.

A set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is symmetric if for every
t\in T
we have that
-t\in T
. Let

\|\cdot\|_{T}:=\sup_{t\in T}\langle t,\cdot\rangle

and note that
\|\cdot\|_{T}
is a norm if
T
is symmetric and spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a vector
X
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
D
and
\partial D
are the unit ball and the unit sphere in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},L_{2}(X))
, respectively.

Finally, absolute constants are denoted by
C
,
c
, etc. These are positive numbers that are independent of any of the parameters of the problem. Their values may change from line to line. If a constant depends on some parameter
\rho
, we specify that by writing
c(\rho)
. We use
a\lesssim b
if there is an absolute constant
c
for which
a\leq cb
, and
a\sim b
if
c_{1}b\leq a\leq c_{2}b
for absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. We denote by
\kappa,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},...
constants whose values remain unchanged throughout this article.

This article is organized as follows. The proof of Theorem
1.8
is presented in Section
2
.
The proof of Theorem
1.6
requires some technical machinery, that is developed in Sections
3
and
4
. In Section
3
, we construct a data dependent function
\widehat{\psi}
that identifies for points in
S^{d-1}
whether the corresponding marginals have “large or small” variance (in a sense to be clarified in what follows). In Section
4
, we show how such a function
\widehat{\psi}
can be used to decompose
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to subspaces
E
and
E^{\perp}
corresponding to “large” and “small” directions. The proof of Theorem
1.6
is relatively simple for directions in either
E
or
E^{\perp}
. The more subtle part is proving the claim for directions that are a “mixture”, which is done in Section
5
using a chaining argument.

2
Lower Bounds

As noted previously, identifying the best possible performance of a directional-dependent covariance estimator is nontrivial, and this section is devoted to that goal. Specifically, exploring how big the function
S
has to be if one is to have any hope of establishing (
1.7
) under the minimal assumptions on
X
from Assumption
1.5
.

Recall that the eigenvalues of
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{d}
, and its effective rank is
r(\Sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{1}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
be the class of centred
d
-dimensional random vectors whose covariance matrix is
\Sigma
.

Our starting point is a minimax lower bound on the performance of covariance estimation procedure from
[
8
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

There exist absolute constants
\beta
and
\kappa_{1}
for which the following holds. For any positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{X\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X}\|_{2\to 2}\geq\kappa_{1}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{r(\Sigma)}{N}}\right)\geq\beta,

(2.1)

where the infimum is over all covariance estimators
\widehat{\Sigma}
.

Thanks to Theorem
2.1
we can establish the next fact, which will prove to be useful in what follows.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\kappa_{1}
and
\beta
as in Theorem
2.1
. For every dimension
d
, covariance estimation procedure
\phi
, and invertible, positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, there is a centred random vector
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
for which

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\phi(Y_{1},...,Y_{N},\delta)\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{k}\right\|_{2\to 2}\geq\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}\right)\geq\beta.

Proof.

Let
\phi
be a covariance estimation procedure. For any invertible, positive semi-definite matrix
A
, set

\phi_{A}(X_{1},...,X_{N},\delta)=A^{-1}\phi(AX_{1},...,AX_{N},\delta)A^{-1},

which is also a covariance estimation procedure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Theorem
2.1
for
\mathcal{P}_{I_{d}}
(i.e., the set of random vectors whose covariance is the identity), there exists a random vector
Z=Z_{A}\in\mathcal{P}_{I_{d}}
satisfying that with probability at least
\beta
,

\|\phi_{A}(Z_{1},...,Z_{N},\delta)-I_{d}\|_{2\to 2}=\|A^{-1}\phi(AZ_{1},...,AZ_{N},\delta)A^{-1}-I_{d}\|_{2\to 2}\geq\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}.

(2.2)

Next, consider an invertible positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma
, let
Y=\Sigma^{1/2}Z
and note that the covariance of
Y
is
\Sigma
. Using (
2.2
) for
A=\Sigma^{1/2}
and
Z=Z_{A}
, we have that

\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\phi(Y_{1},...,Y_{N},\delta)\Sigma^{-1/2}-I_{d}\|_{2\to 2}\geq\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}},

as claimed.
∎

Next, note that an estimate like (
1.7
) is nontrivial only when

S<\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

We will show that ‘beating’
\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
(when that is possible), comes at a price: an additional ‘global term’. The argument presented here is essentially the same as in
[
11
, Proposition 1]
, adapted to the covariance case. To formulate it, let
\beta
and
\kappa_{1}
be the constants from Corollary
2.2
, and recall that
\partial D
is the unit sphere in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},L_{2}(X))
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that there is a constant
\gamma
for which the following holds. If there exists an estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\delta)
that satisfies for every
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
and
\delta\leq\min\{\beta,e^{-1}\}
, that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists u\in S^{d-1}:\ u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},\delta)-\Sigma_{X})u\geq\gamma\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+S\right)\leq\delta,

(2.3)

then there are constants
c_{1}
and
\kappa_{0}(\gamma,\kappa_{1})
for which either

S\geq c_{1}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}\quad\text{or}\quad S\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{{\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.4
.

In what follows we will encounter several terms of the form
C\log(1/\delta)
. To ease notation, we shall assume without loss of generality that these are integers.

Proof.

Let
(e_{i})_{i\leq d}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set
c_{1}>0
. Assume without loss of generality that
\Sigma
is an invertible diagonal matrix with eigenvectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
, and consider the case
S\leq c_{1}\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}
.

Let
\ell
be the unique integer for which

S\in(c_{1}\lambda_{\ell+1}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N},c_{1}\lambda_{\ell}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}]

and set
U_{\ell}:=\text{span}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell})
. It is evident that, for every
u\in B_{2}^{d}\cap U_{\ell}
,

\begin{split}&S\leq c_{1}\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N}\quad\text{and}\\
&\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\frac{u^{T}\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)u}{\sigma^{2}(u)}\leq(c_{1}+\gamma)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}.\end{split}

(2.5)

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}}
be the principal
\ell\times\ell
submatrix of
\widehat{\Sigma}
, set
\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}:=\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
and let
I_{\ell}
be the identity matrix on
U_{\ell}
.
Clearly, for every
u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}
,
\sigma(u)=\|\bigl<X,u\bigr>\|_{L_{2}}=\|\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}u\|_{2}
,
\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}u\in U_{\ell}
and

\begin{split}&\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(u)}u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u=\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\left(\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}\frac{u}{\sigma(u)},\frac{u}{\sigma(u)}\right\rangle-1\right)\\
&=\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\left(\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}}\frac{u}{\|\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{1/2}u\|_{2}},\frac{u}{\|\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{1/2}u\|_{2}}\right\rangle-1\right)=(*).\end{split}

(2.6)

Moreover,

\left\{\frac{u}{\|\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}u\|_{2}}:u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}\right\}=U_{\ell}\cap\partial D=\{\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}v:v\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}\},

and therefore,

(*)=\sup_{v\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\left(\langle\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}}\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}v,\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}v\rangle-1\right)\leq\|\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}}\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}-I_{\ell}\|_{2\to 2}.

By Corollary
2.2
there is
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
for which, with probability at least
\beta
,

\|\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}}\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}^{-1/2}-I_{\ell}\|_{2\to 2}\geq\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{N}},

and thus

\sqrt{N}\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(u)}u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u\geq\kappa_{1}\sqrt{\ell}.

Recalling that by (
2.5
), with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sqrt{N}\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}\cap S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(u)}u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u\leq(c_{1}+\gamma)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)},

and that
\delta\leq\beta
, we have that

\ell\leq\left(\frac{c_{1}+\gamma}{\kappa_{1}}\right)\log(1/\delta).

Next, by the choice of
\ell
,

\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u\leq 2S.

(2.7)

One may follow the same argument used previously, this time for
U_{\ell}^{\perp}
instead of
U_{\ell}
;
\widehat{\Sigma}_{U_{\ell}^{\perp}}
(the
d-\ell\times d-\ell
submatrix formed by the last
d-\ell
rows and columns of
\widehat{\Sigma}
) instead of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\ell}
; and
\Sigma_{U_{\ell}^{\perp}}:=\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda_{\ell+1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
instead of
\Sigma_{U_{\ell}}
. Since
\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}
maps
U_{\ell}^{\perp}
onto itself,

\sup_{u\in U_{\ell}^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}u^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u=\sup_{v\in S^{n-\ell-1}}v^{T}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{d-\ell}-\Sigma_{U_{\ell}^{\perp}})v.

Moreover,
\delta\leq e^{-1}
, and therefore
\ell+1\leq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)
. Finally,
\|\Sigma_{U_{\ell}^{\perp}}\|_{2\rightarrow 2}=\lambda_{\ell+1}
and the effective rank satisfies
r(\Sigma_{U_{\ell}^{\perp}})=\sum_{i\geq\ell+1}\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{\ell+1}
. Setting
\kappa_{0}:=(c_{1}+\gamma)/\kappa_{1}+1
, it follows from (
2.7
) and Theorem
2.1
that

S\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell+1}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\ell+1}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\geq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}.

∎

The lower bound from Theorem
2.3
is not sharp. As it happens, another term is necessary. To see that, consider
\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)u,u\rangle=\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{2\rightarrow 2}
. If the lower bound on
S
were sharp, there would have been a covariance estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}
, for which, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\lesssim\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}.

(2.8)

However, it follows from (
1.6
) and (
2.1
) that the best one can hope for (in the uniform sense over
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
) is

\begin{split}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{2\rightarrow 2}&\sim\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\sigma^{2}(u)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\\
&=\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\\
&\geq\lambda_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{N}}.\end{split}

(2.9)

Thus, for any
\delta<\beta
, any estimator

On the other hand, observe that if

\lambda_{1}\ll\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\lambda_{i}}{\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)}\ll\lambda_{1},

The analysis presented here relies on a sharp lower bound from

Theorem 2.5
.

There exists an absolute constant
\kappa_{2}
for which the following holds. Consider an estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\delta)
for the means of the gaussian random vectors
G

(2.12)

Corollary 2.6
.

There exists an absolute constant
\kappa_{2}^{\prime}
for which the following holds. Consider an estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\delta)
for the means of gaussian random vectors
G

(2.14)

We leave the straightforward proof to the Appendix.

Now with all the necessary ingredients in place, we can present a lower bound that complements Theorem
2.3
. Recall that
\kappa_{0}
is the constant from Theorem
2.3
, fix a subspace
E^{\perp}
of co-dimension
\kappa_{0}\log(1/\delta)
, and define
P_{E^{\perp}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow E^{\perp}
to be the orthogonal projection onto
E^{\perp}
. Also, for
u\in E\cap S^{d-1}
let
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma,u}^{\prime}
be the set of all random vectors
X
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and whose covariance matrix
\Sigma_{X}
satisfies

u^{T}\Sigma_{X}u=u^{T}\Sigma u\quad\text{and}\quad\tr(P_{E^{\perp}}\Sigma_{X}P_{E^{\perp}}^{T})\leq\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}}).

(2.15)

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\kappa_{2}^{\prime}
be as in Corollary
2.6
and
\gamma\kappa_{2}^{\prime}/3
. Set
N\geq 18
. Let
\widehat{\Sigma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},\delta)
be an estimator which satisfies that for every

and
\delta\in(0,1/8)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{v\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}|\langle\widehat{\Sigma}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},\delta)v,v\rangle-\langle\Sigma_{X}v,v\rangle|\geq\gamma\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}\right)\leq\delta.

(2.16)

Then there is
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma,u}^{\prime}
for which, with probability at least
\delta
,

\begin{split}&\sup_{v\in(E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1})\cup\{0\}}|\langle\widehat{\Sigma}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},\delta)(u+v),u+v\rangle-\langle\Sigma_{X}(u+v),u+v\rangle|\\
&\geq\gamma\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}.\end{split}

(2.17)

Proof.

The first step of the proof is to construct a suitable collection of centred vectors
X_{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma,u}^{\prime}
. To that end, set

R^{\prime}:=3\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}},

and fix a gaussian random vector
\tilde{G}
, taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\perp}|}
and whose covariance matrix
\widetilde{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\perp}|\times|E^{\perp}|}
satisfies that

\tr(\widetilde{\Sigma})=\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})-\frac{R^{\prime 2}}{\sigma^{2}(u)}.

(2.18)

Since

\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})-\frac{R^{\prime 2}}{\sigma^{2}(u)}=\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})-9\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}\geq\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})>0,

there is a valid choice of
\tilde{G}
. Fix
\mu\in R^{\prime}B_{2}^{E^{\perp}}
and define
X_{\mu}
through its projections on
E
and
E^{\perp}
as follows.

Firstly, let
\varepsilon
be a Rademacher random variable (i.e., symmetric,
\{-1,1\}
valued) that is independent of
\tilde{G}
. The law of the orthogonal projection of
X_{\mu}
onto
E^{\perp}
is

P_{E^{\perp}}X_{\mu}:=\tilde{G}+\frac{1}{\sigma(u)}\varepsilon\mu.

Secondly, to define the law of the orthogonal projection of
X_{\mu}
onto
E
, let
u_{2},\ldots,u_{|E|}
be unit vectors for which the set
\{u,u_{2},\ldots,u_{|E|}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
E
. Consider independent Rademacher random variables
\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{|E|}
and the variances
\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{|E|}
of
\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{|E|}
, respectively. The law of
P_{E}X_{\mu}
is given by

P_{E}X_{\mu}:=\varepsilon\sigma(u)u+\sum_{i=2}^{|E|}\varepsilon_{i}\sigma_{i}u_{i},

and by the natural embedding of
P_{E}X_{\mu}
and
P_{E^{\perp}}X_{\mu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the law of
X_{\mu}
is defined by
X_{\mu}:=P_{E}X_{\mu}+P_{E^{\perp}}X_{\mu}
. Let us verify that
X_{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma,u}^{\prime}
. Clearly,

\langle X_{\mu},u\rangle=\langle P_{E}X_{\mu},u\rangle=\sigma(u)\varepsilon,

and then
\mathbb{E}\langle X,u\rangle^{2}=\sigma^{2}(u)=u^{T}\Sigma_{X}u
. It is straightforward to verify that (
2.18
) implies that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}(X_{\mu}\otimes X_{\mu})

satisfies that
\tr(P_{E^{\perp}}\Sigma_{\mu}P_{E^{\perp}}^{T})\leq\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})
, therefore
X_{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma,u}^{\prime}
.

Next, observe that for every vector
X_{\mu}
with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{{\mu}}
, we have that

\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})(u+v),u+v\rangle=\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})u,u\rangle+\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})v,v\rangle+2\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})u,v\rangle,

(2.19)

and if (
2.17
) does not hold, then it follows that for every
X_{\mu}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{v\in(E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1})\cup\{0\}}\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{\mu})(u+v),u+v\rangle\leq\gamma\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}.

(2.20)

Moreover, since
\delta<1/8
, (
2.16
) and (
2.20
) hold together with probability at least

1-(\delta+\delta)>3/4.

On that event, invoking (
2.19
),

\begin{split}&\sup_{v\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})u,v\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})u,u\rangle|\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{v\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}\left(|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})v,v\rangle|+|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})(u+v),u+v\rangle|\right)\\
&\leq\sup_{v\in(E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1})\cup\{0\}}|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})(u+v),u+v\rangle|+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{v\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma_{{\mu}}}-\Sigma_{{\mu}})v,v\rangle|\\
&\leq\gamma\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{2}^{\prime}\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{N}}:=r^{\ast},\end{split}

(2.21)

where the last step follows from the fact that
\gamma<\kappa^{\prime}_{2}/3
.

Thus, to establish (
2.17
), it suffices to show that (
2.21
) cannot hold. Indeed, notice that

\langle X,u\rangle P_{E^{\perp}}X=\sigma(u)\tilde{G}+\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}(u)\widetilde{\Sigma}),

(2.22)

and that (
2.21
) implies that for every
\mu\in R^{\prime}B_{2}^{|E^{\perp}|}

\begin{split}&\frac{3}{4}<\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{v\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}}|\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu}-\Sigma_{\mu})u,v\rangle|\leq r^{\ast}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\|P_{E^{\perp}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu}u-\mathbb{E}\langle X_{\mu},u\rangle(P_{E^{\perp}}X_{\mu})\|_{\ell_{2}(E^{\perp})}\leq r^{\ast}\right).\end{split}

It follows that
P_{E^{\perp}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\mu}u
is a mean estimation procedure with respect to Euclidean norm in
(\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\perp}|},\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{2}(E^{\perp})})
. More accurately, invoking (

) the procedure estimates with accuracy at least
r^{\ast}
and confidence at least
3/4
the mean of every gaussian random vector of the form
\langle X_{\mu},u\rangle P_{E^{\perp}}X_{\mu}
that takes value in
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\perp}|}
. This includes all gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\perp}|}
that have mean
\mu\in R^{\prime}B_{2}^{|E^{\perp}|}
and covariance
\sigma^{2}(u)\tilde{\Sigma}
. Therefore by Corollary
2.6
followed by (

),

r^{\ast}\geq\kappa_{2}^{\prime}\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\tilde{\Sigma})}{N}}\geq\kappa_{2}^{\prime}\sigma(u)\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\Sigma_{E^{\perp}})}{2N}},

which clearly contradicts (
2.21
).
∎

The proof of Theorem
1.8
follows easily from Theorem

and Theorem

.

3
The
L_{2}
Isomorphic Distance Oracle

Here we take the first steps towards the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
In what follows, all the constants will either be absolute, or depend only on the
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence constant
\kappa
. However, to make it easier to keep track them, we will, at times, mention whether the constants depend on each other as well (i.e., even though
c=c(\kappa)
and
C=C(\kappa)
, we may write
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(c,C)
).

Our aim here is to construct an
L_{2}
(isomorphic) distance oracle: a (random) function
\widehat{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
that satisfies the following: With high probability with respect to the
N
-product measure endowed by X, for every
u,v\in S^{d-1}
,
\widehat{\psi}
returns a “good guess” of the distance
\|u-v\|_{L_{2}}=\left({\mathbb{E}}\bigl<X,u-v\bigr>^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. Naturally, the best that one can hope for is that

c^{-1}\|u-v\|_{L_{2}}\leq\widehat{\psi}(u-v)\leq c\|u-v\|_{L_{2}},

for a constant
c
that is close to
1
. Such a distance oracle is
almost isometric
, whereas for an
isomorphic
distance oracle, the constant
c
can be a large absolute constant.

As it happens, a weaker notion suffices for our purposes, as described in Proposition
3.2
. In fact, we only use the distance oracle in the next section to identify a high-dimensional section of the Euclidean sphere
E\cap S^{d-1}
, on which
\sigma^{2}(u)
is large, while for
u\in E^{\perp}\cap S^{d-1}
,
\sigma^{2}(u)
is small — and the meaning of ‘large’ and ‘small’ is clarified in what follows. Finding an accurate estimate on
\sigma^{2}(u)
that holds for every
u\in E
is possible only if the dimension of
E
is at most
\kappa_{E}n
, for some suitable (small) constant
\kappa_{E}
and the choice of
E
is explained in Section
4
. We set
\rho
to be a free parameter that ranges from
0
to
\kappa_{0}
. The choice of
\rho
is specified in what follows.

Set
n=\log(1/\delta)
, recall that
D
is the unit ball in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},L_{2}(X))
and that the random vector
X
satisfies an
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
\kappa
. Also, recall that
\kappa_{0}
is the constant from Theorem
2.3
— and is an absolute constant.

Remark 3.1
.

Our goal is to produce a covariance estimator that is also an optimal one-dimensional procedure for directions in a subspace spanned by the eigenvectors corresponding to the largest
\sim n
singular values of
\Sigma
(of course, without having prior knowledge on the identity of that subspace). A far weaker constraint — that the performance of the estimator is better than the trivial one on that subspace—, has immediate consequences. Indeed, let
u\in S^{d-1}
that satisfies
\sigma^{2}(u)=\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}
. By (
1.9
), the best the performance of
\widehat{\Sigma}
as a one-dimensional estimator in the direction
u
that one can hope for is that with probability at least
1-e^{-n}
,

\begin{split}&\langle(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{X})u,u\rangle\\
&\leq\kappa_{low}\left(\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}n}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\right).\end{split}

Thus,
\widehat{\Sigma}
is outperformed in the direction
u
by the trivial estimator
0
unless

\kappa_{low}\sqrt{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}n}\lambda_{i}}{N}}<\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}\quad\text{and}\quad N\geq\kappa_{low}^{2}n.

(3.1)

Hence, to have any hope of obtaining a procedure that is ‘nontrivial’ in the entire subspace spanned by the
\kappa_{0}n
”largest directions”, (
3.1
) must hold. To that end, we assume that
N
satisfies

N\geq\widetilde{\kappa}\max\left\{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}n}\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}},n\right\},

for some constant
\widetilde{\kappa}
that is specified in what follows.

Next, let us describe the features of
\widehat{\psi}
that we require.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
0<\rho\leq\kappa_{0}
, there exist constants
\theta(\kappa),\kappa_{3}(\rho,\kappa)
, and a function
\widehat{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which the following holds: if
\widetilde{\kappa}\geq\kappa_{3}\theta^{-3/2}
then with probability at least
1-2e^{-\theta^{2}N/16}
,

1.

For
u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D
,
\widehat{\psi}(u)\leq\frac{16}{\theta^{2}}\lambda_{\rho n}
.

2.

For
u\in B_{2}^{d}
that satisfies
\sigma^{2}(u)\geq\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}
, we have
\widehat{\psi}(u)\geq\frac{\theta^{3/2}}{4\sqrt{2}(1-\theta)}\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}(u)
.

Note that
\rho\leq\kappa_{0}
, therefore every
u\in S^{d-1}
satisfies either (1) or (2),(where for
u
with
\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}\leq\sigma^{2}(u)\leq\lambda_{\rho n}
both (1) and (2) hold).

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\rho
will be an absolute constant,
\theta
and
\kappa_{3}
— and therefore
\tilde{\kappa}
as well — depend only on the norm equivalence constant
\kappa
.

For
u\in S^{d-1}
, let
J_{+}(u)
be the set of the
\theta N
largest indices of
(\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{2})_{i=1}^{N}
. Define
\widehat{\psi}
by

\widehat{\psi}(u)=\frac{1}{N(1-\theta)}\sum_{i\in[N]/J_{+}(u)}\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{2},

which, at least intuitively, should be close to
\sigma^{2}(u)
for every
u\in S^{d-1}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
(1).
The heart of the argument is showing that

\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\left(\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{2}\geq\frac{16}{\theta^{2}}\lambda_{\rho n}\right)\leq\frac{\theta}{2}.

(3.2)

Indeed, set

\xi(x):=\begin{cases}\ 0,\quad\text{if}\ x<2\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}/\theta\\
\ \frac{\theta x}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}}-1,\quad\text{if}\ 2\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}/\theta\leq x\leq 4\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}/\theta\\
\ 1,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Clearly,

\mathds{1}\left(x\geq\frac{4\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}}{\theta}\right)\leq\xi(x)\leq\mathds{1}\left(x\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}}{\theta}\right)

and
\xi
is a
\theta/2\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}
-Lipschitz function that passes through the origin. By the Giné-Zinn symmetrization theorem
[
5
]
and the contraction principle for Bernoulli processes (see e.g.
[
7
]
), we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\left(\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{2}\geq\frac{16}{\theta^{2}}\lambda_{\rho n}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi(|\langle X_{i},u\rangle|)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{2}{N}\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\xi(|\langle X_{i},u\rangle|)\right|+\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\mathbb{E}\xi(|\langle X,u\rangle|)\\
&\leq\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle X_{i},u\rangle\right|+\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\mathbb{E}\xi(|\langle X,u\rangle|)\\
&=(I)+(II)\end{split}

By Markov’s inequality,

(II)\leq\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\lambda_{\rho n}}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\sigma^{2}(u)<\frac{\theta}{4}.

The estimate on (I) follows from the same arguments as in
[
11
]
: observe that
D=\Sigma^{-1/2}B_{2}^{d}
and that the random vector
W=\Sigma^{-1/2}X
is isotropic. Clearly,
\Sigma^{1/2}B_{2}^{d}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}B_{2}^{d}
is contained in an ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
whose principal axes are proportional to
\min\{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}\}
, and setting
W_{i}:=\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{i}
, it is evident that there is an absolute constant
C_{1}
for which

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle X_{i},u\rangle\right|=\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in B_{2}^{d}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}\Sigma^{-1/2}B_{2}^{d}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle W_{i},\Sigma^{1/2}u\rangle\right|\\
&=\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in\Sigma^{1/2}B_{2}^{d}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}B_{2}^{d}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle W_{i},v\rangle\right|\\
&\leq C_{1}\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle W_{i},v\rangle\right|\\
&\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min\{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{\rho n}\}}{N}}\\
&\leq C_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\rho n\lambda_{\rho n}}{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\geq\rho n}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\right)\\
&\leq C_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\rho n\lambda_{\rho n}}{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\rho n}(\kappa_{0}-\rho)n+\sum_{i\geq\kappa_{0}n}\lambda_{i}}{N}}\right)\end{split}

(3.3)

By the assumption on
N
(see Remark
3.1
) and since
\lambda_{\rho n}\geq\lambda_{\kappa_{0}n}
, we may set

\widetilde{\kappa}\geq 128C_{1}^{2}\max\{\kappa_{0},1\},

(3.4)

thus ensuring that

\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle X_{i},v\rangle\right|\leq\frac{\theta}{8}+\frac{\theta}{8}=\frac{\theta}{4},

and establishing (
3.2
).

The wanted high probability estimate follows from the bounded difference inequality (see e.g.
[
16
]
): for every
\theta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-e^{-\theta^{2}N/8}
,

\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\left(\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{2}\geq\frac{16}{\theta^{2}}\lambda_{\rho n}\right)\leq\theta N.

Thus, on that event

\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}\cap\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho n}}D}\widehat{\psi}(u)\leq\frac{16}{\theta^{2}}\lambda_{\rho n}.

Turning to the second part of the proof, our starting point here is from
[

]
(see also

).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
A>0
be a positive number. For

If

then with probability at least

,

\inf_{u\in T}\frac{A}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}(|\langle X_{i},u\rangle|\geq A)\geq A\inf_{u\in T}Q_{2A}(u)-\frac{2}{N}\mathbb{E}\sup_{u\in T}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}\langle X_{i},u\rangle\right|-A\frac{t}{\sqrt{N}}.

(3.5)

Proof of Proposition
3.2
(2).

For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, set
x^{\ast}
to be the non-decreasing rearrangement of
(|x_{i}|)_{i=1}^{N}
, in particular,
x_{1}^{\ast}\leq\ldots\leq x_{N}^{\ast}
. Also, recall that
\partial D
denotes the unit sphere in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},L_{2}(X))

Step 1 - nontrivial

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{(1-\theta)N}\sum_{i=1}^{(1-\theta)N}(\langle X_{i},u\rangle^{\ast})^{2}\\

, setting

In this appendix, we prove Corollary

.

Proof.

Let

\sim\mathcal{N}(0,N^{-1}\Sigma)

T=B_{2}^{d}

(5.18)

then
