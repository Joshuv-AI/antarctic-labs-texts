Selina Burkhard, Benjamin Dörich, Marlis Hochbruck, Caroline Lasser

Abstract
. In the present paper we consider the semiclassical magnetic Schrödinger equation, which describes the dynamics of particles under the influence of a magnetic field. The solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is approximated by a single Gaussian wave packet via the time-dependent Dirac–Frenkel variational principle. For the approximation we derive ordinary differential equations of motion for the parameters of the variational solution. Moreover, we prove L 2 L^{2} -error bounds and observable error bounds for the approximating Gaussian wave packet.



1.
Introduction

In the present paper we study
the semiclassical magnetic Schrödinger equation

(1.1a)

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}\psi(t)=H(t)\psi(t),\quad\psi(0)=\psi_{0},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with magnetic Hamiltonian

(1.1b)

H(t)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla_{x}+A(t,x)\bigr)^{2}+V(t,x),

and initial value
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with semiclassical parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. Here,
A
is a magnetic vector potential, and
V
is the electric potential. This equation arises in the modeling of the quantum dynamics of nuclei in a molecule subject to external magnetic fields.
From a numerical point of view, solving this time-dependent partial differential equation raises three major problems. First, it is a high-dimensional problem, since the space dimension is typically given by
d=3N
, where

N
is the number of nuclear particles in the system. Further, the computational domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is naturally unbounded, and thus most numerical methods require truncation before discretization. For the method of lines (first discretize space, then time), high dimension combined with an unbounded domain leads to
inadequately if not unattractably large systems that have to be integrated in time.
Another challenge is given by the high oscillations induced by the small semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
. For standard time integration schemes severe stepsize restrictions have to be imposed and leave these methods impracticable.

We consider the case that the initial value
\psi_{0}
is strongly localized and given by a Gaussian wave packet,

(1.2)

\psi_{0}(x)=\exp{\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(x-q)^{T}\mathcal{C}(x-q)+(x-q)^{T}p+\zeta\bigr)\Bigr)},

where
q,p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the packet’s position and momentum center,
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
is the width matrix of the envelope, and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
a phase and weight parameter.
For
A=0
it is well established that it is possible to reasonably approximate the solution by a Gaussian wave packet with parameters that are evolved according to ordinary differential equations.
First studies in this direction are due to K. Hepp
[
21
]
and G. Hagedorn
[
13
]
from the perspective of mathematical physics, and E. Heller
[
19
,
20
]
as well as R. Coalson, M. Karplus
[
6
]
with already an eye on numerical computation. The evolution equations for the parameters of all Gaussian wave packet approximations can be classified in two categories:

Variational:
The variational approach
relies on
the time-dependent Dirac–Frenkel principle
for deriving the parameter equations of motion. By the variational construction, the Gaussian wave packet automatically inherits several conservation properties of the exact solution.

Semiclassical:
The semiclassical approach expands the wave packet ansatz with respect to the
semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
and derives
\varepsilon
independent parameter equations by matching terms
with the same order.

Both types of ordinary differential equations have the advantageous property, that their solutions are non-oscillatory.
Both approximations have the same convergence order with respect to the semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
in
L^{2}
-norm, and both reproduce the exact solution for the special case of Schrödinger operators with linear magnetic potential
A
and quadratic electric potential
V
.
For a further discussion, we refer to
[
5
, Chapter 10.2]
for a monograph that covers the semiclassical construction, to
[
25
, Chapter II.4]
or
[
24
, Chapter 3]
for a short book and a review presenting the variational case, and to
[
31
]
for a general presentation of Gaussian wave packet dynamics.

Contributions of the paper

Our main contribution in this paper is to first show that for the magnetic Schrödinger equation the variational approximation is still given by a system of ordinary differential equations for the parameters defining the Gaussian wave packet.
Second, we prove rigorous error bounds for this approximation on finite time intervals
[0,T]
in terms of the semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
.
The presented results generalize the bounds established in
[
25
,
24
]
to non-vanishing magnetic potentials
A

and further allow for time-dependencies in both the electric and the magnetic potential.
We also treat the more general case where the dynamics are generated by the Weyl quantization
of a smooth and subquadratic Hamiltonian function.
This includes convergence in the
L^{2}
-norm with order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
as well as for expectation values of observables,
which resemble certain measurable physical quantities of the wave function,
with order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
. These estimates extend and improve the observable bound of
[
24
, Theorem 3.5]
and the result of
[
28
]
from the case of vanishing magnetic potential.
Let us point out that the design and the analysis of time integrators for the magnetic variational equations of motion are currently under investigation.

Further wave packet results for
A=0

Hagedorn wave packets
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
are a multivariate anisotropic generalization of the Hermite functions. They are Gaussian wave packets with a polynomial prefactor, such that a family of them
constitutes an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In
[
8
,
10
,
1
]
, time splitting integrators
for Hagedorn wave packet approximations are proposed, that combine parameter propagation by ordinary differential equations with a Galerkin step. A spawning method for several families of Hagedorn wave packets is introduced in
[
29
]
.
For variational Gaussian wave packets, a time splitting integrator, which is robust in the semiclassical parameter
\varepsilon
, is proposed in
[
9
]
.
Recently in
[
28
]
, T. Oshawa has analysed the expectation values of position and momentum for a variational Gaussian wave packet and proved
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3/2})
accuracy.
Our results here generalize and improve this error bound in two ways: First, we allow for general sublinear observables. Second, our method of proof shows
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
observable accuracy also for the case
A\neq 0
. It is worthwhile emphasizing, that from the perspective of the observable error variational Gaussians are more accurate than their semiclassical counterparts.

Related wave packet results for
A\neq 0

The most general result for the semiclassical wave packet approach is given in

[
30
, Theorem 21]
of the monograph by D. Robert and M. Combescure.
There, the propagation of Gaussian and Hagedorn wave packets is covered for a general class of
time-dependent Hamiltonian operators
H(t)
, that includes the magnetic Schrödinger operator.
The error analysis is with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm, but not for observables. The semiclassical construction there also receives corrections, such that it can be accurate to order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{k/2})

for any
k\geq 1
.
In
[
2
]
, magnetic Schrödinger operators with polynomially bounded, time-independent magnetic
fields and zero potential are considered. The initial coherent state has zero initial energy and its propagation
is analysed for the long-time horizon
[0,T/\varepsilon]
.
In
[
23
]
, N. King and T. Ohsawa derive the equations of motion for variational Gaussians in the presence of a magnetic field. They conduct numerical experiments for the expectation value of the position and the momentum operator suggesting that the variational Gaussians are more accurate than the semiclassical ones.
An extension of the Hagedorn Galerkin method
[
8
]
to the case of magnetic Schrödinger equations is studied in
[
35
]
, including an error analysis with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm. However, no error bounds for the observables are investigated there.
For linear magnetic potentials of a particular structure, in
[
11
]
a problem adapted splitting method for Hagedorn wave packets is derived but without error analysis.
A slightly different approach, called the Gaussian wave packet transform, is proposed for the magnetic Schrödinger equation in
[
34
]
. There, the ordinary differential equations for the Gaussian parameters are the semiclassical ones except for an additional term for the scalar parameter
\zeta
.

2.
General setting

We first discuss the analytic framework for our analysis and introduce the Gaussian wave packets. We further call some results on the wellposedness from the literature.
For the vector potential we choose the
Coulomb gauge, i.e.
\operatorname{div}A=0
.
In order to shorten notation, we rewrite the Hamiltonian in (

(2.1)

H(t)=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon A(t)\cdot\nabla+\widetilde{V}(t),\qquad{\widetilde{V}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}|A|^{2}+V}.

Throughout this paper we make the following smoothness and growth assumption on the potentials.

Assumption 2.1
.

The scalar potential
\widetilde{V}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

and the vector valued potential

is subquadratic, i.e.

is bounded for all

, and

is sublinear, i.e.

is bounded for all

, and satisfies

If in addition to

, we assume that

is sublinear, then it can be shown that the initial value problem (
1.1a
)

[

or the remarks after
[

or
[

.
In particular,
the following wellposedness result on the unitarity of the time evolution guarantees that the norm of the solution of (
1.1a
) is the same as the one of the initial data. However, for our analysis here, only

will be used.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

is sublinear.
There exists a unitary evolution family

A further remarkable property of Gaussian wave packets is the conservation of several physical quantities. In the following, we recall the definitions of the linear and angular momentum for quantum dynamical systems.

3.5.
Observable error bound

In classical mechanics physical states are described by the position and momentum parameters
{\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Observables are functions depending smoothly on
({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see, for example,
[
18
,
32
]
. Classical mechanics can be linked to quantum mechanics via Weyl quantization, which asigns a classical observable to a quantum mechanical one using semiclassical Fourier transformation, cf.
[
7
, Thm. 4.14]
or
[
26
,
18
]
. Formally, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an observable
\bm{{a}}
, we define

(3.27)

\displaystyle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{{a}})\varphi(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\bm{{a}}\Bigl(\frac{x+{\widetilde{q}}}{2},{\widetilde{p}}\Bigr)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\widetilde{p}}\cdot(x-{\widetilde{q}})/\varepsilon}\varphi({\widetilde{q}})}\,\mathrm{d}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}}).

The Weyl quantization of the projections to the first or second component of the classical variables are

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{p}})\varphi=-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla\varphi\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{q}})\varphi=x\varphi.

Further examples of physically relevant observables stemming from classical symbols are

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(|{\widetilde{p}}|^{2})\psi(x)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\psi(x)

and, due to
\operatorname{div}A=0
,

(3.30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(A({\widetilde{q}})\cdot{\widetilde{p}})\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(A(x)\cdot(-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla)+(-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla)\cdot A(x)\right)\psi(x)

(3.31)

\displaystyle=\left(A(x)\cdot(-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla)\right)\psi(x),

and, of course,

(3.32)

\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(h(t))\psi(x)=H(t)\psi(x)

for the Hamiltonian function (
3.2
) and the magnetic Schrödinger operator (
1.1b
).
An observable
\mathbf{A}=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{{a}})
defines for an
L^{2}
-normalised function

\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
an expectation value,

(3.33)

\bigl\langle\varphi|\mathbf{A}\varphi\bigr\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{\varphi(x)}(\mathbf{A}\varphi)(x)\mathrm{d}x,

and we investigate how expectation values issued by the variational approximation
u(t)
differ from the ones of the true solution
\psi(t)
. For an error estimate relying on
L^{2}
bounds, we have to restrict ourselves to sublinear classical observables.

Definition 3.9
.

The class of sublinear classical symbols is defined as smooth functions
\bm{{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}_{0}
with
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\geq 1
there exists
C_{\alpha}>0

(3.34)

\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}\bm{{a}}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})\right\rvert\leq C_{\alpha}

for all
({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For the expectation values of classical sublinear observables, we obtain the following error
estimate that generalizes and improves the findings of N. King and T. Ohsawa
[
23
,
28
]
, where asymptotic accuracy of the order
\varepsilon^{3/2}
has been observed and proved for the variational position and momentum expectation value.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\psi,u
be the solution of (
1.1a
) and (
2.5
), respectively,
and let
u_{0}
satisfy (
3.1
).
Moreover, let
\mathbf{A}=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{{a}})
be an observable stemming from a classical sublinear observable
\bm{{a}}
in the sense of
Definition

3.9

such that
\bm{{a}}\circ\Phi^{t,s}
is sublinear.
Then we have the error bound

(3.35)

\bigl|\bigl\langle\psi(t)|\mathbf{A}\psi(t)\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle u(t)|\mathbf{A}u(t)\bigr\rangle\bigr|\leq t\,c\,\varepsilon^{2},

for all
t\in[0,T]
. The error constant

depends on the parameter bounds of
Lemma

3.7
for the time-interval
[0,T]
, in particular on
the bounds for the width matrix
\mathcal{C}
, on the potentials, and on
\bm{{a}}
, but is independent of
\varepsilon
and
t

Note that the convergence in the observables is of order

while the convergence in the

-norm presented in

The rest of the paper is devoted to the proofs of the equations of motion and the error estimates presented in this section.

4.1.
Equations of motion for a general Hamiltonian

The findings of

|\partial^{\alpha}h(t,{\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})|\leq C_{\alpha}

and

of unit norm,

\{\varphi_{n}\}_{|n|\leq 2}

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\varphi_{e_{j}+e_{k}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}}\left(P^{T}\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{q}}-q)-Q^{T}\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{p}}-p)\right),

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}

(4.28)

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)=\beta+b^{T}(x-q)+\frac{1}{2}(x-q)^{T}B(x-q)

(4.29)

\displaystyle\beta

an observable. We introduce the notation

(7.1)

\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)\coloneqq U(s,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,s),\qquad t,s\in\mathbb{R}.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\psi
be the solution of (
1.1a
) and
u
the solution of (
2.5
). If the initial value
\psi_{0}=u_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is a Gaussian wave packet with
\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1
, then the error of the observables takes the form

(7.2)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\psi(t)|\mathbf{A}\psi(t)\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle u(t)|\mathbf{A}u(t)\bigr\rangle

(7.3)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\bigl\langle u(s)\Big|\left(\overline{W}_{u(s)}\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)W_{u(s)}\right)u(s)\bigr\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where the remainder potential
W_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
depends on the Gaussian wave
packet
u
. In the general subquadratic case, it has been previously defined in (
5.22
).
In the magnetic Schrödinger case, it satisfies

(7.4)

\displaystyle W_{u}

\displaystyle=X_{u}(q)-\langle X_{u}\rangle_{u}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathrm{tr}({\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}^{-1}\langle\nabla^{2}X_{u}\rangle_{u})+\left(\nabla X_{u}(q)-\langle\nabla X_{u}\rangle_{u}\right)^{T}(x-q)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(x-q)^{T}\left(\nabla^{2}X_{u}(q)-\langle\nabla^{2}X_{u}\rangle_{u}\right)(x-q)+R(X_{u}),

with
X_{u}=Y_{u}+\widetilde{V}
defined in (
5.14
) and (
2.1
), respectively, and
R(X_{u})
being the remainder potential of the
quadratic Taylor expansion of
X_{u}
around the point
q
.
For the non-magnetic Schrödinger case
A=0
, we have
Y_{u}=0
and

W_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
U(t,s)
be the evolution family, such that the exact solution of (
1.1a
) is given by (
2.2
). Using
\psi_{0}=u_{0}
and
U(t,t)=\mathrm{Id}
we calculate

(7.5)

\displaystyle\langle u(t)|\mathbf{A}u(t)\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle\psi(t)|\mathbf{A}\psi(t)\rangle

(7.6)

\displaystyle=\langle u(t)|U(t,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,t)u(t)\rangle-\langle U(t,0)u(0)|\mathbf{A}U(t,0)u(0)\rangle

(7.7)

\displaystyle=\langle u(t)|U(t,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,t)u(t)\rangle-\langle u(0)|U(0,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,0)u(0)\rangle

(7.8)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}{\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\langle u(s)|\underbrace{U(s,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,s)}_{=\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)}u(s)\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Employing the differential properties of the evolution family, that is,

\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}U(t,s)=H(t)U(t,s)
and
-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}U(s,t)=U(s,t)H(t)
,
we obtain

(7.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\bigl(H(s)U(s,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,s)-U(s,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,s)H(s)\bigr)

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\bigl(H(s)\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)H(s)\bigr).

Since the variational evolution satisfies
\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}u(t)=P_{u(t)}H(t)u(t)
, we then have

(7.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\langle u(s)|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)u(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\left(\langle(\mathrm{Id}-P_{u(s)})H(s)u(s)|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)u(s)\rangle\right)

(7.12)

\displaystyle\left.-\langle u(s)|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)(\mathrm{Id}-P_{u(s)})H(s)u(s)\rangle\right).

We arrive at (
7.2
), using that

(7.13)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Id}-P_{u(s)})H(s)u(s)

\displaystyle=X_{u(s)}u(s)-P_{u(s)}(X_{u(s)}u(s))=W_{u(s)}u(s).

The claimed form of the remainder potential
W_{u(s)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
follows
from
[
24
, Proposition 3.14]
,
since the proof of the projection formula there also applies for
the potential function
X_{u(s)}
even though it is complex-valued.
∎

7.2.
Egorov’s theorem

Further, to prove
Theorem

3.10
we have to establish a variant of Egorov’s theorem, which connects the time-evolved quantum observable
\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)
, in case it originates from
a Weyl-quantized
\mathbf{A}=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{{a}})
, with the evolution map
of the classical Hamiltonian system.

Recall that since
A
and
\widetilde{V}
are sublinear and subquadratic, respectively, we obtain a unique global solution to the ordinary differential equation (
3.12
).
We denote by

(7.14)

\displaystyle\Phi^{t,s}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d},\quad({\widetilde{q}}_{s},{\widetilde{p}}_{s})\mapsto({\widetilde{q}}_{s}(t),{\widetilde{p}}_{s}(t))

the classical propagator, which maps initial values at time
s
to the solution of (
3.12
) at time
t
.
For any
{\widetilde{z}}=({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})
, it satisfies the evolution equation

(7.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi^{t,s}({\widetilde{z}})

\displaystyle=-J(\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}h)(t,\Phi^{t,s}({\widetilde{z}})),

(7.16)

\displaystyle\Phi^{s,s}({\widetilde{z}})

\displaystyle={\widetilde{z}}.

Both in the magnetic and the general subquadratic case, the classical propagator
\Phi^{t,\tau}
is a diffeomorphism with inverse
(\Phi^{t,\tau})^{-1}=\Phi^{\tau,t}
.
For time-independent, subquadratic Hamiltonians it is well-established that

(7.17)

\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,0)=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\bm{{a}}\circ\Phi^{t,0}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

However, to the best of our knowledge, in the literature a proof of the Egorov approximation for the non-autonomous case is not available, and the proofs presented for example in

[
4
]
,
[
36
, Chapter 11]
, or
[
30
, Thm. 12]
assume time-independent or compactly supported Hamiltonians and thus do not cover our more general situation.
The main difficulties are the time-dependence of the Hamiltonian operator
H(t)
, which prevents energy conservation,
and the allowed sublinear growth of the observables.

Proposition 7.2

(time-dependent Egorov–theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{{a}})
be a quantum observable stemming from a smooth, sublinear classical observable
\bm{{a}}
in the sense of
Definition

3.9
. Further, let
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})\mapsto\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})
be defined by

(7.18)

\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})=\bm{{a}}\circ\Phi^{t,s}({\widetilde{z}}).

We consider two cases.

(a)

The Hamiltonian operator stems from a classical, subquadratic function
h
. Then, the observable given in (
7.18
) is sublinear and for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(7.19)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\bigl(\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)-\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s)\right)\bigr)\varphi\bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{C|t-s|}\,\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

The Hamiltonian operator is a magnetic Schrödinger operator. We assume that the observable given in (
7.18
) is of time-exponential growth in the following sense. There exists a smooth nonnegative function
\Gamma(t,s)\geq 0
such that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
there exists
C_{\alpha}>0
with

(7.20)

|\partial^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})|\leq C_{\bm{{a}},\alpha}\ \exp(|\alpha|\,\Gamma(t,s))

for all
{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for any
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we then have

(7.21)

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\Big(\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)-\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s)\right)\varphi\Big)\Bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{C|t-s|}\,\left\lVert\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The constant
C>0
depends on derivative bounds of the potentials
A
,
V
and the observable
\bm{{a}}
,
but not on
\varepsilon,t,s
. In particular,
C=0
for
A
linear and
V
quadratic.

Proof.

(1) We start by discussing the growth of the function
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})
for case (a).
For first order derivatives with respect to
({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})
of the classical propagator we have

(7.22)

\displaystyle D\Phi^{t,s}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}+J^{-1}\int_{s}^{t}\nabla^{2}h(\tau,\Phi^{\tau,s})\,D\Phi^{\tau,s}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

and thus

(7.23)

\displaystyle\left\lVert D\Phi^{t,s}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{s}^{t}\sup_{{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}^{2}h(\tau,{\widetilde{z}})\|\left\lVert D\Phi^{\tau,s}\right\rVert_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Since the Hamiltonian function
h(t,\cdot)
is subquadratic, we have

(7.24)

\Gamma(t,s)\coloneqq\int_{s}^{t}\sup_{{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}^{2}h(\tau,{\widetilde{z}})\|\mathrm{d}\tau<\infty,

and by Gronwall’s lemma

(7.25)

\left\lVert D\Phi^{t,s}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\exp(\Gamma(t,s)).

Moreover, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

(7.26)

|\partial^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{z}}\Phi^{t,s}({\widetilde{z}})|\leq C_{\alpha}\exp(|\alpha|\,\Gamma(t,s))

for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
and all
{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, see
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
for
a proof that literally applies to the non-autonomous case. Then, the same argument as for

[
4
, Lemma 2.4]
yields that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
there exists a constant
C_{\bm{{a}},\alpha}>0
such that

(7.27)

|\partial^{\alpha}_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,{\widetilde{z}})|\leq C_{\bm{{a}},\alpha}\ \exp(|\alpha|\,\Gamma(t,s))

for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
and all
{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. In particular,
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s,\cdot)
is sublinear.

(2) Next we compare the operators
\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s))
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)=U(s,t)\mathbf{A}U(t,s)
. Since on the diagonal

\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,t,\cdot)=\bm{{a}}
and
U(s,s)=\mathrm{Id}
, we obtain similarly as for (
7.10
)

(7.28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)-\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s)\right)

(7.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}U(s,\tau)\Big(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\big[H(\tau),\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\right)\big]+\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\right)\Big)U(\tau,s)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(7.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}U(s,\tau)\Big(\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\{h(\tau),\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\})+\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\left(\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\right)\Big)U(\tau,s)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\rho(t,s),

where the last equation relies on the product rule of Weyl quantization
[
30
, Theorem]
. Here,

(7.31)

\displaystyle\{h(\tau),\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}h(\tau)\cdot J\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)

denotes the Poisson bracket of
h(\tau)
and
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)
. It remains to show that the integral vanishes and that the remainder
\rho(t,s)
is of order
\varepsilon^{2}
.

(3) For the estimation of the remainder, we use that

(7.32)

\rho(t,s)=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{s}^{t}U(s,\tau)\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{r}(t,\tau))U(\tau,s)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
\bm{r}(t,\tau,\cdot)
is a smooth function depending on the derivatives of the order
\geq 3
of
the function
h(\tau,\cdot)
and of the sublinear
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau,\cdot)
.
In order to estimate

(7.33)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\rho(t,s)\varphi\bigr\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}\int_{s}^{t}\bigl\|\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{r}(t,\tau))U(\tau,s)\varphi\bigr\|_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

we investigate the above integrand.

(i)

If
h
is subquadratic, then, due to the estimates given in (a), for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}
there exist

c_{1,\alpha},c_{2,\alpha}>0
such that

(7.34)

|\partial_{\widetilde{z}}^{\alpha}\bm{r}(t,\tau,{\widetilde{z}})|\leq c_{1,\alpha}\exp(c_{2,\alpha}|t-\tau|)

for all
t,\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and the Calderón–Vaillancourt Theorem, see e.g.
[
30
, Theorem 4]
, provides the claimed constant
C>0
for part (a).

(ii)

In the magnetic case, we rewrite the remainder function

(7.35)

\bm{r}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})=\bm{r}(\cdot,\cdot,{\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})=\bm{b}_{0}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})+\bm{b}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})^{T}{\widetilde{p}},

where
\bm{b}_{0}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{b}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are bounded with all their derivatives. For the first summand, we proceed as in the subquadratic case, using Calderón–Vaillancourt. For the second summand containing an unbounded linearity in
{\widetilde{p}}
,
we use the product rule and obtain that

(7.36)

\displaystyle\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\big(\bm{r}_{2}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})\big)=-\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\big(\bm{b}({\widetilde{q}},{\widetilde{p}})\big)\cdot\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nabla+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

Then, the boundedness of
\bm{b}
provides
C_{\bm{b}}>0
such that

(7.37)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\bm{b}(t,\tau))\varepsilon\nabla\big(U(\tau,s)\varphi\big)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\bm{b}}\,\bigl\|\varepsilon\nabla\big(U(\tau,s)\varphi\big)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}.

In the next step, we analyse
\bigl\|\varepsilon\nabla\big(U(\tau,s)\varphi\big)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}
.

(4) Let
t\geq s
and set
f(t)=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})U(t,s)\varphi
. We argue as in the proof for

[
5
, Lemma 10.4]
and observe that
f(t)
solves the perturbed magnetic Schrödinger equation

(7.38)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\partial_{t}f(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})H(t)U(t,s)\varphi=H(t)f(t)+\delta(t)

with source term

(7.39)

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle=[\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}}),H(t)]U(t,s)\varphi=\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{i}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{p}}-A({\widetilde{q}}))\\
\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}\big(\nabla\widetilde{V}({\widetilde{q}})-\nabla A({\widetilde{q}})\cdot{\widetilde{p}}\big)\end{pmatrix}U(t,s)\varphi,

where we used the product rule for the second equation. In the same spirit as in step (3), we estimate

(7.40)

\displaystyle\bigl\|\delta(t)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon\bigl\|\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})U(t,s)\varphi\bigr\|_{L^{2}}=C\varepsilon\bigl\|f(t)\bigr\|_{L^{2}},

where we exploited the sublinearity of
A
and that
\widetilde{V}
is subquadratic.
By the variation of constants formula
followed by Gronwall’s lemma, we obtain that

(7.41)

\bigl\|f(t)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{C(t-s)}\bigl\|f(s)\bigr\|_{L^{2}}=\mathrm{e}^{C(t-s)}\bigl\|\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}({\widetilde{z}})\varphi\bigr\|_{L^{2}}.

(5) In the following step we show that
\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}

satisfies the transport equation

(7.42a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=-\{h(\tau),\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)\},

(7.42b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\bm{{a}}

for
\tau\in[s,t]
. Then the integrand in question indeed vanishes, and we obtain

(7.43)

\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}(t,s)=\mathrm{op}_{\mathrm{Weyl}}(\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,s))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

as claimed. We rewrite the transport equation (
7.42
) as

(7.44)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=J\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}h(\tau)\cdot\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau),

(7.45)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\bm{{a}}.

The following argument crucially uses that
\Phi^{t,\tau}
is a diffeomorphism with inverse
(\Phi^{t,\tau})^{-1}=\Phi^{\tau,t}
. We observe that

\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau,\Phi^{\tau,t}({\widetilde{z}}))=\bm{{a}}({\widetilde{z}})
for all
{\widetilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and calculate

(7.46)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}\bm{{a}}({\widetilde{z}})

(7.47)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau,\Phi^{\tau,t}({\widetilde{z}}))

(7.48)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}})(t,\tau,\Phi^{\tau,t}({\widetilde{z}}))-J(\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}h)(\tau,\Phi^{\tau,t}({\widetilde{z}}))\cdot(\nabla_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{\bm{{a}}})(t,\tau,\Phi^{\tau,t}({\widetilde{z}})),

where, we used in the last step, the chain rule and
(
7.15
).
Since
\Phi^{\tau,t}
is a diffeomorphism, this proves that

\widetilde{\bm{{a}}}(t,\tau)
indeed solves the transport equation (

).
∎

7.3.
Averages with respect to Gaussian wave packets

The a posteriori error representation of
Lemma

7.1
involves an average with
respect to the variational solution. By Egorov’s theorem,
7.2
, the time-evolved quantum observable can be approximated by the Weyl-quantized classical observable evolved along the classical flow. We therefore derive an asymptotic expansion of averages of Weyl-quantized operators with respect to Gaussian wave packets. For obtaining this expansion, the following phase space moments will be useful.

Lemma 7.3

(Gaussian moments)
.

We consider a Gaussian
u\in\mathcal{M}
of unit norm,
\|u\|=1
, with phase space center
z=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and width matrix

\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. We denote by

(7.49)

\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C})=\pi^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\widetilde{z}}^{\ell}\,\exp(-{\widetilde{z}}\cdot G{\widetilde{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}\quad\text{ with }\quad G=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}+{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}&-{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\\
{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}&{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}\end{pmatrix},

where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
is symmetric, positive definite and symplectic. Then, for any multi-index
\ell=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{2d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}
, we have

(7.50)

\langle({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\rangle_{u}=\varepsilon^{|\ell|/2}\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C}).

If the length
|\ell|
of the multi-index is odd, then we have
\langle({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\rangle_{u}=0
.

Proof.

The claimed representation becomes evident, when using the Wigner function of the
Gaussian wave packet
u
. The Wigner function of a Gaussian wave packet centered in
z
satisfies

(7.51)

\mathcal{W}_{u}({\widetilde{z}})=(\pi\varepsilon)^{-d}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}({\widetilde{z}}-z)\cdot G({\widetilde{z}}-z)),

where the matrix
G

is symplectic, symmetric, positive definite,
see
[
24
, Proposition 6.15]
. The average of any Weyl-quantized observable can be written as the phase space integral of the symbol versus the Wigner function,
see for example
[
24
, Theorem 6.5]
. In particular,

(7.52)

\displaystyle\langle({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\,\mathcal{W}_{u}({\widetilde{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}

(7.53)

\displaystyle=(\pi\varepsilon)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}({\widetilde{z}}-z)\cdot G({\widetilde{z}}-z))\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}

(7.54)

\displaystyle=\pi^{-d}\varepsilon^{|\ell|/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\widetilde{z}}^{\ell}\exp(-{\widetilde{z}}\cdot G{\widetilde{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}},

where we have used that symplecticity implies
\det(G)=1
.
We observe, that if the length
|\ell|
of the multi-index is odd, then the above integral vanishes, and consequently
\langle({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{\ell}\rangle_{u}=0
as well.
∎

We now use these
moments for expanding Gaussian averages with respect to general observables.

Proposition 7.4

(Gaussian averages)
.

We consider a Gaussian
u\in\mathcal{M}
of unit norm,
\|u\|=1
, with phase space center
z=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and complex width matrix

\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. Then, for any smooth function

\bm{{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded sixth order derivatives,

(7.55)

\langle\bm{{a}}\rangle_{u}=\bm{{a}}(z)+\varepsilon f_{2}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})+\varepsilon^{2}f_{4}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})+\rho^{\varepsilon}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C}),

where

(7.56)

f_{k}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})=\sum_{|\ell|=k}\frac{1}{\ell!}\,\partial^{\ell}\bm{{a}}(z)\,\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C}),\qquad k=2,4.

The second order contribution satisfies

(7.57)

f_{2}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})=\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)_{\mathcal{C}}\,{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}^{-1})

with

(7.58)

\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)_{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\mathcal{C}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\\
\mathcal{C}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}.

The remainder satisfies
|\rho^{\varepsilon}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})|\leq C\varepsilon^{3}
with a constant
C>0
that only depends on sixth order derivatives of
\bm{{a}}
as well as on the width matrix
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

We start by Taylor expanding the symbol around
z
with sixth order remainder,

(7.59)

\bm{{a}}({\widetilde{z}})=\sum_{|k|\leq 5}\frac{1}{k!}\partial^{k}\bm{{a}}(z)({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{k}+\bm{r}_{6}({\widetilde{z}};z),

where

(7.60)

\bm{r}_{6}({\widetilde{z}};z)=\sum_{|k|=6}r_{k}({\widetilde{z}};z)({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{k},\qquad r_{k}({\widetilde{z}};z)=\frac{6}{k!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\vartheta)^{5}\partial^{k}\bm{{a}}(z+\vartheta({\widetilde{z}}-z))\ \mathrm{d}\vartheta.

We have

(7.61)

\langle\bm{r}_{6}({\widetilde{z}};z)\rangle_{u}=\sum_{|k|=6}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}r_{k}({\widetilde{z}};z)\,({\widetilde{z}}-z)^{k}\,\mathcal{W}_{u}({\widetilde{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}.

Therefore, using
Lemma

7.3
,

(7.62)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{{a}}\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{{a}}(z)+\varepsilon f_{2}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})+\varepsilon^{2}f_{4}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})+\langle\bm{r}_{6}({\widetilde{z}};z)\rangle_{u},

and, with the same substitution as in the proof of
Lemma

7.3
, we bound

(7.63)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\bm{r}_{6}({\widetilde{z}};z)\rangle_{u}\right|\leq C(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})\,\varepsilon^{3}\quad\text{ with }\quad C(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})=\sum_{|k|=6}\|r_{k}(\cdot;z)\|_{\infty}\ \left\lvert\rho_{k}(\mathcal{C})\right\rvert.

The constant
C(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})>0
depends on fourth order derivatives of
\bm{{a}}
and on the width matrix
\mathcal{C}
. It remains to rewrite the second order contribution as

(7.64)

\displaystyle f_{2}(\bm{{a}},\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\pi^{-d}\sum_{|\ell|=2}\frac{1}{\ell!}\partial^{\ell}\bm{{a}}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(G^{-1/2}{\widetilde{z}})^{\ell}\exp(-|{\widetilde{z}}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}

(7.65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ \pi^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\widetilde{z}}\cdot G^{-1/2}\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)G^{-1/2}{\widetilde{z}}\ \exp(-|{\widetilde{z}}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{z}}

(7.66)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)G^{-1}).

Since
G
is symplectic and symmetric, its inverse satisfies

(7.67)

\displaystyle G^{-1}

\displaystyle=JGJ^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}&{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}\\
{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}&{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}+{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}\end{pmatrix}.

We decompose the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)
in block form as

(7.68)

\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{T}&D\end{pmatrix}.

Using the cyclicity of the trace, we calculate that

(7.69)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)G^{-1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}((A+B{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}+{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}B^{T}+{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}D{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}+{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}D{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{R}}}){\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}})

(7.70)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)_{\mathcal{C}}\,{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}^{-1}}),

where
\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)_{\mathcal{C}}
was defined in (
7.58
) and has the form

(7.71)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\bm{{a}}(z)_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=A+B\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{C}^{*}B^{T}+\mathcal{C}^{*}D\mathcal{C},

which gives the claim.
∎

For our analysis of the observable error, we will use

also for observables

Corollary 7.5

(Gaussian averages)
.

In the situation of

applied to a sublinear classical observable

\bm{{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}

(7.72)

f_{2}(b^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{C})=\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^{2}b^{\varepsilon}(q){\mathcal{C}_{\textup{I}}}^{-1}).

then

(b)

\displaystyle\quad F_{1,n}(\bm{{a}},b^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{C})=\sum_{|\ell|=n+1}\sum_{\beta\leq\ell,|\beta|=1}\frac{1}{(\ell-\beta)!}\ \partial^{\beta}\bm{{a}}(z)\ \partial^{\ell-\beta}b^{\varepsilon}(q)\ \rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C}),\quad n=1,3.

Proof.

For the trace formula, it is enough to observe that the matrices
B
and
D
in the block matrix (

) vanish, since

only depends on

.

For proving the expansions of the averages, we crucially use the Leibniz formula for the
\ell
th derivative of the product, that is,

(7.79)

\partial^{\ell}(\bm{{a}}b^{\varepsilon})(z)=\sum_{\beta\leq\ell}\binom{\ell}{\beta}\ \partial^{\beta}\bm{{a}}(z)\ \partial^{\ell-\beta}b^{\varepsilon}(q)

for any multi-index
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}

(a)

In the situation of statement (a), we only consider

and obtain

(7.80)

\varepsilon\partial^{\ell}(\bm{{a}}b^{\varepsilon})(z)=\varepsilon\bm{{a}}(z)\partial^{\ell}b^{\varepsilon}(q)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Then,

implies

\displaystyle\langle\bm{{a}}b^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{{a}}(z)b^{\varepsilon}(q)+\varepsilon f_{2}(\bm{{a}}b^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{C})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

(7.82)

\displaystyle=\bm{{a}}(z)\left(b^{\varepsilon}(q)+\varepsilon f_{2}(b^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{C})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

(b)

In the situation of statement (b), we aim for a higher order expansion and need to consider

In the same spirit as the proof of part (a),
7.4

\langle\bm{{a}}b^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{u}
.

Appendix A
Gaussian moments

By an application of Isserlis’ theorem, the fourth order Gaussian moments can
be written as sums of products of second order moments. That is, for a

2d
-dimensional Gaussian random vector

(A.1)

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{2d})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,G)

with mean zero
0\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and covariance matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
, the fourth order moments satisfy

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{4})=3g_{ii}^{2},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{3}X_{j})=3g_{ii}g_{ij},

(A.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2})=g_{ii}g_{jj}+2g_{ij}^{2}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2}X_{j}X_{k})=g_{ii}g_{jk}+2g_{ij}g_{ik}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}X_{\ell})=g_{ij}g_{k\ell}+g_{ik}g_{j\ell}+g_{i\ell}g_{jk}

with
i,j,k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}
. We crucially use this for proving that the fourth order summations
that appeared in the proof of
Theorem

3.10
can be expressed in terms of second order
summations.

Lemma A.1

(Resummation)
.

For any family
(a_{\beta,m})_{\beta,m}
of real numbers, indexed by
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}
and

\beta\leq m
with
|\beta|=1
, we have

(A.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{|m|=4}\sum_{\beta\leq m,|\beta|=1}\frac{1}{(m-\beta)!}\ a_{\beta,m}\,\rho_{m}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{|k|=2}\sum_{|\ell|=2}\sum_{\beta\leq\ell,|\beta|=1}\frac{1}{k!}\ a_{\beta,k+\ell}\,\rho_{k}(\mathcal{C})\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C}).

Proof.

We write a multi-index
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}
of order
|m|=4
as

(A.8)

m=\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle+\langle j_{3}\rangle+\langle j_{4}\rangle

with coordinates
j_{1},\ldots,j_{4}\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}
, where
the bracket
\langle j\rangle=e_{j}
denotes the
j
th canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
We distinguish five different cases for the order four multi-index
m
.

(a)
m
has one non-zero component, that is,
m=4\langle j\rangle
with
j=1,\ldots,2d
. Then,

(A.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(m-\beta)!}\ a_{\beta,m}\,\rho_{m}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}\,a_{\langle j\rangle,4\langle j\rangle}\,3\rho_{2\langle j\rangle}(\mathcal{C})^{2}

(A.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\,a_{\beta,k+\ell}\,\rho_{k}(\mathcal{C})\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C})

with
k=2\langle j\rangle=\ell
and
\beta=\langle j\rangle
.

(b)
m
has two different non-zero components, that is,

m=3\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle
with
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
.
In this case,
m
dominates two multi-indices
\beta
of order one, and generates the terms

(A.11)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2!}\,a_{\langle j_{1}\rangle,m}+\frac{1}{3!}\,a_{\langle j_{2}\rangle,m}\right)3\rho_{2\langle j_{1}\rangle}(\mathcal{C})\rho_{\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle}(\mathcal{C})

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2!}\left(a_{\langle j_{1}\rangle,m}+a_{\langle j_{2}\rangle,m}\right)\rho_{2\langle j_{1}\rangle}(\mathcal{C})\rho_{\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle}(\mathcal{C})

(A.13)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{1!}\ a_{\langle j_{1}\rangle,m}\ \rho_{\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle}(\mathcal{C})\rho_{2\langle j_{1}\rangle}(\mathcal{C}).

This amounts to the two
(k,\ell)
pairs

(A.14)

\displaystyle k=2\langle j_{1}\rangle,\ \ell=\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle,\ \beta\in\{\langle j_{1}\rangle,\langle j_{2}\rangle\},

(A.15)

\displaystyle k=\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle,\ \ell=2\langle j_{1}\rangle,\ \beta=\langle j_{1}\rangle.

In a similar manner, we show that in the cases

(c)
m
has two identical non-zero components, that is,
m=2\langle j_{1}\rangle+2\langle j_{2}\rangle
with
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
,

(d)
m
has three non-zero components, that is,

m=2\langle j_{1}\rangle+\langle j_{2}\rangle+\langle j_{3}\rangle
with pairwise distinct
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}
,

(e)
m
has four non-zero components, that is,
m=\langle j_{1}\rangle+\cdots+\langle j_{4}\rangle
with distinct
j_{1},\ldots,j_{4}
,

we obtain the appropriate format of the resulting summands, that is,

(A.16)

\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\beta\leq\ell,|\beta|=1}a_{\beta,k+\ell}\,\rho_{k}(\mathcal{C})\,\rho_{\ell}(\mathcal{C})

with
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2d}
such that
|k|=|\ell|=2
and
k+\ell=m
.
For concluding the proof, we have
to verify that any possible
(k,\ell)
pairing of order two multi-indices has appeared in one
of the five cases (a)–(e). Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{4}\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}
be such that

(A.17)

k=\langle i_{1}\rangle+\langle i_{2}\rangle,\quad\ell=\langle i_{3}\rangle+\langle i_{4}\rangle.

The combinatorics of this situation falls into the following five cases:

(
\alpha
)

All four coordinates agree,
that is,
i_{1}=\cdots=i_{4}=:j
.
Then,
k+\ell=4\langle j\rangle
,
and we recognize the previous case (a).

(
\beta
)

Three of the four coordinates coincide with each other,
which is case (b).

(
\gamma
)

The four coordinates form two different pairs,
and we are in case (c).

(
\delta
)

Two of the four coordinates agree, while the other two are different,
which is case (d).

(
\varepsilon
)

All four coordinates are distinct as in case (e).

Hence, the combinatorics of the order four multi-indices and the one of pairs of order two multi-indices are the same, and we have indeed proven that the two different summation formats yield the same result as claimed.
∎
