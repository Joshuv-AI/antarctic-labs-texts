Mitigating Bias in Visual Transformers via Targeted Alignment
Sruthi Sudhakar, Viraj Prabhu, Arvindkumar Krishnakumar, Judy Hoffman

Abstract
As transformer architectures become increasingly prevalent in computer vision, it is critical to understand their fairness implications. We perform the first study of the fairness of transformers applied to computer vision and benchmark several bias mitigation approaches from prior work. We visualize the feature space of the transformer self-attention modules and discover that a significant portion of the bias is encoded in the query matrix. With this knowledge, we propose TADeT \xspace , a targeted alignment strategy for debiasing transformers that aims to discover and remove bias primarily from query matrix features. We measure performance using Balanced Accuracy \xspace and Standard Accuracy \xspace , and fairness using Equalized Odds \xspace and Balanced Accuracy Difference \xspace . TADeT \xspace consistently leads to improved fairness over prior work on multiple attribute prediction tasks on the CelebA dataset, without compromising performance.

Introduction

Over the past decade, deep learning-based computer vision has been advancing at a rapid pace and is being deployed in high-stakes applications ranging from candidate job hiring to facial recognition systems. Several recent studies have shown that deep models take advantage of spurious correlations between attributes in the data when learning to make predictions. Such unintended correlations can cause the model to act in a biased way, such as having lower accuracy on certain sub-populations of the data
[
Buolamwini and Gebru(2018)
,
Singh et al.(2020)Singh, Mahajan, Grauman, Lee, Feiszli, and
Ghadiyaram
,
Wilson et al.(2019)Wilson, Hoffman, and Morgenstern
,
Kay et al.(2015)Kay, Matuszek, and Munson
,
du2020fairness
]
. These biases can be harmful and cause discrimination towards these populations once deployed.

While the fairness of computer vision tasks learnt using convolutional neural network (CNN) models is well explored, the fairness implications of
Visual Transformers
are yet to be studied. Transformers were first introduced in NLP
[
Vaswani et al.(2017)Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez,
Kaiser, and Polosukhin
]
, and later found to be effective on computer vision tasks
[
Dosovitskiy et al.(2020)Dosovitskiy, Beyer, Kolesnikov, Weissenborn,
Zhai, Unterthiner, Dehghani, Minderer, Heigold, Gelly, Uszkoreit, and
Houlsby
]
due to their ability to learn long-range relationships, unlike CNN’s which have have a limited field of view at any given layer. There has been a rise in the use of transformers in vision tasks
[
khan2021transformers
]
. In this paper, we perform the first study of visual transformers with respect to fairness. Transformers have a naturally separated and spatially cohesive feature space
[
Vaswani et al.(2017)Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez,
Kaiser, and Polosukhin
]
, and we investigate how this unique architecture may be leveraged to ensure fairness.

There are several ways to measure the fairness of a model
[
Dwork et al.(2011)Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and
Zemel
,
Hardt et al.(2016)Hardt, Price, and Srebro
,
Zhao et al.(2017)Zhao, Wang, Yatskar, Ordonez, and Chang
,
Dieterich et al.(2016)Dieterich, Mendoza, and
Brennan
]
.
In this work, we focus on an image classification task for which all predictive outputs are equally desirable, and hence measure performance using
balanced
(multiclass) accuracy
[
Park et al.(2020)Park, Kim, Hwang, and Byun
]
. Further, we focus on two fairness metrics: Equalized Odds
\xspace

[
Dwork et al.(2011)Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and
Zemel
]
, as it focuses on equal true positive and false positive rates across the protected attribute, and a new auxiliary measure we introduce,
Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
(
\Delta
BA) which measures the difference in Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
across the two groups. This metric, which is similar to accuracy equity
[
Dieterich et al.(2016)Dieterich, Mendoza, and
Brennan
]
, gives practitioners an overall idea of the
difference
in model performance across a protected attribute.

Several debiasing algorithms have been proposed and studied for CNNs, including re-weighting instances
[
Wang et al.(2019)Wang, Zhao, Yatskar, Chang, and
Ordonez
]
, MMD alignment
[
Long et al.(2015)Long, Cao, Wang, and Jordan
]
, domain independent training
[
Dwork et al.(2011)Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and
Zemel
,
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
, and adversarial training
[
Zhang et al.(2018)Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell
]
. However, many of these techniques suffer from a trade-off where increased bias mitigation results in reduced predictive performance
[
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
. In addition, these works either require a large number of the protected class data or are designed for learning with a CNN model, making it unclear how well these methods will translate to newer backbone architectures like visual transformers.

TADeT

We perform the first fairness assessment of visual transformer models and benchmark existing bias mitigation algorithms.
We then introduce
TADeT
\xspace
, Targeted Alignment for Debiasing Transformers, a novel algorithm that applies class-specific Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
[
Gretton et al.(2008)Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Scholkopf, and
Smola
]
alignment and class-specific adversarial
[
Zhang et al.(2018)Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell
]
debiasing, to a
targeted
part of the visual transformer feature space. We discover that when bias exists for a particular attribute, the core piece of the model which differs between two subgroups (one with and one without said attribute) can be observed through the query activations. For example, in Figure
1
we consider a Transformer trained to predict the presence of the “Smiling” attribute, and visualize its
average
query and key matrix activations over the dataset. We find that for the subset of images where smiling is present, a large discrepancy exists in average query activations across the protected attribute of “High cheekbones” (box 1, column 1), whereas the key activations are much more similar (box 1, column 2). This result is also observed in the case of
not
smiling images (box 2).
Thus, by imposing alignment
[
Ganin and Lempitsky(2015)
]
directly on the query activations we may be able to effectively reduce model bias while limiting the impact on predictive performance.

We evaluate our method on the task of single-label attribute prediction on the CelebA dataset
[
Liu et al.(2015)Liu, Luo, Wang, and Tang
]
, while requiring invariance to a defined protected attribute. We measure the performance of our method using Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
and Standard Accuracy
\xspace
, and the fairness of our method using Equalized Odds
\xspace
[
Hardt et al.(2016)Hardt, Price, and Srebro
]
and Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
.
We make the following contributions:

1.

We perform the first assessment of the fairness of representations learned by visual transformers, and benchmark several existing debiasing methods from the literature.

2.

We visualize the transformer feature space and discover that a significant portion of learned bias is encoded in the activations of the transformer’s query matrices.

3.

Motivated by this, we propose
TADeT
\xspace
, a debiasing strategy for visual transformers using
targeted
,
class-specific
, MMD alignment and adversarial debiasing to improve upon existing fairness algorithms without compromising accuracy.

Related Work

Transformers in computer vision.

Transformers were first introduced in NLP by Vaswani
et al.

[
Vaswani et al.(2017)Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez,
Kaiser, and Polosukhin
]
, which used self-attention to develop better context and allow models to attend to relevant words across the entire corpus of text. Recently, ViT (Visual Transformer)
[
Dosovitskiy et al.(2020)Dosovitskiy, Beyer, Kolesnikov, Weissenborn,
Zhai, Unterthiner, Dehghani, Minderer, Heigold, Gelly, Uszkoreit, and
Houlsby
]
has emerged as a new architecture for vision capable of achieving state-of-the-art performance on standard recognition tasks. Given the strong performance of this architectural paradigm, many future works are bound to leverage the visual transformer model, necessitating a study into the fairness implications and mitigation approaches designed for transformers.

Measuring Biases.

Several metrics exist for measuring bias in machine learning models.
Demographic parity
[
Dwork et al.(2011)Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and
Zemel
]
measures whether the rates of positive outcomes across all protected groups are equal, making it susceptible to the self-fulfilling prophecy and subset targeting.
Bias Amplification
[
Zhao et al.(2017)Zhao, Wang, Yatskar, Ordonez, and Chang
]
measures how a model
amplifies
biases, though it does not provide feedback about the inherent biases common in visual recognition datasets
[
Tommasi et al.(2015)Tommasi, Patricia, Caputo, and
Tuytelaars
,
Bhargava and Forsyth(2019)
,
Wang et al.(2019)Wang, Zhao, Yatskar, Chang, and
Ordonez
,
Denton et al.(2020)Denton, Hutchinson, Mitchell, Gebru, and
Zaldivar
]
.
Equalized Odds
\xspace

[
Hardt et al.(2016)Hardt, Price, and Srebro
]
, one of the metrics we report, offers a stricter definition of Equality of Opportunity, by adding an extra constraint on the Demographic Parity measure where each group must receive the positive and negative outcomes at equal rates if the datapoints in this group qualify for that outcome.
Finally accuracy equity
[
Dieterich et al.(2016)Dieterich, Mendoza, and
Brennan
]
compares accuracy across subpopulations. However, when a dataset is class imbalanced, this measurement will have an implicit bias against the minority class. Therefore, we introduce Balanced Accuracy Difference as a way to measure the difference in a model’s performance across a protected attribute while accounting for class imbalance.

Mitigation Methods.

Some of the most straightforward mitigation methods focus on re-sampling or re-weighting techniques
[
Elkan(2001)
,
Bickel et al.(2009)Bickel, Brückner, and
Scheffer
]
, but these methods may have limited information gain through resampling and in some cases suffer from overfitting
[
Weiss et al.(2007)Weiss, McCarthy, and
Zabar
]
.
A different style of approach seeks to directly impose alignment of feature spaces by optimizing an MMD loss between features from different subgroups
[
Long et al.(2015)Long, Cao, Wang, and Jordan
]
, though mitigation benefits are variable
[
Madras et al.(2018)Madras, Creager, Pitassi, and
Zemel
]
.
A domain discriminative
[
Dwork et al.(2011)Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and
Zemel
]
approach trains a classifier for each protected attribute and task combination and hence attempts to induce ’fairness through awareness’, though recent works
[
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
demonstrate limitations including a lack of leveraging between-class information and an increased number of parameters.
A domain independent approach
[
Wang et al.(2019)Wang, Zhao, Yatskar, Chang, and
Ordonez
]
trains an ensemble of classifiers with a shared feature representation to ensure that rare attribute values are adequately represented. While this approach can lead to large bias reductions, it is limited in that it assumes adequate labeled data for each setting of the protected attribute in order to train a representative model.

Many recent approaches have leveraged the success of adversarial learning techniques for domain alignment
[
Ganin and Lempitsky(2015)
]
. Adversarial Debiasing
[
Zhang et al.(2018)Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell
]
alternates between training a classifier to distinguish the protected attribute and updating the representation to be invariant to the protected attribute.
LAFTR
[
Madras et al.(2018)Madras, Creager, Pitassi, and
Zemel
]
is an adversarial method with the ability to modify the training objective based on the desired fairness measure (equalized odds, equality of opportunity, and demographic parity). However, in our experiments using this method with the equalized odds objective, we find that it is unable to consistently achieve fairness improvements while maintaining high performance.

We benchmark MMD, Domain Independent, Adversarial Debiasing, and LAFTR methods on transformers. We then propose
TADeT
\xspace
, a targeted alignment approach to debias transformers (leveraging the query matrix features) that achieves improved fairness with minimal reduction in predictive performance.

3
Approach

Recently, visual transformers
[
Dosovitskiy et al.(2020)Dosovitskiy, Beyer, Kolesnikov, Weissenborn,
Zhai, Unterthiner, Dehghani, Minderer, Heigold, Gelly, Uszkoreit, and
Houlsby
]
have emerged as a popular architecture for learning visual recognition models. While the fairness of CNN models has been documented
[
Buolamwini and Gebru(2018)
]
and mitigation strategies have been proposed
[
Zhang et al.(2018)Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell
,
Madras et al.(2018)Madras, Creager, Pitassi, and
Zemel
]
, we study the bias which is present within a visual transformer-based model and propose
TADeT
\xspace
, a new bias mitigation algorithm designed for debiasing visual transformers.
TADeT
\xspace
focuses alignment on query activations within the transformer feature space to remove bias against a protected attribute.

3.1
Notation and Background

Let
{\bm{x}}\xspace
denote an input image and
{y}\xspace
the corresponding task label. Our goal is to learn a classifier
{h}\xspace
, on top of deep encoder,
{f}\xspace
, such that
{h}\xspace({f}\xspace({\bm{x}}\xspace))\xrightarrow{}{y}\xspace

achieves high heldout task accuracy while being fair with respect to a protected attribute,
{a}\xspace
. Fairness has many definitions; in this work we focus on designing a model which is invariant with respect to the protected attribute (see Sec
2
).

The model
{f}\xspace
employs the visual transformer architecture introduced in Dosovitskiy
et al.

[
Dosovitskiy et al.(2020)Dosovitskiy, Beyer, Kolesnikov, Weissenborn,
Zhai, Unterthiner, Dehghani, Minderer, Heigold, Gelly, Uszkoreit, and
Houlsby
]
. The input image
{\bm{x}}\xspace\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
is reshaped into
N
patches
\{{x}\xspace_{1},..,{x}\xspace_{N}\}
, where each patch
x_{i}
is of size
\frac{HW}{N}\times\frac{HW}{N}\times C
. Each patch is then flattened and mapped to
{D}\xspace
dimensions with a trainable linear projection. A final learnable class token
{x}\xspace_{\text{class}}
of the same dimension,
{D}\xspace
, is appended to the sequence of patches. This class token is randomly initialized and updated throughout training.
These
N+1
patch embeddings are appended with a positional embedding to retain positional information, and then fed through
{L}\xspace
layers of the transformer encoder. Each layer of the encoder is identical and includes a multi-headed self-attention module followed by alternating MLP blocks and layernorms.

The multi-headed self-attention module includes
{M}\xspace
attention heads. For each attention head,
{m}\xspace
, the concatenated
N+1
patch embeddings are split into 3 branches and each fed to a linear layer. The 3 branches result in learning Query (
{Q}\xspace
), Key (
{K}\xspace
), and Value (
{V}\xspace
) activations respectively, each of dimension
{M}\xspace\times(N+1)\times{D}\xspace
, which attempt to encode specific pieces of information. The value matrix is tasked with learning an encoding of the actual attributes of the data (
eg.
for a face image, information such as hair color, eye color, etc.). The key matrix encodes the location of
where
specific information is stored in the value matrix (
eg.
where the eye color is located). Finally, the query matrix is trained to “ask” the key matrix for the information it requires for the task. Activations produced from these 3 matrices are then combined to produce the self-attention defined in Vaswani
et al.
[
Vaswani et al.(2017)Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez,
Kaiser, and Polosukhin
]
as:

\text{Attention}({Q}\xspace,{K}\xspace,{V}\xspace)=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{{Q}\xspace{K}\xspace^{T}}{\sqrt{{D}\xspace}}\right){V}\xspace

(1)

The activations from the self-attention module are fed into alternating MLP and layernorms in each layer of the encoder. Finally, the features for the class token,
{x}\xspace_{\text{class}}
, are extracted from the output of the final layer transformer encoder and used as the feature representation of input
{\bm{x}}\xspace
to predict output
{y}\xspace
.

Figure 2:
Overview of
TADeT
\xspace
. First, a preprocessed input image and an additional class token are passed through
{L}\xspace
transformer encoder layers, and features extracted from the class token are used for task classification. Second, query activations of the last layer of the encoder are used for Targeted Debiasing in two ways: i) query activations corresponding to the class token are used for class-specific adversarial alignment (Sec.
3.2.1
), and ii) for each class, the
L_{2}
distance between average query activations for each setting of the protected attribute is minimized (Sec.
3.2.2
).

3.2
TADeT
\xspace
: Targeted Alignment for Debiasing Transformers

Unlike CNNs, transformers possess a naturally interpretable feature space due to separation into queries and keys. On studying the average query and key activations of the last layer of a visual transformer encoder before making a prediction, we find a measurable difference across average query activations across the protected attribute values, but only minimal differences between average key activations across the protected attribute values (see Figure
1
). These differences generalize across the D dimensions and M attention heads of the query matrix. This observation of a large difference in query activations motivates our focus on alignment of query activations.

We introduce
TADeT
\xspace
, Targeted Alignment for Debiasing Transformers, a bias mitigation algorithm that focuses on aligning
query activations
(see Figure
2
).
TADeT
\xspace
considers a visual transformer model trained using a standard task loss and performs debiasing through the introduction of two class-specific alignment losses, 1) an adversarial loss which aligns the class token of the query activations,
L_{\text{adv}}
, (Sec
3.2.1
) and, 2) an
L_{2}
loss which penalizes large deviations between the average query activations for values of the protected attribute,
L_{q}
, (Sec
3.2.2
). Our overall model considers the joint optimization between these two objectives together with a task supervised cross-entropy loss,
L_{\text{CE}}
.

3.2.1
Class-specific Adversarial Alignment

We begin alignment across populations with different attributes by leveraging adversarial mitigation
[
Zhang et al.(2018)Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell
]
.
In adversarial training for feature alignment, an adversary
{g}\xspace
is applied to a feature space to determine if two groups are distinguishable. Optimization alternates between training the discriminator to distinguish the groups,
{a}\xspace
in our case, and updating the feature encoder,
{f}\xspace
, to confuse the discriminator. Whereas prior work aligns the penultimate features for an instance,
{\bm{x}}\xspace
, we seek to align the class token of the query matrix,
{Q}\xspace_{\bm{x}}\xspace[:,N+1,:]
. This results in a standard min/max optimization over this targeted feature space:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}({\bm{x}}\xspace;{f}\xspace,{g}\xspace)=\max_{{f}\xspace}\min_{{g}\xspace}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\xspace,{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace}[\mathcal{L_{\text{CE}}}({g}\xspace({Q}\xspace_{\bm{x}}\xspace[:,N+1,:]),{a}\xspace)]

(2)

Furthermore, prior works propose aligning the features across the protected attribute by aligning
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=0)
and
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=1)
. However, this does not take into account the ground truth task label
{y}\xspace
, allowing two datapoints with different task labels to be aligned, which will lead to incorrect classification. Therefore, we propose using
class-specific
alignment where we align the features based on both the protected attribute value
and
the task label value (
i.e.
we only align
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=0,{y}\xspace=1)
with
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=1,{y}\xspace=1)
, and only align
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=0,{y}\xspace=0)
with
p(\hat{{y}\xspace}=y|{a}\xspace=1,{y}\xspace=0)
).

3.2.2
Direct Alignment of Class-Specific Query Activations

Our hypothesis is that by reducing the differences between the average query activations, we can encourage invariance to the protected attribute and improve fairness. Following this we add a Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) loss
[
Gretton et al.(2008)Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Scholkopf, and
Smola
]
to our objective function that minimizes the L
2
distance between the average query activations for instances from each attribute. We exclude the class token of the query activation,
{Q}\xspace_{\bm{x}}\xspace[:,N+1,:]
, as it does not encode spatial information. We first compute the average query activation in each minibatch, B
\xspace
, for each task label (y) and protected attribute (a) combination, where
{Q}\xspace_{{\bm{x}}\xspace_{i}}
indicates the query activation for image
{\bm{x}}\xspace_{i}
:

\hat{{Q}\xspace}_{{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace}=\frac{1}{|{B}\xspace|}\sum_{i\in{B}\xspace,\;\text{s.t.}\;{y}\xspace_{i}={y}\xspace,{a}\xspace_{i}={a}\xspace}{Q}\xspace_{{\bm{x}}\xspace_{i}}[:,1:N,:]

(3)

Next, we compute the average of
L_{2}
differences across the protected attribute for each element in
\hat{{Q}\xspace}_{{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace}
independently, and then average these differences across the
{D}\xspace
channels and
{M}\xspace
attention heads of the matrix. Due to the reasoning provided in Sec.
3.2.1
, we utilize class-specific alignment of the query loss to ensure that the appropriate features are aligned. For binary attributes this yields the following alignment loss:

\mathcal{L}_{q}({\bm{x}}\xspace;{f}\xspace)=\frac{1}{2\cdot{M}\xspace{D}\xspace}\sum_{{m}\xspace\in{M}\xspace}\sum_{{d}\xspace\in{D}\xspace}\sum_{{y}\xspace\in\{0,1\}}||\hat{{Q}\xspace}_{{y}\xspace,0}[{m}\xspace,:,{d}\xspace]-\hat{{Q}\xspace}_{{y}\xspace,1}[{m}\xspace,:,{d}\xspace]||_{2}

(4)

Altogether, our model results from the following joint minimization:

\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}}\xspace,{y}\xspace;{f}\xspace,{h}\xspace,{g}\xspace)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}({h}\xspace({f}\xspace({\bm{x}}\xspace)),{y}\xspace)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}({\bm{x}}\xspace;{g}\xspace,{f}\xspace)+\beta\mathcal{L}_{q}({\bm{x}}\xspace;{f}\xspace)

(5)

where hyper-parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are tuned and optimized for fairness.

4
Experiments

4.1
Setup & Implementation Details

We evaluate
TADeT
\xspace
on CelebA
[
Liu et al.(2015)Liu, Luo, Wang, and Tang
]
, a dataset containing 200k celebrity face images with annotations for 40 binary attributes. We present results on three settings, each with a corresponding binary task (
{y}\xspace
) that the model is trained to predict, and a binary protected attribute (
{a}\xspace
) over which we wish the model to be unbiased. The three settings described as a tuple (
{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace
) are as follows: i) (Smiling, High Cheekbones), ii) (Wavy Hair, Male), and iii) (Wavy Hair, Wearing Lipstick).
We take careful steps to choose these settings: First we train a baseline Transformer model on different task attributes (including Smiling and Wavy Hair). Then we measure the widely-used Equalized Odds
\xspace
fairness metric to estimate how much bias is correlated with each protected attribute (results for Smiling reported in Figure
3
). Finally, we choose the protected attribute with the highest Equalized Odds
\xspace
and thus the most bias (more details in the supplement).

Figure 3:
We choose our evaluation settings by measuring the fairness of a Transformer model trained to predict a given task attribute across different protected attributes.
This figure above shows the fairness (measured by Equalized Odds
\xspace
) across each protected attribute for a Transformer trained to predict Smiling.
We select High Cheekbones (in green) as it is the attribute with the greatest bias. A similar process is followed for the (Wavy Hair, Male) and (Wavy Hair, Wearing Lipstick) settings.

Implementation Details.
We first train a transformer from scratch on each of these 3 settings. The transformer consists of 196 patches (each representing a 16x16 area of the image), 1 class token patch, 12 transformer encoder layers, and 8 attention heads. We flatten and project each patch into a 64-dimensional vector and add positional embeddings
[
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
. The embedded patches are fed into the transformer encoder. We use parameter sharing across key and value weights
[
Wang et al.(2020a)Wang, Li, Khabsa, Fang, and
Ma
]
. After the transformer encoder processes the patch embeddings, the class token patch is fed into 2 fully-connected layers and a sigmoid layer to produce a single normalized output score (since we deal with binary classification). We use the Adam optimizer
[
kingma2017adam
]
with a learning rate of 1
\times 10^{-4}
, train for 50 epochs, and following Wang
et al.
[
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
, select the model with the best weighted average precision (AP) on the validation set.

We implement
TADeT
\xspace
by applying a class-specific adversarial and class-specific query loss on top of our pretrained transformer model to perform targeted debiasing.
We align the query class token activations by applying one adversary head per setting of the task label.
The adversary takes in the 512-dimensional vector query class token of the final (12
{}^{\text{th}}
) layer of the transformer encoder. The class token is processed through 2 fully-connected layers and a sigmoid layer to produce a single normalized output score of the binary protected attribute
{a}\xspace
.
We perform a sweep across multiple loss weights for the adversarial (
\alpha
) and query (
\beta
) losses, and select the one yielding the best fairness metrics.

Metrics.
As performance metrics, we report Standard Accuracy
\xspace
and Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
[
Park et al.(2020)Park, Kim, Hwang, and Byun
]
,
\dfrac{1}{4}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=0}+TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=1}]
, which is needed when task data is imbalanced.
As fairness metrics, we report Equalized Odds
\xspace
[
Hardt et al.(2016)Hardt, Price, and Srebro
]
and introduce Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
. Equalized Odds
\xspace
is used to understand the discrepancy in true positive rates and false positive rates across the protected attribute. Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
looks at the difference in Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
between the binary values of the protected group. By computing the difference in Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
, as opposed to difference in Standard Accuracy
\xspace
proposed by Dieterich
et al.
[
Dieterich et al.(2016)Dieterich, Mendoza, and
Brennan
]
, we are able to account for the class imbalance in the original dataset.
A detailed use case is described in the supplement.

4.2
Baselines

We evaluate the following debiasing algorithms with the pretrained transformer as baselines.

i) Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
[
Long et al.(2015)Long, Cao, Wang, and Jordan
]
:

MMD computes a mean of penultimate layer feature activations for each setting of the protected attribute, and then minimizes their L
2
distance. We select a loss weight that yields the highest validation results.

ii) Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN)
[
Ganin and Lempitsky(2015)
]
:

Following successful adversarial debiasing
[
Hardt et al.(2016)Hardt, Price, and Srebro
]
, we build on domain adversarial alignment
[
Ganin and Lempitsky(2015)
]
using an attribute adversary learned on top of the penultimate layer activations.
The adversarial head consists of 2 linear layers that take in a 512-dimensional vector class token, followed by a sigmoid, and select the adversarial loss weight that yields the highest validation results.

iii) LAFTR
[
Madras et al.(2018)Madras, Creager, Pitassi, and
Zemel
]
:
We train a model with a modified adversarial objective which attempts to satisfy the Equalized Odds fairness measure. This objective is implemented by minimizing the average absolute difference on each task attribute-protected attribute (
{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace
) combination. This allows the model to directly optimize for the equalized odds measure.

iv) Domain Independent Training
[
Wang et al.(2020b)Wang, Qinami, Karakozis, Genova, Nair,
Hata, and Russakovsky
]
:

We learn a shared feature representation with an ensemble of classifiers (two), trained with the same baseline hyperparameters. Given the predictions, we perform inference by averaging the class decision boundaries.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we perform the first benchmarking of several existing debiasing algorithms on visual transformers. We visualize the feature space learned by the transformer self-attention modules and find a significant portion of the bias is encoded in the query matrices. Using this information, we introduce
TADeT
\xspace
, a targeted debiasing algorithm for transformers that aims to remove bias encoded within the query matrix.

Our method suffers from some limitations. First, we note that it is important for users to consider the application of their model before using our findings. In this work, we measure fairness using Equalized Odds
\xspace
and Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
, but if one’s intended use case does not align with these definitions of fairness, the results presented may not be applicable. Furthermore, all previous methods benchmarked in this paper, along with
TADeT
\xspace
, require annotations for the protected attribute, which may not always be feasible.

6.1
Evaluation Setting Details

In Sec 4.1 we mention that we choose three settings to benchmark prior work and evaluate
TADeT
\xspace
. These three settings, described as a tuple (
{y}\xspace,{a}\xspace
), are as follows: i) (Smiling, High Cheekbones), ii) (Wavy Hair, Male), and iii) (Wavy Hair, Wearing Lipstick). Here we provide details on the choice of these three settings.

6.1.1
Smiling with High Cheekbones

Figure 5:
Full dataset distribution of task, protected attribute tuple (Smiling, High Cheekbones).

The first setting we evaluate on is (Smiling, High Cheekbones) (See Fig
5
). We notice that there is a significant skew in the distribution of the CelebA dataset, where most “Smiling” faces are correlated with “High Cheekbones” and most “Not Smiling” faces are correlated with “Not High Cheekbones”. However, we understand that not all such correlations will necessarily translate to a
model
bias. Therefore, we analyze the true positive rate and false positive rate of a transformer trained on this dataset to predict the “Smiling” attribute, for each setting of the protected attribute “High Cheekbones”. We obtain the following results:
\text{TPR}_{{a}\xspace=1}=92.83\%
,
\text{TPR}_{{a}\xspace=0}=67.76\%
,
\text{FPR}_{{a}\xspace=1}=9.65\%
, and
\text{FPR}_{{a}\xspace=0}=2.94\%
. Clearly, the model performs significantly worse on correctly predicting “Smiling” when the individual does
not
have “High Cheekbones” (
{a}\xspace=0)
. Furthermore, the model has a
6.71\%
larger FPR for the “High Cheekbones” group (
{a}\xspace=1)
. This confirms that the model indeed has a strong bias of (spuriously) correlating the presence of “High Cheekbones” with whether they are “Smiling”. Due to this clear bias, we choose (Smiling, High Cheekbones) as our first setting for evaluation.

6.1.2
Wavy Hair with Male

Figure 6:
Full dataset distribution of task, protected attribute tuple (Wavy Hair, Male).

Next, we evaluate on (Wavy Hair, Male) (See Fig
6
). First, we notice that the dataset contains less “Male” individuals (28.5%) than “Not Male” individuals (71.5%). Due to the fact that “Male” is underrepresented in the data, we suspect that the model will not be able to learn as strong of a representation of the images with the “Male” attribute as it will for the “Not Male” attribute, which could lead to some type of bias. Next, we notice that most “Male” individuals are labeled as “Not Wavy Hair”, which could cause a spurious correlation between these two attributes. When analyzing a Transformer trained on the “Wavy Hair” prediction task, we notice exactly that: a high difference in TPR where
\text{TPR}_{{a}\xspace=1}=36.06\%
and
\text{TPR}_{{a}\xspace=0}=67.92\%
. This indicates that a (spurious) dataset correlation of “Male” with “Not Wavy Hair” is being learned by the model, leading to a large bias wherein the model is overpredicting “Male” to have “Not Wavy Hair”.

6.1.3
Wavy Hair with Wearing Lipstick

Figure 7:
Full dataset distribution of task, protected attribute tuple (Wavy Hair, Wearing Lipstick).

Finally, we evaluate on (Wavy Hair, Wearing Lipstick) (See Fig
7
). There is a natural correlation in the dataset where most “Male” individuals are “Not Wearing Lipstick”, and vice versa. Since “Wearing Lipstick” and “Male” are correlated, and “Male” and “Wavy Hair” are correlated (as shown in
6.1.2
), we want to see if the bias that is present when the protected attribute is “Male” will persist when we set the protected attribute as “Wearing Lipstick”. Looking at the dataset distributions themselves, we notice that the number of people “Wearing Lipstick” and “Not Wearing Lipstick” is closer to 50/50 than in the previous setting (protected attribute=“Male”). Next, we notice that there is a correlation in the dataset of people with “Wavy Hair” and people “Wearing Lipstick”. After training a transformer, we notice that the Equalized Odds
\xspace
and Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
are almost as high as the (Wavy Hair, Male) setting. Therefore, we conclude that this setting will be a good test-bed, as the distribution is not as clearly skewed as (Wavy Hair, Male), but there is poor performance on both fairness metrics Equalized Odds
\xspace
and Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
.

6.2
Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
Use Cases

In Sec. 4.1 of the main paper, we introduce Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
, a fairness metric that shares some of the motivation behind accuracy equity
[
Dieterich et al.(2016)Dieterich, Mendoza, and
Brennan
]
, but with an important implementation difference to account for real-world data distributions. While accuracy equity suggests taking the difference in Standard Accuracy
\xspace
across the protected attribute, we take the difference in Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
(our performance metric) across the protected attribute. By doing so, we can account for class imbalance in the dataset, as we saw in Sec.
6.1
. Furthermore Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
is important because it provides a more holistic understanding of the Equalized Odds
\xspace
metric, as we now elaborate.

Consider a situation wherein after debiasing a model, the true positive rate (TPR) difference across a protected attribute
increases
slightly while false positive rate (FPR) difference decreases substantially. Since Equalized Odds
\xspace
is an average of TPR difference and FPR difference (
\text{{Equalized Odds}\xspace}=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]+\dfrac{1}{2}[FPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-FPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]
), the resulting Equalized Odds
\xspace
measure will reduce, indicating that the model is fairer than the original model. However due to the
increased
TPR difference, predicting the positive outcome for the protected attribute is actually
more
unfair than before debiasing! This means that for the positive outcome, a large bias across the protected attribute still exists, which the Equalized Odds
\xspace
metric does not adequately capture.

However, in this situation, the Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
will be high as it looks at the
differences
in Balanced Accuracy
\xspace
within each subgroup. More specifically, Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
can be rewritten as:

\text{Balanced\ Acc.\ Difference}(\Delta\text{BA})=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=0}]-\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=1}]

(7)

=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=1}]-\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}+TNR_{{a}\xspace=0}]

(8)

=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]+\dfrac{1}{2}[(TNR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TNR_{{a}\xspace=0}]

(9)

=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]+\dfrac{1}{2}[(1-FPR_{{a}\xspace=1})-(1-FPR_{{a}\xspace=0})]

(10)

=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]+\dfrac{1}{2}[(FPR_{{a}\xspace=0}-FPR_{{a}\xspace=1})]

(11)

=\dfrac{1}{2}[TPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-TPR_{{a}\xspace=0}]-\dfrac{1}{2}[(FPR_{{a}\xspace=1}-FPR_{{a}\xspace=0})]

(12)

By Eq.
12
, it is clear that given our situation where TPR difference is slightly higher than before debiasing, and FPR difference is lower than before debiasing, Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
would
increase
, indicating that the model is behaving in a biased manner, even though the Equalized Odds
\xspace
measure decreases. Therefore, a user will realize that the drop in Equalized Odds
\xspace
does not tell the full story, as Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
will indicate that their model still encodes a bias, especially towards predicting the positive value for one setting of the protected attribute. Hence, we advocate for using Balanced Accuracy Difference
\xspace
as an
additional
fairness metric, along with Equalized Odds
\xspace
.
