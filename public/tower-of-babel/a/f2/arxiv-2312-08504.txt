1 / 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} -Approximate 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} Allocation for Separable Piecewise Linear Concave Valuations
Chandra Chekuri, Pooja Kulkarni, Rucha Kulkarni, Ruta Mehta

Abstract
We study fair distribution of a collection of m m indivisible goods among a group of n n agents, using the widely recognized fairness principles of Maximin Share ( 𝖬𝖬𝖲 ) ({\sf{MMS}}) and Any Price Share ( 𝖠𝖯𝖲 ) ({\sf{APS}}) . These principles have undergone thorough investigation within the context of additive valuations. We explore these notions for valuations that extend beyond additivity. First, we study approximate 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} under the separable (piecewise-linear) concave ( 𝖲𝖯𝖫𝖢 {\sf SPLC} ) valuations, an important class generalizing additive, where the best known factor was 1 / 3 1/3 - 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} . We show that 1 / 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} - 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} allocation exists and can be computed in polynomial time, significantly improving the state-of-the-art. We note that 𝖲𝖯𝖫𝖢 {\sf SPLC} valuations introduce an elevated level of intricacy in contrast to additive. For instance, the 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} value of an agent can be as high as her value for the entire set of items. 1 1 1 Also the equilibrium computation problem, which is polynomial-time for additive valuations, becomes intractable for 𝖲𝖯𝖫𝖢 {\sf SPLC} [ VY11 ] . We use a relax-and-round paradigm that goes through competitive equilibrium and LP relaxation. Our result extends to give (symmetric) 1 / 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} - 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} , a stronger guarantee than 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} . 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} is a stronger notion that generalizes 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} by allowing agents with arbitrary entitlements. We study the approximation of 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} under submodular valuation functions. We design and analyze a simple greedy algorithm using concave extensions of submodular functions. We prove that the algorithm gives a 1 / 3 \nicefrac{{1}}{{3}} - 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} allocation which matches the best-known factor by [ UF23 ] . Concave extensions are hard to compute in polynomial time and are, therefore, generally not used in approximation algorithms. Our approach shows a way to utilize it within analysis (while bypassing its computation), and hence might be of independent interest.

{\sf{MMS}}

are

{\sf{MMS}}

n

\Pi_{{\mathcal{N}}}({\mathcal{M}})

among the
n
agents. If
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})

n

{\sf{MMS}}_{i}=\max_{(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\Pi_{{\mathcal{N}}}({\mathcal{M}})}\min_{j\in[n]}v_{i}(A_{j})

An

{\sf{MMS}}

]

b_{i}

{\sf{APS}}_{i}=\min_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\in\mathcal{P}}\max_{S\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}:\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}\leq b_{i}}v_{i}(S)

We note that when
b_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
,
{\sf{APS}}_{i}\geq{\sf{MMS}}_{i}

[
BEF21
]

{\sf{APS}}_{i}

Allocations achieving

{\sf{APS}}

-
{\sf{MMS}}

\frac{1}{2}
-
{\sf{MMS}}

One of the difficulties in going beyond additive valuations is the lack of good upper bound on the

2.1
Fair division model

We study the problem of fairly dividing a set of
m
indivisible goods
{\mathcal{M}}=[m]
among a set of
n
agents
{\mathcal{N}}=[n]
who can have asymmetric entitlements. The entitlement or weight of agent
i
is denoted by
b_{i},
and the weights are normalized so that their sum is
1,
that is,
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}b_{i}=1.

The preferences of an agent
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
are defined by a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{{\mathcal{M}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
over the set of goods. We represent a fair division problem instance by
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}},(b_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
. When all agents have the same weight, we denote the instance by
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
and call it the symmetric fair division instance. An allocation
A\coloneqq(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is a partition of all the goods among the
n
agents, i.e. for all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
,
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
and
\cup_{i\in[n]}A_{i}=[m]
. Note that empty parts are allowed. We denote the set of all allocations by
\Pi_{[n]}([m])
. We will also sometimes use fractional allocations of goods. We denote these allocations by
\mathbf{x}
and the allocation of a particular agent,
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
by
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Formally,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{ij})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}},j\in{\mathcal{M}}}
such that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}x_{ij}\leq 1
for all
j\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{ij})_{j\in{\mathcal{M}}}
where
(x_{ij})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}},j\in{\mathcal{M}}}
is a fractional allocation.

Throughout this paper, we assume that the valuation functions are monotone and non-negative. Further, Section
4
assumes that valuations functions are
{\sf SPLC}
and Section
5
assumes that the valuation functions are submodular. We define these classes of functions next.

{\sf SPLC}
valuations.

Separable-concave, a.k.a. separable piecewise-linear concave (
{\sf SPLC}
), valuations is a well-studied class that subsumes additive valuations. Under
{\sf SPLC}
valuations, we have
t
types of goods, with each good
j\in[t]
having
k_{j}
copies. For an
{\sf SPLC}
valuation function
f(\cdot)
, a value
f_{jk}
is associated with
k^{th}
copy of good
j
. The functions are concave so that for all
j\in[t]
, we have
f_{j1}\geq f_{j2}\geq\ldots\geq f_{jk_{j}}
. Finally, the valuations are additive across different goods. Formally, we let
{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of all goods (that includes all copies of each type of good). For all
j\in[t]
, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{j}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
the subset of goods that are copies of good
j
. The value for any set
S\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
is given by
f(S)=\sum_{j\in[t]}\sum_{k\leq|S\cap{\mathcal{M}}_{j}|}f_{jk}
.

Throughout this paper, when we refer to fair division problems in the specific context of
{\sf SPLC}
valuations, we denote the instance by
({\mathcal{N}},t,(k_{j})_{j\in[t]},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
.

Submodular Valuations.

Submodular valuations are a popular class of valuations in the complement-free hierarchy. The valuations are characterized by the property of decreasing marginal utility. In particular, a valuation function
f(\cdot)
is said to be submodular if and only if, for all goods
g\in{\mathcal{M}}
and any subsets
S\subset Q\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
,
f(g\mid S)\geq f(g\mid Q)
, where
f(g\mid S)
denotes the marginal utility of good
g
on set
S
, i.e.
f(g\mid S)\coloneqq f(g\cup S)-f(S)
.

The theory of market equilibrium typically considers fractional allocations of goods. Therefore, in this part we will assume that agents have continuous valuation function. Consider an instance

[

2.

{\sf{APS}}
. Section

5.1
Properties of
{\sf{APS}}

We will use the following properties of
{\sf{APS}}
in our analysis. The proofs are simple and we omit them here.

Claim 5.1
.

For any
z\leq APS_{i}
,
v_{i\downarrow z}^{+}(b_{i},b_{i},\ldots,b_{i})=z
. Moreover, if
\alpha_{S},S\subseteq[m]
is a feasible solution to the LP defining the value
v_{i\downarrow z}^{+}(b_{i},b_{i},\ldots,b_{i})

.

Claim 5.2
.

[Scale-freeness]
If a valuation function

is multiplied by any scalar value
\alpha,
the
{\sf{APS}}
value of an agent with the scaled function is
\alpha
times the
{\sf{APS}}
value of an agent with the original function and same entitlement.

Claim 5.3
.

[Capping retains
{\sf{APS}}
]
If an agent with some entitlement
b
and valuation function
v:2^{{\mathcal{M}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
has
{\sf{APS}}
value
\gamma,
then the
{\sf{APS}}
value of an agent with the same entitlement and the capped function
v^{\prime}({\mathcal{S}}):=\min\{{\sf{APS}},v({\mathcal{S}})\}

\forall Set\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
is also
\gamma.

5.2
Algorithm.

The main subroutine of our algorithm is Algorithm
3
, which takes as input a fair division instance
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}},(b_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
along with a vector of numbers
(\beta_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}
. It outputs an allocation with the following property: each agent
i
receives a bundle of value at least
\frac{1}{3}\beta_{i}
if
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
; note that the guarantee holds for agent
i
even if the
\beta
values for other agents are larger than their
{\sf{APS}}
values. In particular, if we have the
{\sf{APS}}
values of all agents, the algorithm is efficient and gives each agent
\frac{1}{3}
fraction of her
{\sf{APS}}
. Note that computing
{\sf{APS}}
values is known to be
{\sf NP}
-hard. In the next section, we give Algorithm
4
which is a wrapper around Algorithm
3
and bypasses this computational issue.

First, we explain Algorithm
3
. This algorithm works as follows. Given an instance of the fair division problem, and guesses for the
{\sf{APS}}
values of all agents (
\beta_{i}
for agent
i
), it performs two pre-processing steps.
The intuition is the following. Suppose we knew the exact
{\sf{APS}}_{i}
values for each
i
. Since our algorithm is greedy, we must normalise valuations.
{\sf{APS}}
values are scale invariant (Claim
5.2
) therefore, we scale
v_{i}
such that
{\sf{APS}}_{i}=nb_{i}
implying
\sum_{i}{\sf{APS}}_{i}=n
. More over, if we knew
{\sf{APS}}_{i}
then truncating
v_{i}
to
{\sf{APS}}_{i}
is convenient and does not affect the value (Claim
5.3
). In our algorithm we do have
\beta_{i}
values rather than
{\sf{APS}}_{i}
values. Nevertheless we will proceed as if these values are correct estimates. After scaling and truncating we have for each agent
i
a valuation function
\hat{v}_{i}
. We have the property that
\hat{v}_{i}
is truncated at
nb_{i}
.

The key part of the algorithm is the following greedy strategy. The algorithm allocates goods in multiple rounds.
Each round greedily chooses an agent and a good that maximizes the objective
\min\{2nb_{i}/3,v_{i}(j\mid A_{i})\}
over all agents and goods. Recall that
v_{i}(j\mid A_{i})
is the marginal value of the (unallocated) good
j
to agent
i

A_{i}
. The selected good is allocated to the selected agent. As soon as an agent receives a bundle of value at least
nb_{i}/3
, it is satisfied and removed from consideration in future rounds.

It is easy to see that the algorithm will terminate in at most
m
rounds. We will show that at termination the following property is true: for each agent
i
such that
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
,
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\beta_{i}/3
where
A_{i}
is the allocation to
i
. This will be used to obtain a polynomial time algorithm for a
\frac{1}{3}
-
{\sf{APS}}

5.3.1
Approximation Guarantee

The following is the main theorem of our section.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
for an agent
i\in\mathcal{A}
, then Algorithm
3
terminates with an allocation such that
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\frac{1}{3}\beta_{i}
.

We prove Theorem
5.1
by contradiction.
Fix an agent
i
and suppose
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
and
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})<nb_{i}/3
. The algorithm removes
i
from consideration during the algorithm if at time
t
,
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i}^{t})\geq nb_{i}/3
, thus
i
must have stayed active until termination and the algorithm allocated all goods to agents. We compute the total sum of marginals that agent
i
sees for all the goods (allocated to her and other agents). We compute this value in two different ways and obtain a contradiction. We now proceed with analysis of both algorithms here.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}
be the allocation produced by Algorithm
3
. Our goal is to prove that for each agent
i
with
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
,
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\beta_{i}/3
.
We will use the following notation. Algorithm
3
irrevocably assigns one good in each iteration of the while loop. We denote the good assigned in any iteration
t
by
j_{t}
, and the round in which any good
j
was assigned by
t_{j}
. Let
\hat{v}_{i}
denote the valuation function of agent
i
after the pre-processing steps. Also let
A_{i}^{t}
denote the bundle allocated to agent
i
at the beginning of round
t
(that is before
j_{t}
is allocated).

Now Step
3
iteratively chooses an (agent, good) pair using a greedy strategy - the appropriately capped maximum marginal value of a good. We define three terms that arise from the greedy strategy definition. First, suppose the pair
(i,j)
was chosen in some round
t.
Let
\rho(j)
denote the unique capped marginal value that it contributed to
i
when it was assigned, that is,
\rho(j)\coloneqq\min\left\{\frac{2}{3}nb_{i},\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i}^{t})\right\}.
Second, for any round
t
, and an agent
i^{\prime}
that is active in round
t
, denote by
\mu(i^{\prime},t)
the maximum (capped) marginal value that
i^{\prime}

sees
, meaning the greedy strategy value computed by fixing agent
i^{\prime}
and maximizing only over all the remaining goods; formally,
\mu(i^{\prime},t)=\max_{j\in{\mathcal{M}}^{r}}\min\left\{\frac{2}{3}nb_{i^{\prime}},\hat{v}_{i^{\prime}}(j\mid A_{i^{\prime}}^{t})\right\}
. Finally, for each good
j
, we denote by
\mu_{i}(j)
the capped marginal value that agent
i
sees for good
j
in the round that
j
was allocated, that is,
\mu_{i}(j)=\min\left\{\frac{2}{3}nb_{i},\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i}^{t_{j}})\right\}
. To summarize,
\rho(j)
is the value at which good
j
was assigned and
\mu_{i}(j)
is the value that
i
would have received for good
j
. Because of the greedy nature of our algorithm,
\rho(j)\geq\mu_{i}(j)
.

We first make a basic observation.
\hat{v}_{i}(S)=\min\{nb_{i},\frac{nb_{i}}{\beta_{i}}v_{i}(S)\}
for all
S\subseteq[m]
. Thus,

v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\frac{1}{3}\beta_{i}
iff
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})\geq nb_{i}/3
. Therefore, now on we will focus on proving
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})\geq nb_{i}/3
whenever
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
, which will imply Theorem
5.1
. As mentioned in Section
5
, we prove Theorem
5.1
by contradiction.
Fix an agent
i
and suppose
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
and
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})<nb_{i}/3
. The algorithm removes
i
from consideration during the algorithm if at time
t
,
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i}^{t})\geq nb_{i}/3
, thus
i
must have stayed active until termination and the algorithm allocated all goods to agents. We compute the total sum of marginals that agent
i
sees for all the goods (allocated to her and other agents). We compute this value in two different ways and obtain a contradiction.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any agent
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\rho(j)=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)\leq\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}.

Moreover, if
|A_{i}|>1
then
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\rho(j)
.

Proof.

Consider two cases, where
|A_{i}|=1
and
|A_{i}|>1
In the first case, agent
i
wins exactly one good. If
r
is the round when
i
wins, since the marginal of the good she gets is capped at
\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}
, hence by definition,
\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\rho(j)=\rho(j_{r})\leq\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}
.

Now consider the case when
|A_{i}|>1
. Let
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{h}
be the items allocated to
i
in increasing order of time, that is
t_{j_{1}}<t_{j_{2}}\ldots<t_{j_{h}}
. For ease of notation we use
t_{\ell}
in place of
t_{j_{\ell}}
.
Note that
i
is not allotted further goods by the algorithm once the value for their bundle is at least
nb_{i}/3
. This gives us two things. First,
\hat{v}_{i}(j_{1})<nb_{i}/3
. Hence, by greedy choice, for any
\ell>1
,
\hat{v}_{i}(j_{\ell})<nb_{i}/3
, and by submodularity,

\hat{v}_{i}(j_{\ell}\mid A_{i}^{t_{\ell}})<nb_{i}/3
.
Therefore, the marginal value of
i
for all the goods they win, over the empty set, thus by submodularity over any set, is smaller than
nb_{i}/3.
Second,
i
’s value before they received the last good is smaller than
nb_{i}/3
.
Combining these inferences we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\rho(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{h}\min\left\{\frac{2}{3}nb_{i},\hat{v}_{i}(j_{\ell}\mid A_{i}^{t_{\ell}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{h}\hat{v}_{i}(j_{\ell}\mid A_{i}^{t_{\ell}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{h-1}\hat{v}_{i}(j_{\ell}\mid A_{i}^{t_{\ell}})+\hat{v}_{i}(j_{h}\mid A_{i}^{t_{h}})

\displaystyle=\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i}^{t_{h}})+\hat{v}_{i}(j_{h}\mid A_{i}^{t_{h}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}nb_{i}+\frac{1}{3}nb_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}.\qed

Lemma 5.2
.

Consider agent
i
that is active through out the algorithm. The total sum of capped marginals over all goods in
[m]\setminus A_{i}
, according to agent
i
, is at most
\frac{2}{3}n(1-b_{i})
, that is,

\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{k}}\mu_{i}(j)\leq\frac{2}{3}n(1-b_{i}).

Proof.

If
i
is active through out the algorithm then all items are allocated to some agent. Consider any item
j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}
. By the greedy rule
\rho(j)\geq\mu_{i}(j)
, otherwise
j
would be allocated to
i
. Thus

\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)\leq\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\rho(j)
. Since each item in
j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}
is allocated to some other agent, we can use Lemma
5.1
and see that

\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\rho(j)=\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{j\in A_{k}}\rho(j)\leq\sum_{k\neq i}2nb_{k}/3=2n(1-b_{i})/3.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose there is an agent
i
with
\beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
and receives a bundle
A_{i}
such that
\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})<\frac{1}{3}nb_{i}
then,

\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)\geq\frac{2}{3}n.

Proof.

{\sf{APS}}_{i}
is the
{\sf{APS}}
value for agent
i
with valuation function
v_{i}
. Recall that
v^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{nb_{i}}{\beta_{i}}v_{i}
and thus the
{\sf{APS}}
value with respect to
v^{\prime}_{i}
is

\frac{nb_{i}}{\beta_{i}}{\sf{APS}}_{i}\geq nb_{i}
(
\because\ \beta_{i}\leq{\sf{APS}}_{i}
). The function
\hat{v}_{i}
is obtained by truncating
v^{\prime}_{i}
at
nb_{i}
. Thus, from Claim
5.1
, we have
\hat{v}_{i}^{+}(b_{i},b_{i},\ldots,b_{i})=nb_{i}
. From the definition of concave extension and Claim
5.1
, there are sets of items

S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{h}
and reals
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{h}\in[0,1]
such that (i)
\sum_{r=1}^{j}\alpha_{r}=1
and (ii)
\hat{v}_{i}(S_{r})=nb_{i}
for each

and (iii) for each item

\sum_{r:j\in S_{r}}\alpha_{r}\leq b_{i}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum_{r:j\in S_{r}}\alpha_{r}\right)\mu_{i}(j)

(by property (iii) above.)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\sum_{r:j\in S_{r}}\alpha_{r}\mu_{i}(j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum_{r\in[h]}\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\alpha_{r}\mu_{i}(j)

(changing the order of summations.)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum_{r\in[h]}\alpha_{r}\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)

(10)

Now by definition of Step
3
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)=\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\min\left\{\frac{2}{3}nb_{i},\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i}^{t(j)})\right\}

If any of the minimums in the above equation is
\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}
, then
\sum_{j\in S_{r}}\mu_{i}(j)\geq\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}
. On the other hand, suppose all minimums evaluate to the uncapped marginal value. Then we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i}^{t(j)})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i})

. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S_{r}\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S_{r}}\hat{v}_{i}(j\mid A_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\hat{v}_{i}(S_{r}\mid A_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{v}_{i}(S_{r}\cup A_{i})-\hat{v}_{i}(A_{i})

\displaystyle>nb_{i}-\frac{1}{3}nb_{i}=\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}

\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus A_{i}}\mu_{i}(j)\geq\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum_{r\in[h]}\alpha_{r}\frac{2}{3}nb_{i}=\frac{2}{3}n.\qed

5.1
.

Recall that we are assuming that there is an agent
i

Remark 5.1
.

The greedy strategy of Algorithm
3
caps the marginal values to
2nb_{i}/3.
The reason for adding this cap instead of considering the uncapped marginals is single good allocations. The analysis fails for the alternative simpler greedy strategy of uncapped marginals only in the first case of Lemma
5.1
. Therefore, if the input instance satisfies
v_{i}(\{j\})\leq 2{\sf{APS}}_{i}/3
for every
i
and every good

then the simpler greedy strategy will suffice. For obtaining approximate
{\sf{MMS}}
allocations, we can assume this without loss of generality, by what are popularly called single good reductions. However, such reductions do not work for the
{\sf{APS}}
problem, and adding the caps cleverly bypasses this issue.

First, we know from Theorem

we use this guarantee to guess a value of

for every

that is at most

as follows. Note that,

Now iteratively, run Algorithm

B.1
Multilinear Extension

Multilinear extension,
\mathbb{F}(\cdot)
of a function
f(\cdot)
is defined as follows.

Definition B.1

(Multilinear Extension of
f
)
.

\mathbb{F}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{S\subseteq V}\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}\prod_{j\in V\setminus S}(1-x_{j})f(S)

(11)

Both Concave extension and Multilinear extension can be defined for any arbitrary set functions, they satisfy nice properties when functions are submodular.

Multilinear extension has been widely used in previous literature on fair division. However, we prove the following claim which shows that under Multilinear extension with submodular valuations, it is possible to have instances where no fractional allocation is
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
.

Claim B.1
.

There are instances where no fractional
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
allocation exists for submodular valuations under multilinear extension.

Proof.

Consider a fair division instance
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
where all valuations
v_{i}(\cdot)
are monotone, non-negative submodular functions. Recall that
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
denotes the
{\sf{MMS}}
value of agent
i
in this instance. Consider new valuation functions,
\hat{v}_{i}(\cdot)
defined as
\hat{v}_{i}(S)\coloneqq\min\{v_{i}(S),{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\}
. Note that
\hat{v}_{i}(\cdot)
are also monotone, non-negative submodular functions and the
{\sf{MMS}}
value of agent
i
is retained.

Suppose for contradiction, there exists a fractional allocation that under Multilinear extension gives
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
to each agent. Consider the Fair Division instance
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(\hat{v}_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{ij})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}},j\in{\mathcal{M}}}
denote the fractional allocation such that for all
i\in[n]
,
\hat{v}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})={\sf{MMS}}_{i}
. Note that
\mathbf{x}
is also the social welfare maximizing allocation. Therefore we can round
\mathbf{x}
using Pipage rounding
[
AS04
]
without any loss of value to any agent giving us an integral allocation that is
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
. In particular, we show that if there is a fractional
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
allocation under Multilinear extension then there is an integral
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
allocation. However, it is well known that
1
-
{\sf{MMS}}
allocation might not exist with submodular valuations. Therefore, the claim stands proved.
∎
