Abstract
. In this paper we use the Riemann–Hilbert problem, with jumps supported on appropriate curves in the complex plane, for matrix biorthogonal polynomials and apply it to find Sylvester systems of differential equations for the orthogonal polynomials and its second kind functions as well. For this aim, Sylvester type differential Pearson equations for the matrix of weights are shown to be instrumental. Several applications are given, in order of increasing complexity. First, a general discussion of non-Abelian Hermite biorthogonal polynomials in the real line, understood as those whose matrix of weights is a solution of a Sylvester type Pearson equation with coefficients first order matrix polynomials, is given. All these is applied to the discussion of possible scenarios leading to eigenvalue problems for second order linear differential operators with matrix eigenvalues. Nonlinear matrix difference equations are discussed next. Firstly, for the general Hermite situation a general non linear relation (non trivial because the non commutativity features of the setting) for the recursion coefficients is gotten. In the next case of higher difficulty, degree two polynomials are allowed in the Pearson equation, but the discussion is simplified by considering only a left Pearson equation. In the case, the support of the measure is on an appropriate branch of an hyperbola. The recursion coefficients are shown to fulfill a non-Abelian extension of the alternate discrete Painlevé I equation. Finally, a discussion is given for the case of degree three polynomials as coefficients in the left Pearson equation characterizing the matrix of weights. However, for simplicity only odd polynomials are allowed. In this case, a new and more general matrix extension of the discrete Painlevé I equation is found.

In this work we obtain Sylvester systems of differential equations for the orthogonal polynomials and its second kind functions, directly from a Riemann–Hilbert problem, with jumps supported on appropriate curves in the complex plane. The differential properties for the weight function are fundamental. In this case we consider a Sylvester type differential Pearson equation for the matrix of weights.
We also study whenever the orthogonal polynomials and its second kind functions are solutions of a second order linear differential operators with matrix eigenvalues. This is done by stating an appropriate boundary value problem for the matrix of weights.
In particular, special attention is paid to non-Abelian Hermite biorthogonal polynomials in the real line, understood as those whose matrix of weights is a solution of a Sylvester type Pearson equation with given first order matrix polynomials coefficients.

Several applications are given, in order of increasing complexity, as well. First, we return to the non-Abelian Hermite biorthogonal polynomials in the real line, and give nonlinear matrix difference equations for the recurrent coefficients of the non-Abelian Hermite biorthogonal polynomials.
Next, we consider the orthogonal polynomials and functions of second kind associated with matrix of weights, that satisfy a differential matrix Person equation with degree two polynomials as coefficients. To simplify the discussion, only a left Pearson equation is considered. In this case, the support of the measure belongs to an appropriate branch of an hyperbola, and the recursion coefficients are shown to fulfill a non-Abelian extension of the scalar alternate discrete Painlevé I equation.
Finally, a discussion is given for the case of degree three polynomials as coefficients in the left Pearson equation characterizing the matrix of weights. However, for simplicity only odd polynomials are allowed. In this case, a new and more general matrix extension of the discrete Painlevé equation is found. To end this study we present a comparison with the results already obtained by several authors in the scalar and matrix cases.

2.
Riemann–Hilbert problem for Matrix Biorthogonal Polynomials

2.1.
Matrix biorthogonal polynomials

Let

\displaystyle W=\begin{bmatrix}W^{(1,1)}&\cdots&W^{(1,N)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
W^{(N,1)}&\cdots&W^{(N,N)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}

be a
N\times N
matrix of weights with support on a smooth oriented non self-intersecting curve
\gamma
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, i.e.

W^{(j,k)}

is, for each
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, a complex weight with support on
\gamma
.
We define the
moment of order
n
associated with
W
as

\displaystyle W_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}z^{n}W(z)\operatorname{d}z,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}.

We say that
W
is
regular
if

\det\big[W_{j+k}\big]_{j,k=0,\ldots n}\not=0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In this way, we define a
sequence of matrix monic polynomials
,

\big\{{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
left orthogonal
and
right orthogonal
,
\big\{P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to a regular matrix measure
W
, by the conditions,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)W(z)z^{k}\operatorname{d}z=\delta_{n,k}C_{n}^{-1},

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}z^{k}W(z){P}_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\operatorname{d}z=\delta_{n,k}C_{n}^{-1},

for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
where
C_{n}
is an nonsingular matrix.

Notice that neither the matrix of weights is requested to be Hermitian
nor the curve
\gamma
to be the real line, i.e., we are dealing, in principle with nonstandard orthogonality and, consequently, with biorthogonal matrix polynomials instead of orthogonal matrix polynomials.

The matrix of weights induce a sesquilinear form in the set of matrix
polynomials
\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}[z]
given by

(3)

\displaystyle\langle P,Q\rangle_{W}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}{P}(z)W(z)Q(z)\operatorname{d}z.

Moreover, we say that
\big\{P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\big\{P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are biorthogonal with respect to a matrix weight functions
W
if

(4)

\displaystyle\big\langle P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}},{P}_{m}^{\mathsf{R}}\big\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}C_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle\ n,m

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N}.

As the polynomials are chosen to be monic, we can write

\displaystyle{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=Iz^{n}+p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{1}z^{n-1}+p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{2}z^{n-2}+\cdots+p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{n},

\displaystyle{P}_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=Iz^{n}+p_{\mathsf{R},n}^{1}z^{n-1}+p_{\mathsf{R},n}^{2}z^{n-2}+\cdots+p_{\mathsf{R},n}^{n},

with matrix coefficients
p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{k},p_{\mathsf{R},n}^{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,
k=0,\ldots,n
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
(imposing that
p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{0}=p_{\mathsf{R},n}^{0}=I
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
). Here
I\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
denotes the identity matrix.

2.2.
Three term relations

From (
1
) we deduce that the Fourier coefficients of the expansion

\displaystyle z{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\ell_{\mathsf{L},k}^{n}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{k}(z),

are given by
\ell_{\mathsf{L},k}^{n}=0_{N}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,n-2
(here we denote the zero matrix by
0_{N}
),

\ell_{\mathsf{L},n-1}^{n}=C_{n}^{-1}C_{n-1}
(is a direct consequence of orthogonality conditions),
\ell_{\mathsf{L},n+1}^{n}=I\,
(as
{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)
are monic polynomials) and

\ell_{\mathsf{L},n}^{n}=p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{1}-p_{\mathsf{L},n+1}^{1}=:\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}

(by comparison of the coefficients, assuming
C_{0}=I
).

Hence, assuming the orthogonality relations (
1
), we conclude that the sequence of monic polynomials
\big\{{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined by the three term recurrence relations

(5)

\displaystyle z{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle={P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)+\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)+\gamma^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n-1}(z),

\displaystyle\ n

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N},

with recursion coefficients

\displaystyle\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=p_{\mathsf{L},n}^{1}-p_{\mathsf{L},n+1}^{1},

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=C_{n}^{-1}C_{n-1},

with initial conditions,
{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}=0_{N}
and

{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{0}=I
.

Any sequence of monic matrix polynomials,
\big\{P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
\deg P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}=n
, biorthogonal with respect to
\big\{P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
W(z)
, i.e. (
4
)
is fulfilled, also satisfies a three term relation. To prove this we compute the Fourier coefficients of
zP^{\mathsf{R}}_{m}(z)
in the expansion

\displaystyle zP^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}P^{\mathsf{R}}_{k}(z)\ell^{n}_{\mathsf{R},k},

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathsf{R},k}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}zP^{\mathsf{L}}_{k}(z)W(z)P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\operatorname{d}z.

From (
1
) we have

\ell_{\mathsf{R},n+1}^{n}=I
,
\ell_{\mathsf{R},n}^{n}=C_{n}\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}C_{n}^{-1}
,
\ell_{\mathsf{R},n-1}^{n}=C_{n-1}C_{n}^{-1}
, and
\ell_{\mathsf{R},k}^{n}=0_{N}
,
k=0,\dots,n-2
, i.e.
the sequence of monic polynomials
\big\{P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies

(6)

\displaystyle P^{\mathsf{R}}_{-1}

\displaystyle=0_{N},

\displaystyle P^{\mathsf{R}}_{0}

\displaystyle=I,

\displaystyle zP^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n+1}(z)+P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\beta^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}+P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n-1}(z)\gamma^{\mathsf{R}}_{n},

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N},

where

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}

\displaystyle:=C_{n}\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}C_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=C_{n}\gamma^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}C_{n}^{-1}=C_{n-1}C_{n}^{-1},

and the orthogonality conditions (
2
) are satisfied.

2.3.
Second kind functions

We define the
sequence of second kind matrix functions
by

(7)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{P^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}{W(z^{\prime})}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime},

(8)

\displaystyle{Q}_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}W(z^{\prime})\frac{P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
From the orthogonality conditions (
1
) and (
2
) we have, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the following asymptotic expansion near infinity for the sequence of functions of the second kind

(9)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=-C_{n}^{-1}\big(Iz^{-n-1}+q_{\mathsf{L},n}^{1}z^{-n-2}+\cdots\big),

(10)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=-\big(Iz^{-n-1}+q_{\mathsf{R},n}^{1}z^{-n-2}+\cdots\big)C_{n}^{-1}.

Assuming that the measures
W^{(j,k)}
,
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
are Hölder continuous we obtain, by the Plemelj’s formula applied to (
7
) and (
8
), the following fundamental jump identities

(11)

\displaystyle\big(Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}-\big(Q_{n}(z)^{\mathsf{L}}\big)_{-}

\displaystyle={P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)W(z),

(12)

\displaystyle\big(Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{+}-\big(Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)_{-}

\displaystyle=W(z){P}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z),

z\in\gamma
, where,

\big(f(z)\big)_{\pm}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{\pm}}f(z+i\epsilon)
;
here
\pm
indicates the the positive/negative region according to the orientation of the curve
\gamma
.

Now, multiplying this equation on the right by
W
and integrating we get, using the definition (
7
) of
\big\{Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{z^{\prime}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}{W(z^{\prime})}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime}=Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)+\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)+C_{n}^{-1}C_{n-1}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n-1}(z).

As
\frac{z^{\prime}}{z^{\prime}-z}=1+\frac{z}{z^{\prime}-z}
, from the orthogonality conditons (
1
) we conclude that

\displaystyle zQ^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)+\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)+C_{n}^{-1}C_{n-1}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n-1}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N},

with initial conditions

\displaystyle Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}(z)=Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{-1}(z)=-C_{-1}^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{0}(z)=Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{0}(z)=S_{W}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{W(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime},

where
S_{W}(z)
is the
Stieltjes–Markov
transformation of the matrix of weights
W
, which is a complex measure of orthogonality for
\big\{{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
–direct consequence of Fubini theorem and Cauchy integral formula.
It can be seen that

\displaystyle{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)Q_{0}(z)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int\frac{{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z^{\prime})-{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)}{z^{\prime}-z}{W(z^{\prime})}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int\frac{{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}{W(z^{\prime})}\operatorname{d}z^{\prime},

i.e. we have the Hermite–Padé formula for the left orthogonal polynomials,

\displaystyle{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)S_{W}(z)+{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z),

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N},

where

\displaystyle{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{L},(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int\frac{P^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z^{\prime})-P^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)}{z^{\prime}-z}{W(z^{\prime})}\operatorname{d}w,

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N},

is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
said to be the
first kind associated polynomial with respect to

\big\{P^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
W(z)
.
Similarly, for the right situation we have the associated

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\mathsf{R},(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}W(z^{\prime})\frac{P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n+1}(z^{\prime})-P^{\mathsf{R}}_{n+1}(z)}{z^{\prime}-z}\operatorname{d}w,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N},

and the corresponding Hermite–Padé formula for the right orthogonal polynomials,

\displaystyle S_{W}(z){P}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)+{P}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{R},(1)}(z)

\displaystyle=Q^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}.

2.4.
Reductions: from biorthogonality to orthogonality

We consider two possible reductions for the matrix of weights, the symmetric reduction and the Hermitian reduction.

i)

A matrix of weights
W(z)
with support on
\gamma
is said to be symmetric if

\displaystyle(W(z))^{\top}

\displaystyle=W(z),

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\gamma.

ii)

A matrix of weights
W(x)
with support on
\mathbb{R}
is said to be Hermitian if

\displaystyle(W(x))^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=W(x),

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

These two reductions leads to orthogonal polynomials, as the two biorthogonal families are identified; i.e., for the symmetric case

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\top},

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\top},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C},

and for the Hermitian case, with
\gamma=\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(\bar{z})\big)^{\dagger},

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(\bar{z})\big)^{\dagger},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}.

In both cases biorthogonality collapses into orthogonality, that
for the symmetric case reads as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\gamma}P_{n}(z)W(z)\big({P}_{m}(z)\big)^{\top}\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}C_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle\ n,m

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N},

while for the Hermitian case can be written as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\operatorname{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{n}(x)W(x)\big({P}_{m}(x)\big)^{\dagger}\operatorname{d}x

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}C_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle\ n,m

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N},

where
P_{n}=P_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}
.

2.5.
Fundamental and transfer matrices vs Riemann–Hilbert problems

We can summarize the left three term relation as follows

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)&Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n+1}(z)\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)&-C_{n}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}zI-\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}&C_{n}^{-1}\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n}&{0}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)&Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n-1}{P}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n-1}(z)&-C_{n-1}Q^{\mathsf{L}}_{n-1}(z)\end{bmatrix};

and

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{P}_{n}^{{\mathsf{L}},(1)}(z)\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n}{P}_{n-1}^{{\mathsf{L}},(1)}(z)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}zI-\beta^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}&C_{n}^{-1}\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n}&{0}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{P}_{n-1}^{{\mathsf{L}},(1)}(z)\\[1.42271pt]
-C_{n-1}{P}_{n-2}^{{\mathsf{L}},(1)}(z)\end{bmatrix}.

In terms of the left
fundamental matrix

Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and the left
transfer matrix

T_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
,

\displaystyle Y^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

3.
Constant jump on the support, structure matrices and zero curvature

So far we have discuss the connection between biorthogonal families of matrix polynomials for a given matrix of weights

W
and a specific Riemann–Hilbert problem. Now, to derive difference and/or differential equations satisfied by these families of matrix polynomials we will we move to a simpler setting and we will assume that the following holds

i)
The matrix of weights factors out as
W(z)=W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
,
z\in\gamma
.

ii)
The factors
W^{\mathsf{L}}
and
W^{\mathsf{R}}
are the restriction to the curve
\gamma
of matrices of entire
functions
W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and
W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

iii)
The right logarithmic derivative
h^{\mathsf{L}}(z):=\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\prime}\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{-1}
and the left logarithmic derivative

h^{\mathsf{L}}(z):=\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{-1}\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{\prime}
are also entire functions.

We underline that for a given matrix of weights
W(z)
we will have many possible factorization
W(z)=W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
. Indeed, if we define an equivalence relation
(W^{\mathsf{L}},W^{\mathsf{R}})\sim(\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{L}},\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{R}})
if and only if
W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}=\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{L}}\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{R}}
, then each matrix of weights
W
can be though as a class of equivalence, and can be described the orbit

\displaystyle\Big\{(W^{\mathsf{L}}\phi,\phi^{-1}W^{\mathsf{R}}),\ \phi(z)\ \text{ is a nonsingular matrix of entire functions}\Big\}\,.

3.1.

W=W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}

.

Proposition 4

(Computing the structure matrices)
.

If the subindex

\displaystyle=\Bigg(S^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\begin{bmatrix}\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\prime}\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{-1}&0_{N}\\

\displaystyle{M}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\big({S}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{-1}\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{\prime}&0_{N}\\

Using assumption i) in Proposition
2

4.
Matrix Pearson equations and Differential equations

4.1.
Matrix Pearson equations

As we have seen, the left and right logarithmic derivatives,
h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)=\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\prime}\big(W^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{-1}
and
h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)=\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{-1}\big(W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\big)^{\prime}
, play an important role in the discussion of the structure matrices.
This motivates us to adopt the following strategy:
assume that instead of a given matrix of weights we are provided with two matrices, say
h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and
h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
, of entire functions such that the following two matrix Pearson equations are satisfied

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}W^{\mathsf{L}}}{\operatorname{d}z}

\displaystyle=h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)W^{\mathsf{L}}(z),

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}W^{\mathsf{R}}}{\operatorname{d}z}

\displaystyle=W^{\mathsf{R}}(z)h^{\mathsf{R}}(z);

and given solutions to them we construct the corresponding matrix of weights
W=W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}
. Moreover, this matrix of weights is also characterized by a Pearson equation.

Proposition 5

(Pearson Sylvester differential equation)
.

Given two matrices of entire functions
h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and
h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
, any solution of the Sylvester type matrix differential equation, which we call Pearson equation for the weight,

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}W}{\operatorname{d}z}=h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)W(z)+W(z)h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

is of the form
W=W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}
where the factor matrices
W^{\mathsf{L}}
and
W^{\mathsf{R}}
are solutions of (
34
) and (
35
), respectively.

Proof.

Given solutions
W^{\mathsf{L}}
and
W^{\mathsf{R}}
of (
34
) and (
35
), respectively, it follows intermediately, just using the Leibniz law for derivatives, that
W=W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}
fulfills (
36
). Moreover, given a solution
W
of (
36
) we pick a solution
W^{\mathsf{L}}
of (
34
), then it is easy to see that
(W^{\mathsf{L}})^{-1}W
satisfies (
35
).
∎

Remark 5
.

The matrix of weights
W
does not uniquely determine the left and right factors; indeed if
W=W^{\mathsf{L}}W^{\mathsf{R}}
, with factors solving (
34
) and (
35
), respectively, then

\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{L}}=W^{\mathsf{L}}C
and
\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{R}}=C^{-1}W^{\mathsf{R}}
for
C
a nonsingular matrix, gives also another possible factorization
W=\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{L}}\widetilde{W}^{\mathsf{R}}
, with factors solving the partial Pearson equations (
34
) and (
35
). This indeterminacy disappears when one considers the right and left derivatives of the factors.

Remark 6
.

Given two matrices of entire functions
h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and
h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
and a matrix of weights
W
characterized by the matrix Pearson equation (

) we have the left and right fundamental matrices
Y_{n}^{\mathsf{L}}(z)
and

satisfying corresponding Riemann–Hilbert problems. The associated structure matrices are from (
32
) and (
33
) given by,

(37)

\displaystyle M^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(S^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\begin{bmatrix}h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)&0_{N}\\
0_{N}&-h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)\end{bmatrix}\big(S^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}(z)\big)^{-1}\Bigg)_{\boldsymbol{+}},

(38)

\displaystyle{M}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\big({S}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\big)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)&0_{N}\\
0_{N}&-h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\end{bmatrix}{S}^{\mathsf{R}}_{n}(z)\Bigg)_{\boldsymbol{+}}.

.

For the symmetric and Hermitian reductions, we have

\displaystyle h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(h^{\mathsf{L}}(z)\big)^{\top},

\displaystyle\text{symmetric},

\displaystyle h^{\mathsf{R}}(z)
