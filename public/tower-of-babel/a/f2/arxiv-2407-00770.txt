Quantitative tightness for three-dimensional contact manifolds: a sub-Riemannian approach
Andrei A. Agrachev 1, Stefano Baranzini 2, Eugenio Bellini 3, Luca Rizzi 1

Abstract
. Through the use of sub-Riemannian metrics we provide quantitative estimates for the maximal tight neighbourhood of a Reeb orbit on a three-dimensional contact manifold. Under appropriate geometric conditions we show how to construct closed curves which are boundaries of overtwisted disks. We introduce the concept of contact Jacobi curve, and prove lower bounds of the so-called tightness radius (from a Reeb orbit) in terms of Schwarzian derivative bounds. We compare these results with the corresponding ones from [ EKM12 , EKM16 ] , and we show that our estimates are sharp for classical model structures. We also prove similar, but non-sharp, estimates in terms of sub-Riemannian canonical curvature bounds. We apply our results to K-contact sub-Riemannian manifolds. In this setting, we prove a contact analogue of the celebrated Cartan–Hadamard theorem.



1.
Introduction

A (co-oriented) contact structure
\xi
on a
3
-manifold
M
is a totally non-integrable plane field, i.e.
\xi=\ker\omega
for some one-form
\omega
satisfying the non-integrability condition
\omega\wedge d\omega\neq 0
. A contact manifold is called
overtwisted
if it contains an embedded disk such that
\xi
is tangent to the disk in a unique interior point and along the boundary (see
2.4
). A contact manifold is called
tight
if it is not overtwisted.

The two categories defined by the tight/overtwisted dichotomy have demonstrated surprisingly different properties. Even tough overtwisted contact structures on 3-manifolds have been classified
[
Eli89
]
and tight contact structures have been extensively studied, it is not easy to determine whether a given contact structure is tight or overtwisted.

The well-known Darboux theorem states that contact structures can be locally normalized: any point in a contact manifold has a neighbourhood diffeomorphic to the standard contact structure
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{st}})
, where
\omega_{\mathrm{st}}=dz+\tfrac{1}{2}(xdy-ydx)
. It is well-known that the latter structure is tight
[
Ben83
]
. Combining this fact and Darboux theorem we deduce that every contact structure is locally tight. If we endow the contact manifold with a metric structure, then it makes sense to ask the following question: what is the size of the maximal tight neighbourhood of a given point? In
[
EKM12
,
EKM16
]
the authors estimated the answer in the context of Riemannian geometry, unveiling surprising connections between different notions of convexity, Riemannian curvature, and tightness.

In this paper, instead, we investigate tightness criteria and geometric detection of overtwisted disks via sub-Riemannian geometry. The fact that the sub-Riemannian geodesics are tangent to
\xi
makes this framework natural and well-adapted to contact geometry. In this regard, recent works
[
BBC22
,
BB23
]
have linked sub-Riemannian geometry to contact topology.

In order to introduce our results, we anticipate some definitions. A (three-dimensional) contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
is a co-oriented contact manifold
(M,\xi)
, with
\xi=\ker\omega
, endowed with a bundle metric
g
on
\xi
. Any positive rescaling of
\omega
defines the same contact structure. We always normalize
\omega
via the sub-Riemannian metric, see (
45
), so that
\omega
is uniquely determined by
g
and
\xi
. This choice, in turn, yields a canonical complement of
\xi
: the so-called
Reeb field
, denoted by
f_{0}
.

To every
(M,\omega,g)
there is an associated
sub-Riemannian distance

d
. For any
p,q\in M
,
d(p,q)
is defined as the infimum of the lengths of the curves tangent to
\xi
and joining the two points. Thanks to the contact condition,
(M,d)
is a metric space with the same topology of
M
. In contact sub-Riemannian manifolds, geodesics (i.e., locally length-minimizing curves parametrized by constant speed) are projections of solutions of a Hamiltonian system on
T^{*}M
. To introduce it, we define the sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian
H:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
as

(2)

H(\lambda):=\max\left\{\langle\lambda,v\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{g}^{2}\,\Big|\,v\in\xi_{\pi(\lambda)}\right\},\qquad\lambda\in T^{*}M,

which is a smooth and fiber-wise non-negative quadratic form (albeit degenerate and with non-compact level sets). We denote with
\vec{H}
the vector field on
T^{*}M
defined as the symplectic gradient of
H
. Sub-Riemannian geodesics on a contact structure are precisely the projections of integral curves of
\vec{H}
. In the following, we tacitly assume that
(M,d)
is a complete metric space, in which case
\vec{H}
is a complete vector field.

1.1.
Tightness radius from a Reeb orbit

Let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit (which can also be periodic, see
Section

3
). The annihilator bundle of
\Gamma
is

(3)

A\Gamma:=\{\lambda\in T^{*}M\mid\pi(\lambda)\in\Gamma,\,\,\langle\lambda\,,f_{0}\rangle=0\}.

Given
r>0
we also define the following bundles:

(4)

A^{<r}\Gamma:=A\Gamma\cap\{\sqrt{2H}<r\},\qquad A^{1}\Gamma:=A\Gamma\cap\{\sqrt{2H}=1\}.

A\Gamma
(resp.
A^{1}\Gamma
) plays the same role of the normal bundle (resp. unit normal bundle) in Riemannian geometry. The injectivity radius of
\Gamma
is defined as

(5)

r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)=\sup\{r>0\mid\text{$E:A^{<r}\Gamma\to M$ is a diffeomorphism onto its image}\},

where
E
is the tubular neighbourhood map, namely the restriction to
A\Gamma
of the sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian flow:

(6)

E:A\Gamma\to M,\qquad E(\lambda):=\pi\circ e^{\vec{H}}(\lambda).

It turns out that, for
\lambda\in A^{<r}\Gamma
, and
r=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, we have
d(\Gamma,E(\lambda))=\sqrt{2H(\lambda)}
so that sub-Riemannian geodesic involved in this construction realize the distance from
\Gamma
. We are interested in the size of the largest such neighbourhood on which the contact structure is tight.

Definition

(Tightness radius)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. The tightness radius is

(7)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma):=\sup\left\{0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\text{$(A^{<r}\Gamma,\ker E^{*}\omega)$ is tight}\right\}.

If
\Gamma
is a (whole) Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
, then letting
B_{r}(\Gamma)
the set of points at sub-Riemannian distance from
\Gamma
smaller than
r
, it holds
E\left(A^{<r}\Gamma\right)=B_{r}(\Gamma)
for all
0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
. Therefore the tightness radius can be expressed (for whole orbits) as

(8)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\sup\left\{0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\left(B_{r}(\Gamma),\ker\omega|_{B_{r}(\Gamma)}\right)\,\,\text{is tight}\right\}.

See
Remarks

3.5
and
4.2
for details.

1.2.
Contact Jacobi curves

The main tool that we introduce to study the tightness radius is the
contact Jacobi curve
. These are inspired by Jacobi curves in (sub-)Riemannian geometry and geometric control theory, which are curves in the Lagrange Grassmannian whose dynamics is intertwined with the presence of conjugate points and curvature (see
[
AG97
,
AZ02a
,
AZ02b
,
ABR18
]
and references within). The dynamics of contact Jacobi curves, instead, is related with the presence of overtwisted disks.

Definition

(Contact Jacobi curve)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of a Reeb orbit. Consider the following one-parameter family of one-forms on
A^{1}\Gamma
:

(9)

\omega_{r}:=\left.\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})^{*}\omega\right)\right|_{A^{1}\Gamma},\qquad r\in\mathbb{[}0,+\infty).

The
contact Jacobi curve
at
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
is the projectivization of
\omega_{r}|_{\lambda}
:

(10)

\Omega_{\lambda}:[0,+\infty)\to P(T_{\lambda}^{*}A^{1}\Gamma)\simeq\mathbb{RP}^{1},\qquad\Omega_{\lambda}(r):=P\left(\omega_{r}|_{\lambda}\right).

The
first singular radius
of the contact Jacobi curve is

(11)

r_{o}(\lambda):=\inf\{r>0\mid\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0)\}.

A first key result is a structural theorem for the contact Jacobi curve, namely
Theorem

5.12
, where we also prove that the definition above is well-posed for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Theorem

5.12
is technical, and for this reason it is omitted from this introduction.

The first singular time of contact Jacobi curves detects the presence of overtwisted disks. This is our first main result, corresponding to
Theorem

5.16
in the body of the paper.

Theorem A

(Geometric tightness radius estimates)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Then, letting

(12)

r_{o}^{-}(\Gamma):=\inf\{r_{o}(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma\},\qquad r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma):=\sup\{r_{o}(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma\},

the following estimates hold:

(13)

\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),r_{o}^{-}(\Gamma)\right\}\leq r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\leq\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma)\right\}.

Moreover, if
r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma)<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, then for any
q\in\Gamma
, the set

(14)

D_{q}:=\{E(r\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A_{q}^{1}\Gamma,\,\,r\leq r_{o}(\lambda)\}

is an overtwisted disk, and thus
(M,\omega)
is an overtwisted contact manifold.

1.3.
Tightness radius estimates

We apply
A
by estimating the first singular radius in two conceptually different ways. The first method assumes a control on the Schwarzian derivative of the contact Jacobi curve, while the second method requires bounds on the so-called sub-Riemannian canonical curvature. In the next two sections, we state the results obtained with either method. Then, we compare our tightness radius estimates with the corresponding ones obtained in
[
EKM12
,
EKM16
]
under Riemannian curvature bounds.

1.3.1.
Tightness radius estimates via Schwarzian derivative

This first method, natural in view of the definition of the contact Jacobi curve as curve in
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
, is based on the concept of Schwarzian derivative (see
Section

5.1
). This yields a sharp comparison theorem for the tightness radius, corresponding to
Theorem

5.17
.

Theorem B

(Tightness radius estimates with Schwarzian derivative bounded above)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Assume that there exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the Schwarzian derivative of the contact Jacobi curves are bounded above by:

(15)

\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)\leq-\frac{3}{4r^{2}}+k_{1}r+k_{2}r^{2},\qquad\forall\,r\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)],\quad\forall\,\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma.

Then for the tightness radius of
\Gamma
it holds

(16)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\geq\min\left\{r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2}),r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\right\},

where

(17)

r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2}):=\begin{cases}\frac{-k_{1}+\sqrt{8\pi k_{2}^{3/2}+k_{1}^{2}}}{2k_{2}},&\text{if }k_{1},k_{2}>0,\\
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{k_{2}^{1/4}},&\text{if }k_{1}\leq 0,\,k_{2}>0,\\
\left(\frac{3j_{2/3}}{2}\right)^{2/3}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{1/3}},&\text{if }k_{1}>0,\,k_{2}\leq 0,\\
+\infty&\text{if }k_{1},k_{2}\leq 0,\end{cases}

and
j_{2/3}\sim 3.37
is the first positive root of the Bessel function of first kind
J_{2/3}
.

The form of the upper bound (
15
) is motivated by the structure of contact Jacobi curves
Theorem

5.12
. All three terms in the r.h.s. of (
15
) are needed in order to get sharp and general results.
We note that the upper bound in (
15
) is always verified for a
given
\lambda
if
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)<+\infty
(e.g. when
M
is compact). We refer to
Remark

5.18
and
Remark

5.20
for details.
Furthermore
B
is sharp for the standard sub-Riemannian contact structure (resp. overtwisted structure) of
Example

2.9
(resp.
Example

2.10
), and all left-invariant K-contact structures. See
Remark

5.19
.

1.3.2.
Tightness radius estimates via canonical curvature

A second way to estimate the singular radius of a contact Jacobi curve, and thus the tightness radius from a Reeb orbit, is via curvature-type invariants. Our result if formulated in terms of the so-called
canonical curvatures
. The canonical curvature plays the role of sectional curvature in sub-Riemannian comparison geometry. Historically, it was introduced in
[
AG97
,
AZ02a
]
, formalized in
[
ZL09
]
, and further studied and developed in
[
LZ11
,
LLZ16
,
AL14
,
AL15
,
ABR18
,
ABR17
,
BR16
,
BR20
,
BR17
]
. See
Section

5.5
for details. In the contact case, there are only two such curvatures, associated to a given sub-Riemannian geodesic
\gamma^{\lambda}:[0,T]\to M
, with initial covector
\lambda\in T^{*}M
, and are denoted by
R_{a}^{\lambda},R_{c}^{\lambda}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
. The next result corresponds to
Theorem

5.24
.

Theorem C

(Tightness radius estimates with curvature bounded above)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Assume that there exist
A,C>0
such that

(18)

\sqrt{1+R_{a}^{\lambda}(r)^{2}}\leq A,\quad\sqrt{1+R_{c}^{\lambda}(r)^{2}}\leq C,\qquad\forall\,r\in[0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)],\quad\forall\,\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma,

where
R_{a}^{\lambda}(r),R_{c}^{\lambda}(r)
are the canonical curvatures at
\lambda
. Then for the tightness radius of
\Gamma
it holds

(19)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\geq\min\left\{\tau(A,C),r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\right\},

where

(20)

\tau(A,C):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{Au^{2}+Cu+1}\,\mathrm{d}u.

C
links tightness with sub-Riemannian curvature, for general three-dimensional contact structures. As it will be clear from the proof,
C
is non-sharp, even for the standard contact structure.

1.4.
Cartan–Hadamard theorem for K-contact structures

A contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
is called K-contact if the Reeb flow acts on
M
by isometries.

Denote with
\kappa
the Gaussian curvature of the surface obtained by locally quotienting
M
under the action of the Reeb flow, see
Remark

2.8
. It is a well-defined function on
M
, constant along the Reeb orbits. We prove a contact analogue of the celebrated Cartan–Hadamard theorem, corresponding to
Theorem

6.5
.

Theorem D

(Contact Cartan–Hadamard)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete and simply connected K-contact sub-Riemannian manifold. If
\kappa\leq 0
then
(M,\ker\omega)
is contactomorphic to the standard contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see
Example

2.2
).

It follows from
[
Nie06
,
NP09
]
that every K-contact structure is tight (see
[
EKM16
, Rmk. 1.3]
). Under the non-positive curvature and simply connected assumptions,
D
tells us that these structures are contactomorphic to the standard one. The proof of
D
has the following consequence, corresponding to
Corollary

6.8
.

Corollary E
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete K-contact sub-Riemannian manifold. If
\kappa\leq 0
then any periodic orbit of the Reeb field is the generator of an infinite cyclic subgroup of the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(M)
.

In
Section

6.1
we apply
D
to the contact structures coming from the Boothby-Wang construction. Such a construction yields K-contact structures on principal circle bundles over a Riemannian surface
(B,\eta)
with prescribed curvature
\kappa_{\eta}
(see
Theorem

6.9
). We show that any such contact manifold, provided that
\kappa_{\eta}\leq 0
, has tight universal cover.

In
Section

6.2
we show that, analogously to the classical Cartan–Hadamard theorem, the assumption of non-positive
\kappa
in
D
and
E
can be weakened. In particular, we show an example of K-contact sub-Riemannian manifold with
\kappa>0
and for which the proofs of
D
and
E
and their thesis holds unchanged.

1.5.
Comparison with the state of the art

We compare
B
and
C
with corresponding results from
[
EKM16
]
. Let
\xi
be a contact distribution on a 3-manifold
M
. In
[
EKM16
, Def. 2.7]
the authors define a notion of Riemannian metric compatible with
\xi
(see
Section

7
). For such Riemannian structures, given a (whole) Reeb orbit
\Gamma\subset M
the authors estimate the radius of the maximal tight embedded
Riemannian
tube around
\Gamma
. More precisely, let
E_{R}:A\Gamma\to M
denote the
Riemannian
tubular neighbourhood map and let
r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma)
denote the corresponding (Riemannian) injectivity radius. Let also

(21)

B_{r}^{R}(\Gamma):=\{q\in M\mid d_{R}(q,\Gamma)<r\},

where
d_{R}(\cdot,\Gamma)
denotes the Riemannian distance from
\Gamma
. The
Riemannian tightness radius of
\Gamma
is the Riemannian counterpart of (
8
), namely:

(22)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}^{R}(\Gamma):=\sup\left\{0<r<r^{R}_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\left(B_{r}^{R}(\Gamma),\xi|_{B_{r}^{R}(\Gamma)}\right)\,\,\text{is tight}\right\}.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
EKM16
, Thm. 1.8]
)
.

Let
(M,\xi)
be a contact 3-manifold and
g
a complete Riemannian metric that is compatible with
\xi
. Let
\Gamma
be a Reeb orbit with positive injectivity radius, then the following estimate holds

(23)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}^{R}(\Gamma)\geq\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma),\frac{\mathrm{inj}(g)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{K}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{2a+b^{2}}+b}\right\},

where
r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma)
is the Riemannian injectivity radius from
\Gamma
,
\mathrm{inj}(g)
is the Riemannian injectivity radius of
(M,g)
, and

(24)

a=\frac{4}{3}|\mathrm{sec}(g)|,\qquad b=\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\min_{q\in M}\mathrm{Ric}_{q}(f_{0})},

where
\mathrm{Ric}
is the Ricci tensor,
f_{0}
is the Reeb field,
\theta^{\prime}
is the rotation speed of the compatible metric (see
Section

7
),
|\mathrm{sec}(g)|
is the maximum value of the sectional curvature, and
K
is any positive upper bound for it.

Remark 1.2
.

The statement of
[
EKM16
, Thm. 1.8]
assumes the Reeb orbit to be periodic. However, the statement holds for any Reeb orbit, provided that the corresponding tube is embedded. In other words, one has to include
r^{R}_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
in the minimum in the right hand side of
[
EKM16
, Eq. (1.2)]
, resulting in the lower bound (
23
).

Given a contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
, one can obtain a Riemannian metric compatible with the contact distribution in the sense of
[
EKM16
, Def. 2.7]
by declaring the Reeb field to be an orthonormal complement of
\xi=\ker\omega
. We call the resulting metric the
Riemannian extension
of the sub-Riemannian one, and we denote it with the same symbol
g
. Conversely, if
(M,\xi)
is a contact manifold and
g
is a Riemannian metric compatible with it in the sense of
[
EKM16
, Def. 2.7]
, then there exists a unique (normalized) contact form
\omega
such that
\xi=\ker\omega
and
(M,\omega,g)
is a contact sub-Riemannian manifold. The estimate in
[
EKM16
, Thm. 1.8]
is formulated in terms of the Riemannian distance, rather than the sub-Riemannian one, compare (
22
) with (
8
). So, while a Riemannian tightness radius lower bound always implies an identical sub-Riemannian one (just because
d_{R}\leq d
), the converse is not true. However, the following observation allows to compare the corresponding estimates (see
Lemma

7.2
).

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded Reeb orbit. Assume that the sub-Riemannian distance from
\Gamma
coincides with the one determined by the Riemannian extension, then

(25)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}^{SR}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}^{R}(\Gamma).

We examine model structures of particular importance in which
Lemma

1.3
applies, so that one can compare
Theorem

1.1
with
B
and
C
. To this purpose, we denote the right hand side of the corresponding tightness radii estimates as follows:

(26)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle:=\min\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2})\},

(27)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.C}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle:=\min\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),\tau(A,C)\},

(28)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma),\frac{\mathrm{inj}(g)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{K}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{2a+b^{2}}+b}\right\},

where
r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2})
is defined in (
17
),
\tau(A,C)
is defined in (
20
), and
a,b,K
are defined in (
24
). The next result, corresponding to
7.3
, compares the aforementioned lower bounds.

Proposition 1.4

(Comparison with
[
EKM16
]
, I)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a simply connected K-contact left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded Reeb orbit. Then, the Riemannian and sub-Riemannian distances from
\Gamma
coincide. Up to a constant rescaling of the contact form and the sub-Riemannian metric, we have the following three cases:

(i)

(M,\omega,g)
is the left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure on the Heisenberg group (see
Example

6.12
). Moreover

(29)

\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=+\infty,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.C}}(\Gamma)=\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}\approx 1.02,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\leq 1,

(ii)

(M,\omega,g)
is the left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
(see
Example

6.13
). Moreover

(30)

\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\pi,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.C}}(\Gamma)\approx 1.05,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\leq 1.38,

(iii)

(M,\omega,g)
is the left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure on
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2)
(see
Example

6.14
). Moreover

(31)

\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=+\infty,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.C}}(\Gamma)\approx 1.05,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\leq 0.74.

Finally, in the next result, corresponding to
7.4
, we compare
B
with
[
EKM16
, Thm. 1.8]
, in the in the standard sub-Riemannian overtwisted contact structure.

Proposition 1.5

(Comparison with
[
EKM16
]
, II)
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{ot}},g_{\mathrm{ot}})
be the standard sub-Riemannian overtwisted structure (see
Example

2.10
). Let
\Gamma
be the Reeb orbit

(32)

\Gamma=\{(0,0,z)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Then the Riemannian and sub-Riemannian distances from
\Gamma
coincide. It holds

(33)

\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\sqrt{2\pi},\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\leq\sqrt[3]{2}.

We conclude that for the classes of structures covered by
1.4
and
1.5
the tightness radius estimate from

are sharp, while the ones of

and

1.1
are not. While it is difficult to compare all these results in general (lacking a direct formula linking the Schwarzian derivative of a contact Jacobi curve with Riemannian or sub-Riemannian curvatures), it emerges from this analysis that the contact Jacobi curves are a better tool to detect overtwisted disks with respect to other curvature-based methods.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Three-dimensional contact manifolds

We review some preliminaries on contact manifolds. We refer e.g. to
[
Mas14
,
Gei08
]
for more details. A three dimensional contact manifold
(M,\xi)
is a smooth, connected
3
-manifold
M
endowed with a contact distribution, i.e., a plane field
\xi\subset TM
satisfying the non-integrability condition
\xi+[\xi,\xi]=TM
. The latter equation is called the contact condition. We say that two contact manifolds
(M,\xi)
and
(M^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
are
contactomorphic
if there exists a diffeomorphism
\psi:M\to M^{\prime}
such that
\psi_{*}\xi=\xi^{\prime}
.

In the present paper we assume contact manifolds to be co-orientable, i.e., we assume the existence of a smooth one-form
\omega
such that
\xi=\ker\omega
. Such a differential form is called a
contact form
. Any positive rescaling of
\omega
determines the same contact structure. The contact condition can be expressed in terms of the contact form:

(34)

\xi+[\xi,\xi]=TM\iff\text{$\omega\wedge d\omega$ is a volume form}\iff\text{$d\omega|_{\xi}$ is symplectic}.

The geometric meaning of such condition is that the plane field
\xi
twists monotonically along horizontal foliations, i.e., foliations by curves which are tangent to
\xi
. This prevents
\xi
from having any integral surface. A fundamental notion in the study of contact structures is that of a characteristic foliation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M,\xi)
be a contact manifold, and
\Sigma\subset M
be a embedded surface. The characteristic foliation is the singular line field
\xi\cap T\Sigma
i.e., a rank
1
distribution with singularities. We say that
q\in\Sigma
is
singular
if

(35)

\xi_{q}=T_{q}\Sigma.

Let us introduce some important examples, which are needed in the following.

Example 2.2

(Standard contact structure)
.

The standard contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined, in cylindrical coordinates
x=r\cos\theta
,
y=r\sin\theta
,
z
, by the contact form

(36)

\omega_{\mathrm{st}}:=dz+\frac{r^{2}}{2}d\theta,\qquad\xi_{\mathrm{st}}:=\ker\omega_{\mathrm{st}}.

Let
\Sigma=\{z=0\}
. Its characteristic foliation
\xi_{\mathrm{st}}\cap T\Sigma
consists of radial lines and has a unique singularity at the origin. The distribution twists monotonically along radial lines: the angle between
\xi_{\mathrm{st}}
and
\Sigma
is the monotone function

(37)

\phi_{\mathrm{st}}(r):=\arctan\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right),

which reaches
\pi/2
asymptotically as
r\to\infty
, namely:

(38)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{st}}(r)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Example 2.3

(Standard overtwisted structure)
.

The standard overtwisted structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined, in cylindrical coordinates
x=r\cos\theta
,
y=r\sin\theta
,
z
, by the contact form

(39)

\omega_{\mathrm{ot}}:=\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)dz+\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)d\theta,\qquad\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}:=\ker\omega_{\mathrm{ot}}.

Let
\Sigma:=\{z=0\}
. Its characteristic foliation
\xi_{\mathrm{st}}\cap T\Sigma
consists of radial lines and the singularities are the origin and the circles
C_{k}:=\{(x,y,0)\mid x^{2}+y^{2}=2k\pi\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The distribution twists monotonically along radial lines: the angle between
\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}
and
\Sigma
is the monotone function

(40)

\phi_{\mathrm{ot}}(r):=\frac{r^{2}}{2},

which rotates infinitely many times as
r\to\infty
, namely:

(41)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\phi_{\mathrm{st}}(r)=+\infty.

The disk delimited by the first singular circle,

(42)

D_{\mathrm{ot}}:=\{(x,y,0)\mid x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 2\pi\},

together with its characteristic foliation, is called the standard overtwisted disk.

Definition 2.4
.

A contact manifold
(M,\xi)
is
overtwisted
if it contains an overtwisted disk. Namely, there exists an embedded disk
D\subset M
and a diffeomorphism
\psi:D\to D_{\mathrm{ot}}
, such that

(43)

\psi_{*}\left(\xi\cap TD\right)=\left(\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}\cap TD_{\mathrm{ot}}\right).

A contact manifold is called tight if it is not overtwisted.

Remark 2.5
.

2.4
is equivalent to the existence of neighbourhoods
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
of
D_{\mathrm{ot}}
,
V\subset M
, and a diffeomorphism
\Psi:V\to U
such that
\Psi(D)=D_{\mathrm{ot}}
and
\Psi_{*}\xi=\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}
. See
[
Mas14
, Lemma 13 and Exercise p. 48]
.

Remark 2.6

(Universal tightness)
.

Given a contact manifold
(M,\xi)
with universal cover
p:\tilde{M}\to M
, there is a natural way to endow
\tilde{M}
with a contact structure, which at each
q\in\tilde{M}
is determined by the equation
p_{*}\tilde{\xi}_{q}=\xi_{p(q)}
.
If
(\tilde{M},\tilde{\xi})
is tight then
(M,\xi)
is called
universally tight
. If
(M,\xi)
is overtwisted, then any overtwisted disk can be lifted to the universal cover. Therefore universal tightness implies tightness.

The standard overtwisted structure is overtwisted, while the standard contact structure
\xi_{\mathrm{st}}
, as proven by Bennequin
[
Ben83
]
, is tight. A well-known property of contact structures is that they can be locally normalized: according to the Darboux theorem every contact structure is locally contactomorphic to the standard one
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{\mathrm{st}})
. Therefore every contact structure is locally tight. If a contact manifold is endowed with a metric structure, it makes sense to ask what is the size of the maximal tight neighbourhood of a point. In this work the selected metric structure is a sub-Riemannian one. This choice is natural in view of the fact that length-minimizing curves are horizontal and the distribution twists monotonically along them, so that we can expect to detect overtwisted disks in terms of the sub-Riemannian distance.

2.2.
Contact sub-Riemannian geometry

A (three-dimensional) contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
is a co-orientable contact manifold
(M,\xi)
, with
\xi=\ker\omega
, endowed with a bundle metric
g
on
\xi
, called sub-Riemannian metric. Note that as a consequence of the definition,
M
is oriented by the non-vanishing top-form
\omega\wedge d\omega
.
The
Reeb field

f_{0}
is the vector field transverse to
\xi
defined by the conditions

(44)

\omega(f_{0})=1,\qquad d\omega(f_{0},\cdot)=0.

We extend the sub-Riemannian metric
g
to a Riemannian one by declaring
f_{0}
to be orthonormal to
\xi
. We say that
\omega
is
normalized
(with respect to the given sub-Riemannian metric) if

(45)

\omega\wedge d\omega=\mathrm{Vol}_{g},

where
\mathrm{Vol}_{g}
is the volume form induced by the extended metric on the oriented manifold
M
.

Note that for any contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
one can multiply
\omega
by a suitable positive function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
so that the normalization condition (
45
) is satisfied for
(M,f\omega,g)
. For this reason, in the following, we always assume that (
45
) is satisfied.

The sub-Riemannian structure induces an almost-complex structure, i.e. a smooth bundle morphism
J:\xi\to\xi
such that
J^{2}=-\mathbb{1}
, which is defined by

(46)

d\omega(X,JY)=g(X,Y),\qquad\forall\,X,Y\in\xi_{p},\quad\forall p\in M.

Let
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
be a local oriented orthonormal frame (in particular
f_{1},f_{2}
are horizontal). Let
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
the corresponding dual co-frame. Then
\omega=\nu_{0}
and (
45
)-(
46
) read

(47)

d\nu_{0}=\nu_{1}\wedge\nu_{2},\qquad Jf_{1}=f_{2},\qquad Jf_{2}=-f_{1}.

The structural coefficients of the frame
f_{1},f_{2},f_{0}
are the locally defined functions

(48)

c_{ij}^{k}:=g([f_{i},f_{j}],f_{k}),\qquad i,j,k=0,1,2,

or equivalently

(49)

d\nu_{k}=-\sum_{0\leq i<j=2}c_{ij}^{k}\nu_{i}\wedge\nu_{j},\qquad k=0,1,2.

The next result is taken from
[
ABB20
, Ch. 17]
where a different convention is used for the sign of structure coefficient.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a sub-Riemannian structure. Let
f_{0}
be its Reeb vector field, and let
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
be a local oriented orthonormal frame, then

(50)

\displaystyle[f_{1},f_{2}]

\displaystyle=c_{12}^{1}f_{1}+c_{12}^{2}f_{2}-f_{0},

(51)

\displaystyle[f_{1},f_{0}]

\displaystyle=c_{10}^{1}f_{1}+c_{10}^{2}f_{2},

(52)

\displaystyle[f_{2},f_{0}]

\displaystyle=c_{20}^{1}f_{1}+c_{20}^{2}f_{2}.

Moreover, it holds

(53)

c_{01}^{1}+c_{02}^{2}=0.

Furthermore, the following quantities

(54)

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\left(c_{01}^{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(c_{01}^{2}+c_{02}^{1}\right)^{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=f_{1}\left(c_{12}^{2}\right)-f_{2}\left(c_{12}^{1}\right)-\left(c_{12}^{1}\right)^{2}-\left(c_{12}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{02}^{1}-c_{01}^{2}}{2},

do not depend on the choice of
f_{1},f_{2}
and therefore are globally defined functions
\chi,\kappa:M\to\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The functions
\chi
and
\kappa
are invariant under the action of smooth sub-Riemannian isometries of
(M,\omega,g)
(diffeomorphisms that preserve the sub-Riemannian distribution and its metric). The invariant
\chi
vanishes if and only if the flow of the Reeb flow acts by sub-Riemannian isometries. The invariant
\kappa
has a less obvious geometric interpretation. If
\chi=0
, then
\kappa
is the Gaussian curvature of the surface obtained by locally quotienting
M
under the action of the Reeb flow. More precisely, in this case
\kappa
is constant along the Reeb orbits and, if
N=M/f_{0}
, i.e. the quotient of
M
by the Reeb flow, is a smooth surface, then it inherits a Riemannian structure with Gaussian curvature
\kappa
. More generally let
U\subset M
be an open subset. Then
(U,\omega|_{U},g|_{U})
, is a smooth sub-Riemannian manifold. If the quotient manifold
U/\left(f_{0}|_{U}\right)
is well-defined (which is the case when
U
is sufficiently small), then
U/\left(f_{0}|_{U}\right)
has the natural structure of Riemannian surface with Gaussian curvature
\kappa
. For more details see
[
ABB20
, Ch. 17]
.

Example 2.9

(Standard sub-Riemannian contact structure)
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{st}})
be the standard contact structure of
Example

2.2
. We define a sub-Riemannian metric
g_{\mathrm{st}}
on
\xi_{\mathrm{st}}=\ker\omega_{\mathrm{st}}
such that the following global vector fields are orthonormal:

(56)

f_{1}:=\partial_{x}+\frac{y}{2}\partial_{z},\qquad f_{2}:=\partial_{y}-\frac{x}{2}\partial_{z}.

The normalized contact form and the Reeb vector field are, respectively

(57)

\omega_{\mathrm{st}}=dz+\frac{1}{2}(xdy-ydx),\qquad f_{0}=\partial_{z}.

The contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{st}},g_{\mathrm{st}})
is called the
Heisenberg group
, and its metric invariants vanish:
\chi=\kappa=0
.

We introduce in this paper a sub-Riemannian metric on the standard overtwisted structure, which will turn out to be natural for tightness radius estimates.

Example 2.10

(The standard sub-Riemannian overtwisted structure)
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{ot}})
be the standard overtwisted structure of
Example

2.3
. We define a sub-Riemannian metric
g_{\mathrm{ot}}
on
\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}=\ker\omega_{\mathrm{ot}}
such that the following vector fields (in cylindrical coordinates) are orthonormal:

(58)

f_{1}:=\partial_{r},\qquad f_{2}:=\frac{1}{r}\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{r}\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{z}.

The normalized contact form and the Reeb vector field are, respectively

(59)

\omega_{\mathrm{ot}}=\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)dz+\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)d\theta,\qquad f_{0}=\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{z}+\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{\theta}.

The metric invariants of
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega_{\mathrm{ot}},g_{\mathrm{ot}})
are
\chi=\frac{r}{2}
and
\kappa=\frac{r^{2}}{2}
.

Remark 2.11
.

Note that
f_{1},f_{2}
of
Example

2.10
are defined only out of the
z
axis, however, the corresponding sub-Riemannian metric is globally well-defined and smooth.

The sub-Riemannian metric induces a length structure on horizontal curves; given
T>0
and absolutely continuous
\gamma:[0,T]\to M
, with
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\xi_{\gamma(t)}
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, we denote

(60)

\ell(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{T}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{g}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\|\cdot\|_{g}
is the norm induced on
\xi
by
g
. We define the
sub-Riemannian distance
as

(61)

d(q,p):=\inf\{\ell(\gamma)\mid\gamma\,\,\text{is horizontal},\,\gamma(0)=q,\,\,\gamma(T)=p\}.

The next result justifies the above definition, and it is a consequence of the Rashevskii-Chow theorem, see e.g.
[
ABB20
, Ch. 3]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold, then
(M,d)
is a metric space and the metric topology coincides with the manifold topology.

A
sub-Riemannian geodesic
is a horizontal curve
\gamma:[0,T]\to M
, parametrized with constant speed
\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{g}=C
such that
d(\gamma(t),\gamma(s))=C|t-s|
for all
t,s\in[0,T]
. It is well-known that, for contact structures, sub-Riemannian geodesics are smooth and are projections of solutions of a Hamiltonian system on
T^{*}M
, which we now describe.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}
be a local orthonormal frame for the sub-Riemannian structure,
f_{0}
be the Reeb field and let
h_{0},h_{1},h_{2}:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
be the associated hamiltonian functions

(62)

h_{i}(\lambda):=\langle\lambda,f_{i}\rangle,\qquad\lambda\in T^{*}M,\qquad i=0,1,2.

If
c_{ij}^{k}
are the structural coefficients of the frame
f_{1},f_{2},f_{0}
, i.e.,
[f_{i},f_{j}]=c_{ij}^{k}f_{k}
, then the Poisson brackets
\{\cdot,\cdot\}:C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)\times C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)\to C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
satisfy

(63)

\{h_{i},h_{j}\}=\sum_{k=0}^{2}c_{ij}^{k}h_{k}.

For any smooth function
h:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
recall that the symplectic gradient is defined as the unique smooth vector field
\vec{h}\in\Gamma(T^{*}M)
such that
dh=\sigma(\cdot,\vec{h})
, where
\sigma
is the canonical symplectic form on
T^{*}M
. Equivalently,
\vec{h}(\cdot)=\{h,\cdot\}
. The
sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian

H:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
is

(64)

H:=\frac{1}{2}\left(h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}\right).

The right hand side of (
64
) is independent of the choice of
f_{1},f_{2}
and thus yields a well-defined smooth function on
T^{*}M
. The corresponding Hamiltonian vector field satisfies

(65)

\vec{H}=h_{1}\vec{h}_{1}+h_{2}\vec{h}_{2}.

Every sub-Riemannian geodesic
\gamma:[0,T]\to M
is the projection of an integral curve of
\vec{H}
, namely there exists
\lambda\in T^{*}M
such that

(66)

\gamma(t)=\pi\circ e^{t\vec{H}}(\lambda),\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

and conversely, any sufficiently short segment of such projections is a sub-Riemannian geodesic. The following rescaling property holds, as a consequence of the homogeneity of the Hamiltonian:

(67)

e^{\vec{H}}(\alpha\lambda)=\alpha e^{\alpha\vec{H}}(\lambda),\qquad\forall\alpha>0,\quad\lambda\in T^{*}M.

We recall the following sub-Riemannian version of the Hopf-Rinow theorem, see e.g.
[
ABB20
]
.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold, then
(M,d)
is a complete metric space if and only there exists
q\in M
such that the restriction of the Hamiltonian flow
e^{t\vec{H}}|_{T_{q}^{*}M}:T_{q}^{*}M\to T^{*}M
is well-defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

As a consequence the following definition is well-posed.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold. For
q\in M
, we define the exponential map
\exp_{q}:T_{q}^{*}M\to M
as

(68)

\exp_{q}(\lambda):=\pi\circ e^{\vec{H}}(\lambda),

where
\pi:T^{*}M\to M
is the bundle projection.

3.
Exponential coordinates

We describe the contact structure in a special coordinate neighbourhood of a Reeb orbit. For the following, let
(M,\omega,g)
a contact sub-Riemannian manifold.
Let
\Gamma\subset M
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit, which we always assume to be connected, without boundary, and non-trivial. Maximal integral lines of the Reeb field are simply called
Reeb orbits
. We can have two cases: either
\Gamma
is diffeomorphic to an open interval or to a circle.

3.1.
Tubular neighbourhood of a Reeb orbit

We first study the annihilator bundle of
\Gamma
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
annihilator bundle
of
\Gamma
is

(69)

A\Gamma:=\{\lambda\in T^{*}M\mid\pi(\lambda)\in\Gamma,\,\,\langle\lambda\,,f_{0}\rangle=0\}.

Given
r>0
we also define the following bundles:

(70)

A^{<r}\Gamma:=A\Gamma\cap\{\sqrt{2H}<r\},\qquad A^{1}\Gamma:=A\Gamma\cap\{\sqrt{2H}=1\}.

Remark 3.2
.

The restriction
2H|_{A\Gamma}:A\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is a fiber-wise positive definite quadratic form. In particular
A\Gamma
is an Euclidean bundle.

The restriction of the Hamiltonian flow to
A\Gamma
yields coordinates in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
such that the sub-Riemannian distance
\delta:M\to\mathbb{R}
from
\Gamma
, given by

(71)

\delta(q):=\inf_{p\in\Gamma}d(p,q),\qquad\forall\,q\in M,

has a simple expression. The next theorem is a particular case of a sub-Riemannian version of the tubular neighbourhood theorem which holds for general non-characteristic embedded submanifolds, see e.g.
[
BRR24
, Thm. 3.1]
. See also
[
ACEAG98
, Sec. 2.4]
for an earlier version for three-dimensional contact structures.

Theorem 3.3

(Tubular neighbourhood)
.

There exists a neighbourhood
U\subset A\Gamma
of the zero section of
A\Gamma
such that tubular neighbourhood map
E:U\to M
given by

(72)

E(\lambda):=\pi\circ e^{\vec{H}}(\lambda),

is a diffeomorphism onto its image, and for the distance
\delta
from
\Gamma
it holds

(73)

\delta\circ E=\sqrt{2H}.

If
\Gamma
has compact closure, then we can choose
U=A^{<r}\Gamma
for some
r>0
. Furthermore if
q\in M
and
\sigma:[0,T]\to M
is a geodesic realizing the distance between
q
and
\Gamma
, i.e.
\sigma(0)\in\Gamma
,
\sigma(T)=q
and
\delta(q)=\ell(\sigma)=T
,
then there exists
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
such that

(74)

\sigma(t)=E(t\lambda),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit. The
injectivity radius
of
\Gamma
is

(75)

r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma):=\sup\{r>0\mid\text{$E:A^{<r}\Gamma\to M$ is a diffeomorphism onto its image}\},

with the understanding that
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)=0
if the above set is empty.

Remark 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a (whole) Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
.
Theorem

3.3
implies that

(76)

E\left(A^{<r}\Gamma\right)=B_{r}(\Gamma):=\{q\in M\mid d(q,\Gamma)<r\},\qquad\forall\,r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma).

3.2.
Cylindrical coordinates on the annihilator bundle

The following property is crucial for the construction coordinates on
A\Gamma
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The restriction
\xi|_{\Gamma}
is a trivial vector bundle over
\Gamma
. In other words there exist a horizontal orthonormal frame
f_{1},f_{2}
defined in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is diffeomorphic to an interval, then there is nothing to prove. Assume then that
\Gamma
is diffeomorphic to a circle. This means that we can find
\tau>0
and
q\in\Gamma
such that the curve
\gamma:[0,\tau]\to M
given by

(77)

\gamma(z)=e^{zf_{0}}(q),

satisfies
\gamma(0)=\gamma(\tau)
and
\Gamma=\gamma([0,\tau])
. Let
X_{0},Y_{0}
be a basis for
\xi_{q}
. Then

(78)

X_{z}:=e^{zf_{0}}_{*}X_{0},\qquad Y_{z}:=e^{zf_{0}}_{*}Y_{0},\qquad z\in[0,\tau],

is a basis for
\xi_{\gamma(z)}
, since the Reeb flow preserves
\omega
. Notice that
e^{\tau f_{0}}_{*}:\xi_{q}\to\xi_{q}
is an orientation preserving endomorphism,
the orientation being the one induced on
\xi_{q}
by
d\omega
. In particular
e^{\tau f_{0}}_{*}
is homotopic to the identity: there exists a smooth curve
G:[0,\tau]\to\mathrm{GL}(2)
such that

(79)

G_{0}=\mathrm{Id},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}X_{\tau}\\
Y_{\tau}\end{pmatrix}=G_{\tau}\begin{pmatrix}X_{0}\\
Y_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

We obtain a smooth trivializing frame for
\bar{X},\bar{Y}
for
\xi|_{\Gamma}
by setting

(80)

\begin{pmatrix}\bar{X}_{z}\\
\bar{Y}_{z}\end{pmatrix}=G_{z}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}X_{z}\\
Y_{z}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\forall\,z\in[0,\tau].

We can extend
\bar{X}
and
\bar{Y}
to an horizontal frame in a neighbourhood of
\Gamma
, which in turn yields a orthonormal horizontal frame
f_{1},f_{2}
by Gram-Schmidt.
∎

Let
\Gamma\subset M
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit. We recall that either
\Gamma
is diffeomorphic to an open interval or to a circle. Fix
q\in\Gamma
and let
\gamma:I\to\Gamma
be the diffeomorphism

(81)

\gamma(z)=e^{zf_{0}}(q),\qquad\forall\,z\in I,

where
I
is either an open interval or
I=\mathbb{R}/{\tau\mathbb{N}}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}
for some
\tau>0
(the period of the orbit).

Since
\xi|_{\Gamma}
is a trivial bundle over
\Gamma
, there exists an oriented orthonormal frame
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
on
\Gamma
and a corresponding dual frame
\nu_{0}=\omega,\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
, which we fix for the next proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

The map
\Psi:I\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to A\Gamma
defined by

(82)

\Psi(z,x,y):=(x\nu_{1}+y\nu_{2})|_{\gamma(z)},

is an isomorphism of Euclidean bundles satisfying

(83)

2H\circ\Psi=\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}.

In particular, all the bundles of
3.1
are trivial, with

(84)

4.
The tightness radius from a Reeb orbit

In this section we introduce the tightness radius and prove our first estimates for it.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. The tightness radius is

(88)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma):=\sup\left\{0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\text{$(A^{<r}\Gamma,\ker E^{*}\omega)$ is tight}\right\}.

Remark 4.2
.

It follows from
Remark

3.5
that, if
\Gamma
is a (whole) Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
, then

(89)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\sup\left\{0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\left(B_{r}(\Gamma),\ker\omega|_{B_{r}(\Gamma)}\right)\,\,\text{is tight}\right\},

where
B_{r}(\Gamma)
is the set of points at sub-Riemannian distance from
\Gamma
smaller than
r
.

4.1.
A class of models and their tightness radius

We compute the tightness radius for a class of examples. For the next statement we say that a smooth function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is
radial
if, in cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
it holds
\partial_{\theta}\alpha=\partial_{z}\alpha=0

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\omega
be a contact form on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathbb{R}^{3}

(90)

\omega=\alpha dz+\beta d\theta,

for some smooth radial functions
\alpha,\beta

\gamma:=\alpha\partial_{r}\beta-\beta\partial_{r}\alpha

(91)

g:=\left.\left(dr\otimes dr+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}(d\theta\otimes d\theta+dz\otimes dz)\right)\right|_{\ker\omega}

is a sub-Riemannian metric, and
\omega
is normalized. Thus
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\omega,g)
is a contact sub-Riemannian structure. The curve

(92)

\Gamma=\{(0,0,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}

is the orbit of the Reeb field passing through the origin. In cylindrical coordinates on
A\Gamma
of
Section

3.2

is the identity:

(93)

E(z,r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z),

and in particular the distance
\delta:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
from

is

(94)

\delta(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)=r.

Moreover, the frame
\{f_{0},N,JN\}
is orthonormal and oriented, where

(95)

N=\partial_{r},\qquad JN=\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\partial_{\theta}-\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\partial_{z},\qquad f_{0}=\frac{\partial_{r}\beta}{\gamma}\partial_{z}-\frac{\partial_{r}\alpha}{\gamma}\partial_{\theta}.

Finally, the tightness and injectivity radii are:

(96)

r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)=+\infty,\qquad r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\inf\{r>0\mid\beta(r)=0\}.

Proof.

Since
\alpha,\beta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
are smooth radial functions, the maps

(97)

\mathbb{R}\ni r\mapsto\alpha(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z),\qquad\beta(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z),

\partial_{r}^{2j+1}\alpha(0,z)=\partial_{r}^{2j+1}\beta(0,z)=0
for all

and

(0,z)=(0,0,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

The fact that

\omega=\alpha dz+\frac{\beta}{r^{2}}(xdy-ydx).

Therefore

Combining (

One can check that the vector fields

\omega(f_{0})=1,\qquad d\omega(f_{0},\cdot)=0,\qquad d\omega(N,JN)=1.

Using (

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}f_{0}

In particular

The Hamiltonian functions of the vector fields (

4.2.
Tightness radius and singular locus

The tightness radius can be estimated studying the characteristic foliation of the fibers of the annihilator bundle.

Definition 4.6

(Singular locus of Reeb orbits)
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
\rho:=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Consider, for every

, the embedded surfaces

(123)

\Sigma_{q}:=\{E(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A^{<\rho}\Gamma,\,\,\pi(\lambda)=q,\,\,\lambda\neq 0\}.

The singular locus of
\Gamma
is the union, for
q\in\Gamma
, of the singularities of the characteristic foliations of
\Sigma_{q}
in the contact manifold
(M,\xi=\ker\omega)
, namely

(124)

\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma):=\bigcup_{q\in\Gamma}\Big\{p\in\Sigma_{q}\,\Big|\,T_{p}\Sigma_{q}=\xi_{p}\Big\}.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Then
\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)\subset M
is an embedded surface and

(125)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\geq\min\{d(\Gamma,\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)),r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\},

with the understanding that, if
\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
then
r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
.

The equality holds in (
125
) for the models of
Theorem

4.3
, and in particular of the standard sub-Riemannian overtwisted structure of
Example

2.10
.

Proof.

Let
\rho=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, and recall that

is a diffeomorphism onto its image. On

we fix a set of cylindrical coordinates

as defined in
Section

3.2
.

Since

is horizontal, there exist

(126)

E^{*}\omega=N(\cos\phi dz+\sin\phi d\theta).

Combining

3.3
and

it holds

(127)

\delta(E(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z))=r,\qquad\forall\,r<\rho.

Furthermore, if

Hence, singularities of the characteristic foliation are characterized by

(129)

Computing the contact condition

(132)

5.
Contact Jacobi curves and quantitative tightness

In (sub-)Riemannian geometry and geometric control theory, Jacobi curves are curves in the Lagrange Grassmannian whose dynamics is intertwined with the presence of conjugate points and curvature (see
[
AG97
,
AZ02a
,
AZ02b
,
ABR18
]
and references within). Inspired by that approach, we introduce the concept of
contact
Jacobi curves, whose dynamics is related with the presence of overtwisted disks. As a preparation, in
Section

5.1
we recall the concept of Schwarzian derivative and some comparison results adapted to our setting.

5.1.
The Schwarzian derivative

Definition 5.1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and let
f:I\to\mathbb{\mathbb{RP}}^{1}
be a smooth immersion, which in homogeneous coordinates reads

(139)

f(t)=[f_{0}(t):f_{1}(t)].

The
Schwarzian derivative
of
f
, denoted
\mathcal{S}(f):I\to\mathbb{R}
, is defined as

(140)

\mathcal{S}(f):=\frac{\dddot{v}}{\dot{v}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\ddot{v}}{\dot{v}}\right)^{2},\qquad\text{where}\qquad v=\frac{f_{0}}{f_{1}},\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}.

The Schwarzian derivative is independent of the choice of the homogeneous coordinate
v
. In particular if
A:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is any projective transformation then
\mathcal{S}(A\circ f)=\mathcal{S}(f)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Consider the second order linear ODE on an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}

(
\star
)

\ddot{u}(t)+q(t)u(t)=0,

where
q\in C(I)
. There is a one-to-one correspondence between pairs of linearly independent solutions of (

⋆

‣
5.2
) (up to constant rescaling) and smooth immersions
f:I\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
with Schwarzian derivative
\mathcal{S}(f)=2q
.

Proof.

The space of solutions of (

⋆

‣
5.2
) is a vector space of dimension
2
. Choose two linearly independent solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
. Their Wronskian is

(141)

W(u_{1},u_{2}):=\det\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}\\
\dot{u}_{1}&\dot{u}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=u_{1}\dot{u}_{2}-u_{2}\dot{u}_{1}.

From (

⋆

‣
5.2
) it follows that
W(u_{1},u_{2})
is a non-zero constant. In particular,
u_{1},u_{2}
cannot vanish simultaneously on
I
. Thus, the smooth curve
f:I\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
given by

(142)

f(t)=[u_{1}(t):u_{2}(t)],

is well-defined. One can check that it is an immersion and that
2q=\mathcal{S}(f)
.

We show that this correspondence is injective. Let
\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{2}
be another choice of linearly independent solutions of (

⋆

‣
5.2
) yielding the same curve in
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
. Then there is
a(t)\neq 0
such that
\tilde{u}_{i}(t)=a(t)u_{i}(t)
for all
t\in I
and
i=1,2
. It follows that
a:I\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth (since solutions cannot vanish simultaneously) and by (

⋆

‣
5.2
) that

(143)

\ddot{a}(t)u_{i}(t)+2\dot{a}(t)\dot{u}_{i}(t)=0,\qquad i=1,2.

Multiply the first equation by
u_{2}(t)
, the second one by
u_{1}(t)
, and take the difference. We get

(144)

\dot{a}(t)W(u_{1},u_{2})=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\dot{a}(t)=0,

so that

must be a non-zero constant. Thus
(\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{2})=a(u_{1},u_{2})
.

We show that the correspondence is surjective. Let

be an immersion, with

(145)

for some smooth

\beta:I\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\beta(t):=\left(\frac{1}{|\dot{f}_{1}(t)f_{2}(t)-\dot{f}_{2}(t)f_{1}(t)|}\right)^{1/2},

is well-defined. We note that

satisfy

(147)

\dot{u}_{1}u_{2}-\dot{u}_{2}u_{1}=\pm 1.

Therefore, the

are linearly independent solutions of (

⋆

‣
5.2
), for some

. By the first part of the proof, since

∎

We will apply the characterization of
5.2
to contact Jacobi curves. An important fact is that the contact Jacobi curves

have singular Schwarzian derivative at the initial time. The corresponding ODE (

‣

satisfies

This assumption is used for the following asymptotic properties of solutions at the singularity.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider the ODE (

⋆

‣

), and assume that

⋆

‣
5.2
) has linearly independent solutions

such that

(150)

\displaystyle g_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{3/2}\tilde{g}_{1}(t),

(151)

\displaystyle g_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{-1/2}\tilde{g}_{2}(t),

where

,
\tilde{g}_{j}(0)=1
for

Proof.

If

. The case under investigation is treated (in greater generality) in

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
f:[0,T)\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
be a continuous curve, such that its restriction to
(0,T)
is a smooth immersion. Consider the ODE (

⋆

‣
5.2
) with
2q=\mathcal{S}(f)
. Assume that

(152)

q(t)=-\frac{3}{4t^{2}}+\tilde{q}(t),\qquad\forall\,t\in(0,T),

where
\tilde{q}\in C([0,T))
. Let
t_{*}\in(0,T)
such that the curve
f
has a self-intersection:
f(0)=f(t_{*})
. Then there exists a non-trivial solution
u:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
of (

⋆

‣
5.2
) such that

(153)

u(0):=\lim_{t\to 0}u(t)=u(t_{*})=0.

Proof.

By
5.2
there are two independent solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
of (

⋆

‣
5.2
) such that
f(t)=[u_{1}(t):u_{2}(t)]
for
t\in(0,T)
. Since
f(0)=f(t_{*})
we can assume without loss of generality that the following limits exist and are finite

(154)

\alpha:=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{u_{1}(t)}{u_{2}(t)}=\lim_{t\to t_{*}}\frac{u_{1}(t)}{u_{2}(t)}.

In particular since
u_{1},u_{2}
cannot vanish simultaneously on
(0,T)
we must have
u_{2}(t_{*})\neq 0
. We let then
u:=u_{1}-\alpha u_{2}
. By construction,
u(t_{*})=0
. To show that
u(0)=0
we use
Lemma

5.3
. There exist
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
with
ad-bc\neq 0
such that

(155)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=ag_{1}-bg_{2},

(156)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=cg_{1}-dg_{2},

where
g_{1},g_{2}
are independent solutions. By (
154
) we have

(157)

\alpha=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{at^{3/2}-bt^{-1/2}}{ct^{3/2}-dt^{-1/2}}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{at^{2}-b}{ct^{2}-d}.

By the existence of the limit and since
b,d
cannot vanish at the same time, we see that

and

. It follows that

(158)

u=u_{1}-\alpha u_{2}=ag_{1}-\frac{b}{d}cg_{1}=\frac{ad-bc}{d}g_{1}.

Since

.
∎

We have the following version of the Sturm-Picone comparison theorem with singularities.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
u,\bar{u}:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
be non-trivial solutions of

(159)

\displaystyle\ddot{u}(t)

\displaystyle+q(t)u(t)=0,

(160)

\displaystyle\ddot{\bar{u}}(t)

\displaystyle+\bar{q}(t)\bar{u}(t)=0,

where
q:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
q(t)\leq\bar{q}(t)
for
t\in(0,T)
. Then between any two consecutive zeroes
t_{1}<t_{2}\in[0,T)
of
u
there exists at least one zero of
\bar{u}
, unless
q\equiv\bar{q}
on
(t_{1},t_{2})
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
u>0
on
(t_{1},t_{2})
. We claim that there exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
such that
\dot{u}(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{n})>0
. In fact, if for all small enough
\varepsilon>0
we had
\dot{u}\leq 0
on
(0,\varepsilon)
, then using that
u
is continuous on
[t_{1},T)
with
u(t_{1})=0
we have

(161)

u(t)=\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\dot{u}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq 0,\qquad\forall t\in(t_{1},t_{1}+\varepsilon),

which is a contradiction. Similarly one shows that
\dot{u}(t_{2})<0
.

Assume by contradiction that
\bar{u}>0
on
(t_{1},t_{2})
. Therefore by multiplying (
159
)-(
160
) by
\bar{u},u
, respectively, subtracting, and integrating on
(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{n},t_{2})
, we obtain

(162)

[\dot{u}\bar{u}-u\dot{\bar{u}}]_{t_{1}+\varepsilon_{n}}^{t_{2}}=\int_{t_{1}+\varepsilon_{n}}^{t_{2}}(\bar{q}(t)-q(t))u(t)\bar{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Now we take the limit of the above for
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
. By the properties
\dot{u}(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{n})>0
and
\dot{u}(t_{2})<0
, the limit of the left hand side exists and is non-positive. On the other hand, observe that the limit of the right hand side exists (possibly
+\infty
) since it is a non-decreasing sequence, and it must be
\geq 0
. Furthermore the limit is zero if and only if
\bar{q}\equiv q
on
(t_{1},t_{2})
. If this is not the case then we reach a contradiction, so that
\bar{u}
must have a zero in the interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
f:[0,T)\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
be a continuous curve, such that its restriction to
(0,T)
is a smooth immersion. Assume that its Schwarzian derivative
2q=\mathcal{S}(f)
satisfies

(163)

q(t)+\frac{3}{4t^{2}}\in C([0,T)).

Assume also that there exists
\bar{q}\in C((0,T))
such that

(164)

q\leq\bar{q},\qquad\text{on $(0,T)$}.

Let
t_{*}\in(0,T)
such that
f(0)=f(t_{*})
. Let
\bar{u}
be a non-trivial solution of
\ddot{\bar{u}}+\bar{q}\bar{u}=0
. Then

(165)

t_{*}\geq\sup_{\bar{u}}\inf\{t>0\mid\bar{u}(t)=0\},

where the sup is over all non-trivial solutions of
\ddot{\bar{u}}+\bar{q}\bar{u}=0
.

If all solutions of
\ddot{\bar{u}}+\bar{q}\bar{u}=0
on
(0,T)
have a (possibly infinite) limit at
t=0
, and there are solutions with
\bar{u}(0)=0
then the sup is attained on this set.

Remark 5.7
.

In particular, when
\bar{q}
has the form of
Lemma

5.3
, then all solutions have (possibly infinite) limit at
t=0
, and there is only one solution (up to rescaling) with
\bar{u}(0)=0
. Then such a solution yields the optimal estimate in (
165
). This is the case in which we use
Theorem

5.6
.

Proof.

By
5.4
there is a non-trivial solution
u:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
of
\ddot{u}+qu=0
such that

(166)

u(0):=\lim_{t\to 0}u(t)=u(t_{*})=0.

By
5.5
, the non-trivial solution
\bar{u}:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
of
\ddot{\bar{u}}+\bar{q}\bar{u}=0
must have a zero in
(0,t_{*})
. Optimizing over all possible solutions we obtain (
165
).

To prove the final part of the statement, it is sufficient to show that under the additional assumptions the separation theorem holds: if
\bar{u},\bar{v}:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
are linearly-independent solution, with
\bar{v}(0)=\lim_{t\to 0}v(t)\neq 0
, and
0=t_{1}<t_{2}<T
are consecutive zeroes of
\bar{u}
, then
\bar{v}
has exactly one zero in
(t_{1},t_{2})
. To prove this claim, assume by contradiction that
\bar{v}
has no zeroes on
(t_{1},t_{2})
. It follows that
\bar{v}
is non-vanishing on
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. In fact, if
t_{1},t_{2}>0
this is true since
\bar{u},\bar{v}
are linearly independent, while but
\bar{v}(t_{1})=\bar{v}(0)\neq 0
by assumption. Then the function

(167)

\psi(t):=\frac{\bar{u}(t)}{\bar{v}(t)},\qquad t\in[t_{1},t_{2}],

is continuous on
[t_{1},t_{2}]
,
\dot{\psi}
exists on
(t_{1},t_{2})
and
\psi(t_{1})=\psi(t_{2})=0
. By Rolle’s theorem there is
c\in(t_{1},t_{2})
with
\psi^{\prime}(c)=0
. Hence, the Wronskian of
\bar{u},\bar{v}
is zero at
c
which is a contradiction.
∎

5.2.
Contact Jacobi curves

We introduce the contact Jacobi curve, and study its properties. Without further mention, in this section we assume that the sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
is complete, so that the Hamiltonian flow is well-defined for all times.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit. Consider the following one-parameter family of one-forms on
A^{1}\Gamma
:

(168)

\omega_{r}:=\left.\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})^{*}\omega\right)\right|_{A^{1}\Gamma},\qquad r\in\mathbb{[}0,+\infty).

The
contact Jacobi curve
at
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
is the projectivization of
\omega_{r}|_{\lambda}
:

(169)

\Omega_{\lambda}:[0,+\infty)\to P(T_{\lambda}^{*}A^{1}\Gamma)\simeq\mathbb{RP}^{1},\qquad\Omega_{\lambda}(r):=P\left(\omega_{r}|_{\lambda}\right).

The
first singular radius
of the contact Jacobi curve is

(170)

r_{o}(\lambda):=\inf\{r>0\mid\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0)\}.

Remark 5.9
.

The contact Jacobi curve
\Omega_{\lambda}
is defined for any
\lambda\in T^{*}M
such that
\langle\lambda,f_{0}\rangle=0
and
2H(\lambda)=1
. In fact, the definition does not depend the choice of
\Gamma
: if
\Gamma^{\prime}
is any other piece of Reeb orbit containing
\pi(\lambda)
, then
T_{\lambda}(A^{1}\Gamma)=T_{\lambda}(A^{1}\Gamma^{\prime})
, yielding the same contact Jacobi curve
\Omega_{\lambda}
. The same observation holds for
5.10
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit. A real number
r>0
is called a
focal radius
for
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
if

(171)

d_{r\lambda}E:T_{r\lambda}(A^{1}\Gamma)\to T_{E(r\lambda)}M,\qquad\text{is not an isomorphism}.

Remark 5.11
.

Note that
r_{\mathrm{foc}}(\lambda)>0
since all points on the zero section of
A\Gamma
are regular points for
E
. Furthermore, by definition of
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
(see
3.4
), it holds
r_{\mathrm{foc}}(\lambda)\geq r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
.

The next result is a crucial structure theorem for the contact Jacobi curve. In particular it is well-defined, is an immersion outside of focal radii (which is a discrete set of points), and its Schwarzian satisfies the assumptions for the general comparison theory developed in
Section

5.1
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold, and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit. Let
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
.

(i)

The set of focal radii for
\lambda
is discrete, separated from zero;

(ii)

The contact Jacobi curve
\Omega_{\lambda}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
is well-defined and smooth;

(iii)

The contact Jacobi curve is an immersion outside of zero and the focal radius;

(iv)

For any focal radius
\bar{r}>0
, the following asymptotic formula for the Schwarzian derivative holds: there exists
k=k(\bar{r})\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
such that

(172)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{k(k+2)}{2(r-\bar{r})^{2}}+k\,O\left(\frac{1}{r-\bar{r}}\right)+O(1);

(v)

The following asymptotic formula for the Schwarzian derivative holds at
r=0
:

(173)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{3}{2r^{2}}+O(r).

The term
O(r^{n})
denotes a smooth remainder. Namely there exist
\varepsilon>0
and a smooth function
f:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}
, both depending on
\lambda
, such that
O(r^{n})=r^{n}f(r)
for all
r\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.

Remark 5.13
.

A case-by-case analysis shows that only the values
k=0,1,2
can occur in (
172
), see
[
Bel24
]
. This also shows that the contact Jacobi curve can be an immersion at focal radii, in which case
k=0
. As it will be clear from the proof, it is never an immersion at
t=0
.

Proof.

Let
(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)
be cylindrical coordinates on
A\Gamma
as in
Section

3.2
. Note that, out of the zero section, it holds

(174)

T_{\lambda}(A^{1}\Gamma)\simeq\spn\{\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda},\partial_{z}|_{\lambda}\}.

Writing
E^{*}\omega
in these coordinates, we obtain

(175)

\displaystyle(E^{*}\omega)|_{r\lambda}

\displaystyle=\omega(E_{*}\partial_{r}|_{r\lambda})dr+\omega(E_{*}\partial_{\theta}|_{r\lambda})d\theta+\omega(E_{*}\partial_{z}|_{r\lambda})dz

(176)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\pi_{*}\vec{H}|_{e^{\vec{H}}(r\lambda)}\right)dr+\omega\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})_{*}\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda}\right)d\theta+\omega\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})_{*}\partial_{z}|_{\lambda}\right)dz

(177)

\displaystyle=\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})d\theta+\omega_{r}(\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})dz,

where in the second line we used that fact that
\pi_{*}\vec{H}|_{\lambda}
is horizontal, and we also used the homogeneity property of the Hamiltonian (
67
). Consequently, we obtain

(178)

E^{*}(\omega\wedge d\omega)|_{r\lambda}=\frac{\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda},\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})}{r}rdr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz,

where the dot denotes the derivative w.r.t. parameter
r
.

To proceed, we need the following claims: for any
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
and
\bar{r}\geq 0
the one-parameter family of one-forms
\omega_{r}
, evaluated at
\lambda
, has finite order at
\bar{r}
, namely there exists
m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and a non-zero one-form
\beta
on
A^{1}\Gamma
such that

(179)

\omega_{r}|_{\lambda}=(r-\bar{r})^{m}\beta|_{\lambda}+O\left((r-\bar{r})^{m+1}\right),

where
O(r-\bar{r})
denotes a smooth remainder. Furthermore, the one-parameter family of two-forms has a similar property: there exist
n\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
(depending on
\lambda,\bar{r})
, and a non-zero two-form
\alpha
on
A^{1}\Gamma
such that

(180)

\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}|_{\lambda}=(r-\bar{r})^{n}\alpha|_{\lambda}+O\left((r-\bar{r})^{n+1}\right).

The proof of (
179
)-(
180
) is postponed to
Appendix

A
(see
Lemma

A.2
).

In the following we will often omit the evaluation of forms at the fixed
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
, for brevity.

Proof of
(i)
.
By (
178
), and since
\omega\wedge d\omega
is a volume form on
M
, we note that
\bar{r}>0
is a focal radius if and only if
\omega_{\bar{r}}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{\bar{r}}=0
. In particular it follows from (
180
) that the set of focal radii is discrete. Furthermore, by definition,
r=0
cannot be a focal time since
E
is always a diffeomorphism in a neighbourhood of the zero section of
A\Gamma
.

Proof of
(ii)
and
(iii)
.
If
r>0
is non-focal,
\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}\neq 0
. In particular
\omega_{r}\neq 0
. It follows that the contact Jacobi curve

(181)

\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=P(\omega_{r}|_{\lambda})\simeq[\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}):\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})],

is well-defined and smooth in a neighbourhood of
r
. Furthermore, assuming
\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})\neq 0
we have

(182)

\dot{v}(r)=-\frac{\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z})}{\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})^{2}}\neq 0,

and similarly if
\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})\neq 0
. It follows that the contact Jacobi curve is an immersion at all non-focal radii
r>0
. Note that by (
178
), since
rdr\wedge d\theta\wedge dz\neq 0
and smooth at
r=0
, we must have
\omega_{0}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{0}=0
so that at
r=0
the contact Jacobi curve is not an immersion.

We now prove how the contact Jacobi curve is well-defined and smooth also in a neighbourhood of the focal radii. Let
\bar{r}>0
be a focal radius. By (
179
) it follows that in a neighbourhood of
\bar{r}
the one-parameter family of one-forms on
A^{1}\Gamma

(183)

\bar{\omega}_{r}:=\frac{\omega_{r}}{(r-\bar{r})^{m}},

is smooth as a function of
r
and non-zero (when evaluated at
\lambda
). Thus, for all
r\neq\bar{r}
in a neighbourhood of
\bar{r}
, it holds

(184)

\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=P(\omega_{r}|_{\lambda})=P(\bar{\omega}_{r}|_{\lambda})\simeq[\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta}):\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z})],

and so the contact Jacobi curve extends smoothly through
\bar{r}
.

Proof of
(iv)
.
Let
\bar{r}>0
be a focal radius. Let
\bar{\omega}_{r}
be the one-parameter family of one-form in (
183
), which is smooth and non-zero in a neighbourhood of
\bar{r}
. Assume without loss of generality that
\bar{\omega}_{\bar{r}}(\partial_{z})\neq 0
. Then
\upsilon(r):=\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta})/\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z})
defines a smooth coordinate for the contact Jacobi curve in a neighbourhood of
\bar{r}
, and furthermore for all
r\neq\bar{r}
in a neighbourhood of
\bar{r}
it holds

(185)

\dot{\upsilon}(r)=\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta})}{\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z})}=\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})}{\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})}=-\frac{\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z})}{\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})^{2}}.

By (
180
) and the last term of (
185
) it follows that there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
c\neq 0
such that

(186)

\dot{\upsilon}(r)=c(r-\bar{r})^{k}+O(r-\bar{r})^{k+1}.

On one hand,
k\geq 0
since from the first equality of (
185
)
\dot{\upsilon}
is smooth around
\bar{r}
. On the other hand
k\leq 4
by (
180
). By plugging (
186
) in the formula for the Schwarzian derivative (
140
), we obtain the following asymptotic expansion as
r\to\bar{r}
:

(187)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dddot{\upsilon}}{\dot{\upsilon}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\ddot{\upsilon}}{\dot{\upsilon}}\right)^{2}

(188)

\displaystyle=\frac{k(k-1)}{(r-\bar{r})^{2}}-\frac{3k^{2}}{2(r-\bar{r})^{2}}+k\,O\left(\frac{1}{r-\bar{r}}\right)+O(1)

(189)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k(k+2)}{2(r-\bar{r})^{2}}+k\,O\left(\frac{1}{r-\bar{r}}\right)+O(1),

concluding the proof of (
172
).

Proof of
(v)
.
The proof of (
173
) is similar, but we can exploit more precise computations at
r=0
. Assume without loss of generality that
\omega_{0}(\partial_{z})\neq 0
. Consider the smooth coordinate
v(r)=\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})/\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})
, in a neighbourhood of
r=0
. We have:

(190)

\dot{v}(r)=-\frac{\omega_{r}\wedge\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z})}{\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})^{2}}=-\frac{\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z})-\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta})}{\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})^{2}}.

Routine computations, which are presented in
Appendix

A
(see
Lemma

A.2
), show that

(191)

\displaystyle\omega_{0}(\partial_{\theta})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\ddot{\omega}_{0}(\partial_{\theta})

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\omega_{0}(\partial_{z})

\displaystyle=1,

(192)

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}_{0}(\partial_{\theta})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\dddot{\omega}_{0}(\partial_{\theta})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}_{0}(\partial_{z})

\displaystyle=0.

We deduce then from (
190
) that
\dot{v}(r)=r+r^{3}f(r)
, for some
f\in C^{\infty}([0,\varepsilon))
. From the definition of Schwarzian derivative (
140
) and elementary computation we obtain

(193)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{3}{2r^{2}}+3\dot{f}(0)r+O(r^{2}),

which corresponds to (
173
).
∎

Example 5.14

(Contact Jacobi curve for the standard contact structure)

Let

, which is an embedded Reeb orbit of the standard sub-Riemannian contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see

). For all

the contact Jacobi curve is

(194)

\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\left[\frac{r^{2}}{2}:1\right],\qquad\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R}.

A homogeneous coordinate is
v=r^{2}/2
and the Schwarzian derivative is

(195)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{3}{2r^{2}}.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions. In the following corollary we exploit the following useful formulas for the Schwarzian derivative

(196)

\mathcal{S}(f\circ g)=\left(\mathcal{S}(f)\circ g\right)(g^{\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{S}(g).

Example 5.15

(Contact Jacobi curve for the standard overtwisted structure)
.

Let
\Gamma=\{(0,0,z)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}
, which is an embedded Reeb orbit of the standard sub-Riemannian contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see
Corollary

4.5
). For all
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
the contact Jacobi curve is

(197)

\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\left[\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right):\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\right],\qquad\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R}.

A homogeneous coordinate is
v=\tan(r^{2}/2)
. Since
\mathcal{S}(\tan(r))=2
, the Schwarzian derivative is

(198)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{3}{2r^{2}}+2r^{2}.

5.3.
A sharp tightness radius estimate via contact Jacobi curves

The first singular radius of a contact Jacobi curve detects the presence of overtwisted disks.

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Then, letting

(199)

r_{o}^{-}(\Gamma):=\inf\{r_{o}(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma\},\qquad r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma):=\sup\{r_{o}(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma\},

the following estimates hold:

(200)

\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),r_{o}^{-}(\Gamma)\right\}\leq r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\leq\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma),r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma)\right\}.

Moreover, if
r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma)<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, then for any
q\in\Gamma
, the set

(201)

D_{q}:=\{E(r\lambda)\mid\lambda\in A_{q}^{1}\Gamma,\,\,r\leq r_{o}(\lambda)\}

is an overtwisted disk, and thus
(M,\omega)
is an overtwisted contact manifold.

Proof.

We first prove the lower bound in (
200
). We characterize the singular locus of the Reeb orbit and employ
Theorem

4.7
. Let
\rho=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, and recall that
E:A^{<\rho}\Gamma\to M
is a diffeomorphism on its image by
Theorem

3.3
. Let
(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)
be cylindrical coordinates on
A\Gamma
as explained in
Section

3.2
. By the same computation in the proof of
Theorem

5.12
we have

(202)

E^{*}\omega|_{r\lambda}=\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})d\theta+\omega_{r}(\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})dz,

for all
(r,\lambda)\in(0,+\infty)\times A^{1}\Gamma
. By definition of singular locus (see
4.6
) we see that

(203)

\mathrm{Sing}(\mathrm{\Gamma})=E\Big(\left\{r\lambda\in A\Gamma\mid(r,\lambda)\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma))\times A^{1}\Gamma,\,\,\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})=0\right\}\Big).

Note that
\omega_{0}(\partial_{\theta})=\omega(\pi_{*}\partial_{\theta})=0
by construction. For all
(r,\lambda)\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma))\times A^{1}\Gamma
we have then the following characterization in terms of the contact Jacobi curve
\Omega_{\lambda}(\cdot)
:

(204)

E(r\lambda)\in\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0).

Using characterization (
204
) we obtain that, for all
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
, it holds

(205)

\displaystyle r_{0}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\inf\{r>0\mid\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0)\}

(206)

\displaystyle\leq\inf\{0<r<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\mid\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0)\}

(207)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\delta(E(r\lambda))\mid E(r\lambda)\in\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)\},

where
\delta
is the sub-Riemannian distance from
\Gamma
.

Assume now that
\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)\neq\emptyset
. This means that there exists
(r,\lambda)\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma))\times A^{1}\Gamma
such that
\Omega_{\lambda}(r)=\Omega_{\lambda}(0)
and thus inequality (
206
) is actually an equality. Therefore, taking the infimum over all
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
we obtain

(208)

r_{0}^{-}(\Gamma)=d(\Gamma,\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma))\leq r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma),

where the last inequality is
Theorem

4.7
. If
\mathrm{Sing}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
then again by
Theorem

4.7
we have that
r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
. Combining both cases we obtain the left inequality of (
200
).

We now prove the upper bound in (
200
). By definition
r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\leq r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
, so we proceed under the assumption
r_{o}^{+}(\Gamma)<r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)
. It is sufficient to show that the sets (
201
) are overtwisted disks.

As explained in the proof of
Theorem

4.7
there exist smooth functions
\phi,N\in C^{\infty}(A^{<\rho}\Gamma)
, with
N>0
, such that

(209)

E^{*}\omega=N(\sin\phi d\theta+\cos\phi dz).

Comparing (
209
) with (
202
) yields, for all
(r,\lambda)\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma))\times A^{1}\Gamma

(210)

N(r\lambda)\sin\phi(r\lambda)=\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda}),\qquad N(r\lambda)\cos\phi(r\lambda)=\omega_{r}(\partial_{z}|_{\lambda}).

Since
\omega_{0}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})=0
, it follows that
r_{o}(\lambda)
is solution to the equation

(211)

\phi(r_{o}(\lambda)\lambda)=\pi.

Since any positive value of
\phi
is a regular one (see the proof of
Theorem

4.7
),
r_{o}:A^{1}\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth. It follows that, for
q\in\Gamma
, the set
D_{q}\subset M
of (
201
) is a smooth embedded disk. Recall the map
\varphi:A^{<\rho}\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

(212)

\varphi(x,y,z)=\left(\sqrt{\frac{\phi}{H}}x,\sqrt{\frac{\phi}{H}}y,z\right).

As we already observed in the proof of
Theorem

4.7
,
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism on the image, and

(213)

\varphi^{*}\omega_{\mathrm{ot}}=\sin\phi d\theta+\cos\phi dz=\frac{1}{N}E^{*}\omega.

It follows that on the tubular neighbourhood
E(A^{<\rho}\Gamma)\subset M
it holds
(\varphi\circ E^{-1})_{*}\xi=\xi_{\mathrm{ot}}
. Furthermore it is easy to check (recalling that
r\mapsto\phi(r\lambda)
is monotone increasing for
r\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma))
and
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
) that, if
q=E(0,0,z)
, then

(214)

\varphi\circ E^{-1}(D_{q})=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 2\pi\},

which is (up to a translation along the
z
axis) the standard overtwisted disk of
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\xi_{\mathrm{ot}})
. It follows that
D_{q}
is a overtwisted disk. ∎

5.4.
Comparison theorems under Schwarzian derivative bounds

We can apply
Theorem

5.6
to contact Jacobi curves, to estimate their singular radius. Combined with
Theorem

5.16
, we obtain a comparison theorem for the tightness radius.

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a complete contact sub-Riemannian manifold and let
\Gamma
be an embedded piece of Reeb orbit with
r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)>0
. Assume that there exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the Schwarzian derivative of the contact Jacobi curves are bounded above by:

(215)

\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)\leq-\frac{3}{4r^{2}}+k_{1}r+k_{2}r^{2},\qquad\forall\,r\in(0,r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)],\quad\forall\,\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma.

Then for the tightness radius of
\Gamma
it holds

(216)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\geq\min\left\{r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2}),r_{\mathrm{inj}}(\Gamma)\right\},

where

(217)

r_{*}(k_{1},k_{2}):=\begin{cases}\frac{-k_{1}+\sqrt{8\pi k_{2}^{3/2}+k_{1}^{2}}}{2k_{2}},&\text{if }k_{1},k_{2}>0,\\
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{k_{2}^{1/4}},&\text{if }k_{1}\leq 0,\,k_{2}>0,\\
\left(\frac{3j_{2/3}}{2}\right)^{2/3}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{1/3}},&\text{if }k_{1}>0,\,k_{2}\leq 0,\\
+\infty&\text{if }k_{1},k_{2}\leq 0,\end{cases}

and
j_{2/3}\sim 3.37
is the first positive root of the Bessel function of first kind
J_{2/3}
.

Remark 5.18
.

Fix
\lambda\in A^{1}\Gamma
. At
r=0
and at focal radii
\bar{r}
, the following asymptotics hold

(218)

\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{3}{4r^{2}}+O(r),\qquad\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=-\frac{k(k+2)}{4(r-\bar{r})^{2}}+k\,O\left(\frac{1}{r-\bar{r}}\right)+O(1),

with smooth remainders, and

(see

(

)

and

(

⁢

)

of

in a neighbourhood of focal radii. Since focal radii are discrete, one can see that for any

such that

(219)

It follows that for any bounded interval

(Sharpness)

Remark 5.20

(On the necessity of

Therefore, setting

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})(r)=\frac{\dddot{\upsilon}_{\varepsilon,\theta}}{\dot{\upsilon}_{\varepsilon,\theta}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\ddot{\upsilon}_{\varepsilon,\theta}}{\dot{\upsilon}_{\varepsilon,\theta}}\right)^{2}=-\frac{3}{2r^{2}}+15\varepsilon\cos{\left(\theta\right)}r+O\left(r^{2}\right).

5.5.
Comparison theorems under canonical curvature bounds

In this section we develop a different type of tightness radius estimate, assuming a control on the so-called
canonical curvature
, rather than on the Schwarzian derivative of the contact Jacobi curve. The canonical curvature plays the role of sectional curvature in sub-Riemannian comparison geometry. Historically, it was introduced in
[
AG97
,
AZ02a
]
for the study of curves in the Lagrange Grassmannians. The concept of canonical curvature (and the related canonical frame) was then formalized in full generality in
[
ZL09
]
, and was further studied and developed in
[
LZ11
,
LLZ16
,
AL14
,
AL15
,
ABR18
,
ABR17
,
BR16
,
BR20
,
BR17
]
in sub-Riemannian geometry and its comparison theory. We follow here the presentation that can be found in
[
ABR18
]
.

Definition 5.22
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold. Let
\lambda\in T^{*}M\setminus\{H=0\}
. The
canonical frame
is a one-parameter family of moving frames for
T_{\lambda}(T^{*}M)
, denoted by
\{E_{i}(t),F_{i}(t)\}_{i=a,b,c}
, which is determined up to sign by the following properties:

•

It is a one-parameter family of Darboux frames:

(239)

\sigma(E_{i}(t),E_{j}(t))=\sigma(F_{i}(t),F_{j}(t))=\sigma(E_{i}(t),F_{j}(t))-\delta_{ij}=0,\qquad\forall\ t\in\mathbb{R};

•

the “
E
” part of the frame satisfies:

(240)

\ker\pi_{*}=e^{t\vec{H}}_{*}\spn\{E_{a}(t),E_{b}(t),E_{c}(t)\},\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R};

•

It satisfies the
structural equations
:

(241)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=-F_{a}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=R_{a}^{\lambda}(t)E_{a}(t)-F_{c}(t),

(242)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=-F_{b}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(243)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=E_{a}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=R_{c}^{\lambda}(t)E_{c}(t),

where the dot denotes the derivative with respect to
t
, and
R_{a}^{\lambda}(t),R_{c}^{\lambda}(t)
are smooth functions called
canonical curvatures
at
\lambda
.

On three-dimensional contact sub-Riemannian manifolds, the canonical frame can be computed explicitly. Let us recall some notation. Let
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
be a local oriented orthonormal frame and
\nu_{0}=\omega,\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
be the corresponding dual frame. Let
h_{1},h_{2},h_{0}:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
be the linear-on-fibers functions defined in (
62
).
Note that, for any
q\in M
such that
f_{1},f_{2}
are defined, the map
(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2}):T_{q}^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
induces coordinates on the fiber
T^{*}_{q}M
and, in turn, vertical
1
1

1

We call a vector (or a vector field) on
T^{*}M

vertical
if
\pi_{*}V=0
.
vector fields
\partial_{h_{1}},\partial_{h_{2}},\partial_{h_{0}}
locally defined on
T^{*}M
. They are characterized by the following property in terms of the symplectic form

(244)

\sigma(\partial_{h_{i}},\cdot)=(\pi^{*}\nu_{i})(\cdot),\qquad i=0,1,2.

Correspondingly, we define the vertical vector fields

(245)

\partial_{\theta}=h_{1}\partial_{h_{2}}-h_{2}\partial_{h_{1}},\qquad\mathfrak{e}=h_{1}\partial_{h_{1}}+h_{2}\partial_{h_{2}}+h_{0}\partial_{h_{0}}.

Both vector fields are globally well-defined on
T^{*}M
independently on the choice of the
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
. Furthermore
\mathfrak{e}
is the
Euler vector field
, the generator of fiber-wise dilations, namely

(246)

e^{(\ln\alpha)\mathfrak{e}}(\lambda)=\alpha\lambda,\qquad\forall\alpha>0,\quad\lambda\in T^{*}M.

Finally, let also introduce the following globally-defined smooth vector field:

(247)

\vec{H}^{\prime}:=[\partial_{\theta},\vec{H}]=h_{2}\vec{h}_{1}-h_{1}\vec{h}_{2}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{12}^{j}\partial_{\theta}h_{j}\right)\partial_{\theta}+\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}h_{i}c_{i0}^{j}\partial_{\theta}h_{j}\right)\partial_{h_{0}}.

In terms of the above ingredients, the canonical frame has the following expression (see
[
ABR18
, Sec. 7.5]
, to which we refer to for a proof and more details):

(248)

\displaystyle E_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2H}}e^{-t\vec{H}}_{*}\partial_{h_{0}},

\displaystyle F_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2H}}e^{-t\vec{H}}_{*}\left(-[\vec{H},\vec{H}^{\prime}]+R_{a}^{\lambda}(t)\partial_{\theta}\right),

(249)

\displaystyle E_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2H}}e^{-t\vec{H}}_{*}\partial_{\theta},

\displaystyle F_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2H}}e^{-t\vec{H}}_{*}\vec{H}^{\prime},

(250)

\displaystyle E_{b}(t)

6.
K-contact sub-Riemannian manifolds

A contact sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\omega,g)
is called K-contact if the associated Reeb field

is a Killing vector field for the Riemannian extension

Remember the two metric invariants

introduced in

. The first one can be given in terms of an orthonormal frame

and its structural coefficients by:

(281)

\chi=\sqrt{\left(c_{01}^{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(c_{01}^{2}+c_{02}^{1}\right)^{2}}.

The next result is well-known, see

Proposition 6.1

(M,\omega,g)

The second metric invariant is given by the formula:

(282)

\kappa=f_{1}\left(c_{12}^{2}\right)-f_{2}\left(c_{12}^{1}\right)-\left(c_{12}^{1}\right)^{2}-\left(c_{12}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{02}^{1}-c_{01}^{2}}{2}.

We recall that, in K-contact case,

is the Gaussian curvature of the surface obtained by locally quotienting

under the action of the Reeb flow, see

2.8

Remark 6.2
.

Let
(M,\omega,g)
be a contact sub-Riemannian manifold (not necessarily K-contact). Let

be the Hamiltonian of the Riemannian extension, i.e., the Riemannian metric obtained from the sub-Riemannian one, promoting the Reeb field to a unit normal to

H_{R}=H+\frac{1}{2}h_{0}^{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\vec{H}_{R}=\vec{H}+h_{0}\vec{h}_{0},

where

is the sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian.
Since
[\vec{H},\vec{h}_{0}]=0
and

and both are tangent to

(285)

E_{R}(\lambda)=\pi\circ e^{\vec{H}_{R}}(\lambda)=\pi\circ e^{\vec{H}}(\lambda)=E(\lambda),\qquad\forall\,\lambda\in A\Gamma.

.

.

6.2.
Examples with positive curvature

In the proof of
Theorem

6.5
, the assumption
\kappa\leq 0
can be replaced by asking that
E:A\Gamma\to M
has no critical points (this fact is implied by
\kappa\leq 0
through
Theorem

6.3
). Arguing as in the proof of the latter, it is sufficient that there exists a Reeb orbit
e^{zf_{0}}(q)
such that the Riemannian manifold obtained by taking the quotient by the action of the Reeb flow (locally along any sub-Riemannian geodesic
E:A\Gamma\to M
) has no conjugate points along (Riemannian) geodesics starting from the corresponding point in the quotient. The curvature of the quotient, in this case, can also be positive.

We give an example. Let
(N,h)
be a complete, orientable, non-compact Riemannian surface. There exists
\alpha\in\Lambda^{1}N
such that
d\alpha=\mathrm{vol}_{h}
is the volume form of
N
. Let then
M=N\times\mathbb{R}
, with projection
p(q,z)=q
. Define

(302)

\omega:=dz+p^{*}\alpha,\qquad g=p^{*}h+\omega\otimes\omega.

One can check that
\omega\wedge d\omega=\mathrm{vol}_{g}
so that
(M,\omega,g)
is a contact sub-Riemannian manifold. The Reeb vector field is
f_{0}=\partial_{z}
, which is clearly a generator of isometries for
(M,\omega,g)
. Thus, the sub-Riemannian invariants are

(303)

\chi=0,\qquad\kappa=p^{*}\kappa_{h},

where
\kappa_{h}
is the Riemann curvature of
(N,\eta)
. Assume now that
(N,\eta)
has a
pole
, namely, there exists a point
q
such that any geodesic emanating from
q
has no conjugate points along it. Let
\Gamma
be the Reeb orbit passing through from
(q,0)\in M
. Then the construction in the proof of
Theorem

6.3
shows that the map
E:A\Gamma\to M
is an immersion and thus also the proof of
Theorem

6.5
proceeds unchanged. As an example, one can choose as
(N,h)
the Riemannian surface given by
N=\{(x,y,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid w=x^{2}+y^{2}\}
with the induced metric from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The origin is a pole in the above sense, and the curvature
\kappa_{h}
is positive.

6.3.
Left-invariant K-contact structures

A K-contact sub-Riemannian structure
(M,\omega,g)
is called

if
M
is a Lie group, and both the contact form
\omega
and the sub-Riemannian metric

are left-invariant.

Example 6.12
.

(Left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure on the Heisenberg group.) The sub-Riemannian structure

(x,y,z)\star(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)\right).

(Left-invariant sub-Riemannian structure on

The Lie algebra of

7.
Comparison with the state of the art

Let
\xi
denote a contact distribution on a 3-manifold
M
. Following
[
EKM16
, Def. 2.7]
, a Riemannian metric
g
is
compatible
with
\xi
if there exist a contact form
\omega
, an almost-complex structure
J:\xi\to\xi
, and a positive constant
\theta^{\prime}
, such that

(330)

\frac{1}{\theta^{\prime}}d\omega(X,JY)=g(X,Y),\quad\forall\,X,Y\in\xi_{p},\quad\forall\,p\in M,

and the Reeb field is the oriented unit normal to
\xi
. The constant
\theta^{\prime}
is called the
rotation speed
.

Given a co-orientable contact manifold
(M,\xi)
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between Riemannian metrics which are compatible with
\xi
and normalized sub-Riemannian contact structures. Indeed, a choice of sub-Riemannian metric on
\xi
induces a normalized contact form
\omega
as described in
Section

2.2
. Promoting the Reeb field to a unit normal to
\xi
we obtain a Riemannian metric, which we call the
Riemannian extension
of the sub-Riemannian metric. Equation (
46
) shows that the Riemannian extension is compatible with
\xi
and has rotation speed
\theta^{\prime}=1
. Conversely, given a Riemannian metric which is compatible with
\xi
, we can restrict it to the contact distribution to obtain a sub-Riemannian metric which, in turn, induces a unique contact form
\omega
that is normalized, i.e. satisfies (
45
).

Remark 7.1

(Notation)
.

By the above discussion, we work below with two different metric structures: the Riemannian one or the corresponding sub-Riemannian one. The symbol
g
denotes either the Riemannian or sub-Riemannian metric, depending on the context, no confusion will arise. Of course the corresponding metric structures are different. Thus, all quantities with superscript or subscript
R
, such as
d_{R}
,
B^{R}_{r}(\Gamma)
,
r_{\mathrm{tight}}^{R}
,
r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma)
, …, are defined w.r.t. the Riemannian structure, while the corresponding quantities without affix or suffix denote the corresponding sub-Riemannian ones, in agreement with the rest of the paper. An exception is the injectivity radius of a Riemannian manifold
\mathrm{inj}(g)
, because it has no sub-Riemannian counterpart.

\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)=\sqrt{2\pi},\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\leq\sqrt[3]{2}.

Proof.

According to
Example

2.10
the Riemannian extension of
g_{\mathrm{ot}}
is determined by the orthonormal frame

(344)

f_{1}=\partial_{r},\qquad f_{2}=\frac{1}{r}\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{r}\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{z},\qquad f_{0}=\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{z}+\sin\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\partial_{\theta}.

Thus, for such Riemannian structure, the integral curves of
f_{1}
are arc-length parametrized geodesics realizing the distance from
\Gamma
. It then follows from
Theorem

4.3
that the Riemannian and sub-Riemannian distances from
\Gamma
coincide.

The fact that
\rho_{\mathrm{Thm.B}}(\Gamma)=\sqrt{2\pi}=r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)
was observed in
Remark

5.19
.

A computation shows that the Ricci curvature of
g_{\mathrm{ot}}
is unbounded below on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and thus
\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)=0
. However, one can apply the same proof on a tube
B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)
of radius
s>0
, obtaining that there are no overtwisted disks on
B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)
provided that
s
is smaller than the right hand side of (
23
), computed on
B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)
. In other words:

(345)

r_{\mathrm{tight}}(\Gamma)\geq\sup_{s>0}\min\{s,\rho^{s}_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\},

where
\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}^{s}(\Gamma)
is the right hand side of (
23
) with all relevant bounds computed on
B_{s}(\Gamma)
, namely

(346)

\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}^{s}(\Gamma):=\min\left\{r_{\mathrm{inj}}^{R}(\Gamma),\frac{\mathrm{inj}(g)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{K_{s}}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{2a_{s}+b_{s}^{2}}+b}\right\},

where
K_{s}
is an upper bound on the absolute value of the sectional curvature on
B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)
, while

(347)

a_{s}:=\frac{4}{3}\left|\sec\left(g|_{B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)}\right)\right|,\qquad b_{s}:=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\min_{q\in B_{s}^{R}(\Gamma)}\mathrm{Ric}(f_{0})}.

Then, we re-define
\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)
for the case of structures with unbounded curvature by setting

(348)

\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma):=\sup_{s>0}\min\{s,\rho^{s}_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)\}.

In contrast with (
28
), the number provided by (
348
) is always positive, yielding a non-trivial bound in (
346
).

A standard computation yields that, at the point
q=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)
, it holds

(349)

\mathrm{Ric}(f_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{4}),\qquad\sec(\mathrm{span}\{f_{2},f_{0}\})=\frac{1}{4}\left(r^{2}-1\right)^{2},

and consequently

(350)

a_{s}\geq\frac{1}{3}\left(s^{2}-1\right)^{2}\qquad b_{s}=\frac{1}{2}(s^{2}+1).

Then, according to (
346
), we have

(351)

\displaystyle\rho^{s}_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2a_{s}+b_{s}^{2}}+b_{s}}\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\left(s^{2}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(s^{2}+1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(s^{2}+1)\right)^{-1}

(352)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\sqrt{\left(s^{2}-1\right)^{2}+(s^{2}+1)^{2}}+(s^{2}+1)\right)^{-1}\leq\frac{2}{s^{2}}.

Finally, we can estimate

(353)

\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}(\Gamma)=\sup_{s>0}\min\{s,\rho_{\mathrm{EKM}}^{s}(\Gamma)\}\leq\sup_{s>0}\min\left\{s,\frac{2}{s^{2}}\right\}=\sqrt[3]{2},

concluding the proof.

∎

, it follows from the Pontryagin maximum principle that

(401)

\langle\lambda_{r},E_{*}\partial_{\theta}\rangle=\langle\lambda_{r},E_{*}\partial_{z}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\lambda_{r},E_{*}\partial_{r}\rangle=1,

and thus
E^{*}\lambda_{r}=dr
.

The vector field
E_{*}\partial_{r}=\pi_{*}\vec{H}
is horizontal and has norm one, hence
E_{*}\partial_{r},JE_{*}\partial_{r}
is an horizontal oriented orthonormal frame. Therefore using this frame in (
65
) we obtain

(402)

E_{*}\partial_{r}=\pi_{*}\vec{H}=\langle\lambda_{r},E_{*}\partial_{r}\rangle E_{*}\partial_{r}+\langle\lambda_{r},JE_{*}\partial_{r}\rangle JE_{*}\partial_{r}.

It follows that
\langle\lambda_{r},JE_{*}\partial_{r}\rangle=0
.

We compute the pull-back of the contact form. Using the definition (
395
) of
\omega_{r}
we obtain

(403)

\displaystyle(E^{*}\omega)|_{r\lambda}

\displaystyle=\omega(E_{*}\partial_{r}|_{r\lambda})dr+\omega(E_{*}\partial_{\theta}|_{r\lambda})d\theta+\omega(E_{*}\partial_{z}|_{r\lambda})dz

(404)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\pi_{*}\vec{H}|_{e^{\vec{H}}(r\lambda)}\right)dr+\omega\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})_{*}\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda}\right)d\theta+\omega\left((\pi\circ e^{r\vec{H}})_{*}\partial_{z}|_{\lambda}\right)dz

(405)

\displaystyle=\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})d\theta+\omega_{r}(\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})dz.

Thus, the equalities
E^{*}\lambda_{r}=dr
,
\langle\lambda_{r},JE_{*}\partial_{r}\rangle=0
and
\omega(JE_{*}\partial_{r})=0
together imply

(406)

\tilde{J}\partial_{r}:=E_{*}^{-1}JE_{*}\partial_{r}\propto\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})\partial_{\theta}-\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})\partial_{z}.

The factor of proportionality is found imposing
E^{*}d\omega(\partial_{r},\tilde{J}\partial_{r})=1
, and since

(407)

E^{*}d\omega|_{r\lambda}=dr\wedge\Big(\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda})d\theta+\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})dz\Big)+d\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta}|_{\lambda},\partial_{z}|_{\lambda})d\theta\wedge dz,

we find
\iota_{\partial_{r}}E^{*}d\omega=\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta})d\theta+\dot{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{z})dz
(we omit evaluations for brevity) and consequently

(408)

\tilde{J}\partial_{r}=\frac{1}{a}\Big(\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})\partial_{\theta}-\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})\partial_{z}\Big).

To prove that
N=\nabla\delta
satisfies
N=E_{*}\partial_{r}
, observe that according to (
73
) we have

(409)

\delta(E(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z))=r.

Thus for any horizontal vector field
Y
, since
\langle dr,\tilde{J}\partial_{r}\rangle=0
, we have

(410)

Y(\delta)=\langle dr,\left(g(Y,E_{*}\partial_{r})\partial_{r}+g(Y,JE_{*}\partial_{r})\tilde{J}\partial_{r}\right)\rangle=g(Y,E_{*}\partial_{r}).

This yields (
396
) and (
397
). Finally from the coordinate presentations (
405
) for
E^{*}\omega
and (
407
) for
E^{*}d\omega
, one can check that the expression of
f_{0}
appearing in equation (
398
) satisfies the characterizing equations
\omega(f_{0})=1,\,d\omega(f_{0},\cdot)=0
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
B.2
.

To lighten notation, let us set

(411)

A=g([N,JN],JN),\quad\dot{A}=N(A),\quad B=g([N,f_{0}],JN).

An elementary computation exploiting the expressions (
396
)-(
397
)-(
398
) shows

(412)

A=\frac{1}{a}{\omega}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\omega}}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z}),\quad B=-\frac{1}{a}\dot{{\omega}}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\omega}}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z}),

where
a=\dot{\omega}_{r}\wedge\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z})
. We want to prove formula (
394
), which is

(413)

\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda})=S:=B-\frac{\dot{A}}{2}-\frac{A^{2}}{4}.

Let
f:(0,\rho)\to(0,+\infty)
be a positive function, define
\bar{\omega}_{r}=f\omega_{r}
as well as the corresponding barred quantities

(414)

\bar{A}=\frac{1}{\bar{a}}{\bar{\omega}}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z}),\quad\bar{B}=-\frac{1}{\bar{a}}\dot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z}),

where
\bar{a}=\dot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}\wedge\bar{\omega}_{r}(\partial_{\theta},\partial_{z})
. We claim that
\bar{S}=S
. Indeed, computing shows the relations

(415)

\bar{A}=A-2\frac{\dot{f}}{f},\quad\bar{B}=B-\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\bar{A}-\frac{\ddot{f}}{f}.

Therefore we can compute

(416)

\displaystyle\bar{S}=\bar{B}-\frac{\dot{\bar{A}}}{2}-\frac{{\bar{A}}^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=\left(B-\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\bar{A}-\frac{\ddot{f}}{f}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(A-2\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\left(A-2\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\right)^{2}

(417)

\displaystyle=S-\left(\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\bar{A}+\frac{\ddot{f}}{f}\right)+\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\dot{f}}{f}-\left(\left(\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\right)^{2}-A\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\right)

(418)

\displaystyle=S-\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\bar{A}-2\left(\frac{\dot{f}}{f}\right)^{2}+A\frac{\dot{f}}{f}=S.

If we choose
f=1/\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})
, then, denoting
v=\omega_{r}(\partial_{z})/\omega_{r}(\partial_{\theta})
, we have

(419)

\bar{\omega}_{r}=vdz+d\theta,\quad\dot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}=\dot{v}dz,\quad\ddot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}=\ddot{v}dz,

consequently

(420)

\dot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}\wedge\bar{\omega}_{r}=-\dot{v}d\theta\wedge dz,\quad{\bar{\omega}}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}=\ddot{v}d\theta\wedge dz\quad\dot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}\wedge\ddot{{\bar{\omega}}}_{r}=0.

We deduce that
\bar{A}=-\ddot{v}/\dot{v}
and
\bar{B}=0
, therefore

(421)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\bar{S}=-\frac{\dot{\bar{A}}}{2}-\frac{{\bar{A}}^{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dddot{v}}{\dot{v}}-\left(\frac{\ddot{v}}{\dot{v}}\right)^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\ddot{v}}{\dot{v}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dddot{v}}{\dot{v}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\ddot{v}}{\dot{v}}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{\lambda}).

Formula (
394
) is thus proved.
∎
