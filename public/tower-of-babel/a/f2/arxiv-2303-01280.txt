Topological regularity of isoperimetric sets in PI spaces having a deformation property
Gioacchino Antonelli, Enrico Pasqualetto, Marco Pozzetta, and Ivan Yuri Violo

Abstract
We prove topological regularity results for isoperimetric sets in PI spaces having a suitable deformation property, which prescribes a control on the increment of the perimeter of sets under perturbations with balls. More precisely, we prove that isoperimetric sets are open, satisfy boundary density estimates and, under a uniform lower bound on the volumes of unit balls, are bounded. Our results apply, in particular, to the class of possibly collapsed RCD ⁡ ( K , N ) \mathrm{RCD}(K,N) spaces. As a consequence, the rigidity in the isoperimetric inequality on possibly collapsed RCD ⁡ ( 0 , N ) \mathrm{RCD}(0,N) spaces with Euclidean volume growth holds without the additional assumption on the boundedness of isoperimetric sets. Our strategy is of interest even in the Euclidean setting, as it simplifies some classical arguments.

1
Introduction

In this paper we consider length PI spaces, i.e. metric measure spaces
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
where
\mathfrak{m}
is a uniformly locally doubling
Borel measure, there holds a weak local
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality (see Definition
2.4
), and the distance between any two
points
x,y
is realized as the infimum of the lengths of curves joining
x
and
y
. The well-established theory of
BV
functions
on metric measure spaces
[
3
,
35
]
allows the treatment of sets of finite perimeter in this generalized setting.
Hence, it makes sense to consider the classical isoperimetric problem, defined by the following minimization:

\inf\big\{P(E)\;\big|\;E\subseteq{\rm X}\text{ Borel, }\mathfrak{m}(E)=v\big\},

for any assigned volume
v\in(0,\mathfrak{m}({\rm X}))
, where
P(E)
denotes the perimeter of
E
.
A set
E
minimizing the previous infimum is called an
isoperimetric set
, or an
isoperimetric region
.

One of the fundamental questions about isoperimetric sets addresses their topological regularity. Namely, one aims at proving that, up to the choice of a representative,
isoperimetric sets are open, bounded and enjoy density estimates at points of
the topological boundary. In the Euclidean space, topological regularity follows from
[
25
]
, subsequently
generalized in
[
41
]
. The proof in the Euclidean setting can be further simplified, see
[
34
, Example 21.3, Theorem 21.11]
. On Riemannian manifolds the result is due to
[
36
]
.
In
[
6
]
the result has been generalized to the setting of
noncollapsed

\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
spaces

({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
, i.e.
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathfrak{m}
coincides with the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{N}
.
We mention also
[
31
]
, which addresses the case of quasi-minimal sets in PI spaces.

The purpose of this paper is to prove the topological regularity of isoperimetric sets in the general setting of length PI spaces
that enjoy a so-called
deformation property
, which we are going to introduce (we refer to Definition
3.3
for
the precise definition). We say that a metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
has the deformation property provided the following
holds: given a set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
of finite perimeter and a point
x\in{\rm X}
, we can find
R,C>0
such that

P(E\cup B_{r}(y))\leq C\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\setminus E)}{r}+P(E)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }y\in B_{R}(x)\text{ and }r\in(0,R).

(1.1)

Classes of spaces having the deformation property are collected in Remark
3.4
. Notably, the class includes
\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
spaces
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
, thanks to
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
. We shall not introduce

\mathsf{RCD}
spaces here, and we refer the reader to the survey
[
4
]
and to the references therein.

We point out that being a PI space does not imply that the deformation property holds, see the examples in Remark
3.5
and in Remark
3.6
. Anyway, we are not aware of any example of a PI space where the deformation
property fails when tested on an isoperimetric set
E
, nor of an example of a PI space where the essential interior an some isoperimetric set is not topologically open.

Deformation properties for sets of locally finite perimeter are well-known in the smooth context
[
34
, Lemma 17.21]
,
and they represent a tool of crucial importance in several classical arguments. We refer, for instance, to

[
2
, VI.2(3)]
, to
[
24
, Lemma 4.5]
and
[
37
, Lemma 3.6]
in the sub-Riemannian setting,
and to
[
21
,
38
]
which study isoperimetric problems in a weighted setting.

In fact, it is mostly powerful to couple the topological regularity of an isoperimetric set, or of a set minimizing some
variational problem, with the deformation property. For instance, knowing that such a set
E
has an open representative
allows to apply (
1.1
) centered at points
y
in the interior, so that
\mathfrak{m}(E\cup B_{r}(y))>\mathfrak{m}(E)
only
for radii
r
sufficiently large, and thus (
1.1
) implies that one can increase the volume of
E
controlling
the perimeter of the deformed set
E\cup B_{r}(y)

linearly
with respect to the increase of mass
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\setminus E)
.
An analogous observation holds applying (
1.1
) to the complement, in case the complement of the considered set
has an open representative. Observe that the previous improved deformation property with linear control follows from (
1.1
) only
after
topological regularity of the set has been established. This is in contrast with the Euclidean setting, where the stronger form of deformation property is always available
[
34
, Lemma 17.21]
. The latter result follows by deforming sets of finite perimeter by flows of vector fields, an argument out of reach in the metric setting. Hence the simplest Euclidean proof for the topological regularity of isoperimetric sets
[
34
, Example 21.3]
has no hope of being performed in our framework, and we must look for an alternative argument.

We can now state our main result, which yields the topological regularity at the more general level of
volume-constrained
minimizers
of the perimeter, i.e. sets which minimize the perimeter with respect to any bounded variation that locally preserves
the measure, see Definition
3.1
.
We will denote by
E^{(1)}
,
E^{(0)}
, and
\partial^{e}E
the essential interior, the essential exterior, and the essential
boundary, respectively, of a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
; see Section
2.2
for their definitions.

Theorem 1.1

(Topological regularity of volume-constrained minimizers)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space having the deformation property. Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained minimizer
of the perimeter. Then
E^{(1)}={\rm int}(E^{(1)})
and
E^{(0)}={\rm int}(E^{(0)})
. In particular, it holds that
E^{(1)}
,

E^{(0)}
are open sets and
\partial E^{(1)}=\partial E^{(0)}=\partial^{e}E
.

The previous theorem implies density estimates on the volume and on the perimeter measure of a volume-constrained minimizer at
points of the essential boundary, see Theorem
3.9
. For an isoperimetric set, we can additionally prove its boundedness. Namely:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space having the deformation property. Suppose that
\inf_{x\in{\rm X}}\mathfrak{m}(B_{1}(x))>0
.
Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be an isoperimetric set. Then
E^{(1)}
is bounded. In particular, every isoperimetric set in
{\rm X}

has a bounded representative.

Since
\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
spaces with
N<\infty
are length PI spaces (see
[
39
,
40
]
and
[
33
]
),
and as recalled above they have the deformation property, putting together Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
space with
N<\infty
. Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be an isoperimetric set.
Then the sets
E^{(1)},E^{(0)}
are open and
\partial^{e}E=\partial E^{(1)}=\partial E^{(0)}
.
Moreover, if in addition
\inf_{x\in{\rm X}}\mathfrak{m}(B_{1}(x))>0
, then
E^{(1)}
is bounded.

In the case of noncollapsed
\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
spaces,
the above result has been previously proved in
[
6
, Theorem 1.4]
.

As an application of Corollary
1.3
, we can refine the rigidity part in the sharp isoperimetric inequality
on
\mathsf{RCD}(0,N)
spaces
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
with Euclidean volume growth. We recall that “Euclidean volume growth” means that the

asymptotic volume ratio

{\rm AVR}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})\coloneqq\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(p))}{\omega_{N}R^{N}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for some }p\in{\rm X},

of the space is strictly positive. Recall that the existence of the above limit is guaranteed by the monotonicity of

(0,+\infty)\ni r\mapsto\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(p))/\omega_{N}r^{N}
, which in turn follows from the Bishop–Gromov inequality
(see e.g.
[
40
]
). Observe that the condition
{\rm AVR}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})>0
implies that
\inf_{x\in{\rm X}}\mathfrak{m}(B_{1}(x))>0
.
The sharp isoperimetric inequality on these spaces, see (
1.2
) below, was obtained at different levels of
generality in
[
1
,
13
,
9
,
7
,
16
,
17
]
.
In
[
7
]
the rigidity for the isoperimetric inequality was proved for noncollapsed
\mathsf{RCD}(0,N)
spaces.
In the recent
[
17
, Theorem 1.5]
, the authors prove the rigidity for the inequality in all
\mathsf{RCD}(0,N)
spaces with Euclidean
volume growth under the additional assumption that the set achieving the equality is bounded.
An application of our Corollary
1.3
allows to drop the previous boundedness requirement,
thus obtaining the following unconditional rigidity statement.

Theorem 1.4

(Sharp and rigid isoperimetric inequality on
{\sf RCD}(0,N)
spaces with Euclidean volume growth)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an
{\sf RCD}(0,N)
space with
1<N<\infty
having Euclidean volume growth.
Then for every set of finite perimeter
E\subseteq{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(E)<+\infty
it holds that

P(E)\geq N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}{\rm AVR}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})^{\frac{1}{N}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}.

(1.2)

Moreover, the equality in (
1.2
) holds for some set of finite perimeter
E\subseteq{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(E)\in(0,+\infty)

if and only if
{\rm X}
is isometric to a Euclidean metric measure cone over an
{\sf RCD}(N-2,N-1)
space and
E
is isometric,
up to negligible sets, to a ball centered at one of the tips of
{\rm X}
.

In the previous theorem, when we say that
{\rm X}
is a Euclidean metric measure cone over an
{\sf RCD}(N-2,N-1)
space
we mean that there is a compact
{\sf RCD}(N-2,N-1)
metric measure space
(Z,{\sf d}_{Z},\mathfrak{m}_{Z})
such that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is isomorphic, as a metric measure space, to the metric measure cone
(C(Z),{\sf d}_{c},t^{N-1}\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{\mathfrak{m}}_{Z})
, where
{\sf d}_{c}
is the cone metric built using
{\sf d}_{Z}
. In case
1<N<2
, it is understood that in the rigidity part of the previous statement, the space
X
is either a weighted Euclidean half-line or a weighted Euclidean line.

We stress that Theorem
1.4
is not a straightforward consequence of the results in
[
7
]
, according to which the same result holds in the class of noncollapsed spaces. Indeed, an
\mathsf{RCD}(0,N)
space with
1<N<\infty
and with Euclidean volume growth might not be noncollapsed. A simple example is given by the weighted Euclidean half-line
([0,+\infty),{\sf d}_{\mathrm{eu}},t^{N-1}\mathrm{d}t)
, with
N>1
.

We now briefly discuss our strategy for the proof of Theorem
1.1
. As mentioned above, the Euclidean proof
[
34
, Example 21.3]
cannot be adapted to our setting.
As in the classical
[
25
,
41
]
, we gain information on a volume-constrained minimizer
by comparison with suitable competitors exploiting the deformation property, but our argument is different, more direct, and much shorter. The strategy of

[
25
,
41
]
consists in proving first that
E
has an interior and an exterior point, i.e.
{\rm int}(E^{(1)})\neq\varnothing
and
{\rm int}(E^{(0)})\neq\varnothing
(see

[
25
, Theorem 1]
), then one deduces that
E
is a
(\Lambda,r_{0})
-perimeter minimizer, and thus finally that
E
is open. Instead, we prove directly that if

x\in E^{(0)}
and
y\in E^{(1)}
are arbitrary points, then
x\in{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
and
y\in{\rm int}(E^{(1)})
. To do so we avoid deriving quantitative estimates on
the decay of
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\setminus E)
as in
[
25
,
41
,
6
]
, and we rather adopt a more qualitative approach.
More precisely, the key point is to show (see the key Lemma
3.7
) that if the function
v(r)\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)
vanishes, as
r\to 0^{+}
,
slower than the function
w(r)\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\setminus E)
, then
x\in{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
(and vice versa for
y\in{\rm int}(E^{(1)})
).
By “slower” we mean, roughly speaking, that
v(r)\geq w(r)
for many
r>0
in a measure-theoretic sense
(see Lemma
3.7
for the precise statement). However, up to exchanging
E
with its complement
{\rm X}\setminus E
,
we can always ensure that
v(r)
vanishes slower than
w(r)
, thus deducing that
x\in{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
. By symmetry, we get
y\in{\rm int}(E^{(1)})
as well.

We point out that the strategy of
[
25
,
41
]
does not seem to
generalize to our setting, unless we require additional assumptions – such as Ahlfors regularity – which we do not want
to make (in order to obtain a result which applies to the whole class of collapsed
{\sf RCD}(K,N)
spaces). This motivated
us to look for an alternative proof of the topological result, which – we believe – is of interest even in the Euclidean setting, since it brings simplifications to the classical arguments in
[
25
]
, still (necessarily) avoiding the use of the smooth structure of the ambient.

We conclude the introduction by explicitly recording the following open problem.

Question 1.5
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space and let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained minimizer
of the perimeter. Is it true that
E^{(1)}
is open?

2
Preliminaries

Given a metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
, we denote by
{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
the space of all locally Lipschitz functions from
{\rm X}
to
\mathbb{R}
,
i.e. of those functions
f\colon{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
x\in{\rm X}
there exists
r_{x}>0
for which
f
is Lipschitz on
B_{r_{x}}(x)
.
The
slope

{\rm lip}(f)\colon{\rm X}\to[0,+\infty)
of a function
f\in{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
is defined as
{\rm lip}(f)(x)\coloneqq 0
if
x\in{\rm X}
is an isolated point and

{\rm lip}(f)(x)\coloneqq\varlimsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{{\sf d}(x,y)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ if }x\in{\rm X}\text{ is an accumulation point.}

The topological interior and the topological boundary of a set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
are denoted by
{\rm int}(E)
and
\partial E
, respectively.

A Borel measure
\mu\geq 0
on
{\rm X}
is
locally finite
if for any
x\in{\rm X}
there exists
r_{x}>0
such that
\mu(B_{r_{x}}(x))<+\infty
,
while we say that
\mu
is
boundedly finite
if
\mu(B)<+\infty
whenever
B\subseteq{\rm X}
is bounded Borel. Trivially, each boundedly finite measure
is locally finite, while the converse holds e.g. if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is
proper
, i.e. bounded closed subsets of
{\rm X}
are compact.
Notice that locally finite Borel measures on a complete separable metric space are
\sigma
-finite.

2.1
Sets of finite perimeter in metric measure spaces

In this paper, by a
metric measure space

({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
we mean a complete separable metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
together with a boundedly finite Borel measure
\mathfrak{m}\geq 0
on
{\rm X}
.
Following
[
35
]
, we define the
total variation

|{\bf D}f|(B)\in[0,+\infty]
of a given function
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
in a Borel set
B\subseteq{\rm X}
as

|{\bf D}f|(B)\coloneqq\inf_{B\subseteq\Omega\text{ open}}\inf\bigg\{\varliminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}{\rm lip}(f_{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\;\bigg|\;(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega),\,f_{n}\to f\text{ in }L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\bigg\}.

If for some open cover
(\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
{\rm X}
we have that
|{\bf D}f|(\Omega_{n})<+\infty
holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
|{\bf D}f|
is a locally finite Borel measure on
{\rm X}
.
We say that a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
is
of locally finite perimeter
if
P(E,\cdot)\coloneqq|{\bf D}{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}|
is a locally finite measure, called the
perimeter measure

of
E
. When
P(E)\coloneqq P(E,{\rm X})<+\infty
, we say that
E
is
of finite perimeter
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
E\subseteq{\rm X}
is a set of locally finite perimeter and
x\in{\rm X}
is a given point, then
P(E,\partial B_{r}(x))=0
for all but countably many radii
r>0
.
This is due to the fact that
\partial B_{r}(x)\cap\partial B_{s}(x)=\varnothing
whenever
0<r<s
and to the
\sigma
-finiteness of
P(E,\cdot)
.

\blacksquare

Given any
f\in{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
, it holds that
|{\bf D}f|
is a locally finite measure and
|{\bf D}f|\leq{\rm lip}(f)\mathfrak{m}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Coarea formula
[
35
, Proposition 4.2]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space. Fix any
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
such that

|{\bf D}f|
is a locally finite measure. Fix a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
. Then

\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto P(\{f<t\},E)\in[0,+\infty]
is a Borel measurable function and it holds that

|{\bf D}f|(E)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(\{f<t\},E)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space. Fix
x\in{\rm X}
and a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
.
Define
f\colon(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as
f(r)\coloneqq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(E\cap B_{r}(x))
for every
r>0
, where we denote

{\sf d}_{x}\coloneqq{\sf d}(\cdot,x)\in{\rm LIP}({\rm X})
. Then the function
f
is locally absolutely continuous and it holds
that
f^{\prime}(r)=P(B_{r}(x),E)
for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r>0
.

Proof.

By virtue of the coarea formula, we obtain that
f(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P\big(\{{\sf d}_{x}<s\},E\cap B_{r}(x)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}s=\int_{0}^{r}P(B_{s}(x),E)\,{\mathrm{d}}s

for every
r>0
, whence it follows that
f(r)-f(\tilde{r})=\int_{\tilde{r}}^{r}P(B_{s}(x),E)\,{\mathrm{d}}s
for every
r>\tilde{r}>0
.
Hence,
f
is locally absolutely continuous and
f^{\prime}(r)=P(B_{r}(x),E)
for every Lebesgue point
r
of

s\mapsto P(B_{s}(x),E)
, thus for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r>0
.
∎

2.2
PI spaces

Even though the general theory of sets of finite perimeter is meaningful in any metric measure space, a much more refined calculus is
available in the class of doubling spaces supporting a weak form of
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality, which we refer to as
PI spaces
.
Below we recall the definition of PI space we adopt in this paper, referring e.g. to
[
12
,
29
]
for a thorough account of this topic.
We will also recall some key features of sets of finite perimeter in PI spaces.

Definition 2.4

(PI space)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space. Then:

•

We say that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is
uniformly locally doubling
if there is a function

C_{D}\colon(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that

\mathfrak{m}\big(B_{2r}(x)\big)\leq C_{D}(R)\,\mathfrak{m}\big(B_{r}(x)\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }0<r<R\text{ and }x\in{\rm X}.

•

We say that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
supports a
weak local
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality
if
there exist a constant
\lambda\geq 1
and a function
C_{P}\colon(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that
for any function
f\in{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
it holds that

\fint_{B_{r}(x)}\bigg|f-\fint_{B_{r}(x)}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\bigg|\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\leq C_{P}(R)\,r\fint_{B_{\lambda r}(x)}{\rm lip}(f)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }0<r<R\text{ and }x\in{\rm X}.

•

({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is a
PI space
if it is uniformly locally doubling
and it supports a weak local
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality.

We point out that if
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is a uniformly locally doubling space, then
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is proper,
so
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is locally compact, and locally finite Borel measures on
({\rm X},{\sf d})
are boundedly finite.

Remark 2.5
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a PI space such that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a
length space
, i.e. the distance between
any two points in
{\rm X}
is the infimum of the lengths of rectifiable curves joining them. Then
the weak local
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality is in fact
strong
, namely it holds with
\lambda=1
;
see for example
[
26
, Corollary 9.5 and Theorem 9.7]
.
Moreover, the completeness and the local compactness of
({\rm X},{\sf d})
ensure that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is also geodesic.

\blacksquare

Given a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
in a PI space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
, we define its
essential interior
and
essential exterior
as

E^{(1)}\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in{\rm X}\;\bigg|\;\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(E\cap B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}=1\bigg\},\hskip 20.00003ptE^{(0)}\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in{\rm X}\;\bigg|\;\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(E\cap B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}=0\bigg\},

respectively. The
essential boundary
of
E
is defined as
\partial^{e}E\coloneqq{\rm X}\setminus(E^{(1)}\cup E^{(0)})
.
Notice that
E^{(1)}
,
E^{(0)}
, and
\partial^{e}E
are Borel sets and that
\partial^{e}E\subseteq\partial E
.
It follows from the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (which holds on every uniformly locally doubling metric measure space,
see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 1.8]
) that
\mathfrak{m}(E^{(1)}\Delta E)=0
and
\mathfrak{m}(E^{(0)}\Delta({\rm X}\setminus E))=0
. Moreover,
if
E
is a set of finite perimeter, then we know from
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
that
P(E,\cdot)
is concentrated
on
\partial^{e}E
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a PI space. Let
E,F\subseteq{\rm X}
be sets of locally finite perimeter with
P(E,\partial^{e}F)=0
. Then

P(E\cap F,\cdot)\leq P(E,\cdot)\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits F^{(1)}+P(F,\cdot)\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits E^{(1)}.

Proof.

We know from
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
that the perimeter measure
P(G,\cdot)
of a set
G\subseteq{\rm X}
of locally finite perimeter
can be written as
P(G,\cdot)=\theta_{G}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}G}
for some Borel function
\theta_{G}\colon{\rm X}\to(0,+\infty)
,
where
\mathcal{H}^{h}
stands for the codimension-one Hausdorff measure (see
[
3
, Section 5]
). Since

P(E\cap F,\cdot)\leq P(E,\cdot)+P(F,\cdot)
and
P(E,\cdot)|_{{\rm X}\setminus\partial^{e}E}=P(F,\cdot)|_{{\rm X}\setminus\partial^{e}F}=0
,
we deduce that
\theta_{E\cap F}\leq\theta_{E}
and
\theta_{E\cap F}\leq\theta_{F}
hold
\mathcal{H}^{h}
-a.e. in

\partial^{e}E\setminus\partial^{e}F
and
\partial^{e}F\setminus\partial^{e}E
, respectively. Moreover, we deduce from

\int_{\partial^{e}F}\theta_{E}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}E}=P(E,\partial^{e}F)=0
that
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\partial^{e}E\cap\partial^{e}F)=0
.
Given that
\partial^{e}(E\cap F)=(\partial^{e}E\cap F^{(1)})\sqcup(\partial^{e}F\cap E^{(1)})
up to an
\mathcal{H}^{h}
-negligible set, which is shown e.g. in the
proof of
[
6
, Lemma 2.5]
, we conclude that

\begin{split}P(E\cap F,\cdot)&=\theta_{E\cap F}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}(E\cap F)}=\theta_{E\cap F}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}E\cap F^{(1)}}+\theta_{E\cap F}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}F\cap E^{(1)}}\\
&\leq\theta_{E}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}E\cap F^{(1)}}+\theta_{F}\mathcal{H}^{h}|_{\partial^{e}F\cap E^{(1)}},\end{split}

which yields the statement.
∎

The following is a direct consequence of the study in
[
3
]
, taking Remark
2.5
into account.

Theorem 2.7

(Relative isoperimetric inequality
[
3
, Remark 4.4]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space. Then there exists a function

C_{I}=C_{I}(C_{D},C_{P})\colon(1,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that the following property holds: given a set

E\subseteq{\rm X}
of finite perimeter, a radius
R>0
, and an exponent
\alpha>\max\{\log_{2}(C_{D}(R)),1\}
, we have that

\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}\leq C_{I}(\alpha,R)\bigg(\frac{r^{\alpha}}{\mathfrak{m}\big(B_{r}(x)\big)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}P\big(E,B_{r}(x)\big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}},

for every
x\in{\rm X}
and
r\in(0,R)
.

In the next proposition we recall the well-known fact that in the class of PI spaces where unit balls have measure uniformly
bounded away from zero, there holds an isoperimetric inequality for sets of small volume. Such a result is essentially due to

[
23
]
, after
[
15
,
30
,
20
]
. For a proof, we refer the reader to the argument
in
[
19
, Lemma V.2.1]
.

Proposition 2.8

(Isoperimetric inequality for small volumes)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space. Then there exist constants
\alpha>1
,
C>0
such that the following holds.
If
v_{0}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in{\rm X}}\mathfrak{m}(B_{1}(x))>0
, then for all Borel sets
E\subseteq{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(E)<v_{0}/2
it holds that

P(E)\geq Cv_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space and
E\subseteq{\rm X}
a set of finite perimeter such that
\mathfrak{m}(E),\mathfrak{m}({\rm X}\setminus E)>0
.
Then the relative isoperimetric inequality ensures that
P(E)\neq 0
. In order to prove it, fix any
x\in{\rm X}
and notice that
we have
\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\setminus E)>0
for some
R>0
sufficiently large, thus
P(E)\geq P(E,B_{R}(x))>0
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space. Then there exists
c=c(\inf C_{D},\inf C_{P})\in(0,1)
such that

c\,\mathfrak{m}\leq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|\leq\mathfrak{m}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }x\in{\rm X},

(2.1)

where we denote
{\sf d}_{x}\coloneqq{\sf d}(\cdot,x)\in{\rm LIP}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\rm X})
. In particular, it holds that

\mathfrak{m}\big(\partial B_{r}(x)\big)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }x\in{\rm X}\text{ and }r>0.

(2.2)

Proof.

Recall that
|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|\leq{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{x})\mathfrak{m}
. Moreover, we deduce from
[
5
, Equation (4.5)]

that there exists a constant
c=c(\inf C_{D},\inf C_{P})\in(0,1)
such that
c\,{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{x})\mathfrak{m}\leq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|
. To obtain
(
2.1
), observe that
{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{x})\equiv 1
: the inequality
{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{x})\leq 1
holds
in any metric space, while the converse inequality readily follows from the fact that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is geodesic.
Finally, (
2.2
) can be proved by combining (
2.1
) with the coarea formula:
we can estimate

\mathfrak{m}\big(\partial B_{r}(x)\big)\leq\frac{1}{c}|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|\big(\partial B_{r}(x)\big)=\frac{1}{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P\big(B_{s}(x),\partial B_{r}(x)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}s=0,

where the last identity follows from the fact that
P(B_{s}(x),\cdot)
is concentrated on
\partial^{e}B_{s}(x)\subseteq\partial B_{s}(x)
.
∎

3
Topological regularity

Let us begin with the definition of a volume-constrained minimizer of the perimeter.

Definition 3.1

(Volume-constrained minimizer)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space. Then a set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
of locally finite perimeter is said to be
a
volume-constrained minimizer
of the perimeter if the following property is verified: given a Borel
set
F\subseteq{\rm X}
and a compact set
K\subseteq{\rm X}
satisfying
\mathfrak{m}((E\Delta F)\setminus K)=0

and
\mathfrak{m}(E\cap K)=\mathfrak{m}(F\cap K)
, it holds
P(E,K)\leq P(F,K)
.

Observe that
E
is a volume-constrained minimizer if and only if
{\rm X}\setminus E
is a volume-constrained minimizer.

Remark 3.2
.

An
isoperimetric set
, i.e. a set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
of finite perimeter with
0<\mathfrak{m}(E)<+\infty

such that
P(E)\leq P(F)
for any Borel set
F\subseteq{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(F)=\mathfrak{m}(E)
, is a volume-constrained
minimizer of the perimeter.

\blacksquare

Next we introduce our definition of a metric measure space having the
deformation property
, which will be our standing
assumption throughout the rest of the paper.

Definition 3.3

(Deformation property)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space with
({\rm X},{\sf d})
proper. Then we say that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})

has the
deformation property
if the following property holds: for every set of locally finite perimeter
E\subseteq{\rm X}

and any point
x\in{\rm X}
, there exist constants
R\in(0,1]
and
C\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle P(E\setminus B_{r}(y),{B_{2R}(x)})\leq C\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\cap E)}{r}+P(E,{B_{2R}(x)})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in B_{R}(x),\,r\in(0,R),

(3.1a)

\displaystyle P(E\cup B_{r}(y),{B_{2R}(x)})\leq C\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\setminus E)}{r}+P(E,{B_{2R}(x)})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in B_{R}(x),\,r\in(0,R),

(3.1b)

For convenience, we define from now on
R_{x}(E)\in(0,1]
to be the maximal
R\in(0,1]
such that the above holds for some

C\geq 0
and we define
C_{x}(E)\geq 0
to be the minimal constant such that (
3.1a
) and (
3.1b
) hold
with
R=R_{x}(E).
Note that, by symmetry, we have that
R_{x}(E)=R_{x}({\rm X}\setminus E)
and
C_{x}(E)=C_{x}({\rm X}\setminus E)
;
this is the reason why in Definition
3.3
we require the validity of both (
3.1a
) and
(
3.1b
) with the same constants
C
and
R
.

We also observe that if
E\subseteq{\rm X}
is a given set of finite perimeter (resp. of locally finite perimeter), then (
3.1a
) is equivalent to asking that

P(E\setminus B_{r}(y),S)\leq C\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y)\cap E)}{r}+P(E,S)
holds for every
(y,r)\in B_{R}(x)\times(0,R)
and every Borel set (resp. bounded Borel set)

S\subseteq{\rm X}
with
B_{2R}(x)\subseteq S
. Similarly for (
3.1b
). We will often make use of this observation without further notice. Also:

\inf_{x\in B}R_{x}(E)>0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every bounded set }B\subseteq{\rm X}.

(3.2)

Indeed, the compactness of the closure of
B
ensures that
B\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{R_{x_{i}}(E)/2}(x_{i})
for some
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in B
,
which gives
R_{x}(E)\geq\delta\coloneqq\min\big\{R_{x_{i}}(E)/2\,:\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}>0
for every
x\in B
. The same argument shows also that
(
3.1a
) and (
3.1b
) hold for every
x\in B
for some
R
and
C
that depend only on
B
and
E
,
e.g. by taking
R\coloneqq\delta
and
C\coloneqq\max\big\{C_{x_{i}}(E)\,:\,i=1,\ldots,n\big\}
.

Remark 3.4

(Spaces having the deformation property)
.

These are some spaces with the deformation property:

\rm i)

Euclidean spaces (see e.g.
[
25
]
and the references therein).

\rm ii)

Riemannian manifolds (this can be proved e.g. by following the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]

and using the fact that the Ricci curvature is locally bounded from below).

\rm iii)

{\sf RCD}(K,N)
spaces with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in[1,\infty)
(proved in
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]

building upon the Gauss–Green formula in
[
14
, Theorem 2.4]
).

We point out that in the above cases a stronger version of the deformation property holds, since, given an arbitrary
R>0
,
the constants
C_{x}(E)
for which the deformation property holds at every point
x\in{\rm X}
and for every
0<r<R
, can be chosen
to be independent of
E,x
, and to be dependent only on
K,N,R
.

It would be interesting to study whether there are other distinguished examples of PI spaces having the deformation property.
One natural class to investigate is the one of sub-Riemannian manifolds, or, more specifically, the one of Carnot groups.
For example, in the first Heisenberg group one has a sub-Laplacian comparison theorem. Being
r
the Carnot–Carathéodory distance
from the origin, we have that
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}r\leq 4/r
holds in the distributional sense, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}
is the
horizontal Laplacian. See
[
11
]
for the study of sub-Laplacian comparison theorems in more general
sub-Riemannian structures, and
[
18
, Corollary 4.19]
for the Laplacian comparison theorem
in arbitrary essentially non-branching
\sf MCP
spaces. Then, coupling this with the Gauss–Green formulae for Carnot groups
in
[
22
]
, one could argue following the lines of
[
6
, Theorem 2.32 and Theorem 1.1]

to obtain that at least
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, and more in general all the groups that are essentially non-branching

{\sf MCP}(K,N)
spaces, with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in(1,\infty)
(cf.
[
10
]
,
[
8
]
), have the deformation property. Since this is out of the scope
of the present note, and since there are also some regularity issues of the distance function to deal with, we do not treat these
examples here, but we leave it to possible future investigations.

We mention that, on the other hand, the topological regularity of isoperimetric sets is already proved in
[
32
]

in the setting of Carnot groups and in
[
24
]
on a certain class of sub-Riemannian manifolds.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.5
.

There exist PI spaces where the deformation property fails. For example, fix a sequence of pairwise well-separated non-empty balls
B_{n}\coloneqq B_{r_{n}}(x_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
x_{n}\to 0

and
\sum_{n}r_{n}<+\infty
. Now consider the density function
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[1,2]
given by
\rho\coloneqq{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}+2{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus E}
, where
E\coloneqq\bigcup_{n}B_{n}
.
Letting
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq\rho\mathcal{L}^{2}
we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\rho\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{L}^{2}
, so that
(\mathbb{R}^{2},|\cdot|,\mathfrak{m})
is an Ahlfors regular geodesic PI space.
We claim that the deformation property is not valid for the set of finite perimeter
E
at the origin
0
. To check it, notice that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds
P(B_{n})=2\pi r_{n}
, while

P(B_{r_{n}+\varepsilon}(x_{n}))=4\pi(r_{n}+\varepsilon)
and
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}+\varepsilon}(x_{n})\setminus B_{n})=2\pi(2r_{n}\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2})
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{n})
for some
\varepsilon_{n}>0
sufficiently small. Therefore,

\frac{P(E\cup B_{r_{n}+\varepsilon}(x_{n}))-P(E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}+\varepsilon}(x_{n})\setminus E)/(r_{n}+\varepsilon)}=\frac{(2\pi r_{n}+4\pi\varepsilon)(r_{n}+\varepsilon)}{2\pi(2r_{n}\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2})}\to+\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }\varepsilon\searrow 0,

which shows that the deformation property fails at the origin.
However, we are not aware of any example of a PI space where the deformation property fails when tested on an isoperimetric set, nor of an example of a PI space where the essential interior of some isoperimetric set is not topologically open.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.6
.

The validity of the deformation property on a metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
entails a growth condition: given
x\in{\rm X}
, there exist
C_{x},r_{x}>0
such that

P(B_{r}(y))\leq C_{x}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(y))}{r}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }y\in B_{r_{x}}(x)\text{ and }r\in(0,r_{x}).

(3.3)

The previous (
3.3
) follows just by taking
E\coloneqq\varnothing
in the deformation property. We have that (
3.3
) is not equivalent to the deformation property (e.g. in the example in
Remark
3.5
the property (
3.3
) is satisfied). However, there are examples of PI spaces where also (
3.3
) fails.
The example we are going to describe has been pointed out to the authors by Panu Lahti.
Consider the measure
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq|x|^{-1/2}{\mathrm{d}}x
in
\mathbb{R}
. Since the function
|x|^{-1/2}

is an
A_{1}
-Muckenhoupt weight, we know that
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|,\mathfrak{m})
is a PI space (see e.g.
[
28
]
). Using that
\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(0))}{r}=2\fint_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{4}{\sqrt{r}}\to+\infty

as
r\searrow 0
, one can easily check that the codimension-one Hausdorff measure of the singleton
\{0\}
diverges, i.e.
\mathcal{H}^{h}(\{0\})=+\infty
. It follows from
[
3
, Theorem 5.3]
that
B_{|y|}(y)
is not a set of locally finite perimeter when
y\in(0,+\infty)
.
Hence, (
3.3
) fails for
x=0
.

\blacksquare

Given a metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
, a point
x\in{\rm X}
, and a Borel set
E\subseteq{\rm X}
, we introduce the notation

v_{E,x}(r)\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\hskip 10.00002ptw_{E,x}(r)\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }r>0.

(3.4)

The core of the proof of our main Theorem
1.1
is contained in the following technical result.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space having the deformation property. Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained
minimizer of the perimeter. Fix any
x\in E^{(0)}
and
y\in E^{(1)}
. Define the functions

v_{E,x},w_{E,y}\colon(0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)

3.4
). Fix a sequence
(r_{n})_{n}\subseteq(0,1)

such that
r_{n}\to 0

A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}\subseteq(0,r_{n})

A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}\coloneqq\big\{r\in(0,r_{n})\;\big|\;v_{E,x}(r)\geq w_{E,y}(r)\big\}.

(3.5)

Suppose the following conditions are verified:

\rm i)

There exists
\delta\in(0,R_{y}(E))
such that
\bar{B}_{\delta}(x)\cap\bar{B}_{\delta}(y)=\varnothing
,

v_{E,x}(\delta)>0
, and
w_{E,y}(\delta)>0
.

\rm ii)

The inequality
\mathcal{L}^{1}(A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y})\geq r_{n}/2

.

Then it holds that
x\in{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction: suppose that
x\notin{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
. Recalling that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta}(y)\setminus E)=w_{E,y}(\delta)>0
and noticing that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)\to 0
as
r\to 0
,
we can extract a (not relabeled) subsequence of
(r_{n})_{n}
for which

r_{n}<\delta,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}}(x)\cap E)<\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta}(y)\setminus E),\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{L}^{1}(A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y})\geq\frac{r_{n}}{2},

(3.6)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. We claim that for any
r\in A_{n}\coloneqq A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}
there exists

s(r)\in[r,\delta)
such that

v_{E,x}(r)=\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)=\mathfrak{m}(B_{s(r)}(y)\setminus E)=w_{E,y}(s(r)).

Indeed, if
w_{E,y}(r)=v_{E,x}(r)
, then we can take
s(r)\coloneqq r
. If
w_{E,y}(r)\neq v_{E,x}(r)
,
then
v_{E,x}(r)>w_{E,y}(r)
by definition of
A_{n}

w_{E,y}

w_{E,y}(s(r))=v_{E,x}(r)

w_{E,y}

. Moreover, the second inequality in (

.

Given any
r\in A_{n}
, we define the Borel set
E_{r}\subseteq{\rm X}
as
E_{r}\coloneqq(E\setminus B_{r}(x))\cup B_{s(r)}(y)
.
The first inequality in (
3.6
) ensures that
\bar{B}_{r}(x)\cap\bar{B}_{s(r)}(y)=\varnothing
, whence it
follows that
\mathfrak{m}\big(E_{r}\cap(\bar{B}_{r}(x)\cup\bar{B}_{s(r)}(y))\big)=\mathfrak{m}\big(E\cap(\bar{B}_{r}(x)\cup\bar{B}_{s(r)}(y))\big)
.

Denote
K\coloneqq\bar{B}_{2\delta}(x)\cup\bar{B}_{2\delta}(y)
for brevity.

The assumption that
E
is a volume-constrained minimizer of the perimeter then implies that
P(E,K)\leq P(E_{r},K)
. For ease
of notation from now on we will denote
C_{y}(E)
simply by

\begin{split}P(E,K)&\leq P(E_{r},K)\\

C_{y}

P(E,B_{r}(x))\leq C_{y}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{r}+P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{n}.

P(E,B_{r}(x))\geq 2\tilde{C}\min\{v_{E,x}(r),w_{E,x}(r)\big\}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }r\in A_{n},

where we define

,
we can find
\bar{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

v_{E,x}(r)<w_{E,x}(r),\hskip 20.00003ptC_{y}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\tilde{C}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }n\geq\bar{n}\text{ and }r\in A_{n}.

(3.9)

By combining (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
), we deduce that
for every
n\geq\bar{n}
and
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r\in A_{n}
it holds that

\begin{split}2\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)&\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\\
&\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})+\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}C_{y}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\\
&\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})+\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}.\end{split}

Rearranging the terms, we infer that

\tilde{C}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }n\geq\bar{n}\text{ and }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{n}.

(3.10)

Now define the function
f\colon(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as
f(r)\coloneqq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E^{(1)})
for every
r>0
.
Corollary
2.3
tells that
f
is locally absolutely continuous and
f^{\prime}(r)=P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})
for

\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r>0
. Moreover, Lemma
2.10
gives
f(r)\leq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)
for every
r>0
.
Consequently, it follows from (
3.10
) that

\tilde{C}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}f(r)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq f^{\prime}(r)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }n\geq\bar{n}\text{ and }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{n}.

(3.11)

Using that
x\notin{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
, which is the contradiction assumption, and Lemma
2.10
we see that

f(r)\geq c\,\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)>0
for every
r>0
, thus we can divide both sides of (
3.11
)
by
\alpha f(r)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, obtaining that

\frac{\tilde{C}}{\alpha}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\leq\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{\alpha f(r)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}=(f^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(r)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }n\geq\bar{n}\text{ and }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{n}.

(3.12)

The third inequality in (
3.6
) implies that
\mathcal{L}^{1}([r_{n}/4,r_{n}]\cap A_{n})\geq r_{n}/4
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
thus integrating (
3.12
) (and taking into account that
(f^{1/\alpha})^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
holds for

\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r>0
) we get that

\begin{split}\frac{\tilde{C}}{4\alpha}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}/4}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}&\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\alpha}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}/4}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r_{n}}\mathcal{L}^{1}([r_{n}/4,r_{n}]\cap A_{n})\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\alpha}\int_{[r_{n}/4,r_{n}]\cap A_{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}r\\
&\leq\int_{[r_{n}/4,r_{n}]\cap A_{n}}(f^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(r)\,{\mathrm{d}}r\leq\int_{0}^{r_{n}}(f^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(r)\,{\mathrm{d}}r\\
&=f(r_{n})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}}(x)\cap E)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\end{split}

for every
n\geq\bar{n}
. Letting
C\coloneqq\frac{1}{C_{D}(\delta)^{2}}\big(\frac{\tilde{C}}{4\alpha}\big)^{\alpha}
, we can conclude
that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}}(x)\cap E)\geq C\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{n}}(x))
for every
n\geq\bar{n}
. This leads to a contradiction with the fact that

x\in E^{(0)}
. Therefore, the proof of the statement is achieved.
∎

Having Lemma
3.7
at our disposal, we can now easily prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
E^{(1)}=({\rm X}\setminus E)^{(0)}
, it is sufficient to check that
E^{(0)}={\rm int}(E^{(0)})
. To prove it,
we argue by contradiction: suppose there exists a point
x\in E^{(0)}\setminus{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
. This implies that
both
\mathfrak{m}(E)>0
(otherwise
E^{(0)}={\rm X}={\rm int}(E^{(0)})
) and
\mathfrak{m}({\rm X}\setminus E)>0
(otherwise
E^{(0)}=\varnothing
),
thus we know from Remark
2.9
that
P(E)\neq 0
. Since
P(E,\cdot)
is concentrated on
\partial^{e}E
,
we can find a point
z\in\partial^{e}E
. Notice that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(z)\cap E)>0
and
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(z)\setminus E)>0
for all
r>0
.
Since
z\neq x
, we can fix some radius
\delta\in(0,R_{x}(E))\cap(0,2R_{z}(E)/3)\cap(0,{\sf d}(x,z)/3)
. Thanks to the fact that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta/2}(z)\cap E)>0
, we can find a point
y\in E^{(1)}\cap B_{\delta/2}(z)
. Notice that

B_{\delta/2}(z)\setminus E\subseteq B_{\delta}(y)\setminus E
, so that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta}(y)\setminus E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta/2}(z)\setminus E)>0
. The fact that
x\notin{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
implies
that also
\mathfrak{m}(B_{\delta}(x)\cap E)>0
. Hence, letting
v_{E,x}
,
w_{E,y}
be defined in (
3.4
), we have
proved that
v_{E,x}(\delta)>0
and
w_{E,y}(\delta)>0
. By our construction and by the definition of
R_{z}(E),R_{y}(E)

it holds
2R_{z}(E)/3\leq R_{y}(E)
, hence we have
\delta\in(0,R_{y}(E))
. Moreover, the inequality
\delta<{\sf d}(x,z)/3

implies that
{\sf d}(x,y)>2{\sf d}(x,z)/3>2\delta
, which means that
\bar{B}_{\delta}(x)\cap\bar{B}_{\delta}(y)=\varnothing
.
All in all, we showed that item i) of Lemma
3.7
holds. Hence, fixed any sequence
(r_{n})_{n}\subseteq(0,1)

with
r_{n}\to 0
, we deduce from the assumption
x\notin{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
that item ii) of Lemma
3.7
fails.
Letting
A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}
be as in (
3.5
), we get that

\mathcal{L}^{1}(A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y})\geq\frac{r_{n}}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ holds only for finitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.13)

Since
A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}\cup A_{{\rm X}\setminus E,r_{n}}^{y,x}=(0,r_{n})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we infer that

\mathcal{L}^{1}(A_{{\rm X}\setminus E,r_{n}}^{y,x})\geq r_{n}/2
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Given that

v_{{\rm X}\setminus E,y}(\delta)=w_{E,y}(\delta)>0
and
w_{{\rm X}\setminus E,x}(\delta)=v_{E,x}(\delta)>0
, we are in a position
to apply Lemma
3.7
again, obtaining that
y\in{\rm int}(({\rm X}\setminus E)^{(0)})={\rm int}(E^{(1)})
.
This gives some
\bar{r}>0
satisfying
w_{E,y}(r)=0
for every
r\in(0,\bar{r})
. On the other hand, we know from

x\notin{\rm int}(E^{(0)})
that
v_{E,x}(r)>0
for all
r\in(0,\bar{r})
. Choosing
\bar{n}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
r_{n}<\bar{r}

for all
n\geq\bar{n}
, we conclude that
A_{E,r_{n}}^{x,y}=(0,r_{n})
for every
n\geq\bar{n}
, in contradiction with
(
3.13
). This proves that
E^{(0)}={\rm int}(E^{(0)})
.
∎

Remark 3.8

(Some generalizations of Theorem
1.1
)
.

To keep the presentation of Theorem
1.1
as clear as possible, we decided not to prove it in its utmost generality.
However, below we discuss some generalizations of our result that can be obtained by slightly adapting our arguments.
The standing assumption is that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is a length PI space.

\rm i)

By inspecting the proof of Lemma
3.7
, one can see that assuming the validity of a weaker notion
of deformation property is sufficient. Namely, one can allow for the constant
C
appearing in (
3.1a
),
(
3.1b
) to depend on
y
and it is sufficient to require the deformation property only for volume-constrained
minimizers
E
of the perimeter.

\rm ii)

A localized version of Theorem
1.1
holds as well: let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained
minimizer of the perimeter in some open set
\Omega\subseteq{\rm X}
(i.e. as in Definition
3.1
but requiring
that
K\subseteq\Omega
and with
P(\cdot)
replaced by
P(\cdot,\Omega)
) satisfying
P(E,\Omega)>0
. Then

E^{(1)}\cap\Omega
,
E^{(0)}\cap\Omega
are open sets and
\partial E^{(1)}\cap\Omega=\partial E^{(0)}\cap\Omega=\partial^{e}E\cap\Omega
.

\rm iii)

Theorem
1.1
can be generalized to volume-constrained minimizers of a suitable class of

quasi-perimeters
. Fix an open set
\Omega\subseteq{\rm X}
and a functional
G\colon\mathscr{B}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

with
G(\varnothing)<+\infty
having the following property: for any
U\Subset\Omega
open, there exist constants
C=C(U)>0

and
\sigma=\sigma(U)\in\big(1-\frac{1}{\max\{1,\log_{2}(\inf C_{D})\}},1\big]
such that

G(E)\leq G(F)+C\mathfrak{m}(E\Delta F)^{\sigma}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ whenever }E,F\in\mathscr{B}(\Omega)\text{ satisfy }E\Delta F\subseteq U.

We then define the quasi-perimeter
\mathscr{P}_{G}
restricted to
\Omega
as

\mathscr{P}_{G}(E,\Omega)\coloneqq P(E,\Omega)+G(E\cap\Omega)
for every
E\in\mathscr{B}(\Omega)
.
Then an adaption of the previous arguments yields the validity of the following statement: if
E\subseteq{\rm X}

is a volume-constrained minimizer of the quasi-perimeter
\mathscr{P}_{G}
in
\Omega
(i.e. as in Definition

3.1
but requiring that
K\subseteq\Omega
, and with
P(\cdot)
replaced by
\mathscr{P}_{G}(\cdot,\Omega)
)
satisfying
P(E,\Omega)>0
, then
E^{(1)}\cap\Omega
and
E^{(0)}\cap\Omega
are open sets, and it holds that

\partial E^{(1)}\cap\Omega=\partial E^{(0)}\cap\Omega=\partial^{e}E\cap\Omega
.

\blacksquare

Once we know that volume-constrained minimizers of the perimeter have an open representative, we can obtain the following
expected boundary density estimates by suitably adapting the arguments in the proof of Lemma
3.7
.

Theorem 3.9

(Boundary density estimates)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space having the deformation property. Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained minimizer
of the perimeter. Let
B\subseteq{\rm X}
be a given bounded set. Then there exist constants
\bar{r}=\bar{r}(E,B,C_{D},C_{I})>0
and

C=C(E,B,C_{D},C_{I})>1
such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\leq 1-\frac{1}{C},\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{rP(E,B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\leq C,

(3.14)

for every
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and
r\in(0,\bar{r})
.

In particular, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(C,C_{D}(\bar{r}/2))\geq 1
such that

P(E,B_{2r}(x))\leq\tilde{C}\,P(E,B_{r}(x))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B\text{ and }r\in(0,\bar{r}/2).

(3.15)

Proof.

If
\partial^{e}E
contains only one point, the first one in (
3.14
) follows by the definition
\partial^{e}E
,
while the second follows from
[
3
, Theorem 5.4]
. Thus we can assume that
\partial^{e}E
contains at least two distinct
points
z
and
\tilde{z}
, otherwise there is nothing to prove. In particular, letting

\rho\coloneqq\min\big\{R_{z}(E),R_{\tilde{z}}(E),\frac{1}{5}{\sf d}(z,\tilde{z})\big\}
,
we can find two points
y\in B_{\rho/2}(z)\cap E^{(1)}
and
\tilde{y}\in B_{\rho/2}(\tilde{z})\cap E^{(1)}
. In fact, Theorem

1.1
ensures that
y,\tilde{y}\in{\rm int}(E^{(1)})
, so that there exists
r_{0}\in(0,\rho)
such that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{0}}(y)\setminus E)=\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{0}}(\tilde{y})\setminus E)=0.

(3.16)

Notice that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(y)\setminus E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho/2}(z)\setminus E)>0
and similarly
\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(\tilde{y})\setminus E)>0
.
The doubling assumption ensures that the closure
K
of
B
is compact, thus an application of Dini’s theorem yields the
existence of
r_{1}>0
such that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)<\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(y)\setminus E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(\tilde{y})\setminus E)\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }x\in K\text{ and }r\in(0,r_{1}).

(3.17)

Thanks to (
3.2
),
we can also find
r_{2}>0
such that
r_{2}<R_{y}(E)
,
r_{2}<R_{\tilde{y}}(E)
, and

r_{2}<R_{x}(\varnothing)
hold for every
x\in K
. Now define
\bar{r}_{0}\coloneqq\min\{r_{0},r_{1},r_{2}\}>0
.
Let
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
be fixed. Our choice of
\rho
ensures that
\bar{B}_{\rho}(x)
is disjoint from
at least one between
\bar{B}_{\rho}(y)
and
\bar{B}_{\rho}(\tilde{y})
. Up to relabeling
y
and
\tilde{y}
, say that

\bar{B}_{\rho}(x)\cap\bar{B}_{\rho}(y)=\varnothing
. Given any
r\in(0,\bar{r}_{0})
, we deduce from (
3.16
),
(
3.17
), and the continuity of
s\mapsto\mathfrak{m}(B_{s}(y)\setminus E)
that there exists
s(r)\in(\bar{r}_{0},\rho)

such that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)=\mathfrak{m}(B_{s(r)}(y)\setminus E)
. Define the Borel set
E_{r}\subseteq{\rm X}
as

E_{r}\coloneqq(E\setminus B_{r}(x))\cup B_{s(r)}(y)
. By the minimality assumption on
E
, arguing as we did in the proof of Lemma
3.7
we obtain

P(E,B_{r}(x))\leq\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\bar{r}_{0}}+P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)}),

(3.18)

for any
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and
\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in(0,\bar{r}_{0})
.
For any
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
, define

A_{x}(E)\coloneqq\big\{r>0\,:\,|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E)\leq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}
.
Fix
\alpha>\max\{\log_{2}(C_{D}(\rho)),1\}
. Applying the relative isoperimetric inequality to the left-hand side
of (
3.18
) and using Lemma
2.10
, we deduce that

\begin{split}2C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}&|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\\
&=2C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\min\big\{|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E),|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\\
&\leq 2C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\\
&\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})+\frac{\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}}{c^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\frac{r}{\bar{r}_{0}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\end{split}

(3.19)

holds for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
r\in(0,\bar{r}_{0})\cap A_{x}(E)
, where we set

C_{0}\coloneqq 1/\big(2\,C_{I}(\alpha,\rho)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}\big)
for brevity. Therefore, if we let

\bar{r}\coloneqq\min\bigg\{\frac{c^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}C_{0}\bar{r}_{0}}{\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}},\bar{r}_{0}\bigg\}\in(0,\bar{r}_{0}],

then we infer from (
3.19
) that

C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in(0,\bar{r})\cap A_{x}(E).

(3.20)

This also proves (by considering
{\rm X}\setminus E
instead of
E
) that, up to shrinking
\bar{r}>0
, it holds that

C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq P(B_{r}(x),E^{(0)})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in(0,\bar{r})\cap A_{x}({\rm X}\setminus E).

(3.21)

Let us now define the function
f_{x}\colon(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as

f_{x}(r)\coloneqq\min\big\{|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E^{(1)}),|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|(B_{r}(x)\cap E^{(0)})\big\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for every }r>0.

Corollary
2.3
ensures that
f_{x}
is locally absolutely continuous and

f^{\prime}_{x}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P(B_{r}(x),E^{(1)})\\
P(B_{r}(x),E^{(0)})\end{array}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{x}(E),\\
\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in A_{x}({\rm X}\setminus E).\end{array}\right.

Observe that
A_{x}(E)\cup A_{x}({\rm X}\setminus E)=(0,+\infty)
. Arguing as in Lemma
3.7
,
we deduce from (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) that

\frac{C_{0}}{\alpha}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\leq(f_{x}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(r)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\mathcal{L}^{1}\text{-a.e.\ }r\in(0,\bar{r}).

(3.22)

Given any
r\in(0,\bar{r})
, we can integrate the inequality in (
3.22
) over the interval
[r/2,r]
, thus obtaining that

\begin{split}\frac{C_{0}}{2\alpha\,(C_{D}(\bar{r}/2))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}&\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\alpha}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r/2}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\frac{r}{2}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\alpha}\int_{r/2}^{r}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{s}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{s}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{r}(f_{x}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s=f_{x}(r)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.\end{split}

(3.23)

It follows that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))\leq C_{1}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)
for every
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and
r\in(0,\bar{r})
,
where we define
C_{1}\coloneqq C_{D}(\bar{r}/2)\big(\frac{2\alpha}{C_{0}}\big)^{\alpha}
.

Let
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and
r\in(0,\bar{r})
be fixed. Since

\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))\leq C_{1}\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}
by (
3.23
),
by using the relative isoperimetric inequality, and recalling that
2C_{0}=1/C_{I}(\alpha,\rho)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}
, we get that

\frac{2C_{0}}{C_{1}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{r}\leq 2C_{0}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r}\min\big\{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E),\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\setminus E)\big\}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq P(E,B_{r}(x)).

On the other hand,
up to shrinking
\bar{r}
(depending only on
B
), we can find a constant
C_{2}>0
(depending only on
B
) such that

P(B_{\tilde{r}}(x))\leq C_{2}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\tilde{r}}(x))}{\tilde{r}}
for every
\tilde{r}\in(0,\bar{r})
; recall the discussion after (
3.2
). Then

\begin{split}P(E,B_{r}(x))&\leq P(E,B_{\tilde{r}}(x))\leq\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\tilde{r}}(x)\cap E)}{\tilde{r}}+P(B_{\tilde{r}}(x),E^{(1)})\\
&\leq\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\tilde{r}}(x))}{\tilde{r}}+P(B_{\tilde{r}}(x))\leq\big(\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}+C_{2}\big)\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\tilde{r}}(x))}{\tilde{r}},\end{split}

for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
\tilde{r}\in(r,\bar{r})
, thanks to (
3.18
) and to the deformation property.
Hence,
\frac{rP(E,B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\leq\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}+C_{2}
for all
x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and

r\in(0,\bar{r})
. Picking

C\coloneqq\max\big\{C_{1},C_{1}^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}/(2C_{0}),\max\{C_{z},C_{\tilde{z}}\}+C_{2}\big\},

we conclude that (
3.14
) holds. Finally, applying (
3.14
) we conclude that for every

x\in\partial^{e}E\cap B
and
r\in(0,\bar{r}/2)
it holds that

\frac{P(E,B_{2r}(x))}{P(E,B_{r}(x))}\leq\frac{C\mathfrak{m}(B_{2r}(x))}{2r}\frac{Cr}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}\leq\frac{C^{2}C_{D}(\bar{r}/2)}{2},

which proves the validity of (
3.15
). Consequently, the statement is achieved.
∎

We conclude with a final comment on further minimality properties satisfied by volume-constrained minimizers. Such properties can be derived by reproducing well-known arguments, see, e.g.
[
6
, Remark 3.23, Theorem 3.24]
, exploiting Theorem
1.1
and the deformation property.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a length PI space having the deformation property. Let
E\subseteq{\rm X}
be a volume-constrained
minimizer of the perimeter. Using Theorem
1.1
and with arguments similar to those in the proof of Theorem

3.9
, it is possible to prove that for any compact set
K\subseteq{\rm X}
there exist
\Lambda,r_{0}>0
such that

E
is a
(\Lambda,r_{0})
-perimeter minimizer on
K
, i.e. whenever
F\Delta E\subseteq B_{r}(x)
for some

x\in K
and
r<r_{0}
it holds
P(E,B_{r}(x))\leq P(F,B_{r}(x))+\Lambda\,\mathfrak{m}(E\Delta F)
.

Moreover, for any given compact set
K\subseteq{\rm X}
there exist constants
L,r_{0}>0
such that
E
is
(L,r_{0})
-quasi minimal
on
K
, i.e. whenever
F\Delta E\subseteq B_{r}(x)
for some
x\in K
and
r<r_{0}
it holds that
P(E,B_{r}(x))\leq L\,P(F,B_{r}(x))
.
The class of quasi-minimal sets has been studied e.g. in
[
31
]
.

It is worth pointing out that, once we know that volume-constrained minimizers of the perimeter are
(L,r_{0})
-quasi minimal sets,
Theorem
3.9
follows directly from
[
31
, Theorem 4.2 and Lemma 5.1]
. Nevertheless, we opted for a
self-contained proof of Theorem
3.9
, which takes advantage of the openness of volume-constrained minimizers.

\blacksquare

4
Boundedness of isoperimetric sets

In this last section, we prove the boundedness of isoperimetric sets in length PI spaces satisfying the deformation property
and with a uniform lower bound on the volume of unit balls (Theorem
1.2
). The argument makes use of the
topological regularity given by our main result Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Suppose by contradiction that
E
has no bounded representatives, i.e.
\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(x))>0
for all
R>0
and
x\in{\rm X}.

In particular
{\rm X}
is unbounded and, since

v_{0}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in{\rm X}}\mathfrak{m}(B_{1}(x))>0,

we have
\mathfrak{m}({\rm X})=\infty
and

\mathfrak{m}({\rm X}\setminus E)>0
. Then
P(E)>0
and, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we can find
y\in E^{(1)}

and
\rho\in(0,R_{y}(E))
such that
\delta\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(y)\setminus E)>0
. By Theorem
1.1
it holds that

y\in{\rm int}(E^{(1)})
, i.e. there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r_{0}}(y)\setminus E)=0.
We consider the function
f:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(R)\coloneqq|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{y}|(E^{(1)}\setminus B_{R}(y))=|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{y}|(E^{(1)})-|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{y}|(E^{(1)}\cap B_{R}(y)),

and observe that
|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{y}|(E^{(1)})\leq{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{y})\mathfrak{m}(E^{(1)})=\mathfrak{m}(E)<+\infty.
By Corollary
2.3
the function
f
is locally absolutely continuous and satisfies
f^{\prime}(r)=-P(B_{R}(x),E^{(1)})
for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
R>0.

Thanks to Lemma
2.10
and since
\mathfrak{m}(E^{(1)}\Delta E)=0
, there also exists a constant
c>0
such that

0<c\,\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y))\leq f(R)\leq\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,R>0.

(4.1)

Observe that, since
\mathfrak{m}(E)<+\infty
,
it holds
\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y))\to 0
as
R\to+\infty
. Hence
f(R)\to 0
as
R\to+\infty

and so we can find
R_{0}>\rho
such that
f(R)<\min\{\delta,v_{0}/2\}
for all
R\geq R_{0}.

By continuity, for every
R\geq R_{0}
there exists
r(R)\in(0,\rho)
such that

\mathfrak{m}(B_{r(R)}(y)\setminus E)=\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y)).

(4.2)

For every
R\geq R_{0}
we define the set
F_{R}\coloneqq(E\cup B_{r(R)}(y))\cap B_{R}(y)
, which satisfies
\mathfrak{m}(F_{R})=\mathfrak{m}(E)

thanks to (
4.2
) and
r(R)<R
. Hence, by minimality,
P(E)\leq P(F_{R})
for every
R\geq R_{0}
.
Moreover, using Proposition
2.6
and the deformation property, for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
R\geq R_{0}
we have

\displaystyle P(E)\leq P(F_{R})

\displaystyle=P((E\cup B_{r(R)}(y))\cap B_{R}(y))

\displaystyle\leq P(E\cup B_{r(R)}(y),B_{R}(y)^{(1)})+P(B_{R}(y),(E\cup B_{r(R)}(y))^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq P(E\cup B_{r(R)}(y))-P(E\cup B_{r(R)}(y),B_{R}(y)^{(0)})+P(B_{R}(y),E^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq P(E)+C_{y}(E)\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r(R)}(y)\setminus E)}{r_{0}}-P(E,B_{R}(y)^{(0)})+P(B_{R}(y),E^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq P(E)+C_{y}(E)\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r(R)}(y)\setminus E)}{r_{0}}-P(E\setminus B_{R}(y))+2P(B_{R}(y),E^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq P(E)+C_{y}(E)\frac{\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y))}{r_{0}}-Cv_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y))^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}+2P(B_{R}(y),E^{(1)}),

with
C>0,\alpha>1
constants independent of
R,

where in the fifth line we used again Proposition
2.6
and in the last line we used the isoperimetric
inequality for small volumes in Proposition
2.8
(recall that
\mathfrak{m}(E\setminus B_{R}(y))<v_{0}/2
). This combined with (
4.1
) shows that

2f^{\prime}(R)\leq C_{y}(E)c^{-1}r_{0}^{-1}f(R)-Cv_{0}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}f(R)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\leq-C_{1}f(R)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for a.e. }R\geq R_{1},

for some constant
R_{1}\geq R_{0}
big enough and where
C_{1}>0
is a constant independent of
R
. Note that in the last inequality we used that
f(R)\to 0
as
R\to+\infty
and
\alpha>1.
Since
f(R)>0
for all
R>0
, this shows that

(f^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})^{\prime}(R)\leq-\frac{C_{1}}{2\alpha},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for a.e. }R\geq R_{1},

which contradicts the fact that
f(R)
is strictly positive for any
R>0
.

∎
