Nikolay I. Dubrovin

Abstract
. The rings of linear continuous operators on the topological spaces of 𝔊 \mathfrak{G} -zero maps were described, where 𝔊 \mathfrak{G} is a filter on a set with an involution. This applies to modules of formal series with well ordered support over left ordered groups.



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
06F15, 46K05, 54A20

1.
Introduction

If
X
is a topological space, then the ring
C(X)
of continuous functions from

X
to
\mathbb{R}
is a classical object in topology and analysis. For instance, one can be
interested in properties of
C(X)
as a ring, and from this point of view the situation
is well understood (see
[
7
]
).

If
X
has an additional structure, for instance, if
X
is a linear topological space,
one can consider the properties of the ring of linear continuous functions from
X

to
\mathbb{R}
endowing it with different ‘natural’ topologies (see
[
1
]
). However
sometimes we have to deal with the case, when the target,
K
, of maps from
X
is a
noncommutative skew field. For instance, this situation occurs trying to embed a group ring

FG
of a (torsion-free) group
G
over a field
F
into a skew field.

For example, let
\mathbb{G}
be the universal covering of the group
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and

U=\bigl\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
0&a^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mid a,b\in\mathbb{R},\,a>0\bigl\}
is a subgroup of

SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
U
is metabelian and torsion-free, the group ring
FU
(over any field
F
)
is an Ore domain, therefore its classical quotient field
K
is a (noncommutative) skew field.
Trying to extend this
embedding to an embedding of
K\mathbb{G}
into a skew field, the first author developed the
following approach (see
[
3
]
). He considered the space
K\{\CD\mathbb{G}\}
of all formal
series on
\mathbb{G}
with well ordered support, and the group ring
K\mathbb{G}
acting on this space by left
multiplication. Then one can invert the elements of
K\mathbb{G}
as linear maps forming a
rational closure
D
of
K\mathbb{G}
in
K\{\CD\mathbb{G}\}
. The behavior of elements of
D
is
quite complicated, and the aforementioned paper contains a series of algebraic conditions on
elements of
D
. It is quite difficult and tedious to verify that these conditions
respect basic operations. Later (see
[
6
]
) Dubrovin noticed that an essential part of the
proof can be simplified by endowing
K\{\CD\mathbb{G}\}
with a structure of a linear topological
space such that elements of
D
become linear continuous maps. Thus the aforementioned
algebraic conditions can be understood as well known properties of continuous maps.

In this paper we develop a very general approach to tackling this situation. Namely,
with each filter
\mathfrak{G}
on a set
G
we connect a linear space
K\{\mathfrak{G}\}
of all maps from

G
to
K
whose zero set belongs to
\mathfrak{G}
. We endow this space with a linear Hausdorff
topology making it into linear topological space.
The examples of such topologies
include Tychonoff topology on the product of spaces, but also the adic topology on the space
of Laurent power series. However, the example of our main interest will be the space
of formal series
K\{\CD G\}
with well ordered support over a left ordered group
G
.
One of the main result of the paper describes linear continuous maps between topological
spaces
K\{\mathfrak{G}\}
and
K\{H\}
, where
\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathfrak{H}
are filters on sets
G
and
H

with involution (see Theorem
9.6
). In particular, we completely characterize
such maps in terms of zero sets of their (infinite) matrices. Namely, these zero sets must
belong to a special filter on the direct product of
H
and
G
, which were
introduced and investigated in
[
5
]
.

As a corollary we give a matrix description of the ring of continuous operators of the
space
K\{\mathfrak{G}\}
(see Theorem
10.7
).

There is a different approach how to embed a group ring of a countable torsion-free group
into a skew field, based on the theory of
C^{*}
-algebras and operators on Hilbert spaces
(see
[
9
]
). From this point of view this paper is a first step in developing a similar
machinery in a more general and abstract situation. For instance, in Section
5

we introduce the operation of pairing on formal sums which resembles scalar product in
Hilbert spaces.

2.
Filters

In this section we recall some basic facts and definitions, and also
some results from
[
5
]
.

Let
G
be a set. A nonempty collection
\mathfrak{G}
of subsets of
G
is
said to be a
filter
, if it is closed with respect to finite
intersections and supersets. For instance, we allow the set of all
subsets of
G
,
\mathcal{P}(G)
, to be a filter. Clearly, if
\mathfrak{G}
is a
filter, then
\mathfrak{G}=\mathcal{P}(G)
iff
\emptyset\in\mathfrak{G}
. If
\mathfrak{G}\neq\mathcal{P}(G)
,
then
\mathfrak{G}
is said to be a
proper filter
on
G
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a collection of subsets of
G
with the following
property: for all
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
there is
C\in\mathcal{L}
such that
C\subseteq A\cap B
. Then
\mathfrak{G}=\{B\mid A\subseteq B
for some
A\in\mathcal{L}\}
is a

filter generated by
\mathcal{L}
, and
\mathcal{L}
is a
filter base
for
\mathfrak{G}
.

We say that a subset
A
of
G
is
cofinite
, if its
complement
\overline{A}
is a finite set. The
Frechet filter
on

G
,
\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
, consists of all cofinite subsets of
G
. Clearly

\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
is a proper filter iff
G
is an infinite set.

Let
G
be a linearly ordered set. A subset
\Delta\subseteq G
is
said to be
well ordered
, if every nonempty subset of
\Delta

has a minimal element. This is the same as
\Delta
has a

descending chain condition
(d.c.c.): every descending chain
of elements
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\dots
of
\Delta
stabilizes. Clearly

\Delta
has a d.c.c. iff it contains no (strictly) descending chain

a_{1}>a_{2}>a_{3}>\dots
.

Similarly,
\Delta\subseteq G
has an
ascending chain
condition
(a.c.c.), if every ascending chain
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\dots

of its elements stabilizes. Thus
\Delta
has an a.c.c. iff it
contains no (strictly) ascending chain
a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots
iff
G

is well ordered in the dual ordering.

Suppose that
(G,\leq)
is a linearly ordered set. Let
\CD(G)

denote the collection of all subsets of
G
whose complement has a
d.c.c. Since the union of two well ordered subsets of
G
is well
ordered,
\CD(G)
is a filter on
G
, and it is a proper filter iff

G
is not well ordered.

Similarly let
\CA(G)
be a collection of all subsets of
G
whose
complement has an a.c.c. Then
\CA(G)
is a filter on
G
and this
filter is proper iff
G
contains a strictly ascending chain.

We can order the filters on
G
by inclusion:
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{G}_{2}
if

\mathfrak{G}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. It is easily checked that with respect to this
ordering the set of all filters on
G
forms a lattice, that is, for
any filters
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
there is a least filter
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2}
containing
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
, and there is a largest
filter
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\wedge\mathfrak{G}_{2}
which is contained in both

\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

The following remark describes the operations in this lattice.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
be filters on a set
G
. Then
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\wedge\mathfrak{G}_{2}

is given by the intersection of filters:
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2}=\{A\subseteq G\mid A\in\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
A\in\mathfrak{G}_{2}\}
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2}

is the filter generated by all intersections
A\cap B
,
A\in\mathfrak{G}_{1}
,
B\in\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

We define a new operation on filters. In ring theory this operation
corresponds to the quotient of ideals. Suppose that
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and

\mathfrak{G}_{2}
are filters on
G
. Define
\mathfrak{G}_{1}:\mathfrak{G}_{2}=\{A\subseteq G\mid A\cup A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}_{1}
for every
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}_{2}\}
.

Fact 2.2

(
[
5
]
, property 6)
.

\mathfrak{G}_{1}:\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is a filter on
G
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{G}_{1}:\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is
the largest filter
\mathcal{F}
on
G
with the property
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{1}
.

The following remark is straightforward.

Remark 2.3
.

1) If
\mathfrak{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{2}
then
\mathfrak{F}_{1}:\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{2}:\mathfrak{G}
.

2) If
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{2}
then
\mathfrak{F}:\mathfrak{G}_{1}\supseteq\mathfrak{F}:\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

3)
\mathfrak{F}:\mathfrak{G}=(\mathfrak{F}\cap\mathfrak{G}):\mathfrak{G}
.

4)
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}:\mathfrak{G}
.

If
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter, then define

\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\{A\subseteq G\mid A\cup A^{\prime}\text{ is cofinite for every }A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}\}.

For instance,

\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)^{\perp}=\mathcal{P}(G)
and
\mathcal{P}(G)^{\perp}=\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
. It follows
from the definition that
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\mathrm{Cof}\,(G):\mathfrak{G}
, hence

\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
is a filter by Fact
2.2
. Furthermore,
Remark
2.3
, 4) implies that
\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
and
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp\perp}

for any filter
\mathfrak{G}
.

Proof.

We prove that
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
. Fix
A\in\mathfrak{G}
and choose
any
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
. Then (by the definition of
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
)

A\cup A^{\prime}
is cofinite, hence
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
.

Remark
2.3
2) applied to
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
yields

\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\supseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp\perp}
, and the reverse inclusion
follows from what we have just proved.
∎

A filter
\mathfrak{G}
is said to be
balanced
, if

\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}=\mathfrak{G}
. For instance,
\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
and
\mathcal{P}(G)
are
balanced filters. Furthermore, every balanced filter on
G
contains

\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
.

Fact 2.5

(
[
5
]
, Thm. 14)
.

Let
G
be a linearly ordered set. Then
\CD(G)^{\perp}=\CA(G)
and

\CA(G)^{\perp}=\CD(G)
, therefore
\CD(G)
and
\CA(G)
are
balanced filters.

3.
Space of
\mathfrak{G}
-zero functions

Most results of this paper can be proven for normed skew fields
K
.
But to avoid technicalities, in what follows
K
will always denote
a skew field with a discrete topology.

A left (right)
K
-linear space
L
with a topology
\mathcal{T}
is
said to be a
linear topological space
, if the addition of
elements of
L
defines a continuous function
L\times L\to L
,
where
L\times L
is taken with product topology; and the same is
true for any function
k\times L\to L
given by multiplication by
k\in K
.
Since
K
is discrete the last condition can be
replaced by the following: for each open set
U\subseteq L
and every

0\neq k\in K
the product
kU
is open.

Suppose that
U_{i}
,
i\in I
is a collection of subspaces of
L

such that for all
i,j\in I
there exists
k\in I
with
U_{k}\subseteq U_{i}\cap U_{j}
. A subset
V
of
L
is defined to be open, if for
every
a\in V
there exists
i\in I
such that
a+U_{i}\subseteq V
. This
defines a
linear topology

\mathcal{T}
on
L
, therefore
L
is a
topological space with a linear topology. Note that
\mathcal{T}
is
Hausdorff iff
\cap_{i\in I}U_{i}=\{0\}
. In this paper we will
consider only Hausdorff linear topologies.

For instance, let
G
be a set and let
L=\Map(G,K)
be a left
(right) vector space of all maps from
G
to
K
. Let
I
be the
collection of all finite subsets of
G
, and we consider
I
as a
set of indices. For each
i\in I
define a subspace
U_{i}
of
L

consisting of all maps
f:G\to K
such that
f(g)=0
for every

g\in i
. Then the family
U_{i}
,
i\in I
defines a linear topology
on
L
called the
Tychonoff topology
. For instance, if
K
is
a finite field (with discrete topology), then
L
is a compact
space (Tychonoff theorem).

Suppose that
f:G\to K
is a map. Then the
support of
f
,

\supp(f)
, is the following subset of
G
:
\supp(f)=\{g\in G\mid f(g)\neq 0\}
. Similarly the
zero-set of
f
,
\mathcal{Z}(f)
, is
defined as
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\{g\in G\mid f(g)=0\}
. Clearly
G=\supp(f)\cup\mathcal{Z}(f)
is a partition of
G
. Furthermore, if
f,h:G\to K
and

0\neq k\in K
then
\mathcal{Z}(f+h)\supseteq\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap\mathcal{Z}(h)
,
\mathcal{Z}(k\cdot f)=\mathcal{Z}(f)
and
\mathcal{Z}(0)=G
, where
0
stands for the zero function.

If
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter on
G
, then
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=\{f\in\Map(G,K)\mid\mathcal{Z}(f)\in\mathfrak{G}\}
will denote the
space of
\mathfrak{G}
-zero
functions
. Clearly
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
is a left and right subspace of the
(linear) topological space
\Map(G,K)
. For instance,

\FU(\mathcal{P}(G))=\Map(G,K)
.

The following remark shows that operations on linear spaces

\FU(\mathfrak{G})
correspond to operations on the lattice of filters (see
Remark
2.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
be filters on
G
. Then
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})\cap\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2})
and
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})+\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2})
. Furthermore, if
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{2}

then
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})
is a subspace of
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})
.

Proof.

Clearly
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})\cap\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})
iff
\mathcal{Z}(f)\in\mathfrak{G}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2}
iff
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2})
, which proves that

\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})\cap\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{G}_{2})
.

To prove the inclusion
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})+\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})\subseteq\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2})
let
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})+\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})
. Then
f=h_{1}+h_{2}
, where

\mathcal{Z}(h_{i})\in\mathfrak{G}_{i}
. It follows that
\mathcal{Z}(f)\supseteq\mathcal{Z}(h_{1})\cap\mathcal{Z}(h_{2})\in\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2}
, hence
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2})
.

For the reverse inclusion suppose that
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}\vee\mathfrak{G}_{2})
,
that is,
\mathcal{Z}(f)\supseteq A_{1}\cap A_{2}
for some
A_{i}\in\mathfrak{G}_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Define

h\in\Map(G,K)
as follows:

h_{1}(g)=\begin{cases}f(g),&\text{if $g\in\overline{A}_{1}$}\\
\phantom{f(g}0,&\text{if $g\in A_{1}$,}\end{cases}\hskip 42.67912pth_{2}(g)=\begin{cases}f(g),&\text{if $g\in A_{1}\setminus A_{2}$}\\
\phantom{fg)}0,&\text{if $g\notin A_{1}\setminus A_{2}$.}\end{cases}

Then
f=h_{1}+h_{2}
and
\mathcal{Z}(h_{1})\supseteq A_{1}
,
\mathcal{Z}(h_{2})\supseteq A_{2}
,
therefore
h_{i}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{i})
.
∎

Given
A\subseteq G
, we set
U(A,\mathfrak{G})=\{f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})\mid\mathcal{Z}(f)\supseteq\overline{A}\}
. Clearly this is the same as
\supp(f)\subseteq A
.

The proof of the following lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 3.2
.

1)
U(A,\mathfrak{G})
is a left (right) subspace of
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
.

2) If
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq G
then
U(A_{1}\cap A_{2},\mathfrak{G})=U(A_{1},\mathfrak{G})\cap U(A_{2},\mathfrak{G})
.

Now we are in a position to construct a linear topology on

\FU(\mathfrak{G})
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
6
]
, Thm. 1)
.

1) The family of subspaces
\{U(A,\mathfrak{G})\mid A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\}
form a
base of zero neighborhoods of a linear topology
T(\mathfrak{G})
on the
spaces
\Map(G,K)
and
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
, and this topology is Hausdorff.

2) If
\mathfrak{G}
is a balanced filter, then
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
is complete in
this topology.

3) If
\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{G}
then the set of all maps from
\Map(G,K)

with finite support is dense in
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
.

Let us consider some examples of topologies
T(\mathfrak{G})
.

Example 3.4
.

1) If
\mathfrak{G}=\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
, then
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
consists of all functions with
finite support and
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\mathcal{P}(G)
. Then
\emptyset\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
, hence

U(\emptyset,G)=\{0\}
is an open set. It follows that every subset of

\FU(\mathfrak{G})
is open and closed, hence
T(\mathfrak{G})
is a discrete
topology.

2) If
\mathfrak{G}=\mathcal{P}(G)
, then
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
,
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=\Map(G,K)
and

A
runs over all cofinite subsets of
G
. Thus we obtain the
Tychonoff topology whose subbase is given by the subspaces

U_{g}=\{f\in\Map(G,K)\mid f(g)=0\}
.

3) Suppose that
(G,\leq)
is a linearly ordered set and

\mathfrak{G}=\CA(G)
. Then
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}=\CD(G)
, therefore the base of zero
neighborhoods is given by
U_{D}=\{f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})\mid f(g)=0
for every

g\in D\}
, where
D
is a well ordered subset of
G
.

Note that, if
G=(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
, then the space
\Map(\mathbb{Q},K)
with
Tychonoff topology is metrizable and separable. This is not longer
true for the topology
T(\CA G)
. Indeed, suppose that
D_{1},D_{2},\dots
are well ordered subsets of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
U_{D_{1}},U_{D_{2}},\dots
form a basis of zero neighborhoods. This means that for every
well ordered
D\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
there is
k
such that
U_{D_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap U_{D_{k}}\subseteq U_{D}
. It follows easily that
D\subseteq D_{1}\cup\ldots\cup D_{k}
.

Clearly there exists an ascending sequence
d_{1}<d_{2}<\dots
such that

d_{k}\notin D_{1}\cup\dots\cup D_{k}
for every
k
. If
D=\{d_{1},d_{2},\dots\}
,
then
D
has a d.c.c, therefore
D\subseteq D_{1}\cup\ldots\cup D_{k}
for some
k
,
and then
d_{k}\in D_{1}\cup\ldots\cup D_{k}
, a contradiction.

This shows that in the space
(\FU(\CA\mathbb{Q}),T(\CA\mathbb{Q}))
no point has a
countable base of neighborhoods, in particular, this space is not separable.

4) Let
G=\langle t\rangle
be an infinite cyclic group with the usual
linear ordering:
t^{n}\geq t^{m}
iff
n\geq m
. Let
\mathfrak{G}=\CD(G)
,
that is,
\mathfrak{G}
is generated by the following collection of sets:

\{t^{n}\mid n<l\}
,
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
is generated by

\{t^{n}\mid n>m\}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, hence
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
is the space of
Laurent power series
\sum_{i\geq l}k_{i}t^{i}
and
T(\mathfrak{G})
is the

t
-adic topology.

4.
Direct sum decompositions

Let
C
be a subset of
G
. We identify
\Map(C,K)
with a subspace
of
\Map(G,K)
consisting of all maps
f:G\to K
such that the
restriction of
f
to
\overline{C}
is zero. If
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter on
G
,
then the family
\mathfrak{G}_{C}=\{A\cap C\mid A\in\mathfrak{G}\}
will be a filter on

G
called an
induced filter
.
Note that with respect to the above identification,
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{C})\subseteq\FU(\mathfrak{G})
. Indeed, if
f\in\Map(C,K)
, then
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{C})
means
that
\mathcal{Z}(f)=A\cap C
for some
A\in\mathfrak{G}
. If we consider
f
as a
map from
G
to
K
, then
\mathcal{Z}(f)=(A\cap C)\cup\overline{C}\supseteq A
, hence

f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
C\subseteq G
. Then the topology
T(\mathfrak{G}_{C})
on the space

\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{C})
coincides with the topology induced by
T(\mathfrak{G})
.

Proof.

Note that
A\cap C\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}_{C}
for every
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
. Indeed,
every set from
\mathfrak{G}_{C}
can be written in the form
A^{\prime}\cap C
for
some
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
. Since
A\cup A^{\prime}
is cofinite in
G
, it follows
that
(A\cap C)\cup(A^{\prime}\cap C)=(A\cup A^{\prime})\cap C
is cofinite in
C
.
Thus
A\cap C\in\mathfrak{G}_{C}^{\perp}
and clearly
U(A,\mathfrak{G})\cap\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{C})\subseteq U(A\cap C,\mathfrak{G}_{C})
.

Now take any
A_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}_{C}^{\perp}
. We claim that
B=A_{1}\cup\overline{C}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
which would imply the inclusion
U(A_{1},\mathfrak{G}_{C})\subseteq U(B,\mathfrak{G})\cap\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{C})
. Indeed, for every
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
the set
A_{1}\cup(A^{\prime}\cap C)
is cofinite in
C
. Then
(A_{1}\cup\overline{C})\cup A^{\prime}
is
cofinite in
G
because it contains
A_{1}\cup(A^{\prime}\cap C)
(cofinite
in
C
) and
\overline{C}
.
∎

Let
A
be a subset of
G
. For every map
f:G\to K
we have

f=f|_{A}+f|_{\overline{A}}
, where
\supp(f|_{A})\subseteq A
and

\supp(f|_{\overline{A}})\subseteq\overline{A}
. This yields a decomposition of
linear spaces:
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{A})\oplus\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})
, where

\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{A})\subseteq\Map(A,K)
and
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})\subseteq\Map(\overline{A},K)
.

In the following proposition we will single out two important
particular cases.

Proposition 4.2
.

1) If
A\in\mathfrak{G}
then
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{A})\oplus\Map(\overline{A},K)
.
Furthermore,
T(\mathfrak{G})
induces the Tychonoff topology on
\Map(\overline{A},K)
.

2) If
\mathfrak{G}\supseteq\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)
and
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
, then
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=U(A,\mathfrak{G})\oplus\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,\overline{A})
. Furthermore,
T(\mathfrak{G})
induces the
discrete topology on
\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,\overline{A})
.

Proof.

1) Since
A\cap\overline{A}=\emptyset\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})
, the induced filter on
\overline{A}
coincides
with
\mathcal{P}(\overline{A})
, hence
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})=\Map(\overline{A},K)
. Thus, by
Proposition
4.1
and Example
3.4
2), the topology
induced on
\Map(\overline{A},K)
by
T(\mathfrak{G})
will be Tychonoff.
It remains to apply the equality
\FU(\mathfrak{G})=\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{A})\oplus\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})
.

2) By the definition of
\perp
-operation, we obtain
\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}}=\mathrm{Cof}\,(\overline{A})
.
Again, by Proposition
4.1
and Example
3.4
1),

T(\mathfrak{G})
induces discrete topology on
\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{\overline{A}})
. Now the
result follows from the same equality.
∎

5.
Involution and pairing

Now assume that
G
is a set with an involution
*
, that is, with a
map
G\to G
such that
g^{**}=g
. If
A
is a subset of
G
then
we define
A^{*}=\{a^{*}\mid a\in A\}
, and we put
\mathfrak{G}^{*}=\{A^{*}\mid A\in\mathfrak{G}\}
, if
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter on
G
.

The following remark is obvious.

Remark 5.1
.

If
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter, then
\mathfrak{G}^{*}
is also a filter. Furthermore,
the map
\mathfrak{G}\to\mathfrak{G}^{*}
defines an automorphism of the lattice of
filters on
G
. For instance,
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}=\mathfrak{G}^{*\perp}
for every
filter
G
.

A filter
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
is said to be
adjoint
to the filter

\mathfrak{G}
. Thus a filter
\mathfrak{G}
is
self-adjoint
, if
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}=\mathfrak{G}
. Note that every self-adjoined filter is balanced. Indeed,

\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}=\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp**}=\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*\perp*}=(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})^{\perp*}=\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}=\mathfrak{G}
. For instance, let

G
be a linearly orderer group,
\mathfrak{G}=\CD(G)
and the involution
*

is given by
g^{*}=g^{-1}
. Then
\CD(G)^{\perp*}=\CA(G)^{*}
. Since
taking the inverse in the linearly ordered group reverses the
ordering,
\CA(G)^{*}=\CD(G)
, therefore
\CD(G)
is self-adjoint, and
the same is true for
\CA(G)
.

From now on each group
G
will be considered as a group with the
involution
*:g\to g^{-1}
.

Suppose that
G
is a group with a linear ordering
\leq
. We say that
G
is

left (right) ordered
, if
g_{1}\leq g_{2}
implies
hg_{1}\leq hg_{2}
(
g_{1}h\leq g_{2}h
)
for every
g_{1},g_{2},h\in G
. A group
G
is said to be
linearly ordered
,
if it is left and right ordered with respect to
\leq
.

Proposition 5.2
.

A left ordered group
(G,\leq)
is linearly ordered iff the filter

\CD(G)
(or
\CA(G)
) is self-adjoint.

Proof.

We have already proved that for a linearly ordered group
G
,
both
\CD(G)
and
\CA(G)
are self-adjoint filters.

Suppose that
G
is a left ordered group and
\CD(G)
is a
self-adjoint filter. This means that
\CD(G)^{\perp*}=\CD(G)
, which
is the same as
\CD(G)^{\perp}=\CD(G)^{*}
or
\CA(G)=\CD(G)^{*}
.

To prove that
G
is linearly ordered it suffices to check that the
cone
P=\{g\in G\mid g\geq e\}
is invariant, that is,
a^{-1}Pa=P

for every
a\in G
. Moreover it is enough to verify that
a^{-1}Pa\subseteq P
for every
a\in G
. Assuming otherwise we will find
a,b\in G
such that
b>e
and
a^{-1}ba<e
. Multiplying
ba<a
on
the left by
b
we obtain a descending chain
\Delta=\{a>ba>b^{2}a>\dots\}
. Since
\Delta
has an a.c.c.,
\overline{\Delta}\in\CA(G)
. From
\CA(G)=\CD(G)^{*}

it follows that
\overline{\Delta}^{*}\in\CD(G)
where
\Delta^{*}=\{a^{-1}b^{-n}\mid n\in\omega\}
,
hence
\Delta^{*}
has a smallest element. If
a^{-1}b^{-n}
is
such, then
a^{-1}b^{-n}\leq a^{-1}b^{-n-1}
. Multiplying by
b^{n+1}a
on the left
we obtain
b\leq e
, a contradiction.
∎

Now we define a
pairing
on the space
\Map(G,K)
as the
following partially defined non-degenerate bilinear form. If
f,h\in\Map(G,K)
then

(1)

\langle f,h\rangle=\sum_{x\in G}f(x)h(x^{*})=\sum_{x\in G}f(x^{*})h(x)

and the result
\langle f,h\rangle
is defined, if
\supp(f)\cap(\supp h)^{*}
is a finite set.
Clearly this is the same as

(\supp f)^{*}\cap\supp(h)
is finite. In particular, this is the case
when
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
and
h\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})
or vice versa. If

K
is a field, this form is symmetric.

We say that the equality (
1
) defines a
pairing
between
subspaces
L
and
L^{\prime}
of
\Map(G,K)
, if the following holds true:

1) the product
\langle f,h\rangle
is defined for all
f\in L
,
h\in L^{\prime}
;

2) if
f\in\Map(G,K)
and the product
\langle f,h\rangle
is defined
for every
h\in L^{\prime}
, then
f\in L
;

3) if
h\in\Map(G,K)
and the product
\langle f,h\rangle
is defined
for every
f\in L
, then
h\in L^{\prime}
.

In this case
(L,L^{\prime})
is said to be a
dual pair
.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\mathfrak{G}
is a balanced filter on a set
G
with an involution, then

(\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}),\FU(\mathfrak{G}))
is a dual pair and the same is true
for the pair
(\FU(\mathfrak{G}),\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}))
.

Proof.

1) is satisfied by the definition of
\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
.

2) Let
f\in\Map(G,K)
is such that
\langle f,h\rangle
is defined for
every
h\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
. Then
(\supp f)^{*}\cap\supp(h)
is finite for
every
h\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
, therefore
\mathcal{Z}(f)^{*}\cup\mathcal{Z}(h)
is a cofinite
set for every such
h
. Take any
B\in\mathfrak{G}
and let
h_{B}
be the
characteristic function of
G\setminus B
(that is,
h_{B}(g)=0
if
g\in B
, and
h_{B}(g)=1
otherwise). Clearly
h_{B}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
. It follows
that
\mathcal{Z}(f)^{*}\cup B
is a cofinite set, therefore, by the
definition of
\perp
, we obtain
\mathcal{Z}(f)^{*}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
. It follows
that
\mathcal{Z}(f)\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
hence
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})
.

3) Suppose that
h\in\Map(G,K)
and the result
\langle f,h\rangle
is
defined for every
f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})
. As in the proof of 2) it
follows that
\mathcal{Z}(h)\in(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})^{\perp*}=\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}=\mathfrak{G}
,
since
\mathfrak{G}
is balanced.
∎

Thus we obtain the following diagram of pairing:

\textstyle{\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Map(G,K)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Map(G,K)}
\textstyle{\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{1}^{\perp*})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\FU(\mathfrak{G}_{2}^{\perp*})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\,,}

where
\longleftrightarrow
stands for pairing, and
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{2}
are balanced filters.

Because
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
and
\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})
are paired, it follows
that
\FU(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*})
is isomorphic to the space of linear
forms on
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
, that is to the space of linear continuous maps from

\FU(\mathfrak{G})
to
K
. We will derive this fact later from a more
general description of continuous linear operators on spaces of

\mathfrak{G}
-zero functions.

6.
Filters on direct products

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a filter on a set
G
, and let
\mathfrak{H}
be a filter on

H
. In this section we consider different extensions of these
filters to a filter on the direct product
H\times G
. One of these
extensions is well known.

Fact 6.1
.

(see
[
1
, Sec. 6.7]
)
The family
\{B\times A\mid B\in\mathfrak{H},A\in\mathfrak{G}\}
is a base of a filter

\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{G}
on
H\times G
. This filter is proper iff both
\mathfrak{G}
and

\mathfrak{H}
are proper.

But this particular filter bears no significance for topological
spaces of formal sums. The following filter is more useful.

Fact 6.2

(see
[
5
]
, Prop. 16)
.

The family
\{\overline{\overline{B}\times\overline{A}}\mid B\in\mathfrak{H},A\in\mathfrak{G}\}
form a
base of a filter
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
on
H\times G
. The filter
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}

is proper iff
\mathfrak{G}
or
\mathfrak{H}
is proper. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{G}
.

Note that, if
X
is a subset of
H\times G
, then
X\in\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}

iff
X\supseteq\overline{\overline{B}\times\overline{A}}
iff
\overline{X}\subseteq\overline{B}\times\overline{A}
for
some
B\in\mathfrak{H}
and
A\in\mathfrak{G}
.

Fact 6.3

(see
[
5
]
, (6))
.

\mathrm{Cof}\,(H)\otimes\mathrm{Cof}\,(G)=\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)
.

We introduce a new filter
\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
whose subbase is given by
the following collections of sets:
\{\overline{h\times\overline{A}}\mid h\in H,A\in\mathfrak{G}\}
and
\{\overline{\overline{B}\times g}\mid B\in\mathfrak{H},g\in G\}
. Thus

\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
consists of subsets of
H\times G
whose complement is
a subset of a finite union of sets
\{h\}\times\overline{A}
and
\overline{B}\times\{g\}
. But every subset of
\{h\}\times\overline{A}
,
A\in\mathfrak{G}
is of the
form
\{h\}\times\overline{A}^{\prime}
for some
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
, and every subset of

\overline{B}\times\{g\}
,
B\in\mathfrak{H}
is of the form
\overline{B}^{\prime}\times\{g\}
for
some
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}
. It follows that
\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
consists of
complements to the sets
\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{h_{i}\}\times\overline{A}_{i}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\overline{B}_{j}\times\{g_{j}\}\right)
, where
g_{j}\in G
,

h_{i}\in H
and
A_{i}\in\mathfrak{G}
,
B_{j}\in\mathfrak{H}
.

Fact 6.4

(see
[
5
]
, Prop. 18)
.

\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})=(\mathrm{Cof}\,H\otimes\mathfrak{G})\vee(H\otimes\mathrm{Cof}\,G)
.

Fact 6.5

(see
[
5
]
, Prop. 18, 20)
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{G}
contains the Frechet filter on
G
, and
\mathfrak{H}

contains the Frechet filter on
H
. Then

1)
\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)\subseteq\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})\subseteq\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
.

2)
\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})=(\mathrm{Cof}\,H\times\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\cap(\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G})
.

Now we are in a position to introduce the main construction of this
section.

Definition 6.6
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{H}
is a filter on
H
and
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter on
G
.
Let
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
consists of all subsets
X
of
H\times G

with the following properties:

a) for every
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
there exists
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}
such that

B^{\prime}\times\overline{A}\subseteq X
;

b) for every
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}\subseteq X
.

If
t\in G
, then define
H_{t}(X)=\{h\in H\mid(h,t)\in X\}
. Similarly,
if
s\in H
, then set
G_{s}(X)=\{g\in G\mid(s,g)\in X\}
.

It is easily seen that
X\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
iff the following
holds:

a)
′

\bigcap_{t\in\overline{A}}H_{t}(X)\in\mathfrak{H}
for every
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
;

b)
′

\bigcap_{s\in\overline{B}}G_{s}(X)\in\mathfrak{G}
for every
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
.

For instance, the equivalence of a) and a)
′
can be seen as
follows. If a) holds then
B^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcap_{t\in\overline{A}}H_{t}
, hence
this intersection is in
\mathfrak{H}
; and if a)
′
holds then we take
B^{\prime}

to be equal to this intersection.

Fact 6.7

(see
[
5
]
, Thm. 23)
.

\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
is a filter on
H\times G
containing
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
.

Fact 6.8

(see
[
5
]
, L. 24)
.

If
\mathfrak{H}
and
\mathfrak{G}
contain Frechet filters, then
(\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G})\cap(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)
.

The following remark is obvious.

Remark 6.9
.

If
\mathfrak{H}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\prime}
, then
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 6.10
.

If
\mathfrak{H}
and
\mathfrak{G}
are balanced filters, then
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle\supseteq\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}):(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}
.

Proof.

First we prove the equality. We claim that
\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})\cap(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)
. Indeed, by Fact
6.5
2),
we obtain

\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})\cap(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=(\mathrm{Cof}\,H\times\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\cap(\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G})\cap(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})\,.

By Fact
6.8
this is the same as
(\mathrm{Cof}\,H\times\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\cap\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)=\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G)
, as
desired.

Then by Remark
2.3
3) and the definition of
\perp
we obtain

\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}):(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=(\mathrm{Cof}\,(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})\cap(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})):(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=\mathrm{Cof}\,(H\times G):(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})=(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}
.

Now we prove the inclusion. Suppose that
Z\in(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}
and we have to show that
Z\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
.
Take any
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
and define
A^{\prime}\subseteq G
by the following rule:

\overline{A}^{\prime}=\{g\in G\mid(h,g)\in\overline{Z}
for some
h\in\overline{B}\}=\pi_{G}[(\overline{B}\times G)\cap\overline{Z}]
, where
\pi_{G}
is a projection on
G
.

Suppose that
A^{\prime}\notin\mathfrak{G}
. Then
A^{\prime}\notin(\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}
because

\mathfrak{G}
is balanced. It follows that there exists
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
such
that the union
A^{\prime}\cup A
is not cofinite in
G
, that is, the
intersection
\overline{A}^{\prime}\cap\overline{A}
is infinite. Since
\overline{\overline{B}\times\overline{A}}\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
, it follows that
Z\cup\overline{\overline{B}\times\overline{A}}
is cofinite in
H\times G
, hence
\overline{Z}\cap(\overline{B}\times\overline{A})
is a
finite set. On the other hand for every
g\in\overline{A}^{\prime}\cap\overline{A}

(there are infinitely many of them) there exists
h\in\overline{B}
such
that
(h,g)\in\overline{Z}\cap(\overline{B}\times\overline{A})
, hence this set must be
infinite, a contradiction. Thus
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

We prove that
\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}\subseteq Z
. Indeed, otherwise
(h,g)\in\overline{Z}
for some
(h,g)\in\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}
. Since
g\notin\overline{A}^{\prime}
, by the
construction of
\overline{A}^{\prime}
we obtain
h\notin\overline{B}
, a contradiction.

Thus for every
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that

\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}\subseteq Z
. Similarly for every
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
there exists

B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}
such that
B^{\prime}\times\overline{A}\subseteq Z
. It follows that
Z\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
.
∎

Before proving the next lemma, let us recall a useful equality: if

B\subseteq H
and
A\subseteq G
, then
\overline{(\overline{B}\times\overline{A})}=(B\times A)\cup(B\times\overline{A})\cup(\overline{B}\times A)
.

Lemma 6.11
.

\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\subseteq\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle^{\perp}
for all filters

\mathfrak{H}
and
\mathfrak{G}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Z\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
, hence
\overline{Z}\subseteq\overline{B}\times\overline{A}
for some
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
,
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
. We have to prove
that
Z\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle^{\perp}
, that is,
Z\cup X
is a cofinite
set for every
X\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
. Since
X\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
there are
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}
and
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
B^{\prime}\times\overline{A},\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}\subseteq X
. Then

\overline{X}\subseteq\overline{(B^{\prime}\times\overline{A})\cup(\overline{B}\times A^{\prime})}=\overline{B^{\prime}\times\overline{A}}\cap\overline{\overline{B}\times A^{\prime}}=

((\overline{B}^{\prime}\times A)\cup(\overline{B}^{\prime}\times\overline{A})\cup(B^{\prime}\times A))\cap((B\times\overline{A}^{\prime})\cup(B\times A^{\prime})\cup(\overline{B}\times\overline{A}^{\prime}))\,.

Intersecting this with
\overline{Z}\subseteq\overline{B}\times\overline{A}
and taking into account that the intersections of
\overline{B}\times\overline{A}
with
\overline{B}^{\prime}\times A
,
B^{\prime}\times A
,
B\times\overline{A}^{\prime}
and
B\times A^{\prime}
are empty, we obtain
\overline{X}\cap\overline{Z}\subseteq(\overline{B}^{\prime}\cap\overline{B})\times(\overline{A}\cap\overline{A}^{\prime})
. Since
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}
and
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}
, therefore

B\cup B^{\prime}
is a cofinite set, hence
\overline{B}^{\prime}\cap\overline{B}
is finite.
Similarly
\overline{A}^{\prime}\cap\overline{A}
is a finite set, hence
\overline{X}\cap\overline{Z}
is finite, as desired.
∎

Remark 6.12
.

\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\langle\mathfrak{H}^{\perp},\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\rangle^{\perp}
for all filter

\mathfrak{H}
and
\mathfrak{G}.

Proof.

Indeed, by Lemma
2.4
we have
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
and

\mathfrak{H}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}^{\perp\perp}
, therefore
\mathfrak{H}\otimes\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}^{\perp\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}
by Remark
6.9
. Furthermore

\mathfrak{H}^{\perp\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}\subseteq\langle\mathfrak{H}^{\perp},\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\rangle^{\perp}

by Lemma
6.11
.
∎

Theorem 6.13
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{H}
and
\mathfrak{G}
are balanced filters. Then
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle=(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}
. In
particular,
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle
is a balanced filter.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.10
we have
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\rangle^{\perp}
. On the other hand applying
\perp
to
the inclusion in Lemma
6.11
, we obtain
(\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{\perp})^{\perp}\supseteq\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle^{\perp\perp}
. Thus
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathfrak{H}^{\perp},\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}\rangle^{\perp}\supseteq\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle^{\perp\perp}
, hence
\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}\rangle^{\perp\perp}
. Then Lemma
2.4
yields the desired.
∎

7.
\mathfrak{G}
-sums

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{T})
is a typological abelian group and
x_{i}
,

i\in I
is a family of elements of
X
. An element
x\in X
is said to be
a
sum
of this family with respect to
\mathcal{T}
, written

x=\sum_{i\in I}^{\mathcal{T}}x_{i}
, if the following holds. For every
neighbourhood
U
of
x
there is a finite subset
\Delta\subseteq I
such
that
\sum_{i\in\Delta^{\prime}}x_{i}\in U
for every finite set
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq I

containing
\Delta
. Clearly, if
X
is Hausdorff, then the sum is
unique. It is easily seen that, if the family
x_{i}
,
i\in I
is
summable, then the limit of the
x_{i}
with respect to the Frechet
filter on
I
is equal to zero. This means that for every zero
neighbourhood
U
there exists a finite subset
\Delta
of
I
such
that
x_{i}\in U
for every
i\in I\setminus\Delta
.

Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{T}_{X})
and
(Y,\mathcal{T}_{Y})
are topological abelian
groups. If
\varphi
is a continuous morphism from
X
to
Y
then

\varphi
preserves topological sums. This means that, if
x=\sum_{i\in I}^{\mathcal{T}_{X}}x_{i}
in
X
, then the sum
\sum_{i\in I}^{\mathcal{T}_{Y}}\varphi(x_{i})
exists and equal to
\varphi(x)
.

For more on sums in topological abelian groups see
[
2
]

Now we define a sum with respect to a filter.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
h_{j}\in\Map(G,K)
,
j\in J
be a family of maps and let
\mathfrak{G}

be a filter. This family is said to be
\mathfrak{G}
-summable
, and
the map
f:G\to K
is a
\mathfrak{G}
-sum
of this family,

f=\sum_{j\in J}^{\mathfrak{G}}h_{j}
, if the following holds:

1) for every
g\in G
there are only finitely many
j\in J
such
that
g\in\supp(h_{j})
, and
f(g)=\sum_{j\in J}h_{j}(g)
;

2)
\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\in\mathfrak{G}
.

Note that the condition 1) of this definition means that the family

\{h_{j}\}
,
j\in J
is summable with respect to Tychonoff topology
on
\Map(G,K)
. Furthermore, 2) implies that
h_{j}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
for
every
j
; and
\mathcal{Z}(f)\supseteq\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})
yields that

f\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
.

In the following theorem we compare these two types of summability.

Theorem 7.2
.

1) If the family
h_{j}
,
j\in J
is
\mathfrak{G}
-summable, then it is
summable with respect to topology
T(\mathfrak{G})
. Furthermore,
\sum_{j\in J}^{\mathfrak{G}}h_{j}=\sum_{j\in J}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}h_{j}
.

2) If the family
h_{j}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
,
j\in J
is summable with
respect to
T(\mathfrak{G})
and
\mathfrak{G}
is balanced, then it is
\mathfrak{G}
-summable
and
\sum_{j\in J}^{\mathfrak{G}}h_{j}=\sum_{j\in J}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}h_{j}
again.

Proof.

1) Suppose that
f=\sum_{j\in J}^{\mathfrak{G}}h_{j}
and
A=\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\in\mathfrak{G}
. Choose any
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
. Then
A\cup A^{\prime}
is a
cofinite set, hence
A\cup A^{\prime}=G\setminus\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
,
g_{i}\in G
. Let
J_{0}
consist of all
j\in J
such that
g_{t}\in\supp(h_{j})

for some
t=1,\dots,n
. By the assumption
J_{0}
is a finite subset
of
J
.

Suppose that
J^{\prime}
is any finite subset of
J
containing
J_{0}
. Then

\mathcal{Z}(f-\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}h_{j})\supseteq A\cup\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}\supseteq\overline{A}^{\prime}
, therefore
f-\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}h_{j}\in U(A^{\prime},\mathfrak{G})
. This proves
that
f=\sum_{j\in J}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}h_{j}
.

2) Because the family
h_{j}
,
j\in J
is summable with respect to

T(\mathfrak{G})
, by what we have already noticed,
h_{j}
converges to
0

with respect to the Frechet filter on
I
. It follows that for every

g\in G
there exist only finitely many
j\in J
such that

h_{j}(g)\neq 0
, therefore 1) holds true.

Thus it remains to prove that
A=\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\in\mathfrak{G}
.
Let
f=\sum_{j\in J}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}h_{j}
. As above the
h_{j}
converge to
zero with respect to the Frechet filter on
J
. Thus for every

A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
there is a finite set of indices
F(A^{\prime})
such that
for every
j\in J\setminus F(A^{\prime})
we have
h_{j}\in U(A^{\prime},\mathfrak{G})
. Then

\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\supseteq\overline{A}^{\prime}
yields
\bigcap_{j\in J\setminus F(A^{\prime})}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\supseteq\overline{A}^{\prime}
. Since
h_{j}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
and
F(A^{\prime})
is finite, it follows
that
A_{1}=\bigcap_{j\in F(A^{\prime})}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\in\mathfrak{G}
. Then

A\cup A^{\prime}=\bigl(\bigcap_{j\in J\setminus F(A^{\prime})}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\cup A^{\prime}\bigl)\cap\bigl(\bigcap_{j\in F(A^{\prime})}\mathcal{Z}(h_{j})\cup A^{\prime}\bigl)=G\cap(A_{1}\cup A^{\prime})=A_{1}\cup A^{\prime}

is a cofinite set. Since this is true for any
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp}
, we conclude that
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp\perp}=\mathfrak{G}
.
∎

8.
Matrix notations

Suppose that
G
is a set and
H
is a set with an involution
*
.
Each map
\Psi:H\times G\to K
can be consider as an
H\times G

matrix over
K
whose
(h,g)
-entry,
\Psi^{g}_{h}
, is
\Psi(h,g)
.
These notations resemble the notations in tensor calculus and, as we
will see below, they are quite advantageous, when we consider
multiplication of matrices.

The set of all such maps form a (left and right) vector space over

K
and will be denoted by
{}^{H}K^{G}
. If
H
consists of one
element, a map
\Psi:H\times G\to K
is said to be a
row
,
and we use small Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,\dots
to denote rows.
Similarly, if
G
consists of one element, then a map
\Psi:H\to K

is a said to be a
column
, and we use small boldfaced letters

\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
,
\dots
to denote columns. In case, when

H
consists of one element
h
, we simplify notations:

K^{G}={}^{\{h\}}K^{G}
; and similarly
{}^{H}K
means
{}^{H}K^{\{g\}}
,
when
G
consists of one element
g
.

Let
\delta
be the Kronecker symbol on
G
, that is,
\delta
is a map
from
G\times G
to
K
such that
\delta^{g}_{h}=1
if
g=h
and

\delta^{g}_{h}=0
otherwise. If
\Psi\in{}^{H}K^{G}
and
h\in H
, then

\Psi_{h}
will denote the row of
\Psi
with number
h
, therefore

(\Psi_{h})^{g}=\Psi^{g}_{h}
for every
g\in G
. Similarly,
\Psi^{g}
will
denote the column of
\Psi
with number
g
, therefore

(\Psi^{g})_{h}=\Psi^{g}_{h}
for every
h\in H
. In particular,
\delta_{g}
is
a row whose
g
th entry is
1
and all remaining entries are zero,
and similarly for the column
\delta^{g}
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in{}^{J}K^{H}
and
\Psi\in{}^{H}K^{G}
. We say the the
product
\Theta=\Phi\cdot\Psi\in{}^{J}K^{G}
is defined if, for
every pair
(j,g)\in J\times G
, we have
\Phi^{h^{*}}_{j}\cdot\Psi^{g}_{h}\neq 0
only for finitely many
h\in H
and

\Theta^{g}_{j}=\sum_{h\in H}\Phi^{h^{*}}_{j}\cdot\Psi^{g}_{h}
. This defines
a partial operation
{}^{J}K^{H}\times{}^{H}K^{G}\to{}^{J}K^{G}
.

Note that
\sum_{h\in H}\Phi^{h^{*}}_{j}\cdot\Psi^{g}_{h}=\sum_{h\in H}\Phi^{h}_{j}\cdot\Psi^{g}_{h*}
. More precisely, the left and right
parts are defined simultaneously and, if they are defined, they are
equal. Immediately from the definition it follows that
\Theta_{j}=\Phi_{j}\cdot\Psi
and
\Theta^{g}=\Phi\cdot\Psi^{g}
.

If
G
is a finite set, then the multiplication on
{}^{G}K^{G}
is
defined everywhere, therefore
{}^{G}K^{G}
is a ring isomorphic to the
ring of
|G|\times|G|
matrices over
K
. But the unity of this
ring is given by the map
E:G\times G\to G
such that
E^{g^{*}}_{g}=1

for every
g\in G
and zero otherwise. For instance, if
G
consists
of one element
g
, then the ring
{}^{\{g\}}K^{\{g\}}
is isomorphic
to
K
. If
G
is infinite, the partial multiplication we have just
defined is usually not associative. Indeed, suppose that
G=\mathbb{N}
with
the identical involution
*
, and let
\Phi
,
\Psi
and
\Theta
be
the following matrices:

\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\dots\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0&0&\dots\\
0&1&-1&0&\dots\\
0&0&1&-1&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
\end{pmatrix}\mbox{\ and \ }\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Then
(\Phi\cdot\Psi)\cdot\Theta=1
and
\Phi\cdot(\Psi\cdot\Theta)=0
.

The following lemma claims distributivity and can be easily verified
by direct calculations.

Proposition 8.1
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in{}^{J}K^{H}
and
\Psi,\Theta\in{}^{H}K^{G}
. If both
products
\Phi\cdot\Psi
and
\Phi\cdot\Theta
are defined, then

\Phi\cdot(\Psi+\Theta)
is also defined and equal to
\Phi\cdot\Psi+\Phi\cdot\Theta
.

An arbitrary row
\gamma\in K^{G}
is uniquely determined by its
coordinates
\gamma^{g}\in K
,
g\in G
. Thus
\gamma
is a topological sum
(see Section
7
) with respect to Tychonoff topology on

\Map(G,K)
:
\gamma=\sum_{g\in G}^{\mathop{{\rm Tych}}\nolimits}\gamma^{g}\delta_{g}
. Furthermore,
if
\gamma\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
, then clearly
\gamma=\sum_{g\in G}^{\mathfrak{G}}\gamma^{g}\delta_{g}
. Similarly, each column
\mathbf{a}\in{}^{H}K
is uniquely
determined by its coordinates
a_{h}\in K
,
h\in H
, hence we can
write
\mathbf{a}=\sum_{h\in H}^{\mathop{{\rm Tych}}\nolimits}\delta^{h}a_{h}
; and if
\mathbf{a}\in\FU(\mathfrak{H})
, then
\mathbf{a}:=\sum_{h\in H}^{\mathfrak{H}}\delta^{h}a_{h}
.

9.
Continuous linear maps

Recall that
\mathfrak{G}
is a filter on a set
G
and
\mathfrak{H}
is a filter on
a set
H
. In what follows we will always assume that
\mathfrak{G}
and

\mathfrak{H}
contain Frechet filters. Thus
\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,G)\subseteq\FU(\mathfrak{G})
and

\FU(\mathrm{Cof}\,H)\subseteq\FU(\mathfrak{H})
. In this section we describe continuous
linear maps of topological linear spaces
\FU(\mathfrak{G})\to\FU(\mathfrak{H})
.

We will consider
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
as a left or right
K
-vector space. To
specify the side, we will use
K\{\mathfrak{G}\}
to denote this space
considered as a left vector space over
K
, and call it a

space of
\mathfrak{G}
-zero rows
. Similarly
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
will denote

\FU(\mathfrak{G})
considered as a right vector space over
K
, and will be
called a
space of
\mathfrak{G}
-zero columns
. We use
K[G]
to denote

K\{\mathrm{Cof}\,G\}
, and
[G]K
to denote
\{\mathrm{Cof}\,G\}K
. If
G
is a
group, then
K[G]
with the operations defined in
Section
8
is isomorphic to the usual group ring. In
what follows we will always assume that
G
and
H
are endowed with
an involution.

Recall that we agreed to denote rows with small Greek letters
\alpha,\beta\dots
, and columns with small boldfaced letters
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\dots
. Suppose that
\varphi
is a map from
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
to
\Map(H,K)
.
First we consider
\FU(\mathfrak{G})
as a right vector space
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
and

\Map(H,K)
as the space of columns
{}^{H}K
. The image of a column

\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
will be denoted by
\varphi[\mathbf{a}]
. Then we can
assign to
\varphi
an
H\times G
matrix
\Phi
, whose
(h,g^{*})
-entry
is equal to
\varphi[\delta^{g}]_{h}:

\Phi^{g^{*}}_{h}=\varphi[\delta^{g}]_{h}\,.

(Recall that
\delta^{g}
is a column whose
g
th coordinate is
1
and
all the remaining coordinates are zero, and
\varphi[\delta^{g}]_{h}
is the

h
th coordinate of the column
\varphi[\delta^{g}]
). We say that
\Phi

is a
matrix of
\varphi
. For instance,

\Phi^{g^{*}}=\varphi[\delta^{g}]
is the
g^{*}
th column of
\Phi
.

The
zero set of
\Phi
,
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
, is a collection of all

(h,g)\in H\times G
such that
\Phi^{g}_{h}=0
. This is in accordance
with our previous definition of the zero set of a map.

Now we restrict ourselves to the case, when
\varphi
is a map from

\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
to
\{\mathfrak{H}\}K\subseteq\Map(H,K)
.

Definition 9.1
.

A right (left) linear map
\varphi:\FU(\mathfrak{G})\to\FU(\mathfrak{H})
is said to be

\mathfrak{G}
-linear
, if for any
\mathfrak{G}
-summable family
h_{j}
,
j\in J
, where
h_{j}\in\FU(\mathfrak{G})
, the family
\varphi[h_{j}]
,
j\in J
is

\mathfrak{H}
-summable and

\varphi\bigl[\sum\limits_{j\in J}{}^{\mathfrak{G}}{h}_{j}\bigl]=\sum\limits_{j\in J}{}^{\mathfrak{H}}\varphi[{h}_{j}].

Apparently this condition bears no connection with topology. But below
(see Theorem
9.6
) we will see that
\mathfrak{G}
-linearity is the same
as continuity. The following lemma shows that both
\mathfrak{G}
-linear or continuous
linear operators are uniquely determined by their matrices.

Lemma 9.2
.

Suppose that
\varphi:\{\mathfrak{G}\}K\to\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
is either
\mathfrak{G}
-linear or a
linear continuous map. If
\Phi
is a matrix of
\varphi
and
\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
,
then
\varphi[\mathbf{a}]=\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
.

Proof.

We will prove this lemma only when
\varphi
is continuous. The proof
in the case, when
\varphi
is
\mathfrak{G}
-linear, is similar.

As we have already noticed (see a remark after Lemma
8.1
),

\mathbf{a}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}{}^{\mathfrak{G}}\delta^{g}a_{g}
,
a_{g}\in K
, hence

\mathbf{a}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\delta^{g}a_{g}
by Theorem
7.2
.
Since
\varphi
is continuous and linear, we obtain

\varphi[\mathbf{a}]=\sum\limits_{g\in G}{}^{T(\mathfrak{H})}\varphi[\delta^{g}]a_{g}
. Invoking
Theorem
7.2
again, we get
\varphi[\mathbf{a}]=\sum\limits_{g\in G}{}^{\mathfrak{H}}\varphi[\delta^{g}]a_{g}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}{}^{\mathfrak{H}}\Phi^{g^{*}}a_{g}
. On the
other hand, by the definition of product of matrices,
(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})_{h}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}\Phi^{g^{*}}_{h}a_{g}
, therefore

\varphi[\mathbf{a}]=\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
, as desired.
∎

Our next objective is to decide when the left multiplication by an

H\times G
matrix
\Phi
defines a continuous linear map
\varphi

from
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
to
\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
. As a first approximation we
consider the following condition:

(2)

\bigcap_{s\in\overline{B}}\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{s})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\ \text{ for every }\ B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\bot}\,.

It guarantees that
\varphi
is a linear map.

Remark 9.3
.

Let
\Phi
be an
H\times G
-matrix satisfying (
2
). Then

a) The rows of
\Phi
belong to the space
K\{\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\}
.

b) the rule
\mathbf{a}\to\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
, where
\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
, defines a

K
-linear map
\varphi:\{\mathfrak{G}\}K\rightarrow{}^{H}K
.

Proof.

a) If
B=H\setminus\{h\}
, then
\overline{B}=\{h\}
. Since
B\in\mathfrak{H}
,
(
2
) yields
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{h})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
.

b) By what we have just proved,
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{h})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
for
every row
\Phi_{h}
of
\Phi
. Since
\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
and

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{h})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
, the product
\Phi_{h}\cdot\mathbf{a}
is
defined (see Proposition
5.3
) and belongs to
K
. Then

\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
is defined and belongs to
{}^{H}K
.
∎

We need to put one extra condition on
\Phi
to ensure that the
image of
\varphi
is contained in
\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
.

(3)

\bigcap_{t\in\overline{A}}\mathcal{Z}(\Phi^{t^{*}})\in\mathfrak{H}\ \text{ for every }\ A\in\mathfrak{G}.

The next proposition shows that (
2
) and (
3
)
together imply that
\varphi
is continuous.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
\Phi
be an
H\times G
-matrix satisfying (
2
) and
(
3
). Then the rule
\mathbf{a}\to\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
defines a
continuous linear map
\varphi:\{\mathfrak{G}\}K\to\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
.

Proof.

By Remark
9.3
,
\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}\in{}^{H}K
. We prove that

\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
. Indeed, from
\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
it
follows that
A=\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathfrak{G}
. Furthermore, (
3
) implies
that
B=\bigcap_{t\in\overline{A}}\mathcal{Z}(\Phi^{t^{*}})\in\mathfrak{H}
, therefore

B\times\overline{A^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
. To show that
\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
, take any
h\in B
. Then

(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})_{h}=\sum\limits_{t\in G}\Phi_{h}^{t^{*}}a_{t}=\sum\limits_{t\in A}\Phi_{h}^{t^{*}}\cdot 0+\sum\limits_{t\in\overline{A}}0\cdot a_{t}=0.

It follows that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})\supseteq B
, therefore

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})\in\mathfrak{H}
, as desired.

As we have already noticed (see Lemma
8.1
),

\Phi\cdot(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})=\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}+\Phi\cdot\mathbf{b}
for all columns

\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
. Furthermore, clearly
(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})\cdot k=\Phi\cdot(\mathbf{a}\cdot k)
for every
k\in K
. It follows easily that

\varphi
is linear.

To prove that
\varphi
is continuous, it suffices to check that
\varphi

is continuous at zero. This means that for every zero neighbourhood

U(B,\mathfrak{H})
,
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\bot}
in
\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
, there exists a zero
neighbourhood
U(A,\mathfrak{G})
,
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot}
in
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
such that

\Phi\cdot U(A,\mathfrak{G})\subseteq U(B,\mathfrak{H}).

From (
2
) we obtain
C=\bigcap_{s\in\overline{B}}\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{s})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}
. If
A=C^{*}
, then
A\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot}
, and we prove that
A

is as required. By the definition of
A
, we have
\overline{B}\times A^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
, that is,
\Phi_{s}^{g^{*}}=0
for any
s\in\overline{B}
and
g\in A
. Take any
\mathbf{a}\in U(A,\mathfrak{G})
, hence
a_{g}=0
for every

g\in\overline{A}
. Then for any
s\in\overline{B}
we obtain:

(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})_{s}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}\Phi^{g^{*}}_{s}a_{g}=\sum\limits_{g\in\overline{A}}\Phi^{g^{*}}_{s}\cdot 0+\sum\limits_{g\in A}0\cdot a_{g}=0.

It follows that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})\supseteq\overline{B}
, therefore

\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}\in U(B,\mathfrak{H})
.
∎

Below (see Theorem
9.6
) we will show the the converse is
also true: if
\varphi:\{\mathfrak{G}\}K\to\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
is a continuous linear map,
then its matrix
\Phi
satisfies both (
2
) and
(
3
). But first we connect these conditions with filters on
direct products.

Lemma 9.5
.

A matrix
\Phi\in{}^{H}K^{G}
satisfies (
2
) and (
3
)
iff
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle
.

Proof.

Let us rewrite a)
′
from Definition
6.6
replacing

\mathfrak{G}
by
\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}
and
X
by
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
. Then
\mathfrak{G}^{\bot}

should be replaced by
\mathfrak{G}^{*}
.

(4)

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}\ \bigcap\limits_{t\in\overline{A}}H_{t}\in\mathfrak{H}\ \text{ for every }\ A\in\mathfrak{G}^{*};\\
\ \bigcap\limits_{s\in\overline{B}}G_{s}\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\ \text{ for every }\ B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\bot}\,,\\
\end{cases}

where
H_{t}=\left\{h\in H\mid(h,t)\in\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\right\}
and

\ \bigcap\limits_{s\in\overline{B}}\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{s})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\ \text{ for

Applying the involution, we see that the first condition in

\Phi\in{}^{H}K^{G}
is an
H\times G
-matrix such that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}\rangle
. Then

(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.3
3) it follows that

\mathbf{a}=\lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\,\mathbf{a}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{a}_{i}
are columns with
finite support. If
i
is fixed, then
(\mathbf{a}_{i})_{g}\neq 0
for finitely
many
g\in G
. For any such
g
, there exists only finitely many
h

such that
\gamma^{h^{*}}\Phi^{g^{*}}_{h}\neq 0
. Thus there are only
finitely many pairs
(g,h)
such that
\gamma^{h^{*}}\Phi^{g^{*}}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{i})_{g}\neq 0
. This means that hypotheses of
Proposition
10.3
are satisfied.
Now we have

(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\lim_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\mathbf{a}_{i}\,.

Furthermore, by the assumption (and the dual variant of
Proposition
9.4
) the right multiplication by
\Phi
acts
as a linear continuous operator from
K\{\mathfrak{H}^{\perp*}\}
to
K\{\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}\}
. In
particular,
\gamma\cdot\Phi\in K\{\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}\}
. Then, by
Corollary
9.7
, the left multiplication by
\gamma\cdot\Phi
is a
linear continuous map from
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
to
K
. Thus

(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\lim_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\lim\limits_{i}{}^{\mathop{{\rm Disc}}\nolimits}(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}\,,

where
\mathop{{\rm Disc}}\nolimits
denotes the discrete topology on
K
. By
Proposition
10.5
we obtain

(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}=\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i})
, therefore

\lim\limits_{i}{}^{\mathop{{\rm Disc}}\nolimits}(\gamma\cdot\Phi)\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}=\lim\limits_{i}{}^{\mathop{{\rm Disc}}\nolimits}\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i})\,.

Since left multiplications by
\gamma
and
\Phi
are continuous operators
(see Theorem
9.6
), we conclude that

\lim\limits_{i}{}^{\mathop{{\rm Disc}}\nolimits}\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i})=\gamma\cdot\lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{H}^{\bot})}(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i})=\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\mathbf{a}_{i})\,.

Because
\mathbf{a}=\lim_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\mathbf{a}_{i}
, this is equal to
\gamma\cdot(\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a})
, as desired.
∎

Now we give a matrix description of the algebra of linear continuous operators of
the column space
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
.

Theorem 10.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a balanced filter on a set
G
with involution, and
let
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
be a set of all
G\times G
-matrixes
\Phi
such that

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\in\langle\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle
. Then

a)
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
is a ring with respect the above defined addition
and multiplication;

b) If
\Phi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
, then the map
\widehat{\Phi}:\mathbf{a}\to\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}

is a continuous endomorphism of the column space
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
.

c) The map
\Phi\to\hat{\Phi}
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
onto the
ring of all continuous endomorphisms of the space
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
.

Proof.

b) follows from the equivalence of a) and b) in Theorem

9.6
.

a), c) First we prove that
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
is closed with respect to
multiplication. Suppose that
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
. Since
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi),\mathcal{Z}(\Psi)\in\langle\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle
, by Remark
9.3
a) and its dual variant,
we obtain that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{g})\in\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}
and
\mathcal{Z}(\Psi^{h^{*}})\in\mathfrak{G}
for all

g,h\in G
. It follows that the product
\Phi\cdot\Psi
is defined.

Take any
g\in G
. By Proposition
10.6
we obtain

(\Phi_{g}\cdot\Psi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=\Phi_{g}\cdot(\Psi\cdot\mathbf{a})
.
Then by the definition of matrix multiplication we derive

(\Phi_{g}\cdot\Psi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=(\Phi\cdot\Psi)_{g}\cdot\mathbf{a}=((\Phi\cdot\Psi)\cdot\mathbf{a})_{g}\,,

and
\Phi_{g}\cdot(\Psi\cdot\mathbf{a})=(\Phi\cdot(\Psi\cdot\mathbf{a}))_{g}
, therefore
(\Phi\cdot\Psi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=\Phi\cdot(\Psi\cdot\mathbf{a})
. Because the left
multiplication by
\Phi
and
\Psi
is continuous, this equality
implies that the left multiplication by
\Phi\cdot\Psi
is
continuous. By Theorem
9.6
we conclude that
\Phi\cdot\Psi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
.

Furthermore, writing
(\Phi\cdot\Psi)\cdot\mathbf{a}=\Phi\cdot(\Psi\cdot\mathbf{a})
at the level of endomorphisms, we obtain
\widehat{\Phi\cdot\Psi}=\widehat{\Phi}\circ\widehat{\Psi}
, therefore the map
\Phi\to\widehat{\Phi}
preserves multiplication. Clearly this map preserves an
addition, hence it is a morphism of rings (associative or not).

By Theorem
9.6
, if
\varphi
is a continuous linear operator
of the space
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
, then there is a unique matrix
\Phi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
such that
\varphi[\mathbf{a}]=\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}
. It follows that the map
\Phi\to\widehat{\Phi}

is an isomorphism of rings, in particular
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
is an associative ring.
∎

Suppose that
\mathfrak{G}
,
\mathfrak{H}
are balanced filters on sets with involution
G
and
H
.
Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
be the space of all linear continuous maps
\{\mathfrak{G}\}K\to\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
.
By Theorem
9.6
we may identify
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
with the space of

H\times G
-matrices
\Phi
such that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)\in\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle
. Therefore
we may consider
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
as a topological space with the topology

T(\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle)
defined by the filter

\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle
on
H\times G
.

It is well known that for normed linear spaces, the convergency
of a net of continuous operators with respect to the operator norm
implies their strong convergency. The following proposition gives a version of this
result in our situation.

Proposition 10.8
.

Suppose that a net of
H\times G
-matrices
\Phi_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})
converges
to
\Phi
with respect to
T(\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle)
. Then for any column

\mathbf{a}\in\{\mathfrak{G}\}K
and for any row
\gamma\in K\{\mathfrak{H}^{\bot*}\}

\lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}=\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}\ \text{ and }\ \lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{H}^{\bot*})}\gamma\cdot\Phi_{i}=\gamma\cdot\Phi.

Proof.

Clearly we may assume that
\Phi=0
, hence
\Phi\cdot\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
. Thus for the
first equation we have to prove that
\lim\limits_{i}{}^{T(\mathfrak{G})}\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}=0
.

Recall that by Theorem
6.13
we have

\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle^{\bot}=\mathfrak{H}^{\bot}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{*}
.
Furthermore (see Section
3
) the basis of the filter

\mathfrak{H}^{\perp}\otimes\mathfrak{G}^{*}
is
given by the sets
\overline{\overline{B}_{1}\times\overline{A}_{1}}
, where
B_{1}\in\mathfrak{H}
and
A_{1}\in\mathfrak{G}^{*}
.

Let
U(B,\mathfrak{H})
,
B\in\mathfrak{H}^{\bot}
be a zero neighborhood of the space
\{\mathfrak{H}\}K
.
We have to find a zero neighborhood

U=U(\overline{\overline{B}_{1}\times\overline{A}_{1}},T(\langle\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G}^{\bot*}\rangle))
, of the space
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{G})

such that
\Phi_{i}\in U
implies
\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}\in U(B,\mathfrak{H})
.

Take
B_{1}=B
and
A_{1}=\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{a})^{*}
. If
\Phi_{i}\in U
then, by the definition
U
, we have

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{i})\supseteq\overline{B}_{1}\times\overline{A}_{1}=\overline{B}\times\overline{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{a})}^{*}.

If
h
is an arbitrary element of
\overline{B}
, then

(\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a})_{h}=\sum\limits_{g\in G}(\Phi_{i})_{h}^{g^{*}}\mathbf{a}_{g}=\sum\limits_{g\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{a})}}0\cdot\mathbf{a}_{g}+\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{a})}(\Phi_{i})_{h}^{g^{*}}\cdot 0=0.

It follows that
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a})\supseteq\overline{B}
, that is,

\Phi_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}\in U(B,\mathfrak{H})
, as desired.

The second equality can be verified similarly.
∎

By Lemma
10.2
,
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathfrak{G})
is a subring of
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
. The following remark
shows that this subring is dense.

Remark 10.9
.

\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathfrak{G})
is a dense subring of
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
with respect to topology
T(\langle\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{G}^{\perp*}\rangle)
.

Proof.

The set
K[H\times G]
of all matrices with finite support forms a subalgebra of the
algebra
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathfrak{G})
. By Theorem
3.3
, this algebra is dense in
\mathcal{M}(\mathfrak{G})
. It remains
to notice that
K[H\times G]\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathfrak{G})
, because every finite matrix is a linear
combination of matrices
\delta^{g}\cdot\delta_{h}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathfrak{G})
.
∎
