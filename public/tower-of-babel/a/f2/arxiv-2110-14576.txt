Kripke-Joyal forcing for type theory and uniform fibrations
Steve Awodey, Nicola Gambino, Sina Hazratpour

Abstract
. We introduce a new method for precisely relating algebraic structures in a presheaf category and judgements of its internal type theory. The method provides a systematic way to organise complex diagrammatic reasoning and generalises the well-known Kripke-Joyal forcing for logic. As an application, we prove several properties of algebraic weak factorisation systems considered in Homotopy Type Theory.

Introduction

In one of the seminal papers on topos theory
[
12
]
, the links between algebraic topology and mathematical logic are described
as being still embryonic. The situation has changed dramatically since then, thanks to the discovery of a
deep connection between Quillen’s homotopical algebra
[
41
]
and Martin-Löf type theory
[
36
]
,
which has given rise to Homotopy Type Theory
[
52
]
and Voevodsky’s Univalent Foundations programme
[
53
]
. At the core of this connection is the relationship between the orthogonality property of weak factorisation systems (wfs’s for short) and the axioms for identity types
[
7
,
20
]
.

Presheaf categories play a fundamental role in this context because of the abundance of wfs’s and Quillen model structures on them
[
16
]
. For applications in both
type theory
[
17
]
and homotopical algebra
[
42
]
, however, it is sometimes useful to work with refinements of wfs’s known as algebraic weak factorisation systems (awfs’s for short)
[
25
,
26
]
, where maps satisfying an orthogonality property are replaced by maps equipped with algebraic structure, providing diagonal fillers for lifting problems and satisfying suitable uniformity conditions. The theory of awfs’s is analogous to that of wfs’s and is supported by a myriad of examples
[
13
,
14
]
, including the notions of a uniform Kan fibration studied in Homotopy Type Theory
[
2
,
8
,
24
,
44
,
48
,
49
]
. Yet, working with awfs’s diagrammatically can be very complex, since the algebraic structure on a map is in general not unique, and thus needs to be carried around explicitly. Because of this, the construction of some
important objects (such as that of fibration structures on a given map) using only diagrams can be a daunting task.

An alternative approach is provided by what may be called the
internal type theory
of a presheaf category, which is a highly expressive extensional dependent type theory
[
35
,
40
,
45
]
, capable of handling complex categorical structure and amenable to computer-assisted formalisation of proofs using Coq
[
10
]
or Agda
[
37
]
.
This idea, suggested by Thierry Coquand
[
18
]
, has been successfully applied to Homotopy Type Theory in
[
38
]
, where part
of the theory of uniform fibrations was developed type-theoretically and implemented in Agda. As we will see here, this approach makes it feasible to write down, say, the object of fibration structures on a map and reason about it formally. At present, however, in order to link these two approaches one needs to unfold the interpretation of the internal type theory into the presheaf category
[
29
]
, which can be a difficult and laborious process due to the subtleties introduced by dependent types.

Our first main contribution is to introduce and develop a new tool to relate the category-theoretic and the type-theoretic methods for describing structures in presheaf categories: namely, we extend the
Kripke-Joyal forcing of the higher-order logic of a presheaf category
\tE
— a powerful, long-established, technique of categorical logic
[
34
,
39
]
— to a precisely stated internal type theory of
\tE
.
In particular, we show how Kripke-Joyal forcing for this type theory allows us to test the validity of type-theoretic judgements in
\tE
(
4.8
), unfold explicitly what forcing amounts to for various forms of type, such as dependent products (
4.16
), which are very important for our applications, and relate it precisely to the ordinary forcing of higher-order logic (
4.19
). This technique then also permits intricate type-theoretic constructions of bespoke
classifiers
which, via forcing, represent familiar and important fibrations of structures (as studied
in
[
47
,
50
]
), a possibility that promises many further applications.

The second main contribution of the paper is to the study of the awfs’s considered in Homotopy Type Theory, using a combination of the new Kripke-Joyal forcing for the internal type theory and the usual internal reasoning therein. We thereby reconcile the category-theoretic and type-theoretic descriptions of awfs’s considered in
[
2
,
11
,
24
,
44
,
48
,
49
]

and give simple proofs of several properties of cofibrations and uniform (trivial) fibrations. In particular, we use Kripke-Joyal forcing to provide a type-theoretic characterisation of cofibrations (
5.6
) and a new proof that the pointed endofunctor of cofibrant partial elements admits the structure of a monad
6.7
).

One of our key results (
7.5
) shows how the uniformity condition that is part of the definition of a uniform (trivial) fibration arises naturally from the forcing conditions for dependent products. We also use Kripke-Joyal forcing to provide a new, simpler, proof that the uniform fibration structures on a map are exactly the algebra structures for a pointed endofunctor (
7.7
). With these results in place, we then
give an account of the basic properties of uniform fibrations, combining category-theoretic and type-theoretic reasoning. In particular, we describe type-theoretically the object of uniform fibration structures on a map (
8.7
) and use it to construct a universal uniform fibration (
8.9
). We also describe the homotopical semantics of identity types as path types (
9.4
). To the best of our knowledge, all of these results are original and fill an awkward gap in the research in the area, by relating precisely the category-theoretic and the type-theoretic approaches to uniform fibrations.

We hope that these applications demonstrate the utility of Kripke-Joyal forcing for researchers in mathematical logic, category theory and algebraic topology. We should also mention that, while our applications are to the theory of awfs’s and Homotopy Type Theory, our Kripke-Joyal forcing for type theory is applicable to many other kinds of algebraic structures that may occur on presheaves, a topic that we leave for future research.

Propositions and types

Subobjects and small maps can be combined, but some care is required in working with their characteristic maps and classifying maps, respectively. For example, fix
\alpha\colon X\to U
and let
p\colon X.\alpha\to X
be the associated display map. Then consider

\sigma\colon X.\alpha\to\Omega
and let

be the associated subobject,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

By Corollary

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\Sigma_{\alpha}\{\sigma\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma_{\alpha}\{\sigma\}}

Now consider the image factorisation

and the characteristic map

of the subobject

, as in (

).
Composing with the inclusion
\{-\}\colon\Omega\rightarrowtail U
, we can compare the map
\{\exists_{\alpha}(\sigma)\}\colon X\to U

to the propositional truncation
\mathsf{tr}(\Sigma_{\alpha}\{\sigma\})\colon X\to U
,
which also classifies the image
\exists_{p}(S)\rightarrowtail X
(as a small map), by Proposition

.
Indeed, since
\mathsf{tr}=\{-\}\circ\mathsf{supp}
, and

is monic, it suffices to compare the maps

\exists_{\alpha}(\sigma)\colon X\to\Omega
and
\mathsf{supp}(\Sigma_{\alpha}\{\sigma\})\colon X\to\Omega

. Thus

\{\exists_{\alpha}(\sigma)\}=\mathsf{tr}(\Sigma_{\alpha}\{\sigma\})

to

.

Now consider the universal quantification, and compare

with

.
Again we have the subobject

of (

) with its characteristic map

. Since

, which by definition is

.
The characteristic map of this subobject is then
\forall_{\alpha}(\sigma)\colon X\to\Omega
.
Finally, composing again with the inclusion
\{-\}\colon\Omega\rightarrowtail U
we can compare the maps

\forall_{\alpha}(\sigma)=\mathsf{supp}\{\forall_{\alpha}(\sigma)\}=\mathsf{supp}(\Pi_{\alpha}\{\sigma\}).

Bearing this lesson in mind, we have the following relationship between the operations on propositions

The internal category of types

We have emphasized the analogy between the small map classifier

and the subobject classifier

There are, of course, also some important differences; in particular, the universal property of

Notation 3.5

(Weakening)
.

When we perform a substitution along a map of the form

for some

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma.\beta.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma.\beta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\displaystyle\beta$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\textstyle\beta$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\beta$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U\qquad\Gamma\vdash\beta\colon U

\displaystyle\Gamma.\alpha\vdash\beta({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})\colon U\mathrlap{.}

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U\qquad\Gamma\vdash b\colon\beta

\displaystyle\Gamma.\alpha\vdash b({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})\colon\beta({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})\mathrlap{.}

Remark 3.8
.

By

Dependent sum and dependent product types

The category-theoretic structure of
\tE
and the closure properties of the class
\tE
of small maps discussed in
Section

1
correspond to
well-known type-theoretic constructions. In particular, the type theory of
\tE
has empty type
\mathsf{0}
, a unit type
\mathsf{1}
, sum types
\alpha+\beta
,
dependent sum types
\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)
, dependent
product types
\Pi_{\alpha}(\beta)
, a type of propositions
\Omega
and subset types

\{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha\mid\sigma\}
.
With these product types
\alpha\times\beta
and function types
\alpha\rightarrow\beta
can be defined, as explained
in
3.12
below. It also has a type of natural numbers
\mathsf{N}
, as well as many other inductively defined types. We shall not list the rules for all these types, limiting ourselves to those that will be most relevant and illustrative, namely dependent sums, dependent products and propositions.

3.9
and
3.11
and their proofs, for which we refer to
[
3
]
, make use of the convention introduced in
3.4
. Even if we speak of ‘rules’ in their statements, it should be pointed out that these are claims about semantic objects, not generating rules of a dependent type theory.

Proposition 3.9

(Rules for dependent sum types)
.

The following rules are valid in
\tE
.

•

Formation: if
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\mathord{\,:\,}U
and
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash\beta\mathord{\,:\,}U
, then
\Gamma\vdash\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)\mathord{\,:\,}U
.

•

Introduction: if
\Gamma\vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha
and
\Gamma\vdash b\mathord{\,:\,}\beta(a)
then

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{pair}(a,b)\mathord{\,:\,}\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)
.

•

Elimination: if
\Gamma\vdash t\colon\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)
then

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{p}_{1}(t)\colon\alpha
and
\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{p}_{2}(t)\colon\beta(\mathsf{p}_{1}(t))
.

•

Computation: if
\Gamma\vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha
and
\Gamma\vdash b\mathord{\,:\,}\beta(a)

then
\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{p}_{1}(\mathsf{pair}(a,b))=a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha
and
\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{p}_{2}(\mathsf{pair}(a,b))=b\mathord{\,:\,}\beta(a)
.

•

Expansion: if
\Gamma\vdash t\colon\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)
then

\Gamma\vdash t=\mathsf{pair}(\mathsf{p}_{1}(t),\mathsf{p}_{2}(t))\colon\Sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)
. ∎

Notation 3.10
.

In the statement of the next proposition and below we make use not only of the convention introduced in
3.4
, but also write

\lambda(b)
instead of
\lambda((\id_{\Gamma},b^{\sharp}))
for simplicity.

Proposition 3.11

(Rules for dependent product types)
.

The following rules are valid in
\tE
.

•

Formation: if
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\mathord{\,:\,}U
and
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash\beta\mathord{\,:\,}U

then
\Gamma\vdash\Pi_{\alpha}\beta\mathord{\,:\,}U
.

•

Introduction: if
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash b\colon\beta
then
\Gamma\vdash\lambda(b)\colon\Pi_{\alpha}(\beta)
.

•

Elimination: if
\Gamma\vdash t\mathord{\,:\,}\Pi_{\alpha}(\beta)
and
\Gamma\vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{app}(t,a)\colon\beta(a)
.

•

Computation: if
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash b\colon\beta
and
\Gamma\vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha
then
\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{app}\big(\lambda(b),a\big)=b(a)\colon\beta(a)
.

•

Expansion: if
\Gamma\vdash t\mathord{\,:\,}\Pi_{\alpha}(\beta)
then
\Gamma\vdash\lambda(\mathsf{app}(t(p_{\alpha}),q_{\alpha})=t\colon\Pi_{\alpha}(\beta)
. ∎

Notation 3.12

(Product and function types)
.

The rules for dependent sum and dependent product types of
3.9
and
3.11
, in combination with the weakening operation of
3.5
, allow us to define product and function types and derive
deduction rules for them. For
\alpha\colon\Gamma\to U
and
\beta\colon\Gamma\to U
, we define the product type
\alpha\times\beta\colon\Gamma\to U
and the function type
\alpha\to\beta\colon\Gamma\to U
by letting

\alpha\times\beta=_{\mathrm{def}}\Sigma_{\alpha}\beta({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})\mathrlap{,}\qquad\alpha\to\beta=_{\mathrm{def}}\Pi_{\alpha}\beta({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})\mathrlap{,}

where we used the notation for weakening introduced in (
3.5
). We can then derive the deduction rules

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U\quad\Gamma\vdash\beta\colon U

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash\alpha\times\beta\colon U\mathrlap{,}

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash a\colon\alpha\quad\Gamma\vdash b\colon\beta

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{pair}(a,b)\colon\alpha\times\beta

for product types, and the rules

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U\quad\Gamma\vdash\beta\colon U

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash\alpha\rightarrow\beta\colon U\mathrlap{,}

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma.\alpha\vdash b\colon\beta({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash\lambda(b)\colon\alpha\rightarrow\beta

for function types, where we made use of the simplification introduced in
3.10
.

Notation 3.13

(Constant functions)
.

In order to be able to manipulate constant functions conveniently, we introduce a further abuse of notation on top of the
one discussed in
3.10
. If
\Gamma\vdash b\colon\beta
, we write
\Gamma\vdash\lambda(b)\colon\alpha\to\beta
instead of

\Gamma\vdash\lambda(b({p}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}))\colon\alpha\to\beta
, thereby omitting mention of the weakening in this particular case. This convention will be used frequently in
Section

5
.

Remark 3.14
.

The rules for dependent sum and dependent product types stated in
3.9
and
3.11
imply also many familiar properties of these types. In particular, if
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U
and
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash\beta\colon U
and
\Gamma.\alpha.\beta\vdash\rho\colon U
, we can prove that there are isomorphisms of types,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{x\colon\alpha}\,\sum_{y\mathord{\,:\,}\beta}\,\rho

\displaystyle\textstyle\ \cong\ \sum_{z\colon\sum_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\beta}\,\rho(\mathsf{p}_{1}(z),\mathsf{p}_{2}(z))\mathrlap{,}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\textstyle\prod_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\sum_{y\mathord{\,:\,}\beta}\,\rho

\displaystyle\textstyle\ \cong\ \sum_{u\mathord{\,:\,}\prod_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\beta}\,\prod_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\rho(x,\mathsf{app}(u,x))\mathrlap{.}

Also, if
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\colon U
and
\Gamma.\alpha\vdash\beta\colon U
, then there is an isomorphism,

(3.6)

\textstyle\big(\sum_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\beta\big)\mathbin{\shortrightarrow}\rho\ \cong\ \prod_{x\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha}\,\big(\beta\mathbin{\shortrightarrow}\rho\big)\mathrlap{.}

The soundness of the rules in
\tE
,
3.9

and
3.11
, then implies that there are corresponding isomorphisms in the category of types over
\Gamma
,

\tS/_{\Gamma}\ \simeq\ \underline{\tE}(\Gamma,\mathbb{U}),

introduced in (
2.8
) of section
2
.
In particular, it follows that the internal category of types
\mathbb{U}
is locally cartesian closed, much as the subobject classifier
\Omega
is an internal Heyting algebra.

The type of propositions

We conclude this section by fixing some notation for the closed type associated to the subobject classifier of
\tE
, which we shall also write
\Omega
. The elements of this type, to be regarded as propositions, will be written using the standard logical notation,

\mathsf{true}\mathrlap{,}\quad\mathsf{false}\mathrlap{,}\quad a=_{\alpha}b\mathrlap{,}\quad\sigma\land\tau\mathrlap{,}\quad\sigma\lor\tau\mathrlap{,}\quad\sigma\Rightarrow\tau\mathrlap{,}\quad(\forall x\colon\alpha)\tau(x)\mathrlap{,}\quad(\exists x\colon\alpha)\tau(x)\mathrlap{.}

Note that the proposition
a=_{\alpha}b
is a well-formed element of type
\Omega
in context
\Gamma
when

a
and
b
are well-formed elements of type
\alpha
in context
\Gamma
. We refer to this as
propositional equality
; it is to be distinguished from judgemental equality
a=b\colon\alpha
, relating two elements of type
\alpha
(and from
p=q\colon\Omega
, relating two elements of type
\Omega
). In spite of this, we will sometimes drop the subscript from propositional equality, when this does not create confusion (see also
3.16
below).

Definition 3.15
.

We say that a proposition
\sigma
in context
\Gamma
is
valid
if

\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\Omega
factors through
\mathsf{true}\colon 1\to\Omega
. In that case, we write
\Gamma\vdash\sigma
.
Similarly, we say that
\Gamma,\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}

implies

\varphi
,
if the subobject
\{\Gamma\mid\varphi_{1}\land\ldots\land\varphi_{n}\}
factors through
\{\Gamma\mid\varphi\}
.
In this case, we write
\Gamma,\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\vdash\varphi
.

Note that
\sigma
is valid exactly when the associated subobject
\{\Gamma\mid\sigma\}\rightarrowtail\Gamma

admits a section, and hence is isomorphic to
\Gamma
.

Remark 3.16
.

Propositional equality
a=_{\alpha}b\colon\Omega
serves the purpose of the
equality types

\mathsf{Eq}_{\alpha}(a,b)
sometimes used in a system of type theory without a type of propositions. Here judgemental and propositional equality are equivalent, in the sense that the inference rules

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash a_{1}\colon\alpha\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{2}\colon\alpha\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{1}=_{\alpha}a_{2}

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash a_{1}=a_{2}\colon\alpha

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\vdash a_{1}\colon\alpha\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{2}\colon\alpha\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{1}=a_{2}\colon\alpha

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash a_{1}=_{\alpha}a_{2}

are also valid. This is sometimes expressed by saying that the type theory of
\tE
is
extensional
,
in contrast with formulations of Martin-Löf type theories that are
intensional
.

4.
Kripke-Joyal forcing

Definition and basic properties

The aim of this section is to give the definition of the Kripke-Joyal semantics for the type theory
\mathcal{T}_{\tE}
of the category
\tE
of presheaves, introduced in
Section

3
, and establish some of its key properties. In particular,
we unfold the general definition for each of the main type forming operations, so as
to facilitate the work of subsequent sections.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\colon X\to U
and
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
.

(i)

For
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
, we say that

c

forces

a\colon\alpha(x)
, written

c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)\,,

if the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

(ii)

For
a,b\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
and
c\Vdash b\colon\alpha(x)
, we say that

c

forces

a=b\colon\alpha(x)
, written

c\Vdash a=b\colon\alpha(x)\,,

if
a
and
b
are equal maps in
\tE
.

Remark 4.2
.

We provide some equivalent ways of regarding the forcing condition.

(i)

A map
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
is the same thing as an element of
\alpha(x)
in context
\mathsf{y}{c}
. Indeed, we can rewrite the diagram in
4.1
as

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

Consulting (
3.1
), we see that
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
is therefore simply another notation for
\mathsf{y}{c}\vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
. Even if it is just an abbreviation, the forcing notation provides an alternate tool for working with the type theory of
\tE
,
when unfolded for the various type formers, as we shall see later.

(ii)

A map
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
is the same thing
as a dotted map making the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

As we will see in
4.19
, our definition subsumes the Kripke-Joyal forcing for propositions, which
defines

to mean that there is a (now necessarily unique) map in

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{X\mid\sigma\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}

One of the key differences between forcing for types and forcing for propositions is that in
the forcing condition for types we need to record the map

, as it is not unique in
general.

(iii)

A map
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that

also corresponds uniquely to a section of the display map of
{\alpha(x)}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to U
,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}.\alpha(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

The following statements gather some immediate consequences of
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\colon X\to U
. Then
\alpha=\beta
if and only if
c\Vdash\alpha(x)=\beta(x)
for every
c\in\cC
and
x\colon\mathsf{y}c\to X
. ∎

For the next lemma, recall that the
monotonicity
of conventional Kripke semantics states that if
k\Vdash p
and
j>k
then
j\Vdash p
. Something similar holds in our setting as well.

Lemma 4.4

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
\alpha\colon X\to U
,

x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
and
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
. If
c\Vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(x)
then
d\Vdash a(f)\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xf)

for every
f\colon d\to c
.

Just precompose the diagram
4.1
with
f\colon d\to c
.
∎

A
converse to monotonicity
might be stated as follows. Suppose that for every
f\colon d\to c
, we have
a_{f}\colon\mathsf{y}{d}\to E
with
d\Vdash a_{f}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xf)
, and moreover the uniformity condition
e\Vdash a_{f}(g)=a_{fg}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xfg)
holds for all
g\colon e\to d
.
Then there is
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
with
c\Vdash a\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(x)
, and moreover
d\Vdash a(f)=a_{f}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xf)

for all
f\colon d\to c
. By the Yoneda lemma, this is trivial, however, because for
a
we can just take
a_{\id_{c}}
.
What is not so trivial is the analogous statement for a judgement of the form
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
when
X
is not representable. Since the presheaf
X
is a colimit of representables, the validity of such judgements in the internal type theory
\mathcal{T}_{\tE}
can be related to forcing semantics as follows.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
X\vdash\alpha\colon U
. Suppose that for each
{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
there is a given element
a_{x}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that

(4.1)

c\Vdash a_{x}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(x),

and moreover, the following uniformity condition holds for all
f\colon d\to c
,

d\Vdash a_{x}(f)=a_{xf}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xf)\,.

Then there is a unique element
a\colon X\to E
such that

(4.2)

X\vdash a\colon\alpha

and, for all
{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
,

c\Vdash a(x)=a_{x}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(x)\,.

Given any
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
we can set
a_{x}=_{\mathrm{def}}ax
for each
{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
, and we then have
c\Vdash a_{x}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(x)
by precomposing (
3.1
) with
{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
. The uniformity condition
d\Vdash a_{x}(f)=a_{xf}\mathord{\,:\,}\alpha(xf)
holds because
a_{x}(f)=(ax)f=a(xf)=a_{xf}
.

To see that any uniform family of elements
(a_{x}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E)_{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
arises in this way from a unique
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
, note that, for each
c
, the family
(a_{x}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E)_{x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X}
determines a function
\mathbf{a}_{c}\colon X(c)\to E(c)
, while the uniformity condition means that the various functions
\mathbf{a}_{c}
are natural in
c
, so we indeed have a natural transformation
a\colon X\to E
. Recall that
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
means that this
a\colon X\to E
makes the diagram (
3.1
) commute. This follows from the individual conditions (
4.1
).
∎

We can summarise
4.5
as follows.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\alpha\colon X\to U
. Then the following data are in bijective correspondence:

(i)

elements
a\colon X\to E
such that
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
,

(ii)

uniform families of elements
a_{x}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that
c\Vdash a_{x}\colon\alpha(x)
.

Specifically, the bijection is defined by letting
a_{x}=_{\mathrm{def}}a(x)\colon\alpha(x)
. ∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\alpha\colon 1\to U
be a closed type.
Then the following data are in bijective correspondence:

(i)

elements

Theorem 4.8

(Soundness and completeness of forcing)
.

Let

be a closed type and

. Then
\mathcal{T}_{\tE}\vdash a\colon\alpha

\tE\Vdash a\colon\alpha
. ∎

Forcing for types

We now unfold the definition of forcing with respect to each of the main type-forming operations of
\mathcal{T}_{\tE}
. We make use thereby of the term constructors
\mathsf{pair}
,
\mathsf{app}
, etc., of
\mathcal{T}_{\tE}
that were introduced in Section

.

Proposition 4.12

(Forcing for empty and unit type)
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

(i)

c\not\Vdash a\colon\mathsf{0}
for all
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
.

(ii)

c\Vdash a\colon\mathsf{1}
for a unique
a=*\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
.

For
Item

(i)
, if
c\Vdash a\colon\mathsf{0}
, then by
Item

(iii)
of
4.2
we would have a section of the display map

\mathsf{y}{c}.\mathsf{0}\to\mathsf{y}{c}
, but
\mathsf{y}{c}.\mathsf{0}=\{\mathsf{y}{c}\ |\ \bot\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}{c}
is the initial object of
\tE
, so this is impossible, since
\mathsf{y}{c}
always has at least the element
\id\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to\mathsf{y}{c}
.

For
Item

(ii)
,
c\Vdash a\colon\mathsf{1}
for every section
a
of the display map
\mathsf{y}{c}.\mathsf{1}\to\mathsf{y}{c}
, but

\mathsf{y}{c}.\mathsf{1}=\{\mathsf{y}{c}\ |\ \top\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}{c}
is the total subobject, so there is always exactly one such section.
∎

Proposition 4.13

(Forcing for sum types)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\mathord{\,:\,}X\to U
. For every
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
, the following conditions hold.

(i)

If
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
then
c\Vdash\mathsf{inl}(a)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
,
and if
c\Vdash b\colon\beta(x)
then
c\Vdash\mathsf{inr}(b)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
.

(ii)

If
c\Vdash t\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)

then either
c\Vdash t=\mathsf{inl}(a)\colon\alpha(x)\mathsf{+}\beta(x)
for
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)

or
c\Vdash t=\mathsf{inr}(b)\colon\alpha(x)\mathsf{+}\beta(x)
for
c\Vdash b\colon\beta(x)
.

The sum
\alpha+\beta\colon X\to U

classifies the canonical map from the coproduct

[p_{\alpha},p_{\beta}]\colon X.\alpha+X.\beta\to X.

Now if
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
then we have a lift
a\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X.\alpha
of
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
across the display map
p_{\alpha}\colon X.\alpha\to X
. Composing with the coproduct inclusion
\mathsf{inl}\colon X.\alpha\to X.\alpha+X.\beta
we obtain
c\Vdash\mathsf{inl}(a)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
. If instead
c\Vdash b\colon\beta(x)
then in the same way we obtain
c\Vdash\mathsf{inr}(b)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
. Thus
(i)
.

For
(ii)
, if

c\Vdash t\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
, then there is a lift

t\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X.\alpha+X.\beta

of
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
across
[p_{\alpha},p_{\beta}]
.
Since the representable
\mathsf{y}{c}
is indecomposable, the map
t
must factor through one of the coproduct inclusions. Thus we either have
c\Vdash t=\mathsf{inl}(a)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
where
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
or we have
c\Vdash t=\mathsf{inr}(b)\colon\alpha(x)+\beta(x)
where
c\Vdash b\colon\beta(x)
, as required.
∎

Proposition 4.14

(Forcing for dependent sum types)
.

Let
\alpha\colon X\to U
and
\beta\colon X.\alpha\to U
. For every

and

, then

.

(ii)

, then

and

.

The statements (i) and (ii) follow from
3.9
(introduction and elimination) and

(i)
of
4.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.15

(Forcing for product types)
.

For
\alpha,\beta\colon X\to U
, the following hold.

(i)

and

Notation 4.20
.

In light of
4.19
, we shall often write
c\Vdash\sigma(x)
to mean
that there exists an
s
such that
c\Vdash s\colon\{\sigma(x)\}
.

By
4.19
, we obtain forcing conditions for all types

\sigma\colon\Gamma\to U
that factor through the monomorphism
\{-\}\colon\Omega\to U

of (
2.1
).
For such types, the uniformity conditions required for function types
and
\Pi
-types become vacuous and indeed give back the standard Kripke-Joyal conditions.
We illustrate this in a few cases.

Proposition 4.21

(Forcing for propositional equality)
.

Let
\alpha\colon X\to U
and suppose that
X\vdash a\colon\alpha
and
X\vdash b\colon\alpha
.
Then for any
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

c\Vdash a(x)=b(x)
.

(ii)

a(x)
and
b(x)
are equal as maps
\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
.

For each
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
, the element
a\colon\alpha
determines a map
(x,a(x))
as follows (and similarly for
b\colon\alpha
).

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(x,a(x))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

Now consider the following diagram, in which both squares are pullbacks.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha\times\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\mathrlap{.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\delta_{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(x,a(x),b(x))}

c\Vdash a(x)=b(x)
if and only if there is a dotted map as indicated. But this holds if and only if

(x,a(x))=(x,b(x))
, which in turn is equivalent to
a(x)=b(x)
as maps
\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
.
∎

For disjunction, even though
\{\sigma\lor\tau\}
is not the same as
\{\sigma\}+\{\tau\}
, we still
have the following equivalence due to special properties of presheaf categories.

Proposition 4.22

(Forcing for disjunction)
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\mathord{\,:\,}X\to\Omega
. For
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to X
,
the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

c\Vdash\sigma(x)\lor\tau(x)
.

(ii)

c\Vdash\sigma(x)
or
c\Vdash\tau(x)
.

As stated in
2.5
, the disjunction
\sigma\lor\tau\colon X\to\Omega

classifies the subobject
\{\sigma\}\cup\{\tau\}\rightarrowtail X
, which is the image of the canonical map
\{\sigma\}+\{\tau\}\to X
. So if

c\Vdash\sigma(x)\lor\tau(x)
,
then there is a map

v\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to\{\sigma\}\cup\{\tau\}

lifting
x
.
Since the representable
\mathsf{y}{c}
is projective, there is then a further lift
u\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to\{\sigma\}+\{\tau\}
, and so
c\Vdash u\colon\{\sigma(x)\}+\{\tau(x)\}
.
Applying
4.13
we obtain either

c\Vdash a\colon\{\sigma(x)\}
or
c\Vdash b\colon\{\tau(x)\}
, whence either
c\Vdash\sigma(x)
or
c\Vdash\tau(x)
.
The converse is even more direct, using the factorisations

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\sigma\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\sigma\}\cup\{\tau\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\tau\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\mathrlap{.}}

∎

(Forcing for subset types)
.

Let

and

Proposition 4.25

(Forcing for propositional truncation)
.

. For

, the following conditions
are equivalent.

(i)

c\Vdash\mathsf{supp}(\alpha)(x)
.

(ii)

There exists
a
such that
c\Vdash a\colon\alpha(x)
.

Recall from
2.4
that the propositional truncation
\mathsf{supp}(\alpha)\colon X\to\Omega
is the pullback along
\alpha
of the image factorisation of
\pi\colon E\to U
,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.\{\mathsf{supp}(\alpha)\}}

c\Vdash\mathsf{supp}(\alpha)(x)
if and only if there is a factorisation of

through the subobject

.

\mathsf{y}{c}
is projective, this is the case if and only if there is a further lift

.

Remark 4.26
.

Our Kripke-Joyal forcing semantics can be specialised to the system of dependent type theory without a type
\Omega
of propositions, but with the type formers

(4.4)

\quad 1\mathrlap{,}\quad\alpha\times\beta\mathrlap{,}\quad\alpha\to\beta\mathrlap{,}\quad\Sigma_{x\colon\alpha}\beta(x)\mathrlap{,}\quad\Pi_{x\colon\alpha}\beta(x)\mathrlap{,}\quad a=_{\alpha}b\mathrlap{,}

by treating propositional equality
a=_{\alpha}b
equivalently as an (extensional) equality type
\mathsf{Eq}_{\alpha}(a,b)
, as mentioned in
3.16
. This can then be seen as a proof-relevant version of the standard Kripke semantics for intuitionistic first-order logic
[
51
]
. The standard deduction relation
\vdash_{\mathsf{IFOL}}
for that system can be shown to be
sound and complete
with respect to standard Kripke semantics, in the sense that for any closed formula
\sigma
, one has

\vdash_{\mathsf{IFOL}}\sigma
if and only if
K\Vdash\sigma

for all Kripke frames
K
, which are essentially interpretations of the signature of
\sigma
into categories of the form
\mathsf{Set}^{K}
for arbitrary posets
K
.

Similarly, one can infer from the results in
[
6
]
that the system of type theory specified by (
4.4
), with the customary inference rules, as stated e.g. in
ibid.
, is deductively complete with respect to our Kripke-Joyal forcing semantics, in the following sense. Given a closed type
\alpha
(over a basic signature of closed and dependent types and terms), one can construct a closed term
\vdash a\colon\alpha
if and only if, for every interpretation of the signature of
\alpha
into a category of the form
\mathsf{Set}^{P}
for an arbitrary
poset

P
, one has
\mathsf{Set}^{P}\Vdash a\colon\alpha

in the sense of
4.7
. Briefly, extensional dependent type theory is complete with respect to Kripke-Joyal forcing semantics, something that is not true for the system of higher-order logic, with a type of propositions. We leave to the reader the pleasant task of simplifying the forcing conditions given in this section for the special case where the index category
\cC
is a poset.

Basic definitions

This section starts the second part of the paper, in which we use our forcing semantics to describe models of homotopy type theory. In order
to do so, we assume certain auxiliary structures on our fixed presheaf category, as in
[
17
]
, which give rise to (algebraic) weak factorisation systems
[
7
,
9
,
20
]
. As we shall see, forcing allows us to relate precisely category-theoretic concepts, as defined in
[
17
,
23
,
24
]
, with their type-theoretic counterparts, as defined in
[
38
]
. Below, we shall take the categorical point of view as our starting point. In
5.1
, we write
\tE^{\to}_{\mathrm{cart}}
for the category of arrows and pullback
squares in
\tE
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
class of cofibrations in
\tE=\mathrm{Psh}(\cC)
is a class of maps
\mathsf{Cof}
that satisfies the following conditions:

(C1)

The elements of
\mathsf{Cof}
are monomorphisms.

(C2)

The unique map
0\to 1
is in
\mathsf{Cof}
.

(C3)

The identity
\id_{1}\colon 1\to 1
is in
\mathsf{Cof}
.

(C4)

Cofibrations are stable under pullback along all maps.

(C5)

Cofibrations are closed under composition.

(C6)

The full subcategory of
\tE^{\to}_{\mathrm{cart}}
spanned by
\mathsf{Cof}
has a terminal object.

Let us now assume that
\mathsf{Cof}
is a class of cofibrations in
\tE
. We say that an object
X\in\tE
is
cofibrant
if the unique map
!_{X}\colon 0\to X
is a cofibration.

Remark 5.2
.

We note without proof a few elementary consequences of these axioms.

(i)

In light of
(C4)
, axioms
(C2)
and
(C3)
imply that, for every
X\in\tE
,
the unique map
!_{X}\colon 0\to X

and the identity
\id_{X}\colon X\to X
are cofibrations . Thus, every object is cofibrant. See
[
21
,
27
]
for a context where this is not the case.

(ii)

The terminal cofibration posited in
(C6)
will be denoted
\mathsf{t}\colon 1\rightarrowtail\Phi
, note that its domain can be shown to be
1
. In the pullback square

(5.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega\mathrlap{,}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{t}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{true}}

the lower horizontal characteristic map can be shown to be monic.
The map
\mathsf{t}\colon 1\rightarrowtail\Phi
classifies cofibrations, in the sense that a monomorphism

is in

if and only if its characteristic map

factors through

, and thus if and only if it is a pullback of

along a (necessarily unique) classifying map.

(iii)

The full subcategory of

(iv)

The cofibrations with representable codomain

are called

of generating cofibrations in a canonical way (determined by the category of elements of its codomain). As shown in

using Garner’s small object argument

]
, the subcategory of generating cofibrations determines an algebraic weak factorisation system

, with right class the
uniform trivial fibrations
to be considered in

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
\varphi\colon X\to\Omega
.

(i)

The map
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail\{\cof\varphi\}
is a cofibration.

(ii)

\cof\varphi=_{\Omega}\mathsf{true}
if and only if
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail X
is a cofibration
if and only if
\{\cof\varphi\}\rightarrowtail X
is an isomorphism. ∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose

and
\psi\colon X\to\Omega
, and consider the pullback squares

The following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

is a cofibration.

(ii)

.

Assume first that

is a cofibration.
Let

and

. We want to show
c\Vdash\varphi(x)\Rightarrow\cof\psi(x)
,
so let

and assume that

, i.e. that we have a factorisation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{\varphi\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}d}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{y}c}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

and we claim that

. That holds if and only if

factors through

or, equivalently, just if

is a cofibration. But
\{\psi(xf)\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}d
is also the pullback of

along the dotted arrow above. The claim follows by the closure of cofibrations under pullback, Axiom

Conversely, as we just saw,

Proposition 5.8
.

Assuming only axioms (C1–4) and (C6), axiom (C5) can be expressed equivalently as follows.

(i)

Cofibrations are closed under composition.

(ii)

\tE\Vdash(\forall\varphi,\psi\colon\Omega)\cof\varphi\Rightarrow\big((\varphi\Rightarrow\cof\psi)\Rightarrow\cof(\varphi\land\psi)\big)
.

Suppose that
Item

(i)
holds. We need to show that for every
c\in\cC
, we have
c\Vdash(\forall\varphi,\psi\colon\Omega)\cof\varphi\Rightarrow\big((\varphi\Rightarrow\cof\psi)\Rightarrow\cof(\varphi\land\psi)\big)
.
Thus take any
x:d\to c
and
\varphi,\psi:\mathsf{y}{d}\to\Omega
and any
y\colon e\to d
such that
e\Vdash\cof\varphi(xy)
, and let furthermore
z\colon f\to e
with
f\Vdash\varphi(xyz)\Rightarrow\cof(\psi(xyz))
.
Then
\{\varphi(xy)\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}e
is a cofibration and therefore so is
\{\varphi(xyz)\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}f
and, by
Lemma

5.7
,

\{\varphi(xyz)\land\psi(xyz)\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi(xyz)\}
is a cofibration as well.
By the assumption, the composite
\{\varphi(xyz)\land\psi(xyz)\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}f
is then a cofibration, and hence

f\Vdash\cof(\varphi(xyz)\land\psi(xyz))
, as required.

To see that
(ii)
implies
(i)
, note first that arbitrary cofibrations compose just if the same is true of cofibrations of the form
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
and
\{\varphi\land\psi\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi\}
, where
\varphi,\psi\colon\mathsf{y}c\to\Omega
. (Indeed, given arbitrary cofibrations
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail X
and
\{\vartheta\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi\}
, the composite is a cofibration just if its pullback along every
\mathsf{y}c\to X
is one; then take
\{\psi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
to be the composite
\{\vartheta\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
.)
So let
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
and
\{\varphi\land\psi\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi\}
be cofibrations, and assume
Item

(ii)
, so that for all
d\to c
and all
\varphi,\psi:d\to\Omega
, we have

d\Vdash\cof\varphi\Rightarrow((\varphi\Rightarrow\cof\psi)\Rightarrow\cof(\varphi\land\psi))\mathrlap{.}

Thus, in particular, if
c\Vdash\cof\varphi
and
c\Vdash\varphi\Rightarrow\cof\psi
then

c\Vdash\cof(\varphi\land\psi)
. But
the first premise holds since
\{\varphi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
is assumed to be a cofibration, and the second premise holds by
Lemma

5.7
, since
\{\varphi\land\psi\}\rightarrowtail\{\varphi\}
is also assumed to be a cofibration. Thus we have
c\Vdash\cof(\varphi\land\psi)
, and so
\{\varphi\land\psi\}\rightarrowtail\mathsf{y}c
is a cofibration, as required.
∎

8.

The definition of a uniform fibration was originally introduced in the setting of categories of cubical sets in

The fundamental insight of Homotopy Type Theory

\displaystyle x\colon A,\,y\colon A,\,z\colon A^{\mathrm{I}}(x,y)\,\vdash\,\mathsf{j}_{b}(x,y,z)\colon B(x,y,z)\mathrlap{.}

The conclusion of elimination asserts the existence of the indicated diagonal arrow
j
making the
lower
triangle commute, while the computation rule states that the
upper
one does.
To build such a map
j
it suffices to show that for any
c
and
x\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to 1.\alpha.\alpha.\alpha^{\mathrm{I}}
, there is an element
j_{x}\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to E
such that

c\Vdash j_{x}\colon\beta(x)\,,

uniformly in
c
. Since
x
can be rewritten as
(a,a^{\prime},u)\colon\mathsf{y}{c}\to 1.\alpha.\alpha.\alpha^{\mathrm{I}}
, we have
c\Vdash u\colon\alpha^{\mathrm{I}}(a,a^{\prime})
. By
Lemma

9.3
we then obtain
c\Vdash\varepsilon_{u}\colon(\alpha(x)^{\mathrm{I}})^{\mathrm{I}}(\con{(a)},u)
. Substituting
a
in the second premise of the elimination rule yields
c\Vdash b(a)\colon\beta(a,a,\con(a))
. Transporting the latter along
\varepsilon_{u}
by
Lemma

9.1
then provides
c\Vdash\varepsilon_{u}*b(a)\colon\beta(a,a^{\prime},u)
. Now set

j_{(a,a^{\prime},u)}=_{\mathrm{def}}\ \varepsilon_{u}*b(a)\,.

Uniformity in
c
follows from the same for each of the terms in the construction of
j_{(a,a^{\prime},u)}
. Indeed, given
f\colon d\to c
, we have

(9.6)

d\Vdash j_{(a,a^{\prime},u)}(f)=(\varepsilon_{u}*b(a))(f)=\varepsilon_{uf}*b(af)=j_{(af,a^{\prime}f,uf)}\colon\beta(af,a^{\prime}f,uf)\,.

Finally, if
\con\colon A\to A^{\mathrm{I}}
is a cofibration then, looking at (
9.5
), the map
j
can be (uniformly) replaced by a homotopic one
j\sim j^{\prime}
that
also
makes the upper triangle commute, by a standard argument using the fibration on the right (as in e.g.
[
4

). The new map
j^{\prime}
then validates
both
the elimination and computation rules.

Note that the required coherence conditions for
A^{\mathrm{I}}
and
j
with respect to all context maps
Y\to X
are ensured by the same device used for
\Pi
in (

E\to U
, and then obtain all of the instances in a uniform way by composition. See
[

for details.

Remark 9.5
.

The condition that the constant path map
\con\colon A\to A^{\mathrm{I}}
is a cofibration holds, for instance, when
all
monos are taken as cofibrations, as in a Cisinski model structure. When this fails, as it may for presheaves not valued in sets,
one can still recover the computation rule (
9.3
) by factoring
\con\colon A\to A^{\mathrm{I}}
into a cofibration followed by a (uniform) trivial fibration (
7.9
), as shown in
[
49
]
.

10.
Conclusions

We conclude the paper by outlining some directions for future work. First, while here we considered
\mathop{\mathbf{Set}}
-valued presheaves, where
\mathop{\mathbf{Set}}
is the
category of sets associated to ZFC extended with an inaccessible cardinal, it is also interesting to consider presheaf categories
valued in other kinds of categories or categories of internal presheaves in some topos. Such a project may also connect with the idea of developing
the material here in a constructive metatheory, as suggested to us by Thierry Coquand and Michael Rathjen. Secondly, one could try to extend
our results to categories of sheaves. One preliminary issue for this is to find an adequate counterpart of the Hofmann-Streicher universe and
of the classifier of small maps. One may also try to develop the material here without using a universe, adopting the approach of
[
46
]
to
replace quantification over the universe. Finally, one may wish to consider extending the Kripke-Joyal forcing introduced here to other forms of type theory, such as the modal type theory studied in
[
33
]
.
