Abstract
In model-based reinforcement learning, the transition matrix and reward vector are often estimated from random samples subject to noise. Even if the estimated model is an unbiased estimate of the true underlying model, the value function computed from the estimated model is biased. We introduce an operator shifting method for reducing the error introduced by the estimated model. When the error is in the residual norm, we prove that the shifting factor is always positive and upper bounded by 1 + O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) 1+O\left({1}/{n}\right) , where n n is the number of samples used in learning each row of the transition matrix. We also propose a practical numerical algorithm for implementing the operator shifting.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has received much attention following recent successes, such as AlphaGo
and AlphaZero
[
25
,
26
]
. One of the fundamental problems of RL
is policy evaluation
[
29
]
. When the transition dynamics are unknown, one
learns the dynamics model from observed data in model-based RL. However, even if the learned model
is an unbiased estimate of the true dynamics, the policy evaluation under the learned model is
biased. The question of interest in this paper is whether one can increase the accuracy of the
policy evaluation given an estimated dynamics model.

We consider a discounted Markov decision process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}=(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{A},P,r,\gamma)
with discrete state
space
\mathbb{S}
and discrete action space
\mathbb{A}
.
\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert
and
|\mathbb{A}|
are used to denote the size of

\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{A}
, respectively.
P
is a third-order tensor, where for each action
a\in\mathbb{A}
,

P^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
is the transition matrix between the states.
r
is a
second-order tensor that
r_{s,a}
is the reward at state
s\in\mathbb{S}
if action
a\in\mathbb{A}
is
taken. Finally,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. A policy
\pi
is a second-order tensor,
where for each state
s\in\mathbb{S}
,
\pi_{s}
represents the probability distribution over
\mathbb{A}
. At each
time step
t
, one observes a state
s_{t}\in\mathbb{S}
and takes an action
a_{t}\in\mathbb{A}
according to the
policy
\pi_{s_{t}}
. The environment returns the next state
s_{t+1}
according to the distribution

P^{a_{t}}_{s_{t},\cdot}
and an associated reward
r_{s_{t},a_{t}}
. The state value function

v^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
is the expected discounted cumulative reward if one starts from an initial
state
s
and follows a policy
\pi
, i.e., the
s
-th component is

v^{\pi}_{s}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}\sim\pi_{s_{t}}\\
s_{t+1}\sim P^{a_{t}}_{s_{t},\cdot}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r_{s_{t},a_{t}}|s_{0}=s\right].

Given a policy
\pi
, the goal of policy evaluation in MDP is to solve for
v^{\pi}
. Let

b^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert},P^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
be the reward vector and
the transition matrix under policy
\pi
, i.e.,

b^{\pi}=\sum_{a}r_{sa}\pi^{a}_{s},\quad P^{\pi}=\sum_{a}P^{a}_{ss^{\prime}}\pi^{a}_{s}.

(1.1)

The value function
v^{\pi}
satisfies the Bellman equation
[
29
]

\left(I-\gamma P^{\pi}\right)v^{\pi}=b^{\pi}
. For notational simplicity, we drop the dependency on
\pi
and
write this system as

(I-\gamma P)v=b.

(1.2)

In practice, the true transition matrix
P
and the reward vector
b
are often inaccessible. In the
model-based RL, one approximates the transition matrix
P
and the reward vector
b
by the
empirical data
\hat{P}
and
\hat{b}
estimated from samples, respectively

[
16
,
6
,
28
,
31
,
22
]
. A naive
approach is to solve

\left(I-\gamma\hat{P}\right)\hat{v}=\hat{b}.

(1.3)

Even if
\hat{P}
and
\hat{b}
are unbiased estimates for
P
and
b
,
\hat{v}=\left(I-\gamma\hat{P}\right)^{-1}\hat{b}
is a biased estimate for
v
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\hat{v}\neq v
.

The operator shifting idea was introduced in
[
10
,
9
]
to
address this issue. The paper
[
10
]
considers the noisy symmetric elliptic
systems, while the follow-up paper
[
9
]
addresses the asymmetric setting under
the assumption that
\hat{b}
is isotropic, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{b}\hat{b}^{\top}]=I
. However, this
isotropic condition often fails to hold in RL. In this paper, we extend the operator shifting
framework to general MDPs of form (
1.2
). When applying this framework to the MDP
setting, we add an appropriately chosen matrix
\hat{K}
to the operator
\left(I-\gamma\hat{P}\right)^{-1}

so that the shifted estimate
\tilde{v}=\left[\left(I-\gamma\hat{P}\right)^{-1}-\beta\hat{K}\right]\hat{b}
is a better
estimate than
\hat{v}
in the sense that,

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert\tilde{v}-v\right\rVert^{2}<\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert\hat{v}-v\right\rVert^{2}

(1.4)

for a certain norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.

Contributions.

We derive a stable shifted operator for model-based policy evaluation without assumptions on
the underlying transition dynamics or reward vectors. When the approximated transition matrix

\hat{P}
follows the multinomial distribution and
n
samples are used to learn each row of the
transition matrix
P
,

•

we prove that the optimal shifting factor is always positive and upper bounded by

1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
for any

operator, and

•

we propose a numerical algorithm to find the optimal shifting factor, which is more
efficient and accurate than the bootstrapping method proposed in
[
10
]
.

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
,

,

,

,

,

[

,

,

,

2
Operator Shifting for Policy Evaluation

2.1
Problem setup.

As mentioned above,
P
and
b
are the
unknown
underlying transition matrix and reward
vector, while
\hat{P}
and
\hat{b}
are unbiased estimates for
P
and
b
, respectively. For
notational convenience, we introduce
A
and
\hat{A}

A=\left(I-\gamma P\right),\quad\hat{A}=\left(I-\gamma\hat{P}\right).

(2.1)

Since the transition dynamics is not symmetric in general, both
P
and
A
are non-symmetric. The norm of interest is a slightly generalized version of the residual norm

\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}_{M}=x^{\top}A^{\top}MAx,

(2.2)

where
M
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. This paper mainly discusses two cases: (1)
M=I
, which means
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{M}
is the usual residual norm, and (2)
M=A^{-\top}A^{-1}
, which means

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{M}
is the
l_{2}
norm.

In this paper, we choose the shifting matrix
\hat{K}=\hat{A}^{-1}
, which implies that the
shifted estimate is
\left(1-\beta\right)\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{b}
. By using
\varepsilon=\left(1-\beta\right)
instead as the
shifting parameter, one can write the above estimate as
\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\equiv\varepsilon\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{b}
, and the objective is to minimize the following mean square error over
\varepsilon
,

\mathrm{MSE}(\varepsilon)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert v-\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{M}^{2}.

(2.3)

The minimizer
\varepsilon^{*}
to (
2.3
) is referred as the
optimal shifting factor
.

Since (
2.3
) is a quadratic minimization, one can
explicitly write out the optimal shifting factor
\varepsilon^{*}
:

\varepsilon^{*}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left[b^{\top}MA\hat{v}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left[\hat{v}^{\top}A^{\top}MA\hat{v}\right]},\quad\text{where}\;\hat{v}=\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{b}.

(2.4)

The oracle estimate (
2.4
) is not easy to work with as it
depends on the unknown matrix
A
. Our immediate goal is to derive a closed-form approximation of

\varepsilon^{*}
, which is accurate and allows for efficient implementation. To achieve this, we introduce a
second-order approximation
\varepsilon^{\circ}
to
\varepsilon^{*}
. We show that
\varepsilon^{\circ}
takes a simple closed-form
without approximating any expectations under the following mild assumption:

Assumption 1
: The
i
-th row
\hat{p}^{\top}_{i}
of
\hat{P}
is an
random
vector

\frac{1}{n_{i}}X_{i}
, where
n_{i}
is the number of samples for state
i
and
X_{i}

follows the multinomial distribution with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=n_{i}p^{\top}_{i}
.
Moreover,
X_{i}
is independent from
X_{j}
whenever
i\not=j
.
The
i
-th entry of
\hat{b}
is an average of observed reward at state
i
.

The part of Assumption 1 on the estimation of
P
is equivalent to that
\hat{p}^{\top}_{i}
follows the
normalized multinomial distribution, which holds when a tabular maximum likelihood model

[
28
]
is used to estimate the transition dynamics
P
. That is, one generates
sufficiently many transitions according to
P
and lets
\hat{P}_{ii^{\prime}}=n_{ii^{\prime}}/n_{i},
where

n_{ii^{\prime}}
denotes the number of transitions observed from
i
to
i^{\prime}
, and
n_{i}=\sum_{i^{\prime}}n_{ii^{\prime}}
.

Throughout this paper, we assume for simplicity that the number of samples
n_{i}
of each state is
the same, i.e., for any
i\in\mathbb{S}
,
n_{i}\equiv n
. The sample size
n
plays an important role
in determining the magnitude of the operator shifting factor
\varepsilon^{*}
and the performance of the
operator shifting algorithm. If the value of
n_{i}
depends on
i\in\mathbb{S}
, all the theoretical
results still hold with slight modification (see Remark
2.3
for details).

2.2
Second-order approximation for
\varepsilon^{*}
.

To simplify the discussion, we introduce
\hat{Z}
and
\hat{Y}

\hat{Z}=A-\hat{A}=\gamma\left(P-\hat{P}\right),\quad\hat{Y}=\hat{Z}A^{-1}=\left(A-\hat{A}\right)A^{-1},

(2.5)

where
A
and
\hat{A}
are defined in (
2.1
). Some basic algebraic manipulations
lead to the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

When
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{b}\right]=b
, the optimal shifting factor
\varepsilon^{*}
defined in (
2.4
) has the form

\varepsilon^{*}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[b^{\top}M\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}b\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right]+b^{\top}b\right)\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-\top}M\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}\right)\right]},

(2.6)

where
\hat{Y}
is defined in (
2.5
). Moreover, if the values of the reward at state
s

and
s^{\prime}
(i.e.
\hat{b}_{s}
and
\hat{b}_{s^{\prime}}
) are uncorrelated, the matrix
\mathrm{cov}[\hat{b}]
is
diagonal.

Next, we approximate the value of
\varepsilon^{*}
using a Neumann expansion of the matrix
\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}

\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}=I+\hat{Y}+\hat{Y}^{2}+O\left(\frac{\rho(\hat{Y})^{3}}{1-\rho(\hat{Y})}\right),

(2.7)

when the spectral radius
\rho(\hat{Y})<1
. In fact, a modest requirement on
n
guarantees

\rho(\hat{Y})<1
with high probability, as shown in Appendix
E
. The denominator term in (
2.6
) admits the approximation

\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-\top}M\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}\approx M+M\left(\hat{Y}+\hat{Y}^{2}\right)+(\hat{Y}^{\top}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2})M+\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}.

(2.8)

Assumption 1 implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{Y}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{Y}^{\top}\right]=0
as a simple
consequence of
\hat{P}
being an unbiased estimator. Therefore, after taking an expectation, the first
order terms of
\hat{Y}
in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) disappear.

We can further approximate the shifting factor
\varepsilon^{*}
by expanding
\left(I-\hat{Y}\right)^{-1}
in the
numerator and denominator of (
2.6
) up to the second order in
\hat{Y}
. When Assumption
1 holds and
\rho(\hat{Y})<1
, the approximated optimal shifting factor
\varepsilon^{*}
defined in
(
2.4
) has a second-order approximation

\varepsilon^{*}\approx\varepsilon^{\circ}\equiv\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[b^{\top}(M+\frac{M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M}{2})b\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right]+b^{\top}b\right)(M+\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}+M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M)\right)\right]}.

(2.9)

The derivation of (
2.9
) is deferred to Section
4.2
.

Under Assumption 1, this second-order approximation can be written in a form without explicit
expectation. This expectation-free form depends on the transition matrix
P
, the expected reward

b
, and the reward covariance
\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right]
. Let
\hat{p}_{i}
be random vectors
corresponding to the i-th row of
\hat{P}
and
p_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{p}_{i}\right]
, i.e.,

\{\hat{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
and
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
are the row vectors of
\hat{P}
and

P
, respectively:

\hat{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{p}_{1}^{\top}\\
\dots\\
\hat{p}_{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}^{\top}\\
\dots\\
p_{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.10)

Theorem 2.2
.

The second-order approximation
\varepsilon^{\circ}
in (
2.9
) admits the expectation-free form

\varepsilon^{\circ}=\theta(b,P)\equiv\frac{b^{\top}(M+H/2)b}{b^{\top}(M+G+H)b+\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right](M+G+H)\right)},

(2.11)

where

\begin{cases}B_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\diag{p_{i}}-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}\right);\\
G=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}\right]=\gamma^{2}A^{-\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}\left[M\right]_{ii}B_{i}\right)A^{-1};\\
\begin{aligned} H&=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M+M\hat{Y}^{2}\right]\\
&=\gamma^{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}A^{-\top}B_{i}A^{-1}\mathrm{diag}\left(e_{i}\right)\right]M+\gamma^{2}M\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}\mathrm{diag}\left(e_{i}\right)A^{-\top}B_{i}A^{-1}\right]\end{aligned}.\end{cases}

(2.12)

Here
\mathrm{diag}(e_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
is a matrix with elements
0
except
for
1
on the
(i,i)
-th entry,
p_{i}
are row vectors of
P
as defined in (
2.10
), and the matrices
A
and
\hat{Y}
depend on
P
.

The proof of the above theorem is given in Section
4.2
.

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.2
still holds under conditions weaker than Assumption

\hat{P}
and entries for
\hat{b}
are independent unbiased estimators, then
the second-order approximation in (
2.9
) is still valid. Moreover, the expectation-free
form in (
2.11
) holds when one replaces the definition of
B_{i}
in (
2.12
) by
B_{i}=\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{p}_{i}\right]
. This slightly more general

, from which Theorem

is derived as a special case. In particular, if the state

receives

samples, then (

) will still hold with

in (

) replaced by
\frac{1}{n_{i}}\left(\diag{p_{i}}-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}\right)
.

Theorem
2.2
also proves that the choice of
\varepsilon^{\circ}
is
asymptotically as powerful as
\varepsilon^{*}
with

. For

defined in (

), the following estimation holds, with the proof

.

Lemma 2.4
.

The MSE in (

\mathrm{MSE}(\varepsilon)=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)^{2}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+(g+h+t)\varepsilon^{2}-h\varepsilon+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right),

(2.13)

where
g=b^{\top}Gb,h=b^{\top}Hb,t=\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right]\left(M+G+H\right)\right)

with all symbols defined in Theorem

.
In addition,

(2.14)

The relative error reduction factor

), is a

.

Define relative error reduction factor as

\eta\equiv\frac{\mathrm{MSE}(1)-\mathrm{MSE}(\varepsilon^{\circ})}{\mathrm{MSE}(1)}

2.3
Lower and upper bounds for
\varepsilon^{\circ}
.

In this section, we aim to provide bounds to show that
\varepsilon^{\circ}
will
approximately fall in the
(0,1)
range. Throughout this subsection, we conduct the analysis in the
residual norm, which is the case with
M=I
. We present an upper bound and a lower bound for

\varepsilon^{\circ}
in Theorem
2.6
. The relevant parameters are
\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert
,
n
and
\gamma
, which are the number of state, the number of samples per state and
the discount factor, respectively.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
p_{M}=\max P_{i,j}
and
b_{M}=\max_{i}|b_{i}|
. If

\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{\left(1-\gamma\right)}{\gamma}+\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
then
\varepsilon^{\circ}
is bounded by

0<\varepsilon^{\circ}\leq 1+\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right)+\gamma\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}.

The bound comes from technique using the spectral
structure of the covariance matrix of a multinomial distribution and a tight bound for
(I-\gamma P)^{-1}b
. We defer the proof to Section
4
.

We now discuss the implication of Theorem
2.6
. The reward vector
b
is
spread
if

\frac{\max_{i}|b_{i}|}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\sim O\left(\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{-1/2}\right)
. Similarly, the transition matrix

P
is
spread
if
\max P_{ij}\sim o\left(\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{-1/2}\right)
. If both
b
and
P
are spread, it follows that
\epsilon^{\circ}-1
is upper bounded by a term in
O(n^{-1}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{-1/2})
.

3
Practical algorithm

3.1
Algorithm.

In practice, we do not have direct access to
P
or
b
. Therefore, the second-order estimate

\varepsilon^{\circ}
derived in (
2.11
) is an oracle estimator. One can address this issue by
bootstrapping the distribution of
\hat{P}
. More specifically, let
P
be a transition matrix and
denote
\mathcal{M}_{n}(P)
as the normalized multinomial distribution that the estimated transition
matrix
\hat{P}
follows according to Assumption 1. Since one only has access to a single
observation
\hat{P}
,
\mathcal{M}_{n}(P)
is approximated by
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\hat{P})
in the
numerical implementation. In the usual bootstrapping procedure, one needs to simulate
i.i.d. samples
\{\tilde{P}_{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{l}\sim\mathcal{M}_{n}(\hat{P})
. By setting

\tilde{Y}_{(j)}=\gamma(\tilde{P}_{(j)}-\hat{P})\hat{A}^{-1}
and following the form in Theorem
2.2
, one can
approximate
\varepsilon^{\circ}
in (
2.9
) by replacing the expectation with an empirical mean:

\varepsilon^{\circ}\approx\frac{\hat{b}^{\top}(M+\tilde{H}/2)\hat{b}}{\hat{b}^{\top}\left(M+\tilde{G}+\tilde{H}\right)\hat{b}+\mathrm{tr}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}\left(M+\tilde{G}+\tilde{H}\right)\right)},

(3.1)

with

\begin{cases}\tilde{G}=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\tilde{Y}_{(j)}^{\top}M\tilde{Y}_{(j)}\approx\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}\right]=G;\\
\tilde{H}=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}M\tilde{Y}_{(j)}^{2}+\left(\tilde{Y}_{(j)}^{\top}\right)^{2}M\approx\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M\right]=H;\\
\tilde{\Sigma}\approx\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right].\end{cases}

(3.2)

However, there is a major drawback to this scheme. In addition to the error caused by the difference between
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\hat{P})
and
\mathcal{M}_{n}({P})
, the scheme introduces additional errors due to the empirical mean in
place of the expectation. The empirical mean errors
\tilde{G}-G
and
\tilde{H}-H

are of order
O\left(l^{-1/2}\right)
. In addition, the procedure in (
3.1
) has a computational cost of order

O\left(l\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{3}\right)
.

Plug-in estimate.

Luckily in our case, Assumption 1 (i.e.,
\hat{P}
follows the normalized multinomial distribution)
allows for a direct formula for
\varepsilon^{\circ}
, which automatically removes the error in the empirical

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert b-\varepsilon A\hat{v}\right\rVert_{M}^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert A\hat{v}\right\rVert_{M}^{2}-2\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left[b^{\top}MA\hat{v}\right]+\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}.

(4.8)

Using Lemma
2.1
and the second-order approximation in equation (

), one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left[b^{\top}MA\hat{v}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[b^{\top}(M+\frac{M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M}{2})b\right]+h.o.t.,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert A\hat{v}\right\rVert_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[b^{\top}(M+\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}+M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M)b\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right](M+\hat{Y}^{\top}M\hat{Y}+M\hat{Y}^{2}+(\hat{Y}^{\top})^{2}M)\right)\right]+h.o.t.,

where
h.o.t.
stands for high order terms.

We now show that
h.o.t.=O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)
. The expectation of the third order or higher
terms in
\hat{Y}
is computed by moments of third order or
higher in
\hat{Z}
. Under Assumption 1, rows of
\hat{Z}
are independent, and
hence moments of
\hat{Z}
are linear combinations of moments in multinomial
distribution. Each row of matrix

is an average of
n

random variables with mean zero, which is why its moments of third order or higher decay at the
rate of at least
O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)
by use of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality.

One can then plug in the explicit formula from Lemma
4.2

and (
4.6
), leading to

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left[b^{\top}MA\hat{v}\right]=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+b^{\top}Hb/2+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+h/2+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right).

(4.9)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P},\hat{b}}\left\lVert A\hat{v}\right\rVert_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+b^{\top}Gb+b^{\top}Hb+\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{cov}\left[\hat{b}\right]\left(M+G+H\right)\right)+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+h+g+t+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right).

Plugging it into (
4.8
) results in (

We now move to prove (

) and the results before, one has

\varepsilon^{*}=\frac{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+h/2+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{M}^{2}+h+g+t+O\left(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)}.

On the other hand, Theorem

proves that
\varepsilon^{\circ}
follows the following form:

(4.11)

Moreover, from Theorem

. Hence (

Lemma 4.6
.

For any transition matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
, vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

b+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{m}{\bf 1}\leq\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b\leq b+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{M}{\bf 1},

where
b_{m}=\min_{s}b_{s},b_{M}=\max_{s}b_{s}
,
and inequality between vectors denotes an entry-wise inequality
.

Proof 4.7
.

Let
x=\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b
, and
s=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{i}x_{i}
, then the
s
-th row of
\left(I-\gamma P\right)x=b
is

b_{s}=x_{s}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{st}x_{t}\leq x_{s}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{st}x_{s}=\left(1-\gamma\right)x_{s},

which implies

x_{s}\geq\frac{b_{s}}{1-\gamma}\geq\frac{b_{m}}{1-\gamma},

(4.18)

where
b_{m}=\min_{s}b_{s}
. For
\forall j\neq s
, one has

b_{j}=x_{j}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{jt}x_{t}\leq x_{j}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{st}x_{s}=x_{j}-\gamma x_{s}\leq x_{j}-\gamma\frac{b_{m}}{1-\gamma},

which yields,

x_{j}\geq b_{j}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{m}.

(4.19)

Combining (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) gives

x=\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b\geq b+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{m}{\bf 1}.

On the other hand, let
l=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i}x_{i}
, then the
l
-th row of
\left(I-\gamma P\right)x=b
is

b_{l}=x_{l}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{lt}x_{t}\geq x_{l}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{lt}x_{l}=\left(1-\gamma\right)x_{l},

which implies

x_{l}\leq\frac{b_{l}}{1-\gamma}\leq\frac{b_{M}}{1-\gamma},

(4.20)

where
b_{M}=\max_{s}b_{s}
. For
\forall j\neq l
, one has

b_{j}=x_{j}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{jt}x_{t}\geq x_{j}-\gamma\sum_{t}P_{jt}x_{l}\geq x_{j}-\gamma\frac{b_{M}}{1-\gamma},

which yields,

x_{j}\leq b_{j}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{M}.

(4.21)

Combining (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) gives

x=\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b\leq b+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{M}{\bf 1},

which completes the proof.

Lemma 4.8
.

For a vector
v=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
, define
\left\lvert v\right\rvert:=(\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rvert)_{i=1}^{d}
, i.e. the entry-wise absolute value of
v
. Suppose a matrix
Q
has only non-negative entries. Then, for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\left\lvert Qv\right\rvert\leq Q\left\lvert v\right\rvert.

Proof 4.9
.

Define
x=Qv
. Denote by
v_{+},v_{-}
the positive and negative parts of
v
, respectively. That is,
(v_{+})_{i}=v_{i}\mathds{1}_{v_{i}>0}
and
(v_{-})_{i}=v_{i}\mathds{1}_{v_{i}\leq 0}
. Since
Q
has only non-negative entries, it follows
Qv_{+}\geq{\bf 0}
and
Qv_{-}\leq{\bf 0}
.
Because
x=Qv_{+}+Qv_{-}
, one has

x\leq Qv_{+}-Qv_{-}
and

-x\geq-Qv_{+}+Qv_{-}
.
Hence

\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq Qv_{+}-Qv_{-}=Q\left\lvert v\right\rvert.

Corollary 4.10
.

For any transition matrix
P
, vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, one has,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\diag(e_{i})b\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq\left\lVert k\right\rVert^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\diag(e_{i})b\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{2},

b_{M}=\max_{i}|b_{i}|

\left\lVert k\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\frac{1}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\gamma^{2}b_{M}^{2}+2\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right)b_{M}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{1}\right).

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{2}.

\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right)+\gamma\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, the condition that
n>2\gamma^{2}p_{M}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\frac{b_{M}^{2}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}
is simply a restatement of the condition that
\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
.

Then, since
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the theorem’s assumption
\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{\left(1-\gamma\right)}{\gamma}+\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
implies

\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right)+\gamma\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{p_{M}}{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}b_{M}}{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{2}}\right)^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, the theorem’s assumption implies
\epsilon^{\circ}>0
.

For the remainder of the proof, we show that (
4.25
) holds.
Define
B_{i,+}=\frac{1}{n}\diag{p_{i}}
and
B_{i,-}=-\frac{1}{n}p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}
. It follows that
B_{i}=B_{i,+}+B_{i,-}
, and one has

\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}.

(4.26)

We first bound the first term on the right hand side of (
4.26
). Let
b_{+},b_{-}
be the positive and negative parts of
b
as in Lemma
4.8
, and define

a_{i,+}:=\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})b_{+},\quad a_{i,-}:=\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})b_{-}.

In particular, one has
a_{i,+}\geq{\bf 0}
and
a_{i,+}\leq{\bf 0}
, as
\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})
has only non-negative entries.
Thus one can further bound by

\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}.

Due to the non-negativity of the entries in
(-a_{i,-})
and
a_{i,+}
, the right hand side is a monotonically non-decreasing function in the entries of
B_{i,+}
, and therefore one has

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\diag{\left(p_{M}{\bf 1}\right)}\sum_{i}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\diag{\left(p_{M}{\bf 1}\right)}\sum_{i}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left(\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{-}\right\rVert_{1}\right).

Now, note that
\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{+}\right\rVert_{1}={\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{+}
, and
\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{-}\right\rVert_{1}=-{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{-}
. Therefore,

\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert\right\rVert_{1}={\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert={\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left(b_{+}-b_{-}\right).

Hence one has

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,+}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left(\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{+}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}b_{-}\right\rVert_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\frac{1}{1-\gamma}b_{M}.

For the second term in (
4.26
), one similarly has

\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}.

One can check

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i,-}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i,-}a_{i,+}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf 1}^{\top}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{n}p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})\left\lvert b\right\rvert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{1}{n}p_{i}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})\left\lvert b\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{p_{M}}{n}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\text{diag}(e_{i})\left\lvert b\right\rvert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lVert\left(I-\gamma P\right)^{-1}\left\lvert b\right\rvert\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\frac{1}{1-\gamma}b_{M}.

Thus
\left\lVert\sum_{i}B_{i}a_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leq 2\frac{p_{M}}{n}\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert\frac{1}{1-\gamma}b_{M}
.

Appendices

E
Condition for Convergence of Neumann Series.

The spectral radius
\rho(\hat{Y})
of
\hat{Y}
can be bounded by the size of state space
\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert
, the
number of samples
n
used to learn the model
P
and the number of possible transitions. We
define
\kappa
as the largest number of transitions among all states,

\kappa=\max_{s\in\mathbb{S}}\{k:k=\left\lVert P_{s}\right\rVert_{0},P_{s}\text{ is the $s$-th row of $P$}\}.

(A.1)

The following lemma gives the condition for
\rho(\hat{Y})<1
with high probability. The proof relies on
the concentration inequality of
l_{1}
-norm of the multinomial distribution.

Lemma A.1
.

Under Assumption 1, for any
C>0
and any positive integer
q>1
, if
n\geq\frac{2C^{2}\gamma^{2}\kappa}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\log{\left(2\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}\right)}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\rho(\hat{Y})<\frac{1}{C}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q-1}}.

Proof A.2
.

We have

\rho(\hat{Y})\leq\left\lVert\hat{Y}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\hat{Z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert A^{-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left\lVert P-\hat{P}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

By the concentration inequality in
[
32
,
24
]
, for arbitrary
r\in[0,1]

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert e_{i}^{\top}\left(\hat{P}-P\right)\right\rVert_{1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\kappa\log{2/r}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\leq r.

(A.2)

Taking union bound and setting
r=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}}
leads to

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert P-\hat{P}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\kappa\log{2\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\leq 1-\left(1-1/\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}\right)^{\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert}\leq 1/\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q-1},

where the second inequality is by Bernoulli’s inequality: for
r\geq 1
and
x\leq 1
,

(1-x)^{r}\geq 1-rx.

The proof is completed
by noticing

\frac{\sqrt{2\kappa\log{2\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}}}}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{1}{C}\iff n\geq\frac{2C^{2}\gamma^{2}\kappa}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\log{\left(2\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}\right)}

Remark A.3
.

In particular, our goal is to show a bound of
n
to ensure that
\rho(\hat{Y})<1
with high
probability. In this case, the sample size requirement is

n\geq\frac{2\gamma^{2}\kappa}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\log{\left(2\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert^{q}\right)}.

The requirement of sample size
n
only grows at the rate of
O\left(\kappa\log(\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert)\right)
. Even though
\kappa
may grow proportionally to
\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert
, one can generally assume
that
\kappa
grows sublinearly with respect to
\left\lvert\mathbb{S}\right\rvert
. In practice, the numerical examples
are more well-behaved if
\kappa
is large, and usually the convergence of the Taylor series
needs only
n=1
. The bound on the spectral radius is intended for ill-behaved MDP with small

\kappa
.
