An efficient multiscale multigrid preconditioner for Darcy flow in high-contrast media Journal: arXiv
Changqing Ye, Shubin Fu, Eric T. Chung, Jizu Huang

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a multigrid preconditioner to solve Darcy flow in highly heterogeneous porous media. The key component of the preconditioner is to construct a sequence of nested subspaces W ℒ ⊂ W ℒ − 1 ⊂ ⋯ ⊂ W 1 = W h W_{\mathcal{L}}\subset W_{\mathcal{L}-1}\subset\cdots\subset W_{1}=W_{h} . An appropriate spectral problem is defined in the space of W i − 1 W_{i-1} , then the eigenfunctions of the spectral problems are utilized to form W i W_{i} . The preconditioner is applied to solve a positive semidefinite linear system which results from discretizing the Darcy flow equation with the lowest order Raviart-Thomas spaces and adopting a trapezoidal quadrature rule. Theoretical analysis and numerical investigations of this preconditioner will be presented. In particular, we will consider several typical highly heterogeneous permeability fields whose resolutions are up to 1024 3 1024^{3} and examine the computational performance of the preconditioner in several aspects, such as strong scalability, weak scalability, and robustness against the contrast of the media. We also demonstrate an application of this preconditioner for solving a two-phase flow benchmark problem.

2.1
The model problem and the discretization

We consider the following elliptic PDE with the unknown pressure field
p

-\Div\left(\mathbb{K}\nabla p\right)=f\ \text{in}\ \Omega\\

(1)

in a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d=2
or
3
, subject to a no-flow boundary condition

\mathbb{K}\nabla p\cdot\bm{n}=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega,

(2)

where
\mathbb{K}
is the symmetric matrix-valued permeability field that exhibits discontinuity and high heterogeneity, i.e.,

\frac{\max_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}\big(\mathbb{K}(\bm{x})\big)}{\min_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\lambda_{{\operatorfont min}}\big(\mathbb{K}(\bm{x})\big)}\gg 1.

In reservoir simulation, the permeability field may not be point-wisely isotropic but commonly be point-wisely orthotropic, that is,

\mathbb{K}(\bm{x})=\Diag\big(\kappa^{x}(\bm{x}),\kappa^{y}(\bm{x})\big)\ \text{or}\ \Diag\big(\kappa^{x}(\bm{x}),\kappa^{y}(\bm{x}),\kappa^{z}(\bm{x})\big),

and we assume the field
\mathbb{K}
belongs to this class throughout the article. Note that we make a slight abuse of notation for denoting three orthogonal directions by
x
,
y
, and
z
. We emphasize here that the extension of the forthcoming discretization scheme of
Eq.

1
to the full matrix-valued permeability field is possible, while several delicate constructions are necessary
[
49
]
. The divergence theorem implies that the source function
f
must satisfy the so-called compatibility condition
\int_{\Omega}f\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}=0
, and an additional restriction
\int_{\Omega}p\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}=0
must be imposed to guarantee the uniqueness of the solution. However, if we incorporate well modeling (see
[
50
,
51
]
), the source term
f
will be coupled with pressure and we will revisit this situation in
Section

5
. By introducing the flux variable
\bm{v}=-\mathbb{K}\nabla p
, we can rewrite
Eq.

1
and
Eq.

2
as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbb{K}^{-1}\bm{v}+\nabla p&=\bm{0}\ \text{in}\ \Omega,\ &&\text{(Darcy's law)},\\
\Div(\bm{v})&=f\ \text{in}\ \Omega,\ &&\text{(mass conservation)},\\
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n}&=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\ &&\text{(no-flow boundary condition)}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

Note that for Darcy’s law in
Eq.

3
, we implicitly normalize the viscosity for simplicity.

The mixed formulation (ref.
[
52
]
) of
Eq.

3
is to seek
(\bm{v},p)\in\bm{V}\times W
satisfying the following equations:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{w}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega}\Div(\bm{w})p\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{w}\in\bm{V},

(4)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\Div(\bm{v})q\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}fq\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x},

\displaystyle\forall q\in W.

where the space
\bm{V}
is defined as

\bm{V}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\Div(\bm{v})\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ \text{and}\ \bm{v}\cdot\bm{n}=0\;\mbox{on}\;\partial\Omega\right\},

and
W
is

W\coloneqq\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mid\int_{\Omega}q\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}=0\right\}.

By restricting the trial and test functions to finite-dimensional subspaces
\bm{V}_{h}\subset\bm{V}
and
W_{h}\subset W
associated with a prescribed triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega
, the corresponding discrete problem is to find
(\bm{v}_{h},p_{h})\in\bm{V}_{h}\times W_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{w}_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega}\Div(\bm{w}_{h})p_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{w}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h},

(5)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\Div(\bm{v}_{h})q_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}fq_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x},

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in W_{h}.

Several well-known families of mixed finite element spaces
\bm{V}_{h}\times W_{h}
that satisfy the well-known LBB condition can be utilized (see
[
53
]
). We choose the lowest-order Raviart-Thomas element space for simplicity, whose definition is detailed in
[
52
,
54
]
. Let
\left\{\bm{\phi}_{e}\right\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{h}}
and
\left\{q_{\tau}\right\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
be the bases of
\bm{V}_{h}
and
W_{h}
, respectively, where
\mathcal{E}^{0}_{h}
is the set of internal edges/faces, and we drop

of these bases for brevity. Hence, the finite element solution

can be expressed as

\bm{v}_{h}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{0}_{h}}v_{e}\bm{\phi}_{e}\ \text{and}\ p_{h}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}p_{\tau}q_{\tau}.

Then, it is easy to obtain the following algebraic representation of
Eq.

5

\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{M}&-\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\\
-\mathsf{B}&\mathsf{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{v}\\
\mathsf{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where
\mathsf{M}
is a symmetric, positive definite matrix,
\mathsf{v}
,
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{f}
are column vectors.

2.2
The velocity elimination technique

Preconditioning the saddle point system
Eq.

6
directly is not easy, and we may involve several sophisticated field-splitting procedures. Fortunately, if applying the trapezoidal quadrature rule to compute the integration
\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{w}_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x}
on each element, we will obtain a diagonal velocity mass matrix
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont t}
, and hence
Eq.

6
could be replaced by

\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont t}&-\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\\
-\mathsf{B}&\mathsf{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{v}\\
\mathsf{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont t}
could be inverted straightforwardly and the unknown
\mathsf{v}
in
Eq.

6
can be fully eliminated, we boil down to solve a linear system of
\mathsf{p}
as

\mathsf{A}\mathsf{p}=\mathsf{f},

(7)

where
\mathsf{A}\coloneqq\mathsf{B}\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont t}^{-1}\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}
is a symmetric and semi-positive definite matrix. This technique is known as velocity elimination
[
55
,
56
,
57
]
, which could also be interpreted as a cell-centered finite difference scheme on Cartesian meshes. Once
\mathsf{p}
is solved, the velocity field could be recovered by
\mathsf{v}=\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont t}^{-1}\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\mathsf{p}
.

Because the analysis result in
Section

4

where

or

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{i})}\kappa_{e}\llbracket\Phi_{h}\rrbracket_{e}\llbracket q_{h}\rrbracket_{e}\frac{{\left|e\right|}^{2}}{h_{x}h_{y}}=\lambda\int_{K_{\operatorfont c}^{i}}\left(\Tr\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}\right)\Phi_{h}q_{h}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x},\ \forall q_{h}\in W_{h}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{i}),

(12)

where
\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{i})
is the set of all internal edges in
K_{\operatorfont c}^{i}
,
\Phi_{h}
is an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}
is the normalized permeability field. Note that the eigenvalues here are all dimensionless. Compared to
[
36
]
, we modify the right-hand bilinear form because the permeability field is now assumed to be orthotropic rather than isotropic. After solving the spectral problem
Eq.

12
, we construct the local coarse space
W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{i})
as
\Span\{\Phi_{h}^{i,j}\mid j=0,\dots,l_{\operatorfont c}^{i}-1\}
, where
\{\Phi_{h}^{i,j}\}_{j=0}^{l_{\operatorfont c}^{i}-1}
are the eigenvectors associated with the smallest eigenvalues
L^{i}_{\operatorfont c}
. By identifying
W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{i})
as a linear subspace of
W_{h}
, the global coarse space
W_{\operatorfont c}\subset W_{h}
is formed by
W_{\operatorfont c}=W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{1})\oplus\dots\oplus W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont c}^{m_{\operatorfont c}})
, and
n_{\operatorfont c}
—the dimension of
W_{\operatorfont c}
—is determined by
n_{\operatorfont c}=l_{\operatorfont c}^{1}+\dots+l_{\operatorfont c}^{m_{\operatorfont c}}
. Therefore, we establish a
\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\operatorfont c}\times n}
as a restriction matrix from the fine grid to the coarse grid, where each row of it is the algebraic representation of an eigenvector of
Eq.

12
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

To perform a further dimension reduction on
W_{\operatorfont c}
, we propose another spectral problem on each coarse-coarse element
K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont cc}
:

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})}\kappa_{e}\llbracket\varPsi_{\operatorfont c}\rrbracket_{e}\llbracket\varTheta_{\operatorfont c}\rrbracket_{e}\frac{{\left|e\right|}^{2}}{h_{x}h_{y}}=\lambda\int_{K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}}\left(\Tr\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}\right)\varPsi_{\operatorfont c}\varTheta_{\operatorfont c}\operatorname{d}\mathopen{}\bm{x},\ \forall\varTheta_{\operatorfont c}\in W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}),

(13)

where
\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
is the set of all internal edges in
K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}
,
W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
is the restriction of
W_{\operatorfont c}
on
K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}
. It should be addressed that
Eq.

13
is defined in the linear space
W_{\operatorfont c}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
rather than
W_{h}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
, and this fact may be favorable in two aspects: 1) solving
Eq.

13
leads to a smaller algebraic system and should be much easier compared with the latter, and 2) the eigenvectors of
Eq.

13
compress the coarse space, while the latter one is irrelevant with
W_{\operatorfont c}
. Similarly, the local coarse-coarse space
W_{\operatorfont cc}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
could be created from spanning
\left\{\varPsi_{\operatorfont c}^{i,j}\right\}_{j=0}^{l_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}-1}
which are eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest
l_{\operatorfont cc}^{i}
eigenvalues. Furthermore, by identifying
W_{\operatorfont cc}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{i})
as a linear subspace of
W_{\operatorfont c}
, we can build the coarse-coarse space
W_{\operatorfont cc}
as
W_{\operatorfont cc}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{1})\oplus\dots\oplus W_{\operatorfont cc}(K_{\operatorfont cc}^{m_{\operatorfont cc}})
, whose dimension
n_{\operatorfont cc}
equals
l_{\operatorfont cc}^{1}+\dots+l_{\operatorfont cc}^{m_{\operatorfont cc}}
, and thus we achieve a chain of inclusions
W_{\operatorfont cc}\subset W_{\operatorfont c}\subset W_{h}
. Back to
\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont cc}
, we let each row of
\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont c}
be the algebraic representation eigenvector
\varPsi_{c}
of
Eq.

13
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont cc}
has a shape of
n_{\operatorfont cc}\times n_{\operatorfont c}
.

Note that the smallest eigenvalue of
Eqs.

12
and
13
is always zero and the corresponding eigenfunctions take a constant value. This property implies the kernel space of the left-hand operator in
Eq.

8
is contained in
W_{\operatorfont c}
and also
W_{\operatorfont cc}
. Moreover, considering the Raviart-Thomas spaces on
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont c}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont cc}
, the pressure parts take a constant value on each element, we can hence interpret
W_{\operatorfont c}
and
W_{\operatorfont cc}
as enhancements to classic geometric coarse spaces that are based on nested grids.

Another important fact that could be observed from
Eqs.

12
and
13
is that the right-hand operators in algebraic forms are all diagonal matrices. Moreover, if eigenvectors obtained in
Eq.

12
are normalized w.r.t. the right-hand bilinear form and are chosen as the bases of
W_{\operatorfont c}
, the right-hand operator in the algebraic form of
Eq.

13
is an identity matrix, which reduces a generalized eigenvalue problem to a standard one. Those properties are thanks to that the right-hand bilinear forms of
Eqs.

12
and
13
are essentially a weighted
L^{2}
inner-product and our coarse elements do not overlap with each other. Constructing coarse spaces via solving spectral problems is criticized as time-consuming (see
[
62
]
), while the proposed spectral problems with a simple right-hand matrix could be expected to alleviate the difficulty.

To avoid working on singular systems, we suppose that a part of

\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\coloneqq\mathsf{M}^{-\intercal}+\mathsf{M}^{-1}-\mathsf{M}^{-\intercal}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{M}^{-1}+\left(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{M}^{-\intercal}\mathsf{A}\right)\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont c}^{\intercal}\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont c}^{-1}\mathsf{R}_{\operatorfont c}\left(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{A}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\right),

\mathsf{B}_{\operatorfont c}^{-1}

and

\Cond(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont ITG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont ITG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})}{\lambda_{{\operatorfont min}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont ITG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})}\leq C.

suggest estimating

\Cond(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})}{\lambda_{{\operatorfont min}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})}

According to
[

\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})=1

\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\mathsf{Z}}=\sqrt{\mathsf{v}\cdot\mathsf{Z}\mathsf{v}}
as the energy norm induced by
\mathsf{Z}
for a compatible vector
\mathsf{v}
. We usually assume that the smoother
\mathsf{M}
is properly chosen such that
\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}-\mathsf{A}
is a positive definite matrix. According to
[
62
]
, a direct result of this assumption is that two-grid iterations can converge without coarse space corrections, although the convergence rate may be poor. Thanks to the XZ-identity
[
62
]
, we could obtain that

\frac{1}{\lambda_{{\operatorfont min}}(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont TG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})}=\max_{\mathsf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\mathsf{0}\}}\min_{\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\operatorfont c}}\setminus\{\mathsf{0}\}}\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}}^{2}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\mathsf{A}}^{2}},

(16)

where
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}=\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}(\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}-\mathsf{A})^{-1}\mathsf{M}
. The special max-min form of
Eq.

16
inspires an existence of the optimal coarse space, which is an eigenspace of the generalized eigenvalue problem
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{v}=\lambda\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{v}
. Nevertheless, due to the facts that
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}
requires an inversion of
\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}-\mathsf{A}
and the eigenvalue problem needs to be solved in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this optimal coarse space is highly unobtainable.

For a coarse element
K_{\operatorfont c}^{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont c}
, we take
\mathsf{A}_{i}
as the matrix built from the left-hand bilinear form of
Eq.

12
and
\mathsf{S}^{i}
from the right-hand bilinear form, where
\mathsf{S}_{i}
is a diagonal matrix. We have seen that the coarse grid
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont c}
consists of non-overlapping coarse elements. It is natural to introduce binary matrices
\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{E}_{m_{\operatorfont c}}\right\}
with the relation
\mathsf{I}=\mathsf{E}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathsf{E}_{1}+\dots+\mathsf{E}_{m_{\operatorfont c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{E}_{m_{\operatorfont c}}
, where the effect of
\mathsf{E}_{i}
is restricting the DoF of the domain
\Omega
onto the coarse element
K_{\operatorfont c}^{i}
. We have the following relation:

\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\operatorfont c}}\mathsf{E}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{i}\lesssim\mathsf{A},

where
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\lesssim\mathsf{A}
means that
\mathsf{A}-\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
is positive semi-definite. This inequality is from taking a summation of the left-hand parts of
Eq.

12
w.r.t. all coarse elements, the edges in boundaries of coarse elements will not be counted. Therefore, we can derive that

\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\mathsf{A}}^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\operatorfont c}}\left(\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{v}\right)\cdot\mathsf{A}_{i}\left(\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{v}\right).

To analyses
\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}\mathsf{v}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}}
, we inevitably require several assumptions for
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}
. It is known that
\mathsf{A}\lesssim\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}
via

\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{A}^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}^{-1}\mathsf{A}^{1/2}=\left(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{A}^{1/2}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\mathsf{A}^{1/2}\right)\left(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{A}^{1/2}\mathsf{M}^{-\intercal}\mathsf{A}^{1/2}\right).

However, the target here should be bounded
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}
by
\mathsf{A}
. A natural thought is to assume
\mathsf{A}\lesssim\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}\lesssim C_{\star}\mathsf{A}
, which gives exactly an estimate of
\Cond(\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})
, where
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}^{-1}\mathsf{A}
is a preconditioned operator using only the smoother as a preconditioner. Recalling the algebraic representation in

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\widetilde{\kappa}_{i+1/2,j}\left(q_{i+1,j}-q_{i,j}\right)^{2}+\widetilde{\kappa}_{i,j+1/2}\left(q_{i,j+1}-q_{i,j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}})\mathchoice{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigl\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{\small$\bigr\|$}}}{\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\footnotesize\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}{\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{R}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont cc}\mathsf{v}_{\operatorfont cc}\mbox{\tiny\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\|$}}}_{\mathsf{I}}^{2}

Therefore, we can cook up an estimate for
\lambda_{{\operatorfont min}}(\mathsf{B}_{\operatorfont c}^{-1}\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont c})
, which yields the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
C_{\star}
,
C_{\lambda}
be defined in
Lemma

4.1
, the constant
C_{{\operatorfont c},\lambda}
be
\min\left\{\lambda_{l^{i}_{\operatorfont cc}}\right\}_{i=1}^{m_{\operatorfont cc}}
. If
\mathsf{M}+\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}-\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}+\mathsf{M}^{\intercal}_{\operatorfont c}-\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont c}
,
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont c}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont cc}
are all positive definite matrices, then

\Cond(\mathsf{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorfont ITG}}^{-1}\mathsf{A})\leq\max\left\{C_{\operatorfont s}\frac{C_{\star}\lambda_{{\operatorfont max}}(\widetilde{\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}})}{C_{\lambda}C_{{\operatorfont c},\lambda}},1\right\},

(17)

where
C_{\operatorfont s}
is a generic positive constant.

In
Eq.

17
, the constants in the numerator are related to the smoothers
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}
, while the constants in the denominator are determined by dimensions of local coarse and coarse-coarse spaces. We admit the current form of
Theorem

4.3
is far from completeness. For example, the asymptotic rates of
C_{\lambda}
and
C_{{\operatorfont c},\lambda}
are not depicted. However, our experiments hint that using a modestly large number of eigenvectors is adequate to achieve robustness. Therefore, for

4.3
, we pursue theoretical guidance rather than rigorous analysis.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, numerical experiments are presented to illustrate the performance of the proposed preconditioner.
We consider the unit cube
(0,1)^{3}
as the computational domain
\Omega
with uniformly structured meshes, i.e.
N\times N\times N
with
h^{x}=h^{y}=h^{z}=1/N
for all experiments, and
n
the total number of DoF also the fine elements is
N^{3}
. We implement the proposed spectral three-grid preconditioner in PETSc
[
64
]
and leverage DMDA—a module provided by PETSc—for inter-process communications and data management. As described in
Fig.

2
, each MPI process owns a coarse-coarse element also a cuboid subdomain, the number of MPI processes hence equals
n_{\operatorfont cc}
the total number of coarse-coarse elements. Meanwhile, each coarse-coarse element is divided into
\mathtt{sd}
parts in the
x
-,
y
- and
z
-direction, which defines the coarse grid
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont c}
and determines the relation
n_{\operatorfont c}=\mathtt{sd}^{3}\times n_{\operatorfont cc}
. To make the notations more self-explanatory, we take
\mathtt{DoF}
for the number of fine elements (equals
n
),
\mathtt{proc}
for the number of MPI processes (equals
n_{\operatorfont cc}
) in the following tables and figures. For the source term
f
of the model
Eq.

3
, we place
4
long singular sources at
4
corner points on the
x\times y
-plane and an opposite sign long singular source at the middle of the
x\times y
-plane, which mimics the well condition in the SPE10 model (ref.
[
65
]
).

For smoother
\mathsf{M}
on
\mathsf{A}
, we will apply
\nu
times block Jacobi iterations, while the block partition is from the coarse grid
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont c}
. Similarly, for
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}
, we will take
\nu_{\operatorfont c}
times block Jacobi iterations w.r.t. the coarse-coarse grid
\mathcal{T}_{\operatorfont cc}
. Therefore, we can fix only the
1
ghost layer for all cases, and by contrast, we may use different oversampling layers in overlapping domain decomposition methods (cf.
[
36
]
). In the set-up phase, we will perform factorizations for the matrices
\mathsf{M}
,
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}
, and
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont cc}
. Based on our experience, compared to incomplete Cholesky decompositions, full Cholesky decompositions for
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}
could decrease iteration numbers but lead to a significant increase in computation time. Therefore, for
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\operatorfont c}
, only incomplete Cholesky factorizations are utilized, while the full LU factorization of
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont cc}
is facilitated by an external package SuperLU_DIST
[
66
]
.

Solving eigenvalue problems is a crucial ingredient in our method, and we employ SLEPc
[
67
]
—a companion eigensolver package for PETSc—in the implementation. There are two different strategies in determining dimensions of local coarse and coarse-coarse spaces: (1) use predefined
L_{\operatorfont c}
and
L_{\operatorfont cc}
eigenvectors for all coarse and coarse-coarse elements; (2) set eigenvalue thresholds
B_{\operatorfont c}
and
B_{\operatorfont cc}
, solve enough candidate eigenvectors and then select eigenvectors whose corresponding eigenvalues are below
B_{\operatorfont c}
and
B_{\operatorfont cc}
accordingly. The first approach is relatively easy to implement, while the second is based on
Theorem

4.3
. The first strategy tends to result in large dimensions of
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont c}
and
\mathsf{A}_{\operatorfont cc}
but balances computing loads among all processes perfectly, while the second is the opposite. We will present a careful comparison of those two strategies later.

We choose the PETSc GMRES method with default parameters to solve the fine system
Eq.

6
with a different relative tolerance
10^{-6}
, and iteration counts are indicated by
\mathtt{iter}
. The HPC cluster in which we run the program is interconnected with an InfiniBand network, and each node is equipped with dual AMD
®
7452 CPUs (
64
CPU cores in total) and 256 GB of memory. We will frequently write the elapsed wall time
\mathtt{time}
and iteration count
\mathtt{iter}
of an experiment in a format of
\mathtt{time}(\mathtt{iter})
, where one effective digit is reserved in the unit of seconds for
\mathtt{time}
. Our codes are hosted on GitHub
2

{\operatorfont w}

\alpha\in\{{\operatorfont w},{\operatorfont o}\}
is stated as follows:

\phi\frac{\partial S_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\Div{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}=\widetilde{q}_{\alpha},

(18)

where
\phi
is the porosity of the reservoir rock, and for
\alpha\in\{{\operatorfont w},{\operatorfont o}\}
,
S_{\alpha}
is the saturation,
\bm{v}_{\alpha}
is the superficial Darcy velocity that is given by Darcy’s law,
\widetilde{q}_{\alpha}
is the external sources and sinks but divided by phase’s density. Darcy’s law for two-phase flows is expressed in the following form:

\bm{v}_{\alpha}=-\frac{k_{\alpha}^{\operatorfont r}}{\mu_{\alpha}}\mathbb{K}\nabla{p_{\alpha}}.

(19)

where
k_{\alpha}^{\operatorfont r}
is the relative permeability that depends on the saturation,
\mu_{\alpha}
is the viscosity, and
\mathbb{K}
is the orthotropic permeability of the reservoir rock, and
p_{\alpha}
is the pressure. Note that gravity is neglected in
Eq.

19
for brevity. The fact that the two fluids jointly fill the voids implies the relation

S_{\operatorfont w}+S_{\operatorfont o}=1.

(20)

For simplicity, we also neglect the capillary pressure and hence have

p_{\operatorfont o}-p_{\operatorfont w}=0.

(21)

Eqs.

18
,
19
,
20
and
21

p_{\operatorfont o}
and
S_{\operatorfont w}
as the two primary variables to cancel out other equations. By defining the total velocity

\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{\operatorfont w}+\bm{v}_{\operatorfont o}

and redefining
p\coloneqq p_{\operatorfont o}
,
S\coloneqq S_{\operatorfont w}
,
Eqs.

18
,
19
,
20
and
21
are reduced into

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=-\lambda(S)\mathbb{K}\nabla p,

(22)

\displaystyle\Div\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{q}\coloneqq\widetilde{q}_{\operatorfont w}+\widetilde{q}_{\operatorfont o},

(23)

\displaystyle\phi\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+\Div\left(f_{\operatorfont w}(S)\bm{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{q}_{\operatorfont w},

(24)

where
\lambda\coloneqq k_{\operatorfont w}^{\operatorfont r}/\mu_{\operatorfont w}+k_{\operatorfont o}^{\operatorfont r}/\mu_{\operatorfont o}
is called the total mobility, and
f_{\operatorfont w}\coloneqq\left(k_{\operatorfont w}^{\operatorfont r}/\mu_{\operatorfont w}\right)/\lambda

Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a spectral multigrid preconditioner for Darcy flow in high-contrast media. The core component of this preconditioner is to incorporate a sequence of nested multiscale subspaces which is crucial for improving the robustness of the preconditioner. These subspaces can be constructed recursively by solving carefully designed local spectral problems. The condition number of this preconditioner is estimated and rich typical numerical examples are provided to exhibit the robustness and scalability of the algorithm. Applications for incompressible two-phase flow problems are presented. We plan to study this preconditioner for more complicated underground flow problems such as block oil simulations.
