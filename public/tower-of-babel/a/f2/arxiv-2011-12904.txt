Connectedness of the Free Uniform Spanning Forest as a function of edge weights
Marcell Alexy, Márton Borbényi, András Imolay, Ádám Timár

Abstract
Let G G be the Cartesian product of a regular tree T T and a finite connected transitive graph H H . It is shown in [ 3 ] that the Free Uniform Spanning Forest ( 𝖥𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{FSF} ) of this graph may not be connected, but the dependence of this connectedness on H H remains somewhat mysterious. We study the case when a positive weight w w is put on the edges of the H H -copies in G G , and conjecture that the connectedness of the 𝖥𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{FSF} exhibits a phase transition. For large enough w w we show that the 𝖥𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{FSF} is connected, while for a large family of H H and T T , the 𝖥𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{FSF} is disconnected when w w is small (relying on [ 3 ] ). Finally, we prove that when H H is the graph of one edge, then for any w w , the 𝖥𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{FSF} is a single tree, and we give an explicit formula for the distribution of the distance between two points within the tree.

, in other words, the path between them uses
m
bags.

Lemma 2.10
.

For any integer
m\geq 2
,
q_{m}=K\left(\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}\right)^{m}
, where
K=\frac{d(2w+c)^{2}}{(2w+2c)(d-1)^{2}}
is a constant that does not depend on
m
.

Proof.

We call two positive sequences (
u_{n},\ v_{n}
) equivalent (
u_{n}\sim v_{n}
) if
\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}/v_{n}=1
.

We are going to prove, that
t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})
and
t(\hat{T}_{n})
are asymptotically the same as a constant times
a_{n}a_{n-1}
.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}a_{n-1}}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}.

Recall the following constants:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^{\prime}_{n}}{a_{n}}=c\ \ \text{and}\ \ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n-1}^{d-1}}{a_{n}}=s.

Using these, and Lemma
2.5
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}a_{n-1}}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}a_{n-1}}{2a_{n}a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{w}(a_{n}a^{\prime}_{n-1}+a^{\prime}_{n}a_{n-1})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2+\frac{1}{w}(\frac{a^{\prime}_{n-1}}{a_{n-1}}+\frac{a^{\prime}_{n}}{a_{n}})}=\frac{1}{2+\frac{2c}{w}}.

From Lemma
2.6
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(d-1)^{m-2}w\Big(2a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{w}a^{\prime}_{n-1}\Big)\Big(2a_{n-m}+\frac{1}{w}a^{\prime}_{n-m}\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Big(2a_{n-i}+\frac{1}{w}a^{\prime}_{n-i}\Big)^{d-2}}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}.

Here
\big(2a_{n-i}+\frac{1}{w}a^{\prime}_{n-i}\big)\sim a_{n-i}\big(2+\frac{c}{w}\big)
. Hence

t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})\sim wd(d-1)^{m-2}a_{n-1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}a_{n-i}^{d-2}\right)a_{n-m}\Big(2+\frac{c}{w}\Big)^{(d-2)m+2}.

Using the fact, that
a^{d-1}_{n-i}\sim sa_{n-i+1}
, one can prove easily by induction, that
a_{n-1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}a_{n-i}^{d-2}\right)a_{n-m}\sim a_{n-1}a_{n}s^{m}
. Thus, combining the two calculations,

\frac{t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}\sim wd(d-1)^{m-2}\Big(2+\frac{c}{w}\Big)^{(d-2)m+2}s^{m}.

Therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{m}(\hat{T}_{n})}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}=K(d-1)^{m}\Big(2+\frac{c}{w}\Big)^{(d-2)m}s^{m},

where

K=\frac{1}{2+\frac{2c}{w}}\cdot wd\frac{1}{(d-1)^{2}}\left(2+\frac{c}{w}\right)^{2}=\frac{d(2w+c)^{2}}{(2w+2c)(d-1)^{2}}.

It means, that
q_{m}=K\left((d-1)s(2+\frac{c}{w})^{d-2}\right)^{m}.
Using Lemma
2.7
, we know, that
s=\frac{c}{w\left(2+\frac{c}{w}\right)^{d-1}}
, thus
q_{m}=K\left(\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}\right)^{m}.

∎

Lemma 2.11
.

\mathbb{P}(\{(u,0),(u,1)\}\in\mathsf{FSF}_{w}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\square K_{2}))=q_{1}=\frac{(2w+c)c}{2w+2c}.

Proof.

From the
m=1
case of Lemma
2.6
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{1}(\hat{T}_{n})}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{w\left(2a_{n-1}+\frac{1}{w}a^{\prime}_{n-1}\right)^{d}}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{ws_{n}a_{n}a_{n-1}\left(2+\frac{c_{n-1}}{w}\right)^{d}}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}=ws\left(2+\frac{c}{w}\right)^{d}.

In the proof of Lemma
2.10
, we calculated

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}a_{n-1}}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}=\frac{1}{2+\frac{2c}{w}}.

Combining these and using Lemma
2.7
, we have

q_{1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{1}(\hat{T}_{n})}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}a_{n-1}}{t(\hat{T}_{n})}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{1}(\hat{T}_{n})}{a_{n}a_{n-1}}=\frac{1}{2+\frac{2c}{w}}\cdot c\left(2+\frac{c}{w}\right)=\frac{(2w+c)c}{2w+2c}.

∎

Lemma
2.10
gives us that the distance of two vertices in the same bag in the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
has a geometric distribution.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then
(u,0)
and
(u,1)
are in the same component of the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}\square K_{2}
with probability
1
.

Proof.

We want to prove that the path between
(u,0)
and
(u,1)
is almost surely finite, so
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q_{m}=1
.
From (
2.1
) we have
2wc+c^{2}=2w+2c-cd
, hence

\frac{(2w+c)c}{2w+2c}=\frac{2w+2c-cd}{2w+2c}=1-\frac{cd}{2w+2c},

and (
2.1
) can also be transformed to
2w+c=\frac{2w+c-(d-1)c}{c}
, thus

K=\frac{d(2w+c)^{2}}{(2w+2c)(d-1)^{2}}=\frac{d((2w+c)^{2}-(d-1)c(2w+c))}{(2w+2c)(d-1)^{2}c}=\frac{cd}{2w+2c}\cdot\frac{1-\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}}{\left(\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}\right)^{2}}.

Combining these with Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
2.11
, we have

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q_{m}=\frac{(2w+c)c}{2w+2c}+K\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}\right)^{m}=1-\frac{cd}{2w+2c}+K\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{(d-1)c}{2w+c}}=1.

∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
H
be an arbitrary finite connected graph and consider the weighted graph
G=\mathbb{T}^{d}\square wH
. If any pair
a,b
of vertices in the same bag belong to the same component of
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
almost surely, then the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
is almost surely connected.

Proof.

Take two adjacent bags. Let the set of edges between them called
E^{\prime}
. The event that at least one edge of
E^{\prime}
is in
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
is a cylinder event, for every graph
G_{n}
of an exhausting finite sequence for
G
this event has probability one to hold for the
\mathsf{UST}
. Hence in the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
there is an edge from
E^{\prime}
with probability one. Thus there are always two connected vertices in the two adjacent bags. By assumption, all vertices within a bag are in the same component, therefore all vertices in these two adjacent bags are in the same component. This is true for any two adjacent bags, thus for all edges in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Using countable intersection, we conclude that the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
of that graph is connected with probability one.
∎

Now we have everything, to prove the main result of this section:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

From Lemma
2.12
. and Lemma
2.13
. the statement follows.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

It is a natural question to ask whether this method can be generalized to other graphs instead of
K_{2}
. Unfortunately we strongly relied on the fact, that in
\hat{T}_{n}
a path between
(u,0)
and
(u,1)
looks quite nice, while if we change
K_{2}
to some larger graph then plenty of other options arise which we cannot handle with this enumerative method.

3
The general case, large and small weights

In this section we are going to prove Theorem
1.3
. The first part will follow from the next theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given an arbitrary
d>2
and a finite, regular, connected graph
H
, there is a
W>0
such that the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
of the graph
G=\mathbb{T}^{d}\square H
is almost surely connected for all
w>W
.

As before, denote by
T_{n}
the ball of radius
n
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Let
U
be the
central bag
of
T_{n}\square wH
: the bag that corresponds to the center of this ball.

Definition 3.2
.

A
trip
is a walk
(X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{T})
such that

X_{i}\in U
if and only if
i=1
or
T
.

Definition 3.3
.

Bag
D
is
memorable for a trip

(X_{1},...,X_{T})
, if the trip intersects
D
, and satisfies the following. If
\tau\in[1,T]
is the last step when
X_{\tau}\in D
, then for every bag

D^{\prime}
(
\not=U,D
) that separates
U
and
D
,
V(D^{\prime})\not=V(D^{\prime})\cap\{X_{\tau+1},...,X_{T}\}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
{\cal X}=(X_{1},...,X_{T})
be a walk in
T_{n}\square wH
, with
X_{1}
in the central bag
U
of
T_{n}\square wH
. Suppose that
{\cal X}^{\prime}=(X_{k},X_{k+1},...,X_{k^{\prime}})
is some subwalk which is a trip and intersects bag
D
. Assume that
D
is not memorable for
{\cal X}^{\prime}
. Then the loop-erasure of
(X_{1},...,X_{k^{\prime}})
does not intersect
D
.

Proof.

By our assumptions there exists a largest number
\tau
with
k<\tau<k^{\prime}
and
X_{\tau}\in D
.
Since
D
is not memorable, there exists a bag
D^{\prime}
that separates
U
and
D
, and with the property that
V(D^{\prime})=V(D^{\prime})\cap\{X_{\tau+1},...,X_{k^{\prime}}\}
. Let
t
be the first time after
\tau
that we enter

D^{\prime}
. Such a
t
exists, because
X_{\tau}\in D
and
X_{k^{\prime}}\in U
. Let
{\cal L}
be LE
(X_{1},...,X_{t})
.
If
{\cal L}\cap D=\emptyset
, then the claim is proved, because we do not visit
D
after
t>\tau
. Otherwise the first time that
{\cal L}
enters
D^{\prime}
is strictly before
t
(since
{\cal L}
has to enter
D^{\prime}
before entering
D
and reentering
D^{\prime}
at
X_{t}
). Let this first vertex of entrance be
v
.
By assumption on
{\cal X}^{\prime}
,
(X_{t},...,X_{k^{\prime}})
visits every vertex of
D^{\prime}
. Let
t^{\prime}\geq t
be the first time that
X_{t^{\prime}}=v
. Then the loop-erasure of
(X_{1},...,X_{t^{\prime}})
erases everything that happened after the first entrance to
D^{\prime}
at
v
. In particular, it erases every step in
D
before
t^{\prime}
, so LE
(X_{1},...,X_{t^{\prime}})\cap D=\emptyset
. Since
k^{\prime}>t^{\prime}
(
{\cal X}^{\prime}
is a trip), and no step after
t^{\prime}>\tau
is in
D
, the claim is proved.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a
W>0
such that
for any
\alpha>0
there is an
m
such that the following holds for every
w>W
.
Let
u\in U
,
n>m
and
{\cal X}=(X_{1},...,X_{T})
be a trip in
T_{n}\square wH

with

X_{1}=u
. Then
we have

\mathbb{P}({\cal X}\text{ has a memorable bag outside }T_{m}\square wH)<\alpha.

Proof.

Choose
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}
. Let
w>W
, where we specify
W
at the end of this paragraph.
If
(Z_{1},Z_{2},...)
is a random walk in
\mathbb{T}^{d}\square wH
started from a bag
B
, then
let
\lambda
be the first time when it exits
B
. If
w
was large enough, we have for any starting vertex
x=Z_{1}
and last vertex
y=Z_{\lambda}

\mathbb{P}(\{Z_{1},...,Z_{\lambda}\}=B\mid\ Z_{1}=x,\ Z_{\lambda}=y)>1-\varepsilon,

because the minimum over
x
and
y
of the probability on the left tends to
1
as
w
goes to infinity. Fix
W
so that the above inequality holds.

Fix bag
D
; we will use notation from Definition
3.3
. Let
t
be the first time after
\tau

that we enter
D^{\prime}
. Let
A_{x}
be the event that the random walk started from a point
x\in D^{\prime}
visits every vertex of
D^{\prime}
before leaving
D^{\prime}
. Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{x}
the distribution of a random walk
{\cal Y}=(Y_{1},...,Y_{R})
started from
x=Y_{1}
and stopped at the first entrance to
U
.
Let
B_{x}
be the event that
{\cal Y}
does not visit
D
. Then

\mathbb{P}((X_{t},...,X_{T})\in A_{x}|X_{t}=x)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(A_{x}|B_{x})=\mathbb{P}_{x}(A_{x})\geq 1-\varepsilon,

(3.1)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
B_{x}
is independent of
A_{x}
, because it only depends on the steps taken in the tree-coordinate and hence it is independent of the steps in the
H
-coordinates between two tree-coordinate steps. (To see this, note that the random walk path
(Y_{1},...,Y_{R})
by
\mathbb{P}_{x}
could be generated by first generating a suitable random walk path
{\cal T}
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and then adding a suitably chosen random number of random
H
-steps in between every consecutive pair of steps of
{\cal T}
, independently from each other and from
{\cal T}
.)
Since
x
was arbitrary, from (
3.1
) we obtain

\mathbb{P}((X_{t},...,X_{T})\in A_{X_{t}})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(3.2)

Let
U=D_{1},D_{2},...,D_{\ell}=D
be the ray of bags between
U
and
D
. Denote by
t_{i}
the first time that
{\cal X}
enters
D_{i}
after
\tau
and let
r_{i}
be the first time exiting
D_{i}
after
t_{i}
.
Finally, let
A^{i}
be the the event that
(X_{t_{i}},...,X_{T})
visits every vertex of
D_{i}
before leaving
D_{i}
, in other words,
\{X_{t_{i}},...,X_{r_{i}}\}=D_{i}
.
Note that, conditional on
\{X_{t_{i}}\}
and
\{X_{r_{i}}\}
, the events
\{A^{j}\}
are independent, hence from the uniform lower bound (
3.2
) we have:

\mathbb{P}\bigl((\exists i\in[2,...,\ell-1]:\,\,A^{i}\mid X_{t_{2}},...,X_{t_{l-1}},X_{r_{2}},...,X_{r_{l-1}}\bigr)\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\ell-2}.

Using the law of total probability

\mathbb{P}\bigl((\exists i\in[2,...,\ell-1]:\,\,A^{i}\bigr)\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\ell-2}.

We have just shown that
D
is memorable for
{\cal X}
with probability less than
\varepsilon^{\ell-2}
.
There are
d(d-1)^{s-1}
vertices of
T_{n}
with distance
s
from the root for all
1\leq s\leq n
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\cal X}\text{ has a memorable bag outside }T_{m}\square wH)\leq\sum_{B\not\in T_{m}\square wH}\mathbb{P}(B\text{ is memorable for }{\cal X})\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=m+1}^{n}\varepsilon^{s-2}d(d-1)^{s-1}=\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\sum_{s=m+1}^{n}(\varepsilon(d-1))^{s-1}<\frac{d}{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon(d-1))^{m}\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon(d-1)}.

By definition
\varepsilon(d-1)<1
. The number on the right hand side does not depend on
n
, thus we can choose
m
big enough so that it is less than
\alpha
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
W
be as in Lemma
3.5
.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
be arbitrary.
We want to prove that if
w>W
then for all vertices
a,b
of
G
, there exists an
m
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\text{LERW}_{T_{n}\square wH}(a\to b)\text{ leaves }T_{m}\square wH)\leq\alpha_{0}.

This is equivalent with the definition of connectedness of the
\mathsf{FSF}_{w}
in
{\mathbb{T}^{d}\square H}
, because by Wilson’s algorithm
\text{LERW}_{T_{n}\square wH}(a\to b)
has the same distribution as the path between
a
and
b
in
\mathsf{UST}(T_{n}\square wH)
.
By Lemma
2.13
., one may assume that
a
and
b
are in the same bag,
U
.
Define
h
as the minimum of the probability over all pairs
x\not=y\in U
that random walk in
T_{n}\square wH
started from
x
hits
y
before leaving
U
.
Let
k
be a positive integer, chosen to satisfy
\mathbb{P}(\text{Geom}(h)\geq k)<\alpha_{0}/2
, where
\text{Geom}(h)
denotes a geometric random variable of parameter
h
.

Choose
m
as in Lemma
3.5
, with
\alpha:=\alpha_{0}/2k
. Denote by
{\cal X}
a random walk started from
a
in
T_{n}\square wH
and stopped when first hitting
b
.
One can construct
{\cal X}
as follows. Start random walk from
a
. If we hit
b
before leaving
U
, we are finished, otherwise let
X_{s_{1}}
be the last step of this walk in
U
before first leaving
U
. Then consider the trip
(X_{s_{1}},...,X_{t_{1}})
. From the last vertex
X_{t_{1}}\in U
of this trip,
continue the random walk until either hitting
b
or exiting
U
. The probability of the former is at least
h
;
otherwise let
X_{s_{2}}
be the last vertex in
U
before leaving
U
, and starting from this vertex generate
the trip

(X_{s_{2}},...,X_{t_{2}})
. Continue similarly, until at some point we hit
b
and at that point the construction of
{\cal X}
is finished. We see that after the end of every trip
we had probability at least

h
to hit
b
, hence the total number of trips needed is stochastically dominated by a geometric random
variable of parameter
h
. Let
J
be such a random variable. If
\text{LE}({\cal X})
intersects bag
D
, then
D
is memorable for one of the sub-trips of
{\cal X}
by Proposition
3.4
. The probability that a trip has a memorable bag outside
of
T_{m}\square wH
is less than
\alpha
by Lemma
3.5
.
A union bound gives us

\mathbb{P}(\text{LE}({\cal X})\text{ leaves }T_{m}\square cH)<\mathbb{P}(J>k)+k\alpha\leq\alpha_{0},

completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The first part of the theorem is essentially Theorem
3.1
.

For the second part, the case of small
w
, we have the same conditions on the graph as in the unweighted case of Theorem 1.1 in
[
3
]
and
one could repeat the arguments therein, with minor modifications which we sketch next. The result of Section 2 about random walk on the tree is obviously unchanged, while Lemma 3.1 also remains valid, with a different constant
b
, for the following reason. Replace
2k^{6}
in (3.2) by
2k^{6}/w
. Then the entire paragraph containing (3.2) remains valid if we change every occurrence of
d
to
wd
, and that of
2k^{6}
to
2k^{6}/w
. The rest of the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
3
]
goes through without any change. The “second ingredient”, as explained after the proof of Lemma 3.1, is based on the fact that random walk does not spend much time in a bag. The key stochastic domination results are even “more true” than in
[
3
]
when we have small weights on the
H
-edges, while the parts about random walk within a bag, such as Lemma 3.2,
remain unchanged.
The rest of the proof is automatically adapted to our setting.
∎
