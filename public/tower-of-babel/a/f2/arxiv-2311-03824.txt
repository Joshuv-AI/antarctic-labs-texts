A Fast Algorithm for Low Rank + Sparse column-wise Compressive Sensing
Silpa Babu, Namrata Vaswani

Abstract
This paper focuses studies the following low rank + sparse (LR+S) column-wise compressive sensing problem. We aim to recover an n × q n\times q matrix, 𝑿 ∗ = [ 𝒙 1 ∗ , 𝒙 2 ∗ , ⋯ , 𝒙 q ∗ ] \bm{X}^{*}=[\bm{x}_{1}^{*},\bm{x}_{2}^{*},\cdots,\bm{x}_{q}^{*}] from m m independent linear projections of each of its q q columns, given by 𝒚 k := 𝑨 k ​ 𝒙 k ∗ \bm{y}_{k}:=\bm{A}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}^{*} , k ∈ [ q ] k\in[q] . Here, 𝒚 k \bm{y}_{k} is an m m -length vector with m < n m<n . We assume that the matrix 𝑿 ∗ \bm{X}^{*} can be decomposed as 𝑿 ∗ = 𝑳 ∗ + 𝑺 ∗ \bm{X}^{*}=\bm{L}^{*}+\bm{S}^{*} , where 𝑳 ∗ \bm{L}^{*} is a low rank matrix of rank r << min ⁡ ( n , q ) r<<\min(n,q) and 𝑺 ∗ \bm{S}^{*} is a sparse matrix. Each column of 𝑺 \bm{S} contains ρ \rho non-zero entries. The matrices 𝑨 k \bm{A}_{k} are known and mutually independent for different k k . To address this recovery problem, we propose a novel fast GD-based solution called AltGDmin-LR+S, which is memory and communication efficient. We numerically evaluate its performance by conducting a detailed simulation-based study.

I-A

Problem and Notation

We study the problem of recovering an
n\times q
matrix
\bm{X}^{*}
from undersampled measurements of each of its columns, i.e., from

\bm{y}_{k}=\bm{A}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}^{*},\hskip 14.22636ptk\in[q]

Here,
\bm{A}_{k}
is a known matrix of dimension
m\times n
, with
m<n
. The different
\bm{A}_{k}
s are i.i.d. and each is a dense matrix: either a random Gaussian matrix (each entry i.i.d. standard Gaussian) or a random Fourier matrix (a random subset of rows of the discrete Fourier transform matrix).
The vectors
\bm{y}_{k}
and
\bm{x}^{*}_{k}
denote the
k
-th column of matrix
\bm{Y}
and
\bm{X}^{*}
, respectively.

We assume that
\bm{X}^{*}=\bm{L}^{*}+\bm{S}^{*}
, where
\bm{L}^{*}
is a low rank matrix and
\bm{S}^{*}
is a sparse matrix. The rank of matrix
\bm{L}^{*}
is denoted by
r
, and we assume
r\ll\min(n,q)
(low-rank). Each column of
\bm{S}^{*}
has
\rho
non-zero entries. There is no bound on the magnitude of the non-zero entries of matrix
\bm{S}^{*}
. Therefore, the undersampled Gaussian measurements can be expressed as

\bm{y}_{k}=\bm{A}_{k}(\bm{l}^{*}_{k}+\bm{s}^{*}_{k}),\ \ k\in[q]

where
\bm{l}^{*}_{k}
and
\bm{s}^{*}_{k}
is the k-th column of matrix
\bm{L}^{*}
and
\bm{S}^{*}
respectively.
The above problem occurs in dynamic MRI (
\bm{A}_{k}
s are a subset of rows of the 2D-DFT matrix) or in sketching (
\bm{A}_{k}
’s are random Gaussian)
[
5
]

The reduced (rank
r
) Singular Value Decomposition of matrix
\bm{L}^{*}
can be represented as
{\bm{U}}^{*}{\bm{\Sigma}^{*}}{\bm{V}}^{*}
. The matrix
\bm{L}^{*}
can be written as

\bm{L}^{*}={\bm{U}}^{*}\bm{B}^{*}

where
{\bm{U}}^{*}
is an
n\times r
matrix with orthonormal columns, and
\bm{B}^{*}
is an
r\times q
matrix given by
\bm{B}^{*}={\bm{\Sigma}^{*}}{\bm{V}}^{*}
. Consequently, each column of
\bm{X}^{*}
is given by
\bm{x}^{*}_{k}={\bm{U}}^{*}\bm{b}^{*}_{k}+\bm{s}^{*}_{k}
.
Hence the undersampled Gaussian measurements can be written as

\bm{y}_{k}=\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}^{*}\bm{b}^{*}_{k}+\bm{s}^{*}_{k}),\hskip 14.22636ptk\in[q]

We use
\|.\|
without a subscript denotes the
l_{2}
norm of a vector or the induced
l_{2}
norm of a matrix, and we use
\|.\|_{F}
to denote the matrix Frobenius norm. For a tall matrix

\bm{M}^{\dagger}:=(\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{M}^{\top}
denotes its Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse.

We studied the
\bm{S}^{*}=0
special case of the above problem in our recent work
[
6
,
7
]
.
Recall that

\bm{L}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle SVD}}{{=}}{\bm{U}}^{*}{}{\bm{\Sigma}^{*}}{\bm{V}}^{*}{}:={\bm{U}}^{*}{}{\bm{B}^{*}}

denote its reduced (rank
r
) SVD, and
\kappa:={\sigma_{\max}^{*}}/{\sigma_{\min}^{*}}
the condition number of
{\bm{\Sigma}^{*}}
.
Notice that our problem is asymmetric across rows and columns. Also, each measurement

is a global functions of column
\bm{x}^{*}_{k}
, but not of the entire matrix. Hence, we need the following assumption, which is a subset of the assumptions used in the LR matrix completion literature.

Assumption 1.1

We assume that
\max_{k}\|\bm{b}^{*}_{k}\|\leq\mu\sqrt{r/q}{{\sigma_{\max}^{*}}}^{2}

Related Work

To our best knowledge, the above problem has only been studied in the dynamic MRI setting

While the above problem has not been explored in the theoretical or signal processing literature, related LR+S problems have been extensively studied. The robust PCA problem is the above problem with

{\bm{X}^{*}}
. This can be understood as the above problem with

being a 1-0 matrix with exactly one 1 in each row. Provably correct convex
[
1

13
]
, alternating minimization
[
14
]
, and GD-based solutions to robust PCA and robust LRMC
[
15
,
16
]
have been developed in the literature. The work of
[
15
]
factors
\bm{L}
as
\bm{L}={\bm{U}}\bm{B}
and uses an alternating GD (AltGD) algorithm that alternatively updates
{\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S}
. Since
\bm{L}={\bm{U}}\bm{B}={\bm{U}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{R}}^{-1}\bm{B}

r\times r

(the decomposition is not unique), the alternating GD algorithm requires adding a term to the cost function that ensures the norms of
{\bm{U}}
and
\bm{B}
are balanced. Thus, this work develops an AltGD algorithm that minimizes

Another related problem is that of recovering
\bm{X}^{*}:=\bm{L}^{*}+\bm{S}^{*}
from dense linear projections of the entire matrix
{\bm{X}^{*}}
, i.e., from
\bm{y}_{j}:=\mathcal{A}_{j}({\bm{X}^{*}})
,
j=1,2,\dots,j_{max}
(this can be referred as low-rank plus sparse compressive sensing)
[
17

. Here

with
\bm{A}_{j}
being random Gaussian. Notice that this problem is different from ours where we have dense linear projections of each column of
{\bm{X}^{*}}
but not of the entire matrix.

In our recent work
[
6
,
7
,
18
]
, we studied the LR column-wise compressive sensing (LRCCS) problem which is the above problem with
\bm{S}^{*}=\bm{0}
. This is also where we introduced the alternating GD and minimization (AltGDmin) algorithm idea. In
[
6

[
7

18

Via extensive experiments on real MRI image sequences (with simulated Cartesian and radial sampling) and on real scanner data, we showed that AltGDmin-MRI is both significantly faster and more accurate than many state-of-the-art approaches from MRI literature.

I-C

Contributions

We develop a novel GD-based algorithm called Alternating GD and minimization or AltGDmin for solving the above LR+S column-wise compressive sensing problem (LR+S-CCS) and argue why it is both fast and memory and communication-efficient. The communication-efficiency claim assumes a federated setting in which subsets of
\bm{y}_{k},\bm{A}_{k}
are available at different distributed nodes. Using extensive simulation experiments, we also numerically evaluate the sample complexity (value of
m
needed for a given
n
,
q
,
r
,
\rho
) to guarantee algorithm convergence.

The AltGDmin algorithmic framework was first introduced for solving the LRCCS problem in
[
6
]
. Its idea is to split the unknown variables into two parts,
{\bm{Z}}_{a},{\bm{Z}}_{b}
and alternatively update each of them using GD or projected GD for
{\bm{Z}}_{a}
and minimization for
{\bm{Z}}_{b}
. We let
{\bm{Z}}_{b}
be the subset of variables for which the minimization can be “decoupled”, i.e., subsets of
{\bm{Z}}_{b}
are functions of only a subset of
\bm{Y}
.
In problems such as LRCCS or the above LR+S-CCS problem, the decoupling is column-wise. Assume a factored representation of
\bm{L}
, i.e.,
\bm{L}={\bm{U}}\bm{B}
where
{\bm{U}}
and
\bm{B}
are matrices with
r
columns and rows respectively.
In our current problem,
{\bm{Z}}_{b}\equiv\{\bm{B},\bm{S}\}
and
{\bm{Z}}_{a}={\bm{U}}
. Thus, our algorithm alternates between a projected GD step for updating
{\bm{U}}
and a minimization step for updating
\bm{B}
and
\bm{S}
.

Because of the column-wise decoupling, the minimization step is about as fast as the GD step. As we argue later, the per-iteration time complexity is
mqnr
, memory complexity is
\max(n,q)\cdot\max(r,\rho)
and the communication complexity is
nr
per node. Memory and communication cost wise our algorithm is much better than projected GD. Time-wise it is faster than AltMin which solves a minimization problem also for updating
{\bm{U}}
. This problem is coupled across all columns and hence is a much more expensive problem to fully re-solve at each algorithm iteration. Replacing this by a single GD update is what makes AltGDmin much faster than AltMin.

To our knowledge, this work is the first to do a detailed simulation-based study of the above LR+S column-wise compressive sensing problem. As is well known from older compressive sensing literature for sparse recovery problems, as well as for LRCCS, algorithms developed originally for the random Gaussian
\bm{A}_{k}
setting also often work with simple modifications for solving the MRI reconstruction problem, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
]
and
[
21
]
for CS, and
[
6
]
and
[
22
]
for LRCCS.

II
AltGDmin for L+S column-wise compressive sensing (L+S-CCS)

We are interested in developing a fast gradient descent (GD) based algorithm to find matrices
\bm{L}
and
\bm{S}
that minimize the cost function:

f(\bm{L},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\|\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}(\bm{l}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})\|^{2}

subject to the constraints (i) matrix
\bm{L}
has rank
r
or less, (ii) each column of matrix
\bm{S}
is
\rho
sparse. This means that the number of non-zero entries in each column
\bm{s}_{k}
is at most
\rho
. As previously mentioned, we can impose the rank constraint implicitly by expressing the matrix
\bm{L}
as
\bm{L}={\bm{U}}\bm{B}
where
{\bm{U}}
and
\bm{B}
are
n\times r
and
r\times q
matrices. The cost function can then be written as:

f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\|\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}\bm{b}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})\|^{2}

Our objective is to find matrices
{\bm{U}}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{S}
that minimize the cost function
f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})
while satisfying the constraint that each column of
\bm{S}
is
\rho
sparse.

II-A

altGDmin-L+S iteration

The gradient of the cost function with respect to
{\bm{U}}
is given by
\nabla_{U}f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})

\nabla_{U}f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}(\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}\bm{b}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})-\bm{y}_{k})\bm{b}_{k}^{\top}.

At each new iteration, the following steps are performed:

•

The matrix
{\bm{U}}
is updated using projected gradient descent.

•

For a given

, the matrices

and
\bm{B}
are updated by using minimization, as explained in detail in Section
II-B

These steps iteratively update

,

II-B

Minimization: Updating S and B for a given U

For a given
{\bm{U}}
, we need to find
\bm{s}_{k}
and
\bm{b}_{k}
that minimize
f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\|\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}\bm{b}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})\|^{2}
. Observe that
\bm{s}_{k},\bm{b}_{k}
appear only in the
k
-th term. Thus,

\min_{\bm{B},\bm{S}}f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\min_{\bm{s}_{k},\bm{b}_{k}}\|\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}\bm{b}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})\|^{2}

This means that the minimization problem can be decoupled across each columns, making it much faster.
Moreover, notice that, for a given
s_{k}
, the recovery of
b_{k}
is a standard least squares (LS) problem with a closed-form solution that can be written in terms of
\bm{s}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{k}=(\bm{A}_{k}{\bm{U}})^{\dagger}(\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}\bm{s}_{k})

(1)

Recall here that
\bm{M}^{\dagger}=(\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{M}^{\top}
. We can substitute this for
\bm{b}_{k}
and hence get a minimization over only the
\bm{s}_{k}
s, i.e.,

\sum_{k=1}^{q}\min_{\bm{s}_{k},\bm{b}_{k}}\|\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}({\bm{U}}\bm{b}_{k}+\bm{s}_{k})\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\min_{\bm{s}_{k}}\|\bm{z}_{k}-\bm{M}_{k}\bm{s}_{k}\|^{2}

where
\bm{z}_{k}:=\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}{\bm{U}}(\bm{A}_{k}{\bm{U}})^{\dagger}\bm{y}_{k}
and
\bm{M}_{k}:=\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}{\bm{U}}(\bm{A}_{k}{\bm{U}})^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{k}
. In summary,

\min_{\bm{B},\bm{S}}f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\min_{\bm{s}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{s}_{k}),\ \mathcal{L}(\bm{s}_{k}):=\|\bm{z}_{k}-\bm{M}_{k}\bm{s}_{k}\|^{2}

with
\bm{z}_{k},\bm{M}_{k}
as given above. Thus, we have simplified the min over
\bm{B},\bm{S}
into a problem of solving
q
individual problems, each of which is a standard sparse recovery problem,
\min_{\bm{s}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{s}_{k})
.
Various algorithms can be used to solve the sparse recovery problem. In this paper, we choose to use the Iterative Hard Thresholding (IHT) algorithm provided in
[
23

due to simplicity and efficiency.

II-C

altGDmin-L+S initialization

Since the cost function
f({\bm{U}},\bm{B},\bm{S})
is non-convex, it needs a careful initialization. As in all previous work on iterative algorithms for the most related L+S problem, robust PCA, we initialize
\bm{S}
first while letting
\bm{B}=\bm{0}
.
The reason this is done is there is no upper bound on entries of the sparse matrix which usually models large magnitude, but infrequently occurring, outlier entries. On the other hand, when we assume that the condition number of
\bm{L}
is a numerical constant, we are implicitly assuming an upper bound on the entries of
\bm{L}
.
Also, as noted earlier, the recovery of
\bm{s}_{k}
’s is decoupled across columns.

Given an initialize estimate of
\bm{S}
, we initialize
{\bm{U}}
by a standard spectral initialization approach: compute
{\bm{U}}
as the top
r
singular vectors of the matrix

\bm{L}_{0}:=[\bm{A}_{1}^{\top}(\bm{y}_{1}-\bm{A}_{1}\bm{s}_{1}),...,\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}(\bm{y}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}\bm{s}_{k}),...,\bm{A}_{q}^{\top}(\bm{y}_{q}-\bm{A}_{q}\bm{s}_{q})]

II-E

Time, Communication, and Memory Complexity

II-E
1
Time Complexity

The time complexity of the IHT algorithm for recovering a vector
\bm{s}
in the initialization is
O(mn)
per iteration, as previously explained. Consequently, the time complexity of the IHT algorithm for updating the entire matrix
\bm{S}
is
O(\tau_{0max}mnq)
. The initialization step requires a time complexity of
mqn
for computing
\bm{L}_{0}
.
The time needed for computing the
r
-SVD of
\bm{L}_{0}
is given by
nqr\times
(number of iterations of power method).
Summing up the individual time complexities mentioned above, the time complexity of the initialization step is given by
O(\tau_{0max}mqn+nqr\times
(number of iterations of power method)
+mqn)
. However, since the dominant term in this expression is
O(\tau_{0max}mqn)
, the overall time complexity of initialization is
O(\tau_{0max}mqn)
.

The computational time required for updating each column of
\bm{S}
using IHT is
O(mn)
per iteration (explained earlier). The time complexity for
\bm{S}
update using IHT algorithm is
O(\tau_{max}mqn)
, where
\tau_{max}
is the maximum number of iterations in the IHT algorithm.
The update of columns of
\bm{B}
by LS also needs time
O(mnqr)
.
One gradient computation with respect to
{\bm{U}}
needs time
O(mqnr)
. The QR decomposition requires time
nr^{2}
.
We need to repeat these steps
T_{max}
times. Thus, the total time complexity of altGDmin-L+S iterations is
O((mnqr+nr^{2}+mnqr+\tau_{max}mqn)T_{max})=O(mnqrT_{max})
.

Therefore, the total time complexity of the altGDmin-L+S algorithm is
O(mnqrT_{max})
.

q
columns, the total memory required to store
\bm{S}
is
2\rho q
.
The memory required to store
n\times r
matrix
{\bm{U}}
and
r\times q
matrix
\bm{B}
is
nr
and
qr
. In comparison, storing the entire matrix
\bm{X}
would require memory of
nq
. Therefore, by storing
\bm{S}
,
{\bm{U}}
, and
\bm{B}
instead of the full matrix
\bm{X}
, the algorithm can significantly reduce the memory requirements. The memory complexity of storing
{\bm{U}}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{S}
is given by
\max(nr,qr,q\rho)
.

II-F

Settings parameters automatically

In real-world applications, the rank
r
of matrix
{\bm{L}^{*}}
and the exact sparsity of each column in matrix
\bm{S}
are unknown. To estimate a suitable value for
r
, the ”
b\%
energy threshold” method can be employed on the singular values of the initial estimate of matrix
\bm{L}
denoted as
\bm{L}_{0}
. Additionally, the estimated value of
r
should be sufficiently less than the minimum of
n
and
q
to ensure a low-rank property. To determine
r
, we perform the ”
b\%
energy threshold” on the first
{\min}\left(\frac{n}{10},\frac{q}{10},\frac{m}{10}\right)
singular values. In our experiments, a
65\%
energy threshold was used.
In practical scenarios, the exact value of
\rho
(the sparsity level per column) is often unknown. In many cases the maximum possible nonzero entries (upper bound on
\rho
), denoted
\rho_{\max}
is known. We assume this in our code and experiments.
The maximum number of iterations of the IHT algorithm in the initialization
\tau_{0max}
is set to
10
.

For the altGDmin-L+S Algorithm, the maximum number of iterations
T_{max}
is set to
200
. The step size
\eta
for updating the matrix
{\bm{U}}
is set to
\frac{0.14}{\|\nabla_{U}f({\bm{U}}_{0},\bm{B}_{1},\bm{S}_{1})\|}
, where
{\bm{U}}_{0}
is the initial estimate,
\bm{B}_{1}
and
\bm{S}_{1}
are the estimates in the first AltGDmin-L+S iteration. Assuming that the gradient norm decreases over iterations, this implies that at any iteration
t
, we have
\eta\nabla_{U}f({\bm{U}}_{t},\bm{B}_{t},\bm{S}_{t})<1
. The maximum number of iterations for the IHT algorithm, denoted as
\tau_{max}
, used for updating
\bm{S}
is set to
3
.

IV
Conclusions

We developed a fast, memory and communication-efficient gradient descent (GD) based recovery algorithm,
called altGDmin-L+S for the LR+S-CCS problem. Through simulations, we have shown that altGDmin-L+S achieves successful recovery of simulated data from their sketches. In future work, we plan to evaluate the performance of altGDmin-L+S in real-time video applications.
