Understanding Overparameterization in Generative Adversarial Networks
Yogesh Balaji, Mohammadmahdi Sajedi, Neha Mukund Kalibhat, Mucong Ding, Dominik Stöger, Mahdi Soltanolkotabi, Soheil Feizi

Abstract
A broad class of unsupervised deep learning methods such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) involve training of overparameterized models where the number of parameters of the model exceeds a certain threshold. Indeed, most successful GANs used in practice are trained using overparameterized generator and discriminator networks, both in terms of depth and width. A large body of work in supervised learning have shown the importance of model overparameterization in the convergence of the gradient descent (GD) to globally optimal solutions. In contrast, the unsupervised setting and GANs in particular involve non-convex concave mini-max optimization problems that are often trained using Gradient Descent/Ascent (GDA). The role and benefits of model overparameterization in the convergence of GDA to a global saddle point in non-convex concave problems is far less understood. In this work, we present a comprehensive analysis of the importance of model overparameterization in GANs both theoretically and empirically. We theoretically show that in an overparameterized GAN model with a 1 1 -layer neural network generator and a linear discriminator, GDA converges to a global saddle point of the underlying non-convex concave min-max problem. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result for global convergence of GDA in such settings. Our theory is based on a more general result that holds for a broader class of nonlinear generators and discriminators that obey certain assumptions (including deeper generators and random feature discriminators). Our theory utilizes and builds upon a novel connection with the convergence analysis of linear time-varying dynamical systems which may have broader implications for understanding the convergence behavior of GDA for non-convex concave problems involving overparameterized models. We also empirically study the role of model overparameterization in GANs using several large-scale experiments on CIFAR-10 and Celeb-A datasets. Our experiments show that overparameterization improves the quality of generated samples across various model architectures and datasets. Remarkably, we observe that overparameterization leads to faster and more stable convergence behavior of GDA across the board.

Introduction

In recent years, we have witnessed tremendous progress in deep generative modeling with some state-of-the-art models capable of generating photo-realistic images of objects and scenes
(
Brock et al. 2019
;
Karras et al. 2019
;
Clark et al. 2019
)
. Three prominent classes of deep generative models include GANs
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, VAEs
(
Kingma & Welling 2014
)
and normalizing flows
(
Dinh et al. 2017
)
. Of these, GANs remain a popular choice for data synthesis especially in the image domain. GANs are based on a two player
min-max
game between a generator network that generates samples from a distribution, and a critic (discriminator) network that discriminates real distribution from the generated one. The networks are optimized using Gradient Descent/Ascent (GDA) to reach a saddle-point of the min-max optimization problem.

One of the key factors that has contributed to the successful training of GANs is model
overparameterization
, defined based on the model parameters count. By increasing the complexity of discriminator and generator networks, both in depth and width, recent papers show that GANs can achieve photo-realistic image and video synthesis
(
Brock et al. 2019
;
Clark et al. 2019
;
Karras et al. 2019
)
. While these works empirically demonstrate some benefits of
overparameterization
, there is lack of a rigorous study explaining this phenomena. In this work, we attempt to provide a comprehensive understanding of the role of
overparameterization
in GANs, both theoretically and empirically. We note that while
overparameterization
is a key factor in training successful GANs, other factors such as generator and discriminator architectures, regularization functions and model hyperparameters have to be taken into account as well to improve the performance of GANs.

Recently, there has been a large body of work in
supervised
learning (e.g. regression or classification problems) studying the importance of model overparameterization in gradient descent (GD)’s convergence to globally optimal solutions
(
Soltanolkotabi et al. 2018
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2019
;
Oymak & Soltanolkotabi 2019
;
Zou & Gu 2019
;
Oymak et al. 2019
)
. A key observation in these works is that, under some conditions, overparameterized models experience
lazy training

(
Chizat et al. 2019
)
where optimal model parameters computed by GD remain close to a randomly initialized model. Thus, using a linear approximation of the model in the parameter space, one can show the global convergence of GD in such minimization problems.

In contrast, training GANs often involves solving a non-convex concave
min-max
optimization problem that fundamentally differs from a single minimization problem of classification/regression. The key question is whether overparameterized GANs also experience lazy training in the sense that overparameterized generator and discriminator networks remain sufficiently close to their initializations. This may then lead to a general theory of global convergence of GDA for such overparameterized non-convex concave min-max problems.

In this paper we first theoretically study the role of overparameterization for a GAN model with a
1
-hidden layer generator and a linear discriminator. We study two optimization procedures to solve this problem: (i) using a conventional training procedure in GANs based on GDA in which generator and discriminator networks perform simultaneous steps of gradient descent to optimize their respective models, (ii) using GD to optimize generator’s parameters for the optimal discriminator. The latter case corresponds to taking a sufficiently large number of gradient ascent steps to update discriminator’s parameters for each GD step of the generator.
In both cases, our results show that in an overparameterized regime, the GAN optimization converges to a global solution. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result showing the global convergence of GDA in such settings. While in our results we focus on one-hidden layer generators and linear discriminators, our theory is based on analyzing a general class of min-max optimization problems which can be used to study a much broader class of generators and discriminators potentially including deep generators and deep random feature-based discriminators. A key component of our analysis is a novel connection to exponential stability of non-symmetric time varying dynamical systems in control theory which may have broader implications for theoretical analysis of GAN’s training. Ideas from control theory have also been used for understanding and improving training dynamics of GANs in
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
An et al. 2018
)
.

Having analyzed overparameterized GANs for relatively simple models, we next provide a comprehensive empirical study of this problem for practical GANs such as DCGAN
(
Radford et al. 2016
)
and ResNet GAN
(
Gulrajani et al. 2017
)
trained on CIFAR-10 and Celeb-A datasets. For example, the benefit of overparamterization in training DCGANs on CIFAR-10 is illustrated in Figure
1
. We have three key observations: (i) as the model becomes more overparameterized (e.g. using wider networks), the
training
FID scores that measure the training error, decrease. This phenomenon has been observed in other studies as well
(
Brock et al. 2019
)
. (ii) overparameterization does not hurt the
test
FID scores (i.e. the generalization gap remains small). This improved test-time performance can also be seen qualitatively in the center panel of Figure
1
, where overparameterized models produce samples of improved quality. (iii) Remarkably, overparameterized GANs, with a lot of parameters to optimize over, have significantly improved convergence behavior of GDA, both in terms of rate and stability, compared to small GAN models (see the right panel of Figure
1
).

In summary, in this paper

•

We provide the first theoretical guarantee of simultaneous GDA’s global convergence for an overparameterized GAN with one-hidden neural network generator and a linear discriminator (Theorem
2.1
).

•

By establishing connections with linear time-varying dynamical systems, we provide a theoretical framework to analyze simultaneous GDA’s global convergence for a general overparameterized GAN (including deeper generators and random feature discriminators), under some general conditions (Theorems
2.3
and
A.4

•

We provide a comprehensive empirical study of the role of model overparameterization in GANs using several large-scale experiments on CIFAR-10 and Celeb-A datasets. We observe overparameterization improves GANs’ training error, generalization error, sample qualities as well as the convergence rate and stability of GDA.

Theoretical results

2.1
Problem formulation

Given
n
data points of the form
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the goal of GAN’s training is to find a generator that can mimic sampling from the same distribution as the training data. More specifically, the goal is to find a generator mapping
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(z):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, parameterized by
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, so that
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{1}),\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{n})
with
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
generated i.i.d. according to
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d})
has a similar empirical distribution to
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
1
1

1

In general, the number of observed and generated samples can be different. However, in practical GAN implementations, batch sizes of observed and generated samples are usually the same. Thus, for simplicity, we make this assumption in our setup.
. To measure the discrepancy between the data points and the GAN outputs, one typically uses a discriminator mapping
\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
parameterized with
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{p}}
. The overall training approach takes the form of the following min-max optimization problem which minimizes the worst-case discrepancy detected by the discriminator

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\max_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{x}_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}}(\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{i}))+\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}).

(1)

Here,
\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})
is a regularizer that typically ensures the discriminator is Lipschitz. This formulation mimics the popular Wasserstein GAN
(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
(or, IPM GAN) formulations. This optimization problem is typically solved by running Gradient Descent Ascent (GDA) on the minimization/maximization variables.

The generator and discriminator mappings
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
used in practice are often deep neural networks. Thus, the min-max optimization problem above is highly nonlinear and non-convex concave. Saddle point optimization is a classical and fundamental problem in game theory
(
Von Neumann & Morgenstern 1953
)
and control
(
Gutman 1979
)
. However, most of the classical results apply to the convex-concave case
(
Arrow et al. 1958
)
while the saddle point optimization of GANs is often
non convex-concave
. If GDA converges to the global (local) saddle points, we say it is globally (locally) stable. For a general min-max optimization, however, GDA can be trapped in a loop or even diverge. Except in some special cases (e.g.
(
Feizi et al. 2018
)
for a quadratic GAN formulation or
(
Lei et al. 2019
)
for the under-parametrized setup when the generator is a one-layer network), GDA is not globally stable for GANs in general
(
Nagarajan & Kolter 2017
;
Mescheder et al. 2018
;
Adolphs et al. 2019
;
Mescheder et al. 2017
;
Daskalakis et al. 2020
)
.

None of these works, however, study the role of model overparameterization in the global/local convergence (stability) of GDA. In particular, it has been empirically observed (as we also demonstrate in this paper) that when the generator/discriminator contain a large number of parameters (i.e. are sufficiently overparameterized) GDA does indeed find (near) globally optimal solutions. In this section we wish to demystify this phenomenon from a theoretical perspective.

2.2
Definition of Model Overparameterization

In this paper, we use
overparameterization
in the context of model parameters count. Informally speaking, overparameterized models have large number of parameters, that is we assume that the number of model parameters is sufficiently large. In specific problem setups of Section
2
, we precisely compute thresholds where the number of model parameters should exceed in order to observe nice convergence properties of GDA. Note that the definition of
overparameterization
based on model parameters count is related, but distinct from the complexity of the hypothesis class. For instance, in our empirical studies, when we say we
overparameterize
a neural network, we fix the number of layers in the neural network and increase the hidden dimensions. Our definition does not include the case where the number of layers also increases, which forms a different hypothesis class.

2.3
Results for one-hidden layer generators and random discriminators

In this section, we discuss our main results on the convergence of gradient based algorithms when training GANs in the overparameterized regime. We focus on the case where the generator takes the form of a single hidden-layer ReLU network with
d
inputs,
k
hidden units, and
m
outputs. Specifically,
\mathcal{G}\left(\bm{z}\right)=\bm{V}\cdot\text{ReLU}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}\right)
with
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
denoting the input-to-hidden and hidden-to-output weights. We also consider a linear discriminator of the form
\mathcal{D}(\bm{x})=\bm{d}^{T}\bm{x}
with an
\ell_{2}
regularizer on the weights i.e.
\mathcal{R}(\bm{d})=-\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}/2
. The overall min-max optimization problem (equation
1
) takes the form

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\max_{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\quad\mathcal{L}(\bm{W},\bm{d}):=\langle\bm{d},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{V}\text{ReLU}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\right)\rangle-\frac{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{2}.

(2)

Note that we initialize
\bm{V}
at random and keep it fixed throughout the training. The common approach to solve the above optimization problem is to run a Gradient Descent Ascent (GDA) algorithm. At iteration
t
, GDA takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{d}_{t+1}&=\bm{d}_{t}+\mu\nabla_{\bm{d}}\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{d}_{t}\right)\\
\bm{W}_{t+1}&=\bm{W}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{W}}\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{d}_{t}\right)\end{cases}

(3)

Next, we establish the global convergence of GDA for an overparameterized model. Note that a global saddle point
(\bm{W}^{*},\bm{d}^{*})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}^{*},\bm{d})\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}^{*},\bm{d}^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{W},\bm{d}^{*})

for all feasible
\bm{W}
and
\bm{d}
. If these inequalities hold in a local neighborhood, (
\bm{W}^{*},\bm{d}^{*})
is called a local saddle point.

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}}\text{ }\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{V}\text{ReLU}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)-\bar{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2},

While the result proved above shows the global convergence of GDA for a GAN with 1-hidden layer generator and a linear discriminator, for a general GAN model, local saddle points may not even exist and GDA may converge to approximate local saddle points

We acknowledge that the considered GAN formulation of equation

(

Thus, the overall optimization problem is a non-trivial non-convex concave min-max problem. From that perspective, our result in Theorem
2.1

partially
explains the role of model overparameterization in GDA’s convergence for GANs.

2.4
Can the analysis be extended to more general GANs?

In the previous section, we focused on the implications of our results for one-hidden layer generator and linear discriminator. However, as it will become clear in the proofs, our theoretical results are based on analyzing the convergence behavior of GDA on a more general min-max problem of the form

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\text{min }}\underset{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\text{max }}h(\bm{\theta},\bm{d}):=\left\langle\bm{d},f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-\bm{y}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{2},

(7)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes a general nonlinear mapping.

Theorem 2.3
(Informal version of Theorem
A.4
)

Consider a general nonlinear mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the singular values of its Jacobian mapping around initialization obeying certain assumptions (most notably
\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta}_{0}))\geq\alpha
). Then, running GDA iterations of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{d}_{t+1}=\bm{d}_{t}+\mu\nabla_{\bm{d}}h(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{d}_{t})\\
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{d}_{t})\end{cases}

(8)

with sufficiently small step sizes
\eta
and
\mu
obeys

\displaystyle\left\|f(\bm{\theta}_{t})-\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}\sqrt{\left\|f(\bm{\theta}_{0})-\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\left\|\bm{d}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}.

Note that similar to the previous sections one can solve the maximization problem in equation
7
in closed form so that equation
7
is equivalent to the following minimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\text{min }}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|f(\bm{\theta})-\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2},

(9)

with global optima equal to zero. Theorem
2.3
ensures that GDA converges with a fast geometric rate to this global optima. This holds as soon as the model
f(\bm{\theta})
is sufficiently overparameterized which is quantitatively captured via the minimum singular value assumption on the Jacobian at initialization (
\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta}_{0}))\geq\alpha
which can only hold when
m\leq p
). This general result can thus be used to provide theoretical guarantees for a much more general class of generators and discriminators. To be more specific, consider a deep GAN model where the generator
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a deep neural network with parameters
\bm{\theta}
and the discriminator is a deep random feature model of the form
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{d}}(\bm{x})=\bm{d}^{T}\psi(\bm{x})
parameterized with
d
and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
a deep neural network with random weights. Then the min-max training optimization problem equation
1
with a regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\bm{d})=-\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}/2
is a special instance of equation
7
with

\displaystyle f(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{i}))\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{y}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\bm{x}_{i})

Therefore, the above result can in principle be used to rigorously analyze global convergence of GDA for an overparameterized GAN problem with a deep generator and a deep random feature discriminator model. However, characterizing the precise amount of overparameterization required for such a result to hold requires a precise analysis of the minimum singular value of the Jacobian of
f(\bm{\theta})
at initialization as well as other singular value related conditions stated in Theorem
A.4
. We defer such a precise analysis to future works.

In this section, we demonstrate benefits of overparamterization in large GAN models. In particular, we train GANs on two benchmark datasets: CIFAR-10 (

To study the generalization gap, we compute the FID scores by using (1) the training-set of real data, which we call

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we perform a systematic study of the importance of overparameterization in training GANs. We first analyze a GAN model with one-hidden layer generator and a linear discriminator optimized using Gradient Descent Ascent (GDA). Under this setup, we prove that with sufficient overparameterization, GDA converges to a global saddle point. Additionally, our result demonstrate that overparameterized models have a fast rate of convergence. We then validate our theoretical findings through extensive experiments on DCGAN and Resnet models trained on CIFAR-10 and Celeb-A datasets. We observe overparameterized models to perform well both in terms of the rate of convergence and the quality of generated samples.

A.2
Proof sketch of the main results

In this section, we provide a brief overview of our proofs. We focus on the main result in this manuscript, which is about the convergence of GDA (Theorem
2.1
). To do this we study the converge of GDA on the more general min-max problem of the form (see Theorem
A.4
for a formal statement)

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{min }}\underset{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\text{max }}h(\bm{\theta},\bm{d}):=\left\langle\bm{d},f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-\bm{y}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{2}.

(10)

In this case the GDA iterates take the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{d}_{t+1}=\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{d}_{t}+\mu\left(f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)-\bm{y}\right)\\
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)\bm{d}_{t}\end{cases}.

(11)

Our proof for global convergence of GDA on this min-max loss consists of the following steps.

Step 1: Recasting the GDA updates as a linear time-varying system

In the first step we carry out a series of algebraic manipulations to recast the GDA updates (equation
11
) into the following form

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t+1}\\
\bm{d}_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{A}_{t}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t}\\
\bm{d}_{t}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{r}_{t}=f\left(\bm{\theta_{t}}\right)-\bm{y}
denotes the residuum and
\bm{A}_{t}
denotes a properly defined transition matrix.

Step 2: Approximation by a linear time-invariant system

Next, to analyze the behavior of the time-varying dynamical system above we approximate it by the following time-invariant linear dynamical system

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t+1}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right){\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
denotes the initialization. The validity of this approximation is ensured by our assumptions on the Jacobian of the function
f
, which, among others, guarantee that it does not change too much in a sufficiently large neighborhood around the initialization and that the smallest singular value of
\mathcal{J}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
is bounded from below.

Step 3: Analysis of time-invariant linear dynamical system

To analyze the time-invariant dynamical system above, we utilize and refine intricate arguments from the control theory literature involving the spectral radius of the fixed transition matrix above to obtain

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\lesssim\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{0}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Step 4: Completing the proof via a perturbation argument

In the last step of our proof we show that the two sequences
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t}\\
\bm{d}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
will remain close to each other. This is based on a novel perturbation argument. The latter combined with Step 3 allows us to conclude

\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t}\\
\bm{d}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\lesssim\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{0}\\
\bm{d}_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

which finishes the global convergence of GDA on equation
10
and hence the proof of Theorem
A.4
.

In order to deduce Theorem
2.1
from Theorem
A.4
, we need to check that the Jacobian at the initialization is bounded from below at the origin and that it does not change too quickly in a large enough neighborhood. In order to prove that we will leverage recent ideas from the deep learning theory literature revolving around the neural tangent kernel. This allows us to guarantee that this conditions are indeed met, if the neural network is sufficiently wide and the initialization is chosen large enough.

The second main result of this manuscript, Theorem
2.2
, can be deduced more directly from recent results on overparameterized learning (see
Oymak & Soltanolkotabi 2020
). Hence, we have deferred its proof to Section
A.7
.

A.3
Analysis of GDA: A control theory perspective

In this section we will focus on solving a general min-max optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{min }}\underset{\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\text{max }}h(\bm{\theta},\bm{d}):=\left\langle\bm{d},f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-\bm{y}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{2},

(12)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a general nonlinear mapping.
In particular, we focus on analyzing the convergence behavior of Gradient Descent/Ascent (GDA) on the above loss, starting from initial estimates
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\bm{d}_{0}
. In this case the GDA updates take the following form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{d}_{t+1}=\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{d}_{t}+\mu\left(f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)-\bm{y}\right)\\
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)\bm{d}_{t}\end{cases}.

(13)

We note that solving the inner maximization problem in equation
12
would yield

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{min }}\frac{1}{2}\left\|f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

(14)

In this section, our goal is to show that when running the GDA updates of equation
13
, the norm of the residual vector defined as
\bm{r}_{t}:=f\left(\bm{\theta_{t}}\right)-\bm{y}
goes to zero and hence we reach a global optimum of equation
14
(and in turn equation
12
).

Our proof will build on ideas from control theory and dynamical systems literature. For that, we are first going to rewrite the equations
13
in a more convenient way. We define the average Jacobian along the path connecting two points
\bm{x}
,
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\displaystyle{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{y},\bm{x}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{x}+\alpha\left(\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right)\right)d\alpha,

where
{\cal{J}}\left(\theta\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the Jacobian associated with the nonlinear mapping
f
.
Next, from the fundamental theorem of calculus it follows that

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{t+1}=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}\right)-\bm{y}

\displaystyle=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}_{t}^{T}\bm{d}_{t}\right)-\bm{y}

\displaystyle=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)-\eta{\cal{J}}_{t+1,t}{\cal{J}}_{t}^{T}\bm{d}_{t}-\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}_{t+1,t}{\cal{J}}_{t}^{T}\bm{d}_{t},

(15)

where we used the shorthands
{\cal{J}}_{t}:={\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)
and
{\cal{J}}_{t+1,t}:={\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t+1},\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)
for exposition purposes.

Next, we combine the updates
\bm{r}_{t}
and
\bm{d}_{t}
into a state vector of the form
\bm{z}_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t}\\
\bm{d}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
. Using this notation the relationship between the state vectors from one iteration to the next takes the form

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{t+1}=\underset{=:\bm{A}_{t}}{\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{t+1,t}{\cal{J}}_{t}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}}}\bm{z}_{t},~~t\geq 0,

(16)

which resembles a time-varying linear dynamical system with transition matrix
\bm{A}_{t}
. Now note that to show convergence of
\bm{r}_{t}
to zero it suffices to show convergence of
\bm{z}_{t}
to zero. To do this we utilize the following notion of uniform exponential stability, which will be crucial in analyzing the solutions of equation
16
.
(See
Rugh 1996
for a comprehensive overview on stability notions in discrete state equations.)

Definition 1

A linear state equation of the form
\bm{z}_{t+1}=\bm{A}_{t}\bm{z}_{t}
is called uniformly exponentially stable if for every
t\geq 0
we have
\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\lambda^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
, where
\gamma\geq 1
is a finite constant and
0\leq\lambda<1
.

Using the above definition to show the convergence of the state vector
\bm{z}_{t}
to zero at a geometric rate it suffices to show the state equation
16
is exponentially stable.
3
3

3

We note that technically the dynamical system equation
16
is not linear. However, we still use exponential stability with some abuse of notation to refer to the property that
\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\lambda^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
holds. As we will see in the forth-coming paragraphs, our formal analysis is via a novel perturbation analysis of a linear dynamical system and therefore keeping this terminology is justified.
For that, we are first going to analyze a state equation which results from linearizing the nonlinear function
f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
around the initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. In the next step, we are going to show that the behavior of these two problems are similar, provided we stay close to initialization (which we are also going to prove). Specifically, we consider the linearized problem

\displaystyle\underset{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{min }}\underset{\widetilde{\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{\text{max }}h_{\text{lin}}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}},\widetilde{\bm{d}}\right):=\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{d}},f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\bm{d}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{2}.

(17)

We first analyze GDA on this linearized problem starting from the same initialization as the original problem, i.e.
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{0}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{0}=\bm{d}_{0}
. The gradient descent update for
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
takes the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t},

(18)

and the gradient ascent update for
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}
takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t+1}&=\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}+\mu\left(f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}-\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\right)\\
&=\left(1-\mu\right)\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}+\mu\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t},\end{split}

(19)

where we used the linear residual defined as
\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}
. Moreover, the residual from one iterate to the next can be written as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t+1}&=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}\\
&=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}\\
&=\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}.\end{split}

(20)

Again, we define a new vector
\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
and by putting together equations
19
and
20
we arrive at

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=\bm{A}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t},~~t\geq 0,

(21)

which is of the form of a linear time-invariant state equation. As a first step in our proof, we are going to show that the linearized state equations are uniformly exponentially stable. First, recall the following well-known lemma, which characterizes uniform exponential stability in terms of the eigenvalues of
\bm{A}
.

Lemma A.1

(
Rugh 1996
, Theorem 22.11)

A linear state equation of the form
\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\bm{A}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}
with
\bm{A}
a fixed matrix is uniformly exponentially stable if and only if all eigenvalues of
\bm{A}
have magnitudes strictly less than one, i.e.
\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)<1
. In this case, it holds for all
t\geq 0
and all
\bm{z}
that

\|\bm{A}^{t}\bm{z}\|\leq\gamma\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)^{t}\|\bm{z}\|,

where
\gamma\geq 1
is an absolute constant, which only depends on
\bm{A}
.

In the next lemma, we prove that under suitable assumptions on
{\cal{J}}_{0}
and the step sizes
\mu
and
\eta
the state equations
21
indeed fulfill this condition.

Lemma A.2

Assume that
\alpha\leq\sigma_{\text{min}}\left({\cal{J}}_{0}\right)\leq\sigma_{\text{max}}\left({\cal{J}}_{0}\right)\leq\beta
and consider the matrix
\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}
. Suppose that
\frac{\mu}{\eta}\geq 4\beta^{2}
. Then it holds that
\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)\leq 1-\eta\alpha^{2}
.

Proof

Suppose that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
. Hence, there is an eigenvector
\left[\bm{x},\bm{y}\right]^{T}\neq\bm{0}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\end{bmatrix}=\lambda\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\end{bmatrix}

holds. By a direct calculation we observe that this yields the equation

\displaystyle\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\bm{x}=\left(\frac{-\left(1-\lambda\right)^{2}}{\mu}+\left(1-\lambda\right)\right)\bm{x}.

In particular,
\bm{x}
must be an eigenvector of
{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}
. Denoting the corresponding eigenvalue with
s
, we obtain the identity

\frac{\left(1-\lambda\right)^{2}}{\mu}-\left(1-\lambda\right)+\eta s=0.

Hence, we must have

\lambda\in\left\{1-\frac{\mu}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}-\mu\eta s};\ 1-\frac{\mu}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}-\mu\eta s}\right\}.

Note that the square root is indeed well-defined, since

\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}-\mu\eta s\geq\mu\eta\beta^{2}-\mu\eta s\geq 0,

where in the first inequality we used the assumption
\frac{\mu}{\eta}\geq 4\beta^{2}
and in the second line we used that
s\leq\beta^{2}
, which is a consequence of our assumption on the singular values of
{\cal{J}}_{0}
.
Hence, it follows by the reverse triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left|\lambda\right|-\left(1-\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\leq\left|\lambda-\left(1-\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\right|=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\mu}{2}\right)^{2}-\mu\eta s}<\frac{\mu}{2}-\eta s\leq\frac{\mu}{2}-\eta\alpha^{2},

where the second inequality is valid as
\frac{\mu}{2}-\eta s\geq 0
is implied by
\frac{\mu}{2}\geq 2\eta\beta^{2}>\eta s
. In the last inequality we used the fact that
\alpha^{2}\leq s
, which is a consequence of our assumption on the singular values of
{\cal{J}}_{0}
. By rearranging terms, we obtain that
|\lambda|<1-\eta\alpha^{2}
. Since
\lambda
was an arbitrary eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
, the result follows.

\blacksquare

Since the last lemma shows that under suitable conditions it holds that
\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)<1
, Lemma
A.3
yields uniform exponential stability of our state equations. However, this will not be sufficient for our purposes. The reason is that Lemma
A.3
does not specify the constant
\gamma
and that in order to deal with the time-varying dynamical system we will need a precise estimate. The next lemma shows that for the state equations
21
we have, under suitable assumptions,
\gamma\leq 5
.

Lemma A.3

Consider the linear, time invariant system of equations

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=\bm{A}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t},\quad t\geq 0.

Furthermore, assume that
\alpha\leq\sigma_{\text{min}}\left({\cal{J}}_{0}\right)\leq\sigma_{\text{max}}\left({\cal{J}}_{0}\right)\leq\beta

and suppose that the condition
\frac{\mu}{\eta}\geq 8\beta^{2}
is satisfied. Then there is a constant
\gamma\leq 5
such that for all
t\geq 0
it holds that

\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Proof

Denote the SVD decomposition of
{\cal{J}}_{0}
by
\bm{W}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{T}
and note that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{W}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{W}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{W}^{T}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{W}^{T}.\end{bmatrix}

This means we can write

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{W}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{W}\end{bmatrix}\bm{P}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{1}&&{\text{0}}\\
&\ddots&\\
{\text{0}}&&\bm{C}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\bm{P}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{W}^{T}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{W}^{T}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{P}
is a permutation matrix and the matrices
\bm{C}_{i}
are of the form
\bm{C}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}\\
\mu&\left(1-\mu\right)\end{bmatrix}
, for
1\leq i\leq m
, where the
\sigma_{i}
’s denote the singular values of
{\cal{J}}_{0}
. Using this decomposition we can deduce

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{A}^{t}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\|\bm{A}^{t}\right\|\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}=\left(\underset{1\leq i\leq m}{\text{max}}\left\|\bm{C}_{i}^{t}\right\|\right)\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Now suppose that
\bm{V}_{i}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{-1}
is the eigenvalue decomposition of
\bm{C}_{i}
, where the columns of
\bm{V}_{i}
contain the eigenvectors and
\bm{D}_{i}
is a diagonal matrix consisting of the eigenvalues. (Note that it follows from our assumptions on
\mu
and
\eta
that the matrix
\bm{C}_{i}
is diagonalizable.) We have

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{C}_{i}^{t}\right\|=\left\|\bm{V}_{i}\bm{D}_{i}^{t}\bm{V}_{i}^{-1}\right\|\leq\left\|\bm{V}_{i}\right\|\left\|\bm{D}_{i}^{t}\right\|\left\|\bm{V}_{i}^{-1}\right\|=\kappa_{i}\cdot\rho\left(\bm{C}_{i}\right)^{t},

where we defined
\kappa_{i}:=\left\|\bm{V}_{i}\right\|\left\|\bm{V}_{i}^{-1}\right\|
.
From Lemma
A.2
we know that the assumption
\frac{\mu}{\eta}\geq 4\beta^{2}
results in
\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)\leq 1-\eta\alpha^{2}
. Therefore, defining
\gamma:=\underset{1\leq i\leq m}{\text{max}}\kappa_{i}
and noting
\rho\left(\bm{A}\right)=\underset{1\leq i\leq m}{\text{max}}\rho\left(\bm{C}_{i}\right)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(\underset{1\leq i\leq m}{\text{max}}\left\|\bm{C}_{i}^{t}\right\|\right)\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

In order to finish the proof we need to show that
\gamma\leq 5
.
For that, note that calculating the eigenvectors of
\bm{C_{i}}
directly reveals that we can represent this matrix as

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}}}{2}&\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}}}{2}\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\left\|\bm{V}_{i}\right\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}\right)}
and
\left\|\bm{V}_{i}^{-1}\right\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{min}}\left(\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}\right)}
, we calculate
\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}
, which yields

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}1-2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}&1\\
1&2\end{bmatrix}.

This representation allows us to directly calculate the two eigenvalues of
\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}
, which shows that

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}\right)}{\lambda_{\text{min}}\left(\bm{V}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{T}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{3-2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}+\sqrt{\left(1+2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}\right)^{2}+4}}{3-2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}-\sqrt{\left(1+2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}\right)^{2}+4}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3-2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}+\sqrt{\left(1+2\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}\right)^{2}+4}}{2\sqrt{1-4\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{2\sqrt{1-4\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}}}

\displaystyle<5,

where the last inequality holds because of
\frac{\eta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\mu}\leq\frac{\eta\beta^{2}}{\mu}\leq\frac{1}{8}
. Since
\gamma=\underset{1\leq i\leq m}{\max}\kappa_{i}
, this finishes the proof.

\blacksquare

Now that we have shown that the linearized iterates converge to the global optima we turn our attention to showing that the nonlinear iterates
16
are close to its linear counterpart
21
. For that, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1:
The singular values of the Jacobian at initialization are bounded from below

\sigma_{\text{min}}\left({\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right)\geq\alpha

for some positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Assumption 2:
In a neighborhood with radius
R
around the initialization, the Jacobian mapping associated with
f
obeys

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right\|\leq\beta

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right):=\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\left\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq R\}
.

Assumption 3:
In a neighborhood with radius
R
around the initialization, the spectral norm of the Jacobian varies no more than
\epsilon
in the sense that

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)-{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right\|\leq\epsilon

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
.

With these assumptions in place, we are ready to state the main theorem.

Theorem A.4

Consider the GDA updates for the min-max optimization problem
12

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{d}_{t+1}\\
\bm{\theta}_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{d}_{t}+\mu\nabla_{\bm{d}}h(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{d}_{t})\\
\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{d}_{t})\end{bmatrix}

(22)

and consider the GDA updates of the linearized problem
21

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t+1}\\
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}+\mu\nabla_{\bm{d}}h_{\text{lin}}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t})\\
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h_{\text{lin}}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t})\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

Set
\bm{z}_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}_{t}\\
\bm{d}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
and
\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}\\
\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\bm{r}_{t}:=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{t}\right)-\bm{y}
and
\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t}=f\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)+{\cal{J}}_{0}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)-\bm{y}
denote the residuals.

Assume that the step sizes of the gradient descent ascent updates satisfy
\frac{\mu}{\eta}\geq 8\beta^{2}
as well as
0<\mu\leq 1
. Moreover, assume that the assumptions 1-3 hold for the Jacobian
{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
of
f\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
around the initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\epsilon
, and

\displaystyle R:=2\gamma\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{18\epsilon\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

(24)

which satisfy
4\gamma\beta\epsilon\leq\alpha^{2}
. Here,
1\leq\gamma\leq 5
is a constant, which only depends on
\mu
,
\eta
, and
{\cal{J}}_{0}
. By
{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}
we denote the pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian at initialization
{\cal{J}}_{0}
.
Then, assuming the same initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
,
\bm{d}_{0}=\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{0}
(and, hence,
\bm{z}_{0}=\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{0}
), the following holds for all iterations
t\geq 0
.

•

\left\|\bm{z_{t}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
converges to
0
with a geometric rate, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

(25)

•

The trajectories of
\bm{z}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\bm{z}_{t}}
stay close to each other and converge to the same limit, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\bm{z}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}&\leq 2\eta\gamma^{2}\beta\epsilon\cdot t\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{4\gamma^{2}\beta\epsilon}{e\left(15\ln\frac{16}{15}\right)\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.\end{split}

(26)

•

The parameters of the original and linearized problems stay close to each other, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{9\epsilon\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

(27)

•

The parameters of the original problem stay close to the initialization, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{R}{2}.

(28)

Theorem
A.4
will be the main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. However, as discussed in Section
2.4
we believe that this meta theorem can be used to deal with a much richer class of generators and discriminators.

A.3.1
Proof of Theorem
A.4

We will prove the statements in the theorem by induction. The base case for
\tau=0
is trivial. Now assume that the equations equation
25
to equation
28
hold for
\tau=0,\ldots,t-1
. Our goal is to show that they hold for iteration
t
as well.

Part I:
First, we are going to show that
\bm{\theta}_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
. Note that by the triangle inequality and the induction assumption we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\theta_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{R}{2}.

Hence, in order to prove the claim it remains to show that
\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{R}{2}
. For that, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\bm{d}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\left\|{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\right\|\left\|\bm{d}_{t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)-{\cal{J}}_{0}\right\|\left\|\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\left\|{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\right\|\left\|\bm{d}_{t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\leq}\gamma\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\epsilon\cdot\gamma\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{4\beta^{2}\epsilon\gamma^{2}}{e\left(15\ln\frac{16}{15}\right)\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(ii\right)}{\leq}\gamma\beta^{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{3\beta^{2}\epsilon\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

where
\gamma\leq 5
is a constant. Let us verify the last two inequalities. Inequality
\left(ii\right)
holds because
1\leq\gamma
,
1\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}
, and

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{V}\bm{\Sigma}^{T}\bm{W}^{T}&0\\
0&\bm{V}\bm{\Sigma}^{T}\bm{W}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{r}_{0}\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{d}_{0}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{r}_{0}\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{d}_{0}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}=\beta^{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

(29)

Also
\left(i\right)
follows from assumptions 1-3,
\left\|\bm{d}_{t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\|\bm{z}_{t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
together with induction assumption equation
26
,
\left\|\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
, and

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t-1}\\
{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}{\cal{J}}_{0}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{t-2}\\
{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{t-2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}{\cal{J}}_{0}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}^{t-1}\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{0}\\
{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-1}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

(30)

where in the last inequality we applied Lemma
A.3
. Finally, by using
\eta\leq\frac{1}{8\beta^{2}}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\eta\beta^{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{3\eta\beta^{2}\epsilon\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{8}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{3\epsilon\gamma^{2}}{8\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R}{2},

where the last line is directly due to inequality (
24
),
\gamma\leq 5
, and
\alpha\leq\beta
. Hence, we have established
\bm{\theta}_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
.

Part II:
In Lemma
A.3
we showed that the time invariant system of state equations
\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\bm{A}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}
is uniformly exponentially stable, i.e.
\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}_{t}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
goes down to zero exponentially fast. Now by using the assumption that the Jacobian remains close to the Jacobian at the initialization
{\cal{J}}_{0}
, we aim to show the exponential stability of the time variant system of the state equations
16
. For that, we compute

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{A}_{t-1}\bm{z}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{t,t-1}{\cal{J}}_{t-1}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&-\eta{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\\
\mu\bm{I}&\left(1-\mu\right)\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{t-1}+\begin{bmatrix}\eta\left({\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{t,t-1}{\cal{J}}_{t-1}^{T}\right)\bm{d}_{t-1}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=:\bm{A}\bm{z}_{t-1}+\bm{\Delta}_{t-1}.

Now set
\lambda:=1-\eta\alpha^{2}
. By induction, we obtain the relation
\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{A}^{t}\bm{z}_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\bm{A}^{t-1-i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{A}^{t}\bm{z}_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\bm{A}^{t-1-i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{A}^{t}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left\|\bm{A}^{t-1-i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\lambda^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\gamma\lambda^{t-1-i}\left\|\eta\left({\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}\right)\right\|\left\|\bm{d}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\lambda^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\eta\gamma\lambda^{t-1-i}\left(2\beta\epsilon\right)\left\|\bm{z}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

(31)

The second inequality holds because of Lemma
A.3
. The last inequality holds because by combining our assumptions 1 to 3 with
\bm{\theta}_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
and the induction assumption
28
for
0\leq i\leq t-1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}+{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}\right\|\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}-{\cal{J}}_{i}\right\|+\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}\right\|\left\|{\cal{J}}_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}-{\cal{J}}_{i}\right\|+\beta\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\epsilon.

(32)

In order to deal with inequality
31
, we will rely on the following lemma.

Lemma A.5

(
Rugh 1996
, Lemma 24.5)

Consider two real sequences
p\left(t\right)
and
\phi\left(t\right)
, where
p\left(t\right)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\phi\left(t\right)\leq\begin{cases}\psi,&\text{if}\ t=0\\
\psi+\eta\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}p\left(i\right)\phi\left(i\right),&\text{if}\ t\geq 1\end{cases}

where
\eta
and
\psi
are constants with
\eta\geq 0
. Then for all
t\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\phi\left(t\right)\leq\psi\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(1+\eta\cdot p\left(i\right)\right).

Now we define
\phi_{t}=\frac{\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}}{\lambda^{t}}
and rewrite inequality
31
as

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\leq\gamma\phi_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{2\eta\gamma\beta\epsilon}{\lambda}\phi_{i}.

Hence, Lemma
A.5
yields that

\displaystyle\phi_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\phi_{0}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(1+\frac{2\eta\gamma\beta\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma\phi_{0}\left(1+\frac{2\eta\gamma\beta\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\leq}\gamma\phi_{0}\left(1+\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2\lambda}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(ii\right)}{=}\gamma\phi_{0}\left(\frac{1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}}{1-\eta\alpha^{2}}\right)^{t},

where
\left(i\right)
follows from
4\gamma\beta\epsilon\leq\alpha^{2}
and
\left(ii\right)
holds by inserting
\lambda=1-\eta\alpha^{2}
. Inserting the definition of
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{t}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{z}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\gamma\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

This completes the proof of Part II.

Part III:
In this part, our aim is to show that the error vector
\bm{e}_{t}:=\bm{z}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}
obeys inequality
26
.
First, note that

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{z}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(*\right)}{=}\left(\bm{A}\bm{z}_{t-1}+\bm{\Delta}_{t-1}\right)-\bm{A}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{e}_{t-1}+\bm{\Delta}_{t-1},

where in
\left(*\right)
we used the same notation as in Part II for
\bm{\Delta}_{t-1}
. Using a recursive argument as well as
\bm{e}_{0}=0
we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{e}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\bm{A}^{t-1-i}\bm{\Delta}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\eta\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-1-i}\left\|\bm{\Delta}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\eta\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-1-i}\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}-{\cal{J}}_{i+1,i}{\cal{J}}_{i}^{T}\right\|\left\|\bm{d}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(ii\right)}{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}2\eta\beta\epsilon\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-1-i}\left\|\bm{z}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

The first inequality follows from the triangle inequality and Lemma
A.3
. Inequality
\left(i\right)
follows from the definition of
\bm{\Delta}_{i}
. Inequality
(ii)
follows from inequality
32
.
Setting
c:=2\eta\beta\epsilon
we continue

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{e}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}c\gamma\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-i-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(iii\right)}{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}c\gamma^{2}\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t-i-1}\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{i}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(iv\right)}{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}c\gamma^{2}\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=2\eta\gamma^{2}\beta\epsilon\cdot t\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Here
\left(iii\right)
holds because of our induction hypothesis
25
and
\left(iv\right)
follows simply from
1-\eta\alpha^{2}\leq 1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}
. This shows the first part of equation
26
for iteration t. Finally, to derive the second part of equation
26
we observe that for all
t\geq 0
and
0<x\leq\frac{1}{16}
we have
t\left(1-x\right)^{t-1}\leq\frac{1}{e\left(15\ln\frac{16}{15}\right)x}
. Since
\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{\mu\alpha^{2}}{16\beta^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{16}
we can use this estimate, which yields

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{e}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta\gamma^{2}\beta\epsilon\cdot t\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\gamma^{2}\beta\epsilon}{e\left(15\ln\frac{16}{15}\right)\alpha^{2}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Hence, we have shown equation
26
.

Part IV:
In this part, we aim to show that the parameters of the original and linearized problems are close. For that, we compute that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h\left(\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{d}_{i}\right)-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}h_{\text{lin}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{d}_{i}\right)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{i}\right)\bm{d}_{i}-{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left\|\left({\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{i}\right)-{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\right)\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left\|{\cal{J}}^{T}\left(\bm{\theta}_{i}\right)\left(\bm{d}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{i}\right)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\epsilon\left\|\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\beta\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left\|\bm{e}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(ii\right)}{\leq}\gamma\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{i}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+2\eta\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}i\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{i-1}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Here
\left(i\right)
follows from assumptions 2 and 3, and
\left(ii\right)
holds because of Lemma
A.3
and our induction hypothesis
26
. Hence, using the formula
\sum_{i=0}^{t}ix^{i}=\frac{x\left(1+tx^{t+1}-\left(t+1\right)x^{t}\right)}{\left(x-1\right)^{2}}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\epsilon\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{1-\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t}}{\eta\alpha^{2}}+2\eta\beta^{2}\gamma\frac{1-t\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t-1}+\left(t-1\right)\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}}{\left(\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\epsilon\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta\alpha^{2}}+2\eta\beta^{2}\gamma\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\left(iii\right)}{\leq}\gamma\epsilon\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}\gamma}{\eta\alpha^{4}}+\frac{8\beta^{2}\gamma}{\eta\alpha^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{9\epsilon\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\eta\alpha^{4}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

where
\left(iii\right)
holds due to
1\leq\gamma
and
1\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}
. Hence, we have established inequality
27
for iteration
t
.

Part V:
In this part, we are going to prove equation
28
for iteration
t
. First, it follows from the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{9\epsilon\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

where in the second inequality we have used Part IV. Now we bound
\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
from above as follows

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\eta\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left\|{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\leq}\eta\gamma\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{i}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\eta\gamma\frac{1-\left(1-\eta\alpha^{2}\right)^{t}}{\eta\alpha^{2}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(ii\right)}{\leq}\gamma\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

where
\left(i\right)
holds by equation
30
and
\left(ii\right)
holds by equation
29
. Hence, it follows from the definition of
R
(equation
24
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&{\cal{J}}_{0}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{9\epsilon\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\alpha^{4}}\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{2}.

This completes the proof.

A.4
preliminaries for proofs of results with one-hidden layer generator and linear discriminator

In this section, we gather some preliminary results that will be useful in proving the main results i.e. Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. We begin by noting that Theorem
2.1
is an instance of Theorem
A.4
with
f\left(\bm{W}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{V}\cdot\phi\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)
. We thus begin this section by noting that
f\left(\bm{W}\right)
can be rewritten as follows

f\left(\bm{W}\right)=\bm{V}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi\left(\bm{w}_{1}^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\\
.\\
.\\
.\\
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi\left(\bm{w}_{k}^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, the Jacobian of this mapping
f\left(\bm{W}\right)
takes the form

{\cal{J}}(\bm{W})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{V}\cdot\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\right)\right)\otimes\bm{z}_{i}^{T}.

(33)

To characterize the spectral properties of this Jacobian it will be convenient to write down the expression for
{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}){\cal{J}}(\bm{W})^{T}
which has a compact form

\displaystyle{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}){\cal{J}}(\bm{W})^{T}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\left(\bm{V}\cdot\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\right)\right)\otimes\bm{z}_{i}^{T}\Big)\Big(\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{j}\right)\right)\bm{V}^{T}\otimes\bm{z}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\bm{V}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\right)\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}\bm{z}_{j}\right)\right)\bm{V}^{T}\Big)\otimes\Big(\bm{z}_{i}^{T}\bm{z}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{V}\underset{\ell=1,\ldots,k}{\text{diag}}\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\right)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\right)\bm{V}^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{V}\cdot\bm{D}^{2}\cdot\bm{V}^{T},

where
\bm{D}
is a diagonal matrix with entries

D_{\ell\ell}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{z}_{i})\right\|_{\ell_{2}}=\left\|\bm{Z}^{T}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{Z}\bm{w}_{\ell})\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

(34)

and
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
contains the
\bm{z}_{i}
’s in its rows. Note that we used simple properties of kronecker product in (i) and (ii), namely
(A\otimes B)^{T}=A^{T}\otimes B^{T}
and
(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)
.
The next lemma establishes concentration of the diagonal entries of matrix
\bm{D}^{2}
around their mean, which will be used in the future lemmas regarding the spectrum of the Jacobian mapping. The proof is deferred to Appendix
B.1
.

Lemma A.6

Suppose
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a fixed vector,
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2},\cdots,\bm{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2}\bm{I}_{d})
and constitute the rows of
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then for any
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}
the random variable
D=\left\|\bm{Z}^{T}\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{Z}\bm{w}\right)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
satisfies

\left(1-\delta\right)\mathbb{E}\left(D^{2}\right)\leq D^{2}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\mathbb{E}\left(D^{2}\right)

with probability at least
1-2\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
where
c_{1}
is a positive constant. Moreover we have

\mathbb{E}\left(D^{2}\right)=\sigma_{z}^{2}\left(\frac{nd}{2}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2\pi}\right).

Furthermore, using the above equation we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}){\cal{J}}(\bm{W})^{T}\right]=\frac{\sigma_{z}^{2}\left(d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}\right)}{2n}\bm{V}\bm{V}^{T}.

A.5
Lemmas regarding the initial misfit and the spectrum of the Jacobian

In this section, we state some lemmas regarding the spectrum of the Jacobian mapping and the initial misfit, and defer their proofs to Appendix
B
. First, we state a result on the minimum singular value of the Jacobian mapping at initialization.

Lemma A.7

(
Minimum singular value of the Jacobian at initialization
)
Consider our GAN model with a linear discriminator and the generator being a one hidden layer neural network of the form
\bm{z}\rightarrowtail\bm{V}\phi(\bm{W}z)
, where we have n independent data points
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2},\cdots,\bm{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2}\bm{I}_{d})
and aggregated as the rows of a matrix
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
has i.i.d
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{v}^{2})
entries. We also assume that
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
has i.i.d
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
entries and all entries of
\bm{W}_{0}
,
\bm{V}
, and
\bm{Z}
are independent.
Then the Jacobian matrix at the initialization point obeys

\begin{split}\sigma_{min}\left({\cal{J}}(\bm{W}_{0})\right)\geq\left(\sqrt{\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}k-\left(1+\delta\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{m}\left(1+\eta\right)\left(1+\delta\right)\right)\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}\end{split}

with probability at least
1-3e^{-\frac{\eta^{2}m}{8}}-2k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
, where
c_{1}
is a positive constant.

Next lemma helps us bound the spectral norm of the Jacobian at initialization, which will be used later to derive upper bounds on Jacobian at every point near initialization.

Lemma A.8

(
spectral norm of the Jacobian at initialization
)

Following the setup of previous lemma, the operator norm of the Jacobian matrix at initialization point
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
satisfies

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}},~~~~~~0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}

with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}-k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
, with
c_{1}
a positive constant.

The next lemma is adapted from
Van Veen et al. 2018
and allows us to bound the variations in the Jacobian matrix around initialization.

Lemma A.9

(
single-sample Jacobian perturbation
)
Let
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
be a matrix with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{v}^{2}\right)
entries,
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, and define the Jacobian mapping
{\cal{J}}(\bm{W};z)=\left(\bm{V}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\bm{W}z\right)\right)\right)\otimes z^{T}
. Then, by taking
\bm{W}_{0}
to be a random matrix with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}\right)
entries, we have

\left\|{\cal{J}}(\bm{W};z)-{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}_{0};z)\right\|\leq\sigma_{v}\left\|z\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left(2\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\right)

for all
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
obeying
\left\|\bm{W}-\bm{W}_{0}\right\|\leq R
with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}{6}}
.

Our final key lemma bounds the initial misfit
f(\bm{W}_{0})-\bm{y}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{V}\phi\left(\bm{W}_{0}\bm{z_{i}}\right)-\bm{\bar{x}}
.

Lemma A.10

(
Initial misfit
)
Consider our GAN model with a linear discriminator and the generator being a one hidden layer neural network of the form
\bm{z}\rightarrowtail\bm{V}\phi(\bm{W}z)
, where we have n independent data points
\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2},\cdots,\bm{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2}\bm{I}_{d})
and aggregated as the rows of a matrix
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
has i.i.d
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{v}^{2})
entries. We also assume that the initial
\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
has i.i.d
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
entries. Then the following event

\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{V}\phi\left(\bm{W}_{0}\bm{z_{i}}\right)-\bm{\bar{x}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq(1+\delta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{w}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{kdm}+\left\|\bm{\bar{x}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3

holds with probability at least
1-\left(k\cdot e^{-c_{2}n\left(\delta/27\right)^{2}}+e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/9\right)^{2}m}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}kd}{2}}\right)
, with
c_{2}
a fixed constant.

A.6
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.1
by using our general meta Theorem
A.4
. To do this we need to check that Assumptions 1-3 are satisfied with high probability. Specifically, in our case the parameter
\bm{\theta}
is the matrix
\bm{W}
and the non-linear mapping
f
is given by
f\left(\bm{W}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{V}\phi\left(\bm{W}\bm{z_{i}}\right)
. We note that in our result
\bm{d}_{0}=\bm{0}
and thus
\left\|\bm{z}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}=\left\|\bm{r}_{0}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
, which simplifies our analysis.

To prove Assumption 1 note that by setting
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
and
\eta=\frac{1}{3}
in Lemma
A.7
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{min}}\left({\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{k-9}-2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}}

\displaystyle=:\alpha.

This holds with probability at least
1-3e^{-\frac{m}{72}}-4k\cdot e^{-c\cdot n}-2k\cdot e^{-\frac{d}{216}}
, concluding the proof of Assumption 1.
Next, by setting
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
in Lemma
A.8
we have

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|\leq\zeta:=\frac{3}{2}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}}

with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}-2k\cdot e^{-c\cdot n}-k\cdot e^{-\frac{d}{216}}
. Now to bound spectral norm of Jacobian at
\bm{W}
where
\left\|\bm{W}-\bm{W}_{0}\right\|\leq R
(the value of R is defined in the proof of assumption 3 below), we use triangle inequality to get

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}\right)\right\|\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}\right)-{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|.

This last inequality together with assumption 3, which we will prove below, yields

\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}\right)\right\|\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\epsilon\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\gamma\beta}\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\frac{\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}}{4\beta}.

Therefore by choosing
\beta=2\zeta
we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\frac{\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}}{4\beta}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\frac{\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}}{8\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|+\frac{\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|^{2}}{8\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\zeta=\beta,

establishing that assumption 2 holds with

\beta=3\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}}

with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}-2k\cdot e^{-c\cdot n}-k\cdot e^{-\frac{d}{216}}
.

Finally to show that Assumption 3 holds, we use the single-sample Jacobian perturbation result of Lemma
A.9
combined with the triangle inequality to conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}\right)-{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W};z_{i}\right)-{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0};z_{i}\right)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W};z_{i}\right)-{\cal{J}}\left(\bm{W}_{0};z_{i}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{v}}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\right)\left(2\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\sigma_{v}\frac{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(2\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\frac{5}{4}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{d}\left(2\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\right),

(35)

where
\left(i\right)
holds by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and
\left(ii\right)
holds because for a Gaussian matrix
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2})
entries the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{5}{4}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{nd}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\sigma_{z}^{2}nd\right)\geq 1-e^{-\frac{nd}{24}}.

Now we set
\epsilon=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\gamma\beta}
and show that Assumption 3 holds with this choice of
\epsilon
and with radius
\widetilde{R}
, whose value will be defined later in the proof. First, note that

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\gamma\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}\sigma_{z}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{k-9}-2\sqrt{m}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}\right)}{12\gamma\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\left(i\right)}{\geq}\frac{\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{k}\right)^{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\pi}}}{60\left(3\sqrt{k}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{k}}{42000},

where
\left(i\right)
holds by assuming
k\geq C\cdot m
with
C
being a large positive constant. Combining the last inequality with equation
35
, we observe that a sufficient condition for assumption 3 to hold is

\displaystyle\frac{5}{4}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{d}\left(2\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\right)\leq\frac{\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{k}}{42000},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle 105000\sqrt{md}+52500\cdot\sqrt{d}\cdot\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\leq\sqrt{k}.

Now the first term in the L.H.S. is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{k}
if
k\geq\left(210000\right)^{2}md
, and for the second term we need

\displaystyle 105000\cdot\sqrt{d}\cdot\sqrt{6\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\text{log}\left(\frac{k}{3\left(\frac{2kR}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)}\leq\sqrt{k},

which by defining
x=3d\left(\frac{2R}{\sigma_{w}\sqrt{k}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle x\log\frac{d}{x}\leq\frac{1}{2\cdot 105000^{2}}.

This last inequality holds for
x\leq\frac{c}{\log d}
with
c<1

\left|\left\|\bm{D}x\right\|_{\ell_{2}}-\left\|\bm{D}y\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\right|\leq\left\|\bm{D}\bm{x}-\bm{D}\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},

we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{D}\bm{g}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big(\left\|\bm{D}\bm{g}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\Big)-\text{Var}(\left\|\bm{D}\bm{g}\right\|_{\ell_{2}})}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}-\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}}.

This combined with
\omega\left(S^{m-1}\right)\leq\sqrt{m}
and Lemma
B.1
yields that the event

\sigma_{min}\left(\bm{VD}\right)\geq\sigma_{v}\left(\sqrt{\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}-\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}}-\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\sqrt{m}-\eta\right)

(41)

holds with probability at least
1-3e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{8\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}^{2}}}.

Next, using the concentration bound for
\bm{D}_{\ell\ell}^{2}
, which we obtained in Section
B.1
, we bound
\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}
and
\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
, where we have set
\bm{d}_{i}=\bm{D}_{ii}
for
1\leq i\leq k.
For
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}
we compute that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{1\leq i\leq k}{\text{max }}\bm{d}_{i}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]}\right)&=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\\
&\leq k\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\\
&\leq k\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\leq k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right),\end{split}

(42)

as well as

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1-\delta\right)\sqrt{k}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]}\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\\
&\leq k\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\\
&\leq k\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\leq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]\right)\leq k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right).\end{split}

(43)

Finally by replacing
\eta
with
\eta\left\|\bm{d}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\sqrt{m}
in equation
41
, combined with equation
42
and equation
43
, for a random
\bm{W}_{0}
with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}\right)
entries we have:

\begin{split}\sigma_{min}\left({\cal{J}}(\bm{W}_{0})\right)&=\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{min}\left(\bm{VD}\right)\\
&\geq\frac{\sigma_{v}}{n}\left(\sqrt{\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}k-\left(1+\delta\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{m}\left(1+\eta\right)\left(1+\delta\right)\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{d}_{i}^{2}\right]}\\
&=\left(\sqrt{\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}k-\left(1+\delta\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{m}\left(1+\eta\right)\left(1+\delta\right)\right)\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}},~~~~0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2},\end{split}

with probability at least
1-3e^{-\frac{\eta^{2}m}{8}}-2k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
. This completes the proof of Lemma
A.7
.

B.3
Proof of lemma
A.8

Recall that

{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}){\cal{J}}(\bm{W})^{T}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{V}\underset{\ell=1,\ldots,k}{\text{diag}}\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{z}_{i})\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\right)\bm{V}^{T}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{V}\cdot\bm{D}^{2}\cdot\bm{V}^{T},

which implies that

\left\|{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}_{0})\right\|=\frac{1}{n}\left\|\bm{V}\cdot\bm{D}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\|\bm{V}\right\|\left\|\bm{D}\right\|.

For matrix
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
with i.i.d
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{v}^{2})
the event

\left\|\bm{V}\right\|\leq\sigma_{v}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)

holds with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}
. Regarding matrix
\bm{D}
by repeating equation
42
the following event

\left\|\bm{D}\right\|=\underset{1\leq i\leq k}{\text{max}}D_{ii}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[D_{ii}^{2}]}=\left(1+\delta\right)\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\frac{nd}{2}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2\pi}},~~~~0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}

holds with probability at least
1-k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
. Putting these together it yields that the event

\left\|{\cal{J}}(\bm{W}_{0})\right\|\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\left(\sqrt{k}+2\sqrt{m}\right)\sqrt{\frac{d+\frac{n-1}{\pi}}{2n}},~~~~~~~~~0\leq\delta\leq\frac{3}{2}

holds with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{m}{2}}-k\cdot\left(e^{-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{18}}+e^{-\frac{d\delta^{2}}{54}}+e^{-c_{1}n\delta}\right)
, finishing the proof of Lemma
A.8
.

B.4
Proof of lemma
A.10

First, note that if
\bm{W}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}\right)
entries and
\bm{V},\bm{W},\bm{Z}
are all independent, then
\left\|f\left(\bm{W}\right)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\left\|\bm{V}\phi\left(\bm{W}\bm{Z}^{T}\right)\bm{1}_{n\times 1}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
has the same distribution as
\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}
, where
\bm{v}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{v}^{2}\bm{I}_{m}\right)
and
\bm{a}=\frac{1}{n}\phi\left(\bm{W}\bm{Z}^{T}\right)\bm{1}
has independent sub-Gaussian entries, so its
\ell_{2}
-norm is concentrated.
Note that conditioned on
\bm{W}
,
a_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}ReLU\left(\bm{z}_{j}^{T}\bm{w}_{i}\right)
is sub-Gaussian with
\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=C\frac{\left\|\bm{w}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\sigma_{z}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and it is concentrated around
\mathbb{E}a_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left\|\bm{w}_{i}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\sigma_{z}
. This gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{i}\leq(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}a_{i}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-c\frac{\delta^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}a_{i}\right)^{2}}{\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}}=1-e^{-cn\delta^{2}},

which implies that

\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{i}^{2}\leq(1+3\delta)(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{i}^{2}\leq(1+\delta)^{2}(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-cn\delta^{2}},~~~~0\leq\delta\leq 1.

Due to the union bound we get that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}\right\}&\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}\right\}\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{i}^{2}\geq\left(1+\delta\right)(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}\right\}\leq k\cdot e^{-cn\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3.\end{split}

By substituting
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbb{E}a_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sigma_{z}^{2}\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}^{2}
this shows

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{z}\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\frac{1}{2\pi}\sigma_{z}^{2}\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}\geq 1-k\cdot e^{-cn\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3.\end{split}

We also have the following result for
\bm{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{v}^{2}\bm{I}_{m})

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\sigma_{v}\sqrt{m}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}m}{2}}.

By combining the above results we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{m}\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}\right\}&\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta/3\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{m}\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}\right\}\\
&\geq 1-k\cdot e^{-cn\left(\delta/9\right)^{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}m}{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3.\end{split}

Furthermore, we can bound
\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}
by the tail inequality

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{W}\right\|_{F}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\sigma_{w}\sqrt{kd}\right\}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}kd}{2}}.

Hence, by combining the last two results we have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sigma_{w}\sqrt{k\cdot d\cdot m}\right\}&\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\left(1+\delta/3\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{z}\sigma_{w}\sqrt{k\cdot d\cdot m}\right\}\\
&\geq 1-k\cdot e^{-cn\left(\delta/27\right)^{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/9\right)^{2}m}{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}kd}{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3.\end{split}

Therefore, due to the triangle inequality the event

\left\|f(\bm{W}_{0})-\bm{\bar{x}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq(1+\delta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{w}\sigma_{z}\sqrt{k\cdot d\cdot m}+\left\|\bm{\bar{x}}\right\|_{\ell_{2}},~~~0\leq\delta\leq 3

holds with probability at least
1-k\cdot e^{-c_{2}n\left(\delta/27\right)^{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/9\right)^{2}m}{2}}-e^{-\frac{\left(\delta/3\right)^{2}kd}{2}}
for some positive constant
c_{2}
, completing the proof of Lemma
A.10
.
