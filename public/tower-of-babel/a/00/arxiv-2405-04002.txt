Quadratic Varieties of Small Codimension
Kiwamu WATANABE

Abstract
Let X ⊂ ℙ n + c X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+c} be a nondegenerate smooth projective variety of dimension n n defined by quadratic equations. For such varieties, P. Ionescu and F. Russo proved the Hartshorne conjecture on complete intersections, which states that X X is a complete intersection provided that n ≥ 2 ​ c + 1 n\geq 2c+1 . As the extremal case, they also classified X X with n = 2 ​ c n=2c . In this paper, we classify X X with n = 2 ​ c − 1 n=2c-1 .

Introduction

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+c}
be a complex nondegenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n
. Philosophically, when the codimension
c
is small, the structure of
X
is subject to strong constraints. In this direction, R. Hartshorne raised his famous conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

If
n\geq 2c+1
, then
X
is a complete intersection.

As the extremal case, a variety
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+c}
is called a
Hartshorne variety
if
n=2c
and
X
is not a complete intersection.
The Hartshorne conjecture is still widely open. On the other hand, P. Ionescu and F. Russo
[
16
]
proved that the Hartshorne conjecture holds for quadratic varieties, that is, varieties scheme-theoretically defined by quadratic equations. This is a generalization of J.M. Landsberg’s result
[
22
, Corollary 6.29]
obtained by local differential geometric methods. Moreover, Ionescu and Russo also proved that the only quadratic Hartshorne varieties are the
6
-dimensional Grassmann variety
G\bigl(2,{\mathbb{C}}^{5}\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{9}
and the
10
-dimensional spinor variety
S^{10}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{15}
. Let us quickly review the brilliant ideas of Ionescu and Russo’s proof of
[
16
]
. If
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+c}
is a quadratic variety with small codimension, then
X
is a smooth Fano variety covered by lines; in
[
16
]
, an essentially important tool to study
X
is the Hilbert scheme of lines on
X
passing through a general point
x\in X
. We denote it by
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
and the dual vector space of the tangent space
T_{x}X
by
(T_{x}X)^{\vee}
; then
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
is naturally embedded into
{\mathbb{P}}\bigl((T_{x}X)^{\vee}\bigr)\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
. A notable feature of
X
is that
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is once again a quadratic variety, and it is scheme-theoretically defined by at most
c
quadratic equations; moreover, if
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is a complete intersection, then so is
{X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}
.

The purpose of this paper is to give a classification of quadratic varieties with
n=2c-1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+c}
be a nondegenerate smooth projective quadratic variety of dimension
n
. Assume that
n=2c-1
and
X
is not a complete intersection. Then
X
is projectively equivalent to one of the following:

(i)

the Segre
3
-fold
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
;

(ii)

a hyperplane section of the
6
-dimensional Grassmann variety
G\bigl(2,{\mathbb{C}}^{5}\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{9}
;

(iii)

a hyperplane section of the
10
-dimensional spinor variety
S^{10}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{15}
;

(iv)

an
11
-dimensional Fano variety
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{17}
whose Fano index is
8
. Moreover,
X
satisfies the following:

(a)

X
is covered by lines.

(b)

For a general point
x\in X
, we denote by
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
the Hilbert scheme of lines on
X
passing through
x\in X
. Then

\tau_{x}\colon\ {\mathcal{L}}_{x}\to{\mathbb{P}}\bigl((T_{x}X)^{\vee}\bigr),\qquad[\ell]\mapsto[T_{x}\ell]

is a closed immersion, and
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}((T_{x}X)^{\vee})
is projectively equivalent to a Gushel–Mukai
6
-fold
(
see Definition
2.12
for the definition of a Gushel–Mukai
6
-fold
)
.

In this theorem, the assumption that
X
is quadratic is essential because infinitely many smooth nondegenerate projective
3
-folds in
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
are not complete intersections (see, for instance,
[
4
, Section 2.2]
). On the other hand, according to D. Mumford’s famous result
[
28
]
, any projective variety can be realized as a quadratic variety.
Although the same method as Ionescu and Russo
[
16
]
is used to prove Theorem
1.2
, the number of subjects to be treated increases, and the discussion becomes more complicated. The results of Ionescu and Russo and the preliminary results used in the proof are summarized in Section
2
. Cases where the codimension of Theorem
1.2
is less than or equal to
2
are handled in Section
3
. Cases where the codimension is greater than or equal to
3
are treated in Section
4
.
The author needs to determine whether the fourth case that appeared in Theorem
1.2
occurs. If this variety existed, what kind of properties it would satisfy will be discussed in Section
5
.

.

Throughout the paper, we consider
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}

is minimal. Let us set

.
Let

be a family of lines on

{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
the Hilbert scheme of lines on
X
passing through
x\in X

the dual vector space of

tangent map

\tau_{x}\colon\ {\mathcal{L}}_{x}\to{\mathbb{P}}\bigl((T_{x}X)^{\vee}\bigr),\qquad[\ell]\mapsto[T_{x}\ell]

[

When

Definition 2.2
.

When

is scheme-theoretically an intersection of quadric hypersurfaces, i.e.,

Example 2.3
.

For positive integers
r<n
, the Grassmann variety
G(r,{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
is scheme-theoretically an intersection of quadrics via the Plücker embedding. The quadrics are given by the Plücker relations. More generally, W. Lichtenstein
[
24
]
showed that for every fundamental representation of a semisimple linear algebraic group, the orbit of the highest vector is cut out by a system of quadrics. Moreover, Mumford
[
28
]
proves that any projective variety can be realized as a quadratic variety.

We start to recall some classical results of projective geometry. The following is a refinement of Faltings’ theorem

In this section, we work in the following setting.

Setup 4.1

Lemma 4.2

Let

(i)

(ii)

{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
be a variety as in Setup
4.1
. Then we have

2\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\geq n-1.

In particular,
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is smooth, irreducible, and nondegenerate.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is defined by
c
quadratic equations, we have an inequality

\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\geq n-1-c=\frac{n-3}{2}.

If the lower bound is attained, it follows from Theorem
2.8
that
X
is a complete intersection. This is a contradiction. So we have an inequality
\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}>\frac{n-3}{2}
. Since
n=2c-1
, we have

\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}>\frac{(2c-1)-3}{2}=c-2.

As a consequence, we obtain

\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\geq c-1=\frac{n-1}{2}.

The latter part of our assertion follows from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
be a variety as in Setup
4.1
. Then we have

\frac{3}{2}c-3\leq\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}<\frac{3}{2}c-2.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.8
(iii),
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}
is not a complete intersection. As we have seen,
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is scheme-theoretically defined by
c
-independent quadratic equations. Moreover, by Lemma
4.3
, we have

. Thus, applying Theorem
2.4
to
{\mathcal{L}}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
, we have an inequality

\frac{3n-9}{4}\leq\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{x}<\frac{3n-5}{4}.

Since

, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎
