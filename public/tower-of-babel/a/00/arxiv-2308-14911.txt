Let
N_{0}(x)
be the number of integers
n\leq x
that can be written as

n=a^{2}+p^{2}\text{ where }p\text{ is a prime and }a\text{ is a positive integer}.

Theorem 1

We have

N_{0}(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}-\frac{x(\log\log x)^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}.

That is, in estimating
N_{0}(x)
there is a secondary term of size
x/(\log x)^{1+\delta+o(1)}
.

We will conjecture a formula for the secondary term, using a plausible heuristic, which suggests that it is oscillatory:

N_{0}(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}-\{1+o(1)\}\frac{\psi_{0}^{*}(\frac{\log\log x}{\log 2})}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\cdot\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}

where
\psi_{0}^{*}(t)
is the 1-periodic continuous, non-constant, positive-valued function,

\psi_{0}^{*}(t)=\kappa\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(2^{m}\beta)^{-1-\tau}(e^{-2^{m}\beta}-1+2^{m}\beta)\text{ where }\beta=2^{1-t}

with
\tau=\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\log 2})}{\log 2}=0.52876\dots
and
\kappa\approx 0.29356\dots
(defined precisely below).

Let

be the number of representations of

as
a^{2}+p^{2}
where
p
is prime and
a>0

One can use the prime number theorem to prove that

\sum_{n\leq x}r_{1}(n)\sim\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{\log x}
from which
one can deduce that

N_{1}(x)\sim\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{\log x}.

as
\sum_{r\geq 2}N_{r}(x)=o(x/\log x)
.
Our main result gives an estimate for

Theorem 2

For
\delta:=1-\frac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}=0.0860713320\dots

N_{2}(x)=\frac{x(\log\log x)^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}.

Moreover

N_{1}(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}-\frac{x(\log\log x)^{O(1)}}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}

(That is, the difference

Main Conjecture

If

N_{r}(x)\sim\frac{\psi_{r}(\frac{\log\log x}{\log 2})}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\cdot\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}},

we have, for

\psi_{r}(t)=\frac{\kappa}{r!}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(2^{m}\beta)^{r-1-\tau}e^{-2^{m}\beta}.

for some constant
\kappa>0

\psi_{r}(t^{+})>\psi_{r+1}(t^{+})

\psi_{r}(t^{-})<\psi_{r+1}(t^{-})

and
N_{r}(x^{-})<N_{r+1}(x^{-})
.

As a consequence of our formula for the

\#\{n\leq x:r_{1}(n)\geq 2\}\sim\frac{\psi_{2}^{*}(\frac{\log\log x}{\log 2})}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\cdot\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}},

where
\psi_{2}^{*}(t)

\psi_{2}^{*}(t)=\kappa\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(2^{m}\beta)^{-1-\tau}(1-(1+2^{m}\beta)e^{-2^{m}\beta}).

Another consequence of our formula for the
\psi_{r}(t)

\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}-N_{1}(x)\sim\frac{\psi_{1}^{*}(\frac{\log\log x}{\log 2})}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\cdot\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1+\delta}}

\psi_{1}^{*}(t)

To obtain the correct powers of

1.1.
Notation and simple details of sums of squares

Let
r_{0}(n)
denote the number of representations of
n
as the sum of two squares in the upper right quadrant; that is,

r_{0}(n)=\#\{x\geq 0,y>0:n=x^{2}+y^{2}\}
. Corresponding to each such solution we have complex number
x+iy
and there are four distinct “conjugate” integer solutions, multiplying through by
1,i,-1,-i
(unless
n=0
). We are considering prime values in sums of two squares, so we wish to define

r_{0}^{*}(n):=\#\{a\geq 0,b>0:n=a^{2}+b^{2}\text{ and }(a,b)=1\}

which is a multiplicative function with
r_{0}^{*}(2)=1,r_{0}^{*}(2^{k})=0
for all
k\geq 2
, and
r_{0}^{*}(p^{k})=1+(\frac{-1}{p})
for all odd primes and
k\geq 1
.
Therefore
r_{0}^{*}(n)=0
if and only if
n\in\mathcal{N}^{c}
where
\mathcal{N}:=\{n:\ 4\nmid n,\text{ and }p\nmid n\text{ for all primes }p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\}
. If

n\in\mathcal{N}
then
r_{0}^{*}(n)=2^{\omega^{*}(n)}

where
\omega^{*}(n)
is the number of odd prime factors of
n
. Therefore the values of
r_{0}^{*}(n)
are either 0 or a power of 2.

If
(m,n)=1
with
m=a^{2}+b^{2},\ n=c^{2}+d^{2}
with
a,b,c,d>0
and
(a,b)=(c,d)=1
, then there correspond two distinct solutions to
mn=x^{2}+y^{2}
with
(x,y)=1
and
x,y>0
, given by

x+iy=(ac+bd)+i|ad-bc|=|ac-bd|+i(ad+bc)

and as
a^{2}+b^{2}
runs through all the solutions counted by
r_{0}^{*}(m)
, and

c^{2}+d^{2}
all those solutions counted by
r_{0}^{*}(n)
, then
x^{2}+y^{2}
twice runs through all the solutions counted by
r_{0}^{*}(mn)
since the pairs
x+iy
given by
a+ib,c+id
are the same as those given by
b+ia,d+ic
.

Let
r_{1}^{*}(n)
be the number of representations of
n=a^{2}+p^{2}
where
a>0
and
(p,a)=1
.

Let
L:=\log\log x,\lambda=\frac{1}{\log 2}
and
K=\lfloor\lambda L\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{\log\log x}{\log 2}\rfloor
so that
2^{K}\in(\frac{1}{2}\log x,\log x]
.
Denote by
P(n)
the largest prime factor of an integer
n
and by
(\frac{\cdot}{\cdot})
the Legendre symbol. For
k\geq 2
let
d_{k}(n)=\#\{n=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}\}
,
d_{2}(n)=\tau(n)
.

2.
Simple applications of known results

We need to show that most solutions to
n=a^{2}+p^{2}
with
p
prime have
p\nmid a
:

Lemma 1

The number of
a^{2}+p^{2}\leq x
with
(a,p)>1
is

\sum_{n\leq x}(r_{1}(n)-r_{1}^{*}(n))=O(\sqrt{x}\log\log x).

Proof.

For each prime
p
write
a=pm
so that
m^{2}\leq x/(p^{2}+1)
so the number of such
m
is
\ll\sqrt{x}/p
, and so in total we have
\leq\sqrt{x}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}1/p\ll\sqrt{x}\log\log x
.
∎

Lemma 2

Let
z=x^{1/L}
. For any integer
m\geq 1
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)\leq z\text{ or }P(n)^{2}|n\end{subarray}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}\ll_{m}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1000}}

where
P(n)
is the largest prime factor of
n
.

Proof.

For
\sigma=1-\frac{\log(L\log L)}{\log z}\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)\leq z\text{ or }P(n)^{2}|n\end{subarray}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
P(n)\leq z\text{ or }P(n)^{2}|n\end{subarray}}r_{0}^{*}(n)^{m}(x/n)^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma}\prod_{p\leq z}\bigg(1+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p)^{m}}{p^{\sigma}}+\cdots\bigg)\prod_{z<p\leq x}\bigg(1+\frac{r_{0}^{*}(p^{2})^{m}}{p^{2\sigma}}+\cdots\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{(L\log L)^{L}}\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{2^{m}}{p^{\sigma}}\bigg)\ll\frac{x}{L^{L}}

as
\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}\ll\frac{z^{1-\sigma}}{(1-\sigma)\log z}\ll L

and the result follows.
∎

3.
Counting integers with a given number of prime factors

Selberg
[
13
]
gave a method for determining the number of integers with a given number of prime factors, and his method can easily be modified to give estimates in many analogous questions, including estimating

\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x;k):=\#\{n\leq x:n\in\mathcal{N}\text{ and }\omega^{*}(n)=k\}

for all
k\geq 1
. We first need to construct an appropriate family of Dirichlet series:

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}(s,z):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\in\mathcal{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{(2z)^{\omega^{*}(n)}}{n^{s}}=\bigg(1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}\bigg)\prod_{p\equiv 1\pmod{4}}\bigg(1+\frac{2z}{p^{s}-1}\bigg)

since the coefficient of
(2z)^{k}
is the sum over
n\in\mathcal{N}
with
\omega^{*}(n)=k
, and can be picked out by dividing through by
z^{k+1}
and integrating around a close contour including
z=0
. We can compare

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}(s,z)
to
\zeta(s)^{z}
(where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta-function), the quotient being “well-behaved” in a wide enough region of
s
-values, and then the number of such integers up to
x
can be estimated using Perron’s formula and well-known information about
\zeta(s)
. Therefore for any given
A>0
, if
k\leq A\log\log x
then

\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x;k)\sim c_{\kappa}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!},

(2)

where
\kappa=\frac{k-1}{\log\log x}
and the constant
c_{\kappa}>0
is given by the infinite product

c_{\kappa}:=\frac{3}{2^{\kappa+2}\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\cdot\prod_{p\equiv 1\pmod{4}}\bigg(1+\frac{2\kappa}{p-1}\bigg)\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{\kappa}\prod_{p\equiv 3\pmod{4}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{\kappa}.

We also need an upper bound that is uniform in a wide range. Here we can imitate the famous argument of Hardy and Ramanujan, so that there exist constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
such that for all
k\geq 1
we have

\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x;k)\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{x}{\log x}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}.

(3)

This follows from the prime number theorem for
k=1
and we then proceed by induction: Write
n
counted by

\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x;k)
as
2^{e_{0}}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdot p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, where
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\geq 1
and
e_{0}=0
or
1
. Then

n=p_{j}^{e_{j}}m_{j}
where
m_{j}
is counted by
\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x/p_{j}^{e_{j}};k-1)
, for each
j
, and so

k\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x;k)\leq\sum_{p^{e}\leq x/5^{k-1}}\pi_{\mathcal{N}}(x/p_{j}^{e_{j}};k-1)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\leq x/5^{k-1}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\gamma_{1}\frac{x}{p^{e}\log x/p^{e}}\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+\gamma_{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}

since each prime power
p_{j}^{e_{j}}
is
\geq 5
.
Then (
3
) follows from the estimate

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\leq x/5^{k-1}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log x}{p^{e}\log x/p^{e}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+\gamma_{2}

for some
\gamma_{2}>0
, which follows from Dirichlet’s work on primes in arithmetic progression, and partial summation.

3.1.
Estimating the size of a useful set

For a given interval
I\subset[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
and a given integer
m\geq 1
, define
\mathcal{S}_{m,k}(I,R)
to be the
set of
r+is\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
with
\arg(r+is)\in I
, where for
n:=r^{2}+s^{2}
we have

n\in[2R^{2},4R^{2}],\omega^{*}(n)=k\text{ and }(n,2m)=1.

If
I\subset(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
then
r,s\gg_{I}R
, which can be useful.

Proposition 1

If
m=R^{O(1)}
and
I\neq\emptyset
then

\#\mathcal{S}_{m,k}(I,R)\asymp_{k,I}\frac{R^{2}}{\log R}\cdot\frac{(\log\log R)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\cdot L^{O(1)}.

Proof.

It is convenient to write
k=\ell+1
and then,
excluding the various
n
identified in Lemma
2
, we
write each such
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}q
where the
p_{i}
and
q
are distinct primes, each
\equiv 1\pmod{4}

with
q>R^{1/L}
. Therefore

\#\mathcal{S}_{m,k}(I,R)\leq\#\mathcal{S}_{1,k}([0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],R)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\leq\dots\leq p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}\leq q\\
\text{Each }p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
\frac{2R^{2}}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}<q\leq\frac{4R^{2}}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}\end{subarray}}2^{k}+O\bigg(\frac{R^{2}}{(\log R)^{1000}}\bigg).

Now the dyadic interval for
q
must be between
R^{1/L}
and
R
and so the number of primes in the interval is

\asymp\frac{1}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}\frac{R^{2}}{\log R^{1/L}}\leq\frac{L}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}\frac{R^{2}}{\log R}

and so

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{S}_{1,k}([0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],R)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{2^{k}LR^{2}}{\log R}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\leq\dots\leq p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}\leq R^{2}\\
\text{Each }p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{2^{\ell}LR^{2}}{\log R}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell!}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{e}\leq R^{2}\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{e}}\bigg)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{LR^{2}}{\log R}\cdot\frac{(\log\log R+O(1))^{\ell}}{\ell!}=\frac{L^{O(1)}R^{2}}{\log R}\cdot\frac{(\log\log R)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}

for
k\ll\log\log R
.

To get lower bounds we proceed in much the same way. For a lower bound on
\mathcal{S}_{1,k}([0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],R)
we need to give a lower bound for the sum over prime powers. Each term with
p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}\leq B:=R^{1/L}
is really in the sum (not just part of an upper bound), and then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\leq\dots\leq p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}\leq B\\
\text{Each }p_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\ell!}\bigg(\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq B\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{\ell}-\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq B\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq B\\
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{\ell-2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{1}{2}\log\log B+O(1))^{\ell}}{\ell!},

and the same argument as above gives a lower bound which is smaller than the upper bound by a factor of
L^{O_{\ell}(1)}
.

For a lower bound on
\#\mathcal{S}_{m,k}([0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],R)

we need to incorporate that
(n,m)=1
where
m=R^{O(1)}
in the argument above, so we need to exclude
O(\log R)
primes from the sum over the
q
’s, and remove the prime factors of
m
from the sum over the
p
’s, so that the

(\log\log R)^{\ell}
is altered to
(\log\log R-\sum_{p|m}\frac{1}{p})^{\ell}
. Now
\sum_{p|m}\frac{1}{p}\ll\log\log\log m\ll\log\log\log R
, and so
(\log\log R-\sum_{p|m}\frac{1}{p})^{\ell}=(\log\log R)^{\ell}\cdot L^{O(1)}
.

For a lower bound on
\#\mathcal{S}_{m,k}(I,R)
for some non-empty
I\subset[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
we need to incorporate that
\arg(r+is)\in I
in the argument above. We will do so by restricting the angle for
q
. So given an

x+iy
with
x^{2}+y^{2}=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}
, we restrict the

for which

by ensuring that

\arg(u+iv)\in I-\arg(x+iy)
, an interval of the same length as

. Kubilius
[

, and so we simply need to multiply the above estimates by the factor

; in particular if

then the factor is
\frac{1}{2}
.

4.
Upper bounds on
\sum_{n\leq x}\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}

Lemma 3

For any fixed integer
\ell\geq 1
and integer

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega^{*}(n)=k\end{subarray}}\binom{r_{1}(n)}{\ell}\ll_{\ell}\frac{xL^{O_{\ell}(1)}}{(\log x)^{\ell+1}}\frac{(2^{\ell-1}L)^{k}}{k!}.

Proof.

Each

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

8.
Basic heuristic for the conjecture

The basic heuristic claims that “typically”, 1 in
\log\sqrt{x}
, of the representations of

with
(a,b)=1

a prime.

Therefore if

\displaystyle\text{Prob}(r_{1}^{*}(n)=r)

\displaystyle=\binom{R}{r}\bigg(\frac{1}{\log\sqrt{x}}\bigg)^{r}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\log\sqrt{x}}\bigg)^{R-r}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{r}}{r!}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\bigg(1-\frac{j}{R}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\frac{2}{\log x-2}\bigg)^{r}\cdot\exp\bigg(R\log\bigg(1-\frac{2}{\log x}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\bigg(\frac{2R}{\log x}\bigg)^{r}\cdot\exp\bigg(-\frac{2R}{\log x}+O\bigg(\frac{R}{(\log x)^{2}}+\frac{r^{2}}{R}+\frac{r}{\log x}\bigg)\bigg).

There is a remarkable change in behaviour here when
R
is close to
\frac{1}{2}\log x

and

e^{-\alpha}\frac{\alpha^{r}}{r!}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log x}\bigg)\bigg).

Using (

\displaystyle=\#\{n\leq x:\omega^{*}(n)=k\text{ and }r_{1}(n)=r\}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\cdot\frac{2^{(k+1)r}}{r!}\frac{1}{(\log x)^{r}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{2^{k+1}}{\log x}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2r}}{r!}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{r+1}}\cdot T_{k}\text{ where }T_{k}:=\frac{(2^{r-1}\log\log x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\exp\bigg(-\frac{2^{k+1}}{\log x}\bigg)
