Chaotic sets and Hausdorff dimension for Lüroth expansions
Rafael Alcaraz Barrera, Gerardo González Robert

Abstract
. We provide new similarities between regular continued fractions and Lüroth series in terms of topological dynamics and Hausdorff dimension. In particular, we establish a complete analogue for the Lüroth transformation of results by W. Liu, B. Li [ 18 ] and W. Liu, S. Wang [ 19 ] on the distal, asymptotic and Li-Yorke pairs for the Gauss map.

1.
Introduction and statement of results

Since the introduction of the term chaos by T. Li and J. Yorke in
[
17
]
(although without a formal definition), it has played a big role within the theory of topological dynamical systems. On the other hand, during the last 100 years or so, there has been significant interest in studying properties of different representations of real numbers under several mathematical perspectives; most notably: ergodic theory, fractal geometry, number theory and dynamical systems.

Two particularly well-studied representations of numbers in the unit interval are
regular continued fractions
(see
[
6
,
14
,
27
]
and references therein) and
Lüroth series
(see
[
5
,
6
,
8
]
and references therein). Both representations are obtained dynamically as the itineraries of a number
x
under the
Gauss map
and the
Lüroth map
, respectively. It is natural to ask if they share some dynamical or number theoretic properties.

The
Gauss map

\mathscr{G}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
is given by:

(1.1)

\mathscr{G}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{clrr}\frac{1}{x}-\left[\frac{1}{x}\right]&\text{if}\quad x\in(0,1);\\
0&\text{if}\quad x=0,&\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\left[y\right]
denotes the integer part of
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

J. Lüroth introduced in
[
20
]
a representation of real numbers now called
Lüroth series
. For each
x\in(0,1]
, let
a_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\frac{1}{a_{1}(x)}<x\leq\frac{1}{a_{1}(x)-1}

(see (
2.1
)). Notice that
a_{1}(x)\geq 2
. The
Lüroth map

\mathscr{L}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is given by:

(1.2)

\mathscr{L}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{clrr}a_{1}(x)(a_{1}(x)-1)x-(a_{1}(x)-1)&\text{if}\quad x\in(0,1],\\
0&\text{if}\quad x=0,&\\
\end{array}\right.

We will delve deeper into the study of (
1.2
) in
section

2
.

Both maps and their generated expansions have been studied under a dynamical systems’ perspective. In particular, it is a well-known fact that
([0,1),\mathscr{G})
is ergodic with respect to the so-called
Gauss measure

\mu
defined as follows: if
\mathfrak{m}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, then

(1.3)

\mu(B)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\mathop{\int}\limits_{B}\frac{1}{1+x}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}(x)\text{ for all Borel set }B\subseteq[0,1]

[
26
, Section 4.2.4.]
. On the other hand, H. Jager and C. de Vroedt in
[
12
]
showed that
([0,1],\mathscr{L})
is ergodic with respect to
\mathfrak{m}
.

Let us illustrate some dynamical similarities between regular continued fraction expansions and Lüroth series. Denote the regular continued fraction expansion of
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
by

x=[0;c_{1}(x),c_{2}(x),\ldots];

and the Lüroth expansion of
x\in(0,1]
by

x=\left\langle a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),\ldots\right\rangle.

Using Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem, it is straightforward to check that

\mu\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:c_{n}(x)\leq M\,\text{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)=0;

and, if
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}:=\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\geq 2\right\}
,

(1.4)

\mathfrak{m}\left(\left\{x\in(0,1]:a_{n}(x)\leq M\,\text{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)=0.

Also,

\mu\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=0

and

\mathfrak{m}\left(\left\{x\in(0,1]:\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=0.

Moreover, since
\mu
is equivalent with
\mathfrak{m}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:c_{n}(x)\leq M\,\text{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)=0

and

\mathfrak{m}\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=0.

V. Jarník showed in
[
13
]
that

(1.5)

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:c_{n}(x)\leq M\,\text{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)=1.

Here and thereafter,
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}
stands for Hausdorff dimension. Later on, I.J. Good proved in
[
10
, Theorem 1]
that

(1.6)

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

Finally, it can be shown using elementary facts of iterated function systems (see
[
3
, Theorem 2.2.2]
) that

(1.7)

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\left\{x\in(0,1]:a_{n}(x)\leq M\,\text{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right)=1

and

(1.8)

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\left\{x\in(0,1]:\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

Notice that (
1.7
) is a full analogue of (
1.5
) for the Lüroth transformation and, similarly, (
1.8
) of (
1.6
).

1.1.
Dynamical sets and chaotic properties

Within the theory of topological and discrete dynamical systems, it is customary to consider a
topological dynamical system
as a pair
(X,f)
where
X
is a compact (or pre-compact) metric space and
f:X\to X
is a continuous transformation. In fact, the properties given in Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
below have been studied extensively in this setting
[
2
,
16
,
23
]
. However, the results in the aforementioned references cannot be applied neither to the Gauss map nor the Lüroth map, since both transformations are discontinuous. Nonetheless, Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
can be considered when
f
is a piecewise continuous map of the unit interval with a countable number of discontinuities and
X
is pre-compact.

As usual, given a transformation
f:X\to X
,
f^{n}(x)
is the
n
-th iterate of
f
at
x
, i.e.
f\circ\ldots\circ f
,
n
times. In particular,
f^{1}=f
and
f^{0}
is the identity map on
X
. Also, the
orbit of
x
under
f
is the set
\left\{f^{n}(x)\right\}_{n\geq 0}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
and
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y
, we say that
(x,y)
is:

(
i
)

a
proximal pair
if

\mathop{\liminf}\limits_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))=0,

(
ii
)

an
asymptotic pair
if

\mathop{\lim}\limits_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))=0,

(
iii
)

a
distal pair
if

\mathop{\liminf}\limits_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))>0,

(
iv
)

a
Li-Yorke pair
if
(x,y)
is proximal and

\mathop{\limsup}\limits_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))>0.

Also, given
x\in X
, we define the following
dynamical sets
:

(
i
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{PR}_{f}(x):=\left\{y\in X:(x,y)\quad\text{is a proximal pair}\right\};

(
ii
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{AS}_{f}(x):=\left\{y\in X:(x,y)\quad\text{is an asymptotic pair}\right\};

(
iii
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{D}_{f}(y):=\left\{y\in X:(x,y)\quad\text{is a distal pair}\right\};

(
iv
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{LY}_{f}(x):=\left\{y\in X:(x,y)\quad\text{is a Li-Yorke pair}\right\}.

Clearly, the dynamical sets given in Definition
1.1
are symmetric in the following sense:
y\in P(x)
if and only if
x\in P(y)
, where
P(y)
is one of the dynamical sets given in Definition
1.1
. It readily follows from Definition
1.1
that

(
i
)

\operatorname{AS}_{f}(x)\subset\operatorname{PR}_{f}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{LY}_{f}(x)\subset\operatorname{PR}_{f}(x);

(
ii
)

\operatorname{D}_{f}(x)=X\setminus\operatorname{PR}_{f}(x)

and;

(
iii
)

X=\operatorname{AS}_{f}(x)\dot{\cup}\operatorname{LY}_{f}(x)\dot{\cup}\operatorname{D}_{f}(x),

where
\dot{\cup}
stands for disjoint union.

Although our main results do not deal directly with proximal pairs, we have included them in the discussion for completeness.

Consider a compact (or precompact) metric space
X
with metric
d
, and a transformation
f:X\to X
. We say that the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
topologically transitive
if for each pair of open and non-empty subsets
U,V\subset X
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}[U]\cap V\neq\varnothing
. We call a point
x\in X

periodic
with respect to
f
if there exists some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(x)=x
. Finally, we say that
(X,f)
is
sensitive to initial conditions
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
x\in X
and for all
\delta>0
there are
y\in X
with
d(x,y)<\delta
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
verifying
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))\geq\varepsilon
.

Let us recall now the following notions of
chaos
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
X
a pre-compact metric space and
f:X\to X
a transformation. We say that:

(
i
)

the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
Devaney chaotic
if

(a)

(X,f)
is topologically transitive;

(b)

(X,f)
has dense periodic points;

(c)

(X,f)
is sensitive to initial conditions.

(
ii
)

the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
Li-Yorke chaotic
if there exists an uncountable set
S\subset X
such that for all
x,y\in S
with
x\neq y
the pair
(x,y)
is a Li-Yorke pair. Such set
S
is called a
scrambled set under
f
.

W. Huang and X. Ye showed in
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
that Devaney chaos implies Li-Yorke chaos when
f:X\to X
is continuous. In the same setting, F. Blanchard et al. showed in
[
4
, Corollary 2.4]
that if the topological entropy of
(X,f)
is positive, then
(X,f)
is Li-Yorke chaotic. The dimensional properties of the sets given in Definition
1.1
have received attention recently — see
[
21
]
.

1.2.
Main results and structure of the paper

Lately, W. Liu, B. Li in
[
18
]
and W. Liu, S. Wang in
[
19
]
studied chaotic properties of
([0,1),\mathscr{G})
. The following statement summarizes
[
18
, Theorem 1.3, Corollary 1.4]
and
[
19
, Theorem 1.1, 1.2]
:

Theorem 1.3
.

(
i
)

For every
x\in[0,1)
we have:

a)

If
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}^{n}(x)>0
, then
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}(x)
is a countable set;

b)

If
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}^{n}(x)=0
, then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}
;

c)

If
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}^{n}(x)=0
and
\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}^{n}(x)>0
, then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

(
ii
)

For every
x\in[0,1)
, the set
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{G}}(x)
is dense in
[0,1)
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{G}}(x))=0
and
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{G}}(x))=1
.

(
iii
)

For every
x\in[0,1)
, we have that
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{LY}_{\mathscr{G}}(x))=1
.

(
iv
)

The dynamical system
([0,1],\mathscr{G})
is Li-Yorke chaotic. Moreover, there exists a scrambled set under
\mathscr{G}
of full Hausdorff dimension.

Our main results establish new similarities between the Gauss map and the Lüroth map from a dynamical perspective. In fact, we obtain a full analogue of Theorem
1.3
for the Lüroth transformation. Although some of our strategies do follow
[
18
,
19
]
, our work is not a straightforward adaptation of their arguments. For instance, the computations used in the proof about asymptotic pairs for the Gauss map do not carry over into the Lüroth series context.

Theorem A
.

Let
x
be any number in
[0,1]
.

(
i
)

If
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>0
, then
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
is a countable set.

(
ii
)

If
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=0
, then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=\frac{1}{2}
.

(
iii
)

If
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=0
and
\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>0
, then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem B
.

For every
x\in[0,1]
, the set
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
is dense in
[0,1]
. Moreover,

\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=0\quad\text{and}\quad\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=1.

As a straightforward consequence of Theorems
A
and
B
we obtain the following:

Corollary 1.4
.

For every
x\in[0,1]
, we have
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{LY}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=1
.

Finally, we study the chaotic properties of the Lüroth map.

Theorem C
.

The Lüroth map
\mathscr{L}
is Devaney chaotic.

Theorem D
.

The Lüroth map
\mathscr{L}
is Li-Yorke chaotic. In fact, there exists a scrambled set
S
under
\mathscr{L}
which is dense and has full Hausdorff dimension.

In this section, we discuss some basic results on Lüroth series and Hausdorff dimension. We will nevertheless assume some familiarity with both topics. The classical texts
[
6
,
8
]
discuss several aspects of Lüroth series and other number theoretic maps. We refer the reader to
[

2.1.
Elements of Lüroth Series

As mentioned previously, for any
x\in(0,1]
, we call
a_{1}(x)
the unique number in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
determined by

(2.1)

\frac{1}{a_{1}(x)}<x\leq\frac{1}{a_{1}(x)-1}.

The
Lüroth map

\mathscr{L}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is given by

\mathscr{L}(x)=\begin{cases}a_{1}(x)(a_{1}(x)-1)x-(a_{1}(x)-1),\text{ if }x\in(0,1],\\
0,\text{ if }x=0.\\
\end{cases}

Note that
\mathscr{L}(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
. Thus, given any
x\in(0,1]
, we can define the sequence of
Lüroth digits of
x
, denoted
(a_{n}(x))_{n\geq 1}
or
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
if there is no risk of ambiguity, by

(2.2)

a_{n}(x)\colon=a_{1}(\mathscr{L}^{n-1}(x))\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

In this case, the definition of
\mathscr{L}
yields

(2.3)

x=\frac{1}{a_{1}}+\frac{\mathscr{L}(x)}{a_{1}(a_{1}-1)}.

A repeated use of (
2.3
) replacing
x
by the iterates of
\mathscr{L}
gives

(2.4)

x=\frac{1}{a_{1}}+\frac{1}{a_{1}(a_{1}-1)a_{2}}+\frac{1}{a_{1}(a_{1}-1)a_{2}(a_{2}-1)a_{3}}+\ldots.

The series converges because the
n
-th term is positive and is bounded above by
2^{-n}
. We will write
x=\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
to mean (
2.4
).

Lüroth series allow us to identify the interval
(0,1]
with a symbolic space. In what follows, we write

\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}=\left\{(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}:\,x_{n}\in\mathscr{D}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

i.e. the space of sequences in
\mathscr{D}
. We endow
\mathscr{D}
with the discrete topology and
\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the product topology. Additionally, we associate the dynamical system
((0,1],\mathscr{L})
with the symbolic dynamical system
(\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}},\sigma)
where
\sigma:\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the so-called
shift map
given by:

\sigma((a_{n})_{n\geq 1})=(a_{n+1})_{n\geq 1}\text{ for all }(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Proposition 2.1
.

The function
\Lambda:\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}\to(0,1]
given by

(2.5)

\Lambda((a_{n})_{n\geq 1})=\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\;\text{ for all }(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}

is a semi-conjugacy between
(\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}},\sigma)
and
\left((0,1],\mathscr{L}\right)
. That is,
\Lambda
is a continuous and surjective function satisfying

(2.6)

\Lambda\circ\sigma=\mathscr{L}\circ\Lambda.

Proof.

It is shown in
[
8
, Section 4.3]
that
\Lambda
is bijective. The continuity follows from the definition of the product topology. We notice that
\Lambda
is not a homeomorphism, since
(0,1]
is connected and
\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
is totally disconnected. In order to check (
2.6
), take any
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
and note that, by (
2.4
),

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}(\Lambda((a_{n})_{n\geq 1}))&=\mathscr{L}(\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle)\\
&=a_{1}(a_{1}-1)(\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{2},\ldots\right\rangle)-(a_{1}-1)\\
&=(a_{1}-1)+\left\langle a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},\ldots\right\rangle-(a_{1}-1)\\
&=\left\langle a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},\ldots\right\rangle=\Lambda\circ\sigma((a_{n})_{n\geq 1}).\end{split}

and the proposition follows.
∎

We should give a word of warning. We have followed J. Galambos
[
8
]
rather than K. Dajani and C. Kraaikamp
[
6
]
when defining
\mathscr{L}
. While our building blocks are intervals of the form
(\alpha,\beta]
, the other approach uses intervals of the form
[\alpha,\beta)
. We think that our choice is more natural, because the itinerary of any
x\in(0,1]
with respect to
\mathscr{L}
provides the inverse function of
\Lambda
and we do not have to mind the points whose orbit is eventually
0
. Other recent developments in Lüroth series, such as
[
1
,
24
,
25
]
, also use Galambos’ approach. However, the definition in
[
6
]
is precisely the one given by J. Lüroth in
[
20
]
. In any case, this difference affects only countably many points and it is therefore irrelevant to our discussion.

In view of Proposition
2.1
, given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{n}
, the set

\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})\colon=\{x=(0,1]:a_{1}(x)=c_{1},\ldots,a_{n}(x)=c_{n}\}

is non-empty. We refer to the sets
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})
as
fundamental intervals of level
n
, or just as
fundamental intervals
. In particular, for any
c\in\mathscr{D}
, the definition of
a_{1}
translates into

\mathscr{I}_{1}(c)=\left(\frac{1}{c},\frac{1}{c-1}\right].

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the fundamental intervals of order
n
form a partition of
(0,1]
. We say that two fundamental intervals
I
and
J
of the same order are
adjacent
if
I\neq J
and
\inf\{|x-y|:x\in I,y\in J\}=0
. Hence, whenever
I
and
J
are not adjacent, there is at least one fundamental interval of the same order between them.

We denote by
\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{n}(\mathbf{c})
the topological closure
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we write

(2.7)

\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\right\rangle\colon=\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{1}(c_{1}-1)c_{2}}+\ldots+\frac{1}{c_{1}(c_{1}-1)c_{2}(c_{2}-1)\cdots c_{n-1}(c_{n-1}-1)c_{n}}.

The following lemma is an exercise on mathematical induction.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
i
)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{n}
we have

\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})=\left(\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\right\rangle,\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}-1\right\rangle\right].

(
ii
)

Every
x=\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\in(0,1]
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

\frac{1}{c_{n+1}}<\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\leq\frac{1}{c_{n+1}-1}.

The next corollary follows from the first part of Lemma
2.2
and an inductive argument:

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{n}
we have

\left|\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})\right|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{c_{j}(c_{j}-1)};

in particular,

|\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

Let us name now the inverses of the different branches of each
\mathscr{L}^{n}
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{n}
, we define
\mathscr{T}^{n}_{\mathbf{c}}:(0,1]\to\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})
to be inverse of
\mathscr{L}^{n}
restricted to
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{c})
. Hence,
\mathscr{T}^{n}_{\mathbf{c}}
is a linear function with slope

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{c_{j}(c_{j}-1)}

and its action on the Lüroth series of any
x=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\rangle\in(0,1]
is as follows:

\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{c}}^{n}(\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\right\rangle)=\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\right\rangle.

We may extend the definition of
\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{c}}^{n}
to an infinite sequence
\mathbf{c}
: if
\pref(\mathbf{c},n)
is the prefix of
\mathbf{c}
of length
n
, then
\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{c}}^{n}\colon=\mathscr{T}_{\pref(\mathbf{c},n)}^{n}
.

2.2.
Hausdorff dimension

We suppose that the reader is acquainted with the notion of Hausdorff dimension. The next lemma, which we will use without any reference, follows from the definition of Hausdorff dimension (see Definition 1.2.1 and Proposition 1.2.6 in
[
3
]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A,B
be subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. If
A\subseteq B
, then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}A\leq\dim_{\operatorname{H}}B
. If
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, then

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\right)=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(A_{n}).

For any non-empty sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the
diameter
of
A
is

|A|\colon=\sup\{|a-a^{\prime}|:a,a^{\prime}\in A\}

and the
distance
between
A
and
B
is

d(A,B)\colon=\inf\left\{|a-b|\colon a\in A,b\in B\right\}.

Cantor sets are at the core of our proofs, so we need some general estimates for their Hausdorff dimension. Suppose
\mathscr{A}=\{\mathscr{A}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is a family of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
with the following properties:

(i)

The family
\mathscr{A}_{0}
contains exactly one element and each
\mathscr{A}_{n}
is finite,

(ii)

Every
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
has positive diameter for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(iii)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
A,B\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
either
A=B
or
A\cap B=\varnothing
,

(iv)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
B\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
there is some
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n-1}
such that
B\subseteq A
,

(v)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n-1}
there is some
B\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
such that
B\subseteq A
,

(vi)

The quantity
d_{n}(\mathscr{A})\colon=\max\{|A|:A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}\}
tends to
0
as
n
tends to
+\infty
.

The
limit set

\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
of
\mathscr{A}
is

\mathbf{A}_{\infty}:=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}}A.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and any
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
, the
set of descendants of

A
is

D(A)=\{B\in\mathscr{A}_{n+1}:B\subseteq A\}.

This construction is a simpler version of what D. Kleinbock and B. Weiss called
strongly tree-like structure
in
[
15
]
. The next lemmas are proven in
[
9
, Section 3]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be as above. Assume that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(d_{n}(\mathscr{A})^{-1})}{n}>0,

and that there is a sequence
(B_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with the following properties:

(i)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, any
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
and any
Y,Z\in D(A)
, the condition
Y\neq Z
implies

d(Y,Z)\geq B_{n}|A|,

(ii)

The next inequality holds:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log(B_{n}^{-1})}{n}<1.

If
s>0
is such that for every sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
we have

(2.8)

\sum_{B\in D(A)}|B|^{s}\geq|A|^{s},

then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\mathbf{A}_{\infty}\geq s
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a family of compact sets as above except that we allow each
\mathscr{A}_{n}
to be at most countable. If
s>0
is such that for every sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
A\in\mathscr{A}_{n}
we have

\sum_{B\in D(A)}|B|^{s}\leq|A|^{s},

then
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\mathbf{A}_{\infty}\leq s
.

Let us consider an example. Take
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
, define
\mathscr{A}_{0}\colon=\{[0,1]\}
and, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, put

\mathscr{A}_{n}\colon=\left\{\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\colon\mathbf{a}\in[\![2,N]\!]^{n}\right\}.

The limit set of the family
\mathscr{A}=\{\mathscr{A}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is the Cantor set

(2.9)

F_{N}\colon=\left\{x=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\rangle\in(0,1]\colon a_{n}\in[\![2,N]\!]^{n}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Using Lemma
5.6
and that every
s\in(0,1)
satisfies

1=\sum_{b\geq 2}\frac{1}{b(b-1)}<\sum_{b\geq 2}\frac{1}{b^{s}(b-1)^{s}},

we may obtain (
1.7
). We omit the proof of this assertion, for it resembles that of Theorem
B
(see
section

5
).

3.
A symbolic definition

Proposition
2.1
invites us to adopt a symbolic perspective. We now introduce the
R
-shuffle of two sequences. Briefly, given two sequences
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and an infinite set
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with infinite complement, the
R
-shuffle of
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is the result of placing
(y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
on
R
and
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
on
\mathbb{N}\setminus R
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be an infinite set such that
Q=\mathbb{N}\setminus R
is infinite. Write
R=\{r_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
Q=\{q_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
with
r_{n}<r_{n+1}
,
q_{n}<q_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{D}
be a non-empty set. The
R
-shuffle
of two sequences
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 1},\mathbf{y}=(y_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathscr{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence
\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};R)=(c_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\mathscr{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
given by

c_{q_{k}}=x_{k},\;c_{r_{k}}=y_{k}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

If there is some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
such that
R=\{km:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
, we write
\mathscr{B}_{m}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b};R)
. We extend this notation for certain finite sequences: for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\geq 2
,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\mathscr{A}^{(m-1)n}
, and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\mathscr{A}^{n}
, the sequence
\mathscr{B}_{m}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=(c_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\mathscr{A}^{mn}
is given by

\mathscr{B}_{m}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1},b_{1},a_{m},\ldots,b_{n-1},a_{(n-1)(m-1)+1},\ldots,a_{n(m-1)},b_{n}).

In other words, we truncate the corresponding definition for infinite sequences.

4.
Asymptotic pairs: proof of Theorem
A

Take an arbitrary number
x\in[0,1]
. We prove the three parts of Theorem
A
separately. Assume first that
\mathop{\liminf}\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>0
. Then,
x\in(0,1]
and, writing
x=\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
, we have

\mathop{\limsup}\limits_{n\to\infty}a_{n}<+\infty

(see Lemma
2.2
); that is,
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence. Consider
y\in\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. Clearly,
y\neq 0
and we can write
y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
. If
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
were unbounded, there would be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(y)\to 0
when
j\to\infty
. We would then obtain a contradiction:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left|\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(y)\right|=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(x)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>0.

Therefore,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence. Put

M\colon=\max\left\{\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n},\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b_{n}\right\}.

Then, the orbits under
\mathscr{L}
of
x
and
y
are thus contained in the Cantor set

F_{M}=\{\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\right\rangle:c_{n}\in[\![2,M]\!]\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

For each
r\in[\![2,M]\!]
define

\mathscr{K}(r)=\bigcup_{j=2}^{M}\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(r,j).

Observe that the collection of mutually disjoint compact sets
\{\mathscr{K}(r):r\in[\![2,M]\!]\}
is finite, hence

\varepsilon\colon=\min\left\{d\left(\mathscr{K}(r),\mathscr{K}(s)\right)\colon s,r\in[\![2,M]\!],s\neq r\right\}>0.

Let
N=N(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)|<\varepsilon
for
n\geq N
. For such
n
, the points
\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)
and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)
belong to the same set
\mathscr{K}(r)
and hence
a_{n+1}=b_{n+1}
. Moreover, it is obvious that every number
z=\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\in(0,1]
such that
c_{n}=a_{n}
holds for every sufficiently large
n
belongs to
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. This shows that

\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=\displaystyle\left\{y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\colon b_{n}=a_{n}\text{ for sufficiently large }n\right\},

so
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
is countable.

Second, assume that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=0
. If
y\in\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\to 0
if
n\to\infty
, so either
y=0
or
y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
satisfies
b_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
(see Lemma
2.2
). Conversely, every
y
with any of these two properties is an element of
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. Therefore,

\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=\left\{y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\colon\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=\infty\right\}\cup\{0\}

and, by (
1.8
),
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Third, let us assume that
\mathop{\liminf}\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=0
and that
\mathop{\limsup}\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>0
. The second inequality yields
x\neq 0
, so we can write
x=\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
. The argument in this case is much more involved. In a few words, we are going to build a superset of
\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
whose Hausdorff dimension we will estimate with Lemma
2.6
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given
y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},\ldots\right\rangle
,
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P(x,y,M,n)
be the statement

P(x,y,M,n)\colon=(a_{n+1}\leq M\implies a_{n+1}=b_{n+1})\land(a_{n+1}>M\implies b_{n+1}>M).

The following holds:

\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)\subseteq\displaystyle\bigcap_{M\geq 3}\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{n\geq N}\left\{y\in(0,1]\colon P(x,y,M,n)\right\}.

Proposition
4.1
, and in particular
P
, provides a precise statement of the following claim: if
y\in\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
, then the digits in the Lüroth expansions of
x
and
y
which are smaller than a given bound eventually coincide.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}\colon(0,24^{-1})\to\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
be the function that associates to each
\varepsilon\in(0,24^{-1})
the integer

M_{1}(\varepsilon)\colon=\max\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}\colon m(m+1)\leq(4\varepsilon)^{-1}\right\}.

It is easy to check that
M_{1}
is non-increasing, surjective, and that
M_{1}(\varepsilon)\to\infty
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Take
y\in\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. As before,
y\neq 0
and we write
y=\left\langle b_{1},b_{2},\ldots\right\rangle
. Take an arbitrary
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
. We now obtain a natural number
N
such that
P(x,y,M,n)
holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
satisfy
M_{1}(\varepsilon)=M
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\right|<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}.

Pick any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}
.

Assume first that
a_{n+1}\leq M
. We will conclude
a_{n+1}=b_{n+1}
after showing that
a_{n+1}\neq b_{n+1}
leads to the contradiction

(4.1)

|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)|\geq\varepsilon.

The argument amounts to check that
b_{n+1}
cannot belong to any of the sets
[\![2,a_{n+1}-2]\!]
,
\{a_{n+1}-1\}
,
[\![a_{n+1}+2,\infty]\!]
, or
\{a_{n+1}+1\}
. It may happen that some of these sets are empty, but there is nothing to prove if this is true.

Case 1.1

If
b_{n+1}\leq a_{n+1}-2
, then

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{b_{n+1}}-\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-2}-\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}=\frac{1}{(a_{n+1}-1)(a_{n+1}-2)}>\frac{1}{M(M+1)}\geq\varepsilon.

Therefore,
b_{n+1}\geq a_{n+1}-1
.

Case 1.2

Assume that
b_{n+1}=a_{n+1}-1
. We will proceed in three steps: firstly, we show
a_{n+2}=2
; afterwards, we show
b_{n+2}\geq 4
; finally, we obtain a contradiction.

First step.
If we had
a_{n+2}\geq 3
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)\leq 2^{-1}
would hold and

\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)}{a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}\leq\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}.

The previous inequality and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)>b_{n+1}^{-1}=(a_{n+1}-1)^{-1}
yield

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}-\left(\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}-1)}>\frac{1}{2M(M+1)}\geq\varepsilon.

We conclude that
a_{n+2}=2
.

Second step.
If
b_{n+2}\in\{2,3\}
were true, we would have
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)>3^{-1}
and

\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)=\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}+\frac{\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)}{(a_{n+1}-1)(a_{n+1}-2)}\geq\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}+\frac{1}{3(a_{n+1}-1)(a_{n+1}-2)}.

The previous expression and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)<(a_{n+1}-1)^{-1}
give

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}+\frac{1}{3(a_{n+1}-1)(a_{n+1}-2)}-\frac{1}{a_{n+1}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3(M+1)M}\geq\varepsilon.

We conclude that
b_{n+2}\geq 4
.

Third Step.
By the first step,
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)>2^{-1}
and, by the second step,
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)\leq 3^{-1}
; hence,

\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)>\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}>\varepsilon.

We conclude that
a_{n+1}\leq b_{n+1}
.

Case 1.3

Assume that
b_{n+1}\geq a_{n+1}+2
, then

\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\leq\frac{1}{b_{n+1}-1}\leq\frac{1}{a_{n+1}+1}.

The previous inequality together
\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>a_{n+1}^{-1}
give

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}-\frac{1}{a_{n+1}+1}=\frac{1}{a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}\geq\frac{1}{(M+1)M}>\varepsilon.

Hence, we have
b_{n+1}\in\{a_{n+1},a_{n+1}+1\}
.

Case 1.4

Assume that
b_{n+1}=a_{n+1}+1
. The proof is quite similar to that of Case 1.2.

First step.
If we had
a_{n+2}\in\{2,3\}
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)>3^{-1}
would hold and

\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)}{a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{1}{3a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}.

This inequality and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\leq(b_{n+1}-1)^{-1}=a_{n+1}^{-1}
imply

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}+\frac{1}{3a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}-1)}-\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}-1)}>\frac{1}{3M(M+1)}\geq\varepsilon.

Thus, we must have
a_{n+2}\geq 4
.

Second step.
If
b_{n+2}\geq 3
, then
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)\leq 2^{-1}
and

\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)=\frac{1}{b_{n+1}}+\frac{\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)}{b_{n+1}(b_{n+1}-1)}\leq\frac{1}{a_{n+1}+1}+\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}.

The last inequality and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)>a_{n+1}^{-1}
lead the contradiction

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{a_{n+1}}-\left(\frac{1}{a_{n+1}+1}+\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a_{n+1}(a_{n+1}+1)}\geq\frac{1}{2(M+1)M}\geq\varepsilon.

Hence
b_{n+2}=2
.

Third Step.
The first two steps give
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)\leq 3^{-1}
and
\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)>2^{-1}
, so

\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(y)-\mathscr{L}^{n+1}(x)>\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}>\varepsilon

and we may say that
b_{n+1}+1\neq a_{n+1}
. Combining Cases 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, and 1.4, we conclude that
a_{n+1}\leq M
implies
a_{n+1}=b_{n+1}
.

Now assume that
a_{n+1}>M
. If we had
b_{n}\leq M
, the previous argument and the symmetry of the asymptotic relation guarantee
a_{n+1}=b_{n+1}\leq M
, a contradiction. Therefore,
b_{n+1}>M
.

Since
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}
was arbitrary, the proof is done.
∎
For each
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the functions
f_{N}^{M},g_{N}^{M}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathscr{D}\cup\{\infty\}
as follows: for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

f_{N}^{M}(n)=\begin{cases}a_{n},\text{ if }1\leq n\leq N-1,\\
a_{n},\text{ if }N\leq n\text{ and }a_{n}\leq M,\\
M+1,\text{ if }N\leq n\text{ and }M<a_{n},\end{cases}\quad g_{N}^{M}(n)=\begin{cases}a_{n},\text{ if }1\leq n\leq N-1,\\
a_{n},\text{ if }N\leq n\text{ and }a_{n}\leq M,\\
+\infty,\text{ if }N\leq n\text{ and }M<a_{n}.\end{cases}

Also, we define the set

G_{N}^{M}\colon=\left\{y=\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\rangle\in(0,1]:f_{N}^{M}(n)\leq b_{n}\leq g_{N}^{M}(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}\right\}.

With this notation, Proposition
4.1
can be summarized as follows:

(4.2)

\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcap_{M\geq 3}\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}G_{N}^{M}.

Proposition 4.2
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is some
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
such that every
N\in\mathbb{N}
verifies
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M})<\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Take
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
. We first show that
(\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M}))_{N\geq 1}
is a constant sequence. On the one hand,
(\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M}))_{N\geq 1}
is non-decreasing because so is
(G_{N}^{M})_{N\geq 1}
. On the other hand, let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
be arbitrary and note that

(4.3)

G_{N}^{M}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathscr{D}^{N-1}}G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{c}).

For any
\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}\in\mathscr{D}^{N-1}
, the function

\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathbf{d}}\colon G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{c})\to G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{d})

given by

\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathbf{d}}(y)=\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{d}}^{N-1}\circ\mathscr{L}^{N-1}(y)\quad\text{ for all }y\in G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{c})

is bi-Lipschitz as it is a composition of linear functions. From a symbolic perspective,
\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathbf{d}}
replaces the prefix
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1})
with
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{N-1})
; in other words, for any

\langle c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1},b_{N},b_{N+1},\ldots\rangle\in G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{c})

we have

\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathbf{d}}\left(\left\langle c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1},b_{N},b_{N+1},b_{N+2},\ldots\right\rangle\right)=\left\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{N-1},b_{N},b_{N+1},b_{N+2},\ldots\right\rangle.

Therefore,
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{c}))=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(\mathbf{d}))
(see
[
7
, Proposition 3.3]
) and, in view of (
4.3
),

(4.4)

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\right)=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1})\right).

Then, using

G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})=G_{N+1}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}),

we have

\displaystyle\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1},a_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N+1}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1},a_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N+1}^{M}\right).

The argument also holds for
N=1
if we replace
\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1})
with
[0,1]
. Hence,
(\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(G_{N}^{M}))_{N\geq 1}
is a constant sequence.

Take
\varepsilon>0
and put
s=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

(4.5)

M\geq 1+\left(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}}

and take any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We now get an upper estimate for
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1})\right)
using Lemma
2.6
. To each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{n}
such that
f(N-1+j)\leq c_{j}\leq g(N-1+j)
for
j\in[\![1,n]\!]
, we associate the compact set

A_{n}(\mathbf{c})\colon=\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{N+n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})

and we call
\mathscr{A}_{n}
the collection of such sets
A_{n}(\mathbf{c})
. Define
\mathscr{A}_{0}=\{[0,1]\}
. The limit set
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}(M,N)
of the family
\mathscr{A}=\{\mathscr{A}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is precisely
G_{N}^{M}\cap\mathscr{I}_{N-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N-1})
.

We claim that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any valid
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
the following holds:

(4.6)

\sum_{k=f(n+1)}^{g(n+1)}|A_{n+1}(\mathbf{c}k)|^{s}\leq|A_{n}(\mathbf{c})|^{s}.

Indeed, the inequality above is, by the definition of
A_{n}(\mathbf{c})
and Corollary
2.3
, equivalent to

(4.7)

\sum_{k=f(n+1)}^{g(n+1)}\frac{1}{k^{s}(k-1)^{s}}\leq 1.

If
g(n+1)<+\infty
, then (
4.7
) reduces to
a_{n+1}^{-s}(a_{n+1}-1)^{-s}\leq 1
, which is true because
s>0
and
a_{n+1}\geq 2
. Now assume that
g(n+1)=+\infty
. From

\displaystyle\sum_{k=f(n+1)}^{g(n+1)}\frac{1}{k^{s}(k-1)^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=M+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{s}(k-1)^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=M+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k-1)^{2s}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{M-1}^{\infty}x^{-2s}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(M-1)^{-2\varepsilon}}{2\varepsilon}

and (
4.5
) we obtain
\frac{(M-1)^{-2\varepsilon}}{2\varepsilon}\leq 1
. Then, (
4.7
) holds and, by Lemma
2.6
and (
4.4
), we conclude

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(G_{N}^{M}\right)=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\mathbf{A}_{\infty}(M,N)\right)\leq s=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon.

∎

We can now finish the proof of the third point in Theorem
A
. For any
\varepsilon>0
let
M=M(\varepsilon)
be as in Proposition
4.2
. Then,

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}G_{N}^{M}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon;

so, by (
4.2
),
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary,
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{2}
and the proof of Theorem
A
is complete.

5.
Distal pairs: proof of Theorem
B

Let
x\in[0,1]
be arbitrary. We consider two different cases:
x=0
and
x\in(0,1]
.

The first case is already done. In fact, since
\mathscr{L}(0)=0
, a real number
y
belongs to
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(0)
if and only if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)>0,

or, equivalently, if its Lüroth digits are bounded. Hence, by (
1.4
) and (
1.7
), we conclude
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(0))=0
and
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(0)=1
.

Now assume that
x\in(0,1]
and write
x=\left\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
. Both
x
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
will remain fixed for the rest of the proof. We first show that
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=0
relying on a result due to W. Philipp. Afterwards, in order to show
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=1
, we construct a subset of
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
with full Hausdorff dimension.

5.1.
The distal pairs of
x
are null

W. Philipp proved in the 1967 article
[
22
]
a quantitative version of the Borel-Canelli Lemma (Theorem
5.1
below). W. Philipp used it to obtain measure theoretic properties of numeration systems including regular continued fractions and expansions on integer base
b\geq 2
. In what follows,
\mathscr{O}
refers to Landau’s big-O notation.

Theorem 5.1
.

[
22
, Theorem 3]

Let
(X,\mathscr{B},\mu)
be a probability space and let
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of measurable sets. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
and each
t\in X
, define

A(N,t)\colon=\#\left\{n\in[\![1,N]\!]\colon t\in E_{n}\right\}

and

\varphi(N)\colon=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu(E_{n}).

Suppose that there is a summable sequence of non-negative real numbers
(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
m<n
we have

\mu(E_{n}\cap E_{m})\leq\mu(E_{n})\mu(E_{m})+\mu(E_{n})C_{n-m}.

Then, for
\mu
-almost every
t\in X
and every
\varepsilon>0
we have

A(N,t)=\varphi(N)+\mathscr{O}\left(\sqrt{\varphi(N)}\left(\log\varphi(N)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

We follow rather closely W. Philipp’s steps and apply Theorem
5.1
on Lüroth series to conclude the next result:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(I_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of intervals in
[0,1]
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
and each
t\in[0,1]
define

A(N,t):=\#\{n\in[\![1,N]\!]:\mathscr{L}^{n}(t)\in I_{n}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(N):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathfrak{m}(I_{n}).

Then, almost every
t\in[0,1]
verifies the following statement: for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\varepsilon>0
we have

A(N,t)=\varphi(N)+\mathscr{O}\left(\varphi(N)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log\varphi(N))^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathscr{I}_{k}
be a fundamental interval of a given order
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for every interval
(\alpha,\beta]\subseteq(0,1]
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq k}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{L}^{-n}\left[(\alpha,\beta]\right]\cap\mathscr{I}_{k}\right)=\mathfrak{m}\left((\alpha,\beta]\right)\mathfrak{m}(\mathscr{I}_{k}).

Proof.

See (3.4) in
[
12
]
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
I,J\subseteq[0,1]
be two intervals,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
[\frac{n}{2}]
be the integer part of
\frac{n}{2}
, then

\mathfrak{m}\left(I\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)\leq\mathfrak{m}(I)\mathfrak{m}(J)+\frac{2}{2^{[\frac{n}{2}]}}\mathfrak{m}(J).

Proof.

We may further suppose, without losing any generality, that
I
and
J
are left open and right closed intervals. Write
I=(\alpha,\beta]
. Assume that
\alpha>0
. Let
\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}
and
\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta}
be the fundamental intervals of order
k\colon=[\frac{n}{2}]
containing
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively. Put

I_{0}:=I\cap\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha},\quad I_{2}:=I\cap\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta},\quad I_{1}:=I\setminus(I_{0}\cup I_{1}).

If
\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}=\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta}
, then
I\subseteq\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}
and, by Lemma
5.3
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\left(I\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha})\mathfrak{m}(J)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\mathfrak{m}(J)\leq\mathfrak{m}(I)\mathfrak{m}(J)+\frac{2}{2^{k}}\mathfrak{m}(J).

Assume now that
\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}\neq\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta}
. Since
I_{1}
is a union of fundamental intervals of order
k
, we may use Lemma
5.3
to obtain
\mathfrak{m}(I_{1}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J])=\mathfrak{m}(I_{1})\mathfrak{m}(J)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(I\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J])

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\left(I_{0}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)+\mathfrak{m}\left(I_{1}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)+\mathfrak{m}\left(I_{2}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)+\mathfrak{m}(I_{1})\mathfrak{m}(J)+\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta}\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}(I_{1})\mathfrak{m}(J)+\left(\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\alpha}\right)+\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{I}_{k}^{\beta}\right)\right)\mathfrak{m}(J)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}(I)\mathfrak{m}(J)+\frac{2}{2^{k}}\mathfrak{m}(J).

This shows the lemma when
\alpha>0
.

If
\alpha=0
, consider a strictly decreasing sequence
(\alpha_{m})_{m\geq 1}
of real numbers contained in
I=(0,\beta]
converging to
0
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the previous argument shows that

\mathfrak{m}\left([\alpha_{m},\beta)\cap\mathscr{L}^{-n}[J]\right)\leq\mathfrak{m}([\alpha_{m},\beta))\mathfrak{m}(J)+\frac{2}{2^{[\frac{n}{2}]}}\mathfrak{m}(J).

The result follows by considering
m\to\infty
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Define the sequence
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by
E_{n}=\mathscr{L}^{-n}[I_{n}]
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, when
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
m<n
, Lemma
5.3
and the
\mathscr{L}
-invariance of
\mathfrak{m}
give

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(E_{n}\cap E_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{L}^{-n}[I_{n}]\cap\mathscr{L}^{-m}[I_{m}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\left(\mathscr{L}^{-(n-m)}[I_{n}]\cap I_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}(I_{n})\mathfrak{m}(I_{m})+\frac{2}{2^{[\frac{n-m}{2}]}}\mathfrak{m}(I_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}(E_{n})\mathfrak{m}(E_{m})+\frac{2}{2^{[\frac{n-m}{2}]}}\mathfrak{m}(E_{m}).

The result follows now from Theorem
5.1
, because
\sum_{n}\frac{2}{2^{[n/2]}}
converges.
∎

For any sequence of positive real numbers
\mathbf{r}=(r_{n})_{n\geq 1}
define

E(\mathbf{r})=\{y\in[0,1]:|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)|<r_{n}\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Then, we have

\mathfrak{m}(E(\mathbf{r}))=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\sum_{n}r_{n}<+\infty,\\
1,\text{ if }\sum_{n}r_{n}=+\infty.\end{cases}

The convergence part follows from the Borel-Cantelli lemma and the divergence part, from Proposition
5.2
. In particular, if
r_{n}=n^{-1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for almost every
y\in[0,1]
there exists an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that
|\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n_{j}}(y)|<r_{j}
holds for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, almost every
y\in[0,1]
satisfies

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)\right|=0.

Hence, by the definition of distal pairs,
\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x))=0
.

5.2.
The distal pairs of
x
have full dimension

The proof consists of two parts. First, we construct a subset of
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. Then, we estimate its Hausdorff dimension.

5.2.1.
A collection of distal pairs

Given a collection
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}^{m,n})_{m,n\geq 2}
of sequences on
\mathscr{D}
indexed on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, we define for each
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
the set

F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e}):=\left\{\mathscr{B}_{m}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{e}^{m,n}):\mathbf{b}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}\right\}

(see
section

3
).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{e}
be as above. For any
m_{0},n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, the following set is dense in
\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
:

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq m_{0}\\
n\geq n_{0}\end{subarray}}F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e}).

Moreover, the set

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq m_{0}\\
n\geq n_{0}\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})\right].

is dense in
(0,1]
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the definition of the product topology. The second assertion follows from the first one and Proposition
2.1
.
∎

The next lemma tells us how to choose an appropriate
\mathbf{e}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
E\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 6}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\in\mathscr{D}^{m}
. If
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m-1},e)\in[\![2,E]\!]^{m}
satisfies

3\leq e\leq E-1\;\text{ and }\;2\leq|c_{m}-e|,

then
d\left(\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{c}),\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{b})\right)\geq E^{-2m}
.

Proof.

The restriction on
E
is imposed to ensure the existence of some
\mathbf{b}
satisfying the hypotheses. The second condition on
e
implies that the intervals
\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{c})
and
\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{b})
are not adjacent, hence

d\left(\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{c}),\mathscr{I}_{m}(\mathbf{b})\right)\geq|\mathscr{I}_{m}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m-1},e+1)|=\frac{1}{e(e+1)}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{b_{k}(b_{k}-1)}\\
\geq\frac{1}{E^{2m}}.

∎

For the rest of the proof,
E\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 6}
will remain fixed. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
and each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, let
\mathbf{e}^{m,n}=\mathbf{e}^{m}=(e^{m}_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in[\![3,E-1]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence on
\mathscr{D}
such that
|a_{jm}-e_{j}^{m}|\geq 2
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and write
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}^{m,n})_{m,n}
. By Lemma
5.6
, for any
m\geq 2
and any
\mathbf{b}=(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})
, the number
y=\Lambda(\mathbf{b})\in(0,1]
satisfies
|y-x|>E^{-2m}
. In fact, when
j\in[\![0,m-1]\!]
, we have
|\mathscr{L}^{j}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{j}(y)|\geq E^{-2(m-j)}
, so

(5.1)

|\mathscr{L}^{j}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{j}(y)|\geq\frac{1}{E^{2(m-j)}}\geq\frac{1}{E^{2m}}.

Since
\mathscr{L}^{m}(x)=\left\langle a_{m+1},a_{m+2},\ldots\right\rangle
and
\mathscr{L}^{m}(y)=\left\langle b_{m+1},\ldots,b_{2(m-1)-1},e_{2}^{m},\ldots\right\rangle
, inequality (
5.1
) holds for
j=m
. Moreover, an inductive argument gives (
5.1
) for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As a consequence,
\Lambda[F_{m}^{n}(x)]\subseteq\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
and, since
m
and
n
were arbitrary,

(5.2)

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 2\\
n\geq E\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})\right]\subseteq\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x).

5.2.2.
Hausdorff dimension estimate

We will use Lemma
2.5
to show that the smaller set in (
5.2
) has full Hausdorff dimension. To be more precise, for any
s\in(0,1)
we find some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\Lambda(F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e}))\geq s
which, due to (
5.2
), implies
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=1
.

Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, write
\mathscr{A}_{0}=\{[0,1]\}
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k(m-1)})\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}
define

A^{k}(\mathbf{c}):=A^{k}(\mathbf{c};n,m):=\overline{\mathscr{I}_{mk}}\left(\mathscr{B}_{m}\left(\mathbf{c},(e_{1}^{m},e_{2}^{m},\ldots,e_{k}^{m})\right)\right)

and

\mathscr{A}_{k}\colon=\left\{A^{k}(\mathbf{c})\colon\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}\right\}.

The limit set of
\mathscr{A}(m,n)=\{\mathscr{A}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is precisely
\Lambda[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})]
.

Proposition 5.7
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
, there is a constant
B=B(m,n)>0
such that every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}
and
\mathbf{f},\mathbf{b}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}
with
\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{f}
satisfy

d\left(A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{b}),A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{f})\right)\geq B|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
be arbitrary. For any
e\in[\![3,E-1]\!]
, the compact sets belonging to
\{\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{m}(\mathbf{b}e):\mathbf{b}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}\}
are pairwise disjoint. Hence, there is some constant
B_{1}(n,m;e)>0
such that for two different
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{f}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}
we have

d\left(\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{m}(\mathbf{b}e),\overline{\mathscr{I}}_{m}(\mathbf{f}e)\right)\geq B_{1}(m,n;e).

Put

B:=B(m,n):=\min\{B_{1}(m,n;3),\ldots,B_{1}(m,n;E-1)\}>0.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{(m-1)k}
, and
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{f}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}
with
\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{f}
, we write
\mathbf{g}:=\mathscr{B}_{m}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{e}^{m})
. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle d\left(A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{b}),A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{f})\right)

\displaystyle=d\left(\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{g}}^{km}[A^{1}(\mathbf{b})],\mathscr{T}_{\mathbf{g}}^{km}[A^{1}(\mathbf{f})]\right)

\displaystyle=|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|\;d\left(A^{1}(\mathbf{b}),A^{1}(\mathbf{f})\right)

\displaystyle\geq|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|B.

∎

Proposition 5.8
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
define

d_{k}\left(\mathscr{A}(m,n)\right):=\max\left\{|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|:\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}\right\}.

Then, we have

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(d_{k}(\mathscr{A}(m,n))^{-1}\right)}{k}>0.

Proof.

Let
m,n,k
be as in the statement and
\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}
. Then,
|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|\leq 2^{-mk}
and

d_{k}\left(\mathscr{A}(m,n)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2^{km}}.

Direct computations lead to the desired conclusion.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

For each
s\in(0,1)
, there are
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
such that

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\Lambda[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})]\geq s.

Proof.

Take
s\in(0,1)
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq E}
be such that

n\geq E,\quad\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{j^{s}(j-1)^{s}}>1,\;\text{ and }\;\frac{1}{E^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{j^{s}(j-1)^{s}}\right)^{m-1}>1.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{c}\in[\![2,n]\!]^{k(m-1)}
be arbitrary. For every
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m-1})\in[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}
we have

\left|A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{b})\right|=\left|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})\right|\frac{1}{e_{k+1}^{m}(e_{k+1}^{m}-1)}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}\geq|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|\frac{1}{E^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}.

Therefore, letting
\mathbf{b}
run along
[\![2,n]\!]^{m-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{b}}|A^{k+1}(\mathbf{c}\mathbf{b})|^{s}

\displaystyle\geq|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|^{s}\frac{1}{E^{2s}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}^{s}(b_{j}-1)^{s}}

\displaystyle=|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|^{s}\frac{1}{E^{2s}}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{j^{s}(j-1)^{s}}\right)^{m-1}

\displaystyle\geq|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|^{s}\frac{1}{E^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{j^{s}(j-1)^{s}}\right)^{m-1}>|A^{k}(\mathbf{c})|^{s}.

Let
B=B(m,n)
be as in Proposition
5.7
. Taking into account the constant sequence
(B_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
B_{n}=B
and Proposition
5.8
, we can apply Lemma
2.5
to conclude

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\Lambda\left[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})\right]\geq s.

∎

We are now in the position to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
. In view of (
5.2
) and Proposition
5.9
, we have

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 2\\
n\geq E\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})\right]\right)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 2\\
n\geq E\end{subarray}}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\Lambda\left[F_{m}^{n}(\mathbf{e})\right]\right)=1,

so
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=1
. The density of
\operatorname{D}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
follows immediately from Proposition
5.5
and the proof of Theorem
B
is now complete.

6.
Devaney chaos: proof of Theorem
C

Let us show
([0,1],\mathscr{L})
is topologically transitive. Let
U,V
be two non-empty open subsets of
[0,1]
. Pick a non-zero
x\in V
and write
x=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\rangle
. Take a non-zero
y\in U
, write
y=\langle b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots\rangle
, and choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{b})\subseteq U
(it exists by Corollary
2.3
). Then, the point

z=\langle b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\rangle

belongs to
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{b})\subseteq U
and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(z)=x\in V
; that is,
\mathscr{L}^{n}(z)\in\mathscr{L}^{n}[U]\cap V
. Using similar ideas, it is not hard to show that
([0,1],\mathscr{L})
is locally eventually onto; that is, for every non-empty open subset
U
there exists some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}[U]=[0,1]
.

Since
\Lambda\colon\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,1]
is continuous and onto, it maps dense subsets of
\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
onto dense subsets of
[0,1]
. In particular,
\Lambda
maps the set of periodic sequences to a dense subset of
[0,1]
. Moreover, by
\Lambda\circ\sigma=\mathscr{L}\circ\Lambda
, the function
\Lambda
maps periodic sequences onto periodic points with respect to
\mathscr{L}
(see Proposition
2.1
).

Finally, we show that
([0,1],\mathscr{L})
is sensitive to initial conditions. Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary and take
x\in[0,1]
. For
x=0
, take
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
such that
N^{-1}<\delta
. Then, the point

y=\langle N+1,2,2,2,2,2,\ldots\rangle=\frac{1}{N}

satisfies
|x-y|<N^{-1}<\delta
and
|\mathscr{L}(x)-\mathscr{L}(y)|=|0-1|=1
. If
x\in(0,1]
, write
x=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\rangle
. Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\subseteq B(x;\delta)
and let
z
be the only element in the set

\left\{\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\frac{1}{2},\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\right\}\cap(0,1].

Write
z=\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\rangle
and define

y=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\rangle.

Hence, we have
|x-y|<\delta
and
|\mathscr{L}^{n}(x)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(y)|=\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, the system
([0,1],\mathscr{L})
is Devaney chaotic.

It is not hard to adapt the argument above to show that
([0,1),\mathscr{G})
is also Devaney chaotic.

7.
A scrambled set: proof of Theorem
D

The proof is divided into three parts. In the first part, we construct an
\mathscr{L}
-scrambled set
S
. Afterwards, we show that
S
is in fact scrambled with respect to
\mathscr{L}
. Finally, we show that
S
has Hausdorff dimension
1
.

7.1.
Construction of
S

For each
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
, let
g(\mathbf{a})\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the limit of the finite words
(\mathbf{A}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
given by
\mathbf{A}_{1}=2a_{1}
and
\mathbf{A}_{n+1}=\mathbf{A}_{n}2^{n+1}\pref_{n+1}(\mathbf{a})
; that is,

g(\mathbf{a})=(2,a_{1},2,2,a_{1},a_{2},2,2,2,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots).

Consider
R=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}[\![m^{3}+1,m^{3}+2m]\!]
and define
\mathscr{F}:\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{a})=\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{a},g(\mathbf{a});R)\text{ for all }\mathbf{a}\in\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Note that both functions
g
and
\mathscr{F}
are continuous. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
define

\widetilde{S}_{N}\colon=\mathscr{F}\left[[\![2,N]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}\right]\subseteq\mathscr{D}^{\mathbb{N}},\quad S_{N}\colon=\Lambda\left[\widetilde{S}_{N}\right]\subseteq(0,1]

and put
S\colon=\bigcup_{N\geq 3}S_{N}
.

7.2.
The set
S
is
\mathscr{L}
-scrambled

We now show that
S
is scrambled with respect to
\mathscr{L}
. In other words, if
\alpha,\beta\in S
are different, then

\liminf_{n\to\infty}|\mathscr{L}^{n}(\alpha)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(\beta)|=0\;\text{ and }\;\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\mathscr{L}^{n}(\alpha)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(\beta)|>0.

Take
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
. Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 1},\mathbf{b}=(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in[\![2,N]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{b}
. Define

x\colon=\Lambda(\mathbf{a}),\quad\alpha\colon=\Lambda\circ\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{a}),\quad y\colon=\Lambda(\mathbf{b}),\quad\beta\colon=\Lambda\circ\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{b}).

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}}(\alpha)=\langle\underbrace{2,2,\ldots,2}_{m\text{ times}},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},\ldots\rangle\;\text{ and }\;\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}}(\beta)=\langle\underbrace{2,2,\ldots,2}_{m\text{ times}},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m},\ldots\rangle;

hence,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}}(\alpha)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}}(\beta)=1

and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(\alpha)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(\beta)\right|=0.

On the other hand, since

\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}+m}(\alpha)=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},2,\ldots\rangle\;\text{ and }\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}+m}(\beta)=\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{m},2\ldots\rangle,

we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}+m}(\alpha)=x\;\text{ and }\;\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathscr{L}^{m^{3}+m}(\beta)=y.

Because
\Lambda
is bijective, we have
x\neq y
and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathscr{L}^{n}(\alpha)-\mathscr{L}^{n}(\beta)\right|\geq|x-y|>0.

Therefore,
(\alpha,\beta)
is an
\mathscr{L}
-scrambled pair and
S
is an
\mathscr{L}
-scrambled set.

7.3.
Hausdorff dimension estimates

Define
Q:=\mathbb{N}\setminus R
and write
Q=\{q_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
with
q_{n}<q_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\pi_{Q}
be the function

\pi_{Q}\colon\bigcup_{N\geq 3}\widetilde{S}_{N}\to\bigcup_{N\geq 3}[\![2,N]\!]^{\mathbb{N}}

given by

\pi_{Q}\left((a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\right)=(a_{q_{j}})_{j\geq 1}.

Recall that
F_{N}\colon=\Lambda[[\![2,N]\!]]
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
. The map
\mathscr{Y}\colon=\mathscr{F}^{-1}
,

\mathscr{Y}\colon\bigcup_{N\geq 3}\widetilde{S}_{N}\to\bigcup_{N\geq 3}F_{N},

is given by
\mathscr{Y}=\Lambda\circ\pi_{Q}\circ\Lambda^{-1}
and is continuous. Moreover, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
, the restriction of
\mathscr{Y}
to
S_{N}
establishes a homeomorphism between
S_{N}
and
F_{N}
which is, by Lemma
7.1
below, locally Hölder-continuous.

Lemma 7.1
.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
r=r(N,\varepsilon)>0
such that for any
\beta\in S_{N}
and any
\alpha\in S_{N}\cap B(\beta;r)

\left|\mathscr{Y}(\alpha)-\mathscr{Y}(\beta)\right|\ll_{N}|\alpha-\beta|^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}.

We need to two preliminary results for the proof of Lemma
7.1
.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
t(n)\colon=\#(R\cap[\![1,n]\!])
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
t(n)\asymp n^{\frac{2}{3}}
.

Proof.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 8}
and define
m=[n^{\frac{1}{3}}]
. Then,
2\leq m\leq n^{\frac{1}{3}}<m+1
, so

\displaystyle t(n)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{m}j=m(m+1)=m^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{m}\right)\leq 2n^{\frac{2}{3}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle t(n)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}j=(m-1)m=(m+1)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{m+1}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{m+1}\right)\geq\frac{2}{9}n^{\frac{2}{3}}.

∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\gamma=\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
,
\delta=\left\langle h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},\ldots\right\rangle
belong to
F_{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
(see (
2.9
)). If
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
c_{j}=h_{j}
for each
j\in[\![1,n_{0}]\!]
and
c_{n_{0}+1}\neq h_{n_{0}+1}
, then

\left|\gamma-\delta\right|\geq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n_{0}}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n_{0}})\right|\frac{1}{N^{3}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
c_{n_{0}+1}>h_{n_{0}+1}
. From this inequality,

\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{c_{n_{0}+1}-1}\;\text{, and }\;\frac{1}{h_{n_{0}+1}}+\frac{1}{h_{n_{0}+1}(h_{n_{0}+1}-1)N}<\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\delta),

we obtain

\left|\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\gamma)-\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\delta)\right|=\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\delta)-\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}(N-1)}>\frac{1}{N^{3}}.

Writing
\mathbf{c}=(c_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, we conclude

\displaystyle|\gamma-\delta|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathscr{T}^{n_{0}}_{\mathbf{c}}\left(\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\gamma)\right)-\mathscr{T}^{n_{0}}_{\mathbf{c}}\left(\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\delta)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathscr{I}_{n_{0}}(\mathbf{c})\right|\left|\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\gamma)-\mathscr{L}^{n_{0}}(\delta)\right|\geq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n_{0}}(\mathbf{c})\right|\frac{1}{N^{3}}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
are given and let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

(7.1)

\frac{n}{t(n)}\geq 1+\frac{2\log N}{\varepsilon\log 2}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq n_{0}}

(it exists by Proposition
7.2
). Take
\mathbf{b}\in\widetilde{S}_{N}
and put
\beta=\Lambda(\mathbf{b})
. Let
r>0
be such that the first
n_{0}
Lüroth digits of every number in
S_{N}\cap B(\beta;r)
coincide with those of
\beta
. It is not hard to show that such an
r
actually exists since
S_{N}
can be expressed as a countable intersection of a nested sequence compact sets, each of which is defined by restricting the first Lüroth digits. Furthermore,
r
can be chosen independently of
\beta
by Corollary
2.3
. Let
\gamma=\left\langle c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots\right\rangle\neq\beta
belong to
S_{N}\cap B(\beta;r)
, write
\mathbf{c}=(c_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and call
n\in\mathbb{N}
the largest natural number satisfying
\pref(\mathbf{c};n)=\pref(\mathbf{b};n)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle|\mathscr{I}_{n}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})|

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j\in Q\cap[\![1,n]\!]}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in R\cap[\![1,n]\!]}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\prod_{j\in Q\cap[\![1,n]\!]}\frac{1}{b_{j}(b_{j}-1)}\right)\frac{1}{N^{2t(n)}}

\displaystyle=|\mathscr{I}_{n-t(n)}(\pi_{Q}(\mathbf{b}))|\frac{1}{N^{2t(n)}}\geq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n-t(n)}(\pi_{Q}(\mathbf{b}))\right|^{1+\varepsilon}.

The last inequality holds by (
7.1
) and
|\mathscr{I}_{n-t(n)}(\pi_{Q}(\mathbf{b}))|\leq 2^{-(n-t(n))}
. Hence, in view of Proposition
7.3
, we conclude that

\left|\mathscr{Y}_{N}(\gamma)-\mathscr{Y}_{N}(\delta)\right|\leq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n-t(n)}\left(\pi_{Q}(\mathbf{b})\right)\right|\leq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n}(\mathbf{b})\right|^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}\leq\left|\mathscr{I}_{n_{0}}(\mathbf{b})\right|^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}\ll_{N}|\gamma-\delta|^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}.

∎

7.4.
Proof of Theorem
D

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
be arbitrary. Given
\varepsilon>0
, take
r>0
as in Lemma
7.1
and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be points in the compact set
S_{N}
such that

(7.2)

S_{N}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}B(x_{j};r).

Lemma
7.1
guarantees that, for each
j\in[\![1,n]\!]
, the function
\mathscr{Y}
restricted to
B(x_{0};r)\cap S_{N}
is
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}
-Hölder continuous, so

(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\mathscr{Y}\left[B(x_{j};r)\cap S_{N}\right]\right)\leq\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(B(x_{j};r)\cap S_{N}\right)

(see Proposition 3.3 in
[
7
]
). Then, by (

) and

,

\displaystyle\dim_{\operatorname{H}}S_{N}

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}B(x_{j};r)\cap S_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(B(x_{j};r)\cap S_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\mathscr{Y}\left[B(x_{j};r)\cap S_{N}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathscr{Y}\left[B(x_{0};r)\cap S_{N}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\mathscr{Y}\left[S_{N}\right]\right)=(1+\varepsilon)\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(F_{N}).

Since

, we have

and Theorem

follows:

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(S)=\sup_{N}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(S_{N})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(S_{N})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\dim_{\operatorname{H}}(F_{N})=1.

8.
Further problems

Theorem
A
provides uncountably many numbers
x\in[0,1]
such that
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=0
and uncountably many
x\in[0,1]
such that
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}
. It also shows that
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
lies between
0
and
\frac{1}{2}
for any
x\in[0,1]
. However, it does not say what happens between these values. These observations prompt new questions.

Problem 1
.

What is the image of the function
x\mapsto\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
?

Define

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathscr{L};0)

\displaystyle\colon=\left\{x\in[0,1]\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)=0\displaystyle\right\},

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\mathscr{L};\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\colon=\left\{x\in[0,1]\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

By Theorem
A
,
\Theta(\mathscr{L};0)
contains the set of numbers in
[0,1]
whose Lüroth expansion has bounded digits, hence

\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\Theta(\mathscr{L};0)=1.

However, Theorem
A
only allows us to conclude that
\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\Theta(\mathscr{L},\frac{1}{2})\geq\frac{1}{2}
, for it contains all the real numbers in
[0,1]
whose Lüroth digits tend to infinity.

Problem 2
.

What is the Hausdorff dimension of
\Theta(\mathscr{L},\frac{1}{2})
?

We can refine the previous problems if
x\mapsto\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}(x)
takes more than two values.

Problem 3
.

Estimate for each
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
the Hausdorff dimension of the set

\Theta(\mathscr{L};\alpha)\colon=\{x\in[0,1]\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{L}}\displaystyle(x)=\alpha\}.

Recall that Theorems
A
,
B
, and
D
are full analogues of the main results in
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
. It is thus natural to pose problems above for
([0,1),\mathscr{G})
.

Problem 4
.

What is the image of the function
x\mapsto\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}(x)
?

Consider the sets

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathscr{G};0)

\displaystyle\colon=\left\{x\in[0,1)\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}\displaystyle(x)=0\right\},

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\mathscr{G};\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\colon=\left\{x\in[0,1)\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}\displaystyle(x)=\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

Since the set of badly approximable numbers– that is, those real numbers whose regular continued fraction is bounded– has full Hausdorff dimension (see

implies that

. On the other hand, by Good’s Theorem (

.

Problem 5
.

What is the Hausdorff dimension of
\Theta(\mathscr{G};\frac{1}{2})
?

Problem 6
.

For each each
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the set

\Theta(\mathscr{G};\alpha)\colon=\{x\in[0,1)\colon\dim_{\operatorname{H}}\textstyle\operatorname{AS}_{\mathscr{G}}\displaystyle(x)=\alpha\}.
