Heat flow in a periodically forced, thermostatted chain II
Tomasz Komorowski, Joel L. Lebowitz, Stefano Olla

Abstract
. We derive a macroscopic heat equation for the temperature of a pinned harmonic chain subject to a periodic force at its right side and in contact with a heat bath at its left side. The microscopic dynamics in the bulk is given by the Hamiltonian equation of motion plus a reversal of the velocity of a particle occurring independently for each particle at exponential times, with rate γ \gamma . The latter produces a finite heat conductivity. Starting with an initial probability distribution for a chain of n n particles we compute the current and the local temperature given by the expected value of the local energy. Scaling space and time diffusively yields, in the n → + ∞ n\to+\infty limit, the heat equation for the macroscopic temperature profile T ⁡ ( t , u ) , T(t,u), t > 0 t>0 , u ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] u\in[0,1] . It is to be solved for initial conditions T ⁡ ( 0 , u ) T(0,u) and specified T ⁡ ( t , 0 ) = T − T(t,0)=T_{-} , the temperature of the left heat reservoir and a fixed heat flux J J , entering the system at u = 1 u=1 . | J | |J| equals the work done by the periodic force which is computed explicitly for each n n .



1.
Introduction

The emergence of the heat equation from a microscopic dynamics after a diffusive
rescaling of space and time is a challenging mathematical problem
in non-equilibrium statistical mechanics
[
6
]
.
Here we study this problem in the context of conversion of work into heat
in a simple model: a pinned harmonic chain.
The system is in contact at its left end with a thermal reservoir
at temperature
T_{-}
which acts
on the leftmost particle via a Langevin force (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process).
The rightmost particle is acted on by a deterministic periodic force
which does work on the system.
The work pumps energy into the system with the energy then
flowing into the reservoir in the form of heat.

To describe this flow we need to know the heat conductivity of the system.
As it is well known, the harmonic crystal has an infinite heat conductivity
[
19
]
.
To model realistic systems with finite heat conductivity we add to the harmonic dynamics
a random velocity reversal.
It models in a simple way the various dissipative mechanisms in real systems
and produces a finite conductivity (cf.
[
5
]
,
[
1
]
).

In paper
[
14
]
, which is the first part of the present work, we studied this system in the limit

t\to\infty
, see Section
2.1
for rigorous statements
of the main
results obtained there.
In this limit the probability distribution
of the phase space configurations is periodic with the
period of the external force, see Theorem
2.1
below. We also showed that with a proper scaling
of the force and period the averaged temperature
profile satisfies the stationary heat
equation with an explicitly given heat current. In the present paper we study the time dependent evolution of the system,
on the diffusive
time scale, starting with some specified initial distribution.
We derive a heat equation for the temperature profile of the system.

The periodic forcing generates a Neumann type boundary condition for the
macroscopic heat equation, so that the gradient of the temperature
at the boundary must satisfy Fourier law with the boundary energy current
generated by the work of the periodic forcing (see (
2.35
) below).
On the left side the boundary condition is given by the assigned value

T_{-}
, the temperature of the heat bath.
As
t\to\infty
the profile converges to the macroscopic profile obtained in
[
14
]
.

The energy diffusion in the harmonic chain on a finite lattice,
with energy conserving noise and
Langevin heat bath at different temperatures at the boundaries, have been previously
considered
[
4
,
2
,
3
,
13
,
18
]
.
But complete mathematical results, describing the time evolution of
the macroscopic temperature profile, have been obtained only for unpinned chains

[
4
,
13
]
.

This article gives the first proof of the heat equation for the pinned chain in a finite interval,
and the method can be applied with different boundary conditions
(see Remark
2.12
).
Investigation about energy transport in anharmonic chain
under periodic forcing can be found in
[
11
]
,
[
10
]
,
and very recently in
[
20
]
.
In the review article
[
16
]
we considered various extensions
of the present results to
unpinned, multidimensional and anharmonic dynamics.

2.33
) implies

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}{\cal E}_{x}(0)\right]<+\infty.

Furthermore, since the Hamiltonian
{\cal H}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a convex
function, by the Jensen inequality

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{n+1}\mathcal{H}_{n}(\overline{{\bf q}}_{n},\overline{{\bf p}}_{n})\leqslant\sup_{n\geqslant 1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}({\bf q},{\bf p})\right]<+\infty,

so (
2.28
) is satisfied with
\delta=1
.

Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\rm fin}([0,1])
, resp
\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,1])
the space of bounded variation,
Borel, resp. positive, measures on the interval
[0,1]

endowed with the weak topology.
Before formulating the main result we introduce the notion of a
measured valued solution of the following initial-boundary value
problem

(2.35)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}T=\frac{D}{4\gamma}\partial_{u}^{2}T,\quad u\in(0,1),\\
&T(t,0)=T_{-},\quad\partial_{u}T(t,1)=-\frac{4\gamma J}{D},\quad T(0,\mathrm{d}u)=T_{0}(\mathrm{d}u).\end{split}

Here
J
and
D
are defined by (
2.19
)
and (
2.25
), respectively and
T_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm fin}([0,1])
.

Definition 2.9
.

We say that
a function
T:[0,+\infty)\to\mathcal{M}_{\rm fin}([0,1])
is a weak (measured
valued) solution of (
2.35
) if: it belongs to

C\Big([0,+\infty);\mathcal{M}_{\rm fin}([0,1])\Big)
and
for any
\varphi\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that

\varphi(0)=\varphi^{\prime}(1)=0
we have

(2.36)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)T(t,\mathrm{d}u)-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)T_{0}(\mathrm{d}u)=&\frac{D}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u)T(s,\mathrm{d}u)\\
&+\frac{DT_{-}t}{4\gamma}\varphi^{\prime}(0)-Jt\varphi(1).\end{split}

The proof of the uniqueness of the solution of (
2.36
) is quite
routine. For completeness sake we present it
in
Appendix E
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose that the initial configurations
(\mu_{n})
satisfy
(
2.33
). Assume furthermore
that there exists
T_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,1])
such that

(2.37)

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{n+1}\right)\mathcal{E}_{x}(0)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)T_{0}(\mathrm{d}u),

for any function
\varphi\in C[0,1]
- the space of continuous
functions on
[0,1]
.
Here

\mathcal{E}_{x}(t)={\mathcal{E}}_{x}({\bf q}(t),{\bf p}(t))
.
Then,

(2.38)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{n+1}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left({\cal E}_{x}(n^{2}t)\right)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)T(t,\mathrm{d}u).

Here
T(t,\mathrm{d}u)
is the unique weak solution of
(
2.35
), with the initial data given by measure
T_{0}
in (
2.37
).

Remark 2.11
.

The initial energy
\cal E_{x}(0)
can be represented
as the sum
\cal E_{x}^{\rm th}+\cal E_{x}^{\rm mech}
of the
thermal energy

\cal E_{x}^{\rm th}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[(p^{\prime}_{x})^{2}+(q^{\prime}_{x}-q^{\prime}_{x-1})^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}(q^{\prime}_{x})^{2}\right]

and the
mechanical energy

\cal E_{x}^{\rm mech}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[\overline{p}_{x}^{2}+(\overline{q}_{x}-\overline{q}_{x-1})^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}\overline{q}_{x}^{2}\right].

Here

q^{\prime}_{x}=q_{x}-\overline{q}_{x}
and
p^{\prime}_{x}=p_{x}-\overline{p}_{x}
, with

\overline{p}_{x}:=\int_{\Omega_{n}}p_{x}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}{\bf q},\mathrm{d}{\bf p})
and
\overline{q}_{x}:=\int_{\Omega_{n}}q_{x}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}{\bf q},\mathrm{d}{\bf p})
.

If
\cal E_{x}^{\rm mech}\not=0
and
satisfies (
2.28
), with
\delta=1
, then
the initial measure
T_{0}(du)
is the macroscopic distribution of the total energy and
not of the
temperature
, where the latter is understood as the thermal energy.
Nevertheless, as a consequence of Proposition
B.1
,
at any macroscopic positive time the entire mechanical energy is transformed
immediately into the
thermal energy, so that
T(t,\mathrm{d}u)
for
t>0
can be seen as the macroscopic
temperature distribution. The situation is different for the unpinned dynamics
(
\omega_{0}=0
) where the transfer of mechanical energy
to thermal energy happens slowly at macroscopic times (see
[
13
]
).

Remark 2.12
.

Concerning Theorem
2.10
, a similar proof will work
in the case where two Langevin heat baths
at two temperatures,
T_{-}
and
T_{+}

are placed at the boundaries, in the absence of the periodic forcing.
In this case the macroscopic equation
will be the same but with boundary conditions

T(t,0)=T_{-}
and
T(t,1)=T_{+}
.

Also, in the absence of any heat bath, we could apply two periodic forces
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(0)}(t)
and

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(1)}(t)
respectively at the left and right boundary.
They will generate two incoming energy current,
J^{(0)}>0
on the left and
J^{(1)}<0
on the right,
given by the corresponding formula (
2.19
), and we will have
the same equation but with boundary conditions

\partial_{u}T(t,0)=-\frac{4\gamma J^{(0)}}{D}
and
\partial_{u}T(t,1)=-\frac{4\gamma J^{(1)}}{D}
.
Of course in this case the total energy increases in time and
periodic stationary states do not exist.

In the case where both a heat bath and a periodic force are present on the
same side,
say on the right endpoint,
then the macroscopic boundary condition arising
is
T(t,1)=T_{+}
, i.e. the periodic forcing is
ineffective on the macroscopic level,
and all the energy generated by its work
will flow into the heat bath. It would be interesting to
investigate what happens when the amplitude of the forcing is
larger than considered here (
-1/2<a\leqslant 0
in
(
2.30
)). However, it
is not yet clear to us what occurs in this case.

Remark 2.13
.

If the initial data
T_{0}
is
C^{1}
smooth and satisfies the boundary condition in (
2.35
),
then the initial-boundary value problem (
2.35
) has a unique strong solution
T(t,u)
that belongs
to the intersection of the spaces

C\big([0,+\infty)\times[0,1]\big)
and
C^{1,2}\big((0,+\infty)\times(0,1)\big)
- the space of functions
continuously differentiable once in the first and twice in the
second variable, see e.g.
[
8
, Corollary 5.3.2,
p.147]
. This solution coincides then with the unique weak
solution in the sense of Definition
2.9
.

Remark 2.14
.

In the proof of
Theorem 2.10
we need to show a result about the equipartition of energy
(cf. Proposition
4.1
). As a consequence the limit profile of the energy
equals the limit profile of the temperature, i.e. we have

(2.39)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\varphi\left(t,\frac{x}{n+1}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left({p}_{x}^{2}(n^{2}t)\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(t,u)T(t,\mathrm{d}u),

for any test function as in the statement of Theorem

.

3.
Entropy, energy and currents bounds

We first prove that the initial entropy bound (
2.33
) holds for all times.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that the law of the initial configuration admits the density
f_{n}(0,{\bf q},{\bf p})
w.r.t. the Gibbs measure
\nu_{T-}
that satisfies
(
2.33
). Then, for any
t>0
there exists
f_{n}(t,{\bf q},{\bf p})
- the density of the law of the configuration

\big({\bf q}(t),{\bf p}(t)\big)
.
In addition,
for any
t
there exists a constant
C
independent of
n
such that

(3.1)

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\mathbf{H}_{n}(f_{n}(n^{2}s))\leqslant Cn,

Proof.

For simplicity sake, we present here a proof under an additional
assumption that
f_{n}(t,{\bf q},{\bf p})
is a smooth function such
that
\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}{\bf q},\mathrm{d}{\bf p})=f_{n}(t,{\bf q},{\bf p})\mathrm{d}{\bf q}\mathrm{d}{\bf p}
is the solution of the forward equation (
2.15
).
The general case is treated in Appendix
F
.
Using (
2.9
) for the generator
{\cal G}_{t}
we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{n}(f_{n}(n^{2}t))-\mathbf{H}_{n}(f_{n}(0))=\int_{0}^{n^{2}t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}(s){\cal G}_{s}\log f_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}\nu_{T-}={\rm I}_{n}+{\rm II}_{n},

with

\displaystyle{\rm I}_{n}:=\gamma\int_{0}^{n^{2}t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}(s)\left({\cal S}_{\rm flip}+2{\cal S}_{-}\right)\log f_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}\nu_{T-},

\displaystyle{\rm II}_{n}:=\int_{0}^{n^{2}t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{n}(s){\cal A}_{s}\log f_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}\nu_{T-}.

We have that
{\rm I}_{n}\leqslant 0
because
{\cal S}_{\rm flip}
and

{\cal S}_{-}
are symmetric negative operators with respect to the measure
\nu_{T-}
.
The only positive contribution comes from the second term
where the boundary work defined by (
2.27
) appears:

\displaystyle{\rm II}_{n}=\int_{0}^{n^{2}t}\mathrm{d}s{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(s)\int_{\Omega_{n}}\frac{p_{n}}{T_{-}}f_{n}(s)\mathrm{d}\nu_{T-}=-\frac{n}{T_{-}}{J_{n}(t,\mu_{0})},

where
\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}:=f_{n}(0)\mathrm{d}\nu_{T-}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{n}(f_{n}(n^{2}t))\leqslant\mathbf{H}_{n}(f_{n}(0))-\frac{n}{T_{-}}J_{n}(t,\mu_{0}).

The conclusion of the proposition then follows from a direct
application of (
2.33
) and Theorem
2.5
.
∎

To abbreviate the notation we shall omit the index by the
expectation sign, indicating the initial
condition.

Corollary 3.2

(Energy bound)
.

For any
t_{*}\geqslant 0
we have

(3.2)

\sup_{t\in[0,t_{*}]}\sup_{n\geqslant 1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}{\cal E}_{x}(n^{2}t)\right]=E(t_{*})<+\infty.

Proof.

It follows from the entropy inequality, see e.g.

[
9
, p. 338]
, that for
\alpha>0
small enough we can find
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

(3.3)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x=0}^{n}{\cal E}_{x}(n^{2}t)\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(C_{\alpha}n+\mathbf{H}_{n}(t)\big),\qquad t\geqslant 0.

∎

From Theorem
2.5
and Corollary
3.2
we
immediately conclude the following.

Corollary 3.3

(Current size)
.

For any
t_{*}\geqslant 0
there exists
C>0
such that

(3.4)

\sup_{x=0,\ldots,n+1,\,t\in[0,t_{*}]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[j_{x-1,x}(n^{2}s)\right]\mathrm{d}s\right|\leqslant\frac{C}{n},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.

In particular, for any
t>0
there exists
C>0
such that

(3.5)

\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Big\{\mathbb{E}\big[p_{0}^{2}(n^{2}s)\big]-T_{-}\Big\}\mathrm{d}s\Big|\leqslant\frac{C}{n},

Proof.

By the local conservation of energy

(3.6)

n^{-2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathbb{E}}[{\cal E}_{x}(n^{2}t)]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[j_{x-1,x}(n^{2}t)-j_{x,x+1}(n^{2}t)\Big].

Therefore

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}j_{x-1,x}(n^{2}s)\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}j_{n,n+1}(n^{2}s)\mathrm{d}s+n^{-2}\sum_{y=x}^{n}\Big({\mathbb{E}}[{\cal E}_{y}(n^{2}t)]-{\mathbb{E}}[{\cal E}_{y}(0)]\Big),

and bound (
3.4
) follows directly from estimates (
2.29
)
and (
3.2
). Estimate (
3.5
) is a consequence of the
definition of
j_{-1,0}
(see (
2.14
)) and (
3.4
).
∎

4.
Equipartition of energy and
Fluctuation-dissipation relations

4.1.
Equipartition of the energy

In the present section we show the equipartition
property of the energy.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
\varphi\in{C^{1}}[0,1]
is such that
{\rm supp}\,\varphi\subset(0,1)
. Then,

(4.1)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{n+1}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[p_{x}^{2}(n^{2}s)-\big(q_{x}(n^{2}s)-q_{x-1}(n^{2}s)\big)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}q_{x}^{2}(n^{2}s)\Big]\mathrm{d}s=0.

Proof.

After a simple calculation we obtain the following
fluctuation-dissipation relation: for
x=1,\dots,n-1
,

(4.2)

p_{x}^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}q_{x}^{2}-(q_{x}-q_{x-1})^{2}=\nabla^{\star}\left[q_{x}(q_{x+1}-q_{x})\right]+\mathcal{G}_{t}\left(q_{x}p_{x}+\gamma q_{x}^{2}\right),

where the discrete gradient
\nabla
and its adjoint
\nabla^{\star}
are defined in (
A.1
) below.

Therefore,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[p_{x}^{2}(n^{2}s)-\omega^{2}_{0}q_{x}^{2}(n^{2}s)-(q_{x}(n^{2}s)-q_{x}(n^{2}s))^{2}\Big]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\nabla\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[q_{x}(n^{2}s)(q_{x+1}(n^{2}s)-q_{x}(n^{2}s))\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+n^{-2}\mathbb{E}\Big[q_{x}(n^{2}t)p_{x}(n^{2}t)+2\gamma q_{x}^{2}(n^{2}t)\Big]-n^{-2}\mathbb{E}\Big[q_{x}(0)p_{x}(0)+2\gamma q_{x}^{2}(0)\Big].

After summing up against the test function
\varphi
(that has compact support strictly contained in
(0,1)
) and using the energy bound
(
3.2
)
we conclude (
4.1
).

∎

4.2.
Fluctuation-dissipation relation

In analogy to
[
14
, Section 5.1]
define

(4.4)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{f}_{x}&:=\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left(q_{x+1}-q_{x}\right)\left(p_{x}+p_{x+1}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\left(q_{x+1}-q_{x}\right)^{2},\qquad x=0,\dots,n-1,\\
\mathfrak{F}_{x}&:=p_{x}^{2}+\left(q_{x+1}-q_{x}\right)\left(q_{x}-q_{x-1}\right)-\omega_{0}^{2}q_{x}^{2},\qquad x=0,\dots,n,\end{split}

with the convention that
q_{-1}=q_{0}
,
q_{n}=q_{n+1}
.
Then

(4.5)

j_{x,x+1}=-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\nabla\mathfrak{F}_{x}+\mathcal{G}_{t}\mathfrak{f}_{x}-\frac{\delta_{x,n-1}}{4\gamma}{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(t)\left(q_{n}-q_{n-1}\right),\quad x=0,\dots,n-1.

5.1.

Consider the subset

5.3.
Limit identification

Consider a smooth test function
\varphi\in C^{2}[0,1]

In what follows we use the following notation. For a given function

(5.8)

\displaystyle\varphi_{x}:=\varphi(\tfrac{x}{n+1}),\quad(\nabla_{n}\varphi)_{x}:=(n+1)\big(\varphi(\tfrac{x+1}{n+1})-\varphi(\tfrac{x}{n+1})\big),\,\mbox{ for }x\in\{0,\ldots,n\}.

We use the convention

\varphi(-\tfrac{1}{n+1})=\varphi(0)

Let

In what follows we show that, for any

(5.9)

\begin{split}\xi_{n}(t,\varphi)-\xi_{n}(0,\varphi)=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(0)DT_{-}t}{4\gamma}-Jt\varphi(1)+\frac{D}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{n}(s,\varphi^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}s+o_{n}.\end{split}

Then as in (

\begin{split}\xi_{n}(t,\varphi)-\xi_{n}(0,\varphi)=\frac{n^{2}}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\left(\varphi_{x+1}-\varphi_{x}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[j_{x,x+1}(n^{2}\tau)\right]\mathrm{d}\tau\\

By Theorem

.
On the other hand from (

) we have

(5.11)

\frac{n^{2}}{n+1}\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\left(\varphi_{x+1}-\varphi_{x}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[j_{x,x+1}(n^{2}\tau)\right]\mathrm{d}\tau=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathrm{I}_{n,j},

where

It is easy to see from Corollary
3.2
that

\mathrm{I}_{n,2}=\overline{o}_{n}(t).

Here the symbol
\overline{o}_{n}(t)
stands for a quantity that satisfies

(5.12)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sup_{s\in[0,t_{*}]}|\overline{o}_{n}(s)|=0.

Using the fact that
\varphi^{\prime}(1)=0
and the estimate (
B.16
)
respectively we conclude also that
\mathrm{I}_{n,3}=\overline{o}_{n}(t)
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{n,1}^{(2)}:=-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{2}\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\;\mathfrak{F}_{n}(n^{2}s)\big]\mathrm{d}s=\overline{o}_{n}(t),

Since

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{4\gamma(n+1)}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{x}{n+1}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\;p_{x}^{2}(n^{2}s)\Big]\mathrm{d}s+\overline{o}_{n}(t)

Concluding, we have obtained

Appendix E
Uniqueness of solutions to (
2.35
)

Theorem E.1
.

Suppose that
T_{0}\in{\cal M}_{\rm fin}\Big([0,1]\Big)
. Then,
the initial-boundary value problem (
2.35
) has a unique weak
solution in the sense of Definition
2.9
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{T}(s,du)
be the signed measure given by
the difference of two solutions with the same initial and boundary data.
It satisfies the equation

(E.1)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(u)\overline{T}(t,\mathrm{d}u)=\frac{D}{4\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u)\overline{T}(s,\mathrm{d}u)\end{split}

for any
\varphi\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
\varphi(0)=\varphi^{\prime}(1)=0
.

The above implies that also

(E.2)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(t,u)\overline{T}(t,\mathrm{d}u)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\partial_{s}\varphi(s,u)+\frac{D}{4\gamma}\partial_{uu}^{2}\varphi(s,u)\Big)\overline{T}(s,\mathrm{d}u)\end{split}

for any
\varphi\in C^{1,2}([0,+\infty)\times[0,1])
,
such that
\varphi(t,0)=\partial_{u}\varphi(t,1)=0
,
t\geqslant 0
.
Suppose now that
\varphi_{0}\in C^{1}[0,1]
satifies

(E.3)

\varphi_{0}(0)=\varphi_{0}^{\prime}(1)=0

and
\varphi(t,u)
is the strong solution of

(E.4)

\begin{split}&\partial_{s}\varphi(s,u)+\frac{D}{4\gamma}\partial_{u}^{2}\varphi(s,u)=0,\quad u\in(0,1),\,s<t,\\
&\varphi(s,0)=\partial_{u}\varphi(s,1)=0,\quad s<t,\\
&\varphi(t,u)=\varphi_{0}(u).\end{split}

Such a solution exists and is unique, thanks to e.g.
[
8
, Corollary 5.3.2,
p.147]
. It belongs to
C^{1,2}((-\infty,t]\times[0,1])
. We conclude that

(E.5)

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{0}(u)\overline{T}(t,\mathrm{d}u)=0

for any
\varphi_{0}\in C^{1}[0,1]
satifying (
E.3
).

Consider now an arbitrary
\psi\in C[0,1]
. Let
\varphi_{0}(u):=-u\int_{u}^{1}\psi(u^{\prime})\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}-\int_{0}^{u}\psi(u^{\prime})\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}
. It satisfies (
E.3
)
and
\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{0}(u)=\psi(u)
, thus

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{1}\psi(u)\overline{T}(s,\mathrm{d}u)=0

which ends the proof of uniqueness.
∎
