Multiway Online Correlated Selection
Guy Blanc, Moses Charikar

Abstract
We give a 0.5368 0.5368 -competitive algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching. Prior to our work, the best competitive ratio was 0.5086 0.5086 due to Fahrbach, Huang, Tao, and Zadimoghaddam (FOCS 2020). They achieved their breakthrough result by developing a subroutine called online correlated selection (OCS) which takes as input a sequence of pairs and selects one item from each pair. Importantly, the selections the OCS makes are negatively correlated. We achieve our result by defining multiway OCSes which receive arbitrarily many elements at each step, rather than just two. In addition to better competitive ratios, our formulation allows for a simpler reduction from edge-weighted online bipartite matching to OCSes. While Fahrbach et al. used a factor-revealing linear program to optimize the competitive ratio, our analysis directly connects the competitive ratio to the parameters of the multiway OCS. Finally, we show that the formulation of Farhbach et al. can achieve a competitive ratio of at most 0.5239 0.5239 , confirming that multiway OCSes are strictly more powerful.



1
Introduction

The matching problem has played a pivotal role in the development of algorithmic techniques in combinatorial optimization.
The online version of the problem was one of the first graph optimization problems studied in the online literature.
Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani
[
KVV90
]
initiated a study of the problem and gave a
1-1/e
competitive algorithm for unweighted graphs.
Here, the vertices of one side (the offline side) are known to the algorithm in advance.
The vertices of the other side appear in an online fashion.
As they appear, they must be matched irrevocably to an offline vertex (or discarded).
Since the work of
[
KVV90
]
, online bipartite matching received considerable attention and their ideas were applied to a variety of other online assignment problems.

More than a decade later, the emergence of sponsored search auctions gave renewed impetus to the study of online matching and its variants.
There is a natural correspondence here – the offline side corresponds to advertisers and the online side corresponds to impressions (i.e. opportunities to place ads).
Aggarwal et al
[
AGKM11
]
generalized the
[
KVV90
]
result to the vertex-weighted case, obtaining a
1-1/e
competitive algorithm.

The edge-weighted case had remained a tantalizing open problem until very recently.
Here, it is not hard to see that no non-trivial competitive ratio is possible without giving the algorithm additional flexibility.
Researchers have focused on the
free disposal
setting where offline vertices can be assigned multiple online vertices but the contribution of an offline vertex to the objective function is the largest of the edge weights assigned to that vertex.
(i.e. previously matched edges can be disposed for free to make room for higher weight edges).
In the display advertising setting, this corresponds to assigning multiple impressions to an advertiser.

For the edge weighted problem, a natural greedy algorithm achieves competitive ratio 0.5.
This was the best known until the recent breakthrough by Fahrbach, Huang, Tao and Zadimoghaddam
[
FHTZ20
]
who gave a 0.5086 competitive algorithm.
One of their main technical contributions was a novel algorithmic ingredient called
online correlated selection
(OCS).
This is an online subroutine that takes a sequence of pairs of vertices as input and selects one vertex from each pair such that decisions across pairs are negatively correlated.
A suitable quantification of this negative correlation was plugged into a factor revealing linear program to obtain a bound on the competitive ratio.

In this work we give generalize the OCS definition of Fahrbach et al
[
FHTZ20
]
to multiway OCSes which take in multiple vertices at each step.
Our analysis directly connects the parameters of our multiway OCS to the eventual competitive ratio without the need to go through a factor revealing linear program.
We give a general framework for obtaining multiway OCSes and instantiate this framework
to obtain an improved competitive ratio of
0.5368
for online weighted matching.
Finally, we show that the OCS definition of Fahrbach et al
[
FHTZ20
]
cannot lead to a competitive ratio better than 0.5239 demonstrating that our multiway OCSes are strictly more powerful.

1.2
Problem definition: Edge-weighted online bipartite matching

Consider some weighted bipartite graph
G=(L,R,w)
, where
L
and
R
are the left and right vertices respectively. If there is an edge between
i\in L
and
j\in R
, then
w_{ij}>0
is the weight of that edge. Otherwise,
w_{ij}=0
.

At the start, the algorithm is given the entire set of left vertices,
L
, but no information about
R
or
w
. The vertices from
R
appear in an online fashion, one by one. Hence, we refer to
L
as the
offline
vertices
R
as the
online
vertices. When an online vertex
j\in R
appears, the entries
w_{ij}
for each
i\in L
are revealed to the algorithm. The algorithm must irrevocably decide which offline vertex to match
j
to before the next online vertex appears.

The objective is to maximize the total weight of the matching. We operate in the
free disposal model
. This means a single offline vertex
i
may be matched to multiple online vertices, but only the weight of its heaviest edge is counted towards the objective. We say a randomized algorithm is “
\Gamma
-competitive” or “has competitive ratio of
\Gamma
” if the expected objective of the algorithm’s output is within a multiplicative factor of
\Gamma
of the optimal objective with hindsight.

2
Online correlated selection: Motivation and application

We withhold a full preliminaries section until
Section

6
, mentioning for now that we use
boldface
(e.g.
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
) to denote random variables.

It is well-known that even for
unweighted
online bipartite matching, deterministic algorithms cannot achieve a competitive ratio better than
0.5
. In this section, we’ll investigate what form of randomness is needed to push the competitive ratio beyond
0.5
.

At the
j^{\text{th}}
time step, the portion of the graph corresponding to
j\in R
is revealed to the algorithm and it must irrevocably match it to some
\bm{i}_{j}\in L
. In order to achieve a competitive ratio better than
0.5
, the choices
\bm{i}_{1},\bm{i}_{2},\ldots
must use randomness. What if those choices are independent?

Lemma 2.1
.

Even for
unweighted
online bipartite matching, no algorithm making decisions that are independent across time steps can achieve a competitive ratio better than
0.5
.

We include a proof of
Lemma

2.1
in
Appendix

A
. In order to break the
0.5
-competitive barrier, not only does the algorithm need to carefully select a marginal distribution for each
\bm{i}_{j}
, it also needs some “smarts” in deciding how to realize
\bm{i}_{j}
— they cannot simply be independent. The key insight underlying Online Correlated Selection (OCS) is that these tasks can be tackled individually.

We separate the online algorithm into two components. The first component is fully deterministic and has access to the input instance. At the
j^{\text{th}}
time step, it chooses a marginal distribution for
\bm{i}_{j}
as a deterministic function of the portion of the graph revealed so far. In particular, that marginal distribution does not depend on the realizations of
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{j-1}
.

The second component, the OCS, is given the marginal distribution
\bm{i}_{j}
, but
not
given any other information about the input instance — It cannot “directly see” the graph. At the
j^{\text{th}}
time step, it chooses a realization for
\bm{i}_{j}
consistent with the desired marginal as a function of its own randomness. In particular, the realization for
\bm{i}_{j}
can depend on the previous realizations of
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{j-1}
. This separation of responsibilities is summarized in
Figure

.

In the following definition, a
probability vector
is a nonnegative vector whose elements sum to
1
. It represents a marginal distribution over elements in
L
.

Definition 1

(Continuous OCS)
.

Consider a set of ground elements,
L
. A
continuous OCS
is an online algorithm that at each time step
j=1,2,\ldots
, receives a probability vector
p^{(j)}
with
|L|
elements and irrevocably chooses a winner in
\bm{i}_{j}\in L
among those with
(p^{(j)})_{i}>0
.

By
Lemma

2.1
, in order to achieve a competitive ratio of greater than
0.5
, the OCS cannot simply choose winners independently at each round according to the desired marginal distribution. In the next subsection, we examine what properties an OCS needs to have to allow for better competitive ratios.

2.1
What makes a good OCS?

The algorithm’s goal is to maximize the expected weight of the matching over the randomness of the OCS. Having many online vertices
j\in R
matched to the same offline vertex
i\in L
can be wasteful. This is because only the heaviest match counts for the objective. To counteract this, the choices the OCS makes will be
negatively correlated
. In particular we wish for,
j\neq j^{\prime}\in R
, that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}=\bm{i}_{j^{\prime}}=i]<(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot(p^{(j^{\prime})})_{i}

Unfortunately, it is impossible for the OCS to negatively correlated decisions across all time steps by any non-negligible amount. We formalize that impossibility in
Lemma

A.1
, proved in
Appendix

A
. To get around that, we negatively correlated time steps that are “close” temporally. Let
S=\{j_{1},j_{1}+1,\ldots,j_{2}\}
be a set of
consecutive
time steps. We’ll require our OCS to satisfy

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}\neq i\text{ for every }j\in S]\leq f\left(\sum_{j\in S}p^{(j)}_{i}\right)

where
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
is a function quantifying the negative correlation of an OCS. The OCS that chooses winners independently at each round according to the marginal distribution satisfies the above equation with
f_{\mathrm{trivial}}(x)\coloneqq e^{-x}
. OCSes with “good” negative correlation have
f(x)<f_{\mathrm{trivial}}(x)
and the amount
f
is smaller than
f_{\mathrm{trivial}}
directly connects to competitive ratio of the resulting algorithm.

The selections at time steps that are very far apart temporally will essentially be independent, as in the following definition.

Definition 2

(Quantifying a continuous OCS, informal version of
Definition

12
)
.

For any function
f:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
, an
f
-continuous OCS is a continuous OCS with the following guarantee. For any element
i\in L
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
each sets of consecutive time steps,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}\neq i\text{ for every }j\in S_{1}\cup\ldots\cup S_{k}]\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f\left(\sum_{j\in S_{k}}(p^{(j)})_{i}\right).

We give an algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching using an
f
-OCS as a subroutine and are able to directly connect the competitive ratio to
f
.

Theorem 1

(Informal version of
Theorem

6
)
.

For any differentiable and convex
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
, if there is an
f
-continuous OCS, there is an algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching with competitive ratio of

\displaystyle\Gamma\coloneqq 1-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(t)dt.

As expected, using
f_{\mathrm{trivial}}(x)\coloneqq e^{-x}
gives a competitive ratio of
0.5
. Any
f
-OCS for
f
strictly less than
f_{\mathrm{trivial}}
gives a competitive ratio that breaks the
0.5
barrier.

The proof of
Theorem

1
uses a similar online primal-dual formulation as that of
[
FHTZ20
]
. We find that using our definition for an OCS in place of theirs substantially simplifies that analysis. In particular, we derive a simple analytic formula for the competitive ratio whereas they use a factor-revealing LP. The source of that simplification is that their algorithm needs to decide which of three round types, “deterministic,” “randomized,” or “unmatched,” to execute at each time step. Our continuous formulation implicitly interpolates between all three round types.

2.2
Discrete OCSes

We define discrete OCSes for two reasons.

1.

It allows us to relate our results to those of
[
FHTZ20
]
who used (what we call) a discrete OCS.

2.

Later, to construct a continuous OCS, we will first construct a discrete OCS and use a reduction to turn it into a continuous OCS.

The following definition of an
m
-discrete OCS is equivalent to a continuous OCS where each element in the probability vector is forced to be an integer multiple of
\frac{1}{m}
. Therefore, continuous OCSes are generalizations of discrete OCSes.

Definition 3

(Discrete OCS)
.

Consider a set of ground elements,
L
. An
m-discrete OCS
is an online algorithm that at each time step
j=1,2,\ldots
, is given a size-
m
multiset
A_{j}
of elements in
L
and irrevocably selects a single winner
\bm{i}_{j}\in A_{j}
.

We quantify the quality of a discrete OCS similarly as for continuous OCSes.

Definition 4

(Quantifying a discrete OCS)
.

For any
m\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 2}
and
F:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
, an
(F,m)
-discrete OCS is an
m
-discrete OCS with the following guarantee. For any element
i\in L
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
each sets of consecutive time steps,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}\neq i\text{ for every }j\in S_{1}\cup\ldots\cup S_{k}]\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}F\left(\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\mathrm{count}_{i}(A_{j})\right).

where
\mathrm{count}_{i}(A_{j})
is the number of times
i
appears in the multiset
A_{j}
.

For example, the OCS that independently and uniformly
\bm{i}_{j}
from
A_{j}
at each round achieves
F_{\mathrm{trivial}}(n)=(1-1/m)^{n}
. Our goal is to construct OCSes with
F<F_{\mathrm{trivial}}
, which directly leads to competitive ratios better than
\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem 2

(Informal version of
Theorem

7
)
.

For any
m\in\mathds{N}
and convex
F:\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
, if is there an
(F,m)
-discrete OCS, there is an algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching with competitive ratio of

\displaystyle\Gamma\coloneqq 1-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{m^{n}}{(m+1)^{n+1}}\cdot F(n).

We prove (a formal version of)
Theorem

2
by showing how to construct a continuous OCS from a discrete OCS and then applying
Theorem

1
. This is done in
Section

8
.

Lastly, we connect our OCS definition to that of Fahrbach et al.

Definition 5

(
\gamma
-OCS, Definition 2 of
[
FHTZ20
]
)
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, a
\gamma
-OCS is an
(F,2)
-discrete OCS where

\displaystyle F(n)\coloneqq 2^{-n}\cdot(1-\gamma)^{\max(n-1,0)}.

As a straightforward application of
Theorem

2
, we derive an explicit formula mapping
\gamma
to a competitive ratio.

Theorem 3
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, if there is a
\gamma
-OCS, there is an algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching with a competitive ratio of

\displaystyle\Gamma=\frac{3+2\gamma}{6+3\gamma}.

(1)

We experimentally compared the competitive ratio from
Equation

1
to those of the gain-sharing LP used in
[
FHTZ20
]
. For every
\gamma\in\{0,0.01,\ldots 0.46\}
, the competitive ratios are within an additive
10^{-8}
of one-another. That small difference is likely due to rounding issues. For
\gamma\in\{0.47,0.48,\ldots,1\}
, our equation gives a greater competitive ratio. Note that, as we later prove in
Lemma

2.2
,
\gamma
-OCSes can only exist for
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{3}
and our competitive ratio appears to match theirs on that range.

Lastly, we remark that “perfect negative correlation” for
Definition

5
corresponds to
\gamma=1
. As noted in
[
FHTZ20
]
and can be confirmed by plugging
\gamma=1
into
Theorem

3
, that corresponds to a competitive ratio of
\Gamma=\frac{5}{9}
. This is far from the best known upper bound of
\Gamma=1-\frac{1}{e}
. On the other hand, perfect negative correlation for a continuous OCS corresponds to
f(x)=(1-x)_{+}
which gives a competitive ratio of
\Gamma=1-\frac{1}{e}
. While perfect negative correlation is impossible, the fact that it corresponds to a competitive ratio equal to the best known upper bound seems to suggest that continuous OCSes are a step in the right direction towards finding the optimal competitive ratio.

2.3
Negative results

We prove the following for Fahrbach et al.’s formulation of an OCS.

Lemma 2.2
.

No
\gamma
-OCS exists for
\gamma>\frac{1}{3}
.

Both
Theorem

3
and the factor-revealing LP of
[
FHTZ20
]
agree that the competitive ratio for
\gamma=\frac{1}{3}
is less than
0.5239
. This is an upper bound on the competitive ratios that can be achieved simply by constructing better
\gamma
-OCSes. Our competitive ratio of
0.5368
is larger, confirming that multiway OCSes are strictly more powerful.

3
Constructing a multiway OCS: Meta algorithm overview

We give a meta algorithm for constructing
m
-discrete OCSes given a “win distribution,”
\mathcal{W}
, over
[0,1]^{\mathds{N}}
. A sample from
\mathcal{W}
is a sequence of probabilities,
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots)
, with the quality of the resulting OCS (i.e. the function
F
in
Definition

4
) depending on how negatively correlated the elements in that sequence are. During initialization, the meta algorithm draws an independent “win sequence”,
(\bm{x}_{1}(i),\bm{x}_{2}(i),\ldots)
for each
i\in L
. Our OCS also stores an index within the win distribution,
k(i)
for each
i\in L
that is initialized to
k_{i}=1
.

At each time step, the OCS receives
A_{j}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}
. For each
i\in A_{j}
, it determines the “desired win probability” of
i
in this time step. In the case where
i
appears exactly once in
A_{j}
, this desired win probability is exactly equal to
\bm{x}_{k(i)}(i)
. When
i
appears
r\geq 2
times in
A_{j}
, the desired win probability is the probability
i
would win in at least one of the next
r
rounds if it appeared exactly once in each. Then, a tournament algorithm takes in these desired win probabilities and selects a single winner for the round consistent with the desired win probabilities and
k(i)
is incremented
r
times. We include pseudocode for our meta algorithm in
Figure

2
, though defer description of the win distribution and tournament subroutine for later.

, independently draw a

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{j}(i)\coloneqq 1-\prod_{\ell=0}^{r_{j}(i)-1}\left(1-\bm{x}_{k_{j}(i)+\ell}\right)

(2)

(b)

Use a (possibly randomized) tournament subroutine,
\mathcal{T}
, to select a single
i\in A_{j}
as winner. This is done so that the probability each
i\in A_{j}
wins is at least
\bm{w}_{j}(i)
.

The fact that the desired win probabilities of different vertices are
independent
makes analysis tractable. To achieve a competitive ratio better than
0.5
, we need the winners of our OCS to be negatively correlated. We achieve this by making the random variables in the sequence returned by the win distribution negatively correlated. This way, if
i
has a small chance of winning in one round, it has a larger chance of winning in other rounds. In particular, we need two types of negative correlation for
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots)\sim\mathcal{W}
.

1.

A
qualitative
and
long-distanced
negative correlation. We need to ensure there doesn’t exist any positive correlation between
\bm{x}_{k_{1}}
and
\bm{x}_{k_{2}}
even for
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
far apart. We prove that our win probabilities satisfy a generalization of negatively correlated random variables called
negative association
.

Definition 6

(Negative association
[
JDP83
]
)
.

Random variables
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
are said to be
negatively associated
if, for every pair of disjoint subsets
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq[n]
and nondecreasing functions
f_{1},f_{2}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Cov}}[f_{1}(\bm{x}_{A_{1}}),f_{2}(\bm{x}_{A_{2}})]\leq 0

where
\bm{x}_{A}
is the vector
(\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\bm{x}_{i_{2}},\ldots,\bm{x}_{i_{|A|}})
when
A=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{|A|}\}
.

Proving this ends up being essential to showing our OCS has
any
guarantees, even those matching the trivial fully independent OCS.

2.

A
quantitative
and
short-distanced
negative correlation. We want the values of
\bm{x}_{j}
, and
\bm{x}_{j^{\prime}}
to be strongly negatively correlated whenever
j
is “close to”
j^{\prime}
. The magnitude of this negative correlation directly ties into the probability that
i
is selected in consecutive time steps and therefore the quality of our OCS.

We prove a meta theorem on the quality of the algorithm in
Figure

2
as a function of the win distribution.

Theorem 4

(Quality of our meta algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a win-distribution with the following three properties.

1.

Negatively associated:
For any
n\geq 1
and
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{W}
, the variables
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
are negative associated.

2.

Shift-invariant:
For any
n\geq 1
and
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{W}
, the distribution of
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots)
is identical to that of
(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{x}_{n+1},\ldots)
.

3.

Sufficiently small:
For each
r\in[m-1]
and
t\in[0,1]
, defining
\bm{w}^{(r)}
to be the random variable representing the distribution of the desired win probability for a vertex of multiplicity
r
,

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{(r)}\sim\left(1-\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(1-\bm{x}_{\ell}\right)\right)\quad\quad\text{where}\quad\quad\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{W},

the following holds

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\left(\bm{w}^{(r)}-t\right)_{+}\right]\leq 1-t-\frac{m-r}{m}\cdot\left(1-t^{\frac{m}{m-r}}\right)

(3)

where
(z)_{+}
is short hand for
\max(z,0)
.

Then, there exists some tournament subroutine
\mathcal{T}
for which
\mathrm{OCS}_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{T}}
is a
(f,m)
-discrete OCS for

\displaystyle F(n)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}(1-\bm{x}_{\ell})\right].

(4)

Each of the three criteria of
Theorem

4
are desirable for intuitive reasons. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
be disjoints set of time steps. Negative association ensures that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[i\text{ never selected in }\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}S_{\ell}]\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\operatorname{{Pr}}[i\text{ never selected in }S_{\ell}].

Shift-invariance ensures that the probability that
i
is not selected in a set of
n
consecutive appearances is independent of when those appearances occur. Finally,
\bm{w}^{(r)}
being sufficiently small ensures that it’s possible for a tournament subroutine to guarantee that the probability
i
wins a round with desired win probability
w
is at least
w
.

Our proof of
Theorem

4
leverages a number of tools from probability theory. We use many closure properties of negatively associated variables given in
[
JDP83
]
and also prove some additional ones. Later, we formalize what it means for one variable to be “larger and more spread out” than another using the
increasing convex order
. We do so to take advantage of a powerful result by Müller and Rüschendorf
[
MR01
]
which generalizes a classic result of Strassen
[
Str65
]
. That result guarantees the existence of a particular coupling
(\bm{a},\bm{b})
whenever
\bm{b}
is “larger and more spread out” than
\bm{a}
.

5
Comparison to the concurrent work

5.1
The work of Gao et al.

Concurrently and independently, Gao et al. also explored the use of multiway OCSes in the construction of improved online matching algorithms
[
GHH+21
]
. The bulk of their work focuses on
vertex-weighted
online bipartite matching. This is a special case of
edge-weighted
online bipartite matching where we assume that all edges to each offline vertex have the same weight. While an algorithm achieving the optimal competitive ratio competitive ratio of
(1-\frac{1}{e})\approx 0.632
is known in this setting
[
AGKM11
]
, Gao et al. are able to show that OCSes can also be used to tackle this problem and achieve a competitive ratio of
0.593
.

They do so by developing a definition of multiway OCSes similar to our definition of continuous OCSes. In the vertex-weighted setting we only care about whether an element
i
is selected at least once at some time step and do not care about which time step it is selected in. Their definition for a “multi-way semi-OCS” is equivalent to
Definition

2
where we take
k=1
and
S_{1}
the set containing all time steps. For vertex-weighted online bipartite matching, they derive the same formula for the competitive ratio as we do in
Theorem

1
using “multi-way semi-OCSes.” A similar result could be derived using our algorithm by never executing
Equation

10
(since we only care about whether a vertex is matched, not when it is matched). The resulting algorithm and analysis simplifies.

We stress that extending multiway OCSes to the historically challenging edge-weighted setting requires overcoming additional hurdles.

1.

The informal definition of
Definition

2
isn’t strong enough to derive
Theorem

1
. We needed to identify an additional constraint on the OCS (see “partially ignore small gaps” of
Definition

12
) that is both feasible to construct and sufficient to developing an algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching.

2.

Constructing a multiway OCS is more challenging than a “multi-way semi-OCS” and we need to develop a new framework to do so. For example, in the construction of Gao et al., the probability a vertex is selected in a late time step can be made arbitrarily small (since they only need each vertex to be matched once), and so would not meet our definition.

Gao et al. also contribute to the
edge-weighted
setting. In this setting, they give
0.519
-competitive algorithm by constructing an improved
2
-way OCS. Their result improves upon the competitive ratio of Fahrbach et al.
[
FHTZ20
]
though isn’t as large as ours. Finally, they prove that no
\gamma
-OCS exists for
\gamma>\frac{1}{4}
, which is stronger than the
\frac{1}{3}
bound we show in
Lemma

2.2
. For our purposes, the conclusion is the same: Multiway OCSes are strictly more powerful than two-way OCSes for edge-weighted online bipartite matching.

5.2
The work of Shin and An

Also concurrently and independently, Shin and An created a
3
-discrete OCS an applied it to edge-weighted online bipartite matching
[
SA21
]
. They construct their
3
-discrete OCS by running two parallel instance of a
\gamma
-OCS (which is
2
-discrete). Upon receiving a set of three elements, two are chosen uniformly at random to be passed to one of the
\gamma
-OCSes. The winner and the third element are passed into the other
\gamma
-OCS to decide the overall winner of the round.

Then, they show how to use this
3
-discrete OCS for edge-weighted online bipartite matching. To do so, they extend the analysis and factor-revealing LP of Farhbach et al. and derive a competitive ratio of
0.5093
. We note that plugging in the parameters of their
3
-discrete OCS into
Theorem

2
gives the same competitive ratio up to the four digits of accuracy provided in their paper, though with a completely different derivation than theirs.

7
Continuous OCS: Formal definition and theorems

In this section we formalize our definition of the parameters of a continuous OCS and the relation between those parameters and the competitive ratio. Recall in
Definition

2
, we guarantee the probability
i
isn’t picked in two consecutive sequences of time steps,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, is at most

\displaystyle f(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{1}))\cdot f(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{2})).

(5)

where
\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S)=\sum_{j\in S}(p^{(j)})_{i}
. On the other hand, if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are actually consecutive, then the probability
i
isn’t picked is at most

\displaystyle f\big(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{1})+\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{2})\big).

(6)

Good OCSes will have
f(x+y)<f(x)\cdot f(y)
and so
Equation

6
gives a stronger guarantee than
Equation

5
. Now, suppose that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are “almost” consecutive. For
S_{1}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{2}\}
and
S_{2}=\{j_{3},\ldots,j_{4}\}
, perhaps its the case that
j_{3}=j_{2}+2
. Then, intuitively, we should expect a guarantee close to that of
Equation

6
to hold.

Let
G
be the time steps in the “gap”, meaning
G\coloneqq\{j_{2}+1,\ldots,j_{3}-1\}
. For some rate parameter
r
and
w\coloneqq\prod_{j\in G}(1-\cdot p^{(j)}_{i})^{r}
, we will guarantee that the probability
i
is not selected in
S_{1}
or
S_{2}
is at most

\displaystyle w\cdot f\big(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{1})+\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{2})\big)+(1-w)\cdot f(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{1}))\cdot f(\mathrm{weight}_{i}(S_{2}))

Essentially, when determining the probability
i
is picked, we pretend the gap
G
doesn’t exist with probability
w
. This guarantee smoothly interpolates between
Equations

6
and
5
as
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
grow further apart. We generalize this idea to more than two sets in the following definition.

Definition 12

(Parameters of a continuous OCS, formal version of
Definition

2
)
.

For any rate
r\geq 0
and function
f:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
, an
(f,r)
-
continuous OCS
is a continuous OCS with the following guarantee. For any element
i\in L
and set of time steps
S
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}\neq i\text{ for every }j\in S]\leq\mathcal{F}(S,p)

where
p
is the vector whose
j^{\mathrm{th}}
entry is
(p^{(j)})_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}
satisfies

1.

Consecutive:
If
S=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{2}\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S,p)\leq f\left(\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}\right)

2.

Partially ignore small gaps:
For any disjoint (not necessarily consecutive)
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathds{N}
satisfying

\displaystyle j_{1}<j_{2}\text{ for each $j_{1}\in S_{1},j_{2}\in S_{2}$}

Let
j^{\star}
be some time step between
j_{1},j_{2}
for every
j_{1}\in S_{1},j_{2}\in S_{2}
. For

\displaystyle(\mathrm{del}_{j^{\star}}(p))_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}p_{j}&j<j^{\star}\\
p_{j+1}&j\geq j^{\star}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathrm{del}_{j^{\star}}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{j\text{ for each }j\in S_{1}\}\cup\{j-1\text{ for each }j\in S_{2}\}

Then, for
w=(1-p_{j^{\star}})^{r}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S_{1}\cup S_{2},p)\leq w\cdot\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{del}_{j^{\star}}(S_{1}\cup S_{2}),\mathrm{del}_{j^{\star}}(p))+(1-w)\cdot\mathcal{F}(S_{1},p)\cdot\mathcal{F}(S_{2},p)

(7)

Smaller
r
corresponds to ignoring larger gaps, making the OCS anticorrelated over larger distances. Instead of setting
w=(1-p_{j^{\star}})^{r}
, we could have instead set
w=e^{-p_{j^{\star}}\cdot r}
,
w=(1-r\cdot p_{j^{\star}})_{+}
, or other functions where
-r\leq\frac{dw}{dp_{j^{\star}}}\leq 0
, and our proof of
Theorem

6
would still work. The choice we make here plays well with the proof of
Lemma

8.1
.

We next give a formal version of
Theorem

1
, with the main difference being that

can’t be too large.

Theorem 6

(Formal version of
Theorem

1
)
.

For any thrice differentiable convex
f:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
where
f
and
f^{\prime}
are log-concave, meaning

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)\cdot f(x)\leq(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}\quad\quad\text{for all $x\geq 0$}

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\cdot f^{\prime}(x)\leq(f^{\prime\prime}(x))^{2}\quad\quad\text{for all $x\geq 0$}

let,

\displaystyle\Gamma\coloneqq 1-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(t)dt.

(8)

For any
r
satisfying

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{-f^{\prime}(0)-\Gamma}{\Gamma+(1-\Gamma)\cdot f^{\prime}(0)}

(9)

If there is a
(f,r)
-continuous OCS, then there is a
\Gamma
-competitive algorithm for edge-weighted online bipartite matching.

In most cases,
f^{\prime}(0)=-1
as OCSes of interest have
f
between
f_{\mathrm{trivial}}=e^{-x}
and
f_{\mathrm{perfect}}=(1-x)_{+}
. When
f^{\prime}(0)=1
,

9
imposes an upper bound on
r
of
\frac{1-\Gamma}{2\Gamma-1}
, which is a decreasing function of
\Gamma
on the interval
(0.5,1)
. Hence, the larger a competitive ratio we want, the smaller
r
has to be, meaning the OCS must be anticorrelated over larger distances.

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving
Theorem

6
. The algorithm uses an online primal-dual approach.

7.1
Primal dual formulation

We begin with the standard primal linear program for (offline) edge-weighted bipartite matching. Let
w_{ij}
be the weight of the edge between
i\in L
to
j\in R
where
w_{ij}=0
indicate no such edge exists and
x_{ij}
be the probability that
(i,j)
is the heaviest edge matched to vertex
i
.

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in L}\sum_{j\in R}w_{ij}x_{ij}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in R}x_{ij}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall i\in L

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in L}x_{ij}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall j\in R

\displaystyle x_{ij}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i\in L,j\in R

This gives rise to a dual linear program.

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in L}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\in R}\beta_{j}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}\geq w_{ij}

\displaystyle\forall i\in L,j\in R

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall i\in L

\displaystyle\beta_{j}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall j\in R

We use
P
to denote the primal objective and
D
to denote the dual objective. Our online algorithm will simultaneously maintain a matching for the graph and a dual assignment that will meet the following two criteria.

Fact 7.1

(Lemma 2 from
[
FHTZ20
]
)
.

Suppose an algorithm maintains primal and dual assignments such that, for some
0\leq\Gamma\leq 1
, the following conditions hold at all time steps.

1.

Approximate dual feasibility:
For any
i\in L
and
j\in R

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}\geq\Gamma\cdot w_{ij}

2.

Reverse weak duality:
The objectives of the primal and dual assignments satisfy
P\geq D
.

Then the algorithm is
\Gamma
-competitive.

Inspired by
[
DHK+16
,
FHTZ20
]
, we do not directly maintain the primal variables
x_{ij}
. Instead, for each
i\in L
and weight-level
w\geq 0
, we define

\displaystyle y_{i}(w)\coloneqq\operatorname{{Pr}}[i\text{ not matched to a vertex of weight at least }w]

In contrast,
[
DHK+16
,
FHTZ20
]
defines their
\overline{y_{i}(w)}
to be
\overline{y_{i}(w)}=1-y_{i}(w)
. We find our “reversed” definition simplifies later analysis. The primal objective is then

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i\in L}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-y_{i}(w))dw

Similarly, we introduce and maintain new variables
\alpha_{i}(w)
for each
i\in L
and weight-level
w\geq 0
and then set the value of
\alpha_{i}
to

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}(w))dw.

Remark 1
.

Note that
y_{i}(w)
and
\alpha_{i}(w)
are both
step functions with at most one change per unique value in the set
{
w_{ij}\mid j\in R\}
. Hence, they can be stored efficiently.

7.2
High level algorithmic overview

Fix some
(f,r)
-continuous OCS. At each time step
j\in[T]
, the values
w_{ij}
are revealed for every
i\in L
. As a function of these weights and the state of the primal and dual variables, the algorithm will determine a probability vector
p^{(j)}
to pass into the OCS. Then, it updates the primal variables to account for the probability each vertex is matched to
j
and the dual variables to maintain the conditions dual feasibility and reverse weak duality. The OCS selects a winner in
L
based on the
p^{(j)}
it is passed in, and that winner is matched to vertex
j
. We give pseudocode for the entire algorithm at the end of this section in
Figure

3
.

We wish to emphasize that the primal-dual algorithm does not “know” which offline vertex
j
is matched too. Instead, it only maintains the probability that each
i
is matched to a vertex at each weight-level. This separates the algorithm into a deterministic component, the primal-dual portion, and a randomized component, the OCS.

The remainder of this section is divided as follows. In
Section

7.3
and
Section

7.4
, we define how the primal and dual variables respectively are updated given
p^{(j)}
. In particular,
\beta_{j}
is set to exactly the minimum value so that approximate dual feasiblity holds. In
Section

7.5
we will discuss how
p^{(j)}
is determined. In
Section

7.6
, we will prove that reverse weak duality holds, completing the proof that our algorithm is
\Gamma
-competitive.

Throughout this section,
f:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
and
r\in\mathds{R}^{+}
refer to the parameters of our OCS.

7.3
Primal updates

For each
i\in L
and weight-level
w\geq 0
, we maintain two random variables initialized as follows.

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}(w)\overset{\mathrm{init}}{\longleftarrow}0\quad\text{with probability $1$}

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{i}(w)\overset{\mathrm{init}}{\longleftarrow}1\quad\text{with probability $1$}

Our algorithm maintains the
distribution
over
(\bm{x}_{i}(w),\bm{c}_{i}(w))
rather than just a single element of it. See
Remark

2
for the computational complexity of maintaining this distribution.

At time step
j
, our algorithm will pass a vector
p^{(j)}
into the OCS. After doing so, it updates, for each
w\leq w_{ij}
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}(w)\leftarrow\bm{x}_{i}(w)+(p^{(j)})_{i}

For each
w>w_{ij}
, with probability
1-(1-(p^{(j)})_{i})^{r}
we set

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{c}_{i}(w)\leftarrow\bm{c}_{i}(w)\cdot f(\bm{x}_{i}(w))\\
\bm{x}_{i}(w)\leftarrow 0\end{split}

(10)

Otherwise, we leave
\bm{c}_{i}(w)
and
\bm{x}_{i}(w)
unchanged. This is the step that makes
\bm{c}_{i}(w)
and
\bm{x}_{i}(w)
random quantities rather than fixed.

At all steps, we maintain that

\displaystyle y_{i}(w)=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\bm{c}_{i}(w)\cdot f(\bm{x}_{i}(w))]

Note that these updates correspond to
Definition

12
. For
S=\{j\in R|w_{ij}\geq w\}
, that definition guarantees the probability that
i
is not matched to a vertex in
S
is at most
y_{i}(w)
.

Remark 2
.

The full support of
(\bm{x}_{i}(w),\bm{c}_{i}(w))
can be exponentially large, and so inefficient to maintain. However, our updates never need full access to the distribution over
(\bm{x}_{i}(w),\bm{c}_{i}(w)
. It is sufficient to maintain a distribution over the possible values of
\bm{x}_{i}(w)
, and for each possible value for
\bm{x}_{i}(w)=x
, the quantity
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{c}_{i}(w)\mid\bm{x}_{i}(w)=x]
. Since there are only
|R|
many possible values for
\bm{x}_{i}(w)
, that information can be stored and operated on efficiently.

7.4
Dual updates

Our dual updates depend on some function
a:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,\Gamma]
with the properties in the below Lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
f:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
be thrice differentiable, convex, log concave, have a log concave derivative, and satisfy
f(0)=1
. Define
a:\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,\Gamma]
as

\displaystyle a(x)\coloneqq f(x)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(t+x)dt.

(11)

For
r
satisfying
Equation

9
and
\Gamma
defined in
Equation

8
,
a
satisfies the below properties.

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)+a(x)

\displaystyle\forall x\geq 0

(12)

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(x)\leq 0

\displaystyle\forall x\geq 0

(13)

\displaystyle a(0)=\Gamma

(14)

\displaystyle f(x)\cdot a(0)=f(x)\cdot\Gamma\leq a(x)

\displaystyle\forall x\geq 0

(15)

\displaystyle r\cdot(a(x)-f(x)\cdot\Gamma)\leq\Gamma-a(x)

\displaystyle\forall x\geq 0

(16)

We prove

7.2
in
Appendix

B
. For the remainder of this section, let

be as defined in
Equation

.

For any
i\in L
and weight-level
w\geq 0
, we maintain that

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(w)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\Big[\bm{c}_{i}(w)\cdot a\big(\bm{x}_{i}(w)\big)\Big],

where
\bm{c}_{i}(w),

\bm{x}_{i}(w)
are the same as in the primal updates. After
\alpha_{i}
is updated at time step

, we set
\beta_{j}
to the minimum value so that approximate dual feasibility holds. Specifically,

\displaystyle\beta_{j}=\max\Big(0,\max_{i\in L}\,\Gamma\cdot w_{ij}-\alpha_{i}\Big)

(17)

For the above setting of
\beta_{j}
, dual feasibility will hold for
w_{ij}
at time step
j
. It will remain satisfied as long
\alpha_{i}
never decreases in future time steps. At any time step
j^{\prime}
, if
(p^{(j^{\prime})})_{i}=0
, then
\alpha_{i}
is not changed at that time step. The following proposition guarantees that if
(p^{(j^{\prime})})_{i}>0
, then
\alpha_{i}
can only increase, which implies dual feasibility remains satisfied.

Proposition 7.3
.

Fix some time step
j
. For each
i\in L
,
w\geq 0
and
p\in[0,1]
, let
\alpha_{i}[p]
and
\alpha_{i}(w)[p]
denote the value that would be assigned to
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}(w)
respectively at the end of this time step if
(p^{(j)})_{i}=p
. Then,

1.

\alpha_{i}[p]
is a continuous and increasing function of
p
.

2.

7.6
Reverse weak duality

Since we already have that approximate dual feasibility holds, we only need to prove that reverse weak duality holds to complete the proof that this algorithm is
\Gamma
-competitive. At the start of our algorithm we have that
P=D=0
. Let
\Delta_{j}P
and
\Delta_{j}D
be the amount the primal and dual respectively increase after the update corresponding to
j\in R
. We will prove that
\Delta_{j}P\geq\Delta_{j}D
which guarantees reverse weak duality holds.

Lemma 7.5
.

For any
j\in R
, if
p^{(j)}
is set as in
Lemma

7.4
, then
\Delta_{j}P\geq\Delta_{j}D
.

Proof.

For each primal and dual variable, we use a superscript of
(j)
to refer to its value after the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
update. Similarly, a superscript of
(j-1)
refers to its value before the update. For any variable
\mathrm{var}
, we use the shorthand
\Delta_{j}\mathrm{var}
to refer
\mathrm{var}^{(j)}-\mathrm{var}^{(j-1)}
.

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}P-\Delta_{j}D

\displaystyle=\left(-\sum_{i\in L}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)dw\right)-\left(-\sum_{i\in L}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)dw+\beta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}\left(-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)-\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w))dw\right)-\left(\sum_{i\in L}(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot(\Gamma\cdot w_{ij}-\alpha_{i}^{(j)})\right)

Recall that
\alpha_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}(w))dw
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Gamma\cdot w_{ij}-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{w_{ij}}\Gamma\cdot dw\right)-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w))dw\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{w_{ij}}\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)dw\right)-\left(\int_{w_{ij}}^{\infty}(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w))dw\right)

Combining the above equations, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}P-\Delta_{j}D=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in L}\left(\int_{0}^{w_{ij}}\left(-\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)+\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)\right)dw\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in L}\left(\int_{w_{ij}}^{\infty}\left(-\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)+\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)+(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\big(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)\big)\right)dw\right)

We will show that for any choice of
w\geq 0
and
i\in L
, the corresponding term in the above equation is positive. This implies that
\Delta_{j}P-\Delta_{j}D
is also positive.

Case 1:
w\leq w_{ij}
. We wish to show that
-\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)+\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)\geq 0
. We expand each term separately.

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)

\displaystyle=y_{i}^{(j)}(w)-y_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot(f(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-f(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\int_{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}}^{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)}\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot f^{\prime}(x)dx\right]

Similarly,

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)-\alpha_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot(a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\int_{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}}^{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)}\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot a^{\prime}(x)dx\right]

Finally,

\displaystyle(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)

\displaystyle=(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w))\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}}^{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w))\right]dx

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\int_{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)-(p^{(j)})_{i}}^{\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w)}\bm{c}_{i}^{(j)}(w)\cdot a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j)}(w))dx\right]

Comparing the above three equations, it is enough to show that for any
x<x_{i}^{(j)}(w)
, that
-f^{\prime}(x)+a^{\prime}(x)-a(x_{i}^{(j)})\geq 0
.

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}(x)+a^{\prime}(x)-a(x_{i}^{(j)})

\displaystyle\geq-f^{\prime}(x)+a^{\prime}(x)-a(x)

(
a
is decreasing)

\displaystyle=0

(
Equation

12
)

This completes the first case.

Case 2:
w>w_{ij}
. We wish to show that
-\Delta_{j}y_{i}(w)+\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)+(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\big(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)\big)\geq 0
. For
w>w_{ij}
,
y_{i}(w)
does not change. Hence, we only need to expand the other two terms. Recall that, for
w\geq w_{ij}
, we set
\bm{c}_{i}(w)\leftarrow\bm{c}_{i}(w)\cdot f(\bm{x}_{i}(w))
and
\bm{x}_{i}(w)\leftarrow 0
with probability
1-(1-(p^{(j)})_{i})^{r}
. Otherwise, we leave the parameters unchanged. Expanding,

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[(1-(1-(p^{(j)})_{i})^{r})\cdot\bm{c}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)\cdot\big(f(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w))\cdot a(0)-a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w))\big)\right].

Recall from

that

for all

. Therefore, we may replace the

factor with an upper bound on it, in this case

, and bound,

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\alpha_{i}(w)\geq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[r\cdot(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\bm{c}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)\cdot\big(f(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w))\cdot a(0)-a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w))\big)\right].

Using the fact that

, which follows from

7.3
, we expand the other term of our desired inequality.

\displaystyle(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\big(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j)}(w)\big)

\displaystyle\geq(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\big(\Gamma-\alpha_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)\big)

\displaystyle=(p^{(j)})_{i}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\Gamma-\bm{c}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w)\cdot a(\bm{x}_{i}^{(j-1)}(w))\right]

The desired inequality holds as long as, for all

,

\displaystyle r\cdot(f(x)\cdot a(0)-a(x))+(\Gamma-a(x))\geq 0.

which is equivalent to

. This completes the proof of the second case and reverse weak duality.
∎

Lemma 8.1

If there is an

Before analyzing this OCS, we’ll need some well-known facts about the convex order (recall

Fact 8.2

(Theorem 3.A.4 of
[
SS07
]
)
.

For any univariate random variables
\bm{a},\bm{b}
, if there exists a coupling
(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}})

\bm{a}\overset{d}{=}\hat{\bm{a}}
and

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{b}}\mid\hat{\bm{a}}]=\hat{\bm{a}}

\bm{a}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bm{b}
.

Fact 8.3

(Theorem 3.A.12(d) of
[
SS07
]
)
.

The convex order is closed under convolution: Let
\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{k}
and
\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{k}
each be independent univariate random variables. If
\bm{a}_{\ell}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bm{b}_{\ell}
for each

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\bm{a}_{\ell}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\bm{b}_{\ell}.

The following proposition is a simple consequence of the above two facts.

Proposition 8.4
.

For any
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in[0,1]
, let
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{i})
and
\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i})
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bm{y}.

Proof.

By
8.3
, it is enough to prove that
\bm{x}_{i}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\bm{y}_{i}
where
\bm{y}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda=p_{i})
. First, we note that
\bm{y}_{i}
is more likely to be
0
than
\bm{x}_{i}
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{i}=0]

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}^{k}\cdot e^{-{p_{i}}}}{0!}=e^{-(p_{i})}\geq(1-p_{i})=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{x}_{i}=0].

Consider the following coupling: First draw
\hat{\bm{y}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(p_{i})
. If
\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}=0
, then with probability
(\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{x}_{i}=0])/(\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{i}=0])

\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0
as well. Otherwise, set
\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=1
.

We verify that this coupling meets the criteria of
8.2
which implies

. By design, we have that
\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\overset{d}{=}\bm{y}_{i}
and
\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}\overset{d}{=}\bm{x}_{i}

\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0
, then
\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}=0
as well, so

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\mid\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0]=0

Then, using the law of total expectation, we have that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\mid\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0]+\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\mid\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=1]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=1]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}]

Substituting
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\mid\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=0]=0

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{y}_{i}}\mid\hat{\bm{x}_{i}}=1]=1.

Therefore, by

.

Lemma 8.5
.

Choose any
F:\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathds{R}
,

be

\displaystyle f(x)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{k}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(mx)}[F(\bm{k})].

Then, for any

\displaystyle f^{(\ell)}(x)=m^{\ell}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{k}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(mx)}[\Delta^{(\ell)}F(\bm{k})].

Proof.

It is enough to prove the case where
\ell=1
, as larger
\ell
follow by induction. The remainder of this proof is algebraic manipulations. We expand

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-mx}\cdot(mx)^{k}}{k!}\cdot F(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-mx}x^{k}\right)\cdot\frac{m^{k}\cdot F(k)}{k!}

We’ll use the linearity of derivatives. To do so, we first compute the derivative of the above coefficients.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}e^{-mx}x^{k}=\begin{cases}-me^{-mx}\cdot x^{k}&k=0\\
-me^{-mx}\cdot x^{k}+ke^{-mx}x^{k-1}&k\geq 1\end{cases}

Therefore,

will hold. On the other hand, consistency is more challenging. The following fundamental question underlies this subsection.

For which distributions of
\bm{w}^{(r)}
is it possible to design stochastically increasing strength functions such that the probability

wins round

w

Consider the case where

We will show that these two notions – how large and spread out

See

The following Lemma formalizes our intuition that as long as the desired win probabilities are sufficiently small and not spread out, (

Lemma 9.13

(Increasing convex order
\to
(
\diamondsuit

For any random variables
\bm{w}^{(1)}\ldots,\bm{w}^{(m-1)}

\bm{w}^{\prime(1)}\ldots,\bm{w}^{\prime(m-1)}

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{(r)}\preceq_{\mathrm{icx}}\bm{w}^{\prime(r)}\quad\quad\text{for each $r=1,\ldots,m-1$},

Theorem 8

(
[

For univariate random variables

1.

\bm{a}
is smaller than
\bm{b}

2.

There is a coupling of

\bm{a}
and

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\hat{\bm{b}}\mid\hat{\bm{a}}]\geq\hat{\bm{a}}.

Proof of
Lemma

9.13

For each

be the coupling guaranteed to exist by

\diamondsuit

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}(w)\coloneqq\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{w}}^{\prime})\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\sim(\hat{\bm{w}}^{\prime(r)}\mid\hat{\bm{w}}^{(r)}=w).

is stochastically increasing for all

Remark 4

(Computational efficiency of

)
.

Computing

requires knowing the coupling

In order to use

Fact 9.14

(
[
SS07
]
)
.

For univariate random variables
\bm{a},\bm{b}
, the following are equivalent.

1.

\bm{a}
is smaller than
\bm{b}
in the increasing convex order.

2.

For every

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[(\bm{a}-t)_{+}]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[(\bm{b}-t)_{+}].

We complete the proof of
Lemma

9.12
by giving a particular choice for
(\bm{w}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{w}^{(m-1)})
on which (
\diamondsuit
) holds.

Lemma 9.15
.

For each
r\in[m-1]
, let
\bm{w}^{(r)}
be the random variable supported on
[0,1]
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[(\bm{w}^{(r)}-t)_{+}]=1-t-\frac{m-r}{m}\cdot\left(1-t^{\frac{m}{m-r}}\right)\quad\quad\forall t\in[0,1]

(30)

Then, (
\diamondsuit
) holds for
(\bm{w}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{w}^{(m-1)})

Proof.

Straightforward computation verifies that

30
is equivalent to each
\bm{w}^{(r)}
having the CDF.

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{w}^{(r)}\leq t]=t^{\frac{r}{m-r}}\quad\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

Consider the strength functions, for each

,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}(w)\coloneqq w^{\frac{1}{m-r}}.

For each

, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}(\bm{w}^{(r)})\leq t]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{w}^{(r)}\leq t^{m-r}]=t^{r}.

Therefore,
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}(\bm{w}^{(r)})
is distributed according to the max of
r
independent variables drawn from
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
. A vertex

is selected in a round if its strength is larger than the strengths of each of the other vertices. If
i
has multiplicity
r(i)

independent uniforms. Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[i\text{ selected with strength }w]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\text{max of ($m-r(i)$) independent uniforms}<\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r(i)}(w)]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r(i)}(w)^{m-r(i)}

\displaystyle=w,

Furthermore,
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{r}

Constructing a win distribution

In this section we will construct a distribution

(

Lemma 10.4
.

For any
n\geq 1
and indices

, let
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n}
be drawn as

\displaystyle(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n})\sim(\mathcal{D}^{(n)}\mid\bm{y}_{i}=-1\text{ for each $i\in S$})

Then, for any increasing functions

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Cov}}}\/}_{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n}}\big[f_{1}(\bm{y}_{n}),f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\big]\leq 0

The same is true when using
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}_{0}
rather than
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}

We only prove
Lemma

10.4
for
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}
as the proof for
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}_{0}

(\mathcal{D}^{(n)}\mid\bm{y}_{i}=-1\text{ for each $i\in S$})

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
f_{1}(-1)=0
; otherwise, add an appropriate constant to
f_{1}
so that it’s true. Since
f_{1}
is an increasing function of
\bm{y}_{n}
and
\bm{y}_{n}\geq-1

\displaystyle f_{1}(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}g(j)

for an appropriate nonnegative function

. We then expand the first term of the desired covariance.

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n}}\big[f_{1}(\bm{y}_{n})\cdot f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1}\cdot\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}f_{1}(k)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{n}=k]\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\mid\bm{y}_{n}=k\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k}g(j)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{n}=k]\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\mid\bm{y}_{n}=k\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}g(j)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{n}=k]\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\mid\bm{y}_{n}=k\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}g(j)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{n}\geq j]\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\mid\bm{y}_{n}\geq j\big]

We claim that
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\mid\bm{y}_{n}\geq j\big]
is a decreasing function of

. First we note that conditioning on

(31)

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\big[

\displaystyle f_{1}(\bm{y}_{n})\cdot f_{2}(\bm{y}_{1}\cdot\ldots,\bm{y}_{n-1})\big]

Where the inequality follows from

A.2
Proof that it is impossible to negatively correlate all time steps

We prove that there does not exists a continuous OCS that negatively correlated every single time step. This is why we choose to have our OCS only negatively correlate time steps that are close.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
be a function on which there exists some rational
x\in\mathds{Q}
where
f(x)<e^{-x}
. Then, there does not exist a continuous OCS with the following guarantee: For any element
i\in L
and set of time steps
S
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{i}_{j}\neq i\text{ for every }j\in S]\leq f\left(\sum_{j\in S}(p^{(j)})_{i}\right).

Note that
f(x)=e^{-x}
corresponds to an OCS which picks an winner independently at each time step, and negative correlation corresponds to
f(x)<e^{-x}
.

Proof.

Let
x=\frac{a}{b}
for
a,b\in Z
. For
k,n\in Z_{\geq 1}
, consider the input instance where
|L|=b\cdot k
and each of
n
time steps have
p^{(j)}
uniform over
L
, meaning
(p^{(j)})_{i}=\frac{1}{b\cdot k}
for all
i\in L,j\in[n]
. We will show that in the limit of
\frac{n}{k}\to\infty
and
k\to\infty
, the desired guarantee is impossible.

Assume, for the sake of guarantee, the desired result is possible. We will show that this implies that the expected number of total winners is more than
n
, a contradiction as each time step has only a single winner. Fix an arbitrary
i\in V
, and let
\bm{z}_{j}
be the event that
i
is
not
the winner in the
j^{\text{th}}
round. Then, by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j}\right]\leq\sqrt[ak]{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j}\right)^{ak}\right]}.

Expanding
(\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j})^{ak}
, there are
\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}
terms which are the product of
ak
distinct
\bm{z}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{z}_{j_{ak}}
, and
n^{ak}-\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}
which are the product of non-distinct events. Whenever
\bm{z}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\bm{z}_{j_{k}}
are non-distinct, we use the lose upper bound that that
E[\bm{z}_{j_{1}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\bm{z}_{j_{ak}}]\leq 1
. Whenever they are distinct, by the assumption on the quality of the OCS, it must be the case that
E[\bm{z}_{j_{1}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\bm{z}_{j_{ak}}]\leq f(x)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt[ak]{\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}\cdot f(x)+n^{ak}-\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt[ak]{\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}}\cdot\sqrt[ak]{f(x)}+\sqrt[ak]{n^{ak}-\frac{n!}{(n-ak)!}}

In the limit of
\frac{n}{k}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot(1-o(1)\cdot\sqrt[ak]{f(x)}

\displaystyle<n\cdot(1-o(1))\cdot\sqrt[ak]{e^{-\frac{ak}{bk}}}

\displaystyle=n\cdot(1-o(1))\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{bk}}

Note that the expected number of times that
i
is the winner is
n-\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\bm{z}_{j}\right]
. In the limit of
k\to\infty
, this value is strictly more than
n\cdot\frac{1}{bk}
. By symmetry, this is true of all
bk
different vertices in
L
, implying, the total number of winner is more than
n
, a contradiction.
∎
