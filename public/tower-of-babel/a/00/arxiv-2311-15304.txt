Singular layer Physics Informed Neural Network method for Plane Parallel Flows
Teng-Yuan Chang 1 , Gung-Min Gie 2 , Youngjoon Hong 3 , and Chang-Yeol Jung 4

Abstract
. We construct in this article the semi-analytic Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), called singular layer PINNs (or sl-PINNs ), that are suitable to predict the stiff solutions of plane-parallel flows at a small viscosity. Recalling the boundary layer analysis, we first find the corrector for the problem which describes the singular behavior of the viscous flow inside boundary layers. Then, using the components of the corrector and its curl, we build our new sl-PINN predictions for the velocity and the vorticity by either embedding the explicit expression of the corrector (or its curl) in the structure of PINNs or by training the implicit parts of the corrector (or its curl) together with the PINN predictions. Numerical experiments confirm that our new sl-PINNs produce stable and accurate predicted solutions for the plane-parallel flows at a small viscosity.

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left|\hat{u}_{2}-\varepsilon\Delta\hat{u}_{2}+\hat{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\hat{u}_{2}-f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}}\left|\hat{u}_{2}(x,z=0)\right|^{2}+\left|\hat{u}_{2}(x,z=1)\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\hat{u}_{2}(x=0,z)-\hat{u}_{2}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{2}\subset\Omega
is the training data set chosen in the domain,
\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
is the training data set chosen at
z=0,1
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}\subset\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
is the training data set chosen on the periodic boundary.

As mentioned in the introduction above,
excessive computational costs are needed for PINNs to predict the solution which changes rapidly in a region such as boundary layer,
and
in many cases, the conventional PINNs happen to
produce incorrect predictions for this stiff problem.
In order to train a network to learn the solution’s behavior accurately inside the boundary layers,
we will enrich our training solutions by leveraging the corrector, which is similar to our early studies
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. The detail construction of our new training solutions are in the following Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

3.1.
sl-PINN
predictions for
u_{1}^{\varepsilon}

To obtain an accurate prediction of
u_{1}^{\varepsilon}
at a small
\varepsilon
,
we enrich the NN output by incorporating
a proper approximation of the corrector.
More precisely,
following the boundary layer analysis studied in Section
2
,
we modify the traditional PINN structure and
construct
the training solution in the form,

(3.3)

\tilde{u}_{1}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})=\hat{u}_{1}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})+\tilde{\varphi}_{1,L}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})+\tilde{\varphi}_{1,R}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}).

Here

\hat{u}_{1}
is the neural network output
and,
using the explicit profile of the corrector
{\varphi}_{1}
in (
2.6
),
we set

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{1,L}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\hat{u}_{1}(0;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{1,R}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\hat{u}_{1}(1;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

Note that the predicted solution
\tilde{u}_{1}
in (
3.3
) satisfies the zero boundary condition at
z=0,1
, up to negligible
e.s.t.
with respect to
\varepsilon
,
thanks to the fact that the function

e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
(or
e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
)
decays exponentially fast away from the boundary at
z=0
(or
z=1
).
Hence the loss function for this
sl-PINN
for
u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
is defined by

(3.6)

\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\mathcal{T}_{1})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{1}|}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}\left|\tilde{u}_{1}-\varepsilon d_{z}^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}-f_{1}\right|^{2},

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is the set of training points chosen in
\{z\ |\ 0\leq z\leq 1\}
,
i.e., the boundary condition for
u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
is already enforced to be satisfied (up to
e.s.t.
) in the structure of
\tilde{u}_{1}
.

Thanks to (
2.8
),
we notice from the direct computation with (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{1}-\varepsilon d_{z}^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}-f_{1}=-\varepsilon(z-1)d_{z}^{2}\hat{u}_{1}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})-\hat{u}_{1}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})-f_{1}+e.s.t.

Thus the loss function (
3.6
) remains bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This important fact makes the training of our
sl-PINN
for
u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
reliable and
it leads to a solid minimization and accurate prediction.
The schematics are illustrated in Figure
3.1
for both PINN and
sl-PINN
.
The numerical experiments appear in Section
3.3
.

we train the viscous solution
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}

\varphi_{2}
at the same time. That is,
we
construct one neural network,
in part to train
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}
and also to train
\varphi_{2}
as well,
and hence they interact each other in the form,

(3.7)

\tilde{u}_{2}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2})=\hat{u}_{2}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})+\tilde{\varphi}_{2,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,L})+\tilde{\varphi}_{2,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,R}),

and

(3.8)

\displaystyle\vskip 4.2679pt\tilde{\varphi}_{2,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,L})

\displaystyle=-\hat{u}_{2}(x,0;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+ze^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\varphi}_{2,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,L}),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{2,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,R})

\displaystyle=-\hat{u}_{2}(x,1;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+(1-z)e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\varphi}_{2,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,R}),

where
\hat{u}_{2}
,
\hat{\varphi}_{2,L}
and
\hat{\varphi}_{2,R}
are the network outputs
and

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,L},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,R}\}
with

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
,

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,L}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2,s,R}
are
the unknown parameters of the outputs
\hat{u}_{2}
,
\hat{\varphi}_{2,L}
, and
\hat{\varphi}_{2,R}
respectively.
We refer readers to see the picture of NN structure in Figure
3.2
with a comparison to the conventional PINN structure.

As analyzed in (
2.11
); see
[
14
,
11
]
as well,
it is verified that
\varphi_{2,L}
and
\varphi_{2,R}

behave like exponentially decaying functions
from the boundaries at
z=0
and
z=1
(at least in the
H^{k}
sense for
k=0,1,2
).
Using this property,
we construct our
sl-PINN
predicted solutions for
\varphi_{2,L}
and
\varphi_{2,R}

in part by using the exponentially decaying functions with respect to
the stretched variables
z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
;
the first terms on the right-hand side of (
3.8
) and (
3.9
).
In addition, in order to implement the (implicit) interaction between the correctors and the equation,
we add the training parts in the structure, the second terms on
on the right-hand side, in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
).

By our construction, the

sl-PINN
predictions of the correctors satisfies

•

\tilde{\varphi}_{2,L}=-\hat{u}_{2}
at
z=0

and

\tilde{\varphi}_{2,R}=-\hat{u}_{2}
at
z=1
,

•

\tilde{\varphi}_{2,L}\to 0
as
z\to\infty

and

\tilde{\varphi}_{2,R}\to 0
as
z\to-\infty
,

and hence we notice that

•

\tilde{u}_{2}=0
, up to an
e.s.t.
, at
z=0,1
.

Now, because the zero boundary condition is
ensured in the structure of
\tilde{u}_{2}
(up to an
e.s.t
),
we define the loss function as

(3.10)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left|\tilde{u}_{2}-\varepsilon\Delta\tilde{u}_{2}+\tilde{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{2}-f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\tilde{u}_{2}(x=0,z)-\tilde{u}_{2}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is the training set chosen in the domain
\Omega
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}
is the training set chosen on the periodic boundary
\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
. Since there is no boundary layer occurring in
x
direction, the periodic boundary condition is simply enforced by penalizing the equivalent in the second term of the loss function.
It is straightforward
(by direct computations with (
3.7
) - (
3.9
))
to verify that the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{2}
defined in (
3.10
) is bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Therefore, the training process is reliable enough to reach an expected minimizer. The numerical experiments can be found in the next subsection
3.3
.

To examine the performance of the singular layer PINNs (

\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}

for the problem (
1.2
)
where the periodicity is set to be
L=1

u_{1}^{\varepsilon}
as

(3.11)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\varepsilon}(z)=(1-2\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{1-e^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}}{1-e^{-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}}\left(e^{-\frac{z}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}+e^{-\frac{1-z}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}\right)\right).

f_{2}

\displaystyle f_{2}(x,z)=f_{1}(z)(1+\sin(2\pi x))+4\pi^{2}\varepsilon u_{1}^{\varepsilon}(z)\sin(2\pi x)+2\pi(u_{1}^{\varepsilon}(z))^{2}\cos(2\pi x),

u_{2}^{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle u_{2}^{\varepsilon}(x,z)=u_{1}^{\varepsilon}(z)(1+\sin(2\pi x)).

4.
Numerical experiments for the vorticity

In this section, we present the framework and the experiments of our semi-analytic singular layer PINN (
sl-PINN
) methods for
solving the vorticity equations (
1.4
), especially when
the viscosity
\varepsilon
is small.

Following the neural network (NN) architecture, introduced in Section
3
,
we recall the conventional PINN methods for the vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
by constructing one network for each component of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
.
More precisely, we build a NN for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
(sol. of (
1.4
)
2
with (
1.5
)
2
) first.
Then, using this predicted solution for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
as well as the one for
u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
, built by the conventional PINN in the previous section
3
,
we construct the PINN predicted solution for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{3}
(sol. of (
1.4
)
3
with (
1.5
)
3
), and, in a sequel,
one for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}

(sol. of (
1.4
)
1
with (
1.5
)
1
).
We let

\hat{\omega}_{1}=\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})
,
\hat{\omega}_{2}=\hat{\omega}_{2}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})
, and
\hat{\omega}_{3}=\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3})

denote
the outputs of each NNs respectively
where each
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
is the collection of the weights and bias for
\hat{\omega}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
.

Using the equations and the boundary conditions in (
1.4
) - (
1.5
),
we train the conventional PINN by
minimizing the loss function,

\mathcal{L}_{i}
for
\hat{\omega}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
below:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{2}-\varepsilon d^{2}_{z}\hat{\omega}_{2}-d_{z}f_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|d_{z}\hat{\omega}_{2}(0)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{1}(0)\right|^{2}+\left|d_{z}\hat{\omega}_{2}(1)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{1}(1)\right|^{2},

where
\mathcal{T}_{2}\subset[0,1]
is the set of training points,

(4.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{3}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3};\mathcal{T}_{3},\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{3}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{3}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{3}-\varepsilon\Delta\hat{\omega}_{3}+\hat{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\hat{\omega}_{3}-\partial_{x}f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,z=0)\right|^{2}+\left|\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,z=1)\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{3}(x=0,z)-\hat{\omega}_{3}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{3}\subset\Omega
,
\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}\subset\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
are the sets of training points, and

(4.3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1};\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{1}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{1}-\varepsilon\Delta\hat{\omega}_{1}-\hat{\omega}_{2}\hat{\omega}_{3}+\hat{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\hat{\omega}_{1}+\partial_{z}f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}}\left|\partial_{z}\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,0)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{2}(x,0)\right|^{2}+\left|\partial_{z}\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,1)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{2}(x,1)\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{1}(x=0,z)-\hat{\omega}_{1}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\Omega
is the set of training points.

Notice that each of the loss functions
\mathcal{L}_{2}
in (
4.1
) and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in (
4.3
) contains a term of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
,
and hence they are not (expected to be) bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
In fact, because of this unbounded nature of the loss functions,
the conventional PINN methods just fail to produce good predictions
for the vorticity
as we observe from the below numerical experiments in Section
4.2
.

In the following section,
we propose our new semi-analytic singular layer PINNs (
sl-PINNs
)
to predict well the vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
.
By embedding the singular profile of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
in the
sl-PINNs
structure,
we build the corresponding NNs to train the smooth part of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
and finally obtain the accurate predicted solutions for

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
.
As it appears in Section
4.1
,
our
sl-PINNs
produce accurate predictions for

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
independent of a small
\varepsilon
.

4.1.
sl-PINN
predictions for the vorticity

To train predicted solutions for the vorticity

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}=\text{curl }\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}=\big(-\partial_{z}u^{\varepsilon}_{2},\,d_{z}u^{\varepsilon}_{1},\,\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}_{2}\big)

(sol. of (
1.4
) - (
1.5
)),
we follow the methodology introduced in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
and
construct our

sl-PINNs
sequentially for the components

{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{2}
,
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{3}
, and then
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{1}
.
In order to incorporate
the singular behavior near the boundary of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}

in our
sl-PINN
structure,
we use below the profiles of the curl of the corrector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{*}=(\tilde{{\varphi}}_{1,*},\,\tilde{{\varphi}}_{2,*},\,0)
,
*=L,R
, defined in (
3.4
), (
3.5
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
).

Note that
the first component of the corrector
\tilde{{\varphi}}_{1,*}
,
*=L,R
, is given in its explicit form as in (
3.4
), (
3.5
), and hence
we embed the
d_{z}\tilde{{\varphi}}_{1,*}
,
*=L,R
,
in our
sl-PINN
structure for
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{2}
; see below in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
).
On the other hand,
because the explicit expression of second component
\tilde{{\varphi}}_{2,*}
,
*=L,R
,
is not available,
we train the viscous solution
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{3}
(and then
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{1}
) together with training simultaneously
the correctors

\partial_{x}\tilde{{\varphi}}_{2,*}
,
*=L,R
(and then

-\partial_{z}\tilde{{\varphi}}_{2,*}
,
*=L,R
).
This process is exactly what we employed for building
our
sl-PINN
approximations for
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}
in (
3.7
) - (
3.9
), but here
we need to work with a different type of boundary condition, e.g., Neumann boundary condition for
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}_{1}
.

We introduce the
sl-PINN
predictions

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(\widetilde{\omega}_{1},\widetilde{\omega}_{2},\widetilde{\omega}_{3})
enriched by the correctors.
For the
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
,
using the fact that
the corrector for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}=d_{z}u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
is

d_{z}\varphi_{1}
and
its explicit expression is available, i.e.,

\varepsilon^{-1/2}e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
near
z=0
, we write the
sl-PINN
structure as

(4.4)

\tilde{\omega}_{2}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})=\hat{\omega}_{2}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})+\tilde{\psi}_{2,L}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})+\tilde{\psi}_{2,R}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}),

where the correctors are defined by

(4.5)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.2679pt\tilde{\psi}_{2,L}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})=\displaystyle\big(f_{1}(0)+\varepsilon d_{z}\hat{\omega}_{2}(0;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})\big)\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\\
\tilde{\psi}_{2,R}(z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})=\displaystyle\big(-f_{1}(1)-{\varepsilon}d_{z}\hat{\omega}_{2}(1;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})\big)\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.\end{cases}

Thanks to this setting of the correctors
\tilde{\psi}_{2,*}
,
*=L,R
,
we notice that

•

\tilde{\psi}_{2,L}\to 0
as
z\to\infty

and

\tilde{\psi}_{2,R}\to 0
as
z\to-\infty
,

•

d_{z}\tilde{\omega}_{2}=-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{1}
, up to an
e.s.t.
, at
z=0,1
.

For the
\tilde{\omega}_{3}
,
we use exactly the same construction as for the
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}
in (
3.7
) - (
3.9
),
and write

(4.6)

\tilde{\omega}_{3}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{3})=\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3})+\tilde{\psi}_{3,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,L})+\tilde{\psi}_{3,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,R}),

with

(4.7)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.2679pt\tilde{\psi}_{3,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,L})=-\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,0;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3})e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+ze^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\psi}_{3,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,L}),\\
\tilde{\psi}_{3,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,R})=-\hat{\omega}_{3}(x,1;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3})e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+(1-z)e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\psi}_{3,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3,s,R}).\end{cases}

That is,
we
use a neural network
for training
\hat{\omega}_{3}
and
\hat{\psi}_{3}
at the same time.
By our construction (and the analysis in (
2.11
)),
we observe that

•

\tilde{\psi}_{3,L}\to 0
as
z\to\infty

and

\tilde{\psi}_{3,R}\to 0
as
z\to-\infty
,

•

\tilde{\omega}_{3}=0
, up to an
e.s.t.
, at
z=0,1
.

For the first component
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
,
we combine the methods we employed for the other two components
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{3}
.
Namely, we write our
sl-PINN
structure for
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
by training
the
\hat{\omega}_{1}
(smooth part) and the
\hat{\psi}_{1}
(singular part) simultaneously
and by
enforcing the Neumann boundary condition,
in the form,

(4.8)

\tilde{\omega}_{1}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1})=\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})+\tilde{\psi}_{1,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,L})+\tilde{\psi}_{1,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,R}),

with

(4.9)

\begin{cases}\tilde{\psi}_{1,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,L})=&\vskip 4.2679pt\Big(-f_{2}(x,0)+{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,0;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\Big)\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\\
&\vskip 4.2679pt+z^{2}e^{-z/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\psi}_{1,L}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,L}),\\
\tilde{\psi}_{1,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,R})=&\vskip 4.2679pt\Big(f_{2}(x,1)-{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}\hat{\omega}_{1}(x,1;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\Big)\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\\
&+(1-z)^{2}e^{-(1-z)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\hat{\psi}_{1,R}(x,z;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1,s,R}).\end{cases}

We then observe that

•

\tilde{\psi}_{1,L}\to 0
as
z\to\infty

and

\tilde{\psi}_{1,R}\to 0
as
z\to-\infty
,

•

\partial_{z}\tilde{\omega}_{1}=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{2}
, up to an
e.s.t.
, at
z=0,1
.

In the constructions of
\tilde{\omega}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,
above, we used the notation for the learning parameters,

(4.10)

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i,s,L},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i,s,R}\},\quad i=1,2,3.

Because the boundary condition for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is already taken into account in the construction of our
sl-PINNs
,
we define the loss functions for
sl-PINN
s as follows:

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\left|\tilde{\omega}_{2}-\varepsilon d^{2}_{z}\tilde{\omega}_{2}-d_{z}f_{1}\right|^{2},

where
\mathcal{T}_{2}\subset[0,1]
is the set of training points,

(4.12)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{3}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{3};\mathcal{T}_{3},\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{3}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{3}}\left|\tilde{\omega}_{3}-\varepsilon\Delta\tilde{\omega}_{3}+\tilde{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{\omega}_{3}-\partial_{x}f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\tilde{\omega}_{3}(x=0,z)-\tilde{\omega}_{3}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{3}\subset\Omega
,
\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}\subset\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
are the sets of training points, and

(4.13)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1};\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}_{B})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{1}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}\left|\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\varepsilon\Delta\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\tilde{\omega}_{2}\tilde{\omega}_{3}+\tilde{u}_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{\omega}_{1}+\partial_{z}f_{2}\right|^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{B}|}\sum_{(x,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}\left|\tilde{\omega}_{1}(x=0,z)-\tilde{\omega}_{1}(x=L,z)\right|^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\Omega
is the set of training points.

It is noteworthy to point out that
the loss functions in (
4.11
), (
4.12
) and (
4.13
)
stay bounded as the viscosity gets small, i.e., as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Thanks to this important feature, as we shall see below in Section
4.2
,
our
sl-PINN
s produce stable and accurate predictions
for the vorticity, independent of the viscosity
\varepsilon
.

4.2.
Comparison between the conventional PINNs and the
sl-PINNs
for the vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}

To perform the numerical experiments,
we first write the exact solution for
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}

by taking curl of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}
in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
):

(4.14)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.2679pt\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}(x,z)=-\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}(z)(1+\sin(2\pi x)),\\
\vskip 4.2679pt\displaystyle\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}(z)=-(1-2\varepsilon)\frac{1-e^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}}{1-e^{-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}}\left(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-\frac{z}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}e^{-\frac{1-z}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}\right)+1-2z,\\
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{3}(x,z)=2\pi u^{\varepsilon}_{1}(z)\cos(2\pi x).\end{cases}

We infer from (
4.14
) and the boundary layer analysis results in Theorem
1.1
that

•

Asymptotic behavior of
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{3}
is as much singular as that of
u^{\varepsilon}_{1}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. That is, as small as

e^{-\eta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
,
\eta=z
or
1-z
, near the boundary.

•

The
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}
and
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
behave near the boundary as the approximation of identity,
\varepsilon^{-1/2}\,e^{-\eta/\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
,
\eta=z
or
1-z
, which converges as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
to
a positive measure
\delta_{\Gamma}
supported on the boundary
\Gamma
.

The asymptotic behavior at a small viscosity of the vorticity (
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}
and
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
) is more singular than that of the velocity vector field, and hence
it is more challenging to approximate/predict
the vorticity than the velocity at a small viscosity
by employing PINN or any other classical numerical methods, e.g., Finite Elements, Finite Differences, Finite Volumes, or discontinuous Galerkin methods, and so on.

We aim to verify the accurate performance of our
sl-PINN
methods to approximate the solution
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
to (
1.4
) without causing an excessive computation cost.
The experimental settings are the same as those for the velocity in Section
3.3
.
We recall, in particular, that we use single-layer NNs with a small amount of training data to remain a low computational cost while maintaining sharp accuracy.
The training data set
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\Omega
for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}
is the same as
\mathcal{T}_{3}\subset\Omega
for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{3}
.
The experiments for conventional PINNs are conducted for comparison with our
sl-PINNs
.
We present here the results of
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
and
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}
only since the equation for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{3}
is identical to that of
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}
, whose numerical simulations are well-studied in Section
3.3
.

First, the loss value during the training process for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
and
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{1}
are shown in Figures
4.1
and
4.2
respectively.
As expected, the conventional PINNs just fail to minimize the loss functions (
4.1
) and (
4.3
)
because they started with an extremely large value in their minimization, results in an incorrect minimizer.
On the contrary, our new
sl-PINNs
’ loss functions (
4.11
) and (
4.13
) are bounded independent of
\varepsilon
,
and thus they are well minimized for every small
\varepsilon
.
Moreover, the efficiency of
sl-PINNs

outperforms that of PINNs,
as it reaches a minimum with far fewer iterations.

The computational errors are shown in Tables
3
and

for
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{2}
and

We conclude from the simulations that
our

\varepsilon
