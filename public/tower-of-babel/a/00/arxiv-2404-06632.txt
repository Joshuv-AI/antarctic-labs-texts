Relative entropy bounds for sampling with and without replacement
Oliver Johnson, Lampros Gavalakis, Ioannis Kontoyiannis

Abstract
Sharp, nonasymptotic bounds are obtained for the relative entropy between the distributions of sampling with and without replacement from an urn with balls of c ≥ 2 c\geq 2 colors. Our bounds are asymptotically tight in certain regimes and, unlike previous results, they depend on the number of balls of each colour in the urn. The connection of these results with finite de Finetti-style theorems is explored, and it is observed that a sampling bound due to Stam (1978) combined with the convexity of relative entropy yield a new finite de Finetti bound in relative entropy, which achieves the optimal asymptotic convergence rate.

Introduction

1.1
The problem

Consider an urn containing
n
balls, each ball having one
of
c\geq 2
different colours, and suppose we draw out
k\leq n
of them.
We will compare the difference in sampling with and without replacement.
Write
{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{c})
for the vector representing
the number of balls of each colour in the urn, so that there are

\ell_{i}
balls of colour
i
,
1\leq i\leq c

and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{c}=n
.
Write
{\bm{s}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{c})
for the number of balls
of each colour drawn out, so that
s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
.

When sampling without replacement, the probability that the
colors of the
k
balls drawn are given by
{\bm{s}}

is given by the multivariate hypergeometric
probability mass function (p.m.f.),

H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}}):=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{c}\binom{\ell_{i}}{s_{i}}}{\binom{n}{k}}=\frac{\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}\binom{n-k}{{\bm{\ell}}-{\bm{s}}}}{\binom{n}{{\bm{\ell}}}},

(1)

for all
{\bm{s}}
with

0\leq s_{i}\leq\ell_{i}
for all
i
, and

s_{1}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
. In (
1
) and
throughout,
we write
\binom{v}{{\bm{u}}}=v!/\prod_{i=1}^{c}u_{i}!
for the
multinomial coefficient for any
vector
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{c})
with

u_{1}+\cdots+u_{c}=v
.

Our goal is to compare

H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})

with the corresponding p.m.f.
B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})

of sampling with replacement,
which is given by the multinomial distribution p.m.f.,

B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}}):=\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\left(\frac{\ell_{i}}{n}\right)^{s_{i}},

(2)

for all
{\bm{s}}
with

s_{i}\geq 0
and

s_{1}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
.

The study of the relationship between sampling
with and without replacement
has a long history and numerous applications
in both statistics and probability;
see, e.g., the classic review
[
22
]
or the
text
[
24
]
.
Beyond the elementary observation that
B
and
H
have
the same mean, namely, that,

\sum_{{\bm{s}}}B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})s_{i}=\sum_{{\bm{s}}}H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})s_{i}=k\ell_{i}/n,

for each
i
,
it is well known that in certain limiting regimes
the p.m.f.s themselves
are close in a variety of senses.
For example,
Diaconis and Freedman
[
12
, Theorem 4]

show
that
H
and
B
are close in the total variation
distance
\|P-Q\|:=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|
:

\|H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};\cdot)-B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};\cdot)\|\leq\frac{ck}{n}.

(3)

In this paper we give bounds on the relative entropy
(or Kullback-Leibler divergence) between
H
and

B
, which for brevity we denote as:

\displaystyle D(n,k,{\bm{\ell}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle D\left(H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};\cdot)\|B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};\cdot)\right)

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{s}}}H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})\log\Big(\frac{H(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})}{B(n,k,{\bm{\ell}};{\bm{s}})}\Big).

[All logarithms are natural logarithms to base
e
.]
Clearly, if
\ell_{r}=0
for some
r
,
then bounding
D(n,k;{\bm{\ell}})
becomes equivalent
to the same problem with a smaller number of colours
c
,
so we may assume that each
\ell_{r}\geq 1
for simplicity.

Interest in controlling the relative entropy derives
in part from the fact that it
naturally arises in many core statistical problems,
e.g., as the optimal hypothesis testing error exponent
in Stein’s lemma; see, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorem 12.8.1]
.
Further, bounding the relative entropy also provides
control of the distance between
H
and
B
in other senses.
For example, Pinsker’s inequality,
e.g.
[
26
, Lemma 2.9.1]
,
states that for any two
p.m.f.s
P
and
Q
,

2\|P-Q\|^{2}\leq D(P\|Q),

(5)

while the Bretagnolle-Huber bound
[
7
,
8
]

gives:

\|P-Q\|^{2}\leq 1-\exp\{-D(P\|Q)\}.

Our results also follow along a long line of work that has been
devoted to establishing probabilistic theorems in terms of relative
entropy. Information-theoretic arguments often
provide insights into the underlying reasons why the result
at hand holds. Among many others, examples include the
well-known work of Barron
[
4
]
on the information-theoretic
central limit theorem;
information-theoretic proofs of Poisson convergence
and Poisson approximation
[
20
]
;
compound Poisson approximation bounds
[
3
]
;
convergence to Haar measure on compact groups
[
16
]
;
connections with extreme value theory
[
18
]
;
and the discrete central limit theorem
[
15
]
.
We refer
to
[
14
]
for a detailed review of this line of work.

The relative entropy in (
4
) has been studied before.
Stam
[
23
]
established the following bound,

D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)\leq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)},

(6)

uniformly in
{\bm{\ell}}
,
and also provided an asymptotically matching lower bound,

D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)\geq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{4(n-1)^{2}},

(7)

indicating that,
in the regime
k=o(n)
and fixed
c
,
the relative entropy

D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)
is of order
(c-1)k^{2}/2n^{2}
.

More recently, related bounds were
established by Harremoës and Matúš
[
17
]
, who showed that (see
[
17
, Theorem 4.5]
),

D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)\leq(c-1)\left(\log\left(\frac{n-1}{n-k}\right)-\frac{k}{n}+\frac{1}{n-k+1}\right),

(8)

and (see
[
17
, Theorem 4.4]
),

D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)\geq\frac{(c-1)}{2}\left(r-1-\ln r\right),\mbox{\qquad for }r=\frac{n-k+1}{n-1},

(9)

both results also holding uniformly in
{\bm{\ell}}
.
Moreover, in the regime where
k/n\rightarrow s

and
0<\epsilon\leq\ell_{r}/n
for all
r
,
they showed that the lower bound in (
9
) is asymptotically sharp and
the upper bound in (
8
) is sharp to within a factor of 2 by proving that:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right)=\frac{c-1}{2}\left(-\log(1-s)-s\right)\approx(c-1)\left(\frac{s^{2}}{4}+\frac{s^{3}}{6}\right).

(10)

1.2
Main results

Despite the fact that (
6
) and (
8
) are both
asymptotically optimal in the sense described above, it turns out
it is possible to obtain more accurate upper bounds
on
D(n,k,{\bm{\ell}})
if we allow them to
depend on
{\bm{\ell}}
.
1
1

1

Indeed, as remarked
by Harremoës and Matúš
[
17
]
, “a good upper bound
should depend on
{\bm{\ell}}
” (in our notation).

In this vein, our main result is the following;
it is proved in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
1\leq k\leq n/2
,

c\geq 2
, and
{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{c})

with
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{c}=n
and
\ell_{r}\geq 1

for each
r
,
the relative entropy

D(n,k,{\bm{\ell}})
between
H
and
B
satisfies:

\displaystyle D(n,k,{\bm{\ell}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c-1}{2}\Big(\log\Big(\frac{n}{n-k}\Big)-\frac{k}{n-1}\Big)

(11)

\displaystyle+\frac{k(2n+1)}{12n(n-1)(n-k)}\sum_{i=1}^{c}\frac{n}{\ell_{i}}+\frac{1}{360}\Big(\frac{1}{(n-k)^{3}}-\frac{1}{n^{3}}\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{c}\frac{n^{3}}{\ell_{i}^{3}}.\qquad

While the bound of Theorem
1.1
is simple,
straightforward, and works well for the balanced cases
described below, it is least accurate in the case
of small
\ell_{i}
; see Figure
1
.
We therefore also provide an alternative expression
in Proposition
1.2
. For simplicity, we only
give the result in the case
c=2
, though similar
arguments will work in general. It is proved
in Appendix
A.3
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
,
if
c=2
and
\ell\leq n/2
, we have:

\displaystyle D(n,k,(\ell,n-\ell))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\Big[\Big(1-\frac{k}{n}\Big)\log\Big(1-\frac{k}{n}\Big)+\frac{k}{n}-\frac{k}{2n(n-1)}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{k\ell}{(n-1)(n-\ell)(n-k)}

\displaystyle-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n(n-1)}\ell(\ell-1)\log\Big(\frac{\ell}{\ell-1}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{k(k-1)(k-2)}{6n(n-1)(n-2)}\ell(\ell-1)(\ell-2)\log\Big(\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{\ell(\ell-2)}\Big).

Remarks.

1.

The dependence of the bound (
11
)
in Theorem
1.1

on
{\bm{\ell}}
is only via the quantities:

\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}}):=\sum_{r=1}^{c}\frac{n}{\ell_{r}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Sigma_{2}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}}):=\sum_{r=1}^{c}\frac{n^{3}}{\ell_{r}^{3}}.

(12)

For
\Sigma_{1}
,
in the ‘balanced’ case where each
\ell_{r}=n/c
,
we have
\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})=c^{2}
, and using Jensen’s inequality it is
easy to see that we always have
\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})\geq c^{2}
.
In fact, the Schur-Ostrowski criterion shows that (
12
)
is Schur convex, and hence respects the majorization order,
being largest in ‘unbalanced’ cases.
For example, if

\ell_{1}=\cdots=\ell_{c-1}=1
and
\ell_{c}=n-c+1
,
then
\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})=(c-1)n+1/(n-c+1)\sim n(c-1)\to\infty

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
On the other hand, in the regime considered by Harremoës and Matúš
[
17
]
where all

\ell_{r}\geq\epsilon n
for some
0<\epsilon\leq 1/c
,
we have
\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})\leq c/\epsilon
,
which is bounded in
n
.

Similar comments apply to
\Sigma_{2}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})
:
It is bounded in balanced cases, including
the scenario of Harremoës and Matúš
[
17
]
, but can grow like
n^{3}(c-1)
in
the very unbalanced case.

2.

The first term in the bound (
11
) of Theorem
1.1
,

\frac{c-1}{2}\Big(\log\Big(\frac{n}{n-k}\Big)-\frac{k}{n-1}\Big),

matches the asymptotic expression in (
10
), so
Theorem
1.1
may be
regarded as a nonasymptotic version version of (
10
).
In particular, (
10
) is only valid in
the asymptotic case where all
\ell_{r}\geq\epsilon n
,
when, as discussed above,

\Sigma_{1}(n,c,{\bm{\ell}})
is bounded. Therefore, in this balanced
case Theorem
1.1
recovers the asymptotic result
of (
10
).

3.

Any bound that holds uniformly in
{\bm{\ell}}
must hold in particular
for the binary
c=2
case with
\ell_{1}=1,\ell_{2}=n-1
.
Then
H
is Bernoulli with
parameter
k/n
and
B
is binomial with parameters
k
and
1/n
,
and direct calculation
gives the exact expression:

D\left(n,k,(1,n-1)\right)=\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)-k\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right).

Taking
n\to\infty
while
k/n\rightarrow s

for some fixed
s
, in this unbalanced case where
\ell_{1}=1
and
\ell_{2}=n-1
,
we obtain:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D\left(n,ns,(1,n-1)\right)=s+(1-s)\log(1-s)\approx\frac{s^{2}}{2}+\frac{s^{3}}{6}.

(13)

Compared with the analogous limiting expression (
10
)
in the balanced case where
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are both bounded
below by
\epsilon n
,
this suggests that the asymptotic behaviour of
D(n,k,{\bm{\ell}})

in the regime
k/n\to s

may vary by a factor of 2 over different values of
\ell
.

4.

Comparing the earlier asymptotic expression (
10
)
with (
13
), reveals some interesting behaviour
of
D(n,k,(\ell,n-\ell))
.
There is a unique value
s^{*}\approx 0.8834
such that,
for
s<s^{*}
, corresponding to small
k
,
the limiting expression in (
13
) is larger
than that in (
10
),
whereas, for
s>s^{*}
, the expression in (
10
) is larger.

1.3
Finite de Finetti theorems

Diaconis
[
11
]

and Diaconis and Freedman
[
12
]

revealed an interesting and intimate connection between
the problem of comparing sampling with and without
replacement, and finite versions of de Finetti’s theorem.
Recall that de Finetti’s theorem says that the distribution
of a infinite collection of exchangeable random variables
can be represented as a mixture over independent and
identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequences;
see, e.g.,
[
19
]

for an elementary proof in the binary case.
Although such a representation does not always exist
for
finite
exchangeable sequences,
approximate versions are possible:
If
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are exchangeable, then the distribution
of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
is close to a mixture of independent
sequences as long as
k
is relatively small compared to
n
.
Indeed,
Diaconis and Freedman
[
12
]
proved
such a finite de Finetti theorem using
the sampling bound (
3
).

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\}
be an alphabet of size
c
,
and suppose that the random variables

{\bm{X}}_{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, with values
X_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
are exchangeable, that is, the distribution of
{\bm{X}}_{n}

is the same as that
of
\left(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)}\right)
for any
permutation
\pi
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Given a sequence
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{m}
of length
m
,
its
type

{\mathcal{T}}_{m}({\bm{y}})

[
10
]

is the vector of its empirical frequencies: The

r
th component of
{\mathcal{T}}_{m}({\bm{y}})
is:

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{I}}(y_{i}=a_{r}),\quad 1\leq r\leq c.

The sequence
{\bm{X}}_{n}
induces a measure
\mu
on
p.m.f.s
{\bm{p}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}
, via:

\mu({\bm{p}})={\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{T}}_{n}({\bm{X}}_{n})={\bm{p}}\right).

(14)

This is the law of the empirical measure induced
by
{\bm{X}}_{n}
on
{\mathcal{A}}
.

For each
1\leq k\leq n
, let
P_{k}
denote the
joint p.m.f. of
{\bm{X}}_{k}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
,
and write,

M_{k,\mu}({\bm{z}})=\int{\bm{p}}^{k}({\bm{z}})d\mu({\bm{p}}),\quad{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{k},

(15)

for the mixture of the i.i.d. distributions

{\bm{p}}^{k}
with respect to the mixing measure
\mu
.
A key step in the connection between sampling bounds and
finite de Finetti theorems is the simple observation that,
given that its type
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}({\bm{X}}_{k})={\bm{q}}
,
the exchangeable sequence
{\bm{X}}_{k}
is uniformly distributed on the
set of sequences with type
{\bm{q}}
.
In other words, for any
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
,

P_{k}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}}\sum_{{\bm{p}}}H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{p}}),

(16)

where
{\bm{s}}=k{\mathcal{T}}_{k}({\bm{x}})
.
This elementary and rather obvious (by symmetry)
observation already appears implicitly in the
literature, e.g., in
[
11
,
14
]
.

An analogous representation can be easily seen
to hold for
M_{k,\mu}
. Since the probability
of an i.i.d. sequence only depends on its type,
for any
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
we have,
with
{\bm{s}}=k{\mathcal{T}}_{k}({\bm{x}})
:

M_{k,\mu}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}}\sum_{{\bm{p}}}B(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{p}}).

(17)

The following simple proposition clarifies
the connection between finite de Finetti theorems
and sampling bounds. Its proof is a simple
consequence of (
16
) combined
with (
17
) and the
log-sum inequality
[
9
]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Suppose
{\bm{X}}_{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
is a finite
exchangeable sequence of random variables with
values in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and
let the
\mu
denote the law of the empirical measure
defined by (
14
).
Then for any
k\leq n
, we have,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})\leq\sum_{{\bm{p}}}\mu({\bm{p}})D\left(n,k,n{\bm{p}}\right)\leq\max_{\frac{{\bm{\ell}}}{n}\in{\rm supp}(\mu)}D\left(n,k,{\bm{\ell}}\right),

where
M_{k,\mu}
is the mixture defined in (
15
)
and the maximum is taken over all p.m.f.s of
the form
{\bm{\ell}}/n
in the support of
\mu
.

Proof.

Fix
{\bm{s}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{c}s_{i}=k
. The log-sum inequality gives
that for any
{\bm{x}}
of type
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}({\bm{x}})={\bm{s}}/k
:

\displaystyle P_{k}({\bm{x}})\log\left(\frac{P_{k}({\bm{x}})}{M_{k,\mu}({\bm{x}})}\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}^{-1}\left(\sum_{{\bm{p}}}H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{p}})\right)\log\left(\frac{\sum_{{\bm{p}}}H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{p}})}{\sum_{{\bm{p}}}B(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{p}})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}^{-1}\sum_{{\bm{p}}}\mu({\bm{p}})H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\log\left(\frac{H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})}{B(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})}\right).

Hence, summing over the
\binom{k}{{\bm{s}}}
vectors
{\bm{x}}
of type

{\mathcal{T}}_{k}({\bm{x}})={\bm{s}}/k
we obtain,

\sum_{{\bm{x}}:{\mathcal{T}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{s}}/k}P_{k}({\bm{x}})\log\left(\frac{P_{k}({\bm{x}})}{M_{k,\mu}({\bm{x}})}\right)\leq\sum_{{\bm{p}}}\mu({\bm{p}})H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})\log\left(\frac{H(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})}{B(n,k,n{\bm{p}};{\bm{s}})}\right).

Finally, summing over
{\bm{s}}
yields,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})\leq\sum_{{\bm{p}}}\mu({\bm{p}})D\left(n,k,n{\bm{p}}\right),

and each of the relative entropy terms can be bounded by the
maximum value.
∎

Combining Stam’s bound (
6
) and Proposition
1.3

immediately gives:

Corollary 1.4

(Sharp finite de Finetti)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
1.3
,
for
1\leq k\leq n
:

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})\leq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)}.

(18)

Pinsker’s inequality (
5
) and (
18
)
gives,

\|P_{k}-M_{k,\mu}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)}}.

In the
k=o(n)
regime this is in fact optimal,
in that it is of the same order as
Diaconis and Freedman’s
[
12
]

bound,

\|P_{k}-M_{k,\mu}\|\leq\frac{ck}{n},

(19)

which they show is of optimal order in
k
and
n

when
k=o(n)
.

For certain applications, for example
to approximation schemes for minimization
of specific polynomials
[
5
]
,
the dependence on the alphabet size is also of interest;
see also
[
6
]
and the references therein.
The lower bound (
7
) shows that the linear
dependence on the alphabet size
c
is optimal for the sampling problem,
in the sense that any upper bound that holds for any
c
,
k
, and
n
,
must have at least linear dependence on
c
.
However, we do not know
whether the linear dependence on the alphabet size
c
is optimal
for the de Finetti problem under optimal rates in
k,n
.

Information-theoretic proofs of finite de Finetti theorems
have been lately developed
in
[
13
,
14
,
6
]
.
In
[
13
]
, the bound,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})\leq\frac{5k^{2}\log n}{n-k},

(20)

was obtained for binary sequences,
and in
[
14
]
,
the weaker bound,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})=O\left(\Big(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{1/2}\log{\frac{n}{k}}\right),

(21)

was established for finite-valued random variables.
Finally the sharper bound,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu})\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-k-1)}\log c,

(22)

was derived in
[
6
]
, where
random variables with values in abstract spaces were also considered.
Although the derivations of (
20
)–(
22
)
are interesting in that they employ purely information-theoretic
ideas and techniques, the actual bounds
are of strictly weaker rate than the sharp
O(k^{2}/n^{2})
rate
we obtained in Corollary
1.4
via sampling bounds.

A question on monotonicity.

A significant development in the area of information-theoretic
