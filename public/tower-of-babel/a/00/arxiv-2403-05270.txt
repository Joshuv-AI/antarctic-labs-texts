A note on lenses in arrangements of pairwise intersecting circles in the plane
Rom Pinchasi

Abstract
Let ℱ {\cal{F}} be a family of n n pairwise intersecting circles in the plane. We show that the number of lenses, that is convex digons, in the arrangement induced by ℱ {\cal{F}} is at most 2 ​ n − 2 2n-2 . This bound is tight. Furthermore, if no two circles in ℱ {\cal{F}} touch, then the geometric graph G G on the set of centers of the circles in ℱ {\cal{F}} whose edges correspond to the lenses generated by ℱ {\cal{F}} does not contain pairs of avoiding edges. That is, G G does not contain pairs of edges that are opposite edges in a convex quadrilateral. Such graphs are known to have at most 2 ​ n − 2 2n-2 edges.



1
Introduction

Given a family
{\cal{F}}
of circles in the plane we consider
the planar arrangement that is induced by the circles in

{\cal{F}}
and denote it by
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
. The arrangement
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})

consists of
vertices
that are intersection points of circles in
{\cal{F}}
and also of
edges
that are arcs of circles in
{\cal{F}}
delimited by two consecutive vertices.
Finally,
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
consists also of
faces
that are the connected components of the plane after removing from it the union of all circles in
{\cal{F}}
.
A
digon

in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
is a face in the arrangement
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
that has two edges.
We distinguish between two types of digons in arrangements of circles.
A
lens
in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
is a face with two edges in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
that is equal to the intersection of two discs bounded by circles in
{\cal{F}}
. Each of the
two circles in
{\cal{F}}
corresponding to a lens is said to
support
the lens. The lens is said to be

created

by these two circles supporting it.
Lenses are in fact just the convex digons in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
.
The arrangement
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
may contain also digons that are not convex. These are digons that are equal to the difference of two discs bounded by circles in
{\cal{F}}
. They are called
lunes
.

Grünbaum
[
7
]
conjectured that the number of digons in arrangements of
n
pairwise intersecting pseudo-circles is at most
2n-2
.
This conjecture of Grn̈baum was verified in by Agarwal et al.
[
2
]
for
arrangements of pseudo-circles surrounding a common point. In a recent work by Felsner, Roch, and Scheucher
[
5
]
, Grünbaum’s conjecture was verified for any arrangement of pairwise intersecting pseudo-circles under an additional assumption that the family of pseudo-circles contains three pseudo-circles every two of which create a digon in the arrangement.

In this paper we will be concerned with digons that are lenses in a family of pairwise intersecting circles. We will show that the number of lenses in an arrangement of
n
pairwise intersecting circles
without any further assumption is at most
2n-2
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\cal{F}}
be a family of
n
pairwise intersecting circles
in the plane. Then
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
has at most
2n-2
lenses.
This bound is tight for
n\geqslant 4
.

The simple construction in Figure
1
shows
that the bound in Theorem
1
is best possible for
n\geqslant 4
. There are
5
circles in this construction and
8
lenses. One can generalize the construction for any number of circles by suitably adding more circles to the three smaller circles
in the figure.

Figure 1:
A family of
5
pairwise intersecting circles with
8
lenses.

It is interesting to note that the number of lenses
in arrangements of pairwise intersecting unit circles
is different. It is shown in
[
11
]
that
there are at most
n
lenses in any arrangement of
pairwise intersecting unit circles in the plane
and this bound is best possible.

There has been a lot of research about lenses in arrangements of circles and pseudo-circles that are not necessarily pairwise intersecting. We will not survey here the vast literature about digons in arrangements of circles and pseudo-circles and on related situations where we allow curves to intersect more than twice and only refer the reader to
[
6
]
and the many references therein. The case where circles need not be pairwise intersecting is of completely different nature. We remark that in such a case the best constructions show that it is possible that
n
circles will determine
\Omega(n^{4/3})
many lenses. The best known upper bound is
O(n^{3/2}\log n)
given in
[
9
]
, that is following the footsteps of
[
12
]
. The case of unit circles is of particular interest because of its relation to the celebrated unit distance problem posed by Paul Erdős
[
4
]
.

Going back to families of pairwise intersecting circles, the number of lunes in these arrangements was studied in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
{\cal{F}}
be a family of
n
pairwise intersecting circles
in the plane. Let
G
be the geometric graph on the
set of centers of the circles in
{\cal{F}}
whose edges
correspond to pairs of discs bounded by circles in

{\cal{F}}
whose difference is a lune in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
.
Then
G
is a bipartite planar embedding. Consequently,

{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
has at most
2n-4
lunes.

Theorem
2
is used in
[
3
]

to derive a linear upper bound (that is not tight) for the number of lenses
in arrangements of pairwise intersecting circles in the plane. Theorem
1
, that we prove here, provides the tight upper bound for the number of lenses in a family of pairwise
intersecting circles in the plane.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
2
,
the proof of Theorem
1
relies too
on studying the corresponding geometric graph
G=G({\cal{F}})
whose vertices are the centers of circles in
{\cal{F}}
and two centers are connected by an edge in
G({\cal{F}})
iff the corresponding circles in
{\cal{F}}
create a lens in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
.
We will show that unless we allow two circles in
{\cal{F}}

to touch, then
G
does not contain a pair of
avoiding
edges.
Two straight line segments (or edges in a geometric graph
G
) are called
avoiding

if they are opposite edges in a convex quadrilateral. Handling the case of
{\cal{F}}
having pairs of touching circles
will require only a bit more effort. This is because if we allow touching circles in
{\cal{F}}
, the geometric graph
G({\cal{F}})
may contain pairs of avoiding edges. Luckily, we will be able to compensate for this.

The important property of geometric graphs that do not contain pair of avoiding edges is given in
[
8
]
and
[
13
]
(see also
[
10
]
for
a different
and shorter proof of the same result, based on graph drawing).

Theorem 3

(
[
8
,
13
]
)
.

Let
G
be a geometric on
n
vertices. If
G

does not have a pair of avoiding edges, then it has at most
2n-2
edges. This bound is tight for
n\geqslant 4
.

In order to prove Theorem
1
and in order to allow also touching circles in
{\cal{F}}
, we define the geometric graph
G=G({\cal{F}})

on the set of centers of the pairwise intersecting circles in
{\cal{F}}
in the following way. The edges of

{\cal{F}}

{\cal{F}}

of independent interest and is also an intermediate step before proving Theorem

.

.

The geometric graph

does not contain pairs of avoiding edge unless
there are four circles

and

in

passing through a common point at which

touch each other and also

and
C_{4}

touch each other. Only in such a case it is possible to have avoiding edges in

. The avoiding edges in such a case can only be a pair of two opposite red edges in the quadrilateral whose vertices are the centers of such four circles

, and

We assume without loss of generality that
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
, and
A_{4}
is the clockwise cyclic order of
these points as vertices of the convex quadrilateral
A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}A_{4}
(see Figure
8
).
For
i=1,2,3,4
denote by
C_{i}
the circle
in
{\cal{F}}
centered at
A_{i}
. By Theorem
4
,
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
are connected by a blue edge in
G({\cal{F}})
and so are
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
. This corresponds to that
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
must touch, at a point that we denote by
M
, and so are
C_{2}
and
C_{4}
.

A_{1}A_{3}

We remove either the red edge
e
, or the red edge
f

from
G({\cal{F}})
and charge the avoiding pair
e
and
f
to the
blue edge
A_{1}A_{3}
. We claim that the blue edge

A_{1}A_{3}
cannot be charged for another red edge in this way.
This is because once we fix
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
and therefore
also
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
, then we determine also the touching point

M
of
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
. We claim that
the edges
e
and
f
are determined as well. Indeed,
e
is the edge that correspond to the unique lens
L
supported by
C_{1}

such that
M
is a vertex of
L
in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
and
A_{1},A_{2},M
is the clockwise order of these three points, where
A_{2}
is the center of the other circle in
{\cal{F}}
supporting
L
(see Figure
10
). Any other such lens
L^{\prime}
would overlap with
L
, which is impossible.

By a symmetric argument we show that the edge
f
is determined by the blue edge
A_{1}A_{3}
. We conclude from this that the blue edge
A_{1}A_{3}
in
G({\cal{F}})
can be charged to at most one pair of avoiding edges in
G({\cal{F}})
.

After repeating this procedure for every remaining pair of
avoiding edges we are left with a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G({\cal{F}})

in which no two edges are avoiding. The number of edges, red and blue, in
G^{\prime}

is greater than or equal to the number of red edges in
G
.
We apply Theorem
3
to
G^{\prime}
,
after possibly perturbing the vertices of
G^{\prime}
, as we did already before, and conclude that
G^{\prime}
has at most
2n-2

edges.
Therefore, there are at most
2n-2
red edges in
G({\cal{F}})
. Consequently, there are at most
2n-2
lenses in
{\cal{A}}({\cal{F}})
