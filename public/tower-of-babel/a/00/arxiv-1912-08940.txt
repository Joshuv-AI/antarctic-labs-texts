Bootstrapping Massive Quantum Field Theories
Denis Karateev, Simon Kuhn, and João Penedones

Abstract
We propose a new non-perturbative method for studying UV complete unitary quantum field theories (QFTs) with a mass gap in general number of spacetime dimensions. The method relies on unitarity formulated as positive semi-definiteness of the matrix of inner products between asymptotic states ( in and out ) and states created by the action of local operators on the vacuum. The corresponding matrix elements involve scattering amplitudes, form factors and spectral densities of local operators. We test this method in two-dimensional QFTs by setting up a linear optimization problem that gives a lower bound on the central charge of the UV CFT associated to a QFT with a given mass spectrum of stable particles (and couplings between them). Some of our numerical bounds are saturated by known form factors in integrable theories like the sine-Gordon, E 8 E_{8} and O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) models.

Introduction

One can define a quantum field theory (QFT) non-perturbatively as a renormalization group (RG) flow from the UV to the IR fixed point. The fixed points are assumed to have conformal invariance and are described by conformal field theories (CFTs). In this work, we will focus on massive QFTs,
i.e.
the IR CFT is trivial.
To specify a particular QFT it is sufficient to provide the UV CFT and the relevant deformation triggering the RG flow. We would like to determine IR observables like the mass spectrum and scattering amplitudes from the UV data.
However, generically, the RG flow is strongly coupled and it is not possible to compute IR observables using perturbation theory around the UV CFT.
In these cases, one has to resort to numerical methods (like lattice field theory, Hamiltonian truncation, tensor networks, etc) that require a UV cutoff and a costly extrapolation to the continuum limit.
This calls for modern non-perturbative bootstrap methods that can constrain the space of QFTs directly in the continuum.
Unfortunately, the present bootstrap methods study the UV and the IR separately.
Namely, one can use the conformal bootstrap

2.3
Spectral density

In this section we will discuss two important instances of two-point correlation functions, namely the two-point Wightman and time-ordered correlation functions. We will define the notion of spectral density and show how both types of two-point functions can be rewritten in terms of the spectral density.

3
Unitarity as positive semidefiniteness

Positivity constraint

Plugging all these expression into (

(165)

Consequences of the positivity constraint

Let us start with the minor associated to removing the third row and column. The Sylvester’s criterion leads to

(168)

2\pi\rho(s)\left(1-|\mathcal{S}_{0}(s)|^{2}\right)-2w^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{2}(s)|^{2}+w^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{*2}(s)\mathcal{S}_{0}(s)+w^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{2}(s)\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}(s)\geq 0.

In 2d instead of the Mandelstam variable

In case of identical particles

(203)

When

Partial amplitudes

The 2d integrable models possess an infinite number of conserved charges which allow for factorization of any scattering amplitude into a product of
2\rightarrow 2
scattering amplitudes
\mathcal{S}(s)
. The consistency of this factorization leads to the Yang–Baxter factorization equations on
2\rightarrow 2
scattering amplitudes
\mathcal{S}(s)
. Instead of
\mathcal{S}(s)
it is convenient to work with partial amplitudes
\hat{\mathcal{S}}(s)
which differ by a simple normalization, see section

. The unitarity and crossing conditions then read

\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{S}}(-\theta)=1,\qquad\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathcal{S}}(i\pi-\theta).

(204)

The Yang–Baxter equations together with unitarity and crossing (
204
) allow to obtain exact analytic expressions for partial amplitudes up to a CDD ambiguity
Castillejo:1955ed
. The latter states that given the solution to all the above constraints, one can obtain another solution by multiplying it with any number of CDD factors (and their inverses)
25
25

25

An inverse of the CDD factor (
205
) introduces zeros in the amplitude.
defined as

t_{\alpha}(\theta)\equiv\frac{\tanh\frac{\theta+i\pi\alpha}{2}}{\tanh\frac{\theta-i\pi\alpha}{2}}=\frac{\sinh\theta+i\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\sinh\theta-i\sin(\pi\alpha)}.

(205)

Here
\alpha\in(0,1)

26

26

\alpha
. For a discussion see page 12 of
Paulos:2016but

Notice the factors of
\pi
in (

205

and thus encodes the contribution of a given asymptotic state to the amplitude. The correct choice of the CDD factors is usually postulated and then gets checked in perturbation theory for some range of parameters in the model when it is applicable.

Form factors

In 2d the form factors satisfy the following equations

\mathcal{F}_{2}(\theta)=\mathcal{F}_{2}(-\theta)\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\theta),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{2}(i\pi-\theta)=\mathcal{F}_{2}(i\pi+\theta).

(206)

The former is the familiar Watson’s equation and the latter encodes crossing symmetry. Given the analytic expression for the partial amplitude, the equations (
206

. The solution reads as

F(\theta)=R(\theta)F_{min}(\theta),

(207)

where
R(\theta)
is an arbitrary rational function of
\cosh(\theta)
since
\cosh(\theta)
automatically satisfies the second condition in (

R(\theta)=\frac{K_{\alpha_{1}}(\theta)K_{\alpha_{2}}(\theta)\ldots}{K_{\beta_{1}}(\theta)K_{\beta_{2}}(\theta)\ldots}=(A+B\cosh\theta+C\cosh^{2}\theta\ldots)K_{\alpha_{1}}(\theta)K_{\alpha_{2}}(\theta)\ldots,

(208)

where we have defined

K_{\alpha}(\theta)\equiv-\frac{\cos^{2}(\pi\alpha/2)}{\sinh\frac{\theta-i\pi\alpha}{2}\sinh\frac{\theta+i\pi\alpha}{2}}=\frac{2\cos^{2}(\pi\alpha/2)}{\cos(\pi\alpha)-\cosh\theta}.

(209)

In (

) the parameters
\alpha_{i}
define the positions of poles and parameters
\beta_{i}
(or equivalently
A
,
B
,
C

209

K_{\alpha}(i\pi)=1
.
The “minimal” form factor

in (

in 2d due to (

\ln F_{min}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\left(\coth\left(\frac{z-\theta}{2}\right)-\coth\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)\right)\ln\hat{\mathcal{S}}.

(210)

The functions (

205
) for the form factors. The choice (
208

Finally the form factor (

4.1
sine-Gordon model

The quantum sine-Gordon model is defined as the renormalization group (RG) flow triggered by the deformation of the free scalar UV CFT by the vertex operator

V_{\beta}(x)\equiv\;:e^{i\beta\phi}:\,,\quad\Delta_{V_{\beta}}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\pi},

(212)

in the following way

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{SG}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}+\frac{m_{0}^{2}}{2\,\beta^{2}}\,\big(V_{\beta}(x)+V_{\beta}^{*}(x)\big).

(213)

Here
\phi(x)
is the real scalar field,
\Delta_{V_{\beta}}
is the UV scaling dimension of the vertex operator
28
28

28

The scaling dimension of the vertex operator can be straightforwardly deduced from the Euclidean two point function computed in the free massless theory (which posses conformal invariance for
m=0

\langle 0|V_{\beta}(x_{E})V^{*}_{\beta}(0)|0\rangle\sim\exp\left(\beta^{2}\,\langle 0|\phi(x_{E})\phi(0)|0\rangle\right)\sim\left(x_{E}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\pi}}.

In the second step we have used the propagator
\langle 0|\phi(x_{E})\phi(0)|0\rangle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log x_{E}^{2}
.

,
m_{0}
is a mass-like parameter and
\beta
is a real coupling constant.
The sine-Gordon model possesses several remarkable properties. First, the model is dual to the Thirring model
Coleman:1974bu
.
29
29

29

See also chapter 6 of
Coleman:1985rnk
.

Second, it possesses the
O(2)
topological symmetry
Zamolodchikov:1978xm
and thus can also be regarded as the
O(2)

\sigma

The mass spectrum of the sine-Gordon model was first found with semi-classical methods
Korepin:1975vd
;
Dashen:1975hd
and later argued to be exact
Schroer:1976if
;
Nussinov:1975kp

m_{n}=2m\sin\frac{n\gamma}{16},\quad\gamma\equiv\frac{\beta^{2}}{1-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8\pi}},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{8\pi}{\gamma}\right\rfloor.

(214)

Here

denotes the greatest integer not larger than
x

The breathers exist only in the following range of parameters

\gamma\in[0,8\pi]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\beta^{2}\in[0,\,4\pi].

(215)

It is interesting to study the sine-Gordon model in the regime when at least two breathers exist. Then one can define a ratio of masses for the first two lightest breathers

R\equiv\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}=2\cos\frac{\gamma}{16}.

(216)

The range of parameters which allow for this is

\gamma\in[0,4\pi]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\beta^{2}\in[0,8\pi/3]\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad R\in[2,\sqrt{2}].

(217)

The 2d Ising model is a 2d conformal field theory with a

4.3
Non-linear sigma model

The
O(N)
non-linear sigma model (NLSM) with
N\geq 3
is defined in the UV via the Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}_{NLSM}=\frac{1}{2g_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{\mu}n_{i})^{2},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}^{2}=1,

(242)

where
n_{i}(x)
is a
O(N)
vector of real scalar fields and
g_{0}
is a dimensionless coupling.
This model can be seen as a marginally relevant deformation of a theory of
N-1
free massless scalar fields.
32
32

32

One way to see this is to solve the constraint on the scalar fields in (
242
), and write

\mathcal{L}_{O(N)}=\frac{1}{2g_{0}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\partial_{\mu}n_{i})^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x)\right),\quad\mathcal{O}(x)\equiv\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N-1}n_{i}n_{j}(\partial_{\mu}n_{i})(\partial^{\mu}n_{j})}{1-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}n_{k}^{2}}.

(243)

The operator
\mathcal{O}(x)
is marginal since its UV scaling dimension is
\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}=2
.

The NLSM is asymptotically free in the UV and is gapped in the IR. Away from the UV fixed point, its spectrum consists of a single asymptotic state of mass
m
transforming in the vector representation of the
O(N)
group.

The scattering of asymptotic states is described according to (
184

\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
or equivalently by
\hat{S}_{\bullet}
,
\hat{S}_{\textbf{S}}
and
\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}
according to (

The analytic expressions for
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
in the NLSM were found in
Zamolodchikov:1978xm
, they read as

\sigma_{1}(\theta)=-\frac{i\lambda}{i\pi-\theta}\sigma_{2}(\theta),\quad\sigma_{3}(\theta)=-\frac{i\lambda}{\theta}\sigma_{2}(\theta),\quad\lambda\equiv\frac{2\pi}{N-2},

(244)

where
\sigma_{2}(\theta)
is given by the “plus” part of (3.17) and (3.18) in

The results of

\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(\theta)=\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x}\frac{\exp(-x\lambda/\pi)-1}{1+\exp(x)}\sinh\frac{x\theta}{i\pi}\right)

(245)

together with

\hat{S}_{\bullet}(\theta)=-\frac{\pi-i\theta}{\pi+i\theta}\times\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(\theta),\quad\hat{S}_{\textbf{S}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta-i\lambda}{\theta+i\lambda}\times\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(\theta).

(246)

To characterize the strength of the interaction in the NLSM one can evaluate the partial amplitudes at the crossing symmetric point
s=2
(which corresponds to

\hat{S}_{\bullet}(i\pi/2)=-3\,\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(i\pi/2),\quad\hat{S}_{\textbf{S}}(i\pi/2)=\frac{N-6}{N+2}\,\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(i\pi/2),

(247)

\hat{S}_{\textbf{A}}(i\pi/2)=\frac{N+2}{N-2}\times\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{N-2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{N-2}\right)}\right)^{2}.

(248)

We notice that crossing equations have the simplest form for

\sigma_{1}(i\pi/2)=\sigma_{3}(i\pi/2)

The form factor associated to the antisymmetric partial amplitude was computed in

The form factor associated to the scalar operator was reported in

\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}(\theta)=A\times\frac{\sinh\theta}{i\pi-\theta}\times\mathcal{F}_{\textbf{A}}(\theta),

(250)

5
Numerical bootstrap in 2d

We are now in position to formulate the numerical bootstrap problem which allows to obtain the partial amplitude, the form factor and the spectral density of a UV complete massive unitary QFT. We will focus on two dimensions in this section for two reasons: to avoid technical complications due to spin in higher dimensions and to be able to compare our results with analytic results for 2d integrable models discussed in section
4
.

Given a QFT which has at least one asymptotic state with a non-zero mass, we can consider the following three functions

\hat{S}(s),\quad\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\Theta}(s),\quad\rho_{\Theta}(s),

(254)

which are the partial amplitude for 2 to 2 scattering of the lightest particle, the form factor and the spectral density of the trace of the stress-tensor respectively. The form factor of the trace of the stress-tensor is normalized according to (
139
). The spectral density of the trace of the stress-tensor is related to the UV central charge according to (
147
).

We can write the most general ansatz for the functions (
254
) with real coefficients entering linearly. In case of the
\hat{S}(s)
function, these coefficients are further restricted to satisfy crossing. We can then solve the following problem: determine the parameters of the ansatze leading to the minimal possible UV central charge such that the functions (
254
) obey the unitarity condition (
181
).

In section
5.1
we will provide the details of the numerical setup. In section
5.2
we will present the numerical results. We will consider three different cases: partial amplitude with a single pole, partial amplitude with three poles and a partial amplitude with no poles but with a global
O(N)
symmetry. We will see that these cases will reproduce numerically the known results in the sine-Gordon,
E_{8}
and
O(N)
integrable models respectively.

5.1
Setting up the optimization problem

It is convenient to introduce the
{\mathfrak{r}}
variable defined as

{\mathfrak{r}}(s;s_{0})\equiv\frac{\sqrt{4-s_{0}}-\sqrt{4-s}}{\sqrt{4-s_{0}}+\sqrt{4-s}}.

(255)

It maps an
s
complex plane with one branch cut
s\in[4,\infty)
into the unit disc (with the cut mapped to the boundary). The point
s_{0}<4
is a free parameter which is mapped to the center of the disc.
36
36

36

The physical domain is defined via
s+i\epsilon
with
\epsilon>0
. We can thus rotate the cuts using the identity

\sqrt{4-s}=-i\,\sqrt{s-4}.

Another useful variable is
\phi(s_{0})
defined as

e^{i\phi(s_{0})}\equiv{\mathfrak{r}}(s;s_{0})\quad\Rightarrow\quad s=s_{0}+\frac{8-2s_{0}}{1+\cos\phi(s_{0})}.

(256)

In what follows we will often use the
\phi
variable defined as

\phi\equiv\phi(0)\quad\Rightarrow\quad s=\frac{8}{1+\cos\phi},

(257)

which maps a ray into an interval, more precisely

s\in[4,\infty)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\phi\in[0,\pi].

(258)

Let us discuss now the situation when our QFT has
k
asymptotic states below the two-particle threshold

m_{1}=1,\;m_{2},\;\ldots,\;m_{k}.

(259)

According to the discussion of section
2.5.1
these asymptotic states will appear as simple poles in the interacting part of the amplitude and the form factor. We can then write the following ansatze
Paulos:2017fhb
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(s)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{g_{i}^{2}}{s-m_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{max}}a_{n}\times{\mathfrak{r}}(s;2)^{n}+(s\leftrightarrow 4-s),

(260)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\Theta}(s)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{s-m_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{max}}b_{n}\times{\mathfrak{r}}(s;0)^{n},

(261)

\displaystyle\rho_{\Theta}(s)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=0}^{1}c_{n}\times\cos(n\phi)-2\sum_{n=1}^{N_{max}}d_{n}\times\sin(n\phi),

(262)

where we have defined for convenience

\lambda_{i}\equiv g_{i}\mathcal{F}_{1,i}.

(263)

These ansatze depend on the set of real parameters
a_{n}
,
b_{n}
,
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
which enter linearly. The form factor of the trace of the stress-tensor obeys the normalization (
139
). This leads to the linear constraint for the unknown coefficient

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\,m_{i}^{-2}+b_{0}=-2.

(264)

Taking it into account we can write the final ansatz for the form factor as

\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\Theta}(s)=-2-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\times\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{s-m_{i}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{max}}b_{n}\times{\mathfrak{r}}(s;0)^{n}.

(265)

Unitarity constraints

The unitarity constraint is given by (
182
). It should be obeyed for any value of
s\in[4m_{1}^{2},+\infty)
. To implement this requirement in practice we discretize
s
and choose a large set of sample values. All the plots are made with 200 sample points distributed on the Chebyshev grid
\phi\in[0,\pi]
. The entries of the
3\times 3
matrix (
182
) are complex. we can rewrite however the semipositive definite condition (
182
) in terms of
6\times 6
matrices with purely real coefficients by defining

R(s)\equiv\text{Re}B(s),\quad I(s)\equiv\text{Im}B(s),\quad R^{T}=R,\quad I^{T}=-I.

(266)

The semipositive definite constraint reads as

z^{\dagger}B(s)z\geq 0,

(267)

where
z
are some complex 3 dimensional vectors. Due to (
266
) this is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}R(s)&-I(s)\\
I(s)&R(s)\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(268)

Central charge bound

Let us consider now the expression of the UV central charge in terms of the spectral density (
147
). Generalizing it to the case of multiple asymptotic states we can write

c_{UV}=12\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{-4}\,|\mathcal{F}_{1,i}^{\Theta}|^{2}+\int_{4m_{1}^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,s^{-2}\rho_{\Theta}(s)\right).

(269)

There is in principle no bound on how big the spectral density
\rho_{\Theta}(s)
can be. However there is certainly at least a trivial lower bound
\rho_{\Theta}(s)\geq 0
due to (
168
) which implies
c_{UV}\geq 0
.

The expression (
269
) has the unknown constants
\mathcal{F}_{1,i}
entering in a quadratic way. Thus, we cannot directly apply methods of linear programming to minimize (
269
). We can however use a simple trick to rewrite (
269
) in a linear way. Consider the following inequality

c_{UV}\leq c_{UV}^{\text{bound}},\quad c_{UV}^{\text{bound}}\equiv 12\pi\,\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{-4}\,u_{i}+\int_{4m_{1}^{2}}^{\infty}ds\;s^{-2}\rho_{\Theta}(s)\right),

(270)

where we have introduced new non-negative real parameters
u_{i}
which obey the following constraints

\forall i:\quad 0\leq|\mathcal{F}_{1,i}|^{2}\leq u_{i}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 0\leq|\lambda_{i}|^{2}\leq g_{i}^{2}u_{i}.

(271)

The latter inequality is equivalent to

\forall i:\quad\begin{pmatrix}g_{i}^{2}&\lambda_{i}^{*}\\
\lambda_{i}&u_{i}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(272)

Now instead of minimizing
c_{UV}
we can minimize
c_{UV}^{\text{bound}}
defined in (
270
) given that the condition (
272
) is satisfied. At the minimum we will simply get
u_{i}=|\mathcal{F}_{1,i}|^{2}
and
c_{UV}=c_{UV}^{\text{bound}}
.

Central charge minimization

We are finally ready to formulate the numerical bootstrap problem: given a set of asymptotic states and their masses (
259
), determine the linear coefficients

g_{i}^{2},\;\lambda_{i},\;u_{i},\;a_{n},\;b_{n},\;c_{n},\;d_{n},\;

in the ansazte (
260
) - (
262
) and (
270
) such that the semipositive conditions (
182
), (
272
) are satisfied and the central charge
c_{UV}^{\text{bound}}
in (
270
) has the minimal possible value. Sometimes we will also be fixing the values of
g_{i}^{2}
in order to single out known integrable models. To perform the numerics we use the semipositive program solver SDPB
Simmons-Duffin:2015qma
;
Landry:2019qug
.

in three different cases. First, in the presence of a single pole. We find a special point on the central charge bound which corresponds to the sine-Gordon model. We will recover its partial amplitude and the two particle form factor. We will then investigate the dependence of the central charge on the parameter of the sine-Gordon model. Second, in the presence of three poles. Injecting the values of the masses and the residue of the lightest asymptotic state in the
E_{8}

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have extended the S-matrix bootstrap program to include states created by local operators. This gives rise to a bootstrap setup that mixes scattering amplitudes, form factors and spectral densities of local operators.
The latter allows to extract direct information about the UV fixed point which was not possible so far in the pure S-matrix bootstrap approach.

We have established the groundwork for future explorations and limited ourselves to testing the approach in two dimensional QFTs. Our main result is the derivation of a lower bound for the central charge
c
of the UV CFT that can flow to a massive phase with a given particle spectrum (and interactions). For example, imposing
O(N)
global symmetry, in the presence of a single stable particle transforming in the vector representation, we found the universal lower bound
c\geq c_{min}(N)\approx 0.6+0.3N
, see figure
12
(a).

We should however be careful with the meaning of “lower bound”. As in all the recent S-matrix bootstrap works
Paulos:2017fhb
, the “lower bound” decreases when increasing the number of parameters in the ansatz,
i.e.

N_{max}
in equations (
260
) - (
262
).
Strictly speaking our result is an upper bound for the lower bound.
In all the plots presented in this paper we have taken the value of
N_{max}
sufficiently large. However, we have not performed a careful convergence analysis. In order to be rigorous, it is important to generalize the functional method of
Cordova:2019lot
to our setup and obtain rigorous lower bounds on the central charge.

Another direction worth further exploration, is the inclusion of several states created by different local operators. It seems natural to consider the full set of relevant operators of a given CFT. For example, in the 2d Ising model, it would be interesting to consider both
\sigma
and
\epsilon
. This setup would include form factors for both operators and a
2\times 2
matrix of Wightman two-point functions.

In two spacetime dimensions, it would be interesting to further explore the connection with integrable models. In our setup, we have observed that the numerical optimization problem tends to saturate the conditions (
174
) - (
176
), which include Watson’s equation and absence of particle production. Therefore, similarly to the pure S-matrix bootstrap, we found that the optimal solutions often correspond to integrable theories.
In this work, we encountered amplitudes and form factors of
the sine-Gordon,
E_{8}
and
O(N)
models.

In the presence of continuous global symmetries, it is natural to study states created by the conserved currents. Notice that form factors of conserved currents also have a natural normalization (at
s=0
) following from the conserved charges.
This should have interesting applications both in
d=2
and in higher dimensions.
In
d=2
, it seems clear that a detailed study of the
O(N)
model with our approach will benefit from the inclusion of states created by the non-abelian currents. Moreover, it would be useful to obtain the 3D plot of the central charge lower bound above the allowed region in figure
10
.

Let us conclude by discussing our new bootstrap method in higher dimensions. In
d\geq 3
one has to consider the Wightman two-point function of the full stress-tensor
T^{\mu\nu}
and not only its trace
\Theta=T_{\mu}^{\mu}
. Such two-point function can be decomposed into two spectral densities

\rho_{\Theta}(s),\quad\rho_{2}(s),

(286)

where the first spectral density represents the trace of the stress-tensor exactly as in
d=2
and the second spectral density is the new object special to
d\geq 3
. In all dimensions we have the following asymptotic behavior at large energies
39
39

39

This follows from
\Theta(x)\propto g\,\mathcal{O}(x)
and (
79
).

\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\rho_{\Theta}(s)\propto g^{2}s^{\Delta_{r}-\frac{d}{2}}\,,

(287)

corresponding to the relevant deformation
g\int d^{d}x\,\mathcal{O}(x)
of the UV CFT by an operator of dimension
\Delta_{r}<d
and
g
is a dimensionful coupling constant with the mass dimension
[g]=d-\Delta_{r}
.
The value of the central charge in
d=2
is hidden inside
\rho_{\Theta}
and can be extracted using (
147
). We reproduce it here for convenience

c_{UV}=(2\pi)^{2}\times\frac{3}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{\rho_{\Theta}(s)}{s^{2}}.

(288)

In
d\geq 3
there is no known analogous integral expression and the value of the central charge is hidden instead in the asymptotics of the second spectral density in (
286

\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\rho_{2}(s)=const\times C_{T}^{UV}s^{d/2}.

(289)

Here
const
is a numerical factor which depends on the precise definition of
\rho_{2}
and is irrelevant for the present discussion. Reiterating, even though the spectral densities (
286

d=2
and in
d\geq 3
. Interestingly enough, the integral formula (
129
) captures both (
288

289
) in a uniform way. For more details see
Karateev:2020axc
.

Our numerical bootstrap approach can easily bound integrals like (
288
) but it cannot bound coefficients in the asymptotic behaviour of spectral densities like in (
289
). For this reason, we cannot use our bootstrap method to put non-trivial bounds on the central charge
C_{T}^{UV}
in
d\geq 3
.
What we can do instead in higher dimensions is to put lower bounds on the following dimensionless quantity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{d/2+1}}\,\rho_{\Theta}(s),

(290)

which is a simple generalization of (
288
) to higher dimension. We stress however that contrary to (
288
) this quantity
is not directly related to a property of the UV CFT and is thus less interesting than (
288
), see
Cappelli:1990yc
for further discussion. Focusing to
d=4
there is a more interesting quantity we can bound which is the
a
-anomaly. This requires however some modifications in the present formalism. We plan to address this question in the near future.

Fourier transformation

The Fourier transform
\hat{f}(p)

\hat{f}(p)=\int d^{d}x\,e^{-ix\cdot p}f(x),\quad f(x)=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,e^{ix\cdot p}\hat{f}(p).

(291)

The Dirac

(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{(d)}(p)=\int d^{d}x\,e^{ip\cdot x}.

(292)

Spherical coordinates

We will need to evaluate
n
-dimensional integrals in Euclidean signature. It is best done in spherical coordinates which we introduce here. The
n
-dimensional spherical coordinates consist of the radius
r
and a set of
n-1
angles with the following ranges

\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-2}\in[0,\pi],\quad\theta_{n-1}\in[0,2\pi].

(293)

They are related to the Cartesian coordinates as

\displaystyle x^{1}

\displaystyle=r\cos\theta_{1},

(294)

\displaystyle x^{2}

\displaystyle=r\cos\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1},

\displaystyle x^{3}

\displaystyle=r\cos\theta_{3}\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1},

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle x^{n-1}

\displaystyle=r\cos\theta_{n-1}\sin\theta_{n-2}\ldots\sin\theta_{1},

\displaystyle x^{n}

\displaystyle=r\sin\theta_{n-1}\sin\theta_{n-2}\ldots\sin\theta_{1}.

The Jacobian
J
of the variable change from Cartesian to spherical coordinates reads as

J(r;\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-2})=r^{n-1}\sin^{n-2}\theta_{1}\sin^{n-1}\theta_{2}\ldots\sin^{2}\theta_{n-3}\sin\theta_{n-2}.

(295)

The infinitesimal spherical angle in
n

d\Omega_{n}=J(1;\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-2})\times d\theta_{1}\ldots d\theta_{n-1}.

(296)

It is then straightforward to evaluate the spherical angle
\Omega_{n}

40

\Omega_{n}=\frac{n\,\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}.

(297)

The

\delta^{(n-1)}(\Omega)=\frac{\delta(\theta_{1})\ldots\delta(\theta_{n-2})}{J(1;\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-2})}\times\delta(\theta_{n-1}).

(298)

Spherical harmonics

Any normalizable function on the sphere

If the function is invariant under

\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\nu^{k}_{j}}\times C_{j}^{k}(x)C_{j}^{k}(y)=(1-x^{2})^{1/2-k}\times\delta(x-y).

(302)

Appendix B
Källén-Lehmann representation in Euclidean signature

In Euclidean signature there is no notion of Wightman correlation functions, only time-ordered correlators are well defined. Here we obtain the Källén-Lehmann representation of a Euclidean two-point function by applying the Wick rotation to (
72

Consider the Feynman propagator (
74

q^{0}=\pm\sqrt{\vec{q}\,^{2}+\mu^{2}}\mp i\epsilon.

(312)

The integration goes along the real

i\epsilon

+\pi/2
. This is done in such a way that we do not cross the poles. It is represented by the change of variables

x^{0}_{E}=ix^{0},\qquad q^{0}_{E}=iq^{0},

(313)

which is known as the Wick rotation.
43
43

43

More precisely the rotation of the energy
p^{0}
to purely imaginary values is done by
p^{0}\rightarrow p^{0}e^{i\phi}\rightarrow ip^{0}\equiv p^{0}_{E}
, where the angle
\phi

(p\cdot x)=p^{0}x_{0}+\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}

x_{0}\rightarrow x_{0}e^{-i\phi}\rightarrow-ix_{0}\equiv x_{E0}=x_{E}^{0}
.
The subscript

\Delta_{F}(x;\mu^{2})=i\Delta_{E}(x_{E};\mu^{2}),\quad\Delta_{E}(x_{E};\mu^{2})\equiv\int\frac{d^{d}q_{E}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;e^{iq_{E}\cdot x_{E}}\;\frac{1}{q_{E}^{2}+\mu^{2}},

q_{E}\cdot x_{E}=q_{E}^{0}x_{E}^{0}+\vec{q}_{E}\cdot\vec{x}_{E},\quad q_{E}^{2}=(q_{E}^{0})^{2}+\vec{q}_{E}^{\;2}.

(315)

Notice the absence of

Appendix C
Free scalar theory

Let us consider the free field theory with a single real scalar field
\phi(x)
. It is defined via the Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}_{\text{free}}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial\phi(x)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi(x)^{2}.

(317)

From this Lagrangian density the Klein-Gordon equation of motion follows

\left(-\partial^{2}+m^{2}\right)\phi(x)=0.

(318)

It has the following general solution

\phi(x)=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\theta(p^{0})(2\pi)\delta(p^{2}+m^{2})\left(a(p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}+b^{\dagger}(p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\right),

where

are some operator valued functions of the

called the annihilation and creation operators respectively. The reality condition
\phi^{\dagger}(x)=\phi(x)
implies

. The operators
a

are required to satisfy the standard commutation relations

[a(p),a(p^{\prime})]=0,\quad[a(p),a^{\dagger}(p^{\prime})]=2p^{0}\times(2\pi)^{d-1}\delta^{(d-1)}(\vec{p}\,{}^{\prime}-\vec{p}).

(320)

Acting on the vacuum they create
n

|\mathbf{n}\rangle=a^{\dagger}(k_{1})\ldots a^{\dagger}(k_{n})|0\rangle,\quad a(k)|0\rangle=0.

(321)

Due to (
320

n
-particles states (
321

10
) and (
17
) for
n=1
and
n=2
respectively. Free theory provides an explicit construction of the Hilbert space discussed in the beginning of section

.

The stress-tensor for the free real scalar field reads as

T^{\mu\nu}(x)=\;:(\partial^{\mu}\phi)(\partial^{\nu}\phi)-\frac{1}{2}\,\eta^{\mu\nu}\left((\partial\phi)^{2}+m^{2}\phi^{2}\right):.

(322)

The operators are enclosed between two symbols “:” denoting normal-ordering. The expression for the trace of the stress-tensor follows from (
322

\Theta(x)\equiv\eta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}(x)=\;:\left(1-\frac{d}{2}\right)(\partial\phi)^{2}-\frac{d}{2}\,m^{2}\phi^{2}:.

(323)

In the remainder of this appendix let us focus on the the case of

\Theta(x)=-m^{2}:\phi(x)^{2}:.

(324)

Let us compute the one and two particle form factors for the operator (

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\Theta}=-m^{2}\,\langle 0|a_{p_{1}}:\phi(0)^{2}:|0\rangle=0.

(325)
