Optimal Solutions of Well-Posed Linear Systems via Low-Precision Right-Preconditioned GMRES with Forward and Backward Stabilization
Xiangmin Jiao

Abstract
In scientific applications, linear systems are typically well-posed, and yet the coefficient matrices may be nearly singular in that the condition number κ ⁡ ( 𝑨 ) \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) may be close to 1 / ε w 1/\varepsilon_{w} , where ε w \varepsilon_{w} denotes the unit roundoff of the working precision. Accurate and efficient solutions to such systems pose daunting challenges. It is well known that iterative refinement (IR) can make the forward error independent of κ ⁡ ( 𝑨 ) \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) if κ ⁡ ( 𝑨 ) \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) is sufficiently smaller than 1 / ε w 1/\varepsilon_{w} and the residual is computed in higher precision. Recently, Carson and Higham [ SISC , 39(6), 2017] proposed a variant of IR called GMRES-IR, which replaced the triangular solves in IR with left-preconditioned GMRES using the LU factorization of 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} as the preconditioner. GMRES-IR relaxed the requirement on κ ⁡ ( 𝑨 ) \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) in IR, but it requires triangular solves to be evaluated in higher precision, complicating its application to large-scale sparse systems. We propose a new iterative method, called Forward-and-Backward Stabilized Minimal Residual or FBSMR , by conceptually hybridizing right-preconditioned GMRES (RP-GMRES) with quasi-minimization. We develop FBSMR based on a new theoretical framework of essential-forward-and-backward stability ( EFBS ), which extends the backward error analysis to consider the intrinsic condition number of a well-posed problem. We stabilize the forward and backward errors in RP-GMRES to achieve EFBS by evaluating a small portion of the algorithm in higher precision while evaluating the preconditioner in lower precision. FBSMR can achieve optimal accuracy in terms of both forward and backward errors for well-posed problems with unpolluted matrices, independently of κ ⁡ ( 𝑨 ) \kappa(\boldsymbol{A}) . With low-precision preconditioning, FBSMR can reduce the computational, memory, and energy requirements over direct methods with or without IR. FBSMR can also leverage parallelization-friendly classical Gram-Schmidt in Arnoldi iterations without compromising EFBS. We demonstrate the effectiveness of FBSMR using both random and realistic linear systems.

Our “efficiency” goal is closely related to accuracy; otherwise,
one can always achieve accuracy by simply solving (
1
)
using exact or arbitrary precision throughout. More specifically,
we aim to solve the problem using an iterative method with an approximate-inverse
preconditioner, where the overwhelming amount of the computation is
performed using a lower precision of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{w}})

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt[4]{\varepsilon_{w}})

\varepsilon_{w}

\varepsilon_{w}/\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

in terms of computational cost, storage, and energy. It can also potentially
benefit from the increased ubiquity of half-precision floating-point
systems on computers ranging from laptops to exascale computers

To this end, we propose a method called

Stabilized Minimum Residual

For example, given
\boldsymbol{A}

2
Essential Forward and Backward Stability

As alluded to in the introduction, this work focuses on developing
algorithms that are EFBS for problems that are well-posed and unpolluted.
Since EFBS is stronger than the standard backward stability, we offer
a mathematical justification for its significance for practical applications.
We then make an important connection between the intrinsic condition
number of a linear system with the well-posedness of its underlying
problem, especially when the rounding errors in the coefficient matrices
are strongly or weakly correlated. The notion of EFBS will allow us
to develop a new stability analysis of RP-GMRES and identify the key
components that need to be stabilized in RP-GMRES in later sections.

2.1
Justification of EFBS

We start with a justification of the notion of EFBS for unpolluted
problems, by revisiting one of the most fundamental works in numerical
analysis: In the first rigorous analysis of rounding errors of linear
solvers, von Neumann and Goldstine
[
35
]
(abbreviated
as v.N.–G. below) analyzed the accuracy of solving symmetric positive
definite (SPD) linear systems arising from well-posed (or “mathematically
stable”
[
35
]
) problems. v.N.–G. considered
the perturbations in the input (including modeling errors, noise in
observational data, discretization errors, and rounding errors). They
asserted that “those perturbations will cause a parameter to deviate
from its ideal value, but this deviation takes place only once, and
is then valid with a constant value throughout the entire problem.”
As we understand nowadays, random perturbations in
\boldsymbol{A}

can be as damaging as the rounding errors in a backward-sable algorithm

[
20
,
24
]
, so it is justified to
focus on backward stable algorithms
in general
. However,
the perturbations in
\boldsymbol{A}
are not necessarily “random,”
and the perturbed system may remain self-consistent (or “valid”
in the words of v.N.–G.). We demonstrate it with a specific example
as follows.

Example 1
.

Consider the Poisson equation
\Delta u=f(u)
on
a structured mesh over
\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1]
with Dirichlet boundary
conditions
f(u)=g(u)
over
\partial\Omega
. When solving it using
a finite difference method with centered differences (see e.g.,
[
22
]
),
the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is composed of
-4h^{-2}
in the diagonals
and
h^{-2}
for the off-diagonal nonzero entries. Since both
1

and
4
are powers of
2
, all the nonzero entries would have exactly
the same relative roundoff. This matrix is unpolluted despite the
rounding errors because we can scale both
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}

by
h^{2}
so that
\boldsymbol{A}
is exact. As a result, we only
need to consider the “intrinsic” sensitivity of (
1
)
relative to perturbations in
\boldsymbol{b}
(including the effect
of rounding errors in
h^{2}
as well as the rounding errors in evaluating

f
and
g
). If
f
and
g
are continuous, the well-posedness
of the Poisson equation in infinite dimensions guarantees that the
problem is intrinsically insensitive to small perturbations for the
specific
\boldsymbol{b}
on a sufficiently fine mesh.

This example may be overly simple, but it nevertheless
raises an important point.

Claim 1
.

The rounding errors in the input

\boldsymbol{A}

may
be strongly correlated so that the
perturbed system may remain self-consistent.
Hence,

a numerical algorithm should not assume that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

is
always
the intrinsic condition number for the perturbations
in
\boldsymbol{A}
, so backward stability is not always the correct
goal.

Of course, the preceding claim by no means diminishes the usefulness
of backward-stable algorithms: If the perturbations in
\boldsymbol{A}

are truly random, then “we cannot justifiably criticize an algorithm
for returning an inaccurate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
if
\boldsymbol{A}

is ill-conditioned relatively to the unit roundoff” in the sense
of (
3
)
[
20
, p. 102]
.
However, if a modeler (the person who constructs (
1
))
can ensure
\boldsymbol{A}
is unpolluted in the sense that its rounding
errors are highly correlated as in the above example (also see section
2.3
),
then paraphrasing Golub and Van Loan, “we have every ‘right’ to
pursue the development of a linear equation solver that renders the
exact solution to a nearby problem” in the sense of (
2
)
instead of (
3
), so that the errors
introduced by the algorithm do not dominate those caused by the rounding
errors in the input.

2.2
Intrinsic condition numbers
of well-posed problems and inverse

We now formalize the notion of the intrinsic condition number for
a well-posed problem in a Hadamard notion
[
21
]
.
Although Hadamard only considered initial value problems, it is common
to use a generalization of his notion of well-posedness.

Definition 1
.

A problem
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}

is
Hadamard well-posed
if the solution
\boldsymbol{y}
exists,
is unique, and depends continuously on
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Under the assumption of well-posedness, both (
1
)
and (
4
) are bijections, of which the
inverse problem is
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
, which maps from

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{A}^{H})
to
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{A})
.
Hence, we limit our attention to bijections when defining backward
errors.

Definition 2
.

Given a problem
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}

that is a bijection in a neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}

in
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
so that the inverse
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}):\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}

is well defined, let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})

denote a perturbed well-posed problem with some perturbations to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

and the parameters in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
with a corresponding
inverse
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}^{-1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}})

in the neighborhood of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
.
The (relative)
forward error
is
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|/\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
.
The (
relative
)
backward error
is the forward error of
the inverse problem, i.e.,
\left\|\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\right)-\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\|/\|\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)-\boldsymbol{x}\right\|/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|
.

We will consider the “pollution”
\delta\boldsymbol{f}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}-\boldsymbol{f}

in section
2.3
. For the case of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}=\boldsymbol{f}
,
the backward error in Definition
2

reduces to its standard notion, and the condition number is the amplification
factor between the forward and backward errors.

Definition 3
.

The
(intrinsic) condition number

(
ICN
) of
\boldsymbol{f}
in Definition
2

w.r.t. to the perturbation
\delta\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}

is the supremum of the ratio between the forward and backward errors,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|=\epsilon}\left(\left.\frac{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|}{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\|}\right/\frac{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right).

The problem
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
is
intrinsically
well-conditioned
(
IWC
) for a specific
\boldsymbol{x}
if

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}\leq C
for some constant
C>0
.

In Definition
3
,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}

is the standard condition number in numerical analysis (see e.g.,

[
22
,
33
]
). Mathematically,
the continuity in Definition
1
requires
that
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{x})\leq C

for some constant
C>0
, so Hadamard well-posedness implies

IWC if
\boldsymbol{f}
is a bijection. Note that
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}

would be distorted if
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})

are scaled by arbitrary matrices
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}

so that
\|\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\gg\|\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{y}\|/\|\boldsymbol{y}\|

or vice versa. By assuming well-posedness, our definitions allow such
scalings if they make ill-posed problems well-posed, while prohibiting
scalings that make well-posted problems ill-posed.

When applying Definition
3
to (
1
),
the forward problem is
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
. The inverse
problem is
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
, so the backward error
is

\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b})-\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\|(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b})-\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|.

Note that computing the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{A}
cannot be
the inverse problem since
\boldsymbol{A}
has a high
n^{2}
dimensionality
and hence cannot be determined from
n
values in
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Hence, the ICN w.r.t. the perturbation
\delta\boldsymbol{b}
(assuming

\delta\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{0}
) is

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|=\epsilon}\left(\left.\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right/\frac{\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}\right),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
. The following
theorem gives the formula for computing
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})
,
which can also be used as an alternative definition of
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}

in place of (
6
).

Theorem 1
.

Consider the singular value
decomposition (SVD) of a nonsingular
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{H}

and a vector
\boldsymbol{b}=\|\boldsymbol{b}\|(c_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+c_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}+\cdots+c_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
,
where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
is the
i
th
left singular vector corresponding to singular value
\sigma_{i}
.
Then,

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=1/\sqrt{{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}}|c_{i}\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}}.

(7)

Proof.

Observe that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|=\epsilon}\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|=\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|=1/\sigma_{n}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}=\|\boldsymbol{b}\|\sum_{i}c_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}/\sigma_{i}
,

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=\|\boldsymbol{b}\|\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}
.
Substituting these into (
6
), we obtain
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|/\sqrt{\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}},

which simplifies to (
7
).

Obviously,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\leq\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{n}=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
.
However,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
depends on how
\boldsymbol{b}

is distributed in terms of the singular vectors. With a similar argument,
we obtain the following condition number for its inverse problem.

Corollary 1
.

For the matrix-vector multiplication
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}

with
\boldsymbol{x}=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|(d_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+d_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}+\cdots+d_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
,

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})=1/\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|d_{i}\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{1}|^{2}}
.

However, in the context of (
6
), it is more useful
to express
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}
in terms of the components
in
\boldsymbol{b}
instead of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Theorem 2
.

For the matrix-vector multiplication
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}

with
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
, under the
same conditions as in Theorem
1
,

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})=\sqrt{{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}}|c_{i}\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}}.

(8)

Proof.

By definition,

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|=\epsilon}\left(\left.\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}\right/\frac{\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right).

Hence,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})=\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}}
.

From Theorems
1
and
2
,
we obtain the following result, which can be considered as a
conservation
law of the condition number

\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
.

Theorem 3
.

Given
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}

as in Theorems
1
,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
.

Proof.

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}}/\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}}=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{n}=\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
.

To solidify the understanding of Theorems
1
–
3
,
let us consider two examples.

Example 2
.

If a substantial component of
\boldsymbol{b}

falls within the space corresponding to the smallest singular values,
i.e., there exist
C_{1}\geq 1
and
0<C_{2}
independently of
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

such that
\sum_{\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{n}\leq C_{1}}|c_{i}|^{2}\geq C_{2}
,
and then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}\geq\sum_{\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{n}\leq C_{1}}|c_{i}\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}\geq\sum_{\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{n}\leq C_{1}}|c_{i}/C_{1}|^{2}\geq C_{2}/C_{1}^{2},

and then
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\leq C_{1}/\sqrt{C_{2}}
and

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}\geq\sqrt{C_{2}}/C_{1}\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
.

The “effective well conditioned” problems in
[
10
, Theorem 1]

(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{b}
falls within a subspace corresponding to
small singular values) are special cases of Example
2

in terms of
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\leq C_{1}/\sqrt{C_{2}}
.

The following example analyzes random matrices generated using a generalization
of a technique known as “randsvd” (see e.g.,
[
24
, Section 28.3]

and
[
4
, eq. (4.1)]
).

Example 3
.

Suppose
c_{i}
is uniform (i.e.,
c_{i}=1/\sqrt{n}

and
\boldsymbol{b}\approx\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}/\sqrt{n}
) and
the singular values of
\boldsymbol{A}
are
\sigma_{i}=10^{-\alpha(\frac{i-1}{n-1})^{r}}
.
Then,
\sigma_{1}=1
,
\alpha_{n}=10^{-\alpha}
, and
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})=10^{\alpha}
.
If
r=1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}\sigma_{n}/\sigma_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}10^{-2\alpha(\frac{n-i}{n-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}10^{-2\alpha(\frac{j}{n-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1-10^{-2\alpha(\frac{n}{n-1})}}{1-10^{\frac{2\alpha}{n-1}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{n-1}{2\alpha\ln 10n},

for
1\ll\alpha<\log(|\varepsilon_{w}|)\ll n/2
, where the
approximate equality is due to the Taylor series of
10^{\frac{2\alpha}{n-1}}
.
Hence,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}=\mathcal{O}(\log(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}))

for
r=1
and large
n
. Since
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}

decreases as
r
decreases,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
is
o(\log(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}))

for
r<1
and it converges to
\mathcal{O}(1)
as
r\rightarrow 0
.
If
r>1
,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
is unbounded by a constant
as
n
increases, and
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
converges
to
\mathcal{O}(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A}))
as
r\rightarrow\infty

and
n\rightarrow\infty
. For moderately sized
n
,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\leq\sqrt{n}
,
which is significantly smaller than
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
if
\alpha\gg\log_{10}\sqrt{n}
.
In contrast,
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}
is close to
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

if
r\leq 1
or
\alpha\gg\log_{10}\sqrt{n}
. Hence, randsvd matrices
typically have a small
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
but a large

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}
(close to
10^{\alpha}
) if
\boldsymbol{b}

is a random vector with a uniform distribution.

From Theorems
1
–
3

and the above examples, we can see that a large
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

always
leads to overestimation of
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
,

\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{x}}
, or both. For linear systems arising
from well-posed (and even random) problems,
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})

always over-estimates
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
, and the computation
of the residual vector
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

is intrinsically sensitive to the rounding errors in
\delta\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}

and will be as large as
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}\|
.
This phenomenon is particularly important in iterative methods that
use the residual as the stopping criteria (as they almost always do
except for EPIR), for which it is essential to consider the stability
of both the forward and backward errors.

2.3
Pollution in well-posed problems

Definition
2
allows the consideration
of pollution
\delta\boldsymbol{f}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}-\boldsymbol{f}
.
We now derive some measures for this error and its impact in the context
of (
1
), for which
\delta\boldsymbol{f}\equiv\delta\boldsymbol{A}
.
Let
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
denote the error in the solution of
(
1
) due to both
\delta\boldsymbol{b}
and

\delta\boldsymbol{A}
, i.e.,

(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})(\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b}.

(9)

The following two lemmas allow us to analyze
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}

based on
\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
.

Lemma 1
.

The total error
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}

in (
9
) satisfies

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{b}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right).

(10)

Proof.

Since
(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b}-(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{x}=\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
,
we obtain
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)

and

\boldsymbol{A}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)=(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right).

Applying the Taylor series expansion of
(1+x)^{-1}
or using telescoping,
we obtain

\boldsymbol{A}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right).

(11)

Adding
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
to the
two sides, respectively, we then obtain (
10
).

Lemma
1
is useful because of the tight bound
on the right-hand side of (
9
) given by the following
lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\right\|/\text{$\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|$}<C

for some constants
C<1
. Then,

\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{1-C}\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|.

(12)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
\|\boldsymbol{b}\|\neq 0

(since
\boldsymbol{b}=0
, in general, implies that
\delta\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0}

and
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
). Observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left\|\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\left(\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|^{i-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}{1-\text{$\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|$}}\left(1-\text{$\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|$}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-C}\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|,

where the last inequality is due to
\text{$\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right\|$}\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\right\|/\text{$\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|$}<C<1
.

Lemma
2
suggests a measure of sensitivity
in terms of the pollution error
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
.

Definition 4
.

The
intrinsic condition number
(
ICN
)
of (
1
) w.r.t. the pollution error
\delta\boldsymbol{A}

is

\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\|=\epsilon}\left(\left.\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right/\frac{\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}\right).

(13)

We emphasize that ICN is not the standard condition number (e.g.,
as in
[
33
, (18.10)]
) since the backward
error is not measured as
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\|/\|\boldsymbol{A}\|
.
Hence, we put a hat on
\kappa
to avoid confusion.
\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}

is useful since
\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
defines a correlation
of the pollution
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
. The following theorem shows
that
\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}\leq\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}\leq C

for well-posed problems.

Theorem 4
.

\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}\leq\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
,
as defined in (
13
) and (
6
),
respectively.

Proof.

Using a similar argument as in Lemma
1
, we
obtain

(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}=-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{i}\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}.

For any
0<C<1
,
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\right\|/\text{$\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|$}<C

for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{w}
. Using a similar argument
as in Lemma
2
, we obtain

\left\|(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{1-C}\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\|.

Hence, the supremum in
\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}
is no
greater than
(1-C)
times of
\tilde{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}

defined as

\tilde{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\|=\epsilon}\left(\left.\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{x}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right/\frac{\|(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{b}\|}\right).

(14)

Taking the limit of
(1-C)
as
C
approaches
0
,
\hat{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}\leq\text{$\tilde{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}$}
.
Let
\delta\boldsymbol{b}=(\boldsymbol{I}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}

in (
6
), which tends to
\boldsymbol{0}
as
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\|

approaches 0, so
\tilde{\kappa}_{\delta\boldsymbol{A}}\leq\kappa_{\delta\boldsymbol{b}}
.

Theorem
4
suggests a natural definition
for measuring the correlation of
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
.

Definition 5
.

For a well-posed problem, the rounding errors

\delta\boldsymbol{A}
are
strongly correlated
if
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
,

weakly correlated
if
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=o(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})
,
and
uncorrelated
if
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\Omega(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})
.

If the linear system is polluted with uncorrelated
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
,
then backward stability would suffice (within an
\mathcal{O}(1)

factor). We will focus on cases where
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
is strongly
correlated, which we informally refer to as
unpolluted
. Under
this assumption, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}=\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}

and
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b}
in
(
9
). If we solve (
9
) in
the sense of (
2
), i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}+\boldsymbol{e}
,
where
\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-(\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x})\right\|/\left\|\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})

and
\|\boldsymbol{e}\|/\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
,
then

\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}=\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}))\leq\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-(\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x})\right\|+\left\|\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\right\|}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}))=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}),

and similarly,

\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}+\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{b}\right\|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{b}\right\|}=\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}+\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{b}\right\|}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\|}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}))\leq\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{e}\right\|+\|\delta\boldsymbol{b}\|}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}\|}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}))=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w}).

In other words, forward and backward errors are both guaranteed to
be small. Hence, in the remainder of the paper, we will assume that

\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}=\boldsymbol{A}+\delta\boldsymbol{A}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=\boldsymbol{b}+\delta\boldsymbol{b}

are the “ground truth” when solving (
1
)
(as v.N.-G. did in
[
35
]
), for which the exact
solution will be
\boldsymbol{x}+\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
. To avoid
clustering, we will omit the hats in
\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}
.

2.4
Essential forward and
backward stability

We now focus on solving (
1
) with exact input

\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
with small forward and backward
errors.

Definition 6
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}

in (
1
) are exactly represented by a floating-point
system with unit round-off
\varepsilon_{w}
, and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{*}

denote the exact solution to (
1
). Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}

denoted a numerical solution, which may be rounded from a higher-precision
solution
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+\delta\boldsymbol{x}

where
|\delta x_{i}|=|x_{i}|\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
.

•

The method is
essentially forward stable
(
EFS
) if
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
.

•

The method is
essentially backward stable
(
EBS
) if the
residual in terms of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
is
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
,
i.e.,
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{b}\|\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
,
where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
This bound must be verifiable, even if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
is
not formed explicitly.

•

The method is
EFBS
if it is both EFS and EBS.

Definition
6
differs from the standard stability
and backward stability in numerical analysis in two senses. First,
the notion of EFS is stronger than stability
[
20
,
33
]

(i.e.,
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
)
or “forward stability”
[
24
, p. 9]
. Furthermore,
we consider EFS as a goal separate from EBS, since an EFS solution
may be computed using some components that are not backward stable
or even unstable. Second, we define EBS based on a residual
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

instead of
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, even
though
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the final output. This detail is
important because
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\|

is close to
\mathcal{O}(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})

for well-posed problems,
even if
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}

is computed in exact arithmetic
.

3
Forward and Backward Errors
in RP-GMRES

We present a new analysis of forward and backward errors in RP-GMRES.
This analysis serves as the guideline in devising FBSMR, but the reader
can safely skip to section
4
without compromising
the understanding of the implementation.

3.1
Trial and test spaces in RP-GMRES

As we have seen in Algorithm
1
, RP-GMRES solves a

projected least squares
(
PLS
)
problem
,

\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{r}

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}=\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}{}_{k-1}
,
and then
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
.
Using the analogy of variational methods for solving partial differential
equations (PDEs), we refer to the range spaces of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}

and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}
as the
trial
and
test spaces
,
respectively. Since
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{k})
and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k})

differ substantially in general when
\boldsymbol{M}\neq\boldsymbol{I}
,
we consider RP-GMRES as a Petrov-Galerkin (PG) method.
3
3

3

Petrov-Galerkin methods are generalizations of Galerkin (aka Ritz-Galerkin)
methods for PDEs in that the test spaces may differ from trial spaces.
CG is a Galerkin-projection method
[
34
, Section 4.1]
,
and so are multigrid methods for symmetric systems from PDEs (e.g.,

[
31
, Section 13]
).
In contrast, unpreconditioned GMRES solves a PLS problem

\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Q}_{k-1}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}^{H}\boldsymbol{r},

(16)

for which the trial and test spaces are
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k-1})

and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k})
, respectively. Note that
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k-1})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k})

at convergence (or breakdown) in exact arithmetic, so GMRES is a Galerkin
(or ‘‘quasi-Galerkin’’) method.
4
4

4

LP-GMRES may also be considered a PG method in terms of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}

where the trial and test spaces are
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k-1})

and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-H}\boldsymbol{Q}_{k})
, respectively,
although it is typically treated as a quasi-Galerkin method in terms
of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
.
Due to this difference, RP-GMRES requires more sophisticated definitions
and analyses for its trial and test spaces compared to those in
[
16
,
29
,
31
]
.
To this end, let us first refine the definition of the Krylov space
by taking into account the orthogonalization in Arnoldi iterations.

Definition 7
.

Given
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the
Arnoldi-Krylov subspace

(
AKS
) is given by

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{v})=\langle\boldsymbol{q}_{0},\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{q}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}^{k-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{k-1}\rangle,

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}=\boldsymbol{v}/\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
,

\|\boldsymbol{q}_{i}\|=1
, and
\boldsymbol{q}_{i+1}\in\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\perp}\mathcal{K}_{i}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{v})

for
i\geq 1
, where
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal
projector
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\perp}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{i-1}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i-1}^{H}=\boldsymbol{I}-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{j}\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{H}

and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{i-1}\equiv[\boldsymbol{q}_{0},\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{q}_{i-1}]
.
We refer to
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}\mid 0\leq i\leq k-1\}
as the
generating
vectors
of
\mathcal{K}_{k}
.

RP-GMRES involves the following two subspaces.

Definition 8
.

Given
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
a nonsingular right preconditioner
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the
primal Arnoldi-Krylov
subspace
(
PAKS
) is

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1},\boldsymbol{v})=\langle\boldsymbol{q}_{0},\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{0},\dots,\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\right)^{k}\boldsymbol{q}_{k-1}\rangle,

(18)

as defined in (
17
). The
dual Krylov subspace

(
DKS
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1},\boldsymbol{v})\equiv\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}),

(19)

where the
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}
are the same as the generating vectors
of the primal subspace.

Note that the generating vectors of
\mathcal{D}_{k}
(i.e.,
\{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{i}\}
)
are nonorthogonal, and hence we can only refer to
\mathcal{D}_{k}

as a Krylov subspace instead of AKS.

3.2
Stability of PLS in RP-GMRES

The analogy of RP-GMRES with PG allows us to gain some insights in
terms of its stability. First, observe that the basis vectors in PG
for PDEs (such as some finite element methods
[
7
]
)
in general do not need orthogonal basis functions. Hence, we assert
that the orthogonality of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}
plays a minor role
for the stability of RP-GMRES. This assertion seemingly contradicts
the conventional wisdom of GMRES
[
4
,
16
,
29
]
,
but it should not be surprising since QMR
[
17
]
,
which is also a PG method
[
34
]
, does not use orthogonal
basis vectors either and often converges (although less robustly than
GMRES) in practice. The mathematical reason is that the loss of orthogonality
of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}
would lead to a quasi-minimization (QM, instead
of
\ell_{2}
minimization). As long as QM leads to a reduction of
the true residual, then RP-GMERS can continue making progress, as
we show in Theorem
5
below.

Second, in PG the trial space is the most critical for the accuracy
of the solution (see e.g.,
[
12
]
). This statement
partially explains the success of GMRES-IR using LP-GMRES, of which
the trial space is
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k-1})
with orthonormal
basis vectors. For RP-GMRES, we assert that the key to achieve EFBS
is to stabilize the projection of
\boldsymbol{A}
onto
\mathcal{D}_{k}
.
However, this projection cannot be stabilized by making
\mathcal{D}_{k}

an AKS, because Arnoldi orthogonalization would only help achieve
backward stability unless it is done in higher precision, which we
want to avoid. Instead, we will achieve EFBS by stabilizing the projection
based on the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

In RP-GMRES with restart, let
\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}

denote the residual vector at the beginning since the most recent
restart (or start) and
s_{k}
denote the number of steps since the
most recent restart. Suppose PLS in (
15
) solves
a quasi-minimization

\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{y}}\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\boldsymbol{W}}

(20)

for some weighted semi-norm
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{W}}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\boldsymbol{x}\|
,
such that
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

reduces
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|

(in exact arithmetic) by
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-s_{k}}\|\leq C

for some
C<1
before restart. Then, the backward error
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|

converges to
\varepsilon_{w}
after
\log_{C}(\varepsilon_{w})-\log_{C}\left(\|\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\|\right)

restarts. For well-posed problems, the forward error
\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|

also converges to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
in exact arithmetic.

Proof.

By assumption, after
m
restarts, the residual
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|\leq C^{m}\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\|
.
Hence, reducing the residual to
\varepsilon_{w}\|\boldsymbol{b}\|

requires at most
\log_{C}(\varepsilon_{w}\|\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\|)=\log_{C}(\varepsilon_{w})-\log_{C}(\|\boldsymbol{b}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\|)

steps. Since
\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\kappa(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|
,
where
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b})=\mathcal{O}(1)
for
well-posed problems, the forward error (barring pollution in input

\boldsymbol{A}
) also converges to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
.

In Theorem
5
, QM in general does result
in a reduction in
\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}\|

at the
k
th step for small
k
because
\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}\|\lesssim\sqrt{k}\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\boldsymbol{W}}

if
\boldsymbol{W}
is composed of
k
unit vectors. This argument
is also fundamental in QMR
[
18
]
, especially for its convergence
criteria. Note that the
\sqrt{k}
factor may be larger in the presence
of rounding errors, since
\boldsymbol{W}^{H}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}

is not a Hessenberg matrix anymore in general but we treat it as a
Hessenberg matrix (the same also holds in QMR, which treats a non-tridiagonal
matrix as tridiagonal in the presence of rounding errors). The orthogonality
of the vectors in
\boldsymbol{W}
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}
)
can reduce the
\sqrt{k}
factor to a number closer to
1
, but
its effect is not as significant as the stability of the projection
of
\boldsymbol{A}
onto
\mathcal{D}_{k}
. Hence, we expect that
it suffices to use the working precision when performing QM and to
use the unstable but more parallelization-friendly CGS in place of
MGS in Arnoldi orthogonalization, as we will demonstrate in section
5.4
.
By assuming exact arithmetic when computing
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
,
we ensure that the reduction in
\|\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}\|

translates to the reduction in
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|
,
independently of
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})
. In practice, “exact
arithmetic” can be replaced with
\varepsilon_{w}^{2}
precision
(assuming
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\leq 1/\varepsilon_{w}
) when computing

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}
,
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
,

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{x}_{k-s_{k}}+\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
,
and
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
. This completes
the derivation of FBSMR.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
5
shares some similarities in
spirit with
[
15
, Theorem 2.7]
and
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
,
which focus on forward errors in EPIR
[
14
]
. The
residual in EPIR is evaluated in higher precision, but its accuracy
is only guaranteed to about
\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w}

for well-posed problems. This inaccuracy prevented the use of residuals
as the convergence criteria in EPIR. By evaluating
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

in higher precision (at a minimal cost), we can compute the residual
to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
, so that the residual can be used
as the convergence criteria in Theorem
5

and in FBSMR. Since both
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{0,k}

are stabilized, we refer to our approach as
forward and backward
stabilization
(
FBS
).

\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are floating-point
real or complex numbers in working precision. Typically,
\boldsymbol{A}

should be unpolluted, although the algorithm itself does not depend
on this property. The approximate-inverse preconditioner
\boldsymbol{M}

can be in a lower precision with unit roundoff
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{w}}
,
but it may also be in another precision (such as
\varepsilon_{w}

or
\sqrt[4]{\varepsilon_{w}}
; we will pursue the latter in future
work). The algorithm has three control parameters:
\tau
(the convergence
tolerance),
restart
(the maximum number of iterations before
restart), and
maxit
(the maximum number of iterations).
The recommended default values are
\tau=10\varepsilon_{w}
, restart=30,
and maxit=500. The algorithm returns an approximate solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}

in working precision. Optionally, FBSMR returns
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

in higher precision and/or the relative residual
\gamma
computed
from
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
. In general,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

is no more accurate than
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, but it is required
to evaluate the residual accurately for convergence criteria (line
12
)
or for verifying EFBS as discussed in section
2.4
.
Note that line
8
is an approximate check due to
quasi-minimization.

An important detail in Algorithm
2
is that we use

\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
as the initial guess, even though

\boldsymbol{M}
in general may be a grossly inaccurate approximation
to
\boldsymbol{A}
. This choice is different from the standard practice
of GMRES
[
31
]
, although it is consistent with the practice
in IR (see e.g.,
[
4
]
). We will give heuristic
and numerical justifications for its use in the context of RP-GMRES
in section
5.3
. Also note that in line
6
,
either MGS or CGS can be used without compromising EFBS due to Theorem
5
.
It is advisable to use MGS in serial but CGS in parallel (see section

).

In terms of implementation, only
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
needs to
be stored in higher precision; we use ‘
\leftleftarrows
’ to emphasize
the assignment in higher precision. Among the arithmetic operations,
only the gaxpy operations (including matrix-vector multiplications)
that are boxed are in higher-precision. For example, if the working
precision is double precision, then the preconditioner
\boldsymbol{M}

would be in single precision, and the gaxpy operations can be in double-double
precision
[
8
]
. If the working precision
is single precision, then the preconditioner
\boldsymbol{M}
may
be in half precision, and the gaxpy operations can be in double precision.
If an operand for high-precision operation is stored in working precision,
we convert the numbers into higher precision in a just-in-time fashion
before each floating-point arithmetic, which is easy to implement
for gaxpy.

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}

5.3
Effect of initial solutions from
approximate inverses

In FBSMR, we use
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}

as the initial guess even though
\boldsymbol{M}
is known to be
an inaccurate factorization. Inaccurate factorizations often lead
to initial spikes of the residuals, as we have seen in Figure
1
.
Since the initial spikes may increase the number of iterations, one
might question the wisdom of using
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}

as the initial solution. We conjecture that this spike only indicates
that the singular vectors corresponding to the extreme singular values
of
\boldsymbol{A}
are not well resolved by the inaccurate factorization.
The approximate factorization can still resolve some (if not all)
singular vectors corresponding to the interior singular values, so
using
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
as the initial solution
can eliminate some modes corresponding to the interior singular values
from the residual. Hence, we can expect FBSMR to solve the linear
system with lower-dimensional Krylov subspaces, and in turn allowing
a small restart value. A rigorous analysis seems to be challenging
and is beyond the scope of this work. We present some numerical evidence
in Figure
2
for the cases in Table
2
.
It can be seen that using
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
would
make FBSMR converge more slowly in all cases except for
s3rmq4m1
.
More importantly, with
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
, FBSMR
could not converge for
mplate
and
invextr1_new

with restart=30. Using
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
as
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}

allowed FBSMR to converge even with a small restart value, even though

\boldsymbol{M}
may be grossly inaccurate.

5.4
Comparison of CGS versus MGS

In section
3
, we used the argument
of quasi-minimization to support the use of CGS in place of MGS in
FBSMR. To verify this claim, we compare the convergence history of
FBSMR using MGS versus CGS in Figure
3
for the
sparse systems in Table
2
. It
can be seen that FBSMR converged to machine precision with either
MGS or CGS. However, for
radfr1
and
adder_dcop_06
,
FBSMR with MGS used one fewer iteration than with CGS due to a slightly
more accurate estimation of the residual. Hence, we recommend using
MGS in serial but using CGS in parallel to reduce communication overhead
in FBSMR. This practice is common for GMRES, so it would be relatively
easy to implement FBSMR based on existing GMRES implementations.

6
Conclusions and Discussions

In this work, we introduced an iterative method called
FBSMR

by leveraging quasi-minimization in RP-GMERS for well-posed problems
with ill-conditioned coefficient matrices. We showed that FBSMR could
overcome ill-conditioned coefficient matrices and deliver optimal
forward and backward errors at
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
, assuming
the input matrix is unpolluted in the sense that
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})

for the rounding errors
\delta\boldsymbol{A}
in
\boldsymbol{A}
.
In contrast, a traditional backward stable method can only guarantee

\mathcal{O}(\kappa(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})
in forward errors.
FBSMR achieves this optimal accuracy by meeting a stronger stability
requirement, namely EFBS, using higher precision for a small portion
of the computation. Since the most expensive computations (namely
the preconditioner) can be performed in lower precision, FBSMR can
also improve efficiency over direct methods with or without IR, while
being more accurate than other iterative methods. In this work, we
presented experimental results using LU or Cholesky factorizations
as preconditioners in FBSMR. We have done some preliminary studies
with hybrid incomplete factorization
[
11
]
in low
precision and also had great success. We will report more extensive
numerical experimentations, including performance results, when we
release FBSMR as an open-source library in the future.

The concepts developed in this work lead to some interesting new opportunities
in improving numerical methods in several aspects. First, the application
developers can take advantage of EFBS algorithms by devising methods
where the rounding (and discretization) errors in the matrices are
strongly or weakly correlated so that
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})

or
\|\delta\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\|/\|\boldsymbol{b}\|=\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{p}(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})

for some
p<1
(such as
p=1/2
). Such optimization can significantly
benefit high-order discretization methods, for which the discretization
errors are often so small on finer meshes that rounding errors become
the dominant factor. Second, we can further refine the stopping criteria
in FBSMR for problems with weakly correlated rounding errors, so that
it can stop earlier at
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{p}(\boldsymbol{A})\varepsilon_{w})

instead of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{w})
based on the applications.
Third, we used a heuristic argument with numerical experimentation
to justify the use of an inaccurate approximate inverse to compute
the initial guess in section
5.3
; it
would be useful to derive a more rigorous analysis to determine when
it is more efficient to use
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
as
the initial guess, especially when
\boldsymbol{M}
is computed only
in
\sqrt[4]{\varepsilon_{w}}
precision or with incomplete factorization.
Finally, it is possible extend the strategy of FBS to other numerical
problems, such as (rank-deficient) least squares problems and nonlinear
equations, so that more methods can achieve EFBS while taking advantage
of lower-precision preconditioners for improved efficiency. We plan
to explore these research directions in the future.
