Multilevel-in-Layer Training for Deep Neural Network Regression
Colin Ponce, Ruipeng Li, Christina Mao, Panayot Vassilevski

Abstract
A common challenge in regression is that for many problems, the degrees of freedom required for a high-quality solution also allows for overfitting. Regularization is a class of strategies that seek to restrict the range of possible solutions so as to discourage overfitting while still enabling good solutions, and different regularization strategies impose different types of restrictions. In this paper, we present a multilevel regularization strategy that constructs and trains a hierarchy of neural networks, each of which has layers that are wider versions of the previous network’s layers. We draw intuition and techniques from the field of Algebraic Multigrid (AMG), traditionally used for solving linear and nonlinear systems of equations, and specifically adapt the Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) for nonlinear systems of equations to the problem of deep learning. Training through V-cycles then encourage the neural networks to build a hierarchical understanding of the problem. We refer to this approach as multilevel-in-width to distinguish from prior multilevel works which hierarchically alter the depth of neural networks. The resulting approach is a highly flexible framework that can be applied to a variety of layer types, which we demonstrate with both fully-connected and convolutional layers. We experimentally show with PDE regression problems that our multilevel training approach is an effective regularizer, improving the generalize performance of the neural networks studied.

Introduction

A feedforward deep neural network (DNN) consists of a set of layers, each of which applies an affine mapping to its input vectors followed by a nonlinear
activation function
acting componentwise on the transferred vectors. That affine mapping is controlled by a set of tunable parameters, the selection of which determines the overall action of the DNN. The task of training a deep neural network (DNN) is that of selecting these network parameters in order to enable the DNN to approximately capture a desired pattern. This is done by solving, or approximately solving, an optimization problem with an objective function that maps a choice of DNN parameters to a quantitative measure of the DNN’s performance on the problem of interest; this objective function is commonly called a
loss function

[
11
]
.

Training is typically accomplished through stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or its variants, which are cost-efficient and well-supported by existing software
[
1
,
20
]
. A common challenge in the design of neural networks and their training is that, often, providing sufficient degrees of freedom in the network to obtain a high-quality solution also allows for overfitting, which harms generalization accuracy.
Regularization
is a class of strategies that seeks to restrict the freedom of the training method, therefore encouraging simpler solutions. Ideally, this regularization discourages overfitting while still enabling high-quality solutions. A variety of regularization strategies exist, each of which encourages a different kind of simplicity, such as L2 regularization, L1 regularization, or dropout regulariztaion
[
11
]
.

In this paper, we present a novel
multilevel
regularization strategy that constructs and trains a hierarchy of neural networks. In this approach, all networks have the same number and type of layers, but each layer is a wider version of next network’s layers, so networks get ”thinner” as we move through the hierarchy. We base this approach on the techniques of Algebraic Multigrid (AMG), a class of methods traditionally used for solving linear and nonlinear systems of equations, and we leverage teh Full Approximation Scheme (FAS), a multilevel framework traditionally used for nonlinear systems of equations. We use the classic V-cycle in training, and develop a method to pass problem and parameter information, including momentum information, between the levels of the hierarchy during training. The resulting training method encourages the neural networks to learn the problem in a hierarchical manner. This approach is highly flexible, allowing each layer to coarsen (or not) independently of each other layer, and can be applied to a variety of layer types. We specifically demonstrate this for fully-connected and convolutional layers. Our approach is not a replacement for traditional, one-level learning methods such as SGD; instead, it uses that traditional optimizer as an inner iterator within the multilevel framework. We demonstrate the generalization benefits of our approach with a set of PDE-based regression problems.

We refer to our approach as ”multilevel-in-width” to distinguish it from prior multilevel works that change the number of layers in a neural network, which we think of as ”multilevel-in-depth.” Other authors, taking very different approaches from that presented here, have also observed regularization benefits from their multilevel methods
[
27
,
7
]
. The present paper contributes to a small but growing body of evidence that suggests that multilevel neural network training methods have the potential to act as a beneficial regularizer over a significant range of problems, network architectures, and multilevel approaches, though this requires further investigation to fully understand.

Our open-source software, which we call MTNN (short for
Multilevel Training of Neural Networks
, pronounced “mountain”) is built on PyTorch and is free to download at
https://github.com/LLNL/MTNN
. It is designed for modularity and extensibility to facilitate the study of new problems and algorithmic choices.

In Section
2
, we discuss prior work in AMG, current methods for training neural networks, and prior work on building notions of hierarchy into DNNs. We then give a summary of the FAS algorithm from an optimization point of view in Section
3
. Next, in Section
4
, we formulate a two-level FAS in the setting of DNN training.
Our specific choices of the operators (restriction and prolongation) are given in Section
5
.
Section
6
presents our two-level full-approximation scheme, which can be recursively extended to more levels. Section
7
presents the extension of our method to convolutional neural networks. Finally, in Section
8
we demonstrate our techniques on a set of PDE regression problems, and in Section
9
we conclude.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Multilevel Methods

Multilevel methods are a class of iterative methods long used in scientific computing as a scalable solver for linear and non-linear systems of equations
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}
. Multilevel methods is a more general class of algorithms emerging out of
multigrid methods
, where this method was first applied to partial differential problems on grid. Traditional multigrid algorithms construct a hierarchy of discretizations of a geometric problem domain in which each discretization is a coarsened variant of the previous. Algebraic multigrid (AMG) also constructs a hierarchy of problems, but does in a fully-algebraic manner, independent of any underlying geometry.

When applied to (deterministic) systems of equations, multilevel methods accelerate convergence towards a solution through
smoothing
, also known as relaxation methods, and
coarse-grid corrections
, down-sampling residual error on the coarse grid back to the fine grid. Passing solution information between fine and coarse grids is known as
restriction
and
prolongation
.

A single multilevel iteration is known as a
cycle
. Perhaps the most common of these is the V-cycle, the linear two-level version of which proceeds as follows:

1.

Smooth the solution iterate on the fine level problem
g
.

2.

Restrict problem residual to the coarse level problem
g_{c}
with some restriction operator
R
.

3.

Solve the problem on the coarse level to obtain a coarse-grid correction.

4.

Prolong the coarse-grid correction to the fine level and add it to the solution iterate with some prolongation operator
P
.

5.

Smooth the solution iterate on the fine level problem
g
.

This V-cycle is a framework in which one must make algorithmic choices on how to accomplish individual steps such as smoothing, restriction, and prolongation.
For further reference, a more detailed introduction to multilevel and multigrid methods can be provided by Briggs et al.
[
5
]
, Hackbusch
[
13
]
and Wesseling
[
28
]
.

A classic problem for which linear multilevel methods are extremely effective is the Poisson problem,
-\nabla\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ (\kappa\penalty\ \nabla\penalty\ u)\penalty\ =\penalty\ f
, defined over some problem domain, which models diffusion processes. As a result, data from Poisson problems are often amenable to the type of hierarchical representations discussed in the Introduction.

2.1.1
Full Approximation Scheme

When multilevel methods are applied to nonlinear systems of equations, the V-cycle is similar, but passing the residual to the coarse level may no longer be appropriate. A generalization appropriate for nonlinear problems is the
Full Approximation Scheme
(FAS)
[
4
]
. In FAS, one still has a fine problem,
g
, a coarse problem,
g_{c}
, and restriction and prolongation operators,
R
and
P
.

However, instead of restricting the residual as in Step 2 above, one instead restricts the solution iterate, and alters the right-hand side of the coarse problem to capture residual information. The FAS coarse-grid problem, then, is

{\widehat{g}_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=R\mathbf{b}+(g_{c}(R\mathbf{x})-Rg(\mathbf{x})).

(1)

The above modification to the right-hand side is sometimes called a
\tau
-correction
; one can check that in the linear case it reduces back to the coarse-grid residual equation.

An extension of FAS to discretized optimization problems, “MG/OPT”, was introduced by Nash, who also demonstrated its convergence properties
[
21
]
. The MG/OPT algorithm extends FAS by performing a line search on applying the coarse-grid correction (Step 4 in the above V-cycle).

2.2
Deep Neural Networks (DNN)

A neural network (NN) consists of
n_{L}
layers: an input layer,
n_{L}-2
hidden layers, and an output layer. An input vector is denoted by
\mathbf{y}^{in}
. The hidden layers consists of weight matrices
W_{k}
and bias vectors
\mathbf{b}_{k}
,
k=1,...,n_{L}
, and an activation function
\varphi
such as the well-known ReLU function. The output of the neural network calculation is denoted by
\mathbf{y}^{out}
. There is no clear definition for the number of hidden layers required to consider a neural network a “deep” neural network, though often any network with more than 2 hidden layers is considered a DNN.

Forward propagation, also known as a
forward pass
, is the procedure whereby the input (feature) vector is passed through the neural network layer by layer, applying an activation function after each layer to produce intermediate variables and finally an output vector. We discuss the notation and operation of a forward pass in greater detail in Section
4
.

2.2.1
Training Methods for Neural Networks

An important difference between neural network training and traditional optimization is that the true objective function of interest, performance over the entire population of possible examples, is inaccessible; the performance over a training sample is merely an approximation to this true function of interest. This motivates a number of important design choices in neural network training methods. Training is typically done via a stochastic first-order method, such as stochastic gradient descent, AdaGrad
[
8
]
, RMSProp, Adam
[
15
]
, or other variations
[
19
,
18
]
; this both accelerates computation and reduces the chances of getting stuck in a local minimum that is an artifact is the particular training set. Momentum maintains an exponentially decaying sum of past gradients, which helps to accelerate training and jump over local minima by allowing the ”big ideas” to compound
[
25
]
. Finally, training all the way to convergence on the training set would likely result in severe overfitting, even with regularization, and so early stopping is almost universal
[
11
]
.

These methods are all fundamentally variations on gradient descent. Second-order methods such as Newton-Raphson have been standard in optimization for some time
[
3
]
, and in fact is often much more effective than gradient descent-style methods. However, second order methods require the computation or approximation of the Hessian matrix of the objective function, requiring the computation of
O(n^{2})
Hessian elements for
n
parameters. In the case of DNNs, even moderately sized networks contain millions of parameters, and so a Hessian matrix is very slow to compute (and sometimes does not fit in memory); consequently, second-order methods have not become mainstream for DNNs. Approximate second-order methods for DNNs with linear storage requirements, however, continues to be an active area of research and are beginning to become more competitive
[
26
]
.

2.2.2
Hierarchy and Multilevel Methods in Neural Network Architectures

Although SGD and its variations do not naturally capture hierarchy, notions of hierarchy are quite common in neural network architectures. Convolution neural networks (CNNs) for image processing are often described as building up concepts in a hierarchical manner, capturing simple ideas such as edges and shading in the first layer, and capturing more complex image elements in later layers. Beyond CNNs, the autoencoder is one of the most popular architectural designs today
[
11
]
. There are many variations and applications of autoencoders but they all fundamentally work by hierarchically compressing input data into a low-dimension representation before hierarchically decompressing it back to its original size.

Another network architecture that deserves special mention is U-net
[
24
]
. This network, through its layers, progressively makes an images smaller and smaller (less height and width) but deeper and deeper (more “color” channels), before then passing the data through layers that make it smaller and shallower again, eventually producing a processed image of the original size. The architecture design is highly analogous to the V-Cycle that is ubiquitous in multilevel methods
[
5
]
, and works because it processes images in a hierarchical fashion.

In recent years, a small but growing body of literature has explored multilevel methods in the training itself. In
[
6
]
, the authors develop a multilevel-in-width Levenberg-Marquardt method for training a neural network with one hidden layer to find a discretization-free approximate solution of a PDE. Most other works currently in this space focus on multilevel-in-depth methods for deep residual networks (ResNets), exploiting the dynamical perspective that views a ResNet as taking forward Euler steps on an ODE: Günther et. al.
[
12
]
uses multilevel parallel-in-time methods to achieve parallelism in the forward and backward propagation; Kirby et. al.
[
16
]
extends this approach to GPUs, and Cyr et. al.
[
7
]
uses this framework to develop a cascadic initialization strategy with regularization benefits. Gaedke-Merzhäuser et. al.,
[
10
]
and Planta et. al.
[
27
]
also study ResNets, but develop variants of MG/OPT to solve the problem in contrast to the parallel-in-time approach.

Both
[
27
]
and
[
7
]
show that, even though multilevel methods are often motivated by speed, the networks that result from multilevel training methods can have better performance than those trained without them. We observe this in our present work as well.

Application of Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) to an optimization problem

In this section, we develop the framework for applying the Full
Approximation Scheme (FAS) to optimization problems and present some results
that will guide the development of FAS-based methods for deep
learning.

Our optimization problem of interest is

g(\mathbf{x})\mapsto\min,

(2)

for a given nonlinear functional
g:\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
. We assume that the gradient of
g
,
\nabla g
is
computationally available, and we will be using (in the presentation)
the Hessian of
g
, denoted
H_{g}
, which is a matrix of its second partial
derivatives. Using a Taylor expansion, we have the following
approximate expansion

g(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{q})\approx g(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{q}^{T}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{T}H_{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{q}.

Now, to define a two-level FAS, we need a coarse version of
g
,

g_{c}:\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, where
n_{c}\ll n
. We
introduce three mappings:

•

A coarse approximation to a fine quantity,

\mathbf{x}_{c}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}
for a mapping
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}:\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
.

•

A fine-to-coarse restriction mapping

\mathbf{R}:\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}
.

•

A coarse-to-fine interpolation mapping

{\mathbf{P}}:\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{c}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

To be precise, given a fine vector
\mathbf{x}
, the coarse vector

\mathbf{x}_{c}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}
is such that
{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}={\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}
approximates (in some norm) the vector
\mathbf{x}
.

The coarse FAS functional
{\widehat{g}}_{c}
is defined in terms of
the coarse functional
g_{c}
, and in TL FAS is chosen so that

{\widehat{g}}_{c}
approximates
g
. Specifically, let
\mathbf{x}
be a
current approximation to the solution of the original problem. Then,
the FAS method is based on the solution of the auxiliary coarse
problem

{\widehat{g}_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})\equiv g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})-\mathbf{x}^{T}_{c}\left(\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})\right)\mapsto\min.

(3)

Once
\mathbf{x}_{c}
is computed, the next approximation to (
2
) is

\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{x}+{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}).

(4)

The term
\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
is sometimes
refereed to as the
\tau
-correction
. It replaces
\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})
, the gradient of the coarse functional at the coarse
approxmation point, with
\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
, the restriction of the
fine gradient. When computed exactly, this makes
{\widehat{g}_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})
match, up to first
order, a restricted version of
g(\mathbf{x})
(though as we will see in Section
5.6
we do not compute this exactly).

3.1
The FAS coarse problem solution

Let
\mathbf{x}_{c}
solve the coarse problem (
3
), so that

\nabla{\widehat{g}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=0
.
Hence,

\nabla g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})-\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})=0.

Using Taylor expansion of
\nabla g_{c}
about
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}
, gives

\nabla g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})+\sum\limits_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq 1}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}).

Above (and in what follows), we use multi-index notation, whereby for any
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{i})^{d}_{i=1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and any vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})^{d}_{i=1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is shorthand for

\prod\limits_{i:\;1\leq i\leq d}y^{\alpha_{i}}_{i}
. Also, for a scalar function
f=f(\mathbf{x})
of several variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})^{d}_{i=1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we denote
\partial_{i}f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
, and by
\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}f(\mathbf{x})
the repeated partial derivatives of corresponding order
\alpha_{i}
with respect to the
i
th variable
x_{i}
of
f
.

Combining the last two equations above, we obtain

\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})+\sum\limits_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq 1}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})=0.

(5)

3.2
The FAS-correction

Once we have computed
\mathbf{x}_{c}
, we update the new fine approximation

\mathbf{x}_{new}=\mathbf{x}+{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})
.
Our goal is to see how close the updated
\mathbf{x}_{new}
is to the exact solution, or rather how close

\nabla g(\mathbf{x}_{new})
is to zero. For that purpose, we expand
\nabla g(\mathbf{x}_{new})
about

\mathbf{x}
and multiply by
\mathbf{R}
on the left which gives

\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x}_{new})=\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})+\sum\limits_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq 1}({\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}))^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{R}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\nabla g(\mathbf{x}).

(6)

If we want to make the first order terms of (
6
) and
(
5
) equal, we obtain

\sum\limits_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|=1}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|=1}({\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}))^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{R}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\nabla g(\mathbf{x}),

which one can rewrite as

\sum\limits^{n_{c}}_{i=1}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})_{i}\partial_{i}\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}({\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}))_{i}\mathbf{R}\partial_{i}\nabla g(\mathbf{x}).

(7)

Introducing the Hessian matrices (note that they are symmetric)

H_{g}(\mathbf{y})=(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g(\mathbf{y}))^{n}_{i,j=1}\text{ and }H_{g_{c}}(\mathbf{y}_{c})=(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g_{c}(\mathbf{y}_{c}))^{n_{c}}_{i,j=1},

identity (
7
) can be written more concisely as

H_{g_{c}}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{R}H_{g}(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}).

In other words, we have proved the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{R},{\mathbf{P}},{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
and
g
,
g_{c}
satisfy the identity

\mathbf{R}H_{g}(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{P}}=H_{g_{c}}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}).

(8)

Then the updated approximation
\mathbf{x}_{new}
by the FAS algorithm satisfies the approximate first order optimality condition

\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x}_{new})=0.

∎

To satisfy condition (
8
), since
H_{g_{c}}
is a symmetric matrix, we require

\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}.

(9)

For the application of our main interest (training of DNNs), we do not
form the mappings
{\mathbf{P}},\mathbf{R},{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
as matrices, but will instead
derive methods for computing the action of these operators. Also, in
general we do not choose
\mathbf{R}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
. Instead, we choose
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
and

{\mathbf{P}}
such that the product
\mathbf{Q}={\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
admits an approximation
property (that we motivate in Section
3.3
) and
such that

{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}{\mathbf{P}}=I,

(10)

which implies that
\mathbf{Q}
is a projection (
\mathbf{Q}^{2}=\mathbf{Q}
).

Remark 3.1
.

The above theorem shows that the gradient of the functional
g
is orthogonal (up to first order terms) to the coarse space
\text{Range}(\mathbf{R}^{T})=\text{Range}({\mathbf{P}})
.
Hence, what remains to be done, is to reduce the error in a space complementary to the coarse space, which is customarily referred to as
smoothing
. In other words, we are looking at
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}
such that

\nabla g(\mathbf{x}_{new}+\mathbf{x}^{\perp})=0.

The two-level FAS algorithm alternates between a smoothing step and a coarse level solution step as described in detail in Section
6
.

3.3
Motivating guidelines for the design of the coarse functional
g_{c}

One choice is to define

g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=g({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}),

(11)

which admits excellent approximation but is non-optimal in terms of
cost. Let us examine the choice (
11
) to motivate an approximation. Assume that
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
are such that
{\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}

approximates
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{x}-{\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}\|
is “small” in
some norm
\|.\|
. Let
{\mathbf{P}}
, represented as a matrix, admit the form

{\mathbf{P}}=(p_{i,i_{c}})
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
1\leq i_{c}\leq n_{c}
.

Under the coarse functional choice (
11
), we have

\partial_{j_{c}}g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=\sum\limits^{n}_{j=1}\partial_{j}g({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})p_{j,j_{c}},

and similarly

\partial_{i_{c}}\partial_{j_{c}}g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=\sum\limits^{n}_{i,j=1}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})p_{j,j_{c}}p_{i,i_{c}}.

In other words, we have

H_{g_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})={\mathbf{P}}^{T}H_{g}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}){\mathbf{P}}.

Now, suppose
\mathbf{x}_{c}
is derived from the restriction
\mathbf{x}_{c}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}
. Then, we have

H_{g_{c}}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})={\mathbf{P}}^{T}H_{g}({\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{P}}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}H_{g}(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{P}}+{\mathbf{P}}^{T}\left(H_{g}({\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-H_{g}(\mathbf{x})\right){\mathbf{P}}.

Because
\|\mathbf{x}-{\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}\|
is small, the second term in this formula is small, so

H_{g_{c}}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})\approx{\mathbf{P}}^{T}H_{g}(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{P}},

so that condition (
8
) is approximately
satisfied.

The exact choice of
g_{c}
is problem-dependent, and we design such a coarse functional for neural networks below, but this motivates some guidelines:

(A)

It is desirable that
g_{c}
be chosen so that
H_{g_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})\approx{\mathbf{P}}^{T}H_{g}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}){\mathbf{P}}

(B)

It is desirable that
\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}
.

(C)

It is desirable that
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
be chosen so that
\|{\mathbf{P}}^{T}\left(H_{g}({\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-H_{g}(\mathbf{x})\right){\mathbf{P}}\|
is small. As the Hessian matrix is a continuous function of
\mathbf{x}
, this guideline can be simplified to a desire that
\|\mathbf{x}-{\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
be small.

These properties are desirable because, when they are true, Theorem
3.1
approximately holds, ensuring an approximation for first-order consistency.

4
Application of Full Approximation Scheme to Deep Neural Network training

In the previous section, we derived some useful properties of two-level FAS for optimization along with a set of advice to help guide the development of

{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
operators. In this section, we apply those results to
the problem of training deep neural networks via FAS.

A feedforward DNN (deep neural network) is defined as follows:

•

A set of
n_{L}
layers, where the
k
th layer is associated with a vector variable

\mathbf{y}_{k}
.

•

Two consecutive layers are connected by an activation function

\varphi
which acts on each component of the vectors involved.

•

Also, two consecutive layers,
k
and
k+1
, are connected by a transition coefficient matrix

W_{k}
, and a shift vector
\mathbf{b}_{k}
.

•

To compute the parameters
\{W_{k}\}^{n_{L}}_{k=0}
and
\{\mathbf{b}_{k}\}^{n_{L}}_{k=0}
, the DNN makes use of two sets of given quantities,
\{\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s}\}
- input, and
\{\mathbf{y}^{out}_{s}\}
- the respective output.

The DNN is
trained
by computing the weights
\{W_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{b}_{k}\}
, such that
for the set of given inputs
\{\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s}\}
, it produces an accurate approximations to the respective given set of outputs
\{\mathbf{y}^{out}_{s}\}
, i.e., for each
s
,
DNN(\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s})\approx\mathbf{y}^{out}_{s}
. The DNN parameters
\{W_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{b}_{k}\}
, are computed by solving a suitable optimization problem.

For any given set of parameters
W=\{W_{k}\}
and
\mathbf{b}=\{\mathbf{b}_{k}\}
, let
\mathbf{x}
be a vector quantity that unrolls and concatenates them, that is, let

\mathbf{x}=\left[W_{0}[:],W_{1}[:],\dots,\;W_{n_{L}}[:],\mathbf{b}_{0}[:],\mathbf{b}_{1}[:],\dots,\mathbf{b}_{n_{L}}[:]\right]

(12)

be the unrolled vector of all learnable
parameters in the DNN. Given
\mathbf{x}
, the DNN acts on any input vector data
\mathbf{y}^{in}
, i.e., computes

\mathbf{y}^{in}\mapsto\mathbf{y}^{out}:=DNN(\mathbf{x};\;\mathbf{y}^{in})
, by evaluating the recurrence relation

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{y}_{1}&=\varphi\left(W_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}+\mathbf{b}_{0}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}_{k+1}&=\varphi\left(W_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\mathbf{y}^{out}&=\varphi\left(W_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}_{n_{L}}+\mathbf{b}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

(13)

Remark 4.1
.

The above definition extends to convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
as well, using Toeplitz structured
W_{k}
matrices and
b_{k}

vectors. We discuss this and the application of FAS to CNNs in
Section
7
.

Training the network selects the weight matrices
W=\{W_{k}\}
and bias
vectors
\mathbf{b}=\{\mathbf{b}_{k}\}
by approximately solving the optimization
problem

g(\mathbf{x})=g(W,\mathbf{b})=\sum_{s=1}^{N}L_{s}(DNN(\mathbf{x};\;\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s})),

(14)

where
L_{s}
is some loss function associated with the
s
th training sample.

One example of a
loss functional
is the mean-squared error

g(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits^{N}_{s=1}\|\mathbf{y}^{out}_{s}-DNN(\mathbf{x};\;\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s})\|^{2}.

(15)

In the present setting, to train the DNN means to find a set of parameters
\mathbf{x}=(W,\;\mathbf{b})
that approximately minimize the above functional
g(\mathbf{x})
. However, as discussed in Section
2.2.1
, one does not generally wish to exactly minimize this functional due to overfitting concerns. Rather, one attempts to find a set of parameters that approximately minimize
g(\mathbf{x})
, and achieve a small but nonzero gradient.

The popularity of the DNNs is due to the fact that the gradients of
the loss functional
\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
are readily computable by a
backpropagation
, and a number of popular libraries such as
TensorFlow
[
20
]
and PyTorch
[
1
]
readily compute them. Moreover,
in the optimization algorithms employed in the DNN training we may use
only a portion of the sum in (
15
) (or
(
14
)) by employing randomness. That is, for a
minibatch

of
m
randomly selected indices
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\;\dots,\;s_{m})
, we
compute the gradient of

g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits^{m}_{j=1}L_{s_{j}}(DNN(\mathbf{x};\;\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s_{j}})).

(16)

This partial (stochastic) gradient,
\nabla g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x})
, is used to update the iterates
\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{x}-\tau\nabla g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x})
for a chosen step length
\tau\in(0,1]
, in a gradient descent algorithm to minimize
g(\mathbf{x})
.

5
Restriction, Interpolation and
\tau
-correction operators for the DNN training

In this section, we motivate and define our restriction and
interpolation operators, as well as the
\tau
-correction for the case
of training DNNs, in a manner consistent with the guidelines of Section
3.3
.

First, we clarify notation. Consider a DNN with the vector quantity

\mathbf{x}
containing its learnable parameters as in
Equation (
12
). To define a two-level FAS algorithm we
need an interpolation mapping
{\mathbf{P}}
and a fine-to-coarse restriction
mapping
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
such that
\mathbf{Q}={\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
is a projection. We will
choose
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}{\mathbf{P}}=I
.

As the parameters of a DNN are defined layerwise, we will also
decompose the
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
operators in this manner. Consider the
sets
P=\{P_{k}\}^{n_{L}+1}_{k=0}
and
\pi=\{\pi_{k}\}^{n_{L}+1}_{k=0}
,
where
P_{k}
and
\pi_{k}
act on vectors associated with the
k
th
layer; that is, given a data vector
\mathbf{y}_{k}
at layer
k
,
\pi_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
converts
\mathbf{y}_{k}
to a coarsened
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{c}
. We set
\pi_{in}=\pi_{0}
,
P_{in}=P_{0}
and
\pi_{out}=\pi_{n_{L}+1},P_{out}=P_{n_{L}+1}
. We also assume that for each
k
,
\pi_{k}
and
P_{k}
make a
projection, that is,

\pi_{k}P_{k}=I.

(17)

Given a set of coarse parameters
\{W^{c}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\}
, neural networks in fact provide a natural way to produce a coarse functional
g_{c}
that satisfies Guideline (A) of Section
3.3
. We simply define a coarse DNN as a coarse version of the same recursion as in (
13
):

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{1}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{0}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k+1}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}_{n_{L}}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

(18)

That is, we have
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}=DNN_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c};\;\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c})
, where
\mathbf{x}_{c}
combines all parameters
\{W^{c}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\}
as in Equation (
12
).

The training objective function is a summation over a coarse set of training samples:

g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=g_{c}(W^{c},b^{c})=\sum_{s=1}^{N}L^{c}_{s}(DNN_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c};\;\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c,s})).

(19)

If we do not have coarse counterparts of the training samples
\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}
and
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}
, we use the only given (fine level) set of samples, i.e., we let

\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c,s}=\mathbf{y}^{in}_{s}
and
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c,s}=\mathbf{y}^{out}_{s}
. This implies that we let
\pi_{0}=I,P_{0}=I
and
\pi_{n_{L}+1}=I,P_{n_{L}+1}=I
.
Also, in that case we have
L^{c}_{s}=L_{s}
. Because
g_{c}
is designed to approximate
g
here, Guideline (A) is satsified.

In what follows, we construct a coarse DNN from a fine DNN parameters; and vice versa, given a coarse DNN, we embed it in a finer DNN.

5.1
Fine-to-coarse DNN restriction

Let us assume that
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-P_{k}\pi_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}\|
is small in the
sense discussed in Section
3.3
. Then the following
is an approximation to the standard DNN recurrence (
13
):

\begin{array}[]{rl}\pi_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}&=\pi_{1}\varphi\left(W_{0}P_{0}\pi_{in}\mathbf{y}^{in}+\mathbf{b}_{0}\right),\\
\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{y}_{k+1}&=\pi_{k+1}\varphi\left(W_{k}P_{k}\pi_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\pi_{out}\mathbf{y}^{out}&=\pi_{out}\varphi\left(W_{n_{L}}P_{n_{L}}\pi_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}_{n_{L}}+\mathbf{b}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

(20)

By letting
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}=\pi_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
, we have the following recurrence involving coarse vectors
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{1}&=\pi_{1}\varphi\left(W_{0}P_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}+\mathbf{b}_{0}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k+1}&=\pi_{k+1}\varphi\left(W_{k}P_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}&=\pi_{n_{L}+1}\varphi\left(W_{n_{L}}P_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{n_{L}}+\mathbf{b}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

(21)

Finally, we assume the approximation

\varphi(\pi\mathbf{z})\approx\pi\varphi(\mathbf{z}).

(22)

We discuss in Section
5.4
when this is valid. Given this,
recurrence (
21
) can be
approximated as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{1}&=\varphi\left(\pi_{1}W_{0}P_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}+\pi_{1}\mathbf{b}_{0}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k+1}&=\varphi\left(\pi_{k+1}W_{k}P_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}+\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{b}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}&=\varphi\left(\pi_{n_{L}+1}W_{n_{L}}P_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{n_{L}}+\pi_{n_{L}}\mathbf{b}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

That is, letting

W^{c}_{k}=\pi_{k+1}W_{k}P_{k}\text{ and }\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}=\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{b}_{k},

(23)

we end up with the recurrence that implements the actions of a coarse DNN,
\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}\mapsto\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}=DNN_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c};\;\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c})
:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{1}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{0}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k+1}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\right),\;\text{ for }0\leq k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}&=\varphi\left(W^{c}_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{n_{L}}+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

(24)

This motivates our fine-to-coarse mapping,
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
, which we define as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{x}=&[(W_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}_{k})],\\
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}=&[(\pi_{k+1}W_{k}P_{k}),\;(\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{b}_{k})].\end{array}

(25)

Note that, despite the layer-wise decomposition, this is still a
linear mapping of
\mathbf{x}
, so there exists a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
whose action
is this.

5.2
Coarse-to-fine DNN prolongation

Consider the coarse DNN recurrence (
24
). We assume the approximation

P\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{c})\approx\varphi(P\mathbf{z}_{c}),

(26)

discussed in Section
5.4
, and using the property
\pi_{k}P_{k}=I
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rll}P_{1}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{1}&=P_{1}\varphi\left(W^{c}_{0}\pi_{0}(P_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c})+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{0}\right)&\approx\varphi\left(P_{1}W^{c}_{0}\pi_{0}(P_{0}\mathbf{y}^{in}_{c})+P_{1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{0}\right),\\
P_{k+1}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k+1}&=P_{k+1}\varphi\left(W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k}(P_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k})+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\right)&\approx\varphi\left(P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k}(P_{k}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{k})+P_{k+1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}\right),\;k\leq n_{L}-1,\\
P_{out}\mathbf{y}^{out}_{c}&=P_{out}\varphi\left(W^{c}_{n_{L}}\pi_{n_{L}}(P_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{n_{L}})+\mathbf{b}^{c}_{n_{L}}\right)&\approx\varphi\left(P_{n_{L}+1}W^{c}_{n_{L}}\pi_{n_{L}}(P_{n_{L}}\mathbf{y}^{c}_{n_{L}})+P_{n_{L}+1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{n_{L}}\right).\end{array}

The latter formulas motivates us to define the interpolation mapping
{\mathbf{P}}
as follows.
Let
\mathbf{x}_{c}=[(W^{c}_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})]
, then

{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}=[(P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k}),\;(P_{k+1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})].

(27)

Note that this does not require that the
W_{k}
matrices be square. It only requires that the input dimension to layer
k
match the output dimension of layer
k-1
.

5.3
The restriction mapping
\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}

The definition of
\mathbf{R}
is already given by (
9
). The layerwise definition is

\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}=[(P^{T}_{k+1}W_{k}\pi^{T}_{k}),\;(P^{T}_{k}\mathbf{b}_{k})].

(28)

The following result, satisfying Guideline (B) of Section
3.3
, holds.

Lemma 5.1
.

The restriction mapping
\mathbf{R}
defined in (
28
) and the interpolation mapping
{\mathbf{P}}
defined in (
27
) are transpose to each other, i.e.,

\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}.

Proof.

We start with the inner product of
\mathbf{x}=[(W_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}_{k})]
and
{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}={\mathbf{P}}[(W^{c}_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})]
,

(\mathbf{x}_{c})^{T}({\mathbf{P}}^{T}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{T}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})=\sum\limits_{i}x_{i}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})_{i}.

With
\mathbf{b}_{k}=(b_{k,i})
,
\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}=(b^{c}_{k,i_{c}})
,
W_{k}=\left((W_{k})_{r,s}\right)
and
W^{c}_{k}=\left((W^{c}_{k})_{r_{c},s_{c}}\right)
, based on the Definition (
27
), we have the representation

\mathbf{x}^{T}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})=\sum\limits_{i}x_{i}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})_{i}=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i}b_{k,i}(P_{k}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})_{i}+\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i=(r,s)}(W_{k})_{r,s}(P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k})_{r,s}.

(29)

The first term on the right gives

\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i}b_{k,i}(P_{k}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})_{i}=\sum\limits_{k}(\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})^{T}P^{T}_{k}\mathbf{b}_{k}=\sum\limits_{k}(\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})^{T}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}.

(30)

For the second term, by rearranging terms and using the definition (
28
) of
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}
,
we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i=(r,s)}(W_{k})_{r,s}(P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k})_{r,s}&=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i=(r,s)}\sum\limits_{i_{c}=(r_{c},s_{c})}(W_{k})_{r,s}(P_{k+1})_{r,r_{c}}(W^{c}_{k})_{r_{c},s_{c}}(\pi_{k})_{s_{c},s}\\
&=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i_{c}=(r_{c},s_{c})}(W^{c}_{k})_{i_{c}}\sum\limits_{r,s}(P_{k+1})_{r,r_{c}}(W_{k})_{r,s}(\pi_{k})_{s_{c},s}\\
&=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i_{c}=(r_{c},s_{c})}(W^{c}_{k})_{i_{c}}(P^{T}_{k+1}W_{k}\pi^{T}_{k})_{i_{c}}\\
&=\sum\limits_{k}\sum\limits_{i_{c}}(W^{c}_{k})_{i_{c}}(W^{c}_{k})_{i_{c}}.\end{array}

(31)

Thus substituting (
30
) and (
31
) in (
29
), using formulas (
28
) defining the components of
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}
, we arrive at

(\mathbf{x}_{c})^{T}({\mathbf{P}}^{T}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{T}({\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c})=(\mathbf{x}_{c})^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x},

which proves the desired result.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

We note that the linear mappings
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
,
{\mathbf{P}}
, as well as
\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}
are not explicitly formed as matrices. However, from their definitions it is clear that their actions are readily available
via the actions of the layer matrices
P_{k}
,
\pi_{k}
and their transposes.
That is, in an implementation, only the layer matrices
\pi_{k}
and
P_{k}
need to be constructed and stored explicitly as (sparse) matrices.

Lemma 5.2
.

With the definitions (
25
) of
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
and (
27
) of
{\mathbf{P}}
, assuming (
17
) for the layer matrices
\pi_{k}
and
P_{k}
, the following main property holds

{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}{\mathbf{P}}=I,

which implies that
\mathbf{Q}={\mathbf{P}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
is a projection (
\mathbf{Q}^{2}=\mathbf{Q}
).

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{x}_{c}=[(W^{c}_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k})]
, define
\mathbf{x}=[(W_{k}),\;(\mathbf{b}_{k})]={\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}
, i.e.,
W_{k}=P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}=P_{k+1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}
.
Then for
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}=[(\pi_{k+1}W_{k}P_{k}),\;(\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{b}_{k})]
since
\pi_{j}P_{j}=I
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\pi_{k+1}W_{k}P_{k}&=\pi_{k+1}P_{k+1}W^{c}_{k}\pi_{k}P_{k}=W^{c}_{k},\\
\pi_{k+1}\mathbf{b}_{k}&=\pi_{k+1}P_{k+1}\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}=\mathbf{b}^{c}_{k}.\end{array}

That is,
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{c}=\mathbf{x}_{c}
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.4
A simple choice for
\pi_{k}
and
P_{k}

Finally, we must choose
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
and
{\mathbf{P}}
to satisfy Guideline (C) of Section
3.3
. Intuitively, if two neurons have nearly the same weights and biases, then we could average them together to construct a coarse neuron that approximates the two fine neurons. Therefore, we choose
\pi_{k}
to perform pairwise averaging and
P_{k}
to perform piecewise constant interpolation over selected pairwise aggregates.
The choice of the pairwise aggregates is done through a Heavy Edge Matching (HEM) method
[
14
]
.
The mappings are simply written as follows

P_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
&1\\
&1\\
&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n^{c}_{k}},\quad\pi_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
&&1/2&1/2\\
&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{c}_{k}\times n_{k}}.

(32)

When the activation function
\varphi
is the commonly-used ReLU function,

\varphi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}x,&x\geq 0\\
0,&x<0\end{array}\right.,

(33)

these choices for
P_{k}
and
\pi_{k}
justify the approximations
(
22
) and (
26
). In particular, (
22
) is valid because the HEM (discussed in Section
5.5
and shown in Algorithm
1
) matches together neurons whose parameter vectors point in approximately the same direction, and as a result, tend to produce positive and negative dot products with input vectors at the same time. The nonlinearity of the ReLU depends entirely on that positive/negative property of its input, and so (
22
) will be exactly true except when dot products with paired neurons produce different signs. Similarly, (
26
) is in fact an equality, by design, due to the piecewise-constant prolongation. Other activation functions are likely effective here as well; in general, if we approximate
\varphi(\mathbf{z})
near
\mathbf{z}
linearly with a
matrix

\widehat{\varphi}
, then (
26
) holds when
\pi\widehat{\varphi}\approx\widehat{\varphi}\pi
. In particular, other piecewise-linear activation functions such as the ”leaky ReLU” satisfy this.

A variant to (
32
) that is often found better is to
scale
P_{k}
by the row norms of
W_{k}
, i.e.,

\tilde{P}_{k}=D_{k}P_{k},\quad\tilde{\pi}_{k}=(P_{k}^{T}D_{k}P_{k})^{-1}P_{k}^{T},

(34)

where
D_{k}
is the diagonal matrix that contains the row norms of

W_{k}
, so that the rows that correspond to the paired neurons in an aggregate are invariant
with the projector
Q

if they are linear dependent,
i.e., they have the maximum similarity score in the HEM algorithm.
Apparently, (
32
) is a special case where

D_{k}=I
.
Furthermore,
P_{k}^{T}D_{k}P_{k}
is a diagonal matrix due to the structures of
P_{k}
and
D_{k}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Although we write Equations (
32
) and (
34
) as matrices, these are very sparse matrices and the
actions
of these matrices can be efficiently computed without ever directly forming
\tilde{P}_{k}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{k}
. In our software, we have implemented matrix-free versions for fast, practical computation.

5.6
Constructing a tau-correction

As discussed above, the tau correction in optimization is
\tau=\nabla g_{c}({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{R}\nabla g(x)
, where
\mathbf{R}={\mathbf{P}}^{T}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
are the two linear fine-to-coarse mappings,(
28
)-(
25
), defined in our DNN setting.
Note that the operation
\mathbf{R}\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
is
well-defined, as
\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
has the same structure as
\mathbf{x}
.

One wrinkle is that the calculation of a gradient requires a summation
over all training samples in the training set. Often, training sets
have a large number of samples in them, so computing the true gradient
would be computationally unwieldy. We approximate this by simply
calculating a stochastic tau-correction, in which we average over a
small subset
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\;s_{2},\;\dots,\;s_{m})
of the total training set, and then scale appropriately:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x})&\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}L_{s_{j}}(DNN(\mathbf{x},\;y^{in}_{s_{j}})\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{c}}g^{c}_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})&\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{c}}L^{c}_{s_{j}}(DNN_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c},y^{in}_{c,s_{j}})\\
\mathbf{\tau}&\displaystyle=\frac{N}{m}\left(\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{c}}g^{c}_{\mathbf{s}}\right)({\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{R}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x})\right),\end{array}

(35)

where
m
is the size of a tau batch,
s_{j}
is the index of the

j
’th sample in the tau batch,
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}
stands for a gradient with respect to variable
\mathbf{y}
, and
N
is the number of minibatches in the dataset as a whole.

Due to this stochastic approximation, we in general will never construct a coarse problem such that
\nabla\widehat{g}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=0
exactly. This is consistent with how neural networks are trained: Specifically, because traditional training is performed in a stochastic manner over minibatches, exact gradients are never computed, and one never achieves an
\mathbf{x}
such that
\nabla g(\mathbf{x})=0
exactly, or even iteratively converges to such a point. In fact, this behavior is often considered desirable, see Section
2.2.1
.

6
The DNN FAS Algorithm

The original objective function
g
is defined in Equation (
14
), and the
auxiliary objective function in Equation (
19
). Given a tau correction, we can
construct a tau-corrected auxiliary problem like in Equation (
3
).

We recall that the FAS step cannot be our only method for improving

\mathbf{x}
. As discussed earlier, Remark
3.1
, we need
a correction in a space complementary to the coarse space

\text{Range}({\mathbf{P}})
, a step customarily referred to as

smoothing
. That is because
\mathbf{x}_{c}
exists in a
reduced-dimension space and so cannot represent all the possible
changes we might make.

There exist a wide variety of methods one might choose for the
smoothing step. In general, it should be cost-efficient and
effectively target the complement to
\text{Range}({\mathbf{P}})
. The neural network community has already provided a number of efficient first-order training methods which can be leveraged for smoothing. We choose here to use stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Our reason for using this is that SGD in this context is quite analogous to the commonly-used Jacobi smoother in traditional multigrid methods. Indeed, when applying the Full Approximation Scheme to the deterministic problem of minimizing quadratic forms, (deterministic) gradient descent is exactly a Richardson iteration, very similar to the Jacobi iteration commonly used as a multigrid smoother
[
5
]
. A two-level FAS V-cycle, then, consists of the following steps:

1.

Apply one or more iterations of SGD to the original problem
g(\mathbf{x})=g(W,\mathbf{b})
.

2.

Calculate the stochastic tau correction
\mathbf{\tau}
.

3.

Apply the restriction operation to calculate the elements of the coarse neural network
W^{c},\mathbf{b}^{c}
.

4.

Apply one or more iterations of SGD to the corrected auxiliary problem
{\widehat{g}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})={\widehat{g}}(W^{c},\mathbf{b}^{c})
(see (
3
)).

5.

Apply the prolongation operation
{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})
and then the FAS update
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}_{new}=\mathbf{x}+{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})
.

6.

Apply one or more iterations of SGD to the original problem
g(\mathbf{x})=g(W,b)
.

FAS V-cycles of greater than two levels are achieved through recursion at Step 4.

6.1
A Few Notes on Algorithmic Tuning

6.1.1
Minibatch Selection

We perform minibatch selection in a manner intended to closely match that of traditional one-level training. We first shuffle the entire training dataset, and then proceed by cycling through the dataset. When computing the stochastic tau correction, we draw the next
m
minibatches in shuffled order, compute
\mathbf{\tau}
over those
m
minibatches as in Equation (
35
), and then use those same
m
minibatches for SGD at the coarse level. A new set of
m
minibatches are drawn when restricting to the next-coarser level. Because SGD at the coarse level is performed using the same minibatches as were used for the tau-correction, one might view this as a
stochastic V-cycle
. Because multigrid V-cycles typically only apply a small number of smoothing steps at each level, we tend to keep
m
fairly small as well, often between 2 and 8.

6.1.2
Momentum

Momentum
[
25
]
is an important component in training methods, as it helps to smooth out variations and move parameters more consistently in directions that matter. In fact, SGD with momentum (and many traditional training methods) use gradient information to update momentum vectors, and only directly update parameter vectors through momentum. In our setting, important information about desirable directions of movement is learned at the coarse levels, and we should not throw this important information away upon prolongation. Therefore, we modify this approach slightly to restrict and prolong
momentum
as well as network parameters. As there is a momentum parameter for each network parameter, a momentum vector
\mathbf{m}
has the same structure as a parameter vector
\mathbf{x}
.

Upon restriction, we also restrict the momentum vector using the same restriction operator
\mathbf{m}_{c}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{m}
, and upon prolongation, we perform the same coarse-grid correction

\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}+{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{m}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{m}).

(36)

We show the two-level FAS algorithm, with multilevel momentum, in Algorithm
2
.

6.1.3
HEM Re-Matching

The HEM matching in Algorithm
1
depends on the weights, which change during the training process. Therefore, matchings tend to change somewhat throughout the training process. We find that it is generally beneficial to periodically re-compute matchings, but that the training is not particularly sensitive to the rate of re-matching. In our tests, we choose to re-match at a rate that keeps the computational cost of matching minimal compared to the overall training cost, such as every 10 or 50 V-Cycles.

6.1.4
Maintaining Stability

In some cases, this multilevel hierarchies developed here can cause instabilities, and in severe cases, divergence of the method. Here we discuss a number of hyperparameters that can be used in the method to maintain stability when this happens:

•

The learning rate can be decreased at coarser levels of the hierarchy. We often choose to decrease the learning rate by a fixed factor
\eta
at each hierarchy level. This can prevent the coarse parameters from moving outside the area in which the coarse problem approximates the fine problem effectively. However, the learning rate does not impact the stored momentum vector
[
11
]
, so that must be stabilized separately.

•

The coarse-grid parameter correction and coarse-grid momentum correction in Algorithm
2
and in Equations (
4
), (
36
) can be scaled down with parameters
\alpha_{p},\alpha_{m}\in(0,1]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\leftarrow

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{p}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}\leftarrow

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}+\alpha_{m}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{m}_{c}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\mathbf{m}).

This is akin to the
\alpha
parameter used in the line search in MG/OPT
[
21
]
; however, we use constants here rather than the line search of MG/OPT due to the added computational cost.

•

The
\tau
-correction in Algorithm
2
and in Equation (
3
) can be scaled down by
\gamma\in[0,1]
, so that

{\widehat{g}_{c}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})=g_{c}(\mathbf{x}_{c})-\gamma\mathbf{x}_{c}^{T}\mathbf{\tau}.

Because our
\tau
-correction is stochastic, even when we are near the optimum, any given set of minibatches could potentially result in a much larger
\mathbf{\tau}
, which could then cause an inappropriate coarse-grid correction.

We note that in MG/OPT
[
21
]
, it is also suggested one could add a quadratic penalty to the coarse problem to prevent too much movements; however, MG/OPT expects one to fully solve the coarse problem, whereas we only take a few SGD steps. In our experiments, tuning the weight of that quadratic penalty caused its own stability challenges, so we do not use that strategy.

When making these adjustments in Section
8
, we typically choose
\eta\in[\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}],\alpha_{p}=1,\alpha_{m}=0.2,\gamma=0.125
. These were chosen by trial and error.

7
Extension to Convolutional Neural Networks

In this section we describe how the above method can be extended from
fully-connected networks to convolutional neural networks (CNNs). A
CNN typically consists of one or more convolutional layers followed by
one or more fully-connected layers. They are often effective in
learning patterns over array-structured data. In a CNN, the object
analogous to a fully-connected neuron is the
channel
;
therefore, our goal in this section will be to develop methods for
coarsening over the channels of a CNN. The resulting convolution
operators will have the same width and stride, but the number of
channels in each layer will be restricted.

Convolutional operators are often represented as higher-order tensors,
but this tensor acts on the inputs in a linear fashion, and so the
action can be represented as matrix-vector multiplication. As a
result, convolutional layers are mathematically quite similar to
fully-connected layers, and the above method extends in a
straightforward manner. That is, the action of a convolutional layer
can still be viewed in the form

\mathbf{y}_{k+1}=\varphi(W_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{b}_{k}),

where
W_{k}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}
have a particular structure.

To apply our restriction and prolongation operators, we require an
understanding of the structure of the matrix
W
associated with a
convolutional layer. This depends on the dimension of the input array,
the number of input channels, and the number of output channels. We
build up our understanding progressively as follows:

•

Consider input
\mathbf{y}
to a convolutional layer that is a
1-dimensional array and has a single channel; for example, audio data
has this structure. For simplicity, suppose we have a convolutional
layer with a single convolutional channel, which we represent as a
vector
\mathbf{c}=(c)_{i=-\ell}^{\ell}
and a scalar
b
. Then the action of
the convolution is

\sum_{j=-\ell}^{\ell}y(i+\ell-j)\cdot c(j)+b.

(37)

We can represent this as a matrix-vector multiplication
W\mathbf{y}
where
W
is the banded Toeplitz matrix

W=\begin{bmatrix}c_{-\ell}&c_{-\ell+1}&\cdots&c_{k-1}&c_{k}&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&c_{-\ell}&c_{-\ell+1}&\cdots&c_{-1}&c_{k}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&c_{-\ell}&c_{-\ell+1}&\cdots&c_{-1}&c_{k}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&&&&&&&\vdots\end{bmatrix}.

(38)

•

More complex convolutional layers extend this in a natural
way. One-dimensional, single-channel arrays with multiple
convolutional channels results in a matrix

W=[W^{1};\cdots;W^{p}]^{T}

that is a column of banded Toeplitz blocks.

•

Two-dimensional, single-channel arrays with a single
convolutional channel produces a weight matrix
W
that has banded
block-Toeplitz structure. See Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The banded block-Toeplitz structure of a convolutional weight matrix with a single channel applied to a 2D input with a single channel.

•

Adding additional output channels creates a block-column matrix
in which each block has this structure. Thus, two-dimensional,
single-channel arrays with multiple convolutional channels produce a
column of matrix blocks each of the form of Figure
1
. We might say this then has
block banded block
Toeplitz
structure.

•

Finally, multi-dimensional input arrays with multiple inputs
channels and multiple output channels results in a weight matrix
W

of the form

W=\begin{bmatrix}W^{1,1}&W^{1,2}&\cdots&W^{1,\beta}\\
\cdots&\vdots&&\cdots\\
W_{\alpha,1}&W_{\alpha,2}&\cdots&W^{\alpha,\beta}\end{bmatrix}

(39)

in which each submatrix
W^{i,j}
is a matrix of the form of
Figure
1
.

A key observation here is that every block row corresponds to an
output channel and every block column corresponds to an input
channel. As each channel (block row) here is analogous to a neuron
(individual row) in a fully-connected layer, we see that, analogous
to Section
5.4
, we can coarsen two channels
together by simply averaging their channels together.

Therefore, we see that applying Algorithm
2
to
CNNs proceeds in exactly the same manner. Restrictions occurs through
averaging channels as in
\pi_{k}
of Equation (
32
), and
prolongation occurs through piecewise constant interpolation of
channels as in
P_{k}
of Equation (
32
).

8
Experiments

In this section we demonstrate the use of our multilevel FAS trainer on a set of PDE-based regression problems to compare this to SGD. These experiments were performed on the Lassen compute cluster at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. For each experiment, we used a single IBM Power9 nodes with 44 CPU cores at 3.5 Ghz and 256 GB of memory and a single NVIDIA V100 Voltage graphics card. What we find with these results is that in some cases the use of FAS can improve
L_{2}
loss, and in all cases it achieves
L_{2}
loss at least as good as SGD. In addition, in all cases tested, it
also
improves upon
L_{\infty}
loss even though the training was against
L_{2}
. This implies that FAS-trained neural networks has better worst-case performance than those trained with traditional SGD, suggesting better generalization power.

Note that in these experiments, to achieve a more faithful comparison in computational work, we borrow a strategy from the multigrid literature and report
work units
rather than epochs. A single work unit, as we define it, is the computational cost to compute a forward and backpropagation over one minibatch. We do this because coarser levels of the multilevel hierarchy are less expensive to compute over; as a rule of thumb, half as many neurons in each level implies weight matrices with 1/4 as many elements, and so computation at a coarse level requires roughly 1/4 as much work as the next-finer level. For example, if we perform a V-Cycle over a 2-level hierarchy with 2 minibatch presmoothing iterations, 2 minibatch coarse grid iterations, and then 2 minibatch postsmoothing iterations, that costs 4.5 work units. In addition, computing the tau-correction requires both a fine-level and a coarse-level forward and backpropagation over each minibatch that will be used at the coarse level; in our example, that would add an additional 2.5 work units, for a total of 7 work units for the V-Cycle. In general, if
s
is the cost of a forward/backward pass, then work units can be computed as

\displaystyle\mbox{(smoothing cost)}+\mbox{(tau correction work for current level)}+\mbox{(tau correction work for next level)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}(1/4)^{i}\cdot 2s+(1/4)^{k-1}\cdot s\right)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}(1/4)^{i}\cdot 2s+(1/4)^{k-1}\cdot s\right)+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-3}(1/4)^{i}\cdot 2s+(1/4)^{k-1}\cdot s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6s-10s/4^{k-1}.

(40)

We see that, for any hierarchy depth, Work Units is bounded from above by
6s
.

As discussed in Section
2.2.1
, deep learning, despite being formalized in the same way as a traditional optimization problem, has different goals from traditional optimization. Most significantly, the actual objective function of interest, the neural network’s performance over the entire population of
possible
data, is inaccessible, and the training performance is only an approximation to this. For this reason, the goal is not to achieve asymptotic convergence to a fixed minimum point, but rather to achieve good performance on a validation set.

The momentum coarse-grid correction scaling parameter is set to

•

The tau correction scaling parameter is set to

.

•

This is especially true when considering
L_{\infty}

9
Conclusion

In this paper we have presented a multilevel-in-width method for training neural networks by developing a variation of the Full Approximation Scheme as originally used in Algebraic Multigrid. We have shown that DNNs trained in this way for regression problems can achieve better generalization power by improving on multiple validation performance metrics. We also observe that, in some cases, the first auxiliary network achieves better worst-case performance than the original network, likely due to having less opportunity for overfitting.

This paper is perhaps most interesting in the context of
[
27
]
and
[
7
]
, which also showed regularization benefits to multilevel training even though all have taken quite different approaches. Future investigations should include work that seeks to more-fully understand the settings in which multilevel training confers regularization benefits, and to develop principled approaches for making specific algorithmic choices in those settings.

Our open-source PyTorch-based software, MTNN, to implement these methods, is written in a modular fashion to facilitate the study of different algorithmic choices and is available on Github at
https://github.com/LLNL/MTNN
. We have also provided open-source data and drivers that allow users to easily recreate our experiments. Readers are encouraged to download and use our software.
