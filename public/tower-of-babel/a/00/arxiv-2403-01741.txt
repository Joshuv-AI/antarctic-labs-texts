Abstract
. In this paper we investigate the improved Caccioppoli inequality and the reverse Hölder inequality for gradients of weak solutions to nonhomogeneous parabolic systems whose coefficients can be split into a complex-valued and bounded part, which also satisfies the uniform G a ̊ \mathring{a} rding inequality, and a real and anti-symmetric part in BMO. In particular, unbounded coefficients are allowed.



1.
Introduction

In
[
3
]
, Auscher, Bortz, Egert and Saari considered the local regularity of weak solutions to non-homogeneous parabolic systems of the form

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u-\mbox{div}_{x}(A(t,x)\nabla_{x}u)=f+\mbox{div}_{x}F.

Such a system is defined in an open parabolic cylinder
\Omega:=I_{0}\times Q_{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}:={\mathbb{R}}\times{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}},
where
I_{0}
denotes an open interval in
{\mathbb{R}},
and
Q_{0}
is an open ball of
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}.
The coefficents are assumed to be elliptic in the G
\mathring{a}
rding sense, bounded and measurable, notably, without any regularity on time as well as on all spatial variables. In their paper, they revealed a new regularity property (primarily referring to
[
3
, Theorem 8.1]
) of weak solutions to (
1.1
), in contrast to the results in
[
25
,
28
,
16
]
. This property can be roughly summarized as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
u
is a locally weak solution to (
1.1
) with
f
only belonging to
L^{2_{*}}_{loc}(\Omega)
(
2_{*}<2
) and
F\in L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega)
, then in time
u
is bounded and Hölder continuous locally with values in spatial
L^{p}_{loc}(Q_{0})
for some
p>2.

As mentioned in
[
3
, Section 1.2]
, their results also generalize certain statements in
[
9
,
16
]
.

Unlike the usual way, which relies on the local variational methods rooted in the Lions theory
[
25
]
to construct the regularity properties of solutions to parabolic systems, the authors
[
3
]
introduced a global variational approach. This methods utilizes the idea of splitting
\partial_{t}=D_{t}^{1/2}H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}
and explores the hidden coercivity within a parabolic energy space. This technique was first proposed by Kaplan
[
23
]
and later studied for boundary value problems (BVPs) in
[
19
,
6
,
2
]
. Equipped with such an approach, two distinct proofs for Theorem
1.1
were presented: a real analysis proof in
[
3
, Section 6]
, based on a non-local Gehring’s lemma, and an operator theoretic proof in
[
3
, Section 7]
, relying on the
\breve{S}
ne
\breve{I}
berg’s theorem
[
32
]
.

One of the objectives of this work is to show that the bounded assumption imposed on the coefficient matrix
A
in Theorem
1.1
can be removed. More precisely, we study the nonhomogeneous parabolic system with

A(t,x)=S(t,x)+D(t,x),

where
S\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},{\mathbb{C}}^{mn})
is complex-valued and satisfies the G
\mathring{a}
rding inequality (uniformly in
t
), i.e., for any
u\in W^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},{\mathbb{C}}^{m}),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\;\int_{{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}}\;S(t,x)\nabla u(t,x)\cdot\overline{\nabla u(t,x)}

\displaystyle:=\mbox{Re}\;\int_{{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}}\;S^{\alpha\beta}_{kj}(t,x)\partial_{k}u^{\alpha}\overline{\partial_{j}u^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\int_{{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}}|\nabla u|^{2}-\kappa\int_{{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}}|u|^{2}

holds for some
\lambda,\kappa>0.
The matrix
D
is assumed to be real-valued, skew-symmetric and block diagonal, that is,
D=\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}D^{\alpha\beta}\right)_{1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq m},
with
D^{\alpha\beta}
only belonging to the space
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}};BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})).
Hence,
A
is unbounded. Under these hypotheses on
A,
we state our main theorem as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in L_{loc}^{2_{*}}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}}^{m})
and
F\in L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}}^{mn}).
Suppose that
v
is a weak solution of
\partial_{t}v-\mbox{div}((S(t,x)+D(t,x))\nabla v)=f+\mbox{div}F\;\mbox{in}\;\Omega.
Let
\gamma>1
and
I\times Q
be a open paraboli cylinder with
l(I)\approx r(Q)^{2}
such that
\overline{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}\subset\Omega.
If
p>2
is sufficiently close to 2 and
\alpha:=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p},
then

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{I\times Q}|\nabla v|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle+\sup_{t\in I}\left(\fint_{I\times Q}|v(t,\cdot)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+\sup_{t,s\in I}\left(\fint_{Q}\frac{|v(t,\cdot)-v(s,\cdot)|^{p}}{|t-s|^{\alpha p}}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{r(Q)}\left(\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|v|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|F|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+r(Q)\left(\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|f|^{p_{*}}\right)^{1/p_{*}}.

The implicit constant depends only on dimensions,
\|D\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}};BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}))}
,
\gamma,
ellipticity and the constants controlling the ratio
l(I)/r(Q)^{2}.

Theorem 1.3
.

Under the same hypotheses as in Theorem
1.2
, we have

(1.3)

\left(\fint_{I\times Q}|\nabla v|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\lesssim\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|\nabla v|+\left(\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|F|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+r(Q)\left(\fint_{\gamma^{2}I\times\gamma Q}|f|^{p_{*}}\right)^{1/p_{*}},

where the number
p
is greater than 2 but sufficiently close to 2. The implicit constant depends only on
\|D\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}};BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}))}
, dimensions,
\gamma,
ellipticity and the constants controlling the ratio
l(I)/r(Q)^{2}.

Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
serves as natural extensions of
[
3
, Theorem 8.1]
and
[
3
, Theorem 8.2]
, respectively.

Our motivation stems from the investigation of the behavior of solutions of elliptic and parabolic equations with a divergence-free drift
[
27
,
26
,
29
,
33
,
31
]
. Most notably, the authors
[
31
]
studied equations of the form
\partial_{t}-\Delta u+\textbf{b}\cdot\nabla u=0
resp.
-\Delta u+\textbf{b}\cdot\nabla u=0
with
\mbox{div}\textbf{b}=0.
They discovered that the usual regularity assumptions on
b
can be relaxed by the divergence-free condition, and the classical Liouville theorem and Harnack inequality remain valid. It also turns out that the interior theory of De Giorgi, Nash and Moser is preserved for these parabolic operators if
\textbf{b}\in L^{\infty}(BMO^{-1})
resp. elliptic operators if
\textbf{b}\in BMO^{-1}.
For gerneral elliptic and parabolic opertaors with real coefficients in divergence form, this condition is equivalent to the coefficients matrix
A
being expressible as the sum of an
L^{\infty}
elliptic symmetric part and an unbounded anti-symmetric part which belongs to
L^{\infty}(BMO)
in parabolic case resp.
\textbf{b}\in BMO
in elliptic case. The space BMO has a key role in two ways which will be explicitly displayed in following sections as well. First, its right scaling properties enable one to implement the iterative arguments of De Giorgi-Nash-Moser. Second, the BMO condition on the anti-symmetric part of the matrix, combined with the compensated compactness as discussed in
[
10
,
31
,
24
]
, allows for the proper definition of weak solutions.

Operators with a real and skew-symmetric part in
BMO,
of the form
\mathcal{L}:=-\mbox{div}(A\nabla)
or
\mathcal{H}:=\partial_{t}-\mbox{div}(A\nabla),
have gained much attention since
[
31
]
. In
[
30
]
, Qian and Xi showed the existence of the fundamental solution for such parabolic operator
\mathcal{H}
with real coefficients and derived the pointwise Gaussian bounds. The BVPs generated by such an elliptic operator
\mathcal{L},
where the symmetric part in
A
is real, bounded and uniform elliptic, was launched in
[
24
]
. In this work, the authors constructed the Green function and elliptic measure associated to
\mathcal{L},
and proved the boundary Hölder continuity of the solution, the solvability of the continuous Dirichlet problem on non-tangentially accessible (NTA) domains and the well-posedness of the
L^{p}
Dirichlet problem with respect to the elliptic measure. Later, Hofmann, Li, Mayboroda and Pipher
[
21
]
established the solvability of the Dirichlet problem generated by the same operator
\mathcal{L}
with
t
-independent coefficients in upper half space
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n+1}
for some
p\in(1,\infty),
equivalently, the elliptic measure associated to
\mathcal{L}
belongs to the
A_{\infty}
class with respect to the Lebesgue measure. In another direction, for
\mathcal{L}
having a complex-valued, uniform elliptic and symmetric part, Escauriaza and Hofmann
[
12
]
addressed the
L^{2}
Kato square root problem without using Gaussian estimate. Subsequently, adding the real-valued assumption on
A,
Hofmann, Li, Mayboroda and Pipher
[
20
]
generalized these results to the
L^{p}
setting. Regarding the Kato square root problem associated to
\mathcal{H}
with
A=S+D,
where
S\in L^{\infty}
is complex-valued and uniform elliptic, and
D\in L^{\infty}(BMO)
is real and anti-symmetric, Ataei and Nyström
[
2
]
provided a positive answer in
L^{2}
case.

The aforementioned results are centrally concerned with generalizations to BMO skew-symmetric part for elliptic and parabolic equations. There is not much work on such an extension to elliptic and parabolic systems. It is important to emphasize that Dong and Kim
[
11
]
extended the results on fundamental solutions presented in
[
30
]
to second-order parabolic systems with real coefficients, under the assumption that weak solutions of these systems satisfy a specific local boundedness estimate. This advancement has been extended to scenarios involving complex-valued coefficients in the work of Auscher and Egert
[
5
]
. To our knowlege, this represents the most recent progress in the study of parabolic sysytems featuring a BMO skew-symmetric part. Therefore, our results have provided an update in this regard.

In the next Section 2 we introduce relevant notation. Section 3 is devoted to the global variational setup associated to parabolic operators with a BMO skew-symmetric part. In Section 4 the improved Caccioppoli inequality is established. Building on this result, we provide the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
in Section 5. An enhanced version of the Gehring lemma is included in Section 6 as an appendix.

2.
Preliminaries and basic notations

For simplicity, we abbreviate
\nabla:=\nabla_{x}
and
\mbox{div}:=\mbox{div}_{x}
with respect to the gradient and divergence in the spatial variables
x
, respectively. For any
1<p<\infty,

p^{*}
and
p_{*},
defined by

\frac{1}{p^{*}}:=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{n+2},\quad\frac{1}{p_{*}}:=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{n+2},

are separately referred to as the Sobolev upper and lower conjugate. As usual,
p^{\prime}
denotes the Hölder conjugate of
p
, then
2^{*}=2_{*}^{\prime}.

2.1.  Weak solutions.
Given
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega;{\mathbb{C}}^{m})
and
F\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega;{\mathbb{C}}^{mn}),
we say that
u
is a weak solution to

\partial_{t}u-\mbox{div}((S(t,x)+D(t,x))\nabla u)=f+\mbox{div}F\quad\mbox{in}\;\Omega

if
u\in L^{2}_{loc}(I;W^{1,2}_{loc}(Q;{\mathbb{C}}^{m}))
satisfies

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}(S+D)\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla\varphi}-u\overline{\partial_{t}\varphi}=\int_{\Omega}f\cdot\overline{\varphi}-F\cdot\overline{\nabla\varphi}\quad\mbox{for any}\;\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

2.2.  Function spaces.
The operators
D_{t}^{1/2}
and
H_{t}
represent the half-order time derivative and Hilbert transform in time through the Fourier symbols
|\tau|^{1/2}
and
isgn(\tau),
respectively. We define the space
H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
as the set of functions
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
such that
D_{t}^{1/2}f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}).
Obviously, the space of smooth and compactly supported functions
C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
serves as a dense subspace of
H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})).
The function space
V:=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};W^{1,2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})),
endowed with

\|u\|_{V}:=\left(\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

is a Hilbert space, which gives rise to a natural space for global weak solutions to parabolic equations. Furthermore, we also need the Hilbert space
E:=V\cap H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})):=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}};W^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))\cap H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
with norm defined by

\|u\|_{E}:=\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.

2.3.  Floating constants.
For ease of presentation, we adopt the notations
\lesssim
and
\approx.
Specifically, for two positive constants
a,b
, the expression
a\lesssim b
means that there exists a nonessential constant
C
such that
a\leq Cb
, while
a\approx b
signifies that both
a\leq Cb
and
b\leq Ca
hold. The value of the constant
C
might change at each occurrence which depends upon
n,\lambda,\kappa
and other nonessential constants.

3.
The global variational setup

We start this section by referencing two key propositions. The first one is derived from
[
24
,
31
]
and is referred to as the compensated compactness estimate.. The second one proved in
[
20
]
is a variant of it. These are indispensable tools for addressing the contributions of the anti-symmetric BMO part in
A
.

Proposition 3.1
.

(
[
24
,
31
]
)  Let
1<p<\infty,

u\in\dot{W}^{1,p}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
and
v\in\dot{W}^{1,p^{\prime}}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}).
Then for any
1\leq k,j\leq n,

\partial_{k}u\partial_{j}v-\partial_{j}u\partial_{k}v
belongs to Hardy space
\mathcal{H}^{1}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
and

\|\partial_{k}u\partial_{j}v-\partial_{j}u\partial_{k}v\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}.

Proposition 3.2
.

(
[
20
, Proposition 2.6]
)  Let
u,v\in W^{1,2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
and
\varphi
be a Lipschitz function on
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}.
Then for any
1\leq k,j\leq n,

\partial_{k}(uv)\partial_{j}\varphi-\partial_{j}(uv)\partial_{k}\varphi\in\mathcal{H}^{1}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
with

\|\partial_{k}(uv)\partial_{j}\varphi-\partial_{j}(uv)\partial_{k}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\lesssim\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}(\|u\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}+\|v\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}).

In the sequel, we sometimes use the notation for the scalar case
m=1
(even
if
m>1
) when we are only interested in norm estimates.
Lemma
3.3
below states that the operator

\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}:=\partial_{t}-\mbox{div}((S(t,x)+D(t,x))\nabla)+\kappa+1

is invertible from
E
to its dual space
E^{*},
which is a starting point of our proof. In particular, the case
\kappa=0
is addressed in
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume

(3.1)

<\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}u,v>:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}+H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}v}+(\kappa+1)u\cdot\overline{v},\quad u,v\in E.

Then
\mathcal{H}
is bounded from
E
to
E^{*}
and its inverse is also bounded with both norms depending only on ellipticity,
\|D\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}};BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}))}

and dimensions.

Proof.
By the assumption on
D
and Proposition
3.1
,
we see
1
1

1

For the validity of the equation involving the integration of
D
one can see
[
5
, Section 2.16]
.

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}D\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\beta}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}D_{kj}^{\alpha\beta}(\partial_{k}u^{\alpha}\overline{\partial_{j}v^{\beta}}-\partial_{j}u^{\alpha}\overline{\partial_{k}v^{\beta}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\|D(t,\cdot)\|_{BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\|D(t,\cdot)\|_{BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}.

So
\mathcal{H}
is bounded from
E
to
E^{*}.
Following
[
3

, we define the sesquilinear form on
E\times E
by

a_{\delta}(u,v):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla(1+\delta H_{t})v}+H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}(1+\delta H_{t})v}+(\kappa+1)u\cdot\overline{(1+\delta H_{t})v}.

As in (
3.2

it follows that

is bounded. To prove the invertibility, we first claim that
a_{\delta}
is accretive for sufficiently small enough

. Using
S\in L^{\infty}
, (

),
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}\mbox{Re}\;H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}u}=0
and the fact that

and

we obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\;a_{\delta}(u,u)

\displaystyle\geq(\lambda-\|S\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}^{2}+\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}^{2}

\displaystyle-\mbox{Re}\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}D\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla(1+\delta H_{t})u}.

We note that by the anti-symmetry of

(3.3)

\mbox{Re}\;D\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=\beta}D_{kj}^{\alpha\beta}(\xi_{k}\overline{\xi_{j}}-\xi_{j}\overline{\xi_{k}})=0\;\;\mbox{for any}\;\;\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{mn},

moreover, by (

\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}D\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla\delta H_{t}u}\bigg|\lesssim\delta\|\|D(t,\cdot)\|_{BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}.

The claim is proved. Then the Lax-Milgram theorem yields that

<\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}u,(1+\delta H_{t})v>=a_{\delta}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})v)\;\;\mbox{for any}\;\;u,v\in E.

This immediately implies the invertibility of
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}
from
E
to
E^{*},
since
(1+\delta H_{t})
is isometric on

when
\delta
is small enough.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
f\in L^{2_{*}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
and
F\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}).
If

is a weak solution of
\partial_{t}v-\mbox{div}((S(t,x)+D(t,x))\nabla v)=f+\mbox{div}F

\displaystyle(i)\quad v\in H^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}},L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}));

\displaystyle(ii)\quad v\in L^{2^{*}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1});

\displaystyle(iii)\quad v\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}},L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}))\;\mbox{and}\;\;\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}\;\mbox{is absolutely continuous in}\;\;t\in{\mathbb{R}},

moreover, the inequality

(3.4)

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})}+\|v\|_{L^{2^{*}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}+\|D_{t}^{1/2}v\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}\lesssim\|v\|_{V}+\|f\|_{L^{2_{*}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}+\|F\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}

\|D\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}};BMO({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}))}

Proof.
By utilizing self-evident embeddings and repeating the arguments from the beginning of the proof of
[
3
, Proposition 3.1]
we can show
f+(\kappa+1)v+\mbox{div}F\in E^{*}.
Thus it follows from Lemma
3.3
that there exists a unique
\tilde{v}\in E
such that
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}\tilde{v}=f+(\kappa+1)v+\mbox{div}F.
This indicates that, for any
\psi\in E,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}(S+D)\nabla\tilde{v}\cdot\overline{\psi}+H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}\tilde{v}\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}\psi}+(\kappa+1)\tilde{v}\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}\psi}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}(f+(\kappa+1)v)\overline{\psi}-F\cdot\overline{\nabla\psi}.

By restricting to

we can write

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}\tilde{v}\cdot\overline{D_{t}^{1/2}\psi}=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}\tilde{v}\overline{\partial_{t}\psi}.

As a result, by (
2.1
),
u:=v-\tilde{v}\in V
is a weak solution of

(3.5)

\partial_{t}u-\mbox{div}((S(t,x)+D(t,x))\nabla u)+(\kappa+1)u=0\;\;\mbox{in}\;{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}.

In the current setting, we assert that:

(3.6)

\fint_{I\times Q}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq C(\epsilon)\frac{1}{r(Q)^{2}}\fint_{4I\times 2Q}|u|^{2}+\epsilon\fint_{4I\times 2Q}|\nabla u|^{2}

for any parabolic cylinder

\displaystyle|S_{1}|

\displaystyle\sup_{-2<t_{0}<0}\int_{Q}|\hat{v}(t_{0},x)|^{2}dx+\int_{I\times Q}|\nabla v|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon\left(\int_{16I\times 4Q}|\nabla v|^{2}\right)^{2/2}+\left(\int_{16I\times 4Q}|f|^{2_{*}}\right)^{2/2_{*}}

\displaystyle+\int_{16I\times 4Q}|F|^{2}+\int_{-4}^{0}\left(\int_{2Q}|\hat{v}|^{\frac{2s}{2-s}}\right)^{\frac{2-s}{s}}.

\displaystyle+\epsilon\int_{16I\times 4Q}|\nabla v|^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{I\times Q}g_{r}^{\frac{2}{r}}\right)^{r/2}

The classical Gehring lemma (
[
14
, Chapter  V  Proposition  1.1]
) is useful in proving Hölder type estimates for elliptic and parabolic equations (systems). In this section, we develop an upgraded version (Lemma
6.2
), emphasizing that the exponent
\frac{(n+2)p}{n+2+\beta p}
is strictly less than
\frac{ps}{q},
as provided by
[
14
, Chapter  V  Proposition  1.1]
. For a detailed derivation of the exponent
\frac{ps}{q},
we refer to
[
14
, Chapter  V  Theorem 2.2]
(or
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
). As a consequence, the exponent governing the sequence of functions
\{f_{i}\}
in
[
14
, Chapter  V  Theorem  2.2]
can be improved. Such a result also has impact on partial regularity of nonlinear systems on
[
9
,
15
,
16
]
.

The critical exponent
\frac{(n+2)p}{n+2+\beta p}
emerges from the non-local Gehring lemma in
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
, which is formulated for spaces of homogeneous type. However, the proof, more specifically
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
, appears to be inapplicable to the local case with an additionally small tail, like the
\theta-
dependent term in (
6.1
). To overcome the difficulty, we revisit the method outlined in
[
14
, Chapter  V  Proposition  1.1]
, taking full advantage of the weak and strong boundedness properties of the fractional maximal function on
L^{p}(1\leq p<\infty)
. As shown in Theorem
1.3
, Lemma
6.2
is more effective than the classical approach for enhancing the regularity of solutions to linear (or nonlinear) elliptic and parabolic equations, including their systems, with inhomogeneous low order terms.

Lemma 6.2
.

Given a parabolic cube
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}.

Let
g,h\in L^{q}(Q)(q>1)
and
f^{s}\in L^{1}(Q)(0<s<q)
be three nonnegative functions, and set
\beta:=(n+2)(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{q}).
Suppose that

(6.1)

\fint_{Q_{r}(x_{0})}g^{q}\leq a\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}(x_{0})}g\right)^{q}+\fint_{Q_{2r}(x_{0})}h^{q}+r^{\beta q}\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}(x_{0})}f^{s}\right)^{\frac{q}{s}}+\theta\fint_{Q_{2r}(x_{0})}g^{q}

holds for each
x_{0}\in Q
with each
r<\frac{1}{2}\min(dist(x_{0},\partial Q),r_{0}),
where
r_{0},a,\theta
are constants with
a>1,

r_{0}>0,

0\leq\theta<1.
Then
g\in L^{p}_{loc}(Q)
for
p\in[q,q+\epsilon)
and

(6.2)

\left(\fint_{Q_{r}}g^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq b\left(\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}}g^{q}\right)^{1/q}+\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}}h^{p}\right)^{1/p}+r^{\beta}\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}}f^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{1/p_{*,\beta}}\right)

for
Q_{2r}\subset Q,r<r_{0},
where
p_{*,\beta}:=\frac{(n+2)p}{n+2+\beta p}
and the two constants
b
and
\epsilon
depend only on
a,\theta,q,n
and
s
.

Proof.
The case
\theta=0
is indeed covered in
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
, so we only need to address the case
0<\theta<1.
Note that the following method also applies to
\theta=0
.

In line with the procedure used in the proof of
[
14
, Chapter  V  Proposition  1.1]
, our initial task is to build that if almost every on
Q:=Q_{3/2}(0)

(6.3)

M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(g^{q})(x)\leq aM^{q}(g)(x)+M(h^{q})+M^{\beta s}(f^{s})^{\frac{q}{s}}(x)+\theta M(g^{q})(x),

where
d(x):=dist(x,\partial Q),

M
denotes the centered Hardy-Littlewood maximal function and

M_{r_{0}}(u)(x):=\sup_{r(B)<r_{0}}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|u|,\quad M^{\alpha}(u)(x):=\sup_{B\ni x}r(B)^{\alpha}\fint_{B}|u|,

then

(6.4)

\left(\fint_{Q_{1/2}(0)}g^{p}\right)^{1/p}\lesssim\left(\fint_{Q_{3/2}(0)}g^{q}\right)^{1/q}+\left(\fint_{Q_{3/2}(0)}h^{p}\right)^{1/p}+\left(\fint_{Q_{3/2}(0)}f^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{1/p_{*,\beta}}.

Here, the implicit constant is determined only by
a,\theta,q,n,\tau
and
s
. We explicitly assume
g=h=f=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\setminus Q
in the subsequent analysis.

We now split
Q
into

Q=\cup_{k}C_{k},\quad C_{0}:=\{x=(t,x_{1},...x_{n})\in R^{n+1}:|x_{j}|<1/2,\;|t|<1/4\}

and

C_{k}:=\{x\in Q:2^{-k}<d(x)\leq 2^{-k+1}\},\quad k\geq 1.

Set
\sigma>100D^{1/2}
with
D:=n+2.
We also define
\alpha_{k}:=\left(\sigma^{D}2^{kD}\right)^{1/q},

E(u,\lambda):=\{x\in Q:u(x)>\lambda\}
,

G(x):=\frac{\theta^{1/2}g(x)}{\|h\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|g\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|f\|_{L^{s}(Q)}},\quad\tilde{G}(x)=\alpha_{k}^{-1}G(x)\;\mbox{in}\;C_{k},

F(x):=\frac{\theta^{1/2}f(x)}{\|h\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|g\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|f\|_{L^{s}(Q)}},

and

H(x):=\frac{\theta^{1/2}h(x)}{\|h\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|g\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|f\|_{L^{s}(Q)}}.

With these preparations in place, the inequality (
6.3
) transforms into

(6.5)

M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(G^{q})(x)\leq c(n,a)M^{q}[G+M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{1/s}+M(H^{q})^{1/q}](x)+\theta M(G^{q}(x))\quad\;\mbox{a.e.}.

In the above derivation, the well-known fact that
u(x)\leq M(u)(x)
was exploited. In what follows, for the sake of brevity, we let

U:=M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{1/s}+M(H^{q})^{1/q}.

Let
\lambda
be a fixed real number in
[1,\infty).
Define

\mu:=\delta\cdot\lambda,

where
\delta>1
is a constant, to be determined later. According to the definition of
G,

\fint_{Q}G^{q}\leq\theta^{1/2}<\mu^{q}.

Consequently, applying the Calderón-Zygmund decomposition-specifically, a parabolic version of
[
14
, Chapter  V  Lemma  1.3]
-we obtain a disjoint sequence of sub-cubes
\{Q_{k}^{j}\}
of
Q
such that

(6.6)

Q_{k}^{j}\subset C_{k},\;\forall j,k\in{\mathbb{N}},\quad diam(Q_{k}^{j})<\frac{1}{2}dist(Q_{k}^{j},\partial Q),

(6.7)

(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}<\fint_{Q_{k}^{j}}G^{q}\leq\sigma^{n}(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q},

and

G\leq c(n,q)\alpha_{k}\mu\quad\mbox{in}\;C_{k}\setminus\cup_{j}Q_{k}^{j}.

The last two inequalities are plainly equivalent to

\mu^{q}<\fint_{Q_{k}^{j}}\tilde{G}^{q}\leq\sigma^{n}\mu^{q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{G}\leq c(n,q)\mu\quad\mbox{in}\;C_{k}\setminus\cup_{j}Q_{k}^{j}.

Since
|E(\tilde{G},c(n,q)\mu)\setminus\cup_{j,k}Q_{k}^{j}|=0,
then

(6.8)

\int_{E(\tilde{G},c(n,q)\mu)}\tilde{G}^{q}\leq\sum_{j,k}\int_{Q_{k}^{j}}\tilde{G}^{q}\leq\sigma^{n}\mu^{q}\sum_{j,k}|Q_{k}^{j}|.

If we set
\tilde{Q}:=\{x\in Q:\eqref{eq: 5.5}\;\mbox{is true}\}
and
\hat{Q}:=\cup_{j,k}Q_{k}^{j},
then
|Q|=|\tilde{Q}|
and
|Q_{k}^{j}|=|Q_{k}^{j}\cap\tilde{Q}|.
In the sequel we seek to control
|\hat{Q}|=\sum_{j,k}|Q_{k}^{j}|=\sum_{j,k}|Q_{k}^{j}\cap\tilde{Q}|.

To the end, we first focus on annihilating the term
\theta M(G^{q}(x)).
Given any
x\in Q_{k}^{j},
by the definition of
M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}
and (
6.6
)-(
6.7
), we see

(6.9)

(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}<\fint_{Q_{k}^{j}}G^{q}\leq c(n)M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(G^{q})(x)\leq c(n)M(G^{q})(x).

This implies that there exists a parabolic ball
B
centered at
x
such that

(6.10)

c(n)^{-1}(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}\theta^{1/2}\leq\theta^{1/2}M(G^{q})(x)<\fint_{B}G^{q}.

Furthermore, keeping in mind that
\mu>1,
the latter expression readily leads to the conclusion that

|B|\leq\frac{c(n)}{\theta^{1/2}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}^{q}}\int_{B}G^{q}<\frac{c(n)}{\alpha_{k}^{q}},

from which we get

r(B)<\frac{d(x)}{2}.

This, together with(
6.10
), in turn implies that

M(G^{q})(x)\leq\frac{1}{\theta^{1/2}}M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(G^{q})(x).

Hence, if
x\in Q_{k}^{j}\cap\tilde{Q},
(
6.5
) indicates that

(6.11)

M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(G^{q})(x)\leq\frac{c(n,a)}{1-\theta^{1/2}}M^{q}[G+U](x).

Plugging (
6.11
) into (
6.9
) we acheive

\displaystyle(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}<\fint_{Q_{k}^{j}}G^{q}

\displaystyle\leq c(n)M_{\frac{1}{2}d(x)}(G^{q})(x)

\displaystyle\leq c(n)M(G^{q})(x)\leq\frac{c(n,a)}{1-\theta^{1/2}}M^{q}[G+U](x),

Thus, we can find a parabolic ball
B_{x},
centered at
x,
satisfying the following property:

(6.12)

(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}\leq\frac{c(n,a)}{1-\theta^{1/2}}\left(\fint_{B_{x}}(G+U)\right)^{q}.

Moreover, invoking the definition of
G,
we deduce that

\left(\fint_{B_{x}}G\right)^{q}\leq\frac{c(n,q)}{|B_{x}|}\int_{Q}G^{q}\leq\frac{c(n,q,\theta)}{|B_{x}|}.

Regarding the average of
U
, one can prove by the
L^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})\to L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
boundedness of
M
and the Hölder inequality in Lorentz spaces that

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{B_{x}}(M(H^{q})^{1/q}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n,q)}{|B_{x}|}\|M(H^{q})\|_{L^{1,\infty}(B_{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n,q)}{|B_{x}|}\int_{Q}H^{q}\leq\frac{c(n,q,\theta)}{|B_{x}|}.

Recall that
\beta=D(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{q})
and
q>s.
Then
\frac{q}{s}=\frac{D}{D-\beta s},
and the weak
(1,\frac{q}{s})
boundedness of
M^{\beta s}
is true. Utilizing this along with the Hölder inequality in Lorentz spaces, we arrive at

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{B_{x}}(M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{1/s}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n,q)}{|B_{x}|}\|M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{1/s}\|_{L^{q,\infty}(B_{x})}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n,q)}{|B_{x}|}\|M^{\beta s}(F^{s})\|_{L^{q/s,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}^{q/s}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n,q,s)}{|B_{x}|}\left(\int_{Q}|F|^{s}\right)^{q/s}\leq\frac{c(n,q,s,\theta)}{|B_{x}|}.

Summarizing all the estimates above we conclude from (
6.12
) that

(\alpha_{k}\mu)^{q}\leq\frac{c(n,q,s,\theta,a)}{|B_{x}|},

which, by
\lambda>1,
gives that

r(B_{x})\leq\frac{c(n,q,s,\theta,a)}{\delta}2^{-k}.

If
\delta=\delta(n,q,s,\theta,a)
is choosen large enough, the latter estiamte guarantees that the ball
B
at most intersects with three annuli
C_{k-1},C_{k},C_{k+1}.
Bear in mind that
\alpha_{k+1}=2^{D/q}\alpha_{k}.
Returning to (
6.12
) we find that

\mu\leq\left(\frac{c(n,a)}{1-\theta^{1/2}}\right)^{1/q}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\fint_{B_{x}}(G+U)\leq c(n,a,\theta,q)\fint_{B_{x}}(\tilde{G}+\tilde{U}),

where

(6.13)

\tilde{U}(x):=\alpha_{k}^{-1}\left(M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{1/s}(x)+M(H^{q})^{1/q}(x)\right)\quad\mbox{in}\;C_{k}.

From (
6.11
) and the definition of
\mu,
it is apparant that

\delta\lambda|B_{x}|\leq c(n,a,\theta,q)\left[\int_{B_{x}\cap E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}+\int_{B_{x}\cap E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U}+2\lambda|B_{x}|\right].

Then, letting
\delta>2c(n,a,\theta,q),
we see

(6.14)

|B_{x}|\leq\frac{c(n,a,\theta,q)}{\lambda}\left[\int_{B_{x}\cap E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}+\int_{B_{x}\cap E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U}\right].

As
\hat{Q}\cap\tilde{Q}\subset\cup_{x\in\hat{Q}\cap\tilde{Q}}B_{x},
the Vitali’s covering lemma (a parabolic version of
[
14
, Chapter  V  Lemma  1.1]
) yields that there exists a disjoint subsequence
\{B_{l}\}
such that

|\hat{Q}\cap\tilde{Q}|\leq c(n)\sum_{l}|B_{l}|.

Recalling (
6.8
), also using (
6.14
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{E(\tilde{G},c(n,q)\mu)}\tilde{G}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq c(n,q)\lambda^{q}\sum_{l}|B_{l}|

\displaystyle\leq c(n,q,a,\theta)\lambda^{q-1}\sum_{l}\left[\int_{B_{l}\cap E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}+\int_{B_{l}\cap E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c(n,q,a,\theta)\lambda^{q-1}\left[\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}+\int_{E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U}\right].

Combining this with

\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)\setminus E(\tilde{G},c(n,q)\delta\lambda)}\tilde{G}^{q}\leq c(n,q,a,\theta,s)\lambda^{q-1}\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G},

we arrive at

(6.15)

\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}^{q}\leq c(n,q,a,s,\theta)\lambda^{q-1}\left[\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G}+\int_{E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U}\right],\;\forall\;\lambda\geq 1.

Next, we derive (
6.4
) from (
6.15
). For this goal, we set

v(\lambda):=\int_{E(\tilde{G},\lambda)}\tilde{G},\quad V(\lambda):=\int_{E(\tilde{U},\lambda)}\tilde{U},\quad\mbox{for}\;\lambda\geq 1.

By expressing the
L^{p}
norm in terms of the distribution function, we have that for any nonnegative function
u
and
\tau\geq 1,

(6.16)

\int_{E(u,\lambda)}u^{\tau}=-\int_{\lambda}^{\infty}u^{\tau-1}d\left(\int_{E(u,t)}u\right).

Observe that, due to (
6.15
), the requirements of
[
14
, Chapter  V  Lemma  1.2]
are fulfilled (with
h,H,p,q
replaced by
v,V,p-1,q-1
). Therefore, for
p\in[q,q+\epsilon),

-\int_{1}^{\infty}\lambda^{p-1}dv(\lambda)\leq c(n,q,a,s,\theta,p)\left[-\int_{1}^{\infty}\lambda^{q-1}dv(\lambda)+-\int_{1}^{\infty}\lambda^{p-1}dV(\lambda)\right],

which, in conjunction with (
6.16
), gives

\int_{E(\tilde{G},1)}\tilde{G}^{p}\leq c(n,q,a,s,\theta,p)\left(\int_{E(\tilde{G},1)}\tilde{G}^{q}+\int_{E(\tilde{U},1)}\tilde{U}^{p}\right).

Moreover, as
p>q,

\int_{\{x\in Q:\tilde{G}\leq 1\}}\tilde{G}^{p}=\int_{\{x\in Q:\tilde{G}\leq 1\}}\tilde{G}^{q}\tilde{G}^{p-q}\leq\int_{\{x\in Q:\tilde{G}\leq 1\}}\tilde{G}^{q}.

Gathering the above inequalities, we conclude that

(6.17)

\int_{Q}\tilde{G}^{p}\leq c(n,q,a,s,\theta,p)\left(\int_{Q}\tilde{G}^{q}+\int_{Q}\tilde{U}^{p}\right).

Having obtained (
6.17
), we are now in a position to infer (
6.4
).
Since
\beta=D(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{q}),
it follows that
\beta s<D(p-s).
This implies
\gamma:=\frac{p_{*,\beta}}{s}>1
and
\frac{p}{s}=\frac{\gamma D}{D-\beta s\gamma}.
As shown in
[
18
]
,
M^{\beta s}
is bounded form
L^{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
to
L^{\frac{p}{s}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}).
Thus

(6.18)

\int_{Q}M^{\beta s}(F^{s})^{p/s}\leq c(n,q,p,s)\left(\int_{Q}F^{s\gamma}\right)^{\frac{p}{s\gamma}}\approx c(n,q,a,\theta)\left(\int_{Q}F^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p_{*,\beta}}}.

On the other hand,

(6.19)

\int_{Q}M(H^{q})^{p/q}\leq c(n,q,p)\int_{Q}H^{p}

thanks to the fact that
M
is bounded from
L^{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
to
L^{\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
for any
1<\gamma<\infty.
Employing (
6.13
) (
\tilde{U}\leq c(n,q)U(x)
in
Q
) and (
6.17
)-(
6.19
), we deduce that

(6.20)

\left(\int_{Q}\tilde{G}^{p}\right)\leq c(n,q,a,s,\theta,p)\left[\left(\int_{Q}\tilde{G}^{q}\right)+\left(\int_{Q}F^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{p/p_{*,\beta}}+\left(\int_{Q}H^{p}\right)\right].

Based on the definitions of
G,F,H
and
\tilde{G},
and utilizing Young’s inequality, Hölder’s inequality and the inequalities
p_{*,\beta}>s
and
p>q,
(
6.20
) leads to the conclusion that

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{1/2}(0)}|g|^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|h\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|g\|_{L^{q}(Q)}+\|f\|_{L^{s}(Q)}\right)^{p-q}\int_{Q}|g|^{q}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left(\int_{Q}f^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{p/p_{*,\beta}}+\int_{Q}h^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h\|_{L^{q}(Q)}^{p-q}\int_{Q}|g|^{q}+\|f\|_{L^{s}(Q)}^{p-q}\int_{Q}|g|^{q}+\left(\int_{Q}|g|^{q}\right)^{p/q}+\left(\int_{Q}f^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{p/p_{*,\beta}}+\int_{Q}h^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{Q}h^{p}+\left(\int_{Q}|g|^{q}\right)^{p/q}+\left(\int_{Q}f^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{p/p_{*,\beta}},

which readily yields (
6.4
).

At this point, the key remaining step is to establish that the assumption (
6.1
) can be attributed to (
6.3
) by translations and dilatations. In fact, if we assume
Q=Q_{R}(z)
and set

g_{R}^{z}(y):=g(Ry+z),\quad f_{R}^{z}(y):=R^{\beta}f(Ry+z),\quad h_{R}^{z}(y):=h(Ry+z),\quad\tau:=\frac{r}{R},\quad\xi:=\frac{x_{0}-z}{R},

then, by (
6.1
),

\displaystyle\left(\fint_{Q_{\tau}(\xi)}(g_{R}^{z})^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\fint_{Q_{2\tau}(\xi)}g_{R}^{z}\right)+\left(\fint_{Q_{2\tau}(\xi)}(h_{R}^{z})^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle+\tau^{\beta}\left(\fint_{Q_{2\tau}(\xi)}(f_{R}^{z})^{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}+\theta^{1/q}\left(\fint_{Q_{2\tau}(\xi)}(g_{R}^{z})^{q}\right)^{1/q}

for each
\xi\in Q_{1}(0)
with each
\tau<\frac{1}{2}\min(dist(\xi,\partial Q_{1}(0)),\frac{r_{0}}{R}).
This implies that

M_{\frac{1}{2}d(\xi)\wedge\frac{r_{0}}{R}}((g_{R}^{z})^{q})(\xi)\leq aM^{q}(g_{R}^{z})(x)+M((h_{R}^{z})^{q})+M^{\beta s}((f_{R}^{z})^{s})^{\frac{q}{s}}(\xi)+\theta M((g_{R}^{z})^{q})(\xi).

We would like to point out that, throughout our applications, the value of
r_{0}
is always taken to be equal to
l(Q)=R.
As a result, by (
6.4
), we eventually achieve

\left(\fint_{Q_{1/2}(0)}(g_{R}^{z})^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq b\left(\fint_{Q_{1}(0)}(g_{R}^{z})^{q}\right)^{1/q}+\left(\fint_{Q_{1}(0)}(h_{R}^{z})^{p}\right)^{1/p}+\left(\fint_{Q_{1}(0)}(f_{R}^{z})^{p_{*,\beta}}\right)^{1/p_{*,\beta}}.

The inequality is exactly equivalent to (
6.2
), thus completing the entire proof.
