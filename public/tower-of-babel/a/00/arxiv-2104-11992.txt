Decomposition of a Quantum System Into Subsystems in Finite Quantum Mechanics
Vladimir V. Kornyak

Abstract
Any Hilbert space with composite dimension can be factorized into a tensor product of smaller Hilbert spaces. This allows to decompose a quantum system into subsystems. We propose a simple tractable model for a constructive study of decompositions of quantum systems.

Introduction

Mereology
is the study of the relations of part to whole and the relations of part to part within a whole.

Quantum mereology
studies such issues as the bipartite decomposition of a quantum system into a “system” and “environment”, the relationship of a distinguished “system” and an “observer”, the emergence of space and time from quantum entanglement, and other fundamental questions in quantum mechanics
[
1
,
2
]
.
The general scheme is as follows.
The whole is an isolated quantum system

in a given pure state undergoing a given unitary (Schrödinger) evolution.
Then, in a way chosen according to certain criteria, the system is decomposed into a tensor product of subsystems.

Factorization of a Hilbert Space

Tensor product of Hilbert spaces

The (
global
) Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
of a
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}K
-component quantum system is the tensor product of the (
local
) Hilbert spaces
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{k}
of the components:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{H}=\KroneckerProduct_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{H}_{k}\,.}

(1)

If
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\dim\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{N}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\dim\mathcal{H}_{k}=d_{k}
, then
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{N}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}{}d_{k}
.
For any
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}d
-dimensional Hilbert space, we denote the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}i
th orthonormal basis element by
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|i\right\rangle
, that is,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|0\right\rangle=\left(1,0,\ldots\right)^{\top}
,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|1\right\rangle=\left(0,1,0,\ldots\right)^{\top}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\ldots,\left|d-1\right\rangle=\left(0,0,\ldots,1\right)^{\top}
.
Tensor monomials of local basis elements form an orthonormal basis in the global Hilbert space:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left|i\right\rangle=\left|i_{1}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct\left|i_{k}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct\left|i_{K}\right\rangle,}

(2)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|i\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|i_{k}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{i=i_{1}\prod_{m=2}^{K}d_{m}+\ldots+i_{k}\prod_{m=k+1}^{K}d_{m}+\ldots+i_{K}.}

(3)

Tensor decomposition of a Hilbert space

We can reverse the procedure, since (
2
) is a one-to-one correspondence — the sequence
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}i_{1},\ldots,i_{K}
is uniquely recovered from
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}i
using formula (
3
).

Starting with an orthonormal basis in an
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{N}
-dimensional Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
and a decomposition
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{N}=d_{1}\cdots{}d_{K}
,
we can construct a particular isomorphism between
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
and the tensor product of local spaces of the corresponding dimensions.

When constructing an isomorphism, we must take into account the freedom in the choice of bases in Hilbert spaces.
Any two orthonormal bases are related by a unitary transformation.
Using the properties of the tensor product, we can write

\displaystyle U\left|\psi\right\rangle=\,U_{1}\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct{}U_{K}\left|\psi_{K}\right\rangle=\left(U_{1}\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct{}U_{K}\right)\left(\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct\left|\psi_{K}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\implies\left(U_{1}\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct{}U_{K}\right)^{-1}U\left|\psi\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct\left|\psi_{K}\right\rangle,

​​where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H},\,\left|\psi_{k}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{k};\,U,\,U_{k}
are unitary transformations in the corresponding spaces.
We see that local transformations can be absorbed by the global transformation
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U
, so in general we have

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{U\left|\psi\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\cdots\KroneckerProduct\left|\psi_{K}\right\rangle.}

Thus, to specify a factorization of a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
we need a
decomposition
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\dim\mathcal{H}
and a
unitary transformation
that fixes a basis in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
.

The permutation representation of any group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}=\idmat_{\mathcal{N}}-\frac{\left|\omega\right\rangle\!\left\langle\omega\right|}{\mathcal{N}}\,.}

and demonstrated that the choice

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{N}}

This means that to describe evolutions in

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{2^{\mathcal{N}}-2\,.}

Ontic vectors

We call
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}2
nd order vectors
ontic vectors
.
These vectors are attractive for both ontological and computational reasons.
The ontic vector
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|q\right\rangle
can be written as a bit string of length
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{N}
.
Interpreting this string as a
characteristic function
, one can identify the ontic vector with the corresponding nontrivial subset of the set of ontic elements (
4
):

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}q\subset\Omega
.
The complete set of ontic vectors is

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{Q=2^{\Omega}\setminus\left\{\emptyset,\Omega\right\},\penalty\ \penalty\ \left|Q\right|=2^{\mathcal{N}}-2\,.}

The inner product of ontic vectors
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|q\right\rangle
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|r\right\rangle
in the space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{N}}
has the form
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle q\mid r\right\rangle=\left\langle q\&{}r\right\rangle
,
where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\&
is the bitwise AND for bit strings, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle\,\cdot\,\right\rangle
is the number of ones in a bit string (
population number
or

).
For the inner product of normalized projections of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|q\right\rangle
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|r\right\rangle
onto
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{\,\!\star}
, we have

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{S\!\left(q,r\right)\equiv\frac{\left\langle q\left|\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}\right|r\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle q\left|\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}\right|q\right\rangle\left\langle r\left|\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}\right|r\right\rangle}}=\frac{\mathcal{N}\!\left\langle q\,\&\,r\right\rangle-\!\left\langle q\right\rangle\left\langle r\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle q\right\rangle\left\langle\sim\!q\right\rangle\left\langle r\right\rangle\left\langle\sim\!r\right\rangle}},}

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\sim
denotes bitwise inversion.
The obvious identities

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle\sim\!a\right\rangle=\mathcal{N}-\left\langle a\right\rangle
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle a\,\&\,b\right\rangle+\left\langle a\,\&\sim\!b\right\rangle=\left\langle a\right\rangle

imply the folowing symmetry with respect to transpositions of subsets of the ontic set and their complements

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{S\!\left(q,r\right)=-S\!\left(\sim\!q,r\right)=-S\!\left(q,\sim\!r\right)=S\!\left(\sim\!q,\sim\!r\right)\,.}

4
Decomposition of a Quantum System

Since any mixed state of a quantum system can be obtained from a pure state in a larger Hilbert space by taking a partial trace, it is natural to assume that at a fundamental level the state of an isolated system must be pure.
5
5

5

This belief is expressed by the metaphor “the Church of the Larger Hilbert Space” (J.A. Smolin).

The original permutation basis in the space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{N}}
, i.e., the set
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega
, will be referred as the
ontic basis
.
In this basis, the pure density matrix in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{\,\!\star}
associated with the ontic state
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|q\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{N}}
has the form

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{{\rho}_{q}^{o}=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}\left|q\right\rangle\!\left\langle q\right|\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}}{\left\langle q\left|\mathrm{P}_{\!\star}\right|q\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\frac{\left(\left|q\right\rangle-\alpha\left|\omega\right\rangle\right)\left(\left\langle q\right|-\alpha\left\langle\omega\right|\right)}{\alpha\left(1-\alpha\right)}\,,}

(7)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{\left\langle q\right\rangle}{\mathcal{N}}
is the
population density
.
There is an obvious duality: the expression for the density matrix
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle{\rho}_{\sim{}q}^{o}
is obtained from (
7
) by replacements

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}q\rightarrow\,\,\sim\hskip-4.0ptq
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\alpha\rightarrow 1-\alpha
.

Energy basis

In continuous QM, the evolution of an isolated system is described by the one-parameter unitary group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U_{t}=\e^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}{}Ht}
generated by the Hamiltonian
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H
whose eigenvalues are called
energy eigenvalues
.
In finite QM, the evolution is described by a unitary representation of a cyclic group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U\!\left(g\right)^{t}
generated by an element
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}g\in{}G
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t
is an integer parameter.
In our case, the generator of evolution is some matrix
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{P}\!\left(g\right)
from the permutation representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G
in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{N}}
.
We call the
energy basis
an orthonormal basis in which the matrix
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{P}\!\left(g\right)
is diagonal.

Any permutation can be written as a product of disjoint cycles.
It is easy to show that the total number of cycles of length
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\ell
in the whole group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{N}}
is
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{N}!}{\ell}
,
and, therefore, the expected number of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\ell
-cycles in a single permutation is
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}
.
That is, high-frequency (high-energy) evolutions are more common.

The
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\ell
-cycle matrix has the form

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{C_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}\,.}

The diagonal form of this matrix is

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{F_{\ell}C_{\ell}F^{-1}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\zeta_{\ell}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\zeta_{\ell}^{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\zeta_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\end{pmatrix}\,,}

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell}=\e^{{2\pi}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}/{\ell}}
is the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\ell
th primitive root of unity, and

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{F_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&\zeta_{\ell}^{-1}&\zeta_{\ell}^{-2}&\cdots&\zeta_{\ell}^{-\left(\ell-1\right)}\\
1&\zeta_{\ell}^{-2}&\zeta_{\ell}^{-4}&\cdots&\zeta_{\ell}^{-2\left(\ell-1\right)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1&\zeta_{\ell}^{-\left(\ell-1\right)}&\zeta_{\ell}^{-2\left(\ell-1\right)}&\cdots&\zeta_{\ell}^{-\left(\ell-1\right)\left(\ell-1\right)}\end{pmatrix}}

is the
Fourier transform
matrix.

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}F_{\ell}
is both unitary and symmetric, therefore
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle{}F^{-1}_{\ell}=F_{\ell}^{*}.

In general, the matrix of the permutation representation of an element
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}g\in{}\mathsf{S}_{\mathcal{N}}
is the direct sum of cyclic matrices

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle{}\mathcal{P}\!\left(g\right)=\bigoplus\limits_{m=1}^{M}C_{\ell_{m}}
, and the corresponding diagonalizing matrix is
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\displaystyle{}F=\bigoplus\limits_{m=1}^{M}F_{\ell_{m}}
,
which is the transition matrix from the
ontic basis
to the
energy basis
.
The density matrix in the energy basis can be calculated from (
7
) by the formula

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{{\rho}_{q}^{e}=F{\rho}_{q}^{o}F^{-1}.}

5
Entanglement Measures

Quantitatively, quantum correlations are described by
measures of entanglement
, which are based on the concept of entropy.
The most commonly used in physics is the
von Neumann entropy

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}S_{1}\!\left({\rho}\right)=-\tr\!\left({\rho}\log{\rho}\right).
Also often used are entropies from the
Rényi family

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{S_{\alpha}\!\left({\rho}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\tr\!\left({\rho}^{\alpha}\right),\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha\geq 0,\penalty\ \alpha\neq 1.}

The common feature of the von Neumann and Rényi entropies is their additivity on combinations of independent probability distributions determined by the eigenvalues of the density matrices.
The von Neumann entropy is preferred because it additionally satisfies a stronger requirement, the chain rule for conditional entropies.

In our calculations, we use the 2nd Rényi entropy (also called the
collision entropy
)
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}S_{2}\!\left({\rho}\right)=-\log\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)
for the following reasons:

•

It is easy to calculate:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\rho}_{ii}^{2}+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{n}\left|{\rho}_{ij}\right|^{2}.

•

The value
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)
coincides with the Born probability: “the system observes itself.”

•

The value
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)
is the square of the
Frobenius
(
Hilbert-Schmidt
)
norm
of the density matrix.
