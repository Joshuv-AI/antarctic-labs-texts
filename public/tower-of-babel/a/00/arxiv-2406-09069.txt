On the Robustness of Global Feature Effect Explanations
Hubert Baniecki (✉), Giuseppe Casalicchio, Bernd Bischl, Przemyslaw Biecek

Abstract
We study the robustness of global post-hoc explanations for predictive models trained on tabular data. Effects of predictor features in black-box supervised learning are an essential diagnostic tool for model debugging and scientific discovery in applied sciences. However, how vulnerable they are to data and model perturbations remains an open research question. We introduce several theoretical bounds for evaluating the robustness of partial dependence plots and accumulated local effects. Our experimental results with synthetic and real-world datasets quantify the gap between the best and worst-case scenarios of (mis)interpreting machine learning predictions globally.

Related work

2.1
Global feature effect explanations

Partial dependence plots were one of the first methods proposed to interpret black-box ML models like gradient boosting machines
[
10
]
. They provide a simplified view of feature effects without decomposing interaction effects as in the case of functional ANOVA
[
20
]
. Feature effects have a natural interpretation corresponding to feature importance
[
8
]
. In
[
3
]
, authors propose accumulated local effects as an improvement to partial dependence, which corrects the estimation when features in data are correlated. Highly efficient estimation of accumulated local effects is possible when a model is differentiable
[
13
]
, e.g., in the case of neural networks. In
[
19
]
, authors propose regional effect plots to correct explanations when feature interactions are present in the model. Most recently in this line of work, a heterogeneity-aware estimator of accumulated local effects was proposed that improves a naive bin-splitting of features
[
14
]
.

Crucially, feature effect explanations are computed based on a fitted model (or learning algorithm) and the underlying data distribution
[
30
]
.
In
[
5
]
, an adversarial attack on partial dependence that poisons the data is used to manipulate the interpretation of an explanation. In
[
31
]
, the authors propose a method to estimate partial dependence under data shifts that impact a model in the case of incremental learning. Both works can be viewed as specific cases generalized by our theoretical analysis.

Despite their inherent limitations, feature effect explanations are useful to interpret ML models in applied sciences, e.g., the effect of age and cholesterol on the probability of heart disease
[
33
, figure 6]
, the effect of wall properties on shear strength
[
28
, figures 5 & 6]
, the impact of aridity on flood trends
[
23
, figure 5b]
, which motivates our further study on their robustness.

3
Notation and definition of feature effects

We consider a supervised ML setup for regression or binary classification with labeled data
\{(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},y^{(1)}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}^{(n)},y^{(n)})\}
, where every element comes from
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the underlying feature and label space.
Usually, we assume
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We denote the
n\times p
dimensional design matrix by
\mathbb{X}
where
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
is the
i
-th row of
\mathbb{X}
.
This data is assumed to be sampled in an i.i.d. fashion from an underlying distribution defined on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
We denote a random variable vector as
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}
and the random variable for a label by
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Let
s\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
be a feature index set of interest with its complement
\bar{s}=\{1,\dots,p\}\setminus s
.
We often index feature vectors, random variables, and design matrices by index sets
s
to restrict them to these index sets.
We write
f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})
, to emphasize that the feature vector is separated into two parts, usually the “features of interest”
\mathbf{x}_{s}
and the “‘rest”
\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}
.
We use
p(\mathbf{x},y)
for the joint probability density function on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and
we write
p_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})
and
p_{\mathbf{X}_{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{s})
for marginal distributions for
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}_{s}
, respectively, and
p_{\mathbf{X}_{s}|\mathbf{X}_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{s}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
for the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{s}|\mathbf{X}_{t}
.

We denote a prediction model by
f:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
; it predicts an output using an input feature vector
\mathbf{x}
.
In the case of binary classification, the output is either a decision score from
\mathbb{R}
or a posterior probability from
[0,1]
. Without loss of generality, we explain the output for a single class in the case of a multi-class task.
Later, we will make changes to the design matrix of our labeled data on which we train a model.
To make this explicit, we sometimes write
f_{\mathbb{X}}
to denote a model trained on
\mathbb{X}
(and we suppress labels in notation here) but we will simply write
f
when training data is clear from context.

Furthermore, let
g(\cdot\;;f,p_{\mathbf{X}})
denote a general explanation function where we emphasize in notation that the explanation depends both on a given model
f
and the feature density
p_{\mathbf{X}}
– which we will both perturb later. Specifically, let
g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})
denote a global feature effect (e.g., a
\textsc{pd}_{s}
or
\textsc{ale}_{s}
function as defined in Definitions
1
and
3
) for a set of features of interest
s
– often
|s|=1
when the effect is visualized as a line curve or
|s|=2
when it is visualized as a heat map. In practice, an estimator of feature effects denoted by
\widehat{g}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,\mathbb{X})
requires estimating probability density
p_{\mathbf{X}}
using particular input data
\mathbb{X}
.

Definition 1
(Partial dependence
[
10
]
)

Partial dependence for feature set
s
is defined as

\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}})\right]=\int f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}},

which in practice can be estimated using Monte-Carlo estimation:

\widehat{\textsc{pd}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,\mathbb{X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}^{(i)})\right].

Definition 2
(Conditional dependence, i.e., Marginal plot
[
10
,
3
]
)

Conditional dependence is defined as

\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}})\right]=\int f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{x}_{s})d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}},

which can be estimated using

\widehat{\textsc{cd}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,\mathbb{X})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{s})|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{s})}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}^{(i)})\right],

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{s})\coloneqq\{i:\|\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{s}^{(i)}\|\leq\epsilon\}
denotes indices of observations in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{s}
for a given norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Definition 3
(Accumulated local effects
[
3
]
)

For brevity, we define here
\textsc{ale}_{s}
for case when
|s|=1
as

\begin{split}\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})&=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}}\left[\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\right]dz\\
&=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\left[\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\right]p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz,\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}
is a value chosen near the lower bound of the support of feature
s
.
\textsc{ale}_{s}
can be estimated using

\widehat{\textsc{ale}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,\mathbb{X})=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}(k)|}\sum_{\mathcal{B}(k)}\left[f(z^{k},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}^{(i)})-f(z^{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}^{(i)})\right],

(2)

where
(z^{1},\ldots,z^{k_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}})
are grid points spanning the domain of feature
s
up to
\mathbf{x}_{s}
and
\mathcal{B}(k)\coloneqq\{i:\mathbf{x}_{s}^{(i)}\in(z^{k-1},z^{k})\}
are indices of observations in a
k
-th bin.

Otherwise,
\widehat{\textsc{dale}}_{s}

[
13
]
is a more efficient estimator of
\textsc{ale}_{s}
under the assumption that
f
is theoretically differentiable, and auto-differentiable in practice, e.g., a neural network. Both
\widehat{\textsc{ale}}_{s}
and
\widehat{\textsc{dale}}_{s}
are estimated up to a constant (also called an “uncentered” estimator), which in practice is corrected by adding to it the mean prediction of the model.

Feature effect explanation is usually estimated on a finite set of grid points
\mathbf{z}=(z^{1},\ldots,z^{m})
spanning the domain of feature
\mathbf{x}_{s}
.
The most popular choices for grid points are quantile values or an equidistant grid.
Function
\widehat{g}
then returns an estimated
m
-dimensional explanation vector
\mathbf{e}
defined on the grid
\mathbf{z}
, and the curve can be visualized from the finite set of points
\{z^{k},\;\widehat{g}_{s}(z^{k}\mid f,\mathbb{X})\}_{k=1}^{m}
.

4
Theoretical analysis

Let the symbol
\rightarrow
denote a change in a given object, e.g., a small perturbation in the data. In general, our goal is to quantify the change in
global
explanations
\mathbf{e}\rightarrow\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
in means of data change
\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
(e.g., distribution shift) or model change
f\rightarrow f^{\prime}
(e.g., fine-tuning) measured with some distance function
d
.
In the case of model-agnostic interpretability, typically both model and data are used as input to the explanation estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{X})
.
The literature considers the following scenarios for analyzing explanation robustness:

4.1

data perturbation
when
\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
implies
\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
, also known as data poisoning
[
5
]
or biased sampling
[
24
]
,

4.2

model perturbation
when
f\rightarrow f^{\prime}
implies
\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f,\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f^{\prime},\mathbb{X})
, which, in practice, often corresponds to either

•

\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
implies
\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}^{\prime}},\mathbb{X})
in case of data shifts
[
29
]
, or

•

\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
implies
\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}},\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\widehat{g}(\cdot\;;f_{\mathbb{X}^{\prime}},\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
in incremental learning
[
31
]
.

Therefore, quantifying
robustness
can be defined as quantifying bounded relationships between explanation change
d(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}^{\prime})
and data change
d(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
or model change
d(f,f^{\prime})
. We do exactly that for global feature effects.

4.1
Robustness to data perturbation

Consider a simple scenario where the model function is the XOR function of two features

f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{2}>0}.
We want to explain an effect of feature
\mathbf{x}_{1}
so taking a partial dependence of
f
on
\mathbf{x}_{1}
yields

\begin{split}\textsc{pd}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}}\left[f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{2}>0}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{2}>0)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{2}>0),&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{1}>0\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{2}<0),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(3)

We observe that an explanation of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
depends solely on the distribution
p_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}
.

For example, assuming the distribution of the second feature is given by
\mathbf{X}_{2}\sim\mathcal{U}[a,b]\text{ where }a\leq 0\leq b
, we have

\textsc{pd}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{2}>0)=\frac{b}{b-a},&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{1}>0\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{2}<0)=\frac{a}{b-a},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

Figure
1
shows this relationship between a feature effect in grid point
\mathbf{x}_{1}=1
and perturbing distribution
p_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}
computationally; also for a normal distribution.

Figure 1:
Global feature effect explanation (in color) for value
\mathbf{x}_{1}=1
depends
solely
on the parameters of a uniform (
left
) or normal (
right
) distribution of feature
\mathbf{X}_{2}
.

We are interested in finding a theoretical bound for this relationship in a general case.

Assumption 4.1

We assume that the model
f
has bounded predictions, i.e., there exists a constant
B
such that
|f(\mathbf{x})|\leq B
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Theorem 4.2

The robustness of partial dependence and conditional dependence to
data perturbations
is given by the following formulas

\big|\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq 2B\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}\big),

(5)

\big|\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq 2B\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}\big),

(6)

where the total variation distance
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
is defined via the
l_{1}
functional distance.

Proof

We have

\displaystyle\big|\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|=

\displaystyle=\left|\int f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}-\int f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int\big|f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big)\big|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}

\displaystyle=\int\big|f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big|\cdot\left|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\right|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}

from Assumption
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\leq\int B\cdot\left|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\right|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}

\displaystyle=B\cdot\int\left|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\right|\;d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}

\displaystyle=2B\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}\big).

We now apply this argument again, with expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}}
instead of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}\sim p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}}}
, to obtain

\big|\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq 2B\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}\big).

(7)

∎

Theorem
4.2
gives an upper bound on the possible change of global feature effects in terms of distance between data distributions given model-specific constant
B
, e.g. 1 in classification or a maximum value of target domain in regression. Certainly in many scenarios, an average value of model prediction
\big|f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big|
, and hence the bound, is smaller.

Remark 1

In Theorem
4.2
, we can obtain a tighter bound per point
\mathbf{x}_{s}
by taking
B(\mathbf{x}_{s})
such that
|f(\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})|\leq B(\mathbf{x}_{s})
for all
\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-|s|}
.

Remark 2

By definition, if
f
has bounded predictions such that
A\leq f(\mathbf{x})\leq B
, then the feature effect value is bounded, i.e.,
A\leq g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})\leq B
. From this follows that a change in the feature effect value will be smaller than the maximal distance to these bounds (
A
or
B
). We obtain

\big|g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq\max(|g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-B|,|g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-A|),

which makes the bound constant for high-enough values of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}}\big)
. For example, taking

A=0
and
B=1
, for
\mathbf{x}_{s}
such that
g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})=0.5
, we have

\big|g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq\begin{cases}2\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}}\big),&\text{if }d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}}\big)\leq 0.25,\\
0.5,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

We conduct analogous theoretical analysis for accumulated local effects based on the following assumption about the model

, which holds for many predictive functions, e.g., typical neural networks

38
]
.

Assumption 4.3

We assume that the model
f
is globally
L
-Lipschitz continuous, or that we have
\|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|\leq L\cdot\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

It can be easily shown that Assumption
4.3
leads to Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1

If
f
is
L
-Lipschitz, then it holds that
\|\nabla f\|\leq L
almost everywhere.

Theorem 4.4

The robustness of accumulated local effects to
data perturbations
is given by the following formula

\big|\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq 2L\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s})\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z^{*}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z^{*}}\big),

(9)

where
z^{*}=\argmax_{z}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}\big)
.

Proof

We have

\begin{split}&\big|\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})\big|=\\
&=\Bigg|\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\\
&-\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\Bigg|\\
&=\left|\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)\big)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\right|\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\Big|\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)\big)\Big|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\\
&=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\Big|\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\Big|\cdot\Big|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)\Big|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\\
&\text{from Assumption~\ref{ass:lipschitz} and Lemma~\ref{lem:lipschitz}, we have}\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}L\int\left|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)-p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)\right|d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\\
&=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}2L\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}\big)dz\\
&\leq 2L\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s})\cdot\max_{z}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}\big).\end{split}

(10)

∎

Theorem
4.4
is for case when
|s|=1
, but it can be generalized to
|s|>1
. The robustness of accumulated local effects differs from that of partial and conditional dependence as
ale
uses a gradient of a function bounded by
L
instead of the model’s prediction bounded by
B
. In general, estimating
L
is not obvious, but in many cases it can be found computationally
[
38
, see a discussion in]
.

Remark 3

an increasing function of

and

We further support the derived theory concerning the robustness of global feature effects to data perturbations with experimental results in Section

Lemma 2

The robustness of partial dependence and conditional dependence to

\big|\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{pd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f^{\prime},p_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

\big|\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{cd}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f^{\prime},p_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}},

(12)

denotes an infinity norm for a function and
\|f\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|f(\mathbf{x})|
is the same norm taken over the domain
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Proof

Follows directly from
[

, lemmas 5 & 6]

Theorem 4.5

The robustness of accumulated local effects to
model perturbations
is given by the following formula

\big|\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f^{\prime},p_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s})\cdot\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}},

(13)

where

and

denote partial derivatives of

respectively.

Proof

We have

\displaystyle\big|\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{ale}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f^{\prime},p_{\mathbf{X}})\big|=

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\frac{\partial f^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\left(\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial f^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\right)p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\Big|\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial f^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}\Big|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz=(\star)

We can derive two bounds:

(
A
) assuming
f^{\prime}
is globally
L^{\prime}
-Lipschitz continuous, we have

\displaystyle(\star)\leq(L+L^{\prime})\cdot\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz

\displaystyle=(L+L^{\prime})\cdot\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}1dz=(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s})\cdot(L+L^{\prime})

(
B
) substituting
h(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\coloneqq\frac{\partial f(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}
and
h^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\coloneqq\frac{\partial f^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})}{\partial z}
, we have

\displaystyle(\star)=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\int\big|h(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})-h^{\prime}(z,\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}})\big|p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=z}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}|z)d\mathbf{x}_{\bar{s}}dz

from
Lemma
2
, we use
\big|\textsc{cd}_{s}(z;h,p_{\mathbf{X}})-\textsc{cd}_{s}(z;h^{\prime},p_{\mathbf{X}})\big|\leq\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s}}^{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}\;dz=(\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{min},s})\cdot\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}.

We observe that the bound obtained in (

and so (

We provide additional empirical results supporting our theoretical analysis concerning the robustness of feature effects to data perturbation (Section

[

https://github.com/hbaniecki/robust-feature-effects

5.1
Robustness to data perturbation

First, we aim to computationally analyze the relationship of how changes in the input data
d(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
affect changes in the resulting explanations
d(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}^{\prime})
.

Setup.

To this end, we rely on the three datasets from OpenXAI that contain only continuous features: HELOC (
n=9871
,
p=23
) where the task is to predict whether a credit will be repaid in 2 years, Pima (
n=768
,
p=9
) where the task is to predict whether a patient has diabetes, and a Synthetic dataset (aka Gaussian,
n=5000
,
p=20
). We leave considerations concerning the perturbation of categorical features for future work. To each dataset, there is a pretrained neural network with an accuracy of 74% (HELOC), 92% (Synthetic), and 77% (Pima) that outperforms a logistic regression baseline (72%, 83%, 66%, respectively). We explain a neural network on the test sets of HELOC and Synthetic based on the pre-defined splits, but on the train set of Pima, as its test set is too small to reliably estimate conditional distributions and effects.

To analyze a wide spectrum of possible feature effect explanations, we explain three features
s
: the least, “median” and most important to the model. We measure the importance of features with the variance of feature effects, i.e., higher variance means higher importance
[
40
,
15
]
. For each feature, we evaluate
\mathbf{x}_{s}
values on a grid of the three quantile values: 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8.

Data perturbation.

We perturb data using two approaches: a baseline of applying Gaussian noise with varying intensity
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
, and an adversarial perturbation found using a genetic algorithm proposed in
[
5
]
. The latter perturbs a dataset
\mathbb{X}
used for estimating
p_{\mathbf{X}}
so that the change in an explanation value
|g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p_{\mathbf{X}})-g_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s};f,p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})|
is maximized for each
\mathbf{x}_{s}
separately. Further details concerning methods and their hyperparameters are in Appendix
0.A
. In each scenario, we measure the magnitude of perturbation by estimating total variation distance
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p_{\mathbf{X}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}})
for partial dependence and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(p_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}},p^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}_{\bar{s}}|\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}}\big)
for conditional dependence.

5.2
Robustness to model perturbation

Next, we aim to computationally analyze the relationship of how changes in the model parameters
d(f,f^{\prime})
affect changes in the resulting explanations
d(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e}^{\prime})
.

Setup.

We add to experiments the remaining datasets from OpenXAI that can include categorical features: Adult (
n=48842
,
p=13
) where the task is to predict whether an individual’s income exceeds $50K per year, Credit (aka German,
n=1000
,
p=20
) where the task is to distinguish between a good or bad credit score, and Heart (
n=4240
,
p=16
) where the task is to predict whether the patient has a 10-year risk of future heart disease. To each dataset, there is a pretrained neural network with an accuracy of 85%, 75%, and 85% respectively. We excluded the COMPAS dataset as the pretrained neural network does not outperform a logistic regression baseline (85.4%), signaling that the model is underfitted, which might influence its robustness analysis. In this experiment, we want to aggregate results across all features and their values for three feature effects: marginal (partial,
\textsc{pd}_{s}
), conditional (
\textsc{cd}_{s}
), and accumulated (
\textsc{ale}_{s}
). Specifically, we explain features that have more than 2 unique values to exclude one-hot-encoded features for which accumulated local effects are not intuitive to estimate. Finally, we use
\widehat{\textsc{dale}}_{s}

[
13
]
to accurately estimate
\textsc{ale}_{s}
for neural networks.

Model perturbation.

We perform model parameter randomization tests
[
1
]
for global feature effect explanations. The idea is to sequentially perturb weights in consecutive layers of a neural network starting from the end. It was previously shown that gradient-based explanations from the class of local feature attributions are not significantly affected by such a perturbation, which might not be a desired property of an explanation method
[
1
]
. To implement model parameter randomization tests for the pretrained 3-layer neural networks, we add Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma=0.5)
to the weights. We repeat the test 20 times and visualize the average result with a standard error.

Results.

Figure
4
shows results for all datasets. We observe, as expected, that feature effects are influenced by perturbing weights of a neural network. In many cases, (differential) accumulated local effects do not pass the model randomization test, i.e., are significantly less affected by drastic model perturbation than partial and conditional dependence. Our result is consistent with the work comparing removal-based to gradient-based local feature attributions
[
26
]
. In Appendix
0.B
, we provide more analogous results for different
\sigma
values and report the drop in model predictive performance after perturbations.

6
Conclusion

We derived theoretical bounds for the robustness of feature effects to data and model perturbations. They give certain guarantees and intuition regarding how adversarial perturbations influence global explanations of ML models. Our theory can guide future work on improving these explanation methods to be more stable and faithful to the model and data distribution. We made several valuable connections to previous work, e.g., concerning the robustness of local feature attributions
[
26
]
, adversarial attacks on partial dependence
[
5
]
, and model parameter randomization tests for gradient-based explanations
[
1
]
.

Experimental results show that, on average, partial dependence is more robust to data perturbation than conditional dependence. Moreover, accumulated local effects do not pass the model randomization test, i.e., are significantly less affected by drastic model perturbation than partial and conditional dependence.

Limitations and future work.

Theorem
4.4
assumes
model
f
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous
and future work can improve the bound to remove this assumption. It would say more about an explanation of a decision tree or, in general, a step-wise function that has infinite gradients not bounded by
L
. Theorems
4.4
&
4.5
are derived for the most popular case when
|s|=1
, but can be similarly derived for case when
|s|>1
. Our experiments are biased toward pretrained models from OpenXAI. Moreover, we acknowledge that the numerical approximation of total variation distance, as well as conditional distributions and effects, is prone to errors and might impact experimental results. Future theoretical and experimental work can analyze how feature dependence, e.g., correlation and interactions, impacts the robustness of global feature effects to model and data perturbation.

Appendix 0.A
Experiments: robustness to data perturbation

Data perturbation.

We constraint to perturbing only the top 2 most important features (in the case when
s
is the most important, we perturb the 2nd and 3rd important) as measured by the variance of partial dependence.

In random perturbation, we add (once) univariate Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=\{0.01,0.05,0.10,\allowbreak 0.12,0.25\}
to each of the perturbed features.

In adversarial perturbation, a genetic algorithm performs mutation, crossover, evaluation, and selection between a population of 100 individuals (dataset instances) for 200 iterations.
In each iteration, mutation adds univariate Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=\{0.01,0.05,0.10,0.25,0.33\}
to each of the perturbed features.
It always checks if any new value is out of distribution (edges of the domain of a particular feature) and if so, samples a new value from the original distribution. This is to constrain the perturbation to the data manifold.

A crossover operator exchanges values in corresponding row/column indices (
i,j
) of the dataset between the two parent individuals to generate new child individuals.
Evaluation of individuals considers calculating a fitness function, which here is a distance between the original explanation value (e.g.,
0.53
) and a target (in this case
0
or
1
).
Finally, the algorithm uses a standard roulette wheel selection to choose individuals for the next iteration. For further details on the method, refer to the original article
[
5
]
. We set the remaining hyperparameters to default values.

We repeat random and adversarial perturbations 5 times and visualize all the obtained results.
