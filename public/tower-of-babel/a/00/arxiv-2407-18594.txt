Decoupling multistep schemes for elliptic–parabolic problems
Robert Altmann † and Abdullah Mujahid ⋆ and Benjamin Unger ⋆

Abstract
. We study the construction and convergence of decoupling multistep schemes of higher order using the backward differentiation formulae for an elliptic–parabolic problem, which includes multiple-network poroelasticity as a special case. These schemes were first introduced in [Altmann, Maier, Unger, BIT Numer. Math., 64:20, 2024], where a convergence proof for the second-order case is presented. Here, we present a slightly modified version of these schemes using a different construction of related time delay systems. We present a novel convergence proof relying on concepts from G-stability applicable for any order and providing a sharper characterization of the required weak coupling condition. The key tool for the convergence analysis is the construction of a weighted norm enabling a telescoping argument for the sum of the errors.



Keywords:
poroelasticity, decoupling, higher-order discretization, backward differentiation formulae

AMS subject classification:
65M12, 65J10, 76S05

1.
Introduction

This paper explores semi-explicit time integration schemes of higher order for the equations of poroelasticity
[
11
]
or, more generally, for linear elliptic–parabolic problems. Among important applications of this problem are biomechanics, where the human brain and heart are modeled as poroelastic medium with multiple fluid networks
[
32
,
17
]
, and geomechanics, where poroelasticity serves as a model problem
[
34
]
.

The well-posedness of the considered elliptic–parabolic problem is studied in
[
27
]
. To reduce the computational effort resulting from the coupling, several decoupling strategies exist
[
24
,
22
,
23
,
28
,
12
]
, most of which are based on a fixed-point iteration. However, higher-order methods in the iterative framework need many inner iteration steps and hence call for a stronger contraction
[
8
]
.

Alternatively, a semi-explicit method of first order, as proposed and analyzed in
[
5
]
, decouples the system whenever a weak coupling condition is satisfied. The design of the semi-explicit method is based on constructing a related partial differential equation with a time delay, approximating the solution of the original system up to a desired order. An extension to second order is considered in
[
6
]
and to nonlinear problems in
[
4
]
. Unlike the step size restriction in explicit methods, semi-explicit methods require a weak coupling condition, which restricts the problem class given by the material parameters. To lift this restriction, schemes with a fixed number of relaxation steps are introduced in
[
2
,
3
]
.

In this article, we provide closed-form expressions for the construction of
the delay systems and develop the necessary tools to show the convergence of the semi-explicit schemes using the
backward differentiation formulae
(BDF). In the standard analysis of BDF-
k
methods, one can construct a weighted norm with a symmetric positive definite
k\times k
matrix
G
. The weighting allows for setting up a telescoping sum of the errors and, hence, is the key tool for the convergence analysis. Moreover, it exploits the equivalence between A–stability and G–stability
[
15
]
; see also
[
21
, Ch. V. 6]
.
For methods that are not A–stable, one constructs additional multipliers
[
26
]
; see also
[
21
, Ch. V. 8]
.
In practice, explicitly constructing the
G
matrix is often tricky. Hence, the classical way is to prove that the method is A–stable (potentially with appropriate multipliers) and then infer the existence of
G
. In this paper, we proceed differently and explicitly construct the weighted norm. Our main results are:

(i)

We present a novel way to construct the related delay equation of a given order, which, in contrast to the approach presented in
[
6
]
, is explicit and requires fewer delays.

(ii)

We use the norm defined through
G
(assuming that it exists) to present a novel convergence proof that works for any BDF order and provides a sharper characterization of the weak coupling condition compared to
[
6
]
.

(iii)

We explicitly construct the weighting matrix
G
for convergence up to order three.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. After this introduction, the abstract model problem is introduced in
Section

2
with particular examples from poroelasticity.
The design of semi-explicit methods via delay systems and their well-posedness is discussed in
Section

3
. The main result of this article is
Theorem

4.5
, where the convergence analysis of the decoupling multistep methods based on BDF schemes is given. The tool to prove higher-order convergence is
4.3
, which is the basis for the telescoping argument used in the proof.
The evidence for this assumption is discussed thoroughly in
Section

4
. Finally, we verify the theoretical results numerically in
Section

5
.

2.
Abstract Formulation and Examples

Consider the Hilbert spaces

\mathcal{V}\vcentcolon=[H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)]^{m},\qquad\mathcal{Q}\vcentcolon=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{V}$}}\vcentcolon=[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{m},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\vcentcolon=L^{2}(\Omega),

resulting in the Gelfand triples
\mathcal{V}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{V}$}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{V}$}}^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{*}

and
\mathcal{Q}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, cf.
[
33
, Sec. 23.4]
.
The system of interest is the following abstract coupled elliptic–parabolic
problem:
given sufficiently smooth source terms
f\colon[0,T]\to\mathcal{V}^{*}

and
g\colon[0,T]\to\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, seek abstract functions

u\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
and
p\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
such that for
almost every
t\in(0,T]
it holds that

(2.1a)

\displaystyle a(u,v)-d(v,p)

\displaystyle=\langle f,v\rangle,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle d(\dot{u},q)+c(\dot{p},q)+b(p,q)

\displaystyle=\langle g,q\rangle

for all test functions
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
As initial data, we assume

(2.1c)

\displaystyle u(0)=u^{0}\in\mathcal{V},\qquad p(0)=p^{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}

to be
consistent
, i.e.,
we assume
a(u^{0},v)-d(v,p^{0})=\langle f(0),v\rangle
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
.
The bilinear forms
a\colon\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
,

b\colon\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
c\colon\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\times\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\to\mathbb{R}

are assumed to be symmetric, continuous, and elliptic in their respective spaces.
The corresponding ellipticity and continuity constants of
\mathfrak{a}\in\{a,b,c\}
are denoted by

c_{\mathfrak{a}},C_{\mathfrak{a}}>0
, respectively.
For notational convenience we introduce the

b
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{b}\vcentcolon=b(\cdot,\cdot)^{1/2}
and the

c
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{c}\vcentcolon=c(\cdot,\cdot)^{1/2}
satisfying

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{C_{b}}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{b}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{c_{b}}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\tfrac{1}{C_{c}}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{c}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{c_{c}}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{c},

respectively.
Furthermore,
d\colon\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded,
i.e., we assume that there exists a positive constant
C_{d}>0
such
that
d(u,p)\leq C_{d}\|u\|_{\mathcal{V}}\|p\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}
for
all
u\in\mathcal{V}
,
p\in\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}
.
Without the coupling term represented by the bilinear form
d
,
equation (
2.1a
) is elliptic in
u
and equation (
2.1b
)
is parabolic in
p
.
This explains why system (
2.1
) is called an elliptic–parabolic problem.
At this point, we would like to emphasize that
c(\dot{p},q)
– and similarly
d(\dot{u},q)
– should be understood as
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}c(p,q)
, see also
[
25
]
.

Example 2.1

(linear poroelasticity)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
[0,T]
a time interval with
T>0
.
We consider the quasi-static Biot poroelasticity model with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions as introduced in
[
11
]
,
where one seeks the deformation
u\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}

and the pressure
p\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(2.2a)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\sigma(u)+\alpha\nabla p

\displaystyle={\hat{f}}\qquad\text{in }(0,T]\times\Omega,

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\alpha\nabla\cdot u+\tfrac{1}{M}p\big)+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla p)

\displaystyle={\hat{g}}\qquad\text{in }(0,T]\times\Omega.

Here,
\sigma
denotes the stress tensor

\sigma(u)={\mu}\,\big(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf{T}}\big)+{\lambda}\,(\nabla\cdot u)\id

with Lamé coefficients
{\lambda}
and
{\mu}
.
The permeability is denoted with
\kappa
.
Moreover,
\alpha
denotes the Biot–Willis fluid–solid coupling coefficient
and
M
the Biot modulus.
The right-hand sides
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
are
the volumetric load and the fluid source terms, respectively,
modeling an injection or production process.
We refer to
[
5
]
for the definition of the corresponding bilinear forms resulting in (
2.1
).

Example 2.2

(multiple network poroelasticity)
.

A generalization of the Biot poroelasticity model
is given by the multiple network poroelasticity theory, where we seek the
deformation
u\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and pressure
variables
p_{i}\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\dots,J

where
J\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of fluid networks,
satisfying the coupled system

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\sigma(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}\nabla p_{i}

\displaystyle={\hat{f}}\qquad\text{in }(0,T]\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Big(\alpha_{i}\nabla\cdot u+\tfrac{1}{M_{i}}p_{i}\Big)+\nabla\cdot\big(\kappa_{i}\nabla p_{i}\big)+\sum_{j\neq i}^{J}\beta_{ij}(p_{i}-p_{j})

\displaystyle={\hat{g}}_{i}\qquad\text{in }(0,T]\times\Omega.

Given some additional assumptions on the exchange rates between the fluid networks
\beta_{ij}
, this model has a similar structure as (
2.1
).
In practice, the exchange rates are usually symmetric and sufficiently small such that the ellipticity property of the bilinear form
b
is satisfied
[
7
]
.

To describe the stability of the decoupling schemes introduced in this article, we define

(2.3)

\omega\vcentcolon=\tfrac{C_{d}^{2}}{c_{a}c_{c}},

as the
coupling strength
between the elliptic and the parabolic equation.
This value plays a crucial role for the convergence analysis and depends on the physical coefficients of the application. We refer to
[
16
, Tab. 4]
for concrete values appearing in poroelasticity; see also
[
6
, Tab. 3.1]
.

The abstract formulation (
2.1
) can also be written in terms of operators in the respective dual spaces. For this, let
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{*}
,
\mathcal{B}\colon\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{*}
,
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}
,
and
\mathcal{D}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}^{*}
be the operators associated with the bilinear forms
a
,
b
,
c
, and
d
respectively. This then leads to the equivalent formulation

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u-\mathcal{D}^{*}p

\displaystyle=f\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{V}^{*},

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}{\dot{u}}+\mathcal{C}{\dot{p}}+\mathcal{B}p

\displaystyle=g\hskip 22.47748pt\text{in }\mathcal{Q}^{*}.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is invertible, we can eliminate the variable
u
, leading to the parabolic equation

(2.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}+\mathcal{C})\dot{p}+\mathcal{B}p

\displaystyle=r,

where
r\vcentcolon=g-\mathcal{D}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\dot{f}
and
\mathcal{M}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{*}
is a self-adjoint and non-negative operator.

3.
Related Delay Equations

The key idea for the construction as well as convergence analysis of a decoupling time integration scheme in
[
5
]
is to introduce an approximation of the coupled elliptic–parabolic problem (
2.4
) by adding a time delay
\tau>0
in (
2.4a
), where the time delay equals the time step size. In more detail, one considers the delay system

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\bar{u}-\mathcal{D}^{*}\widehat{p}(t;\tau)

\displaystyle=f\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{V}^{*},

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\dot{\bar{u}}+\mathcal{C}{\dot{\bar{p}}}+\mathcal{B}{\bar{p}}

\displaystyle=g\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{Q}^{*},

where
\widehat{p}(t;\tau)
is an approximation of
\bar{p}
using the time delay
\tau
. In the first-order case studied in
[
5
]
we have
\widehat{p}(t;\tau)=\Delta_{\tau}{\bar{p}}(t)
with the shift operator
\Delta_{\tau}{p}\vcentcolon=p(\,\cdot-\tau)
. Let us emphasize that, in contrast to the original system (
2.4
), such a delay system calls for a
history function
, i.e., a prescription of
\bar{p}
for
-\tau\leq t\leq 0
.

If an implicit time discretization scheme of order
k
with time step
size
\tau
is used to discretize (
3.1
), then the
equations (
3.1a
) and (
3.1b
) are
decoupled in the sense that one can first solve (
3.1a
)
for
\bar{u}
and then (
3.1b
) for
\bar{p}
.
This was done and analyzed for the implicit Euler method in
[
5
]
and for
BDF-
2
with a different
\hat{p}
in
[
6
]
.
It goes without saying that a higher-order scheme is only reasonable if the delay
approximation (
3.1
) is of the same order.

3.1.
Higher-order approximation via Taylor series expansion

For a higher-order approximation, a general time delayed approximation of
p(t)
is
sought.
This means that we consider a delay term
{\widehat{p}}(t;\tau)
with

p(t)={\widehat{p}}(t;\tau)+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\delta}).

One straightforward possibility is to consider a Taylor series expansion of
p(t)
around
t-\tau
.
This, however, leads to a
delay differential equations
(DDEs) of advanced type — see
[
10
]
for a precise definition — as shown in the following example, which is motivated from
[
30
, Ex. 2.1]
.

Example 3.1
.

Consider two terms in the Taylor series, i.e.,
\bar{p}\approx\Delta_{\tau}{{{\bar{p}}}}+\tau\Delta_{\tau}{{{\dot{\bar{p}}}}}
. Solving (
3.1a
) for
\bar{u}
and substituting this in (
3.1b
) results the inherent delay differential equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}{\dot{\bar{p}}}+\mathcal{B}{\bar{p}}=r-\mathcal{M}(\Delta_{\tau}{{{\dot{\bar{p}}}}}+\tau\Delta_{\tau}{{{\ddot{\bar{p}}}}}).

If we consider the scalar case where we replace all operators by the constant one and set
f\equiv 0
,
g\equiv 0
, then we obtain
{\dot{\bar{y}}}+{\bar{y}}=\Delta_{\tau}{{{\dot{\bar{y}}}}}+\tau\Delta_{\tau}{{{\ddot{\bar{y}}}}}
. The corresponding first-order formulation

{\dot{\bar{y}}}={\bar{z}},\qquad{\bar{z}}+{\bar{y}}=\Delta_{\tau}{{\bar{z}}}+\tau\Delta_{\tau}{{{\dot{\bar{z}}}}}

equals a DDE of advanced type, since the second equation involves

\Delta_{\tau}{\dot{\bar{z}}}
but not
\dot{\bar{z}}
.
To illustrate the inherent difficulties of DDEs of advances type, we set
\tau=1
and consider

\phi(t)=y^{0}+\tfrac{1}{n}\sin(n\pi t)

for
t\in[-1,0]
as history function.
The solution can be obtained using Bellman’s method of
steps
[
9
, Ch. 3.4]
and yields

\displaystyle{\bar{y}}(t)=\begin{cases}y^{0}+\frac{1}{n}\sin(n\pi t),&-1\leq t\leq 0\\
y^{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}+\pi\cos(n\pi)\mathrm{e}^{-t}-\pi\cos(n\pi(t-1)),&\phantom{-}0\leq t\leq 1\\
y^{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}+\pi\cos(n\pi)\mathrm{e}^{-t}-\pi\mathrm{e}^{-t+1}-n\pi^{2}\sin(n\pi(t-2)),&\phantom{-}1\leq t\leq 2\\
y^{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}+\pi\cos(n\pi)\mathrm{e}^{-t}-\pi\mathrm{e}^{-t+1}\\
\qquad\qquad+n^{2}\pi^{2}\cos(n\pi)\mathrm{e}^{-t+2}-n^{2}\pi^{3}\cos(n\pi(t-3)),&\phantom{-}2\leq t\leq 3\end{cases}.

Although the history function is uniformly bounded for all
n
, the solution in
each successive interval grows with an increasing factor of
n
.
In this sense, the delay system is ill-posed.

Solving DDEs of advanced type requires a distributional solution concept even in the finite-dimensional setting
[
29
,
31
]
. To overcome this difficulty, we instead aim for a higher-order approximation via multiple delays

3.2.
Higher-order approximation via multiple delays

We choose
\widehat{p}(t;\tau)
in (
3.1
) as the Lagrange interpolation polynomial of degree
\delta-1
for the interpolation points
s_{\ell}\vcentcolon=t-\ell\tau

\widehat{p}(t;\tau)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}\,\bar{p}(s_{\ell})L_{\ell}(t),\qquad\qquad L_{\ell}(t)=\prod_{j=1,\,j\neq\ell}^{\delta}\frac{t-s_{j}}{s_{\ell}-s_{j}}.

Substituting the particular form of the interpolation points, we obtain

L_{\ell}(t)\equiv(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}=\vcentcolon c_{\delta,\ell}\quad\ell=1,\dots,\delta.

We would like to emphasize that

,

Remark 3.3
.

The recipe in
[
6
]
to obtain the delay system is to replace the time derivatives of the delayed terms in the Taylor series expansion with finite differences of appropriate order such that the resulting approximation is still of order
\delta
but of neutral delay type. For
\delta\leq 3
, this approach resembles the schemes we obtain here, but for an approximation of order
\tau^{4}
already five delays are required. In contrast, we obtain a closed form expression for the delay term
{\hat{p}}(\,\cdot\,;\tau)
with
\delta
delays.

Substituting the expression for
\widehat{p}(\,\cdot\,;\tau)
from (
3.2
) in (
3.1
) yields the system

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\bar{u}}-\mathcal{D}^{*}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}\Delta_{\ell\tau}{\bar{p}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=f\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{V}^{*},

(3.3b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}{\dot{\bar{u}}}+\mathcal{C}{\dot{\bar{p}}}+\mathcal{B}{\bar{p}}

\displaystyle=g\hskip 22.47748pt\text{in }\mathcal{Q}^{*}

with
\delta
delays.
For
\delta=1
, we recover the already mentioned delay system of order one introduced in
[
5
]
, and for
\delta\in\{2,3\}
the delayed systems from
[
6
]
.
In (
3.3
), we seek solutions
\bar{u}\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
and
\bar{p}\colon[-\delta\tau,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
given sufficiently smooth right-hand sides, initial data, and a history function
\Phi\in C^{\infty}([-\delta\tau,0],\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})
for

that ensures the consistency of initial conditions of the original problem (

).
For this, a sufficient condition reads

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Phi(-\ell\tau)=\Phi(0)=p^{0}\qquad\text{ for all }\ell=1,\cdots,\delta,

since this implies with (

) that
\mathcal{A}{\bar{u}}(0)-\mathcal{D}^{*}p^{0}=\mathcal{A}{\bar{u}}(0)-\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}\Big)\mathcal{D}^{*}p^{0}=f(0)
and hence,
{\bar{u}}(0)=u^{0}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Distance to delay solution)
.

Assume sufficiently smooth right-hand sides
f,g
, consistent initial data (
2.1c
), and a history function
\Phi\in C^{\infty}([-\delta\tau,0],\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})
which satisfies (
3.4
).
Then there exists a solution
({\bar{u}},{\bar{p}})
of the delay problem (
3.3
) which satisfies
{\bar{p}}\in W^{\delta+1,\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})
. Moreover, the solutions of (
2.4
) and (
3.3
) only differ by a term of order
\delta
, i.e., for almost every
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\|{\bar{u}}(t)-u(t)\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}+\|{\bar{p}}(t)-p(t)\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\lesssim\tau^{2\delta}t\Big(\|{\bar{p}}\|_{W^{\delta+1,\infty}(0,t;\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})}^{2}+\|{\Phi}\|_{W^{\delta+1,\infty}(-\delta\tau,0;\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})}^{2}\Big).

Proof.

The boundedness of
\bar{p}
is no different than the one considered in
[
5
]
, except for the specification of the history function. Hence, we refer to
[
5
, Prop. A. 4]
for the proof.
Now define
e_{p}\vcentcolon={\bar{p}}-p
and
e_{u}\vcentcolon={\bar{u}}-u
.
Taking the difference of (
2.4
) and (
3.1
) as well as the difference of the time derivatives of (
2.4a
) and (
3.1a
),
Lemma

A.1
yields

(3.5a)

\displaystyle a(e_{u},v)-d(v,e_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}d\Big(v,{\bar{p}}^{(\delta)}(\zeta)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

(3.5b)

\displaystyle a({\dot{e}}_{u},v)-d(v,{\dot{e}}_{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}d\Big(v,{\bar{p}}^{(\delta+1)}(\zeta)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

and

(3.5c)

\displaystyle d({\dot{e}}_{u},q)+c({\dot{e}}_{p},q)+b(e_{p},q)=0

for all test functions
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
The sum of (
3.5c
) and (
3.5b
) tested with
v={\dot{e}}_{u},q={\dot{e}}_{p}
results in

\displaystyle\|{\dot{e}}_{u}\|_{a}^{2}+\|{\dot{e}}_{p}\|_{c}^{2}

4.2.
Summation assumption

For the ease of presentation, we introduce the following notation.
Given a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and a matrix

,
where

is the set of real-valued symmetric positive definite matrices
of size

\|Y\|_{G}^{2}\vcentcolon=\langle GY,Y\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{s}}\qquad\text{for }Y\in\mathcal{X}^{s}.

Moreover, for a sequence
(y^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
y^{n}\in\mathcal{X}
and
numbers

, we define

\displaystyle Y^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}y^{n}\\
\vdots\\
y^{n-k-\delta+1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}^{k+\delta}.

For the convergence analysis, we need the following algebraic criterion that leads to

–stability.
Here, we follow the multiplier technique of Nevanlinna and Odeh
[
26
]
but formulate the criterion as an assumption which needs to be verified afterwards.

Assumption 4.3

(Summation assumption)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Hilbert space and
\omega,\tau>0
.
Then there exist a parameter
\eta\in[0,1)
and for any
\mu\in[0,\omega]

a matrix

and a vector

such that for any sequence

\{y^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with

for

we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\tau\Xi_{k}{(y^{n})}+\mu\Psi_{\delta}(\tau\Xi_{k}{(y^{n})})

\displaystyle,y^{n}-\eta y^{n-1}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}=\|Y^{n}\|_{G}^{2}-\|Y^{n-1}\|_{G}^{2}+\Big\|\sum_{i=0}^{k+\delta}\gamma_{i}y^{n-i}\Big\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2},

where

is defined as in (

is the discrete delay operator defined

).

Let us emphasize that

,
since

is independent of

,
and that the matrix

In the forthcoming convergence proof, we need to estimate the matrix

Assumption 4.4
.

Let the notation be as in

(4.8)

\displaystyle c_{G}\vcentcolon=\inf_{\mu\in[0,\omega]}\lambda(G(\mu))>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{G}\vcentcolon=\sup_{\mu\in[0,\omega]}\lambda(G(\mu))<\infty,

4.3.
Convergence analysis

In the following, we use

The verification of these assumptions is then subject of the upcoming

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider the solution
{\bar{p}}
of the delay equation (
3.8
)
for a sufficiently smooth right-hand side
r\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.
Let
p^{n}
be its approximation at time step
t^{n}

given by (
4.4
).
If
4.3
and
4.4
hold, then we get

\|p^{n}-{\bar{p}}(t^{n})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}+\tau\sum_{\ell=k}^{n}\|p^{\ell}-{\bar{p}}(t^{\ell})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}\lesssim\tau^{2k}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\|p^{\ell}-{\bar{p}}(t^{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}^{2}.

Proof.

We define the error and the defect by

e^{n}\vcentcolon=p^{n}-{\bar{p}}(t^{n}),\qquad d^{n}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\tau}\,\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\xi_{k,\ell}\bar{p}(t^{n-\ell})\bigg)-\dot{\bar{p}}(t^{n}).

Due to the construction of the BDF operator, we immediately conclude that the defect satisfies the estimate

\|d^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}\lesssim\tau^{k}
.
Considering the difference between (
3.8
)
and (
4.4
) and testing with
e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}
, where
\eta
is the multiplier from
4.3
, we obtain

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathcal{C}\tau\Xi_{k}{(e^{n})}+\mathcal{M}\Psi_{\delta}(\tau\Xi_{k}{(e^{n})})

\displaystyle,e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\Big\rangle+\tau\,\langle\mathcal{B}e^{n},e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tau\,\Big\langle d^{n}+\mathcal{M}\Psi_{\delta}(\tau\Xi_{k}{(d^{n})}),e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\Big\rangle.

Since
\mathcal{B}
is self-adjoint, we have

\displaystyle 2\,\langle\mathcal{B}e^{n},e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\rangle=\|e^{n}\|_{b}^{2}-\eta^{2}\,\|e^{n-1}\|_{b}^{2}+\|e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\|_{b}^{2}\geq\|e^{n}\|_{b}^{2}-\eta^{2}\,\|e^{n-1}\|_{b}^{2}.

We now consider the spectral decomposition of the operator
\mathcal{M}
w.r.t. the inner product defined by the bilinear form
c
. This means that we consider functions
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}
forming an
orthonormal basis
of
\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}
(in the
c
-norm), satisfying

\mathcal{M}\phi_{j}=\mu_{j}\mathcal{C}\phi_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle\mathcal{C}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle=\delta_{ji}

with non-negative eigenvalues
0\leq\mu_{j}\leq\omega
; cf.
[
13
, Thm. 6.11]
.
Now define
\epsilon_{i}^{n}\vcentcolon=\langle\mathcal{C}e^{n},\phi_{i}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}

and
E_{i}^{n}\vcentcolon=[\epsilon_{i}^{n},\ldots,\epsilon_{i}^{n-k-\delta+1}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\delta}
, leading to the basis expansion

e^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}_{i}\phi_{i}
.
Using that all eigenvalues are bounded
above by
\omega
and
4.3
,
the first term in (
4.9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathcal{C}\tau\Xi_{k}{(e^{n})}+\mathcal{M}\Psi_{\delta}(\tau\Xi_{k}{(e^{n})}),e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big\langle\tau\Xi_{k}{(\epsilon^{n}_{i})}+\mu_{i}\Psi_{\delta}(\tau\Xi_{k}{(\epsilon^{n})}),\epsilon^{n}_{i}-\eta\epsilon^{n-1}_{i}\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(\|E^{n}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}-\|E^{n-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}\Big).

Due to the orthonormality of the
\phi_{i}
functions,
we have
\|e^{n}\|^{2}_{c}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\epsilon^{n}_{i})^{2}
.
In the same way, it holds that

\displaystyle\|E^{n}\|^{2}_{c}=\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}E^{n}_{i}\phi_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}E^{n}_{j}\phi_{j}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}^{k+\delta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\mathcal{C}E^{n}_{i}\phi_{i},E^{n}_{j}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}^{k+\delta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|E^{n}_{i}\|^{2}

where
E_{i}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\delta}
and
E^{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}^{k+\delta}

(see (
4.6
)).
Using the weighted Young’s inequality for the right-hand side
of (
4.9
), we obtain with (yet to be determined constants)

\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3},\delta_{4}>0
,

\displaystyle\Big\langle d^{n}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{M}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}d^{n-\ell}\Big),e^{n}-\eta e^{n-1}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle d^{n},e^{n}\rangle-\eta\,\langle d^{n},e^{n-1}\rangle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}\Big(\langle\mathcal{M}d^{n-\ell},e^{n}\rangle-\eta\,\langle\mathcal{M}d^{n-\ell},e^{n-1}\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{2}\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{1}}\|d^{n}\|^{2}\big)+\eta\,\big(\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{2}\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{2}}\|d^{n}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}|c_{\delta,\ell}|\Big(\tfrac{\delta_{3}}{2}\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{3}}\|\mathcal{M}d^{n-\ell}\|^{2}\Big)+\eta\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}|c_{\delta,\ell}|\Big(\tfrac{\delta_{4}}{2}\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{4}}\|\mathcal{M}d^{n-\ell}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{2}+\tfrac{\bar{c}_{\delta}\delta_{3}}{2}\big)\|e^{n}\|^{2}+\eta\,\big(\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{2}+\tfrac{\bar{c}_{\delta}\delta_{4}}{2}\big)\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}+\big(\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{1}}+\tfrac{\eta}{2\delta_{2}}\big)\|d^{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+c_{c}^{2}\,\omega^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{3}}+\tfrac{\eta}{2\delta_{4}}\big)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}|c_{\delta,\ell}|\|d^{n-\ell}\|^{2},

where
\bar{c}_{\delta}\vcentcolon=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}|c_{\delta,\ell}|
.
Note that we have made use of

\|\mathcal{M}\cdot\|\leq c_{c}\,\omega\|\cdot\|

in the last inequality.
With the choices

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{b}}{4},

\displaystyle\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{b}\eta}{2},

\displaystyle\delta_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{b}}{4}\frac{1}{\bar{c}_{\delta}},

\displaystyle\delta_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{b}\eta}{2}\frac{1}{\bar{c}_{\delta}}

we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\|E^{n}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}-\|E^{n-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}\tau\|e^{n}\|_{b}^{2}-\tau\eta^{2}\|e^{n-1}\|_{b}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tau\tfrac{3}{c_{b}}\|d^{n}\|^{2}+\tau c_{c}^{2}\,\omega^{2}\tfrac{3\bar{c}_{\delta}}{c_{b}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}|c_{\delta,\ell}|\|d^{n-\ell}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim(1+\omega^{2})\,\tau^{2k+1}.

Using
4.4
, we observe that

\displaystyle\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}_{b}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{{C^{2}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}C_{b}}{c_{c}}\|e^{n-1}\|^{2}_{c}=\tfrac{{C^{2}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}C_{b}}{c_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\epsilon^{n-1}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{{C^{2}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}C_{b}}{c_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{c_{G(\mu_{i})}}\|E^{n-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}\leq\tfrac{{C_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}C_{b}}{c_{c}c_{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|E^{n-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2},

where
{C_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}}\leq{C_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$$\hookrightarrow$$\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}$}}}\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}
.
We then get the following perturbed sum

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\|E^{n}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}-(1+\tau C)\|E^{n-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}\Bigg)+\tau\|e^{n}\|^{2}\lesssim(1+\omega^{2})\,\tau^{2k+1},

.
To see that the Grönwall-type inequality from
Lemma

A.2

can be applied, define
{a}^{n}\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|E^{n}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|E^{n}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}+\tau\sum_{\ell=k}^{n}\|e^{\ell}\|^{2}\lesssim e^{Cn\tau}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|E^{k-1}_{i}\|_{G(\mu_{i})}^{2}+t^{n}\tau^{2k}\Bigg).

6.
Conclusions

The convergence of semi-explicit methods based on BDF-
k
schemes is proven for
k\leq 3
. The essential tool of the proof is the assumption on the G-stability of the schemes. In contrast to the standard G-stability concept, the inclusion of delay terms leads to a dependence on the spectrum of the involved operators of the problem. The evidence for this assumption is verified in this article.

In future work, we aim to show that the semi-explicit schemes are A-stable for appropriate multipliers, which would directly imply G-stability. Moreover, we plan to extend the convergence proof to the semi-explicit methods based on Runge–Kutta schemes.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider
{\bar{p}}\in C^{\delta+1}([-\delta\tau,T];\mathcal{H}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathcal{Q}$}})

and
\widehat{p}(\,\cdot\,;\tau)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}\Delta_{\ell\tau}{{\bar{p}}}
.
Then,

{\bar{p}}(t)-{\widehat{p}}(t;\tau)=-R_{T}^{\delta,\delta}=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\delta})\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\dot{\bar{p}}}(t)-{\dot{\widehat{p}}}(t;\tau)=-R_{T}^{\delta,\delta+1}=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\delta})

with remainder
R_{T}^{\delta,k}=\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}{\bar{p}}^{(k)}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta
.

Proof.

The Taylor series expansion of
\Delta_{\ell\tau}{{\bar{p}}}
around
t
is

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ell\tau}{{\bar{p}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\delta-1}\frac{(-\ell\tau)^{i}}{i!}{\bar{p}}^{(i)}(t)+\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}{\bar{p}}^{(\delta)}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Substituting this in (
3.2
), we obtain

(A.1)

\displaystyle{\widehat{p}}(t;\tau)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}c_{\delta,\ell}\Delta_{\ell\tau}{{\bar{p}}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\delta-1}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}\ell^{i}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\bigg)\frac{(-\tau)^{i}}{i!}{\bar{p}}^{(i)}(t)+R_{T}^{\delta,\delta},

where

(A.2)

\displaystyle R_{T}^{\delta,\delta}=\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}{\bar{p}}^{(\delta)}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

The first summand in (
A.1
), i.e., for
i=0
, we obtain

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\,(-1)^{(\ell-1)}{\bar{p}}(t)=\big(-(1-1)^{\delta}+1\big)\,{\bar{p}}(t)={\bar{p}}(t).

Observe that for
0<i<\delta
, we can obtain the inner sum in (
A.1
) by substituting
x=-1
in

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\,\ell^{i}\,x^{(\ell-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\Big[x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\,\cdot\,)\Big]^{i}\big((1+x)^{\delta}-1\big)=\frac{1}{x}\Big[(1+x)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\,\cdot\,)-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\,\cdot\,)\Big]^{i}(1+x)^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\binom{i}{\ell}\bigg(\Big[(1+x)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\,\cdot\,)\Big]^{i-\ell}(1+x)^{\delta}\bigg)\bigg(\Big[-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(\,\cdot\,)\Big]^{\ell}(1+x)^{\delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\binom{i}{\ell}\bigg(\delta^{(i-\ell)}(1+x)^{\delta}\bigg)\bigg((-1)^{\ell}\binom{\delta}{\ell}(1+x)^{\delta-\ell}\bigg),

which gives
0
for all
0<i<\delta
.
Therefore,
{\bar{p}}(t)-{\widehat{p}}(t;\tau)=-R_{T}^{\delta,\delta}=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\delta})
.
Similarly, it can be shown that
{\dot{\bar{p}}}(t)-{\dot{\widehat{p}}}(t;\tau)=-R_{T}^{\delta,\delta+1}=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\delta})
with

\displaystyle R_{T}^{\delta,\delta+1}=\frac{1}{(\delta-1)!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta}(-1)^{(\ell-1)}\binom{\delta}{\ell}\int_{t-\ell\tau}^{t}(t-\zeta)^{\delta-1}{\bar{p}}^{(\delta+1)}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.\qed

We use the following perturbed Grönwall-type inequality in the proof of
Theorem

4.5
.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider sequences of positive numbers
\{a^{n}\}
,
\{b^{n}\}
, and
\{c^{n}\}

satisfying

a^{n}-(1+\tau C)\,a^{n-1}+b^{n}\leq c^{n}

for some positive constants
\tau,C>0
. Then it holds that

{a}^{n}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}{b}^{\ell}\leq e^{Cn\tau}\Big({a}^{0}+\frac{1}{C\tau}\max_{1\leq\ell\leq n}{c^{\ell}}\Big).

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to transform the perturbed sum into a telescoping sum, see also
[
18
]
.
For this, we set

{\tilde{a}}^{n}\vcentcolon=(1+\tau C)^{-n}a^{n}
.
Then the assumed inequality implies

\displaystyle{\tilde{a}}^{n}-{\tilde{a}}^{n-1}=(1+\tau C)^{-n}\big(a^{n}-(1+\tau C)\,a^{n-1}\big)\leq(1+\tau C)^{-n}c^{n}-(1+\tau C)^{-n}b^{n}.

Summing up from
1
to
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\tilde{a}}^{n}\leq{a}^{0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(1+\tau C)^{-\ell}\max_{1\leq\ell\leq n}{c^{\ell}}-(1+\tau C)^{-n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}b^{\ell},

where we have used that
-(1+\tau C)^{-\ell}\leq-(1+\tau C)^{-n}
.
With the observations

(1+\tau C)^{n}\leq e^{Cn\tau}\quad\text{and}\quad(1+\tau C)^{-n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(1+\tau C)^{\ell}\leq(1+\tau C)^{-n}\frac{(1+\tau C)^{n}-1}{C\tau}\leq\frac{1}{C\tau}

we get the claimed result.
∎
