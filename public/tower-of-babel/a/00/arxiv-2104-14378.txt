Turbulent Prandtl number and characteristic length scales in stably stratified flows: steady-state analytical solutions
Sukanta Basu, Albert A. M. Holtslag

Abstract
In this study, the stability dependence of turbulent Prandtl number ( P ​ r t Pr_{t} ) is quantified via a novel and simple analytical approach. Based on the variance and flux budget equations, a hybrid length scale formulation is first proposed and its functional relationships to well-known length scales are established. Next, the ratios of these length scales are utilized to derive an explicit relationship between P ​ r t Pr_{t} and gradient Richardson number. In addition, theoretical predictions are made for several key turbulence variables (e.g., dissipation rates, normalized fluxes). The results from our proposed approach are compared against other competing formulations as well as published datasets. Overall, the agreement between the different approaches is rather good despite their different theoretical foundations and assumptions.



I
Introduction

According to the K-theory, based on the celebrated hypothesis of Boussinesq in 1877, turbulent fluxes can be approximated as products of the eddy exchange coefficients (known as the Austausch coefficients in earlier literature) and the mean gradients
Lumley and Panofsky 1964
. Specifically, for incompressible, horizontally homogeneous, boundary layer flows, the along-wind momentum flux (
\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}
) and the sensible heat flux (
\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}
) can be simply written as follows:

\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}=-K_{M}S,

(1a)

\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}=-K_{H}\Gamma.

(1b)

Here
S
and
\Gamma
denote the vertical gradients of the mean along-wind velocity component and the mean potential temperature, respectively. The eddy viscosity and diffusivity for heat are represented by
K_{M}
and
K_{H}
, respectively. In contrast to molecular diffusivities, these eddy exchange coefficients are not intrinsic properties of the fluid
Arya 2001
;
Stull 1988
; rather, they depend on the nature of the turbulent flows (e.g., stability) and position in the flow (e.g., distance from the wall).

The ratio of
K_{M}
and
K_{H}
is known as the turbulent Prandtl number:

Pr_{t}=\frac{K_{M}}{K_{H}}.

(2)

This variable is fundamentally different from the molecular Prandtl number:

Pr_{m}=\frac{\nu}{\alpha},

(3)

where,
\nu
and
\alpha
denote kinematic viscosity and thermal diffusivity, respectively. According to a vast amount of literature,
Pr_{t}
is strongly dependent on buoyancy and somewhat weakly dependent on other factors (see below).

For non-buoyant (also called neutral) flows, in this paper, the turbulent Prandtl number is denoted as
Pr_{t0}
. In the past, for simplicity, a number of studies assumed
Pr_{t0}=1
by invoking the so-called ‘Reynolds analogy’ hypothesis
Reynolds 1974
;
Sutton 1955
;
Tennekes and Lumley 1972
. Basically, they implicitly assume that the turbulent transport of momentum and heat are identical. However, this assumption of
Pr_{t0}=1
is not supported by the vast majority of experimental data (see
Kays 1994
and the references therein). On this issue, Launder
Launder 1978
commented:

“It would also be helpful to dispel the idea that a turbulent Prandtl number of unity was in any sense the “normal” value. We shall see […] that a value of about 0.7 has a far stronger claim to normality.”

Perhaps, it is not a mere coincidence that the theoretical study of Yakhot et al.
Yakhot et al. 1987
predicted that
Pr_{t0}
asymptotically approaches 0.7179 in the limit of infinite
Re
(see also
Sukoriansky et al. 2005
). One of the most cited studies in atmospheric science, by Businger et al.
Businger et al. 1971
, also reported
Pr_{t0}=
0.74. According to a review article by Kays
Kays 1994
, for laboratory flows,
Pr_{t0}
typically falls within the range of 0.7 to 0.9; the most frequent value being equal to 0.85. Most commercial computational fluid dynamics packages (e.g., Fluent, OpenFOAM) assume 0.85 to be the default
Pr_{t0}
value.

There is some evidence that
Pr_{t0}
may not be a universal constant; it might weakly depend on
Pr_{m}
,
Re
, and/or position in the flow. However, there is no general agreement in the literature on this matter (e.g.,
Antonia and Kim 1991
). Reynolds
Reynolds 1975
summarized numerous empirical and semi-empirical formulations capturing such dependencies for a wide range of fluids (including air, water, liquid metal) and engineering flows (e.g., pipe flow, jet flow, shear flow). However, to the best of our knowledge, these formulations are yet to be confirmed for high-
Re
atmospheric flows. In such flows, buoyancy effects have been found to be far more dominant than any other factors.

In atmospheric flows, especially under stably stratified conditions, the value of
Pr_{t}
departs significantly from
Pr_{t0}
. Over the decades, several empirical formulations have been developed by various research groups (see
Li 2019
for a recent review). For example, by regression analysis of aircraft measurements from different field campaigns, Kim and Mahrt
Kim and Mahrt 1992
proposed:

Pr_{t}=1+3.8Ri_{g},

(4)

where,
Ri_{g}
is the gradient Richardson number, commonly used to quantify atmospheric stability. It is defined as follows:

Ri_{g}=\frac{\left(\frac{g}{\Theta_{0}}\right)\Gamma}{S^{2}}=\frac{\beta\Gamma}{S^{2}}=\frac{N^{2}}{S^{2}}.

(5)

Where,
g
is the gravitational acceleration and
\Theta_{0}
represents a reference temperature. The variable
\beta
is known as the buoyancy parameter. The so-called Brunt Väisälä frequency is denoted by
N
.

More recently, Anderson
Anderson 2009
conducted rigorous statistical analysis of observational data from the Antarctic. By avoiding the self-correlation issue, he proposed the following empirical relationship for
0.01<Ri_{g}<0.25
:

Pr_{t}^{-1}=(0.84\pm 0.03)Ri_{g}^{-0.105\pm 0.012}.

(6)

Clearly, the
Ri_{g}
-dependence of
Pr_{t}
becomes rather weak as the stability of the flow decreases.

In addition to field observational data, laboratory and simulated data were also utilized to quantify the
Pr_{t}
–
Ri_{g}
relationship. In this regard, a popular semi-empirical formulation by Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
is worth noting:

Pr_{t}=Pr_{t0}\exp\left(-\frac{Ri_{g}}{Pr_{t0}R_{f\infty}}\right)+\frac{Ri_{g}}{R_{f\infty}},

(7)

where,
R_{f\infty}
is the asymptotic value of the flux Richardson number (
R_{f}=Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}
) for strongly stratified conditions. Recently, Venayagamoorthy and Stretch
Venayagamoorthy and Stretch 2010
used direct numerical simulation (DNS) data and revised the formulation by Eq. (
7
) as follows:

Pr_{t}=Pr_{t0}\exp\left[-\frac{Ri_{g}\left(1-R_{f\infty}\right)}{Pr_{t0}R_{f\infty}}\right]+\frac{Ri_{g}}{R_{f\infty}}.

(8)

For all practical purposes, the differences between Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
) are quite small.

In parallel to observational and simulation studies, there have been a handful of attempts to derive the
Pr_{t}
—
Ri_{g}
formulations from the governing equations with certain assumptions. In the appendices, we have summarized two competing hypotheses by Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
and Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
. The readers are also encouraged to peruse the following papers describing other relevant hypotheses:
Cheng et al. 2002
,
Cheng et al. 2020
, and
Kantha and Luce 2018
. In the present study, we report an alternative analytical derivation which leads to a closed-form
Pr_{t}
—
Ri_{g}
relationship.

II
Analytical Derivations

In this section, based on the variance and flux budget equations, we first derive a hybrid length scale (
L_{X}
) and establish its relationship with three well-known length scales: the Hunt length scale (
L_{H}
,
Hunt et al. 1989
;
Hunt et al. 1988
), the buoyancy length scale (
L_{b}
,
Brost and Wyngaard 1978
;
Wyngaard 2010
), and the Ellison length scale (
L_{E}
,
Ellison 1957
). Next, the ratios of various length scales (e.g.,
L_{b}/L_{E}
) are shown to be explicit functions of
Ri_{g}
and
Pr_{t}
. Equating these functions with one another results in a quadratic equation for
Pr_{t}
. One of the roots of this quadratic equation provides an explicit
Pr_{t}
—
Ri_{g}
relationship.

II.1
Budget Equations

The simplified budget equations for turbulent kinetic energy (TKE), variance of temperature (
\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
), and sensible heat flux (
\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}
) can be written as
Fitzjarrald 1979
;
Nieuwstadt 1984
;
Wyngaard 1975
:

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}\right)S+\beta\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}},

(9a)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-2\left(\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}\right)\Gamma,

(9b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{w}^{2}\Gamma+\left(1-a_{p}\right)\beta\sigma_{\theta}^{2}-\frac{\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}}{\tau_{R}}.

(9c)

where
\overline{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{\chi}_{\theta}
denote the dissipation rates of TKE and
\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
, respectively. The variance of vertical velocity is
\sigma_{w}^{2}
. In Eq. (
9c
), the parameter
a_{p}
influences the buoyant contribution to the pressure-temperature interaction term; whereas, the last term of this equation is a parameterization of the turbulent-turbulent component of the pressure-temperature interaction. The return-to-isotropy time scale is denoted by
\tau_{R}
. Please refer to Appendix 1 for further technical details on the parameterization of pressure-temperature interaction.

The Eqs. (
9a
), (
9b
), and (
9c
) assume steady-state and horizontal homogeneity. Furthermore, the terms with secondary importance (e.g., turbulent transport) are neglected. Eqs. (
9a
) and (
9b
) assume that production is locally balanced by dissipation. Please refer to Wyngaard
Wyngaard 1975
and Fitzjarrald
Fitzjarrald 1979
for further details. The celebrated ‘local scaling’ hypothesis by Nieuwstadt
Nieuwstadt 1984
also utilizes these equations.

II.2
A Hybrid Length Scale

In analogy to Prandtl’s mixing length hypothesis (see
Arya 2001
;
Monin and Yaglom 1971
;
Weinstock 1981
), let us assume that
\sigma_{w}
is a characteristic velocity scale for stably stratified flows. Further assume that
L_{X}
and
L_{X}/\sigma_{w}
are characteristic length and time scales, respectively. Then, the eddy diffusivity, the dissipation rates, and turbulent-turbulent component of the pressure-temperature interaction can be re-written as follows:

\displaystyle K_{M}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sigma_{w}L_{X},

(10a)

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\left(\frac{L_{X}}{\sigma_{w}}\right)}=c_{2}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{3}}{L_{X}},

(10b)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=c_{3}\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{\left(\frac{L_{X}}{\sigma_{w}}\right)}=c_{3}\frac{\sigma_{w}}{L_{X}}\sigma_{\theta}^{2},

(10c)

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}}{\tau_{R}}

\displaystyle=c_{4}\frac{\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}}{\left(\frac{L_{X}}{\sigma_{w}}\right)}=-c_{1}c_{4}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{Pr_{t}}\Gamma.

(10d)

Here the unknown (non-dimensional) coefficients are denoted as
c_{i}
, where
i
is an integer. The parameterizations for the dissipation rates (i.e.,
\overline{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{\chi}_{\theta}
) are further discussed in Section
IV
.

If we now make use of Eqs. (
1a
), (
1b
), (
2
), (
10a
), (
10b
) and substitute all the terms of Eq. (
9a
), we arrive at:

\displaystyle c_{2}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{3}}{L_{X}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sigma_{w}L_{X}S^{2}-c_{1}\sigma_{w}L_{X}\left(\frac{\beta}{Pr_{t}}\right)\Gamma,

(11a)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }c_{2}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{3}}{L_{X}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sigma_{w}L_{X}S^{2}\left(1-\frac{Ri_{g}}{Pr_{t}}\right).

(11b)

By simplifying Eq. (
11b
), we get:

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right),

(12a)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }L_{X}

\displaystyle=c_{H}L_{H}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right)=\frac{c_{H}L_{H}}{\sqrt{1-R_{f}}},

(12b)

where
L_{H}(=\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S})
is the Hunt length scale and
c_{H}
is an unknown proportionality constant. The length scale equation, Eq. (
12a
), was originally derived by Holtslag
Holtslag 1998
.

The Hunt length scale is related to the so-called buoyancy length scale (
L_{b}
) as follows:

L_{H}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S}\right)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{N}\right)\frac{N}{S}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{N}\right)\sqrt{Ri_{g}}=L_{b}\sqrt{Ri_{g}}.

(13)

Thus, Eq. (
12b
) can be re-written as:

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle=c_{H}L_{b}\left(\frac{\sqrt{Ri_{g}}}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right).

(14)

If we substitute the individual terms of Eq. (
9b
) by utilizing Eqs. (
1b
), (
2
), (
10a
), and (
10c
), we get:

\displaystyle c_{3}\frac{\sigma_{w}}{L_{X}}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}=2c_{1}\frac{\sigma_{w}L_{X}}{Pr_{t}}\Gamma^{2}.

(15)

Simplification of this equation leads to:

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{c_{3}}{2c_{1}}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\theta}}{\Gamma}\right)\sqrt{Pr_{t}},

(16a)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }L_{X}

\displaystyle=c_{E}L_{E}\sqrt{Pr_{t}}.

(16b)

where
L_{E}\left(=\sigma_{\theta}/\Gamma\right)
is the Ellison length scale and
c_{E}
is an unknown (nondimensional) coefficient.

We would like to point out that in the appendices of Basu et al.
Basu et al. 2021a
;
Basu et al. 2021b
we have summarized the characteristics of Hunt, buoyancy, Ellison, Bolgiano, Ozmidov, and several other length scales. For brevity, we do not repeat them here.

II.3
Ratios of Length Scales

By comparing Eq. (
12b
) with Eq. (
16b
), it is rather straightforward to derive:

\displaystyle Pr_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{c_{H}L_{H}}{c_{E}L_{E}}\right)^{2}+Ri_{g},

(17a)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }\frac{L_{H}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(Pr_{t}-Ri_{g}\right)}{c_{P}},

(17b)

where
c_{P}=\frac{c_{H}^{2}}{c_{E}^{2}}
. Using Eq. (
13
), this equation can be re-written as follows:

\frac{L_{b}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}=\frac{\left(Pr_{t}-Ri_{g}\right)}{c_{P}Ri_{g}}=\frac{\left(1-R_{f}\right)}{c_{P}R_{f}}.

(18)

An alternative expression for
\left(\frac{L_{b}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}\right)
can be found if we use Eqs. (
1b
), (
2
), (
10a
), and (
10d
) to substitute the individual terms of Eq. (
9c
) as follows:

\displaystyle-c_{1}c_{4}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{Pr_{t}}\Gamma

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{w}^{2}\Gamma+\left(1-a_{p}\right)\beta\sigma_{\theta}^{2},

(19a)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }\left(1-\frac{c_{5}}{Pr_{t}}\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}\Gamma

\displaystyle=\left(1-a_{p}\right)\beta\sigma_{\theta}^{2},

(19b)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }\left(1-\frac{c_{5}}{Pr_{t}}\right)L_{b}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-a_{p}\right)L_{E}^{2},

(19c)

\displaystyle\mbox{or, }\frac{L_{b}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-a_{p}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{c_{5}}{Pr_{t}}\right)},

(19d)

where
c_{5}(=c_{1}c_{4})
is an unknown proportionality constant.

II.4
Derivation of Prandtl Number

By equating Eq. (
18
) and Eq. (
19d
), we immediately get the following quadratic equation:

Pr_{t}^{2}-\left[c_{5}+Ri_{g}+\left(1-a_{p}\right)c_{P}Ri_{g}\right]Pr_{t}+c_{5}Ri_{g}=0.

(20)

Since
Pr_{t}=Pr_{t0}
for neutral conditions (
Ri_{g}=0
), via Eq. (
20
), we find:

c_{5}=Pr_{t0}.

(21)

The roots of Eq. (
20
) are:

Pr_{t}=\frac{X\pm\sqrt{X^{2}-4Pr_{t0}Ri_{g}}}{2},

(22)

where,
X=\left[Pr_{t0}+Ri_{g}+\left(1-a_{p}\right)c_{P}Ri_{g}\right]
. Only the larger root is physically meaningful. Eq. (
22
) includes three unknown parameters (i.e.,
Pr_{t0}
,
a_{p}
, and
c_{P}
). Similarity theory can be used to estimate
c_{P}
(discussed in the following section). However,
Pr_{t0}
and
a_{p}
must be prescribed.

We would like to emphasize that Eq. (
22
) is a closed form analytical solution for the stability-dependence of
Pr_{t}
. It is derived directly from the budget equations without any additional simplification. Since our derivation makes use of certain length scale ratios (LSRs), we refer to our proposed approach as the LSR formulation.

III
Estimation of Unknown Coefficients

For near-neutral conditions, Eqs. (
12b
) and (
16b
) simplify to the following expressions, respectively:

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle\approx c_{H}\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S},

(23a)

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle\approx c_{E}\frac{\sigma_{\theta}}{\Gamma}\sqrt{Pr_{t0}}.

(23b)

In order to be consistent with the logarithmic velocity profile in the surface layer,
L_{X}
should be equal to
\kappa z
in the surface layer, where
\kappa
is the von Kármán constant. Therefore,

\displaystyle c_{H}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa zS}{\sigma_{w}},

(23c)

\displaystyle c_{E}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa z\Gamma}{\sqrt{Pr_{t0}}\sigma_{\theta}}.

(23d)

Numerous studies reported that
\sigma_{w}=c_{w}u_{*}
and
\sigma_{\theta}=c_{\theta}\theta_{*}
in near-neutral stratified surface layer. The surface friction velocity and temperature scale are denoted by
u_{*}
and
\theta_{*}
, respectively. Thus, we get:

\displaystyle c_{H}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa zS}{c_{w}u_{*}}=\frac{1}{c_{w}},

(23e)

\displaystyle c_{E}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa z\Gamma}{\sqrt{Pr_{t0}}c_{\theta}\theta_{*}}=\frac{\sqrt{Pr_{t0}}}{c_{\theta}}.

(23f)

Please note that the non-dimensional velocity gradient,
\left(\kappa zS/u_{*}\right)
, equals to unity according to the logarithmic law of the wall. Whereas, the non-dimensional temperature gradient,
\left(\kappa z\Gamma/\theta_{*}\right)
, equals to
Pr_{t0}
.

By using Eqs. (
1a
), (
10a
), and (
12b
), we can expand the along-wind momentum flux as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-c_{1}c_{H}\sigma_{w}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}},

(24a)

Thus, the normalized momentum flux can be written as:

\displaystyle R_{uw}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)=-\frac{c_{1}c_{H}}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}.

(24b)

For neutral condition,
R_{uw}
simplifies to:
R_{uw0}=-c_{1}c_{H}
. Since,
\sigma_{w}=c_{w}u_{*}
, we get:

\displaystyle R_{uw0}=-\frac{1}{c_{w}^{2}}=-c_{1}c_{H}.

(24c)

Since,
c_{H}\approx\frac{1}{c_{w}}
, the unknown coefficient
c_{1}
is also approximately equal to
\frac{1}{c_{w}}
.

R_{uw0}

IV
Parameterizations of Dissipation Rates

IV.1
Energy Dissipation Rate

The energy dissipation rate is commonly parameterized as follows
Mellor and Yamada 1982
:

\overline{\varepsilon}=\frac{q^{3}}{B_{1}L_{M}},

(26)

where
q^{2}
is twice TKE.
L_{M}
is known as the master length scale and
B_{1}
is a constant coefficient. In this study, following Townsend
Townsend 1958
, we use Eq. (
10b
) as an alternative parameterization for
\overline{\varepsilon}
which makes use of
\sigma_{w}^{3}
instead of
q^{3}
. Using Eqs. (
12b
), and (
25e
), we can re-write this parameterization as follows:

\overline{\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{c_{2}}{c_{H}}\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}S\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}=\left(\frac{1}{c_{w}^{2}}\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}S\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}.

(27a)

If the value of
c_{w}
is approximately in the range of 1.25–1.30 (refer to Table T1), for small values of
Ri_{g}
(i.e., weakly stable conditions), we get:

\overline{\varepsilon}=0.60\sigma_{w}^{2}S.

(27b)

It is important to note that Eq. (
27b
) (with an unknown proportionality constant) was originally proposed by Hunt
Hunt et al. 1988
using heuristic arguments. He hypothesized that the energy dissipation in weakly/moderately stably stratified flows is dictated by mean shear (
S
) and root-mean-square value of vertical velocity fluctuations (i.e.,
\sigma_{w}
) which is the characteristic velocity scale in the direction of
S
. Later on Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
analyzed various observational and simulation datasets and validated Hunt’s parameterization (see their Figure 1). More recently, Basu et al.
Basu et al. 2021b
utilized a database of direct numerical simulations and found:

\overline{\varepsilon}=0.23\overline{e}S=0.63\sigma_{w}^{2}S,

(27c)

for
0<Ri_{g}<0.2
. TKE is denoted by
\overline{e}
.

It is remarkable that the DNS-based empirical formulation of
Basu et al. 2021b
is virtually identical to our analytical prediction, i.e., Eq. (
27b
). However, we are unable to ascertain the validity of either Eq. (
27b
) or Eq. (
27c
) for
Ri_{g}>0.2
. We will discuss more on this issue in Section
VI
.

The exact value of
B_{1}
in Eq. (
26
) is not settled in the literature. Over the years, a number of researchers estimated its value from diverse observational and simulated datasets; see a brief summary in Table
2
. By combining the analytical results from the present study with the DNS results from Basu et al.
Basu et al. 2021b
, we can also estimate
B_{1}
as follows.
From Eq. (
27c
), for
0<Ri_{g}<0.2
, we can write:

\overline{e}=\frac{q^{2}}{2}=\left(\frac{0.63}{0.23}\right)\sigma_{w}^{2}=2.74\sigma_{w}^{2}.

(28)

Next, if we assume our proposed length scale (
L_{X}
) is equal to the master length scale (
L_{M}

B_{1}=\frac{q^{3}}{\overline{\varepsilon}L_{X}}=\frac{(2\times 2.74)^{3/2}\sigma_{w}^{3}}{\left(0.63\sigma_{w}^{2}S\right)\left(c_{H}\sigma_{w}/S\right)}=25.5.

(29)

Here we have assumed
c_{H}=0.8
and
\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}\approx 1
for small values of
Ri_{g}
. Clearly, our estimated value of

Basu et al. 2021b
incorrectly reported
B_{1}
= 12.3 instead of 25.8.

Once again, following Townsend

Our proposed formulation for the turbulent Prandtl number, Eq. (

is selected from Table

In Fig.

We would like to emphasize out that Eq. (

) in Appendix 2 have nearly identical mathematical form despite the fundamental differences in the LSR and CSB approaches. The CSB approach includes prescribed coefficients from Kolmogorov-Obukhov-Corrsin hypotheses and from a parameterization of the pressure-temperature decorrelation (refer to Appendix 2); they are all lumped into a variable called
\omega^{CSB}
in Eq. (
72b

In contrast, Eq. (

) from the CSB approach leads to:

Pr_{t}\approx\omega^{CSB}Ri_{g}\approx 4Ri_{g}.

(32)

Thus, the CSB approach predicts

. On the other hand, for

= 0 and

= 4.27,

equals to 0.19 for the LSR approach. However, for

= 0.5 and

= 2.4,

increases to 0.46. In the literature (see

,

has been reported to be within the limits of 0.15 and 0.5; both the LSR-based and CSB-based predictions are in this range.

We first consider the ratio of the turbulent potential energy (TPE; denoted as
\overline{e}_{p}

\displaystyle\overline{e}_{p}

\displaystyle\overline{e}_{w}

\overline{e}_{p}=\frac{1}{2}N^{2}L_{E}^{2}.

(35a)

as follows:

R_{pw}=\frac{c_{P}Ri_{g}}{\left(Pr_{t}-Ri_{g}\right)}=\frac{c_{P}R_{f}}{\left(1-R_{f}\right)}.

(35b)

In the top panel of Fig.

. In the denominator of

in the numerator of

VI
Discussions

In this section, we elaborate on a few limitations of the proposed LSR approach and how to overcome them in a practical manner.

VI.1
Vertical Anisotropy of Turbulence

In this study, we have used Eq. (
10b
) to parameterize energy dissipation rate (
\overline{\varepsilon}
). A more common practice would be to use Eq. (
26
) or its following variant:

\overline{\varepsilon}=c_{2}^{*}\frac{q^{2}}{\left(\frac{L_{X}}{\sigma_{w}}\right)}=c_{2}^{*}\frac{\sigma_{w}^{3}}{A_{z}L_{X}},

(40a)

where,

A_{z}=\frac{\overline{e}_{w}}{\overline{e}}=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{q^{2}},

(40b)

and
c_{2}^{*}
is an unknown coefficient. In Section
II
, we have implicitly assumed
c_{2}^{*}A_{z}
to be a constant (
c_{2}
). In the literature, there is some evidence that the anisotropy parameter,
A_{z}
, may be dependent on
Ri_{g}
.

Based on observational and simulation data of turbulent air flows, Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
proposed the following empirical equation for
0<Ri_{g}<1
:

A_{z}=0.15+0.02Ri_{g}+0.07\exp{\left(-\frac{Ri_{g}}{0.25}\right)}.

(41)

According to this equation
A_{z}
is weakly dependent on
Ri_{g}
; as a matter of fact, Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
stated “the conclusions do not change much” if
A_{z}=0.22
is used. Based on a DNS database, Basu et al.
Basu et al. 2021b
reported
A_{z}
to be approximately equal to 0.18 for
0<Ri_{g}<0.2
. The parameterizations of Canuto et al.
Canuto et al. 2008
, Kantha and Clayson
Kantha and Carniel 2009
, and Cheng et al.
Cheng et al. 2020
predict gradual decrease of
A_{z}
from near-neutral to strongly stratified conditions. Their predicted
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}
range from 0.22 to 0.26; whereas,
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}>1)}
vary from about 0.15 to 0.20. In contrast, Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
used an empirical formulation which assumes
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}
= 0.20 and
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}>1)}\approx
0.03. The published datasets documented by Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
(see their Figure 6) and Cheng et al.
Cheng et al. 2020
(see their Figure 3c), in order to corroborate their respective formulations, do not portray any clear trends. A case in point are the wind tunnel measurements by Ohya
Ohya 2001
which exhibit random fluctuating behavior. Surprisingly, a strongly increasing trend of
A_{z}
with respect to
Ri_{g}
was predicted by large-eddy simulation data of
Zilitinkevich et al. 2007
(see their Figure 4); this was in direct contradiction to their analytical prediction. Given this diversity in the
A_{z}
-vs-
Ri_{g}
relationship, we strongly recommend more research in this arena.

If we utilize Eq. (
40a
) instead of Eq. (
10b
), it is straightforward to re-derive all the equations reported in earlier sections. Some of the key equations are given here:

L_{X}=\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}^{*}}{c_{1}A_{z}}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right),

(42a)

\frac{L_{H}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}=\frac{\left(Pr_{t}-Ri_{g}\right)A_{z}}{c_{P}^{*}},

(42b)

R_{pw}=\frac{c_{P}^{*}Ri_{g}}{\left(Pr_{t}-Ri_{g}\right)A_{z}}=\frac{c_{P}^{*}R_{f}}{\left(1-R_{f}\right)A_{z}},

(42c)

\frac{R_{uw}}{R_{uw0}}=\sqrt{\frac{A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}}{A_{z}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}}{A_{z}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-R_{f}}}\right).

(42d)

Here
c_{P}^{*}
is an unknown coefficient and can be estimated following the procedure for
c_{P}
. Furthermore, the quadratic equation for the turbulent Prandtl number becomes:

Pr_{t}^{2}-\left[c_{5}+Ri_{g}+\frac{\left(1-a_{p}\right)c_{P}^{*}}{A_{z}}Ri_{g}\right]Pr_{t}+c_{5}Ri_{g}=0.

(43)

VI.2
Imbalance of Production and Dissipation of TKE

In Eq. (
9a
), we have assumed that the production and dissipation of TKE balances exactly. Following Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
, we can define their ratio, termed a ‘growth factor’, as follows:

G=\frac{-\left(\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}\right)S}{-\beta\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}+\overline{\varepsilon}}.

(44)

It is likely that under strongly stratified condition, dissipation exceeds production. Thus,
G
can become less than unity for high values of
Ri_{g}
. We can re-write Eq. (
44
) as follows:

\overline{\varepsilon}=-\left(\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}\right)\frac{S}{G}+\beta\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}=-\left(\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}\right)S^{*}+\beta\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}.

(45)

The key equations will then become:

\displaystyle L_{X}

\displaystyle=c_{H}L_{H}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/G-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right)

(46a)

\displaystyle=c_{H}L_{b}\left(\frac{\sqrt{Ri_{g}}}{\sqrt{1/G-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right),

(46b)

\frac{L_{H}^{2}}{L_{E}^{2}}=\frac{\left(Pr_{t}/G-Ri_{g}\right)}{c_{P}},

(46c)

R_{pw}=\frac{c_{P}Ri_{g}}{\left(Pr_{t}/G-Ri_{g}\right)}=\frac{c_{P}R_{f}}{\left(1/G-R_{f}\right)},

(46d)

\frac{R_{uw}}{R_{uw0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/G-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/G-R_{f}}}.

(46e)

In this case, the quadratic equation for the turbulent Prandtl number becomes:

Pr_{t}^{2}-\left[c_{5}+Ri_{g}G+\left(1-a_{p}\right)c_{P}GRi_{g}\right]Pr_{t}+c_{5}GRi_{g}=0.

(47)

We would like to emphasize that the exact dependence of
G
on stability is not well studied in the literature. Schumann and Gerz
Schumann and Gerz 1995
proposed an empirical (exponential decay) equation for
G
-vs-
Ri_{g}
based on limited data. We hypothesize that for very stable conditions (
Ri_{g}>1
),
G
should be proportional to
Ri_{g}^{-1}
. For practical applications, we propose the following heuristic parameterization for
G
:

G=\min\left(1,Ri_{g}^{-1}\right).

(48)

Thus, for
Ri_{g}<1
,
G
equals to 1. In other words, production and dissipation of TKE balance each other for weakly and moderately stable condition. However, the balance is lost (i.e.,
G<1
) for very stable conditions.

If Eq. (
48
) is valid, then according to Eq. (
46b
),
L_{X}
will be approximately equal to the buoyancy length scale (
L_{b}
) for very stable conditions. Perhaps more interestingly, if Eq. (
48
) indeed holds, Eq. (
47
) predicts that
Pr_{t}
should saturate to a constant value for
Ri_{g}>1
. Such a prediction is not in agreement with some of the datasets reported in Fig. (
1
). However, it is consistent with the findings reported by
Kitamura et al. 2013
based on wind tunnel experiments and large-eddy simulations; refer to their Fig. 3.

We would like to emphasize that Eq. (
48
) is based on a heuristic argument and has not been verified yet. In our future work, we will leverage on Eq. (
46e
) to extract a reliable formulation for
G
.

VI.3
Combined Scenario

For the most general case, one should account for the effects of both anisotropy and decay of TKE. In such a combined scenario, both
A_{z}
and
G
terms will appear in the aforementioned equations. For example, the length scale equation will read:

L_{X}=\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}^{*}}{c_{1}A_{z}}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{S}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/G-Ri_{g}/Pr_{t}}}\right).

(49)

Similar to Eq. (
31
), for very stable condition (i.e.,
Ri_{g}\gg 1
), the Prandtl number equation will be simplified to:

Pr_{t}\approx\left(G+\frac{\left(1-a_{p}\right)c_{P}^{*}G}{A_{z}}\right)Ri_{g}=\frac{Ri_{g}}{R_{f\infty}},

(50)

Thus, the exact value of
R_{f\infty}
depends on
a_{p}
,
A_{z}
,
G
and
c_{P}^{*}
. Since stability dependencies of
a_{p}
,
A_{z}
and
G
are rather uncertain, empirical parameterizations for the combined terms (e.g.,
G/A_{z}
) might be more practical for certain applications. High quality data from laboratory experiment (e.g., wind tunnel) and/or direct numerical simulation will be needed to derive such parameterizations.

VII
Conclusions

In this study, we have analytically derived an explicit relationship between the Prandtl number and the gradient Richardson number. Our derivation is rather simple from a mathematical standpoint and does not make elaborate assumptions beyond variance and sensible heat flux budget equations. Most of the unknown coefficients of the proposed relationship are easily estimated from well-known surface layer similarity relationships. Our proposed Prandtl number formulation agrees very well with other competing approaches of quite different theoretical foundations and assumptions.

Our original analysis can be easily extended to include the effects of vertical anisotropy. It can also account for an imbalance of production and dissipation of TKE under very stable conditions. We have provided generalized formulations to account for these effects. However, these generalized formulations require stability-dependent formulations for a few parameters (e.g.,
A_{z}
,
G
) which are not well established in the literature. Currently, we are analyzing wind tunnel measurements and DNS-generated datasets to derive these formulations in a robust manner.

One of the limitations of the present study is that, for simplicity, it omits any discussion of internal gravity waves
Staquet and Sommeria 2002
;
Sun et al. 2015
. However, in stable boundary layers, specially under strong stratification, wave-turbulence interactions are extremely important. Thus far, only a handful of analytical studies have looked into such interactions
Kleeorin et al. 2019
;
Kurbatskii and Kurbatskaya 2019
;
Sukoriansky and Galperin 2008
;
Zilitinkevich et al. 2009
. We hope to further advance our proposed LSR approach along this direction in the future.

Appendix 1: Parameterization of the Pressure-Temperature Interaction Term

In the prognostic equation of sensible heat flux (
\overline{u^{\prime}_{i}\theta^{\prime}}
), a pressure-temperature interaction term
\Pi_{i}=\left(-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\overline{\theta^{\prime}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial x_{i}}}\right)
appears
Arya 1975
;
Garratt 1992
. This loss term is significant for atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) flows and requires a reliable parameterization. Using the product rule of calculus,
\Pi_{i}
can be decomposed as follows
Hanjalić and Launder 2011
;
Kantha and Clayson 1994
;
Stull 1988
:

-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\overline{\theta^{\prime}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial x_{i}}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\overline{p^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}\delta_{ik}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\overline{p^{\prime}\frac{\partial\theta^{\prime}}{\partial x_{i}}}.

(51)

Here
p
and
\rho_{0}
denote pressure and a reference density, respectively. The symbol
\delta_{ik}
represents Kronecker delta.

The first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
51
) represents turbulent diffusion of temperature field by pressure fluctuations and is sometimes neglected under the assumption of isotropy or using scaling argument
Stull 1988
. As an alternative, in a number of modeling studies, it has been combined with the turbulent transport term, and in turn, the total term is parameterized via K-theory
Moeng and Wyngaard 1986
.

The second term
\left(\Phi_{i}=\frac{1}{\rho}\overline{p^{\prime}\frac{\partial\theta^{\prime}}{\partial x_{i}}}\right)
is known as the pressure scrambling of the fluctuating temperature field. This term is split into three separate components representing different interactions
Cheng et al. 2002
;
Hanjalić and Launder 2011
:

\Phi_{i}=\Phi_{i}^{TT}+\Phi_{i}^{S}+\Phi_{i}^{B}.

(52)

The term
\Phi_{i}^{TT}
captures turbulence-turbulence interactions. Following Rotta’s celebrated return-to-isotropy hypothesis
Rotta 1951
, Monin
Monin 1965b
parameterized this term as follows:

\Phi_{i}^{TT}=-\frac{\overline{u^{\prime}_{i}\theta^{\prime}}}{\tau_{R}},

(53)

where
\tau_{R}
is the return-to-isotropy time scale. In the absence of external forces, this term relaxes turbulence to an isotropic state with zero overall heat flux
Umlauf and Burchard 2005
. Even though Eq. (
53
) is the most popular in the literature, alternative parameterizations for
\Phi_{i}^{TT}
have been proposed in the past (please refer to
Hanjalić and Launder 2011
).

The mean shear-turbulence interaction is denoted by
\Phi_{i}^{S}
and is parameterized as follows
Andrén and Moeng 1993
;
Chen and Jaw 1998
;
Hanjalić and Launder 2011
:

\Phi_{i}^{S}=a_{s}\overline{u^{\prime}_{k}\theta^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\overline{u}_{i}}{\partial x_{k}}.

(54)

In the absence of significant subsidence or under quiescent synoptic condition, the vertical component (i.e.,
\Phi_{3}^{S}
) is negligible in the ABL flows since
\overline{u}_{3}=\overline{w}\approx 0
; a comprehensive modeling study by
Andrén and Moeng 1993
provides supporting results.

The following equation is often used for representing the buoyancy-turbulence interaction
Hanjalić and Launder 2011
;
Launder 1975
:

\Phi_{i}^{B}=-a_{p}\beta\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\delta_{i3}.

(55)

Even though this term is known to be important for non-neutral flows, quite interestingly, the well-known parameterizations of Mellor and Yamada
Mellor and Yamada 1982
disregarded it.

Appendix 2: Co-spectral Budget (CSB) Approach

In this section, we re-derive the relevant equations of the co-spectral budget (CSB) approach following the footsteps of Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
. Along the way, we point out some of their (implicit) assumptions and differences to our newly proposed LSR approach. During this exercise, we noted certain sign errors in the original derivations of
Katul et al. 2014
. D. Li
Li 2021
confirmed our findings and pointed out additional sign errors in
Katul et al. 2014
. Fortunately, all these errors cancel out and do not have any effect on the key results. We have communicated our findings to G. G. Katul
Katul 2021
and he has kindly verified them.

The starting point of the CSB approach is vertical sensible heat and momentum flux budget equations in wavenumber space:

\displaystyle\underbrace{P_{w\theta}(k_{x})}_{\text{production}}+\underbrace{\beta F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})}_{\text{buoyancy}}+\underbrace{\Pi_{\theta}(k_{x})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{pressure-temperature}\\
\text{decorrelation}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=0,

(58a)

\displaystyle\underbrace{P_{uw}(k_{x})}_{\text{production}}+\underbrace{\Pi_{u}(k_{x})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{pressure-velocity}\\
\text{decorrelation}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=0.

(58b)

Here
F_{\theta\theta}
is the one dimensional temperature spectrum.
k_{x}
denotes wavenumber in the along-wind direction. Both these equations assume steady-state condition. They neglect turbulent transport and molecular diffusion terms. Interestingly, the momentum flux equation also neglects the buoyancy term.

The pressure-temperature and pressure-velocity interactions are parameterized as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\theta}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-A_{T}\frac{F_{w\theta}}{\tau(k_{x})}-c^{CSB}_{1T}P_{w\theta}(k_{x}),

(59a)

\displaystyle\Pi_{u}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-A_{u}\frac{F_{uw}}{\tau(k_{x})}-c^{CSB}_{1U}P_{uw}(k_{x}).

(59b)

Where
F_{w\theta}
and
F_{uw}
are the cospectra between
w
–
\theta
and
w
–
u
, respectively.
\tau(k_{x})
is a relaxation time-scale.
A_{T}
,
A_{U}
,
C^{CSB}_{1T}
, and
C^{CSB}_{1U}
are constants which should be prescribed. Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
assumed:
A_{T}=A_{U}=1.8
, and
c^{CSB}_{1T}=c^{CSB}_{1U}=3/5
.

Please note that Eq. (
59a
) does not include the commonly used buoyancy-turbulence interaction term [see Eq. (
55
) in Appendix 1]. Instead, it includes an unorthodox term which is proportional to the production term.

The production terms are expressed as follows:

\displaystyle P_{w\theta}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-\Gamma F_{ww}(k_{x}),

(60a)

\displaystyle P_{uw}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=-SF_{ww}(k_{x}).

(60b)

Here, the one dimensional vertical velocity spectrum is denoted by
F_{ww}
. Please note that both these equations in
Katul et al. 2014
contain sign errors as pointed out by
Li 2021
.

By combining Eqs. (
58a
), (
59a
), and (
60a
), we get:

F_{w\theta}=-\left(\frac{\tau(k_{x})}{A_{T}}\right)\left[\left(1-c^{CSB}_{1T}\right)\Gamma F_{ww}(k_{x})-\beta F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})\right].

(61a)

Similarly, by using Eqs. (
58b
), (
59b
), and (
60b
), we arrive at:

F_{uw}=-\left(\frac{\tau(k_{x})}{A_{U}}\right)\left[\left(1-c^{CSB}_{1U}\right)SF_{ww}(k_{x})\right].

(61b)

Next, Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
assumed that
F_{ww}(k_{x})
,
F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})
, and
\tau(k_{x})
follow the inertial-range scaling behavior within the range
k_{a}\leq k\leq\infty
as hypothesized by Kolmogorov-Obukhov-Corrsin:

\displaystyle F_{ww}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=c^{CSB}_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}^{2/3}k_{x}^{-5/3},

(62a)

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=c^{CSB}_{T}\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}\overline{N}_{\theta}k_{x}^{-5/3},

(62b)

\displaystyle\tau(k_{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}k_{x}^{-2/3}.

(62c)

Where
\overline{N}_{\theta}
is simply half of dissipation rate of temperature variance (
\overline{\chi}_{\theta}
).
c^{CSB}_{0}
and
c^{CSB}_{T}
are supposed to be universal constants. Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
assumed
c^{CSB}_{0}=0.65
and
c^{CSB}_{T}=0.80
. Please note that, for simplicity, they assumed that the inertial-range scaling also holds in the dissipation range.

For low wavenumbers (
0\leq k_{x}\leq k_{a}
), the CSB approach assumes flat (i.e., white noise) spectra:

\displaystyle F_{ww}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=c^{CSB}_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}^{2/3}k_{a}^{-5/3},

(63a)

\displaystyle F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})

\displaystyle=c^{CSB}_{T}\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}\overline{N}_{\theta}k_{a}^{-5/3},

(63b)

\displaystyle\tau(k_{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}k_{a}^{-2/3}.

(63c)

Over the decades, several competing hypotheses (e.g.,
Bolgiano Jr. 1959
;
Bolgiano Jr. 1962
;
Lumley 1964
;
Monin 1965a
;
Shur 1962
) have been put forward to characterize the low wavenumber (aka buoyancy-range) spectra. We would like to point out that none of these hypotheses are in line with the assumption of the CSB approach. Furthermore, in the surface layer, there are ample evidence in the literature (e.g.,
Kader and Yaglom 1991
;
Li et al. 2016a
) that temperature spectra follow
k_{x}^{-1}
scaling and not
k_{x}^{0}
scaling as assumed by the CSB approach.

By integrating and summing Eqs. (
62a
) and (
63a
) we get:

\begin{split}\sigma_{w}^{2}&=\int_{0}^{k_{a}}F_{ww}(k_{x})dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}F_{ww}(k_{x})dk_{x}\\
&=\int_{0}^{k_{a}}c^{CSB}_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}^{2/3}k_{a}^{-5/3}dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}c^{CSB}_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}^{2/3}k_{x}^{-5/3}dk_{x}\\
&=\frac{5}{2}c^{CSB}_{0}\overline{\varepsilon}^{2/3}k_{a}^{-2/3}.\end{split}

(64)

Similarly, from Eqs. (
62b
) and (

) we get:

\begin{split}\sigma_{\theta}^{2}&=\int_{0}^{k_{a}}F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}F_{\theta\theta}(k_{x})dk_{x}\\
&=\int_{0}^{k_{a}}c^{CSB}_{T}\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}\overline{N}_{\theta}k_{a}^{-5/3}dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}c^{CSB}_{T}\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}\overline{N}_{\theta}k_{x}^{-5/3}dk_{x}\\
&=\frac{5}{2}c^{CSB}_{T}\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/3}\overline{N}_{\theta}k_{a}^{-2/3}.\end{split}

(65)

By making use of Eq. (
61a
) in conjunction with Eqs. (

)–(
63

\begin{split}\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}&=\left[\int_{0}^{k_{a}}F_{w\theta}(k_{x})dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}F_{w\theta}(k_{x})dk_{x}\right],\\
&=-\left(\frac{7c^{CSB}_{0}\Gamma\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/3}Q}{10A_{T}}\right)k_{a}^{-4/3},\end{split}

(66a)

where

Q=\left[1-\frac{\beta c^{CSB}_{T}\overline{N}_{\theta}}{(1-c^{CSB}_{1T})c^{CSB}_{0}\Gamma\overline{\varepsilon}}\right].

(66b)

Both these equations in
Katul et al. 2014
contain sign errors.

In an analogous manner, we get from Eq. (
61b
) and Eqs. (

)–(
63

\begin{split}\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}&=\left[\int_{0}^{k_{a}}F_{uw}(k_{x})dk_{x}+\int_{k_{a}}^{\infty}F_{uw}(k_{x})dk_{x}\right],\\
&=-\left(\frac{7c^{CSB}_{0}S\left(\overline{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/3}}{10A_{U}}\right)k_{a}^{-4/3}.\end{split}

(67)

This equation in
Katul et al. 2014
contains a sign error.

From Eqs. (

), and (

Pr_{t}=\frac{-\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}/S}{-\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}/\Gamma}=\frac{A_{T}}{A_{U}Q}=\frac{1}{Q}.

(68)

Note that Katul et al.
Katul et al. 2014
assumed
A_{T}=A_{U}
.

The budget equations of TKE and
\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}\right)S+\beta\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}},

(69a)

\displaystyle\overline{N}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-\left(\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}\right)\Gamma,

(69b)

Unfortunately, a sign error appears in the equation for

Dividing Eq. (

\left(\frac{\beta\overline{N}_{\theta}}{\Gamma\overline{\varepsilon}}\right)=\frac{R_{f}}{1-R_{f}}.

(70)

Hence,

Q=1-\frac{c^{CSB}_{T}}{\left(1-c^{CSB}_{1T}\right)c^{CSB}_{0}}\left(\frac{R_{f}}{1-R_{f}}\right)=\left(\frac{1-\omega^{CSB}R_{f}}{1-R_{f}}\right),

(71a)

and

(71b)

Where,

and

Only the larger root is physically meaningful.

Appendix 3: Energy-and Flux-Budget (EFB) Approach

Over the past several years, Zilitinkevich and his co-workers have proposed the so-called energy-and flux-budget (EFB) approach and its several modifications. In this appendix, we briefly discuss some of the salient features of this approach. We follow one of the later versions of the EFB approach as documented by Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
.

The EFB approach makes use of the steady-state budget equations for both sensible heat and momentum fluxes. As a reminder to the readers, our proposed LSR approach does not utilize the momentum flux equation. In the case of the sensible heat flux equation,
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
parameterizes the pressure-temperature interaction term as follows:

\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\overline{\theta^{\prime}\frac{\partial p^{\prime}}{\partial z}}=\left(1-c^{EFB}_{\theta}\right)\beta\sigma_{\theta}^{2},

(78a)

where
c^{EFB}_{\theta}
is an unknown coefficient. In the LSR approach, we use the term
a_{p}
to denote
(1-c^{EFB}_{\theta})
. Interestingly, Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
neglects the commonly used turbulence-turbulence interactions [i.e., Eq. (
53
)] in the pressure-temperature interaction term. However, they use this exact term to parameterize the dissipation term (commonly neglected in the literature) of the sensible heat flux equation as follows:

\varepsilon_{z}^{(F)}=\frac{\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}}{c^{EFB}_{F}\tau_{\varepsilon}}.

(78b)

Where,
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is the dissipation time scale and
c^{EFB}_{F}
is an unknown coefficient, assumed to be equal to 0.25.

Effectively, both the EFB and the LSR approaches use the same form of parameterized sensible heat flux equation. From this equation, with minor algebraic manipulations,
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
derived:

\overline{w^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}}=-K_{H}\Gamma=-2c^{EFB}_{F}\tau_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{e}_{w}-c^{EFB}_{\theta}\overline{e}_{p}\right)\Gamma,

(79a)

or,

K_{H}=2c^{EFB}_{F}\tau_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{e}_{w}-c^{EFB}_{\theta}\overline{e}_{p}\right).

(79b)

Please refer to Eqs. (
33a
) and (
33b
) for the definitions of
\overline{e}_{p}
and
\overline{e}_{w}
, respectively.

In the case of the momentum flux equation, Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
makes several approximations. They neglect the dissipation term. In addition, they combine the buoyancy and pressure-velocity interaction terms and call it an ‘effective dissipation rate’. This combined term is parameterized like a return-to-isotropy term. The resultant momentum flux equation is written as follows:

\overline{u^{\prime}w^{\prime}}=-K_{M}S=-2c^{EFB}_{\tau}\tau_{\varepsilon}\overline{e}_{w}S,

(80a)

where,
c^{EFB}_{\tau}
is an unknown coefficient, assumed to be equal to 0.2. Thus, the eddy diffusivity can be represented as:

K_{M}=2c^{EFB}_{\tau}\tau_{\varepsilon}\overline{e}_{w}.

(80b)

Based on Eqs. (
79b
) and (
80b
), one can write:

Pr_{t}=\frac{K_{M}}{K_{H}}=\frac{\left(\frac{c^{EFB}_{\tau}}{c^{EFB}_{F}}\right)}{\left(1-c^{EFB}_{\theta}\frac{\overline{e}_{p}}{\overline{e}_{w}}\right)}.

(81)

Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
argued that if
Pr_{t}\to\infty
as
Ri_{g}\to\infty
, then in the limiting case:

c^{EFB}_{\theta}=\left(\frac{\overline{e}_{w}}{\overline{e}_{p}}\right)_{Ri_{g}\to\infty}.

(82)

Even though this equation is only valid for
Ri_{g}\to\infty
, the EFB approach uses
c^{EFB}_{\theta}
as a constant, being equal to 0.105, for all stability conditions. In our proposed LSR approach, the related coefficient is
(1-a_{p})
and we have also assumed it to be a constant in lieu of a reliable stability-dependent parameterization.

From the budget equations of TKE and variance of potential temperature, along with the definition of flux Richardson number (
R_{f}
),
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
derived the following ratios:

\frac{\overline{e}}{\overline{e}+\overline{e}_{p}}=\frac{1-R_{f}}{1-\left(1-c^{EFB}_{P}\right)R_{f}},

(83a)

and,

\frac{\overline{e}_{p}}{\overline{e}+\overline{e}_{p}}=\frac{c^{EFB}_{P}R_{f}}{1-\left(1-c^{EFB}_{P}\right)R_{f}}.

(83b)

Where
c^{EFB}_{P}
is an unknown coefficient. Based on available data,
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
assumed it to be equal to 0.86.

By plugging in Eq. (
83b
) in Eq. (
81
) and using the definition
A_{z}=\frac{\overline{e}_{w}}{\overline{e}}
, one gets the following equation after simplification:

Pr_{t}=\frac{\left(\frac{c^{EFB}_{\tau}}{c^{EFB}_{F}}\right)}{\left(1-c^{EFB}_{\theta}c^{EFB}_{P}\frac{R_{f}}{A_{z}(1-R_{f})}\right)}.

(84)

For neutral condition (i.e.,
R_{f}=0
), with the chosen values of
c^{EFB}_{\tau}
and
c^{EFB}_{F}
, the EFB approach predicts
Pr_{t0}=0.8
.

Please note that Eq. (
84
) requires a parameterization for
A_{z}
. Zilitinkevich et al.
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
proposed heuristic equations for the redistribution of TKE among various velocity components due to the effects of stratification. Those equations lead to a specific formulation for
A_{z}
; please refer to Eq. (50c) of
Zilitinkevich et al. 2013
. Using limited data, they further assumed
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}=0.2
and
A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}\to\infty)}=0.03
. In Section
VI.1
we have provided more information on
A_{z}
.

It is straightforward to derive the following normalized variances and fluxes from the EFB approach (see
Li et al. 2016b
):

R^{EFB}_{pw}=\frac{c^{EFB}_{P}R_{f}}{A_{z}(1-R_{f})},

(85a)

\left(\frac{R_{uw}}{R_{uw0}}\right)^{EFB}=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{A_{z}^{(Ri_{g}=0)}}{A_{z}}}}{\sqrt{1-R_{f}}},

(85b)

\left(\frac{R_{w\theta}}{R_{w\theta 0}}\right)^{EFB}=\sqrt{\frac{Pr_{t0}}{Pr_{t}}}.

(85c)

In Section
V.2.4
, we have compared these equations against the predictions from the LSR and the CSB approaches.
