Approximations of solution concepts of cooperative games
Martin Černý

Abstract
The computation of a solution concept of a cooperative game usually depends on values of all coalitions. However, in some applications, values of some of the coalitions might be unknown due to various reasons. We introduce a method to approximate standard solution concepts based only on partial information given by a so called incomplete game. We demonstrate the ideas on the class of minimal incomplete games. Approximations are derived for different solution concepts including the Shapley value, the nucleolus, or the core. We show explicit formulas for approximations of some of the solution concepts and show how the approximability differs based on additional information about the game.



keywords
cooperative games, incomplete games, solution concepts, approximation

1
Introduction

The model of cooperative games is long studied since its original formulation by von Neumann and Morgenstern in 1944
Neumann1944
. Since then, one of its main disadvantages, which makes it often infeasible in applications prevails. If we want to model a real world problem using a cooperative game of
n
players, we need to collect
2^{n}
real values representing the worth of cooperation between different subsets of players. In the best case scenario, this process is both resource and time demanding but in many scenarios, such information is too complex and unfeasible to collect. Researchers try to deal with these problems in many ways; using stochastic approach
Granot1977
;
Borm2002
, approach employing (fuzzy) intervals
Gok2009
;
Bok2015
;
Branzei2010
;
Deng2016
;
Mares2013
, among other approaches are e.g. ellipsoidal games
Gok2014
, multi-choice games
Branzei2008
, or restricted games
Grabisch2016
. When computing the solution concepts, in many of these models the unknown values are either ignored, their values are substituted with
0
or the solution concept is computed with respect to a special cooperative game. This is hardly satisfactory, because the unknown values are either not taken into account or there is no guarantee on their relation with the substituted values.

In this text, we derive approximations of standard solution concepts, given only partial information about an underlying cooperative game. Section
2
contains preliminaries to cooperative games and section
3
is dedicated to the model of incomplete cooperative games that we use as the tool to capture partial information about an underlying complete game. Section
4
is dedicated to approximations of standard solution concepts and in Section
5
, we conclude this paper with open problems and future research.

Definition 2

Let

1.

We denote the sets of superadditive and convex

Unanimity games

(N,u_{T})

0&\text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

are important when we view the set of all
n

as a vector space. Shapley

Definition 3
.

A cooperative game
(N,v)
is

We denote the set of all positive cooperative
n
-person games by

.

It is straightforward that convex games form a subset of superadditive games. Further, positive games form a subset of convex games and unanimity games are themselves positive games. Positive games are well-studied in the theory of capacities, where they are also called

(see

).

The
upper vector

defined as

captures each player’s marginal contribution to the grand coalition. The

is defined as
a^{v}_{i}=\max_{S:i\in S}v(S)-b^{v}(S)
. Finally, the

g^{v}\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

. The following lemma shows that the upper vector and the gap function are linear with respect to cooperative games.

Lemma 1
.

Driessen1985-thesis

For a linear combination
v=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}v_{i}
of games

, it holds

1.

b^{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}b^{v_{i}},

2.

b^{v}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}b^{v_{i}}(N),

3.

g^{v}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}g^{v_{i}}(N).

.

Let
(N,v)
be a cooperative game. The game is 1-convex if for all coalitions
S\subseteq N
,
S\neq\emptyset
the inequality

v(S)\leq v(N)-b^{v}(N\setminus S)

(1)

holds and also

(2)

The set of 1-convex
n
-person games is denoted by

2.1
Solution concepts and payoff vectors

One of the goals of cooperative game theory is to distribute the value of the grand coalition
v(N)
between all players. To be able to work with individual payoffs more easily,
payoff vectors
are introduced. Those are vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
x_{i}
represents the individual payoff of player
i
. The definition of the payoff vector is quite general. This is why, for a cooperative game
(N,v)
, one usually considers
preimputations

\mathcal{I}^{*}(v)
and
imputations

\mathcal{I}(v)
,

•

\mathcal{I}^{*}(v)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x(N)=v(N)\}
,

•

\mathcal{I}(v)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x(N)=v(N)\text{ and }x_{i}\geq v(i)\text{ for }i\in N\}
.

Preimputations are payoff vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are
efficient
, i.e.
x(N)=v(N)
. Imputations are also
individually rational
, meaning every player
i
receives at least his singleton value, i.e.
x_{i}\geq v(i)
. To work with imputations more easily, for
k\in N
, we denote by
I^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the imputation defined as

(I^{k})_{i}=\begin{cases}v(i)+\delta&\text{if }i=k,\\
v(i)&\text{if }i\neq k.\\
\end{cases}

and for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
such that
\alpha(N)=1
, we denote by
I^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the imputation defined as

I^{\alpha}_{i}=v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta.

Definition 5
.

Let
C\subseteq\Gamma^{n}
be a class of
n
-person cooperative games. Then a function
f\colon C\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is a
solution concept
(on class
C
).

Solution concepts might be equivalently defined as subsets of payoff vectors. Both approaches are useful and we often switch between them. If the image
f(v)
of every cooperative game
v\in C
is exactly one vector, we write
f\colon C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we say
f
is a
one-point
solution concept or a
value
. Otherwise, we say
f
is a
multi-point
solution concept. Each solution concept follows a different goal, e.g.  the
Shapley value
(Definition
6
) is a one-point solution concept, which strives to distribute the payoff as
fairly
as possible. Another example is the
core
(Definition
8
), a multi-point solution concept, focused on
stability
. In the rest of this section, we introduce solution concepts considered in our research.

2.1.1
The Shapley value

It is one of the most studied one-point solution concept originally defined by Shapley

.

The

\phi_{i}(v)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{S\subseteq N\setminus i}{n-1\choose s}^{-1}(v(S\cup i)-v(S)).

The Shapley value is defined for every cooperative game and has many nice properties, e.g. the

\phi(\alpha v+\beta w)=\alpha\phi(v)+\beta\phi(w).

(3)

In general, the Shapley value is not individually rational, i.e.

Lemma 2
.

The Shapley value of a unanimity game
(N,u_{T})
can be expressed as

\phi_{i}(u_{T})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lvert T\rvert}&\text{if }i\in T,\\
0&\text{if }i\notin T.\\
\end{cases}

2.1.2
The
\tau

The

.

The

.

\tau_{i}(v)=\begin{cases}b^{v}_{i}-\frac{g^{v}(N)}{\sum_{i\in N}g^{v}(i)}g^{v}(i)&\text{if }g^{v}(N)=0,\\
b^{v}_{i}&\text{if }g^{v}(N)>0.\\
\end{cases}

Theorem 4
.

Driessen1985-thesis

For a 1-convex
(N,v)
, the
\tau
-value can be expressed as

\tau_{i}(v)=b^{v}_{i}-\frac{g^{v}(N)}{n}.

The

2.1.3
The core and the Weber set

Definition 8
.

The
core

\mathcal{C}(v)
of a cooperative game
(N,v)
is a multi-point solution concept defined as

\mathcal{C}(v)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x(N)=v(N)\text{ and }x(S)\geq v(S)\text{ for }S\subseteq N\}.

Notice,

.

2.1.4
The (pre)kernel

The
excess
of
(N,v)
with respect to

.

The

\mathcal{K}^{*}(v)

(N,v)
is a multi-point solution concept defined as

\mathcal{K}^{*}(v)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(v)\mid s_{ij}(x,v)=s_{ji}(x,v)\text{ }\forall i\neq j\}.

The definition of the kernel is slightly more restrictive than that of the prekernel. The main difference is in individual rationality of the payoff vectors.

Definition 11
.

The

\mathcal{K}(v)
of a cooperative game
(N,v)
is a multi-point solution concept defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(v)=\Big\{x\in\mathcal{I}(v)\mid\forall i\neq j:\left(s_{ij}(x,v)-s_{ji}(x,v)\right)(x_{j}-v_{j})\leq 0\text{ \,}

\displaystyle\text{ or }\left(s_{ij}(x,v)-s_{ij}(x,v)\right)(x_{i}-v_{i})\leq 0\Big\}

.

Either
s_{ij}(x,v)=s_{ji}(x,v)
or, from individually rational,
s_{ij(x,v)}>s_{ji}(x,v)
implies
x_{j}=v_{j}
and similarly
s_{ji}(x,v)>s_{ij}(x,v)

. Although different in general, both solution concepts coincide for superadditive games.

.

(N,v)

The nucleolus

The essential component of nucleolus is

3
Incomplete cooperative games

Definition 13
.

(Incomplete game)

An incomplete game is a tuple
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
where
N=\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\mathcal{K}\subseteq 2^{N}
is the set of coalitions with known values and
v\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is the characteristic function of the incomplete game. Further,
\emptyset\in\mathcal{K}
and
v(\emptyset)=0
.

For
minimal incomplete games
, we have
\mathcal{K}=\{\emptyset,N\}\cup\{\{i\}\mid i\in N\}
. This class is considered minimal, because we want
N\in\mathcal{K}
to be able to distribute
v(N)
among the players. Combined with that, the knowledge of singleton values allows to define the imputation set
\mathcal{I}(v)
of
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
.

Definition
13
is actually identical to the definition of a
restricted game
(see
Grabisch2016
). The crucial difference between both models is in the interpretation of the set
\mathcal{K}
. For restricted games,
\mathcal{K}
represents all feasible coalitions and the cooperation of coalitions outside
\mathcal{K}
is impossible. Thus the values are considered non-existent and are not taken into account while computing solution concepts. For incomplete games, though,
\mathcal{K}
is the set of coalitions with known values. This means that we consider there is an underlying complete game
(N,v)
, however, all we know about this game is represented by
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
. It is further assumed we know the underlying game
(N,v)
is from a class
C\subseteq\Gamma^{n}
. If we want to approximate the solution concept of
(N,v)
, we can utilize this knowledge and consider all games from
C
, which are extending
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
.

Definition 14
.

(
C
-extension)

Let
C\subseteq\Gamma^{n}
be a class of
n
-person games. A cooperative game
(N,w)\in C
is a
C
-extension
of an incomplete game
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
, if
w(S)=v(S)
for every
S\in\mathcal{K}
.

The set of all
C
-extensions of an incomplete game
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is denoted by

. We write
C(v)
-extension
whenever we want to emphasize the game
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
. In some situations, we might not know the class of the underlying game. In this case, it even makes sense to ask if it has a
C
-extension. If it does, we say
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C
-extendable
. The set of all
C
-extendable incomplete games with fixed
\mathcal{K}
is denoted by

.

The sets of

-extensions,

-extensions,

-extensions and

-extensions form polyhedral sets, because they are defined by systems of linear inequalities. This allows to switch to the dual description of these sets, employing their extreme points and extreme rays. We refer to the extreme points as to

4
Approximations of solution concepts

Imagine that we want to determine a solution
\mathcal{S}(v)
of a cooperative game
(N,v)
. However, all we know about this game is represented by an incomplete game
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
and the knowledge that the complete game is from a class
C\subseteq\Gamma^{n}
. The set of
C
-extensions represents the set of possible candidates with
(N,v)
among them. This means, that by computing
\mathcal{S}(w)
for every
C
-extension
(N,w)
, we also compute
\mathcal{S}(v)
. The only problem is that we cannot distinguish which one of the
C
-extensions is
(N,v)
. However, by considering the union of
\mathcal{S}(w)
for all
C
-extensions
(N,w)
, we are sure that
\mathcal{S}(v)
is contained in this set. Similarly, if we consider the intersection of all solutions
\mathcal{S}(w)
, we have a set of payoff vectors which are guaranteed to be a subset of
\mathcal{S}(v)
. The idea is formally captured by the following definition.

Definition 15
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
C
-extendable incomplete game and
\mathcal{S}\colon C\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
a solution concept on
C\subseteq\Gamma^{n}
. Then by
weak solution

\cup\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})\colon C(\mathcal{K})\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, we mean

\cup\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})(v)\coloneqq\bigcup_{w\in C(v)}\mathcal{S}(w).

We write
\cup\mathcal{S}(C)
instead of
\cup\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})
whenever
\mathcal{K}
is apparent from the context. In a similar way, we can define the
strong solution

\cap\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})
where the union is replaced by the intersection. It is clear from the definition that

\cap\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})\subseteq\mathcal{S}(v)\subseteq\cup\mathcal{S}(C,\mathcal{K})

(14)

and the difference between the sets depend heavily on both
C
and
\mathcal{K}
. If for example
\mathcal{K}=2^{N}
, all three sets coincide. If on the contrary
\mathcal{K}=\{\emptyset\}
, for most of the standard solution concepts, the relations become
\emptyset\subseteq\mathcal{S}(v)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Also, the more restrictive
C
is, the less
C
-extensions are considered, thus the stronger the approximations get. The ultimate goal is to find a compromise between information provided by
(C,\mathcal{K})
and the strength of the approximations.

We initiate the research by studying the weak and the strong solutions of minimal incomplete games. An advantage of this class of incomplete games is that the imputation set
\mathcal{I}(v)
is determined as it depends only on values of singletons and the grand coalition. Moreover, it holds for every extension
(N,w)
that
\mathcal{I}(w)=\mathcal{I}(v)
. Since many solution concepts are subsets of the imputation set (all but one studied in this paper), we get an initial upper bound
\cup\mathcal{S}(C)(v)\subseteq\mathcal{I}(v)
for the weak solution. For the strong solution, an initial lower bound is given by the empty set,
\emptyset\subseteq\cap\mathcal{S}(v)
. The goal of this paper is to consider different sets of
C
-extensions and decide if the weak and the strong solutions lead to better approximations than the initial bounds. We consider standard classes of games such as the superadditive, convex, positive extensions but also 1-convex extensions as their analysis helps in understanding of the former ones.

4.1
The Shapley value

First a technical lemma, which expresses the Shapley value of every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension.

Lemma 16
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds for every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
that

\footnotesize{\phi_{i}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta+\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{T\in E:i\notin T}\beta_{T}{n-1\choose t}^{-1}-\sum_{T\in E:i\in T}\beta_{T}{n-1\choose t-1}^{-1}\right)}.

(15)

Proof.

From linearity of the Shapley value and the definition of
{w_{\alpha,\beta}}
(
12
), it holds
\phi({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\sum_{i\in N}\alpha_{i}\phi(v^{k})+\sum_{T\in E}\beta_{T}\phi(e_{T})
. We compute both
\phi(v^{k})
and
\phi(e_{T})
using the definition of the Shapley value. For
S\subseteq N\setminus i
, it holds

v^{k}(S\cup i)-v^{k}(S)=\begin{cases}v(i)+\Delta&\text{if }k=i,\\
v(i)&\text{if }k\neq i.\\
\end{cases}

(16)

Notice, (
16
) is dependent only on
i
and not on
S
. Further, for
X\in\{v(i),v(i)+\Delta\}
, it holds that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus i}{n-1\choose s}^{-1}X=X\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus i}{n-1\choose s}^{-1}.

(17)

Modifying the sum is an easy exercise using the following identity:

\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus i}{n-1\choose s}^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}{n-1\choose j}{n-1\choose j}^{n-1}=n.

(18)

Combining together (
16
), (
17
) and (
18
) in the expression of
\phi_{i}(v^{k})
from Definition
6
, it follows

\phi_{i}(v^{k})=\begin{cases}v(i)+\Delta&\text{if }i=k,\\
v(i)&\text{if }i\neq k.\\
\end{cases}

In a similar manner, for game
(N,e_{T})
, we can derive

e_{T}(S\cup i)-e_{T}(S)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }S\cup i=T,\\
1&\text{if }S=T,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(19)

Combining (
19
) with Definition
6
, it follows

\phi_{i}(e_{T})=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{n}{n-1\choose t-1}^{-1}\text{if }i\in T,\\
\frac{1}{n}{n-1\choose t}^{-1}\text{if }i\notin T.\\
\end{cases}

This concludes the proof.
∎

Another lemma is about the boundedness of
\cup\phi(C_{1}^{n})
.

Lemma 17
.

The weak Shapley value
\cup\phi(C_{1}^{n})
is unbounded.

Proof.

For every lower bound
b\in\mathbb{R}
, there is
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
with
\alpha_{i}=0
and
\beta_{T}=0
for every

except for exactly one

such that
i\in S
, for which
\beta_{S}={n-1\choose t-1}b+\epsilon
for

. It holds
\phi_{i}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})<b
.
∎

Results from Lemma

and

, combined with Lemma

on the relation between

.

be minimal incomplete game. it holds

\cup\phi(C_{1}^{n})\supsetneq\mathcal{I}(v)
if

\cup\phi(S^{n})\supseteq\mathcal{I}(v)

Proof.

From Lemma

, it follows

\bigcup\limits_{w_{\alpha}\in C_{1}^{n}(v)}\phi(w_{\alpha})=I(v).

(20)

Together with Lemma

, we have

Lemma 19

Combining (

\mathcal{I}(v)
and

Theorem 20
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
P^{n}
-extandable minimal incomplete game. It holds

\cup\phi(P^{n})\subsetneq\mathcal{I}(v).

Proof.

The inclusion
\cup\phi(P^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(v)
is immediate from
\cup\phi(P^{n})\subseteq\cup\phi(C^{n})
and Theorem
18
. To prove that the strict inclusion holds, we show that
I^{k}\notin\cup\phi(P^{n})
for every
k\in N
. For a contradition, suppose

. It means, there is a
P^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{A}})
such that
\phi({w_{A}})=I^{k}
. From

, we get

\sum_{T\in N_{1},i\in T}\frac{\alpha_{T}}{\lvert T\rvert}=0\text{ for every }i\neq k.

Since

∎

4.2
The
\tau
-value

The analysis of the weak
\tau
-value can be done is a similar manner as the analysis of the weak Shapley value. We begin by an explicit description of the
\tau
-value of every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension. Interestingly, even tough the set of
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions forms an unbounded convex cone, the weak
\tau
-value is a bounded set.

Lemma 21
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds for every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
that

\tau({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=I^{\alpha}.

Proof.

The assertion follows immediately from Theorem
4
, if we show that
g^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)=0
. From Lemma
1
, it holds

g^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)=\sum_{i\in N}\alpha_{i}g^{v^{i}}(N)+\sum_{T\in E}\beta_{T}g^{e_{T}}(N).

For every
i\in N
, it holds
b^{v^{i}}(N)=\sum_{j\in N}v(j)+\Delta=v(N)
, thus
g^{v^{i}}(N)=b^{v^{i}}(N)-v^{i}(N)=0
. Similarly, for every
T\in E
,
g^{e_{T}}(N)=0
. We conclude
g^{{w_{\alpha,\beta}}}(N)=0
.
∎

Theorem 22
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be minimal incomplete game. It holds

1.

\cup\tau(C_{1}^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable,

2.

\cup\tau(C^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C^{n}
-extendable,

3.

\cup\tau(S^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
S^{n}
-extendable.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the fact that

\bigcup\limits_{{w_{\alpha,\beta}}\in C_{1}^{n}(v)}\tau({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\\
\alpha(N)=1,\\
\forall i:\alpha_{i}\geq 0\end{subarray}}I^{\alpha}=\mathcal{I}(v).

The second follows from Lemma
15
and the fact that

\mathcal{I}(v)=\bigcup\limits_{{w_{\alpha}}\in C_{1}^{n}(v)}\tau({w_{\alpha}})\subseteq\cup\tau(C^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(v)

and the final assertion from
\mathcal{I}(v)=\cup\tau(C^{n})\subseteq\cup\tau(S^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(v)
.
∎

The weak
\tau
-value do not yield non trivial approximation for classes of
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions,
S^{n}
-extensions and
C^{n}
-extensions. This changes when we further restrict to positivity.

Lemma 23
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be
P^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds for every
P^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{A}})
that

\tau_{i}({w_{A}})=v(i)+\Delta\frac{\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1}:i\in T}\alpha_{T}}{\sum\limits_{\tiny{T\in N_{1}}}\alpha_{T}\lvert T\rvert}.

Proof.

From Theorem
4
, it holds for every
P^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{A}})
that

\tau_{i}({w_{A}})=b_{i}^{w_{A}}-\frac{g^{w_{A}}(N)}{\sum_{i\in N}g^{w_{A}}(i)}g^{w_{A}}(i).

From linearity of upper vectors and the gap function (Lemma
1
), we can express

g^{w_{A}}(N)=\sum_{T\in N_{1}}\alpha_{T}g^{v_{T}}(N)\text{ and }b^{w_{A}}(N)=\sum_{T\in N_{1}}b^{v_{T}}(N).

From the definition of the upper vector, we have

b_{i}^{v_{T}}=\begin{cases}v(i)+\Delta&\text{if }i\in T,\\
v(i)&\text{if }i\notin T.\\
\end{cases}

Further, it is easy to express

•

b^{v_{T}}(N)=\Delta\lvert T\rvert+\sum_{i\in N}v(i)
,

•

b^{{w_{A}}}(N)=\Delta\sum_{T\in N_{1}}\lvert T\rvert+\sum_{i\in N}v(i)
,

•

g^{{w_{A}}}(N)=\Delta\left(\sum_{T\in N_{1}}\lvert T\rvert-1\right)
,

•

b_{i}^{{w_{A}}}=v(i)+\Delta\sum_{T\in N_{1},i\in T}\alpha_{T}
,

•

g^{{w_{A}}}(i)=\Delta\sum_{T\in N_{1},i\in T}\alpha_{T}
.

Combining these expressions concludes the proof.
∎

.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
P^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds

\cup\tau(P^{n})\subsetneq\mathcal{I}(v).

, we show
I^{k}\notin\cup\tau(P^{n})
for any

. For a contradition, if

P^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{A}})
such that

that

\sum_{T\subseteq N,i\in T}\alpha_{T}=0\text{ for every }i\neq k.

(24)

and (

4.3
The nucleolus and the (pre-)kernel

Although different in general, for the class of convex games, the nucleolus, the prekernel, and the kernel coincide. The prekernel and the kernel even coincide for the class of superadditive games and for both superadditive and convex games, these three solution concepts are subsets of
\mathcal{I}(v)
. We show that for superadditive and convex extensions, the approximations do not yield any strengthening over
\emptyset\subseteq\mathcal{S}(v)\subseteq\mathcal{I}(v)
. For positive extensions, however, we are able to show that there is an improvement for the weak solution.

Lemma 25
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds for every

-extension
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
that

\eta^{*}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=I^{\alpha}.

Proof.

From Theorem
9
and Lemma
21
, we have
\eta({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\tau({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=I^{\alpha}
.
∎

Theorem 26
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a minimal incomplete game. It holds

1.

\cup\eta(S^{n})=\cup\mathcal{K}(S^{n})=\cup\mathcal{K}^{*}(S^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
S^{n}
-extendable,

2.

\cup\eta(C^{n})=\cup\mathcal{K}(C^{n})=\cup\mathcal{K}^{*}(C^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C^{n}
-extendable,

3.

\cup\eta(C_{1}^{n})=\cup\mathcal{K}(C_{1}^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable,

4.

\cup\mathcal{K}^{*}(C_{1}^{n})\supseteq\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)

C_{1}^{n}
-extendable.

Proof.

The theorem follows immediately from Lemma

, the relation between the sets of

) and Equation (

∎

As the coincidence of the prekernel and kernel with the nucleolus suggests, from Lemma

-extensions,

-extensions and

Also, the weak nucleolus is not a non trivial approximation even when convexity is considered. Yet again, this changes when restricting to positivity. However, the positivity is still not strong enough for a non trivial lower approximation.

Theorem 27
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
P^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. It holds

\cup\mathcal{K}^{*}(P^{n})\subsetneq\mathcal{I}(v).

Proof.

We show that
\mathcal{I}^{k}\notin\cup\mathcal{K}^{*}(P^{n})
for every
k\in N
. For a contradiction, let
(N,{w_{A}})\in P^{n}(v)
such that
\mathcal{K}^{*}({w_{A}})=\{I^{k}\}
. The prekernel is single-valued because by Theorem
8
,
\mathcal{K}^{*}({w_{A}})=\eta({w_{A}})
. We can express the excess of every
S\subseteq N
as

e(S,I^{k},{w_{A}})=\begin{cases}\Delta\left(\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq S}\alpha_{T}-1\right)&\text{if }k\in S,\\
\Delta\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq S}\alpha_{T}&\text{if }k\notin S.\\
\end{cases}

It holds that

e(S,I^{k},{w_{A}})\leq e(S\cup i,I^{k},{w_{A}})\text{ for }i\neq k

and

e(S,I^{k},{w_{A}})\leq 0\text{ if }k\in S.

Therefore, the maximal surplus
s_{ij}(I^{k},{w_{A}})
is attained for
N\setminus\{j,k\}
if
i\neq k
and for
N\setminus\{j\}
if
i=k
. Consider the relation
s_{ki}(I^{k},{w_{A}})=s_{ik}(I^{k},{w_{A}})
. It can be equivalently expressed as

\Delta\left(\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus i}\alpha_{T}-1\right)=\Delta\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus k}\alpha_{T},

or

\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus i}\alpha_{T}-\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus k}\alpha_{T}=1.

From
\alpha_{T}\geq 0
for
T\in N_{1}
and
\sum_{T\in N_{1}}\alpha_{T}=1
, it holds for every
T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus k
that
\alpha_{T}=0
. This means that

\sum\limits_{T\in N_{1},T\subseteq N\setminus i}\alpha_{T}=1\text{ for }\forall i\neq k.

To satisfy all these conditions, there has to be a coalition
S
with
\alpha_{S}=1
from

\bigcap\limits_{i\neq k}\{T\in N_{1}\mid T\subseteq N\setminus i\}=\emptyset.

This is a contradiction.
∎

To show that the strong nucleolus is empty, one only has to show there are two
P^{n}
-extensions with different nucleolus. This is trivial and left to the reader.

4.4
The core and the Weber set

The core is always a selection from the imputation set
\mathcal{I}(v)
. We show that even for the set of
P^{n}
-extensions, the weak solution is equal to the imputation set. However, there is a
P^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{A}})
for which
\mathcal{C}({w_{A}})=\mathcal{I}(v)
, therefore without further knowledge, it might be that actually the approximation is equal to the solution.

Theorem 28
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a minimal incomplete game. It holds

1.

\cup\mathcal{C}(P^{n})=\cup\mathcal{W}(P^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
P^{n}
-extendable,

2.

\cup\mathcal{C}(C^{n})=\cup\mathcal{W}(C^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
C^{n}
-extendable,

3.

\cup\mathcal{C}(S^{n})=\cup\mathcal{C}(S^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v)
if
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
is
S^{n}
-extendable.

Proof.

One of the vertices of the set of
P^{n}
-extensions,
(N,v_{N})
, defined in (
5
), satisfies
\mathcal{W}(v_{N})=\mathcal{I}(v)
. The rest follows from the relation between the core and the Weber set (Theorem
6
) and the relation between sets of
C
-extensions from (
8
).
To show that
\mathcal{W}(v_{N})=\mathcal{I}(v)
, we actually show that the vertices of these sets coincide. Consider
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
and
S_{\sigma(1)},\dots,S_{\sigma(n)}
. For the correspoing marginal vector
m_{\sigma}^{v_{N}}
it holds

(m_{\sigma}^{v_{N}})_{i}=v_{N}(S_{\sigma(i)}\cup i)-v_{N}(S_{\sigma(i)})=\begin{cases}v(i)+\Delta&\text{if $\sigma(i)=n$,}\\
v(i)&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

It follows
m_{\sigma}^{v_{N}}=I^{k}
where
\sigma(k)=n
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Game
(N,v_{N})
is not 1-convex, therefore the previous result cannot be applied for
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions. We also have to be more cautious in our analysis, because it does no longer hold that
\mathcal{W}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\mathcal{C}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})
because for
\beta\neq 0
, extension
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
might not be convex anymore. However, we are able to show much more for the set of
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions.

Lemma 29
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable incomplete game. For every
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
it holds

\mathcal{C}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\{I^{\alpha}\}.

Proof.

If we show that
g^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)=0
, it follows from Theorem
5
, that
\mathcal{C}({w_{\alpha,\beta}})=\{b^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}\}
. Further, for every
i\in N
,

b^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}_{i}={w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)-{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N\setminus i)=v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta=I^{\alpha}_{i},

which concludes the proof. To prove that
g^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)=0
, we employ Lemma
1
, to express

g^{w_{\alpha,\beta}}(N)=\sum_{i\in N}\alpha_{i}g^{v^{i}}(N)+\sum_{T\in E}\beta_{T}g^{e_{T}}(N).

It follows from the definition of the gap function and definitions of games
(N,v^{i})
and
(N,e_{T})
that both

g^{v^{i}}(N)=0\text{ and }g^{e_{T}}(N)=0.

∎

Theorem 30
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete games. It holds that

\cup\mathcal{C}(C_{1}^{n})=\mathcal{I}(v).

Proof.

Follows immediatelly from Lemma
29
.
∎

To analyse the weak Weber set, we can yet again explicitly express the Weber set of every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension.

Lemma 31
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be a
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable minimal incomplete game. for every
C_{1}^{n}
-extension
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
it holds
\mathcal{W}({w_{\alpha}})=conv\{m^{{w_{\alpha,\beta}}}_{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}\}
, where

\left(m^{{w_{\alpha,\beta}}}_{\sigma}\right)_{i}=\begin{cases}v(i)&\sigma(1)=i\\
v(i)+(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})\Delta-\beta_{S_{\sigma(j)}\cup\{j\}}&\sigma(1)=j,\sigma(2)=i\\
v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta+\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}}&\sigma(n-1)=i\\
v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta&\sigma(n)=i\\
v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta+(\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}}-\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}\cup\{i\}})\end{cases}

Proof.

Follows from the definition of marginal vectors, defined in (
4
) and the definition of
(N,{w_{\alpha,\beta}})
, defined in (
12
).
∎

Theorem 32
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be
C_{1}^{n}
-extendable incomplete game. It holds

\mathcal{I}(v)\subsetneq\cup\mathcal{W}(C_{1}^{n}).

Proof.

From Lemma
31
, we know

\mathcal{I}(v)=\bigcup\limits_{{w_{\alpha}}\in C_{1}^{n}(v)}\mathcal{C}({w_{\alpha}})=\bigcup\limits_{{w_{\alpha}}\in C_{1}^{n}(v)}\mathcal{W}({w_{\alpha}})\subseteq\cup\mathcal{W}(C_{1}^{n}).

The strict inclusion can be derived from Lemma
31
, when considering
(m_{\sigma}^{w_{\alpha,\beta}})_{i}
with
i\neq\sigma(1)
such that

(m_{\sigma}^{w_{\alpha,\beta}})_{i}=v(i)+\alpha_{i}\Delta+(\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}}-\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}\cup\{i\}}).

Fixing other
\beta_{T}
, with
\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}}
going to infinity,
(m_{\sigma}^{w_{\alpha,\beta}})_{i}
tends to infinity and with
\beta_{S_{\sigma(i)}\cup\{i\}}
going to infinity,
(m_{\sigma}^{w_{\alpha,\beta}})_{i}
tends to minus infinity.
∎

From Lemma
29
, it is easy to see that the strong core of the set of
C_{1}^{n}
-extensions (thus also
C^{n}
-extensions and
S^{n}(v)
-extensions) is an empty set. The Lemma does not apply to the set of
P^{n}
-extensions, because none of games
(N,{w_{\alpha}})
is positive. To prove the emptyness of the strong core of
P^{n}
-extensions, we show that for every
I^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{I}(v)
, there is
(N,{w_{A}})
such that
I^{\alpha}\notin\mathcal{C}({w_{A}})
.

Theorem 33
.

Let
(N,\mathcal{K},v)
be
P^{n}
-extendable minimal game. It holds

\cap\mathcal{C}(P^{n})=\cap\mathcal{W}(P^{n})=\emptyset.

Proof.

Let
I^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{I}(v)
and distinguish two cases. If
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
then choose
(N,{w_{A}})
such that for
i,j
different from
1,2
, we have
\alpha_{\{i,j\}}=1
and
\alpha_{T}=0
otherwise. It follows that

I^{k}(\{i,j\})=v(i)+v(j)<v(i)+v(j)+\Delta={w_{A}}(\{i,j\}),

therefore
I^{\alpha}\notin\mathcal{C}({w_{A}})
because the coalitional rationality for
\{i,j\}
is not satisfied.

Similarly, if
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<1
, choose
(N,{w_{A}})
such that
\alpha_{\{1,2\}}=1
and
\alpha_{T}=0
otherwise. Again,

I^{k}(\{1,2\})=v(1)+v(2)+(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\Delta<v(1)+v(2)+\Delta={w_{A}}(\{1,2\}),

meaning the coalitional rationality is not satisfied for
\{1,2\}
, thus
I^{\alpha}\notin\mathcal{C}({w_{A}})
.
∎

5
Conclusion

We introduced a new method to approximate the solution concepts of cooperative games when only partial information is given. The method was demonstrated on a class of minimal incomplete cooperative games. We investigated different solution concepts and showed how the approximations depend on the knowledge about the underlying game, specifically on the class of the underlying game. We note that incomplete games with different structure of
\mathcal{K}
can be analysed in a similar manner. Interesting candidates might be e.g.

1.

\mathcal{K}=\{T\subseteq N\mid i\in T\}
,

2.

\mathcal{K}=\{T\subseteq N\mid\lvert T\rvert\leq k\}
,

3.

\mathcal{K}=\{N,\emptyset\}\cup\{N\setminus i\mid i\in N\}
(for motivation, see
Bok2020
),

4.

classes of games given in
Grabisch2011
.

We showed that for minimal incomplete games, most of the weak solutions become non trivial (not equal to the imputation set) when we switch between the sets of
C^{n}
-extensions and
P^{n}
-extensions. The first thing we would like to understand in the future is when exactly this change happens. A possible approach might be to study the weak solutions for sets of
k
-monotonic extensions
(see
Grabisch2016
). In this view, convex games are
2
-monotonic and positive games are
(2^{n}-2)
-monotonic. It further holds that
k
-monotonic games form a subset of
(k-1)
-monotonic games.

Among other solution concepts, the core stands out because using our method, it is the only solution concept which do not yield non trivial approximation even for
P^{n}
-extensions. To understand the core and also to be able to approximate it, it is important to find sets of
C
-extensions for which we get non trivial approximations. A possible candidate might be to further restrict
P^{n}
-extensions which are
k
-additive, and the most restrictive class of 2-additive games (see
Grabisch2016
). We note that the proof of Theorem
33
shows that the strong core of 2-additive
P^{n}
-extensions is still empty, however, it might be still a different case for the weak core.

The next big step regarding the approximations is to derive tools to further analyse the
strength
of the approximations. So far, we considered only the inclusion as a measure that one approximation is better than another. For example, if there is a
C
-extensions for which the solution concept is equal exactly to the weak solution (see proof of Theorem
28
), the approximation is clearly best possible we can get if we consider that the underlying game is from
C
. But consider weak solutions of one-point solution concepts. Clearly,
smaller
the weak solution (in volume, range of values, …), the better approximation we are getting. We also want to address these questions in near future.
