Quantum fluctuating theory for one-dimensional shock waves
Andrew Urilyon, Stefano Scopa, Giuseppe Del Vecchio Del Vecchio, Jacopo De Nardis

Abstract
We study the formation and the subsequent dynamics of shock waves in repulsive one-dimensional Bose gases during the free expansion of a density hump. By building coherent Fermi states for interacting Bethe fermions, we define a quantum fluctuating initial state expressed in terms of universal quantities, namely the density and the Luttinger parameter. In the integrable case, this fluctuating state is then evolved by generalized hydrodynamics (GHD) and, differently from non-fluctuating initial states, it develops density ripples on top of the hydrodynamic mean value. Our analysis gives a general theory of quantum ripples and wave breaking in integrable and quasi-integrable one-dimensional liquids and clarifies the role of the interaction strength. In particular, for strongly/intermediately interacting bosons, we find quantum ripples originating from low-energy modes at the Fermi surface interfering when transported by GHD. In the low coupling limit, near the quasicondensate regime, we find instead that density ripples have a semi-classical nature, and their description requires information on the curvature of the Fermi surface.

Introduction

Quantum gases in one dimension often respond to external perturbations through collective excitations rather than single-particle effects. An illustrative perturbation consists of a spatial modulation of the fluid density, induced e.g. by an external potential (Figure

A celebrated example is the formation of shock waves in a fluid of impenetrable bosons (or noninteracting fermions). Here, denser regions have higher particle velocities, causing an overturn of fast modes over the background, and initiating nonlinear physics after the shock time

In this work, we take a different path to merge quantum fluctuations with hydrodynamic descriptions, in particular focusing on the case of shock wave dynamics. Analogously to what is done in classical
fluctuating hydrodynamics

Castro-Alvaredo et al. 2016

Bertini et al. 2016

Panfil et al. 2023
;

;

;

This approach allows us to give a complete characterization of quantum and semi-classical density ripples during the formation and propagation of the shock, thereby establishing a direct relation between the types of ripples and the underlying microscopic interaction in the gas.

In general, characterizing such fluctuating initial states for an interacting quantum gas is a hard task. In the case of Fig.

;
Keeling et al. 2006
;

Here we extend this description of coherent states of fermions to interacting quantum gases, such as the interacting Lieb-Liniger model. Namely, we construct fluctuating initial states obtained as coherent states of the refermionized degrees of freedom describing quantum fluctuations at low energies. In particular, for a given initial macroscopic density pertubation

Our analysis finally clarifies the different nature of density ripples in one-dimensional shock waves depending on the interaction coupling of the gas.
To clarify this, we focus on the post-shock dynamics
t>t_{s}
and we consider a number of Bethe fermions
\delta N_{F}(x)
contained within a small subregion of size
\delta x\sim{\cal O}(\ell_{\text{hydro}})
around position
x
, see Fig.
2
for an illustration. Here, we introduced the hydrodynamic scale
\ell_{\text{hydro}}
satisfying
\ell_{\text{hydro}}\ll\ell
, with
\ell\sim\rho/|\partial_{x}\rho|
the scale of the external perturbation. A quantization condition in this subregion requires that momenta satisfy
(\delta k\cdot\delta x)/\Delta N_{F}=\delta N_{F}/\Delta N_{F}
, with
\Delta N_{F}
the total number of particles in the hump. We then distinguish the following cases:

–
Quantum regime
, if
\delta N_{F}/\Delta N_{F}\sim{\cal O}(1)
. Quantum ripples are associated to the notion of a quantized phase space, and are generated from the interference of low-energy modes at the Fermi surface. This class includes those effects that are captured by field-theory approaches, such as quantum GHD, e.g.
Ruggiero et al. 2020
;
Ruggiero et al. 2021
;
Scopa et al. 2023
.

–
Semi-classical regime
, if
\delta N_{F}/\Delta N_{F}\ll 1
. In this case, to properly characterize the density ripples around position
x
, further information on the Fermi surface in a surrounding region scaling as
\sim{\cal O}(\ell^{2/3})
becomes necessary. Such semi-classical ripples might be viewed as a background effect originating from higher-derivative corrections
Fagotti 2017
;
Fagotti 2020
;
De Nardis and Doyon 2023
;
Bocini 2023
, and/or by universal corrections in the form of Airy kernels at the propagating front, e.g.
Balazs and Zipfel 1973
;
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
;
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
;
Eisler and Rácz 2013
;
Dean et al. 2018
;
Dean et al. 2019
;
De Bruyne et al. 2021
;
Gouraud 2024
.

Both quantum and semi-classical ripples are captured by our fluctuating state, with the dominant contribution fixed by the choice of the initial density hump
\Delta\rho
and the value of the coupling. At strong repulsion
c\to\infty
, Bethe fermions coincide with physical degrees of freedom (
K=1
), thus one can choose a density perturbation such that
\delta N(x)\sim{\cal O}(\Delta N)
, and quantum ripples give the leading contribution inside the shock region. Moreover, since the gas is non homogeneous, semi-classical ripples are also present, particularly in regions
\sim{\cal O}(\ell^{2/3})
around the shock fronts
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
;
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
. In the weak coupling regime
c\to 0^{+}
and
\rho\sim 1/c\sim{\cal O}(\ell)
to the quasicondensate, the density ripples only exhibit a semi-classical nature. In this limit, the Luttinger parameter diverges as
K\sim 1/\sqrt{c}

Popov 1977
, leading to a diverging Bethe fermion density
\rho_{F}\sim 1/\sqrt{c}
, with semi-classical ripples spreading over a size
{\cal O}(c^{-2/3})
. Finally, for intermediate couplings both quantum and semi-classical ripples are present with the quantum ones rapidly vanishing as interactions are decreased.

II
Model and refermionization of low-energy excitations

Our analysis takes the Lieb-Liniger model
Lieb 1963
;
Lieb and Liniger 1963
as a foundational reference, although our derivation maintains full generality. Describing
N
contact-interacting bosonic particles subject to an external potential
V(x)
, the Lieb-Liniger model’s Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{i}}^{2}}+c\sum_{i<j=1}^{N}\delta(x_{i}-x_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i}),

(1)

with the local density operator
\hat{\rho}(x)=\sum_{j}\delta(x-x_{j})/L
and with
L
the system size. Below we specify to the repulsive regime
c>0
and we shall use
\gamma=c/\rho
as the rescaled coupling strength. This model serves as a paradigmatic representation for one-dimensional interacting systems and cold atomic gases, as extensively discussed in Refs.
Cazalilla 2004
;
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Bouchoule and Dubail 2022
.

In the absence of external potentials (or equivalently when
V(x)=
const), the Hamiltonian (
1
) is integrable. Namely, for even (resp. odd)
N
, an eigenstate of
\hat{H}
is associated to a sequence of half-integers (resp. integers)
\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
that specifies the set quasi-momenta
\theta_{i}\in[-\infty,\infty]
(or rapidities) through the solution of the Bethe equations

\theta_{j}=\frac{2\pi I_{j}}{L}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})

(2)

with scattering phase
\phi(\theta)=2\arctan(\theta/c)
and the associated scattering shift
T=\partial_{\theta}\phi
. Physically, one can interpret
2\pi I_{j}/L
as the momenta of
N
non-interacting fermions, and the Bethe equations as a non-trivial quantization condition accounting for the interactions. The system’s eigenstates can be parameterized by either the integers or the rapidities, by application of the mapping from
\ket{\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}
to
\ket{\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}
given in eq. (
2
). For instance, the ground state set of rapidities
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is obtained by solving (
2
) from an equally spaced sequence of Bethe integers
\{I_{i}\equiv i-\frac{N-1}{2}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

In the thermodynamic limit
N,L\to\infty
, the set of rapidities densely populate the real line and it is conveniently described in terms of a density distribution. Precisely, macrostates of the Lieb-Liniger gas are described in terms of a Fermi-Dirac occupation function
n(\theta)
(usually called filling function) for fermionic quasiparticles featuring a bare energy
\varepsilon(\theta)=\theta^{2}/2
and bare momentum
p(\theta)=\theta
. Its precise expression is dictated by the value of temperature and chemical potential, see e.g. Ref.
Takahashi 1999
for a detailed discussion. At zero temperature, the filling function becomes a perfect Fermi sea between the two Fermi edges
\pm\theta_{F}
, namely
n(\theta)=\Theta(\theta^{2}-\theta_{F}^{2})
where
\Theta(\cdot)
is the Heaviside step function. Expectation values of local observables are then obtained as integrals in rapidity space weighted with
n(\theta)
. The density is
\rho_{0}=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}1_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)n(\theta)
, with dressing operation respect to filling
n
, defined as

f_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)=f(\theta)+\int\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}T(\theta-\alpha)n(\alpha)f_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(\alpha),

(3)

and similarly for other quantities.

As
n(\theta)
describes a perfect Fermi sea, any information on ground state fluctuations is lost. A standard way to reintroduce these quantum fluctuations is offered by refermionization methods. For instance, quantum fluctuations of the density operator
\delta\hat{\rho}(x)=\hat{\rho}(x)-\rho_{0}
, can be written as

\delta\hat{\rho}(x)=\sqrt{K}(\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{+}(x)\hat{\psi}_{+}(x)+\hat{\psi}_{-}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\psi}_{-}(x))+\text{h.o.c.}

(4)

where
\hat{\psi}_{\pm}
are chiral fermions whose properties are established by a free massless Dirac field theory, see e.g. Refs.
Sénéchal 1999
;
Gogolin et al. 1998
for further discussion. The two-point function is

\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(x)\hat{\psi}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(y)\rangle=\frac{\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}}{{\rm i}\sigma(x-y)},\quad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=\pm

(5)

and higher correlations can be obtained using Wick’s theorem. Alternatively, one can encode low-energy fluctuations in terms of Tomonaga-Luttinger chiral bosons
\hat{\varphi}_{\pm}
satisfying
\langle\hat{\varphi}_{\sigma}(x)\hat{\varphi}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(y)\rangle=\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\log({\rm i}\sigma(x-y))
. The two descriptions are connected via bosonization,

\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(x)\propto:e^{-{\rm i}\sigma\hat{\varphi}_{\sigma}(x)}:

(where
:\cdot:
is the normal ordering of fields), see Appendix
A
for more details.

Eq. (
4
) can be also understood on physical grounds. Low-energy states of the Lieb-Liniger model can be obtained by creating particle-hole pairs around the left/right Fermi edges, and can be realized by left/right fermionic operators
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{p\lessgtr 0}=\int dx\ e^{-\frac{{\rm i}2\pi px}{L}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\mp}(x)
acting on the sequence of ground state Bethe integers. For instance,

\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\frac{N}{2}}\hat{c}_{\frac{N+1}{2}}\ket{\{\dots\underset{\frac{N-1}{2}}{\bullet}\underset{\frac{N+1}{2}}{\bullet}\underset{\frac{N}{2}}{\circ}\}}=\ket{\{\dots\underset{\frac{N-1}{2}}{\bullet}\underset{\frac{N+1}{2}}{\circ}\underset{\frac{N}{2}}{\bullet}\}}.

(6)

Hence, Eq. (
4
) encodes the quantum fluctuations of density as coming from particle-hole pairs generated at the Fermi edges. The amplitude in eq. (
4
) can be in fact interpreted as the thermodynamic form factor
\langle\text{part.-hole}|\delta\hat{\rho}(0)|\text{g.s.}\rangle/L=\sqrt{K}

Nardis and Panfil 2015
;
Bouchoule et al. 2023
, with
K=(1^{\rm dr}(\pm\theta_{F}))^{2}
the Luttinger parameter of the gas. Notice that there are short-wavelength non-universal corrections to Eq. (
4
) accounting for backscattering processes between the two Fermi edges. Since below we are interested in the evolution of chiral states, we shall not consider these terms in the density expansion.

In the presence of an external potential
V(x)
, integrability is lost. A first approximation that is usually considered is the local density approximation (LDA): at each position
x
the state is a Fermi sea with a
\theta_{F}(x)
fixed by the local density
\langle\hat{\rho}(x)\rangle
. We denote filling functions obtained this way as
n_{\rm LDA}(\theta,x)=\Theta(\theta^{2}-\theta^{2}_{F}(x))
. Such filling functions however do not include any quantum fluctuations. Therefore, in the rest of this work, we shall incorporate in the Bethe ansatz description of the gas the fluctuating part (
4
) obtained via refermionization. As a result, we obtain an improved initial state, denoted below as
n_{\text{Fluct}}(x,\theta)
. In doing so, our starting point is Ref.
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
(which we revisit in the following section) where the case at strong repulsion
c\to\infty
has been investigated.

III
Tonks-Girardeau limit and Fermi coherent states

At strong repulsion
c\to\infty
, the system enters the Tonks-Girardeau regime. For simple observables having no Jordan-Wigner strings, the latter is equivalent to a free Fermi gas, with fermionic degrees of freedom
\theta_{i}=2\pi I_{i}/L
now coinciding with the physical ones. In this limit, as
K\equiv 1
, there is no need to distinguish the fermionic from the bosonic density in eq. (
4
). Focusing on the right chirality, the density operator is

\delta\hat{\rho}_{+}(x)=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{+}\hat{\psi}_{+}=\sum_{k>0}e^{{\rm i}kx}\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{+,k}

(7)

with
\hat{A}_{+,k}
creating a right-moving phonon, or equivalently a superposition of particle-hole pairs generated around the right Fermi edge
\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{+,k}=\sum_{p>0}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{p}\hat{c}_{p+k}

Bouchoule et al. 2023
.

Now, as anticipated in the introduction, Fermi gases in a chiral potential
V(x)
are found in a coherent state
\ket{V}=\hat{\cal U}|0\rangle
, obtained as unitary transformation of the ground state operated by

\hat{\cal U}=\exp\left({\rm i}\int\Phi(x)\hat{\rho}(x)dx\right).

(8)

The function
\Phi(x)
is fixed by the expectation value of density, namely

\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}=\langle V|\hat{\rho}(x)|V\rangle\equiv\rho_{\rm LDA}(x)=\frac{p_{F}(x)}{\pi}

(9)

since no corrections to LDA are expected in the thermodynamic limit for an infinite system. Here, the two Fermi edges
\pm p_{F}(x)=\pm\sqrt{2(\mu-V(x))}
and
n_{\text{LDA}}=\Theta(\theta^{2}-p_{F}^{2}(x))
, such that

\rho_{\rm LDA}(x)=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}n_{\text{LDA}}(\theta,x)=\frac{p_{F}(x)}{\pi}.

(10)

\Phi(x)
carries also the physical interpretation of a semi-classical phase for long-wavelength modulation of density, similarly to a height field in the bosonization language, see e.g. Refs.
Giamarchi 2003
;
Cazalilla 2004
.

Importantly,
\hat{A}_{+,k}\ket{V}=p_{k}\ket{V}
, with eigenvalues given by the Fourier modes
p_{k}=\int e^{-{\rm i}kx}d\Phi(x)
. We can therefore conclude that:
i)

\ket{V}
is a coherent state for the low-energy quantum fluctuations in (
4
);
ii)
it has a density of excitations modulated by the external potential
V(x)
via eq. (
9
).

At this point, by noticing that the unitary transformation on the chiral fermi operators amounts to an overall phase
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011

\hat{\cal U}\ \hat{\psi}_{\pm}(x)\ \hat{\cal U}^{\dagger}=e^{\mp{\rm i}\Phi(x)}\ \hat{\psi}_{\pm}(x),

(11)

we can compute two-point function on
\ket{V}
as

\langle V|\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}(x_{1})\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(x_{2})|V\rangle=\frac{2\sin(\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}p_{F}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime})}{x_{1}-x_{2}}.

(12)

Taking the Wigner transform of the two-point function, one obtains the occupation function

n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta)=\int{dy}e^{{\rm i}y\theta}\ \frac{\sin(\int_{x-y/2}^{x+y/2}p_{F}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime})}{\pi y}

(13)

or, equivalently, in terms of the initial potential

n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta)=\int{dy}e^{{\rm i}y\theta}\ \frac{\sin(\int_{x-y/2}^{x+y/2}\sqrt{2(\mu-V(x^{\prime}))}dx^{\prime})}{\pi y},

(14)

where the notation
n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta)
denotes a (fluctuating) filling function taking also negative values, i.e. carrying the physical interpretation of a quasi-probability of finding a particle in the same spirit of semi-classical Wigner functions
Wigner 1932
;
Hillery et al. 1984
;
Ferrie 2011
;
Moyal 1949
. In the limit where the external potential is switched off, then
p_{F}=\text{const}
and the integral in eq. (
13
) gives
n_{\text{Fluct}}=n_{\text{LDA}}
.

As shown already e.g. in Refs.
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
;
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
;
Protopopov et al. 2013
;
Protopopov et al. 2014
;
Veness and Glazman 2019
;
Ruggiero et al. 2021
;
Scopa et al. 2023
;
Dean et al. 2019
, the time evolution after a trap release of such fluctuating state can be described by the hydrodynamic equation

\partial_{t}n_{\text{Fluct}}=-\theta\partial_{x}n_{\text{Fluct}},

(15)

with solution
n_{\text{Fluct}}(t;x,\theta)=n_{\text{Fluct}}(0;x-\theta t,\theta)
.

To begin with, we associate a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x,\theta)
to each phase-space point, and define the Fermi contour as the geometric locus satisfying at
t=0

\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,\theta):\ \theta^{2}=p_{F}^{2}(x)\}.

(17)

\Gamma_{0}
can be described as a simple curve in phase space since the system is characterized by two symmetric Fermi edges
\pm p_{F}(x)
at each
x
. In order to take advantage of this, we introduce a coordinate
s(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}
along
\Gamma_{0}
such that (with slight abuse of notation)

\Gamma_{0}(s)=\{s(\mathbf{x})\ :\ \theta_{0}(s)=p_{F}(x_{0}(s))\}.

(18)

For simplicity, below we specialise to the right-moving front of density
x>0
(the left-moving front is obtained by symmetry). It is then possible to choose the coordinate
s
to be

s(x_{0})=\int^{x_{0}}_{-\infty}\frac{dx^{\prime}}{p_{F}(x^{\prime})},

(19)

which conveniently replaces the spatial coordinate
dx
with the propagating time
ds=dx/p_{F}(x)
on the non-flat Fermi surface, see e.g. Refs.
Brun and Dubail 2017
;
Brun and Dubail 2018
;
Scopa et al. 2020
. This curve holds also another physical meaning, being directly related to a phase-space quantization condition, see Refs.
Berry 1977
;
Arnold 1978
and Appendix
B
. In terms of this coordinate, the dynamics generated by eq. (
15

\theta_{t}(s)\equiv\theta_{0}(s)
is conserved)

x_{t}(s)=x_{0}(s)+t\theta_{0}(s)

(20)

which can be solved requiring that
x_{t}(s)\equiv x
, yielding the replacement of
x_{0}
with
x-\theta t
in eq. (
19
).

We then write the time-evolved density in eq. (
16

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\rho}(t,x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{d\theta\,dy}{2\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\left|\frac{ds(x_{1})}{dx}\right|\left|\frac{ds(x_{2})}{dx}\right|}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\sin\left(\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}dx_{0}\ \frac{dx^{\prime}_{t}}{dx_{0}}p_{F}(x^{\prime}_{t})\right)e^{{\rm i}\theta y}}{(s(x_{2})-s(x_{1}))}\,,

(21)

with backward-evolved coordinates

x_{1,2}=x-\theta t\mp y/2,

(22)

and time-dependent phase difference

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}dx_{0}(s)\ \frac{dx^{\prime}_{t}(s)}{dx_{0}(s)}\;p_{F}(x^{\prime}_{t}(s))

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}dx^{\prime}\ p_{F}(x^{\prime})+\frac{t\,p_{F}^{2}(x^{\prime})}{2}\bigg|_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}.

(23)

The second term in (
III.1
) is nothing but the dynamical phase cumulated by a single particle with momentum
p_{F}(x)
that freely expands for a time
t

Dubail et al. 2017
;
Ruggiero et al. 2019
;
Scopa et al. 2023
. The jacobians are easily evaluated as (see Appendix
B
)

\frac{ds(x_{1,2})}{dx}=\frac{1}{p_{F}(x_{1,2})[1+tp_{F}^{\prime}(x_{1,2})]}.

(24)

One can then introduce the semi-classical action

S(\theta,y)=-\theta y+\Delta\Phi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})

(25)

and look for the saddle point configuration
\nabla S(\theta,y)=0
. Away from singularities, the saddle point fixes the major contribution to eq. (
III.1
) as coming from
y\to 0
,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\rho}(t,x)\rangle\approx

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\in\{x^{\star}+y/2\}\\
x_{2}\in\{x^{\star}-y/2\}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\left|\frac{ds(x_{1})}{dx}\right|\left|\frac{ds(x_{2})}{dx}\right|}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{\sin\left(\Delta\Phi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})\right)}{2\pi(s(x_{2})-s(x_{1}))}

(26)

with
x_{1,2}=x^{\star}\pm y/2
projected to the Fermi contour value, that is

x^{\star}=x-tp_{F}(x^{\star}).

(27)

Clearly,
x^{\star}\equiv x^{\star}(t;x)
. This self-consistent equation can display multiple solutions. Precisely, multiple solutions are found in the
shock region

x_{-}(t)\leq x\leq x_{+}(t)
with boundaries
x_{\pm}(t)
satisfying

p^{\prime}_{F}[x^{\star}(t;x)]\bigg|_{x=x_{\pm}(t)}=\infty,

(28)

as also explained in Appendix
B
.

It is then easy to see that for
x\notin[x_{-}(t);x_{+}(t)]
, the limit
y\to 0
corresponds to
x_{1}\to x_{2}
, and one recovers the LDA result for the density from eq. (

\lim_{x_{1}\to x_{2}}\Delta\Phi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})\approx p_{F}(x^{\star})y
. On the other hand, inside the shock region corrections to LDA density are observed, as derived below. For convenience, we separate the
x\approx x_{\pm}(t)
region from the rest of the shock region,
x_{-}(t)<x<x_{+}(t)
, discussion.

III.1.1
Saddle point contribution in the shock region

In the region
x_{-}(t)<x<x_{+}(t)
, eq. (
27
) has three different solutions
x^{\star}_{j}
satisfying
x^{\star}_{1}<x^{\star}_{2}<x^{\star}_{3}
. They correspond to 12 terms in the sum of eq. (
III.1
). The “diagonal” contributions
x_{1,2}=x^{\star}_{j}\pm y/2
reproduce the LDA result, that is

\rho_{\text{LDA}}(t,x)=\frac{p_{F}(x^{\star}_{1})-p_{F}(x^{\star}_{2})}{2\pi}+\frac{p_{F}(x^{\star}_{3})}{2\pi}+\frac{\rho_{0}}{2},

(29)

\rho_{0}=\sqrt{2\mu}/\pi
, while the non-diagonal terms give rise to LDA corrections

\displaystyle\langle\delta\hat{\rho}(t,x)\rangle\bigg|_{x^{\star}_{i},x^{\star}_{j}}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\left|\frac{ds(x^{\star}_{i})}{dx}\right|\left|\frac{ds(x^{\star}_{j})}{dx}\right|}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\sin\left(\Delta\Phi_{t}(x^{\star}_{i},x^{\star}_{j})-{\cal I}_{ij}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}{2\pi(s(x^{\star}_{i})-s(x^{\star}_{j}))}\,,

where
{\cal I}_{ij}
is the Maslov index along the path
s(x^{\star}_{i})\to s(x^{\star}_{j})
on the Fermi surface, see Appendix
B
for more details. Combining all terms together, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\delta\hat{\rho}(t,x>0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-J_{12}\frac{\cos\left(\Delta\Phi_{t}(x^{\star}_{1},x^{\star}_{2})\right)}{\pi|s(x^{\star}_{1})-s(x^{\star}_{2})|}+J_{23}\frac{\cos\left(\Delta\Phi_{t}(x^{\star}_{2},x^{\star}_{3})\right)}{\pi|s(x^{\star}_{2})-s(x^{\star}_{3})|}-J_{13}\frac{\sin\left(\Delta\Phi_{t}(x^{\star}_{1},x^{\star}_{3})\right)}{\pi|s(x^{\star}_{1})-s(x^{\star}_{3})|},

(31)

where the shorthand
J_{ij}=\sqrt{|ds(x^{\star}_{j})/dx||ds(x^{\star}_{i})/dx|}
has been used for convenience. Notice that the same analytical expression in eq. (
31
) can be obtained from quantum GHD
Dubail and Scopa
;
Ruggiero et al. 2021
. It is easy to see that
\Delta\Phi_{t}(x^{\star}_{i},x^{\star}_{j})
is nothing but the number of particles in the hump between points
s(x^{\star}_{i})
and
s(x^{\star}_{j})
along the time-evolved Fermi surface (cf. Fig.
2
), see Appendix
B
for more details. Eq. (
31

\Delta\Phi_{t}\sim{\cal O}(\Delta N)
,
\Delta N
the total number of particles in the hump, therefore it can be understood as a quantum correction to LDA density according to the classification of Sec.
I
.

III.1.2
Expansion at the shock points

Close to the shock points
x\approx x_{\pm}(t)
, the limit
y\to 0
becomes more complicated. If we approach the shock points from inside the shock region, namely
x=x_{\pm}(t)\mp\epsilon
for
\epsilon\to 0
, two roots in eq. (
27
) coincide and lead to contact singularities in eq. (
31
). On the other hand, if
x\to x_{\pm}\pm\epsilon
then eq. (
27
) is single valued and (
III.1
) reproduces the LDA density.

As noted already in Refs.
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
;
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
;
Doyon et al. 2017
;
Gouraud 2024
, a good characterization of this region requires an expansion of the phase difference in eq. (
III.1
) beyond saddle-point contribution, that is

\Phi(x_{2})-\Phi(x_{1})\approx p_{F}(x^{\star})y+\frac{p_{F}^{\prime\prime}(x^{\star})y^{3}}{24}.

(32)

Using this, one finds universal corrections at the edges of the shock region in the form of a Airy kernel
Doyon et al. 2017
;
Fagotti 2017
;
Bettelheim and Wiegmann 2011
;
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
;
Gouraud 2024

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\rho}(0,x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi{\rm i}}\text{Ai}_{1}\left(\frac{\theta-p_{F}(x^{\star})}{\sqrt[3]{{\rm i}p^{\prime\prime}_{F}(x^{\star})/8}}\right),

(33)

\text{Ai}_{1}(\cdot)
being the incomplete Airy function defined as
\text{Ai}_{1}(x)=\int dq/(2\pi{\rm i})\exp({\rm i}qx+{\rm i}q^{3}/3)/q
. By recasting the expression in terms of Fermi positions at
t=0
, an equivalent expression close to
x_{\pm}(t)
and valid for any time
t
after the shock formation time is found
Bettelheim and Glazman 2012

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\rho}(t,x)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\text{Ai}_{1}\left(\frac{x-x^{\star}}{\sqrt[3]{x^{\prime\prime}_{F}(\theta^{\star})/(8{\rm i})}}\right)

(34)

this expression is single-valued for
x\approx x_{\pm}(t)
. Here,
x_{F}(\theta)
is defined such that
p_{F}(x_{F}(\theta))=\theta
, and
x_{F}(\theta^{\star})\equiv x^{\star}=x-\theta^{\star}t
. By expanding this result for small
\delta x_{\pm}=\mp(x-x_{\pm}(t))
, one obtains the following correction to the LDA density

\displaystyle\langle\delta\hat{\rho}(t,x\approx x_{\pm}(t))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\kappa^{\pm})^{2}\bigg[\delta x_{\pm}[\text{Ai}(\kappa^{\pm}\delta x_{\pm})]^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad-\frac{[\text{Ai}^{\prime}(\kappa^{\pm}\delta x_{\pm})]^{2}}{\kappa^{\pm}}\bigg]

where
\kappa^{\pm}=\left[2{\rm i}/(x_{F}^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{\star}))\right]^{1/3}\sim{\cal O}(\ell^{2/3})
. This result can be combined with the LDA solution (
29
) and with regular terms of (
31
) to correctly determine the behavior of density at the shock points. Finally, we briefly comment on the range of validity of eq. (
35
). Since these corrections are centered around the shock points
x_{\pm}(t)
where the local number of excess particles becomes very small,
\lim_{y\to 0}\Delta\Phi_{t}=0
, they cannot belong to the quantum regime. On the other hand, the total number of particles contained in the density hump
\Delta N
, provides a useful estimate as to the width of the Fermi surf and sets the range of validity to be approximately
|\delta x_{\pm}|\leq\Delta x_{\pm}\sim(\Delta N)^{2/3}/\kappa^{\pm}

Bettelheim and Glazman 2012
, which is indeed finite in the semi-classical regime.

IV
Fluctuating initial state at finite interaction

At finite interactions, a (universal) fermionized description of the quantum fluctuations of the Bose gas is given by Eq. (
4
). For non-homogeneous systems,

\hat{\rho}(x)=\sqrt{K(x)}\hat{\rho}_{F}(x)

(36)

where we denoted
\hat{\rho}_{F}=\sum_{\sigma}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}
and
K(x)
is the LDA value of the Luttinger liquid constant, related to the local compressibility of the gas
Giamarchi 2003
;
Cazalilla et al. 2011
. It is then possible to build a coherent Fermi state as in Sec.
III
but now for the refermionized degrees of freedom, see also Ref.
Protopopov et al. 2014
. We write this state as

|V\rangle=\exp\left({\rm i}\int dx\ \Phi_{\rm int}(x)\hat{\rho}_{F}(x)\right)|0\rangle

(37)

with function
\Phi_{\rm int}(x)
again fixed by the expectation value of density. Using eq. (
36
), we have

\frac{\sqrt{K(x)}}{\pi}\frac{d\Phi_{\rm int}}{dx}=\langle V|\hat{\rho}(x)|V\rangle=\rho_{\rm LDA}(x)

(38)

implying that
d\Phi_{\rm int}/dx=\pi\rho_{\text{LDA}}(x)/\sqrt{K(x)}
and therefore, repeating the previous steps

n_{\rm Fluct}(x,k)=\int dye^{{\rm i}yk}\ \frac{\sin\left(\int_{x-y/2}^{x+y/2}\frac{\pi\rho_{\text{LDA}}(x^{\prime})}{\sqrt{K(x^{\prime})}}dx^{\prime}\right)}{\pi y}.

(39)

This expression is universal, as it depends only on the value of density
\rho_{\text{LDA}}
and on the Luttinger parameter
K
of the one-dimensional gas. Specifying to the Lieb-Liniger model, we can express the phase in eq. (
39
) as
d\Phi_{\text{int}}/dx=p_{F}^{\rm Dr}/\pi
. Here we have introduced the physical momentum, which differs from the momentum
p_{F}
of a noninteracting system due to scattering shift in eq. (
2
). The physical dressing operation (notice that this “Dr” operation is different from “dr” appearing in eq. (
3
)) of a function
h(\theta)
respect to any filling
n
, is written as

\displaystyle h_{[n]}^{\rm Dr}(\theta)=h(\theta)+\int\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}\ \phi(\theta-\alpha)(h^{\prime})^{\rm dr}_{[n]}(\alpha)n(\alpha).

(40)

We denote with
p^{\rm Dr}_{F}(x)\equiv p^{\rm Dr}_{[n]}(\theta_{F}(x))
the dressed Fermi momentum computed at the Fermi point, which is related to the LDA density as

\rho_{\rm LDA}(x)=\int^{\theta_{F}(x)}_{-\theta_{F}(x)}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}1^{\rm dr}_{[n_{\text{LDA}}]}(x,\theta)=\int_{-p^{\rm Dr}_{F}(x)}^{p^{\rm Dr}_{F}(x)}\frac{dp}{2\pi}.

(41)

Using the relation

p^{\rm Dr}_{F}(x)=\theta_{F}^{\rm dr}(x)\sqrt{K(x)},

(42)

with
\theta^{\rm dr}_{F}(x)\equiv\theta_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(x,\theta_{F}(x))
, and dressing in eq. (
3
) performed using
n_{\text{LDA}}(x,\theta)
at each position
x
, we rewrite (
39
) as

n_{\rm Fluct}(x,k)=\int dye^{{\rm i}yk}\ \frac{\sin\left(\int_{x-y/2}^{x+y/2}dx^{\prime}\theta^{\rm dr}_{F}(x^{\prime})\right)}{\pi y}.

(43)

This object carries the physical interpretation of quasi-probability in the same sense of eq. (
13
), but for refermionized degrees of freedom rather than for the physical ones. It becomes clear then that the fluctuating macrostate in (
43
) is related to the macrostate of rapidities
n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta)
via a (nonlinear) reparametrization of the quasimomentum space

n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta)=n_{\rm Fluct}(x,k\equiv p_{[n_{\rm Fluct}]}^{\rm Dr}(\theta,x)/\sqrt{K(x)}).

(44)

This also shows that the density (and all other thermodynamic quantities) can be obtained as

\displaystyle\rho(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{K(x)}\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}n_{\rm Fluct}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}1_{[n_{\rm Fluct}]}^{\rm dr}(x,\theta)\ n_{\rm Fluct}(x,\theta).

(45)

Eq. (
43
) together with the mapping to standard rapidities, eq. (
44
), (or equivalently eq. (
39
) for the non-integrable gas) represent our main result. It specifies the quantum fluctuating initial state for the interacting Bose gas in terms of universal quantities. Its time evolution requires the knowledge of a Boltzmann-like equation in phase space
Panfil et al. 2023
;
Bertini et al. 2015
;
Durnin et al. 2021
;
Lopez-Piqueres and Vasseur 2023
;
Essler et al. 2014
;
Vecchio et al. 2022
;
Bastianello et al. 2021
. For instance, one can consider the kinetic equation for the refermionized quasiparticles (
39
) proposed in Ref.
Protopopov et al. 2014
. For the Lieb-Liniger gas, it can be easily carried out using GHD, as discussed in the following paragraph.

IV.1
Hydrodynamic evolution

In this paragraph, we discuss the evolution of the setting in Fig.
1
for the interacting Lieb-Liniger gas
\gamma=c/\rho_{0}\sim{\cal O}(1)
, with
\rho_{0}
the LDA background density. The latter is evolved using standard GHD equations
Castro-Alvaredo et al. 2016
;
Bertini et al. 2016

\partial_{t}n_{\rm Fluct}(t;x,\theta)+v^{\rm eff}_{[n_{\rm Fluct}]}(\theta)\partial_{x}n_{\rm Fluct}(t;x,\theta)=0,

(46)

with effective velocity
v^{\rm eff}_{[n]}(\theta)=\theta_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)/1_{[n]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)
. Higher-order corrections to eq. (
46
), in particular dispersive terms
\propto\partial_{x}^{3}
, are present, see eq. (
69
). Their expression has been determined in Ref.
De Nardis and Doyon 2023
.
However, we checked that these terms remain small in the setup of Fig.
1
for the times that we investigate, and thus will be neglected in the following. Moreover we remark that for small initial modulation of the density, namely when we can approximate the Luttinger parameter to be space independent
K(x)\simeq K_{0}
, we can also write the evolution directly in the coordinate
k
as

\partial_{t}n(t;k,x)+\sqrt{K_{0}}\ \partial_{k}\theta^{\rm Dr}_{[n_{\rm Fluct}]}\partial_{x}n(t;k,x)=0.

(47)

We recall that applying Euler GHD (
46
) to
n_{\rm LDA}(t;x,\theta)
yields to the so-called zero-entropy (or zero-temperature) GHD
Doyon et al. 2017
, with Fermi points being the only hydrodynamic modes governing the time evolution. Indeed, by applying spatial and time derivatives to the LDA state give Dirac delta functions
\delta(\theta-\theta_{F}^{\zeta}(t,x))
around the Fermi points
\theta_{F}^{\zeta}(t,x)
, allowing to recast
(\partial_{t}+v^{\rm eff}\partial_{x})n_{\rm LDA}=0
as

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=\pm}\zeta\delta(\theta-\theta_{F}^{\zeta})[\partial_{t}\theta_{F}^{\zeta}+v^{\rm eff}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})\partial_{x}\theta_{F}^{\zeta}]=0.

(48)

Here, we assumed that there is a single Fermi sea with boundaries
\theta_{F}^{\pm}(t,x)
, the generalization to multiple Fermi seas proceeds similarly, see Ref.
Doyon et al. 2017
.
However, this is not the case when considering
n_{\rm Fluct}(t;x,\theta)
as initial state, and retaining the full
\theta
-dependence becomes necessary to properly characterize the hydrodynamic evolution.

In order to illustrate this, we focus on the simplified case where the spatial inhomogeneity is large
\ell\sim\partial_{x}\rho_{\text{LDA}}/\rho_{\text{LDA}}\gg 1
, so that only the semi-classical ripples of
n_{\rm Fluct}
need be considered. In this case,
\partial_{x}\theta_{F}\sim{\cal O}(1/\ell)
and thus one can expand the phase difference entering in eq. (
43
) as

\displaystyle\int^{x+y/2}_{x-y/2}{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\approx\theta^{\rm dr}_{F}(x)y+\frac{y^{3}}{24}\partial_{x}^{2}{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}(x).

(49)

This expansion motivates the definition of
\alpha\equiv\partial_{x}^{2}\theta^{\rm dr}_{F}/8
, and can be reinserted into
n_{\rm Fluct}
(
43
) to determine

\displaystyle n_{\rm Fluct}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dy}{\pi y}e^{{\rm i}yk}\sin\left({\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}y+\frac{y^{3}}{24}\partial_{x}^{2}\theta^{\rm dr}_{F}+\ldots\right)

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dq\left[\Theta(k+{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}-q\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}})-\Theta({\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}-k+q\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}})\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{Ai}(q).

Now, it follows that the application of a spatial derivative acts only on
{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}
and
\alpha
so that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Theta(k+{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}-q\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(k+{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}-q\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}})

(51)

\displaystyle\times(\partial_{x}{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}-q\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}}{24\alpha^{\frac{2}{3}}}).

As a result, it follows that, for the case of generic number of Fermi points, labelled by the index
\zeta
, and with signs
s_{\zeta}
, such that Fermi point position is
\theta_{F}^{\zeta}(t,x)
, we obtain

\partial_{x}n(k=\theta^{\rm dr})=\sum_{\zeta}\ s_{\zeta}\frac{\delta\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})}{\delta(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})}\frac{\text{Ai}(\frac{k+\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})}{\alpha^{1/3}})}{\alpha^{1/3}}\partial_{x}\theta_{F}^{\zeta}+{\cal O}(\partial_{x}^{3}),

(52)

and, since
\int dk\alpha^{-1/3}\text{Ai}(k\alpha^{-1/3})=1
, we find

\frac{\text{Ai}(\frac{k+\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})}{\alpha^{1/3}})}{\alpha^{1/3}}=\delta(k+\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta}))+{\cal O}(\ell^{-2/3}).

(53)

Hence, at small
\alpha\sim 1/\ell^{2}
(or equivalently for large spatial variations
\ell\gg 1
), zero-temperature Euler GHD (
48
) is indeed recovered.
However, higher-order corrections coming from the second term in eq. (
51
) generates also
higher-order hydrodynamics terms
, precisely dispersive terms at leading order, correcting the Euler evolution of the Fermi points

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta}\frac{s_{\zeta}}{24\alpha}\text{Ai}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{k+{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta})}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{3}}}\right)\partial_{x}^{3}{\theta}^{\rm dr}_{F}(\theta_{F}^{\zeta}).

(54)

Here, we used the known identity
x\text{Ai}(x)=\text{Ai}^{\prime\prime}(x)
. Importantly, the hydrodynamic coefficient in eq. (
54
) has a finite limit in terms of derivative of Dirac delta for large

, that is

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Ai}^{\prime\prime}(\frac{k+\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\sigma})}{\alpha^{1/3}})}{\alpha}\overset{\ell\gg 1}{\approx}\delta^{\prime\prime}(k+\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}^{\sigma})).

(55)

Remarkably, this shows that the Euler evolution of

contains dispersive hydrodynamics terms even at large

, once expressed in terms of only LDA quantities, such as the Fermi points
\theta^{\zeta}
. It moreover shows that even for large

(or small

, see Sec.

), the phase terms of

cannot be neglected, if one wants to go to times which are beyond Euler scale.

IV.2
Gross-Pitaevskii limit

An important question concerns what happens to
n_{\rm Fluct}
in the limit of weak interaction
c\to 0^{+}
, especially at large density
\rho\sim 1/c
.
In this limit, it is known that the ground state of (
1
) is a quasicondensate
Popov 1977
;
Prolhac 2017
, and it is described by a bosonic mean-field single-particle wavefunction
\Psi(x,t)
playing the role of order parameter for the superfluid regime. Its dynamics is captured by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation (GPE)
Damski 2004
;
Hoefer et al. 2006
;
Meppelink et al. 2009b
;
Peotta and Ventra 2014
;
Bezzaz et al. 2023
;
del Vecchio del Vecchio et al. 2020
;
Koch et al. 2022
, which is purely classical and reads as

{\rm i}\partial_{t}\Psi=\left(-\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+V(x)-\mu+c|\Psi|^{2}\right)\Psi.

(56)

In the homogeneous case
V(x)=0
, GPE is known in the mathematical literature as Non-Linear Schrödinger (NLS) equation. The thermodynamic Bethe ansatz structure of NLS has been considered in detail in Ref.
del Vecchio del Vecchio et al. 2020
.

The limit of the zero temperature condensate in the NLS is particularly tricky. One of the best way to proceed is to take the
c\to 0^{+}
limit of the Lieb-Liniger dressing and express the result in terms of principal value integral
\text{p.v.}\int\equiv\fint
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}T(\theta-\alpha)f_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=f_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)-2c\fint_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}f_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)}{\theta-\alpha},

(57)

so that the NLS dressing operation in the quasi-condensate becomes

f(\theta)=2c\fint_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}f_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)}{\theta-\alpha}.

(58)

From this expression (see Appendix
C
),
f^{\rm dr}(\theta)
can be explicitly determined. In particular, one finds the well known semi-circle distribution for the
1^{\rm dr}(\theta)

1_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)=\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{\theta_{F}^{2}-\theta^{2}}+{\cal O}(c^{-\frac{1}{4}}),

(59)

with Fermi edges
\pm\theta_{F}
given in terms of the density
\rho
as

\rho(x)=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}1_{[n_{\rm LDA}]}^{\rm dr}(\theta)=\frac{(\theta_{F}(x))^{2}}{4c}=\frac{\mu-V(x)}{c}.

(60)

This expression agrees with the ground state density compute in LDA from the GP equation (
56
): in the bulk the gradient term can be put to zero giving (
60
).
As explicitly derived below, the zero coupling limit of
n_{\rm Fluct}
yields the semi-classical ripples only. In this case, one can expand the phase in eq. (
43
) around the Fermi surface as done in Sec.
IV.1
. Our starting point in assessing the zero coupling limit of

is therefore the calculation of

(61)

implying that the zero-temperature effective velocity

lying inside the Fermi points, and

\theta

where, in order to take the limit to the classical NLS evolution, we have defined the rescaled NLS density in the limit

VI
Conclusions and outlooks

In this paper we have provided a unified theory for density ripples and wave breaking effects in the one-dimensional Bose gas, by combining the hydrodynamic evolution and initial quantum fluctuations. We have shown that typical density ripples observed in density waves propagation emerges from quantum fluctuations of the initial state, which can be characterized in terms of effective fermionic excitations around the Fermi seas. By evolving the initial Wigner function for such fermionic degrees of freedom with Euler GHD evolution, we are able to compute the quantum correction to the hydrodynamics, or LDA, mean density.

Our work open numerous different directions. First, our approach based on
n_{\rm Fluct}
can be directly applied and extended to different experimentally relevant settings, such harmonically trapped bosons or domain walls in spin chains. Moreover, it would be desirable to benchmark it with the more standards approaches of quantum GHD and Luttinger liquid, based on bosonized excitations
Ruggiero et al. 2020
. Given the new approach to Luttinger liquid correlations that our work employs, it is reasonable to expect that such comparison will shine some lights in different aspects of the connection between Luttinger liquid and Bose gas, as for example the Luttinger liquids’ prefactors for the static and dynamic correlation functions
Shashi et al. 2012
;
Kitanine et al. 2011
, for which a thermodynamic closed-form expression is still out of reach.
Moreover, a more precise connection with bosonization will be important to understand how to extend our formalism to compute observables beyond one-point functions of local densities, as two-point functions
Ruggiero et al. 2019
;
Scopa et al. 2023
;
Takács et al. 2024
;
Del Vecchio et al. 2023
;
Vecchio and Doyon 2022
and entaglement entropy
Scopa et al. 2021b
;
Collura et al. 2020
.

The inclusion of higher-order derivatives to the Euler GHD evolution of
n_{\rm Fluct}
also represent a challenging topic for the near future, and in particular the strictly classical limit to the NLS equation. Moreover it would be tempting to extend known concept of classical diffusion, to the quantum case. In integrable models indeed diffusion is manifested by classical, finite temperature, fluctuations due to random density waves
Nardis et al. 2019
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018b
;
Medenjak et al. 2020
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024a
. At zero temperature such thermal fluctuations clearly vanish but quantum ones are instead present here inhomogenous cases: while
n_{\rm LDA}
is a zero-entropy distribution (namely taking only
0
and
1
values), this is not the case for
n_{\rm Fluct}
, allowing therefore for diffusive-like fluctuations. How to extend the formalism of GHD to include such quantum diffusive terms and quantum fluctuating terms to access large deviation functions
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024b
;
Krajnik, Žiga and Ilievski, Enej and Prosen,
Tomaž 2022
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024a
, is an exciting quest for the near future.

Appendix C
Dressing relations in the low-coupling limit

This appendix contains some basic results that are used at small coupling in Sec.
IV.2
. We remark that this small coupling limit is different from that of NLS at finite temperature considered in Ref.
del Vecchio del Vecchio et al. 2020
. Here, we are interested to zero temperature and weak coupling, which turns out to be a rather singular limit. An important formula, from which the dressing operation follows, is the so-called Tricomi formula
Tricomi 1985
, as we now explain. Let us consider the principal value integral (or finite Hilbert transform)

\fint_{a}^{b}\frac{f(x^{\prime})}{x-x^{\prime}}dx^{\prime}=g(x).

(114)

The latter can be inverted using the following formula

f(x)=\frac{A-\pi^{-1}\int_{a}^{b}dt\frac{\sqrt{t-a}\sqrt{b-t}}{x-t}g(t)}{\pi\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}},

(115)

with

A=\int_{a}^{b}dx\ f(x).

(116)

However, it is easy to see that this constant is zero for the quantities below. In fact, this solution only gives the leading behaviour in
c
when taking the low coupling limit of the Lieb-Liniger gas. The values at
\theta=\theta_{F}
, instead, require a different treatment. Specifying to the quasicondensate regime, the dressing operation (
58
) for
f(\theta)=1
gives

1=\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\mu}{2\pi}\frac{2c}{\lambda-\mu}\partial_{\mu}1^{\rm dr}(\mu),

(117)

which is solved by

\partial_{\theta}1^{\rm dr}(\theta)=\frac{-\theta}{c\sqrt{\theta_{F}^{2}-\theta^{2}}}.

(118)

Once integrated, the last equation gives the semi-circle expression (
59
). Recalling that the root density is
2\pi\rho(\theta)=1^{\rm dr}(\theta)
, we can then access all thermodynamic quantities in homogeneous states starting from (
59
). For non-homogeneous states, one can use LDA and replace
\theta_{F}\to\theta_{F}(x)
. The effective velocity is obtained using
\varepsilon(\theta)=\theta^{2}/2
,

\theta=\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\mu}{2\pi}\frac{2c}{\theta-\mu}\partial_{\mu}[\theta^{\rm dr}(\mu)],

(119)

which we invert to get

\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta)=\frac{\theta\sqrt{\theta^{2}_{F}-\theta^{2}}}{2c}=v^{\rm eff}(\theta)1^{\rm dr}(\theta).

(120)

This equation can be solved for effective velocity. Furthermore, it also reproduces the static pressure
\mathcal{P}
of a quasicondensate

\mathcal{P}=\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}d\theta\rho(\theta)v^{\rm eff}(\theta)\theta=\frac{\theta_{F}^{4}}{16c}=c\rho^{2}.

(121)

Appendix D
Proof of a useful identity

In this appendix, we prove the useful identity

\pi\rho_{\rm LDA}=p^{\rm Dr}_{F}=\theta^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})\sqrt{K},

(122)

where here and in the following we drop the subscript
[n]
since the relation is independent of the filling
n
with respect to which the dressing is done.
To show this, we consider the well-known form of LDA density, and we perform integration by parts

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm LDA}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)=\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}(\partial_{\theta}\theta){1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)

(123)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{F}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})}{\pi}-\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\theta\partial_{\theta}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta).

Now an identity for derivatives of dressed functions
\partial_{\theta}{f}^{\rm dr}(\theta)
is inserted, which quickly simplifies the expression

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{LDA}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{F}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})}{\pi}+\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\theta({T}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\theta_{F}){1}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}))

\displaystyle-\int_{-\theta_{F}}^{\theta_{F}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\theta\left({T}^{\rm dr}(\theta,-\theta_{F}){1}^{\rm dr}(-\theta_{F})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}({\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F}){1}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})-{\theta}^{\rm dr}(-\theta_{F}){1}^{\rm dr}(-\theta_{F})),

having identified
{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\pm\theta_{F})
with its definition. By recalling
{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})=-{\theta}^{\rm dr}(-\theta_{F})
and
{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta_{F})={1}^{\rm dr}(-\theta_{F})\equiv\sqrt{K}
, the identity in eq. (
122
) follows.

Appendix E

In this appendix, we determine the saddle point corrections to LDA density, corresponding to the quantum fluctuations, in the Gross-Pitaevskii limit discussed in Sec.

. In doing so, we shall approximate the Luttinger parameter with its background value
K(x)\simeq K_{0}

, and thus it does not change our conclusion below.

The calculation proceeds similarly to the Tonks-Girardeau case (see Sec.
III

B

19

with

\sim{\cal O}(1)
