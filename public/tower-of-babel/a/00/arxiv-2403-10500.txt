A lozenge triangulation of the plane with integers Thanks: * Corresponding author: Cristian Cobeli: cristian.cobeli@gmail.com Thanks: Key words and phrases: dynamical lozenge tiling with integers, Löschian numbers, rhombus number tiling, modular prime covering, lattice path
Raghavendra N. Bhat, Cristian Cobeli *, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. We introduce and study a three-folded linear operator depending on three parameters that has associated a triangular number tilling of the plane. As a result the set of all triples of integers is decomposed in classes of equivalence organized in four towers of two-dimensional triangulations. We provide the full characterization of the represented integers belonging to each network as families of certain quadratic forms. We note that one of the towers is generated by a germ that produces a covering of the plane with Löschian numbers.

Introduction

The study of discrete dynamical systems has gained attention because of
their capacity to model complex phenomena through the iterative
application of simple rules.
Understanding the common features and applications of these systems can provide valuable insights into the broader field and their practical implications.

A few examples that have been discussed again in recent times are
related to various aspects of the Ducci game rule (see
[
14
,
17
,
15
]
),
the study of phenomena occurring in Pascal-like triangles (see
[
31
,
16
]
) and to the contrasting patterns
produced by the iteration of the PG
[
4
,
6
]
operator that calculates the gaps between
the neighbor elements of a sequence in relation to the Proth-Gilbreath
Conjecture
[
30
,
24
,
23
,
20
]
.

In this context, we introduce here the following three-folded linear operator that has nonlinear characteristics.
Let
H=H(x,y,x)
be the set
H=\{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}\}
of transformations
that each leaves two components unchanged while to the negative third adds
1
and the other two, that is,

\begin{split}H^{\prime}(x,y,z)&=(-x+1+y+z,y,z),\\
H^{\prime\prime}(x,y,z)&=(x,-y+1+z+x,z),\\
H^{\prime\prime\prime}(x,y,z)&=(x,y,-z+1+x+y).\end{split}

(1)

As we will show, the repeated application of any combination of these operators
produce a set of interconnected integers with remarkable properties.
A special instance of these are the Löschian numbers.
Introduced in an economic model
[
26
, Chapter 10]
,
in which the producers and consumers are organized in a
convenient hexagonal network, Löschian numbers appear also in the more
general geographical Central Place theory
[
3
,
27
,
2
]
.
Their special properties make them useful in other
theoretical
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
19
,
25
]
or
practical contexts
(see
[
29
, Chapter 3]
,
[
1
,
29
,
32
]

and the references mentioned there).

We denote by
H^{[n]}
,
n\geq 0
, the iterations of
H
, which are the successive
compositions of
H
, where at any step any of
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}
is applied.
Thus, if
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, then

H^{[n]}(a,b,c)
contains
3^{n}
triples of integers, not necessarily distinct.
For example,
H^{[0]}(1,2,3)=\{(1,2,3)\}
and

(5,9,5)
is one of the
243
elements of
H^{[5]}(1,2,3)
, because

\begin{split}(1,2,3)&\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime}$ }}(5,2,3)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(5,2,5)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime}$ }}(5,9,5)\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime}$ }}(10,9,5)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime}$ }}(5,9,5).\end{split}

(2)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
denote the union of all these triples, that is,

\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c):=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}H^{[n]}(a,b,c)\,.

(3)

We note that if the union were disjoint, then it would be possible for a triple to repeat, because for example if the sum of two components, let’s say
a
and
b
, is odd and the other is
c=\frac{a+b+1}{2}
, then

H^{\prime\prime\prime}(a,b,c)=(a,b,c)
. Other type of repetitions also occur, all due
to the symmetries created by all possible orders in which operators (
1
) are applied.

We say that an integer is
represented
by
H^{[n]}(a,b,c)
if it appears as
a component of a triple in
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
.
Since a represented integer can appear multiple times in the triples from
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
,
we call the
length
of
m
as
the smallest possible number of compositions required to go from
(a,b,c)
to
a triple that has
m
as a component.
Thus, following the sequence in (
2
), we see that
1,2
and
3

have length
0
,
then
5
has length
1
, and it can be checked that
9
has length
3
, as there is no shorter
path to obtain it. However, the length of
10
is not
4
, but also
3
, since
10
is represented
in this shorter branch:

(5,2,3)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime}$ }}(5,7,3)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(5,7,10)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)
denote the set of all integers represented by
H(a,b,c)
, that is,

\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c):=\big\{m\in\{x,y,z\}:(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)\big\}\,.

Given a triple
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, from the way definitions (
1
) are introduced, one should not expect that all or at least most integers are represented in

\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)
.
And the reason is not the fact that two variables are added and only one subtracted in formulas (
1
),
and therefore depending on the signs of
a,b,c
very small or very large numbers would not be represented.
The real main factor is actually the addition of
1
in formulas (
1
),
which always makes that only very few small numbers are represented.
The following theorem proves this fact for numbers less than any given threshold.

Theorem 1
.

Let
M
be a fixed integer. Than,

\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)\cap(-\infty,M]
is finite for any
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let us note that the sets of represented numbers by
H
are actually closely related to one another
by the equality

\mathcal{R}_{H}(a+h,b+h,c+h)=\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)+h

(4)

for any
a,b,c,h\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Indeed, this follows since if the triple
(A,B,C)
is obtained from
(a,b,c)
through the
sequence of operations
H^{[n]}
, then through exactly the same sequence of operations we obtain (see Lemma
2.1
)

(a+h,b+h,c+h)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{[n]}$ }}(A+h,B+h,C+h)\,.

In Section
2
we show that essentially there are only two distinct
sets of representatives, namely

\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,0,0)
and
\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)
,
and all the others are obtained by translations (
4
).
A combined graphical representation of the
fundamental sets

\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,0,0)
and
\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)

and the set of triples

\mathcal{T}_{H}(0,0,0)
and
\mathcal{T}_{H}(0,1,1)

is shown in Figure

.

These tessellations of the plain with integers have
the particular characteristic that in any basic adjacent triangles that together form a
lozenge
(diamond consisting of any four close circles positioned in such a way that each circle is adjacent to at least two of the remaining three)
the sum of the numbers in the nodes on the longer diagonal
is with
1
larger than the sum of the numbers on the shorter diagonal.

The geometrical representation of the core of

and

Lozenge tilings of the plane or of some distinguished
domains with special characteristics have been intensively studied
[

,

,
8

]
, in part due to the connection with practical applications of a random tiling

While the main focus in these mentioned works is the evaluation of the number of distinct tilings,
our objectives include characterizing the weights in the nodes of a network generated by a certain
triple, classifying the networks of numbers and highlighting certain unique paths that connect the network nodes.

Once we observe that any integer
a
is represented in
\mathcal{R}_{H}(*,*,*)
,
with
a
being one of the stars, the natural problem is to find
an efficient algorithm to decide whether or not a positive
a
belongs to

\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,0,0)
,
\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)
,
\mathcal{R}_{H}(1,0,1)
or
\mathcal{R}_{H}(1,1,0)
.
For example, one finds that
2024\not\in\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)
,
but
2023\in\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)

(see the graphical representation in Figure
9
). For
2023
, there are infinitely many
nontrivial paths to get from
(0,1,1)
to a triple that represents

2023
, but one of the shortest takes
99
steps.
Such a path passes by the intermediate triple
(1089,1156,1123)
through
the sequence of operations:

(0,1,1)\xrightarrow{\text{ $\big(H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\big)^{[22]}$ }}(1089,1151,1123),

and then, changing the direction, reaches the target through:

(0,1,1)\xrightarrow{\text{ $\big(H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\big)^{[11]}$ }\circ\text{ $\big(H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\big)^{[22]}$ }}(1956,1939,2023).

Searching for common patterns among the sets of triples
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
, one
can sometimes notice that starting from two different triples

(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
and
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
,
and performing an identical sequence of operations, it can happen that one of the components of the resulting triples
ends up being the same.
For example,
G=H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}
transforms

(1,3,6)
and
(6,7,9)
into two triples that have the third component
equal to
17
:

\begin{split}(1,3,6)&\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime}$ }}(9,3,6)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(9,3,7)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime}$ }}(9,14,7)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(9,14,17)\\
(6,7,9)&\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime}$ }}(11,7,9)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(11,7,10)\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime}$ }}(11,15,10)\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{ $H^{\prime\prime\prime}$ }}(11,15,17)\,.\end{split}

(5)

Continuing from this point, the search reveals that even more triples
obtained in a similar way satisfy the same property.
Thus, in the example above, one even finds an infinite sequence of such
triples obtained through a periodic series of transformations.
Indeed, if
F=H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}
, then the third component of
the sequences

\{F^{(k)}(9,14,17)\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{F^{(k)}(11,15,17)\}_{k\geq 0}

are equal, and the sequence of these equal components starts with

17,19,27,41,

61,

87,119,157,201,251,307,\dots
,
the general formula for the general term being

\{3k^{2}-k+17\}_{k\geq 0}
.
In the following remark we note that this observation is universally valid.

Remark 1.1
.

Let
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
and
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{2})
be triples of integers.
Suppose
G
is a composition of a finite sequence of operators from
\{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}\}
and

G(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})=(A,*,*)
and
G(a_{2},b_{2},c_{2})=(A,*,*)
,
where
A\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the
*
’s may represent any integer.
Then, there exists an infinite sequence of distinct integers
\{A_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

and a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, where each
G_{n}
is a sequence of compositions of

H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}
, such that

G_{n}(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})=(A_{n},*,*)=G_{n}(a_{2},b_{2},c_{2})\ \ \text{ for $n\geq 0$.}

We will revisit this remark in Section
5.2
after we will
obtain the characterization of the set of triples
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
.

The next result shows that the represented numbers in both
fundamental tessellations

The graphical representations

.

Let

be prime and let

denote the limit density of
the residue class

of the represented integers in

, for
u=0,1,\dots,p-1
.
Then:

(1) If the germ is
(0,0,0)
, then the values of the density are:

\circ

If
p=2
, then
d_{2}(0)=3/4
and
d_{2}(1)=1/4
.

\circ

If
p=3
, then
d_{3}(0)=3/9
and
d_{3}(1)=3/9
and
d_{3}(2)=3/9
.

\circ

If

, then

and
d_{p}(u)=(p-1)/p^{2}

\circ

and
d_{p}(u)=(p+1)/p^{2}
for
u=0,\dots,p-1
and
\neq(2p-1)/3
.

(2) If the germ is
(0,1,1)
, then the values of the density are:

\circ

If
p=2
, then
d_{2}(0)=1/4
and
d_{2}(1)=3/4
.

\circ

If
p=3
, then
d_{3}(0)=3/9
and
d_{3}(1)=6/9
and
d_{3}(2)=0
.

\circ

If
p\geq 5
and
p\equiv 1\pmod{6}
, then
d_{p}(0)=(2p-1)/p^{2}
and
d_{p}(u)=(p-1)/p^{2}
for
u=1,\dots,p-1
.

\circ

If
p\geq 5
and

The work is structured as follows.
In Section

Next, in Section

Continuing on, in Section

2.
Properties of
H

Lemma 2.1
.

For any integer
a,b,c,h
we have:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{H}(a+h,b+h,c+h)&=\mathcal{T}(a,b,c)+h\,,\\
\mathcal{R}_{H}(a+h,b+h,c+h)&=\mathcal{R}(a,b,c)+h\,.\end{split}

(6)

Proof.

We use the usual notations for the translations of a tuple and of a set,
by which
(a,b,c)+h:=(a+h,b+h,c+h)
, and
\mathcal{S}+h
represents the set obtained
by adding
h
to each element in the set
\mathcal{S}
.

The equality of the sets of represented integers in (
6
)
follows from the equality of the sets of triples above, which in turn is implied
by the definitions of the three analogous relations for
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime\prime}
, of which,
for exemplification, the first one is

\begin{split}H^{\prime}(a+h,b+h,c+h)&=\big(-(a+h)+1+(b+h)+(c+h),b+h,c+h\big)\\
&=(-a+1+b+c,b,c)+h\\
&=H^{\prime}(a,b,c)+h\,.\end{split}

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proposition 2.1
.

We have:

(1)

The operators
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}
are involutions.

(2)

Any two distinct operators
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}
do not commute.

(3)

H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}=H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}
.

(4)

\big(H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\big)^{[3]}=Id
.

Proof.

It is enough to check that
H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime}=Id
and

H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\neq H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}
, as for the other operators
the analoguos relations follow by a rotation of the variables.
Also, let us note that the other permutations of the components
as in part (3) or part (4) can be obtained in the same way through the corresponding composition
of the analogous permuted operators.

Let
x,y,z
be fixed. (1) For the fist part, we have:

\begin{split}H^{\prime}\big(H^{\prime}(x,y,z)\big)&=H^{\prime}(-x+1+y+z,y,z)\\
&=\big(-(-x+1+y+z)+1+y+z,y,z\big)\\
&=(x,y,z).\end{split}

(2) The necommutativity follows since

\begin{split}H^{\prime}\big(H^{\prime\prime}(x,y,z)\big)&=H^{\prime}(x,-y+1+z+x,z)\\
&=\big(-x+1+(-y+1+z+x)+z,-y+1+z+x,z\big)\\
&=(2-y+2z,1-y+z+x,z),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}H^{\prime\prime}\big(H^{\prime}(x,y,z)\big)&=H^{\prime\prime}(-x+1+y+z,y,z)\\
&=\big(-x+1+y+z,-y+1+z+(-x+1+y+z),z\big)\\
&=(1-x+y+z,2-x+2z,z),\end{split}

so that
\big(H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\big)(x,y,z)=\big(H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\big)(x,y,z)
if and only if

y=x
and
z=x-1
.

(4) As before, we have

\begin{split}H^{\prime\prime}\big(H^{\prime\prime\prime}(x,y,z)\big)&=(x,2+2x-z,1+x+y-z),\end{split}

which, applied twice, implies

\begin{split}\big(H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\big)^{[2]}(x,y,z)&=\big(x,2+2x-(1+x+y-z),\\
&\phantom{(x,xX}1+x+(2+2x-z)-(1+x+y-z)\big)\\
&=(x,1+x-y+z,2+2x-y),\end{split}

and finally, for the third time,

\begin{split}\big(H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\big)^{[3]}(x,y,z)&=\big(x,2+2x-(2+2x-y),\\
&\phantom{(x,xX}1+x+(1+x-y+z)-(2+2x-y)\big)\\
&=(x,y,z).\end{split}

The same calculation with components and operators interchanged
proves formula (4), and also the fact that

\big(H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\big)^{[3]}=\big(H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\big)^{[3]}=Id
.

(3)
By part (4) we know that
H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}=Id
,
from which, composing on the right with
H^{\prime\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime\prime}
,
in that order, and using (1),
we obtain
H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}=H^{\prime\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}
.
These completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, there exists
h\in\mathbb{Z}
such that either

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)&=\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,0,0)+h\quad\text{ or }\\
\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)&=\mathcal{R}_{H}(0,1,1)+h=\mathcal{R}_{H}(1,0,1)+h=\mathcal{R}_{H}(1,1,0)+h.\end{split}

Proof.

From Theorem
1
we know that the set
\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)
has a first element.
Then, let

m=\min\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)
,
and let
(m,u,v)\in\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
be a triple in which
m
is represented.
We do not know in advance on which position
m
appears in the triple,
but the other two situations in which
m
would be in the second or
the third position are treated the same.

Then there are two possibilities:
m\leq v\leq u
or
m\leq u\leq v
.
Again, it is enough to discuss only one of the cases, the other being treated similarly by rotating the variables and the corresponding operators involved.
Moreover, we will see that the cases blend together, because we will find that in fact
u=v
.

Thus, without restricting the generality, we may also suppose that
m\leq u\leq v
.
Since
(m,u,v)\in\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
, it follows that
the third component of
H^{\prime\prime\prime}(m,u,v)
, which equals

-v+1+m+u
belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)
.
Then, since
m
is the minimum,
m\leq-v+1+m+u
,
which implies
v\leq u+1
. Therefore, either
v=u
or
v=u+1
.

Case
v=u+1
.

Since
(m,u,u+1)\in\mathcal{T}(a,b,c)
it follows that

H^{\prime\prime\prime}(m,u,u+1)

=(m,u,u)
is also in
\mathcal{T}(a,b,c)
, so that it is
enough to consider the case
u=v
.

Case
v=u
.

Starting from
(m,u,u)
, the following neighbor triples are also in

3.
The lozenge representation of the operators
H

Given three integers
a,b,c

the
base triangle
. Then, by fixing any two of the numbers

1
1

1

generated by the base

By applying this procedure to every pair of two numbers from

Continuing in the same way, we proceed with the newly obtained equilateral triangles,

Remark 3.1
.

In essence, the geometric interpretation of relations (

is a
2

which has the property that in any lozenge of it,
the sum of the numbers on the long diagonal is always

more than the sum of the numbers on the short diagonal.

It is fundamental, and in Section

we will ascertain, that this construction is indeed consistent,
meaning that any triangle, which is obtained in exactly three ways as the intersection of three lozenges is uniquely and unequivocally determined.
One can check the outlined procedure starting with any triple of numbers from the adjacent circles in
Figures

and

With this geometric representation it becomes clear that for any two triangles or triples of
represented integers,

connects one triple to the other.
In order to mark the association, on the set of all triples
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
,
let us define a relation according to which two triples are

This is the same as saying that the triples in any set
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
defined
by (

The equivalence classes of this relation are these parallel triangular tilings,
each of them being generated by any base triangle it contains.

It should be noted that formally, any triangle of adjacent numbers obtained by iterating the operators
from
\{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}\}

The classification of triples in the equivalence classes

\left(\frac{-3}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\cdot(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{3-1}{2}}\left(\frac{p}{3}\right)=\left(\frac{p}{3}\right).

This conclude the proof of Theorem
2
.

5.
The number tiling of the plane and the proof of Theorem
1

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1

To do so, we will begin by offering a more detailed account of the tiling coverage of the plane with integers,
which will lead to a parameterization of all nodes within the triangular network
\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)

5.1.
A parametrization of the represented integers

Let
a,b,c
be fixed integers.
In order to determine which elements belong to the sequence of integers represented

\mathcal{R}_{H}(a,b,c)

\mathcal{T}_{H}(a,b,c)
.
In this set, although the components of the triples appear multiple times in different
triples, one can track the step-by-step generation of triples starting from
the basic germ
(a,b,c)

We start building an interlinked network of numbers based at the nodes of a
2
-dimensional
triangular network by placing
a,b,c
in the nodes of one of
the smallest equilateral triangles of the grid. This is the the base triangle
that stores the germ
(a,b,c)
. We will identify and then refer to the nodes by the
integers they contain.
The integers in the nodes will be called
weights.

Then, we place the weight

d=H^{\prime}(a,b,c)=-a+b+c+1
in the fourth corner of the lozenge
abdc
with the shorter
diagonal
bc

The process of expanding towards a future node from a previous one, by placing the child-integer
symmetrically across a side of a triangle, as shown in Figure

across any side of the triangles in the current generation.
The expansion can be done in any order using either of
the corresponding
operators
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime\prime\prime}

to produce a certain linear development in a given direction,

We need to ensure that this procedure can be repeated indefinitely to fill
all the nodes in the triangular network covering the plane.
The construction must be consistent, meaning that the same weights are obtained
regardless of the path taken to reach the nodes.
To verify this, we will check the correctness on the minimal paths around a node
and use the fact that
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime\prime}
are involutions (as we know from Proposition

This will guarantee a unique and consistent filling of the nodes of the entire triangular tiling,
as we will then have established step by step the uniqueness
of the weights on any path between two fixed nodes.

In counterclockwise direction, the weights in Figure

\begin{split}d&=H^{\prime}(a,b,c)=-a+b+c+1\\

The weights of the nodes on the straight line determined by

5.2.
Two-dimensional parametrization of the weights

Let
\varrho=1/2+\sqrt{3}i/2

be a root of order
6
of
1

Any node of the triangular lattice can then be uniquely expressed using the base
\{1,\varrho\}

as the linear combination
m+n\varrho

the elements of the set

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}:=\{m+n\varrho:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\,.\end{split}

Let
G(a,b,c\mid m,n)
denote the value of the weight associated to the node
m+n\varrho

in the triangular network generated by the base triangle of weights

,

To compare, it should be noted that

L(a,b\mid m)=G(a,b,c\mid m,0)\ \ \text{ for any $a,b,c,m\in\mathbb{Z}$.}

The next theorem provides the closed-form expression of
G(a,b,c\mid m,n)

Theorem 4
.

For any base triple
(a,b,c)
and any integers

G(a,b,c\mid m,n)=-(m+n-1)a+mb+nc+(m^{2}+n^{2}+mn-m-n)\,.

Proof.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
and let

While the linear expansion in Lemma
5.1

c
just as a support to
generate the weights on the line

on the line that starts at
c
and is adjacent to

Then, iterating the process, the general formula will follow.

5.3.1.
Evaluation of the number of negative weights

Let us find the path from
(0,0,c)
, where
c\geq 0
, to the minimum triple in the covering that contains
(0,0,c)
.
Along the way we will meet three remarkable sequences and also find the tower to which
(0,0,c)
belongs to.

Let
H
be the composition operator defined by
H:=H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime}\circ H^{\prime}\circ H^{\prime\prime\prime}
.
A direct calculation shows that, for any triple
(a,b,c)
with
a=b
, we have

H(a,a,c)=\big(3a-2c+4,3a-2c+4,2a-c+1\big)\,.

This is a zigzag operator whose iterations define the shortest path in the triangular network
from
(0,0,c)
towards the center. (The
center
is the triple in the network
whose components are the smallest and closest to each other.)
Iterating the application of
H
, by induction we find that

\begin{split}H^{[n]}(a,a,c)=\big(&(2n+1)a-2nc+n(3n+1),\\
&(2n+1)a-2nc+n(3n+1),\ \\
&2na-(2n-1)c+n(3n-2)\big)\end{split}

(23)

for
n\geq 1
.

Remark 5.2
.

Formula(
23

a=c=0
.

The first one is
4,14,30
,
52,80
,
114
,
154
,
200
,
252
,

310,\dots
and
the formula for the
n
-th element is
\mathtt{NE}(n):=n(3n+1)
for
n\geq 1
.
It is the so-called sequence of the

twice second pentagonal numbers

[
28

A049451
]
.

The second sequence is that of
octagonal
or the

[

,

and the formula for the

\mathtt{SW}(n):=n(3n-2)

We note that creating a spiral pattern by arranging the natural numbers,
starting from the center and moving towards the bottom left, and then

Case

Starting with

Remark 5.3
.

Based on the analysis above, and in accordance with Theorem
1
,
it is possible to make an accurate estimation and even calculate algorithmically
the number of nodes with negative weights.
Thus, since the considered triples
(0,0,c)
, with
c\geq 0
,
are located in the triangular network they generate on the boundary
that separates negative weights from positive ones,
and since the minimum triple is in the center,
it follows that the number of negative weights is
asymptotically equal to

\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}c^{2}

as
c\to\infty
,
at any level, in both towers that contain the considered triples.

In order to give a sense of scale, we mention that the
minimum weight in a node of the triangular network that contains

(0,0,c)
, with
c=100
, is
-3300
,
and there are precisely
11946
negative weights in the nodes.
Then, the ratio between the number of negative weights
and the just mentioned approximation
is, in this case, only

11946\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2\pi c^{2}}\approx 0.98793

instead of the limit
1
, which will be attained as
c
tends to infinity.

Conclusion

We have introduced a three folded operator (
1
), which, starting with a
triple of integers and moving back and forth from any point in any direction,
generates a triangular tessellation of the plane
with integers in the nodes, called weights. This operator further leads to a natural relation that defines
as equivalent tessellations obtained from each other through a translation.
As a consequence, we obtain exactly four classes of equivalence of
tessellations organized in four towers, generated in their very beginnings by

(0,0,0)
, on one hand, and the more closely related
the other three built around
(0,1,1)
,
(1,0,1)

and
(1,1,0)
, on the other hand.
We have obtained a characterization of the weights
(see Theorems
1
,
3
,
4
) and the densities of their distribution in residue classes modulo a prime number (Theorem
2
),
and along the way, we have encountered remarkable sequences that reveal intricate patterns concealed within the networks.
This has the potential to offer a fresh perspective or open up an alternative approach
to the practical applications in which they appear, including the geographical model known as the
Central Place Theory
[
3
,
27
]
, recently enhanced with a fractal insertion
[
1
,
2
]
,
in a molecule or a virus model
[
29
, Chapter 3]
,
at the intersection between arts and mathematics
[
32
]
,
or in the modern communication systems (see
[
25
]
and the references therein).
Additionally, it could be worthwhile to continue studying the potential implications
of the tesselations with integers studied above in relation to the special lozenge tilings
[
11
,
9
]

and their implications in two dimensional electrostatics.
