Random Tensor Inequalities and Tail bounds for Bivariate Random Tensor Means, Part I
Shih Yu Chang

Abstract
In this work, we apply the concept about operator connection to consider bivariate random tensor means. We first extend classical Markov’s and Chebyshev’s inequalities from a random variable to a random tensor by establishing Markov’s inequality for tensors and Chebyshev’s inequality for tensors. These inequalities are applied to establish tail bounds for bivariate random tensor means represented by operator perspectives based on various types of connection functions: tensor increasing functions, tensor decreasing functions, and tensor concavity functions. We also consider tail bounds relations for the summation and product of eigenvalues based on majorization ordering of eigenvalues of bivariate random tensor means. This is Part I of our work about random tensor inequalities and tail bounds for bivariate random tensor mean. In our Part II, we will consider bivariate random tensor mean with respect to non-invertible random tensors and their applications.



Index terms—
Markov’s inequality, Chebyshev’s inequality, random tensors, bivariate tensor mean, Löwner ordering,
majorization ordering.

Introduction

Random tensors have both theoretical and practical applications in various fields such as machine learning, physics, and computer science. In machine learning, random tensors are commonly used for weight initialization in neural networks. A neural network is a collection of interconnected nodes (neurons) that take input data, perform computations, and produce an output. The weights of these connections between neurons are typically initialized with random values before training. This helps to break the symmetry of the network and ensure that each neuron learns different features of the data

1

. In physics, random tensors are used in the study of quantum entanglement and the geometry of entangled states. In particular, random tensor networks have been used to simulate quantum systems and understand the properties of entangled states. These studies have applications in quantum computing, quantum field theory, and condensed matter physics
[
2
]
. In computer science, random tensors are used in the design and analysis of algorithms. For example, randomized matrix algorithms use random tensors to efficiently compute matrix decompositions, which have applications in data analysis, signal processing, and machine learning. Randomized algorithms are also used in graph theory to solve problems such as graph partitioning and clustering
[
3
]
. Overall, the theory and application of random tensors have a wide range of practical uses across different fields, from improving the performance of machine learning models to advancing our understanding of quantum physics and optimizing algorithms

2
Tensor Basics

In this section, we will review necessary basic facts about tensors. Given
\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{M}\times J_{1}\times\dots\times J_{N}}
and
\mathcal{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(y_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{M}\times J_{1}\times\dots\times J_{N}}
, the
Einstein product
of
\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{M}\times K_{1}\times\dots\times K_{L}}
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{Y})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},k_{1},\dots,k_{L}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}y_{j_{1},\dots,j_{N},k_{1},\dots,k_{L}}.

(2)

Definition 1

A tensor whose entries are all zero is called a
zero tensor
, denoted by
\mathcal{O}
.

Definition 2

An
identity tensor

\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
is defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I})_{i_{1}\times\dots\times i_{N}\times j_{1}\times\dots\times j_{N}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{i_{k},j_{k}},

(3)

where
\delta_{i_{k},j_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}1
if
i_{k}=j_{k}
; otherwise
\delta_{i_{k},j_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}0
.

In order to define
Hermitian
tensor
[
16
]
, the
conjugate transpose operation
(or
Hermitian adjoint
) of a tensor is specified as follows.

Definition 3

Given a tensor
\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{M}\times J_{1}\times\dots\times J_{N}}
, its conjugate transpose, denoted by

\mathcal{X}^{H}
, is defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{H}})_{j_{1},\dots,j_{N},i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}x^{*}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}},

(4)

where the star “
*
” symbol indicates the complex conjugate of the complex number
x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}
. If a tensor
\mathcal{X}
satisfies
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{H}}=\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathcal{X}
is a
Hermitian tensor
.

Following definition is about untiary tensors
[
16
]
.

Definition 4

Given a tensor
\mathcal{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(u_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N},i_{1},\dots,i_{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
, if

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{H}}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{H}}=\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}},

(5)

then
\mathcal{U}
is a
unitary tensor
.

Definition 5

Given a
square tensor

\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
, if there exists
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}\star_{N}\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{I},

(6)

then
\mathcal{Y}
is the
inverse
of
\mathcal{X}
. We usually write
\mathcal{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{X}^{-1}
thereby.

We also list other crucial tensor operations here. The
trace
of a square tensor is equivalent to the summation of all diagonal entries such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{1\leq i_{j}\leq I_{j},\hskip 1.42271ptj\in[N]}\mathcal{X}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N},i_{1},\dots,i_{N}},

(7)

where
[N]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
. The
inner product
of two tensors
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times J_{1}\times\dots\times J_{N}}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{X}^{H}\star_{M}\mathcal{Y}\right).

(8)

As the matrix eigen-decomposition theorem is crucial in various linear algebra theory and applications, we will have a parallel decomposition theorem for Hermitian tensors. From Theorem 5.2 in
[
16
]
, every Hermitian tensor
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
has the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{U}_{i}\star_{1}\mathcal{U}^{H}_{i},\mbox{
\penalty\ with\penalty\ \penalty\ $\langle\mathcal{U}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{i}\rangle=1$ and $\langle\mathcal{U}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{j}\rangle=0$ for $i\neq j$,}

(9)

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}\times 1}
. Here tensors
\mathcal{U}_{i}
are orthogonal tensors each other since
\langle\mathcal{U}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{j}\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
. The values
\lambda_{i}
are named as
Hermitian eigenvalues
, and the minimum integer of
r
to decompose a Hermitian tensor as in Eq. (
9
) is called
Hermitian tensor rank
. In this work, we assume that all Hermitian tensors discussed in this work are full rank, i.e.,
r=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{N}I_{j}
. A
positive definite
(PD) tensor is a Hermitian tensor with all
Hermitian eigenvalues
are positive. A
semipositive definite
(SPD) tensor is a Hermitian tensor of which all
Hermitian eigenvalues
are nonnegative.

We use
\preceq
to repsent Löwner ordering between two tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
, which indicates that
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
is a SPD tensor. On the other hand, we use
\succeq
to repsent Löwner ordering between two tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as
\mathcal{A}\succeq\mathcal{B}
, which indicates that
\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}
is a SPD tensor.

3
Tensor Markov’s/Chebyshev’s Inequalities

Given two Hermitian tensors
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
, we use
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
indicates that the tensor
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
is SPD. Such ordering relation among Hermitian tensors can be considered as Loewner ordering. On the other hand, we use
\mathcal{A}\npreceq\mathcal{B}
to represent that
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
is
not
SPD. This means that
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
must contain some negative eigenvalues.

We will present the following theorem: Markov’s inequality for tensors.

Theorem 1
(Markov’s Inequality for Tensors)

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be a PD deterministic tensor, and let
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be a random Hermitian tensor such that
\mathcal{X}\succeq\mathcal{O}
almost surely. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}\right)\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}]\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right).

(10)

Proof:

We have to show the following inequality first.

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}}\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\right),

(11)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}}
is the indicator function with respect to the condition
\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}
. For the case that
\mathcal{X}\preceq\mathcal{A}
, this inequality provided by Eq. (
11
) is valid since
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}}
is equal to zero and the tensor
\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}
is a PD tensor.

For the case that
\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}
, we know that at least one tensor
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times 1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{X}\right)\mathcal{U}<0.

(12)

Because the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is an invertible tensor, we can transform the tensor
\mathcal{U}
to the tensor
\mathcal{V}
by setting
\mathcal{V}
=
\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}
and obtain the following inequality from Eq. (
12
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}<0;

(13)

and this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}}>1.

(14)

By the Rayleigh quotient form of the maximum eigenvalue of a Hermitian tensor, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathcal{W}\neq\mathcal{O}}\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{W}}{\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{W}}

(15)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{T}}\star_{N}\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 1

where
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\right)
represents the largest eigenvalue for the tensor
\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}
. Because
\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}
is a PD tensor, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\right)\geq\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star\mathcal{X}\star\mathcal{A}^{1/2}\right)>1.

(16)

This proves Eq. (
11
).

By taking the expectation for both sides of Eq. (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}}

(17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{X}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}\right]\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}\right]\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right),

where
=_{1}
holds by the linearity of trace and expectation, and
=_{2}
comes from the cyclic multiplication invariance property of trace.

\hfill\Box

Before proving tensor Chebyshev’s inequality, we need to have the following Lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be two Hermitian tensors with
\mathcal{A}^{2}
\preceq
\mathcal{B}^{2}
. Then, we have
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
\preceq
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
.

Proof:

We consider the first case that the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is an SPD tensor and
\mathcal{B}
is a PD tensor . From
\mathcal{A}^{2}
\preceq
\mathcal{B}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{-1}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{2}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{-1}\preceq\mathcal{I}.

(18)

Then, we have the following inequalities:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\geq_{1}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{2}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{-1}\right)=\sigma^{2}_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle\geq_{2}

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle=_{3}

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{-1/2}\right),

where
\geq_{1}
comes from Eq. (
18
),
\geq_{2}
comes from the Weyl’s inequality between eigenvalues and singular values, and
=_{3}
comes from the eigenvalue set invariance under the cyclic permutation of tensors. From Eq. (
19
), we have

\displaystyle 1\geq\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-1/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{-1/2}\right)\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}.

(20)

Next, we consider the situation that the tensor
\mathcal{A}
is SPD tensor and
\mathcal{B}
is a SPD tensor. From eigen-decomposition of tensor
\mathcal{B}
, we can express
\mathcal{B}
as
\mathcal{B}
=
\mathcal{U}\star_{N}\mathcal{D}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the unitary tensor and
\mathcal{D}
is the diagonal tensor. For any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\mathcal{B}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}
=
\mathcal{U}\star_{N}(\mathcal{D}+\epsilon\mathcal{I})\star_{N}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{T}}
, which is PD. From the following relation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\star_{N}\mathcal{D}^{2}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{T}}

(21)

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\star_{N}\left(\mathcal{D}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}\right)^{2}\star_{N}\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{B}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}\right)^{2},

and
\mathcal{A}^{2}
\preceq
\mathcal{B}^{2}
, we have
\mathcal{A}^{2}
\preceq
\left(\mathcal{B}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}\right)^{2}
. Since
\left(\mathcal{B}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}\right)
is a PD tensor, this implies that
\mathcal{A}
\preceq
\mathcal{B}+\epsilon\mathcal{I}
. By taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we have
\mathcal{A}
\preceq
\mathcal{B}
.

The remaining case is that both
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are not SPD. Since both
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are Hermitian tensors, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{2}\preceq\mathcal{B}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\left|\mathcal{A}\right|^{2}\preceq\left|\mathcal{B}\right|^{2}.

(22)

Because both
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
are SPD tensors, we have
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
\preceq
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
.

\hfill\Box

From Lemma
1
, we can have following tensor Chebyshev’s inequality.

Theorem 2
(Chebyshev’s Inequality for Tensors)

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be a PD deterministic tensor, and let
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be a random Hermitian tensor. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathcal{X}\npreceq\mathcal{A}\right)\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}^{2}]\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-2}\right).

(23)

Proof:

From Lemma
1
, we have
\mathcal{X}^{2}
\preceq
\mathcal{A}^{2}\Longrightarrow\left|\mathcal{X}\right|
\preceq
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
This is equivalent that
\left|\mathcal{X}\right|
\npreceq
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\Longrightarrow\mathcal{X}^{2}
\npreceq
\mathcal{A}^{2}
.

By monotonicity of probability, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(\left|\mathcal{X}\right|\npreceq\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathcal{X}^{2}\npreceq\mathcal{A}^{2}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}^{2}\right]\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-2}\right),

where the last inequality comes from Theorem
1
.

\hfill\Box

Actually, we can have more general power, instead of 2, in tensor Chebyshev’s inequality. We need the following Lemma
2
.

Lemma 2

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
be two SPD tensors with
\mathcal{A}\succeq\mathcal{B}
, and let
q
\in
[0,1]
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{q}\succeq\mathcal{B}^{q}.

(25)

Proof:

Let us define the following set,
\mathrm{S}
, for the real number
q
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{q\in\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{A}^{q}\succeq\mathcal{B}^{q}\}.

(26)

It is clear that both
0
and
1
belong to the set
\mathrm{S}
. We wish to show that
[0,1]\in\mathrm{S}
. Since dyadic numbers are dense in
[0,1]
, this is equivalent to show the following:

\displaystyle q,r\in\mathrm{S}\Longrightarrow\frac{q+r}{2}\in\mathrm{S}.

(27)

If
q\in\mathrm{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{q}\succeq\mathcal{B}^{q}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{q}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\preceq\mathcal{I}.

(28)

Then, we can have the following

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\left(\mathcal{B}^{q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\right)^{\mathrm{H}}\star_{N}\left(\mathcal{B}^{q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\right)\right)

(29)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{B}^{q}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\right)\leq 1

This implies that
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{q/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-q/2}\right)\leq 1
. Because we also have
r\in\mathrm{S}
, similarly, we have
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathcal{B}^{r/2}\star_{N}\mathcal{A}^{-r/2}\right)\leq 1
