1.1
Binomial coefficients

Let us first recall the definition of binomial coefficients:
2
2

2

We use
the notation
\mathbb{N}
for the set
\left\{0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, the

binomial coefficient

\dbinom{m}{n}
is a rational number defined by

\dbinom{m}{n}=\dfrac{m\left(m-1\right)\cdots\left(m-n+1\right)}{n!}.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
n
be a negative integer. Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then, the
binomial coefficient

\dbinom{m}{n}
is a rational number
defined by
\dbinom{m}{n}=0
.

(This is the definition used in
[
GrKnPa94
]
and
[
Grinbe17b
]
. Some authors
follow other conventions instead.)

The following proposition is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
Grinbe17b
, Proposition
1.9]
):

Proposition 1.3
.

We have
\dbinom{m}{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
for any

m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition
1.3
shows that
\dbinom{m}{n}
is an integer
whenever
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We shall tacitly use this
below, when we study congruences involving binomial coefficients.

One advantage of Definition
1.2
is that it makes the
following hold:

Proposition 1.4
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, the
binomial coefficient
\dbinom{n}{m}
is the coefficient of
X^{m}
in the
formal power series
\left(1+X\right)^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[\left[X\right]\right]
. (Here, the coefficient of
X^{m}
in any formal power
series is defined to be
0
when
m
is negative.)

1.2
Classical congruences

The behavior of binomial coefficients modulo primes and prime powers is a
classical subject of research; see
[
Mestro14
, §2.1]
for a survey of
much of it. Let us state two of the most basic results in this subject:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
a
and
b
be two integers. Let
c

and
d
be two elements of
\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Then,

\dbinom{ap+c}{bp+d}\equiv\dbinom{a}{b}\dbinom{c}{d}\operatorname{mod}p.

Theorem
1.5
is known under the name of
Lucas’s theorem
,
and is proven in many places (e.g.,
[
Mestro14
, §2.1]
or
[
Hausne83
, Proof of
§4]
or
[
AnBeRo05
, proof of Lucas’s theorem]
or
[
GrKnPa94
, Exercise
5.61]
) at least in the case when
a
and
b
are nonnegative integers.
The standard proof of Theorem
1.5
in this case uses generating
functions (specifically, Proposition
1.4
); this proof
applies (mutatis mutandis) in the general case as well. See
[
Grinbe17b
, Theorem
1.11]
for an elementary proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Another fundamental result is the following:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
a
and
b
be two integers. Then,

\dbinom{ap}{bp}\equiv\dbinom{a}{b}\operatorname{mod}p^{2}.

Theorem
1.6
is a known result, perhaps due to Charles Babbage. It
appears with proof in
[
Grinbe17b
, Theorem 1.12]
; again, many sources prove it
for nonnegative
a
and
b
(for example
[
Stanle11
, Exercise 1.14
c
]
or
[
GrKnPa94
, Exercise 5.62]
). Notice that if
p\geq 5
, then
the modulus
p^{2}
can be replaced by
p^{3}
or (depending on
a
,
b
and

p
) by even higher powers of
p
; see
[
Mestro14
, (22) and (23)]
for the
details. See also
[
SunTau11
, Lemma 2.1]
for another strengthening of
Theorem
1.6
.

1.3
The three modulo-
p^{2}
congruences

Definition 1.7
.

For any
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define an integer
\eta_{p}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
by

\eta_{p}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }p\equiv 0\operatorname{mod}3;\\
1,&\text{if }p\equiv 1\operatorname{mod}3;\\
-1,&\text{if }p\equiv 2\operatorname{mod}3\end{cases}.

Notice that
\eta_{p}
is the so-called
Legendre symbol

\left(\dfrac{p}{3}\right)
known from number theory.

we shall prove in the sequel. The first one is
[
ApaZei16
, Super-Conjecture
1]
:

Let

2.1
Identities and congruences from the literature

Before we come to the proofs of Theorems
1.8
,
1.9
and

1.10
, let us collect various well-known results that will prove useful.

The following properties of binomial coefficients are well-known (see, e.g.,

[
Grinbe17
, §3.1]
and
[
Grinbe17b
, §1]
):

Proposition 2.1
.

We have
\dbinom{m}{0}=1
for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

We have
\dbinom{m}{n}=0
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
m<n
.

Proposition 2.3
.

We have
\dbinom{m}{n}=\dbinom{m}{m-n}
for any

m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
m\geq n
.

Proposition 2.4
.

We have
\dbinom{m}{m}=1
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

We have

\dbinom{m}{n}=\left(-1\right)^{n}\dbinom{n-m-1}{n}

for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

We have

\dbinom{m}{n}=\dbinom{m-1}{n-1}+\dbinom{m-1}{n}

for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and

y\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\dbinom{x+y}{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\dbinom{x}{k}\dbinom{y}{n-k}.

Proposition
2.7
is the so-called

Vandermonde convolution identity
, and is a particular case of

[
Grinbe17
, Theorem 3.29]
.

Corollary 2.8
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(-1\right)^{i}\dbinom{n-1-i}{i}=\left(-1\right)^{n}\cdot\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n\equiv 0\operatorname{mod}3;\\
-1,&\text{if }n\equiv 1\operatorname{mod}3;\\
1,&\text{if }n\equiv 2\operatorname{mod}3\end{cases}.

Corollary
2.8
is
[
Grinbe17
, Corollary 8.63]
. Apart from
that, Corollary
2.8
can be easily derived from
[
GrKnPa94
, §5.2,
Problem 3]
,
[
BenQui03
, Identity 172]
or
[
BenQui08
]
.

Another simple identity (sometimes known as the “absorption identity”) is
the following:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

k\dbinom{n}{k}=n\dbinom{n-1}{k-1}
.

Proposition
2.9
appears in
[
GrKnPa94
, (5.6)]
, and is
easily proven just from the definition of binomial coefficients.

Finally, we need the following result from elementary number theory:

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that

k
is not a positive multiple of
p-1
. Then,

\sum\limits_{l=0}^{p-1}l^{k}\equiv 0\operatorname{mod}p.

Theorem
2.10
is proven, e.g., in
[
Grinbe17b
, Theorem 3.1]

and (in a slightly rewritten form) in
[
MacSon10
, Theorem 1]
.

2.2
Variants and consequences of Vandermonde convolution

We are now going to state a number of identities that are restatements or
particular cases of the Vandermonde convolution identity (Proposition

2.7
). We begin with the following one:

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
and

w\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\sum\limits_{m=0}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+m}\dbinom{l}{m}=\dbinom{u+l}{w+l}.

Proof 2.12

(Proof of Corollary
2.11
.)
.

Proposition

2.7
(applied to
x=u
,
y=l
and
n=w+l
)
yields

\displaystyle\dbinom{u+l}{w+l}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{w+l}\dbinom{u}{k}\dbinom{l}{w+l-k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{w-1}\dbinom{u}{k}\underbrace{\dbinom{l}{w+l-k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=0\\
\text{(by Proposition \ref{prop.binom.0}}\\
\text{(since
}l<w+l-k\text{ (because }k<w\text{)))}\end{subarray}}+\sum\limits_{k=w}^{w+l}\dbinom{u}{k}\dbinom{l}{w+l-k}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{here, we have split the sum at }k=w\text{,}\\
\text{since }0\leq w\leq w+l\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{w-1}\dbinom{u}{k}0}_{=0}+\sum\limits_{k=w}^{w+l}\dbinom{u}{k}\dbinom{l}{w+l-k}=\sum\limits_{k=w}^{w+l}\dbinom{u}{k}\dbinom{l}{w+l-k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+m}\underbrace{\dbinom{l}{w+l-\left(w+m\right)}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\dbinom{l}{l-m}=\dbinom{l}{m}\\
\text{(by
Proposition \ref{prop.binom.symm})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have substituted }w+m\text{ for
}k\text{ in the sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+m}\dbinom{l}{m}.

This proves Corollary
2.11
.

Let us also state another corollary of Proposition

2.7
:

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and
y\in\mathbb{N}
and

n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

\dbinom{x+y}{n}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{y}\dbinom{x}{n-i}\dbinom{y}{i}.

See
[
Grinbe17b
, Corollary 2.2]
for a proof of Corollary

2.13
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}
and
w\in\mathbb{N}
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then,

\dbinom{u+2l}{w+l}=\dbinom{u}{w}\dbinom{2l}{l}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\left(\dbinom{u}{w+i}+\dbinom{u}{w-i}\right)\dbinom{2l}{l-i}.

Proof 2.15

(Proof of Lemma
2.14
.)
.

Corollary
2.13
(applied to

x=u
,
y=2l
and
n=w+l
) yields

\displaystyle\dbinom{u+2l}{w+l}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{2l}\dbinom{u}{w+l-i}\dbinom{2l}{i}=\sum\limits\limits_{i=-l}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have substituted }l-i\text{ for
}i\text{ in the sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\left\{-l,-l+1,\ldots,l\right\};\\
i\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}+\dbinom{u}{w}\dbinom{2l}{l}

(here, we have split off the addend for
i=0
from the sum). Hence,

\displaystyle\dbinom{u+2l}{w+l}-\dbinom{u}{w}\dbinom{2l}{l}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\left\{-l,-l+1,\ldots,l\right\};\\
i\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}+\sum\limits\limits_{i=-l}^{-1}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{here, we have split the sum into two:}\\
\text{one for \textquotedblleft positive }i\text{\textquotedblright\ and one
for \textquotedblleft negative }i\text{\textquotedblright}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}+\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w-i}\underbrace{\dbinom{2l}{l+i}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\dbinom{2l}{l-i}\\
\text{(by Proposition \ref{prop.binom.symm})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{here, we have substituted }-i\text{ for }i\\
\text{in the second sum}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w+i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}+\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{u}{w-i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\left(\dbinom{u}{w+i}+\dbinom{u}{w-i}\right)\dbinom{2l}{l-i}.

In other words,

\dbinom{u+2l}{w+l}=\dbinom{u}{w}\dbinom{2l}{l}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\left(\dbinom{u}{w+i}+\dbinom{u}{w-i}\right)\dbinom{2l}{l-i}.

This proves Lemma
2.14
.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let

l\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Then,

\dbinom{cp+2l}{l}=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{p-1}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}.

Proof 2.17

(Proof of Lemma
2.16
.)
.

Corollary
2.13

(applied to
x=cp+l
,
y=l
and
n=l
) yields

\displaystyle\dbinom{cp+l+l}{l}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{l-i}\dbinom{l}{i}=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\underbrace{\dbinom{l}{l-k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\dbinom{l}{k}\\
\text{(by Proposition \ref{prop.binom.symm})}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have substituted }k\text{ for
}l-i\text{ in the sum}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}.

Comparing this with

\displaystyle\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{p-1}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}+\sum\limits\limits_{k=l+1}^{p-1}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\underbrace{\dbinom{l}{k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=0\\
\text{(by Proposition
\ref{prop.binom.0}}\\
\text{(applied to }m=l\text{ and }n=k\text{)}\\
\text{(since }l<k\text{))}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have split the sum at
}k=l\text{, since }0\leq l\leq p-1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}+\underbrace{\sum\limits\limits_{k=l+1}^{p-1}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}0}_{=0}=\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k},

we obtain
\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{p-1}\dbinom{cp+l}{k}\dbinom{l}{k}=\dbinom{cp+l+l}{l}=\dbinom{cp+2l}{l}
. This proves Lemma
2.16
.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{p}{i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}=\dbinom{p+2l}{l}-\dbinom{2l}{l}.

Proof 2.19

(Proof of Lemma
2.18
.)
.

Proposition

2.7
(applied to
x=p
,

and

)
yields

\displaystyle\dbinom{p+2l}{l}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{l}\dbinom{p}{k}\dbinom{2l}{l-k}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{l}\dbinom{p}{i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have renamed the summation index
}k\text{ as }i\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\dbinom{p}{0}}_{=1}\underbrace{\dbinom{2l}{l-0}}_{=\dbinom{2l}{l}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{p}{i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}=\dbinom{2l}{l}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\dbinom{p}{i}\dbinom{2l}{l-i}.

2.3
A congruence of Bailey’s

Next, we shall prove a modulo-
p^{2}
congruence for certain binomial
coefficients that can be regarded as a counterpart to Theorem
1.6
:

Theorem 2.20
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}
and
K\in\mathbb{Z}
and
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Then:

(a)
We have

\dbinom{Np}{Kp+i}\equiv N\dbinom{N-1}{K}\dbinom{p}{i}\operatorname{mod}p^{2}.

(b)
We have

\dbinom{Np}{Kp-i}\equiv N\dbinom{N-1}{K-1}\dbinom{p}{i}\operatorname{mod}p^{2}.

(c)
We have

\dbinom{Np}{Kp+i}+\dbinom{Np}{Kp-i}\equiv N\dbinom{N}{K}\dbinom{p}{i}\operatorname{mod}p^{2}.

Theorem
2.20

(a)
is essentially the result
[
Bailey91
, Theorem
4]
by Bailey (see also
[
Mestro14
, (26)]
); in fact, it
transforms into
[
