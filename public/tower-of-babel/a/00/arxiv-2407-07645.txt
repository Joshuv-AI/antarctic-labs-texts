Andreas Galanis, Alkis Kalavasis, Anthimos Vardis Kandiros

Abstract
We consider the problem of sampling from the Ising model when the underlying interaction matrix has eigenvalues lying within an interval of length γ \gamma . Recent work in this setting has shown various algorithmic results that apply roughly when γ < 1 \gamma<1 , notably with nearly-linear running times based on the classical Glauber dynamics. However, the optimality of the range of γ \gamma was not clear since previous inapproximability results developed for the antiferromagnetic case (where the matrix has entries ≤ 0 \leq 0 ) apply only for γ > 2 \gamma>2 . To this end, Kunisky (SODA’24) recently provided evidence that the problem becomes hard already when γ > 1 \gamma>1 based on the low-degree hardness for an inference problem on random matrices. Based on this, he conjectured that sampling from the Ising model in the same range of γ \gamma is NP-hard. Here we confirm this conjecture, complementing in particular the known algorithmic results by showing NP-hardness results for approximately counting and sampling when γ > 1 \gamma>1 , with strong inapproximability guarantees; we also obtain a more refined hardness result for matrices where only a constant number of entries per row are allowed to be non-zero. The main observation in our reductions is that, for γ > 1 \gamma>1 , Glauber dynamics mixes slowly when the interactions are all positive (ferromagnetic) for the complete and random regular graphs, due to a bimodality in the underlying distribution. While ferromagnetic interactions typically preclude NP-hardness results, here we work around this by introducing in an appropriate way mild antiferromagnetism, keeping the spectrum roughly within the same range. This allows us to exploit the bimodality of the aforementioned graphs and show the target NP-hardness by adapting suitably previous inapproximability techniques developed for antiferromagnetic systems.

The Ising model with a symmetric interaction matrix

Our result.

Our aim in this work is to address Kunisky’s conjecture and close the gap between algorithmic and
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness results. In particular, we answer in the affirmative the conjecture in
[
Kun24
]
, obtaining
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness results that complement the algorithmic results of
[
EKZ22
,
AJK+21
]
. This completes the program initiated in
[
Kun24
]
, i.e., showing that Glauber is effectively optimal for “general-purpose” Ising model sampling, and clarifies the picture in terms of the computational complexity landscape under spectral constraints.

To formally state the result, we define the following computational problem.

Problem:

\mbox{{SpectralIsing}}(\gamma)

Input:

A symmetric matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, with
\lambda_{\max}(J)-\lambda_{\min}(J)<\gamma
.

Output:
The partition function
Z_{J}=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\intercal}J\sigma\right)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix any real
\gamma>1
. Then, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate
\mbox{{SpectralIsing}}(\gamma)
, even within an exponential factor
2^{cN}
for some constant
c=c(\gamma)>0
.

This confirms Conjecture 1.9 of
[
Kun24
]
and complements the algorithm of
[
EKZ22
,
AJK+21
]
. Using
Theorem

1.1
, we get the following result using the standard reduction
[
JVV86
]
from counting to sampling (the problem is self-reducible under scaling of the matrix
J
). Recall, the total variation distance between probability distributions
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as
\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

For every real
\gamma>1
, the following holds. Suppose there is a poly-time sampler that, on input a symmetric matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with
\lambda_{\max}(J)-\lambda_{\min}(J)<\gamma
and
\delta>0
, returns a configuration
\sigma
whose distribution is within
\mathrm{TV}
distance
\delta
from
\mu_{J}
.
Then
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{RP}.

As we will explain next, it is also possible to obtain a more refined version of
Theorem

1.1
, for the restricted case where each row of the interaction matrix
J

d

.

Problem:

\mbox{{BoundedSpectralIsing}}(d,\gamma)

Input:

A symmetric matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, with
\leq d

\lambda_{\max}(J)-\lambda_{\min}(J)<\gamma
.

Output:
The partition function
Z_{J}=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\intercal}J\sigma\right)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix any integer
d\geq 4
and real
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\frac{2}{d-3})(d-1+2\sqrt{d-2})
. Then, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate
\mbox{{BoundedSpectralIsing}}(d,\gamma)
, even within an exponential factor
2^{cN}
for some constant
c=c(\gamma)>0
.

Note that when taking the limit
d\to\infty
in the above bound, we recover the spectral condition
\gamma>1
of
Theorem

1.1
, so asymptotically the bound is tight; we are not aware of algorithmic results that apply specifically to the
d
-sparse setting under the spectral condition. We remark further that applying the results of
[
SS12
,
GŠV16
]
would yield hardness only in the setting where
\gamma>d\ln(1+\frac{2}{d-2})
(see
[
Kun24
, Section 1.2]
for a detailed description on how to translate the results), so

improves on this by roughly a factor of 2 asymptotically. It should be noted however that the setting in these results is more restrictive (negative weights, which have the same value on all edges) and hence not directly comparable.

Techniques.

Before giving the proofs, we explain briefly the main idea behind
Theorem

1.1
, the idea for
Theorem

1.3
is almost identical, modulo the gadget used in the reduction.

The key ingredient in obtaining
Theorem

1.1
is to exploit the slow mixing of Glauber dynamics on the complete graph in a suitable way. Recall that
[
LLP10
]
showed exponential mixing time for Glauber dynamics on the
N
-vertex complete graph when the weights on the edges are ferromagnetic equal to
\beta/N
(entry-wise) for any
\beta>1
(note that the corresponding matrix
J
has
\lambda_{\max}(J)-\lambda_{\min}(J)=\beta
). Intuitively, the slow mixing is caused because the distribution exhibits bimodality, i.e., it is concentrated around two modes/“phases” corresponding roughly to the all-plus and all-minus configurations (see
Section

2
for more details). Therefore, we would like to use the binary behaviour of the complete graph as a gadget in the reduction. The main trouble here is caused by the ferromagnetic interactions which cannot typically be related to NP-hard problems; by contrast, in the antiferrromagnetic case
\beta<0
, the max-probability configurations in the Ising distribution correspond to maximum cuts (when
J
encodes the adjacency matrix of a graph), and the respective gadgets in the constructions had bipartite structure.
2
2

2

As a side note, we remark that the factor-2 gap from the antiferromagnetic setting (mentioned below
Theorem

1.3
) comes from the use of bipartite gadgets in these results, which have a symmetric spectrum around zero and hence effectively double the range of the eigenvalues.

Hence, in order to get
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness, we need to introduce some “mild” antiferromagnetism (small negative weights): mild to keep the spectrum unchanged and antiferromagnetic to allow us to reduce from an
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problem (we will use MaxCut); this is quite different than the approach of
[
Kun24
]
where the positive and negative entries in the constructed instance are more heavily mixed up (randomly). At this stage, the main observation is that the previous reductions used in the antiferromagnetic case
[
Sly10
,
SS12
,
GŠV16
]
can accommodate this relatively easily; the only difference here is that we need to use small negative weights to connect disjoint copies of the gadgets, and amplify their effect using appropriately-sized matchings; conveniently, since the matchings (with the small weights on their edges) correspond to a low-rank perturbation, the spectrum of the underlying matrix is close to that of the complete graph.

The proof of
Theorem

1.3
is almost identical. The main difference needed to make our construction sparse is to use a random
d
-regular graph as the gadget, which is known to exhibit slow mixing when
\beta>\beta_{d}:=\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\frac{2}{d-2})

[
GM07
,
DM10
,
MS13
,
MMS12
]
, with a similar bimodal behaviour to that of the complete graph for
\beta>1
. Relative to the spectrum, the well-known result of Friedman
[
Fri08
]
shows that the adjacency matrix
A
of a random
d
-regular graph satisfies w.h.p.
\lambda_{\max}(A)-\lambda_{\min}(A)\leq\lambda_{d}+\epsilon
for any constant
\epsilon>0
, where
\lambda_{d}:=d+2\sqrt{d-1}
. For technical reasons (see
Remark

3.6
for details), we need to actually use a
(d-1)
-regular graph as a gadget in the reduction, so the argument sketched above yields
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness when
\gamma>\beta_{d-1}\lambda_{d-1}
and
d\geq 4
.

The Gadget of
Theorem

1.1

Our main gadget will be a clique graph
K_{n}=(V,E)
with
n
vertices, where
V=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We will consider
n
to be an absolute (large) constant that we will choose later.
For a small integer
t>0
, let
S\subseteq V
be an arbitrary subset of
V
with
|S|=t
. Let
r=n-t
. Intuitively,
S
contains the nodes that will be used to connect the gadgets with each other.

We define the phase of the configuration
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
on
V\backslash S
as

Y_{\sigma}=\mathbf{1}\bigg\{\sum_{i\in V\setminus S}\sigma_{i}>0\bigg\}-\mathbf{1}\bigg\{\sum_{i\in V\setminus S}\sigma_{i}\leq 0\bigg\}.

Note that the phase of a configuration is defined using only the spins in
V\setminus S
.
For any fixed
\beta>0
, consider solutions to the equation

\ln\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}+2\beta(2\alpha-1)=0

(2.1)

for
\alpha\in[0,1]
. It is not hard to see that for
\beta>1
there are exactly three solutions
\alpha=q^{-},1/2,q^{+}
which satisfy
q^{+}-1/2=1/2-q^{-}>0
.
Using these, we define the product measure
Q_{S}^{+}
(resp.
Q_{S}^{-})
on configurations on
S
, where each spin takes the value
+1
with probability
q^{+}
, and
-1
with probability
1-q^{+}
(resp.
q^{-}
and
1-q^{-}
). Concretely, for
\tau\in\{-1,+1\}^{S}
, we have

Q^{\pm}_{S}(\tau)=(q^{\pm})^{\frac{\sum_{i\in S}\tau_{i}+t}{2}}(1-q^{\pm})^{\frac{t-\sum_{i\in S}\tau_{i}}{2}}=\left(q^{\pm}(1-q^{\pm})\right)^{t/2}\left(\tfrac{q^{\pm}}{1-q^{\pm}}\right)^{\frac{\sum_{i\in S}\tau_{i}}{2}}\,.

(2.2)

We now state a lemma that presents the basic properties of the Ising model on our gadget graph. A similar lemma appears in the seminal results of
[
Sly10
,
SS12
]
.
Informally, the lemma states that conditioned on the phase of the spins in
V\setminus S
, the spins in
S
behave almost
independently
from each other, with bias depending on the phase.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\beta>1
. Then, for any real
\epsilon>0
and integer
t\geq 1
, for all sufficiently large integers
n=n(t,\epsilon)
such that
n-t
is odd, the following hold for the Ising model with interaction matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
given by
J=\frac{\beta}{n-t}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the
n
-dimensional vector with all ones.

Let
S\subseteq[n]
be a subset of the vertices with
|S|=t
. Then:

1.

The phases on
V\backslash S
appear with the same probability, i.e.,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}[Y_{\sigma}=+]=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}[Y_{\sigma}=-]=1/2
.

2.

Conditioned on the phase, the joint distribution of the spins in
S
is approximately given by the product distribution
Q^{\pm}_{S}
, i.e.,

for any
\tau\in\{-1,+1\}^{S}
, it holds that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}\big[\sigma_{S}=\tau\mid Y_{\sigma}=\pm\big]=(1\pm\epsilon)Q_{S}^{\pm}(\tau)
.

Proof.

Let
r=n-t
. For
r
odd (as in the statement of the lemma), we have by symmetry that the phases appear with equal probability. So, we focus on proving the second item. For a vector
x
with entries
+1
or
-1
, we denote by
|x|
the sum of its entries.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
be such that
\alpha r
is an integer. For a configuration
\tau\in\{-1,+1\}^{S}
, let
Z^{\alpha}(\tau)
be the contribution to the partition function of configurations
\sigma
with
\alpha r
spins from
V\backslash S
set to
+1
,
(1-\alpha)r
spins from
V\backslash S
set to
-1
and
\sigma_{S}=\tau
.
Concretely,

Z^{\alpha}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{V};\,\sigma_{S}=\tau,\,|\sigma_{V\backslash S}|=(2\alpha-1)r}\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{\top}J\sigma).

The number of configurations
\sigma
with
\sigma_{S}=\tau
and exactly
\alpha r
of the spins in
V\backslash S
equal to
1
is
{r\choose\alpha r}
. Using that
J=\frac{\beta}{r}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, for each such
\sigma
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\top}J\sigma=\frac{\beta}{r}(|\sigma_{V\backslash S}|+|\tau|)^{2}=\frac{\beta}{2r}\big((2\alpha-1)r+|\tau|\big)^{2}
. So,

Z^{\alpha}(\tau)=\binom{r}{\alpha r}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2}(2\alpha-1)^{2}r+\beta(2\alpha-1)|\tau|+\frac{\beta}{2r}|\tau|^{2}\right).

(2.3)

We use the well-known approximation of the binomial coefficient using Stirling’s approximation. This yields, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, that

{r\choose\alpha r}=\exp(rH(\alpha)+o(r)).

(2.4)

where
H(\alpha):=-\alpha\ln\alpha-(1-\alpha)\ln(1-\alpha)
is the binary entropy function.
Asymptotically in
r
, we can also ignore the term
\exp(\frac{\beta}{2r}|\tau|^{2})
, so we obtain that

\displaystyle Z^{\alpha}(\tau)=\exp\big(rf(\alpha)+o(r)\big)\mbox{ where }f(\alpha):=H(\alpha)+\frac{\beta}{2}(2\alpha-1)^{2}.

(2.5)

The function
f(\alpha)
plays a key role since for large
r
it controls the asymptotic order of
Z^{\alpha}(\tau)
. The important point, as we will see below, is that the global maximum of
f
is attained for
\alpha=q^{\pm}
.

Indeed, we have

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\ln(\alpha)+\ln(1-\alpha)+2\beta(2\alpha-1)

and
f^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}+4\beta
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}
has at most two zeros, we have that
f^{\prime}
has at most three distinct zeros and hence
f
has at most three critical points. For
\beta>1
, we have
f^{\prime}(1/2)=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(1/2)=-4+4\beta>0
, so
f
has a local minimum at
\alpha=1/2
; therefore, the maximum of
f
in the interval
[0,1]
is attained at some point
\alpha\neq 1/2
. Using the symmetry of
f
around
\alpha=1/2
, there must be at least two global maxima, one in the interval
(0,1/2)
and
(1/2,1)
. Since
f
has at most three critical points (and 1/2 is one of them), we conclude that there are exactly two critical points/maxima other than
\alpha=1/2
, which must therefore be the values
q^{+},q^{-}
as defined in (
2.1
).

We are now ready to establish the second item of the lemma. We will argue about the
+
phase, but the other phase is completely symmetric. Let
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{S}
be two configurations of spins in
S
. We have that

\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau^{\prime}|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}.

(2.6)

We will show that the sums in the numerator and denominator are dominated by
\alpha
values that are close to
q^{+}
. First, note that since
q^{+}
is the unique global maximum of
f(\alpha)
in the interval
[1/2,1]
, for any arbitrarily small constant
\delta>0
, there is
\eta>0
such that
f(\alpha)\leq f(q^{+})-3\eta
for all
\alpha>1/2
with
\alpha\notin[q^{+}-\delta,q^{+}+\delta]
. We pick
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
r>0
sufficiently large so that
\exp(4\beta t\delta+\beta\tfrac{t^{2}}{r})<\epsilon/2
. Since
|\tau|\leq t
, it follows that for
r
large enough it holds that

\sum_{\alpha>1/2;\,|\alpha-q^{+}|>\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)\leq\exp(r(f(q^{+})-2\eta)).

By the continuity of
f
, for
\alpha=q^{+}+O(1/r)
we have
f(\alpha)=f(q^{+})+O(1/r)
and therefore

\sum_{\alpha>1/2;\,|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)\geq\exp(r(f(q^{+})-\eta)).

It follows that

\frac{\sum_{\alpha>1/2;\,|\alpha-q^{+}|>\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{\alpha>1/2;\,|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}\leq\exp(-\eta r)\leq\epsilon/2.

(2.7)

for all sufficiently large
r
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha>1/2}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}\cdot\frac{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon/2)\frac{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})},

(2.8)

where the last inequality follows from (
2.7
).

On the other hand, for any
\alpha
with
|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta
,
using (
2.3
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\beta(2\alpha-1)(|\tau|-|\tau^{\prime}|)+\tfrac{\beta(|\tau|^{2}-|\tau^{\prime}|^{2})}{2r}\right)\leq\exp(4\beta t\delta+\beta\tfrac{t^{2}}{r})\exp\left(\beta(2q^{+}-1)(|\tau|-|\tau^{\prime}|)\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon/2)\exp\left(\beta(2q^{+}-1)(|\tau|-|\tau^{\prime}|)\right),

(2.9)

where the last inequality follows from the choice of
\delta
and

. Using the definition (
2.2
) and the fact that

is a solution of (

), i.e., that

, we have that

\exp\left(\beta(2q^{+}-1)(|\tau|-|\tau^{\prime}|)\right)=\left(\frac{q^{+}}{1-q^{+}}\right)^{\frac{|\tau|-|\tau^{\prime}|}{2}}=\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}.

Hence, from (
2.9
) we obtain that
\frac{Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}\leq(1+\epsilon/2)\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}
. Since this holds for all
\alpha
with
|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta
, we have

\frac{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau)}{\sum_{|\alpha-q^{+}|\leq\delta}Z^{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})}\leq(1+\epsilon/2)\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}.

(2.10)

Combining this with (
2.6
) and (
2.8
), we obtain that

\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau^{\prime}|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}\leq(1+\epsilon)\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}.

By interchanging the roles of
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
, we also obtain the inverse inequality, so

(1-\epsilon)\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}\leq\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau^{\prime}|Y(\sigma_{V\setminus S})=+]}\leq(1+\epsilon)\frac{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau^{\prime})}.

(2.11)

For
\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{S}
, observe that we can expand the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau|Y=+]}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}=\frac{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}Q^{+}_{S}(\tau^{\prime})\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau\mid Y=+]}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau^{\prime}\mid Y=+]}

so using that
\min_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\leq\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}}{\sum_{i}b_{i}}\leq\max_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
for non-negative
(a_{i})_{i},(b_{i})_{i}
, we obtain from (

) that

\left|\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau|Y=+]}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)}-1\right|\leq\max_{\tau^{\prime}}\left|\frac{Q^{+}_{S}(\tau^{\prime})\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau\mid Y=+]}{Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\sigma_{S}=\tau^{\prime}\mid Y=+]}-1\right|\leq\epsilon.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma
3.4
.

Let
d\geq 3
be an integer and
\beta>\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\frac{2}{d-2})
. Then, for any real
\epsilon>0
and integer
t\geq 1
, for all sufficiently large integers
n=n(t,\epsilon)
with
n-t
odd, the following holds with probability
1-\epsilon
over the choice of
G\sim G_{n,d}
. Let
S\subseteq V
be a subset of vertices with
|S|=t
.

Consider the Ising model with interaction matrix
J=\beta A
where
A
is the adjacency matrix of
G
.
Then:

1.

\lambda_{\max}(J)-\lambda_{\min}(J)\leq\beta(d+2\sqrt{d-1})+\epsilon
.

2.

The phases appear with the same probability, i.e.,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}[Y_{\sigma}=+]=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}[Y_{\sigma}=-]=1/2
.

3.

Conditioned on the phase, the joint distribution of the spins in
S
is approximately given by the product distribution
Q^{\pm}_{S}
, i.e.,

for any
\tau\in\{-1,+1\}^{S}
, it holds that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}\big[\sigma_{S}=\tau\mid Y_{\sigma}=\pm\big]=(1\pm\epsilon)Q_{S}^{\pm}(\tau)
.

Proof.

The first item is Friedman’s result
[
Fri08
]
, see also
[
Bor20
]
. The second item is by symmetry of the configuration space (since
n
is odd).
Let’s focus on proving the third item. Let
\mathbb{T}_{d}
denote the rooted infinite
d

\mathbb{T}^{t}_{d}
be the finite subtree of
\mathbb{T}_{d}
that contains all nodes at distance at most
t
from the root. Let
\nu^{+}_{t}
(resp.
\nu^{-}_{t}
) be the distribution of an Ising model on
\mathbb{T}_{d}^{t}
with parameter
\beta
where all nodes at distance
t
from the root are conditioned to be
+1
(resp.
-1
). Then we can define the Gibbs measure
\nu^{+}
(resp.
\nu^{-}
) on
\mathbb{T}_{d}
to be the weak limit of
\nu^{+}_{t}
(resp.
\nu^{-}_{t}
) as
t\to\infty
. For more details see
[
Geo11
]
.

We will use
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]
; it is a standard fact that
G_{n,d}
satisfies w.h.p. the assumptions of the theorem, i.e.,
G_{n,d}
is locally tree-like and an expander. Therefore, assume that we have a Finally, let
\mu_{n}
be the Ising model measure as defined in the statement of the Lemma and
\mu_{n}^{+}
(resp.
\mu_{n}^{-}
) be the conditional distribution of
\mu_{n}
, conditioned on
\sum_{i\in V}\sigma_{i}\geq 0
(resp.
\sum_{i\in V}\sigma_{i}<0
).
We know that
G_{n,d}
satisfies the assumptions of
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]
with probability
1-o(1)
, so all the subsequent statements hold with high probability. Then, by
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]
(see also
[
DM10
, Theorem 2.7]
), we know that
\mu_{n}^{+}
(resp.
\mu_{n}^{-}
) converges locally in probability to
\nu^{+}
(resp.
\nu^{-}
).
Moreover, by
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.5]
(applied to the function
f_{i,n}(\sigma)=\sigma_{i}
for
\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
) where
\sigma\sim\mu_{n}^{+}

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}-\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\mu_{n}^{+}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right]\right|\to 0

in probability. Also, by
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]

\mu_{n}^{+}
converges locally in probability to
\nu^{+}
, which implies that

\left|\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\nu^{+}}[\sigma_{o}]-\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\mu_{n}^{+}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right]\right|\to 0

where
\sigma_{o}
is the spin of the root of
\mathbb{T}_{d}
. We conclude that, under
\mu_{n}^{+}

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\to\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\nu^{+}}[\sigma_{o}]

in probability. Similarly, under
\mu_{n}^{-}

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\to\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\nu^{-}}[\sigma_{o}]

in probability.
These imply that under
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(\nu^{-}+\nu^{-})

\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{Y}(\sigma)\sum_{i\in V}\sigma_{i}\to\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\nu^{+}}[\sigma_{0}]-\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\nu^{-}}[\sigma_{0}]\right)=\nu^{+}(\sigma_{o}=1)-\nu^{-}(\sigma_{o}=1)

(B.1)

in probability.
We now use an argument similar to
[
SS12
, Proposition 4.2]
.
For any integer
l>0
, let
B_{l}
be the union over all
u\in S
of the
l
-hop neighborhood of
u
. For any fixed
l>0
, with probability
1-o(1)
these neighborhoods will be disjoint from each other. Let
R_{l}
be the nodes in the boundary of
B_{l}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}_{l}(\sigma)=\sum_{i\in V\setminus B_{l}}\sigma_{i}
be the phase defined only using the spins outside of
B_{l}
. By
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]
, we know that
\mu_{n}^{+}(\sigma_{B_{t}}=\cdot)
converges to
\nu^{+}(\sigma_{B_{t}}=\cdot)
.
Using (
B.1
) we conclude that for any
l>0
, with probability
1-o(1)
we have
\mathcal{Y}(\sigma)=\mathcal{Y}_{l}(\sigma)
. Thus,
[
MMS12
, Theorem 2.4]
implies that
\mu_{n}(\sigma_{B_{t}}=\cdot|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)
converges to
\nu^{+}(\sigma_{B_{t}}=\cdot)
as well.
For any
\epsilon>0
, define the set

U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u):=\{\eta_{l}\in\{-1,1\}^{|B_{l}|}:|\mu_{n}(\sigma_{u}=+|\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l})-\nu^{+}(\sigma_{u}=+)|>\epsilon\}.

Intuitively, our goal is to show that
U_{\epsilon}
will have small mass under
\mu_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)
for sufficiently large
l
.
Formally, we would like to prove that

\limsup_{l\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u)|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)=0.

(B.2)

Suppose we have (
B.2
). Then,
by a union bound over all
u\in S
(since the size of the set
|S|=t
is considered fixed) we obtain

\limsup_{l\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\cup_{u\in S}U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u)|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)=0.

This implies that for any
\epsilon>0
, we can find
n=n(\epsilon),l=l(\epsilon)
such that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\eta}\left(\forall u\in S:|\mu_{n}(\sigma_{u}=+|\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta)-\nu^{+}(\sigma_{u}=+)|<\epsilon|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+\right)>1-\epsilon.

Hence, by law of total probability and the conditional independence of the spins in
S
given
\sigma_{B_{l}}
, for any
\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{t}
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\sigma_{S}=\tau|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta_{l}}\mu_{n}(\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l}|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)\mu_{n}(\sigma_{S}=\tau|\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l})

\displaystyle=O(\epsilon)+\sum_{\eta_{l}\in(\cup_{u\in S}U_{\epsilon}^{l})^{c}}\mu_{n}(\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l}|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)\mu_{n}(\sigma_{S}=\tau|\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l})

\displaystyle=O(\epsilon)+\sum_{\eta_{l}\in(\cup_{u\in S}U_{\epsilon}^{l})^{c}}\mu_{n}(\sigma_{B_{l}}=\eta_{l}|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)+O(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)\pm 3\epsilon.

Since this holds for all
\epsilon>0
, taking
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/\max_{\tau}Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)
, we get that for any
\epsilon,t
, we can find large enough

, such that with probability
1-o(1)

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\sigma\sim\mu_{J}}\big[\sigma_{S}=\tau\mid Y_{\sigma}=\pm\big]=(1\pm\epsilon)Q_{S}^{\pm}(\tau)=(1\pm\epsilon)Q_{S}^{+}(\tau)

for all
\tau
.

Finally, again using (
B.1
) we get that the above holds if we replace
\mu_{n}(\sigma_{S}=\cdot|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)

\mu_{n}(\sigma_{S}=\cdot|\mathcal{Y}=+)
.
From this, it follows that we can find a large enough
n

It remains to establish (
B.2
). Keeping
l

\eta_{l}

l
-hop neighborhood of

\mu_{n}(\cdot|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u)|\mathcal{Y}_{l}=+)=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\eta\sim\nu^{+}}(U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u))

where
\eta
is sampled with law
\nu^{+}
and
\eta_{l}
is the set of leaves at distance
l
from the root.
Now, we also have that

\limsup_{l\to\infty}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\eta\sim\nu^{+}}(U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u))\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\eta\sim\nu^{+}}(\limsup_{l\to\infty}U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u)).

Now notice that the event
\limsup_{l\to\infty}U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u)
belongs to the tail
\sigma
-algebra of
\nu^{+}
(where the tail is defined as
\cap_{l}\mathcal{F}_{l}
and
\mathcal{F}_{l}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the spins at distance at least
l
from the root).
Since
\nu^{+}
is extremal, by applying
[
Geo11
, Theorem 7.7(a)]
it follows that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\eta\sim\nu^{+}}(\limsup_{l\to\infty}U_{\epsilon}^{l}(u))\in\{0,1\}.

Since
\epsilon>0
, this probability cannot be
1
.
Hence, this tail event has zero probability
and so we get (
B.2
).
