A Note on the Practice of Logical Inferentialism: The State-Effect Interpretation, Definitional Reflection, and Completeness
Alexander V. Gheorghiu, Tao Gu, David J. Pym

Abstract




The prevailing paradigm of semantics in logic is
denotationalism
, wherein the meaning of logical structures is grounded in reference to abstract algebraic structures, exemplified by
model-theoretic semantics
(M-tS). An alternative paradigm is
inferentialism

(
Brandom 2009
)
, wherein meaning emerges from rules of inference. It may be viewed in the context of the
meaning-as-use
principle
(
Wittgenstein 2009
)
, with ‘use’ understood in logic as ‘inference’.

For instance, consider the statement ‘Luna is a vixen’, which intuitively means ‘Luna is a fox’
and
‘Luna is female’. In inferentialism, its meaning is given by the following rules:

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Luna is a vixen}\lx@proof@logical@and\text{Luna is a fox}\text{Luna is female}\qquad\text{Luna is female}\text{Luna is a vixen}\qquad\text{Luna is a fox}\text{Luna is a vixen}\\
\end{array}

These merit comparison with the laws governing conjunction (
\land
)
(
Szabo 1969
)
, which justify the ‘and’ above,

\phi\land\psi\lx@proof@logical@and\phi\psi\qquad\phi\phi\land\psi\qquad\psi\phi\land\psi

Proof-theoretic semantics
(P-tS) provides a mathematical expression of inferentialism
(
Schroeder-Heister 2018
)
. Unlike M-tS, P-tS views
proofs

as the basis of meaning (as opposed to
truth
). Below, we propose an analysis of P-tS that explicates the semantic clauses for various standard logics. In particular, we justify the P-tS for
intuitionistic propositional logic
(IPL) by
Sandqvist 2015
(cf.
Piecha & Schroeder-Heister 2019
), and its subsequent developments by
Gheorghiu et al. 2023
and
Gu et al. 2023
.

Heuristically, logical constants are
defined
by their introduction rules; for instance,
\land_{\mathsf{{I}}}
can be understood as
defining

\land
. A central challenge in P-tS is to formalize this heuristic rigorously. However, merely providing introduction rules for a logic is insufficient for a complete definition; one requires elimination rules. Notably, the choice of elimination rules is not arbitrary
(
Prior 1961
)
, leading to the study of
harmony

(
Tennant 1978b
)
and
inversion

(
Prawitz 2006
)
in P-tS.

Notwithstanding these challenges, the elimination rules in the natural deduction systems on which much of P-tS has been based — for example,
NJ

(
Szabo 1969
)
— are thought of as being
consequences
of their corresponding introduction rules. Indeed, a central approach in P-tS has been to witness and exploit this relationship
(
Schroeder-Heister 2006
)
. One way to characterize the relationship is through the following idea:

The introductions represent the ‘state’ required for a logical constant to be asserted, and the corresponding eliminations represent the ‘effect’ of that constant.

This
state-effect
duality is useful for explicating the differences between M-tS and P-tS. Of course, the ‘effect’ of a logical constant must be warranted by the ‘state’ it signifies; whence, harmony and inversion principles.

Some explanation is required for what we mean by state and effect. They correspond to two perspectives on logic, the
ontological
and the
teleological
.

The ontological perspective views logic as the study of properties of objects
(
Hofweber 2023
)
and ‘state’ refers to the state of objects. This view is the foundation for M-tS and the clauses of the logical constants are, therefore, closely related to their corresponding introduction rules. For example, in the standard possible-world semantics for IPL by
Kripke 1965
, the clause for disjunction closely mirrors the introduction rule
\lor_{\mathsf{1{I}}}
and
\lor_{\mathsf{2{I}}}

(
Szabo 1969
)
,

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\phi\lor\psi\qquad\mbox{iff}\qquad\mbox{$\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\phi$ or $\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\psi$}

The correctness of the elimination rules
follows from ‘backward’ reasoning on states, which are represented by possible worlds
(
Gheorghiu & Pym 2023c
;
Gheorghiu & Pym 2023a
;
Gheorghiu 2024
)
.

In contrast, we shall call the ‘teleological’ perspective on logic the view that is concerned with understanding
reasoning
.
Tennant 1978a
exemplifies this view on logic in the ‘consequentialist’ reading of entailment. By ‘effect’ we mean what reasoning is rendered possible by the assertion of the logical constants. This explains why clauses of the semantics typically mirror the elimination rules. For example, in the work on IPL by
Sandqvist 2015
, the clause for disjunction (
\lor
) closely mirrors the elimination rule
\lor_{\mathsf{{E}}}

(
Szabo 1969
)
restricted to atoms,

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Vdash_{\mathscr{B}}\phi\lor\psi&\mbox{iff}&\mbox{for any $\mathscr{C}\supseteq\mathscr{B}$ and any atom $p$,}\\
&&\mbox{if $\phi\Vdash_{\mathscr{C}}p$ and $\psi\Vdash_{\mathscr{C}}p$, then $\Vdash_{\mathscr{C}}p$}\end{array}

The details — such as the significance of
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{C}
— are not essential for this discussion. The point is that forcing a clause informed by a state-based view yields incompleteness; that is,
Piecha et al. 2015
have shown that using

\Vdash_{\mathscr{B}}\phi\lor\psi\qquad\mbox{iff}\qquad\Vdash_{\mathscr{B}}\phi\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad\Vdash_{\mathscr{B}}\psi

does not give a semantics for IPL. The correctness of the introduction rules follows from the transitivity of the judgment relation.

In the practice of logical inferentialism, while we treat introduction rules as definitions, our central concern is to understand the effects implied by a state. More broadly, how one transitions from an ontological interpretation to a teleological one. We argue that
Definitional Reflection
(DR)
(
Hallnäs 1987
;
Hallnäs & Schroder-Heister 1990
;
Hallnäs & Schroder-Heister 1991
;
Schroeder-Heister 1993
)
plays an essential role:

“Whatever follows from all the defining conditions of an assertion follows from the assertion itself.”

Not only does DR offer a mathematically elegant solution to these issues but also embodies the intended conceptual shift.

Indeed, DR gives one approach for generating elimination rules from introduction rules. For example, let
\lor_{\mathsf{1{I}}}
and
\lor_{\mathsf{2{I}}}
be the ‘definition’ of disjunction (
\lor
),

\phi\lor\psi\phi\qquad\phi\lor\psi\psi

Hence, the defining conditions of
\phi\lor\psi
are
\phi
and
\psi
. Whence, by DR, whatever follows from both
\phi
and
\psi
also follows from
\phi\lor\psi
. This motivates
\lor_{\mathsf{{E}}}
,

\chi\lx@proof@logical@and\phi\lor\psi\chi[\phi]\chi[\psi]

as required. Observe that
\land_{\mathsf{1{E}}}
and
\land_{\mathsf{2{E}}}
are together obtained from and equally expressive as the generalized elimination rule for
\land_{\mathsf{{I}}}

(
Schroeder-Heister 1984
)
.This is all very well as a mechanism, but why ought we accept it?

According to
Schroeder-Heister 1993
, DR follows from reading the introduction rules as expressing
all
conditions for the assertion to be made. Essentially, this embodies a form of
closed-world assumption

(
Reiter 1981
)
, which in the form
negation-as-failure

(
Clark 1977
)
has been studied more directly in the context of the P-tS of IPL
(
Gheorghiu & Pym 2023b
)
. In this sense, it is the completion
(
Clark 1977
)
of the inference system afforded by the introduction rules; in the state-effect reading proposed above, the generalized elimination rules represent the
maximal
effect of a logical constant corresponding to its introduction rules.

This justifies DR as a general phenomenon. It remains to show that it represents the intended conceptional shift from the ontological to the teleological perspective when doing logical inferentialism.

Goldfarb 2016
has given an alternative sound and complete inferential semantics of IPL that is based on the ontological view of logic where the
objects
are ‘canonical’ proofs in the Dummett-Prawitz sense
(
Schroeder-Heister 2006
)
. In this case, canonical proofs act as states, and the introduction rules act as
constructors
, yielding clauses based on introduction rules.
Gheorghiu & Pym 2022
have shown that moving from constructors to constructiveness — that is, from states to effects — in the approach by
Goldfarb 2016
recovers the semantics by
Sandqvist 2015
. The move is conceptionally simple and best illustrated by an example:

Consider the judgment
\phi\lor\psi\vdash\psi\lor\phi
. We must show that on the assumption that there is a valid argument for
\phi\lor\psi
, there is a valid argument for
\psi\lor\phi
. Without loss of generality, a valid argument for
\phi\lor\psi
ends by a constructor
\lor_{\mathsf{1{I}}}
or
\lor_{\mathsf{2{I}}}
. Hence, a valid argument exists for either
\phi
or
\psi
. Whence, by the use of a constructor
\lor_{\mathsf{1{I}}}
or
\lor_{\mathsf{2{I}}}
, there is a valid argument for
\psi\lor\phi
, as required.

Observe that we have implicitly used DR: we have argued from
all the defining conditions
of the assertion
\phi\lor\psi
that we can conclude
\psi\lor\phi
.

To summarize, adopting the state-effect interpretation of introduction and elimination rules aids in delineating the distinctions between P-tS and M-tS. This approach offers a unified understanding of the practice of logical inferentialism. Additionally, DR emerges as a central tenet in transitioning from a state-based semantics to an effect-based semantics of a logical system. It not only works at a mathematical level, but actually arises naturally as one moves between the perspectives.
