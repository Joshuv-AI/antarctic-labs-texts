Random Volumes in d d -dimensional Polytopes
Tomasz Tkocz

Abstract
Suppose we choose N N points uniformly randomly from a convex body in d d dimensions. How large must N N be, asymptotically with respect to d d , so that the convex hull of the points is nearly as large as the convex body itself? It was shown by Dyer-Füredi-McDiarmid that exponentially many samples suffice when the convex body is the hypercube, and by Pivovarov that the Euclidean ball demands roughly d d / 2 d^{d/2} samples. We show that when the convex body is the simplex, exponentially many samples suffice; this then implies the same result for any convex simplicial polytope with at most exponentially many faces.

Introduction

Suppose that points
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots
are sampled uniformly and independently from a convex body
X\subseteq\Re^{d}
. We are interested in the asymptotics of the random variable
V_{X,N}
given by the volume of the convex hull of
{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{N}
. In particular, we would like to know how large
N
has to be to ensure that w.h.p.
1
1

1

with high probability: probability approaching 1 as
d\to\infty
the volume of the convex hull of
{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{N}
is a significant fraction of the volume of
X
.

This problem is well understood when
X
is a product space (i.e., a hypercube) or a Euclidean ball. In the case where
X
is the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
, the coordinates of the
{\bf q}_{i}
are independent uniform random variables in
[0,1]
, and Dyer, Füredi, and McDiarmid
[
5
]
proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Dyer, Füredi, McDiarmid, 1992)
.

If
X
is the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
,
\lambda=e^{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{u}-\frac{1}{e^{u}-1}\right)^{2}du}\approx 2.14
, and
\varepsilon>0
, then as
d\to\infty
we have that

\mathbb{E}V_{X,N}\to\begin{cases}0&\text{if }N=N(d)\leq\left(\lambda-\varepsilon\right)^{d},\\
1&\text{if }N=N(d)>\left(\lambda+\varepsilon\right)^{d}.\end{cases}

In particular, an exponential number of sample points suffice to capture the volume of the hypercube with the convex hull of the sample (and they even determine the correct base of the exponent). This was generalized in 2009 by Gatzouras and Giannopoulos in
[
6
]
to the case of random points with i.i.d. coordinates which instead of being uniform are drawn from any even, compactly supported distribution that satisfies certain mild conditions.

On the other hand, if
X
is the Euclidean ball, Pivovarov proved in
[
9
]
that the threshold is super-exponential.

Theorem 1.2

(Pivovarov, 2007)
.

If
X
is the unit Eulidean ball in
\Re^{d}
,
X=\{x\in\Re^{d},\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\leq 1\}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, then as
d\to\infty
we have that

\frac{\mathbb{E}V_{X,N}}{\mathrm{Vol}(X)}\to\begin{cases}0&\text{if }N=N(d)\leq d^{\frac{d}{2}(1-\varepsilon)},\\
1&\text{if }N=N(d)>d^{\frac{d}{2}(1+\varepsilon)}.\end{cases}

For results concerning a more general rotationally symmetric model of the so-called
\beta
-polytopes (also exhibiting super-exponential thresholds), see the recent papers
[
1
,
2
]
. For general bounds on
N
concerning arbitrary log-concave and
\kappa
-concave distributions see
[
3
]
.

We analyze the case where
X
is a convex simplicial polytope: that is, a polytope whose facets are all simplices. In particular, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\Re^{d}
be a convex simplicial polytope with
m
facets, let
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots
be a sequence of points chosen independently and uniformly from
X
, and let
Q_{j}=Q_{j,d}\subseteq\Omega
be the convex hull of
\{{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{j}\}
. There are positive universal constants
c_{0},C_{0}
such that if
d
is sufficiently large and
N>C_{0}^{d}m
, then
\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})\geq(1-e^{-c_{0}\sqrt{d}})\mathrm{Vol}(X)
.

Since any convex simplicial polytope with
m
faces can be partitioned into at most
m
simplices, which are all affine equivalent, it suffices to prove Theorem
1.3
in the case where
X
is a simplex. In particular, we let
\Omega_{d}
denote the standard embedding of the
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex in
d
-dimensional space:

\Omega_{d}=\left\{{\bf x}\geq{\bf 0}:x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{d}=1\right\}.

The heart of our results is thus the following statement.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots
be a sequence of points chosen independently and uniformly from
\Omega=\Omega_{d}
, and let
Q_{j}=Q_{j,d}\subseteq\Omega
be the convex hull of
\{{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{j}\}
. There are positive constants
c_{0},C_{0}
such that if
d
is sufficiently large and
N>C_{0}^{d}
, then
\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})\geq(1-e^{-c_{0}\sqrt{d}})\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega)
.

Remark 1.5
.

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it follows that if we take a sequence of instances
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\dots,
then
\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N,d})/\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{d})\to 1
as
d\to\infty
with probability 1.

Remark 1.6
.

For clarity, we do not try to optimize any constants in our proofs. We get the theorem with
c_{0}=\frac{1}{4}
and
C_{0}=300
.

The following lower bound shows that an exponential dependence is necessary.

Theorem 1.7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, if
N<e^{(\gamma-\varepsilon)d}
, then we have
\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{d})}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})\to 0
as
d\to\infty
, where
\gamma=0.577\ldots
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

A similar lower bound with a worse constant follows from Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
. To prove Theorem
1.7
, we use the approach from
[
5
]
. We conjecture that the value of the constant
e^{\gamma}
is sharp. (The method from
[
5
]
yields sharp results in the independent case as well as rotationally symmetric ones – see
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
9
]
– where the dependence between components is mild, as in the case of a simplex.) For the upper bound, we follow a different strategy, which is summarized at the beginning of the next section.

The rest of the paper comprises two sections, which are devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.7
.

2
Proof of the upper bound: Theorem
1.4

We begin by sketching the structure of the whole proof. For
i=1,\dots,d
we define the
\alpha
-caps

C_{i}(\alpha)
of the simplex to be the sets

C_{i}(\alpha):=\Omega\cap\left\{{\bf x}\mid x_{i}\geq 1-\alpha\right\}.

(1)

Note that they are disjoint as long as
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, and the volume
\mathrm{Vol}(C_{i}(\alpha))
of
C_{i}(\alpha)
is precisely
\alpha^{d-1}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega)
. In particular, when examining the sequence
\{{\bf q}_{j}\}
, we expect to see a point in
C_{i}(\alpha)

every
(\tfrac{1}{\alpha})^{d-1}
steps. And for
\alpha
a constant, after exponentially many steps, we can collect points from each cap
C_{i}(\alpha)
. A routine calculation shows that the expected measure of the convex hull of a random set of
d
points with one from each
C_{i}(\alpha)
is exponentially small compared with
\Omega
, though it is not a priori clear how much overlap to expect from multiple such random simplices. The basic strategy of the proof is to define a large set
\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)\subseteq\Omega
, and then show that for any
fixed

{\bf x}\in\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
, the point
{\bf x}
is very likely to lie in the convex hull of some simplex with one point
{\bf p}^{\bf x}_{i}
in each in cap
C_{i}(\alpha)
, where all the the points
{\bf p}^{\bf x}_{1},{\bf p}^{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf p}^{\bf x}_{d}
occur among the first
C_{0}^{d}
terms of the sequence
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots
. We do this by showing (in Lemma
2.5
) that every exponentially many steps, one obtains not only a point
{\bf p}^{\bf x}_{i}
which lies in the cap
C_{i}(\alpha)
, but one which is similar to
{\bf x}
with respect to its proximity to a lower dimensional face close to
{\bf x}
– this provides points which give a good chance of containing
{\bf x}
in the convex hull reasonably quickly. (The fact that the points
p_{i}^{\bf x}
are large in coordinate
i
lets us view them as a diagonally dominant matrix, which we exploit to show that
{\bf x}
is likely to lie in their convex hull.) Linearity of expectation will then show that the measure of the uncovered part of
\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
is very small, and Markov’s inequality can then give a w.h.p statement as in the theorem. In particular, although
{\bf x}
lying in the convex hull of
\left\{{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{N}\right\}
is of course equivalent to
{\bf x}
lying in some simplex
S_{x}
with vertices in
\left\{{\bf q}_{1},\dots,{\bf q}_{N}\right\}
, it is perhaps surprising that we prove the theorem by actually identifying
S_{x}
, rather than, say, considering whether
{\bf x}
is separated from the convex hull by a hyperplane.

2.1
The exponential model

A basic tool we use is the standard fact that the coordinate vector of a uniformly random point in the simplex
\Omega
can be simply described using independent exponentials, as encapsulated in the first part of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

If we generate a random point
{\bf q}\in\Omega
by generating the coordinates
q_{j}
as

q_{j}=\frac{E_{j}}{E_{1}+\dots+E_{d}},

(2)

where the
E_{i}
’s are independent, mean 1 exponentials, then
{\bf q}
is uniform in
\Omega
. Moreover, if we generate points
{\bf p}_{i}

(i=1,\dots,d)
by generating the coordinates as

\displaystyle\ p_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha E_{i,j}}{\sum_{k\neq i}E_{i,k}+\alpha E_{i,i}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\neq j

(3)

\displaystyle p_{i,i}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)+\frac{\alpha^{2}E_{i,i}}{\sum_{k\neq i}E_{i,k}+\alpha E_{i,i}},

(4)

where the
E_{i,j}
s are independent mean-1 exponentials, then each
{\bf p}_{i}
is uniform in the cap
C_{i}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

The statement about
{\bf q}
is well-known and follows from the fact that the coordinate vector of a random point in
\Omega
has the same distribution as the vector of
d
gaps among
d-1
independent uniforms in
[0,1]
, and that these gaps are distributed as exponentials with a conditioned sum (see e.g.,
[
4
]
, Ch 5, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2).

Consider now a point
{\bf p}_{i}\in\Omega
which is uniform except that we condition it to lie in
C_{i}(\alpha)
. Then for any Borel subset
B
of
C_{i}(\alpha)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left({\bf p}_{i}\in B\right)=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(B\cap C_{i}(\alpha))}{\mathrm{Vol}(C_{i}(\alpha))}=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(B)}{\mathrm{Vol}(C_{i}(\alpha))},

so
{\bf p}_{i}
is uniform on
C_{i}(\alpha)
. Thus, in view of (
1
) and (
2
), the coordinates
p_{i,j}
of
{\bf p}_{i}
are distributed as

p_{i,j}\sim\frac{E_{i,j}}{E_{i,1}+\dots+E_{i,d}}\quad\mbox{conditioned on}\quad E_{i,i}\geq(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{d}E_{i,d}.

(5)

for independent mean-1 exponentials
E_{i,j}
. After solving for
E_{i,i}
, this conditioning is equivalent to conditioning on

E_{i,i}\geq\frac{(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\neq i}E_{i,j}}{\alpha}.

Note that for an exponential random variable
X
, the memoryless property implies that
X
conditioned on
X>a
has the same distribution as
X+a
. Thus, rather than the condition in (
5
), thanks to the independence of
E_{i,i}
and
\{E_{i,j}\}_{j:j\neq i}
, we could have instead replaced
E_{i,i}
in that expression with a random variable
\widehat{E}_{i}
generated as

\widehat{E}_{i}=\frac{(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\neq i}E_{i,j}}{\alpha}+E_{i,i},

and (
3
) and (
4
) follow by substitution.
∎

Lemma 2.2

(Janson
[
7
]
)
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{m}
be independent exponentials with means
\frac{1}{a_{i}},i=1,2,\ldots,m
. Let
a_{*}=\min_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
and let
W=W_{1}+W_{2}+\cdots+W_{m}
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}(W)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{a_{i}}
. Then, for any
\lambda\leq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(W\leq\lambda\mu)\leq e^{-a_{*}\mu(\lambda-1-\log\lambda)}.

2.2
The large typical set

Recall that our proof works by defining a large set of “typical” points in
\Omega
, and then showing that any such point is very unlikely to be still uncovered after exponentially many steps.

To define and work with the appropriate typical set, we will be interested in the magnitudes of the smallest coordinates of points
{\bf x}
in the set. (Roughly speaking, the typical set
\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
defined below is one where none of smallest coordinates are much too small.) For this purpose, we make the following definitions:

Definition 1
.

Given a point
{\bf x}\in\Omega
,
r_{\bf x}(i)
is the integer giving the ranking of
x_{i}
among the coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
of
{\bf x}
, where ties are broken arbitrarily. More precisely,
r_{\bf x}:\{1,\dots,d\}\to\{1,\dots,d\}
is any fixed bijection such that
r_{\bf x}(i)\leq r_{\bf x}(j)
implies
x_{i}\leq x_{j}
.

Definition 2
.

Given a point
{\bf x}\in\Omega
,
i_{\bf x}
is the integer
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
such that
i=r_{\bf x}(j)
.

In other words, if
(x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{d}^{*})
is the nondecreasing rearrangement of
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, that is
x_{1}^{*}\leq\ldots\leq x_{d}^{*}
, then
(x_{i_{\bf x}})_{i=1}^{d}=(x_{i}^{*})_{i=1}^{d}
.

We now define our typical set as follows:

\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)=\left\{{\bf x}\in\Omega:x_{i_{\bf x}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}},1\leq i\leq\gamma d\text{ and }x_{i_{\bf x}}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2d},i>\gamma d\right\},

(7)

where the coordinates of the vector
{\bm{\varepsilon}}
are defined in terms of a constant
\varepsilon>0
and by

\ \varepsilon_{i}=\begin{cases}e^{-\sqrt{d}},&1\leq i\leq\sqrt{d},\\
\varepsilon,&i>\sqrt{d}.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
\gamma<1
, there is a positive constant
c_{\gamma}
such that for every
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}
and
d
large enough, we have

\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma))}{\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)}\geq 1-e^{-c_{\gamma}\sqrt{d}}.

(8)

Proof.

Let
{\bf x}
be a random vector uniform on
\Omega
. In view of Lemma
2.1
and (
2
), the vector
(x_{i_{\bf x}})_{i=1}^{d}=(x_{i}^{*})_{i=1}^{d}
of the order statistics of
{\bf x}
has the same distribution as the vector of the order statistics of i.i.d. mean one exponentials normalised by their sum. We recall the following classical result.

Theorem 2.4

(Theorem 2.3, Chapter 5,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
be independent mean one exponential random variables and let
E_{(1)}\leq E_{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq E_{(n)}
be their order statistics, that is a nondecreasing rearrangement of the sequence
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
. Then the vector
(E_{(1)},\ldots,E_{(n)})
has the same distribution as the vector

\left(\frac{E_{1}}{n},\frac{E_{1}}{n}+\frac{E_{2}}{n-2},\ldots,\frac{E_{1}}{n}+\dots+\frac{E_{n}}{1}\right).

This gives that
(x_{i_{\bf x}})_{i=1}^{d}
has the same distribution as the vector

\left(\frac{E(d)+E(d-1)+\cdots+E(d-i+1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}jE(j)}\right)_{i=1}^{d},

(9)

where the
E(j)
’s are independent exponentials with rate
j
, that is the
jE(j)
are independent mean one exponentials. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma))}{\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left(x_{i_{x}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}},\;\forall 1\leq i\leq\gamma d\ \right)\wedge\left(x_{i_{x}}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2d},\;\forall\,\gamma d<i\leq d\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left(x_{i_{x}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}},\;\forall 1\leq i\leq\gamma d\ \right)\wedge\left(x_{(\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor+1)_{x}}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i\leq\gamma d}\mathbb{P}\left(x_{i_{x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(x_{(\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor+1)_{x}}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2d}\right).

We estimate these probabilities using Janson’s inequality (
6
). First define the event

U=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}jE(j)>\frac{8d}{5}\right\}.

By (
6
), applied with
W_{j}=jE(j)
,
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{d}=1
,
\mu=d
,
a_{*}=1
,
\lambda=\frac{8}{5}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(U\right)\leq e^{-d(\frac{8}{5}-1-\log\frac{8}{5})}<e^{-d/10}.

Now consider the events

U_{i}=\left\{E(d)+E(d-1)+\dots+E(d-i+1)\leq\frac{8\varepsilon_{i}i}{5d}\right\}.

Set

\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}(E(d)+E(d-1)+\cdots E(d-i+1))=\sum_{j=d-i+1}^{d}\frac{1}{j}.

Lower-bounding all the terms by the last one
\frac{1}{d}
, we have

\mu_{i}\geq\frac{i}{d}.

By (
6
),

\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i}\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{(d-i+1)i(1.6\varepsilon_{i}-1-\log(1.6\varepsilon_{i}))}{d}\right\}.

Since
u-1-\log u>-\frac{1}{2}\log u
for
u\leq 0.2
, we get for
i\leq\gamma d
, as long as
1.6\varepsilon\leq 0.2
,

\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i}\right)\leq(1.6\varepsilon_{i})^{(1-\gamma)i/2}.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(x_{i_{x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(U\right)\leq(1.6\varepsilon_{i})^{(1-\gamma)i/2}+e^{-0.1d}

and

\sum_{i\leq\gamma d}\mathbb{P}\left(x_{i_{x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{i}i}{d^{2}}\right)\leq\sum_{i\leq\sqrt{d}}(1.6e^{-\sqrt{d}})^{(1-\gamma)i/2}+\sum_{\sqrt{d}<i\leq\gamma d}(1.6\varepsilon)^{(1-\gamma)i/2}+\gamma de^{-0.1d}=e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{d})}.

Similarly, for
i=\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor+1
, we get
\mu_{i}\geq\frac{i}{d}\geq\gamma
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(x_{(\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor+1)_{x}}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(E(d)+\ldots+E(d-i+1)\leq 0.8\gamma\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(U\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(d-i+1)\gamma(0.8-1-\log 0.8)}+e^{-0.1d}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-0.02d(1-\gamma)\gamma}+e^{-d/10}.

Putting these bounds together finishes the proof.
∎

2.3
A lightly conditioned candidate simplex

We now fix an arbitrary
{\bf x}\in\Omega(\varepsilon,\gamma)
, and consider choosing a
{\bf p}_{i}
randomly from
C_{i}(\alpha)
, for some
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, using Lemma
2.1
. To use
{\bf p}_{i}
as the vertex of a candidate simplex to contain
{\bf x}
, we hope to find that

p_{i,j_{\bf x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{j}j}{2d^{2}}\leq x_{j_{\bf x}}/2,\quad\text{where}\quad j=1,2,\ldots,\gamma d

(10)

runs over the smallest
\gamma d
coordinates of
{\bf x}
; recall that
j_{\bf x}
denotes the coordinate of the
j
th smallest component of
{\bf x}
. Indeed, we will later argue that conditioning on this event for every
i
, the random points
{\bf p}_{1},\dots,{\bf p}_{d}
would have a reasonable chance of containing
{\bf x}
in their convex hull. The following lemma shows that we can ensure that (
10
) is not too unlikely to be satisfied, without much conditioning on the random variables
E_{i,j_{\bf x}}
for
j>\gamma d
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{6}
and
2\varepsilon\gamma\leq 5\alpha
. Let
{\bf x}\in\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
and let
{\bf p}_{i}
be chosen randomly from
C_{i}(\alpha)
for some fixed
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, as in Lemma
2.1
. Then for the event

{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}=\bigg\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\neq i\\
r_{\bf x}(k)>\gamma d\end{subarray}}{E_{i,k}}\geq\frac{4d}{5}\bigg\}

(11)

and an event
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
depending only on the
E_{i,j}
for which
r_{\bf x}(j)\leq\gamma d
(and so independent of
{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
), we have

\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{5e\alpha}\right)^{\gamma d}e^{-d},\quad\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{B}}_{{i,{\bf x}}}\right)\geq 1-e^{-10^{-4}d},

(12)

and

{\mathcal{C}}_{{i,{\bf x}}}\supseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{{i,{\bf x}}}\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}},

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{{i,{\bf x}}}
is the event that

\forall(1\leq j\leq\gamma d,\,j_{\bf x}\neq i)\ p_{i,j_{\bf x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{j}j}{2d^{2}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{{i,{\bf x}}}=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{\alpha E_{i,j_{\bf x}}}{\alpha E_{i,i}+\sum_{k\neq i}{E_{i,k}}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{j}j}{2d^{2}},\quad\forall\;1\leq j\leq\gamma d,j_{\bf x}\neq i\right\}

\displaystyle\supseteq

\displaystyle\left\{\alpha E_{i,j_{\bf x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{j}{j}}{2d^{2}}\sum_{k\neq i,{j_{\bf x}}}{E_{i,k}},\quad\forall\;1\leq j\leq{\gamma}d,j_{\bf x}\neq i\right\}

\displaystyle\supseteq

\displaystyle\left\{\alpha E_{i,{j_{\bf x}}}\leq\frac{{2}\varepsilon_{j}{j}}{{5d}},\quad\forall\;1\leq j\leq{\gamma}d,j_{\bf x}\neq i\right\}\cap\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{j}>\gamma d}\\
j_{{\bf x}}\neq i\end{subarray}}{E_{i,j_{{\bf x}}}}\geq\frac{4d}{5}\right\},

The second event in the last line is
{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
, and we define
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
to be the first event in the last line. We have the claimed probability bound on
{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
from Lemma
2.2
. Indeed, for the mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{j}>\gamma d}\\
j_{{\bf x}}\neq i\end{subarray}}{E_{i,j_{{\bf x}}}}
, we have
\mu\geq(1-\gamma)d
, so (
6
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{j}>\gamma d}\\
j_{{\bf x}}\neq i\end{subarray}}{E_{i,j_{{\bf x}}}}\leq\frac{4d}{5}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{{j}>\gamma d}\\
j_{{\bf x}}\neq i\end{subarray}}{E_{i,j_{{\bf x}}}}\leq\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\mu\left(\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}-1-\log\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-d(1-\gamma)\left(\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}-1-\log\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}\right)\right\}

and for
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{6}
, we have
(1-\gamma)\left(\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}-1-\log\frac{4}{5(1-\gamma)}\right)>10^{-4}
.

For
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bf x},i}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha E_{i,{j_{\bf x}}}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon_{j}{j}}{5d},\quad\forall\;1\leq j\leq d,j_{\bf x}\neq i\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq\gamma d\\
j_{\bf x}\neq i\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i,{j_{\bf x}}}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon_{j}{j}}{5\alpha d}\right)

\displaystyle{=}

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq\gamma d\\
j_{\bf x}\neq i\end{subarray}}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon_{j}j}{5\alpha d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq\gamma d\\
j_{\bf x}\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon_{j}j}{5\alpha d}

for
2\varepsilon\gamma\leq 5\alpha
, since
1-e^{-b}\geq b-\frac{b^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{b}{2}
for
b\leq 1
.
Thus we have

\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}})\geq{\frac{\lfloor\gamma d\rfloor!}{(5\alpha d)^{\gamma d}}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq\gamma d\\
j_{\bf x}\neq i\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{j}}\geq\frac{(\gamma d/e)^{\gamma d-1}}{(5\alpha d)^{\gamma d}}\cdot\left(e^{-\sqrt{d}}\right)^{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\gamma d}\geq\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{5e\alpha}\right)^{\gamma d}e^{-d}.\qed

As a consequence of Lemma
2.5
, we will have that if we sample exponentially many points in
C_{i}(\alpha)
, we will with probability at least
1-e^{-d}
have at least one one point
{\bf p}_{i}
for which the corresponding event
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
occurs. In particular, we will with probability at least
1-de^{-d}
have one such point
{\bf p}_{i}
for each
i=1,\dots,d
. Furthermore, with probability
1-de^{-10^{-4}d}
, we have that all the corresponding events
{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
occur. These points
{\bf p}_{1},\dots,{\bf p}_{d}
form the vertices of a candidate simplex; note that the Lemma gives us that these points
{\bf p}_{i}
satisfy
p_{i,j_{\bf x}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{j_{\bf x}}j_{\bf x}}{2d^{2}}
for all
1\leq j_{\bf x}\leq\gamma d
,
j\neq i
. In the next section, we show that they are not too unlikely to contain the fixed vertex
{\bf x}\in\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
. In particular, this will mean that after collecting exponentially many such simplices (in time
\text{exponential}(d)\cdot N=\text{exponential}(d)
), the probability that
{\bf x}
is not covered by any such simplex will be exponentially small.

2.4
Enclosing a fixed
{\bf x}\in\Omega(\varepsilon,\gamma)

In this section we show that for any fixed
{\bf x}\in\Omega(\varepsilon,\gamma)
, it is only exponentially unlikely to be contained in a simplex whose vertices
{\bf p}_{i},\,i=1,2,\ldots,d
are each chosen randomly from the corresponding set
C_{i}(\alpha)
.

In particular, our goal in this section is to prove:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{6}
and
2\varepsilon\gamma\leq 5\alpha
. Fix
{\bf x}\in\Omega(\varepsilon,\gamma)
and suppose that for each
i=1,\dots,d
, the point
{\bf p}_{i}
is chosen randomly from
C_{i}(\alpha)
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
be the events from Lemma
2.5
and let
{\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}=\bigcap_{i=d}^{n}{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left({\bf x}\in\conv\{{\bf p}_{1},\ldots,{\bf p}_{d}\}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\geq\delta_{d},

where

\delta_{d}=\left(1-\frac{5\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^{(1-\gamma)d}-de^{-10^{-4}d}.

We define the matrix
{\cal P}=p_{j,i}
whose rows are the random points
{\bf p}_{i}
, and write
{\cal P}={\mathcal{D}}+{\mathcal{R}}
where
{\mathcal{D}}
is the diagonal of
{\cal P}
, and
{\cal M}={\mathcal{D}}^{-1}{\mathcal{R}}
.

We will apply Gershgorin’s Circle Theorem to this matrix
{\cal M}
.

Theorem 2.7

(Gershgorin)
.

Suppose
{\cal M}=[m_{ij}]
is a real or complex
d\times d
matrix where for each
i=1,\dots,d
,
R_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}|m_{ij}|
is the sum of the absolute values of the non-diagonal entries of the
i
th row, and the
i
th
Gershgorin disc

D_{i}
is the disc of radius
R_{i}
centered at
m_{ii}
. Then every eigenvalue of
{\cal M}
lies in one of the Gershgorin discs (and, applying this to
{\cal M}^{T}
, the same applies where we define the Gershgorin discs with respect to the columns).∎

In particular, we use it to prove the following statement.

Lemma 2.8
.

We have that

{\cal P}^{-1}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}{\cal M}^{k}\right){\mathcal{D}}^{-1}.

(13)

Proof.

Observe that if the sum in (
13
) converges, then we can write

{\cal P}^{-1}=({\cal I}+{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}{\mathcal{R}})^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}({\mathcal{D}}^{-1}{\mathcal{R}})^{k}\right){\mathcal{D}}^{-1}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}{\cal M}^{k}\right){\mathcal{D}}^{-1}.

(14)

Thus it remains just to prove that the sum converges. Recall first from the definition of
C_{i}(\alpha)
in (
1
) that diagonal entries of
{\cal P}
are all at least
1-\alpha
, while the sum of each row is 1. In particular, Gershgorin’s Circle Theorem implies the eigenvalues of
{\cal M}
have absolute value at most
\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
, which is less than 1 assuming
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.

Next we argue that
{\cal M}
is a.s. diagonalizeable. This is the case if the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of
{\cal M}
is nonzero. This discriminant is a polynomial expression involving only products of the off-diagonal entries of
{\cal M}
; in particular, it is nonzero with probability 1.

Thus finally we write
{\cal M}=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}
and
{\cal M}^{k}=Q\Lambda^{k}Q^{-1}
. This converges exponentially fast, confirming convergence of the sum and thus the lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

As the
{\bf p}_{i}
lie in general position, we can always write the given
{\bf x}
(uniquely) as a linear combination

{\bf x}=\lambda_{1}{\bf p}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}{\bf p}_{d};

our goal is to show that given
\bigcap_{i}{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
, there is probability at least
c^{d}
for some
c>0
that the
\lambda_{i}
are all nonnegative. Observe that these coefficients are determined as

{\bm{\lambda}}={\bf x}{\cal P}^{-1}.

From (
13
), we can write

{\bf x}{\cal P}^{-1}={\bf x}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}{\cal M}^{k}\right){\mathcal{D}}^{-1}={\bf x}\big(I-{\cal M}\big)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal M}^{2k}\right){\mathcal{D}}^{-1},

(15)

which is nonnegative so long as
{\bf x}(I-{\cal M})
is, since
{\cal M}
has only nonnegative entries.

Note that the
j
th coordinate
y_{j}
of the product
{\bf y}={\bf x}(I-{\cal M})
is given by

y_{j}=x_{j}-\sum_{i:i\neq j}D_{i,i}^{-1}p_{i,j}x_{i}.

(16)

Thus

\mathbb{P}\left({\bf x}\in\conv\{{\bf p}_{1},\ldots,{\bf p}_{d}\}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(y_{j}\geq 0:\,\forall j\leq d\big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right).

Recall from Lemma
2.5
the events
{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
which are all independent of the events
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{{\bf x}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}{\mathcal{B}}_{i,{\bf x}}
. Each of the values of
j
corresponding to small coordinates of
{\bf x}
—that is the
j
for which
r_{\bf x}(j)\leq\gamma d
—must satisfy
y_{j}\geq 0
if
{\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}
occurs. Indeed, from Lemma
2.5
, we know that for all
i\neq j
we have
p_{i,j}\leq x_{j}/2
in this case, and so in particular we have that

y_{j}=x_{j}-\sum_{i\neq j}D_{i,i}^{-1}p_{i,j}x_{i}\geq x_{j}-\frac{x_{j}}{2(1-\alpha)}\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}\geq x_{j}-\frac{x_{j}}{2(1-\alpha)}>0

(17)

(by (
4
), we have
D_{i,i}\geq 1-\alpha
). This shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(y_{j}\geq 0:\forall j\leq d\big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left((y_{j}\geq 0:\forall j\leq d)\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left((y_{j}\geq 0:\forall j\leq d\text{ s.t. }r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d)\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right).

It therefore remains to handle the case of
r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d
. On the event
{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}
, for
i\neq j
, we have
p_{i,j}\leq\frac{5\alpha}{4d}E_{i,j}
(recall (
3
)), thus, bounding
D_{i,i}^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\leq 2
(see (
22
)) and using that
x_{j}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2d}
for
j
such that
r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d
, we obtain (by using the first equality in (
17
)) that

\mathbb{P}\left((y_{j}\geq 0:\forall j\leq d,r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d)\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\\
\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\sum_{i:i\neq j}E_{i,j}x_{i}\leq\frac{\gamma}{5\alpha}:\forall j\leq d\text{ s.t. }r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d\right)\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right).

By a simple inequality
\mathbb{P}\left(A\cap B\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(B^{c}\right)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(y_{j}\geq 0:\forall j\leq d\ \ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i:i\neq j}E_{i,j}x_{i}\leq\frac{\gamma}{5\alpha}:\forall j\leq d\text{ s.t. }r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}^{c}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)

The fact that the
E_{i,j}

r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d
are not conditioned by
{\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}
, Markov’s inequality and independence yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i:i\neq j}E_{i,j}x_{i}\leq\frac{\gamma}{5\alpha}:\;\forall j\leq d\text{ s.t. }r_{\bf x}(j)>\gamma d\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\\

The independence of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}
, a simple union bound and (

) yields

\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}^{c}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{B}}_{\bf x}^{c}\right)\leq de^{-10^{-4}d}.

Altogether,

\mathbb{P}\left({\bf x}\in\conv\{{\bf p}_{1},\ldots,{\bf p}_{d}\}\ \big|\ {\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{5\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^{(1-\gamma)d}-de^{-10^{-4}d}.

(18)

∎

2.5
Covering most of the simplex in exponentially many steps

We are now ready to combine the ingredients to prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Recall that we draw
N
random points
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\dots,{\bf q}_{N}
independently and uniformly from the simplex
\Omega
and
Q_{N}
denotes their convex hull. First, note that by Fubini’s theorem, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})=\mathbb{E}\int_{\Omega}\textbf{1}_{\{x\in Q_{N}\}}dx=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)dx\geq\int_{\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)dx,

(19)

where
\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
is the typical set defined in (
7
). Fix
x\in\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma)
. By Lemma
2.6
, we will have a good lower bound on
\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)
provided we know that among the
{\bf q}_{i}
there are
d
points, one from each cap
C_{i}(\alpha)
which moreover fulfill the events
{\mathcal{A}}_{\bf x}
. To use that, we condition on all possibilities for the
{\bf q}_{i}
and then argue that the majority of the possibilities are
good
, provided
N
is large enough. Formally, given two sequences
l=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}^{N}
and
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N})\in\{0,1\}^{N}

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{l,\theta}=\bigcap_{j\leq N}\Bigg\{\text{if $l_{j}=0$, then }q_{j}\notin\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}C_{i}(\alpha);\

\displaystyle\text{if $l_{j}>0$, then }q_{j}\in C_{l_{j}}(\alpha)

{\bf q}_{j}
fall in the caps and among those which satisfy
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
. Let

be the set of those pairs of sequences
(l,\theta)
for which there are
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{d}\leq N
such that
\{l_{j_{1}},\ldots,l_{j_{N}}\}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\theta_{j_{1}}=\ldots=\theta_{j_{d}}=1
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)=\sum_{l,\theta}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\ |\ {\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)\geq\sum_{(l,\theta)\in\textsf{Good}}\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\ |\ {\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right).

For
(l,\theta)\in\textsf{Good}
, by Lemma
2.6
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\ |\ {\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)\geq\delta_{d}
, so it remains to estimate the sum
\sum_{(l,\theta)\in\textsf{Good}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)
. Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and let
S_{i}
be the number of points among the
{\bf q}_{j}
which are in
C_{i}(\alpha)
and satisfy
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}
. We have,

\sum_{(l,\theta)\in\textsf{Good}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}>0:\;\forall i\leq d\right)

By independence,

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=0\right)=\Big(1-\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\bf q}_{1}\in C_{i}(\alpha)\right)\Big)^{N},

where
\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{i,{\bf x}}\right)
is taken with respect to the uniform probability on
C_{i}(\alpha)
. Therefore, by (
12
) and a union bound,

\sum_{(l,\theta)\in\textsf{Good}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal E}_{l,\theta}\right)\geq 1-d\cdot\Bigg(1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{5e\alpha}\right)^{\gamma d}e^{-d}\alpha^{d-1}\Bigg)^{N}.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)\geq\delta_{d}\cdot\left(1-d\cdot\exp\left\{-N\cdot\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{5e\alpha}\right)^{\gamma d}e^{-d}\alpha^{d-1}\right\}\right).

(20)

Set
\gamma=\frac{1}{6}
and then choose
\alpha
to be a small enough constant such that

\delta_{d}=\left(1-\frac{5\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^{(1-\gamma)d}-de^{-10^{-4}d}>e^{-10^{-4}d}.

(21)

Choose
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}
(allowing the use of Lemma
2.3
later) such that
2\varepsilon\gamma\leq 5\alpha
(allowing the use of Lemma
2.5
). Then we take
N=C_{1}^{d}
with

large enough so that the exponential term in (

d\cdot\exp\left\{-N\cdot\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{5e\alpha}\right)^{\gamma d}e^{-d}\alpha^{d-1}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

We then have

\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{N}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-10^{-4}d}.

Then, by independence, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(x\in Q_{2^{d}N}\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(x\notin Q_{N}\right)^{C_{2}^{d}}\geq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-10^{-4}d}\right)^{2^{d}}\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}e^{-10^{-4}d}\cdot 2^{d}\right\}>1-e^{-d}.

Finally, thanks to (

2.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{C_{1}^{d}N})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega({\bm{\varepsilon}},\gamma))(1-e^{-d})\geq 1-e^{-c_{0}\sqrt{d}},

for a positive universal constant

.
∎

Proof of the lower bound: Theorem
1.7

Since the quantity
\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{d})}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})
is affine invariant, we can work with the standard orthogonal simplex
S_{d}
in
\Re^{d}
instead of
\Omega_{d}

The following fundamental lemma from
[
5
]
is a starting point.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Suppose
{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. copies of a continuous random vector
{\bf q}
in
\Re^{d}
. Define a random polytope
Q_{N}=\text{conv}\{{\bf q}_{1},\ldots,{\bf q}_{N}\}
and consider the function
\xi=\xi_{\bf q}
defined by

\xi(x)=\inf\{\mathbb{P}\left({\bf q}\in H\right),\ H\text{ half-space containing $x$}\},\qquad x\in\Re^{d}.

(23)

Then for every subset
A
of
\Re^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})\leq\mathrm{Vol}(A)+N\cdot\left(\sup_{A^{c}}\xi\right)\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(A^{c}\cap\{x\in\Re^{d},\ \xi(x)>0\})

(24)

and

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Vol}(Q_{N})\geq\mathrm{vol}(A)\left(1-2\binom{N}{d}\left(1-\inf_{A}\xi\right)^{N-d}\right).

(25)

We will only need the first part of Lemma
3.1
, that is (
24
), which will be applied to sets of the form
A=\{x\in\Re^{d},\ \xi(x)>\lambda\}
, the (convex) level sets of the function
\xi
. To get an upper bound on the volume of such sets, we shall use a standard lemma concerning the Legendre transform
\Lambda_{\bf q}^{\star}
of the log-moment generating function
\Lambda_{\bf q}
of
{\bf q}
,

\Lambda_{\bf q}(x)=\log\mathbb{E}e^{\left\langle{\bf q},x\right\rangle}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{\bf q}^{\star}(x)=\sup_{\theta\in\Re^{d}}\left\{\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle-\Lambda_{\bf q}(\theta)\right\}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
\alpha>0
, we have

\{x\in\Re^{d},\ \xi_{\bf q}(x)>e^{-\alpha}\}\subset\{x\in\Re^{d},\ \Lambda_{\bf q}^{\star}(x)<\alpha\}.

(26)

Proof.

Plainly, for the infimum in the definition (
23
) of
\xi_{\bf q}(x)
, it is enough to take half-spaces for which
x
is on the boundary, that is

\xi_{\bf q}(x)=\inf_{\theta\in\Re^{d}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle{\bf q}-x,\theta\right\rangle\geq 0\right),

(27)

where
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle=\sum_{i}u_{i}v_{i}
is the standard scalar product in
\Re^{d}
. By Chebyshev’s inequality for the exponential function,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle{\bf q}-x,\theta\right\rangle\geq 0\right)\leq e^{-\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle}\mathbb{E}e^{\left\langle\theta,{\bf q}\right\rangle}.

Consequently,
\xi_{\bf q}(x)\leq e^{-\Lambda_{\bf q}^{*}(x)}
.
∎

The next lemma is a crucial bound on the moment generating function
\Lambda_{\bf q}
for
{\bf q}
uniform on the simplex
S_{d}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
{\bf q}
be a random vector uniform on
S_{d}
. For every
\theta\in(-\infty,d)^{d}
and
d\geq 7
, we have

\mathbb{E}e^{\left\langle\theta,{\bf q}\right\rangle}\leq d\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-\theta_{i}/d}.

Proof.

We have,

\mathbb{E}e^{\left\langle\theta,{\bf q}\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(S_{d})}\int_{S_{d}}e^{\sum\theta_{i}x_{i}}dx=d!\int_{\{x\in(0,\infty)^{d},\ \sum x_{i}\leq 1\}}e^{\sum\theta_{i}x_{i}}dx.

A change of variables
x_{i}=y_{i}/d
and a simple pointwise estimate
1\leq e^{d-\sum y_{i}}
valid on the domain of the integration yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\left\langle\theta,{\bf q}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq d!d^{-d}\int_{\{y\in(0,\infty)^{d},\ \sum y_{i}\leq d\}}e^{\sum\theta_{i}y_{i}/d}e^{d-\sum y_{i}}dy

\displaystyle\leq d!d^{-d}e^{d}\int_{(0,\infty)^{d}}e^{\sum-(1-\theta_{i}/d)y_{i}}dy

\displaystyle=(d!d^{-d}e^{d})\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-\theta_{i}/d}.

Finally,
d!<\sqrt{2\pi d}d^{d}e^{-d}e^{\frac{1}{12d}}
. For
d\geq 7
, we have
\sqrt{2\pi d}e^{\frac{1}{12d}}\leq d
.
∎

1.7
.

. Let
\xi

{\bf q}

\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathrm{vol}(Q_{N})}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{d})}\leq\frac{\mathrm{vol}(A)}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{d})}+e^{-\varepsilon d/2}

By (
26
),

\frac{\mathrm{vol}(A)}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{d})}=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi({\bf q})>e^{-\alpha d}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\Lambda^{\star}({\bf q})<\alpha d\right).

By Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\star}(x)\geq\sup_{\theta\in(-\infty,d)^{d}}\left\{\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle-\log\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-\theta_{i}/d}-\log d\right\}

\displaystyle=-\log d+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sup_{\theta_{i}<d}\left\{\theta_{i}x_{i}+\log\left(1-\frac{\theta_{i}}{d}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=-\log d+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(x_{i}d),

where

\psi(t)=t-1-\log t,\qquad t>0.

As a result, for
d
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\Lambda^{\star}({\bf q})<\alpha d\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(q_{i}d)<\alpha+\frac{\log d}{d}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(q_{i}d)<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)

(here the
{\bf q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})
, so the
q_{i}
are the components of
{\bf q}
). To finish the proof, it remains to argue that the right hand side is
o(1)
. We use the fact that
{\bf q}
has the same distribution as the vector
(\frac{Y_{1}}{Z},\ldots,\frac{Y_{d}}{Z})
, where
Z=Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{d}+W
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d},W
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter
1
(which can be deduced e.g. from (
2
) of Lemma
2.1
). For
\delta\in(0,1)
(to be chosen later), we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(q_{i}d)<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi\left(d\frac{Y_{i}}{Z}\right)<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi\left(d\frac{Y_{i}}{Z}\right)<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4},\ Z\in((1-\delta)d,(1+\delta)d)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(Z<(1-\delta)d\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Z>(1+\delta)d\right).

The last two probabilities are exponentially small (which can be argued in a number of ways, e.g. using Lemma
2.2
, Bernstein’s inequality, or estimates for the incomplete gamma function). To handle the first probability, we decompose
\psi
as follows

\psi(t)=\psi_{1}(t)+\psi_{2}(t),

where
\psi_{1}(t)=\psi(t)\textbf{1}_{(0,1]}(t)
is nonincreasing and
\psi_{2}(t)=\psi(t)\textbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(t)
is nondecreasing. Having this monotonicity, if
Z\in((1-\delta)d,(1+\delta)d)
, we get
\psi\left(d\frac{Y_{i}}{Z}\right)\geq\psi_{1}\left(\frac{Y_{i}}{1-\delta}\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\frac{Y_{i}}{1+\delta}\right)
. Thus, setting

f(t)=\psi_{1}\left(\frac{t}{1-\delta}\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\frac{t}{1+\delta}\right),

we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi\left(d\frac{Y_{i}}{Z}\right)<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4},\ Z\in((1-\delta)d,(1+\delta)d)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(Y_{i})<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right).

It remains to find the mean of
f(Y_{1})
and use the law of large numbers. We have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(Y_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\delta}\psi\left(\frac{t}{1-\delta}\right)e^{-t}dt+\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\psi\left(\frac{t}{1+\delta}\right)e^{-t}dt

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)e^{-t}e^{\delta t}dt+(1+\delta)\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(t)e^{-t}e^{-\delta t}dt

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)e^{-t}dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(t)e^{-t}(1-\delta t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(t)e^{-t}dt-\delta\left(\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)e^{-t}dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(t)te^{-t}dt\right).

Since

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(t)e^{-t}dt=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\log tdt=\gamma

(which was derived by Euler – see (2.2.8) in the survey
[
8
]
)
and

\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)e^{-t}dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(t)te^{-t}dt<1,

we can conclude that

\mathbb{E}f(Y_{1})>\gamma-\delta.

Choosing, say
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
, we thus get

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(Y_{i})<\gamma-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(Y_{i})<\mathbb{E}f(Y_{1})-\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)

and by the (weak) law of large numbers, the right hand side converges to
0
as
d\to\infty
.
∎
