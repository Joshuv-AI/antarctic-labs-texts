The Lefschetz defect of Fano varieties
C. Casagrande

Abstract




Smooth, complex Fano varieties are a very natural class of projective varieties, and have been intensively studied both classically and in recent times, due to their significance in the framework of the Minimal Model Program.

The Lefschetz defect is an invariant of smooth Fano varieties that has been recently introduced in
[
7
]
; it is related to the Picard number
\rho_{X}
of
X
, and to the Picard number of prime divisors in
X
.
This paper is a survey on this new invariant and its properties: we explain the definition of the Lefschetz defect
\delta_{X}
and its origin,
the known results, and several examples, especially in dimensions
3
and
4
; we do not give new theoretical results.

In particular, we explain how the results on the Lefschetz defect allow to recover the classification of Fano
3
-folds with
\rho_{X}\geq 5
, due to Mori and Mukai. We also review the known examples of Fano
4
-folds with
\rho_{X}\geq 6
, that are remarkably few:
apart products and toric examples, there are only 6 known families, with
\rho_{X}\in\{6,7,8,9\}
.

1.
Glossary

We collect here the notation and terminology used in the paper; we suggest the reader to skip this section, and come back when needed.

Let
X
be a projective variety.

\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
(respectively
\mathcal{N}^{1}(X)
) is the vector space of one-cycles (respectively Cartier divisors) in
X
, with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
, up to numerical equivalence.

[\,\text{-}\,]
stands for the numerical equivalence class of a curve or a divisor.

\rho_{X}=\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)=\dim\mathcal{N}^{1}(X)
is the Picard number of
X
.

\operatorname{NE}(X)
is the cone of effective curves in
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
, an extremal ray
R
of
\operatorname{NE}(X)
is a one-dimensional face.
If
D
is a divisor,
D\cdot R>0
means
D\cdot\gamma>0
for any non-zero class
\gamma\in R
.

A contraction is a surjective morphism with connected fibers
\varphi\colon X\to Y
between normal projective varieties;
\varphi
is elementary if
\rho_{X}-\rho_{Y}=1
.

A conic bundle is a contraction of fiber type such that every fiber is isomorphic to a plane conic.

A small modification is a birational map
X\dasharrow X^{\prime}
that is an isomorphism in codimension one.

When
X
is smooth and Fano, or more generally of Fano type,

2.
The definition of Lefschetz defect and the case
\delta_{X}\geq 4

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety. We introduce an invariant of
X
, the Lefschetz defect, which relates the Picard number of
X
to that of its prime divisors.

Given a prime divisor
D
in
X
, let us consider the natural pushforward
\iota_{*}\colon\mathcal{N}_{1}(D)\to\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
given by the inclusion
\iota\colon D\hookrightarrow X
, and set

\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X):=\iota_{*}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D))\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{1}(X).

This is the linear span, in
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
, of classes of curves contained in
D
. The Lefschetz defect of
X
is defined as:

\delta_{X}:=\max\bigl\{\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\,|\,D\subset X\text{ a prime divisor}\bigr\}.

Remarks

1.

The linear subspace
\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
is always non-zero,
because if
C\subset D
is any curve, we have
[C]\neq 0
in
\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
. Therefore

\delta_{X}\in\{0,\dotsc,\rho_{X}-1\}
; in particular we see that
\delta_{X}=0
whenever
\rho_{X}=1
.

2.

When
\dim X=2
, a prime divisor
D\subset X
is an irreducible curve, and
\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=\mathbb{R}[D]
, thus
\delta_{X}=\rho_{X}-1
.

3.

We can consider the dual picture:
let
D\subset X
be a prime divisor, and consider the restriction
r_{D}\colon H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})\to H^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})
; then we have
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=\dim\ker r_{D}
.

Indeed there is a commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{N}^{1}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{N}^{1}(D)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{r}_{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\wr}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{D}}

where
\tilde{r}_{D}
is dual to
\iota_{*}
, and
\mathcal{N}^{1}(X)\cong H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})
because
X
is Fano.

4.

One can also define the Lefschetz defect as:

\delta_{X}=\max\bigl\{\dim\ker\bigl(r_{D}\colon H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})\to H^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})\bigr)\,|\,D\text{ an effective divisor in }X\bigr\}

(where with a slight abuse of notation we identify
D
with its support).

Indeed let
D
be an effective divisor, and
D_{1}
an irreducible component of
D
. Then
r_{D_{1}}
factors as
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})\to H^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})\to H^{2}(D_{1},\mathbb{R})
, so that
\ker r_{D}\subseteq\ker r_{D_{1}}
, and the maximum above is always attained for some prime divisor.

If
\dim X\geq 3
and
D
is ample, then
r_{D}
is injective by Lefschetz’s theorem on hyperplane sections, so that the Lefschetz defect depends on non-ample effective divisors, and measures the failure of this instance of Lefschetz’s theorem.

Lemma 5

(the Lefschetz defect of a product variety)
.

If
X\cong S\times T
, then
\delta_{X}=\max\{\delta_{S},\delta_{T}\}
.

In particular, if
S
is a surface, then
\delta_{X}=\max\{\rho_{S}-1,\delta_{T}\}
.

Proof.

We can assume
that
\delta_{S}\geq\delta_{T}
. Let
D_{S}\subset S
be a prime divisor with
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{S},S)=\delta_{S}
. Then
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{S}\times T,X)=\delta_{S}
, so that
\delta_{X}\geq\delta_{S}
.

Conversely,
let
D\subset X
be a prime divisor, and
\pi\colon X\to S
the projection.

If
\pi(D)\subsetneq S
, then
D=\pi(D)\times T
and
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(\pi(D),S)\leq\delta_{S}
.

Suppose instead that
\pi(D)=S
, and consider the pushforward
\pi_{*}\colon\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)\to\mathcal{N}_{1}(S)
. Then we have

\pi_{*}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X))=\mathcal{N}_{1}(S)
, and hence
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=\rho_{S}+\dim(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\cap\ker\pi_{*})
.

Let
p\in S
; we have a natural identification
\mathcal{N}_{1}(T)\cong\mathcal{N}_{1}(\pi^{-1}(p),X)=\ker\pi_{*}
. Moreover
\pi^{-1}(p)\cap D
is either
T
or a divisor in
T
; in both cases
\dim(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\cap\ker\pi_{*})\geq\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(\pi^{-1}(p)\cap D,T)\geq\rho_{T}-\delta_{T}\geq\rho_{T}-\delta_{S}
, so that

\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\geq\rho_{X}-\delta_{S}
, and we conclude that
\delta_{X}=\delta_{S}
.

Finally if
\dim S=2
we have
\delta_{S}=\rho_{S}-1
by Remark
2
.
∎

Example 6

(
\delta_{X}
and disjoint divisors)
.

Let
D,E_{1},\dotsc,E_{r}\subset X
be prime divisors, and assume that
D\cap(E_{1}\cup\cdots\cup E_{r})=\emptyset
. If the classes
[E_{1}],\dotsc,[E_{r}]\in\mathcal{N}^{1}(X)
are linearly independent, then
\delta_{X}\geq\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\geq r
.

Indeed for every curve
C\subset D
we have
E_{i}\cdot C=0
for every
i=1,\dotsc,r
, so that
[C]\in E_{1}^{\perp}\cap\cdots\cap E_{r}^{\perp}
(where
E_{i}^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)
is the hyperplane of classes orthogonal to
E_{i}
), and finally
\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\subseteq E_{1}^{\perp}\cap\cdots\cap E_{r}^{\perp}
.

For instance, as soon as
X
contains two disjoint prime divisors, we have
\delta_{X}>0
.

The next example concerns the case
\dim X=3
, and shows how birational geometry can be used to find prime divisors
D
with small
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
.

Example 7

(
[
17
]
, Lemma 5.1)
.

Let
X
be a Fano
3
-fold.
We show that
X
always contains a prime divisor
D
with
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\leq 2
, so that
\delta_{X}\in\{\rho_{X}-2,\rho_{X}-1\}
.

Let
\varphi\colon X\to Y
be an elementary contraction. It is well-known that
\varphi
cannot be small: it is either divisorial, or of fiber type. In any case there exists a prime divisor
D\subset X
such that
\dim\varphi(D)\leq 1
.

Consider the pushforward
\varphi_{*}\colon\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)\to\mathcal{N}_{1}(Y)
; this is a surjective linear map with one-dimensional kernel. We have
\varphi_{*}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X))=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\varphi(D),Y)
; on the other hand
\mathcal{N}_{1}(\varphi(D),Y)=\{0\}
if
\varphi(D)=\{pt\}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{1}(\varphi(D),Y)=\mathbb{R}[\varphi(D)]
if
\varphi(D)
is a curve. In any case we have
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(\varphi(D),Y)\leq 1
and hence
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\leq 2
.

The following is the main property of the Lefschetz defect.

Theorem 8

(
[
7
]
, Th. 3.3)
.

For every smooth Fano variety
X
we have
\delta_{X}\in\{0,\dotsc,8\}
.

Moreover, if
\delta_{X}\geq 4
, then
X\cong S\times T
where
S
is a del Pezzo surface with

\rho_{S}=\delta_{X}+1
, and
\delta_{T}\leq\delta_{X}
.

Thus, if a Fano variety
X
is not a product, then
\delta_{X}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.

Remark 9

(bounding the Picard number of
X
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety and
D\subset X
a prime divisor.
We have
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\leq\rho_{D}
and hence

\delta_{X}\geq\rho_{X}-\rho_{D}
. Thus Theorem
8
implies that
\rho_{X}\leq\rho_{D}+8
, and if
\rho_{X}\geq\rho_{D}+4
, then
X
is a product.

Note however that this result does not immediately give an inductive bound on
\rho_{X}
in terms of the dimension of
X
, because
we do not know whether
X
contains a prime divisor
D
which is smooth and Fano.

It is indeed expected that
\rho_{X}\leq\frac{9}{2}\dim X
; this is attained in every even dimension
2m
by products
X=S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{m}
of degree one del Pezzo surfaces, which have
\rho_{X}=9m
and
\delta_{X}=8
. We will discuss the maximal Picard number for Fano
3
-folds and
4
-folds in Corollary
14
and §
6
respectively.

Remark 10

(the origin of the notion of Lefschetz defect)
.

Bonavero, Campana, and Wiśniewski
[
4
]
have classified smooth Fano varieties obtained by blowing-up a point in a smooth variety; this has been later generalized by Tsukioka
[
31
]
to smooth Fano varieties containing a divisor
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
with negative normal bundle. In both papers, the main strategy is to consider the divisor
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, an extremal ray
R
of
\operatorname{NE}(X)
such that
D\cdot R>0
, and the associated elementary contraction
\varphi\colon X\to Y
.

The pushforward
\varphi_{*}\colon\mathcal{N}_{1}(X)\to\mathcal{N}_{1}(Y)
is a surjective linear map with one-dimensional kernel.
Since
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, the space
\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
is one-dimensional, and we have two possibilities: either
\ker\varphi_{*}=\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
and
\varphi(D)=\{pt\}
, or
\ker\varphi_{*}\cap\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=\{0\}
and
\varphi
is finite on
D
. In this last case, if
F\subset X
is a non-trivial fiber of
\varphi
, we have
D\cap F\neq\emptyset
(because
D
has positive intersection with every curve contracted by
\varphi
) and
\dim(D\cap F)=0
, so that
\dim F=1
.
On the other hand,
elementary contractions of smooth Fano varieties having only fibers of dimension
\leq 1
are classified
[
1
,
32
]
: they are either a conic bundle, or a blow-up of a smooth, codimension
2
subvariety in a smooth variety.

In this way one gets a very special elementary contraction of
X
, and this argument may be iterated in the target
Y
of
\varphi
. Here the relevant property is that
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=1
, more than
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.

Later, in
[
6
]
, the author has studied Fano varieties having a birational elementary contraction
f\colon X\to X^{\prime}
sending the exceptional divisor
E
to a curve (for instance the blow-up of a smooth curve in
X^{\prime}
). It is not difficult to see that
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(E,X)=2
in this case (see Example
7
), and
prime divisors
D
with
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=2
play a special role in
[
6
]
. This assumption is still applied by studying
extremal rays
R
such that
D\cdot R>0
, and the associated elementary contractions.

Finally this led the author to generalize this strategy in

[
7
]
, using birational geometry and the MMP. We give an overview in the following section.

3.
Studying
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
via birational geometry

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety and
D\subset X
a prime divisor.
In this section we explain a construction, based on birational geometry, which allows to get information on
X
as soon as
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)>0
.
We refer the reader to
[
20
,
18
]
for the terminology in birational geometry and Mori dream spaces, and to
[
7
, §2]
for more details.

Since Fano varieties are Mori dream spaces

[
3
, Corollary 1.3.2]
, one can run a MMP for any divisor
[
7
, Proposition 2.2]
. We consider here
a MMP for
-D
, which means that we consider
extremal rays having positive intersection with
D
, as in Remark
10
. Since
-D
can never become semiample in the MMP, the program must end with a fiber type contraction. This means that we get a sequence

X=X_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma_{0}}}{{\dasharrow}}X_{1}\dasharrow\quad\cdots\quad\dasharrow X_{k-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma_{k-1}}}{{\dasharrow}}X_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y

such that:

\bullet

every
X_{i}
is a normal and
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial projective variety,
and the transform
D_{i}\subset X_{i}
of
D
is a prime divisor in
X_{i}
;

\bullet

for every
i=0,\dotsc,k-1
there exists an extremal
ray
R_{i}
of
X_{i}
such that
D_{i}\cdot R_{i}>0
,
\operatorname{Locus}(R_{i})\subsetneq X_{i}
, and

\sigma_{i}
is either the contraction of
R_{i}
(if
R_{i}
is
divisorial), or its flip (if
R_{i}
is small);

\bullet

there exists an extremal ray
R_{k}
in
X_{k}
, with a fiber type
contraction
\varphi\colon X_{k}\to Y
,
such
that
D_{k}\cdot R_{k}>0
.

Moreover, since
X
is Fano, using an MMP with scaling of
-K_{X}
, in the sequence above we can also assume that
-K_{X_{i}}\cdot R_{i}>0
for every
i=0,\dotsc,k

[
7
, Proposition 2.4]
.

Then we analyse how
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})
varies along the steps of the MMP. It is not difficult to show that, for
every
i=0,\dotsc,k-1
, we have:

\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i+1},X_{i+1})=\begin{cases}\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})\quad&\text{if $R_{i}\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})$,}\\
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})-1\quad&\text{if $R_{i}\not\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})$}\end{cases}

(see
[
7
, Lemma 2.6(3)]
).

Let us consider now the last step, given by the fiber type contraction
\varphi\colon X_{k}\to Y
. Since
D_{k}\cdot R_{k}>0
, the prime divisor
D_{k}\subset X_{k}
intersects every fiber of
\varphi
, namely
\varphi(D_{k})=Y
.
Let
\varphi_{*}\colon\mathcal{N}_{1}(X_{k})\to\mathcal{N}_{1}(Y)

be the pushforward; then

\varphi_{*}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{k},X_{k}))=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\varphi(D_{k}),Y)=\mathcal{N}_{1}(Y)
. On the other hand
\varphi
is elementary, thus
\dim\ker\varphi_{*}=1
, and we get:

\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{k},X_{k})\leq 1.

In conclusion:
we have at least
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)-1
steps where
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})
drops, namely where
R_{i}\not\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})
.
These steps are very special, let us describe them.

Fix
i\in\{0,\dotsc,k-1\}
such that
R_{i}\not\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})
, and let
\psi\colon X_{i}\to Z
be the associated elementary birational contraction (divisorial or small).

Let
F\subset X_{i}
be a non-trivial fiber of
\psi
. We have

D_{i}\cdot R_{i}>0
, thus
D_{i}
has positive intersection with every curve contracted by
\psi
, so that

D_{i}\cap F\neq\emptyset
. On the other hand, a curve
C\subset D_{i}\cap F
would have class
[C]\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})\cap R_{i}
, which is impossible because
R_{i}\not\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(D_{i},X_{i})
by assumption. We conclude that
\dim(D_{i}\cap F)=0
and

\dim F=1
.

It turns out that since
-K_{X_{i}}\cdot R_{i}>0
, the property of having fibers of dimension
\leq 1
makes
\psi
very special. If
X_{i}
is smooth, then

[
1
,
32
]
yield that
\psi
is divisorial (so that
\psi=\sigma_{i}\colon X_{i}\to X_{i+1}
)
and is the blow-up of a smooth, codimension
2
subvariety.

In general
X_{i}
is singular, but a careful analysis shows that
\psi=\sigma_{i}\colon X_{i}\to X_{i+1}
is a divisorial contraction, that
\operatorname{Exc}(\sigma_{i})
is contained in the open subset where the birational map
X\dasharrow X_{i}
is an isomorphism (in particular, it is contained in the smooth locus of
X_{i}
), and that
\sigma_{i}
is the blow-up of a smooth, codimension
2
subvariety contained in the smooth locus of
X_{i+1}

[
7
, Lemma 2.7(1)]
. For this argument, it is crucial
to have the assumption that
-K_{X_{j}}\cdot R_{j}>0
for every
j
.

Let
E_{i}\subset X
be the transform of
\operatorname{Exc}(\sigma_{i})\subset X_{i}
. Then
E_{i}
is a smooth prime divisor, with a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle structure, such that if
f_{i}\subset E_{i}
is a fiber, we have
E_{i}\cdot f_{i}=-1
,
D\cdot f_{i}>0
, and
[f_{i}]\not\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)

[
7
, Lemma 2.7(3)]
.

In the end we get the following.

Proposition 11

(
[
7
]
, Proposition 2.5)
.

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety and
D\subset X
a prime
divisor with
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)>0
.

Then there exist
pairwise disjoint smooth prime divisors
E_{1},\dotsc,E_{s}\subset X
, with

s=\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)-1
,
such that
every
E_{j}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle with
E_{j}\cdot f_{j}=-1
, where
f_{j}\subset E_{j}
is a fiber;
moreover
D\cdot f_{j}>0
and
[f_{j}]\not\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)
. In particular

E_{j}\cap D\neq\emptyset
and
E_{j}\neq D
.

This construction is the starting point of the proof of Theorem
8
. The rest of the proof
[
7
, §3]
is very technical and we will not enter into it.

Let us give now
an application to Fano varieties with
maximal Lefschetz defect.
We have seen in Remark
1
that
\delta_{X}\leq\rho_{X}-1
;
the condition
\delta_{X}=\rho_{X}-1
is equivalent to asking that
X
contains a prime divisor
D
with
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=1
, for instance a prime divisor
D
with
\rho_{D}=1
. This implies that
\rho_{X}\leq 9
by Theorem
8
, but when
\dim X\geq 3
, the stronger bound
\rho_{X}\leq 3
holds. This was originally proved in
[
31
]
; we give a different proof using the previous proposition.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety with
\dim X\geq 3
and
\delta_{X}=\rho_{X}-1
. Then
\rho_{X}\leq 3
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that
\rho_{X}\geq 4
, and let
D\subset X
be a prime divisor with
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=1
. By Proposition
11
, there exist
s=\rho_{X}-2\geq 2
pairwise disjoint prime divisors
E_{1},\dotsc,E_{s}
, all intersecting
D
, and distinct from
D
.

Since
\dim X\geq 3
, we have
\dim(D\cap E_{1})\geq 1
; let
C\subset D\cap E_{1}
be an irreducible curve.
We have
E_{2}\cdot C=0
because
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\emptyset
. On the other hand
\dim\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=1
, hence for every irreducible curve
C^{\prime}\subset D
, there exists some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

7.
Special Fano’s: index
>1
and the toric case

We recall that the
index

r_{X}
of a Fano variety is the divisibility of
-K_{X}
in
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
, namely

r_{X}=\max\{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,|\,-K_{X}\sim mH\text{ for }H\in\operatorname{Pic}(X)\}.

A related invariant is
the
pseudo-index
, defined
as

\iota_{X}=\min\{-K_{X}\cdot C\,|\,C\text{ a rational curve in }X\},

which is a multiple of
r_{X}
.

One expects “most” Fano varieties to have
r_{X}=\iota_{X}=1
, and that Fano varieties with
\iota_{X}>1
or
r_{X}>1
should be simpler. For instance, Fano 4-folds with
r_{X}>1
are classified and have
\rho_{X}\leq 4
, see
[
19
, Cor. 3.1.15, Table 12.1, §5.2, and Table 12.7]
, so that the missing step in the classification of Fano
4
-folds is the case of index
r_{X}=1
.

With respect to the Lefschetz defect, we have the following.

Theorem 26

(
[
7
]
, Th. 1.2)
.

Let
X
be a smooth Fano variety with
r_{X}>1
, or more generally with
\iota_{X}>1
. Then one of the following holds:

(i)

\delta_{X}=0
;

(ii)

\delta_{X}=1
,
\iota_{X}=2
,
r_{X}\in\{1,2\}
, and there exists a smooth morphism
X\to Y
with fiber
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, where
Y
is smooth, Fano, and
\iota_{Y}>1
.

Let us consider now the case of toric Fano varieties; we have the following.

Proposition 27
.

Every smooth toric Fano variety
X
has
\delta_{X}\leq 3
.

Moreover, if
\delta_{X}=3
, then there exist a toric Fano variety
T
and
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\in\operatorname{Pic}(T)
with

-K_{T}+L_{i}-L_{j}
ample for every
i,j=1,2,3
, such that
X
is obtained by blowing-up
Z:=\mathbb{P}_{T}(L_{1}\oplus L_{2}\oplus L_{3})
along the three torus invariant sections of the
\mathbb{P}^{2}
-bundle
Z\to T
.

Proof.

If
X
is a Fano variety with
\delta_{X}\geq 4
, then by Theorem
8
we have
X\cong S\times T
where
S
is a del Pezzo surface with
\rho_{S}=\delta_{X}+1\geq 5
. In particular
S
is not toric, as toric del Pezzo surfaces have
\rho\leq 4
, therefore
X
is not toric either.

Now if
X
is a toric Fano variety with
\delta_{X}=3
, then we can apply Theorem
17
; moreover, since
X
is toric, only case
A
can occur, and
T
must be toric too, see
[
16
, Remark 6.1]
.
∎

It is interesting to note that Proposition
27

is very much related to

[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
, proved much earlier with completely combinatorial methods. This last result says that for every torus invariant prime divisor
D\subset X
, one has
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)\leq 3
, and if there is a
D
with

\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=3
, then
X
is a toric
(\operatorname{Bl}_{3}\mathbb{P}^{2})
-bundle onto a toric Fano variety
T
.
If instead
\operatorname{codim}\mathcal{N}_{1}(D,X)=2
, then one gets a statement similar
to Theorem
21
.

8.
Beyond smooth Fano’s

The notion of Lefschetz defect can be introduced for any projective variety; anyway, the good properties of
\delta_{X}
strongly depend on the Fano assumption. Indeed, it seems that already when
X
is weak Fano there is no analog of Theorem
8
, as the following example shows.

Example 28

(a toric weak Fano
3
-fold)
.

After
[
21
]

we know that
\rho_{X}\leq 35

for every smooth, toric weak Fano
3
-fold
X
, and the bound is sharp.
One example with
\rho_{X}=35
is described in detail in

[
29
, §7]
, together with the extremal rays of
\operatorname{NE}(X)
. In particular, this
X
contains a divisor isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, so that
\delta_{X}=34
.

For singular Fano varieties, we have the following.

Theorem 29

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial Gorenstein Fano variety, with canonical singularities, and at most finitely many non-terminal points. Then
\delta_{X}\leq 8
.

Moreover, if
\delta_{X}\geq 4
, then there is a finite morphism
X\to S\times T
, where
S
and
T
are normal with rational singularities, and
\dim S=2
.

The main point where the technique used in the proof of Theorem
8
breaks, when
X
is too singular or not Fano, is the following. As explained in §
3
, the properties of elementary birational contraction with fibers of dimension
\leq 1
play a crucial role in the construction which is the starting point for the proof of the theorem. In particular, if
X
is smooth and Fano, such a contraction is always divisorial. Instead,
when
X
is weak Fano, it can have small (crepant) elementary contractions having fibers of dimension
\leq 1
. Similarly, if
X
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial Gorenstein Fano variety with canonical singularities, again it can have small elementary contractions having fibers of dimension
\leq 1
, see
[
17
, Example 2.11]
.
