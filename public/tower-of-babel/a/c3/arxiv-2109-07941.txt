Abstract
. We study mean convergence of multiple ergodic averages, where the iterates arise from smooth functions of polynomial growth that belong to a Hardy field. Our results include all logarithmico-exponential functions of polynomial growth, such as the functions t 3 / 2 , t ​ log ⁡ t t^{3/2},t\log t and e log ⁡ t e^{\sqrt{\log t}} . We show that if all non-trivial linear combinations of the functions a 1 , … , a k a_{1},...,a_{k} stay logarithmically away from rational polynomials, then the L 2 L^{2} -limit of the ergodic averages 1 N ​ ∑ n = 1 N f 1 ​ ( T ⌊ a 1 ​ ( n ) ⌋ ​ x ) ⋅ … ⋅ f k ​ ( T ⌊ a k ​ ( n ) ⌋ ​ x ) \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{1}(T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}x)\cdot...\cdot f_{k}(T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}x) exists and is equal to the product of the integrals of the functions f 1 , … , f k f_{1},...,f_{k} in ergodic systems, which establishes a conjecture of Frantzikinakis. Under some more general conditions on the functions a 1 , … , a k a_{1},...,a_{k} , we also find characteristic factors for convergence of the above averages and deduce a convergence result for weak-mixing systems.

1.1.
Statement of the problem and main results

In order to state our theorems below, we shall work with Hardy fields
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{LE}

Theorem 1

Let

\ \lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|a(t)-p(t)|}{\log t}=+\infty\text{ for any polynomial}\ p\in C\mathbb{Z}[t].

Remark
.

The above condition is sufficient, but not necessary for convergence in the single iterate case. However, it encompasses most typical functions in

Definition 1.1

We will call a function

The term 1-good indicates the relation of these functions with single ergodic averages averages.

We show that a natural extension of the above condition implies norm convergence in the case of multiple averages. If

Let

\int f_{1}\ d\mu\cdot...\cdot\int f_{k}\ d\mu.

It is a consequence of our proof that the condition on the linear combinations of the functions

If we do not impose an ergodicity assumption on the system

1.2.
Characteristic factors and the case of weak-mixing systems

If our only objective is to find characteristic factors for the averages in (
2
), we can relax the conditions of Theorem
1.2
considerably. More precisely, we have the following theorem which appeared as a conjecture in
[
11
, Problem 3]
. The notion of the Host-Kra factor of a system is defined in the following section.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
have polynomial growth and satisfy

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|a_{i}(t)|}{\log t}=+\infty\ \ \ \text{ for all }\ 1\leq i\leq k

and

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|a_{i}(t)-a_{j}(t)|}{\log t}=+\infty\ \ \ \text{ for all }\ \ i\neq j.

Then, there exists a positive integer
s
such that, for any measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}\widehat{f}_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}\widehat{f}_{k}\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0,

where
\widehat{f}_{i}:=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f_{i}|Z_{s}(X))
is the projection of
f_{i}
to the
s
-step Host-Kra factor of the system.

The conditions above are necessary (one can consider some weakly-mixing systems that are not strongly-mixing to see this).
Since for weak-mixing systems, the Host-Kra factors of any order are trivial, we get the following corollary, which extends the results in
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
where the iterates are polynomials taking integer values on the integers, as well as some of the results in
[
2
]
involving tempered functions.

Corollary 1.4
.

Assume that the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
have polynomial growth and satisfy

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|a_{i}(t)|}{\log t}=+\infty\ \ \ \text{ for all }\ 1\leq i\leq k

and

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|a_{i}(t)-a_{j}(t)|}{\log t}=+\infty\ \ \ \text{ for all }\ \ i\neq j.

Then, for any weak-mixing system
(X,\mu,T)
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k}=\int f_{1}\ d\mu\cdot...\cdot\int f_{k}\ d\mu,

where convergence takes place in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Remark
.

The proof of this restricted form of Theorem
1.3
still requires a large portion of the arguments that are used in this article.

1.3.
Combinatorial Applications

As a corollary of Theorem
1.2
, we get the following multiple recurrence result.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
a_{1},...,a_{k}
be functions from a Hardy field
\mathcal{H}
such that every non-trivial linear combination of the functions is 1-good. Then, for any measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and any set
A\subset X
with
\mu(A)>0
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ \mu(A\cap T^{-\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}A)\geq\mu(A)^{k+1}.

A similar result was established in
[
4
]
with
\limsup
in place of the limit, but under more general conditions on the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
.

Utilizing Furstenberg’s correspondence principle, we can deduce a combinatorial result about large sets of integers. First of all, we give a definition of the asymptotic density of a set.

Assume
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
. Then, we define the upper density of the set
\Lambda
as the limit

{\bar{d}}(\Lambda):=\limsup\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\Lambda\cap[1,N]|}{N}

and the lower density
\underline{d}
is defined similarly with
\liminf
instead of
\limsup
. If those limits coincide, then we say that the set
\Lambda
has natural density
d
equal to the limit.

Theorem

(Furstenberg’s correspondence principle)
.

For any set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
with positive upper density, there exists an invertible measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and a measurable set
A\subset X
, such that
\overline{d}(\Lambda)=\mu(A)
and for any
r_{1},...,r_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\bar{d}(\Lambda\cap(\Lambda-r_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(\Lambda-r_{k}))\geq\mu(A\cap T^{-r_{1}}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-r_{k}}A).

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
have positive upper density and let
a_{1},...,a_{k}
be functions from a Hardy field
\mathcal{H}
, such that every non-trivial linear combination of these functions is 1-good. Then,

\liminf\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{d}(\Lambda\cap(\Lambda-\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor)\cap\cdots\cap(\Lambda-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor))\geq(\bar{d}(\Lambda))^{k+1}.

1.4.
General overview of the proof and organization of the paper

Similarly to the work in
[
12
,
11
,
4
]
, our approach is to show that the Host-Kra factor introduced in
[
21
]
(see also
[
22
]
for a presentation of the general theory) is characteristic for convergence of our averages. This is a technique used extensively in the literature to reduce the problem of convergence in general measure preserving systems to the case where the system is a rotation in a nilpotent homogeneous space. However, we shall use a recent result of Frantzikinakis
[
14
]
which roughly asserts that in order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we only need to prove that the Host-Kra factor is characteristic for the averages in (
2
) plus some simple equidistribution results on the torus (which are simple consequences of the equidistribution results in
[
5
]
). This bypasses the usual hassle of proving convergence of the corresponding averages in nilmanifolds. We remark that this technique can only be used when we expect convergence of certain ergodic averages to the product of the integrals of the involved functions, which is the case in this article.

Furthermore, there are also several differences between our methods and the methods used in
[
4
]
and
[
12
]
to establish seminorm estimates, where a standard PET induction argument was utilized to reduce to the case of functions with sub-linear growth rate. This technique restricts the cases that can be handled, because the van der Corput operation may eventually yield functions that do not satisfy the condition (
A
) (a typical example in this case is the pair of functions
(t\log t,t\log\log t)
, which "drop" to functions that have growth rate smaller than
\log t
after applications of the van der Corput inequality). In order to overcome this, we use the fact that Hardy field functions of polynomial growth behave "locally" as polynomials. This observation was used in
[
11
]
to handle the special case of the family
\{\lfloor a(n)\rfloor,2\lfloor(a(n)\rfloor,...,k\lfloor a(n)\rfloor\}
, where there is only one Hardy field function. It was shown in this case that the corresponding multiple ergodic averages over small intervals converge to 0, provided that
f_{1}
is orthogonal to one particular Host-Kra factor of the system. This technique does not extend to the more general case that we wish to cover here. However, we can prove instead that these averages can be bounded by finite ergodic averages, where the iterates are polynomials. We use then a double averaging trick and the asserted asymptotic bounds to show that the Host-Kra factor is, indeed, characteristic for convergence of the multiple averages in our setting.
The price to pay is that our argument has to be somewhat finitary in nature and this makes the proof slightly more technical and cumbersome. We also note here that our work concerns finding bounds for ergodic averages involving families of variable polynomials. Some general convergence results regarding multiple ergodic averages of variable polynomials were recently established in
[
26
]
.

A general difficulty in the proofs is that functions in
f\in\mathcal{H}
that satisfy
f(t)\ll t^{\delta}
for all
\delta>0
(such as the functions
(\log t)^{c}
, where
c>1
) behave differently from functions that dominate some fractional power. We describe this difference more clearly in the Appendix, where we also provide several propositions and lemmas that will be used extensively in Sections
3
through
6
. In addition, we will revisit the ideas discussed above in Section
3
and also present some examples that we believe help illustrate the argument of the main proofs.

Our results do not cover the case of general convergence (not necessarily to the product of the integrals) of the averages in (
2
). In this case, the 1-good assumption on the functions can be relaxed further to include more functions, like the polynomials with integer coefficients. In order to establish this, we need to deal with the case of convergence in nilsystems, which will be done in a subsequent article.

1.5.
Some open problems

An interesting problem that arose when trying to prove our main result is whether sequences of the form
\lfloor a(n)\rfloor^{\ell}
are good for the multiple ergodic theorem, where
\ell
is a natural number. In the special case
\ell=2
, we present the following problem:

Conjecture 1
.

Let
c_{1},...,c_{k}
be distinct positive non-integers. Do the averages

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{\lfloor n^{c_{1}}\rfloor^{2}}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{c_{k}}\rfloor^{2}}f_{k}

converge in mean?

Conjecture 1 seems non-trivial even in the case where the fractional powers
n^{c_{i}}
are replaced by general (non-integer) real polynomials.
In the case
k=1
, we can use the spectral theorem and the equidistribution results presented in
[
10
]
to give a positive answer. In particular, it was proven in the same article that
\lfloor a(n)\rfloor^{k}
is good for the ergodic theorem when
a
stays logarithmically far from real multiples of integer polynomials (this is condition (
A
)). If we apply the van der Corput inequality, the resulting sequences at each step become very complicated and may oscillate substantially. As a consequence, the classical methods of finding characteristic factors do not seem to yield a result in this case.

Furthermore, we expect that the above averages are jointly ergodic for totally ergodic systems:

Conjecture 2
.

Let
c_{1},...,c_{k}
be distinct positive non-integers and let
(X,\mu,T)
be a totally ergodic system. Show that the averages

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{\lfloor n^{c_{1}}\rfloor^{2}}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor n^{c_{k}}\rfloor^{2}}f_{k}

converge to the product of the integrals
\int f_{1}\ d\mu\cdot...\cdot\int f_{k}\ d\mu
for any functions
f_{1},...,f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
.

The above problem is interesting even for weak-mixing systems.
In addition, the problem of multiple recurrence is also open. In its simplest form, we have the following open question.

Conjecture 3
.

Show that any set of positive upper density contains patterns of the form

\{m,m+\lfloor n^{a}\rfloor^{2},m+\lfloor n^{b}\rfloor^{2},\ m,n\in\mathbb{N}\},

where
a,b>1
are distinct non-integers.

In order to establish this, it may be possible to sidestep the more difficult problem of proving convergence of the corresponding ergodic averages (see, for example, the arguments in
[
16
]
). We do not concern ourselves with this here, however.

Background material

2.1.
Preliminaries on Hardy fields

Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of germs at infinity of real valued functions defined on a half-line
[x,+\infty]
. Then,
(\mathcal{B},+,\cdot)
is a ring. A sub-field
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{B}
that is closed under differentiation is called a Hardy field. We will say that
a(n)
is a Hardy sequence, if for
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough we have
a(n)=f(n)
for some function
f\in\mathcal{H}
. We will make some small abuse of language and sometimes also refer to sequences of the form
\lfloor f(n)\rfloor
as Hardy sequences.

An example of a Hardy field is the field
\mathcal{LE}
of logarithmico-exponential functions. These are the functions defined on some half line of
\mathbb{R}
by a finite combination of the operations
+,-,\cdot,\div,\ \exp
,
\log
and composition of functions acting on a real variable
t
and real constants. The set
\mathcal{LE}
contains functions such as the polynomials
p(t)
,
t^{c}
for all real
c>0
,
t\log t
,
t^{(\log t)^{2}}
and
e^{\sqrt{t}}/t^{2}
.

The main advantage when working with functions in a Hardy field (instead of just the
C^{\infty}
functions) is that any two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
are comparable. That means that the limit

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}

exists and thus it makes sense to talk about and compare their growth rates. In addition, since every function in our Hardy field has a multiplicative inverse, we can easily infer that every function in
\mathcal{H}
is eventually monotone (and, therefore, has constant sign eventually).

It will be crucial in the proof to assume that
\mathcal{H}
is closed under composition and compositional inversion of functions, when defined. More precisely, if
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
are such that
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}g(t)=+\infty
, then we have that
f\circ g\in\mathcal{H}
and
g^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}
. The Hardy field
\mathcal{LE}
does not have this property. This can be achieved by working with the Hardy field
\mathcal{P}
of Pfaffian functions
[
24
]
, which contains
\mathcal{LE}
and satisfies the previously mentioned assumptions. This field can be defined inductively as follows:

i) Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
be the set of the smooth functions satisfying the differential equation
f^{\prime}=p(t,f)
for some polynomial
p
with integer coefficients.

ii) Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}
be the set of the smooth functions satisfying the differential equation
f^{\prime}=p(t,f_{1},...,f_{k})
for some polynomial
p
with integer coefficients and
f_{i}\in P_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k-1
.
Then
\mathcal{P}
contains all germs at infinity of the set
\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

From now on, we will assume that
\mathcal{H}
has all the above properties. In the appendix, we have gathered some lemmas regarding growth rates of functions in
\mathcal{H}
, which will play a crucial role in the approximations in the following sections.

Finally, we give some definitions for functions whose growth rate is of particular interest.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
has sub-linear growth rate (or is sub-linear), if
f(t)\prec t
.
We say that a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
has sub-fractional growth rate (or is sub-fractional), if for all
\delta>0
, we have
f(t)\ll t^{\delta}
.

Typical examples of sub-linear functions are
\sqrt{t}
,
e^{\sqrt{\log t}}
and
\log^{3}(t)
. Among these, the functions
e^{\sqrt{\log t}}
and
\log^{3}(t)
are also sub-fractional, while the first one is not sub-fractional.

Definition 2.2
.

We will call a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
of polynomial growth strongly non-polynomial, if there exists a non-negative integer
d
, such that

t^{d}\prec f(t)\prec t^{d+1}.

For example, the functions
t^{3/2}
and
\log^{3}(t)
are strongly non-polynomial, while the function
t^{2}+\sqrt{t}
is not.

2.2.
Background in ergodic theory

2.2.1.
Ergodicity and factors

A measure preserving system is a probability space
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
equipped with an invertible measure preserving transformation
T
. We call a system ergodic, if the only
T
-invariant functions in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
are the constant ones. The system
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
is called weak-mixing, if the product system
(X\times X,\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X},\mu\times\mu,T\times T)
is ergodic.
We say the system
(Y,\mathcal{Y},\nu,S)
is a factor of
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
, if there exist
X^{\prime}\subset X
,
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
of full measure that are invariant under
T
and
S
respectively and a map
p:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
such that
\nu=\mu\circ p^{-1}
and
p\circ T(x)=S\circ p(x)
for all
x\in X^{\prime}
. If
p
is a bijection, we say that the two systems are isomorphic. A factor of the system
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
corresponds to a
T
-invariant sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{X}
(in the above example this
\sigma
-algebra is
p^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})
). From now on, we will omit the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
from the quadruple
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)
.

2.2.2.
Host-Kra seminorms and factors

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an invertible measure preserving system and let
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
. We define the Host-Kra uniformity seminorms inductively as follows:

\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{0,T}:=\int f\ d\mu

and, for
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

(3)

\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s+1,T}^{2^{s+1}}:=\lim\limits_{H\to\infty}\underset{0\leq h\leq H}{\mathbb{E}}\lvert\!|\!|\bar{f}\cdot T^{h}f|\!|\!\rvert_{s,T}^{2^{s}}.

The existence of the limits above was proven in
[
21
]
in the ergodic case (for the non-ergodic case, see
[
22
]
for a proof) and it was also established that the
\lvert\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!\rvert_{s}
are indeed seminorms (for
s\neq 0)
. More importantly, it was also shown in the same article that the seminorms
\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s,T}
define a factor
Z_{s-1}(X)
of
X
, which is characterized by the following property:

f\perp L^{2}(Z_{s-1}(X))\iff\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s,T}=0.

It can be shown that the factors
Z_{s}(X)
form an increasing sequence of factors. This follows from the inequality
\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s,T}\leq\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s+1,T}
, for all non-negative integers
s
. For weak-mixing systems, it can be shown that all the factors
Z_{s}(X)
are trivial.

Furthermore, it is easy to prove that
\lvert\!|\!|\bar{f}\otimes f|\!|\!\rvert_{s,T\times T}\leq\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s+1,T}^{2}
, where
\bar{f}\otimes f
denotes the function
(x,y)\to\overline{f(x)}f(y)
on
(X\times X,\mu\times\mu,T\times T)
. Finally, when there is no danger of confusion, we will omit the subscript
T
in the seminorms and write simply
\lvert\!|\!|f|\!|\!\rvert_{s}
.

2.2.3.
Joint ergodicity of sequences

Let
a_{1}(n),...,a_{k}(n)
be sequences of integers. Following the terminology in
[
14
]
, we call these sequences
jointly ergodic
, if for any ergodic measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and functions
f_{1},...,f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{a_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k}(n)}f_{k}=\int f_{1}\ d\mu\cdot....\cdot\int f_{k}\ d\mu,

where convergence takes place in
L^{2}(\mu)
. We also give the following definitions:

Definition 2.3
.

We say that a collection of sequences
a_{1},...,a_{k}
of integers:

i) is good for seminorm estimates, if for every ergodic system
(X,\mu,T)
there exists an
s\in\mathbb{N}
, such that if
f_{1},...,f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and
\lvert\!|\!|f_{\ell}|\!|\!\rvert_{s}=0
for some
\ell\in\{1,...,k\}
, then
3
3

3

In
[
14
]
, this property is called ”
very good for seminorm estimates
”.

\lim\limits_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T^{a_{1}(n)}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k}(n)}f_{k}=0

in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

ii) is good for equidistribution, if for all
t_{1},...,t_{k}\in[0,1)
, not all of them zero, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(t_{1}a_{1}(n)+\cdots+t_{k}a_{k}(n))=0.

The main result in
[
14
]
, which we are also going to use is the following:

Theorem 2
.

[
14
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
a_{1},...,a_{k}
be a collection of sequences of integers. Then, the following are equivalent:

i) The sequences
a_{1},...,a_{k}
are jointly ergodic.

ii) The sequences
a_{1},...,a_{k}
are good for seminorm estimates and good for equidistribution.

Proof that Theorem
1.2
follows from Theorem
1.3
.

Note that every 1-good function dominates the logarithmic function
\log t
. Therefore, if the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
are such that every non-trivial linear combination of them is 1-good,
then the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
are satisfied, which means that the sequences
\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor,...,\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor
are good for seminorm estimates. Therefore, due to Theorem
2
we only need to prove that they are good for equidistribution. This, however, follows from the equidistribution results in
[
5
]
and has been established in
[

, Proposition 6.3]
.
∎

3.
Characteristic factors for Hardy sequences

In this section, we present the main proposition that asserts that the Host-Kra factors of a given system are characteristic for the convergence of the averages (
2
). That means that if we substitute the functions
f_{i}
by their projections on
Z_{s}(X)
for some suitable
s\in\mathbb{N}
, then the limiting behavior of the average in (
2
) remains unchanged. We will also make some small reductions to the original problem and prove some useful lemmas. We also provide a brief overview of the proof and some examples that present the main ideas, while avoiding most of the technicalities. The following proposition will be proven in subsequent sections.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that the functions
a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
have polynomial growth and suppose that the following two conditions hold:

i) The functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
dominate the logarithmic function
\log t
.

ii) The pairwise differences
a_{i}-a_{j}
dominate the logarithmic function
\log t
for any
i\neq j
.

Then, there exists a positive integer
s
depending only on the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
, such that for any measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, functions
f_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and
f_{2,N}...,f_{k,N}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, all bounded by
1
, with
f_{1}\perp Z_{s}(X)
, the expression

(4)

\sup_{|c_{n}|\leq 1}\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}\ c_{n}\ T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{2}(n)\rfloor}f_{2,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k,N}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}

converges to 0, as
N\to+\infty
.

Remarks
.

i) It is possible to establish Proposition
3.1
under the weaker assumption that only the functions
a_{1},a_{1}-a_{2},...,a_{1}-a_{k}
dominate the logarithmic function, but this requires a few more details in the proof and is not required for the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

ii) It may be possible to establish that the number
s
does not, in fact, depend on the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
, but it can be bounded by a function involving the number
k
of functions and the highest degree
4
4

4

This means the smallest integer
d
, for which
a_{i}(t)\ll t^{d}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

d
of the involved functions. However, we do not concern ourselves here with the optimal value of
s
. In particular, we will use polynomial expansions of the functions
a_{1},...,a_{k}
with degrees very large compared to the number
d
, which means that any possible dependence on
d
will be lost in the proof.

It is obvious that Proposition
3.1
implies Theorem
1.3
(this follows from a standard telescoping argument). Therefore, all of our remaining results follow if we establish this proposition.

The reason that we work with sequences of functions and the bounded sequence
c_{n}
is because that will be helpful in some spots to absorb some of the error terms that will appear in the iterates and also allows us to "transform" the sequences in the iterates, so that we can reduce our problem to the case that the first sequence
a_{1}
has some specific properties depending on the situation. As an example, we claim that we only need to consider the case when the function
a_{1}(t)
has maximal growth in the family
\{a_{1},...,a_{k}\}
. Indeed, suppose that this is not the case. Then, there exists a function
a_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,...,k\}
with
a_{1}\prec a_{i}
. Without loss of generality, assume that the function
a_{k}
has maximal growth rate. It is sufficient to show that for any sequence of functions
g_{N}
with
||g_{N}||_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\leq 1
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}c_{n,N}\int g_{N}\ T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k,N}\ d\mu=0.

Then, we can choose the function
g_{N}
to be the conjugate of the average

\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}c_{n,N}\int T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k,N}\ d\mu

to get our claim. Composing with
T^{-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}
and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it is sufficient to show that

\lim\limits_{N\to+\infty}\sup_{|c_{n}|\leq 1}\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}\ c_{n}\ T^{-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}g_{N}\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{1}(n)\rfloor-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor a_{k-1}(n)\rfloor-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor}f_{k-1,N}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0.

We can write
\lfloor a_{i}(n)\rfloor-\lfloor a_{k}(n)\rfloor=\lfloor a_{i}(n)-a_{k}(n)\rfloor+e_{i,n}
, where the errors
e_{i,n}
take values in
\{0,\pm 1\}
. Using Lemma
3.2
below, the errors can be absorbed by the supremum outside the average and, therefore, the function that corresponds to
f_{1}
is equal to
a_{1}-a_{k}
, which now has maximal growth rate among the new family of functions. It is also easy to check that the new family satisfies the conditions of Proposition
3.1
.

This notion of absorbing the errors that we described above can be made more precise by the next lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that the integers
e_{i,n,N}
take values in a finite set
S
. Then, for any sequences
a_{i,N}
of integers, complex numbers
c^{\prime}_{n,N}
bounded in magnitude by 1 and any 1-bounded functions

, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}\ c^{\prime}_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)+e_{1,n,N}}f_{1,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)+e_{k,n,N}}f_{k,N}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\ll_{k,S}

\displaystyle\sup_{|c_{n,N}|\leq 1}\ \sup_{||f_{2}||_{\infty}\leq 1,...,||f_{k}||_{\infty}\leq 1}\ \lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}\ c_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)}f_{1,N}\cdot T^{a_{2,N}(n)}f_{2}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)}f_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}

.

As a consequence, there exist 1-bounded functions
f^{\prime}_{i,N}
, such that the original expression is bounded by a constant multiple of the quantity

\sup_{|c_{n,N}|\leq 1}\lVert\mathbb{E}_{1\leq n\leq N}\ c_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)}f_{1,N}\ T^{a_{2,N}(n)}f^{\prime}_{2,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)}f^{\prime}_{k,N}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}+o_{N}(1).

Proof.

We partition the integers
n
into a finite number of sets, in which all the quantities
e_{i,n,N}
are constant (as
n
varies). There are at most
|S|^{k}
such sets. If
A_{1},...,A_{|S|^{k}}
are these sets, then we have

\displaystyle\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}c^{\prime}_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)+e_{1,n,N}}f_{1,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)+e_{k,n,N}}f_{k,N}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|S|^{k}}\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in A_{i}}\ c^{\prime}_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)+e_{1,n,N}}f_{1,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)+e_{k,n,N}}f_{k,N}\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq

\displaystyle|S|^{k}\max_{1\leq i\leq|S|^{k}}\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\ c^{\prime}_{n,N}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(n)\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)}f_{1,N}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)+e_{k,n,N}-e_{1,n,N}}f_{k,N}\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq

\displaystyle|S|^{k}\sup_{|c_{n,N}|\leq 1}\ \sup_{||f_{2}||_{\infty}\leq 1,...,||f_{k}||_{\infty}\leq 1}\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq N}{\mathbb{E}}c_{n,N}\ T^{a_{1,N}(n)}f_{1,N}\ T^{a_{2,N}(n)}f_{2}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,N}(n)}f_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)},

which is the required result. In the second to last relation, we composed with
T^{-e_{1,n,N}}
, because
e_{1,n,N}
is constant when
n
is restricted to the set
A_{i}
.
∎

Remark
.

In the following sections, we will encounter situations where we have some error terms in the iterates. The above lemma is not applied verbatim to all cases below. However, the reasoning presented above (i.e. partitioning into sets where the error sequences are constant) can be applied directly every time to remove these error terms. In particular, we can also show (using the same arguments) that a similar statement holds for double averages, that is, if
I_{r}
are a sequence of intervals with lengths going to infinity,
d
is a natural number and the error terms
e_{i,n,R}
take values on a finite set
S
of integers, then

\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\big\lVert\underset{n\in I_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}c^{\prime}_{n,R}\ T^{a_{1,R}(n)+e_{1,n,R}}f_{1,R}\cdot...\cdot T^{a_{k,R}(n)+e_{k,n,R}}f_{k,R}\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{d}\ll_{S,k,d}\\
\sup_{||f_{2}||_{\infty}\leq 1,...,||f_{k}||_{\infty}\leq 1}\ \underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\ \sup_{|c_{n,R}|\leq 1}\ \big\lVert\underset{n\in I_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}c_{n,R}\ T^{a_{1,R}(n)}f_{1,R}\cdot T^{a_{2,R}(n)}f_{2}...\cdot T^{a_{k,R}(n)}f_{k}\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{d},

where we also use the Hölder inequality (which gives dependence on the exponent

3.1.
Overview of the proof

Our main objective is to reduce our problem to the study of ergodic averages of some variable polynomials. Therefore, we will first study asymptotic bounds for certain polynomial families in Section
4
, since they will be required for the proof of Proposition
3.1
. This will rely on the van der Corput inequality and an induction argument on the complexity of the family. In Section
5
, we will establish bounds for Hardy sequences of a specific form, namely when the involved functions are a sum of a sub-linear function and a polynomial. This will also be required for the general case. In Section
6
, we shall finish the proof.

The main idea is that we can approximate the given Hardy functions by Taylor polynomials (possibly constant) in suitable smaller intervals (with lengths going to infinity). We shall reduce our problem to proving a statement of the form

(5)

\lim\limits_{R\to+\infty}\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\lVert\underset{n\in I_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}\ c_{n,R}\ T^{\lfloor p_{1,r}(n)\rfloor}f_{1,r}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor p_{k,r}(n)\rfloor}f_{k,r}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2^{t}}=0,

where the iterates are variable polynomials and
f_{1,r}
has the form

f_{1,r}=f_{1}\cdot T^{\lfloor b_{1}(r)\rfloor}h_{1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor b_{\ell}(r)\rfloor}h_{\ell}

for sub-linear functions
b_{1},...,b_{\ell}
and
h_{1},...,h_{\ell}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
.

After this reduction, we bound the innermost average using the results from Section
4
. More precisely, we claim that the inner average can be bounded by a quantity of the form

\underset{{\bf m}\in[-M,M]^{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big|\int T^{\lfloor q_{1}(r,{\bf m})\rfloor}g_{r,1}\cdot...\cdot T^{\lfloor q_{\ell}(r,{\bf m})\rfloor}g_{r,\ell}\ d\mu\Big|

plus some small error terms, where
M
is a finite integer (independent from the rest of our parameters) and all the functions
g_{r,i}
are either
\tilde{f}_{r}
or
\overline{\tilde{f}_{r}}
. In addition, the functions
q_{i}(r,{\bf m})
in the iterates are such that, for (almost all)
{\bf m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
, they can be written as a sum of a sublinear function plus a polynomial, which is the special case that we discussed above. Thus, taking first the limit
R\to+\infty
to use the bounds established in the special case and then taking the limits
M\to+\infty
, we shall reach our conclusion.

The fact that we can reduce our original problem to (
5
) is based on the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and consider a two-parameter sequence
\big(A_{R,n}\big)_{R,n\in\mathbb{N}}
in a normed space such that
\lVert A_{R,n}\rVert\leq 1
for all possible choices of
R,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
L(t)\in\mathcal{H}
be an eventually positive function such that
1\prec L(t)\prec t
and assume that

\limsup\limits_{R\to+\infty}\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\ \big\lVert\underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big\rVert^{d}\leq C

for some
C>0
. Then, we also have

\limsup\limits_{R\to+\infty}\big\lVert\underset{1\leq n\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big\rVert\leq C^{1/d}.

Proof.

Combining the power mean inequality and the triangle inequality, we can easily deduce that

\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\ \big\lVert\underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big\rVert^{d}\geq\big\lVert\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big)\big\rVert^{d}.

Therefore, our result will follow if we show that

\big\lVert\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big)-\underset{1\leq n\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big\rVert=o_{R}(1).

Let
u
be the compositional inverse of the function
t+L(t)
. Our assumptions on the Hardy field
\mathcal{H}
imply that
u\in\mathcal{H}
. In addition, it is easy to check that
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)/t=1
. Now, we have

\underset{1\leq r\leq R}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\ \underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}A_{R,n}\big)=\frac{1}{R}\big(\sum_{n=1}^{R}p_{R}(n)A_{R,n}+\sum_{n=R+1}^{R+L(R)}p_{R}(n)A_{R,n}\big)

for some real numbers
p_{R}(n)
. Assuming that
n
(and thus
R
) is sufficiently large (so that
u(n)
is positive) we can calculate
p_{R}(n)
to be equal to

p_{R}(n)=\frac{1}{L(\lfloor u(n)\rfloor)+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{L(n)+1}+o_{n}(1),

since the number
A_{R,n}
appears on the average
\underset{r\leq n\leq r+L(r)}{\mathbb{E}}
if and only if
u(n)\leq r\leq n
. Note that
p_{R}(n)
is actually independent of
R
(for
n
large enough) and therefore, we will denote it simply as
p(n)
from now on.
We claim that

(6)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}p(n)=1.

Let us first see how this finishes the proof. Since for
n
large enough we must have
p(n)\leq 2
, we can easily deduce that

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{n=R+1}^{R+L(R)}p(n)A_{R,n}=o_{R}(1).

Here, we used the fact that
L(t)\prec t
. In addition, we have

\big\lVert\frac{1}{R}\sum_{n=1}^{R}p(n)A_{R,n}-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{n=1}^{R}A_{R,n}\big\rVert\leq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{n=1}^{R}|p(n)-1|,

which is also
o_{R}(1)
. Combining the above we reach the desired conclusion.

In order to establish (
6
), we observe that
L(t)
is eventually strictly increasing, and therefore, we can easily get

\int_{\lfloor u(n)\rfloor}^{n+1}\frac{1}{L(t)+1}\ dt\leq p(n)\leq\int_{\lfloor u(n)\rfloor-1}^{n}\frac{1}{L(t)+1}\ dt.

Thus, it suffices to show that the integrals on both sides of the above inequality converge to 1. It is straightforward to check that each of these integrals is
o_{n}(1)
close to the integral

I_{n}=\int_{u(n)}^{n}\frac{1}{L(t)+1}\ dt.

Therefore, we only need to prove that
I_{n}\to 1
. Using the mean value theorem, we can find a real number
h_{n}\in[u(n),n]
such that,

Note that we started with three sub-linear functions in the iterates and now we have an average with only one sub-linear function (our argument in the general case is based on this induction scheme).
The result follows by working similarly to step 3 in the previous example.

4.
Bounds of polynomial averages

Our main goal in this section is to establish Proposition
4.5
below. Before stating that proposition, we will first give some definitions.

4.1.
Families of variable polynomials

Assume we are given a family
P_{N}=\{p_{1,N},...,p_{k,N}\}
of essentially distinct (i.e. their pairwise differences are non-constant polynomials) variable polynomials, such that the degrees of the polynomials in
P_{N}
and of their pairwise differences are independent of
N
(for
N
large enough). Then, we can assign to
p_{1,N}
its own vector
(v_{1,N},...,v_{k,N})
, where
v_{1,N}
is the leading coefficient of
p_{1,N}
and
v_{j,N}
is the leading coefficient of
p_{1,N}-p_{j,N}
for
j\neq 1
. We symbolize this by
\mathcal{S}(p_{1,N})
and call this the
leading vector
of the family
P_{N}
corresponding to
p_{1,N}
. We similarly define
\mathcal{S}(p_{i,N})
for every
i\in\{1,...,k\}
and call it the
leading vector
corresponding to
p_{i,N}
. Let us remark that the leading vector has no elements equal to 0, because we have assumed that the polynomials are essentially distinct. Finally, we call
P_{N}

ordered
, if the degrees of the polynomials
p_{i,N}
are non-increasing. In this case, the polynomial
p_{1,N}
has maximal degree and we call it the
leading polynomial
. The
leading vector
of an ordered polynomial family is defined as the leading vector corresponding to its leading polynomial.

4.2.
Types of polynomial families

We define the
type

(d,w_{d},...,w_{1})
of the polynomial family, where
d
is the largest degree appearing in the polynomials of
P_{N}
and
w_{i}
is the number of distinct leading coefficients of the members of
P_{N}
with degree exactly
i
among all polynomials in the family. Note that for families of variable polynomials, the value of this vector may depend on the variable
N
. We order the types by the value of
d
and then order types of same degree lexicographically. We observe that a decreasing sequence of types must eventually be constant. The type of a family is a classical quantity used in the literature when an induction scheme on polynomial families is required.

4.3.
Good sequences and nice polynomial families

Now, we define the notion of a nice polynomial family. Namely, we will deal with polynomials whose coefficients are well-behaved sequences. Our arguments fail to work in the general case where the coefficients can be arbitrary sequences.

Definition 4.1
.

a) A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers is called "good", if there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
with
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}f(t)\neq 0
such that

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{f(n)}=1.

b) Let
P_{N}=\{p_{1,N},...,p_{k,N}\}
be a collection of polynomials. The family
P_{N}
is called nice, if all the degrees of the polynomials
p_{i,N}
and
p_{i,N}-p_{j,N}
are independent of
N
for
N
large enough and their leading coefficients are good sequences, for all admissible values of the
i,j
.

Note that any good sequence has a limit (possibly infinite). An example of a good sequence that is not a Hardy sequence is the sequence
\frac{\lfloor N^{2/3}\rfloor}{\sqrt{N}}
, which is asymptotically close to
N^{1/6}
. In particular, all sequences of the form
\lfloor f(n)\rfloor
, where the function
f\in\mathcal{H}
does not converge to
0
(as
t\to+\infty
), are good sequences, while, for example,
\lfloor\frac{1}{\log n}\rfloor
is not a good sequence.

Lemma 4.2
.

The type of a nice polynomial family is well-defined (independent of
N
) for
N
large enough.

Proof.

This is fairly straightforward. Indeed, assume that the polynomials
p_{i,N}
and
p_{j,N}
of the given family have the same degree
s
. Let
a_{i}(N),a_{j}(N),a_{ij}(N)
be the leading coefficients of
p_{i,N},p_{j,N}
and
p_{i,N}-p_{j,N}
, which are all good sequences. The degree of the polynomial
p_{i,N}-p_{j,N}
does not depend on
N
for
N
sufficiently large. Then, we have either one of the following:

i) If the polynomial
p_{i,N}-p_{j,N}
has degree equal to
s
, then for
N
large enough,
a_{ij}(N)=a_{i}(N)-a_{j}(N)\neq 0
and therefore the polynomials
p_{i,N},p_{j,N}
have distinct leading coefficients eventually.

ii) If the polynomial
p_{i,N}-p_{j,N}
has degree smaller than
s
, then that means that, for
N
large enough, we have
a_{i}(N)-a_{j}(N)=0
and the polynomials
p_{i,N},p_{j,N}
have equal leading coefficients eventually.

The claim easily follows.
∎

4.4.
The van der Corput inequality

We shall rely heavily on the following variant of the van der Corput inequality in our proofs.

Lemma 4.3
.

For a sequence
u_{n}
in a Hilbert space with
\lVert u_{n}\rVert\leq 1
and a quantity
M=o(N)
, we have

\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_{n}\big\rVert^{2^{d}}\ll_{d}\frac{1}{M}+\underset{-M\leq m\leq M}{\mathbb{E}}\ \Big|\underset{0\leq n\leq N-1}{\mathbb{E}}\langle u_{n+m},u_{n}\rangle\Big|^{2^{d-1}}+o_{N}(1).

Proof.

This follows from the basic van der Corput inequality

\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_{n}\big\rVert\ll\frac{1}{M^{1/2}}+\big(\underset{-M\leq m\leq M}{\mathbb{E}}\Big|\underset{0\leq n\leq N-1}{\mathbb{E}}\langle u_{n+m},u_{n}\rangle\Big|\big)^{1/2}+\frac{M^{1/2}}{N^{1/2}}

by successively squaring and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

We will use this inequality to derive asymptotic bounds for multiple ergodic averages involving polynomials. The above inequality holds, in particular, when
M
is a fixed positive integer.
We state here the equivalent result for variable sequences, since this is more consistent with the notation used in the proof below.

Lemma 4.4
.

For sequences
(u_{n,N})_{n,N\in\mathbb{N}}
in a Hilbert space with
\lVert u_{n,N}\rVert\leq 1
and a quantity
M=o(N)
, we have

\big\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_{n,N}\big\rVert^{2^{d}}\ll_{d}\frac{1}{M}+\underset{|m|\leq M}{\mathbb{E}}\ \Big|\underset{0\leq n\leq N-1}{\mathbb{E}}\langle u_{n+m,N},u_{n,N}\rangle\Big|^{2^{d-1}}+o_{N}(1).

For a polynomial

Appendix A
Some properties of Hardy sequences

A.1.
Growth rates of Hardy functions

We assume that we are working with a Hardy field
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies the properties mentioned in Section
2
. Such a field contains the Hardy field
\mathcal{LE}
of logarithmico-exponential functions and, for any two functions
f,g
that belong to
\mathcal{H}
, we have that the limit

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}

exists. We also have the assumptions of closure under composition and compositional inversion that we made in Section
2
. We will use these properties freely.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
have polynomial growth. Then, for any natural number
k
, we have

f^{(k)}(t)\ll\frac{f(t)}{t^{k}}.

In addition, if
t^{\delta}\prec f(t)
or
f(t)\prec t^{-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, we have

f^{\prime}(t)\sim\frac{f(t)}{t}.

Proof.

We will show that the limit

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{tf^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}

is finite. Using L’Hospital’s rule, the above limit is equal to the limit

(70)

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log|f(t)|}{\log t}.

Since
f
has polynomial growth, the above limit is bounded. In particular, this implies that

f^{\prime}(t)\ll\frac{f(t)}{t}.

The first part now follows by repeated application of this relation.

For the second part, we can easily see that the given condition implies that the limit in (
70
) is positive in the first case and negative in the second case. Therefore, the limit is non-zero and the claim follows.

∎

The above proposition implies that, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
of polynomial growth, all derivatives of sufficiently large order of
f
will converge monotonically to
0
. In addition, we get that for every
k
sufficiently large, we must have

f^{(k+1)}(t)\sim\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{t}.

Indeed, assume that
f(t)\prec t^{s}
, for some non-integer
s
. Then, we must have
f^{(k)}(t)\prec t^{s-k}
. Thus, if
k
is large enough, then
f^{(k)}\prec t^{-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
, which yields our claim.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
be strongly non-polynomial with
f(t)\succ\log t
. Then, for
k
sufficiently large, we have
20
20

20

All the functions defined here belong to
\mathcal{H}
due to the assumptions we have made on our Hardy field, namely, that it is closed under composition of certain functions.

1\prec|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-1/k}\prec|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-1/(k+1)}\prec t.

Remark
.

The above proposition can be proven under the slightly more general condition that
|f(t)-p(t)|\succ\log t
for all real polynomials
p
(cf.
[
10
, Lemma 3.5]
), but we will not need this for the proofs of our main results. We give the proof here for completeness.

Proof.

The function
f
has non-vanishing derivatives of all orders, since it is not a polynomial. Let
d
be an integer, such that
t^{d}\prec f(t)\prec t^{d+1}
.
Then, Proposition
A.1
implies that
|f^{d+1}(t)|\to 0
. Therefore, for any
k\geq d+1
, we have
f^{(k)}(t)\prec 1
. This, of course, gives the leftmost part of the required inequality. In particular,
(d+1)
is minimal among the integers
k
, for which
f^{(k)}(t)
converges to 0.

To prove the rightmost inequality of the proposition, it is sufficient to prove that

f^{(d+1)}(t)\succ t^{-d-1}.

For
k\geq d+1
, the result then follows by successive applications of L’ Hospital’s rule. In the case
d=0
, the above relation follows easily from L’Hospital’s rule. Therefore, we may assume that
d\geq 1
. Now, since
f
is strongly non-polynomial, we have that the function
f^{(d)}(t)
goes to infinity. We will show that

(71)

a^{\prime}(t)\gg\frac{a(t)}{t\log^{2}t}

where
a
is any one of the functions
f,f^{\prime},...,f^{(d)}
(cf.
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
). The result then follows by noting that

f^{(d+1)(t)}\gg\frac{f(t)}{t^{d+1}(\log t)^{2d+2}}\gg\frac{1}{t(\log t)^{2d+2}}\succ\frac{1}{t^{d+1}}.

Equation (
71
) follows by showing that the limit

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{a^{\prime}(t)t(\log t)^{2}}{a(t)}

is infinite. If that is not the case, then we must have

(\log|a(t)|)^{\prime}\ll\frac{1}{t(\log t)^{2}}.

Integrating, we get

\log|a(t)|\ll\frac{1}{\log t}+c

for some real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the function
\log|a(t)|
is bounded. However, note that for any choice of the function
a
, we have
|a(t)|\to+\infty
, since the original function
f
dominates the function
t^{d}
. This gives a contradiction.

It remains to establish the middle part, namely that if
k\geq d+1

|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{k}\prec|f^{(k)}(t)|^{k+1}.

However, we have
|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{k}\ll|f^{(k)}(t)|^{k}/t^{k}
by Proposition
A.1
and we easily get the conclusion by combining this relation with the relation
t^{-k}\prec f^{(k)}(t)
that we established in the previous step.
∎

We give here a description of the polynomial approximations that we use in our arguments. Consider a strongly non-polynomial function
f\in\mathcal{H}
that satisfies
f(t)\succ\log t
. Then, if
k
is large enough, we can find a function

such that

(72)

|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-1/k}\prec L(t)\prec|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-1/(k+1)}.

Such a function always exists (one can take the geometric mean of the functions
|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-1/k}
and
|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-1/(k+1)}
). We study the function
f
in small intervals of the form
[N,N+L(N)]
. Observe that if
0\leq h\leq L(N)
, then we have

f(N+h)=f(N)+\cdots+\frac{h^{k}f^{(k)}(N)}{k!}+\frac{h^{k+1}f^{(k+1)}(\xi_{h,N})}{(k+1)!}

for some
\xi_{h,N}\in[N,N+h]
. We know that
|f^{(k+1)}(t)|\to 0

\Big|\frac{h^{k+1}f^{(k+1)}(\xi_{h,N})}{(k+1)!}\Big|\leq\Big|\frac{L(N)^{k+1}f^{(k+1)}(N)}{(k+1)!}\Big|\prec 1,

because
L(t)\Big|f^{(k+1)}(t)\Big|^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\prec 1
, by our initial choice of

. Using an entirely similar argument, we can prove that

\Big|\frac{L(N)^{k}a^{(k)}(N+L(N))}{k!}\Big|\to+\infty.

Indeed, since
L
is a sublinear function, we can easily check that the functions
a^{(k)}(t+L(t))
and
a^{(k)}(t)
have the same growth rate and thus we only need to prove that

(73)

\Big|\frac{L(N)^{k}a^{(k)}(N)}{k!}\Big|\to+\infty

and this follows similarly as above.

In conclusion, functions that satisfy (
72

f(n)
, when restricted to intervals of the form
[N,N+L(N)]
, is asymptotically equal to a polynomial sequence (that depends on

) of degree exactly

. This motivates us to study the properties of functions
L(t)
that satisfy (
72
).

A.2.
The sub-classes
S(f,k)

In the proofs of the main theorems, we need to do the above approximation for several Hardy field functions in tandem. In order to achieve this, we will use the results of this subsection.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
be a strongly non-polynomial Hardy function such that
f(t)\gg t^{\delta}
, for some
\delta>0
. For example, we exclude functions that grow like
(\log t)^{c}
, where
c>1
.
For such a function
f
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large (it is only required that
f^{(k)}(t)\to 0
), we define the subclass
S(f,k)
of
\mathcal{H}
as

S(f,k)=\{g\in\mathcal{H}\ {:}\;|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k}}\preceq g(t)\prec|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k+1}}\},

where the notation
g(t)\preceq f(t)
means that the limit
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}|f(t)/g(t)|
is non-zero. Note that every
g\in S(f,k)
is a sub-linear function, that is
g(t)\prec t
. Some very basic properties of the classes
S(f,k)
are established in the following lemma.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
be a strongly non-polynomial function with
f(t)\gg t^{\delta}
, for some
\delta>0
.

i) The class
S(f,k)
is non-empty, for
k
sufficiently large.

ii) For any
0<c<1
sufficiently close to 1, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the function
t\to t^{c}
of
\mathcal{H}
belongs to
S(f,k_{0})
.

iii) The class
S(f,k)
does not contain all functions of the form
t\to t^{c}
, for
c
sufficiently close to 1.

Proof.

i) This follows immediately from Proposition
A.2
. We can actually show something stronger, namely, that if
f(t)\gg t^{\delta}
for some
0<\delta<1
, then

(74)

\frac{|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k+1}}}{|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k}}}\gg t^{\frac{\delta}{k(k+1)}},

which means that the functions at the "endpoints" of
S(f,k)
differ by a fractional power.
This last inequality follows by combining the relations

f^{(k)}(t)\gg tf^{(k+1)}(t)\ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ f^{(k)}(t)\gg t^{\delta-k}.

ii) It is sufficient to show that for large
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
t^{c}\ll|f^{(k)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k}}
. Fix a non-integer
q
, such that
f(t)\ll t^{q}
. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
f^{(k)}(t)\ll t^{q-k}
. It suffices to show that for large enough

t^{q-k}\ll t^{-ck}\implies t^{k-q}\gg t^{ck}.

This is obvious, since
c<1

iii) Similar to ii).
∎

In essence, the claim implies that the classes
S(f,k)
form a "partition" of the subclass

A=\{g(t)\gg t^{c}{:}\;\ \exists\ \delta>0,\ \text{ with }g(t)\ll t^{1-\delta}\}

for some
c>0
. That means that any sub-linear function that grows approximately as a (sufficiently large) fractional power must be contained in the union of the

. Although inaccurate, it is instructive to imagine the classes

as (disjoint) intervals on the real line. For example, if
S(f,k)=\{g(t){:}\;\sqrt{t}\preceq g(t)\prec t^{2/3}\}

is represented by the interval
[\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{3})
.

The following proposition relates the behavior of the subclasses
S(f,k)
and

For any two functions

i) The relation

.

ii) If

and

.

i) It is a straightforward application of L’ Hospital’s rule.

ii) Since the given intersection is non-empty, we must necessarily have

|g^{(\ell)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{\ell}}\preceq|f^{(k+1)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{k+1}}\preceq|f^{(l)}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{\ell}}
, which implies that
f^{(l)}(t)\preceq g^{(l)}(t)
. Because we also have

, we can easily deduce that

using the fact that both of these functions are strongly non-polynomial. Thus, the intersection

is non-empty if and only if

For the proof of the second part, we use immediately the fact that

, which follows by the first part. Suppose that
k=l

. This contradicts the assumption that

(apply L’ Hospital’s rule

(this follows from the second statement of Lemma

∎

Remark

It is straightforward to generalize the third statement of the above proposition to the case of

A.3.
