Are Large Language Models Chameleons? An Attempt to Simulate Social Surveys
Mingmeng Geng, Sihong He, Roberto Trotta

Abstract
Can large language models (LLMs) simulate social surveys? To answer this question, we conducted millions of simulations in which LLMs were asked to answer subjective questions. A comparison of different LLM responses with the European Social Survey (ESS) data suggests that the effect of prompts on bias and variability is fundamental, highlighting major cultural, age, and gender biases. We further discussed statistical methods for measuring the difference between LLM answers and survey data and proposed a novel measure inspired by Jaccard similarity, as LLM-generated responses are likely to have a smaller variance. Our experiments also reveal that it is important to analyze the robustness and variability of prompts before using LLMs to simulate social surveys, as their imitation abilities are approximate at best.



1
Introduction

Although often referred to as “stochastic parrots”
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
, large language models (LLMs) can do more than simply “talk”
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023a
)
. Their adaptability and versatility remind us of another animal: the chameleon.

Starting from the very first roles of this LLMs boom - chatbots - can we simulate social surveys through question-and-answer interactions with LLMs? In survey research, several demographic variables are routinely used for sampling, such as gender, race, and age
(
Goldberg et al. 1998
;
Herek 2002
;
Kyriakidis et al. 2015
)
. Even before the LLMs frenzy began, researchers were already talking about the human values presented in language models
(
Hendrycks et al. 2020a
;
Santurkar et al. 2023
)
.

Researchers also found that LLMs can infer people’s gender, age, and location from redacted and anonymized input text
(
Staab et al. 2023
)
. Conversely, prompting LLMs with appropriate demographic variables as we have done here produces answers that appear to be generally matched with real people’s views, although still suffering from biases and stereotypes.

Bias in LLMs is almost impossible to avoid, even with subsequent adjustments and alignments
(
Navigli et al. 2023
)
. Some researchers have also tried to include demographic variables in the prompt for LLMs. Gender and race are two important perspectives to consider for bias in LLMs
(
Sun et al. 2023
;
Kotek et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2024
)
. The sensitivity of LLMs to age was also analyzed, for example, based on six age groups
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

There are almost certainly differences in opinions between people and LLMs, but the key is where and how big the gaps are. Furthermore, biases and stereotypes present in LLMs are likely to slowly influence their users
(
Röttger et al. 2024
)
, so it is essential to measure them.

Prompts affect the output of LLMs, and the demographic variables in the surveys above can also be viewed as part of the prompt engineering. However, the influence and robustness of the prompts are rarely discussed in papers dealing with opinions expressed by LLMs
(
Röttger et al. 2024
)
. The ability of LLMs to simulate surveys needs to be explored in more depth.

There are usually no standard answers to public opinion in survey questionnaires. Comparing the responses of LLMs to subjective questions with those provided by human beings can be achieved in multiple ways
Durmus et al. 2023
. Importantly, human opinions are not distributed regularly among people.

LLMs such as ChatGPT may also be more familiar with American culture, compared to other countries
Cao et al. 2023
. While most research has focused on the United States, our concern is with the European countries, which are more heterogeneous in terms of culture and languages. Therefore, the generalization ability of LLMs can be better compared using European datasets.

In the face of these challenges and gaps, our objectives and contributions are mainly as follows:

•

Comparison of LLMs responses with real survey data, especially for Europeans;

•

Comparing the effects of different models and analyzing the impact of prompts using appropriate comparison criteria;

•

Suggestions and caveats for simulating social surveys using LLMs.

LLMs have demonstrated the ability and potential to transform the field of computational social science
(
Ziems et al. 2024
)
, including survey research
(
Jansen et al. 2023
)
. However, some more fundamental issues need to be validated and addressed, which is the main target of this paper.

2
Related work

LLM simulation

People have also used LLMs to create “silicon samples” for simulations, from GPT-3
Argyle et al. 2023
;
Santurkar et al. 2023
to other newer models
Takemoto 2024
;
Boelaert et al. 2024
. What could be called the “personality traits” of LLMs have also been widely explored in several papers
(
Safdari et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023b
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Mei et al. 2024
)
. While most of the papers are based on American data
Bisbee et al. 2023
;
Sanders et al. 2023
, very few specifically relate to Europeans. Most researchers focus on the mean of the LLMs simulation and few studies pay attention to the variance
Boelaert et al. 2024
.

LLM bias

Bias in natural language processing (NLP) arises for several reasons, such as data, models, and research design
(
Hovy and Prabhumoye 2021
)
. The risks and shortcomings of LLMs were addressed even before they became popular
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
. While LLMs solve many of the difficulties that traditional NLP methods have failed to overcome, the challenge of bias persists
(
Navigli et al. 2023
)
. For example, results based on GPT-3 show that the opinions of certain demographic groups in the United States are not well represented
(
Santurkar et al. 2023
)
. The political bias of ChatGPT in other countries has also been quantified
(
Motoki et al. 2024
)
. Bias also looks like a problem shared by other LLMs
(
Boelaert et al. 2024
)
, and not specific to ChatGPT.

LLM evaluation

The pervasiveness and influence of LLMs is increasing rapidly, and it is becoming more important to better evaluate and compare different LLMs
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
. Platforms that build on users’ comparative ratings have also emerged, for example,
Chatbot Arena

(
Chiang et al. 2024
)
. Traditional objective evaluation criteria for solving various tasks, such as MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding)
(
Hendrycks et al. 2020b
)
, do not meet current needs. Therefore, novel evaluation methods have been proposed, such as uncertainty quantification
(
Ye et al. 2024
)
.

LLM alignment

How to align LLMs with human behavior is an exciting and challenging task
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. Despite the many limitations
(
Wolf et al. 2023
;
Kirk et al. 2023
)
, we can see the attempts and results of many researchers on the alignment of LLMs
(
Shen et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. LLMs are used in a variety of applications, so corresponding datasets and benchmarks are also needed for different usage scenarios
(
Köpf et al. 2024
;
Koo et al. 2023
)
.

Prompt engineering

Prompts affect the output of LLMs
White et al. 2023
, such as the language used
Agarwal et al. 2024
;
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
, the order of inputs in the in-context learning setting
Lu et al. 2021
, the order of the options in the question
Hartmann et al. 2023
;
Dominguez-Olmedo et al. 2023
, and of course the demographic variables included
Beck et al. 2024
.

3
Data

The data and results obtained from surveys also deviate to a greater or lesser extent from the “ground truth”, while the representativeness of the sample is often debated
(
Russell et al. 2022
;
Grafström and Schelin 2014
)
. Therefore, we want to analyze the bias in the responses of LLMs, using real survey data as the “ground truth”, which means that the survey results are assumed to be representative. Therefore, we use survey data rather than crowd-sourced data as a benchmark.

To better compare with real people’s opinions, we made use of data from the European Social Survey (ESS)

4.2
Measurements

The literature tells us that LLMs are usually biased, but there is no agreement on how large these biases are. As previously discussed, there are many ways to measure this bias, with varying conclusions.

Unlike many question-and-answer type tasks with LLMs, social surveys do not have standardized correct answers, but do have references to the distribution of real data. In orde to compare responses from LLMs to the ones from humans, we want to evaluate not only averages but also their distributions, as it’s not binary classification.

Inspired by Jaccard similarity
(
Chierichetti et al. 2010
)
, we define the J-index
J_{q}(G)
for statement
q
and people group
G
that we are interested in (e.g., a country) as:

J_{q}(G)=\frac{\sum_{g\in G}I\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g))\right)}{\sum_{g\in G}U\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g))\right)}

(1)

where
I\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and
U\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
are the intersection and union of two sets of responses,
g
is the subgroup of
G
,
R^{h}_{q}(g)
and
R^{m}_{q}(g)
represent the responses from survey data and LLM simulations, respectively. A J-index of 1 thus corresponds to perfect congruence between between survey and LLM simulation.

Taking advantage of the data from the survey,
I\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g)\right)
and
U\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g)\right)
are defined as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&I\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g)\right)\\
=&\sum_{v\in V_{q}}\left(\min\left(\sum_{i\in g}w_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{r^{h}_{q}(i)=v},\sum_{i\in g}w_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{r^{m}_{q}(i)=v}\right)\right)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&U\left(R^{h}_{q}(g),R^{m}_{q}(g)\right)\\
=&\sum_{v\in V_{q}}\left(\max\left(\sum_{i\in g}w_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{r^{h}_{q}(i)=v},\sum_{i\in g}w_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{r^{m}_{q}(i)=v}\right)\right)\end{split}

where
v
corresponds to the value of the response,
V_{q}
is the set of all possible values for question
q
,
r^{h}_{q}(i)
represents the answer in survey data,
r^{m}_{q}(i)
means the response of LLM given the same information as participant
i
, and
w_{i}
is the analysis weight (combination of post-stratification weight and population weight) provided in the ESS dataset, which could improve the representativeness of survey respondents concerning the target population.

The measure we defined above allows for a better comparison of how similar the distributions are, bringing in some information different from the mean.

The ordinal numbers of the responses from
1
(“Agree strongly”) to
5
(“Disagree strongly”) were used as a scale to calculate the mean of their disagreement level.

Parameters

We know that the parameters of the LLMs also have an impact on the results, so we also tested different parameters.

When we set
top_p
to
0.2

0.9
, the J-index becomes worse in all 72 cases with prompts

and

. (The specific values are listed in Table

For the parameter

Compared to previous papers, more detailed simulations and analyses are performed, such as considering precise age information and using the weights of the survey data to better estimate the target population.

In general, these parameters have an effect on the effectiveness of LLMs in simulating survey data, but not a fundamental one.

6
Conclusions

Can LLMs simulate social surveys? Generally speaking, the simulations are not bizarre, but the details are very rough.

Real survey data were used for comparisons, which should be more representative of public opinion than crowd-sourced data. We also presented a metric for LLM responses to subjective questions that focuses more on the distribution than the mean, and crucially, suggested comparing with actual survey data to highlight potential gender and cultural biases in the response.

Although LLMs show the potential to perform simulations of social surveys or to replace human participants in limited settings, more advanced LLMs do not necessarily produce simulation results that are more similar to survey data.

As with many LLM applications, prompts are important for our simulations, such as the order of options. The instability associated with prompts is also a limitation of simulating social surveys with LLMs.

7
Discussions

Some researchers have attempted to obtain more “reliable” results by reordering the options
Dominguez-Olmedo et al. 2023
, but the order in which questions are asked affects the respondents’ answers in real surveys
(
Carlsson et al. 2012
)
. So we didn’t adopt a similar strategy.

Are LLMs chameleons? It depends on the object of imitation and the capabilities of the given LLM. A model with higher ratings or more parameters is not necessarily more suitable for survey simulation, for example, they may be accompanied by too many human adjustments. A completely neutral LLM is not necessarily a good aim. Should we align the model to real distribution or the ideal situation? And if the latter, how should it be defined?

Using synthetic data generated by LLMs in social science research is also an up-and-coming research area. But many challenges await if we want LLMs to behave truly like the designated person.

Limitations

Due to budgetary and computational resource constraints, we “only” performed about 1 million simulations, although more simulations would be beneficial in obtaining more complete conclusions, for example for investigating how the language used in the prompt may affect the outcome
(
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2024
)
.

Our approach could be further improved, e.g. in the handling of missing data. In addition, we are currently considering only 4 countries and 9 questions, which could be expanded to obtain a fuller picture of how European cultural diversity is captured (if at all) in the current generation of LLMs.
