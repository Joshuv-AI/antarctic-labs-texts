Uniformity in cube-covering systems
J. Beck, W.W.L. Chen, Y. Yang

Abstract
. We establish various analogs of the Kronecker–Weyl equidistribution theorem that can be considered higher-dimensional versions of results established in our earlier investigation in [ 1 ] of the discrete 2 2 -circle problem studied in 1969 by Veech [ 6 ] . Whereas the Veech problem can be viewed as one of geodesic flow on a 2 2 -dimensional flat surface, here we study geodesic flow in higher-dimensional flat manifolds. This is more challenging, as the overwhelming majority of the available proof techniques for non-integrable flat systems are based on arguments in dimension 2 2 . For higher dimensions, we need a new approach.

Let

Let

The problem is then to describe the distribution of this sequence in the union of the two tori,

We can visualize this discrete

Theorem 1

Let

be an integer, and let

2.
Preparation for the proof of Theorem
4

We work with an equivalent discrete form of the problem.

For every integer
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, let

U_{i}=\{i\}\times[0,1)^{2}

denote the
i
-th
yz
-parallel square face of
\mathcal{M}
, and let

X_{0}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{i}.

Since each
yz
-parallel square face has a
2
-coloring, for every integer
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, there exist two sets
\mathscr{G}_{i}
and
\mathscr{R}_{i}

such that

U_{i}=\mathscr{G}_{i}\cup\mathscr{R}_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathscr{G}_{i}\cap\mathscr{R}_{i}=\emptyset.

(2.1)

Since the collection of non-Kronecker vectors
\mathbf{v}=(1,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
has measure zero,
we may therefore start our discussion by assuming that
\mathbf{v}
is a Kronecker vector.
Let
\mathbf{v}=(1,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Kronecker vector, and let
\mathbf{v}_{0}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
.
We define an invertible transformation
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{v}}:X_{0}\to X_{0}
as follows.

For any point
P=(i,\mathbf{y})\in U_{i}\subset X_{0}
where
\mathbf{y}=(y,z)\in[0,1)^{2}
, let

\mathbf{T}(P)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(i+1,\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\}),&\mbox{if $P+\mathbf{v}=(i+1,\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\})\in\mathscr{G}_{i+1}$},\\
(i^{*},\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\}),&\mbox{if $P+\mathbf{v}=(i+1,\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\})\in\mathscr{R}_{i+1}$},\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

where the addition in
P+\mathbf{v}
is modulo
n
for the first coordinate and modulo
1
for the remaining coordinates.
Thus

\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\}=(\{y+\alpha_{1}\},\{z+\alpha_{2}\}),

(2.3)

where
0\leqslant\{\beta\}<1
denotes the fractional part of a real number
\beta
.
Furthermore, the value of
i^{*}
is determined by

i^{*}=\max\{j<i+1:(j,\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\})\in\mathscr{R}_{j}\},

(2.4)

with the convention that

i+1>i>i-1>\ldots>1>0>n-1>n-2>\ldots>i+2.

(2.5)

Note that (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) are motivated by Figure 1.9, and Figure 2.1 illustrates the special case when
n=4
.
If
P+\mathbf{v}
is a red point, we then move from
P+\mathbf{v}
in the direction of the vector
(-1,0,0)
until we hit the first red point,
and this red point lies on the
yz
-parallel square face
U_{i^{*}}
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[scale]{figure-2-1.pdf}\\
\mbox{Figure 2.1: when $P+\mathbf{v}$ is red}\end{array}

Projecting the transformation
\mathbf{T}
by ignoring the first coordinate, we obtain the invertible transformation

\mathbf{T}_{0}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{v}_{0}}:[0,1)^{2}\to[0,1)^{2}:\mathbf{y}\mapsto\{\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\}.

It is easy to see that
\mathbf{T}
preserves the
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{2}
.
Our goal is to establish that for almost every vector
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in[0,1]^{2}
, the transformation
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{v}}:X_{0}\to X_{0}
is ergodic, where
\mathbf{v}=(1,\mathbf{v}_{0})
.
The basic idea is quite surprising, as we prove ergodicity for this non-integrable system by taking advantage of the split singularities.

Remark
.

We are going to use Birkhoff’s well known pointwise ergodic theorem on measure preserving transformations.
Since we simply apply ergodic theory, we do not expect the reader to have any serious expertise in the subject.
Thus knowledge of Lebesgue integral and basic measure theory suffices.
The theorem concerns a measure-preserving system
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu,\mathbf{T})
.
Here
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a measure space, where
X
is the underlying space,
\mathcal{A}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
X

and
\mu
is a non-negative
\sigma
-additive measure on
X
with
\mu(X)<\infty
,
while
\mathbf{T}:X\to X
is a measurable map which is measure-preserving, so that
\mathbf{T}^{-1}A\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mu(\mathbf{T}^{-1}A)=\mu(A)
for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
L^{1}(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
denote the space of measurable and integrable functions in the measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
.
Then the general form of Birkhoff’s pointwise ergodic theorem says that for every function
f\in L^{1}(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, the limit

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}f(\mathbf{T}^{j}\mathbf{x})=f^{*}(\mathbf{x})

(2.6)

exists for
\mu
-almost every
\mathbf{x}\in X
, where
f^{*}\in L^{1}(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a
\mathbf{T}
-invariant measurable function satisfying the condition

\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{X}f^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

A particularly important special case is if
\mathbf{T}
is
ergodic
, when every measurable
\mathbf{T}
-invariant set
A\in\mathcal{A}
is
trivial

in the precise sense that
\mu(A)=0
or
\mu(A)=\mu(X)
.
This is equivalent to the assertion that every measurable
\mathbf{T}
-invariant function is constant
\mu
-almost everywhere.

If
\mathbf{T}
is ergodic, then (
2.6
) simplifies to

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}f(\mathbf{T}^{j}\mathbf{x})=\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

(2.7)

and the right-hand side of (
2.6
) is the same constant for
\mu
-almost every
\mathbf{x}\in X
.

The remarkable intuitive interpretation of (
2.7
) is that the
time average
on the left hand side
is equal to the
space average
on the right hand side.

3.
Proof of Theorem

We focus on the particular measure-preserving system

Lemma 3.1

Consider the measure-preserving system

Since

f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a
3
-times continuously differentiable function for which the conditions (
6.2
)–(
6.4
)
as well as the technical condition concerning the functions (
6.5
) hold.
For any even positive integer
H
, let

G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)=|\{j=0,1,\ldots,H-1:\mathbf{s}_{0}+j(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathcal{C}(f;u)\}|,

where the
u
-neighborhood
\mathcal{C}(f;u)
is defined by (

).
Then for any parameter
\kappa>1
, we have

\lambda_{2}\left(\left\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}:G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)\geqslant\frac{u(b-a)H}{4}-\kappa\psi(u)\right\}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\kappa},

where

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\psi(u)=c_{13}\left(u^{-1/2}+c_{14}\right),

(6.6)

and the constants
c_{13}=c_{13}(f;a,b)>0
and
c_{14}=c_{14}(f;a,b)>0

depend at most on the function
f
in the interval
[a,b]
.

Remark
.

Note that
(b-a)/2
and
u
play the roles of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in Lemma
5.1
,
and that
G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)
is the analog of the counting function

We also have non-zero curvature instead of rotation there.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

We proceed by a number of steps corresponding to those in the proof of Lemma
5.1
.
We also use similar notation as much as possible.

Step 1
.

Here we give a good description of
G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)
.
As before, we consider the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
defined by (
5.3
) and (
5.4
).
Then writing

B=(\mathcal{C}(f;u)-\mathbf{s}_{0})\times[0,H)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

we then have

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)=|\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\cap B|=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\chi_{B}(M\mathbf{n}).

Applying the Poisson summation formula (
5.6
) with
f=\chi_{B}
, we have, analogous to (
Step 1
), the formal identity

G(\mathbf{s}_{0};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2};H)=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{B}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}M^{-1}\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}.

(6.7)

Note, in particular, that for
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{0}
, we have

\int_{B}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}M^{-1}\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=\lambda_{3}(B)=\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{C}(f;u))H=2u(b-a)H.

(6.8)

Then, using (
Step 1
) and analogous to (
5.10
)–(
5.12
), for every
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we can write

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\int_{B}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}M^{-1}\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u)-\mathbf{s}_{0})\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m};\mathbf{v};H),

(6.9)

where

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u)-\mathbf{s}_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{C}(f;u)-\mathbf{s}_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(z_{1}m_{2}+z_{2}m_{3})}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\,\mathrm{d}z_{2}

(6.10)

and

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{m};\mathbf{v};H)=\int_{0}^{H}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}z_{3}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\,\mathrm{d}z_{3}=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}H\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{m}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{m}}.

(6.11)

To study the term (
6.10
), note from (
6.1
) that a typical point in
\mathcal{C}(f;u)
is of the form
(x,f(x)+t)
,
where
x\in[a,b]
and
-u\leqslant t\leqslant u
.
We therefore use the substitution

(z_{1},z_{2})=(x,f(x)+t)-\mathbf{s}_{0},\quad\mbox{with Jacobian}\quad f^{\prime}(x),

so that, writing
\mathbf{m}^{*}=(m_{2},m_{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u)-\mathbf{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{-u}^{u}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}((x,f(x)+t)-\mathbf{s}_{0})\cdot\mathbf{m}^{*}}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{s}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{*}}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{-u}^{u}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(x,f(x)+t)\cdot\mathbf{m}^{*}}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{s}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{*}}\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))\mathcal{I}_{2}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u)),

(6.12)

where

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(6.13)

and

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))=\int_{-u}^{u}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}tm_{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\sin(2\pi um_{3})}{\pi m_{3}},

(6.14)

provided that
m_{3}\neq 0
.
If
m_{3}=0
, then clearly

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))=\int_{-u}^{u}\mathrm{d}t=2u.

(6.15)

The analysis of the integral (
6.13
) is complicated.
However, the smoothness of the function
f
makes it possible to have effective estimates.
We observe that it is possible to establish good estimates for complex exponential integrals
whenever the exponential function exhibits
rapid fluctuations
.
The worst case scenario is when the exponential function
\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}
is almost constant
in a small neighborhood of some point
x_{0}\in[a,b]
.

Let us consider some heuristics.
Write
h(x)=m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x)
.
Then we have the finite Taylor expansion

\displaystyle h(x)-h(x_{0})=m_{2}(x-x_{0})+m_{3}(f(x)-f(x_{0}))

\displaystyle\quad=(x-x_{0})(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}))+\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}m_{3}f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{2}+\frac{(x-x_{0})^{3}m_{3}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)}{6}

(6.16)

for some appropriate
y
between
x_{0}
and
x
.
Suppose further that there exists some
x_{0}\in[a,b]
such that
h^{\prime}(x_{0})=m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
.
Then for every
x\in[a,b]
, the Taylor expansion (
Step 1
) simplifies to

h(x)-h(x_{0})=\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}m_{3}f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{2}+\frac{(x-x_{0})^{3}m_{3}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)}{6}

(6.17)

for some
y\in[a,b]
.
The Taylor expansions (
Step 1
) and (
6.17
) show that the complex exponential function

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}h(x)}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}

starts to exhibit increasing fluctuations if
x
is
relatively far
from
x_{0}
.

The estimation of
fluctuating integrals
is a well known general problem in number theory
and especially in the theory of the Riemann zeta-function.
The following result can be found in the treatise of Titchmarsh
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
on the latter.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that for real functions
F
and
G
, the quotient
F^{\prime}(x)/G(x)
is monotonic in the interval
[a,b]
,
and
|F^{\prime}(x)/G(x)|\geqslant\mu>0
for every
x\in[a,b]
.
Then

\left|\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}F(x)}G(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leqslant\frac{4}{\mu}.

To estimate the integral (
6.13
), we consider the functions

F(x)=2\pi(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))\quad\mbox{and}\quad G(x)=f^{\prime}(x)

in the interval
[a,b]
.
Note that

\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{G(x)}=\frac{2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x))}{f^{\prime}(x)}=\frac{2\pi m_{2}}{f^{\prime}(x)}+2\pi m_{3},

(6.18)

and the derivative

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{G(x)}=-\frac{2\pi m_{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)}{(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}}

has constant sign in
[a,b]
, since
f^{\prime}(x)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)
are continuous and non-zero in
[a,b]
, in view of (
6.2
) and (
6.3
).
Hence the function (
6.18
) is monotonic in
[a,b]
.
To apply Lemma
6.2
, we need to ensure that the function (
6.18
) is also bounded away from
0
.
If no
x_{0}
is in or near the interval
[a,b]
such that
F^{\prime}(x_{0})=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}))=0
,
then we can find a good value for
\mu
in Lemma
6.2
.
However, we clearly have a problem if there exists
x_{0}\in[a,b]
such that
F^{\prime}(x_{0})=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}))=0
.

The following is the worst case scenario.
Suppose that
x_{0}\in(a,b)
.
Let
\delta>0
be a
small
parameter, to be specified later, such that
a<x_{0}-\delta<x_{0}+\delta<b
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(-)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))+\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(0)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))+\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(+)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u)),

(6.19)

where

xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(-)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{x_{0}-\delta}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(0)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}-\delta}^{x_{0}+\delta}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(+)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}+\delta}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(m_{2}x+m_{3}f(x))}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

For the integral
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(0)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))
, we have the trivial estimate

|\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(0)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))|\leqslant 2\delta\max_{x\in[a,b]}|f^{\prime}(x)|=2c_{7}\delta,

(6.20)

in view of (
6.2
).
For the integral
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(+)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))
, we use the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(x_{0}+\delta)

\displaystyle=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}+\delta))

\displaystyle=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}))+2\pi m_{3}\left(\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})+\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{+})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi m_{3}\left(\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})+\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{+})}{2}\right)

for some
y_{+}\in(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta)
.
Since
F^{\prime}(x_{0})/G(x_{0})=0
and
F^{\prime}(x)/G(x)
is monotonic in
[a,b]
, it follows that for every
x\in[x_{0}+\delta,b]
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{G(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(x_{0}+\delta)}{G(x_{0}+\delta)}\right|=\frac{2\pi|m_{3}|}{|f^{\prime}(x_{0}+\delta)|}\left|\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})+\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{+})}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2\pi|m_{3}|}{|f^{\prime}(x_{0}+\delta)|}\left(\delta|f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})|-\frac{\delta^{2}|f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{+})|}{2}\right).

(6.21)

For the integral
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(-)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))
, we use the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(x_{0}-\delta)

\displaystyle=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}-\delta))

\displaystyle=2\pi(m_{2}+m_{3}f^{\prime}(x_{0}))+2\pi m_{3}\left(-\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})+\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{-})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi m_{3}\left(-\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})+\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{-})}{2}\right)

for some
y_{-}\in(x_{0}-\delta,x_{0})
.
Since
F^{\prime}(x_{0})/G(x_{0})=0
and
F^{\prime}(x)/G(x)
is monotonic in
[a,b]
, it follows that for every
x\in[a,x_{0}-\delta]
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{G(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(x_{0}-\delta)}{G(x_{0}-\delta)}\right|=\frac{2\pi|m_{3}|}{|f^{\prime}(x_{0}-\delta)|}\left|\delta f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})-\frac{\delta^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{-})}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2\pi|m_{3}|}{|f^{\prime}(x_{0}-\delta)|}\left(\delta|f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})|-\frac{\delta^{2}|f^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{-})|}{2}\right).

(6.22)

Combining (
6.2
)–(
6.4
), (
Step 1
) and (
Step 1
),
we conclude that for any

or any

\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{G(x)}\right|\geqslant\frac{2\pi|m_{3}|}{c_{7}}\left(c_{8}\delta-\frac{c_{11}\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\geqslant c_{15}\delta|m_{3}|,

provided that

, where

and
c_{16}=c_{16}(f;a,b)>0

are constants depending at most on the function

Using this bound, Lemma
6.2
then gives the estimates

|\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(\pm)}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))|\leqslant\frac{4}{c_{15}\delta|m_{3}|}.

(6.23)

Combining (
Step 1

If

\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathbf{m};\mathcal{C}(f;u))=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}m_{2}x}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

Step 4
.

It follows from (

As before in Section

Write
