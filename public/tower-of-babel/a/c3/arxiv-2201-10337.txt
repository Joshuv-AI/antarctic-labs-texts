The matrix-weighted dyadic convex body maximal operator is not bounded
F. Nazarov, S. Petermichl, K. A. Škreb, S. Treil

Abstract
. The convex body maximal operator is a natural generalization of the Hardy–Littlewood maximal operator. In this paper we are considering its dyadic version in the presence of a matrix weight. To our surprise it turns out that this operator is not bounded. This is in a sharp contrast to a Doob’s inequality in this context. At first, we show that the convex body Carleson Embedding Theorem with matrix weight fails. We then deduce the unboundedness of the matrix-weighted convex body maximal operator.

1.
Introduction

The simple dyadic maximal function

\mathcal{M}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\left|\langle f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|

and the dyadic Hardy-Littlewood maximal function

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\langle\left|f\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}

together with their scalar weighted analogues

\mathcal{M}_{w}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\frac{1}{w(I)}\left|\int_{I}f(y)w(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

and

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{w}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\frac{1}{w(I)}\int_{I}\left|f(y)\right|w(y)\mathrm{d}y

for positive
w\in L^{1}
are classical objects in harmonic analysis. (The reason for the superscript
\mathrm{c}
will become clear in the next section.)

The inequality

\|\mathcal{M}_{w}\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}\leqslant 2

is a special case of Doob’s inequality and the
same inequality for
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{w}
follows immediately from the
observations that
\mathcal{M}_{w}^{\mathrm{c}}f=\mathcal{M}_{w}\left|f\right|
and
\|\left|f\right|\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}=\|f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}
.

The boundedness of
\mathcal{M}_{w}
or
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{w}
in
L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
can be rewritten as the boundedness of the operators

M_{w}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\left|w^{1/2}(x)\frac{1}{w(I)}\int_{I}f(y)w(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}

and

M^{\mathrm{c}}_{w}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}w^{1/2}(x)\frac{1}{w(I)}\int_{I}|f(y)|w(y)\mathrm{d}y

from
L_{w}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to the usual
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The natural counterpart (see the next section for details) of
M_{w}f
and
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{w}f
in the case when
f
is a vector-valued
function and
W
a matrix weight (a positive definite matrix function) are

M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}

and

M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{D}\,:\,x\in I\\
\varphi_{I}:I\to[-1,1]\end{subarray}}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}.

When
d=1
use the signum of
Wf
on
I
for
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
to get the usual absolute value.
This definition will be shown in the next section to be equivalent to a Christ-Goldberg type definition.
The boundedness of these operators from
L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
when
W=\operatorname{Id}_{d}
remains a
simple consequence of Doob’s inequaliy, but is more complicated for general
matrix weights. The positive
result for
M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f
was established in
[
PePoRe18
]
by reducing it to the weighted
Carleson Embedding Theorem from
[
CuTr15
]
. :

Theorem 1.1
.

(Culiuc, Treil) Let
W
be a matrix weight and
(A_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})_{{}_{\scriptstyle I\in\mathcal{D}}}
a
sequence of positive definite matrices. Then the
following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists
c_{\textup{(i)}}>0
such that for all
K\in\mathcal{D}
,

\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}(K)}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}A_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\leqslant c_{\textup{(i)}}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle K}}.

(ii)

There exists
c_{\textup{(ii)}}>0
such that for all
f\in L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}\left|A^{1/2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}^{2}\leqslant c_{\textup{(ii)}}\|f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}.

Moreover, the best possible constants
c_{(\mathrm{i})}
and
c_{(\mathrm{i}\mathrm{i})}
in
(\mathrm{i})
and
(\mathrm{i}\mathrm{i})

satisfy

with

only depending
on the dimension

but not on

.

The bound for the norm
\|M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}
follows by the so-called linearization technique and the exact
statement is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

(Petermichl, Pott, Reguera) Let
W
be a matrix weight. Then

\|M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}\leqslant C,

where

depends only on
d
.

These results raised hopes that the larger maximal
function
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
may be bounded from

to

as well, so it came as a surprise to us that in general it

also quite surprising for us and which may be of independent interest. Here are

Theorem 1.3
.

For any fixed dimension

, there exist a matrix weight

such that for all

(1.1)

but there exist

and a sequence

,

\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}\left|A^{1/2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}^{2}=\infty.

.

For any fixed dimension

.

2.
Discussion of definitions

Let
f
be a vector-valued function with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
Hardy-Littlewood maximal function of
f
at
x
is a quantity
allowing one to control all averages of
f
over
intervals containing
x
. The most straightforward quantity of that type is
just
\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\langle\left|f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
. However, this quantity is often too crude when
f
is large in
some directions and small in some other ones (all such information is
completely lost here) and is too strongly tied to the particular choice of the
Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to yield useful bounds in
the case when the size of
f
is measured in some other norm, especially in a
norm depending on a point, as it is the case in
matrix-weighted spaces.

A more reasonable idea would be to use the convex-body average
\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
of an
L^{1}
function
f
over an interval
I
, defined as

\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\{\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\rightarrow[-1,1]\}.

\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
is always a symmetric compact convex set
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(the compactness follows from the weak-
\ast
compactness
of the unit ball in
L^{\infty}(I,\mathbb{R})=L^{1}(I,\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
, where these spaces consist of functions defined on the interval
I
) but, in general, it may contain no inner points.
However, contrary to the terminology of some convex body geometry books, we
will still call it a convex body.

This convex body average makes sense even if
d=1
, in which case it is
just the interval
[-\langle\left|f\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},\langle\left|f\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}]
, so there it carries just as
much information as
\langle\left|f\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
.

Let now
\rho
be any norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define its norm as

\rho(\Omega)=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\rho(x).

If
\rho(x)=\left|x\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
is the usual Euclidean norm, then we
have

d^{-1}\langle\left|f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\leqslant\rho(\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})\leqslant\langle\left|f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}.

The right inequality is just the triangle inequality. To obtain the left one,
just write
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})
and note that for all
k

\langle\left|f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\leqslant\sum^{d}_{k=1}\langle\left|f_{k}\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},

so there exists
k
with
\langle\left|f_{k}\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\geqslant d^{-1}\langle\left|f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
. On the other hand, choosing
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\operatorname{sign}f_{k}
, we get

\rho(\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})\geqslant(\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})_{k}=\langle\left|f_{k}\right|\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}

and the left inequality follows.

The advantage of the convex body approach is that this inequality is preserved
if we change the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to any other
Euclidean norm, i.e., if we take a positive definite matrix
A
and consider

\rho_{A}(x)=\left|A^{1/2}x\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
. The corresponding estimates

(2.1)

d^{-1}\langle\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle A}}(f)\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\leqslant\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle A}}(\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})\leqslant\langle\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle A}}(f)\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}

immediately follow from the standard Euclidean ones by considering
A^{1/2}f
instead of
f
.

Inspired by the idea of the convex body average, we can now define a convex
body valued maximal function.

The simple maximal function
\mathbf{M}
will be just

\mathbf{M}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\leqslant 1\right\}

i.e.,
\mathbf{M}f(x)
is the absolute convex hull of averages of

f
over intervals containing
x
. The generalization of the Hardy-Littlewood
maximal function will now be

\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\left|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|\leqslant 1\right\},

where the sum of convex bodies is understood in the Minkowski sense:
A+B=\{a+b:\;\;a\in A,b\in B\}
. Plugging in the definition of

\llangle f\rrangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
, we can also rewrite this as

\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\leqslant 1,\;\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\rightarrow[-1,1]\right\}.

For a matrix weight
W
, one can easily write the
weighted analogues
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{\mathrm{c}}
of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}
:

\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\leqslant 1\right\}

and

\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\leqslant 1,\;\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\rightarrow[-1,1]\right\}.

The values here are again convex bodies.

The weighted space
L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is just the space of all measurable
functions
f
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which

\|f\|_{L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\int\left|W^{1/2}f\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}^{2}=\int\langle Wf,f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}<+\infty.

In other words, it is the
L^{2}
space in which the size of
f
is measured not
by the usual Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but by the norm
\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle W(x)}}
.
In line with our definition at the beginning of this section, we can now write

\|\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\int\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle W(x)}}(\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x))^{2}\mathrm{d}x

and similarly for
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
, where, as before, the norm of a subset of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
is understood as the supremum of the norms of the vectors
contained in it. Since
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)
is the absolute convex hull of
\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
with
I\in\mathcal{D}
such that
x\in I
, by
the convexity property of the norm,

\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle W(x)}}(\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x))=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle W(x)}}(\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{D}:x\in I}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}

and, by the same logic,

\rho_{{}_{\scriptstyle W(x)}}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x))=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{D}\,:\,x\in I\\
\varphi_{I}:I\to[-1,1]\end{subarray}}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}

leading to the maximal functions
M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
and
M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{\mathrm{c}}
used in the introduction so
that

\|\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}=\|M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}

and similarly for
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
and
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
.

The reader familiar with Christ-Goldberg definitions of maximal functions in
the context of matrix weighted spaces may prefer to consider the quantity

\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W(y)f(y)\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\mathrm{d}y

instead of our

\sup_{\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\rightarrow[-1,1]}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{I}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}.

However, our inequalities in (
2.1
) show that these
quantities are the same up to a factor of
d
.

The final aspect we want to discuss is the
measurability of
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
and another natural way to define the

L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm of set-valued functions. We shall do this
discussion for
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{\mathrm{c}}
. The case of
\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}
is the same but simpler.

Let us introduce the truncated maximal functions
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}
in which we take into consideration only the intervals
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}
, so

\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)=\left\{\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}:x\in I}a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:\;\;a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}:x\in I}|a_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\leqslant 1,\;\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\rightarrow[-1,1]\right\}

and

M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}f(x)=\sup_{v\in\mathbf{M}_{W,n}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)}\left|W^{1/2}(x)v\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{D}\,:\,x\in I\\
\varphi_{I}:I\to[-1,1]\end{subarray}}\left|W^{1/2}(x)\langle W\rangle^{-1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}.

Notice that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}f(x)
is a symmetric compact convex set. Moreover,
this set depends only on the
n
-th level dyadic interval
I\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
the point
x
belongs to.

Let now
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any convex compact set and let
\varepsilon>0.
Then there exists a finite set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}\subset K
such that

K\subset(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{conv}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})
. In particular, it
implies that for every
x\in I,

\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{j}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\leqslant\sup_{v\in K}\left|W^{1/2}(x)v\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{j}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}.

For each
x\in I
, define
j(x)
as the least index
j
for which
\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{j}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
is maximal. In other words,
j(x)=J
if and
only if
\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{j}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}<\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{{}_{\scriptstyle J}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
for
j<J
and
\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{j}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\leqslant\left|W^{1/2}(x)v_{{}_{\scriptstyle J}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
for
j>J
. Then
g(x)=v_{j(x)}
is a measurable function on
I
such that
g(x)\in K
and

\left|W^{1/2}(x)g(x)\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\sup_{v\in K}\left|W^{1/2}(x)v\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}

for all
x\in I
.

Applying this construction to every
I\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
with
K=\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}^{\mathrm{c}}f|_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
, we get a measurable
g:I_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
g(x)\in\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}^{\mathrm{c}}f(x)
and

\left|W^{1/2}(x)g(x)\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}}}>(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}f(x)

for all
x\in I_{0}
. Letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(along some
sequence), we obtain that
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W,n}}f
is the supremum of a countable family
of measurable functions and, thereby, measurable. Finally, letting
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get that
M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f
is measurable and, moreover,

\|M^{\mathrm{c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}=\sup\{\|g\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}:\;\;g:I_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},\;g\,\operatorname{is}\,\operatorname{measurable},\;g\in\mathbf{M}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}^{\mathrm{c}}f\}.

3.
Proofs

We first prove Theorem
1.3
, which states that the convex body analogue of the weighted
Carleson Embedding Theorem (i.e., of Theorem
1.1
) fails.

Using this result we then conclude the failure of the maximal function estimate.

3.1.

n_{k}
be such that for the weights
W_{k}:=W_{n_{k},s_{k}}
on
I_{0}
there exist
non-zero
f_{k}\in L^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W_{k}}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
supported on
I_{0}
such that

(3.25)

\displaystyle\left\|M_{{}_{\scriptstyle W_{k}}}^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}>4^{k}\|f\|^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W_{k}}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}.

Note, that this inequality is invariant under rescaling, meaning that it does not change if we
multiply
W_{k}
and
f_{k}
by some non-zero constants. It is also easy to see that it does not change
under an affine change of variables (applied to all objects simultaneously).

To construct the weight
\widetilde{W}
on
I_{0}
let us represent
I_{0}
as a union of disjoint intervals

I_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
,
k\geqslant 1
(for example, take
I_{k}:=[2^{-k},2^{-k+1})
). Let
\widetilde{W}_{k}
be
the weight
W_{k}
transplanted via an affine change of variables to the interval
I_{k}
and normalized
(by multiplying by a non-zero constant) in such a way that
\langle W_{k}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{k}}}\leqslant\mathbf{I}
(the identity matrix).

Let also
\tilde{f}_{k}
be the function
f_{k}
transplanted by the same affine change as
W_{k}
to the
interval
I_{k}
and normalized by
\|\tilde{f}_{k}\|^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{W}_{k}}}=2^{-k}
.

Defining

\widetilde{W}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{k}}}(x)\widetilde{W}_{k}(x),\qquad\tilde{f}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{k}}}(x)\tilde{f}_{k}(x),

we immediately see that
\|\tilde{f}\|^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{W}}}=1
, and the estimates (
3.25
) imply
that

\left\|\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{k}}}M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{W}}}\tilde{f}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geqslant\left\|\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{k}}}M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{W}_{k}}}\tilde{f}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}>4^{k}\|\tilde{f}_{k}\|^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{W}_{k}}}\geqslant 2^{k},

which gives the desired blow-up.

3.2.2.
An a priori estimate

Now, let us assume that we have the uniform estimate (
3.24
) just for one function

f=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a
, where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the same as in inequality (
3.21
) in Remark
3.1
.

Our goal is to arrive at a contradiction to (
3.21
). From our assumption we first deduce some
weaker estimate, from which using a trick from
[
CuTr15
]
we will get the desired conclusion.

To get to the final contradiction we need to estimate the weighted maximal function from below. We start
with the trivial observation that for a weight
W
and a function
f\in L^{2}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and any
collection of disjoint measurable sets
S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\subset I
and measurable functions
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\to[-1,1]

parametrized by
I\in\mathcal{D}
, the function
F=F[f,W,S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}]
,

\displaystyle F(x):=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}\left|\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle S_{I}}}(x)W(x)^{1/2}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}

is pointwise estimated as
F(x)\leqslant M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)
. Indeed, if
x\notin\cup_{I\in\mathcal{D}}S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
, then
F(x)=0
.
Otherwise the sum defining
F(x)
consists of exactly one term

(3.26)

\displaystyle F(x)=\left|W(x)^{1/2}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}

where
I=I(x)
is the unique
I\in\mathcal{D}
such that
x\in S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
; uniqueness of
I
follows from the
disjointness of
S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
. To get the
maximal function
M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f(x)
, we need to take the supremum of the right hand side of
(
3.26
) over all
I\in\mathcal{D}
,
I\ni x
and over all
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
, so no particular choice of
I

and
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
can give us more than the maximal function.

It is easy to compute the norm of
F
and rewrite the trivial inequality
\|F\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}^{2}\leqslant\|M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}^{2}
as

(3.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}|S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}|\left|\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle S_{{}_{I}}}}^{1/2}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant\|M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}^{2}.

The above inequality (
3.27
) holds for any collection of disjoint measurable
sets
S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\subset I
and functions
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}:I\to[-1,1]
.

We remark that if we take in the left hand side
of (
3.27
) the supremum over all such collections, we will get exactly
\|M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W}}f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}^{2}
. This is well known to the experts as the
linearization
for the
maximal function, that reduces without loss of generality the estimates of a nonlinear operator
(the maximal function) to the estimates of the (linear) embedding operator. Since for the current paper we only need the trivial estimate (
3.27
), we will leave
the proof of the details of this
linearization
as an exercise for a curious reader.

We specify the estimate (
3.27
) to the case of
W=W_{n,s}
constructed above and
f=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a
, with
S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
defined in Section
3.2.1

and
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{+}}}
for all
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}
and
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=0
else, similarly as in Section
3.1.4
. By noticing
that for
I\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\leqslant n}
we have

\displaystyle W_{n,s}(S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})=W(S_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}})+s\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\geqslant s\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},

we get from the estimate (
3.27
) that

(3.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}s\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W_{n,s}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W_{n,s}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant\Bigl\|M^{\scriptstyle\text{\rm c}}_{{}_{\scriptstyle W_{n,s}}}f\Bigr\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}}^{2}.

But for
f=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a
,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{}_{\scriptstyle L^{2}_{W_{n,s}}}}^{2}=\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}+s\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\left\langle\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}\leqslant(1+s)\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}},

so we get from (
3.28
) that

(3.29)

\displaystyle s\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W_{n,s}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W_{n,s}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant(1+s)C\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}.

which is our a priori estimate.

3.2.3.
From estimate (
3.29
) to a contradiction

For
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}
define

\displaystyle R_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s):=\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W_{n,s}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W_{n,s}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2},

so (
3.29
) can be rewritten as

(3.30)

\displaystyle s\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}R_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)\leqslant(1+s)C\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}.

By the cofactor inversion formula, the entries of the matrix
\left\langle W_{n,s}\right\rangle_{I}^{-1}
are rational functions of
s
of the form
p_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)/Q_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)

where
p_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)
is affine in
s
and
Q_{n,I}(s)=\det\left(\left\langle W_{n,s}\right\rangle_{I}\right)
has degree
2
.

The components of the vector
\left\langle W_{n,s}\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{+}}}\!\!\!a
are polynomials
of
degree 1, so we can write

\displaystyle R_{n,I}(s)=\frac{P_{n,I}}{Q_{n,I}^{2}}(s),\quad Q_{n,I}(s)=\det\left(\left\langle W_{n,s}\right\rangle_{I}\right)

and
P_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}
is a polynomial of degree at most
4
.

Recall that for
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{n,s}\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}+s\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\leqslant n}(I)}\tilde{A}_{J}\leqslant(1+s)\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}

and thus for all
s\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle Q_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)\leqslant(1+s)^{2}\operatorname{det}\left(\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right)=(1+s)^{2}Q_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(0).

Therefore, the estimate (
3.30
) implies that

(3.31)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}\frac{P_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)}{Q_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(0)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{(1+s)^{5}}{s}C\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}

We need the following Lemma, see
[
CuTr15
, Lemma 2.2]
:

Lemma 3.2
.

If
p
is a polynomial such that

|p(s)|\leqslant\frac{(1+s)^{N}}{s}\qquad\forall s>0,

then
|p(0)|\leqslant e^{2}N^{2}
.

We apply this lemma to the polynomial
p
,

\displaystyle p(s):=\left(C\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}\frac{P_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(s)}{Q_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(0)^{2}}.

Note that
P_{n,I}(s)
are non-negative for
s\geqslant 0
.
Estimate (
3.31
) means that
p
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
3.2

with
N=5
, therefore

(3.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}}R_{{}_{\scriptstyle n,I}}(0)

\displaystyle\leqslant Ce^{2}5^{2}\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}.

Recall that for
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n}_{+}

\displaystyle R_{n,I}(0)=\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W_{n}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W_{n}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2},

where
f=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a
and
\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\mathbf{1}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{+}}}
. Noticing that for
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n-1}_{+}

\displaystyle\langle W_{n}\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},\qquad\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}W_{n}f\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}},

we can deduce from (
3.32
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\leqslant n-1}_{+}}\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant Ce^{2}5^{2}\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}.

Letting
n\to\infty
we get that

(3.33)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\left|\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant Ce^{2}5^{2}\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}.

Recall that by (
3.22
) we have
\widetilde{A}_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}=C^{-1}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}A_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}
, so
we rewrite the above estimate (
3.33
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{+}}\left|A_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}^{1/2}\langle\varphi_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}Wf\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I}}\right|_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}^{2}\leqslant Ce^{2}5^{2}\left\langle\langle W\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle I_{0}}}a,a\right\rangle_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{2}}}<\infty,

which contradicts the blow-up estimate (
3.21
) obtained before.
∎
