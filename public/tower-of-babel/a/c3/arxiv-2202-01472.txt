Lattice QCD calculation of the two-photon exchange contribution to the muonic-hydrogen Lamb shift
Yang Fu, Xu Feng, Lu-Chang Jin, Chen-Fei Lu

Abstract
We develop a method for lattice QCD calculation of the two-photon exchange contribution to the muonic-hydrogen Lamb shift. To demonstrate its feasibility, we present the first lattice calculation with a gauge ensemble at m π = 142 m_{\pi}=142 MeV. By adopting the infinite-volume reconstruction method along with an optimized subtraction scheme, we obtain Δ ​ E TPE = − 28.9 ​ ( 4.9 ) ​ μ ​ eV + 93.72 ​ μ ​ eV / fm 2 ⋅ ⟨ r p 2 ⟩ \Delta E_{\text{TPE}}=-28.9(4.9)\penalty\ \mu\text{eV}+93.72\penalty\ \mu\text{eV}/\text{fm}^{2}\cdot\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle , or Δ ​ E TPE = 37.4 ​ ( 4.9 ) ​ μ \Delta E_{\text{TPE}}=37.4(4.9)\penalty\ \mu eV , which is consistent with the previous theoretical results in a range of 20-50 μ \mu eV.

Introduction

Historically, the discovery of hydrogen’s Lamb shift laid the foundation for the modern quantum electrodynamics.
In recent years the precise measurements of muonic-hydrogen (

In 2019, two experiments reported results which agree with the

On the theoretical side, the puzzle has triggered many efforts to improve

Among them, the two-photon exchange (TPE) contribution, see Fig.

It introduces the largest theoretical uncertainty to both the Lamb shift and hyperfine splitting in

In addition, it plays an important role in extracting the charge radius from scattering experiments at high precision

and resolving the proton electric to magnetic form factor ratio puzzle

induced by using the Rosenbluth separation
Rosenbluth 1950
and the polarization transfer methods

II
Two-photon exchange contribution

We start with the spin-averaged forward doubly-virtual Compton scattering tensor in Euclidean space

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi M}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}xe^{iQ\cdot x}\langle p|\operatorname{T}[j_{\mu}(x)j_{\nu}(0)]|p\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(-\delta_{\mu\nu}+\frac{Q_{\mu}Q_{\nu}}{Q^{2}}\right)\mathcal{T}_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle-\left(P_{\mu}-\frac{P\cdot Q}{Q^{2}}Q_{\mu}\right)\left(P_{\nu}-\frac{P\cdot Q}{Q^{2}}Q_{\nu}\right)\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})}{M^{2}},

(2)

where
\nu=P\cdot Q/M
with
P=(iM,\mathbf{0})
and
Q=(Q_{0},\mathbf{Q})
the Euclidean proton and photon four-momenta.
M
is the proton mass and
j_{\mu,\nu}
are the electromagnetic quark currents.

The relative energy shift to the
nS
-state is given by
Pachucki 1999

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{8m\alpha^{2}}{\pi}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}Q

\displaystyle\times\frac{(Q^{2}+2Q_{0}^{2})\mathcal{T}_{1}(iQ_{0},Q^{2})-(Q^{2}-Q_{0}^{2})\mathcal{T}_{2}(iQ_{0},Q^{2})}{Q^{4}(Q^{4}+4m^{2}Q_{0}^{2})},

(3)

where
m
is the lepton mass and
\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}={m_{r}^{3}\alpha^{3}}/{(\pi n^{3})}
is the square of the
nS
-state wave function
at the origin with
m_{r}=mM/(M+m)
the reduced mass and
\alpha
the fine structure constant. Note that the
nP
-state wave function vanishes at the origin, hence it does not
receive any corrections from TPE at this order.

The TPE correction to the
\mu
H Lamb shift has no infrared (IR) divergence because the binding energy serves as an IR regulator.
By treating the proton as a point-like particle with the corrections from charge radius
Carlson and Vanderhaeghen 2011

one can calculate such contribution based on bound-state QED
Pachucki 1996
;
Pachucki 1999
;
Borie 2005
.
However, more precise comparison between
theory and experiment is limited due to the lack of the information on the proton structure. The energy shift
E
defined
in Eq. (
II
) contains all the required structure information, but is unfortunately IR divergent
as the lepton in the Compton scattering is no more bounded.
Here, the idea is to obtain the structure-dependent TPE correction from Eq. (
II
)
by subtracting the contributions from a point-like proton and
the third Zemach moment
Carlson and Vanderhaeghen 2011
.
For the former, the contribution can be described by
the proton-photon vertex function
\Gamma_{\mu}=\gamma_{\mu}
with

\mathcal{T}_{1}^{pt}=\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{\nu^{2}}{Q^{4}-4M^{2}\nu^{2}},\quad\mathcal{T}_{2}^{pt}=\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{Q^{2}}{Q^{4}-4M^{2}\nu^{2}}.

(4)

For the latter, it is given by

E^{Z}=-\alpha^{2}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{2}}\frac{16mM}{(M+m)Q}G_{E}^{\prime}(0),

(5)

where
G_{E}(Q^{2})
is the proton electric form factor and its derivative is related to proton charge radius via
\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle=-6\,G_{E}^{\prime}(0)
.
After the subtraction of
E^{pt}
and
E^{Z}
, one obtains the IR-finite TPE contribution
\Delta E=E-E^{pt}-E^{Z}
.
The quantity
\Delta E_{\text{TPE}}
shown in Eq. (
1
) is defined as the difference between energy shifts to the
nP
- and
nS
-states and thus we have
\Delta E_{\text{TPE}}=-\Delta E
.

For a realistic lattice QCD calculation, Eq. (
II
) cannot be used directly as it is IR divergent. Thus the first difficulty we
need to conquer is to write
\Delta E_{\text{TPE}}
in terms of the hadronic functions calculable via lattice simulations and maintain the IR cancellation automatically.

III
Lattice QCD methodology

On the lattice, we prefer to rewrite Eq.(
II
) in terms of
T_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{00}
and
T_{2}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{T}_{ii}
as

E=-16m\alpha^{2}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,(\alpha_{1}T_{1}+\alpha_{2}T_{2}),

(6)

with

\alpha_{1}(Q)=\frac{1-\sin^{4}\theta}{1+\sin^{2}\theta/\tau_{\ell}},\quad\alpha_{2}(Q)=\frac{\sin^{2}\theta(1-\sin^{2}\theta)}{1+\sin^{2}\theta/\tau_{\ell}}

(7)

and
\tau_{\ell}=\frac{Q^{2}}{4m^{2}}
.
Here the angle
\theta
is defined as
Q_{0}=Q\sin\theta
and
|\mathbf{Q}|=Q\cos\theta
.
The notation
\int_{\varepsilon}
indicates that the integral is performed in the region of
Q^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}
with an IR regulator
\varepsilon
.

Combining Eqs. (
II
) and (
6
), we obtain

E=\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\penalty\ \bar{\omega}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t),

(8)

where the hadronic functions are defined as

\displaystyle H_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\langle p|\operatorname{T}[j_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)j_{0}(0)]|p\rangle,

\displaystyle H_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\langle p|\operatorname{T}[\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{j}(0)]|p\rangle.

(9)

The weight functions
\bar{\omega}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
are given by

\bar{\omega}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{i}(Q)f(Q;x)

(10)

with

f(Q;x)=\cos(Q_0t)j_{0}(|\mathbf{Q}||\mathbf{x}|).

(11)

Here an average over the spatial directions is taken and
j_{n}(x)
are the spherical Bessel functions.

Using the infinite-volume reconstruction method
Feng and Jin 2019
,
we split the time integral in Eq. (
8
) into the regions
|t|<t_{s}
and
|t|\geq t_{s}
and have

E=E^{<t_{s}}+E^{\geq t_{s}}.

(12)

At sufficiently large
t_{s}
, ground-state dominance allows us to relate
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
at
|t|\geq t_{s}

to
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
. Thus,
E^{\geq t_{s}}
can be written as

E^{\geq t_{s}}=\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\penalty\ \bar{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s}),

(13)

where the weight function
\bar{L}_{i}
is defined as

\bar{L}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=-\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{i}(Q)g(Q;\mathbf{x},t_{s})

(14)

with

g(Q;\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\frac{1}{M}\frac{A(Q,t_{s})}{\tau_{p}+\sin^{2}\theta}j_{0}(|\mathbf{Q}||\mathbf{x}|),\quad\tau_{p}=\frac{Q^{2}}{4M^{2}},

(15)

and

\displaystyle A(Q,t_{s})=A_{c}(Q)\cos(Q_0t_s)-A_{s}(Q)\sin(Q_0t_s),

\displaystyle A_{c}(Q)=\left(1/4+\tau_{p}\cos^{2}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1/2-\sin^{2}\theta,

\displaystyle A_{s}(Q)=\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt{\tau_{p}}}\left[\left(1/4+\tau_{p}\cos^{2}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1/2+\tau_{p}\right].

(16)

We originally hope that the IR divergent part is isolated by the large-
t
contribution
E^{\geq t_{s}}
and thus only
the weight function
\bar{L}_{i}
is divergent when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
However, the situation is more complicated than expected as the small-
t
contribution
E^{<t_{s}}

is also IR divergent.
(Although associated with
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
at small
t
,
\bar{\omega}_{i}
receives
significant long-distance contributions from the leptonic part
and thus is IR singular.)
To solve this difficulty, we split the weight functions into two parts

\bar{\omega}_{i}=\hat{\omega}_{i}+\delta\omega_{i},\quad\bar{L}_{i}=\hat{L}_{i}+\delta L_{i},

(17)

where the divergent part is absorbed by
\delta\omega_{i}
and
\delta L_{i}
with

\displaystyle\delta\omega_{i}=-\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{i}(Q),

\displaystyle\delta L_{i}=-\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{i}(Q)g_{0}(Q;\mathbf{x},t_{s}),

(18)

and

g_{0}(Q;\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\frac{1}{M}\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{\tau_{p}+\sin^{2}\theta}-2t_{s}.

(19)

One can confirm that
\hat{\omega}_{i}
and
\hat{L}_{i}
are now IR finite.
Accordingly, the energies
E^{<t_{s}}
and
E^{\geq t_{s}}
are written as

E^{<t_{s}}=\hat{E}^{<t_{s}}+\delta E^{<t_{s}},\quad E^{\geq t_{s}}=\hat{E}^{\geq t_{s}}+\delta E^{\geq t_{s}}.

(20)

To determine
\delta E^{<t_{s}}
, we split
T_{i}(Q)
into the small-
t
and large-
t
parts

\displaystyle T_{i}(Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi M}\int_{-t_{s}}^{t_{s}}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,f(Q;x)H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)

(21)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi M^{2}}\frac{A(Q,t_{s})}{\tau_{p}+\sin^{2}\theta}\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathbf{Q},t_{s}),

where
\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathbf{Q},t_{s})
is Fourier transformation of
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
.
By assuming the ground-state dominance at
t_{s}
,

\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathbf{Q},t_{s})
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathbf{Q},t_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M}{E_{\mathbf{Q}}}e^{-(E_{\mathbf{Q}}-M)t_{s}}

(22)

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}(E_{\mathbf{Q}}+M)G_{E}^{2}(Q_{\mathrm{on}}^{2}),&i=1,\\
-(E_{\mathbf{Q}}-M)\left[G_{E}^{2}+2G_{M}^{2}\right](Q_{\mathrm{on}}^{2}),&i=2,\end{cases}

where
G_{E/M}
are the proton electric and magnetic form factors with
Q_{\mathrm{on}}^{2}=2M(E_{\mathbf{Q}}-M)
and
E_{\mathbf{Q}}=\sqrt{M^{2}+\mathbf{Q}^{2}}
.
On the other hand,
T_{i}(Q)
can be written as a combination of Born and non-Born terms:
T_{i}(Q)=T_{i}^{B}(Q)+T_{i}^{NB}(Q)
,
where the Born term represents the elastic box and crossed box contributions and its analytical form is known
Carlson and Vanderhaeghen 2011
;
Birse and McGovern 2012
;
Gasser et al. 2015
.
For the non-Born term, as it does not contain the pole structure, Ward identity requires it to vanish
as
Q\to 0

Gasser et al. 2015
. By requiring
\lim_{Q\to 0}T_{i}(Q)-T_{i}^{B}(Q)=0
, we obtain with large
t_{s}

K_{i}=\frac{1}{2M}\int_{-t_{s}}^{t_{s}}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=\begin{cases}2\,t_{s},&i=1,\\
\frac{3}{M},&i=2.\end{cases}

(23)

This relation allows us to rewrite
\delta E^{<t_{s}}
as

\delta E^{<t_{s}}=-\frac{4m\alpha^{2}}{\pi}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{i}(Q)K_{i}.

(24)

The last step is to perform the subtraction of

\Delta E=E-E^{pt}-E^{Z}
as mentioned earlier.
Here
E^{pt}
can be calculated using the point-like proton contributions

T_{1}^{pt}=\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{Q^{2}-Q_{0}^{2}}{Q^{4}+4M^{2}Q_{0}^{2}},\quad T_{2}^{pt}=\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{3Q_{0}^{2}}{Q^{4}+4M^{2}Q_{0}^{2}}.

(25)

The same IR regulator
\varepsilon
shall be introduced to make
E^{pt}
and
E^{Z}
finite.
One can relate the form factor
G_{E}(0)
and the charge radius
\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle
to the hadronic function
as

\displaystyle G_{E}^{2}(0)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,L_{0}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})H_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s}),

\displaystyle\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\,L_{r}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})H_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s}),

(26)

with
t_{s}
sufficiently large for ground-state dominance and

L_{0}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\frac{1}{2M},\quad L_{r}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\frac{1}{4M}\left(\mathbf{x}^{2}-\frac{3+6Mt_{s}}{2M^{2}}\right).

(27)

These relations allow us to write
E^{pt}
and
E^{Z}
as an integral of
H_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
.
Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\left\{\sum_{i=1,2}\left[\int_{-t_{s}}^{t_{s}}\mathrm{d}^{4}x\penalty\ \omega_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)\right.\right.

(28)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\left.+\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}\penalty\ L_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})\right]

\displaystyle\left.-2M\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{2}(Q)\left(K_{2}-4\pi T_{2}^{pt}\right)\right\},

where
\omega_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{\omega}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
L_{2}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\hat{L}_{2}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
and

\displaystyle L_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}{Q^{4}}\bigg\{\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\alpha_{1}(Q)[g-g_{0}](Q;\mathbf{x},t_{s})

(29)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{4\pi M^{2}Q}{3(M+m)}L_{r}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})\bigg\}.

After the IR cancellation, the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
can be taken for Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
) now.
The third line of Eq. (
28
) does not depend on
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
and thus can be calculated directly. It contributes

-0.60

\mu
eV to

IV
Optimized subtraction scheme

The method described above
provides a direct way to calculate the TPE contribution using hadronic functions
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
as input, but it suffers from both the finite-volume effects and the signal-to-noise problem due to the fact that
L_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
increases rapidly as the spatial distance
\absolutevalue{\vec{x}}
increases.
To solve this difficulty, we define a reduced weight function
L_{1}^{(r)}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
via

L_{1}^{(r)}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=L_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})-c_{0}L_{0}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})-c_{r}L_{r}(\mathbf{x},t_{s}).

(30)

After the replacement of
L_{1}\to L_{1}^{r}
, the energy shift
\Delta E
is now given by

\Delta E=-0.60+\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\left(c_{0}+c_{r}\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle\right)+\Delta E_{\mathrm{lat}},

(31)

with
\Delta E_{\mathrm{lat}}
computed using the first two lines of Eq. (
28
) but with
L_{1}
replaced by
L_{1}^{(r)}
.

and

4\pi\absolutevalue{\vec{x}}^{2}L_{1}^{(r)}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})H_{1}(\mathbf{x},t_{s})
at
t_{s}=1

Using the least squares method,

by minimizing the following integral

(32)

Here
c_{0}
and
c_{r}
depend on
t_{s}
,
R_{\text{min}}
and
R_{\text{max}}
.
By examining the charge conservation from

and
the vanishing behavior of
\tilde{H}_{2}(\mathbf{0},t_{s})
, we find the ground-state saturation at

fm.
In Eq. (

By using a dipole functional form

with

we mimic the distributions of

and

in Fig.

For the former, the main contribution comes from the range of 1-3 fm and the saturation occurs at

For the latter,
we set

\displaystyle\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\,c_{0}=-0.17\penalty\ \mu\text{eV},

\displaystyle\frac{2m\alpha^{2}}{\pi M}\absolutevalue{\phi_n(0)}^{2}\,c_{r}=-93.72\penalty\ \mu\text{eV}/\text{fm}^{2}.

(33)

With the replacement
L_{1}\to L_{1}^{(r)}
,
now the large-

contribution is significantly reduced and the saturation occurs at
\sim 2.5
fm.
With the values of
c_{0}
and

IV

\Delta E_{\text{TPE}}=-\Delta E=0.77+93.72\cdot\langle r_{p}^{2}\rangle-\Delta E_{\mathrm{lat}}.

V
Numerical results

To demonstrate the feasibility of the methodology, we use a single gauge ensemble near the physical point, generated by the RBC-UKQCD Collaboration using
2+1
-flavor domain wall fermion
Blum et al. 2016a
. Ensemble parameters are listed in Table
1
. We calculate the four-point correlation function

\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{f},\mathbf{x}_{i}}\mathcal{P}\langle\psi_{p}(\mathbf{x}_{f},t_{f})j_{\mu}(x)j_{\nu}(y)\psi_{p}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}_{i},t_{i})\rangle
using the random field sparsening technique
Detmold et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
, with the projection matrix
\mathcal{P}=(1+\gamma_{0})/2
and the time slices chosen as
t_{i}=\min\{t_{x},t_{y}\}-\Delta t_{i}
and
t_{f}=\max\{t_{x},t_{y}\}+\Delta t_{f}
.
The time separation

VI
Conclusion

We have developed a method to calculate the TPE correction to the
\mu
H Lamb shift using lattice QCD. The methodology includes

•

the derivation of the master formula (
28
) to remove IR divergence automatically and to compute the IR-finite
\Delta E
using the hadronic functions
H_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
calculable from lattice QCD,

•

the design of an optimized subtraction scheme to significantly reduce finite-volume effects and statistical noise.

Using the new method, we perform a lattice calculation at
m_{\pi}=142
MeV.
It demonstrates that lattice QCD can extend its horizon to study the important quantities relevant for atomic spectroscopy.
Within both statistical and systematic errors better controlled in the future, lattice studies can help answer more accurately
the natural question - how large the proton is.
