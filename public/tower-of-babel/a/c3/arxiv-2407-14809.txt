Central extensions, derivations, and automorphisms of semi-direct sums of the Witt algebra with its intermediate series modules
Lucas Buzaglo, Girish S Vishwa

Abstract
. Lie algebras formed via semi-direct sums of the Witt algebra 𝒲 = Der ⁡ ( ℂ ⁡ [ t , t − 1 ] ) \mathcal{W}=\Der(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]) and its modules have become increasingly prominent in both physics and mathematics in recent years. In this paper, we complete the study of (Leibniz) central extensions, derivations and automorphisms of the Lie algebras formed from the semi-direct sum of the Witt algebra and its indecomposable intermediate series modules (that is, graded modules with one-dimensional graded components). Our techniques exploit the internal grading of the Witt algebra, which can be applied to a wider class of graded Lie algebras.

1.
Introduction

Throughout, we work over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. All vector spaces are
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces.

The Witt algebra
\mathcal{W}\coloneqq\Der(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}])=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\frac{d}{dt}
of vector fields on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and its universal central extension, the Virasoro algebra, are ubiquitous in both mathematics and theoretical physics. In this paper, we study Lie algebras obtained from taking semi-direct sums of the Witt algebra with its indecomposable intermediate series modules (see Definition
2.7
). Intermediate series modules of the Witt algebra arise naturally, since they are precisely those modules which have the same Hilbert series as the Witt algebra. In some sense, they are the simplest infinite-dimensional representations of
\mathcal{W}
. Defining an abelian Lie algebra structure on these modules, one can then take the semi-direct sum of the Witt algebra with its intermediate series modules to obtain new families of Lie algebras.

Indecomposable intermediate series modules of the Witt algebra were classified by Kaplansky and Santharoubane
[
KS85
]
into are three families – one parametrised by
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the other two parametrised by
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The modules parametrised by
\mathbb{C}^{2}
are the well-known
tensor density modules

I(a,b)
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. We denote the other two families by
A(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
(see (
2.3
) for the definitions of the modules). These correspond to blowing up
\mathbb{C}^{2}
at the points
(0,0)
and
(0,1)
. The modules
I(0,0)
and
I(0,1)
are the only reducible tensor density modules. Indeed, we have
I(0,0)\cong B(0)
and
I(0,1)\cong A(0)
. Thus, the indecomposable intermediate series modules of the Witt algebra are parametrised by the plane blown up at two points. In some sense, the
A(\lambda)
s and
B(\lambda)
s are infinitely close to
I(0,1)
and
I(0,0)
respectively, in the moduli space of intermediate series modules of
\mathcal{W}
. The resulting semi-direct sums are denoted
\mathcal{W}(a,b)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes I(a,b)
and
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes X(\lambda)
, where
X=A
or
B
.

The only reducible tensor density modules
I(0,0)
and
I(0,1)
are intimately linked to the irreducible bounded modules of the Witt algebra, where we say that a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded module is
bounded
if its homogeneous components are all finite-dimensional. The unique nontrivial proper submodule of
I(0,0)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
is
\mathbb{C}
. The quotient
\widetilde{I}\coloneqq I(0,0)/\mathbb{C}
is irreducible, self-dual, and isomorphic to the nontrivial proper submodule
\widetilde{J}\coloneqq\spn\{t^{n}\ dt\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1\}\}
of
I(0,1)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
. It was shown independently by Mathieu
[
Mat92
]
, and Martin and Piard
[
MP91
]
that
\mathbb{C}
,
\widetilde{I}
and
\{I(a,b)\ |\ (a,b)\neq(0,0),(0,1)\}
are the only irreducible bounded
\mathcal{W}
-modules.

In
[
GJP11
]
, the authors computed central extensions, derivations, and automorphisms of the Lie algebras
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
. With such a strong interest in the properties of semi-direct sums of the Witt algebra and its tensor density modules, it is only natural to consider the algebras formed from the semi-direct sum with its two other families of indecomposable intermediate series modules. In this paper, we commence the study of the resulting two families of Lie algebras by computing their central extensions, derivations, and automorphisms. We further compute the central extensions and derivations of the Lie algebras
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes\widetilde{I}
, thereby completing the computation of these properties for Lie algebras formed from the semi-direct sum of the Witt algebra with its indecomposable intermediate series modules and with its irreducible bounded modules.

Our strategy to compute cohomology heavily exploits the
internally graded
structure of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
(see Definition
3.8
), which means that the cohomology of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is entirely supported in degree zero. In other words, the degree zero cohomology of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is isomorphic to its full cohomology. This significantly simplifies our computations, since we only have to consider cocycles of degree zero. These techniques can be applied in many more cases, since most of the representations of the Witt algebra that one might be interested in are internally graded. For example, our programme can be readily extended to semi-direct sums of the Virasoro algebra with its length 3 uniserial modules which were classified by Martin and Piard in
[
MP92
]
.

For the interested reader, we note that special cases of
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
have been of interest in theoretical physics (see
[
BvdBM62
,
BH86
,
FPSSJ19
,
GPSJ+20
,
BBB+24
,
FOV24
]
and references therein). Larger semi-direct sums with the Witt algebra and multiple copies of its intermediate series modules are also gaining interest
[
GLP16
,
BCG20
,
BFOGV24
]
, owing largely to their appearances in modern non-Loretnzian physics
[
BG09
,
Aiz12
]
.

2

\mathcal{W}_{x}(\lambda)
in Sections
3

6
.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorems
3.5
and
6.3
)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then the spaces of (Lie) central extensions of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
are given by

\displaystyle\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{A}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda=0.\\
\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Ab}^{B}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{B},

where the cocycles are defined in Theorem
3.5
and (
3.7
).

Furthermore,
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
has one extra nontrivial Leibniz central extension (up to multiplication by a scalar) given by the Leibniz 2-cocycle induced by the symmetric, invariant bilinear form

\theta_{A}(A_{n},A_{m})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n=m=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

while
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
does not have any Leibniz central extensions which are not already extensions of Lie algebras. In other words,

\displaystyle\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle\cong\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\theta}_{A},

\displaystyle\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\displaystyle\cong\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)),

where
\overline{\theta}_{A}
is the image of
\theta_{A}
in
\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
.

We then move on to computing automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
in Section
7
.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
7.8
)
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=A\text{ and }\lambda\in\{0,-1\},\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}^{2}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=0,\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=-1,\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=A\text{ and }\lambda\notin\{0,-1\},\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda\notin\{0,-1\},\end{cases}

where
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
and
\mathbb{C}_{0}^{\infty}
are defined in (
7.2
) and (
7.3
), and the explicit group structures are given in Theorem
7.8
.

In Section
8
, we compute derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
. Our computation of automorphisms allows us to define derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
by differentiating one-parameter subgroups of the corresponding automorphism group. All outer derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
can be obtained in this way.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
8.6
)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\lambda}^{A};

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{0}^{B}\oplus\mathbb{C}\partial_{0}^{B},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\lambda}^{B},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\end{cases}

where the derivations are defined in Notation
8.1
and Lemma
8.5
. Consequently,

\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda);\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=2,\quad\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda);\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=\begin{cases}3,&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
2,&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

A key technique that we use throughout is the spitting of cohomology groups of semi-direct sum Lie algebras, summarised in the following proposition:

Proposition 1.4

(Proposition
3.1
)
.

For any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathfrak{M}
, the cohomology groups of
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}
, where
\mathfrak{M}
is endowed with an abelian Lie bracket, split as follows:

\HH^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\bigoplus_{k+\ell=n}\HH^{k}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*}),

where
\mathfrak{M}^{*}=\Hom(\mathfrak{M},\mathbb{C})
is the dual of
\mathfrak{M}
.

In particular, we exploit the fact the cohomology of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
with values in the trivial module can be split into sums over cohomology groups of
\mathcal{W}
with values in antisymemtric powers of the
restricted dual
of
X
. We need only consider the restricted dual since the modules with which we are working are
\mathbb{Z}
-graded. This allows us to use the fact that
A(\lambda)
is the restricted dual of
B(\lambda)
to minimise the number of computations required. For example, when computing derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, we encounter equations that appear when computing central extensions of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, so we only have to compute solutions to the equations once. This is not surprising in light of Proposition
1.4
: setting
n=2
, we observe that one of the components of
\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
is
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda)^{\prime})
, which is part of the computation of derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Finally, we end the paper by computing (Leibniz) central extensions and derivations of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
in Section
9
. The techniques and proofs are almost identical to those for
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorems
9.1
,
9.4
, and
9.5
)
.

The space of central extensions of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is given by

\HL^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\cong\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Ab},

where
\Omega_{\Vir}
is the Gelfand–Fuchs cocycle in (
3.7
), and the other cocycles are defined in Theorem
9.1
. Furthermore,

\Der(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\Inn(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab},

where
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are defined in Section
2
, and
d_{\Ab}
is defined in Theorem
9.5
. Consequently,

\dim(\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}))=4,\quad\dim(\HH^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}))=3.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Lie algebra cohomology

In this section, we provide a brief review of Lie algebra cohomology. We refer the reader to
[
Fuc86
, Chapter 1]
for a more thorough introduction to the subject. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra and
\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\End\mathfrak{M}
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module, both possibly infinite-dimensional.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. The
p
-cochains of
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
\mathfrak{M}
, denoted
C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
, are the
\mathfrak{M}
-valued alternating
p
-linear functionals on
\mathfrak{g}
. Under the identification

C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})=\Hom\left(\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!p}}\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M}\right)\cong\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!p}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\displaystyle\otimes$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\textstyle\otimes$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptstyle\otimes$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\otimes$}}}}}\mathfrak{M},

the space of cochains is a
\mathfrak{g}
-module by natural extension of the coadjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. For completeness, also note that
C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})=0
if
p<0
and
C^{0}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\cong\mathfrak{M}
.

The
Lie algebra cohomology of
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
\mathfrak{M}
, denoted
\HH^{*}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
, is the cohomology of the cochain complex

\cdots\to C^{p-1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\to C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\to C^{p+1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\to\cdots

with differential

(2.1)

\begin{split}d\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{p+1})&=\sum_{i=1}^{p+1}(-1)^{i+1}\rho(x_{i})\varphi(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{p+1})\\
&+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq p+1}(-1)^{i+j}\varphi([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{p+1}),\end{split}

where
\hat{\ }
denotes omission, for all
\varphi\in C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
and
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{p+1}\in\mathfrak{g}
.

We denote the space of
p
-cocycles (respectively,
p
-coboundaries) of
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
\mathfrak{M}
by
Z^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
(respectively,
B^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
), and so

\HH^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\coloneqq Z^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})/B^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M}).

If
\mathfrak{M}=\mathbb{C}
regarded as the trivial
\mathfrak{g}
-module, the first term in (
2.1
) vanishes. For convenience, we write
C^{p}(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq C^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{C})
,
Z^{p}(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq Z^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{C})
,
B^{p}(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq B^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{C})
, and
\HH^{p}(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq\HH^{p}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{C})
.

We briefly discuss some interpretations of the low-dimensional cohomology spaces of a Lie algebra. Firstly, it is easy to see from the definition of Lie algebra cohomology that
\HH^{1}(\mathfrak{g})\cong\mathfrak{g}/[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
is the abelianisation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

The cohomology space
\HH^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
of
\mathfrak{g}
with coefficients in a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathfrak{M}
also has an interpretation: it is the space of
outer derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
into
\mathfrak{M}
: we have

\HH^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\cong\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})/\Inn(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M}).

We recall the notion of a derivation of a Lie algebra below.

Definition 2.2
.

A
derivation
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with values in a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathfrak{M}
is a linear map
d\colon\mathfrak{g}\to M
such that

d([x,y])=x\cdot d(y)-y\cdot d(x)

for
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. We write
\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
for the set of derivations
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
\mathfrak{M}
, and simply write
\Der(\mathfrak{g})
instead of
\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
.

A derivation
d\in\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
is
inner
if there exists
m\in\mathfrak{M}
such that
d(x)=x\cdot m
. We write
\Inn(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
for the set of inner derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
\mathfrak{M}
. As before, we write
\Inn(\mathfrak{g})
instead of
\Inn(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
.

The second cohomology
\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
classifies one-dimensional
central extensions
of
\mathfrak{g}
, as we now explain.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
0\to\mathfrak{a}\to\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathfrak{g}\to 0
be a short exact sequence of Lie algebras. We say that
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
is a
central extension
of
\mathfrak{g}
(by
\mathfrak{a}
) if
\mathfrak{a}
is contained in the center of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
.

It is easy to check that every one-dimensional central extension corresponds to a 2-cocycle (with values in
\mathbb{C}
) and vice versa
[
Sch08
, Chapter 4]
. Central extensions corresponding to 2-coboundaries are always trivial (i.e. give rise to split exact sequences), and
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in Z^{2}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{a})
generate equivalent central extensions if and only if
\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\in B^{2}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{a})
. Thus, the second cohomology group
\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{a})
is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of equivalence classes of central extensions by
\mathfrak{a}
.
Given that any
\mathfrak{a}
can be written as a direct sum of one-dimensional Lie algebras, it is natural to ask if studying the one-dimensional central extensions of
\mathfrak{g}
informs us about the
universal
central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
, defined below.

Definition 2.4
.

A central extension
0\to\mathfrak{a}\to\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathfrak{g}\to 0
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
universal
if for every other central extension
0\to\mathfrak{b}\to\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathfrak{g}\to 0
, there exist unique maps
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\to\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\mathfrak{a}\to\mathfrak{b}
such that the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{a}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

It is well-known that a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has a universal central extension if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
is perfect
[
Wei94
, Theorem 7.9.2]
.

The following well-known result makes explicit the relationship between
\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
(which classifies
one-dimensional
central extensions of
\mathfrak{g}
) and universal central extensions of a perfect Lie algebra.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a perfect Lie algebra and let
n=\dim\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
. Let
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{n}\in Z^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
be 2-cocycles whose images in cohomology form a basis for
\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
. Define

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathbb{C}c_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{C}c_{n},

where the
c_{i}
are central, and the other brackets are given by

(2.2)

[x,y]_{\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}}=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}(x,y)c_{i},

where
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
is the universal central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
[
Wei94
, Theorem 7.9.2]
and the duality between
H_{2}(\mathfrak{g})
and
H^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
(see
[
Fuc86
, p.16]
).
∎

2.2.
Intermediate series modules of the Witt algebra

We now introduce the Lie algebras of interest in this paper.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be the
Witt algebra
, with basis
\{L_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and Lie bracket

[L_{n},L_{m}]=(m-n)L_{n+m}.

The Witt algebra has a natural
\mathbb{Z}
-grading, with
\deg(L_{n})=n
. It is the Lie algebra of derivations of the ring of Laurent polynomials. Under the identification
\mathcal{W}=\Der(\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}])=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]\frac{d}{dt}
, we have
L_{n}=t^{n+1}\frac{d}{dt}
.

In this paper, we study semi-direct sums of
\mathcal{W}
with its intermediate series modules, defined below.

Definition 2.7
.

An
intermediate series module
of a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{n}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathfrak{M}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{M}_{n}
such that
\dim\mathfrak{M}_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Indecomposable intermediate series modules of
\mathcal{W}
were classified in
[
KS85
]
: they consist of a family
\{I(a,b)\mid a,b\in\mathbb{C}\}
, whose elements are known as
tensor density modules
, and two families
\{A(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\}
and
\{B(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\}
. An analogous classification of the intermediate series modules of
generalised
(or
higher rank
)
Witt algebras
was obtained in
[
Su94
]
.

In this paper, we identify
\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, where, for
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, the point
[a:b]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
corresponds to
\frac{a}{b}
if
b\neq 0
, or
\infty
if
b=0
. Fixing
a,b,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and letting
\{I_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be bases for
I(a,b),A(\lambda),B(\lambda)
, respectively, the action of
\mathcal{W}
on these modules is given by

(2.3)

\begin{gathered}L_{n}\cdot I_{m}=(a+bn+m)I_{n+m},\\
L_{n}\cdot A_{m}=(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})A_{n+m},\\
L_{n}\cdot B_{m}=(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})B_{n+m},\end{gathered}

where
\delta_{n}^{0}
is the Kronecker delta symbol. When
\lambda=\infty
, the
n(n+1)\lambda
term becomes
n^{2}
, in other words, the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
A(\infty)
and
B(\infty)
is given by

\displaystyle L_{n}\cdot A_{m}

\displaystyle=(n+m+n^{2}\delta_{m}^{0})A_{n+m},

\displaystyle L_{n}\cdot B_{m}

\displaystyle=(m-n^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})B_{n+m}.

Note that
A(0)\cong I(0,1)
and
B(0)\cong I(0,0)
, while
I(a,b)\cong I(a+k,b)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
I(\frac{1}{2},0)\cong I(\frac{1}{2},1)

[
GJP11
, Lemma 2.1]
. For this reason, following the conventions of
[
GJP11
]
, we always assume that
a=0
in
I(a,b)
if
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Since the modules
A(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
seem much more mysterious than the tensor density modules, we now spend some time explaining how they arise, and describe their relationship to the other intermediate series modules. The tensor density modules can be viewed as

I(a,b)=t^{a-b}\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt^{b}.

Under this identification, the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
I(a,b)
is the following:

f\partial\cdot(g\ dt^{b})=(fg^{\prime}+bf^{\prime}g)dt^{b}.

Thus,
I(0,0)
is
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
with its usual action of
\mathcal{W}
by derivations,
I(0,-1)
is
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt^{-1}\cong\mathcal{W}
with the adjoint action of
\mathcal{W}
, and
I(0,1)
is
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
, the space of 1-forms.

Recall that the tensor density modules
I(a,b)
are irreducible for all
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, except for the functions
I(0,0)
and 1-forms
I(0,1)
. These two modules are mutually dual with unique nontrivial proper submodules
\mathbb{C}
and
\widetilde{J}\coloneqq\spn\{t^{n}\ dt\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1\}\}
respectively. Note that when we say “dual”, we mean that
I(0,0)
is the
restricted dual
of
I(0,1)
, and vice-versa. The restricted dual of a graded
\mathcal{W}
-module
\mathfrak{M}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{M}_{n}
with
\dim\mathfrak{M}_{n}<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is the graded
\mathcal{W}
-module
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}_{n}
with
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}_{n}=\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathfrak{M}_{-n},\mathbb{C})
. Furthermore, the quotient
\widetilde{I}\coloneqq I(0,0)/\mathbb{C}
is irreducible, self-dual, and isomorphic to the nontrivial proper submodule
\widetilde{J}
.

This relationship between
I(0,0)
and
I(0,1)
can be explained by introducing the following natural
\mathcal{W}
-maps. First, we have the
de Rham differential

\displaystyle d\colon I(a,0)

\displaystyle\to I(a,1)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto df=f^{\prime}\ dt.

Using this map, we see that the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
I(0,1)
can be described as follows:

f\partial\cdot(g\ dt)=(fg^{\prime}+f^{\prime}g)dt=(fg)^{\prime}\ dt=d(fg).

When
a=0
, it is clear that the kernel of
d
consists of the constant functions
\mathbb{C}
, and that the image of
d
is
\widetilde{J}
, which yields the isomorphism
\widetilde{I}\cong\widetilde{J}
.

Another natural
\mathcal{W}
-map is the
residue map
, defined by

\displaystyle\Res\colon I(0,1)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle f\ dt

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{0}f\ dt.

More concretely, the residue map extracts the coefficient of
t^{-1}
in the Laurent polynomial
f
. It is clear that
\ker(\Res)=\widetilde{J}
.

Additionally, we have the
multiplication map

\displaystyle\cdot\ \colon I(a_{1},b_{1})\otimes I(a_{2},b_{2})

\displaystyle\to I(a_{1}+a_{2},b_{1}+b_{2})

\displaystyle f\ dt^{b_{1}}\otimes g\ dt^{b_{2}}

\displaystyle\mapsto fg\ dt^{b_{1}+b_{2}}.

The self-duality of
\widetilde{I}
is witnessed by the invariant skew-symmetric form

(2.4)

\langle f,g\rangle\coloneqq\Res(f\cdot dg)=\oint_{0}fg^{\prime}\ dt,

where an invariant form is defined below.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra and
\mathfrak{M}
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module. A bilinear form
\theta\colon\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{M}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be
invariant
if it satisfies

\theta(x\cdot u,v)+\theta(u,x\cdot v)=0

for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
u,v\in\mathfrak{M}
.

Similarly, we can see the duality between
I(0,b)
and
I(0,1-b)
via the form

(2.5)

\mathcal{B}(f\ dt^{b},g\ dt^{1-b})\coloneqq\Res(f\ dt^{b}\cdot g\ dt^{1-b})=\oint_{0}fg\ dt.

Finally, we have the
Lie bracket

\displaystyle[-,-]\colon I(a,b_{1})\otimes I(a,b_{2})

\displaystyle\to I(a,b_{1}+b_{2}+1)

\displaystyle f\ dt^{b_{1}}\otimes g\ dt^{b_{2}}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-b_{1}fg^{\prime}+b_{2}f^{\prime}g)dt^{b_{1}+b_{2}+1}.

The multiplication map and the Lie bracket equip the space
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{b\in\mathbb{Z}}I(0,b)
with a Poisson algebra structure. See
[
Sch16
, Theorem 2.10]
for more details.

The modules
A(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
arise as follows:
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
is two-dimensional. Noting that
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})\cong\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{I})/\Inn(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{I})
, we can view an element of
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
as a derivation of
\mathcal{W}
with coefficients in
\widetilde{I}
. Given
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{I})
, we can form a new
\mathcal{W}
-module
\widetilde{I}_{\delta}
as follows: as a vector space,
\widetilde{I}_{\delta}=\widetilde{I}\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta
with
\mathcal{W}
-action given by letting
\widetilde{I}
be a submodule of
\widetilde{I}_{\delta}
, and defining

w\cdot\delta=-\delta(w),

where
w\in\mathcal{W}
. This yields a family of extensions of
\mathbb{C}
by
\widetilde{I}
, but not all of these are indecomposable. The indecomposable members of this family are the modules
A(\lambda)
, parametrised by
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We then get the dual family
B(\lambda)
of indecomposable extensions of
\widetilde{I}
by
\mathbb{C}

[
SŠ17
, Remark 3.1]
.

Therefore, we may view
A(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
as the spaces of 1-forms
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
and functions
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
respectively, with deformed actions of
\mathcal{W}
. Under this perspective, we have
A_{n}=t^{n-1}\ dt
and
B_{n}=t^{n}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, to define the cocycle giving rise to
A(\lambda)
as an extension of
\mathbb{C}
by
\widetilde{I}\cong\widetilde{J}
, we introduce the following derivation

\displaystyle\delta_{1}\colon\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{J}

\displaystyle f\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto df^{\prime}=f^{\prime\prime}\ dt.

We can then write the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
A(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
as

f\partial\cdot(g\ dt)=\Big(fg^{\prime}+f^{\prime}g+\lambda\Res(g\ dt)f^{\prime\prime}\Big)dt=d(fg+\lambda\Res(g\ dt)f^{\prime}).

It is easy to check that the above action coincides with the definition of
A(\lambda)
as a deformation of
I(0,1)
given in (
2.3
), provided
\lambda\neq\infty
. For the case
\lambda=\infty
, we must also use the derivation

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\colon\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{J}

\displaystyle f\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto d(t^{-1}f)=(t^{-1}f-t^{-2}f)dt.

Then the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
A(\infty)
is

\displaystyle f\partial\cdot(g\ dt)

\displaystyle=\Big(fg^{\prime}+f^{\prime}g+\Res(g\ dt)(f^{\prime\prime}-t^{-1}f^{\prime}+t^{-2}f)\Big)dt

\displaystyle=d\Big(fg+\Res(g\ dt)(f^{\prime}-t^{-1}f)\Big),

which we get from the derivation
\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\in\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})
. We can easily see that
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are the compositions of the derivations

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{1}\colon\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\to I(0,0),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{2}\colon\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\to I(0,0)

\displaystyle f\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto f^{\prime},

\displaystyle f\partial

\displaystyle\mapsto t^{-1}f

with the de Rham differential
d
. The classes of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
span the space
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{J})\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
(see Corollary
9.2
).

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the cocycle defining
B(\lambda)
is dual to
\delta_{1}
, and the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
B(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
can be written as

f\partial\cdot g=fg^{\prime}+\lambda\langle f^{\prime},g\rangle,

where
\langle-,-\rangle
is the skew-symmetric bilinear form defined in (
2.4
). For
\lambda=\infty
, we use the cocycle dual to
\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
, giving the formula

f\partial\cdot g=fg^{\prime}+\langle f^{\prime}-t^{-1}f,g\rangle

for the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
B(\infty)
.

By the discussion above, we see that the module
A(\lambda)
has an irreducible submodule
\mathcal{W}\cdot A(\lambda)=\spn\{A_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}\cong\widetilde{I}
with a one-dimensional trivial quotient
A(\lambda)/(\mathcal{W}\cdot A(\lambda))\cong\mathbb{C}
, while
B(\lambda)
has a one-dimensional trivial submodule
\mathbb{C}B_{0}
with an irreducible quotient
B(\lambda)/\mathbb{C}B_{0}
isomorphic to
\widetilde{I}
. This can be seen by noticing that the de Rham differential is still a
\mathcal{W}
-map under the deformed
\mathcal{W}
-actions on
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
and
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
, as we prove next.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then the de Rham differential
d\colon B(\lambda)\to A(\mu)
is a homomorphism of
\mathcal{W}
-representations, where we view
A(\mu)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
and
B(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
as vector spaces. The map
d
induces an isomorphism
B(\lambda)/\mathbb{C}B_{0}\cong\mathcal{W}\cdot A(\mu)
.

Proof.

First, note that
d(B_{n})=nA_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n+m\neq 0
. Then

\begin{gathered}d(L_{n}\cdot B_{m})=md(B_{n+m})=m(n+m)A_{n+m},\\
L_{n}\cdot d(B_{m})=mL_{n}\cdot A_{m}=m(n+m+n(n+1)\mu\delta_{m}^{0})A_{n+m}=m(n+m)A_{n+m},\end{gathered}

where we used that
m\delta_{m}^{0}=0
in the last equality, so we see that
d(L_{n}\cdot B_{m})=L_{n}\cdot d(B_{m})
whenever
n+m\neq 0
. We also have

\begin{gathered}d(L_{n}\cdot B_{-n})=-(n+n(n+1)\lambda)d(B_{0})=0,\\
L_{n}\cdot d(B_{-n})=-nL_{n}\cdot A_{-n}=0,\end{gathered}

where we used that
L_{n}\cdot A_{-n}=0
in the last equality. Combining the above, we conclude that
d(L_{n}\cdot B_{m})=L_{n}\cdot d(B_{m})
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
d
is a homomorphism of
\mathcal{W}
-modules, as required.

For the final sentence, we simply note that
\ker(d)=\mathbb{C}B_{0}
, while
\im(d)=\spn\{A_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}=\mathcal{W}\cdot A(\mu)
.
∎

Semi-direct sums of
\mathcal{W}
with its tensor density modules
I(a,b)
, which we denote

\mathcal{W}(a,b)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes I(a,b),

have been studied in
[
GJP11
]
. In particular, the authors computed (Leibniz) central extensions, derivations, and automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
. We summarise their results below.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
GJP11
, Theorems 2.3, 3.5, 4.7, and 5.2]
)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. The space of central extensions of
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
has the following dimension:

\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}(a,b)))=\begin{cases}3,&\text{if }(a,b)=(0,0)\text{ or }(0,1),\\
2,&\text{if }(a,b)=(0,-1)\text{ or }(\frac{1}{2},0),\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, there are only two values of
(a,b)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
for which
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
has extra non-trivial Leibniz central extensions:

\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}(a,b)))=\begin{cases}\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}(a,b)))+1,&\text{if }(a,b)=(0,1)\text{ or }(a,b)=(0,2),\\
\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}(a,b))),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The space of outer derivations of
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
has the following dimension:

\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}(a,b);\mathcal{W}(a,b)))=\begin{cases}3,&\text{if }(a,b)=(0,0),\\
2,&\text{if }(a,b)=(0,1)\text{ or }(0,2),\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Finally, the automorphism group of
\mathcal{W}(a,b)
has the following structure:

\Aut(\mathcal{W}(a,b))\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}),&\text{if }a\notin\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z},\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.11
.

Note that the Leibniz cohomology result for
\mathcal{W}(0,1)
in Theorem
2.10
is different to the one in
[
GJP11
, Theorem 3.5]
. In particular, they claim that
\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}(0,1)))=\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}(0,1)))+2
. This is due to an error in
[
GJP11
, Lemma 3.2]
, which we correct in Section
6
.

We focus on semi-direct sums of
\mathcal{W}
with the intermediate series modules
A(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and prove analogous results to those of Theorem
2.10
. We denote the resulting semi-direct sums by

\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes A(\lambda),\quad\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes B(\lambda),

spanned by
\{L_{n},A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{L_{n},B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, respectively. For
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, the Lie brackets are given by

\begin{gathered}=(m-n)L_{n+m}\\
[L_{n},A_{m}]=(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})A_{n+m},\quad[L_{n},B_{m}]=(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})B_{n+m}\\
[A_{n},A_{m}]=0,\quad[B_{n},B_{m}]=0.\end{gathered}

Note that
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
is perfect (in other words,
[\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda),\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)]=\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
), while
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
is not: we have

[\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda),\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)]=\spn\{L_{n},A_{m}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z},m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}=\mathcal{W}\ltimes(\mathcal{W}\cdot A(\lambda))\cong\mathcal{W}\ltimes\widetilde{I}.

When discussing
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
simultaneously, we write
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, where
X=A
or
B
. In such cases, we also write
X_{n}
instead of
A_{n}
or
B_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, we define

\omega_{A(\lambda)}(n,m)=n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0},\quad\omega_{B(\lambda)}(n,m)=m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0}.

Thus, when we discuss the brackets
[L_{n},A_{m}]
and
[L_{n},B_{m}]
simultaneously, we write

[L_{n},X_{m}]=\omega_{X(\lambda)}(n,m)X_{n+m}.

3.
Central extensions

Our first goal is to compute
\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, which classifies central extensions of these Lie algebras. In other words, we want to look for non-trivial 2-cocycles on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, which are antisymmetric bilinear maps
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\times\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Since
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
is perfect, it follows by
[
Wei94
, Theorem 7.9.2]
that
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
has a universal central extension, while
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
does not. Regardless, we can still classify one-dimensional central extensions of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
by computing
\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
, although there is no universal one.

The computation of 2-cocycles is simplified by the decomposition formula given in
[
GLP16
, Proposition 1]
. We present a more general version of this result here.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module over some Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. The cohomology groups of
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}
, where
\mathfrak{M}
is endowed with an abelian Lie bracket, split as follows:

(3.1)

\HH^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\bigoplus_{k+\ell=n}\HH^{k}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*}),

where
\mathfrak{M}^{*}=\Hom(\mathfrak{M},\mathbb{C})
is the dual of
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Given
\psi\in C^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
and
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
k+\ell=n
, we define
\psi_{k\ell}\in C^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
as follows:

\psi_{k\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},v_{1},\ldots,v_{s})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}),&\text{if }r=k,s=\ell,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
r+s=n
,
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
v_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then

\psi=\sum_{k+\ell=n}\psi_{k\ell}

for all
\psi\in C^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
. Define

C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\coloneqq\{\psi_{k\ell}\mid\psi\in C^{k+\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\}.

It is clear that
C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\Hom\left(\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!k}}\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M},\mathbb{C}\right)
. The space of cochains
C^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})=\Hom(\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!n}}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}),\mathbb{C})
splits as

C^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})=\bigoplus_{k+\ell=n}C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}).

Now we show that
d
preserves
\ell
, leading to a subcomplex
(C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$},\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}),d)
for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the fact that
[x_{i},x_{j}]\in\mathfrak{g}
,
[x_{i},v_{j}]\in\mathfrak{M}
,
[v_{i},v_{j}]=0
, the formula (
2.1
) applied to
\psi_{k\ell}\in C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
reduces to

(3.2)

d\psi_{k\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},v_{1},\ldots,v_{s})\\
=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r}(-1)^{i+j}\psi_{k\ell}([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{r},v_{1},\dots,v_{s})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{i+j+r}\psi_{k\ell}([x_{i},v_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{r},v_{1},\dots,\hat{v}_{j},\dots,v_{s}),

where
r+s=n+1
,
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
v_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}
. In both of the summations in (
3.2
), the number of entries in
\mathfrak{g}
is reduced by one while the number of entries in
\mathfrak{M}
is left the same. It follows by definition of
\psi_{k\ell}
that (
3.2
) is non-trivial only if
r=k+1
and
s=\ell
. This proves that
d\psi_{k\ell}\in C^{k+1,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
, as claimed.

We therefore define
\HH^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$},\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
to be the cohomology of the cochain complex
(C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$},\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}),d)
. It follows that

\HH^{n}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\bigoplus_{k+\ell=n}\HH^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}).

To prove the isomorphism in the statement of the proposition, we show that the cochain complexes
C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$},\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
and
C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$}}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*})
are isomorphic, which will yield

\HH^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\HH^{k}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*}).

This isomorphism is constructed as follows:

\Phi\colon C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\to C^{k}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*}),

defined by
\Big(\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})=\psi_{k\ell}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})
, where
\psi_{k\ell}\in C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
,
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
v_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}
. It is clear that
\Phi
is bijective for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

We now show that
\Phi
is a chain map. To that end, let
\psi_{k\ell}\in C^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
. The definition of the differential in (
2.1
) gives

(3.3)

d\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+1}x_{i}\cdot\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1})\\
+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+j}\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{k+1}).

We briefly remark that one should, strictly speaking, use a different symbol for the differential in (
3.3
), since it is meant to refer to the differential of the cochain complex whose cochains are given by
C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$}}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*})
. However, since it is constructed canonically from the one defined in (
2.1
), we abuse notation by using the same symbol, thereby also avoiding superfluous notation.
On the other hand, using (
3.2
), we get

\Big(\Phi(d\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})=d\psi_{k\ell}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1},v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+j}\psi_{k\ell}([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{k+1},v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{i+j+k+1}\psi_{k\ell}([x_{i},v_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1},v_{1},\dots,\hat{v}_{j},\dots,v_{\ell}).

In the second summation, we can move the
[x_{i},v_{j}]
term to the right as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{i+j+k+1}\psi_{k\ell}([x_{i},v_{j}],x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1},v_{1},\dots,\hat{v}_{j},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{i}\psi_{k\ell}(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1},v_{1},\dots,v_{j-1},[x_{i},v_{j}],v_{j+1},\dots,v_{\ell}).

By definition of
\Phi
, we get

\Big(\Phi(d\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+j}\Big(\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1}\dots\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{i}\Big(\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{j-1},[x_{i},v_{j}],v_{j+1},\dots,v_{\ell}).

The definition of the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\Hom(\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M},\mathbb{C})
now gives

\Big(\Phi(d\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+j}\Big(\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1}\dots\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+1}\Big(x_{i}\cdot\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1})\Big)(v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}).

We conclude that

(3.4)

\Phi(d\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+j}\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})([x_{i},x_{j}],x_{1}\dots\hat{x}_{i},\dots,\hat{x}_{j},\dots,x_{k+1})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+1}x_{i}\cdot\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{k+1}).

Combining (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we see that
\Phi(d\psi_{k\ell})=d\Phi(\psi_{k\ell})
, which proves that
\Phi
is a chain map. Since
\Phi
is bijective for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that
\Phi
is an isomorphism of cochain complexes

C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$},\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\overset{\Phi}{\cong}C^{\mbox{$\bm{\cdot}$}}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*})

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that
\HH^{k,\ell}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong\HH^{k}(\mathfrak{g};\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}\mathfrak{M}^{*})
for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, as claimed, which concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.8

If

\mathfrak{g}

Definition 3.9

Let

Theorem 3.10

C_{0}^{*}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\to C^{*}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
induces an isomorphism in cohomology
\HH_{0}^{*}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\cong\HH^{*}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})
.

By Theorem
3.10
, the internally graded structure of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
means that any 2-cocycle with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
can only be non-trivial on the degree zero part of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\times\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
. In other words, if
\Omega\in Z^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
, we may assume that

\Omega(L_{n},L_{m})=\Omega(L_{n},X_{m})=\Omega(X_{n},X_{m})=0

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n+m\neq 0
.

4.
Abelian cocycles

The goal of this section is to compute
\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
X=A
or
B
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\HH_{\Ab}^{2}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))=0,

\displaystyle\dim(\HH_{\Ab}^{2}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=1.

By the isomorphism
\HH^{2}_{\Ab}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))
, Proposition
4.1
implies that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(A(\lambda))=0,

\displaystyle\dim(

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(B(\lambda)))=1.

By Theorem
3.10
, we only need to compute abelian cocycles of degree 0 to prove Proposition
4.1
. In other words, we may assume that any abelian cocycle of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is of the form

(4.1)

\Omega(X_{n},X_{m})=\alpha(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0},

where
\alpha\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
. By the antisymmetry of
\Omega
, the function
\alpha
must satisfy
\alpha(-n)=-\alpha(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and in particular,
\alpha(0)=0
.

Definition 4.2
.

A function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is called an
abelian cocycle function
on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
if the antisymmetric bilinear map
\Omega\colon\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\times\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\Omega(X_{n},X_{m})=\alpha(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

and
\Omega(L_{n},L_{m})=\Omega(L_{n},X_{m})=0
is a 2-cocycle, where
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The space of all abelian cocycle functions on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is denoted
\Ab_{X}(\lambda)
, where
X=A
or
B
.

Note that there are no nonzero abelian coboundaries (that is, abelian cocycles which are coboundaries): this is because if
\varphi\colon\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
gives rise to an abelian cocycle
d\varphi
, then

d\varphi(L_{n},L_{m})=d\varphi(L_{n},X_{m})=0,

and
d\varphi(X_{n},X_{m})=\varphi([X_{n},X_{m}])=\varphi(0)=0
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
d\varphi=0
. Therefore, we see that

\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\Ab_{X}(\lambda).

If
\Omega
is a cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, then

(4.2)

\Omega(X_{n},[X_{m},L_{-n-m}])+\Omega(X_{m},[L_{-n-m},X_{n}])+\Omega(L_{-n-m},[X_{n},X_{m}])=0

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We first look for abelian cocycles of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. We aim to prove the following:

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then there are no non-trivial abelian cocycles on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, in other words,
\Ab_{A}(\lambda)=0
. Consequently,
\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))=0
.

We split this into two cases:
\lambda\neq\infty
(in other words,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
) and
\lambda=\infty
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in\Ab_{A}(\lambda)
. Then
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

The cocycle condition (
4.2
) reads

(4.3)

(m+(m+n)(1-m-n)\lambda\delta^{0}_{n})\alpha(m)-(n+(m+n)(1-m-n)\lambda\delta^{0}_{m})\alpha(n)=0,

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider
m\neq 0,\ n=0
. Then (
4.3
) gives

(4.4)

m\big(1+(1-m)\lambda\big)\alpha(m)=0\iff(1+(1-m)\lambda\big)\alpha(m)=0.

Setting
m=1
above forces
\alpha(1)=0
, and thus
\alpha(-1)=0
due to the antisymmetry of
\alpha:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Likewise, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we obtain
\alpha(m)=0
from the above, which finishes the proof.
∎

Similarly to the case
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, it is straightforward to prove that
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\infty)
also does not have any non-trivial abelian cocycles.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\Ab_{A}(\infty)
. Then
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

The cocycle condition reads

(4.5)

(-n+(m+n)^{2}\delta^{0}_{m})\alpha(n)-(-m+(m+n)^{2}\delta^{0}_{n})\alpha(m)=0,

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Once again, we consider
m\neq 0,\ n=0
, so (
4.5
) gives

(4.6)

m(1-m)\alpha(m)=0.

By the same argument as for the
\lambda\neq\infty
case, the above tells us that
\alpha(m)=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

This concludes the proof of Proposition
4.3
.

The situation for
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
is very different to that of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
: we will see that there is a non-trivial abelian cocycle, which we define below.

Notation 4.6
.

Define
\iota\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\iota(n)=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We now prove that
\iota
is an abelian cocycle function for
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, and that
\Ab_{B}(\lambda)
is one-dimensional, spanned by
\iota
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\iota
is the unique (up to scalar multiplication) abelian cocycle function on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, in other words,
\Ab_{B}(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}\iota
. In particular, if
\Omega
is an abelian cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
of degree zero, then, up to multiplication by a scalar, we have

\Omega(B_{n},B_{m})=n\delta_{n+m}^{0}.

Consequently,
\dim(\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=1
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\Ab_{B}(\lambda)
. The cocycle condition (
4.2
) reads

(4.7)

(m+(m+n)(1-m-n)\lambda\delta^{0}_{n})\alpha(n)-(n+(m+n)(1-m-n)\delta^{0}_{m})\alpha(m)=0

for
\lambda\neq\infty
, with
-(m+n)^{2}
instead of
(m+n)(1-m-n)\lambda
for
\lambda=\infty
. The only non-trivial case to consider is
m,n\neq 0
, in which case, equation (
4.7
) gives

(4.8)

m\alpha(n)-n\alpha(m)=0.

Any abelian cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
has to satisfy (
4.8
) for
m,n\neq 0
, with no other constraints. To show that there is a unique (up to scalar multiplication) solution, we set
m=1
in (
4.8
), which gives
\alpha(n)=n\alpha(1)
. We split the rest of the proof in two cases:
\alpha(1)=0
and
\alpha(1)\neq 0
.

If
\alpha(1)=0
, the above gives
\alpha(n)=0
for all
n\neq 0
. But the antisymmetry of
\alpha\colon\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
implies
\alpha(0)=0
, so
\alpha(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

If
\alpha(1)\neq 0
, we obtain the nonzero solution
\alpha=\alpha(1)\iota
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
4.1
now follows by combining Propositions
4.3
and
4.7
.

5.
Mixing cocycles

In this section, we now turn our attention to mixing cocycles on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, with the goal of computing
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\dim(\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
1,&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\end{cases}

while
\dim(\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=1
.

By the isomorphism
\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda)^{\prime})
, Proposition
4.1
implies that

\displaystyle\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};A(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};B(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
1,&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\end{cases}.

Where we have used that
A(\lambda)^{\prime}\cong B(\lambda)
and
B(\lambda)^{\prime}\cong A(\lambda)
.

Similarly to before, we may assume that a mixing cocycle
\Omega\colon\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\times\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
is of the form

(5.1)

\Omega(L_{n},X_{m})=\beta(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0},

where
\beta\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
. Note that in this case, we no longer have
\beta(-n)=-\beta(n)
.

Definition 5.2
.

A function
\beta\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is called a
mixing cocycle function
on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
if the antisymmetric bilinear map
\Omega\colon\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\times\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\Omega(L_{n},X_{m})=\beta(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

and
\Omega(L_{n},L_{m})=\Omega(X_{n},X_{m})=0
is a 2-cocycle, where
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\Omega
is a coboundary, then we call
\beta
a
mixing coboundary function
.

The space of all mixing cocycle functions on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is denoted
\ZMix_{X}(\lambda)
, while the space of mixing coboundary functions on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is denoted
\BMix_{X}(\lambda)
.

In this case, we have

\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\ZMix_{X}(\lambda)/\BMix_{X}(\lambda),

by Theorem
3.10
. The cocycle condition gives

(5.2)

\Omega(L_{n},[L_{m},X_{-n-m}])+\Omega(L_{m},[X_{-n-m},L_{n}])+\Omega(X_{-n-m},[L_{n},L_{m}])=0,

where
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, which gives a condition that mixing cocycle functions must satisfy. As we did for abelian cocycles, we begin by considering
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. We first define a mixing cocycle function for
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
.

Notation 5.3
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, define
\beta_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\beta_{\lambda}(n)=\begin{cases}\lambda+1,&\text{if $n=0$},\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We define
\beta_{\infty}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\beta(n)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition
5.1
for
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
will follow from the refinement in the next result.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\ZMix_{A}(0)=\mathbb{C}\beta_{0}\oplus\mathbb{C}\iota
, where
\iota
is as in Notation
4.6
, and
\ZMix_{A}(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}\beta_{\lambda}
if
\lambda\neq 0
. In particular, if
\Omega
is a mixing cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
of degree zero, then for
\lambda\neq 0
we have, up to multiplication by a scalar,

\Omega(L_{n},A_{m})=\begin{cases}(\lambda+1)\delta_{n+m}^{0},&\text{if }n=0\text{ and }\lambda\neq\infty,\\
\delta_{n+m}^{0},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

while for
\lambda=0
, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\Omega(L_{n},A_{m})=(an+b)\delta_{n+m}^{0}.

Furthermore,
\BMix_{A}(\lambda)=0
, so

\dim(\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
1,&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

Since it is not entirely obvious, we first explicitly prove that
\beta_{\lambda}\in\ZMix_{A}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then the map
\beta_{\lambda}
is a mixing cocycle function on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. In other words, there is a mixing cocycle
\Omega\in Z^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
defined by

\Omega(L_{n},A_{m})=\begin{cases}(\lambda+1)\delta_{n+m}^{0},&\text{if }n=0\text{ and }\lambda\neq\infty,\\
\delta_{n+m}^{0},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Consider
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. In this case, the cocycle condition (
5.2
) gives

(5.3)

(-n+m(m+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta(n)-(-m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta(m)+(n-m)\beta(n+m)=0.

If
n=0
or
m=0
, then (
5.3
) for
\beta_{\lambda}
is trivially satisfied. Furthermore, if
m\neq-n
with
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, then (
5.3
) is once again easily checked.

Therefore, it suffices to check (
5.3
) for
m=-n\neq 0
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle(-n+m(m+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta_{0}(n)-

\displaystyle(-m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta_{0}(m)+(n-m)\beta_{0}(n+m)

\displaystyle=-n+n(n-1)\lambda-(n+n(n+1)\lambda)+2(\lambda+1)n

\displaystyle=-n+\lambda n^{2}-\lambda n-n-\lambda n^{2}-\lambda n+2\lambda n+2n=0,

as required.

Now let
\lambda=\infty
. Here, the cocycle condition gives

(5.4)

(-n+m^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta(n)-(-m+n^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta(m)+(n-m)\beta(n+m)=0.

We need to check that
\beta_{\infty}
satisfies (
5.4
). For
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle(-n+m^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta_{\infty}(n)-

\displaystyle(-m+n^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})\beta_{\infty}(m)+(n-m)\beta_{\infty}(n+m)

\displaystyle=-n+m^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0}-(-m+n^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0})+n-m

\displaystyle=(n+m)(m-n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}=0,

as required.
∎

We now prove that there are no nonzero mixing coboundary functions on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then there does not exist any nonzero mixing coboundary function on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. In other words,
\BMix_{A}(\lambda)=0
. Consequently,
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\cong\ZMix_{A}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in\BMix_{A}(\lambda)
, so there exists some
\varphi\colon\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
such that
d\varphi(L_{n},A_{-n})=\beta(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\beta(n)=d\varphi(L_{n},A_{-n})=-\varphi([L_{n},A_{-n}])=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
\beta=0
.
∎

We now complete the proof of Proposition
5.4
by proving that
\beta_{\lambda}
is the unique (up to scalar multiplication) mixing cocycle function on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\neq 0
. Note that when
\lambda=0
, we can apply
[
GJP11
, Theorem 2.3]
, since
A(0)\cong I(0,1)
as representations of
\mathcal{W}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

Let
\beta\in\ZMix_{A}(\lambda)
. As mentioned above, the proof for
\lambda=0
follows by
[
GJP11
, Theorem 2.3]
: we have

\dim(\ZMix_{A}(0))=\dim(\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(0)))=\dim(\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}(0,1)))=2.

Indeed,
\beta_{0}
gives an element of
\ZMix_{A}(0)
by Lemma
5.5
, while
\iota
from Notation
4.6
is also a mixing cocycle function on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(0)
. Therefore,
\ZMix_{A}(0)
is spanned by
\beta_{0}
and
\iota
.

Thus, we may assume that
\lambda\neq 0
. We analyse the cases
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\lambda=\infty
separately.

First, consider
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\beta^{\prime}=\beta-\beta(1)\beta_{\lambda}
, so that
\beta^{\prime}
is still a mixing cocycle function on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\beta^{\prime}(1)=0
. We claim that
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
\beta^{\prime}(-1)\neq 0
. Rescaling
\beta^{\prime}
if necessary, we may assume that
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=1
. Taking
n=2
and
m=-1
in (
5.3
), we get
\beta^{\prime}(2)=-\frac{1}{2}
, while
n=-2
and
m=1
gives
\beta^{\prime}(-2)=\frac{3}{2}
. Now we have two ways of computing
\beta^{\prime}(0)
: we can substitute
n=1,m=-1
or
n=2,m=-2
into (
5.3
). The former gives
\beta^{\prime}(0)=\lambda+\frac{1}{2}
, while the latter gives
\beta^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{2}(5\lambda+1)
. Combining these two equations, we conclude that
\lambda=0
. But we assumed that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, so this is a contradiction. This proves the claim.

Since
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
, we can take
n=1
and
m=-1
in (
5.3
) to get
\beta^{\prime}(0)=0
. Letting
n\geq 2
and
m=-1
, it follows that

n\beta^{\prime}(n)=(n+1)\beta^{\prime}(n-1).

By induction, we conclude that
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for all
n\geq 2
. Similarly, taking
n\leq-2
and
m=1
, we get

n\beta^{\prime}(n)=(n-1)\beta^{\prime}(n+1),

so
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for
n\leq-2
by induction. Therefore,
\beta^{\prime}=0
and
\beta=\beta(1)\beta_{\lambda}
, as required. This concludes the proof for
\lambda\neq\infty
.

Now consider
\lambda=\infty
, and let
\beta^{\prime}=\beta-\beta(0)\beta_{\infty}
. Then
\beta^{\prime}\in\ZMix_{A}(\infty)
and
\beta^{\prime}(0)=0
. We claim that
\beta^{\prime}(1)=0
.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
\beta^{\prime}(1)\neq 0
. Rescaling
\beta^{\prime}
if necessary, we may assume that
\beta^{\prime}(1)=1
. Taking
n=1
and
m=-1
in (
5.4
), we get
-2\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
, so that
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
. Now, substituting
n=2
and
m=-1
in (
5.4
) gives
\beta^{\prime}(2)=\frac{3}{2}
, while taking
n=-2
and
m=1
gives
\beta^{\prime}(-2)=-\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, consider
n=2
and
m=-2
in (
5.4
):

(-2+2^{2})\beta^{\prime}(2)-(2+2^{2})\beta^{\prime}(-2)+4\beta^{\prime}(0)=3+3+0=6\neq 0,

a contradiction. This proves the claim.

Since
\beta^{\prime}(1)=0
, we can take
n=1
and
m=-1
in (
5.4
) to get
-2\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
, so that
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
. Taking
n\geq 2
and
m=-1
in (
5.4
), we get

n\beta^{\prime}(n)=(n+1)\beta^{\prime}(n-1).

Therefore, it follows by induction that
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for all
n\geq 2
. Similarly, if we take
n\leq-2
and
m=1
in (
5.4
), we get

n\beta^{\prime}(n)=(n-1)\beta^{\prime}(n+1),

so we get
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for all
n\leq-2
. We conclude that
\beta^{\prime}=0
, so
\beta=\beta(0)\beta_{\infty}
, as required.
∎

The final goal of this section is to compute
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
. Note that, unlike
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, the Lie algebra
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
does have nonzero mixing coboundary functions for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

We start by introducing notation for some mixing cocycle functions on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
and stating the result on mixing cocycles of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
.

Notation 5.7
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Define
\eta_{\lambda},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
as follows:

\eta_{\lambda}(n)=n+n(n+1)\lambda,\quad\gamma_{1}(n)=n,\quad\gamma_{2}(n)=n^{2}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
n(n+1)\lambda
is understood to be
n^{2}
if
\lambda=\infty
. In other words,
\eta_{\lambda}=(\lambda+1)\gamma_{1}+\lambda\gamma_{2}
if
\lambda\neq\infty
and
\eta_{\infty}=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}\gamma_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\gamma_{2}
, while
\BMix_{B}(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}\eta_{\lambda}
, where
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
and
\eta_{\lambda}
are as in Notation
5.7
. In other words, if
\Omega
is a mixing cocycle of degree 0 on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, then

\Omega(L_{n},B_{m})=(an^{2}+bn)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, if
\lambda\neq\infty
, then
\Omega
is a coboundary if and only if there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that
a=c\lambda
and
b=c(\lambda+1)
, while if
\lambda=\infty
, then
\Omega
is a coboundary if and only if
a=b
.
Consequently,

\dim(\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=\dim(\ZMix_{B}(\lambda))-\dim(\BMix_{B}(\lambda))=1.

We first compute the space of mixing coboundary functions on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\BMix_{B}(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}\eta_{\lambda}
, where
\eta_{\lambda}
is as in Notation
5.7
. In particular, the mixing cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
defined by

\Omega(L_{n},B_{m})=(n+n(n+1)\lambda)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

is a coboundary.

Proof.

Let
\chi\in\BMix_{B}(\lambda)
. Let
\psi\colon\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{C}
be a linear map giving rise to the mixing coboundary defined by
\chi
. In other words,
\psi\in\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)^{*}
has the property that

d\psi(L_{n},L_{m})=d\psi(B_{n},B_{m})=0,\quad d\psi(L_{n},B_{m})=\chi(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. This implies that, for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
,

0=d\psi(L_{n},L_{m})=(n-m)\psi(L_{n+m}),

so
\psi(L_{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

If
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
with
m\neq-n
, we have

0=d\psi(L_{n},B_{m})=-\psi([L_{n},B_{m}])=-m\psi(B_{n+m}).

Therefore,
\psi(B_{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Hence, the only possibly nonzero value of
\psi
is
\psi(B_{0})
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\chi(n)=d\psi(L_{n},B_{-n})=-\psi([L_{n},B_{-n}])=(n+n(n+1)\lambda)\psi(B_{0})=\psi(B_{0})\eta_{\lambda}(n),

where, as before,
n(n+1)\lambda
is understood to be
n^{2}
if
\lambda=\infty
. It follows that
\chi=\psi(B_{0})\eta_{\lambda}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We now compute
\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\beta\in\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
, the cocycle condition (
5.2
) gives

(5.5)

(-(n+m)-m(m+1)\lambda\delta_{n}^{0})\beta(n)+(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\beta(m)+(n-m)\beta(n+m)=0,

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, where once again
n(n+1)\lambda
is understood to be
n^{2}
when
\lambda=\infty
.

Suppose
m=-n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then (
5.5
) becomes
2n\beta(0)=0
, and thus
\beta(0)=0
. It follows that
n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0}\beta(m)=0
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, so (
5.5
) becomes

(5.6)

(n+m)(\beta(m)-\beta(n))+(n-m)\beta(n+m)=0.

Therefore, the space of mixing cocycle functions of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
does not depend on
\lambda
. Given (
5.6
), we immediately get two possible forms that
\beta
can take.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
. In other words, there are mixing cocycles
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in Z^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
defined by

\Omega_{1}(L_{n},B_{m})=n\delta_{n+m}^{0},\quad\Omega_{2}(L_{n},B_{m})=n^{2}\delta_{n+m}^{0}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from (
5.6
).
∎

We now prove Proposition
5.8
by showing that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
span
\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
. Recall that there is a linear combination of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
which gives a coboundary function: we have
(\lambda+1)\gamma_{1}+\lambda\gamma_{2}=\eta_{\lambda}\in\BMix_{B}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, while
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=\eta_{\infty}\in\BMix_{B}(\infty)
.

Proof of Proposition
5.8
.

As mentioned above, it is enough to prove that
\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
is two-dimensional. We claim that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
form a basis for
\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
.

Let
\beta\in\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
and define

\beta^{\prime}=\beta+\beta(1)(\gamma_{2}-2\gamma_{1})-\frac{\beta(2)}{2}(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}),

so
\beta^{\prime}\in\ZMix_{B}(\lambda)
. Note that
\beta^{\prime}(1)=\beta^{\prime}(2)=0
. Since
\beta^{\prime}
is a mixing cocycle function for
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, the function
\beta^{\prime}
satisfies (
5.6
). Substituting
m=1
into (
5.6
) for
\beta^{\prime}
, we get

(5.7)

(n-1)\beta^{\prime}(n+1)=(n+1)\beta^{\prime}(n)

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\beta^{\prime}(2)=0
, applying (
5.7
) inductively implies that
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now, substituting
n=-1,m=2
into (
5.6
) for
\beta^{\prime}
, we get
\beta^{\prime}(-1)=0
. Therefore, applying (
5.7
) inductively, we get
\beta^{\prime}(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
\beta^{\prime}=0
, which means that
\beta
is a linear combination of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, proving the claim.
∎

Combining Propositions
5.4
and
5.8
, we have proved Proposition
5.1
. We now put together all our previous computations to prove Theorem
3.5
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We will use the decomposition

\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\HH_{\Vir}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\oplus\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\oplus\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)).

As stated before,
\HH_{\Vir}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
is one-dimensional, spanned by the Virasoro cocycle
\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}
.

We now analyse
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. By Proposition
4.3
,
\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))=0
. Note that, for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\Omega_{0}^{A}(L_{n},A_{m})=\iota(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}
and
\Omega_{\Mix}^{A}(L_{n},A_{m})=\beta_{\lambda}(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}
. Therefore, Proposition
5.4
implies that

\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{A}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda=0.\\
\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

This completes the proof for
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. We now move on to
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
.

Note that
\Omega_{\Ab}^{B}(B_{n},B_{m})=\iota(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}
, so Proposition
4.7
implies that
\HH_{\Ab}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))=\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Ab}^{B}
.

For the mixing cocycle, we can take the 2-cocycle induced by the function

\displaystyle\theta_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}n,&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\\
n(n+1),&\text{if }\lambda=0,\end{cases}

as the representative in cohomology, since Proposition
5.8
implies that
\theta_{\lambda}\notin\BMix_{B}(\lambda)
. Now,
\theta_{\lambda}
is a mixing cocycle function which is not a coboundary function and

\Omega_{\Mix}^{B}(L_{n},B_{m})=\theta_{\lambda}(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0},

so
\Omega_{\Mix}^{B}
has nonzero image in
\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
. Since
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
is one-dimensional by Proposition
5.8
, it follows that
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))=\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{B}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

6.
Leibniz central extensions

The study of Leibniz cohomology was pioneered by the works of Loday and Pirashvili
[
LP93
,
Lod98
]
as a non-commutative analogue of Chevalley–Eilenberg cohomology. Leibniz cohomology can be defined for a more general class of objects called Leibniz algebras, of which Lie algebras are a special case. Analogously to Lie central extensions, the second Leibniz cohomology
\HL^{2}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
is in one-to-one correspondence with equivalence classes of one-dimensional Leibniz central extensions. We refer the reader to
[
LP93
]
and
[
FW21
]
for a more thorough introduction to Leibniz algebras than the one presented here.

Definition 6.1
.

A
right Leibniz algebra

\mathfrak{l}
is a vector space with a bilinear map
[-,-]\colon\mathfrak{l}\times\mathfrak{l}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying the Leibniz identity

[[x,y],z]=[x,[y,z]]+[[x,z],y]

for all
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{l}
. In other words, taking brackets on the right with an element
z\in\mathfrak{l}
is a derivation of
\mathfrak{l}
.

A
Leibniz 2-cocycle
on
\mathfrak{l}
is a bilinear map
\alpha\colon\mathfrak{l}\times\mathfrak{l}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

(6.1)

\alpha([x,y],z)=\alpha(x,[y,z])+\alpha([x,z],y)

for all
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{l}
.

Remark 6.2
.

An analogous definition exists for left Leibniz algebras, but we only consider right Leibniz algebras to maintain consistency with
[
GJP11
]
. When the product is alternating (i.e.
[x,x]=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{l}
), then the Leibniz identity is equivalent to the Jacobi identity and
\mathfrak{l}
is a Lie algebra.

We also note that if a Leibniz 2-cocycle is alternating (in other words, if
\alpha\in\Hom(\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}\mathfrak{l},\mathbb{C})
), then the Leibniz cocycle condition (
6.1
) is equivalent to the Chevalley–Eilenberg cocycle condition of Lie algebra cohomology.

In this section, we compute the inequivalent Leibniz central extensions of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, given by the second Leibniz cohomology
\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. The main result of this section is summarised in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We have

\displaystyle\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))+1=\begin{cases}4,\quad\lambda=0,\\
3,\quad\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))

\displaystyle=\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=3,

In particular,

\displaystyle\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\theta}_{A}

\displaystyle\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)),

where
\overline{\theta}_{A}
is the image of the Leibniz 2-cocycle defined by the symmetric, invariant bilinear form

\theta_{A}(A_{n},A_{m})=\begin{cases}1,\quad n=m=0,\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

in
\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
.

We start by stating a useful result for computing Leibniz cohomology of Lie algebras.

Proposition 6.4

(
[
HPL08
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

Let
\Inv(\mathfrak{g})
be the space of symmetric invariant bilinear forms on a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then there exists an exact sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\HH^{2}(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\HL^{2}(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Inv(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\HH^{3}(\mathfrak{g}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

where
\phi(\chi)(x,y)=\chi(x,y)+\chi(y,x)
and
h(\theta)(x,y,z)=\theta([x,y],z)
for all
\chi\in\HL^{2}(\mathfrak{g})
,
\theta\in\Inv(\mathfrak{g})
, and
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. The map
h
is known as the
Cartan–Koszul map
.

Thus, it suffices to look for symmetric invariant bilinear forms on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
and then check which of these satisfy the Leibniz cocycle condition (
6.1
).

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then, for all
\theta\in\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
, we have

\displaystyle\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{Z};

\displaystyle\theta(L_{n},X_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }n+m\neq 0;

\displaystyle\theta(X_{n},X_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}.

Proof.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. We repeatedly use the invariance of
\theta
on specific combinations of generators. First,

\theta([L_{0},L_{n}],L_{m})=-\theta([L_{n},L_{0}],L_{m})=-\theta(L_{n},[L_{0},L_{m}]).

Since
[L_{0},L_{n}]=nL_{n}
and
[L_{0},L_{m}]=mL_{m}
, we get

n\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=-m\theta(L_{n},L_{m}).

Therefore,
(n+m)\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=0
, which implies that

(6.2)

\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=0\quad\text{if }n+m\neq 0.

On the other hand,
\theta([L_{0},L_{n}],L_{m})=\theta(L_{0},[L_{n},L_{m}])
, so

(6.3)

n\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=(m-n)\theta(L_{0},L_{m+n})\quad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Letting
m=-n\neq 0
in (
6.3
) gives

(6.4)

\theta(L_{n},L_{-n})=-2\theta(L_{0},L_{0}).

Furthermore,

\theta([L_{2},L_{-1}],L_{-1})=-3\theta(L_{1},L_{-1})=6\theta(L_{0},L_{0}),

where we used (
6.4
) for the last equality. On the other hand,

\theta([L_{2},L_{-1}],L_{-1})=\theta(L_{2},[L_{-1},L_{-1}])=\theta(L_{2},0)=0.

It follows that

(6.5)

\theta(L_{0},L_{0})=0.

Thus, (
6.2
), (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) imply that
\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=0
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, as required.

Next, proceeding as above and using the fact that
[L_{0},X_{m}]=mX_{m}
for both
X=A
and
X=B
, we get
\theta(L_{n},X_{m})=0
if
n+m\neq 0
.

Finally, we have
\theta([L_{0},X_{n}],X_{m})=\theta(L_{0},[X_{n},X_{m}])=\theta(L_{0},0)=0
, and thus

n\theta(X_{n},X_{m})=0\quad\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Since
\theta
is symmetric, this proves the last statement of the lemma, and thereby completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
6.5
narrows down which components of any symmetric invariant bilinear form on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
can be non-zero.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We have

\dim(\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)))=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{if }X=A,\\
0,\quad&\text{if }X=B.\end{cases}

Proof.

Equipped with Lemma
6.5
, we now treat
X=A
and
X=B
as separate cases.

Case 1:

X=A
.

For any
\theta\in\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

n\theta(L_{n},A_{-n})=\theta([L_{0},L_{n}],A_{-n})=\theta(L_{0},[L_{n},A_{-n}])=0,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
[L_{n},A_{-n}]=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,

(6.6)

\theta(L_{n},A_{-n})=0\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

Now, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,

2\theta(L_{0},A_{0})=\theta([L_{-1},L_{1}],A_{0})=\theta(L_{-1},[L_{1},A_{0}])=\omega_{A(\lambda)}(1,0)\theta(L_{-1},A_{1})=0,

where we used (
6.6
) for the final equality. Thus
\theta(L_{0},A_{0})=0
, so we conclude that
\theta(L_{n},A_{m})=0
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, by Lemma
6.5
.

The only component that is unconstrained is
\theta(A_{0},A_{0})
. Hence,
\theta(A_{0},A_{0})=1
defines the unique symmetric invariant bilinear form on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, up to rescaling. In other words,
\dim(\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=1
.

Case 2:

X=B
.

Again, for any
\theta\in\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

n\theta(L_{n},B_{-n})=\theta([L_{0},L_{n}],B_{-n})=\theta(L_{0},[L_{n},B_{-n}])=\omega_{B(\lambda)}(n,-n)\theta(L_{0},B_{0}),

and thus, choosing
N\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)=-(N+N(N+1)\lambda)\neq 0
, we get

(6.7)

\theta(L_{0},B_{0})=\frac{N}{\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)}\theta(L_{N},B_{-N}).

Furthermore,

-2n\theta(B_{n},L_{-n})=\theta([B_{2n},L_{-n}],L_{-n})=\theta(B_{2n},[L_{-n},L_{-n}])=0,

which implies that

(6.8)

\theta(B_{n},L_{-n})=0\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

Letting
n=-N
in (
6.8
), we get
\theta(L_{N},B_{-N})=0
, so (
6.7
) becomes
\theta(L_{0},B_{0})=0
. Hence,
\theta(L_{n},B_{m})=0
for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, by Lemma
6.5
.

Again, the only component left to determine is
\theta(B_{0},B_{0})
. Unlike the case where
X=A
, this component can be constrained because
B_{0}\in[\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda),\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)]
. As before, choose
N\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)\neq 0
. Then
\theta([L_{N},B_{-N}],B_{0})=\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)\theta(B_{0},B_{0})
, so

\theta(B_{0},B_{0})=\frac{\theta([L_{N},B_{-N}],B_{0})}{\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)}=\frac{\theta(L_{N},[B_{-N},B_{0}])}{\omega_{B(\lambda)}(N,-N)}=0.

Together with Lemma
6.5
, we have shown that for any
\theta\in\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
, we have

\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=\theta(L_{n},B_{m})=\theta(B_{n},B_{m})=0

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, meaning
\dim(\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=0
, as required. This completes the proof.
∎

It follows form Propositions
6.4
and
6.6
that

\begin{gathered}\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))=\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda));\\
\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))\leq\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))\leq\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))+1.\end{gathered}

We now finish the proof of Theorem
6.3
by constructing a nontrivial Leibniz 2-cocycle on
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
which maps to a nonzero element of
\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
, proving that
\dim(\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=\dim(\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))+1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

As mentioned above, we have already shown the result for
X=B
, so assume that
X=A
.

It is straightforward to check that
\theta_{A}
satisfies the Leibniz cocycle condition (
6.1
). Since
\theta_{A}
maps to a nonzero element of
\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
under the map
\phi
of Proposition
6.4
, it follows that
\theta_{A}
gives an element of
\HL^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\setminus\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

Theorem
6.3
is consistent with the analogous results for
\mathcal{W}(0,1)
and
\mathcal{W}(0,0)
(see Theorem
2.10
), which are the
\lambda=0
cases of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, respectively. As pointed out in Remark
2.11
, our result for
\mathcal{W}(0,1)
is different to that of
[
GJP11
, Theorem 3.5]
. This is because the map
f^{0,1}
from
[
GJP11
, Lemma 3.2]
is not an invariant bilinear form, which can be seen in the proof of Proposition
6.6
for
X=A
and
\lambda=0
.

7.
Automorphisms

In this section, we compute the automorphism groups of the Lie algebras
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
. We start by defining some automorphisms.

Notation 7.1
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, define
\sigma_{\alpha}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
as follows:

\sigma_{\alpha}(L_{n})=\alpha^{n}L_{n},\quad\sigma_{\alpha}(X_{n})=\alpha^{n}X_{n}.

Define
\tau_{0}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(0))
as follows:

\tau_{0}(L_{n})=-L_{-n},\quad\tau_{0}(X_{n})=X_{-n}.

Define
\tau_{-1}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(-1))
as follows:

\tau_{-1}(L_{n})=-L_{-n},\quad\tau_{-1}(X_{n})=\begin{cases}-X_{0},&\text{if }n=0,\\
X_{-n},&\text{if }n\neq 0.\end{cases}

For
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, we define
\mu_{\xi}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
as follows:

\mu_{\xi}(L_{n})=L_{n},\quad\mu_{\xi}(X_{n})=\xi X_{n}.

For
X=A
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
, we define
\varphi^{A(\lambda)}_{a},\psi^{A(\lambda)}_{a}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
as follows:

\begin{gathered}\varphi^{A(\lambda)}_{a}(L_{n})=L_{n}+an^{2}A_{n},\quad\varphi^{A(\lambda)}_{a}(A_{n})=A_{n};\\
\psi^{A(\lambda)}_{a}(L_{n})=L_{n}+anA_{n},\quad\psi^{A(\lambda)}_{a}(A_{n})=A_{n}.\end{gathered}

For
X=B
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
, we define
\varphi^{B(\lambda)}_{a}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
and
\psi^{B(0)}_{a}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{B}(0))
as follows:

\begin{gathered}\varphi^{B(\lambda)}_{a}(L_{n})=\begin{cases}L_{0}+(\lambda+1)aB_{0},&\text{if }n=0\text{ and }\lambda\neq\infty,\\
L_{n}+aB_{n},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\varphi^{B(\lambda)}_{a}(B_{n})=B_{n};\\
\psi^{B(0)}_{a}(L_{n})=L_{n}+anB_{n},\quad\psi^{B(0)}_{a}(B_{n})=B_{n}.\end{gathered}

Finally, letting
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, we define

(7.1)

\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}=\tau_{\lambda}^{k}\circ\varphi_{a}^{X(\lambda)}\circ\psi_{b}^{X(\lambda)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},

where we let
\tau_{\lambda}=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)}
if
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
and
\psi_{b}^{B(\lambda)}=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)}
if
\lambda\neq 0
.

Although it is not entirely obvious from the definitions that the linear maps defined above are automorphisms, it will become clear that they preserve the Lie brackets in the proof of Proposition
7.14
.

Next, we consider inner automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, defined below.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra and let
x\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\ad_{x}
is locally nilpotent (in other words, for all
y\in\mathfrak{g}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\ad_{x}^{n}(y)=0
). The
exponential

\exp(\ad_{x})\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
of
\ad_{x}
is defined by

\exp(\ad_{x})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\ad_{x}^{n}=\id_{\mathfrak{g}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\ad_{x}^{n}.

The subgroup of
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
generated by
\{\exp(\ad_{x})\mid x\in\mathfrak{g}\text{ such that }\ad_{x}\text{ is locally nilpotent}\}
is called the group of
inner automorphisms
of
\mathfrak{g}
, denoted
\InnAut(\mathfrak{g})
.

Given a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the group of inner automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
. This is because

\sigma\circ\exp(\ad_{x})\circ\sigma^{-1}(y)=\sigma\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ad_{x}^{n}(\sigma^{-1}(y))\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ad_{\sigma(x)}^{n}(y)=\exp(\ad_{\sigma(x)})(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
, so
\sigma\circ\exp(\ad_{x})\circ\sigma^{-1}=\exp(\ad_{\sigma(x)})\in\InnAut(\mathfrak{g})
.

Remark 7.3
.

Even though we only defined the exponential of a locally nilpotent adjoint map, we can sometimes define exponentials of adjoint maps even when they are not locally nilpotent. For example, this is the case for
\ad_{L_{0}}
, which is not locally nilpotent, but if
t\in\mathbb{C}
, then
\exp(t\ad_{L_{0}})=\sigma_{e^{t}}
, where
\sigma_{\alpha}
is defined in Notation
7.1
.

On the other hand,
\exp(\ad_{L_{n}})
is not defined for
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, because the infinite sum

\exp(\ad_{L_{n}})(L_{m})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\ad_{L_{n}}^{k}(L_{m})

is not defined for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{n\}
.

We now compute
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then the locally nilpotent adjoint maps of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
are
\ad_{w}
for
w\in X(\lambda)
. Therefore,
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
is generated by

\{\exp(c\ad_{X_{n}})\mid n\in\mathbb{Z},c\in\mathbb{C}\}.

Letting
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
the action of
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is given by

\displaystyle{\left.\kern-1.2pt\exp(c\ad_{X_{n}})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X(\lambda)}}

\displaystyle=\id_{X(\lambda)},

\displaystyle\exp(c\ad_{X_{n}})(L_{m})

\displaystyle=L_{m}-c\omega_{X(\lambda)}(m,n)X_{n+m}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}L_{m}-c(n+m+m(m+1)\lambda\delta_{n}^{0})A_{n+m},&\text{if }X=A,\\
L_{m}-c(n-m(m+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})B_{n+m},&\text{if }X=B.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
w\in\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
such that
\ad_{w}
is locally nilpotent. Then
w=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}(\alpha_{i}L_{i}+\beta_{i}X_{i})
for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. It is clear that if
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
\ad_{w}
is not locally nilpotent. Thus,
w\in X(\lambda)
.

Conversely, let
w\in X(\lambda)
. Since
X(\lambda)
is an ideal of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, we have
\ad_{w}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\subseteq X(\lambda)
. Now,
X(\lambda)
is an abelian Lie algebra, so
\ad_{w}^{2}
is the zero map on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
. It follows that
\ad_{w}
is locally nilpotent, as required.

For the final sentence, note that

\exp(c\ad_{X_{n}})=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)}+c\ad_{X_{n}}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, since all the higher powers of
\ad_{X_{n}}
are zero. The result now follows by the definition of the bracket on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.
∎

By Lemma
7.4
, the action of an arbitrary element of
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
is given by

\prod_{i\in I}\exp(c_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})(L_{n})=L_{n}-\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\omega_{X(\lambda)}(n,i)X_{n+i},

where
I
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}
and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Furthermore, since
X(\lambda)
is an abelian subalgebra of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, it follows from Lemma
7.4
that
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
is an abelian normal subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. We now compute the group structure of the group of inner automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
, where

(7.2)

\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\coloneqq\{(z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{C},\text{ all but finitely many }z_{i}\text{ are zero}\}

is the additive group of finite
\mathbb{Z}
-sequences over
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Let
e_{n}
be the element of
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
with zeros everywhere except for a one in the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate. The map

\displaystyle\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(c\ad_{A_{n}})

\displaystyle\mapsto ce_{n}

is easily seen to be an isomorphism in the case
X=A
.

In the case
X=B
, we need a slightly different argument, since
B_{0}
is central, so
\exp(c\ad_{B_{0}})=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)}
for all
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore, the map

\displaystyle\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(c\ad_{B_{n}})

\displaystyle\mapsto ce_{n}\quad(n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})

is not an isomorphism, since
e_{0}
is not in the image. However, the image of the map is the group

(7.3)

\mathbb{C}_{0}^{\infty}\coloneqq\{(z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{C},\text{ all but finitely many }z_{i}\text{ are zero},z_{0}=0\},

which is easily seen to be isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then, given
a\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
\lambda\varphi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}+(\lambda+1)\psi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
if
\lambda\neq\infty
, while
\varphi_{a}^{A(\infty)}+\psi_{a}^{A(\infty)}\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\infty))
if
\lambda=\infty
. Defining

(7.4)

\varpi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\psi_{a}^{A(\lambda)},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\\
\varphi_{a}^{A(0)},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\end{cases}

then
\varpi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\notin\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Furthermore, none of the automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
from Notation
7.1
are inner.

Proof.

Given
a\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\exp(-a\ad_{A_{0}})(L_{n})=L_{n}+a(n+n(n+1)\lambda)A_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
\lambda\varphi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}+(\lambda+1)\psi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}=\exp(-a\ad_{A_{0}})
if
\lambda\neq\infty
, while
\varphi_{a}^{A(\infty)}+\psi_{a}^{A(\infty)}=\exp(-a\ad_{A_{0}})
if
\lambda=\infty
. Since
\ad_{A_{0}}
is the unique (up to multiplication by a scalar) locally nilpotent adjoint map of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, it follows that
\varpi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}
is not inner for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

On the other hand,
B_{0}
is central in
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, so
\exp(a\ad_{B_{0}})=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore, there are no inner derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
of degree zero, so the result follows.
∎

Despite the results of Lemma
7.6
, it will still be useful to consider both
\varphi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}
and
\psi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}
.

Given the observations of Lemma
7.6
, we introduce the following notation.

Notation 7.7
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Define

(7.5)

\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}=\tau_{\lambda}^{k}\circ\varpi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and as before, we let
\tau_{\lambda}=\id_{\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)}
if
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
. In other words,

\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}=\begin{cases}\Phi_{k;0,a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\\
\Phi_{k;a,0;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)},&\text{if }\lambda=0.\end{cases}

Define

G_{X}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\{\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},a\in\mathbb{C},\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\},&\text{if }X=A,\\
\{\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{B(\lambda)}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},a,b\in\mathbb{C},\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\},&\text{if }X=B,\end{cases}

where
\Psi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}
is defined in (
7.5
), and
\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{B(\lambda)}
is defined in (
7.1
).

Note that, by Lemma
7.4
, we have
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cap G_{X}(\lambda)=\{\id_{\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)}\}
for all
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Having introduced all the necessary notation, we now state the main result of this section, which computes the group structure of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
G_{X}(\lambda)
from Notation
7.7
is a subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
and

\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\rtimes G_{X}(\lambda).

Consequently,

\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=A\text{ and }\lambda\in\{0,-1\},\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}^{2}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=0,\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=-1,\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=A\text{ and }\lambda\notin\{0,-1\},\\
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda\notin\{0,-1\},\end{cases}

where
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
and
\mathbb{C}_{0}^{\infty}
are defined in (
7.2
) and (
7.3
). Given
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a,b,c,d,y_{i},z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
with only finitely many
y_{i}
and
z_{i}
being nonzero, and
\alpha,\beta,\xi,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the structures of the groups above are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{B}(0)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle((y_{i}),k,a,b,\alpha,\xi)\cdot((z_{i}),\ell,c,d,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=((y_{i}+\alpha^{\varepsilon_{1}i}\xi z_{\varepsilon_{1}i}),k+\ell,\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,b+\xi d,\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{A}(0),\mathcal{W}_{B}(-1)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle((y_{i}),k,a,\alpha,\xi)\cdot((z_{i}),\ell,c,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=((y_{i}+\alpha^{\varepsilon_{1}i}\xi z_{\varepsilon_{1}i}),k+\ell,\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{A}(-1)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle((y_{i}),k,b,\alpha,\xi)\cdot((z_{i}),\ell,d,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=((y_{i}+\varepsilon_{1}^{\delta_{i}^{0}}\alpha^{\varepsilon_{1}i}\xi z_{\varepsilon_{1}i}),k+\ell,b+\xi d,\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle((y_{i}),a,\alpha,\xi)\cdot((z_{i}),c,\beta,\xi)

\displaystyle=((y_{i}+\alpha^{i}\xi z_{i}),a+\xi c,\alpha\beta,\xi\zeta);

where
\varepsilon_{1}=(-1)^{k},\varepsilon_{2}=(-1)^{\ell}
, and
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
, and we assume that
y_{0}=z_{0}=0
if
X=B
.

Remark 7.9
.

Certainly, we have
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\times\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}_{0}^{\infty}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}\times\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
. We have chosen to leave these products of groups in the unsimplified forms to make the group structure clearer. Indeed, the automorphism groups of Theorem
7.8
are all isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}))
or
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})
with appropriate semi-direct product structures, but writing the groups this way makes the group structure less clear.

We have already considered inner automorphisms, so we now move on to computing arbitrary automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
to complete the proof of Theorem
7.8
. We begin by analysing the action of an automorphism of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
on the abelian ideal
X(\lambda)
of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. Then
\sigma(X(\lambda))\subseteq X(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Note that
\sigma(X(\lambda))
is an infinite-dimensional abelian ideal of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
, and therefore must be contained in
X(\lambda)
.
∎

The next result shows that we can exploit the structure of the automorphism group of the Witt algebra to get information about automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. Write
\overline{\sigma}
for the composition

\mathcal{W}\xrightarrow{\sigma}\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)/X(\lambda)\cong\mathcal{W}.

Then
\overline{\sigma}\in\Aut(\mathcal{W})
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
\overline{\sigma^{-1}}
is the inverse of
\overline{\sigma}
.
∎

Given Lemma
7.11
, it will be useful to consider automorphisms of the Witt algebra. These are well-known.

Proposition 7.12

(
[
Bav16
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, define automorphisms
\sigma_{\alpha},\tau\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
by

\sigma_{\alpha}(L_{n})=\alpha^{n}L_{n},\quad\tau(L_{n})=-L_{-n},

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\Aut(\mathcal{W})=\{\sigma_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\}\rtimes\{\id_{\mathcal{W}},\tau\}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

The next result shows that, after composing with an inner automorphism, we can greatly simplify the form which an automorphism of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
can take.

Proposition 7.13
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. Then there exists
\rho\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(\rho\circ\sigma)(L_{n})=\varepsilon\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+\gamma_{n}X_{\varepsilon n},\quad(\rho\circ\sigma)(X_{n})=\xi_{n}X_{\varepsilon n},

for some
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
,
\alpha,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, if
X=A
, then
\gamma_{0}=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.11
and Proposition
7.12
, we have
\overline{\sigma}=\tau^{k}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
k\in\{0,1\}
(where we write
\overline{\sigma}
as in the notation of Lemma
7.11
). Letting
\varepsilon=(-1)^{k}
, we therefore see that

(7.6)

\sigma(L_{n})=\varepsilon\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta_{i}^{(n)}X_{i},

for some
\beta_{i}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{C}
, where, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, only finitely many
\beta_{i}^{(n)}
are nonzero.

Let
\rho=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\exp(\frac{\varepsilon\beta_{i}^{(0)}}{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. By Lemma
7.4
, it follows that
\rho(X_{n})=X_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and

(7.7)

\rho(L_{0})=L_{0}-\varepsilon\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\beta_{i}^{(0)}X_{i},

since
[L_{0},X_{n}]=nX_{n}
for both
X=A
and
X=B
. Now, (
7.6
) and (
7.7
) imply that

\displaystyle\rho(\sigma(L_{0}))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\rho(L_{0})+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon L_{0}-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\beta_{i}^{(0)}X_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon L_{0}+\beta_{0}^{(0)}X_{0}.

Letting
\sigma^{\prime}=\rho\circ\sigma
, we can proceed as before to conclude that

(7.8)

\sigma^{\prime}(L_{n})=\varepsilon\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{i}^{(n)}X_{i},

for some
\gamma_{i}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{C}
, where, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, only finitely many
\gamma_{i}^{(n)}
are nonzero. By construction, we have
\gamma_{0}^{(0)}=\beta_{0}^{(0)}
and
\gamma_{i}^{(0)}=0
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

We have
n\sigma^{\prime}(L_{n})=\sigma^{\prime}([L_{0},L_{n}])=[\sigma^{\prime}(L_{0}),\sigma^{\prime}(L_{n})]
and thus, using (
7.8
) to expand,

(7.9)

\varepsilon n\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+n\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}=\varepsilon n\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+\varepsilon\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}i\gamma_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}-\varepsilon\alpha^{n}\gamma_{0}^{(0)}\omega_{X(\lambda)}(\varepsilon n,0)X_{\varepsilon n}.

It follows that, for
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{\varepsilon n\}
, we have
n\gamma_{m}^{(n)}=\varepsilon m\gamma_{m}^{(n)}
, so
(m-\varepsilon n)\gamma_{m}^{(n)}=0
. Therefore,
\gamma_{m}^{(n)}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{\varepsilon n\}
. Furthermore, comparing coefficients of
X_{\varepsilon n}
in (
7.9
), we see that

(7.10)

\omega_{X(\lambda)}(n,0)\gamma_{0}^{(0)}=0

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
\omega_{B(\lambda)}(n,0)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, so (
7.10
) does not give anything in the case
X=B
. On the other hand, if
X=A
, then (
7.10
) implies that
\gamma_{0}^{(0)}=0
.

Letting
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{\varepsilon n}^{(n)}
, we have shown that

(7.11)

\sigma^{\prime}(L_{n})=\varepsilon\alpha^{n}L_{\varepsilon n}+\gamma_{n}X_{\varepsilon n}.

Now, by Lemma
7.10
, we have

(7.12)

\sigma^{\prime}(X_{n})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi_{i}^{(n)}X_{i},

for some
\xi_{i}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{C}
, where, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, only finitely many
\xi_{i}^{(n)}
are nonzero. Since
[L_{0},X_{n}]=nX_{n}
, we have
[\sigma^{\prime}(L_{0}),\sigma^{\prime}(X_{n})]=n\sigma^{\prime}(X_{n})
. Therefore, it follows from (
7.11
) and (
7.12
) that

\varepsilon\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}i\xi_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}=n\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi_{i}^{(n)}X_{i}.

Hence,
(m-\varepsilon n)\xi_{m}^{(n)}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
\xi_{m}^{(n)}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{\varepsilon n\}
. Thus, letting
\xi_{n}=\xi_{\varepsilon n}^{(n)}
, we have

\sigma^{\prime}(X_{n})=\xi_{n}X_{\varepsilon n}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that, together with inner automorphisms, the automorphisms defined in Notation
7.1
generate the whole automorphism group of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. Then there exists
\rho\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
such that
\rho\circ\sigma=\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, where
\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}
is defined in (
7.1
).

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
. By Proposition
7.13
, possibly by replacing
\sigma
with
\rho\circ\sigma
for some
\rho\in\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
, we may assume that

(7.13)

\sigma(L_{n})=\alpha^{n}(\varepsilon L_{\varepsilon n}+\gamma_{n}X_{\varepsilon n}),\quad\sigma(X_{n})=\alpha^{n}\xi_{n}X_{\varepsilon n},

for some
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
,
\alpha,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\gamma_{0}=0
if
X=A
.

Since
[L_{n},L_{m}]=(m-n)L_{n+m}
, we have
[\sigma(L_{n}),\sigma(L_{m})]=(m-n)\sigma(L_{n+m})
. Therefore, applying (
7.13
) and comparing coefficients of
X_{\varepsilon n}
, we deduce that

(7.14)

\varepsilon(\omega_{X(\lambda)}(\varepsilon n,\varepsilon m)\gamma_{m}-\omega_{X(\lambda)}(\varepsilon m,\varepsilon n)\gamma_{n})=(m-n)\gamma_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Similarly, we have
[\sigma(L_{n}),\sigma(X_{m})]=\omega_{X(\lambda)}(n,m)\sigma(X_{n+m})
, so we can apply (
7.13
) and simplify to get

(7.15)

\varepsilon\omega_{X(\lambda)}(\varepsilon n,\varepsilon m)\xi_{m}=\omega_{X(\lambda)}(n,m)\xi_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, any bijective linear map
\sigma
defined as in (
7.13
) which satisfies (
7.14
) and (
7.15
) is an automorphism of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

We now consider the cases
X=A
and
X=B
separately.

Case 1:

X=A
.

Recall that in this case, we have
\gamma_{0}=0
. It follows from (
7.14
) and the definition of
\omega_{A(\lambda)}
that

(7.16)

(n+m+n(\varepsilon n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\gamma_{m}-(n+m+m(\varepsilon m+1)\lambda\delta_{n}^{0})\gamma_{n}=(m-n)\gamma_{n+m}.

Since
\gamma_{0}=0
, we have
\delta_{n}^{0}\gamma_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, we can simplify (
7.16
) as follows:

(7.17)

(n+m)(\gamma_{m}-\gamma_{n})=(m-n)\gamma_{n+m}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that (
7.17
) has the same form as (
5.6
), which we already solved in the proof of Proposition
5.8
: we have
\gamma_{n}=an^{2}+bn
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
(in fact,
a=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}
and
b=2\gamma_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{2}
).

We now compute
\xi_{n}
. By (
7.15
), we have

(7.18)

(n+m+n(\varepsilon n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\xi_{m}=(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\xi_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows from (
7.18
) that
\xi_{m}=\xi_{n+m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-m\}
. Letting
\xi=\xi_{1}
, we therefore conclude that
\xi_{n}=\xi
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

To compute
\xi_{0}
, we substitute
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
m=0
into (
7.18
) to get

(7.19)

(n+n(\varepsilon n+1)\lambda)\xi_{0}=(n+n(n+1)\lambda)\xi

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\varepsilon=1
, then (
7.19
) implies that
\xi_{0}=\xi
. It follows that
\sigma=\varphi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\circ\psi_{b}^{A(\lambda)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi}
(in other words,
\sigma=\Phi^{A(\lambda)}_{0,a,b,\alpha,\xi}
).

Now assume that
\varepsilon=-1
. In this case, if
\lambda\neq\infty
, (
7.19
) becomes

(\xi+\xi_{0})\lambda n+(\xi-\xi_{0})(\lambda+1)=0

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore,
(\xi+\xi_{0})\lambda=0
and
(\xi-\xi_{0})(\lambda+1)=0
. Similarly, if
\lambda=\infty
, then (
7.19
) gives
(\xi+\xi_{0})n+\xi-\xi_{0}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, from which it follows that
\xi_{0}=\xi=0
. We conclude that, when
\varepsilon=-1
, there are three possibilities:

(1)

\lambda=0
and
\xi_{0}=\xi
;

(2)

\lambda=-1
and
\xi_{0}=-\xi
;

(3)

\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,-1\}
and
\xi_{0}=\xi=0
.

However,
\sigma
is an automorphism, so we cannot have
\xi=0
. Therefore, the case
\varepsilon=-1
is not possible if
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,-1\}
. Therefore, in the two cases where
\varepsilon=-1
is possible (
\lambda=0
or
\lambda=-1
), we get
\sigma=\tau_{\lambda}\circ\varphi_{a}^{A(\lambda)}\circ\psi_{b}^{A(\lambda)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi}
(in other words,
\sigma=\Phi_{1;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}
). This concludes the proof when
X=A
.

Case 2:

X=B
.

Let
\gamma_{n}^{\prime}=\gamma_{\varepsilon n}
and
k=\varepsilon n
,
\ell=\varepsilon m
. Then (
7.14
) becomes

(7.20)

\omega_{B(\lambda)}(k,\ell)\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}-\omega_{B(\lambda)}(\ell,k)\gamma_{k}^{\prime}=(\ell-k)\gamma_{k+\ell}^{\prime},

for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. By (
7.20
) and the definition of
\omega_{B(\lambda)}
, we have

(7.21)

(\ell-k(k+1)\lambda\delta_{k+\ell}^{0})\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}-(k-\ell(\ell+1)\lambda\delta_{k+\ell}^{0})\gamma_{k}^{\prime}=(\ell-k)\gamma_{k+\ell}^{\prime}.

Note that (
7.21
) has the same form as (
5.3
), so we already know the solutions, thanks to the proof of Proposition
5.4
: if
\lambda=0
, then
\gamma_{n}^{\prime}=a^{\prime}n+b
for some
a^{\prime},b\in\mathbb{C}
, while for
\lambda\notin\{0,\infty\}
, we get

\gamma_{n}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}(\lambda+1)c,&\text{if }n=0;\\
c,&\text{if }n\neq 0.\end{cases}

for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. For
\lambda=\infty
, we get
\gamma_{n}^{\prime}=c
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, letting
a=\varepsilon a^{\prime}
, we conclude that
\gamma_{n}=an+b
if
\lambda=0
, while

\gamma_{n}=\begin{cases}(\lambda+1)c,&\text{if }n=0;\\
c,&\text{if }n\neq 0.\end{cases}

if
\lambda\notin\{0,\infty\}
, and
\gamma_{n}=c
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
if
\lambda=\infty
.

We finish by computing
\xi_{n}
. By (
7.15
), we get

(7.22)

(m-n(\varepsilon n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\xi_{m}=(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\xi_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Similarly to before, letting
\xi=\xi_{1}
, (
7.22
) immediately implies that
\xi_{n}=\xi
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

Finally, in order to compute
\xi_{0}
, we substitute
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
n=-m
into (
7.22
):

(7.23)

(m-m(\varepsilon m-1)\lambda)\xi=(m-m(m-1)\lambda)\xi_{0}

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. If
\varepsilon=1
, then it follows from (
7.23
) that
\xi_{0}=\xi
. Hence,

\sigma=\begin{cases}\varphi_{a}^{B(0)}\circ\psi_{b}^{B(0)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\varphi_{c}^{B(\lambda)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0.\end{cases}

In other words,
\sigma=\Phi_{0;a,b,\alpha,\xi}^{B(0)}
if
\lambda=0
, while
\sigma=\Phi_{0;c,0;\alpha,\xi}^{B(\lambda)}
if
\lambda\neq 0
.

Therefore, it remains to consider
\varepsilon=-1
. In this case, if
\lambda\neq\infty
, (
7.23
) becomes

(\xi+\xi_{0})\lambda m+(\xi-\xi_{0})(\lambda+1)=0

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. For
\lambda=\infty
, we instead get
(\xi+\xi_{0})m+\xi-\xi_{0}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Proceeding as we did in Case 1, we conclude that there are three possibilities when
\varepsilon=-1
:

(1)

\lambda=0
and
\xi_{0}=\xi
;

(2)

\lambda=-1
and
\xi_{0}=-\xi
;

(3)

\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,-1\}
and
\xi_{0}=\xi=0
.

It follows that, as before, the case
\varepsilon=-1
is not possible if
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,-1\}
. Therefore, in the cases where
\varepsilon=-1
is possible (
\lambda=0
or
\lambda=-1
), we get

\sigma=\begin{cases}\tau_{0}\circ\varphi_{a}^{B(0)}\circ\psi_{b}^{B(0)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\tau_{-1}\circ\varphi_{c}^{B(-1)}\circ\sigma_{\alpha}\circ\mu_{\xi},&\text{if }\lambda=-1.\end{cases}

In other words,
\sigma=\Phi_{1;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{B(0)}
if
\lambda=0
, while
\sigma=\Phi_{1;c,0;\alpha,\xi}^{B(-1)}
if
\lambda=-1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 7.15
.

As we saw before when we noted that
A(\lambda)
is the adjoint of
B(\lambda)
, once again we see the close relationship between
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
in the proof of Proposition
7.14
: when computing automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
, we got an equation (
7.17
) that we already saw when we computed central extensions of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
(
5.6
), and similarly for automorphisms of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
with (
7.21
) and (
5.3
). We will see this once again when we compute derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
in Section
8
.

The next result computes the group structure of the subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
generated by the automorphisms defined in Notation
7.1
.

Proposition 7.16
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Let
G_{X}(\lambda)
be as in Notation
7.7
. Then
G_{X}(\lambda)
is a subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
and

G_{X}(\lambda)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}^{2}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=0,\\
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ltimes(\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})),&\text{if }X=A\text{ and }\lambda\in\{0,-1\},\text{ or }X=B\text{ and }\lambda=-1,\\
\mathbb{C}\rtimes(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}),&\text{if }\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}.\end{cases}

Given
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\xi,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the structure of the groups above is the following:

\displaystyle G_{B}(0)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle(k,a,b,\alpha,\xi)\cdot(\ell,c,d,\ell,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=(k+\ell,\varepsilon a+\xi c,b+\xi d,\alpha^{\varepsilon}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle G_{A}(0),G_{B}(-1)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle(k,a,\alpha,\xi)\cdot(\ell,c,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=(k+\ell,\varepsilon a+\xi c,\alpha^{\varepsilon}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle G_{A}(-1)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle(k,b,\alpha,\xi)\cdot(\ell,d,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=(k+\ell,b+\xi d,\alpha^{\varepsilon}\beta,\xi\zeta);

\displaystyle G_{X}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\colon

\displaystyle(a,\alpha,\xi)\cdot(c,\beta,\zeta)

\displaystyle=(a+\xi c,\alpha\beta,\xi\zeta);

where
\varepsilon=(-1)^{\ell}
and
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
. Furthermore,
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\rtimes G_{X}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Let
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\xi,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, where we assume that
k=\ell=0
if
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
. We claim that

(7.24)

\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\circ\Phi_{\ell;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}=\Phi_{k+\ell;(-1)^{\ell}a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha^{(-1)^{\ell}}\beta,\xi\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}.

We will only prove the claim in the case
X=A
, since the case
X=B
is similar.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}=(-1)^{k},\varepsilon_{2}=(-1)^{\ell}
, and
\varepsilon=(-1)^{k+\ell}
(recall the assumption that
k=\ell=0
if
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
, so
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon=1
in these cases). We have

\displaystyle\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(L_{n})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n}(\varepsilon_{1}L_{\varepsilon_{1}n}+(an^{2}+bn)A_{\varepsilon_{1}n}),

\displaystyle\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\xi A_{0},&\text{if }\lambda=-1,n=0,\text{ and }k=1,\\
\alpha^{n}\xi A_{\varepsilon_{1}n},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(\Phi_{\ell;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{A(\lambda)}(L_{n}))

\displaystyle=\beta^{n}\left(\varepsilon_{2}\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(L_{\varepsilon_{2}n})+(cn^{2}+dn)\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{\varepsilon_{2}n})\right)

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta)^{n}\left(\varepsilon L_{\varepsilon n}+\varepsilon_{2}(an^{2}+\varepsilon_{2}bn)A_{\varepsilon n}+(cn^{2}+dn)\xi A_{\varepsilon n}\right)

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta)^{n}\left(\varepsilon L_{\varepsilon n}+((\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c)n^{2}+(b+\xi d)n)A_{\varepsilon n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{k+\ell;\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta}^{A(\lambda)}(L_{n}),

\displaystyle\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(\Phi_{\ell;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{n}))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\zeta\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{0}),&\text{if }\lambda=-1,n=0,\text{ and }\ell=1,\\
\beta^{n}\zeta\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{\varepsilon_{2}n}),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\xi\zeta A_{0},&\text{if }\lambda=-1,n=0,\text{ and }k+\ell=1,\\
(\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta)^{n}\xi\zeta A_{\varepsilon n},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{k+\ell;\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta}^{A(\lambda)}(A_{n}).

This proves the the claim.

The computation of the group structures now follows immediately from the claim, the definition of
G_{X}(\lambda)
in the statement of Theorem
7.8
, and the definitions of
\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}
and
\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(\lambda)}
from Notation
7.1
and Lemma
7.4
. The difference in the group structures of
G_{A}(-1)
and
G_{A}(0)
comes from the fact that, by definition,
G_{A}(-1)
is generated by
\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(-1)}=\Phi_{k;0,a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(-1)}
, while
G_{A}(0)
is generated by
\Psi_{k;a;\alpha,\xi}^{A(0)}=\Phi_{k;a,0;\alpha,\xi}^{A(0)}
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

For the final sentence, note that
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))G_{X}(\lambda)
by Proposition
7.14
. Since
\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cap G_{X}(\lambda)=\{\id_{\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)}\}
by Lemma
7.4
, it follows that this is a semi-direct product. This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
7.8
.

Proof of Theorem
7.8
.

By Proposition
7.16
, we have

\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\InnAut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\rtimes G_{X}(\lambda).

Let
a,b,c,d,y_{i},z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\xi,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, where we assume that only finitely many
y_{i}
and
z_{i}
are nonzero. These parameters allow us to take the product of two elements of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
: we will compute the product

(7.25)

\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})\Phi_{0;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}\right).

Recall that if
X=B
, we may assume that
y_{0}=z_{0}=0
since
B_{0}
is central. Note that

(7.26)

\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})=\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\exp(z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})(\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)})^{-1}\right)\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}.

Letting
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(7.27)

\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\exp(z\ad_{X_{n}})(\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)})^{-1}=\exp\left(z\ad_{\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}(X_{n})}\right)=\exp(z\alpha^{n}\xi\ad_{X_{n}}).

Therefore,

(7.28)

\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})=\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(\alpha^{j}\xi z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)},

by (
7.26
) and (
7.27
). Using (
7.28
), the product (
7.25
) becomes

\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{j}\alpha^{j}\xi\ad_{X_{j}})\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\Phi_{0.;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp((y_{i}+\alpha^{i}\xi z_{i})\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{0;a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha\beta,\xi\zeta}^{X(\lambda)},

where we used (
7.24
) for the final equality. In summary, we have

\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{0;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{j}\ad_{X_{j}})\Phi_{0;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}\right)=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left((y_{i}+\alpha^{i}\xi z_{i})\ad_{X_{i}}\right)\Phi_{0;a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha\beta,\xi\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}.

This proves the result in the case
\lambda\notin\{0,-1\}
.

To complete the proof, assume that
\lambda\in\{0,-1\}
. For these values of
\lambda
, we need to consider how
\tau_{\lambda}
interacts with the other automorphisms. We proceed similarly to above to deduce that

\displaystyle\tau_{\lambda}\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\tau_{\lambda}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\tau_{\lambda}\right)\tau_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{\tau_{\lambda}(X_{i})})\right)\tau_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{-i}\ad_{X_{i}})\tau_{0},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp((-1)^{\delta_{i}^{0}}y_{-i}\ad_{X_{i}})\tau_{-1},&\text{if }\lambda=-1,\end{cases}

Therefore, if we multiply two arbitrary elements of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
, we get

\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(y_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{k;a,b;\alpha,\xi}^{X(\lambda)}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(z_{i}\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{\ell;c,d;\beta,\zeta}^{X(\lambda)}\right)\\
=\begin{cases}\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp((y_{i}+\alpha^{\varepsilon_{1}i}\xi z_{\varepsilon_{1}i})\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{k+\ell;\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta}^{X(\lambda)},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp((y_{i}+\varepsilon_{1}^{\delta_{i}^{0}}\alpha^{\varepsilon_{1}i}\xi z_{\varepsilon_{1}i})\ad_{X_{i}})\Phi_{k+\ell;\varepsilon_{2}a+\xi c,b+\xi d;\alpha^{\varepsilon_{2}}\beta,\xi\zeta}^{X(\lambda)},&\text{if }\lambda=-1,\end{cases}

where
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\varepsilon_{1}=(-1)^{k}
,
\varepsilon_{2}=(-1)^{\ell}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

8.
Derivations

Next, we compute derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
. As remarked in Section
2
, computing derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
is equivalent to computing
\HH^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{g})
. There is a close relationship between the automorphisms of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and its derivations: if we have a (smooth) one-parameter subgroup of
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
, differentiating it at
0
gives a derivation of
\mathfrak{g}
. In other words, derivations of
\mathfrak{g}
can be seen as “infinitesimal automorphisms”. We now introduce notation for the derivations that arise from one-parameter subgroups of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
.

Notation 8.1
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Recall the automorphisms defined in Notation
7.1
. For both
X=A
and
X=B
, define

d_{\Ab}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\mu_{e^{t}}\in\Der(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)).

For
X=A
, define

\delta_{\lambda}^{A}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\varphi_{t}^{A(\lambda)},\quad\partial_{\lambda}^{A}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\psi_{t}^{A(\lambda)}\in\Der(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)).

For
X=B
, define

d_{\lambda}^{B}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\varphi_{t}^{B(\lambda)}\in\Der(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)).

For
X=B
and
\lambda=0
, define

\partial^{B}_{0}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\psi_{t}^{B(0)}\in\Der(\mathcal{W}_{B}(0)).

Remark 8.2
.

Certainly, there is another one-parameter subgroup
\sigma_{e^{t}}
of
\Aut(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
, giving rise to another derivation. However, the derivation we obtain from this one-parameter subgroup is inner: we have

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\sigma_{e^{t}}=\ad_{L_{0}}\in\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)).

Indeed, we can immediately see this from the observations of Remark
7.3
.

Having defined these derivations that arise from automorphisms, we now compute their actions on
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\begin{gathered}d_{\Ab}(L_{n})=0,\quad d_{\Ab}(X_{n})=X_{n};\\
\delta_{\lambda}^{A}(L_{n})=nA_{n},\quad\delta_{\lambda}^{A}(A_{n})=0;\\
\partial_{\lambda}^{A}(L_{n})=n(n+1)A_{n},\quad\partial_{\lambda}^{A}(A_{n})=0;\\
d_{\lambda}^{B}(L_{n})=\begin{cases}(\lambda+1)B_{0},&\text{if }n=0\text{ and }\lambda\neq\infty,\\
B_{n},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad d_{\lambda}^{B}(B_{n})=0;\\
\partial^{B}_{0}(L_{n})=nB_{n},\quad\partial^{B}_{0}(B_{n})=0,\end{gathered}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from the definitions of the automorphisms in Notation
7.1
.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

Using the identifications
A(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]dt
and
B(\lambda)=\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
, we now give formulas for the derivations of Notation

in terms of polynomials. We have

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}^{A}(f\partial)

\displaystyle=d(t^{-1}f)=(t^{-1}f^{\prime}-t^{-2}f)dt,

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}^{A}(f\partial)

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}^{B}(f\partial)

t^{-1}f,&\text{if }\lambda=\infty,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\partial_{0}^{B}(f\partial)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}-t^{-1}f.

By the observations of Remark
3.6
, it is now clear that, under the isomorphism
\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda)^{\prime})
, we have the following correspondences:

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}^{A}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\Omega^{B}_{\Mix}\quad(\lambda\neq 0),

\displaystyle\partial_{0}^{A}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\Omega^{B}_{\Mix}\quad(\lambda=0),

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}^{B}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\Omega^{A}_{\Mix},

\displaystyle\partial_{0}^{B}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\Omega^{A}_{0},

where we have used that
A(\lambda)^{\prime}\cong B(\lambda)
and

under the form
\mathcal{B}
.

We now consider the question of when the derivations defined in Notation
8.1
are inner.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. If
\lambda\neq\infty
, then
(\lambda+1)\delta_{\lambda}^{A}+\lambda\partial_{\lambda}^{A}\in\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
, while if
\lambda=\infty
, then
\delta_{\infty}^{A}+\partial_{\infty}^{A}=\ad_{-A_{0}}\in\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\infty))
. Defining

d_{\lambda}^{A}=\begin{cases}\delta_{\lambda}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\\
\partial_{0}^{A},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\end{cases}

then
d_{\lambda}^{A}\notin\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Certainly, we have

d_{\lambda}^{A}=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\varpi_{t}^{A(\lambda)},

where
\varpi_{t}^{A(\lambda)}
is defined in (
7.4
).

Furthermore,
d_{\lambda}^{B}\notin\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
, and there is no nonzero linear combination of
d_{0}^{B}
and
\partial^{B}_{0}
which produces an inner derivation of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(0)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
8.3
, we can easily see that
(\lambda+1)\delta_{\lambda}^{A}+\lambda\partial_{\lambda}^{A}=\ad_{-A_{0}}
if
\lambda\neq\infty
, while
\delta_{\infty}^{A}+\partial_{\infty}^{A}=\ad_{-A_{0}}
if
\lambda=\infty
. Since
\ad_{A_{0}}
and
\ad_{L_{0}}
span the space of inner derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
of degree zero, it follows immediately that
d_{\lambda}^{A}
is not inner for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

The only nonzero (up to multiplication by a scalar) inner derivation of
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
of degree zero is
\ad_{L_{0}}
. This is because
B_{0}
is central in
\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)
, so

. The result follows.
∎

The main goal of this section is to prove that, together with inner derivations, the derivations defined in Notation

span the entire space of derivations of

.

Theorem 8.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\lambda}^{A};

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))

\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\lambda}^{B},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\end{cases}

where the derivations are defined in Notation

and Lemma

. Consequently,

\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda);\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)))=2,\quad\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda);\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda)))=\begin{cases}3,&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\

Remark 8.7
.

Theorem
8.6
says that all derivations of
\mathcal{W}(0,1)
extend to the one-parameter family
\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda)
. On the other hand, all derivations in
\Inn(\mathcal{W}(0,0))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{0}^{B}\subseteq\Der(\mathcal{W}(0,0))
extend to the one-parameter family

, while
\partial_{0}^{B}
does not.

The next result greatly simplifies the computation of derivations of
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{g})=0
, and let
\mathfrak{M}
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module such that
\Hom_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M},\mathfrak{g})=0
. Then

H^{1}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M};\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\oplus\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M}),

where we endow
\mathfrak{M}
with an abelian Lie algebra structure.

Proof.

Define

\alpha\colon\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})\hookrightarrow\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}),\quad\beta\colon\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M})\hookrightarrow\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})

by

\alpha(D)(x)=D(x),\quad\alpha(D)(v)=0,

\beta(\varphi)(x)=0,\quad\beta(\varphi)(v)=\varphi(v),

where
D\in\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
,
\varphi\in\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M})
,
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
v\in\mathfrak{M}
. It is straightforward to check that
\alpha(D)
and
\beta(\varphi)
are elements of
\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
.

Let
D\in\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
. Let
\overline{D}
be the composition

\mathfrak{g}\xrightarrow{D}\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}\twoheadrightarrow(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})/\mathfrak{M}\cong\mathfrak{g}.

It is easy to see that
\overline{D}\in\Der(\mathfrak{g})
. But
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{g})=0
, so
\Der(\mathfrak{g})=\Inn(g)
. Therefore,
\overline{D}\in\Inn(\mathfrak{g})
, so there exists
w\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\overline{D}=\ad_{w}
. Define

D^{\prime}\coloneqq D-\ad_{w}\in\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}).

By construction,
D^{\prime}(x)\in\mathfrak{M}
for all
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. In other words,
{\left.\kern-1.2ptD^{\prime}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{g}}}\in\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
.

We claim that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptD^{\prime}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{M}}}
is a homomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. Indeed, for
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
v\in\mathfrak{M}
, we have

D^{\prime}(x\cdot v)=D^{\prime}([x,v])=[x,D^{\prime}(v)]+[D^{\prime}(x),v]=[x,D^{\prime}(v)]=x\cdot D^{\prime}(v),

where we used that
D^{\prime}(x)\in\mathfrak{M}
to deduce that
[D^{\prime}(x),v]=0
. Now let
\widehat{D}^{\prime}
be the composition

\mathfrak{M}\xrightarrow{D^{\prime}}\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{g}.

Since
\widehat{D}^{\prime}
is the composition of two
\mathfrak{g}
-module homomorphisms,
\widehat{D}^{\prime}
is also a
\mathfrak{g}
-module homomorphism. But since
\Hom_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M},\mathfrak{g})=0
by assumption, we have
\widehat{D}^{\prime}=0
. Therefore,
D^{\prime}(v)\in\mathfrak{M}
for all
v\in\mathfrak{M}
, and thus
{\left.\kern-1.2ptD^{\prime}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{M}}}\in\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M})
.

Define
D_{1}\coloneqq\alpha({\left.\kern-1.2ptD^{\prime}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{g}}})
and
D_{2}\coloneqq\beta({\left.\kern-1.2ptD^{\prime}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathfrak{M}}})
. Certainly,
D=\ad_{w}+D_{1}+D_{2}
. It follows that

(8.1)

\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})=\Big(\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})+\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})\Big)\oplus\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M}),

where we view
\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
and
\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M})
as subsets of
\Der(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
. Note that
\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})\cap\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})=\Inn(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})
, and thus

\frac{\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})+\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})}{\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})}\cong\frac{\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})}{\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})\cap\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})}=\frac{\Der(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})}{\Inn(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{M})}\cong H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M}).

Therefore, taking the quotient by
\Inn(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})
on both sides of (
8.1
), we get

H^{1}(\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M};\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{M})\cong H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathfrak{M})\oplus\End_{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathfrak{M}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that Proposition
8.8
applies to
\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda),\mathcal{W})=0
.

Proof.

The internally graded structures of
\mathcal{W}
and
X(\lambda)
immediately imply that any element of the space
\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda),\mathcal{W})
must be graded of degree 0. Therefore, if
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda),\mathcal{W})
, then

\varphi(X_{n})=\eta_{n}L_{n}.

We now split the proof into the cases
X=A
and
X=B
.

Case 1:

X=A
.

The equality
\varphi([L_{n},A_{m}])=[L_{n},\varphi(A_{m})]
gives

(8.2)

(m-n)\eta_{m}=(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\eta_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Letting
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
n=-m
in (
8.2
), we get
\eta_{m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. If we set
m=0,n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
in (
8.2
), we also deduce that
\eta_{0}=0
. This proves the case
X=A
.

Case 2:

X=B
.

The equality
\varphi([L_{n},B_{m}])=[L_{n},\varphi(B_{m})]
gives

(8.3)

(m-n)\eta_{m}=(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\eta_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Setting
m=0
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
(
8.3
), we get
\eta_{0}=0
. If we let
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
n=-m
in (
8.3
), we also deduce that
\eta_{m}=0
for
m\neq 0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
8.8
, together with Lemma
8.9
, and the well-known fact that
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\mathcal{W})=0
(see
[
Buz24
, Proposition 7.2]
or
[
IK90
]
), imply the following.

Corollary 8.10
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\Der(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\Inn(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))+\Der(\mathcal{W},X(\lambda))+\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda)),

and thus

\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))=\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda))\oplus\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda)).\qed

Therefore, to prove Theorem
8.6
it suffices to compute
\Der(\mathcal{W},X(\lambda))
and
\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))
. For
\Der(\mathcal{W},X(\lambda))
, we have the isomorphism

\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda)^{\prime})\cong\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda)),

so we do not need to perform any further computations, since
\HH_{\Mix}^{2}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
was already computed in Proposition
5.1
.

Proposition 8.11
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W},A(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Inn(\mathcal{W},A(\lambda))\oplus d_{\lambda}^{A},

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{W},B(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Inn(\mathcal{W},B(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{0}^{B}\oplus\mathbb{C}\partial_{0}^{B},&\text{if }\lambda=0,\\
\Inn(\mathcal{W},B(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{0}^{B},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\end{cases}

where the derivations are defined in Notation
8.1
and Lemma
8.5
.

Proof.

By Propositions
5.1
and
5.8
, we know that
\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))
is spanned by
\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{B}
, where
\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{B}
is defined in Theorem
3.5
. By the isomorphism

(8.4)

\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}_{B}(\lambda))\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};B(\lambda)^{\prime})\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};A(\lambda)),

it follows that
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};A(\lambda))
is also one-dimensional. By Remark
8.4
, the image of
\overline{\Omega}_{\Mix}^{B}
under the isomorphism (
8.4
) is
\overline{d}_{\lambda}^{A}
, where
\overline{d}_{\lambda}^{A}
is the class of
d_{\lambda}^{A}
in
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};A(\lambda))
. Therefore,

\Der(\mathcal{W},A(\lambda))=\Inn(\mathcal{W},A(\lambda))\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\lambda}^{A},

as required.

The computation of
\Der(\mathcal{W},B(\lambda))
follows similarly by applying Propositions
5.1
and
5.4
, and the isomorphism

\HH^{2}_{\Mix}(\mathcal{W}_{A}(\lambda))\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};A(\lambda)^{\prime})\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};B(\lambda)),

using the correspondences in Remark
8.4
.
∎

We now proceed with the computation of
\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))
, which is the last step in the proof of Theorem
8.6
.

Proposition 8.12
.

Let
X\in\{A,B\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then

\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))=\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab},

where
d_{\Ab}
is defined in Notation
8.1
.

Proof.

The internally graded structures of
\mathcal{W}
and
X(\lambda)
imply that any
\mathcal{W}
-endomorphism of
X(\lambda)
is graded of degree 0. Letting
\varphi\in\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))
, this means that

\varphi(X_{n})=\theta_{n}X_{n}

for some
\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Replacing
\varphi
with
\varphi-\theta_{0}d_{\Ab}
, we may assume that
\theta_{0}=0
. To prove the result, it suffices to show that
\varphi=0
. We now analyse the cases
X=A
and
X=B
separately.

Case 1:

X=A
.

The equality
\varphi(L_{n}\cdot A_{m})=L_{n}\cdot\varphi(A_{m})
gives

(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\theta_{m}=(n+m+n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{m}^{0})\theta_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
\theta_{n}=\theta_{0}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. This completes the proof in the case
X=A
.

Case 2:

X=B
.

The equality
\varphi(L_{n}\cdot B_{m})=L_{n}\cdot\varphi(B_{m})
gives

(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\theta_{m}=(m-n(n+1)\lambda\delta_{n+m}^{0})\theta_{n+m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
\theta_{n}=\theta_{0}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, so we are done.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
8.6
.

Proof of Theorem
8.6
.

By Corollary
8.10
, we have

\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda);\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda))\oplus\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda)).

Proposition
8.11
computes the cohomology space
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};X(\lambda))
, and Proposition
8.12
computes
\End_{\mathcal{W}}(X(\lambda))
.
∎

9.
Cohomology of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}

We finish the paper by computing one-dimensional central extensions and derivations of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\ltimes\widetilde{I}
. For ease of notation, we view
\widetilde{I}
as the space of Laurent polynomials with no constant term. Furthermore, thanks to the isomorphism
\widetilde{I}\cong\widetilde{J}
, we will often switch between
\widetilde{I}
and
\widetilde{J}
depending on the situation.

We start with the computation of one-dimensional central extensions.

Theorem 9.1
.

We have
\dim(\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}))=4
. Specifically,

\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Vir}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}\overline{\Omega}_{\Ab},

where
\Omega_{\Vir}
is the Gelfand–Fuchs cocycle in (
3.7
), and the other cocycles are defined as follows:

\begin{gathered}\Omega_{1}(f\partial,g)=\mathcal{B}(df^{\prime},g)=\Res(g\ df^{\prime}),\\
\Omega_{2}(f\partial,g)=\mathcal{B}(d(t^{-1}f),g)=\Res(g\ d(t^{-1}f)),\\
\Omega_{\Ab}(f,g)=\langle f,g\rangle,\end{gathered}

where
\langle-,-\rangle
and
\mathcal{B}
are defined in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) respectively, and we have only specified the non-zero components of the cocycles.

To prove Theorem
9.1
, we use the decomposition

\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W})\oplus\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})\oplus\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})

from Proposition
3.1
, recalling the self-duality
\widetilde{I}^{\prime}\cong\widetilde{I}
.

As mentioned in Section
2
, the classes of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
span
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
. The proof of this follows as an immediate consequence of Proposition
8.11
.

Corollary 9.2
.

We have
\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{I})=\Inn(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{I})\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{2}
, where
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are defined in Section
2
. Consequently,

\dim(\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I}))=2.

Proof.

Recall the isomorphism

\widetilde{I}\cong\widetilde{J}=\mathcal{W}\cdot A(0)=\spn\{A_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}.

Since
\widetilde{J}\subseteq A(0)
, we have

\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})\subseteq\Der(\mathcal{W},A(0)).

Therefore,
\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})
consists of the elements of
\Der(\mathcal{W},A(0))
whose images are contained in
\widetilde{J}
. By Proposition
8.11
, all elements of
\Der(\mathcal{W},A(0))
have this property, in other words,

\Der(\mathcal{W},A(0))=\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J}).

Noting that
\partial_{0}^{A}=\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{0}^{A}=\delta_{2}
, it follows that
\Der(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})=\Inn(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{2}
. It is easy to see that the sum is direct, because
\Inn(\mathcal{W},\widetilde{J})
does not contain any elements of degree zero.
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
9.1
, it remains to compute
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})
, which we do next.

Lemma 9.3
.

The unique (up to multiplication by a scalar)
\mathcal{W}
-invariant skew-symmetric form on
\widetilde{I}
is
\langle-,-\rangle
, where
\langle-,-\rangle
is defined in (
2.4
). Consequently,

\dim(\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I}))=1.

Proof.

Suppose
\Omega\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{J})\setminus\{0\}
. By Theorem
3.10
,

\Omega(A_{n},A_{m})=\alpha(n)\delta_{n+m}^{0}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, where
\alpha\colon\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}
. By the
\mathcal{W}
-invariance of
\Omega
, we have

\Omega(A_{n},L_{-n-m}\cdot A_{m})+\Omega(L_{-n-m}\cdot A_{n},A_{m})=0

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore,

n\alpha(n)-m\alpha(m)=0

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Setting
m=1
, we get
\alpha(n)=\frac{\alpha(1)}{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

By assumption,
\Omega\neq 0
, so
\alpha(1)\neq 0
. Rescaling if necessary, we may assume that
\alpha(1)=1
. Therefore,

\Omega(A_{n},A_{m})=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{n+m}^{0}

is the unique (up to multiplication by a scalar)
\mathcal{W}
-invariant skew-symmetric form on
\widetilde{J}
. Under the isomorphism
\widetilde{J}\cong\widetilde{I}
, the form
\Omega
is precisely
\langle-,-\rangle
.
∎

The computation of
\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
follows easily.

Proof of Theorem
9.1
.

We use the decomposition

\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\HH^{2}(\mathcal{W})\oplus\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})\oplus\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})

from Proposition
3.1
, and the correspondences between
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
and mixing cocycles, and between
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})
and abelian cocycles.

It is easy to see that
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
give rise to the cocycles
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively. Therefore,
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
and
\overline{\Omega}_{2}
span the space of mixing cocycles of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
, by Corollary
9.2
.

Certainly
\Omega_{\Ab}
is simply the form
\langle-,-\rangle
trivially extended to the whole of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
. Therefore, by Lemma
9.3
,
\Omega_{\Ab}
is the unique (up to scalar multiplication) abelian cocycle on
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.
∎

Next, we compute Leibniz central extensions of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
. As we did in Section
6
, we do this by computing
\Inv(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
. The computation of
\Inv(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
follows easily from the computation of
\Inv(\mathcal{W}_{X}(\lambda))
from Section
6
.

Theorem 9.4
.

We have
\HL^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\cong\HH^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.4
, it suffices to show that
\Inv(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=0
. Letting
\theta\in\Inv(\mathcal{W})
, the proof of Lemma
6.5
implies that

\displaystyle\theta(L_{n},L_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{Z};

\displaystyle\theta(L_{n},A_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }n\in\mathbb{Z},m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\text{ and }n+m\neq 0;

\displaystyle\theta(A_{n},A_{m})=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

where we view
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
as
\mathcal{W}\ltimes\widetilde{J}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{A}(0)
. Therefore, it remains to show that
\theta(L_{n},A_{-n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. This is done in the proof of Proposition
6.6
.
∎

We end with the computation of derivations of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

Theorem 9.5
.

We have
\Der(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})=\Inn(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\delta_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}d_{\Ab}
, where
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are defined in Section
2
, and
d_{\Ab}
is defined as:

d_{\Ab}(f\partial)=0,\quad{\left.\kern-1.2ptd_{\Ab}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\widetilde{I}}}=\id_{\widetilde{I}},

for all
f\in\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
. Consequently,
\dim(\HH^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}};\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}))=3
.

To prove Theorem
9.5
, we would like to apply Proposition
8.8
, which requires us to prove that
\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I},\mathcal{W})=0
.

Lemma 9.6
.

We have
\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I},\mathcal{W})=0
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{J},\mathcal{W})=0
. The internally graded structures of
\widetilde{J}
and
\mathcal{W}
imply that there exist
\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\varphi(A_{n})=\eta_{n}L_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
\varphi([L_{n},A_{m}])=[L_{n},\varphi(A_{m})]
, we get

(n+m)\eta_{n+m}=(m-n)\eta_{m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Setting
n=-m
, we deduce that
\eta_{m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, so
\varphi=0
.
∎

By Proposition
8.8
and Lemma
9.6
, we know that
\HH^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
decomposes into

\HH^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})\oplus\End_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I}).

Since
\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})
was already computed in Corollary
9.2
, it only remains to compute
\End_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})
.

Lemma 9.7
.

We have
\End_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})=\mathbb{C}\id_{\widetilde{I}}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\End_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{J})\setminus\{0\}
. By the internally graded structures of
\mathcal{W}
and
\widetilde{J}
, there exist
\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\varphi(A_{n})=\theta_{n}A_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
\varphi
is a map of
\mathcal{W}
-modules, we have
\varphi(L_{n}\cdot A_{m})=L_{n}\cdot\varphi(A_{m})
, and thus

(n+m)\theta_{n+m}=(n+m)\theta_{m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. It follows that
\theta_{n}=\theta_{1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
\varphi\neq 0
by assumption, we know that
\theta_{1}\neq 0
. Rescaling if necessary, we may assume that
\theta_{1}=1
. Therefore,
\varphi(A_{n})=A_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, so
\varphi=\id_{\widetilde{J}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
9.5
.

Follows immediately from the decomposition

\HH^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})\cong\HH^{1}(\mathcal{W};\widetilde{I})\oplus\End_{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{I})

combined with Corollary
9.2
and Lemma
9.7
.
∎
