Baker–Akhiezer function for the deformed root system B ​ C ​ ( l , 1 ) BC(l,1) and bispectrality
Iain McWhinnie, Liam Rooke, Martin Vrabec

Abstract
We show that a Sergeev–Veselov difference operator of rational Macdonald–Ruijsenaars (MR) type for the deformed root system B ​ C ​ ( l , 1 ) BC(l,1) preserves a ring of quasi-invariants in the case of non-negative integer values of the multiplicity parameters. We prove that in this case the operator admits a (multidimensional) Baker–Akhiezer eigenfunction, which depends on spectral parameters and which is, moreover, as a function of the spectral variables an eigenfunction for the (trigonometric) generalised Calogero–Moser–Sutherland (CMS) Hamiltonian for B ​ C ​ ( l , 1 ) BC(l,1) . By an analytic continuation argument, we generalise this eigenfunction also to the case of more general complex values of the multiplicities. This leads to a bispectral duality statement for the corresponding MR and CMS systems of type B ​ C ​ ( l , 1 ) BC(l,1) .

Introduction

The Calogero–Moser–Sutherland (CMS) models are an important example of integrable many-body Hamiltonian systems in one spatial dimension. Their study goes back to the works of Calogero
[
1
]
, Sutherland
[
30
]
, and Moser
[
22
]
, who investigated certain problems of pairwise interacting particles on a line or a circle.
Olshanetsky and Perelomov observed a connection between the original CMS Hamiltonians and the root system
A_{l}
(
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
), and they generalised these Hamiltonians to the case of arbitrary root systems of Weyl groups in
[
23
,
24
]
in a way that preserved integrability.

Chalykh, Feigin, and Veselov showed in
[
31
,
6
,
7
]
in the quantum case that there exist further integrable generalisations related to other special configurations of vectors that are not root systems. The examples they discovered were certain one-parametric deformations
A(l,1)
and
C(l,1)
of the root systems
A_{l+1}
and
C_{l+1}
, respectively. Other examples have been discovered since, one of which is the deformation
BC(l,l^{\prime})
of the root system
BC_{l+l^{\prime}}
that was first considered in
[
28
]
by Sergeev and Veselov. An elliptic version of the special case
BC(l,1)
appeared earlier in
[
5
]
.

In general, the eigenfunctions of quantum integrable systems may be complicated. However, the generalised CMS operators associated with any root system with Weyl-invariant integer values of multiplicity parameters admit as (singular) eigenfunctions so-called multidimensional Baker–Akhiezer (BA) functions, which are relatively elementary functions
[
8
,
32
,
7
]
. They have the form

\psi(z,x)=P(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle},

where
P
is a polynomial in a set of complex variables
z
, with
P
depending also on another set of complex variables
x
that are the variables in which the CMS operators act, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard real Euclidean inner product extended
\mathbb{C}
-bilinearly.

The function
\psi
can be characterised by its properties as a function of the variables
z
.
Such an axiomatic definition of a multidimensional BA function was proposed by Chalykh, Styrkas, and
Veselov for an arbitrary finite collection of non-collinear vectors with
integer multiplicities in
[
32
]
— the case of (positive subsystems of) reduced root systems was considered earlier in
[
8
]
— and see also
[
12
]
for a weaker version of the axiomatics. For the (only) non-reduced root system
BC_{l}
, an axiomatic definition of the BA function was given by Chalykh in
[
2
]
.
Recently, Feigin and one of the authors extended the definition to the case of configurations where collinear vectors are allowed more generally as long as all subsets of collinear vectors are of the form
\{\alpha
,
2\alpha\}

[
14
]
.
The key properties that the function needs to satisfy are
quasi-invariance conditions of the form

\psi(z+s\alpha,x)=\psi(z-s\alpha,x)

at
\langle\alpha,z\rangle=0
for vectors
\alpha
in the configuration, where
s
takes
special integer values depending on the multiplicities.

Such a BA function can exist only for very special configurations. The corresponding generalised CMS operators have to be algebraically integrable, that is, contained in a large commutative ring of differential operators; and if the BA function exists, then it is a common eigenfunction for all the operators in this ring
[
32
,
12
,
14
]
.
In addition to the root systems case, BA functions have been found also for some other configurations. The known
examples include the aforementioned deformations from
[
6
,
7
]
of the root systems of type
A
and
C
in the case when the multiplicity parameters are integer. The corresponding BA functions were constructed in
[
2
,
12
]
, respectively. Another deformation
A_{l,2}
of the root system of type
A
appeared in
[
9
]
, and the BA function (satisfying the weakened axiomatics) for it was given in
[
12
]
. More recently, a new configuration, called
AG_{2}
, emerged in the work of Fairley and Feigin
[
11
]
, for which the BA function was constructed in
[
14
]
. The corresponding generalised CMS operator was studied in
[
13
]
.

The deformed CMS operator associated with the configuration
BC(l,l^{\prime})
from
[
28
]
is algebraically integrable in the case when
l^{\prime}=1
and all the multiplicity parameters are integer
[
4
]
, which suggested that in this case there might exist a BA function for it. This has been the only remaining known example of an algebraically integrable monodromy-free CMS-type operator for which a BA function had not been written down.
In this paper, we explicitly construct it by a method modelled on Chalykh’s one from the paper
[
2
]
(see
[
12
,
14
]
for further examples where such a technique has been applied), utilising also some general results proved in
[
14
]
. The construction uses an integrable difference operator, acting in the variables
z
, of rational Macdonald–Ruijsenaars type for the configuration
BC(l,1)
that was introduced by Sergeev and Veselov in
[
29
]
.

The Macdonald–Ruijsenaars (MR) difference operators may be viewed as difference versions of the CMS Hamiltonians and their quantum integrals. They were introduced by Ruijsenaars in
[
26
]
for the root system of type
A
, and in the case of arbitrary reduced
root systems by Macdonald
[
21
]
, and for the root system
BC_{l}
by Koornwinder
[
18
]
.
Their rational versions satisfy a bispectral duality relation with the (trigonometric) CMS Hamiltonians associated with the same root system. This duality was conjectured by Ruijsenaars
[
27
]
, and proved by Chalykh in
[
2
]
. Namely, there exists a function
\Psi
of two sets of variables
x
and
z
such that

H\Psi=\lambda\Psi,\quad D\Psi=\mu\Psi,

(1.1)

where
H=H(x,\partial_{x})
is the generalised CMS Hamiltonian or its integral,
D
is a rational MR operator acting in the spectral variables
z
, and
\lambda=\lambda(z)
,
\mu=\mu(x)
are the respective eigenvalues.
The relations (
1.1
) are a higher-dimensional differential-difference analogue of the one-dimensional differential-differential bispectrality relation studied by Duistermaat and Grünbaum
[
10
]
.

In the case of integer (and Weyl-invariant) multiplicities, the function
\Psi
in (
1.1
) can be taken to be the BA function for the root system in question — and the case of non-integer multiplicities can be handled by an analytic continuation argument
[
2
]
. Moreover, Chalykh showed that the operator
D
can be used to explicitly construct the BA function itself. The key property needed for this from the operator
D
is the preservation of a space of quasi-invariant analytic functions.

We note that the relation (
1.1
) for the root system
A_{l}

with multiplicity parameter greater than
1
and for a function
\Psi

given by a Mellin–Barnes type integral was obtained by Kharchev and Khoroshkin in
[
17
]
.

A form of bispectrality may also be seen in terms of special families of multivariable orthogonal polynomials (rather than a single function
\Psi
depending on spectral parameters). In the case of the root system
A_{l}
, these are the Jack polynomials, and for other root systems they are the multivariable Jacobi polynomials, which admit Pieri-type formulas that can be interpreted as bispectrality between the CMS Hamiltonians and difference operators acting on the weights indexing the polynomials
[
20
,
15
,
2
]
.

Let us also mention that a version of the notion of a BA function exists for the rational degeneration of the CMS Hamiltonians
H
as well. When suitably normalised, this function is symmetric under the exchange of
x
and
z
, and the rational version of
H
is bispectrally self-dual
[
32
]
.
BA functions for the trigonometric MR operators and their bispectrality properties were investigated in
[
3
]
.

The paper
[
2
]
proved also an analogue of the duality (
1.1
) for the deformed root system
A(l,1)
and corresponding generalisation
D
of the rational limit of Ruijsenaars’ operators. For the deformed root systems
C(l,1)
and
A_{l,2}
, this was done
(in the case of integer multiplicity parameters) in
[
12
]
, and the case of
AG_{2}
was treated in
[
14
]
.
In this paper, we prove an analogue of the duality (
1.1
) for the deformed root system
BC(l,1)
, where
D
will be Sergeev’s and Veselov’s rational difference operator for
BC(l,1)

[
29
]
, and
H
their
BC(l,1)
-type generalised CMS Hamiltonian
[
28
]
. In particular, for special integer values of the multiplicities, one recovers the results from
[
12
]
for the configuration
C(l,1)
.
Another bispectrality property of the Sergeev–Veselov operators for
BC(l,1)
(as well as for
BC(l,l^{\prime})
) in terms of super-Jacobi polynomials was proved in
[
29
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the configuration
BC(l,1)
and its associated deformed CMS Hamiltonian. In Section
3
, we recall the Sergeev–Veselov difference operator for
BC(l,1)
and prove that, when the multiplicity parameters are non-negative integers, it preserves a ring of quasi-invariants. We use this property in Section
4
to construct the BA function for
BC(l,1)
and prove that it satisfies bispectrality. We generalise that bispectral duality statement to more general complex values of the multiplicities in Section
5
.

2
Deformed root system
BC(l,1)

The root system
BC_{l+1}
has
a positive half

BC_{l+1,+}=\{e_{i},\,2e_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l+1\}\cup\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l+1\},

where
e_{i}
denote the standard orthogonal unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{l+1}
.
In this paper, we consider a deformation
BC(l,1)
of the root system
BC_{l+1}
with a positive half

BC(l,1)_{+}=\{e_{i},\,2e_{i},\,e_{i}\pm\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}\cup\{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1},\,2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\}\cup\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{l+1},

where
k
is a non-zero complex parameter
[
5
,
28
]
.
Let
BC(l,1)^{r}
denote the reduced version of this system whose positive half is

\displaystyle BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}\,|\,\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha\notin BC(l,1)\}

\displaystyle=\{e_{i},\,e_{i}\pm\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}\cup\{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\}\cup\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l\}.

The vectors in the set
BC(l,1)_{+}
are assigned certain multiplicities, which we specify below.

A multiplicity map on a set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
is a function
\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
which assigns to each vector
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
a complex number
m_{\alpha}
called its multiplicity. In Sections
3
and
4
, we will be specifically interested in the case when the multiplicities are contained in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Then for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the set

A_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,m_{\alpha}\}\cup\{m_{\alpha}+2,m_{\alpha}+4,\dots,m_{\alpha}+2m_{2\alpha}\},

where
m_{2\alpha}\coloneqq 0
if
2\alpha\notin\mathcal{A}
.

The set
BC(l,1)_{+}
has its multiplicity map given by
m_{e_{i}}=m
,
m_{2e_{i}}=n
,
m_{e_{i}\pm\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}=1
,
m_{e_{i}\pm e_{j}}=k
,
m_{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}=p
, and
m_{2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}=r
for complex parameters
m,n,p,r
, subject to the constraint that
m=kp
and
2n+1=k(2r+1)
. For
m=p=0
, the configuration
BC(l,1)
reduces to the configuration
C(l,1)
, which was considered in
[
12
]
.
For
k=1
, the configuration
BC(l,1)
reduces to the root system
BC_{l+1}
with a Weyl-invariant assignment of multiplicities such that the vectors
e_{i}\pm e_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq l+1
have multiplicity
1
. In Sections
3
and
4
, we assume that
m,n,p,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and if
l>1
then also that
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

The generalised CMS operator associated with a finite collection of vectors
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
with prescribed multiplicities has the form

H=-\Delta+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{m_{\alpha}(m_{\alpha}+2m_{2\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\sinh^{2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle},

(2.1)

where
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{l+1})\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
and
\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}
(
\partial_{x_{i}}=\partial/\partial x_{i}
) is the Laplace operator on
\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
.
The Olshanetsky–Perelomov operators correspond to letting
\mathcal{A}
be a positive half of a root system with a Weyl-invariant assignment of multiplicities.
If one puts
\mathcal{A}=BC(l,1)_{+}
in the formula (
2.1
), one obtains the generalised CMS operator associated with the configuration
BC(l,1)

[
28
]
.

3
Sergeev–Veselov difference operator for
BC(l,1)

In this section, we recall the rational difference operator introduced by Sergeev and Veselov for the deformed root system
BC(l,1)

[
29
]
, which deforms the rational version of Koornwinder’s operator and also generalises an operator associated with
C(l,1)
from
[
12
]
.
We prove that, when all the multiplicity parameters are non-negative integers, the operator preserves a ring of quasi-invariants
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
defined as follows.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
be the ring of all analytic functions
f\colon\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

f(z+s\alpha)=f(z-s\alpha)\,\,\mathrm{at}\,\langle{z,\alpha}\rangle=0

(3.1)

for all
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
and
s\in A_{\alpha}
.

Let
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{l+1})\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
.
The difference operator for
BC(l,1)
introduced in
[
29
]
has the form

D=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\big(a_{2e_{i}}(z)(T_{2e_{i}}-1)+a_{-2e_{i}}(z)(T_{-2e_{i}}-1)\big)+a_{2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}(z)\big(T_{2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}-1\big)+a_{-2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}(z)\big(T_{-2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}-1\big),

where
T_{v}
denotes for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
the shift operator defined by
T_{v}f(z)=f(z+v)
for any function
f(z)
, and

\displaystyle a_{\pm 2e_{i}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(1\mp\frac{m+2n}{z_{i}}\right)\left(1\mp\frac{m}{z_{i}\pm 1}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{l}\left(1\mp\frac{2k}{z_{i}-z_{j}}\right)\left(1\mp\frac{2k}{z_{i}+z_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(1\mp\frac{2}{z_{i}+\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}\pm(1-k)}\right)\left(1\mp\frac{2}{z_{i}-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}\pm(1-k)}\right),

\displaystyle a_{\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\left(1\mp\frac{\sqrt{k}(p+2r)}{z_{l+1}}\right)\left(1\mp\frac{\sqrt{k}p}{z_{l+1}\pm\sqrt{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{i=1}^{l}\left(1\mp\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+z_{i}\pm(k-1)}\right)\left(1\mp\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}-z_{i}\pm(k-1)}\right).

In this and the next section, we are assuming that
m,n,p,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and if
l>1
then also that
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

In order to prove below in Theorem
3.2
that the operator
D
preserves the ring
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
, we first establish the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
. Then
Df
is analytic.

Proof.

Based on the form of the functions
a_{\pm 2e_{i}}
and
a_{\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}
, the only possible singularities of
Df
are potential simple poles at
z_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq l+1
, as well as
z_{l+1}=\pm\sqrt{k}
,
z_{i}=\pm 1
,
z_{i}=\pm\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1
, and
z_{i}=\pm\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k
for
1\leq i\leq l
, and also
z_{i}=\pm z_{j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq l
. The strategy is to show that at each of the possible poles,
Df
has zero residue. It will follow that
Df
is analytic everywhere. We describe the computation of the residue for most of the cases, the procedure for the remaining ones being analogous.
Let us denote the residue of a function
f
at a simple pole at
z_{i}=c\in\mathbb{C}
by

\mathrm{Res}(f,z_{i}=c)=\lim_{z_{i}\to c}(z_{i}-c)f(z).

We may assume, for simplicity, that
m\neq 0\neq p
, as the case
m=p=0
was covered in
[
12
]
.
Let us first compute the residue of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq l
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{l+1}\setminus\{0\}
, let
s_{\alpha}
denote the orthogonal reflection sending
\alpha
to
-\alpha
and fixing pointwise the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
. We note that

a_{2e_{i}}(z)=a_{-2e_{i}}(s_{e_{i}}(z)),

(3.2)

which implies

\Res(a_{2e_{i}},z_{i}=0)=-\Res(a_{-2e_{i}},z_{i}=0).

Also, if
m\neq 1
then

T_{2e_{i}}f(z)|_{z_{i}=0}=T_{-2e_{i}}f(z)|_{z_{i}=0},

where we use that
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
.
Note that if
m=1
then
\Res(a_{\pm 2e_{i}},z_{i}=0)=0
. It follows that for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
the residue of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=0
is zero. Its residue at
z_{l+1}=0
can be shown to vanish in an analogous way.

Next, we consider
z_{i}=-1
. The only coefficient function in
D
that has a pole there is
a_{2e_{i}}
, and so
\Res(Df,z_{i}=-1)=0
follows from the fact that

(T_{2e_{i}}f(z)-f(z))|_{z_{i}=-1}=0,

where we again used that
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
. Similarly, the residues of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=1
and
z_{l+1}=\pm\sqrt{k}
are zero.

Let now
1\leq i<j\leq l
, and
let us compute the residue of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=z_{j}
. We note that

a_{\pm 2e_{i}}(z)=a_{\pm 2e_{j}}(s_{e_{i}-e_{j}}(z)),

(3.3)

which implies

\Res(a_{\pm 2e_{i}},z_{i}=z_{j})=-\Res(a_{\pm 2e_{j}},z_{i}=z_{j}).

Also, for any
u\in\mathbb{C}
and
q=u+1
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2e_{i}}f(z)|_{z_{i}=z_{j}=u}

\displaystyle=f(z+e_{i}-e_{j})|_{z_{i}=z_{j}=q}=f(z-e_{i}+e_{j})|_{z_{i}=z_{j}=q}=T_{2e_{j}}f(z)|_{z_{i}=z_{j}=u},

where in the penultimate equality we used that
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
. Similarly,

T_{-2e_{i}}f(z)=T_{-2e_{j}}f(z)
at
z_{i}=z_{j}
.
It follows that the residue of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=z_{j}
is zero. The case of
z_{i}=-z_{j}
is similar.

Finally, we consider
z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1
for
1\leq i\leq l
. We calculate the following residues:

\displaystyle\Res(a_{2e_{i}},\,z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1)=-2\left(1+\frac{m+2n}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k}\right)\left(1+\frac{m}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}-k}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{l}\left(1+\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+z_{j}+1-k}\right)\left(1+\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}-z_{j}+1-k}\right),

\displaystyle\Res(a_{-2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}},\,z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1)=2\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{k}(p+2r)}{z_{l+1}}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{k}p}{z_{l+1}-\sqrt{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1+\frac{k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{l}\left(1+\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+z_{j}+1-k}\right)\left(1+\frac{2k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}-z_{j}+1-k}\right).

In order to compare them, we observe that

\frac{\sqrt{k}p}{z_{l+1}-\sqrt{k}}=\frac{m}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}-k},

since
m=kp
; and that moreover, since
2n+1=k(2r+1)
, we also have

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{k}(p+2r)}{z_{l+1}}\right)\left(1+\frac{k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+m+2n+1-k}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{m+2n}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}}\right).

Therefore,

\Res(a_{2e_{i}},\,z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1)=-\Res(a_{-2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}},\,z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1).

Also, for any
u\in\mathbb{C}
and
q=-u+\sqrt{k}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2e_{i}}

\displaystyle f(z)|_{z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}u+k-1,\,z_{l+1}=u}=f(z+e_{i}+\sqrt{k}e_{l+1})|_{z_{i}=\sqrt{k}q,\,z_{l+1}=-q}

\displaystyle=f(z-e_{i}-\sqrt{k}e_{l+1})|_{z_{i}=\sqrt{k}q,\,z_{l+1}=-q}=T_{-2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}}f(z)|_{z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}u+k-1,\,z_{l+1}=u},

where in the penultimate equality we used that
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
.
It follows that the residue of
Df(z)
at
z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+k-1
is zero. The cases
z_{i}=-\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}+1-k
and
z_{i}=\sqrt{k}z_{l+1}\pm(1-k)
are similar. This completes the proof.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
. Then
Df\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1

4
Construction of the Baker–Akhiezer function

Even though the configuration
BC(l,1)_{+}
is non-reduced, all of its subsets of collinear vectors are of the form
\{\alpha
,
2\alpha\}
. Thus, we can use the axiomatic definition of BA functions proposed in
[
14
, Definition 2.1]

(see
[
8
,
32
]
for the original axiomatic definition of BA functions formulated by Chalykh, Styrkas, and Veselov for arbitrary finite collections of
non-collinear
vectors with
integer multiplicities, and also
[
12
]
for a weaker version of that axiomatics).

Definition 4.1
.

A function
\psi\colon\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\to\mathbb{C}
is a BA function for the configuration
BC(l,1)
with non-negative integer multiplicities if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

\psi(z,x)=P(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
for a polynomial
P
in
z
with highest-order term
\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}\langle\alpha,z\rangle^{m_{\alpha}}
,

2.

\psi(z+s\alpha,x)=\psi(z-s\alpha,x)
at
\langle z,\alpha\rangle=0
for all
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
and
s\in A_{\alpha}
.

Note that condition 2 in Definition
4.1
is similar to the functional identities (
3.1
) satisfied by elements of the ring
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
; and indeed for any
x\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
for which
P(z,x)
is non-singular, the function
\psi_{x}\colon z\mapsto\psi(z,x)
belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}.

It follows from the general results proved in
[
14
, Proposition 2.7, Theorem 2.8]
that if a function
\psi
satisfying Definition
4.1
exists then it is unique, and it is a joint eigenfunction for a large commutative ring of differential operators in
x
. By
[
14
, Proposition 2.10]
, this ring contains the Hamiltonian (
2.1
) for
\mathcal{A}=BC(l,1)_{+}
with the non-negative integer values of the multiplicity parameters. Moreover, this ring also contains a complete set of (quantum) integrals for this Hamiltonian, as well as extra integrals that correspond to the algebraic integrability of this system (see
[
8
,
32
]
for the definition of algebraic integrability). Namely, for every polynomial
p(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
, there is a differential operator in
x
that commutes with the Hamiltonian and whose highest symbol is
p_{0}(\partial_{x})
, where
p_{0}
is the highest homogeneous term of
p
.

The following theorem gives an explicit construction of the BA function for
BC(l,1)
by using the difference operator
D
from the previous section. The BA function will be an eigenfunction for
the operator
D
, which shows bispectrality of the generalised CMS
Hamiltonian for
BC(l,1)
for non-negative integer values of the multiplicity parameters (the case of non-integer multiplicities will be considered in the next section). See
[
2
,
12
]
and
[
14
, Theorems 5.3 and 6.3]
for earlier examples of analogous results obtained by similar methods for other configurations.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M=\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}=l(m+n+(l-1)k+2)+p+r
, and let
S=\{\pm 2e_{i},\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|

1\leq i\leq l\}.
For
x\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
, let

\mu(x)=\sum_{\tau\in S}\kappa_{\tau}(e^{\langle\tau,x\rangle}-1),

and

c(x)=\frac{M!}{2^{ln+r}}\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}^{r}}\left(\sum_{\tau\in S}\kappa_{\tau}\langle\tau,\alpha\rangle e^{\langle\tau,x\rangle}\right)^{m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}},

(4.1)

where
\kappa_{\tau}=k^{-1}
if
\tau=\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}
, and
\kappa_{\tau}=1
otherwise. For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
, let
Q(z)
be the polynomial in
\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
given by

Q(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}^{r}\\
s\in A_{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\left(\langle\alpha,z\rangle^{2}-s^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{2}\right).

Then the function

\psi(z,x)=c(x)^{-1}\left(D-\mu(x)\right)^{M}[Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}]

(4.2)

is the BA function for
BC(l,1)
. Moreover,
\psi
is an eigenfunction of the operator
D
with

D\psi(z,x)=\mu(x)\psi(z,x).

The proof is analogous to the case of
AG_{2}

[
14
, Theorem 5.3]
, it just uses the following two lemmas in place of
[
14
, Lemma 5.1]
and
[
14
, Lemma 5.2]
, respectively.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
\tau\in S=\{\pm 2e_{i},\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}
, the coefficient function
a_{\tau}
in the operator
D
can be expanded as

a_{\tau}(z)=\kappa_{\tau}-\kappa_{\tau}\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}\frac{\langle\tau,\alpha\rangle(m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,z\rangle}+R_{\tau}(z),

where
\kappa_{\tau}
are as in Theorem
4.2
, and
R_{\tau}
is a rational function with
\deg R_{\tau}\leq-2
.

The above way of expanding the coefficients of the operator
D
can be used to prove the following lemma (in a completely analogous way to how
[
14
, Lemma 5.2]
is proved by using
[
14
, Lemma 5.1]
).

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
, let
n_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be arbitrary. Let
N=\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}n_{\alpha}
. Let
\mu(x)
be as in Theorem
4.2
, and let
A(z)=\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}\langle\alpha,z\rangle^{n_{\alpha}}
. Then we have
(D-\mu)[A(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}]=R(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
for a rational function
R(z,x)
in
z
of the form

R(z,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}(n_{\alpha}-m_{\alpha}-m_{2\alpha})\left(\sum_{\tau\in S}\kappa_{\tau}\langle\tau,\alpha\rangle e^{\langle\tau,x\rangle}\right)A(z)\langle\alpha,z\rangle^{-1}+S(z,x),

where
S(z,x)
is a rational function in
z
of degree less than or equal to
N-2
.

In particular, for
any
polynomial
B(z,x)
in
z
, we have that
(D-\mu)[B(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}]=U(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
for a rational function
U(z,x)
in
z
with
\deg U(z,x)\leq\deg B(z,x)-1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

The idea of the proof is as follows. We have
Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
because
Q(z+s\alpha)=Q(z-s\alpha)=0
at
\langle z,\alpha\rangle=0
for all
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
and
s\in A_{\alpha}
. The property that
(D-\mu)^{M}[Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}]
satisfies condition 2 in Definition
4.1
thus follows from Theorem
3.2
. Moreover, each repeated application of
D-\mu
on
Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
gives a function of the form
R(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
with
R(z,x)
a polynomial in
z
, which follows from the form of the operator
D
and Theorem
3.2
. More specifically, for all

, we have that
(D-\mu)^{b}[Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}]=R_{b}(z,x)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}
where
R_{b}(z,x)
is a polynomial in
z
of degree at most

whose highest-order homogeneous component can be kept track of by using Lemma
4.4
(similarly to how this is done in the case of
AG_{2}
in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 5.3]
by using
[
14
, Lemma 5.2]
). This makes it possible to see that

essentially satisfies also condition 1 in Definition

, except that the highest-order term of its polynomial part has an extra factor of

given by formula (

). It follows that

defined by the expression (

At the next application of

as a consequence of
[

, which implies that

.
∎

\mathcal{A}=BC(l,1)_{+}
. The operator can be rearranged as follows

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\Delta+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}\frac{(m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha})(m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\sinh^{2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle}-\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}\frac{m_{2\alpha}(m_{2\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\cosh^{2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle}.

This form of the operator
H
makes it possible to see, by Laurent expanding the eigenfunction
\psi
in
x
around suitable hyperplanes, that
\psi
must have either a pole of order
m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}
or a zero of order
m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}+1
along each of the hyperplanes
\sinh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
for
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
; and similarly a pole of order
m_{2\alpha}
or a zero of order
m_{2\alpha}+1
along the hyperplanes
\cosh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
for
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
.
The expression for
c(x)
given in Lemma
4.5
suggests that the order of the poles of
\psi
at
\cosh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
might be higher than
m_{2\alpha}
, but the local expansion of
\psi
and the eigenvalue equation
H\psi=-z^{2}\psi
imply that this cannot happen. It follows from the form of
c(x)
that
\psi
cannot have any additional other singularities either.
Hence
\delta(x)\psi

is analytic (in both
x
and
z
variables) due to the property (
4.4
).

By construction,
\psi(z,x)=c(x)^{-1}\Phi(z,x)
for the quasi-polynomial in
z
function

\Phi(z,x)=(D-\mu(x))^{M}[Q(z)e^{\langle z,x\rangle}].

From the fomulas for
D
and
\mu
, it is clear that

\Phi
is analytic in
x
and that it can be expanded as

\Phi=e^{\langle z,x\rangle}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}}b_{\nu}(z)e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle}

(4.8)

for some polynomials
b_{\nu}(z)
.

In view of Lemma
4.6
, the analyticity of
\delta(x)\psi
implies that the trigonometric polynomial in
x
given by (
4.8
) must be divisible by

\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}\cosh^{m_{\alpha}}\langle\alpha,x\rangle\propto e^{\langle\rho^{r},x\rangle}\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}(e^{-2\langle\alpha,x\rangle}+1)^{m_{\alpha}}=e^{\langle\rho,x\rangle}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}}d_{\nu}e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle},

(4.9)

where
d_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}
and

\rho^{r}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha,

and we used that
2\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
for all
\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}
.
The quotient of the function (
4.8
) by its divisor (
4.9
) will still be a trigonometric polynomial in
x
, and we get that

\psi=\delta(x)^{-1}e^{\langle z-\rho,x\rangle}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}}c_{\nu}(z)e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle}

for some polynomials
c_{\nu}(z)
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{L}\,|\,c_{\nu}\neq 0\}
. We need to show that
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{L}_{+}
.

Let
y_{i}=e^{\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle}
for
i=1,\dots,l+1
. The potential in the Hamiltonian
H
can be written as

\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}\frac{4m_{\alpha}(m_{\alpha}+2m_{2\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle e^{2\langle\alpha,x\rangle}}{(e^{2\langle\alpha,x\rangle}-1)^{2}}.

(4.10)

By using that
2\alpha\in\mathcal{L}_{+}=\oplus_{i=1}^{l+1}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\alpha_{i}
for any
\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}
, we can rewrite the potential (
4.10
) in terms of the variables
y_{i}
. We can then expand it (for small

, that is for
x
in
\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\,|\,\re\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle<0,\ i=1,\dots,l+1\}
) into a Taylor series in
y_{i}
, which will have no constant term, and thus obtain

H=-\Delta+\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}g_{\mu}e^{\langle\mu,x\rangle}

for some constants
g_{\mu}
. Similarly, one can expand the function

\delta(x)^{-1}=e^{\langle\rho,x\rangle}\prod_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}(e^{\langle 2\alpha,x\rangle}-1)^{-m_{\alpha}}=e^{\langle\rho,x\rangle}\left((-1)^{t}+\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}h_{\eta}e^{\langle\eta,x\rangle}\right),

where
h_{\eta}
are constants and
t=l(m+n)+p+r
. Thus, the eigenfunction equation
(H+z^{2})\psi=0
gives that

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle c_{\nu}(z)e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle}\bigg(\left(z^{2}-(z+\nu)^{2}\right)(-1)^{t}+\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}h_{\eta}\left(z^{2}-(z+\nu+\eta)^{2}\right)e^{\langle\eta,x\rangle}

(4.11)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}(-1)^{t}g_{\mu}e^{\langle\mu,x\rangle}+\sum_{\mu,\eta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}g_{\mu}h_{\eta}e^{\langle\mu+\eta,x\rangle}\bigg)=0.

Since the set
\mathcal{P}
is finite, it contains (one or several) minimal elements
\nu_{\min}
with respect to the partial order on
\mathcal{L}
defined by
\alpha>\beta
if and only if
\alpha-\beta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}
.
The term

appears only once in the left-hand side of equality (
4.11
), and its coefficient must hence vanish. We get that
(z+\nu_{\min})^{2}=z^{2}
for generic

, and it follows that
\nu_{\min}=0
.
Suppose that there is some
\nu\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{+}
. Then
\nu\neq 0
, so it cannot be minimal in
\mathcal{P}
, hence there must be some
\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{+}
with
\nu>\nu_{2}
.
By iterating this argument, we get an infinite chain
\nu>\nu_{2}>\nu_{3}>\dots
of elements in
\mathcal{P}
, contradicting the finiteness of this set. It follows that
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{L}_{+}
, as required.
∎

We end this section with the following bispectral duality statement, in the spirit of analogous results from
[
2
,
12
]
for other configurations. By
[

, Theorem 2.8]
, the above BA function

is
a common eigenfunction for a commutative ring of differential operators in

, and the following theorem states that a similar situation occurs in the variables

.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
p(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{BC(l,1)}
be a polynomial, and let
p_{0}
be its highest-degree homogeneous term. Then there exists a differential operator
H_{p}(x,\partial_{x})
with highest symbol
p_{0}(\partial_{x})
and a difference operator
D_{p}
in
z
such that

H_{p}\psi(z,x)=p(z)\psi(z,x),\qquad D_{p}\psi(z,x)=\mu_{p}(x)\psi(z,x).

The difference operator is given by

D_{p}=\frac{1}{(\deg p)!}\ad_{D}^{\deg p}(\widehat{p}),

where
\widehat{p}
is the operator of multiplication by
p(z)
and
\ad^{r}_{A}
is the
r
-th iteration of the operation
\ad_{A}(B)=AB-BA
. The eigenvalue
\mu_{p}(x)
is obtained by substituting
4\sinh(2x_{i})
in place of
z_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq l
) and

4(\sqrt{k})^{-1}\sinh(2\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})
in place of
z_{l+1}
into

.

The operators
H_{p}
are pairwise-commuting, and
H_{-z^{2}}
coincides with the Hamiltonian (
2.1
) for
\mathcal{A}=BC(l,1)_{+}
. The operators
D_{p}
commute with
D
and with each other.

Proof.

The differential side of the theorem follows from
[
14
, Theorem 2.8, Proposition 2.10]
. By Theorem
4.2
, we have
D\psi=\mu\psi
. The fact that
\psi
is an eigenfunction of the operators

follows by a standard argument about bispectral systems (see e.g. the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
) which gives that

D_{p}\psi=\frac{(-1)^{\deg p}}{(\deg p)!}\ad_{\mu}^{\deg p}(H_{p})\psi.

Here
\ad_{\mu}^{\deg p}(H_{p})=\ad_{\mu}^{\deg p}(p_{0}(\partial_{x}))(1)
is a zeroth-order operator (that is, an ordinary function of

decreases the order of a linear differential operator and
H_{p}=p_{0}(\partial_{x})+\text{ lower terms}
.
This also allows, in particular, to compute the eigenvalue.

From the expression for the function

5
Bispectrality for non-integer multiplicities

In this section, we carry out an analytic continuation in the parameters of the BA function (
4.2
) in order to extend to more general complex values of the parameters the statement proved in the previous section about the bispectrality of the Sergeev–Veselov operators for
BC(l,1)
. We do this by adapting to our present setting the approach developed in
[
2
, Section VI. C]
. The corresponding generalisation of the function
\psi(z,x)
will be a deformation of a Heckman–Opdam multidimensional hypergeometric function
[
15
]
.

We begin with a few preliminary results on the properties of the generalised CMS operator
H
(
2.1
) for
\mathcal{A}=BC(l,1)_{+}
,
where we are now no longer assuming that the parameters
m_{\alpha}
are integer.
The next lemma gives a potential-free gauge-equivalent form of this operator. It is stated in
[
28
]
. We include a proof below for completeness.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
28
]

Consider the differential operator

L=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}2m_{\alpha}\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\partial_{\alpha},

(5.1)

where
\partial_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\langle\alpha,e_{i}\rangle\partial_{x_{i}}
is the directional derivative along
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
.
The operator
L
is gauge-equivalent to the operator
H
from above:

L+\rho^{2}=-\delta(x)\circ H\circ\delta(x)^{-1},

where
\rho
is given by formula (
4.7
) and
\delta(x)
by formula (
4.3
).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}[\delta(x)^{-1}]

\displaystyle=-\delta(x)^{-1}\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha^{(i)}\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle,

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}[\delta(x)^{-1}]

\displaystyle=\delta(x)^{-1}\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}\left((m_{\alpha}+1)\sinh^{-2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle+m_{\alpha}\right)(\alpha^{(i)})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta(x)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\alpha^{(i)}\beta^{(i)}\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle

for all
i=1,\dots,l+1
, where
\alpha=(\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(l+1)})
and
\beta=(\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(l+1)})
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle

\displaystyle-\delta(x)\circ H\circ\delta(x)^{-1}=-\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}2m_{\alpha}m_{2\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\sinh^{-2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle

(5.2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}2m_{\alpha}\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\partial_{\alpha}+\Delta.

By using that
\coth(u)\coth(2u)=\frac{1}{2}(\sinh^{-2}(u)+2)
for any
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}\pi i
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\notin\{\beta,2\beta,\frac{1}{2}\beta\}\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}2m_{\alpha}m_{2\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle(\sinh^{-2}\langle\alpha,x\rangle+2);

and by substituting this into equality (
5.2
), we obtain that
-\delta(x)\circ H\circ\delta(x)^{-1}
equals

\displaystyle L+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\notin\{\beta,2\beta,\frac{1}{2}\beta\}\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}4m_{\alpha}m_{2\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle.

Since we can write

\rho^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}4m_{\alpha}m_{2\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\notin\{\beta,2\beta,\frac{1}{2}\beta\}\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle,

the proof is completed with the help of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

[
28
, Equality (12)]

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\notin\{\beta,2\beta,\frac{1}{2}\beta\}\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle\coth\langle\beta,x\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\\
\alpha\notin\{\beta,2\beta,\frac{1}{2}\beta\}\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle.

(5.3)

Proof.

We first indicate how to prove that the left-hand side of (
5.3
) is non-singular. One can show that singularities at
\sinh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
for
\alpha\in\{e_{i},2e_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}\cup\{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1},2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\}\cup\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l\}
cancel by using symmetry — namely that those
\alpha

— and using also that for those

for all

.

It remains to show that there are no singularities at
\sinh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
for
\alpha=e_{i}\pm\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}
(

) either.
Let
\alpha=e_{i}+\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}

\sinh\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0

. We will show that the terms multiplying

The terms in question are (up to a factor of

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\coth(x_{i})+2n\coth(2x_{i})+kp\coth(\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})+2kr\coth(2\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})+(1-k)\coth(x_{i}-\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\

By using

(kp-m)\coth(\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})+(2kr-2n+k-1)\coth(2\sqrt{k}x_{l+1})=0,

as required, since

This completes the proof that the left-hand side of (

(

) and

To calculate the constant, let us put

for all
\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}

∎∎

When

(5.5)

Let

Assume that there is a solution

By substituting the series (

q_{0}=1
) determine all
q_{\nu}
uniquely. Indeed, one can solve for them recursively by height of
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}=\oplus_{i=1}^{l+1}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\alpha_{i}
, since
\nu-2j\alpha
has a strictly lower height than
\nu
, where for
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}\alpha_{i}
, its height is defined by
h(\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}
. We note that all
q_{\nu}(z)
are rational functions of
z
, and their dependence on
m,n,p,r
, and
\sqrt{k}
is also rational.

For
k
with
\re k>0
, the next lemma below, applied with
x
replaced by
x/2
, can be used to show that the resulting series (
5.7
) converges absolutely in the region
B
.
Moreover, since it is a power series in
y_{i}=e^{\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle}
(
i=1,\dots,l+1
), it converges uniformly in the open sets
\{x\in B\,|\,\re\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle<\varepsilon<0,

i=1,\dots,l+1\}
for any
\varepsilon<0
, and so
\varphi
is analytic in
x
on
B
. In the case of root systems, an analogue of the next lemma is proved in
[
16
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume
\re k>0
.
For
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
, let
\Sigma_{\nu}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\,|\,\langle\nu,\nu+2z\rangle=0\}
.
Suppose
z\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
does not lie on any of the hyperplanes
\Sigma_{\nu}
, and let
x\in B
. Then there exists a constant
K=K(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}
(depending on
z
and
x
but not on
\nu
) such that

|q_{\nu}(z)e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle}|\leq K

for all
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
and
\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}
be arbitrary. We have

|\langle\nu-\rho+z,\alpha\rangle|\leq|\langle z-\rho,\alpha\rangle|+\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}|\langle\alpha_{i},\alpha\rangle|\leq\lambda_{1}(h(\nu)+1),

(5.9)

where we let
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(z)>0
be the maximum (depending on
z
but not on
\nu
) of the finite set

\{|\langle z-\rho,\beta\rangle|\colon\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+}\}\cup\{|\langle\alpha_{i},\beta\rangle|\colon\beta\in BC(l,1)_{+},\ i=1,\dots,l+1\}.

Further, we have

\re\langle\nu,\nu\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}\nu^{(j)}\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{i},\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\rangle=\lVert\widetilde{\nu}\rVert^{2}

(5.10)

for
\widetilde{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}
, where
\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{l+1}
are obtained from
\alpha_{i}
by replacing
\sqrt{k}
with
\sqrt{\re k}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Here
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the usual (real) Euclidean norm.
Since
\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}
form a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{l+1}
,
the expression (
5.10
) is a positive-definite (real) quadratic form in the variables
\nu^{(i)}
with associated symmetric matrix

A=(\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{i},\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\rangle)_{i,j=1}^{l+1}
. By Sylvester’s criterion, all leading principal minors
M_{i}
of
A
are positive,
M_{i}=\det A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is the top left
i\times i
corner of
A
. For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the expression

\re\langle\nu,\nu\rangle-ch(\nu)^{2}

is also a quadratic form in the variables
\nu^{(i)}
with associated matrix
A^{c}=(\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{i},\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\rangle-c)_{i,j=1}^{l+1}
. By the matrix determinant lemma, the leading principal minors
M_{i}^{c}
of
A^{c}
are

(1-c\langle u,A_{i}^{-1}u\rangle)M_{i}
, where
u=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
.
Since
M_{i}
are positive,
M_{i}^{c}
are, too, for
c=\min\{2^{-1}\langle u,A_{i}^{-1}u\rangle^{-1}\,|\,i=1,\dots,l+1\}>0
, and then Sylvester’s criterion implies

|\langle\nu,\nu\rangle|\geq\re\langle\nu,\nu\rangle\geq ch(\nu)^{2}.

We also have

|\langle\nu,z\rangle|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\nu^{(i)}|\langle\alpha_{i},z\rangle|\leq Mh(\nu),

where we let
M=M(z)=\max\{|\langle\alpha_{i},z\rangle|\colon i=1,\dots,l+1\}>0
.
Whenever
h(\nu)\geq 4M/c
, it follows that
|\langle\nu,z\rangle|\leq ch(\nu)^{2}/4
, and then by the reverse triangle inequality

|\langle\nu,\nu+2z\rangle|\geq\big||\langle\nu,\nu\rangle|-2|\langle\nu,z\rangle|\big|\geq ch(\nu)^{2}/2.

Letting
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2}(z)>0
be the minimum of
c/2
and the finitely-many, positive values
|\langle\nu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}+2z\rangle|/h(\nu^{\prime})^{2}
for
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
with
h(\nu^{\prime})<4M/c
(here we are using that
z\notin\Sigma_{\nu^{\prime}}
by assumption), we thus get that

|\langle\nu,\nu+2z\rangle|\geq\lambda_{2}h(\nu)^{2}

(5.11)

for any
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
.

By using inequalities (
5.9
), (
5.11
), and the recurrence relation (
5.8
), we get for all
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
that

|q_{\nu}(z)|\leq 4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{-1}h(\nu)^{-1}\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}|m_{\alpha}|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|q_{\nu-2j\alpha}(z)|,

(5.12)

since
h(\nu-2j\alpha)\leq h(\nu)-1
. Let
\lambda=4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{-1}
.

Since
x\in B
and the geometric series is absolutely convergent on the open unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
, there is
N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that

\lambda\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}|m_{\alpha}|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|e^{2j\langle\alpha,x\rangle}|\leq N_{0}.

(5.13)

Let
K
be such that

|q_{\eta}(z)e^{\langle\eta,x\rangle}|\leq K

(5.14)

for those (finitely many)
\eta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
which have
h(\eta)\leq N_{0}
. One can prove that (
5.14
) holds for all
\eta\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
by induction on
h(\eta)
. Indeed, assume that (
5.14
) holds for those
\eta
with
h(\eta)<N
for some
integer
N>N_{0}
. Then for
\nu\in\mathcal{L}_{+}
with
h(\nu)=N
, we have by inequalities (
5.12
), (
5.13
), and the induction hypothesis that

|q_{\nu}(z)|\leq\lambda N^{-1}\sum_{\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}}|m_{\alpha}|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}K|e^{-\langle\nu-2j\alpha,x\rangle}|\leq KN_{0}N^{-1}|e^{-\langle\nu,x\rangle}|<K|e^{-\langle\nu,x\rangle}|,

which completes the proof by induction.
∎

As a corollary of Lemma
5.3
, we have the following statement.

Proposition 5.4
.

The series (
5.7
) defines an analytic function in
z
,
x
, and
k
on an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2l+3}
.

Proof.

Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\re k_{0}>0
.
Since
\re\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle
is continuous in
x
and
k
, there exists an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}
centred at
k_{0}
and
an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{X}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
such that
\re k>0
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{X}}\subset B(k)
for all
k\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}
. Take any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\setminus\cup_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}(k_{0})_{+}\setminus\{0\}}\Sigma_{\nu}
.
Consider the constant
c=c(k_{0})
from the proof of Lemma
5.3
for
k=k_{0}
. Note that
\langle u,A_{i}^{-1}u\rangle^{-1}
is continuous in
k
at
k_{0}
. Consider also the constant
M=M(z_{0},k_{0})
defined as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
. Note that
|\langle\alpha_{i},z\rangle|
is continuous in
z
and
k
at
(z_{0},k_{0})
. Therefore, there exists an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}
centred at
k_{0}
and an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{l+1}
centred at
z_{0}
such that
for all
(z,k)\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime}
we have
c(k)>c(k_{0})/2
and
M(z,k)<2M(z_{0},k_{0})
.

Since
|\langle\nu(k_{0}),\nu(k_{0})+2z_{0}\rangle|>0
for all
\nu(k_{0})\in\mathcal{L}(k_{0})_{+}
, and
|\langle\nu(k),\nu(k)+2z\rangle|
is continuous in
k
and
z
, there exists an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime}
and an open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}
such that for the
finitely many

\nu=\nu(k)\in\mathcal{L}(k)_{+}
with
h(\nu)\leq 16M(z_{0},k_{0})/c(k_{0})
we have

|\langle\nu,\nu+2z\rangle|>0
for all
k\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime\prime}
and
z\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}
. On the other hand, for those
\nu
with
h(\nu)>16M(z_{0},k_{0})/c(k_{0})
, we have by the definition of the constants
c(k)
and
M(z,k)
that
|\langle\nu,\nu\rangle|\geq c(k)h(\nu)^{2}>c(k_{0})h(\nu)^{2}/2
and
|\langle\nu,z\rangle|\leq M(z,k)h(\nu)<2M(z_{0},k_{0})h(\nu)<c(k_{0})h(\nu)^{2}/8
, and so by the reverse triangle inequality

|\langle\nu,\nu+2z\rangle|\geq\big||\langle\nu,\nu\rangle|-2|\langle\nu,z\rangle|\big|>c(k_{0})h(\nu)^{2}/4>0.

In other words, for any
(z,x,k)\in U\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{X}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{K}}^{\prime\prime}
, we have
\re k>0
,
z\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\setminus\cup_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}(k)_{+}\setminus\{0\}}\Sigma_{\nu}
, and
x\in B(k)
.
Then the sum
\varphi(z,x)
of the series (
5.7
) is well-defined by the discussion preceding Lemma
5.3
, and
\varphi(z,x)
is on
U
the pointwise limit of a sequence of functions (the partial sums) that are analytic
jointly
in all the variables
z
,
x
, and
k
. As a consequence of (the multivariable version of) Osgood’s Theorem
[
19
, Theorem 8]
, there exists an open dense subset
V\subseteq U
on which
\varphi(z,x)
is analytic (jointly) in the variables
z
,
x
, and
k
and on which the convergence is locally uniform (for the original, single-variable case of Osgood’s Theorem see
[
25
, Theorem II]
).
∎

If
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, then by the uniqueness of the solution of the system (
5.8
), we must have that
\delta(x)\psi(z,x)

is proportional to
\varphi(z,x)
with the factor of proportionality being
c_{0}(z)
, that is

\psi(z,x)=2^{ln+r}\delta(x)^{-1}\varphi(z,x)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in BC(l,1)^{r}_{+}\\
s\in A_{\alpha}\end{subarray}}(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle-s\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle).

For the case when
m_{\alpha}
are not necessarily in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, but rather we have arbitrary
k,m,n,p,r\in\mathbb{C}
with
\re k>0
,
m=kp
, and
2n+1=k(2r+1)
, let us define the following function

\Psi(z,x)=C(z)\delta(x)^{-1}\varphi(z,x),

where we take any branch of
\delta(x)
, and where we defined the function

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha\in\{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1},\,e_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}}\frac{\Gamma\left(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}\right)\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}-m_{\alpha}\right)\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}-m_{2\alpha}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{\alpha\in\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l\}}\frac{\Gamma\left(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}-k\right)}\prod_{\alpha\in\{e_{i}\pm\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}}(-\langle\alpha,z\rangle-1-k).

Here
\Gamma(u)
is the classical gamma-function.
Then
H\Psi=-z^{2}\Psi
, since
C(z)
does not depend on
x
and
\varphi
solves equation (
5.6
) by construction.
Moreover,
\Psi(z,x)
coincides (up to a constant factor) with
\psi(z,x)
when
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, since then
C(z)\propto c_{0}(z)
as
\Gamma(u)/\Gamma(u-N)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(u-i)
for
u\in\mathbb{C}
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

With that, the proof of the next theorem is then essentially the same as that of
[
2
, Theorem 6.9]
. It just uses Theorem
4.2
in place of
[
2
, Theorem 6.2]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

For any
k,m,n,p,r\in\mathbb{C}
with
\re k>0
,
m=kp
, and
2n+1=k(2r+1)
, the function
\Psi(z,x)
satisfies

\displaystyle H\Psi

\displaystyle=-z^{2}\Psi,

\displaystyle D\Psi

\displaystyle=\mu(x)\Psi.

(5.15)

Proof.

It only remains to show equality (
5.15
).
For
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, it follows from Theorem
4.2
.
More generally,
equation (
5.15
) is equivalent to
\varphi
satisfying
\widetilde{D}\varphi=\mu(x)\varphi
for the difference operator

\widetilde{D}=C(z)^{-1}\circ D\circ C(z)=\sum_{\tau\in S}a_{\tau}(z)\left(C(z)^{-1}C(z+\tau)T_{\tau}-1\right),

where
S=\{\pm 2e_{i},\pm 2\sqrt{k}e_{l+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}
, since
D
was of the form
D=\sum_{\tau\in S}a_{\tau}(z)(T_{\tau}-1)
.
For any
\tau\in S
, the function
a_{\tau}(z)
is rational in
z,m,n,p,r,\sqrt{k}
, and so is the function
C(z)^{-1}C(z+\tau)
, since
\frac{1}{2}\langle\alpha,\tau\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\alpha\in\{\sqrt{k}e_{l+1},\,e_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq l\}
and
\langle\alpha,\tau\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\alpha\in\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq l\}
.

By substituting the series (
5.7
) into the equation
\widetilde{D}\varphi=\mu(x)\varphi
, the latter reduces (by looking at the coefficient of
e^{\langle\nu,x\rangle}
for each
\nu\in\mathcal{L}\supset S
) to an infinite number of identities, each involving a finite number of the coefficients
q_{\nu}(z)
and only involving rational functions of
z
,
m,n,p,r,\sqrt{k}
. Explicitly, these identities are

\sum_{\tau\in S}(\kappa_{\tau}-a_{\tau}(z))q_{\nu}(z)+\sum_{(\mu,\tau)\in\mathcal{L}_{+}\times S\colon\mu+\tau=\nu}\left(a_{\tau}(z)C(z)^{-1}C(z+\tau)q_{\mu}(z+\tau)-\kappa_{\tau}q_{\mu}(z)\right)=0

for
\nu\in\mathcal{L}
, where we put
q_{\nu}=0
if
\nu\notin\mathcal{L}_{+}
.
Theorem
4.2
implies that these identities hold when
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and then it follows that they hold in general. This completes the proof.
∎

By analyticity, the bispectrality property of Theorem
5.5
holds in a bigger domain of analyticity of the function
\varphi
. To be more precise,
by Proposition
5.4
we have on an open set
V
an analytic function
\varphi
that satisfies equation (
5.6
) and by the proof of Theorem
5.5
also
\widetilde{D}\varphi=\mu(x)\varphi
. Suppose
(z,x,k)\in\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{l+1}\times\mathbb{C}
is such that
\varphi
can be analytically extended to a function
\widetilde{\varphi}(z,x,k)
on some neighbourhood
W
of
(z,x,k)
containing
V
. The function
L\widetilde{\varphi}-(z^{2}-\rho^{2})\widetilde{\varphi}
is analytic in
z,x,k
away from the singularities of
\coth\langle\alpha,x\rangle
for
\alpha\in BC(l,1)_{+}
, and on
V
it is identically zero. Thus, it must be zero on all of its domain of analyticity.
Similarly,
\widetilde{D}\widetilde{\varphi}-\mu(x)\widetilde{\varphi}
is analytic in
z,x,k
away from the union
P
of the poles of the functions
a_{\tau}(z)
and
C(z+\tau)C(z)^{-1}
, and it vanishes on the open set
V\setminus P
. Hence
\widetilde{D}\widetilde{\varphi}=\mu(x)\widetilde{\varphi}
on
W\setminus P
.
In terms of the function
\widetilde{\Psi}\coloneqq C\widetilde{\varphi}/\delta
, this means that the following bispectrality relation is satisfied:

\displaystyle H\widetilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle=-z^{2}\widetilde{\Psi},

\displaystyle D\widetilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\mu(x)\widetilde{\Psi}.
