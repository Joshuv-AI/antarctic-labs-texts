On Blow-up solutions to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in the exterior of a convex obstacle Thanks: 1 LAGA, UMR 7539, Institut Galilée, Université Sorbonne Paris Nord
Oussama Landoulsi 1

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the Schrödinger equation with a mass-supercritical focusing nonlinearity, in the exterior of a smooth, compact, convex obstacle of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with Dirichlet boundary conditions. We prove that solutions with negative energy blow up in finite time. Assuming furthermore that the nonlinearity is energy-subcritical, we also prove (under additional symmetry conditions) blow-up with the same optimal ground-state criterion than in the work of Holmer and Roudenko on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . The classical proof of Glassey, based on the concavity of the variance, fails in the exterior of an obstacle because of the appearance of boundary terms with an unfavorable sign in the second derivative of the variance. The main idea of our proof is to introduce a new modified variance which is bounded from below and strictly concave for the solutions that we consider.

1.
Introduction

We consider the focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rrrrrrrr}\begin{aligned} i\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{\Omega}u&=-|u|^{p-1}u\qquad&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\\
u(t_{0},x)&=u_{0}(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
u(t,x)&=0&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Theta
is the exterior of a smooth, compact and convex obstacle on
\mathbb{R}^{d},

\Delta_{\Omega}
is the Dirichlet Laplace operator,
\partial_{t}
is the derivative with respect to the time variable and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is the initial time. The function
u
is complex-valued,
u:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},\,(t,x)\longmapsto u(t,x).

The local well-posedness for the NLS
Ω
equation in the exterior of a smooth, compact and convex domain was studied in several articles and is now well understood in many cases. Local existence and uniqueness are usually proved by contraction mapping methods via Strichartz estimates. The Cauchy theory for the NLS
Ω
equation with initial data in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),
was initiated in
[
4
]
. The authors proved
first a Strichartz estimates under some restriction, which led to a local existence result for
p<3,d=3.
This results was later extended for all
1<p\leq 5,
in dimension
d=3
, in particular, see
[
1
]
for the cubic nonlinearity,
[
18
]
for
1<p<5
and
[
14
]
for
p=5,
see also
[
2
]
. The full range of Strichartz estimates for NLS
Ω
except the end point was obtained in
[
12
]
. Therefore, the NLS
Ω
equation is locally-well posed in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),
for
1<p<\frac{d+2}{d-2},d\geq 4
and
1<p<\infty,
for
d=2
(Cf. Proposition
1.1
below for a precise local well posedness statement needed for our purpose).

The solutions of (NLS
Ω
) satisfy the mass and energy conservation laws:

\displaystyle M[u(t)]

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}|u(t,x)|^{2}dx=M[u_{0}],

\displaystyle E[u(t)]

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(t,x)|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,x)\right|^{p+1}dx=E[u_{0}].

In
[
15
]
, the authors proved global existence and scattering of solutions for the focusing
3
d cubic NLS
Ω
equation, whenever the initial data satisfies a smallness criterion given by the ground state threshold, see also
[
22
]
for
\frac{7}{3}<p<5,d=3
. The criterion is expressed in terms of the scale-invariant quantities
\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\nabla u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}
and

Moreover, in
[
5
]
the authors revisited the proof of scattering using Dodson and Murphy’s approach
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and the dispersive estimate established in
[
13
]
. In
[
17

, we constructed a solitary wave solution for (NLS
Ω
) behaving asymptotically as a soliton on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, as large time. This solution is global, does not scatter and prove the optimality of the threshold for scattering given above.

All results mentioned above for (NLS
Ω
) concern global solutions but the existence of blow-up solutions is still an open question which is the purpose of this paper.

Before stating our blow-up results for the NLS
Ω
equation, let us recall the proof of the classical blow-up criterion of Vlasov-Petrishev-Talanov
[
19
]
, Zakharov
[
20
]
and Glassey
[
8
]
which states that finite variance, negative energy solutions break down in finite time. This proof is a convexity argument on the variance
V(t)
defined as the following,

V(t):=V(u(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}|u(t,x)|^{2}dx.

Assuming
V(0)<\infty
, the following virial identity holds:

\frac{1}{16}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}V(t)=E_{\footnotesize{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}(u)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d+2}{p+1}\right)\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1},

where
E_{\footnotesize{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}-\frac{1}{p+1}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}.
If
p>1+\frac{d}{4}
and
E_{\footnotesize{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}(u)<0
then
u
blows up in finite time. As proved in
[
10
]
, in the energy subcritical case (
d\leq 2
or
p<1+\frac{4}{d-2}
), the assumption
E_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u)<0
can be weakened to a condition on the initial data which can be formulated in term of the ground state (see Theorem
1.7
below).

This proof does not adapt directly to the case of an exterior domain because the boundary term in the virial identity does not have a favorable sign,

\frac{1}{16}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}V(u(t))=E[u]-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d+2}{p+1}\right)\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p+1}\,dx-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\,(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x),

where
\vec{n}
is the unit outward normal vector. One can see that
x.\vec{n}\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega.
In this work, we will define a new shifted variance
\mathcal{V}(t)
which will allow us to control the boundary term and to prove the existence of blow-up solution for the NLS
Ω
equation. In the energy subcritical case, with an additional symmetry assumption on the initial data, we will prove blow-up with the sufficient condition obtained in
[
10
]
on the Euclidean space.

2.
Properties of the Ground State

Weinstein
[
21
]
proved that the sharp constant
C_{GN}
in the Gagliardo-Nirenberg estimate

(2.1)

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p+1}}^{p+1}\leq C_{GN}\left\|\nabla f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d(p-1)}{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2-\frac{(d-2)(p-1)}{2}}

is attained at the function
Q
(the ground state described in the introduction), i.e

C_{GN}=\frac{\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}}{\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{\frac{d(p-1)}{2}}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2-\frac{(d-2)(p-1)}{2}}}\;.

Multiplying (
1.3
) by
Q
and integrating by parts, we obtain

(2.2)

-\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}-\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}+\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}=0.

Multiplying (
1.3
) by
x.\nabla Q
and integrating by parts, we obtain the following identity

(2.3)

\frac{d}{2}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}+\frac{d-2}{2}\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}-\frac{d}{p+1}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}=0.

These two identities (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) enable us to obtain these relations

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d(p-1)}{(d+2)-p(d-2)}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(p+1)}{d(p-1)}\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}

and thus, reexpress

(2.4)

C_{GN}=\left(\frac{2(p+1)}{d(p-1)}\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{\frac{4-(d-2)(p-1)}{d(p-1)-4}}\right)^{-\frac{d(p-1)-4}{2}}

We also compute

(2.5)

E[Q]:=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}-\frac{1}{p+1}\left\|Q\right\|_{L^{p+1}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{p+1}=\frac{d(p-1)-4}{2d(p-1)}\left\|\nabla Q\right\|_{L^{2}\footnotesize{\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}}^{2}.

3.
Pohozaev’s identities outside obstacle

This section is devoted to the proof of the Pohozaev’s Identity in exterior domain. In the following Proposition
\Omega
can be the exterior of any regular obstacle.

Proposition 3.1

(Pohozaev’s identity)
.

Let
u\in H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),|x|\,u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
then we have,

(3.1)

\re\int_{\Omega}\Delta\overline{u}\;(\,\frac{d}{2}\,u+x.\nabla u)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\;(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x).

(3.2)

\re\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta\overline{u}\;\left(\nabla u.\frac{x}{|x|}+(\frac{d-1}{2})\frac{u}{|x|}\right)\,dx=-\frac{(d-1)(d-3)}{4}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u|^{2}}{|x|^{3}}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\left|{/\penalty\!\!\!\!\nabla}u\right|^{2}}{|x|}\,dx\\
+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\frac{x.\vec{n}}{|x|}\,d\sigma(x),

where
\left|{/\penalty\!\!\!\!\nabla}u\right|^{2}:=\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}-\left|\frac{x}{|x|}.\nabla u\right|^{2}
and
\vec{n}
is the outward unit normal vector.

Proof.

Using integration by parts and the fact that
u
satisfies Dirichlet boundary condition (i.e
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
), we obtain

\displaystyle\re\int_{\Omega}\Delta\overline{u}\,(x.\nabla u)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\re\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{j}}\overline{u}\,\partial_{x_{j}}u\,dx-\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\re\int_{\Omega}\,x_{k}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}u\,\partial_{x_{j}}\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\;(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,x_{k}n_{k}\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\;(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{d}{2}\re\int_{\Omega}\Delta\overline{u}\,u\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\;(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x).

This conclude the proof of (
3.1
). Now let us prove (
3.2
), using the same argument as above and the fact that,

\partial_{x_{k}}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{|x|}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|x|}-\frac{x_{j}^{2}}{|x|^{3}}\;\quad\text{ if }j=k,\\
\\
-\frac{x_{j}\,x_{k}}{|x|^{3}}\quad\quad\text{ if }j\neq k,\end{cases}

we obtain

\displaystyle\re\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta\overline{u}\;\nabla u.\frac{x}{|x|}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{k}}\overline{u}\;\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{j}}u\;\frac{x_{j}}{|x|}+\partial_{x_{k}}u\;\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{j}}\overline{u}\;\frac{x_{j}}{|x|}\,dx\right]

\displaystyle-\re\left[\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{j}}\overline{u}\;\partial_{x_{j}}u\left(\frac{1}{|x|}-\frac{x_{j}^{2}}{|x|^{3}}\right)\,dx\right]+\re\left[\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k=1\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{k}}\overline{u}\;\partial_{x_{j}}u\;\frac{x_{j}\,x_{k}}{|x|^{3}}\,dx\right]

5.
Existence of blow-up symmetric solution

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
. For the sake of simplicity, we will give the proof for
d=2,
then we will generalize the result to any dimension
d\geq 3.

Assume that
d=2,\;p\geq 3
and
\Theta
is invariant by the symmetry,
x_{j}\longmapsto-x_{j},
Recall that define
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is defined as the following

\mathcal{S}_{2}:=\{u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\backslash u_{0}(-x_{1},x_{2})=u_{0}(x_{1},-x_{2})=-u_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\},

here, we can consider
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).
By uniqueness for the Cauchy problem, we have

u(t,-x_{1},x_{2})=u(t,x_{1},-x_{2})=-u(t,x_{1},x_{2}).

Due to the two symmetry assumptions, we will reduce the problem to a quarter of the space. We define

\displaystyle\Omega:

\displaystyle=\bigcup\Omega^{\pm\pm}:=\Omega^{+}\,\cup\,\Omega^{-}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega^{\pm\pm}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega\backslash\,x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\pm}\text{ and }x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\pm}\},

\displaystyle\;\Omega^{\pm}:

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega\backslash\,x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\pm}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega_{\pm}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega\backslash\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\pm}\}.

Furthermore, since
u_{{|_{x_{1}=0}}}=0
and
u_{|_{x_{2}=0}}=0,
one can see that
u
satisfies the Dirichlet boundary conditions on each set defined above,
\Omega^{\pm\pm}
and
\Omega^{\pm}.

The variance identity here is the following:
Let
C>0
be a positive constant to be specified later,

{\rm{V}}(u(t)):=\int_{\Omega}\left(|x|^{2}-C|x_{1}|-C|x_{2}|+C^{2}\right)\left|u(t,x)\right|^{2}\,dx.

Denote

\Gamma_{1}(u(t)):=\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}||u(t,x)|^{2}dx,\qquad\qquad\;\,\Gamma_{2}(u(t)):=\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}||u(t,x)|^{2}dx.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
|x|\,u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
u_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
and let
u
be the corresponding solution of the
NLS
Ω

equation. Then

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{1}(u(t))

\displaystyle=8\,\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\partial_{x_{1}}u(t,x)\,\overline{u}(t,x)\,dx,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{1}(u(t))

\displaystyle=8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u(t,x)\right|^{2}|n_{1}|\,dx,

and

(5.3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{2}(u(t))

\displaystyle=8\,\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\partial_{x_{2}}u(t,x)\,\overline{u}(t,x)dx,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(u(t))

\displaystyle=8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u(t,x)\right|^{2}|n_{2}|\,dx.

Proof.

Multiply the equation by
|x_{1}|\bar{u}
and take the imaginary part to get:

\im\int_{\Omega}i\partial_{t}u|x_{1}|\overline{u}\,dx+\im\int_{\Omega}\Delta u|x_{1}|\overline{u}\,dx=-\underbrace{\im\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p-1}u|x_{1}|\overline{u}\,dx}_{=0},

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}||u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}|\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{1}(u(t)):

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}||u(t,x)|^{2}dx=-2\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}|\,\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx.

Integration by parts ensures,

\displaystyle\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{1}|\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\im\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{1}}(|x_{1}|)\,\partial_{x_{1}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\left(\im\int_{\Omega^{+}}\partial_{x_{1}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx-\im\int_{\Omega^{-}}\partial_{x_{1}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=-4\;\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\partial_{x_{1}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx,

which yields (
5.1
). Now, let us compute the second derivative of
\Gamma_{1}(u(t)).

Denote:

\alpha(t):=\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\overline{u}(t,x)\;x.\nabla u(t,x)dx,\quad\beta(t):=\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\overline{u}(t,x)\,(x-e_{1}).\nabla u(t,x)dx.

Thus, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{1}(u(t))=8(\alpha(t)-\beta(t)).

Due to the symmetry properties of
u
, one can see that
\alpha(t)
is equal to
\frac{1}{16}\frac{d}{dt}\Upsilon_{2}(t)
and
\beta(t)
is equal to
(
4.1
)
applied to
(x-e_{1})
, where
e_{1}=(1,0)
.
By (
4.2
), we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=4E_{+}[u]-2\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d+2}{p+1}\right)\int_{\Omega^{++}}|u|^{p+1}\,dx-\int_{\partial{\Omega^{++}}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\,(x.\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\beta(t)

\displaystyle=4E_{+}[u]-2\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d+2}{p+1}\right)\int_{\Omega^{++}}|u|^{p+1}\,dx-\int_{\partial{\Omega^{++}}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\,((x-e_{1}).\vec{n})\,d\sigma(x).

where,
\displaystyle E_{+}[u]=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{++})}-\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\Omega^{++}}|u|^{p+1}dx.
Taking the difference between these two equalities, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\alpha(t)-\beta(t)\right)=-\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}(e_{1}.\vec{n})\,dx=-\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}n_{1}\,dx,

which yields

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{1}(u(t)):=-8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}n_{1}\,dx.

Using the symmetry properties, the convexity of the obstacle
\Theta
and the fact that
\vec{n}
is the unit outward normal vector, we have
n_{1}\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega^{++}.

Then, we obtain

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{1}(u(t))=8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}|n_{1}|\,dx.

Recall that

\Gamma_{2}:=\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}||u(t,x)|^{2}dx.

We multiply the equation by
|x_{2}|\bar{u}
and take the imaginary part to get:

\im\int_{\Omega}i\partial_{t}u|x_{2}|\overline{u}\,dx+\im\int_{\Omega}\Delta u|x_{2}|\overline{u}\,dx=-\im\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p-1}u|x_{2}|\overline{u}\,dx=0.

Which yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}||u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}|\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma_{2}(u(t)):

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}||u(t,x)|^{2}dx=-2\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}|\,\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx.

Integration by parts ensures,

\displaystyle\im\int_{\Omega}|x_{2}|\Delta u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\im\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{2}}(|x_{2}|)\,\partial_{x_{2}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\left(\im\int_{\Omega_{+}}\partial_{x_{2}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx-\im\int_{\Omega_{-}}\partial_{x_{2}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=-4\im\int_{\Omega^{++}}\partial_{x_{2}}u\,\overline{u}\,dx,

which yields (
5.3
).
The computation of
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(t)
is similar to the one of
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{1}(t),
the only difference is to apply (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) to
(x-e_{2}),
where
e_{2}=(0,1)
instead of
(x-e_{1}).
Thus, we get

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(u(t)):=-8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}n_{2}\,dx,

As
\vec{n}
is the unit outward normal vector, we have
n_{2}\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega^{++}
using the symmetry properties and the convexity of the obstacle
\Theta.
Then we obtain,

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(u(t)):=8\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}|n_{2}|\,dx.

This concludes the proof of Proposition
5.1
.
∎

.

and

. Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}

|x|u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
E[u_{0}]<0
and llet

be the corresponding solution of (NLS
Ω
) with maximal time interval

of existence. From Proposition

4.1
, we deduce the second derivative of the variance for

(5.5)

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}{\rm{V}}(u(t))=16E[u]-8\left(\frac{p-3}{p+1}\right)\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p+1}\,dx\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega^{++}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\,\big[16|x.\vec{n}|-8C\left(|n_{1}|+|n_{2}|\right)\big]\,d\sigma(x).

Using the fact that

1.5
in dimension

For any dimension

One can check that

(5.6)

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}{\rm{V}}(u(t))=16E[u]-8\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d+2}{p+1}\right)\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p+1}\,dx\\

Using the fact that

Then

∎
