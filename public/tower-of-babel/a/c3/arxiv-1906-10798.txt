k-stretchability of entanglement, and the duality of k-separability and k-producibility
Szilárd Szalay

Abstract
The notions of k k -separability and k k -producibility are useful and expressive tools for the characterization of entanglement in multipartite quantum systems, when a more detailed analysis would be infeasible or simply needless. In this work we reveal a partial duality between them, which is valid also for their correlation counterparts. This duality can be seen from a much wider perspective, when we consider the entanglement and correlation properties which are invariant under the permutations of the subsystems. These properties are labeled by Young diagrams, which we endow with a refinement-like partial order, to build up their classification scheme. This general treatment reveals a new property, which we call k k -stretchability, being sensitive in a balanced way to both the maximal size of correlated (or entangled) subsystems and the minimal number of subsystems uncorrelated with (or separable from) one another.

2
Multipartite correlation and entanglement

Here we recall the structure of the classification and quantification
of multipartite correlation and entanglement
[
40
,
14
,
41
]
.
Our goal is to do this in the way sufficient to see how the
permutation invariant properties can be formulated parallel to this in the next section.

2.1
Level 0: subsystems

The classification scheme we present here is rather general.
The elementary and composite subsystems can be any discrete finite systems
possessing probabilistic description,
supposed that the joint systems can be represented by the use of
tensor products,

which is the basic tool in the constructions.
Such systems can be
distinguishable quantum systems,
second quantized bosonic systems,
second quantized fermionic systems with fermion number parity superselection rule imposed,
or even classical systems, with significant simplification in the structure in the latter case
[
41
]
.

Let
L
be the set of the labels of
{\lvert L\rvert}=n

elementary subsystems.

All the (possibly composite)
subsystems
are then labeled by the subsets
X\subseteq L
,
the set of which,
P_{0}:=2^{L}
, naturally possesses a Boolean lattice structure
with respect to the inclusion
\subseteq
, which is now denoted with
\preceq
.
For every elementary subsystem
i\in L
,
let the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be associated with it,
where
1<\dim\mathcal{H}_{i}<\infty
.
From these,
for every subsystem
X\in P_{0}
,
the Hilbert space associated with it is
\mathcal{H}_{X}=\bigotimes_{i\in X}\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
(For the trivial subsystem
X=\emptyset
,
we have the one-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\emptyset}=\Span\{|\psi\rangle\}\cong\mathbb{C}

[
40
]
.)
The
states
of the subsystems
X\in P_{0}

are given by density operators (positive semidefinite operators of trace
1
)
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{X}
,
the sets of those are denoted with
\mathcal{D}_{X}
.

The
mixedness
of a state
\varrho_{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
of a subsystem
X

can be characterized by the
von Neumann entropy

[
63
,
64
,
65
,
7
]

S(\varrho_{X})=-\Tr\varrho_{X}\ln\varrho_{X};

(1a)

and the
distinguishability
of two states
\varrho_{X},\sigma_{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}
of subsystem
X

can be characterized by the
Umegaki relative entropy

[
66
,
67
,
68
,
64
,
65
,
6
,
7
]

D(\varrho_{X}\|\sigma_{X})=\Tr\varrho_{X}(\ln\varrho_{X}-\ln\sigma_{X}).

(1b)

The von Neumann entropy is monotone increasing in bistochastic quantum channels,
and the relative entropy is monotone decreasing in quantum channels
[
67
,
65
,
6
]

and
S(\varrho_{X})=0\,\Leftrightarrow\,\varrho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
,

D(\varrho_{X}\|\sigma_{X})=0\,\Leftrightarrow\,\varrho_{X}=\sigma_{X}
.

2.2
Level I: set partitions

For handling the different possible splits of a composite system into subsystems,
we need to use the mathematical notion of (set) partition
[
44
]
of the system
L
.
The
partitions
of
L
are sets of subsystems,

\xi=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{{\lvert\xi\rvert}}\}\in\Pi(L),

(2a)

where the
parts
,
X\in\xi
, are nonempty disjoint subsystems, for which
\bigcup_{X\in\xi}X=L
.
The set of the partitions of
L
is denoted with

P_{\text{I}}:=\Pi(L).

(2b)

Its size is given by the
Bell numbers

[
69
]
, which are rapidly growing with
n
.
There is a natural partial order over the partitions,
which is called
refinement

\preceq
,
given as

\upsilon\preceq\xi\;\;\overset{\text{def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\;\forall Y\in\upsilon,\exists X\in\xi\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;Y\subseteq X.

(2c)

(For illustrations, see Figure
1
.)
This grabs our natural intuition of comparing splits of systems.
Note that the refinement is a partial order only,
there are pairs of partitions which cannot be ordered,
for example,

12|3\npreceq 13|2
and

12|3\nsucceq 13|2
, see Figure
1
.
(We use a simplified notation for the partitions, for example,
12|3\equiv\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}
,
where this does not cause confusion.)
Note that
P_{\text{I}}
with the refinement is a lattice,
with minimal and maximal elements
\bot=\{\{i\}\;|\;i\in L\}
and
\top=\{L\}
,
and the least upper and greatest lower bounds,
\vee
and
\wedge
, can be constructed
[
44
,
45
]
.

With respect to the partitions
\xi\in P_{\text{I}}
,
we can define the partial correlation and entanglement properties,
as well as the measures quantifying them.

The
\xi
-uncorrelated states
are those which are products
with respect to the partition
\xi
,

\begin{split}&\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}:=\\
&\Bigl\{\varrho_{L}\in\mathcal{D}_{L}\;\Big|\;\forall X\in\xi,\exists\varrho_{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}:\varrho_{L}=\bigotimes_{X\in\xi}\varrho_{X}\Bigr\};\end{split}

(3a)

the others are
\xi
-correlated states
.
With respect to the finest partition
\xi=\bot
, we call

\bot
-uncorrelated states
\bigotimes_{i\in L}\varrho_{i}
simply uncorrelated states.
The expectation values of all
\xi
-local observables factorize if and only if the state is
\xi
-uncorrelated.
The
\xi
-uncorrelated states are exactly those
which can be prepared from uncorrelated states by
\xi
-local operations.
(This is abbreviated as
\xi
-LO. With respect to the finest partition
\xi=\bot
, we write simply LO.)
The
\xi
-separable states
are
convex combinations (or statistical mixtures) of
\xi
-uncorrelated states,

\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-sep}}:=\Conv\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}\equiv\Bigl\{\sum_{j}p_{j}\varrho_{j}\;\Big|\;\varrho_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}\Bigr\};

(3b)

the others are
\xi
-entangled states
.
(Here, and in the entire text,

\{p_{j}\;|\;j=1,2,\dots,m\}
is a probability distribution,

p_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
.)
The
\xi
-separable states are exactly those
which can be prepared from uncorrelated states
by
\xi
-local operations and classical communication among the parts
X\in\xi
.
(This is abbreviated as
\xi
-LOCC. With respect to the finest partition
\bot
, we write simply LOCC.)
Another point of view is that

\xi
-separable states are exactly those
which can be prepared from uncorrelated states
by mixtures of
\xi
-LOs.
We also have that

\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}
is closed under
\xi
-LO,

\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-sep}}
is closed under
\xi
-LOCC
[
40
,
41
]
.
Clearly, if a state is product with respect to a partition,
then it is also product with respect to any coarser partition,
it is always free to forget about some tensor product signs.
This means that these properties
show the same lattice structure as the partitions
[
40
,
41
]
,
P_{\text{I}}
,
that is,

\displaystyle\upsilon\preceq\xi

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\mathcal{D}_{\upsilon\text{-unc}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\upsilon\preceq\xi

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\mathcal{D}_{\upsilon\text{-sep}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-sep}}.

(4b)

The lattices of the three-level structure of multipartite correlation and entanglement for

.
Only the maximal elements of the down-sets of

One can define the corresponding (information-geometry based)
correlation and entanglement measures
[
40
,
14
]

for all
\xi
-correlation and
\xi
-entanglement.
These are the most natural generalizations of
the
mutual information

[
6
,
7
]
,
the
entanglement entropy

[
16
]
,
and the
entanglement of formation

[
17
]

for Level I of the multipartite case.

The
\xi
-correlation
of a state
\varrho
is
its distinguishability by the relative entropy (
1b
)
from the
\xi
-uncorrelated states

[
70
,
4
,
40
,
14
]
,

C_{\xi}(\varrho):=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}}D(\varrho||\sigma)=\sum_{X\in\xi}S(\varrho_{X})-S(\varrho),

(5a)

given in terms of the von Neumann entropies (
1a
) of the
reduced, or marginal states
\varrho_{X}=\Tr_{L\setminus X}\varrho
of subsystems
X\in\xi
.
Pure states of classical systems are always uncorrelated,
the correlation in pure states is of quantum origin, this is what we call entanglement
[
1
,
2
,
15
,
3
,
40
,
14
,
41
]
.
So, for pure states, the measure of entanglement should be that of correlation.
The
\xi
-entanglement
of a pure state
\pi=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\in\mathcal{D}_{L}
is
its
\xi
-correlation,

E_{\xi}(\pi):=C_{\xi}\big|_{\text{pure}}(\pi)={\sum}_{X\in\xi}S(\pi_{X}),

(5b)

with
\pi_{X}=\Tr_{L\setminus X}\pi
,
and for mixed states, one can use, for example, the convex roof extension
[
17
,
71
,
72
]

to define the
\xi
-entanglement of formation

\begin{split}&E_{\xi}(\varrho):={\bigl(C_{\xi}\big|_{\text{pure}}\bigr)}^{\cup}(\varrho)\\
&\equiv\min\Bigl\{{\sum}_{j}p_{j}C_{\xi}\big|_{\text{pure}}(\pi_{j})\;\Big|\;{\sum}_{j}p_{j}\pi_{j}=\varrho\Bigr\},\end{split}

(5c)

where
{\sum}_{j}p_{j}\pi_{j}
is a pure decomposition of the state
[
40
]
.
We have that

C_{\xi}
is a correlation monotone (not increasing with respect to
\xi
-LO, for the proof see Appendix
B.1
),

E_{\xi}
is a strong entanglement monotone (convex and not increasing on average with respect to selective
\xi
-LOCC
[
22
,
23
,
40
]
),
and both of these are faithful,

C_{\xi}(\varrho)=0\,\Leftrightarrow\,\varrho\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}
,

E_{\xi}(\varrho)=0\,\Leftrightarrow\,\varrho\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-sep}}

[
40
]
,
moreover, they show the same lattice structure as the partitions,
P_{\text{I}}
,
that is,

\displaystyle\upsilon\preceq\xi

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;C_{\upsilon}\geq C_{\xi},

(6a)

\displaystyle\upsilon\preceq\xi

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;E_{\upsilon}\geq E_{\xi},

(6b)

which is called
multipartite monotonicity

[
40
,
14
]
.

Note that, for two subsystems,
for the only nontrivial partition

, we have that
E_{1|2}(\pi)=2S(\pi_{1})=2S(\pi_{2})
is just two times the usual
entanglement entropy
.
However, we have a way of derivation completely different than the usual,
based on asymptotic LOCC convertibility from Bell-pairs
[
16
]
.
The usual way cannot be generalized to the multipartite scenario
(there is no “reference state”, which was the Bell-pair in the bipartite scenario),
while our correlational, or statistical physical approach above could straightforwardly be generalized to the multipartite scenario
not only here, but also for the Level II properties in the next subsystem.

2.3
Level II: multiple set partitions

In multipartite entanglement theory,
it is necessary to handle mixtures of states uncorrelated with respect to different partitions
[
35
,
37
,
40
]
.
For example, there are tripartite states which
cannot be written as a mixture of a given kind of, e.g.,
12|3
-uncorrelated states,
that is, not
12|3
-separable,
while can be written as a mixture of
12|3
-uncorrelated and
13|2
-uncorrelated states.
Such states should not be considered fully tripartite-entangled,
since there is no need for genuine tripartite entangled states in the mixture
[
35
,
37
,
39
,
40
]
.
Also, if a state is
12|3
-separable and also
13|2
-separable,
it is not necessarily
1|2|3
-separable.
On the other hand,
the order isomorphisms (
4a
)-(
4b
) tell us that
if we consider states uncorrelated (or separable) with respect to a partition,
then we automatically consider states uncorrelated (or separable) with respect to all finer partitions.

To embed these requirements in the labeling of the multipartite correlation and entanglement properties,
we use the
nonempty down-sets (nonempty ideals) of partitions

[
40
]
,
which are sets of partitions

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{{\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert}}\}\subseteq P_{\text{I}}

(7a)

which are closed downwards with respect to
\preceq
.
That is, if
\xi\in\boldsymbol{\xi}
, then for all partitions
\upsilon\preceq\xi
we have
\upsilon\in\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
The set of the nonempty partition ideals
is denoted with

P_{\text{II}}:=\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}(P_{\text{I}})\setminus\{\emptyset\}.

(7b)

This possesses a lattice structure with respect to the standard inclusion
as partial order, which we call
refinement
again, and denote with
\preceq
again,

\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\preceq\boldsymbol{\xi}\;\;\overset{\text{def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\;\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\xi}.

(7c)

(For illustrations, see Figure
1
.)
The least upper and greatest lower bounds,
\vee
and
\wedge
,
are then the union and the intersection,
\cup
and
\cap
.

With respect to the partition ideals
\xi\in P_{\text{II}}
,
we can define the partial correlation and entanglement properties,
as well as the measures quantifying these.

The
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-uncorrelated states
are those which are
\xi
-uncorrelated (
3a
)
with respect to a
\xi\in\boldsymbol{\xi}
,

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-unc}}:=\bigcup_{\xi\in\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}};

(8a)

the others are
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-correlated states
.
The
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-uncorrelated states are exactly those
which can be prepared from uncorrelated states by
\xi
-LO for a partition
\xi\in\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
The
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-separable states
are convex combinations of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-uncorrelated states,

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}:=\Conv\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-unc}};

(8b)

the others are
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-entangled states
.
The
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-separable states are exactly those
which can be prepared from uncorrelated states
by mixtures of
\xi

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\preceq\boldsymbol{\xi}

2.4
Level III: classes

The partial correlation and entanglement properties form an inclusion hierarchy (
9a
)-(
9b
),
for example, if a state is

-separable, then it is also
12|3
-separable.
We are interested in the labeling of the strict, or exclusive properties,
for example, those states which are
12|3
-separable and not
1|2|3
-separable.
In general, we would like to determine all the possible nonempty intersections of the state sets

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-unc}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}
.
We call these intersections
partial correlation
and
partial entanglement classes,

containing states of well-defined partial correlation and partial entanglement properties.

To embed these requirements in the labeling of the strict properties of multipartite correlation and entanglement,
we use the
nonempty up-sets (nonempty filters) of nonempty down-sets (nonempty ideals) of partitions

[
40
]

\preceq

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\succeq\boldsymbol{\xi}

\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

The set of the nonempty up-sets of nonempty down-sets of partitions of

(12b)

This possesses a lattice structure with respect to the standard inclusion
as partial order, which we call

again, and denote with
\preceq
again,

\underline{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\preceq\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\;\;\overset{\text{def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\;\underline{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\subseteq\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

(12c)

(For illustrations, see Figure
1
.)
Note that here, contrary to Level I and II, we call
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
finer and
\underline{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}

In the generic case,

then the possible intersections can be described by

(for the proof, see appendix A.2 in
[

One may make the classification coarser

[

,
41
]

With respect to the partition ideal filters
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in P_{\text{III*}}

we can define the strict partial correlation and entanglement properties.

The

-uncorrelated states

(13a)

The
strictly
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
-separable states
are those which are
separable with respect to all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
,
and entangled with respect to all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\overline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
,
so the
class
of these

, or
partial entanglement class

\mathcal{C}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{-sep}}:=\bigcap_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\overline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}\overline{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}}\cap\bigcap_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}.

(13b)

The meaning of the Level III hierarchy could also be clarified.
If there exists a

mapping it into

\underline{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\preceq\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

[

]

[
40
]

In this sense, the Level III hierarchy compares the strength of

The filters
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
are

for the description of the

of the correlation and entanglement properties,
but they are
not necessary
in general.
For the strictly
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
-uncorrelated states,
the structure

simplifies significantly
[

]

(For example, for the finest classification

as the unique labeling of the nontrivial partial correlation classes is given as

for the partitions

[

On the other hand, it is still a conjecture that

\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
-separability is nontrivial for all

[

The conjecture holds for three subsystems, for which explicit examples were constructed

\hat{\xi}\in\hat{P}_{\text{I}}
as

\displaystyle h(\hat{\xi})

\displaystyle:={\lvert\hat{\xi}\rvert},

(46a)

\displaystyle w(\hat{\xi})

\displaystyle:=\max\hat{\xi},

(46b)

\displaystyle r(\hat{\xi})

\displaystyle:=w(\hat{\xi})-h(\hat{\xi}),

(46c)

and also for set partition
\xi\in P_{\text{I}}
as

h(\xi):=h(s(\xi))={\lvert\xi\rvert}
,

w(\xi):=w(s(\xi))=\max_{X\in\xi}{\lvert X\rvert}
,

r(\xi):=r(s(\xi))=w(\xi)-h(\xi)
.
It is enlightening now to arrange the poset
\hat{P}_{\text{I}}

according to the height and width of the partitions,
in the way as can be seen in Figure
6
,
i.e.,
the height is increasing downwards,
the width is increasing to the right, then
the rank is increasing up-right.
It is easy to see that
the height is strictly decreasing,
the width is increasing,
and the rank is strictly increasing monotone
with respect to the refinement (
18c
),

\begin{split}&\hat{\upsilon}\prec\hat{\xi}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\\
&\quad h(\hat{\upsilon})>h(\hat{\xi}),\quad w(\hat{\upsilon})\leq w(\hat{\xi}),\quad r(\hat{\upsilon})<r(\hat{\xi}).\end{split}

(47)

(For illustration, see Figure
6
:
the arrows point always upwards, and possibly to the up-right,
never to the up-left, horizontally or downwards.)

Now the partitionability and producibility properties (
36a
) and (
36b
)
can be formulated with the height and width as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-part}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\hat{\xi}\in\hat{P}_{\text{I}}\;\big|\;h(\hat{\xi})\geq k\bigr\},

(48a)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-prod}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\hat{\xi}\in\hat{P}_{\text{I}}\;\big|\;w(\hat{\xi})\leq k\bigr\},

(48b)

so
k
-partitionability is the minimal height,

k
-producibility is the maximal width of the partition.
In the height-width based arrangement of
\hat{P}_{\text{I}}
in Figure
6
,
the
k
-partitionable partitions (
48a
) are below the
k
-th rows (counting from the top),
and
k
-producible partitions (
48b
) are to the left from the
k
-th column (counting from the left).
We can also introduce another property, called
k
-stretchability
, as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}=\bigl\{\hat{\xi}\in\hat{P}_{\text{I}}\;\big|\;r(\hat{\xi})\leq k\bigr\},

(48c)

for
k=-(n-1),-(n-2),\dots,n-2,n-1
,
so
k
-stretchability is the maximal rank of the partition.
It is easy to see that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}\in\hat{P}_{\text{II}}
, that is, it is a down-set for all
k
,
by the third inequality in (
47
).
We elaborate on the arising correlation and entanglement properties later, in Section
5.3
.

-partitionability and

\hat{P}_{\text{I}}
for

and

.
(The two kinds of down-sets contain the elements below the green and to the left from the yellow dashed lines.)
For the first three cases, compare with

.

On the set of Young diagrams,
an involution arises naturally, called
conjugation
,
being the reflection of the diagram with respect to its “diagonal”
[
45
]
.
Considering the integer partitions themselves, the conjugation is given as follows.
For decreasingly ordered values
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{{\lvert\hat{\xi}\rvert}})
of the elements of
\hat{\xi}
,
the number of elements
x^{\prime}
that equal to
i
is
x_{i}-x_{i+1}
in the conjugated partition (setting
x_{i+1}=0
for
i=h(\hat{\xi})
).
With multisets, the conjugation is given as
the map

\hat{P}_{\text{I}}\to\hat{P}_{\text{I}}
,

\hat{\xi}\mapsto\hat{\xi}^{\dagger}=\bigl\{{\lvert\{x\in\hat{\xi}\;|\;x\geq i\}\rvert}\;\big|\;i=1,2,\dots,w(\hat{\xi})\bigr\},

(49)

where both sets on the right-hand side are multisets.

The partial order (
18c
) does not show nice properties with respect to the conjugation.
(For illustration, see the height-width based arrangement in Figure
6
.)
The conjugation is not an anti-automorphism,
if
\hat{\upsilon}\preceq\hat{\xi}
then it does not follow that

\hat{\upsilon}^{\dagger}\succeq\hat{\xi}^{\dagger}

(the first example is for
n=4
, where we have
\{2,1,1\}\preceq\{2,2\}

and
\{2,1,1\}^{\dagger}=\{3,1\}\not\succeq\{2,2\}=\{2,2\}^{\dagger}
).
The conjugation is neither an automorphism of course,
if
\hat{\upsilon}\preceq\hat{\xi}
then it does not follow that

\hat{\upsilon}^{\dagger}\preceq\hat{\xi}^{\dagger}

(the first example is for
n=2
, where we have
\{1,1\}\preceq\{2\}

and
\{1,1\}^{\dagger}=\{2\}\not\preceq\{1,1\}=\{2\}^{\dagger}
).
An integer partition
\hat{\xi}\in\hat{P}_{\text{I}}

and its conjugate cannot be ordered by refinement
(the first example is for
n=6
, where we have
\{2,2,2\}
and
\{3,3\}
,
which are conjugates of each other, and cannot be ordered).

The conjugation interchanges the height and width, and multiplies the rank with
-1
,

h(\hat{\xi}^{\dagger})=w(\hat{\xi}),\quad w(\hat{\xi}^{\dagger})=h(\hat{\xi}),\quad r(\hat{\xi}^{\dagger})=-r(\hat{\xi}).

(50)

(For illustration, see the height-width based arrangement in Figure
6
:
the conjugation brings to the position mirrored with respect to the diagonal
w(\hat{\xi})=h(\hat{\xi})
.)
Note that,
although the conjugation interchanges the height and width,
it does not interchange
the partitionability (
48a
) and producibility (
48b
) properties,
since these are given in different ways by height and width,
namely, by lower and upper bounds.

5.3
k
-stretchability of correlation and entanglement

In Section
5.2
,
we introduced the permutation invariant property
k
-stretchability (
48c
)
for
k=-(n-1),-(n-2),\dots,n-2,n-1
.
Because of the third inequality in (
47
),
these form a chain in the lattice
\hat{P}_{\text{II}}
,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{l\text{-str}}\preceq\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;l\leq k.

(51)

(For illustration, see the height-width based arrangement in Figure
6
.)

The corresponding

k
-stretchably uncorrelated states
(
25a
) are

\begin{split}&\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str unc}}:=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}\text{-unc}}\\
&=\Bigl\{\bigotimes_{X\in\xi}\varrho_{X}\;\Big|\;\forall\xi\in P_{\text{I}}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\max_{X\in\xi}{\lvert X\rvert}-{\lvert\xi\rvert}\leq k\Bigr\},\end{split}

(52a)

which are products
of density operators
of as few subsystems as possible, of size as large as possible, of difference upper-bounded by
k
.
In this sense, this is a combination of
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility.
The corresponding
k
-stretchably separable states
(
25b
) are

\begin{split}&\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str sep}}:=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}\text{-sep}}=\Conv\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str unc}}\\
&=\Bigl\{\sum_{j}p_{j}\bigotimes_{X\in\xi_{j}}\varrho_{X,j}\;\Big|\;\forall j,\\
&\qquad\qquad\forall\xi_{j}\in P_{\text{I}}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\max_{X\in\xi_{j}}{\lvert X\rvert}-{\lvert\xi_{j}\rvert}\leq k\Bigr\},\end{split}

(52b)

which can be decomposed into
k
-stretchably uncorrelated states.
Because of (
26a
),
these properties show the same chain structure
as the chains of the corresponding partition ideals (
51
),
that is,

\displaystyle l\leq k

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\mathcal{D}_{l\text{-str unc}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str unc}},

(53a)

\displaystyle l\leq k

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\mathcal{D}_{l\text{-str sep}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str sep}},

(53b)

that is,
if a state is
l
-stretchably uncorrelated (separable)
then it is also
k
-stretchably uncorrelated (separable) for all
l\leq k
.

The corresponding

k
-stretchability correlation
(
27a
) is

\displaystyle C_{k\text{-str}}:=C_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}}=C_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}}=\min_{\xi:\max\limits_{X\in\xi}{\lvert X\rvert}-{\lvert\xi\rvert}\leq k}C_{\xi}.

(54a)

The corresponding

k
-stretchability entanglement
(
27c
) is

\displaystyle E_{k\text{-str}}:=E_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k\text{-str}}}=E_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}}.

(54b)

Because of (
28
),
these measures show the same chain structure
as the chain of the corresponding partition ideals (
51
),
that is,

\displaystyle l\leq k

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;C_{l\text{-str}}\geq C_{k\text{-str}},

(55a)

\displaystyle l\leq k

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;E_{l\text{-str}}\geq E_{k\text{-str}},

(55b)

that is,

l
-stretchability correlation (entanglement) is always stronger than

k
-stretchability correlation (entanglement) for all
l\leq k
,
which is the multipartite monotonicity of these measures.

For the labeling of the strict stretchability properties,
we have the ideal filters

\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{k\text{-str}}:=\upset\bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k\text{-str}}\bigr\}=\bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{l\text{-str}}\;\big|\;l\geq k\bigr\},

(56)

by (
48c
) and (
51
).
The corresponding

strictly
k
-stretchably uncorrelated states
(
31a
) are

\mathcal{C}_{k\text{-str unc}}:=\mathcal{C}_{\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{k\text{-str}}\text{-unc}}=\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str unc}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{(k-1)\text{-str unc}},

(57a)

which are products
of nonproduct density operators of as few subsystems as possible, of size as large as possible, of difference exactly
k
.
The corresponding

strictly
k
-stretchably separable states
(
31b
) are

\mathcal{C}_{k\text{-str sep}}:=\mathcal{C}_{\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{k\text{-str}}\text{-sep}}=\mathcal{D}_{k\text{-str sep}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{(k-1)\text{-str sep}},

(57b)

which can be decomposed into
k
-stretchably but not
(k-1)
-stretchably uncorrelated states.
(
\mathcal{D}_{(-n)\text{-str unc/sep}}=\emptyset
is understood.)

k
-partitionability (
48a
),
k
-producibility (
48b
) and
k
-stretchability (
48c
)
give, of course, a rather coarsened description of permutation invariant correlation or entanglement properties.
Because of this, they must show some disadvantages.
First,
k
-partitionability (
48a
)
takes into account only the number of subsystems uncorrelated with (or separable from) one another,
and does not distinguish between the cases
when subsystems of roughly equal sizes are uncorrelated (or separable),
and when some elementary subsystems are uncorrelated with (or separable from) the rest of a large system,
although these two cases represent highly different situations form a resource-theoretical point of view.
(For illustration, see the rows in Figure
6
.)
Second,
k
-producibility (
48b
)
takes into account only the size of the largest subsystem uncorrelated with (or separable from) the other part of the system,
and does not distinguish between the cases
when a large subsystem is uncorrelated with (or separable from) a slightly smaller subsystem,
or many elementary subsystems,
although these two cases represent again highly different situations form a resource-theoretical point of view.
(For illustration, see the columns in Figure
6
.)
Third,
k
-stretchability (
48c
) combines the advantages of the previous two properties in a balanced way,
it takes into account a kind of “difference” of them,
and does not distinguish among cases with rather different types.
(For illustration, see the lines parallel to the diagonal in Figure
6
.)

6
Summary, remarks and open questions

In this work we investigated
the
partial correlation and entanglement properties
which are
invariant under the permutations of the subsystems.

The set partition based three-level structure,
describing the classification of partial correlation and entanglement (Section
2
)
was mapped to a parallel, integer partition based three-level structure,
describing the permutation invariant case (Section
3
).
This mapping is easy to understand on Level I of the construction,
however, to see that it is working well through the whole construction,
a formal proof was given (Appendix
A
).
The construction can be made more compact, although less transparent (Section
4
),
which can be used as a starting point of more advanced investigations.

We also investigated
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility,
fitting naturally into the structure of permutation invariant properties.
A kind of combination of these two gives
k
-stretchability,
which is sensitive in a balanced way to
both the maximal size of correlated (or entangled) subsystems
and the minimal number of subsystems uncorrelated with (or separable from) one another.
We studied their relations, and a duality, connecting the former two (Section
5
).

In the following, we list some remarks and open questions.

The first point to note is that
we have followed a treatment of entanglement,
which is somewhat different than the standard LOCC paradigm
[
15
]
.
The LOCC paradigm grabs the essence of entanglement
as a correlation which cannot be created or increased by classical communication (classical interaction).
This point of view leads to a classification too detailed and practically unaccomplishable for the multipartite scenario.
Our treatment is rooted more in statistics,
by noticing that
(i)
pure states of classical systems are always uncorrelated,
so in pure states, correlations are of quantum origin,
and this is what we call entanglement
[
1
]
;
and
(ii)
mixed states of classical systems can always be formed by forgetting about the identity of pure (hence uncorrelated) states,
so in mixed states, correlations which cannot be described in this way are of quantum origin,
and this is what we call entanglement.

These principles are working painlessly in the multipartite scenario,
entangled states and correlation based definitions of entanglement measures
were defined based on these in the first two levels of the structure of multipartite entanglement.
In particular,
from (i) it follows that entanglement in pure states should be measured by correlation
(see (
5b
) and (
10b
), and the corresponding quantities for the permutation invariant case,
and the notes in the end of Section
2.2
).
We emphasize that this statistical point of view is fully compatible with LOCC,
and has led to a much coarser, finite classification.

Being uncertain (or forgetting) about the identity of the state of the system
is a guiding principle in the definition of the different aspects of multipartite entanglement
in Section
2

(see also in point (ii) in Section VII.A. in
[
40
]
).

States in
\mathcal{D}_{L}
:

We are uncertain about the (pure) state, by which the system is described
(Section
2.1
).

States in
\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}
(
\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-sep}}
):

We are uncertain about the pure state, by which the system is described,
but we are certain about the partition with respect to which the state is uncorrelated (separable)
(Section
2.2
).

States in
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-unc}}
(
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}
):

We are uncertain about the pure state, by which the system is described,
and we are also uncertain about the partition with respect to which the state is uncorrelated (separable),
but we are certain about the possible partitions with respect to which the state is uncorrelated (separable)
(Section
2.3
).

States in
\mathcal{C}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{-unc}}
(
\mathcal{C}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{-sep}}
):

We are uncertain about the pure state, by which the system is described,
and we are also uncertain about the partition with respect to which the state is uncorrelated (separable),
but we are certain about the possible partitions with respect to which the state is uncorrelated (separable),
and we are also certain about the possible partitions with respect to which the state is correlated (entangled)
(Section
2.4
).

We also mention here that
for the state sets of given multipartite correlation (entanglement) properties

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-unc}}
(
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{-sep}}
),
except from the fully uncorrelated (or fully separable) case
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\{\bot\}
,
we cannot formulate semigroups of quantum channels
which could play the role of “free operations” for these “free state” sets
in a usual resource-theoretical scenario
[
75
]
.
Instead of this, being uncertain (or forgetting) about the identity of the
maps
is the guiding principle here
(see the characterizations in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
,
as well as in the permutation invariant case in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
).
Note that this is physical: only the fully uncorrelated (fully separable) states are for free,
the others are resource states, possibly of different “value.”

Note that, after the general case (Section
2
),
we considered the
permutation invariant (correlation or entanglement)
properties
(from Section
3
).
The use of these is not restricted to permutation invariant
states
:
these are simply the permutation invariant properties of also non-permutation-invariant states.
If the states considered are permutation invariant
(for example, in the first quantization of bosonic or fermionic systems),
then these are the only relevant properties.

The permutation invariant properties were described by the use of
integer partitions,

for which a refinement-like order was given,
which is just the coarsening of the refinement order of
set partitions.

In the literature,
there are several partial orders constructed for integer partitions,
such as
the
dominance order

[
76
]

(based on majorization, leading to a lattice),
the
Young order

[
62
,
45
]

(based on diagram containment,
given for partitions of all integers, leading to
Young’s lattice
,
here partitions of the same integer cannot be ordered,
and the order is invariant to the conjugation),
or the
reverse lexicographic order

(leading to a chain).
The
refinement order
for integer partitions,
introduced in (
18c
) through the
refinement order
for set partitions (
2c
),
is different from the above orders,
and, to our knowledge
[
62
,
45
]
, was not considered upon its merits in the literature before.
Although Birkhoff mentioned this partial order in his book
[
77
]
as an example, it was not used for any reasonable purpose.
The reason for this might be that the resulting poset shows properties not so nice or powerful as those shown by the others.
However, this order is what needed is in the classification problem of permutation invariant properties,
that is reflected also by the monotonicities (
47
).

k
-partitionability is about the natural gradation
of the lattice of set partitions
P_{\text{I}}
or
of the poset of integer partitions
\hat{P}_{\text{I}}
,
but it was not clear, how
k
-producibility can be understood.
The conjugation of integer partitions has explained the role of the latter,
by establishing a kind of duality between the two properties.
Note, however, that this duality is only partial:
although the conjugation of integer partitions (
49
) interchanges the height and the width (
50
),
by which the
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility properties are given,
but the latter properties are given in an opposite way by height and width, see (
48a
) and (
48b
).
So the conjugation does not interchange
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility,
it establishes a connection on the deeper level of integer partitions only.
This is also reflected in the relations among the different
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility properties,
given in (
59a
) and (
59c
).

For
n\leq 6
, the height and width determine
\hat{\xi}
uniquely.
For
n=7
, we have
\{3,2,2\}
and
\{3,3,1\}
, being of height
3
and width
3
.
Note that two different integer partitions of the same height and width can never be ordered.
(Ordered pairs are of different height,
because of the strict monotonicity of the height (
47
).)

The property of
k
-stretchability (
48c
) was introduced by the rank of the partition.
We note that the
rank
of an integer partition (
46c
) was defined and used originally
in a very different context in number theory and combinatorics.
It was introduced by Freeman Dyson
[
78
]

in his investigations of Ramanujan’s congruences in the partition function
[
74
]
.

For
n
subsystems,
the number of nontrivial
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility properties is
n-1
in both cases
(
1
-partitionability and
n
-producibility are trivial),
while
the number of nontrivial
k
-stretchability properties is
2(n-1)

(
(n-1)
-stretchability is trivial).
All of these three properties form chains, see (
39a
), (
39b
) and (
51
),
and the relations among them are given in (
59
).
Note that
k
-stretchability combines the advantages of
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility,
in the sense that
it rewards large correlated (or entangled) subsystems,
while it punishes the larger number of subsystems uncorrelated (or separable) with one another.
Also,
while the relations between
k
-partitionability and
k
-producibility
are highly nontrivial (
59a
), (
59c
),
the
k
-stretchability properties form a chain (
51
).
The price to pay for this is that
sometimes there are rather different properties not distinguished by stretchability,
for example,
\{3,3\}
and
\{4,1,1\}
are both
1
-stretchable.
Taking stretchability seriously
leads to a highly nontrivial, balanced comparison of permutation invariant multipartite correlation or entanglement properties.

Appendix A
On the structure of the classification of permutation invariant correlations

A.1
Coarsening a poset

Let us have a
finite set

P
endowed with a
binary relation

\preceq
,
and a
function

f
, mapping
P
to
P^{\prime}:=f(P)
.
(In the appendix we consider finite sets only, even if it is not mentioned explicitly.)
The same function transforms also the relation naturally as follows.
A binary relation can be considered as a set of ordered pairs in
P
,
written as
\preceq\;\equiv\{(b,a)\in P\times P\;|\;b\preceq a\}
,
then
\preceq^{\prime}:=(f\times f)(\preceq)=\{(f(b),f(a))\;|\;\forall(b,a)\in\;\preceq\}
by the elementwise action of
f\times f
.
We use the shorthand notation
\preceq^{\prime}=f(\preceq)
for this.
The meaning of this is that

\begin{split}&\forall a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in P^{\prime},\quad b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\\
&\qquad\exists a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime}),\exists b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime})\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;b\preceq a.\end{split}

(62)

(Here
f^{-1}(a^{\prime})=\{a\in P\;|\;f(a)=a^{\prime}\}\subseteq P
denotes the inverse image of the singleton
\{a^{\prime}\}
.)

If the binary relation
\preceq
is a
partial order

[
43
,
45
]
,
the transformed one
\preceq^{\prime}
is not necessarily that.
To make it a partial order, we need some constraints imposed on
f
.
Let us introduce the following three conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in P^{\prime},\;\;\text{if}\;\;b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}\;\;\text{then}\;\;\\
&\qquad\forall a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime}),\;\exists b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime}),\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;b\preceq a;\end{split}

(63a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in P^{\prime},\;\;\text{if}\;\;b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}\;\;\text{then}\;\;\\
&\qquad\forall b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime}),\;\exists a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime}),\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;b\preceq a;\end{split}

(63b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall a,b,c\in P,\;\;\text{if}\;\;c\preceq b\preceq a\\
&\qquad\;\;\text{and}\;\;f(c)=f(a)\;\;\text{then}\;\;f(b)=f(a).\end{split}

(63c)

These conditions, although being rather strong, hold in the construction in which we need them
(see Appendix
A.4
, and Lemma
16
).
They turn out to be sufficient for
\preceq^{\prime}
to be a partial order,
as the following lemma states.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(P,\preceq)
be a poset, then
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})=(f(P),f(\preceq))
is a poset
if (
63a
) and (
63c
) hold, or
if (
63b
) and (
63c
) hold.

Proof.

We need to prove that
\preceq^{\prime}
is a partial order in these cases.

(i)
Reflexivity
(
\forall a^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
,
a^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
):
for all
a^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
we have
f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, since
f
is surjective,
and we need
b\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
and
a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
for which
b\preceq a
by (
62
);
this holds for the choice
b=a
, since the partial order
\preceq
is reflexive,
a\preceq a
.
(This proof does not use any of the constraints (
63
).)

(ii)
Antisymmetry
(
\forall a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
, if
a^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, then
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime}
):
let
b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, which means that there exist
a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
and
b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime})
such that
b\preceq a
, by (
62
).
Fix such a pair
b
and
a
.
Also, let
a^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}b^{\prime}
, applying (
63a
) for
b
, we have that there exists
c\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
, such that
c\preceq b
.
Then, applying (
63c
) to
c\preceq b\preceq a
, we have that
b^{\prime}=f(b)=f(a)=a^{\prime}
.
(This proof uses (
63a
) and (
63c
).
A similar proof can be given by using (
63b
) and (
63c
).)

(iii)
Transitivity
(
\forall a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
, if
c^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}b^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, then
c^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
):
let
b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, which means that there exist
a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
and
b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime})
such that
b\preceq a
, by (
62
).
Fix such a pair
b
and
a
.
Also, let
c^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}b^{\prime}
, applying (
63a
) for
b
, we have that there exists
c\in f^{-1}(c^{\prime})
, such that
c\preceq b
.
Then, since the partial order
\preceq
is transitive, we have that
c\preceq a
for an
a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
and
c\in f^{-1}(c^{\prime})
,
which means that
c^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
by (
62
).
(This proof uses (
63a
).
A similar proof can be given by using (
63b
).)
∎

The following construction would be much simpler if
the conditions (
63
) would also be necessary.
Note that this is not the case:
if
\preceq^{\prime}
is a partial order, then (
63c
) holds,
but (
63a
) and (
63b
) do not hold.
For example, for the poset
P=\{a_{1},a_{2},b\}
with the only arrow
b\prec a_{1}
,
and the function given as
f(a_{1})=f(a_{2})=a^{\prime}\neq f(b)=b^{\prime}
, we have
b^{\prime}\prec^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, but (
63a
) does not hold.

If (
63a
) or (
63b
), and (
63c
) hold, then
the new poset
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
can be considered as a coarsening of
(P,\preceq)
by
f
.
(
f:P\to P^{\prime}
is surjective by definition.
If it is also injective, then
(P,\preceq)
and
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
are isomorphic,
and the coarsening is trivial.)
Note that, because of the construction,
f:P\to P^{\prime}
is automatically monotone,

b\preceq a\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;f(b)\preceq^{\prime}f(a).

(64)

(Indeed,
a\in f^{-1}(f(a))
and
b\in f^{-1}(f(b))
,
so (
62
) holds.)
Note also that,
if the poset
(P,\preceq)
is a lattice,
the transformed one
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
is not necessarily that.
To make it a lattice, further conditions have to be imposed; this problem is not addressed here.

A.2
Down-sets and up-sets

In a poset
P
,
a
down-set
(
order ideal
) is a subset
\mathbf{a}\subseteq P
, which is closed downwards
[
43
,
45
]
,

\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}(P)\;\;\overset{\text{def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\;\forall a\in\mathbf{a},\;\forall b\preceq a:\;b\in\mathbf{a};

(65a)

while an
up-set
(
order filter
) is a subset
\mathbf{a}\subseteq P
, which is closed upwards
[
43
,
45
]
,

\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{O}_{\uparrow}(P)\;\;\overset{\text{def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\;\forall a\in\mathbf{a},\;\forall b\succeq a:\;b\in\mathbf{a}.

(65b)

These form lattices with respect to the inclusion
\subseteq
,
with the join
\vee
(least upper bound), being the union
\cup
,
and the meet
\wedge
(greatest lower bound), being the intersection
\cap
.

As in the previous section,
let us have the poset
(P,\preceq)
,
the function
f
,
by which
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime}):=(f(P),f(\preceq))
,
and
for which (
63a
) or (
63b
), and (
63c
) hold.
Additionally, let
P
have
bottom
and
top elements
.
(Then also
P^{\prime}
has
bottom
and
top elements
.
Indeed, this is because the bottom and top elements in
P
are mapped
to the bottom and top elements in
P^{\prime}
, because of the (
64
) monotonicity of
f
.)
Now let us form from the posets
(P,\preceq)
and
(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})

the lattices of nonempty down-sets

\displaystyle(Q,\sqsubseteq)

\displaystyle:=(\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}(P)\setminus\{\emptyset\},\subseteq),

(66a)

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=(\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}(P^{\prime})\setminus\{\emptyset\},\subseteq).

(66b)

(The existence of the bottom elements in
P
and
P^{\prime}

ensures that not only the down-sets but also the nonempty down-sets form lattices in both cases
[
40
]
.)
Then, by denoting the elementwise action of
f
with
g:2^{P}\to 2^{P^{\prime}}
,
that is,
g(\mathbf{a})=\{f(a)\;|\;a\in\mathbf{a}\}
,
in the following lemmas we will show
that
(Q^{\prime},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})=(g(Q),g(\sqsubseteq))
,
that is, the diagram

\textstyle{(Q,\sqsubseteq)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{(Q^{\prime},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})}
\textstyle{(P,\preceq)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}\setminus\{\emptyset\}}
\textstyle{(P^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}\setminus\{\emptyset\}}

(67)

commutes.
Here
g(\sqsubseteq)
is the partial order transformed by
g
in the same way as
\preceq
was transformed by
f
in (
62
),
and we also have automatically that
g
is monotone for
\sqsubseteq
and
g(\sqsubseteq)
, in the same way as in (
64
).

The following two technical lemmas concerning the sets
\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\subseteq P

will be used several times later.

Lemma 2
.

In the above setting
(and assuming (
63
)),
for all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
, we have
g\bigl(\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\bigr)=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

This is because
g
is the elementwise action of
f
, so

g\bigl(\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\bigr)=g\bigl(\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}\{a\in P\;|\;f(a)=a^{\prime}\}\bigr)=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}g\bigl(\{a\in P\;|\;f(a)=a^{\prime}\}\bigr)=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}\{a^{\prime}\}=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

In the above setting
(and assuming (
63
)),
for all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
, we have
\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\in Q
,
that is, it is a nonempty down-set.

Proof.

First, denote
\mathbf{a}:=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
.
On the one hand,
\mathbf{a}\neq\emptyset
, since
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
f
is surjective.
On the other hand,
if
b\preceq a
for an
a\in\mathbf{a}
,
then
f(b)\preceq^{\prime}f(a)
because of the monotonicity (
64
).
Since
f(a)\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
by the construction of
\mathbf{a}
,
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
is a down-set (
65a
),
we have that
f(b)\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.
Then
b\in f^{-1}(f(b))\subseteq\mathbf{a}
by the construction of

\mathbf{a}=f^{-1}(f(b))\cup\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime},a^{\prime}\neq f(b)}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
,
and then
b\in\mathbf{a}
, so
\mathbf{a}
is a down-set.
Altogether we have that
\mathbf{a}\in Q
.
∎

The following two lemmas show (
67
).

Lemma 4
.

In the above setting
(and assuming (
63
)),
we have
g(Q)=Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We need to prove both inclusions.

(i) We need that
g(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathbf{a}\in Q
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}:=g(\mathbf{a})
.
Then for all
a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
let us have
a\in f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\cap\mathbf{a}
.
(Note that
f^{-1}(a^{\prime})\cap\mathbf{a}\neq\emptyset
by construction.)
Then let
b^{\prime}\preceq^{\prime}a^{\prime}
,
and applying (
63a
) for
a
, we have that there exists
b\in f^{-1}(b^{\prime})
such that
b\preceq a
.
Since
a\in\mathbf{a}
, and
\mathbf{a}
is a down-set (
65a
), we have that
b\in\mathbf{a}
,
and then
b^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
, so
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
is a down-set.

(ii) We also need that
g(Q)\supseteq Q^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
, then we construct an
\mathbf{a}\in Q
, for which
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=g(\mathbf{a})
.
The element
\mathbf{a}:=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
fulfills these criteria,
we have
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=g(\mathbf{a})
by Lemma
2
,
and
\mathbf{a}\in Q
by Lemma
3
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

In the above setting
(and assuming (
63
)),
we have
g(\sqsubseteq)=\;\sqsubseteq^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We need to prove both directions.

(i) We need that for all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
,
if
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}g(\sqsubseteq)\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
then
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.

\mathbf{b}^{\prime}g(\sqsubseteq)\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
means that

\exists\mathbf{b}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
and

\exists\mathbf{a}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
such that
\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a}
.
(Again,
g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})=\{\mathbf{a}\in Q\;|\;g(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\}
is the inverse image of the singleton
\{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\}
.)
In this case,

\mathbf{b}^{\prime}=g(\mathbf{b})=\{f(b)\;|\;b\in\mathbf{b}\}\subseteq\{f(a)\;|\;a\in\mathbf{a}\}=g(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
,
so
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.

(ii) We also need that for all
\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
,
if
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
then
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}g(\sqsubseteq)\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.
From
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
,
let us form
\mathbf{b}:=\bigcup_{b^{\prime}\in\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(b^{\prime})

and
\mathbf{a}:=\bigcup_{a^{\prime}\in\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(a^{\prime})
.
We have that

\mathbf{b}\in Q
and
\mathbf{a}\in Q

(they are nonempty down-sets) by Lemma
3
,
also
g(\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
and
g(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{a}^{\prime}

by Lemma
2
,
and if
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
then
\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a}
by construction.
That is, we have constructed

\mathbf{b}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
and

\mathbf{a}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
, for which
\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a}
,
so we have
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}g(\sqsubseteq)\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
.
∎

Summing up,
Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
together state that (
67
) commutes.
Later we also need that the properties (
63a
), (
63b
) and (
63c
) are inherited.

Lemma 6
.

In the above setting
(and assuming (
63
)),
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime},\;\;\text{if}\;\;\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\;\;\text{then}\;\;\\
&\qquad\forall\mathbf{a}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\;\exists\mathbf{b}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a};\end{split}

(68a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime},\;\;\text{if}\;\;\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\;\;\text{then}\;\;\\
&\qquad\forall\mathbf{b}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\;\exists\mathbf{a}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a};\end{split}

(68b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\forall\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in Q,\;\;\text{if}\;\;\mathbf{c}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a}\\
&\qquad\;\;\text{and}\;\;g(\mathbf{c})=g(\mathbf{a}),\;\;\text{then}\;\;g(\mathbf{b})=g(\mathbf{a}).\end{split}

(68c)

Proof.

(
68a
) can be proven by the explicit construction of
\mathbf{b}
.
Let
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
, and
\mathbf{a}\in g^{-1}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
,
then
\mathbf{b}:=\bigl(\bigcup_{b^{\prime}\in\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(b^{\prime})\bigr)\cap\mathbf{a}
.
By construction,
\mathbf{b}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{a}
.
Also,
\mathbf{b}\in Q
, that is, it is a nonempty down-set, since
it is the intersection (meet) of two nonempty down-sets:

\mathbf{a}\in Q
, and

\bigcup_{b^{\prime}\in\mathbf{b}^{\prime}}f^{-1}(b^{\prime})\in Q
by Lemma
3
,
and
Q
is a lattice.
In addition, we need that

B.1
Monotonicity of correlation measures

Here we show
the
\xi
-LO monotonicity of the
\xi
-correlation (
5a
), from which
the LO monotonicity of the
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-correlation (
10a
) follows,
because the latter one is a minimum of some of the former ones.
A quantum channel (completely positive trace preserving map
[
6
,
7
]
)
\Phi
is
\xi
-LO,
if it can be written as
\Phi=\bigotimes_{X\in\xi}\Phi_{X}
with quantum channels

\Phi_{X}:\mathcal{D}_{X}\to\mathcal{D}_{X}
for all
X\in\xi
.
With this,

\begin{split}C_{\xi}\bigl(\Phi(\varrho)\bigr)&\overset{\eqref{eq:CI}}{{\;\;=\;\;}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}}D\bigl(\Phi(\varrho)\big\|\sigma\bigr)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:free}}{{\;\;\leq\;\;}}\min_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\Phi(\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}})}D\bigl(\Phi(\varrho)\big\|\sigma^{\prime}\bigr)\\
&{\;\;=\;\;}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}}D\bigl(\Phi(\varrho)\big\|\Phi(\sigma)\bigr)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:contr}}{{\;\;\leq\;\;}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}}}D\bigl(\varrho\big\|\sigma\bigr)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:CI}}{{\;\;=\;\;}}C_{\xi}(\varrho),\end{split}

where the first inequality comes from

\Phi(\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}})\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\xi\text{-unc}},

(75)

which holds for
\xi
-LOs;
and the second inequality comes from the monotonicity of the relative entropy (
1b
),

D\bigl(\Phi(\varrho)\big\|\Phi(\sigma)\bigr)\leq D(\varrho\|\sigma),

(76)

which holds for all quantum channels
[
6
,
7
]
.

Note that the monotonicity shown here is a particular case of a much more general property
of monotone distance based
geometric measures

[
65
]
in resource theories
[
75
]
:
the monotonicity with respect to free maps, by which the set of free states is mapped onto itself.
