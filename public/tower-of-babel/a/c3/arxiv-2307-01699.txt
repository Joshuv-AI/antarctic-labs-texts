1.
Introduction

In recent years, there has been a lot of efforts devoted to entanglement entropy (EE).
The motivation of the present work is to understand the transition between a (strict) area law and an enhanced area law for the EE of fermionic ground states.

Even in the simplest situation when there are no particle interactions present, the entanglement (or rather local) entropy of ground states is a complicated and interesting function of the defining parameters. Also, the asymptotic behaviour (for large domains) of this entropy is related to the asymptotic behaviour of Szegő-type asymptotics of Toeplitz or Wiener–Hopf operators which has been studied since more than a century. It was Harold Widom who conjectured in 1990 (see
[
23
]
) a formula in the higher-dimensional setting and proved a special case. That conjecture led D. Gioev and I. Klich
[
3
]
to conjecture in 2006 the asymptotic expansion of the EE of ground states of the ideal Fermi gas. In 2013, A.V. Sobolev
[
17
]
proved Widom’s conjecture which in turn paved the way to prove the conjecture on the EE by Leschke, Sobolev and one of the present authors in
[
6
]
.

In 2021, the same authors proved the area law for the EE of ground states of the ideal Fermi gas in a constant magnetic field in the two-dimensional case, see
[
8
]
. In the three-dimensional case the situation is different from the start since the spectrum of the Landau Laplacian is now purely absolutely continuous. We proved a logarithmically enhanced area-law recently in
[
15
]
.

Some connections between the appearance of a strict area-law versus a logarithmically enhanced area-law are obvious. For example, if the off-diagonal integral kernel of the Fermi projection (characterizing the ground state) is decaying fast (exponentially, say) then an area law holds. On the other hand, purely absolutely continuous spectrum does not guarantee a logarithmically enhanced area-law.

Let us recall some more mathematical results that add to the understanding of EE of non-interacting Fermi gases. In
[
16
]
, Pfirsch and Sobolev treat a periodic (electric) potential
V
in dimension one and prove a logarithmically enhanced area-law. What is particularly interesting is that the second-order term (or “surface” term of the order
\ln(L)
) is the same as for the Laplacian, that is, with
V=0
. The higher dimensional case remains an open problem.

Stability of the enhanced area-law by a local (compactly supported) perturbation
V
was proved by Müller and Schulte in
[
12
,
10
]
. Motivated by these papers, the first named author of the present paper proved the stability of the area law for the two-dimensional Landau Hamiltonian by allowing a perturbation on the magnetic potential and a perturbation by an electric potential, see
[
14
]
.

There are also results on the EE of random systems described by an Anderson-type Hamiltonian. They concern the surprising logarithmic enhancement of EE in the one-dimensional dimer model at a certain Fermi energy proved by Müller, Pastur and Schulte
[
11
]
. In a more general case, Pastur and Slavin
[
13
]
and Elgart, Pastur and Shcherbina
[
2
]
proved an area law for the EE at a Fermi energy in the localisation regime for an Anderson model on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. However, their formula for the leading coefficient is not very explicit and it is not known how it depends on the disorder parameter. No rigorous result is available when the Fermi energy lies in the delocalisation regime of a random Hamiltonian, but this question touches on the notoriously difficult problem of the existence of such a regime in the first place.

We continue here the study of the local or entanglement entropy of ground states of the ideal Fermi gas in a constant magnetic field in dimension two. To this end, we fix some Fermi energy
\mu>0
and denote by
P_{B}\coloneqq 1(H_{B}\leq\mu)
the spectral (or Fermi) projection of the Landau Hamiltonian

(1.1)

H_{B}\coloneqq(-i\nabla-a)^{2}\,,

where
i
is the imaginary unit and
\nabla=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}})
is the gradient. We choose the symmetric gauge
a(x)=(a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x))\coloneqq(x_{2},-x_{1})B/2
for the vector potential
a:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
generating the constant magnetic field (vector) perpendicular to the plane with Cartesian coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
. The strength of magnetic field is given by the real number
B>0
. On (a suitable domain of)
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
H_{B}
acts as a (positive) self-adjoint operator.

For some (bounded) Borel set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with Lebesgue volume
|\Lambda|
, we consider the localised Fermi projection

(1.2)

P_{B}(\Lambda)\coloneqq 1_{\Lambda}\,P_{B}\,1_{\Lambda}\,,

where
1_{\Lambda}
is the multiplication operator with the indicator function of
\Lambda
. For our asymptotic results we assume in addition that
\Lambda
is an open domain (that is,
\Lambda
has only finitely many connected components) with some “smoothness” properties of the boundary,
\partial\Lambda
. The latter may be piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth in our first main result, piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth in our second main result and a polygon or
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth in our third main result.

For some (suitable) “test” function
f
we are then interested in the Hilbert-space trace
\mathrm{tr}f(P_{B}(\Lambda))
. The most relevant cases are the quadratic polynomial
f(t)=t(1-t)
related to particle number fluctuations and
f(t)=-t\ln(t)-(1-t)\ln(1-t)
related to the von Neumann EE. For fixed
\Lambda
, this trace is too complicated but it is of interest to study the behaviour for large domains. To this end, we introduce a scaling parameter
L>0
and consider, for fixed
\Lambda
and fixed
\mu
, the function
(L,B)\mapsto\mathrm{tr}f(P_{B}(L\Lambda))
as we let
L\to\infty
. This has been completely analysed in
[
8
]
for fixed
B
, namely, the following area law has been proved (under the condition that
\partial\Lambda
is
\mathsf{C}^{3}
-smooth),

(1.3)

\mathrm{tr}f(P_{B}(L\Lambda))=L^{2}B\frac{|\Lambda|}{2\pi}(n+1)f(1)+L\sqrt{B}\,|\partial\Lambda|\,\mathsf{M}_{\leq n}(f)+o(L)\,,

see
[
8
, Theorem 2]
. Here,
n\coloneqq\lfloor(\mu/B-1)/2\rfloor
is the number of Landau levels below
\mu
and the coefficient
\mathsf{M}_{\leq n}(f)
is defined in (
8.3
). Hence, if
f(1)=0
, then the leading contribution as
L\to\infty
is of the order
L|\partial\Lambda|
, which is the reason why it is called an area law. We speak of an enhanced area-law if the leading term is larger. The most prominent example is when there is an extra factor of
\ln(L)
. Such a logarithmically enhanced area-law is present for the (free) Laplacian (set
B=0
in the above Hamiltonian
H_{B}
), as was conjectured by Gioev and Klich in
[
3
]
and proved in
[
6
]
.

The concrete purpose of the present paper is to study the transition from an area law to a logarithmically enhanced area-law as
B
vanishes and the number of Landau levels
n
tends to infinity. The results may also be interpreted as a high energy limit where the magnetic strength
B
is kept fixed and the Fermi energy (or the number of Landau levels) and the scaling parameter
L
tend to infinity.

We cannot use the above result (
1.3
) from the constant
B
case directly as we have no control over lower order error terms, which depend, in general, on
n
and might, a priori, blow up as
n\to\infty
. The joint limit
B\to 0
and
L\to\infty
(for fixed
\mu
) depends crucially on the product
BL
. We venture to state the following conjecture, which we will prove in certain relevant circumstances, in particular only for polynomials
f
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

For any Hölder-continuous function
f
with Hölder exponent strictly bigger than
0
, which satisfies
f(0)=f(1)=0
, any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Lambda
, and any
\mu>0
we have the asymptotic expansion

(1.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}f(1_{L\Lambda}1(H_{B}\leq\mu)1_{L\Lambda})=\begin{cases}\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert\,\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}\,\mathsf{I}(f)L\ln(\sqrt{\mu}L)+o(\sqrt{\mu}L\ln(\sqrt{\mu}L))\,&\text{ if }BL<\sqrt{\mu}\,,\\
\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert\,\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}\,\mathsf{I}(f)L\ln(\mu/B)+o(\sqrt{\mu}L\ln(\mu/B))\,&\text{ if }BL\geq\sqrt{\mu}\,,\end{cases}

as
L\to\infty
and
B\to 0
, where we defined the functional

(1.5)

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathsf{I}(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(t)}{t(1-t)}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

In order to explain the numerical factors in this formula we recall the asymptotic formula for the Laplacian in two dimensions. As mentioned, it was proved that for a Hölder-continuous function
f
with
f(0)=f(1)=0
(using the notation of
[
6
, (7)]
)

(1.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}f(1_{L\Lambda}1(-\Delta\leq\mu)1_{L\Lambda})=J(\partial\Gamma,\partial\Lambda)\,\mathsf{I}(f)\sqrt{\mu}L\ln(\sqrt{\mu}L)+o(\sqrt{\mu}L\ln(\sqrt{\mu}L))\,,

as
L\to\infty
, where
\Gamma\coloneqq\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:p^{2}\leq\mu\}
,
\hbar=1
, and

(1.7)

\displaystyle J(\partial\Gamma,\partial\Lambda)=\frac{2}{(\frac{1}{2})!}\left(\frac{\mu}{4\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}|\partial\Lambda|=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\pi}\,|\partial\Lambda|\,,

with
(1/2)!\coloneqq\sqrt{\pi}/2
being defined by Euler’s gamma function.

This transition of area laws is similar in spirit to the transition that happens for the free Laplacian as one lets the temperature
T
go to zero in the study of the EE of equilibrium states. As proved by A.V. Sobolev
[
20
]
in arbitrary spatial dimension and prior by Leschke, Sobolev and one of the present authors in
[
7
]
in dimension one, there are two regions for
(T,L)
: if
TL\to 0
, then there is an enhanced area-law of the order
L^{d-1}\ln(L)
. On the other hand, if
TL\to\infty
, then we have an enhanced area-law of the order
L^{d-1}\ln(T_{0}/T)
, for some temperature
T_{0}>0
. To draw the connection between the two scenario one could identify
T
with
B
.

However, from a technical point of view, the two cases differ in the sense that there is a full-fledged pseudo-differential calculus for the study of the Laplacian, or more general, of translation-invariant operators. This is not so much the case for the magnetic Laplacian
H_{B}
but we can rely on well-established (asymptotic) properties of Hermite and Laguerre polynomials.

At the end of this section, we will argue by scaling that it suffices to consider
\mu=2
, only. Moreover, we find it more convenient to switch from
B
to
\mu/(2B)\approx K\in\mathbb{N}
and hence let
K\to\infty
. Our main results in this paper are therefore formulated for
\mu=2
and in the joint limit
L\to\infty,K\to\infty
.

Our first main result is
Theorem 3.1
which deals with particle number fluctuations (that is, the function
f(t)=t(1-t)
) and the full parameter set of
K
and
L
. We can handle the quadratic test function
f
because the phase
\exp(ix\wedge y/(2K))
, which appears in the integral kernel of
P_{B}
, cancels in the computation of the trace and is out of our way. Nevertheless, it is an important case and we believe that is yields the right picture in the general case. Therefore, we venture to state
1.1
. We discuss the quadratic case in Section
3
.

Our second main result
Theorem 4.1
is the logarithmically enhanced area-law of the order
L\ln(L)
if
K
is much larger than
L
and the Landau Hamiltonian
H_{B}
is “close” to the (free) two-dimensional Laplacian. Of course, for any finite
K
(or strictly positive
B
), the spectrum of
H_{B}
is never anything like that of the Laplacian and the off-diagonal integral kernel
P_{B}(x,y)
decays exponentially to 0 as the distance
\|x-y\|
tends to infinity. But the rate is given by
1/K
and that goes to 0 in the end and we do get the convergence to the integral kernel of
1(-\Delta\leq\mu)
. More precisely, we are able to prove the enhanced area-law under the condition
L\leq CK^{2/5}
but, as just said, we believe that this holds true up to the transition line
K=CL
. We should note that we use (but do not reprove) the known result for the Laplacian (
1/K=0
in a way) and consider the case with small
1/K
as a perturbation.

Our third main result is
Theorem 5.2
and deals with the region
K\ll L
. Here, due to the slow vanishing of the magnetic field, we are more in the regime of a constant magnetic field and the area law is of the order
L\ln(K)
. Interestingly, the
\ln(K)
is a result of an enhanced area-law for the one-dimensional Laplacian where
K
is the effective scaling parameter. Distilling the one-dimensional Laplacian is the result of the so-called sine-kernel asymptotics for Hermite and Laguerre functions. The difficulty here is that we need this asymptotics on a global scale, which takes up some space to prove. We succeed to prove
1.1
(almost) over the full range of parameters
K\leq CL
when the domain
\Lambda
is a polygon; in fact, we have to assume
K\leq CL/\ln(L)
. When
\partial\Lambda
is
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth we lose control over some error terms and end up with the restriction
K^{2}\leq CL
.

We return to some open questions in
Appendix D
.

1.1.
Some notations and preliminary definitions

A
domain

\Lambda
is a (non-empty) bounded, open set in the two-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with finitely many connected components. It is called
\mathsf{C}^{r}
-smooth or piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{r}
-smooth if the boundary
\partial\Lambda=\bar{\Lambda}\setminus\Lambda
is a
\mathsf{C}^{r}
-smooth curve, respectively a piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{r}
-smooth curve, for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
\Lambda
is called Lipschitz if the boundary is Lipschitz continuous. The surface area
|\partial\Lambda|
is the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\partial\Lambda
.

By
D_{R}(x)
we denote the open disk of radius
R>0
at the centre
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and by
D_{R}(S)\coloneqq\bigcup_{x\in S}D_{R}(x)
the
R
-neighbourhood of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We denote the set of natural numbers by
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
the set including
0
.

The parameter
L
is a positive real number which scales the domain
\Lambda
and goes to infinity in our asymptotic results. The parameter
K
is another positive (in most cases natural) number and the inverse of the magnetic field strength, which also tends to infinity (in most statements). It determines the Fermi projection
P_{K}
, see (
1.9
).

The indicator function of a set
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
1_{I}
and our notation does not distinguish between this function and the multiplication operator by this function. The identity operator is denoted by
\mathds{1}
.

We use the standard big-
\mathcal{O}
and little-
o
notation. That is, for two functions
f
and
g>0
,
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
if
|f(L)|\leq Cg(L)
for some (finite) constant
C
and sufficiently large
L
and
f=o(g)
if
\limsup|f|/g(L)=0
. In the latter case, we also write
f\ll g
or
g\gg f
. In a series of estimates the specific value of a constant may change from line to line without changing its name. Constants are always finite real numbers and usually strictly positive.

1.2.
Reduction to the case
\mu=2
, introduction of
K
and
L

As is well-known, the spectrum of the Landau Hamiltonian
H_{B}
of (
1.1
) equals
B(2\mathbb{N}_{0}+1)
. Each eigenvalue is infinitely degenerate. Let
\Pi_{\ell,B}
be the projection onto the eigenspace with eigenvalue
B(2\ell+1)
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For some fixed (Fermi energy)
\mu>0
we work with the spectral projection
1(H_{B}\leq\mu)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\nu}\Pi_{\ell,B}
with
\nu\coloneqq\lfloor(\mu/B-1)/2\rfloor
, also called Fermi projection.

The expressions we are interested in are only dependent on the eigenvalues of the operator
1_{\Lambda}1(H_{B}\leq\mu)1_{\Lambda}
for some domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, this operator is unitarily equivalent to
1_{\lambda^{-1}\Lambda}1(H_{\lambda^{2}B}\leq\lambda^{2}\mu)1_{\lambda^{-1}\Lambda}
. We define
K\coloneqq\lfloor(\mu/B-1)/2\rfloor+1
.
We can then assume without loss of generality that
B=1/K
, as both sides of (
1.4
) are invariant under this scaling. For the rescaled
\mu^{\prime}
we observe
K-1=\lfloor(\mu^{\prime}K-1)/2\rfloor=\lfloor(2K-1)/2\rfloor
and this implies
1(H_{1/K}\leq\mu^{\prime})=1(H_{1/K}\leq 2)
. This shows
\mu^{\prime}=2+\mathcal{O}(1/K)
and thus, replacing
\mu^{\prime}
by
2
on the right-hand side of (
1.4
) only changes the leading term of the right-hand side by an additive error term of order
L\ln(\min(L,K))/K=\mathcal{O}(L)
. This is why, in the following, we always assume

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mu=2\quad\mbox{ and }\quad B=1/K\;\mbox{ for }\;K\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Finally, we redefine the projection

(1.9)

P_{K}\coloneqq 1(H_{B}\leq 2)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}\Pi_{\ell,1/K}\,.

2.
Preliminary asymptotic results on the integral kernel of the Fermi projection

This section starts with the integral kernel of the Fermi projector
P_{K}
and collects various estimates on this projector, which are needed throughout the paper. Most of them are probably well-known and we list them here for completeness and the convenience of the reader. We do not claim any novelty.

We introduce the function
F_{K}^{(\alpha)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, which is related to the (generalised) Laguerre polynomial of degree
K-1
. Then we study its asymptotic properties as
K
becomes large. This is split into two subsections, one is devoted to small arguments
x
, that is, to
x\leq 1/2
and the other one to large arguments, that is, to
x\geq 1/2
. The main results on the asymptotic expansion of the translation invariant part,
G_{K}
, of the integral kernel of

are collected in
Theorem 2.4
and
2.11
. The last subsection contains an integral bound on
G_{K}
, which is of immediate use in the next section on particle number fluctuations.

In this section, we study the integral kernel of the Fermi projection and in particular, how it behaves asymptotically for small magnetic fields. We will see in which specific way it converges to the free projector. This kernel is given for both
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and

in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle P_{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1(H_{1/K}\leq 2)(x,y)

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4K}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+i\frac{1}{2K}x\wedge y\right)\mathcal{L}_{K-1}^{(1)}\left(\frac{1}{2K}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\frac{1}{2K}x\wedge y\right)G_{K}(\lVert x-y\rVert/\sqrt{8})\,,

where
x\wedge y\coloneqq x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{K-1}^{(\alpha)}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}{K-1+\alpha\choose K-1-j}t^{j}\,,\quad t\geq 0

(for any

), see

and

(2.4)

\displaystyle G_{K}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi K}\exp\left(-4K\frac{t^{2}}{2K^{2}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{K-1}^{(1)}\left(4K\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right),\quad t\geq 0\,.

We need to be a bit more general and define for

and

the function

(2.5)

\displaystyle F_{K}^{(\alpha)}(x)\coloneqq 2^{\alpha}\sqrt{\nu}x^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}}\lvert 1-x\rvert^{\frac{1}{4}}\exp(-\nu x/2)\mathcal{L}_{K-1}^{(\alpha)}(\nu x)\,,\quad x>0\,.

\displaystyle\eta(t)=\begin{cases}1/2\left(t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+\arcsin(t)\right),\quad&\text{ if }-1\leq t\leq 1\,,\\

\displaystyle\eta(\sqrt{t})=\xi(t)\quad\mbox{for any }t\in[0,1]\mbox{ and }\eta(1)=\pi/4\,.

\displaystyle\zeta(s)\coloneqq-\left[3/2(\pi/4-\eta(\sqrt{s}))\right]^{2/3}\,,\quad s\geq 0\,.

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=-\left[3/2(-i)\,\mathfrak{Im}(\eta(\sqrt{s}))\right]^{2/3}=\left[3/2\,\mathfrak{Im}(\eta(\sqrt{s}))\right]^{2/3}\,,

where

refers to the imaginary part.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
h\in(-1,1)
, the function
\eta
satisfies the following properties:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\eta(h)

\displaystyle=h+\mathcal{O}(h^{3})\,,

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\eta(h)|

\displaystyle\geq(\pi/4)|h|\,,

(2.17)

\displaystyle 3/2\big(\pi/4-\eta(1-h)\big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\Big(\sqrt{h}^{3}-\frac{3}{20}\sqrt{h}^{5}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lvert h\rvert}^{7})\Big)\,,

where
\sqrt{h}=i\sqrt{-h}
for
h\leq 0
. Furthermore,
\eta^{-1}
is
2/3
-Hölder continuous on
\eta([-1,2])
.

Proof.

The expansion for
\eta(h)
is trivial. The lower bound for
\eta(h)/h
holds for positive

, as
\eta
is concave on
[0,1]
and thus, the line from
(0,0)
to
(1,\pi/4)
lies below the graph of
\eta
. For negative

, the claim follows as
\eta(-h)=-\eta(h)
.

The expansion for
\eta(1-h)
follows from
\eta(1)=\pi/4
and

(2.18)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(1-h)=\sqrt{1-(1-h)^{2}}=\sqrt{h(2-h)}=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{h}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\sqrt{h}^{3}+\mathcal{O}(|h|^{5/2})\,,

where we choose the branch of the square root, which satisfies
\sqrt{-t}=i\sqrt{t}
for any

. Integrating this equation leads to

(2.19)

\displaystyle 3/2\big(\pi/4-\eta(1-h)\big)=\sqrt{2}\Big(\sqrt{h}^{3}-\frac{3}{20}\sqrt{h}^{5}+\mathcal{O}(|h|^{7/2})\Big)\,.

The inverse

is locally Lipschitz continuous away from

, as

only vanishes at

. From the expansion of

is

-Hölder continuous on
\eta([-1,2])
.
∎

We start with an integral estimate, which will help to calculate some Hilbert–Schmidt norms.

Lemma 2.12

Combining

3.
On the particle number fluctuations

This section is devoted to the study of the asymptotic expansion of the trace of
f(P_{K}(L\Lambda))
when

is a quadratic function. Since we assume
f(0)=f(1)=0
, we may restrict to
f(t)=t(1-t)
. It is related to the fluctuations of the local particle number in the ground state and hence of physical interest.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth domain. Then, with the above test function
f(t)=t(1-t)
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}f(P_{K}(L\Lambda))=\operatorname{tr}\left(1_{L\Lambda}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi^{3}}\begin{cases}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert\ln(K)+\mathcal{O}(L)\quad&\text{ if }K<L\,,\\
L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert\ln(L)+\mathcal{O}(L)\quad&\text{ if }K>L\,.\end{cases}

Remark
.

In line with our
1.1
the value
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi^{3}}
equals
\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\mathsf{I}(t\mapsto t(1-t))
.

Proof.

The first trace identity is rather obvious, see the short proof following (
5.2
). As we are then just calculating the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
1_{L\Lambda}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}
, we have (see (
2.3
))

(3.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(1_{L\Lambda}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{d}x\int_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}y\,\lvert G_{K}(\|x-y\|/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)\,.

The last step relies on changing to polar coordinates in

and Fubini, where

(3.4)

\displaystyle F(s)\coloneqq s\int_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}(x+s(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)))\,.

Trivially, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle F(s)\leq s\int_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{d}x\,2\pi=2\pi sL^{2}\lvert\Lambda\rvert=\mathcal{O}(sL^{2})\,.

As
\partial\Lambda
is piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth, we have the expansion

(3.6)

\displaystyle F(s)=2s^{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert+\mathcal{O}(s^{3})\,.

See
Appendix C
for a proof. Thus, for any
s>0
, we observe

(3.7)

\displaystyle F(s)\leq C\min\big\{2sL^{2},s^{2}(L+s)\big\}=\begin{cases}2CsL^{2}&\text{ if }s>L\,,\\
Cs^{2}(L+s)\leq 2Cs^{2}L&\text{ if }s\leq L\,,\end{cases}

or equivalently,

(3.8)

\displaystyle F(s)\leq CsL\min\{s,L\}\,.

We want to replace the integral in (
3.3
) over
\mathbb{R}^{+}
by an integral over
(\sqrt{8},\min\{K,L\})
. Let us consider the resulting error terms. The first one is trivial, the second and third one rely upon

.

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}s\,s^{2}L\leq CL\,,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle=C\int_{L/\sqrt{8}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\lvert G_{K}(t)\rvert^{2}L^{2}t\leq CL\,,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\int_{K}^{10K}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle\leq CK\int_{K/\sqrt{8}}^{10K/\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}t\,\lvert G_{K}(t)\rvert^{2}Lt\leq CL\,,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{10K}^{L}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{10K/\sqrt{8}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\exp(-\beta t)t^{2}L\leq CL\exp(-\beta K)\,.

Thus, as the integrand is always positive, we get

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\sqrt{8}}^{\min\{K,L\}}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}\left(2s^{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert+\mathcal{O}(s^{3})\right)+\mathcal{O}(L)

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{8}\int_{1}^{\min\{K,L\}/\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}t\,\lvert G_{K}(t)\rvert^{2}\left(16t^{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})\right)+\mathcal{O}(L)\,.

Next, we utilize
Theorem 2.4
. As
1<t<K/\sqrt{8}
the singularities of the denominator are outside the integration domain. As the singularity around

is substantially away from the domain, we can easily bound that factor. Thus, we observe

\displaystyle\lvert G_{K}(t)\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{\cos(\omega_{K}(t)-3\pi/4)}{4\sqrt{2}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}t^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi^{3}t^{3}}\left(\cos(\omega_{K}(t)-3\pi/4)\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(\omega_{K}(t)-3\pi/4)^{2}}{32\pi^{3}t^{3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{tK^{2}}+\frac{1}{t^{4}}\right).

We estimate the next batch of error terms of (

) and (

Thus, we have shown

(3.20)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{8}\int_{1}^{\min\{K,L\}/\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}t\,\frac{\cos(\omega_{K}(t)-3\pi/4)^{2}}{32\pi^{3}t^{3}}16t^{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert+\mathcal{O}(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert}{\pi^{3}}\int_{1}^{\min\{K,L\}/\sqrt{8}}\mathrm{d}t\,\frac{\cos(\omega_{K}(t)-3\pi/4)^{2}}{t}+\mathcal{O}(L)

is invertible on the range

and need to estimate the inverse function

and the differential
\omega_{K}^{\prime}

\displaystyle 4t\geq\omega_{K}(t)\geq 4\sqrt{7/8}\,t\,.

Thus, we conclude

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,

\displaystyle\lvert G_{K}(s/\sqrt{8})\rvert^{2}F(s)

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert}{\pi^{3}}\int_{\omega_{K}(1)}^{\omega_{K}(\min\{K,L\}/\sqrt{8})}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\cos(s-3\pi/4)^{2}}{\omega_{K}^{-1}(s)\omega_{K}^{\prime}(\omega_{K}^{-1}(s))}+\mathcal{O}(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert}{\pi^{3}}\int_{\omega_{K}(1)}^{\omega_{K}(\min\{K,L\}/\sqrt{8})}\mathrm{d}s\,\Big[\frac{\cos(s-3\pi/4)^{2}}{s}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s}{K^{2}}\right)\Big]+\mathcal{O}(L)

We used that

Appendix A
Two simple trace(-norm) inequalities

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A,B
be two Hilbert–Schmidt operators with
\lVert A\rVert\leq 1,\lVert B\rVert\leq 1
and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, we have

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lVert(A^{*}A)^{m}-(B^{*}B)^{m}\rVert_{1}\leq m\lVert A^{*}A-B^{*}B\rVert_{1}\leq m(\lVert A\rVert_{2}+\lVert B\rVert_{2})\lVert A-B\rVert_{2}\,.

Proof.

We just observe that

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lVert(A^{*}A)^{m}-(B^{*}B)^{m}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert(A^{*}A)^{j-1}(A^{*}A-B^{*}B)(B^{*}B)^{m-j}\right\rVert_{1}

(A.3)

\displaystyle\leq m\lVert A^{*}A-B^{*}B\rVert_{1}

(A.4)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(\lVert A^{*}(A-B)\rVert_{1}+\lVert(A-B)^{*}B\rVert_{1}\right)

(A.5)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(\lVert A\rVert_{2}\lVert A-B\rVert_{2}+\lVert A-B\rVert_{2}\lVert B\rVert_{2}\right)

(A.6)

\displaystyle=m(\lVert A\rVert_{2}+\lVert B\rVert_{2})\lVert A-B\rVert_{2}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be two Hilbert–Schmidt operators. Then for
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
,

(A.7)

\displaystyle\lvert\operatorname{tr}(A_{1}^{m}-A_{2}^{m})\rvert\leq m\max(\lVert A_{1}\rVert,\lVert A_{2}\rVert)^{m-2}\,\max(\lVert A_{1}\rVert_{2},\lVert A_{2}\rVert_{2})\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\rVert_{2}\,.

Proof.

The proof goes along the same line as the previous one. We write the difference
A_{1}^{m}-A_{2}^{m}
in the form
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(A_{1}^{m-i+1}A_{2}^{i-1}-A_{1}^{m-i}A_{2}^{i})
and use the triangle and Hölder inequality.

\mathcal{K}_{K}
and prove the convergence to the sine-kernel on a global scale, see
Theorem B.3
. In the second subsection, this is used to deal with the asymptotics of an integral of certain traces involving this kernel for large
K
and we evaluate the asymptotic coefficient
\mathsf{M}_{<K}(f)
to leading order in
K
for polynomials
f
. The proof is based on a seminal result by Landau and Widom
[
5
]
and an improvement by Widom
[
22
]
.

B.1.
Sine-kernel asymptotics on a global scale

For the results in this section we were inspired by the results of Kriecherbauer, Schubert, Schüler, and Venker in
[
4
]
. While the convergence of
\mathcal{K}_{K}
“in the bulk”
(-(2K-1)\pi/4,(2K-1)\pi/4)
to the sine-kernel is standard on a local scale of order one, this is not the case on the much larger scale of order
K
needed here. To go this scale, we use the function
\eta_{2K\pm 1}
.

Recall that
\eta(s)=\xi(s^{2})
, see (
2.12
). The advantage of
\eta
over
\xi
is that
\eta^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{1-s^{2}}
for
|s|\leq 1
. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, let
\eta_{\lambda}
be the rescaled function

(B.1)

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(s)\coloneqq\lambda\eta(s/\sqrt{\lambda})\,,\quad|s|\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\,.

Thus,
\eta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)\coloneqq\left(\eta_{\lambda}\right)^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{\lambda(1-s^{2}/\lambda)}=\sqrt{\lambda-s^{2}}
and
\eta_{\lambda}^{-1}(s)\coloneqq\left(\eta_{\lambda}\right)^{-1}(s)=\sqrt{\lambda}\eta^{-1}(s/\lambda)
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert s\rvert\leq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{2n+1}
we have the asymptotic expansion as
n\to\infty
,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\eta_{2n+1}^{\prime}(s)}\psi_{n}(s)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\cos(\eta_{2n+1}(s)-n\pi/2)+\mathcal{O}(1/(1+n\varepsilon^{3/2}))\,.

Proof.

Let
n=2\ell
or
n=2\ell+1
. According to
[
1
,
(18.7.19)
,
(18.7.20)
]
, the Hermite polynomials can be expressed in terms of Laguerre polynomials. Thus, we have

(B.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{2\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{2\ell}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}2^{2\ell}(2\ell)!\right)^{1/2}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{(-1/2)}(s^{2})\exp(-s^{2}/2)

(B.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{2\ell}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}2^{2\ell}(2\ell)!\right)^{1/2}}\frac{F_{\ell+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(s^{2}/(4\ell+1)\right)}{2^{-1/2}\sqrt{4\ell+1}\lvert 1-s^{2}/(4\ell+1)\rvert^{1/4}}

(B.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}(2\ell)!\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{2\ell+1/2}}\frac{F_{\ell+1}^{(-1/2)}\left(s^{2}/(4\ell+1)\right)}{\lvert 1-s^{2}/(4\ell+1)\rvert^{1/4}}\,,

(B.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{2\ell+1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{2\ell+1}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}2^{2\ell+1}(2\ell+1)!\right)^{1/2}}s\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{(+1/2)}(s^{2})\exp(-s^{2}/2)

(B.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{2\ell+1}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}2^{2\ell+1}(2\ell+1)!\right)^{1/2}}\frac{sF_{\ell+1}^{(+1/2)}\left(s^{2}/(4\ell+3)\right)}{2^{+1/2}\sqrt{4\ell+3}\frac{s}{\sqrt{4\ell+3}}\lvert 1-s^{2}/(4\ell+3)\rvert^{1/4}}

(B.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}(2\ell)!\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{2\ell+1}}\frac{F_{\ell+1}^{(+1/2)}\left(s^{2}/(4\ell+3)\right)}{\lvert 1-s^{2}/(4\ell+3)\rvert^{1/4}}\,.

In both cases, the first factor depends only on
\ell
and points to a Stirling approximation. The version of the Stirling approximation we are using is
m!=\sqrt{2\pi m}\left(m/e\right)^{m}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(1/m\right)\right)
. Thus, for
a\in\{1/2,1\}
, we observe

(B.9)

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\ell!}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}(2\ell)!\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{2\ell+a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\sqrt{2\pi\ell}(\ell/e)^{\ell}}{\left(\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{4\pi\ell}(2\ell/e)^{2\ell}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{2\ell}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(1/\ell)\right)

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\sqrt{\ell}}{\left(\sqrt{\ell}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{2\ell}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(1/\ell)\right)

(B.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\left(2n+1\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\right).

So far, we have shown that

(B.12)

\displaystyle\left((2n+1)\lvert 1-s^{2}/(2n+1)\rvert\right)^{1/4}\psi_{n}(s)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(-1)^{\ell}F_{\ell+1}^{(-1/2)}(s^{2}/(2n+1))(1+\mathcal{O}(1/n))\,,&\text{ if }n=2\ell\,,\\
(-1)^{\ell}F_{\ell+1}^{(+1/2)}(s^{2}/(2n+1))(1+\mathcal{O}(1/n))\,,&\text{ if }n=2\ell+1\,.\end{cases}

We will use the asymptotics for
F_{K}^{(\alpha)}
, which we developed in
2.3
and
2.10
. The parameter
\nu
appearing in the asymptotics of
F_{K}^{(\alpha)}
, is given by
\nu=4K+2(\alpha-1)
, where
K=\ell+1
. If
n=2\ell
, this simplifies to
4(\ell+1)+2(-1/2-1)=4\ell+1=2n+1
. In the other case
n=2\ell+1
, it is
4(\ell+1)+2(1/2-1)=4\ell+3=2n+1
. Thus, independent of the parity of
n
, the parameter
\nu
is given by
2n+1
. Thus,

(B.13)

\displaystyle\nu\xi(s^{2}/(2n+1))=(2n+1)\eta(s/\sqrt{2n+1})=\eta_{2n+1}(s)\,.

We observe that
\alpha=-1/2\,(-1)^{n}
. We can now insert this into the asymptotics granted by
2.3
and
2.10
for the case
\lvert s\rvert\leq\sqrt{2n+1}
and get

(B.14)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\eta_{2n+1}^{\prime}(s)}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\psi_{n}(s)

(B.15)

\displaystyle=\left((2n+1)\lvert 1-s^{2}/(2n+1)\rvert\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\psi_{n}(s)

(B.16)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\cos(\nu\xi(s^{2}/(2n+1)-\alpha\pi/2-\pi/4)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1+(\ell+1)(1-s^{2}/(2n+1))^{3/2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

(B.17)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\cos(\eta_{2n+1}(s)-\pi/4(1-(-1)^{n}))+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1+n(1-s^{2}/(2n+1))^{3/2}}\right)

(B.18)

\displaystyle=\cos(\eta_{2n+1}(s)-\pi/4(1-(-1)^{n})-\ell\pi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1+n(1-(1-\varepsilon)^{2})^{3/2}}\right)

(B.19)

\displaystyle=\cos(\eta_{2n+1}(s)-n\pi/2)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1+n\varepsilon^{3/2}}\right).

The last step relies on the identity
n=2\ell+(1-(-1)^{n})/2
.
∎

The next lemma deals with the integral kernel of
\mathcal{K}_{K}
under scaling, that is, a change of coordinates by
\eta_{2K-1}
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
and let
I,J\subset(-\sqrt{2K-1},\sqrt{2K-1})\eqqcolon\Omega_{1}
be intervals. We define for
s,t\in(-(2K-1)\pi/4,(2K-1)\pi/4)\eqqcolon\Omega_{2}
,

(B.20)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(s,t)\coloneqq\sqrt{\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(s)\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(t)}\,\mathcal{K}_{K}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s),\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)\big)\,.

Then, the operators
1_{I}\mathcal{K}_{K}1_{J}
and
1_{\eta_{2K-1}(I)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}1_{\eta_{2K-1}(J)}
are unitarily equivalent.

Proof.

As
I,J\subset\Omega_{1}
, it suffices to consider
\mathcal{K}_{K}
as an integral operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{1})
and as
\eta_{2K-1}(\Omega_{1})=\Omega_{2}
, it suffices to consider
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}
as an integral operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{2})
. We define the shorthand
\theta\coloneqq\eta_{2K-1}
. All we need to know about
\theta
is that
\theta
is a
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth bijection from
\Omega_{1}
to
\Omega_{2}
. We can now define the unitary operator

(B.21)

\displaystyle A_{\theta}^{-1}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{2})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{1})\,,

\displaystyle f\mapsto

\displaystyle(t\mapsto f(\theta(t))\sqrt{\theta^{\prime}(t)})\,,

(B.22)

\displaystyle A_{\theta}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{1})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{2})\,,

\displaystyle g\mapsto

\displaystyle(t\mapsto g(\theta^{-1}(t))\sqrt{\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(t)})\,.

We see that
A_{\theta}1_{I}A_{\theta}^{-1}=1_{\theta(I)}
and
A_{\theta}1_{J}A_{\theta}^{-1}=1_{\theta(J)}
. Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{2})
and
a\in\Omega_{2}
. Then, we observe that

(B.23)

\displaystyle\left(A_{\theta}\mathcal{K}_{K}A_{\theta}^{-1}f\right)(a)

\displaystyle=\left(A_{\theta}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\mathcal{K}_{K}({\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}},t)f(\theta(t))\sqrt{\theta^{\prime}(t)}\mathrm{d}t\right)(a)

(B.24)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\sqrt{\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(a)}\,\mathcal{K}_{K}\left(\theta^{-1}(a),\theta^{-1}(\theta(t))\right)f(\theta(t))1/\sqrt{\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(\theta(t))}\,\mathrm{d}t

(B.25)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{2}}\sqrt{\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(a)}\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(b)\,\mathcal{K}_{K}\left(\theta^{-1}(a),\theta^{-1}(b)\right)f(b)1/\sqrt{\left(\theta^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(b)}\,\mathrm{d}b

(B.26)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{2}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(a,b)f(b)\,\mathrm{d}b

(B.27)

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}f)(a)\,.

Thus,
1_{I}\mathcal{K}_{K}1_{J}=A_{\theta}^{-1}1_{\theta(I)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}1_{\theta(J)}A_{\theta}
and
1_{\theta(I)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}1_{\theta(J)}
are unitarily equivalent, which was the claim.
∎

The next result is the mentioned sine-kernel asymptotics of
\mathcal{K}_{K}
in the bulk, at least in the most relevant portion of it.

Theorem B.3
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/2,K\in\mathbb{N}
with
\varepsilon^{6}K>C
. (This
C
is fixed, but currently unknown.) For any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
with
(2K-1)\eta(-1/2)<s<t<(2K-1)\eta(1-\varepsilon)
and
1/2\leq\lvert s-t\rvert\leq K\varepsilon^{6}
, we have with
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(s,t)
defined in (
B.20
),

(B.28)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(s,t)=\frac{\sin(s-t)}{\pi(s-t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\lvert s-t\rvert}\right).

Remark
.

That this asymptotics also holds for
\lvert s-t\rvert\leq 1/2
(local scale) is well-known (for instance in random matrix theory), but our approach would need estimates on
\psi_{\ell}^{\prime}
, which we don’t look into.

Proof.

We define

(B.29)

\displaystyle D(s,t)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(s)\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(t)}

(B.30)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta_{2K-1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))\eta_{2K-1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t))}^{-1}

(B.31)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(2K-1)\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))}^{-1}\,.

We recall that by the Christoffel–Darboux formula

(B.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{K}(x,y)=\sqrt{K/2}\,\frac{\psi_{K}(x)\psi_{K-1}(y)-\psi_{K-1}(x)\psi_{K}(y)}{x-y}\,.

Therefore, we write

(B.33)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\overbrace{\frac{D(s,t)}{\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)-\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)}}^{\eqqcolon T_{1}(s,t)}\sqrt{K/2}\,D(s,t)

(B.34)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[\underbrace{\frac{\psi_{K}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))\psi_{K-1}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)\big)}{D(s,t)}}_{\eqqcolon T_{2}(s,t)}-\underbrace{\frac{\psi_{K-1}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)\big)\psi_{K}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)\big)}{D(s,t)}}_{\eqqcolon T_{3}(s,t)}\Bigg]\,.

We take a closer look at the condition
t\leq(2K-1)\eta(1-\varepsilon)
. Together with
s\geq(2K-1)\eta(-1/2)
and
s<t
, this tells us that
s/(2K-1)
and
t/(2K-1)
are in
\eta((-1,1))=(\eta(-1),\eta(1))
. Thus, the expressions
\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s),\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)
are well-defined. Furthermore, as
\eta
is increasing on
(-1,1)
, its inverse is increasing as well and thus, we can conclude

(B.35)

\displaystyle-1/2\leq\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))<\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1))\leq 1-\varepsilon\,.

The assumption
\lvert s-t\rvert<K\varepsilon^{6}
now leads to

(B.36)

\displaystyle\big\lvert\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))-\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1))\big\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert s-t\rvert}{K\inf_{a\in(-1/2,1-\varepsilon)}\eta^{\prime}(a)}

(B.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert s-t\rvert}{K\inf_{a\in(-1/2,1-\varepsilon)}\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}<\frac{\lvert s-t\rvert}{K\sqrt{\varepsilon}}<\varepsilon^{5}\,.

Next, we need a technical result. Let
u,v\in(-1/2,1-\varepsilon)
with
\lvert u-v\rvert<\varepsilon
. Then, we claim that

(B.38)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(u)/\eta^{\prime}(v)=1+\mathcal{O}(\lvert u-v\rvert/\varepsilon)\,.

To this end, we consider the function
a\mapsto 2\ln(\eta^{\prime}(a))
and take its derivative at some
a\in(-1/2,1-\varepsilon)
. Thus, we get

(B.39)

\displaystyle\lvert 2\ln(\eta^{\prime}(a))^{\prime}\rvert=\lvert\ln(1-a^{2})^{\prime}\rvert=2\lvert a\rvert/(1-a^{2})<1/\varepsilon\,.

Thus,
1/\varepsilon
is a Lipschitz constant for
a\mapsto\ln(\eta^{\prime}(a))
. Since
\lvert u-v\rvert<\varepsilon
, our claim

(B.40)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(u)/\eta^{\prime}(v)=\exp(\mathcal{O}(\lvert u-v\rvert/\varepsilon))=1+\mathcal{O}(\lvert u-v\rvert/\varepsilon)

follows.

We begin by showing that
T_{1}(s,t)\approx 1/(s-t)
.

We recall that
\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}
is smooth and strictly increasing on the interval
(s,t)
. Let
\hat{s}\in(s,t)
be (uniquely) determined by the mean-value theorem for
\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}
on
(s,t)
, as seen next. We consider the expression

(B.41)

\displaystyle\frac{(s-t)D(s,t)}{\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)-\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(s)\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(t)}}{\left(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(\hat{s})}

(B.42)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2K-1}\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(\hat{s}/(2K-1)))}{\sqrt{(2K-1)\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))}}

(B.43)

\displaystyle=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}/\varepsilon\right)=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)\,.

The final step relies on (
B.38
) and (
B.36
). Thus, we have just shown that

(B.44)

\displaystyle T_{1}(s,t)=\frac{1}{s-t}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\right).

Next, we consider
T_{2}
and expand

(B.45)

\displaystyle T_{2}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{K}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))\psi_{K-1}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t)\big)}{D(s,t)}

(B.46)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\eta_{2K-1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))}}{\sqrt{\eta_{2K+1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))}}\left(\sqrt{\eta_{2K+1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))}\psi_{K}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))\right)

(B.47)

\displaystyle\times\left(\sqrt{\eta_{2K-1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t))}\psi_{K-1}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t))\right).

The final two terms are already in the form of
B.1
. For the first factor, we just observe

(B.48)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{2K-1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))}{\eta_{2K+1}^{\prime}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2K-1}\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))}{\sqrt{2K+1}\eta^{\prime}\left(\sqrt{(2K-1)/(2K+1)}\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))\right)}

(B.49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\mathcal{O}(1/K)\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}(1/(K\varepsilon))\right)=1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})\,.

We used
\sqrt{(2K-1)/(2K+1)}=1+\mathcal{O}(1/K)
, (
B.35
) and (
B.38
). Since
|\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{2K-1}
(and the same with
t
replacing
s
) by (
B.35
) and
1/(K\varepsilon^{3/2})<\varepsilon^{4}
we can apply
B.1
to see that

(B.50)

\displaystyle T_{2}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})\right)\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\cos(\eta_{2K+1}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))-K\pi/2)+\varepsilon^{4}\right)

(B.51)

\displaystyle\times\left(\cos(\eta_{2K-1}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(t))-(K-1)\pi/2)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\right)

(B.52)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\cos(\eta_{2K+1}(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s))-K\pi/2)\sin(t-K\pi/2)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

Our next goal is to understand
\eta_{2K+1}\circ\eta_{2K-1}(s)
. By definition,

(B.53)

\eta_{2K-1}(s)=(2K-1)\eta(s/\sqrt{2K-1})

so that
\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)=\sqrt{2K-1}\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))
. We also recall
\eta^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
. Using the mean-value theorem and (
B.38
), we can conclude

(B.54)

\displaystyle\eta_{2K+1}\big(\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)\big)

(B.55)

\displaystyle=(2K+1)\eta\Big(\frac{\eta_{2K-1}^{-1}(s)}{\sqrt{2K+1}}\Big)

(B.56)

\displaystyle=(2K+1)\eta\Big(\eta^{-1}\left(s/(2K-1)\right)\Big(1-\frac{1}{2K+1}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K^{2}}\right)\Big)\Big)

(B.57)

\displaystyle=(2K+1)\Bigg[\frac{s}{2K-1}-\eta^{\prime}\left(\eta^{-1}\left(\frac{s}{2K-1}\right)\right)\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(1/(K\varepsilon))}{2K+1}\eta^{-1}\left(\frac{s}{2K-1}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K^{2}}\right)\Bigg]

(B.58)

\displaystyle=(2K+1)\Big[\frac{s}{2K-1}-\sqrt{1-(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))^{2}}\,\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})}{2K+1}\,\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5}/K)\Big]

(B.59)

\displaystyle=s+2s/(2K-1)-\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))\sqrt{1-(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})

(B.60)

\displaystyle-\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))

(B.61)

\displaystyle=s+2s/(2K-1)-2\eta(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})

(B.62)

\displaystyle=s+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5})\,.

Thus, as the cosine is Lipschitz-continuous, we can conclude

(B.63)

\displaystyle T_{2}(s,t)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\cos\Big(s+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))-K\pi/2\Big)\sin(t-K\pi/2)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

The identity
-2\cos(a)\sin(b)+\sin(a+b)=\sin(a-b)
yields

(B.64)

\displaystyle\pi T_{2}(s,t)+\sin\left(s+t+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))-K\pi\right)

(B.65)

\displaystyle=\sin\left(s-t+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

(B.66)

\displaystyle=\sin(s-t)\cos\circ\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\cos(s-t)\sin\circ\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

(B.67)

\displaystyle=\sin(s-t)\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\cos(s-t)\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

This allows us to conclude

(B.68)

\displaystyle\pi T_{2}(s,t)-\pi T_{3}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\pi T_{2}(s,t)-\pi T_{2}(t,s)

(B.69)

\displaystyle=\sin(s-t)\left(\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))+\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))\right)

(B.70)

\displaystyle+\cos(s-t)\left(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))-\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1))\right)

(B.71)

\displaystyle-\sin\left(s+t+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))-K\pi\right)

(B.72)

\displaystyle+\sin\left(s+t+\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))-K\pi\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

(B.73)

\displaystyle=\sin(s-t)\left(2\sqrt{\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))}\right)

(B.74)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\lvert\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))-\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))\right\rvert\right)

(B.75)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\lvert\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1))-\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1))\right\rvert\right)

(B.76)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\lvert\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))-\sin^{-1}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))\right\rvert\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

(B.78)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sin(s-t)}{\sqrt{2K-1}D(s,t)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

(B.79)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(s-t)}{\sqrt{K/2}D(s,t)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

), (
B.44
), and (

), we can conclude

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{K}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s-t}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\right)\sqrt{K/2}D(s,t)\left(\frac{\sin(s-t)}{\pi\sqrt{K/2}D(s,t)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\right)

(B.81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(s-t)}{\pi(s-t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{4}+\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{K}D(s,t)}{\lvert s-t\rvert}\right).

We are left to show that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sqrt{K}D(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{(2K-1)\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(s/(2K-1)))\eta^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}(t/(2K-1)))}}

(B.83)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\inf_{a,b\in(-1/2,1-\varepsilon)}\sqrt{\eta^{\prime}(a)\eta^{\prime}(b)}}

(B.84)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\inf_{\varepsilon<h<1}\sqrt{1-(1-h)^{2}}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}<1\,.

This concludes the proof.

Appendix C
Proof of the expansion (
3.6
)

In this section, we prove the expansion (
3.6
), that is, we show that for any
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smooth boundary
\partial\Lambda

(C.1)

\displaystyle F(s)=s\int_{L\Lambda}\mathrm{d}x\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}(x+s(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)))=2s^{2}L\lvert\partial\Lambda\rvert+\mathcal{O}(s^{3})\,.

Lemma C.1
.

For any piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\|v\|=1
, and any
s\in[0,\infty)
, we have the identity

(C.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,f(x)1_{\Lambda^{\complement}}(x+sv)=\int_{\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}x\,f(x)-\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}t\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\,f(y-tv)\,n(y)\cdot v\,,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hausdorff (surface) measure on
\partial\Lambda
and where
n(y)
is the outward unit normal vector of
\partial\Lambda
at
y
, which is well-defined for
\mathcal{H}
almost all
y
.

Remark
.

While this lemma is proved for piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth domains
\Lambda
, the final result of this section requires a somewhat stronger condition and we are content with piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-smoothness.

Proof.

We note that the identity is trivial for
s=0
.

Let us now assume that
f\in\mathsf{C}^{1}_{\text{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We differentiate the right-hand side by
s
and arrive at

(C.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,f(x)1_{\Lambda^{\complement}}(x+sv)\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,f(x-sv)1_{\Lambda^{\complement}}(x)\right)

(C.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}x\,\partial_{s}f(x-sv)

(C.5)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}x\,Df(x-sv)v

(C.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda^{\complement}}\mathrm{d}x\,\operatorname{div}(f(x-sv)v)

(C.7)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\,f(y-sv)\,v\cdot n(y)\,.

The final step is the divergence theorem applied to the Lipschitz domain
\Lambda^{\complement}\cap D_{R}(0)
, where
R
is so large that
L\Lambda\subset D_{R/2}(0)
and
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset D_{R}(0)
. The minus sign appears as the outward normal vector of
\Lambda^{\complement}
at
y\in\partial\Lambda
is
-n(y)
. This equals the differential in
s
of the right-hand side of (
C.2
) and thus, as both sides of (
C.2
) agree for
s=0
and their differentials in
s
agree, we have shown the claim for
f\in\mathsf{C}^{1}_{\text{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

To conclude the statement for arbitrary
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, we need to construct a function
\hat{f}\in\mathsf{C}^{1}_{\text{c}}
such that both sides of (
C.2
) are bounded by
\varepsilon_{0}
for the function
f-\hat{f}
. To that end, let
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
to be chosen later and let
\hat{f}
be given as the convolution of
f
with a mollifying kernel, such that

(C.8)

\displaystyle\lVert f-\hat{f}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\,,\quad\lVert\hat{f}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

The
\mathrm{L}^{1}
estimate deals with the left-hand side and the first expression on the right-hand side of (
C.2
). The final expression requires a bit more attention. Let
\varepsilon_{2}>0
satisfy

(C.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\{y\in\partial\Lambda\colon\lvert n(y)\cdot v\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{2}\}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\,\lvert n(y)\cdot v\rvert<\varepsilon_{0}/\left(4s\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right).

Such an
\varepsilon_{2}>0
exists, as the expression on the left-hand side vanishes for
\varepsilon_{2}=0
and is right-continuous. Let
\partial\Lambda_{1}\coloneqq\{y\in\partial\Lambda\colon\lvert n(y)\cdot v\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{2}\}
and
\partial\Lambda_{2}\coloneqq\partial\Lambda\setminus\partial\Lambda_{1}
. Thus, we can conclude that

(C.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}t\int_{\partial\Lambda_{1}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\,\lvert(f-\hat{f})(y-tv)\rvert\,\lvert n(y)\cdot v\rvert\leq\left(2s\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right)\varepsilon_{0}/\left(4s\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right)=\varepsilon_{0}/2\,.

On the remaining set, we will use
\lVert f-\hat{f}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
. To that end, we estimate

(C.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}t\int_{\partial\Lambda_{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\,\lvert(f-\hat{f})(y-tv)\rvert\,\lvert n(y)\cdot v\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Lambda_{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\,\lvert(f-\hat{f})(y-tv)\rvert

(C.12)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\partial\Lambda_{2}}\left(\#\left\{(y-v\mathbb{R})\cap\partial\Lambda_{2}\right\}\right)\,\lVert f-\hat{f}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

(C.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\#\left\{(y-v\mathbb{R})\cap\partial\Lambda_{2}\right\}\right)\varepsilon_{1}\,.

As we are still free to choose
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
, we only need to show that the supremum is finite for any fixed
\Lambda
and
\varepsilon_{2}
. Because
\partial\Lambda
is piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth, it is a finite union of
\mathsf{C}^{1}
-smooth paths. For
i=1,\dots,r
, let
\Psi_{i}\colon[0,\lambda_{i}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be these paths with the normalization
\lVert\Psi_{i}^{\prime}(t)\rVert=1
for all
t\in[0,\lambda_{i}]
. For any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we observe (recall,
\wedge
is the wedge product in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

Appendix D
Concluding remarks

In this final section we make some concluding remarks.

(1)

We formulated our main results for polynomial test functions
f
with
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=0
and bounded domains
\Lambda
. While the vanishing of
f
at 0 is necessary for the trace of the corresponding operators to exist, the vanishing of
f
at 1 has the effect to cancel the “volume” term of the order
L^{2}
. It is trivial to go back to functions that do not vanish at 1 by adding the linear function
-tf(1)
. However, since the number of Landau levels below some Fermi energy
\mu
is a discontinuous (step) function, the leading volume term may contain lower order terms that would scramble with the surface term when taking the limits
K,L\to\infty
. We are not interested in these terms. Moreover, under the additional condition
f(t)=f(1-t)
, we may replace
\Lambda
by its complement without changing the result in the relevant traces, in particular in
1.1
. In other words, we may assume that
\Lambda
or its complement is a bounded domain.

Secondly, the most important application is the entropy function
f
, which in the von Neumann case is the function
f(t)=-t\ln(t)-(1-t)\ln(1-t)
for
t\in(0,1)
and zero outside. While it is a standard exercise to extend our result from polynomials
f
to continuous functions
f
which are (one-sided) differentiable at the two end-points (see for instance
[
6
]
and references therein), it is a serious issue to extend our results to functions that are only Hölder continuous (with exponent
\alpha
less than 1) at the two endpoints. The above entropy function is such an example. Given the length of the present paper, we did not dive into this question but we are confident that it will be accomplished in a forthcoming paper. What is needed is a trace-norm estimate of the form
\|(1_{L\Lambda}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda^{\complement}}P_{K}1_{L\Lambda})^{\alpha}\|_{1}\leq CL\min\{\ln(K),\ln(L)\}
with a constant
C
depending only on the domain
\Lambda
. Such estimates do not trivially follow from known estimates in
[
18
]
for the Laplacian and in
[
8
]
for the Landau Hamiltonian and require substantial work.

(2)

Except for the simple but important case of a quadratic function we are not able to cover the full range of parameters
K
and
L
. The quadratic function is important since it is the first non-trivial example in the Widom conjecture (or Szegő asymptotics) and
f(t)=4\ln(2)t(1-t)
serves as a lower bound to the entropy function. We can analyse the full range of parameters because the phase (caused by the magnetic field) cancels in the computation of the relevant trace. For higher order polynomials and
L\ll K
this phase is a nuisance and we only have a rough bound to control it.

On the other hand, if
K\ll L
then the phase caused by the magnetic field is absolutely crucial. When the domain
\Lambda
is a polygon we are able to cover essentially the full range of parameters
K\leq CL
and prove an area law of the order
L\ln(K)|\partial\Lambda|
. Going to general (piecewise
\mathsf{C}^{2}
) smooth domains
\Lambda
we loose some range in order to control additional error terms. Still we believe that the above area law holds in the larger region (of parameters
K,L
) but we are not able to prove it.

For fixed
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
7.2
,
7.4
and
8.1
prove that the leading order asymptotic expansion in (
1.3
) also holds for domains
\Lambda
, which are polygons or
\mathsf{C}^{2}
-domains, instead of
\mathsf{C}^{3}
-domains as in
[
8
]
. This result can be extended to merely Hölder continuous functions
f
, as the estimate
[
8
, Theorem 13]
only needs
\Lambda
to be a Lipschitz domain.

(3)

Throughout the paper we worked with a constant (that is, translation invariant) magnetic field whose strength goes to zero. One can ask what happens with an arbitrary magnetic field
\lambda B(x)
in the limit
\lambda\to 0
,
L\to\infty
and the function
B
fixed. In
[
14
]
, one of the present authors has analysed the stability of the area law under a varying magnetic field. Roughly speaking, if the magnetic field is asymptotically (for large
\|x\|
) constant then we observe the same area law as for a constant magnetic field. It is reasonable to expect that the same stability holds in the joint limit
\lambda\to 0,L\to\infty
discussed here “only” for a spatially constant magnetic field.

The situation is unclear for example for a magnetic field
B(x)
which tends to 0 as
\|x\|\to\infty
. Depending on the rate of convergence, it may create absolutely continuous spectrum or even change completely to an absolutely continuous spectrum for
H_{B}
on
[0,\infty)
, see
[
9
]
. We do not know whether an area law or an enhanced area-law holds as the appearance of absolutely continuous spectrum may still yield an area law. As a warning, we have found ground states of Hamiltonians with purely absolutely continuous spectrum that display an area law.
