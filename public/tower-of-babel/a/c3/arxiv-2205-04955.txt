The Global Existence of Solutions to a Quasi-Relativistic Incompressible Navier-Stokes Model
Jaroslaw S. Jaracz

Abstract
. We introduce a new modified Navier-Stokes model in 3 3 dimensions by modifying the convection term in the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations. This is done by replacing the convective term ( u ⋅ ∇ ) u (\textbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\textbf{u} by ( v ⋅ ∇ ) u (\textbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\textbf{u} with v = c ​ u / c 2 + | u | 2 \textbf{v}=c\textbf{u}/\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}} where c c is the speed of light. Thus we have that | v | ≤ c |\textbf{v}|\leq c and for | u | ≪ c |\textbf{u}|\ll c we have v ≈ u \textbf{v}\approx\textbf{u} . Thus the solutions to this system should yield a good approximation to the solutions of the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations under physically reasonable conditions. The modification of the convective term is a natural progression of the work done in [ 4 ] . The property that | v | ≤ c |\textbf{v}|\leq c embodies the notion that in relativity matter can’t travel faster than the speed of light, giving the model its name. We prove that there exists a strong solution u ∈ L 2 ​ ( 0 , T , H 2 ) ∩ L ∞ ​ ( 0 , T , V ) \textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{H}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\textbf{V}) with u ′ ∈ L 2 ​ ( 0 , T , L 2 ) \textbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2}) to our system of equations on either a smooth bounded domain U ⊂ R 3 U\subset\textbf{R}^{3} or the flat 3 3 -torus 𝕋 \mathbb{T} for any initial velocity u 0 ∈ V \textbf{u}_{0}\in\textbf{V} and any forcing function f ∈ L 2 ​ ( 0 , T , L 2 ) \textbf{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2}) . No assumption on the smallness of the data is necessary. Here V is the space of weakly divergence free vector fields with components in H 1 H^{1} which vanish on the boundary. We also prove the uniqueness of this strong solution. Though our modification is somewhat ad-hoc, it suggests that though more complicated, equations incorporating aspects of special and general relativity might have better existence and uniqueness properties than the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations.



1.
Introduction

The ordinary incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for a fluid of constant density take the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\alpha\Delta\textbf{u}+(\textbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\textbf{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\textbf{f}

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textbf{u}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\textbf{u}_{0}(x)

where
\textbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
is the velocity,
p
is a scalar function representing the pressure and
f
is some forcing function, with all quantities depending on
(x,t)=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},t)
. The incompressibility condition
\nabla\cdot\textbf{u}=0
rules out the possibility shock or pressure waves, and experimentally the model usually holds up well up to fluid speeds of around Mach
0.3\approx 100\,m/s
, which is vanishingly small compared to the speed of light
c\approx 3\times 10^{8}\,m/s
.

As is well known, the existence and uniqueness of so-called strong solutions the the Navier-Stokes equations in
3
-dimensions has not been established without assuming that the initial data and forcing function are ”small” in some appropriate sense. We introduce a modification to these equations which allows us to prove the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions without any such smallness assumptions.

The study of relativistic fluid mechanics dates all the way back to the work of Landau and Lifshitz
[
5
]
in the late 1950’s. Lately, the focus has been on developing relativistic hydrodynamic equations which are stable and causal
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
, with focus on well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for such equations. Of course, considering how little we know about the Navier-Stokes equations themselves, the applications of such relativistic fluid dynamics to terrestial applications will remain limited, at least in the short term.

In this paper, we study a model which is somewhere in between the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations and fully relativistic equations (much closer to the former than the latter). The simplest and probably most well known consequence of special and general relativity is that no ordinary physical objects can exceed the speed of light. We would like to somehow embody this simple notion in a modified version of the Navier-Stokes equations.

In a recent paper
[
4
]
we studied a modified model of visco-elastic fluids. Based on physical and experimental observations, one of the modifications to the ordinary model was the change

(\textbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\textbf{u}\rightarrow(J\textbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\textbf{u}

to the convective term in the Navier-Stokes equations. Here
J
is a regularizing operator (simply a convolution with a smooth bump function supported in some small ball which serves to average out the velocity). This allowed for the proof of the existence of global and regular solutions to the modified system. The smoothening was justified both experimentally and theoretically. Experimentally, visco-elastic flows are observed to be quite smooth. Also, theoretically the smoothing can be justified by taking Brownian motion of the fluid molecules into account where the exchange of molecules between fluid parcels serves to average out some of the velocity.

Thus, it is natural to apply other well known physical principles to the model. We propose the following modified Navier-Stokes equations

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\alpha\Delta\textbf{u}+\left(\frac{c\textbf{u}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\cdot\nabla\right)\textbf{u}+\nabla p&=\textbf{f}\\
\nabla\cdot\textbf{u}&=0\\
\textbf{u}(x,0)&=\textbf{u}_{0}(x)\end{split}

where
c
is the speed of light. Notice that for
|\textbf{u}|\ll c
we have

(1.2)

\textbf{u}\approx\frac{c\textbf{u}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}

and so for velocities much smaller than the speed of light, the model should give results very close to the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations.

Also notice that

\left|\frac{c\textbf{u}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right|\leq c

which partially encapsulates the relativistic notion that matter can’t move faster than the speed of light. Thus we call this model the quasi-relativistic Navier-Stokes equations. It is only quasi-relativistic since in principle nothing prevents
|\textbf{u}|\geq c
. Of course, this is merely an approximation to real world fluid flow, just as the Navier-Stokes themselves are. The notion of incompressibility is on its own a physical impossibility.

Whether the model is useful will depend on how closely it models real world situations, which is to be determined. However we mention that computational rheologists often drop the convection term when numerically simulating the Navier-Stokes equations by applying certain simplifying assumptions. Thus in the cases where the convective term can be dropped, there is no difference between our model and the ordinary Navier-Stokes equations.

We will consider our equations on a bounded domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
or on the
3
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}
. We collectively refer to these spaces as
\Omega
, since the proof is the same in both cases. Of course we require some kind of boundary condition for the equations and so we take the Dirichlet bundary condition

\textbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0

which is of course vacuously true for the empty boundary when
\Omega=\mathbb{T}
.

1.1.
Main Result

The notation we use for the Sobolev spaces can be found in the next section. We now state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the system of equations (
1.1
) with Dirichlet boundary condition on either a smooth bounded domain

or on the
3

\mathbb{T}
. Then for any
\textbf{u}_{0}\in\textbf{V}
and

there exists a strong solution
\textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{H}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\textbf{V})
with
\textbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})
. Moreover, this solution is unique on
0\leq t\leq T
.

There is no difference in the proofs for
\Omega
and
\mathbb{T}
. The theorem collects the results of Propositions

,
2.2
, and
2.3

We also briefly remark on the terminology which might be slightly confusing to those who have not seen it before. A velocity satisfying

\textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V}),\quad\text{with}\quad\textbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V}^{\prime})

is called a

solution. On the other hand, a velocity satisfying

\textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{H}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\textbf{V}),\quad\text{with}\quad\textbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})

2.1.
Existence of a Weak Solution

For the moment we assume that

(2.1)

\textbf{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V}^{\prime})

and this is the minimum regularity on

we require. Later on, when we wish to obtain higher regularity for our solution, we will assume a stronger condition on

We now wish to weakly solve the system of equations

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\textbf{u}-\alpha\Delta\textbf{u}+\left(\frac{c\textbf{u}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\cdot\nabla\right)\textbf{u}+\nabla p&=\textbf{f}\\
\nabla\cdot\textbf{u}&=0\\
\textbf{u}(x,0)&=\textbf{u}_{0}(x)\\
\textbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}&=0\end{split}

in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary condition. There are two equivalent ways of defining what we mean by this.
First we define

A[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]\coloneqq\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla v_{i}\,dx

and

B[\textbf{y},\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(\frac{c\textbf{y}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}\cdot\nabla\right)\textbf{u}\right]\cdot\textbf{v}\,dx=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{\Omega}\frac{cy_{j}(\partial_{j}u_{i})v_{i}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}\,dx.

Notice that
A[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]
is linear in both arguments, while
B[\textbf{y},\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]
is linear in
u
and
v
but not in

. However, we see we can make the estimate

(2.3)

\left|B[\textbf{y},\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]\right|\leq C_{B}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\textbf{u}||\textbf{v}|\,dx\leq C_{B}\|\nabla\textbf{u}\|\|\textbf{v}\|

where the constant
C_{B}>0
depends only on

(2.4)

|A[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]|\leq\alpha\|\nabla\textbf{u}\|\|\nabla\textbf{v}\|

Let

\textbf{V}^{\prime}

\langle A(\textbf{u}),\textbf{v}\rangle\coloneqq A[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}],\quad\langle B(\textbf{u}),\textbf{v}\rangle\coloneqq B[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})+A[\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]+B[\textbf{u},\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]

\displaystyle=\langle\textbf{f},\textbf{v}\rangle

(2.6)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}(0)

\displaystyle=\textbf{u}_{0}

Suppose that

\displaystyle=\textbf{u}_{m0}

\displaystyle+\alpha\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{m}\|^{2}\leq\|\textbf{f}\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|_{\textbf{V}}+C_{B}\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{m}\|\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\textbf{f}\|^{2}_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}}{4\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}+C_{B}\varepsilon\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{m}\|^{2}+\frac{C_{B}\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|^{2}}{4\varepsilon}

.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}\leq K\left(\|\textbf{f}\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}\right)

and thus applying Gronwall’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{Kt}\left(\|\textbf{u}_{m}(0)\|^{2}+K\int_{0}^{t}\|\textbf{f}(s)\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\,ds\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{Kt}\left(K+1\right)\left(\|\textbf{u}_{m}(0)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\textbf{f}(s)\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\,ds\right).

Since

\|\textbf{u}_{m}(0)\|^{2}\leq\|\textbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}

we have

(2.13)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}\leq e^{KT}\left(K+1\right)\left(\|\textbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\textbf{f}(s)\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\,ds\right)\coloneqq C_{1}(T)

where in particular
C_{1}(T)
is independent of
m
. Next, returning to (
2.12
) and integrating with respect to
t
we obtain

(2.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}\,dt&\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\|\textbf{u}_{m}(0)\|^{2}-\|\textbf{u}_{m}(T)\|^{2}+K\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{f}(t)\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\,dt+K\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|^{2}\,dt\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(2C_{1}(T)+KTC_{1}(T)+K\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{f}(t)\|_{\textbf{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\,dt\ \right)\coloneqq C_{2}(T)\end{split}

and so we can estimate

(2.15)

\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|^{2}_{L(0,T;\textbf{V})}\leq TC_{1}(T)+C_{2}(T)

where the right hand side is independent of
m
.

As a result of (
2.13
) and (
2.15
) there exists a function

\textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H)

and a subsequence
m^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}\rightarrow\textbf{u}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(0,T;V)\quad\text{weakly, and}\\
L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\quad\text{weak-star, as}\quad m^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty\end{array}\right\}.

Moreover, it can be shown that in fact

(2.18)

\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}\rightarrow\textbf{u}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(0,T;H)\quad\text{strongly}.

The proof is the same as the proof of (3.3.41) in
[
6
]
.

Next, we need to show that
u
in fact is a solution of (
2.5
). To do so, we let
\psi
be a continuously differntiable function on
[0,T]
with
\psi(T)=0
. We multiply (
2.9
) by
\psi
and integrate by parts to obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}(\textbf{u}_{m}(t),\psi^{\prime}(t)\textbf{w}_{k})\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}A[\textbf{u}_{m}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}]\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m}(t),\textbf{u}_{m}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}]\,dt

\displaystyle=(\textbf{u}_{m0},\textbf{w}_{k})\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\textbf{f}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}\rangle\,dt.

Passing to the limit for the subsequence
m^{\prime}
is trivial for the linear terms by definition of weak convergence. We have to deal with the nonlinear term. This can be done with the use of the following lemma, whose proof is elementary.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
\{x_{m}\}\subset X
a sequence of elements such that
x_{m}\rightarrow x
weakly, and
\{l_{k}\}\subset X^{*}
a sequence of linear functionals such that
l_{k}\rightarrow l
in the operator norm. Then
l_{k}(x_{k})\rightarrow l(x)
.

With this lemma in hand, define
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T]
. Let
\textbf{v}=\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}
. Let

(2.19)

C_{v}=\sup_{(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}}|\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}(x)|

which exists since
\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}(x)\in C^{1}(\mkern 1.6mu\overline{\mkern-1.6mu\Omega\mkern-1.6mu}\mkern 1.6mu_{T})
by the smoothness of the components of
\textbf{w}_{k}
. For such
v
we have

\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\cdot,\textbf{v}]\,dt\in(L^{2}(0,T;V))^{*}

where using (
2.3
) and (
2.19
) we have

\left|\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\cdot,\textbf{v}]\,dt\right|\leq C_{B}C_{v}\sqrt{|\Omega|T}\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}

where
|\Omega|
is the volume of
\Omega
.

Now, we want to show that for
\textbf{v}=\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}

(2.20)

\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\cdot,\textbf{v}]\,dt\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u},\cdot,\textbf{v}]\,dt

in the operator norm.

The easiest way to do this is as follows. Suppose that

\textbf{y}\in L^{2}(0,T;H)

then we have

\|\textbf{y}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{y}\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}\,dt\right)^{1/2}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\textbf{y}|^{2}\,dx\,dt\right)^{1/2}=\|\textbf{y}\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

and thus by (
2.18
) we have

\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}\rightarrow\textbf{u}\quad\text{strongly in}\quad\textbf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T}).

It is then well known that (passing to a subsequence if necessary which we continue to denote by
m^{\prime}
)

(2.21)

\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}(x,t)\rightarrow\textbf{u}(x,t)\quad\text{almost everywhere in}\;\Omega_{T}.

Now, take an arbitrary
\textbf{y}\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
and consider

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\textbf{y},\textbf{v}]\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u},\textbf{y},\textbf{v}]\,dt\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{c(u_{m^{\prime}})_{j}(\partial_{j}y_{i})v_{i}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}|^{2}}}-\frac{cu_{j}(\partial_{j}y_{i})v_{i}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right)\,dx\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}C_{v}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{c(u_{m^{\prime}})_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}|^{2}}}-\frac{cu_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right||\partial_{j}y_{i}|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\leq 3C_{v}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{T}\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{c(u_{m^{\prime}})_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}|^{2}}}-\frac{cu_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right|^{2}\,dx\,dt\right)^{1/2}\|\textbf{y}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}

as can be easily checked by applying Hölder’s inequality. Now we can estimate

\left|\frac{c(u_{m^{\prime}})_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}|^{2}}}-\frac{cu_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right|^{2}\leq 4c^{2}

and so by (
2.21
) and the dominated convergence theorem we obtain

\lim_{m^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{T}\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{c(u_{m^{\prime}})_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}}|^{2}}}-\frac{cu_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{u}|^{2}}}\right|^{2}\,dx\,dt=0

and so (
2.20
) does indeed hold. As a result, by Lemma
1
we obtain

(2.22)

\lim_{m^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\textbf{u}_{m^{\prime}},\textbf{v}]\,dt\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u},\textbf{u},\textbf{v}]\,dt

for any
v
of the form
\textbf{v}=\psi(t)\textbf{w}_{k}
. As a result, (
2.22
) holds for any linear combination of such functions, and by a continuity argument it holds for any
\textbf{v}\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
. Hence in the limit we obtain

(2.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\int_{0}^{T}(\textbf{u}(t),\psi^{\prime}(t)\textbf{v})\,dt&+\int_{0}^{T}A[\textbf{u}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}]\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}(t),\textbf{u}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}]\,dt\\
\quad&=(\textbf{u}_{0},\textbf{v})\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\textbf{f}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}\rangle\,dt\end{split}

for any
v\in V
. Thus (
2.5
) holds in the weak sense.

The last thing which we need to verify is that
\textbf{u}(0)=\textbf{u}_{0}
. Taking (
2.5
), multiplying by
\psi(t)
and integrating, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\int_{0}^{T}(\textbf{u}(t),\psi^{\prime}(t)\textbf{v})\,dt&+\int_{0}^{T}A[\textbf{u}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}]\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}B[\textbf{u}(t),\textbf{u}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}]\,dt\\
\quad&=(\textbf{u}(0),\textbf{v})\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\textbf{f}(t),\psi(t)\textbf{v}\rangle\,dt\end{split}

which upon comparing with (
2.23
) we obtain

(\textbf{u}(0)-\textbf{u}_{0},\textbf{v})\psi(0)=0

and by choosing
\psi(t)
with
\psi(0)=1
we get

(\textbf{u}(0)-\textbf{u}_{0},\textbf{v})=0,\quad\forall\quad\textbf{v}\in V

from which (
2.6
) follows.

Using the usual identifications we can write

(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\rangle

and thus by Lemma 3.1.1 in
[
6
]
we can write (
2.5
) as

\frac{d}{dt}(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=\frac{d}{dt}\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\rangle=\langle\textbf{u}^{\prime},\textbf{v}\rangle=\langle\textbf{f}-A(\textbf{u})-B(\textbf{u}),\textbf{v}\rangle

so

\textbf{u}^{\prime}=\textbf{f}-A(\textbf{u})-B(\textbf{u})

for almost every
t
. By (
2.3
), (
2.4
), and (
2.15
) we see that

\textbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V}^{\prime})

with a bound of the form

(2.24)

\|\textbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V}^{\prime})}\leq C\left(\|\textbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T,\textbf{V}}+\|\textbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,T,\textbf{V}^{\prime}}\right)

for some constant
C
. Therefore, by Theorem 3 in Section 5.9.2 in
[
2
]
we have
\textbf{u}\in C([0,T];H)
.
∎

Now that we have obtained that a weak solution exists, we would like to obtain better regularity for the solution.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that

\textbf{u}_{0}\in\textbf{V},\quad\textbf{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2}).

\textbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{V})

\esssup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\textbf{u}(t)\|_{V}+\|\textbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{H}^{2})}+\|\textbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})}\leq C\left(\|\textbf{f}\,\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})}+\|\textbf{u}_{0}\|_{V}\right)

where the constant
C
depends only on
c,\alpha,T
and
\Omega

Proof.

If
\textbf{f}\in\textbf{L}^{2}
then using the usual identifications we can write
\langle\textbf{f},\textbf{v}\rangle=(\textbf{f},\textbf{v})

|B[\textbf{u}_{m},\textbf{u}_{m},\textbf{u}_{m}^{\prime}]|\leq\frac{C_{B}^{2}}{4\epsilon}\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}+\epsilon\|\textbf{u}_{m}^{\prime}\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2},\quad|(\textbf{f},\textbf{u}_{m}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{4\epsilon}\|\textbf{f}\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\|\textbf{u}_{m}^{\prime}\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}

Therefore, integrating we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{u}_{m}^{\prime}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}A[\textbf{u}_{m}(t),\textbf{u}_{m}(t)]\leq A[\textbf{u}_{m}(0),\textbf{u}_{m}(0)]+\int_{0}^{T}2C_{B}^{2}\|\textbf{u}_{m}(t)\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}2\|\textbf{f}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\textbf{u}_{m}(0)\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}+2C_{B}^{2}[TC_{1}(T)+C_{2}(T)]+2\int_{0}^{T}\|\textbf{f}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left(\|\textbf{u}_{0}\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}+\|\textbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})}^{2}\right)

for some constant
C_{3}
depending only on
T
and
\alpha
by the way we defined
C_{1}(T)
and
C_{2}(T)
in (
2.13
) and (
2.14
).

Therefore, since

and so combining with (

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\textbf{u}_{m}\|_{{\textbf{V}}}^{2}\leq C_{4}\left(\|\textbf{u}_{0}\|_{\textbf{V}}^{2}+\|\textbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\textbf{L}^{2})}^{2}\right)

for some constant

depending only on
T
and
\alpha
. Passing to limits, we find that

amd

with the stated bounds.

As a result, for a.e.

we have the identity

2.3.
Uniqueness of the Solution

The proof of the uniqueness of the strong solution is standard, with just some minor modifications for our particular model.

2.28

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\alpha\|\nabla\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla\textbf{U}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{C_{B}^{2}}{2\alpha}\|\textbf{U}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{1}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{4}\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha\|\nabla\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left(\frac{C_{B}^{2}}{2\alpha}+C_{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{1}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{4}\right)\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Now, since
\textbf{u}_{1}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\textbf{V})
we can use (
2.25
) to bound
C_{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\textbf{u}_{1}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{4}\leq K_{1}
for some
K_{1}
for a.e.
0\leq t\leq T
. Thus rearranging by subtracting the
\alpha\|\nabla\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}
term from both sides and multiplying by
2
we can write

\frac{d}{dt}\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq K\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}

for some
K\geq 0
for almost every
0\leq t\leq T
. Hence by Gronwall’s inequality we have

\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{Kt}\|\textbf{U}(0)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}

for
0\leq t\leq T
. But since
\textbf{U}(0)=0
we get

\|\textbf{U}(t)\|_{\textbf{L}^{2}}^{2}=0

for all
0\leq t\leq T
completing the proof of uniqueness.
∎

Recovering the Pressure

We would also like to make sense of the pressure term in (
2.2
). The pressure can be recovered (in the distribution) exactly as at the end of Section 3.3.5 in

6
]

3.
Conclusion

In this paper we suggested a modification to the usual Navier-Stokes model based on some intuition derived from special relativity. This modification led us to establishing the existence of unique global strong solutions for the incompressible equations in dimension
n=3
for any
T>0
as long as the forcing function
f
was in an appropriate space. If the model proves interesting to other mathematicians, in a future paper we will investigate the higher regularity of the solutions, which should not be too difficult as the solutions should be amenable to the standard techniques.

We comment that though justifiable, the introduction of the modified term in our equations could be considered somewhat ad-hoc, though no more so than in
[
4
]
. As mentioned, in that paper, and others, the modifications are introduced by considering Brownian motion, while here we introduced them as a consequence of special and general relativity. In a future paper we will study what happens when we derive the equations by directly using some spacial relativistic principles. One possibility is replacing the mass term in Newton’s law by the special relativistic mass and studying the consequences of this. This might lead to a model which should be more intuitively palatable for some. However, the fact that incompressibility has no real place in special relativity might produce interesting consequences.

We consider the difference

\frac{\tilde{\textbf{y}}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{\textbf{y}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}

by analyzing this vector component wise. In general, this could be an
n
-vector, though of course we are interested in the case
n=3
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}+\frac{y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}-y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}+y_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}\right).

Next we see

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}-\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}\right)\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}+\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}+\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\textbf{y}|^{2}-|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}(\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}+\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(y_{k}+\tilde{y}_{k})(y_{k}-\tilde{y}_{k})}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}(\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}+\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}})}

and therefore

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}-\frac{y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\tilde{y}_{j}-y_{j}}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}}\right|+|y_{j}|\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(|y_{k}|+|\tilde{y}_{k}|)|y_{k}-\tilde{y}_{k}|}{\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}(\sqrt{c^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2}}+\sqrt{c^{2}+|\tilde{\textbf{y}}|^{2}})}

and so we can take
C_{n}=n(n+1)

C_{3}=12
