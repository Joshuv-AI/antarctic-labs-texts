Limited depth bandit-based strategy for Monte Carlo planning in continuous action spaces
Ricardo Quinteiro, Francisco S. Melo, Pedro A. Santos

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of optimal control using search trees. We start by considering multi-armed bandit problems with continuous action spaces and propose LD-HOO, a limited depth variant of the hierarchical optimistic optimization (HOO) algorithm. We provide a regret analysis for LD-HOO and show that, asymptotically, our algorithm exhibits the same cumulative regret as the original HOO while being faster and more memory efficient. We then propose a Monte Carlo tree search algorithm based on LD-HOO for optimal control problems and illustrate the resulting approach’s application in several optimal control problems.

Introduction

Recent years have witnessed remarkable successes of artificial intelligence, brought about by the effective combination of powerful machine learning algorithms based on neural networks and efficient planning approaches based on Monte Carlo tree search. Perhaps the most celebrated is AlphaGo and its successors
[
15
,
17
,
16
,
14
]
, which could attain super-human proficiency in several complex games such as chess, Go, or Shogi. Even before AlphaGo, Monte Carlo tree search approaches were already widely used in complex planning problems due to their efficiency, mild requirements, and robust theoretical guarantees
[
3
]
.

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) is a family of online planning methods initially designed to solve sequential decision problems such as Markov decision problems
[
7
,
6
]
. At each step
t
, the decision-maker builds a search tree with its current state,
s_{t}
, as the root node. Its children correspond to the actions available to the agent at
s_{t}
, and the algorithm proceeds by estimating a value for each such action by using
rollouts
and
efficient exploration
. Rollouts provide empirical estimates for the nodes’ values in the tree, while efficient exploration directs tree expansion towards the more promising regions of the state space (see Fig.
1
for an illustration).

Figure 1
:
Illustration of MCTS. An adequate exploration policy is used to determine the next node selected for expansion. Rollouts (i.e., sampled trajectories) from the expanded nodes are used to build estimates about how good the nodes are.

In this paper, we extend the idea behind MCTS to control problems with a continuous action space. The main challenge lies in ensuring efficient exploration. Standard MCTS approaches assume that the decision-maker has only a finite set of possible actions available and typically use multi-armed bandit algorithms to guide exploration. Hence, our first contribution is a novel multi-armed bandit algorithm for continuous action spaces.

Several works have addressed multi-armed bandits with continuous action spaces
[
11
]
. Most such approaches consider an action space that corresponds to a hypercube in
\mathds{R}^{P}
, i.e., a set

\mathcal{X}=\prod_{p=1}^{P}[a_{p},b_{p}],\qquad\text{for $a_{p},b_{p}\in\mathds{R}^{P}$.}

Multi-armed bandit problems in continuous action settings generally apply some form of
hierarchical partitioning

[
11
]
. Hierarchical partitioning successively splits
\mathcal{X}
into smaller hypercubes in such a way that the resulting partition can be represented as a tree (see Fig.
2
). Examples of such algorithms include
deterministic optimistic optimization
(DOO)
[
10
]
,
hierarchical optimistic optimization
(HOO)
[
5
]
,
stochastic simultaneous optimistic optimization
(StoSOO)
[
11
]
,
truncated hierarchical optimistic optimization
(T-HOO)
[
4
]
, or
polynomial hierarchical optimistic optimization
(Poly-HOO)
[
9
]
.

Figure 2
:
Hierarchical partitioning. The set
\mathcal{X}
is successively split in uniform subsets, and the resulting partition can be represented as a
K
-ary tree. The sets in level
h
are referred as “cells” and denoted as
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
, with
0\leq i\leq K^{h}-1
. Each set
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
is represented by a specific element
x_{h,i}
, referred as the
center
of
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
.

All the methods above also build on the principle of
optimism in the face of uncertainty
, widely used in the multi-armed bandit literature
[
1
]
and MCTS. The most adequate for the settings considered in this paper are HOO and its truncated and polynomial variants. Unlike DOO, HOO admits stochastic rewards. Unlike StoSOO, it is an anytime algorithm, a desirable property when used within MCTS. Unfortunately, HOO exhibits quadratic runtime, which is inconvenient for MCTS. Poly-HOO and T-HOO are faster, but exhibit a worse performance in terms of cumulative regret.

This paper contributes LD-HOO, a modified version of HOO that is computationally faster but retains HOO’s guarantees in terms of cumulative regret. We also contribute an analysis of LD-HOO in terms of cumulative regret. Finally, we propose LD-HOOT (LD-HOO for trees), a novel MCTS algorithm for problems with continuous action spaces that uses LD-HOO for efficient exploration. We illustrate the application of LD-HOOT in several well-known optimal control problems.

Background

This section provides an overview of key concepts regarding the decision problems addressed in this paper and sets the notation used throughout.

2.1
The (stochastic) multi-armed bandit problem

We consider the problem of a decision maker that must, at each time step
t=1,2,\ldots
, select a (possibly random) action
X_{t}
from a set
\mathcal{X}
of possible actions. Upon selecting an action
X_{t}=x
, the decision maker receives a (possibly random) reward
Y_{t}
with mean value
f(x)
. The process then repeats.

We assume that
Y_{t}\in[0,1]
and the distribution governing
Y_{t}
is stationary and independent of the past given the action
X_{t}
. The function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathds{R}
is unknown to the decision maker. The goal of the decision maker is to select the actions
X_{t},t=1,2,\ldots
, to maximize the total reward received.

A
policy
is a sequence
\pi=\left\{\pi_{t},\;t=1,2,\ldots\right\}
, where each
\pi_{t}
maps the history of the agent up to time step
t
to a distribution over
\mathcal{X}
. The
cumulative regret
of a policy
\pi
at time step
n
is defined as

\hat{R}_{n}=nf(x^{*})-\sum_{t=1}^{n}Y_{t},

where
x^{*}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
, and the
cumulative pseudo-regret
is

R_{n}=nf(x^{*})-\sum_{t=1}^{n}f(x_{t}).

The cumulative regret measures how much the agent’s policy lost in terms of cumulative reward compared with the optimal action.

2.2
Markov decision problems

Markov decision problems (MDPs) extend multi-armed bandit problems by considering that the reward at each time step
t
,
Y_{t}
, is no longer independent of the past given only the action
X_{t}
. In an MDP, a decision-maker observes, at each time step
t=1,2,\ldots
, the
state
of a Markov chain
\left\{S_{t},t=1,2,\ldots\right\}
, where each
S_{t}
takes values in some set
\mathcal{S}
. It must then select a (possibly random) action
X_{t}
from a set
\mathcal{X}
of possible actions. Upon selecting an action
X_{t}=x
when the chain is in state
S_{t}=s
, the decision maker receives a (possibly random) reward
Y_{t}
with mean value
f(s,x)
. The Markov chain then transitions to a new state
S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}
according to the transition probabilities

P(s,x,s^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}\left[S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}\mid S_{t}=s,X_{t}=x\right].

As before, we assume that the distribution governing
Y_{t}
is stationary and independent of the past given
X_{t}
and
S_{t}
. As before, the function
f
is unknown to the learner. The goal of the decision maker is to select the actions
X_{t},t=1,\ldots
, to maximize the value

J=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}f(S_{t},X_{t})\right],

(1)

where
\gamma
is a discount factor. MDPs are stochastic optimal control problems and find many applications in artificial intelligence, operations research and optimal control
[
13
]
.

3
Limited Depth Hierarchical Optimistic Optimization

This section introduces our first contribution, the LD-HOO algorithm. In terms of structure, LD-HOO is similar to HOO
[
5
]
. LD-HOO also uses hierarchical partitioning to successively build a binary tree like the one depicted in Fig.
2
. The key difference is that, in LD-HOO, the depth of the tree depth is limited to a maximum depth,
\hat{H}
. By limiting the tree’s depth, we introduce significant computational savings without sacrificing performance.

At a very high-level, LD-HOO proceeds as follows. At each time step
t
, LD-HOO navigates the current partition tree,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, from the root to some leaf node
X_{h_{t},i_{t}}
using an “optimistic” path (we make this notion precise ahead). It then selects an arbitrary action
x_{t}\in X_{h_{t},i_{t}}
and observes the corresponding reward,
y_{t}
. If
h_{t}<\hat{H}
the node
X_{h_{t},i_{t}}
is expanded—i.e., its children nodes are added to the tree. Finally, the value of the nodes in the tree is updated accordingly.

To properly formalize the LD-HOO algorithm, we introduce some auxiliary notation. We denote by
\mathcal{T}_{t}
the partition tree at time step
t
. The sets at each level
h
of the tree are generally referred as “cells”, and denoted as
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
, with
0\leq i\leq 2^{h}-1
. Each cell
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
originates, in level
h+1
, two children cells,
\mathcal{X}_{h+1,2i-1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{h+1,2i}
. We denote by
\mathcal{C}(h,i)
the children of
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}(h,i)=\left\{(h+1,2i-1),(h+1,2i)\right\},

and by

T_{h,i}(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}\left[X_{\tau}\in\mathcal{X}_{h,i}\right]

the number of times that an action from
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
was selected up to time step
t
. The estimated reward associated with a node
(h,i)
at time step
t
can be computed as

\hat{\mu}_{h,i}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{h,i}(t)}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}y_{\tau}\mathbb{I}\left[X_{\tau}\in\mathcal{X}_{h,i}\right].

Finally, we write
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}_{t})
to denote the set of leaf nodes of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

The “optimism in the face of uncertainty” principle comes into play by associating, with each node
(h,i)
in the tree, an upper confidence bound
b_{h,i}
that is used to guide the traversal of the tree. The computation of this upper confidence bound relies on two key assumptions.

The first assumption concerns the existence of a semi-metric in
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 1
.

The action space
\mathcal{X}
is equipped with a semi-metric
{\ell\!:\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathds{R}}
.

Recall that
\ell
is a semi-metric if, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\ell(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
,
\ell(x,x^{\prime})=\ell(x^{\prime},x)
and
\ell(x,x^{\prime})=0
if and only if
x=x^{\prime}
. We can now define the diameter of a set
U\subset\mathcal{X}
as

\diam(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in U}\ell(x,x^{\prime}).

An
\ell
-open ball centered around
x\in\mathcal{X}
with radius
\varepsilon
is defined as

\mathcal{B}(x,\varepsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\ell(x,x^{\prime})<\varepsilon\right\}.

The second assumption is concerned with hierarchical partitioning.

Assumption 2
.

There are constants
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}>0
and
0<\rho<1
such that, for all
h\geq 0
and all
i\in\left\{0,\ldots,2^{h}-1\right\}
, there is
x_{h,i}\in\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
such that

\displaystyle\diam(\mathcal{X}_{h,i})\leq\nu_{1}\rho^{h},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x_{h,i},\nu_{2}\rho^{h})\subset\mathcal{X}_{h,i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x_{h,i},\nu_{2}\rho^{h})\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{h,j},\nu_{2}\rho^{h})=\emptyset,\qquad\text{for $i\neq j$.}

Assumption
2
ensures that the construction of the hierarchical partition and the semi-metric
\ell
are such that the diameter of the cells in level
h
decreases uniformly, and a suitable center can be found for each cell. The choice of partitioning method thus suggests a semi-metric, while the choice of
\ell
limits the way that the partition can be done
[
4
]
.

From the above assumptions we can now compute an upper confidence bound for the values of
f
in a cell
(h,i)
of the tree at time step
t
as

u_{h,i}(t)=\hat{\mu}_{h,i}(t)+\sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{T_{h,i}(t)}}+\nu_{1}\rho^{h},

(2)

where
\nu_{1}
and
\rho
are the constants from Assumption
2
. It is interesting to note the similarity between the bound in (
2
) and the upper confidence bound in the well-known UCB1 algorithm
[
1
]
. The additional term,
\nu_{1}\rho^{h}
, accounts for the variation of
f
within
\mathcal{X}_{h,i}
.

The actual value used to traverse the tree is referred as the
b
-value, and is computed from the upper confidence bound in (
2
) as

b_{h,i}(t)=\min\left\{u_{h,i}(t),\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}(h,i)}b_{c}(t)\right\},

(3)

where the
b
-value of a non-visited node is set to
+\infty
. These values can be computed recursively from the leaves to the root.

3.1
Regret analysis for LD-HOO

In this section we provide a regret analysis for LD-HOO. Our analysis closely follows that of Bubeck et al.
[
4
]
, adapted to take into consideration the fact that LD-HOO considers limited-depth partition trees.

Our analysis requires one additional assumption regarding the behavior of the mean payoff function,
f
, around its global maximizer,
x^{*}
.

Assumption 3
.

The mean payoff function,
f
, is such that, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

f(x^{*})-f(x^{\prime})\leq f(x^{*})-f(x)+\max\left\{f(x^{*})-f(x),\ell(x,x^{\prime})\right\}.

Assumption
3
states that there is no sudden decrease in the mean payoff function around
x^{*}
. Also, setting
x=x^{*}
, Assumption
3
actually establishes
f
as locally Lipschitz around
x^{*}

[
5
]
. The set of
\varepsilon
-optimal actions
,
\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon}
, is defined as

\mathcal{X}_{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid f(x)\geq f(x^{*})-\varepsilon\right\}.

We have the following definition.

Definition 1

(
\varepsilon
-packing number)
.

Given a set
\mathcal{X}
with a semi-metric
\ell
, the
\varepsilon
-packing number of
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
\ell
is denoted as
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\ell,\varepsilon)
and defined as the largest integer
K
for which there are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathcal{B}(x_{k},\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{B}(x_{k},\varepsilon)\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{k^{\prime}},\varepsilon)=\emptyset
, for all
k,k^{\prime}\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
.

In other words,
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\ell,\varepsilon)
is the maximum number of disjoint
\varepsilon
-balls contained in
\mathcal{X}
. We now introduce the notion of near-optimality dimension, which describes the size of the sets
\mathcal{X}_{c\varepsilon}
as a function of
\varepsilon
.

Definition 2

(Near-optimality dimension)
.

Given constants
c>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, the
(c,\varepsilon_{0})
-near-optimality dimension
of
f
with respect to
\ell
is defined as

d\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\inf\left\{\delta\geq 0\mid\exists C>0\text{ s.t. }\forall\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X}_{c\varepsilon},\ell,\varepsilon)\leq C\varepsilon^{-\delta}\right\},

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
.

In other words
d
is the smallest value such that the maximum number of disjoint
\varepsilon
-balls that “fit” in
\mathcal{X}_{c\varepsilon}
is no larger than a
C\varepsilon^{-d}
. A small near-optimality dimension implies that there is a small number of neighborhoods where the value of
f
is close to its maximum.

We now introduce our main result.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that Assumptions
1
through
3
are satisfied for semi-metric
\ell
and constants
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{2}
and
\rho
. Let
d<+\infty
be the
(4\nu_{1}/\nu_{2},\nu_{2})
-near-optimality dimension of
f
with respect to
\ell
. Then, for all horizons
n>0
and depth limit
\hat{H}>0
, the expected cumulative regret of LD-HOO verifies:

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]\in O\left(\rho^{\hat{H}}n+\rho^{-\hat{H}(d+1)}\log n+\hat{H}\max\{\rho^{\hat{H}(1-d)},\hat{H}\}\right).

Due to space limitations, we provide only a sketch of the proof, and refer to the supplementary material for a complete proof.

Proof sketch.

The proof proceeds in four steps. We start by providing a bound on the number of times that each “suboptimal” node in the tree is selected. In a second step we break down the value of
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]
into three components. In the third step we show that two of the regret components are naturally bounded as a consequence of our assumptions, and the third depends on the number of visits to the nodes in the tree (which was bounded in the first step). The fourth step wraps the proof by combining the intermediate results into a final regret bound.

First step.

For a node
(h,i)
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
f_{h,i}^{*}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{h,i}}f(x)
, and define the set

\mathcal{I}_{h}=\left\{(h,i)\mid f^{*}_{h,i}\geq f(x^{*})-2\nu_{1}\rho^{h}\right\},

i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{h}
is the set of
2\nu_{1}\rho^{h}
-optimal nodes at depth
h
. Let
\mathcal{J}_{h}
be the set of nodes at depth
h
that are not in
\mathcal{I}_{h}
but whose parents are in
\mathcal{I}_{h-1}
. We can bound the expected number of times that an action from a node
(h,i)\in\mathcal{J}_{h}
is selected up to time step
n
as

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{h,i}(n)\right]\leq\frac{8\log n}{\nu_{1}^{2}\rho^{2h}}+2(\hat{H}+1).

(4)

Furthermore, we can bound the number of nodes in
\mathcal{I}_{h}
as

\left\lvert\mathcal{I}_{h}\right\rvert\leq C(\nu_{2}\rho^{h})^{-d}.

(5)

Second step.

We partition the nodes of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
into three disjoint sets,
\mathcal{T}^{1}
,
\mathcal{T}^{2}
, and
\mathcal{T}^{3}
, such that
\mathcal{T}^{1}\cup\mathcal{T}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}^{3}=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. In particular,

•

\mathcal{T}^{1}
is the set of nodes at depth
\hat{H}
that are
2\nu_{1}\rho^{\hat{H}}
-optimal, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}^{1}=\mathcal{I}_{\hat{H}}
.

•

\mathcal{T}^{2}
is the sets of all
2\nu_{1}\rho^{h}
-optimal nodes, for all depths
h\neq\hat{H}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}^{2}=\cup_{h=0}^{\hat{H}-1}\mathcal{I}_{h}
.

•

\mathcal{T}^{3}
is the set set of all remaining nodes, i.e.,
\cup_{h=1}^{\hat{H}}\mathcal{J}_{h}
and corresponding descendant nodes.

We now define the regret
R_{n,k}
of playing actions in
\mathcal{T}^{k},k=1,2,3
, as

R_{n,k}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(f(x^{*})-f(X_{t}))\mathbb{I}\left[(H_{t},I_{t})\in\mathcal{T}^{k}\right]

(6)

where
f(X_{t})
is the expected reward collected at time step
t
given the action
X_{t}
and
(H_{t},I_{t})
is the (random) node selected in that same time step. It follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,1}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,3}\right].

(7)

Third step.

We now bound each of the terms in (
7
) to establish a bound for the total cumulative regret after
n
rounds.

Starting with
R_{n,1}
, in the worst case we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,1}\right]\leq 4n\nu_{1}\rho^{\hat{H}}.

(8)

In turn, the nodes
(h,i)\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
are
2\nu_{1}\rho^{h}
-optimal. This means that

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,2}\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}4\nu_{1}\rho^{H_{t}}\mathbb{I}\left[(H_{t},I_{t})\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\right]

However, each node
(h,i)\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
is selected only once and then expanded—only nodes at depth
\hat{H}
can be selected multiple times. As such, we can use the bound in (
5
) to get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,2}\right]\leq\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}-1}4\nu_{1}\rho^{h}\left\lvert\mathcal{I}_{h}\right\rvert\leq 4C\nu_{1}\nu_{2}^{-d}\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}-1}\rho^{h(1-d)}

(9)

Finally, let us consider the nodes in
\mathcal{T}^{3}
. Each node
(h,i)\in\mathcal{J}_{h}
is the child of a node in
\mathcal{I}_{h-1}
and, as such,
f(x^{*})-f^{*}_{h,i}\leq 4\nu_{1}\rho^{h-1}
. Additionally,
\left\lvert\mathcal{J}_{h}\right\rvert\leq 2\left\lvert\mathcal{I}_{h-1}\right\rvert
, with equality if
\mathcal{I}_{h-1}
was fully expanded and all the direct children of the nodes
(h-1,i)\in\mathcal{I}_{h}
are in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Using (
4
), we can now bound
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,3}\right]
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n,3}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{\hat{H}}4\nu_{1}\rho^{h-1}\sum_{(h,i)\in\mathcal{J}_{h}}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{h,i}(n)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{\hat{H}}4\nu_{1}\rho^{h-1}\left\lvert\mathcal{J}_{h}\right\rvert\left(\frac{8\log n}{\nu_{1}^{2}\rho^{2h}}+2(\hat{H}+1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{\hat{H}}8\nu_{1}\rho^{h-1}\left\lvert\mathcal{I}_{h-1}\right\rvert\left(\frac{8\log n}{\nu_{1}^{2}\rho^{2h}}+2(\hat{H}+1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 8C\nu_{1}\nu_{2}^{-d}\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}-1}\rho^{h(1-d)}\left(\frac{8\log n}{\nu_{1}^{2}\rho^{2h}}+2(\hat{H}+1)\right).

(10)

Fourth step.

We now combine the bounds (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) to get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]\leq 4n\nu_{1}\rho^{\hat{H}}+4C\nu_{1}\nu_{2}^{-d}\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}-1}\rho^{h(1-d)}\left(\frac{8\log n}{\nu_{1}^{2}\rho^{2h}}+4\hat{H}+5\right)

(11)

Rewriting (
11
) using asymptotic notation, we finally get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]\in O\left(n\rho^{\hat{H}}+\log n\rho^{-\hat{H}(1+d)}+\hat{H}\max\{\rho^{\hat{H}(1-d)},\hat{H}\}\right),

and the proof is complete.∎∎

We get the following corollary to Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 1

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, selecting
\hat{H}
so that

\rho^{\hat{H}}=c\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{d+2}},

for some constant
c>0
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{n}\right]\in O\left(n^{\frac{d+1}{d+2}}(\log n)^{\frac{1}{d+2}}\right).

(12)

Corollary
1
establishes that, through an adequate choice of the maximum depth
\hat{H}
, the bound on the cumulative regret of LD-HOO matches that of HOO
[
4
]
. In the continuation, we establish that both the running time and the size of the partition tree of LD-HOO are better than those of HOO.

3.2
Time and space complexity of LD-HOO

The number of nodes in the partition tree generated by LD-HOO is upper bounded by

\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}}2^{h}\in O(2^{\hat{H}}).

However, under the conditions of Corollary
1
,
\hat{H}\in O(\log n)
. As such, the space complexity of LD-HOO is
O(n^{\log 2})
. In contrast, the number of nodes in a partition tree generated by HOO is
O(n)
. Thus, the space complexity of LD-HOO is asymptotically better than HOO’s.

Similarly, the running time of LD-HOO is proportional to the number of updates to the
b
-values. After
n
iterations, the total amount of updates is, at most,
n
times the number of nodes in the tree. As such, the time complexity of LD-HOO is
O(n^{1+\log 2})
, against the
O(n^{2})
time of HOO
[
4
]
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we contributed LD-HOO, a novel algorithm for stochastic multi-armed bandit problems with a continuous action spaces. We provided a regret analysis for LD-HOO and establish that, under mild conditions, LD-HOO exhibits an asymptotic regret bound similar to that of other algorithms in the literature, while incurring in smaller computational costs. Our analysis was then complemented by an empirical study, in which we show that LD-HOO generally outperforms similar algorithms.

We then propose LD-HOOT, a Monte Carlo tree search algorithm that uses LD-HOO to drive the expansion of the search tree. We tested LD-HOOT in several optimal control problems from the literature, and the results show that LD-HOO can be successfully used within MCTS to address optimal control problems with continuous actions spaces. Additionally, our results show that LD-HOOT is competitive with existing algorithms in terms of performance, while being computationally more efficient.

An interesting line for future research is to build on the results presented herein—namely Theorem
3.1
—to establish the consistence of LD-HOOT in the sense that the probability of selecting the optimal action can be made to converge to 1 as the number of samples grows to infinity. Such results exist for MCTS with finite action spaces
[
7
]
, and we conjecture that a similar line of proof could be used to establish similar guarantees for LD-HOOT.
