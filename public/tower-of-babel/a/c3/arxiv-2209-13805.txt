Abstract
. We adapt the abstract concepts of abelianness and centrality of universal algebra to the context of inverse semigroups. We characterize abelian and central congruences in terms of the corresponding congruence pairs. We relate centrality to conjugation in inverse semigroups. Subsequently we prove that solvable and nilpotent inverse semigroups are groups.

Primary: 20M18; Secondary: 20F19, 08A30

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Abstract commutator theory of universal algebra
[
5
]
was developed to capture, among other things, the concepts of abelianness and centrality, and consequently,
solvability and nilpotence for general algebraic structures. Commutator theory proved immensely useful in solving various problems of universal algebra, equational logic
and combinatorics of functions; see
[
12
]
for a list of applications and references.

Interpreting the abstract concepts of commutator theory in a concrete class is, in general, not an easy task. In groups, it is not difficult to show that the universal algebraic notions coincide with the classical ones. In less familiar structures like loops (quasigroups with identity elements), this is not the case: universal algebraic nilpotence coincides with the classical central nilpotence, but classical and universal algebraic solvability are different
[
27
,
28
]
. Another example of a successful adaptation of commutator theory is the class of racks and quandles
[
4
]
.

The ideas surrounding commutator theory, such as term conditions, were first adapted to semigroup theory by McKenzie
[
14
]
, Taylor
[
29
]
and Warne
[
30
]
. The latter showed, for example, that an abelian regular semigroup is a subsemigroup of a rectangular group with commutative group component. We note that although in semigroup theory, the word “abelian” is often used as a synonym for “commutative” (as in group theory), in this paper we will only use “abelian” in the universal algebra sense. Commutator theory itself has recently been applied to Rees matrix semigroups
[
24
]
, inverse semigroups
[
25
]
and regular semigroups
[
26
]
.

In the present paper, we focus on inverse semigroups
[
10
,
21
]
. Inverse semigroups include groups and semilattices as special cases, and as advocated in
[
10
]
, they are the natural algebraic framework for the study of partial symmetries. They have applications in a variety of areas, such as logic and
\lambda
-calculus, operator algebras
[
19
]
, automata theory and computer science. It is therefore rather natural to see what exactly commutator theory can bring to inverse semigroups.

Commutator theory has strongest properties for congruence modular varieties; for instance, this assumption is used to prove commutativity of the commutator. Inverse semigroups possess a weak difference term
[
11
]
, but do not form a congruence modular variety, hence one cannot expect the commutator to shine in its full strength. Therefore we specialize to the derived notions of abelianness and centrality. We characterize abelian and central congruences (Theorem
1.1
), we describe the center of an inverse semigroup (Theorem
1.2
), and subsequently characterize solvable / nilpotent inverse semigroups as solvable / nilpotent groups (Theorem
1.3
).

Our three main theorems are formulated more precisely in the next subsection, and their proof occupies most of the paper. Alternative approaches to nilpotence and solvability in inverse semigroups can be found in
[
8
,
13
,
15
,
22
]
; we will discuss these in Section
7
.

1.2.
Main results

Our first theorem is a characterization of abelian and central congruences in terms of their corresponding congruence pairs.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. Then

(1)

abelian congruences of
S
correspond to congruence pairs
(N,0_{E(S)})
such that
N
is a commutative normal inverse subsemigroup of
S
.

(2)

central congruences of
S
correspond to congruence pairs
(N,0_{E(S)})
such that
N
is a normal inverse subsemigroup of
S
contained in

Z(S)=\{a\in S\mid axa^{-1}a=aa^{-1}xa,\ \forall x\in S\}\,.

Consequently,
(Z(S),0_{E(S)})
corresponds to the largest central congruence, called (in universal algebra) the
center
.

Example
.

If
S
is a group, then
Z(S)
is the classical group-theoretic center. If
S
is a semilattice, then the center is trivial, i.e.,
Z(S)=E(S)
. The center of the symmetric inverse semigroup
\mathcal{I}(X)
of partial bijections on a set
X
is also trivial.

There is a remarkable analogy to the group theoretic center. The center of a group can be described in terms of conjugation.
Indeed, the center of a group
G
, although usually just defined as the set of elements that commute with all elements, turns out to be precisely the kernel of the homomorphism
G\to\mathrm{Aut}(G);\quad g\mapsto\phi_{g}
, where
\phi_{g}
is the inner automorphism
x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
.

In a semigroup
S
, the word “center” usually refers to the subsemigroup
C(S)=\{a\in S\mid ax=xa\,\forall x\in S\}
; see Section
7
for how this is related to our
Z(S)
. A better analogy to the group-theoretic center is based on conjugation. As an equivalence relation, conjugation in inverse semigroups is thoroughly studied in
[
3
]
. Here we think of it as defining mappings on inverse semigroups. In fact, conjugation by a fixed element
g
defines two closely related maps. First, it defines the total transformation
\psi_{g}:S\to S;\quad x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
. Second, and more closely analogous to the group case, is the partial mapping
\phi_{g}:g^{-1}Sg\to gSg^{-1}
, which turns out to be a partial automorphism, i.e., an isomorphism of inverse subsemigroups. As we will see, the center of an inverse semigroup
S
is both the kernel (relation) of the homomorphism

\Psi:S\to T_{S};\quad g\mapsto\psi_{g}

into the semigroup
T_{S}
of all total transformations of
S
, and also the kernel of the homomorphism

\Phi:S\to\mathcal{\mathrm{PAut}}(S);\quad g\mapsto\phi_{g}

into the inverse semigroup
\mathrm{PAut}(S)
of partial automorphisms of
S
. (Details are in Section
3
.)

Preston
[
23
]
was the first to study the homomorphism
\Phi:S\to\mathrm{PAut}(S)
and to describe its corresponding congruence pair. Mel’nik
[
16
]
and Petrich
[
20
]
later proved similar results independently, the latter as part of his larger theory of conjugate extensions. Petrich’s paper does not cite Preston’s, but in his later book
[
21
]
, Petrich credits both Preston and Mel’nik. Petrich called our
Z(S)
the
metacenter
of
S
; Mel’nik had earlier called it the
quasicenter
.

The center in our (universal algebra) sense can also be characterized syntactically, in analogy with the standard group-theoretical description. All descriptions are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. The following congruences are equal:

(1)

the center of
S
in the sense of
[
5
]
,

(2)

\ker(\Psi)
,

(3)

\ker(\Phi)
,

(4)

\greenH\,\cap\,\{(a,b)\in S\times S\mid axb=bxa,\ \forall x\in S\}
.

The corresponding congruence pair is
(Z(S),0_{E(S)})
.

As a consequence of our description of abelian and central congruences we characterize solvable and nilpotent (in the sense of univeral algebra) inverse semigroups in Section
6
. Sadly, they are groups.

Theorem 1.3
.

An inverse semigroup is nilpotent (resp. solvable) if and only if it is a nilpotent (resp. solvable) group.

Theorem
1.3
was independently found by Radović and Mudrinski
[
25
]
using a completely different methodology.

2.
Facts about inverse semigroups

2.1.
Basic facts

In the present section, we summarize basic facts about inverse semigroups we need in out proofs. For details, we refer to the textbooks
[
6
,
21
]
.

A semigroup
S
is
regular
if each element
x\in S
has an
inverse

x^{\prime}\in S
satisfying
xx^{\prime}x=x
and
x^{\prime}xx^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
. In case
each element
x
has a unique inverse
x^{-1}
,
S
is said to be an
inverse semigroup
. Equivalently, a semigroup
S
is inverse if and only if it is regular and the set
E(S)=\{e\in S\mid s^{2}=s\}
of idempotents is a commutative subsemigroup, i.e. a semilattice.

As is customary, we will view an inverse semigroup
S
as an algebraic structure
(S,\cdot,{}^{-1})
with both a binary operation and a unary operation. As such, inverse semigroups also satisfy the identities
(x^{-1})^{-1}=x
and
(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}
. Subsemigroups of inverse semigroups which are closed under inversion
x\mapsto x^{-1}
are called
inverse subsemigroups
.

In semigroups, Green’s relation
\greenL
is defined by
a\,\greenL\,b
if and only if
a=b
or
a=ub
and
b=va
for some
u,v\in S
. Green’s relation
\greenR
is defined dually, and Green’s relation
\greenH=\greenL\cap\greenR
. The relation
\greenL
is a right congruence, that is,
a\,\greenL\,b
implies
ax\,\greenL\,bx
for all
x
. Dually,
\greenR
is a left congruence. In an inverse semigroup, the
\greenL
-class containing an element
a
has a unique idempotent, namely
a^{-1}a
. Similarly, the
\greenR
-class containing
a
has a unique idempotent, namely
aa^{-1}
. In particular,
\greenL
and
\greenR
can be determined equationally:
a\,\greenL\,b
if and only if
a^{-1}a=b^{-1}b
and
a\,\greenR\,b
if and only if
aa^{-1}=bb^{-1}
. Another consequence is that an inverse semigroup
S
is a group if and only if
|E(S)|=1
.

2.2.
Congruences

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. An equivalence
\alpha
on
S
is called a
congruence
of
S
if it is invariant with respect to multiplication and inversion, that is, it is an inverse subsemigroup of
S\times S
. For any homomorphism
\phi:S\to T
of inverse semigroups,
\ker(\phi)=\{(a,b)\in S\times S\mid\phi(a)=\phi(b)\}
is a congruence.

The following is a version of Lallement’s Lemma (
[
6
]
, Lem. 2.2.4), which will be suitable for our purposes.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup and let
\phi:S\to T
be a semigroup homomorphism into a semigroup
T
. Then
\mathrm{img}(\phi)
is an inverse semigroup and
\phi
preserves inverses. Consequently,
\ker(\phi)
is a congruence.

Proof.

For every
a\in S
, we have
\phi(a)\phi(a^{-1})\phi(a)=\phi(aa^{-1}a)=\phi(a)
; similarly,
\phi(a^{-1})\phi(a)\phi(a^{-1})=\phi(a^{-1})
.
Thus
\mathrm{img}(\phi)
is regular. If
\phi(a)\in\mathrm{img}(\phi)
is an idempotent, set
h=aa^{-2}a
. Then
hh=aa^{-2}a^{2}a^{-2}a=aa^{-2}a=h
and
\phi(h)=\phi(a)\phi(a^{-2})\phi(a)=\phi(a)^{2}\phi(a^{-2})\phi(a)^{2}=\phi(a^{2}a^{-2}a^{2})=\phi(a^{2})=\phi(a)^{2}=\phi(a)
. Thus every idempotent in
\mathrm{img}(\phi)
is the image of an idempotent in
S
. For
e,f\in E(S)
,
\phi(e)\phi(f)=\phi(ef)=\phi(fe)=\phi(f)\phi(e)
, which shows that idempotents in
\mathrm{img}(\phi)
commute. Therefore
\mathrm{img}(\phi)
is an inverse semigroup. Since
\phi(a^{-1})
is an inverse of
\phi(a)
, it must be the unique inverse, that is,
\phi(a)^{-1}=\phi(a^{-1})
for all
a\in S
. For the remaining assertion, it is enough to show that
(a,b)\in\ker(\phi)
implies
(a^{-1},b^{-1})\in\ker(\phi)
. This follows from
\phi(a^{-1})=\phi(a)^{-1}=\phi(b)^{-1}=\phi(b^{-1})
.
∎

An inverse subsemigroup
N
of an inverse semigroup
S
is said to be
normal
if it is stable under conjugation (
g^{-1}Ng\subseteq N
for all
g\in S
) and full (
E(S)\subseteq N
).

Congruences of an inverse semigroup
S
are in one-to-one correspondence with
congruence pairs

(N,\varepsilon)
where
N
is a normal inverse subsemigroup of
S
,
\varepsilon
is a congruence of
E(S)
and

(CP1)

for every
a\in S
and
e\in E(S)
, if
ae\in N
and
e\,\varepsilon\,a^{-1}a
then
a\in N
;

(CP2)

for every
a\in N
and
e\in E(S)
,
a^{-1}ea\,\varepsilon\,a^{-1}ae
.

For a congruence
\alpha
of
S
, the corresponding pair is
(N_{\alpha},\mathrm{tr}(\alpha))
where

N_{\alpha}
is the
kernel
of
\alpha
, defined as the union of all those
\alpha
-classes which contain idempotents;
and
\mathrm{tr}(\alpha)
is the
trace
of
\alpha
, defined as
\alpha|_{E(S)}
, the restriction of
\alpha
to
E(S)
.

In the other direction, for a congruence pair
(N,\varepsilon)
, the corresponding congruence
\alpha
is given by
a\,\alpha\,b
if and only if

ab^{-1}\in N
and
aa^{-1}\,\varepsilon\,bb^{-1}
.

The smallest and the largest congruences
0_{S}=\mathrm{id}_{S}
,
1_{S}=S\times S
correspond to congruence pairs
(E(S),0_{E(S)})
and
(S,1_{E(S)})
, respectively.
In case
\mathrm{tr}(\alpha)=0_{E(S)}
,
\alpha
is said to be
idempotent-separating
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
18
]
, Prop. 2)
.

Let
N
be a normal inverse subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
S
. Then
(N,0_{E(S)})
is a congruence pair if and only if every element of
N
commutes with every element of
E(S)
.

Proof.

For idempotent-separating congruences, condition (CP1) is always satisfied, and condition (CP2) says that
a^{-1}ea=a^{-1}ae
for every
e\in E(S)
and
a\in N
. This is certainly true if every element of
N
commutes with every
e\in E(S)
. Conversely, multiplying by
a
on the left and using commutativity of
E(S)
, we obtain
ae=a\underline{a^{-1}ae}=aa^{-1}ea=eaa^{-1}a=ea
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a congruence on an inverse semigroup
S
. Then
\alpha
is idempotent-separating if and only if
\alpha\subseteq\greenH
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
a\,\alpha\,b
, then
aa^{-1}\,\alpha\,bb^{-1}
and
a^{-1}a\,\alpha\,b^{-1}b
. Since
\alpha
is idempotent-separating,

aa^{-1}=bb^{-1}
and
a^{-1}a=b^{-1}b
, which is equivalent to
a\,\greenH\,b
.
(
\Rightarrow
) The restriction of
\greenH
to
E(S)
is
0_{E(S)}
.
∎

3.
Partial automorphisms of normal inverse subsemigroups

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. A partial bijection
\phi:X\to Y
,
X,Y\subseteq S
, is said to be a

partial automorphism
if
X
and
Y
are inverse subsemigroups of
S
and
\phi
is an isomorphism from
X
onto
Y
.
The set
\mathrm{PAut}(S)
of all partial automorphisms of
S
is an inverse submonoid of
\mathcal{I}(S)
, which we call
the
partial automorphism monoid
of
S
.

Throughout this subsection, let
N
be a normal inverse subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
S
. Let
T_{N}
denote the monoid of all (total) transformations of
N
. For each
g\in S
, define
\psi_{g}:N\to N
by
\psi_{g}(a)=gag^{-1}
for all
a\in N
. Further, we define

(
\Psi_{N}
)

\Psi_{N}:S\to T_{N};\quad g\mapsto\psi_{g}\,.

Because of the identity
(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}
and Lemma
2.1
, the following holds.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
g\in S
,
\Psi_{N}:S\to T_{N}
is a homomorphism and
\ker(\Psi_{N})
is a congruence.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
g\in S
,
gNg^{-1}=gg^{-1}Ngg^{-1}
is an inverse submonoid of
N
.

Proof.

We have
gNg^{-1}=gg^{-1}gNg^{-1}gg^{-1}\subseteq gg^{-1}Ngg^{-1}\subseteq gNg^{-1}
, using normality of
N
. This
establishes the desired equality. It is immediate that
gNg^{-1}
is a subsemigroup of
N
,
gg^{-1}
is its unit element, and since

(gag^{-1})^{-1}=ga^{-1}g^{-1}
,
gNg^{-1}
is also closed under inversion.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
g,h\in S
,

(1)

g\greenR h
if and only if
gNg^{-1}=hNh^{-1}
,

(2)

g\greenL h
if and only if
g^{-1}Ng=h^{-1}Nh
.

Proof.

(1) If
g\greenR h
, then
gg^{-1}=hh^{-1}
and so we have
gNg^{-1}=gg^{-1}Ngg^{-1}=hh^{-1}Nhh^{-1}=hNh^{-1}
, using Lemma
3.2
. Conversely, if
gNg^{-1}=hNh^{-1}
, then
gg^{-1}=g\cdot g^{-1}g\cdot g^{-1}=hbh^{-1}
for some
b\in N
since
E(S)\subseteq N
. Thus
hh^{-1}\cdot gg^{-1}=hh^{-1}\cdot hbh^{-1}=hbh^{-1}=gg^{-1}
. Reversing the roles of
g
and
h
, we obtain
gg^{-1}\cdot hh^{-1}=hh^{-1}
. Since idempotents commute,
gg^{-1}=hh^{-1}
, that is,
g\greenR h
.

(2) We have
g\greenL h
if and only if
g^{-1}\greenR h^{-1}
if and only if
g^{-1}Ng=h^{-1}Nh
by part (1).
∎

For each
g\in S
, we define partial mappings
\phi_{g}:g^{-1}Ng\to gNg^{-1}\,;\ x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
g\in S
,
\phi_{g}
is a partial automorphism of
N
, specifically, it is an isomorphism of
g^{-1}Ng
onto
gNg^{-1}
.

Proof.

For all
a\in N
,
\phi_{g^{-1}}\phi_{g}(g^{-1}ag)=g^{-1}gg^{-1}agg^{-1}g=g^{-1}ag
and similarly
\phi_{g}\phi_{g^{-1}}(gag^{-1})=gag^{-1}
. Thus
\phi_{g}
is a bijection from
g^{-1}Ng
to
gNg^{-1}
with inverse bijection
\phi_{g^{-1}}
. For all
a,b\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{g}(g^{-1}ag\cdot g^{-1}bg)

\displaystyle=gg^{-1}a\underline{gg^{-1}}bgg^{-1}

\displaystyle=gg^{-1}agg^{-1}\cdot gg^{-1}bgg^{-1}=\phi_{g}(g^{-1}ag)\phi_{g}(g^{-1}bg)\,,

hence
\phi_{g}
is a partial automorphism.
∎

We define

(
\Phi_{N}
)

\Phi_{N}:S\to\mathrm{PAut}(N);\quad g\mapsto\phi_{g}\,.

Lemma 3.5
.

\Phi:S\to\mathrm{PAut}(N)
is a homomorphism and
\ker(\Phi_{N})
is a congruence.

Proof.

We first show that the domains of
\phi_{g}\phi_{h}
and
\phi_{gh}
coincide. The domain of
\phi_{g}\phi_{h}
is

X=h^{-1}Nh\cap\{a\in N\mid hah^{-1}\in g^{-1}Ng\}

and the domain of
\phi_{gh}
is
Y=(gh)^{-1}Ngh
.

If
h^{-1}ah\in X
, then

h^{-1}ah=h^{-1}(hh^{-1}ahh^{-1})h\in h^{-1}(g^{-1}Ng)h=(gh)^{-1}Ngh=Y\,;

thus
X\subseteq Y
.
For the other inclusion,
Y=(gh)^{-1}Ngh\subseteq h^{-1}Nh
, and for all
x\in N
, we have

\displaystyle h((gh)^{-1}xgh)h^{-1}

\displaystyle=\underline{hh^{-1}}\underline{g^{-1}g}g^{-1}xg\underline{g^{-1}g}\underline{hh^{-1}}

\displaystyle=g^{-1}ghh^{-1}g^{-1}xghh^{-1}g^{-1}g\in g^{-1}Ng

since idempotents commute. Thus
Y\subseteq X
.

Finally, it is clear that
\phi_{g}\phi_{h}(a)=\phi_{gh}(a)
for all
a\in(gh)^{-1}Ngh
.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup, let
N
be a normal inverse subsemigroup of
S
, and let
\Psi_{N}:S\to T_{N}
and
\Phi_{N}:S\to\mathrm{PAut}(N)
be defined by (

Ψ
N

) and (

Φ
N

). Then
\ker(\Psi_{N})=\ker(\Phi_{N})
. This congruence
\zeta_{N}
is idempotent-separating, and the corresponding congruence pair is
(Z_{S}(N),0_{E(S)})
where

Z_{S}(N)=\{g\in S\mid gg^{-1}ag=gag^{-1}g\,,\forall a\in N\}\,.

Proof.

Assume
(g,h)\in\ker(\Psi_{N})
. Since
(g^{-1},h^{-1})\in\ker(\Psi_{N})
, we have
\psi_{g^{-1}}=\psi_{h^{-1}}
. In particular,
g^{-1}Ng=\psi_{g^{-1}}(N)=\psi_{h^{-1}}(N)=h^{-1}Nh
. Thus
\phi_{g}
and
\phi_{h}
have the same domains. Clearly
\phi_{g}(x)=\phi_{h}(x)
for all
x\in g^{-1}Ng
, and so
(g,h)\in\ker(\Phi_{N})
.

Conversely, assume
(g,h)\in\ker(\Phi_{N})
. Since the domains and images of
\phi_{g}
and
\phi_{h}
coincide,
g^{-1}Ng=h^{-1}Nh
and
gNg^{-1}=hNh^{-1}
. By Lemma
3.3
,
g\greenH h
. For
a\in N
, we have

\displaystyle h^{-1}ah

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\underline{hh^{-1}}a\underline{hh^{-1}}h=h^{-1}gg^{-1}agg^{-1}h=h^{-1}\phi_{g}(g^{-1}ag)h

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\phi_{h}(g^{-1}ag)h=\underline{h^{-1}h}(g^{-1}ag)\underline{h^{-1}h}=g^{-1}gg^{-1}agg^{-1}g=g^{-1}ag\,.

Thus
(g^{-1},h^{-1})\in\ker(\Psi_{N})
, and therefore
(g,h)\in\ker(\Psi_{N})
.

The preceding paragraph showed
\zeta_{N}\subseteq\greenH
, hence
\zeta_{N}
is idempotent-separating by Lemma
2.3
.

Next we verify that
(Z_{S}(N),0_{E(S)})
is a congruence pair.
For all
g\in Z_{S}(N)
,
e\in E(S)
,
ge=g\underline{g^{-1}ge}=geg^{-1}g=\underline{gg^{-1}e}g=egg^{-1}g=eg
, using
g\in Z_{S}(N)
in the third equality. Thus
g
commutes with every idempotent. Therefore
(Z_{S}(N),0_{E(S)})
is a congruence pair by Lemma
2.2
.

Suppose
g\in S
,
e\in E(S)
and
g\,\zeta_{N}\,e
. Since
\zeta_{N}\subseteq\greenH
as shown above,
g^{-1}g=gg^{-1}=e
. Then for all
a\in N
,
\underline{gag^{-1}}g=eaeg=eag=gg^{-1}ag
. Thus
g\in Z_{S}(N)
.

Conversely, suppose
(g,h)
is in the congruence corresponding to
(Z_{S}(N),0_{E(S)})
. Then
gh^{-1}\in Z_{S}(N)
and
g\greenH h
.
Consequently,
gg^{-1}=hh^{-1}
,
g^{-1}g=h^{-1}h
, and also
(gh^{-1})^{-1}gh^{-1}=hg^{-1}gh^{-1}=hh^{-1}hh^{-1}=hh^{-1}=gg^{-1}
. Now, for
a\in N
,

\displaystyle gag^{-1}

\displaystyle=gg^{-1}\cdot gag^{-1}\cdot gg^{-1}

\displaystyle=(gh^{-1})^{-1}\underline{gh^{-1}\cdot gag^{-1}\cdot(gh^{-1})^{-1}gh^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\underline{(gh^{-1})^{-1}gh^{-1}(gh^{-1})^{-1}}\cdot gag^{-1}\cdot gh^{-1}

5.
Centralizing congruences

5.1.
Terms

For all universal algebraic purposes, inverse semigroups are considered in the language
\{\cdot,\,^{-1}\}
.
(Note that the universal algebraic concepts, such as congruences, or the term condition introduced below, are sensitive to the choice of language. For example, it is fairly difficult to characterize abelian semigroups with respect to the language
\{\cdot\}

[
30
]
, while in our setting, abelian inverse semigroups are just abelian groups).

In inverse semigroups, thanks to associativity and the identities
(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}
and
(x^{-1})^{-1}=x
,
every term
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is equivalent to a term of the form
x_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}
,
where
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,...,n\}
and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}
, to be called a
normal form
of
t
.

5.2.
The term condition

Let
A
be an algebraic structure with congruences
\alpha,\beta
. The
term condition
for an
(n+1)
-ary term
t
with respect to
\alpha,\beta
, shortly
\mathrm{TC}(t,\alpha,\beta)
, is the following condition:
for every
a\,\alpha\,b
and every
u_{1}\,\beta\,v_{1}
,
\dots
,
u_{n}\,\beta\,v_{n}
,

t(a,u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=t(a,v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\quad\text{implies}\quad t(b,u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=t(b,v_{1},\dots,v_{n}).

We say that
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
, if the term condition
\mathrm{TC}(t,\alpha,\beta)
holds for every term
t
(of any arity
\geq 2
).
It is easy to show that
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
if and only if
TC(t,\alpha,\beta)
is satisfied for every term
t
in which the first variable occurs only once: indeed, we can use the term condition several times to replace every occurrence one-by-one (see
[
27
, Lemma 4.1]
for a formal proof).
A congruence
\alpha
is called

•

abelian
if
\alpha
centralizes
\alpha
,

•

central
if
\alpha
centralizes
1_{A}
.

Subsequently, the
center
of
A
, denoted by
\zeta(A)
, is the largest congruence of
A
which centralizes
1_{A}
(it is not obvious that such a congruence always exists; for inverse semigroups, we show that in Lemmas
5.6
and
5.7
). Hence
\alpha
is central if and only if
\alpha\leq\zeta(A)
.

In what follows, we will frequently use three special terms:

{\ell}(x,y)=xy,\quad m(x,y,z)=yxz,\quad r(x,y)=yx\,.

The principle observation is that in inverse semigroups, centralization reduces to the term condition for a single term.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with congruences
\alpha,\beta
. Then
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
if and only if
\mathrm{TC}(m,\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

The necessity is clear from the definitions. For the sufficiency, we first prove
\mathrm{TC}(m,\alpha,\beta)
implies both
\mathrm{TC}(\ell,\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathrm{TC}(r,\alpha,\beta)

Assume that

,

\ell(a,u)=\ell(a,v)
, that is,

. Then

Applying
\mathrm{TC}(m,\alpha,\beta)

Thus
\mathrm{TC}(\ell,\alpha,\beta)
and a dual argument yields
\mathrm{TC}(r,\alpha,\beta)
.

Consider a term
t(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{i_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}
in normal form with a single occurrence of
x_{0}
.
Again assume

and
u_{i}\,\beta\,v_{i}
for all

. Then
u_{i_{p}}^{\epsilon_{p}}\cdots u_{i_{q}}^{\epsilon_{q}}\,\beta\,v_{i_{p}}^{\epsilon_{p}}\cdots v_{i_{q}}^{\epsilon_{q}}

First, assume that
x_{0}
occurs with a positive exponent. There are several cases:

•

t=x_{0}
. Then
\mathrm{TC}(t,\alpha,\beta)
is always true.

•

x_{0}
is the leftmost variable of
t
. Then
\mathrm{TC}(t,\alpha,\beta)
follows from applying

\mathrm{TC}(\ell,\alpha,\beta)
to
\ell(a,u_{i_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots u_{i_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}})=\ell(a,v_{i_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}})

•

x_{0}
is the rightmost variable of

follows from
\mathrm{TC}(r,\alpha,\beta)

•

follows from applying

to

Finally, assume that
x_{0}
occurs with a negative exponent. Since

is a permutation of
S
, and congruences are invariant with respect to inversion,
\mathrm{TC}(t,\alpha,\beta)
follows from
\mathrm{TC}(t^{\prime},\alpha,\beta)
where

results from

by replacing

for

.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Nilpotent and solvable inverse semigroups

An algebraic structure
A
is called
abelian
if
\zeta(A)=1_{A}
, or, equivalently, if the congruence
1_{A}
is abelian.
It is called
nilpotent
(resp.
solvable
) if there is a chain of congruences

0_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\alpha_{n}=1_{A}

such that
\alpha_{i+1}/\alpha_{i}
is a central congruence (resp. an abelian congruence) of
A/\alpha_{i}
, for all
i
.
The length of the smallest such series is called the
nilpotency class
(resp.
solvability length
) of
A
.
Alternatively, we can define the
upper central series
inductively by
\zeta_{0}(A)=0_{A}
and
\zeta_{i+1}(A)
to be the preimage of
\zeta(A/\zeta_{i}(A))
and say that
A
is nilpotent of class
\leq n
if
\zeta_{n}(A)=1_{A}
.

In groups, the notions of abelianness, nilpotence and solvability coincide with the classical terminology. It follows immediately from Theorem
1.2
(4) that an abelian inverse semigroup is an abelian group, which can also be seen as an application of the main result of
[
30
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup and let
\alpha
be an idempotent-separating congruence. Then

.

Proof.

The congruence
\alpha
is idempotent-separating, hence
|E(S/\alpha)|\geq|E(S)|
.
On the other hand, if

, then, by Lemma

, there exists

such that

, hence
|E(S/\alpha)|\leq|E(S)|
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Since nilpotence implies solvability, it is sufficient to show that solvable inverse semigroups are groups.
We proceed by induction on
n
, the solvability length of
S
. If

, then

is an abelian group and the statement holds.
In the induction step, consider the chain

witnessing solvability. Then

is solvable of class

is abelian, it is idempotent-separating by Lemma

7.
Earlier ideas on centrality and nilpotence

7.1.
Classical center

Classically, the term “center” in semigroup theory refers to the subsemigroup

C(S)=\{a\in S\mid ax=xa\text{ for all }x\in S\}\,,

analogous to the use of the word in groups and rings.
Note that
C(S)\subseteq Z(S)
. Indeed, if
a\in C(S)
, then for all
x\in S
,

aa^{-1}xa=aa^{-1}\underline{xa}a^{-1}a=aa^{-1}\underline{ax}a^{-1}a=axa^{-1}a
.

An inverse semigroup
S
is a
Clifford semigroup
if
E(S)\subseteq C(S)
,
that is, if idempotents commute with all elements of
S
. A Clifford semigroup
S
is a semilattice of groups,

S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
.

Proposition 7.1
.

An inverse semigroup
S
is a Clifford semigroup if and only if
Z(S)=C(S)
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
. Since
E(S)\subseteq Z(S)
in every inverse semigroup, we obtain
E(S)\subseteq C(S)
and so
S
is Clifford.

(\Rightarrow)
. If
S
is a Clifford semigroup and if
a\in Z(S)
, then for all
x\in S
,

ax=aa^{-1}ax=axa^{-1}a=aa^{-1}xa=xaa^{-1}a=xa
, and so
a\in C(S)
.
∎

7.2.
Kowol-Mitsch, Meldrum, and Piochi

Kowol and Mitsch
[
8
]
and Meldrum
[
15
]
studied a notion of nilpotence for Clifford semigroups.
Instead of the upper central series of congruences, they considered the ascending series
E(S)=Z_{0}(S)\leq Z_{1}(S)\leq\cdots

of kernel parts.
A Clifford semigroup
S
is
KMM-nilpotent
if, for some
n\geq 0
,
Z_{n}(S)=S
; the smallest such
n
is the
KMM-nilpotency class

of
S
. (This is not the definition of nilpotence in either
[
8
]
or
[
15
]
, but it is equivalent
[
15
, Cor. 3.9]
.)
A Clifford semigroup
S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
is KMM-nilpotent of class
n
if and only if the groups
S_{i}
are all nilpotent of class
at most
n
and at least one of the groups attains that class.

While a nilpotent (in the universal algebra sense) Clifford semigroup is certainly KMM-nilpotent (because it is a group), the converse need
not be true. This is because the condition
Z_{n}(S)=S
does not imply
\zeta_{n}(S)=1_{S}
. For instance, a Clifford semigroup
S

is KMM-nilpotent of class
0
if and only if it is a semilattice, while
S
is KMM-nilpotent of class
1
if and only if it is commutative.

Meldrum also defined a notion of solvability for Clifford semigroups which was later extended to arbitrary inverse semigroups by
Piochi
[
22
]
. We will call this
MP-solvability
. We refer to the original papers for the definition. MP-solvability is implied by
universal algebra solvability but the converse is false; for example, an inverse semigroup is MP-solvable of class
1
if and only if it is commutative.

7.3.
Mal’cev

For variables
a,b,z_{0},z_{1},\ldots
, define two sequences of semigroup words by

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=a,\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=b

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_{n}z_{n}\rho_{n},\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}=\rho_{n}z_{n}\lambda_{n}

Then the semigroup
S
is
Mal’cev nilpotent

[
13
]
if there exists
n
such that the identity
\lambda_{n}=\rho_{n}
holds for all
a,b,z_{0},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in S
. The nilpotency class of
S
is the smallest
n
satisfying this condition. Mal’cev proved that in groups, his nilpotence is the same as the usual nilpotence, and thus group nilpotence can be entirely described in terms of semigroup identities.

Mal’cev nilpotence and its variants have been extensively studied in the semigroup literature
[
1
,
2
,
7
]
. For simplicity, we are following Mal’cev’s original definition, but some authors follow Lallement
[
9
]
and assume the variables
z_{0},z_{1},\ldots
are allowed to take values in
S^{1}
, giving a slightly stronger notion of nilpotence.

Meldrum
[
15
]
observed that for
n>0
, a Clifford semigroup
S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
is Mal’cev nilpotent of class at most
n
if and only if each group
S_{i}
is nilpotent of class at most
n
, and thus KMM-nilpotence and Mal’cev nilpotence coincide for Clifford semigroups
[
15
, p. 10]
. (The two notions differ at class
0
.)

According to Theorem
1.3
, inverse semigroups which are nilpotent in the universal algebra sense are groups. By contrast, the
Brandt semigroup of order
5
is Mal’cev nilpotent of class
2
. Thus the two notions of nilpotence do not coincide.

Now suppose we view the equation
\lambda_{n}=\rho_{n}
as defining a binary relation on
S
:

a\,\mu_{n}\,b\text{ iff }\lambda_{n}=\rho_{n}\text{ for all }z_{0},...,z_{n-1}\in S\,.

In inverse semigroups, these relations turn out to be tolerances: reflexive, symmetric and compatible (i.e., inverse subsemigroups of
S\times S
). We will call these the
Mal’cev tolerances
of
S
. Here are the first few written explicitly:

\displaystyle a\,\mu_{0}\,b

\displaystyle\text{ iff }a=b,

\displaystyle a\,\mu_{1}\,b

\displaystyle\text{ iff }az_{0}b=bz_{0}a\text{ for all }z_{0}\in S\,,

\displaystyle a\,\mu_{2}\,b

\displaystyle\text{ iff }az_{0}bz_{1}bz_{0}a=bz_{0}az_{1}az_{0}b\text{ for all }z_{0},z_{1}\in S\,.

By Theorem
1.2
,
\zeta_{1}(S)=\greenH\cap\,\mu_{1}
. Using Prover9
[
17
]
, we have verified that
\zeta_{2}(S)=\greenH\cap\,\mu_{2}
.
