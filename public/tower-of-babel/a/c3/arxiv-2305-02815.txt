Variational Quantum Algorithms for Simulation of Lindblad Dynamics
Tasneem Watad, Netanel H. Lindner

Abstract
We introduce a variational hybrid classical-quantum algorithm to simulate the Lindblad master equation and its adjoint for time-evolving Markovian open quantum systems and quantum observables. Our method is based on a direct representation of density matrices and quantum observables as quantum superstates. We design and optimize low-depth variational quantum circuits that efficiently capture the unitary and non-unitary dynamics of the solutions. We benchmark and test the algorithm on different system sizes, showing its potential for utility with near-future hardware.



I
Introduction

Great efforts are currently being put into the development of new generations of quantum computers (QCs), showing impressive progress in terms of their scale and noise rates.
Triggered by this exciting progress, much attention is directed towards unraveling how devices available in the near future can advance various science and technology fields
Preskill 2018
.
Quantum simulations (QS)
Feynman 1982
;
Lloyd 1996
is considered one of the top natural and immediate applications in which quantum devices can give an advantage over classical digital computers. Numerous quantum algorithms have been proposed for QSs, that aim to approximate the time evolution operator with a controllable error
Berry et al. 2015
;
Berry et al. 2007
;
Jordan et al. 2012
;
Low and Chuang 2017
. However, these methods are challenging for current and near-term quantum devices which do not operate in the fault-tolerant regime.

Recent efforts focus on developing a class of variational algorithms based on the approach of hybrid quantum-classical computation which aim to be better suited for near-term, intermediate scale QCs. The most known variational hybrid algorithm is the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) algorithm
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
McClean et al. 2016
, which is based on the quantum variational principle and aims to estimate the ground state energy of a physical Hamiltonian. This algorithm regards the quantum device as a co-processor that measures the energy of a quantum system and its derivatives evaluated for a set of variational states, and the optimization of the parameters in the variational manifold is carried by a classical co-processor. Motivated by this algorithm, several variational algorithms for quantum dynamics simulations (VQDS)
Lin et al. 2021
;
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Li and Benjamin 2017
;
Endo et al. 2020
;
Cirstoiu et al. 2020
;
Benedetti et al. 2021
;
Barison et al. 2021
;
Berthusen et al. 2021
have been proposed to solve the problem of time-evolving the state of an isolated quantum system.
Many quantum systems of interest are inevitably coupled to an external environment. In order to study such systems, methods for simulating open systems dynamics are needed. In many cases, a Markovian approximation gives a good description of the dynamics of the external environment. The dynamics of such systems is governed by the Lindblad master equaiton.

In this letter, we propose and demonstrate an algorithm for simulating the Lindblad master equation for open quantum systems based on the recently proposed Hybrid variational quantum algorithms, the time-dependent variational algorithm (TDVA)
Li and Benjamin 2017
and the variational Trotter compression (VTC) algorithm

By generalizing the ansatz used for this task, we obtain a method for performing variational simulations of the Lindblad master equation for open quantum systems with unitary quantum circuits, and without the need for postselection of ancilla qubits

Han et al. 2021

II
Quantum Variational Solver for the Lindblad Master Equation

The Lindblad equation capturing the dynamics of an open quantum system state
\hat{\rho}
in the Schroedinger picture is given by
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976

\dot{\hat{\rho}}=\mathcal{L}^{s}(\hat{\rho})\equiv\mathcal{L}^{s}_{H}(\hat{\rho})+\mathcal{D}^{s}(\hat{\rho}).

(1)

The Liouvillian superoperator
\mathcal{L}^{s}
as defined here is composed of a separated unitary part
\mathcal{L}_{H}^{s}(\hat{\rho})=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
and a non-unitary dissipative part, known as the dissipator and is defined as

\mathcal{D}^{s}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{i}{\left[\hat{L_{i}}\hat{\rho}\hat{L_{i}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L_{i}}^{\dagger}\hat{L_{i}}\hat{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\rho}\hat{L_{i}}^{\dagger}\hat{L_{i}}\right]},

(2)

where
\hat{L}_{i}^{\prime}s
are the jump (Lindblad) operators of the model. In the following, we use the notation
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\cdot}\kern 0.88887pt\right\rangle\kern-2.22221pt\right\rangle}
to describe the representation of operators as superstates in the Hilbert–Schmidt space of operators. The representation of the superoperators
\mathcal{L}^{s}
,
\mathcal{L}_{H}^{s}
and
\mathcal{D}^{s}
in this space are denoted as
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{L}_{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
, respectively. The evolution equation for the density matrix of
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
is then written as

i\partial_{t}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}=i\mathcal{L}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle},

(3)

\mathcal{H}_{SQ}
is an effective “super Hamiltonian” which can be non-Hermitian due to the dissipator part. The formal solution of this equation reads

\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}=e^{-i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}t}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}.

(4)

Analogously, the dynamics of a quantum observable
\hat{O}
under the Heisenberg pictures is governed by the adjoint master equation,

\dot{\hat{O}}=\mathcal{L}^{s^{\dagger}}(\hat{O})=\mathcal{L}_{H}^{s^{\dagger}}+\mathcal{D}^{s^{\dagger}}(\hat{O}),

(5)

\mathcal{L}^{s^{\dagger}}
is known as the adjoint Liouvillian, and we introduce the adjoint super Hamiltonian defined as
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}^{\dagger}\coloneqq i\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
where
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is the operator representation of
\mathcal{L}^{s^{\dagger}}
.
Similar to the density matrix, regarding the quantum observable
\hat{O}
as a superstate, the evolution of
\hat{O}
will then be given by
\mathinner{\left|\left.{O(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}=\exp\left(-i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}^{\dagger}t\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{O(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
.
Throughout this section, we will focus on solving Eq. (
1
). However, the method can be straightforwardly adapted for obtaining a variational solution for
\mathinner{\left|\left.{O(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
.

A central step of the algorithm is mapping the time evolution problem of the density matrix
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}
, representing the state of a quantum system of
N
qubits which we will refer to as “spins”, (or similarly, the quantum operator
\mathinner{\left|\left.{O}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$O$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.46666pt\right\rangle}
), into a problem of time evolving a quantum system of
2N
qubits state under a local Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}
(
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}^{\dagger}
).
To achieve this, we represent the superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}
on a quantum computer comprising of
2N
qubits using the identification:
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}=\sum_{\vec{\alpha}}c_{\vec{\alpha}}

\mathinner{\left|\left.{P_{\vec{\alpha}}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$P_{\vec{\alpha}}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.46666pt\right\rangle},

where
{\vec{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{N})
,
\alpha_{j}\in\{0,x,y,z\}
,
c_{\vec{\alpha}}
’s are real coefficients and
\mathinner{\left|\left.{P_{\vec{\alpha}}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$P_{\vec{\alpha}}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.46666pt\right\rangle}
’s are the superstate representation of the regular Pauli basis elements
P_{\vec{\alpha}}
’s. The
P_{\vec{\alpha}}
’s are represented via the map

P_{\vec{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\otimes_{j=0}^{N-1}\sigma_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\rightarrow\mathinner{\left|\left.{P_{\vec{\alpha}}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$P_{\vec{\alpha}}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.46666pt\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\otimes_{j=0}^{N-1}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\sigma_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\sigma_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.31425pt\right\rangle}

(6)

and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\sigma_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\sigma_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.31425pt\right\rangle}
is equal to
\mathinner{\left|\left.{00}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$00$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.15556pt\right\rangle}
,
\mathinner{\left|\left.{01}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$01$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.15556pt\right\rangle}
,
\mathinner{\left|\left.{10}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$10$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.15556pt\right\rangle}
and
\mathinner{\left|\left.{11}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$11$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.15556pt\right\rangle}
for
\alpha_{j}=0,x,y
and
z
respectively.
In the new basis, we consider
k_{1}
-local jump operators
L_{j}
’s, see Eq. (
2
), and
k_{2}
-local Hamiltonian of the form
H=\sum_{j}{h_{j}}

L_{j}
and

acts on at most
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
qubits respectively. Under these locality conditions,
\mathcal{L}
will be
2\cdot max(k_{1},k_{2})-
local. In addition, each unit cell composed of one spin in the original physical system is mapped into a larger unit cell composed of two qubits.
For the single-site jump operators and
2
-local Hamiltonian case, i.e.,
k_{1}=1
and
k_{2}=2
,
\mathcal{L}_{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
read as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{H}}=\sum_{\underset{even}{i=0}}^{2N-2}\sum_{\vec{\alpha}}{c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\sum_{\underset{even}{i=0}}^{2N-2}\sum_{\vec{\beta}}{c^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}\Delta^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}}+\sum_{\underset{even}{i=0}}^{2N-2}\sum_{\vec{\gamma}}{c^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}\Delta^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}}

where
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
,
\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1})
,
\vec{\gamma}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
and
\alpha_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
all take values
\in\{0,x,y,z\}
.
Here
\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}=\sigma^{\alpha_{0}}_{i}\sigma^{\alpha_{1}}_{i+1}\sigma^{\alpha_{2}}_{i+2}\sigma^{\alpha_{3}}_{i+3}
and
c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}
are pure imaginary coefficients. Note that when the dissipator,
\mathcal{D}^{s}
, is mapped to an operator acting on the superstate, it can be decomposed into its Hermitian and an anti-Hermitian components. These components correspond to
\Delta^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}=\sigma^{\gamma_{0}}_{i}\sigma^{\gamma_{1}}_{i+1}
and
\Delta^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}=\sigma^{\beta_{0}}_{i}\sigma^{\beta_{1}}_{i+1}
respectively, where
c^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}
’s are real coefficients and
c^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}
’s are purely imaginary.
Given this representation, we use the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}
to time evolve the superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}
using a variational algorithm for time-evolving quantum states.
To time evolve the superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}
under the super Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}
, we consider a family of parametrized superstates,
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)$}}\right\rangle\kern-7.20003pt\right\rangle},
which comprise a variational manifold in the Hilbert-Schmidt space. Our goal is to find the parameters
\vec{\theta}
for which
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)$}}\right\rangle\kern-7.20003pt\right\rangle}
gives the best description of
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
. To this end, we will consider two types of hybrid classical-quantum variational algorithms: the time-dependent variational algorithm (TDVA)
Li and Benjamin 2017
and the variational Trotter compression (VTC) algorithm
Lin et al. 2021
;
Berthusen et al. 2021
;
Barison et al. 2021
. The TDVA method based on the classical McLachlan’s variational principle
McLachlan 1964
is obtained from minimizing the error of the evolution of the variational superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)$}}\right\rangle\kern-7.20003pt\right\rangle},
according to the evolution equation Eq. (
3

L^{2}=\left\lVert\left(d/dt+i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\left(\vec{\theta}\left(t\right)\right)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}\right\rVert^{2}.

(8)

Assuming
\vec{\theta}
is real, this minimization yields a set of linear equations of motion for the variational parameters
\vec{\theta}
, given by

\sum_{j}A_{ij}^{R}\dot{\theta_{j}}=C_{i}^{I},

(9)

where the elements of the real part of matrix
A^{R}
and the imaginary part of vector
C^{I}
are

\displaystyle A_{ij}^{R}=Re\left(\frac{\partial\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-4.5pt\left\langle{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right|\right.}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle C_{i}^{I}=Im\left(\frac{\partial\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-4.5pt\left\langle{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right|\right.}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}\right).

While Eq. (
10
) was originally considered for simulations of a quantum state evolving under a Hermitian Hamiltonian, in Appendix
A
we show that it holds also in the case of a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}
.
Due to the Hermitian part of the dissipator, Eq. (
7
), the state
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
is not normalized. However, as we will show in the following section, we can rewrite this ansatz as

\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)$}}\right\rangle\kern-7.20003pt\right\rangle}=a(t)\cdot\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{2}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathinner{\left|\left.{0^{\otimes 2N}}\kern 1.68242pt\right\rangle\kern-5.88855pt\right\rangle}\equiv a\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{u}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}^{u}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)$}}\right\rangle\kern-7.20003pt\right\rangle},

(11)

where the gates
{\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R}_{1}}
are unitaries and depend on the parameters
\vec{\theta}
,
a(t)
is a complex parameter and
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{u}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}^{u}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}
is a normalized state.
With this form, and regarding
a(t)
as a complex variational parameter, the elements of
A^{R}
and
C^{I}
in Eq. (
9
) can be expressed as a linear combination of expectation values of the form

\displaystyle\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-5.88855pt\left\langle{0^{\otimes 2N}}\right|\right.}

\displaystyle\left(\otimes_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}_{m-k+1}\mathcal{R}_{m-k+1}^{\dagger}\right)\cdot

(12)

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\otimes_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{k}\mathcal{V}_{k}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{0^{\otimes 2N}}\kern 1.68242pt\right\rangle\kern-5.88855pt\right\rangle}.

The gates
\mathcal{V}_{k}
’s and

’s are obtained from the derivatives of
\mathcal{R}_{k}
’s and their complex conjugates, respectively, and are trivial for most values of

elements. The variational algorithm proceeds as follows. First, the elements

are evaluated on the quantum computer. Appendix
B
explains how to evaluate these elements using a quantum computer with short-depth circuits for unitary

;

9

\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)+\delta t\cdot\dot{\vec{\theta}}\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-6.29997pt\right\rangle}
where

For simulations based on the VTC method, we consider the ansatz described in Eq. (

(13)

The variational parameters are then optimized to minimize this cost function. To achieve this, since
a(t)
cannot be considered as a variational parameter, an alternative approach is required to either measure it or express it in terms of the parameter set
\vec{\theta}(t)
. One approach for measuring

The purity of the state,
\mathcal{P}=Tr\big(\tilde{\rho}^{2}\big)=|a|^{2}
, evolves in time according to

\frac{d\mathcal{P}}{dt}=Tr\biggl(\tilde{\rho}\frac{d\tilde{\rho}}{dt}+\frac{d\tilde{\rho}}{dt}\tilde{\rho}\biggl)

(14)

Substituting Eq. (
1

, and rearranging this equation gives

\biggl|\frac{a(t-\delta t)}{a(t)}\biggl|=\biggl(1+2\delta t\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-4.5pt\left\langle{\tilde{\rho}^{u}\big(\vec{\theta}(t-\delta t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right|\right.}\mathcal{D}\left(\delta t\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{u}\big(\vec{\theta}(t-\delta t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}\biggl)^{-1/2}.

\mathcal{D}

7

\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-4.10002pt\left\langle{P_{0}}\kern 1.36664pt\big|{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.36664pt\right\rangle\kern-4.10002pt\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}

a=\left(2^{N/2}\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-6.14993pt\left\langle{P_{0}}\kern 1.36664pt\big|{\tilde{\rho}^{u}}\kern 1.36664pt\right\rangle\kern-6.14993pt\right\rangle}\right)^{-1}.

(16)

Again, substituting Eq. (

As long as the steady state purity

To address the non-unitarity problem associated with

V(\delta t)=I^{\otimes 2N}+\mathcal{L}\delta t+\mathcal{L}^{2}\delta t^{2}+\cdots\mathcal{L}^{n}\delta t^{n}+O(\delta t^{n+1}).

(17)

Using this decomposition, one can rewrite the fidelity as a sum of expectation values of unitaries.
The value of

.
The optimal variational parameters,
\vec{\theta}(t)
can be determined by classically minimizing the cost function
1-|f|^{2}
(refer to Appendix
B
for different methods to measure this quantity).

III
Ansatz Construction

Embedding the physical properties of the specific problem into the design of the ansatz is crucial for optimizing the algorithm’s performance. We focus on variational ansatze inspired by the conventional Suzuki-Trotter approximation structure.

III.1
Ansatz for unitary time evolution

We first consider the case of a closed quantum system. For a quantum state
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho}\kern 0.86108pt\right\rangle\kern-2.15277pt\right\rangle}
that time evolves under Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{H}
as described in Eq. (
7
), the unitary time evolution superoperator
\mathcal{U}(t)=\exp\left(-i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}t\right)
can be approximated using the Suzuki-Trotter expansion as

\mathcal{U}(t)=e^{-i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}t}=\left(\Pi_{i:even,\vec{\alpha}}e^{c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}t/n}\right)^{n}+O(t^{2}/n).

(18)

Here,
\Delta t=t/n
is small, and each operation
\hat{V}(t)=\Pi_{i:even,\vec{\alpha}}\ \exp\left(c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}t/n\right)
is a single Trotter step.
The corresponding variational ansatz takes the form

\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{c}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}=\Pi_{r=1}^{m}\Pi_{i:\text{even},\vec{\alpha}}{\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\right)}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\rho(0)$}}\right\rangle\kern-6.00002pt\right\rangle},

(19)

where
\vec{\theta}=\{\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\}_{i,r,\vec{\alpha}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\right)=\exp\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}c^{h}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}\right)
.
Equivalently, the ansatze are composed of a sequence of “large” Trotter steps. In this ansatz, the increment
\Delta t
in the conventional Trotterization approximation is replaced by
\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}
that can take any value in the range
[0,2\pi)
. This type of ansatz gives an efficient description of the superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
if it gives a good approximation with a small value of
m
.

In the limit of short evolution time, the exact evolved state resides on the tangent space of the Trotter ansatz’s variational manifold, providing an accurate description of the state for small values of
t
. This is crucial for the algorithm’s performance in approximating the evolution at large times, as each time step heavily relies on the evolution history.

Moreover, the unitary operator
\exp\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\right)
representing a four-qubit or three-qubit gate can be efficiently decomposed into single-qubit and two-qubit gates, as demonstrated in
Clinton et al. 2021
. Additionally, any two-qubit gate can be decomposed into single-qubit gates and CNOT gates, as shown in
Vatan and Williams 2004
;
Vidal and Dawson 2004
;
Kraus and Cirac 2001
.

We further partition the set of gates
\{\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}
into completely commuting subsets, where the chosen partition corresponds to the fewest number of subsets. Within each Trotter step, all the gates,
\{\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}
, within each subset, are assigned an equal parameter. This significantly reduces the parameters vector
\vec{\theta}
. The resulting ansatz is denoted by
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{c}_{1}(\vec{\theta})}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-3.75pt\right\rangle}
(see Appendix
B
for details about the construction of the anzats).

III.2
Ansatz for Lindblad dynamics

In the open system problem, since the dynamics of
\hat{\rho}
under Eq. (
1
) is non-unitary, the norm of the superstate
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
,
\sqrt{\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-5.24997pt\left\langle\ {\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt|{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}}
, changes throughout the evolution.
A trotter-like ansatz for
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(t)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}(\vec{\theta})}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-3.75pt\right\rangle}=\Pi_{r=1}^{m}\biggl[\biggl(\Pi_{i:even}\Pi_{\vec{\alpha}}{\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\right)}\biggr)\cdot

(20)

\displaystyle\biggl(\Pi_{i:even}\Pi_{\vec{\beta}}{\mathcal{U}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}^{u}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{{\beta}}}\right)}\biggr)\cdot\biggl(\Pi_{i:even}\Pi_{\vec{\gamma}}{\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\gamma}}\right)}\biggr)\biggr]\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}

Where
\vec{\theta}=\{\{\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\}_{i,r,\vec{\alpha}},\{\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\beta}}\}_{i,r,\vec{\beta}},\{\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\gamma}}\}_{i,r,\vec{\gamma}}\}
.
\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\gamma}}\right)=\exp\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\gamma}}c^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}\Delta^{n}_{i,{\vec{\gamma}}}\right)
are non-unitaries corresponding to the non-unitary part of the dissipator
\mathcal{D}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}^{u}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\beta}}\right)=\exp\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\beta}}c^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}\Delta^{u}_{i,{\vec{\beta}}}\right)
are unitaries corresponding to the unitary part of the dissipator
\mathcal{D}
. As aforementioned,
\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\alpha}}^{h}\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}\right)=\exp\left(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\alpha}}c^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\Lambda^{h}_{i,{\vec{\alpha}}}\right)
, corresponding to
\mathcal{L}_{H}
. See Eq. (
7
) for the decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
. Similar to
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}_{1}^{c}}\kern 1.44441pt\right\rangle\kern-5.05553pt\right\rangle}
, all gates corresponding to
\mathcal{L}_{H}
involved in each Trotter step will be partitioned into commuting subsets, and equal parameters are assigned to all the variational gates within each subset. A similar partition is performed for the all the gates corresponding to the unitary part of the dissipation.

In the context of non-unitary dynamics, a significant challenge lies in efficiently implementing the non-unitary gates,
\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}
’s, involved in the quantum circuits needed for computing matrices
A
and
C
in the TDVA framework and the fidelity
f
in the VTC framework. One approach to achieve this is to embed the non-unitary gate in a larger unitary gate acting on an ancilla qubit, utilizing post-selection or projection
Daskin and Kais 2017
;
Terashima and Ueda 2005
;
Lin et al. 2021
. However, as the number of such non-unitary gates is linear in the system size, the postselection procedure will result in an exponential cost. To avoid this cost, we propose an alternative method of replacing the dissipative non-unitary part with a unitary implementation. This is based on the fact that given a non-unitary gate
G
acting on a given qubits quantum state
|\psi\rangle
, it is always possible to find a unitary
U
such that

G|\psi\rangle=a\cdot U|\psi\rangle,

(21)

where
a
is a complex factor. Note that
U
and
a
depend on
G
and
|\psi\rangle
.
If
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
is a product state, and the jump operators
\hat{L}_{i}
’s in Eq. (
2
) are single-site, a simplification can be made for the first large Trotter step of the non-unitary part. Namely, a set of 2-qubit unitaries
\mathcal{U}_{i}^{n}
’s, simulating the action of the non-unitary gates
\Pi_{\vec{\gamma}}\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}(\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}})
’s according to Eq. (
21
) can be classically computed. Each unitary
\mathcal{U}_{i}^{n}
satisfies
a_{i,1}\mathcal{U}_{i}^{n}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}=\Pi_{\vec{\gamma}}\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}(\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}})\mathinner{\left|\left.{\rho(0)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
, where
a_{i,1}
is a complex factor. In a similar way, a set of unitaries
\mathcal{U}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}
’s for simulating the derivative of
\Pi_{\vec{\gamma}}\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}(\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}})
’s with respect to

\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}}
’s and the corresponding complex factors
a_{i,r=1,\vec{\gamma}}
’s can be classically computed.
However, this is more challenging for the later Trotter steps
(r>1)
where the computation qubits are already in an entangled state. For these Trotter steps, we replace each one of the terms
\Pi_{\vec{\gamma}}\mathcal{G}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}^{n}(\theta_{i,r}^{\vec{\gamma}})
in the ansatz, Eq. (
20
), with
a_{i,r}\mathcal{G}^{g}_{i}
, where
a_{i,r}
is a complex factor and
\mathcal{G}^{g}_{i}
is a general two-qubit variational quantum circuit. Each of these circuits depends on
15
variational parameters:
\vec{\theta}^{g}_{r,i}=\left(\theta_{r,i,0}^{g},\theta_{r,i,1}^{g}\cdots\theta_{r,i,14}^{g}\right)
. The obtained ansatz is denoted by
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{o}(\vec{\theta})}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-3.75pt\right\rangle}
. The set
\{\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}}\}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}
is reduced to
\{\theta_{i,r=1}^{\vec{\gamma}}\}^{\prime}_{i,\vec{\gamma}}
using a similar partition to this performed on the unitary parts.
This ansatz can be rewritten as

\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{o}\left(\vec{\theta}(t)\right)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}=a(t)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{u}\left(\vec{\theta}(t)\right)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}

(22)

where
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{u}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}^{u}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}
is a quantum state with a unit norm that is given by unitaries acting on the initial state, and
a(t)=\Pi_{i,r>0}a_{i,r}
.

We use this ansatz,
\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}^{o}\left(\vec{\theta}(t)\right)}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}
for both the TDVA and VTC methods. With this form, the elements of
A^{R}
and
C^{I}
, Eq. (
9
), and the fidelity
f
, Eq. (
13
), can be rewritten as a combination of expectation values of unitaries, and thus can be evaluated on a quantum computer using short depth circuits as shown in Appendix
B
.

V
Summary

In summary, we discussed a variational algorithm for a hybrid quantum-classical computer to simulate both the Lindblad master equation and its adjoint. The algorithm builds upon previously introduced variational quantum algorithms and has similar hardware requirements, up to a linear factor. We further benchmarked the algorithm on two numerical experiments. In the first experiment, we solved the adjoint master equation for time-evolving quantum observables for a closed TFI model reducing this problem to solving the Heisenberg equation of motion. Throughout this experiment, we demonstrated that the ansatz gives an excellent approximation. The second experiment aimed to time-evolve the quantum state using an open version of the TFI model. We showed that a variational state involving non-unitary gates that can be prepared with unitary gates can be used. We also demonstrated that this ansatz gives a good approximation.

Derivation of the TDVA-Based Dynamics Equation Eq. (
9
)

To derive Eq. (
9
), we start by applying McLachlan’s variational principle on the evolution equation Eq. (
3
). McLachlan’s principle is obtained by minimizing the distance between the two sides of the evolution equation, i.e., demanding

\delta\left\lVert\left(\frac{d}{dt}-\mathcal{L}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}(\vec{\theta})}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}(\vec{\theta})$}}\right\rangle\kern-6.00002pt\right\rangle}\right\rVert=0.

(24)

Or equivalently,
\delta\left\lVert\left(\frac{d}{dt}+i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}(\theta(t))}\kern 1.49997pt\right\rangle\kern-5.24997pt\right\rangle}\right\rVert^{2}=0
, where
\mathcal{H}_{SQ}=i\mathcal{L}
is what we defined to be the super Hamiltonian. From here, we obtain,

\delta\left[\left(\left(\frac{d}{dt}+i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\left(\frac{d}{dt}+i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\right)\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}\right)\right]=0

(25)

Substituting
\frac{d}{dt}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}=\sum_{j}{\dot{\theta}_{j}\frac{d}{d\theta_{j}}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}\big(\vec{\theta}(t)\big)}\kern 1.79997pt\right\rangle\kern-4.5pt\right\rangle}}
yields

\displaystyle\delta\left[\left(\sum_{j}{\dot{\theta}_{j}\frac{d\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-5.7778pt\left\langle\ {\tilde{\rho}}\kern 1.44441pt\right|\right.}}{d\dot{\theta}_{j}}}-i\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-5.7778pt\left\langle\ {\tilde{\rho}}\kern 1.44441pt\right|\right.}{\mathcal{H}_{SQ}}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\sum_{i}{\dot{\theta}_{i}\frac{d\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}}{d\dot{\theta}_{i}}}+i\mathcal{H}_{SQ}\mathinner{\left|\left.{\tilde{\rho}}\ \vphantom{\hbox{$\tilde{\rho}$}}\right\rangle\kern-5.7778pt\right\rangle}\right)\right]

(26)

Appendix C
Tikhonov Method

A main serious concern arises when matrix
A^{R}
in Eq. (
9
) is ill-conditioned. In particular, for ansatze with complex structures, it can be strictly singular, and therefore, it is necessary to employ regularization methods to find a stable solution for this equation. One of the most widely used variational regularization methods for obtaining approximate solutions for such problems is the Tikhonov regularization method. Tikhonov regularization was implemented in the simulations shown in Fig.
2
and Fig.
3
where the main idea is as follows. Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with rank
r
an
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
a given vector. The least squares solution of the linear equations
Ax=b
is a minimization problem that aims to find an approximate solution
x_{LSM}
that satisfies

x_{LSM}=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lVert Ax-b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(41)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
denotes the 2-norm (Euclidean norm). Solving this problem by substituting the SVD decomposition of the
A
matrix,
A=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{T}
where
u_{1},u_{2},\cdots u_{m}
and
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots v_{n}
are two sets of orthonormal bases and
\sigma_{i}
’s are the singular values, yields

x_{LSM}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\frac{u_{i}^{T}bv_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}}.

(42)

Such a solution is contaminated by the noise in directions corresponding to small singular values of the matrix
A
and, therefore, can be very unstable, and its norm can be very large. A small change in vector
b
can lead to a large change in the solution vector. With the hope to filter the effect of the noise out, Tikhonov proposed to replace this problem with the penalized minimization problem

x_{TIK}=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lVert Ax-b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(43)

Again, using the SVD decomposition of the matrix
A
, the solution to the modified minimization problem is

x_{TIK}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}u_{i}^{T}bv_{i}}.

(44)

Notice that the obtained solution is similar to
x_{LSM}
, but each term in the summation is multiplied by a filter factor which is equal to
\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}
. The solution efficiency depends on the choice of the regularization parameter,
\lambda
, that controls a trade-off between minimizing the 2-norm of
x
, and hence the solution being stable, and how small the residual
\left\lVert Ax-b\right\rVert_{2}
is which defines how accurate the solution is. An efficient way to determine the regularization parameter is using what is known as the L-curve
Lawson and Hanson 1974
;
Hansen and O’Leary 1993
;
Hansen 1992
. L-curve is a plot of the points
\left(\rho(\lambda),\eta(\lambda)\right)
where
\rho(\lambda)=\left\lVert Ax_{TIK}-b\right\rVert_{2}
and
\eta(\lambda)=\left\lVert x_{TIK}\right\rVert_{2}
. The L-curve has a shape similar to the letter L where the chosen regularization parameter is chosen to be the one corresponding to the corner of the
L-
shaped curve. A justification for this is that the flat part of this shape corresponds to when the solution
x_{TIK}
is affected by regularization errors, and the vertical part of the L-curve is affected by errors due to noise etc. Thus, a good choice that gives a good balance between the accuracy and the stability of the solution is the corner. However, sometimes the curve does not have one corner, and hence it is recommended to choose the corner which is the leftmost of some bound,
M
, on the
2-norm
of the solution.

Another alternative method that can be implemented is the truncated SVD (TSVD) method, according to which the solution is given by
x_{TSVD}=A^{-1}_{k}b
where
A^{-1}_{k}=V\Sigma_{k}^{-1}U^{T}
.
A^{-1}_{k}
is the pseudoinverse of
A_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{T}}
and
\Sigma_{k}^{-1}\in R^{n\times m}
is a diagonal matrix with the values
\{1/\sigma_{1},1\sigma_{2},\cdots,1/\sigma_{k},0\cdots,0,0\}
on the diagonal.
Moreover, as in the Tikhonov method, regularization methods can be implemented to choose where the SVD decomposition should be truncated, i.e.,
k
.
